Probabilistic Linear Logic Programming with an application to Bayesian Networks computations
Matteo Acclavio, Roberto Maieli

Abstract
Bayesian networks are a canonical formalism for representing probabilistic dependencies, yet their integration within logic programming frameworks remains a nontrivial challenge, mainly due to the complex structure of these networks. In this paper, we propose probLO ( probabilistic Linear Objects ) an extension of Andreoli and Pareschi’s LO language which embeds Bayesian network representation and computation within the framework of multiplicative-additive linear logic programming. The key novelty is the use of multi-head Prolog-like methods to reconstruct network structures, which are not necessarily trees, and the operation of slicing , standard in the literature of linear logic, enabling internal numerical probability computations without relying on external semantic interpretation.

Introduction

Probabilistic logic programming (PLP) has emerged as a key paradigm for integrating structured logical knowledge with uncertain information
[
7

. However, representing and reasoning about probabilistic dependencies in a Bayesian network within logic programming frameworks remains a nontrivial challenge due to the complex structure of these networks, where variables can have multiple parents and children.
In fact, most existing approaches in probabilistic logic programming relies on classical or Horn-clause logic as their logical framework, where each method typically has a head made of a single atom, thus suggesting a tree-like structure of dependencies.
Furthermore, the absences of cyclic dependencies in Bayesian networks suggests that, even if a variable can be influenced by multiple parent variables, its use in a computation should be linear, i.e., each variable should be computed exactly once in a given query.

To understand why the possibility of multi-head clauses is crucial to represent Bayesian networks, let us briefly recall some basic notions about them.
A Bayesian network is a graphical model that represents a set of random variables and their conditional dependencies via a directed acyclic graph, where each node represents a random variable, and the directed edges represent the conditional dependencies between these variables.
See
Figure

1
for an example of a simple Bayesian network.
Each row of the
conditional probability table
, associated to a node, specifies
the
conditional probability

\mathsf{Pr}({\mathsf{X}}\mid{\mathsf{Y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{Y}}_{m})
that the (Boolean) variable
{\mathsf{X}}
takes a specific value, given that as
evidences
the (Boolean) parent variables
{\mathsf{Y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}
take values.
In the general case, conditional probabilities for
random variables
{\mathsf{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{X}}_{n}
given evidences random variables
{\mathsf{Y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}

are defined by Bayes’ Theorem as follows:

\mathsf{Pr}({\mathsf{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{X}}_{n}\mid{\mathsf{Y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{Y}}_{m})=\frac{\mathsf{Pr}({\mathsf{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{X}}_{n},{\mathsf{Y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{Y}}_{m})}{\mathsf{Pr}({\mathsf{Y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{Y}}_{m})}

where

\mathsf{Pr}({\mathsf{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{X}}_{n},{\mathsf{Y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{Y}}_{m})
is the
joint probability
that all the events
{\mathsf{X}}_{1},...,{\mathsf{X}}_{n},{\mathsf{Y}}_{1},...,{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}
happen together,
and

\mathsf{Pr}({\mathsf{Y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{Y}}_{m})
is the
marginal probability
that the evidences
{\mathsf{Y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}
happen, regardless of the values of
{\mathsf{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{X}}_{n}
.

Our driving intuition on the need of multi-head methods to represent conditional probabilities is that, as in conditional probabilities, we can interpret the outcomes
{\mathsf{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{X}}_{n}
as being
produced
simultaneously given the evidences
{\mathsf{Y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}
; thus, we can represent their computation in a logic programming framework by means of
multi-head methods
having
{\mathsf{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{X}}_{n}
as head and
{\mathsf{Y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}
as body.
In
probLO
, we can interpret conditional probabilities as multi-head methods annotated with a probability value
p\in[0,1]
, using a syntax inspired by the one adopted in
ProbLog

[
5
,
9
]
.

\underbrace{p}_{probability}::{\tt[\underbrace{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}}_{multi-head}]:\!\!-\;[\underbrace{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}}_{body}]}

(2)

The effect of the probabilistic annotation is to modify the operational semantics of the method application in such a way that, when the method is applied during a proof search, the probability of the conclusion is obtained by multiplying the probability of the premises by
p
.

As a first application of
probLO
, we show how to represent Bayesian networks and how to compute joint and marginal probabilities directly within the logic programming framework, without relying on an external semantic interpretation. We show how derivations in
probLO
naturally mimic the process of computing over a Bayesian network where probabilities, annotating the current state, are updated according to rules of the operational semantics, each corresponding to the application of a conditional probability defined in the network.
For example, in
Figure

2
we show the
probLO
derivation that computes the marginal probability of the variables of Cloudy (
{\mathsf{C}}
), and Rain (
{\mathsf{R}}
) being
True
while Wet Grass (
{\mathsf{W}}
), and Traffic Jam (
{\mathsf{T}}
) being
False
with respect to Sprinklers (
{\mathsf{S}}
) in the Bayesian Network of
Figure

1
.

Definition 2

Let

•

A

is a probabilistic graphical model representing a set of random variables and their conditional dependencies via a directed acyclic graph.
The interest of Bayesian networks lies in the fact that they allow to represent joint probability distributions in a compact way, by exploiting conditional independencies between variables explicitely encoded in the network structure.
Moreover, they allow to efficiently compute marginal and conditional probability distributions of interest by exploiting these independencies.

From Logic Programming to Linear Objects

Logic programming originated with
Prolog

Prolog
’s computational interpretation relies on
unrestricted

While this assumption is suitable for many symbolic tasks, it limits
Prolog
’s expressiveness when modeling computations where resource usage matters as, e.g., state changes, process calculi, or probabilistic reasoning.

The introduction of
linear logic
(LL) by Girard provided a refined logical foundation where resources are treated explicitly: in linear logic, propositions are consumed when used (unless marked as reusable).
This perspective leads naturally to programming languages where the flow of computation corresponds to the controlled consumption and production of resources. Linear logic programming thus differs fundamentally from classical logic programming: instead of deriving consequences from static facts, it orchestrates a dynamic evolution of states represented as multisets of linear formulas.

Exploiting these ideas, Andreoli and Pareschi introduced
Linear Objects
(
LO
)
[
1
,
2
]
, a logic programming language grounded in the multiplicative-additive fragment of linear logic (
\mathsf{MALL}
).
In
LO
, methods are
Prolog
-like multi-head clauses where both preconditions and postconditions are represented as multisets of linear formulas (see
Equation

1
).
Its operational semantics is defined in terms of proof search in linear logic:
a state of the system is represented as a sequent containing the current multiset of goals and the current methods available to the program program itself, and a program execution corresponds to a proof search in linear logic, where the execution of each method corresponds to the application of a synthetic inference rule associated to a bipole formula representing the method.
See

Theorem 3.1

Let
\mathcal{G}=\langle\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\},\abxcurvearrowright\rangle
be a graph.
Then
\mathcal{G}
is acyclic iff
\vdash_{\mathsf{MLL}^{\mathsf{mix}}}{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{G}},a_{v_{1}},\ldots,a_{v_{n}}
.

Proof

The idea is that the proof search progressively visit the nodes of
\mathcal{G}
from leaves to roots, exploring a new node as soon as all its children have been processed.
We proceed by induction on the
diameter
of
\mathcal{G}
defined as the maximal length
n
of a path in
\mathcal{G}
(from a root to a leaf). Note that the diameter is well defined because
\mathcal{G}
is acyclic.

If
n=0
, then the sequent to be proved is
\mathcal{F}_{v_{1}},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{v_{n}},a_{v_{1}},\ldots,a_{v_{n}}
where
\mathcal{F}_{v_{i}}=a^{\bot}_{v_{i}}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
because all nodes are roots. The proof is immediate by applying all the bipoles.
Notice that in this passage is clear the need for the use of the
\mathsf{mix}
rule to partition the sequent into sequents of the form
\vdash\mathcal{F}_{v_{i}},a_{v_{i}}
for each vertex (i.e., connected component) of
\mathcal{G}
.

Otherwise,
n>0
and we apply all the rules associated to the bipoles
\mathcal{F}_{v}
for each leaf
v
of
\mathcal{G}
.
We obtain a sequent of the form
{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}},a_{u_{1}},\ldots,a_{u_{m}}
where
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
is the graph with vertices
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}
obtained by removing all leaves from
\mathcal{G}
.
Since
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
is still a directed acyclic graph with strictly smaller diameter than
\mathcal{G}
, we conclude by inductive hypothesis.

To prove the converse, it suffices to note that, if a cycle existed in
\mathcal{G}
, then there would be at least one bipole that could never be processed by the proof search because at least one of its ‘input’ atoms would never be available due to the cyclic dependency between nodes.

Remark 1

Even if we adopt the same way to annotate probabilities as in
ProbLog

[
5
,
9
]
, we also weight methods (not only facts) with probabilities.

Moreover, our
probLO
-conditionals do not support a constructor in which we can inject the choice directly in the head of a method.
The corresponding formula would be, in fact, of the form
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}a^{\bot}\mathop{\otimes}\cdots\mathop{\otimes}a^{\bot}})\mathop{\otimes}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}b_{1}^{i}\mathop{\bindnasrepma}\cdots\mathop{\bindnasrepma}b_{m}^{i}})
.
However, extending our syntax with such a constructor would allow us to use a single formula
\left({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}(h^{\bot}\mathop{\otimes}\cdots\mathop{\otimes}h^{\bot})}\right)\mathop{\otimes}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}b_{1}^{i}\mathop{\bindnasrepma}\cdots\mathop{\bindnasrepma}b_{m}^{i}})
to represent the conditional probability distribution, in a way similar to LP methods of
LPAD

[
21
]
.
We opt not to do so to avoid adding an additional constructor in the syntax of
probLO
.

The intuition behind a
probLO
-table is that it represents a conditional probability distribution of a variable
{\mathsf{X}}
given some evidences
{\mathsf{Y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}
.
Each
probLO
-conditional in the
probLO
-table represents the two possible outcomes of the variable
{\mathsf{X}}
(
True
or
False
) given a specific assignment of values to the evidences
{\mathsf{Y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}
represented by the body of the methods in the conditional.
We show later when modeling Bayesian networks that, during the program execution, every time a
probLO
-table is called, the current goal in the state allows it to uniquely choose a single simple method in it.
At the same time, having
probLO
-tables instead of simple
probLO
-modules only allows for having a general purpose program goal-independent that can adapt to the given goal.

In
probLO
, we want to keep the same operational semantics of
LO
, where the usage of each method prevents its reuse, but we want to extend it to account for the probabilistic nature of the methods.
Therefore, other than keeping track of the current state of the program, i.e., its current goal and the current available methods, we also want to keep track of the probability of reaching the current state from the initial state of the program.
To this aim, we represent a state of a
probLO
program as a
weighted sequent
of the form

\underset{\text{probability}}{p}::\;\underset{\text{program}}{\mathsf{P}}\;;\;\underset{\text{goal}}{\Gamma}{}

(10)

In
Figure

7
we show the rules of the operational semantics of
probLO
.
Derivations in this system are built bottom-up and represent the possible executions of a
probLO
program.
The probability value annotating the sequent root of a derivation has to be interpreted as the probability of successfully reaching the initial goal.
We may also annotate these rules with the method
{\tt M}
applied by the rule instead of
\mathsf{termination}
and
\mathsf{expansion}
.
The rule
\mathsf{branching}
is used to process goals containing additive conjunctions (
\mathop{\binampersand}
) that represent uncertain events; it splits the current execution into two sub-executions, one for each branch of the conjunction, summing up their probabilities.

If we forget about probability, we can easily see that each rule in the operational semantics of

Lemma 1
(Boolean consistency)

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a Bayesian network and
\Gamma

\{{\tt t\!t_{{\mathsf{X}}}},{\tt f\!f_{{\mathsf{X}}}},{\tt t\!t_{{\mathsf{X}}}}\mathop{\binampersand}{\tt f\!f_{{\mathsf{X}}}}\}

{\mathsf{X}}

\mathcal{B}
.
Then each branch of a successful derivation in the operational semantics of
probLO
starting from a state with goal
\Gamma
, never contains both atoms
{\tt t\!t_{{\mathsf{X}}}}
and
{\tt f\!f_{{\mathsf{X}}}}
.

Proof

By definition of the program
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{B}}
as in
Definition

6

probLO

for a variable
{\mathsf{X}}
only contains either
{\tt t\!t_{{\mathsf{Z}}}}
or
{\tt f\!f_{{\mathsf{Z}}}}
in the body of its simple methods, never both.
If a method
{\tt T}_{{\mathsf{X}}}
applied in the derivation would introduce the ‘complementary’ literal for a variable
{\mathsf{Z}}
such that
{\tt t\!t_{{\mathsf{Z}}}}
(resp.
{\tt f\!f_{{\mathsf{Z}}}}
) is already present in the current goal, then, as a consequence of
Theorem

3.1
some method will eventually go in starvation because the required precondition will never be satisfied, therefore, the proof search will fail.

Finally, we can prove that the encoding of a Bayesian network
\mathcal{B}
into a
probLO
program
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{B}}
correctly captures both joint and marginal probabilities over the variables of
\mathcal{B}
.

Theorem 4.1
(Encoding BN within probLO)

Let
\mathcal{B}=\langle V,\abxcurvearrowright,\mathcal{P}\rangle
be a Bayesian network with leaves
\{{\mathsf{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{X}}_{n}\}
.
Then

1.

the state
\mathsf{Pr}(x_{1}\ldots,x_{n})::\;\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{B}}\;;\;{\tt v\!\!v_{{\mathsf{X}}_{1}}},\ldots,{\tt v\!\!v_{{\mathsf{X}}_{n}}}
is derivable in the operational semantics of
probLO
;

2.

if
p
is the marginal probability of the variables
{\mathsf{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{X}}_{n}
with respect to the variables
{\mathsf{Y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}
, then the state

p::\;\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{B}}\;;\;{\tt v\!\!v_{{\mathsf{X}}_{1}}},\ldots,{\tt v\!\!v_{{\mathsf{X}}_{n}}},({\tt t\!t_{{\mathsf{Y}}_{1}}}\mathop{\binampersand}{\tt f\!f_{{\mathsf{Y}}_{1}}}),\ldots,({\tt t\!t_{{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}}}\mathop{\binampersand}{\tt f\!f_{{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}}})

is derivable in the operational semantics of
probLO
.

Proof

We prove each item separately, since the second one relies on the first one.

1.

By the factorization theorem (
Theorem

2.1
), we can write the joint probability as
\mathsf{Pr}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
as the product of the conditional probabilities of each variable given its parents.
For each variable
{\mathsf{X}}
in
V
, the conditional probability of
{\mathsf{X}}
given its parents
{\mathsf{Y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{Y}}_{n}
is encoded as a simple method
{\tt M}_{\,{\mathsf{X}}}^{\,\langle y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\rangle}
composing the
probLO
-table
{\tt T}_{{\mathsf{X}}}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{B}}
.
We can then build a derivation of the state
\mathsf{Pr}(x_{1}\ldots,x_{n})::\;\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{B}}\;;\;{\tt v\!\!v_{{\mathsf{X}}_{1}}},\ldots,{\tt v\!\!v_{{\mathsf{X}}_{n}}}
similarly to the proof of
Theorem

3.1
, by progressively visiting the nodes of the Bayesian network from leaves to roots, applying the methods corresponding to each conditional probability in the factorization.

Notice also that we make crucial use of
Lemma

1
to extract the appropriate simple method from each
probLO
-table.
In fact, we could see as if each atom
{\tt v\!\!v_{{\mathsf{X}}}}
in the goal acts as a ‘filter’ that only allows the application of the method corresponding to the correct row in the conditional probability table of
{\mathsf{X}}
.

To conclude, it suffices to note that the probability of the state at the bottom of the derivation is the product of the probabilities of each method application, which corresponds to the product of the conditional probabilities in the factorization, which is equal to
\mathsf{Pr}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.

2.

The proof uses a similar argument as in the previous case, but now we have to deal with the sums that occur in the marginal probability.
We proceed by induction on the number
m
of evidence variables in the marginal.
If
m=0
, then
p
is a joint probability, and we conclude by
Item

1
.
Otherwise, we proceed by induction on the number of additive conjunctions in the goal.
We can assume, without loss of generality, that the derivation starts with a
\mathsf{branching}
rule as follows:

\scriptstyle-

\scriptstyle\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}_{1}\;

\scriptstyle\mskip-4.0mu\mathchar 781\mskip-4.0mu

\scriptstyle\mskip-4.0mu\mathchar 781\mskip-4.0mu

\scriptstyle\mskip-4.0mu\mathchar 781\mskip-4.0mu

p_{1}::\;\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{B}}\;;\;\Delta,{\tt t\!t_{{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}}}

\scriptstyle-

\scriptstyle\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}_{2}\;

\scriptstyle\mskip-4.0mu\mathchar 781\mskip-4.0mu

\scriptstyle\mskip-4.0mu\mathchar 781\mskip-4.0mu

\scriptstyle\mskip-4.0mu\mathchar 781\mskip-4.0mu

p_{2}::\;\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{B}}\;;\;\Delta,{\tt f\!f_{{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}}}

\scriptstyle\mathsf{branching}\;

\scriptstyle-

\scriptstyle-

\scriptstyle-

p_{1}+p_{2}::\;\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{B}}\;;\;\Delta,({\tt t\!t_{{\mathsf{Y}}_{n}}}\mathop{\binampersand}{\tt f\!f_{{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}}}),

where
\Delta={\tt v\!\!v_{{\mathsf{X}}_{1}}},\ldots,{\tt v\!\!v_{{\mathsf{X}}_{n}}},({\tt t\!t_{{\mathsf{Y}}_{1}}}\mathop{\binampersand}{\tt f\!f_{{\mathsf{Y}}_{1}}}),\ldots,({\tt t\!t_{{\mathsf{Y}}_{m-1}}}\mathop{\binampersand}{\tt f\!f_{{\mathsf{Y}}_{m-1}}})
.
By inductive hypothesis, we have that the two sub-derivations
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
compute the marginal probability of
{\mathsf{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{X}}_{n},{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}
with respect to the variables
{\mathsf{Y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{Y}}_{m-1}
, once with
{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}=\textsf{True}
and once with
{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}=\textsf{False}
respectively, that is:

\begin{array}[]{l}p_{1}=\sum_{y_{1}\in{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{1},\ldots,y_{m-1}\in{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{m-1}}\mathsf{Pr}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m-1},{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}=\textsf{True})\\
p_{2}=\sum_{y_{1}\in{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{1},\ldots,y_{m-1}\in{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{m-1}}\mathsf{Pr}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m-1},{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}=\textsf{False})\end{array}

We conclude, since, by definition,
p_{1}+p_{2}=\sum_{y_{1}\in{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\in{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{m}}\mathsf{Pr}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})
.

Figure

we provide an alternative example of application of
Theorem

4.1
with respect to the one in
Figure

to further highlight the hidden role of the
\mathsf{mix}
in the corresponding
\mathsf{MALL}^{\mathsf{mix}}
, which is required because of the presence of multiple roots.

Remark 3

It is possible to optimize the execution of
probLO
programs encoding Bayesian networks to avoid backtracking due to the (wrong) choice of simple methods in a
probLO
-table.
In fact, by
Lemma

1
, we know that each successful derivation cannot contain both atoms
{\tt t\!t_{{\mathsf{X}}}}
and
{\tt f\!f_{{\mathsf{X}}}}
for any variable
{\mathsf{X}}
in
V
.
Therefore, we can statically analyze the initial goal of the program to determine, for each variable
{\mathsf{X}}
, which of the two atoms
{\tt t\!t_{{\mathsf{X}}}}
or
{\tt f\!f_{{\mathsf{X}}}}
; if both are present, then it is because the state contains
{\tt t\!t_{{\mathsf{X}}}}\mathop{\binampersand}{\tt f\!f_{{\mathsf{X}}}}
in the goal, and a rule
\mathsf{branching}
will be applied to branch the execution to treat the two cases separately.

This analysis allows us to determine, for each
probLO
-table
{\tt T}_{{\mathsf{X}}}
, which simple method
{\tt M}_{\,{\mathsf{X}}}^{\,\langle x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{h}\rangle}
can be applied in each branch of the derivation as soon as this information is complete for a variable
{\mathsf{X}}
, that is, if we know which of the two atoms
{\tt t\!t_{{\mathsf{Y}}}}
or
{\tt f\!f_{{\mathsf{Y}}}}
will be present for
{\mathsf{X}}
and all its parents and descendants.
At the same time, to optimize the computation, we can delay the application of the rule
\mathsf{branching}
on
{\tt t\!t_{{\mathsf{X}}}}\mathop{\binampersand}{\tt f\!f_{{\mathsf{X}}}}
until the inforamtion about
{\mathsf{X}}
is strictly necessary to aboid unnecessary duplications of the state via branching. For example, in
Figure

2
we could have start the execution by applying
\mathsf{branching}
, but this would have caused an unnecessary duplication of applications of the
{\tt T}_{{\mathsf{T}}}
in both branches.

5
Conclusion, related and future works

We presented
probLO
, a linear logic programming languages whose multi-head methods are endowed with discrete probability values.
This language adopts an operational and modular interpretation of probabilistic reasoning within the framework of Linear Objects (
LO
).
We proved that
probLO
is expressive enough to encode Bayesian networks, and that its operational semantics correctly captures both joint and marginal probabilities through proof search.

Related works on Bayesian Networks and Linear Logic.

Other related works, based on Bayesian Proof Nets, as
[
15
]
and
[
8
]
, provide a proof-theoretic foundation for Bayesian inference based on a graphical interpretation of linear logic proof nets.
In their approach, the structure of a Bayesian network is reflected directly in the topology of the net, and probabilistic inference corresponds to normalization
[
8
]
or construction
[
15
]
steps in the proof net.
Our approach differs in both methodology and operational interpretation:
probLO
implements Bayesian reasoning through computations using probabilistic
LO
-methods, which are interpreted as proof search in a system of derivable rules in
\mathsf{MALL}^{\mathsf{mix}}
.
Said differenlty, while Bayesian Proof Nets emphasize a global and graph-based representation of probabilistic dependencies relying on the
correctness criterion
of proof nets,

probLO
adopts a modular rule-based perspective in which each node of the network is encoded as a
\mathsf{MALL}^{\mathsf{mix}}
bipole (called
Bayesian bipoles
in
[
15
]
).
This yields a more operational view of inference, aligning closely with logic programming and resource-sensitive computation.

Related works on Probabilistic Logic Programming Systems.

The landscape of probabilistic logic programming is dominated by languages such as
ProbLog

[
6
]
,
PRISM

[
19
,
20
]
,
LPAD
s
[
21
]
and others founded on the distribution semantics.
These frameworks extend classical logic programming by attaching probabilities to facts or to alternative rule heads, enabling probabilistic inference through weighted model counting or explanation-based inference. In
ProbLog
and
PRISM
, probabilistic choices are represented as sets of annotated alternatives whereas
LPAD
s extend
Prolog
with probabilistic disjunctive rule heads.
In
probLO
, the probabilistic aspect is limited to the measure of the reachability of the goal, whether the non-deterministic choices made during the proof search are controlled by the goal itself.

Future work.

A direction for future work is to investigate how classical optimization techniques for Bayesian inference can be expressed within our framework.
In particular, we aim to study whether algorithms such as
clique tree
(or
junction tree
)
[
14
,
13
]
can be captured by the operational semantics of
probLO
, by simple extensions of the language (and the underlying logic
\mathsf{MALL}^{\mathsf{mix}}
), or encoded as a collection of
probLO
methods.
The idea is to exploit resource sensitivity of
probLO
and modular bipole structure to model optimized inference procedures directly at the proof-theoretic level.
More generally, we plan to explore the encoding of additional inference strategies, such as
variable elimination

[
22
]
and
belief propagation

[
18
]
, to assess the expressiveness and computational adequacy of
probLO
for Bayesian reasoning.
