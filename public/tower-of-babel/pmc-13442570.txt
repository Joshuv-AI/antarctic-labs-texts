Adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles reduce glycolysis and polarize macrophages toward M2 phenotype in sepsis-associated acute kidney injury via the miR-574-5p/GLUT1 axis
Authors: Jinbo Zhang, Jinling Li, Xiaohong Jin, Jinqiang Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extracellular vesicles (EVs) from adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADSCs) may exert a therapeutic benefit in sepsis-associated organ dysfunction by delivering cargos to target cells. The aims of this study were to explore the therapeutic efficacy and potential mechanism of microRNA (miR)-574-5p delivered by ADSC-EVs in sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI). EVs were isolated from mouse ADSCs and characterized by multilineage differentiation potency, morphology, and surface markers. miR-574-5p expression in ADSC-EVs was analyzed by biochemical testing. Luciferase reporter and RNA pull-down assays were employed to identify the binding relation between miR-574-5p and glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1). Cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced mouse SA-AKI model administered with ADSC-EVs was used to evaluate therapeutic effects on kidney injury. ADSC-EVs were co-cultured with RAW264.7 macrophages to assess effects on inflammation, glycolysis (lactate levels, extracellular acidification rate), and macrophage polarization. ADSC-EVs elevated miR-574-5p expression and polarized macrophages toward M2 phenotype, reducing kidney dysfunction and inflammatory response in CLP mice. In vitro, ADSC-EVs arrested lipopolysaccharide-induced M1 macrophage polarization and induced M2 macrophage polarization by upregulating miR-574-5p expression. Mechanistically, ADSC-EVs transferred miR-574-5p into macrophages, where miR-574-5p targeted GLUT1 and limited its expression. This effect contributed to the inhibition of glycolysis and promotion of M2 macrophage polarization. In vivo studies confirmed these positive effects of ADSC-EVs in SA-AKI via the miR-574-5p/GLUT1 pathway. Altogether, our findings suggest that ADSC-EVs carrying miR-574-5p target GLUT1 to restrain glycolysis and prime M2 macrophage polarization in a SA-AKI model, paving the way for establishing a therapeutic approach for SA-AKI.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Sepsis is a heterogeneous syndrome with myriad pathways contributing to life-threatening organ dysfunction and extremely high mortality [1]. The kidney is one of the most frequently affected organs during sepsis, and up to 60% of patients with sepsis experience acute kidney injury (AKI), known as sepsis-associated AKI (SA-AKI) [2]. AKI is generally defined as a disorder characterized by a rapid decrease in kidney function, which significantly worsens the already adverse prognosis of sepsis and increases the cost of health care [3, 4]. Accordingly, there is an urgent need to clarify the molecular mechanisms driving SA-AKI progression and to identify novel therapeutic targets.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are stromal cells capable of self-renewing and differentiating into multiple types of cells, which can be isolated from an array of tissues, such as adipose tissue, umbilical cord, and bone marrow [5]. Adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADSCs) have demonstrated therapeutic effects in multi-system diseases, including liver and kidney injuries, bone and joint diseases, and cancers, primarily attributable to their capabilities of repressing inflammation and initiating tissue regeneration by paracrine effects, which are partially mediated by extracellular vesicles (EVs) [6–9]. EVs are heterogeneous cell-derived membranous structures mainly encompassing exosomes and microvesicles, involved in numerous physiological processes [10]. EVs derived from ADSCs (ADSC-EVs) affect disease outcomes by directly transferring their bioactive cargoes [DNA, messenger RNAs (mRNAs), microRNAs (miRNAs or miRs), proteins/peptides, lipids, and organelles] to recipient cells [11]. For instance, miR-100 enriched in ADSC-EVs can target mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and arrest epithelial-mesenchymal transition phenotype of kidney tubular epithelial cells, thereby ameliorating kidney fibrosis and injury [12]. miR-574-5p was found to be highly expressed in EVs from MSCs (MSC-EVs) via searching expression profiles in EVmiRNA database (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/EVmiRNA//#!/). Sepsis patients with AKI have significantly lower miR-574-5p expression than those without AKI, and miR-574-5p downregulation predicts the onset of AKI in sepsis patients [13]. In the current study, it was predicted that miR-574-5p harbored binding sites for glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) using a bioinformatics database (StarBase). As a member of the major facilitator superfamily of membrane transporters, GLUT1 mediates the transport of glucose and/or fructose across various tissues and cell types [14]. Prior work has documented that GLUT1-mediated glucose metabolism in macrophages boosts a pro-inflammatory phenotype, shown by elevated secretion of inflammatory mediators, while this phenomenon can be reversed by inhibition of glycolysis [15].

Macrophages are a type of heterogeneous cells primarily classified as having two major phenotypes: M1 (pro-inflammatory) and M2 (anti-inflammatory); of them, M1 macrophages promote inflammation and elicit extensive tissue damage by producing pro-inflammatory cytokines and IL-23; in contrast, M2 macrophages secrete high amounts of anti-inflammatory cytokines to curb inflammation, facilitate tissue repair, and retain homeostasis [16]. In the event of AKI, M1 macrophages predominate and act to accelerate kidney inflammation, and conversely, M2 macrophages play a central role in the attenuation of inflammation and tissue regeneration [17]. Regulation of glycolysis has a direct influence on activation and immune function of macrophages. Glycolysis is specific to M1 polarization, and suppressing glycolysis drives transformation from M1 to M2 phenotype [18, 19]. Remarkably, miRNAs carried by exosomes from ADSCs exert therapeutic benefits for kidney stones by inducing M2 macrophage polarization for anti-inflammatory responses [20]. Taken together, the aforementioned findings allow a hypothesis that ADSC-EVs might regulate glycolysis and polarize macrophages toward M2 phenotype via the miR-574-5p/GLUT1 pathway, contributing to the alleviation of SA-AKI. Therefore, in this study, we isolated EVs from ADSCs and conducted co-culture experiments with the aim of illuminating this pathway as a potential target for combating SA-AKI.

Materials and methods

Extraction and identification of ADSC-EVs

Mouse ADSCs (PC-145m) were purchased from Saios Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (11965092, Gibco, Carlsbad, California), which contains 1% penicillin–streptomycin (acquired from Gibco, Grand Island, NY) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. The medium was renewed every 2–3 days, and cells were passaged until reaching 80–90% confluence.

Upon reaching 80% confluence, ADSCs at passage 3 were cultured in osteogenic differentiation medium. After 21 days of induction, cells were stained with alizarin red S (G1450, acquired from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) in order to detect calcified nodule formation. For inducing adipogenic differentiation, ADSCs were cultured in adipogenic differentiation medium, lasting 14 days. The cells then underwent oil red O (G1262, Solarbio) staining to observe lipid droplet formation using an inverted microscope. Expression of ADSC surface antigens (cluster of differentiation [CD]49D, CD105, CD90, CD34, CD45, and CD106) was detected using flow cytometry.

To ensure the phenotype of ADSCs and the accuracy of subsequent experiments, only ADSCs at passage 2–3 were used. FBS was centrifuged at 100,000 g for 7 h at 4 °C, whereupon the supernatant was collected to prepare EV-free complete medium. Cells at passage 2–3 were cultured in the EV-free medium for 48 h to obtain supernatant, from which EVs were isolated by ultracentrifugation. Briefly, supernatant was spun at 300 g, 4 °C for 10 min, and 3000 g for 20 min to remove the precipitate. Next, centrifugation at 100,000 g for 70 min was conducted to precipitate the ADSC-EVs. The ADSC-EVs were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), filter-sterilized using a 0.22 µm filter, resuspended again in PBS, and frozen at -80 °C for later use. A negative control for EVs (ADSC-GW) was established to verify EV specificity; these cells were treated with 10 μM of EV release inhibitor GW4869 during culture and followed the same processing steps for EV isolation.

The morphology of ADSC-EVs was examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, HT7700, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Particle size and concentration were determined via nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) on a NanoSight NS300 analyzer (Malvern PANalytical). Western blot was performed to detect EV surface markers, employing antibodies against TSG101 (ab125011, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), CD63 (ab217345, Abcam), and Calnexin (ab22595, Abcam).

In silico analysis

Expression of miR-574-5p in MSC-EVs was analyzed using the EVmiRNA database (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/EVmiRNA/). Binding sites between miR-574-5p and GLUT1 were predicted by means of the StarBase database (https://rnasysu.com/encori/).

Establishment of mouse SA-AKI models

A total of 72 specific-pathogen-free C57BL/6 mice (weighing 20–25 g, an equal number of males and females) were acquired from Hangzhou Ziyuan Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). They were housed in a controlled environment (ambient temperature, 22–24 °C; 12-h light/12-h dark cycle; 60% relative humidity) with free access to food and water. All procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of The First People’s Hospital of Wenling. The procedures were performed in accordance with the guidelines established by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th Edition, 2011), ensuring humane treatment of animals and minimizing their pain and discomfort. A SA-AKI model was induced by means of cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) method [21]. Mice were intraperitoneally injected with 50 mg/kg pentobarbital sodium (acquired from Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO). After disinfection, the abdominal cavity was opened by a 1–1.5-cm longitudinal midline incision into the lower quadrant, followed by blunt dissection of the cecum. The cecum was then ligated with a 3–0 surgical suture at a designated point, 5 mm away from its tip. Two holes were made with a 22-gauge needle, and a small amount of cecal contents was extruded in order to ensure hole patency. After that, the cecum was returned to the abdominal cavity, and the incision was closed layer by layer using 4–0 silk thread. Each mouse received an intraperitoneal injection of 1 mL of pre-warmed normal saline post-CLP. Additionally, they were subcutaneously administered with 25 mg/kg broad-spectrum antibiotic (imipenem/cilastatin) and received subcutaneous injections of 1.5 mL of 3/4 normal saline at 6 h and 18 h post-CLP. Mice in the Sham group underwent the same procedures, but without CLP.

The sample size was calculated using G*Power software. The mice were randomly allocated into the following 6 groups according to the random number table method, with 12 mice in each group: Sham group, CLP group, CLP + EVs group (mice following CLP were injected with 100 µL ADSC-EVs [5 × 108 particles] via tail vein), CLP + GW group (mice following CLP were injected with 100 µL particles isolated from GW4869-treated ADSCs [diluted at the same ratio as the CLP + EVs group] via tail vein), CLP + EVs + antagomirs negative control (NC) group (mice following CLP were injected with ADSC-EVs and 100 nM antagomirs NC via tail vein), CLP + EVs + miR-574-5p antagomirs group (mice following CLP were injected with ADSC-EVs and 20 nM miR-574-5p antagomirs) [22, 23]. Both antagomirs NC and miR-574-5p antagomirs were acquired from GenePharma Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). At 5 h after CLP, drug administration via tail vein injection was performed. After injection for 24 h, mice were euthanized, before which 0.2 mL of blood was drawn from the orbital sinus. The drawn blood samples were spun at 3600 rpm for 10 min to obtain serum, which was stored at -20 °C. Immediately, kidney tissue samples were randomly selected from 6 mice in each group and fixed by means of 4% paraformaldehyde (30525–89-4, from Sigma-Aldrich) for histological analysis. A portion of the kidney tissue samples of the remaining 6 mice was processed into single cell suspension, while the other was made into tissue homogenate and stored at a low temperature of -80 °C for future use [24].

Hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining

The kidney tissue samples were dewaxed, hydrated, and cut into 5-μm-thick sections. The sections underwent staining with eosin and hematoxylin (H8070, Solarbio). Histological images were obtained using a microscope (BX53, from Olympus Optical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Three high magnification fields of view were randomly selected from each section, and the kidney tissue morphology was observed in each field of view. Kidney tubular damage was scored on a scale from 0 to 5, on the basis of tubular dilation, tubular necrosis, vacuolar degeneration, brush border loss, and cast formation: 0, no damage; 1, 1–15%; 2, 15–25%; 3, 25–50%; 4, 50–75%; 5, > 76% [25].

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded tissue samples were sectioned, subjected to antigen retrieval, and incubated in 1% bovine serum albumin (abbreviated as BSA) (A602440-0050, from Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) to block nonspecific antibody binding. The sections were probed overnight at 4 °C with primary rabbit antibody to KIM-1 (ab78494, 1:100, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Then sections were re-probed with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibody, namely goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) (ab205718, 1:5000, Abcam), at 37 °C for 1 h. Finally, the staining processes were performed with 3, 3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB-1031, from Fuzhou Maixin Biotechnology Development Co., Ltd., Fuzhou, China) and hematoxylin (H8070, from Solarbio). At last, images were captured using a microscope (BX53, Olympus), and the proportion of KIM-1-positive cells was quantified by means of ImageJ software (from National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland), with the average value taken.

Serum biochemical testing

Kidney function was estimated by serum creatinine (abbreviated as SCr) and blood urea nitrogen (abbreviated as BUN), measured using SCr (C011-2–1, from JianCheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) and BUN Assay Kits (C013-2–1, from JianCheng Bioengineering Institute), respectively.

Cell culture and treatment

RAW264.7, a murine-derived macrophage cell line, was purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Shanghai, China) and cultured in DMEM, which contains 10% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. RAW264.7 cells were polarized into M1 macrophages by incubation for 24 h with 1 μg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [26].

Upon reaching 70–80% confluence, RAW264.7 cells were transfected with miR-574-5p inhibitor, inhibitor NC, overexpression (oe)-GLUT1, and oe-NC (pcDNA3.1 vector) plasmids (all from GenePharma) using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). Subsequent experiments were implemented 24 h after cell transfection.

RAW264.7 cells were grouped as follows: Blank, LPS (cells were incubated with LPS for 24 h), LPS + EVs (cells were treated with LPS and 5 × 108 particles/mL ADSC-EVs simultaneously for 24 h) [27], LPS + GW (cells were treated with LPS and an equal volume of ADSC-GW to ADSC-EVs simultaneously for 24 h), LPS + EVs + inhibitor NC (cells were transfected with inhibitor NC and then treated with LPS and ADSC-EVs simultaneously for 24 h), LPS + EVs + miR-574-5p inhibitor (cells were transfected with miR-574-5p inhibitor and then treated with LPS and ADSC-EVs simultaneously for 24 h), LPS + EVs + oe-NC (cells were transfected with oe-NC and then treated with LPS and ADSC-EVs simultaneously for 24 h) and LPS + EVs + oe-GLUT1 (cells were transfected with oe-GLUT1 and then treated with LPS and ADSC-EVs simultaneously for 24 h).

Internalization of ADSC-EVs by RAW264.7 cells

EV suspension (100 μL) was pre-stained with 1 mL of diluted PKH67 dye (green, 4 μL/mL, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA). EVs were extracted again. The 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Thermo Fisher Scientific, blue) was used to stain RAW264.7 cell nuclei. The PKH67-labeled EVs underwent incubation with RAW264.7 cells at 5% CO2 and 37 °C for 12 h. At last, images were captured by means of a Zeiss LSM 780 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss Inc., Jena, Germany).

Luciferase reporter assay

To validate the binding of miR-574-5p to GLUT1, the predicted binding sites were amplified. Subsequently, the amplified GLUT1 gene fragments, GLUT1-wild type (abbreviated as WT) and GLUT1-mutant (abbreviated as MUT), were cloned into the pMIR-reporter plasmid (Beijing Huayueyang Biotechnology, Beijing, China). The luciferase reporter plasmids (GLUT1-WT and GLUT1-MUT) were co-transfected into RAW264.7 cells with miR-574-5p mimics or NC mimics. After 48 h, luciferase activity was analyzed by means of the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (K801-200; from Biovision, Mountain View, CA).

RNA pull-down assay

RNA pull-down assay was performed to detect the binding of miR-574-5p to GLUT1. Utilizing miR-574-5p and its control RNA sequence as templates, in vitro transcription was carried out with T7 RNA polymerase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). During transcription, biotin-labeled nucleotides were incorporated using a Biotin RNA Labeling Mix (Roche, Indianapolis, IN, USA) to generate biotin-labeled miR-574-5p probe and control probe. Following transcription, the products were treated with the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD). RNA probes were mixed with cell lysates at 4 °C for 3 h, whereupon the complex was reacted with streptavidin magnetic beads (acquired from Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 h. Quantitative real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (abbreviated as qRT-PCR) was adopted to detect expression of GLUT1.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspension of mouse kidney tissues or macrophages were incubated with allophycocyanin-conjugated anti-CD86 (ab134385, Abcam), phycoerythrin-conjugated anti-CD68 (ab216701, Abcam), and fluorescein isothiocyanate-conjugated anti-CD206 (ab270647, Abcam) at ambient temperature in the dark for 20 min. Then 1 mL of PBS was added, and centrifugation was followed for 5 min. A blank control group was set at the same time. After resuspending in PBS, cells were analyzed by a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA) for macrophage polarization. For flow cytometric analysis, the following gating strategies were employed: cellular debris located in the lower-left region was initially excluded by gating on forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC); diagonal gating was then used to remove doublets deviating from the diagonal line; finally, using isotype controls as references, positive thresholds for each fluorescence channel were established, and the percentages of M1 (CD86⁺) and M2 (CD206⁺) macrophages were determined through quadrant gating.

Detection of inflammation-related indicators and glycolysis assessment

Levels of interleukin (IL)-10, IL-6, tumor necrosis factor (abbreviated as TNF)-α, and transforming growth factor (abbreviated as TGF)-β were measured using separate enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (abbreviated as ELISA) kits (IL-10, JL20242; IL-6, JL20268; TNF-α, JL10484; TGF-β, JL11507; all from Jonln, Shanghai, China) based on the instructions. Glycolysis was evaluated using a lactic acid assay kit (JL-T1068, Jonln) in strict compliance with the kit instructions.

RNA isolation and quantitation

Total RNA was extracted from cells and tissues by means of the RNeasy Mini Kit (acquired from Qiagen, Valencia, CA) and reverse transcribed into complementary DNA using the Reverse Transcription kit (RR047A, acquired from Takara, Tokyo, Japan). qRT-PCR was conducted using the SYBR® Premix Ex TaqTM II (Perfect Real Time) kit (DRR081, acquired from Takara) on an ABI 7500 Real Time PCR Instrument (acquired from Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). PCR amplification consisted of a 30-s pre-denaturation step at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles: 5 s at 95 °C, and 34 s at 60 °C. Three replicate wells were set up for each sample. U6 was used as an internal reference for miR-574-5p, and GAPDH was applied for GLUT1. Fold changes were calculated using relative quantification (the 2−ΔΔCt method). Western blot

Total protein was extracted from tissues or cells with RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime), with concentration quantified by BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0011, Beyotime). Protein (20 µg) was loaded onto an SDS-PAGE gel and transferred onto a 0.45 µm PVDF membrane. Blocking was performed for 1 h at ambient temperature in 5% non-fat milk before overnight incubation with primary rabbit antibodies at 4 °C. The membranes were incubated with HRP-labeled secondary antibody, that is, goat anti-rabbit IgG (ab205718 ,1:5000, Abcam), for 1 h at ambient temperature. Chemiluminescence reagent (from Thermo Fisher Scientific) was adopted to detect protein bands, after which gray scale values were measured. GAPDH (ab8245, 1:3000, Abcam) served as a loading control. The primary antibodies used included GLUT1 (ab115730, 1:5000, Abcam), HK2 (ab209847, 1:1000, from Abcam) and LDHA (ab52488, 1:5000, from Abcam).

Measurement of extracellular acidification rate (ECAR)

ECAR was analyzed using an XF-24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Briefly, cells were seeded into XF-24 cell culture microplates and treated according to the designated experimental groups. Glucose (10 mM; Agilent Technologies), oligomycin (1 µM; Agilent Technologies), and 2-deoxyglucose (100 mM; Agilent Technologies) were injected at the designated time points during the ECAR measurement, following the sequential operational steps as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA). To ensure blinding, statistical analyses were performed by an investigator who was unaware of the group allocation. Normally distributed data determined with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test were summarized as mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (abbreviated as ANOVA) with Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied for multi-group data comparisons. A two-tailed test with p < 0.05 indicates a significant difference.

Results

ADSC-EVs upregulate miR-574-5p and prime M2 macrophage polarization, thereby alleviating kidney function impairment and inflammatory response in SA-AKI mice

ADSCs were cultured in vitro, and flow cytometric data showed that ADSCs were highly positive for CD49D, CD90, and CD105, while these cells did not express CD34, CD45, and CD106 (Fig. 1A). Besides, alizarin red S and oil red O staining confirmed the osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation potential of ADSCs (Fig. 1B). EVs were isolated from the ADSCs (ADSC-EVs). The particle and protein concentrations of ADSC-EVs were determined by NTA and BCA assay, respectively. The particle-to-protein ratio (particle concentration/protein concentration) of the isolated ADSC-EVs was reported to be 5 × 1010 – 9 × 1010 particles/μg, which met the criteria for highly purified EVs [28]. As demonstrated by TEM, ADSC-EVs exhibited intact, typical, cup-shaped, and lipid bilayer membrane structures (Fig. 1C). Through NTA, ADSC-EVs showed the characteristic EV size distribution within the range of 30 – 200 nm (Fig. 1D). With regard to the Western blot results, ADSC-EVs were positive for TSG101 and CD63 but negative for Calnexin. To decipher the mechanism of ADSC-EVs in SA-AKI, we applied the CLP method to establish a mouse model of SA-AKI. H&E staining results demonstrated that CLP caused significant pathological changes in the kidney tissues, including blurred kidney structure, kidney tissue edema, inflammatory cell infiltration, deformed and enlarged glomeruli with scattered hemorrhagic spots in the interstitium, tubule dilation, epithelial cell swelling, degeneration, and detachment, and tissue edema, degeneration and exudation, when compared to Sham operation (Fig. 1F). In addition, the kidney tubular damage score was noticeably increased in the CLP group relative to the Sham group (Fig. 1G, p < 0.01). Immunohistochemistry revealed a marked increase in the proportion of KIM-1-positive cells in the kidney tissue of the CLP group compared with the Sham group (Fig. 1H, p < 0.01). As shown in Fig. 1I, kidney injury biomarkers (BUN and SCr levels) were significantly elevated in the CLP group versus the Sham group (p < 0.01). These results indicate a successful establishment of the SA-AKI mouse model.

It was noted that kidney injury in SA-AKI mice was significantly alleviated after treatment with ADSC-EVs (Fig. 1F-I, all p < 0.01). Based on previous findings that miR-574-5p is remarkably lower in patients with the SA-AKI compared to those with sepsis alone [13], we employed qRT-PCR in the present study to determine miR-574-5p expression in mouse kidney tissues. In comparison to the Sham group, the CLP group had lower miR-574-5p expression; however, this low expression was compromised by ADSC-EVs (Fig. 1J, all p < 0.01).

Macrophages play a crucial role in regulating the inflammatory response in the context of SA-AKI [29, 30]. Flow cytometry was used to measure M1 (CD68+CD86+) and M2 (CD68+CD206+) polarization of macrophages in mouse kidney tissues. Relative to the Sham group, the CLP group exhibited a reduced proportion of M2 macrophages and an increased proportion of M1 macrophages; conversely, these alterations were reversed after treatment with ADSC-EVs (Fig. 1K-L, all p < 0.01). Moreover, M1 (TNF-α, IL-6) and M2 polarization-associated markers (IL-10, TGF-β) were detected by means of ELISA in mouse kidney tissues. Levels of TNF-α and IL-6 were observed to be markedly amplified, while IL-10 and TGF-β levels were evidently decreased in the CLP group compared with the Sham group. In contrast to the CLP and CLP+GW groups, the CLP + EVs group had strikingly lower TNF-α and IL-6 levels and higher IL-10 and TGF-β levels (Fig. 1M, all p < 0.01). Above all, ADSC-EVs may elevate miR-574-5p expression and polarize macrophages toward M2 phenotype, reducing kidney function impairment and inflammatory response in SA-AKI mice.

ADSC-EVs increase miR-574-5p expression to stimulate M2 polarization but suppress M1 polarization of LPS-induced macrophages

RAW264.7 cells were cultured in vitro and polarized toward the M1 phenotype by incubation with LPS. M1 polarized macrophages (CD68+CD86+) and M2 polarized macrophages (CD68+CD206+) were quantified by flow cytometry. There was an upward trend in M1 polarization (p < 0.01 ), while no profound difference was found in M2 polarization in the LPS group compared to the Blank group (Fig. 2A). In addition, ELISA data presented significantly higher levels of TNF-α and IL-6 but no changes regarding IL-10 and TGF-β levels in the LPS group than in the Blank group (Fig. 2B). ADSC-EVs transfer miR-574-5p into macrophages, where miR-574-5p targets GLUT1 and limits its expression

GLUT1-driven metabolic function is essential for macrophage activity during inflammation [31]. In this study, we detected mRNA expression of GLUT1 in RAW264.7 cells by qRT-PCR. LPS induction led to a noticeable increase in GLUT1 mRNA expression, while this increase was abated by ADSC-EVs (Fig. 3A, p < 0.01). Similar results were also observed at the protein level, revealed by Western blot (Fig. 3B, p < 0.01). Prediction using the EVmiRNA database (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/EVmiRNA//#!/) showed that miR-574-5p was highly expressed in MSC-EVs (Fig. 3C). For further validation, qRT-PCR was employed to determine miR-574-5p expression in ADSC-EVs. RNase treatment elicited no statistically significant changes in miR-574-5p expression; by contrast, there was a dramatic reduction of miR-574-5p expression in ADSC-EVs treated with both RNase and SDS (Fig. 3D, p < 0.01). This indicates that miR-574-5p is encapsulated in ADSC-EVs. It can be reasonable to hypothesize that ADSC-EVs may deliver miR-574-5p into macrophages, in which miR-574-5p targets GLUT1 and blocks its expression. To test this hypothesis, we retrieved the StarBase database and found the presence of binding sites between miR-574-5p and GLUT1 (Fig. 3E). Dual-luciferase reporter assay confirmed the binding of miR-574-5p to GLUT1 (Fig. 3F, p < 0.01). Meanwhile, RNA pull-down assay demonstrated that the binding of miR-574-5p to GLUT1 mRNA was significantly weakened in RAW264.7 cells incubated with LPS; in contrast, treatment with ADSC-EVs enhanced this binding (Fig. 3G, p < 0.01). Discussion

The present study demonstrates that ADSC-EVs can attenuate SA-AKI in mice via shuttling miR-574-5p, where miR-574-5p negatively regulates GLUT1 to inhibit glycolysis and accelerate macrophage M2 polarization. This study might shed new light on the application of ADSC-EVs as a potential therapeutic tool for SA-AKI.

Initial results in the current research illustrated that administration of ADSC-EVs had the potency to reduce kidney function impairment and inflammatory response in CLP-induced SA-AKI mice. In agreement with this, a recent study has confirmed that exosomes derived from ADSC-EVs decrease inflammation and improve kidney function and structure significantly in rats with LPS-induced AKI [33]. Subsequently, in this study, we elucidated that ADSC-EVs induced miR-574-5p upregulation, then inhibited M1 polarization of LPS-induced macrophages, and motivated macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype. Liang et al. performed miRNA sequencing to identify differentially expressed miRNAs in EVs isolated from human umbilical cord MSCs and found upregulation of hsa-miR-574-5p [34]. Here, our study provides the first evidence that ADSC-EVs elevated miR-574-5p expression by in vivo and in vitro experiments. Macrophages are known to have a great plasticity to adapt to the environmental signals, and their roles in tissue damage and/or repair are phenotype-dependent [35, 36]. M1 macrophages predominate at the onset of kidney tissue damage through releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines, like TNF-α, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), IL-1β, cyclooxygenase-2 (Cox-2), and IL-6, while M2 macrophages are involved in kidney inflammation resolution and subsequent tissue repair by secreting anti-inflammatory mediators, including IL-10, TGF-β, IL-4, and IL-13 [37, 38]. In accordance with our results, published data indicate that MSC-derived exosomes, including ADSC-EVs, exert potent anti-inflammatory effects by inducing a switch from M1 to M2 macrophage polarization, thereby mitigating kidney injury [39–41]. From the perspective of molecular mechanisms, knockdown of miR-574-5p increases the proportion of CD16/32+ M1 macrophages but decreases the proportion of CD206+ M2 macrophages in the mouse retinal tissues of diabetic retinopathy; further, the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) can be rescued and anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10, TGF-β and IL-4) can be downregulated upon miR-574-5p knockdown [42]. These results collectively support the notion that ADSC-EVs carried anti-inflammatory miR-574-5p to strengthen macrophage M2 polarization, which may represent a novel and promising anti-inflammatory therapeutic strategy for SA-AKI.

Simultaneously, we found that the therapeutic benefits of ADSC-EVs may be attributed to their transfer of miR-574-5p into macrophages, in which miR-574-5p directly targeted GLUT1 and downregulated its expression. In support, evidence has shown that miR-574-5p can directly target and bind to GLUT1; downregulation of GLUT1 expression as a consequence of long noncoding RNA OGRU silencing can be abrogated by miR-574-5p inhibitor in macrophages of diabetic retinopathy mice [42]. Furthermore, miR-574-5p inhibition or ectopic GLUT1 expression was observed to provoke glycolysis in LPS-induced macrophages and partly undermine the promoting effect of ADSC-EVs on M2 macrophage polarization. This observation concurs with the finding that exosomes from ADSCs can impair glycolysis and induce M1-to-M2 transition of macrophages, which is related to blocked GLUT1 expression [32]. It has become increasingly evident that glycolysis has important roles in inflammation by producing lactate, which acts as a critical mediator of the macrophage inflammatory response [43, 44]. M1 macrophages induced by LPS are characterized by increased glycolysis as these cells increase glucose consumption and lactate release while decreasing oxygen consumption; in contrast, M2 macrophages principally employ oxidative glucose metabolism pathways, and enhanced glycolysis underlies the cause of inhibited macrophage polarization to M2 phenotype and the resultant augmented inflammatory response [45–47]. Strikingly, glycolysis is upregulated in CLP-induced SA-AKI, evidenced by increased mRNA expression of glycolysis-related genes (pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 [PDK1], lactate dehydrogenase A [LDHA], and pyruvate kinase M2 [PKM2]) and lactate levels in both the kidney tissue of mice with SA-AKI and LPS-induced human kidney tubular epithelial cells [48]. Also, ECAR levels in the LPS-induced macrophages can rescued after treatments with ADSC-EVs based on our findings. As expected, inhibition of glycolysis confers significant protection against SA-AKI [49–51]. Considering these published reports combined with current results, it can be deduced that ADSC-EVs inhibit glycolysis and promote macrophages to polarize to M2 phenotype via the miR-574-5p/GLUT1 pathway, ultimately ameliorating SA-AKI in mice. Yet, to our knowledge, the effect of miR-574-5p on glycolysis has not been previously characterized, and our study is the first to report its anti-glycolytic role. Further studies are thus warranted for validation of the established negative correlation between them.

Overall, our study indicated that ADSC-EVs carrying miR-574-5p contributed to the alleviation of SA-AKI in mice by hampering glycolysis and inducing M2 macrophage polarization, which was associated with GLUT1 downregulation. These findings may provide a more enhanced understanding of the interplay between ADSC-EVs and macrophage metabolism and polarization. Meanwhile, this study reveals a novel mechanism of ADSC-EVs in SA-AKI, which may provide new therapeutic strategies in patients.

Limitations

Firstly, this study merely focused on the short-term effects of ADSC-EVs, and the long-term effects remain to be supplemented in the future. Secondly, clinical data and samples are urgently needed to support our findings. Thirdly, additional studies should also be conducted to determine whether ADSC-EVs may regulate the expression of other target genes or signaling pathways. Meanwhile, attention should be paid to the EV uptake by other renal cell types other than macrophages in future studies. Furthermore, while EVs hold great promise as natural nanomedicine delivery vehicles, their clinical translation still faces challenges including batch-to-batch variability, insufficient in vivo targeting efficiency, a lack of standardized, and GMP-compliant manufacturing processes. Moreover, their short half-life in the bloodstream limits the maintenance of safe and effective therapeutic concentrations [52]. Endogenous loading of EVs is difficult to standardize and susceptible to variations in parent cell growth conditions, while exogenous loading methods may compromise EV integrity [52]. Additionally, the structural instability of EV membranes during storage, transport, and processing further limits their clinical applicability. All these issues remain to be resolved in future studies.
