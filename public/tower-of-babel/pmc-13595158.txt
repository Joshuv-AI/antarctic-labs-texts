Maternal complete heart block in pregnancy: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shabnam Kottummal, Varun Muppidi, Yavanasuriya Jeganathan, Deepthy M Sadanandan, Rohan D'Souza, Arun Keepanasseril, N Sreekumaran Nair, Anish Keepanasseril
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComplete heart block (CHB) is an uncommon bradyarrhythmia complicating pregnancy, posing challenges to the treating team.ObjectiveTo evaluate the maternal outcomes, other cardiac complications, and pregnancy outcomes among pregnant women with CHB and compare these outcomes among those managed with and without pacing.MethodsWe searched two databases, i.e., MEDLINE and EMBASE, for studies reporting outcomes in pregnancies complicated by CHB. The bibliography of relevant articles was examined to identify additional articles. Maternal and perinatal outcomes, including obstetric and cardiac complications, were extracted. Using the random-effects model, pooled estimates of various outcomes are reported as percentages with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsA total of 294 pregnancies among 220 women with CHB, identified from 12 studies, were included in the analysis. Two-thirds had either temporary or permanent pacing during pregnancy. Arrhythmias other than CHB were the most common cardiac complications noted (2.7%, 95% CI: 0.9%–4.5%). Lead-related complications, such as lead migration (1.6%, 95% CI: 0.1%–3.0%), were uncommon. One maternal death was reported among the included studies, during the postpartum period in an asymptomatic mother who did not have pacing. Low birth weight was the most common complication (43.1%, 95% CI: 17% - 67%) in the studies reporting neonatal outcomes.ConclusionPregnancies complicated with CHB have low rates of adverse maternal and fetal/neonatal outcomes under multidisciplinary care. The outcomes were similar among those with and without pacemakers, suggesting that women can cope with the physiological changes of pregnancy even without pacing.Keywords: Complete heart block, Lead-related complications, Cardiac pacemaker, Temporary cardiac pacing, Pregnancy, Low birth weight

Introduction

Complete heart block (CHB) is an uncommon bradyarrhythmia, predominantly affecting the elderly. There is an absence of conduction to the ventricle through the atrioventricular node, which dissociates atrioventricular electrical activity. CHB occurs in 1:20000 live births [1]. In young adults, it may be associated with congenital cardiac lesions, and more commonly following in-utero damage to the fetal conduction system following transplacental transfer of maternal autoimmune antibodies. It may also be associated with viral myocarditis, damage during prior valve surgery, or systemic disease such as sarcoidosis or mitochondrial disease. It may remain asymptomatic or may present weakness, syncope, exercise intolerance, or develop heart failure, due to a low cardiac output state from the bradyarrhythmia, or heart failure secondary to a low cardiac output state resulting from the bradyarrhythmia. Permanent pacing in CHB is indicated for all symptomatic patients or those without a reversible cause, and it has been shown to improve quality of life and survival [2].

Physiological changes in pregnancy require an adaptive increase in heart rate and cardiac output to meet the needs of the growing fetus [3]. However, women with CHB may not undergo a similar change and mount this physiological response. Many pregnant women develop symptoms such as syncopal attacks, while others remain asymptomatic, both posing challenges to the treating team, especially when they are detected for the first time during pregnancy. Earlier reports suggested the use of temporary pacing, especially during labour and delivery, due to concerns about their ability to cope with hemodynamic changes, but recent case reports and case series have described favourable outcomes in pregnant women without pacing [4]. Subsequently, the recent Heart Rhythm Society guidance suggests that among women with CHB who are hemodynamically stable, temporary pacing is not recommended, and that it may be safer to defer device implantation after delivery [5].

In this systematic review, we aimed to evaluate the maternal outcomes, such as lead-related and other cardiac complications and pregnancy outcomes among pregnant women with complete heart block. We also compared these outcomes among those with and without pacing during pregnancy.

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration

The protocol for this review was registered in the PROSPERO database under the registration number CRD42022351043. The review protocol is available at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42022351043 [6]. It was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [7].

Information sources and search

In two bibliographic databases, viz. MEDLINE (via PubMed) and Embase (via Ovid) were searched using the indexing terms shown in the search strategy (Supplementary data S1). All relevant studies published from January 1990 until June 2023, restricted to English, were included.

Study selection criteria

All studies reporting outcomes among pregnant women with complete heart block, including prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case-control studies, cross-sectional studies, and case series with 4 or more pregnancies, were included. Systematic reviews, meta-analyses, commentaries, editorials, case reports, and conference proceedings were excluded. If more than one publication including the same patients was identified, only the most recent was included in the review.

Data collection process and quality assessment

Two reviewers (YSJ and SK) independently screened all relevant titles and abstracts of the search results to assess whether they should be included in the review. Full texts of potentially eligible studies were retrieved after duplicates identified during screening were manually excluded and the search results were exported into the Rayyan software [8]. These full texts were considered for eligibility, and reference lists were screened for additional articles that may have been missed in the original search. Based on the selection criteria, new manuscripts thus identified from these lists were added to the review. Discrepancies, if any, were discussed to reach a consensus. If disagreement persisted, it was resolved by a discussion with senior reviewers (AnK and ArK).

A pre-piloted form was used to extract the data from the included studies for evidence synthesis. Information retrieved from the articles included the title, author, year of publication, number of patients and pregnancies, timing of diagnosis of complete heart block, interventions before or during pregnancy, and outcomes. The data was retrieved individually by one reviewer (SK) and subsequently cross-verified by another reviewer (AnK). To avoid omitting studies with insufficient information, authors were contacted and reminded via email before exclusion from the meta-analysis following screening. The risk of bias assessment of included studies was performed using the critical appraisal tools proposed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) [9].

Type of outcomes

The outcomes were classified into three groups: (i) Maternal complications primarily attributed to complete heart block, (ii) Obstetric complications (maternal and fetal), and (iii) Neonatal complications. Maternal complications reviewed include maternal mortality, lead-related complications such as lead thrombus and lead migration, pulmonary oedema, cardiac arrhythmias, and systemic or pulmonary thromboembolism. Obstetric complications extracted were miscarriages (pregnancy loss at less than 20 weeks of gestation), caesarean section rates, preterm birth (PTB), and stillbirth (pregnancy loss more than 20 weeks) as given in the individual studies. Neonatal outcomes included low birth weight (LBW), neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission, congenital malformation, neonatal hypoglycaemia, neonatal arrhythmias and neonatal loss.

Statistical analysis

A fetus-at-risk approach was used to calculate fetal and neonatal risks, that is, (i) the denominator for assessing caesarean sections, stillbirths, fetal growth restriction and preterm births, excluding miscarriages and (ii) that for neonatal mortality and low birth weight, excluding both miscarriages and stillbirths.

A random-effects meta-analysis was used to pool proportions from different studies, as they were heterogeneous and unlikely to share a standard effect size. Study-specific and pooled effect estimates, along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), were graphically represented using forest plots for overall and subgroup analyses. A variance-stabilising Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation was applied to minimise the effect of extremely small or large proportions (close to 0 or 1) on the overall estimate. Values less than zero for transformed proportions and lower CI using Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation were set to zero [29]. A random-effects meta-analysis was conducted to pool birth weight means across studies. The pooled relative risk (RR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) was estimated to compare adverse outcomes, such as small for gestational age (SGA), preterm birth, and low birth weight, between the pacemaker and non-pacemaker groups. Evidence for heterogeneity was tested using Cochrane's Q statistic and quantified using the I2 statistic (I2 < 25% none, 25–49% low, 50–74% Moderate and ≥75% High) [30]. Publication bias was assessed using a funnel plot, and Egger's test was used to assess asymmetry. The statistical analyses used the “Meta package” in R software version 4.0.4.

Patient and public involvement

Since this was a systematic review of published studies, patients and the public were not involved in its design, conduct, execution, or reporting.

The majority of pregnancies were among those who had pacing (n = 247, 84.0% (95% CI: 79.8%–88.2%)), with 166/247 pregnancies (51.5%, 95% CI: 30.0%–73.1%) among those with a permanent pacemaker in situ. Lead-related complications from pacing included lead migration in 2 pregnancies and lead fracture in one pregnancy, among the pregnancies reporting these complications. There were no reported cases of lead dysfunction, lead perforation, lead infection, or infective endocarditis in any of the studies. The occurrence of arrhythmias other than CHB was the most common cardiac complication (n = 19/288, 2.7%, 95% CI: 0.9%–4.5%).

Obstetric and neonatal outcomes

Miscarriages occurred in 4.5% (95% CI: 1.7%–7.3%) of all pregnancies, with four of them being medical termination of pregnancy. In 81/274 pregnancies (36.8%, 95% CI: 18.1%–55.5%), the mode of birth was via caesarean section (Fig. 2). In four studies, the neonatal outcomes were not reported. Epidural analgesia or anaesthesia was given in 81/118 pregnancies (63.3%, 95% CI: 41.2%–85.4%). Preterm birth complicated 13.2% (95% CI: 8.7%–17.7%) cases, stillbirths occurred in 1.7% (95% CI: 0.2%–3.2%), and there were no neonatal deaths reported. The LBW occurred in 43.1% (95% CI: 17% -%67%). Neonatal arrhythmias occurred in 2.6% (95%CI 0.1%–5.2%), and neonatal hypoglycaemia in 3.0% (95%CI 0.4%–5.6%). Long QT syndrome was diagnosed in the four babies with arrhythmia.

Comparison of outcomes among those with and without pacing in pregnancy

A comparison of reported major outcomes in patients with complete heart block, with and without pacemakers, was conducted to compare proportions, as shown in Table 3. The proportion of heart failures was 2.28% (95% CI: 0%–10.12%), and the occurrence of arrhythmias was 0.64% (95% CI: 0%–4.55%) among those with pacemakers, whereas no heart failures or arrhythmias occurred among those without pacemakers. Quality assessment

The JBI critical appraisal tool for case series (n = 7) and cohort studies (n = 5) was used to assess the risk of bias among the included studies. Most of the included studies were deemed to have a low risk of bias (supplementary data). In the case series, 100% of studies reported inclusion criteria, participant demographics, clinical information, and participants' outcomes, while 85.7% reported demographic information of the presenting site and included participants. However, only 71.4% used valid case-identification methods and measured the condition in a standardised way; only 14.3% reported consecutive inclusion. In cohort studies, 100% met the criteria for valid exposure and outcome measurements, statistical analysis, and baseline outcome-free status. Confounders were identified in 80% of cases, but strategies to manage them were adequate in only 40%. Follow-up was complete in 40%, and sufficient in 60%. Overall, both study designs showed good quality.

Publication bias

Funnel plots assessing publication bias for the outcomes are shown in Supplementary Data S8. Egger's test revealed significant bias in the reporting of low birth weight. In contrast, there was no significant bias in the reporting of complications such as lead migration, lead thrombus, heart failure, arrhythmias, and maternal death.

GRADE

Assessment using the GRADE framework revealed that all outcomes were consistently rated as low, except for the preterm birth, caesarean section and small for gestational age. The key factor reducing the initial certainty to low is the study design of the included studies, which are observational rather than randomised controlled trials. Due to the concerns in two domains, imprecision and, for some of them, the inconsistency were other reasons for the lower rating. Most studies had small sample sizes and low event rates for many outcomes, leading to this uncertainty. The inconsistency domain was substantial, as indicated by high I2 values. Discussion

Main findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis included 12 studies with 294 pregnancies, of which more than two-thirds received temporary or permanent pacing during pregnancy. Two of the three studies were conducted in high-income countries and were mainly case series. Arrhythmias other than CHB were the most common cardiac complications noted. Lead-related complications, such as lead migration and lead fracture, were uncommon. There was one maternal death among the studies included, which occurred in the postpartum period in an asymptomatic mother who did not have pacing. Low birth weight, noted in one-third of the pregnancies, was the most common neonatal complication. Using the GRADE approach, the certainty of the evidence regarding the outcomes was low except for preterm birth, underscoring significant gaps and highlighting the need for cautious interpretation of the pooled estimates.

Interpretation

Physiological changes to meet the demands of pregnancy increase cardiac output. This depends on variations in heart rate and stroke volume, as early as the 8th week of gestation [3]. In pregnancies with CHB, heart rate does not increase, and changes in cardiac output are mainly due to variations in stroke volume. In earlier studies, concern about hemodynamic status during delivery may have been the main reason for temporary pacing, rather than for symptomatic patients on permanent pacing [28]. However, in recent studies, the number of women without pacing has increased, with better monitoring facilities and information on better tolerability during labour and delivery [4], [12]. The occurrence of arrhythmias other than the CHB, probably from the underlying disease per se and the effects of hemodynamic/hormonal changes in pregnancy, also indicates the need for more frequent monitoring among pregnant women with CHB.

Challenges in comparing perinatal outcomes were noted in the review, including inconsistent reporting of outcomes and their definitions [29]. Some reported low birth weight, based on the absolute weight being less than 2500 g, many of which probably resulted from the preterm birth of appropriately grown babies for that gestation. In contrast, others reported that the more appropriate outcome is small for gestational age, which is adjusted for gestational age at birth. The incidence of preterm birth was similar to that of the general population. Neonatal arrhythmias were noted in 2.6% of cases, suggesting that neonates born to mothers with CHB should also be monitored for the development of arrhythmias and maternal autoantibodies, such as anti-SSA (Sjögren's Syndrome-Related antigen A)/Ro or SSB (anti-Sjögren's Syndrome Type B)/La antibodies. Even as the majority had a vaginal delivery, the incidence of caesarean deliveries was higher than the overall caesarean section rates. This may reflect the practices and probable low threshold for caesarean section due to concerns from high-risk pregnancies. Epidural analgesia/anaesthesia was the most common method of labour analgesia/anaesthesia, and it was reported to be well tolerated during labour and delivery.

Clinical implications

Indications for pacemaker therapy for women with CHB detected during pregnancy should remain the same as those for nonpregnant cases, as observed in this review, wherein the physiological changes seem to be well tolerated in patients without pacing. Recent articles reporting the experiences of those without pacing during pregnancy and postpartum may suggest that temporary pacing during pregnancy may not be required, and that permanent pacing may be considered as per standard indications. As many cases in the review noted postpartum complications requiring pacing, all women should be informed of the need for a carefully planned, regular long-term follow-up to optimise outcomes. The increase in maternal complications in those with pacemakers noticed in the subgroup analysis might reflect the effect of longstanding cardiac disease, rather than the pacing therapy.

Research implications

Researchers should be encouraged to use the core outcomes set and reporting checklist for cardio-obstetric studies, which provide comprehensive, uniform reporting of patient-important outcomes [30]. Since pregnancy-related changes can worsen or change the course of CHB, more data on long-term outcomes in these women is needed. More research is needed to determine whether pacing, even temporary, is always necessary in cases of CHB during pregnancy, as this review indicated that many may tolerate the physiological changes of pregnancy without requiring a pacemaker. Multicentre collaborative studies or registries are required to gain better insights into outcomes and the factors that affect them.

Strengths and limitations

A comprehensive search and selection strategy identified 12 studies that reviewed various outcomes among pregnant women with CHB. It also allowed us to compare the outcomes between those with and without pacing during pregnancy. However, the study has certain limitations. Firstly, there was considerable heterogeneity in outcome reporting across the included studies. The etiology of maternal CHB was reported inconsistently across studies, limiting our ability to assess whether neonatal arrhythmia burden reflected a shared underlying maternal-fetal process rather than CHB itself. Future studies should systematically report maternal etiology to allow this distinction. The robustness of subgroup analyses was limited by small sample sizes and few outcome events, which limited the ability to rule out publication bias. Outcomes from the use of temporary or permanent pacing need to be reported separately to enable a pooled analysis later, as individual studies may be limited by small sample sizes. Most publications are case reports or series with fewer than 4 cases, which limits their inclusion in this review. Lastly, despite concerted efforts to obtain missing data, some authors did not respond, resulting in the exclusion of certain studies due to insufficient outcome data.

Conclusion

Pregnancies complicated with CHB have low rates of obstetric, cardiac and fetal/neonatal complications under multidisciplinary care. The incidence of cardiac or obstetric complications and the neonatal outcomes were similar among those with and without pacemakers, suggesting the probability of women coping with the physiological changes in pregnancy even without pacing. Multi-centre studies with uniform reporting of core outcomes could help identify modifiable risk factors for short- and long-term adverse consequences, enabling better care.
