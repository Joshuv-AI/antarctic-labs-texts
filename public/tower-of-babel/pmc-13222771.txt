“The Ventilator Means Death”: Patient and Family Beliefs, Cost Anxiety and Trust as Drivers of Mechanical Ventilation Refusal in Somali Intensive Care
Authors: Mohamed Abubakar Ahmed, Abdullahi Abdirahman Omar, Ahmed Omar Siyad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMechanical ventilation is a life-sustaining therapy, yet in Somali intensive care settings it may be perceived by families as a marker of inevitable death. This commentary examines how refusal of intubation, pressure to discontinue ventilatory support, and self-discharge may emerge from interacting system constraints, belief-based fatalism, out-of-pocket cost anxiety, limited ICU knowledge, and trust and communication gaps related to sedation, prognosis, and goals of care. Using a patient-preference and adherence lens, we argue that some refusal events may represent modifiable non-adherence to a medically indicated plan rather than simple rejection of treatment, while emphasizing that this framing should not blame patients or families facing distress, uncertainty, and financial pressure. We propose pragmatic, culturally responsive steps that are feasible in resource-constrained ICUs, including standardized family orientation to ventilation and sedation, structured daily updates, early cost transparency linked to social support pathways, and documented non-coercive escalation for high-risk refusal. These actions may strengthen informed preference formation, reduce misunderstanding, protect patient rights, and improve alignment between family decisions and appropriate life-sustaining care. Future qualitative, audit, and implementation studies are needed to evaluate these proposed interventions in local critical care settings.

Introduction

In Somali intensive care units, mechanical ventilation may be interpreted by some families not as temporary organ support while clinicians treat the underlying disease, but as a symbolic threshold suggesting that death is near. This perception can contribute to refusal of intubation, pressure to discontinue ventilatory support, or self-discharge despite ongoing respiratory failure. Because mechanical ventilation is often time-sensitive and life-sustaining, refusal or delayed acceptance may create preventable clinical risk, ethical tension, and conflict between families and ICU teams. In fragile and predominantly out-of-pocket health care environments, such decisions may be shaped by uncertainty, fear, economic vulnerability, limited understanding of intensive care technologies, and prior experiences of critical care. Previous Somalia-based critical care commentary has similarly described how perceptions of intensive care as a place of death, family decision delay, trust concerns, and financial barriers may affect timely acceptance of ICU admission, supporting the relevance of decision-making, communication, and affordability as local critical care priorities.1

Intensive care unit professionals manage critically ill patients in technologically complex environments that include alarms, ventilators, monitoring devices, and repeated laboratory investigations; therefore, clear and consistent communication with patients and families is essential, particularly when mechanical ventilation is being proposed or continued.2 In settings where families have limited prior exposure to ICU technologies, structured communication may be particularly important because sedation, alarms, tubes, restricted movement, and restricted visiting can otherwise be misinterpreted as signs of deterioration, concealment, or futility.1,2

Related respiratory technology literature has also shown the expanding role of physiologic monitoring, sleep-related breathing assessment, and sensor-based respiratory diagnostics; however, in low-resource ICU decision-making, the clinical value of respiratory technologies depends not only on technical availability but also on whether families understand the purpose, limitations, and expected benefits of respiratory support.3–5

Ventilator refusal in this context should not be reduced to misinformation alone. Family interpretations may be shaped by clinical realities in which patients often present late and severely ill, while intensive care capacity remains variable. Families may witness poor outcomes among the sickest patients who require ventilation, reinforcing the plausible but inaccurate causal narrative that the ventilator causes death rather than representing rescue for patients already at high risk. This narrative may be strengthened when sedation, immobility, or restricted visiting is perceived as concealment, suffering, or evidence that the patient has already died. Therefore, the central problem addressed in this commentary is not only the availability of mechanical ventilation, but also whether families can form informed, trusted, and financially realistic preferences at the moment ventilation is recommended.

Objective

This commentary synthesizes the principal drivers of mechanical ventilation refusal in Somali intensive care units and proposes feasible, culturally responsive strategies that support informed preference formation, promote adherence to clinically indicated life-sustaining care, and reduce preventable harm.

Context and Clinical Significance in Somali ICUs

Mechanical ventilation refusal is clinically important because decisions about intubation often occur under time pressure, emotional distress, and prognostic uncertainty. In such situations, families may be required to make urgent surrogate decisions while simultaneously processing fear, financial concerns, and unfamiliar ICU practices. When communication is brief, inconsistent, or overly technical, families may rely on community narratives or previous experiences rather than on a clear understanding of the patient’s current condition and treatment options.

Family Beliefs and Interpretation of Ventilation and Sedation

When families equate ventilation with futility, refusal may become a protective choice intended to avoid perceived harm, indignity, or an emotionally difficult death in the ICU. This belief may be reinforced when clinicians provide explanations that are too brief or technical, or when families do not have a coherent mental model of what ventilation does, why sedation may be needed, and what clinical improvement may look like. A common illustrative scenario is that relatives may see an intubated and sedated patient who no longer speaks or responds normally and may interpret this as evidence of death, concealment, or treatment-related harm unless the purpose of sedation and the expected clinical trajectory are explained repeatedly and consistently.

Misinterpretation of sedation is a particularly important trigger. Reduced responsiveness may be interpreted as death, concealment, or treatment-induced suffering. In practice, this misunderstanding can contribute to requests for extubation, accusations of wrongdoing, or withdrawal of consent for ongoing support. Trust is fragile in any ICU, but it may be especially vulnerable when families cannot freely observe the patient, cannot access consistent updates, or cannot anticipate the expected trajectory and uncertainty of recovery.

Financial Toxicity and Oxygen-Related Affordability Shocks

In this setting, decisions about life-sustaining treatment often occur under intense affordability constraints. ICU charges are frequently paid out of pocket, and oxygen costs can become especially visible and burdensome when supply relies on cylinders. During periods of scarcity in Mogadishu, public reporting described oxygen cylinder prices rising dramatically, sometimes reaching several hundred US dollars in private facilities, illustrating the scale of affordability shocks a family may face during prolonged respiratory failure.6 This example is used as contextual public reporting rather than systematic cost data, and it should therefore be interpreted cautiously as an illustration of affordability pressure rather than as a generalizable estimate of ICU oxygen costs.6

Financial pressure may operate at two levels. In the short term, families may face immediate out-of-pocket ICU charges, oxygen costs, medication costs, and transport or caregiver expenses, which can influence urgent decisions about intubation or continuation of ventilation. In the longer term, financial toxicity after critical illness may affect household stability, recovery, follow-up care, and family distress.7 This distinction is important because acute affordability shocks may drive immediate refusal, while longer-term financial toxicity may shape trust, expectations, and future willingness to accept intensive care.7 In this context, the immediate effect may be more severe because payment often precedes continued care and because affordable alternatives may be limited.

Why Ventilation Refusal is a Patient Preference and Adherence Issue

Mechanical ventilation decisions are often surrogate decisions negotiated across multiple relatives, competing authority structures, uncertain prognosis, and limited time for counseling. In this environment, refusal may function as nonadherence to a clinically indicated plan when it is driven by modifiable determinants such as understanding, trust, and affordability. Here, the term non-adherence is used analytically to describe divergence from a medically indicated plan, not to assign blame to families facing distress, uncertainty, and financial constraint.

Evidence from ICU research supports structured approaches to improve communication and surrogate decision-making. A stepped-wedge cluster randomized trial of a family support intervention demonstrated improvements in ratings of communication and patient- and family-centeredness and was associated with shorter ICU length of stay.8 A systematic review and meta-analysis of surrogate decision-making interventions found associations with selected resource-use outcomes while emphasizing the complexity of surrogate decision-making and the need for structured support.9 Although much of this evidence comes from higher-resource settings, it provides useful principles that can be adapted cautiously to resource-constrained ICUs. This framing is important because it shifts the clinical response from judging refusal as irrational to identifying modifiable barriers to informed, trusted, and affordable acceptance of clinically indicated care.

Practical, Culturally Responsive Interventions for Low-Resource ICUs

ICUs in resource-constrained settings do not need to wait for ideal infrastructure to reduce preventable refusal or early discontinuation of ventilation. High-yield changes may be brief, repeatable, and compatible with staffing constraints. Standardized orientation at the moment ventilation is proposed should explain what the ventilator does, why sedation may be used, what improvement may look like, what risks exist, and what outcomes are possible, while acknowledging uncertainty.

A short daily update at a predictable time using a consistent structure can reduce contradictory messages and help families follow progress indicators. Early cost transparency should also be treated as a safety intervention. A written cost range and explanation of major cost drivers, coupled with rapid linkage to social support mechanisms, may reduce the likelihood that clinicians are pressured to negotiate costs during emergencies and may decrease the perception that ventilation recommendations are financially motivated.

When refusal persists despite adequate explanation and imminent risk remains, a documented escalation pathway can help protect patients while maintaining respectful communication.

Such escalation should be non-coercive and should protect patient dignity, autonomy, and the right of patients and families to receive clear, consistent, and understandable information before high-risk decisions are finalized.

These interventions should be understood as communication and safety processes rather than persuasion tools; their purpose is to support informed preference formation, reduce misunderstanding, and protect patient rights during time-sensitive decisions.

System Implications for Oxygen Access and Financing

Oxygen ecosystem strengthening may reduce both clinical risk and cost-driven refusal. Global guidance emphasizes that oxygen access depends on an integrated ecosystem that includes production, maintenance, distribution, clinical use, and financing rather than equipment alone.10

There have been ongoing efforts to improve oxygen supply in Somalia, including WHO-supported initiatives involving pressure swing adsorption plants and national-level support to expand availability.11 Such investments may decrease dependence on costly cylinders, reduce price shocks, and strengthen confidence that ICU care is both clinically meaningful and financially predictable.

Conclusion

Mechanical ventilation refusal in Somali intensive care is likely shaped by the interaction of belief-based fatalism, economic vulnerability, and trust and communication gaps. A patient-preference and adherence framework clarifies that some refusal events may represent modifiable non-adherence to a medically indicated plan rather than simple rejection of care. This commentary adds by reframing ventilation refusal as a social, communicative, economic, and ethical process that can be addressed through structured family communication, transparent cost discussions, and respectful escalation pathways.

The proposed interventions are consistent with existing intensive care literature discussed in the manuscript, including evidence supporting structured family communication, surrogate decision-making support, and health-system approaches to oxygen access and financing. Standardized family orientation, consistent daily updates, early cost transparency with social support linkage, and documented escalation for high-risk refusal are feasible steps that may strengthen informed decision-making, reduce preventable harm, and rebuild confidence in life-sustaining therapies. Because this is a commentary informed by contextual experience and selected literature rather than primary empirical research, it does not provide generalizable empirical results; therefore, the proposed interventions should be evaluated in future qualitative, audit, and implementation studies in local critical care settings.
