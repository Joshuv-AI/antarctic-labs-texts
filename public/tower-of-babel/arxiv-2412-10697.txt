Abstract




Partial Chebyshev Polynomials and Fan Graphs

Abstract
Motivated by the product formula of the Chebyshev polynomials
of the second kind
U_{n}(x)
, we newly introduce
the partial Chebyshev polynomials

U^{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(x)
and
U^{\mathrm{o}}_{n}(x)

and derive their basic properties,
relations to the classical Chebyshev polynomials,
and new factorization formulas for
U_{n}(x)
.
In order to calculate the quadratic embedding constant
(QEC) of a fan graph
K_{1}+P_{n}
,
we derive a new polynomial
\phi_{n}(x)
which is factorized
by partial Chebyshev polynomial
U^{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(x)
.
We prove that
\mathrm{QEC}(K_{1}+P_{n})
is given in terms of the
minimal zero of
\phi_{n}(x)
,
and obtain the explicit value of
\mathrm{QEC}(K_{1}+P_{n})
for
an even
n
and its reasonable exstimate for an odd
n
.

Keywords
Chebychev polynomials, factorization,
fan graph, distance matrix,
partial Chebychev polynomials, quadratic embedding constant

05C50, 06C76, 51K99, 42C05

Introduction

The Chebyshev polynomials, known as classical ortho-gonal polynomials,
are widely used in various fields such as
differential and integral equations, approximation theory,
asymptotic theory, mathematical statistics, and so on,
see e.g.,
[
25
,
37
]
.
On the other hand, the algebraic graph theory has a long history
and recently, much attention has been drawn not only to
the adjacency and combinatorial Laplace matrices
but also to the distance matrix

[
5
,
8
,
10
]
.
A first connection between the Chebyshev polynomials and
algebraic graph theory is likely found in the fact that
the characteristic polynomial
of the adjacency matrix
A_{n}
of the path
P_{n}
is given by
the Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind
in such a way that
\det(xI-A_{n})=U_{n}(x/2)=\tilde{U}_{n}(x)
,
see e.g.,
[
8
,
12
]
.
From this observation
we may expect and forsee many fruitful developments.
Indeed, in chemical graph theory the Chebyshev polynomials
have been efficiently used for the study of characteristic polynomials
and matching polynomials of some special graphs

[
3
,
4
,
18
]
.
Moreover, the distance matrix provides important characteristics of
graphs such as distance level patterns, Wiener indices,
and distance spectra
[
27
,
16
]
.

Motivated by the aforementioned background and the embedding problem
in Euclidean distance geometry

[
1
,
2
,
13
,
20
,
22
]

tracing back to Blumenthal
[
9
]
and
Schoenberg
[
39
,
40
]
,
we arrived at the introduction of the quadratic embedding constant (QEC)
of a finite connected graph
[
32
,
35
]
.
In recent years, research on the QEC has progressed for various classes of
graphs
[
11
,
19
,
28
,
34
,
24
,
29
]
,
and interest in classification based on the QEC has been increasing

[
6
,
7
,
31
,
33
]
.

In this paper,
using the newly introduced “partial Chebyshev polynomials,”
we solve the problem of evaluating the QEC of a fun graph
K_{1}+P_{n}
,
that is, the graph join of the singleton graph
K_{1}
and the path
P_{n}
with
n\geq 1
.
The fan graphs, as one of the basic families of graphs,
have been studied from various aspects,
for example,
matching polynomials
[
4
]
,
coloring and chromatic polynomials

[
15
,
26
,
38
]
,
the distance matrices
[
17
]
,
graph spectrum
[
23
]
,
subtree problem
[
42
]
, and so forth.
The calculation of
\mathrm{QEC}(K_{1}+P_{n})
is
not straightforward, contrary to the seemingly simple structure of a fan graph.
However, this can also be inferred from the heavy work on

\mathrm{QEC}(P_{n})
in
[
28
]
.
In the previous paper
[
30
]
,
after rather tedius computation based on differential calculus,
we evaluated
\mathrm{QEC}(K_{1}+P_{n})
and
conjectured that
\mathrm{QEC}(K_{1}+P_{n})
forms a strictly
increasing sequence converging to
0
.
In this paper, by using the partial Chebyshev polynomials,
not only is the evaluation of
\mathrm{QEC}(K_{1}+P_{n})
greatly simplified,
but the conjecture is also positively resolved.
Thus, our results form the foundation for the problem of
classifying graphs along the increasing sequence

-1/2=\mathrm{QEC}(K_{1}+P_{3})<\mathrm{QEC}(K_{1}+P_{4})<\dots\rightarrow 0
,
while an attempt to classify graphs
along the increasing sequence

-1=\mathrm{QEC}(P_{2})<\mathrm{QEC}(P_{3})<\dots\rightarrow-1/2
has already begun
[
6
,
7
]
.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we define

In Section
3

we formulate the main problem of calculating

\mathrm{QEC}(K_{1}+P_{n})
,

and state the main results

3.6

and
3.7
).

4

2
Partial Chebyshev Polynomials

2.1
Motivation

Following the standard notation,
for
n\geq 0
the
Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind

(of order
n
) is defined by

U_{n}(x)=\frac{\sin(n+1)\theta}{\sin\theta},\qquad x=\cos\theta.

(2.1)

The point of our departure is the
product formula of
U_{n}(x)
given by

U_{n}(x)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}2\left(x-\cos\frac{k\pi}{n+1}\right),\qquad n\geq 1,

(2.2)

which follows by examining the zeroes of
U_{n}(x)

in the definition (
2.1
).
As we will show in Theorems
2.1

and
2.6
,
this product can be naturally split into “even part” and “odd part.”
In the following sections,
for clear comparison with classical Chebyshev polynomials
we define the partial Chebyshev polynomials
as quotients of trigonometric functions in a similar spirit
as the classical ones.
We then derive the factorization

U_{n}(x)=U^{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(x)\cdot U^{\mathrm{o}}_{n}(x)

and other basic properties.

2.2
Definition and Recurrence Relations

With each
n\geq 0
we associate new polynomials

U^{\mathrm{o}}_{n}(x)
and
U^{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(x)
defined by

\displaystyle U^{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\sin(n+1)\theta/2}{\sin\theta/2}&\text{if $n$ is even,}\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\sin(n+1)\theta/2}{\sin\theta}&\text{if $n$ is odd,}\end{cases}\qquad x=\cos\theta,

(2.3)

\displaystyle U^{\mathrm{o}}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\cos(n+1)\theta/2}{\cos\theta/2}&\text{if $n$ is even,}\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle 2\cos\frac{(n+1)\theta}{2}&\text{if $n$ is odd,}\end{cases}\qquad x=\cos\theta.

(2.4)

In fact, by elementary trigonometric formulas and induction
argument we easily see that the right-hand sides of
(
2.3
) and (
2.4
) are
polynomials of
\cos\theta
.
Moreover, the following results are obtained
directly from (
2.1
),
(
2.3
) and (
2.4
)
with elementary trigonometric formulas.

Theorem 2.1
.

For
n\geq 0
we have the factorization

U_{n}(x)=U^{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(x)\cdot U^{\mathrm{o}}_{n}(x)
.

Theorem 2.2
.

For
n\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle U^{\mathrm{e}}_{2n}(x)

\displaystyle=U_{n}(x)+U_{n-1}(x),

\displaystyle U^{o}_{2n}(x)

\displaystyle=U_{n}(x)-U_{n-1}(x),\qquad

(2.5)

\displaystyle U^{\mathrm{e}}_{2n+1}(x)

\displaystyle=U_{n}(x),

\displaystyle U^{\mathrm{o}}_{2n+1}(x)

\displaystyle=U_{n+1}(x)-U_{n-1}(x),

(2.6)

where we tacitly understand that
U_{-1}(x)=0
.

Remark 2.3
.

It is well known that the Chebyshev polynomials
U_{n}(x)
fulfill
the three-term recurrence relation:

P_{n+2}(x)-2xP_{n+1}(x)+P_{n}(x)=0,\qquad n\geq 0.

(2.7)

We see that the four series of polynomials

U^{\mathrm{e}}_{2n}(x),U^{\mathrm{o}}_{2n}(x),U^{\mathrm{e}}_{2n+1}(x)
and
U^{\mathrm{o}}_{2n+1}(x)

satisfy the same three-term recurrence relation as in
(
2.7
) with different initial conditions.
Since any polynomials
P_{n}(x)
satisfying
(
2.7
) is expressible
as a linear combination of
U_{n}(x)
and
U_{n-1}(x)
,
by matching the coefficients (possibly polynomials)
we may obtain Theorem
2.2
.

In some contexts, the “compressed” Chebyshev polynomials
of the second kind defined by
\tilde{U}_{n}(x)=U_{n}(x/2)
are useful.
It is well known that
\tilde{U}_{n}(x)=x^{n}+\dotsb
is a monic polynomial
with integer coefficients.
In this connection we also note the following result,
of which the proof is immediate by Theorem
2.2
.

Proposition 2.4
.

For
n\geq 0
, the compressed partial Chebyshev polynomials

\tilde{U}^{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(x)=U^{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(x/2)

and
\tilde{U}^{\mathrm{o}}_{n}(x)=U^{\mathrm{o}}_{n}(x/2)
are
monic polynomials with integer coefficients.

Remark 2.5
.

Following
[
25
,
37
]
let

T_{n}(x)
,
V_{n}(x)
and
W_{n}(x)
be the Chebyshev polynomials
of the first, third and fourth kinds.
It is shown by comparing the definitions that

U^{\mathrm{e}}_{2n}(x)=W_{n}(x)
,

U^{\mathrm{o}}_{2n}(x)=V_{n}(x)
,

U^{\mathrm{e}}_{2n+1}(x)=U_{n}(x)
and

U^{\mathrm{o}}_{2n+1}(x)=2T_{n+1}(x)
for
n\geq 0
.

2.3
Factorization

Theorem 2.6
.

For
n\geq 0
we have

\\
U^{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(x)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq n\\
\text{$k$: even}\end{subarray}}2\left(x-\cos\frac{k\pi}{n+1}\right),\qquad U^{\mathrm{o}}_{n}(x)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq n\\
\text{$k$: odd}\end{subarray}}2\left(x-\cos\frac{k\pi}{n+1}\right),

(2.8)

where we tacitly understand that

U^{\mathrm{e}}_{0}(x)=U^{\mathrm{e}}_{1}(x)=U^{\mathrm{o}}_{0}(x)=1

(the range of
k
in the product is empty).

Proof.

We start with the “even” part.
Suppose first that
n\geq 2
is even.
By definition we have

U^{\mathrm{e}}_{n}\left(\cos\frac{2l\pi}{n+1}\right)=\frac{\sin l\pi}{\sin l\pi/(n+1)}=0,\qquad 1\leq l\leq n/2.

Since
U^{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(x)
is a polynomial of order
n/2
,
we see that
\cos 2l\pi/(n+1)
with
1\leq l\leq n/2

exhaust the zeroes of
U^{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(x)

and they are all simple.
The case of an odd
n\geq 3
is examined in a similar manner.
As a result, for
n\geq 2
the zeroes of
U^{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(x)

are all simple and given by

\cos 2l\pi/(n+1)
with
1\leq l\leq[n/2]
.
Therefore,
U^{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(x)
is a constant multiple of

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq n\\
\text{$k$: even}\end{subarray}}\left(x-\cos\frac{k\pi}{n+1}\right).

It follows from Theorem
2.2
that
the leading term of
U^{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(x)
coincides with the one of

U_{m}(x)=(2x)^{m}+\dotsb
,
where
m=n/2
for an even
n
and
m=(n-1)/2
for an odd
n
.
Then, matching the coefficients of the leading terms,
we obtain the first relation
in (
2.8
).
The second one follows in a similar manner.
∎

We note that
Theorem
2.1
is reproduced from
Theorem
2.6
with
the product formula (
2.2
).
Moreover, we obtain factorization formulas for
the Chebyshev polynomials
U_{n}(x)
, which are not found in
literatures so far as we know.
While, other types of factorizations are known, see e.g.,

[
21
,
36
,
41
]
.

From now on, we often write
U_{n}
for
U_{n}(x)
.

Theorem 2.7
.

For
n\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle U_{2n}

\displaystyle=(U_{n}+U_{n-1})(U_{n}-U_{n-1}),

\displaystyle U_{2n+1}

\displaystyle=U_{n}(U_{n+1}-U_{n-1}),

(2.9)

\displaystyle U_{2n}-1

\displaystyle=U_{n-1}(U_{n+1}-U_{n-1}),

\displaystyle U_{2n+1}-1

\displaystyle=(U_{n+1}-U_{n})(U_{n}+U_{n-1}),

(2.10)

\displaystyle U_{2n}+1

\displaystyle=U_{n}(U_{n}-U_{n-2}),

\displaystyle U_{2n+1}+1

\displaystyle=(U_{n+1}+U_{n})(U_{n}-U_{n-1}),

(2.11)

where we tacitly understand
that
U_{-1}=U_{-1}(x)=0
and
U_{-2}=U_{-2}(x)=-1
.

Proof.

We obtain (
2.9
)
by combining Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
.
Then, (
2.10
) and (
2.11
) follow from
(
2.9
)
with the help of the well-known relation

U_{n}^{2}-U_{n+1}U_{n-1}=1
.
∎

Below we record some more factorization formulas
based on the partial Chebyshev polynomials.
The proofs are computational and omitted.

Theorem 2.8
.

For
n\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle U_{2n}-U_{2n-1}-1

\displaystyle=2(x-1)U_{n-1}(U_{n}+U_{n-1}),

(2.12)

\displaystyle U_{2n}+U_{2n-1}-1

\displaystyle=2(x+1)U_{n-1}(U_{n}-U_{n-1}),

(2.13)

\displaystyle U_{2n}-U_{2n-1}+1

\displaystyle=(U_{n}-U_{n-1})(U_{n}-U_{n-2}),

(2.14)

\displaystyle U_{2n}+U_{2n-1}+1

\displaystyle=(U_{n}+U_{n-1})(U_{n}-U_{n-2}),

(2.15)

and

\displaystyle U_{2n+1}-U_{2n}-1

\displaystyle=2(x-1)U_{n}(U_{n}+U_{n-1}),

(2.16)

\displaystyle U_{2n+1}+U_{2n}+1

\displaystyle=2(x+1)U_{n}(U_{n}-U_{n-1}),

(2.17)

\displaystyle U_{2n+1}-U_{2n}+1

\displaystyle=(U_{n}-U_{n-1})(U_{n+1}-U_{n-1}),

(2.18)

\displaystyle U_{2n+1}+U_{2n}-1

\displaystyle=(U_{n}+U_{n-1})(U_{n+1}-U_{n-1}).

(2.19)

3
Quadratic Embedding Constants of Fan Graphs

3.1
Quadratic Embedding Constants of Graphs

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite connected graph on
n=|V|\geq 2
vertices.
For two distinct vertices
i,j\in V

the length of a shortest walk connecting them
is denoted by
d(i,j)
and is called
the
graph distance
between
i
and
j
.
We set
d(i,i)=0
for any
i\in V
.
The
distance matrix
of
G
is defined by

D=[d(i,j)]_{i,j\in V}
.
Following
[
32
,
35
]

the
quadratic embedding constant

(
QEC
for short) of
G
is defined by

\mathrm{QEC}(G)=\max\{\langle f,Df\rangle\,;\,f\in C(V),\,\langle f,f\rangle=1,\,\langle{\bf 1},f\rangle=0\},

(3.1)

where
C(V)\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
the space of real column vectors
f=[f(i)]_{i\in V}
,

{\bf 1}\in C(V)
the column vector whose entries are all one,
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
the canonical inner product.

The QEC provides a criterion for
a finite connected graph
G=(V,E)
to admit
a
quadratic embedding
in a Euclidean space,
i.e., a map
\pi:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that

\|\pi(i)-\pi(j)\|^{2}=d(i,j)
for any
i,j\in V
,
where the left-hand side stands for the square of the Euclidean
distance.
Such a graph is called
of QE class
.
It follows from the classical result of
Schoenberg
[
39
,
40
]

that
G
is of QE class if and only if
the distance matrix
D
is conditionally negative definite,
and hence if and only if
\mathrm{QEC}(G)\leq 0
.
Beyond this motivating point there is a growing interest in
exploring how effective the QEC works
in the classification of graphs.

3.2
Fan Graphs

For
n\geq 1
let
P_{n}
be the path graph on
n
vertices.
The
fan graph
on
n+1
vertices
is the graph join of the singleton graph
K_{1}

and the path
P_{n}
, denoted by
K_{1}+P_{n}
,
namely, a graph on the vertex set

V=\{0,1,2,\dots,n\}
with the edge set

E=\{\{i,i+1\}\,;\,1\leq i\leq n-1\}\cup\{\{0,i\}\,;\,1\leq i\leq n\}
, see Figure
1
.
The distance matrix of
K_{1}+P_{n}
is written down
in a block matrix form:

D=\begin{bmatrix}0&J\\
J&2J-2I-A_{n}\end{bmatrix},\qquad A_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&\\
1&0&1&\\
&1&0&1&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&1&0&1\\
&&&&1&0\end{bmatrix},

(3.2)

where
J
is the all-one matrix (the size is understood in the context),

I=I_{n}
the
n\times n
identity matrix
and
A_{n}
the adjacency matrix of
P_{n}
.

Figure 1:
Fan graph
K_{1}+P_{n}
with
n=5

3.3
Preliminary Formula

For
\mathrm{QEC}(K_{1}+P_{n})
we need to find the conditional maximum
of the quadratic function:

\displaystyle\psi(f,g)=\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}f\\
g\end{bmatrix},D\begin{bmatrix}f\\
g\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2\langle f,Jg\rangle+\langle g,(2J-2I-A_{n})g\rangle

\displaystyle=2f\langle{\bf 1},g\rangle+2\langle{\bf 1},g\rangle^{2}-2\langle g,g\rangle-\langle g,A_{n}g\rangle,

(3.3)

under the conditions

f^{2}+\langle g,g\rangle=1
and
f+\langle{\bf 1},g\rangle=0
,
where
f\in\mathbb{R}
and
g\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
see (
3.1
)
and (
3.2
).
Upon applying the method of Lagrange’s multipliers,
we set

\varphi(f,g,\lambda,\mu)=\psi(f,g)-\lambda(f^{2}+\langle g,g\rangle-1)-\mu(f+\langle{\bf 1},g\rangle).

(3.4)

Let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of stationary points

(f,g,\lambda,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
of (
3.4
),
that is, the solutions to

\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial f}=\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial g_{1}}=\dotsb=\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial g_{n}}=\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\lambda}=\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\mu}=0.

(3.5)

By general theory, the conditional maximum is
attained at some stationary point.
On the other hand, we see easily
that
\psi(f,g)=\lambda
for any
(f,g,\lambda,\mu)\in\mathcal{S}
.
Thus, we come to the useful formula:

\mathrm{QEC}(K_{1}+P_{n})=\max\lambda(\mathcal{S}),

(3.6)

where
\lambda(\mathcal{S})
is the set of
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

appearing in the stationary points
\mathcal{S}
,
see
[
35
]
for more details.

Calculating the derivatives
in (
3.5
) explicitly
and switching
\lambda
to a new variable
\alpha=-\lambda-2
,
the equations (
3.5
) are transferred
into the following equations:

\displaystyle(\alpha+1)f

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{2},

(3.7)

\displaystyle(A_{n}-J-\alpha I)g

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mu}{2}\,{\bf 1},

(3.8)

\displaystyle f^{2}+\langle g,g\rangle

\displaystyle=1,

(3.9)

\displaystyle f+\langle{\bf 1},g\rangle

\displaystyle=0.

(3.10)

Let
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}
be the minimal
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
appearing
in the solutions
(f,g,\alpha,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}

to the system of
equations (
3.7
)–(
3.10
).
Then,
\max\lambda(\mathcal{S})=-\tilde{\alpha}_{n}-2
and
(
3.6
) becomes

\mathrm{QEC}(K_{1}+P_{n})=-\tilde{\alpha}_{n}-2,\qquad n\geq 1.

For
n=1,2
the value is already known
without having to recalculate it.
In fact,
K_{1}+P_{1}=K_{2}
and
K_{1}+P_{2}=K_{3}
are
the complete graphs of which the QEC are
-1

[
35
]
.
Therefore, we have

\mathrm{QEC}(K_{1}+P_{1})=\mathrm{QEC}(K_{1}+P_{2})=-1
and

\tilde{\alpha}_{1}=\tilde{\alpha}_{2}=-1
.

Hereafter we focus on the case of
n\geq 3
.
It is known
[
6
]
that

\mathrm{QEC}(G)\geq-1
for any graph
G

and equality holds only for a complete graph
G=K_{n}
with
n\geq 2
.
Therefore, for
n\geq 3
we have
\mathrm{QEC}(K_{1}+P_{n})>-1

and
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}<-1
.
Thus, to obtain
\mathrm{QEC}(K_{1}+P_{n})
we need only to
find the solutions to
equations (
3.7
)–(
3.10
)
with
\alpha<-1
.
Under this condition, from (
3.7
) we obtain

f=\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\,\frac{\mu}{2}\,.

(3.11)

Since
Jg=\langle{\bf 1},g\rangle{\bf 1}=-f{\bf 1}
by
(
3.10
), with the help of
(
3.11
) we see that (
3.8
) becomes

(A_{n}-\alpha I)g=-\frac{\alpha+2}{\alpha+1}\,\frac{\mu}{2}\,{\bf 1}.

(3.12)

Thus, our task is to solve
the system of equations (
3.11
), (
3.12
),
(
3.9
) and (
3.10
),
and to determine the minimal
\alpha\in(-\infty,-1)

appearing in the solutions, which is
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}
.
We solve this task by dividing it into two cases.
Let
\mathrm{ev}(A_{n})
denote the set of eigenvalues of
A_{n}
.

(Case I)

Find the solutions with

\alpha\in(-\infty,-1)\backslash\mathrm{ev}(A_{n})

and determine the minimal
\alpha
appearing therein,
which is denoted by
\sigma_{n}
.

(Case II)

Find the solutions with

\alpha\in(-\infty,-1)\cap\mathrm{ev}(A_{n})

and determine the minimal
\alpha
appearing therein,
which is denoted by
\tau_{n}
.

Summing up, we arrive at the preliminary formula for

\mathrm{QEC}(K_{1}+P_{n})
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For
n\geq 3
let
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}
be the minimal
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

appearing in the solutions to
the system of equations (
3.11
), (
3.12
),
(
3.9
) and (
3.10
).
Then we have

Case I: Calculating

We will find the solutions

does not appear in the solutions,
by simple algebra we see that (

(\alpha+2)\langle{\bf 1},(A_{n}-\alpha I)^{-1}{\bf 1}\rangle-1=0.

(3.16)

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
n\geq 1
.
For
\alpha\not\in\mathrm{ev}(A_{n})\cup\{\pm 2\}
we have

\langle{\bf 1},(A_{n}-\alpha I)^{-1}{\bf 1}\rangle=\frac{1}{(2-\alpha)^{2}}\left\{n(2-\alpha)+2-2\left(\frac{\tilde{U}_{n-1}(\alpha)}{\tilde{U}_{n}(\alpha)}+\frac{1}{\tilde{U}_{n}(\alpha)}\right)\right\}.

(3.17)

Proof.

We set
g=(A_{n}-\alpha I)^{-1}{\bf 1}
and
g=[g_{1},\dots,g_{n}]^{T}
.
Then
g
satisfies
(A_{n}-\alpha I)g={\bf 1}
,
which is equivalent to the three-term recurrence relation:

g_{k+2}-\alpha g_{k+1}+g_{k}=1,\qquad 0\leq k\leq n-1,

(3.18)

with boundary condition
g_{0}=g_{n+1}=0
.
The unique solution is explicitly given by

g_{k}=\frac{1}{2-\alpha}\bigg(1-\frac{\xi^{k}}{1+\xi^{n+1}}-\frac{\eta^{k}}{1+\eta^{n+1}}\bigg),\qquad 0\leq k\leq n+1,

where
\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{C}
be the characteristic roots of
(
3.18
),
satisfying
\xi+\eta=\alpha
and
\xi\eta=1
.
Noting that
\xi\neq\eta
by the assumption
\alpha\neq\pm 2
,
by direct computation we come to

\displaystyle\langle{\bf 1},(A_{n}-\alpha I)^{-1}{\bf 1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\bf 1},g\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}g_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2-\alpha)^{2}}\left\{n(2-\alpha)+2-2\left(\frac{\xi^{n}-\eta^{n}}{\xi^{n+1}-\eta^{n+1}}+\frac{\xi-\eta}{\xi^{n+1}-\eta^{n+1}}\right)\right\}.

(3.19)

On the other hand,
by comparing the recurrence relations we obtain

\frac{\xi^{n+1}-\eta^{n+1}}{\xi-\eta}=\tilde{U}_{n}(\alpha),\qquad n\geq 0.

(3.20)

Then, (
3.17

(
3.19
) and (
3.20
).
∎

With the help of Lemma
3.2
and by simple algebra
we see that (
3.16

((n+1)\alpha^{2}-6\alpha-4n)\tilde{U}_{n}(\alpha)+2(\alpha+2)(\tilde{U}_{n-1}(\alpha)+1)=0.

(3.21)

Setting
\alpha=2x
in the left-hand side,
we define the key polynomial

\phi_{n}(x)=((n+1)x^{2}-3x-n)U_{n}(x)+(x+1)(U_{n-1}(x)+1).

(3.22)

Obviously,
\phi_{n}(x)
is a polynomial of
order
n+2

Lemma 3.3
.

For

we have

\sigma_{n}=\min\{\alpha\in(-\infty,-1)\backslash\mathrm{ev}(A_{n})\,;\,\phi_{n}(\alpha/2)=0\}.

(3.23)

Proof.

Any

), (

Then (

For the evaluation of (

3.5
Case II: Calculating
\tau_{n}

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
n\geq 1
.
For
1\leq k\leq n
let
\omega^{(n)}_{k}
be the eigenvalue of
A_{n}

defined as in (
3.15
).
Then the eigenspace associated to
\omega^{(n)}_{k}

is orthogonal to
{\bf 1}
if and only if
k
is even.

Proof.

It is easily verified that any eigenvector associated to
an eigenvalue
\alpha=\omega^{(n)}_{k}
is
a constant multiple of
g=[g_{1},\dots,g_{n}]^{T}
given by

g_{l}=\sin\frac{lk\pi}{n+1}\,,\qquad 1\leq l\leq n\,.

Moreover, with the help of an elementary formula
of trigonometric series we obtain

\langle{\bf 1},g\rangle=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\sin\frac{lk\pi}{n+1}=\sin\dfrac{k\pi}{2}\sin\dfrac{nk\pi}{2(n+1)}\bigg(\sin\dfrac{k\pi}{2(n+1)}\bigg)^{-1}.

Since
nk/(2(n+1))
is not an integer for
1\leq k\leq n
,
we see that

\langle{\bf 1},g\rangle=0
if and only if
k
is even.
(In that case
g
is skew-palindromic, see
[
14
]
.)
∎

Now we find the solutions
(f,g,\alpha,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}

to equations (
3.11
), (
3.12
),
(
3.9
) and (
3.10
)
with
\alpha\in(-\infty,-1)\cap\mathrm{ev}(A_{n})

by dividing them into two cases.

(Case II-1)

\mu=0
.
Then
f=0
by (
3.11
), and (
3.12
),
(
3.9
) and (
3.10
) become

A_{n}g=\alpha g,\qquad\langle g,g\rangle=1,\qquad\langle{\bf 1},g\rangle=0,

(3.24)

respectively.
It then follows from Lemma
3.4
that

\alpha=\omega^{(n)}_{k}
appears in the solutions
to (
3.11
), (
3.12
),
(
3.9
) and (
3.10
)
with
\alpha\in(-\infty,-1)\cap\mathrm{ev}(A_{n})

if and only if
\omega^{(n)}_{k}<-1
and
k
is even.
Note that for
1\leq k\leq n
we have
\omega^{(n)}_{k}<-1

if and only if
2(n+1)<3k
.

(Case II-2)

\mu\neq 0
.
Since the right-hand side of (
3.12
) is not zero
in this case,
the equation has a solution if and only if

\mathrm{rank}(A_{n}-\alpha I)=\mathrm{rank}[A_{n}-\alpha I,{\bf 1}],

(3.25)

where
[A_{n}-\alpha I,{\bf 1}]
is an

matrix
obtained by adding
{\bf 1}
as the
(n+1)
-th column vector.
We will show that (
3.25
) fails for

\alpha=\omega_{k}^{(n)}
with an odd
k
.

For
1\leq i\leq n
let
v_{i}
be the
i
th row vector of

[A_{n}-\alpha I,{\bf 1}]
, that is,

v_{i}=[(A_{n}-\alpha I)_{i1},\dots,(A_{n}-\alpha I)_{in},1].

We first show that
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}
are linearly independent.
In fact, suppose that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}v_{i}=0,\qquad\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\in\mathbb{R}.

Comparing the
j
th components of both sides,
we obtain

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}(A_{n}-\alpha I)_{ij}=0\quad\text{for $1\leq j\leq n$},\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}=0\quad\text{for $j=n+1$}.

(3.26)

We set
\xi=[\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}]^{T}
.
Since
A_{n}-\alpha I
is a symmetric matrix,
(
3.26
) is equivalent to

(A_{n}-\alpha I)\xi=0,\qquad\langle{\bf 1},\xi\rangle=0.

(3.27)

If
\alpha=\omega^{(n)}_{k}
with an odd

then

.
Therefore,

are linearly independent.
Since they are the row vectors of the

[A_{n}-\alpha I,{\bf 1}]
, we have

\mathrm{rank}[A_{n}-\alpha I,{\bf 1}]=n
.
On the other hand, since
\alpha
is an eigenvalue of
A_{n}
, we have

\mathrm{rank}(A_{n}I-\alpha)\leq n-1
.
Thus, (
3.25
) fails
and (

Consequently,
\alpha=\omega^{(n)}_{k}
with an odd

never appears in the solutions,
while
\alpha=\omega^{(n)}_{k}
with
an even

may appear therein.

Combining the results in (Case II-1) and (Case II-2),
we conclude that

\tau_{n}=\min\bigg\{\omega^{(n)}_{k}=2\cos\frac{k\pi}{n+1}\,;\,1\leq k\leq n,\,\,\text{$k$ is even}\bigg\}\cap(-\infty,-1),

of which the explicit value is given by

\displaystyle=\omega^{(2n+1)}_{2n}=2\cos\frac{2n}{2n+2}\pi,\qquad\qquad n\geq 3,

(3.29)

\displaystyle\tau_{2n}

\displaystyle=\omega^{(2n)}_{2n}=2\cos\frac{2n}{2n+1}\pi,\qquad n\geq 2.

(3.30)

Note that
\tau_{3}
and
\tau_{5}
are not determined

3.28
) is empty.

For
n\geq 2
let
\beta_{n}
be the minimal zero of
U^{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(x)
.
In view of Theorem

we have

\beta_{2n}=\cos\frac{2n}{2n+1}\pi,\qquad\beta_{2n+1}=\cos\frac{2n}{2n+2}\pi,\qquad n\geq 1.

(3.31)

Then, comparing (

)
and (

For any

4
Key Polynomials
\phi_{n}(x)

In this section we examine the zeroes of the key polynomial:

\phi_{n}(x)=((n+1)x^{2}-3x-n)U_{n}(x)+(x+1)(U_{n-1}(x)+1),\qquad n\geq 0,

introduced in (
3.22
)
and complete the proof of the main results
(Theorems
3.6
and

3.7
).
For that purpose, with each
n\geq 0
we associate
polynomials
S_{n}(x)
defined by

\displaystyle S_{2n}(x)

\displaystyle=(2nx+x+2n-1)U_{n}(x)-(2nx+3x+2n+1)U_{n-1}(x),

(4.1)

\displaystyle S_{2n+1}(x)

\displaystyle=2(2nx^{2}+2x^{2}+2nx-x-1)U_{n}(x)-2(2nx+3x+2n+1)U_{n-1}(x).

(4.2)

4.1
Factorization

Theorem 4.1
.

For
n\geq 0
we have a factorization:

\phi_{n}(x)=(x-1)\cdot U_{n}^{\mathrm{e}}(x)\cdot S_{n}(x).

(4.3)

Proof.

Let
n\geq 0
.
In view of (
2.9
),
(
2.11
) and (
2.5
) we obtain

\displaystyle U_{2n}

\displaystyle=(U_{n}+U_{n-1})(U_{n}-U_{n-1})=U^{\mathrm{e}}_{2n}(U_{n}-U_{n-1}),

\displaystyle U_{2n-1}+1

\displaystyle=(U_{n}+U_{n-1})(U_{n-1}-U_{n-2})=U^{\mathrm{e}}_{2n}(U_{n-1}-U_{n-2}).

Then we have

\displaystyle\phi_{2n}(x)

\displaystyle=((2n+1)x^{2}-3x-2n)U_{2n}+(x+1)(U_{2n-1}+1)

\displaystyle=U^{\mathrm{e}}_{2n}\big\{((2n+1)x^{2}-3x-2n)(U_{n}-U_{n-1})+(x+1)(U_{n-1}-U_{n-2})\big\}.

Now we apply
U_{n-2}=2xU_{n-1}-U_{n}
to get

\displaystyle\phi_{2n}(x)

\displaystyle=U^{\mathrm{e}}_{2n}\big\{((2n+1)x^{2}-2x-2n+1)U_{n}-((2n+3)x^{2}-2x-2n-1)U_{n-1}\big\}

\displaystyle=U^{\mathrm{e}}_{2n}(x-1)\big\{(2nx+x+2n-1)U_{n}-(2nx+3x+2n+1)U_{n-1}\big\}

\displaystyle=(x-1)U^{\mathrm{e}}_{2n}S_{2n},

which proves (
4.3
) for an even
n
.
By a similar calculation using

\displaystyle U_{2n+1}

\displaystyle=U_{n}(U_{n+1}-U_{n-1})=U^{\mathrm{e}}_{2n+1}(U_{n+1}-U_{n-1}),

\displaystyle U_{2n}+1

\displaystyle=U_{n}(U_{n}-U_{n-2})=U^{\mathrm{e}}_{2n+1}(U_{n}-U_{n-2}),

which follow from
(
2.9
),
(
2.11
) and (
2.6
),
we come to (
4.3
) for an odd
n
.
∎

4.2
Zeroes of
S_{n}(x)

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
n\geq 1
.
For
1\leq k\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle S_{2n}\left(\cos\frac{2k\pi}{2n+1}\right)

\displaystyle=2(-1)^{k}\left(\cos\dfrac{k\pi}{2n+1}\right)^{-1}\left(n+(n+1)\cos\dfrac{2k\pi}{2n+1}\right),

(4.4)

\displaystyle S_{2n}\left(\cos\frac{2k-1}{2n+1}\pi\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\left(\sin\dfrac{2k-1}{2(2n+1)}\pi\right)^{-1}\left(1+\cos\dfrac{2k-1}{2n+1}\pi\right),

(4.5)

and

S_{2n}(1)=0,\qquad S_{2n}(-1)=2(-1)^{n-1}(2n+1).

(4.6)

Proof.

By definition (
4.1
) for any
l\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

\displaystyle S_{2n}\left(\cos\frac{l\pi}{2n+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left((2n+1)\cos\frac{l\pi}{2n+1}+2n-1\right)U_{n}\left(\cos\frac{l\pi}{2n+1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\left((2n+3)\cos\frac{l\pi}{2n+1}+2n+1\right)U_{n-1}\left(\cos\frac{l\pi}{2n+1}\right).

(4.7)

Setting
l=0
and
l=2n+1
, we obtain
(
4.6
) by using
U_{n}(\pm 1)=(\pm 1)^{n}(n+1)
.
For
1\leq k\leq n
, (
4.4
)
and (
4.5
) follow by
setting
l=2k
and
l=2k-1
in (
4.7
)
and applying the following formulas:

\displaystyle U_{n}\bigg(\cos\frac{2k\pi}{2n+1}\bigg)=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{2}\bigg(\cos\dfrac{k\pi}{2n+1}\bigg)^{-1},

\displaystyle U_{n-1}\bigg(\cos\frac{2k\pi}{2n+1}\bigg)=\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{2}\bigg(\cos\dfrac{k\pi}{2n+1}\bigg)^{-1},

\displaystyle U_{n}\bigg(\cos\frac{2k-1}{2n+1}\pi\bigg)=U_{n-1}\bigg(\cos\frac{2k-1}{2n+1}\pi\bigg)=\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{2}\bigg(\sin\dfrac{2k-1}{2(2n+1)}\pi\bigg)^{-1},

which are verified directly by the original definition
(

).
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
n\geq 1
and set

\xi_{0}=1,\qquad\xi_{n+1}=-1,\qquad\xi_{k}=\cos\frac{2k-1}{2n+1}\pi,\qquad 1\leq k\leq n.

has a unique zero in the interval

.

n

the zeroes of
S_{2n}(x)
, which are all simple.
(The statement remains true for
n=0

by natural interpretation since

is the
only zero of
S_{0}(x)=x-1
.)

Proof.

We will show that

S_{2n}(\xi_{k})S_{2n}(\xi_{k+1})<0,\qquad 1\leq k\leq n.

(4.8)

It follows from Lemma
4.2
that

S_{2n}(\xi_{k})=(-1)^{k}\bigg(\sin\dfrac{2k-1}{2(2n+1)}\pi\bigg)^{-1}\bigg(1+\cos\dfrac{2k-1}{2n+1}\pi\bigg),\qquad 1\leq k\leq n,

and hence

for any
1\leq k\leq n-1
.
Namely, (
4.8
) is valid for
1\leq k\leq n-1
.
Moreover, since

4.2
,
we have

\displaystyle S_{2n}(\xi_{n})S_{2n}(\xi_{n+1})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\bigg(\sin\dfrac{2n-1}{2(2n+1)}\pi\bigg)^{-1}\bigg(1+\cos\dfrac{2n-1}{2n+1}\pi\bigg)\times(-1)^{n-1}(2n+1)<0,

which means that
(

) is valid also for

.
We see from

) with the intermediate value theorem
that
S_{2n}(x)
has at least one zero in the interval

for each

.
On the other hand, we know that

.
Since

is a polynomial of order

,
each interval

contains exactly one zero

,
and all zeroes of

∎

(4.12)

In fact, (

) is verified for
