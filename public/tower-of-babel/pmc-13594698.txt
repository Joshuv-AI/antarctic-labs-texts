11-Oxo-mogroside V attenuates lung adenocarcinoma progression by disrupting metabolic homeostasis and suppressing TGFβ2/Myc-dependent oncogenic signaling
Authors: Xinyu Chen, Lihua Yang, Jiao Li, Liang Zhao, Qian Yu, Xuexin Yan, Qianyu Zhang, Haiping Zheng, Jinliang Kong, Qing Bu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•11-oxo-mogroside V suppresses LUAD progression in vitro and in vivo.•It disrupts metabolic homeostasis by reducing ATP, glucose, and LDH.•The compound induces G0/G1 arrest and promotes apoptosis in cancer cells.•It directly targets TGFβ2, HDAC1, and c-Myc to block oncogenic signaling.•Multi-pathway intervention positions it as a promising LUAD therapeutic.

Introduction

Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), the most common subtype of non-small cell lung cancer, remains a major contributor to cancer-related mortality worldwide owing to its aggressive behavior, metastatic potential, and frequent resistance to conventional therapies [1,2]. Despite advances in targeted therapies and immunomodulatory approaches, the 5-year survival rate of patients with advanced-stage disease remains poor, highlighting the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies [3,4].

Natural products derived from medicinal plants have played an important role in anticancer drug discovery, with compounds such as paclitaxel and camptothecin derivatives demonstrating established clinical efficacy [5,6]. In recent years, phytochemicals—including alkaloids, flavonoids, and terpenoids—have garnered renewed attention for their multimodal mechanisms against oncogenic signaling, angiogenesis, and metastatic pathways, often with fewer off-target effects than synthetic agents [7,8]. Emerging evidence suggests that plant-derived extracts or multi-component formulations may exert superior anticancer activity compared to isolated compounds, likely through synergistic interactions that enhance bioavailability, target multiple hallmarks of cancer, and mitigate drug resistance [9,10]. However, the mechanistic underpinnings of such synergies remain poorly characterized, particularly in the context of LUAD.

11-oxo-mogroside V, a bioactive triterpenoid saponin derived from Siraitia grosvenori (Luo Han Guo), possesses a wide variety of pharmacological activities [11]. It exhibits antioxidant, anticarcinogenic, and anti-inflammatory properties, making it a potential candidate for cancer prevention and therapy owing to its diverse pharmacological activities [12,13]. Recently, we demonstrated that 11-oxo-mogroside V effectively regulates metabolism and proliferation in NCI-H1573 cells and suppresses tumor progression in NCI-H1573 xenograft mouse models, suggesting its potential therapeutic value in LUAD. Nevertheless, the detailed molecular mechanisms and drug targets for the action of 11-oxo-mogroside V remain unknown. Therefore, as part of ongoing research, we systematically assessed the impact of 11-oxo-mogroside V against LUAD in vivo and in vitro, and further investigated the associated molecular mechanisms, which could aid in the development of this natural compound as a therapeutic drug for treating LUAD in clinical settings.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

11-oxo-mogroside V was purchased from Bio CuiYuan (Hubei, China). Annexin V-FITC/PI kits were purchased from the Chinese Tissue Culture Collections (CTCC, https://cctcc.whu.edu.cn/).

The primary antibodies were purchased from Abcam (c-Myc, #ab32072; TGIF, #ab52955), Bioss (TGFB2, #BS-20412R; HDAC1, #BS-1414R), HUABIO (SMAD2/3, #RT1566) and Proteintech (Bax, #50,599-2-Ig; Bcl2, #68,103-1-Ig; GAPDH, #60,004-1-Ig).

Cell culture

NCI-H1573 cells (ATCC CRL-5877, lung adenocarcinoma) and A549 cells (lung adenocarcinoma) were purchased from CTCC and cultured in RPMI 1640 Medium‌ (SH30011.03, HyClone, USA) and DMEM (SH30243.01, HyClone, USA), respectively. The media were supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2.

CCK-8 assay

For the cell viability assay, 5 × 103 NCI-H1573 cells were seeded into 96-well plates (n = 6). After incubation for 24 h, NCI-H1573 cells were treated with different concentrations of 11-oxo-mogroside V at concentrations of 100, 200, 400, 800 and 1000μg/mL for 24 h, 48 h and 72 h, respectively. Subsequently, the drug-containing medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 10% cell counting kit-8 solution (M006, CTCC). After incubation for another 1.5 h, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured by using MK3 microplate reader (Themo, USA). The experiment was repeated three times.

Flow cytometry

The density of 1 × 105 cells/mL NCI-H1573 cells was seeded in 6-well plates overnight and treated with 800 μg/mL of 11-oxo-mogroside V for 72 h (n = 6). Cells treated with 0.25% trypsin without EDTA were harvested, washed twice with ice-cold PBS.

Cells were resuspended in 300 µL of 1 × Binding Buffer, then sequentially stained with 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 10 µL propidium iodide (PI; M009, CTCC). After gentle mixing, samples were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 15 min (Annexin V-FITC) and 10 min (PI), respectively. Apoptotic cells were immediately analyzed using the flow cytometer (FACSVerse, BD).

The cells were preserved in 70% ethanol at 4 °C for 24 h and were centrifuged (1500 rpm, 5 min), resuspended in PBS containing 50 μg/mL RNase A (ST579, Beyotime), and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. 400 μL Propidium iodide (PI, 50 μg/mL; ST512, Beyotime) was added for 30 min in the dark to stain DNA. Flow cytometry (FACSVerse, BD) was used to analyze cell cycle distribution.

Experiments were conducted in triplicate, with untreated cells serving as negative controls. FlowJo 7.6 software was used to analyze the data.

Cell scratch assay

Cell migration was assessed using a standardized scratch wound healing assay as follows: 3.5 cm culture dishes were pre-marked with parallel horizontal lines (0.5–1.0 cm intervals) on the outer bottom surface. NCI-H1573 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were trypsinized with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA, counted, and seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells per dish. Following overnight incubation under 5% CO2 at 37 °C, confluent monolayers (≥90% coverage) were subjected to uniform linear scratches vertically aligned with the pre-marked guidelines. Dislodged cells were removed by triple PBS washing, and baseline images (0 h) were acquired under phase-contrast microscopy. After grouping, experiments were maintained in 37 °C, 5% CO2, and cell migration into the wound area was quantitatively evaluated through comparative imaging at 72 h post-scratch. The experiment was repeated three times.

Determination of the content of ATP, glucose, and LDH

ATP Chemiluminescence Assay Kit (E-BC-F002, Elabscience), Glucose Assay Kit with O-toluidine (S0201S, Beyotime) and LDH Activity Assay Kit (E-BC-K046-M, Elabscience) was purchased. Cell samples were lysed, and intracellular LDH activity was measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance at specific wavelengths was detected with a microplate reader (MK3, Thermo), and LDH activity was calculated using the formula provided in the kit. The experiment was repeated three times.

Acute toxicity assay

Male C57BL/6 J mice (7 weeks, 19 ± 2 g) were randomly assigned to five dose groups (12.5, 25, 50, 100, 200 mg/kg; n = 4 per group) and received 11-oxo-mogroside V or vehicle by oral gavage once daily for 7 days. General condition, body weight, behavior, and mortality were monitored daily. No death or gross organ abnormalities were observed at any dose, giving an estimated LD50 > 200 mg/kg.

Xenograft tumor experiment

Male BALB/c-nu nude mice (6-week old) were subcutaneously injected with 1 × 10⁶ NCI-H1573 cells in the axillary region. After 3 days of acclimatization, mice were randomly assigned to two groups (n = 6 per group). The treatment group received 11-oxo-mogroside V (100 mg/kg) by oral gavage daily, while the control group received sterile water. Tumor growth was monitored by bioluminescence imaging on days 0, 14, and 28. At endpoint (day 28), mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised, weighed, and photographed.

Tunel assay

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were dewaxed through sequential immersion in xylene I/II/III (20 min each) followed by rehydration in an ethanol gradient (100%, 95%, 90%, 80%, 70%; 5 min each) and distilled water (5 min). Antigen retrieval was performed by incubating sections with Proteinase K working solution (20μg/mL in PBS, pH 7.4) at 37 °C for 20 min within a hydrophobic barrier (ImmEdge™ pen), followed by three 5-min PBS washes on a decolorizing shaker. Sections were equilibrated with reaction buffer (TUNEL Assay Kit, Servicebio, G1504) at room temperature for 10 min. A TdT-mediated dUTP-biotin nick-end labeling mixture (TdT enzyme: dUTP: buffer = 1:5:50) was applied to fully cover tissues, and slides were incubated in a humidified chamber at 37 °C for 1 h. Post-labeling, sections were washed thrice with PBS and counterstained with DAPI (1μg/mL, 10 min, RT, dark). Slides were rinsed in PBS, air-dried, and sealed with anti-fade mounting medium. Fluorescence imaging was conducted using a confocal microscope (OLYMPUS, IX71) with predefined spectral parameters: DAPI (λex = 358 nm, λem = 461 nm). The experiment was repeated three times.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA extraction kit was used to extract RNA from NCI-H1573 cells treated with 11-oxo-mogroside V (800 μg/mL, 72 h) or vehicle control. RNA purity and concentration were measured by NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, and RNA integrity was verified via 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. Qualified RNA was quantified using a quantitative fluorescent PCR kit. Subsequently, total RNA was enriched, quantified, and sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (MGI Tech Co., Ltd., China) with a dedicated mRNA library preparation kit for generating 200–500 bp library products. Raw sequencing data were filtered using Trimmomatic 0.36, an open-source high-throughput sequencing data processing platform. DEGs between groups were identified using the edgeR 3.12.1 package, and KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed with KOBAS 2.1.1 software.

All raw RNA-seq data have been submitted to the NCBI SRA with the BioProject accession code PRJNA1300636.

Molecular docking

The PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) to download the 2D structure of 11-oxo-mogroside V (number: 122,706,002), and ChemBio3D Ultra15.0 software was used to get the lowest energy 3D structure. The structure of the receptor proteins (TGF-β2, PDB-4XJJ; C-myc, PDB-6G6K; and HDAC1da, PDB-6Z2J) were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (PDB, https://www.rcsb.org/). Protein and small-molecule structures were preprocessed using the Pre-preparation module in Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) 2022.09. For molecular docking, 1000 independent poses were generated and the top 100 poses (ranked by binding affinity ΔG) were selected. The lowest-energy conformation from the most populated cluster was selected for structural analysis to confirm docking reliability.

Western blot (WB)

Proteins were extracted from the tumor tissue of nude mice or from NCI-H1573 cells treated with 11-oxo-mogroside V (800 μg/mL, 72 h) using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After quantification with a BCA kit (BL521A), the 5–10 μL proteins (25–30 μg) were separated by 10% SDS‒PAGE. Following gel transfer onto a 0.45 μm PVDF membrane (Millipore, IPVH00010), the membrane was incubated in quick-closing solution for 1 h. The antibody was then diluted at a ratio of 1:1000 (or 1:500 or 1:5000) and incubated with the PVDF membrane overnight at 4 °C. The membrane was subsequently washed with TBST, incubated for 2 h with an HRP-conjugated Affini Pure goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) secondary antibody (ZSGB, ZB2305; diluted 1:1000) and then visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system. The WB analyses were performed three independent replicates.

Co-Immunoprecipitation (CO-IP)

We used Pierce™ Immunoprecipitation Kit provided by Thermo Scientific™ (catalog number: 26,149) to detect potential interaction proteins. NCI-H1573 cells were respectively treated with complete culture medium containing 0.1% DMSO and complete culture medium containing 800 ug/mL of 11-oxo-mogroside V for 72 h. The cells were lysed on ice for 30 min using a cell lysate with a protease inhibitor cocktail, following the manufacturer's protocol for subsequent steps. The immunoprecipitated proteins were finally detected by WB. The experiment was repeated three times.

Data statistics

Data were expressed as mean ± SD, processed and visualized with GraphPad Prism 7. Statistical comparisons were performed using Student's t-test (for two groups), one-way ANOVA (for three or more groups), or two-way ANOVA (for two-factor data) as appropriate. Statistical significance was determined at P < 0.05.

Results

11-oxo-mogroside V induces apoptosis in NCI-H1573 cells

To identify suitable methods for 11-oxo-mogroside V intervention, we initially employed CCK-8 assay assess its impact on the viability of NCI-H1573 cells. Based on the experiment results, we screened the working concentration and time of 11-oxo-mogroside V and 800 μg/mL for 72 h (without significant effects on proliferation of cells) was selected as the working concentration for induction of NCI-H1573 cells (Fig. 1A and 1B). To elucidate the biological effects of 11-oxo-mogroside V on LUAD NCI-H1573 cells, apoptosis was assessed using Annexin V-FITC/PI dual staining, while cell cycle distribution was analyzed via PI single staining. Results demonstrated that 11-oxo-mogroside V (800 μg/mL, 72 h) significantly induced apoptosis compared to untreated controls, with apoptotic rates increasing from (3.01 ± 0.41) % in the control group to (12.00 ± 0.62) % (P < 0.0001, n = 3) (Fig. 1C and 1D). In cell cycle analysis, drug treatment markedly elevated the proportion of G0/G1-phase cells (P < 0.0001) while reducing the S-phase population (P < 0.0001), indicating that 11-oxo-mogroside V arrested the cell cycle at the G0/G1 phase and suppressed DNA synthesis (Fig. 1E and 1F). These findings collectively suggest that 11-oxo-mogroside V exerts antitumor activity through dual mechanisms: induction of programmed cell death and inhibition of proliferative progression. Results from the scratch wound healing assay further demonstrated that 11-oxo-mogroside V significantly suppressed the migratory capacity of NCI-H1573 cells, with wound closure rates decreasing from (77.66 ± 1.81) % in untreated controls to (43.98 ± 3.89) % following treatment (800 μg/mL, 72 h; P < 0.0001, n = 3) (Fig. 2A and 2B). To investigate the metabolic effects of 11-oxo-mogroside V on LUAD NCI-H1573 cells, intracellular ATP levels, glucose consumption, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity were quantified using commercial assay kits. As shown in Figs. 2C–E, treatment with 11-oxo-mogroside V (800 μg/mL, 72 h) significantly reduced LDH activity (P < 0.0001), concomitant with decreased intracellular ATP content (from 0.47±0.01nmol/mg protein to 0.38±0.02nmol/mg protein; P < 0.0001) and glucose utilization (from 0.12±0.01μmol/L to 0.06±0.01μmol/L; P < 0.0001). Validation of anti-LUAD activity in A549 cells

To confirm that the anti-proliferative effects of 11-oxo-mogroside V are not restricted to NCI-H1573 cells, we performed CCK-8 and TUNEL assays in A549, a bona fide lung adenocarcinoma cell line. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S1A, treatment with 11-oxo-mogroside V (800 μg/mL, 72 h) significantly reduced A549 cell viability compared to control (P < 0.0001). TUNEL staining revealed a marked increase in apoptotic cells following treatment (Supplementary Fig. S1B-C), with the apoptotic rate being significantly higher in the treatment group than in the control group (P < 0.0001). These results demonstrate that 11-oxo-mogroside V exerts anti-LUAD activity independent of cell line origin.

11-oxo-mogroside V delays the progression of LUAD in nude mice

In the animal experiment of acute toxicity, it was observed that after the intervention of 11-oxo-mogroside V, the activity of the animals was reduced and quiet, and gradually returned to the normal state after 1 hour. No other significant poisoning symptoms were observed (Fig. 3). During the whole observation period, the nude mice in each group did not die, urination and defecation were normal, the hair was shiny, eating and daily activities were normal, and there were no abnormal secretions from the mouth, nose and eyes, and other abnormal pathological conditions. During the dissection, no obvious pathological changes were found in the organs by gross observation. After this preliminary experiment, the mortality of animals in 12.5mg/kg, 25mg/kg, 50mg/kg, 100mg/kg and 200mg/kg dose groups was 0, and the LD50 value of 11-oxo-mogroside V was higher than that of 200 mg/kg. Transcriptomics analysis explores the molecular mechanisms

To systematically elucidate the antiproliferative effects of 11-oxo-mogroside V on both in vitro NCI-H1573 cells and in vivo nude mice, we performed RNA sequencing on NCI-H1573 cells treated with 11-oxo-mogroside V (800 μg/mL, 72 h) or vehicle control. Total RNA was extracted from these cells, not from mouse tumor tissues, to profile transcriptomic changes directly induced by the compound in LUAD cells.

Volcano plot and heatmap of transcriptomic assay indicated the genes in NCI-H1573 cells were differentially expressed between the control and treatment groups. A total of 1998 DEGs were detected, among which 729 genes were up-regulated and 1269 genes were down-regulated after 11-oxo-mogroside V treatment (Fig. 5A). Among the disparity in genes expression, 11-oxo-mogroside V significantly interfered with TGFB2, MYC and other genes related to cell proliferation and migration (Fig. 5B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified significant associations of DEGs with both functional and immune-related processes. Pathways related to cell migration and autophagy, including PI3K-Akt signaling, Focal adhesion and Rap1 signaling pathway, exhibited the highest number of enriched DEGs, while those with the most stringent statistical significance (FDR <0.05, Benjamini-Hochberg correction) were predominantly immune-modulatory pathways, such as AGE-RAGE signaling pathway, Complement and coagulation cascades and ECM-receptor interaction (Fig. 5C). Literature review revealed that the Hippo signaling pathway and TGFβ signaling pathway are closely associated with diseases such as hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and intestinal injury, where they suppress malignant transformation of HCC cells and maintain intestinal morphology to promote regeneration [14,15]. Furthermore, the TGFβ signaling pathway is a canonical downstream effector of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and previous studies have showed that TGFβ/Smad and PI3K/AKT pathways inhibit survival and metastasis of cancer cells, including melanoma and breast cancer[16,17]. During differential gene screening, TGFB2 was identified as a significantly correlated gene between the two groups. Integrating these findings with the upstream-downstream regulatory hierarchy of signaling pathways, we prioritized two core pathways - TGFβ signaling and Myc signaling - along with three key therapeutic targets: TGFβ2, HDAC1, and c-Myc.

To more comprehensively understand the mechanism by which 11-oxo-mogroside V improves LUAD, verify the interactions between the 11-oxo-mogroside V and core targets of LUAD and the accuracy of the above prediction results, molecular docking simulations were applied to 11-oxo-mogroside V and 3 key targets——TGFβ2, HDAC1 and c-Myc. The binding energies were calculated, and the results are visually displayed (Fig. 6). Among them, 11-oxo-mogroside V displayed good binding activity to the active pocket of HDAC1, forming five hydrogen bonds with the Met37, Lys31, Tyr303, Pro29 and Asp99 residue (Fig.6A–6C). Meanwhile, it also showed high affinity for binding to TGFβ2 with the binding energy of −9.3 kcal/mol (Fig.6D–6F). In addition, as shown in Fig. 6H, 11-oxo-mogroside V also had strong binding affinity for c-Myc, possessing not only hydrogen bonds but also π interactions. 11-oxo-mogroside V improves LUAD by interfering with cellular metabolism

Based on transcriptomic sequencing-derived KEGG enrichment and preliminary molecular docking validation, we conducted an in-depth literature review. TGFβ2, one of the three mammalian TGF-β isoforms and the protein product encoded by the TGFB2 gene, plays a pivotal role in TGFβ signaling by modulating the transcriptional repressor TGIF to enhance Smad2/3 expression and facilitate their nuclear translocation [[18], [19], [20]]. This process drives proto-oncogene c-Myc activation, thereby promoting cancer cell glycolysis, proliferation, metastasis, and immune evasion [[21], [22], [23]]. Furthermore, c-Myc expression is epigenetically regulated by HDAC1, and HDAC1 inhibition suppresses c-Myc transcription, reversing its oncogenic effects [24,25]. Previous studies suggest that Bax and Bcl2, pro- and anti-apoptotic, respectively and antagonistic members of the Bcl2 family, may synergize with c-Myc to orchestrate tumor progression in breast and lung cancers through apoptosis-homeostasis dysregulation [[26], [27], [28], [29], [30]].

Therefore, tumor tissues from each group were subjected to Western blotting to quantify protein expression levels of Bax, Bcl2, c-Myc, HDAC1, Smad2/3, TGFβ2, and TGIF. As shown in Fig. 7A–7H, compared with those of the NCI-H1573 group, 11-oxo-mogroside V significantly suppressed the expression of Bcl2, c-Myc, HDAC1, Smad2/3, TGFβ2, and TGIF (P < 0.01), while upregulating Bax (P < 0.01). Consistent results were observed in vitro: Western blot analysis of NCI-H1573 cells treated with 11-oxo-mogroside V (800 μg/mL, 72 h) showed the same directional changes in Bax, Bcl2, c-Myc, HDAC1, and TGFβ2 (Supplementary Fig. S2). These findings demonstrate that 11-oxo-mogroside V suppresses tumor progression in nude mice by dual targeting of HDAC1 and TGFβ2. TGFβ2 inhibition attenuates TGIF-mediated Smad2/3 activation, thereby downregulating c-Myc transcription and subsequent glycolytic flux. Concurrently, 11-oxo-mogroside V induces apoptosis via Bax activation and Bcl2 suppression, effectively eliminating malignant cells. This coordinated regulation of proliferative signaling and metabolic reprogramming underscores its therapeutic potential in LUAD. In addition, Co-IP assays confirmed the interaction between TGIF1 and HDAC1 in 11-oxo-mogroside V-treated NCI-H1573 cells. Discussion

LUAD, as a representative type of non-small cell lung cancer, is one of the most prevalent and deadly malignancies worldwide, especially in China [2]. Current clinical strategies for LUAD, including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapies, are constrained by their non-selective cytotoxicity, which inflicts irreversible damage on normal tissues while targeting malignant cells. In contrast, traditional Chinese herbal compounds, have emerged as promising candidates due to their multi-target mechanisms and holistic therapeutic potential.

Tumor cells rely on hyperactive metabolic pathways, such as aerobic glycolysis (Warburg effect), to sustain uncontrolled proliferation and invasion [31]. Our in vitro studies demonstrated 11-oxo-mogroside V disrupts these critical processes in NCI-H1573 cells. CCK-8 assays revealed time- and dose-dependent inhibition of proliferation (P < 0.0001), while flow cytometry confirmed G0/G1 phase arrest, preventing S-phase entry and subsequent mitotic division. Scratch assays further corroborate impaired migratory capacity. In addition, 11-oxo-mogroside V significantly reduced intracellular ATP, glucose, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels, indicating suppression of glycolytic flux and energy metabolism, thereby triggering apoptosis.

In vivo validation using NCI-H1573 xenograft models demonstrated that 11-oxo-mogroside V administration (100 mg/kg, once a day) markedly reduced tumor volume and bioluminescence signals at days 14 and 28 post-treatment (P < 0.0001). Transcriptomic analysis of NCI-H1573 cells revealed that DEGs were predominantly enriched in immune-related and cell migration-associated pathways, including the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and Hippo signaling pathway. Integrated with literature review and differentially expressed gene heatmap analysis, three core therapeutic targets—TGFβ2, HDAC1, and c-Myc—were identified to be associated with the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway, TGF-β signaling pathway, and Myc signaling pathway, respectively. Molecular docking simulations predicted potential high-affinity binding between 11-oxo-mogroside V and these proteins; however, these in silico findings require experimental validation (e.g., surface plasmon resonance, isothermal titration calorimetry, or rescue experiments with target overexpression or knockdown) to confirm direct physical interactions and functional causality. Western blotting validated 11-oxo-mogroside V-mediated downregulation of Bcl2, Smad2/3, TGFβ2, HDAC1, and TGIF, alongside Bax upregulation at the animal level, and the result of Co-IP assays show that the TGIF1 protein interacts with HDAC1. The antitumor efficacy of 11-oxo-mogroside V in LUAD is mediated through coordinated inhibition of HDAC1 and TGFβ2. By suppressing TGFβ2, this compound disrupts the TGIF-Smad2/3 signaling axis, leading to transcriptional downregulation of c-Myc and impaired glycolytic metabolism. Parallelly, 11-oxo-mogroside V activates mitochondrial apoptosis via Bax upregulation and Bcl2 suppression, effectively eradicating neoplastic cells. These dual mechanisms—targeting both proliferative signaling cascades and metabolic reprogramming—collectively underscore its capacity to impede LUAD progression through multi-pathway intervention. We acknowledge that while our molecular docking and Western blot data are consistent with the hypothesis that 11-oxo-mogroside V modulates these proteins, direct binding and the absence of off-target effects need to be further validated by biochemical and genetic rescue experiments in future studies.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the in vitro working concentration of 11-oxo-mogroside V (800 μg/mL) is relatively high, which is attributable to its poor membrane permeability as a hydrophilic saponin (∼1300 Da); however, pathway‑specific effects and lack of overt toxicity in normal cells argue against non‑specific cytotoxicity. Second, our metabolic characterization relied on functional readouts (ATP, glucose, LDH); future studies using Seahorse and glycolytic enzyme assays are needed. Third, acute toxicity was assessed only by gross organ inspection without histopathological confirmation (H&E staining). Fourth, Western blot data were obtained from tumor tissues while Co-IP experiments were performed in vitro; future studies should include in vitro Western blot validation to establish a fully continuous mechanistic chain.

Collectively, these findings illustrate that 11-oxo-mogroside V exerts antitumor effects by targeting of metabolic reprogramming (glycolysis suppression) and proliferative signaling (G0/G1 arrest, Myc inhibition). This pharmacological action disrupts LUAD progression through coordinated regulation of apoptosis, cell cycle, and epigenetic pathways, positioning 11-oxo-mogroside V as a viable therapeutic agent for further preclinical development.

Conclusion

This study investigated the therapeutic potential of 11-oxo-mogroside V against LUAD through in vitro and in vivo models. In NCI-H1573 cells, 11-oxo-mogroside V induced G0/G1 cell cycle arrest and suppressed migration, concomitant with significantly reduced intracellular levels of ATP, glucose, and LDH, indicating metabolic inhibition and apoptosis promotion. In xenograft-bearing nude mice, 11-oxo-mogroside V administration markedly attenuated tumor growth and proliferation. Mechanistically, integrated transcriptomic profiling and molecular docking suggested that 11-oxo-mogroside V may interact with TGFβ2, HDAC1, and c-Myc, thereby modulating downstream pathways including mitochondrial apoptosis, TGF-β signaling, and Myc-driven proliferative regulation. Western blotting and Co-IP validated the downregulation of Bcl2, Smad2/3, TGFβ2, HDAC1, and c-Myc, alongside enhanced Bax expression, and there is an interaction between HDAC1 and TGIF1.

In conclusion, our current results demonstrate that 11-oxo-mogroside V exerts antitumor effects against LUAD by dual targeting of proliferative signaling and metabolic reprogramming. Nevertheless, additional studies (e.g., comparison with standard clinical agents, long-term toxicity, and pharmacokinetics) are required to fully assess its therapeutic potential.
