Tighter Value-Function Approximations for POMDPs
Merlijn Krale, Wietze Koops, Sebastian Junges, Thiago D. Simão, Nils Jansen

Abstract
. Solving partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs) typically requires reasoning about the values of exponentially many state beliefs. Towards practical performance, state-of-the-art solvers use value bounds to guide this reasoning. However, sound upper value bounds are often computationally expensive to compute, and there is a tradeoff between the tightness of such bounds and their computational cost. This paper introduces new and provably tighter upper value bounds than the commonly used fast informed bound . Our empirical evaluation shows that, despite their additional computational overhead, the new upper bounds accelerate state-of-the-art POMDP solvers on a wide range of benchmarks.

1.
Introduction

Partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs) are a versatile modeling framework for stochastic environments where the decision maker (the agent) cannot fully observe the current state of its environment
(
Kaelbling
et al., 1998
)
.
Finding optimal policies for POMDPs is generally undecidable
(
Madani
et al., 1999
)
.
Yet, in recent years, methods like POMCP
(
Silver and Veness, 2010
)
, DESPOT
(
Ye
et al., 2017
)
, and AdaOPS
(
Wu
et al., 2021
)
have been able to find policies for increasingly large POMDPs.

Although such methods often provide a (statistical)
lower bound
on the value of the policy, they are typically unable to find an
upper bound
on the optimal value.
Such further certification of the quality of a policy may be essential for safety-critical problems.
For example, planning medical treatments
(
Hauskrecht and
Fraser, 2000
)
, scheduling infrastructure maintenance
(
Morato et al., 2022a
;
Morato et al., 2022b
)
or computing safe flight paths
(
Temizer et al., 2010
)
require us not only to know how well our policy will perform, but also that we cannot (reasonably) do any better.

So-called
\epsilon
-optimal solvers such as
SARSOP

(
Kurniawati
et al., 2008
)
and HSVI
(
Smith and Simmons, 2005
)
compute both a policy and an upper bound.
These algorithms make use of heuristic search to find good policies quickly.
However, they often struggle to find upper bounds that are reasonably tight, since this requires reasoning over
all
possible policies.

Both HSVI and
SARSOP
use the
fast informed bound
, or
FIB

(
Hauskrecht, 2000
)
, to initialize their upper bound computations.
Intuitively,
FIB
computes values in a simplified POMDP, where the agent fully observes the state of the environment with a delay of one time step.
However, these bounds are often loose in practice, while tighter upper bounds could improve the performance of
\epsilon
-optimal solvers.

We contribute three different methods to obtain bounds that exhibit varying levels of tightness and computational overhead.

We first introduce the
tighter informed bound
(
TIB
) as an alternative for
FIB
.
Intuitively,
TIB
uses a delay of two time steps rather than one time step.
TIB
can be computed using value iteration, as employed by
(
Bellman, 1957
;
Puterman, 1994
)
, on all
one-step beliefs
, that is, beliefs the agent can have one time step after knowing the state.
These precomputations are more expensive than for
FIB
, but allow to compute a bound for any belief at the same computational cost as
FIB
.
However, we show that increasing the delay further would significantly increase these computational costs.

Closer inspection of
TIB
shows that it expresses posterior beliefs of the agent as a convex combination of one-step beliefs.
However, choosing
different
combinations may further tighten the bound.
The
optimized tighter informed bound
(
OTIB
) uses the convex combinations that yield the tightest possible bound.
However, finding this convex combination requires solving a linear program for each posterior belief in each iteration step, which is usually too expensive.
Instead, the
entropy-based tighter informed bound
(
ETIB
) heuristically chooses a single combination for each posterior belief by maximizing the weighted entropy of the chosen one-step beliefs.
This combination is reused for each iteration, thus greatly reducing computational cost.

Empirically,
TIB
and
ETIB
provide better bounds than
FIB
on a large range of benchmarks with reasonable computational cost.
To test the practical relevance of our bounds, we adapt the offline state-of-the-art solver
SARSOP

(
Kurniawati
et al., 2008
)
to use our upper bounds as initialization.
With this alteration,
SARSOP
finds tighter optimality bounds more quickly on a wide range of benchmarks, which means the additional computational overhead of our bounds is compensated by a speedup in convergence.
Moreover, this positive effect grows as the discount factor increases.

Contributions.

To summarize, our
main contributions
are introducing three novel bounds for POMDPs, namely
TIB
,
ETIB
, and
OTIB
.
These bounds both theoretically and empirically improve prior methods.
Moreover, integrating these novel bounds with the state-of-the-art
\epsilon
-optimal solver SARSOP
(
Kurniawati
et al., 2008
)
leads to significant speedups and smaller optimality gaps.

2.
Problem Setting

In this section, we provide a formal definition of our problem setting in order to formalize our problem statement.
We first introduce some basic notation:
\Delta(X)
denotes the set of probability distributions over a finite set
X
.
Given a function
F\colon X\rightarrow\Delta(Y)
and elements
x\in X
,
y\in Y
,
F(\cdot~|~x)
denotes the conditional probability distribution over
Y
given
x
,
F(y~|~x)
the probability of element
y
given
x
, and
y\sim F(x)
an element
y
randomly sampled from
F(x)
.

POMDPs

An (infinite-horizon, discounted)
partially observable Markov decision process
(POMDP)
(
Kaelbling
et al., 1998
;
Spaan, 2012
)
is defined as a tuple
\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},T,\mathcal{O},O,R,\gamma\rangle
, with
\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},T,R,\gamma\rangle
an MDP
(
Puterman, 1994
)
with a finite set of
states

\mathcal{S}
, a finite set of
actions

\mathcal{A}
, a
transition function

T\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{S})
, a
reward function

R\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and a
discount factor

\gamma\in(0,1)
.
Additionally,
\mathcal{O}
is a finite set of
observations
and
O\colon\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{O})
is the
observation function
.

A POMDP models the interaction between a stochastic environment and an agent. Let
b_{0}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
be the fixed
initial distribution
(aka
initial belief
).
The
initial state

s_{0}
of the environment is sampled from
b_{0}
.
At each time step
t
, the agent picks an action
a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
.
As a result, the environment transitions to a new state
s_{t+1}\sim T(\cdot~|~s_{t},a_{t})
and returns a reward
r_{t}=R(s_{t},a_{t})
.
However, unlike for MDPs, the agent does not observe the state
s_{t+1}
, but instead receives an observation
o_{t+1}\sim O(\cdot~|~a_{t},s_{t+1})
.
In general, agents make decisions based on their history
(b_{0},a_{0},o_{1},\dots,a_{t},o_{t+1})
.
As shown by
Åström (1965)
, this history can be summarized by a
belief

b_{t}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
.
Therefore, we can assume that the agent chooses actions according to a (deterministic) belief-based policy
\pi\colon\Delta(\mathcal{S})\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
.
Given a policy
\pi
and an initial belief
b
, we define the
value
as the expected discounted return over an infinite horizon:

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r_{t}|s_{0}\sim b\right].

The agent aims to maximize the value for the initial belief
b_{0}
.

Probabilities

We now introduce additional notation that will be used throughout this paper.
For any belief
b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
, let
R(b,a)=\sum_{s\in S}b(s)R(s,a)
be the expected reward of action
a
in belief
b
and let
T(s^{\prime}~|~b,a)=\sum_{s\in S}b(s)T(s^{\prime}~|~s,a)
be the probability of transitioning to state
s^{\prime}
when taking action
a
in belief
b
.

We define a shorthand for four probabilities.
Given a state
s
and an action
a
, the probability of transitioning to state
s^{\prime}
and observing
o
is denoted by

\Pr(s^{\prime},o~|~s,a)=O(o~|~a,s^{\prime})T(s^{\prime}~|~s,a),

while the probability of observing
o
is denoted by

\Pr(o~|~s,a)=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\Pr(s^{\prime},o~|~s,a).

Given a belief
b
and action
a
, we denote the probability of transitioning to
s^{\prime}
and observing
o
by

\Pr(s^{\prime},o~|~b,a)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}[b(s)\Pr(s^{\prime},o~|~s,a)],

while the probability of observing
o
is given by

\Pr(o~|~b,a)=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\Pr(s^{\prime},o~|~b,a).

We also define notation for specific beliefs.
For any

If

Q

Lastly, to reason about the decision-making process of an agent, we define the

Let

The

With our problem setting defined, we formalize our problem statement as follows:

Problem Statement 0.

​Find tractable methods of computing tight overapproximations (or

\epsilon
-optimal solvers.

3.1.
Fast Informed Bound (
FIB
)

A common method of over-approximating the value of a POMDP is to (partially) ignore the effect of partial observability.
The most straightforward example of this is the
QMDP bound

(
Littman
et al., 1995
)
, which intuitively corresponds to the assumption that agents can fully observe their state in the future.
We can define this as follows:

Definition 3.2.

Q_{\mathrm{MDP}}
is the fixed point of the operator
H_{\text{QMDP}}
:

(4)

\displaystyle H_{\text{MDP}}Q(b,a)=R+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}[\Pr(s^{\prime}|b,a)\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}Q(b_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})].

To further tighten this bound,
the
fast informed bound
(
FIB
)
(
Hauskrecht, 2000
)
assumes an agent fully observes the current and future states with a delay of 1 time step
.
More precisely, we define
Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}
, the
Q
-value function for this bound, as follows:

Definition 3.3.

Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}
is
the
fixed point of the operator
H_{\text{FIB}}
:

(5)

\displaystyle H_{\text{FIB}}Q

\displaystyle(b,a)=R(b,a)

\displaystyle+\gamma\cdot\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}~\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}~\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\Pr(o,s^{\prime}~|~b,a)Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right].

App.

B
provides a proof that this fixed point exists and is unique (based on the original proof from
Hauskrecht (2000)
).

In
Eq.

5
, the next action
a^{\prime}
is picked independently of the next state
s^{\prime}
but must depend only on the current belief
b
and received observation
o
.
However, for all future time steps, we use
Q
-values computed for the unit belief
\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}}
, i.e., as if
s^{\prime}
is revealed.
Thus, the formula matches the intuitive description of full observability delayed by one time step.
In contrast to
Eq.

3
,
H_{\text{FIB}}
depends only on the
Q
-values of the (finite) set of beliefs
\mathfrak{b}_{s}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{S}}
.
Thus, the value of
Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}
for
any
belief
b
can be computed efficiently by (approximately) computing the fixed point for beliefs in
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{S}}
.
Both the QMDP bound and
FIB
are commonly used in POMDP literature due to their tractability but tend to be loose.

Running example.

Recall the
Guessing
POMDP.
Under the QMDP assumption, taking action
w
would fully reveal the agent’s state.
In that case, an agent can always guess correctly after taking action
w
, which yields an expected value of
\gamma
.
Similarly, under the
FIB
assumption, taking action
w
would fully reveal the agent’s previous state.
The probability of still being in this state after this action is
0.8
.
Thus, taking action
w
and guessing the revealed initial state yields an expected return of
0.8\gamma
.
Both are strict overapproximations of the optimal value
0.5
of the POMDP, and both incorrectly give higher
Q
-values for action
w
than for
x
or
y
.

3.2.
Point Set Bounds

To compute tighter approximations than
FIB
, we consider a general value bound that uses
point sets

(
Pineau
et al., 2003
)
: sets of beliefs with known upper bounds.
To make the connection with our own method more clear, we define them using our own (non-standard) notation.
We start by defining a
weight function
as follows:

Definition 3.4.

Let
b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
be a belief and let
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\Delta(\mathcal{S})
be a point set.
A
weight function

w\colon\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is any function satisfying
b(s)=\sum_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}}w(b^{\prime})b^{\prime}(s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
.

\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{B},b}
denotes the set of all possible weight functions for belief
b
given point set
\mathcal{B}
.

2
2

2

\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{B},b}
may be empty if
b
does not lie in the convex hull of
\mathcal{B}
.

We can use weight functions to compute upper bounds as follows:

Theorem 3.5 (Point set bound).

Given a belief
b
, a
point set

\mathcal{B}
, and a function

Q\colon\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which over-approximates the
Q_{\mathrm{POMDP}}
-values of all beliefs-action pairs
(b^{\prime},a)\in\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{A}
.
Then, any weight function
w\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{B},b}
gives an upper bound on the value of
b
:

(6)

Q_{\mathrm{POMDP}}(b,a)\leq\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a\right):=\sum_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}}w(b^{\prime})Q(b^{\prime},a).

This theorem follows directly from the convexity of the value function for POMDPs
(
Sondik, 1978
)
.
To understand how
Theorem

3.5
is used implicitly by
FIB
,
consider using point set
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{S}}
and the weight functions
w_{b,a,o}(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}})=\frac{\Pr(o,s^{\prime}~|~b,a)}{\Pr(o~|~b,a)}
.
In that case, we find:

(7)

\!H_{\text{FIB}}Q(b,a)=R(b,a)\!+\!\gamma\!\!\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\!\left[\Pr(o~|~b,a)\widetilde{Q}\left(w_{b,a,o},a^{\prime}\right)\right],\!

with
\widetilde{Q}
the weighted sum over values of
Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}
as defined in
Eq.

6
.

Given a point set
\mathcal{B}
and belief
b
, the
tightest
upper bound we can compute using
Theorem

3.5
is found using the linear program (LP):
\min_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{B},b}}\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a\right).

However, POMDP solvers often need to compute upper bounds for many beliefs using large point sets, in which case this method is computationally expensive.
Thus, instead of solving these LPs exactly, solvers may approximate their outcome instead.
One such approximation method is the
sawtooth bound

(
Hauskrecht, 2000
)
.
This bound is based on the observation that, for point sets of the form
\{b^{\prime}\}\cup\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{S}}
, an upper bound can be computed in only
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{S}|)
time.
Thus, if
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
, we can compute such a bound for all beliefs
b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
and take their minimum.
This takes only
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{B}|)
time, but still yields tight bounds in practice.
We refer to
Kochenderfer
et al. (2022)
for a detailed implementation of the sawtooth bound.

3.3.
Using Bounds in Point-Based Solvers

Point set bounds are an important component of
point-based solvers
, a type of algorithm that uses a finite set of beliefs to compute both upper- and lower bounds on the value of a POMDP.
Early methods use predefined sets of beliefs to cover the entire belief space evenly
(
Lovejoy, 1991
;
Zhou and Hansen, 2001
;
Bonet, 2002
)
, but these methods typically scale poorly to large POMDPs.
Instead, state-of-the-art algorithms such as HSVI
(
Smith and Simmons, 2004
;
Smith and Simmons, 2005
)
and
SARSOP

(
Kurniawati
et al., 2008
)
use
heuristic search
to find beliefs that closely resemble those encountered by an optimal policy, which is sufficient for finding
\epsilon
-optimal solutions
(
Kurniawati
et al., 2008
)
.

SARSOP

(
Kurniawati
et al., 2008
)
is a state-of-the-art point-based solver that uses a variant of value iteration
(
Smallwood and
Sondik, 1973
)
to compute lower bounds and the sawtooth bound for upper bounds.
The latter requires precomputing value bounds for the set of unit beliefs
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{S}}
, which is traditionally done using
FIB
. The next section proposes methods of computing tighter bounds for this set in tractable time.

Eq.

5
that for
FIB
, we can compute
Q
-values for any belief using only
Q
-values of unit beliefs
\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}}
.
This intuitively corresponds to state
s^{\prime}
being observed with a delay of 1 time step.
In contrast, in
Eq.

8
, we use
Q
-values of one-step beliefs
\mathfrak{b}_{s,a,o}
, which corresponds to state
s
being revealed with a delay of
2
time steps and thus aligns with the intuitive definition of
TIB
.

Next, we highlight some important properties of
Q_{\mathrm{TIB}}
:

Theorem 4.2.

Q_{\mathrm{TIB}}
has the following properties:

(1)

Soundness:

\forall b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}),a\in\mathcal{A}\colon Q_{\mathrm{TIB}}(b,a)\geq Q_{\mathrm{POMDP}}(b,a)
;

(2)

Tightness:

\forall b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}),a\in\mathcal{A}\colon Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}(b,a)\geq

Q_{\mathrm{TIB}}
(b,a).

Full proofs of
Theorem

4.2
are provided in
Sect.

B.2
.
Intuitively, we recall that
TIB
and
FIB
correspond with the agent observing the state with a delay, which gives them additional information.
Since this information can only help the agent,
Q_{\mathrm{TIB}}
is a sound upper bound (1).
Moreover, since our model is Markovian and the delay of
FIB
is lower than that of
TIB
, the additional information of
FIB
is at least as useful.
Thus, for any belief-action pair,
Q_{\mathrm{TIB}}
is never higher than
Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}
(2).

Running example.

To provide some intuition on the tightness of
TIB
, we recall the
Guessing
POMDP.
Under the
TIB
assumption, taking action
w
twice lets an agent observe its initial state.
The probability of still being in this state after these actions is
0.8^{2}+0.2^{2}=0.68
.
Thus, taking action
w
twice and guessing the revealed initial state yields an expected return of
0.68\gamma^{2}
.
This is a strict overapproximation of the optimal value of the POMDP, which is
0.5
, but significantly tighter than the bound of
0.8\gamma
found by
FIB
.

Complexity analysis.

Q_{\mathrm{TIB}}
can be computed up to an arbitrary precision using value iteration, as shown in
Algorithm

1
.

When performing
h
iterations, the computed bound for any belief-action pair is at most
\max_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{1-\gamma}R(s,a)
away from the fixed point.
For an explanation of the precision parameter, see
App.

The computational costs for a single Bellman operation are equal for
FIB
and

Precomputations for

are a factor
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{|\mathcal{B}_{1}|}}{{|\mathcal{S}|}})\in\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{O}|)
more expensive than for

The empirical evaluation shows that this is often manageable in practice (

Further increasing delays.

One method of computing even tighter bounds is to consider even longer observation delays.
However, increasing the observation delay also means we need to consider a larger set of beliefs.
Thus, as shown in
App.

C
, a delay of 3 time steps already yields a computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{S}|^{2}|\mathcal{A}|^{5}|\mathcal{O}|^{4}h)
, which is a factor
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{A}|^{2}|\mathcal{O}|^{2})
larger than for
TIB
.
It may be possible to efficiently compute such bounds regardless, but we will not consider this line of research here.
Instead, we focus on methods that can tighten our bounds without further increasing the delay.

4.2.
Optimized Tighter Informed Bound (OTIB)

Like for
FIB
, we notice
TIB
can be rewritten using
Theorem

3.5
with point set
\mathcal{B}_{1}
, as follows:

H_{\text{TIB}}Q(b,a)=R(b,a)+\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\Pr(o~|~b,a)\widetilde{Q}\left(w_{b,a,o},a\right)\right],

where we use the following weight function:

w_{b,a,o}(\mathfrak{b}_{s,a,o})=\frac{b(s)\Pr(o~|~s,a)}{\Pr(o~|~b,a)}.

In contrast to
FIB
, however, these weights are not necessarily unique, and
Theorem

3.5
tells us
any
weight that represents our belief gives a viable upper bound.
Thus, we define the

optimized tighter informed bound
(
OTIB
), which assumes the value for future beliefs is equal to the minimal point set bound (
Theorem

3.5
) using point set
\mathcal{B}_{1}
.

We define the corresponding
Q
-value function as follows:

Definition 4.3.

Write
\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}=\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathfrak{b}_{b,a,o}}
.
Then,
Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}
is
the
fixed point of the operator
H_{\text{OTIB}}
:

(9)

\displaystyle H_{\text{OTIB}}

\displaystyle Q(b,a)=R(b,a)

\displaystyle+\gamma\!\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}[\Pr(o~|~b,a)\!\!\min_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)].

We note that
w_{b,a,o}\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}
, which means the minimization in
Eq.

9
always has a feasible solution.
A full proof of the existence and uniqueness of
Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}
is provided in
App.

B
.
We highlight a number of properties of
Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}
:

Theorem 4.4.

Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}
has the following properties:

(1)

Soundness:

\forall b\hskip-0.75pt\in\hskip-0.75pt\Delta(\mathcal{S}),a\hskip-0.75pt\in\hskip-0.75pt\mathcal{A}\colon Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}(b,a)\hskip-0.75pt\geq\hskip-0.75ptQ_{\mathrm{POMDP}}(b,a)
;

(2)

Tightness:

\forall b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}),a\in\mathcal{A}\colon Q_{\mathrm{TIB}}(b,a)\geq Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}(b,a)
.

Proofs are provided in
App.

B
.
Soundness follows as for
Theorem

4.2
, tightness from the observation that
w_{b,a,o}\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}
.

Running example.

We consider the
Guessing
POMDP (
Example

3.1
).
Under the
OTIB
assumption, the belief after taking action
w
can be expressed using any weight function in
\mathcal{W}_{b_{0},w,\bot}
.
In particular, since
b_{0}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
, one valid choice uses weight 1 for
b_{0}
and 0 for all others.
In that case, the action is suboptimal (with value
0.5\gamma
), and the
OTIB
bound corresponds with the real value
0.5
.

4.3.
Entropy-based Tighter Informed Bound (
ETIB
): A Heuristic Approach

To reduce the complexity of the precomputations of
OTIB
, we consider using a single weight for each belief that we reuse for all iterations.
More precisely, we approximate the worst-case weights by those that maximize the
weighted entropy
.
This gives higher weights to more uncertain beliefs, which should intuitively give a tighter bound.
To formalize this, we first define the
maximal entropy weight function
for a belief
\mathfrak{b}_{b,a,o}
as follows:

(10)

\hat{w}_{b,a,o}\in\argmax\limits_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\sum_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{S}}}H(b^{\prime})w(b^{\prime}).

This equation always has a feasible solution, since
w_{b,a,o}\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}
.
Then,
the entropy-based tighter informed bound (
ETIB
) assumes the value for future beliefs is equal to the point set bound (
Theorem

3.5
) using point set
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and maximal entropy weight functions (
Eq.

10
)
.
We define the corresponding
Q
-value function as follows:

Definition 4.5.

Q_{\mathrm{ETIB}}
is
the
fixed point of the operator
H_{\text{ETIB}}
:

(11)

\displaystyle H_{\text{ETIB}}

\displaystyle Q(b,a)=R(b,a)

\displaystyle+\gamma\!\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}[\Pr(o~|~b,a)\widetilde{Q}\left(\hat{w}_{b,a,o},a^{\prime}\right)].

As for the other bounds, we provide a proof that this unique fixed point exists in
App.

B
.
We highlight the following properties of
Q_{\mathrm{ETIB}}
:

Theorem 4.6.

Q_{\mathrm{ETIB}}
has the following properties:

(1)

Soundness:

\forall b\hskip-0.75pt\in\hskip-0.75pt\Delta(\mathcal{S}),a\hskip-0.75pt\in\hskip-0.75pt\mathcal{A}\colon Q_{\mathrm{ETIB}}(b,a)\hskip-0.75pt\geq\hskip-0.75ptQ_{\mathrm{POMDP}}(b,a)
;

(2)

Tightness:

\forall b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}),a\in\mathcal{A}\colon Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}(b,a)\geq Q_{\mathrm{ETIB}}(b,a)
.

The proof for
Theorem

4.6
is provided in
App.

B
and follows the same intuition as those of
Theorem

4.2
.
In contrast to
OTIB
, we note that
ETIB
does not necessarily provide tighter bounds than
TIB

, since there is no guarantee
\hat{w}_{b,a,o}
yields tighter bounds than
w_{b,a,o}
.
In practice, however, we find
ETIB
is at least as tight as
TIB
, and sometimes (significantly) tighter.

Running example.

Consider the
Guessing
environment.
Under the
ETIB
assumption, the value of taking action
w
is approximated using the maximal entropy weight function
\hat{w}_{b_{0},w,\bot}
.
Since
b_{0}
is the belief with the largest entropy in
\mathcal{B}_{1}
, this weight function is the weight function defined by
w(b)=1
if
b=b_{0}
, and
w(b)=0
otherwise.
Thus,
ETIB
and
OTIB
find the same optimal bound.

Complexity analysis.

As for our other proposed bounds,
Q_{\mathrm{ETIB}}
can be approximated using
Algorithm

1
, with corresponding complexities shown in
Table

1
.
The computational complexity of a single Bellman operation is a factor
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{A}|)
smaller for
ETIB
as compared to
OTIB
, since the same weight can be used for each next action
a^{\prime}
.
Moreover, since these weights can be reused at each iteration, the complexity for precomputations is significantly lower as well.
In practice, the number of beliefs
b^{\prime}
with
w_{b,a,o}(b^{\prime})>0
is often much smaller than
|\mathcal{B}_{1}|
, in which case the iterations take significantly less time than the complexity bound suggests.

Bounds tightness.

Due to the computational cost, we only test on smaller environments, but we expect this behavior to translate to larger environments as well.

SARSOP
profits more from tighter initial bounds for high discount factors.

As shown in
Fig.

3
, the effect of different initialization is minimal for goal-oriented environments, such as
RockSample
(5).
However, for non-goal-oriented problems (such as
Tiger
and
K-out-of-N
), we find that the absolute speedup of using
TIB
and
ETIB
increases with the discount factor.

6.
Related Work

Besides QMDP
(
Littman
et al., 1995
)
, FIB
(
Hauskrecht, 2000
)
and point-based methods
(
Lovejoy, 1991
;
Zhou and Hansen, 2001
;
Bonet, 2002
;
Pineau
et al., 2003
;
Smith and Simmons, 2004
;
Smith and Simmons, 2005
)
which we introduced in
Sect.

2
, we mention a number of other methods used for computing upper bounds.
Firstly, a number of works consider simplifying the set of reachable beliefs by discretizing the belief space in a similar style as point-based solvers
(
Bork
et al., 2022
;
Grover and
Dimitrakakis, 2021
;
Wray and
Zilberstein, 2017
;
Norman
et al., 2017
;
Bork
et al., 2020
)
.
Next,
Yoon
et al. (2008)
introduce ‘hindsight optimization’, which uses deterministic planning in a number of sampled ‘situations’ to approximate the value of a (PO)MDP.

Haugh and
Lacedelli (2020)
use ‘information relaxation’ in a similar way.

Barenboim and
Indelman (2023)
consider only a subset of possible outcomes of the transition- and observation function to compute upper bounds in online solvers.
However, all these methods are typically less tight (though computationally cheaper) than our proposed bounds.
Lastly, some bounds are based on the properties of a particular type of POMDP.
For example,
Sinuany-Stern et al. (1997)
consider POMDPs that model maintenance, while
Krale
et al. (2023)
consider POMDPs where agents have explicit measuring actions.

Our empirical analysis focuses on
SARSOP

(
Kurniawati
et al., 2008
)
, but we mention a few related state-of-the-art POMDP solvers.
Firstly, POMCP
(
Silver and Veness, 2010
)
, and AdaOPS
(
Wu
et al., 2021
)
are both variants of Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) adapted for POMDPs.
DESPOT
(
Ye
et al., 2017
)
is also based on tree search but uses a method based on hindsight optimization to increase tractability.
Lastly, many methods make use of (deep) reinforcement learning to find approximate solutions to POMDPs
(
Hausknecht and
Stone, 2015
;
Lee
et al., 2020
;
Han
et al., 2020
)
.
However, all these methods focus on large (continuous) state-, action- and observation spaces where our bounds are computationally intractable.

Deterministic Delay MDPs
(DDMDPs)
(
Altman and Nain, 1992
;
Katsikopoulos
and Engelbrecht, 2003
;
Walsh
et al., 2009
)
are MDPs where the agent can fully observe its state with some constant delay, which is conceptually similar to FIB and TIB.
Finding exact solutions to DDMDPs is NP-hard
(
Walsh
et al., 2009
)
, but efficient approximate solvers exist
(
Agarwal and
Aggarwal, 2021
;
Derman
et al., 2021
)
.
However, FIB and TIB take into account (partial) observations occurring before the state is fully revealed, while such observations do not exist in DDMDPs.
This means that in POMDPs with no observations, FIB and TIB correspond to the solutions of DDMDPs with delays 1 and 2, respectively.
However, solutions for DDMDPs are not sound upper bounds for POMDPs in general, so we do not compare our method with DDMDP solvers.

Lastly, we mention a number of other works related to
\epsilon
-optimal POMDP solving.

Walraven and
Spaan (2017)
and
Hansen and Bowman (2020)
propose methods to speed up the incremental pruning of
\alpha
-vectors, which constitutes a considerable amount of the computation time of
SARSOP
.
Relatedly,
Dujardin
et al. (2017)
propose a method that uses less
\alpha
-vectors instead.

Wang
et al. (2006)
proposes to use quadratic functions instead of piecewise-linear functions to represent the upper bound.

7.
Conclusion

To improve the performance of
\epsilon
-optimal solvers, we introduced three novel bounds for POMDPs (
TIB
,
OTIB
, and
ETIB
).
We prove these bounds are tighter than the commonly used
FIB
, and show they can be computed using value iteration.
Empirically, both
TIB
and
ETIB
are computationally tractable on a large range of benchmarks.
Moreover, using these bounds to initialize state-of-the-art solver
SARSOP
improves its performance.

Future work may focus on increasing the tractability of our bounds.
For example, instead of computing bounds for all beliefs
b\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
, it may be quicker to use
FIB
for those that have a low probability of being reached.
Alternatively, more research could be done on different heuristic choices for weights, particularly choices that do not require solving LPs.
We consider and test one such choice in
App.

A
with limited success, but using different (combinations of) heuristic(s) could potentially get tight bounds at lower computational costs than
ETIB
.
Lastly, future work could consider how our bounds can be applied to other settings, such as finite- or indefinite horizon problems.

Technical Details Experiments

In this section, we provide additional details about our experimental evaluation that did not fit in the main paper.
All code can be found in the supplementary materials and will be made publicly available after publication.
All experiments are performed on a 3.7GHz Intel Core i9 CPU running Ubuntu 22.04.1 LTS.

A.1.
Implementation details

In this section, we give a high-level overview of how we implemented the precomputation of our bounds.

A.1.1.
FIB
and QMDP

We initially used the
FIB.jl
framework for computing
FIB
, but found that it did not perform well on larger environments.
Thus, we used a custom implementation to precompute
FIB
using value iteration.
To speed up convergence, we additionally wrote a custom implementation for QMDP (using value iteration) that we use as an initialization for
FIB
.
To guarantee we obtain sound upper bounds, we initialize QMDP using
\max\limits_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}R(s,a)\frac{1}{1-\gamma}
for all state-action pairs.

A.1.2.
TIB
,
ETIB
and
OTIB

The precomputation of
TIB
,
ETIB
, and
OTIB
essentially follows
Algorithm

1
.
To improve computational speeds, we cache transition probabilities and reachable beliefs for all beliefs in
\mathcal{B}_{1}
.
For solving LPs, we use the
JuMP.jl
framework
(
Lubin et al., 2023
)
and the Clp optimizer
(
Forrest et al., 2024
)
, which we found to perform best in practice.
We initialize our values using
FIB
, which guarantees soundness.

A.1.3.
Implementation
SARSOP

Our experiments use the native Julia
SARSOP
solver (
https://github.com/JuliaPOMDP/NativeSARSOP.jl
), which we alter in two ways:

Initialisation with different bounds:

Firstly, to be able to use our bounds, we altered
SARSOP
such that it can use any solver to compute an initial upper bound.
More precisely, the Julia implementation of
SARSOP
uses a vector
cornervalues
to represent the values of beliefs
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{S}}
, which are initialized using
FIB
by default.
We alter the code so that it can be initialized using
any
solver.
We additionally add two vectors
B_heuristic
and
V_heuristic
, which get treated as part of the point set when computing upper bounds.
For our experiments, however, we initialize these with empty vectors, meaning they have no impact.

Relative precision:

Secondly, by default,
SARSOP
uses an
absolute
target precision
\epsilon
as a stopping condition and a planning precision
\epsilon_{p}
to determine the sampling depth.
To allow for better comparison between benchmarks, we alter
SARSOP
such that the precision is
relative
, i.e. the algorithm terminates when
\frac{\bar{V}(b_{0})-\underline{V}(b_{0})}{\underline{V}(b_{0})}\leq\epsilon
.
Prior works have found that picking
\epsilon_{p}>\epsilon
often gives better performance, but then there is no guarantee that an
\epsilon
-optimal policy can ever be found.
As a compromise between practical and theoretical performance, we let
\epsilon_{p}
linearly decrease with the number of iterations, as
\epsilon_{p}=\max\{\epsilon,\frac{i+1}{N+1}\}
, with
i
the iteration number and
N=1000
a hyperparameter.
In practice, we found that this yields good performance on both large and small benchmarks.

A.2.
Environment Descriptions

Next, we give a short overview of our novel benchmarks environments.
Both are implemented using the
POMDPs.jl
framework and can thus be used by others.

Grid

We consider a
6\times 6
Grid POMDP.
This POMDP has 36 states, each representing that the agent is in a particular cell in the grid.
The possible actions are staying in-place and moving in one of the four directions: up, down, left, right.
Staying in place is always successful.
However, moving is only successful with probability 0.6, with there also being a probability of 0.1 for moving in each of the other three directions or staying in-place.
If moving in a particular direction would cause the agent to go outside the grid, the probability for that direction is instead added to the probability for staying in-place.
The goal is to navigate to the top right corner.
Accordingly, the reward is 1 when the
new
state is the top right corner, and 0 otherwise (using the transition function, this can be translated to expected rewards given the old state).
The initial belief assigns probability 1 to the bottom left corner.
There are six observations, one for each column.
Regardless of the action taken, the agent is always informed in which column it is, but not in which row it is.

K-out-of-N

We consider a POMDP that consists of
N
components, which can all be in one of
s_{\text{max}}=4
degradation states, where degradation state
s_{\text{max}}
represents a broken component.
For each component and at each time step, the agent can choose either to do nothing, inspect the component, or repair it.
When repairing, the component returns to a non-degraded state.
Otherwise, each component has a chance to degrade (i.e., its degradation state increases by 1), with a probability that depends on the number of other components that are currently broken.
More precisely, with probability
0.2
if no components are broken, probability
0.5
if one component is broken, and
0.9
otherwise.
The agent cannot observe the degradation state of each component unless they take an inspection action.
However, this yields a negative reward of
0.05
.
Moreover, the agent gains a reward of
-0.5
for every broken component and a negative reward of
0.25
when repairing.

K-out-of-N
(x) denotes a K-out-of-N problem with x components.
We note that using a larger number of components increases both the state space as well as the action- and observation spaces.

In response to one of our reviewers, we implement and test an additional heuristic which we refer to as
Closeness-based Tighter Informed Bound
(CTIB).
For CTIB, for any belief
b

b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}

(12)

b_{\text{closest}}(b)=\argmax_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{b(s)}{b^{\prime}(s)}.

b_{\text{closest}}

(13)

\hat{w}^{\prime}_{b,a,o}(b^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{b(s)}{b^{\prime}(s)}&\text{ if }b^{\prime}=b_{c}(b)\\
\sum_{s}\max\big(0,b(s)-b_{c}(s)\big)&\text{ if }b\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{S}}\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

This weight function performs well if the belief with the highest minimum ratio also has high state uncertainty, which is often the case.
Moreover, since finding
b_{\text{closest}}
does not require solving an LP, it can be significantly quicker than ETIB for some benchmarks.

and
(Q1)
(

Thus, as mentioned in the conclusion, we believe more research is required into cheaper heuristics before they are a viable alternative to

Appendix B
Proofs

In this section, we provide detailed proofs of all theorems stated in the main text.
We first provide some auxiliary lemmas, and then we proceed by proving the main results, ordered by bound.
We also provide results for
FIB
, since our exposition differs slightly from the exposition in
Hauskrecht (2000)
.
Finally, we provide some general properties of the value iteration algorithm that apply to each of our bounds.

B.1.
Auxiliary Lemmas

B.1.1.
Weight Functions

We start by showing a simple property of weight functions.

Lemma B.1.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a point set. If
w\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{B},b}
is a weight function, then
\sum_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}}w(b^{\prime})=1
.

Proof.

By
Def.

3.4
, a weight function
w\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{B},b}
satisfies
\sum_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}}w(b^{\prime})b^{\prime}(s)=b(s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Summing these equalities and interchanging the order of summation yields

\sum_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}}w(b^{\prime})=\sum_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}}w(b^{\prime})\left(\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}b^{\prime}(s)\right)=\sum_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}w(b^{\prime})b^{\prime}(s)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}}w(b^{\prime})b^{\prime}(s)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}b(s)=1,

where we use that
b
and each
b^{\prime}
are probability distributions and hence sum to 1 over their domain
\mathcal{S}
.
∎

B.1.2.
Contraction Mappings

Next, we prove a property that we will use when showing that our Bellman operators are contraction mappings.

Lemma B.2.

Let
X
be a set and let
f,g\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be functions. Then

\left|\sup_{x\in X}f(x)-\sup_{x\in X}g(x)\right|\leq\sup_{x\in X}|f(x)-g(x)|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left|\inf_{x\in X}f(x)-\inf_{x\in X}g(x)\right|\leq\sup_{x\in X}|f(x)-g(x)|.

Proof.

We have

\sup_{x\in X}f(x)-\sup_{x\in X}g(x)=\sup_{x\in X}\left[f(x)-\sup_{x^{\prime}\in X}g(x^{\prime})\right]\leq\sup_{x\in X}\left[f(x)-g(x)\right]\leq\sup_{x\in X}|f(x)-g(x)|,

where for the middle inequality we use that
\sup\limits_{x^{\prime}\in X}g(x^{\prime})\geq g(x)
for any
x\in X
. By exchanging the role of
f
and
g
, we similarly get

\sup_{x\in X}g(x)-\sup_{x\in X}f(x)\leq\sup_{x\in X}|g(x)-f(x)|=\sup_{x\in X}|f(x)-g(x)|,

which altogether implies the first inequality
\displaystyle\left|\sup_{x\in X}f(x)-\sup_{x\in X}g(x)\right|\leq\sup_{x\in X}|f(x)-g(x)|
.

For the second inequality, we apply the first inequality to the functions
-g
and
-f
, which yields

\left|\inf_{x\in X}f(x)-\inf_{x\in X}g(x)\right|=\left|\sup_{x\in X}(-g)(x)-\sup_{x\in X}(-f)(x)\right|\leq\sup_{x\in X}|(-g)(x)-(-f)(x)|=\sup_{x\in X}|f(x)-g(x)|.\qed

In practice, when we apply Lemma
B.2
, we know that the suprema and infima on the left hand side are actually attained (and are hence written as maxima and minima), although on the right side we will write suprema when
X
is an infinite set.

B.1.3.
Uniqueness of the Fixed Point.

Next, we explain why showing that our Bellman operators are contraction mappings is sufficient to show that they have a unique fixed point.
For this, we recall Banach’s fixed point theorem
(
Banach, 1922
)
:

Theorem B.3 (Banach’s fixed point theorem).

Let
(X,d)
be a non-empty complete metric space and let
T\colon X\rightarrow X
be a contraction mapping. Then
T
has a unique fixed point
x^{*}
. Moreover, we can find
x^{*}
in the limit by starting from an arbitrary element
x\in X
and repeatedly applying
T
: if we define the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
by
x_{0}=x
and
x_{n+1}=T(x_{n})
, then
x^{*}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}x_{n}
.

Recall that
\mathcal{Q}
is the set of functions
Q\colon\Delta(\mathcal{S})\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Using Banach’s fixed point theorem, we prove the following:

Lemma B.4.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a finite point set, let
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{B}}
be the set of functions
Q\colon\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and let
H\colon\mathcal{Q}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}
be a contraction mapping. Equip
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{B}}
with the
\infty
-norm.
Assume that we can compute
HQ(b,a)
for any
(b,a)\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})\times\mathcal{A}
using only the
Q
-values on
\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{A}
. Then we can also see
H
as an operator mapping
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{B}}
to
\mathcal{Q}
, which induces an operator
H|_{\mathcal{B}}\colon\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{B}}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{B}}
by restricting the domain
\Delta(\mathcal{S})\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of the each function
HQ
to
\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
Then
H|_{\mathcal{B}}\colon\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{B}}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{B}}
has a unique fixed point
Q^{*}|_{\mathcal{B}}\colon\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, if we can compute the
Q
-value for any belief
b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
using the
Q
-values on
\mathcal{B}
, this induces a unique fixed point
Q^{*}\colon\Delta(\mathcal{S})\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of
H
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{B}
is finite and
\mathcal{A}
is finite, the set
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{B}}
can be seen as a finite-dimensional normed vector space (isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{A}|}
). Since the metric space corresponding to any finite-dimensional normed vector space is complete, Banach’s fixed point theorem (
Theorem

B.3
) now implies that
H|_{\mathcal{B}}
has a unique fixed point
Q^{*}|_{\mathcal{B}}\colon\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Since we can compute the
Q
-value for any belief
b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
using the
Q
-values on
\mathcal{B}
, we can use this to compute a function
Q^{*}\colon\Delta(\mathcal{S})\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Since
H
fixes the restriction of
Q^{*}
to
\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{A}
, and these values determine all other values, it follows that
H
fixes
Q^{*}
.
∎

Note that we go via the finite-dimensional space
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{B}}
here, since infinite-dimensional spaces are not necessarily complete.

B.1.4.
Inequalities.

Finally, we prove a lemma that we can use to show inequalities between different
Q
-functions. For this, we will use that all Bellman operators
H
we consider in this paper (
H_{\text{FIB}}
,
H_{\text{TIB}}
,
H_{\text{OTIB}}
,
H_{\text{ETIB}}
and
H_{\text{POMDP}}
) are
monotone
: If
Q\leq Q^{\prime}
pointwise (i.e.
Q(b,a)\leq Q^{\prime}(b,a)
for all
(b,a)\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})\times\mathcal{A}
), then also
HQ\leq HQ^{\prime}
pointwise. With this, we can show the following:

Lemma B.5.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a finite point set, let
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{B}}
be the set of functions
Q\colon\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and let
H_{\mathrm{X}},H_{\mathrm{Y}}\colon\mathcal{Q}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}
be monotone contraction mappings. Assume that
H_{\mathrm{Y}}
has the property that we can compute
H_{\mathrm{Y}}Q(b,a)
for any
(b,a)\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})\times\mathcal{A}
using only the
Q
-values on
\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{A}
. Let
Q_{\mathrm{X}}
and
Q_{\mathrm{Y}}
be the fixed points of
H_{\mathrm{X}}
and
H_{\mathrm{Y}}
. Assume that
H_{\mathrm{Y}}Q_{\mathrm{X}}\leq Q_{\mathrm{X}}
. Then
Q_{\mathrm{Y}}\leq Q_{\mathrm{X}}
. Similarly, if
H_{\mathrm{Y}}Q_{\mathrm{X}}\geq Q_{\mathrm{X}}
, then
Q_{\mathrm{Y}}\geq Q_{\mathrm{X}}
.

Proof.

Since the proofs of the ‘
\leq
’-statement and the ‘
\geq
’-statement are similar, we only prove the ‘
\leq
’-statement. Define a sequence
(Q_{n})_{n\geq 0}
by
Q_{0}=Q_{\mathrm{X}}
and
Q_{n+1}=H_{\mathrm{Y}}Q_{n}
. We prove by induction on
n\geq 0
that
Q_{n}\leq Q_{\mathrm{X}}
. The base case states
Q_{\mathrm{X}}\leq Q_{\mathrm{X}}
, so we proceed with the induction step. Assume that
Q_{n}\leq Q_{\mathrm{X}}
. Applying the monotone operator
H_{\mathrm{Y}}
to both sides then yields
Q_{n+1}=H_{\mathrm{Y}}Q_{n}\leq H_{\mathrm{Y}}Q_{\mathrm{X}}
. Moreover, we are given that
H_{\mathrm{Y}}Q_{\mathrm{X}}\leq Q_{\mathrm{X}}
, so we conclude that
Q_{n+1}\leq Q_{\mathrm{X}}
. This completes the induction, so we conclude that
Q_{n}\leq Q_{\mathrm{X}}
for all
n\geq 0
. By Banach’s fixed point theorem (applied in a similar way as in
Lemma

B.4
), it follows that
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}Q_{n}=Q_{\mathrm{Y}}
on
\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{A}
. By taking the limit
n\to\infty
in the inequality
Q_{n}\leq Q_{\mathrm{X}}
, it hence follows that
Q_{\mathrm{Y}}\leq Q_{\mathrm{X}}
on
\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{A}
. But since the values on
\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{A}
are the only ones that are relevant for computing
H_{\mathrm{Y}}Q
and since
H_{\mathrm{Y}}Q
is monotone, it follows that
Q_{\mathrm{Y}}=H_{\mathrm{Y}}Q_{\mathrm{Y}}\leq H_{\mathrm{Y}}Q_{\mathrm{X}}\leq Q_{\mathrm{X}}
.
∎

B.2.
Fast Informed Bound

We start by providing proofs for some properties of the Fast Informed Bound, since our exposition is slightly different from the exposition in
Hauskrecht (2000)
and since we will actually use these properties directly in our proof.

Theorem B.6.

H_{\text{FIB}}
is a contraction operator w.r.t. the
\infty
-norm with Lipschitz constant
\gamma<1
, and
H_{\text{FIB}}
has a unique fixed point
Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}
.

Proof.

Let
Q,Q^{\prime}\colon\Delta(\mathcal{S})\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be any
Q
-functions. Fix
b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Then we have

\displaystyle|H_{\text{FIB}}Q(b,a)-H_{\text{FIB}}Q^{\prime}(b,a)|

\displaystyle=\left|\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\Pr(o,s^{\prime}~|~b,a)Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right]-\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\Pr(o,s^{\prime}~|~b,a)Q^{\prime}(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\left|\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\Pr(o,s^{\prime}~|~b,a)Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right]-\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\Pr(o,s^{\prime}~|~b,a)Q^{\prime}(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left|\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\Pr(o,s^{\prime}~|~b,a)Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right]-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\Pr(o,s^{\prime}~|~b,a)Q^{\prime}(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\Pr(o,s^{\prime}~|~b,a)\left|Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})-Q^{\prime}(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\Pr(o,s^{\prime}~|~b,a)\left\|Q-Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\gamma\left\|Q-Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\Pr(o,s^{\prime}~|~b,a)=\gamma\left\|Q-Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty},

where the inequalities follow from the triangle inequality,
Lemma

B.2
, the triangle inequality and the definition of the
\infty
-norm.

By the definition of the
\infty
-norm, it follows that

\left\|H_{\text{FIB}}Q-H_{\text{FIB}}Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\max_{(b,a)\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})\times\mathcal{A}}|H_{\text{FIB}}Q(b,a)-H_{\text{FIB}}Q^{\prime}(b,a)|\leq\gamma\left\|Q-Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty},

which shows that
H_{\text{FIB}}
is a contraction operator with Lipschitz constant
\gamma
. Since
H_{\text{FIB}}
can be computed using only the finite set of unit beliefs,
Lemma

B.4
now implies that
H_{\text{FIB}}
has a unique fixed point
Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}
.
∎

Theorem B.7.

Let
a\in\mathcal{A}
be given. Then
b\mapsto Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}(b,a)
is convex.

Proof.

Let
Q
be any
Q
-function. We first show that for given
a\in\mathcal{A}
, the function
b\mapsto H_{\text{FIB}}Q(b,a)
is convex.
The function
b\mapsto\Pr(o,s^{\prime}~|~b,a)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}b(s)\Pr(o,s^{\prime}~|~s,a)
is linear in
b
(since
\Pr(o,s^{\prime}~|~s,a)
does not depend on
b
). Hence, also

b\mapsto\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\Pr(o,s^{\prime}~|~b,a)Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right]

is linear in
b
(since the values of
Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})
do not depend on
b
), and hence convex in
b
.
Since the pointwise maximum of convex functions is convex and the sum of convex functions is convex, it follows that

b\mapsto\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\Pr(o,s^{\prime}~|~b,a)Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right]

is convex in
b
. Finally, since
b\mapsto R(b,a)
is linear (and hence convex) in
b
, we conclude that
b\mapsto H_{\text{FIB}}Q(b,a)
is convex by
Eq.

5
. Since
Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}
satisfies
Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}=H_{\text{FIB}}Q
, this in particular implies that
b\mapsto Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}(b,a)
is convex for any given
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
∎

. We first show that the fixed point

exists and is unique.

Theorem B.8.

H_{\text{TIB}}
is a contraction operator w.r.t. the
\infty
-norm with Lipschitz constant
\gamma<1
, and
H_{\text{TIB}}
has a unique fixed point

.

Let
Q,Q^{\prime}\colon\Delta(\mathcal{S})\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be any
Q
-functions. Fix

and
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Then we have

\displaystyle|H_{\text{TIB}}Q(b,a)-H_{\text{TIB}}Q^{\prime}(b,a)|

\displaystyle=\left|\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left[b(s)\Pr(o~|~s,a)Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s,a,o},a^{\prime})\right]-\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left[b(s)\Pr(o~|~s,a)Q^{\prime}(\mathfrak{b}_{s,a,o},a^{\prime})\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left|\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left[b(s)\Pr(o~|~s,a)Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s,a,o},a^{\prime})\right]-\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left[b(s)\Pr(o~|~s,a)Q^{\prime}(\mathfrak{b}_{s,a,o},a^{\prime})\right]\right|

B.2

\left\|H_{\text{TIB}}Q-H_{\text{TIB}}Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\max_{(b,a)\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})\times\mathcal{A}}|H_{\text{TIB}}Q(b,a)-H_{\text{TIB}}Q^{\prime}(b,a)|\leq\gamma\left\|Q-Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty},

which shows that
H_{\text{TIB}}

\gamma
. Since
H_{\text{TIB}}
can be computed using only the finite set of one-step beliefs,

B.4
now implies that
H_{\text{TIB}}
has a unique fixed point
Q_{\mathrm{TIB}}
.
∎

Theorem B.10.

Finally, we show that TIB is indeed tighter than FIB.

Theorem B.11.

For any belief
b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
and any
a\in\mathcal{A}
, it holds that
Q_{\mathrm{TIB}}(b,a)\leq Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}(b,a)
.

Proof.

Let
Q
be any convex
Q
-function. We first show that
H_{\text{TIB}}Q\leq H_{\text{FIB}}Q
, i.e. that
H_{\text{TIB}}Q(b,a)\leq H_{\text{FIB}}Q(b,a)
for all
b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
We write the belief
\mathfrak{b}_{s,a,o}
as a convex combination of state beliefs:

\mathfrak{b}_{s,a,o}=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\frac{\Pr(s^{\prime},o~|~s,a)}{\Pr(o~|~s,a)}\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}}\right].

Hence, the convexity of
Q
implies

Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s,a,o},a^{\prime})\leq\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\frac{\Pr(s^{\prime},o~|~s,a)}{\Pr(o~|~s,a)}Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right].

Using this, we find

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left[b(s)\Pr(o~|~s,a)Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s,a,o},a^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left[b(s)\Pr(o~|~s,a)\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\frac{\Pr(s^{\prime},o~|~s,a)}{\Pr(o~|~s,a)}Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[b(s)\Pr(s^{\prime},o~|~s,a)Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}[b(s)\Pr(s^{\prime},o~|~s,a)]Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\Pr(s^{\prime},o~|~b,a)Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right].

Hence, we get

\displaystyle H_{\text{TIB}}Q(b,a)

\displaystyle=R(b,a)+\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left[b(s)\Pr(o~|~s,a)Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s,a,o},a^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle\leq R(b,a)+\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\Pr(s^{\prime},o~|~b,a)Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right]=H_{\text{FIB}}Q(b,a).

Since
Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}
is convex (by
Theorem

B.9
), applying the result
H_{\text{TIB}}Q\leq H_{\text{FIB}}Q
to
Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}
shows that
H_{\text{TIB}}Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}\leq H_{\text{FIB}}Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}=Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}
. By
Lemma

B.5
, it now follows that
Q_{\mathrm{TIB}}\leq Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}
, i.e. that
Q_{\mathrm{TIB}}(b,a)\leq Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}(b,a)
for all
b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
∎

B.4.
Optimized Tighter Informed Bound

Now we turn to
OTIB
. We again start by showing that the fixed point
Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}
exists and is unique. The techniques are mostly similar to the techniques used in the proof of
Theorem

B.8
, but we need a second application of
Lemma

B.2
to deal with the minimization over the weights.

Theorem B.12.

H_{\text{OTIB}}
is a contraction operator w.r.t. the
\infty
-norm with Lipschitz constant
\gamma<1
, and
H_{\text{OTIB}}
has a unique fixed point
Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}
.

Proof.

Let
Q,Q^{\prime}\colon\Delta(\mathcal{S})\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be any
Q
-functions. Fix
b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Then we have

\displaystyle|H_{\text{OTIB}}Q(b,a)-H_{\text{OTIB}}Q^{\prime}(b,a)|

\displaystyle=\left|\gamma\!\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}[\Pr(o~|~b,a)\!\!\min_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)]-\gamma\!\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}[\Pr(o~|~b,a)\!\!\min_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q^{\prime}}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\left|\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}[\Pr(o~|~b,a)\!\!\min_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)]-\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}[\Pr(o~|~b,a)\!\!\min_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q^{\prime}}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\Pr(o~|~b,a)\left|\min_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)-\min_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q^{\prime}}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\Pr(o~|~b,a)\sup_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\left|\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)-\widetilde{Q^{\prime}}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\Pr(o~|~b,a)\sup_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\Bigg|\sum_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}w(b^{\prime})\left(Q(b^{\prime},a^{\prime})-Q^{\prime}(b^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\Pr(o~|~b,a)\sup_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\sum_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}w(b^{\prime})\left|\left(Q(b^{\prime},a^{\prime})-Q^{\prime}(b^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\Pr(o~|~b,a)\sup_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\sum_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}w(b^{\prime})\left\|Q-Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\gamma\left\|Q-Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\Pr(o~|~b,a)\sup_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\sum_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}w(b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\gamma\left\|Q-Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\Pr(o~|~b,a)=\gamma\left\|Q-Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}.

The first three inequalities follow from the triangle inequality, and applying
Lemma

B.2
twice. Next, we use
Eq.

6
, the triangle inequality, the definition of the
\infty
-norm, and
Lemma

B.1
. By the definition of the
\infty
-norm, it follows that

\left\|H_{\text{OTIB}}Q-H_{\text{OTIB}}Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\max_{(b,a)\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})\times\mathcal{A}}|H_{\text{OTIB}}Q(b,a)-H_{\text{OTIB}}Q^{\prime}(b,a)|\leq\gamma\left\|Q-Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty},

which shows that
H_{\text{OTIB}}
is a contraction operator with Lipschitz constant
\gamma
. Since
H_{\text{OTIB}}
can be computed using only the finite set of one-step beliefs,
Lemma

B.4
now implies that
H_{\text{OTIB}}
has a unique fixed point
Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}
.
∎

Next, we show that
Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}
is convex in
b
.

Theorem B.13.

Let
a\in\mathcal{A}
be given. Then
b\mapsto Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}(b,a)
is convex.

Proof.

Let
Q
be any
Q
-function. We first show that for given
a\in\mathcal{A}
, the function
b\mapsto H_{\text{OTIB}}Q(b,a)
is convex using the definition of convexity. Let
b,b^{\prime}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
be given. Write
b^{\prime\prime}=\lambda b+(1-\lambda)b^{\prime}
. Then

\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime\prime},a)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}b^{\prime\prime}(s)\Pr(o~|~s,a)=\lambda\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}b(s)\Pr(o~|~s,a)+(1-\lambda)\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}b^{\prime}(s)\Pr(o~|~s,a)=\lambda\Pr(o~|~b,a)+(1-\lambda)\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime},a).

We now show that if
w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}
and
w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{b^{\prime},a,o}
are weight functions for
\mathfrak{b}_{b,a,o}
and
\mathfrak{b}_{b^{\prime},a,o}
, then
w^{\prime\prime}=\frac{\lambda\Pr(o~|~b,a)}{\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime\prime},a)}w+\frac{(1-\lambda)\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime},a)}{\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime\prime},a)}w^{\prime}
is a weight function for
\mathfrak{b}_{b^{\prime\prime},a,o}
. This follows from

\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{b}\in\mathcal{B}}w^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{b})\tilde{b}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\Pr(o~|~b,a)}{\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime\prime},a)}\sum_{\tilde{b}\in\mathcal{B}}w(\tilde{b})\tilde{b}(s)+\frac{(1-\lambda)\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime},a)}{\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime\prime},a)}\sum_{\tilde{b}\in\mathcal{B}}w^{\prime}(\tilde{b})\tilde{b}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\Pr(o~|~b,a)}{\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime\prime},a)}\mathfrak{b}_{b,a,o}(s)+\frac{(1-\lambda)\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime},a)}{\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime\prime},a)}\mathfrak{b}_{b^{\prime},a,o}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\Pr(o~|~b,a)}{\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime\prime},a)}\frac{\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}b(s)\Pr(s^{\prime},o~|~s,a)}{\Pr(o~|~b,a)}+\frac{(1-\lambda)\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime},a)}{\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime\prime},a)}\frac{\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}b^{\prime}(s)\Pr(s^{\prime},o~|~s,a)}{\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime},a)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}b(s)\Pr(s^{\prime},o~|~s,a)+(1-\lambda)\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}b^{\prime}(s)\Pr(s^{\prime},o~|~s,a)}{\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime\prime},a)}.

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}b^{\prime\prime}(s)\Pr(s^{\prime},o~|~s,a)}{\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime\prime},a)}=\mathfrak{b}_{b^{\prime\prime},a,o}(s).

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime\prime},a)\widetilde{Q}\left(w^{\prime\prime},a^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime\prime},a)\sum_{\tilde{b}\in\mathcal{B}}w^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{b})Q(\tilde{b},a^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\lambda\Pr(o~|~b,a)\sum_{\tilde{b}\in\mathcal{B}}w(\tilde{b})Q(\tilde{b},a^{\prime})+(1-\lambda)\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime},a)\sum_{\tilde{b}\in\mathcal{B}}w^{\prime}(\tilde{b})Q(\tilde{b},a^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\lambda\Pr(o~|~b,a)\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)+(1-\lambda)\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime},a)\widetilde{Q}\left(w^{\prime},a^{\prime}\right).

Hence, for any
w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}
and
w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{b^{\prime},a,o}
, there exists a
w^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{b^{\prime\prime},a,o}
such that

\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime\prime},a)\widetilde{Q}\left(w^{\prime\prime},a^{\prime}\right)=\lambda\Pr(o~|~b,a)\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)+(1-\lambda)\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime},a)\widetilde{Q}\left(w^{\prime},a^{\prime}\right).

By taking
w\in\argmin\limits_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)
and
w^{\prime}\in\argmin\limits_{w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{b^{\prime},a,o}}\widetilde{Q}\left(w^{\prime},a^{\prime}\right)
, this implies

\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime\prime},a)\!\!\min\limits_{w^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{b^{\prime\prime},a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}\left(w^{\prime\prime},a^{\prime}\right)\leq\lambda\Pr(o~|~b,a)\!\min\limits_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)+(1-\lambda)\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime},a)\!\min\limits_{w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{b^{\prime},a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}\left(w^{\prime},a^{\prime}\right).

This in turn implies that

\displaystyle\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime\prime},a)\!\!\min\limits_{w^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{b^{\prime\prime},a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}\left(w^{\prime\prime},a^{\prime}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\lambda\Pr(o~|~b,a)\!\min\limits_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)+(1-\lambda)\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime},a)\!\min\limits_{w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{b^{\prime},a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}\left(w^{\prime},a^{\prime}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\Pr(o~|~b,a)\!\min\limits_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)\right]+(1-\lambda)\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime},a)\!\min\limits_{w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{b^{\prime},a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}\left(w^{\prime},a^{\prime}\right)\right]

Summing over all
o\in\mathcal{O}
and using that
R(b^{\prime\prime},a)=\lambda R(b,a)+(1-\lambda)R(b^{\prime},a)
now yields

\displaystyle H_{\text{OTIB}}

\displaystyle Q(b^{\prime\prime},a)=R(b^{\prime\prime},a)+\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime\prime},a)\!\!\min\limits_{w^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{b^{\prime\prime},a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}\left(w^{\prime\prime},a^{\prime}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\left[R(b,a)\!+\!\gamma\!\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\Pr(o~|~b,a)\!\min\limits_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)\right]\right]+(1\!-\!\lambda)\left[R(b^{\prime},a)\!+\!\gamma\!\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\Pr(o~|~b^{\prime},a)\!\min\limits_{w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{b^{\prime},a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}\left(w^{\prime},a^{\prime}\right)\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda H_{\text{OTIB}}Q(b,a)+(1\!-\!\lambda)H_{\text{OTIB}}Q(b^{\prime},a).

Since
b,b^{\prime}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
were given arbitrarily, we conclude that the function
b\mapsto H_{\text{OTIB}}Q(b,a)
is convex.
Since
Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}
satisfies
Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}=H_{\text{OTIB}}Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}
, this in particular implies that
b\mapsto Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}(b,a)
is convex for any given
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
∎

Next, we show that
OTIB
is a sound upper bound.

Theorem B.14.

For any belief
b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
and any
a\in\mathcal{A}
, it holds that
Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}(b,a)\geq Q_{\mathrm{POMDP}}(b,a)
.

Proof.

Let
Q
be any convex
Q
-function. We first show that
H_{\text{POMDP}}Q\leq H_{\text{OTIB}}Q
.
By definition, a weight function
w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}
writes the belief
\mathfrak{b}_{b,a,o}
as a convex combination of state beliefs:

\mathfrak{b}_{b,a,o}=\sum_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}\left[w(b^{\prime})b^{\prime}\right].

Hence, the convexity of
Q
implies

Q(\mathfrak{b}_{b,a,o},a^{\prime})\leq\sum_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}\left[w(b^{\prime})Q(b^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right]=\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right).

Since this holds for any
w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}
, we find
Q(\mathfrak{b}_{b,a,o},a^{\prime})\leq\min\limits_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)
.
Hence, we get

\displaystyle H_{\text{POMDP}}Q(b,a)

\displaystyle=R(b,a)+\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\Pr(o~|~b,a)Q(\mathfrak{b}_{b,a,o},a^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle\leq R(b,a)+\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\Pr(o~|~b,a)\!\min\limits_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)\right]=H_{\text{OTIB}}Q(b,a).

Since
Q_{\mathrm{POMDP}}
is convex, applying the result
H_{\text{POMDP}}Q\leq H_{\text{OTIB}}Q
to
Q_{\mathrm{POMDP}}
shows that
Q_{\mathrm{POMDP}}=H_{\text{POMDP}}Q_{\mathrm{POMDP}}\leq H_{\text{OTIB}}Q_{\mathrm{POMDP}}
. By
Lemma

B.5
, it now follows that
Q_{\mathrm{POMDP}}\leq Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}
, i.e. that
Q_{\mathrm{POMDP}}(b,a)\leq Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}(b,a)
for all
b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
∎

Finally, we show that
OTIB
is tighter than
TIB
and
ETIB
.

Theorem B.15.

For any belief
b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
and any
a\in\mathcal{A}
, it holds that
Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}(b,a)\leq Q_{\mathrm{TIB}}(b,a)
and
Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}(b,a)\leq Q_{\mathrm{ETIB}}(b,a)
.

Proof.

Let
Q
be any
Q
-function. We first show that
H_{\text{TIB}}Q\geq H_{\text{OTIB}}Q
. Recall that we can write

H_{\text{TIB}}Q(b,a)=R(b,a)+\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\Pr(o~|~b,a)\widetilde{Q}\left(w_{b,a,o},a\right)\right],

with
w_{b,a,o}(\mathfrak{b}_{s,a,o})=\frac{b(s)\Pr(o~|~s,a)}{\Pr(o~|~b,a)}\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}
. Hence, we get
\widetilde{Q}\left(w_{b,a,o},a\right)\geq\min\limits_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)
, so

\displaystyle H_{\text{TIB}}Q(b,a)

\displaystyle=R(b,a)+\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\Pr(o~|~b,a)\widetilde{Q}\left(w_{b,a,o},a\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq R(b,a)+\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\Pr(o~|~b,a)\!\min\limits_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)\right]=H_{\text{OTIB}}Q(b,a).

Applying the result
H_{\text{OTIB}}Q\leq H_{\text{TIB}}Q
to
Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}
shows that
Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}=H_{\text{OTIB}}Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}\leq H_{\text{TIB}}Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}
. By
Lemma

B.5
, it now follows that
Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}\leq Q_{\mathrm{TIB}}
. The proof that
Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}\leq Q_{\mathrm{ETIB}}
is analogous, except that we now use that the entropy-based weights
\hat{w}_{b,a,o}
satisfy
\hat{w}_{b,a,o}\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}
and hence
\widetilde{Q}\left(\hat{w}_{b,a,o},a\right)\geq\min\limits_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{b,a,o}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}\left(w,a^{\prime}\right)
,
∎

B.5.
Entropy-Based Tighter Informed Bound

Now we turn to
ETIB
. We again start by showing that the fixed point
Q_{\mathrm{ETIB}}
exists and is unique.

Theorem B.16.

H_{\text{ETIB}}
is a contraction operator w.r.t. the
\infty
-norm with Lipschitz constant
\gamma<1
, and
H_{\text{ETIB}}
has a unique fixed point
Q_{\mathrm{ETIB}}
.

Proof.

Let
Q,Q^{\prime}\colon\Delta(\mathcal{S})\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be any
Q
-functions. Fix
b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Then we have

\displaystyle|H_{\text{ETIB}}Q(b,a)-H_{\text{ETIB}}Q^{\prime}(b,a)|

\displaystyle=\left|\gamma\!\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}[\Pr(o~|~b,a)\widetilde{Q}\left(\hat{w}_{b,a,o},a^{\prime}\right)]-\gamma\!\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}[\Pr(o~|~b,a)\widetilde{Q}\left(\hat{w}_{b,a,o},a^{\prime}\right)]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\left|\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}[\Pr(o~|~b,a)\widetilde{Q}\left(\hat{w}_{b,a,o},a^{\prime}\right)]-\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}[\Pr(o~|~b,a)\widetilde{Q}\left(\hat{w}_{b,a,o},a^{\prime}\right)]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\Pr(o~|~b,a)\left|\widetilde{Q}\left(\hat{w}_{b,a,o},a^{\prime}\right)]-\widetilde{Q}\left(\hat{w}_{b,a,o},a^{\prime}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\Pr(o~|~b,a)\Bigg|\sum_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}\hat{w}_{b,a,o}(b^{\prime})\left(Q(b^{\prime},a^{\prime})-Q^{\prime}(b^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\Pr(o~|~b,a)\sum_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}\hat{w}_{b,a,o}(b^{\prime})\left|\left(Q(b^{\prime},a^{\prime})-Q^{\prime}(b^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\Pr(o~|~b,a)\sum_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}\hat{w}_{b,a,o}(b^{\prime})\left\|Q-Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\gamma\left\|Q-Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\Pr(o~|~b,a)\sum_{b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}\hat{w}_{b,a,o}(b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\gamma\left\|Q-Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\Pr(o~|~b,a)=\gamma\left\|Q-Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}.

The first three inequalities follow from the triangle inequality, and applying
Lemma

B.2
twice. Next, we use
Eq.

6
, the triangle inequality, the definition of the
\infty
-norm, and
Lemma

B.1
.

By the definition of the
\infty
-norm, it follows that

\left\|H_{\text{ETIB}}Q-H_{\text{ETIB}}Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\max_{(b,a)\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})\times\mathcal{A}}|H_{\text{ETIB}}Q(b,a)-H_{\text{ETIB}}Q^{\prime}(b,a)|\leq\gamma\left\|Q-Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty},

which shows that
H_{\text{ETIB}}
is a contraction operator with Lipschitz constant
\gamma
. Since
H_{\text{ETIB}}
can be computed using only the finite set of one-step beliefs,
Lemma

B.4
now implies that
H_{\text{ETIB}}
has a unique fixed point
Q_{\mathrm{ETIB}}
.
∎

The next theorem shows that
ETIB
is a sound upper bound.

Theorem B.17.

For any belief
b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
and any
a\in\mathcal{A}
, it holds that
Q_{\mathrm{ETIB}}(b,a)\geq Q_{\mathrm{POMDP}}(b,a)
.

Proof.

By
Theorem

B.14
and
Theorem

B.15
, we have that
Q_{\mathrm{ETIB}}(b,a)\geq Q_{\mathrm{OTIB}}(b,a)\geq Q_{\mathrm{POMDP}}(b,a)
.
∎

Finally, we show that
ETIB
is tighter than
OTIB
.

Theorem B.18.

For any belief
b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
and any
a\in\mathcal{A}
, it holds that
Q_{\mathrm{ETIB}}(b,a)\leq Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}(b,a)
.

Proof.

Let
Q
be any convex
Q
-function. We first show that
H_{\text{ETIB}}Q\leq H_{\text{FIB}}Q
.
We can write any belief
b^{\prime}
as a convex combination of state beliefs:

b^{\prime}=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[b^{\prime}(s^{\prime})\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}}\right].

Hence, the convexity of
Q
implies

Q(b^{\prime},a^{\prime})\leq\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[b^{\prime}(s^{\prime})Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right].

Using this, we find

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}\left(\hat{w}_{b,a,o},a^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}\left[\hat{w}_{b,a,o}(b^{\prime})Q(b^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}\left[\hat{w}_{b,a,o}(b^{\prime})\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[b^{\prime}(s^{\prime})Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\hat{w}_{b,a,o}(b^{\prime})b^{\prime}(s^{\prime})Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}\left[\hat{w}_{b,a,o}(b^{\prime})b^{\prime}(s^{\prime})\right]Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\mathfrak{b}_{b,a,o}(s^{\prime})Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\frac{\Pr(s^{\prime},o~|~b,a)}{\Pr(o~|~b,a)}Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right].

Hence, we get

\displaystyle H_{\text{ETIB}}Q(b,a)

\displaystyle=R(b,a)+\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\Pr(o~|~b,a)\widetilde{Q}\left(\hat{w}_{b,a,o},a^{\prime}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq R(b,a)+\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max\limits_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\Pr(s^{\prime},o~|~b,a)Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s^{\prime}},a^{\prime})\right]=H_{\text{FIB}}Q(b,a).

Since
Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}
is convex (by
Theorem

B.9
), applying the result
H_{\text{ETIB}}Q\leq H_{\text{FIB}}Q
to
Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}
shows that
H_{\text{ETIB}}Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}\leq H_{\text{FIB}}Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}=Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}
. By
Lemma

B.5
, it now follows that
Q_{\mathrm{ETIB}}\leq Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}
, i.e. that
Q_{\mathrm{ETIB}}(b,a)\leq Q_{\mathrm{FIB}}(b,a)
for all
b\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
∎

B.6.
Value Iteration

We approximate the fixed points of our Bellman operators using value iteration. The following result shows a termination criterion for the value iteration, by showing how close the actual solution and the final iteration are based on the distance between the last two iterations.

Lemma B.19.

Let
H
be any contraction operator with Lipschitz constant
\gamma
, and let
Q^{*}
be its fixed point. Then
\|Q^{*}-HQ\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\|HQ-Q\|_{\infty}
.

Proof.

Let
H
be any contraction operator with Lipschitz constant
\gamma
, and let
Q^{*}
be its fixed point. Then

\|Q^{*}-Q\|_{\infty}\leq\|Q^{*}-HQ\|_{\infty}+\|HQ-Q\|_{\infty}=\|HQ^{*}-HQ\|_{\infty}+\|HQ-Q\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma\|Q^{*}-Q\|_{\infty}+\|HQ-Q\|_{\infty},

using the triangle inequality, the fact that
Q^{*}
is a fixed point of
H
and the fact that
H
is a contraction operator with Lipschitz constant
\gamma
. This implies that
\|Q^{*}-Q\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\|HQ-Q\|_{\infty}
, and hence that
\|Q^{*}-HQ\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma\|Q^{*}-Q\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\|HQ-Q\|_{\infty}
.
∎

Appendix C
Analyzing increased delays

In this section, we give an intuitive analysis of the effect of considering longer observation delays.
We start with a concrete variant of
TIB
where we assume an agent observes its state after 3 time steps instead of 2, which we denote as
TIB3
.
We first introduce some notation: similarly to
\mathfrak{b}_{s,a,o}
, we denote
\mathfrak{b}_{s,a,o,a^{\prime},o^{\prime}}=\mathfrak{b}_{\mathfrak{b}_{s,a,o},a^{\prime},o^{\prime}}
as a
two-step belief
, i.e. the belief after taking actions
a
and
a^{\prime}
from state
s
and observing
o
and
o^{\prime}
.
Furthermore, let
\mathcal{B}_{2}
denote the set of all such beliefs plus the initial belief, with
|\mathcal{B}_{2}|\in\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|^{2}|\mathcal{O}|^{2})
.
Then, we can write the Bellman operator for
TIB3
as follows:

(14)

H_{\text{TIB3}}Q(b,a)=R(b,a)+\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{o^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}}\max_{a^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\Bigg[\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}b(s)\Pr(o~|~s,a)\bigg[R(\mathfrak{b}_{s,a,o},a^{\prime})+\gamma\Pr(o^{\prime}~|~\mathfrak{b}_{s,a,o},a^{\prime})Q(\mathfrak{b}_{s,a,o,a^{\prime},o^{\prime}},a^{\prime\prime})\bigg]\Bigg]

We omit a proof that this operator has a unique fixed point, but this can be proven analogously to the other bounds.
In the equation, the actions
a^{\prime}
and
a^{\prime\prime}
are chosen independently of the state
s
, as opposed to only an action
a^{\prime}
for
TIB
.
Thus, we increased the observation delay from 2 time steps to 3.

Assuming all probability calculations can be computed in
O(1)
amortized time (e.g. they are cached), then the computational complexity of a single operation is
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{B}_{2}|)\in\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|^{2}|\mathcal{O}|^{2})
, which is a factor
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{O}|)
larger than for
TIB
.
Furthermore, let us assume we approximate this bound using value iterations, similarly to
Algorithm

1
.
Then, a single value iteration step requires iterating over all beliefs in
\mathcal{B}_{2}
instead of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
.
Thus, the total complexity for precomputations is
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{B}_{2}|^{2}|\mathcal{A}|h)\in\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{S}|^{2}|\mathcal{A}|^{5}|\mathcal{O}|^{4}h)
, a factor
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{A}|^{2}|\mathcal{O}|^{2})
larger than for
TIB
.

It is easy to see that this pattern extends to larger delays, i.e. given a delay
n\geq 2
, the complexity for precomputations is given as
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{S}|^{2}|\mathcal{A}|^{1+2(n-1)}|\mathcal{O}|^{2(n-1)}h)
.
Since beliefs in
\mathcal{B}_{2}
may be identical, computational costs may be lower in practice, but such analysis falls outside the scope of this work.
