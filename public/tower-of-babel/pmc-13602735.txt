Toxic metals differentially affect bacterial and fungal seed endophytes in Arabidopsis arenosa
Authors: Agnieszka Domka, Ayesha Aziz, Roman J Jędrzejczyk, Oleksandra Kutinova, Weronika Kosowicz, Artur Pliszko, Piotr Rozpądek, Rafał Ważny
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeed endophytes are the earliest microbial companions of plants. They play a beneficial role in seed germination, seedling development, and plant growth. Endophytes help plants to adapt to changing environments; however, their role in the aspect of toxic metal-polluted environments is poorly known. We investigated the impact of metal toxicity on the structure of seed-associated microbiota in Arabidopsis arenosa and explored the role of these microbes in host plant adaptation to metal-rich, post-mining environments.ResultsToxic metals markedly altered the alpha and beta diversity of bacterial seed endophytes, whereas fungal endophyte diversity was largely unaffected. Similar patterns were observed in a vertical transmission experiment, indicating that metal exposure can influence seed-associated microbiota across generations. To explore the functional relevance of these changes, we assembled synthetic microbial communities from culturable bacterial and fungal isolates recovered from seeds of plants originating from non-metalliferous and metalliferous sites. A synthetic community composed of isolates from non-metalliferous populations enhanced plant growth under metal stress, whereas the community assembled from metalliferous populations showed no growth-promoting effect. Crude seed extracts also did not reproduce the growth-promoting effect of the defined synthetic community. Further fractionation indicated that the beneficial effect was mainly associated with the fungal component, while bacterial fractions did not enhance plant growth under the tested metal-stress conditions. Fungal isolates from non-metalliferous populations promoted plant biomass under metal stress, whereas this effect was not observed for isolates from metalliferous populations under the tested conditions.ConclusionsLong-term exposure to toxic metals exerts strong selective pressure on seed-associated microbiota, markedly reducing bacterial diversity and reshaping community composition while leaving fungal diversity largely unaffected. Metal toxicity causes losses in the abundance of bacteria. High metal tolerance of these microorganisms indicates rather that metal exposure affects the interaction between the microbe and its host and the ability of the microbe to be retained in the seeds. Fungal endophytes from metal-free populations proved more effective in promoting plant growth under metal stress than those from metalliferous populations, suggesting that intrinsic strain-level traits, rather than prior environmental adaptation, are key to enhancing host tolerance. This challenges the assumption that endophytes from contaminated sites are inherently better suited for phytoremediation and highlights the potential of “naive” microbial sources. Together, these results expand our understanding of vertical transmission, microbial adaptation, and host–microbe interactions under heavy metal stress, and underscore the value of broadening the search for effective endophytes beyond contaminated habitats.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40168-026-02497-4.Keywords: Seed endophytes, Toxic metal pollution, Arabidopsis arenosa, Endophyte vertical transmission, Sporobolomyces, Cladosporium, Pantoea

Background

Among the earliest microbial companions of plants are seed endophytes—microorganisms that inhabit the reproductive organs of plants. Seeds represent a critical phase in the plant life cycle. Germination is a developmental bottleneck that determines future growth and health [1], thus maintaining an optimal seed microbiota seems to be imperative for the plants well-being.

The presence of heavy metals in soil, whether of natural or anthropogenic origin, poses a significant threat to living organisms due to their toxicity and persistence in the environment. These metals disrupt entire ecosystems, starting with soil microorganisms and extending through plants into food chains, thereby compromising food safety and human health [2, 3]. As sessile organisms, plants have evolved various adaptive mechanisms to cope with the toxic effects of soil-borne metals—adaptations that are increasingly understood to be supported by microorganisms coexisting within the same ecological niche [4, 5]. According to the holobiont theory, plant responses to environmental stressors represent the combined effort of the plant host and its associated microbiota, which are now considered an evolutionary unit [6, 7]. Microbiota can respond rapidly to environmental stress through shifts in community composition as well as genetic or phenotypic plasticity, whereas plant hosts often respond more slowly due to longer generation times and smaller population sizes [8]. Among these microbes, the core microbiota—defined as persistent members of the microbial community that appear in nearly all assemblages associated with a particular host—largely belong to abundant taxa and are thought to include key microbial lineages critical for plant health [9]. Numerous studies have shown that under stress, plants actively recruit beneficial microbial communities to enhance their tolerance and survival, underscoring the vital role of microbiota in supporting plant adaptation to challenging environments [10–13]. However, it remains unclear how environmental stress influences the seed microbiota, which represents the initial microbial pool for the next plant generation.

Heavy metals can enter seeds predominantly through uptake by the maternal plant and are transported directly from the soil into the seeds via the phloem, rather than being remobilized from other plant tissues. Soil is therefore considered the primary source of these metals, as shown for iron [14–17]. Once accumulated in seeds, toxic metals may impair germination in a dose-dependent manner by disrupting water uptake and exerting general toxicity [18]. Moreover, metal exposure often negatively impacts seed yield and viability [19]. Seedlings that germinate in metal-rich soils are exposed to oxidative stress from the outset of their life cycle, as heavy metals generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) that damage seed components and hinder early plant development [20]. In this context, seed-associated microbiota, which may be transmitted vertically from parent plants or acquired horizontally from the environment, represent a crucial first line of microbial support [21]. These microbes help plants respond to environmental stressors like metal toxicity and may shape long-term plant adaptation and microbial community assembly [22, 23].

Seed microbiota are known to affect germination, seedling development, and further microbial colonization. In plants growing in metal-polluted soils, seeds form a unique microbial niche. Here, microbes must withstand desiccation, utilize stored seed resources, and cope with internal metal accumulation [24, 25]. Seed microbiota often comprise a narrow group of co-evolved microorganisms essential for early plant survival [26, 27]. As in other plant organs, the seed microbiome consists of a stable core and a variable component shaped by environmental factors [28, 29]. Emerging evidence is shedding light on the previously underestimated role of seed microbiota in plant resilience to environmental stressors. Previous studies, including Truyens et al. [30], have shown that cadmium exposure alters the seed bacterial community structure in Arabidopsis thaliana. Subsequent research has indicated that heavy metals primarily influence microbial richness rather than taxonomic composition [31, 32].

In this study, we investigated how metal toxicity shapes the structure of seed-associated microbiota of Arabidopsis arenosa collected from post-mining wastes in the Upper Silesian area and explored the role of these microbes in host plant adaptation to metal toxicity. We hypothesized that an important factor in plant subpar performance under environmental constraints results from the loss of biodiversity in seed microbiota. Prolonged exposure to toxic metals significantly alters the community structure of seed endophytes, impairing vertical transmission and reducing the plant’s adaptive potential. To test this, we selected A. arenosa, a well-studied pseudometallophyte known for its natural growth in metal-rich and unpolluted soils [33, 34]. We evaluated the seed microorganism communities from plants growing in post-mining waste dumps and compared it to plants from unpolluted environments. Subsequently, we tested the persistence of seed endophytic microorganisms subject to culture under metal pollution mimicking mine dump metal exposure. We found genera of microorganisms particularly susceptible to metal toxicity and showed their significance for vegetation.

Methods

Experimental design and study sites

The investigations were carried out in three steps (Fig. 1). In the first step, the effect of toxic metals on the seed microbiota in environmental conditions was studied. In the second step, the results of the environmental study were verified in a vertical transmission laboratory experiment. In the third step, the role of seed endophytes for the growth of plant under metal toxicity was determined.

Experimental design overview. Arabidopsis arenosa seeds and soil were sampled from three toxic metal–polluted (TM) and three reference (TM‑free) sites in southern Poland for microbiota profiling. To evaluate TM effects on vertical endophyte transmission, a multigenerational pot experiment was carried out across three seed generations (F1–F3), comparing TM‑adapted and non‑adapted populations under TM and TM‑free conditions. Bacterial communities were characterized in F2 and F3 generation. Finally, representative strains from TM and TM‑free sites were assessed for their plant‑growth–promoting activity in vitro and in pot trials

The study was carried out on the Arabidopsis arenosa seed and soil materials collected in 3 toxic metal-polluted sites (TM) and in 3 reference toxic metal-free sites (TM-free), located in the Southern Poland (Silesia-Kraków Upland). The Silesian-Kraków Monocline in Poland contains the country’s largest zinc and lead ore deposits, primarily in Triassic carbonate rocks [35]. Centuries of mining and smelting have heavily contaminated soils and water, creating metal-rich, post-mining habitats with extreme conditions that challenge plant survival [36]. TM sites were localized in the following toxic metal-polluted areas: Bolesław (BO) mine dump (GPS coordinates: 50°17.328′N, 19°29.798′E), where zinc and lead ores are mined, Bytom (BY) mine dump (GPS coordinates: 50°20.333′N, 18°57.178′E), where zinc and lead ores were mined and Tarnowskie Góry (TG) mine dump (GPS coordinates: 50°24.874′N, 18°51.238′E), where lead, zinc and silver ores were mined and heap have been remediated by planting woody and herbaceous plants [37]. Reference sites were located in Alwernia (AL, GPS coordinates: 50°02.641′N, 19°32.740′E, the ruderal ground within the pine-oak forest), Kraków (KR, GPS coordinates: 50°01.776′N, 19°54.087′E, ruderal ground between construction area and riparian forest) and Ponik (PO, GPS coordinates: 50°43.784′N, 19°27.109′E, dry meadow on sandy fallow land).

In July 2022, within 1 week for each study site, mature siliques from at least 100 plants were collected in paper envelopes and air-dried for 7 days in the laboratory. Then 8 samples of 50.0 mg each weighted precisely seeds (approx. 500 seeds) were prepared for endophyte isolation and microbiome identification (8 samples × 6 study sites = 48 seed samples).

For each site, six soil samples were collected (6 samples × 6 sites = 36 soil samples); each sample was collected to polypropylene bag at volume 1 L and transported to the laboratory the same day. Next day, after cleaning larger fragments (stones, etc.), the soil was sieved and secured in polypropylene bags and stored at − 80 °C for DNA isolation (for microbiota analysis; n = 6) and were stored at 4 °C (for chemical analysis; n = 6) until analysis.

Floristic inventory

In July 2022, floristic inventories of vascular plants were conducted to better characterize the study sites. As the researchers suggest [38], vegetation composition is one of the most important environmental factors influencing the seed microbiome. Therefore, we considered it appropriate to list the vascular plant species co-occurring with Arabidopsis arenosa, even though this was not our research objective. Plant nomenclature followed Plants of the World Online (2025; http://www.plantsoftheworldonline.org (accessed November 12, 2025). The plant taxa are listed in Supplementary Table S1 and summarized in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Soil parameters

Soil parameters were investigated in the same way as described in our last paper by Ważny et al. [39]. Zn, Cd, Pb, N, P, and Plabile concentrations, soil organic matter and the pH were measured.

Elemental analysis of seeds

Seeds obtained from Arabidopsis arenosa plants, weighing precisely 50.0 mg, were subjected to a rigorous analytical procedure. First, the seeds were meticulously dried and weighed to ensure analytical precision. Subsequently, they underwent a thorough washing step with a 10 mM EDTA solution. The resulting pellet was collected for further analysis. For the initial mineralization of the seeds, they were quantitatively transferred to a beaker and exposed to a 2 mL aliquot of 65% nitric acid solution for a duration of one hour. Following this, a 0.5 mL volume of 30% hydrogen peroxide was added to the suspension. The entire mixture was subjected to thermal treatment at 100 °C for an additional hour to achieve complete mineralization. The resulting mineralisate underwent filtration through an analyte-free filter and was subsequently transferred to 10 mL volumetric flasks, where it was made up to the mark with deionised water. Following necessary dilution (with 2% HNO3), the mineralizations were assayed to quantify the concentrations of essential elements—iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), cadmium (Cd), and lead (Pb). The analytical determination of these elements was conducted using either Flame Atomic Absorption Spectrometry (FAAS) or Graphite Furnace Atomic Absorption Spectrometry (GF-AAS), depending on the respective concentration levels. The elemental analysis of the samples was performed using an Atomic Absorption Spectrometer (AAS spectrometer, Thermo). For calibration purposes, an external calibration curve, prepared with Merck standards, was employed to accurately determine the concentrations of the target elements. The reliability and accuracy of the obtained concentrations were further validated against certified reference material, specifically Rey grass (ERM-CD281) reference material, ensuring the quality and accuracy of the analytical results.

Isolation and identification of endophytic fungi and bacteria

Endophytic fungi and bacteria were isolated from the seeds. The seeds (6 samples per site; 1 sample = 50.0 mg) were provided for surface sterilization according to the following protocol: deionized water for 1 min, 70% ethanol for 30 s, 1% sodium hypochloride (with a drop of Tween) for 1 min, 70% ethanol for 30 s followed by 5-times rinsing (5 × 30 s) in deionized water. The seeds were homogenized with micropestle in 200 µL 10 mM MgSO4 and suspended in additional MgSO4 up to 1.5 mL. Next, 100 µL of sample was spread on 3 solid media for fungi: PDA (39 g·L−1 of potato dextrose agar) droplets supplemented with antibiotics (200 mg·L−1 streptomycin, 200 mg·L−1 ampicillin and 100 mg·L−1 tetracycline) and 2% MEA (malt extract agar) and TSA (tryptic soy agar; 15 g·L−1 of tryptone, 5 g·L−1 of soy peptone, 5 g·L−1 of NaCl; 5 g·L−1 of agar) droplets supplemented with antibiotics and 3 solid media for bacteria: NA (5 g·L−1 of peptone, 3 g·L−1 of meat extract, 15 g·L−1 of agar), YPG (10 g·L−1 of yeast extract, 10 g·L−1 of peptone, 70 g·L−1 of glucose, 15 g·L−1 of agar) and 869 (10 g·L−1 of tryptone, 3 g·L−1 of yeast extract, 5 g·L−1 of NaCl, 1 g·L−1 of glucose, 0.345 g·L−1 of CaCl2·2H2O, 17 g·L−1 of agar). For each sample and medium 3 plates were provided for endophyte isolation. Water from the last rinse (50 µL) were provided to confirm sterility by polymerase chain reaction of 16S and ITS rDNA regions. The samples were incubated in the dark at 26 °C for fungi and at 30 °C for bacteria and inspected every 1–3 days for 4 weeks. Emerging cultures were sequentially passaged to new media.

Pure cultures of endophytic bacteria were identified based on the sequence of the 16S rRNA gene. The 16S rDNA was amplified using the 27 F (5′-GAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′) primers [40]. Liquid culture (1 μL) of each bacterial strain was used for colony polymerase chain reaction (PCR). PCR was performed in 20 μL reaction mixtures containing 1 μL of bacterial culture, 7 μL of nuclease-free water, 10 μL of DreamTaq HS Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific, USA), and 1 μL of each of the primers at a 10 pmol concentration for each sample. PCR was performed in the following conditions: (1) an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; (2) 30 cycles consisting of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 58 °C for 30 s, and elongation at 72 °C for 60 s; and (3) a final elongation at 72 °C for 5 min. The amplicons were visualized on a 1.5% agarose gel stained with GelRed (Biotium), purified with AMPure XP Reagent (Beckman Coulter, USA) and sequenced by Macrogen (Amsterdam, The Netherlands) using 27 F and 1492R primers.

Pure cultures of endophytic fungi were identified based on the sequence of the internal transcribed spacer 1, the 5.8S rRNA gene, the internal transcribed spacer 2 and the large subunit rDNA region (ITS-LSU). DNA was extracted using cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) as described by [41] and with DNA Mini Kit (Syngen, PL). The ITS-LSU rDNA region was amplified with ITS1F [42] and LR5 primers [43]. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed in 25 μL reaction mixtures containing 20 ng of DNA matrix, 9.5 μL of nuclease-free water, 12.5 μL of DreamTaq HS Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific, US), and 1 μL of each of the primers at a 10 pmol concentration for each sample. The amplicons were visualized on a 1.5% agarose gel stained with GelRed (Biotium), purified with AMPure XP Reagent (Beckman Coulter, USA) and sequenced by Macrogen (Amsterdam, The Netherlands) using ITS1F and LR5 primers.

The sequences were edited using Geneious Prime (www.geneious.com) and compared with sequences published in the NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information) database (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) using the BLASTn algorithm. Sequences were deposited in the NCBI database under accession numbers listed in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3.

Bacterial and fungal amplicons metabarcoding

For the metabarcoding of seeds, the homogenates (n = 8) prepared for isolation of fungi and bacteria were used. Seed homogenate (100 µL) was thawed out and incubated in 700 µL of CTAB at 65 °C for 75 min [39]. Forty-five minutes into the process, the sample was treated with 12 U proteinase K for 30 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 95 °C for 10 min. After incubation, the sample was centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 60 min at room temperature (RT). The supernatant was transferred to a new tube, and the pellet was treated with 700 µL of CTAB, incubated at 65 °C for 60 min and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 60 min at RT. Subsequently, the supernatant was treated with 700 µL of chloroform and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 30 min at RT (twice). Subsequently, the supernatant was transferred to 600 µL of ice-cold 2-propanol and incubated at − 80 °C overnight. DNA was centrifuged at 15,000 × g at 4 °C for 60 min. The pelleted nucleic acids were washed twice with 80% ethanol, dried at 55 °C in a laminar hood, and suspended in 50 µL of TE buffer. The purity and concentration of DNA were quantified spectrophotometrically.

DNA isolation from soil samples was performed as described in our publications [39, 44].

Amplicons were generated in two-step PCR with primers 799f AACMGGATTAGATACCCKG [45] and 1193r ACGTCATCCCCACCTTCC [46] specific for bacterial V5–V7 16S rDNA region and with primers ITS1F CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA [42] and ITS2 GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC [47] specific for ITS1 region with the barcodes. PCR reactions were carried out with 15 μL of Phusion® High—Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs); 0.2 μM of forward and reverse primers, and 10 ng DNA template. PCR was performed in the following conditions: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 50 °C for 30 s, and elongation at 72 °C for 30 s and final elongation at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were visualised in agarose gel and purified with Universal DNA Purification Kit (TianGen, China). DNA libraries were generated using NEB Next® Ultra™ II FS DNA PCR-free Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, USA) following manufacturer’s instruction. The library was checked with Qubit and real-time PCR for quantification and bioanalyzer for size distribution detection. Quantified libraries were pooled and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (2 × 250 bp paired end) by Novogene (UK). Sequencing depth was 100,000 reads per sample.

Bioinformatics analysis pipeline and processing of NGS data

Paired-end reads were merged using FLASH [48] and quality filtering on the raw tags was performed using the fastp software [49]. Chimera sequences were detected and removed with the UCHIME algorithm [50]. Then effective tags were denoised with DADA2 using QIIME2 software [51]. Species annotation of the obtained initial amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) was performed with the UNITE database [52] for fungi and the Silva database for bacteria [53]. Mitochondrial and chloroplast sequences were removed. ASVs were rarefied (to 20,699 reads for fungi and 10,180 reads for bacteria), filtered for very low abundance, and ASVs with a relative abundance of less than 0.01% and a frequency of one sample in the study object were removed from further analysis.

The observed ASVs were used to estimate alpha diversity. Bray–Curtis distance was calculated for beta diversity. To visualize dissimilarities, principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) was performed on the log2 transformed data of ASV tables using Bray–Curtis distances between all samples in PAST [54]. ANOSIM was coupled with PCoA to compare the mean of ranked dissimilarities between groups to the mean of those within groups. The ANOSIM R value ranges from – 1 to 1 (although usually reported from 0 to 1). A value close to 1 indicates strong dissimilarity between groups, while a value close to 0 indicated similarity between the groups [55] R values are described as follows: 0.75 < R < 1, highly different; 0.5 < R < 0.75, different; 0.25 < R < 0.5, different with some overlap; 0.1 < R < 0.25, similar with some differences or high overlap; R < 0.1, similar [56]. Heatmaps were drawn using the R package superheat (https://cran.r-project.org). The Manhattan distance method and average linkage method were used to generate hierarchical clustering, a method for grouping samples. Venn diagrams were prepared in R to show the relationship in the number of taxa identified in study sites.

Functional analysis of seed and soil microbiota

Fungal functional profiles were inferred using FUNGuild from the ASV table generated via ITS amplicon sequencing [57]. The ASV data were merged with sample metadata. Before analysis, ASV counts were converted to relative abundances within each sample (% of total reads). Multiple assignments and inconsistent category labels (e.g., differing dash styles, duplicated modes) were standardized for uniformity. Guilds present in fewer than three samples were removed, and optional filtering based on confidence rankings was applied. For each functional category, the mean relative abundance per experimental group was computed, and group differences were tested using Kruskal–Wallis tests with Dunn’s post hoc comparisons and Bonferroni correction. Results were visualized as stacked bar plots displaying mean relative abundances per group, highlighting variation and significant differences among the main trophic modes.

Functional predictions for bacterial communities in root and soil samples were inferred using PICRUSt2 [58]. The resulting unstratified KO table was imported into R, merged with the corresponding sample metadata, and converted to relative abundances. Data quality was evaluated by screening for singletons. Alpha diversity (Shannon and Simpson indices) and beta diversity (Bray–Curtis dissimilarity) were computed with the phyloseq and vegan packages. Group differences were tested using Kruskal–Wallis tests for alpha diversity and PERMANOVA for community structure. To detect functional shifts among treatments, Kruskal–Wallis tests with Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment were applied to identify differentially abundant KOs. The 10 most significant KOs were selected, averaged within each group, and transformed into Z-scores to construct a scaled abundance matrix. Heatmaps were generated using the pheatmap package, applying hierarchical clustering to both rows (KO functions) and columns (experimental groups).

Vertical transmission of endophytes

The aim of this experiment was to investigate how TMs change the vertical transmission of endophytes in the three subsequence seed generations. Seeds collected in 2022 and used for microbiome analyses (described earlier) were used as zero generation (G0). Seed generations of F1, F2, and F3 were collected in three series of laboratory experiments carried out in pot cultures. In the first step the concentration of toxic metals for plant watering was optimized. We used a mixture of three metals Zn, Fe, and Cd in relation determined in the Bolesław mine dump [59]. Concentrations of these metals were selected based on experiments described in Supplementary Fig. S3A, B. Finally, concentrations of 2.5 mM of Zn, 2.5 mM of Fe and 0.025 mM of Cd were selected for TM-treated plants. Non TM-adapted populations (AL, KR, PO) were examined under TM and TM-free supplementation. As a control TM-adapted populations (BO, BY, TG) were grown in TM-polluted soil.

The seeds were provided for surface sterilization according to the following protocol: 4% sodium hypochloride for 4 min, 96% ethanol for 2 min, 70% ethanol for 4 min, followed by three times (3 × 30 s) rinsing in deionized water. For the germination, seeds were sieved in sterile (121 °C for 60 min followed by 100 °C for 60 min) soil (Aro, PL), vernalized in 4 °C for 48 h and transferred to the growth room (day/night temperature 22/17 °C, illumination 150 µmol × m2 × s−1, photoperiod 16 h). After 14 days, the plants were transferred to the pots (vol 200 mL) with a mixture of sterilized (121 °C for 60 min followed by 100 °C for 60 min) sand and perlite (v:v, 1:1) and grown at the same conditions. Plants were divided into two groups TM-treated (all populations) and non-TM-treated (non-TM-adapted populations only). Plants were watered with Hoagland solution (25 mL) every 2–3 days. Once a week TM-treated plants were watered with Hoagland solution supplemented with mixture of TMs (2.5 mM of Zn, 2.5 mM of Fe and 0.025 mM of Cd). At the same time control plants were watered with Hoagland solution without TMs. Seeds were collected successively to obtain the required amount of the seeds. Seeds collected from F1 generation were used for set up the next experiment (F2) and F2 seeds were used to obtain plants for seed F3 generation. Seeds of each generation were used for bacterial amplicon sequencing analysis according to protocol presented above. For each object 6 samples (1 sample = 25 mg of seeds) were provided (3 generations × 9 populations × 6 samples = 144 samples). NGS data were analysed according to the above description.

Preparation of endophytes for plant inoculation

Endophytes isolated from the seeds were used to investigate their effect on plant growth. Pure cultures of filamentous fungi were grown on PDA medium at 26 °C in darkness for 7 days. Yeasts were cultured in liquid 2% malt extract at 26 °C for 72 h; cells were then harvested by centrifugation (7000 × g) and washed twice with 10 mM MgSO4. Bacterial strains were cultured in NA medium at 28 °C for 24 h, harvested by centrifugation and washed twice with 10 mM MgSO4.

For single-strain plant inoculation in vitro, a mycelial plug was placed onto the medium between plant roots for filamentous fungi; for both yeasts and bacteria, plant inoculation was performed by smearing microbial colonies directly onto the plant roots.

For in vitro tests with synthetic communities (SynComs), fungal SynComs were prepared by mixing mycelial plugs of different species and homogenizing them with a laboratory knife. This mixed inoculum was then placed onto the medium between plant roots. For yeasts and bacterial SynComs, two mL of washed liquid culture (OD600 = 0.5) were mixed and centrifuged. Plants were subsequently inoculated by smearing the roots with an inoculation loop. When the plant was inoculated with complete SynComs (bacteria + fungi), filamentous inoculum was placed between plant roots and yeasts and bacterial inoculum were mixed and smeared onto roots.

For pot experiments, fungal SynComs were prepared as for the in vitro tests, and the homogenized mycelia were suspended in 35 mL of 10 mM MgSO4. For yeasts and bacterial SynComs, 2 mL of washed liquid culture (OD600 = 0.5) were mixed. The bacterial and fungal inocula were then mixed, filled up to 350 mL with deionized water, and 5 mL of the final suspension was used to inoculate each plant by adding the inoculum to the soil.

TM tolerance index of bacteria

TM tolerance index of bacteria was tested in liquid cultures on LB medium supplemented with 0.5 mM Zn, 0.5 mM Fe and 0.005 mM Cd. Growth of the bacteria (n = 3) was determined after 24 h by determination of optical density at 600 nm.

Plant in vitro cultures

Surface sterilized (according to protocol described in vertical transmission experiment) seeds were germinated on ¼ strength Murashige-Skoog (MS) medium (Duchefa) supplemented with 0.8% sucrose. Initially, seeds were vernalized for 48 h at 4 °C in darkness, next they were transferred to a growth chamber for 10 days under controlled conditions (day/night temperature 22/17 °C, illumination 150 µmol m2 s−1, humidity 50%, photoperiod 16 h). After 10 days, seedlings were transferred on Strullu Romand (MSR) medium (Duchefa) supplemented with 0.256 g L−1 Ca(NO3)2 and 6 g L−1 phytoagar (Duchefa). For experiments with toxic metals, growth MSR medium was supplemented with 0.5 µM Zn, 0.5 µM Fe, 0.005 µM Cd. For inoculation with filamentous fungi, the mycelial plug was placed between the seedlings. For yeasts and bacteria, roots were smeared with the microbial culture. Plants were harvested 19–21 days after initial seed sterilization.

Seed extract preparation

For seed extract preparation, seeds from AL and PO populations were pooled to obtain extract 1, whereas seeds from BO and BY populations were pooled to obtain extract 6. Seeds were surface-sterilized in sterile deionized water for 1 min, 70% ethanol for 30 s, 1% NaOCl with Tween 20 for 1 min, 70% ethanol for 30 s, and rinsed five times with sterile water. Sterilized seeds were homogenized in 200 µL of 10 mM MgSO₄ using a sterile micropestle, and the resulting seed extracts were applied directly on to the plant root surface.

Pot experiments

Seeds were surface sterilized according to protocol described in vertical transmission experiment and sown into a mixture of sterile garden soil (supplied by Aro, Poland) and sand (1:1; v;v). Soil was sterilized at 121 °C for 1 h followed by 100 °C for 1 h. Seeds were vernalized in darkness in 4 °C for 48 h and germinated in growth room for 12 days under controlled conditions (day/night temperature 22/17 °C, illumination 150 µmol m2 s−1, photoperiod 16 h). Next, the seedlings were transferred to pots (volume of 40 mL) filled with a mixture of sterile sand and perlite (1:1; v:v) and grown up to 4 weeks. Plants were inoculated 2 days after transfer into pots. Plants were watered with Hoagland’s solution every 2–3 days. TMs treatments were watered once a week with toxic metals (2.5 mM Zn, 2.5 mM Fe and 0.025 mM Cd). Plants were harvested 14 days after inoculation.

Persistence of beneficial fungal traits under toxic metal stress

We observed that S. ruberrimus OP324 and C. cladosporioides OP328 isolated from not TM-adapted seeds were beneficial for plants, in contrast to the TM-adapted strains of the same (or closely related) species. Thus, we attempted to investigate if culturing these strains under TM conditions causes a loss in their ability to promote plant growth. We cultured the not TM-adapted strains on PDA medium supplemented with toxic metals (0.5 mM Zn, 0.5 mM Fe and 0.005 mM Cd). Cultures were passaged every week for 2 months and were then used to inoculate A. arenosa plants in vitro (populations BO and BY). Cultures not treated with TMs were used as a control. Plant fresh biomass was investigated.

Effect of Pantoea strains on the seed germination

We observed a limited presence of Pantoea bacteria in seeds from A. arenosa populations adapted to TMs. Only one out of 27 strains originated from TM-exposed plants. This led us to investigate the impact of these strains on the A. arenosa growth under TM and in the reference conditions. Our initial findings indicated that Pantoea was only beneficial in TM-free environment. To further understand their role, we investigated the effect of these strains on the germination of A. arenosa seeds. Fifty seeds were surface sterilized, imbibed in bacterial culture for 6 h, and subsequently sown on sterile Aro soil. Daily observations were conducted to record the number of germinated seeds.

Results

Toxic metal deposition limits the biodiversity of soil microbiota

Toxic metal concentrations in mine dump soils were significantly higher than in the non-polluted sites (Table 1). In mine dumps zinc concentration ranged from 8.94 to 23.10 g·kg−1, cadmium—from 0.34 to 1.03 mg·kg−1, and lead—from 99.8 to 940 mg·kg−1. These concentrations were significantly higher compared to the non-polluted sites (Zn 0.03–0.08 g·kg−1, Cd 0.01 to 0.02 mg·kg−1, Pb 92.2–104.4 mg·kg−1).

Toxic metals deposited in the soil limited the biodiversity of both soil fungi and bacteria (Fig. 2A, E). The number of fungal ASVs in TM-free sites ranged from 245 to 259 and was higher than in TM sites, where it reached 91–164 ASVs (Fig. 2A). Differential response of seed endophytes to TMs: fungal stability vs bacterial sensitivity

In contrast to the soil, the TM pollution did not limit the diversity of fungal endophytes in A. arenosa seeds (Fig. 3A). The observed number of fungal ASVs in seeds from TM sites (ranging from 72 to 154) was comparable to that in seeds from TM-free sites (61–139). Out of 239 fungal taxa identified, 132 (55%) were shared between seeds from both types of sites, while 65 (27%) were specific to TM and 42 (18%) were specific to TM-free sites (Fig. 3B). PCoA revealed an overlap between the two fungal communities (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, ANOSIM (R = 0.129; p = 0.004) indicated that the communities were similar with some differences (Fig. 3C). The similarity was further supported by the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity index and clearly illustrated by sample distribution on heatmap (Supplementary Fig. S6). Among the top 30 fungal genera identified in seeds from both types of environments, significant differences were observed for only one, Cladosporium. Its relative abundance decreased from 39% in TM-free sites to 24% in TM sites (Fig. 3D). Despite this decline, Cladosporium and Alternaria remained the two dominant genera, with Alternaria being more abundant in both environments (44% in TM-free, 31% in TM).

In contrast, the observed number of bacterial ASVs in the seeds in TM sites (ranging from 45 to 147) was lower than in TM-free sites (79–186) (Fig. 3E). Functional analysis of seed and soil microbiota

To determine whether the compositional shifts observed in seeds and soils were associated with changes in their putative functional potential, we inferred fungal trophic guilds using FUNGuild and bacterial functional profiles using PICRUSt2. These taxonomy- and phylogeny-based inferences should be interpreted with caution, as amplicon sequencing does not directly measure microbial gene content, gene expression, or metabolic activity. Their accuracy depends on the completeness and representativeness of reference databases and may be limited by functional variation among closely related species and strains. Accordingly, the inferred profiles presented here represent putative functional potential and should primarily be considered hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of actual microbial functions [60]. FUNGuild analysis indicated that metal contamination drives a pronounced reorganization of trophic functions essential for nutrient cycling and plant-microorganism interactions. TM soils were strongly depleted in saprotrophic and symbiotrophic guilds and showed a relative increase in pathotroph-associated groups compared with TM-free soils, indicating a disruption of microbiota functionality. In contrast, seed-associated guilds were less strongly reshaped by metal exposure, suggesting that the seed niche partially buffers against environmental filtering. However, even these subtler seed-level changes involved a loss of beneficial guilds, potentially reducing the pool of vertically transmitted mutualists and weakening early plant–microbe interactions (Fig. 4A). Taken together, these results show that metal pollution alters not only the taxonomic structure of fungal communities but also their functional potential, with the strongest effects observed in soil.

Functional analysis of seed and soil microbiota. A Mean relative abundances of fungal trophic guilds in soils and seeds from TM-free and TM locations. B Predicted bacterial KEGG functional profiles (PICRUSt) in the same sample groups

To assess whether similar pollution-driven functional shifts occur in bacterial communities, we performed PICRUSt2 (Fig. 4B). PICRUSt2-based predictions revealed that toxic metal (TM) exposure markedly reshaped the metabolic potential of both seed- and soil-associated microbiomes, with the strongest effects observed in soils. Toxic metal exposure was associated with a depletion of many central metabolic functions in seed-associated communities. In seeds from TM populations, the predicted abundances of genes involved in central carbon metabolism and energy generation, including malate dehydrogenase (mdh), components of the succinate dehydrogenase complex (sdhA–C, frdB/C) and multiple NADH:quinone oxidoreductase subunits (nuoB–M), were consistently reduced, indicative of suppression of the tricarboxylic acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation. Biosynthetic functions that support growth were also diminished: predicted levels of nucleotide-processing (pcnB, purL), amino-acid synthesis (trpC, metB) and fatty-acid/carboxylation machinery (accA, accC, acyP) were lower in TM seeds, suggesting limited anabolic activity under metal stress. By contrast, TM-free communities showed relatively higher representation of carbohydrate-degrading and aromatic-amino-acid biosynthetic functions (pullulanase, aroF/G/H), consistent with a less-stressed, more growth-oriented metabolic state. These patterns, a marked reduction in respiratory and biosynthetic gene potential in TM-exposed seeds with TM-free samples enriched for degradative and anabolic functions, point to a pronounced metabolic down-regulation in seed microbiota under metal exposure. However, these predictions do not demonstrate metabolic down-regulation or reduced microbial activity and require validation using direct functional approaches, such as shotgun metagenomics, metatranscriptomics, metabolomics, or targeted experimental assays.

Vertical transmission confirms differential endophyte responses to TMs

Following the evaluation of microbial communities of soil and seeds from plants growing in their natural environments, we conducted an experiment under controlled conditions to verify these results. We expected that supplementation of the growth substrate: sand:perlite (1:1, v/v) with Fe, Zn, and Cd in a ratio found in Bolesław mine dump (for concentrations refer to MM) will (1) reduce the number of bacterial endophytes in plant seeds, and (2) particularly affect the susceptible taxa. To verify this, we cultured three successive generations (F1–F3) of plants, both reference and metal-adapted, in a substrate amended with toxic metals. We applied metal treatments to initially metal-free (reference) populations, using two controls: untreated reference plants and metal treated metal-adapted plants. Finally, we performed amplicon V5–V7 16S rDNA sequencing for F2 and F3 seeds. Since the seed fungal community of plants growing in polluted soil exhibited remarkable stability, we did not analyse the intergenerational dynamics of the seed mycobiota.

We found a very clear decrease in the number of bacterial ASVs in the seeds of F2 and F3 generations. Specifically, the number of taxa decreased by 36% in F2 and 57% in F3, compared to non-TM treated populations (Fig. 5A). TM exposure had no detectable effect on the beta diversity of F2 generation seeds (p = 0.67), whereas a significant impact on bacterial communities emerged in the F3 generation (p = 0.0167) (Fig. 5B, C). Furthermore, these communities became similar to the bacterial communities in the seeds from TM populations treated with TM (p = 0.1601) (Fig. 5C). In the F2 generation seeds the genus-level profiles remained stable across metal treatments: TM-free plants grown on non-polluted soil (TM-free/TM–), TM-free plants on polluted soil (TM-free/TM +), and TM-adapted plants on polluted soil (TM/TM +). Each treatment was co-dominated by unclassified taxa (15–23%), Escherichia–Shigella (6–27%), and Cutibacterium (9–17%), with consistent contributions from Bacillus, Pseudomonas, Comamonas, and Stenotrophomonas. No directional shifts in relative abundance were observed in response to direct metal exposure in F2 (Fig. 5D).

By the F3 generation, metal treatment began to leave a clear signature on community composition. Bacillus dominated across all conditions, comprising 10.9% in TM-free plants on TM-, 30.0% in TM-free on TM +, and 41.1% in TM-adapted plants on TM + (TM/TM +), suggesting potential resistance to metal exposure. TM exposure induced also abundance of Escherichia-Shigella in the seeds of TM-free populations from 7.5 to 11.6% and Cutibacterium from 13.7 to 18.5%. Decreased abundance upon TM exposure was shown for Pantoea (drop from 4.2 to 0.8%), Corynebacterium (drop from 4.3 to 1.3%) and Halomonas (drop from 2.5 to 0.5%) (Fig. 5E). These findings indicate that the seed bacterial endophytes become increasingly affected by toxic metals over successive generations. Overall, the laboratory findings closely mirrored those observed in the field.

Fungal, but not bacterial seed endophytes promote A. arenosa growth under TM

Arabidopsis arenosa plants collected from reference, non-polluted sites (TM-free) exhibited greater resistance to metal toxicity compared to those originating from post-mining waste areas. Shoot fresh mass (Fig. 6A) and main root length (Fig. 6B) decreased under toxic metal supplementation in both TM-free and TM-site populations. However, populations from metal-contaminated sites showed lower growth under toxic metal exposure than TM-free populations. These findings, together with evidence showing the detrimental impact of toxic metals on seed endophytic microbial communities, suggest that the increased sensitivity of TM‐site populations may stem from the loss of key microbiota components following prolonged metal exposure.

To test this possibility, we used two complementary seed-derived inoculation approaches. First, we prepared crude seed extracts from TM-free and TM-site populations, which were expected to contain also microorganisms not recovered in culture. Second, we assembled two defined synthetic microbial communities (SynComs) composed of culturable bacterial and fungal strains isolated from seeds of TM-free populations (SynCom 1) and TM-adapted populations (SynCom 6). The SynComs used in our experiment were assembled from microbial isolates obtained from A. arenosa seeds collected from all reference and post-mining sites investigated in this study (for molecular identification of fungi, bacteria and SynCom composition see Supplementary Tables S1, S2, and S3, respectively). In total, 26 fungal and 28 bacterial taxa were isolated and identified from seeds collected from both TM-free and TM-polluted sites (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Experiments were first conducted in vitro and subsequently verified in pot cultures. In the initial in vitro assay, we inoculated A. arenosa plants from two TM populations (BY, BO) with seed extracts and independently with seed-derived SynComs and assessed their effects on plant growth under metal toxicity. This design enabled us to test whether seed-derived microbial fractions and culturable synthetic communities originating from TM-free and TM-site populations differed in their ability to support host growth under metal stress. In contrast to the defined SynCom 1, neither seed extract enhanced plant fresh weight under metal stress across repeated experimental attempts; plants treated with extract 1 or extract 6 showed biomass values comparable to the uninoculated control (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S7). In the same experimental system, SynCom 1 significantly enhanced plant growth, increasing biomass production of the BO and BY populations by 82% and 79%, respectively (Fig. 6D, E), whereas SynCom 6 had no detectable effect on plant growth. To verify the results, we performed a follow-up pot experiment (sand:perlite 1:1, v/v spiked with Fe, Zn, and Cd as described previously). Consistent with the in vitro results, inoculation with SynCom1 significantly increased BO plant biomass by 188%, whereas SynCom6 failed to enhance growth (Fig. 6F, G).

Because bacteria and filamentous fungi often engage in competitive interkingdom interactions that shape root microbiome assembly and suppress one another’s activities [61] we separated these groups in our inoculation design. In vitro–grown BO and BY plants were exposed to the following treatments: (1) a complete SynCom 1 (bacteria + fungi, SynCom1_BF); (2) a complete SynCom 6 (SynCom6_BF); (3) only bacteria from SynCom 1 (SynCom1_B); (4) only bacteria from SynCom 6 (SynCom6_B); (5) only fungi from SynCom1 (SynCom1_F), (6) only fungi from SynCom 6 (SynCom6_F). We found that only the complete SynCom 1 and surprisingly, its fungal fraction significantly enhanced plant growth under metal stress (Fig. 7A, B, Supplementary Fig. S8). In contrast, bacterial fractions did not promote plant growth in this experimental system, suggesting that the growth-promoting effect of SynCom 1 was mainly associated with its fungal component. The lack of a comparable effect of SynCom 6 and its subfractions indicates that seed microorganisms recovered from TM-adapted populations were less effective in promoting plant growth under the tested conditions. However, this result should be interpreted in relation to the culturable fraction examined here and does not exclude the presence of beneficial microorganisms in the native seed microbiota of TM populations. Together, these findings suggest that long-term metal exposure is associated not only with changes in seed microbial community composition, but also with differences in the growth-promoting potential of culturable seed endophytes. Our results show that fungal seed communities were less strongly affected by toxic metal exposure than bacterial communities, as indicated by the relatively stable fungal diversity. However, functional assays revealed that this stability did not necessarily translate into plant growth promotion. In our functional assays, culturable fungi isolated from seeds of plants growing in metal-free sites promoted plant growth under metal stress, whereas this effect was not observed for fungi isolated from metal-adapted populations under the tested conditions. This suggests that the growth-promoting potential of culturable fungal endophytes may depend on their population of origin. Nevertheless, further studies are needed to determine whether these differences are caused by long-term metal exposure, natural strain-level variation, or other ecological factors. Among the 26 fungal endophytes identified, three taxa—Alternaria sp., Penicillium sp., and Sporobolomyces ruberrimus were shared between both site types (Supplementary Fig. S2). Fourteen fungal taxa were unique to TM-site seeds, while nine were specific to the reference populations. In the case of bacterial taxa, 3 out of 28 were shared between TM and TM-free seeds; 14 were TM-specific, and 11 were TM-free-specific. TM populations were dominated by Bacillus and related taxa, whereas TM-free populations were enriched in Pantoea and Pseudomonas. These results were convergent with NGS results, highlighting a strong divergence in seed microbiome composition between metal-polluted and unpolluted sites (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Table S4).

Interestingly, although S. ruberrimus and C. pseudocladosporioides (closely related to C. cladosporioides) were also present in SynCom 6 (as strains OR313 and OR321, respectively), they did not confer any growth-promoting effects in this experimental setup (Fig. 8A). Both strains were repeatedly passaged for 2 months on medium supplemented with Zn, Fe, and Cd. Following this treatment, the strains were used to inoculate plants from two TM populations (BO and BY). Despite the chronic metal exposure, both strains preserved their ability to enhance plant growth under TM stress conditions (Fig. 8B, Supplementary Fig. S9.).

Loss of seed-associated Pantoea in TM-environments is not linked to metal sensitivity

Seeds from plants growing in non-polluted environments were dominated by Pantoea species, which were notably reduced in seeds from toxic metal (TM)-rich sites. TM concentration in the seeds from TM-free populations was significantly lower than in the seed from TM sites (Supplementary Fig. S11). We verified the susceptibility of Pantoea to metal toxicity in a laboratory setup, showing that its intergenerational transmission is severely limited under metal stress. This trend was also reflected in culturable isolates: 27 out of 49 bacterial strains isolated from reference seeds belonged to Pantoea, while only 1 out of 28 isolates from TM-site seeds were members of this genus (Supplementary Table S3). These observations suggested a potential sensitivity of Pantoea to metal toxicity. However, in vitro tests on metal-supplemented media showed that all tested Pantoea strains were resistant to Zn, Fe, and Cd (Supplementary Fig. S10), indicating that their decline in TM environments is not due to direct metal toxicity.

To explore their functional role, we assessed the plant growth-promoting potential of Pantoea strains in both control and TM-supplemented conditions. Under control conditions, approximately half of the strains significantly enhanced plant growth (Fig. 9A). In contrast, none of the strains improved plant performance when toxic metals were present (Fig. 9B). We also evaluated their effects on seed germination in soil without TM-supplementation. Interestingly, germination-promoting activity did not always correlate with growth promotion, some strains enhanced germination without affecting later growth, and vice versa (Fig. 9C).

Together, these findings suggest that Pantoea strains may contribute to plant growth under non-stress conditions, but this beneficial effect is not maintained under metal stress in our assays, despite their in vitro metal tolerance. This indicates that metal toxicity may affect the outcome of the A. arenosa–Pantoea interaction, potentially weakening an otherwise beneficial plant–bacterial association. This also raises questions about the persistence of Pantoea in metal-contaminated soils and whether its close association with Arabidopsis may contribute to this phenomenon. Further work is required to determine the mechanisms involved and to assess the relevance of this interaction for plant adaptation in metal-rich habitats.

Discussion

This study provides new insight into how environmental pollution shapes plant adaptation by altering the composition and function of the seed microbiota. Our findings show that bacteria are significantly more susceptible to metal stress, as reflected in pronounced changes in both alpha and beta diversity. In contrast, fungal communities appear more resilient, exhibiting only minor structural shifts. Nevertheless, even subtle changes may result in the loss of microbial traits that support optimal plant growth. Furthermore, we identified that bacteria of the genus Pantoea, the most abundant genus in A. arenosa populations from unpolluted environments, were significantly reduced in seeds from polluted environments. Most Pantoea strains we analyzed possessed plant growth-promoting traits, suggesting that their reduced abundance in metal-exposed seeds may be relevant for plant performance. Together, these findings indicate that metal stress may weaken otherwise beneficial interactions between A. arenosa and Pantoea, potentially contributing to reduced plant performance in metal-contaminated habitats.

The impact of toxic metal pollution on seed microbiota remains poorly understood, and the few available studies suggest that responses may be strongly host- and habitat-dependent [62, 63]. For example, Awais et al. [64] reported increased seed microbial diversity in Zea mays from highly polluted soils. In contrast, in our study metal contamination was associated with reduced bacterial diversity in A. arenosa seeds, with fungal diversity remaining largely unchanged. We also observed a shift in bacterial community composition: Ralstonia, Flavobacterium, Mycobacterium, Pseudonocardia, and Lactobacillus were more abundant among bacteria in seed of TM populations. Some of these genera have previously been reported in plant-associated microbiomes exposed to metal stress; for example, Flavobacterium, Mycobacterium, and Pseudonocardia were linked to enhanced cadmium tolerance in Arabidopsis thaliana seeds [65–67]. However, because our results are based on amplicon sequencing, they should not be interpreted as direct evidence of microbial function. Similarly, FUNGuild and PICRUSt2 analyses were used only as exploratory tools to generate hypotheses about potential functional shifts associated with the observed community changes. These analyses suggested relatively limited changes in predicted fungal guilds and possible changes in bacterial metabolic potential under metal exposure, but these predictions require validation by metagenomic, metatranscriptomic, or targeted functional assays. In summary, heavy metal contamination exerted a strong selective pressure on seed-associated bacteria, reducing their diversity and possibly favoring taxa with known metal resistance or stress-tolerance traits. Fungal seed communities, by contrast, retained their diversity under metal stress, though composition shifted subtly, with a reduction in Cladosporium. This divergence suggests that bacterial endophytes, many of which are environmentally acquired generalists, are more sensitive to environmental filtering by toxic metals, while fungi may maintain community stability through traits such as metal sequestration in hyphae. Our experiment concerning the influence of metal toxicity on vertical transmission of endophytes, confirms environmental observations.

As seed microbiota are inherited across generations, they are expected to be well-adapted to the host niche, potentially contributing more effectively to host survival than later-acquired, horizontally transmitted microbes (reviewed by [68]). In our study, only the synthetic community composed of microorganisms isolated from seeds of the toxic metal–free population (SynCom1) was effective in supporting plant growth under metal stress. Notably, crude seed extracts, which were expected to represent a broader fraction of seed-associated microorganisms, did not reproduce the growth-promoting effect of SynCom1, further suggesting that this response is depended on specific culturable community members and/or defined microbial interactions rather than on microbial complexity alone. The growth-promoting effect observed only for SynCom 1 suggests that some endophytes from non-metalliferous populations possess traits that can promote plant growth under metal stress, despite originating from environments without long-term metal exposure. An important question is why comparable growth-promoting effects were not observed for the SynCom assembled from seeds of metal-adapted populations. One possibility is that prolonged metal exposure affects the persistence, transmission, or activity of selected beneficial microorganisms in seeds. However, our cultivation-based approach captured only a subset of the seed microbiota, and we cannot exclude that beneficial taxa were present in TM seeds but were not recovered under the culture conditions used here. Therefore, the weaker effect of SynCom 6 should be interpreted as a difference within the culturable fraction examined in this study, rather than as evidence for the complete absence of beneficial microorganisms from the seed microbiota of metal-contaminated populations. Further culture-independent and functional studies will be needed to determine whether toxic metals influence the vertical transmission or activity of beneficial seed endophytes and whether this contributes to reduced plant performance in polluted habitats. Endophytes originating from environments not historically exposed to toxic metals might also represent a broader and more diverse genetic pool of beneficial traits. In contrast, populations continually exposed to metals may converge on a narrower set of adaptive strategies, whereas “naive” populations could harbour novel or more strong determinants for metal tolerance that have not yet been subjected to selection in polluted environments. Moreover, adaptation to chronic metal stress can entail trade-offs, where resources or genetic potential are allocated to resilience mechanisms at the expense of other plant-beneficial traits such as growth promotion or broad-spectrum stress tolerance. Endophytes from metal-naive populations may avoid these costs, retaining their full range of beneficial functions that can indirectly or directly contribute to plant metal tolerance. We attempted to experimentally reduce the ability of two fungal strains from unpolluted populations, Sporobolomyces ruberrimus and Cladosporium pseudocladosporoides, to enhance plant growth under metal toxicity by repeatedly culturing them on media containing toxic metals. These attempts were unsuccessful, possibly because the duration of exposure was insufficient or because the underlying mechanisms are more complex than simple adaptive loss. This aspect requires further investigation.

These observations have practical implications for strategies in phytoremediation. Rather than focusing exclusively on endophytes from contaminated sites, screening strains from diverse, even unpolluted environments may reveal microorganisms with higher potential to enhance plant tolerance and metal uptake for phytoextraction, or to immobilize metals for phytostabilization. The beneficial effects in our experiment appeared to be driven more by fungal constituents than by bacterial ones. This may reflect the generally higher resilience of fungi to metal toxicity compared with bacteria, a pattern also observed for soil microorganisms [69–71].

In our study, seeds from toxic metal-adapted plant populations showed a pronounced alteration in bacterial community composition, most notably a sharp reduction in the relative abundance of Pantoea. This is of particular interest because Pantoea agglomerans has been widely reported as a plant growth-promoting bacteria capable of supporting host tolerance to heavy metal stress. It can act through multiple mechanisms, including the synthesis of phytohormones, secretion of siderophores that improve nutrient acquisition, and biosorption of metals from the surrounding environment [72]. Genomic studies have further revealed that Pantoea species possess genes associated with metal resilience, such as multidrug efflux pumps and copper/silver transport systems [73], which may support their ability to survive in metal-rich soils and reduce heavy metal stress in plants. In our experiments, Pantoea strains enhanced plant growth only under control conditions without toxic metal supplementation, consistent with their reduced abundance in seeds of TM populations and in seeds of TM-free population grown under TM supplementation in vertical transmission experiment. This reduced growth-promoting effect cannot be attributed to an overall lack of metal tolerance, as both metal-sensitive and metal-resistant strains were present among those tested. The reason why Pantoea isolates failed to improve plant growth under metal toxicity remains unclear and needs further investigation.

Conclusions

Our findings reveal that long-term exposure to toxic metals exerts strong selective pressure on seed-associated microbiota, markedly reducing bacterial diversity and reshaping community composition while leaving fungal diversity largely unaffected. Fungal endophytes isolated from metal-free populations, particularly within synthetic community, proved more effective in promoting plant growth under metal stress than those from metalliferous populations, suggesting that intrinsic strain-level traits, rather than prior environmental adaptation, are key to enhancing host tolerance. This challenges the assumption that endophytes from contaminated sites are inherently better suited for phytoremediation and highlights the potential of “naive” microbial sources. The greater and more consistent benefits observed for fungal constituents point to their generally higher metal resilience and functional stability. Together, these results expand our understanding of vertical transmission, microbial adaptation, and host–microbe interactions under heavy metal stress, and underscore the value of broadening the search for effective endophytes beyond contaminated habitats.
