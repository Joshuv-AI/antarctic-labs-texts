Protocol for fluorescence-activated mitochondria sorting in endoplasmic reticulum-mitochondria contact reporter cells
Authors: Drew C Stark, Brandon Chen, Kayla Hickman, Costas A Lyssiotis, Yatrik M Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMitochondria exchange metabolites bidirectionally with the endoplasmic reticulum to maintain bioenergetic homeostasis and respond to cellular stress. Here, we describe a protocol for generating cells that contain a split-GFP contact-site reporter of ER-mitochondria interactions and the sorting of heterogeneous mitochondrial populations based on fluorescence intensity. This approach enables reproducible functional profiling of mitochondrial subpopulations, validated by proteomic analysis demonstrating the enrichment of endoplasmic reticulum-associated proteins in GFP-positive mitochondria.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Chen et al.1Subject areas: Cell Biology, Cell separation/fractionation, Flow Cytometry, Metabolism

Before you begin

The protocol below outlines specific steps for using SW480 colorectal adenocarcinoma and HT1080 fibrosarcoma cell lines. However, the protocol can be generalized to any ER-mitochondria Split GFP based contact site (SPLICSSER/MT, hereafter referred to as SPLICS) reporter cell lines.

1.Obtain cell lines of interest.

Obtain cell lines of interest.

Note: Obtain low passage cell lines to generate stable SPLICS reporter cells from a reliable source such as the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC).

2.Perform G418/Geneticin kill curve.

Perform G418/Geneticin kill curve.

Note: Different cell lines have varying susceptibility to the G418 antibiotic. It is important to identify the minimal dose necessary to eliminate all cells without neomycin resistance after 7–14 days of culture.

3.Obtain and sequence SPLICS plasmids.

Obtain and sequence SPLICS plasmids.

Note: pCMV-HyPBase, pPB-rtTAM2-IresNeo, and pPB-TRE-Dest-SPLICSSER/MT are available upon request from the lead author or from Giamogante, et al.2 Sequence plasmids to confirm fidelity of the piggyBac transposase, rtTA, Neomycin resistance, and SPLICS cassettes. Other iterations of the SPLICSSER/MT plasmid, such as Addgene #164108, can also be used. Stable integration into the genome via this approach or others, such as lentiviral transduction, should also be feasible.

4.Determine maximum DNA tolerance of cell line.

Determine maximum DNA tolerance of cell line.

Note: The degree of toxicity induced by exogenous DNA during transfection can vary by cell line. It is imperative to determine the maximum amount of DNA tolerated by the cell line of interest before beginning to determine the concentrations of each plasmid to be used during transfection.

5.Prepare and label sterile conicals and Eppendorf tubes for cell line generation.

Prepare and label sterile conicals and Eppendorf tubes for cell line generation.

Note: Two 15 mL conicals and four 2 mL Eppendorf tubes are required for cell line generation with the LTX/PLUS lipofectamine transfection system and plasmid controls per cell line. Label Eppendorf tubes according to the step-by-step instructions below.

6.Prepare collection vessels for SPLICS cell sorting.

Prepare collection vessels for SPLICS cell sorting.

Note: After performing antibiotic selection of SPLICS stable cell lines, prepare at least two 96 well plates with 200 μL medium supplemented with 20% FBS and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic to each well. These plates will be used to sort individual GFP positive cells for expansion into monoclonal cell lines. Additionally, prepare 5 mL complete, sterile medium in a 15 mL conical to collect a polyclonal population of GFP positive SPLICS cells per cell line.

7.Prepare Mitochondria Isolation Buffer (MIB).

Prepare Mitochondria Isolation Buffer (MIB).

Note: Follow recipe for MIB under “Materials and equipment” to prepare buffer. Calculate the amount of buffer needed for an experiment. For example, each condition requires 0.5 mL MIB for collection and 4 mL MIB in specified flow tube, for a total of 5 mL MIB when accounting for overage. The protocol also requires 5 mL MIB to prime the flow cytometer.

8.Prepare materials for mitochondria isolation.

Prepare materials for mitochondria isolation.

Note: Per condition, label a 15 mL conical to collect cell suspension and a 5 mL Eppendorf tube to perform cell lysis. Prepare a 3 mL syringe with a 27-gauge needle per condition. Three 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes are required per condition for clearing the nuclear fraction and resuspending the mitochondrial fraction. Additionally, prepare and label a 5 mL polystyrene flow cytometry tube with 4 mL MIB per condition. Cool centrifuges to 4 °C prior to beginning and perform all steps after cell scraping on ice.

9.Ensure flow cytometer configuration.

Ensure flow cytometer configuration.

Note: If your institution has a flow cytometry core, it is strongly recommended to coordinate with core staff to ensure the specific cytometer can detect sub-micron particles for accurate results. Additionally, a 70 μm nozzle is used for mitochondria analysis and sorting. Speak with core staff or a cytometer technician to validate these parameters before beginning.

Innovation

Fluorescently activated mitochondria sorting (FAMS) is a powerful assay for the isolation and profiling of heterogeneous mitochondria subpopulations.3,4 However, mitochondria also associate with other organelles, such as the endoplasmic reticulum, to exchange metabolites and support cellular function.5 Endoplasmic reticulum-mitochondria contact sites (ERMCS) are remodeled in response to various stressors, and we have identified that the degree of ER contact with the mitochondria can vary depending on the type of stress.1 Thus, we have adapted the above protocol to sort mitochondria based on the fluorescence intensity of a split-GFP based contact site reporter (SPLICS).2,6 We used this protocol to identify differences in the proteomes of mitochondria in contact with the ER and those with low ER association in Chen et al.1 This protocol provides a detailed approach for the generation of ER-mitochondria reporter cell lines with the SPLICS reporter and step-by-step instructions for sample preparation, instrument set up, and important controls for mitochondria sorting. By incorporating the use of SPLICS, this procedure allows for systematic analysis of ERMCS heterogeneity across perturbations and cell types.

Step-by-step method details

Cell culture

Timing: 1 h

The steps below are used to routinely maintain cell lines and prepare cells for subsequent experiments in an aseptic environment. Completion of this section is necessary for generation of SPLICS cell lines and preparing FAMS experiments.

CRITICAL: All steps must be performed in a sterile laminar flow hood approved for BSL2 work. Ensure that all materials are sterile before work.

1.Detaching cells.a.Obtain cell line(s) of interest in a 10 cm dish.i.Grow cells to 70–80% confluency.ii.Remove spent medium with gentle aspiration.iii.Wash cells once with 37 °C sterile 1× PBS.iv.Add 1 mL Trypsin-EDTA and tilt dish to saturate cells.v.Incubate at 37 °C 5% CO2 for 5 minutes.vi.Add 4 mL complete medium to deactivate Trypsin.vii.Transfer homogeneous cell suspension to a 15 mL conical.viii.Centrifuge at 300g for 3 minutes to pellet cells.ix.Gently aspirate supernatant.x.Resuspend pellet in 5 mL complete medium with serum.

Generating SPLICS reporter cell lines

Timing: 3–5 weeks

The steps outlined below are used to generate stable cell lines expressing the green fluorescent ER-Mitochondria contact site reporter (SPLICSSER/MT) that will be used for FAMS in later steps. Successful completion of this step results in multiple clones per cell type that fluoresce upon the induction ER-mitochondria proximity (within 8–10 nm distance) by the reconstitution of a split GFP molecule at the ERMCS interface.

CRITICAL: All steps must be performed in a sterile laminar flow hood approved for BSL2 work. Ensure that all materials are sterile and the flow cytometer being used maintains a sterile environment before work.

Timing: Variable

The steps below are specific to identifying a mitochondria population and analyzing or sorting this population of mitochondria via small particle detection on the Bigfoot spectral cell sorter. Successful completion of this protocol results in quantitative data on isolated mitochondria or sorted mitochondria based on SPLICS fluorescence for use in downstream analysis, such as proteomics.

CRITICAL: For sorting and analysis, a 70 μm nozzle is equipped on the flow cytometer as it produces smaller droplet sizes to minimize multiple mitochondria accumulating within the droplet and the ability to achieve a higher sort rate. However, the 70 μm nozzle imparts greater sample pressure and may produce greater droplet drive noise than the 100 μm nozzle. The Bigfoot spectral cell sorter manufacturer describes both nozzles to be suitable for small particle detection, and the user should consider droplet size, sample pressure, and droplet drive noise when considering nozzle diameter.

CRITICAL: For small particle detection using the Bigfoot cell sorter, the use of the 405 nm FSC small particle detector (FSC-SPD), droplet maintenance, and a successful instrument quality control (QC) process are critical. However, other cell sorters may be used (such as the BD FACS Aria III cytometer4) that use thresholding and detectors with high enough sensitivity to resolve the submicron bead populations down to 200 nm.

For FAMS, the critical first step is ensuring the detection of populations that are microns and sub-micrometer in size. Figure 4A shows the resolution of 2 μm, 1 μm, 500 nm, and 200 nm beads using side scatter (SSC) area on the y-axis and forward scatter single particle detector (FSC-SPD) area on the x-axis, after thresholding using FITC as the trigger parameter. Using both scatter parameters to gate for particle size is important, as Figure 4B shows poor resolution at the higher particle sizes when just using the FSC-SPD detector. The size gate is generated around the 200 nm – 2 μm bead range and is used for mitochondria in future analysis.

When starting with FAMS, it is important to perform functional controls to ensure the ability to detect changes in mitochondria biology with the assay. 2 controls worth performing are measuring mitochondria membrane potential using membrane potential dependent mitochondria dyes and mitochondria calcium using Rhod-2 AM dye. Figure 6 shows the expected peak shifts in HT1080 and SW480 SPLICS cell lines expressing mitoTagRFP and stained with the mitochondria membrane potential dependent dye Mitotracker Deep Red for 1 hour, pretreated with or without the mitochondrial protonophore carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP). In each case, FAMS can detect changes in mitochondria membrane potential and matrix calcium when these functions are perturbed, providing confidence in using FAMS for identifying functionally intact populations of mitochondria.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The experiments indicated here are representative of single biological replicates and display relevant fluorescent metrics as histograms to highlight variance. When performing experiments, it is important to perform biological replicates to ensure reproducibility. Parameters of interest should be averaged between replicates and means compared with appropriate statistical assumptions and corrections for multiple hypotheses to test statistical significance.

Quantification of positive and negative proportions of whole cell and mitochondrial populations in flow cytometry experiments is done using FlowJo (v10.10.1) software. Gating must be consistent across all treatments within the same cell line for comparative analysis.

Limitations

Mitochondria isolation by cell fractionation results in crude mitochondria isolates. Figures 6, 7, and 8 illustrate the efficacy of the protocol; however this property of the mitochondria isolate and potential for swarm detection with small particle flow cytometry can affect results. The contribution of swarm detection to the results can be identified by performing serial dilutions of the isolated mitochondria samples, running these samples at a constant flow rate, and recording the event rate. Over 10 fold dilutions, the range of each parameter where event rate decreases linearly with higher fold dilution is that where swarm detection is absent and should be targeted for FAMS.8

Flow cytometry exposes samples to high-pressure conditions during analysis that can be detrimental to the stability of proteins or small molecules of interest. This stability is further compromised by run time requiring samples to be off ice during acquisition and collection during sorting. These factors should be critically assessed when planning experiments for sorting mitochondria subpopulations and appropriate controls included for downstream analysis to ensure the enrichment of mitochondria in the final sample.

The SPLICS reporter used here is specific to short (<10 nm) contact distances between the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria. It is thus limited in identifying mitochondria subpopulations with varying degrees of ER contact at greater distances than 10 nm. Environmental conditions that induce tight contact between the organelles are necessary, and alternative methods (electron microscopy, alternative fluorescent contact site reporters) are important to corroborate findings for conclusions about ERMCS biology. An alternative SPLICS reporter incorporates an extension of a flexible linker domain and can monitor up to 50 nm contact site distances (SPLICSLER/MT). This may also be used similarly as the SPLICSSER/MT version with slight adjustment of the protocol.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Ineffective G418/Geneticin selection of resistant cell clones (related to step 6).

G418/Geneticin, while still a protein synthesis inhibitor, is generally not as potent as puromycin or blasticidin for selection across cell lines. Specifically, effective G418/Geneticin selection relies heavily on actively dividing cells and requires 7–14 days to effectively select resistant cells. When performing an initial kill curve, use G418 concentrations from 200 μg/mL up to 2 mg/mL. Maintain cultures below 80% confluency and identify the dose of G418 that effectively kills all cells between 7–14 days of selection.

Potential solution

•Maintain cells at low confluency. Selection relative to the untransfected control may be ineffective due to cultures that are highly confluent, rendering the antibiotic minimally effective. For some cell lines, it may be necessary to maintain cultures at or below 50% confluency.

Maintain cells at low confluency. Selection relative to the untransfected control may be ineffective due to cultures that are highly confluent, rendering the antibiotic minimally effective. For some cell lines, it may be necessary to maintain cultures at or below 50% confluency.

•Maintain patience with selection; G418/Geneticin selection is unlikely to occur effectively in 3–5 days but rather requires at least 7 days and up to 14 days depending on the cell line of interest. Performing a kill curve prior to starting is critical for planning.

Problem 2

Clonal heterogeneity in SPLICS cell lines (related to step 8f).

We have observed heterogeneity between clonal populations in generated SPLICS cells related to the degree of SPLICS expression. For example, Figure 9 shows a high-expressing clone that exhibits toxicity simply with 100 ng/mL doxycycline, likely due to artificial ER-Mitochondria tethering because of the SPLICS reporter. Figure 10 is a better clone to work with, as it tolerates doxycycline and indicates functional reporter activity based on treatment with fedratinib.

Potential solution

•It is imperative to profile multiple clones for further analysis. Be sure to take at least 15 clones for expansion after sorting and incubation to ensure moving forward with at least one clone that tolerates SPLICS expression and does not exhibit alternative phenotypes with doxycycline induction. Compare SPLICS clones to the parental population of cells when selecting the clone for future work.

Problem 3

Low mitochondrial yield (related to step 11).

The number of mitochondria events collected during sorting can vary greatly. A confluent 10 cm dish of HT1080 cells should yield over 5 million sorted mitochondria, and a confluent 15 cm dish over 15 million. As such, for analyses that require extremely high mitochondria yield (such as proteomics), multiple plates are required. Low yields are typically the result of not enough cells plated at the start of the experiment. It is important to perform appropriate pilot experiments prior to running final experiments to determine the amount of plates necessary when setting the experiment up.

Potential solution

•Identify how many mitochondria can be isolated from 1 confluent 10 or 15 cm dish before planning a full experiment when configuring the flow cytometer for FAMS. This number gives an indication of the amount of mitochondria expected to be isolated per dish.

Problem 4

Identifying and generating size gate for FAMS (related to step 23).

It can be difficult to identify the bead populations corresponding to the 200 nm – 2 μm sizes when first configuring the flow cytometer. This size gate is an imperative first pass to ensure the analysis and sorting of mitochondria from the isolated preparation.

Potential solution

•Run MIB only through the machine first to identify background particle noise. Use the FITC detector as the trigger parameter to resolve the 200 nm bead events above background noise.

Problem 5

Dye fluorescence too low or too bright (related to step 10).

Fluorescent dye fluorescence varies by concentration and between cell types. It is important to identify the appropriate concentration of dye for functional readouts of isolated mitochondria.

Potential solution

•Test multiple dye concentrations during piloting with appropriate controls. For example, we have found 100–200 nM Mitobrilliant646 sufficient for mitochondria staining and 50 nM Mitotracker Deep Red for readout of mitochondria membrane potential.
