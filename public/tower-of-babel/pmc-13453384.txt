Synchronous Metastatic Esophageal Squamous Cell Carcinoma and Acute Myeloid Leukemia: A Case Report
Authors: Youssef Abbas, Mohammad Alaa Aldakak, Hamza Hdeoa, Bassel Ibrahim, Younes Souleiman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSynchronous presentation of solid and hematologic malignancies is uncommon, and coexistence of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) at initial evaluation is exceptionally rare. Cytopenias may be misattributed to malnutrition, chronic disease, or the solid tumor itself.Case PresentationA 57-year-old Syrian man who was a heavy smoker presented with progressive dysphagia, profound weight loss, weakness, and pallor. Endoscopy revealed a circumferential obstructing esophageal lesion. Computed tomography demonstrated a distal esophageal mass extending to the gastric cardia, para-aortic lymphadenopathy, and multiple hepatic lesions radiologically consistent with metastases. Histopathology showed invasive high-grade carcinoma with pseudo–signet ring morphology; mucin staining was negative, and immunohistochemistry supported high-grade ESCC (p40 positive; CK7 and CEA negative). Laboratory testing revealed severe pancytopenia (white blood cell count approximately 0.6 × 109/L, hemoglobin 6.9 g/dL, and platelets approximately 55 × 109/L) with markedly elevated lactate dehydrogenase. Bone marrow evaluation and flow cytometry identified approximately 46% myeloid blasts expressing CD117, CD34, CD33, CD13, myeloperoxidase, HLA-DR, and dim CD25, with aberrant CD7 expression in 31% of cells, establishing the diagnosis of AML. A feeding jejunostomy was placed for nutritional support, and the patient was referred for multidisciplinary management.Case discussionSevere or unexplained cytopenias in patients with esophageal malignancy warrant prompt hematologic evaluation, including bone marrow examination, to exclude a concomitant myeloid neoplasm and guide individualized treatment sequencing.ConclusionSynchronous metastatic ESCC and AML require early multidisciplinary assessment to avoid diagnostic delay and optimize supportive and disease-directed care.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer remains a major global malignancy, with GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates reporting 510,716 new cases and 445,129 deaths worldwide.1 In the Syrian Arab Republic, GLOBOCAN 2024 estimated 152 new esophageal cancer cases and 139 deaths.2 In the same national estimates, leukemia accounted for 1,319 new cases and 876 deaths and was among the more frequently reported cancer groups in Syria, although AML-specific national data remain limited.2 Regionally and globally, the burden of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) varies by age, sex, geography, and sociodemographic context, and recent Global Burden of Disease analyses emphasize the need for country- and region-specific interpretation of AML epidemiology.3

Alcohol drinking and cigarette smoking are established risk factors for esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), and their joint effect has been reported as synergistic rather than additive.4 AML is the most common acute leukemia in adults and is characterized by clonal expansion and impaired differentiation of myeloid progenitor cells.3,5 In the United States, the age-adjusted incidence rate of AML is 4.4 per 100,000 persons per year, based on cases diagnosed during 2019–2023.6 Synchronous multiple primary cancers are generally defined as malignancies diagnosed within 6 months of each other, and one single-center series of synchronous hematologic and solid tumors reported an incidence of approximately 0.05%.7 No standard treatment options have been established for synchronous hematologic and solid malignancies; therefore, management should be individualized according to the characteristics of both malignancies and the patient’s general condition.7

This case describes a rare synchronous presentation of high-grade ESCC and AML in a 57-year-old Syrian man presenting with severe pancytopenia. The case is clinically relevant because it highlights the need for early bone marrow evaluation when cytopenias are severe or unexplained in patients with esophageal malignancy, particularly in settings where regional epidemiologic data and access to advanced molecular risk stratification may be limited.

Case Presentation

A 57-year-old Syrian man, employed as a dental prosthetics technician, presented with progressive dysphagia and severe weight loss. He was a heavy smoker, with an estimated 40 pack-year smoking history, and denied alcohol consumption. His medical history included arterial hypertension for 11 years, ischemic heart disease for which he had undergone coronary angiography with stent placement 5 years earlier, and cholecystectomy 25 years earlier. He reported no known drug allergies and had no relevant family history. A detailed occupational exposure assessment for benzene, formaldehyde or other potentially myelotoxic agents was not available.

Symptoms began approximately 1 year before presentation with vague, dull abdominal pain. During the 4 months before admission, he developed progressively worsening dysphagia, initially to solids and later to liquids during the final 15 days before admission. This was accompanied by profound unintentional weight loss of approximately 30 kg over 1 year, generalized weakness, and marked pallor. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed a circumferential obstructing esophageal stricture with a mass-like appearance beginning approximately 30 cm from the incisors and extending distally, preventing passage of the endoscope. Multiple biopsies were obtained.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the chest and abdomen demonstrated irregular circumferential wall thickening of the distal esophagus measuring approximately 7 cm in length and extending to the gastric cardia. Enlarged para-aortic lymph nodes were noted, measuring up to 13 mm in short-axis diameter. Multiple focal hepatic lesions involving both hepatic lobes were also identified, and were radiologically consistent with metastatic disease. Representative axial CT images are shown in Figure 1. Histopathological examination of the esophageal biopsies revealed invasive high-grade carcinoma with signet ring-like, or pseudo-signet ring, morphology (Figures 2 and 3). Mucin staining was negative. Immunohistochemistry showed tumor cell positivity for p40, while cytokeratin 7 (CK7) and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) were negative, supporting squamous differentiation and the diagnosis of high-grade esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated severe pancytopenia, with leukopenia (white blood cell count approximately 0.6 × 109/L), anemia (hemoglobin 6.9 g/dL), and thrombocytopenia (platelet count approximately 55 × 109/L). Serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was markedly elevated (>1000 U/L). Peripheral blood smear showed macrocytosis with a mixed population of normochromic and hypochromic red blood cells and marked anisopoikilocytosis, including target cells, spherocytes, teardrop cells, and elongated forms (Figure 4). No circulating blasts were identified. Because the degree of pancytopenia was severe and not fully explained by the esophageal malignancy alone, further hematologic evaluation was performed. Bone marrow biopsy showed variable cellularity, predominantly with an approximately 60:40 marrow-to-fat ratio and focally lower cellularity (Figure 5).

Large irregular immature cells with histiocytoid-like morphology were identified. Immunohistochemistry showed lysozyme positivity, scattered CD68 expression, and MPO-positive myeloid elements, while pancytokeratin was negative, arguing against marrow involvement by metastatic carcinoma and supporting myeloid/monocytoid differentiation. Bone marrow flow cytometry identified approximately 46% myeloid blasts expressing CD117, CD34, CD33, CD13, MPO, HLA-DR, and dim CD25, with aberrant CD7 expression in approximately 31% of cells. The blast population was negative for CD14, CD64, CD3, CD5, CD8, CD10, CD19, CD20, TdT, glycophorin A, CD36, CD138, and kappa and lambda light chains. Overall, these findings established the diagnosis of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and were suggestive of AML with maturation (FAB AML-M2), as reported by flow cytometry. Cytogenetic and molecular testing, including karyotype and FLT3, NPM1, and CEBPA status, was not available; therefore, contemporary genetic classification and risk stratification could not be completed.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of approximately 50%, regional wall motion abnormalities, and moderate mitral regurgitation.

Because of severe esophageal obstruction and the inability to maintain adequate oral intake, the patient underwent feeding jejunostomy placement for nutritional support. After stabilization, he was referred to a specialized oncology center for multidisciplinary oncologic and hematologic management. No post-referral treatment outcomes or long-term follow-up data were available at the time of manuscript preparation.

Discussion

Severe cytopenias in patients evaluated for esophageal carcinoma can create diagnostic uncertainty, particularly when they are initially attributed to malnutrition, chronic disease, marrow suppression, or advanced solid malignancy. Published case reports indicate that bone marrow examination should be considered when cytopenias are severe or unexplained rather than being attributed to the esophageal tumor alone.8,9 A reported case of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) with concomitant myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) progressing rapidly to acute myeloid leukemia (AML) highlighted that marked thrombocytopenia prompted bone marrow evaluation and was central to identifying the coexisting myeloid neoplasm.8 A separate report describing therapy-related AML after chemoradiotherapy for esophageal cancer emphasized that persistent pancytopenia should not automatically be interpreted as prolonged treatment-related marrow suppression and recommended bone marrow assessment when cytopenias do not recover as expected.9 In the present case, profound pancytopenia at initial presentation prompted bone marrow aspiration, biopsy, immunohistochemistry and flow cytometry, which established the diagnosis of AML synchronous with ESCC.

Synchronous hematologic and solid malignancies are rare. One single-center series defined synchronous multiple primary cancers as malignancies diagnosed within 6 months of each other and reported an incidence of approximately 0.05% for synchronous hematologic and solid tumors.7 The same series reported a median overall survival of 17.7 months, illustrating the clinical complexity and limited survival observed in that cohort.7 The literature closest to the present clinical scenario includes reports in which esophageal carcinoma coexisted with primary MDS or MDS progressing to AML.8,10 Liu et al described ESCC with MDS with excess blasts that transformed to AML within approximately 10 days, with a complex karyotype and molecular characterization of both the esophageal tumor and bone marrow.8 That report illustrated how two aggressive malignancies can severely constrain therapeutic options and treatment timing. In contrast, reports involving selected patients with esophageal cancer and primary MDS have shown that individualized treatment may remain feasible when hematologic risk, solid tumor stage, comorbidities, and the patient’s general condition are considered together.10,11

An important diagnostic consideration in the present case was distinguishing a a concomitant myeloid neoplasm from bone marrow involvement by the esophageal carcinoma. Bone marrow biopsy demonstrated variable cellularity and large irregular immature cells with histiocytoid-like morphology. Lysozyme positivity, MPO-positive myeloid elements, and scattered CD68 expression supported myeloid/monocytoid differentiation, while negative pancytokeratin staining argued against marrow involvement by metastatic carcinoma. Flow cytometry identified approximately 46% myeloid blasts expressing CD117, CD34, CD33, CD13, MPO, HLA-DR, and dim CD25. Aberrant CD7 expression was reported in approximately 31% of cells. In this setting, CD7 is best interpreted as part of the abnormal leukemia-associated immunophenotype rather than as an independent prognostic marker.

The flow-cytometric report established the diagnosis of AML and suggested AML with maturation according to the French-American-British classification (FAB AML-M2). However, this designation should be interpreted cautiously because contemporary AML classification and risk stratification require integration of morphology, immunophenotyping, cytogenetics, and molecular findings.12 In the present case, karyotyping and testing for FLT3, NPM1, and CEBPA alterations were not available; therefore, contemporary genetic classification and definitive risk stratification could not be completed. This limitation also affected the ability to discuss mutation-directed treatment options. Nevertheless, the available marrow morphology, immunohistochemistry, and flow-cytometric findings were sufficient to establish AML and justify referral for specialized hematologic-oncologic management.

The coexistence of ESCC and AML in this patient should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal relationship. The patient was a heavy smoker, and cigarette smoking is an established risk factor for ESCC.4 Smoking and occupational exposure to agents such as benzene and formaldehyde have also been evaluated in AML epidemiology.3,5 Although the patient worked as a dental prosthetics technician, a detailed occupational exposure assessment was not available. Therefore, his occupation should not be considered evidence of exposure to a specific leukemogenic agent, and no etiologic relationship between his occupation and AML can be established.

This case also highlights the importance of coordinated multidisciplinary care. Gastroenterology was essential for endoscopic identification and biopsy of the obstructing esophageal lesion. Pathology and immunohistochemistry confirmed high-grade ESCC despite pseudo–signet ring morphology and negative mucin staining. Hematology was required because the severe pancytopenia was disproportionate and unexplained, leading to bone marrow examination and the diagnosis of AML. Sergical and nutritional support were necessary because the obstructing esophageal tumor prevented adequate oral intake and required feeding jejunostomy. Oncology and hematology were subsequently needed to determine treatment priorities while considering ESCC, with radiologically suspected hepatic metastases, synchronous AML, cardiac comorbidity, nutritional compromise, and the patient’s overall condition. Because no standardized treatment pathway exists for synchronous hematologic and solid malignancies, treatment sequencing must be individualized.7

Overall, the available case-based literatureemphasizes two practical points. First, severe or unexplained cytopenias accompanying esophageal malignancy should prompt timely hematologic evaluation and consideration of bone marrow evaluation.8–11 Second, treatment sequencing must be individualized according to the urgency and solid tumors vary widely in disease burden of each malignancy, the patient’s treatment urgency, physiologic reserve, comorbities and the available therapeutic options.7,10,11 In the present patient, the immediate priorities were diagnostic clarification, supportive care, nutritional stabilization through feeding jejunostomy, and referral to a specialized oncology center for integrated disease-directed management.

This report has several limitations. First, cytogenetic and molecular testing, including karyotypeand FLT3, NPM1, and CEBPA status, was not available, preventing contemporary AML classification and definitive genetic risk stratification. Second, the hepatic lesions were considered metastatic on radiologic grounds but were not confirmed histologically. Third, a detailed occupational exposure assessment was not performed, therefore no causal relationship can be inferred between the patient’s occupation and either malignancy. Fourth, post-referral treatment details and long-term follow-up outcomes were unavailable at the time of manuscript preparation. Finally, as a single case report, this observation cannot establish a biological relationship between ESCC and AML. Despite these limitations, the case adds to the limited literature on synchronous esophageal squamous malignancy and primary AML and reinforces the importance of early bone marrow evaluation and multidisciplinary treatment planning when severe cytopenias accompany esophageal cancer.

Conclusion

Synchronous ESCC and AML at initial presentation is rare but clinically important. Severe or unexplained cytopenias in patients with esophageal malignancy should prompt early bone marrow examination to avoid diagnostic delay. Management requires individualized multidisciplinary prioritization based on disease burden, hematologic risk, and patient status.
