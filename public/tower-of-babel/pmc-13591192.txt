Thumb Positioning Modulates Median Nerve Mobility and Morphology During Postural Changes in Carpal Tunnel Syndrome: Implications for Nerve Gliding Exercises
Authors: Yi-Wei Chang, Chii‑Jen Chen, You‑Wei Wang, Valeria Chiu, Yi-Shiung Horng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the modulation of median nerve (MN) mobility and morphology by thumb positioning during postural changes in patients with carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS), with implications for nerve gliding exercises.DesignCross-sectional case–control study.SettingOutpatient physical medicine and rehabilitation department at a community hospital.ParticipantsPatients aged 18 to 65 years with symptoms of CTS, confirmed using nerve conduction studies, were consecutively enrolled. Those with a history of wrist trauma or surgery were excluded.InterventionsNot applicable.Main Outcome MeasuresMN and flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) tendon excursion and cross-sectional area (CSA) of the MN in wrist neutral/extended and thumb-resting/abducted positions.ResultsA total of 79 CTS hands and 88 healthy hands were enrolled. The CTS group had a significantly larger MN CSA (p<.0001), with MN CSA decreasing when the thumb moved from rest to abduction in both wrist-neutral and wrist-extended positions (p=.0170 and p=.0007, respectively). The CTS group also had a significantly lower MN excursion (p<.05), with MN excursion increasing as the wrist extended (p=.0454) but slightly decreasing as the wrist extended with thumb abducted. No between-group difference in FDS tendon excursion was observed for the wrist-neutral position (p=.1126); for the wrist-extended with thumb-at-rest position, the CTS group exhibited slightly greater FDS tendon excursion (p=.0476). Both groups exhibited larger FDS excursion during wrist extension and when the thumb moved from rest to abduction (p=.0026 in CTS group and p=.0060 in control group), indicating posture-related tendon mobility.ConclusionsMN mobility and morphology are affected by thumb positioning in CTS. It is recommended to avoid overstretching the thumb in the final step of nerve gliding exercises.KEYWORDS: Carpal tunnel syndrome, Doppler, Median nerve, Rehabilitation, Ultrasonography

Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS), an entrapment neuropathy of the median nerve (MN) inside the carpal tunnel, causes pain or numbness in the distribution of the MN. CTS affects approximately 1% to 5% of the general population, with higher incidence rates observed in certain occupational groups, particularly those engaged in repetitive wrist activities.1 The pathogenesis of CTS includes increased carpal tunnel pressure, tenosynovitis of the flexor tendons, and even fibrosis/adhesion inside the carpal tunnel, which might lead to decreased mobility of the MN in both transverse and longitudinal planes.2 In addition, fibrosis impairs the smooth movement of the MN and flexor tendons, resulting in ongoing repetitive injury during ordinary hand activity.3

Management of CTS typically begins with conservative treatment options aimed at alleviating symptoms and preventing progression of the condition. Night wrist splints help maintain neutral wrist alignment and lower MN pressure, while nerve gliding exercises that target adhesion reduction in the carpal tunnel serve as another common conservative option. These exercises promote movement of the MN within the carpal tunnel and were reported to reduce pain, improve functionality, and increase grip strength.4 Other conservative approaches include modifications to daily activities and ergonomic adjustments in the workplace. If conservative treatments fail to provide relief, surgical interventions, such as carpal tunnel release, may be considered.

Neural mobilization or neurodynamic techniques are a set of movements designed to gently move and slide peripheral nerves through surrounding tissues.5 These exercises typically involve coordinated movements of multiple joints to alternately elongate and shorten the nerve bed, with the aim of increasing longitudinal excursion and reducing nerve strain. These techniques are often used in the conservative management of CTS to alleviate pain and improve nerve function by addressing concerns such as nerve adherence and intraneural pressure. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that neurodynamic techniques not only reduced symptom severity and functional impairment but also improved neurophysiological parameters in CTS.6

Totten and Hunter7 developed a nerve gliding exercise for CTS that involves moving the fingers and wrist through 6 different positions: grasp, finger extension, wrist extension, thumb extension, forearm supination, and gentle stretch of the thumb by the opposite hand. Liu et al.8 discovered that excursions of the MN and flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) tendon were significantly greater in the wrist-extended position than in the neutral position in both patients with CTS and healthy volunteers, which suggests that including wrist extensions in gliding exercises for CTS can enhance movement of the MN. A recent ultrasound study involving healthy participants demonstrated that dynamic wrist positions, together with finger pinch activities, significantly alter the morphology of the MN within the carpal tunnel.9 Despite increasing interest in the biomechanical behavior of the MN in CTS, most previous studies have primarily focused on the influence of wrist or finger movements on MN mobility.8, 9, 10, 11 The potential role of thumb positioning in modulating MN dynamics during postural changes has received relatively little attention. Accordingly, the present study aimed to evaluate the modulation of MN mobility and morphology by thumb positioning during postural changes in patients with CTS, with implications for nerve gliding exercises.

Methods

Patient enrollment

We enlisted individuals with CTS on a voluntary basis from the outpatient department of a physical medicine and rehabilitation center at a community hospital. Hospital personnel, relatives of patients, and other volunteers were additionally recruited to participate as healthy controls. The institutional review board of the study hospital granted ethical approval for the study (IRB number: 99-IRB-021-XD), and all participants provided informed consent. All procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients with CTS were considered eligible for the study if they had subjective symptoms (eg, discomfort or paresthesia within the MN distribution of the fingers) and either a positive Phalen’s test or a positive Tinel’s sign, with CTS confirmed using electrophysiology. Individuals were excluded if they were younger than 18 years or older than 65 years; had diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism, renal insufficiency, or an autoimmune disorder; were pregnant; or had experienced prior wrist trauma or surgical interventions.

Physical examinations

This study recorded the personal characteristics of each participant, and they underwent a series of physical evaluations, which encompassed assessments of the Tinel and Phalen signs.12 Palmar pinch strength and wrist circumference were measured, and Semmes–Weinstein monofilament sensory evaluations were conducted. Semmes–Weinstein monofilament evaluations assessed hand sensation by applying a force-calibrated nylon filament to 7 designated regions. Participants with eyes closed who correctly identified the tested digit received positive results. Filaments were scored from 1 to 5 based on computed values, with the cumulative score from all regions analyzed as a continuous variable.13

Nerve conduction study

A nerve conduction study (NCS) was conducted using a Neuropack M1 MEB-9200 J/K system (Nihon Kohden Corporation). Participants were placed in a supine posture, and measurements were obtained in a controlled environment at room temperature (26°C). The NCS involved supramaximal stimulation with a constant-current stimulator and surface-electrode recordings. The MN criteria used to validate the presence of CTS were (1) distal motor latency>4.4 ms; (2) distal sensory latency>3.4 ms; or (3) midpalm MN peak latency>2.2 ms.14,15

Ultrasonography

Ultrasonographic examinations utilized a 12-MHz linear array transducer (GE LOGIQ S8; General Electric Medical Systems). Participants lay supine with arms extended, forearms supinated, and wrists resting comfortably on the bed. Wrist splints maintained neutral and 30° extended positions (fig 1). The transducer was oriented perpendicular to the MN for optimal echogenicity, with care to avoid wrist pressure and preserve nerve configuration.

The wrist is maintained in a 30° extended with thumb-abducted position within the wrist splint, while the transducer is kept perpendicular to the median nerve.

Transverse ultrasonographic images were obtained at the pisiform level, where the cross-sectional area (CSA) of the MN was quantified using the ultrasound system’s digital measurement tools by delineating the inner boundary of the perineural hyperechoic rim surrounding the hypoechoic MN; concurrently, the flattening ratio (FR) was derived by calculating the ratio of the long axis to the short axis of the MN at the same level, with all assessments performed by a single board-certified physiatrist, possessing 8 years of musculoskeletal ultrasonography experience and blinded to both clinical and nerve conduction study results (fig 2).

Transverse ultrasonographic images of the wrist reveal the following anatomical structures: at the pisiform level: (1) flexor digitorum superficialis tendon of index finger; (2) median nerve. P: pisiform, S: scaphoid.

Duplex Doppler imaging was utilized to evaluate the morphological and hemodynamic properties of the MN by positioning a longitudinal ultrasonography probe at the lunate–capitate intercarpal joint. This technique allowed for the observation of longitudinal gliding movements of the MN and the FDS tendon, with specific configurations of Doppler settings to ensure precise sampling within the MN.16 After the recording of MN excursions, the sample volume indicator was repositioned to lie within the FDS tendon at the same anatomical level, and the angle indicator was adjusted to run parallel to the tendon. Because of the greater excursion of the FDS tendon relative to the MN, the pulse repetition frequency was increased to yield a maximum y-axis velocity of 40 cm/second (fig 3).

Duplex Doppler imaging is utilized to observe the longitudinal gliding movements of the (A) median nerve and (B) flexor digitorum superficialis tendon.

Participants were directed to execute repetitive movements of their index fingers transitioning from full extension to a semiflexed position, defined as a 90° angle at the metacarpophalangeal and proximal interphalangeal joints. Before ultrasonographic evaluation, they practiced isolating flexion at these joints to primarily engage the flexor digitorum superficialis tendon, with Doppler measurements taken while controlling movement speed via a metronome and adjusting wrist and thumb positions accordingly. Doppler recordings were obtained under varying wrist and thumb positions: (1) wrist in neutral and thumb at rest; (2) wrist extended 30° and thumb at rest; and (3) wrist extended 30° with thumb abducted.

Image analysis

Doppler waveforms illustrating the excursions of the MN and the FDS tendon were recorded and preserved. Each ultrasonographic recording included a minimum of 3 complete flexion–extension cycles of the respective excursion waveforms. The recorded images were subsequently transferred to a computer for analysis by an independent researcher blinded to both the identity and group classification of the participants. In-house software was used to quantify the total number of calibrated pixels corresponding to the spike area in the velocity-time integral of the Doppler spectrum—which reflects the gliding distance of the MN or FDS tendon per flexion–extension cycle.8

Statistical analyses

Student’s t tests and chi-square tests were employed to compare basic demographic characteristics between the CTS and control groups. To account for within-participant correlations arising from bilateral CTS data and measurements from controls, this study applied generalized estimating equations. This approach was used to compare findings from physical examinations, the NCS, B-mode ultrasonography, and duplex Doppler imaging between the CTS and control groups, with adjustments made for age and sex. Paired t tests were used to evaluate the Doppler ultrasonography data. These tests were used specifically to assess differences between thumb positions at rest and in abduction within each group. Repeated-measures analysis of variance was conducted to compare the excursions of the MN and FDS tendon across varying wrist and thumb positions within each group. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute). A post-hoc power analysis was conducted using SAS PROC POWER (SAS version 9.4), with adjustment for the clustered nature of the data through a design effect correction (Donner & Klar, 2000)17: DEg=1+(m̄g−1)ρ, where m̄g is the average cluster size in group g and ρ=.78 is the within-subject correlation estimated from the unstructured working correlation matrix of the generalized estimating equation model. The experimental group comprised 49 subjects contributing 79 hand observations (mean cluster size=1.61; design effect=1.48; effective N=53.5), and the control group comprised 44 subjects contributing 88 hand observations (mean cluster size=2.00; design effect=1.78; effective N=49.4). Given the observed mean difference of 3.5 units between groups and unequal standard deviations (SD experimental=4.7, SD control=2.4), the Satterthwaite t test approximation was used to accommodate unequal variances. Under a 2-sided alpha of 0.05, the achieved statistical power was 0.997, indicating that our sample size provided ample power to detect the reported between-group difference.17

Results

A total of 49 patients with CTS exhibiting either a positive Tinel or Phalen sign as well as positive NCS findings were consecutively enrolled. Of these patients, 31 had bilateral CTS. Additionally, 47 healthy volunteers matched for age and sex were recruited. As detailed in table 1, our analysis revealed no significant disparities in the demographic characteristics between the CTS and control groups. We excluded 18 unaffected hands and one hand exhibiting a bifid MN within the CTS group. We also excluded 6 asymptomatic hands with abnormal NCS results within the control group; consequently, the final analyses encompassed 79 CTS hands and 88 healthy hands. Notable differences emerged between the CTS and control groups with respect to the results of the physical examinations and NCS findings (table 2).

Discussion

This study showed the CSAs of the MN were smaller when the thumbs were abducted compared to resting, especially in the extended wrist posture. The excursions of MN were significantly smaller than those of healthy groups in all tested positions. Excursions of the MN were greater when the wrist was extended from the neutral position, and the excursions became smaller when the thumb was moved to the abducted position from the resting position, especially for patients with CTS. However, both groups exhibited a gradual increase in FDS tendon excursion from the neutral wrist/resting thumb to extended wrist/abducted thumb positions. These findings indicate that CTS is characterized by an enlarged and less mobile MN, with preserved but posture-sensitive tendon mechanics.

Decreased gliding of the MN in patients with CTS, especially those with more affected hands, has been reported in other studies.16,18 Adhesion and thickening of the subsynovial connective tissue (SSCT) have been proposed as playing a critical role in this phenomenon. The SSCT serves as a structural scaffold for tendons and vascular elements within the carpal tunnel, facilitating the movement of these structures and protecting the MN from mechanical stress.19 Repetitive wrist motion can induce microtears and subsequent fibrosis and noninflammatory thickening of the SSCT.20 A cadaver study revealed that SSCT gliding velocities are significantly lower than tendon velocities in the carpal tunnel and that excessive SSCT thickness impairs MN mobility.21 Thus, the fibrosis and adhesion of SSCT in the carpal tunnel could potentially be alleviated by nerve or tendon gliding exercises. Accordingly, MN gliding exercises are frequently recommended as part of conservative treatment for individuals with CTS.4 This therapeutic intervention might facilitate the enhancement of MN mobility by encouraging nerve movement through adjacent soft tissues and by mitigating adhesions, additionally alleviating clinical symptoms such as discomfort, paresthesia, and sensory loss.22

Several studies have demonstrated that wrist and finger positioning influences MN gliding patterns. For example, Echigo et al.23 observed that forearm supination induced more-distal gliding of the MN in healthy volunteers, which confirms that upper limb posture influences MN mobility. Meng et al.24 investigated MN gliding in the upper extremities of fresh whole-body human cadavers and discovered that nerve excursions were markedly smaller in the carpal tunnel than in proximal regions. Moreover, maximal abduction of the first and fourth fingers resulted in a substantial distal excursion of the MN. Conversely, one study comparing the effectiveness of tendon and nerve gliding exercises in patients with CTS noted mild functional deterioration after nerve gliding. This result suggests that excessive MN stretching during full wrist extension might increase neural strain, potentially triggering ectopic discharges.25

In line with the findings of other studies, our results revealed that MN excursions increase when wrists extend 30° from the neutral position, regardless of CTS status. Furthermore, additional thumb abduction during wrist extension does not further increase MN excursion; instead, MN excursion decreases when the thumb is kept in the abducted position, especially for patients with CTS. By contrast, FDS tendon excursions increased not only in the wrist-extended position but also in the wrist-extended with thumb-abducted position, regardless of CTS status. As demonstrated in a previous study, thumb flexion resulted in transverse movement of the MN in the radial direction.10 A possible explanation for our findings is that maintaining the thumb in abduction may stretch and tighten the recurrent motor branch of the MN, potentially restricting MN excursion within the carpal tunnel during index-finger movements when the wrist is extended. Although nerve gliding exercises benefit both the MN and flexor tendon mobility, caution should be taken not to overstretch the MN—particularly during the final step involving thumb stretching with the opposite hand. This step may even be omitted if necessary. Clinicians may also advise patients to avoid prolonged thumb abduction during daily activities.

In this study, the CTS group exhibited significantly larger CSAs than did the control group in all positions, which is similar to the findings of other studies26,27 and further supports the validity of our results. Regarding the FR, the results in the literature are inconsistent.26,28 In the current study, we observed no significant difference between the CTS and control groups across different wrist and thumb positions. These findings indicate that the FR may be less accurate than the CSA of the MN in diagnosing CTS.

The morphology of the MN within the carpal tunnel has been demonstrated to be affected by different wrist and finger positions. For example, Wolny et al.11 demonstrated that the CSA of MN decreases in positions of maximum extension and 45° flexion and in maximum flexion relative to the resting position. A previous study evaluating the CSA of the MN during tendon gliding exercises (straight, hook, full fist, table top, and straight fist) demonstrated significant changes in MN CSA during transitions from straight to hook and from hook to full fist. Notably, in the full-fist position, the CSA of the MN significantly decreased, regardless of CTS status.29 Similarly, van Doesburg et al.30 discovered that both the CSA and the perimeter of the MN were smaller during index finger or the thumb flexion than during extension. By comparison, we observed that MN CSA was smaller in the thumb-abducted position than in the neutral position, regardless of whether the wrist was in a neutral or extended position. This morphological change can be explained by the stretching and elongation of the recurrent motor branch of the MN in the carpal tunnel during thumb abduction, which decreases MN CSA.

In this study, we specifically examined how thumb positioning (rest vs abduction), combined with wrist posture (neutral vs extension), modulates MN mobility and morphology in patients with CTS compared with healthy controls. In addition to MN excursion, we simultaneously evaluated the CSA of the MN and the excursion of the FDS tendon. This integrated assessment allowed us to better understand the biomechanical interaction between nerve and tendon structures during posture-related changes. Our findings demonstrate that thumb positioning significantly influences MN morphology and mobility, particularly showing a reduction in MN CSA with thumb abduction and posture-dependent changes in MN excursion in CTS patients. These results provide new insight into the biomechanical effects of thumb movement on the MN and have potential clinical implications for optimizing nerve gliding exercises in CTS rehabilitation.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the study excluded individuals with certain medical conditions and those with a history of wrist trauma or surgery, which limits the generalizability of the current findings. Second, this is a cross-sectional study—comparisons can only be between individuals with and without CTS, and establishing causal relationships between MN gliding and the development of CTS is not possible. Third, Doppler imaging captures only longitudinal motion. The method cannot detect out-of-plane movements caused by transverse motion beyond the beam width.2 To address this, we instructed the participants of this study to repeatedly flex and extend their index fingers in sync with a 1-Hz metronome until 3 clear waveforms were captured. Fourth, although the posthoc power analysis indicated excellent statistical power (0.997) for our primary findings, we acknowledge that an a priori power calculation was not conducted at the design stage. Future confirmatory studies would benefit from prospective power planning.

Conclusions

Excursions of the MN were greater when the wrist was extended from the neutral position, and the excursions were smaller when the thumb was maintained in the abducted position, especially for patients with CTS. Accordingly, we suggest avoiding overstretching the thumb when the final step of a nerve gliding exercise is being performed. Additional longitudinal studies should be conducted to evaluate the effects of gliding exercises on the mobility and morphology of the MN and clinical indicators.
