Sub-diffusive regimes for long range self-interacting path measures
Volker Betz, Tobias Schmidt, Mark Sellke

Abstract
. We study Brownian motion perturbed by a long range self-interaction. We provide variance bounds in terms of the spatial interaction strength and the order of time decay.



1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Motivation and Results

Recently, there has been a lot of activity studying self-interacting path measures of the form

(1.1)

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbf{self}}({\mathrm{d}}x)\propto\exp\left(\alpha\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}sW(|x_{t}-x_{s}|,|t-s|)\right){\mathbb{P}}_{0}({\mathrm{d}}x),

where
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
is the path measure of
d
-dimensional Brownian motion, and

(x,t)\mapsto W(x,t)
is suitably regular, unimodal in
x
for all
t
, and maximal at
x=0
. See for example

[
36
,
6
,
7
,
9
,
4
,
8
,
31
]
. An important source of interest
for these models is their relation to quantum field theory, specifically polaron models, on which we will give some details below. For now, let us focus on the probabilistic object
at hand.

The probability measure (
1.1
) is reminiscent of a Gibbs measure
as known from statistical mechanics. There is a short (infinitesimal) range potential
due to the Brownian motion, and a pair potential
W
. By the spatial unimodality assumption on
W
, the interaction is
attractive, and so it is natural to expect the paths to fluctuate less between times

0
and
T
under
{\mathbb{P}}_{\rm self}
than they would under Brownian motion. A convenient
quantity to measure such effects is the behaviour of the mean square displacement

s_{T}={\mathbb{E}}_{\rm self}(|x_{T}|^{2})

for large
T
.

There are two possible cases. The first one, which is also the one that is best understood,
is when the decay of
W
in its second (temporal) argument is fast. Then, it has been shown in fair generality

[
38
,
10
]
that

\sigma^{2}:=\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{s_{T}}{T}>0
, meaning that the paths still
behave diffusively. The diffusion constant
\sigma^{2}
is expected to be strictly less than

1
for a large class of attractive interactions
W
, reflecting the fact that the
fluctuations are indeed reduced by the presence of the potential. Since
\sigma^{2}
has
intimate connections with the effective mass of polaron models (more on this below), it has
been investigated a lot. We now have good upper bounds for
\sigma^{2}
under a variety of
conditions, either by direct investigation of the path measures
(
1.1
) (see
[
4
,
7
,
36
,
9
]
), or by analytic
means via the connection to polaron models
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
).

The second possibility is that
s_{T}
grows sublinearly as
T\to\infty
.
In other words, the paths behave sub-diffusively between times
0
and
T
under

{\mathbb{P}}_{\rm self}
, or even stay localized in the extreme case that
s_{T}

stays bounded as
T\to\infty
. This regime is much less understood, and it is the one
that we explore in the present paper. Our first result is as follows: let

(1.2)

\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\xi}({\mathrm{d}}x)\propto\exp\left(-\alpha\int_{0}^{T}{\rm d}s\int_{0}^{T}{\rm d}t\,\frac{f(x_{t}-x_{s})}{1+|t-s|^{\xi}}\right){\mathbb{P}}({\mathrm{d}}x),

where

(1)

f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is radially symmetric.

(2)

There exists
\zeta>0
such that
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\ni z\mapsto f(z)-\zeta|z|^{2}
is quasi-convex.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
0\leq\xi<3
and
\alpha>0
. Then, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all measures as in (
1.2
):

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\xi}}[|x_{T}|^{2}]\leq 16\left(C\min\{\alpha^{-1/2},1\}+\alpha^{-1}\max\left\{\zeta^{-1}\log(T)^{2}T^{\xi-2},1\right\}\right).

For
\xi\in(0,1)
we even find

\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\xi}}[|x_{T}|^{2}]=0.

We thus obtain sub-diffusive behaviour in the range
1<\xi<3
, and localization in the
range
0\;\leqslant\;\xi<2
; indeed, we show that the mean square displacement converges to zero
in case
\xi\in(0,1)
. From the point of view of statistical mechanics, this can be expected
due to the following heuristic:
for
\xi<1
, the interaction is so long range that

\int_{0}^{T}{\rm d}s\int_{0}^{T}{\rm d}t\,(1+|s-t|^{\xi})^{-1}
grows faster than linearly in
T
.
Since the probability of a Brownian path to stay inside a compact subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
up to
time
T
decays (not faster than) exponentially in
T
(see
[
1
]
for some very
precise asymptotics), this means that the energy cost of
being away from the minimum of
f
(which is at
x\equiv 0
) for an amount of time proportional to

T
outweighs the entropy cost of staying in this area. Hence one expects typical paths to remain close to zero in most of
[0,T]
.

For the case where
f(x)=|x|^{2}
, the measure
\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\xi}
is Gaussian. In this
case, formula (3.13) in
[
39
]
suggests that

s_{T}\sim\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}k\frac{1-\cos(2\pi Tk)}{4\pi^{2}k^{2}+\alpha(\hat{g}(0)-\hat{g}(k))}

for large
T
, where
g(r)=\frac{1}{1+|r|^{\xi}}
with
\xi>1
, and
\hat{g}

denotes the Fourier transform. No justification of this formula is given in
[
39
]
,
but the result can be reproduced with a Gaussian calculation for the case where Brownian
paths are considered on the time interval
[0,T]
with periodic boundary conditions,
and the interaction is modified to reflect these boundary conditions as well. We provide the details in the Appendix. These
boundary modifications are not necessarily harmless in view of the long range potential, but it is reasonable to expect that at least the leading order behavior in
T
is correct. When

g(x)
decays like
|x|^{-\xi}
at infinity, then
\hat{g}(0)-\hat{g}(k)\sim|k|^{\xi-1}

as
|k|\to 0
, since
\hat{g}
has a maximum at
k=0
. By re-scaling, it follows that for

1<\xi\;\leqslant\;3
,

(1.3)

s_{T}\sim T\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}u\,\frac{1-\cos(2\pi u)}{4\pi^{2}u^{2}+\alpha T^{2}(\hat{g}(0)-\hat{g}((u/T))}\sim T^{\xi-2}.

This means that sub-diffusive behaviour can be expected for
2<\xi<3
, and localization
for
1<\xi<2
.
This is also what we verify.

The global domination assumption (2) above is critical to our result, as we rely on the
Gaussian correlation inequality (GCI). When this assumption fails, things become much more
difficult. One would still anticipate a regime of subdiffusivity and one of localization at least
when
f
diverges at infinity, and possibly also for bounded
f
that make
W
attractive. This is because, heuristically, slower time decay can compensate for the weaker ’spatial penalty’ as compared to the square case.
In this work we focus on the case where we have at least some divergence at infinity. For any path measure
\mu
,
\gamma>0
,
\xi>0
and
\alpha>0
, let

(1.4)

\tilde{\mu}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}({\mathrm{d}}x)\propto\exp\left(-\alpha\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}s\frac{|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{\gamma}}{1+|t-s|^{\xi}}\right)\mu({\mathrm{d}}x),

and

\hat{\mu}_{\alpha,T,f,\xi}({\mathrm{d}}x)\propto\exp\left(-\alpha\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}s\frac{f(x_{t}-x_{s})}{1+|t-s|^{\xi}}\right)\mu({\mathrm{d}}x).

We will write
Z_{\mu}
for the corresponding partition sum of
\tilde{\mu}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}
in case it is clear from the context what all occurring parameters are.
It will happen that
\mu
has an upper and lower index itself. We then write
(\tilde{\mu}^{a}_{b})_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}
instead of
\widetilde{(\mu^{a}_{b})}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}
for aesthetic purposes. If
\mu
just has an upper index, we similarly write
\tilde{\mu}^{a}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}
instead of
\widetilde{(\mu^{a})}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}
. In case
\gamma=2
we abbreviate
\tilde{\mu}_{\alpha,T,2,\xi}=\tilde{\mu}_{\alpha,T,\xi}
.
We then have

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
0<\gamma<2
.
Suppose that
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is radially symmetric, and there exists
\zeta>0
such that

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\ni z\mapsto f(z)-\zeta|z|^{\gamma}

is quasi-convex.
Then, there exists a constant
C=C(\zeta,\gamma)>0
such that

(1)

for
\xi\in(1+\gamma/2,2+\gamma/2)
we have

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,f,\xi}}[|x_{T}|^{2}]\leq C\log(T)^{4}T^{\xi-1-\gamma/2},

(2)

for
\xi\in(\gamma/2,1+\gamma/2)
we have

\limsup\limits_{T\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,f,\xi}}[|x_{T}|^{2}]\leq C,

(3)

for
\xi\in(0,\gamma/2)
we have

\limsup\limits_{T\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,f,\xi}}[|x_{T}|^{2}]=0.

The leading order in the regime
\xi\in(2,2+\gamma/2)
is suboptimal. The next Theorem improves the rate to
2(\xi-2)/\gamma
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
0<\gamma<2
and
\xi\in(2,\infty)
.
Suppose that
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is radially symmetric, and there exists
\zeta>0
such that

(1.5)

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\ni z\mapsto f(z)-\zeta|z|^{\gamma}

is quasi-convex.
Then, there exists a constant
C=C(\zeta,\gamma)>0
such that for all
\alpha>0

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,f,\xi}}[|x_{T}|^{2}]\leq CT^{\frac{2}{\gamma}(\xi-2)}\log(T)^{2}.

The above bound is only interesting for
\xi\in(2,2+\gamma/2)
. Otherwise, a simple application of (GCI) leads to the simpler bound
T
.

Finally, we are interested in letting
\alpha
tend to infinity together with
T
. Here, we can prove that a transition exists as soon as
\xi<2
: for any
\gamma\in(0,2)
, fluctuations are at most logarithmic.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
0<\gamma<2
and
\xi\in(0,2)
.
Suppose that
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is radially symmetric, and there exists
\zeta>0
such that

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\ni z\mapsto f(z)-\zeta|z|^{\gamma}

is quasi-convex.
Then, there exists a constant
C=C(\zeta,\gamma)>0
such that for all
\alpha\geq C\log(T)^{1/2}

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,f,\xi}}[|x_{T}|^{2}]\leq C\log(T)^{5/2}.

Our technique inherently does not provide lower bounds on the variance, so we can only talk about the different regimes in terms of upper bounds.
While logarithmic divergence occurs in the Gaussian case only for
\xi=2
, we find that there exists an entire interval of
\xi
for the general
\gamma\in(0,2)
case where logarithmic divergence holds if one increases
\alpha
only mildly with
T
. The fact that we obtain only logarithmic divergence could be an artifact of our approach: we always sum over
\log(T)
factors. This is necessary, because we rely on a dyadic decomposition of the path. Therefore, it might be that the logarithmic divergence can be improved to actual boundedness.

The provided bounds are the natural generalizations of the Gaussian case, which can be recovered by (formally) setting
\gamma=2
. To summarize, we find that the regime of sub-diffusivity shrinks for
\gamma<2
: compared to the Gaussian case, sub-diffusive behaviour only occurs for
\xi\in(2,2+\gamma/2)
. Moreover, the interval
(\gamma/2,1+\gamma/2)
becomes the regime with bounded variance, whereas the interval
(1+\gamma/2,2+\gamma/2)
becomes the sub-diffusive regime. Finally, we verify the convergence of variance to
0
only for the parameter range
\xi\in(0,\gamma/2)
. Therefore, all regimes shift by
\gamma/2
to the left. Interestingly, by letting
\alpha
only grow logarithmically in
T
, much stronger bounds on the concentration can be obtained in the regime
\xi\in(1+\gamma/2,2)
. The following Figure

To prove Theorem
1.1
we will argue by Gaussian domination via (GCI) that it suffices to study the case
f(x)=x^{2}
. In fact, one can also argue by an FKG-based extension of (GCI) recently introduced in
[
4
,
37
]
that it suffices to prove Theorem
1.3
and the following Theorems for
f(x)=|x|^{\gamma}
. Both reductions will be performed in Section
2.1
.

Let us continue by giving a brief intuition on why we expect the bounds in Theorem
1.3
to be near-optimal. A more detailed explanation of our proof strategy is given in Section
1.3
.
Suppose we can show that, at length scale
2^{j}
, the path fluctuates at most
2^{j(\xi-2)/\gamma}
. In other words, suppose it holds that

r_{j}=\sup\limits_{s,t\in[0,2^{j+1}]}|x_{t}-x_{s}|\leq 2^{\frac{j}{\gamma}(\xi-2)}.

Then,

\int_{0}^{2^{j}}{\mathrm{d}}s\int_{2^{j}}^{2^{j+1}}{\mathrm{d}}t\frac{|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{\gamma}}{1+|t-s|^{\xi}}\geq\int_{0}^{2^{j}}{\mathrm{d}}s\int_{2^{j}}^{2^{j+1}}{\mathrm{d}}t\frac{|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{2}}{2r_{j}^{2-\gamma}2^{j\xi}}.

A quadratic form estimate (see Proposition
2.3
) then yields

\int_{0}^{2^{j}}{\mathrm{d}}s\int_{2^{j}}^{2^{j+1}}{\mathrm{d}}t\frac{|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{\gamma}}{1+|t-s|^{\xi}}\geq\frac{2^{-j(\xi-2)}}{2r_{j}^{2-\gamma}}\left|\frac{1}{2^{j}}\int_{0}^{2^{j}}x_{s}{\mathrm{d}}s-\frac{1}{2^{j}}\int_{2^{j}}^{2^{j+1}}x_{s}{\mathrm{d}}s\right|^{2}.

The last estimate ought to imply that the random variable

\frac{1}{2^{j}}\int_{0}^{2^{j}}x_{s}{\mathrm{d}}s-\frac{1}{2^{j}}\int_{2^{j}}^{2^{j+1}}x_{s}{\mathrm{d}}s

has variance bounded by
r_{j}^{2-\gamma}2^{j(\xi-2)}
under
\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}
. By assumption
r_{j}\leq 2^{j(\xi-2)/\gamma}
, which yields that the variance is at most
2^{2j(\xi-2)/\gamma}
. In particular, the averaged increments are bounded with very high probability by something strictly smaller than
2^{j(\xi-2)/\gamma}
. Since
x_{t}-x_{s}
can be bounded pointwise by averaged increments (see Proposition
5.3
), it is possible to take
j\to j+1
and continue the argument recursively. Together with formula (
2.1
), which expresses
x_{T}
as a sum of averaged increments, the previous discussion indicates that the process behaves sub-diffusively.

1.2.
Connection to polaron models and related work

Polaron models describe the interaction of a quantum particle with a quantized field. The
basic intuition is that the coupling to the field increases the inertia of the particle,
making it appear heavier. In principle, this effect could be so strong that the particle
becomes localized, even though no external potential is applied.
In 1986, Herbert Spohn published a paper
[
38
]
entitled ‘Roughening and
pinning transition of the polaron’. In it, he argued that the (at the time conjectured) self-trapping of the Fröhlich polaron into a
localized state does not happen at any coupling strength. He observed that the emergence of
a localized state would correspond to a roughening transition
in the functional integral representation. This would require long-range interactions in the
‘time’-argument of the paths.
Since these interactions decay exponentially for the Fröhlich polaron, a roughening
transition can not be expected from a statistical mechanics point of view.

Since then, the impossibility of a localized ground state has been verified rigorously in
several ways. To name just two, in
[
10
]
, a calculation similar to one in

[
38
]
together with the proof of a functional central limit theorem showed that for
path measures with sufficiently fast decay of interactions, the diffusively rescaled path
measure has strictly positive diffusion constant. Together with the relations between
diffusion constant and effective mass (see
[
39
,
20
]
), this implies that no
localization can occur. More recently, sharp, rigorous lower bounds have been proved on the
effective mass of the Fröhlich polaron proper
[
16
]
, also implying the absence of
localization.

A somewhat weaker effect is what is in
[
38
]
referred to as the pinning transition,
and has since been much studied under the name of enhanced binding. It means that when
placing the particle into an external potential that is too weak to lead to a localized state
for the free particle, a sufficiently strong coupling to the field will result in the
existence of a localized state. Enhanced binding is now well established in many different
models, using both analytic
[
26
,
24
,
30
]
and functional integral
[
8
,
26
,
29
]
methods.

The topic of localization is much less understood. In order to explain what localization
means, and how it connects with the mean square displacement of functional integrals,
let us briefly introduce the basics of polaron models.
The relevant Hamiltonian is defined on the
Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\otimes{\mathcal{F}}
, where
{\mathcal{F}}
denotes the Fock space. We
denote by
\omega:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
the energy-momentum relation of the field
modes, and by
{\mathrm{d}}\Gamma(\omega)
its differential second quantization.
a_{k}^{\dagger}
and

a_{k}
are the formal annihilation and creation operators satisfying the canonical
commutation relations
[a^{\dagger}_{k},a_{k^{\prime}}]=\delta(k-k^{\prime})
. The energy of the coupled
quantum system is given in suitable units by

H=-\frac{\Delta_{x}}{2}+{\mathrm{d}}\Gamma(\omega)+\alpha\int{\mathrm{d}}k\frac{\hat{\rho}(k)}{\sqrt{2\omega(k)}}\left(\,{\rm e}^{ik\cdot x}\,a^{\dagger}_{k}+\text{h.c.}\right),

which is defined on a dense domain of
{\mathcal{H}}
. The specific polaron model is determined by
the parameters
\omega
and
\rho
, the latter describing the coupling of the (smeared
out) particle position to the field modes.
Assuming
\hat{\rho}/\omega\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
\hat{\rho}/\sqrt{\omega}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, standard arguments can be
used to show that
H
is well-defined and bounded from below.

The connection with the path integrals we study is given by the functional integral representation of the polaron. It is
derived by applying a Feynman-Kac formula and then integrating over the field degrees of freedom
[
27
]
. The relevant identity reads

(1.6)

\left\langle f\otimes\Omega,\,{\rm e}^{-TH}\,g\otimes\Omega\right\rangle=\int{\mathrm{d}}zf(z){\mathbb{E}}^{z}\left[\exp\left(\alpha\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}sW(x_{t}-x_{s},|t-s|)\right)g(x_{T})\right],

where the inner product is in
{\mathcal{H}}
,
\Omega
denotes the Fock vacuum, and where the
pair interaction

(1.7)

W(x,t)=\int{\mathrm{d}}k\,{\rm e}^{-|t|\omega(k)}\,\frac{|\hat{\rho}(k)|^{2}}{2\omega(k)}\,{\rm e}^{ik\cdot x}\,

results from integrating out the field. Among the physically relevant choices of the
parameters
\omega
and
\rho
we single out two: the choice of
\omega(k)=1,\hat{\rho}(k)=\frac{1}{|k|^{2}}
(up to constants) in three dimensions gives the Fröhlich
polaron, where
W(x,t)=\frac{1}{|x|}\,{\rm e}^{-|t|}\,
. This is an example of a short range (in

t
) interaction, where localization can be ruled out. Another choice is (again up to
constants and in three dimensions)
\omega(k)=|k|,\hat{\rho}(k)=1
, which gives the
(singular) Nelson model. Here
W(x,t)=\frac{1}{|x|^{2}+|t|^{2}}
, i.e. a long range pair
potential. While in the precise form given, the Nelson model is too singular for the Hamiltonian
to be well-defined, it can be regularized without impacting the decay of
W
for large
t
. For
details see
[
32
,
22
,
23
,
28
,
25
]
.

The connection between the effect of the field on the movement of the particle
can be described in terms of the mobility of paths in the functional integral. The best
studied case, also here, is where the pair potential
W
is short ranged, and then
the relevant notion is the effective mass.
This quantity, which has been the subject of much recent research (e.g.

[
36
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
9
,
4
,
7
]
), can be defined as follows:

H
commutes with the total momentum operator
-i\nabla_{x}+\int ka_{k}^{\dagger}a_{k}{\mathrm{d}}k
, which means that it can be diagonalized in terms of the total momentum of the system. This yields

H=\int{\mathrm{d}}PH(P)

for a suitable family of operators
\{H(P):P\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\}
.
It can be shown that
E(P)=\inf\text{spec }H(P)
is a real number for any combination of
\alpha,P
and that
P\mapsto E(P)
is radial. The effective mass is then given by

m_{\text{eff}}^{-1}=2\lim_{|P|\to 0}\frac{E(P)-E(0)}{|P|^{2}}.

The connection with path integrals requires, in its rigorous form,
first an infinite volume limit and then a central limit theorem. Namely, it can be shown that
families of measures of the type

(1.8)

{\mathbb{P}}_{T}({\rm d}x)\propto\exp\left(\alpha\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}{\mathrm{d}}t\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}{\mathrm{d}}sW(x_{t}-x_{s},|t-s|)\right){\mathbb{P}}_{0}({\rm d}x)

(where
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
is Brownian motion started at time
-T/2
) converge locally weakly as
T\to\infty
on the
\sigma
-algebra generated by increments; see
[
10
,
31
,
6
]
for various results of this
type. The same references show that the limiting path measure converges to a Brownian motion under diffusive rescaling. In cases where
x\mapsto W(x,t)
is rotationally invariant, the emergent diffusion matrix is a multiple of the unit matrix, and the relevant diffusion constant is the inverse
m_{\text{eff}}^{-1}
of the effective mass.
This was first argued in
[
38
]
and made fully rigorous in
[
20
]
.
Often, (for example in
[
31
]
), the procedure of first taking the limit
T\to\infty
and then the diffusive re-scaling is replaced by taking the diagonal limit

(1.9)

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{dT}{\mathbb{E}}_{T}[|x_{T/2}-x_{-T/2}|^{2}]=:\tilde{m}_{\text{eff}}^{-1},

which also gives the connection to the mean square displacement.
For short range potentials, this is expected to be equivalent, i.e.
m_{\text{eff}}=\tilde{m}_{\text{eff}}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{s_{T}}{dT}
.
This can be shown at
least in cases like the Fröhlich polaron by a representation of the measure as a
mixture of Gaussians
[
31
]
. In more general cases, and in particular for the longer range
potentials below, we are not aware of an argument that gives

m_{\text{eff}}=\tilde{m}_{\text{eff}}
.

For longer range potentials we expect that
s_{T}
grows slower than linearly for
large
T
. Modulo the caveat of the previous paragraph about the use of the mean square
displacement, this means that the effective mass of the related polaron model is infinite.
It is tempting to conjecture that in these cases, the second derivative of the function

P\mapsto E(P)
vanishes at
P=0
; but note that the theory of
[
20
]
connecting these
two quantities explicitly requires a nonnegative diffusion constant.

The third scenario is that
s_{T}
does not diverge at all as
T\to\infty
,
i.e. paths stay localized. Then
s_{T}
stays bounded as
T\to\infty
. This case corresponds
to localization of the polaron, although also here the mean square displacement is not quite
the right quantity to study. Instead, an external potential of the form
\varepsilon|x|^{2}
is added to
the polaron Hamiltonian
H
. In the functional integral, this means that we replace the measure

{\mathbb{P}}_{T}
from (
1.8
) by a measure

{\mathbb{P}}_{T,\varepsilon}
that has an additional factor of the form
\,{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}|x_{s}|^{2}\,{\rm d}s}\,
in its density with respect to

We begin with a discussion of our approach for proving Theorem
1.1
. There are two main ingredients: first, we require a path decomposition which lets us write
x_{T}
in a deterministic fashion as a sum of averaged increments (cf. equation (

) and Figure

, i.e. we will assume

for some

. The decomposition of

in terms of averaged increments is not particularly useful if one considers Brownian motion, as it is apriori not clear why the resulting random variables are helpful. If one considers
\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\xi}
, however, there is a quadratic form inequality first introduced in
[
36

which becomes very handy (see Proposition

). In fact, it holds that
\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\xi}
is stochastically dominated (in a suitable sense introduced in the next section) by another path measure
{\mathbb{P}}^{\mathbf{hier}}_{\alpha,T,\xi}
. This domination is the second ingredient in our proof; if one considers
{\mathbb{P}}^{\mathbf{hier}}_{\alpha,T,\xi}
, the averaged increments have a variance which can be calculated easily. In combination, the path decomposition and the change of measure suffice to prove Theorem
1.1
.

For our second result, Theorem
1.2
, note that

|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{\gamma}=\frac{|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{2}}{|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{2-\gamma}}.

That is, one can interpret

\frac{|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{\gamma}}{1+|t-s|^{\xi}}

as a Gaussian density, which is dampened by a path-dependent factor

\left(|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{2-\gamma}(1+|t-s|^{\xi})\right)^{-1}.

By assumption this additional factor decreases (recall the condition
\gamma<2
in Theorem
1.3
) in the size of the increments. Thus, if it would hold that

\sup\limits_{s,t\in[0,2^{j+1}]}|x_{t}-x_{s}|\leq c_{j},

then a simple quadratic form estimate yields

(1.10)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{2^{j}}{\mathrm{d}}t\int_{2^{j}}^{2^{j+1}}{\mathrm{d}}s\frac{|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{\gamma}}{1+|t-s|^{\xi}}&\;\geqslant\;\int_{0}^{2^{j}}{\mathrm{d}}t\int_{2^{j}}^{2^{j+1}}{\mathrm{d}}s\frac{|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{2}}{2c_{j}^{2-\gamma}2^{j\xi}}\\
&\succeq\frac{1}{2c_{j}^{2-\gamma}2^{j(\xi-2)}}\left|\frac{1}{2^{j}}\int_{0}^{2^{j}}x_{s}{\mathrm{d}}s-\frac{1}{2^{j}}\int_{2^{j}}^{2^{j+1}}x_{t}{\mathrm{d}}t\right|^{2}.\end{split}

This estimate is pointwise true whenever path increments are bounded by
c_{j}
.
Because Brownian increments are unbounded, the above bound only holds (for suitable
c_{j}
) with high probability. We rely on theory developed in
[
36
]
to make our arguments rigorous: For each averaged increment, we split Brownian motion into a good part, where fluctuations are actually bounded, and a bad part. On the good part we can implement the previous heuristic rigorously and obtain a Gaussian measure that we can work with. By choosing appropriate
c_{j}
, we can also show that the bad part can be neglected, which suffices to show Theorem
1.2
. Theorem
1.3
is a rigorous implementation of a recursive Gaussian confinement approach. In a first step, we restrict the increments of Brownian motion to fluctuate at most
\sqrt{T}
away from the origin (up to
\log
factors) over the entire time interval
[0,T]
. A simple Gaussian domination argument yields that
\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}
is more strongly concentrated (in a way rigorously introduced in Section
2.1
) than
\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha T^{\gamma/2-1},T,\xi}
. An application of Theorem
1.1
yields that the increments under this measure are not only bounded by
T^{1/2}
with high probability, but even (up to some corrections) by
T^{\beta/2}
with high probability. Here,
\beta
can be determined explicitly, depending on
\gamma
and
\xi
. In case
\beta<1
,
this implies that the increments of
\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}
are even more strongly concentrated, which yields a new improved variance. Iterating this leads in the exponent to the fixed-point of the map

\beta\to\xi-2+\beta-\frac{\beta\gamma}{2},

which evaluates to be
2(\xi-2)/\gamma
. For a more technical sketch of this approach we refer to the paragraph following Proposition
4.2
. Finally, we discuss the proof of Theorem
1.4
. As before, we are trying to get rid of

and replace it by a square. The shortcoming of the proof of Theorem
1.2
is that, at a higher level, we do not use that we expect already good concentration at lower levels.
To understand what we mean by this, consider the base case
j=0
. In a first step we condition Brownian motion to have ’small’ fluctuations on every interval of length
1
. This is true with probability at least
1-T^{-10}
for the radius
\sqrt{20\log(T)}
. The important thing here is to obtain a bound that tends to

, and this is why we need to choose

. An application of Lemma

lets us split Brownian motion into a good part, and a bad part. On the bounded increment part of Brownian motion we find that the averaged increments of neighboring intervals can be dominated by a Gaussian with variance as in equation (

). One can then bound increments of length
4
by these averaged increments; we refer to Proposition
5.3
for details.
These averages have very high probability to stay close to the origin, and so another application of (

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2}x_{s}{\mathrm{d}}s-\frac{1}{2}\int_{2}^{4}x_{t}{\mathrm{d}}t

is bounded, with very high probability. Doing this recursively yields (again with high probability) a dominating Gaussian measure with density as defined in (

in case no failure arises (i.e. having all increments bounded), and stop whenever the inductive reasoning breaks down. In case the induction fails, we simply dominate our measure by a Brownian motion with twice the variance; this is possible by an application of (GCI). It is crucial that we

follow the approach presented in

and split the problem into smaller blocks. This approach will always lead to

be the index set of the Riemann sum approximation. Because the density

\exp\left(\sum\limits_{(s,t)\in{\mathcal{A}}}\frac{\zeta|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{\gamma}-f(x_{t}-x_{s})}{1+|t-s|^{\xi}}\right)

is symmetric and quasi-concave on path space by assumption, the claim follows by the FKG-GCI inequality
[
37
, Theorem 2.7]
.
∎

2.2.
Variance decomposition

This section is devoted to proving Theorem
1.1
. We start by presenting a simpler argument which yields suboptimal bounds.
It is easily seen by (GCI) that

\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\xi}\preceq{\mathbb{P}}^{*}_{\alpha,T}

with

{\mathbb{P}}^{*}_{\alpha,T}({\mathrm{d}}x)\propto\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{T^{\xi}}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}s|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{2}\right){\mathbb{P}}({\mathrm{d}}x).

By a simple re-scaling argument and results from
[
36
]
we find that

{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}^{*}_{\alpha,T}}[|x_{T}|^{2}]/T={\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}^{*}_{T^{3-\xi}\alpha,1}}[|x_{1}|^{2}]=O_{T\to\infty}(T^{-(3-\xi)/2}).

We conclude that

{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}^{*}_{\alpha,T}}[|x_{T}|^{2}]\leq O(T^{(\xi-1)/2}).

This already suffices to show the second claim in Theorem
1.1
. Observe, however, that this estimate does not yield localized behaviour for any
\xi>1
. Compare this to the prediction made in (
1.3
), which predicts localized behaviour for
\xi<2
.
The problem of the one-step estimate is that locally, the time-decay is much smaller than
T^{\xi}
under
\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\xi}
. It is therefore
natural to expect that the path decomposition obtained in (
2.1
) together with tighter time-decay estimates for each averaged increment improves the order with which the normalized mean-square displacement vanishes.
Let us now make this multi-scale approach rigorous. For the remainder of this section
we treat slightly more general measures compared to
{\mathbb{P}}^{\mathbf{hier}}_{\alpha,T,\xi}
; this generalization will become useful later on. Let

\varphi_{\alpha,T}({\mathrm{d}}x)\propto\exp\left(-\alpha\sum\limits_{l=1}^{t}\frac{1}{2A_{l}^{2}}|\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{l}}|^{2}-\alpha\sum\limits_{l=1}^{t-1}\frac{1}{2A_{l}^{2}}|\overline{s}_{T-2^{l}}^{2^{l}}|^{2}\right){\mathbb{P}}({\mathrm{d}}x)

for some positive constants
A_{1},\dots,A_{t}
.
At the beginning of this section, we will work with general
A_{j}
and only specialize in the proof of Theorem
1.1
to
A_{j}=2^{(\xi-2)j/2}
. At the beginning of Section
5
, we finally define specific
A_{j}
which will remain unchanged for the remainder of this work.

Lemma 2.6
.

Take
A^{*}:=\max\{A_{j}^{2}:j\leq t\}
. Then,

(2.6)

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}^{\varphi_{\alpha,T}}\left[|\sigma_{T-1}-\sigma_{0}|^{2}\right]&\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t-1}{\mathbb{E}}^{\varphi_{\alpha,T}}\left[\big|\overline{s}_{T-2^{i}}^{2^{i}}\big|^{2}\right]+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}{\mathbb{E}}^{\varphi_{\alpha,T}}\left[\big|\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}\big|^{2}\right]\\
&+2\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{t-1}{\mathbb{E}}^{\varphi_{\alpha,T}}\left[|\overline{s}_{T-2^{j}}^{2^{j}}|^{2}\right]^{1/2}{\mathbb{E}}^{\varphi_{\alpha,T}}\left[|\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}|^{2}\right]^{1/2}\\
&\leq 2\alpha^{-1}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}A_{i}^{2}+4\alpha^{-1}A^{*}t^{2}.\end{split}

Proof.

Recall equation (
2.1
); this directly implies that we have the equality

(2.7)

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}^{\varphi_{\alpha,T}}\left[|\sigma_{T-1}-\sigma_{0}|^{2}\right]&={\mathbb{E}}^{\varphi_{\alpha,T}}\Big[\Big|\sum\limits_{j=1}^{t-1}\overline{s}_{T-2^{j}}^{2^{j}}\Big|^{2}\Big]\\
&+{\mathbb{E}}^{\varphi_{\alpha,T}}\Big[\Big|\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}\Big|^{2}\Big]+2\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{t-1}{\mathbb{E}}^{\varphi_{\alpha,T}}\left[\overline{s}_{T-2^{j}}^{2^{j}}\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}\right].\end{split}

The cross terms can be estimated by Cauchy-Schwarz; indeed,

{\mathbb{E}}^{\varphi_{\alpha,T}}\left[\overline{s}_{T-2^{j}}^{2^{j}}\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}\right]\leq{\mathbb{E}}^{\varphi_{\alpha,T}}\left[|\overline{s}_{T-2^{j}}^{2^{j}}|^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbb{E}}^{\varphi_{\alpha,T}}\left[|\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}|^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\alpha^{-1}A^{*}

as
\varphi_{\alpha,T}
specifies the variance of all averaged increments. As there are
(t-1)t\leq t^{2}
cross-terms, we find

2\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{t-1}{\mathbb{E}}^{\varphi_{\alpha,T}}\left[\overline{s}_{T-2^{j}}^{2^{j}}\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}\right]\leq\alpha^{-1}2t^{2}A^{*}.

The first two sums in (
2.7
) can be treated similarly, leading to the bound

{\mathbb{E}}^{\varphi_{\alpha,T}}\left[|\sigma_{T-1}-\sigma_{0}|^{2}\right]\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t-1}{\mathbb{E}}^{\varphi_{\alpha,T}}\left[\big|\overline{s}_{T-2^{i}}^{2^{i}}\big|^{2}\right]+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}{\mathbb{E}}^{\varphi_{\alpha,T}}\left[\big|\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}\big|^{2}\right]+2\alpha^{-1}(t+t^{2})A^{*}.

The second inequality in (
2.6
) follows again by using the variance given in the Gaussian measure
\varphi_{\alpha,T}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let us first discuss the case
\xi\geq 2
. Here, the proof is a direct application of Lemma
2.6
.
By Lemma
2.4
there exists
\beta>0
so that

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\xi}}[|\sigma_{T-1}-\sigma_{0}|^{2}]\leq{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}^{\mathbf{hier}}_{\beta,T,\xi}}[|\sigma_{T-1}-\sigma_{0}|^{2}].

Moreover,
{\mathbb{P}}^{\mathbf{hier}}_{\beta,t,\xi}
simply is
\varphi_{\beta,T}
with
A_{j}^{2}=2^{(\xi-2)j}
. It then follows from equation (
2.6
) and results from
[
36
, Section 4]
that

\begin{split}\frac{1}{4}{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\xi}}[|x_{T}|^{2}]&\leq{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\xi}}[|x_{T}-\sigma_{T_{1}}|^{2}]+{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}^{\mathbf{hier}}_{\beta,T,\xi}}[|\sigma_{0}|^{2}]+{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\xi}}\left[|\sigma_{T-1}-\sigma_{0}|^{2}\right]\\
&\leq 3\min(1,\alpha^{-1/2})+\frac{2}{\beta}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}A_{i}^{2}+4\beta^{-1}A^{*}\log(T)^{2}\\
&=3\min(1,\alpha^{-1/2})+\frac{2}{\beta}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{t}2^{(\xi-2)l}+4\beta^{-1}T^{\xi-2}\log(T)^{2}.\end{split}

As
l\leq t
the claim follows.
In case of
\xi<2
, we find that the
A_{j}
are summable (as they decay exponentially). Together with the first bound obtained in (
2.6
), the result follows by summing all
A_{j}
and the same argument as before.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

Studying the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we find that the bound on the mean-square displacement improves from

to
T^{\xi-2}\log(T)^{2}
. In particular, we obtain sub-diffusivity for

and localization for
\xi\in(1,2)

]
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

This Section is devoted to proving Theorem
1.2
. Let us continue to write
A_{j}=2^{j(\xi-2)/2}
. Our approach for the proof will rely on the previously obtained decomposition

(3.1)

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}\left[|\sigma_{T-1}-\sigma_{0}|^{2}\right]&\leq{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}\left[\big|\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t-1}\overline{s}_{T-2^{i}}^{2^{i}}\big|^{2}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}\left[\big|\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}\big|^{2}\right]\\
&+2\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{t-1}{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}\left[|\overline{s}_{T-2^{j}}^{2^{j}}|^{2}\right]^{1/2}{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}\left[|\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}|^{2}\right]^{1/2}\\
&\leq 2\alpha^{-1}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}A_{i}^{2}+4\alpha^{-1}A^{*}t^{2}\\
&\leq\alpha^{-1}Ct^{2}A^{*}\end{split}

for some
C>0
.
The first two inequalities in (
3.1
) follow simply by Cauchy-Schwarz. Note that the first estimate is better in case one can show that the series all converge. Indeed, in this case one can avoid the logarithmic divergence present in the final bound. We will use the second bound in the regime
\xi>1+\gamma/2
and the first bound in case
\xi<1+\gamma/2
. Moreover, note that it suffices to bound
{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}\left[|\sigma_{T-1}-\sigma_{0}|^{2}\right]
by the same argument as before.

In both regimes of
\xi
we need to bound
A_{j}
for
\gamma
fixed. Finally, recall that the we can study the measure
\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}
instead of
\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,f,\xi}
thanks to the results in Section
2.1
.
Define for
i\leq t
the sets

B_{i}=\left\{\sup\limits_{0\leq s<t\leq 2^{i}}|x_{t}-x_{s}|\leq\sqrt{2^{i}20\log(2^{i})}\right\}.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any
i\leq t
,

{\mathbb{P}}(B_{i})\geq 1-2^{-10i}
.

Proof.

By Brownian rescaling it suffices to show

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup\limits_{0\leq s<t\leq 1}|x_{t}-x_{s}|\geq\sqrt{20\log(2^{i})}\right).

Moreover,

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup\limits_{0\leq s<t\leq 1}|x_{t}-x_{s}|\geq\sqrt{20\log(2^{i})}\right)&\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup\limits_{0<t\leq 1}|x_{t}|\geq 2\sqrt{20\log(2^{i})}\right)\\
&=2{\mathbb{P}}\left(x_{1}\geq 2\sqrt{20\log(2^{i})}\right)\\
&\leq 2^{-10i}.\end{split}

Here, the last equation follows by the symmetry of the distribution of
x_{1}
and the reflection principle.
∎

Our goal is now to apply Lemma
2.2
. Because this only works in finite dimensions, we need to approximate
{\mathbb{P}}
weakly by finite-dimensional Gaussians. This is possible by e.g. Theorem 3.1 in
[
9
]
. In what follows
\mu\in\{\mu^{m}:m\geq 1\}
will always reference an approximation to
{\mathbb{P}}
. Unless otherwise specified, the following results hold uniformly for all
\mu=\mu^{m}
with
m
large enough. Moreover, note that the sets
B_{i}
have
{\mathbb{P}}
-boundary measure
0
, which implies that for
m
large enough,
\mu^{m}(B_{j})\geq 1-2^{-10^{i}}+\epsilon(m)
. We will omit this additional
\epsilon
for the sake of readability. As we have noted earlier, it suffices to bound

{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}\left[\big|\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}\big|^{2}\right]

for every
i\leq t
. We therefore fix
i\leq t
for the remainder of this Section and show that each variance is bounded by essentially
2^{i(\xi-1-\gamma/2)}
.

Recall that

{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\big|\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}\big|^{2}\right]=O(2^{i})

due to the normalization in the definition of
\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}
.
Let us now write

\mu=(1-\delta)\rho+\delta\rho^{\prime},

where the decomposition stems from Lemma
2.2
applied to the set
B_{i}
. Therefore,
\delta\leq 2^{-10i}
and
\supp(\rho)\subseteq 64B_{i}
. Moreover,
\rho^{\prime}\preceq\mu^{\times 2}
.
Let us now write

\tilde{\mu}_{\alpha,T,\xi,\gamma}=(1-w_{1})\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha,T,\xi,\gamma}+w_{1}\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}_{\alpha,T,\xi,\gamma}.

Proposition 3.2
.

The inequality

w_{1}\leq\delta

holds.

Proof.

We can explicitly calculate

(1-w_{1})=(1-\delta)\frac{Z_{\rho}}{Z_{\mu}}.

Because
\rho\preceq\mu
we have
Z_{\rho}/Z_{\mu}\geq 1
(as the function we integrate over is the (uniformly bounded) limit of symmetric and quasi-concave functions). Therefore, the claim is immediate.
∎

Define the measure

\mu^{\prime}({\mathrm{d}}x)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha(2^{i/2}\sqrt{20\log(2^{2i})})^{\gamma-2}}{2^{i(\xi-2)}}\Big|\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}\Big|^{2}\right)\mu({\mathrm{d}}x).

Proposition 3.3
.

It holds that

\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha,T,\xi,\gamma}\preceq\mu^{\prime}.

Proof.

Simply writing down the density, we find

\begin{split}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha,T,\xi,\gamma}}{{\mathrm{d}}\mu^{\prime}}\propto\exp\left(-\alpha\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}s\frac{|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{\gamma}}{1+|t-s|^{\xi}}+\frac{\alpha(2^{i/2}\sqrt{20\log(2^{2i})})^{\gamma-2}}{2^{2i(\xi-2)}}\Big|\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}\Big|^{2}\right).\end{split}

On the support of
\rho
we have that

|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{\gamma-2}\geq\left(2^{i/2}\sqrt{20\log(2^{2i})}\right)^{\gamma-2}.

Moreover, the time decay is at worst
2^{i(\xi-2)}
. This, together with
\rho\preceq\mu
shows

\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}\preceq\tilde{\mu}_{\frac{\alpha(2^{i/2}\sqrt{20\log(2^{2i})})^{\gamma-2}}{2^{i(\xi-2)}},2^{i},0}.

The result then follows from Proposition
2.3
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
, (1) and (2).

As we argued before, it suffices to bound

{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}\left[\big|\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}\big|^{2}\right]

for every
i\leq t
.
Write
f_{n}(x)=\min(n,|\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}|^{2})
. Note that
x\mapsto\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}
is a linear function on path space. Therefore,
f_{n}
is a symmetric,quasi-convex, continuous and bounded function on path space.
By what we have just shown,

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{\mu}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}\left[f_{n}\right]&\leq{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}\left[\big|f_{n}\big|^{2}\right]+2^{-10i}{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{\rho^{\prime}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}\left[\big|f_{n}\big|^{2}\right]\\
&\leq{\mathbb{E}}^{\mu^{\prime}}\left[\big|f_{n}\big|^{2}\right]+2^{-8i}.\end{split}

First letting
m\to\infty
and then
n\to\infty
therefore yields the bound

{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}\left[\big|\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}\big|^{2}\right]\leq{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}^{\prime}}\left[\big|\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}\big|^{2}\right]+2^{-8i}

Now, a simple computation shows the existence of a constant
C>0
so that

\frac{\alpha(2^{i/2}\sqrt{20\log(2^{2i})})^{\gamma-2}}{2^{i(\xi-2)}}\geq Ci^{-2}\alpha 2^{i(\gamma/2+1-\xi)}.

In particular,

{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}^{\prime}}\left[\big|\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}\big|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{C}\alpha^{-1}i^{2}2^{-i(\gamma/2+1-\xi)}.

This shows the desired claim by setting
i=t
, which is the worst index for
\xi\in(1+\gamma/2,2+\gamma/2)
. In the case
\xi<1+\gamma/2
, we use the same approach to bound

{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}\left[\big|\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}\big|^{2}\right]\leq{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}^{\prime}}\left[\big|\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{i}}\big|^{2}\right]+2^{-8i}\leq C2^{-i\delta}

for some
\delta>0
and
C>0
. In particular, the sum over all scales of averaged increments is now a convergent series, which implies via the first variance bound in (
2.4
) that the variance stays bounded. This shows the claim.
∎

Proof of Theorem

(3).

For the last assertion, we simply copy the proof in the beginning of Section

. By restricting Brownian motion on

to the set

\left\{\sup\limits_{s,t\in[0,T]}|x_{t}-x_{s}|\leq\sqrt{20T\log T}\right\}

and doing all finite-dimensional approximations again,
we find that

{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}[|x_{T}|^{2}]\leq{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{T^{-\xi+\gamma/2-1}(20\log(T))^{\gamma-2},T,0}}[|x_{T}|^{2}]+T^{-10}.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.3
: recursive Gaussian confinement

Recall that by FKG-GCI, in order to prove Theorem
1.3
, it suffices to show

{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}[|x_{T}|^{2}]\leq T^{-1}+64^{2-\gamma}\frac{2}{\alpha\gamma}T^{\frac{2}{\gamma}(\xi-2)+10\log_{T}\log(T)^{2}/\gamma}.

As our approach relies on iterating and improving localization bounds, we start by studying the type of maps we iterate.

Proposition 4.1
.

Fix
C>0
and
d<1
. Let

h:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}},x\mapsto C+dx.

Then,
h
has a unique fixed point at
x^{*}=C/(1-d)
.
Moreover, with
c=|\log(d)|

|x^{*}-h^{n}(1)|\leq\frac{C}{1-d}\exp(-nc).

Here,
h^{n}
is defined recursively for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
by
h^{n}(x)=h(h^{n-1}(x))
and
h^{1}:=h
.

Proof.

The fact that
x^{*}
is the unique fixed point is obvious. Exponential speed of convergence can be seen by writing

h^{n}(1)=d^{n}+C\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}d^{k}.

This yields via the geometric series

|h^{n}(1)-x^{*}|\leq d^{n}\frac{C}{1-d}.

The claim follows directly.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Fix
\beta>0
. Let
\mu_{\beta}
be a Gaussian path measure satisfying
\mu_{\beta}\preceq{\mathbb{P}}
as well as
\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mu_{\beta}}[x_{s}^{2}]\leq T^{\beta}
. Then,

\mu_{\beta}\left(\sup\limits_{0\leq s\leq T}|x_{s}|\geq T^{\beta/2}\sqrt{40\log(T)}\right)\leq 4T^{-9}.

Proof.

Abbreviate
C=T^{\beta/2}\sqrt{40\log(T)}
. Note the set inclusion

\left\{\sup\limits_{0\leq s\leq T}|x_{s}|\geq C\right\}\subseteq\left\{\sup\limits_{j\in[0,T]\cap{\mathbb{N}}}|x_{j}|\geq C/2\right\}\cup\left(\bigcup\limits_{j=0}^{T-1}\left\{\sup\limits_{j\leq s<t\leq j+1}|x_{t}-x_{s}|\geq C/2\right\}\right).

We bound all sets individually and perform a union bound.
First, by (GCI), standard tail bounds for Gaussians and the definition of
C
it holds that

\mu_{\beta}\left(\sup\limits_{j\in[0,T]\cap{\mathbb{N}}}|x_{j}|\leq C/2\right)\geq\prod\limits_{j=0}^{T}\mu_{\beta}\left(|x_{j}|\leq C/2\right)\geq(1-T^{-10})^{T}\geq 1-T^{-9}.

Similarly,

\begin{split}\mu_{\beta}\left(\sup\limits_{j\leq s<t\leq j+1}|x_{t}-x_{s}|\geq C/2\right)&\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup\limits_{0\leq s<t\leq 1}|x_{t}-x_{s}|\geq C/2\right)\\
&\leq 2{\mathbb{P}}(|x_{1}|\geq C/2)\\
&\leq 2\exp(-T^{\beta})T^{-10}.\end{split}

Here, the first inequality follows from (GCI) and the second inequality via the reflection principle of Brownian motion. The overall claim follows by union bounding.
∎

In what follows we abbreviate
C(T)=\sqrt{40\log(T)}
and
D(T)=\log_{T}(\log(T)+\log(T)^{2})
.
The previous Proposition has the following implication: with very high probability and up to logarithmic corrections,

\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}\preceq\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha T^{(\gamma-2)/2},T,\xi}.

This follows by bounding the probability of fluctuations of
\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma}
by that of Brownian motion and estimating on the set
\{\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}|x_{t}-x_{s}|\leq T^{1/2}\}

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}s\frac{|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{\gamma}}{1+|t-s|^{\xi}}&=\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}s|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{\gamma-2}\frac{|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{2}}{1+|t-s|^{\xi}}\\
&\geq T^{(\gamma-2)/2}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}s\frac{|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{2}}{1+|t-s|^{\xi}}.\end{split}

An application of Theorem
1.1
and (GCI) yields

\sup\limits_{s\leq T}{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha T^{(\gamma-2)/2},T,\xi}}[|x_{s}|^{2}]\leq 1+\alpha^{-1}T^{(2-\gamma)/2+\xi-2}(\log(T)+\log(T)^{2}).

To leading order this variance bound behaves as
T^{\xi-1-\gamma/2}
, which already yields sub-diffusive behaviour as soon as
\xi<2+\gamma/2
. Let us take the new exponent to be
\beta=\xi-1-\gamma/2<1
. Ignoring the log-factors again for a second, we find that the increments under
\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}
are not only contained in
T^{1/2}
, but even in
T^{\beta/2}
with (still) very high probability. This follows from another application of Proposition
4.2
to the measure
\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha T^{(\gamma-2)/2},T,\xi}
. Iterating this procedure yields an exponent of
T
getting close to the fix-point of the map

(4.1)

\beta\mapsto\xi-2+\beta-\frac{\beta\gamma}{2},

see Proposition
4.1
. The iteration we perform therefore does not yield different regimes of sub-diffusivity, but rather improved rates. Indeed, let us compare the iterated bound and the one-step bound. While it is not true for any combination of
\gamma>0
and
\xi>0
that

\frac{2}{\gamma}(\xi-2)<\xi-1-\frac{\gamma}{2},

simple algebra shows that the inequality holds whenever
\xi-1-\gamma/2<1
. As this is exactly the range of parameters that we are interested in, iterating always improves the exponent. Moreover, note that a first iteration only yields an exponent smaller than
1
whenever
\xi<2+\gamma/2
. Therefore, our approach does not work outside of this regime.

In what follows we will implement our iteration strategy rigorously.
We define the set

R(\beta):=\left\{\sup\limits_{0\leq s<t\leq T}|x_{t}-x_{s}|\leq\beta\right\},

which will serve as our choice for
K
in Lemma
2.2
.
In order to implement the desired recursion we will rely heavily on Lemma
2.2
. As noted in the previous section, this Lemma is only valid for finite-dimensional Gaussians. Therefore, we will approximate
{\mathbb{P}}
weakly by a sequence of finite-dimensional, centred Gaussian measures
(\mu^{m})_{m\geq 1}
on
C\left([0,T];{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\right)
. Let us also note that the set
R(\beta)
has
{\mathbb{P}}
-boundary measure
0
for any
\beta>0
. It follows that, as
m\to\infty
,

\tilde{\mu}^{m}_{\alpha,T,\xi}\left(R(\beta)\right)\to\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\xi}\left(R(\beta)\right).

Theorem
1.1
together with Proposition
4.2
yield with
\eta=C(T)\sqrt{D(T)T^{(\xi-2)}}
that

\tilde{\mu}^{m}_{\alpha,T,\xi}\left(R(\eta)\right)\geq 1-4T^{-9}-\tilde{\epsilon}(m).

Due to
\tilde{\epsilon}(m)\to 0
as
m\to\infty
we will omit this additional factor for sake of convenience. Alternatively, one could replace
c
by
c/(1+\delta)
, where
\delta
can be arbitrarily small (by choosing
m
arbitrarily big).
In what follows
\mu\in\{\mu^{m}:m\geq 1\}

5.
Proof of Theorem

We begin this Section by discussing some differences and similarities to the approach presented in the previous Section. On a technical level, we will again approximate Brownian motion by a finite-dimensional Gaussian and then iteratively decompose the approximation into sets with better and better confinement (compare Lemma
5.4
and Lemma
4.3
). The decomposition is, however, a different one. In the previous Section, we replaced the time-decay by its worst case estimate and restricted the path to have bounded fluctuations. We iterated this procedure, obtaining better and better concentration results for almost all mass. Here, we provide a different approach: instead of recursively iterating ’global confinement’, we recursively improve spatial localization. In a first step, we bound the fluctuation of level
0
increments (which in our language are just
\sigma_{j}
for
0\leq j\leq T-1
). This gives us the possibility to obtain localization results for level
1
averaged increments. Level
1
averaged increments together with level
0
increments can then be used to pointwise bound fluctuations on twice the length scale (see Proposition
5.3
). Basically, we are using localization properties of the previous level to obtain localization properties for the next level and repeat. We then observe that if
\xi<2
and
\alpha=O(\log(T))
, this iteration leads to bounded variances for all averaged increments! Because the variance of the path at time
T
is the sum of logarithmically many averaged increments, this implies logarithmic fluctuations at most. We include all details once again (which we believe leads to greater clarity, at the cost of some repetition).

Fix
\gamma,\xi
and
\alpha
.
In this section the following sequence of numbers will be used throughout. At first, define

. To determine the next numbers in the sequence, the following recursive relations are used:

For future use we abbreviate

w^{*}=\prod\limits_{s=1}^{t}(1-\delta_{s})\frac{Z_{\nu_{t}}}{Z_{\mu}}\geq(1-T^{-8})^{t}\geq 1-T^{-7}.

Here, the first inequality follows as
\nu_{t}\preceq\mu
and the function we integrate over is a uniformly bounded limit of symmetric and quasi-concave functions. We therefore find that

(5.4)

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{t}w(j)\leq T^{-7},

as
\sum_{j=1}^{t}w(j)+w^{*}=1
.

Lemma 5.5
.

The following inequalities hold

(1)

(\tilde{\nu}_{t})_{\alpha\beta,T,\gamma,\xi}\preceq\Lambda_{\alpha,t,T}(\mu)
;

(2)

For all
s\leq t
,
(\tilde{\nu}^{\prime}_{s})_{\alpha\beta,T,\gamma,\xi}\preceq\mu^{\times 2}
.

Proof.

We start with showing

(\tilde{\nu}_{t})_{\alpha\beta,T,\gamma,\xi}\preceq\mu_{\alpha,T}^{\mathbf{hier}},

which is just the first claimed inequality by recalling the introduced notation.
Explicitly writing the density we find

\begin{split}&\frac{{\mathrm{d}}(\tilde{\nu}_{t})_{\alpha\beta,T,\gamma,\xi}}{{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\alpha,T}^{\mathbf{hier}}}\\
&\propto\exp\left(-\alpha\beta\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}s\frac{|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{\gamma}}{1+|t-s|^{\xi}}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{t}A_{l}^{-2}\sum\limits_{s=0}^{{T}-2^{l}}\big|\overline{s}_{s2^{l}}^{2^{l}}\big|^{2}\right).\end{split}

Recall from Lemma
5.4
that

\supp(\nu_{t})\subseteq\bigcap\limits_{s=1}^{t}\left\{R_{s}\leq 256\sqrt{20t}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{s-1}A_{k}\right\}.

Moreover, recall also that

A_{j}=r_{j}^{1-\frac{\gamma}{2}}2^{\frac{j}{2}(\xi-2)}.

By definition we then have, for
s,t
being in the same block of length
2^{j}
,

(5.5)

\begin{split}\frac{|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{\gamma}}{1+|t-s|^{\xi}}&=|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{2}\frac{1}{|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{2-\gamma}}\frac{1}{1+|t-s|^{\xi}}\\
&\geq|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{2}\frac{1}{2r_{j}^{2-\gamma}2^{j\xi}\beta}\\
&=|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{2}\frac{1}{2A_{j}^{2}2^{2j}\beta}.\end{split}

Together with Proposition
2.3
this shows

\beta\int_{0}^{{2^{j-1}}}{\mathrm{d}}t\int_{{2^{j-1}}}^{2^{j}}{\mathrm{d}}s\frac{|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{\gamma}}{1+|t-s|^{\xi}}\geq\frac{1}{2A_{j}^{2}}|\overline{s}_{0}^{2^{j-1}}-\overline{s}_{2^{j-1}}^{2^{j-1}}|^{2}.

This, in turn, already verifies the first claimed statement. A similar argument works to prove the second statement. Indeed, by construction
\Lambda_{\alpha,j-1,T}(\nu^{\prime}_{j})\preceq\mu^{\times 2}
. But since
\supp(\nu_{j}^{\prime})\subseteq 64(C_{0}\cap\dots\cap C_{j-2})
it holds true that

\beta\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}s\frac{|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{\gamma}}{1+|t-s|^{\xi}}+\sum\limits_{l=1}^{j-1}A_{l}^{-2}\sum\limits_{s=0}^{T-2^{l}}\big|\overline{s}_{s2^{l}}^{2^{l}}\big|^{2}

is the uniformly bounded limit of symmetric and quasi-concave functions on path space
\nu_{j}^{\prime}
-almost surely by the same calculations as before via Proposition
5.3
.
The second claim then simply follows from the equation

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}(\tilde{\nu}^{\prime}_{j})_{\alpha\beta,T,\gamma,\xi}}{{\mathrm{d}}\mu^{\times 2}}=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}(\tilde{\nu}^{\prime}_{j})_{\alpha\beta,T,\gamma,\xi}}{{\mathrm{d}}\Lambda_{\alpha,j-1,T}(\nu_{j}^{\prime})}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\Lambda_{\alpha,j-1,T}(\nu_{j}^{\prime})}{{\mathrm{d}}\mu^{\times 2}}.\qed

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

Write
f_{n}(x):=\min\{x^{2},n\}
. Note that
f_{n}
is increasing, symmetric, continuous and bounded.
By Lemma
5.5
it is seen that

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}^{(\tilde{\mu}^{m})_{\alpha\beta,T,\gamma,\xi}}[f_{n}(x_{T})]&\leq\sum\limits_{j=1}^{t}w(j){\mathbb{E}}^{(\tilde{\nu}^{\prime}_{j})_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}[f_{n}(x_{T})]+w^{*}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mu_{\alpha,T}^{\textbf{hier}}}[f_{n}(x_{T})]\\
&\leq\sum\limits_{j=1}^{t}w(j){\mathbb{E}}^{(\mu^{m})^{\times 2}}[f_{n}(x_{T})]+w^{*}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mu_{\alpha,T}^{\textbf{hier}}}[f_{n}(x_{T})]\\
&\leq 2T^{-6}+(1-T^{-7}){\mathbb{E}}^{\mu_{\alpha,T}^{\textbf{hier}}}[f_{n}(x_{T})].\end{split}

Here, the first inequality follows by (GCI), and the second by recalling equation (
5.4
).
By first taking
m\to\infty
and then by monotone convergence, we obtain the bound

{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha\beta,\gamma,\xi,T}}[|x_{T}|^{2}]\leq(1-T^{-7}){\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{\alpha}^{\textbf{hier}}}[|x_{T}|^{2}]+T^{-6}.\qed

In order to prove Theorem
1.3
it remains to estimate
{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}^{\mathbf{hier}}_{\alpha,T}}[|x_{T}|^{2}]
. We begin by checking that the variances of the averaged increments are not too large; this is done by studying the recursive relations that are used to define
A_{j}
and
r_{j}
. In a second step we once again rely on Lemma
2.6
to bound the endpoint variance by averaged increments, for which we can read the variance directly off the measure.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
\xi<2
. Then, there exists a constant
c_{\ref{prop:bounded_recursion}}>0
such that

r_{j}\leq(\sqrt{20}+c_{\ref{prop:bounded_recursion}})^{2/\gamma}

for all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.

Recall that by definition with
\epsilon=\frac{\gamma}{2}\in(0,1)
,
C=256
and
c=(2-\xi)/2>0
that

r_{j+1}=r_{j}+Cr^{1-\epsilon}2^{-c(j+1)}.

Clearly
r_{j+1}\geq r_{j}
for all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Take
s_{n}:=r_{n}^{\epsilon}
. By the mean-value Theorem applied to
x\to x^{\epsilon}
, there exists
\xi\in[r_{n},r_{n+1}]
so that

s_{n+1}-s_{n}=\epsilon\xi^{\epsilon-1}(r_{n+1}-r_{n}).

By definition
r_{n+1}-r_{n}=C2^{-cn}r_{n}^{1-\epsilon}
. As
1-\epsilon<0
,
\xi^{1-\epsilon}\leq r_{n}^{1-\epsilon}
. Therefore,

s_{n+1}-s_{n}\leq\epsilon C2^{-cn}.

Using the geometric series and telescoping, we obtain the bound

s_{n}-s_{0}\leq\epsilon C\frac{1}{1-2^{-c}}.

Re-arranging and taking roots, we find

r_{n}\leq\left(20^{\gamma/2}+\epsilon C\frac{1}{1-2^{c}}\right)^{2/\gamma}.

The right hand side is independent of
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, which shows the claim.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

The proof follows from Lemma
2.6
by noting that
A^{*}
is bounded. Therefore, we sum over logarithmically many terms, which shows the claim.
∎

Appendix A
Closed-form solution for periodic Gaussian measures

In this section we show how to find an asymptotic formula for the variance of the path at arbitrary time under a Gaussian path measure. While the formula has been known since at least
[
39
]
(see especially formula (3.13)), we could not find a source on how to actually arrive at the result in detail. In the following all calculations are performed and additional assumptions/alterations to the model are mentioned explicitly.

In comparison to the models that we treat in general, one has to add the assumption that
\rho(t)=\rho(T-t)
for all
t\leq T
. If this is apriori not the case, it is possible to add the density

\exp\left(-\alpha\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t{\mathrm{d}}s\rho(|t-s-T|)|x_{t}-x_{s}|^{2}\right)

to symmetrize
\rho
.
Moreover, we cannot take
{\mathbb{P}}
as Brownian motion started at
0
and no boundary condition at the end. Instead, we will take the underlying Brownian motion to have periodic boundary conditions. The resulting process is denoted by
{\mathbb{P}}^{*}_{T}
and only defined on the
\sigma
-algebra generated by increments of the path. The path measure we are interested in is given by

(A.1)

\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}^{*}_{\epsilon,\alpha,T}({\mathrm{d}}x)\propto\exp\left(-\epsilon\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}sx_{s}^{2}-\alpha\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t{\mathrm{d}}s\rho(|t-s|)(x_{t}-x_{s})^{2}\right){\mathbb{P}}^{*}_{T}({\mathrm{d}}x).

For
\epsilon=0
we abbreviate
\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{0,\alpha,T}^{*}=\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}^{*}
. This measure is not pinned down, which implies that the variance of
x_{s}
for
s\leq T
is unbounded. We fix this by studying the variance of
x_{T}-x_{T/2}
, which is a translation invariant quantity. It is intuitive to say that

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}}[x_{T}^{2}]\approx{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}^{*}_{\alpha,T}}\left[(x_{T}-x_{T/2})^{2}\right]

for
T
large.
We will not rigorously justify this approximation here, but instead postpone it to future work. Similarly, we will first take the limit in volume and then look at the variance at large times; i.e. we let
\lim_{T\to\infty}\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}^{*}_{\alpha,T}=\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}^{*}_{\alpha,\infty}
and then calculate
{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}^{*}_{\alpha,\infty}}[x_{n}^{2}]
for
n>0
arbitrary. The fact that the joint limit and the iterated limit coincide is again intuitively clear, but will not be shown rigorously in this work. The goal for the remainder of this note is to show the following formula.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
m,n>0
. It holds that

\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}^{*}_{\alpha,T}}[|x_{m}-x_{n}|^{2}]=2\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}}k\frac{1-\cos\big(2\pi(n-m)k\big)}{4\pi^{2}k^{2}+\alpha(\hat{\rho}(0)-\hat{\rho}(k))}.

Discretizing the measure in (
A.1
), we find that the variance of
x_{m}-x_{n}
is equivalent to calculating the variance of
x_{\tilde{N}m}-x_{\tilde{N}n}
under the measure proportional to

(A.2)

\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{\tilde{N}^{2}}\sum\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq\tilde{N}T}\rho(|i-j|/\tilde{N})\|x_{i/\tilde{N}}-x_{j/\tilde{N}}\|^{2}-\frac{\tilde{N}}{2}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\tilde{N}T}\|x_{j/\tilde{N}}-x_{(j-1)\tilde{N}}\|^{2}\right){\mathrm{d}}x

as
\tilde{N}\to\infty
. In what follows we abbreviate
N=\tilde{N}T
and assume
N
to be even.
Writing the interaction in matrix form, we find that the above can be expressed as

\,{\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{2}\langle x,Ax\rangle}\,{\mathrm{d}}x,

where
A
is given by

A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}&a_{N-1}&...&a_{2}&a_{1}\\
a_{1}&a_{0}&a_{N-1}&...&a_{2}\\
...&a_{1}&a_{0}&...&...\\
a_{N-2}&...&...&...&a_{N-1}\\
a_{N-1}&a_{N-2}&...&...&a_{0}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

A
is not invertible since the process is not pinned down. We fix this by replacing
a_{0}
by
a_{0}+\epsilon
, where we will let
\epsilon\to 0
later on. In our above notation this means that we switch to working with
\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\epsilon,\alpha,T}^{*}
and take
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\epsilon,\alpha,T}^{*}
subsequently. Taking
\epsilon>0
leads to
A
being invertible without destroying any of the symmetry. We denote the resulting matrix by
A^{\epsilon}
.

Since
A^{\epsilon}
is symmetric, we have the additional symmetry
a_{j}=a_{N-j}
. Calculating the constants explicitly gives

a_{0}=\tilde{N}+\frac{\alpha}{\tilde{N}^{2}}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{N}\rho(l/\tilde{N})+\epsilon,

a_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{N}-\frac{\alpha}{2\tilde{N}^{2}}\rho(1/\tilde{N}),

a_{j}=\frac{\alpha}{2\tilde{N}^{2}}\rho(j/\tilde{N})

for all
j\leq N/2
. All other variables are fixed by symmetry.
Stated in matrix form, it holds that

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}^{*}_{\alpha,T}}\left[(x_{m}-x_{n})^{2}\right]=\lim\limits_{\tilde{N}\to\infty}\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}(A^{\epsilon}_{\tilde{N}m,\tilde{N}m})^{-1}+(A^{\epsilon}_{\tilde{N}n,\tilde{N}n})^{-1}-2(A^{\epsilon}_{\tilde{N}m,\tilde{N}n})^{-1}.

One identifies
A
and therefore
A^{\epsilon}
as being circulant, which means that
A^{\epsilon}
acts as convolution by the vector

a=(...,a_{-1},a_{0},a_{1},...)

with
a_{j}=a_{j+N}
for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. This directly implies that the discrete Fourier transform
{\mathcal{F}}_{N}
of order
N
diagonalizes
A^{\epsilon}
in the usual sense.
In fact, a simple calculation shows that
{\mathcal{F}}_{N}A=\hat{a}{\mathcal{F}}_{N}
. Here,
\hat{a}
indicates multiplication by the Fourier modes. By the fact that
A^{\epsilon}
is positive definite and symmetric, we find that
\hat{a}(k)>0
for all
k\leq N
.
It then makes sense to define the multiplication operator
1/\hat{a}
, which implies
(A^{\epsilon})^{-1}={\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{-1}\frac{1}{\hat{a}}{\mathcal{F}}_{N}
.

Proposition A.2
.

Let
n,m\leq N
. Then, it holds that

\begin{split}(A^{\epsilon})^{-1}_{n,m}&=\langle\delta_{n},(A^{\epsilon})^{-1}\delta_{m}\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{k\in V}\frac{\,{\rm e}^{i2\pi(n-m)k/N}\,}{\!\!\epsilon\!+\!\tilde{N}\!+\!\frac{\alpha}{\tilde{N}^{2}}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{N}\rho(l/\tilde{N})-\cos(-2\pi k/N)\tilde{N}-\frac{\alpha}{\tilde{N}^{2}}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{N-2}\cos(-2\pi lk/N)\rho(l/\tilde{N})}.\end{split}

In particular,

\begin{split}&{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}^{*}_{\alpha,T}}\left[(x_{m}-x_{n})^{2}\right]\\
&=\lim\limits_{\tilde{N}\to\infty}\frac{2}{N}\sum\limits_{k\in V}\frac{1-\cos(2\pi(n-m)k/N)}{\tilde{N}+\frac{\alpha}{\tilde{N}^{2}}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{N}\rho(l/\tilde{N})-\cos(-2\pi k/N)\tilde{N}-\frac{\alpha}{\tilde{N}^{2}}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{N-2}\cos(-2\pi lk/N)\rho(l/\tilde{N})}.\end{split}

Proof.

Define the sequence

x_{m}(k)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\,{\rm e}^{-i2\pi mk/N}\,.

We find

\begin{split}\langle\delta_{n},(A^{\epsilon})^{-1}\delta_{m}\rangle&=\langle\delta_{n},{\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}\hat{a}}{\mathcal{F}}_{N}\delta_{m}\rangle\\
&=\langle\delta_{n},{\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}\hat{a}}x_{m}\rangle.\end{split}

By definition

(A.3)

\left[{\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}\hat{a}}x_{m}\right](n)=\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}\sum\limits_{l\in V}\,{\rm e}^{i2\pi nl/N}\,\frac{1}{\hat{a}(l)}\,{\rm e}^{-i2\pi ml/N}\,=\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}\sum\limits_{l\in V}\,{\rm e}^{i2\pi(n-m)l/N}\,\frac{1}{\hat{a}(l)}.

Inserting the definitions again, we find how
\hat{a}
relates to
\rho
. In fact,

\begin{split}&\sqrt{N}\hat{a}(k)=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{N-1}\cos(-2\pi lk/N)a(k)\\
&=\tilde{N}+\frac{\alpha}{\tilde{N}^{2}}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{N}\rho(l/\tilde{N})+\epsilon-\cos(-2\pi k/N)\left(\tilde{N}+\frac{\alpha}{\tilde{N}^{2}}\rho(1/\tilde{N})\right)+\sum\limits_{l=2}^{N-2}\cos(-2\pi lk/N)a(k)\\
&=\!\!\tilde{N}\!+\!\frac{\alpha}{\tilde{N}^{2}}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{N}\rho(l/\tilde{N})\!+\!\epsilon\!-\!\cos(-2\pi k/N)\left(\!\!\tilde{N}\!\!+\!\frac{\alpha}{\!\tilde{N}^{2}}\rho(1/\tilde{N})\right)\!-\!\frac{\alpha}{2\tilde{N}^{2}}\sum\limits_{l=2}^{N-2}\cos(-2\pi lk/N)\rho(l/\tilde{N})\\
&=\tilde{N}(1-\cos(-2\pi k/N))+\frac{\alpha}{\tilde{N}^{2}}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{N}\rho(l/\tilde{N})+\epsilon-\frac{\alpha}{2\tilde{N}^{2}}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{N-1}\cos(-2\pi lk/N)\rho(l/\tilde{N}).\end{split}

Here, the second-to-last equality follows from the fact that the expression

l\mapsto\cos(-2\pi lk/N)\rho(l/\tilde{N})

is
-\cos(-2\pi k/N)\left(\tilde{N}+\frac{\alpha}{\tilde{N}^{2}}\rho(1/\tilde{N})\right)/2
for
l\in\{1,N-1\}
. Substituting into (
A.3
) yields

\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{k\in V}\frac{\,{\rm e}^{i2\pi(n-m)k/N}\,}{\epsilon+\tilde{N}(1-\cos(-2\pi k/N))+\frac{\alpha}{\tilde{N}^{2}}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{N}\rho(l/\tilde{N})-\frac{\alpha}{2\tilde{N}^{2}}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{N-1}\cos(-2\pi lk/N)\rho(l/\tilde{N})}.

The second claim follows directly by adding the corresponding matrix elements, letting
\epsilon\to 0
and using symmetry.
∎

Proof of Proposition
A.1
.

An easy calculation shows that

1-\cos(-2\pi k/n)=4\pi^{2}\frac{k^{2}}{N^{2}}+o(N^{-3}).

Plugging this into the formula obtained in Proposition
A.1
and letting
\tilde{N}\to\infty
, we find that (using in the formula
m=\tilde{N}m
and the same for
n
to remove the discretization)

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}^{*}_{\alpha,T}}[(x_{m}-x_{n})^{2}]=\frac{2}{T}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1-\cos(2\pi k(m-n)/T)}{4\pi^{2}(k/T)^{2}+\alpha\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}x\rho(x)-\alpha\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}l\,{\rm e}^{-2\pi ilk/T}\,\rho(l)}.

Note that all variables we sum over are normalized by
T
. Taking
T\to\infty
yields the claim.
∎
