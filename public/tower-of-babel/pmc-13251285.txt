Estimation of human brain fluid clearance using intrathecal imaging contrast agents
Authors: Per Kristian Eide, Aslan Lashkarivand, Susanne Sørensen Hernes, Geir Ringstad, Markus Hovd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtra-vascular brain fluid dynamics, including exchange through glymphatic and meningeal lymphatic pathways, has been proposed essential for waste clearance and solute transport in the central nervous system. Although animal studies provide evidence for these pathways’ role in clearing neurotoxic proteins and distributing solutes delivered to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), translation to humans is limited. Quantitative, in vivo measures of inter-individual variability in brain fluid clearance in clinical populations are largely lacking. This study investigated the feasibility of estimating brain fluid clearance indices using intrathecal imaging contrast agents.MethodsIn a prospective and observational study, iodixanol (Visipaque, GE Healthcare, USA, 270 mg I/mL, 3 mL) was administered intrathecally as a CSF tracer and its clearance from CSF to blood was quantified using population pharmacokinetic modeling. Intracranial tracer enrichment in CSF was assessed by its radiodensity (Hounsfield units) within predefined regions of interest (cisterna magna, Sylvian fissure and vertex) on cranial computed tomography (CT) performed 2-, 4- and 24-hours post-injection. These results were compared with brain fluid clearance indices derived from a separate population pharmacokinetic model using intrathecal gadobutrol (Gadovist, Bayer AG, GE, 1.0 mmol/mL, 0.50 mL), with its enrichment in CSF defined as gadobutrol-induced T1 signal increase from baseline within corresponding subarachnoid spaces on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).ResultsThe study included 52 subjects receiving intrathecal iodixanol and 45 individuals receiving intrathecal gadobutrol. Marked inter-individual variability was observed in brain fluid clearance to blood for both contrast agents, as well as their distribution in CSF as assessed by CT and MRI. Brain fluid clearance from iodixanol and gadobutrol were significantly correlated. Notably, clearance indices showed stronger correlations with intracranial distribution in CSF for gadobutrol-derived MRI metrics than for iodixanol-derived CT metrics.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate the feasibility of estimating brain fluid clearance in humans using intrathecal imaging contrast agents as CSF tracers. Brain fluid clearance capacity exhibits pronounced inter-individual variability and correlates with the degree of contrast enrichment in intracranial subarachnoid spaces, particularly when assessed by MRI. The results underscore the importance of individualized assessment of brain fluid clearance, with implications for studies on pathogenetic mechanisms of neurodegeneration and optimization of intrathecal drug delivery.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12987-026-00826-1.Keywords: Cerebrospinal fluid tracer; Computed tomography imaging; Magnetic resonance imaging; Intrathecal contrast agents; Brain fluid clearance, humans

Background

Over the past decade, converging lines of evidence have established brain fluid dynamics as a fundamental determinant of brain homeostasis and neurological disease. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), traditionally viewed as largely confined to the cerebral ventricles and subarachnoid space, is now recognized to exchange extensively with interstitial fluid through perivascular pathways within the brain. These pathways, collectively termed the glymphatic system following their seminal description in 2012, enable convective clearance of solutes from the brain parenchyma [1]. Complementing this paradigm shift, the discovery in 2015 of functional lymphatic vessels within the dura mater demonstrated a pathway via CSF for brain-immune cross talk and solute drainage to the systemic lymphatic circulation [2, 3]. Together, these findings have fundamentally reshaped our understanding of how metabolic waste products, and therapeutic agents circulate within and exit the central nervous system (CNS).

Experimental studies in animals provide evidence that perivascular CSF–interstitial fluid exchange contributes to the clearance of neurotoxic proteins, including amyloid-β, tau, and α-synuclein, which are key molecular drivers of major neurodegenerative diseases [4, 5]. In parallel, perivascular pathways appear to facilitate the intracerebral distribution of therapeutics delivered intrathecally or intraventricularly, offering potential advantages over systemic administration, where entry of water-soluble substances into the brain is highly limited by the blood–brain barrier (BBB) [6–11]. Despite these advances, translation to clinical practice has been slow. Current clinical assessments of “brain clearance” rely primarily on static biomarker concentrations in CSF or blood, or on metabolic imaging modalities. Direct, quantitative, in vivo measures of CSF or glymphatic clearance in humans remain largely unavailable.

Intrathecal drug delivery is increasingly employed for the treatment of a broad spectrum of CNS disorders, including inflammatory, degenerative, neoplastic, and genetic diseases. Therapeutic modalities administered via this route include antisense oligonucleotides, intrathecal chemotherapy, and viral vector–mediated gene therapies [12–16]. Examples include antisense oligonucleotides such as nusinersen (Spinraza) for spinal muscular atrophy [13, 16–18], intrathecal methotrexate in oncological indications [19, 20], and adeno-associated viral vector–based gene delivery for conditions such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, neurodegenerative disorders, and spinocerebellar ataxias [12, 21–24]. However, critical aspects of intrathecal pharmacokinetics remain poorly understood. It is unknown how inter-individual variability in CSF clearance of intrathecally administered drugs influences systemic uptake, intracranial distribution in CSF spaces, and parenchymal enrichment.

Recent in vivo studies have demonstrated pronounced person-to-person variability in the clearance of intrathecally administered agents to blood, as well as in their capacity to distribute through CSF-mediated extravascular pathways to the brain and spinal cord [25]. Consistent with this, the degree to which intrathecal agents enrich the brain [26] and spinal cord [27] varies substantially across individuals. These observations highlight a critical knowledge gap: the amount of drug retained within the CSF, and thus available for propagation throughout the CNS, appears to differ markedly between patients. A mechanistic and quantitative understanding of this variability is essential for optimizing intrathecal drug delivery and minimizing the risk of under- or overdosing.

To address these gaps, we have previously employed the MRI contrast agent gadobutrol as a CSF tracer to characterize glymphatic transport and brain fluid clearance in humans [25, 28]. Given that a CSF tracer exchanges extensively with the brain extravascular compartment, and that tracer clearance from brain is highly associated with tracer clearance from CSF [26], assessment of clearance to blood of an intrathecally injected tracer can therefore also be regarded as an assessment of extravascular brain clearance, from here on referred to as just brain fluid clearance. Building on previous work [25, 29], the present study investigates brain fluid clearance of the iodinated CT contrast agent iodixanol following intrathecal administration. Both contrast agents are well suited as markers of extra-vascular brain fluid clearance as their leakage from CSF into blood circulation over the intact BBB can be regarded as negligible [30]. By combining dense venous blood sampling with population pharmacokinetic modeling, we derived quantitative indices of brain fluid clearance and assessed intracranial subarachnoid tracer distribution using CT imaging. Furthermore, we compared clearance profiles obtained with iodixanol and gadobutrol and examined how clearance rates relate to their distribution in intracranial CSF spaces, reflected by CT radiodensity and MRI T1 signal changes (Fig. 1).

Together, these investigations provide new evidence of substantial inter-individual variability in brain fluid clearance and enable direct comparison between intrathecal imaging tracers. Such insights are critical for advancing diagnostic approaches to brain fluid dynamics and for informing individualized strategies for intrathecal drug delivery in clinical practice.

Methods

Patient cohorts

This prospective, observational study included patients referred to the Department of Neurosurgery, Oslo University Hospital–Rikshospitalet, for evaluation of suspected CSF disturbance, independent of sex. The study cohorts included patients with specific diagnoses and subjects who after thorough examination turned out to have no identified neurological or CSF disorder, denoted as reference (REF) subjects. Two patient cohorts were enrolled:

Intrathecal iodixanol cohort. Patients received intrathecal iodixanol (Visipaque; GE Healthcare, USA; 270 mg I/mL), typically at a volume of 3 mL. Deviations included one patient receiving 2 mL, three patients receiving 5 mL, and one patient receiving 10 mL. Cranial CT was performed approximately 2, 4, and 24 hours after injection. Patients were enrolled between September 2021 and June 2025.

Intrathecal gadobutrol cohort. Patients received intrathecal gadobutrol (Gadovist; Bayer AG, Germany; 1.0 mmol/mL, 0.5 mL). To verify correct intrathecal needle placement, iodixanol (Visipaque; 270 mg I/mL) was administered via the same spinal needle immediately before gadobutrol, in an estimated volume of 2–5 mL. MRI was performed before and after intrathecal injection. Patients were enrolled between January 2017 and May 2018.

For both cohorts, exclusion criteria included age < 18 or >80 years, pregnancy or breastfeeding, prior adverse reactions to contrast agents or history of severe allergy and known renal dysfunction.

Intrathecal injection procedure

Spinal puncture was performed at the lumbar level and correct needle position was verified by CSF backflow (iodixanol-cohort) or by injection of iodixanol during X-ray fluoroscopy (gadobutrol-cohort). The patients remained supine for at least 2 hours before being allowed to move freely. The intrathecal injection was performed in the morning and in some instances during working day before afternoon.

The timing of blood samples were based on our previous experience [25].

Quantification of iodixanol and gadobutrol in blood

Venous blood samples were collected for each patient in conjunction with the CT and MRI examinations, centrifuged, and stored in an ultra-freezer (−80 °C) until analysis.

Iodixanol analysis was performed at Vitas – Analytical Services (Oslo, Norway) using the Vitas AM-546 LC–ICP-MS method. Whole blood and plasma samples were prepared by mixing 400 µL blood with 90 µL TCA in 96-well plates, sealing the plates, and shaking for 1 h at 750 rpm, followed by centrifugation (10 min, 4000 rpm). A 100 µL aliquot of supernatant was diluted with 300 µL water, resealed, shaken for 10 min at 750 rpm, and centrifuged for 5 min at 4000 rpm before injection. Dried blood spot (DBS) samples were prepared by extracting a single 3 mm punch in 200 µL water for 15 min at 600 rpm, adding 20 µL TCA, shaking for 5 min at 600 rpm, and centrifuging for 10 min at 4000 rpm; 200 µL of the extract was transferred for analysis. Chromatographic separation was achieved on an Agilent 1100 LC system equipped with a Zorbax SB-C18 column (4.6 × 50 mm, 1.8 µm), using mobile phase A (10% MeOH in water) and B (100% MeOH) with a gradient of 0 min: 0% B; 2 min: 20% B; 5 min: 20% B; 6 min: 0% B, at 0.4 mL/min. The LC was coupled to an Agilent 7900 ICP-MS with standard nickel cones, using a 1:4 post-column split (Acumate ICP-04–20). Calibration was performed using Iodixanol CRS (EDQM), with potassium iodide and thyroxine (T4) as method performance references, and the lower limit of quantification was 19.5 ng/mL.

Gadolinium in blood was analyzed as previously described [25, 29], with a lower limit of quantification of 1 ng/mL in plasma, and 4 ng/mL in blood. The samples were prepared, controlled for quality, and the concentrations of iodixanol and gadolinium in the samples were quantified. Iodixanol whole blood concentrations were interpolated to plasma concentrations using linear regression.

Population pharmacokinetic modeling

A population pharmacokinetic model was developed for iodixanol and compared with a previously established model for gadobutrol using similar methodology. The purpose of the model was to describe individual pharmacokinetics of intrathecally administered tracer. Modelling was performed using the Pmetrics (version 3.0.4) package for R (version 4.5.1), which employs a non-parametric adaptive grid algorithm for parameter estimation [31].

Model development and selection were primarily based on comparison of the mean individual relative root mean squared predictive error (RMSE, %), calculated as the square root of the mean of squared differences between the predicted and observed concentration, normalized by the mean of the observations for everyone. Additionally, the linear regression slope, R2-values of the observed versus predicted plots, Akaike’s information criteria (AIC) and the Bayesian information criteria (BIC) also guided model development to some extent. Covariates were not considered during model development. Individual posterior predictions were used to compute the following parameters of interest; the maximum concentration in plasma (Cmax) and the time to the maximum concentration (Tmax), and the area under the plasma-concentration time curve (AUC). The individual Bayesian posterior value of the absorption coefficient (Ka) was used to calculate the half-life of absorption (T1/2, abs), i.e. the time for half the amount of tracer to be cleared from the CSF to plasma, and the model estimated lag time of absorption (Tlag) was also compared across diagnostic groups. We have previously established these as surrogate markers of CSF clearance [25, 29]. Individual predictions of tracer in blood were calculated using the mean Bayesian posterior estimates of the concentration-time profiles and were dose-normalized when compared or aggregated with subjects receiving different doses.

CT acquisitions and image analysis

Non-contrast head CT was performed on two multidetector CT systems (Aquilion ONE, Canon Medical Systems, Otawara, Japan; and SOMATOM Force, Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany). Volumetric acquisitions were obtained without intravenous contrast. Images were reconstructed and reformatted in axial, coronal, and sagittal planes with a slice thickness of 3 mm. Scanner-specific acquisition and reconstruction parameters (e.g., tube potential/current, pitch, and rotation time) followed local standard clinical protocols and were aligned as closely as feasible between systems. CT images were analyzed using the hospital’s picture and archiving system (PACS), Sectra, version 7 (Sectra, Sweden). Regions of interests (ROIs) were manually placed within the subarachnoid space at the cisterna magna, Sylvian fissure, and at vertex. Regions of interest (ROIs) were adapted to avoid partial volume averaging effects from adjacent parenchyma. The Hounsfield units within the respective ROIs were determined at the respective time points.

MRI acquisitions and image analysis

The change in T1 signal following intrathecal gadobutrol was assessed with the same 3 Tesla Philips Ingenia MRI scanner (Philips Medical Systems) with equal imaging protocol settings at all time points: 3D T1-weighted image (T1 mprage) in sagittal plane. Repetition time: “shortest” (typically 5.1 ms). Echo time: “shortest” (typically 2.3 ms). Flip angle: 8 degrees. Field of view: 256 × 256 mm. Matrix: 256 × 256 pixels (reconstructed to 512 × 512 pixels). Hundred and eighty-four overlapping 1 mm isotropic voxels were sampled and automatically reconstructed to 368 slices with 0.5-mm thickness; Acquisition time: 6 minutes and 29 seconds. Patients were scanned before contrast, and after 1, 3, 4, 6, 24 and 48 hours. These timepoints were chosen based on our previous work [26].

The image data were obtained from the hospital’s picture and archiving system (PACS), Sectra, version 7 (Sectra, Sweden), and without any preprocessing of the images, regions of interests (ROIs) were placed manually within the SAS at the cisterna magna, Sylvian fissure, at vertex, and within the retrobulbar fat to correct for image grayscale changes after the contrast administration. ROIs within CSF spaces were adapted to avoid partial volume averaging effects from adjacent parenchyma. Percentage change in normalized T1 signal intensity was determined for each image. We have previously used the retrobulbar fat as reference since this tissue is not expected to enhance after intrathecal contrast injection and therefore may serve as a consistent reference tissue throughout all time points.

Analyses of all CT and MRI acquisitions were performed by an experienced neuroradiologist (GR), with 19 years of experience.

Statistics

Statistical significance was accepted at the pre-determined 0.05 level (2-tailed). Associations between continuous variables were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Comparisons of values between diagnostic groups were performed using the Wilcoxon Rank sum test.

Population pharmacokinetic modeling of brain fluid clearance using intrathecal iodixanol

A population pharmacokinetic model for intrathecal iodixanol was developed using data from the 52 subjects who received iodixanol. The final model consisted of central and peripheral compartments, with first-order absorption (Ka) from the CSF into the central compartment and first-order elimination from the central compartment only (Supplementary Figure 1). Overall model performance was satisfactory. The mean individual prediction error (bias) was −0.75 mg/L, the absolute prediction error (imprecision) was 1.50 mg/L, and the mean individual root-mean-squared error was 30%. Observed-versus-predicted concentrations demonstrated good agreement, with the regression line closely approximating the line of identity and an R2 value of 0.89 (Fig. 2A). Prediction errors were symmetrically distributed around zero over time (Fig. 2B), although a tendency toward underprediction was observed at early time points, particularly at lower concentrations (Fig. 2C). This pattern did not materially affect individual-level predictions, as illustrated by representative subject profiles (Fig. 2D).

Impact of disease on brain fluid clearance

Compared with the reference cohort, patients with spontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH) exhibited a significantly higher dose-normalized maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), with a median difference of 8.5 units (p = 0.039). No other pharmacokinetic parameters differed significantly between the reference group and the remaining diagnostic categories (Supplementary Figure 3).

Concordance of brain fluid clearance indices derived from intrathecal iodixanol and gadobutrol

The cohort of 45 subjects who received intrathecal gadobutrol was included for cross-tracer comparisons, as these individuals also received intrathecal iodixanol to confirm correct spinal needle placement. Iodixanol was administered shortly before gadobutrol via the same spinal needle, with an estimated dose range of 2–5 mL. Plasma iodixanol concentrations in this cohort were analyzed using the pharmacokinetic model derived from the iodixanol-only cohort (n = 52). Correlations between brain fluid clearances indices (Tmax, Cmax, Tlag and T1/2, abs) estimated from iodixanol and gadobutrol within the same individuals are shown in Fig. 5. Notably, good correlations were observed for Tmax and T1/2, abs, and for Cmax. Interpretation of these correlations is, however, limited by uncertainty regarding the exact timing and dose of iodixanol administration in this cohort, which may have attenuated the observed associations.

Association between brain fluid clearance indices and distribution of intrathecal tracer in intracranial CSF spaces

Distribution in intracranial CSF spaces following intrathecal iodixanol are shown in Fig. 6A–C. Radiodensity measured in Hounsfield units (HU) within the cisterna magna, Sylvian fissure, and subarachnoid space at the vertex demonstrated pronounced inter-individual variability at 2-, 4-, and 24-hours post-injection. Tracer propagation followed a consistent spatial pattern, with the most rapid enrichment observed in the cisterna magna, followed by the Sylvian fissure, and lastly the vertex. Peak radiodensity occurred at 4 hours in the Sylvian fissure, whereas no peak was reached at the vertex within 24 hours. There was a significant correlation between CSF tracer enrichment in cisterna magna and Sylvian fissure at all three time points but at vertex only after 24 hours (Supplementary Table 1).

Imaging markers of CSF flow defined by radio density within intracranial subarachnoid spaces at CT (A-C), and by signal change within intracranial subarachnoid spaces at MRI (D-F). Utilizing CT, radio density was measured as Hounsfield units (HUs) within the regions of interest, including (A) cisterna magna, (B) Sylvian fissure and (C) subarachnoid space at vertex. For cisterna magna (A), linear mixed model analysis showed that HUs were significantly lower at 24 hours, compared to 2 hours (p < 0.001) and 4 hours (p < 0.001). Within the Sylvian fissure (B) HUs had increased significantly from 2 to 4 hours (p = 0.005) and dropped from 4 to 24 hours (p = 0.001). Within CSF of the subarachnoid space at vertex (C), the HUs were significantly higher at 24 hours, compared to at 2 hours (p < 0.001) and 4 hours (p < 0.001). A comparable temporal and spatial pattern were observed for intrathecal gadobutrol, assessed as percentage change in normalized T1 signal on MRI (Fig. 6D–F). Marked inter-individual variability was evident across all regions and time points. Tracer enrichment peaked at approximately 3 hours in the cisterna magna, 4 hours in the Sylvian fissure, and 24 hours at the vertex, consistent with the CT-based findings. While tracer enrichment correlated significantly within cisterna magna and Sylvian fissure at all three time points, though more pronounced at 24 and 48 hours, correlation between tracer enrichment in cisterna magna and at vertex became significant only after 48 hours (Supplementary Table 2). These findings compare with earlier observations how tracer enrichment propagates from cisterna magna, via Sylvian fissure and peaks several hours later at the vertex [32].

For iodixanol, higher Cmax values, reflecting greater transfer of tracer from CSF to blood, were associated with lower radiodensity in CSF at 4 and 24 hours, indicating reduced tracer retention within the subarachnoid space. Overall, clearance indices derived from gadobutrol showed stronger and more consistent correlations with tracer levels in CSF than those derived from iodixanol (Table 4). These findings suggest that MRI-based assessment using gadobutrol may be more sensitive for relating brain fluid clearance capacity to intracranial CSF tracer distribution.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that brain fluid clearance in humans can be quantified using intrathecal imaging contrast agents combined with population pharmacokinetic modeling. Both iodixanol and gadobutrol proved feasible as CSF tracers, yielding clearance indices that were significantly correlated. Notably, clearance indices derived from intrathecal gadobutrol showed stronger and more consistent associations with imaging of tracer levels in intracranial CSF spaces than those derived from iodixanol. Across tracers and diagnostic categories, both brain fluid clearance capacity and CSF tracer enrichment in subarachnoid spaces exhibited pronounced inter-individual variability, underscoring the heterogeneity of human brain fluid dynamics.

Iodixanol versus gadobutrol as CSF tracers

Iodixanol is an iodinated CT contrast agent approved for intrathecal use. It is neutral and hydrophilic (logP 0.7) with a molecular weight of 1,550 Da. Gadobutrol is a neutral, highly hydrophilic MRI contrast agent (logP −9.8) with a molecular weight of 604 Da and is administered off-label intrathecally under special permission. Despite differences in molecular size and imaging modality, both agents share key properties relevant to CSF tracer studies, including hydrophilicity and inability to substantially cross the blood–brain barrier.

The observed correlations between brain fluid clearance indices estimated using iodixanol and gadobutrol indicate that both tracers capture related aspects of brain fluid clearance to blood clearance. However, clearance indices derived from gadobutrol showed closer correspondence with imaging evidence of intracranial CSF tracer distribution. This likely reflects the higher sensitivity of MRI to low CSF tracer concentrations and the superior ability of gadobutrol-enhanced T1-weighted imaging to detect subtle differences in CSF enrichment across time and anatomical regions.

Inter-individual variability in brain fluid clearance

One of the most striking findings of this study is the marked inter-individual variability in brain fluid clearance capacity. This observation is consistent with our previous work [25], and was evident across tracers, imaging modalities, and diagnostic categories. No clear associations were observed between clearance indices and demographic variables such as age, sex, height, or weight, or lab values (GFR, creatinine). An exception was a significant negative correlation between weight and dose-normalized maximum plasma concentrations of iodixanol. Moreover, patients with spontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH) exhibited higher dose-normalized maximum plasma concentrations of iodixanol compared with reference subjects, consistent with pathological CSF leakage and accelerated clearance. This finding aligns with previous reports in SIH [33]. Nevertheless, the dominant feature across all diagnostic categories was the breadth of variability within each group. The clinical implications of this variability remain to be fully elucidated but may be highly relevant for individualized assessment of brain fluid dynamics and therapeutic response to drugs delivered directly to CSF.

Furthermore, whether brain fluid clearance capacity represents a stable individual trait or varies over time within individuals remains unknown and will require longitudinal investigation.

Implications for intrathecal drug delivery

In clinical practice, renal clearance, quantified by plasma-based glomerular filtration rate (GFR), is routinely assessed to guide dosing of systemically administered drugs, particularly those with narrow therapeutic windows [34]. By contrast, intrathecal drug delivery is not accompanied by routine assessment of CSF or brain fluid clearance capacity, largely because such variability has not previously been quantifiable in vivo. The present findings challenge this paradigm.

For intrathecally administered agents that are neurotoxic in high doses, low clearance may lead to prolonged CSF exposure and increased risk of neurotoxicity, whereas high clearance may limit therapeutic efficacy by reducing CNS exposure. These considerations are particularly relevant for intrathecal chemotherapy and other agents with narrow safety margins. Quantitative assessment of brain fluid clearance could therefore provide a rational basis for individualized dosing strategies in intrathecal therapies.

From a translational perspective, broader clinical implementation of clearance assessment will require protocol simplification, standardization, and validation across centers with varying imaging and sampling capabilities.

Brain fluid clearance, brain waste removal and neurodegeneration

An expanding body of evidence supports a critical role for CSF-mediated clearance in the removal of metabolic waste from the brain [4]. We have previously reported associations between plasma biomarkers of neurodegeneration and indices of brain fluid clearance, with disease-specific patterns [35]. These observations raise the possibility that impaired brain fluid clearance may contribute to the accumulation of neurotoxic proteins and increase vulnerability to cognitive decline and dementia.

An important open question is whether brain fluid clearance capacity itself could serve as a biomarker, complementary to established CSF and blood biomarkers, reflecting the efficiency of waste removal rather than static metabolite concentrations. Longitudinal studies will be required to determine whether impaired clearance precedes, accompanies, or follows neurodegenerative processes.

At present, it remains unclear whether impaired brain fluid clearance represents a causal contributor to neurodegeneration, a downstream consequence of disease processes, or a parallel biomarker reflecting broader pathophysiological changes.

CSF efflux routes

For both iodixanol and gadobutrol, the time to maximum plasma concentration occurred within several hours, whereas peak tracer enrichment at the vertex was observed much later, at approximately 24 hours. This temporal dissociation indicates that CSF clearance to blood does not primarily depend on clearance at the cranial convexities. Instead, it is consistent with prior evidence showing that intrathecal tracers are predominantly cleared at the spinal level [28, 36], and via cranial nerve pathways [37]. This interpretation is further supported by the present observation of a patient with spinal stenosis who demonstrated rapid tracer clearance and minimal intracranial enrichment (Fig. 3H), indicating preferential spinal efflux. Therefore, the appearance of tracer in blood likely reflects the integrated contribution of multiple efflux pathways, including spinal absorption, meningeal lymphatic drainage, cranial nerve routes, and venous interfaces, rather than a single anatomical clearance route. Experimental studies in rodents have demonstrated CSF drainage to lymphatic vessels outside the spinal dura mater [38], while human in vivo studies have shown tracer passage to parasagittal dura, skull bone marrow, and extracranial lymph nodes [39]. Together, these findings underscore the multiplicity of CSF efflux routes and their relevance for brain fluid clearance.

Terminology: “brain fluid clearance”

In this study, we deliberately use the term brain fluid clearance rather than CSF clearance. This choice reflects the observation that intrathecally administered CSF tracers distribute throughout the brain via extravascular pathways [26], demonstrating the continuity of CSF with interstitial and meningeal fluids. Brain [40, 41], dura mater [39], and skull bone marrow [42] enrichment all depend on the amount of tracer retained within this interconnected fluid system.

The term “CSF clearance” risks implying restriction to the ventricular and subarachnoid compartments, whereas “brain fluid clearance” may more accurately capture the integrated nature of fluid exchange and its relevance for brain-wide solute transport and waste removal.

While the term “brain fluid clearance” may not directly capture alternative or contributory mechanisms, such as direct spinal absorption, variability in meningeal permeability and venous uptake independent of lymphatic routes, these contributory mechanisms may indirectly affect clearance from brain given our consistent observations that the concentration of solutes in CSF is decisive for the brain clearance [40, 41]. We therefore believe “brain fluid clearance” (BFC) is the most appropriate terminology.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. In the cohort of 45 subjects receiving both iodixanol and gadobutrol, the iodixanol dose was estimated (approximately 2–5 mL), and the exact injection time was not recorded, limiting the precision of cross-tracer comparisons. Normalization of pharmacokinetic parameters may mitigate, but not eliminate, this source of variability. Additionally, the iodixanol pharmacokinetic model was derived from the 52 subjects with known dosing, which may limit its generalizability to cohorts with less precise administration data.

Furthermore, current limitations in the non-parametric approach to population pharmacokinetic modelling make estimates of the uncertainty in brain fluid clearance infeasible, and as such a single best estimate is provided for each patient.

Additionally, physiological factors known to influence CSF dynamics, including posture, intracranial pressure, cardiopulmonary activity, and injection-related variability, were not explicitly modeled and may contribute to the observed inter-individual differences.

Another limitation is that gadobutrol enrichment in intracranial subarachnoid spaces was assessed using relative changes in normalized T1 signal rather than absolute tracer concentrations. Future studies incorporating quantitative T1 mapping may enable absolute concentration measurements and more refined analyses.

Prospects

If brain fluid clearance capacity proves modifiable, it may represent a therapeutic target for enhancing CNS drug exposure or facilitating removal of neurotoxic waste products. This should be explored in future studies.

Conclusion

Brain fluid clearance in humans can be quantified using intrathecal imaging contrast agents, with both iodixanol and gadobutrol providing informative clearance indices. At present, gadobutrol appears more suitable for correlating clearance indices with imaging markers of intracranial CSF tracer distribution. The findings confirm pronounced inter-individual variability in brain fluid clearance across demographic variables and disease categories. Elucidating the mechanistic basis and clinical implications of this variability represents an important avenue for future research.
