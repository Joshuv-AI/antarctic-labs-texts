Adaptation and Content Validation of the Adherence to Treatment of Neurological Physiotherapy Questionnaire (AdT-NeuroPhysio) in Spanish People with Stroke
Authors: Adrián Arranz-Escudero, Rosa María Ortiz-Gutiérrez, Ester Carpio-Calatayud, María José Díaz-Arribas, Roberto Cano-de-la-Cuerda, Patricia Martín-Casas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdherence to rehabilitation programmes is often poor after stroke, which limits functional recovery. Although adherence to rehabilitation programmes is frequently inadequate following a stroke and limits functional recovery, there is a need for more measures based on patient-reported information to assess this adherence.PurposeThis study aimed to adapt and validate the content of the Adherence to Treatment of Neurological Physiotherapy (AdT-NeuroPhysio) questionnaire for Spanish persons with subacute stroke.Patients and methodsAfter initial item adaptation by authors, a two-round Delphi study with 165 national stroke rehabilitation experts assessed relevance, comprehensibility, and content validity. Content Validity Indices (I-CVI, S-CVI), probability of chance agreement (Pc), and internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) were calculated. Open-ended questions were proposed to gather author’s extended opinion on items. Cognitive testing with 12 participants evaluated comprehensibility and feasibility.ResultsAfter the two rounds, the AdT-NeuroPhysio demonstrated excellent content validity (I-CVI range: 0.80–0.98; S-CVI=0.90), negligible chance agreement, and good internal consistency (α=0.819). The compliance subscale showed high consistency (α=0.804), while the beliefs subscale was acceptable (α=0.685). Two open-ended sections on environmental barriers/facilitators were added. In the pilot study, participants found the questionnaire highly comprehensible (S-CVI=1), feasible, and easy to complete (mean time: ~3 minutes).ConclusionThe AdT-NeuroPhysio is a content-validated, comprehensible and feasible assessment for evaluating therapeutic adherence from the patient’s perspective in Spanish subacute stroke populations. Further psychometric studies in larger samples are needed.Keywords: patient health questionnaire, reproducibility of results, stroke rehabilitation, treatment adherence and compliance, validation study

Introduction

Stroke is the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of disability globally,1,2 with significant physical, cognitive, and economic impacts on individuals and families.3 These impacts, alongside comorbidities and the need for specialized care, significantly alter personal and family life, reducing quality of life of people with stroke.3 Interdisciplinary neurorehabilitation programmes based in exercise aim to mitigate functional limitations.3–6 However, functional and motor recovery varies between individuals, even when following the same therapeutic protocols.7 Previous investigations have demonstrated that clinical factors can influence in the outcome of the neurorehabilitation programmes. These include socio-demographic variables such as age, sex, and race; clinical variables such as stroke subtype, initial injury severity, and comorbidities (diabetes and hypertension); and rehabilitation-related variables such as frequency, intensity, and type of therapies.8,9 In contrast, personal and relational factors such as disease awareness, health beliefs, healthcare system access and social support have been suggested as potential determinants in stroke recovery.8 Furthermore, research into constructs that consider these factors, such as adherence to rehabilitation or the patient-therapist relationship, remains limited.10,11

Adherence to rehabilitation programs at facilities or at home after discharge is often suboptimal.12–14 Strategies like logbooks15 or motivational sessions16 can improve compliance, but no studies have explored adherence from a holistic perspective considering personal goals, beliefs, expectations, and communication.10 Qualitative studies indicate that the therapeutic alliance—the quality of the relationship between therapist and patient—positively influences the experience of persons with stroke11,17 and clinical outcomes at discharge.18

The impact of these constructs is evident in populations where suggestibility is relevant, such as geriatric population with fear of falling19 and persons experiencing chronic pain.20 Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), like the Scale of Adherence to Treatment of Physiotherapy (AdT-Physio) by del Corral et al,21 evaluate adherence to therapy and expectations.

Other self-administered tools validated in stroke, like the Adherence to Rehabilitation Regimen Scale (ARRS)22 or Out-of-Hospital Adherence Questionnaire for Stroke Patients (OHAQ-SP),23 include constructs of exercise participation, compliance, daily living activities, and psychological follow-up. In contrast, tools like the Questionnaire of Exercise Adherence (EAQ)24 or the Stroke-Specific Measure of Adherence to Home-based Exercises (SS-MAHE)25 are based solely on session compliance ratios, while the Occupational Therapy Engagement Scale (OTES),26 the Hopkins Rehabilitation Engagement Rating Scale (HRERS),27 and the Pittsburgh Rehabilitation Participation Scale (PRPS)28 rely on therapists’ subjective evaluations. An exploratory review of the literature did not identify any validated questionnaires in Spain for assessing therapeutic adherence in neurorehabilitation from the patient’s perspective.

Using questionnaires that incorporate the patient’s perspective is crucial for encouraging participation, performance, and adherence to recommendations in areas like fall risk or fatigue.29,30 Furthermore, analysing the interrelationship between patients, professionals, and families, in line with the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health,31 is key for developing more effective and efficient stroke rehabilitation programmes, particularly during the acute and sub-acute phases when interventions can maximize neuroplasticity.32

Given the limited research on these variables and available PROMs,10,12 this study aimed to adapt the AdT-Physio questionnaire for subacute stroke population to obtain a measure which considers the perspective of people with stroke and context during rehabilitation.

Methods

Study Design

A cross-cultural adaptation study was conducted following the COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement INstruments (COSMIN) guidelines33 and the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research group (ISPOR-TCA).34 Authorization to use and adapt the original AdT-Physio was obtained from its main author. The protocol for this observational study has been reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Hospital Universitario Clínico San Carlos (Madrid, Spain), under reference number 23/438, in July 2023. The study was conducted in accordance with the 2024 version of the Declaration of Helsinki and the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) 2018/1725. All participants read and signed an informed consent document before participating in the study.

Scale of Adherence to Treatment of Physiotherapy (AdT-Physio)

The AdT-Physio is a self-administered questionnaire that assesses adherence to physical therapy. The items are scored using a 1- or 4-point Likert scale. The instrument comprises two subscales: (1) compliance (items 1, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 13, 14); and (2) beliefs about treatment/therapist (items 2, 3, 7, 8, 9, 11). The total score sums item scores (range 14–56), with higher values indicating superior compromise with the adherence. As in the original article the authors did not study any thresholds or established cut-off points to classify low, moderate, or high adherence in which the results can be classified, the score should be interpreted as a continuous measure where higher values indicate greater adherence to treatment.21 The scale has high internal consistency and convergence with similar constructs in persons with cystic fibrosis.21

Content Adaptation: Delphi Study with Spanish Experts

Two physiotherapists (>5 years of experience in stroke rehabilitation) independently conducted a preliminary review and adaptation of item terminology to create an initial proposal of the Adherence to Neurological Treatment of Physiotherapy questionnaire (AdT-NeuroPhysio). Since the original AdT-Physio questionnaire was developed in Spain and focused on the patient-therapy relationship without referencing the pathology, minimal adaptations were required to obtain the preliminary AdT-NeuroPhysio. These included broadening item content to address both exercises and recommendations; personalizing terminology (“my physiotherapist”) and shifting wording from limitations to patient goals. Subsequently, a panel of Spanish stroke rehabilitation experts was recruited via digital professional networks and social media. Eligible experts were neurologists, physicians in neurological rehabilitation, physiotherapists, and occupational therapists with ≥3 years of experience and fluent in Spanish. Individuals of diverse age, employment sectors and geographical locations across Spain were included in the panel to ensure a comprehensive representation of the country’s therapist demographic.35 After reading the study information and signing consent, participants were formally included. A sample size of 140 experts was deemed sufficient based on the recommendation of a minimum of 60–80 participants or 10 participants per item in order to obtain a replicability of more than 80%.36

The Delphi study comprised two rounds. In each, the panel rated relevance, understandability, and comprehensiveness of each item on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = completely disagree, 4 = completely agree). Seven open-ended questions were included as a secondary qualitative measure37 (Figure 2), allowing experts to explain scores and assess comprehensibility, usefulness, feasibility, and applicability. The principal investigator (AAE) collected expert suggestions and made changes to the items according to the following principles of the panel: simplifying wording and concepts, increasing emphasis on exercises and recommendations, and broadening patients’ personal context (Table 2). During both rounds of the Delphi study, these modifications were overseen by another researcher (PMC), and any discrepancies were resolved by a third researcher (RMOG).

The Content Validity Index (CVI) was calculated to determine expert consensus. After the first round, a revised AdT-NeuroPhysio version was sent back. It incorporated modifications with ≥20% agreement on the necessity for change37 to enable a better consensus and ensure that suggestions from evaluators are not overlooked. This second-round version included the original Likert questionnaire and open-ended questions without sufficient consensus. Experts had 30–45 days per round, with reminders if needed.38 Only completed questionnaires were analysed.

Pilot Study: Cognitive Testing in People with Stroke

A pilot study was conducted using convenience sampling of individuals with subacute stroke undergoing hospital neurorehabilitation to explore the questionnaire’s applicability in clinical practice. Exclusion criteria included additional neurological or psychiatric conditions or inability to read/understand the questionnaire. Sociodemographic and clinical variables were collected.

The cognitive testing was conducted using paper-based questionnaires completed with a pen. The test was administered individually by the principal investigator to each patient in a quiet room with no other people or distractions. In case of writing difficulties or if the patient requested assistance, the investigator helped to complete the questionnaire providing care without influencing the patient’s responses.

The participants completed the pre-final AdT-NeuroPhysio with the principal investigator and rated item understandability (1=difficult, 4=completely easy). Items with average score <3.5 or ≥25% “difficult” ratings were revised. Three short questions assessed perceived difficulty in reading, difficulty in comprehension and fatigue. The completion time, measured with a stopwatch and expressed in seconds, and item omissions, registered as the items that were not answered, were recorded. After a 7–10-day washout period to minimise recall bias,39 participants repeated the evaluation. A sample of 12 participants was deemed sufficient for pilot testing.34,40

Statistical Analysis

Analyses used SPSS (v27.0). Descriptive statistics characterized sociodemographic/clinical features. The results were analysed with frequencies for qualitative variables and mean ± standard deviation for quantitative variables. The responses of experts and patients were analysed using content validation formulas to measure agreement.

Item-level CVI (I-CVI) was defined as the proportion of experts assigning ratings of 3 or 4 to each item for relevance, understandability and comprehensiveness. Scale-level CVI (S-CVI/Ave) was the average of I-CVI values.41 Chance agreement (Pc) was calculated: Pc = 0.5n.42 Cronbach’s alpha (α) and Kappa adjusted to Pc (k*), defined as: k* = (I-CVI – Pc) / (1 – Pc),42 estimated internal consistency and feasibility. Thresholds for acceptable agreement were I-CVI > 0.78 and S-CVI > 0.80;41,42 α > 0.7 (acceptable) and α > 0.9 (excellent).43 For open-ended questions, ≥80% consensus was optimal; for questionnaire modification, while ≥20% agreement supported item modification.37

Results

Delphi Study

Experts Panel Composition

Out of 248 eligible stroke rehabilitation experts who were contacted, 165 completed both Delphi rounds. The panel included 119 physiotherapists (72.12%), 39 occupational therapists (23.64%) and 7 physicians (4.24%). The sample comprised 101 females (67.3%), with a mean age of 32.21 (SD: 7.36) years and mean experience of 8.33 (SD 6.36) years. Participants worked at neurorehabilitation centres (n=126), hospitals (n=34), and universities (n=5), mainly in the following Spanish regions: Madrid, Galicia, Valencia, and Andalucía. All regions of the country were represented and all professions, in different types and workplaces, that were initially proposed for participation were well represented. All questionnaires were returned completed.

First Round

With 165 subjects, Pc was 2.14 · 10–50, confirming negligible chance agreement and S-CVI was 0.82, with acceptable internal consistency Cronbach’s α was 0.747 (95% CI: 0.687–0.800). Consequently, Kappa adjusted to chance agreement (k*) was found to be virtually equivalent to the I-CVI.

All items except 2, 4, 10, and 14 showed I-CVI > 0.78, therefore changes were required to improve consensus on the relevance of the items. Qualitative responses showed high agreement that the questionnaire was simple to understand (97.56%), necessary (90.30%), useful for its purpose (98.20%), short and easy to answer (98.20%), and feasible in clinical practice (80%). Some experts (17.58%) suggested that persons with cognitive impairments might complete it with assistance of family members or therapists.

Regarding item comprehension, 21.82% recommended simplifying wording, and 21.21% suggested adding more personal or neurorehabilitation-related content. Items 2 (42.64%), 10 (40.31%), 4 (32.56%), and 14 (27.91%) had the greatest consensus for modification, aligning with their lower than 0.78 I-CVI values. Furthermore, 21.61% proposed the addition of sections for persons with stroke to document or describe barriers or facilitators of therapeutic adherence.

In response to missing items suggestion, two open-response sections were incorporated: “Difficulties in my environment that limit me from carrying out the exercises and recommendations” and “Characteristics of my environment that help me to carry out the exercises and recommendations”.

After a one-month washout to minimise observational and learning bias,36 experts received the modified items along with a summary table of all first-round changes. Items were rated again using the same 4-point Likert scale, and only open questions 2 and 3 were re-administered, as these were the only ones requiring further evaluation (>20% modification agreement). In this occasion, 90.30% of respondents reached a consensus that the questionnaire is clear and understandable, and 92.12% agreed that no additional elements were required. The data set was complete, and all items were returned by all participants, with no missing data.

The two open-response spaces on limiting and facilitating contextual factors received favourable evaluations, with 43.03% considering them an improvement.

The S-CVI increased to 0.90 and internal consistency was Cronbach’s α = 0.819 (95% CI: 0.775–0.857). Subscale Cronbach’s α values were 0.804 (95% CI: 0.755–0.846) for compliance and 0.685 (95% CI: 0.604–0.754) for beliefs about treatment/therapist.

Pilot Study

Twelve participants with subacute stroke who were undergoing neurorehabilitation, including 7 men (58%), with a mean age of 67.23 (SD: 4.31) years, participated in the pilot study.

Understandability scores averaged 3.98 (SD: 0.13) in the first assessment and 3.99 (SD: 0.08) in the second, both exceeding the 3.5 threshold. All I-CVI and S-CVI values scored 1, indicating that all items were considered “quite easy” or “completely easy” to understand.

Regarding qualitative responses, all participants indicated that the questionnaire was legible, comprehensible, and easy to complete. None of the participants reported experiencing fatigue or tiredness during the evaluation. Completion time averaged 3 minutes and 10 seconds (SD: 40) in the first administration and 2 minutes and 37 seconds (SD: 51) in the second. The mean interval between evaluations was 7.58 days. The questionnaire was administered paper-based in all the evaluations and only one of the patients requested the physiotherapist to read two items aloud because of a mild vision impairment. All participants completed the questionnaire in full, no items remained unanswered or omitted.

Since no revisions were required, the final versions of AdT-NeuroPhysio were obtained in both English (Figure 3) and Spanish (Supplementary material).

Discussion

The main findings of this study demonstrate the successful adaptation and content validation of the AdT-NeuroPhysio for people with stroke in Spain. In the Delphi study, the S-CVI increased from 0.82 to 0.90 after two rounds, with acceptable overall internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.819) and all items achieved an I-CVI > 0.78 in the second round. In the pilot study in people with subacute stroke, the mean comprehensibility Likert score was almost perfect (3.98 and 3.99 out of 4), above the threshold of 3.5. No participant omitted any items or reported fatigue, and the mean completion time was less than 4 minutes in both assessments (3:10 and 2:37). All participants considered the questionnaire legible, understandable and easy to complete. These findings confirm that the AdT-NeuroPhysio is an understandable, feasible and adequate instrument for assessing therapeutic adherence from the perspective of people with subacute stroke in Spain.

Following the revision of the items, the experts reached a consensus in the open-ended questions section that the questionnaire had become easier to understand thanks to the use of simpler language, the elimination of sources of confusion, and the increased specificity of the questions for the target population. Except for item 6, all items were modified in terms of content or meaning. However, these can be interpreted separately depending on the complexity of the modification. In nine items (1, 3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 11, 12, 13), changes were made to replace a word with a simpler one that the public would find more accessible, and to broaden the possible factors influencing adherence. Also, these were made to involve considering rehabilitation programmes and recommendations, support from the patient’s personal environment beyond rehabilitation, and the consideration of the patient’s personal goals, in accordance with clinical practice guidelines on the person-centred neurorehabilitation.4,10 In contrast, modifications to four items (2, 4, 10, and 14) were proposed to enhance question precision, based on the key factors influencing adherence to neurorehabilitation in stroke patients identified by the experts. Item 2 explores patients’ beliefs regarding the benefits and structure of physiotherapy exercises, with the aim of capturing their perspective on the proposed rehabilitation plan. In item 4, the question has been changed to emphasise how the physiotherapy exercises are performed (intensity and frequency) rather than the time of day, aiming to obtain the patient’s opinion on how they carry them out and to underline the importance of these parameters. In items 10 and 14, the question changes to highlight intrinsic and extrinsic motivation and commitment to the rehabilitation program, to ask for the patient’s honesty regarding whether their mood or possible cognitive deficits influence the process.

Among the changes introduced, one of the most significant was the inclusion of two open-ended sections on environmental barriers and facilitators. These sections directly respond to the lived experience of this population, who often face home-based obstacles such as architectural barriers, limited family support and dependency on health public services that closed-ended items cannot contemplate.10,44

One of the most relevant contributions of the AdT-NeuroPhysio is that it captures dimensions that are increasingly recognised as central to contemporary neurorehabilitation, including patients’ beliefs, expectations, personal goals, motivation, and perceptions of the therapeutic process. Growing evidence suggests that patient-centred rehabilitation approaches, shared decision-making, and active patient involvement may positively influence rehabilitation engagement and long-term outcomes.4,10 Therefore, understanding these dimensions may help clinicians identify modifiable factors associated with poor adherence and design more individualised rehabilitation strategies.

In view of the low compliance with rehabilitation and the inadequate intensity of the exercises that comprise it among persons with stroke,14,24 tools such as the AdT-NeuroPhysio are particularly valuable for understanding the factors that influence participation in rehabilitation29,30 and for identifying barriers that prevent patients from achieving optimal engagement with treatment.10 Therapeutic alliance to stroke rehabilitation extends beyond attendance and exercise completion and is strongly influenced by factors such as the adherence, patient engagement, motivation, self-efficacy, emotional status, family support, accessibility to services, and treatment beliefs.24,45,46 Accordingly, the adaptation of the AdT-Physio scale was motivated by its ability to capture these dimensions from the patient’s perspective, offering a more comprehensive assessment of adherence than traditional compliance-based measures. Moreover, because the original questionnaire was developed in the same language and country as the present study, the adaptation process may have been facilitated and the risk of translation-related bias reduced.

The predominance of physiotherapists on the expert panel reflects the physiotherapy-specific focus of the AdT-NeuroPhysio . However, consistent with the interdisciplinary nature of stroke rehabilitation,4,6 professionals from other neurorehabilitation disciplines were also included to incorporate diverse perspectives. This sample characteristic may have influenced the relative importance assigned to some items and therefore should be considered when interpreting expert consensus.47

The authors found no studies in the literature that employed the same methodology and population as in this research, limiting direct comparison of the results. Nevertheless, a comparison of the results of content validity with assessments such as the King’s Parkinson’s Disease Pain Scale (KPPS)48 and the tool developed by Chen et al to assess post-stroke depression (PSD),49 reveals very similar data for the S-CVI/Ave, ranging from 0.85 to 0.88, and for the I-CVI, with even greater variance in these studies, ranging from 0.5 to 1. In terms of internal consistency, Cronbach’s α was found that in both the original AdT-Physio study21 and in questionnaires analogous to AdT-NeuroPhysio, such as ARRS22 or OHAQ-SP,23 these values are comparable and marginally elevated: 0.897, 0.96, and 0.916, respectively.

However, the subscale measuring beliefs about treatment/therapist obtained a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.685, which falls slightly below the threshold of 0.70 for adequate internal consistency. This finding should be interpreted with caution since may help explain this value. Cronbach’s alpha is highly sensitive to the number of items; therefore, shorter and multidimensional subscales (more complex than a binary compliance measure) tend to produce lower coefficients in exploratory studies.50 As this is a predictable mathematical event, it does not invalidate the overall scale and may not interfere with the reliability of the total adherence score.50

Regarding the pilot study, participants achieved a completion time close to the original AdT-Physio average of 2 minutes 26 seconds (SD: 25 seconds),21 improving between measurements. While direct comparisons in rehabilitation adherence are limited due to a lack of reported response times, reviews of similar-length questionnaires in medication adherence report acceptable ranges of 2–5 minutes.51,52 Therefore, the completion time for the AdT-NeuroPhysio is commensurate with the stated timeframe, although it should be noted that the two optional free-text spaces may slightly extend it. Although no patient reported difficulties or fatigue during the pilot study, a standardised procedure should be in place for clinical practice. The AdT-NeuroPhysio has been developed as a self-administered tool. Although it is designed for patient completion, it is recommended that a physiotherapist or other healthcare professional remains present while the patient completes it. This would allow the professional to clarify doubts, guide the patient through the items, and help in case of fatigue, reading difficulties, cognitive impairments, motor difficulties or any other inconvenience. If a patient is unable to complete the questionnaire within a reasonable time, the professional may read the items to the patient as an interview format or allow a short rest period before the re-attempt completion. In all cases, the professional should provide the require care without influencing the patient’s responses. Furthermore, this questionnaire could also be administered using adapted computer systems such as mice, optical scanners, etc, or through digital platforms such as online forms with professional supervision.

The key strength of our study is that it complies with current consensus-based methodological standards for the content-validation of the AdT-NeuroPhysio.33 The final version of the questionnaire incorporates the evaluations of both parties involved in the rehabilitation process: the content analysis by the professionals and the comprehensibility analysis in the rehabilitation context. Consequently, the questionnaire offers a broader and more humanised perspective on the patient’s experience and expectations during neurorehabilitation. This approach can help people feel that their needs are being met and enable non-adherent patients to articulate the underlying factors that prevent them from achieving the expected level of adherence. In the case of Spain’s public healthcare system, the delayed start of neurorehabilitation and the lower intensity and multidisciplinary nature of interventions in certain regions of the country result in greater financial hardship for families, as they are forced to seek care at private centres. This also leads to patients being more severely affected by the after-effects of stroke.44

However, the national model for stroke neurorehabilitation aims to follow the recommendations set out in clinical practice guidelines,44 which advocate for high-intensity, individualised rehabilitation tailored to the patient’s goals.53 To analyse whether the patient undergoes that level of therapy or perceives the interventions as individualised, the AdT-NeuroPhysio can be a useful tool. In future research, this questionnaire can also be linked to motor and functional changes over time. As the Spanish public healthcare system evolves, there is an opportunity to explore research and intervention strategies based on home-based rehabilitation programmes, as implemented in the United Kingdom and Sweden.54 In these models, therapeutic intervention is optimised so that stroke patients who have completed a period of neurorehabilitation at a specialist centre, or who have greater functional abilities, can be cared for in the home setting. This approach frees up resources for those with greater care needs.55 Patients with higher levels of compliance, beliefs and motivation, as assessed by this questionnaire, could benefit from this type of home-based programme, with closer long-term monitoring. Meanwhile, more severely affected patients could be treated in neurorehabilitation centres.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged, as they may affect the generalisability of the findings. Firstly, although the inclusion criteria allowed participation of professionals with fewer than 10 years of experience, this decision was intended to capture a broader range of perspectives. Furthermore, the large Delphi panel (n = 165) may have helped mitigate any potential influence of differences in professional experience on the consensus process. Secondly, the sample size of the pilot study could be considered relatively narrow. Although this sample size is considered acceptable for cognitive testing and pilot studies,34,40 it may limit the generalisability of the findings to the wider stroke population.

Additionally, the lower internal consistency observed in the beliefs about treatment/therapist subscale compared with the compliance subscale may reflect the substantial heterogeneity of stroke survivors regarding their beliefs, expectations, therapeutic priorities, and informational needs. Clinical guidelines and previous research have shown that patients with stroke often differ considerably in their values and preferences concerning rehabilitation strategies and participation in decision-making processes.47,55 Therefore, a greater variability in responses within this construct may be expected compared with more behaviour-oriented aspects of adherence, such as treatment compliance, which may partially explain the lower internal consistency coefficient observed in this subscale.

At last, this study focused exclusively on content validity and feasibility; therefore, other important psychometric properties, including structural validity, construct validity, test–retest reliability, responsiveness, measurement error, and minimal detectable change, were not assessed. Finally, neither this study nor the original AdT-Physio publication established clinical cut-off values or adherence categories,21 therefore practitioners should interpret the scores as a continuous measure, with higher values indicating greater self-reported adherence; meanwhile, the open-ended questions allow for an analysis of the contextual factors that influence patient adherence. Future studies should evaluate the full psychometric properties of the AdT-NeuroPhysio in larger and more diverse samples, establish clinically meaningful thresholds, and explore its applicability across different stroke severity levels and other neurological populations.

Conclusions

The AdT-NeuroPhysio is a content-validated, comprehensible and feasible measurement for assessing adherence to a rehabilitation programme from the perspective of Spanish people with stroke. The availability of this instrument to assess therapeutic adherence in neurological physiotherapy from the patient’s perspective can contribute to improve compliance, optimise therapeutic interventions and help to build a strong patient-therapist relationship. Further research is required to analyse its psychometric properties in larger sample samples and across different neurological disorders.
