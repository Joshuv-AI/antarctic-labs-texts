Interdependence of dental parameters, mandibular mobility and static postural sway as well as load distribution in male subjects of advanced working age
Authors: Christian W, Krüger D, Erbe C, Maurer-Grubinger C, Oremek G, Groneberg DA, Ohlendorf D
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaintaining static postural sway as well as load distribution is an essential component of overall physical stability in older age. The temporomandibular system is a measurable influencing factor due to functional interdependencies, although it has not yet been sufficiently investigated with regard to sex- and age-specific differences. This cross-sectional analysis therefore aims to examine the association between occlusion, mandibular mobility and static postural sway as well as load distribution in men aged 51 to 60, taking into account body mass index (BMI) and mandibular mobility.MethodsA total of 98 healthy men, aged between 51 and 60 years (mean age: 55.9 ± 2.72 years), volunteered for the study. Their mean BMI was 27.01 ± 3.91 kg/m² (mean height: 1.81 ± 0.07 m; mean weight: 88 ± 14.13 kg). BMI classification was based on WHO criteria. Postural measurements were conducted using a pressure platform. Mandibular movements (laterotrusion, protrusion and mouth opening) were recorded using an axiograph. Additionally, a model analysis according to Schopf was performed (midline deviation, supporting zone, and transverse width differences in the maxilla and mandible on both the right and left; occlusion of the canines [3s] and first molars [6s] on both sides; overjet; and overbite). The significance level was set at 5%.ResultsDental model analysis revealed that no participant was classified as Angle Class I, (mean overjet 3.93 mm and mean overbite 3.44 mm). All subjects exceeded the normal ranges defined by the Schopf classification, and the majority (75%) presented with Angle Class II.No significant association was found between BMI and static postural sway and well as load distribution in the overall analysis (p > 0.05).BMI subgroup analysis: In the normal-weight group, occlusion of the right first molar (6) exhibited a modest positive correlation with ellipse area (p ≤ 0.01), while occlusion of the right canine (3) exhibited a modest positive correlation with frontal sway (p ≤ 0.01). In the pre-obese group, only a weak negative correlation was observed between occlusion of the right first molar (6) and forefoot load (p ≤ 0.01). In the obese group, occlusion of the right canine (3) showed a strong positive correlation with rearfoot load (p ≤ 0.01) and right laterotrusion showed a significant correlation with sagittal sway (p ≤ 0.02; moderate). Mandibular mobility subgroups in relation to BMI: in participants with hypomobile mouth opening, BMI correlated positively with frontal sway (p ≤ 0.03, strong). In the group with normal right laterotrusion, increasing BMI was associated with significant increases in frontal and sagittal sway, as well as ellipse parameters (p ≤ 0.01–0.05; all weak). In individuals with hypermobile mandibular function, BMI showed a strong negative correlation with occlusion of the first molars (6) in the left forefoot (p ≤ 0.001), as well as a strong positive correlation with the left rearfoot (p ≤ 0.01). DiscussionWhile mandibular mobility parameters (laterotrusion, protrusion, mouth opening) were predominantly within normal limits, dental model analysis revealed that the majority (75%) presented with Angle Class II. Additional exploratory subgroup indicated that deviations in canine and molar occlusion were associated with greater body sway and larger sway ellipses. Background

Maintaining physical mobility and postural control is particularly important for older men, many of whom remain in full-time employment in Germany beyond the statutory retirement age of 67 [1–7, 29]. The maintenance of postural stability is a critical factor in physical resilience, particularly as age-related changes affect sensorimotor regulation, thereby counteracting age-related changes and disease progression, is well established [8–10]. These factors help individuals to meet continuously increasing qualitative and quantitative demands, including those in the workplace [11–14]. In this context, another potentially relevant factor, especially with regard to resilience to occupational stress [15], is the temporomandibular system. Due to its neurofunctional capacities, it can act as a form of ‘valve’ for reducing or relieving stress, as psychological and emotional tension can be transferred to the teeth and surrounding tissues through bruxism [16]. Minor disturbances are often compensated for by the body, particularly via the cervical and neck musculature, as well as through local sensorimotor regulation involving the temporomandibular joint [11, 17, 18]. Due to the functional interdependence between motor control of posture and movement, and mandibular position, these loads can have a significant impact on postural control [16, 19–21].

Several systematic reviews have investigated these relationships. Cezar Ioniță et al. [22] generally confirmed that various occlusal positions influence postural control, the most frequently assessed parameter compared to other components. Similarly, Solano et al. [23] reported that mandibular positions predominantly induced postural changes in the mediolateral direction, whereas malocclusions primarily affected the anteroposterior axis. Furthermore, Stancker et al. [24] concluded that malocclusion (an imbalance between the masticatory and neuromuscular systems) can have a negative impact on body posture and postural parameters. Malocclusion can also influence the function of the temporomandibular system, which transmits information to the vestibular system that is involved in regulating postural balance. Disruption to one of the main sensory systems (such as the feet, eyes or temporomandibular system) may therefore result in altered posture.

Nowak et al. [25, 26] and Alessandro et al. [27] observed a significant tendency towards anterior displacement of the centre of pressure (CoP) in young adults with Angle Class II, along with a significantly more anterior head position and increased cervical and lumbar lordosis, compared to individuals with Angle Classes I and III. In contrast, participants with Angle Classes I and III showed posterior displacement of the centre of pressure (p < 0.001).

The aforementioned systematic reviews [22–25], which examined the associations between occlusion and postural control, either lacked sex-specific analyses or considered them to be of minor importance. Furthermore, they did not take into account age-related neurophysiological changes or the potential influence of body mass index (BMI). However, sex-specific differences become apparent from around 12–13 years of age and can be attributed to neurophysiological and hormonal differences in development [28–32]. Age-related neurophysiological changes also affect coordination abilities.

The relationship between permanent dental parameters, mandibular mobility, and postural control in relation to age and gender has already been investigated in women aged 21–30 and 41–50 [33–35]. In the younger group, a higher BMI was associated with increased forefoot loading, albeit with a small effect size. Participants with greater forefoot loading also exhibited increased mouth opening, but no changes in protrusion or laterotrusion [33, 34]. In women aged 41–50, restricted right laterotrusion was associated with reduced forefoot loading and increased rearfoot loading. Meanwhile, excessive left laterotrusion was associated with reduced frontal sway, as well as a smaller sway ellipse in terms of area, height, and width [32].

BMI is frequently analysed as a negative influencing factor on postural control in addition to age and sex [36–38]. Individuals with a higher BMI typically have greater body mass in the thoracic and abdominal regions, which leads to an anterior shift in the centre of pressure [38, 39]. Increased load may therefore impair balance stability [36], with men additionally exhibiting specific instability in the mediolateral axis [40, 41]. Conversely, Teasdale et al. demonstrated that weight loss linearly improves postural control. The observed sex differences may be partly explained by the prevalence of abdominal obesity in men [36, 37]. Overall, these findings suggest that a higher BMI negatively affects postural control. It should also be noted that obesity in adults can lead to reduced mobility and functional limitations [39, 42], which in turn increase the risk of falls — an especially important issue as we age.

The aim of the present analysis, conducted as part of a larger project [43], is to investigate the relationship between occlusion, mandibular mobility and static postural sway as well as load distribution in men aged 51 to 60, while taking BMI [34, 35, 44] and mandibular mobility [45, 46] into account. This age group is of particular interest because labour force participation in Germany exhibits a clear gender-specific pattern: the traditional male breadwinner model still dominates the German labour market [4, 47–49], meaning men are more likely to be employed full-time than women [1–4, 29]. Furthermore, in high-income countries, including Germany, men often continue working beyond the current statutory retirement age of 67 [5–7], and an increase to 68 has been under political discussion for some time [50–54].

Methods

Participants

A total of 98 healthy men aged between 51 and 60 years (mean: 55.9 years; median: 56 years) voluntarily took part in this study. All participants were recruited from two geographically distinct general dental practices.

The proportion of right-handed individuals was 86%. On average, participants were 1.81 ± 0.07 m tall and weighed 88 ± 14.13 kg, resulting in a mean BMI of 27.01 ± 3.91 kg/m² according to the WHO classification [55, 56]. According to WHO criteria [55], 36 men were of normal weight (group II; 18.50–24.90 kg/m²), 45 were overweight (group III; 25.00–29.90 kg/m²), and 17 were obese (group IV; ≥30.00 kg/m²). In total, 84 participants were right-handed, 10 were left-handed and four were ambidextrous.

Twenty-one (21.42%) of the 98 participants had completed orthodontic treatment. The distribution of Angle classes was as follows: n = 0 for Class I, n = 75 for Class II and n = 23 for Class III.

Regarding physical activity, 44 participants reported engaging in regular sports activities, while 54 reported little to no regular exercise. Occupational activity varied: Forty-six men (46.94%) reported predominantly sedentary work (e.g. office employees); 44 men (44.89%) reported predominantly standing occupations (e.g. hairdressers); and eight men (8.17%) reported physically active jobs involving substantial movement (e.g. carpenters or industrial mechanics).

At baseline, all participants completed a medical history questionnaire from the Centre for Dentistry, Oral and Maxillofacial Medicine at Goethe University Frankfurt am Main (Germany). This questionnaire covered general conditions such as diabetes mellitus, tinnitus, osteoporosis and rheumatic diseases. It also included questions on acute joint and musculoskeletal pain, headaches and migraines, acute temporomandibular joint (TMJ) pain or sounds, accidents or surgeries affecting the musculoskeletal system or TMJ in the past two years, a deep bite of more than 3 mm and current medication use.

Only participants who subjectively considered themselves to be healthy and did not report any of the predefined criteria in the questionnaire were included in the study. The classification of participants as healthy was based on self-reported information. Specifically, participants reported no acute symptoms and subjectively considered themselves to be in good health at the time of assessment.

Participants who reported occasional TMJ sounds or pain underwent a brief clinical examination (palpation) to assess the likelihood of TMJ disorders, which constituted a strict exclusion criterion. Further exclusion criteria address other relevant dental factors, including fixed prosthodontic restorations (e.g., crowns, bridges, and implants), missing teeth, and extensive restorative treatments. Moderate musculoskeletal complaints were permitted provided they did not interfere with daily activities. No radiation-based imaging (e.g. X-rays or CT scans) was performed to verify spinal health or subjective reports, as unnecessary radiation exposure is considered unethical in Germany (ALARA principle) [57–59]. Combining the use of this medical history questionnaire with additional questions on completed orthodontic treatment and physical activity [60] resulted in a study population that was considered to be free of acute health symptoms at the time of data collection.

The rights of the participants were protected, and all subjects were thoroughly informed about the study design before providing written informed consent. The study was approved by the local ethics committee of the Medical Faculty of Goethe University (No. 103/16) and was conducted in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and subsequent amendments.

Measurement systems

Posturography

A pressure measurement platform (GP MultiSens, GeBioM, Münster, Germany) was used to record load distribution and body sway. Measurements were taken over a surface area of 38.5 × 38.5 cm containing 2,304 resistive sensors (two sensors per cm²), each measuring 8 × 8 mm. At a sampling rate of 200 Hz, the pressure generated by loading was recorded in a matrix arrangement of sensor units. Pressure changes were detected via high-impedance amplifiers (unit: pascal, force per unit area), with signals captured through 48 connections arranged in rows and columns. According to the manufacturer’s specifications, the potential measurement error is ± 5%.

During data acquisition, all participants stood barefoot on the platform in their habitual posture with their eyes open and their jaw relaxed. Three trials of 30 s each were recorded per participant. The mean value across the three trials was calculated for each postural parameter and used in all subsequent analyses.

Axiography

Three-dimensional mandibular movements were recorded using the ARCUS DIGMA SD II system (KaVo/Zebris Medical GmbH, Biberach, Germany), which uses ultrasound. This system comprises a 250 g facebow (340 g including sensors) and a 100 g transmitter, which is attached to a mandibular bite fork (paraocclusal tray) via magnets. The bite fork is fixed to each participant individually using bite registration material (Luxabite, DMG).

A 40 kHz ultrasound navigator, aligned in three dimensions, recorded spatial positional changes at a sampling rate of 50 Hz. According to the manufacturer, the measurement accuracy ranges between ± 0.1 and 0.2 mm, ensuring clinically relevant reproducibility [60–63]. The recorded data were processed using the associated WinJAW+ software (version 1.0).

Model analysis

For this, impressions of the maxilla and mandible were taken using alginate (Trealgin Chromatic, Schütz Dental, Rosbach vor der Höhe, Germany). These impressions were subsequently cast in dental stone (Natura, DIN EN ISO 6873, Type 3, Siladent, Dr. Böhm & Schöps GmbH, Goslar, Germany).

To register the individual habitual occlusion, modelling wax (Standard Modelling Wax 175/80, 1.25 mm, Gebrüder Steinhart Wachswarenfabrik GmbH & Co. KG) was used to relate the model segments.

A model analysis was performed for quantitative assessment of occlusion (alginate impressions, plaster models and measurements using a ‘Beerendonk’ caliper). This precision instrument, which is commonly used in orthodontics and dental technology, provides measurement accuracy to 0.1 mm [64]. Classification of occlusal parameters followed the criteria described by Schopf [65, 66]. To ensure measurement reliability, the examiner completed a structured calibration protocol prior to data collection, following established standards for plaster model analysis [67, 68]. Intra-examiner reliability was assessed by a single examiner who repeated the measurements on 10 randomly selected dental models after a 14-day interval. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was 0.93.

Evaluation parameters

Postural control: percentage distribution of load (%) across all four quadrants (left/right forefoot and rearfoot), as well as aggregated values for the left/right foot and forefoot/rearfoot. Additionally, maximum frontal and sagittal displacement of the center of pressure (mm), and the width (cm), height (cm), and area (cm²) of the sway ellipse were analyzed.

Statistical analysis

An a priori sample size estimation was performed based on the primary objective of investigating associations between dental parameters, mandibular mobility, static postural sway, and plantar load distribution in healthy men aged 51–60 years. A target sample size of approximately n = 100 participants was defined. For correlation analyses, a sample size of approximately n = 100 provides 80% power to detect correlation coefficients of r ≥ 0.25 (α = 0.05), corresponding to at least a weak association according to Evans’ classification.

The final sample consisted of n = 98 participants, as five initially available datasets had to be excluded due to incomplete data for relevant parameters required for the analyses. The achieved sample size was therefore considered adequate for the primary correlation analyses. However, subsequent stratified subgroup analyses (e.g., according to BMI categories) resulted in smaller subgroup sizes. These analyses were considered exploratory and were interpreted with caution due to the limited statistical power of individual subgroups.

Statistical analyses were performed using the software package BIAS (version 10). Initially, data were tested for normal distribution using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov goodness-of-fit test. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables, including the median, first and third quartiles (Q1–Q3), tolerance ranges, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). As the data were predominantly not normally distributed, non-parametric statistical methods were applied. Differences between groups were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, Wilcoxon matched-pairs test, Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney U test, and Conover–Iman post hoc test, as appropriate. In cases where subgroup sizes were small (n < 10), these groups were excluded from inferential statistical analyses due to limited statistical reliability p-values obtained from multiple group comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni–Holm correction.

Correlations were calculated using Spearman’s rho and Kendall’s tau. Effect sizes (rho) were interpreted according to Evans as follows: <0.2 (poor), 0.2–0.4 (weak), 0.4–0.6 (moderate), 0.6–0.8 (strong), and > 0.8 (optimal). Due to the exploratory nature of the correlation analyses, no adjustment for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni–Holm correction was applied.

The level of significance was set at 5%.

This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies.

Discussion

The present study aimed to analyse the interdependencies between dental parameters, mandibular mobility and static postural sway as well as load distribution in male subjects of advanced working age. The 98 participants, who perceived themselves as healthy, were predominantly overweight to mildly obese according to their BMI (mean BMI: 27 ± 4 kg/m²) and exhibited balanced weight distribution between the left (51%) and right (49%) sides of their body. The forefoot–hindfoot ratio (38:62) corresponded to values reported in other studies involving normal-weight and pre-obese adults [25, 35].

Similarly, median postural sway of 14 mm in the medio-lateral direction and 20 mm in the antero-posterior direction, with an ellipse area of 22.24 cm², falls within the normal range for healthy middle-aged to older adults of normal weight or with moderate obesity (BMI 25–35 kg/m²) described in the literature [25, 35, 69–72]. Axiographically measured laterotrusion to the left and right, mouth opening and protrusion were within normal ranges for the majority of participants. Mandibular mobility parameters (laterotrusion, protrusion, mouth opening) were within normal ranges for the majority of participants [22, 23]. In contrast, dental model analysis revealed that none of the participants exhibited Angle Class I occlusion: 75% were classified as Class II and 25% as Class III. Heren, the mean overjet (3.93 mm) and mean overbite (3.44 mm) both exceeded slightly the reference ranges of 2.1–2.9 mm and 1–3 mm, respectively, as defined by the Schopf classification [65, 66] This indicates that while mandibular function was largely preserved, dental alignment in this cohort of men aged 51 to 60 deviated substantially from ideal norms, consistent with the low proportion who had undergone orthodontic treatment (21%). These findings indicate that the cohort was characterized by prevalent dental malalignment despite largely normal mandibular function [22, 23].

Furthermore, postural control parameters were not influenced by physical activity, orthodontic treatment or BMI across all participants. With small effect sizes, increasing body height was associated with increased hindfoot loading, greater sagittal sway and greater ellipse width.

No significant correlations were found between axiographic (mouth opening, protrusion and laterotrusion to the left and right) and postural sway and load distribution.

A more detailed subgroup analysis based on normative classifications of mandibular mobility revealed that leftward laterotrusion and mouth opening did not affect postural stability. However, the opposite was true for laterotrusion to the right and protrusion: participants with laterotrusion to the right within the normative range (7–12 mm) exhibited a shift in load from the right foot to the left foot, as well as an increase in ellipse area, with increasing laterotrusion. For protrusion, changes were only observed in men with hyperfunctional (above normal) or hypofunctional (below normal) mandibular movement. In hypofunctional protrusion, an increase in hyperfunction was associated with an increase in ellipse height, whereas an increase in hypofunction led to greater sagittal sway.

The most significant correlations between the model analysis and parameters of postural sway and load distribution were observed in occlusion, particularly with regard to the relationships between the canines and the first molars. However, classification according to Angle classes (Classes II [II:1 + II:2] and III) had no significant impact on postural parameters (p > 0.05). Deviations in canine occlusion from ideal interdigitation were associated with increased frontal and sagittal sway and a larger sway ellipse on both the right and left sides. For example, these relationships were evident in the occlusion of the right first molar, where marked deviations from the physiological norm (class I relationship) (e.g. hyperocclusion > 2 mm or hypoocclusion < − 2 mm) were associated with increased frontal and sagittal sway, as well as a larger ellipse area and width. Furthermore, an increased overbite was associated with an increased load on the right foot.

However, it should be noted that most correlations showed weak to poor effect sizes and should therefore be interpreted cautiously as trends with limited clinical relevance.

The present results differ from findings in women aged 21–30 and 41–50 years using a similar study design [33]. In contrast, older men showed more complex patterns. Here, BMI did not show a general association with postural sway and load distribution, however some BMI stratified subgroup analyses showed associations with postural components in combination with certain mandibular or occlusal variables.

Systematic reviews [22–24] support the idea that malocclusions can negatively impact postural stability, demonstrating weak to moderate effects and showing that different occlusal positions can influence postural control, particularly centre-of-pressure displacement and ellipse parameters. Changes in mandibular position are reported to primarily affect medio-lateral sway, while malocclusions have a stronger influence on antero-posterior sway. However, the present findings do not confirm these conclusions, regardless of whether mandibular movement patterns or static occlusion are emphasised. Instead, effects were observed in both antero-posterior and medio-lateral sway (along with ellipse changes), while stance pattern (percentage load distribution) remained largely unchanged.

BMI has not previously been considered as a potential influencing factor in systematic reviews on this topic [22–25]. However, Hirjaková et al. [35] and Fischer et al. [37] reported negative effects of BMI on postural control in mildly obese adults, and Teasdale et al. [41] demonstrated that weight loss improves postural stability linearly. Ku et al. [36] also found increased mediolateral instability in men with abdominal obesity.

Based on these findings, the present data were analysed further in BMI-stratified subgroups exploratory. Neither correlation analyses nor group comparisons across BMI categories (normal weight, pre-obese and obese) revealed significant differences in postural control parameters such as postural sway and load distribution.

Within the normal-weight subgroup (n = 36), axiographic analysis showed that greater mouth opening was associated with significantly higher loading of the right foot. Model analysis indicated that malocclusion of the right first molar (with a deviation of less than 2 mm) combined with canine malocclusion was significantly associated with increased ellipse parameters (area, width and height) and increased frontal and sagittal sway (with weak effect sizes). Only the ellipse area and width increased on the left side with greater horizontal canine distance (weak effect sizes). Only model analysis showed correlations in pre-obese participants: an increase in the distance of the left canine was associated with a reduction in left hindfoot loading and an increase in right foot loading and sagittal sway (weak effect sizes). Additionally, frontal sway increased with greater midline deviation (weak effect size). In the 17 obese men (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²), sagittal sway, ellipse area and width decreased with an increased range of right laterotrusion. Most correlations had weak effect sizes and should be interpreted as trends rather than clinically relevant findings. Consistent with the previous findings, the small effect sizes observed in this analysis should be considered when interpreting the results, as they suggest limited clinical relevance. Regarding occlusion, greater molar deviation from the ideal position was associated with increased ellipse area and height (moderate to strong effect sizes), while greater canine deviation was associated with increased right hindfoot loading (strong effect size). Additionally, greater differences in transverse mandibular width were associated with a shift in load towards the left forefoot, the right hindfoot and both feet overall (moderate to strong effect sizes). These findings suggest that local malocclusions, particularly in the right canine region and transverse mandibular discrepancies, are associated with measurable changes in load distribution and postural stability, particularly in obese men.

This asymmetric distribution of findings may reflect a genuine functional lateralization; however, it may also be influenced by the exploratory nature of the analyses and the number of statistical comparisons performed, which increases the possibility of chance findings. Given that 86% of participants in our cohort were right-handed, a potential contribution of functional dominance and habitual asymmetrical loading cannot be excluded. Previous studies [73–76] have suggested associations between handedness, masticatory preference, and asymmetrical neuromuscular function, which could provide a possible explanation for the observed right-sided predominance. Nevertheless, as chewing preference, masticatory muscle activity, and lateralisation of postural strategies were not assessed in the present study, this interpretation remains hypothetical.

The relationship between BMI and axiographic or model parameters was not linear across the full sample. Some associations became visible only in subgroup analyses, which suggests a possible interaction that should be examined in further investigations. The more detailed subgroup analysis, which combined BMI categories with axiographic classifications of mandibular mobility (normal, hypofunctional or hyperfunctional), showed that, in men with right laterotrusion within the physiological range (7–12 mm), a higher BMI was significantly associated with increased frontal and sagittal body sway, as well as larger sway ellipse parameters. In groups with functional impairments, a higher BMI combined with greater lateral deviation resulted in an increased sagittal displacement of the sagittal sway, as well as a larger ellipse area and height. Again, although statistically significant, these effects were predominantly small and should be interpreted as trends.

Overall, the findings suggest that future studies should investigate BMI, occlusal characteristics, and mandibular mobility within an integrated framework rather than as isolated factors. While BMI was not generally associated with postural control parameters in this cohort, the observed subgroup differences should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating findings rather than evidence of a modifying effect. In summary, BMI did not show a general association with postural control parameters in this cohort, and the subgroup findings should be regarded as preliminary observations rather than evidence of a modifying effect.

Here, as limiting factor the cross-sectional design has to be considered as it only allows correlations to be identified, not causal relationships. Some variables, such as perceived health status and physical activity, were self-reported and may therefore be subject to bias. Furthermore, the inclusion of only asymptomatic men who subjectively perceive themselves to be healthy limits the generalizability of these findings to clinical populations with manifest temporomandibular disorders or chronic pain. A further methodological limitation concerns the assessment of participants’ health status and dental characteristics. Although removable dental prostheses were defined as exclusion criteria, other relevant dental factors, including fixed prosthodontic restorations (e.g., crowns, bridges, and implants), missing teeth, and extensive restorative treatments, were not systematically recorded. Moreover, health status was based on self-reported information and therefore reflects participants’ subjective perception of being healthy rather than an objective clinical evaluation. Consequently, undiagnosed or subclinical conditions and unrecorded dental alterations cannot be completely excluded.

Regarding the distribution of Angle classifications, it should be considered that the cohort was not intended to represent the prevalence of different occlusal classes in the general population, but rather to investigate potential associations between occlusal characteristics, mandibular function, and postural control. The absence of participants with Angle Class I occlusion and the predominance of Class II relationships may reflect characteristics of patients attending general dental practices rather than asystematic selection bias. Nevertheless, this limited representation of occlusal categories may restrict the generalizability of the findings to populations with a broader range of occlusal relationships. Previous restorative dental treatments, which are relatively common in individuals aged 51–60 years, may have influenced occlusal contact patterns, especially at the canine and first molar sites investigated in this study. As these factors were not systematically documented, their potential contribution to the observed associations between occlusal parameters and postural control remains uncertain. Future studies should include comprehensive dental examinations, including assessment of restorations, missing teeth, implant-supported prostheses, and objective health parameters, to better account for potential confounding factors affecting occlusal and postural outcomes.

A limitation of the present study is that some stratified subgroup analyses (e.g., according to BMI categories) were based on relatively small sample sizes, which may have reduced statistical power and increased the uncertainty of the estimated associations. Therefore, findings from these subgroup analyses, particularly strong correlations observed in small subgroups, should be interpreted with caution.

Future longitudinal or interventional studies should examine whether the observed associations are reproducible and whether they have any practical relevance. It should also be noted that the present study design used only a single static condition (eyes open, habitual jaw position). The standard paradigm for probing functional links between the occlusal system and postural control typically includes additional conditions, such as clenching versus rest, or eyes-closed trials, which increase postural demand and make any occlusion-related effects more detectable [22, 23]. By contrast, correlating static dental morphology with postural control under a single, low-demand condition represents a conservative and indirect test of a functional relationship. The absence of an eyes-closed condition means that visual compensation for any postural relevant input from the temporomandibular system could not be assessed. Future studies should incorporate task manipulation (e.g., clenching, protrusion) and visual condition variations to provide a more direct test of the mechanisms hypothesized here.

The findings of this study are primarily applicable to healthy, asymptomatic men aged 51–60 years. Therefore, the generalizability of the results to women, other age groups, or clinical populations with temporomandibular disorders or other musculoskeletal conditions is limited. Future studies including more diverse populations are warranted to determine whether the observed associations are consistent across different demographic and clinical groups.

Conclusion

Therefore, clinical relevant association between postural sway and load distribution and occlusion, or mandibular mobility parameter when analyzed across the full sample of 98 men aged 51 to 60 years. In addition, static postural control parameters were not influenced by physical activity, orthodontic treatment or BMI across all participants. Additional exploratory subgroup analyses indicated that deviations in canine and molar occlusion were associated with increased postural sway and larger sway ellipses. An increased BMI does not appear to directly cause postural instability but may be associated with, or contribute to, pre-existing functional limitations or occlusal dysfunctions. Therefore, clinical assessment should adopt a holistic approach that considers BMI alongside individual constitutional, occlusal, and functional parameters.

The cross-sectional design limits interpretation to associations rather than causal relationships. Additionally, the inclusion of only healthy, asymptomatic men limits generalizability to clinical populations, while unrecorded factors such as restorative treatments, missing teeth, implant-supported prostheses, and the use of a single static posturographic condition (eyes open, habitual jaw position) may have influenced the observed relationships.
