Effects of resisted movement training on change-of-direction speed in recreationally active and trained individuals: A systematic review with network meta-analysis and meta-regression
Authors: Mingyang Zhang, Urs Granacher, Ruixiang Yan, Hallvard Nygaard Falch, Rodrigo Ramirez-Campillo, Kai Xu, Xing Zhang, Jinming Li, Shubo Xu, Yulin Luo, Duanying Li, Jian Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Resisted movement training (R-MT) is a conditioning modality that applies additional loads during plyometric or sprint training exercises for example through the wearing of weighted vests.•Pairwise meta-analysis demonstrates moderate and significant effects of R-MT on change-of-direction speed over both active controls (e.g., unresisted sprint and plyometric training, strength training) and passive controls (e.g., no exercise), with pronounced benefits against passive comparators.•Bayesian network meta-analysis identifies that vertically resisted training (e.g., plyometric training using weighted vests) exerts superior improvement on change-of-direction speed compared with horizontally resisted training (e.g., sprint training using sleds) and unresisted training.•The adaptations induced by R-MT appear to be independent of the turning angle during change-of-direction tests.

Introduction

Change-of-direction (COD) speed is a critical determinant of sporting success in multidirectional sports, requiring coordinated neuromuscular control and metabolic efficiency.1,2 Within the broader framework of multidirectional performance, agility is commonly conceptualized as a multifactorial ability that encompasses both perceptual-cognitive components (e.g., decision-making) and COD speed.1 In this context, COD speed refers to the closed-skill movement performed in response to a pre-planned task.3 Elite athletes perform hundreds of directional changes per match, with loss of movement velocity occurring as the turning angle increases from 45° to 180°.4,5 In addition to performance decrements, sharp turning angles increase multiplanar joint loading and impose greater biomechanical demands on the knee, which is associated with an elevated risk of non-contact anterior cruciate ligament ruptures.6, 7, 8, 9 Therefore, the COD performance is crucial for enhancing athletic performance and preventing injuries.10,11

The potential mechanism to improve COD is underpinned by 3 interconnected adaptative pathways: First, neuromuscular efficiency;12, 13, 14 second, force-vector specificity;15,16 and third, mechanical overload.17,18 A variety of physical training modalities, such as sprint, plyometric, and strength training, have been shown to improve measures of COD speed.19, 20, 21 While traditional strength training effectively increases strength, it may not optimally facilitate power development due to the significant deceleration phases inherent in such exercises.22 In contrast, resisted movement training (R-MT) exemplifies the transferability23 and specificity of the principle of training specificity,24, 25, 26, 27 aiming to enhance power and athletic performance by incorporating added resistance into sport-specific exercises while preserving movement coordination and integrity.28, 29, 30 The resistance applied during R-MT is characterized by its vector orientation, which can be predominantly vertical (e.g., weighted vests), horizontal (e.g., sleds), or a combination of both during plyometric jump training or sprint training. This vector-specific application of load facilitates targeted improvements in force production that align with the primary movement directions of athletic tasks. For instance, resisted sprint training and resisted plyometric training have proven to enhance various aspects of athletic performance through targeted force–velocity profiling.29,31 More specifically, R-MT allows for continuous acceleration throughout the range of motion, providing a greater stimulus to the working muscles than other unspecific training modalities.22,26,32, 33, 34, 35 R-MT methods have the potential to enhance movement efficiency through dynamic exercises that apply additional resistance, to promote the transmutation of strength gains into athletic performance and improving overall training transfer.31,36,37

Furthermore, understanding the turning angles underlying COD speed is crucial for optimizing training adaptations. Bourgeois et al.38 and Nygaard Falch et al.19 have categorized COD tasks into velocity-oriented (<90°) and force-oriented (>90°) tasks, thereby simplifying the interpretation of angle-specific mechanical determinants. However, this binary framework may overlook the biomechanical complexity of intermediate angles (90°–120°), which require a balance between the rate of force development and eccentric strength.39, 40, 41, 42 The 0°–90° turns are primarily classified as velocity-oriented COD tasks, emphasizing speed-strength. In contrast, the 90°–120° range appears to represent a “hybrid” zone that targets power-oriented COD tasks (strength-speed).20,42, 43, 44 Finally, tasks ranging from 120° to 180° are classified as strength-oriented.45, 46, 47

The COD assessments have predominantly focused on single-turn activities,44,48 whereas game scenarios involve multi-directional movements with cumulative energy dissipation and dynamically shifting angles.3,49,50 This complicates the determination of biomechanically optimal COD angles.51 For instance, larger angle turns demand greater braking forces for deceleration, leading to kinetic energy loss.51, 52, 53 Therefore, COD angles where energy conversion and velocity reduction are maximized may be prioritized in our meta-analysis, as these exert the greatest biomechanical influence.54 Considering that COD angles differ across sports, positions, and tactical tasks, prioritizing the most frequently occurring turning angles of a test or game play scenario better represents sport-specific demands.5,55

Therefore, we hypothesize that R-MT outperforms other physical training modalities when it comes to enhancing COD speed. Accordingly, this systematic review aimed to (a) compare R-MT’s effectiveness against different physical training modalities, such as unresisted sprint, for COD improvement through Bayesian network meta-analysis (NMA), thereby clarifying its sport-specific benefits; and (b) conduct multilevel meta-regression to quantify the impact of the turning angle on COD adaptations in recreationally active and trained individuals.

The PRISMA-NMA checklist is available in Supplementary Table 1. An angle-based taxonomy model was established to classify COD tasks according to turning angles. Search strategy

A systematic literature search was performed from inception until August 2025 across 7 databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, SPORTDiscus, CINAHL, Scopus, and Google Scholar. The search utilized Medical Subject Headings and text-word terms without applying age or sex restrictions. Gray literature and reference lists of relevant reviews were also examined.65 Further, we incorporated 2 AI-assisted search engines, including Elicit and Consensus.66 Authors were contacted via email to clarify ambiguities or request missing data as needed. Database-specific search syntaxes are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Study selection and data extraction

All eligible studies were independently assessed by 2 reviewers (MZ and RY) based on predefined criteria. Inter-rater agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa statistic, with k ≥ 0.8 indicating substantial to almost perfect agreement.67,68 Full texts of potentially relevant studies were retrieved and assessed according to the predefined PICOS criteria.69 Discrepancies at any stage were resolved through discussion or adjudication by a 3rd reviewer (KX). Articles that raised uncertainties were included for full-text review. The full texts of the identified studies were evaluated for eligibility based on the inclusion criteria.

Data extraction was performed independently using a standardized form, capturing key domains such as study characteristics (e.g., first author, publication year, study design), participant characteristics (e.g., age, sex, sample size), intervention details (e.g., type/modality, frequency, duration, intensity, control conditions), and outcome measures related to COD speed and other relevant performance metrics. The extracted data were systematically recorded for further analysis, ensuring clarity and consistency in reporting. Performance times reported in seconds were normalized to a consistent time unit. Studies were classified as angle-specific if the training explicitly included COD tasks at specific angles, and as unreported or non-specific if the training did not involve angle-matched COD tasks or if it involved only linear sprint or plyometric training. For studies with multiple time points, data collected at the end of the training program were prioritized to evaluate the chronic effects of the intervention.70 When available, the mean change from baseline and the standard deviation (SD) of change were extracted. If SD was not reported, it was calculated from available data, such as standard errors, 95% confidence intervals (95%CIs), p values, or t statistics. In cases where neither value was provided, SD was imputed using pre- and post-intervention SD values, assuming a correlation coefficient of 0.5.71 For studies with multiple groups performing the same intervention, each group was analyzed separately to maintain data clarity.72,73 In instances where data were missing or unclear, authors were contacted for clarification or additional information.

Risk of bias (ROB) assessment

The risk of bias for each study was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool.74 Each study was evaluated across 5 domains: (a) randomization process, (b) deviations from intended interventions, (c) missing outcome data, (d) measurement of outcomes, and (e) selection of reported results. Two independent reviewers (MZ and RY) conducted the assessments, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion and consensus with a 3rd reviewer (XZ). Studies were categorized as low, medium (some concerns), or high risk of bias. The overall risk of bias for each study was determined based on the classifications of the individual domains.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata/SE 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) and R 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), employing packages such as netmeta, gemtc, and metafor. Data synthesis followed a sequential approach, progressing from pairwise meta-analyses to Bayesian NMA. For pairwise comparisons, the pooled effect was quantified as Hedges’ g,75,76 a small-sample corrected standardized mean difference (SMD), along with its 95%CI.56 To enhance the practical interpretation, we also computed 95% prediction intervals (95%PIs) to estimate the range within which the true effect of a future, similar study would be expected to fall.77 Effect sizes were standardized so that positive values consistently indicated improvements in COD speed. The magnitude of Hedges’ g was interpreted using the following thresholds: <0.2 for trivial; 0.2−0.5 for small; >0.5−0.8 for moderate; and >0.8 for large.78 Statistical significance was defined by a two-tailed p < 0.05.

Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the Q statistic (p < 0.10) and quantified by estimating the between-study variance (τ²), with thresholds interpreted as low (<0.04), low-to-moderate (0.04–0.16), moderate-to-high (>0.16–0.36), and high (>0.36).79,80 Given the anticipated clinical and methodological diversity, a random-effects model was adopted as the primary approach for pairwise meta-analyses. Pre-specified subgroup analyses were conducted based on control group and training modality using mixed-effects models.

The NMA was conducted within a Bayesian framework using Markov Chain Monte Carlo simulation. We ran 4 chains for 50,000 iterations each, discarding the first 20,000 as a burn-in period and thinning by 10. Convergence of the models was confirmed using the Gelman-Rubin diagnostic, where a potential scale reduction factor close to 1.0 (<1.1) indicated satisfactory convergence.81 Both fixed-effects and random-effects NMA models were fitted, and model selection was guided by the deviance information criterion and posterior mean residual deviance, with the better-fitting model being retained for inference.82, 83, 84

The fundamental assumption of consistency was evaluated globally and locally. Global inconsistency was assessed by comparing the fit of the consistency model with an unrelated mean effects model using posterior predictive checks.85 Local inconsistency was assessed using Bayesian node-splitting.85,86 The relative effectiveness of the interventions was summarized using surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) values, which range from 0% to 100%.87, 88, 89 The probability of each intervention being ranked among the top 3 most effective was also calculated.90 The NMA results are presented as posterior SMDs with 95% credible intervals in league tables and forest plots, providing a comprehensive overview of both direct and indirect comparisons.91

To investigate the potential influence of the turning angle on training adaptations, we performed multilevel meta-regression analyses. We tested both linear and nonlinear (using restricted cubic splines with 4 knots) angle-response relationships.92 The optimal model (linear or nonlinear) was selected based on the lowest Akaike and Bayesian information criteria.93 Furthermore, based on the biomechanical demands, we pooled effect estimates for velocity-oriented (≤90°) and force-oriented (>90°) COD tasks.19,94

Publication bias

The reporting bias was assessed using Galbraith plots and comparison-adjusted funnel plots, along with Egger’s regression asymmetry test (p < 0.1), and Bayesian Egger’s regression for NMA to evaluate small-study effects.95,96 If significant asymmetry indicating potential small-study effects was detected, the trim-and-fill method was applied, restricted to pairwise comparisons with ≥10 trials available.97 This includes re-running the analyses with a random-effects model and conducting leave-one-out analyses to determine if any single study disproportionately influenced the pooled results.98

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for each finding derived from both pairwise and network meta-analyses was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) guidelines.99,100 Two reviewers (MYZ and XZ) independently judged the evidence for key outcomes across 5 domains: (a) randomized controlled trials (RCTs) start as high-certainty evidence. The rating was downgraded by 1 level if the body of evidence was predominantly from studies with “some concerns” as assessed by the RoB 2.0 tool, and by 2 levels if it was predominantly from studies with a high risk of bias; (b) substantial heterogeneity (τ² > 0.04) coupled with poor confidence interval overlap; (c) downgraded by 1 (serious) or 2 (very serious) levels based on judgments about the directness of the PICOS approach; (d) publication bias with substantial funnel plot asymmetry (Egger’s p < 0.10); and (e) downgraded by 1 level if the 95%CI crossed 1 minimal important difference or 2 levels if it crossed both boundaries. The final rating for each outcome was classified into 1 of 4 grades: high, moderate, low, or very low.

Results

Descriptive characteristics of studies

The study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 2). After removing duplicates, 1566 records were screened based on their titles and abstracts, resulting in the exclusion of 1281 records due to clear irrelevance. Of the 285 full-text articles assessed for eligibility, 261 were excluded for specific methodological reasons. The most common reasons for exclusion were the absence of resisted movement training, lack of eligible outcome measures, absence of a control group, unavailability of full text or data, and unsuitable study design. Ultimately, 24 articles30,34,35,101, 102, 103, 104, 105, 106, 107, 108, 109, 110, 111, 112, 113, 114, 115, 116, 117, 118, 119, 120, 121 reporting on 744 participants and 10 intervention modalities met the eligibility criteria for qualitative and quantitative synthesis. The articles were published between 2005 and 2024, and were conducted across various regions, with 15 studies (62.5%) from Europe.30,35,102,103,108,109,112, 113, 114, 115, 116, 117, 118,120,121 Participants (9.4% female) across included studies were predominantly adolescent athletes or recreationally active or trained adults, with an average age ranging from 13 years to 29 years. Publication bias

Publication bias was systematically evaluated using multiple complementary approaches, including funnel plots, Galbraith plots, and Egger’s regression analyses.122, 123, 124 Funnel plot demonstrated visual symmetry in the distribution of effect estimates around the pooled mean, with no overt asymmetry to suggest small-study effects or selective reporting (Supplementary Fig. 3A). This observation was statistically corroborated by Egger’s linear regression test (β = 0.87, 95%CI: −0.32 to 2.06; p = 0.14), confirming no significant funnel plot asymmetry. The trim-and-fill method under a random-effects model imputed 0 hypothetical missing studies, further supporting the completeness of the evidence base. In the Bayesian NMA, a comparison-adjusted funnel plot was employed to account for heterogeneity across 24 comparisons (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Effect sizes exhibited symmetrical dispersion around the null line, with no evidence of outliers or differential reporting. Extended Egger’s regression for NMAs (β = 1.12; p = 0.18) revealed no significant small-study effects. Node-splitting analyses further assessed inconsistency between direct and indirect evidence for critical comparisons (e.g., combined resisted sprint training vs. vertically resisted sprint training, p = 0.29; strength training versus plyometric training, p = 0.42), identifying no meaningful discrepancies suggestive of bias. Supplementary Fig. 4 provided additional validation, plotting standardized Hedge’s g against precision (1/SE). Studies were symmetrically distributed around the “no effect” line (θ/SE = 0), with no clustering of low-precision studies (1/SE < 2) toward extreme effect magnitudes. A non-significant regression slope (β = 0.24, 95%CI: −0.15 to 0.63; p = 0.22) and 95% of points within pseudo-confidence limits reinforced minimal bias. Sensitivity analyses excluding outliers (θ/SE > 2.5) demonstrated stable pooled estimates (Δg = 0.07). However, limited statistical power to detect subtle bias persists due to sparse data in specific comparisons (e.g., plyometric plus strength training vs. usual training, n = 2) and the modest number of low-precision studies (n = 3).

Pairwise analyses: Main effect and subgroup analyses

The pairwise meta-analysis revealed a small overall effect of R-MT on improving COD speed (g = −0.28, 95%CI: −0.40 to −0.16; 95%PI: −0.52 to −0.04; p < 0.001) compared to active control, with low heterogeneity (τ² = 0.01; Q = 48.12, p = 0.39) (Supplementary Fig. 5). Additionally, Supplementary Fig. 6 displays comparative effectiveness between resisted training and unresisted training with the same model or not. Subgroup analyses revealed that same-model training (g = −0.22, 95%CI: −0.41 to −0.03; 95%PI: −0.58 to 0.14; p = 0.02) yielded a statistically significant small ES, especially in resisted sprint and COD-specific training over active control, with low heterogeneity. In contrast, the effect of different-model training did not reach statistical significance (g = −0.37, 95%CI: −0.74 to −0.00; 95%PI: −1.18 to 0.44; p = 0.05). The overall analysis indicated a significant effect (g = −0.29, 95%CI: −0.47 to −0.11; 95%PI: −0.89 to 0.31; p < 0.001) with moderate heterogeneity. Supplementary Fig. 7 displays, more specifically, how the comparative effects of different training types on COD enhancement varied: compared with body-weight sprint (k = 7, g = −0.28, 95%CI: −0.51 to −0.06; 95%PI: −0.74 to 0.18; p = 0.01), compared with plyometric training (k = 7, g = −0.25, 95%CI: −0.52 to 0.02; 95%PI: −0.91 to 0.41; p = 0.07), compared with strength training (k = 5, g = −0.22, 95%CI: −0.57 to 0.12; 95%PI: −1.22 to 0.78; p = 0.20), compared with plyometric plus strength training (k = 3, g = −0.27, 95%CI: −0.91 to 0.37; 95%PI: −2.15 to 1.61; p = 0.42). Furthermore, there were no statistically significant differences among the subgroups (Q = 0.10, p = 0.99).

Discussion

This meta-analysis included 24 RCTs (n = 744) and showed a small overall effect of various R-MT modalities on COD speed (g = −0.28, 95%CI: −0.41 to −0.15, p < 0.05). Compared with active controls, vertically resisted plyometric training (VR-plyo: 93.0%) and vertically resisted sprint training (VR-sprint: 80.7%) outperformed horizontal or combined modalities. Further, meta-regression revealed angle-independent adaptations following R-MT.

Comparison of current findings with previous research

This study advances current understanding by addressing a critical gap in the literature regarding the effects of turning angle modulation effects on COD speed—a dimension largely overlooked in previous reviews focused on linear sprint and vertical jump performance.31,125,126 Our meta-analyses revealed statistically significant R-MT effects vs. active control, reinforcing R-MT’s superiority for athletic conditioning. Interestingly, the findings also suggest that the effectiveness of R-MT may vary depending on the specific training modality employed. For example, Bayesian NMA showed that VR-plyo and VR-sprint had the most favorable effects, corroborating emerging evidence on resistance vector specificity.127,128 These observations align with those of Suchomel et al.20 who emphasized the role of ballistic strength training in enhancing athletic performance. The pronounced effects of VR-plyo can likely be attributed to the combined strength and power development specific to plyometric exercises, which are essential for executing rapid directional changes effectively.31,129 Specifically, VR-plyo emphasizes explosive movements that utilize the stretch-shortening cycle (SSC), enhancing both muscle strength and power output. This adaptation is crucial for athletes who require quick bursts of speed during COD tasks. In contrast, modalities such as VR-sprint and horizontally resisted sprint training (HR-sprint) primarily focus on enhancing linear sprint speed and force production against resistance.130 While these training modalities improve force generation and movement velocity, they may not optimally stimulate the rate of force development and SSC efficiency required for power output during COD actions.128,131 Therefore, the adaptations from VR-plyo may provide a more tailored benefit for athletes engaged in sports requiring rapid directional changes. Our meta-regression analysis further supports the notion that the COD angle does not significantly influence the effects of R-MT,19,24,125,132 suggesting that the benefits of R-MT may be generalized across different angles rather than being restricted to specific directional movements.51 These results are in line with the findings of Chaabene et al.21 who proposed that strength gains from resistance training enhance performance across a variety of movement patterns.133 Specifically, R-MT-induced improvements were not dependent on the turning angle, suggesting that cross-planar force transfer mechanisms may overcome directional specificity, thus enhancing intermuscular coordination.134,135 This finding warrants further targeted investigation, as it contrasts with previous assumptions primarily derived from studies involving various training modalities, which suggested that angle-specific conditioning is required for optimizing COD performance.

Mechanisms of training-induced adaptation

R-MT not only induces general neuromuscular adaptations, such as earlier recruitment of type II motor units and improved intermuscular coordination,13 but it also facilitates the transfer of these adaptations during COD tasks through its kinetic specificity.136,137 Specifically, resisted movements replicate the eccentric–concentric force profiles and impulse momentum characteristics of deceleration–acceleration transitions.49,138,139 This mechanical congruence likely enhances the transfer of generalized neuromuscular adaptations to sport-specific movement patterns compared with less task-specific training modalities.140,141 Importantly, the nature of these adaptations is further modulated by resistance vector orientation. R-MT with vertical loading (e.g., weighted vests) aligns external resistance with the vertical forces experienced during COD braking phases, which may enhance a more efficient coupling of eccentric and concentric actions while reducing the amortization phase during rapid deceleration-acceleration transitions.142,143 These adaptations optimize SSC efficiency and reactive strength, both of which are critical for facilitating rapid transitions from the eccentric braking to the concentric propulsive phase.144,145 This optimization is specific to vertical loading, as it aligns resistance with the body’s natural deceleration and re-acceleration patterns. Conversely, horizontally resisted modalities emphasize horizontal ground reaction force production but may exhibit reduced transfer to multiplanar COD tasks due to vector–force incongruence.142,146, 147, 148 Consistent with these mechanistic considerations, our NMA demonstrates the superiority of VR-plyo for improving COD speed. This advantage can be attributed to (a) enhanced vertical power expression through improved triple-extension kinetics in the sagittal plane,147 and (b) reductions in braking-phase ground contact time.149,150 Further, vertical loading operates within a world-fixed reference frame, in which the direction of external resistance remains constant relative to gravity regardless of changes in body orientation during COD maneuvers. In contrast, horizontally resisted modalities function within a body-fixed reference frame, whereby the direction of applied resistance varies with body orientation during turning and re-acceleration.139 Consequently, adaptations induced by vertical loading may generalize more effectively across COD tasks, as the training stimulus remains mechanically consistent with the gravitational and braking-propulsion demands encountered in sport-specific movements. This direction-agnostic efficacy aligns with the mechanical overload doctrine, whereby chronic neuromuscular adaptations (e.g., motor unit synchronization) exhibit non-specific transferability across COD geometries.12,138

Effects of resisted movement training on COD speed

Pairwise meta-analyses demonstrated small COD improvements, with resisted sprint and plyometric training outperforming active controls. While previous studies have shown that linear sprint and plyometric training can improve COD speed,1,151 our results suggest that specific resisted modalities (e.g., VR-plyo) elicited superior gains compared to body weight sprint or unresisted plyometric protocols.2,152, 153, 154 The application of external resistance loading during sprint or plyometric movements may enhance neuromuscular adaptations, which are directly related to improved COD speed.20,155 These findings therefore suggest that the integration of resisted movements targeting COD-specific tasks may enhance athletic performance. Subgroup analyses further demonstrated that, within same-model comparisons, resisted modalities (e.g., resisted sprint training) resulted in greater improvements in COD speed compared with their unresisted counterparts performing the same training exercise (i.e., unresisted sprint training). This superiority of R-MT was observed across cohorts ranging from recreationally active to highly trained individuals.156 This suggests that for athletes at these performance calibers, the mechanical overload provided by R-MT is more effective at driving the neuromuscular adaptations (e.g., enhanced motor unit recruitment) required for COD tasks than unresisted movement training.157 Further, this finding aligns with the principle of training specificity, as biomechanically similar movements directly enhance the muscular adaptations required for COD tasks.116,157,158 Comparatively, different-model comparisons (e.g., resisted sprint training vs. unresisted plyometric training) exhibited heterogeneity, reflecting divergent biomechanical and physiological adaptations. Plyometric training prioritizes tendon elastic energy utilization,50,132 whereas resisted sprints emphasize concentric force production, suggesting that athlete-specific programs are essential to address individual deficits in strength or explosive power.22,159,160 Despite modality differences, the subgroup superiority between resistance loading and physical training supports their complementary roles in COD enhancement, as both specific and functional adaptations are prerequisites for optimal athletic performance.161,162

Comparative effectiveness of different resisted training modalities on COD speed

The Bayesian NMA establishes a distinct efficacy hierarchy among resisted training modalities. VR-plyo emerged as the most effective intervention, followed by VR-sprint and unresisted plyometric plus strength training. These findings align with the biomechanical specificity of COD mechanics, where vertical resistance (e.g., weighted vests) uniquely enhances neuromuscular adaptations crucial for both deceleration and reacceleration phases.132,133 VR-plyo’s superiority stems from its dual-phase neuromuscular targeting. By applying vertical loading during depth jumps, VR-plyo optimizes eccentric braking by controlling the amplification of vertical ground reaction forces, which differs from simply increasing the magnitude of ground reaction force through drop height elevation in unresisted plyometric training.163 This controlled force amplification enhances energy storage in tendinous tissues while simultaneously optimizing SSC efficiency through intermuscular coordination.140,164,165 These adaptations may help explain VR-plyo’s greater efficacy compared to HR-sprint, which primarily targets sagittal plane force production.17 Moreover, VR-sprint’s effectiveness stems from velocity-specific overload: a weight vest with 10%–15% body mass preserves sprint mechanics while selectively overloading propulsion kinetics.166 Comparatively, hybrid approaches like plyometric plus strength training underperform due to conflicting motor unit recruitment patterns during multi-plane loading, which impair SSC efficiency during rapid vector shifts.17,164 This challenges multimodal training dogma, showing that task-specific vertical resistance is more effective than generic strength-power combinations for COD adaptation. These outcomes support dynamic correspondence principles: interventions that replicate sport-specific force vectors result in superior transfer compared to misaligned modalities.167 Horizontally resisted drills, while beneficial for linear acceleration, do not address the critical vertical deceleration phase of COD, explaining HR-sprint’s lower efficacy (SUCRA = 41.2%).152 Notably, VR-plyo achieved comparable short term improvements to combined strength and plyometric training, suggesting targeted vertical overload could reduce reliance on complex periodization for COD-specific adaptations in time-constrained scenarios.168 Crucially, resisted training outperforms unresisted modalities when force vectors align with COD kinematics. Indiscriminate application of resistance (e.g., horizontal loads during cuts) may fail to enhance performance, highlighting the necessity of biomechanical congruence between training stimuli and competition demands.169 These findings support vector-specific resistance prescriptions over generic overload strategies in athletic programs.

Effects of the turning angle on COD performance

Meta-regression analyses showed no significant relation between COD angles (≥90° vs. <90°) and training-induced improvements in COD speed after R-MT (Fig. 4). Importantly, all included studies utilized non-specific training protocols with unrestricted turning angles, rather than strictly prescribed COD-specific training. Training-induced adaptations are most pronounced when the task closely aligns with the specific demands of the sport, including the precise turning angles encountered in sport-specific movements.24, 25, 26, 27 The absence of angle-dependent effects in our dataset may reflect generalized neuromuscular adaptations that are not tied to task-specific kinematics.170 Despite the angle-independent response, biomechanical specificity was observed in the directional application of resistance. HR-sprint likely enhance acceleration phase kinetics by increasing horizontal propulsive impulse, as demonstrated in force plate analyses.139,171 This aligns with Newtonian action-reaction principles governing GRF. Conversely, combined vertically and horizontally resisted training may optimize lateral displacement efficiency through inferred pre-activation of stabilizing musculature (e.g., gluteus medius), improving coronal plane control during COD tasks.172 This mechanism is biomechanically plausible based on electromyography evidence in similar tasks.173 These observations support the force vector theory, highlighting the importance of aligning resisted direction with sport-specific movement dynamics to maximize transfer efficacy.174 Unlike previous hypotheses that advocate for angle-based programs (e.g., 45° vs. 180°),38,132,175 the current findings highlight that R-MT yields comparable improvements in COD adaptation, irrespective of turn angles. This indicates that practitioners should prioritize resisted training modalities that replicate sport-specific force vectors rather than focusing on angle-centric protocols. The resisted training approach may enhance adaptability across various game contexts, where COD tasks naturally differ in complexity and angular requirements. By integrating biomechanically targeted resistance with neuromuscular adaptations, training programs can balance both specific and functional improvements, essential for optimizing athletic performance in multidirectional sports.

Practical implications

The hierarchical efficacy of R-MT modalities reshapes evidence-based approaches to optimizing COD speed. Vertically resisted strategies, particularly for plyometric training with weighted vests, demonstrate superior transfer to COD tasks compared to horizontal or unresisted modalities. Conversely, horizontally resisted protocols show limited multiplanar transfer due to force vector specificity, particularly during acceleration phases in sprinting.139,176 Notably, R-MT-induced COD adaptations differ from traditional angle-centric programs, advocating for sport-specific vector alignment instead. For example, soccer players who require rapid deceleration and reacceleration transitions may prioritize vertical loading to optimize eccentric–concentric coupling, while rugby linemen might use horizontal resistance to enhance drive-phase kinetics.132 Practitioners should use resisted training protocols involving plyometric jumps loaded at approximately 8% of body mass and sled towing sprints with 10%–20% velocity loss. These sessions should be performed twice weekly as foundational conditioning, balancing neuromuscular overload with sport specificity.31,114,136 For young athletes, R-MT should be organized around a progressive training principle implemented within 4–6-week mesocycles. Initial training is best limited to low external resistance, below 10% of body mass, to allow consolidation of COD mechanics under conditions that do not compromise postural control. After the athlete demonstrates consistent trunk stability and movement quality, intensity may be increased ∼2%–5% of body mass, or movement velocity may be emphasized. Furthermore, combined vertical and horizontal resistance models should be implemented with caution due to conflicting motor unit recruitment patterns across movement planes. While potentially useful as transitional tools within periodization frameworks, their long-term use in programming may compromise kinetic chain synchronization.164

Emerging research priorities require urgent attention to refine R-MT prescriptions. The underrepresentation of female participants (9.4%) hinders understanding of sex-specific adaptations, especially estrogen-mediated collagen remodeling effects on tendon stiffness—a gap that requires investigation through menstrual cycle-stratified trials.177 Standardizing resisted dosing is equally important, as current variability in loading parameters (e.g., percentage-based vs. velocity-based methods) hinders cross-study synthesis. A consensus framework linking intensity to kinetic benchmarks, rather than percentages, would improve prescriptive accuracy.178,179 Additionally, methodological advancements are crucial to refining our understanding of duration and mechanisms. Longitudinal designs tracking neural and architectural adaptations over competitive seasons could optimize periodization strategies. Meanwhile, integrated biomechanical analyses combining 3D motion capture with force plate technology could precisely clarify joint power sequencing during multiplanar deceleration.111,146 While electromyography may offer supplementary neuromuscular insights, it is subject to criticism in the context of rapid movements. Volume-equated comparisons of resistance vectors, as shown in controlled trials by Carlos-Vivas et al.102 and de Hoyo et al.103 should isolate velocity-specific adaptations from workload confounders. Practical alternatives to velocity measures, such as cluster sets or rest redistribution strategies, need validation for horizontal resisted modalities, where velocity decay patterns differ from vertical loading paradigms.180 Addressing these priorities above may help the field to optimally transition from empirical tradition to evidence-based prescriptions, ensuring R-MT methodologies evolve in line with research advances. This evolution will empower practitioners to design interventions tailored not only to sport-specific demands but also to individual athletes’ kinetic chain efficiencies and competition schedules, thereby redefining the role of resisted training in COD development.

Limitations and future research

The current findings must be tempered by methodological constraints. Firstly, the reliance on timing-based COD assessments (e.g., T-test and Illinois test) limited biomechanical interpretation and constrained insight into joint kinematics and neuromuscular activation patterns. Secondly, a limitation arises from the NMA framework, which prioritized R-MT as the experiment node. While this approach facilitated direct comparisons between R-MT modalities, it necessitated the classification of physical training methods (e.g., unresisted plyometric plus strength training) as comparators, despite their established roles as active interventions in COD development. Sparse data networks introduced uncertainty in the Bayesian NMA, leading to wider credible intervals and diminished rank probability discrimination.85 Thirdly, female underrepresentation precluded sex-specific analyses, despite estrogen’s known influence on tendon remodeling.177 Furthermore, this analysis represents an exploratory attempt to examine the moderator effect of COD angle on training adaptation, with no significant effects observed. This imbalance limits our ability to isolate the effects of angle-specific training from general neuromuscular adaptations. Methodologically, variability in R-MT parameters, including differences in load quantification and vector orientation (vertical vs. horizontal resistance), introduced heterogeneity that may obscure dose–response relationships.

In terms of future directions, further mechanistic investigations are warranted, particularly those utilizing three-dimensional motion capture and electromyography to examine the effects of R-MT on joint power sequencing and eccentric-concentric coupling during the deceleration phases of COD. Furthermore, sex-specific trials should explore the influence of hormonal modulation on R-MT efficacy, with menstrual cycle-stratified trials offering insight into how estrogen may affect tendon stiffness and SSC efficiency in female athletes. Further research is needed (particularly high-quality, large-scale RCTs) to investigate the long-term benefits of resisted training on other athletic performance and to explore moderating factors (number of directional changes, total distance, and approach speed) that may influence the outcomes. Regarding the relationship between turning angle and training adaptations, future research should prioritize the explicit specification and documentation of COD angles in both training and testing protocols. Furthermore, embedding R-MT within periodization models to optimize acute and chronic workload ratios could bridge laboratory evidence to sport-specific programming during the competitive season. Addressing these gaps will refine the theoretical underpinnings and practical utility of R-MT in COD development.

Conclusion

This study revealed that R-MT induces small, significant overall improvements in COD speed, with greater gains observed compared to active controls (e.g., unresisted sprint and plyometric training, strength training, specific training). Among the various R-MT modalities, vertically resisted training, particularly VR-plyo and VR-sprint, demonstrates superior effect over horizontally resisted training or unresisted training. Of note, meta-regression analysis revealed that COD angles do not modulate training adaptation, suggesting that R-MT induces generalized neuromuscular adaptations. This highlights the need for sport-specific training programs that integrate resisted movements to optimize athletic performance in COD tasks. Therefore, R-MT represents a specific and functional training approach, designed to optimize neuromuscular adaptations that directly enhance performance in multidirectional athletic movements. From a practical standpoint, recreationally active individuals exhibit strong training responses to rather general training stimuli, while high-performance athletes need specific stimuli to elicit further adaptive gains. More specifically, highly trained and elite athletes require greater force-vector specificity to achieve continued gains. For these athletes, R-MT may go beyond general movement patterns, focusing instead on precise kinetic vectors to address adaptive stagnation. This approach is also in accordance with the law of diminishing returns. Future research should prioritize cohort stratification based on established classification frameworks to refine these training and performance caliber-specific prescriptions. Furthermore, these findings offer significant translational potential for coaches and practitioners aiming to enhance performance in diverse sporting populations and Olympic elite athletes. The certainty of evidence was moderate for primary comparisons (R-MT vs. active control) and for most network estimates. However, comparisons involving smaller subgroups and indirect comparisons (e.g., R-MT vs. different training exercises) demonstrated low certainty, primarily due to imprecision and limited sample sizes. This suggests that further studies with stronger methodologies (e.g., larger samples) are necessary to achieve a more robust conclusion regarding the effect of R-MT on COD speed.
