Performance of Ambulatory Electrocardiographic Data for Prediction of Stroke and Heart Failure Events
Authors: Hannah T. Schwennesen, Zhen Li, Bradley G. Hammill, Amy G. Clark, Sean Pokorney, Evangelos Hytopoulos, Mintu P. Turakhia, Justin Cambra, Jonathan P. Piccini
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite clear associations between arrhythmia burden and cardiovascular risk, clinical risk scores that predict cardiovascular events do not incorporate individual-level arrhythmia characteristics from long-term continuous monitoring (LTCM).ObjectivesThis study evaluated the performance of risk models that use data from LTCM and patient claims for prediction of heart failure (HF) and ischemic stroke.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed features extracted from up to 14 days of LTCM electrocardiogram (ECG) data linked to patient-level claims data for 320,974 Medicare beneficiaries who underwent ZioXT ambulatory monitoring. We created predictive models for HF hospitalization, stroke hospitalization, and new-onset HF within 1 year using LASSO Cox regression for variable selection among ambulatory ECG variables and components of the CHA2DS2-VASc score.ResultsA model that included components of the CHA2DS2-VASc and all ambulatory ECG variables had greater discrimination for HF hospitalization (C-statistic 0.85, 95% CI: 0.84-0.86) than the CHA2DS2-VASc (C-statistic 0.73, 95% CI: 0.72-0.74), but performed similarly to the CHA2DS2-VASc for prediction of stroke hospitalization (C-statistic 0.75 [95% CI: 0.74-0.77] vs 0.71 [95% CI: 0.70-0.72], respectively). Atrial fibrillation was associated with greater risk in the most predictive models (HF hospitalization, HR: 1.53 [95% CI: 1.35-1.72]; stroke hospitalization, HR: 1.58 [95% CI: 1.30-1.93]), and premature ventricular couplets were associated with greater risk of HF hospitalization (HR: 1.54, 95% CI: 1.43-1.65).ConclusionsThe CHA2DS2-VASc performed modestly for prediction of stroke and HF events; predictive ability improved significantly with addition of LTCM ECG covariates. The presence of atrial fibrillation and ventricular ectopy on 14-day LTCM were strongly associated with HF events.

Risk stratification is an important part of clinical care for individual patients and an important tool for optimizing population health. Thromboembolic risk prediction via the CHA2DS2-VASc score is used to guide oral anticoagulant use in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF),1 and multiple studies have shown that the CHA2DS2-VASc can predict risk of stroke, cardiovascular hospitalization, and death in patients without AF.2,3 The CHA2DS2-VASc has also been demonstrated to predict the risk of cardiovascular hospitalization and death in persons with heart failure (HF).4 Patients at risk of HF and those with HF are at particularly elevated risk of arrhythmias and adverse cardiovascular events.5,6

Arrhythmia data gathered via ambulatory electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring could provide insight into a patient’s risk of adverse cardiovascular events.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 However, despite the significant association between arrhythmia burden and risk of stroke and HF, heart rhythm data are not currently being used for risk stratification, and physicians are instead utilizing information from static 12-lead ECGs. More accurate risk prediction of HF and stroke events could allow for preventative interventions that may reduce the morbidity, mortality, and health care expenditure associated with HF and stroke.13,14 Therefore, this study aimed to develop and compare risk models to predict HF and stroke using demographic, clinical, and heart rhythm data from ambulatory ECG monitoring in older patients. We hypothesized that information from ambulatory monitoring would enhance the predictive ability of the CHA2DS2-VASc score for stroke and HF events.

Methods

Data sources

Data from ambulatory long-term continuous monitoring (ZioXT, iRhythm Technologies, Inc) from 2014 to 2020 were linked to fee-for-service (FFS) Medicare claims from 2013 to 2021 accessed through the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services Virtual Research Data Center using Medicare Beneficiary Identifiers and Social Security Numbers. Medicare claims included inpatient, outpatient, and carrier claims, Part D prescription drug claims, and the corresponding Master Beneficiary Summary Files (MBSF). The inpatient files contain institutional claims for hospital inpatient services. The outpatient files contain institutional claims for hospital outpatient services. The carrier files contain noninstitutional provider claims for services rendered in any setting. Each of these files includes dates of service, diagnosis codes, procedure codes, and other institution- or physician-specific information. The MBSF contains information on demographics, birth and death dates, and program eligibility and enrollment. The Duke University Health System Institutional Review Board reviewed and declared this project exempt from Institutional Review Board review with waivers of HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) authorization and informed consent.

Analytic timeline

The Zioambulatory XT monitoring patch recording end date was defined as the index date. The follow-up time for outcomes was 1 year after the index date. Comorbid conditions and prior history were assessed during the 12 months prior to the index date.

Study sample

We included patients who: 1) had ZioXT ambulatory monitoring from 2014 to 2020 and were successfully linked to Medicare claims; 2) had continuous FFS Medicare enrollment during the 12 months prior to the index date; and 3) had at least 90 days of Part D eligibility prior to the index date. If multiple XT ambulatory monitor records were linked for a single patient, we used the earliest record for the analysis.

Patients were excluded if they had evidence of sustained ventricular tachycardia from XT monitoring or based on ICD-CM (International Classification of Diseases-10th Revision-Clinical Modification) codes found in Medicare claims (ICD-9 427.1 or ICD-10 147.2) as patients with ventricular arrhythmias are a unique patient population with a particularly increased risk of arrhythmic death. Similarly, we excluded patients with known ventricular fibrillation, ventricular flutter, or cardiac arrest in the prior year. For the purpose of this analysis, we also excluded those who had an established history of treatment for arrhythmias: ablation of AF, left atrial appendage occlusion, or those who had a prescription fill of oral anticoagulants, antiarrhythmic drugs, or digoxin in the prior 90 days and had pill coverage at index date.

Study variables

Information on age, sex, and race were derived from the MBSF. We searched inpatient, outpatient, and carrier claims between the index date and 1 year prior for comorbid conditions and to derive the CHA2DS2-VASc scores based on validated coding algorithms.15,16

The ZioXT ambulatory monitoring data contains patient-level patch recording end date and records containing summary atrial arrhythmia measures derived from device monitoring. The core variables from the ZioXT ambulatory monitoring database included AF burden (measured as the proportion of analyzable time spent in AF), longest AF episode duration (in seconds), total AF duration (in seconds), single premature atrial complex (PAC) burden, PAC couplet burden, and PAC triplet burden. The expanded arrhythmia variables from the XT ambulatory monitoring database were AF density as defined by Charitos et al17 (reported using the following quantiles: 5%, 25%, 50%, 75%, 95%), AF burden analyzed per time of day (08:00-22:00, 22:00-02:00, and 02:00-08:00), single premature ventricular complex (PVC) burden, PVC couplet burden, PVC triplet burden, supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) episode count, longest SVT episode duration (in seconds), longest SVT episode (in beats), AF episode count, overall minimum heat rate, overall average heart rate, overall maximum heart rate, fraction of sinus time spent in sinus tachycardia, fraction of sinus time spent in sinus bradycardia, and a binary variable of confirmed patients with AF reporting any AF symptoms during monitoring. We created binary indicators to manage zero-inflated and skewed continuous measures such as PAC singlet burden, PAC couplet burden, PAC triplet burden, PVC singlet burden, PVC couplet burden, PVC triplet burden, AF density quantiles, AF burden by time of day, any AF, any ventricular tachycardia, and any SVT.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of interest for our analysis were HF hospitalization and stroke hospitalization. HF hospitalization was defined as a primary diagnosis of HF (ICD-9-CM 402.x1, 404.x1, 404.x3 or 428.∗ or ICD-10-CM I11.0, I13.0, I13.2 or I50.∗) on an inpatient Medicare claim. Stroke hospitalization was defined as a primary diagnosis of ischemic stroke (ICD-9-CM 433.x1, 434.x1 or ICD-10-CM I63.∗) on an inpatient claim. We performed an additional analysis with new-onset HF as the outcome of interest, defined as an HF diagnosis in any position on a single inpatient claim or on 2 or more carrier or outpatient claims within 12 months. We selected the appropriate subset cohorts among patients with no HF diagnosis in 12 months prior to the index date. The date of the incident diagnosis was defined as the earlier of: 1) the discharge date of the earliest inpatient diagnosis; or 2) the thru date of the second outpatient or carrier diagnosis.

We searched for all outcomes in the 1 year following the index date. We censored data for patients at the earliest of: 1) 1 year after the index date; 2) the end of claims data availability (12/31/2021); 3) the date when enrollment in FFS Medicare ended; and 4) their death date.

Statistical analysis

We described baseline characteristics of the study sample using frequencies with percentages for categorical variables and mean ± SD or median (IQR) for continuous variables. We randomly split our data set into a 70% training data set and 30% test data set. In the training data set, we calculated 1-year overall event rates for HF hospitalization, new-onset HF, and stroke hospitalization outcomes.

We derived 8 models for each of the 3 outcomes, which differed by the variables initially considered or ultimately included: 1) CHA2DS2-VASc score (composite score calculated from patient characteristics that include the presence of congestive HF, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, prior stroke, transient ischemic attack or thromboembolism, and vascular disease, the patient’s age, and the patient’s sex category)18 alone; 2) AF burden; 3) all ECG covariates; 4) CHA2DS2-VASc and AF burden; 5) CHA2DS2-VASc and all ECG covariates; 6) individual components of CHA2DS2-VASc; 7) individual components of CHA2DS2-VASc and AF burden; and 8) individual components of CHA2DS2-VASc and all ECG covariates. The CHA2DS2-VASc score was used as the clinical risk prediction score based on prior work demonstrating its effectiveness for predicting risk of ischemic stroke, cardiovascular hospitalization, and death in diverse patient populations with and without AF, including persons with HF.2, 3, 4,19,20

Except for models 1 and 2 described above, we used LASSO Cox regression for variable selection. We built our LASSO Cox models on the training set and evaluated them using our testing set. For prespecified one-variable models, we ran basic Cox models on the training set. Lasso cox models were implemented in R package Glmnet. We selected the tuning parameter by 10-fold cross-validation, using the R function cv.glmnet and the 1-standard-error rule. We reviewed the variables selected from each of the LASSO models and tested for multicollinearity using variance inflation factor, then removed the covariates from the model if the variance inflation factor was >10.

All validation measures (Harrell c-statistic,21 calibration plots) were calculated in each respective testing data set. The calibration plots graphed the observed cumulative incidence rate vs the model predicted rate by decile of the predicted values. The best model was selected for each outcome based on its Harrell’s C-statistic and its calibration plot. We used Cox proportional hazards models to examine univariate and multivariable associations of covariates selected by the best model and each outcome. We plotted the time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curves at 365 days after index date for the best model of each outcome.

For all analyses, a 2-tailed alpha level of 0.05 was set a priori to establish statistical significance, and 95% CI were reported. A 95% CI was obtained from the standard error on either side of the estimated Harrell’s c-statistic generated by the proc phreg procedure in SAS. All analyses utilized SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Incidence of events

Of the 224,682 patients in the training data set, 22.7% (n = 49,637) were hospitalized for any cause within 1 year after the index date, with 1.8% (n = 4,021) hospitalized for HF and 1.0% (n = 2,128) hospitalized for stroke. 3.7% of patients (n = 8,075) died within 1 year of the index date. Of the 191,405 without a diagnosis of HF in the 12 months prior to the index date, 5.8% (n = 10,854) developed a diagnosis of HF within 1 year.

Predicting HF hospitalization

Six of 8 models showed at least moderate predictive ability for HF hospitalization within 1 year, with C-statistics ranging from 0.71 to 0.85 (Table 3). AF burden alone was the only model with minimal predictive ability (C-statistic 0.59, 95% CI: 0.58-0.60). The CHA2DS2-VASc risk score had modest predictive ability (C-statistic 0.73, 95% CI: 0.72-0.74). The models that demonstrated the greatest predictive ability were the components of the CHA2DS2-VASc (C-statistic 0.82, 95% CI: 0.81-0.83), the components of the CHA2DS2-VASc and AF burden (C-statistic 0.84, 95% CI: 0.83-0.85), and the components of the CHA2DS2-VASc and all ECG covariates (C-statistic 0.85, 95% CI: 0.84-0.86) (Central Illustration); all 3 models showed good calibration (Supplemental Figure 1). Predicting stroke

Six of 8 models had moderate predictive ability for stroke hospitalization within 1 year, with C-statistics from 0.71 to 0.75 (Table 3). Models that included AF burden alone and the combination of all ECG variables had minimal predictive ability (C-statistic 0.53, 95% CI: 0.52-0.54; C-statistic 0.53, 95% CI: 0.52-0.54, respectively). Models with the greatest predictive ability were the components of the CHA2DS2-VASc (C-statistic 0.75, 95% CI: 0.74-0.76), the components of the CHA2DS2-VASc and AF burden (C-statistic 0.75, 95% CI: 0.74-0.77), and the components of the CHA2DS2-VASc and all ECG covariates (C-statistic 0.75, 95% CI: 0.74-0.77); all 3 models showed good calibration (Supplemental Figure 2).

Figure 1B and Supplemental Table 2 show the predictive ability of variables in the model with the greatest discrimination (the components of the CHA2DS2-VASc and all ECG covariates). Among clinical variables, diagnoses of stroke/transient ischemic attack (HR: 4.17; 95% CI: 3.82-4.55; P < 0.001) and hypertension (HR: 1.5; 95% CI: 1.32-1.76; P < 0.001) were most associated with stroke hospitalization. Among ECG variables, the presence of AF (HR: 1.58; 95% CI: 1.30-1.93; P < 0.001) was most associated with stroke hospitalization.

Predicting new-onset HF

We performed an additional sensitivity analysis to assess the validity of the models for predicting new-onset HF. All models had modest to moderate predictive ability for hospitalization for new-onset HF, with C-statistics ranging from 0.56 to 0.70 (Table 3). The CHA2DS2-VASc risk score had modest predictive ability for new-onset HF (C-statistic 0.61, 95% CI: 0.60-0.62). A model that included the components of the CHA2DS2-VASc risk score and all ECG covariates exhibited the greatest discrimination (C-statistic 0.70, 95% CI: 0.69-0.71) and good calibration (Supplemental Figure 3). The predictive ability of the model that included the components of the CHA2DS2-VASc and all ECG covariates was the same after the removal of multicollinear variables from the model (C-statistic 0.70, 95% CI: 0.69-0.71). Figure 1C and Supplemental Table 3 detail the variables in the model with the greatest discrimination for new-onset HF (the components of the CHA2DS2-VASc and all ECG covariates after removal of multicollinear variables). Predictive ECG covariates included the presence of AF (HR: 1.33; 95% CI: 1.17-1.51; P < 0.001) and of PVCs (for PVC singlets, HR: 1.30; 95% CI: 1.21-1.40; P < 0.001). The presence of SVT was associated with lower risk of new-onset HF (HR: 0.87; 95% CI: 0.81-0.92; P < 0.001). Supraventricular singlets were associated with lower risk of new-onset HF (HR: 0.72; 95% CI: 0.63-0.83; P < 0.001), while supraventricular couplets and triplets were associated with greater risk (HR: 1.17, 95% CI: 1.10-1.23, P < 0.001; HR: 1.31, 95% CI: 1.25-1.38, P < 0.001, respectively).

Discussion

In this nationwide analysis of patients undergoing ambulatory cardiac monitoring, we found that a novel risk score combining 14-day ECG features with the comorbidities of the CHA2DS2-VASc score can predict risk of hospitalization for HF and new-onset HF in a sample at high risk for cardiovascular events. Ventricular ectopy and AF were highly associated with greater risk of hospitalization for HF and new-onset HF in this model. In a sample with an AF prevalence of 10%, most markers of AF burden did not have strong predictive value for any outcome in the most successful models. In contrast to the prediction of HF, the addition of 14-day ECG features to a model with the comorbidities of the CHA2DS2-VASc score did not improve prediction for stroke.

Clinical risk scores have long been validated for use in guiding the management of anticoagulation for patients with AF to prevent thromboembolism. The CHA2DS2-VASc score has moderate predictive ability for risk of stroke onset in patients with AF.18 Prior work has demonstrated that the addition of AF burden data to the CHA2DS2-VASc score further enhances its predictive ability for stroke and may be useful for risk stratification of patients in intermediate-risk categories.22,23 More recently, the CHA2DS2-VASc has also been shown to predict stroke risk in patients without evidence of AF.2,24 In our sample of older patients undergoing ambulatory cardiac monitoring, a model that included ECG variables and the components of the CHA2DS2-VASc had similar predictive ability for stroke as the CHA2DS2-VASc score alone. Much of the explanatory value of the model lies in patient comorbidities rather than ECG variables, which may be explained by the significant association between increasing age and the presence of arrhythmias.25 It is not surprising that the addition of ECG variables did not enhance the predictive ability of the CHA2DS2-VASc for stroke, as only a small minority of patients in the study sample experienced any amount of AF. Thus, despite prior evidence showing increased risk of stroke with greater burden of AF,7,8,26 markers of AF burden did not add predictive value to the model. Future work should focus on identifying markers of AF burden with the strongest predictive value for stroke among patients with pre-existing AF.

Both atrial and ventricular arrhythmias increase the risk of development of HF and portend poor outcomes in patients with pre-existing HF. Over one-half of patients with new-onset of HF have preceding AF, and HF is the most frequent complication of AF; patients with AF have greater rates of hospitalization for HF than for stroke.5,27,28 The presence of AF increases risk for HF hospitalization and mortality in patients with asymptomatic left ventricular dysfunction, HF with reduced ejection fraction, and HF with preserved ejection fraction, even after adjustment for other risk factors.6,29,30 Greater burden of PVCs is also associated with greater incidence of and mortality from HF.31,32 Despite these strong associations, few risk prediction models have been developed to stratify patients at risk of adverse HF outcomes utilizing extended arrhythmia data. Clinicians often rely on 12-lead ECGs to ascertain heart rhythm data for patients at high risk of adverse cardiovascular events despite the fact that extended ECG monitoring is necessary to accurately quantify burden of AF and ventricular ectopy.33,34 We have developed a model that incorporates the comorbidities included in the CHA2DS2-VASc risk score and heart rhythm characteristics from 14 days ambulatory rhythm monitors to accurately predict risk of new-onset HF and hospitalization for HF in older patients. Such models have greater predictive ability for HF events when compared to the CHA2DS2-VASc risk score alone, which has previously been shown to accurately predict risk for HF hospitalization and mortality in HF patients with and without AF.2,3,35 When evaluating patient risk, clinicians often use clinical data or risk calculators that are based upon traditional risk factors. Additionally, they will often perform ambulatory testing based upon patient-reported symptoms. Integration of these data for risk stratification has the potential to prompt interventions to reduce the morbidity and mortality associated with HF progression.36 Such risk stratification can also guide risk factor modification in patients at risk of developing AF, including those with stage 1 and stage 2 AF as per the most recent AF guidelines.1

In the model with greatest predictive ability for the development of new-onset HF and hospitalization for HF (the model that included the components of the CHA2DS2-VASc and all ECG covariates), the presence of ventricular ectopy was strongly associated with both outcomes, consistent with prior data.37 Supraventricular triplets were associated with greater risk of new-onset HF. Frequent supraventricular ectopy has been associated with increased risk of stroke, HF, and death,12,38 which may explain greater risk with longer runs of atrial ectopy seen in our study. Extended, continuous monitoring allows for more accurate assessment of ectopy burden than 12-lead ECG or short-term monitoring39 and thus improves the value of risk stratification models. The presence of AF was highly associated with greater risk of new-onset HF and HF hospitalization. However, markers of AF burden (including proportion of time in AF, longest AF episode duration, AF density, and AF timing) did not have significant predictive value within the model, in stark contrast to prior work that has demonstrated greater risk of HF and HF hospitalization with greater burden of AF.10,11 This finding is likely explained by the overall low prevalence of AF in the study sample, limiting the model’s ability to use AF burden in its risk calculation. Measures of atrial ectopy may thus have greater sensitivity and value in risk stratification across broader patient populations.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these data. First, all clinical data (including diagnoses and outcomes) was derived from administrative claims information which is primarily intended for billing justification and therefore can be inconsistent and incomplete. The definition of new-onset HF was most affected by inconsistencies in documentation, as the timing of onset was determined by the first appearance of HF as a diagnosis code. In order to minimize misclassification of new-onset HF, we required that the diagnosis was linked to at least 1 inpatient claim or 2 or more outpatient claims. Additionally, second, conclusions drawn from this study may not be applicable to younger patients undergoing ambulatory cardiac monitoring, as the sample consisted solely of patients over the age of 65. The included sample also had a high rate of comorbid conditions and thus event rates may be higher than those seen in the general population.

Conclusions

A risk prediction model that incorporates both clinical factors and ambulatory ECG data accurately predicts the risk of new-onset HF and hospitalization for HF in older patients undergoing ambulatory cardiac monitoring.
