Molecular Genetic and Clinical Characteristics of Fetuses With Chromosome 16 Short‐Arm Microdeletions/Microduplications
Authors: Meiying Cai, Na Lin, Nan Guo, Hailong Huang, Xiangqun Fan, Meimei Fu, Min Zhang, Liangpu Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe short arm of chromosome 16 is highly susceptible to homologous recombination through nonallelic genes. This results in microdeletions/microduplications that can lead to neurodevelopmental disorders. However, incomplete penetrance and phenotypic diversity after birth exacerbate the uncertainty in prenatal genetic counseling.MethodsA total of 24,000 cases with prenatal diagnoses were retrospectively analyzed. Chromosome microarray analysis (CMA) was performed on 17,000 cases, of which 81 (0.48%) had chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications.ResultsOf the 81 fetuses with chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications, 36 and 28 had 16p11.2 and 16p13.11 microdeletions/microduplications, respectively. Ten, four, and three fetuses had 16p12.2, 16p13.12p13.11, and 16p13.12p1.3 microdeletions, respectively. Among the 36 fetuses with 16p11.2 microdeletions/microduplications, 33 had abnormal intrauterine ultrasound phenotypes, the most common being skeletal system abnormalities. Among the 28 fetuses with 16p13.11 microdeletions/microduplications, 19 had abnormal intrauterine ultrasound phenotypes, including 15 with abnormal ultrasonic soft markers. Among the 10 fetuses with the 16p12.2 microdeletions, six had abnormal ultrasound findings, and four had skeletal system abnormalities. After genetic counseling, 44 patients were selected and tested for family verification, of which 22 were de novo, while 22 were inherited from phenotypically normal parents. Among the 47 live births, 39 had no abnormalities.ConclusionAll fetuses with the 16p13.11 microdeletions/microduplications, and 16p12.2, 16p13.12p13.11, and 16p13.12p1.3 microdeletions were healthy after birth. Hence, chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications should not be the sole basis for abandoning pregnancy, and clinicians should consider prenatal diagnostic data to maximize diagnostic accuracy.

Introduction

Approximately 5% of the sequences in the human genome are low‐copy repeats (LCRs) which are grouped together by nonallelic homologous recombination (NAHR), resulting in abnormal genome rearrangement. Human chromosome 16 is a small mesocentric chromosome belonging to group E that spans 90.4 Mb in length. Nearly 10% of its sequence consists of repeats, which predisposes it to rearrangements via frequent NAHRs, thereby resulting in genomic instability [1]. The microdeletion/microduplication of 16p11.2 and 16p13.11 are examples of such rearrangements [2]. Chromosome microarray analysis (CMA) is widely used in the prenatal diagnoses of chromosomal anomalies and has resulted in an increase in the number of people being detected with microdeletions/microduplications in the short arm of chromosome 16 [3]. The phenotypic features associated with this chromosomal abnormality have been reported in related cases in adults and children. However, the rarity of these cases, difficulty in identifying prenatal phenotypes, incomplete penetrance, and phenotypic diversity makes prenatal counseling very challenging. For example, it has been reported that patients with 16p11.2 microdeletion have obvious phenotypic heterogeneity, and individuals with the same genotype may have normal or abnormal phenotypes with varying penetrance [4, 5]. Further clinical studies are needed on the association between chromosome 16 microdeletions/microduplications and poor fetal prognosis, as well as the effect of couples' CMA verification on the CMA outcomes.

Prenatal studies of short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications for chromosome 16 are limited, and patients lack a specific phenotype before birth [5, 6, 7, 8]. In this study, CMA was applied to the genetic testing data obtained from 17,000 fetuses with prenatal diagnoses, and fetuses with chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications were identified. The relevant clinical characteristics and postpartum outcomes were retrospectively analyzed according to the type of chromosomal abnormality, and neonatal follow‐ups were conducted.

Materials and Methods

Patient Data

The data for 24,000 cases of interventional prenatal diagnosis at Fujian Maternal and Child Health Hospital were retrospectively collected from 1/7/2016 to 1/3/2023. Of these, 17,000 cases were further evaluated using CMA. The gestational age of the pregnant women ranged from 16+1 to 37+6 weeks, while their ages ranged from 18 to 49 years. The amniotic fluid (20–35 mL) was extracted at 16–24 weeks of pregnancy using transabdominal ultrasound‐guided amniocentesis. The umbilical cord blood (1.0–2.5 mL) was extracted at > 24 weeks of pregnancy via umbilical cord blood puncture. The main indicators of interventional prenatal diagnosis included: (1) advanced age (expected delivery age ≥ 35 years); (2) fetal intrauterine abnormalities indicated by ultrasonography; (3) serological screenings for women with high‐risk pregnancies; (4) a history of adverse fertility with chromosome microdeletion/microduplication syndrome; (5) abnormal noninvasive prenatal testing (NIPT); (6) presence of an abnormal gene; and (7) exhibiting chromosome balanced translocation. The criteria for inclusion in this study were CMA results showing microdeletions or microduplications of chromosome 16 in fetuses. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Maternal and Child Health Hospital (Approval No. 2020KY098). Written informed consent was obtained from the participants of this study.

Chromosome Microarray Analysis (CMA)

Genomic DNA was extracted from the amniotic fluid and umbilical cord blood samples using the QIAGEN kit (Ningbo Bosheng Medical Technology) according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNA amplification, tagging and hybridization to gene chips were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions of Affymetrix (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and CytoScan TMHD chip (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA), and data analysis was performed using Chromosome Analysis Suite (ChAS 2.0; Affymetrix). For comparison and analysis of the fragments with a CNV (copy number variation) ≥ 100 Kb, the following databases were utilized: Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM), Database of Chromosomal Imbalance and Phenotype in Humans using Ensemble Resources (DECIPHER), the Database of Genomic Variants (DGV), the University of California Santa Cruz (University of California Santa Cruz; UCSC) Genome Browser, Gene Reviews, and PubMed. According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines [9], CNVs are classified as follows: pathogenic; a variant of uncertain significance; benign; likely pathogenic; or likely benign.

Data Collection and Follow‐Up

All prenatal diagnoses and follow‐up results were recorded at Fujian Maternal and Child Health Hospital's Prenatal Diagnosis Center. Retrospective analysis was conducted on all patients whose prenatal diagnosis results indicated a chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletion/microduplication. All patients were followed up by telephone, and the electronic medical record system was searched for the presence of ultrasonographic structural abnormalities, pregnancy complications, delivery mode, birth weight, and health status of newborns.

Results

This study identified 24,000 fetuses with an interventional prenatal diagnosis, and 17,000 (70.8%) of these fetuses were further analyzed using CMA. Chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications were identified by the CMA in 81 fetuses (0.48%). This included 36 (44.4%) and 28 (34.6%) microdeletions/microduplications in 16p11.2 and 16p13.11, respectively, and 10 (12.3%), 4 (4.9%), and 3 (3.7%) microdeletions in 16p12.2, 16p13.12p13.11, and 16p13.12p1.3, respectively. After genetic counseling, 44 (54.3%) patients underwent family verification, while 37 (45.7%) patients refused to do so. Of the 44 patients that underwent verification, 22 were identified as de novo cases and 22 were inherited from phenotypically normal parents. Of the de novo cases, 16 (72.7%) patients chose to terminate their pregnancy, while seven patients with inherited cases also chose the same. In addition, one patient was lost in the follow‐up, while 32 patients chose to terminate their pregnancy. There was one case of stillbirth and 47 cases of live birth. Among the 47 live births, one child had neurodevelopmental delay, while two underwent surgical procedures. One child had esophageal atresia after premature birth and died after giving up treatment. Four children had short statures, but the remaining 39 children showed no abnormalities during a 6‐year follow‐up period.

16p11.2 Microdeletion/Microduplication

This study showed that 36 fetuses had 16p11.2 microdeletions/microduplications, of which 24 had 16p11.2 microdeletions and 12 had 16p11.2 microduplications, involving fragments ranging in size from 222 Kb to 1.04 Mb and containing 9–45 OMIM genes (Figure 1). Of the 36 patients with 16p11.2 microdeletions/microduplications, 11 refused family verification (Table 1). The distribution of indicators for prenatal diagnosis showed that 33 of these fetuses had ultrasound abnormalities, while two were classified as high‐risk in serological screenings, and one was a genomic carrier in pregnant women (Table 1). Of the 33 fetuses with 16p11.2 microdeletions/microduplications, 18 (54.5%, 18/33) showed structural abnormalities in ultrasound assessment, and 15 (45.5%, 15/33) had abnormal fetal ultrasonic soft markers. The most common ultrasonic structural abnormalities were of the skeletal system (seven fetuses, with spine abnormalities observed in five fetuses), followed by the urinary and cardiovascular system (five fetuses). One fetus was lost during a follow‐up of fetuses with 16p11.2 microdeletions/microduplications, while 35 fetuses were successfully identified. Of these, 20 pregnancies were terminated, while one was stillborn, and 14 were live births. Among the 14 live births, one had neurodevelopmental delay while another had short stature. One child had esophageal atresia after premature birth and died after giving up treatment, while another underwent heart surgery after birth and was considered healthy thereafter. The remaining 10 children were followed up to nearly 6 years of age.

16p13.11 Microdeletion/Microduplication

The results showed that 28 fetuses had 16p13.11 microdeletions/microduplications, of which 9 had 16p13.11 microdeletions and 19 had 16p13.11 microduplications, involving fragments of size 121 Kb to 2.92 Mb and contained 5–45 OMIM genes (Figure 2). Of the 28 fetuses with 16p13.11 microdeletions/microduplications, 17 refused family verification (Table 2). Furthermore, the distribution of the indicators for prenatal diagnosis showed that 19 fetuses had abnormal ultrasounds, and 5 were identified as high‐risk during serological screenings. While three were identified as carriers of the chromosomal balanced translocation from one fetal parent, one was of advanced age (Table 2). Of the 19 cases of 16p13.11 microdeletions/microduplications with fetal intrauterine ultrasound phenotypes, 4 had structural abnormalities and 15 had ultrasonic soft markers (the most common being thickened nuchal translucency). Among the 16p13.11 microdeletions/microduplications that were followed up, six underwent termination of pregnancy, while two had short stature. The remaining 20 fetuses did not show any abnormality.

16p12.2 Microdeletion

The results of this study showed that 10 fetuses had 16p12.2 microdeletions, involving fragments that were approximately 511–994 Kb and containing 3–22 OMIM genes (Figure 3). Of these, six refused family verification (Table 3). The distribution of indicators for prenatal diagnosis showed that six of these fetuses had abnormal ultrasounds, and one was identified as high‐risk during serological screening. While one was of advanced age, another was NIPT positive, and one had an adverse pregnancy history (Table 3). Among the six patients with 16p12.2 microdeletions, four had skeletal abnormalities and two had ultrasonic soft markers. A follow‐up of the fetuses with 16p12.2 microdeletions showed that three pregnancies were terminated while there was one case of short stature. The remaining six fetuses showed no abnormalities.

Discussion

Chromosome microarray analysis (CMA) is now widely applied in prenatal diagnoses [3, 10], and the resulting data show that it can detect an additional 2%–10% of pathogenic microdeletion/microduplication syndromes when compared with traditional chromosome karyotype analysis technologies [11]. In this study, 81 (0.48%, 81/17000) fetuses carrying chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications were detected using prenatal CMA in 17,000 pregnant women. Chromosome 16 is rich in repetitive fragments and has undergone intensive fragment replication during the evolution of primates. Consequently, more than 10% of its short‐arm euchromatin sequence comprises highly complex LCRs. This complex structure has made chromosome 16 a hotspot for alterations in the human genome and has led to many microdeletion syndromes, which may result in the development of neurodevelopmental disorders [12]. The main phenotypes for patients with microdeletion syndromes are developmental delay, intellectual disability, schizophrenia, and autism [1, 2, 13, 14, 15]. However, the cause of heterogeneity in the clinical phenotype remains unclear. Furthermore, the incomplete penetrance and phenotypic diversity after birth can exacerbate the difficulties associated with prenatal genetic counseling. In this study, prenatal examination and postpartum follow‐up were performed on the fetuses with chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications.

The results of the CMA analysis showed that 16p11.2 microdeletions/microduplications in chromosome 16 were the most common. The 16p11.2 microdeletions may reportedly lead to spinal deformities, including scoliosis, syringomyelia, and hemivertebra [16, 17]. Previous studies have shown that TBX6 gene mutations affect the transcriptional activity of the protein and lead to spinal costal dysplasia [18, 19, 20]. In this study, the most common abnormality observed in fetuses with 16p11.2 microdeletions was spinal malformation, followed by urinary system and cardiovascular system abnormalities. Deletion of the SH2B1 gene in the 16p11.2 region leads to urinary system abnormalities [21, 22], and an insufficient dose of the HIRIP3 gene in the 16p11.2 region results in cardiovascular system abnormalities [8]. The 16p11.2 microduplication has been reported to be associated with microcephaly [23]. However, in this study, we did not observe microcephaly in fetuses with 16p11.2 microduplications. Two fetuses with 16p11.2 microduplications had ventricular septal defect. Of the 35 successful follow‐up 16p11.2 microdeletion/microduplication fetuses, 14 were live births, of which four had phenotypic abnormalities. One fetus had a ventricular septal defect and the phenotype after birth also showed cardiac abnormalities. In the second fetus, the phenotype of intrauterine ultrasound was an abnormal kidney; however, the phenotype at the time of premature birth was esophageal atresia, and the fetus died after discontinuing treatment. The third fetus had a choroid plexus cyst, nasal bone dysplasia, and mild mitral regurgitation on intrauterine ultrasonography. The phenotype was short in stature after birth and was followed up until 2 years of age. The diversity of phenotypes after birth in the above three fetuses exacerbates the difficulty of prenatal genetic counseling. Further research will help in accumulating relevant data to provide a stronger foundation for genetic counseling practices.

The 16p13.l1 region is also a dose‐sensitive region [24]. Current studies have shown that the NDE1 gene in 16p13.11 microdeletions is expressed in the brain and influences the development of the cerebral cortex and nerves through interaction with the LIS‐1 protein and fibrin [25]. NDE1 knockout mouse models show microcephaly and structural abnormalities in the cerebral cortex, indicating that the NDE1 gene is involved in the abnormal development of the nervous system [26]. Bakircioglu et al. [27] and Paciorkowski et al. [28] reported that patients with 16p13.11 microdeletions clinically presented with severe microcephaly, agenesis of the corpus callosum, and brain malformation, thereby indicating that the NDE1 gene has a dose‐dependent effect. In this study of fetuses with 16p13.11 microdeletions, the most common feature was thickened nuchal translucency. This study shows that the most common type of abnormality in the intrauterine ultrasound of fetuses with 16p13.11 microdeletions was abnormal ultrasonic soft markers. However, the role of the microduplication of 16p13.11 in the clinical phenotype is uncertain and may be benign [4]. In this study, out of 19 fetuses with 16p13.11 microduplications, 11 fetuses had abnormal ultrasounds (the most common being abnormal ultrasonic soft markers). A follow‐up of the 28 fetuses with 16p13.11 microdeletions/microduplications showed pregnancy termination of six fetuses. One fetus of an intrauterine ultrasound phenotypic pulmonary cystadenomatosis lesion received operative treatment after birth and was reported as normal thereafter, while two fetuses had short statures at approximately 4 and 1 year of age, respectively. The remaining 19 patients were followed up until the age of 5 and showed no abnormalities. Of the three fetuses with the 16p13.12p1.3 microdeletions, two terminated their pregnancies, and the third fetus showed no abnormality. For the four fetuses with 16p13.12p13.11 microdeletions, one terminated their pregnancy, and the remaining three showed no abnormality. In summary, this study found that 16p13.11 microdeletions/microduplications did not have a negative health impact after birth. However, the development of the nervous system may not be discernable due to the young age of the patients, and further long‐term follow‐up is required.

The clinical phenotype of the microdeletions in the 16p12.2 region is highly heterogeneous with variable clinical manifestations, and does not constitute an identifiable syndrome [29]. The 16p12.2 microdeletions include the remote end and the proximal end. In this study, all 10 fetuses with 16p2.2 microdeletions had proximal deletions, and the missing fragments included three genes: UQCRC2, CDR2, and EEF2K. UQCRC2 is a protein that assembles the mitochondrial respiratory chain complex, CDR2 is a tumor neuroantigen associated with cerebellar ataxia. EEF2K is associated with learning and memory, synaptic plasticity, and the short‐term antidepressant effects of ketamine [30, 31]. At present, few fetuses of prenatal diagnosis have been reported in studies investigating the microdeletions in the16p12.2 region. In this study, six of the 10 fetuses with 16p12.2 microdeletions showed abnormal intrauterine ultrasound findings, and four of them showed skeletal abnormalities. To date, there have been no studies on the intrauterine phenotype of fetuses with the 16p12.2 microdeletion as a skeletal abnormality [1]. Further investigation is required to conclude whether the fetal skeletal abnormalities in this study are related to 16p12.2 microdeletions. After being fully informed of the risks, three out of 10 patients with 16p2.2 microdeletions terminated their pregnancies, and the remaining seven chose to continue their pregnancies until term. Seven infants were followed up for 5 years after birth. Except for one infant with short stature, the development of the remaining six infants was normal. In summary, fetuses with 16p13.11 microdeletions were found to be in good health after birth. However, due to differences in the degree of 16p12.2 (proximal) microdeletion recurrence, attention should be paid to the development of the nervous system during individual growth and development.

For the abnormal CMA results, parental verification should be performed if needed to provide a basis for genetic counseling [32]. However, genetic counseling for chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications is complicated due to the incomplete penetrance and phenotypic diversity. If one parent carries this heterozygous mutation and has no abnormal phenotype, it cannot be used to accurately predict if a fetus with a chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletion/microduplication will have an abnormal phenotype. In this study, of the 81 patients with chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications, 37 (45.7%, 37/81) refused family verification, while the remaining 44 (54.3%, 44/81) underwent the procedure. Among the 44 verified patients, 22 were de novo, and 22 were inherited from phenotypically normal parents. Up to 72.7% (16/22) of the 22 de novo patients with chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications chose to terminate the pregnancy, and 7 of the 22 patients with chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications were confirmed to be inherited from phenotypically normal parents. The prenatal diagnosis report for chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications shows that even though the fetus may have no clinical manifestations from the parents, some pregnant women still choose to terminate their pregnancy. Another 15 fetuses with chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications that were inherited from phenotypic normal parents chose to continue pregnancy, and except for four patients of short stature, the remaining 11 patients showed no abnormalities in their most recent follow‐up. Pedigree verification could thus help reduce unnecessary pregnancy terminations. However, in view of the delayed neurological development and other manifestations in children, attention should be paid to ensuring that there are continuous and regular follow‐ups after birth. Relevant data should, thus, continue to be acquired in the future to provide a basis for genetic counseling.

This study had some limitations. First, as it was a retrospective study, not all cases of chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications were verified by couples. Hence, the selection of cases was limited, which may cause a data bias. Second, the object of this study was chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications. Among the 80 successful follow‐up cases, 32 chose to terminate their pregnancy, which may have affected the research data. Third, in fetuses with chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications inherited from parents, parents may have abnormal test indicators, but these may be missed due to a lack of typical clinical manifestations. Four, nearly half of the patients did not undergo family verification, which adds to the difficulty of genetic counseling.

The discovery of chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications in the fetus should, thus, not be the sole basis for abandoning pregnancy, and clinicians need to continuously accumulate prenatal diagnostic data to maximize diagnostic accuracy. In addition to the prenatal ultrasound phenotype of fetuses with 16 short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications, emphasis should be placed on family verification and genetic counseling of carrier relatives. The evaluation and treatment of fetuses with chromosome 16 short‐arm microdeletions/microduplications, and their careful screening, are required to ensure effective counseling for prospective parents.
