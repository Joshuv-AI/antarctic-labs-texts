Diffusion models for brain imaging computing: a survey of frameworks and applications
Authors: Yousuf Babiker M. Osman, Aden Hassan Margani Elsanosi, Changhong Jing, Yanyan Shen, Hongzhi Kuai, Shanshan Wang, Michael Kwok-Po Ng, Baiying Lei, Shuqiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Advances in brain imaging have generated unprecedented volumes of high-dimensional data, yet extracting meaningful information from complex, noisy, and incomplete brain imaging data remains a significant challenge. Diffusion models (DMs) have introduced a paradigm shift in this field, surpassing traditional generative approaches. This review systematically examines the theoretical foundations of diffusion models, and their practical applications in eight brain imaging computing tasks: registration, super-resolution, cross-modal reconstruction and synthesis, segmentation, classification, brain network analysis, brain-computer interface (BCI) signals augmentation, and BCI decoding. Additionally, we emphasize obstacles that hinder deployment in practice, including computational scalability and sampling inefficiency, limited generalization under domain shift sensitivity, as well as multimodal integration and alignment constraints, while outlining potential future directions that emphasize the convergence of diffusion models with large-scale foundation models, which hold the potential to advance scalable, reliable, and clinically embedded brain imaging solutions. Throughout this review, we aim to establish a roadmap of progress and translational hurdles to guide emerging research and accelerate collaboration spanning DMs, clinical brain imaging, and engineering disciplines.

Introduction

Brain imaging computing integrates neuroscience, signal processing, and computational intelligence, enabling quantitative exploration of the brain’s structure, dynamics, and connectivity [1]. Over the past decade, advances in multimodal neuroimaging including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [2], functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) [3], functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) [4], magnetoencephalography (MEG) [5], and electroencephalography (EEG) [6]; have generated unprecedented volumes of high-dimensional data capturing both static and dynamic aspects of brain activity [7]. While these modalities have substantially advanced neuroscience and clinical diagnosis, extracting meaningful information from complex, noisy, and incomplete brain data remains a challenge. Traditional statistical models and even deep learning architectures often fail to achieve robust generalization due to data scarcity, acquisition variability, and the intrinsic stochasticity of neural signals [8, 9].

The emergence of diffusion models has introduced a paradigm shift in computational imaging, redefining how high-dimensional brain imaging data can be modeled, reconstructed, and synthesized [10–12]. In brain imaging, diffusion models have surpassed traditional generative approaches like generative adversarial networks (GANs) [13] and variational autoencoders (VAEs) [14], delivering superior mode coverage, sample diversity, and training stability [15]. This performance advantage stems from their foundational probabilistic mechanics: unlike deterministic frameworks, diffusion models learn to invert a gradual noising process, iteratively transforming random noise into structured images. By leveraging these probabilistic diffusion trajectories, they explore the entire data manifold, which yields their unmatched stability and likelihood-based interpretability [16, 17].

Diffusion models have rapidly emerged as transformative tools in brain imaging computing, offering probabilistic, data-consistent representations that capture the intrinsic structure of neural signals with exceptional fidelity [18–20]. By learning to approximate the underlying distributions of biological and anatomical patterns, these models excel across a wide spectrum of brain imaging tasks, including segmentation [19], classification [20], reconstruction [21], super-resolution [22], cross-modality synthesis and translation [23], and the modeling of brain disease networks [24]. Their iterative denoising process enables the restoration of fine-grained cortical and subcortical details while maintaining physiological plausibility, effectively bridging the gap between generative modeling and clinical interpretation [25]. Beyond traditional imaging pipelines, diffusion frameworks are increasingly applied to brain–computer interface decoding and BCI signal augmentation, where they enable the reconstruction and simulation of complex neural representations [26, 27]. Notably, diffusion-based BCI systems have demonstrated the ability to decode and generate visual stimuli, imagined text, and imagined voice patterns, representing brain states as continuous, structured trajectories in latent space rather than as discrete class boundaries [28–31]. This probabilistic representation allows diffusion models to handle the inherent noise and variability of neural data while maintaining robustness under limited training conditions [32–34]. The diverse applications of diffusion models significantly broaden the operational scope of brain imaging computing, improving its flexibility and integration across clinical and computational pipelines. Their demonstrated utility spans medical rehabilitation [35, 36], intelligent human–computer interaction [37–39], and neuroscience research [40–42], underscoring their growing role as a foundational technology for multimodal brain analysis. In short, diffusion modeling offers not merely a computational tool but a theoretical bridge between statistical physics, signal diffusion, and brain data representation, introducing a physically interpretable generative mechanism for the human brain’s high-dimensional information space. The search strategy combined terminology related to diffusion models ("Denoising Diffusion", "Score-based Diffusion", "Latent Diffusion", "Conditional Diffusion") with key concepts in brain imaging computing and brain-computer interfaces ("MRI", "Noninvasive", "EEG", "fMRI", "fNIRS", "Invasive", "ECoG", "sEEG", "LFP"). Publications underwent quality and relevance screening, with exclusion criteria comprising: (1) studies not employing diffusion models for brain imaging computing, or (2) insufficient methodological rigor. The selection process involved initial title/abstract screening followed by full-text evaluation. Review and perspective papers were not part of the primary analysis set; however, they were cited when their discussions provided useful conceptual insights, methodological context, or critical perspectives on emerging diffusion-based neuroimaging research. Diffusion frameworks

Diffusion models employ a probabilistic framework to systematically transform data distributions through forward and reverse processes [43, 44]. During the forward phase, Gaussian noise is incrementally added to the input data via a Markov chain. The reverse phase, conversely, learns to denoise by approximating the original data distribution. This section is dedicated to the foundational concepts of diffusion models, as well as an overview of their most significant variants found in the literature.

Conditional diffusion models (CDMs)

Controlling the generation process in diffusion models is essential for aligning outputs with desired semantic or structural conditions such as class labels, text prompts, or image attributes. Practical applications in brain imaging computing

Diffusion models have emerged as transformative tools in brain imaging and neuroscience, advancing far beyond creative domains to address critical clinical and research needs. They have proven effective in enhancing image quality through registration, super-resolution, enabling cross-modality translation and generating synthetic medical data,, segmenting anatomical structures, classifying neurological disorders, modeling disease progression, BCI signal augmentation, and BCI decoding. Registration

Diffusion models are emerging as a powerful paradigm for brain image registration, where the goal is to achieve accurate spatial alignment across subjects, time points, or imaging modalities. By modeling the registration task as a probabilistic transformation process, diffusion-based frameworks iteratively refine deformation fields from noise to anatomically consistent mappings, enabling robust alignment even under large inter-subject variability or cross-modal appearance differences. A first theme is direct diffusion modeling of deformation fields for unsupervised registration. Kim et al. [56] formulated deformable registration as a conditional diffusion process, where a DDPM-style generator produces deformation fields conditioned on moving–fixed image pairs, coupled with a VoxelMorph-like warping and regularization backbone. Similarly, Wu et al. [60] proposed LDM-Morph for unsupervised deformable medical image registration, where a latent diffusion model is used as a semantic feature extractor and coupled with a dual-stream Transformer-style encoder with latent global cross-attention.

A second theme uses diffusion as a shape-aware atlas and trajectory generator, where the output is not merely alignment but a population or condition-specific anatomical reference. Starck et al. [57] presented Diff-Def, a conditional LDM framework for synthesizing 3D brain MRI atlases that reflect sub-population characteristics, such as age and ventricular volume. Instead of directly generating image intensities, the model produced diffusion-generated deformation fields that warp a general population atlas into condition-specific versions, integrating a morphology-preserving registration module to maintain anatomical plausibility. Relatedly, Zhang et al. [58] proposed a development-driven diffusion model (DDM) for longitudinal prediction of fetal brain MRI, addressing the absence of paired temporal scans in clinical practice. The study employed a conditional and guided diffusion model trained in a latent space to capture population-level developmental patterns across gestational weeks, while introducing individual-specific perceptual feature guidance during inference to preserve subject identity.

Building on these approaches, a third complementary direction leverages diffusion models as priors for registration similarity, rather than as generators. Tursynbek et al. [59] proposed diffusion-guided image registration (DGIR) that utilized features extracted from a pre-trained DDPM to enhance deformable registration performance across challenging multimodal and monomodal scenarios. Instead of retraining the diffusion model, DGIR employed diffusion-derived feature representations as a semantic similarity loss.

These studies mark an early but pivotal shift toward generative registration frameworks driven by diffusion models. Despite their promising performance, the number of studies applying diffusion models to registration remains limited compared with reconstruction and synthesis tasks. This scarcity highlights a critical research opportunity: diffusion-based formulations could unify deformation modeling, similarity learning, and uncertainty estimation within a single probabilistic framework, paving the way for anatomically consistent, interpretable, and cross-modal registration systems in neuroimaging.

Extending this direction, Li et al. [62] developed DiffMSR, a multi-contrast diffusion-driven framework that reconstructs high-resolution MR images from low-resolution inputs using auxiliary contrast guidance. By integrating a DDPM operating in a compact latent space with a prior-guided large-window transformer (PLWformer), DiffMSR efficiently embedded high-frequency anatomical priors while substantially reducing the number of sampling iterations.

Further advancing this anatomically driven paradigm, Chen et al. [63] proposed the edge-guided conditional diffusion model (Eg-Diff), which explicitly integrated edge and high-frequency structural cues into a conditional DDPM framework for multi-contrast MRI. Eg-Diff introduced a high-frequency-injected multi-contrast encoder using learnable Sobel and Laplacian operators and employed adaptive fusion with contrast-weighted gating and balanced attention to preserve fine cortical boundaries. Liu et al. [65] further unified super-resolution and denoising through SRDSNF, a stochastic normalizing flow–based DDPM architecture that models both restoration and enhancement within a single generative process. By employing a pretrained encoder for conditional guidance at each reverse diffusion step, the model preserved anatomical coherence while suppressing noise-induced artifacts. Similarly, Safari et al. [66] proposed Res-SRDiff, an efficient DDPM framework incorporating residual shifting and a Swin Transformer–enhanced U-Net backbone to accelerate MRI SR reconstruction, achieving high-quality synthesis with markedly fewer diffusion steps. In another approach, Zhao et al. [67] introduced the mamba-enhanced diffusion model (MambaDiff) for perceptual blind super-resolution, integrating a mamba state-space module to capture long-range dependencies and introducing a perception-aware blur kernel noise (PBKN) estimator along with a semantic information fusion (SIF-Mamba) block to enhance perceptual realism and stability under unknown degradation conditions. Meanwhile, Liu et al. [64] proposed IM-Diff, an implicit multi-contrast diffusion model that generalized SR to arbitrary scaling factors. Through a hierarchical multi-contrast fusion (HMF) module and a reference-aware cross mamba (RCM) mechanism, IM-Diff captured inter-contrast dependencies, while a wavelet implicit neural representation magnification (WINRM) decoder enabled continuous-scale reconstruction without retraining. Besides, Yao et al. [68] introduced PFEDiff, a prior feature efficient diffusion model designed for 3D MRI super-resolution. Built upon a conditional DDPM backbone, PFEDiff integrated a prior information enhancement module (PIEM) and a residual attention noise predictor to improve feature extraction efficiency and anatomical preservation.

Beyond MRI, diffusion-based SR has also been extended to electrophysiological modalities. Wang et al. [22] developed a spatio-temporal adaptive conditional diffusion framework to upsample low-density EEG recordings into high-density representations, facilitating improved neuroimaging and cognitive decoding. The model employed a conditional DDPM guided by a spatio-temporal conditioning module and a transformer-based denoiser, enabling accurate multi-band EEG signal reconstruction and preserving dynamic neural correlations across both time and space.

These methodologies collectively demonstrate how diffusion models achieve unprecedented resolution enhancement through latent-space optimization, cross-modal conditioning, and computational acceleration, paving the way for more precise quantitative brain imaging.

One major theme is accelerated reconstruction and translation under limited supervision. The model employed an adversarial mapper to implement rapid diffusion over large reverse steps, followed by an adaptation phase that refines the reconstruction by updating the before minimize data-consistency loss. Extending to unsupervised translation, Özbey et al. [16] proposed SynDiff, an adversarial diffusion model designed for unsupervised medical image translation across different modalities. SynDiff employed a conditional diffusion process with large steps and an adversarial projector for efficient and accurate reverse diffusion sampling, complemented by a cycle-consistent architecture for unpaired training. Addressing long MRI acquisition times under limited supervision, Safari et al. [73] introduced a self-supervised adversarial diffusion (SSAD-MRI) architecture that employed an adversarial mapper and a ReconFormer generator to reconstruct images without requiring fully sampled ground truth data.

A second line of work emphasizes synthetic generation and missing-modality completion, motivated by the reality that multi-contrast protocols are often incomplete in routine practice. Peng et al. [70] developed a two-stage generative model to synthesize high-resolution 3D T1-weighted brain MRI scans conditioned on clinical metadata such as age and sex. The architecture combined a fine-grained vector-quantized variational autoencoder with a masked transformer-based diffusion probabilistic model to synthesize anatomically plausible volumes. Meng et al. [74] presented the multi-modal modality-masked diffusion network (M2DN) for brain MRI synthesis, particularly addressing scenarios where random modalities are missing. The proposed architecture employed a diffusion model with multi-input, multi-output processing to generate missing modalities while self-reconstructing available ones, improving synthesis performance.

Diffusion models have also been extended to cross-domain clinical and neurophysiological translation. Li et al. [75] proposed a cross-modality semi-supervised framework that used a conditional DDPM-style diffusion model (SL-DDPM) to translate pre-operative 3D brain MRI into synthetic intra-operative ultrasound, followed by a hybrid transformer–CNN (nnU-OctTN) for brain-tumor segmentation. Beyond imaging-to-imaging translation, Yao et al. [71] proposed CATD, an EEG-to-fMRI cross-modal generation method that synthesizes cortical BOLD functional maps from noninvasive EEG, using a diffusion model built on a diffusion transformer backbone with conditionally aligned block (CAB) and a dynamic time-frequency segmentation module (DTFS). Similarly, Li et al. [4] introduced SCDM, a conditional diffusion–based generative framework designed for EEG-to-fNIRS cross-modal synthesis in motor imagery (MI) brain–computer interfaces, where denoising diffusion is guided by spatial and temporal constraints to learn a unified multimodal representation.

Finally, diffusion-based cross-modal fusion has shown promise in dose reduction and angiographic synthesis. Niu et al. [76] introduced a multicontrast MR-guided diffusion model, specifically a variant of the improved denoising diffusion probabilistic model (IDDPM), designed for ultra-low-dose brain PET denoising in temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE). The framework integrated T1-weighted, T2 FLAIR, and hippocampal-optimized T2 HIPPO MR sequences, leveraging a parallel cross-modal fusion (PCMF) module to dynamically extract and integrate features from multicontrast MR and PET images. Koch et al. [72] investigated the use of diffusion-based models for cross-modality image synthesis, specifically generating computed tomography angiography (CTA) images from time-of-flight magnetic resonance angiography (TOF-MRA) inputs.

These studies map a coherent progression: diffusion priors first improved reconstruction and enabled unpaired translation, then expanded to synthetic generation and missing-modality recovery, and are now being deployed as cross-domain bridges spanning intraoperative imaging and brain signal modalities. This trajectory positions diffusion models as a unifying probabilistic framework for cross-modal reconstruction and synthesis in brain imaging computing.

Their strength lies in tasks demanding pixel-level accuracy, such as tumor segmentation and lesion detection, where they leverage high-fidelity synthetic data generation and noise refinement capabilities. The core architecture employed independent generative modules for each modality, utilizing score-based diffusion models to map Gaussian noise to the target modality distribution, coupled with an adaptive conditional integration mechanism to dynamically adjust weight fusion between missing and available modalities.

Innovations in architecture and learning strategies have also been prominent. Li et al. [83] introduced CorrDiff, a corrective diffusion model designed for precise brain tumor segmentation in MRI images, specifically addressing systematic errors in existing segmentation methods. The model incorporated a vector quantized variational autoencoder (VQ-VAE) to reduce data dimensionality and enhance stability, along with a multi-fusion attention mechanism to improve flexibility and reliability. Ding et al. [84] introduced the FDiff-Fusion network, a framework that integrated denoising diffusion models with fuzzy learning for 3D medical image segmentation. The authors leveraged MRI images and proposed a hybrid architecture combining a U-Net network with a fuzzy learning module and an iterative attention fusion mechanism to improve segmentation accuracy, particularly for uncertain boundaries and blurry regions. Liu et al. [85] developed a mamba-enhanced diffusion model (MambaDiff) for 3D medical image segmentation utilizing MRI. The model integrated a diffusion-based architecture with a semantic hierarchical embedding (SHE) mechanism and global-slice perception mamba (GSPM) layer to preserve fine-grained features and ensure structural consistency in segmentation. This framework introduced a binary Bernoulli diffusion model (BBDM) as a diffusion refiner, which enhanced the modeling of discrete segmentation targets and improved efficiency through binarization and a cross-transformer for interactive feature exchange.

For example, Tosato et al. [102] proposed the use of DDPM to generate synthetic EEG data, addressing the challenge of limited high-quality data in BCI applications. Similarly, Vetter et al. They used DDPMs to model multichannel, densely sampled brain recordings, with the goal of generating realistic synthetic data. Besides, Luo et al. The study used a DDPM architecture to generate augmented MI-EEG samples in both multivariate time-series and time-frequency image formats.

Beyond addressing data scarcity, diffusion models enhance privacy protection and classification performance. Chen e et al. [104] introduced EEGCiD, a framework for synthesizing high-quality EEG data using a DDIM. The model condensed EEG signals into smaller, informative synthetic datasets, improving classification performance and protecting privacy against attacks like membership inference. Similarly, Chen et al. [32] developed a data augmentation framework for EEG-fNIRS hybrid BCIs, combining a DDPM with Gaussian noise to enhance model performance.

Further innovations include task-specific diffusion frameworks tailored to particular BCI applications. Huang et al. [105] introduced SAD-VER, a self-supervised diffusion probabilistic model-based framework for EEG data augmentation in visual-stimulus EEG recognition. The model utilized an adaptive variance diffusion probabilistic model (AV-DPM) to generate high-quality EEG samples. Siddhad et al. [106] advanced the field of EEG-based emotion recognition by generating synthetic data using a conditional denoising diffusion model. Torma and Szegletes [20] employed an enhanced diffusion probabilistic model framework to synthesize visual evoked potentials (VEP) and motor imagery data using EEG signals, integrating DDIM and progressive distillation to generate high-fidelity signals in a single step.

These studies demonstrate the power of diffusion models in enhancing BCI performance by generating high-quality synthetic EEG data. These models have been applied to diverse tasks such as motor imagery classification, emotion recognition, and EEG-fNIRS hybrid systems, effectively addressing data scarcity and noise issues.

BCI decoding (Visual Stimulus, Imagined Text, and Imagined Voice)

BCI decoding involves translating brain signals into actionable insights, enabling control of external devices or detecting mental states. Practical applications of BCI decoding include interpreting responses to visual stimuli, imagined speech, and motor imagery tasks. Diffusion models are applied to generate high-quality, synthetic brain signals that enhance the decoding process. By refining noisy or incomplete data, these models improve the accuracy and reliability of BCI systems, facilitating better real-time communication and control for individuals with disabilities or neurological conditions. In visual decoding, diffusion-based frameworks have demonstrated remarkable capability in reconstructing perceptual content from both fMRI and EEG signals. Takagi and Nishimoto [111] presented a method for reconstructing high-resolution images from human brain activity using LDM. The study utilized fMRI to capture brain signals, with the stable diffusion model used to generate images from these signals. Similarly, Chen et al. [107] introduced the MinD-Vis framework, which combined fMRI with a conditional LDM for visual stimulus decoding. MinD-Vis employed a self-supervised representation learning approach based on sparse masked brain modeling (SC-MBM). Qian et al. [33] proposed the NeuroDM framework, which combined EEG-guided diffusion models to decode and visualize brain activity associated with visual stimuli. The model used an EEG-visual-transformer (EV-Transformer) to extract visual-related features from EEG signals, followed by an EEG-guided diffusion model (EG-DM) to generate corresponding images. Ferrante et al. [112] employed fMRI recordings primarily from the visual cortex to decode and reconstruct semantically similar images using an LDM architecture conditioned via cross-attention and time-step embedding. Li et al. [108] employed fMRI to reconstruct visual stimuli using a guided latent diffusion model enhanced with primary visual feature guidance decoded from fMRI. Xiang et al. [113] proposed EEG-RegionalDiff for EEG-based natural image reconstruction, conditioning a latent diffusion model on semantic/location-aware latent EEG codes learned via joint training of masked EEG signal recovery and EEG visual classification. extending beyond static images. Liu et al. [114] presented EEG2Video, a framework for decoding dynamic visual perception from EEG signals using a Seq2Seq architecture. The model reconstructed video clips by aligning high-temporal-resolution EEG signals with dynamic visual stimuli. Similarly, Yeung et al. [109] proposed BrainNRDS, a framework integrating fMRI-decoded motion representations with motion-conditioned video diffusion models to reconstruct dynamic visual stimuli. The model decoupled static image encoding from motion prediction by learning optical flow from neural signals and initial frames, enabling realistic video reanimation.

Beyond vision, diffusion models have been applied to imagined speech reconstruction and decoding. For instance, Xu et al. [110] introduced a framework for generating imagined speech from brain signals in patients with language impairments, using a denoising diffusion model for signal preprocessing and a transformer-based architecture for speech generation.

Beyond communication, diffusion models enhance decoding of motor intentions for device control. Dere et al. [27] utilized EEG and EMG signals for motor-intent decoding, employing a conditional denoising diffusion probabilistic model conditioned on EMG prompts to generate synthetic EEG data.

These studies demonstrate that diffusion models provide a unifying generative framework for BCI decoding across perception, language, and action, enabling both high-quality reconstruction and effective data augmentation while exposing new opportunities for multimodal integration and real-time neuroadaptive systems. Although this review covers diffusion-based BCI decoding across visual, imagined text, and imagined voice paradigms, the current literature is substantially richer for visual decoding. Diffusion-based studies on imagined text and imagined voice remain limited, and therefore these directions are discussed as emerging rather than equally mature subfields.

Challenges and future direction

Diffusion models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in synthesizing and analyzing brain imaging data, yet significant challenges remain unresolved. This section critically examines key limitations identified in contemporary research and proposes targeted research paths grounded in empirical constraints.

Computational scalability and sampling inefficiency

A consistent limitation across diffusion-based neuroimaging studies is the computational burden associated with high-dimensional sampling. Traditional denoising diffusion probabilistic models require hundreds to thousands of iterative reverse steps, resulting in prohibitive memory and time demands during both training and inference. In brain imaging, high-resolution volumetric modalities such as 3D MRI, fMRI, and PET exacerbate this challenge. Translation to clinical workflows is hindered by dependence on specialized GPUs, long training times, and poor scalability across hardware environments. Denoising diffusion implicit models [115] and progressive distillation [116] to reduce inference steps without compromising structural fidelity. Latent diffusion models operating in compressed feature spaces can yield major efficiency gains but necessitate careful control of anatomical and functional information loss [3]. Integrating diffusion with discriminative architectures, as in hybrid frameworks, can enhance performance while mitigating computational cost [117]. Hardware/software co-design leveraging neuromorphic accelerators, AI-specialized chips, and distributed computing promises scalable clinical deployment [118]. Neuromorphic implementations may enable energy-efficient, event-driven inference tailored to neural signal sparsity [119, 120]. However, neuromorphic accelerators offer potential for enhancing diffusion model performance in brain imaging but face limitations in memory bandwidth, power consumption, and scalability. Current systems, like Intel’s Loihi and IBM’s TrueNorth, struggle with the large memory requirements of high-resolution neuroimaging data, are not optimized for real-time, high-throughput tasks, and lack standardization, hindering their widespread adoption in clinical applications. Together, these strategies define the roadmap toward computationally efficient, clinically viable diffusion-based intelligence systems.

Limited generalization and domain shift sensitivity

Diffusion models exhibit high performance within controlled datasets but often fail to generalize across imaging centers, modalities, or subjects. This domain sensitivity arises from the inherent variability of neuroimaging data, such as scanner hardware, acquisition parameters, and population demographics, which produce systematic distribution shifts that diffusion models are not explicitly trained to handle [121]. While substantial methodological progress has addressed distribution shifts in brain imaging data, significant challenges persist in achieving robust cross-context generalization. Current approaches deploy a hierarchical strategy: marginal distribution discrepancies are primarily addressed through normalization protocols [122], complemented by increasingly sophisticated data alignment techniques [123, 124]. However, these methods often prove inadequate for handling conditional distribution shifts and label space inconsistencies, which demand fine-grained approaches that explicitly model the causal mechanisms underlying distributional differences [125]. Recent advances have introduced robust learning objectives specifically for neural network architectures [126], yet their clinical utility remains limited by a fundamental reality documented across multiple studies is that distribution shifts occur not merely between subjects but critically within the same subject across different recording sessions [127]. This intra-subject variability is driven by physiological state changes, measurement conditions, and biological adaptation that represents the next frontier for developing truly robust brain imaging computing frameworks. Future progress will require methods that explicitly model temporal dynamics of distribution shifts rather than treating them as static phenomena.

Multimodal integration and alignment

One of the primary challenges in brain imaging computing lies in the effective integration of heterogeneous data modalities. models fusing MRI with PET [76], EEG with fNIRS [4], or EEG with fMRI [71] often exhibit degraded performance due to misaligned spatiotemporal dynamics and non-linear neurovascular coupling. While diffusion models have demonstrated strong performance in single-modality scenarios, their application to multimodal fusion remains an area with considerable potential for refinement. One promising future direction is the integration of different diffusion models to create hybrid architectures that leverage the strengths of each model. For example, score-based models can improve the efficiency of gradient-based learning in regions with fewer data points, while latent diffusion models are ideal for handling high-dimensional and large-scale datasets. Combining these models could improve performance across neuroimaging tasks that involve diverse datasets. Building upon this, Future research should investigate enhanced strategies for integrating multiple modalities within the diffusion modeling frameworks, aiming to improve representational coherence and performance. Multi-scale diffusion models that operate at different spatial and temporal resolutions for different modalities could better capture the hierarchical organization of brain structure and function [128]. For instance, a unified framework could employ high-resolution diffusion processes for structural MRI, intermediate-resolution processes for fMRI, and low-resolution processes for PET, with cross-scale attention mechanisms enabling information flow between levels [129]. The hierarchical multi-contrast fusion module could provide a foundation for multi-scale integration, but extension to temporal dynamics and explicit biophysical modeling remains unexplored. In addition, uncertainty quantification for multi-modal integration is essential, as different modalities provide complementary but sometimes conflicting information [130]. Bayesian diffusion models that maintain posterior distributions over latent representations could quantify the uncertainty in cross-modal translations and identify cases where modalities provide inconsistent information [131, 132].

While diffusion models demonstrate significant potential, their real-world clinical deployment faces persistent hurdles. Computational scalability remains critical: inference times for high-resolution 3D volumes (e.g., MRI reconstruction) can be prohibitive in time-sensitive clinical workflows, and GPU memory demands limit accessibility on standard hospital hardware. Domain shift sensitivity severely impacts reliability: studies show performance degradation when models trained on curated research datasets (e.g., ADNI) encounter data from different scanners or diverse patient populations in clinical practice (e.g., diagnostic classifiers), leading to accuracy drops of 10–15% in some reported cases. Multimodal integration and alignment challenges hinder practical utility: temporal and spatial mismatches between modalities (e.g., EEG and fMRI cross-modal synthesis) complicate real-time BCI operation or unified diagnostic platforms. The applied studies (e.g., see Table VII for a summary) reveal these limitations concretely, including slower inference speeds compared to conventional methods and reduced robustness under data heterogeneity. Overcoming these barriers requires co-designed efficient architectures, robust domain adaptation techniques, and standardized multimodal fusion frameworks tailored for clinical environments.

Beyond the technical challenges, there are significant ethical implications associated with synthetic brain data generation and BCI decoding. Key concerns include data privacy, patient anonymity, and data ownership. It is crucial to ensure that synthetic data is anonymized and encrypted to protect patient privacy. Furthermore, as these models are trained on real patient data, clear guidelines on data ownership must be established. For BCI decoding, ethical risks such as mental privacy violations and potential cognitive manipulation must also be addressed. Ensuring compliance with regulatory frameworks is critical to safeguard individual rights and ensure responsible clinical use of these technologies.

The brain foundation models, introduced in the following subsection, exemplify a next-generation framework designed to unify diverse neuroimaging methodologies, offering a compelling pathway toward scalable, general-purpose intelligence in brain imaging analysis.

Integrating brain imaging with foundation models

Foundation models (FMs) are large neural networks pretrained on extensive datasets designed to overcome domain heterogeneity, data inefficiency, and limited task generalization. These models, powered by large-scale transformers, leverage massive multimodal pretraining to learn transferable representations that can be fine-tuned for diverse downstream tasks such as brain tumor segmentation [133, 134] and BCI decoding [135]. In brain imaging, integrating FMs with modalities such as MRI, fMRI, EEG, and PET offers significant potential for diagnosing and treating neurological diseases [136]. To achieve this integration, three keys are essential. First, large-scale transformer architectures must be optimized to handle high-dimensional brain imaging data, enabling comprehensive global reasoning across spatial and temporal dimensions. Second, foundation models training strategies, such as self-supervised learning [137, 138], need adaptation to brain imaging data constraints, ensuring robust generalization without extensive annotation. Third, novel tokenization and embedding schemes must transform complex 3D and 4D brain representations into semantically meaningful token spaces compatible with transformer architectures.

Future research must prioritize pathways for seamless integration into clinical workflows and foster targeted interdisciplinary collaboration. To bridge the technical-clinical gap, developing lightweight, deployable versions of diffusion models optimized for hospital-grade hardware (e.g., integrated within MRI scanner consoles) is essential. Interdisciplinary teams comprising medical engineers, radiologists, neurologists, and data scientists are crucial for: (1) defining clinically meaningful performance metrics and validation protocols; (2) co-designing user interfaces that prioritize actionable outputs (e.g., automated lesion quantification integrated into neuroradiology workflows); and (3) establishing standardized data pipelines for real-world model refinement, including federated learning approaches to develop scanner-agnostic models. Initiatives like joint development of open-source, regulatory-compliant toolkits could accelerate adoption. Such partnerships ensure that diffusion models evolve beyond technical novelty into reliable tools enhancing diagnostic speed, treatment planning, and patient-specific outcome prediction in neurology and psychiatry.

Conclusion

This review systematically explores the formulations, variations, and applications of diffusion models, underscoring their transformative impact on generative tasks in brain imaging. To equip researchers with foundational knowledge, we first delineate the four principal classes of diffusion models. Subsequently, we provide a comprehensive review of DM applications across key brain imaging domains such as reconstruction, classification, segmentation, and BCI decoding, alongside other emerging application frontiers; thereby offering a curated guide to the latest literature. Despite substantial progress, the surveyed literature reveals persistent obstacles to real-world translation, notably high computational cost, limited data availability, challenges in generalization, and heterogeneity across brain imaging modalities. Addressing these issues will require continued refinement of model architectures for efficiency and closer alignment with multimodal clinical brain imaging workflows. In this context, the integration of diffusion models with large-scale foundation models emerges as a promising pathway toward more scalable and personalized brain imaging systems.
