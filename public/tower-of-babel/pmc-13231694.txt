TFRC promotes ferroptosis to suppress breast cancer bone metastasis via the activation of HIF-1 pathway
Authors: Lulin Liu, Yan Gao, Dongmin Yu, Deping Li, Sheng Liu, Chunlei He, Chunlin Xiao, Yongjun Ye, Kai Zhao, Fangtian Xu, Yanming Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast cancer with bone metastasis remains a significant clinical challenge. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent cell death process, may influence metastasis. However, the role of key regulators like transferrin receptor (TFRC) is unclear.MethodsTranscriptome sequencing identified ferroptosis-related differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in tibial tumors from a breast cancer mouse model with bone metastasis versus breast cancer cells. Hub genes were identified via protein-protein interaction network analysis. The function of TFRC was validated in vitro (MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231) through gain/loss-of-function studies combined with ferroptosis modulators (Erastin, Ferrostatin-1) and in a BALB/c nude mouse intratibial model. Cell apoptosis, proliferation, invasion, and migration were evaluated by flow cytometry, cell-counting kit-8, Transwell, and wound healing assays, respectively. Ferroptosis markers and epithelial-mesenchymal transition-related proteins were assessed. The involvement of the hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) pathway was investigated using the HIF-1α inhibitor PX-478.ResultsBioinformatics identified 22 ferroptosis-related DEGs, with TFRC among the top 10 hub genes significantly downregulated in bone metastasis, a finding corroborated in human clinical specimens. Overexpressing TFRC in vivo suppressed tumor growth, upregulated E-cadherin, downregulated N-cadherin, and promoted ferroptosis. In vitro, TFRC overexpression consistently inhibited malignant phenotypes and induced ferroptosis in both cell lines, effects reversible by ferroptosis inhibition. Conversely, ferroptosis activation upregulated TFRC expression, and TFRC knockdown attenuated cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis inducers, indicating a reciprocal regulatory relationship. Mechanistically, HIF-1 pathway was identified as the downstream pathway of TFRC, and TFRC overexpression upregulated HIF-1α. PX-478 attenuated TFRC-mediated ferroptosis induction and suppression of malignancy.ConclusionTFRC inhibits bone metastasis in breast cancer by promoting ferroptosis via HIF-1α activation, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-026-02269-w.Keywords: TFRC, Breast cancer bone metastasis, Ferroptosis, HIF-1 signaling pathway

Introduction

Breast cancer constitutes the most prevalent malignancy among women globally and represents a leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1, 2]. Over the past 5 years, its global incidence has risen to approximately 2.3 million new cases annually [3]. Bone constitutes the predominant site of distant recurrence in breast cancer, frequently giving rise to debilitating skeletal-related events—pathological fractures, spinal cord compression, and hypercalcemia—that markedly impair quality of life [4]. Current diagnostic approaches exhibit limited sensitivity for detecting occult metastases, while therapeutic options remain constrained by intrinsic and acquired resistance to conventional treatments [5]. Hence, elucidating the molecular drivers of bone metastasis and identifying predictive biomarkers constitutes a critical unmet need in breast cancer management.

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of cell death driven by lethal lipid peroxidation, exhibits a distinct biochemical execution mechanism compared to apoptosis or necrosis [6]. This process necessitates intracellular iron overload and inactivation of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), culminating in uncontrolled oxidative damage [7]. In breast cancer, ferroptosis modulation significantly influences tumor proliferation, metastasis, and drug resistance [8]. Isopropylester C has been demonstrated to induce autophagy-dependent ferroptosis and suppress progression and metastasis in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) through GPX4 ubiquitination-mediated degradation [9]. Complementarily, SBFI26 inhibits tumor cell proliferation and metastasis in TNBC by triggering ferroptosis via lipid peroxidation induction [10]. Collectively, these findings establish targeted ferroptosis induction as a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating breast cancer progression and metastatic dissemination.

The transferrin receptor (TFRC) serves as the primary conduit for cellular iron uptake via receptor-mediated endocytosis of transferrin-bound iron [11]. Notably, TFRC-mediated functions exhibit context-dependent roles across malignancies. In colorectal cancer, TFRC-driven iron accumulation activates β-catenin signaling through tankyrase stabilization, promoting tumor cell nucleotide biosynthesis and DNA repair [11]. Conversely, suppressing TFRC protein expression attenuates ferroptosis in endometrial cancer cells in a ubiquitin-proteasome-dependent manner, thereby promoting tumor progression [12]. TFRC contributes to mitochondrial respiration and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, playing a critical role in the growth and survival of pancreatic cancer [13]. However, the role of TFRC overexpression specifically in breast cancer with bone metastasis remains unexplored. Given that the bone marrow constitutes an iron-rich niche where iron availability influences osteolytic activation [14, 15], elucidating the function of TFRC in regulating the ferroptosis response within the bone metastatic microenvironment is biologically and therapeutically imperative.

This study integrated transcriptomic profiling of intratibial tumors with functional validation to establish TFRC as a metastasis-suppressive hub gene in breast cancer bone colonization. We then employed in vitro and in vivo models to investigate the effects of TFRC overexpression on malignant behaviors of breast cancer cells and its impact on ferroptosis induction. Furthermore, we explored whether the hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) signaling pathway mediated TFRC’s regulatory functions through pharmacological inhibition and molecular profiling, providing new insights into the molecular mechanisms of bone metastasis and suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy targeting the TFRC/HIF-1/ferroptosis axis.

Materials and methods

Establishment of breast cancer bone metastasis mouse model

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) female BALB/c nude mice (6–8 weeks old, 18–20 g) were obtained from SiPeiFu (Beijing, China). 4T1 cells were prepared in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (1 × 10⁵ cells/10 µL). Under isoflurane anesthesia (1.5% maintenance), the right tibia was surgically exposed. With the knee in flexion, 4T1 cell suspension was injected into the proximal tibial marrow space using a 29-gauge microsyringe [16]. Following a four-week period, mice underwent terminal anesthesia via a 2% isoflurane-oxygen mixture prior to cervical dislocation for euthanasia. Subsequently, tumor tissues were excised. All procedures received prior approval from the local ethics committee and adhered strictly to the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Tumor cells were isolated from tibial tumor tissues within a breast cancer bone metastasis mouse model. Total RNA was extracted from these cells, along with an untreated breast cancer cell line serving as a control. Transcriptome sequencing was subsequently performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) to generate paired-end 150 bp reads. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 package (v1.36.0) in R. DEGs were identified by applying a threshold of absolute log2 fold change ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value (q-value) ≤ 0.05, and ferroptosis associated genes were selected. Their expression patterns were visualized using a heatmap. The Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID; https://david.ncifcrf.gov) was employed to conduct Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses on the identified DEGs [17]. From the resultant GO-KEGG dataset, significantly enriched pathways meeting the criterion of a p-value < 0.05 were selected for presentation. Enrichment bubble plots were generated for visualization. TFRC co-expressed genes were obtained from Coxpresdb database (https://coxpresdb.jp/). Breast cancer-associated genes and ferroptosis-related genes were downloaded from Genecards database (https://www.genecards.org/).

Construction of the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network and identification of Hub Genes

The Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins online database (STRING, https://www.string-db.org/) was utilized to analyze co-expressed DEGs and predict potential PPI relationships among proteins encoded by genes potentially playing pivotal roles in the pathogenesis of breast cancer bone metastasis. The resultant PPI network was subsequently visualized using Cytoscape software. To identify hub genes, the CytoHubba plugin within Cytoscape was applied, implementing the Degree algorithm to compute node centrality scores. The top 10 genes exhibiting the highest degree scores were designated as hub genes for further investigation. Finally, functional enrichment analysis was performed on these 10 hub genes.

Key gene analysis

Expression box plots were generated using ggplot2 package (v3.4.0) in R to visualize the expression profiles of hub genes using their raw expression values across the original sample dataset. Subsequently, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on these expression patterns. Following computational processing using R language functions, the two principal components (PC1 and PC2) were extracted for further interpretation.

Clinical diagnostic evaluation via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis

The ROC curves were constructed using the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) platform to evaluate the diagnostic potential of the selected genes. The diagnostic accuracy of each candidate gene was quantitatively assessed by calculating the Area Under the Curve (AUC), with an AUC > 0.7 indicating clinically relevant discriminatory power.

Tissue specimen collection and processing

Human breast cancer bone metastasis tissues (n = 6) and adjacent tissues (n = 6) were prospectively collected from breast cancer patients undergoing surgery at the First Affiliated Hospital of Gannan Medical University. Affirmative written informed consent was obtained from all participants preceding their enrollment, stipulating explicit permission for biobanking and genetic materials research. Ethical endorsement for this study was secured from the local ethics committee (NO. LLCS-2025405), ensuring full adherence to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cell culture and treatment

Human breast cancer cell lines (MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231) and murine breast cancer cell line 4T1 were procured from iCell (Shanghai, China). MCF-7 cells were cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S). MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in Leibovitz’s L-15 medium with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. 4T1 cells were grown in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% P/S. All cell lines were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂.

Human breast cancer cell lines (MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231) were divided into Control, Erastin, Erastin + Fer-1, si-NC, si-TFRC, si-NC+Erastin, si-TFRC+Erastin, oe-NC, oe-TFRC, oe-NC + Fer-1, oe-TFRC + Fer-1, oe-NC+PX478, and oe-TFRC+PX478. To manipulate TFRC expression, cells were transfected using Lipofectamine reagents: for knockdown, with negative control siRNA (si-NC) or TFRC-specific siRNA (si-TFRC) via Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA); for overexpression, with a control vector (oe-NC) or a TFRC overexpression construct (oe-TFRC) via Lipofectamine 3000, following the manufacturers’ protocols. Following a 48-hour transfection, cells were harvested for downstream analysis. For pharmacological interventions, MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells underwent treatment with the ferroptosis inducer Erastin (10 µM), the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, 1 µM) or HIF-1α inhibitor (PX478, 60 µM) [18] for 24 h prior to collection.

Overexpression of TFRC in vivo

SPF female BALB/c nude mice (6–8 weeks old, 18–20 g) were obtained from SiPeiFu (Beijing) and randomized into the LV-oe-NC and LV-oe-TFRC groups. 4T1 cells stably transduced with lentiviral vectors expressing either control or TFRC constructs were prepared in sterile PBS (1 × 10⁵ cells/10 µL), and then the model of breast cancer bone metastasis was constructed. After a 4-week period, mice were humanely euthanized via cervical dislocation under terminal anesthesia induced by a 2% isoflurane-oxygen mixture. Tibiae and tumor tissues were then collected for subsequent histological and molecular analyses. All procedures involving animals were approved by the local ethics committee and performed in compliance with the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) staining

Tissue specimens (murine tumors, tibial bones, and human breast cancer/adjacent normal tissues) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C for 24 h. Following PBS washes, samples underwent graded ethanol dehydration. Tissues were cleared in xylene and embedded in paraffin. serial 4 μm sections were cut on a rotary microtome, mounted onto poly-L-lysine-coated slides, and dried at 60°C for 2 h. Deparaffinization was performed in xylene, followed by rehydration via graded ethanol concentrations. After final rinses in distilled water, nuclear staining was achieved using Harris hematoxylin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 10 min. Differentiation was performed in 0.7% acid ethanol for 30 s, followed by 15 min of blueing in Scott’s tap water substitute. Cytoplasmic counterstaining employed alcoholic eosin Y (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 s. Sections were dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with synthetic resin. Histomorphological evaluation was performed under brightfield microscopy.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Paraffin-embedded sections were first deparaffinized and rehydrated using a standard protocol. Antigen retrieval was carried out in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer at pH 6.0. To suppress endogenous peroxidase activity, sections were treated with 0.3% H₂O₂ in methanol. Non-specific sites were blocked by incubation with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS for 30 min. Subsequently, the sections were incubated at 4°C overnight with Ki67 primary antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, UK, ab15580) and anti-TFRC primary antibody (Abcam, ab214039) diluted at 1:400. Following PBS washes, a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Abcam, 1:200) was applied and incubated for 80 min at room temperature. Signal development used 3,3’-diaminobenzidine under light-protected conditions. Nuclear counterstaining employed Mayer’s hematoxylin (Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min with differentiation in acid ethanol and bluing in tap water. Sections were dehydrated, cleared, and resin-mounted as above. Quantitative analysis of Ki-67 positivity was performed using ImageJ software.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Human breast cancer cells (MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231, 4 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates in complete growth medium. Following 24-h pre-incubation (37°C, 5% CO₂) for adherence, cells underwent experimental treatments. At designated timepoints (24, 48, 72 h), culture medium was replaced with 90 µL fresh serum-free medium and 10 µL CCK-8 reagent. Plates were incubated for 2 h under light-protected conditions. Optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Flow cytometric analysis

MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells were harvested through trypsinization at 37°C for 3 min, neutralized with complete medium, and centrifuged (300 g, 5 min). Cell pellets were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and resuspended in 1× binding buffer. Aliquots of 100 µL cell suspension (1 × 10⁵ cells) were transferred to flow tubes. Cells were stained with 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL propidium iodide for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Immediately prior to analysis, 400 µL binding buffer was added. Samples were processed within 30 min on a flow cytometer (Cytoflex, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The levels of ROS, malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione (GSH), and Fe2+ were measured using the appropriate ELISA kits (Esebio, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s requirements. The optical density (OD) of each well was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm within 15 min.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Isolation of total RNA from human breast cancer cells and tissue specimens was carried out using TRIzol™ Reagent as per the manufacturer’s protocol. The purity of the isolated RNA was assessed on a NanoDrop™ One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with all samples yielding A260/A280 ratios of 1.8-2.0. For cDNA synthesis, 1 µg of total RNA was utilized as input for the SweScript All-in-One First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Supermix (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). incorporating genomic DNA removal. qPCR reactions were conducted in triplicate using SYBR Green Master Mix (Servicebio) on a QuantStudio™ 6 Pro system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Reaction parameters: initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s; 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s, 60°C for 30 s; followed by melt curve analysis (60°C to °C). Gene expression levels were normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and analyzed by the 2−ΔΔCt method. The corresponding primer sequences were listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blot (WB)

Protein lysates were extracted from murine tumor tissues or cultured cells using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were resolved on 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gels and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 2 h at 22–25°C and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies (1:1000, Abcam) diluted in blocking buffer: TFRC (ab214039), E-cadherin (ab40772), N-cadherin (ab76011), solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11, ab307601), GPX4 (ab125066), HIF-1α (ab308433) and β-actin (ab8227) loading control. After TBST washes, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit IgG, Abcam, 1:5000) for 1 h at 22–25°C. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate on a Tanon 5200 Chemiluminescent Imaging System with optimal exposure times. Protein expression levels were quantified using ImageJ software.

Wound healing assay

Human breast cancer cells (MCF-7, MDA-MB-231) were seeded in 6-well plates (1 × 10⁵ cells/well) and allowed to adhere overnight. Upon reaching > 80% confluence, a uniform linear wound was created in the monolayer using a sterile pipette tip guided by a straight edge to ensure perpendicularity. Dislodged cells were removed by washing with PBS. Serum-free medium was then added to the wells. Cells were incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator. Phase-contrast images of the wound area were captured immediately (0 h) and after 24 h of incubation. Cell migration into the wound area was quantified by measuring the change in wound width using ImageJ software.

Transwell assay

A Transwell invasion assay was executed using Matrigel-coated membranes (8 μm pore size). Briefly, after polymerization and rehydration of the Matrigel, serum-starved MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells (5 × 10⁴ per insert) were seeded in the upper chamber. Complete medium supplemented with 20% FBS served as a chemoattractant in the lower chamber. Following a 24-hour incubation at 37°C and 5% CO₂, non-invading cells on the upper surface were carefully removed. Invaded cells attached to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and visualized under an optical microscope for counting.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are summarized as the mean ± standard deviation. Analysis was completed in GraphPad Prism (version 8.0). For direct comparisons between two independent groups, a two-tailed Student’s t-test was utilized. When comparing three or more groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted, and post hoc pairwise comparisons were carried out using Tukey’s test. A probability value of less than 0.05 (P < 0.05) was interpreted as statistically significant.

Results

Identification of DEGs and functional enrichment analysis

Transcriptome sequencing was performed on RNA extracted from tumor cells isolated from tibial lesions in a breast cancer bone metastasis mouse model, with untreated breast cancer cells serving as controls. Comparative analysis identified 22 ferroptosis-related DEGs (Supplementary Table 2), whose expression patterns were visualized in a hierarchical clustering heatmap (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Functional annotation of these 22 ferroptosis-associated DEGs was conducted through GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. GO analysis revealed that ferroptosis-related DEGs were enriched in cellular iron ion homeostasis, iron ion transport, and long-chain fatty acid metabolic process in Biological Process (BP) (Supplementary Fig. 1B), autolysosome, mitochondrial outer membrane, and endoplasmic reticulum membrane in Cellular Component (CC) (Supplementary Fig. 1C), and arachidonate-CoA ligase activity, long-chain fatty acid-CoA ligase activity, and glutamate-cysteine ligase activity in Molecular Function (MF) (Supplementary Fig. 1D). KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated significant enrichment in the ferroptosis pathway, which ranked among the top 10 most significantly enriched pathways visualized in the bubble plot (Supplementary Fig. 1E).

Identification of Hub genes

A PPI network of the 22 ferroptosis-related DEGs was constructed using the STRING database (minimum interaction score: 0.7) and visualized in Cytoscape (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Topological analysis employing the Degree algorithm via CytoHubba identified 10 hub genes with the highest connectivity scores: NCOA4, SLC40A1, CP, TFRC, HMOX1, STEAP3, FTL, LPCAT3, SLC39A14, SLC7A11 (Supplementary Fig. 2B). GO and KEGG functional analysis was performed on 10 hub genes. The most significant top 10 GO term entries in each GO classification were selected to draw the enrichment analysis bar chart. The hub genes were mainly enriched in BP’s cellular iron ion homeostasis, iron ion transport, CC’s autolysosome, basolateral plasma membrane, and MF’s The iron ion transmembrane transporter activity, ferrous iron transmembrane transporter activity (Supplementary Fig. 2C), and bar plot of the KEGG enrichment analysis showed that the hub genes were significantly enriched to ferroptosis (Supplementary Fig. 2D).

Analysis of hub gene expression and diagnostic potential

Transcriptomic profiling revealed distinct expression patterns of hub genes between experimental groups. Hierarchical clustering analysis of hub gene expression in raw sequencing data demonstrated clear segregation of tumor and control samples (Fig. 1A). PCA performed on hub gene expression profiles accounted for 99.9% of total variance in the first two principal components (PC1: 89.2%, PC2: 10.7%), effectively discriminating control from tumor-bearing cohorts (Fig. 1B). ROC curve analysis established exceptional diagnostic accuracy for all 10 hub genes: NCOA4, SLC40A1, CP, TFRC, HMOX1, STEAP3, FTL, LPCAT3, SLC39A14, SLC7A11, and each gene exhibited AUC > 0.9 (Fig. 1C), confirming their robust discriminatory power. Differential expression of hub genes was validated in intra-tibial tumor cells versus untreated breast cancer cell lines using RT-qPCR. Comparative analysis demonstrated significant downregulation of NCOA4, TFRC, STEAP3, and LPCAT3, and significant upregulation of SLC39A14 and SLC7A11 in intra-tibial tumor cells compared with untreated breast cancer cells (Fig. 1D). These results were fully concordant with transcriptomic sequencing data. Integrated analysis of transcriptomic data and literature evidence identified TFRC as a candidate regulator for functional validation.

TFRC overexpression suppresses bone metastasis progression in breast cancer

HE staining of human breast cancer bone metastasis versus paracancerous tissues revealed densely packed, morphologically intact tumor cells with minimal necrosis or inflammatory infiltration in cancerous specimens, contrasting with paracancerous tissues (Fig. 2A). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated significantly elevated Ki-67 proliferation indices in bone metastasis tissues compared with paracancerous tissues (Fig. 2B). To validate TFRC expression in human breast cancer bone metastasis, immunohistochemical staining was performed on paired clinical samples. As shown in Fig. 2C, TFRC-positive staining was significantly lower in bone metastatic tissues compared with paracancerous tissues, consistent with our transcriptomic findings.

A bone metastasis model was established in female BALB/c nude mice through intra-tibial injection of luciferase-tagged 4T1 murine breast cancer cells. Cells were transduced with LV-oe-NC or LV-oe-TFRC. The qRT-PCR and WB confirmed successful TFRC overexpression (Fig. 2D-E). Histopathological evaluation of tibial lesions revealed aggressive tumor growth with cohesive malignant cells in the LV-oe-NC group, and in the LV-oe-TFRC group, there was significant tumor suppression accompanied by extensive inflammatory infiltration and disrupted tumor architecture (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, WB was performed to detect of epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related proteins (E-cadherin and N-cadherin) expression in intraosseous tumor tissues, revealing that compared with the LV-oe-NC group, TFRC overexpression significantly upregulated E-cadherin expression and downregulated N-cadherin expression (Fig. 2G). ELISA assessment of oxidative stress-related indicators demonstrated that TFRC overexpression significantly elevated ROS and MDA, and depleted GSH levels compared with the LV-oe-NC group (Fig. 2H). Quantification of Fe²⁺ levels via ELISA revealed significantly elevated concentrations in intraosseous tumors from the LV-oe-TFRC group compared to LV-oe-NC controls (Fig. 2I). Concurrent WB analysis demonstrated marked downregulation of key ferroptosis suppressors (SLC7A11 and GPX4) compared with the LV-oe-NC group (Fig. 2J). The above findings suggested that overexpression of TFRC might inhibit tumor progression by promoting ferroptosis.

TFRC expression is upregulated by ferroptosis and mediates ferroptosis sensitivity in vitro

To investigate the interplay between ferroptosis and TFRC, we examined whether ferroptosis activation regulated TFRC expression. Treatment of MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells with the ferroptosis inducer Erastin significantly upregulated TFRC protein levels, while concurrently downregulating SLC7A11. This effect was reversed by co-treatment with the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 (Fig. 3A-B), indicating that ferroptosis signaling can positively regulate TFRC expression. Efficient knockdown of TFRC was achieved using specific siRNA, as confirmed by WB and qRT-PCR (Fig. 3C-D). In proliferation assays, Erastin treatment significantly inhibited the growth of control (si-NC) cells. While TFRC knockdown enhanced cell proliferation, the anti-proliferative effect of Erastin was significantly attenuated in TFRC-deficient cells (si-TFRC+Erastin) compared to si-NC+Erastin group (Fig. 3E). Consistent with this phenotype, the Erastin-induced accumulation of ROS and intracellular Fe²⁺ was markedly blunted in TFRC-knockdown cells (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, the downregulation of key ferroptosis defense proteins GPX4 and SLC7A11 by Erastin was less pronounced in cells lacking TFRC (Fig. 3G). Collectively, these data demonstrate that TFRC is not only upregulated by ferroptosis but is also a critical determinant of cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis inducers.

TFRC overexpression inhibits the proliferation, migration, and invasion of breast cancer cells by promoting ferroptosis

To further investigate the impact of TFRC expression on ferroptosis in breast cancer cells, MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cell lines were divided into oe-NC, oe-NC + Fer-1, oe-TFRC, and oe-TFRC + Fer-1. Successful TFRC overexpression in both MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells was confirmed by RT-qPCR analysis and WB, which demonstrated no significant difference in TFRC expression between the Control and oe-NC groups. In contrast, TFRC expression was significantly higher in the oe-TFRC group compared to the oe-NC group (Fig. 4A-B).

Cell proliferation assessed via CCK-8 assay delineated distinct regulatory effects among experimental cohorts (Fig. 4C). Specifically, oe-NC + Fer-1 cells displayed significantly enhanced viability relative to oe-NC controls. In stark contrast, TFRC overexpression markedly suppressed proliferation compared to oe-NC. Crucially, ferroptosis inhibition partially counteracted TFRC’s anti-proliferative impact: although proliferation in oe-TFRC + Fer-1 remained significantly attenuated versus oe-NC + Fer-1, it showed partial recovery relative to oe-TFRC alone. Complementary flow cytometry analysis of apoptosis revealed congruent trends: oe-NC + Fer-1 suppressed apoptosis versus oe-NC, while oe-TFRC elevated apoptotic rates; ferroptosis inhibition likewise mitigated these pro-apoptotic effects in the oe-TFRC + Fer-1 cohort (Fig. 4D). Concurrent evaluation of metastatic capacity corroborated these findings. Wound healing and Transwell assays demonstrated that oe-NC + Fer-1 cells exhibited augmented migration and invasion relative to oe-NC controls (Fig. 4E–F). Conversely, TFRC overexpression profoundly impaired both phenotypes. Notably, oe-TFRC + Fer-1 cells manifested an intermediate metastatic phenotype—significantly suppressed compared to oe-NC + Fer-1 yet partially restored relative to oe-TFRC alone (Fig. 4E–F).

WB analysis characterized EMT marker proteins (E-cadherin/N-cadherin) in MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells across experimental conditions. Compared to oe-NC, oe-NC + Fer-1 exhibited decreased E-cadherin and increased N-cadherin. Conversely, oe-TFRC elevated E-cadherin while suppressing N-cadherin (Fig. 4G). Dual modulation in oe-TFRC + Fer-1 yielded intermediate levels: E-cadherin was higher than in oe-NC + Fer-1 but lower than oe-TFRC, whereas N-cadherin showed the reciprocal pattern (Fig. 4G).

ELISA analysis of ROS, MDA, GSH, and Fe²⁺ levels in breast cancer cells revealed that relative to the oe-NC group, the oe-NC + Fer-1 group exhibited significantly decreased ROS, MDA, and Fe²⁺ levels, alongside significantly increased GSH levels. The oe-TFRC group showed significantly elevated ROS, MDA, and Fe²⁺ levels, coupled with significantly reduced GSH levels (Fig. 5A-D). In the oe-TFRC + Fer-1 group, ROS, MDA and Fe²⁺ levels were significantly higher than in the oe-NC + Fer-1 group but significantly lower than in the oe-TFRC group. Conversely, GSH levels were significantly lower than in the oe-NC + Fer-1 group but significantly higher than in the oe-TFRC group.

WB analysis of key ferroptosis-related proteins, SLC7A11 and GPX4, yielded consistent results. Relative to the oe-NC group, the oe-NC + Fer-1 group showed significantly upregulated expression of both SLC7A11 and GPX4, and the oe-TFRC group exhibited significantly downregulated expression of SLC7A11 and GPX4 (Fig. 5E-F). The oe-TFRC + Fer-1 group demonstrated SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression levels that were significantly lower than in the oe-NC + Fer-1 group but significantly higher than in the oe-TFRC group (Fig. 5E-F). These collective findings indicated that TFRC overexpression suppressed metastatic potential, while Fer-1 treatment partially reversed these inhibitory effects.

TFRC inhibits breast cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by activating the HIF-1 signaling pathway

Bioinformatic analysis identified HIF-1 signaling as a key pathway linked to TFRC. Intersection analysis of three datasets – 1000 TFRC co-expressed genes from Coxpresdb, 50,000 breast cancer-associated genes from Genecards, and 1839 ferroptosis-related genes – yielded 197 common genes (Fig. 6A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these common genes revealed significant enrichment in the HIF-1 signaling pathway, alongside ferroptosis and AMPK signaling pathways (Fig. 6B). Consistent with bioinformatic prediction, TFRC overexpression upregulated HIF-1α in vivo. RT-qPCR and WB analysis of HIF-1α expression in intratibial tumors from mice demonstrated that the LV-oe-TFRC group exhibited significantly higher HIF-1α levels compared to the LV-oe-NC group (Fig. 6C-D).

To directly investigate the role of TFRC in modulating the HIF-1 pathway, MDA-MB-231 cells were divided into four groups: oe-NC, oe-TFRC, oe-NC + PX-478 (a HIF-1α inhibitor), and oe-TFRC + PX-478. RT-qPCR and WB analysis confirmed that HIF-1α and TFRC expression were significantly elevated in the oe-TFRC group relative to the oe-NC group. The oe-NC + PX-478 group showed significantly reduced expression of both HIF-1α and TFRC compared to oe-NC. In the oe-TFRC + PX-478 group, HIF-1α and TFRC expression were significantly higher than in oe-NC + PX-478 but significantly lower than in oe-TFRC alone (Fig. 7A-B).

Regarding proliferation, the oe-TFRC group showed a significant deficit compared to oe-NC. In stark contrast, adding PX-478 dramatically boosted proliferation in the oe-NC background. When comparing the combinatorial effect (oe-TFRC + PX-478), proliferation was significantly higher than in oe-TFRC alone but still significantly lower than in the oe-NC + PX-478 group (Fig. 7C). Apoptosis rates, analyzed by flow cytometry, followed a complementary pattern: oe-TFRC caused a significant increase compared to oe-NC, while PX-478 led to a significant decrease in the oe-NC context. Critically, the combination treatment resulted in apoptosis levels significantly lower than oe-TFRC alone but significantly higher than oe-NC + PX-478 (Fig. 7D). Aligning with its effects on proliferation, TFRC overexpression also impeded cell migration and invasion. Wound healing and Transwell assays both indicated that the oe-TFRC group had significantly compromised migration and invasion abilities compared to oe-NC. Conversely, the oe-NC + PX-478 group displayed significantly enhanced migration and invasion. The oe-TFRC + PX-478 group occupied an intermediate position, with migration and invasion capabilities significantly higher than oe-TFRC alone but significantly lower than oe-NC + PX-478 (Fig. 7E-F).

WB analysis of EMT markers yielded complementary results. Relative to the oe-NC group, TFRC overexpression significantly upregulated E-cadherin expression and downregulated N-cadherin expression, indicative of suppressed EMT. The oe-NC + PX-478 group exhibited significantly downregulated E-cadherin expression and significantly upregulated N-cadherin expression relative to the oe-NC group, suggesting promotion of EMT upon HIF-1α inhibition. Furthermore, in the oe-TFRC + PX-478 group, E-cadherin expression was significantly higher than in the oe-NC + PX-478 group but significantly lower than in the oe-TFRC group. Conversely, N-cadherin expression in the oe-TFRC + PX-478 group was significantly lower than in the oe-NC + PX-478 group but significantly higher than in the oe-TFRC group (Fig. 7G). This demonstrated that HIF-1α inhibition partially reversed the EMT-suppressive effects induced by TFRC overexpression.

TFRC promotes ferroptosis in breast cancer cells through HIF-1 pathway activation

ELISA analyses revealed that TFRC overexpression elevated ROS and MDA levels while diminishing GSH expression relative to oe-NC controls. Conversely, pharmacological inhibition of HIF-1α using PX-478 reversed these oxidative stress markers, curbing ROS/MDA accumulation and boosting GSH abundance compared to oe-NC. Crucially, the combined oe-TFRC + PX-478 intervention yielded an intermediate redox state: ROS and MDA levels were significantly elevated above oe-NC + PX-478 yet remained lower than oe-TFRC alone, whereas GSH exhibited the converse pattern—lower than oe-NC + PX-478 but higher than oe-TFRC (Fig. 8A). Aligning with this induced oxidative stress, intracellular Fe²⁺ dynamics were governed by the TFRC/HIF-1 axis. ELISA demonstrated significant Fe²⁺ accumulation in oe-TFRC versus oe-NC. Independent PX-478 treatment reduced Fe²⁺ pools in oe-NC + PX-478 compared to oe-NC. Consistently, Fe²⁺ levels in the dual-treated oe-TFRC + PX-478 group occupied an intermediate territory, surpassing oe-NC + PX-478 but falling short of oe-TFRC levels (Fig. 8B). WB analysis of ferroptosis regulators corroborated the mechanism. TFRC overexpression suppressed SLC7A11 and GPX4 protein expression relative to oe-NC. In contrast, HIF-1α blockade via PX-478 augmented SLC7A11/GPX4 expression in oe-NC + PX-478 versus oe-NC. Significantly, the oe-TFRC + PX-478 group displayed intermediate expression levels of both SLC7A11 and GPX4—lower than oe-NC + PX-478 but higher than oe-TFRC alone (Fig. 8C).

Discussion

Bone metastasis in breast cancer represents a major clinical challenge, often leading to severe morbidity and therapeutic resistance [19]. This study demonstrated that TFRC functioned as a critical suppressor of bone metastasis in breast cancer progression. Through integrated bioinformatics and extensive experimental validation, we identified TFRC as a key ferroptosis-related hub gene (Supplementary Fig. 3). Overexpression of TFRC significantly inhibited the progression of breast cancer. Crucially, these anti-tumor effects were mediated through the induction of ferroptosis and activation of the HIF-1 signaling pathway.

Iron-dependent cell death, termed ferroptosis—a form of regulated cell demise driven by lipid peroxidation—is increasingly recognized as a critical determinant in cancer progression and metastasis [20]. Elevated CST1 expression confers protection against ferroptotic cell death in gastric cancer cells, thereby fostering tumor advancement and metastatic dissemination [21]. Tumor-infiltrating neutrophils exhibit resistance to ferroptosis and facilitate breast cancer metastasis via aconitate decarboxylase 1 [22]. Liu et al. demonstrate that dysregulation of cholesterol homeostasis engenders resistance to ferroptosis, amplifying oncogenic potential and metastatic capacity in malignancies [23]. Our transcriptomic profiling identified TFRC as a central hub among DEGs associated with ferroptosis within tibia-localized tumor cells, displaying exceptional diagnostic potential (AUC > 0.9). Primarily characterized by its role in cellular iron uptake through transferrin-mediated endocytosis [24]. Suppression of TFRC effectively attenuates ferroptosis in T24 human bladder carcinoma cells [25]. Meanwhile, IGF2BP2 drives colorectal cancer progression by elevating TFRC expression and potentiating iron metabolism [26]. Furthermore, Zhang et al. substantiate that Methionine Adenosyltransferase-Like 3 mitigates ferroptosis sensitivity in lung cancer through regulatory modulation of TFRC [27]. TFRC activates iron-responsive PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling cascades to drive proliferation in endometrial carcinoma [28]. In breast cancer, estrogen receptor-mediated suppression of transferrin receptor expression diminishes sulfasalazine-induced ferroptosis [29]. This study provided compelling evidence that TFRC overexpression suppressed malignant phenotypes of breast cancer cells within the osteometastatic microenvironment. Our gain-of-function data established that TFRC overexpression induced ferroptosis. Importantly, our new loss-of-function experiments revealed a reciprocal relationship: ferroptosis activation by Erastin upregulated TFRC expression, and conversely, knockdown of TFRC rendered breast cancer cells less sensitive to Erastin-induced ferroptosis, as evidenced by attenuated growth inhibition, lipid peroxidation, and iron accumulation. This establishes TFRC not merely as a downstream effector but as a critical amplifier within a pro-ferroptotic signaling circuit, whose expression is bolstered by the process it facilitates. This positive feedback loop may be crucial for committing cells to ferroptotic death in the bone metastatic niche.

Comprehensive bioinformatics analyses coupled with experimental validation revealed that TFRC exerted its functional impact partially through the activation of the HIF-1 signaling cascade. As the principal transcriptional regulator governing cellular adaptation to hypoxia, HIF-1α assumes well-characterized yet multifaceted oncogenic roles in malignancies, encompassing angiogenesis, metabolic reprogramming, invasiveness, and metastatic dissemination [30]. Feng et al. recently have demonstrated that Coactivator-Associated Arginine Methyltransferase 1 synergizes with HIF-1 A to propel triple-negative breast carcinoma progression [31]. Emerging evidence indicates that stabilization of HIF-1 confers anti-tumorigenic effects against breast cancer cells both in vitro and in vivo [32]. Within this study, enforced overexpression of TFRC markedly upregulated HIF-1α expression across orthotopic bone marrow xenograft models and cultured breast cancer cells. Mechanistic dissection uncovered that HIF-1α activation constituted an indispensable prerequisite for TFRC’s metastasis-suppressive functionality. In cervical carcinogenesis, elevated TFRC expression correlates significantly with advanced disease stage, aggressive tumor phenotype, and lymph node metastasis. Pathway enrichment analysis via the KEGG database further implicates TFRC-mediated modulation of the HIF-1 signaling axis, suggesting its potential therapeutic relevance in cervical cancer pathogenesis [33]. Notably, HIF-1α-driven upregulation of TFRC combined with ferroptosis activation effectively reverses doxorubicin resistance in adjuvant breast cancer chemotherapy [34]. Herein, pharmacological inhibition of HIF-1α using PX-478 profoundly compromised TFRC-induced proliferation, motility, invasive capacity, EMT reversal, and induction of hallmark ferroptotic markers. This establishes the functional significance of the TFRC/HIF-1/ferroptosis regulatory axis in specifically suppressing breast cancer bone metastasis.

A strength of this study is the validation of TFRC’s function across breast cancer cell models with divergent inherent properties. MCF-7 (ER+, luminal) and MDA-MB-231 (TNBC, basal-like) differ profoundly in their hormone receptor status, transcriptional programs, and basal metastatic potential. The consistent observation that TFRC overexpression inhibits malignant phenotypes and induces ferroptosis in both lines suggests that its tumor-suppressive role is not confined to a specific molecular subtype but may represent a broader mechanism applicable to heterogeneous breast cancers. Future studies should investigate whether the magnitude of TFRC’s effect correlates with specific genetic backgrounds or metastatic tendencies.

In conclusion, this study employed integrated bioinformatics, functional genomics, and rigorous in vitro and in vivo models to identify and characterize TFRC as a pivotal suppressor of breast cancer bone metastasis. We elucidated a novel mechanism whereby TFRC overexpression activated the HIF-1 pathway, leading to the induction of ferroptosis, ultimately inhibiting tumor growth, proliferation, migration, invasion, and EMT. These findings significantly advance our understanding of iron metabolism and cell death regulation within the bone metastatic niche. Targeting the TFRC/HIF-1/ferroptosis axis presents a promising therapeutic strategy for combating the progression of breast cancer bone metastases.
