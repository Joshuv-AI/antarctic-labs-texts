Splittings in varieties of logic
Brian A. Davey, Tomasz Kowalski and Christopher J. Taylor

Abstract
. We study splittings, or lack of them, in lattices of subvarieties of some logic-related varieties. We present a general lemma, the Non-Splitting Lemma, which when combined with some variety-specific constructions, yields each of our negative results: the variety of commutative integral residuated lattices contains no splittings algebras, and in the varieties of double Heyting algebras, dually pseudocomplemented Heyting algebras and regular double p-algebras the only splitting algebras are the two-element and three-element chains.



1.
Introduction

A very natural divide-and-conquer method of studying a lattice
\mathbf{L}
is to
dismantle it into a disjoint pair of a principal filter and a principal
ideal. If such a
splitting
is possible, then the structure of

\mathbf{L}
is completely determined by the filter, the ideal, and the way
they are put together to make up
\mathbf{L}
. This concept of splitting was
introduced by Whitman
[
33
]
, and later used by McKenzie
[
22
]
to
investigate the lattice of varieties of lattices. In fact, several of McKenzie’s
results apply to lattices of subvarieties of any variety
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
– we will
frequently use one of these results.

Logic-related applications of
splittings began with Jankov
[
15
]
who used splittings to investigate
the lattice of superintuitionistic logics.
Jankov’s results were extended in various ways for other
classes of logics, notably modal and superituitionistic logics,
for which splitting methods proved to be very useful.
We refer the interested reader to Chagrov, Zakhariaschev
[
5
]

and Kracht
[
21
]
for surveys and much more. Beyond superintuitionistic
and modal logics, splittings are not too common.

In the lattice of subvarieties of a variety
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}

every splitting is induced by a single subdirectly irreducible algebra;
such algebras are called
splitting algebras
.
McKenzie
[
22
]
proved that if
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
is congruence distributive and
generated by its finite members, then every splitting algebra in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
is
finite. Day
[
9
]
showed that if
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
is congruence distributive and
locally finite, then the converse is true as well: every finite subdirectly
irreducible algebra is a splitting algebra.

Blok, Pigozzi
[
4
]
showed that in varieties with
equationally
definable principal congruences
(EDPC) every finitely presented subdirectly
irreducible algebra is a splitting algebra.
Since EDPC implies congruence distributivity but
not local finiteness, and congruence distributivity together with local
finiteness do not imply EDPC, the results of Day
[
9
]
and of
Blok, Pigozzi
[
4
]
complement each other.

Our goal in this article lies in the opposite direction. We will exhibit a
number of varieties
(congruence distributive, related closely to logics, and generated by
their finite members) for which very few splittings exist. This will give some
empirical evidence for the claim that the assumptions of local finiteness or
EDPC are optimal, and no better general results can be expected.

A class of logics to which our results immediately apply is the class of

substructural logics
, whose
algebraic semantics is the variety of
residuated lattices
.
Substructural logics form a comprehensive class: they include superitutionistic
logics, linear logic, relevant logics and many-valued logics.
Residuated lattices have a rich and complex theory, at one end connected to
classical algebra (via idempotent semirings and lattice-ordered groups),
and at the other to proof theory (via sequent systems). For more on residuated
lattices we refer the reader to Galatos
et al.

[
14
]
.

Relative pseudocomplements play a crucial role both in varieties with EDPC
(principal congruences of algebras in such varieties have relative
pseudocomplements), and in locally finite varieties (see the next section).
Thus, we will begin in Sections
2

and
3
with a few general results connecting relative
pseudocomplements and
splittings, from which in particular Day’s result directly follows.

In Section
4
we formalise what we mean by a

variety of logic
and present a basic result, the
Non-splitting Lemma
4.3
, that we shall use repeatedly to prove
our non-splitting theorems.
Indeed, all negative results on splittings in varieties of logics, known to the
authors, can be viewed as applications of the Non-splitting Lemma.
Using the lemma we will prove a number of new negative results stating that in a
certain variety
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
no algebra is splitting, except for those on a (short,
finite) list. Each of these results involves a pair
of constructions: an expansion followed by a distortion.

In Section
5
we prove that the variety
\mathsf{CIRL}
of
commutative integral residuated lattices contains no splittings algebras at all
– in fact, if
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
is a variety of residuated lattices that contains

\mathsf{CIRL}
, then no finite algebra from
\mathsf{CIRL}
is splitting
in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
(Corollary
5.20
). In Sections
6

and
7
we turn our attention to three cousins of Heyting
algebras, namely the varieties
\mathsf{DH}
of double Heyting algebras,

\mathsf{H}^{+}
of dually pseudocomplemented Heyting algebras and
\mathsf{RDP}

of regular double p-algebras. Jankov
[
15
]
proved that in the variety

\mathsf{H}
of Heyting algebras every finite subdirectly irreducible is a
splitting algebra. In stark contrast, we prove that in each of
\mathsf{DH}
,

\mathsf{H}^{+}
and
\mathsf{RDP}
the only splitting algebras are the
two-element and three-element chains
(Corollarys
7.41
).
Unlike the proof for the variety
\mathsf{CIRL}
, which is purely algebraic, the proofs for the varieties
\mathsf{DH}
,
\mathsf{H}^{+}
and
\mathsf{RDP}
use the restricted Priestley duality for each of the varieties.

2.
Splittings and relative pseudocomplements

Definition 2.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{L}}
be a lattice and let
a,b\in L
. The relative pseudocomplement
b\to a
and dual relative pseudocomplement
b\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}a
are defined by

\displaystyle x\wedge b\leqslant a

\displaystyle\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\leqslant b\to a,

or

\displaystyle b\to a

\displaystyle=\max\{\,y\in L\mid y\wedge b=a\wedge b\,\},

\displaystyle x\vee a\geqslant b

\displaystyle\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\geqslant b\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}a,

or

\displaystyle b\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}a

\displaystyle=\min\{\,y\in L\mid a\vee y=a\vee b\,\}.

(Some authors write
x\leftarrow y
or
x\Leftarrow y
instead of
y\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}x
.)
A
Heyting algebra
is an algebra
\langle A;\vee,\wedge,\to,0,1\rangle
such that
\langle A;\vee,\wedge,0,1\rangle
is a bounded lattice and
\to
is a relative pseudocomplement operation; a
dual Heyting algebra
is defined analogously.
A
double Heyting algebra
is an algebra
\langle A;\vee,\wedge,\to,\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}},0,1\rangle
such that

{\langle A;\vee,\wedge,\to,0,1\rangle}
is a Heyting algebra and
{\langle A;\vee,\wedge,\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}},0,1\rangle}
is a dual Heyting algebra.
Note that the underlying lattice of a (double) Heyting algebra is necessarily distributive.

For each element
x
of an ordered set
{\mathbf{X}}=\langle X;\leqslant\rangle
we define
{\downarrow}x:=\{y\in X\mid y\leqslant x\}
and
\mathord{\uparrow}x:=\{y\in X\mid y\geqslant x\}
.
For basic lattice-theoretic concepts, such as join-irreducible, join-dense and algebraic lattice, we refer to Davey and Priestley
[
8
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

A pair
(c,d)\in L^{2}
is a
splitting pair
(in
{\mathbf{L}}
) if
L=\mathord{\uparrow}c\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}{\downarrow}d
.

The following two lemmas are completely straightforward to prove.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
{\mathbf{L}}
be a complete lattice and let
c,d\in L
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

(c,d)
is a splitting pair,

(2)

c
is completely join-prime and
d=\bigvee\{\,x\in L\mid x\not\geqslant c\,\}
,

(3)

d
is completely meet-prime and
c=\bigwedge\{\,x\in L\mid x\not\leqslant d\,\}
.

Given
x,y
in an ordered set
{\mathbf{X}}
, we write
x\prec y
if
x
is covered by
y
in
{\mathbf{X}}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
(c,d)
be a splitting pair in a lattice
{\mathbf{L}}
. Then

(1)

c\wedge d\prec c
, and

(2)

c\to(c\wedge d)=d
.

We see from Lemma
2.4
that every splitting pair gives rise to a cover and a corresponding relative pseudocomplement. Our first aim is to prove a form of converse. The following easy lemma will be useful.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
a,b
be elements of a lattice
{\mathbf{L}}
such that
a\prec b
and assume that
b\to a
exists in
{\mathbf{L}}
. For all
x\in L
with
x\vee a\not\geqslant b
, we have
x\leqslant b\to a
.

Proof.

Let
x\in L
with
x\vee a\not\geqslant b
. Thus
(x\vee a)\wedge b<b
. As
a\leqslant(x\vee a)\wedge b\leqslant b
and
a\prec b
, it follows that
(x\vee a)\wedge b=a
, and hence

x\leqslant x\vee a\leqslant b\to a
, as required.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
a,b
be elements of a lattice
{\mathbf{L}}
such that
a\prec b
and assume that
d:=b\to a
exists in
{\mathbf{L}}
. If
c
is a join-prime element of
{\mathbf{L}}
with
c\leqslant b
and
c\not\leqslant a
, then
(c,d)
is a splitting pair with
b\in\mathord{\uparrow}c
and
a\in{\downarrow}d
.

Proof.

Let
c
be a join-prime element of
{\mathbf{L}}
with
c\leqslant b
and
c\not\leqslant a
.
We first prove that
\mathord{\uparrow}c\cap{\downarrow}d=\varnothing
.
Suppose that
\mathord{\uparrow}c\cap{\downarrow}d\neq\varnothing
.
Then we have
c\leqslant d=b\to a
and hence
c=c\wedge b\leqslant a
, a contradiction. Hence
\mathord{\uparrow}c\cap{\downarrow}d=\varnothing
.

We now prove that
\mathord{\uparrow}c\cup{\downarrow}d=L
. Let
x\in L
and assume that
x\not\geqslant c
. We shall prove that
x\leqslant d
. As
a\not\geqslant c
and
c
is join-prime, we have
x\vee a\not\geqslant c
, and hence
x\vee a\not\geqslant b
, as
b\geqslant c
. Thus, by Lemma
2.5
,
x\leqslant b\to a=d
,
as required.
∎

The following corollary is an immediate consequence of Lemmas
2.3
and
2.6
.

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
a,b
be elements of a lattice
{\mathbf{L}}
such that
a\prec b
and assume that
b\to a
exists in
{\mathbf{L}}
. Then there is at most one join-prime element
c
of
{\mathbf{L}}
with
c\leqslant b
and
c\not\leqslant a
and such an element
c
is necessarily completely join-prime.

We obtain the following result of Nešetřil, Pultr and Tardif
[
23
]
as an easy consequence of Lemma
2.6
.

Theorem 2.8
.

Assume that
{\mathbf{L}}
is a Heyting algebra in which the join-irreducible elements are join-dense. Let
a,b\in L
with
a\prec b
. Then there exists a splitting pair
(c,d)
in
{\mathbf{L}}
with
b\in\mathord{\uparrow}c
and
a\in{\downarrow}d
.

A strengthening of the assumption that the join-irreducible elements are join-dense will guarantee that a lattice forms a dual Heyting algebra.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
{\mathbf{L}}
be a lattice in which each element is the join of a finite set of join-prime elements. Then all dual relative pseudocomplements exist in
{\mathbf{L}}
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that
b\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}a
exists for all
a\leqslant b
in
{\mathbf{L}}
, so let
a,b\in L
with
a\leqslant b
. By assumption, there are finite sets
A
and
B
of join-prime elements such that
a=\bigvee A
and
b=\bigvee B
. Let

F_{a}:=\{\,x\in A\cup B\mid x\leqslant a\,\}\quad\text{and}\quad F_{c}:=\{\,x\in A\cup B\mid x\not\leqslant a\,\};

then
\bigvee F_{a}=a
and
\bigvee(F_{a}\cup F_{c})=b
. Define
c:=\bigvee F_{c}
. We claim that
c=b\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}a
. We must prove that, for all
x\in L
,

x\vee a\geqslant b\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\geqslant c.

Let
x\in L
. We have

x\geqslant c\implies x\vee a\geqslant c\vee a=\bigvee F_{c}\vee\bigvee F_{a}=\bigvee(A\cup B)=b.

Now assume that
x\vee a\geqslant b
. Let
y\in F_{c}
; so
y\leqslant b
and
y\not\leqslant a
. Hence
x\vee a\geqslant b\geqslant y
. As
y\not\leqslant a
and
y
is join-prime, we have
y\leqslant x
. Thus,
x
is an upper bound of
F_{c}
and so
x\geqslant\bigvee F_{c}=c
. Hence,
c=b\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}a
.∎

If both
b\to a
and
b\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}a
exist in
{\mathbf{L}}
, then in Lemma
2.6
we can drop the requirement that
a
and
b
are separated by a join-prime element of
{\mathbf{L}}
.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
a,b
be elements of a lattice
{\mathbf{L}}
such that
a\prec b
and assume that both
c:=b\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}a
and
d:=b\to a
exist in
{\mathbf{L}}
. Then
(c,d)
is a splitting pair with
b\in\mathord{\uparrow}c
and
a\in{\downarrow}d
. In particular,
b\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}a
is completely join-prime and
b\to a
is completely meet-prime.

Proof.

A very simple calculation using the definitions of
b\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}a
and
b\to a
shows that

b\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}a\leqslant b\to a\ \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\ a\geqslant b\ \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\ (b\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}a=0\And b\to a=1).

Since
a\prec b
, we therefore have
b\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}a\not\leqslant b\to a
, and it remains to show that
\mathord{\uparrow}(b\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}a)\cup{\downarrow}(b\to a)=L
.
Let
x\in L
with
b\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}a\not\leqslant x
. As
b\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}a\not\leqslant x
, we have
b\not\leqslant x\vee a
, and consequently, by Lemma
2.5
,
x\leqslant b\to a
,
as required.
∎

The next result is an immediate corollary.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
{\mathbf{L}}
be a double Heyting algebra, let
a,b\in L
with
a\prec b
and define
c:=b\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}a
and
d:=b\to a
. Then
(c,d)
is a splitting pair with
b\in\mathord{\uparrow}c
and
a\in{\downarrow}d
. In particular,
b\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}a
is completely join-prime and
b\to a
is completely meet-prime.

If
{\mathbf{L}}
is a Heyting algebra (or double Heyting algebra) and
u<v
in
{\mathbf{L}}
, then we denote the induced Heyting algebra (or double Heyting algebra) on the interval
[u,v]
by
{\mathbf{L}}_{uv}
.

Corollary 2.12
.

Assume that
{\mathbf{L}}
is a Heyting algebra in which the join-irreducible elements are join-dense or that
{\mathbf{L}}
is a double Heyting algebra, and let
u<v
in
{\mathbf{L}}
. For all
a,b\in[u,v]
with
a\prec b
, there exists a splitting pair
(c,d)
in
{\mathbf{L}}_{uv}
with
b\in\mathord{\uparrow}_{{\mathbf{L}}_{uv}}c
and
a\in{\downarrow}_{{\mathbf{L}}_{uv}}d
.

Proof.

Let
a,b\in[u,v]
with
a\prec b
. By Theorem
2.8
or Theorem
2.11
there exists a splitting pair
(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})
in
{\mathbf{L}}
with
b\in\mathord{\uparrow}_{\mathbf{L}}c^{\prime}
and
a\in{\downarrow}_{\mathbf{L}}d^{\prime}
. It is easy to check that
(c^{\prime}\vee u,d^{\prime}\wedge v)
is the required splitting pair in
{\mathbf{L}}_{uv}
.
∎

Remark 2.13
.

This corollary can be used (in the contrapositive) to show that an interval in a Heyting algebra or double Heyting algebra is
dense
, that is, contains no covers. For example, the homomorphism lattice
\mathcal{G}
of finite symmetric graphs forms a Heyting algebra in which the join-irreducibles (the finite connected graphs) are join dense. In fact, since every finite graph is the disjoint union of its connected components, an application of Lemma
2.9
shows that
\mathcal{G}
forms a double Heyting algebra. An easy application of a deep result of Erdős
[
10
]
shows that the interval in
\mathcal{G}
above its unique atom contains no splitting pair. Hence, by Corollary
2.12
, the interval in
\mathcal{G}
above the unique atom is dense – see Nešetřil and Tardif
[
24
]
and Nešetřil, Pultr and Tardif
[
23
]
.

3.
Splittings in a lattice of subvarieties

In this section we investigate the applicability of the results of the previous
section to the lattice
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
of subvarieties of a variety
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
. We will use, without further comment, the fact that
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
is always a dually algebraic lattice.
Splittings in subvariety lattices have been intensively studied since the foundational paper of McKenzie
[
22
]
. The following lemma is well known and follows easily from Lemma
2.3
and the facts that every variety is generated by its finitely generated subdirectly irreducible members and is the class of all models of a set of equations.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{A}},\boldsymbol{\mathscr{B}})
be a splitting pair in the lattice
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
of subvarieties of
some variety
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
. Then
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}=\Var({\mathbf{A}})
, for some finitely generated
subdirectly irreducible algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{B}}=\Mod(\Sigma\cup\{\varepsilon\})
, where
\Sigma
is a set of equations such that

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}=\Mod(\Sigma)
and
\varepsilon
is a single equation.

Resulting from this lemma (and Lemma
2.3
), a finitely generated subdirectly irreducible algebra in a variety
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
such that
\Var({\mathbf{A}})
is completely join-prime in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
is called a
splitting algebra
in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
.

Recall that a complete lattice underlies a Heyting algebra if and only if it satisfies the
join-infinite distributive law
(JID). We will use both this fact and its dual in the discussion below.

In order to apply Theorem
2.8
to the lattice
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
, we need
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
to form a Heyting algebra. In fact, in that case
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
will
form a double Heyting algebra and therefore Theorem
2.11
will also be applicable. Indeed, if
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
forms a Heyting algebra, then it will be a distributive and dually algebraic lattice, and consequently will satisfy the meet-infinite distributive law, whence it also forms a dual Heyting algebra. Before stating the characterisation of when
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
forms a Heyting algebra, we note that congruence distributivity is sufficient to guarantee that the join-irreducible subvarieties are join-dense in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
, a condition necessary to be able to apply Theorem
2.8
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
be a congruence-distributive variety.

(1)

\Var({\mathbf{A}})
is join-prime and therefore join-irreducible in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
, for every subdirectly irreducible algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
.

(2)

The join-irreducible elements are join-dense in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
.

Proof.

(1) follows from the fact that, for all subvarieties
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}_{2}
of
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
, a subdirectly irreducible algebra belongs to
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}_{1}\vee\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}_{2}
if and only if it belongs to either
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}_{1}
or
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}_{2}
(see Jónsson
[
16
, Lemma 4.1]
). Since every variety is generated by its subdirectly irreducible algebras, (2) is an immediate consequence of (1).
∎

We do not know of an intrinsic characterisation of varieties
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
such that
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})

forms a Heyting algebra (and therefore is a double Heyting algebra). Nevertheless, the following theorem gives a characterisation of when
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
forms a Heyting algebra, in terms of the lattice itself.

Other than for the inclusion of (1), the result is given in Davey
[
6
]
where it is derived as an immediate consequence of the characterisation of down-set lattices – see Davey
[
6
, Proposition 1.1]
and Davey and Priestley
[
8
, Theorem 10.29]
. A subset
Y
of an ordered set
{\mathbf{X}}
is a
down-set
if
{\downarrow}x\subseteq Y
, for all
x\in Y
.
We denote the lattice of all down-sets of
{\mathbf{X}}
by
\mathbf{Dn}({\mathbf{X}})
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
be a variety. The following are equivalent
:

(1)

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
forms a Heyting algebra
(
and therefore a double Heyting algebra
);

(2)

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
satisfies
(JID);

(3)

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
is distributive and algebraic
;

(4)

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
is completely distributive
;

(5)

every completely join-irreducible element of
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
is completely join-prime
;

(6)

the completely join-prime elements are join-dense in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
;

(7)

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
is isomorphic to
\mathbf{Dn}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}})
via
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{A}}\mapsto\{\,\boldsymbol{\mathscr{B}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathscr{B}}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\mathscr{A}}\,\}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}
is the ordered set of subvarieties of
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
that are completely join-prime in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
.

The following lemma gives a simple sufficient condition for
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
to be algebraic.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
be a locally finite variety. Then
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
is an algebraic lattice. A subvariety
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{A}}
of
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
is compact in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
if and only if it is finitely generated, that is,
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{A}}=\Var({\mathbf{A}})
for some finite algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
.

Proof.

Every variety is generated by its finitely generated members. Hence, since
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
is locally finite, every subvariety of
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
equals the join in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
of finitely generated subvarieties. Thus, it remains to prove that
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{A}}
is compact in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
if and only if it is finitely generated.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{A}}
is compact in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
. Since
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{A}}
is the join in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
of its finitely generated subvarieties, it follows that there are finitely many finite algebras
{\mathbf{A}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{A}}_{n}
in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{A}}
such that

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{A}}=\Var(\{{\mathbf{A}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{A}}_{n}\})=\Var({\mathbf{A}}_{1}\times\dots\times{\mathbf{A}}_{n}),

whence
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{A}}
is finitely generated. Now assume that
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{A}}=\Var({\mathbf{A}})
, for some finite algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
. We shall prove that
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{A}}
is compact in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
. Let
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}_{i}
be subvarieties of
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
, for
i\in I
, with
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{A}}\subseteq\bigvee_{i\in I}\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}_{i}
. Hence
{\mathbf{A}}\in\Var(\bigcup_{i\in I}\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}_{i})=\HSP(\bigcup_{i\in I}\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}_{i})
. As
{\mathbf{A}}
is finite, it follows that
{\mathbf{A}}
is a homomorphic image of a finitely generated (and therefore finite) subalgebra
{\mathbf{B}}
of a product
\prod_{s\in S}{\mathbf{A}}_{s}
, with
{\mathbf{A}}_{s}\in\bigcup_{i\in I}\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}_{i}
, for all
s\in S
. As
{\mathbf{B}}
is finite, there is a finite subset
T
of
S
such that
{\mathbf{B}}
embeds into
\prod_{t\in T}{\mathbf{A}}_{t}
. It follows at once that there is a finite subset
J
of
I
such that
{\mathbf{A}}\in\HSP(\bigcup_{j\in J}\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}_{j})
, whence
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{A}}\subseteq\bigvee_{j\in J}\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}_{j}
. Hence
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{A}}
is compact in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})
, as claimed.
∎

By combining Theorem

Theorem 3.5

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
be a locally finite congruence-distributive variety.

(1)

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{L}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}})

(2)

(ii)

ordered by

{\mathbf{A}}\sqsubseteq{\mathbf{B}}
if and only if

Proof.

We now prove (2). Assume (i); so

. Hence (iii) holds. The uniqueness claim is an easy consequence of Jónsson’s Lemma

4.
Splitting algebras in varieties of logic: the Non-splitting Lemma

By a
variety of logic
we mean any variety of algebras that forms
an algebraic semantics for some well-behaved logic.
We will not enter into details, but intuitively, we wish to
include all logics that have a conjunction, an equivalence, a truth constant, and
a unary (term-defined, possibly trivial) connective resembling a modal operator.

Thus, in this section we will work with a fixed ambient variety
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
of
algebras of finite signature
\tau
, such that there exist binary terms

\wedge
,
\leftrightarrow
, a unary term
\delta
, and a constant term
1
, whose
interpretations in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
have the following properties:

(P1)

\wedge
is a semilattice operation,

(P2)

x\leftrightarrow y\leqslant 1
, and
x\leftrightarrow y=1
if and only if
x=y
,

(P3)

\delta
is order preserving and satisfies
\delta x\leqslant x
,

(P4)

for each congruence
\vartheta
, the filter

\mathord{\uparrow}(1/\vartheta)
is closed under
\delta
,

(P5)

each filter closed under
\delta
and containing
1
is of the form

\mathord{\uparrow}(1/\vartheta)
for some congruence
\vartheta
.

Note that (P2) implies that
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
is congruence regular with respect to

1
, as we have
x\equiv_{\vartheta}y
if and only if

x\leftrightarrow y\equiv_{\vartheta}1
.
Moreover, (P2) implies that every non-trivial congruence
\vartheta
has
a\equiv_{\vartheta}1
for some
a<1
. Further, (P3) together with (P4) imply that
\delta 1=1
in every algebra
\mathbf{A}\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
.

The following lemma characterises finite subdirectly irreducible algebras in

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
. Given a unary operation
f
we denote its
n
-fold composite by
f^{n}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
be finite and subdirectly irreducible. Let
\mu

be its monolith. Then there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
each
a\in 1/\mu
with
a<1
we have
\delta^{n+1}a=\delta^{n}a
, and
\mathord{\uparrow}(1/\mu)=\mathord{\uparrow}(\delta^{n}a)
.

Proof.

Note that if
\delta a=a
for some
a<1
, then

\mathord{\uparrow}(a\wedge 1)
satisfies the conditions in (P5), and so it is
of the form
\mathord{\uparrow}(1/\vartheta)
for some congruence

\vartheta\geqslant\mu
. Let
F=\mathord{\uparrow}(1/\mu)
. By finiteness,
F
is principal, so
F=\mathord{\uparrow}b
. By (P2), (P3) and (P4) we get that
b<1
and
\delta b=b
.
Moreover,
b
is the only element in
F
with these properties. For if

b<a<1
and
\delta a=a
hold, then
\mathord{\uparrow}a=\mathord{\uparrow}(1/\alpha)
for some congruence

\alpha
with
0<\alpha<\mu
contradicting the fact that

\mu
is the monolith of
\mathbf{A}
. Thus, for every
a
with
b<a<1

we have
\delta a<a
, so by finiteness
\delta^{k}a=b=\delta^{k+1}a
for some
k
.
Also by finiteness, we can take
n\in\mathbb{N}
large enough to satisfy

\delta^{n}a=b=\delta^{n+1}a
for every
a
with
b<a<1
.
∎

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
be the subvariety of
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
defined by

\delta^{n+1}x=\delta^{n}x
. Every finite subdirectly irreducible algebra
\mathbf{A}\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
belongs to
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
for some

. We will use
\mu_{\bot}
to denote the smallest element of the filter

is the monolith of
\mathbf{A}

4.1.
Algebras describing themselves

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
be finite and subdirectly irreducible.
Fix a set
X
of variables with
|X|=|A|
, and index them by the elements of

. Define the
term-diagram
of
{\bf A}
to be the
|A|
-ary term

\Delta_{\bf A}=\bigwedge\{x_{f(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})}\leftrightarrow f(x_{a_{1}},\dots,x_{a_{n}})\mid a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in A,f\in\tau\}.

Throughout the paper, we will use
\mathbf{A}\leqslant\mathbf{B}
to indicate that
{\mathbf{A}}
embeds into
{\mathbf{B}}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
be algebras from
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
, with

\mathbf{A}
subdirectly irreducible and
|A|=k
. Then

\mathbf{A}\leqslant\mathbf{B}
if and only if there exists a

of elements of

\mathbf{B}\models(\Delta_{\mathbf{A}}\approx 1)[\overline{b}]
and

\mathbf{B}\models(x_{\mu_{\bot}}\not\approx 1)[\overline{b}]

Proof.

For the forward direction,
define
\overline{b}
putting
b_{a}=a
for every

.
Clearly
x_{\mu_{\bot}}[\overline{b}]=b_{\mu_{\bot}}\neq 1
, so

\mathbf{B}\models(x_{\mu_{\bot}}\not\approx 1)[\overline{b}]
. Moreover,
for each

\displaystyle(x_{f(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})}\leftrightarrow f(x_{a_{1}},\dots,x_{a_{n}}))[\overline{b}]

\displaystyle=b_{f(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})}\leftrightarrow f(b_{a_{1}},\dots,b_{a_{n}})

\displaystyle=f(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\leftrightarrow f(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=1.

Hence,
\mathbf{B}\models(\Delta_{\mathbf{A}}\approx 1)[\overline{b}]
.

For the converse, let

be a

Non-splitting Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
be finite and subdirectly irreducible.
The following are equivalent

(1)

\mathbf{A}
is not a splitting algebra in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}

(2)

\forall i\in\mathbb{N}\;\exists{\bf B}\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}\colon\mathbf{A}\not\in\Var(\mathbf{B})

\mathbf{B}\not\models\delta^{k}(\Delta_{\mathbf{A}})\leqslant x_{\mu_{\bot}}

Proof.

To prove the implication from (2) to (1) take algebras

, such that

\mathbf{B}_{i}\not\models\delta^{i}(\Delta_{\mathbf{A}})\leqslant x_{\mu_{\bot}}

\delta^{i}(\Delta_{\mathbf{A}})[b(i)_{1},\dots,b(i)_{k-1}]\not\leqslant s(i)\ \text{in $\mathbf{B}_{i}$}.

We will now show that

5.
Residuated lattices

A
residuated lattice
is an algebra

\mathbf{A}=\langle A;\wedge,\vee,\backslash,/,\cdot,1\rangle

such that
\langle A;\wedge,\vee\rangle
is a lattice, and

\langle A;\cdot,\backslash,/,1\rangle
is a residuated monoid, that is, an ordered
monoid satisfying

y\leqslant x\backslash z\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad xy\leqslant z\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\leqslant z/y.

The operations
\backslash
and
/
are called, respectively,

left division
(or
right residuation
) and

right division
(or
left residuation
).
Multiplication binds stronger than divisions, which
bind stronger than the lattice operations.
The following identities will be important later.

(1)

1\geqslant x
,

(2)

xy=yx
,

(3)

x^{n+1}=x^{n}
,

A residuated lattice satisfying (1), (2), or (3) is called

integral
,
commutative
, or
n
-potent
, respectively.
We write
\mathsf{RL}
for the variety of all residuated lattices, and

\mathsf{IRL}
,
\mathsf{CRL}
,
\mathsf{CIRL}
, respectively, for
the varieties of integral, commutative, and commutative integral residuated
lattices. In the commutative case, the left and right residuals become
opposites, for we have
x\backslash y=y/x
. It is then customary to blur the
distinction between then and write write
x\rightarrow y
for both.
For a residuated lattice
\mathbf{A}
, an element
a\in A
is called
negative

if
a\leqslant 1
, and
strictly negative
if
a<1
;
(
strictly
)
positive

elements are defined dually. If a residuated lattice
\mathbf{A}
has a unique largest strictly
negative element, then
\mathbf{A}
is subdirectly irreducible. For commutative
residuated lattices the converse is also true.

For more details on residuated lattices, and for any unexplained
nomenclature, we refer the reader to Galatos
et al.

[
14
]
.
Residuated lattices expanded by a constant
0
, are known as FL-algebras
(especially among logicians, because of the connection with
Full Lambek
calculus
). In older literature, the name ‘residuated lattices’ was used for
what is now called FL
ew
-algebras: a subvariety of FL-algebras consisting
of commutative, integral FL-algebras satisfying
0\leqslant x
. This was the
terminology used in Kowalski and Ono
[
19
]
, for example.

The variety
\mathsf{CRL}
of commutative residuated lattices
satisfies (P1)–(P5), with
x\leftrightarrow y=(x\rightarrow y)\wedge(y\rightarrow x)\wedge 1
and

\delta x=x^{2}
, so the Non-splitting Lemma

applies in principle. To apply it in practice, we need two constructions given below. Each will be given in a rather general form, with a view to possible applications in a wider class of residuated lattices. However, the generality will be somewhat
evasive, as the constructions seem to generalise in incompatible ways.

5.1.
Expansions of commutative residuated lattices

\displaystyle x\in A,y=d_{a}\in D

\displaystyle\text{ and }x\leqslant^{\mathbf{A}}ca,

\displaystyle x=d_{a},y=d_{b}\in D

\displaystyle\text{ and }a\leqslant^{\mathbf{A}}b.

Intuitively, we insert a new element between each pair

in such a way
that

holds. In particular,
c<d_{1}<1
.

It is not difficult to show that the relation
\leqslant
defined above is an
order on
P
. Next, for all
x,y\in P
, we put:

x\cdot y=y\cdot x=\begin{cases}xy&\text{ if }x,y\in A,\cr d_{ay}&\text{ if }x=d_{a}\in D,\ y\in A,\ cay<ay,\cr ay&\text{ if }x=d_{a}\in D,\ y\in A,\ cay=ay,\cr cab&\text{ if }x=d_{a}\in D,\ y=d_{b}\in D.\end{cases}

Lemma 5.1
.

The structure
\mathbf{P}=\langle P;\leqslant,\cdot,1\rangle
is
an ordered commutative monoid. Moreover, if
\mathbf{A}
is integral, then

x\leqslant 1
holds for all

x\in P
.

Proof.

The main part of the proof is a tedious case-checking exercise, most of which we
omit, especially that it is nearly identical to the proof of Fact 4 in Kowalski
and Ono
[
19
]
. Here is one case
as an example. Let
x=d_{a}
,
y\in A
, and let
z=d_{b}
. Assume moreover that

cay<ay
and
cyb=yb
. Then we have
(d_{a}\cdot y)\cdot d_{b}=d_{ay}\cdot d_{b}=cayb
, but observe that
cayb=ayb
since
cyb=yb
.
Next, associating the other way we obtain
d_{a}\cdot(y\cdot d_{b})=d_{a}\cdot yb=ayb
, as
cayb=ayb
.

The moreover part follows immediately from the construction of
\mathbf{P}
.
∎

The next lemma shows that every element of
P
either
belongs to
A
or is of the form
d_{1}\cdot a
, for some
a\in A
.
We will write
d
instead of
d_{1}
from now on.

Lemma 5.2
.

For all
x,y\in A
, the following hold
:

(1)

if

, then
d\cdot x=d_{x}
, otherwise
d\cdot x=x
,

(2)

if and only if

,

(3)

d\cdot x\cdot d\cdot y=cxy
,

(4)

if and only if

.

Proof.

All claims are easily derived from the definition of multiplication in
\mathbf{P}
.
∎

Although
\mathbf{P}
is in general neither a residuated monoid, nor a lattice,
it will be convenient to view it as a partial algebra in the signature of
residuated lattices, with meet, join, and the residual only partially defined.
This makes the statement of the next lemma clear.

Lemma 5.3
.

The residuated lattice
\mathbf{A}
is a subalgebra of
\mathbf{P}
.

Proof.

The proofs of preservation of meet, join and multiplication from
\mathbf{A}
are
straightforward, so we will only show that

satisfies

\forall x\in P\colon\;a\cdot x\leqslant b\Longleftrightarrow x\leqslant a\rightarrow^{\mathbf{A}}b

in
\mathbf{P}
, for all
a,b\in A
. This equivalence clearly holds for
all
x\in A
,
by residuation in
A
. Let
x=d_{s}
for some
s\in A
. Then
x=d\cdot s
,
so we have
a\cdot x=a\cdot d\cdot s=d\cdot a\cdot s
, and thus

\displaystyle a\cdot x=a\cdot d\cdot s\leqslant b

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow d\cdot as\leqslant b

\displaystyle(\text{since $a\cdot s=as$})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow as\leqslant b

\displaystyle(\text{by Lemma~\ref{multiplication-properties}(4)})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow s\leqslant a\rightarrow^{\mathbf{A}}b

\displaystyle(\text{by residuation in $\mathbf{A}$})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow d\cdot s\leqslant a\rightarrow^{\mathbf{A}}b

\displaystyle(\text{by Lemma~\ref{multiplication-properties}(4)}).\qed

Next, we will expand
\mathbf{P}
to a residuated lattice. To this end, we will
use a version of

, defined and put to good use
in Galatos and Jipsen
[

]
.

Let
\mathbf{M}=\langle M;\leqslant,\cdot,1\rangle
be a commutative ordered monoid,
and
W
a set. A binary relation
N\subseteq M\times W
is called
a
nuclear relation on

\mathbf{M}
if, for every

and

there exists a subset

of

such that for every

the following equivalence holds

x\cdot y\ N\ w\quad\text{ if and only if }\quad y\ N\ x\Rightarrow w

where
y\ N\ x\Rightarrow w
abbreviates

for all
u\in x\Rightarrow w
.
The importance of being nuclear resides in the fact that every nuclear
relation
N
on
\mathbf{M}
gives rise to a residuated lattice which
preserves all partial residuated lattice structure that exists in
\mathbf{M}
.

Lemma 5.4

(
[
13
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}=\langle M;\leqslant,\cdot,1\rangle

be an ordered monoid. Let
W
be any set, and let

be nuclear. Further, let

\gamma_{N}
be the closure operator on

associated with the polarities of

.
Then the complete lattice

of closed subsets of

carries a residuated lattice structure

\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{M}]=\langle L[M];\wedge,\vee,\cdot,\rightarrow,1\rangle
, such that

(1)

The operations in
\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{M}]
are given by:

•

X\wedge Y=X\cap Y
,

•

X\vee Y=\gamma_{N}(X\cup Y)
,

•

X\cdot Y=\gamma_{N}\{x\cdot^{\mathbf{M}}y\mid x\in X,\ y\in Y\}
,

•

X\rightarrow Y=\{z\in M\mid\forall x\in X\colon z\cdot x\in Y\}
,

•

1=\gamma_{N}(1^{\mathbf{M}})
,

for all closed
X,Y\subseteq M
.

(2)

\mathbf{M}
embeds into
\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{M}]
as an ordered monoid.

(3)

If
\mathbf{M}
is integral, so is
\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{M}]
.

(4)

If
\mathbf{M}
is commutative, so is
\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{M}]
.

(5)

If
\mathbf{M}
is finite, so is
\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{M}]
.

(6)

The embedding preserves all existing meets, joins and residuals from

\mathbf{M}
.

We will now exhibit a suitable nuclear relation on
\mathbf{P}
.
For each

define
\lambda_{x}\colon P\to P
by

\lambda_{x}(y)=x\cdot y
. Let

and

. Define a binary relation

putting

if
\lambda(x)\leqslant a
. Next, for
x\in P
,
\lambda\in\Lambda
and

define

to be the singleton

\{(\lambda_{x}\circ\lambda,a)\}
. Then we have

\displaystyle x\cdot y\ N\ (\lambda,a)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\lambda(x\cdot y)\leqslant a

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\lambda\circ\lambda_{x}(y)\leqslant a

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow y\ N\ (\lambda\circ\lambda_{x},a)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow y\ N\ x\Rightarrow(\lambda,a)

and so

is nuclear, as claimed. The next result is an immediate corollary.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{P}=\langle P;\leqslant,\cdot,1\rangle

be as in
Lemma
5.1
, and let
N
be the nuclear relation defined above.
Then
\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{P}]
is a residuated lattice such that

{\mathbf{A}\leqslant\mathbf{P}\leqslant\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{P}]}
.

From now until Lemma
5.9
, we will keep
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{P}
and

\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{P}]
fixed. To proceed, we need to
describe the elements of
\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{P}]
(the closed sets of

\mathbf{P}
) more concretely. To lighten the notation, we put

\widehat{X}=\bigvee\{x\in A\mid x\in X\}
and

\widetilde{X}=\bigvee\{x\in A\mid d\cdot x\in X\}
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
X
be a subset of
P
satisfying the following conditions
:

(1)

X
is a non-empty down-set,

(2)

\forall x,y\in A\colon x\in X
and
y\in X

x\vee y\in X
,

(3)

\forall x,y\in A\colon d\cdot x\in X
and
d\cdot y\in X
imply

d\cdot(x\vee y)\in X
.

Then
X=\mathord{\downarrow}\widehat{X}\cup\mathord{\downarrow}(d\cdot\widetilde{X})
.

Proof.

Assume
X\subseteq P
satisfies (1)–(3). By the finiteness of
P
,
we have
\widehat{X}\in X
and
d\cdot\widetilde{X}\in X
, so

\mathord{\downarrow}\widehat{X}\cup\mathord{\downarrow}(d\cdot\widetilde{X})\subseteq X
, since
X
is a down-set.
Let
x\in X
. If
x\in A
, then
x\leqslant\widehat{X}
. If
x=d_{a}\in D
, then

a\leqslant\widetilde{X}
, so
x=d\cdot a\leqslant d\cdot\widetilde{X}
. Thus,

X=\mathord{\downarrow}\widehat{X}\cup\mathord{\downarrow}(d\cdot\widetilde{X})
.
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

Every closed
X\subseteq P
is of the form:

X=\mathord{\downarrow}\widehat{X}\cup\mathord{\downarrow}(d\cdot\widetilde{X}).

Moreover, for every
a\in A
, the sets
\mathord{\downarrow}a
and
\mathord{\downarrow}d\cdot a
are both closed.

Proof.

Assume
X
is closed. We will show that
X
satisfies (1)–(3)
of Lemma
5.6
.
Note that these conditions are preserved by intersections; for (2) and (3) it is
immediate, for (1) it follows from the fact that
{\mathbf{P}}
has the smallest
element. Thus, it suffices to prove that (1)–(3) hold for basic
closed sets, that is, sets of the form
\{x\in P\mid x\ N\ (\lambda_{u},s)\}
for some
u\in P
,
s\in A
. We will use the standard
notation for basic closed sets, writing
X^{\triangleleft}
for

\{z\mid\forall x\in X\colon z\ N\ x\}
, and simplifying

\{x\}^{\triangleleft}
to
x^{\triangleleft}
.

Let
\bot
be the smallest element of
{\mathbf{P}}
.
We claim that
\bot\in(\lambda,s)^{\triangleleft}
. Indeed,
we have
\bot\in(\lambda_{u},s)^{\triangleleft}
if and only if

u\cdot\bot\leqslant s
, and this holds for all
u\in P
and
s\in A
.
Next, let
x\in(\lambda_{u},s)^{\triangleleft}

and
y\leqslant x
. Then we have
u\cdot x\leqslant s
, so by the
monotonicity of multiplication in
\mathbf{P}
, we get

u\cdot y\leqslant s
, and thus
y\ N\ (\lambda_{u},s)
. This proves (1).

Now let
x,y\in(\lambda_{u},s)^{\triangleleft}
. Then

u\cdot x\leqslant s
and
u\cdot y\leqslant s
. If
u\in A
, we get

ux\vee uy=u(x\vee y)\leqslant s=u\cdot(x\vee y)
, so

x\vee y\in(\lambda_{u},s)^{\triangleleft}
. If
u=d_{a}\in D
, then
from
u\cdot x\leqslant s
and
u\cdot y\leqslant s
we get

ax\leqslant s
and
ay\leqslant s
, so reasoning as before we get
a\cdot(x\vee y)\leqslant s
, and hence
x\vee y\in(\lambda_{u},s)^{\triangleleft}
again. This proves (2).

Next, let
d\cdot x,d\cdot y\in(\lambda_{u},s)^{\triangleleft}
. Then

u\cdot d\cdot x\leqslant s
and
u\cdot d\cdot y\leqslant s
. If
u\in A
, these imply

d\cdot ux\leqslant s
and
d\cdot uy\leqslant s
, and further

ux\leqslant s
and
uy\leqslant s
. These hold if and only if
u(x\vee y)\leqslant s
, from
which it follows that
d\cdot u\cdot(x\vee y)\leqslant s
. Since

d\cdot u\cdot(x\vee y)=u\cdot d\cdot(x\vee y)
, we have that

d\cdot(x\vee y)\in(\lambda_{u},s)^{\triangleleft}
.
If
u=d_{a}\in D
, we obtain

d\cdot d\cdot a\cdot x\leqslant s
and
d\cdot d\cdot a\cdot y\leqslant s
; therefore,

cax\leqslant s
and
cay\leqslant s
. This holds if and only if

ca(x\vee y)\leqslant s
, which implies
d_{a}\cdot d\cdot(x\vee y)\leqslant s
, which
in turn implies
d\cdot(x\vee y)\in(\lambda_{u},s)^{\triangleleft}
.
This proves (3).

It remains to show that
\mathord{\downarrow}a
and
\mathord{\downarrow}(d\cdot a)
are closed.
For
\mathord{\downarrow}a
consider
Z=\bigcap\{(\lambda_{u},s)^{\triangleleft}\mid u\cdot a\leqslant s\}
.
For every
z\leqslant a
, we have
z\in Z
, by monotonicity of
multiplication. For the converse, note that the basic set

(\lambda_{1},a)^{\triangleleft}
is a member of

\{(\lambda_{u},s)^{\triangleleft}\mid u\cdot a\leqslant s\}
, so

z\in Z
implies
z\in(\lambda_{1},a)^{\triangleleft}
, that is

1\cdot z\leqslant a
. Thus,
\mathord{\downarrow}a=Z
.

For
\mathord{\downarrow}(d\cdot a)
consider

Z^{\prime}=\bigcap\{(\lambda_{u},s)^{\triangleleft}\mid u\cdot d\cdot a\leqslant s\}
.
For every
z\leqslant d\cdot a
we have
z\in Z^{\prime}
, as before. For the converse,
note that
(\lambda_{1},a)^{\triangleleft}
and
(\lambda_{d},ca)^{\triangleleft}
are members of

\{(\lambda_{u},s)^{\triangleleft}\mid u\cdot d\cdot a\leqslant s\}
. If

z\in Z\cap A
, then since
z\in(\lambda_{d},ca)^{\triangleleft}
, we have

d\cdot z\leqslant ca
, and so
z\leqslant ca
; therefore
z\leqslant d\cdot a
.
If
z\in Z\cap D
, say
z=d_{b}
for some
b\in A
, then
since
z\in(\lambda_{1},a)^{\triangleleft}
, we have

d\cdot b\leqslant a
, and so
b\leqslant a
; hence
d\cdot b\leqslant d\cdot a
.
∎

Let
\mathbf{L}
be a subdirectly irreducible commutative residuated lattice,
let
\mu
be the monolith of
\mathbf{L}
, and let
F_{\mu}=\mathord{\uparrow}(1/\mu)
.
We define the

of
\mu
to be the least

such that,
for all

with

, we have
a^{n+1}=a^{n}
. If no such
n
exists,
the the depth of
\mu
is undefined.

Lemma 5.8
.

Assume
\mathbf{A}
is subdirectly irreducible with monolith
\mu
of depth
n
,
and
c\prec 1
is the unique largest strictly negative element of
A
.
The following hold
:

(1)

\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{P}]
is subdirectly irreducible.

(2)

The monolith
\nu
of
\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{P}]
has depth
at least
2n
.

(3)

\mu=\nu{\upharpoonright_{\mathbf{A}}}
.

(4)

\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{P}]/\nu
is isomorphic to
\mathbf{A}/\mu
.

Proof.

For (1), note
that
\mathord{\downarrow}d
is a closed set whose unique cover in the inclusion ordering
is
\mathord{\downarrow}1=P
. Claim (2) follows by construction. To see it, note that

d^{2k}=c^{k}
, for all
k
, so if
k<n
we have

d^{2k+1}=d\cdot d^{2k}=d\cdot c^{k}<c^{k}
, since
c^{k+1}<c^{k}
.
For
k=n
, we have
d^{2n+1}=d\cdot d^{2n}=d\cdot c^{n}=c^{n}
, since
cc^{n}=c^{n+1}=c^{n}
. As multiplication is preserved in

\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{P}]
, we have
(\mathord{\downarrow}d)^{2n-1}>(\mathord{\downarrow}d)^{2n}=(\mathord{\downarrow}d)^{2n+1}
,
whence the monolith of
\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{P}]
has depth at least
2n
.
Next, (3) follows from (2) using the fact that
\mathbf{A}\leqslant\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{P}]
.
To prove (4), we begin by showing that

X\equiv_{\nu}\mathord{\downarrow}\widehat{X}
holds for every closed

X\subseteq P
. For each
closed
X\subseteq P
, by Lemma
5.7
, we have

X=\mathord{\downarrow}\widehat{X}\cup\mathord{\downarrow}(d\cdot\widetilde{X})
,
so
\mathord{\downarrow}\widehat{X}\subseteq X
, and thus
\mathord{\downarrow}\widehat{X}\rightarrow X=P=1^{L[P]}
.
Now, since
\nu=\mathbf{Cg}^{\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{P}]}(\mathord{\downarrow}d,P)
,
it suffices to show that
\mathord{\downarrow}d\subseteq X\rightarrow\mathord{\downarrow}\widehat{X}
. This is further
equivalent to
d\in X\rightarrow\mathord{\downarrow}\widehat{X}
. Let
x\in X
and consider

d\cdot x
. If
x\leqslant\widehat{X}
, we have
d\cdot x\leqslant x\leqslant\widehat{X}
, so

d\cdot x\in\mathord{\downarrow}\widehat{X}
. Assume

, so that

. In particular,

, so

for some

. Then we get

and thus

d\cdot x=c\cdot b\leqslant c\cdot\bigvee\{y\in A\mid d\cdot y\in X\}=\bigvee\{cy\in A\mid d\cdot y\in X\}.

But

as required.
We have shown that

holds for

. It follows that we have
\mathord{\downarrow}\widehat{X}\in X/\nu
, from
which it follows in turn that

contains (an image of) an element of

.
This proves (4).
∎

5.2.
Truncated products of residuated lattices

Let
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}
be residuated lattices, and let
c
and
q
be strictly
negative elements of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
, respectively. The
truncated product

\mathbf{A}\odot\mathbf{B}
of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
is the algebra with the universe

A\odot B=\{a\in A\mid a\leqslant c\}\times\{b\in B\mid b\leqslant q\}\cup\{(1,1)\}

and operations defined below.

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=(1,1),

\displaystyle(a,i)\wedge(b,j)

\displaystyle=(a\wedge b,i\wedge j),

\displaystyle(a,i)\vee(b,j)

\displaystyle=(a\vee b,i\vee j),

\displaystyle(a,i)\cdot(b,j)

\displaystyle=(a\cdot b,i\cdot j),

\displaystyle(a,i)\backslash(b,j)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(a\backslash b\wedge c,q)&\text{ if }a\not\leqslant b,\ i\leqslant j,\\
(c,i\backslash j\wedge q)&\text{ if }a\leqslant b,\ i\not\leqslant j,\\
(a\backslash b\wedge c,i\backslash j\wedge q)&\text{ if }a\not\leqslant b,\ i\not\leqslant j,\\
(1,1)&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

\displaystyle(a,i)/(b,j)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(a/b\wedge c,q)&\text{ if }a\not\leqslant b,\ i\leqslant j,\\
(c,i/j\wedge q)&\text{ if }a\leqslant b,\ i\not\leqslant j,\\
(a/b\wedge c,i/j\wedge q)&\text{ if }a\not\leqslant b,\ i\not\leqslant j,\\
(1,1)&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Until the end of this subsection we will keep

\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}
,
c
and
q
fixed.

Lemma 5.10
.

\mathbf{A}\odot\mathbf{B}
is a subdirectly irreducible integral residuated
lattice.

Proof.

Note that
A\odot B
, viewed
as a subset of
A\times B
is closed under meet, join, and multiplication.
Thus,
\mathbf{A}\odot\mathbf{B}
is a lattice ordered commutative monoid; in fact,
it is a lattice ordered submonoid of the the direct product
\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}
.
Next, since
\backslash
and
/
are defined symmetrically, it suffices to
verify residuation equivalences for one of these. Moreover, the third case in the
definition of
\backslash
is precisely what it would be
in the direct product, so only two first cases remain.

Let
a\not\leqslant b
and
i\leqslant j
, so that
(a,i)\backslash(b,j)=(a\backslash b\wedge c,q)
.
Note that we must have
j<1
. For all
(s,k)
, we have

(a,i)\cdot(s,k)=(a\cdot s,i\cdot k)\leqslant(b,j)
if
and only if

a\cdot s\leqslant b
and
k\cdot i\leqslant j
. Therefore

s\leqslant a\backslash b
and since
a\backslash b<1
we have
(s,k)<1
, so

s\leqslant c
and
k\leqslant q
.
Thus,
(s,k)\leqslant(a\backslash b\wedge c,q)
.
Conversely,
(s,k)\leqslant(a\backslash b\wedge c,q)
if and only
if
s\leqslant a\backslash b\wedge c
and
k<1
, which implies
a\cdot s\leqslant b
and
i\cdot k\leqslant i\leqslant j
. The case
a\leqslant b
and
i\not\leqslant j
is symmetric.

To show that
\mathbf{A}\odot\mathbf{B}
is subdirectly irreducible it suffices to note that

(c,q)
is its unique coatom.
∎

If
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
are themselves integral and have unique coatoms,
all nontrivial quotients of
\mathbf{A}\odot\mathbf{B}
coincide with quotients of

\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}
.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}
be integral residuated lattices, with unique coatoms

,

, respectively. Let
\alpha
be a
non-trivial congruence on
\mathbf{A}\odot\mathbf{B}

on
\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}
that extends
\alpha

(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B})/\alpha^{\prime}\cong(\mathbf{A}\odot\mathbf{B})/\alpha

\alpha^{\prime}=(\rho_{1}\vee\alpha^{\prime})\times(\rho_{2}\vee\alpha^{\prime})
, where
\rho_{1}
,
\rho_{2}
are
the kernels of the respective projection homomorphisms.

Proof.

Let
F
be the filter of
\mathbf{A}\odot\mathbf{B}
corresponding to the congruence
\alpha
.
Note that the ordered monoid reduct of
\mathbf{A}\odot\mathbf{B}
is a subalgebra
of the ordered monoid reduct of
\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}
.
Consider
\mathord{\uparrow}F
taken in
{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}
. We have

\mathord{\uparrow}F=F\cup\{(x,1)\in A\times B\mid\exists y\in B\colon(x,y)\in F\}\\
\cup\{(1,y)\in A\times B\mid\exists x\in A\colon(x,y)\in F\}.

Then
\mathord{\uparrow}F
is a filter on
\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}
, and it is also closed under
multiplication. Let
\alpha^{\prime}
be the congruence on

\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}
determined by
\mathord{\uparrow}F
. Then
\alpha^{\prime}{\upharpoonright_{A\odot B}}=\alpha
,
so
\alpha^{\prime}
extends
\alpha
.

As
\alpha
is non-trivial we have
((c,q),(1,1))\in\alpha
,
whence

((c,1),(1,1))\in\alpha^{\prime}
and

((1,q),(1,1))\in\alpha^{\prime}
.
Therefore,
((c,i),(1,i))\in\alpha^{\prime}
and

((a,q),(a,1))\in\alpha^{\prime}
for all

i\in B
,
a\in A
.
It follows that every congruence class of
\alpha^{\prime}
contains
a representative from
A\odot B
, so the natural map

a/\alpha\mapsto a/\alpha^{\prime}
is bijective.
Inspecting the definitions of the operations in
\mathbf{A}\odot\mathbf{B}

we see that the map above is also a homomorphism.
The moreover part follows from congruence distributivity.
∎

Truncated products of integral residuated lattices with unique coatoms commute
with ultrapowers. In will be important in subsection
5.4
below.

Lemma 5.12
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}
be as in Lemma
5.11
. Then,
any ultrapower
(\mathbf{A}\odot\mathbf{B})^{I}/U
is isomorphic to

\mathbf{A}^{I}/U\odot\mathbf{B}^{I}/U
.

Proof.

This is a slight variation of the standard proof of the fact that ultraproducts commute with finite products. The reader is asked to verify that the map

\bigl((a_{i}\mid i\in I)/U,(b_{i}\mid i\in I)/U\bigr)\mapsto\bigl((a_{i},b_{i})_{i}\mid i\in I\bigr)/U

is the required isomorphism. The only differences from the standard proof for
products are: (i) the condition that
a_{i}=1
if and only if
b_{i}=1
, and
(ii) the definition of residuation.
∎

5.3.
No splittings: algebras with at least three elements

Now, let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite subdirectly irreducible commutative integral
residuated lattice, with at least three elements and with monolith
\mu
of depth

n
. We shall apply the Non-splitting Lemma
4.3
to prove that
{\mathbf{A}}

does not split the subvariety lattice of
\mathsf{CIRL}
.
Since
\delta x=x^{2}
,

\mathbf{B}\not\models\delta^{k}(\Delta_{\mathbf{A}})\leqslant x_{\mu_{\bot}}\quad\text{becomes}\quad\mathbf{B}\not\models(\Delta_{\mathbf{A}})^{2k}\leqslant x_{\mu_{\bot}}.

Let
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
let
\mathbf{E}
be the expansion of
\mathbf{A}

of even depth
m=2k
with
k\geqslant i
and
m>n
.
For the distortion part of the construction, we will make use of
Wajsberg
hoops
(see Blok and Ferreirim
[
3
]
for more on hoops).
Recall that a Wajsberg hoop
\mathbf{C}_{n}
is the commutative lattice-ordered
monoid on the universe
\{0,-1,\dots,-n+1\}
, with truncated addition and
with residuation defined naturally by

i\rightarrow j=\max\{0,i-j\}
. For consistency with previous notation (and
tradition) we will present Wajsberg hoops multiplicatively, defining

q^{i}=-i
, so that
1=q^{0}=0
,
q=q^{1}=-1
, and so on. Clearly,

q
is the unique coatom of
\mathbf{C}_{n}
.

Now, for the first prime
p
with
p\geqslant|E|
, consider
\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
.
Note that
\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
is strictly simple, and has
p+1
elements.
Form
\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
with
c
and
q
chosen to be the unique coatoms
of
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
, respectively.
In the next three lemmas we show that

{\mathbf{A}}\notin\Var(\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1})
and

\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1}\not\models(\Delta_{\mathbf{A}})^{m}\leqslant x_{\mu_{\bot}}
,
hence establishing Condition (3) of the Non-splitting
Lemma
4.3
with
{\mathbf{B}}:=\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
.

Note that since
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
are commutative
and integral, and
c
and
q
are their largest strictly negative elements, the definitions of residuals in
\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
simplify to:

\displaystyle(a,i)\rightarrow(b,j)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(a\rightarrow b,q)&\text{ if }a\not\leqslant b,\ i\leqslant j,\\
(c,i\rightarrow j)&\text{ if }a\leqslant b,\ i\not\leqslant j,\\
(a\rightarrow b,i\rightarrow j)&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Lemma 5.13
.

For each non-trivial congruence
\vartheta
on
\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
,
the quotient algebra
(\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1})/\vartheta
is isomorphic to a proper
homomorphic image of
\mathbf{A}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.11
we have that
(\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1})/\alpha
is
isomorphic to
(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{C}_{p+1})/\alpha^{\prime}
, where
\alpha^{\prime}
is the
natural extension of
\alpha
. Since
\alpha
is non-trivial, we have

(d,q)\in\alpha^{\prime}
, and so

\alpha^{\prime}=\beta\times\gamma
for some congruences

\beta
on
\mathbf{E}
and
\gamma
on
\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
such that

(d,1)\in\beta
and
(q,1)\in\gamma
. But
\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
is simple, so

\gamma
is the full congruence on
\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
, and therefore

\alpha^{\prime}=\beta\times 1
, that is,
\alpha^{\prime}
properly contains the projection
onto the first coordinate. Since
\beta
is non-trivial, the claim follows.
∎

Lemma 5.14
.

If
\mathbf{A}
is not isomorphic to
\mathbf{2}
, then

\mathbf{A}\not\in\Var(\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1})
.

Proof.

By Jónsson’s Lemma, the congruence extension property and finiteness, we have
that if
\mathbf{A}\in\Var(\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1})
, then

\mathbf{A}\in\SH(\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1})
.
By Lemma
5.13
, for each non-trivial congruence
\vartheta

on
\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
we have
|E\odot C_{p+1}/\theta|<|A|
, and
thus
\mathbf{A}\notin\SH^{+}(\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1})
, where

\mathbb{H}^{+}
stands for nontrivial homomorphic images.

It follows that there is an embedding
e\colon\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
of
\mathbf{A}
into
\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
. We will derive a contradiction from this. First we shall show that
\pi_{2}\circ e\colon{\mathbf{A}}\to\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
is an embedding, where
\pi_{2}\colon\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1}\to\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
is the restriction of the second projection.
Suppose that
\pi_{2}\circ e
is not an embedding; then there exist
a,b\in A
with
a\not\leqslant b
such that
e(a)=(v,i)
and
e(b)=(u,i)
. Then
a\rightarrow b\neq 1
and so

(v,i)\rightarrow(u,i)\neq(1,1)
and
v\not\leqslant u
, so

e(a\rightarrow b)=(v,i)\rightarrow(u,i)=(v\rightarrow u,q)
.
Put
w=v\rightarrow u
. Consider the chain of powers of

\big\{(w,q)^{s}\mid 1\leqslant s\leqslant p\big\}
.
By definition,
(w,q)^{s}=(w^{s},q^{s})
, and
since
q,q^{2},\dots,q^{p}
are all distinct, this chain has
p
distinct
elements and so the element
a\rightarrow b
generates
p
distinct
elements in
\mathbf{A}
. But
p\geqslant|E|>|A|
so this is a contradiction.
It follows that
\pi_{2}\circ e\colon{\mathbf{A}}\to\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
is an embedding.

Since
\mathbf{A}
has at least three elements, the subalgebra
(\pi_{2}\circ e)({\mathbf{A}})
of
\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
contains a non-zero and non-unit element. But in
\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
each non-zero and non-unit element generates the whole algebra, so
(\pi_{2}\circ e)({\mathbf{A}})=\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
, whence
\pi_{2}\circ e\colon{\mathbf{A}}\to\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
is an isomorphism. This gives
|A|=p+1
, contradicting the fact that
|A|<p
.
∎

Lemma 5.15
.

\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1}\not\models(\Delta_{\mathbf{A}})^{m}\leqslant x_{\mu_{\bot}}
.

Proof.

Define an
|A|
-tuple
\overline{w}
putting

w_{1}=(1,1)
and
w_{a}=(a,q)
, if

a\neq 1
. Note that
w_{\mu_{\bot}}=(\mu_{\bot},q)
.
For
\diamond\in\{\vee,\wedge,\rightarrow\}
, we have

w_{a\diamond b}\leftrightarrow w_{a}\diamond w_{b}=(a\diamond b,q)\leftrightarrow(a,q)\diamond(b,q)=(a\diamond b,q)\leftrightarrow(a\diamond b,q)=(1,1).

In the case of multiplication, we have:

w_{a\cdot b}\leftrightarrow w_{a}\cdot w_{b}=(a\cdot b,q)\leftrightarrow(a,q)\cdot(b,q)=(a\cdot b,q)\leftrightarrow(a\cdot b,q\cdot q)\\
=(a\cdot b,q)\rightarrow(a\cdot b,q^{2})=(d,q\rightarrow q^{2})=(d,q).

It follows that
\Delta_{\mathbf{A}}[\overline{w}]=(d,q)
.
Recall that the depth of the monolith of
\mathbf{E}
is
m
. We then reason as follows.
First, the lattice operations and multiplication
in
{\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1}}
coincide with the operations in the direct product

\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{C}_{p+1}
. Thus, for each
k
with
1<k<m
,
we have
(d,q)^{k}=(d^{k},q^{k})
.
Since
k<m<|E|\leqslant p
, we have that
(d^{k},q^{k})\not\leqslant(d^{m},q)=(\mu_{\bot},q)
. Hence,

(\Delta_{\mathbf{A}})^{k}[\overline{w}]\not\leqslant w_{\mu_{\bot}}
, that is,

{\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1}\not\models(\Delta_{\mathbf{A}})^{k}\leqslant x_{\mu_{\bot}}}
for all

k\leqslant m
. In particular,
\mathbf{E}\odot\mathbf{C}_{p+1}\not\models(\Delta_{\mathbf{A}})^{m}\leqslant x_{\mu_{\bot}}
, as claimed.
∎

Theorem 5.16
.

No finite subdirectly irreducible algebra
\mathbf{A}\in\mathsf{CIRL}

By the Non-splitting Lemma

5.15
.

Lemma 5.18
.

Let
\mathbf{E}
be an expansion of

5.5.
More negative results and some questions

We have applied the expand-and-distort technique inside the variety
of commutative integral residuated lattices, but it clearly applies in any
variety of residuated lattices containing
\mathsf{CIRL}
. Thus, the next
result is immediate.

Corollary 5.20
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
be a variety of residuated lattices containing

\mathsf{CIRL}
. Let
\mathbf{A}\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
be finite, subdirectly irreducible,
commutative and integral. Then
\mathbf{A}
is not a splitting algebra in

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
. In particular, the variety
\mathsf{CIRL}
has no splittings.

Outside
\mathsf{CIRL}
the situation is less clear. In an arbitrary variety

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
containing
\mathsf{CIRL}
there may exist non-integral or
non-commutative splitting algebras, perhaps infinite, if

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
is not generated by its finite members.
For subvarieties of
\mathsf{CIRL}
, the following result is
all we know.

Corollary 5.21
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
be a subvariety of
\mathsf{CIRL}
. If
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}

contains the variety of Wajsberg hoops and is closed under expansions and
truncated products, then no finite subdirectly irreducible algebra in

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}
is splitting.

Proof.

It is known (cf.
[
3
]
) that the variety of Wajsberg hoops is
generated by
the algebras
\mathbf{C}_{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The conclusion follows immediately.
∎

The variety of Wajsberg hoops, and the variety of all hoops are
closed under expansions. In fact, the expansion part of our constructions is
modelled after Wajsberg hoops (we encourage the reader to verify that the first
expansion of
\mathbf{C}_{n}
is
\mathbf{C}_{2n-1}
). Neither is closed under
truncated products, so it would be interesting to characterise the smallest
variety containing (Wajsberg) hoops and closed under truncated products.
By Corollary
5.21
that variety contains no finite splitting
algebras, or no splitting algebras at all, if it is generated by its finite
members.

6.
Cousins of double Heyting algebras

Recall that a double Heyting algebra is an algebra
\langle A;\vee,\wedge,\to,{\sim},0,1\rangle
such that
\langle A;\vee,\wedge,\to,0,1\rangle
is a Heyting algebra and
\langle A;\vee,\wedge,\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}},0,1\rangle
is a dual Heyting algebra.

Definition 6.1
.

An algebra
\langle A,\vee,\wedge,\to,{\sim},0,1\rangle
is a
dually pseudocomplemented Heyting algebra
(H
+
-algebra for short) if
\langle A;\vee,\wedge,\to,0,1\rangle
is a Heyting algebra and
{\sim}
is a
dual pseudocomplement
operation, i.e.,

x\vee y=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y\geqslant{\sim}x.

For algebras with a Heyting algebra term reduct, we define the
pseudocomplement
operation by
\neg x=x\to 0
.
Similarly, for algebras with a dual Heyting algebra term reduct, the dual pseudocomplement operation is given by
{\sim}x=1\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}x
.
If
\mathbf{A}
is a double Heyting algebra, then
\mathbf{A}^{\flat}
will denote the
\langle\vee,\wedge,\to,{\sim},0,1\rangle
-term reduct of
\mathbf{A}
.
Let
\mathsf{H}
denote the class of Heyting algebras, let

\mathsf{H}^{+}
denote the class of H
+
-algebras and let
\mathsf{DH}
denote the class of double Heyting algebras.

Remark 6.2
.

The abbreviation to H
+
-algebra is derived from an alternative notation
x^{+}
instead of
{\sim}x
for the dual pseudocomplement.

For some algebraic properties of double Heyting algebras,
see Rauszer
[
27
,
28
]
,
Wolter
[
34
]
, Sankappanavar
[
29
]
,
and Taylor
[
30
]
; refer to
[
29
]

and
[
30
]
for more on H
+
-algebras.
In particular, both
\mathsf{DH}
and
\mathsf{H}^{+}
are equational classes.
The following result
shows that the theory of H
+
-algebras encapsulates much of the theory of double Heyting algebras.

Theorem 6.3

(Sankappanavar
[
29
]
)
.

Congruences on a double Heyting algebra
\mathbf{A}
are exactly the congruences on the H
+
-algebra term reduct of
\mathbf{A}
.
More succinctly,

\operatorname{Con}(\mathbf{A})=\operatorname{Con}(\mathbf{A}^{\flat}).

Also related is the class of congruence-regular double p-algebras.
A
double p-algebra
is an algebra
\mathbf{A}=\langle A;\vee,\wedge,\neg,{\sim},0,1\rangle
such that
\langle A;\vee,\wedge,0,1\rangle
is a bounded lattice and
\neg
and
{\sim}
are pseudocomplement and dual pseudocomplement operations, respectively.
An algebra
\mathbf{A}
is
congruence-regular
(or
regular
for short) if, whenever two congruences on
\mathbf{A}
share a class, they are
equal. For the next result, Varlet
[
31
,
32
]
proved the equivalence of conditions
(1)
,
(2)
, and
(3)
; condition
(4)
was included under the assumption of distributivity.
Katriňák
[
17
]
extended this by proving that
(2)
implies distributivity.

Theorem 6.4

(Varlet
[
31
,
32
]
, Katriňák
[
17
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a double p-algebra.
The following are equivalent
:

(1)

\mathbf{A}
is regular
;

(2)

for all
x,y\in A
, if
\neg x=\neg y~\text{and}~{\sim}x={\sim}y
, then
x=y
;

(3)

every prime filter of
\mathbf{A}
is minimal or maximal
;

(4)

\mathbf{A}
is distributive and
\mathbf{A}\models{\sim}x\wedge x\leqslant y\vee\neg y
.

Notice that
(4)

provides an equational characterisation of regular double p-algebras.

Definition 6.5
.

Let
\mathsf{RDP}
denote the variety of regular double p-algebras.

The following result of Katriňák shows that regular double p-algebras form a natural class of double Heyting algebras.

Theorem 6.6

(Katriňák
[
17
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a congruence-regular double p-algebra.
Then
\mathbf{A}
is term-equivalent to a double Heyting algebra via the term

x\rightarrow y=\neg\neg(\neg x\vee\neg\neg y)\wedge[{\sim}(x\vee\neg x)\vee\neg x\vee y\vee\neg y]

and its dual.

Thus
\mathsf{RDP}
is term-equivalent to a subvariety of
\mathsf{H}^{+}
and
\mathsf{DH}
.
By Theorem
6.3
, all double Heyting algebras and regular double p-algebras have their
congruences determined by their H
+
-algebra term reducts, and we will now see that they
fit exactly into the framework defined in Section

Definition 6.7
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an algebra with a Heyting algebra term reduct.

.

on

.

Theorem 6.8

(Sankappanavar
[

]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an H

-algebra and let

.

.

.

It is easily verified that the term

; thus the variety

-algebras satisfies the properties (P1)–(P5) from Section

.
Then, by Theorem

, the variety of double Heyting
algebras also fits into the framework.
The next result lists without proof some properties of H
+
-algebras we will apply.
The interested reader will find a characterisation of the subdirectly
irreducible algebras in Sankappanavar

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an H

-algebra.

(1)

For all

(a)

(b)

(c)

(d)

Naturally, these results also hold for double Heyting algebras and regular double p-algebras.
The subvariety of

satisfying the identity

.
That subvariety is term-equivalent to the variety of Boolean algebras, so we will call it

6.1.
The restricted Priestley duality for
\mathsf{H}
,
\mathsf{H^{+}}
and
\mathsf{DH}

To apply the Non-splitting Lemma
4.3
, we will make use of a similar “expand and distort” technique as for residuated lattices.
Instead of distorting the lattice structure directly, we will utilise the topological duality for distributive lattices.

Definition 6.10
.

Let
{\mathbf{X}}=\langle X;\leqslant\rangle
be an ordered set and let
Y\subseteq X
.
We will let
\leqslant_{Y}
denote the order
\leqslant
restricted to
Y
, that is,
{\leqslant_{Y}}=Y^{2}\cap{\leqslant}
.
The set of minimal elements of
{\mathbf{X}}
will be denoted by
\min({\mathbf{X}})
, and for each
Y\subseteq X
, we let
{\min_{\mathbf{X}}(Y)=\min({\mathbf{X}})\cap Y}
.
Similarly, the set of maximal elements of
{\mathbf{X}}
will be denoted by
\max({\mathbf{X}})

and we let
\max_{\mathbf{X}}(Y)=\max({\mathbf{X}})\cap Y
.
Define
\mathord{\uparrow}Y:=\bigcup\{\mathord{\uparrow}y\mid y\in Y\}
and
{\downarrow}Y:=\bigcup\{{\downarrow}y\mid y\in Y\}
and
let
{\updownarrow}Y=\mathord{\uparrow}Y\cup{\downarrow}Y
.
Note that there is a distinction between
{\updownarrow}Y
and the sets
\mathord{\uparrow}{\downarrow}Y
and
{\downarrow}\mathord{\uparrow}Y
.
We will say that
{\mathbf{X}}
is
connected
if, for all
x\in X
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
{\updownarrow}^{n}x=X
. The set
Y
is an
up-set
of
{\mathbf{X}}
if
\mathord{\uparrow}x\subseteq Y
, for all
x\in Y
, and the lattice of up-sets of
{\mathbf{X}}
will be denoted by
\mathbf{Up}({\mathbf{X}})
.

We will not dwell on
Priestley duality for distributive
lattices and treat it as assumed knowledge, referring
readers to Davey and Priestley
[
8
]
for further detail.
To ensure that the reader is oriented correctly, we will note that for the purposes of this paper, the dual space of a distributive lattice is the space of prime filters, and the lattice is recovered by taking clopen up-sets.

Definition 6.11
.

Let
\mathbf{L}
be a bounded distributive lattice and let
\mathcal{F}_{p}(\mathbf{L})
denote the set of prime filters of
\mathbf{L}
.
Then the
Priestley dual
of
\mathbf{L}
is the ordered topological space
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{F}}_{\!p}(\mathbf{L})=\langle\mathcal{F}_{p}(\mathbf{L});\subseteq,\mathcal{T}\rangle
, where the topology
\mathcal{T}
is generated by the sub-basis

\{X_{a}\mid a\in L\}\cup\{\mathcal{F}_{p}(\mathbf{L}){\setminus}X_{a}\mid a\in L\},

with
X_{a}=\{F\in\mathcal{F}_{p}(\mathbf{L})\mid a\in F\}
.
A
Priestley space
is a structure
{\mathbf{X}}=\langle X;\leqslant,\mathcal{T}\rangle
such that
\langle X;\leqslant\rangle
is an ordered set,

\langle X;\mathcal{T}\rangle
is a compact topological space and, for all
x,y\in X
with
x\not\leqslant y
, there exists a clopen up-set
U
such that
x\in U
and
y\notin U
.
The lattice of clopen up-sets of a Priestley space
{\mathbf{X}}
is denoted by
\mathbf{Up}^{\!\mathcal{T}}\!({\mathbf{X}})
, and the context will determine any further algebraic structure.

Priestley duality establishes that the category of bounded distributive lattices with bounded lattice homomorphisms is dually equivalent to the category of Priestley spaces with continuous order-preserving maps.
The properties in the next result will be used at various times without reference.

Proposition 6.12
.

Let
{\mathbf{X}}
be a non-empty Priestley space.

(1)

The sets
\min({\mathbf{X}})
and
\max({\mathbf{X}})
are non-empty.
Moreover, for all
x\in X
, both
\min_{\mathbf{X}}({\downarrow}x)
and
\max_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathord{\uparrow}x)
are non-empty.

(2)

Let
Y
and
Z
be disjoint closed subsets of
{\mathbf{X}}
such that

is an up-set and
Z
is a down-set. Then there exists a clopen up-set
W
such that

and
W\cap Z=\varnothing
.

(3)

If
\mathbf{Up}^{\!\mathcal{T}}\!({\mathbf{X}})
is pseudocomplemented, then

\mathbf{Up}^{\!\mathcal{T}}\!({\mathbf{X}})
is dually pseudocomplemented, then

For
(1)
and

[

]
.
A proof of

can be found in
[
26
]
.

Definition 6.13
.

Let
{\mathbf{X}}
be a Priestley space.
Consider the following three conditions on
{\mathbf{X}}

(S1)

{\downarrow}U
is open, for every open set

in
{\mathbf{X}}
,

(S2)

\mathord{\uparrow}U
is open, for every open set
U
in
{\mathbf{X}}
,

is open, for every clopen down-set

in

.

if it satisfies (

+
-space

In
[
25
]
, Priestley classified the dual spaces of distributive

[

]
.
The restricted Priestley

[

A detailed exposition can be found in the appendix of Davey and

Combining the results of those papers and dualising appropriately yields the next theorem.

Theorem 6.14

Let

)
.

Lemma 6.15
.

Let
{\mathbf{X}}
be a Priestley space and let

Definition 6.16
.

Let
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
be Priestley spaces and let

We will then say that

A morphism is a

if it satisfies (

), an

if it satisfies (

) and (

), and a

) and (

, let

.

By combining results from the papers cited earlier we obtain the next result.

.

.

Let

6.2.
Finite embeddability property

To obtain a complete characterisation of splitting algebras in a variety
with the help of the Non-splitting Lemma
4.3
, we need to work with a variety generated by its finite members. It is well known that
\mathsf{H}
is generated by its
finite members, and various proofs of this result exist. A standard
algebraic proof uses the fact that distributive lattices are locally finite.
Using a straightforward modification of that proof, one obtains the same result
for
\mathsf{H}^{+}
and
\mathsf{DH}
. However, no easy modification of the
standard proof seems to work for
\mathsf{RDP}
. We will therefore prove a
stronger generic result, from which all the other results we need follow as
corollaries.
We note however that, using Priestley duality, Adams, Sankappanavar, and Vaz de Carvalho
[
1
]
recently proved that
\mathsf{RDP}
is generated by its finite members. They also studied the subvariety
\mathsf{RDP}_{n}
of
\mathsf{RDP}
determined by the identity
\delta^{n+1}(x)=\delta^{n}(x)
and proved that they are also generated by their finite members. It is worth remarking that
\mathsf{RDP}_{1}
is locally finite and so has many splittings.

We need a few technical concepts first. Let
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
be a variety, let

\mathbf{A}\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
, and let
P\subseteq A
. The algebraic structure

\mathbf{P}
with the universe
P
and partial operations defined
by putting

f^{\mathbf{P}}(\overline{x})=\begin{cases}f^{\mathbf{A}}(\overline{x})&\text{if }f^{\mathbf{A}}(\overline{x})\in P\\
\text{undefined}&\text{if }f^{\mathbf{A}}(\overline{x})\in P\end{cases}

will be called a
partial algebra in

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
. There are other, inequivalent,
ways of defining partial algebras in a variety, but our result does not depend
on which definition we choose. Note that although the class of partial algebras
in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
is closed under isomorphism (because
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
is), it is customary to call
partial algebras in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}

partial algebras embeddable in

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
. We will
follow that custom.

Definition 6.20
.

A variety
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
is said to have the
finite embeddability property
(FEP), if
for every finite partial algebra
\mathbf{P}
embeddable in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
, there exists
a finite algebra
\mathbf{B}\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
such that
\mathbf{P}
embeds into
\mathbf{B}
.

The finite embeddability property was implicitly known in 1940s in the context
of word problems, but it was formally introduced by Evans
[
12
]
, where
it was shown that FEP implies solvability of the word problem. FEP for classes
of residuated structures was investigated in a series of articles around the turn
of the millenium; a representative example is
[
2
]
, where an important general construction was devised. The following well-known result, whose proof can
be found in
[
2
]
, is crucial for our purposes.

Proposition 6.21
.

For a variety
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
the following are equivalent
:

(1)

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
has the finite embeddability property
;

(2)

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
is generated as a quasivariety by its finite members.

We are now ready to present our construction, which the reader familiar with modal
logic will recognise as a variant of
filtration
.
For the remainder of this subsection,

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
will be a subvariety of
\mathsf{DH}
or of
\mathsf{H}^{+}
. Let

\mathbf{P}
be a finite partial algebra embeddable in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
. Further, let

\mathbf{A}\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
be an algebra into which
\mathbf{P}
is embedded, and
let
{\mathbf{X}}
be the dual space of
\mathbf{A}
. Then
P
can be identified with a
finite collection
\mathcal{P}
of clopen up-sets of
{\mathbf{X}}
. Define a binary relation
\simeq
on
X
by putting

x\simeq y\text{ if }\forall U\in\mathcal{P}\colon x\in U\Longleftrightarrow y\in U.

Clearly,
\simeq
is an equivalence relation. Since
\mathcal{P}
is finite,

\simeq
has finitely many equivalence classes. Let
Y=X/{\simeq}
.
Next, we define a binary relation
\sqsubseteq^{Y}
on
Y
by

[x]\sqsubseteq^{Y}[y]
if
\exists x^{\prime}\in[x],y^{\prime}\in[y]\colon x^{\prime}\leqslant y^{\prime}
.
Finally, we define
\leqslant^{Y}
to be the transitive closure of

\sqsubseteq^{Y}
. Note that
x\leqslant y
implies
[x]\leqslant^{Y}[y]
, but not conversely.
Clearly the structure
{\mathbf{Y}}:=\langle Y;\leqslant^{Y}\rangle
is a finite ordered set,
but in general not a quotient space of
{\mathbf{X}}
.

Let
\mathbf{Up}({\mathbf{Y}})
be an algebra of an appropriate signature, whose elements are
the up-sets of
{\mathbf{Y}}
.
We will call this algebra the
\mathbf{P}
-filtrate
of
\mathbf{A}
,
and typically denote it by
\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{P}}
.
Let
\varphi\colon\mathcal{P}\to\mathrm{Up}({\mathbf{Y}})

be defined by
\varphi(U)=\{[u]\mid u\in U\}
.
The next lemma shows that
\varphi
preserves
the structure of
\mathcal{P}
, and so
\mathbf{P}
is
isomorphic to a partial subalgebra of

\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{P}}
.

Lemma 6.22
.

Let
U
and
V
be elements of
\mathcal{P}
, let
x,y\in X
,
and let
\star
be any operation in
the set
\{\neg,{\sim},\cap,\cup,\to,\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}\}
. Then the following hold
:

(1)

If
[x]\leqslant^{Y}[y]
and
x\in U
, then
y\in U
.

(2)

\varphi(U)
is an up-set of
Y
.

(3)

If
U\star V\in\mathcal{P}
, then

\varphi(U)\star\varphi(V)=\varphi(U\star V)
.

Proof.

For (1), by the definition of
\leqslant^{Y}
, we have
[x]\leqslant^{Y}[y]
if and only if
for some elements
z_{1},u_{1},\dots,z_{n},u_{n}\in X
we have

x\simeq z_{1}\leqslant u_{1}\simeq z_{2}\leqslant\cdots\simeq z_{n}\leqslant u_{n}\simeq y
.
If
x\in U
, then using alternately the definition of
\simeq
and
the fact that
U
is an up-set, we get that
y\in U
. Next, (2) is an immediate
consequence of (1). For (3), we will only consider
\rightarrow
as an example. Assume

U\rightarrow V\in\mathcal{P}
. By Lemma
6.15

U\rightarrow V=X{\setminus}\mathord{\downarrow}(U{\setminus}V)
, equivalently,

x\in U\rightarrow V\quad\text{ if and only if }\quad\forall y\geqslant x\colon y\in U\Longrightarrow y\in V.

Let
[x]\in\varphi(U)\rightarrow\varphi(V)
. Take any
x^{\prime}\in[x]
;
we claim that
x^{\prime}\in U\rightarrow V
.
Assume that
x^{\prime}\leqslant z
and
z\in U
. Then
[x]\leqslant^{Y}[z]
and

[z]\in\varphi(U)
, so by assumption
[z]\in\varphi(V)
. Hence,
z\in V
, proving
one inclusion. For the other inclusion, let
[x]\in\varphi(U\rightarrow V)
; take

[y]\geqslant^{Y}[x]
with
[y]\in\varphi(U)
. Thus
x\in U\rightarrow V
and

y\in U
. Since
[x]\leqslant^{Y}[y]
, by (1) we get that
y\in U\rightarrow V
.
Hence,
y\in V
, showing that
[y]\in\varphi(V)
as required.
∎

Corollary 6.23
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
, let
\mathbf{P}
a finite partial subalgebra of
\mathbf{A}
and let
\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{P}}
be the resulting
\mathbf{P}
-filtrate of
\mathbf{A}
. Then
\mathbf{P}
is isomorphic to a partial subalgebra of
\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{P}}
.

Note that
\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{P}}=\mathbf{Up}({\mathbf{Y}})

is a finite double Heyting algebra, or an
H
+
-algebra, by construction. Thus the next result follows at once.

Theorem 6.24
.

The finite embeddability property holds for the varieties

\mathsf{DH}
and
\mathsf{H}^{+}
and thus they are generated by their finite members.

To conclude that FEP holds for an arbitrary subvariety
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
of either of
\mathsf{DH}
or
\mathsf{H}^{+}
, we need to make sure that

\mathbf{Up}({\mathbf{Y}})
belongs to
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
. In general it is not the case,
but if the membership in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
is determined by some property of
{\mathbf{X}}
preserved by

{\mathbf{Y}}
, then it is. For example, if
{\mathbf{X}}
is a chain then so is
{\mathbf{Y}}
; hence the
property of being a chain is preserved. In fact any property definable by a
positive first-order formula in the language of
\leqslant
is preserved, since by a
classical model-theoretic preservation result positive formulas are preserved
by homomorpisms. More refined preservation results are often
obtained by first expanding the partial algebra
\mathbf{P}
somewhat, to include
some desired up-sets of the dual space. Below is an example which will
suffice for our purposes.

Lemma 6.25
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{V}}
and let
\mathbf{P}
be a finite partial subalgebra of

\mathbf{A}
that is closed under
{\sim}
.
If
{\mathbf{A}}
is an
\mathsf{RDP}
-algebra, then so is the corresponding

\mathbf{P}
-filtrate
\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{P}}
of
{\mathbf{A}}
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathbf{X}}
be the dual space of
{\mathbf{A}}
, so by Theorem
6.4
, every element of
{\mathbf{X}}
is either minimal or maximal. Suppose that
\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{P}}
is not
an
\mathsf{RDP}
-algebra. Then, by Theorem
6.4
again,
[x]<^{Y}[z]<^{Y}[y]
holds for some
x,y,z\in X
.
By construction, there exist clopen up-sets

U,V
of
{\mathbf{X}}
such that
x\notin U
,
z\in U
, and
z\notin V
,
y\in V
.
Reasoning as in the proof of Lemma
6.22
(1), we
obtain a configuration
{a<u\simeq v<b}
, with
u,v,b\in U
and

a\notin U
, so
a\in{\sim}U
and
u\in{\sim}U
.
Since
\mathbf{P}
is closed under
{\sim}
,
we have
{\sim}U\in\mathcal{P}
; hence
v\in{\sim}U
.
As
v\in{\sim}U
, by Lemma
6.15
(2) there exists
w\leqslant v
with
w\notin U
. But
v<b
, so
v
is a minimal element of
{\mathbf{X}}
. Hence
v=w\notin U
, contradicting the fact that
v\in U
.
∎

Theorem 6.26
.

The variety of regular double p-algebras has the finite embeddability property
and thus it is generated by its finite members.

Proof.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a regular double p-algebra and let
\mathbf{S}
be a finite partial subalgebra of
{\mathbf{A}}
.
As the equality

{\sim}{\sim}{\sim}x\approx{\sim}x
holds in
\mathsf{H}^{+}
, the set

{P:=S\cup\{{\sim}s,{\sim}{\sim}s\mid s\in S\}}
(where
{\sim}
is taken in
\mathbf{A}
) is closed under
{\sim}
, and finite. Let
\mathbf{P}
be the

Lemma 7.21

Let

\mathbf{T}

Proof.

Assume that

Let
\mathbf{S}

Lemma 7.34
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
be finite connected double-pointed ordered sets.
Let
U
and
V
be up-sets in
\mathbf{X}
and then, for each
\star\in\{\vee,\wedge,\to,\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}\}

U\mathbin{\mathring{\star}}V
be shorthand for
U\mathbin{\star}^{\mathbf{Up}({\mathbf{X}})}V
, and similarly for
\mathring{{\sim}}U
.
Then, when evaluated in
\mathbf{Up}(\mathbf{X}\mathbin{\searrow}\mathbf{Y})
, for each
\star\in\{\vee,\wedge,\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}\}

U\star V=U\mathbin{\mathring{\star}}V,

for
{\sim}
we have

{\sim}U=\mathring{{\sim}}U\cup Y\cup\{\top^{\mathbf{X}}\},

and for
\to
we have

U\to V=\begin{cases}(U\mathbin{\mathring{\to}}V)\cup Y&\text{if $\top^{\mathbf{X}}\notin U{\setminus}V$,}\\
(U\mathbin{\mathring{\to}}V)\cup Y{\setminus}\{\bot^{\mathbf{Y}}\}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

First note that
U
and
V
are also up-sets in
\mathbf{X}\mathbin{\searrow}\mathbf{Y}
.
Let
\Uparrow
and
\Downarrow
denote the operations
\mathord{\uparrow}
and
{\downarrow}
with respect to the order on
\mathbf{X}
.
Recall by Lemma
6.15
that the operations listed above are given by:

\displaystyle U\mathbin{\mathring{\vee}}V

\displaystyle=U\cup V,

\displaystyle U\mathbin{\mathring{\wedge}}V

\displaystyle=U\cap V,

\displaystyle U\mathbin{\mathring{\to}}V

\displaystyle=X{\setminus}{\Downarrow}(U{\setminus}V),

\displaystyle\qquad U\mathbin{\mathring{\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}}}V

\displaystyle={\Uparrow}(U{\setminus}V),

\displaystyle\mathring{{\sim}}U

\displaystyle={\Uparrow}(X{\setminus}U).

The calculations for the lattice operations are trivial.
For
{\sim}
, in
\mathbf{Up}(\mathbf{X}\mathbin{\searrow}\mathbf{Y})
we have

\displaystyle{\sim}U

\displaystyle=\mathord{\uparrow}\big[(X\cup Y){\setminus}U\big]=\mathord{\uparrow}\big[X{\setminus}U\cup Y{\setminus}U\big]=\mathord{\uparrow}(X{\setminus}U)\cup\mathord{\uparrow}Y.

Since
\mathord{\uparrow}(X{\setminus}U)
and
Y
are disjoint, we have
\mathord{\uparrow}(X{\setminus}U)={\Uparrow}(X{\setminus}U)=\mathbin{\mathring{{\sim}}}U
, and by construction, we have
\mathord{\uparrow}Y=Y\cup\{\top^{\mathbf{X}}\}
.
Thus
{\sim}U=\mathring{{\sim}}U\cup Y\cup\{\top^{\mathbf{X}}\}
.
For
\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}
, we have
U\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}V=\mathord{\uparrow}(U{\setminus}V)
.
Since
U,V\subseteq X

\mathord{\uparrow}(U{\setminus}V)
and
{Y}
are disjoint.
So
\mathord{\uparrow}(U{\setminus}V)={\Uparrow}(U{\setminus}V)
, which proves the claim.
For
\to
, we have

\displaystyle U\to V

\displaystyle=(X\cup Y){\setminus}{\downarrow}(U{\setminus}V)

\displaystyle=\big[X{\setminus}{\downarrow}(U{\setminus}V)\big]\cup\big[Y{\setminus}{\downarrow}(U{\setminus}V)\big]

\displaystyle=U\mathbin{\mathring{\to}}V\cup\big[Y{\setminus}{\downarrow}(U{\setminus}V)\big].

If
\top^{\mathbf{X}}\notin U{\setminus}V

Y\cap{\downarrow}(U{\setminus}V)=\varnothing
, and otherwise,
Y\cap{\downarrow}(U{\setminus}V)=\{\bot^{\mathbf{Y}}\}
.
So,

Y{\setminus}{\downarrow}(U{\setminus}V)=\begin{cases}Y&\text{if $\top^{\mathbf{X}}\notin U{\setminus}V$,}\\
Y{\setminus}\{\bot^{\mathbf{Y}}\}&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 7.35
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
be finite connected double-pointed ordered sets.
Let
U
and
V
be up-sets in
\mathbf{X}

\star\in\{\vee,\wedge,\to,\mathbin{\vphantom{+}\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\kern-1.50696pt\cr$\smash{\cdot}$\cr\kern 1.50696pt\cr$-$\cr}}}}\}

\mathring{{\sim}}U

\mathbf{Up}(\mathbf{X}\mathbin{\searrow}\mathbf{Y})

{\sim}U\leftrightarrow\mathring{{\sim}}U=\begin{cases}X&\text{if $\top^{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathring{{\sim}}U$},\\

The first part holds because

{\sim}
, we have
\mathring{{\sim}}U\subseteq{\sim}U

\displaystyle{\sim}U\leftrightarrow\mathring{{\sim}}U

\displaystyle={\sim}U\to\mathring{{\sim}}U=(X\cup Y){\setminus}{\downarrow}({\sim}U{\setminus}\mathring{{\sim}}U).

{\downarrow}({\sim}U{\setminus}\mathring{{\sim}}U)=\begin{cases}Y&\text{if $\top^{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathring{{\sim}}U$,}\\
Y\cup\{\top^{\mathbf{X}}\}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Hence,

\displaystyle{\sim}U\to\mathring{{\sim}}U

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(X\cup Y){\setminus}Y&\text{if $\top^{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathring{{\sim}}U$},\\
(X\cup Y){\setminus}(Y\cup{\downarrow}\top^{\mathbf{X}})&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}X&\text{if $\top^{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathring{{\sim}}U$},\\
X{\setminus}{\downarrow}\top^{\mathbf{X}}&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

For
\to
, we have
U\mathbin{\mathring{\to}}V\subseteq U\to V
, so

(U\to V)\leftrightarrow(U\mathbin{\mathring{\to}}V)=(U\to V)\to(U\mathbin{\mathring{\to}}V).

First observe that

(U\to V){\setminus}(U\mathbin{\mathring{\to}}V)=\begin{cases}Y&\text{if $\top^{\mathbf{X}}\notin U{\setminus}V$,}\\
Y{\setminus}\{\bot^{\mathbf{Y}}\}&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(U\to V)\to(U\mathbin{\mathring{\to}}V)=(X\cup Y){\setminus}{\downarrow}[(U\to V){\setminus}(U\mathbin{\mathring{\to}}V)]=(X\cup Y){\setminus}Y=X,

∎
