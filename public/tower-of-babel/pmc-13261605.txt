Influence of Aerobic vs Anaerobic Pyrolysis on the Electrochemical Behavior of Mesocrystals
Authors: Leonhard Rochels, Tereza Václavů, Asma Qdemat, Germán Salazar-Alvarez, Bertold Rasche, Sabrina Disch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of surface-active materials to improve the performance of electrochemical processes is becoming increasingly important in response to the growing challenges of sustainability. Due to their high active surface area, arrangements of nanoparticles are strong candidates for electrode materials. However, the surface activity of nanoparticles is often affected by the presence of stabilization agents such as organic ligand layers on the particle surface. This work investigates the postsynthetic aerobic and anaerobic pyrolysis of iron oxide mesocrystals to transform the organic ligand shell into an electrochemically active surface. This process introduces fissures into the nanoparticle assemblies while concomitantly reducing the particle separation distances, thereby enhancing both capacitance and magnetic coupling. Our findings demonstrate that anaerobic pyrolysis preserves mesocrystalline order, whereas aerobic pyrolysis disrupts order but significantly improves electrochemical activity by enhancing the active surface area.

Introduction

The ongoing increase in energy demand, coupled with the global transition toward more sustainable energy sources, has made the development of electrochemical methods for converting and storing energy a key area of research. − This demand has driven the development of functional electrode materials for catalysis as well as energy transformation and storage. ,

In order to ensure the effective and sustained performance of electrochemically active materials, three key factors must be taken into consideration: high chemical reactivity, shortened charge transport pathways, and enhanced electronic properties. Nanomaterials meet these criteria due to their intrinsically high surface area and are therefore highly relevant for potential applications as electrode materials in catalysis, , optoelectronics, photoelectrochemical water splitting, and in the context of battery materials.

Mesocrystals are a class of nanoparticle-based materials that are characterized by the oriented attachment of nanoparticles into long-range ordered superstructures with mutual crystallographic alignment of their nanoparticle subunits. , The uniform crystallographic orientation of these mesostructured materials with high porosity and surface area render them highly relevant in biomedicine, catalysis, and as electrode materials. , The self-assembly of nanoparticles into mesocrystals is typically accomplished by means of slow evaporation of a colloidal dispersion with organic surfactants (e.g., oleic acid, oleylamine) as a stabilizer in solution. A disadvantage of this method is the low conductivity of the surfactant layer which remains attached to the nanoparticle surface. The presence of this insulating layer has a detrimental effect on the applicability of such nanoparticle arrangements as an electrode material. ,

Removal of the organic surfactant layer on nanoparticles is typically pursued by heat treatment or plasma processing approaches. , Fast thermal annealing has been shown to enhance the catalytic activity of supported nanoparticles. Applied to mesocrystals, a thermally induced cross-linking of the nanoparticle building blocks through the stabilizing ligand shell has been demonstrated to yield exceptional mechanical stability. Enhanced electronic properties have been reported after heat treatment of Pt, PbS and Fe3O4 mesocrystals. − Moreover, the deliberate coating of iron oxide nanoparticles with carbon as a conductive layer using thermal decomposition has been shown to improve their electrochemical performance. The resulting material, a conjunction of iron-oxide-based ion intercalating and carbonized electron conducting phases, can additionally accommodate the volume expansion/contraction-induced stress of the nanoparticles on the mesostructure, potentially enhancing the battery lifetime. − However, the performance of nanostructured devices is frequently found to be critically impacted by the degradation of mesocrystalline order and the subsequent formation of cracks and fissures within the material during the process of ligand removal. , It is evident that the type of heat treatment is of paramount importance in the fine-tuning of the electrochemical properties of the treated material.

In this study, we resolve the different impact of aerobic and anaerobic pyrolysis as a postassembly method on the transformation of oleic acid-capped nanoparticle mesocrystals into electrode materials, with the aim of enhancing their surface activity. We explore the relationship between the degree of mesocrystalline long-range order, its concomitant interparticle interactions, and the consequent implications for electrochemical properties. Our findings are based on a combination of detailed structural analysis, magnetization measurements, and electrochemical cycling.

Experimental Details

Synthesis and Self-Organization

Materials

Sodium hydroxide (90%), n-hexane (97%), and ethanol (≥99.8%) were purchased from Fisher Chemicals. Oleic acid (90%) and iron­(III) chloride hexahydrate (≥99%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. 1-octadecene (90%) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. All chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Nanoparticle Synthesis

Iron oxide nanocubes were synthesized by thermal decomposition of iron oleate according to previously described synthesis routes.

127 mL (360 mmol) of oleic acid was added dropwise to a solution of 14.4 g (360 mmol) of sodium hydroxide in 60 mL of deionized water and 60 mL of ethanol. The obtained solution of sodium oleate was combined with a solution of 32.44 g (120 mmol) FeCl3 ·6H2O in 120 mL of deionized water and 180 mL of ethanol as well as 400 mL of n-hexane. The reaction mixture was heated to reflux for 4 h, then cooled to room temperature and washed four times with 60 mL of water. After removal of the remaining solvents under reduced pressure, 105 g of iron oleate were obtained as a dark brown viscous liquid.

For nanoparticle synthesis, 30.52 g (33.9 mmol) of freshly prepared iron oleate and 2.99 mL (8.47 mmol) of oleic acid were dissolved in 200 mL 1-octadecene in a three-neck flask. A solution of sodium oleate, freshly prepared by dropping 2.99 mL (8.47 mmol) of oleic acid into a solution of 0.34 g (8.36 mmol) of sodium hydroxide in 3 mL of ethanol and 3 mL of water, was added. Under a blanket of inert gas, the reaction mixture was heated to 140 °C for 1 h and then heated with a heating rate of 3 °C/min to reflux temperature (330 °C), which was held for 30 min. The obtained nanoparticles were precipitated with acetone and redispersed in n-hexane after centrifugation three times.

Self-Assembly

Evaporation-induced self-organization of iron oxide nanocubes was performed using 10 × 10 mm silicon wafers and polished glassy carbon substrates. All substrates were cleaned by ultrasonication in acetone and isopropanol for 10 min each. For simultaneous preparation of several samples under identical conditions, up to six substrates were mounted vertically on a custom-built PTFE sample holder that was placed into a small beaker. The beaker was then filled with a 0.40 mg/mL dispersion of nanocubes that was allowed to evaporate slowly within 2–3 days.

The as prepared assemblies were pyrolyzed either under aerobic or anaerobic conditions in a tube furnace. After flooding with inert gas (Ar) for 2 h, the sample chamber was heated to up to 400 °C over the course of 2 h (heating rate: 3 °C) and kept at this temperature for 12 h after which the samples were allowed to cool down to room temperature. The constant Ar flow was maintained for the entire duration of the anaerobic pyrolysis. The aerobic pyrolysis was conducted using the same heating parameters but in air atmosphere without any applied air flow.

Characterization

X-ray Diffraction (PXRD)

Powder XRD was measured on a Stoe Stadi-P instrument in Debye–Scherrer geometry, using Mo K α1 radiation in the range of 2θ = 0–60° in increments of 0.015°. For the measurement, a nanoparticle powder was prepared by removal of excess oleic acid and evaporation of the solvent and was filled into glass capillaries with 0.5 mm diameter. Grazing-incidence X-ray diffraction (GI-XRD) measurements were performed on a Panalytical EMPYREAN instrument in Bragg–Brentano geometry, using Cu K α radiation (ratio K α2 and K α1: 0.5) in the range of 2θ = 5–90° in steps of 0.05°. The nanoparticle mesocrystals assembled on silicon substrates were measured as prepared and as pyrolyzed.

Transmission Electron Microscopy

TEM was performed on a ZEISS Leo 912 transmission electron microscope operated at an acceleration voltage of 120 kV. A dilute nanoparticle dispersion was dried on a carbon-coated copper grid. Particle-size histograms were obtained by manual measurements of the edge lengths of more than 200 particles using the ImageJ software tool, and were evaluated according to a log–normal size distribution.

Scanning Electron Microscopy

SEM of the nanoparticle assemblies was performed on a Zeiss Neon 40 CrossBeam microscope operating at an acceleration voltage of 5 kV. SEM micrographs were obtained using the detectors for secondary electrons (SE) and the in-lens secondary electron detector (InLens). In addition to conventional top-views, cross sections of nanoparticle superstructures were measured with a tilt of the SEM sample stage at an angle of 10°.

Magnetization Measurements (VSM)

VSM was carried out using a Quantum Design PPMS Evercool II. A 4 mg/mL nanoparticle dispersion in cyclohexane was sealed into a glass ampule. Nanoparticle assemblies on silicon substrates were cut into 5 × 5 mm2 samples and mounted on a VSM Quartz paddle sample holder.

Isothermal magnetization measurements were carried out in a magnetic field range of up to ±9 T at 5 and 300 K. The magnetization M(H) was corrected for the diamagnetic contributions of the solvent and glass vial as determined by reference measurements and then evaluated according to the Langevin equation (see Supporting Information).

Temperature dependent magnetization measurements were carried out upon heating by 2K/min in a temperature range of 5–400 K in an applied magnetic field of 10 mT after cooling in either zero field (ZFC) or applied magnetic field (FC). An estimate of the blocking temperature was calculated by the derivative of ZFC/FC magnetization d(ZFC – FC)/dT, which was determined from an interpolated spline applied to the raw ZFC/FC data.

(Grazing-Incidence) Small-Angle X-ray Scattering

SAXS and GISAXS measurements were performed at the Gallium Anode Low-Angle X-ray Instrument (GALAXI) at the Forschungszentrum Jülich. GALAXI uses an X-ray wavelength of λ = 1.3414 Å and a Dectris Pilatus 1 M detector.

For SAXS measurements, the 4 mg/mL nanoparticle dispersion was sealed into a 1.5 mm borosilicate glass capillary and measured at two detector distances of 853 mm and 3548 mm, giving access to a Q range of 2.4·10–3 Å–1 ≤ Q ≤ 7.2·10–1 Å–1. The data were radially averaged and normalized to absolute units using fluorinated ethylene propylene 1400 Å (d = 0.35 mm) as reference material. Background scattering of the n-hexane solvent was subtracted. The reduced data was analyzed according to the superball form factor model using SASView (5.0.3).

For GISAXS measurements, the samples were oriented horizontally with incident angles between α i = 0.1° and 0.2° and a sample-detector distance of 1733 mm. Measurements of the as-deposited mesocrystal on a silicon substrate were performed at five different incident angles around the critical angle. Integrations in the Yoneda condition were performed at Q z = 0.15 nm–1 for the as-deposited mesocrystal, at 0.24 nm–1 for the mesocrystal after anaerobic and at 0.27 nm–1 after aerobic pyrolysis with an integration range of ΔQ z = 0.1 nm–1.

X-ray Reflectometry

X-ray reflectometry was measured on a Bruker AXS reflectometer operated using Cu Kα X-ray radiation in a range of 2θ = 0–6° with a step size of 0.005°.

Cyclic Voltammetry

The electrochemical behavior of the nanoparticle samples assembled on the glassy carbon substrates was characterized by performing cyclic voltammetry (CV) experiments in a three-electrode setup.

An airtight saturated calomel electrode (SCE) and a graphite rod (5 mm diameter) were used as the reference (RE) and counter (CE) electrodes, respectively. The samples were connected as the working electrode (WE) and placed into an aqueous citric acid buffer electrolyte solution (pH = 4.7). The buffer solution is used to prevent unwanted pH-dependent phase changes of Fe (see Pourbaix diagram of Fe) in an aqueous electrolyte subjected to redox processes. All measurements were performed at room temperature in inert atmosphere in an applied potential range between −1.1 and 1.1 V with respect to SCE and an optimized scan rate of 5 mV/s, using a Gamry Interface 1010B potentiostat. For the determination of the capacitance C, the current of all samples was normalized to the weight of the material deposited on the glassy carbon substrate (m = 0.14 mg in all cases). Since the area of the glassy carbon substrate exposed to the electrolyte was the same for all experiments, the apparent decrease in capacity after deposition is a clear signature of a reduction of the conductive surface area.

Results and Discussion

Iron oxide mesocrystals deposited on silicon and glassy carbon substrates were pyrolyzed to convert the surfactant layer to a conducting carbon layer. Figure shows the as-deposited mesocrystals and the pyrolyzed mesocrystals under aerobic and anaerobic conditions. Monodisperse cuboidal iron oxide nanoparticles stabilized with oleic acid were chosen as the model system. The maghemite (γ-Fe2O3) nanocubes with an overall edge length of 13.4 ± 0.9 nm and a superball shape parameter p = 1.88(4) readily self-organize into long-range ordered mesocrystals. Arrangements of micron-sized lateral dimensions and about 200 nm in height were achieved using the vertical deposition technique typically applied to photonic arrays of much larger, colloidal nanoparticles. , The evolution of structural integrity from the as-deposited mesocrystals (Figure a) through pyrolysis under anaerobic (Figure b) and aerobic (Figure c) conditions was followed by a combination of SEM and GISAXS.

The SEM image in Figure a shows that the as-deposited nanocubes arrange face-to-face with a top-view square symmetry, indicating mutual crystallographic orientation of the individual nanocubes. The formation of such mesocrystals with dimensions in the order of several μm is well in agreement with previous reports on cuboidal nanoparticles of similar sizes. −

The three-dimensional mesocrystal structure is accessible by the Grazing-Incidence Small-Angle X-ray Scattering (GISAXS) technique. Sharp reflections observed in Figure a confirm the long-range ordering of the nanocubes into mesocrystals. The strong texture of the scattering intensities into distinguishable reflections indicates preferred orientation of most of the mesocrystalline material as a 2D powder with respect to the substrate, whereas the ring-like distribution of some scattering intensity originates in misorientation of a minor part of the sample. Crystallographic analysis of the reflection positions in Q y and Q z directions (see Supporting Information) confirms the square lateral symmetry observed using SEM with a lattice parameter of a = 16.80(1) nm and reveals a nearly equal perpendicular lattice parameter of c = 16.9(1) nm along with a critical angle of total reflection of α c = 0.175°. These in-plane and out-of-plane lattice parameters agree well with SEM and XRR analysis, respectively (Figure S5). The absence of any systematic extinctions as well as volume considerations lead us to a slightly distorted simple cubic (sc) packing of the nanocubes into mesocrystals. Considering the superball edge length and volume of the nanocubes, we derive a separation distance of 3.42(4) nm between the inorganic surfaces of adjacent particles and a packing density of 40(4)% based on the inorganic material in the mesocrystals. Interparticle separation distances of 2.5–4.5 nm are typically found for similar oleic acid-capped nanoparticles in dispersion and mesocrystals. , This overall mesocrystal structure is in excellent agreement with the previously reported self-organization behavior of cuboidal iron oxide nanoparticles, where size and shape direct the symmetry in face-to-face arrangements ranging from body-centered tetragonal to simple cubic packing symmetries.

Pyrolysis under aerobic and anaerobic conditions affects both the morphology and structural integrity of the mesocrystals. SEM reveals the formation of extended cracks between the otherwise intact mesocrystals after anaerobic pyrolysis (Figure b), and nearly disintegrated mesocrystals after pyrolysis under aerobic conditions (Figure c). The structural degradation of the mesocrystals manifests in significantly more diffuse reflections in the respective GISAXS pattern. After anaerobic pyrolysis, mesocrystal reflections are still visible, albeit significantly broader than in the pristine sample, indicating that long-range order still exists. The reflection broadening corresponds to a drastically reduced mesocrystalline coherence length. This is in line with previous reports that pyrolysis significantly alters the continuity of nanoparticle assemblies while leaving the overall structure types largely intact. , After aerobic pyrolysis, however, the structural integrity of the mesocrystals is completely lost, as evident by the absence of mesocrystal structure reflections (Figure c).

A direct comparison of GISAXS intensities in the Yoneda lines (Figure a) illustrates the effect of pyrolysis on the lateral mesocrystal structure. Anaerobic pyrolysis induces a contraction of the square lattice parameter from 16.80(1) nm to 14.86(1) nm as derived from the shift of the Yoneda reflections to higher Q. This contraction corresponds to a significant decrease of the inorganic interparticle separation distance from 3.42(4) nm to 1.50(4) nm upon decomposition of the oleic acid ligand shell. Together with the comparable contraction in the out-of-plane direction (16.9(1) nm to 14.7(5) nm) we derive an increase in the packing density from 40(4)% to 58(6)%. The considerable reflection broadening after anaerobic pyrolysis corresponds to a decrease in the structural coherence of the mesocrystals from 52(1) nm to 34(1) nm (Supporting Information). This illustrates that, despite the clear reduction in mesocrystal size and long-range order, the nanoparticle packing symmetry in the mesocrystalline arrays is maintained after anaerobic pyrolysis (Figure b).

After aerobic pyrolysis the mesocrystal order appears completely destroyed, as indicated by the absence of individual mesocrystal structure reflections in GISAXS (Figures c and a). In line with the SEM observation, we relate this destruction to the formation of more volatile, expanding gaseous decomposition products (such as CO2) resulting from the oxidation, which ultimately destroy the mesocrystalline face-to-face arrangement and long-range order.

Whereas also in the anaerobic pyrolysis process gaseous decomposition products are expected to form, their amount will be significantly lower. We speculate that cohesive capillary forces as well as van der Waals interactions between the particles lead to the observed mesocrystal lattice contraction. During the more disruptive decomposition process in aerobic pyrolysis, on the other hand, the expansion due to the extensive formation of gaseous decomposition products is likely dominant and ultimately disintegrates the mesostructure.

Zero-field-cooled (ZFC) and field-cooled (FC) magnetization measurements indirectly indicate a variation in dipolar interparticle interactions through the superparamagnetic blocking temperature T B (Figure b, Supporting Information Figure S6). We observe a continuous increase of the blocking temperature from the noninteracting nanoparticles in a frozen dilute dispersion (T B = 58(1) K) to the interacting nanoparticles in a mesocrystalline arrangement (T B = 61(1) K) and in even closer packing after anaerobic pyrolysis (T B = 96(1) K). A relative increase in superparamagnetic blocking temperature in otherwise identical samples is a signature of enhanced dipolar interactions. Our results therefore indicate a trend of increasing dipolar interparticle interactions with decreasing average particle separation distance. A slight decrease of the field-cooled magnetization in the lower temperature range after anaerobic pyrolysis further indicates enhanced interaction contributions. The mesocrystals pyrolyzed in aerobic conditions exhibit an intermediate blocking temperature of 89(1) K in between those of the pristine and anaerobically pyrolyzed mesocrystals. We understand this as an indication of close contact between the nanoparticles despite the observed loss of mesocrystalline order. The average particle separation distance here is higher, meaning the interactions are lower, than after anaerobic pyrolysis, giving rise to the intermediate blocking temperature.

Electrochemical Activity

To qualitatively assess the electrochemical properties, cyclic voltammetry (CV) was conducted. Generally, currents of high magnitude arise at the upper and lower end of the potential range which are the result of oxidation and reduction processes occurring due to the limited potential window of the aqueous electrolyte.

An overview of all CV cycles is shown in Figure a-c. From the first CV cycle of the as-prepared mesocrystals we qualitatively find a decreased capacitance compared to the bare glassy carbon electrode. This implies that adding the mesocrystals onto the glassy carbon electrode effectively reduces the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA). This can be understood as the particles embedded in a nonconductive oleic-acid layer essentially reducing the conductive area of the electrode. For both kinds of pyrolyzed samples, on the other hand, the first cycle shows strong irreversible reductive signals (Figure b,c).

Cyclic voltammograms of the (a) as-prepared, (b) anaerobically pyrolyzed, and (c) aerobically pyrolyzed mesocrystals deposited on glassy carbon substrates. (d) Direct comparison of cyclic voltammograms of the second cycles for each sample.

To evaluate the decisive differences between the samples, the second and third cycle after the first irreversible reactions are best suited for qualitative comparison. It has to be noted that after the irreversible Faradaic reactions in the first cycle, there remains a small Faradaic contribution besides a mainly capacitive current. This could be attributed to the reduction of Fe3+ ions in maghemite (γ – Fe2O3) to Fe2+ in solution and vice versa, particularly considering the conductive percolation network following the carbonization of the oleic acid. The sluggish kinetics would agree with this assumption. We therefore restricted the determination of capacitances to a region of linear currents between −0.5 and 0.0 V. In Figure d, the second cycles of the CVs of the as-prepared mesocrystal, the aerobically pyrolyzed and the anaerobically pyrolyzed mesocrystal are shown. Most prominently, one finds significantly enhanced oxidative and reductive currents for both samples after pyrolysis compared to the oleic acid-covered as-prepared mesocrystals (estimated capacitance: C initial = 2 mF/g) and the bare glassy carbon electrode. This increase in currents is indicative of a significant increase in the capacitance, meaning a dramatic increase in the ECSA. The sample after the aerobic pyrolysis has the highest capacitance (C aero = 56mF/g), where oleic acid is partially oxidized to gaseous decomposition products such as CO2, followed by the sample after anaerobic pyrolysis (C anaero = 18 mF/g) (Table ). The basic mechanism behind the increase in capacitance after pyrolysis is that oleic acid is likely transformed into a conductive and therefore polarizable carbon layer, leading to a significant increase in capacitance. Similar effects were reported for similar iron oxide nanocubes , and other inorganic nanoparticle assemblies.

Our findings further suggest that oleic-acid decomposition under aerobic and anaerobic conditions leads to various decomposition products forming at the nanoparticle surface, visible as signals occurring in the first CV cycle (Figure b,c). For the mesocrystals after anaerobic pyrolysis, two signals are observed in the cathodic potential region at −0.37 V vs SCE and −0.72 V vs SCE. Aerobic pyrolysis of the mesocrystals leads to one signal at −0.36 V vs SCE. We attribute these signals to various incompletely oxidized organic compounds, that are likely to form as products of the oleic acid decomposition in the absence or presence of oxygen. The observed signals may correspond to the reduction of various functional groups such as carboxylic groups, aldehydes or ketones which are reported to occur in this potential range (Supporting Information). − Consequently, the presence or absence of oxygen in the aerobic or anaerobic pyrolysis could lead to the observed differences in the CV. We furthermore surmise that since more organic material is removed in the form of gaseous decomposition products such as CO2 during aerobic pyrolysis, there is less carbonized material left on the nanoparticle surface. We speculate that this could also lead to an increased porosity of the surface. In addition, the effects of the pyrolysis on the structure of the mesocrystal arrangements, namely the disintegration of mesocrystalline order leading to the formation of cracks, can be linked to an increased effective surface exposed to the electrolyte. This effect of decreasing mesocrystalline order is especially pronounced in the case of the aerobic pyrolysis.

Conclusions

Using iron oxide nanocube mesocrystals as a model system, the effect of aerobic vs anaerobic pyrolysis of nanoparticle assemblies was explored qualitatively in terms of their potential toward nanostructured electrode materials. Distinct effects of aerobic and anaerobic pyrolysis on the structural, magnetic, and electrochemical properties of the nanoparticle mesocrystals were observed. The results presented here enable us to directly link the superstructure long-range order to the electrochemical behavior.

We infer that the transformation of the insulating oleic acid into a potentially more conductive carbonized layer on the nanoparticles enhances the ECSA of the obtained electrode materials in both cases. Aerobic pyrolysis leads to heavily disrupted assemblies with weaker dipolar interaction of the nanoparticles. Even stronger capacitative signals are detected that we attribute to an increased effective electrode surface exposed to the electrolyte. Hence, the nanoparticle disorder with high overall surface area observed after aerobic pyrolysis appears most beneficial for an enhanced ECSA. A large amount of organic surfactant in the nanoparticle solution used for self-assembly might yield even stronger pyrolysis-induced disintegration of the assemblies and may thus be desirable for destined usage as an electrode material.

The presented results draw a direct correlation between the degree of long-range order, accessible through GISAXS, and its implications for magnetic and electrochemical properties. While the electrochemical results show that the pyrolysis-induced decomposition of such organic surfactant-capped nanostructures activates the ECSA, an extended analysis on the long-term cycling performance and stability will guide further research on these materials.

With future electrode material design in mind, it appears imperative to maximize surface area while maintaining structural integrity within an ordered mesocrystal lattice to withstand cycling-related lattice strain. Future strategies should be based on fine-tuning the organic surfactant content and pyrolysis conditions such as heating rate, temperature, or atmosphere conditions to balance the enhancement of the surface area while maintaining structural integrity. This approach may render self-assembled fractal-like architectures derived from nanoparticle building blocks as a potentially viable material.
