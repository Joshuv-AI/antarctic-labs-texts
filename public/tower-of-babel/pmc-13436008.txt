The educational impact of a serious game for airway management skills of medical trainees
Authors: Sebastian Mohar, Ana María Crawford, Ulises Sánchez Vásquez, Ricardo Serna-Muñoz, Rodrigo Rubio Martínez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective airway management is essential for patient safety across prehospital, perioperative, and critical care settings. Traditional training, largely based on lectures and brief skills stations, may be insufficient to develop structured decision-making under time pressure. Serious games have emerged as an active learning strategy that can complement conventional instruction. This study evaluated the educational impact of PLAN A, a tabletop serious game, as an adjunct to airway management training for novice medical trainees.MethodsIn this prospective randomized pilot trial, 34 novice trainees, including medical interns and first-year residents with no prior formal airway management training, were randomized 1:1 to an intervention group or a control group. All participants received a 30-minute didactic lecture and a 40-minute manikin-based simulation session. The intervention group additionally completed a 40-minute PLAN A session, in which players managed progressively complex airway scenarios using card-based instruments and strategies aligned with established algorithms. The primary outcome was performance on a written case-based assessment consisting of six clinical vignettes with a maximum total score of 18 points, administered immediately before and after training. Scores were analyzed using Wilcoxon signed-rank and Mann–Whitney U tests, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Trial registration: Not applicable.ResultsAll 34 participants completed the pre- and post-training assessments. Baseline performance was low in both groups. The intervention group achieved a median pre-test score of 4.5, whereas the control group achieved a median pre-test score of 5.0. After training, both groups showed significant improvement from baseline. The intervention group improved from a median of 4.5 to 10.0, and the control group improved from a median of 5.0 to 10.0, with p < 0.001 for both within-group comparisons. Post-intervention total scores did not differ significantly between groups. Item-level analysis showed overall pre- versus post-training improvement across the six clinical scenarios, while group-specific gains varied by case.ConclusionsPLAN A appears to be a feasible, low-cost, and acceptable educational adjunct for airway management training in novice learners. In this small randomized pilot study, adding PLAN A to standard airway training did not result in statistically significant between-group differences in total knowledge outcomes, and the findings should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Introduction

Airway management is a core skill across anesthesiology, emergency medicine, critical care, and prehospital practice. Failure to secure the airway can rapidly lead to hypoxemia, brain injury, or death, and airway-related complications remain a key contributor to preventable morbidity in perioperative practice (1, 2).

Training early-stage learners typically combines didactic teaching with simulation-based technical skills practice. While these approaches support acquisition of procedural skills, they may be less effective when training relies predominantly on task trainers or manikin-based practice without structured, scenario-based decision-making that targets clinical judgment, escalation under uncertainty, and retention of algorithmic reasoning in high-stakes situations (3, 4).

Serious games are structured, rule-based learning activities designed around explicit educational objectives. By requiring learners to make sequential decisions under constraints, serious games may complement traditional instruction for domains that rely on both procedural knowledge and cognitive strategies, such as airway management (5, 6).

Despite growing use of serious games in health professions education, airway management remains relatively underrepresented. Existing airway-focused initiatives include virtual reality simulations and blended packages that incorporate algorithm rehearsal and rescue pathways, but evidence remains limited and outcomes vary across settings and assessment approaches (7–9). To address this gap, we developed PLAN A, a tabletop serious game aligned with established airway management frameworks, and conducted a prospective randomized controlled trial to evaluate its add-on value to conventional instruction. We assessed case-based clinical reasoning before and after training and hypothesized that trainees assigned to PLAN A would demonstrate greater improvement in airway management performance than those receiving conventional instruction alone.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a prospective, randomized controlled trial at Centro Médico ABC, a tertiary care academic hospital in Mexico City. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Being a pilot study, we did not perform a formal sample size calculation. We determined the sample size pragmatically based on expected participant availability during the study period. The primary aims were to assess feasibility and generate preliminary estimates of effect; therefore, the study was not powered for definitive between-group comparisons or subgroup analyses. This manuscript was prepared in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 statement and the CONSORT extension for randomized pilot and feasibility trials. A completed CONSORT checklist is provided as an additional file. Trial registration was not required because this was an educational simulation-based study without patient enrollment, patient-level interventions, or clinical outcomes.

Participants

Eligible participants were medical interns or first-year residents from different specialties with no formal airway management training. First-year residents were recruited during their induction week before initiating clinical patient care. Exclusion criteria were advanced airway management experience or prior participation in an airway management simulation course. Recruitment occurred in January and February 2024 through institutional announcements disseminated via institutional email and posted flyers. Participation was voluntary, with no financial incentives, academic credit, or linkage to clinical evaluations or residency performance. To reduce perceived coercion, recruitment and informed consent were conducted by study staff not involved in participants’ formal assessment, and program leadership did not have access to individual study data. We acknowledge that voluntary participation may introduce self-selection bias; however, all eligible trainees reached through the same announcements were invited to participate.

Participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to the intervention or control group using a computer-generated allocation sequence prepared by a study investigator not involved in outcome assessment. Study staff enrolled participants and disclosed group assignment at the beginning of each training session. Baseline assessment was completed after allocation disclosure and before any study-related training activity.

Interventions

All participants received a standardized curriculum consisting of:

A 30-minute didactic lecture covering principles of airway management.

A 40-minute simulation-based practical session using airway manikins, facilitated by anesthesiology faculty.

In addition, participants in the intervention group completed a 40-minute session with PLAN A, a tabletop serious game designed to reinforce decision-making in airway emergencies. The control group received no further intervention.

PLAN A game description

PLAN A is a tabletop serious game designed to rehearse airway decision-making and escalation across progressively complex scenarios using instrument/strategy and patient-case cards (Fig. 1). At the start of play, each participant receives a hand of instrument and strategy cards. On each turn, a player draws a patient card describing a clinical case, including contextual information and anticipated airway difficulty and/or laryngoscopic findings. To resolve the case, players select interventions from their hand and combine instruments and techniques according to predefined rules aligned with established airway management algorithms, including the Vortex Approach and major difficult airway guidelines from the American Society of Anesthesiologists and the Difficult Airway Society [10–12].

PLAN A tabletop serious game. Representative image of the PLAN A card-based format, including the instrument/strategy deck, patient scenario cards, and a selection of airway device and pharmacologic intervention cards. A surgical airway card is shown as an escalation option within the game rules

Certain interventions are gated by an experience threshold; players accrue experience by successfully resolving earlier cases, which unlocks access to higher-level interventions in subsequent turns. When a player lacks the required cards or experience to resolve a case, the rules allow requesting assistance from other players through predefined support actions (e.g., sharing a card or contributing an unlocked capability). Cases that cannot be resolved at the time of drawing may be deferred and revisited once the required resources are available. When escalation is required, a surgical airway option is available as a defined end-point pathway within the game rules. Invalid or clinically implausible combinations of interventions (e.g., mutually incompatible devices used simultaneously) trigger predefined penalties, including loss of accrued experience. Each resolved scenario yields points and experience values that contribute to individual progression.

For transparency and reproducibility, we provide a concise facilitator guide and standardized gameplay instructions in the Supplementary Materials, including setup, turn structure, scoring, experience thresholds, predefined support actions, and end-point escalation pathways used during the intervention (Supplementary Appendix S1).

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the change in performance on a written, case-based clinical assessment administered immediately before and after the intervention. The assessment consisted of six open-ended clinical vignettes, each depicting a distinct airway management scenario. Responses were scored using a standardized rubric (0–3 points per item; maximum total score 18 points) that emphasized three domains: recognition of difficult airway predictors, appropriateness of sequential management plans (Plans A, B, and C), and clarity of clinical reasoning.

The vignettes and scoring rubric were developed through an expert content review process. Three board-certified anesthesiologists with recognized expertise in airway management education met with the study team to review the draft materials, including the case prompts, the proposed scoring anchors, and the planned assessment workflow. During this session, the panel evaluated clinical plausibility, item relevance, scoring clarity, and alignment with established airway management algorithms and difficult airway guidelines, specifically the Vortex Approach and guidelines from the American Society of Anesthesiologists and the Difficult Airway Society [10–12]. Feedback from the panel led to minor revisions to improve wording consistency and scoring objectivity. This process supported the content validity of the instrument; however, no formal psychometric validation (e.g., inter-rater reliability testing, internal consistency, or factor analysis) was performed. Statistical analysis

The primary objective of this study was to determine whether the addition of PLAN A to conventional instruction improved participants’ case-based clinical reasoning in airway management. Accordingly, we analyzed changes in pre- and post-training assessment scores within and between groups.

Descriptive statistics summarized baseline characteristics and outcomes, reported as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range) for continuous variables, and counts (percentages) for categorical variables. Given the small sample size, between-group comparisons of baseline variables and post-training scores were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Within-group changes from pre- to post-test were evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Categorical variables (e.g., gender) were compared using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were small.

Responses to the case-based assessment were scored using the prespecified standardized rubric (0–3 points per item) reviewed by airway experts. All assessments were graded by a single evaluator who was blinded to group allocation and assessment timepoint (pre vs. post). Scoring followed the rubric’s explicit anchors for each domain. Prior to scoring study responses, the evaluator completed a calibration exercise using a set of sample responses and the rubric to standardize application of scoring rules.

The evaluator’s role and qualifications were an anesthesia faculty member experienced in airway education and assessment. Scoring was performed independently without access to participant identifiers.

A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No correction for multiple testing was applied, consistent with the exploratory nature of this pilot trial.

All analyses were conducted using RStudio (R version 4.3.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing) with the packages tidyverse (data management), rstatix (non-parametric testing), and ggplot2 (visualization). Analyses were independently cross-validated in Python (version 3.11, Python Software Foundation) using pandas, numpy, scipy.stats, and matplotlib. This dual-platform approach enhanced reproducibility and transparency of the findings.

A CONSORT-style flow diagram was constructed to depict the progression of participants through the trial. This includes enrollment, randomization, group allocation, follow-up, and analysis. The diagram visually summarizes participant inclusion and exclusions, adherence to interventions, and data availability for analysis, in alignment with CONSORT 2010 recommendations for reporting randomized trials. Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 34 participants were enrolled. Allocation was balanced, with 18 participants (52.9%) in the intervention group and 16 (47.1%) in the control group. Most participants were medical interns (73.5%), followed by first-year residents (26.5%). First-year residents represented anesthesiology, general surgery, emergency medicine, and internal medicine. Regarding prior airway exposure, 67.6% reported limited exposure and 32.4% reported no exposure; limited exposure did not meet exclusion criteria because it referred to observation or basic assistance without performing advanced airway procedures, as specified in the Methods. No adverse events, participant withdrawals, or unintended educational harms were identified during the study.

Participants represented multiple medical schools in Mexico City. Baseline performance on the six-case assessment was low, with an overall mean item score of 0.81 (SD 0.45) on the 0–3 rubric, corresponding to a mean total score of 4.85 (SD 2.69) out of 18. After training, overall performance increased to a mean item score of 1.51 (SD 0.33) and a mean total score of 9.24 (SD 1.95) out of 18.

All participants (n = 34) completed both the pre- and post-intervention assessments. At baseline, both groups demonstrated low performance, consistent with their limited prior training. The intervention group (n = 18) achieved a median pre-test score of 4.5 (mean 4.61 ± 2.52), whereas the control group (n = 16) achieved a median pre-test score of 5.0 (mean 5.12 ± 2.92); this difference was not statistically significant. Following training, both groups showed significant improvement from baseline (intervention group: p < 0.001; control group: p < 0.001, Wilcoxon signed-rank test). The intervention group reached a median post-test score of 10.0 (mean 9.06 ± 2.26), while the control group reached a median of 10.0 (mean 9.44 ± 1.59). The median gain was 4.5 in the intervention group and 4.0 in the control group. A direct comparison of post-intervention scores between groups showed no statistically significant difference (U = 137.5, p = 0.83, Mann–Whitney U test).

Item-level analysis

To gain deeper insight into how participants approached each clinical scenario, we conducted an item-level analysis comparing pre- and post-test performance across the six cases in both study arms. Within-group pre–post comparisons showed significant improvement in total score in the intervention group and in the control group (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, both p < 0.001), indicating a strong overall educational effect of the session. At the case level, the intervention group demonstrated significant pre–post gains in five of six scenarios (Cases 1, 2, 3, 5, and 6), including the failed intubation scenario (Case 5). Notably, the largest within-group improvements in the intervention arm occurred in scenarios that are most directly aligned with PLAN A’s instructional targets—structured escalation, rapid re-appraisal, and decision-making under time pressure—supporting the intervention’s educational plausibility at the level of clinical reasoning. The control group also improved in most scenarios (Cases 1, 2, 4, 5, and 6), suggesting that learning was not exclusive to the game condition. When comparing change scores (post − pre) between groups, no statistically significant differences were observed for any individual case or for the total score (all p > 0.05). Accordingly, these item-level findings support robust learning in both groups and are consistent with PLAN A contributing to clinically relevant reasoning gains, while the incremental effect of the game beyond standard instruction remains hypothesis-generating in this sample. Participant feedback

Exploratory, informal feedback was obtained from participants in the intervention group. Learners consistently described PLAN A as engaging, interactive, and helpful in clarifying the rationale for escalation pathways and device prioritization. Several emphasized that the game fostered collaborative discussion and active reasoning, in contrast to the more passive learning dynamics of lectures. Although not formally measured, these perceptions are consistent with the observed quantitative advantage in complex scenarios, underscoring the potential of serious games to complement traditional methods by increasing motivation and learner engagement.

Discussion

This study explored the educational impact of PLAN A, a tabletop card game, on the airway management knowledge of novice medical trainees. In this pilot randomized controlled trial, we did not detect a statistically significant between-group difference in total case-based assessment scores. Both groups demonstrated improvement from pre to post, consistent with learning after structured airway instruction. Taken together, these findings support the feasibility of implementing PLAN A as an add-on activity and highlight the need for adequately powered, time-matched studies to determine whether serious games provide incremental benefit beyond conventional training.

Placed in the broader serious games literature, our findings align with a heterogeneous evidence base. While some randomized trials have reported superiority of game-based learning over traditional methods (13, 14), other studies mirror our results. For example, Adjedj et al. reported that serious games were more engaging for medical students but did not yield significantly higher knowledge retention compared with multiple-choice questioning (15). This variability, also noted in systematic reviews and meta-analyses (16), suggests that outcomes may depend less on the game format per se and more on the underlying instructional design, including whether the intervention targets complex decision-making and escalation under uncertainty rather than simple factual recall.

Within this framework, the intervention group showed better scenario-level post-intervention performance relative to baseline in the case requiring airway difficulty recognition, defined here as identifying anticipated difficulty and the likely domain of difficulty (e.g., mask ventilation, laryngoscopy, or intubation) to guide escalation. The largest scenario-specific gain was observed in Case 5 (failed intubation), measured as an increase in case score on the prespecified 0–3 rubric (Table 3). This scenario is clinically relevant because delayed recognition and rescue of a failed airway contributes to preventable morbidity (1), and the combination of difficult mask ventilation with difficult laryngoscopy, although uncommon, is associated with high risk (17). These findings support the hypothesis that PLAN A may help trainees rehearse escalation and rescue decision points in high-stakes scenarios, even when overall between-group differences in total scores were not statistically significant. Item-level analyses were exploratory and were not designed to support definitive between-group conclusions.

However, the absence of a statistically significant between-group difference should also be interpreted in light of measurement and design considerations, including the measurement properties of the case-based instrument. Although the assessment captured baseline case-based reasoning and detected pre–post improvement, its ability to discriminate higher-order clinical reasoning may have been limited. Post-intervention total scores clustered around ~ 9–10 points out of 18 in both groups, suggesting limited sensitivity of a brief written rubric-based assessment to detect incremental differences attributable to the serious-game add-on. In addition, compression of the total-score distribution after training is consistent with a potential ceiling effect, which could further reduce sensitivity to small between-group differences. Future studies should therefore incorporate additional outcome measures with greater discriminative capacity, including performance-based outcomes such as high-fidelity simulation or OSCE-style assessments to evaluate escalation behaviors and decision-making under time pressure, and should formally evaluate the reliability and responsiveness of the assessment tool (2, 3).

A key design limitation is that the control group did not receive a time-matched active control task. An active control condition with equivalent instructional time would have strengthened internal validity and interpretability. Without time-matching, any observed differences between groups could reflect additional instructional exposure rather than a game-specific effect. Future studies should incorporate time-matched comparators (e.g., case-based discussion or alternative non-game activities) to isolate the specific contribution of the serious game and support causal inference.

From a pedagogical perspective, the tabletop format of PLAN A may offer advantages consistent with social constructivist learning theories. Unlike solitary screen-based simulations, a physical card game can require face-to-face negotiation and social play, which Pellis and Pellis describe as important for developing social competence and behavioral flexibility (18). This aligns with Vygotsky’s framework of tool-mediated learning, in which peer interaction can scaffold clinical reasoning (19). By verbalizing strategies and negotiating choices, trainees engage in collaborative learning processes described by Alexiou and Schippers (6), which may support communication and leadership behaviors relevant to airway management. The cooperative gameplay format may approximate team-based airway management workflows and encourage early communication and escalation; repeated exposure to structured rescue pathways may help trainees rehearse transition points when initial strategies fail.

In addition, the low-tech and physical nature of tabletop games may support implementation in settings with constrained resources. Compared with high-fidelity simulation or virtual reality platforms that require specialized equipment and infrastructure, tabletop games can be portable and reproducible, which may facilitate dissemination and local adaptation for airway education in resource-limited environments.

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size limits statistical power and generalizability. Second, baseline heterogeneity in prior airway exposure may have affected learning gains and reduced our ability to detect between-group differences in this pilot sample. Third, although the case-based assessment and rubric underwent expert content review, the instrument was not formally validated; therefore, score interpretation warrants caution. Fourth, use of a single blinded evaluator may introduce rater bias despite rubric-based scoring; future studies should include multiple evaluators and report inter-rater reliability. Future studies should enroll larger samples, stratify randomization by baseline exposure, incorporate time-matched comparators, and assess longer-term outcomes including knowledge retention and transfer to clinical performance. In addition, formal psychometric validation of assessment tools and structured qualitative evaluation of learner engagement and implementation factors would strengthen the evidence base for tabletop serious games in medical education.

Conclusion

Educational games like PLAN A can be promising low-cost, low-tech interventions that may enhance learner engagement and comprehension in airway management education. While results are preliminary, this study contributes early evidence that serious games can complement traditional teaching and may improve performance in specific clinical scenarios. Further investigation is warranted to confirm these findings and explore broader applications in clinical training.
