Hybrid type 1 randomized controlled trial of technology-assisted stepped care behavioral health intervention for caregivers and children following pediatric traumatic injury
Authors: Leigh E Ridings, Kari N Thomsen, Margaret A Prentice, Ebonie Powell, Gabriela Becerra, Martina Mueller, Rochelle Hanson, Christian J Streck, Peter Ehrlich, Linda Ewing-Cobbs, Meghan L Marsac, Ryan Spurrier, Tatiana Davidson, Kenneth J Ruggiero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPediatric traumatic injury (PTI) has substantial physical and emotional health impacts, sometimes resulting in hospitalization and/or the development of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and depression in children. Caregivers are a key source of support for children following PTI; however, many caregivers experience their own distress and psychological reactions following injury. The CAARE (Caregivers’ Aid to Accelerate Recovery after pediatric Emergencies) model is a technology-assisted, stepped care intervention to support the emotional and behavioral recovery of preadolescent children hospitalized after PTI and their caregivers. The CAARE model seeks to provide a systematic, evidence-based screening and intervention procedure that is feasible to implement within pediatric trauma centers. This protocol paper describes a study to evaluate the effectiveness of the CAARE model in improving quality of life, clinical and functional outcomes, and treatment engagement in children and their caregivers following PTI. We also aim to explore potential inequities regarding race, ethnicity, and injury type. This hybrid type 1 effectiveness-implementation trial design allows for the examination of contextual factors related to implementation of CAARE. This paper includes a description of the intervention and control conditions, participant selection and recruitment procedures, assessment measures, and planned quantitative and qualitative methodologies. Potential implications and future directions are discussed.This study was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04579198).Keywords: Pediatric traumatic injury, Hybrid type 1 trial, Stepped care intervention, Digital mental health, Caregiver support

Introduction

Approximately 8 million children receive emergency care due to injury annually, over 125,000 of whom experience pediatric traumatic injury (PTI). PTI includes injuries of sufficient severity that often warrant hospitalization. Examples of PTIs include motor vehicle crashes, falls, animal attacks, gunshot wounds, non-accidental trauma, or being struck by a car or object [1]. About one-third of these children develop posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and/or depression within the first year following PTI, which are risk factors for poor physical recovery, social and school-related impairment, and disruption of roles and routines [2–5]. Moreover, greater than 50% of caregivers of children with PTI are highly distressed in the acute stages of recovery and themselves have high risk of PTSD and depression [6,7]. Caregivers are a key source of support for preadolescent children following PTI, and caregivers’ emotional health is highly correlated with children’s outcomes [8–10], highlighting the need for interventions targeting caregiver and young child distress following PTI. Pediatric trauma centers recognize the need to support behavioral recovery, particularly in response to the American College of Surgeons’ 2022 guidelines to provide mental health screening and intervention to trauma patients at high risk [11]. However, no clear roadmap exists for these centers to support emotional recovery. Established, scalable and sustainable models of care are lacking.

These challenges are compounded by existing inequities across race, ethnicity, and injury type. Minority youth face disproportionate trauma exposure yet are significantly less likely to access mental health care [12]. Nearly 50% of U.S. children with a mental health disorder do not receive timely treatment, and children from racial and ethnic minority groups experience even greater disparities in access and outcomes [13]. Latinx youth report high rates of violence exposure and poorer mental health outcomes and treatment rates compared to their White counterparts [14,15]. Similarly, Black youth experience elevated posttraumatic stress symptoms through higher polyvictimization and exposure to violent death [16]. Injury type further exacerbates mental health risk, as children with firearm injuries have 55% increased odds of a new mental health diagnosis compared to those with motor vehicle injuries [17]. These disparities underscore the need for intervention models that not only address emotional recovery broadly but also intentionally examine and address inequities across race, ethnicity, and injury type.

The CAARE (Caregivers’ Aid to Accelerate Recovery after pediatric Emergencies) model is a technology-assisted, stepped care intervention (i.e., a model that adjusts service intensity based on symptom monitoring, stepping patients up to more intensive services or down to discontinuation as clinically indicated) for young children (<12 years) hospitalized after PTI and their caregivers. We conducted pilot testing of CAARE with 54 caregiver-child pairs diverse with regard to child age, sex, race, and injury type. Of the 75 caregiver-child pairs approached, 54 were consented (72% enrollment rate), and only 2 withdrew due to competing time demands. Forty-nine downloaded the CAARE app and 68% responded to at least one text-based symptom monitoring prompt. A total of 62% completed the 30-day behavioral health screen. Pilot testing demonstrated that the model was well-received by caregivers and providers and has high potential to address service barriers by providing real-time assistance to help caregivers manager their own distress and their children’s emotional and behavioral recovery.

This protocol paper describes an innovative hybrid type 1 effectiveness-implementation study – a design that primarily evaluates clinical intervention effectiveness while secondarily gathering implementation-related data [18] – that seeks to address three aims: 1) examine the effectiveness of CAARE in improving quality of life, clinical symptoms, functional outcomes, and follow-up treatment engagement compared to enhanced usual care (EUC); 2) explore potential inequities with respect to race, ethnicity, and injury type (e.g., violent injury) and identify opportunities to address them in the CAARE model; and 3) examine inner and outer contextual factors related to implementation of CAARE.

Method

Study overview

The CAARE model

A hybrid type 1 effectiveness-implementation design was proposed to evaluate and inform future dissemination and implementation of CAARE. CAARE was developed based on guidance from caregivers through needs assessments, quality improvement initiatives, and national survey data from U.S. pediatric trauma centers [19–25]. The CAARE model is designed for children aged <12 years hospitalized after PTI and their caregivers who receive brief, validated acute screens for child distress and caregiver distress. CAARE targets children younger than 12 years because 1) caregivers remain the primary mediators of children’s coping and emotional recovery during the preadolescent developmental period [26,27]; 2) adolescents respond differently to caregiver-mediated behavioral strategies than younger children [28]; and 3) research evaluating child PTSD interventions often uses age 12 years as the cutoff to differentiate child from adolescent samples [29]. CAARE’s focus on caregivers is designed to optimize the developmental period during which caregivers demonstrate a significant influence on children’s behavioral health. Children and/or caregivers who screen positive for acute distress receive CAARE via four steps.

Step 1 includes a brief bedside risk-reduction intervention delivered by an embedded healthcare professional (e.g., social worker, psychologist). The intervention is tailored to meet the needs of the family and provides education, demonstration, and role play of basic coping strategies. Education about avoidance, potential trauma triggers, and common child responses to trauma is included. The intervention also includes guidance on identification and scheduling of healthy activities consistent with principles of behavioral activation [30,31], as well as leveraging of social and family support. During Step 2, patients are provided access to two digital mobile health (mHealth) tools beginning in the hospital: a Smartphone application (CAARE app) and a text-message-based symptom monitoring service. The CAARE app reinforces risk-reduction strategies taught in-hospital via Step 1, such as caregiver-child activities to improve mood, audio narrations to guide relaxation skills, interactive activities to target PTSD symptoms and avoidance, and information about local and national mental health resources to address impairing trauma symptoms. The 30-day automated text messaging services facilitate symptom tracking, an evidence-based approach recommended as a frontline intervention during the first 30 days following a traumatic event [2]. Caregivers receive daily messages for 30 consecutive days that include 1) one question assessing their own emotional recovery post-injury, followed by a corresponding educational tip when endorsed and 2) one question assessing their child’s emotional/behavioral recovery post-injury, followed by a corresponding educational tip when endorsed to promote recovery in that area. Questions for caregivers’ own emotional recovery included six items from the Kessler-6 [32] as well as four additional items developed for the service assessing worry, avoidance, trauma-related distress, and sleep quality [19]. Questions for children’s behavioral recovery included seven items from the Early Childhood Screening Assessment [33] and three additional items from the Pediatric Symptom Checklist [34] to assess emotions and behaviors commonly observed in children after traumatic events. The text messaging service has been described in previous work [35]. A growing body of evidence supports the effectiveness of asynchronous, technology-facilitated interventions for trauma-related symptoms. This includes RCTs supporting the effectiveness of smartphone apps on trauma-related mental health symptoms [36,37] and meta-analytic support that digital mental health interventions – particularly those incorporating Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)-based techniques – are associated with medium-to-large effects on PTSD symptoms [38,39]. However, self-management apps alone may not be sufficient to address trauma symptoms [40], which lends support for CAARE’s stepped-care model where digital health tools are offered as one component of a broader intervention framework.

In Step 3, behavioral health screens are completed 30 days post-injury. Participants complete a brief, automated behavioral health screen assessing caregiver and child PTSD, depression, and child externalizing behaviors. Results of this screen determine if there is a need for a more intensive screening by a behavioral health provider. During Step 4, caregivers and children who do not have clinically elevated symptoms will discontinue CAARE services. Those with positive symptoms from the Step 3 screening are stepped up to a comprehensive screen-and-refer process. During this process, the trauma team contacts caregivers and uses a shared decision-making approach to discuss the results of the behavioral health screen, refer to providers who specialize in evidence-based treatments, discuss barriers to care, and invite them to contact the team for consultation around behavioral health services at any time. The 30-day timepoint for behavioral health screening was chosen because 1) PTSD cannot be diagnosed until symptoms have persisted for at least one month following a traumatic event, per DSM-5 diagnostic criteria [41] (prior to this threshold, symptoms may represent acute stress disorder, a transient response that typically remits within one month in approximately half of trauma survivors); 2) prevalence of PTSD in children decreases by about 50% in the first 1–6 months post-trauma, and symptoms beyond this window are not likely to remit without intervention [42]; and 3) the CAARE model is designed as an early prevention and intervention framework that actively supports families during the acute recovery phase while also addressing persisting symptoms warranting more thorough intervention early in the recovery process. This approach is consistent with recommendations by the ACS Best Practice Guidelines [43] and the International Society for Traumatic Stress Studies [26].

Training and implementation protocol

Prior to implementation of the randomized controlled trial (RCT; Aim 1), study staff will be selected based on prior training, discipline, and experience. Site personnel will undergo a robust, 2-week hybrid (virtual and onsite) training process to equip them with the necessary skills to successfully approach and enroll trauma patients and their caregivers in the study. At each site, the study team will consist of a minimum of one site PI, one part-time clinical interventionist, and 1–2 part-time research coordinators. The first week will include four half-days conducted virtually. The second week will consist of a 2–3-full day onsite visit with each site to provide direct, hands-on training led by the study investigator and CAARE coordinator. See Fig. 1 for an overview of this training process. After the onsite training, biweekly all-site coordinator meetings and monthly all-site PI meetings will be scheduled to ensure clinical and research protocol fidelity, discuss recruitment milestones and data management, and problem-solve barriers to recruitment or to implementation of research and/or clinical protocols.

Aim 1: examining the effectiveness of CAARE

Participants and recruitment

Three hundred, forty-eight caregiver-child pairs will be recruited from four partnering Level 1 pediatric trauma centers across the Western, Southern, and Midwestern United States. The four sites are geographically dispersed, admit a moderate to high volume of patients within the target age range (0–11 years) each year, and do not currently offer embedded, systematic behavioral health services. Inclusion criteria are (1) caregivers (at least 18 years old) of children hospitalized with PTI and (2) children hospitalized with PTI <12 years old that (3) screen positive on the Acute Stress Checklist for Children (ASC-Kids, aged 8–11 years) [26,44,45]; or Peritraumatic Distress Inventory (PDI) – Caregiver [46,47] measure of acute distress. The ASC-Kids and PDI are standard measures to identify acute stress and risk for PTSD and depression in children and caregivers after pediatric injury, respectively. Families will be included if either or both caregiver and child screen positive. Past work strongly supports the relation between caregiver psychological functioning and child trauma-related outcomes [48,49], indicating that it is important to manage the emotional recovery of both child and caregiver following trauma. Exclusion criteria include: (1) a caregiver who does not speak English or Spanish; (2) a cognitive challenge (caregiver or child) that would impair ability to consent; (3) presence of self-inflicted injury; (4) presence of injuries resulting from caregiver abuse or neglect (these patients will follow an alternative treatment path).

Potentially eligible patients will be identified via electronic medical record using each site’s daily trauma registry report. Families with negative screens on both the ASC-Kids and PDI will not be eligible for the study and will receive brief education about emotional and behavioral health recovery as part of standard care. If a patient or caregiver both meet inclusion criteria and either one has a positive screening result, they will be considered eligible and will be informed about the study by research staff at each site. Study staff at each site will obtain consent from caregivers who are interested in participating. In cases where two primary caregivers are present in the hospital, caregivers will be asked to select which one of them will complete the screen and enroll, but both caregivers will be given access to the mHealth CAARE components if requested. Randomization

Participants will be randomized to the CAARE or EUC study conditions. This study aims to recruit 174 caregiver-child pairs for each arm (n = 348 total). Families will be randomized to study arms using a permuted block randomization strategy with varying block sizes, stratified by site, child age, and race/ethnicity.

Enhanced usual care (EUC)

Participants in the EUC arm will be provided an educational packet at the bedside that includes behavioral health education for children and families after PTI, common trauma symptoms and how to recognize them in themselves and their child, a list of evidence-based treatments for common symptoms, and a list of local and national emotional and behavioral health resources.

Assessments

Participants will complete automated, post-baseline assessments related to quality of life, service engagement, and clinical and functional outcomes validated in English and Spanish over a 1-year period post-injury (i.e., 3-, 6-, and 12-months post-baseline). See Table 1 for a description of measures used. Surveys will be administered by study personnel at each site electronically or in person per participant preference. Pilot data demonstrated that retention was significantly improved when follow-up screens were automatically administered via REDCap-generated emails or texts versus via telephone calls by study staff. Thus, these automated methods will also be used in this project. Automated reminders will be sent to participants who do not respond within 2 days and then again at 5 days. For those who still do not respond by 7 days, they will be contacted by a trained interviewer blinded to study condition to complete screens via telephone. Participant medical record data (e.g., injury severity score, injury mechanism, length of hospital stay) will be retrieved by study personnel at each site.

Aim 2: exploring potential inequities

Qualitative interviews with caregivers from marginalized racial and ethnic groups (i.e., non-White and/or Latinx), as well as underrepresented trauma groups (e.g., violent injury, burn) after the 6-month follow-up will be conducted. Interviews will assess reactions to the CAARE model and the child’s or caregiver’s experiences with each step, including reactions to education and brief intervention components (e. g., “How well did the brief session during your child’s hospital stay meet your family’s individual needs?”) and reasons for use, nonuse, or variable use of digital health elements (e.g., “What would you change about the CAARE app or texting service to improve likelihood that you would use it in the weeks following your child’s hospitalization?” “What features would you add to make them more helpful?”). Interviews will also ask for feedback on initiation and completion of treatment, as well as how decisions for recommended follow-up treatment, if indicated, were navigated at the final step (e.g., “What issues were most important for your family at the time of follow-up about a month after your child’s injury?” “What problems or challenges did you or your child face to finding a therapist if you needed it?”). Further, the interviews will explore how to tailor content and support to their unique needs. Twenty-five caregivers will be purposively recruited from the CAARE group to ensure representation across marginalized racial (~10 Black), ethnic (~10 Latino/a/x), and underrepresented trauma (i.e., ~5–10 with firearm or burn injury) groups. Because individuals from racial and ethnic minorities are disproportionately represented among violent injury groups, we anticipate some overlap across these categories and some participants may be recruited to represent more than one sub-group. The interview guide will be structured to explore experiences related to both racial/ethnic identity and injury type to allow us to examine how these factors influence engagement with and perceptions of the CAARE model. Both high engagers and low/moderate engagers in the CAARE model will be recruited. CAARE engagement will be determined by assessing level of engagement with each component of the model, including number of interactions in the CAARE app, number of responses to the symptom monitoring service, and engagement at the time of the 30-day screen and treatment referral. A table of barriers and facilitators associated with engagement at each step will be generated and used to supplement and enhance findings from quantitative data collection in Aim 1. Together, these data will inform refinements to the CAARE model to better meet the needs of underserved and high-risk patients. All interviews are audio-recorded and transcribed for analysis. Participants will receive $40 for their time.

Aim 3: Examine inner/outer contextual factors related to implementation of CAARE

Aim 3 is guided by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [50]. CFIR is a conceptual framework to guide the systematic evaluation of factors that might influence intervention implementation and effectiveness. CFIR consists of five domains that house 39 constructs which can influence implementation efforts: 1) Intervention characteristics (e.g., relative advantage, complexity); 2) Outer setting (e.g., patient needs and resources); 3) Inner setting (e.g., implementation climate, engagement); 4) Characteristics of individuals involved (e.g., knowledge and beliefs about the intervention); and 5) Implementation efforts (e.g., planning, engaging, executing). These constructs can be used to assess ongoing implementation efforts to determine which strategies work best for each site and under which conditions. Here, three main targets are being examined: acceptability (i.e., perception among stakeholders that CAARE is satisfactory), feasibility (i.e., extent to which CAARE can be delivered successfully within trauma centers), and appropriateness (i.e., perceived fit and relevance of CAARE for trauma patients by trauma centers and CAARE’s capacity to meet families’ behavior health needs). Acceptability, feasibility, and appropriateness will be examined during each of three study phases: pre-implementation, implementation, and post-implementation.

Procedures

During the pre-implementation phase, the research team will conduct direct observations at each partnering site and will record observations via field notes. Specifically, we will assess each site’s resources (e.g., number of beds, referral networks), staff roles and responsibilities (e.g., who provides which service), interactions between staff and patients (e.g., timeliness, care coordination), and organizational workflow (e.g., team structure, communication).

During the implementation phase, biweekly consultation calls between each site and main study site staff will occur to address implementation barriers and facilitators, which will be recorded via field notes. Site coordinators will also systematically report on progress of study activities, record barriers and facilitators, and document implementation strategies.

During the post-implementation phase, individual, semi-structured qualitative interviews (informed by field notes and quantitative implementation data) will be conducted with key constituents (i.e., pediatric trauma directors, trauma program managers, and clinical coordinators who delivered CAARE and EUC) from all four sites. Interviews will assess perceived barriers and facilitators to CAARE delivery, overall impressions of the CAARE model, and identify potential implementation strategies to maximize its reach. Interviews with trauma directors will target CFIR constructs related to policies and laws, funding from external entities to support implementation, quality or benchmarking metrics that will drive implementation, and cost. Interviews with trauma program managers and coordinators will target CFIR constructs related to organizational infrastructure, team connections and communication, and roles and characteristics of providers implementing CAARE. Interviews will take approximately 45 min and will be audio recorded and transcribed for analysis.

In Year 4, we will virtually conduct an implementation “premortem” exercise with key pediatric trauma center stakeholders from three additional trauma centers not partnering on this study to generate potential threats to the future widescale implementation of CAARE. The premortem group will consist of trauma center directors (n = 1 per site), program managers (n = 1 per site), and clinical providers (e.g., social work, advanced practice providers; n ~ 1 per site) from three additional pediatric trauma centers (~9 total staff members or 3 per site). Pre-mortem techniques have potential to guide future implementation strategies [51] as they leverage prospective hindsight via a brainstorming session between key stakeholders and field leaders to anticipate potential failures and identify possible solutions to address it. Trauma center stakeholders participating in the premortem exercise will be recruited nationally from US pediatric trauma centers.

Data analysis plan

Aim 1

General analytic approach.

This study first seeks to examine the effectiveness of CAARE in improving quality of life, clinical symptoms, functional outcomes, and follow-up treatment engagement compared to EUC. Descriptive statistics will be used to characterize demographic and clinical baseline features, as appropriate. Distributional assumptions underlying the proposed statistical tests will be examined; if assumptions are not met, appropriate alternative methods will be considered. All randomized participants will be included in the intent-to-treat (ITT) sample for primary analyses. Participants for whom all required measurements have been made and who were compliant with protocol requirements will be included in a secondary per-protocol (completer) analysis. Sensitivity analysis will then be carried out via comparison of findings from ITT and per-protocol samples to investigate the impact of nonadherence and attrition on our conclusions. Several approaches to handling missing data will be considered in the ITT analysis set. Longitudinal methods (e.g., generalized linear models) capable of dealing with missing data will be used for analysis of longitudinal outcome data [52,53]. Little and Rubin’s [54] multiple imputation methods will be used for continuous variables using a single end-of-study value (e.g., difference in change over time).

Primary and secondary outcome analyses.

We will apply several analytic methods to examine whether receipt of the CAARE intervention affects primary and secondary outcomes. For example, the proportion of participants (pairs) who engage in follow-up services will be compared between the two groups via a chi-squared test. To examine component effects at the 3 time points and over time on primary outcomes, different analytical methods will be used. First, we will assess effects between study arms on PTSD and depression by comparing mean PTSD scores between the CAARE and EUC groups using two-sample t-tests (or non-parametric tests as appropriate).

Second, a generalized linear models (GLM) approach will be used as a general analytic framework for inferential analyses. GLM accommodates missing data, correlation among repeated measurements, and a wide range of distributional assumptions for outcome variables through specification of link functions. Appropriate link functions (e.g., identity function for continuous outcomes and logit function for categorical measures) will be applied in the models with group assignment as the primary independent variable. The difference in change over time (i.e., the 3-, 6-, or 12-month timepoints) will be compared between the CAARE and EUC groups. This will be modeled by inclusion of a group-by-time point interaction term, with month 12 being considered the primary endpoint.

Covariates and moderators.

Further, models will be adjusted for site and the stratification variables of child age and race/ethnicity. Caregiver and child sex as biological variables will be investigated for potential differences in outcomes. We will also explore candidate variables that may moderate the effect of CAARE/EUC on quality of life, clinical outcomes, functional outcomes, and follow-up treatment engagement. These include sociodemographic, setting (rural vs. urban, trauma center size), and injury-related (violent injury, penetrating trauma) correlates. These variables will be included as fixed effects in the models.

Dyadic effects.

We will explore the effect of outcomes from one member of the child-caregiver pair on those of the other member (e.g., a model with child quality of life outcome as the dependent variable would be adjusted for caregiver quality of life or vice versa).

Sample size and power analysis.

Sample size and power considerations were based on the primary clinical outcomes of quality of life, PTSD, depression, child externalizing behaviors, as well as secondary dependent variables of engagement in follow-up services and functional outcomes. For categorical outcomes, we will require 240 child-caregiver pairs (n = 120 per group) to achieve >80% power to detect a difference in group proportions of 0.18, assuming the proportion in the control group is 0.5. For continuous outcomes, based on a 0.5 standard deviation benchmark for minimally important clinical differences proposed by Norman and colleagues [55], 120 caregiver-child pairs per treatment group will provide >95% power to detect a standardized effect size for continuous outcomes of 0.50 SDs, assuming complete data. The desired sample size was inflated by 30% to account for drop-out after randomization and the dilution effect of ITT analysis, leading to a desired final sample size of n = 348 for ITT analysis.

Aims 2 and 3

This study also seeks to explore potential inequities with respect to race, ethnicity, and injury type (Aim 2), and to examine inner and outer contextual factors related to implementation of CAARE (Aim 3). All interviews will be audio-recorded and transcribed by a professional transcription service. An abductive thematic analysis approach [56] will be employed for coding data to identify both emerging and pre-identified themes from interview transcripts (NVivo15 software). Content analysis of responses will be conducted by two coders who will independently generate lists of thematic categories and subcategories. Coders will then meet to discuss identified categories, resolve questions, and refine themes. Once complete, higher-order categories will be developed. A third study member will meet with coders during development of the codebook and as needed to resolve discrepancies. Inter-rater reliability will be continuously assessed with goals for thematic coding agreement of 80%. For Aim 3, we will create a matrix to combine the qualitative data with quantitative measures of implementation outcomes to examine inner and outer contextual factors related to implementation of CAARE.

Conclusions

At the time of manuscript submission, recruitment and data collection for this study are currently underway. No data have been analyzed. All aspects of this federally funded study have been approved by the institutional review board (IRB) at the institutions where this research is being conducted.

This project aims to assess the extent to which CAARE, a novel, sustainable service model, improves children’s and caregiver’s quality of life, clinical and functional health outcomes, and follow-up treatment engagement following PTI. The hybrid type 1 effectiveness-implementation design will allow for the examination of CAARE’s effectiveness, as well as assess CAARE’s acceptability, feasibility, and appropriateness and identify barriers and facilitators that influence implementation, sustainability, and ability to equitably address the needs of families from underrepresented racial, ethnic, and trauma groups.

The ultimate goal of this line of work is to achieve population-level impact in U.S. pediatric trauma centers through enhanced service quality, reach, and equity. While there has been a clear call for the need to support emotional and behavioral recovery following traumatic injury [11], there remains no clear guideline for pediatric trauma centers to follow. CAARE seeks to provide a systematic, evidence-based screening and intervention model that is feasible to implement for pediatric trauma centers with a wide range of resources and support. This stepped-care model provides a cost-efficient, sustainable, systems-level strategy for pediatric trauma centers to identify, track, and actively support patients’ and families’ emotional and behavioral recovery after PTI. This model uniquely aims to support hospital systems’ efficiency and effectiveness in improving access to behavioral healthcare among families that otherwise likely would not know how and when to seek help on their own. The CAARE model’s stepped approach is feasible and integrates innovative digital health tools that may cost-efficiently improve service reach and reduce provider burden [22,35].

Initiating CAARE at the bedside immediately after PTI uniquely capitalizes on the ability of hospital systems to identify high-risk patients, intervene early, achieve continuity of care, provide active communication and recommendations, and facilitate timely access to best-practice treatment. This approach may serve as an important model for other stepped care approaches. Under more traditional models, best-practice treatment for traumatic stress populations typically is received by patients months or even years after a traumatic event, after patients have experienced symptoms and functional impairment over a long period of time. Further, CAARE is designed to address clinical and functional outcomes of both caregivers and their young children. Other PTI interventions typically target only the caregiver or the child, focus mostly on older children, and do not provide coping skills training, self-care tools, or mental health referrals [45,57,58]. This study uniquely will allow us to examine how symptom trajectories among children and caregivers correlate to one another over time in the context of the intervention. This expands beyond studies that have examined these associations via cross-sectional research.

Implementation challenges are being anticipated well in advance, and the CAARE model will be adjusted to fit these challenges as needed following results of this study. These efforts can lead to an exploration of implementation strategies that will work well for all populations, across race, ethnicity, and injury type. By assessing the effectiveness and the implementation of the CAARE model, this project also seeks to set the stage for a future, multi-site hybrid type 3 implementation-effectiveness trial that will identify key implementation strategies and outcomes to overcome historical barriers to adoption of critically needed interventions such as CAARE in pediatric and adult trauma centers. If successful, the CAARE model has high potential to be adapted for other settings (e.g., emergency departments, primary care, child advocacy centers) and populations (e.g., children with chronic illnesses or violent injuries).
