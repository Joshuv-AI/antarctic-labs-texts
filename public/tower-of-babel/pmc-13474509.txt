Utilization of non-chemical pest control practices among agro-pastoralist farmers in Gambella region, Ethiopia
Authors: Chuol Bor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAgricultural production continues to face critical problems, including persistent pest invasion, a lack of modern farm inputs, and climate change. Non-chemical pest control practices are vital, as they signify both cultural and practically embedded assets for sustainable agricultural practices in resource-limited environments. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the utilization of non-chemical pest control practices among agro-pastoralist farmers in the Gambella region of Ethiopia.MethodsA mixed research design was used to assess the utilization of non-chemical pest control practices among farmers from a total sample size (n = 274). The purposive sampling technique was used to select kebelles, and the systematic random sampling technique was used to select the target households. Primary data were collected from the sample respondents using structured questionnaires, key informant interviews, focus group discussions, and direct observations. Secondary data were collected from the documented sources. Frequency, percentage, chi-square, H-test, and ordinal logit model were used for quantitative data analysis, while thematic analysis was employed for qualitative data.ResultsThe common non-chemical pest control practices used by farmers were the application of ash (49.6%), crop rotation and intercropping (16.4%), use of neem extract (13.5%), physical removal of pests (10.2%), timing of planting based on the pest cycle (6.6%), and the use of chili or tobacco spray (3.7%). The ordinal logit model showed that farmers level of education negatively and significantly influenced the utilization of such practices (p < 0.01), and farmland ownership was positively and statistically associated with farmers non-chemical pest management systems (p < 0.01). The crops cultivated also showed a positive and statistically significant association with farmers’ utilization of the practices (p < 0.05).ConclusionsThis study concludes that the implementation of non-chemical pest control practices is shaped by socioeconomic and farmland-level factors rather than being regularly practiced across all households. Therefore, improving the application of locally available pest management systems with appropriate agricultural support should strengthen sustainable pest control and enhance farmland resilience in the Gambella region.Keywords: Indigenous knowledge, Ash application, Non-chemical pest control practices, Neem extract

Introduction

Agricultural production continues to face critical problems in developing countries, including persistent pest invasion, a lack of modern farm inputs, and changes in climate conditions. Farmers in these less developed countries frequently rely on non-chemical pest control techniques and local practices to protect crops and preserve crop productivity to improve household food security [1, 2, 3]. Such methods involve local skills and knowledge, pest practices, and innovation systems that originated over generations, providing real intervention to farming constraints where formal and extension systems are limited [4–6]. Having a clear understanding of these practices is critical for establishing practical and locally embedded assets for sustainable farming systems in less-resourced environments.

In sub-Saharan Africa, non-chemical pest control (N-CPC) practices play a vital role in smallholder farming systems and agro-pastoral communities. Many farmers in this region use the integration of botanical, physical, and agronomic techniques to control pests and improve soil fertility, which is tailored to rural ecological and socioeconomic conditions [7, 8]. As evident from these regions, pesticide practices such as ash application, neem use, intercropping, and physical pest removal are largely used and effective for reducing crop losses while minimizing environmental impact [9, 10]. Nevertheless, the extent of knowledge transmission, utilization, and integration with modern pest practices varies widely depending on resources, labor, and cultural factors. This highlights the need for location-specific studies to document and support these practices [11].

Ethiopia is an agrarian country in which most smallholder farmers and agro-pastoralist populations depend on agriculture as their main source of livelihood, with limited access to formal training and chemical fertilizers [12, 13]. In such situations, non-chemical pest control methods are the primary strategy for sustainable agricultural production and pest management. Research from semi-arid and lowland Ethiopia has highlighted the use of techniques such as crop rotation, neem, and ash, as well as the timing of planting based on pest cycles, which are essential for local pest management practices [14, 15]. Regardless of the effectiveness of non-chemical pest control (N-CPC) systems, the practices are under-documented, and their degree of utilization and socio-cultural, economic, and demographic factors are not fully understood, limiting efforts to support and combine these systems into broader rural and agricultural development programs [16]. The Gambella region is characterized by lowland plains, seasonal flooding, conflict, and tropical climate conditions, creating challenges and opportunities for agricultural development [17]. Farmers practice integrated crop and livestock farming to enhance their livelihoods. They cultivate sesame, local leafy crops, maize, and sorghum and rear livestock, such as cattle, poultry, sheep, and goats, to increase household incomes and reduce household food insecurity [18]. Limited access to formal farming inputs, environmental barriers, and reliance on subsistence agriculture have strengthened the development and maintenance of pest management practices [19].

Previous studies on non-chemical pest control practices in different countries have consistently shown gaps in the literature. An empirical gap appears in the study by [20] on the assessment of indigenous farming practices of Nilotic Nuer farmers in the Gambella region, southwest Ethiopia; however, their study does not focus on the non-chemical pest control (N-PCP) practices used by agro-pastoralist farmers. A clear location gap was also found by [21] in their study on the investigation of indigenous methods developed by agro-pastoralists to cope with climate change and vulnerability in the agro-pastoralism region of Rwanda. However, the current study was conducted in the Nuer Zone of Gambella, Ethiopia. A methodological gap was found by [22] on the linkage between traditional and scientific knowledge about enset disease prevention in the Gamo Ethiopian highland; nevertheless, their study missed the utilization index of non-chemical pest control practices. A population gap also existed in the study by [23] on the pesticide use and practices of local farmers in the Central Rift Valley (CRV) of Ethiopia as an implication for environmental and health hazards. However, their findings did not assess the practices and utilization of non-chemical pesticides by agro-pastoralist farmers in the Gambella Region.

Despite the documented empirical, locational, methodological, and population gaps, the current study aims to fill this gap by assessing the utilization of non-chemical pest control practices among agro-pastoralist farmers in the Gambella Region. The current study contributes to the field by intensifying the empirical evidence on such practices in the underdeveloped agro-pastoral communities of the Gambella region in Ethiopia, while placing non-chemical pest control as a central analytical framework, rather than peripheral considerations. This study also differentiates between non-chemical pesticides, referring to organic or naturally derived pest prevention substances, and non-chemical pest control practices, including cultural, biological, and mechanical strategies that reduce pest invasion without pesticide application. By generating context-specific insights into how local ecology shapes pesticide practices, this study has practical implications for integrated pest management (IPM) initiatives, agricultural extension services, and sustainable agricultural policies. Moreover, this study advances the academic discourse on the systematic integration of indigenous and modern pest control techniques, thereby enhancing the evidence base for culturally grounded, environmentally friendly, and socially inclusive agricultural intervention programs.

Methodology

Study area description

This study was conducted in the Nuer Zone of the Gambella Region in southwestern Ethiopia. The zone mainly covers lowland terrain, with flat plains scattered by seasonal floods around the Baro and Akobo Rivers. The climate of the area is tropical, with high annual temperatures ranging from 25 to 39 °C, a distinct wet season from April to October, and a dry season from November to March, with annual rainfall between 900 and 1600 mm [24]. Seasonal flooding significantly influences livestock rearing and crop cultivation, thereby affecting agricultural productivity and the frequency of pests. Agro-pastoralist farmers practice an integrated crop and livestock management system in this region. The population of the zone relies on a subsistence farming system, cultivating maize, sorghum, fruit, root, and vegetable crops, as well as leafy crops that are adaptable to local soil and climate conditions [25]. Cattle, sheep, goats, and poultry are the main components of rural livelihoods, providing food, income, and sociocultural values [26]. Socio-economic activities are largely local, with inadequate access to formal markets, extension services, and agricultural inputs. Their dependence on local resources has improved the development and preservation of non-chemical pest control systems and crop protection [27]. The map of the study area is shown in Fig. 1.

Research design

A mixed research design, a cross-sectional study, was employed to assess the utilization of non-chemical pest control practices among agro-pastoralist farmers in the Gambella region of Ethiopia. This study design allowed for data collection at one point in time by providing a snapshot of the farmers current pest practices and factors influencing them in the study area from January1–15, 2026. The study integrates a quantitative method to capture measurable patterns of practices and a qualitative approach to examine the cultural, social, and ecological dimensions of knowledge transfer and its utilization. This mixed design enabled data triangulation, improving validity by integrating statistical indicators with in-depth insights from local leaders, agricultural officers, and sample respondents.

Sampling techniques and size determination

A multistage sampling technique was applied to ensure that the research obtained important and representative data on non-chemical pest control practices. In the first step, purposive sampling methods were employed to select kebelles (Ethiopia’s smallest administrative unit) inhabited by agro-pastoralist farmers, allowing the study to focus on communities where those practices are actively implemented and confirming the relevance and richness of the information gathered. In the second step, a systematic random sampling technique was used to choose households within the selected kebelles at regular intervals from a complete list of households, providing each household with an equal opportunity for inclusion in the study sample. This technique was applied because it minimizes selection bias and ensures that the sample is representative, enabling the generalization of statistical validation of research findings regardless of the practices and utilization of pest control practices among agro-pastoralist households in the study area. The sample size was calculated from 956 farmers in the study area using [28]. In total, 274 target farmers were included in this study. The response rate was obtained by dividing the number of completed and usable questionnaires by the total number of target respondents and multiplying by 100. The study achieved a 100% response rate because all 274 questionnaires were completed successfully.

Accordingly, the final sample size for this study was 274 target farmers.

Sources of data

This study applied both primary and secondary data sources to achieve a comprehensive understanding of the use of non-chemical pest control practices. Primary data were collected directly from farmers’ households by providing first-hand information on the current practices and factors influencing the utilization of these techniques among agro-pastoralist farmers in the Gambella region. Furthermore, secondary data were drawn from district and regional agricultural offices, published documents on pest management practices in Ethiopia, and peer-reviewed findings on agro-pastoralist farming systems, providing circumstantial and proportional insights to assist the data analysis and interpretation of primary data.

Methods of data collection

Data collection involved the integration of quantitative and qualitative methods to ensure the holistic documentation of the pest control system used by the farmers. A structured pre-tested survey was administered to the target respondents to obtain detailed information on non-chemical pest control practices, along with farmers’ socio-economic and demographic characteristics, during early January 2026. To support the household survey, five key informant interviews were conducted with local leaders and agricultural officers to document and explore how these practices are transmitted from the same ethnic group through the local Nuer language that lasts for 20–30 min. Participants in the key informant interview were purposively selected based on their relevant experiences and knowledge of farming. Five focus group discussions (each approximately half an hour) were conducted, each comprising eight to ten participants drawn from sample farmers to gather information on practices. Direct field observations were also carried out as a standard checklist to verify household pesticide preparation practices, application, and storage of those practices, with photographs and detailed notes used to support and triangulate data collection.

Methods of data analysis

Data analysis integrated both quantitative and qualitative techniques to provide a comprehensive understanding of farmers’ utilization of non-chemical pest control practices. Quantitative household survey data were analyzed by frequency, percentage, mean, standard deviation, chi-square test, and Kruskal–Wallis test to understand the farmers’ socioeconomic, institutional, and demographic factors and the pest control practices used by the farmers using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 24. The factors influencing farmers’ utilization of such practices were tested using an ordinal logit model. Complementing the quantitative analysis, qualitative data from key informant interviews and focus group discussions were analyzed thematically. Under this method of analysis, five themes were identified: ethno-ecological observation and insect detection, dependency on locally available resources, intergenerational knowledge transfer, integrated pest control system, and the influences of worker- and crop-specific pest pressure on selected practices. Therefore, triangulation of quantitative and qualitative findings was used to validate the results, providing evidence and strengthening the robustness of the research conclusions. Furthermore, the ordinal logit model is appropriate for testing the ordinal dependent variable, where the outcomes refer to categories with a natural order but with unknown distances between the categories. The utilization of non-chemical pest control practices as a dependent variable was measured as follows: 1 = low utilization for farmers who rarely or rarely used non-chemical control practices; 2 = moderate utilization for respondents who practiced such techniques at an intermediate level; and 3 = high utilization for farmers who frequently and consistently utilized a wide range of such practices, as shown in Table 1. The gender of farmers shows that 63.3% were male and the remaining 30.7% were female. The chi-square test (χ² =0.42; p = 0.517) underscored that there was no statistical relationship between gender and farmers utilization of non-chemical control practices at any significant level. This may show that the gender of farmers does not determine the utilization of these practices.

Regarding the level of education, 62.8% of farmers were illiterate, and 37.2% were literate. The chi-square result (χ² =0.85; p = 0.357) shows the non-statistical association between farmers education level and farmers utilization of non-chemical pest control practices at any level of significance. This non-association revealed that such practices may be widely disseminated through informal means, such as traditional knowledge and community experiences, rather than through formal systems.

In terms of access to agricultural training, 65.7% of the farmers had no access to agricultural training, while the remaining 34.3% had access to training. The chi-square result (χ² =1.12; p = 0.290) underscores that farmers access to agricultural training is not statistically associated with farmers utilization of pest management practices at any significance level. The test results show that farmers may depend more on variables than on formal training schemes when using nonchemical pest control methods.

About 51.1% of the farmers were not cooperative members, while 48.9% were cooperative members. The chi-square results (χ² =3.84; p = 0.05) highlight a statistically significant relationship between cooperative membership and the level of utilization of non-chemical pest management practices at a 5% significance level. This shows that farmers with cooperative membership might have a better chance of sharing experiences and information related to non-member farmers during utilization.

Farmland ownership shows that 92% of the farmers owned land and 8% did not own land. The chi-square test (χ² =6.63; p = 0.01) shows a statistical relationship between farmland ownership and utilization of non-chemical pest control at the 1% level of significance. This indicates that farmers who own farmland are more likely to utilize such methods due to the wider long-term incentive to practice a sustainable farming system.

In terms of access to credit, 73% of the farmers had no access, and the remaining 27% had access to credit. The chi-square results (χ² =0.76; p = 0.383) reveal no statistically significant relationship between farmers credit access and the utilization of non-chemical pest control systems at any significant level. This reflects that farmers’ access to financial services may not directly influence their trust in using such practices.

The use of ash on crops was reported by 49.6% of the farmers and ranked first as a method of preventing insects. This type of pest control is mostly collected from household cooking fires and applied directly to crop leaves or the lower part of the crops. This technique is valued for its availability, accessibility, and cost-effectiveness in deterring a variety of insect pests during the planting period.

Crop rotation and intercropping were the second most commonly reported practice, with 16.4% of the agro-pastoral farmers using them as a mechanism to protect crops from pests. This technique is used to disrupt the insect life cycle and decrease the build-up of pest populations in farmlands. The practices also demonstrate farmers understanding of the ecological interaction between pests and crops and their efforts to maintain soil fertility and reduce pest invasion through diversified cropping patterns.

Neem extract was applied by 13.5% of the farmers and ranked third among pest prevention practices. Farmers prepare the extract by grinding neem leaves or seeds and diluting them with water to create a spray solution that is sprayed onto crops. Its application reflects the combination of botanical knowledge into pest prevention techniques, even though its adaptability may be limited by the availability of the neem tree in some places.

Physical removal of insects on crops was reported by 10.2% of the farmers and ranked fourth as a system for controlling crop pests. This practice is labor-intensive and prevents some pests during the early stage of pest infestation in small plots of farmland. Through these practices, farmers have shown frequent field monitoring and direct engagement with crop health.

The timing of planting based on the pest cycle was reported by 6.6% of the farmers and ranked fifth as a technique for managing crop insects. This practice is applied by farmers to determine the optimal planting time to avoid peak pest invasion. The practices used by farmers on seasonal pest changes underscore the role of ecological knowledge in reducing crop damage without external inputs.

Lastly, the application of chili and local tobacco was reported by 3.7% of the farmers and ranked as the least common type of non-chemical pest control practice. Farmers prepare it by mixing crushed chili or tobacco leaves with water and, occasionally, soap to improve adhesion, and then apply the combination to the affected crops. This practice also reflects the innovative use of locally available resources for pest management.

Qualitative findings

Qualitative results from key informant intervie3w and focus group discussions support the quantitative findings by providing a deeper understanding of farmers’ pest control techniques. The study identified five major themes: ethno-ecological observation and insect detection, dependency on locally available resources, intergenerational knowledge transfer, integrated pest control systems, and the influences of worker- and crop-specific pest pressure on selected practices.

Theme 1: Ethno-ecological observation and pest detection

Farmers reported that pest control decisions were primarily based on continuous field observations and experiential ecological knowledge. Visible signs such as leaf damage, wilting, and pest invasion are commonly used to identify pest infestations and provide timely interventions. One farmer reported, “We begin pest management when we see holes crop leave or insect moving on crops” (male farmer, 24 years old). Key informants supported the idea that farmers’ solid local ecological knowledge assists early pest detection systems, even though these methods remain largely weak and informal compared to formal extension systems (female key informant, 37 years old).

Theme 2: Reliance on locally available resources

The application of non-chemical pest control methods was strongly determined by the affordability and accessibility of local resources to the farmers. Farmers frequently depend on ash, neem, chili, and tobacco because of the availability and low cost of these resources. One participant stated, “We use ash for insect control due to its availability at home” (female farmer, 41 years old). Key informants underscored the reliance on locally available resources because of economic hardship and adaptive self-reliance, even though their effectiveness may differ across pest types (male key informant, 28 years old).

Theme 3: Intergenerational transmission of indigenous knowledge

Pest control practices are deeply rooted in traditional and seasonal framing experiences. Farmers explained that crop rotation, pest anticipation, and planting time adjustment were adapted from their parents and grandparents, respectively. One respondent stated, “We learned these farming technique from ours elder and forefathers over thousands of year to control pest invasion” (male farmer, 31 years old). Furthermore, key informants noted that Indigenous knowledge is closely linked to long-term environmental observation and seasonal farming, even though the knowledge remains poorly documented and may decline among the younger generation (female key informant, 26 years old).

Theme 4: Integrated and flexible pest management strategies

Farmers frequently apply multiple pest control techniques rather than depending on one practice alone. They frequently combine ash application, hand picking, botanical spraying, crop rotation, and intercropping methods. One farmer expressed that, “We use multiple pest control techniques depending on how serious the pest are” (female farmer, 39 years old). A key informant further stated that this approach is an adaptive and flexible system based on accumulated local experiences, although inconsistent usage may affect the effectiveness of such practices (male key informant, 40 years old).

Theme 5: Labor constraints and crop-specific pest pressure

Labor availability and crop vulnerability seriously influence farmers’ selection of pest control systems. Labor-insensitive techniques such as botanical spray and handpicking were less frequently applied on large-scale farms due to their time consumption, but ash application was largely preferred because of its simplicity and cost-effectiveness. One farmer stated, “We largely plant maize for consumption and easily pest attack, so we use different pest control practices at the same time” (male farmer, 40 years old). Key informants also reported that farmers perceived maize as the most vulnerable crop, leading to more combined and intensive pest control practices in maize farms than in other crops (female key informant, 23 years old).

Discussion

The widespread use of ash as a non-chemical pest control practice among agro-pastoralist farmers highlights the importance of locally accessible, low-cost, and environmentally friendly pest management strategies for sustainable agriculture. Farmers valued ash not only for its immediate effectiveness against a wide range of pests but also for its contribution to soil fertility. Previous studies have similarly reported that ash acts as a physical barrier that reduces insect feeding behavior and is commonly used in resource-limited rural communities because of its affordability and availability [29, 30]. Nonetheless, its reduced effectiveness during severe infestations and on larger farms indicates that reliance on ash alone may not provide sustainable and long-term pest control [31, 32]. This finding emphasizes the importance of integrating indigenous pest management practices with modern pest control approaches to improve their effectiveness and sustainability. Crop rotation and intercropping are also widely practiced, reflecting farmers’ ecological understanding of pest population dynamics. These strategies help disrupt pest life cycles and reduce infestation risks, while simultaneously improving soil fertility and resource-use efficiency [33, 34]. The findings suggest that farmers rely on biodiversity-based approaches as adaptive pest management strategies rather than depending entirely on external agricultural inputs. Nevertheless, the effectiveness of such practices may be constrained by limited land size, labor shortages, and planning required for implementation [35]. The use of neem extract demonstrates how indigenous botanical knowledge contributes to pest management. Farmers perceive neem as safe for humans and livestock and value its pesticidal and repellent properties [36, 37]. Nonetheless, its lower utilization compared with ash suggests that its adoption is influenced not only by effectiveness but also by practical factors such as the availability of neem trees, labor-intensive preparation, and limited knowledge transfer between generations [38]. Similarly, the use of chili and tobacco sprays remained limited, despite their recognized pesticidal effects. This indicates that labor requirements, material availability, and preparation efforts strongly shape farmers’ decisions regarding the adoption of botanical pest control methods [39]. Manual insect removal also remains an important practice, particularly during the early stages of crop growth, when infestations are easier to control. This practice reflects farmers’ close observation of crops and their detailed ecological knowledge [40]. Nevertheless, its labor-intensive nature reduces its practicality on larger farms and may encourage some farmers to rely on synthetic pesticides, which provide faster and broader coverage during severe pest outbreaks [41]. Adjusting planting time according to pest cycles further demonstrates farmers’ adaptive ecological knowledge. By synchronizing planting periods with lower pest activity, farmers can reduce infestation risks without relying heavily on external inputs [42]. Similar findings from sub-Saharan Africa indicate that indigenous pest management practices combine both material- and time-based strategies within broader agroecological systems [43]. However, the effectiveness of these approaches depends heavily on labor availability, accurate seasonal observation, and climatic stability, all of which may be affected by rainfall variability and changing pest dynamics [44, 45].

The utilization of non-chemical pest control (N-CPC) practices among farmers is influenced by several socioeconomic, institutional, and demographic factors. Educational status plays an important role in shaping farmers’ pest management decisions. Previous studies have shown that formal education often encourages the adoption of commercially available pesticides and modern agronomic practices, sometimes reducing reliance on indigenous pest control methods [46, 47]. This may be because educated farmers perceive such approaches as less effective or outdated, despite their relevance to local agroecological systems. However, other studies suggest that education can also support the integration of indigenous and modern pest management approaches when extension programs promote knowledge blending and adaptive learning [48, 49]. These findings indicate that education influences pest management decisions through exposure, perception, and access to agricultural information, rather than through a single direct pathway. Furthermore, farmland ownership was strongly associated with the use of non-chemical pest control practices. Farmers with secure land ownership are more likely to invest in sustainable and locally adapted pest management strategies that support long-term soil fertility and farm productivity [50, 51]. In contrast, farmers without secure land access may prioritize short-term productivity and rely more heavily on externally supplied agricultural inputs to achieve this goal. In areas characterized by land insecurity, even farmers with substantial indigenous knowledge may be less willing to invest labor and time in ecological pest management practices because of uncertainty regarding future land use [52, 53]. Crop type also influenced the adoption of non-chemical pest control methods. Maize, which is highly vulnerable to pest infestation, has received more intensive pest management attention than other crops. Farmers often combine indigenous and modern practices to protect high-risk crops and minimize production losses [54, 55]. Conversely, in monocropping systems or lower-risk crop environments, farmers may increasingly depend on chemical pesticides because of their perceived efficiency and rapid action [56, 57]. These findings suggest that the adoption of non-chemical pest control practices is shaped by the interaction between farmers’ ecological knowledge, economic considerations, land use conditions, and crop-specific pest risks.

Implication for policies and practices

The findings of this study have important implications for agricultural policies and farming systems aimed at promoting sustainable pest control practices. First, the prevalent use of ash application, crop rotation, intercropping, neem extract, and other pest control systems shows that locally available and environmentally friendly techniques should be formally documented and integrated into agricultural extension systems in the region. Development practitioners and policymakers should support the documentation, validation, and diffusion of effective non-chemical pest control practices to improve their role in integrated pest management strategies. Second, the negative relationship between farmer literacy level and the utilization of that practice underscores the demand for extension and education systems that link pest control practices with modern scientific approaches rather than substituting them. Agricultural training programs should improve balancing methods, where formal pest control practices are blended with recognized traditional practices to foster sustainable farming systems and reduce dependence on costly chemical pesticides. Moreover, the positive association between farmland ownership and maize cultivation with non-chemical pest control practices highlights the need for policies encouraging secure land tenure and crop-specific pest control training to further improve the adoption of sustainable local pest control practices.

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional study design limits the ability to establish a causal association between the identified factors and the utilization of non-chemical pest control practices, as the data were collected at a single point in time and may not capture seasonal or temporal variations. Similarly, this research may also be subjected to interviewer bias, as the interviewer’s attitude, interaction, and interpretation during data collection could have influenced the participants’ responses. Second, the dependency on self-reported data presents the likelihood of recall and social desirability biases that may affect the reliability and accuracy of the reported practices. Third, although mixed-method research was applied, the relatively limited qualitative section and the study focus on selected kebelles in the Nuer Zone of the Gambella region, Ethiopia, may restrict the generalization and representativeness of the findings to other agro-pastoral and socio-cultural contexts. Furthermore, the findings document the use and perceived effectiveness of pest control practices without experimentally validating their actual efficacy under controlled environments and do not fully discover potentially influential variables such as extension service quality, environmental variability, and cultural beliefs. Finally, although the ordinal logit model is appropriate for ordered outcomes, it relies on the proportional odd assumption, and the violation of this assumption may affect the robustness of the estimation. Therefore, future research should incorporate a longitudinal study design, experimental validation, and larger geographical coverage to strengthen evidence-based research.

Conclusion

The study revealed that agro-pastoralist farmers in the Gambella region preserve a rich repertoire of non-chemical pest control practices for protecting crops from pests, showing deep-rooted ecological understanding and adaptive measures that have been established and refined over generations. Among these practices, ash application, crop rotation, intercropping, botanical extracts, physical removal of pests, timing of planting, and use of chili or tobacco spray constitute a set of techniques utilized in farming routines to prevent pest damage and sustain farming productivity. The most pests controlled in the study area were fall armyworms, maize stem borer, African bollworm, grasshoppers/locusts, aphids, and cutworms. The predominance of ash use demonstrates farmers’ dependence on accessible and cost-effective materials, whereas the presence of agronomic and botanical methods reflects the diversity of pest control. The determinant analysis indicated that farmers’ utilization of non-chemical pest control (N-CPC) methods is shaped by socioeconomic and farm-related factors. Most educated farmers depend on such practices, showing a movement toward modern pest control systems, whereas farmland ownership and maize cultivation motivate farmers to apply these methods because of stronger encouragement for sustainable land management. The botanical system reflects the diversity of pest-management practices. Strengthening the dissemination and application of these methods through community participation, knowledge-sharing platforms, and formal pesticide practices should improve resilience, promote sustainable farming practices, and ensure the continuity of these culturally embedded pest management strategies for future generations.
