Characterizing morphological variations of feathery cataract using AS-OCT in post-vitrectomy eyes
Authors: Mousumi Banerjee, Aayush Majumdar, Shorya V Azad, Pradeep Venkatesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:To characterize the morphological variations of feathery cataracts observed in post-vitrectomy gas-filled eyes using anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT).Methods:A prospective observational study with a total of 25 eyes with transient feathery cataracts following gas-filled vitrectomy was included for AS-OCT evaluation of the posterior lens and capsule. Imaging was performed on postoperative day 1 and day 7 for all cases. The morphological variations of feathery cataracts were characterized using AS-OCT.Results:Three distinct morphological patterns were identified based on the appearance of vacuoles on AS-OCT: dome-shaped, cuboidal-shaped, and diffuse thickening with poorly defined cystic spaces. Complete resolution of AS-OCT-defined morphological patterns was observed in all eyes within 7–10 days.Conclusions:The morphological characterization of feathery cataracts using AS-OCT is a novel finding and has not been previously reported. This novel imaging-based classification can aid in refining postoperative monitoring protocols and contribute to a better understanding of cataractogenesis mechanisms in gas-filled eyes.

Feathery cataract, also referred to as “transient posterior subcapsular cataract,” is commonly observed following vitrectomy surgery when the vitreous cavity is filled with gas tamponade. It is characterized by early onset following surgery and typically disappears within a week.

Transient posterior subcapsular cataract in post-vitrectomized eyes with gas tamponade has been reported to be around 89% within 24 hours.[1]

The transient nature of these cataracts poses a challenge for conducting histopathological examinations to better understand them. Additionally, the limited resolution of anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) hinders detailed studies of these transient posterior subcapsular cataracts.

We have classified these feathery cataracts into three morphological variants based on AS-OCT findings.

Methods

A total of 25 eyes with transient feathery cataracts following gas-filled vitrectomy were included for AS-OCT analysis of the posterior lens and capsule. All consecutive post-vitrectomy gas-filled eyes that developed transient subcapsular opacities were included; however, eyes with poor imaging quality or pre-existing capsular opacities that could interfere with accurate postoperative assessment were excluded.

The demographic details of the patients and their surgical indications are tabulated in Table 1. AS-OCT imaging was conducted on postoperative day 1 and again on day 7 for all cases. The devices used for imaging were the Optovue, Inc. machine, which has an axial resolution of 5 µm and a transverse resolution of 15 µm, and the ANTERION (Heidelberg, Germany), depending on availability. Scans were acquired with focus on the posterior lens capsule using high-resolution cross-sectional line scans oriented along the visual axis. Imaging was performed under pharmacologically dilated pupil conditions wherever feasible to ensure optimal visualization of posterior subcapsular changes. All cases were enrolled consecutively, and AS-OCT image analysis was independently performed by two senior vitreo-retina specialists, each with over 10 years of experience. Inter-observer reproducibility was high, with a percentage agreement of 98%.

Results

The images revealed three distinct morphological variations of feathery cataract across the cases studied.

We classified these morphological variations based on the appearance of the vacuoles on AS-OCT as dome-shaped, cuboidal-shaped, and diffuse thickening with poorly defined cystic spaces [Fig. 1].

Discussion

Feathery cataract is an understudied condition due to its transient nature. However, prolonged exposure to intravitreal tamponade can result in the progression of these cataracts, necessitating surgical intervention.[2]

The feathering of the crystalline lens is observed secondary to lens dehydration, and alteration in the lens metabolism after tamponade with gas, air, or silicon oil.[34] It has been postulated that excessive anterior vitrectomy can alter the microenvironment around the lens and adversely affects its metabolism. Another theory suggests that vitrectomy leads to transient impairment of the activity of the sodium phosphate adenosine triphosphate (Na+/K + ATPase) and calcium-activated ATPase present in the epithelium and the superficial cortical fibers of the lens.[5]

Enaida et al. investigated the progression of feathery cataract using AS-OCT in a single patient and identified a dome-shaped pattern of cystic spaces in the posterior subcapsular region.[5] They noted that the vacuoles observed are similar to those seen in fundus photography. The left image in Fig. 3 shows large vacuoles that correspond to the cuboidal-shaped vacuolar lesions observed on AS-OCT, whereas the smaller vacuoles in the right image align with the dome-shaped pattern identified on AS-OCT.

Each morphological pattern may reflect a distinct underlying biophysical process at the posterior subcapsular level. The dome-shaped pattern likely represents localized osmotic imbalance or focal hydration leading to discrete vacuole formation beneath the posterior capsule. The cuboidal-shaped pattern, with vacuoles spanning the full posterior subcapsular thickness, may correspond to more pronounced epithelial vacuolization or uniform fluid ingress across lens fiber layers. In contrast, the diffuse thickening with ill-defined cystic spaces likely reflects widespread hydration gradients or generalized lens fiber swelling, resulting in less sharply demarcated cystic changes.

The clinical appearance of a feathery cataract can often be mistaken for a typical posterior subcapsular cataract resulting from aging or lens contact during surgery. In posterior subcapsular cataracts, epithelial cells of the lens migrate toward the posterior pole and proliferate, transforming into the bladder cells of Wedl, leading to an irreversible change that progresses over time. In contrast, a feathery cataract arises from a temporary malfunction of the ion channel, which is reversible once the effects of the gas dissipate.

This morphological classification provides clinicians with a structured framework to recognize and differentiate transient feathery cataract patterns on AS-OCT in the early postoperative period. By identifying whether the opacities present as dome-shaped, cuboidal, or diffuse with poorly defined spaces, clinicians can better anticipate the natural course, reassure patients regarding the transient nature of these changes, and avoid unnecessary surgical intervention. Furthermore, it enhances postoperative monitoring by allowing more precise documentation of lens changes, supports research into underlying mechanisms of cataractogenesis in gas-filled eyes, and may eventually guide tailored follow-up intervals or management strategies.

Enaida et al.[5] reported gas-induced posterior subcapsular cystic changes on AS-OCT in a single case. In contrast, our 25 eyes series expand on this observation by providing a systematic morphological classification into dome-shaped, cuboidal, and diffuse patterns, thereby offering a more comprehensive structural framework.

Conclusion

Feathery cataract represents a distinct, transient postoperative lens change seen in gas-filled vitrectomized eyes. Through AS-OCT, we identified three reproducible morphological variants—dome-shaped, cuboidal, and diffuse thickening with poorly defined cystic spaces—all of which resolved spontaneously within 7–10 days. Recognizing these patterns can help clinicians differentiate them from irreversible posterior subcapsular cataracts, reassure patients, and avoid unnecessary interventions. This classification provides a foundation for further research into the pathophysiology and natural history of these transient cataracts.

To the best of our knowledge, detailed morphological characterization of feathery cataract on AS-OCT has not been systematically described in the literature and has previously been limited to isolated case reports.
