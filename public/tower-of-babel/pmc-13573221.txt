Does the breathing response limit exercise tolerance in patients weaning from mechanical ventilation?

To the Editor:

During exercise, most healthy individuals achieve an increase in minute ventilation by first augmenting tidal volume, followed by an increase in respiratory frequency [1]. This coordinated breathing pattern allows efficient adaptation to aerobic demand. However, abnormal breathing responses to exercise, characterised by an inappropriately high respiratory frequency or erratic fluctuations in both frequency and tidal volume during exercise, may occur. Such patterns can be identified subjectively using validated questionnaires [2] or objectively using cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) [3]. Abnormal breathing patterns due to dysfunctional breathing are estimated to occur in ∼9% of the general population, and are associated with disproportionate breathlessness and reduced exercise tolerance [2].

In the intensive care unit (ICU), early mobilisation of mechanically ventilated patients is recommended [4], although empirical evidence to support this practice remains limited [5–9]. We previously demonstrated that exercise performance in these patients can be systematically analysed using nine-panel plots [10]. In that study, some ventilated patients terminated exercise without meeting any accepted limitation criteria, leading to the hypothesis that abnormal breathing responses to exercise may contribute to exercise intolerance.

The present study aimed to determine the incidence of abnormal breathing responses to exercise in mechanically ventilated ICU patients and to examine whether these responses predict exercise tolerance.

A prospective, observational study was conducted. 24 patients in the weaning phase from mechanical ventilation performed CPET using an incremental, symptom-limited exercise protocol on a bedside cycle ergometer [11]. Inclusion criteria were age >18 years and the ability to exercise on a bed cycle. Contraindications to exercise have been described previously [12]. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Medical Ethics Review Committee of Isala Hospital, Zwolle, the Netherlands. Respiratory gas exchange and volumetric data were recorded using a Cosmed system. For comparison, 11 healthy controls aged >50 years completed a submaximal incremental exercise test on the same ergometer, connected to the calorimeter via a face mask, in the supine position.

Breathing data from both groups were truncated to the phase between the onset of unloaded cycling and the point of maximal oxygen consumption (V′O2max). Three-panel plots were constructed, displaying tidal volume and respiratory frequency against time, tidal volume against minute ventilation, and respiratory frequency against minute ventilation (examples shown in figure 1a and b). These data were pooled and independently evaluated in a blinded fashion by three investigators (T. Nijhoff, S. Zoethout and H.L.A. van den Oever).

Examples of blinded plots as presented to the raters illustrating an a) abnormal and b) normal breathing pattern during exercise. a) External power output of patients with normal and abnormal breathing patterns. VT: tidal volume; fR: respiratory frequency; V′E: minute ventilation.

A normal breathing response to exercise was defined as an initial increase in tidal volume, followed later, if at all, by an increase in respiratory frequency. To minimise misclassification, a breathing response was classified as abnormal only when all three investigators rated it as such; all other patterns were considered normal. Exercise tolerance was assessed by comparing the peak externally generated power, as measured by the ergometer, between patients exhibiting normal and abnormal breathing responses.

The patient cohort comprised 12 men and 12 women with a mean±sd age of 67.5±15.9 years, a mean body weight of 80.6±20.1 kg and a mean body mass index of 27.4±6.7 kg·m−2. The mean duration of mechanical ventilation prior to testing was 16.2 days. All patients were receiving pressure support ventilation and had been on this mode for ≥24 h at the time of CPET. Three (12.5%) patients had a history of COPD in Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease stage 3 or 4.

Interobserver agreement among the three raters, expressed as Krippendorff's α, was 0.55 (95% CI 0.45–0.74), indicating moderate agreement. Abnormal breathing responses were observed in six (25.0%) out of 24 patients and one (9.1%) out of 11 controls (p=0.275, χ2 test).

Patients with normal breathing responses generated a mean external power of 7.06±0.91 W, whereas those with abnormal breathing responses produced 2.67±1.59 W. The mean difference was 4.39 W (p=0.024, two-sided t-test) (figure 1c). The corresponding effect size, estimated using Cohen's d, was 1.14, indicating a large effect. As a sensitivity analysis, we also calculated the percentage of the individually predicted V′O2max achieved by each patient. Those with normal breathing responses reached 35.9% of their predicted V′O2max, compared with 32.8% in those with abnormal breathing responses (p=0.520).

In this exploratory study among mechanically ventilated ICU patients, an abnormal breathing response to exercise was observed in 25.0% of cases and was associated with substantially lower external power output, supported by a large effect size and an accompanying (though not statistically significant) reduction in oxygen consumption.

These findings suggest that altered breathing responses may contribute to reduced exercise tolerance in this population. Abnormal breathing responses may therefore represent a previously under-recognised mechanism underlying the limited exercise performance observed during early mobilisation. It was not possible to determine whether the observed patterns reflected true dysfunctional breathing or an appropriate physiological response to underlying disease, as physiological determinants of tidal volume limitation were not assessed.

Our data do not clarify why some patients demonstrated a fully normal breathing response to exercise, while others did not. Several nonmutually exclusive explanations may be considered. Patients with pre-existing poor exercise tolerance and an abnormal breathing response, potentially resembling dysfunctional breathing as described in nonventilated populations, may have been more likely to experience difficulty weaning from mechanical ventilation, potentially leading to overrepresentation in our cohort. In addition, impaired lung compliance, as seen in acute respiratory distress syndrome or fibrotic lung disease, may render increases in tidal volume energetically unfavourable during exercise, necessitating a compensatory rise in respiratory rate to augment minute ventilation. Abnormal breathing responses could also reflect diaphragmatic muscle weakness. Finally, suboptimal patient–ventilator interaction during pressure support ventilation could contribute to abnormal breathing responses, consistent with previous evidence that proportional modes of ventilatory assistance can improve respiratory efficiency during exercise [13].

Our results provide the first indication that abnormal breathing patterns may have clinical relevance in mechanically ventilated patients. When interpreting nine-panel plots in this setting, an abnormal breathing response may reasonably be considered a potential exercise-limiting factor of respiratory origin. Recognition of such patterns could assist in distinguishing ventilatory from cardiovascular, muscular or psychogenic contributors to exercise intolerance, thereby refining physiological assessment during weaning.

The possibility of a ventilatory limitation to exercise in mechanically ventilated patients is supported by previous studies showing that improving patient–ventilator synchrony can enhance respiratory efficiency and exercise performance [13]. Comparable associations between breathing irregularities and impaired exercise performance have also been described in spontaneously breathing individuals in outpatient settings. The incidence of 9.1% observed in our cohort of healthy controls aged >50 years using CPET was consistent with published outpatient data, where a prevalence of 9% has been reported using a questionnaire-based assessment [2]. Together, these observations suggest that abnormal ventilatory control may occur across a continuum of clinical conditions. In mechanically ventilated patients, such abnormalities may arise from ICU-specific mechanical and neuromuscular factors but may also share features with dysfunctional breathing described in nonventilated populations.

The main limitations of this study are the small sample size and the subjective classification of breathing patterns. In addition, as this study was exploratory, the reproducibility of breathing pattern assessment is unknown. Nevertheless, the use of blinded, independent raters probably reduced classification bias and interobserver agreement was moderate. Additionally, exercise testing in mechanically ventilated patients requires a modified protocol, as has been done in other difficult patient categories [14]. Lung compliance and respiratory muscle strength were not assessed, which did not allow distinction between true dysfunctional breathing and adaptations to mechanical limitations. Future research employing automated breath-by-breath variability analysis and longitudinal assessments during weaning is warranted to determine whether interventions aimed at improving ventilatory control can improve exercise tolerance and rehabilitation outcomes. Such studies would benefit from including a more thorough assessment of tidal volume limitation, such as compliance of the respiratory system and respiratory muscle strength.

In conclusion, in patients in the weaning phase of mechanical ventilation, evaluation of the breathing response to exercise may provide valuable physiological insight. Recognising abnormal breathing responses as a potential exercise-limiting factor could inform individualised mobilisation strategies and help explain the markedly reduced external power output observed in many mechanically ventilated ICU patients.
