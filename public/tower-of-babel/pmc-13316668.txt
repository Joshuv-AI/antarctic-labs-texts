Fulminant bilateral upper limb thrombophlebitis following traumatic brain injury in a patient with chronic substance use: A fatal case of trauma-associated immunothrombosis
Authors: Naman Jain, Minakshi Gadhire, Ashish Kumar Lallan Prasad Yadav, Kalyani Dnyaneshwar Aher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBilateral upper limb superficial thrombophlebitis is rare in trauma patients and typically indicates systemic thromboinflammatory activation rather than localized vascular injury. Trauma-associated immunothrombosis is increasingly recognized as distinct from classical disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).Case presentationA 35-year-old male presented with severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) following an unwitnessed fall. On hospital day six, he developed progressive discoloration of the left hand, rapidly involving the right upper limb. Background

Superficial thrombophlebitis in trauma patients is typically unilateral and related to intravenous cannulation [1]. Bilateral upper limb involvement is uncommon and should raise suspicion for systemic thromboinflammatory activation [2].

TBI is associated with endothelial disruption, tissue factor release, platelet activation, and inflammatory cytokine surge, creating a transient hypercoagulable state [3], [4]. Acute traumatic coagulopathy may initially manifest as hypercoagulability before progressing to consumptive patterns [5], [6].

Immunothrombosis represents the interface between innate immune activation and coagulation pathways, mediated by neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), monocyte-derived tissue factor, and platelet–leukocyte aggregates [7], [8], [9]. Unlike classical DIC, global coagulation factors may remain preserved.

Chronic alcohol and cannabis use may exacerbate endothelial dysfunction and platelet activation, potentially amplifying trauma-induced thromboinflammatory responses [10], [11], [12], [13].

Discussion

Bilateral superficial thrombophlebitis following isolated TBI is rare and suggests systemic thromboinflammatory activation rather than localized vascular injury [2].

The stable intracranial hematoma on repeat CT excluded active bleeding and argues against consumptive coagulopathy. Severe thrombocytopenia with preserved PT/INR and elevated fibrinogen supports trauma-associated immunothrombosis rather than classical DIC.

HRCT demonstrated multilobar consolidation with surrounding ground-glass opacities. In the absence of large central pulmonary embolism on CTPA, these findings likely reflect microvascular thrombosis and diffuse alveolar injury secondary to systemic endothelial activation [14], [15].

Although a postmortem examination was performed, the report could not be accessed because of legal and administrative restrictions; therefore, diffuse alveolar hemorrhage could not be histopathologically confirmed in our review. The pulmonary findings should therefore be interpreted as radiologically suggestive rather than definitive, although the combination of bilateral diffuse ground-glass opacities, multilobar consolidation, refractory hypoxemia, and absence of large central pulmonary embolism strongly supported severe diffuse alveolar injury with possible alveolar hemorrhage.

Anticoagulation decisions in severe TBI remain individualized because evidence-based guidelines are limited. In many neurocritical care settings, repeat CT brain imaging within 24–48 h is performed to assess hematoma stability. Stable or improving intracranial hemorrhage on repeat imaging may support consideration of pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis in selected patients, although profound thrombocytopenia and ongoing bleeding risk may still preclude therapeutic anticoagulation. In the present case, despite stable repeat neuroimaging, the platelet count of 10,000/μL represented a major contraindication to anticoagulation.

Retrospective review suggested that earlier serial monitoring of platelet count, D-dimer, fibrinogen, and inflammatory markers may have identified progressive thromboinflammatory activation before overt limb discoloration became evident. Earlier multidisciplinary involvement, including neurocritical care, hematology, vascular surgery, and pulmonology teams, may have improved assessment of evolving thrombotic risk and the feasibility of preventive interventions.

To our knowledge, bilateral upper limb superficial venous thrombosis following isolated TBI with concurrent diffuse thromboinflammatory lung involvement has not been previously described.

Limitations

This report has several limitations. Although a postmortem examination was performed, the report could not be accessed because of legal restrictions, and histopathologic confirmation of alveolar hemorrhage was therefore unavailable to the treating team. In addition, serial inflammatory biomarkers and coagulation assays such as thromboelastography were not available, limiting further characterization of the thromboinflammatory process.

Conclusion

This case illustrates a rare and fatal systemic thromboinflammatory complication following traumatic brain injury. In patients with severe TBI, serial monitoring of platelet count, D-dimer, fibrinogen, PT/INR, and inflammatory markers during the first week after injury may help identify evolving thromboinflammatory complications. New limb discoloration, unexplained thrombocytopenia, or worsening hypoxemia should prompt urgent vascular imaging and repeat chest imaging. Early multidisciplinary discussion involving neurocritical care, hematology, vascular surgery, pulmonology, and radiology teams may assist in balancing thrombotic risk against hemorrhagic risk and guide decisions regarding anticoagulation and further imaging.
