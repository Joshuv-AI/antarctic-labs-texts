Transition from CSII to second-generation basal insulin analogues in hospitalized T2DM patients: a CGM-based study
Authors: Ruiqing Dong, Hui Liu, Lifan Gu, Qiange Zhao, Chenwen Luo, Ting Zhang, Xuna Kou, Yuanna Hu, Bingyin Shi, Hui Guo, Wei Qiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe optimal approach for transitioning hospitalized patients with type 2 diabetes (T2DM) from continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII) to second-generation basal insulin analogues remains unclear. This exploratory study aimed to: (1) evaluate whether a smooth transition from CSII to each second‑generation basal insulin analogue (insulin degludec [IDeg] or insulin glargine U300 [IGlar U300]) could be achieved; (2) compare post‑transition glycemic outcomes between IDeg and IGlar U300; and (3) identify patient characteristics associated with transition outcomes.MethodsThis single-center retrospective analysis included 135 hospitalized adults with T2DM who received CSII therapy upon admission and were transitioned to basal insulin analogues before discharge (IDeg, n = 77; IGlar U300, n = 58). Among these, 90 patients (IDeg: 50; IGlar U300: 40) were insulin-naive at admission. Glycemic outcomes were assessed using continuous glucose monitoring metrics.ResultsWithin‑group comparisons showed significant improvements from CSII to post‑transition in both groups: time in range increased from 56.4% to 73.0% in the IDeg group (P < 0.001) and from 58.3% to 73.6% in the IGlar U300 group (P < 0.001). Between‑group comparisons after transition revealed comparable profiles between IDeg and IGlar U300 across key metrics: overall control (time in range: 73.0% vs. 73.6%, P = 0.912), glycemic variability (coefficient of variation: 29.8% vs. 29.2%, P = 0.629), and hypoglycemia risk (time below range: 0.8% vs. 0.5%, P = 0.915). IDeg demonstrated lower fasting glucose variability (coefficient of variation of fasting glucose: 23.8% vs. 28.3%, P = 0.024) and a later nocturnal glucose nadir. Lower body mass index correlated strongly with poorer post-transition outcomes, coinciding with more frequent use of multiple daily injection regimens in these patients (36.1% vs. 6.3%, P < 0.001). Lower serum albumin consistently associated with greater glycemic variability.ConclusionsTransition from CSII to either IDeg or IGlar U300 is effective and safe. IDeg may provide greater fasting glucose stability. Transition success requires personalized discharge planning based on BMI and albumin levels.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-026-02291-1.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a significant global health challenge, affecting over 589 million adults worldwide and projected to rise to 853 million by 2050 [1]. Effective glycemic control is crucial for preventing these complications, which include cardiovascular disease, nephropathy, retinopathy, and neuropathy, significantly impacting patient quality of life and increasing healthcare costs [2].

Current management strategies for T2DM include various insulin therapies, with basal insulin analogues playing a pivotal role in achieving and maintaining target blood glucose level [3]. Insulin degludec (IDeg) and insulin glargine U300 (IGlar U300) are both long-acting insulin analogues that have been widely studied for their efficacy and safety in T2DM management [4, 5]. Previous studies have shown that both insulin are effective in achieving glycemic control and reducing the risk of hypoglycemia [6, 7].

In China, a higher proportion of patients with T2DM initiate insulin therapy during hospitalization compared to Western countries, reflecting differences in healthcare delivery systems and clinical practice patterns [8, 9]. Continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII) allows for precise insulin delivery, reducing the risk of hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia and is a preferred method for achieving rapid glycemic control in hospitalized patients [10]. Consequently, the transition from inpatient CSII to a simplified discharge regimen—most commonly a basal insulin analogue—represents a frequent and critical juncture in clinical practice within the Chinese healthcare setting [11, 12]. This short-term transition period is characterized by significant changes in patient lifestyle and glucose dynamic, which requires the determination of appropriate insulin doses within a brief timeframe to achieve optimal glycemic control with minimized risk of hypoglycemia.

Previous research on IDeg or IGlar U300 has primarily focused on their long-term efficacy and safety compared to first-generation long-acting insulin analogues, with only a limited number of head-to-head comparisons with inconsistent results [13, 14]. In hospitalized patients with diabetes, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) has been shown to reduce recurrent hypoglycemia and time spent in hypoglycemic ranges without compromising overall glycemic control [15]. The 2026 American Diabetes Association Standards of Care recommends CGM for adults with diabetes on insulin therapy, recognizing its benefits across diverse populations regardless of age, sex, or socioeconomic status [16]. The current study addresses a significant gap by focusing on the specific transition period from inpatient CSII to discharge regimens with IDeg or IGlar U300. First, we evaluated whether a smooth transition from CSII to each basal insulin analogue (IDeg or IGlar U300) could be achieved, focusing on within‑group changes in glycemic control, variability, and safety. Second, we compared post‑transition outcomes between IDeg and IGlar U300 to identify potential differences between the two second‑generation basal insulins. Additionally, we performed exploratory analyses to identify patient characteristics associated with transition outcomes.

Methods

Research design and participants

We performed a retrospective analysis of CGM data and clinical features in inpatients with T2DM who received CSII therapy between January 1, and December 31, 2024 at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. The median duration of CSII therapy was 3 days (inter-quartile range [IQR]: 3–5 days) in both groups. CGM sensors were worn for a median total of 14 days (IQR: 14–14 days) in both groups. The CSII phase was defined as the period from CGM sensor placement (within 24 h of admission, concurrently with CSII initiation) until discontinuation of CSII. The post‑transition phase was defined as the period from the first dose of basal insulin (administered after CSII discontinuation) until CGM sensor expiration (14 days after placement). The median duration of post‑transition CGM monitoring was 11 days (IQR: 9–11 days) in both groups. Eligible participants were aged ≥ 18 years with a diagnosis of T2DM, who received CSII therapy for intensive insulin therapy at admission and then transitioned to IDeg or IGlar U300 with equivalent dose, when necessary, other antidiabetic drugs were used. According to the China Insulin Pump Clinical Guideline (2021) and Expert consensus on short-term insulin intensive therapy for type 2 diabetes mellitus (2021 edition) [10, 17], CSII is typically used for short-term intensive insulin therapy in hospitalized patients with poor glycemic control (e.g., newly diagnosed T2DM patients with HbA1c ≥ 9.0% or FPG ≥ 11.1 mmol/L or marked hyperglycemic symptoms; or T2DM patients on glucose-lowering pharmacotherapy > 3 months with subsequent significant hyperglycemia, pronounced glycemic variability, or hyperglycemic symptoms progressing to ketosis) to achieve rapid glycemic stabilization. CSII was discontinued when the attending physician judged that glycemic control had been sufficiently stabilized for safe outpatient management, based on: (1) stable glucose levels without hypoglycemia or marked hyperglycemia, (2) minimal day-to-day variation in CSII total daily dose over two consecutive days, and (3) feasibility of a simplified discharge regimen (e.g., basal insulin with or without oral agents rather than multiple daily injections). No strict quantitative thresholds were mandated; the decision was based on clinical judgment. CGM data were collected in an unblinded manner, as the glucose readings were accessible to both patients and treating clinicians for clinical decision-making. Exclusion criteria included patients receiving systemic corticosteroids or other medication affected glucose level (e.g., atypical antipsychotics, high-dose beta-blockers), those with malignant tumors or a history of malignant tumors, patients with severe infections, hyperthyroidism, acromegaly, or Cushing’s syndrome, and other conditions that affect blood glucose levels, as well as those with incomplete clinical or CGM data. The study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (No. XJTU1AF2023LSK-458).

Study objectives

The primary objective was to assess whether a smooth transition from CSII to each basal insulin analogue (IDeg or IGlar U300) could be achieved, defined as maintenance or improvement of glycemic control without a clinically significant increase in hypoglycemia risk. The secondary objective was to compare post‑transition CGM metrics between IDeg and IGlar U300. Exploratory objectives included correlational analyses of clinical characteristics with CGM metrics and evaluation of nocturnal glucose nadir patterns.

Collection of clinical data

Patients’ information on age, gender, body mass index (BMI), duration of T2DM, family history of T2DM, smoking history, drinking history, laboratory parameters (HbA1c, albumin, estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR]), complications (diabetic nephropathy, diabetic retinopathy, diabetic peripheral vascular disease, diabetic foot, diabetic ketosis), and comorbidity (hypertension, coronary artery disease, and fatty liver disease) was collected through a standardized electronic inpatient medical record data collection form.

CGM metrics

Participants had CGM sensors (GS1, SIBIONICS, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) implanted in their non-dominant upper arm, each with a lifespan of 14 days. This device operates by implanting a tiny electrode into the subcutaneous tissue, with a reported mean absolute relative difference of 8.01% during fasting conditions [18]. All patients included in this study had ≥ 70% of valid CGM data during the collection period, ensuring compliance with quality standards [19]. These sensors continuously recorded glucose levels every 5 min. We calculated a range of summary statistical CGM indices from the raw downloaded glucose data using Python (version 3.10.11). CGM metrics included glucose management indicator (GMI), mean glucose (MG), time in range (TIR), time in tight range (TITR), time above range (TAR), time below range (TBR), low blood glucose index (LBGI), high blood glucose index (HBGI), coefficient of variation (CV), CVnight (CV from 0000 h to 0600 h), the mean of daily differences (MODD) and CVFBS (CV of fasting blood sugar). The definitions of the percentages of time spent in different ranges of glucose and the calculations of the glycemia risk index are detailed as follows [19]: TIR: 3.9–10.0 mmol/L, TAR: > 10.0 mmol/L, with further distinction of Level 1 TAR (10.0-13.9 mmol/L) and Level 2 TAR (> 13.9 mmol/L); TBR: < 3.9 mmol/L, with further distinction of Level 1 TBR (3.0-3.9 mmol/L) and Level 2 TBR (< 3.0 mmol/L); TITR: 3.9–7.8 mmol/L. MODD was calculated as the mean difference between glucose values measured at the same time of the day on two consecutive days under standardized condition [20]. MODD provides complementary information to CV by specifically quantifying day-to-day glycemic variability, which is particularly relevant during transitional periods when glycemic stability across days is a key clinical goal. LBGI quantifies the risk of hypoglycemic episodes, whereas HBGI quantifies hyperglycemic risk. Established risk stratification thresholds are: for LBGI, Minimal (≤ 1.1), Low (> 1.1–2.5), Moderate (> 2.5–5.0), and High (> 5.0); for HBGI, Low (≤ 4.5), Moderate (> 4.5–9.0), and High (> 9.0) [21]. For the calculation of HBGI and LBGI, we used the formulae described by Fabris et al. [22].

Classification of concomitant glucose-lowering therapy at discharge

Notably, no patient in this cohort received sulfonylureas or glinides. At discharge, glucose-lowering regimens in combination with basal insulin (IDeg or IGlar U300) were classified based on two defining features: (1) the use of prandial insulin, and (2) the inclusion of specific newer agent classes (sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors [SGLT-2i] and/or glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist [GLP-1RA]). The mutually exclusive categories were defined as follows: (1) Multiple Daily Injections (MDI): Defined as a regimen that included both basal insulin and prandial insulin (requiring ≥ 3 daily injections). This regimen could also include other oral agents (e.g., metformin, α-glucosidase inhibitors, SGLT-2i, or dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors [DPP-4i]). (2) Basal Insulin + SGLT-2i: Regimens containing an SGLT-2i without prandial insulin. These could also include other oral agents (metformin, α-glucosidase inhibitors, or DPP-4i). (3) Basal Insulin + GLP-1RA: Regimens containing a GLP-1RA without prandial insulin. These could also include metformin or α-glucosidase inhibitors. Consistent with their mechanisms, no regimens contained both a GLP-1RA and a DPP-4i. (4) Basal Insulin + SGLT-2i + GLP-1RA: Regimens containing both an SGLT-2iand a GLP-1RA without prandial insulin, with possible concomitant use of other oral agents. (5) Basal Insulin + Other Oral Agents: Regimens that included only traditional oral agents (metformin, α-glucosidase inhibitors, and/or DPP-4i) in combination with basal insulin, excluding prandial insulin, SGLT-2i, and GLP-1RA.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data were presented using the number and proportion. Normally distributed data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{-}{x}$$\end{document}±SD). Non-normally distributed data were described using the median and IQR. For within-group comparisons (CSII vs. post-transition), paired t-tests were used for normally distributed data, and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for non-normally distributed data. For between-group comparisons (IDeg vs. IGlar U300), unpaired t-tests were used for normally distributed data, and Mann-Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed data. To evaluate whether the degree of change from CSII differed between groups, we calculated Δ values (post-transition minus CSII) for each CGM metric and compared these Δ values between groups using unpaired t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests, as appropriate. The primary endpoint was TIR. Secondary endpoints were TBR and CV. Other CGM metrics (GMI, MG, TITR, TAR, LBGI, HBGI, MODD, CVnight, CVFBS) were reported descriptively. Given the exploratory nature of this retrospective study, no adjustment for multiple testing was performed. Enumeration data were expressed as a percentage and analyzed using Pearson’s χ2 test. Pearson analysis was used for correlation analysis of normally distributed data, whereas Spearman analysis was employed for non-normally distributed data. For the descriptive interpretation of the strength of correlation coefficients, the following thresholds were used based on conventional guidelines: weak (|r| < 0.3), moderate (0.3 ≤ |r| < 0.5), and strong (|r| ≥ 0.5) [23]. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P value of < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Product and Service Solutions software version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL).

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study included 135 hospitalized patients with T2DM who transitioned from CSII to either IDeg (n = 77) or IGlar U300 (n = 58). The overall cohort had a mean age of 50.79 years, a median diabetes duration of 48.0 months, and poorly controlled hyperglycemia at admission (mean HbA1c 11.30%). In the overall cohort, 90 patients (66.7%) were insulin-naive at admission, with similar proportions between groups (IDeg: 64.9%; IGlar U300: 69.0%; P > 0.05). The two groups had similar baseline characteristics (Table 1). No statistically significant differences were observed in demographics, diabetes duration, HbA1c, BMI, renal function (eGFR), albumin levels, complications, or comorbidities (all P > 0.05). Given the well-balanced baseline characteristics between the two groups and the exploratory nature of the between-group comparisons, unadjusted analyses were performed.

Treatment and glycemic outcomes after transition

Insulin dosing and concomitant glucose-lowering therapy

The initial daily dose of basal insulin was similar between the IDeg and IGlar U300 groups (18.74 ± 6.89 U [0.27 ± 0.11 U/kg·d] vs. 18.22 ± 5.79 U [0.26 ± 0.11 U/kg·d], P > 0.05). Doses at discharge remained comparable between groups (17.77 ± 7.05 U [0.25 ± 0.10 U/kg·d] vs. 17.05 ± 5.57 U [0.24 ± 0.10 U/kg·d], P > 0.05). Notably, the reported doses reflect basal insulin only, as prandial insulin and other glucose‑lowering agents were administered separately according to the prescribed discharge regimens. We elected to report only basal insulin doses in the primary analysis because the inclusion of prandial insulin in MDI regimens would reflect the intensity of the regimen rather than the basal insulin component per se, and presenting basal doses alone allows for a cleaner comparison between the two basal insulin analogues without confounding by variable prandial insulin requirements.

The distribution of concomitant regimens at discharge did not differ between groups (P = 0.171, Table 2). In the overall cohort (N = 135), the most common regimen was Basal Insulin + Other Oral Agents (43.7%), followed by MDI (22.2%), Basal Insulin + SGLT-2i (16.3%), Basal Insulin + GLP-1 RA (10.4%), and Basal Insulin + SGLT-2i + GLP-1RA (7.4%).

Within-group glycemic changes (CSII vs. post‑transition by insulin type)

Glycemic outcomes following the transition from CSII to basal insulin were evaluated across three aspects: glycemic control, hypoglycemia risk, and glycemic variability (Table 3). Both regimens enabled a smooth transition after CSII therapy. TIR increased by approximately 17% points in both groups, reaching a mean above 73% (both P < 0.001). This was accompanied by substantial expansions in TITR and reductions in TAR. Profiles of hypoglycemia risk differed between insulins. In the IDeg group, TBR increased from a median of 0.21% (IQR: 0.00–1.12) during CSII to 0.81% (IQR: 0.00–2.29) after transition (P = 0.002). Despite this increase, the post-transition TBR remained well below the 4% safety threshold. In the IGlar U300 group, TBR remained stable (0.6% [0.1–1.4] vs. 0.52% [0.0–1.8], P = 0.720). The LBGI did not change significantly from the CSII period in either group (IDeg: P = 0.077; IGlar U300: P = 0.813). Glycemic variability was markedly reduced. The CV, MODD, and the coefficient of variation of fasting blood sugar (CVFBS) decreased significantly in both groups (all P < 0.01).

Between-group comparison (IDeg vs. IGlar U300 after transition)

Glycemic control after transition was comparable between the IDeg and IGlar U300 groups (Table 3). The two groups achieved similar TIR (IDeg: 73.0 ± 21.5% vs. IGlar U300: 73.6 ± 22.5%, P = 0.912) and TITR (IDeg: 51.6 ± 23.1% vs. IGlar U300: 52.0 ± 24.9%, P = 0.884). Key safety metrics were also comparable, with low and similar TBR (IDeg: 0.8% [0.0–2.3] vs. IGlar U300: 0.5% [0.0–1.8], P = 0.915) and LBGI (IDeg: 1.7 [0.7–2.8] vs. IGlar U300: 1.6 [1.1–2.5], P = 0.790). Measures of overall glycemic variability showed no difference, including the CV (IDeg: 29.8 ± 7.4% vs. IGlar U300: 29.2 ± 5.9%, P = 0.629) and the MODD (IDeg: 2.3 ± 1.0 mmol/L vs. IGlar U300: 2.3 ± 0.8 mmol/L, P = 0.581). However, CVFBS was significantly lower in the IDeg group than in the IGlar U300 group (23.8 ± 11.8% vs. 28.3 ± 11.5%, P = 0.024). Analysis of the change (Δ) from CSII to post-transition revealed comparable improvements between the two groups across all CGM metrics (all P for between-group Δ > 0.05).

Regarding glycemic control, the correlation between BMI and GMI strengthened from a weak, non-significant trend during CSII (r = -0.165, P = 0.058) to statistically significant correlations after transition, which were moderate in the IDeg group (r = -0.350, P = 0.002) and strong in the IGlar U300 group (r = -0.538, P < 0.001). A similar strengthening from weak to moderate/strong was observed for TIR.

In terms of glycemic variability, correlations between BMI and indices such as MODD, which were moderate and significant during CSII (r = -0.358, P < 0.001), increased further after transition, reaching strong correlation in the IDeg group (r = -0.550, P < 0.001) and moderate-to-strong correlation in the IGlar U300 group (r = -0.470, P < 0.001).

Concerning hypoglycemia risk, the weak but significant negative correlation between BMI and TBR during CSII (r = -0.268, P = 0.002) strengthened to a moderate correlation in the IDeg group (r = -0.351, P = 0.002). Notably, this correlation was absent in the IGlar U300 group after transition (r = -0.020, P = 0.884).

Correlations with age

In contrast to the consistent strengthening of BMI correlations, the associations between age and CGM metrics were more variable and differed by insulin type after transition (Table 4). During CSII, correlations were generally weak. After transition, in the IDeg group, older age correlated with higher GMI (r = 0.236, P = 0.039), lower TIR (r = -0.226, P = 0.048), and higher TAR (r = 0.241, P = 0.035). In the IGlar U300 group, older age was also associated with higher GMI (r = 0.332, P = 0.011) and TAR (r = 0.274, P = 0.038), but with a lower TBR (r = -0.328, P = 0.012).

Correlation with serum albumin

Serum albumin level emerged as a stable correlate of glycemic variability across both treatment phases and insulin groups (Table 4). Lower albumin levels were consistently associated with greater variability, as indicated by negative correlations with MODD (CSII: r = -0.333, P < 0.001; IDeg post-transition: r = -0.406, P = 0.001; IGlar U300 post-transition: r = -0.340, P = 0.012) and the coefficient of variation from 0000 h to 0600 h (CVnight) (CSII: r = -0.238, P = 0.009; IDeg: r = -0.333, P = 0.006; IGlar U300: r = -0.367, P = 0.007).

Discharge therapy stratified by BMI

Given the strengthened association between lower BMI and less favorable glycemic parameters after transition, we examined the treatment patterns at discharge. Analysis revealed a distinct distribution of therapies by BMI category (Table 5). Patients with BMI < 25 kg/m² were prescribed MDI more frequently than those with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² (36.1% vs. 6.3%, P < 0.001). Conversely, regimens containing SGLT-2i and/or GLP-1RA were more common in the higher BMI group (61.9% vs. 9.7%). This prescribing pattern was consistent within both the IDeg and IGlar U300 subgroups (both P < 0.001). Notably, the decision to use MDI in low-BMI patients was not influenced by the type of basal insulin, as the proportion of such patients receiving MDI was comparable between the IDeg and IGlar U300 groups (42.9% vs. 26.7%, P = 0.289).

Nocturnal nadir patterns and nocturnal hypoglycemia events

Hypoglycemia events

During the CSII period, a total of 26 nocturnal hypoglycemic events (Level 1, < 3.9 mmol/L) were documented, with an equal distribution between the groups (IDeg: 13 events; IGlar U300: 13 events). After transitioning to long-acting insulin analogues, a total of 47 nocturnal hypoglycemic events (Level 1) were recorded (IDeg: 22 events; IGlar U300: 25 events). No Level 2 (< 3.0 mmol/L) nocturnal hypoglycemic events occurred during either study period. A subset of Level 1 events was verified by capillary blood glucose measurement (IDeg: 3 events; IGlar U300: 1 event).

Distribution of nocturnal glucose nadir

The temporal distribution of the nocturnal glucose nadir differed markedly between the two insulin groups (Fig. 1). In the IGlar U300 group, nadirs were evenly distributed throughout the night, with the proportion of patients per 30-minute interval remaining stable at approximately 4–9%. In contrast, the IDeg group showed a clear shift towards later timing: nadirs were less frequent before 03:45 (3–6% per interval) and increased progressively thereafter, culminating in a distinct peak in the final pre-awakening interval (05:45–06:15), where nearly 19% of IDeg patients experienced their nadir compared to ~ 13% in the IGlar U300 group. These observations are descriptive only; no statistical test was applied.

Nocturnal nadir patterns by concomitant treatment regimen

Given the limited sample size precluding meaningful statistical stratification by both insulin type and specific concomitant therapy, the nocturnal nadir distributions are presented for the entire cohort, aggregated by major concomitant treatment regimen (Supplementary Fig. 1). These supplementary figures illustrate the variation in nocturnal glucose nadir timing associated with different concomitant glucose-lowering regimens (MDI, Basal Insulin + SGLT-2i, Basal Insulin + GLP-1RA, Basal Insulin + SGLT-2i + GLP-1RA, and Basal Insulin + Other Oral Agents).

Discussion

This real-world study addresses the critical, yet under-investigated, transition from inpatient CSII to ambulatory regimens based on second-generation basal insulin analogues in T2DM. By employing CGM, we provide a multidimensional assessment of glycemic control, variability, and hypoglycemia risk during this pivotal period. Our findings demonstrate that transitioning to either IDeg or IGlar U300 is viable and safe. More importantly, the outcomes are influenced less by the choice between these two analogues and more by the interplay of patient characteristics and the subsequent clinical decisions they inform.

The transition from CSII to either IDeg or IGlar U300 proved both effective and safe. CGM metrics revealed comprehensive improvements across all key glycemic aspects. Compared to the CSII period, significant enhancements were observed in overall glycemic control, evidenced by increased TIR and TITR, alongside a reduction in TAR. This was accompanied by a marked decrease in overall glycemic variability. Critically, safety was preserved. Although the TBR increased in the IDeg group post-transition, it remained well within the recommended safety threshold. Analysis of the LBGI indicated no significant increase in the risk of significant hypoglycemia, further confirming the safety profile [19]. No statistically significant differences were observed between IDeg and IGlar U300 in these primary endpoints of glycemic control, hypoglycemia risk, or overall variability, aligning with prior comparisons [24, 25]. The IDeg group demonstrated a lower CVFBS compared to the IGlar U300 group. This observation aligns with the ultra-long and flat pharmacokinetic profile of IDeg and could contribute to a more predictable foundation for daytime glycemic management [26]. Given that IDeg is highly albumin‑bound, we performed exploratory stratified analyses by albumin level (< 35 g/L vs. ≥35 g/L) to evaluate potential differential effects. These analyses suggested that the lower CVFBS associated with IDeg was more apparent in patients with normal albumin levels, whereas no significant between‑group difference was observed in the hypoalbuminemia subgroup (data not shown). This was particularly evident for the hypoalbuminemia subgroup (albumin < 35 g/L; IDeg: n = 7; IGlar U300: n = 5), which precluded meaningful between-group comparison of potential albumin-binding differences between the two insulins. Furthermore, given the multiple between-group comparisons performed without adjustment for multiplicity, this exploratory finding should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of a clinical advantage for IDeg. Confirmatory studies with pre-specified endpoints are warranted. It is also important to note that the post‑transition CGM metrics reflect the entire discharge regimen (including prandial insulin, SGLT‑2i, GLP‑1RA, and other oral agents) rather than the effect of the basal insulin analogue alone. Although the distribution of concomitant therapies was well balanced between the IDeg and IGlar U300 groups (P = 0.171), this does not guarantee pharmacological equivalence, particularly for exploratory findings such as the lower CVFBS observed with IDeg. Therefore, the between‑group comparison should be interpreted as a comparison of real‑world treatment strategies rather than a pure drug‑level comparison.

Beyond comparing insulins, a central finding is the “amplification” of the association between lower BMI and adverse glycemic outcomes post-transition. This association, weak during the flexible CSII phase, strengthened considerably after switching to a fixed injection regimen. This observation appears to be associated with clinical decision-making: patients with lower BMI were disproportionately prescribed more complex MDI regimens, whereas those with higher BMI more frequently received regimens incorporating SGLT-2i or GLP-1RA. This pattern suggests that the “amplification effect” may be partly attributable to the greater self-management demands of the MDI regimen itself. Consequently, for lower-BMI patients transitioning from CSII, clinicians should critically assess the automatic selection of MDI. Prioritizing evaluation of simpler, potent combinations—such as a combination of basal insulin with a GLP-1RA [27–29]—could achieve glycemic targets without imposing the burden of intensive injection therapy. However, given the observational design, these associations should not be interpreted as causal or predictive.

Further insights emerged from other patient characteristics. Older age was associated with a higher overall glycemic profile post-transition, a pattern consistent with the conservative management strategies often employed to prioritize hypoglycemia avoidance in elderly patients [30, 31]. Separately, lower albumin was observed to be a stable, insulin-independent biomarker associated with heightened glycemic variability, showing correlations with greater intra-day and nocturnal glucose fluctuations both during and after CSII. In contrast, eGFR showed no significant associations in this cohort, which was limited by the absence of individuals with severe renal impairment. This divergence underscores the potential value of albumin as a routine marker for identifying patients predisposed to glycemic instability, warranting closer monitoring irrespective of renal function or insulin type [32–34].

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the single‑center, retrospective design may limit the generalizability of our findings to other healthcare settings with different patient populations and clinical practices. Moreover, this study was conducted in a Chinese hospital setting; differences in BMI distribution (typically lower in Chinese populations), beta‑cell reserve (often more pronounced insulin deficiency at diagnosis in Asian patients), and standard management practices between Chinese and Western populations should be considered when extrapolating these findings to other populations. Second, the retrospective, non‑randomized design introduces potential selection bias, as treatment allocation was based on clinical judgment and practical factors rather than a standardized protocol. Although baseline characteristics were well balanced, the lack of covariate adjustment may not fully account for unmeasured confounders inherent to the retrospective design. Third, the sample size was relatively small for certain subgroup analyses, which may constrain meaningful interpretation. Fourth, the follow‑up period was focused on short‑term glycemic outcomes during hospitalization; the absence of follow‑up data after the CGM monitoring window limits our understanding of long‑term glycemic durability and clinical outcomes. Furthermore, the study design involves structural asymmetry between the two phases: the CSII period was managed with real‑time CGM‑guided titration in a supervised inpatient setting, whereas the post‑transition period included both inpatient and outpatient days with less intensive monitoring. The observed ~ 17% point improvement in TIR therefore cannot be attributed solely to the switch to basal insulin analogues; it likely also reflects differences in clinical oversight, dietary and activity patterns, and the resolution of acute hyperglycemic toxicity following initial CSII stabilization. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the within‑group improvements. Fifth, while the SIBIONICS GS1 sensor has demonstrated acceptable accuracy, it may vary under real‑world conditions, and the sensor has not been as extensively validated as some other commercially available CGM devices. Consequently, our findings should be interpreted with caution and considered hypothesis‑generating.

Conclusion

In this single-center retrospective study, transitioning from CSII to either IDeg or IGlar U300 was effective and safe, with comparable overall control. Exploratory analysis suggested that IDeg may be associated with more stable fasting glucose, but this finding requires confirmation in future studies. The key to successful transition may involve personalized strategies: considering simpler regimens for low-BMI patients and monitoring variability closely in those with low albumin. Given the exploratory, retrospective nature of these findings, prospective validation is warranted.
