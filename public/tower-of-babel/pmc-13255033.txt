The SMAC-mimetic xevinapant differentially enhances cisplatin and immune checkpoint blockade efficacy in high-risk HPV-positive tumors
Authors: Tsung-You Tsai, Mutsuki Kawabe, Arisa Nishikawa Kaga, Kento Okamoto, Jeffrey N. Myers, Abdullah A. Osman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•HPV+ HNSCC exhibits selective vulnerability to IAP inhibition by xevinapant.•Xevinapant enhances cisplatin-induced DNA damage and apoptosis in HNSCC HPV+ models.•IAP blockade disrupts the HPV E7–Rb–E2F axis and activates interferon signaling.•Xevinapant potentiates anti–PD-1 efficacy and antitumor immunity in HPV+ HNSCC model.

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is a major global health concern, with approximately 878,000 new cases and 444,000 deaths reported each year [1]. The current standard of care for locally advanced HNSCC involves surgical resection followed by radiation therapy or cisplatin-based chemoradiotherapy (CRT), or definitive CRT for advanced tumors in particular subsites including of cancers of the oropharynx, larynx, and hypopharynx [2]. Despite these intensive multimodal treatment strategies, patient outcomes remain unsatisfactory, as evidenced by a 5-year overall survival rate of approximately 50% [3,4].

The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has provided new therapeutic options with a more favorable toxicity profile compared to conventional chemotherapy. However, only a minority of patients achieve durable responses, and the overall response rate (ORR) of immunotherapy in HNSCC remains modest, ranging from 13% to 20% [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. The KEYNOTE 048 trial established PD 1 blockade as the first line backbone in recurrent/metastatic HNSCC and highlighted PD-L1 combined positive score stratification. However, in this trial, neither pembrolizumab monotherapy nor pembrolizumab combined with chemotherapy significantly improved progression-free survival compared to standard treatment [7]. Although HPV-positive (HPV⁺) HNSCC generally exhibits higher response rates and improved survival following immunotherapy compared with HPV-negative (HPV⁻) disease, these benefits remain incomplete and insufficient for a substantial proportion of patients, underscoring persistent biological and therapeutic heterogeneity within HPV-associated tumors [9]. In the KEYNOTE-012 trial, HPV-positive tumors demonstrated superior outcomes with pembrolizumab, including longer progression-free survival (4 vs. 2 months) and higher objective response rate (32%vs. 14%) [[10], [11], [12]]. Collectively, these findings indicate that HPV-positive HNSCC represents a biologically distinct yet therapeutically under-exploited subgroup, in which additional vulnerabilities beyond PD-1 signaling remain to be defined. The limited efficacy of PD-1-targeted therapy and the significant toxicity associated with conventional CRT highlight the need for novel, safer, and more effective treatment strategies for HNSCC [5,6,13].

HNSCC cells are characterized by complex genomic alterations and frequently evade apoptosis through multiple mechanisms, such as overexpression of anti-apoptotic proteins (e.g., Bcl-xL, MCL-1, and Survivin) and suppression of pro-apoptotic signaling pathways. Targeting these resistance mechanisms using BH3-mimetics or inhibitor of apoptosis protein (IAP) antagonists has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy. Xevinapant (previously known as Debio 1143) is an oral, first-in-class small-molecule SMAC mimetic that targets inhibitor of apoptosis proteins (IAPs), such as XIAP and cIAP1/2 [[14], [15], [16]]. By neutralizing these proteins, xevinapant restores apoptotic signaling and enhances tumor sensitivity to chemotherapy or radiation across multiple cancer types, including ovarian, non–small cell lung, and breast cancers [[17], [18], [19]]. Moreover, inhibition of cIAP1/2 can potentiate antitumor immunity by activating the noncanonical nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway, thereby promoting pro-inflammatory cytokine production and immune cell recruitment within the tumor microenvironment [[20], [21], [22]]. These immunomodulatory properties position IAP inhibition as a context-dependent strategy with the potential to reshape tumor–immune interactions rather than uniformly intensify cytotoxic therapy.

Early clinical studies have suggested that xevinapant can enhance the efficacy of chemoradiotherapy in selected high-risk population, with a phase II trial demonstrated significantly improved survival in patients with high-risk, locally advanced HNSCC treated with xevinapant plus CRT compared to CRT alone [14]. However, the subsequent phase III TrilynX trial failed to replicate these results and was terminated early due to lack of efficacy [23,24]. Emerging evidence indicates that combining xevinapant with chemoradiotherapy may negatively impact the tumor immune microenvironment and suppress antitumor immunity, further underscoring the context-dependent nature of its biological effects [24]. Rather than negating the biological activity of xevinapant, the TrilynX results highlight the challenges of further intensifying an already aggressive CRT backbone and underscore the importance of patient selection, treatment context, and mechanistic alignment when deploying IAP inhibition clinically [14,25]. Together, these observations support the rationale for evaluating xevinapant in non-radiotherapy settings, where its dual apoptotic and immunomodulatory properties may be more effectively leveraged.

Because cisplatin is still the gold standard regimen for the treatment of HNSCC, in this study, we aim to evaluate whether xevinapant sensitizes chemo-resistant HNSCC to cisplatin and inhibits tumor growth both in vitro and in vivo. In parallel, we investigated whether xevinapant enhances the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade in preclinical immunocompetent models of both HPV-positive and HPV-negative HNSCC, with particular emphasis on defining HPV-associated biological vulnerabilities independent of radiotherapy.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and cell lines

Human HNSCC cell lines included HPV16-positive UMSCC-47 (RRID: CVCL_7759) and HMS-001 (RRID: CVCL_UH26), and HPV-negative HN30 (RRID: CVCL_5525), HN31 (RRID: CVCL_5526), MDA-1586 (RRID: CVCL_6981), TR-146 (RRID: CVCL_2736), UMSCC-17A (RRID: CVCL_7724), and HN5 (RRID: CVCL_8128). The cisplatin-resistant derivative HN30-R8 was generated as previously described [[26], [27], [28]].

Unless otherwise indicated, cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), l-glutamine, sodium pyruvate, non-essential amino acids, and vitamins at 37 °C in 5% CO₂; HMS-001 was cultured in DMEM/F-12 with identical supplements. All experiments used early-passage cells. Cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma and authenticated by short tandem repeat analysis within 6 months of use [29].

Generation of stable murine cell lines expressing HPV16-E6 and E7 oncogenes

For murine experiments requiring HPV oncogene expression, MOC1 and MOC2 cells (RRID: CVCL_ZD32 and RRID: CVCL_ZD33 respectively) were transduced with retroviral pBabe-puro–HPV16 E6/E7 or empty pBabe-puro (control) plasmids kindly provided by Dr Dennis McCance (Queen's University Belfast). After co-transfection of these plasmids in 293T cells with appropriate retroviral packaging vectors, the viral supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 1000 rpm to remove cellular debris and added to MOC1and MOC2 cells in combination with polybrene (5 µg/mL). After 1 passage, the cells underwent selection with puromycin (1.0 µg/mL). Expression of E6/E7 in stable cell lines was confirmed by western blotting for E7 and functional loss of p53 protein.

Clonogenic survival assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated for 24 h with cisplatin (0–5 µmol/L), xevinapant (0–10 µmol/L), or a fixed 1:1 molar-ratio. Xevinapant was maintained throughout the assay. After 10–14 days, colonies were fixed, stained, and quantified as previously described [30]. Xevinapant (Debio-1143) was obtained from Merck KGaA and prepared as a 10 mmol/L DMSO stock.

Drug interactions were analyzed using the Chou–Talalay method. Combination index (CI) values were calculated with CalcuSyn software, where CI < 1 indicates synergy.

Western blot analysis

Drug concentrations for mechanistic studies were selected based on clonogenic IC₅₀ values. Cells were treated with xevinapant (10 µmol/L), cisplatin (1.5 µmol/L), or the combination for 24–48 h. Whole-cell lysates were prepared and analyzed by Western blotting as previously described [29].

Cell-cycle analysis and apoptosis detection

Human and murine HNSCC cell lines were seeded in 60-mm dishes and treated with cisplatin (1.5 µmol/L), xevinapant (10 µmol/L) or their combination for 24, 48 and 72 h as indicated. The cell cycle analysis was performed as previously described [29]. Apoptosis was detected using the Annexin V-FITC/PI staining kit obtained from BD Bioscience according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

UMSCC-47 cells were treated for 24 h with vehicle, xevinapant, cisplatin, or the combination (n = 3 biological replicates per condition). RNA sequencing was performed by LC Sciences using Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (2 × 150 bp). Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 and edgeR, with significance defined as FDR < 0.05 and |fold change| ≥ 2. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed using fgsea with MSigDB Hallmark gene sets.

In vivo orthotopic mouse model of head and neck cancer

All animal experiments were approved by the IACUC of MD Anderson Cancer Center. For xenograft studies, athymic nude mice were orthotopically injected with UMSCC-47 cells and randomized to receive vehicle, xevinapant, cisplatin, or the combination. Xevinapant (100 mg/kg) was administered orally 5 days per week, and cisplatin (5 mg/kg) was given intravenously once weekly.

For immunocompetent studies, C57BL/6 mice were injected with MOC1-vector or MOC1-E6/E7 cells and treated with xevinapant and/or anti–PD-1 antibody. Tumor growth was monitored bi-weekly. Tumor volume was calculated as A × B² × (π/6). Animals were euthanized upon predefined humane endpoints, and tissues were collected for histologic analysis.

Immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections were stained for RB, cIAP1, CD8, FoxP3, CD4, and phospho-STING. RB and cIAP1 expression were quantified using H-scores, while immune cell infiltration was quantified as cells/mm². All analysis were performed blinded to treatment.

Statistical analysis

In vitro experiments were conducted with at least three independent biological replicates, while in vivo studies included 9–12 mice per group. Data are presented as mean ± SD for in vitro experiments and mean ± SEM for in vivo studies. Statistical tests included two-way ANOVA, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, Fisher’s exact test, and Kruskal–Wallis tests with appropriate post-hoc corrections. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

HNSCC cell lines display differential sensitivity to the SMAC-mimetic xevinapant in vitro

To assess the relative sensitivity of HNSCC cells to xevinapant, a panel of HNSCC cell lines with distinct mutational profiles and HPV statuses were treated with increasing concentrations of xevinapant (0–10 μmol/L) for 48 h, and assessed by clonogenic survival assays. The results revealed marked differences in xevinapant sensitivity among the tested cell lines irrespective of genomic alterations known to occur in HNSCC (Fig. 1A–B). Notably, HPV-positive UMSCC-47 and HMS-0 01 cells exhibited substantially greater sensitivity to xevinapant (half maximal inhibitory concentration IC₅₀ = 0.12–0.27 μmol/L) compared with HPV-negative lines such as HN-30 and HN-30R8, which displayed IC₅₀ values exceeding 8 μmol/L (Fig. 1A–B). Given these differential cytotoxic responses, we next evaluated whether xevinapant effectively engaged its molecular targets. Xevinapant synergizes with cisplatin in vitro in HNSCC cells through induction of DNA damage response associated with apoptosis

Given that cisplatin remains the standard-of-care chemotherapy for HNSCC, we next investigated whether xevinapant could potentiate the therapeutic efficacy of conventional cisplatin-based treatment. To date, xevinapant has not been evaluated in HPV-positive HNSCC tumor cells. Therefore, the HPV-positive UMSCC-47 cell line, which exhibited the highest sensitivity to xevinapant (submicromolar IC₅₀), was selected as a representative model for subsequent combination studies. UMSCC-47 cells were treated with xevinapant and cisplatin either individually or in combination, and clonogenic survival assays revealed that the co-treatment markedly reduced colony formation compared with either single agent alone (Fig. 2A). To quantitatively assess the nature of the drug interaction, the combination index (CI) method of Chou and Talalay [31] was used. Isobologram analysis of effective doses (ED₅₀, ED₇₅, and ED₉₀) derived from Fig. 2A showed CI values below 1.0 (UMSCC-47; CI = 0.41), indicating strong synergy between xevinapant and cisplatin (Fig. 2B). To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying this synergy, Western blot analyses were performed and demonstrated enhanced poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) cleavage, pronounced depletion of cIAP1, and accumulation of the DNA damage marker phosphorylated histone H2AX (γ-H2AX) following combination treatment (Fig. 2C). Consistent with these findings, Annexin V/PI staining revealed a significantly higher proportion of apoptotic cells at both 48 and 72 h post-treatment compared with monotherapy (Fig. 2D–E). At the signaling level, the combination regimen suppressed the oncogenic E7 protein and the transcription factor E2F1 while upregulating Rb/pRb expression, activated the cyclic GMP–AMP synthase (cGAS)–stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway (as indicated by increased p-STING and p-TBK1), and markedly inhibited signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) phosphorylation at both Y705 and S727 residues (Figure 2F; Supplementary Figure S1). To further investigate the functional contribution of E7 to xevinapant sensitivity, E7 knockdown was performed in UMSCC-47 cells. E7 depletion significantly reduced clonogenic survival and further enhanced sensitivity to xevinapant over a range of concentrations, supporting a role for E7 in maintaining tumor cell survival and modulating therapeutic response (Supplementary Figure S2A-S2C). In addition, co-treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 partially restored E7 protein levels following xevinapant exposure, suggesting that xevinapant-induced E7 downregulation may occur through a proteasome-dependent mechanism (Supplementary Figure S2D). Furthermore, UMSCC-47 cells were co-treated with the pharmacologic STING inhibitor H-151 during exposure to xevinapant and cisplatin. To better understand the longer-term effects of drug treatment, UM-SCC-47 cells were treated with cisplatin and xevinapant, and examined at 24–72 h for cell cycle kinetics. Flow cytometric analysis showed that xevinapant alone induced G1-phase accumulation, indicative of partial G1 arrest. In contrast, combination treatment increased the S-phase population, consistent with cisplatin-induced replication stress, while maintaining a modest G1 enrichment relative to cisplatin alone, suggesting that xevinapant partially preserves its G1-arrest effect even under cisplatin exposure (Fig. 2G–H).

To determine whether this synergistic response was consistent across different molecular contexts, additional HNSCC cell lines were examined, including another HPV-positive model (HMS-001), an HPV-negative line (HN31), and a cisplatin-resistant derivative (HN30-R8). In all cell lines tested, the combination of xevinapant and cisplatin suppressed XIAP and cIAP1 protein expression, enhanced PARP cleavage, increased Annexin V–positive apoptosis, and significantly reduced clonogenic survival (Supplementary Figures S4–S6). Notably, the HPV-positive HMS-001 line exhibited particularly high sensitivity to the xevinapant–cisplatin combination (Supplementary Figure S4A–B), accompanied by increased accumulation of sub-G1 phase cells following treatment (Supplementary Figure S4E–F), consistent with apoptotic cell death. Collectively, these results demonstrate that xevinapant synergizes with cisplatin to enhance cytotoxicity and apoptotic cell death, particularly in HPV-positive HNSCC cells that are intrinsically sensitive to cisplatin.

Combination of xevinapant and cisplatin causes significant in vivo tumor growth delay and improves overall animal survival

Building upon the findings from the 2D in vitro clonogenic survival assays, we next evaluated the antitumor efficacy of cisplatin, xevinapant, and their combination in an orthotopic nude mouse model of HPV-positive HNSCC. Mice were injected in the tongue with UM-SCC-47 cells and subsequently treated with cisplatin or xevinapant alone, or with both agents in combination. While monotherapies with either xevinapant or cisplatin partially inhibited tumor growth, the combination treatment resulted in a significant suppression of oral tumor growth compared with either single agent or vehicle control (****P < 0.0001; Fig. 3A). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis further demonstrated that the combination therapy markedly prolonged overall survival, extending median survival beyond 35 days, compared with 25–30 days in the monotherapy groups (Fig. 3B). Importantly, no mice in the single-agent or combination treatment groups exhibited >10% body weight loss or clinical signs of toxicity, indicating that the regimen was well tolerated (Fig. 3C). Histopathological analysis revealed that combination therapy substantially reduced cervical lymph node metastasis to <20% incidence, compared with over 60% in untreated controls (Fig. 3D–E). Transcriptomic profiling identifies enrichment of interferon and apoptotic signaling pathways following treatment with xevinapant and cisplatin in HPV-positive HNSCC cells

To gain further insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying the response to xevinapant and cisplatin, we performed transcriptomic profiling of HPV-positive UMSCC-47 cells following treatment. Hierarchical clustering of the top differentially expressed genes represented in the heatmap revealed distinct transcriptional signatures in both the xevinapant and combination treatment groups compared with non-treated (NT) controls (Fig. 4A). The combination group clustered independently, indicating a broader transcriptional reprogramming effect. Volcano plot analysis also demonstrated that treatment with xevinapant or its combination with cisplatin led to extensive transcriptional alterations. Using thresholds of Log₂FC≥ 1 and adjusted P < 0.05, xevinapant alone significantly upregulated multiple interferon-stimulated and innate immune genes (e.g., CCL5, MX2, OAS2, OAS3, IFI6, BIRC3, NFE2L3, ADAM8, KLHDC7B) while downregulating epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and differentiation-related genes (BMP4, MIR34AHG, FOLR1, KRT4, HSD17B3) (Fig. 4B, Left). The combination therapy produced a broader transcriptional response, with stronger enrichment of interferon and inflammatory signaling genes (e.g., CCL5, RSAD2, MX2, ISG20, GBP4, LAMP3, IFI44L, BIRC3, HSH2D) and suppression of metabolic and cell-cycle regulators (KRT19, SLC7A5, ALDH3A2, ID1) (Fig. 4B, Right). The increased density of highly significant genes highlights the amplified transcriptional impact of the combination treatment. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further confirmed the activation of both type I and type II interferon pathways. The Interferon Alpha Response and Interferon Gamma Response gene sets were significantly enriched in the xevinapant and combination-treated groups, with the combination showing stronger enrichment characterized by higher normalized enrichment scores (NES) and lower false discovery rates (FDR) (Fig. 4C). Additional enrichment was observed for TNF-α signaling via NF-κB, which also exhibited a more pronounced activation in the combination group (Fig. 4C). Hallmark pathway analysis illustrated in the bubble plot revealed that xevinapant monotherapy upregulated interferon response and NF-κB signaling pathways, highlighting its role in enhancing inflammatory and immune-related transcriptional programs (Supplementary Figure S7A). To further assess the reproducibility of interferon-related transcriptional responses across HPV-positive tumor models, qPCR validation was performed in additional HPV-positive cell line HMS-001. Xevinapant treatment induced marked upregulation of the IFN pathway genes IFI6, MX2, and OAS2 at 48 h, consistent with the RNA-seq findings observed in UMSCC-47 cells (Supplementary Figure S7B). The combination treatment elicited more pronounced activation pattern, with significant upregulation of interferon-related, TNF-α/NF-κB, and apoptosis-associated signatures, accompanied by marked downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis, and E2F target gene sets (Fig. 4D–E). Notably, suppression of E2F target and G₂/M checkpoint pathways was consistent with the previously observed decrease in E2F1 expression and enhanced Rb activation, indicating a coordinated anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic response. HPV16 E6/E7 expression enhances xevinapant sensitivity through induction of apoptosis and modulates the cGAS/STING signaling pathway in MOC1 cells

Our data demonstrates that HPV-positive HNSCC cell lines were markedly more sensitive to xevinapant treatment than HPV-negative counterparts. Notably, xevinapant treatment reduced the expression of the HPV E7 oncoprotein, suggesting that susceptibility to IAP inhibition may be linked to E6 and/or E7 oncogene expression. Previous studies have shown that E7 binds to the retinoblastoma tumor suppressor (Rb) and disrupts its ability to repress replication gene expression, thereby driving cell-cycle progression [32]. Furthermore, silencing of E6 and/or E7 has been reported to restore p53 and pRb function and induce apoptosis in HPV16-positive oropharyngeal cancer cells [33]. Collectively, these findings suggest that HPV-positive tumors rely on E7 expression to tolerate replication stress and survive DNA damage. Based on this, we examined whether IAP inhibition could disrupt oncogenic E7 signaling in HPV-positive tumor cells and sensitize them to xevinapant. Therefore, murine MOC1 cells were transduced with a pBABE-HPV16 E6/E7 expression vector, and successful integration was confirmed by Western blotting (Fig. 5A). E6/E7 expression markedly suppressed p53 protein levels compared with vector controls (Fig. 5B), consistent with the well-established ability of HPV oncoproteins to promote p53 degradation. Clonogenic survival assays revealed that E6/E7 expression significantly enhanced xevinapant sensitivity, as demonstrated by a leftward shift in the dose–response curve and a reduction in IC₅₀ from 1.647 µmol/L to 0.918 µmol/L (Fig. 5C–D). Western blot analysis further showed that xevinapant treatment induced DNA damage (increased γH2AX) and activated apoptosis-related signaling in both pBABE control and E6/E7-expressing cells (Fig. 5E). Consistent with findings in human HPV-positive models, xevinapant treatment in MOC1 E6/E7 murine cells resulted in downregulation of E2F1, increased pRb expression, activation of the cGAS–STING–TBK1 pathway, and suppression of STAT3 signaling. However, unlike endogenous HPV-positive human HNSCC cells, ectopically expressed E7 levels in the pBABE-E6/E7 system were not substantially altered following treatment. Xevinapant enhances immune-associated antitumor responses and synergizes with PD-1 blockade

Immunotherapy has emerged as a key therapeutic strategy for HNSCC due to its potential to elicit durable antitumor responses. However, its efficacy remains limited by tumor immune escape mechanisms, including cytokine dysregulation, immune checkpoint alterations, and recruitment of suppressive immune cells. Our in vitro transcriptomic analyses revealed that combined cisplatin and xevinapant treatment enriched inflammatory and interferon signaling pathways and activated distinct immune–stress networks. Consistently, western blot analysis showed that xevinapant alone was associated with increased signaling through the cGAS–STING pathway, which is considered as an activator of innate immunity. These findings led us to hypothesize that xevinapant could enhance the efficacy of the immune checkpoint blockade in HNSCC. To test this, we evaluated xevinapant alone or in combination with anti–PD-1 antibody in syngeneic mice bearing MOC1 pBABE control or MOC1 E6/E7 tumors. In both models, monotherapy with xevinapant or anti–PD-1 modestly inhibited tumor growth compared with controls, whereas the combination therapy induced marked tumor regression and delayed progression (Fig. 6A–B). Combination-treated mice exhibited the smallest tumor volumes and highest rates of complete response, particularly in the MOC1 E6/E7 group. Kaplan–Meier analysis further showed that xevinapant plus anti–PD-1 significantly prolonged overall survival compared with either monotherapy (Fig. 6C–D), confirming a strong synergistic antitumor effect. No significant body weight loss or treatment-related toxicity was observed (Supplementary Figure S8A–B). Moreover, pathological analysis revealed a significantly lower incidence of cervical lymph node metastasis in the combination group across both models (Supplementary Figure S8C–E). Immunohistochemical staining further revealed that xevinapant monotherapy increased intratumoral CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration. The combination group also exhibited higher CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration compared with the untreated control; however, this increase did not exceed that observed with xevinapant monotherapy (Fig. 6E–F). Conversely, the proportion of immunosuppressive FoxP3⁺ regulatory T cells was markedly reduced following xevinapant or combination treatment (Fig. 6G–H). In addition, immunohistochemical analysis of phosphorylated STING (pSTING) revealed a slight increase in STING activation within tumor tissues from xevinapant-treated mice, particularly in the combination group, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (Supplementary Figure S9A–B). Discussion

This study establishes an integrated mechanistic framework in which the SMAC mimetic xevinapant exerts antitumor activity in HPV-positive HNSCC through coordinated modulation of apoptotic priming, cell-cycle regulation, and innate immune activation. Notably, these effects were consistently more pronounced in HPV-positive than in HPV-negative models, supporting the concept that HPV-driven tumors possess a context-dependent vulnerability to IAP inhibition rather than a uniform class-wide response. A hallmark pharmacodynamic feature of SMAC mimetics is rapid degradation of cIAP1 [34], which was reproducibly observed across our models confirming effective drug target engagement. In contrast, XIAP and cIAP2 (BIRC3) displayed variable modulation, consistent with their known context-dependent regulation [35,36]. RNA-seq analysis revealed induction of BIRC3 following xevinapant exposure, consistent with NF-κB–mediated compensatory feedback after cIAP1 loss [[34], [35], [36], [37]]. Importantly, this transcriptional response did not attenuate apoptotic signaling, indicating preserved drug activity.

A central finding of this study is the increased sensitivity of HPV-positive HNSCC to xevinapant. The HPV16-E6 and-E7 oncoproteins disrupt p53- and Rb-dependent cell-cycle checkpoints, resulting in constitutive E2F activation and replication stress. This state may increase tumor cell dependence on anti-apoptotic IAP signaling [38,39], thereby conferring enhanced sensitivity to xevinapant. In current study, xevinapant rapidly decreased E7 abundance and increased total Rb protein in UMSCC-47 cells before major shifts in cell-cycle distribution became evident. This early biochemical phenotype was mirrored in vivo, where oral xenografts treated with xevinapant, or combination therapy displayed increased Rb staining. Although prior preclinical studies have employed murine HPV-associated models such as mEER, those systems incorporate additional oncogenic drivers and are typically evaluated in the context of definitive chemoradiotherapy, which may fundamentally alter immune and tumor-intrinsic dependencies [24]. In contrast, our findings reveal an HPV oncogene-linked vulnerability to IAP inhibition that emerges in the absence of radiotherapy. In endogenous HPV-positive HNSCC models, xevinapant consistently reduced HPV16 E7 protein levels together with downstream modulation of the Rb–E2F axis. To our knowledge, a direct modulation of HPV oncogene output by xevinapant has not been previously reported. The observed reduction in E7 levels may be mediated, at least in part, through post-translational mechanisms involving proteasome-dependent degradation, as co-treatment with MG132 partially restored E7 expression. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms linking IAP inhibition to suppression of HPV oncogene expression.

Temporal analyses further revealed distinct contributions of xevinapant and cisplatin to cell-cycle regulation. Xevinapant induced early Rb stabilization whereas cisplatin predominantly triggered delayed checkpoint activation and S/G2–M accumulation. This biphasic pattern supports a model in which xevinapant lowers apoptotic resistance upstream of DNA damage-induced arrest. Consistent with this model, xevinapant synergized with cisplatin to reduce colony survival, enhance apoptosis, and amplify DNA damage signaling. Transcriptomic profiling of combination treatment revealed coordinated suppression of E2F targets and cell-cycle progression programs, consistent with impaired recovery from genotoxic stress.

In addition to promoting tumor-intrinsic apoptosis, xevinapant induced transcriptomic and biochemical changes consistent with activation of innate immune signaling pathways. Both biochemical and transcriptomic analyses demonstrated engagement of the cGAS–STING axis and enrichment of interferon-α, interferon-γ, and TNF-α/NF-κB signaling programs. These responses are consistent with established consequences of cIAP1 loss and may enhance antigen presentation and immune cell recruitment [34,37,[39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44], [45]]. Although additional functional studies are needed to delineate the precise interactions among these pathways, the integrated innate immune landscape identified here provides a biologically plausible framework linking IAP inhibition to immune-associated transcriptional remodeling.

In syngeneic models, xevinapant increased CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and markedly reduced FoxP3⁺ Tregs, producing a more inflamed and less suppressive microenvironment. This shift in the intratumoral immune balance may facilitate improved responsiveness to PD-1 blockade by reducing immunosuppressive signaling while supporting cytotoxic T-cell activity. CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration in the combination group did not exceed that of the xevinapant monotherapy, which may reflect near-complete tumor regression limiting quantitative immune assessment; however, functional outcomes clearly demonstrated preserved immune-mediated tumor control. The clinical context of these findings is particularly noteworthy, as xevinapant has not been systematically evaluated in HPV-positive HNSCC, and pivotal trials, including TrilynX, excluded patients with HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancer [25, 48, 49]. Moreover, TrilynX tested xevinapant in combination with full-dose cisplatin and definitive radiotherapy, a setting associated with substantial cumulative treatment burden [25,46]. Our data suggest that HPV-positive tumors, characterized by intrinsic apoptotic priming, cell cycle deregulation, replication stress, and immune responsiveness, may derive benefit from alternative therapeutic contexts, including lower-intensity regimens or rational combinations with immunotherapy. Importantly, outcomes from unselected, radiotherapy-intensive settings should not preclude further investigation in biologically defined populations. Although HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancers often show favorable outcomes, substantial heterogeneity persists, particularly among patients with smoking history, bulky nodal disease, or immune-cold phenotypes [[47], [48], [49], [50]]. Targeting HPV-linked vulnerabilities while enhancing antitumor immunity may therefore address unmet needs in intermediate- and high-risk subsets.

Although xevinapant-containing regimens were generally well tolerated in our preclinical models, with no significant bodyweight loss or overt toxicity observed, the long-term safety profile of SMAC mimetics remains an important consideration for clinical translation. Previous clinical studies have reported manageable but potentially significant toxicities, particularly when xevinapant is combined with intensive chemoradiotherapy regimens [25,46]. In addition, because IAP inhibition can modulate inflammatory and immune signaling pathways, further investigation will be required to better define potential immune-related and tissue-specific toxicities during prolonged treatment or combination therapy. Careful safety evaluation and biomarker-guided patient selection will therefore be important in future clinical development.

This study has several limitations. First, although the MOC1 E6/E7 model enabled controlled evaluation of HPV oncogene-associated effects in an immunocompetent setting, it does not fully recapitulate the biological complexity of naturally occurring HPV-positive versus HPV-negative HNSCC. Second, although our in vivo IHC analyses demonstrated increased CD8⁺ infiltration and reduced immune suppressive regulatory T cells, comprehensive immune profiling by flow cytometry was not performed due to the markedly reduced tumor size observed following treatment, which limited more detailed characterization of innate and adaptive immune cell subsets. Third, radiation-based treatment regimens were not evaluated, as our objective was to isolate the tumor-intrinsic and immune-modulatory effects of xevinapant independent of the confounding effects of radiotherapy-induced tissue injury and immune suppression. While chemoradiotherapy remains a standard curative modality, our findings may nevertheless have clinical relevance for patient populations in whom radiotherapy is not feasible and for treatment settings in which systemic therapy plays a more central role. Additional studies will be required to further define the mechanistic interactions between IAP inhibition, HPV-associated signaling, and antitumor immune responses.

In summary, this study defines a context-dependent framework in which inhibition of IAP signaling drives coordinated apoptotic and immunomodulatory effects in HPV-positive head and neck squamous cell carcinoma. Our data reveal a specific vulnerability of HPV-positive tumors to IAP inhibition, marked by disruption of the E7–Rb–E2F axis, enhanced apoptotic priming, and activation of innate immune programs. In the absence of radiotherapy, xevinapant cooperated with cisplatin and synergized with PD-1 blockade to promote immune-mediated tumor control. These results underscore that the biological effects of SMAC mimetics are highly dependent on therapeutic context. Rather than supporting uniform treatment escalation, our findings suggest that these agents may be most effective when applied in biomarker-defined settings that exploit tumor-intrinsic apoptotic dependence and immune responsiveness while avoiding excessive treatment intensity. HPV-positive tumors with higher PD-L1 expression may constitute a subgroup with increased likelihood of benefiting from the immunomodulatory effects of xevinapant-based combination therapies. Collectively, our data support viewing SMAC mimetics as biologic modulators whose benefit is maximized through alignment with disease biology and immune context.
