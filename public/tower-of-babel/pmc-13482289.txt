Optimization of milk and coconut milk proportions in the formulation of ice cream and evaluation of its quality characteristics
Authors: Priyanka Suvera, Atanu H. Jana, Ankit Bihola, Amit Kumar, Shaikh Adil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to select the proportion of dairy milk and coconut milk for the development of a value-added tender coconut-based ice cream with improved sensory and physicochemical properties with functional potential. Ice cream mixes were formulated by blending standardized mixed milk (4.50% fat, 8.50% SNF) with coconut milk in three proportions (3:2, 1:1, and 2:3 w/w), while maintaining constant levels of milk fat (10.50%), milk solids-not-fat (12.00%), sucrose (15.00%), stabilizer (0.20%), and emulsifier (0.15%). The resultant ice creams were evaluated for proximate composition, pH, viscosity, hardness, melting rate, overrun, total phenolic content (TPC), and sensory characteristics. Increasing the proportion of coconut milk significantly (p < 0.05) increased total fat, total solids (TS), mix viscosity, hardness, melting resistance, and TPC, while reducing overrun. Sensory evaluation revealed that ice cream prepared using an equal proportion of milk and coconut milk (1:1, w/w) achieved the highest flavour score and total sensory score (93.60/100), reflecting optimal flavour balance, desirable body and texture, and improved melting quality. This optimized formulation exhibited a compositional profile (~ 19.0% total fat, 4.30% protein, 49.02% TS) and enhanced TPC value (0.25 mg GAE g−1). Overall, the study demonstrates that a 1:1 dairy milk–coconut milk blend is optimal for producing an acceptable quality hybrid ice cream, offering a scientifically validated approach for developing functional dairy–plant frozen desserts.

Introduction

Ice cream is a popular frozen dairy dessert consumed across diverse age groups due to its appealing sensory attributes and indulgent texture. Ice cream is a complex multiphase colloidal system produced by freezing and aerating a pasteurized mix containing milk fat, milk solids-not-fat (MSNF), sugars, stabilizers, emulsifiers, and flavouring and colouring agents1,2. The final product structure comprises ice crystals, air cells, partially coalesced fat globule networks, and a freeze-concentrated serum phase, the interactions among which govern texture, melting behaviour, and sensory perception3,4. Regulatory frameworks such as the Food Safety and Standards Regulations classify ice creams into low-, medium-, and high-fat categories based on milk fat content, reflecting the central role of fat in determining product quality, consumer perception, and market positioning2,5.

In recent years, ice cream has evolved from a conventional indulgent dessert into an effective delivery system for functional and health-oriented ingredients. Growing consumer awareness of diet-related disorders, gastrointestinal health, and clean-label foods has stimulated interest in incorporating phytochemicals, dietary fibre, prebiotics, probiotics, and high-protein ingredients into ice cream formulations6,7. Parallel efforts have focused on replacing or partially substituting conventional ingredients, particularly refined sugars and fats, with nutritionally superior alternatives such as honey, jaggery, brown sugar, and plant-derived components, while maintaining desirable sensory quality8,9. These formulation strategies improve nutritional and functional attributes while preserving consumer acceptability.

Premium ice cream represents a distinct segment within the frozen dessert market and is typically characterized by higher fat content (approximately 14.0–18.0%), lower overrun, and superior sensory attributes. Increased fat levels enhance creaminess, mouthfeel, and flavour release while limiting ice crystal growth, resulting in a smoother and more cohesive microstructure2,10. Reduced overrun yields a denser product with improved body, texture, and melting resistance, attributes highly valued in artisanal and specialty markets11,12. From a structural perspective, fat crystal morphology, partial coalescence, and fat destabilization during freezing play a decisive role in determining melting behaviour, mechanical strength, and sensory acceptance of ice cream13–15.

Coconut (Cocos nucifera) milk has gained increasing attention as a plant-derived alternative or complement to dairy milk in food systems such as ice cream, yoghurt, beverages, and cheese analogues. Coconut milk is defined as a dilute oil-in-water emulsion prepared by comminuting coconut endosperm in water16,17. Nutritionally, coconut milk is relatively easy to digest and provides essential minerals including calcium, phosphorus, and potassium, along with B-complex vitamins, vitamin C, and vitamin E17,18. It is also a source of phenolic compounds, including gallic acid, caffeic acid, and catechin, which contribute antioxidant activity and potential functional benefits18–20. The lipid fraction of coconut milk is rich in medium-chain saturated fatty acids, particularly lauric acid, with smaller proportions of oleic acid, may contribute to potential health-related functionality such as antimicrobial activity and cardiometabolic benefits21–24.

Despite the recognized nutritional benefits of milk and dairy products, their consumption may be constrained by lactose intolerance, perceived cholesterol-related health risks, allergenicity, and economic considerations. In this context, blending dairy ingredients with non-dairy components such as coconut milk, oilseeds, legumes, cereals, fruits, and vegetables has emerged as a promising strategy for product innovation25,26. Such hybrid formulations enable partial retention of dairy functionality while exploiting the nutritional, sensory, and economic advantages of plant-derived ingredients7,25. Previous studies have demonstrated that vegetable milks, particularly coconut milk, can substantially modify the rheological, physical, and sensory attributes of ice cream, including viscosity, overrun, melting resistance, and flavour perception27–29.

From a technological perspective, blending dairy milk with coconut milk in ice cream formulation presents both opportunities and challenges. Coconut fat differs markedly from milk fat in fatty acid composition, melting profile, and crystallization behaviour, which can significantly influence mix viscosity, fat destabilization, air incorporation, and melting resistance of the final product3,13,14. Increased proportions of coconut milk have been reported to enhance mix viscosity and melting resistance while reducing overrun, thereby improving structural stability but necessitating careful formulation control27,30,31. Moreover, coconut-derived phenolic compounds and medium-chain lipids can enhance the bio-functional properties of ice cream, positioning such products within the functional dessert category6,28.

Beyond nutritional and technological considerations, the incorporation of coconut milk into dairy formulations offers socioeconomic advantages by promoting value addition to coconut crops and supporting coconut-producing regions. The increasing presence of coconut-based and hybrid dairy and plant based ice creams in Indian and global markets reflects a broader transition toward innovative, and functionally enriched frozen desserts17,32,33.

Within this framework, the present investigation was carried out to evaluate the effect of varying dairy milk–coconut milk ratios (3:2, 1:1, and 2:3, w/w) on the quality characteristics of ice cream. Specifically, the study aimed to assess the influence of these formulations on physicochemical properties, melting characteristics, overrun, total phenolic content, and sensory attributes, and to identify the most suitable formulation that provides a desirable balance between structural, functional potential, and sensory quality in a hybrid dairy–plant ice cream system.

Materials and methods

This research focused on optimizing the proportion of mixed milk (dairy) and coconut milk in the formulation of ice cream mix while maintaining constant levels of key formulation components, namely total fat, milk solids-not-fat (MSNF), sucrose, and stabilizer–emulsifier blend. Mixed milk (cow–buffalo) was standardized to 4.50% fat and 8.50% solids-not-fat (SNF) and used as the primary dairy base for ice cream mix preparation.

Materials

Fresh mixed milk was procured from the Livestock Research Station, Sardarkrushinagar Dantiwada Agricultural University (SDAU), Sardarkrushinagar, Dantiwada, Gujarat, India. Cream containing 42.00 ± 2.00% fat was obtained by separating milk using a mechanical cream separator (Goma Engineering Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India) and was used to adjust the fat content of the ice cream mix as required. Skim milk powder (SMP) of the ‘Sagar’ brand (Gujarat Cooperative Milk Marketing Federation, Anand, Gujarat, India) was incorporated to achieve the desired MSNF content in the mix formulation.

Food-grade sucrose (‘Madhur’ brand) was sourced from the local market. Commercially available coconut milk (Urban Platter®) was used as the plant-based beverage. A stabilizer system consisting of sodium alginate (Meron®) and guar gum (Urban Platter®) was employed, while glycerol monostearate (GMS; Foodfrillz®) was used as the emulsifier. Tender coconut flavour (Ossoro®) was added to impart characteristic flavour characteristics and Fast Green FCF (Foodfrillz®) was used as the colouring agent.

Preparation of ice cream mix

The ice cream mix was formulated to achieve a target composition of 10.50% milk fat, 12.00% MSNF, 15.00% sucrose, 0.20% stabilizer (guar gum and sodium alginate in a 1:1 w/w ratio), and 0.15% GMS as emulsifier. The quantities of individual ingredients were calculated using the serum point method to achieve the desired composition in resultant ice creams.

Milk and coconut milk were blended and heated to 45–50 °C to facilitate dissolution and uniform dispersion of dry ingredients. The stabilizer–emulsifier blend was dry-mixed with sucrose in a 1:10 (w/w) ratio, and the mixture was incorporated into the liquid phase when the mix temperature reached about 65 °C. The ice cream mix was subsequently homogenized at this temperature using a two-stage homogenizer operated at pressures of 13.79 MPa and 3.45 MPa for the first and second stages, respectively.

Following homogenization, the mix was pasteurized at 80 °C for 5 min, then rapidly cooled to 4 °C. The cooled mix was aged at 4 ± 2 °C for 12 h. Prior to freezing, tender coconut flavour and food-grade green colour were added to the aged mix at usage levels of 1.80 mL kg−1 mix and 2.00 mL kg−1 mix, respectively, and mixed thoroughly.

The flavoured and coloured ice cream mix was frozen in a direct-expansion type batch freezer (capacity: 4.0 kg mix per batch). The refrigerant temperature during freezing was maintained between − 25 °C and − 30 °C. Freezing was continued until the ammeter reading increased from an initial value of 2.00 to 2.30, indicating sufficient ice formation. Overrun in ice cream was achieved by injecting compressed air at a pressure of 10.0 ± 2.0 psi during the freezing process.

The semi-frozen ice cream was drawn from the freezer at − 5 ± 1 °C, packaged in 90 mL polystyrene cups, and subjected to hardening at − 23 ± 2 °C for 5 h in a blast tunnel hardener. The hardened ice cream samples were finally transferred to a deep-freeze storage cabinet maintained at − 18 ± 2 °C until further analysis.

Physico-chemical and functional analyses

Compositional analysis

The fat content of the ice cream mix was determined using the Rose–Gottlieb extraction method34. Protein content was estimated by the Kjeldahl method34, wherein total nitrogen content was determined and converted to protein using an appropriate nitrogen-to-protein conversion factor. Ash content was obtained following the procedure of AOAC34. The TS content was determined by the oven-drying method34. The pH of the ice cream mix and ice cream samples was measured at ambient temperature using a calibrated digital pH meter (Genie, Bengaluru, India).

Determination of total phenolic content

The TPC of ice cream samples was determined to evaluate their functional and antioxidant potential. Phenolic compounds were extracted from ice cream samples using methanol, followed by quantification using the Folin–Ciocalteu colorimetric method35. Absorbance was measured spectrophotometrically, and the TPC was calculated using a gallic acid calibration curve. Results were expressed as mg of gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per g of ice cream.

Rheological and textural properties

The apparent viscosity of the aged ice cream mix was determined using a Brookfield rotational viscometer (Model DV-II + Pro, Brookfield Engineering Laboratories Inc., Middleboro, MA, USA). Measurements were carried out using an appropriate spindle (e.g., spindle No. 4) at a rotational speed of 60 rpm, and the temperature was maintained at 20 ± 1 °C. Viscosity readings were recorded after the attainment of steady-state torque to ensure measurement stability and reproducibility.

The hardness of ice cream was determined using a cone penetrometer (Model PNR 10, Anton Paar GmbH, Graz, Austria). The depth of penetration of the standardized cone into the ice cream sample was measured and expressed in tenths of a millimetre (1/10 mm).

Melting characteristics

The melting behaviour of ice cream was assessed following the procedure described by Muse and Hartel36.

Overrun determination

Overrun, representing the extent of air incorporated in ice cream during freezing, was calculated using the standard volumetric method described by Marshall et al.37. Overrun was expressed as a percentage increase in volume of ice cream relative to the unfrozen ice cream mix.

Sensory evaluation of ice cream

Sensory evaluation of the ice cream samples was carried out by an expert panel (n = 24) using the modified American Dairy Science Association (ADSA) ice cream score card as described by Marshall et al.37. The total sensory score was 100 points, comprising the following attributes: flavour (45 points), body and texture (30 points), colour and appearance (5 points), melting quality (5 points), and bacterial quality (15 points).

Panelists (12 males and 12 females), aged between 24 and 45 years, were selected from faculty members, postgraduate students, and technical staff who had prior experience in sensory evaluation of dairy products. Selection criteria for expert panel involved their demonstrated sensory acuity, familiarity with ice cream quality attributes, consistency in scoring, and willingness to participate in ice cream judging. Prior to evaluation, panelists were oriented and briefed on the ADSA score card descriptors and evaluation procedure to ensure uniformity and reproducibility of scoring.

Ice cream samples were tempered to a serving temperature of approximately − 10 ± 2 °C before sensory evaluation. A maximum of three samples were presented to each panellist during a single session to minimize sensory fatigue and carry-over effects. Samples were served in identical cups coded with random three-digit numbers and presented to the judges in a randomized order. Panelists were instructed to rinse their mouth with potable water between successive samples.

All sensory evaluations were conducted in a controlled sensory evaluation laboratory under uniform lighting and minimal external distractions. Individual score sheets were used to record panellists responses, and the mean scores for each attribute were used for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

The experiment was conducted using a Completely Randomized Design (CRD) and data generated from the analysis of ice cream mixes and ice cream samples were obtained from duplicate measurements for each experimental unit, with three treatments replicated four times. Mean values derived from these measurements were subjected to statistical analysis.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (Version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to determine the significance of treatment effects. All results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and differences among treatment means were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

The progressive increase in fat content of ice creams with increasing proportion of coconut milk in the ‘milk blend’, T3 (20.85%) > T2 (19.12%) > T1 (17.26%), can be attributed to the higher fat content of coconut milk (≈17.00%) compared with standardized mixed milk (4.50%). Coconut milk fat is rich in medium-chain saturated fatty acids, which contribute substantially to the total lipid fraction even at moderate inclusion levels. Consequently, although the ice cream mix was standardized to 10.50% milk fat, the addition of coconut milk resulted in a higher overall fat content in the finished product19,21. This outcome underscores the importance of considering both dairy and non-dairy fat contributions when formulating hybrid ice cream formulations.

From a functional standpoint, the increased fat content noted in coconut milk-rich formulations is expected to influence the microstructural characteristics of ice cream. Higher fat levels promote increased fat globule collision and partial coalescence of fat during freezing, leading to the formation of a more continuous fat network3,12. Such structural modifications are known to enhance creaminess, lubrication, and melting resistance that are desirable in ice cream products3,12.

A trend similar to that noted for fat content was evident for TS content too. The TS of ice creams increased significantly (p < 0.05) with increasing proportion of coconut milk in the formulation i.e. T3 (51.04%) > T2 (49.02%) > T1 (46.83%). The increase in TS of such ice creams can be attributed to the higher lipid content of coconut milk and the presence of coconut-derived non-fat solids. Increased TS levels reduce the proportion of free water in the ice cream matrix, leading to greater freezing point depression with consequent formation of smaller ice crystals during freezing. This phenomenon contributes to improved body, texture, and thermal stability of ice cream4,11.

In contrast, protein content of ice creams exhibited only a slight increase with increasing coconut milk inclusion. This stability in protein content reflects deliberate formulation adjustments made to maintain a constant MSNF level (12.00%), achieved by increases in SMP and corresponding reductions in the use of mixed milk. From a technological perspective, maintaining a relatively constant protein level is advantageous, as milk proteins play a critical role in emulsification, air cell stabilization, and water binding in ice cream systems. Milk proteins adsorb at the oil–water interface of fat globules during homogenization, forming a stable protein layer that enhances emulsion stability and promotes desirable fat destabilization during freezing, which contributes to desired texture and structural stability. The milk proteins also participate in foam formation and stabilization by aiding the formation of smaller, stable air cells, which are essential for smooth body and texture38. Furthermore, protein-based networks improve water binding and gelation behavior, which influences mix viscosity and melting resistance of ice cream. The absence of marked variation in protein content among treatments suggests that the observed differences in physical and sensory properties were primarily driven by changes in fat and TS rather than by alterations in protein content2,39.

The compositional parameters of ice creams observed in the present research are in close agreement with earlier findings on coconut milk-based ice creams28,32,33. Similarly, the TS content fall within the range observed for coconut milk-based ice cream formulations as reported by Aboulfazli et al.27 and Perera and Perera32.

pH of ice cream

The pH of the ice creams varied within a narrow range (non-significant), from 6.46 to 6.54 (Table 1). This indicates that varying the proportion of milk and coconut milk in the formulation did not markedly alter the acid–base balance of the ice cream system.

The pH values obtained in the present investigation are in close agreement with earlier reports on coconut milk-based ice cream systems. Perera and Perera32 reported a pH of 6.33 for coconut milk-based spiced soft-serve ice cream, which closely aligns with the values observed in this investigation. Maintenance of pH within a narrow near-neutral range is technologically advantageous in frozen dairy and dairy–plant hybrid products, as it supports protein and emulsion stability and helps preserve flavour balance during processing and frozen storage. At pH values close to neutrality, proteins are sufficiently charged to maintain electrostatic repulsion and reduce aggregation, which enhances interfacial stability and minimizes protein destabilization during processing. These effects contribute to uniform droplet dispersion, reduced phase separation, and sustained emulsion stability, while avoiding protein coagulation or phase separation under acidic conditions32.

Viscosity of mix and hardness of ice cream

The apparent viscosity of the aged ice cream mixes was significantly (p < 0.05) influenced by the proportion of coconut milk incorporated into the ‘milk blend’. A clear and progressive increase in mix viscosity was observed as the proportion of coconut milk in the milk blend increased, with the mixes arranged in decreasing order of viscosity as follows: T3 > T2 > T1 (Table 2). The viscosity values ranged from 125.74 to 141.21 cP, and each treatment differed significantly (p < 0.05) from each other, indicating a formulation-dependent effect.

The observed increase in the mix viscosity with higher inclusion levels of coconut milk in the milk blend can be mechanistically attributed to the compositional and physicochemical characteristics of coconut milk fat. Coconut milk and coconut oil are rich in saturated triglycerides, particularly medium-chain components such as lauric and myristic acids, which contribute to a high proportion of solid fat at typical mix ageing and freezing temperatures. Saturated fats with higher solid fat content (SFC) promote enhanced fat crystallization and stronger fat–fat interactions, which contribute to the development of a more continuous and rigid fat network within the mix28. Higher solid fat fractions increase resistance to flow, thereby elevating apparent viscosity of pertinent mixes. Furthermore, increased TS associated with greater proportion of coconut milk inclusion in the milk blend contributed to serum phase thickening and improved stabilizer hydration, both of which led to further increase in the mix viscosity. The influence of fat composition, particularly saturated fatty acid content and the consequent fat crystallization behaviour, on the rheological and stability characteristics of ice cream has been demonstrated,higher saturated fat levels correlating with enhanced ice cream structure formation and resistance to deformation7,14.

From a functional standpoint, increased mix viscosity is a desirable attribute in ice cream system, as it is closely associated with improved air cell stability, reduced ice crystal mobility, and positive influence on the body and texture of the final product. Higher mix viscosity facilitates the formation of a more cohesive fat–protein network during freezing, which improves structural integrity and creaminess of ice cream40. However, excessive viscosity may restrict air incorporation, thereby influencing overrun, a phenomenon observed in coconut milk-rich formulations28.

The observed trends are consistent with earlier reports on coconut milk-based ice cream systems. Surapat and Rugthavon31 reported higher viscosity in reduced-fat coconut milk ice cream mixes containing starch-based fat replacers, attributing the effect to increased water binding. Similarly, Aboulfazli et al.27 reported markedly higher apparent viscosity of coconut milk-based ice cream mixes compared with conventional cow milk-based mixes, underscoring the dominant role of coconut-derived lipids and solids in governing the rheological behaviour of such ice cream system.

An increase in mix viscosity with higher inclusion levels of coconut milk in the milk blend reflected in corresponding differences in the hardness of the resultant ice creams. Ice creams formulated with greater proportions of coconut milk in the milk blend exhibited significantly (p < 0.05) greater hardness, as indicated by lower penetration depth (DP) values of penetrometer (Table 2). This relationship is primarily attributable to the formation of a more continuous and rigid fat crystal network within the ice cream matrix as a result of higher SFC of coconut fat at frozen storage temperatures. Due to its predominantly saturated fatty acid profile, coconut fat crystallizes more readily than milk fat, thereby contributing to enhanced structural stability of ice cream and its resistance to deformation14.

Penetrometer measurements corroborated the observed textural trends noted in ice creams, as the depth of penetration (DP), an inverse indicator of ice cream hardness, showed a significant (p < 0.05) increase with decreasing proportion of coconut milk utilized in the ‘milk blend’. The DP values (expressed as 1/10th mm) exhibited an increase in value, ranked in following order T3 (184.38) < T2 (187.80) < T1 (190.47) (Table 2), indicating that ice cream T3, formulated with the highest proportion of coconut milk, exhibited the greatest hardness, whereas T1 was comparatively softer. The statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) among all treatments underscore the pronounced influence of coconut milk proportion used in the milk blend on the textural firmness of the resultant ice creams.

The increased hardness associated with coconut milk-rich formulations can be attributed to the combined effects of higher total fat content, enhanced fat crystallinity, elevated TS, and reduced overrun. Coconut fat, characterized by a high proportion of saturated fatty acids, contributes to a greater solid fat fraction at frozen storage temperatures, thereby promoting the formation of a more continuous and dense fat crystal network14,15. Concurrently, higher mix viscosity limits air incorporation during freezing, resulting in a denser product with a lower volume fraction of air. This reduction in overrun, together with the development of a stronger fat–protein matrix, enhances structural compactness and resistance to penetration, ultimately manifesting as increased hardness and improved body in the finished ice cream14,15.

Fuangpaiboon and Kijroongrojana8 reported increased hardness in low-glycemic-index ice cream formulated with 42.10% coconut milk, attributing this effect to higher fat content and reduced overrun.

Melting rate

The melting rate of ice cream represents the quantity of frozen product that melts and drains through a wire mesh under ambient conditions over a fixed time period and is widely used as an indicator of structural stability and resistance of the product to temperature fluctuations. The melting rate of ice creams was significantly (p < 0.05) affected by the proportion of coconut milk incorporated into the ‘milk blend’ used in the mix formulation (Table 2).

A clear and progressive reduction in melting rate was observed with increasing levels of coconut milk used in the milk blend, indicating enhanced melting resistance of the ice creams. All three treatments differed significantly (p < 0.05) from one another, with the melting rate following the order T1 > T2 > T3. The superior melting resistance exhibited by ice cream T3, which contained the highest proportion of coconut milk, reflects improved structural integrity and resistance of the frozen product to collapse during melting.

The enhanced melting resistance associated with coconut milk-rich formulations can be attributed to several interrelated factors. Higher proportions of coconut milk contributed to increased total fat and TS content, resulting in elevated mix viscosity and a more compact internal structure14,15. Coconut fat, characterized by a high proportion of saturated fatty acids, exhibits a greater solid fat fraction at frozen and near-frozen temperatures, which promotes the formation of a stronger fat crystal network. This network effectively stabilizes air cells and restricts the mobility of the unfrozen serum phase, thereby slowing melt-down of ice cream. Additionally, reduced overrun in coconut milk-rich formulations led to a denser ice cream matrix with lower air content, further enhancing resistance of ice cream to melting14,15.

The findings of the present investigation are consistent with earlier reports on coconut milk-based ice cream systems. Sigdel33 reported a melting rate of 0.54 g over 5 min for ice cream prepared using a 1:1 blend of milk and coconut milk, indicating moderate melting resistance comparable to the intermediate treatment in the present study. Aboulfazli et al.27 noted a lower melting rate in ice cream formulated solely utilizing coconut milk compared with conventional cow milk ice cream. They reported that use of a 1:1 blend of coconut milk and soy milk yielded maximum melting stability. Similarly, Afriana and Kurniya30 demonstrated enhanced melting resistance in vanilla ice cream prepared using 6:4 (w/w) blend of coconut milk and cashew milk relative to a 1:1 blend of the same plant-based beverages used in ice cream mix formulation.

Overrun

Overrun, which reflects the extent of air incorporation during freezing, is a critical quality parameter influencing the texture, density, melting behaviour, and overall sensory acceptability of ice cream. In the present investigation, the overrun of ice creams was significantly (p < 0.05) affected by the proportion of coconut milk incorporated into the ‘milk blend’ used in the mix formulation (Table 2).

A statistically significant (p < 0.05) reduction in the overrun of ice creams was observed with increasing usage levels of coconut milk in the mix formulation. The overrun values, expressed in per cent, was in decreasing order as follows: T1 (87.78%) > T2 (80.94%) > T3 (76.02%), with each treatment differing significantly (p < 0.05) from one another. This trend clearly indicated that higher inclusion levels of coconut milk in the milk blend progressively limited air incorporation during dynamic freezing.

The reduction in overrun of ice creams associated with coconut milk-rich formulations can be mechanistically attributed to the increased viscosity of such ice cream mixes, arising from higher total fat and TS contents and the distinct physicochemical characteristics of coconut fat14,15. Increased mix viscosity restricts bubble formation and air diffusion, thereby hindering the entrapment and stabilization of air cells during freezing. Furthermore, the formation of a denser fat–protein network in mixes containing higher proportions of coconut milk further limited the capacity of the system to incorporate air, resulting in lower overrun values14,15.

Despite the significant (p < 0.05) decline in overrun, the minimum overrun recorded (76.02%) in ice cream exceeded 75.0%, which is considered quite satisfactory for ice cream14,15. Furthermore, relatively lower overrun is often desirable in high fat ice creams, as it contributes to a denser structure, enhanced creaminess, improved mouthfeel, and superior melting resistance. Therefore, the reduction in overrun in ice cream observed with increasing usage levels of coconut milk in the milk blend can be regarded as a favourable quality attribute for this type of frozen product, rather than a limitation.

The overrun values obtained in the present study compared favourably well with those reported in the literature for coconut milk-based frozen desserts. Goral et al.29 reported relatively low overrun values (10.92–15.31%) in coconut milk-based ice creams containing increasing levels of inulin in the formulation. Iguttia et al.26 recorded a low overrun of 20.00% in a lactose-free frozen dessert containing 41.00% green coconut pulp, highlighting the inherent difficulty of air incorporation in coconut-rich systems. Similarly, Sigdel33 reported an overrun of 48.30% in coconut ice cream formulated by replacing 50.0% of dairy milk with coconut milk. Compared to these studies, the substantially higher overrun values achieved in the present investigation demonstrate that the optimized formulation and processing conditions were effective in achieving satisfactory aeration even at elevated coconut milk inclusion levels.

Total phenolic content

TPC is widely recognized as an important indicator of the bio-functional and antioxidant potential of food products containing plant-derived ingredients. In the present study, the TPC of ice creams was significantly (p < 0.05) influenced by the proportion of coconut milk incorporated into the ‘milk blend’ used in the mix formulation (Table 2).

A clear and progressive increase in TPC of ice creams was observed as the level of coconut milk became predominant in the mix formulation. Each ice cream treatment differed significantly (p < 0.05) from one another, with TPC values (expressed as mg GAE/g product) decreasing in the order: T3 (0.31) > T2 (0.25) > T1 (0.20). This trend clearly demonstrated that coconut milk served as the primary contributor of phenolic compounds to the ice cream system and increasing its proportion in the mix formulation directly enhanced the phenolic content of the final product.

The increase in TPC of ice creams when utilizing higher usage levels of coconut milk can be attributed to the presence of naturally occurring phenolic compounds in coconut milk, such as gallic acid, caffeic acid, and catechins19. These compounds under the thermal conditions typically employed during ice cream mix pasteurization and freezing, permits their partial retention in the finished product19.

The TPC values obtained in the present study are consistent with previously reported values for coconut milk and coconut milk-based frozen desserts. Tulashie et al.17 reported TPC values ranging from 0.31 mg GAE/g in coconut milk obtained from West African tall-type coconuts, highlighting coconut milk as a rich source of phenolic compounds. Perera and Perera32 reported a TPC of 0.093 mg GAE/g in coconut milk-based spiced soft-serve ice cream (11.66% fat, 38.02% TS) formulated exclusively utilizing coconut milk. Such value reported by them is lower than the values obtained in the present study, possibly due to differences in formulation, processing conditions, and phenolic retention. In contrast, Beegum et al.28 reported substantially higher TPC value (38.0 mg GAE/g) in coconut ice cream prepared using multiple coconut derivatives, including coconut milk, coconut water, tender coconut pulp, and coconut sugar. This indicated that combined usage of multiple coconut fractions can markedly enhance the phenolic content of the resultant frozen dessert.

Colour and appearance

As shown in Table 3, all the three ice creams received statistically comparable scores for colour and appearance, with no significant differences observed among treatments. Such comparable colour and appearance scores of ice creams can be attributed to the standardized addition of tender coconut flavour and green colour across all formulations, which ensured consistent visual appearance, irrespective of the proportion of coconut milk used in the milk blend.

The consistently high colour and appearance scores of the ice creams across treatments further indicate good dispersion of colour, absence of visible defects such as coarseness (due to ice crystals) or fat separation, and effective control of processing conditions during freezing and hardening. Maintaining uniform appearance is particularly important for ice cream products, where appearance strongly influences the initial consumer acceptance of the product.

Flavour

The flavour scores of the ice creams were significantly (p < 0.05) influenced by the proportion of milk and coconut milk used in the mix formulation (Table 3), indicating that coconut milk usage level played a decisive role in contributing to flavour perception and overall palatability. All the three ice creams differed significantly (p < 0.05) from one another with respect to flavour score.

Ice cream T1, formulated with the least proportion of coconut milk in the ‘milk blend’, developed a mild yet discernible coconut flavour that was perceived as well balanced and pleasant by the panelists. The subtle coconut notes complemented the inherent dairy flavour without masking it, resulting in a flavour score of 37.63 out of 45.00 in ice cream T1 (Table 3). This suggests that lower levels of coconut milk contribute to aromatic complexity while preserving the characteristic creamy dairy flavour profile in such ice cream.

In contrast, ice cream T2, prepared using an equal proportion of milk and coconut milk (1:1, w/w) was associated with the highest flavour score (42.21 out of 45.00) among all treatments. The balanced formulation provided optimal flavour synergy, wherein coconut-derived aroma compounds were present at levels sufficient to impart a distinct coconut character while remaining harmoniously integrated with the creamy dairy base. The enhanced creaminess associated with this ice cream formulation likely facilitated better flavour release and mouth-coating, thereby intensifying the overall flavour perception of the product.

Further increasing the proportion of coconut milk in ice cream T3 (milk: coconut milk, 2:3 w/w) resulted in an intense coconut flavour, which was perceived as dominant and less harmonious by the panelists. The excessive coconut notes overshadowed the subtle dairy flavour, leading to a markedly reduced flavour score of 33.21 out of 45.00 (Table 3). This outcome highlights the sensory threshold beyond which coconut flavour intensity may become excessive, thereby impairing the overall flavour acceptability of the product.

The present findings indicate that incorporation of coconut milk up to T2 level (1:1 milk: coconut milk, w/w) offers superior flavour compatibility by achieving an optimal balance between coconut aroma and dairy flavour notes. Beyond this level, the dominance of coconut flavour adversely affected the flavour harmony and consumer acceptability. These findings underscore the importance of optimizing coconut milk proportion in conjunction with dairy milk to achieve desirable flavour profiles in hybrid dairy–plant ice cream formulations.

Body and texture

The body and texture scores of the ice creams were significantly (p < 0.05) influenced by the proportion of coconut milk incorporated into the ‘milk blend’ used in the mix formulation (Table 3). A progressive decline in the body and texture scores was noted with increasing levels of coconut milk used in the milk blend, indicating that coconut milk proportion played a critical role in determining the structural and mouthfeel attributes of the ice creams.

The body and texture scores (out of 30.0) decreased in the order T1 (28.09) > T2 (27.66) > T3 (25.71). Statistical analysis revealed that ice creams T1 and T2 exhibited comparable body and texture scores; such scores were significantly (p < 0.05) superior than the score associated with ice cream T3. This suggests that moderate incorporation of coconut milk (up to T2 level) did not adversely affect the structural aspects, whereas higher inclusion levels led to perceptible textural deterioration in ice cream.

The impaired body and texture observed in ice cream T3, which utilized the highest proportion of coconut milk in the milk blend, can be attributed to the comparatively lower protein content in coconut milk relative to dairy milk solids-not-fat (MSNF). Milk proteins, especially caseins and whey proteins, play a pivotal role in binding water, stabilizing air cells and forming a cohesive protein–fat network that imparts smoothness and structural strength to ice cream. Partial replacement of dairy milk with coconut milk in the mix formulation reduces the availability of proteins, thereby weakening the structural matrix28.

Furthermore, ice creams containing higher proportions of coconut milk exhibited a softer and slightly coarser texture, characterized by reduced smoothness and eliciting moderate creaminess. Such textural perception of ice creams can be linked to differences in the fat crystallization behaviour and emulsification properties between coconut fat and milk fat, as well as reduced protein-mediated stabilization of the ice cream structure in case of mixes possessing greater proportion of coconut milk in its formulation. Although increased fat content generally enhances creaminess, excessive replacement of dairy milk with coconut milk appears to compromise the balance between fat and protein necessary for attaining the optimal body and texture in ice cream15.

Melting quality

The melting quality scores of the ice creams were significantly (p < 0.05) influenced by the proportion of coconut milk incorporated into the ‘milk blend’ used in the mix formulation (Table 3). An improvement in the melting quality of ice creams was noted with increasing usage levels of coconut milk in the milk blend, as reflected by the higher sensory scores for resistance to melting and shape retention.

The melting quality scores (out of 5.00) decreased in the order T3 (4.44) > T2 (4.39) > T1 (4.11). Ice cream T1, containing the least proportion of coconut milk in the milk blend, exhibited a significantly (p < 0.05) lower melting quality score compared to ice creams T2 and T3; the latter two treatments were statistically at par with each other in this regard (Table 3). These results indicate that incorporation of coconut milk beyond a threshold level markedly enhances the melting behaviour, whereas further increase in coconut milk proportion leads to only marginal sensory score gain in this regard.

The observed differences in the melting quality score can be mechanistically attributed to variations in fat content, TS content, and fat crystallization behaviour of the ice cream mixes (Table 1). Utilization of higher proportion of coconut milk in the milk blend tended to increase both the fat and TS contents of ice creams, which are known to reduce the proportion of free water in the ice cream matrix and slows down the structural collapse during melting15. In addition, the combined milk fat–coconut fat system likely altered the crystallization dynamics of the fat phase, thereby strengthening the internal fat network and stabilizing the air cells during thermal changes15.

Thermodynamic and structural studies have demonstrated that higher concentrations of saturated fatty acids promote fat crystallization and increases the SFC at sub-zero temperatures, resulting in improved melting resistance and shape retention of ice creams14,41. Coconut fat, which contains ≥ 90.0% saturated fatty acids, with a substantial proportion (65.0–75.0%) of medium-chain fatty acids22, exhibits a relatively high solid fat fraction at freezing and near-freezing temperatures. Incorporation of coconut fat into the ice cream mix therefore contributes to the formation of a more rigid and continuous fat crystal network, enhancing the emulsion stability and the resistance of ice cream to meltdown.

From a sensory standpoint, improved melting quality is perceived as slower dripping, better shape retention, and smoother melt-down behaviour, all of which are highly desirable attributes in ice cream products. The comparable melting quality scores of ice creams T2 and T3 suggest that the optimal balance between improved melting resistance and overall sensory acceptability is achieved at moderate to high coconut milk inclusion levels in the milk blend, without causing any excessive rigidity or waxy mouthfeel.

Total sensory score

The total sensory scores (out of 100.00) of the ice creams differed significantly (p < 0.05) among treatments and exhibited scores shown in descending order as follows: T2 (93.60) > T1 (89.19) > T3 (82.61) (Table 3). Each formulation differed markedly from one another, indicating that variation in the proportion of milk and coconut milk in the milk blend exerted a pronounced influence on the total sensory acceptability score.

Ice cream T2, formulated with an equal proportion of milk and coconut milk (1:1, w/w), was associated with the highest total sensory score. This superior performance can be primarily attributed to its highest flavour score, which carries the greatest weightage (45.0 score) in the ADSA score card. In addition to flavour score, ice cream T2 also exhibited favourable body and texture, melting quality as well as appearance scores, reflecting a well-balanced sensory profile. The synergistic interaction between dairy-derived creaminess and coconut-derived flavour notes in T2 resulted in optimal sensory harmony, thereby maximizing its total sensory acceptability.

Ice cream T1, prepared from mix involving use of least proportion of coconut milk in the milk blend, was awarded an intermediate total sensory score. While its body and texture scores was relatively high, the comparatively milder coconut flavour limited its overall sensory appeal relative to T2. Conversely, ice cream T3, formulated with the highest proportion of coconut milk in the milk blend, received the least total sensory score despite exhibiting superior melting quality (Table 3). The reduced sensory acceptability of ice cream T3 can be attributed to excessive coconut flavour intensity and slightly impaired body and texture, which collectively overshadowed its favourable melting behaviour. This outcome underscores the importance of balancing functional improvements with sensory preference, as excessive incorporation of coconut milk may compromise flavour harmony and textural smoothness.

The sensory scores of ice creams observed in the present study are consistent with earlier reports on coconut milk-based ice creams. Beegum et al.28 reported favourable sensory scores for attributes, particularly flavour and taste, in coconut milk-based ice cream formulated using multiple tender coconut components. Aboulfazli et al.27 reported an overall sensory score of 12.96 out of 20.00 for coconut milk-based ice cream containing 10.50% fat and 14.00% MSNF, while Goral et al.29 reported full score (i.e. 5.00 out of 5.00) for coconut milk-based ice cream containing high fat (19.91%) and TS (46.89%). These studies collectively indicate that coconut milk can contribute positively to the sensory quality of resultant ice cream, when incorporated at appropriate levels and balanced with other formulation components.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that blending of dairy milk with coconut milk in optimal proportion is an effective approach for developing a value-added tender coconut ice cream with enhanced functional and sensory attributes. Among the formulations evaluated, a milk: coconut milk ratio of 1:1 (w/w) emerged as the optimal blend, achieving a desirable balance between creaminess and coconut flavour in resultant ice cream, while maintaining favourable physicochemical properties, functional trait and sensory attributes. Premium Ice cream produced using the optimized milk blend had desired compositional profile (~ 19.0% total fat, 4.30% protein, 49.02% TS), with a substantial portion of coconut-derived lipids. This formulation also demonstrated enhanced bio-functional properties, as reflected by the TPC content, attributable to the phenolic constituents sourced from coconut milk. From a technological perspective, increasing the proportion of coconut milk in the milk blend improved key physicochemical properties, including mix viscosity, ice cream hardness, and melting resistance, primarily due to the higher SFC and altered fat crystallization behaviour. Sensory evaluation further confirmed the superiority of product made utilizing the optimized formulation, which enabled attaining superior flavour acceptability (93.8% of maximum score) and the highest total sensory score (93.60 out of 100.0), reflecting optimal flavour harmony, desirable body and texture, and improved melting quality. In contrast, formulations containing lower or higher proportions of coconut milk in the milk blend exhibited either subdued coconut flavour or excessive coconut flavour intensity accompanied by compromised textural attributes, underscoring the importance of blend optimization. Overall, the findings establish that a 1:1 dairy milk–coconut milk blend, supplemented with tender coconut flavour and green colour, enables the production of a highly acceptable hybrid premium-grade ice cream. Moreover, this study provides an optimized mix formulation for the development of hybrid dairy–plant ice cream, offering opportunities for product diversification, value addition to coconut products, and expansion of ice cream portfolios in both artisanal and industrial sectors. Future studies should investigate the microstructural characteristics, fatty acid profile, antioxidant activity, and storage stability of dairy–coconut milk ice cream to better understand the long-term quality and functionality of such unique hybrid formulations.
