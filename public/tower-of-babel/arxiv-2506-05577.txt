Collaborative Learning in Agentic Systems: A Collective AI is Greater Than the Sum of Its Parts
Saptarshi Nath, Christos Peridis, Eseoghene Benjamin, Xinran Liu, Soheil Kolouri, Peter Kinnell, Zexin Li, Cong Liu, Shirin Dora, Andrea Soltoggio

Abstract
Agentic AI has gained significant interest as a research paradigm focused on autonomy, self-directed learning, and long-term reliability of decision making. Real-world agentic systems operate in decentralized settings on a large set of tasks or data distributions with constraints such as limited bandwidth, asynchronous execution, and the absence of a centralized model or even common objectives. We posit that exploiting previously learned skills, task similarities, and communication capabilities in a collective of agentic AI are challenging but essential elements to enabling scalability, open-endedness, and beneficial collaborative learning dynamics. In this paper, we introduce Modular Sharing and Composition in Collective Learning (MOSAIC) , an agentic algorithm that allows multiple agents to independently solve different tasks while also identifying, sharing, and reusing useful machine-learned knowledge, without coordination, synchronization, or centralized control. MOSAIC combines three mechanisms: (1) modular policy composition via neural network masks, (2) cosine similarity estimation using Wasserstein embeddings for knowledge selection, and (3) asynchronous communication and policy integration. Results on a set of RL benchmarks show that MOSAIC has a greater sample efficiency than isolated learners, i.e., it learns significantly faster, and in some cases, finds solutions to tasks that cannot be solved by isolated learners. The collaborative learning and sharing dynamics are also observed to result in the emergence of ideal curricula of tasks, from easy to hard. These findings support the case for collaborative learning in agentic systems to achieve better and continuously evolving performance both at the individual and collective levels.



1
Introduction

The ability to handle multiple dynamic tasks concurrently and over long-term time windows
De Lange et al. 2022
;
Parisi et al. 2019
is a coveted feature of autonomous, adaptive agents that operate in open-ended environments involving physical-, vision-, and language-based tasks. Dynamic environments demand resilient agents that continuously learn useful behaviors from local data, generalize across diverse tasks, and effectively share useful knowledge
(
Tarale et al. 2025
)
. Such requirements are central to the emerging vision of agentic AI systems, which emphasize self-directed learning, adaptation, asynchronous operation, and scalable autonomy
(
Colas et al. 2022
;
Forestier et al. 2022
)
. Recent work on collaborative and collective AI further highlights the importance of decentralized learning, peer-to-peer sharing, and modular reuse for building scalable societies of AI agents
(
Soltoggio et al. 2024
)
.

In such scenarios, reinforcement learning (RL) signals that can be sourced from the environment allow for policy optimization without external supervision, making RL an effective tool used both in robotics and language models
(
Sutton and Barto 2018
;
Jaderberg et al. 2017
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Sun 2023
)
. Crucially, knowledge reuse has been a central assumption in multi-task (MTRL) and meta reinforcement learning (Meta-RL)
(
Hospedales et al. 2021
)
, in which learning of many tasks is facilitated by exploiting similarities across tasks. The successful deployment of pre-trained models in LLMs is another example of effective reuse of previously acquired knowledge across tasks
Han et al. 2021
;
Qiu et al. 2020
. Reliability of RL algorithms when dealing with evolving streams of data is improved with lifelong RL approaches
(
Ben-Iwhiwhu et al. 2023
)
.

Most existing MTRL, Meta-RL, and lifelong RL methods assume a centralized setup with shared policy networks and known task labels
(
Wang et al. 2023
;
Sun et al. 2022
;
Hendawy et al. 2024
;
Amani et al. 2023
;
Zeng et al. 2021
;
D’Eramo et al. 2020
)
. These assumptions limit their applicability in fully decentralized or real-world agentic scenarios, where agents typically operate independently, and communication, when possible, is asynchronous and not coordinated. To address these realistic conditions, recent work in lifelong learning and multi-agent systems explored machine-learned knowledge sharing among many agents
(
Nath et al. 2023
;
Rostami et al. 2017
;
Gao et al. 2021
;
Ge et al. 2023
;
Gerstgrasser et al. 2023
)
. Similar objectives are pursued by recent approaches that share and compose LoRA modules in LLMs
(
Huang et al. 2024
;
Roy et al. 2025
)
, demonstrating that this challenge is crucial to scale current AI approaches. A full coverage of the related works in the literature can be found in appendix
A
.

While the idea of sharing and reusing knowledge in agentic machine learning systems is intuitive and appealing, a key algorithmic problem has not yet been well addressed: in a collective of AI agents solving different tasks, what specific knowledge should be shared, with whom, and when?
DARPA/MTO 2021
;
Soltoggio et al. 2024
Moreover, does a decentralized collective of agents have an advantage over isolated agents learning the same tasks?

To address these unanswered questions, this study proposes a new algorithm,
Modular Sharing and Composition in Collective Learning (MOSAIC)
, that describes what policies to exchange among peers, specifically among which agents, when to do so, and how such policies can be integrated. Extending and integrating recent advances in lifelong machine learning, the approach employs modular knowledge representations in the form of network masks and proposes a knowledge exchange mechanism based on cosine similarity computed with Wasserstein task embeddings. Performance measures and similarities are used by individual agents to determine specifically what knowledge to retrieve, from whom, and when.

The experimental evidence and the ablation studies show that precise selection of specific knowledge is essential to exploit the advantages of sharing. This evidence indicates that not all knowledge is beneficial to all agents, and that modular composition and cosine similarity metrics are essential ingredients in a collective of collaborating agents. MOSAIC agents learn faster than isolated agents by selectively leveraging the available knowledge from peers working on similar tasks. In some cases, by combining knowledge retrieved from other agents working on similar tasks, MOSAIC agents are able to solve tasks that are too difficult for isolated learners. Further analysis reveals that the collective implicitly discovers an ideal curriculum in which agents working on simpler tasks subsequently help agents working on harder tasks. The MOSAIC learning dynamics indicate that a collective of collaborative MOSAIC agents can achieve superior performance than that of an equivalent number of isolated learners, making a strong case for targeted knowledge sharing in agentic systems.

1.1
Contributions

This work introduces MOSAIC, an agentic system, i.e., an autonomous decision-making and self-contained individual agent, that implements autonomous and self-directed knowledge exchange with other MOSAIC peers. MOSAIC is a novel integration of the following algorithmic steps: (i) modularized knowledge learning and representation; (ii) online Wasserstein task embeddings computed from reinforcement learning datasets; (iii) knowledge querying and retrieval with cosine similarity (between embeddings) and performance-based selection heuristics.

The performance evaluations reveal significant learning advantages for a collective of collaborating agents with respect to isolated learners, which, in some cases, results in successful policies that cannot be discovered in isolation.

Lastly, the analysis of learning dynamics revealed that agents choose what to learn in ways that lead to the emergence of implicit curricula, task clusters, and task hierarchies. This property enables an open-ended training behavior in which agents autonomously determine their learning trajectories by reusing specific subsets from the available collective knowledge.

Policy Representations

The ability to encapsulate specific knowledge in isolated module representations

)

Preliminaries.

Each task
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
is modeled as an MDP defined by the tuple
\mathcal{M}=\left\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{R},\gamma\right\rangle
, where
\mathcal{S}
is the state space,
\mathcal{A}
is the action space,
\mathcal{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)
is the transition probability function,
\mathcal{R}(s,a)\in\mathbb{R}
is the reward received after taking action
a
in the state
s
, and
\gamma\in[0,1]
is the discount factor. An agent interacts with its environment and aims to learn a policy
\pi_{\tau}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{A}
that maximizes the expected discounted return,

J_{\tau}(\pi_{\tau})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\tau}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}_{\tau}\mathcal{R}_{\tau}(s_{t},a_{t})\right].

(1)

Mask Representations.

The mask representation used in this study relies on a frozen backbone network
\Phi
that must be the same for all agents. Each policy
\pi_{\tau}
is implicitly parameterized through a sparse mask
\phi_{\tau}
, such that
\pi_{\tau}=\pi_{\Phi\odot g(\phi_{\tau})}
, where
\odot
denotes the Hadamard product, and
g(\cdot)
is a binarization function applied during the forward pass. The mask
\phi_{\tau}
consists of a set of real-valued score vectors, one per layer of the shared backbone network
\Phi
, which select a sparse, task-specific subnetwork from
\Phi
without modifying its parameters. The scores are binarized during the forward pass to produce a hard mask that is used for inference. The binarization is performed during inference, using the element-wise thresholding function,

g(\phi_{\tau})_{\ell}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }(\phi_{\tau})_{\ell}>\epsilon\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},\quad\text{with }\epsilon=0\quad,

(2)

where
g(\phi_{\tau})
denotes the full binary mask and
\ell
indexes the layers of the backbone network
\Phi
. During the backward pass, gradients are computed with respect to the real-valued mask parameters
\phi_{\tau}
, and updates are performed using the straight-through estimator (STE)
(
Wortsman et al. 2020
)
to enable learning through the non-differentiable binarization step.

2.2
Wasserstein Task Embeddings for Online Reinforcement Learning

A key intuition in this study is that knowledge composition is effective if policies from other agents are acquired only when they are likely to be beneficial, and because they have been derived from related tasks. This hypothesis was verified with an ablation study. MOSAIC implements this idea by computing task embeddings from trajectory data and using them to identify semantically related peers via geometric proximity. This idea builds on work in latent task inference and representation learning
(
Zintgraf et al. 2021
;
Achille et al. 2019
)
, adopting a non-parametric approach aligned with prototype-based and optimal transport methods
(
Yarats et al. 2021
;
Chu et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2022
)
.

Unlike gradient-based adaptation techniques
(
Lan et al. 2019
)
, which are unstable in RL and costly at inference time, MOSAIC computes task embeddings through training-free distributional comparisons, making them well-suited for decentralized, real-time systems requiring modular policy reuse without supervision. To support these requirements, MOSAIC extends the Wasserstein Task Embedding (WTE) framework of
Liu et al. 2022
to the RL domain by defining an online embedding function over batches of state-action-reward (SAR) tuples drawn from an agent’s replay buffer. For task
\tau
, the agent maintains an empirical task distribution
\mu_{\tau}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, defined as,

\mu_{\tau}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{t}},\quad\text{where}\quad x_{t}=(s_{t},a_{t},r_{t})\quad\text{and}\quad x_{t}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\tau},

(3)

where
\mathcal{D}_{\tau}
denotes the replay buffer for task
\tau
, and each
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a state-action-reward (SAR) tuple. The tuple dimensionality is given by
d=d_{s}+d_{a}+d_{r}
, where
d_{s}
,
d_{a}
, and
d_{r}
are the dimensions of the state, action, and scalar reward, respectively. In our experiments, we use
N=128
samples to compute the empirical task distribution.
\delta_{x_{t}}
denotes the Dirac measure centered at the sample
x_{t}
. A fixed synthetic reference distribution
\mu_{0}
is defined as,

\mu_{0}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\delta_{x_{m}^{0}},

(4)

where
x_{m}^{0}\sim\text{Uniform}(-1,1)^{d}
is sampled once during initialization and fixed thereafter. We fix
M=50
as the number of reference points in the synthetic distribution. This shared reference is used across all agents and enables them to align their embeddings in a shared latent space without centralized training or supervision. The 2-Wasserstein distance between
\mu_{\tau}
and
\mu_{0}
is computed by solving the optimal transport problem,

\gamma^{*}=\arg\min_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\gamma_{tm}\|x_{t}-x^{0}_{m}\|^{2},\quad\text{s.t. }\gamma_{tm}\geq 0,\gamma\mathbf{1}_{M}=\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{1}_{N},\gamma^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{N}=\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{1}_{M},

(5)

where
\mathbf{1}_{N}
and
\mathbf{1}_{M}
are vectors of ones in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\mathbb{R}^{M}
, respectively. The Wasserstein task embedding operator
\psi
maps the task distribution
\mu_{\tau}
to a vector
v_{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}
via the barycenter projection of
\gamma^{*}
on
\{x_{t}\}_{t=1}^{N}
,

v_{\tau}=\psi(\mu_{\tau})=\left[\sum_{t=1}^{N}\gamma_{tm}^{*}x_{t}\right]_{m=1}^{M}.

(6)

This mapping places each task
\tau
in a shared latent space, where metrics can be used to quantify task relationships. A 2-point moving average is used to smooth behavioral fluctuations and stabilize evolving policies in MOSAIC. This embedding strategy enables agents to reason about task-relatedness in constant time, without additional training or forward passes. Importantly, it fits the online and asynchronous nature of agentic RL systems, while remaining robust to evolving policies and lightweight enough for continuous use in edge-deployed systems.

2.3
Knowledge Selection and Sharing

Agents communicate maintaining a list of IP addresses and ports for other agents, which allows them to be located anywhere on the Internet. Each agent
i\in\mathcal{I}
can directly communicate with all other agents
j\in\mathcal{I},\ j\neq i
at any time.

Phase 1: Query-Metadata.

At any time, an agent
i
can initiate a task embedding query (TEQ) by broadcasting its current task embedding
v_{i}
, iteration performance
r_{i}
, and its IP/port (Figure
1
(B)). Upon receiving the TEQ, each peer agent
j\neq i
responds with its most recent task embedding
v_{j}
and associated performance score
r_{j}
.

Phase 2: Mask Selection and Acquisition.

Once the agent has received query responses (QR) with relevant embeddings from other agents, it computes the cosine similarity between its own embedding and those of each peer,

d_{\text{cos}}(v_{i},v_{j})=\frac{\langle\text{vec}(v_{i}),\text{vec}(v_{j})\rangle}{|\text{vec}(v_{i})|\cdot|\text{vec}(v_{j})|},\quad d_{\text{cos}}\in[-1,1].

(7)

In practice, cosine similarities between Wasserstein task embeddings are typically positive. Cosine similarity was observed to perform better than Euclidean distance in preliminary tests, and showed scale-invariance and empirical robustness in high-dimensional settings. To identify useful policies, agent
i
applies two heuristic filters based on similarity and performance:

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{\text{align}}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }d_{\text{cos}}(v_{i},v_{j})>\theta,\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(Criterion 1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{\text{perf}}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }r_{j}>r_{i},\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(Criterion 2)

with
\theta=
set to
0.5
in all experiments. The first criterion promotes semantic alignment between tasks, revealing latent similarities that may lead to effective transfer (see Figure
4
). The second ensures that agents only acquire masks from peers whose performance exceeds their own, avoiding noisy or under-trained policies that can degrade performance. Criterion 2 guards against the frequent false positives seen when comparing poorly trained policies that yield deceptively similar embeddings. The selection criteria can also be used on the peers after they receive the query. This method acts as a bandwidth-efficient alternative that enables communication without affecting the overall outcome of the approach. Additionally, this method offers a scalable pathway for extensions to lifelong learning, which are discussed in Appendix
B
.

The peer masks that pass both criteria are stored as the set
P_{c+1}=\{\phi_{1},...,\phi_{K}\}
, where each
\phi_{k}
denotes a mask received from a selected peer. This two-phase strategy ensures that agents acquire knowledge that is not only relevant but also demonstrably effective.

2.4
Knowledge Composition

Masks received from other agents meeting the two selection criteria above have been pretrained on tasks that are likely related to, or share similarities with, the agent’s current task. As originally shown in
Ben-Iwhiwhu et al. 2023
, a linear combination of masks, weighted with trainable parameters
\beta
, can lead to beneficial policy search in RL. MOSAIC exploits this idea by combining policies that have been trained on the local agent, plus those received from other agents. In this study, the
\beta
values are computed via softmax over a set of beta parameters optimized in log-space,
\tilde{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+|P_{c}|}
, where
P_{c}
is the number of masks received. During the forward pass, the agent constructs the linearly combined, binarized mask,

\phi_{\tau}^{\text{lc}}=g\left(\beta_{\tau}\phi_{\tau}+\sum_{k=1}^{|P_{c+1}|}\beta_{k}\phi_{k}\right),

(8)

where
\phi_{\tau}
is the agent’s own task mask, and
\phi_{k}
is the
k
-th peer mask. The parameters
\beta_{\tau}
and
\beta_{k}
are the softmax-normalized values derived from
\tilde{\beta}
, for
\phi_{\tau}
and
\phi_{k}
, respectively. The resulting binary mask,
\phi_{\tau}^{\text{lc}}
, is then used to modulate the backbone parameters, yielding the final policy
\pi_{\tau}
. Using the STE method, the agent updates the
\beta
parameters and
\phi_{\tau}
via backpropagation, keeping all the
\phi_{k}
masks fixed.

Reward-Guided Initialization.

The trainable
\beta
parameters are ideal to enable gradient descent to discover which policy is most beneficial on the current task. However, given the available information, one question is: What is a good initial weighting for the set of masks that include the local mask and those received from other agents? MOSAIC proposes a reward-guided weighting mechanism to set the initial values. While such values can be later tuned by gradient descent, an ablation study suggested that performing a good initial guess leads to a significant advantage. The intuition is that an agent with low performance may favor external knowledge, while an agent with higher returns could rely more on their own policies. The initial set of
\beta
is given by

\beta_{\tau}=0.5+0.5r,\quad\beta_{k}=\frac{0.5(1-r)}{|P_{c+1}|}\quad,

(9)

where
r\in[0,1]
is the agent’s normalized average return over the most recent iteration and
|P_{c+1}|
is the number of peer masks received during a communication event. This scheme encourages a smooth shift from exploration to self-reliance as learning progresses.

The process just described occurs multiple times, and specifically, each time new masks are received. Therefore, before a new set of peer masks
P_{c+1}
is integrated, the agent performs a consolidation operation that combines its current task mask
\phi_{\tau}
with the set of previous peer masks
P_{c}
. This is done using a weighted linear combination similar to the forward pass. The resulting consolidated mask updates the existing
\phi_{\tau}
,

\phi_{\tau}\leftarrow\beta_{\tau}\phi_{\tau}+\sum_{k=1}^{|P_{c}|}\beta_{k}\phi_{k}\quad.

(10)

The consolidation reduces multiple currently held peer masks to one without affecting the policy, effectively maintaining scalability with respect to memory requirements.

Summary

The complete MOSAIC algorithm executes the processes described above in a loop. Figure

MOSAIC computes embeddings from batches of state-action-reward (SAR) data collected under the current policy, and these embeddings evolve over time as the policy improves. Knowledge reuse is governed by a linear combination of task-specific masks selected using similarity (computed from embeddings) and performance heuristics, with beta parameters re-initialized at each communication event and then fine-tuned to reflect downstream utility to the current task. 3.1
Image Sequence Learning

This benchmark is an RL problem based on the Configurable Tree Graph (CT-Graph benchmark
Soltoggio et al. 2019
), where a set of procedurally generated navigation tasks implement a variable length RL problem, and where each node in a tree-graph is an RL state encoded as image. We use this benchmark to define a curriculum of 28 tasks with navigation paths of increasing depths of the graph (2 to 8) from four independent sets of images. This setting creates four sets of unrelated tasks with seven related tasks in each set. The seven tasks in a set have an increasing depth of the navigation tree, generating a range of RL tasks from easy (depth 2) to very hard (depth 8), effectively creating a hierarchy of task’s complexity or difficulty. The sparse reward and exponential branching of the tree-graph structure make this benchmark a challenging RL setting, with reward probabilities as low as
\approx 7.74\times 10^{-9}
for the most difficult tasks with depth 8
(
Soltoggio et al. 2019
)
.

Results in Figure
2
(Top) show that MOSAIC achieves significantly faster learning and broader task coverage, outperforming MOSAIC-NoComm by 2.7
\times
, reaching a maximum total return of 26.0 across all 28 tasks. MOSAIC reaches 50% performance (14) in 37 iterations (18,944 steps). MOSAIC-NoComm plateaus at a maximum of 9.6. On average, MOSAIC-NoComm fails on 18 tasks, whereas MOSAIC fails on only 2.

3.2
MiniHack MultiRoom

MiniHack defines a grid-based navigation challenge with sparse rewards and pixel-based observations. Agents must navigate rooms and obstacles to reach a goal location. MOSAIC is evaluated on 14 navigation tasks organized into two clusters of increasing difficulty: one with room sizes of
4\times 4
, and another with
6\times 6
. Each level in a cluster adds an additional room, connected by a closed (but unlocked) door. The agent receives a reward of +1 for completing the task and a penalty of -0.01 for colliding with walls. Ablation studies were conducted on the image sequence learning problem (Section

The ablation without cosine similarity-based selection (criterion 1) reaches a total return of 20.1. MOSAIC achieves 26.0, outperforming it by a factor of 1.29. We speculate that this gap could widen further in settings in which the number of unrelated tasks grows, i.e., where selecting the most relevant knowledge becomes critical.

The second ablation study without performance-based selection (criterion 2) performs comparably with MOSAIC at the end of the training (25.6), but it shows significantly slower learning. These dynamics make sense because, as all agents improve their performance on their respective tasks, selecting for high reward becomes less critical. 4
Discussion

The results, while showing different performance advantages across different tasks and curricula, indicate that the principles introduced in MOSAIC are effective in diverse settings. MOSAIC showed significantly faster and more successful learning dynamics with respect to isolated agents working on the same sets of tasks. These observations strongly suggest that knowledge composition enables effective reuse of previously learned policies across tasks and agents, supporting similar observations in a centralized single-agent lifelong learning setting
(
Ben-Iwhiwhu et al. 2023
)
. In particular, masks from some tasks were observed to accelerate learning when selectively transferred to agents dealing with other tasks. We conclude that the selection and compositional mechanisms lead to the discovery of successful policies with a significantly better sample efficiency. We observed the advantages to be particularly significant in sparse-reward and hierarchical reinforcement learning environments (image sequence learning with varying depth), where agents learning difficult tasks benefit from acquiring policies from agents that are learning simpler tasks, supporting findings in
Florensa et al. 2018
;
Nachum et al. 2018
;
Uchendu et al. 2023
.

The ablation studies highlight the importance of the selection criteria–cosine similarity for relevance, reward-based selection for utility–as well as the reward-based weighting to optimize the initial policies. The results of the ablation studies are particularly significant because they suggest that knowledge sharing and integration may not be effective in a vanilla setup where all knowledge is shared
(
Isele and Cosgun 2018
)
. It is conceivable that such criteria are crucial for scalability, for example, when implementing a system of a large number of interconnected agentic AI working on a large set of diverse tasks in a fully asynchronous setting
(
Foerster et al. 2016
)
.

Interestingly, removing the reward-guided initialization of policies introduces periodic instability. Despite achieving a slightly higher final return (26.4 vs. 26.0), this variant suffers sharp drops after communication events, suggesting that newly acquired policies can interfere with learning when not weighted adaptively. It is also conceivable that this setting could become critical for the safe deployment of MOSAIC: new externally acquired policies, even if vetted by criteria 1 and 2 that should ensure relevance and performance, may nevertheless require “careful” introduction in the agent’s own policy
(
Amodei et al. 2016
)
.

MOSAIC currently relies on raw episodic reward as a selection and weighting signal, which may limit generalization across environments with unstandardized or sparse reward functions. Future variants could use reward-normalized scores or task-progress metrics to support more environment-agnostic policy reuse
(
Jaques et al. 2019
)
. Additionally, MOSAIC assumes a fully connected communication topology, which may not scale well in systems with many agents or bandwidth constraints; exploring adaptive or sparse topologies could improve scalability to real-world applications
(
Kaimakamidis et al. 2024
;
Marino et al. 2025
;
Tang et al. 2024
)
. The current design also restricts mask composition to positive, additive combinations, limiting the ability to suppress interference from poorly aligned peer policies. Introducing signed or non-linear composition strategies could address this issue
(
He et al. 2022
)
. Finally, the use of a shared, frozen backbone across agents may constrain representational diversity and reduce adaptability in highly heterogeneous task settings. Allowing for backbone specialization or dynamic composition techniques may improve transfer and robustness in more diverse environments
(
Houlsby et al. 2019
)
.

While this work uses masking as the modular knowledge representation, similar mechanisms based on parameter isolation could be tested. For example, LoRA-like modules or adapters have shown significant promise in transformer architectures and effectively act as pre-trained modules that have been shown to work also with various fusion and combination approaches
(
He et al. 2022
;
Wu et al. 2024
;
Han et al. 2021
;
Qiu et al. 2020
)
. This consideration opens the possibility for compositional reuse in transformer-based models and large-scale language agents.

Communication and selection in MOSAIC were introduced as a proof-of-concept to assess the validity of the hypothesis that knowledge selection (what and when to transfer
(
DARPA/MTO 2021
;
Soltoggio et al. 2024
)
) is a key element in agentic, decentralized, and fully asynchronous systems. To achieve an even higher level of agency in MOSAIC agents, more aspects related to communication could be learned by a policy
(
Foerster et al. 2016
)
. Agents learning and optimizing their communication could lead to more dynamic behaviors that are better suited to each agent’s task(s) and resources. However, learnable and dynamics communication policies could lead to potential safety concerns, as agents that learn to collaborate could learn greedy, or uncooperative policies that may benefit the agent but not the collective as a whole. In the simplest instance, an agent that does not share knowledge exploits the collective knowledge without contributing to it. In a worst-case scenario, malicious agents could share harmful policies. Such an extension of MOSAIC could lead to complex learning and collaboration dynamics described in Game Theory
(
Myerson 2013
)
. In summary, we are aware that the MOSAIC algorithm raises safety considerations
(
Hammond et al. 2025
;
Bengio et al. 2025
)
: without appropriate filters or constraints, agents may transfer misaligned or flawed policies, leading to instability or harmful behavior in sensitive environments. MOSAIC-like systems are also vulnerable to attacks by malicious actors who exploit the peer-to-peer nature, poisoning models, or extracting policies or training data
(
Vassilev et al. 2024
)
.

The knowledge composition mechanism in MOSAIC derives from approaches in lifelong machine learning, where agents encounter a stream of continuously changing tasks. Therefore, the algorithm is particularly suitable to be extended to a lifelong learning setting. In such an extension, each policy is a composition of three sources: a task-specific mask for the current task, prior masks from previously learned tasks, and peer-acquired masks from other agents. Effectively, MOSAIC is ideally designed to reuse prior knowledge from previous tasks when learning a continuous stream of tasks
(
Serra et al. 2018
)
. For these considerations, we hypothesize that the reuse and integration of policies acquired from other agents within a set of own-policies could enable scalable and open-ended lifelong learning
(
Leibo et al. 2019
;
Stanley 2019
)
across diverse task streams.

5
Conclusion

This paper introduced MOSAIC, a collaborative agentic algorithm for policy sharing and composition in reinforcement learning. By exploiting network masks with reward- and cosine similarity-driven selection, MOSAIC enables effective and efficient knowledge sharing, producing a collaborative collective system whose performance is significantly superior than that of isolated agents. The findings presented in this study support the broader hypothesis that effective knowledge transfer and reuse is possible in agentic systems supported by appropriate knowledge selection and integration principles. Interestingly, the proposed algorithm improves learning efficiency and effectiveness by exploiting parallel agentic learning that is shown to result in naturally emergent curricula, where agents learning easy tasks achieve their goals sooner, and subsequently support agents learning harder tasks. The capabilities shown by MOSAIC provide a proof-of-concept for a collaborative and fully autonomous AI collective composed of independent and cooperating agents.

Broader Impacts

MOSAIC introduces a collaborative and autonomous knowledge-sharing algorithm to enable agentic systems to seek and share machine-learned knowledge. Potential positive impacts include advancing autonomous learning in real-world multi-agent settings, such as agentic language models, robotics, disaster response, multi-agent search & rescue robotic teams, satellite swarms, or systems where privacy, data ownership, legislation, or bandwidth constraints preclude centralized coordination. The policy composition approach in MOSAIC promotes interpretability, as task compositions can be inspected and traced back to specific prior knowledge sources. Policy reuse may extend across domains of language, perception, and control, enabling more transparent and collaborative AI systems in human-facing applications.

The autonomous knowledge-sharing capabilities of MOSAIC also pose new risks. Agents could transfer flawed or misaligned policies at a fast rate, effectively spreading potential malicious information across a large network, a process that could be difficult to reverse. Future work should focus on developing mechanisms to ensure policy transfers are safe and aligned with each agent’s objectives.

Appendix B
Extending MOSAIC to Lifelong Reinforcement Learning

MOSAIC can be naturally extended to a lifelong learning (LL) setting
[
De Lange et al. 2022
,
Kudithipudi et al. 2022
]
, in which each agent learns a sequence of tasks while minimizing catastrophic forgetting and promoting forward transfer. LL MOSAIC agents can exploit their knowledge integration capabilities to accumulate knowledge that may derive from other agents, or from their own learning on previously encountered tasks. MOSAIC employs knowledge composition approaches that were developed in the field of lifelong reinforcement learning
[
Ben-Iwhiwhu et al. 2023
]
. In the experimental settings for MOSAIC, however, we employed a one task per agent approach to maintain the focus on knowledge sharing and composition. This setting can be extended to a more general case in which one agent is not limited to learning one task. Therefore, MOSAIC can be easily adapted to a fully-fledged lifelong learning scenario by modifying the linear combination and
\beta
parameter adjustment.

Let agent
i
currently be learning task
\tau_{k}+1
. Its mask is composed from three sources: the current task mask
\phi_{\tau_{k}+1}
, prior task masks
\{\phi_{\tau_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, and peer masks
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
, acquired from other agents. The composed mask for task
\tau_{k+1}
for the forward pass is thus given by,

\phi_{\tau_{k+1}}^{\text{lc}}=\beta_{\tau_{k+1}}\phi_{\tau_{k+1}}+\sum_{k=1}^{k}\beta_{\tau_{k}}\phi_{\tau_{k}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}\phi_{j},

(11)

where each
\beta
is a scalar weight normalized via softmax. In this case, the initialization problem includes setting the weighting for previous task masks, the current task mask, and the newly acquired masks from other agents. One simple approach could simply be to extend Eq.
9
to

\beta_{\tau_{k+1}}=\frac{1}{3}+\frac{2}{3}r,\quad\beta_{\tau_{k}}=\frac{(1-r)}{3k},\quad\beta_{j}=\frac{(1-r)}{3n}.

(12)

The addition of lifelong learning presents an additional challenge. Boundaries in the sequence of tasks learned by an agent need to be identified, so that a new mask can be learned for each new task. One solution to this could be to use the cosine similarity computed from the Wasserstein embeddings to monitor the incoming data stream and decide when a new task starts using statistical methods
[
Dick et al. 2020
,
Dick et al. 2024
]
.

Additional ablation studies
An additional ablation study was conducted to assess the effect of communication frequency on MOSAIC’s performance in the image sequence learning benchmark. Specifically comparing frequencies of 1, 5, 10 (default), 25, and 40 iterations, where the frequency indicates how often agents query and integrate peer masks. As shown in Figure
7
, overly frequent communication (i.e., frequency of 1) results in performance degradation. In this setting, newly acquired peer masks continually override the agent’s own beta parameter adjustments, preventing meaningful adaptation and fine-tuning outside of the performance-based adjustment. Frequencies of 5 and 10 yield comparable early performance, with a frequency of 10 achieving higher final return. Conversely, infrequent communication (25 or 40 iterations) reduces the opportunity for useful knowledge transfer, leading to weaker overall performance. These results highlight the importance of balancing between querying for peer knowledge often enough for a benefit to occur, and allowing agents to then tailor peer masks towards their own tasks.

D.1
Further Analysis of Similarity and Converged Beta Parameters

The image sequence learning benchmark consists of four distinct image datasets, resulting in four independent state spaces. Four unique trajectories are chosen, one for each image dataset. Seven hierarchically related tasks are selected from each trajectory-dataset combination resulting in a total of 28 tasks with four clusters denoted by (0 to 6), (7 to 13), (14 to 20), (21 to 27). Figure
15
shows the annotated results of the heatmaps presented in Figure
4
(D) and (E) for each of these clusters, identified by the WPGMA (Figure
4
(C)). In Figure
16
, each pair of tasks is categorized as "Within Distribution" (e.g., (0-1) and "Across Distributions" (e.g., 0-14).

In the MiniGrid benchmark, tasks are divided into two families: SimpleCrossing (SC; tasks 0–6) and LavaCrossing (LC; tasks 7–13). Each SC task has a corresponding LC task with a matched structural layout but different obstacles (walls versus lava), forming seven pairs of canonical tasks (e.g. 0-7, 1-8) that are structurally matched. Surprisingly, the pairwise cosine similarity heatmap reveals that these canonical pairs do not have the highest similarity (see also Figure
18
). Instead, many within-family pairs show stronger similarity, particularly among SC tasks. Many non-structurally matched pairs also exhibit unexpectedly high similarity. These results suggest that the Wasserstein task embeddings are able to encode non-trivial task relationships which MOSAIC can then leverage for knowledge selection.

The corresponding
\hat{\beta}
matrix indicates that knowledge reuse in MiniGrid is selective and is not determined solely by the canonical structure. Reuse tends to favor structurally simple or early-learned tasks rather than those with the highest embedding similarity. Figure
18
confirms this pattern: canonical pairs show lower average similarity than both within-group and non-structurally-matched pairs, suggesting that MOSAIC’s embedding space captures aspects of task dynamics that go beyond explicit structural symmetry. Reuse decisions are shaped not only by similarity but also by policy quality and learning progress. This pattern may also reflect the temporal bias in reuse. Tasks such as SC0, which reach high performance early in training, become preferred sources of knowledge. Structurally matched tasks that appear later in training are often underutilized, due to the performance-based selection mechanism (criterion 2) that filters knowledge transfer based on relative performance.

The MiniHack benchmark extends the MiniGrid MultiRoom curriculum. It consists of tasks ranging from two to eight rooms in size, with task IDs 0–6 corresponding to room size
4\times 4
and tasks 7–13 to size
6\times 6
. Canonical pairs are defined by room count; for example, task 0 and task 7 are both 2-room versions of different sizes. Cosine similarity analysis reveals clear grouping by room size, with tasks within the same spatial resolution exhibiting stronger similarity than canonical pairs across sizes, indicating that visual and navigational complexity induced by room dimensions plays a more significant role in shaping the embedding space than shared structural depth alone.

The matrix
\hat{\beta}
shows that knowledge reuse in MiniHack occurs predominantly within the same room size group. Smaller tasks, such as the 2-room and 3-room configurations, are reused more frequently and earlier in training, consistent with their faster convergence. As in MiniGrid, structural pairing alone does not guarantee high reuse. Figure
20
shows that canonical pairs (e.g., 0–7, 1–8) have only moderate similarity, while many within-group pairs yield stronger transfer potential. The dominance of reuse within room size groups may also be explained in part by the timing of performance gains. Tasks with smaller rooms converge earlier and thus become eligible for reuse earlier, which reinforces their influence even when structurally similar tasks emerge later in training.

Algorithm
2
describes how the forward pass is computed using a weighted linear combination of the current task mask, peer masks, and the backbone network. Algorithm
5
presents an alternative communication strategy in MOSAIC, where agents perform selection on the server side by returning only the most relevant masks from their knowledge base to querying agents. This alternative approach improves scalability and communication efficiency without changing the way MOSAIC works. This alternative approach is especially useful in the lifelong learning scenario where each agent encounters multiple tasks. By reducing the exchange of irrelevant information, this server-side selection mechanism enables MOSAIC to adapt effectively to lifelong learning, where agents would be expected to learn multiple task-masks. Rather than share all information of all the available knowledge, peer MOSAIC agents would perform selection locally for the querying agents and send the appropriate mask(s). The lifelong learning adaptation of MOSAIC is discussed in Section
B
.
