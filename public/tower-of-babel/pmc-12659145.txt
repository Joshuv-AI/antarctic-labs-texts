Temporal variations and spatial clusters of diarrheal diseases before and during the COVID-19 pandemic in Jakarta province, Indonesia
Authors: Erni Astutik, Zida Husnina, Laura Navika Yamani, Kinley Wangdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiarrheal diseases continue to be an important public health problem in developing countries including Indonesia, possibly made worse by COVID-19 pandemic. Therefore, this study aimed to outline the epidemiology of diarrheal diseases in Jakarta Province in Indonesia.MethodsThe diarrhoeal data from 2018 to 2021 were obtained from the Epidemiological Surveillance Unit, Jakarta Provincial Health Office. Seasonal and trend decomposition using Loess was used to analyse the temporal analysis. The Local Indicators for Spatial Association was used to detect spatial clustering of diarrheal cases at the sub-district level. Multivariable Poisson regression was used to study the association between diarrhoea and climatic factors.ResultsDiarrheal diseases were highly seasonal with cases peaking in the beginning of each year. The diarrheal diseases incidence showed an increase from 2018, peaking in 2020, and then gradually decreasing until middle 2021, and it slightly increased towards the end of 2021. High-risk sub-districts of diarrhoea were observed in Kalideres and Cengkareng throughout the study period. Additionally, there was a spatial variation of cases across the province with hotspots located in sub-districts in the western part of the province in the peri-urban region. Diarrhoea decreased by 17.0% (RR = 0.83,95% CI = 0.83–0.84) for 1 °C increase in average temperature. Similarly, diarrhoea decreased by 3.0% (RR = 0.97,95%CI = 0.97–0.97) and 27.0% (RR = 0.73, 95%CI = 0.72–0.73) for 1% in average humidity and 1 m/s wind speed. Meanwhile, diarrhoea increased by 3% (RR = 1.03, 95%CI = 1.02–1.03) and 1% (RR = 1.01, 95%CI = 1.01–1.01) in 1 h duration of sunshine per week and 1 mm increase average rainfall respectively.ConclusionSeveral sub-districts demonstrated disease clusters which indicate the area has a significantly higher risk. In addition, climatic factors were associated with diarrheal incidence. Interventions such as environmental and climatic adaptation and climatic data monitoring need to be carried out in areas that have a high risk as an early warning system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25055-3.Keywords: Jakarta, Cluster, Hotspot, Diarrhea, Spatial, Modelling, Climate

Introduction

Diarrhoea diseases continue to be a leading cause of preventable disease in developing countries, especially for children < 5 years [1]. Each year, around 525,000 children < 5 years old died from diarrhoeal disease globally [2]. In addition, repeated diarrheal episodes is one of the main causes of malnutrition in this vulnerable age population [2]. Diarrhoea is caused by a number of microorganisms including bacteria, viral, and parasites [3]. Prompt treatment of diarrheal cases through rehydration, zinc supplements and nutrient-rich diet can prevent death from diarrheal diseases significantly [2].

In Indonesia, diarrhoeal diseases cause significant morbidity and mortality in all age groups, with the highest prevalence is in children aged < 5-year-olds [4]. As the main cause of death for children aged 12–59 months [5], multiple factors were indicated to drive diarrheal incidence in densely populated areas in Indonesia such as the source of water [6], unsafe households water storage mostly in open containers [7], and poor knowledge [6]. Other risk factors of diarrhoeal diseases are unsafe disposal of child faeces [8], inadequate toilet facilities, and lower education [9]. Meanwhile, the prevalence of diarrheal disease increased from 7% to 8% between 2013 and 2018 [4, 10].

During the COVID-19 pandemic, most of the public health activities were focused on the prevention and control of the COVID-19. This has resulted in an increase of other infectious diseases such as severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS), leptospirosis [11], and Dengue Hemorrhagic Fever (DHF) [12]. On the other hand, COVID-19 pandemic intervention related activities including lockdowns, hand hygiene, and face mask use [13, 14] could have led to reduction of other infectious diseases such as salmonellosis, Hand, Foot, and Mouth Disease (HFMD), hepatitis E, influenza and scarlet fever, Tuberculosis, varicella, rubella, and measles cases [11, 15–17].

Jakarta province is the capital of Indonesia with highest population density, 16,084 per km2 in the country [18]. Jakarta province reported one of the highest numbers of COVID-19 cases in Indonesia. To tackle the COVID-19, the government of Jakarta Province made a decision on the Large-Scale Social Restriction- stage 1–5 and Enforcement of Restrictions on Community Activities stage [19] following the regulation of Indonesia government. The increased community behaviour about personal hygiene during COVID-19 pandemic [20] as well as the community awareness about healthy lifestyle improved during the pandemic [21, 22]. However, the effects of COVID-19 pandemic on diarrhoea have not been quantified in Jakarta Province.

Spatial and temporal analytical methods with geographic information systems (GIS) are commonly used in public health research to understand the distribution of disease in relation to space and time [23]. Infectious diseases including diarrhoeal diseases are not homogenous because of variation of risk factors such as socio-demographic, environmental and climatic factors. Hotspot analysis can identify areas with higher incidences of events expected in that locality by chance. Such information can be used as evidence for initiating control measured to reduce diarrheal diseases [24]. In addition, diarrhoeal diseases are forecasted to increase due to climate change [25]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates approximately 32,954 additional diarrheal deaths worldwide among children aged 0–15 years due to climate change by 2050 [26]. Additionally, during the pandemic most effort, resources, and actions were directed toward addressing COVID-19, possibly at the expense of other public health concerns.

Despite existing studies on diarrheal diseases, there is limited research exploring the combined influence of climatic factors on the incidence of diarrheal diseases before and during pandemic COVID-19, particularly in highly urbanized areas like Jakarta. Therefore, the aim of this study was to quantify spatial and temporal patterns of diarrheal diseases before and during the COVID-19 pandemic and identify climatic risk factors in Jakarta Province, Indonesia.

Methods

Study location

Jakarta Province has a land area of 661.52 km2 and sea area of 6,977.5 km2 and has recorded ± 110 islands spread across the Kepulauan Seribu. Administratively, Jakarta Province is divided into five municipalities and one administrative district, namely Central Jakarta with a land area of 47.56 km2; North Jakarta with a land area of 147.46 km2, West Jakarta with a land area of 125 km2; South Jakarta with a land area of 144.94 km2; East Jakarta with a land area of 185.54 km2 and the Kepulauan Seribu administrative district with a land area of 10.73 km2 [18] (Fig. 1).

Location of Jakarta Province and Indonesia

Study design

We used ecological study design to conduct this research by utilizing surveillance data on diarrhoeal diseases managed by the Provincial Health Office of Jakarta, Indonesia. Spatial analysis was undertaken for 44 sub-districts level in Jakarta Province, Indonesia [18].

Data sources

The diarrhoeal data from 2018 to 2021 were obtained from the official website of Epidemiological Surveillance Unit, Jakarta Provincial Health Office, Indonesia (https://surveilans-dinkes.jakarta.go.id/sarsbaru/index.php). The number of diarrheal cases was recorded weekly by health centres. It is compiled and reported routinely to the Epidemiological Surveillance Unit at the provincial level. Climate data including average temperature (°C), average humidity (%), rainfall (mm), duration of sunshine (hours), average wind speed (m/s) was obtained from the Meteorology, Climatology, and Geophysics Agency (BMKG). An electronic map of district boundaries in shapefile format was obtained from the Global Administrative Areas database [27].

Data analysis

Temporal analysis

The average weekly number of diarrheal cases was calculated from the full time series, 1 January 2018 until 31 December 2021. Seasonal and Trend Decomposition using Loess (STL) was used to analyse the temporal analysis. Loess, locally weighted regression, and scatterplot smoothing, was a method for estimating nonlinear relationships. Spatial analysis

Geographic distribution was visually depicted from incidence rates (IR) data as outlined in the form of choropleth map. At a global level across the study area, the Global Moran I test was used. The Local Indicators for Spatial Association (LISA) test was undertaken to cluster analysis.

Global Moran I explored spatial autocorrelation and its strength and tested the assumption of spatial independence of observed data across the study area. Assessment was done by checking the resulting visual curve, Moran’s Index, Z-Score and p-value. There are three possible outcomes based on statistical analysis: clustered, random, or scattered. Moran’s I values ​​range from − 1 to 1. A positive value means positive spatial autocorrelation (meaning clustering) and a negative value means negative spatial autocorrelation (means that similar values are dispersed), while a value of zero means that there is no spatial autocorrelation, indicating that the observed cases are distributed at random [29].

LISA was used to compare the similarity of risk between a sub-district and its neighbours. High-high cluster is defined as high-risk sub-district surrounded by high-risk sub-districts, while low-low cluster indicates low-risk sub-district is surrounded by sub-districts with low-risk. Low-high and high-low are outliers and indicates low-risk sub-district is surrounded by high-risk sub-districts, and high-risk sub-district is surrounded by low-risk sub-districts, respectively [30].

Risk factor analysis

The preliminary analysis of univariate Poisson regression was conducted to examine the association of diarrheal case numbers with climatic variables. The climatic variables were weekly average temperature (°C), average relative humidity (%), total hours of sunshine per week, rainfall (mm), and average wind speed (m/s). Since all climatic variables were significant in the univariate analysis, they were included in the final analysis. Multivariable Poisson regression was used to evaluate a relationship between diarrhoea and climatic variables. The results were presented as risk ratio (RR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). The association was considered significant at p value < 0.05. A variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to measure the collinearity.

Spatial analysis and maps were created in ArcGIS software (ESRI, Redlands, CA). Data were analysed using RStudio 4.0.3 version software.

Results

Descriptive analysis

The median incidence rate of diarrheal diseases was 40.51 cases per 100,000 population (IQR 14.58–53.90). The median temperature during the time period was 28.66 °C (IQR 28.14–29.16 °C). The median of humidity was 75.71% (IQR 71.82–79.29%). The median wind speed was 1.43 (m/s) (IQR 1.22–1.577 (m/s)), while the median rainfall was 3.13 mm (IQR 0.16–8.16,64 mm). The median duration of sunshine was 4.64 h per week (IQR 3.71–5.57 h) (Table 1). The incidence of diarrheal decreased drastically in 2020 and 2021 compared to 2018 and 2019 (Table 2).

Multivariable regression analysis

The results of Poisson regression showed that average temperature (°C), average of humidity (%), rainfall (mm), duration of sunshine (hours) and average of wind speed (m/s) had a significant association with the diarrheal cases in Jakarta Province. A 1 °C increase of average temperature was associated with 17% (RR = 0.83, 95% CI = 0.83–0.84) decrease in diarrheal cases. Additionally, an increase in a 1% humidity and a 1 m/s wind speed led to 3% (RR = 0.97, 95% CI = 0.97–0.97) and 27% (RR = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.72–0.73) decrease in diarrheal cases. While an increase in a 1 mm of rainfall and a 1 h of sunshine per week was associated with 3% (RR = 1.03, 95% CI = 1.02–1.03) and 1% (RR = 1.01, 95% CI = 1.01–1.01) increase in diarrheal cases (Table 4).

Discussion

Diarrhoeal incidence in Jakarta Province, Indonesia was highly seasonal with cases peaking in the beginning of the year. Diarrheal hotspots were mostly located in the western part of the province in the peri-urban region. The temperature, humidity, and wind speed were associated with decrease in diarrheal case; while rainfall and duration of sunshine were significantly associated with increase in diarrheal cases.

The diarrheal disease was highly seasonal with the peak in the beginning of the year with cases decreasing towards the end of the year. The result of this study was similar to another study in East Surabaya, Indonesia that reported the incidence of diarrheal disease increased in November to March or during the rainy season [31]. A study conducted in Bangladesh also found that the highest number of diarrheal cases occurred in the rainy season [32]. This can be explained that during the rainy season, communities faces difficulties in obtaining clean water sources and having proper sanitation access [33, 34]. Furthermore, Jakarta province routinely experiences flooding [35, 36], which could contaminate drinking water. Increasing access to clean and safe water could significantly decrease diarrheal morbidity and mortality in Jakarta. In addition, health authorities should encourage households to engage in safe water practices including boiling all drinking water particularly during the rainy season.

The diarrheal diseases decreased in 2020 and 2021. This research finding was similar to other studies, which reported on a decreased diarrheal trend in Ethiopia [37] and China [11] and other infectious diseases including dengue [38], respiratory disease [39], enterovirus infections, influenza, and scarlet fever [40]. This decreasing trend of infectious disease during the COVID-19 pandemic could be attributed to various pandemic control and mitigation efforts [38]. These control measures in Indonesia included lockdown, face-mask use in the public places, social distancing and implementation of hand hygiene [19]. Hand hygiene was one of the main factors that prevented gastrointestinal and respiratory infections [41].

Diarrheal hotspots were reported at sub-districts during the study period. High-high clusters were observed in Kalideres and Cengkareng sub-districts (in 2018–2021), Koja sub-district (in 2018), Kembangan and Kebon Jeruk sub-district (in 2020–2021). The hotspot sub-districts are located along the border with Banten Province, which reports high diarrheal cases [42, 43]. Further, these hotspot sub-districts are flood prone areas and slum areas that are crowded with fewer amenities including insufficient safe water supply. Based on the Jakarta Health Profile (2021), there were 67% and 7.3% of sources of drinking water that met the requirements in West Jakarta and North Jakarta respectively [44]. Even though water is an essential need for people to meet domestic needs. It is difficult to access clean water with guaranteed quality. Especially for people living on islands, including Kepulauan Seribu. Therefore, one common source of clean water that is utilized is rainwater [45, 46].

There were a negative association of average temperature and the incidence of diarrheal diseases. Our finding is consistent with other studies from Indonesia [47] and Afghanistan [48] while different from other studies [49, 50]. This was explained by improved replication of viruses thereby increasing the incidence of diarrheal [51, 52]. However, the surveillance data for this study did not report the diarrhoeal diseases caused by pathogens. So, we couldn’t run analysis stratified by casual organisms.

Humidity was associated with decrease in diarrheal cases in this study. Evidence of the humidity on diarrheal disease is inconsistent. Some studies showed a positive association with diarrheal diseases [32, 53] but the other studies reported negative association with those [54, 55]. Our study is consistent with a study conducted in Surabaya, Indonesia that reported that low humidity was associated with incline in diarrheal cases [56]. This can be explained by the fact that some diarrheal disease-causing agents like rotavirus can remain viable at low relative humidity on surfaces like plastic, glass, and stainless steel for over 10 days at room temperature [54, 55]. However, in this study, we did not explore the specific disease-causing agents for lack of information on them. Therefore, further research is needed to delve into the relationship between humidity and diarrheal incidence by evaluating pathogen-related factors.

Our study found that wind speed was associated with decrease in diarrheal cases. This result was consistent with study conducted in Hunan Province, China [57]. Wind speed has a correlation with the transmission of disease such as dysentery [57]. High wind speed can lead to increased evaporation, and viruses cannot easily survive and proliferate in dry food and environments. High wind speed will also reduce human contact, making it unfavourable for the spread of bacillary dysentery [57]. However, the positive correlation reported in the previous study between diarrhoea and low wind speed [58]. The study reported that high wind speed could lower the detection rate of the virus because transmission of rotavirus increases when aerosols can persist in slower moving air and are hindered by stronger winds that carry particles away from vulnerable individuals [59].

Rainfall was positively associated with diarrheal cases. Similar findings were reported in other studies [31, 60]. Plausible explanations are; first, increased rainfall can lead to contamination of drinking water as had been reported [33, 61]. It was stated that the quality of water sources in Jakarta is heavily impaired [62], whereas most communities in Jakarta still used unimproved water sources such as tap water [63]. Second, rainfall could lead to flooding that is suitable for the growth of many pathogens and lead to food contamination [56]. In addition, the poor drainage could cause diarrheal diseases [64]. Thus, the rainfall affects the water quality that induces higher the transmission of pathogens [33].

Our study found that duration of sunshine was significantly associated with incidence of diarrheal cases. Our finding is similar to a study conducted in China. The study found that after extreme sunshine duration, it was followed by lower diarrheal diseases [65]. Sunlight can act as a disinfectant so that causal organisms such as bacteria might be killed. Moreover, sunlight exposure can lead to increased vitamin D absorption which was associated with decreased diarrheal cases [66]. The results of this study could improve the understanding of diarrheal cases distribution, formulate better intervention to tackle the diarrheal outbreak and increase the quality of public health in vulnerable populations before and during COVID-19 pandemic. Policy makers should focus on strengthening healthcare resilience and establish an integrated health system that can simultaneously manage diarrheal disease, especially in climate-vulnerable regions. The government can develop an early warning system to predict diarrheal diseases based on climatic parameters trends such as temperature, humidity, and rainfall pattern. Additionally, spatial analysis can identify geographic areas where diarrheal diseases are most prevalent, often correlating with climatic factors. Therefore, it enables optimizing of resources for areas with higher risk.

This study needs to be interpreted in light of some limitations. First, diarrhoea diseases from the surveillance system were not all laboratory confirmed. Followed by quality of surveillance data could not be ascertained. Next, patients or groups of population with mild forms of diarrhoea may not seek medical care and remain unreported leading to under-reporting. The last is access to health care for diarrheal diseases that may vary across sub-districts leading to differences in reporting of diarrhoea.

Conclusion

Diarrheal cases in Jakarta province were highly seasonal and strongly associated with local weather factors, including rainfall, sunshine and wind speed. Therefore, public health intervention to reduce diarrheal risks of climate change should be undertaken with greater consideration of local climatic conditions. The lower diarrhoeal trend was observed during the COVID-19 pandemic. There was a spatial variation of cases across the province with hotspots located in sub-districts in the western part of the province in the peri-urban region. The diarrhoeal incidence was associated with climatic factors. Interventions need to be carried out in areas that have a high risk, especially in the peri-urban sub-districts. Additionally, the intervention is required to increase the public health measures to tackle the climate dynamics impacts in these areas, such as the clean and healthy living behaviour in the community.
