Accurate and Efficient Numerical Simulation of Land Models Using SUMMA With SUNDIALS
Authors: Raymond J Spiteri, Ashley E Van Beusekom, Kyle Klenk, Reza Zolfaghari, Sean J Trim, Wouter J M Knoben, Andrew M Ireson, Martyn P Clark
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNumerical simulation of land models without error control can be highly inaccurate. We present the incorporation of the Suite of Nonlinear and Differential‐Algebraic Equation Solvers (SUNDIALS) package to solve the equations that simulate thermodynamics and hydrologic processes in the Structure for Unifying Multiple Modeling Alternatives (SUMMA) land model. The algorithmic features of SUNDIALS, such as error estimation and adaptive order and step‐size control, result in a SUMMA‐SUNDIALS model that delivers substantially improved accuracy and relative computational efficiency compared to integration with the previous SUMMA model, which uses the low‐order backward Euler method with no rigorous error control. The results are demonstrated through simulations over the North American continent with more than 500,000 spatial elements. Compared to the previous SUMMA model, we find that the simulations produced by the SUMMA‐SUNDIALS model are orders of magnitude closer to converged solutions for the same computational cost. Being able to efficiently perform more reliable simulations makes the SUMMA‐SUNDIALS model a powerful tool for improving our understanding of the terrestrial component of the Earth System.

Introduction

Land models simulate the terrestrial component of the Earth System. The processes in land models include thermodynamics, hydrology, and biogeochemistry, which are typically described by partial differential equations. Land models are used for a myriad of applications, including quantifying sustainable water supplies and predicting water‐related hazards. Land models are an important part of Earth System models, which simulate coupled interactions between the atmosphere, land, ocean, and sea ice.

The development of process‐based land models is a multi‐faceted, interdisciplinary, and highly iterative exercise. Many model decisions must be evaluated in order to determine their impact, including algorithms and model parameter values to represent energy and mass fluxes. Decisions on the numerical implementation of the model representations pose yet another challenge. The Structure for Unifying Multiple Modeling Alternatives (SUMMA) advances land model development by offering a flexible software framework in which different model hypotheses, spatial structures, process parameterizations, parameter values, and time‐stepping schemes can be implemented and tested (Clark et al., 2015a).

A common approach utilized by many land models, including SUMMA, is to discretize the partial differential equations describing the conservation of energy and mass using the method‐of‐lines (MOL) approach. The MOL proceeds in two steps. First, spatial derivatives are discretized via finite differences, finite volumes, finite elements, or other approximations to yield a system of ordinary differential or differential‐algebraic equations (ODEs or DAEs). Second, the result is numerically integrated in time.

In the previous version of SUMMA, herein called SUMMA‐BE, the time integration was performed using the implicit (or backward) Euler method. Such an approach is common, for example, see (Tubini et al., 2020). The ODE/DAE system is typically stiff, and backward Euler is often an effective alternative for achieving solutions in an acceptable amount of time relative to explicit methods, such as forward Euler, which may require a large number of time steps due to stability constraints. In SUMMA‐BE, some adaptation of the numerical method and step size is made based on stability considerations; for example, step‐size reduction or operator splitting when there is a lack of convergence of the Newton iteration to the backward Euler solution (see Algorithm 1 below).

The approach taken in SUMMA‐BE has several limitations. The main one is the lack of error control. The lack of error control generally results in a solution that is either inaccurate (using time steps that are too large in places) or inefficient (using time steps that are smaller than necessary), or, to some extent, both. Proper adjustment of step sizes according to an error estimate improves both accuracy and efficiency. Furthermore, backward Euler is only first order. For problems with smooth solutions, higher‐order methods achieve lower errors at less computational cost than lower‐order methods. Accordingly, the efficiency of first‐order accurate methods is often deemed unsatisfactory in practice.

Each of these limitations can be addressed using state‐of‐the‐art solvers such as the Suite of Nonlinear and Differential‐Algebraic Equation Solvers (SUNDIALS) (Hindmarsh et al., 2005). A general‐purpose solver in SUNDIALS is the Implicit Differential‐Algebraic (IDA) solver that solves initial‐value problems for systems of differential‐algebraic equations. In the context of land modeling, IDA can be used to integrate systems of differential equations over a given time interval. The time interval is defined here as a “data window” over which the meteorological forcing data are constant in time, for example, 30‐minute averages of meteorological variables from a flux tower.

The time‐integration method used in IDA is much more robust than the common implementations in land models. IDA generally takes multiple sub‐steps within each data window. IDA starts with an initial sub‐step length that is much shorter than the length of the data window and increases the sub‐step length until the local truncation error estimate matches user‐prescribed tolerances. IDA uses multistep methods (specifically, backward differentiation formulas) where the solutions from past sub‐steps within the data window are used to compute the solution for the current sub‐step in the data window. The objective of this paper is to evaluate the gains in accuracy and efficiency that are possible by replacing SUMMA's rudimentary backward Euler solver with the SUNDIALS solver. To this end, various numerical experiments are presented from running the SUMMA‐SUNDIALS model with over 500,000 spatial elements comprising over the entire continent of North America. The results from these experiments are evaluated with respect to the accuracy and relative efficiency of SUNDIALS solutions with respect to SUMMA‐BE. We find that the SUMMA‐SUNDIALS model produces simulations that are orders of magnitude closer to converged solutions for the same computational cost as the original implementation of SUMMA‐BE that has no rigorous error control.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 gives the physical and mathematical details behind SUMMA, including model equations in the form of conservation of energy and mass, vertical fluxes, and diagnostic variables. Section 3 gives details on the spatial and temporal discretizations and the solution algorithm. Section 4 presents the results of the numerical experiments over North America in terms of accuracy and relative efficiency. Finally, Section 5 summarizes the results and conclusions.

Land Model Equations

Land models are first discretized horizontally into vertical columns, for example, a lattice of grid cells or, in this study, over 500,000 irregular elements (“sub‐basins”) across the North American continent (see Figure 1). The horizontal discretization forms a geospatial “fabric,” termed a “hydrofabric” in hydrologic applications (Blodgett et al., 2023). Typically, the vertical columns in land models are decoupled from one another, with lateral fluxes of water gathered up by a routing algorithm, which does not interact with the soil columns (Lawrence et al., 2019).

Numerical Simulation

In this section, we describe the method to solve 2, 3, and 5 numerically using the MOL. The backward differentiation formulas (BDF) are a popular class of methods for solving such classes of problems and form one of the fundamental methods for time integration in SUNDIALS.

Horizontal Discretization

As noted earlier, land models are first discretized horizontally into vertical columns, for example, a lattice of grid cells or, in this study, over 500,000 irregular elements (“sub‐basins”) across the North American continent. In many models, including SUMMA, there is a further horizontal discretization that represents sub‐grid scale heterogeneity, sometimes called tiles. In SUMMA, the sub‐grid scale horizontal unit is termed a hydrological response unit (HRU), and the grid cell itself (in our case, a sub‐basin) is termed a GRU. An HRU has a distinct set of properties (e.g., soil or vegetation parameters) to represent distinct sub‐areas of the GRU (e.g., different elevations or vegetation types). In SUMMA, it is possible to couple HRUs—this is done by adding lateral source and sink terms to the state equations in each HRU to represent processes such as lateral water flow.

In the model configurations evaluated in this paper, we use one HRU per GRU (i.e., we do not have any further horizontal discretization of the GRUs in the hydrofabric).

Vertical Discretization

Within an HRU, SUMMA calculates the vertical fluxes within the vegetation and canopy air space, snowpack, soil, and aquifer using a one‐dimensional finite difference approach. The number of layers is handled differently for the different model sub‐domains. For vegetation, it is common to use a single vegetation layer (the so‐called “big‐leaf” model (Dai et al., 2004)). For snow, the number of layers is typically adaptive (with a limit on the maximum number of layers). The depth of each snow layer is recomputed at the end of each data window to account for processes such as new snowfall or compaction of the snowpack. Snow layers are sub‐divided or merged when layer depths meet specific criteria; that is, a snow layer is sub‐divided into two when its depth is larger than a prescribed threshold and merged with one of its neighbors when its depth is smaller than a prescribed threshold. For soil, the number of layers is typically fixed, but they may be of variable thickness. Thinner soil layers are used near the top of the soil sub‐domain to better capture the effects of diurnal temperature forcing and large moisture gradients. For a visualization of the vertical discretization, see Figure 2.

We integrate the implicit ordinary differential Equation 38 over a time interval 0,tend described in terms of a sequence of data windows tn−1,tn, n=1,2,…,Ndata, over which certain inputs or other quantities are fixed and then updated from one window to the next. Fluxes such as precipitation and shortwave radiation are often taken to be averages over a data window, for example, shortwave downwelling radiation changes during daylight hours and can change rapidly during otherwise innocuous events such as the sun going behind a cloud; similarly, precipitation can start and stop abruptly. Because of the discontinuities that may consequently arise in the terms of the differential equations, the integration is restarted on each data window. Data windows are typically fixed in length for a given simulation, for example, 1 hr; however, we use notation Δtn=tn−tn−1 that does not make this assumption of fixed data window length while also serving to index each interval. We compare the results from the SUMMA‐SUNDIALS solver to the results using the previously implemented SUMMA solver, which we call SUMMA‐BE, as documented in (Clark et al., 2015b). Model Domain and Error Calculations

The model domain for the simulations was the North American continent, parameterized by 517,315 GRUs (median GRU size is 33 km2; mean size is 40 km2) derived from the global MERIT basins data set (Lin et al., 2019). The model setup followed the workflow in Knoben et al. (2022), using forcing, elevation, landuse, and soil data from the ERA5, MERIT Hydro, MODIS MCD12Q1, and SoilGrids 250m data sets (Friedl & Sulla‐Menashe, 2019; Hengl et al., 2017; Hersbach et al., 2020; Yamazaki et al., 2019), respectively. The data windows were taken to be hourly from 1 January 1979–31 December 1984 (52,608 hr); that is, Algorithm 1 is run for Δtn=1 hour, n=1,2,…,Ndata=52,608, and each GRU. All simulations were run on the Digital Research Alliance of Canada cluster Graham at the University of Waterloo in 998 batches of 518 basins and one batch of 351 basins, where each of the 999 batches was run on one of the 1,146 Broadwell nodes, which have slightly different performance characteristics.

In order to have a measure of the error, we first compute a so‐called reference solution. A reference solution involves solving the model equations to a degree of accuracy that is not warranted in most practical applications but provides a way to quantify the error in other numerical solutions. In this case, we compute the reference solution using SUMMA‐SUNDIALS with relative and absolute tolerances rtol=atol=1e−8. Roughly speaking, these settings are meant to produce a solution with 8 digits of accuracy with components of magnitude less than 1e−8 considered negligible. The SUMMA‐BE solver was used to compute solutions with N=1 (SUMMA‐BE1), N=16 (SUMMA‐BE16), and N=32 (SUMMA‐BE32). The SUMMA‐SUNDIALS solver was used to compute solutions with rtol=atol=1e−6. The reference solution required about two to three times the wall clock time required by this SUMMA‐SUNDIALS solution. In the analysis reported below, no advantage is conferred to the SUMMA‐SUNDIALS solver by virtue of its use in computing the reference solution: in principle, the way in which a sufficiently converged reference solution is produced is solver independent. The SUMMA‐BE solutions indeed converge to this reference solution, but it would not be feasible to produce a reference solution using SUMMA‐BE because it is too inefficient.

We note that the goal of this study is examine the performance of SUMMA‐BE relative to SUMMA‐SUNDIALS with respect to numerical error and computational efficiency and not with respect to observation. The comparisons made between the various solutions in each GRU were based the Relative Root Mean Square Error (RRMSE) with a set of characterizing variables, chosen due to their ubiquity in analysis of hydrologic systems. These variables are the diagnostic variables total soil water content, total evapotranspiration, and average routed runoff. When looking at individual GRUs, the error in a physical quantity accumulates until the quantity reaches a “contact point” near enough one of its physical bounds. For example, the contact point of the total soil water content would occur during a large soil drying event where the soil water content reaches a low value for that GRU. The true contact point would be when the soil water content reaches its physical lower bound of zero; however, in reality the soil water content would not reach zero in most GRUs. In Figure 3b, we see a persistent over‐estimation in the total soil water content of SUMMA‐BE1 that is not corrected until a later drying event (almost a year later, see Figure 3a). Accordingly, we observe that an inaccurate solution on one time step may cause the subsequent accuracy of a simulation to suffer for many subsequent time steps. In contrast, numerical solutions of variables that frequently reach their bounds are more likely to appear reasonable regardless of the accuracy of the solution (e.g., canopy water, which often dries out daily).

The subsequent errors in flux variables lead to over‐estimation or under‐estimation in the severity of events, instead of error accumulation over time. In Figures 3c and 3d, we observe large spikes in the total evapotranspiration of SUMMA‐BE1 for the example GRU. In Figure 3e, the average routed runoff of SUMMA‐BE1 shows flood peaks are underestimated, and in the 2‐week time series, three of the small events are missed entirely.

These results clearly illustrate the potential risks with running simulations in the absence of rigorous error estimation. Adaptation for the purposes of stability, such as reducing time step size or operator splitting based on the convergence of the nonlinear equations solver, is effective in enhancing convergence of the simulation and hence offers some value; however, the inescapable truth is that such a strategy offers no indication on solution quality in terms of accuracy. The ability for a solver to adapt step size (and possibly order) in response to error estimates is crucial to the solver efficiency. The goal of adaptive methods in this scenario is to take the largest (and cheapest) step that still meets the user‐defined error tolerance.

One could easily have the impression that numerical errors in simulations of land models are unlikely to be significant compared to other sources of error, but here we show that is not the case. We acknowledge that we are using an uncalibrated model with a single default parameter set, and calibrating the model would yield results closer to given observations. However, in this study, we are quantifying the error in the solution of the equations themselves; calibrating a model with that solves the equations incorrectly would be using parameters to compensate for model problems (i.e., parameter equifinality; see Beven (2019)).

As can be seen from Figures 6d and 10, SUMMA‐SUNDIALS is approximately as expensive as SUMMA‐BE16 and approximately 5 times more computationally expensive on average than SUMMA‐BE1. SUMMA‐BE32 is twice as expensive as SUMMA‐SUNDIALS and generally less accurate. It is not clear how large N would need to be for SUMMA‐BEN to generally achieve the accuracy achieved by SUMMA‐SUNDIALS in this experiment.

Summary and Conclusions

Numerical simulations of process‐based land models can be highly inaccurate when rigorous error control is absent. Furthermore, such simulations can also be inefficient without adaptive step sizes or high‐order time‐stepping methods. To address these issues of accuracy and efficiency within a process‐based land model, we have incorporated the state‐of‐the‐art time‐integration library SUNDIALS into the SUMMA land model to create a new model that we call SUMMA‐SUNDIALS.

We demonstrate the effectiveness of the SUMMA‐SUNDIALS implementation by running a simulation over North America with more than 500,000 GRUs. We find that the normal operation of SUMMA‐BE1 with initial step size equal to data window of 1 hr is able to provide a solution (for example, the solution did not diverge), but errors relative to the reference solution can be widespread in excess of 50% in some commonly used characterizing variables such as routed average runoff. The SUMMA‐SUNDIALS solution is generally of much higher quality (RRMSE values uniformly no larger than about 10% for three characterizing variables, total soil water content, total evapotranspiration, and average routed runoff) than that obtained with SUMMA‐BE1; however, it is also approximately 5 times more expensive than SUMMA‐BE1. That being said, simulations using SUMMA‐BE with a refined uniform time step quickly became significantly more computationally expensive than SUMMA‐SUNDIALS while still being decidedly less accurate.

Although a successful first step, there is still much left to be accomplished with the SUMMA‐SUNDIALS model. A first goal would be to reduce the run‐time of SUMMA‐SUNDIALS to be more comparable (or even better) than SUMMA‐BE1 while maintaining sufficient accuracy. One path to this goal may be to take advantage of fine‐grained parallelization of routines in SUMMA, for example, flux calculations or domain decomposition, or parallelization capabilities already present in SUNDIALS, for example, GPU parallelization. Another path may involve operator splitting (in particular multi‐rate methods), whereby the right‐hand side of the MOL ODEs is not solved monolithically but rather as a sum of individual operators that can be treated by their own specialized solvers, for example, Reynolds et al. (2023). Both SUMMA and SUNDIALS already have some functionality for operator splitting, but how to design an effective splitting strategy, that is, one that can give a sufficiently accurate solution in less time than a monolithic approach, is not yet an exact science.

Progress has been made in reducing wall clock time to solution and automating the workflow for large embarrassingly parallel simulations for SUMMA‐BE through the actor model of concurrent computing (Hewitt, 2010). We have demonstrated significant increases in resource utilization and fault tolerance (Klenk & Spiteri, 2024) as well as computational throughput within a resilient ad hoc cluster (Klenk et al., 2024) when using SUMMA‐BE within an actors‐based framework. A clear next step would be to extend the actors functionality to SUMMA‐SUNDIALS. We expect the actors framework to benefit the workflows for SUMMA‐SUNDIALS even more than for SUMMA‐BE because there are many more options (for example, tolerances) that can be used for automated runtime optimization. This benefit to workflows can be expected to be particularly useful in parameter estimation studies, where the variability of problem difficulty (and hence computational run times) increases dramatically as simulations are pushed to the limits of what is physically realistic.
