Diagnostic performance of artificial intelligence models in hepatocellular carcinoma: a systematic review and diagnostic meta-analysis
Authors: Zheng Ren, Jinping Sun, Xiaochun Liu, Yongqiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo systematically evaluate the diagnostic performance of artificial intelligence (AI) models for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and to pool sensitivity and specificity using a diagnostic meta-analysis, with further assessment of the robustness of findings across different validation levels.MethodsOriginal studies on AI-based diagnosis of HCC were systematically searched. Literature screening, data extraction, and quality assessment were independently performed by two reviewers. Extracted data included basic study characteristics, model type, validation level, and diagnostic performance metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, area under the curve (AUC), and extractable true-positive (TP), false-positive (FP), false-negative (FN), and true-negative (TN) values. If a single study reported results from different validation levels or analytical scenarios, these were extracted as separate study records for quantitative synthesis. Methodological quality was assessed using the QUADAS-2 tool. Formal validation sets (validation, independent validation, or external validation) were included in the primary analysis, whereas development sets and internal validation sets were included in the sensitivity analysis. Because only one eligible external validation study was available for early HCC, this outcome was presented descriptively only. For studies with extractable 2 × 2 table data, pooled sensitivity and specificity were estimated using the Reitsma bivariate random-effects model, and a summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curve was constructed. An additional sensitivity analysis retaining a single highest-validation-level record per study was performed to assess the influence of potential non-independence among records from the same study.ResultsA total of 11 studies were included in the qualitative analysis. Because some studies reported results from different validation levels or analytical scenarios, separate study records were extracted for quantitative synthesis. Of these, four formal validation records were included in the primary analysis, seven records were included in the sensitivity analysis, and one early HCC record was included for descriptive presentation. The primary analysis showed that the pooled sensitivity and specificity of AI models for HCC diagnosis were 0.904 (95% CI: 0.845–0.942) and 0.971 (95% CI: 0.891–0.993), respectively. In the sensitivity analysis, after inclusion of development and internal validation datasets, the pooled sensitivity was 0.895 (95% CI: 0.831–0.936) and the pooled specificity was 0.935 (95% CI: 0.846–0.974). Overall, the currently available records reporting AUC values suggested that AI models had good discriminative ability for HCC diagnosis. In the early HCC analysis, a single external validation study reported a sensitivity of 0.88 and a specificity of 1.00; however, no pooled analysis was performed because of the limited evidence. In the additional study-level sensitivity analysis, the pooled sensitivity and specificity were 0.889 and 0.964, respectively, which were close to the record-level primary estimates. Exploratory assessment according to QUADAS-2 domains suggested that patient selection bias, particularly selected case-control comparisons, may have contributed to the high pooled specificity.ConclusionCurrent evidence suggests that AI models may have promising potential as auxiliary diagnostic tools for HCC. However, because the primary pooled estimates were based on only four formal validation records with heterogeneous data modalities, reference standards, study populations, and validation designs, these results should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of clinical readiness. Further high-quality, multicenter, prospective external validation studies with complete patient-level 2 × 2 data and clinically relevant comparator populations are needed to clarify the real-world clinical utility of AI models for HCC diagnosis, particularly for early HCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04927-x.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common histological subtype of primary liver cancer and remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related death worldwide [1]. Because HCC often develops insidiously and presents with nonspecific manifestations in its early stages, many patients are diagnosed at an intermediate or advanced stage, thereby missing the opportunity for curative treatment [2, 3]. Even among patients with early-stage HCC who are eligible for radical liver resection, postoperative recurrence remains a clinically important challenge, and recent evidence suggests that biological features such as lymphatic vessel density in the tumor capsule may help identify patients at higher risk of recurrence and intrahepatic spread [4]. Therefore, improving early detection, diagnostic accuracy, and risk stratification for HCC has long been a major focus in both clinical practice and research.

Beyond tumor detection itself, accurate characterization of HCC is clinically important because biological aggressiveness and surgical strategy are closely related to prognosis. Microvascular invasion (MVI) and satellite nodules are well-recognized indicators of aggressive tumor biology and postoperative recurrence risk. Recent evidence suggests that imaging-related features such as tumor compression of the hepatic or portal vein may help predict the presence of MVI and satellite nodules in patients with HCC [5]. In addition, surgical decision-making, including anatomical resection and vascular resection strategies such as middle hepatic vein resection during hemihepatectomy, has been investigated in relation to both short-term surgical outcomes and long-term prognosis [6]. These advances further highlight the need for accurate, reproducible, and generalizable diagnostic and risk-stratification tools for HCC.

At present, the diagnosis of HCC mainly relies on a comprehensive assessment of imaging findings, serological biomarkers, and clinical background [7]. In high-risk populations, traditional serum biomarkers such as alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) have certain clinical utility; however, their sensitivity and specificity remain limited across different settings [8, 9]. Although imaging-based diagnosis represents a cornerstone of clinical evaluation, its performance is highly dependent on reader experience, lesion characteristics, and examination quality. Diagnostic challenges remain particularly evident in early HCC, borderline lesions, and complex cirrhotic backgrounds [10, 11]. In this context, the development of more efficient, robust, and generalizable diagnostic support tools is of considerable clinical importance.

In recent years, with the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning, deep learning, and multimodal fusion algorithms have been increasingly applied in oncologic diagnosis [12]. Compared with conventional approaches based on single indicators, AI models are capable of integrating multidimensional data, including blood-based biomarkers, proteomics, glycomics, cell-free DNA (cfDNA), exosome-related indicators, and medical imaging, thereby enabling the extraction of latent feature patterns from complex datasets and offering new technical avenues for HCC detection [13, 14]. Previous studies have suggested that different types of AI models may achieve favorable discriminative performance in HCC diagnosis. However, the available evidence remains highly heterogeneous in terms of data modality, algorithmic workflow, study design, and validation strategy, and systematic integration of these findings is still lacking [15, 16].

Importantly, the performance of AI diagnostic models is often substantially influenced by the validation level. Development datasets, internal validation datasets, and external validation datasets differ considerably in evidential strength and clinical interpretability. Evaluating model performance solely on the basis of development-stage or internal validation results may overestimate its applicability in real-world external populations. Therefore, when systematically assessing AI diagnostic models, it is necessary to distinguish between validation levels and to prioritize formal validation evidence in order to more objectively determine their potential for clinical translation. The present study was designed on this basis, with formal validation datasets serving as the primary analytic population, while development datasets and internal validation datasets were incorporated into the sensitivity analysis.

Accordingly, this study aimed to systematically evaluate the diagnostic performance of AI models for HCC and to pool sensitivity and specificity through a diagnostic meta-analysis. In addition, primary and sensitivity analyses were conducted according to different validation levels to assess the robustness of the current evidence and the potential clinical applicability of AI models for HCC diagnosis. Evidence related to early HCC was also presented as a supplementary analysis, with the goal of providing a reference basis for future high-quality study design and clinical application.

Primary analytical strategy

This study was conducted as a systematic review and diagnostic meta-analysis. Given that the level of evidence differs across the model development, internal validation, and external validation stages, we prespecified that only formal validation datasets (validation, independent validation, or external validation) would be included in the primary analysis, whereas development datasets and internal validation datasets would be incorporated into the sensitivity analysis. This approach was intended to reduce duplicate weighting and improve the clinical interpretability of the primary findings. For early HCC, because only one eligible external validation dataset was currently available, the result was presented descriptively as a single-study finding, and no pooled meta-analysis was performed.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were developed according to the PICOS framework and the methodological recommendations for diagnostic test accuracy reviews, in line with the objective of this diagnostic meta-analysis [17]. Because the aim of the present study was to evaluate the diagnostic performance of AI-based models for HCC, eligible studies were required to include a clinically relevant HCC diagnostic population, an AI-based index test, and diagnostic performance outcomes such as sensitivity, specificity, AUC, or extractable 2 × 2 table data. Studies were excluded if their primary purpose was prognosis assessment, recurrence prediction, treatment response prediction, or molecular mechanism exploration rather than HCC diagnosis, because such studies addressed different clinical questions and could not be combined in a diagnostic meta-analysis. Reviews, comments, case reports, conference abstracts, animal experiments, and in vitro studies were excluded to ensure that the evidence base consisted of original human diagnostic accuracy studies.

Study selection and data extraction

Study selection

Two reviewers independently screened the literature according to the prespecified inclusion and exclusion criteria. Titles and abstracts were first reviewed to exclude clearly irrelevant records, followed by full-text assessment to determine final eligibility. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus, with adjudication by a third reviewer when necessary. The study selection process was organized and reported in accordance with the PRISMA statement.

Data extraction

Data were independently extracted by two reviewers and cross-checked for accuracy. The extracted information included first author, publication year, study region, study design, sample size, number of HCC cases and non-HCC controls, AI model type, data modality, comparator, validation level, and diagnostic performance metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, AUC, and extractable TP, FP, FN, and TN values.

For studies reporting results from development sets, internal validation sets, and external validation sets simultaneously, data from each dataset were extracted separately. Formal validation sets (validation, independent validation, or external validation) were included in the primary analysis, whereas development sets and internal validation sets were included in the sensitivity analysis. Studies reporting only early HCC and meeting the eligibility criteria were extracted separately and presented descriptively.

Data handling

If sensitivity, specificity, and sample size were directly reported, these data were extracted as presented. If TP, FP, FN, and TN were not directly reported but could be derived from sensitivity, specificity, and case numbers, they were calculated accordingly. Studies with key information missing that could not be accurately extracted or derived were not included in the primary analysis but could be considered in the qualitative review. For duplicate publications based on the same cohort, the study with the larger sample size, more complete information, or higher validation level was preferentially included.

The diagnostic thresholds and model parameters used to calculate sensitivity, specificity, and AUC were those reported by the original studies. The present meta-analysis did not develop a new prediction model, did not perform multivariable regression modeling, and did not define new cut-off values for clinical covariates. Because the included AI models differed substantially in data modality, algorithmic structure, feature selection strategy, and threshold determination method, and because individual-level prediction data were unavailable, it was not appropriate to re-estimate or harmonize diagnostic cut-off values across studies. Therefore, diagnostic performance metrics were extracted as reported or were derived from reported sensitivity, specificity, and sample size when sufficient information was available.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) tool, as recommended for diagnostic test accuracy reviews [18]. QUADAS-2 consists of four domains for risk of bias, namely patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing, as well as three domains for applicability concerns, namely patient selection, index test, and reference standard. Each domain was judged as having low risk, high risk, or unclear risk.

For the patient selection domain, studies were rated as high risk if they used an obvious case-control design, enrolled a highly selected population, used healthy controls as the main comparator, or excluded difficult-to-diagnose cases in a way that could overestimate diagnostic performance. Studies were rated as unclear risk if the recruitment method, sampling frame, or inclusion process was insufficiently described. Studies were rated as low risk if patients or controls were enrolled consecutively or randomly from a clinically relevant diagnostic or surveillance population.

For the index test domain, studies were rated as low risk if the AI model was evaluated using prespecified thresholds or clearly separated training and validation/test datasets. Studies were rated as high risk if model thresholds were optimized on the same dataset used for performance evaluation or if there was clear leakage between model development and testing. Studies were rated as unclear risk if threshold selection, blinding, or separation between development and validation datasets was insufficiently reported.

For the reference standard domain, studies were rated as low risk if HCC diagnosis was confirmed by pathology or by accepted clinical/imaging diagnostic criteria according to contemporary guidelines. Studies were rated as high risk if the reference standard was likely to misclassify HCC or non-HCC status. Studies were rated as unclear risk if the diagnostic criteria or reference standard process was not clearly described.

For the flow and timing domain, studies were rated as low risk if all or nearly all participants received the same reference standard, the interval between the index test and reference standard was clinically appropriate, and all enrolled participants were included in the analysis. Studies were rated as high risk if there was differential verification, substantial exclusion after enrollment, or an inappropriate interval between index test and reference standard. Studies were rated as unclear risk if the timing between index test and reference standard, participant exclusions, or missing data handling was insufficiently reported.

Quality assessment was independently performed by two reviewers according to the above criteria, and disagreements were resolved through discussion, with adjudication by a third reviewer if necessary. The results of the quality assessment were presented graphically and were further considered in the interpretation of pooled diagnostic performance.

Statistical analysis

This study was conducted as a diagnostic meta-analysis. Data from formal validation levels (validation, independent validation, or external validation) were used for the primary analysis, whereas development datasets and internal validation datasets were included in the sensitivity analysis. For studies from which true-positive (TP), false-positive (FP), false-negative (FN), and true-negative (TN) values could be extracted, pooled sensitivity and specificity were estimated using the Reitsma bivariate random-effects model, and a summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curve was constructed.

No multivariable regression model was constructed in the present meta-analysis. All pooled estimates were based on diagnostic accuracy data extracted from the included studies, and model-specific diagnostic thresholds were retained as originally reported.

Between-record heterogeneity was assessed quantitatively and descriptively. For sensitivity and specificity, Cochran’s Q test and I² statistics were calculated separately using univariate random-effects models, and τ² was reported where estimable. I² was interpreted as the proportion of total variability attributable to between-record heterogeneity rather than sampling error. Because the number of formal validation records in the primary analysis was small, all heterogeneity statistics were interpreted cautiously.

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, we performed an exploratory descriptive comparison according to data modality. Records were broadly categorized as imaging-based AI models and liquid biopsy/proteomics-based AI models. Given the small number of records within each category, formal meta-regression was not performed, and modality-based comparisons were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating only. Forest plots were used to present the sensitivity and specificity of individual studies as well as the pooled estimates, together with 95% CI. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed comprehensively based on sensitivity, specificity, and study characteristics. Studies for which only AUC values were available, but from which 2 × 2 table data could not be extracted or derived, were included in the qualitative review rather than the primary meta-analysis. For early HCC, because only one eligible external validation record was available, no pooled analysis was performed and the result was presented descriptively as a single-study finding. Given the limited number of included studies, no formal quantitative assessment of publication bias was conducted.

To assess the potential influence of extracting multiple records from the same study, we performed an additional sensitivity analysis using a “single highest-validation-level record per study” strategy. For studies that reported multiple datasets or analytical scenarios, only one record per study was retained according to the following hierarchy: external validation or independent validation, validation, internal validation, and development set. When more than one record was available at the same validation level, the record with the more clinically relevant comparator was selected preferentially. For example, comparisons with liver cirrhosis or high-risk non-HCC controls were prioritized over comparisons with healthy controls. This strategy was used to reduce potential unit-of-analysis bias arising from treating multiple records from the same study as statistically independent observations. The results of this analysis were compared with those of the record-level primary analysis.

To assess whether methodological quality influenced the pooled estimates, we performed exploratory analyses stratified by QUADAS-2 risk-of-bias level. For each QUADAS-2 domain, records were categorized as low risk versus high or unclear risk. Because the number of records was very small, especially in the primary analysis, formal subgroup meta-analysis or meta-regression was not considered reliable. Therefore, we summarized diagnostic performance descriptively according to risk-of-bias category and compared the results with the main pooled estimates. These analyses were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating only.

Results

Study selection

A total of 300 potentially relevant records were identified through database searching. After removal of duplicates, 136 records remained. Following title and abstract screening, 50 records were excluded, and 86 articles were assessed in full text. Of these, 75 full-text articles were further excluded, leaving 11 studies for qualitative analysis. Because some studies reported results from different validation levels or analytical scenarios, separate study records were extracted for quantitative synthesis. Of these records, four formal validation records were included in the primary analysis, and seven records were included in the sensitivity analysis. Basic characteristics of the included studies

The included studies primarily focused on the application of AI models for the diagnosis of HCC. Study populations comprised patients with HCC, patients with suspected HCC, and individuals at high risk for HCC. The data modalities were diverse and included blood-based biomarkers, proteomics, glycomics, cell-free DNA (cfDNA), and medical imaging. The AI approaches used across studies included machine learning models, deep learning algorithms, and multimodal fusion models. Validation levels varied among studies and involved different stages, including development, internal validation, validation, independent validation, and external validation. To reduce duplicate weighting and improve clinical interpretability, formal validation datasets were prespecified for inclusion in the primary analysis, whereas development datasets and internal validation datasets were included in the sensitivity analysis. Basic Characteristics of the Included Studies

Quality assessment of the included studies

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the QUADAS-2 tool. Overall, most studies showed a low risk of bias in the domains of the index test and reference standard. However, some studies presented unclear risk or potential high risk in the domains of patient selection and flow and timing. Regarding applicability, most studies were generally consistent with the objective of the present review in the domains of the index test and reference standard. Nevertheless, because some studies differed in study populations, validation levels, and data sources, the applicability concerns related to patient selection should be interpreted with caution. Overall, the methodological quality of the included studies varied across domains. Although the index test and reference standard domains were generally rated as low risk in most studies, unclear or high risk of bias was identified in the patient selection and flow and timing domains for some studies. Therefore, the pooled diagnostic estimates should be interpreted in light of these potential sources of bias rather than as purely technical measures of model performance. The results of the quality assessment are presented in Fig. 2A and B.

Primary analysis: diagnostic meta-analysis of formal validation datasets

A total of four formal validation study records were included in the primary analysis. The forest plots showed that the pooled sensitivity and specificity of AI models for the diagnosis of HCC were 0.904 (95% CI: 0.845–0.942) and 0.971 (95% CI: 0.891–0.993), respectively, indicating favorable overall diagnostic performance at the formal validation level. Across individual studies, sensitivity ranged from 0.852 to 0.975, whereas specificity ranged from 0.915 to 1.000. Overall, AI models in the formal validation studies demonstrated both high discriminatory ability for identifying HCC and strong capability for excluding non-HCC cases. The results are shown in Fig. 3A and B.

Sensitivity analysis

On the basis of the primary analysis, development datasets and internal validation datasets were further incorporated, yielding a total of seven study records for the sensitivity analysis. The results showed that the pooled sensitivity and specificity of AI models for the diagnosis of HCC were 0.895 (95% CI: 0.831–0.936) and 0.935 (95% CI: 0.846–0.974), respectively. Compared with the primary analysis, both pooled sensitivity and pooled specificity were slightly lower in the sensitivity analysis, with specificity decreasing from 0.971 to 0.935 in particular. This finding suggests that the estimated model performance may be influenced by differences in validation level after inclusion of development and internal validation datasets. Across individual studies, sensitivity ranged from 0.808 to 0.975, whereas specificity ranged from 0.791 to 1.000, with a more dispersed distribution of results than that observed in the primary analysis. The results are shown in Fig. 4A and B.

Heterogeneity assessment and exploratory modality-based comparison

Quantitative heterogeneity was further assessed using Cochran’s Q test, I² statistics, and τ² estimates. In the primary analysis including four formal validation records, the Q statistic for sensitivity was 6.016 (df = 3, P = 0.111), with an I² of 50.1% and τ² of 0.214. For specificity, the Q statistic was 4.665 (df = 3, P = 0.198), with an I² of 35.7% and τ² of 0.695.

In the sensitivity analysis including seven all-HCC records with extractable 2 × 2 data, heterogeneity was more evident. For sensitivity, the Q statistic was 15.297 (df = 6, P = 0.018), with an I² of 60.8% and τ² of 0.254. For specificity, the Q statistic was 20.189 (df = 6, P = 0.003), with an I² of 70.3% and τ² of 0.789. These findings suggest moderate to substantial between-record heterogeneity, particularly after inclusion of development and internal validation records.

Exploratory comparison by data modality suggested that liquid biopsy/proteomics-based models generally showed high specificity, whereas imaging-based AI studies showed more variable diagnostic performance in the qualitative synthesis. However, because imaging-based records did not consistently provide extractable patient-level 2 × 2 data and the number of records within each modality subgroup was limited, no formal subgroup pooling or meta-regression was performed.

Sensitivity analysis using a single highest-validation-level record per study

To examine the potential influence of treating multiple records from the same study as independent observations, we conducted an additional sensitivity analysis using a single highest-validation-level record per study. In this analysis, one record was retained from each eligible study. For Xing et al., the HCC versus liver cirrhosis comparison was selected instead of the HCC versus healthy control comparison because liver cirrhosis represents a more clinically relevant comparator for HCC diagnosis. For Mahalingam et al. and Udomruk et al., the independent or external validation records were retained.

This study-level sensitivity analysis included three records. The pooled sensitivity and specificity were 0.889 and 0.964, respectively. These estimates were close to those obtained from the record-level primary analysis, in which the pooled sensitivity and specificity were 0.904 and 0.971, respectively. Therefore, restricting the analysis to a single highest-validation-level record per study did not materially change the overall interpretation. However, because only three independent study-level records were available, these results should be interpreted as exploratory.

Evidence for early HCC

In the early HCC subgroup, only one externally validated study record was currently available; therefore, no pooled meta-analysis was performed, and the result was presented descriptively as a single-study finding. That study reported a sensitivity of 0.88 and a specificity of 1.00 for AI-based identification of early HCC, suggesting potential diagnostic value in the early detection setting. However, given the limited amount of available evidence, these findings should be further confirmed by additional external validation studies. The result is shown in Supplementary Figure S1.

Overview of AUC values in the included studies

A summary of the study records with available AUC data showed that the AUC values of different AI models were generally high. In most study records, the AUC exceeded 0.88, and in some records it approached or exceeded 0.95, indicating overall good discriminative performance of AI models for the diagnosis of HCC. It should be noted that some studies reporting only AUC values, without sufficient information to extract 2 × 2 table data, were included only in the qualitative review and were not entered into the primary meta-analysis. An overview of AUC values is presented in Supplementary Figure S2.

Exploratory analysis according to QUADAS-2 risk-of-bias level

We further explored whether QUADAS-2 risk-of-bias ratings may have influenced the pooled diagnostic estimates. Because the number of records included in the primary analysis was very small, especially after stratification by individual QUADAS-2 domains, formal subgroup meta-analysis or meta-regression was not performed. Instead, diagnostic performance was descriptively compared according to whether records were rated as low risk or high/unclear risk in each QUADAS-2 domain.

The exploratory assessment suggested that records with high or unclear risk in the patient selection domain tended to report very high specificity, particularly when highly selected non-HCC controls or healthy controls were used. This pattern indicates that the high pooled specificity in the primary analysis may partly reflect patient selection and comparator selection rather than purely superior diagnostic discrimination of AI models. In contrast, the index test and reference standard domains showed less apparent variation across included records. For the flow and timing domain, several records were rated as unclear because the interval between the index test and reference standard, participant exclusions, or missing data handling was not sufficiently described. Therefore, the influence of flow and timing bias could not be fully excluded.

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the application value of AI models in the diagnosis of HCC and further performed stratified analyses according to validation level. The results showed that, in the primary analysis including only formal validation datasets, the pooled sensitivity and specificity of AI models for HCC diagnosis were 0.904 and 0.971, respectively, indicating both strong overall case identification ability and robust exclusion of non-HCC cases. After further inclusion of development datasets and internal validation datasets, the pooled sensitivity and specificity decreased to 0.895 and 0.935, respectively, suggesting that model performance varies to some extent across different validation levels. Overall, the current evidence suggests that AI models may have promising potential in HCC diagnosis. However, given the limited number of formally validated records and the heterogeneity across data modalities, reference standards, and validation designs, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence rather than definitive proof of clinical readiness. For early HCC, only one externally validated record was included in the present study. Although this study showed favorable diagnostic performance, the currently available evidence remains limited and is still insufficient to support firm conclusions.

The findings of the present study are generally consistent with those of related studies published in recent years, showing that AI models developed from different data modalities generally demonstrate good discriminative performance in the diagnosis of HCC. In the field of liquid biopsy, Xing et al. developed a serum proteomics-based P4 panel comprising HABP2, CD163, AFP, and PIVKA-II, which achieved high diagnostic performance in distinguishing HCC from liver cirrhosis and healthy controls in an independent validation cohort, suggesting that multi-protein combined modeling may provide stronger discriminative ability than traditional single biomarkers [19]. In the imaging domain, Yu et al. developed a deep learning model based on triphasic contrast-enhanced CT, which also showed favorable diagnostic performance in an independent external test set, indicating the promising application of AI in extracting complex imaging features and identifying lesions [20]. In addition, Wang et al. constructed a multimodal fusion model integrating clinical, imaging, and peripheral immune features, and this model demonstrated high performance in both internal and external validation across multicenter cohorts, further supporting the potential of integrating heterogeneous multi-source data to improve the diagnostic accuracy of HCC [21]. Notably, in 2026, Udomruk et al. developed the CEliver model based on cfDNA fragmentomics, which also achieved high sensitivity and specificity in external validation and showed promising performance for the identification of early-stage HCC [22]. Collectively, these studies are in line with the overall findings of the present analysis, suggesting that AI has the potential to enhance the auxiliary diagnostic performance for HCC, whether based on liquid biopsy information such as proteomics and cfDNA or on medical imaging modalities such as CT. Meanwhile, by further stratifying the evidence according to validation level, our study found that the pooled estimates derived from formally validated datasets were more robust than those from development and internal validation datasets. This finding further suggests that the clinical translational value of AI diagnostic models should not be judged solely on the basis of development-stage or internal validation results. The originality and clinical reference value of the present study should be interpreted from the perspective of evidence synthesis rather than from the development of a new surgical technique or a single prediction model. Unlike individual retrospective cohorts or single-model studies, which usually evaluate one algorithm, one data source, or one institutional population, this review systematically summarized AI-based diagnostic evidence across multiple data modalities, including serum proteomics, mass spectrometry/proteomics, cfDNA fragmentomics, medical imaging, and multimodal clinical-imaging-biomarker approaches. This broader evidence synthesis helps clarify whether the promising performance reported in individual studies is consistent across different technical pathways.

Another distinctive feature of the present study is the emphasis on validation level. Many AI diagnostic studies report excellent performance in development or internal validation datasets, but these estimates may not reflect real-world generalizability. Therefore, we prespecified formal validation datasets as the primary analytic population and incorporated development and internal validation datasets only in the sensitivity analysis. In addition, we performed a study-level sensitivity analysis retaining a single highest-validation-level record per study and explored the influence of QUADAS-2 risk-of-bias domains. This validation-oriented framework provides a more cautious and clinically interpretable assessment than simply pooling all available model-development results. Accordingly, the main academic value of the present work is to provide a structured reference for interpreting AI diagnostic performance in HCC and to identify the methodological conditions needed for future clinical translation, including external validation, clinically relevant comparator groups, complete patient-level 2 × 2 data, and transparent reporting of model thresholds and participant flow. An important consideration in interpreting the present findings is the methodological heterogeneity across the included records. The formal validation records with extractable 2 × 2 data differed in data modality, including serum proteomics, serum mass spectrometry/proteomics, and cfDNA fragmentomics. In the broader qualitative synthesis, additional imaging-based AI models were also identified, including CT- and MRI-based approaches. These studies differed not only in data source but also in reference standard, target population, diagnostic setting, and validation strategy.

Therefore, the pooled sensitivity and specificity should not be interpreted as the expected diagnostic performance of a single unified AI model or a single clinical testing strategy. Rather, they should be viewed as a preliminary quantitative summary of currently available formal validation evidence with extractable diagnostic 2 × 2 data. The broader evidence base suggests that AI models for HCC diagnosis are promising, but substantial heterogeneity limits direct comparability across studies.

Another methodological issue concerns the extraction of multiple records from the same study. Although record-level extraction allowed us to preserve information from different validation datasets and diagnostic scenarios, records derived from the same study may share the same training data distribution, algorithmic workflow, study design, and participant selection process. Therefore, they are not fully statistically independent. To examine the potential influence of this issue, we performed an additional sensitivity analysis using a single highest-validation-level record per study. The pooled sensitivity and specificity were 0.889 and 0.964, respectively, which were close to the primary record-level estimates of 0.904 and 0.971. This suggests that the main interpretation was not materially altered by the record-splitting strategy. Nevertheless, because the number of independent studies remained very small, the findings should still be interpreted cautiously.

The promising performance of AI models in HCC diagnosis may be attributable to their ability to extract latent features from high-dimensional and heterogeneous data that are difficult for conventional approaches to capture consistently. On the one hand, imaging-based AI can identify enhancement patterns, lesion margin characteristics, textural heterogeneity, and the complex relationships between lesions and the surrounding liver parenchyma, thereby improving the detection of atypical lesions and borderline abnormalities [23–25]. On the other hand, AI models based on biomarkers such as proteomics, mass spectrometry, cfDNA, and exosomes are able to integrate abnormal signals across multiple molecular layers and may therefore reflect the biological changes associated with tumor initiation and progression more comprehensively than traditional single markers such as AFP [26]. Furthermore, multimodal fusion strategies can simultaneously incorporate clinical variables, imaging features, and molecular biomarker data, and use the advantages of nonlinear modeling to capture interactions among different variables. In theory, this framework is better aligned with the diagnostic demands of HCC, a highly heterogeneous disease. However, because model training is inherently dependent on specific data distributions and sample characteristics, performance in external populations is often influenced by factors such as data drift, equipment variation, case-mix differences, and threshold settings. This may partly explain why the findings derived from the formal validation level deserve greater emphasis in the present study.

Several limitations should be emphasized. First, only four formal validation records were available for the primary bivariate meta-analysis. In diagnostic test accuracy meta-analysis, bivariate random-effects models jointly estimate sensitivity and specificity and require estimation of a between-record variance–covariance structure. When the number of included records is very small, this estimation may be unstable, which can lead to imprecise pooled estimates and potentially unreliable confidence intervals. Therefore, although a bivariate random-effects model was used to provide a quantitative summary, the pooled sensitivity and specificity from the primary analysis should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as preliminary evidence rather than definitive estimates of real-world clinical performance.

Second, the included records were clinically and methodologically heterogeneous in terms of data modality, reference standard, study population, and validation design. Although we supplemented the analysis with Cochran’s Q tests, I² statistics, τ² estimates, and exploratory modality-based comparisons, the small number of records limited our ability to perform robust subgroup analyses or meta-regression. Future diagnostic meta-analyses should be updated when more externally validated AI models with complete patient-level 2 × 2 data become available.

Third, some study records were derived from different validation datasets or analytical scenarios within the same original study. Such records may share the same training data distribution, algorithmic workflow, study design, and participant selection process, and therefore are not fully statistically independent. Treating them as independent records may introduce a unit-of-analysis issue and may inflate the effective sample size or underestimate between-study heterogeneity. Although we conducted an additional sensitivity analysis using a single highest-validation-level record per study and found similar pooled estimates, this limitation cannot be fully eliminated because of the small number of available studies.

Fourth, patient selection bias may have influenced the pooled specificity. Several included records were derived from case-control or highly selected validation settings, and some comparisons involved healthy controls or selected non-HCC controls rather than consecutive real-world surveillance populations. Such designs may overestimate specificity by making HCC and non-HCC groups more clearly separable than they would be in routine clinical practice. Although we explored QUADAS-2 risk-of-bias domains descriptively, the limited number of records prevented robust subgroup pooling or meta-regression by risk-of-bias level. Therefore, the high pooled specificity should be interpreted cautiously and may not be directly generalizable to real-world HCC screening or diagnostic pathways.

Conclusion

Current evidence suggests that AI models may show promising diagnostic performance for HCC. However, because the primary analysis was based on only four formal validation records with substantial differences in data modality, reference standard, study population, and validation design, the pooled estimates should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating. More high-quality, multicenter, externally validated studies are required before the clinical utility of AI models for HCC diagnosis can be firmly established.

In summary, the available evidence suggests that AI models may have promising potential as auxiliary diagnostic tools for HCC. However, the primary pooled estimates were derived from only four formal validation records and should be interpreted cautiously because of between-record heterogeneity and statistical instability related to the small number of records. Further high-quality, multicenter, prospective external validation studies are needed to clarify the real-world clinical utility of AI models for HCC diagnosis, particularly for early HCC. Therefore, greater attention should be paid to validation level when assessing the performance of AI diagnostic models. Further high-quality, multicenter external validation studies are still needed to better clarify the true clinical utility of different data modalities and AI strategies in the diagnosis of HCC, particularly early HCC.
