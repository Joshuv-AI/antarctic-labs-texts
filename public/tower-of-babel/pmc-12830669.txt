Structural, optical and dielectric properties of Cobalt incorporated Zinc oxide nanostructures: doping effect of Cobalt
Authors: Muhammad Usman, Sohail Ahmad, Ahmed M. Zidan, Abdulkafi Mohammed Saeed, Ahmed Shafee, Nedal M. Mohammed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This research studies the synthesis of pure (undoped) and Cobalt-doped Zinc oxide nanostructures used the co-precipitation synthesis. Both the Pure and Cobalt-doped Zinc oxide nanostructures exhibit a consistent hexagonal symmetry in their X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) micrographs reveal that Zinc oxide, in both its pure and Cobalt-doped forms, appears rod shaped. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) confirms the presence of only zinc and oxygen in pure Zinc oxide samples, while doped samples show the presence of Cobalt, zinc, and oxygen. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis indicates various stretching vibrations within the Zinc Oxide structure. The calculated band gap energy of pure Zinc oxide is 2.74 eV. While for Zn0.98Co0.02O, Zn0.96Co0.04O, Zn0.94Co0.06O Cobalt doped nanostructures the band gap energies are 2.69 eV, 2.63 eV, and 2.38 eV, respectively. As the number of Cobalt increases, so does the strength of the UV-Vis absorption spectrum. Our samples exhibit a near-band transition when stimulated by a wavelength of 233 nm. A first peak at 398 nm and a second peak at 558 nm in the photoluminescence (PL) spectra signify the presence of defects. High dielectric constant values are obtained using Cobalt-doped Zinc oxide nanostructures, indicating dispersion behavior. The substantial percentage of oxygen vacancies within the Cobalt-doped Zinc Oxide nanostructure results in enhanced electrical conductivity.

Introduction

Specifically, nanostructures based on transition metal doped semi-conductors have garnered a great deal of attention because of their unique optical characteristics and possible uses in optoelectronic devices. such as UV detectors1, field effect transistors2, short wavelength lasers3, sensors4, Solar cells5, catalysts6, display devices7, Photo electrochemical8, photocatalysis9, light emitting devices10, visitors11. Zinc Oxide has gained more study attention than any other semiconductor because it exhibits a broad band gap in the near UV spectral range when measured at ambient temperature. Zinc oxide, commonly known as Zinc Oxide, is a type of semi- conductor that belongs to the II-VI group. This material likewise has a Wurtzite crystal structure, a direct and broadband excitation energy of 3.37 eV at room temperature, a significant exciton binding energy of 60 meV at room temperature, and a high optical gain12,13. Wurtzite Zinc Oxide has been used in a range of applications since it was found, including dye-sensitized solar cells14–16 and solid-state lighting, amongst others17. Other characteristics of Zinc Oxide that set it apart from other materials are determined by its size, shape, and various morphologies. These morphologies include nanowire18, Nano belts19, Nano rods20, and Nano flower21. There are different type of Zinc oxide Nano crystal such as 1-D44, 2-D45 and 3-D46.

Investigating the effects of transition metal doping on Zinc Oxide nanostructures might be beneficial to both basic and applied research for a number of reasons. In order to construct optoelectronics and spintronics devices at room temperature, a large number of scientists and researchers have concentrated their attention on the process of transition metal (TM) doping on host semiconductor Zinc nanostructures. Doping, which is generally acknowledged as a method that is effective, can be used to control and improve the optical, structural, magnetic, and electric properties of the Zinc – material that serves as the host. Due to the incomplete nature of their d states, a significant amount of focus has been placed on doping with 3 d transition metal (TM) ions22. It was the presence of unpaired electrons in these partially filled d states that was responsible for the magnetic behavior that was seen. Doping a material with 3 d transition metal (TM) ions can change the energy band gap and, consequently, the optical properties of the material. This is accomplished by increasing the maximum of the valence band and decreasing the minimum of the conduction band. The magnetic and optical properties of Zinc – nanostructures can be improved using a variety of techniques, such as the addition of metal ions, the attachment of surfactants, and the sintering process. These techniques can be combined to achieve the desired effect23,24.

AC measures, such as conductance, capacitance, dielectric constant, and dielectric loss tangent, can be used to investigate the dynamic properties of semiconducting and dielectric materials. The said data can be utilized to gain a deeper understanding of the structure of the material, particularly in the region where it has a low conductivity. Because of its high excitonic binding energy (60 meV), which is reliant on the dielectric constant of the materials, Zinc – is a promising candidate in the field of optoelectronics. This is because the dielectric constant of the materials influences the excitonic binding energy. In recent years, impedance spectroscopy has emerged as a useful instrument for the electrical characterization of materials. Furthermore, it offers a very sophisticated experimental approach to the investigation of transport in the bulk as well as at the interfaces of grains25. The electric charge carriers that make up a dielectric substance are structured in such a way that they are able to be shifted when an external electric field is applied to the substance. Materials with a high dielectric constant are used in a wide variety of different sorts of microelectronics. One of the most important characteristics of materials is their dielectric behavior, which is extremely sensitive to the conditions that were present during the material’s preparation. Because of the unique dielectric features it possesses, the Zinc – system has been the focus of a significant amount of research26,27.

There are many different ways to produce Zinc – nanoparticles, such as using chemical and physical methods28, the hydrothermal process29,30, the sol-gel method31, solvothermal method32, co-precipitation method33,34. The co-precipitation method has seen a lot of use in the synthesis of Zinc – nanoparticles due to the fact that it produces a high yield of nanoparticles, has a low cost, and has excellent uniformity35. Co-precipitation is utilized in the present investigation to synthesis both pure and co-doped Zinc – nanostructures. The optical and dielectric properties of pure Zinc – and cobalt doped Zinc – nanostructures have been studied.

Experimental section

Chemicals

Zinc acetate [Zn (CH3COO) 2H2O], potassium hydr– (KOH], Cobalt (II) chloride hexahydrate [CoCl2.6H2O] were purchased from (Sigma Aldrich, 99%). All the chemicals in the experiment were used as traditional without further purification. All the chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich with 99% purity.

Sample preparation

In the experiments detailed herein, zinc acetate dehydrates [Zn (CH3COO) 2.2H2O] (2.202 g) (as a Zn supply), potassium hydr– (KOH) (1.125 g) (Sigma Aldrich 99%), and deionized water (100 ml) were utilized. The precursors for the production of Pure and co-doped Zinc – nanostructures were zinc acetate di hydrate, potassium hydr– and Cobalt (II) chloride hexahydrate [CoCl2.6H2O]. First, the required amounts of zinc acetate di hydrate (Sigma Aldrich, 99%) and potassium hydr–s (Sigma Aldrich 99%) were dissolved in deionized water. The KOH (10 ml) solution was added drop wise while vigorously stirring at room temperature to different concentrations of Cobalt and zinc acetate di hydrate and then Pure and Co doped Zinc – samples were papered in this manner. The reactions were carried out at 70 ◦C for 3 h on hotplate and washed by deionized water after that the samples is washed with help of centrifuge and dried at room temperature at 70 ◦C overnight. The Zinc – powder is obtained. Then after that the required powder was annealed at 600 ◦C for 4 h. The Synthesis diagram for pure and Cobalt doped Zinc – nanostructures is shown in Fig. 1. With increasing Cobalt concentration variation in color is seen which is due to Cobalt concentration as shown in Fig. 2.

Characterization techniques

The synthesized Zinc – (Zinc –) nanostructures were subjected to various characterization techniques to analyze their structural, morphological, optical, and electrical properties.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

X-ray power diffraction (XRD) studies were done on a Bruker, D8 ADVANCE XRD diffraction spectrometer with a CuKα.5406 and a Ni filter for an angle range of 2θ = 20–80 to characterize the Zinc – nanoparticles.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The morphology of the Zinc – nanostructures was examined using a Philips XL30 scanning electron microscope (SEM) operated at an accelerating voltage of 17 kV. This technique provided high- resolution images of the particle size, shape, and surface features.

UV-visible spectroscopy

The optical properties of the samples were characterized using a Shimadzu MPC-2200 UV-vis spectrophotometer with a resolution of 2.0 nm. The absorbance spectra were recorded within a wave- length range of 350 nm to 600 nm. This analysis provided insights into the optical band gap and light absorption behavior of the Zinc – nanostructures.

Photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy

The photoluminescence (PL) emission spectra of the Zinc – samples were measured at room temperature using a 325 nm He-Cd laser beam as the excitation source. A spectrometer with a resolution of 1.0 nm was employed to record the emission spectra. This technique helped to understand the radiative recombination processes and defect states within the Zinc – nanostructures.

Dielectric spectroscopy

The dielectric properties of the pure Zinc – and cobalt doped zinc – samples were investigated using a Hioki 3532-50 Hi-Tester. Measurements were conducted at various temperatures and frequencies ranging from 200 Hz to 5 MHz. This analysis provided information on the electrical permittivity, dielectric constant, and loss tangent of the pure Zinc – and cobalt doped zinc – nanostructures samples.

Sample preparation for dielectric measurement

The acquired sample was then crushed into fine powder and pelletized hydraulic press at a pressure of 5 tons for 3 min to achieve maximum densification.

The pellet was 10 mm in diameter and 1 mm thick. It was sintered at 500 ◦C in a furnace for 1 h before being progressively cooled to room temperature to eliminate voids developed during the palletization process. Conducting electrodes were silver pellets on both sides of the sample. The dielectric measurements were made with a Hioki 3532-50 Hi-Tester at room temperatures and frequencies ranging from 200 Hz to 5 MHz.

Results and discussion

X-ray diffraction analysis

The crystal structure of the pure and cobalt doped zinc – synthesized samples was investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectroscopy. The analysis employs Cu-kα radiation to generate diffraction patterns that reveal the structural characteristics of the samples. The results confirm the presence of a wurtzite hexagonal structure in both Pure Zinc – and Cobalt-doped Zinc – nanostructures as shown in Fig. 3. The observed diffraction peaks closely match the reference data from the Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS) card no. 01–075-0576, indicating high sample purity and the absence of any additional contaminant phases arising from the preparation process. The crystal phase of Cobalt –s (Co3O4)36, metallic Cobalt (Co)37, or Cobalt hydr– (Co(OH)2) were detected within the measurement sensitivity limits. This suggests that the majority of Cobalt ions are successfully incorporated into the wurtzite crystal lattice of Zinc –38.

The intensity of the diffracted peaks exhibits an interesting trend with increasing Cobalt doping concentration. As the Co content is increased from 0 wt% to 4 wt% (Zn0.96Co0.04O), the peak intensity strengthens. However, a further increase to 6% Co (Zn0.94Co0.06O) results in a decrease in peak intensity, as shown in Fig. 2. This observation, along with the reduced peak intensities for the sample 6 wt% Co (Zn0.94Co0.06O), suggests the successful substitution of Zn+ 2 (ionic radius: 0.60 Å) by Co+ 2 ions (ionic radius: 0.58 Å) within the Zinc – crystal lattice. The comparable ionic radii of Zn + 2 and Co + 2 explain the minimal changes observed in the Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) of the diffraction peaks. Photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy

PL spectroscopy is a valuable tool for investigating defects in semi-conductors, both those originating from the material itself and those introduced by the environment. It also provides insights into the material’s optical properties. Figure 9 shows the absorption peaks for Pure Zinc Oxide at 423 nm, 470 nm, 486 nm, and 558 nm. These peaks correspond to emission from excitons. The peak at 413 nm results from an electron transitioning from the valence band of zinc, causing a violet emission. The blue emission with a peak at 470 nm is likely caused by electrons moving from the shallow donor level, created by oxygen vacancies, to the valence band43. The blue-green emission peak at 486 nm might be attributed to an ionized oxygen vacancy in the valence band43. The blue-green emission peak at 486 nm might be attributed to an ionized oxygen vacancy in the valence band. The green emission peak at 558 nm is attributed to the transfer of electrons from the deep level donor of ionized oxygen vacancies to the valence band44. While there is a weak near-band emission in our samples, a clear UV peak is absent. This suggests a high concentration of defect states or impurities in our samples, significantly impacting the crystal’s luminescence. These defects and impurities act as scattering centers, reducing the lifespan of free excitons and consequently lowering the emission intensity. As a result, the absence of a UV peak indicates that imperfections or impurities function as non-radiative recombination centers. The presence of distinct vacancies was confirmed by the PL emission spectra. It is evident that the doped samples with Zn0.98Co0.02O Co exhibit the fewest defects, as evidenced by their relatively weak emission spectra conversely, the doped sample with Zn0.94Co0.06O Co shows the most defects, as evidenced by its strong emission spectrum (Fig. 7). These observations are consistent with the UV-visible absorption spectra. Additionally, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) examination reveals that an increase in Co concentration hinders the growth of the Zn0.94Co0.06O sample into rods. Dielectric properties

Dielectric constant

Figure 10a illustrates the frequency dependence of the dielectric constant (εr) for all samples measured at room temperature. The dielectric constant shows a decreasing trend as the frequency of the applied electric field increases. Variations in the dielectric constant are closely linked to the spatial polarization of charges. At lower frequencies, the dipoles influenced by the external field accumulate at the grain boundaries, leading to Space Charge Polarization (SCP). This phenomenon is confirmed by SEM examination, which reveals structural inhomogeneities in the Zinc Oxide Nano rods. Due to their high surface to volume ratio, Nano rods have a variety of surface defects, including dangling bonds, vacancies, and micro pores at the grain boundaries. These surface defects influence the distribution of positive and negative space charges at interfaces. When an external electric field is applied, these space charges move and become trapped by imperfections at the interfaces, resulting in the formation of numerous dipole moments. These structural in homogeneities capture mobile electrons at low frequencies. Therefore, Space Charge Polarization (SCP) dominates at low frequencies. Orientational polarization becomes the dominant polarization process at frequencies exceeding 10 kHz. This orientational polarization arises from the rotational motion of dipoles within the Nano rods. The presence of numerous oxygen vacancies and zinc interstitials in the Nano rods could be a potential source of orientational polarization. Dielectric loss

Figure 10b displays the variation in dielectric loss ε′′ with frequency. The data shows a significant decrease in dielectric loss as the frequency of the applied electric field increases. Minimizing space charge polarization is crucial for reducing dielectric loss at higher frequencies. The observed trend of decreasing dielectric loss with increasing frequency might be linked to space charge polarization (SCP). It is theorized that dielectric loss, influenced by the resonance of domain walls, forces a rotational shift in the polarization. Overall, losses are minimal at higher frequencies. This behavior could be explained by a reduction in the movement (velocity) of grain boundaries. Due to their low dielectric loss and constant values at high frequencies, Cobalt-doped Zinc Oxide Samples are promising candidates for high frequency device applications. Electrical conductivity

Figure 11 compares the AC conductivity (σac) of Pure and Cobalt-doped Zinc Oxide nanostructures across various frequencies. As expected from previous studies, σac increases with frequency, particularly at high frequencies. This translates to a significant rise in conductivity at higher frequencies45. The transition in conductivity begins at relatively low frequencies and becomes more pronounced at higher frequencies. The Maxwell-Wagner (MW) interfacial model explains the observed conductivity behavior. Grain boundaries and defects within the material can act as obstacles for charge carriers traveling through the grains. These barriers impede the movement of charge carriers until a certain frequency is reached. However, as the frequency of the external field increases, a higher number of carriers can overcome these boundaries, leading to an increase in conductivity. Doping Zinc Oxide with Cobalt introduces a significant number of charge carriers, resulting in a substantial increase in conductivity. The AC electrical conductivity σac = 0 for both pure and Cobalt-doped Zinc Oxide samples can be represented by the equation σac = ε0εrω where ε0 is the permittivity of free space, εr is the imaginary component of the permittivity, and ω = 2π is the angular frequency. Since only the imaginary part of the permittivity (dielectric loss) affects AC conductivity, higher frequencies result in lower dielectric loss and consequently, higher AC conductivity. The increasing trend of σac with frequency is evident in the Fig. 11a. An increase in the Cobalt concentration leads to more oxygen vacancies, which in turn translates to a higher density of free charge carriers and enhanced conductivity.

Conclusion

This research article used the co-precipitation method to synthesize both pure and Cobalt-doped Zinc Oxide nanostructures. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis confirmed the hexagonal crystal structure of the fabricated samples. The crystallinity of the materials exhibited an initial rise with increasing Cobalt doping concentration, from pure Zinc oxide to Zn0.96Co0.04O. However, a decrease in crystallinity was observed for the Zn0.94Co0.06O Cobalt-doped sample. The crystallite size followed a similar trend, increasing from 10.79 nm for Pure Zinc oxide to 10.87 nm and 12.50 nm for Zn0.98Co0.02O and Zn0.96Co0.04O respectively, before dropping to 9.24 nm for the Zn0.94Co0.06O sample. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed a rod-like morphology for the synthesized nanostructures. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analysis confirmed the presence of Zinc (Zn), Oxygen (O), and Cobalt (Co) without any detectable contaminants. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) data indicated the presence of Co-O bonds between 2300 and 2400 cm− 1, while Zinc oxide stretching vibrations were observed at 560 cm− 1. UV-Visible spectroscopy investigations demonstrated an increase in Cobalt concentration with a corresponding rise in the absorption spectra. The calculated optical band gap values were 2.74 eV for Pure Zinc oxide, decreasing to 2.69 eV, 2.63 eV, and 2.38 eV for Zn0.98Co0.02O, Zn0.96Co0.04O, and Zn0.94Co0.06O Cobalt-doped samples, respectively. Photoluminescence (PL) spectra displayed a prominent emission peak at 328 nm attributed to the near-band edge emission. Additionally, a peak at 558 nm was observed, likely due to material defects when excited at a wave- length of 233 nm. The high dielectric constant observed in the low-frequency range can be attributed to the presence of minor defects. As the frequency increases, the lagging of charge carriers leads to a dsecrease in the dielectric constant. At sufficiently high frequencies, the dielectric constant becomes independent of frequency. Cobalt-doped Zinc oxide holds promise for various applications, including the development of cost-effective and efficient solar cells, fuel cells, photo electrochemical devices, and photocatalysis46,47. It also plays a significant role in the fields of spintronics, medicine, and photoluminescence. Notably, Co-doped Zinc oxide nanoparticles have shown potential in antibacterial applications47,48.

Novelty of this work

This research exhibits novelty through the systematic exploration of the optical and dielectric characteristics of coprecipitation route synthesized Co-doped Zinc oxide nanostructures with an interest to correlate cobalt concentration with material behavior. The findings show that 4% Co is an optimal doping level to achieve the best crystallinity and dielectric constant, whereas higher dopant (6%) causes structural defects and increasing oxygen vacancies, resulting in higher electrical conductivity. The overall structural, optical, and dielectric analysis, particularly the reduction observed for band gap and the contribution toward dielectric and associated conduction mechanisms due to oxygen vacancies, allows for the introduction of novel Zinc oxide materials with tenability for next-generation electronic and optoelectronic applications.
