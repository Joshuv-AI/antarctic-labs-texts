The impact of psychological factors on fertility intention among women who have given birth in Shanghai: multiple mediating effects analysis based on the theory of planned behavior
Authors: Taiqing Luo, Qi Tang, Jun Lu, Yelin Li, Anni Wang, Fanlei Kong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe declining fertility rate posed significant risks to social sustainability and development. However, few studies have explored the psychological factors and their potential mediating effects on the fertility intention for another child. Based on the theory of planned behavior (TPB), this study aimed to investigate the relationships among subjective norm (SN), perceived behavioral control (PBC), attitude (AT), and fertility intention (FI) for another child.MethodsA multicenter cross-sectional survey was conducted from November to December 2022, using a sample of 455 women who had given birth from 3 districts in Shanghai, China. Structural equation modeling was employed to validate the proposed model. Multi-group analysis was conducted to explore differences across groups with different number of children.ResultsPBC (β = 0.212) and AT (β = 0.183) had positive direct effects on FI, while SN had a negative direct effect on FI (β=-0.123). PBC (β=-0.057) and AT (β=-0.035) mediated the impact of SN on FI respectively, functioning as sequential mediators (β=-0.028). The mediating effect of SN on FI accounts for 49.38% of the total effect. Among women with one child group, the path coefficient from PBC to FI was larger than that for women with multiple children (CR = -2.012).ConclusionsSN exerted both direct and indirect negative effects on FI through the multiple mediating effects of PBC and AT, which also acted as sequential mediators. PBC had the strongest direct effect, compared with SN and AT. The effect from PBC to FI for women with one child was stronger than that for women with multiple children. Policy recommendations were provided to increase fertility intentions based on these results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-025-02252-2.Keywords: Fertility intention, The theory of planned behavior, Subjective norm, Perceived behavioral control, Attitude, Mediation

Background

Fertility intention refers to the extent to which individuals are willing or planning to make efforts to take action toward achieving a certain reproductive behavior [1]. It is widely recognized as a key predictor of actual fertility behavior and overall fertility levels [1, 2]. The decline in fertility rate has become an inevitable trend all over the world, particularly in developed countries [3]. In China, the total fertility rate fell to 1.3 in 2020 [4], below the international warning line of 1.5 [5]. In 2024, the total fertility rate among Shanghai’s registered population dropped to 0.72 [6], highlighting a severe demographic challenge. This trend is likely to give rise to a host of social issues, including a growing elderly population and a shrinking workforce of young and middle-aged individuals [7].

In response to the challenges of population aging and declining fertility rates, China has gradually adjusted its fertility policies to relax fertility restrictions and enhance supporting measures. The government introduced a series of policies from the “Conditional Two-Child Policy” (allowing couples in which at least one of the partners was a single child to have two children) in 2013 to the “Universal Two-Child Policy” (allowing all couples to have two children) in 2016 and then to the “Three-Child Policy” (allowing all couples to have up to three children) in 2021 [8]. These policies primarily targeted women who already had one or more children, with the goal of encouraging them to have additional children [9, 10]. However, the effectiveness of these measures appeared to be limited, as the birth rate continued to decline, from 8.82‰ in 2020 to 6.77‰ in 2024 [11, 12]. By 2023, the total fertility rate had declined to approximately 1.0 [13].

Given that low fertility rates are closely linked with low fertility intention, increasing attention has been paid to the direct effects of demographic, sociological, and economic factors on fertility intention [14–22]. However, most research failed to provide a comprehensive understanding of the internal mechanisms shaping the fertility intention. In addition, previous studies have highlighted significant differences in fertility intentions between childless women and mothers [23]. Research on fertility intention for additional children is particularly critical for countries experiencing low fertility rate, especially as fertility rate continued to decline in more and more countries worldwide [24]. Therefore, it is essential to clarify the factors influencing fertility intention for subsequent children among parous women and to provide valuable insights into addressing fertility challenges.

The theory of planned behavior (TPB) is a popular social and psychological model of human intention and behavior. It was first proposed by Ajzen, building on the theory of reasoned action [25]. According to TPB, intention is primarily influenced by three key variables: attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control. The strength of intention is positively correlated with the likelihood of it translating into actual behavior. Subjective norm is the perceived social pressure from important individuals or organizations to perform or not perform a certain behavior. Attitude refers to an individual’s subjective evaluation of the positive or negative outcomes that a certain behavior may bring them. Perceived behavioral control refers to the individual’s perception of the ease or difficulty of performing a particular behavior.

According to TPB, these three factors-attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control-directly influence intention [1] (Fig. 1). Building on this foundation, recent studies have focused on the relationships between these three factors and the mechanisms through which they shape intention, thus expanding the theory. Some studies have explored the mediating effects of perceived behavioral control and attitude between subjective norm and intention. A study using an extended planned behavior model to explain the willingness to pay to reduce noise pollution hypothesized and verified subjective norm indirectly influences the intention through attitudes and perceived behavioral control [26]. Anderson demonstrated that personal attitudes and perceived behavioral control mediated or partially mediated the relationships between subjective norms and entrepreneurial intentions [27]. Moreover, a small but growing body of research has focused on the chain mediation model of perceived behavioral control and attitude. A study predicting nurses’ intention to undertake dual practice found that subjective norm could affect intention indirectly through the sequential mediation of perceived behavioral control and attitude [28]. Fawehinmi et al. identified the sequential mediating effects of perceived behavioral control and attitude between green human resource management and employee green behavior [29]. External opinions, support, and pressure could significantly influence an individual’s perception and judgment of their ability to perform a certain behavior and their attitude toward it. When external support and assistance is positive, individuals are more likely to feel confident in their ability to complete the behavior and perceive favorable outcomes from it [26].

The theory of planned behavior applied to fertility decisions

Based on the above, the hypotheses of this study were proposed as illustrated in Fig. 2 (as follow).

Research hypothesis model

Hypothesis 1

Subjective norm, attitude, and perceived behavioral control are positively associated with fertility intention.

Hypothesis 2

Perceived behavioral control and attitude play a mediating role in the relationship between subjective norm and fertility intention.

Hypothesis 3

Perceived behavioral control and attitude sequentially mediate the relationship between subjective norm and fertility intention.

Existing research has found that fertility intention and influencing factors vary based on the number of children women already have [23, 30, 31]. After childbirth, women’s experiences of pregnancy, childbirth, and child-rearing may shape their subjective norm, attitude, and perceived behavioral control, subsequently influencing their fertility intention. This suggests that the mechanism by which the TPB influences fertility intention may differ depending on the number of children a woman has.

Therefore, from a social psychological perspective, based on TPB, this study aimed to investigate the relationship among subjective norm, perceived behavioral control, attitude, and fertility intention of women who have given birth. Specifically, it explored the sequential mediating effects of perceived behavioral control and attitude. Furthermore, this study also examined how the mechanisms these three factors influenced fertility intentions may differ across groups with different number of children.

Methods

Study design and sample

This multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted from November to December 2022 in Shanghai, China. Considering the influence of economic development and geographical location, simple random sampling was employed to select one central urban district, one inner suburban district, and one outer suburban district from the 16 districts of Shanghai as the sample regions. Due to the lack of a full sampling list, convenience sampling was then used to distribute questionnaires to eligible participants from maternal and child health care centers in the sample area.

The inclusion criteria for participants were as follows: women aged 20–45 years; having at least one living child; residing in Shanghai for more than three years; no history of severe physical or mental diseases; and voluntary to participate in this study. Participants who were pregnant or had undergone permanent sterilization were excluded. Participants were well-informed and had the right to refuse or quit the study at any point.

There are different guidelines for calculating the sample size required for structural equation modeling (SEM). Generally, approximately 200 samples are considered appropriate for such analyses. Kline suggests that the sample size should be proportional to the number of free parameters, with each free parameter requiring 5–10 samples [32]. In the model used in this study, there are a total of 36 free parameters, which means the minimum required sample size should be 180. Based on these criteria, the minimum sample size should be 200.

The study received ethical approval from the Medical Research Ethics Committee of the School of Public Health, Fudan University, China (Grant Number: IRB#2019-10-0783).

Data collection

The survey questionnaire consisted of three sections (Supplementary File 1): (1) an introduction to the study purpose, (2) demographic and socioeconomic information (e.g., age, education level, and number of children), and (3) items assessing participants’ perceptions of constructs in the proposed model. The third section was developed based on established TPB-related literature and scales, adapted to the study context. The questionnaire contained 15 items: four measuring subjective norm, six measuring perceived behavioral control, four measuring attitude, and one measuring fertility intention. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from (1) strongly disagree to (5) strongly agree (see Table 1). Prior to the formal survey, the questionnaire was pilot tested to refine its details further.

Items of constructs

The investigators, who were familiar with the study background, objectives, target population, and questionnaire content, distributed the survey with assistance from doctors at maternal and child health care centers. The questionnaire was edited using Questionnaire Star, a widely used online survey platform in China, and was accessed by participants via QR code or WeChat link. The survey took approximately 5 min to complete, and the platform automatically flagged any missing responses before submission. To ensure data quality, each IP address and WeChat account was limited to one submission. In total, 500 questionnaires were collected, of which 455 were valid, yielding an effective response rate of 91.0%.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed in SPSS 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and AMOS 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants. Internal reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s α and composite reliability, employing a criterion of α and composite reliability values > 0.70. Convergent validity was evaluated based on the average variance extracted (AVE) and factor loadings, with an AVE threshold of at least 0.50 and a factor loading threshold of at least 0.70. Discriminant validity was examined using the square root of the AVE and a cross-loading matrix. For satisfactory discriminant validity, the square root of the AVE for each construct should be greater than the correlation coefficients between constructs [32].

The chi-square (χ2), degrees of freedom (df), χ2/df, incremental fit index (IFI), comparative fit index (CFI), goodness-of-fit index (GFI), adjusted goodness of fit index (AGFI), normed fit index (NFI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) were employed as indicators of model fit [32]. χ2/df < 5, values of IFI, CFI, GFI, and NFI greater than 0.90, and value of AGFI greater than 0.80 are considered to reflect a good model fit. RMSEA value less than 0.05 suggests good fit, while value up to 0.08 indicates reasonable errors of approximation. To make the results more reliable, the bias-corrected bootstrapping with 1,000 subsamples was used to compute the 95% confidence interval (CI) and p value [32].

Multi-group analysis was conducted to examine differences and similarities across subgroups. Considering the sample size, the data were categorized into two subgroups: one group with one child and another with multiple children [37]. The change in RMSEA (ΔRMSEA) and the change in CFI (ΔCFI) were used to assess the measurement invariance for the comparison of the less restricted model with the more constrained model, and ΔCFI < 0.010 and ΔRMSEA < 0.015 imply that measurement invariance has been successfully established [38]. The critical ratios (CR) of differences between parameters were calculated to evaluate group differences in regression coefficients for each path [39, 40]. The significance level for all analyses was set as p < 0.05.

Using the maximum likelihood method, the research model exhibited an ideal goodness of fit, with all fitness indices meeting practical criteria or threshold values (χ2 = 307.298, df = 140, χ2/df = 2.195, IFI = 0.972, CFI = 0.972, GFI = 0.936, AGFI = 0.936, NFI = 0.949, RMSEA = 0.051), indicating that the data fitted our conceptual research model very well.

Discussion

The impact of subjective norm on fertility intention

Subjective norm had a significant direct negative impact on fertility intention (β=-0.123). This was contrary to our hypothesis and inconsistent with previous research findings [41, 42]. For instance, a study investigating the childbearing intentions among women in Iran found that subjective norm was a significant positive predicting factor for childbearing intention [43]. This discrepancy may be due to the high expectations from family, friends, and society for women to have more children, which could translate into negative pressure [44]. This phenomenon could be seen especially in countries with strong family ties, such as Italy [45]. According to psychological reactance theory, when individuals perceive external expectations as threatening their autonomy, they may develop resistance or oppositional motivation toward the expected behavior, thereby reducing their intention to comply [46].

The impacts of perceived behavioral control and attitude on fertility intention

Perceived behavioral control and attitude had a significant direct positive impact on fertility intention, validating our hypothesis. Consequently, hypothesis 1 was partially supported. This finding was consistent with existed research [45, 47, 48]. Interestingly, among the three variables, perceived behavioral control had the strongest impact on fertility intention. This was different from previous research findings, which typically indicated that attitude has the greatest impact [1, 27, 28]. This may be due to women adopting a more rational approach, considering both subjective and objective factors related to childbirth and child-rearing. Particularly in Shanghai, an international metropolis characterized by a fast-paced lifestyle, high living costs, and intense work pressure, women tend to prioritize their independence and personal development [49]. As a result, they focus more on material conditions such as financial stability, health, career development, and available support, rather than solely on external influences or psychological evaluations. This shift reflects the current trend where women view childbirth as a rational decision [50], weighing the actual pros and cons, rather than being driven solely by traditional family values, gender roles, or the desire to continue the family lineage. Similarly, Novelli et al. realized that in conditions of mounting uncertainty, women in Italy appeared to be more careful in their childbearing plans [51].

The mediating effect of perceived behavioral control and attitude on fertility intention

Perceived behavioral control and attitude respectively mediated the impact of subjective norm on fertility intention, and they also functioned as sequential mediators. The mediating effect of subjective norm on fertility intention accounted for 49.38% of the total effect, nearly half. The finding validated our hypotheses 2 and 3, aligning with Bai’s study [28]. While previous research primarily focused on the direct impacts of those variables on intentions, this study was one of the few that examined their mediating relationships [26]. Among the three mediation paths, the mediating effect of subjective norm through perceived behavioral control accounted for 52.68% of the total mediating effect, indicating that it played a major role, similar to the results of direct effects. According to stimulus-organism-response theory [52], external influences must be psychologically evaluated before they can concretely impact intentions or behaviors. The external pressure for women to have more children will only translate into corresponding intentions when they weigh the positive and negative outcomes of this behavior and assess whether their subjective and objective conditions support it.

The difference in path coefficients between subgroups

In the multi-group analysis, it was found that the path coefficient from perceived behavioral control to fertility intention for women with one child was larger than that for women with multiple children (CR=-2.012). This difference may stem from the fact that mothers with only one child have less parenting experience and are less familiar with the challenges of raising children. As a result, they place more emphasis on evaluating whether they have sufficient resources and support to raise a second child, making perceived behavioral control a more influential factor in their decision-making. In contrast, mothers with multiple children have more parenting experience and a clearer expectation regarding the challenges of raising additional children. Their evaluations of available resources and support are grounded in prior parenting experiences. Consequently, their fertility intention is likely based more on actual experiences rather than perceived behavioral control. Additionally, Zhu et al. found that the ideal number of children among couples in Shanghai with one child was 1.61 ± 0.52, whereas for couples with two kids, it was 2.06 ± 0.34 [14]. This indicates the intention of women with multiple children to have another child may be more stable and thus less influenced by other factors [3].

Implication

Based on the findings of this study, several practical suggestions are offered. First, efforts should be made to respect mothers’ reproductive autonomy by fostering equal communication and avoiding external pressure from families and society. Second, both family members and society should promote the equal sharing of childcare responsibilities to alleviate the disproportionate burden on women. Reducing the conflict between family obligations and career development can help ease women’s psychological stress and enhance their positive perceptions of childbearing. Third, the government could enhance the effectiveness of fertility policies by expanding financial support, such as including childcare expenses in the scope of special additional deductions for individual income tax and providing housing subsidies for families with children. This is particularly important in Shanghai, where the per capita expenditure on education and culture ranks first in China [3]. Employers should be encouraged to provide childcare-friendly work environments by providing nursing rooms, flexible work schedules, and protection against discrimination during pregnancy and career development [53]. Moreover, differentiated support strategies can be tailored for various groups: for women with one child, psychological support and enhancing perceived control over childbearing may be prioritized; for women with multiple children, the emphasis may be placed on reinforcing their existing parenting experiences and addressing the practical challenges encountered in raising additional children.

Limitations and future research directions

This study had some limitations. First, the factors influencing women’s fertility intention are multifaceted and their underlying mechanisms are complex. This study explored the relationships among TPB variables from a social psychological perspective. Future research could incorporate factors from various angles, such as policy, society, family, and individual, to comprehensively analyze their impact on fertility intention. Second, the sample in this study was drawn from Shanghai, which is at the forefront of economic and social development in China. Therefore, the findings may not be representative of the entire country. Future research could expand the sample to include multiple cities with varying economic and social profiles. Third, although the study districts were selected through simple random sampling, convenience sampling was used at the participant level, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. The results should be interpreted with caution. Future research could consider multi-stage probability sampling to further improve representativeness.

Conclusions

Subjective norm negatively impacted fertility intention, while perceived behavioral control and attitude had positive effects, with perceived behavioral control having the strongest direct effect. Perceived behavioral control and attitude sequentially mediated the effect of subjective norm on fertility intention. The effect from perceived behavioral control to fertility intention for women with one child was stronger than that for women with multiple children. The findings suggest that the government and society should avoid placing additional external pressure on women and provide substantial fertility support to help alleviate the burdens of childbirth and child-rearing. This was essential for genuinely increasing their fertility intention and achieving the goals of fertility policies, particularly in cities like Shanghai, where high living costs and societal pressures create additional challenges for women.
