Usefulness and safety of trans-radial endovascular therapy for puncture-site bleeding in a patient with acute coronary syndrome requiring mechanical circulatory support
Authors: Yutaro Nakano, Yusuke Sato, Tatsuhiro Kataoka, Hiroyuki Ikeda, Hiroshi Tada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 66-year-old man with acute coronary syndrome underwent emergency percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) using Impella CP (Abiomed, Danvers, MA, USA) via the left common femoral artery (CFA). After PCI, he experienced shock and severe anemia. A computed tomography scan revealed a large retroperitoneal hematoma caused by femoral puncture-site bleeding. He was in hemorrhage and cardiogenic shock, so we needed to achieve hemostasis without removing Impella CP. We performed endovascular therapy (EVT) through the radial artery system. Angiography showed ongoing bleeding in the left CFA. Initially, we attempted balloon tamponade with an 8-mm semi-compliant balloon, but this was unsuccessful. We then performed percutaneous thrombin injections combined with balloon tamponade. Ultimately, we achieved hemostasis at the puncture site. Our case suggests that trans-radial EVT combined with percutaneous thrombin injection is a useful and safe method for controlling femoral puncture-site bleeding.Learning objectiveTrans-radial endovascular therapy combined with percutaneous thrombin injection is a useful and safe method for controlling femoral puncture-site bleeding, even in patients requiring mechanical circulatory support.

Introduction

Endovascular therapy (EVT) is recognized as the first-line treatment for revascularization in patients with symptomatic lower-extremity arterial disease, given its minimally invasive nature and improved durability [1]. Puncture-site bleeding is a major perioperative complication of trans-femoral EVT, leading to fatal situations. Recently, the trans-radial approach has become a common access site for EVT, especially in aorto-iliac artery disease, owing to advances in devices [2]. Additionally, trans-radial access can reduce access-site bleeding complications compared to trans-femoral access.

This case highlights the usefulness and safety of trans-radial EVT as a bailout for femoral access-site bleeding complications in a patient with cardiogenic shock who required mechanical circulatory support (MCS).

Case report

A 66-year-old man with a history of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia presented with acute chest pain. The patient experienced shock upon arrival by ambulance. The electrocardiogram showed ST-segment elevation in leads V2–5, and the transthoracic echocardiography revealed severely hypokinetic motion of the anteroseptal wall. We diagnosed ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction and then performed emergency percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). We punctured via the right common femoral artery (CFA) for PCI. Angiography showed subtotal coronary occlusion in the proximal left anterior descending artery (LAD) and mild stenosis in the mid left circumflex artery. Then, we punctured via the left CFA using duplex ultrasound and inserted MCS (Impella CP; Abiomed, Danvers, MA, USA). We put the drug-eluting stent in the proximal LAD. Intravascular ultrasound confirmed that the stent was fully expanded without tissue protrusion. Final angiography also showed optimal vessel expansion with no residual stenosis and thrombolysis in myocardial infarction grade 3 flow. Lastly, we removed the 7-French sheath via the right femoral artery access using a vascular closure device (Perclose ProGlide; Abbott Vascular, Temecula, CA, USA).

Five hours after the PCI procedure, he experienced shock again, and the Impella console was showing a suction alarm. First, we considered the suction event of the Impella device. Laboratory tests showed severe anemia (hemoglobin 7.0 g/dL) and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) (platelet 23 × 103/μL). In addition, a computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a large retroperitoneal hematoma caused by bleeding at the left CFA puncture site and a subcutaneous hematoma at the right femoral puncture site (Fig. 1A). Therefore, hemorrhagic shock coincided with cardiogenic shock. Thus, we performed EVT via the trans-radial approach to reduce the risk of further puncture-site bleeding. First, we aimed to keep the activated clotting time between 160 and 180 s, as recommended. Then, we advanced a 6-French guiding sheath (BRITE TIP RADIANZ; Cordis, Miami Lakes, FL, USA) via the left radial artery to the left external iliac artery. Angiography showed ongoing bleeding in the left CFA (Fig. 1B: red arrow). Initially, we used a 0.014-inch guidewire (Vasallo NS1; Cordis) and attempted balloon tamponade with an 8-mm semi-compliant balloon (SABERX RADIANZ; Cordis). Angiography revealed complete blood flow obstruction caused by inflating an 8-mm semicompliant balloon at low pressure, even with the Impella sheath in place. However, balloon tamponade did not affect the symptoms of limb ischemia and Impella support. Fifteen minutes after balloon inflation, initial hemostasis was achieved; however, recurrent bleeding occurred 5 min later. We then decided to perform percutaneous thrombin injection combined with balloon tamponade. First, we needed to confirm that the needle tip was in the hematoma, not in the artery. The left CFA and bleeding point were not invisible due to a huge hematoma and subcutaneous edema by duplex ultrasound. To visualize a hematoma, we injected contrast through the needle for percutaneous thrombin injection using multi-angle angiography. Then, we injected 300 IU of thrombin into a hematoma. Ultimately, we achieved hemostasis at the puncture site after five thrombin injections (Fig. 1C and D). This patient required a massive blood transfusion. Fortunately, his cardiac function recovered, and Impella CP was surgically removed on Day 3. The CT findings revealed that the retroperitoneal hematoma gradually decreased in size and was completely absorbed on Day 14. Ultimately, his general condition improved, and he was discharged from the hospital on Day 30.

Discussion

Previous studies and guidelines recommended using ultrasound-guided thrombin injection for femoral artery pseudoaneurysms [3], [4]. Additionally, trans-catheter thrombin injection, also known as EVT, has been reported as a safe and effective method for treating femoral artery pseudoaneurysms [5]. Moreover, previous studies have shown that a combination of endovascular balloon inflation and percutaneous thrombin injection is effective for treating pseudoaneurysms and achieving hemostasis at the distal puncture site [6], [7]. Endovascular balloon inflation can prevent distal embolization due to contaminated thrombin in the artery. Thus, the combination of EVT and thrombin injection is safer than thrombin injection alone. In our case, we expect that inflation of a large semicompliant balloon at low pressure will conform well to the vessel wall, achieving balloon tamponade even with the Impella sheath in place.

The proportion of trans-radial EVT in aortoiliac lesions has been increasing annually in Japan due to the innovation of devices and the reduction in time to hemostasis and ambulation [2]. Basically, trans-radial EVT never causes femoral puncture-site bleeding; therefore, it is the best option to reduce puncture-site complications. We should consider selecting the trans-radial approach, particularly in cases of complications at the femoral puncture site. In this case, the patient presented with bilateral femoral artery hematoma and DIC, which had a risk of further fatal bleeding. Additionally, he needed to be supported by MCS due to cardiogenic shock. We expected that replacing MCS would be difficult because he also experienced hemorrhagic shock. Thus, we selected the trans-radial approach for EVT to achieve hemostasis of the femoral puncture-site bleeding without replacing the MCS.

Preventing fatal complications such as retroperitoneal hematoma is crucial. In this case, even though the author punctured the left CFA under ultrasound guidance, this complication still occurred. There are two main reasons for retroperitoneal hematoma: high puncture and posterior puncture. We punctured the CFA in axial view, making the needle tip invisible in this instance. Angiography showed that the bleeding originated from the posterior-lateral wall of the high CFA. We should learn and educate on optimal ultrasound-guided femoral artery access to prevent such serious complications [8]. Additionally, to prevent the progression of serious complications, we needed to recognize the factors delaying a more timely approach in the current case, namely the clinical assumption that the anemia was due to device-related hemolysis and that shock was caused by the Impella device's suction event.

Future large-scale studies are warranted to determine the effectiveness and safety of trans-radial EVT combined with percutaneous thrombin injection for bailout of femoral access-site complications. However, few reports have demonstrated trans-radial EVT for femoral puncture-site complications [6]. Our report suggests that the trans-radial approach is a suitable option for EVT of femoral access-site complications.

Conclusion

Our case suggests that trans-radial EVT combined with percutaneous thrombin injection is a useful and safe method for controlling femoral puncture-site bleeding.
