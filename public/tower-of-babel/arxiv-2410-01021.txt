A Naturally-Colored Translation from LTL to Parity and COCOA Thanks: Supported by DFG through Grant No. 322591867 (GUISynth) and the BMWi through Grant No. 19A21026E (SafeWahr).
Rüdiger Ehlers, Ayrat Khalimov

Abstract
Chains of co-Büchi automata (COCOA) have recently been introduced as a new canonical representation of omega-regular languages. The co-Büchi automata in a chain assign to each omega-word its natural color, which depends only on the language itself and not on its automaton representation. The automata in such a chain can be minimized in polynomial time and are good-for-games, making the representation attractive for verification and reactive synthesis applications. However, since in such applications, a specification is usually given in linear temporal logic (LTL), to make COCOA useful, the specification first has to be translated into such a chain of automata. Currently, the only known translation procedure involves a detour through deterministic parity automata (LTL → \,{\to}\, DPW → \,{\to}\, COCOA), where the first step neglects the natural colors and requires intricate constructions by Safra or Esparza et al. This observation raises the question whether, by leveraging the definition of the natural color of words, these complex constructions can be avoided, leading to a more direct translation from LTL to COCOA. In this paper, we present a surprisingly simple translation from LTL to COCOA, along with a variant that translates from LTL to DPW. Our procedure relies on standard operations on weak alternating automata, Miyano/Hayashi’s breakpoint construction, an augmented subset construction, and simple graph algorithms. With weak alternating automata as a starting point, the procedure can also handle specifications in linear dynamic logic. Although the translation procedure runs in sub-optimal triply-exponential time (vs. doubly-exponential optimal), it constitutes a novel path for translating from LTL to DPW, avoiding the aforementioned intricate constructions entirely.



I
Introduction

Canonical forms play an important role in computer science.
A typical canonical form is born from an insight about the nature of objects it describes,
leading to the development of efficient algorithms manipulating the objects.
In the context of finite words, the Myhill-Nerode theorem about regular languages brought to life a canonical form of finite automata, and led to efficient automata minimization and learning algorithms.

Omega-regular languages do not possess a simple Myhill-Nerode-like characterization,
which led to the flourishing of canonical forms based on representing a language as a family of automata over finite words
[
3
,
16
,
1
,
15
]
.
These models are based on the insight that an omega-regular language can be characterized by the language of its ultimately-periodic words (those of the form
uv^{\omega}
).
As a result, these models operate on finite words of the form
u\cdot v
,
which makes them a perfect fit for automata-learning tasks but ill-suited for classical problems of model checking and synthesis.
An alternative canonical representation that does not assume words to be ultimately periodic, and hence is more conventional, was recently introduced by Ehlers and Schewe in
[
5
]
.
It is based on the notion of
natural colors
.

Natural colors assign colors to words based solely on an omega-regular language and independently of its representation.
This allows an omega-regular language
L
to be represented as a unique chain of co-Büchi languages
L^{1}\supset\ldots\supset L^{k}
,
where
L^{i}
is the set of words whose natural color is greater or equal
i
,
and
k
is the smallest number of colors in a parity automaton recognizing
L
.
By definition, a word belongs to
L
if and only if its natural color wrt.
L
is even.
Each co-Büchi language
L^{i}
can be represented by a canonical (and minimal) transition-based good-for-games co-Büchi automaton
[
20
]
.
Thus, every omega-regular language
L
has a unique representation as a chain of such co-Büchi automata (
COCOA
)
\mathcal{A}^{1},\ldots,\mathcal{A}^{k}
.

The two distinguishing features of COCOA —
(1) the chain representation with clearly defined semantics, and
(2) the minimality and good-for-gameness of each-level automaton —
can potentially be exploited to design better verification and synthesis algorithms. For instance,
COCOA were recently used to solve a reactive synthesis problem that previous tools were unable to handle
[
4
]
.

Verification and synthesis problems are usually defined over system behavior descriptions written in linear temporal logic (LTL).
Therefore, to make COCOA useful to verification and synthesis applications,
one needs a procedure for translating LTL to COCOA.
Currently, the only known way to obtain COCOA from LTL formulas is by going through deterministic parity word (DPW) automata:
LTL
\to
DPW
\to
COCOA
[
5
]
.
Although the second step is relatively simple, the first step represents a challenge.
For instance,
one can first translate the LTL property to a nondeterministic Büchi automaton and then apply Safra’s determinization construction
[
22
]
.
However, it is well-known that implementing Safra’s construction efficiently is challenging.
Alternatively, one can use the LTL-to-DRW translation of Esparza et al.
[
6
]
that aims to generate automata with a more regular structure and is thus more amenable to efficient implementations.
Nevertheless, in both cases, the overall translation is involved, and the insight of natural colors in the main challenging step is neglected.
This observation raises the question “
Is there a direct translation from LTL to COCOA?
”.

The second question tackled in this paper concerns the problem of translating from LTL to DPW.
Modern approaches to reactive synthesis and probabilistic verification often reduce to solving parity games
[
21
,
17
,
14
]
, and hence translate from LTL to DPW as an integral part.
Experience from reactive synthesis
[
10
]
shows that current approaches to this translation still constitute a bottleneck, indicating a need for further research.
We hence ask: “
Can natural colors enable a simpler translation LTL
\,{\to}\,
DPW?
”

This paper positively answers both questions.
We describe how using the natural colors of words as a starting point leads to a surprisingly simple
translation from LTL to DPW and COCOA.
Our translation procedure starts from weak alternating automata,
hence it can also translate formulas in linear dynamic logic (LDL)
[
9
,
8
]
.
The procedure uses simple operations on weak alternating automata,
Miyano/Hayashi’s breakpoint construction
[
18
]
, an
augmented subset construction
[
2
]
, and easy graph algorithms.
The resulting automata are highly structured,
making them amenable to further optimizations.
Although the translation procedure runs in sub-optimal triply-exponential time (vs. doubly-exponential optimal),
it constitutes a novel path for translating from LTL to DPW, avoiding the intricate constructions entirely.
We now describe ideas behind the new translations.

LTL
\,\to\,
COCOA

We translate a given LTL or LDL formula into a weak alternating automaton
\mathcal{A}
using standard procedures
[
19
,
8
]
.
Using the Miyano-Hayashi’s breakpoint construction
[
18
]
,
we remove universal transitions from the dual of the weak alternating automaton
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
,
which yields an automaton
\mathcal{G}
, called an obligation graph, with language
\overline{L(\mathcal{A})}
.
We then build a machine that tracks the suffix languages of
L(\mathcal{A})
using simple operations on weak alternating automata,
and use this machine to maximize the set of initial vertices of the obligation graph
\mathcal{G}
while preserving its language.
Finally,
we apply the augmented subset construction
[
2
]
,
which is roughly the product
2^{\mathcal{G}}\!\times{\mathcal{G}}
of the subset construction of graph
\mathcal{G}
with itself.
This yields a nondeterministic co-Büchi automaton
\mathcal{A}^{1}_{N}
whose language contains all words from
\overline{L(\mathcal{A})}
as well as words from
L(\mathcal{A})
with the natural color larger than
1
– that is exactly the language
L^{1}
of the first level of the COCOA.
The size of
\mathcal{A}^{1}_{N}
is in
2^{2^{O(n)}}
, where
n
is the number of states in
\mathcal{A}
.
Thanks to the special structure of
\mathcal{A}^{1}_{N}
,
it can be turned into a deterministic co-Büchi automaton
\mathcal{A}^{1}_{D}
without incurring a blow-up beyond
2^{2^{O(n)}}
,
which is then canonized into a good-for-games automaton
\mathcal{A}^{1}
using the effective procedure of
[
20
]
.
The construction of automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N}
for level
\ell>1
is similar, except that it also requires building a product with the previous-level automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\ell-1}_{N}
to ensure
L(\mathcal{A}^{\ell-1}_{N})\supset L(\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N})
.
Since the number of levels can be exponential in
n
,
determinization of
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N}
using the breakpoint construction
yields a deterministic co-Büchi automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{D}
of triply-exponential size,
which is then minimized-canonized into the good-for-games automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}
.

LTL
\,\to\,
DPW

The deterministic co-Büchi automata
\mathcal{A}^{1}_{D},\ldots,\mathcal{A}^{k}_{D}
built as a part of translation LTL
\to
COCOA can be used to construct the DPW
\mathcal{P}
by taking their product.
The parity color of the combined product transition is the largest
\ell
such that
\mathcal{A}^{1}_{D},\ldots,\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{D}
take accepting transitions.
When a word has the natural color
\ell
—
thus the co-Büchi automata
\mathcal{A}^{1}_{D},\ldots,\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{D}
eventually always take accepting transitions while
\mathcal{A}^{\ell+1}
infinitely often takes a rejecting transition —
the minimal color visited infinitely often by the run of
\mathcal{P}
is also
\ell
.
The DPW constructed in this way has the language
L(\mathcal{A})
, accepts the words by associating them with their natural color, and
has at most triply-exponential size.

II
Preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,\ldots\}
be the set of natural numbers including
0
.
We assume that the reader is familiar with the logic LTL;
the formulas of this logic are built over atomic propositions
using the standard Boolean operators and the temporal operators
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathsf{X}
as well as operators that can be derived from these two, such as
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{F}
. For an LTL formula
\varphi
, we denote its set of models by
L(\varphi)
.

II-A

Weak alternating automata

A useful automaton model for representing LTL specifications on a more technical level are
weak alternating automata
.
We introduce them in a variant that simplifies the presentation from the next section onwards, but is equivalent to the classical definition.
For a set
X
, let
\mathcal{PC\!N\hskip-2.56073ptF}(X)
denote the set of positive Boolean formulas over
X
in conjunctive normal form, where the special cases of formulas that are equivalent to
\mathbf{true}
or
\mathbf{false}
are not contained.

A
weak alternating Büchi automaton
is a tuple
\mathcal{A}=(\Sigma,Q,q_{0},\delta,F)
where

\Sigma
is an alphabet,

Q
is a finite set of states,

\delta:Q\times\Sigma\to\mathcal{PC\!N\hskip-2.56073ptF}(Q)
is a transition function,

q_{0}
is an initial state, and

F\subseteq Q
is a set of accepting states.
The prefix “weak” in the name means that the automaton has a total order
\geq
on states
Q

which satisfies:
(i) for every
q,x,q^{\prime}
where
q^{\prime}
appears in
\delta(q,x)
:
q^{\prime}\leq q
, and
(ii) states equivalent wrt.
\geq
are either all accepting or all rejecting (not in
F
).

Intuitively, an alternating automaton is weak if in every of its strongly connected component (when ignoring edge labels),
either all states are accepting, or all states are rejecting.

The notation
\mathcal{A}_{q}
for
q\in Q
denotes the automaton having the same structure as
\mathcal{A}
except that the initial state is
q
. Let furthermore
\mathit{Clauses}
be the set of clauses that appear in any
\delta(q,x)
for
(q,x)\in Q\times\Sigma
.

Alternating automata accept or reject words,
which is defined through word-checking games defined next.
There are two players, rejector and acceptor,
who on a given word
w=x_{0}x_{1}\ldots
construct a play
q_{0}q_{1}\ldots\in Q^{\omega}
as follows.
The play starts in the initial state
q_{0}
of
\mathcal{A}
;
suppose at moment
i
the play is in state
q_{i}
.
The rejector player picks a disjunctive clause of
\delta(q_{i},x_{i})
,
the acceptor player picks a state in that clause,
say
q^{\prime}
,
then
i
is updated to
i+1
and
q_{i}
is set to
q^{\prime}
.
The strategies of the rejector have type
\mathit{Rej}:Q^{+}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathit{Clauses}

and
\mathit{Acc}:\mathit{Clauses}^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\to Q
for the acceptor.
The players construct a play
q_{0}q_{1}\ldots
as described above;
it is denoted
\pi(\mathit{Rej},\mathit{Acc})
for given rejector and acceptor strategies.
The play is
won
by the acceptor if eventually it visits only accepting states
F
of
\mathcal{A}
,
otherwise it is won by the rejector.
The word-checking game is
won
by the rejector if it has a strategy
\mathit{Rej}:Q^{+}\to\mathit{Clauses}
to pick clauses to win against every accceptor strategy
\mathit{Acc}:\mathit{Clauses}^{*}\to Q
,
otherwise it is won by the acceptor.
Note that both players know the whole word beforehand, and the second parameter to
\mathit{Acc}
and
\mathit{Rej}
denotes the current index of the word.
Since the word-checking games are of Borel type,
they are determined, implying that
\exists\mathit{Rej}.\forall\mathit{Acc}:\pi(\mathit{Rej},\mathit{Acc})\models\mathit{Rej}
is equivalent to
\forall\mathit{Acc}.\exists\mathit{Rej}:\pi(\mathit{Rej},\mathit{Acc})\models\mathit{Rej}
.

With a weak alternating automaton
\mathcal{A}=(\Sigma,Q,q_{0},\delta,F)
,
we can associate a directed graph
(Q,\to)

whose vertices
Q
are the states of
\mathcal{A}

and
q\to q^{\prime}
if and only if
there is
x
such that
q^{\prime}
appears in the formula
\delta(q,x)
.
Then,
an SCC (strongly connected component) of
\mathcal{A}
is an SCC of the associated graph
(Q,\to)
.
Note that we will always reason about maximal SCCs in this paper, i.e., SCCs that cannot be extended by a state or vertex without losing the property that it is indeed an SCC.
When the automaton is weak,
every maximal SCC of
(Q,\to)
either consists entirely of accepting states or rejecting states.

Although weak alternating automata are rarely used in verification and synthesis directly,
they are a crucial intermediate representation for translating LTL and LDL formulas into other automata models.

Fact 1

(​​
[
8

.

Every LDL (and hence LTL) formula

into a weak alternating Büchi automaton with the number of states linear in

|\psi|

Note that since for notational simplicity, we assume that the transition functions of alternating automata map every combination of state and input to a conjunctive normal form Boolean formula, the representation of the transition function may be of size exponential in the length of the LDL/LTL formula.

II-B

Chain of co-Büchi automata (COCOA)

This section defines COCOA:
a canonical representation of omega-regular languages introduced in
[
5
]
.
We first define co-Büchi automata,
state a known result about canonisation of such automata,
then we define a canonical separation of a given language into subsets,
and finally we combine those notions into a canonical representation of omega-regular languages.

II-B
1
Canonical co-Büchi automata

A
transition-based nondeterministic co-Büchi automaton
(NCW)

\mathcal{A}=(\Sigma,Q,q_{0},\delta,F)

consists of an alphabet
\Sigma
,
a set of states
Q
,
an initial state
q_{0}\in Q
,
a transition relation
\delta\subseteq Q\times\Sigma\times Q
,
and a set of accepting transitions
F\subseteq\delta
.

A
path
of
\mathcal{A}
is a (finite or infinite) sequence of transitions

\pi=(q_{1},x_{1},q_{2})(q_{2},x_{2},q_{3})\ldots
where
(q_{i},x_{i},q_{i+1})\in\delta
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,|\pi|\}
(or
i\in\mathbb{N}
if
\pi
is infinite).
A
run
of
\mathcal{A}
on an infinite word
x_{0}x_{1}\ldots

is an infinite path
(q_{0},x_{0},q_{1})(q_{1},x_{1},q_{2})\ldots
that starts in the initial state
q_{0}
;
a word might have several runs or no run at all.
A run is
accepting
if eventually only accepting transitions appear in it.
A word is
accepted
if it has an accepting run.
The
language
of
\mathcal{A}
is the set of words accepted by
\mathcal{A}
.

A co-Büchi automaton
\mathcal{A}
is
good-for-games
(GFG) if there exists a function

\lambda:\Sigma^{*}\to Q
, called strategy,
such that for every word
x_{0}x_{1}\ldots
accepted by
\mathcal{A}
,
the sequence
(q_{0},x_{0},q_{1})(q_{1},x_{1},q_{2})\ldots
, where
q_{i+1}=\lambda(x_{0}\ldots x_{i})
for all
i
,
is an accepting run of
\mathcal{A}
on the word.

We remind the reader that deterministic and nondeterministic co-Büchi automata have the same expressive power,
and so do NCWs and GFG-NCWs.
Size-wise,
GFG-NCWs are exponentially more succinct than DCWs
[
11
]
.
Moreover, GFG-NCWs have a canonical form;
these automata in the canonical form are also the smallest in the class of GFG-NCWs.
Turning DCW into GFG-NCW in the canonical form can be done in polynomial time
[
20
]
.

Finally, a language is
co-Büchi
if there is an NCW having this language.

II-B
2
Canonical chain representation (COCOA)

We first introduce a few helpful notions, then proceed to the main definition.

Given a language
L
over
\Sigma
and a finite prefix
p\in\Sigma^{*}
,
the
suffix language
of
L
after reading
p
is
L^{\mathit{suff}}(p)=\{w\in\Sigma^{\omega}\mid p\cdot w\in L\}
.
The set of all suffix languages of
L
is denoted
L^{\textit{suff}}
.
A finite word
u\in\Sigma^{*}

preserves
the suffix language of
L
wrt.
p

if
L^{\mathit{suff}}(p\cdot u)=L^{\mathit{suff}}(p)
.
For an infinite word
w
and an infinite set
J\subseteq\mathbb{N}
of positions
j_{1}<j_{2}<\ldots
,
a word
w^{\prime}
is the result of a
suffix-language-preserving injection
into
w
at
J

if it is of the form
w^{\prime}=w_{0}...w_{j_{1}-1}u_{1}w_{j_{1}}...w_{j_{2}-1}u_{2}w_{j_{2}}\ldots

where
u_{i}
for every
i
preserves the suffix language of
w_{0}\ldots w_{j_{i}-1}
wrt.
L
.
Notice that due to
J
being infinite,
a suffix-language-preserving injection into a word
w\in L
may result in
w^{\prime}\not\in L
, and vice versa.
For instance,
consider the language
L(\mathsf{FG}a)
over
\Sigma=\{a,\bar{a}\}
and its word
a^{\omega}
,
then
(a\bar{a})^{\omega}
is the result of a suffix-language-preserving injection into
a^{\omega}
at positions
J=\{1,3,5,\ldots\}

but
(a\bar{a})^{\omega}\not\in L(\mathsf{FG}a)
.
We will write
w^{\prime}\in\text{SLII}^{L}_{J,w}
, or simply
\text{SLII}^{L}_{J,w}\ w^{\prime}
,
when
w^{\prime}
is the result of a suffix-language-invariant injection into
w
at positions
J
,
and we may omit specifying
L
,
J
,
w
, whenever they are clear from the context.

The
canonical chain-of-co-Büchi-automata representation (
COCOA
)
of an omega-regular language
L

is the sequence of canonical GFG-NCWs
(\mathcal{A}^{1},\ldots,\mathcal{A}^{k})

where each automaton
\mathcal{A}^{i}
is defined through its language
L^{i}
:
let
L^{0}=\Sigma^{\omega}
, then

\displaystyle L^{\text{odd }i}=\big\{w\in L^{i-1}\mid\forall J.\,\exists\text{SLII}^{L}_{J,w}w^{\prime}{:~}w^{\prime}\in\bar{L}\cap L^{i-1}\,\big\},

\displaystyle L^{\text{even }i}=\big\{w\in L^{i-1}\mid\forall J.\,\exists\text{SLII}^{L}_{J,w}w^{\prime}{:~}w^{\prime}\in L\cap L^{i-1}\,\big\}.

The
natural color
of
w
wrt.
L

is the maximal index of an automaton in the chain accepting the word or
0
if
\mathcal{A}^{1}
rejects
w
.
Ehlers and Schewe
[
5
]
showed that
every omega-regular language has a unique COCOA, and
a word
w
belongs to
L
if and only if the natural color of
w
wrt.
L
is even.. The number of colors in the COCOA is the smallest value
k
such that
L^{k+1}=\emptyset
. Representing the COCOA as a finite sequence, ending with
L^{k}
, is justified by the fact that by the definition of languages in the COCOA, we have
\emptyset=L^{k+1}=L^{k+2}=\ldots
.

We now explain the definition, then give examples.
First, observe that COCOA elements have shrinking languages:
L^{1}\supset\ldots\supset L^{k}
.
The language
L(\mathcal{A}^{1})
contains all words of
\bar{L}
,
as well as those words
w\in L
that can be made into
w^{\prime}\in\bar{L}
by the SLII operation for every given
J
.
The language
L(\mathcal{A}^{2})
removes from
L(\mathcal{A}^{1})
every
\bar{L}
-word that cannot be made into an
L
-word in
L(\mathcal{A}^{1})
by the SLII operation for every given
J
.
Similarly,
L(\mathcal{A}^{3})
is the result of removing
L
-words from
L(\mathcal{A}^{2})
that cannot be made into an
\bar{L}
-word by the SLII operation.
And so on.
Thus, COCOA structures the original language into an onion-like manner,
where each
L^{\text{odd }i}
removes certain words in
L\cap L^{i-1}
and
each
L^{\text{even }i}
removes certain words in
\bar{L}\cap L^{i-1}
.

Examples.

Consider the safety language
L(\mathsf{G}a)
over the alphabet
\Sigma=\{a,\bar{a}\}
.
Its COCOA consists of a single
\mathcal{A}^{1}
with
L(\mathcal{A}^{1})=L(\mathsf{F}\bar{a})
:
indeed, for every
w\in L(\mathcal{A}^{1})
and suffix indices
J\subseteq\mathbb{N}
,
we take the SLII
w^{\prime}=w
to exhibit
w^{\prime}\in\bar{L}
.
Note that
L(\mathcal{A}^{1})
contains no words from
L(\mathsf{G}a)
,
because only injecting subwords of the form
a^{*}
would be suffix-language preserving,
and injecting them cannot move the word outside of
L(\mathsf{G}a)
.
Thus, every word
w\in L(\mathsf{G}a)
has the natural color
0
.
Consider the language
L(\mathsf{FG}a)
.
Its COCOA
(\mathcal{A}^{1},\mathcal{A}^{2})
has
L(\mathcal{A}^{1})=\Sigma^{\omega}
and
L(\mathcal{A}^{2})=L(\mathsf{FG}a)
, justified as follows.
For any prefix
p
, the suffix language of
L(\mathsf{FG}a)
after reading
p
equals
L(\mathsf{FG}a)
,
hence inserting anything preserves the suffix language.
In particular, we can insert
\bar{a}
, yielding the SLII
w^{\prime}\in\overline{L(\mathsf{FG}a)}
regardless of

,
thus
L(\mathcal{A}^{1})=\Sigma^{\omega}
.
The case of
\mathcal{A}^{2}
is straightforward.
Consider the language
L(\mathsf{GF}a\to\mathsf{GF}b)
over
\Sigma=2^{\{a,b\}}
:
it has the COCOA
(\mathcal{A}^{1},\mathcal{A}^{2})
with

L(\mathcal{A}^{1})=L(\mathsf{FG}\bar{b})
and
L(\mathcal{A}^{2})=L(\mathsf{FG}\bar{a}\land\mathsf{FG}\bar{b})
, justified as follows.
The language
L(\mathcal{A}^{1})
must contain every word from
L(\mathsf{GF}a\land\mathsf{FG}\bar{b})
and
all
L
-words that can be turned into an
\bar{L}
-word by the SLII operation for every given
J
.
It is not hard to see that
L(\mathcal{A}^{1})=L(\mathsf{FG}\bar{b})
.
The language
L(\mathcal{A}^{2})
is the result of removing
\bar{L}
-words from
L(\mathcal{A}^{1})

for which there exists
J
such that no SLII at
J
results in a word in
L\cap L(\mathcal{A}^{1})
.
We skip the precise analysis and only state that
L(\mathcal{A}^{2})=L(\mathsf{GF}a\land\mathsf{FG}\bar{b})
.
Finally, the language
L(\mathsf{GF}a\to(\mathsf{GF}b\land\mathsf{FG}c))
over
\Sigma=2^{\{a,b,c\}}
has the COCOA
(\mathcal{A}^{1},\mathcal{A}^{2},\mathcal{A}^{3},\mathcal{A}^{4})
with

L(\mathcal{A}^{1})=\Sigma^{\omega}
,

L(\mathcal{A}^{2})=L(\mathsf{FG}\bar{a}\lor\mathsf{FG}c)
,

L(\mathcal{A}^{3})=L(\mathsf{FG}c\land\mathsf{FG}\bar{b})
, and

L(\mathcal{A}^{4})=L(\mathsf{FG}c\land\mathsf{FG}\bar{b}\land\mathsf{FG}\bar{a})
.
In all these examples, we do not define COCOA elements explicitly but rather through their languages.

Fact 2

(
[
5
]
)
.

Every weak alternating automaton
\mathcal{A}
with
n
states can be translated into the COCOA
with the number of levels in
2^{O(n)}
and
the total number of states in
2^{2^{O(n)}}
,
in time in
2^{2^{O(n)}}
.

III
Translating Weak Alternating Automata to COCOA

This section describes our main result: a translation from weak alternating Büchi automata to COCOA.
Section
III-A
defines
obligation graphs
,
which are the nondeterministic Büchi automata resulting from applying the Miyano-Hayashi’s construction to the dual of input weak alternating automata.
Based on obligation graphs,
Section
III-B
defines
suffix-language-tracking machines
:
these machines track the suffix language of our language after reading a finite prefix word.
With the aid of the suffix machine, we then saturate the set of initial vertices of the obligation graph.
After these preparations,
Section
III-D
describes the construction of level automata
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}
of COCOA.

III-A

Obligation graphs

Given a weak alternating Büchi automaton
\mathcal{A}=(\Sigma,Q,q_{0},\delta,F)
,
its
obligation graph
is an NBW
\mathcal{G}=(\Sigma,V,V_{0},\delta^{\mathcal{G}},F_{\mathcal{G}})

resulting from applying the Miyano-Hayashi’s construction to
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
.
Thus,
L(\mathcal{G})=\overline{L(\mathcal{A})}
.

III-B

The suffix-language tracking machine

We use the following notation:
for a transition relation
\delta^{\mathcal{G}}
of an obligation graph and a prefix word
p\in\Sigma^{*}
,

\delta^{\mathcal{G}}(v,p)
denotes the vertices in which a run of
\mathcal{G}
can be after reading
p
when starting in
v
; we write
v_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}v
when
v\in\delta^{\mathcal{G}}(v_{0},p)
.

Let
L
be a language over an alphabet
\Sigma
.
The
suffix-language tracking machine (SLTM)
of
L

is a Moore machine
\mathcal{M}=(\Sigma,L^{\textit{suff}},Q^{S},q^{S}_{0},\delta^{S},\lambda)

with input alphabet
\Sigma
,
output alphabet
L^{\textit{suff}}
(the suffix languages of
L
),
transition function
\delta^{S}:Q^{S}\times\Sigma\to Q^{S}
,
which we overload in the standard way to accept finite prefixes in addition to single letters,
and the labelling
\lambda:Q^{S}\to L^{\textit{suff}}
such that
for each prefix
p\in\Sigma^{*}
:

\lambda(\delta^{S}(q_{0}^{S},p))=L^{\textit{suff}}(p)
.

We now define SLTMs with a special structure.
A
canonical SLTM
for a weak alternating automaton
\mathcal{A}
is an SLTM

(\Sigma,L^{\textit{suff}},Q^{S},q^{S}_{0},\delta^{S},\lambda)

with
Q^{S}\subseteq 2^{V}
,
where
V
are the vertices of the obligation graph
\mathcal{G}
of
\mathcal{A}
,

L^{\textit{suff}}
is the set of all suffix-languages of
L(\mathcal{A})
,
and every
q^{S}\in Q^{S}
satisfies

q^{S}=\big\{v\mid\exists p\in\Sigma^{*}\textit{such that }L^{\textit{suff}}(p)=\lambda(q^{S})\textit{ and }v_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}v\big\}.

(1)

Note that applying the subset construction to
\mathcal{G}
does
not
in general yield a canonical SLTM,
which is easy to see, such as by comparing the definition of the inital states in both constructions.

We now prove the main technical lemma about SLTMs.

Lemma 1
.

A canonical SLTM for a weak alternating automaton
\mathcal{A}
with
n
states and a transition function representation that is at most exponential in
n
can be constructed in
2^{2^{O(n)}}
time.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\Sigma,V,V_{0},\delta^{\mathcal{G}})
be the obligation graph
(i.e., the MH’s construction applied to
\mathcal{\bar{A}}
)
with the singleton initial set
V_{0}=\{v_{0}\}
;
it can be constructed in
2^{O(n)}
time.
Given
\mathcal{G}
,
we construct a canonical SLTM by first constructing a naive SLTM and then minimizing it.

In the following,
let
\mathsf{Suffix}:2^{\Sigma^{\omega}}\!\!\times\Sigma^{*}\to 2^{\Sigma^{\omega}}
be a function mapping a language
L\subseteq\Sigma^{\omega}
and a finite word
p\in\Sigma^{*}
to
\{w\in\Sigma^{\omega}\mid p\cdot w\in L\}
, i.e., the suffix language of
L
for
p
.

Step 1: naive SLTM.

A naive SLTM

\mathcal{M}=(\Sigma,L^{\textit{suff}},Q^{S},q_{0}^{S},\delta^{S},\lambda)

is constructed by applying the subset construction to
\mathcal{G}
,
and the labeling function
\lambda
intersects the suffix languages of individual vertices.
The initial state is
q_{0}^{S}=\{v_{0}\}
.
Given a state
q^{S}\in 2^{V}
of
\mathcal{M}
and a letter
x\in\Sigma
,
the successor state is

\delta^{S}(q^{S}\!,x)=\bigcup_{v\in q^{S}}\delta^{\mathcal{G}}(v,x)
.
All states reachable from the initial state constitute the set of states of
\mathcal{M}
.
The number of states of
\mathcal{M}
is not larger than
2^{|V|}
hence it is in
2^{2^{O(n)}}
​​.
The label is

\lambda(q^{S})=\bigcap_{(Q_{1},Q_{2})\in q^{S}}\bigcup_{q\in Q_{1}\cup Q_{2}}L(\mathcal{A}_{q})
.
Thus,
the label function ignores the separation of states in the Miyano-Hayashi’s construction,
so we could have used the subset construction instead.
We now show that the constructed machine satisfies the definition of SLTMs.
To this end,
we are going to prove the following single-step condition (
\dagger
):

\lambda(q_{0}^{S})=L(\mathcal{A})
, and
for every state
q^{S}
and letter
x
:

\lambda(\delta^{S}(q^{S}\!\!,x))=\mathsf{Suffix}(\lambda(q^{S}),x)
.
By induction, this condition implies the satisfaction of the definition of SLTMs,
namely, for every prefix
p
,
the label
\lambda(\delta^{S}(q_{0}^{S}\!,p))=\mathsf{Suffix}(L(\mathcal{A}),p)
.

Step 2: minimization.

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}=(\Sigma,L^{\textit{suff}},\tilde{Q}^{S},\tilde{q}_{0}^{S},\tilde{\delta}^{S},\tilde{\lambda})
be the naive SLTM constructed in the first step.
The SLTM
\tilde{M}
may violate Equation
1
,
because a state of the naivse SLTM may miss vertices (for example, the initial state).
We derive the minimized SLTM
\mathcal{M}=(\Sigma,L^{\textit{suff}},Q^{S},q^{S}_{0},\delta^{S},\lambda)
from
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
by merging the states with the same suffix language.
For every suffix language
L^{\prime}
of
L
,
define

q^{S}=\bigcup_{\tilde{q}^{S}:\tilde{\lambda}(\tilde{q}^{S})=L^{\prime}}\tilde{q}^{S}

to be a state of
\mathcal{M}
,
i.e.,

q^{S}
merges all states of
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
whose label equals
L^{\prime}
.
To find out which states should be merged,
we check whether
\tilde{\lambda}(\tilde{q}^{S}_{1})=\tilde{\lambda}(\tilde{q}^{S}_{2})
for every possible pair
(\tilde{q}^{S}_{1},\tilde{q}^{S}_{2})
of states of
\tilde{M}
.
Each such comparison can be performed in
2^{O(n)}
time
1
1

1

We represent the label
\tilde{\lambda}
as a weak alternating Büchi automaton
derived from label’s formula.
Recall that Boolean operations on weak alternating Büchi automata
can be done in polynomial time in the numbers of states of the automata.
Given two labels
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
represented by two automata
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
,
checking whether
L_{1}=L_{2}
amounts to checking whether the alternating automaton
\mathcal{A}_{1}\land\neg\mathcal{A}_{2}\lor\neg\mathcal{A}_{1}\land\mathcal{A}_{2}
has the empty language.
Checking nonemptiness of alternating automata can be done in singly-exponential time,
for instance,
by first removing universal transitions using Miyano-Hayashi construction
[
18
]
,
then checking nonemptiness of the resulting nondeterministic automaton.
This gives a singly-exponential cost of the label-equality check. This also holds if the transition relation representation is exponentially bigger than the number of states, as it does not affect the number of states in the nondeterministic automaton.
,
hence the set
Q^{S}
of states of
\mathcal{M}
can be computed in
2^{2^{O(n)}}
time.
Let us associate with each state
q^{S}
of
\mathcal{M}
one arbitrary state
\tilde{q}^{S}
of
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
that it merged.
We now define the transition function of
\mathcal{M}
.
Given a state
q^{S}
and a letter
x
,
define
\delta^{S}(q^{S},x)
to be the (unique) state
q^{S}_{\textit{next}}\in Q^{S}

such that
\lambda(q^{S}_{\textit{next}})=\tilde{\lambda}(\tilde{q}^{S}_{\textit{next}})
,
where
\tilde{q}^{S}_{\textit{next}}=\tilde{\delta}^{S}(\tilde{q}^{S},x)
and
\tilde{q}^{S}
is the state of
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
associated with
q^{S}
.
The label-equality check can be performed in
2^{O(n)}
time
for every given pair of states,
hence
\delta^{S}
can be computed in
2^{2^{O(n)}}
time.
The resulting
\mathcal{M}
is indeed a canonical SLTM,
because we started from the (naive) SLTM,
the operation of state merging preserves the definition of STLMs,
and also ensures the satisfaction of Equation
1
.
∎

Later, we will use the following properties P1–P4 of canonical SLTMs,
where properties P1–P3 follow easily from the definitions and P4 follows from the way we constructed the canonical SLTM in the proof of Lemma
1
.

P1.

For every state
q^{S}
of
\mathcal{M}
and
v\in q^{S}
,
there exists a prefix
p
:
v_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}v\textit{ and }L^{\textit{suff}}(p)=\lambda(q^{S})
.

P2.

For every
prefix word
p
,

v
s.t.
v_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}v
, and

q^{S}
s.t.
q_{0}^{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}q^{S}
:

q^{S}
contains
v
.

P3.

For every prefixes
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
with
L^{\textit{suff}}(p_{1})=L^{\textit{suff}}(p_{2})
:

\delta^{S}(q^{S}_{0},p_{1})=\delta^{S}(q^{S}_{0},p_{2})
,
i.e.,
canonical SLTMs have the minimal numbers of reachable states.

P4.

For every state
q^{S}
and prefix
p
:

\delta^{S}(q^{S}\!\!,p)=\bigcup_{v\in q^{S}}\delta^{\mathcal{G}}(v,p)
.

III-C

Saturated obligation graphs

Let us consider what happens when the set of initial vertices in obligation graphs is
saturated
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be a weak alternating automaton,

\mathcal{G}
its obligation graph with vertices
V
and initial vertices
V_{0}\subseteq V
, and

\mathcal{M}
its canonical SLTM whose initial state is
q^{S}_{0}\in 2^{V}
.

Initial-states-saturated
(or simply
saturated
for brevity) obligation graph is the obligation graph derived from
\mathcal{G}

by setting its initial vertices
V_{0}=q^{S}_{0}
.

Lemma 2
.

L(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})=L(\mathcal{G})
,
where
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
is the saturated obligation graph for
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

Recall that
q^{S}_{0}=\{v\mid\exists p:L^{\textit{suff}}(p)=L(\mathcal{A})\land\exists v_{0}\in V_{0}:v_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}v\}
.
Fix a word
w\in L(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})
.
It has an accepting run
\rho
starting in
v\in q^{S}_{0}
.
Since
v\in q^{S}_{0}
,
there exists a language-preserving prefix
p
and
v_{0}\in V_{0}
such that
v_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}v
.
The run
(v_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}v)\cdot\rho
is still accepting,
hence
p\cdot w\in L(\mathcal{G})
,
and since
p
preserves the suffix language,

w\in L(\mathcal{G})
.
The other direction is trivial.
∎

Finally, we are ready to state a crucial lemma,
which says says that in a saturated obligation graph,
the set of reachable vertices on a given prefix characterizes the suffix language.

Lemma 3
.

For every two prefixes
p_{1},p_{2}\in\Sigma^{*}
:

L^{\textit{suff}}(p_{1})=L^{\textit{suff}}(p_{2})~\Leftrightarrow~\delta^{\mathcal{G}}(V_{0}^{\mathcal{G}}\!,p_{1})=\delta^{\mathcal{G}}(V_{0}^{\mathcal{G}}\!,p_{2}),

where
\mathcal{G}
is the saturated obligation graph for a given weak alternating automaton with language
L
.

Proof.

The left implication holds already for non-saturated obligation graphs.
Indeed,
L=\overline{L(\mathcal{G})}
,
so

L^{\textit{suff}}(p)=\overline{L(\mathcal{G})^{\textit{suff}}(p)}=\overline{\bigcup_{v\in\delta^{\mathcal{G}}(v_{0},p)}L(\mathcal{G}_{v})}
,
where
v_{0}
is the initial state of the non-saturated obligation graph.
For
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, the latter language expressions are equal, hence

L^{\textit{suff}}(p_{1})=L^{\textit{suff}}(p_{2})
.

The direction
\Rightarrow
follows from properties P
3
and P
4
:

\delta^{\mathcal{G}}(V_{0}^{S}\!,p_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textit{by P\ref{SLTM:P4}}}}{{=}}\delta^{S}(q_{0}^{S}\!,p_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textit{by P\ref{SLTM:P3}}}}{{=}}\delta^{S}(q_{0}^{S}\!,p_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textit{by P\ref{SLTM:P4}}}}{{=}}\delta^{\mathcal{G}}(V_{0}^{S}\!,p_{2}).

∎

III-D

Constructing NCW representations of COCOA levels

This section describes how to construct the level automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}
of the COCOA
for the language of a given weak alternating automaton
\mathcal{A}
.
We first define a nondeterministic co-Büchi automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N}
,
then determinize it into
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{D}
, and finally turn it into a canonical GFG-NCW
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}
representing the
\ell
th COCOA level.

The nondeterministic automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N}
will be defined inductively for
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
,
where
k
is the number of COCOA levels for
L(\mathcal{A})
.
We do not need to know the number
k
beforehand:
since all levels have a non-empty language, the number
k
can be inferred.
Intuitively, the automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N}
is the product
\mathcal{A}^{\ell-1}_{N}\times\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{G}

of the previous level
\mathcal{A}^{\ell-1}_{N}
, the canonical SLTM
\mathcal{M}
, and the saturated obligation graph
\mathcal{G}
,
where the SLTM
\mathcal{M}
and graph
\mathcal{G}
are built for
\mathcal{A}\,
/
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
for odd/even
\ell
.
The acceptance condition of
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N}
inherits the acceptance condition of the previous level
\mathcal{A}^{\ell-1}_{N}
and of the graph
\mathcal{G}
,
modulo suffix-language-invariant insertions.

We proceed to the definition.
Let
\mathcal{A}=(\Sigma,Q,q_{0},\delta,F)
be a weak alternating automaton.
Let
\mathcal{A}^{0}_{N}
be the co-Büchi single-state automaton with the universal language
\Sigma^{\omega}

(it is not a part of the COCOA).
Fix
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, and suppose the automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\ell-1}_{N}=(\Sigma,Q^{\ell-1}\!,Q^{\ell-1}_{0}\!,\delta^{\ell-1}\!,F^{\ell-1})
has already been constructed (and its language is not empty).
Let
\mathcal{G}=(\Sigma,V,V_{0},\delta^{\mathcal{G}})
be the saturated obligation graph and

\mathcal{M}=(\Sigma,L^{\textit{suff}},Q^{S}\!,q^{S}_{0},\delta^{S}\!,\lambda)
be the canonical SLTM,
both for
\mathcal{A}\,
/
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
for odd/even
\ell
,
where
L^{\textit{suff}}
are the suffix languages of
\mathcal{A}\,
/
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
for odd/even
\ell
.

Definition 1

(nondeterministic
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N}
)
.

We define the following weak nondeterministic transition-based co-Büchi automaton

\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N}=(\Sigma,Q^{\ell}\!,Q^{\ell}_{0},\delta^{\ell}\!,F^{\ell})
:

•

The set
Q^{\ell}\!\subseteq Q^{\ell-1}\!\times Q^{S}\!\times V
consists of the states reachable under
\delta^{\ell}
from the initial states.

•

The initial states
Q^{\ell}_{0}=Q^{\ell-1}_{0}\times\{q_{0}^{S}\}\times V_{0}
.

•

For given
q^{\ell-1}\!,q^{S}
​,
v
, and
x
:

\displaystyle\delta^{\ell}((q^{\ell-1}\!,q^{S}\!\!,v),x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta^{\ell-1}

\displaystyle(q^{\ell-1}\!,x)\times\{\delta^{S}(q^{S}\!\!,x)\}\times\delta^{\mathcal{G}}(v,x).

•

To define accepting transitions,
we decompose the structure into maximal strongly connected components

\{(Q_{1}^{\ell},\delta_{1}^{\ell}),\ldots,(Q_{n}^{\ell},\delta_{n}^{\ell})\}
;
the transient transitions are set to be rejecting.

Consider one such SCC
(Q^{\ell}_{i},\delta^{\ell}_{i})
.
Since the automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\ell-1}_{N}
is weak,
all states of the SCC
(Q^{\ell}_{i},\delta^{\ell}_{i})
have the
\mathcal{A}^{\ell-1}_{N}
-components from the same SCC of
\mathcal{A}^{\ell-1}_{N}
.
Let
(Q^{\ell-1}_{i},\delta^{\ell-1}_{i})
be the SCC of
\mathcal{A}^{\ell-1}_{N}
to which all the
\mathcal{A}^{\ell-1}_{N}
components in
(Q^{\ell}_{i},\delta^{\ell}_{i})
belong to.
(Thus,
when a run of
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N}
loops in
(Q^{\ell}_{i},\delta^{\ell}_{i})
,
the simulated run of
\mathcal{A}^{\ell-1}_{N}
loops in
(Q^{\ell-1}_{i},\delta^{\ell-1}_{i})
.)

The transitions of
\delta_{i}^{\ell}
are either all accepting or all rejecting; the transitions are accepting if and only if the following two conditions are satisfied.

(a1)

The SCC
(Q^{\ell-1}_{i}\!,\delta^{\ell-1}_{i})
is an accepting SCC of
\mathcal{A}^{\ell-1}_{N}
.

(a2)

The set
Q^{\ell}_{i}
contains a state
(q^{\ell-1}\!,q^{S}\!,v)
where
v
is a
\mathcal{G}
-accepting state.

The definition is simpler when
\ell=1
:
the transition function becomes

\delta^{\ell}((q^{S}\!\!,v),x)=\{\delta^{S}(q^{S}\!\!,x)\}\times\delta^{\mathcal{G}}(v,x)
,
and the acceptance definition uses only the condition (a2).

Recall that we defined
\mathcal{G}
depending on
\ell
in such a way that
L(\mathcal{G})=\overline{L(\mathcal{A})}
for odd
\ell
and
L(\mathcal{G})=L(\mathcal{A})
for even
\ell
.

Lemma 4
.

For
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
where
k
is the number of COCOA levels for
L(\mathcal{A})
,
every word accepted by
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N}
has a natural color of at least
\ell
:

L(\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N})~\subseteq~\big\{w\mid\forall J.\,\exists\textnormal{SLII}^{L(\mathcal{A})}_{J,w}w^{\prime}:w^{\prime}\in L(\mathcal{A}^{\ell-1}_{N})\cap L(\mathcal{G})\big\}.

Proof.

Consider a word
w
accepted by
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N}
.
For a given index set
J
,
we will build an SLII
w^{\prime}
with an accepting run in
\mathcal{G}
and in
\mathcal{A}^{\ell-1}_{N}
.

The original word
w
has an accepting run
\rho
in
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N}

that gets trapped in some SCC
(Q^{\ell}_{i},\delta^{\ell}_{i})
of
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N}

consisting solely of accepting transitions.
Consider an arbitrary state
(q^{\ell-1}_{1}\!,q^{S}_{1}\!,v_{1})
that appears infinitely often in the run when looking only at positions
J
,
and let
J^{\prime}\subseteq J
be those positions.
By definition of
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N}
,
from the state
(q^{\ell-1}_{1}\!,q^{S}_{1}\!,v_{1})
,
there exists
a path

(q^{\ell-1}_{1}\!,q^{S}_{1}\!,v_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{x_{1}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\ldots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{x_{m-1}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}(q^{\ell-1}_{m}\!,q^{S}_{m},v_{m})

in
(Q^{\ell}_{i},\delta^{\ell}_{i})

such that the
\mathcal{G}
-path

v_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{x_{1}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\ldots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{x_{m-1}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}v_{m}

visits a
\mathcal{G}
-accepting state.
We now construct an auxiliary finite word
u
and a finite path
\pi
.
Consider the following finite word
u=x\cdot y
:
it starts with the prefix
x=x_{1}\ldots x_{m-1}
mentioned previously,
followed by a finite suffix
y\in\Sigma^{*}
that brings
(q^{\ell-1}_{m}\!,q^{S}_{m},v_{m})
back to
(q^{\ell-1}_{m}\!,q^{S}_{1},v_{1})
;
such a suffix
y
exists since both states belong to the same SCC of
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N}
.
Let
\pi
be the corresponding finite path in
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N}
on reading
u
.
We insert
u
at positions
J^{\prime}
of
w
and nothing in other positions
J\setminus J^{\prime}
,
which yields the word
w^{\prime}
.
Note that since the SLTM’s state is the same before and after reading
u
,
each insertion of
u
preserves the suffix language wrt.
L(\mathcal{A})
for odd
\ell
and wrt.
\overline{L(\mathcal{A})}
for even
\ell
.
Preserving the suffix language wrt.
\overline{L(\mathcal{A})}
implies preserving the suffix language wrt.
L(\mathcal{A})
.
Thus,
w^{\prime}\in\text{SLII}^{L(\mathcal{A})}_{J,w}
.
By inserting the path
\pi
into
\rho
at positions
J^{\prime}
,
we get the run
\rho^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N}
on
w^{\prime}
.
By our construction,
the projection of
\rho^{\prime}
on the vertices of
\mathcal{G}
yields an accepting run of
\mathcal{G}
.
Finally, by definition of acceptance of
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}_{N}
,
the
\mathcal{A}^{\ell-1}_{N}
-projection of the original run
\rho
loops in an accepting SCC of
\mathcal{A}^{\ell-1}_{N}
, and since the new run
\rho^{\prime}
stays within the same SCC of
\mathcal{A}^{\ell-1}_{N}
,

This section describes a procedure to translate a weak alternating automaton into a deterministic parity automaton.

Every word has a unique run in
\mathcal{P}

We now define the product.
Given a weak alternating automaton

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we provided a direct translation procedure from weak alternating automata to a chain-of-co-Büchi automata representation and to deterministic parity automata. Together with the straight-forward encoding of linear temporal logic (LTL) into alternating weak automata, the procedure provides a novel way of translating from LTL to deterministic parity automata (and COCOA) that is arguably simpler than previous constructions when considering all required steps together.

The new approach exploits the definition of the natural color of words from
[
5
]
.
This enables basing the definition of which words to accept at a level
L^{i}
on analyzing SCCs in a graph structure, called obligation graph, that is easy to build from an alternating weak automaton.
To account for the fact that the definition of the natural color reasons about suffix languages,
we compose the graph with a suffix language tracking machine.
Since COCOA has a falling chain of languages, we also compose it with a nondeterministic co-Büchi word automaton for the previous level.
We then determinize the result.
Finally, we minimize-canonize (when translating to COCOA) or build a product of all levels (when translating to parity automata).

Since linear dynamic logic (LDL) can be translated to weak alternating automata
[
8
]
,
our procedure is also suitable for LDL as a starting point.
The procedure can also be used for translating non-weak alternating automata,
by first converting them into weak ones with a quadratic blow-up in the number of states
[
13
]
.
Finally, our procedure computes highly structured automata, allowing for the application of heuristic optimization techniques to the intermediate automata, which is crucial for efficient implementations.
