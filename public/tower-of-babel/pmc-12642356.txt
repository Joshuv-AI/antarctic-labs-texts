Resourcefulness and resilience: jugaad in the lives of middle-aged women
Authors: Reenu Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This exploratory study investigates the creative problem-solving strategies—specifically jugaad—employed by middle-aged women as they navigate the complexities of balancing work, home, financial, and health-related responsibilities. The research aims to understand how women adapt and innovate amidst response to resource constraints to meet competing demands across life domains. A cross-sectional survey for the pilot study was conducted with a sample of 20 women aged 40 and above, born and raised in India but currently residing in diverse urban settings around the world. By focusing on this group, the study aimed to generate preliminary insights into the adaptive behaviors and coping mechanisms shaped by cultural context and lived experiences. Participants completed a self-administered questionnaire capturing both quantitative (Likert-scale and multiple-choice) and qualitative (open-ended) responses on their use of “jugaad”—creative problem-solving strategies—in managing work, home, finances, and health. Data were anonymized, and quantitative responses were analyzed using regression and correlation methods. While qualitative responses were thematically examined to enrich understanding of participants’ lived experiences. Findings revealed that age, health concerns, and financial pressures were significant predictors of the use of resourceful strategies - jugaad. Those aged between 40 and 50 were more likely to utilize time management techniques and low-cost solutions. Age, health, and financial pressures significantly impacts the adoption of these strategies. Women relied on health-focused problem-solving strategies. Introduction

In this study, jugaad – a phenomenon explained as a culturally specific, innovative way of solving problems using available resources - is explored from daily coping mechanisms. While coping refers broadly to strategies individuals use to manage stress or adapt to challenges, jugaad - rooted in Indian sociocultural contexts - embodies a distinct form of frugal innovation, improvisation, and creative problem-solving under constraints. Women, often placed in multitasking roles with limited resources and support, are compelled to take charge of situations in order to pursue their personal and professional goals. They have perfected the art of jugaad in every aspect of life at work including at work and at home. A confirmation that women have a remarkable ability to devise unconventional, low cost solutions in response to systemic gaps and limited resource limitations. This reflects resilience and ingenuity [1, 2]. The study emphasizes jugaad not merely as a survival tactic but as a culturally embedded, adaptive mindset. This research contributes to innovation studies, behavioral science, and gender studies by showing how culturally informed resourcefulness shapes women’s responses to complex life demands across global contexts.

Jugaad integrates foundational constructs of resilience, adaptive behavior, and gendered innovation practices. Resilience is understood as the capacity to recover from stressors while maintaining functional stability, particularly relevant in women’s lives where multitasking and managing intersecting roles are routine [3]. Adaptive behavior encompasses the dynamic and often improvised actions people, more specifically women, take to meet situational demands, such as reconfiguring responsibilities or using available resources innovatively [4–6]. These behaviors often manifest as jugaad—a frugal, self-driven innovation strategy developed in response to systemic gaps or resource limitations1. Jugaad manifests across multiple domains of women’s lives. Financial and technological adaptability further illustrates how women use free or low-cost tools to overcome challenges, demonstrating digital resilience and a capacity for tech-enabled frugality [7].

Women demonstrate financial and technological adaptability through a range of context-specific strategies shaped by limited resources and systemic barriers. Financially, they adopt resource optimization, diversify income streams, engage in community-based savings or microfinance networks, and rely on cautious investment and savings practices to build resilience. Technologically, they learn through experiential methods, peer networks, and intergenerational exchange, while also improvising with shared devices or low-cost digital tools. This aligns with the social feminist view that women’s entrepreneurial behaviors, including informal innovation like jugaad, are shaped by gendered roles and access to social capital [8]. Likewise, women are adept in using resourcefulness in managing health and wellness. They often weave reminders in their daily routine. Oftentimes grouping minor tasks with reminders. Such health and cognitive management strategies, such as using visual, auditory, or tactile cues to remember medication routines, also exemplify how women use jugaad to navigate age-related memory decline and manage chronic conditions [9].

Jugaad as a gendered strategy

Adaptive behavior encompasses the dynamic and often improvised actions individuals take to meet situational demands, such as reconfiguring responsibilities or using available resources innovatively [10]. Women are multitaskers. They take upon themselves lot more responsibilities at home and at work than men. In the workplace, women’s use of jugaad often manifests as skillful management of multiple projects, strategic navigation of organizational dynamics, and inventive problem-solving to overcome structural constraints and achieve objectives. Women adapt their approaches contextually, whether by leveraging social networks, negotiating for improved opportunities, or developing alternative methods to accomplish tasks when conventional practices prove inadequate. Women frequently juggle numerous responsibilities at lower hierarchical levels, where the sheer volume of work adversely affects their mental and physical health. Prolonged work schedules further reduce opportunities for self-care or relaxation, exacerbating the strain they experience [11–13].

Despite these challenges, many women demonstrate notable resilience and ingenuity in pursuing improved living conditions. These emergent behaviors [14], including creative strategies to manage work and family responsibilities, present valuable insights for designing interventions that promote sustainable coping mechanisms. For example, informal arrangements, such as seeking a neighbor’s help to watch over toddlers—are frequently based on mutual trust and empathy rather than formal or financial agreements. Such practices reflect women’s capacity for resourcefulness, which enables them to manage roles more effectively, particularly in high-pressure environments [15].

At home, women have long been adept multitaskers. They often employ jugaad to maximize limited resources, ensuring family needs are met through creative solutions, such as repurposing household items or improvising meals from available ingredients. This practice of everyday problem-solving is not merely a temporary fix but a survival strategy embedded in daily life [16]. In their personal lives, women similarly use jugaad to adapt to changing circumstances—whether in managing finances, fulfilling social obligations, or responding to unexpected challenges—opting for solutions that may be unconventional but consistently effective.

Ultimately, jugaad extends beyond improvised solutions; it embodies a deeply adaptive and resilient mindset. For many women facing systemic limitations, it has evolved into a way of life that empowers perseverance and success despite structural and personal constraints. It reflects the ability to creatively mobilize available resources and continuously find practical pathways to progress against significant odds.

Cognitive challenges and creative management

Women are particularly susceptible to gender-specific health ailments, such as hypertension, forgetfulness, anxiety, depression, and chronic fatigue, due to the cumulative burden of paid employment combined with unpaid domestic work. The persistent need to balance professional duties, household management, and caregiving creates continuous physical and psychological stress, contributing over time to these adverse health outcomes [17, 18]. Such challenges may impair physical agility required for everyday movements, such as rising from a chair, turning quickly, or getting out of bed [19]. This decline in physical well-being further complicates daily functioning, making multitasking and self-management increasingly inseparable.

Research also suggests that older women may experience more forgetfulness than older men, particularly in certain types of memory decline. Several studies indicate that women are more prone to Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias compared to men, which can contribute to higher levels of memory impairment [20–26]. This has a notable impact on their day-to-day lives, making routine tasks such as managing medications, keeping appointments, or handling household responsibilities more challenging. Memory lapses can lead to serious consequences, such as missed or incorrect medication doses, which is especially concerning for women managing chronic health conditions. Emotionally, these difficulties can lead to anxiety, embarrassment, and a reluctance to participate in social or professional settings, ultimately affecting their confidence and sense of independence. For women balancing multiple roles across work and home, even minor forgetfulness can disrupt daily routines and increase mental stress. In some cases, early signs of cognitive decline may be dismissed as normal aging, delaying timely support, while in others, natural lapses may cause unnecessary worry. Addressing these challenges requires supportive strategies, including structured routines, assistive tools, cognitive stimulation, and empathetic social environments. Hormonal changes, particularly the decline in estrogen during menopause, have been linked to memory difficulties in women [27]. This affects their self-oral medication routines as well, which is a pressing issue these days among not just women but men alike. However, the extent of forgetfulness varies based on individual health, lifestyle, and genetic factors. While women may report more subjective memory complaints, men tend to show a greater decline in visuospatial memory and executive function with age.

Older women often adopt creative strategies, or jugaad tactics, to ensure they take their oral medication on time despite memory-related challenges. Common techniques include visual cues, such as placing medicine bottles in frequently visited spots like beside the toothbrush or near the coffee maker, to serve as reminders [28]. Some also use DIY (Do It Yourself) pill organizers, repurposing containers or labeled compartments to sort medications by day and time, reducing confusion. Household reminders, such as sticky notes on mirrors or calendars with checkboxes, further help track medication consumption. Auditory triggers, including kitchen timers or TV show schedules, also act as mental prompts, and some individuals rely on phone calls from family members. Many women link medication-taking to daily rituals, such as meal times or prayer sessions, reinforcing the habit through routine. Wearable reminders, such as tying ribbons or using rubber bands, serve as a physical cue, while digital adaptations like mobile alarms or recorded voice notes assist those who are more tech-savvy [29–31]. Additionally, community support networks within neighborhoods or senior groups provide further assistance by checking in on each other’s medication routines. This survey-based exploratory study aimed to investigate the prevalence, nature, and interconnectedness of creative coping strategies—commonly referred to as jugaad—among women managing career disruptions and work-life stress. The objective was not to generalize findings but to uncover emerging behavioral patterns that illustrate how participants navigate personal and professional challenges with resourcefulness and resilience.

Method

A survey was carried out to examine “jugaad” as a resourceful and creative problem-solving strategy employed by 20 women aged 40 and above in managing the challenges of balancing work, home, finances, and health responsibilities (Appendix D). The sample size of 20 was deemed appropriate given the purposive nature of the study and the focus on exploring detailed lived experiences of a homogeneous group. This size allowed for in-depth qualitative insights while maintaining feasibility. Similar sample sizes have been supported in previous exploratory studies [32–35] which suggests that data saturation often occurs within the first 12 interviews, with most themes identified by 20 interviews.

The survey employed a qualitative approach to explore the lived experiences of women in applying resourcefulness, adaptability, and innovation in their daily lives. Data were collected through an online, self-administered questionnaire designed not only to gather structured responses but also to capture rich, descriptive insights into participants’ personal experiences. Participants were asked to reflect on specific instances where they applied jugaad to solve work-related or technology-related problems, and to describe any personalized systems they had developed to manage daily responsibilities. They were also invited to share their perspectives on the role of jugaad as a survival strategy, particularly in the context of balancing work and home demands. The survey was conducted with full anonymity and voluntary participation, ensuring ethical rigor in data collection. A purposive sampling strategy was adopted, targeting women from similar educational backgrounds who were personally known or accessible to the researcher. This focus allowed the study to generate in-depth, contextual insights within a demographically comparable group, rather than striving for broad generalizability. Overall, the qualitative design enabled a nuanced understanding of how jugaad functions as a practical tool for problem-solving and personal innovation in women’s everyday lives.

Hence, the sample was limited by access and feasibility. The sample consisted of 20 women born and brought up in India, aged 40 years and above, residing in urban areas in different countries, including Dubai (2), India (12), Japan (1), North America (2), the Netherlands (1) and the United Kingdom (2). These countries were selected because the participants—though born and brought up in India—are currently working and residing in diverse global contexts that reflect varying degrees of urbanization, work cultures, and access to resources. Including women in both Western and Asian contexts offered comparative insight into how “jugaad” manifests across different socio-cultural and technological environments while still grounded in a shared Indian upbringing. The online survey remained open for responses, and one participant submitted their responses after the data analysis had already been completed. As a result, their data was not included in the final analysis. The respondents pursued similar programs during their undergraduate studies, while their postgraduate and doctoral degrees varied. Some were classmates, indicating shared social perceptions and a common upbringing. Currently, they are employed in diverse fields in different regions across the world.

The questionnaire was divided into six sections, each targeting a different area of life: Section A: Demographic Information included questions on age, educational background, occupation, marital status, and number of dependents. This section provided context for understanding the participants’ socio-economic profiles. Section B: Workplace Jugaad focused on how participants used creative solutions to meet work-related challenges, manage deadlines, and adapt their roles. Participants were asked to rate the frequency of unconventional solutions used at work and describe specific instances of workplace Jugaad. Section C: Home and Household Management captured how participants apply creativity to solve household problems, save money, manage childcare, and adapt meal plans when resources are limited. It included both Likert scale items and open-ended questions. Section D: Financial & Technological Adaptability examined how women handle financial constraints and adapt to technological challenges using free or low-cost tools. Questions in this section also explored the use of creative solutions to solve financial and tech-related issues. Section E: Coping with Stress and Health asked about stress management strategies, work-life balance, and how women innovate to adjust responsibilities when overwhelmed. This section also explored whether using Jugaad helps in reducing stress. Section F: Overall Perspective on Jugaad assessed participants’ views on the importance of Jugaad in their daily lives, including whether they believe it is essential for balancing work and home responsibilities. It also asked about areas in life where they feel Jugaad is most needed.

Besides the demographic questions, there were 5 mixed multiple choice and Likert scale items, including ‘Use of unconventional solutions’, Modifying job responsibilities to suit personal needs’, ‘Creating alternative meal plans based on limited ingredients’, ‘Finding creative ways of saving money at home’ and ‘Does Jugaad help in reducing stress’. In this study, the dataset included ordinal data and several Likert scale responses rated on a scale from 1 to 5. Multiple-choice responses were converted into individual columns and recoded into nominal values using binary coding (0 and 1). Likert-scale responses (e.g., “Always,” “Sometimes,” “Neutral,” “Never”) were converted into ordinal variables to preserve the true nature of the data and ensure methodological appropriateness. Likert items are best analyzed as ordinal, as they represent ranked categories without assuming equal intervals between them [36]. This treatment allows for the use of non-parametric statistical techniques, such as Spearman’s rank-order correlation, which are more suitable for ordinal data than parametric methods like Pearson’s correlation that require continuous, normally distributed variables [37]. Treating responses as ordinal also supports a more valid interpretation of relationships, acknowledging that a shift from “Neutral” to “Sometimes” does not necessarily carry the same weight as a shift from “Sometimes” to “Always.” Furthermore, this approach facilitated thematic integration by allowing researchers to meaningfully link ordinal responses with open-ended qualitative data (e.g., examples of jugaad use or stress-reduction strategies), enhancing the depth and contextual relevance of the analysis. The relationship between various demographic factors and the prevalence of creative problem-solving strategies was explored through regression analysis carried out in MS Excel. The regression model accounted for occupation, marital status, number of dependents, and other relevant variables as predictors of career breaks, work-related stress, and stress management behaviors.

Results

The descriptive statistics (Appendix A) reveal distinct behavioral patterns in both work and home settings, underscoring the widespread use of adaptive and frugal strategies, often associated with jugaad. Respondents reported frequently modifying job responsibilities to fit personal needs (mean = 3.75) and using unconventional solutions in the workplace (mean = 3.55). At home, creative approaches to financial and household management were especially prominent, such as saving money (mean = 4.3) and creating alternative meal plans with limited ingredients (mean = 4.15).

Participants also reported using digital tools (mean = 0.9) and low-cost methods like DIY solutions (mean = 0.75), reflecting an orientation toward affordable innovations. While strategies like working harder during unexpected challenges (mean = 0.7) and innovating new approaches (mean = 0.75) were common, reliance on community support (mean = 0.05), neighbors (mean = 0.3), or family (mean = 0.85) was comparatively lower. This indicates a preference for self-driven coping mechanisms rather than collective support. Emotional coping strategies such as seeking help (mean = 0.7), talking to someone (mean = 0.75), or taking breaks (mean = 0.75) were reported at moderate levels. Notably, a low score for “pushing through without adjusting anything” (mean = 0.4) suggests participants favored adaptation over endurance. Importantly, the perception that jugaad helped reduce stress (mean = 3.8) reinforces its significance as a coping strategy. Together, these findings highlight strong individual agency, adaptability, and resourcefulness.

Correlational insights and reliability of jugaad-related behaviors

Spearman’s rank-order correlations (Fig. 1) demonstrate meaningful associations between behavioral and demographic variables. The strongest positive relationship was between use of jugaad at work and perception of stress reduction (ρ = 0.52), showing that improvised solutions are not only practical but also psychologically beneficial.

Similarly, modifying job responsibilities correlated with stress relief (ρ = 0.49), emphasizing the adaptive value of flexibility in work arrangements. Career breaks were strongly associated with the use of digital tools (ρ = 0.54), suggesting that individuals experiencing interruptions leaned on accessible technologies for reintegration and multitasking.

A moderate correlation was also observed between household money-saving practices and stress relief through jugaad (ρ = 0.42). On the contrary, negative correlations emerged between occupation type and career breaks (ρ = −0.55) and between digital tool use and role innovation (ρ = −0.41), indicating possible occupational constraints or preferences for tech-driven adaptation over task restructuring. Overall, the analysis highlights the interdependence of jugaad behaviors across domains—work, home, and finance—and their relationship with stress management. While causality cannot be inferred, these correlations point to behavioral flexibility and creative problem-solving as central to navigating complex demands.

Reliability testing of survey items yielded a Cronbach’s Alpha of 0.7534 (Appendix B), indicating moderate to good internal consistency across variables [38, 39] such as career breaks, occupation, dependents, financial strategies, childcare, and home management. This suggests the items reliably measure the underlying construct of jugaad-related behavior, though refinement (e.g., item-total analysis) could further strengthen reliability [40].

Predictors of career breaks

The regression analysis conducted to explore the factors contributing to career breaks indicated a strong explanatory power for the model, accounting for approximately 91% of the variation in the dependent variable (Career breaks) (R² = 0.91). The overall model is statistically significant (F = 6.16, p = 0.0115), suggesting that at least some of the predictor variables meaningfully contribute to explaining career breaks. Occupation (β = −0.30, p = 0.0185) emerges as a significant negative predictor, indicating that certain job roles may be linked to a decrease in the likelihood of taking career breaks. Similarly, marital status (β = −0.88, p = 0.0494) shows a significant negative effect, implying that married individuals might have fewer career breaks, possibly due to shared family responsibilities or financial constraints. The number of dependents (β = 0.61, p = 0.0505) shows a marginally significant positive relationship with career breaks, suggesting that individuals with more dependents may be more likely to take career breaks due to the increased responsibilities associated with family care. However, other variables such as “Worked Harder to Handle Unexpected Challenges” (β = 0.29, p = 0.57), “DIY Solutions” (β = 0.41, p = 0.46), and “Home Management” (β = −0.06, p = 0.91) did not show significant effects, suggesting that adaptive behaviors related to problem-solving or home management may not be strongly associated with career breaks in this context. Key constructs of jugaad

The survey further revealed five key constructs, These are as follows – (1) Resourcefulness and Innovation was vividly demonstrated by participants’ frequent use of creative, low-cost solutions to overcome workplace and household challenges. Examples include repurposing household items such as using reusable jars for plants, turning storage box covers into palettes, and converting old scarves into furniture covers. Professionally, improvised solutions like automating repetitive tasks with free scripts or modifying building facades reflect bounded rationality—making the best decisions within constraints—and adaptive preferences to optimize resources. This construct highlights a pragmatic, inventive mindset that blends sustainability with frugal innovation. (2) The Work-Life Balance and Task Management construct was exemplified through participants’ varied childcare strategies and flexible work arrangements, such as exchanging employee duties temporarily or adjusting work hours to meet client needs. Many respondents relied heavily on family support (parents, in-laws, siblings) and informal community networks to manage childcare when formal options were unavailable. These findings align with gender studies literature that explores the disproportionate caregiving burdens women shoulder and how they negotiate overlapping roles. The ability to juggle these demands while maintaining productivity reflects complex self-management shaped by socio-cultural contexts. (3) Under Adaptation and Learning, respondents described quickly acquiring new skills or adjusting work environments—such as working from unconventional locations (bed, dining table) or learning online to avoid hiring external help. These narratives underscore resilience theory’s emphasis on cognitive and behavioral flexibility as key to navigating changing conditions without external reliance. This agility in learning and adaptation is critical in sustaining professional performance amid evolving challenges. (4) The Sustainability and Cost Efficiency construct emerged through recurring themes of eco-consciousness and budget mindfulness. Participants reported creatively repurposing items, such as turning cardboard into furniture or reusing fabric as curtains, effectively integrating environmental sustainability with financial prudence. This dual focus illustrates jugaad’s role not just as a problem-solving approach but also as a responsible stewardship of resources. (5) Finally, Flexibility and Adaptability in Work Environments was captured through accounts of coping with technological failures, power outages, and pandemic-related shifts to remote work. Improvisations like patching multiple GIS layers manually or repurposing an old room heater reflect adaptive resilience in the workplace, where unpredictable disruptions require rapid, innovative responses.

The findings underscore how jugaad is deeply embedded in daily life and professional practice, serving as both a coping mechanism and an innovation strategy. It is closely tied to socio-economic realities, caregiving responsibilities, and the demonstration of resilience under constraints.

Discussion

Workplace adaptability and financial decision-making share common cognitive and behavioral traits, such as risk-taking, resourcefulness, and improvisation. Individuals who apply Jugaad in their workplace (e.g., improvising solutions with limited resources) may naturally extend these skills to their financial decisions, finding alternative ways to manage expenses or investments under constraints. This explains the strong correlation between Workplace Jugaad and Financial Decisions (r = 8). Women, particularly those balancing work and home responsibilities, experience improved psychological health when they have structured home management strategies, reducing stress and increasing overall well-being [41]. The ability to manage multiple small tasks simultaneously, coupled with strong organizational and prioritization skills, closely aligns with resourcefulness. As a crucial aspect of adaptive problem-solving, resourcefulness influences how individuals handle healthcare, nutrition, and stress management [42]. Therefore, women with good management skills are well-equipped to effectively oversee both their own and their family’s health and well-being (r = 7).

When faced with challenges like managing childcare and unexpected life events, parents often use a jugaad approach to resolve issues efficiently. They may need to work harder in the sense of being stretched thin, such as finding ways to manage both their child’s needs and their personal or social commitments. For instance, if a family member suddenly requires help or there’s a social obligation to fulfill, parents might figure out how to meet those needs while still ensuring their child’s well-being. This resourceful problem-solving often demands more time and energy, leading to working harder (r = 7). The strong positive correlations observed between occupation and creative ways to meet work deadlines and occupation and workplace stress suggest that individuals in demanding or high-pressure jobs tend to adopt creative problem-solving strategies and experience increased work stress [43, 44]. Moreover, the positive relationship between workplace stress and community support aligns with literature indicating that employees often rely on social networks, including family and community, to cope with work-induced stress [45]. However, the negative correlation between marital status and occupation implies that more demanding occupations may lead to less time spent with spouses, affecting marital satisfaction. Similarly, the inverse relationship between occupation and health and well-being is consistent with findings that high occupational stress negatively impacts long-term health outcomes [46]. The low variance in age contributed insignificantly to the career breaks. The negative correlation between DIY solutions and financial decisions suggests that individuals who employ creative, resourceful strategies in their daily lives often make financial decisions based on necessity, a phenomenon highlighted in studies on resource-constrained individuals. Additionally, moderate correlations between occupation and quick-fix work-from-home adjustments and DIY solutions and workplace and financial decision support show that individuals in high-pressure roles often rely on workarounds and adjustments to balance work and personal life. Finally, several variables, including cutting costs and seeking emotional support, showed near-zero correlations, indicating that these behaviors may be influenced more by individual circumstances than by occupation, as noted in studies on personal coping mechanisms [47]. These findings suggest that work-related and family-related factors may exert a more substantial influence on career breaks than individual coping or management strategies. Further research with a larger sample or more refined variables could clarify the weaker relationships and provide deeper insights into the factors influencing career breaks.

The conducted regression analysis and correlation matrix, while providing valuable insights into potential relationships among the variables, may suffer from overfitting. This overfitting indicates that the model is too closely tailored to the current sample data and, as a result, may not generalize well to different samples or populations. Therefore, caution should be exercised when interpreting the model’s predictive capability beyond the present dataset. Nonetheless, the findings highlight probable relationships that warrant further investigation. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples can help refine the model, improve variable contributions, and develop a more robust and generalizable predictive framework. Future research should aim to test and validate the hypotheses identified in this study on a larger and more diverse population to deepen the understanding of jugaad and self-management techniques. For example, Hypothesis 1 suggests that greater use of jugaad strategies in the workplace is positively associated with perceived stress reduction, as evidenced by a moderate positive Spearman correlation (ρ = 0.52). This indicates that women who frequently apply creative and improvised problem-solving methods may experience psychological benefits related to stress mitigation. Testing this hypothesis further could clarify the role of jugaad as an adaptive coping mechanism across varied work environments. Similarly, Hypothesis 2 highlights occupation type as a significant negative predictor of career breaks (β = −0.30, p = 0.0185), pointing to how certain jobs with more flexibility or security may support continuous professional engagement. Future studies could explore this relationship in different occupational sectors to better understand systemic factors influencing women’s career trajectories. Lastly, Hypothesis 3 indicates that the number of dependents is positively associated with taking career breaks (β = 0.61, p = 0.0505), underscoring the impact of caregiving responsibilities on career interruptions. Investigating this association on a wider scale will help contextualize how family demands affect women’s self-management strategies and career decisions.

Limitations

A key limitation of the study lies in its sampling approach, which relied on purposive and snowball recruitment within networks of individuals with comparable educational and professional experiences. This may have introduced selection bias, potentially limiting the socioeconomic, cultural, and occupational diversity of the sample. As a result, the findings may not be fully generalizable to all working women, particularly those in informal, low-income, or non-professional settings. Future research should aim to mitigate this bias by employing stratified or randomized sampling techniques and recruiting participants through broader outreach channels, such as community organizations, public sector platforms, and online panels. While this method facilitated access to a group likely to engage meaningfully with the survey themes, we acknowledge it may have led to overrepresentation of individuals from similar educational and occupational backgrounds. Moreover, given the small sample and ordinal nature of data, these findings are not meant to be generalizable but serve as exploratory insights into behavioral tendencies that warrant deeper investigation through larger-scale studies. Future studies with a larger sample could further refine these models and investigate the long-term impact of such adaptive.

Conclusion

This study highlights the resourcefulness and adaptability of women in balancing various responsibilities across personal, professional, health and family domains. It demonstrated how modern women, facing pressures related to career, household management, and health, increasingly rely on creative problem-solving strategies—known as jugaad—to navigate daily challenges. The findings suggest that occupation, marital status, and the number of dependents play significant roles in shaping career breaks, with women in demanding jobs and those with larger families being more likely to experience interruptions in their careers. The study’s regression model explains 91% of the variance in career breaks, underlining the importance of external life factors like job structure and family obligations. Qualitative data further revealed recurring themes of adaptability, resilience, and cost-efficiency. Women employed a variety of tactics such as DIY solutions, creative time management, and cost-saving innovations to manage competing demands. Resourcefulness was especially evident in how they repurposed materials, adapted technologies, and reorganized tasks in both personal and work settings. These strategies not only facilitated practical solutions but also helped manage stress, suggesting that resourceful problem-solving can alleviate some of the pressures of balancing multiple roles. While jugaad serves as an effective short-term solution to resource constraints, the study also highlights potential concerns, including burnout and the sustainability of relying on such strategies long-term. In conclusion, this research sheds light on the intersection of innovation, adaptability, and work-life balance in middle aged women’s lives. It calls for further exploration into how creative problem-solving strategies can be nurtured and supported, especially in work environments that demand constant adaptation and flexibility. The findings suggest that both internal (individual coping mechanisms) and external (occupation, marital status, and dependents) factors significantly shape women’s experiences of career breaks and the methods they adopt to manage their daily responsibilities. The descriptive analysis reveals distinct trends in how participants adapt to personal and professional challenges using creative, resourceful strategies often associated with the concept of jugaad. Participants frequently reported modifying job responsibilities (mean = 3.75), using unconventional solutions at work (mean = 3.55), and finding creative ways to save money at home (mean = 4.3), suggesting a high reliance on adaptive behaviors. While support from colleagues and emotional coping strategies were moderately used, individual-driven approaches like working harder, innovating new methods, and relying on digital tools were more prominent. These patterns, along with moderate variability and clustering around higher response categories, point to the widespread integration of jugaad in everyday problem-solving. The findings underscore that women’s ability to innovate under constraints is key to managing career breaks and other aspects effectively.
