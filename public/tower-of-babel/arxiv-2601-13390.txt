Linear relations on star coefficients of the chromatic symmetric function
Rosa Orellana, Foster Tom

Abstract
. We prove that the coefficient of the star 𝔰 ​ 𝔱 21 n − 2 \mathfrak{st}_{21^{n-2}} in the chromatic symmetric function X G X_{G} determines whether a connected graph G G is 2 2 -connected. We also prove new linear relations on other star coefficients of chromatic symmetric functions. This allows us to find new bases for certain spans of chromatic symmetric functions. Finally, we relate the coefficient of the star 𝔰 ​ 𝔱 n \mathfrak{st}_{n} to acyclic orientations.

1.
Introduction

In 1995, Stanley
[
12
]
introduced the
chromatic symmetric function

X_{G}(\bm{x})
defined for any simple graph
G=(V,E)
and set of commuting variables
{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots
). The chromatic symmetric function is a generalization of the
chromatic polynomial

\chi_{G}(t)
, which can be recovered by setting
\chi_{G}(t)=X_{G}(\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{t\text{ ones}},0,\ldots)
. Although the chromatic polynomial
\chi_{G}(t)
can tell us whether
G
is connected,
2
-connected
[
14
]
, or whether
G
is a tree, it is completely unable to distinguish trees because
\chi_{T}(t)=t(t-1)^{n-1}
for all
n
-vertex trees
T
. By contrast, Stanley observed that trees can have different chromatic symmetric functions, which has led to the following conjecture:

Conjecture 1.1
.

[
12
]
If
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are non-isomorphic trees, then
X_{T_{1}}(\bm{x})\neq X_{T_{2}}(\bm{x})
.

The Tree Isomorphism Conjecture has been checked for all trees up to 29 vertices
[
8
]
and for the classes of caterpillars
[
10
]
, spiders
[
11
]
, and trees with diameter at most 5
[
6
]
. The chromatic symmetric function of a tree determines its subtree polynomial
[
11
]
and its generalized degree polynomial
[
2
,
5
]
, which in turn determines information such as its double-degree sequence and leaf adjacency sequence
[
9
]
. For general graphs, the chromatic symmetric function encodes information about acyclic orientations
[
12
]
and connected partitions
[
15
]
.

In this paper, we use the deletion-near-contraction relation of Aliste-Prieto, de Mier, Orellana, and Zamora
[
1
]
to study the expansion
X_{G}=\sum_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}\mathfrak{st}_{\lambda}
in the star basis for any simple connected graph. In Section
3
, we show how a single star coefficient characterizes whether
G
is
2
-connected.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G
be a connected graph with
n\geq 3
vertices and let
X_{G}=\sum_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}\mathfrak{st}_{\lambda}
. Then the coefficient
c_{21^{n-2}}
is nonzero if and only if
G
is
2
-connected.

This theorem is especially intriguing because we have an infinite family of examples demonstrating that
k
-connectivity for

cannot be determined from

.

In Section
4
, we prove new linear relations on other star coefficients

. For instance, we find formulas for star coefficients corresponding to partitions of the same length. These formulas were used in
[
3
]
to find the sizes of cycles in bicyclic graphs. In general, the sums studied in Section

\mathcal{C}_{n}=\text{span}_{\mathbb{Q}}\{X_{G}:\ G\text{ connected }n\text{-vertex graph}\}\text{ and }\mathcal{T}_{n}=\text{span}_{\mathbb{Q}}\{X_{T}:\ T\ n\text{-vertex tree}\}.

We also find a linear algebraic significance of the star graph

.

For

is the unique connected

Finally, in Section

to acyclic orientations of

4.
Bases for spans of chromatic symmetric functions

In this section, we prove new linear relations on star coefficients. This will give us new insight into the linear algebra of chromatic symmetric functions.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
G
be a connected
n
-vertex graph with

and let
X_{G}=\sum_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}\mathfrak{st}_{\lambda}
. Consider the sum of hook star coefficients

(4.1)

Then we have

(4.2)

h(G)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }G\neq\text{St}_{n},\\
1,&\text{ if }G=\text{St}_{n}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We use induction on
n

n=3

are the star
\text{St}_{3}
and the complete graph

) holds, so suppose that

. We also use induction on the number of internal edges of

, and all the other

. Now suppose that

has some internal edge

h(G)=h(G\setminus e)-h((G/e)^{\circ})+h((G/e)^{\multimap})=h(G\setminus e)-h(G/e)+h((G/e)^{\multimap}).

Our induction hypothesis allows us to calculate the right hand side of (

, and
(G/e)^{\multimap}=\text{St}_{n}

∎

We can use leading partitions to show that the path
P_{n}
is not a linear combination of other trees.

Corollary 4.7
.

The chromatic symmetric function
X_{P_{n}}
does not lie in the space

(4.5)

\text{span}_{\mathbb{Q}}\{X_{T}:\ T\ n\text{-vertex tree},\ T\neq P_{n}\}.

Proof.

If
n=3
, the path
P_{n}
is the only
n
-vertex tree, so suppose that
n\geq 4
. By Theorem
4.3
, the leading partition for
P_{n}=\text{Cat}_{21^{n-4}2}
is
\lambda=221^{n-4}
. All other trees
T
have at least three leaves, so the forest obtained by deleting all internal edges has at least three connected components of size at least
2
. Therefore, the leading partition is lexicographically larger than
\lambda
and
\mathfrak{st}_{221^{n-4}}
does not appear in the star expansion of
X_{T}
.
∎

We now show a linear relation that is satisfied by almost all trees.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
T
be a connected
n
-vertex tree with
n\geq 5
and let
X_{T}=\sum_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}\mathfrak{st}_{\lambda}
. Consider the sum of star coefficients

(4.6)

nh(T)=c_{221^{n-4}}+c_{321^{n-5}}+\cdots+c_{(n-3)21}+c_{(n-2)2}.

Then we have

(4.7)

nh(T)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }T\neq\text{Cat}_{(n-2)2},\ T\neq P_{n},\\
1,&\text{ if }T=\text{Cat}_{(n-2)2},\\
(-1)^{n-1},&\text{ if }T=P_{n}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We use induction on
n
. If
n=5
, the only possibilities for
T
are the star
\text{St}_{5}
, the caterpillar
\text{Cat}_{32}
, and the path
P_{5}
, and it is routine to check that (
4.7
) holds. We also use induction on the number of internal edges of
T
. If
T
has no internal edges, then
T=\text{St}_{n}
,
c_{n}=1
, and all the other
c_{\lambda}=0
, so
nh(\text{St}_{n})=0
. Now suppose that
T
has some internal edge
e=\{u,v\}
. By the DNC relation, we have

(4.8)

nh(T)=nh(T\setminus e)-nh((T/e)^{\circ})+nh((T/e)^{\multimap})=nh(T\setminus e)-nh(T/e)+nh((T/e)^{\multimap}).

Our induction hypothesis allows us to calculate the right hand side of (
4.8
). We consider different cases, depending on whether
T\setminus e
,
T/e
, and
(T/e)^{\multimap}
are paths or caterpillars.

We first consider
T\setminus e
. Because
T
is a tree and
e
is an internal edge,
T\setminus e
consists of trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
with some sizes
n_{1}\geq n_{2}\geq 2
. Suppose that
n_{2}\geq 3
. Now the trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
have at least three vertices and are not
2
-connected, so by Theorem
3.3
, their star coefficients
c_{21^{n_{1}-2}}(T_{1})
and
c_{21^{n_{2}-2}}(T_{2})
are both
0
. Therefore, the star coefficients
c_{a21^{n-a-2}}(T\setminus e)
are all
0
and
nh(T\setminus e)=0
. If
n_{2}=2
, then we have

(4.9)

nh(T\setminus e)=c_{21^{n-6}}(T_{1})+c_{31^{n-5}}(T_{1})+\cdots+c_{n-2}(T_{1})=h(T_{1})-c_{21^{n-6}}(T_{1}).

Because
T_{1}
has at least three vertices and is not
2
-connected, we have
c_{21^{n-6}}(T_{1})=0
by Theorem
3.3
. By Lemma
4.1
, we have
h(T_{1})=0
unless
T_{1}=\text{St}_{n-2}
, which can only occur if
T=\text{Cat}_{(n-2)2}
, or if
T=\text{Cat}_{(n-3)12}
and
e
is the internal edge where
T\setminus e=\text{St}_{(n-2)2}
. Therefore

(4.10)

nh(T\setminus e)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ if }T=\text{Cat}_{(n-2)2},\\
1,&\text{ if }T=\text{Cat}_{(n-3)12}\text{ and }T\setminus e=\text{St}_{(n-2)2},\\
0,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We next consider the trees
T/e
and
(T/e)^{\multimap}
. We have
T/e=P_{n-1}
if and only if
T=P_{n}
. We have
T/e=\text{Cat}_{(n-3)2}
if and only if
T
is either
\text{Cat}_{2(n-4)2}
or
\text{Cat}_{(n-3)12}
. Because
e
is an internal edge of
T
, the contracted vertex
uv
of
(T/e)^{\multimap}
has degree at least
3
so
(T/e)^{\multimap}
is never
P_{n}
. We have
(T/e)^{\multimap}=\text{Cat}_{(n-2)2}
if and only if either
T=\text{Cat}_{2(n-4)2}
, or
T=\text{Cat}_{(n-3)12}
and
e
is the internal edge where
T\setminus e=\text{St}_{(n-3)3}
. Putting this together, we have

(4.11)

nh(T)=\begin{cases}1-0+0=1,&\text{ if }T=\text{Cat}_{(n-2)2},\\
0-(-1)^{n-2}+0=(-1)^{n-1},&\text{ if }T=P_{n},\\
1-1+0=0,&\text{ if }T=\text{Cat}_{(n-3)12}\text{ and }T\setminus e=\text{St}_{(n-2)2},\\
0-1+1=0,&\text{ if }T=\text{Cat}_{(n-3)12}\text{ and }T\setminus e=\text{St}_{(n-3)3},\\
0-1+1=0,&\text{ if }T=\text{Cat}_{2(n-4)2},\\
0-0+0=0,&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

as desired.
∎

Corollary 4.9
.

The chromatic symmetric function
X_{\text{Cat}_{(n-2)2}}
does not lie in the space

(4.12)

\text{span}_{\mathbb{Q}}\{X_{T}:\ T\ n\text{-vertex tree},T\neq\text{Cat}_{(n-2)2}\}.

Proof.

Suppose that there are coefficients
a,a_{T}\in\mathbb{Q}
for which

(4.13)

X_{\text{Cat}_{(n-2)2}}=aX_{P_{n}}+\sum_{T\neq P_{n}}a_{T}X_{T}.

By Corollary
4.7
, we cannot write the path
P_{n}
as a linear combination of other trees, so we must have
a=0
. But now by Lemma
4.8
, we have
nh(\text{Cat}_{(n-2)2})=1
, while the corresponding star coefficient sum on the right hand side is
0
.
∎

We have seen that the star
\text{St}_{n}
, the path
P_{n}
, and the caterpillar
\text{Cat}_{(n-2)2}
cannot be written as linear combinations of other trees. We will show in Theorem
4.15
that for
n\geq 8
, these are the
only
trees with this property. We first find bases for the vector space

(4.14)

\mathcal{T}_{n}=\text{span}_{\mathbb{Q}}\{X_{T}:\ T\ n\text{-vertex tree}\}.

Gonzalez, Orellana, and Tomba found an elegant description of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
using the star basis.

Lemma 4.10
.

[
6
, Proposition 6.9]
The set of chromatic symmetric functions of caterpillars

(4.15)

B=\{X_{\text{Cat}_{\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}\cdots\lambda_{\ell}\lambda_{1}}}:\ \lambda=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\cdots\lambda_{\ell}\vdash n,\text{ either }\lambda=n\text{ or }\lambda_{2}\geq 2\}

is linearly independent.

Proof.

By Corollary
4.5
, these caterpillars have distinct leading partitions.
∎

They also proved the following relation on star coefficients with a given length.

Definition 4.11
.

Let
G
be an
n
-vertex graph and let
X_{G}=\sum_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}\mathfrak{st}_{\lambda}
. Fix an integer
1\leq\ell\leq n
. We define
\sigma_{\ell}(G)
to be the sum of star coefficients

(4.16)

\sigma_{\ell}(G)=\sum_{\lambda:\ \ell(\lambda)=\ell}c_{\lambda}.

Lemma 4.12
.

[
6
, Lemma 6.10]
If
T
is a tree and
\ell\geq 2
, then
\sigma_{\ell}(T)=0
.

Corollary 4.13
.

We have that

(4.17)

\mathcal{T}_{n}=\left\{\sum_{\lambda}a_{\lambda}\mathfrak{st}_{\lambda}:\ a_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{Q},\ \sum_{\ell(\lambda)=\ell}a_{\lambda}=0\text{ for every }2\leq\ell\leq n\right\}.

Moreover, the set
B
in (
4.15
) is a basis for
\mathcal{T}_{n}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\prime}
be the right hand side of (
4.17
). The inclusion
\mathcal{T}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\prime}
holds by Lemma
4.12
. By Lemma
4.10
, we have that
\dim\mathcal{T}_{n}\geq|B|=p(n)-n+1=\dim\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{n}
, where
p(n)
is the number of integer partitions of size
n
. Therefore
\mathcal{T}_{n}=\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\prime}
and
B
is a basis for
\mathcal{T}_{n}
.
∎

We will also need the following lemma for the caterpillars
\text{Cat}_{a1^{k}b}
and
\text{Cat}_{aaa}
, which are tricky to handle because they are the only trees with their leading partition. The proof is a lengthy calculation and not too enlightening so we will postpone it to the end of this section.

Lemma 4.14
.

(1)

Fix integers
a\geq b\geq 2
and
k\geq 0
with
a\geq 3
,
a+b+k\geq 8
, and either
b\geq 3
or
k\geq 1
. The caterpillar
X_{\text{Cat}_{a1^{k}b}}
can be written as a linear combination of other trees.

(2)

Fix an integer
a\geq 3
. The caterpillar
X_{\text{Cat}_{aaa}}
can be written as a linear combination of other trees.

Theorem 4.15
.

Let
n\geq 8
and let
T
be an
n
-vertex tree other than
\text{St}_{n}
,
P_{n}
, and
\text{Cat}_{(n-2)2}
. Then
X_{T}
can be written as a linear combination of other trees.

Proof.

By Corollary
4.13
, the caterpillars in (
4.15
) form a basis for
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, so we need only consider consider the case where
T
is one of these caterpillars. Let
T=\text{Cat}_{\alpha}
and
\lambda=\text{sort}(\alpha)
. Because
T\neq\text{St}_{n}
, we have
\lambda_{2}\geq 2
. Our strategy will be to find another tree
T^{\prime}
with the same leading partition
\lambda
. Then the set
B^{\prime}=B\setminus\{X_{T}\}\cup\{X_{T^{\prime}}\}
will be linearly independent because the leading partitions are distinct. Because
|B^{\prime}|=|B|=\dim\mathcal{T}_{n}
, the set
B^{\prime}
will be a basis for
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and we can write
X_{T}
as a linear combination of trees in
B^{\prime}
, as desired.

Let
t\geq 2
be maximal with
\lambda_{t}\geq 2
, so that
\lambda
is of the form
\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{t}1^{k}
for some
k\geq 0
. First suppose that
t\geq 4
and
k=0
. In this case, we can take
T^{\prime}
to be the tree obtained from the star
\text{St}_{t}
by attaching
(\lambda_{t}-1)
leaves to the central vertex and attaching
(\lambda_{i}-1)
leaves to the
i
-th leaf of
\text{St}_{t}
for
1\leq i\leq t-1
. Then
X_{T^{\prime}}
has leading partition
\lambda
and
T^{\prime}
is not a caterpillar because its non-leaves form the star
\text{St}_{t}
, not a single path.

The same construction works if
t=3
and
k\geq 1
. If
t\geq 4
and
k\geq 1
, we can take
T^{\prime}
to be the tree obtained from the caterpillar
\text{Cat}_{(t-1)1^{k-1}2}
, attaching
(\lambda_{i}-1)
leaves to the
i
-th leaf of the degree-
(t-1)
vertex for
1\leq i\leq t-2
, and attaching
(\lambda_{t-1}-1)
leaves to the other leaf of
\text{Cat}_{(t-1)1^{k-1}2}
. Again,
X_{T^{\prime}}
has leading partition
\lambda
and
T^{\prime}
is not a caterpillar because its non-leaves form the caterpillar
\text{Cat}_{(t-1)1^{k-1}2}
, not a single path.

Now suppose that
t=3
and
k=0
, so
T=\text{Cat}_{\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}\lambda_{1}}
. If
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{3}
, then the tree
T^{\prime}=\text{Cat}_{\lambda_{2}\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3}}
also has leading partition
\lambda
and we are done as before. If
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{3}
, then
T=\text{Cat}_{aaa}
for some
a\geq 3
and
X_{T}
can be written as a linear combination of other trees by Part (2) of Lemma
4.14
.

Finally, if
t=2
, then
T=\text{Cat}_{a1^{k}b}
for some
a\geq b\geq 2
and
k\geq 0
. Because
T\neq P_{n}
, we must have
a\geq 3
, and because
T\neq\text{Cat}_{(n-2)2}
, we have either
b\geq 3
or
k\geq 1
. Therefore
X_{T}
can be written as a linear combination of other trees by Part (1) of Lemma
4.14
.
∎

Remark 4.16
.

We can also use computer calculations to describe the cases of
n\leq 7
. When
n\leq 5
, the only trees that are not linear combinations of others are still
P_{n}
,
\text{Cat}_{(n-2)2}
, and
\text{St}_{n}
, although these can coincide (for example,
P_{3}=\text{St}_{3}
and
P_{4}=\text{Cat}_{22}
). When
n=6
, all six trees are linearly independent because they have distinct leading partitions. For
n=7
, the trees that are not linear combinations of others are
P_{7}
,
\text{St}_{7}
,
\text{Cat}_{52}
, and
\text{Cat}_{43}
.

We now return to the vector space spanned by all connected graphs

(4.18)

\mathcal{C}_{n}=\text{span}_{\mathbb{Q}}\{X_{G}:\ G\text{ connected }n\text{-vertex graph}\}.

We can extend a basis of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
to a basis of
\mathcal{C}_{n}
by adding certain connected
unicyclic
graphs, which are graphs with a single cycle.

Definition 4.17
.

Let
c\geq 3
and
\ell\geq 0
. The
cuttlefish

\text{Cut}_{c,\ell}
is the
(c+\ell)
-vertex graph formed by taking a cycle of
c
vertices and attaching
\ell
leaves to a fixed vertex
v
.

Gonzalez, Orellana, and Tomba also identified the leading partition for connected unicyclic graphs.

Theorem 4.18
.

[
3
, Theorem 5.6]

Let
G
be a connected unicyclic graph that consists of a tree attached to a vertex
v
of a cycle. Let
e
be an edge of the cycle incident to
v
and consider the tree
T=G\setminus e
. Then the leading partition of
X_{T}
is the same as that of
X_{G}
.

Corollary 4.19
.

The cuttlefish chromatic symmetric function
X_{\text{Cut}_{c,n-c}}
has leading partition
\lambda=(n-c+1)21^{c-3}
.

Lemma 4.20
.

The set of chromatic symmetric functions of cuttlefish

(4.19)

B^{\prime}=\{X_{\text{Cut}_{c,n-c}}:\ 3\leq c\leq n\}

is linearly independent.

Proof.

By Corollary
4.19
, these cuttlefish have distinct leading partitions.
∎

In order to show that cuttlefish are independent from caterpillars, we will use the following relation for the sum of star coefficients in a given length.

Lemma 4.21
.

Let
G
be a connected unicyclic graph with a cycle of length
c
and fix an integer
1\leq\ell\leq n
. Then we have

(4.20)

\sigma_{\ell}(G)=(-1)^{\ell-1}\binom{c-1}{\ell}.

Proof.

We use induction on
c\geq 3
and the DNC relation. First suppose that
c=3
. If
T
is a tree, then
\sigma_{\ell}(T)=0
if
\ell\geq 2
by Lemma
4.12
and
\sigma_{1}(T)=c_{n}(T)=1
by
[
6
, Section 3]
. Let
e
be an edge of the cycle of
G
and note that
G\setminus e
,
G/e
, and
(G/e)^{\multimap}
are trees. Then we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{\ell}(G)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\ell}(G\setminus e)-\sigma_{\ell}((G/e)^{\circ})+\sigma_{\ell}((G/e)^{\multimap})=\sigma_{\ell}(G\setminus e)-\sigma_{\ell-1}(G/e)+\sigma_{\ell}((G/e)^{\multimap})

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{cases}1-0+1=2,&\text{ if }\ell=1,\\
0-1+0=-1,&\text{ if }\ell=2,\\
0,&\text{ if }\ell\geq 3,\end{cases}\right)=(-1)^{\ell-1}\binom{3-1}{\ell}.

Similarly, for
c\geq 4
, if
e
is an edge of the cycle, then
G\setminus e
is a tree, while
(G/e)
and
(G/e)^{\multimap}
are connected unicyclic graphs with a cycle of length
(c-1)
. Therefore, by induction, we have for
\ell\geq 2
that

\displaystyle\sigma_{\ell}(G)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\ell}(G\setminus e)-\sigma_{\ell}((G/e)^{\circ})+\sigma_{\ell}((G/e)^{\multimap})=\sigma_{\ell}(G\setminus e)-\sigma_{\ell-1}(G/e)+\sigma_{\ell}((G/e)^{\multimap})

\displaystyle=0-(-1)^{\ell-2}\binom{c-2}{\ell-1}+(-1)^{\ell-1}\binom{c-2}{\ell}=(-1)^{\ell-1}\binom{c-1}{\ell}.

Similarly, for
\ell=1
, we have
\sigma_{1}(G)=\sigma_{1}(G\setminus e)+\sigma_{1}((G/e)^{\multimap})=1+(c-2)=c-1
.
∎

Remark 4.22
.

This shows that a connected unicyclic graph
G
with a cycle of length
c
has
c_{n}=c-1
. Therefore, the size of the cycle can be read off from the star expansion of
G
.

Remark 4.23
.

If
G
is
bicyclic
, meaning it has exactly two cycles, a similar relation on
\sigma_{\ell}(G)
can be used to determine the cycle lengths from the star expansion of
X_{G}

[
3
, Theorem 7.6]
.

Corollary 4.24
.

The set of chromatic symmetric functions
B\cup B^{\prime}
is linearly independent, where
B
is given in (
4.15
) and
B^{\prime}
is given in (
4.19
).

Proof.

Suppose that we have a nontrivial linear dependence

(4.21)

\sum_{c=3}^{n}a_{c}X_{\text{Cut}_{c,n-c}}+\sum_{\lambda:\ \lambda_{2}\geq 2}a_{\lambda}X_{\text{Cat}_{\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}\cdots\lambda_{\ell}\lambda_{1}}}+a\mathfrak{st}_{n}=0.

By Lemma
4.10
, the caterpillars are linearly independent, so we must have
a_{c^{\prime}}\neq 0
for some maximal
3\leq c^{\prime}\leq n
. But now by Lemma
4.12
and Lemma
4.21
, we have
\sigma_{c^{\prime}}(T)=0
for every caterpillar
T
,
\sigma_{c^{\prime}}(\text{Cut}_{c,n-c})=0
for
c<c^{\prime}
, and
\sigma_{c^{\prime}}(\text{Cut}_{c^{\prime},n-c^{\prime}})\neq 0
. Therefore, by taking the sum of star coefficients of length
c^{\prime}
on each side, we get that
a_{c^{\prime}}=0
, a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 4.25
.

Let
n\geq 2
. We have that

(4.22)

\mathcal{C}_{n}=\left\{\sum_{\lambda}a_{\lambda}\mathfrak{st}_{\lambda}:\ a_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{Q},\ a_{1^{n}}=0\right\}.

Moreover, the set
B\cup B^{\prime}
is a basis for
\mathcal{C}_{n}
, where
B
is given in (
4.15
) and
B^{\prime}
is given in (
4.19
).

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{n}
be the right hand side of (
4.22
). The inclusion
\mathcal{C}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{n}
holds because when we use the DNC relation to calculate the star expansion of
X_{G}
, we only apply it to internal edges, so we can never get the star
\text{St}_{1^{n}}
in a DNC tree. By Corollary
4.24
, the set
B\cup B^{\prime}
is linearly independent, so we have that

(4.23)

\dim\mathcal{C}_{n}\geq|B|+|B^{\prime}|=(p(n)-n+1)+(n-2)=p(n)-1=\dim\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{n}.

Therefore
\mathcal{C}_{n}=\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{n}
and
B\cup B^{\prime}
is a basis for
\mathcal{C}_{n}
.
∎

The following lemma will give us some useful linear relations on cuttlefish.

Lemma 4.26
.

(1)

Fix integers
3\leq c\leq n-1
and let
G
be the graph obtained from
\text{Cut}_{c,n-c-1}
by attaching a leaf to a vertex in the cycle adjacent to the vertex of degree
(n-c+1)
. Then we have

(4.24)

X_{\text{Cut}_{c,n-c}}=X_{\text{Cat}_{(n-c+1)1^{c-3}2}}-X_{\text{Cat}_{(n-c)1^{c-2}2}}+X_{G}.

(2)

Let
C_{n}
be the cycle on
n\geq 4
vertices and let
G
be the graph obtained from

by adding a new vertex and joining it to two adjacent vertices of

. Then we have

(4.25)

X_{C_{n}}=X_{P_{n}}+X_{G}-X_{\text{Cut}_{n-1,1}}.

(3)

Let
G
be the graph obtained from
\text{St}_{n}
by adding two edges
e
and

joining a leaf

to two others. Then we have

(4.26)

X_{\text{Cat}_{(n-2)2}}=X_{\text{Cut}_{4,n-4}}-X_{G}+X_{(G/e)^{\multimap}}.

(1)

Let
e
be an edge of the cycle of
\text{Cut}_{c+1,n-c-1}
incident to the vertex of degree
(n-c+2)
. By the DNC relation, we have

(4.27)

X_{\text{Cut}_{c+1,n-c-1}}=X_{\text{Cat}_{(n-c)1^{c-2}2}}-X_{\text{Cut}_{c,n-c-1}}\mathfrak{st}_{1}+X_{\text{Cut}_{c,n-c}},

and by applying the DNC relation to an edge

adjacent to

in the cycle, we have

(4.28)

X_{\text{Cut}_{c+1,n-c-1}}=X_{\text{Cat}_{(n-c+1)1^{c-3}2}}-X_{\text{Cut}_{c,n-c-1}}\mathfrak{st}_{1}+X_{G}.

Now the result follows by subtracting (
4.28
) from (
4.27
).

(2)

By applying the DNC relation to an edge
e
of
C_{n}
, we have

(4.29)

X_{C_{n}}=X_{P_{n}}-X_{C_{n-1}}\mathfrak{st}_{1}+X_{\text{Cut}_{n-1,1}},

and by applying the DNC relation to an edge

not in the
(n-1)
-cycle of

, we have

(4.30)

X_{G}=X_{\text{Cut}_{n-1,1}}-X_{C_{n-1}}\mathfrak{st}_{1}+X_{\text{Cut}_{n-1,1}}.

Now the result follows by subtracting (
4.30
) from (
4.29
).

(3)

By applying the DNC relation to an edge of the cycle in
\text{Cut}_{4,n-4}
not incident to the vertex of degree
(n-2)
, we have

(4.31)

X_{\text{Cut}_{4,n-4}}=X_{\text{Cat}_{(n-2)2}}-X_{\text{Cut}_{3,n-4}}\mathfrak{st}_{1}+X_{\text{Cut}_{3,n-3}},

and by applying the DNC relation to the edge

of

, we have

(4.32)

X_{G}=X_{\text{Cut}_{3,n-3}}-X_{\text{Cut}_{3,n-4}}\mathfrak{st}_{1}+X_{(G/e)^{\multimap}}.

Now the result follows by subtracting (
4.32
) from (
4.31
).

∎

Theorem 4.27
.

Let
n\geq 4
and let
G
be a connected
n
-vertex graph other than
\text{St}_{n}
. Then
X_{G}
can be written as a linear combination of other connected
n
-vertex graphs.

Proof.

By Corollary
4.25
, the caterpillars in (
4.15
) and the cuttlefish in (

5.
The coefficient of
\mathfrak{st}_{n}
and acyclic orientations

In this section, we give an interpretation of the coefficient
c_{n}
in
X_{G}=\sum_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}\mathfrak{st}_{\lambda}
in terms of
acyclic orientations
of
G
.

Definition 5.1
.

An
acyclic orientation

\theta
of a graph
G=(V,E)
is an assignment of directions to the edges so that there are no directed cycles. A vertex
v\in V
is a
sink
of
\theta
if all edges incident to
v
are directed toward
v
.

Stanley
[
13
, Corollary 1.3]
proved that the number of acyclic orientations of
G
is
|\chi_{G}(-1)|
. Greene and Zaslavsky proved that the linear coefficient of the chromatic polynomial counts acyclic orientations of
G
with a particular sink.

Theorem 5.2
.

[
7
, Theorem 7.3]
Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and fix a vertex
v\in V
. Then
|\chi_{G}^{\prime}(0)|
is the number of acyclic orientations of
G
with unique sink
v
. In particular, this number does not depend on
v
.

Stanley further showed that the
e
-expansion of the chromatic symmetric function encodes even more information about acyclic orientations.

Theorem 5.3
.

[
12
, Theorem 3.3]

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph, let
X_{G}=\sum_{\lambda}d_{\lambda}e_{\lambda}
, and fix an integer
k
. Then

(5.1)

\sum_{\ell(\lambda)=k}d_{\lambda}=\text{the number of acyclic orientations of }G\text{ with exactly }k\text{ sinks.}

In particular, the coefficient of
e_{n}
is equal to the number of acyclic orientations with a unique sink, which by Theorem
5.2
is equal to
n|\chi_{G}^{\prime}(0)|
. We now show a similar result about the coefficient of
\mathfrak{st}_{n}
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an
n
-vertex graph, let
X_{G}=\sum_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}\mathfrak{st}_{\lambda}
, and fix a vertex
v\in V
. Then
c_{n}
is the number of acyclic orientations of
G
with unique sink
v
.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.3
, the coefficient of
e_{n}
in
\mathfrak{st}_{\lambda}
is the number of acyclic orientations of the star forest
\text{St}_{\lambda}
with a unique sink. Because every connected component must have a sink, this coefficient is
0
unless
\lambda=n
, in which case it is
n
. Therefore, if we write
X_{G}=\sum_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}\mathfrak{st}_{\lambda}=\sum_{\mu}d_{\mu}e_{\mu}
, we see that
c_{n}=\frac{1}{n}d_{n}=|\chi_{G}^{\prime}(0)|
is the number of acyclic orientations of
G
with unique sink
v
.
∎

This allows us to recover some known results connecting the coefficient
c_{n}
to properties of the graph
G
.

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an
n
-vertex graph and let
X_{G}=\sum_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}\mathfrak{st}_{\lambda}
.

(1)

We have
c_{n}\neq 0
if and only if
G
is connected.

(2)

[
6
, Section 3]
We have
c_{n}=1
if and only if
G
is a tree.

(3)

[
3
, Corollary 3.4]
If
G
is a connected unicyclic graph with a cycle of size
c
, then
c_{n}=c-1
.

Proof.

In each case, we fix a vertex
v\in V
and count acyclic orientations with unique sink
v
.

(1)

If
G
is not connected, then every acyclic orientation of
G
will have at least one sink in each component of
G
, so
c_{n}=0
. Conversely, if
G
is connected, then fix a vertex
v\in V
and order the vertices of
G
as follows. We start with
v
, and at each step, select a vertex adjacent to a previously chosen one. Because
G
is connected, every vertex will be selected at some point. Then we define an acyclic orientation of
G
by orienting each edge toward the vertex chosen earlier. This acyclic orientation will have unique sink
v
, so
c_{n}\geq 1
.

(2)

If
G
is a tree and
v\in V
is fixed, then the only acyclic orientation with unique sink
v
is that where each edge is directed toward the vertex closer to
v
. Conversely, if
G
is a connected graph with a cycle
C
, fix a vertex
v
of the cycle and let
u
and
w
be its neighbours. Because
v
is the unique sink, the edges
\{u,v\}
and
\{v,w\}
must be directed toward
v
. The other edges of
C
can be directed as either a path from
u
to
w
or a path from
w
to
u
. Therefore, there are at least two acyclic orientations of
C
with unique sink
v
. These acyclic orientations of
C
can be extended to those of
G
by ordering the remaining vertices as in Part (1).

(3)

Fix a vertex
v
of the cycle
C
of
G
. There are exactly
(c-1)
acyclic orientations of
C
with unique sink
v
, given by choosing one of the other vertices
w
of
C
and directing the edges as paths from
w
to
v
. These
(c-1)
acyclic orientations each have a unique extension to
G
by directing each edge toward the vertex closer to the cycle.

∎

Cho and van Willigenburg described a general method for constructing bases of
\Lambda_{n}
using chromatic symmetric functions.

Theorem 5.6
.

[
4
, Theorem 5]

Fix a family
\mathcal{F}=(G_{1},G_{2},\ldots)
of connected graphs where
G_{i}
has
i
vertices for every
i
. For a partition
\lambda\vdash n
, let
G_{\lambda}
be the disjoint union of
G_{\lambda_{1}},\ldots,G_{\lambda_{\ell}}
. Then the set
B_{\mathcal{F}}=\{X_{G_{\lambda}}:\ \lambda\vdash n\}
is a basis of
\Lambda_{n}
.

In particular, we can write every chromatic symmetric function
X_{G}
as a rational linear combination of the
X_{G_{\lambda}}
. We could ask which
\mathcal{F}
have the property that we can write every chromatic symmetric function
X_{G}
as an
integer
linear combination of the
X_{G_{\lambda}}
. Our interpretation of
c_{n}
will give us a simple answer.

Theorem 5.7
.

Fix a family
\mathcal{F}=(G_{1},G_{2},\ldots)
of connected graphs where
G_{i}
has
i
vertices for every
i
. Every chromatic symmetric function
X_{G}
can be written as an integer linear combination of the
X_{G_{\lambda}}
if and only if every
G_{i}
is a tree.

Proof.

We first suppose that
\mathcal{F}
is a family of trees. Let
G
be an
n
-vertex graph. Recall that we can write
X_{G}
as an integer combination in the star basis because the coefficient
c_{\lambda}
of
\mathfrak{st}_{\lambda}
is equal to
(-1)^{a_{1}}
multiplied by the number of paths in a DNC tree from
G
to
\text{St}_{\lambda}
. We now consider the change-of-basis between the star basis and the basis
B_{\mathcal{F}}
. By Corollary
5.5
, Part (2), the star expansion of

is of the form

(5.2)

X_{G_{i}}=\mathfrak{st}_{i}+\sum_{\lambda\vdash i,\ \lambda\neq i}c_{\lambda}(G_{i})\mathfrak{st}_{\lambda}

for some coefficients
c_{\lambda}(G_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore, for a partition
\mu\vdash n
, the star expansion of
X_{\mu}=X_{G_{\mu_{1}}}\cdots X_{G_{\mu_{\ell}}}
is of the form

(5.3)

X_{\mu}=\mathfrak{st}_{\mu}+\sum_{\lambda\vdash n,\ \lambda<_{\text{lex}}\mu}c_{\lambda}\mathfrak{st}_{\lambda}.

Let
M
be the change-of-basis matrix from the basis

to the star basis, where we order the indexing partitions so that they decrease lexicographically. By Equation (
5.3
),
M
has integer entries and is upper triangular with
1

also has integer entries, so we can write any
\mathfrak{st}_{\mu}
as an integer combination of the
X_{\lambda}
. Because
X_{G}
can be written as an integer combination of the
\mathfrak{st}_{\mu}
, it can be written as an integer combination of the
X_{\lambda}
.

Now suppose that
\mathcal{F}
is not a family of trees, and let
n
be minimal such that
G_{n}
has a cycle. By Corollary
5.5
, Part (2), we have

(5.4)

X_{G_{n}}=c_{n}(G_{n})\mathfrak{st}_{n}+\sum_{\lambda\vdash n,\ \lambda\neq n}c_{\lambda}(G_{n})\mathfrak{st}_{\lambda}

for some coefficients
c_{\lambda}(G_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}
and
c_{n}(G_{n})\geq 2
. By Corollary
5.5
, Part (1), the star
\mathfrak{st}_{n}
does not appear in the star expansion of any other
X_{G_{\mu}}
for
\mu\neq n
. Therefore, in the expansion of
\mathfrak{st}_{n}
in the basis
B_{\mathcal{F}}
, the coefficient of
G_{n}
is
\frac{1}{c_{n}(G_{n})}\notin\mathbb{Z}
, so we cannot write
\mathfrak{st}_{n}
as an integer combination of the
X_{G_{\lambda}}
.
∎

This result suggests that if we seek combinatorial interpretations of the coefficients of chromatic symmetric functions
X_{G}
in these chromatic bases

, it may be best to take families of trees.
