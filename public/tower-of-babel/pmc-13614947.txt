Effect of soluble amylose separation on the faster cooking time of sago analog rice
Authors: Sabirin, Mardonius Budi Kusarpoko, Abdullah Darussalam, Yanuar Sigit Pramana, Dyah Primarini Meidiawati, Bambang Triwiyono, Ika Mulawati Purwanti Noviana, Fidrianto, Arry Darmawan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aims to observe the effect of soluble amylose separation on the cooking time of analog sago rice. This rice is made from 50% sago starch, 5% sago fiber, 15% sago starch that has been partially pregelatinized, and each is mixed with 30% white rice flour, 30% red rice flour, and 30% black rice flour. Each mixture is extruded using a twin-screw extruder at a moisture content of 30%, barrel temperatures of 60, 70, 90, and 90 °C (zones 1–4), and screw speeds of 20–50 rpm. A post-installation washing step is carried out to separate surface amylose (in the form of mucus), followed by drying. Observations of the morphology show that the total porosity of the rice after separation is greater than before amylose separation, resulting in a shorter cooking time. Smaller crystallinity results in a shorter cooking time. A greater onset of gelatinization results in a shorter cooking time. Antioxidant analysis shows that washing does not remove antioxidants. Analog sago rice without amylose separation showed a cooking time of 14–16 min, while analog sago rice with separation treatment showed a cooking time of 5–9 min. This amylose-washed analog sago rice is intended for applications requiring a faster cooking process, such as emergency relief operations or fast-paced urban lifestyles.

Introduction

The increasing demand for convenient and functional foods has driven the development of starch-based products with shorter rehydration and cooking times without compromising nutritional quality and texture. The shift in consumption patterns toward instant products demands innovative approaches to starch structural engineering, particularly for granular staple foods. In this context, starch systems with rapid rehydration capabilities are a key focus because these characteristics are strongly influenced by amylose distribution, gelatinization level, surface morphology, and changes in crystalline structure.

Sago (Metroxylon sagu) is a tropical starch source with great potential for alternative food development. Sago starch has a high carbohydrate content (up to 88.67%)1, a relatively low glycemic index of 282, and is gluten-free, making it relevant for functional food formulations. In addition, sago is known as a plant that is tolerant to marginal land conditions and climate change3, making it a strategic resource in sustainable food diversification. However, the natural characteristics of sago starch, which tend to have a stable crystalline structure and a certain swelling capacity, can limit the rehydration rate in solid grain-based products.

The development of analog rice as a substitute for conventional rice has been widely reported4,5. Various studies have shown that extrusion techniques and formulation engineering are capable of producing grains with textural properties similar to rice6. However, most of the reported analog rice still requires a relatively long cooking time, similar to native rice7. Literature specifically exploring the mechanisms of accelerated cooking time through starch structural engineering—including modification of the degree of gelatinization, amylose redistribution, and integration of fiber and bioactive components in the sago starch matrix—is still limited. In addition, the relationship between changes in surface morphology, starch crystallinity, and functional properties such as antioxidant capacity and resistant starch formation in instant analog rice systems has not been comprehensively studied.

The study of developing instant sago analog rice has both scientific and practical urgency. Conceptually, this system offers an interesting model for understanding how starch structural engineering can modify the hydration and gelatinization kinetics of granular products8. The integration of pigmented rice flour (red and black rice) as a source of phenolic compounds also opens up opportunities for increasing functional value through the contribution of antioxidant activity and other bioactive components9.

This study contributes to the literature by (i) expanding the understanding of the structure-function relationship in instant granular sago starch systems, (ii) evaluating the implications of changes in crystallinity and morphology on cooking time and texture, and (iii) integrating nutritional and bioactivity aspects in the development of alternative staple foods based on local resources. This approach is expected to strengthen Sago’s position as a strategic raw material in sustainable instant food innovation.

Proximate analysis

Proximate analysis was conducted to determine moisture, ash, fat, starch, protein, and crude fiber. The results of the proximate analysis of sago analog rice that has been separated with soluble amylose mixed with white rice are called instant sago analog rice mixed with white rice (ISARWR), instant sago analog rice mixed with red rice (ISARRR), and instant sago analog rice mixed with black rice (ISARBR) are presented in Table 1. The starch content of ISARBR (64.06 ± 0.01c % w/w) was significantly higher than that of ISARRR (62.78 ± 0.01b % w/w) and ISARWR (61.62 ± 0.01a % w/w). The lipid content of ISARWR (0.10 ± 0.006a % w/w), ISARRR (0.11 ± 0.002a % w/w) and ISARBR (0.10 ± 0.001a % w/w) insignificantly different. The low lipid content is advantageous in preventing rancidity and sourness, thereby contributing to a longer shelf life of the product10. Ash content reflects the total mineral composition of a food material. Ash is the inorganic residue that remains after the combustion of a sample at high temperatures. Analog rice products with higher protein content have been reported to exhibit lower glycemic activity, as protein can inhibit glucose absorption in the small intestine11. This difference is attributed to the addition of 5% sago fiber. Dietary fiber is closely associated with the glycemic index (GI) of foods and contributes to lowering the GI value13. Starch crystallinity

The X-ray diffractograms of the three types of ISAR were presented in Fig.2. The ISARWR exhibited major diffraction peaks at 2θ angles of 17.61° and 32.84°, with a crystallinity degree of 17.45%. ISARRR displayed main peaks at 2θ angles of 12.95°, 19.70°, and 34.47°, with a crystallinity degree of 19.40%. Meanwhile, ISARBR showed predominant peaks at 2θ angles of 12.79°, 18.20°, and 37.99°, and had the highest crystallinity degree of 24.33%. All three instant sago analog rice samples exhibited V-type crystalline patterns, which are characteristic of pregelatinized starch, typically resulting from the formation of amylose–lipid complexes or related compounds15. The crystallinity of starch is influenced by factors such as amylopectin content and chain length, crystallite size, and the orientation of double helices relative to the X-ray beam16. Reducing crystallinity from SARRR 31.28%5 to ISARRR 19.40% affects cooking time. The lower the crystallinity, the faster the cooking time. Antioxidant, total phenol, and resistant starch analysis

Antioxidant analysis refers to the measurement of a compound’s or extract’s ability—such as that of ISAR to scavenge or neutralize free radicals. Free radicals are unstable molecules that can damage cells, proteins, and DNA, and are associated with various degenerative diseases such as cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and premature aging. The DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) assay was employed to evaluate antioxidant capacity by measuring the ability of the extract to reduce DPPH radicals. The antioxidant concentration was calculated based on the percentage of DPPH radical inhibition by the sample extract. The antioxidant contents of ISARWR (446 ± 0.02a AAEµg/g), ISARRR (448 ± 4.62a AAEµg/g), and ISARBR (448 ± 0.02a AAEµg/g). AAE µg/g (Ascorbic Acid Equivalent micrograms per gram). Insignificant difference among them. This indicates that all three types of ISAR possessed comparable antioxidant capacities, effectively scavenging free radicals. The addition of 30% white rice, red rice, or black rice to the formulation contributed to the enhancement of antioxidant levels in the final products.

Inhibitory activity refers to the percentage of free radicals that are successfully neutralized by a given concentration of antioxidant compounds. The inhibitory activities of ISARWR (71 ± 0.01a %), ISARRR (82 ± 0.11c %), and ISARBR (75 ± 2.63b %). These values indicate that free radicals were effectively eradicated by the antioxidants present in each formulation.

Total phenolic content (TPC) measures the concentration of phenolic compounds in the extract of ISAR. Phenolic compounds are a group of bioactive substances known for their strong antioxidant properties, with gallic acid being a common example. The Folin–Ciocalteu assay is the most widely used method for determining TPC, and results are expressed as µg GAE/100 g extract (gallic acid equivalents). Generally, higher total phenolic content correlates with stronger antioxidant potential. The TPC of instant sago analog rice mixed with white rice, red rice, and black rice was 20 ± 9.36a µg GAE/100 g extract, 86 ± 14.66b µg GAE/100 g extract, and 145 ± 38.21c µg GAE/100 g extract, respectively, with significant differences among the samples. This variation is believed to be influenced by the polyphenol content present in sago starch and the added rice types. According to Duque et al.17, the total polyphenol content of sago fine flour fractions (28.3–61.7 µg GAE/100 g sample). These findings confirm that all three formulations of ISAR possess antioxidant capabilities and that the addition of 30% white rice, red rice, or black rice contributes to the enhancement of antioxidant levels.

Resistant starch (RS) is a type of starch (a complex carbohydrate) that resists digestion in the small intestine and passes into the large intestine, where it is fermented by gut microbiota. Because it is resistant to digestive enzymes such as amylase, RS is not readily converted into glucose, thereby avoiding rapid spikes in blood sugar levels. It functions similarly to dietary fiber. Instant sago analog rice primarily contains RS type 3 (RS3), which refers to starch that has undergone cooking and subsequent cooling, leading to the formation of a crystalline structure that is difficult to digest due to retrogradation. Among the formulations tested, ISARBR (17 ± 0.22b g/100 g) and ISARRR (17 ± 0.09b g/100 g) exhibited the highest RS content, the lowest RS was significantly ISARWR (12 ± 0.15a g/100 g). RS3 formation occurs through the extrusion process, where gelatinized starch forms RS3, not through washing. The difference in RS results from the type of rice flour, not simply amylose separation. Gelatinization profile

Pasting properties (gelatinization profiles) are presented in Table 4 and Fig. 3. In this study, ISARWR exhibited a higher peak viscosity compared to formulations ISARRR and ISARBR. The presence of sago starch in the instant analog rice appears to act as a heat conductor, facilitating more efficient heat transfer directly to the starch granules18. A small breakdown value was observed in ISARR and ISARBR, whereas ISARWR showed a breakdown value of zero. This indicates that ISARWR has greater stability during heating and shear processes19. The setback value of ISARWR was the highest among ISARRR and ISARBR. Setback is associated with the retrogradation capacity and gel-forming ability of starch pastes20. A higher setback is generally linked to a higher amylose content21, besides that increased setback values also indicate the formation of a cohesive paste and a firm gel, with greater retrogradation potential and lower storage stability at low temperatures22. The color properties of ISARWR, ISARRR, and ISARBR show a general decrease in luminosity (L)*, indicating reduced brightness. After cooking, ISARBR maintained significantly higher hardness (5554.8 ± 602.80c g) compared to ISARRR (5063 ± 440.72b g) and ISARWR (4454 ± 609.02a g). This persistence of firmness after thermal treatment suggests that the starch matrix in ISARBR resists structural breakdown to a greater extent, likely due to long-chain amylose associations forming stable gel networks during gelatinization.

ISARBR also demonstrated the highest adhesiveness (−200.40 ± 38.52b g s), indicating greater surface stickiness after cooking, which may be associated with partial amylose leaching and increased surface interaction. Although springiness values were not significantly different among treatments, ISARBR showed a numerically higher elasticity (0.90 ± 0.16), suggesting improved structural recovery after deformation. The highest cohesiveness, gumminess, and chewiness were likewise observed in ISARBR, indicating a more internally bonded and mechanically resistant structure. Since gumminess and chewiness are positively correlated with hardness, these results confirm that the black-rice formulation forms a stronger three-dimensional starch network, contributing to enhanced textural integrity after cooking27.

Discussion

The removal of surface amylose during processing results in granule brittleness, which is visible in FESEM images as pores and cracks28. This more open structure reduces the physical resistance of the granules to hot water, thus accelerating the gelatinization rate, which directly explains the reduction in cooking time. At the same time, the released amylose forms an amylose–lipid complex with a V-type crystal pattern in XRD29. This complex is relatively stable, but not sufficient to maintain the hardness of the product, resulting in a lower hardness in the final texture. Thus, the microstructural changes resulting from amylose removal significantly influence the acceleration of cooking and the modification of the texture profile30.

Low-amylose starch cannot maintain grain integrity31. For analog rice, the grain structure must not disintegrate during soaking/cooking, not stick together, and not become mushy. Low-amylose starch, or waxy starch, forms a highly cohesive and sticky gel. It cannot form stable grains, causing agglomeration and a soft, rice cake-like texture. Therefore, using low-amylose rice flour or waxy starch does not produce analog grains that meet rice quality standards, even though the cooking time may be short32.

In extruded sago analog rice, post-processing washing is an important texture-correction step because it removes part of the leached/free amylose on the grain surface formed during extrusion and drying. If not removed, this surface amylose promotes rapid retrogradation, dense recrystallization, and adhesive film formation during cooking and cooling, which leads to hard, compact, and clumped grains. Washing reduces this surface amylose, thereby decreasing intergranular adhesion and post-cooking hardening. It may also shorten cooking time by improving water penetration and making hydration faster and more uniform. Although this step may be considered less efficient for industrial processing because it requires additional water and handling, it is still justified because it improves texture and reduces cooking time, which can also help lower cooking energy demand33. The use of low-amylose rice is optional in achieving short cooking times.

Extrusion modifies starch, but it does not remove the soluble amylose fraction. High-shear and high-temperature extrusion breaks down starch granules, but amylose remains soluble and migrates to the surface of the product. Precisely because extrusion has high shear, the soluble amylose fraction increases, not decreases. In other words, extrusion does not replace the function of amylose breakdown; in fact, it makes breakdown more relevant because free amylose is more abundant. Functional modification through extrusion does not automatically improve cooking behavior. Extrusion modifies gelatinization, molecular fragmentation, viscosity changes, and crystal denaturation. However, the effects on cooking quality are not always positive. Analogous sago rice products that still contain free amylose exhibit longer cooking times, a tougher texture, clumping, and loss of grain integrity. This suggests that engineering modification through extrusion is insufficient to directly lead to the structural problems caused by soluble amylose34.

Although direct substitution with low-amylose rice or glutinous rice flour may seem simpler, this approach can be used to produce grains with a soft and sticky structure. Low-amylose starch tends to produce overly cohesive gels, preventing the formation of stable analogous grains. Therefore, an extrusion-washing step is still necessary to achieve a balance between rapid rehydration and intact grain texture35. The observed increase in hardness in ISARBR can be attributed to the presence of long-chain amylose molecules, which can form stronger molecular associations and result in a higher degree of crystallinity, thereby increasing the overall chewiness of cooked rice27.

Collectively, these studies demonstrate that deliberate and controlled structural changes or adjustments to achieve specific functional effects on amylose dissociation provide a structural framework for engineering starch-based instant staple foods that align accelerated cooking performance with mechanical integrity, thereby advancing sustainable sago innovation.

Separation of soluble amylose can shorten cooking time. ISARWR is the fastest, while ISARBR has the longest cooking time. All three samples of instant sago analog rice showed no significant differences in antioxidant content. The highest inhibition activity was observed in ISARRR, and the lowest in ISARWR. The highest total phenolic content was found in ISARBR, and the lowest in ISARWR. The highest resistant starch content was found in ISARRR and ISARBR. The three types of instant sago analog rice displayed a morphological structure consisting of many coarse and damaged grains that stuck together. The crystallinity of ISARBR is the highest, while ISARWR has the lowest crystallinity. The highest maximum viscosity, breakdown, and recovery values are found in ISARWR. The highest hardness and adhesiveness are found in ISARBR. The values for extensibility, cohesiveness, elasticity, and chewiness showed no significant differences among the three samples of instant sago analog rice.

The ability to control cooking time, texture, and functional properties through amylose separation provides a significant opportunity to develop instant staple foods tailored to different population groups, including health-conscious consumers, emergency food programs, and modern markets prioritizing convenience. Therefore, soluble amylose separation represents a promising technological strategy to improve both the functionality and market competitiveness of instant sago analog rice.

Methods

Raw material

Sago, partially pregelatinized sago starch (PPSS)1, and sago fiber are produced by PT Bangka Asindo Agri (PT BAA) in Sungai Liat-Bangka, Indonesia. White rice, red rice, and black rice are produced by Hasil Bumiku in Bantul, Indonesia.

Producing Sago Analog Rice

The addition of Partially Pregelatinized Sago Starch (PPSS) in the production of sago analog rice was previously conducted5. (1) The process involved the following steps. Formulation: Three formulations were developed. Formulation 1 consisted of 50% sago starch, 15% PPSS, 5% sago fiber, and 30% white rice flour. Formulations 2 and 3 used the same base composition, but the white rice flour was replaced by red rice flour and black rice flour, respectively. (2) Ingredient preparation and mixing: all ingredients were accurately weighed and mixed. (3) Moisture content adjustment: the initial moisture content was measured, and distilled water was added accordingly to achieve 30% moisture; (4) Blending: the mixture was stirred until a homogeneous blend was obtained. (5) Extrusion: the homogeneous mixture was extruded using a twin-screw extruder at a feed moisture of 30%, with barrel temperatures of 60 °C for zones 1 and 2, and 80 °C and 90 °C for zones 3 and 4, respectively. Screw speed was maintained at 30–50 rpm. (6) Drying: the extruded product was sun-dried. (7) Cooking: the dried analog rice was cooked using a rice cooker. (8) Washing: the cooked analog rice was washed to remove dissolved amylose. (8) Final drying: the washed rice was sun-dried again to reduce moisture content. (9) Final product: the process yielded three types of instant sago analog rice based on the type of rice flour used (white, red, and black).

Proximate analysis

The first test conducted was moisture, lipid, protein, ash, total starch, and crude fiber, which were calculated based on the AOAC methods 925.10, 920.85, 992.15, 923.03, 996.11, and 991.42, respectively36. Total amylose content was measured by following Hoover and Ratnayake37, a modification from the work of Mcgrance38.

Cooking time

The cooking time determination, following the AACC (2000) procedure39. The test was performed using a water-to-sample ratio of 10:1. Specifically, 5 g of the sample were placed in 50 mL of water. The mixture was then cooked at a temperature of 100 °C. Cooking was considered complete when the rice granules, upon being pressed firmly between two glass slides, no longer exhibited a visible white core, indicating that the sample was fully gelatinized.

Surface morphology and porosity analysis by FESEM–ImageJ

The surface morphology and porosity were determined by scanning electron microscopy analysis using Thermoscientific, Quattro S type, The Netherlands, with 5000× magnification. A thin gold layer first covered the surface of the samples. After that, the sample was placed into the FE-SEM, and the surface image was obtained using a secondary electron (SE) detector with a working distance (WD) of 8–9 mm and HV 20 kV Total.

The surface porosity of analog sago rice was quantified from FESEM micrographs using ImageJ software. For each sample, micrographs were captured from at least three different fields of view to ensure representative surface characterization. The FESEM micrographs were imported into ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA; version 1.54 g for porosity analysis.

Starch crystallinity

The spectrum pattern and the crystallinity of materials were analyzed using XRD (Panalytical, x’pert3 powder, Malvern, The Netherlands). The measurement was performed using a tube with 2Ɵ: 5°–80° at 40 kV, 20 mA, and a speed of 2° per minute.

Antioxidant analysis

An antioxidant activity test was conducted using a diphenylpicrylhydrazyl (DPPH) reagent. Initially, 0.5 mM (DPPH) stock solution was made in ethanol. Each extract was added with 1 mL of DPPH stock solution and left for half an hour. After that, the absorption density was assessed in a spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific UV-Visible Spectrophotometers, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) at a wavelength of 517 nm and was measured using a linear regression equation from the DPPH standard curve. Total phenol

Total phenolic content in a sample can be determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method, as standardized by AOAC 2017.1341. The Folin–Ciocalteu reagent reacts with phenolic compounds to form a blue-colored complex. Phenol extraction: The sample is dissolved in 80% ethanol. Then, 0.5 mL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent is added to 0.5 mL of the sample solution. The mixture is incubated for 5 minutes. Subsequently, 2 mL of 7% Na₂CO₃ solution is added and mixed thoroughly. The reaction is allowed to proceed for 30–60 min at room temperature in the dark. Absorbance is then measured using a spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 765 nm. The total phenolic content is quantified by comparing the absorbance to a standard calibration curve prepared with gallic acid and expressed as µg GAE/100 g extract (micrograms Gallic Acid Equivalent per 100 g extract).

Pasting properties

Brabender Viscograph-E (GmbH & Co. KG, Germany) was utilized to examine the pasting properties. The experiment was performed by heating and cooling cycles with constant stirring (75 rpm) on the 500 g sample. The heating was performed from 50 °C to 93 °C, and the achieved temperature was maintained for 15 min. After that, the temperature declined to 50 °C, and was kept for 10 min. This process was performed once, presenting the result as a viscosity profile.

Statistical analysis

Some measurements were conducted in triplicate. The results obtained in this investigation were presented as the average ± standard deviation. The data was analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences 26.0 (SPSS). The statistical techniques of analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan’s multi-range test were employed to ascertain the presence of statistically significant differences (p < 0.05).
