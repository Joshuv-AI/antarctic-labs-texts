Perceptions of a comprehensive physical activity behavior intervention among endometrial cancer survivors with obesity. A qualitative study
Authors: Amerigo Rossi, Mónica Ortiz Rossi, Rosa Torres-Panchame, Nina Brojan Heyman, Carol E Garber, Nicole Nevadunsky
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Low-burden physical activity programming leads to large improvements in perceived wellness.•Endometrial cancer survivors with obesity are motivated by fun, nurturing atmospheres for exercise.•A primary concern among endometrial cancer survivors with obesity is the ability to maintain independence.•Physical activity interventions should emphasize body composition improvements instead of weight loss.Keywords: Behavior change, Exercise, Motivation, Obesity, Physical Activity, Survivorship

Introduction

In 2022, endometrial cancer was the fourth-leading cause of cancer in women, with approximately 66,000 new cases (Siegel et al., 2022). Because of its relatively high survival rate, endometrial cancer survivors are the second largest cancer survivor group among women, with nearly 900,000 endometrial cancer survivors living in the United States (Miller et al., 2022). Furthermore, the incidence is expected to continue increasing due to rising prevalence of obesity, a primary risk factor (Felix and Brinton, 2018). Over half of women with early-stage endometrial cancer die from comorbid conditions such as diabetes and obesity (Binder et al., 2016), but patient knowledge of the relationship between endometrial cancer and comorbidities remains low (Sekhon et al., 2019).

Regular participation in physical activity has been shown to reduce endometrial cancer recurrence by 46 % and increase disease-free survival by 67 % (Friedenreich et al., 2020), likely by reducing adiposity (Aune et al., 2015) and improving inflammation and insulin sensitivity (Schmid and Leitzmann, 2015). Approximately 2/3 of endometrial cancer survivors have tried to lose weight by increasing exercise (Wilson et al., 2021), but the majority still have obesity (Onstad et al., 2016). Physical activity interventions for endometrial cancer survivors have produced a positive impact on physical activity behavior and chronic disease risk factors (McCarroll et al., 2014, Rossi et al., 2018), but the long-term sustainability of the improvements has been mixed (Rahimy et al., 2021, Rossi et al., 2020). To develop interventions that lead to sustained improvements in physical activity for endometrial cancer survivors, it is important to better understand how to improve physical activity participation (Felix and Brinton, 2018), especially among racially diverse endometrial cancer survivors (Burse et al., 2020).

Despite the high prevalence of endometrial cancer survivors, and the potential long-term benefits of physical activity participation, no studies have qualitatively evaluated physical activity perceptions among endometrial cancer survivors who participate in exercise programming. Endometrial cancer survivors who were not part of a physical activity intervention stated that exercise is an essential part of a healthy lifestyle post-cancer, and that tailored in-person interventions would be optimal (Koutoukidis et al., 2017). However, only 12 % of those participants achieved the minimum recommended dose of physical activity, indicating the significant potential for improvement to be conferred by successful physical activity interventions.

In contrast, endometrial cancer survivors with obesity who completed a 12-week theory-based physical activity intervention achieved the minimum recommended volume of physical activity, and experienced meaningful improvements in body composition, quality of life, and physical function (Rossi et al., 2016). This study was a secondary analysis to evaluate perceptions of a 12-week in-person physical activity intervention for the endometrial cancer survivors who completed the program to evaluate their unique perspectives of physical activity participation.

Methods

Study design

We performed structured interviews to explore the perceptions and experiences of endometrial cancer survivors who participated in a 12-week comprehensive physical activity intervention which was administered at a fitness center associated with the participants’ respective gynecologic oncologists. During the week following the completion of the intervention, participants completed a 20–30-minute one-on-one interview to gain participant insight regarding their perceptions of physical activity in general, their experience with different facilitators and barriers to physical activity, and their feelings about the effectiveness of the intervention. The interviewer was the same person who had led the class sessions and had therefore developed a significant level of trust, leading to increased credibility. A descriptive qualitative approach to the analysis of the interview transcripts was employed to identify themes related to physical activity participation (Vaismoradi et al., 2013). The study protocol was approved by the site Institutional Review Board and all participants completed an informed consent process prior to study initiation.

Intervention

The details of the wait-list controlled intervention have been published elsewhere (Rossi et al., 2016). In brief, 28 endometrial cancer survivors with obesity were enrolled in a twice-weekly 12-week comprehensive physical activity intervention that had been culturally tailored for a socioculturally diverse population. Each 90-minute class period included 30 minutes of group counseling to improve exercise self-efficacy and 60 minutes of multi-modal exercise class including body weight resistance exercises and cardiovascular fitness training. Participants were also instructed to increase their walking at home to 90 min per week. Several aspects of the intervention were tailored to the diverse sample. For example, the lead class instructor was a Black Dominican woman from New York City who spoke native English and Spanish, and who was able to effectively relate and communicate with the ethnically diverse sample. Musical choices and aerobic dance steps were modified throughout the intervention following participant input. Additionally, participant feedback of walking maps was utilized to create individually tailored walking maps, which included greater emphasis on high-density areas. Although recruitment was low (20 %), participants who completed the program (n = 17) exhibited large effect size improvements in physical activity, physical function, waist circumference and quality of life.

Data collection

During the week following the completion of the intervention, participants returned for post-testing. Immediately following the post-testing, participants who had completed the intervention were asked to participate in a 30-minute one-on-one interview in a private space. The semi-structured interviews were guided by several open-ended questions adapted from previous work with older adults (Mathews et al., 2010) and breast cancer survivors (Sander et al., 2012) regarding perceived facilitators, barriers, and self-efficacy for physical activity. The interviewer was trained to follow introductory questions with probing and specifying questions, and to allow for periods of silence following answers, to gain as much detail as possible (Interviewing in Field Research, 2003). Interpreting questions were also used to clarify meaning when necessary. Audio recordings were made of each interview and were professionally transcribed verbatim.

Participants

English-speaking women with obesity (Body Mass Index ≥ 30 kg·m−2) who had been diagnosed with endometrial cancer between 6 months and 5 years prior, and who were not currently with active disease were recruited. Several Spanish-speaking participants had learned English as a second language, but the bilingual class instructor was able to translate and clarify when necessary. Participants were excluded if they were currently undergoing cancer treatment, or had any limitation that prevented moderate-intensity physical activity, in order to ensure participant safety. Participants were also excluded if they were currently physically active, defined as regular moderate- to vigorous-intensity physical activity at least three times per week, because this group may not need structured physical activity programming to increase their physical activity.

The lead author attempted to contact 140 potential participants via phone. Of the 83 who were successfully contacted, 28 enrolled in the wait-list controlled trial, 25 started the intervention, and 17 completed the intervention and post-testing. Sixteen of the 17 who completed post-testing agreed to sit for an interview. One participant declined due to time conflict. Participant characteristics can be found in Table 1.

Data analysis

Interview transcripts were input into the Dedoose web-based application to allow for independent and collaborative review of the data. Transcript data were analyzed using thematic analysis: 1) To become familiar with the text, all transcripts were read once by the two lead authors, AR and MOR. 2) All transcripts were then reread independently a second time to develop preliminary codes and themes. 3) AR and MOR then met to discuss codes and themes and came to a preliminary consensus. 4) The two authors then read the transcripts a third time to ensure that the confirmed themes were represented in the transcripts, and to find relevant examples of each theme.

Creditability and scientific rigor of the qualitative data analysis were supported by following the guidelines developed by Cooney (2011), including independent analysis by multiple authors, cross-checking emerging concepts, and checking with the exercise instructor after themes were derived to ensure that their experiences were properly described. Additionally, the trustworthiness of findings was supported by implementing strategies for credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability, as described by Lincoln and Guba (1985) and Nowell et al. (2017). To ensure credibility, the two lead researchers had prolonged engagement and familiarity with the participants and conducted peer debriefing sessions to review the qualitative methods and findings. Transferability was obtained by thick description of the study processes and results. To ensure dependability, a researcher not involved in the initial data analysis (RTP) reviewed the data for adequacy and confirmed the accuracy of the findings. Lastly, confirmability was obtained through field and process-specific notes to ensure neutrality and transparency throughout the study.

Once the themes and subthemes had been characterized, the author RTP independently analyzed the data to confirm the accuracy of the data analysis. While all data underwent thematic analysis, data saturation occurred after the 11th interview (P18). Data saturation was determined when no new themes, data, or coding emerged and when the data was deemed both rich (quality) and thick (quantity) (Fusch & Ness, 2015). The data were also reviewed by all three transcript reviewers for any negative case scenarios.

Results

Qualitative analysis

Sixteen participants who had completed a 12-week comprehensive physical activity (PA) intervention were interviewed individually to evaluate their perceptions of PA participation and the impact of the intervention on their PA. The major themes that emerged from the interviews were: 1) Most common physical activities; 2) Motivation for physical activity; 3) Barriers to physical activity; 4) Motivation for adherence to the 12-week program; 5) Perceived benefits of the physical activity intervention; and 6) Feedback on the 12-week intervention. The emergent themes, subthemes, and relevant representative quotes are included in Table 2.

Theme 1. The primary types of physical activity were clearly walking and household chores. Dancing was also discussed by several participants but usually after the first two. Some relevant quotes were:

“Going for walks, being busy around the home. Dancing, although I don’t always do that.”

“I make the beds. I do the bathroom. I sweep the carpet with the carpet sweeper. I wash and-- I don't do much ironing, but I wash. I cook. I walk the dog. I sweep the yard, and things.”

“I'm always walking. I'm always, always walking. I don't bike. I don't jog. I don't skate. I don't swim, even though that's a good thing.”

Although participants were aware of the health benefits related to different modes of dedicated gym-related exercise, most were more comfortable walking, doing chores, and dancing around the home.

Theme 2. Participants provided a wide variety motivating factors for physical activity participation which were explained in detail and at length. This was a topic of great importance about which the participants were animated. The primary subthemes for staying physically active were improving health, maintaining independence, being able to participate in family life (including their dogs) and losing weight and/or inches around the waist. These motivators also had a significant amount of overlap. For example, one participant explained how losing weight would give her greater independence, which would allow her to better care for her family.

“Being better able to take care of my family… my husband has certain illnesses, my son has certain illnesses, and there’s a lot of pressure on me… There are some restrictions because of my weight and all of that. Being able to not have those restrictions.”

“Well, I like to do the activities because it makes me feel better. Mainly that's the main reason. I want to be more flexible and exercise helps me to do that. I feel that if I don't exercise my flexibility decreases. For some reason I feel more sluggish, even the way I get out of a chair. Even with these exercises that I did in this past- I had so much trouble getting the blouses off. Like my arms would be so rusty and now I don't feel any pain. That's my main motivator. I don't want to feel my age [chuckles]. Let me put it that way.”

Theme 3. The overwhelming majority of participants reported weather (heat and/or cold) and pain from various injuries to be their greatest barriers to physical activity. A good example of the intersection of these two barriers was:

“Cold weather. Because my joints get a little stiff. They’re not painful anymore, but they get stiff, so I say, ‘I’m not going to do that.”

The barrier of pain, whether related to the cancer diagnosis or not, was a reported barrier among many participants, such that the physical activity intervention needed to be individually adapted in many cases.

“Because of, you know, like I explained to you, I have this problem with my shoulder. That prevents me from doing a lot of things that I normally do. Then I have a problem with vertigo. Sometimes I get up and I'm trying to do things, and I feel dizzy.”

Several other participants mentioned that fatigue and family responsibilities, and the intersection between the two, were a significant barrier to physical activity.

“You know what affects me? I have a lot of stress in my life, so that makes me tired. So that's what hinders me from doing more. It's like my mom. I need to walk her… And my daughter gets tired very quickly.”

Theme 4. Participants also discussed at length their reasons for maintaining adherence to the 12-week intervention. The subtheme that came up most often was the positive impact of the social interactions with their peers regarding both motivating them to come to class and also being able to share with friends. One illustrative statement was:

“That was the best thing for me, because at least I knew I was out and with other people twice a week. I mean, I have my grandchildren and my family, but it’s not the same, so I really, really enjoyed it. I loved it, as a matter of fact.”

Many of the participants also enjoyed that the class seemed caring and fun, which intersected with the social aspect of the class:

“And then seeing the other ladies in the class, because they're fun to be with. Even if we didn't develop a lot of talking together, but it was still fun to see them to know that they were coming, too. I think it was a good thing for all of us just to see each other. “Oh, I came and you came, too.” So that's a good thing.”

“The program was great and it had nice people, and I had fun which was more important [chuckles]. I hadn't laughed really out loud in a long time since I had the first surgery three years ago. But I did. People are funny [laughter] and so I feel good. I hope they do work it out so they make it a lifelong program.”

The fact that the entire class was women who had shared similar experiences was another big draw for the participants, and helped them maintain motivation to continue:

“I only exercise with this group, and that's why I joined… Because I found that doing it with the community of people that feel the same way in the same room keeps me motivated. Whereas when I go out with my husband, he pushes me, and I told him, “I can't do it anymore.” He said, “Just try it,” and it really pisses me off.”

Theme 5. Participants reported many meaningful perceived improvements associated with the 12-week intervention. The primary subtheme was improved self-efficacy, including becoming able to get up off of the floor.

"It's wonderful that you taught us how to exercise without hurting ourselves and for me that was really huge."

"They got to get over that fear. To me, it was one of things I wrote down - oh my god - to be able to get up and down of the floor. It was very important to me. And not just for the exercise, if you're dropping something on the floor, I had to wait until [Husband] came. And if I dropped something and-- that is a total pain in the ass. And just that getting up and down off the floor was very, very, very important to me personally, to me personally."

The improved self-efficacy had a great impact on participants’ ability to do activities of daily living that led to improved quality of life. For example:

"I went to my grandson's Pittsburgh graduation and my son, my grandchildren, my three grandchildren… and they're young people. They like to do things. And one of the things that I was most afraid before going was the fact that I was going to hold them back from doing their activities. Pittsburgh is very hilly. I didn't think I was going to be able to maneuver the hill, but I was very, very happy that I had started the program [chuckles] and I was able to keep up with them."

"I couldn't walk, for not even five minutes. I used to go shopping with my daughters and they'd have to leave me, leave me in the back or somebody stay back with me to walk slow. I'd just stop for a while and rest, because my right foot used to hurt so much, and now it's not hurting anymore."

Other reported changes were improved body composition, which importantly was not always correlated to weight loss, and improved health, which were both related to improved confidence and well-being.

"I can wear a bathing suit without feeling embarrassed about my swollen legs. And it has helped me-- keep me regular, go to the bathroom, which before I had to take three stool softeners to be regular. Yeah. And it's made me feel stronger and I feel really good. None of that stiffness or anything that I had. I even notice my complexion has improved because sweating…. And my waist has gone-- My waist has come down."

"My scale and I are not friends, because every time I look, I don't understand. I'm exercising, I'm moving, I'm not losing weight. But in essence, everybody has noticed, not only me. I notice I'm wearing a belt that I wasn't able to wear before. And the pants aren't leaving marks around my skin, and even though I don't like wearing tight clothing, the pants are very, very loose."

Several participants also reported sleeping better after having started the program, which led to improved quality of life.

"My sleeping habits improved. I can sleep better at night. I still toss and turn, but not like I was. So, with the weight down and that and the exercise, it makes me tired at night. So, I have been sleeping better."

Theme 6. The general feedback about the program was highly positive. Participants mostly wanted a more robust intervention. For example, participants generally wanted the program to be offered more times per week and for a longer period of time.

"But you need a longer program. You need ongoing, ongoing. So that those that have been there can come if they want to, and then get new people. We're not saying you can't have new people [laughter], but we want to be there, too. That would be like-- you could do a new people program and an older."

Several participants expressed interest in adding pool workouts and many also wanted more dancing and adding a nutritional component:

“I think that what everyone wanted to do – the dancing – because we want the music”.

“Going to the pool maybe? Exercising in a pool. Talking about nutrition, that could be important, at least a guest speaker.”

Discussion

Endometrial cancer survivors are at an increased risk for poor quality of life outcomes (Basen-Engquist et al., 2009), and physical activity has been associated with general health improvements and reduced five-year all-cause mortality among survivors (Robertson et al., 2019, Arem et al., 2013). The primary aims of this study were to develop a better understanding of endometrial cancer survivors’ perceptions of an in-person, comprehensive physical activity intervention and evaluate the impact of the experience on their physical activity habits to provide support for future evidence-based physical activity interventions. The main findings of this study were participants who completed the intervention cared deeply about maintaining their independence and being part of a fun, caring, and social environment where they could share with other endometrial cancer survivors. They indicated that the most important benefits were reduced fatigue and pain, and increased ability to perform activities of daily living, and wished that the program were more robust. Recently, the American Society of Clinical Oncology strongly recommended the use of exercise during cancer treatment (Ligibel et al., 2022). These findings support the use of exercise post-treatment as well.

Many participants expressed the motivational value of the social structure of the intervention was important. For example, one participant stated, “I only exercise with this group, and that’s why I joined… because I found that doing it with the community of people that feel the same way in the same room keeps me motivated.” These results support previous literature where social support was a factor that influenced participation in physical activity in endometrial cancer survivors and was reported to be a common facilitator for physical activity and a source of external motivation (Burse et al., 2020, Koutoukidis et al., 2017). Previous literature suggests that participating in a community-based exercise program tailored to meet the needs of the individual can lead to significant benefits as a survivor (Rajotte et al., 2012). As such, the results from this study are in alignment with previous studies that highlight the positive influence of social support on physical activity in endometrial cancer survivors and indicate that health care providers should emphasize social interactions when developing physical activity interventions for this population.

Motivation for adherence to the 12-week program was discussed in depth, with nearly all participants indicating that intrinsic motivations were the primary drivers of continued participation. Several participants expressed that they were motivated to adhere to the program because of the relationships that were developed and the common experiences that they shared. Previous research comparing the impact of extrinsic and intrinsic motivation on exercise adherence found that intrinsic motivation was more effective at changing human behavior and participants with higher levels of intrinsic motivation were more likely to adhere to their exercise routine (Thøgersen-Ntoumani et al., 2016). These findings differ from one of the few qualitative studies of endometrial cancer survivors, in which external motivation were a factor that influenced participation in health behaviors in endometrial cancer survivors (Hardcastle et al., 2017). This discrepancy is likely due to the fact that the current study was conducted among women who had completed a physical activity intervention, whereas the other study asked women what they thought would motivate them to participate. Although more research should be done to evaluate difference rationale for physical activity initiation and adherence, these findings clearly highlight the importance of identifying and fostering intrinsic motivators in a group setting (i.e., social setting) to produce high adherence for this population. Future research with endometrial cancer survivors should also consider including past participants and/or physically active participants to be peer-mentors, which has been shown to improve physical activity among breast cancer survivors (Sezgin and Bektas, 2023).

In the current study, the most common forms of routine physical activity outside of the 12-week intervention were walking and household chores, which suggest that activities of choice were aligned with the survivors’ particular needs (i.e., activities of daily living). This confirms the findings of another qualitative study that explored the attitudes, challenges, and needs of endometrial cancer survivors regarding physical activity, which found that most participants preferred non-vigorous activities (Koutoukidis et al., 2017). These data suggest that it’s important for healthcare and/or exercise professionals to consider emphasizing activities of daily living when prescribing exercise for this population to increase and maintain physical activity participation and help maintain their physical independence. Furthermore, selecting low- to moderate-intensity activities may increase levels of self-efficacy in populations with obesity, which can lead to increased participation in exercise and higher levels of physical activity (Hussien et al., 2022).

Another important finding of this study was that many participants expressed that their clothes fit looser but that they did not lose weight. This participant perception supports the quantitative findings of the intervention, in which waist circumference was reduced by approximately 5 cm but weight stayed the same (Rossi et al., 2016). A few participants expressed frustration about the lack of weight loss, especially because the part of the intervention was to provide participants with a weight scale. Future interventions should prepare participants to expect positive changes in body composition that may occur in the absence of weight loss, and body mass should be de-emphasized. In this sort of intervention with populations with obesity, waist circumference or body fat analysis may be better tools for body composition assessment than body mass.

Participants also reported several barriers to physical activity participation, which included weather, pain, fatigue, family, and stress. At times, weather and pain were reported together due to experiencing joint stiffness and arthritis symptoms in cold or rainy weather. However, one participant reported that despite feeling achy from her arthritis, exercise helped. The perceived barriers to physical activity expressed in this population are similar to a recent systematic review, which found that the most common reported physical activity barriers included fatigue, weather, illness or health issues (Burse et al., 2020). Educating this population on the benefits of physical activity and providing them with various options that provide flexibility will enable them to maintain adherence despite significant barriers.

The primary limitation of this study is that only participants who enrolled and completed the intervention were included so the interviews did not capture the sentiments of endometrial cancer survivors for whom the intervention was not convenient. Future studies should qualitatively evaluate endometrial cancer survivors who do not enroll in physical activity interventions to determine what aspect would be necessary for them to enroll. Another limitation was the exclusion of physically active cancer survivors. The goal of this study was to evaluate whether a 12-week intervention could change the physical activity perceptions of insufficiently active endometrial cancer survivors. However, habitually physically active cancer survivors may provide very different assessments of motivators and barriers to physical activity. Participants who had a physical limitation that prevented physical activity were also excluded from the study which prevented the capture of physical activity perceptions by this significant part of the population.

The intervention instructor also served as the interviewer, which may have led to participant response bias. This arrangement was intentionally implemented to ensure immediate rapport between interviewer and interviewee to gain rich interviews, however the participants may not have felt comfortable expressing their unvarnished opinions of the instructor qualities that they found important.

Previous literature suggests that cancer survivors who engage in an active lifestyle have improved quality of life, sense of well-being, and reduced recurrence of disease (Basen-Engquist et al., 2009); Robertson et al., 2019). The sense of community provided by this program was a powerful and common theme in the experience of these survivors. The feedback shared through these experiences highlights the importance of group settings for physical activity intervention, which can serve as sources of support and caring. These connections lead to intrinsic motivations that positively influence participation in physical activity and improve health and well-being. Participant feedback on the intervention was overwhelmingly positive, with many participants asking for the program to be more frequent and longer lasting.

This study provides unique data regarding PA perceptions from socioculturally diverse endometrial cancer survivors with obesity who completed a 12-week physical activity program. Although this low-cost, low-burden physical activity program was well-received, safe, and improved health outcomes in the short (Rossi et al., 2016) and longer (Rossi et al., 2020) term, it could not be continued because of lack of sustaining resources and personnel. Future research should be conducted to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of such low-burden programs to increase quality adjusted life years in endometrial cancer survivors to provide evidence regarding whether physical activity treatment should be included in the standard of care.
