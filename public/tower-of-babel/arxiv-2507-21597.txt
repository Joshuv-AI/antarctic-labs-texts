Lunar Reference Timescale
A. Bourgoin, P. Defraigne, F. Meynadier

Abstract
Setting up a relativistic lunar reference frame is of a prime importance in the context of future exploration missions to the Moon. If the procedure for building a consistent reference frame within the framework of the general theory of relativity is well established (cf. resolutions B.3 of IAU 2000), there is still some freedom in the choice of the coordinate timescale to be adopted as reference in the cislunar region. In this paper, we review the orders of magnitude of the relativistic effects resulting from (i) the gravitational redshift of a clock on the lunar surface and (ii) the time transformations between a clock on the surface of the Moon and a clock on the surface of the Earth. We then discuss possible options for a lunar reference timescale with their advantages and drawbacks. Finally, we propose possible realizations of the lunar reference timescale as well as its traceability to UTC.



Introduction

The interest in Moon exploration has substantially grown in the latest years. With the gradual launches of large-scale lunar exploration missions, the demand for high-precision navigation and positioning of lunar probes has become increasingly urgent. To support the next generation of institutional and private lunar exploration missions, space agencies are developing lunar communications and navigation services that would allow positioning accuracy during the final descent of landers on the Moon surface. In particular, NASA is proposing the LunaNET
[
Israel et al. 2020
]
, and ESA is proposing the Moonlight program
[
Giordano et al. 2022
]
, both aiming to provide communication and Position Navigation and Timing (PNT) services to institutional and commercial lunar missions. China has also proposed its own “Queqiao comprehensive constellation for communications, navigation and remote sensing”
[
Ma et al. 2019
]
, which is planned to be completed in three phases to provide relay communications, navigation, positioning and timing services for cislunar space transfer and lunar surface exploration activities. The lunar exploration constellation composed of Moon orbiters can achieve continuous coverage of specific areas of the lunar surface or even the entire lunar surface, and can provide necessary relay communication, navigation, positioning and other services for landers, or later human beings, to fully carry out lunar exploration in the future.

Future scientific or more industrial applications on the lunar surface, as well as cislunar communication, will require a standard reference timescale. One particular application requiring a reference timescale is certainly the positioning, navigation and timing as envisaged by the satellite navigation systems like Moonlight, LunaNET or Queqiao. As in classical Earth Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS), the different satellite clocks should be synchronized between them, or more precisely, their offset with respect to a common reference should be known at user level
[
Teunissen and Montenbruck 2017
]
. Furthermore, the lunar explorers should always be able to communicate with the Earth so that the difference between the reference timescale on the Moon and UTC should be known and predictable—at least up to a certain level of accuracy.
This implies the urgent need to define a theoretical scale to be used in the cislunar environment, as well as to propose some solutions for its realization.

The requirement that future localization on the lunar surface is expected within a meter precision which translates to the nanosecond precision for time keeping implies that general relativity cannot be neglected. Let us remind that on the Earth, the GNSS relativistic transformations imply corrections at the level of several tens of microsecond per day
[
Ashby 2003
]
which can of course not be ignored when a navigation precision of one meter is targeted. The Moon is orbiting the Earth in its annual revolution around the Sun, and the respective gravitational and relative velocity differences suggest that a common reference timescale cannot be used for both the Moon and the Earth and that a new definition and a new terminology should be fixed to be adopted by the whole scientific and industrial communities.

The terminology for astronomical reference systems and timescales in the solar system, with the associated relativistic framework, have been provided by the International Astronomical Union (IAU)
[
IAU 1992
]
. They defined two different celestial reference systems: a global one called the Barycentric Celestial Reference System (BCRS) with the associated Barycentric Coordinate Time (TCB), and a local one referred to as the Geocentric Celestial Reference System (GCRS) with the associated Geocentric Coordinate Time (TCG).
IAU 1992
also provided the coordinate transformations between global and local coordinates. These definitions were then refined in the resolution B1.3 of the 24th IAU General Assembly
[
IAU 2001
]
. The IAU 2024 resolution II
[
IAU 2025
]
recommends constructing a Lunar Celestial Reference System (LCRS), with its coordinate time designated Lunar Coordinate Time (TCL), using the same techniques as to construct the GCRS and TCG, and keeping the unit of measurement of TCL consistent with the SI second. From there, a reference timescale for the Moon should now be defined as well as its realization, as needed for the practical use in the cislunar environment. Recently, studies of the relations between lunar and terrestrial timescales have been proposed by
Ashby and Patla 2024
;
Kopeikin and Kaplan 2024
, and
Turyshev et al. 2025
. We propose here a more comprehensive development of the time rate at the surface of the Moon, and from there we discuss possible options for the future reference timescale on the Moon.

Section
1
of this paper proposes a review of the timing system in the Earth environment, summarizing the transformation between the proper time of clocks and the coordinate time of the geocentric reference system. The definition of the different coordinate times, as recommended by the IAU and adopted by the metrological community, are presented. Section
2
describes the relation between the proper time of a clock in cislunar environment and the coordinate time of the lunar reference system. Section
3
details the relation between the time on Earth and on the Moon, including proper and coordinate time differences, and their link to the light travel time in the context of a way-one time transfer. We then present in Sect.
4
some features of possible scaled versions of TCL that can be used as lunar reference timescale, with their possible advantages and drawbacks. Finally, we discuss the realization of the lunar timescale as well as its traceability to UTC in Sect.
5
.

1
Timescales in the geocentric reference system

Time and frequency comparisons at the level of the current atomic clock performances should be described in a relativistic reference system. According to general relativity, the proper time of an ideal clock is a space-time invariant, meaning that no matter the coordinate system being used to model it, the result shall remain the same eventually. Nevertheless, not any coordinate system will do the job. Indeed, it is well known that non-inertial observers cannot attribute unambiguous coordinates to distant point-events. One thus needs a coordinate system whose origin remains as close as possible to the clock trajectory. The GCRS was precisely introduced to describe clocks’ behavior in the Earth environment. Hereafter, we recall some features of the local geocentric system.

1.1
Geocentric Celestial Reference System

For an observer in the Solar System, the IAU Resolution B1.3 (2000)
[
IAU 2001
]
defined the global BCRS—with its origin at the Solar System barycenter—and its associated coordinate time TCB.
However, for an observer on or around the Earth, it is more appropriate to rely on a local coordinate system whose origin is at the center-of-mass of the Earth. Accordingly, the IAU Resolution B1.3 (2000)
[
IAU 2001
]
also defined the GCRS with its associated coordinate time TCG. The GCRS is a non-rotating reference system, and can be considered as quasi-inertial in the vicinity of the Earth. It is classically used in the region of space from the Earth up to the geostationary altitude.

The components of the metric tensor in GCRS coordinates
(X^{\mu})=(cT,\bm{X})
was recommended by the IAU
[
IAU 2001
]
, with
c
the speed of light in a vacuum,
T
the coordinate time TCG, and
\bm{X}
the GCRS position of any observer. Detailed description of the relativistic theory for time and frequency comparisons around the Earth can be found for instance in
Wolf and Petit 1995
or
Petit and Wolf 2005
. Only a brief summary will be given here.

A clock can usually be considered as representing a time-like observer, meaning that its space-time trajectory, and hence its spatial coordinates in a given reference system, might be seen as functions of the corresponding coordinate time. In the GCRS, the position of a clock ‘
\mathrm{e}
’ is a given function of TCG, namely
\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}}=\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}}(T)
; its coordinate velocity is thus
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{e}}=\bm{V}_{\mathrm{e}}(T)
with
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{e}}=\mathrm{d}\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}}/\mathrm{d}T
. The proper time of that clock can be expressed in the IAU recommended framework as

\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau_{\mathrm{e}}-\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}T}=-\frac{1}{2c^{2}}\left[V_{\mathrm{e}}^{2}(T)+2W(T,\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}})\right]+\mathcal{O}(c^{-4})\,,

(1)

where
V_{\mathrm{e}}=|\bm{V}_{\mathrm{e}}|
and where the effective potential
W
is given by

W(T,\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}})=W_{\mathrm{E}}(T,\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}})+W_{\mathrm{tidal}}(T,\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}})+W_{\mathrm{iner}}(T,\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}})\,.

(2)

W_{\mathrm{E}}
is the Newtonian potential of the Earth,
W_{\mathrm{tidal}}
is the tidal contribution from the Moon, the Sun, and the other planets, and
W_{\mathrm{iner}}
is the inertial potential caused by the non-geodesic acceleration of the Earth center-of-mass (namely the couplings between the Earth non-spherical part of the gravity potential to external gravity fields from other bodies). All potentials are evaluated at the clock geocentric position
\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}}
. Note that only scalar potentials are considered here, as the vector potentials contribute at the
\mathcal{O}(c^{-4})
level.

The three components of the effective potential
W
should be characterized separately.
The inertial potential provides frequency deviations lower than
10^{-20}

[
Blanchet et al. 2001
]
and can be ignored at the level of performances of existing atomic clocks.
The tidal potential is due to the gravitational attraction of the solar system bodies (limited here to their monopole term) and reads such as

W_{\mathrm{tidal}}(T,\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}})=\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{E}}\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{A}}}{|\bm{X}_{\mathrm{A}}|}\sum_{\ell=2}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{|\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}}|}{|\bm{X}_{\mathrm{A}}|}\right)^{\!\ell}P_{\ell}(\bm{N}_{\mathrm{e}}\cdot\bm{N}_{\mathrm{A}})\,,

(3)

where
G
is the gravitational constant,
m_{\mathrm{A}}
is the mass of the body
\mathrm{A}
,
P_{\ell}
are the Legendre polynomials of degree
\ell
, and
\bm{N}_{\mathrm{e}}=\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}}/|\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}}|
. The GCRS position of body
\mathrm{A}
is seen as a function of TCG:
\bm{X}_{\mathrm{A}}=\bm{X}_{\mathrm{A}}(T)
and so is
\bm{N}_{\mathrm{A}}=\bm{X}_{\mathrm{A}}/|\bm{X}_{\mathrm{A}}|
. For a clock at the Earth surface, the impact of the tidal potential is periodic with a magnitude at the level of
2\times 10^{-17}
in relative frequency difference between proper time and TCG (see e.g.,
Wolf and Petit 1995
). Finally, for a clock in the vicinity of the Earth, at a distance
|\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}}|
from the center-of-mass of the Earth, the Newtonian potential of the Earth is given by

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{E}}(T,\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}})

\displaystyle=\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{E}}}{|\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}}|}\,\Bigg\{1+\sum_{\ell=2}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{E}}}{|\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}}|}\right)^{\!\ell}

\displaystyle\times P_{\ell m}(\sin\theta_{\mathrm{e}})\left[C^{\mathrm{E}}_{\ell m}\cos(m\varphi_{\mathrm{e}})+S^{\mathrm{E}}_{\ell m}\sin(m\varphi_{\mathrm{e}})\right]\Bigg\}\,,

(4)

where
m_{\mathrm{E}}
is the mass of the Earth,
R_{\mathrm{E}}
is its equatorial radius,
\theta_{\mathrm{e}}
and
\varphi_{\mathrm{e}}
are the latitude and longitude of the clock (measured from Earth’s equator and Greenwich prime meridian, respectively),
P_{\ell m}
are the associated Legendre polynomials of degree
\ell
and order
m
, and where
C^{\mathrm{E}}_{\ell m}
and
S^{\mathrm{E}}_{\ell m}
are the Stockes coefficients depending on mass distribution inside Earth. The most important term in this potential is of course the monopole. When introduced into Eq. (
1
) its contribution to the clock rate with respect to TCG, is about
7\times 10^{-10}
for a clock on the surface of the Earth. The second most important term corresponds to the Earth flattening,
C_{20}^{\mathrm{E}}
, and gives a contribution of about
8\times 10^{-13}
. All other coefficients of the Earth gravitational field are at least three orders of magnitude smaller than
J_{2}^{\mathrm{E}}
’s and should be considered only for high precision clocks and measurements.

For a clock at rest on the Earth surface, the term proportional to the square of the GCRS velocity of the clock in Eq. (
1
) corresponds to the centrifugal potential, caused by Earth’s proper rotation. Considering a clock on Earth’s equator, this returns a contribution to the clock rate of about
1\times 10^{-12}
with respect to TCG.

1.2
Terrestrial Time

The sum of the averaged centrifugal and effective potentials gives the mean equipotential at any position in the rigidly rotating frame of the Earth. All the clocks located at rest on a mean equipotential surface in the Earth vicinity, will be synchronous in the GCRS (up to small periodic terms of order
10^{-17}
), namely they will nearly beat the same rate with respect to TCG. Note that of course they can have rate differences due to their own limited accuracies.

One particular equipotential surface is originally the geoid, which coincides with the mean sea level. The IAU
[
IAU 1992
]
defined in 1991 a new coordinate time: the Terrestrial Time (TT), a scaled version of the coordinate time TCG, so that the mean rate of TT agrees as much as possible with that of the proper time of an observer located on the geoid. However, noting the intricacy and temporal changes of the definition and realization of the geoid, the IAU in 2000
[
IAU 2001
]
re-defined TT by fixing the scaling constant to
L_{G}=6.969\,290\,134\times 10^{-10}
such that

\mathrm{TT}-\mathrm{TCG}=-L_{G}\times(\mathrm{JD}_{\mathrm{TT}}-T_{0})\times 86\,400\ \mathrm{s}\,.

(5)

where
\mathrm{JD}_{\mathrm{TT}}
is the TT Julian date and
T_{0}=2\,443\,144.500\,372\,5
.

Let us note that according to
Klioner 2008
, scaling the coordinate time is necessarily accompanied by the corresponding scaling of spatial coordinates:
\bm{X}^{*}=(1-L_{G})\bm{X}
, and mass parameter of celestial bodies:
(Gm)^{*}=(1-L_{G})\,(Gm)
. This is needed to ensure that a range (difference of proper times) satisfies the general covariance principle as discussed in Sect.
3
. The scaled coordinate times and spatial coordinates can be thought of as defining
a new reference systems: one with coordinates
(cT^{*},\bm{X}^{*})
where
T^{*}
denotes TT. This
new reference system can be characterized by its own metric tensor, different from
the one of the GCRS
[
Klioner et al. 2009
]
.

Fixing the constant
L_{G}
also fixes the value to the reference Earth potential
W_{0}
used for defining TT.
Clocks located exactly on the geoid
W_{0}=62\,636\,856\ \mathrm{m}^{2}\,\mathrm{s}^{-2}
provide a direct realization of TT up to a precision of one part in
10^{-17}

[
Klioner 2008
]
, with an uncertainty depending on the clock accuracy.
The proper time of a clock ‘
\mathrm{e}
’ located at an orthometric altitude
H_{\mathrm{e}}
(i.e., altitude above the geoid) then deviates from TT by

\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau_{\mathrm{e}}-\mathrm{d}T^{*}}{\mathrm{d}T^{*}}=-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\int_{0}^{H_{\mathrm{e}}}g_{\mathrm{e}}(T^{*},h)\,\mathrm{d}h\,,

(6)

where
g_{\mathrm{e}}
is the gravity acceleration on Earth (i.e., the gradient of centrifugal plus effective potentials).
This rate deviation corresponds to a frequency shift of about
10^{-16}
per altitude meter.
A more detailed derivation can be found for instance in
Wolf and Petit 1995
.

One realization of TT is the International Atomic Time (TAI) with an offset of 32.184 s. This timescale is calculated a posteriori every month by the Bureau International des Poids et Mesures (BIPM), from a clock ensemble algorithm combining about 450 atomic clocks distributed around the world. To ensure the best agreement between TAI and the SI second duration, the result of this clock ensemble (called Echelle Atomique Libre, EAL) is then steered using the comparison with a dozen of primary and secondary frequency standards, namely cold atoms atomic fountains or more recently optical clocks
[
Panfilo and Arias 2019
]
.
As a consequence, the primary frequency standards measurements used to ensure the accuracy of the TAI have to be first corrected for their relativistic redshift, as TAI is a realization of TT which was defined in such a way that its rate is indiscernible from the mean rate of an ideal clock on the geoid defined by the equipotential
W_{0}
up to terms of order
10^{-17}

[
Klioner 2008
]
.
As an example, the frequency standards operating at the US National Institute of Standard and Technology (NIST), located at about
1\,660\ \mathrm{m}
above the geoid, must be corrected for a relativistic redshift of
(-1.798\,7\pm 0.000\,3)\times 10^{-13}

[
Pavlis and Weiss 2017
]
.
This value can be estimated from precise altitude measurements combined with geoid models and gravity measurements, see for instance
Pavlis and Weiss 2017
for more details.

Finally, the international reference for time and frequency is UTC, obtained from TAI after applying leap seconds. UTC being computed a posteriori, it is not available for a real time use. All time laboratories
contributing to UTC by submitting their clock data therefore maintain a realization of UTC called UTC(k), where ‘k’ is the acronym of the laboratory. The BIPM publishes each month the differences between the different UTC(k) and UTC
[
BIPM 2025
]
. The best realizations of UTC currently stay within a few nanoseconds of UTC. In most cases they are available in real time as a physical signal for synchronization and can be used to demonstrate the traceability to UTC.

2
Timescales in the selenocentric reference system

In this section, we aim at deriving the rate of proper time of a clock at rest on the surface of the Moon. To this end, we first introduce the LCRS and its associated space-time metric. Then, we derive the expression of the rate of proper time with respect to TCL for any cislunar clock. This expression is applied to the clock on the lunar surface. We systematically neglect physical effects smaller than one part in
10^{16}
, which is the long-term stability of UTC
[
Petit et al. 2015
]
—this threshold is well beyond the accuracy of the future clocks that will live in the cislunar region in the next decade. Finally, mimicking what was done for the Earth, the rate of proper time of the clock on the Moon is determined with respect to a rescaled version of TCL by invoking a lunar equipotential
\mathcal{W}_{0}
(similar to
W_{0}
in GCRS).

2.1
Lunar Celestial Reference System

The IAU 2024 resolution II
[
IAU 2025
]
recommends constructing a LCRS—with TCL as the coordinate time—using the same approach than for constructing the GCRS— with TCG as the coordinate time. It also recommends that the unit of measurement of TCL be consistent with the SI second.

The local LCRS coordinate system is denoted by
(\mathcal{X}^{\mu})=(c\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})
; it is centered at the center-of-mass of the Moon, with the
X
-axis directed towards the vernal equinox of J2000.0, the
Z
-axis pointing towards the celestial pole of Earth’s J2000.0 equator, and the
Y
-axis completing the right-handed triad. The spatial axis of LCRS are thus non-rotating with respect to distant extra galactic sources.

According to IAU 2024 resolution II, the LCRS space-time metric is constructed in a similar fashion than GCRS’s, namely based on the resolution B1.3 of the IAU 2000
[
Soffel et al. 2003
]
. The LCRS space-time metric, denoted by
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha\beta}
, is thus identical to the GCRS one, with Earth related quantities replaced by Moon’s ones, leading to the following components:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{00}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})

\displaystyle=-1+2c^{-2}\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})-2c^{-4}\mathcal{W}^{2}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})+\mathcal{O}(c^{-5})\,,

(7a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{0a}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})

\displaystyle=-4c^{-3}\mathcal{W}^{a}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})+\mathcal{O}(c^{-5})\,,

(7b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{ab}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})

\displaystyle=\delta_{ab}\left[1+2c^{-2}\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})\right]+\mathcal{O}(c^{-4})\,.

(7c)

The effective potentials
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{W}^{a}
are called the scalar potential and the (
a
-th component of the) vector potential
\bm{\mathcal{W}}
, respectively. The scalar potential reads

\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})=\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})+\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tidal}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})+\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{iner}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})\,,

(8)

with
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}
the lunar gravity potential,
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tidal}}
the tidal potential (mainly from Earth and Sun), and
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{iner}}
the inertial potential (caused by the non-geodesic acceleration of the Moon center-of-mass). The vector potential is given by

\bm{\mathcal{W}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})=\bm{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})+\bm{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{tidal}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})+\bm{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{iner}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})\,.

(9)

See for instance
Soffel et al. 2003
and references therein (in particular
Damour et al. 1991
;
Damour et al. 1992
) for full expressions of the different contributions in
\mathcal{W}
and
\bm{\mathcal{W}}
including terms up to
\mathcal{O}(c^{-2})
.

In LCRS, the position and the (coordinate) velocity of a cislunar clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ can be seen as functions of TCL, namely
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}=\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})
and
\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{l}}=\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})
with
\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{l}}=\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}/\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}
. Hence,
\tau_{\mathrm{l}}
, the proper time of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’, is given by

\mathrm{d}\tau_{\mathrm{l}}=\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}\big[-\mathcal{G}_{00}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})-2c^{-1}\,\mathcal{G}_{0a}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})\,\mathcal{V}^{a}_{\mathrm{l}}-c^{-2}\mathcal{G}_{ab}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})\,\mathcal{V}^{a}_{\mathrm{l}}\mathcal{V}^{b}_{\mathrm{l}}\big]^{1/2}\,,

(10)

where the components of the LCRS space-time metric
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha\beta}
are evaluated at time
\mathcal{T}
along the world-line of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’.
\mathcal{V}^{a}_{\mathrm{l}}
denotes the
a
-th component of
\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{l}}
. Then, by substituting Eqs. (
7
) for
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha\beta}
into Eq. (
10
), we find, after Taylor expanding the so-obtained expression in power series of
c^{-1}
, the rate of proper time of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ with respect to TCL:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau_{\mathrm{l}}-\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}}=-\frac{1}{2c^{2}}\left[\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{l}}^{2}+2\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{8c^{4}}\left[\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{l}}^{4}+12\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{l}}^{2}\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})-4\mathcal{W}^{2}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})-32\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{W}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})\right]+\mathcal{O}(c^{-5})\,,

(11)

where
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{l}}
represents the norm of the LCRS velocity of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’, namely
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{l}}=|\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{l}}|
. The term
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{l}}^{2}/2
is called the centrifugal potential at clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ and Eq. (
11
) is also referred to as the relative frequency difference between clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’s proper time and TCL.

2.2
Rate of proper time with respect to TCL

Let us now consider the clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ to be at rest on the lunar surface. All terms present in Eq. (
11
) are discussed and then estimated in
A
in this context. We summarize the discussion hereafter.

The position of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ is given, to a sufficient accuracy, in terms of its (spherical) selenographic coordinates
(\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}},\theta_{\mathrm{l}},\varphi_{\mathrm{l}})
by the following relation:

\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})=\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}\,\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})\,,

(12)

where
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}
is the (mean) radial distance of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ measured from the center-of-mass of the Moon and
\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}
is the unit vector defined such as

\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})=\bm{e}_{A}(\mathcal{T})\cos\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\cos\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}+\bm{e}_{B}(\mathcal{T})\cos\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\sin\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}+\bm{e}_{C}(\mathcal{T})\sin\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\,.

(13)

\theta_{\mathrm{l}}
and
\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}
being the (mean) selenographic latitude and longitude of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’, respectively (with
-90^{\circ}\leqslant\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\leqslant 90^{\circ}
and
-180^{\circ}<\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}\leqslant 180^{\circ}
). The unit vectors
\bm{e}_{A}
,
\bm{e}_{B}
, and
\bm{e}_{C}
form the vectorial basis attached to the principal axis of inertia of the Moon. The
A
and
C
-axis are directed along the directions of minimal and maximal moments of inertia of the Moon, respectively. The
B
-axis completes the right-handed triad.

The coordinate velocity of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ is given, to sufficient a accuracy, by

\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})=n_{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}\,\bm{e}_{C}(\mathcal{T})\times\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})\,,

(14)

n_{\mathrm{L}}
being the lunar mean motion; it corresponds to a month sideral period of
27.32\ \mathrm{day}
, namely
n_{\mathrm{L}}=2.66\times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{rad}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
. This expression neglects lunar physical librations that shall not exceed
10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad}
(see discussion below and in
A
).

The clock trajectory describes a time-like world-line in space-time and its velocity tells us how fast the clock moves along it. From that world-line, we can now deduce the rate of the proper time
\tau_{\mathrm{l}}
of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ with respect to TCL, up to a precision of one part in
10^{16}
. We find the following expression:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau_{\mathrm{l}}-\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\Phi(\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}},\theta_{\mathrm{l}},\varphi_{\mathrm{l}})\,,

(15)

where the equipotential
\Phi
reads as

\Phi(\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}},\theta_{\mathrm{l}},\varphi_{\mathrm{l}})=\frac{\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{l}}{}^{2}(\theta_{\mathrm{l}})}{2}+\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}(\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}},\theta_{\mathrm{l}},\varphi_{\mathrm{l}})+\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tidal}}(\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}},\theta_{\mathrm{l}},\varphi_{\mathrm{l}})\,.

(16)

According to results derived in
A
, the equipotential is constant in time at that precision and is given by the following relation:

2.3
Rate of proper time with respect to a rescaled TCL

The form of the solution in Eq. (
15
) suggests that, similar to what has been done for GCRS, we could introduce an equipotential surface close to the physical surface of the Moon. As discussed in
B
, the time averaging of the equipotential
\Phi
in Eq. (
16
) can be used to impose a numerical value for the equipotential of the selenoid. For instance, we can choose to define the selenoid such as
\bar{\Phi}_{\mathrm{sel}}=2.82\times 10^{6}\ \mathrm{m}^{2}\,\mathrm{s}^{-2}
(see
B
for more details); let us call this equipotential
\mathcal{W}_{0}=\bar{\Phi}_{\mathrm{sel}}
in order to match GCRS’s notations.

Then, by expanding
\Phi(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})
around
\mathcal{W}_{0}
in Eq. (
15
), the rate of proper time with respect to TCL, for a clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ located at an orthometric altitude
H_{\mathrm{l}}
relative to the equipotential surface of reference
\mathcal{W}_{0}
, will then be given by

\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau_{\mathrm{l}}-\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}}=-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\left[\mathcal{W}_{0}+\int_{0}^{H_{\mathrm{l}}}g_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T},h)\,\mathrm{d}h\right]\,,

(20)

where
g_{\mathrm{l}}
is the lunar gravity acceleration (i.e., the gradient of centrifugal plus effective potentials) at the clock location.
The integral in Eq. (
20
) corresponds to a frequency shift of about
2\times 10^{-17}
per altitude meter. While this factor is about 5 times smaller than on the Earth, the topography of the Moon surface is much more contrasted than on the Earth, with minima and maxima reaching 7.5 km with respect to the equipotential surface
\mathcal{W}_{0}
(see Fig.
13
and
Barker et al. 2015
). The frequency shift between the proper time of two clocks at the lowest and highest altitudes on the Moon is therefore about
3.0\times 10^{-13}
[see
(b)
-label in Fig.
1
].

The reader can notice that the term
\mathcal{W}_{0}
has been kept in Eq. (
20
) while no
W_{0}
was present in the corresponding Eq. (
6
) for a clock on the Earth. The reason is that Eq. (
6
) is not written in term of TCG but rather in term of TT, a rescaled coordinate time which was precisely defined to remove
W_{0}
from the averaged rate of proper time of a clock on the geoid. An equivalent approach for the Moon (if needed) would require us to introduce a new coordinate time being a scaled version of TCL, in a similar way as TT is a rescaled coordinate time with respect to TCG. Such a scaled coordinate time (labelled
\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}
hereafter), if it is constructed with the same purpose as TT in the geocentric context, would be given by
\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{T}+[\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}-\mathcal{T}]_{(\mathcal{T})}
, where the function
[\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}-\mathcal{T}]_{(\mathcal{T})}
is

[\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}-\mathcal{T}]_{(\mathcal{T})}=-L_{L}(\mathcal{T}-\mathcal{T}_{0})+\mathrm{const.}\,,

(21)

with
L_{L}=\mathcal{W}_{0}/c^{2}
a defining constant. As expected, making use of this new coordinate timescale rather than TCL allows one to remove the constant rate
\mathcal{W}_{0}
from the proper time equation which shall now reduce (at leading order in
c^{-1}
) to

\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau_{\mathrm{l}}-\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}}=-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\int_{0}^{H_{\mathrm{l}}}g_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T}^{\dagger},h)\,\mathrm{d}h\,.

(22)

This result can alternatively be written such as

[\tau-\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}]_{(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{l}}^{\dagger},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}^{\dagger})}=-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\left[\Phi(\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}},\theta_{\mathrm{l}},\varphi_{\mathrm{l}})-\mathcal{W}_{0}\right](\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}-\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\dagger})+\mathrm{const.}

(23)

Relying on
\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}
rather than
\mathcal{T}
for computing the proper time of a clock on the lunar surface will remove the constant rate of
3.14\times 10^{-11}
due to the monopole contribution [see
(a)
-label in Fig.
1
] and leave behind a peak-to-peak amplitude of
3.0\times 10^{-13}
mainly due to the lunar topography as depicted in the
(b)
-label of Fig.
1
.

Let us emphasize that the scaling of the lunar coordinate time will force similar scaling into distances and mass parameters
[
Klioner 2008
]
:
\bm{\mathcal{X}}^{\dagger}=(1-L_{L})\bm{\mathcal{X}}
and
(Gm)_{\dagger}=(1-L_{L})\,(Gm)
, respectively. Note also that the scaled
(Gm)_{\dagger}
of the current section would be different from the one introduced in Sect.
1.2
[i.e.,
(Gm)_{*}
] as expected from the occurrence of parameters
L_{L}
and
L_{G}
in the scaling relations.

It is important to note that
\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}
, the scaled counterpart of TCL, has neither been established nor adopted in any IAU resolution to date. Hence, introducing
\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}
for the lunar system—in a similar way as TT is introduced in the geocentric context—is a possible way (see e.g.,
Ashby and Patla 2024
;
Kopeikin and Kaplan 2024
, and
Turyshev et al. 2025
), but not a required one. Indeed, before the adoption of a fully relativistic framework by the IAU at the end of the 20th century, timescales similar to TT and Barycentric Dynamical Time (TDB) were already in widespread operational use. In other words, TT and TDB existed (with different definitions) long before the coordinate timescales TCB and TCG were introduced by the IAU in 1991. Abandoning completely the pre-existing timescales was not feasible due to their long-standing practical utility. It was thus preferable to equip them with consistent definitions within the new relativistic formalism. In 2000 and 2006, TT and TDB were thus defined from TCG and TCB, respectively.

For the lunar system, there is no historical precedent of
\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}
being employed independently of any Earth-based timescales. Therefore, the necessity of defining and adopting
\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}
, as introduced in Eq. (
21
), remains open to question. This point will be further discussed in Sects.
4
and
6
.

3
Geocentric and selenocentric timescale difference

In previous sections, we derived the rates of proper times with respect to local coordinate timescales in GCRS and LCRS for clocks on the Earth and on the Moon, respectively. In view of the future communication between the Earth and the Moon it is also needed to establish the relation between the local coordinate times employed around the Moon and around the Earth. As it will be shown in this section, this relation is not unique, it depends on the intermediate reference system which is used for the light travel time computation.

Hereafter we consider a clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ on or around the Moon and a clock ‘
\mathrm{e}
’ on or around the Earth,
and a one-way synchronisation between them. We consider that the synchronizing link is an electromagnetic signal (encoding the proper time of clock ‘
\mathrm{e}
’) emitted by clock ‘
\mathrm{e}
’ at geocentric coordinate time
T_{\mathrm{e}}
and then received by clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ at selenocentric coordinate time
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{l}}
. The light travel time is computed in an intermediate reference system using its associated coordinate time (rescaled or not). We consider three possible intermediate reference systems: (i) BCRS with TCB or TDB, (ii) GCRS with TCG or TT, and finally (iii) LCRS with TCL or a rescaled TCL. Each of these cases is discussed in turn with their advantages and drawbacks.

3.1
Using BCRS with TCB or TDB

Let us start investigating the range equation (i.e., the difference between proper times) in BCRS while using TCB as intermediate timescale. The range between clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ and clock ‘
\mathrm{e}
’ obtained through a one-way signal reads as follows:

\tau_{\mathrm{l}}-\tau_{\mathrm{e}}=[\tau-\mathcal{T}]_{(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})}+[\mathcal{T}-t]_{(t_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}})}+[t_{\mathrm{l}}-t_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}})}-[T-t]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}})}-[\tau-T]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}})}\,,

(24)

where
[\tau-\mathcal{T}]_{(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})}
and
[\tau-T]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}})}
are the transformations between the proper time of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ and TCL, and the proper time of clock ‘
\mathrm{e}
’ and TCG, respectively. These two functions have been presented in Eqs. (
18
) and (
1
), respectively considering clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ on the lunar surface. Note that, according to Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
), the function
[\tau-T]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}})}
is usually separated into two pieces [cf. Eq. (
37
) below]: (i) the transformation from proper time of clock ‘
\mathrm{e}
’ to TT, namely
[\tau-T^{*}]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}}^{*},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}}^{*})}
, and (ii) the transformation from TT to TCG, that is
[T^{*}-T]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}})}
.

Then, the term
[t_{\mathrm{l}}-t_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}})}
in Eq. (
24
) corresponds to the light travel time of the signal expressed in the global coordinate system: the BCRS with TCB. For the one-way experiment under consideration, the time of reception of the signal is unknown. Therefore, the light travel time is only function of the barycentric time of emission
t_{\mathrm{e}}
, the barycentric position of emitter
\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}}=\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}}(t_{\mathrm{e}})
, and the barycentric position of receiver
\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}=\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}(t_{\mathrm{l}})
. The function
[t_{\mathrm{l}}-t_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}})}
is thus called an emission time transfer function
[
Teyssandier and Le Poncin-Lafitte 2008
]
. It returns the difference between the (coordinate) time of reception and the (coordinate) time of emission:

t_{\mathrm{l}}-t_{\mathrm{e}}=[t_{\mathrm{l}}-t_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}})}\,.

(25)

In most applications (except lensing effect), the time transfer function is the one corresponding to the so-called quasi-Minkowskian light path
[
Linet and Teyssandier 2016
]
, and is thus expressible such as

[t_{\mathrm{l}}-t_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}}(t_{\mathrm{e}}),\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}(t_{\mathrm{l}}))}=\frac{|\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}(t_{\mathrm{l}})-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}}(t_{\mathrm{e}})|}{c}+\frac{\Delta(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}}(t_{\mathrm{e}}),\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}(t_{\mathrm{l}}))}{c}\,,

(26)

with the following associated condition:
|\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}(t_{\mathrm{l}})-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}}(t_{\mathrm{e}})|\gg\Delta(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}}(t_{\mathrm{e}}),\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}(t_{\mathrm{l}}))
. In these expressions,
\Delta/c
is called a delay function
[
Teyssandier and Le Poncin-Lafitte 2008
]
; it usually contains a gravitational delay, called the Shapiro delay, and an atmospheric delay due to the presence of the Earth atmosphere (see e.g.,
Bourgoin 2020
for an expression of the delay function containing simultaneously gravitational and atmospheric delays). Note that the light-time Eq. (
25
) is implicit in
t_{\mathrm{l}}
, it must thus be solved iteratively in order to eventually determine
\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}(t_{\mathrm{l}})
. In the
D
, we present analytical expressions of
t_{\mathrm{l}}
including
\mathcal{O}(c^{-3})
terms.

Finally, the functions
[\mathcal{T}-t]_{(t,\bm{x})}
and
[T-t]_{(t,\bm{x})}
in Eq. (
24
) are part of 4D relativistic transformations between (i) LCRS and BCRS, and (ii) GCRS and BCRS, respectively. Hereafter, we shall discuss each in turn.

3.1.1
Transformation from TCB to TCG.

The one-before-last term in Eq. (
24
), namely
[T-t]_{(t,\bm{x})}
, is the difference between the geocentric and barycentric coordinate times, expressed at barycentric coordinate time
t
and at barycentric position
\bm{x}
. This difference is given by the IAU Resolution B1.5 (2000) (considering here only
c^{-2}
terms):

[T-t]_{(t,\bm{x})}=-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\left\{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left[\frac{v^{2}_{\mathrm{E}}(t^{\prime})}{2}+w_{\lnot\mathrm{E}}(\bm{x}_{\mathrm{E}}(t^{\prime}))\right]\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}+\bm{v}_{\mathrm{E}}(t)\cdot[\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{E}}(t)]\right\}+\mathcal{O}(c^{-4})\,,

(27)

where
\bm{x}_{\mathrm{E}}
and
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{E}}
are the position and velocity of the geocenter in BCRS, and
w_{\lnot\mathrm{E}}
is the Newtonian potential (evaluated at the geocenter) due to all bodies in the solar system besides Earth. The reference epoch
t_{0}
is 1977 January 1st,
0^{\mathrm{h}}0^{\mathrm{m}}32.184^{\mathrm{s}}
in TCB, or 1977 January 1st,
0^{\mathrm{h}}0^{\mathrm{m}}0^{\mathrm{s}}
TAI.

3.1.2
Transformation from TCB to TCL.

The second term of Eq. (
24
), namely
[\mathcal{T}-t]_{(t,\bm{x})}
, is the equivalent for TCL of the term just described for TCG; it gives the difference between the selenocentric and barycentric coordinate times, expressed at barycentric coordinate time
t
and at barycentric position
\bm{x}
. IAU Resolution II (2024) recommends that the transformation between TCL and TCB be given by a relation similar to the one described in IAU Resolution
B1.5 (2000), with Earth related quantities replaced by Moon’s. The relationship between TCB and TCL is therefore given by the time part of the full 4D relativistic transformation between the barycentric and selenocentric reference systems:

[\mathcal{T}-t]_{(t,\bm{x})}=-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\left\{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left[\frac{v^{2}_{\mathrm{L}}(t^{\prime})}{2}+w_{\lnot\mathrm{L}}(\bm{x}_{\mathrm{L}}(t^{\prime}))\right]\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}+\bm{v}_{\mathrm{L}}(t)\cdot[\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{L}}(t)]\right\}+\mathcal{O}(c^{-4})\,,

(28)

where
\bm{x}_{\mathrm{L}}
and
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{L}}
are the position and velocity of the Moon center-of-mass in BRCS, and
w_{\lnot\mathrm{L}}(\bm{x}_{\mathrm{L}})
is the Newtonian potential (evaluated at the Moon center-of-mass) due to all the bodies in the solar system besides Moon. The reference epoch
t_{0}
is the same as for the difference
\mathrm{TCG}-\mathrm{TCB}
. Currently, the difference between TCG and TCB [cf. Eq. (
27
)] can be computed using the IAU service establishing and maintaining an accessible and authoritative set of algorithms and procedures that implement models used in Standards Of Fundamental Astronomy (SOFA)
[
IAU SOFA Board 2025
]
. The difference is then obtained for a given coordinate time and position
(t,\bm{x})
. The lunar-equivalent is not available to date in the SOFA but could, in principle, be provided in a similar way.

From these expressions, it appears that in BRCS, it is not possible to provide a unique relation for
\mathrm{TCG}-\mathrm{TCB}
and
\mathrm{TCL}-\mathrm{TCB}

3.1.4
Using TDB instead of TCB.

Let us emphasize that the numerical value of the range in Eq. (
24
) is independent of the barycentric timescale being employed for the computation of the light travel time. Either TCB or its rescaled version, TDB, could have been used indifferently. TDB was defined by the IAU Resolution B3 (2006) as a scaled version of TCB with a scaling factor chosen in order to have the same average rate than TT at the geocenter:

\mathrm{TDB}-\mathrm{TCB}=-L_{B}\times(\mathrm{JD}_{\mathrm{TCB}}-T_{0})\times 86\,400\ \mathrm{s}+\mathrm{TDB}_{0}\,,

(29)

where
\mathrm{JD}_{\mathrm{TCB}}
is the TCB Julian date;
T_{0}
was given earlier in Eq. (
5
).
L_{B}
and
\mathrm{TDB}_{0}
are two defining constants:
L_{B}=1.550\,519\,768\times 10^{-8}
and
\mathrm{TDB}_{0}=-6.55\times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s}
, respectively. For the range to be independent of using either
\mathrm{TDB}
or TCB in Eq. (
24
), some cancellations of coordinate-dependent terms must occur. For this to happen, the scaling of TDB-compatible quantities such as distances and mass parameters has to be carried out closely as discussed in
E
. There, we indeed show that the range is generally covariant only if the scaling of TDB-compatible quantities is properly accounted for in the light travel time expression. Ignoring this scaling would induce relative errors of the order of
L_{B}
in the computation of proper times difference
\tau_{\mathrm{l}}-\tau_{\mathrm{e}}
; this is because the scaling is affecting the leading order term in the light travel time equation [i.e., the Minkowskian term in Eq. (
26
)]. Considering that planetary ephemerides are computed and distributed in a TDB-compatible frame (see e.g., INPOP19
[
Fienga et al. 2019
]
and DE440/441
[
Park et al. 2021
]
), it is more advantageous and safe to compute the light-time in TDB rather than in TCB, and this means inserting Eq. (
143
) into (
24
), so that we eventually obtain the following symmetrical expression for the range:

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{l}}-\tau_{\mathrm{e}}

\displaystyle=[\tau-\mathcal{T}]_{(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})}+[\mathcal{T}-t]_{(t_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}})}-[t^{**}-t]_{(t_{\mathrm{l}})}+[t^{**}_{\mathrm{l}}-t^{**}_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(t^{**}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}^{**}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}^{**}_{\mathrm{l}})}

\displaystyle+[t^{**}-t]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}})}-[T-t]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}})}-[\tau-T]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}})}\,,

(30)

where
t^{**}
is TDB and
\bm{x}^{**}
denotes TDB-compatible coordinates.

3.2
Using GCRS with TCG or TT

The clock comparison presented in the previous section involved two coordinate time transformations computed at two different positions when using BCRS as intermediate system for the computation of the light travel time (relying either on TCB or TDB). An alternative path would be to use instead GCRS as the intermediate system. In this case, a single coordinate time transformation is required at the level of Moon’s clock with the light travel time computed in GCRS (relying either on TCG or TT). Indeed, the range between clock ‘
\mathrm{e}
’ and clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ through the one-way signal then reads

\tau_{\mathrm{l}}-\tau_{\mathrm{e}}=[\tau-\mathcal{T}]_{(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})}+[\mathcal{T}-T]_{(T_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{l}})}+[T_{\mathrm{l}}-T_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{l}})}-[\tau-T]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}})}\,,

(31)

where the light travel time is expressed in the GCRS with emission and reception coordinate times in TCG. Let us emphasize that the difference
[\mathcal{T}-T]_{(T_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{l}})}

\bm{X}_{\mathrm{l}}
, namely the geocentric position of the lunar clock; the position of the clock on Earth does not show up in the coordinate time transformation.

3.2.1
Timescales transformation in GCRS.

This is the approach followed by
Kopeikin and Kaplan 2024
to derive the coordinate time transformation between TCL and TCG. Reworking Eq. (
28

27
) to it, allows one to reduce the influence of external bodies to the Earth-Moon system to tidal terms only. As emphasized by

\mathrm{TCL}-\mathrm{TCG}

\displaystyle[\mathcal{T}-T]_{(T_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{l}})}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\Bigg\{\int_{T_{0}}^{T_{\mathrm{l}}}\left[\frac{V^{2}_{\mathrm{L}}(T)}{2}+\frac{G(m_{\mathrm{E}}-2m_{\mathrm{L}})}{|\bm{X}_{\mathrm{L}}(T)|}+W_{\mathrm{tidal}}(T,\bm{X}_{\mathrm{L}}(T))\right]\mathrm{d}T

(32)

with

(33)

with
\bm{N}_{\mathrm{S}}=\bm{X}_{\mathrm{S}}/|\bm{X}_{\mathrm{S}}|
and
\bm{N}_{\mathrm{L}}=\bm{X}_{\mathrm{L}}/|\bm{X}_{\mathrm{L}}|

32

Kopeikin and Kaplan 2024

32
) cannot be retrieved from Eq. (20) of
Fienga et al. 2024
because of differences in velocity-dependent terms.

The so-obtained difference
\mathrm{TCL}-\mathrm{TCG}
shows the same

(or

3.2.2
Using TT instead of TCG.

TCG is not the only coordinate time which might be employed to compute the light travel time in GCRS coordinates; TT could be used too, and hence, the light-time would split into the three following contributions:

[T_{\mathrm{l}}-T_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{l}})}=[T^{*}-T]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}})}+[T_{\mathrm{l}}^{*}-T_{\mathrm{e}}^{*}]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}}^{*},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}}^{*},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{l}}^{*})}-[T^{*}-T]_{(T_{\mathrm{l}})}\,,

(35)

where
[T_{\mathrm{l}}^{*}-T_{\mathrm{e}}^{*}]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}}^{*},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}}^{*},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{l}}^{*})}
is the TT-compatible light travel time, and where
[T^{*}-T]_{(T)}
is given in Eq. (
5
). To ensure the general covariance while computing the difference of proper times
\tau_{\mathrm{l}}-\tau_{\mathrm{e}}
, it is important to employ TT-compatible distances and mass parameters in the light travel time expression (this statement can be demonstrated in a similar way as in
E
for BCRS with TDB or TCB). However, let us emphasize that mass parameters and distances which are distributed by planetary ephemerides are usually TDB-compatible. This means that two scalings have to be employed to go from TDB-compatible quantities to TT-compatible ones. For instance, to pass from a TDB-compatible mass parameter
(Gm)_{**}
to the TT-compatible one,
(Gm)_{*}
, a first scaling to go from TDB to TCB/TCG-compatible framework must be applied, and then, a second scaling to go from TCG to the TT-compatible mass parameter. The relation between
(Gm)_{*}
and
(Gm)_{**}
thus eventually reads such as

(Gm)_{*}=\left(\frac{1-L_{G}}{1-L_{B}}\right)(Gm)_{**}\,.

(36)

Considering that planetary ephemerides are computed and distributed in a TDB-compatible frame, it is more advantageous and safe to compute the light-time in TCG rather than in TT—in TCG-compatible frame, only one scaling shall be applied to TDB-compatible parameters whereas two scalings are needed in a TT-compatible frame. However, note that if TT is used somewhere else in the range calculation, the double scaling issue will persist. Indeed, TT is usually not employed at the level of the light-time computation but rather at the level of the proper time of the clock on Earth as stated earlier below Eq. (
24
). Therefore, the rate of clock ‘
\mathrm{e}
’s proper time with respect to TCG in Eq. (
31
) is usually replaced by

[\tau-T]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}})}=[\tau-T^{*}]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}}^{*},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}}^{*})}+[T^{*}-T]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}})}\,,

(37)

while the light-time computation is maintained in TCG. Note however that, from a theoretical point of view, this does not change the double scaling issue since the proper time must now be computed with TT-compatible quantities as it is clearly visible from the presence of TT-compatible arguments in the function
[\tau-T^{*}]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}}^{*},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}}^{*})}
.

3.3
Using LCRS with TCL or rescaled TCL

Finally, we can also consider working in the LCRS, where the corresponding range equation reads

\tau_{\mathrm{l}}-\tau_{\mathrm{e}}=[\tau-\mathcal{T}]_{(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})}+[\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{l}}-\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})}-[T-\mathcal{T}]_{(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{e}})}-[\tau-T]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}})}\,.

(38)

In this expression, the light travel time is now computed in LCRS coordinates and a unique coordinate time transformation is actually needed:
[T-\mathcal{T}]_{(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{e}})}

Using the same analytical development as in

where

3.4
Summary

3.4.1
General covariance.

As already discussed in this section, the expression of the range between two clocks depends on the intermediate reference system and its associated coordinate timescale being adopted for the computation of the light travel time. However, as imposed by the general covariance principle, the numerical value of the range has to remain unchanged no matter the intermediate reference system being employed. To ensure a full covariance, the structure of the equation relating the two proper times invariably relies on three contributions: (i) a function relating observer’s proper time to a local coordinate time, (ii) a transformation between a local coordinate time and an intermediate coordinate time (used for expressing the light travel time), and (iii) a time transfer function expressed in the intermediate coordinate time. In a one-way experiment, when the intermediate reference system is BCRS, the transformation (ii) involves two timescale transformations applied at the two space-time events
(t_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}})
and
(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}})
. In GCRS (resp. LCRS) only one timescale transformation is required; it is applied to the space-time event
(T_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{l}})
[resp.
(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{e}})
]. However, let us emphasize that this greater simplicity is only apparent, it is indeed counterbalanced by the fact that the light travel time must be expressed in GCRS (or LCRS) whereas the positions and velocities are usually distributed in BCRS by planetary ephemerides. Thus, a spatial relativistic transformation from BCRS to GCRS (or LCRS) is generally required into the light-time expression. Moreover, if the intermediate coordinate time is a rescaled coordinate time, the general covariance imposes one to use rescaled distances and mass parameters in the light-time expression or in the proper time computation, and this might be a source of confusion and errors (we recall that the scaling on distances impacts the leading order term in the time transfer function). This is even more critical if the intermediate coordinate time is TT or a rescaled version of TCL since two scalings are required for a complete invariance: a first scaling from TDB to TCB/TCG/TCL and a second one to TT or to the rescaled version of TCL. Therefore, in a given application, if planetary ephemerides must be called to compute a light-time and a proper time, we would advise to rely on expression (
30
) (without rescaled coordinate times) in order to avoid possible confusion at the level of coordinate transformations and scaling factors.

3.4.2
Practical use.

As we have seen, the difference between TCG and TCL is showing a secular term of about
1.5\textnormal{\penalty\ $\mu$s}/\mathrm{day}
plus periodic variations with magnitude depending on the coordinate system and on the clock positions where this difference is computed.
Users on Earth willing to communicate with users on the Moon (or inversely), will have to use timescale transformations which are consistent with the intermediate reference system being adopted for the computation of the light travel time. As discussed earlier, these transformations being part of 4D relativistic transformations, they depend on a location. For users on the surface of the Earth and on the surface of the Moon, the location-dependent terms seem to have straightforward dependency to latitudes and longitudes. It would therefore be possible to derive analytical expressions for those such that the users should just provide their local coordinates which would be broadcast by the lunar communication and navigation services. The difference with a lunar reference coordinate time and UTC is therefore not unique, and only the secular term is unambiguous. The exact value of this frequency shift will slightly vary over long time scales due to the secular relative motion of the Moon and the Earth. However, a standard value could be used by the different space agencies as a convention, avoiding the need to broadcast it continuously. The variations with respect to the conventional value, could then be broadcast as a polynomial.

4
Three options for the lunar reference timescale

In order to allow interoperability between different systems of the different actors on the Moon, it is important to define a common reference timescale, exactly as TT (and its realization TAI, or further UTC) has been chosen and commonly adopted for all activities in the vicinity of the Earth. We suggest to call the reference timescale in the Moon environment the Lunar Time (TL)

1

We do not recommend using LT since this abbreviation is used for “Local Time”.
. Several criteria have to be considered for this lunar timescale to be adopted as a reference.

•

The lunar reference timescale should be defined from TCL.

•

A physical realization should be available so that all actors could steer their clocks and internal timescales on this reference. Note that this physical realization should be based on Earth’s clocks as long as there is no accurate clock on the Moon.

•

This reference should have a clear and documented relationship with UTC. UTC produced by the BIPM, based on TAI, is indeed the only recommended timescale for international reference as stated in the Resolution 2 of the 26th CGPM in 2018
CGP 2019
.

The first criteria implies that TL is an affine function of TCL, namely scaled by some constant frequency offset, namely

\mathrm{TL}=\mathrm{TCL}+\Delta f\,(\mathrm{TCL}-\mathrm{TCL}_{0})+\mathrm{const.}

(45)

1.

\Delta f=0
;

2.

is such that the rate of TL corresponds, on average, to the rate of proper time of a clock on a given selenoid;

3.

\Delta f
is such that there are only periodic variations between TL and TT.

4.1
TL equal to TCL

This option implies that an ideal clock located on the surface of the Moon would have a frequency shift about
3.14\times 10^{-11}
, that is to say
2.7\textnormal{\penalty\ $\mu$s}/\mathrm{day}
, with respect to the reference TCL. However, this frequency offset would be visible only if the clock accuracy is sufficient, namely better than the
10^{-11}
level. Note that to date only Caesium clocks, H-masers and optical clocks reach that level on the Earth. Nevertheless, in order to avoid possible large differences between the reference and the time indicated by an accurate clock on the lunar surface, its frequency could be adjusted by a synthesizer from which the output signal would tick at a rate corresponding to the TCL. This is similar to what is currently done on the Earth for the UTC(k) which are steered on UTC. These UTC(k) are driven from clocks at different altitudes, hence with different rates with respect to TT, and their frequency is therefore adjusted to compensate the gravitational redshift and the clock instability in order to provide a timescale aligned on UTC. It is also similar to what is done for some GNSS satellite clocks which are pre-tuned to tick UTC while their proper time would deviate from UTC by about
38\textnormal{\penalty\ $\mu$s}/\mathrm{day}
.

Depending on user needs, any clock in cislunar environment could be adjusted to the reference time TCL broadcast for instance by the lunar navigation systems, or some ground station—on Earth or even on the Moon in the future. For Mars, the frequency offset is about
1.4\times 10^{-10}
between a clock on the surface and TCM; the exact same approach as just explained for the Moon, namely introducing frequency corrections, could be employed, depending on user needs.

The main advantage of this option with respect to options (
2
) and (
3
) below is that no additional coordinate time besides TCL has to be introduced, which avoids new definition and avoids the need to introduce new scaling of mass parameters and distances. If this choice is also adopted for Mars and other planets in a near future, it would drastically simplify future transformations between reference systems and timescales. There would be only one time scale per local reference system except for the geocentric system which would still involve TCG and TT for historical reasons.

4.2
TL rate aligned on the average clock rate on a selenoid

This option consists in mimicking what has been done for the Earth with TT (see e.g.,

Moreover, as seen in Sect.

Note that, unlike option (

4.3

In this option, the difference between TL and TT would reduce to periodic terms only (see e.g.,
Fienga et al. 2024

5
Realization of TL and traceability to UTC

In the first years, having no atomic clock on the Moon, there will be no local realization of TL. The reference used for the cislunar timing will necessarily be based on Earth clocks and some time transfer between lunar or cislunar clocks and the Earth clocks.
The best references available in real-time on the Earth are the realizations of UTC, named UTC(k).
From what we have seen before, there is no unique relation between TT and TL, as it depends on the reference system in which the observer is, and on the clock locations. However, it is possible to monitor the difference between a lunar clock and the lunar reference timescale using time and frequency transfer with a clock realizing UTC on the Earth. The lunar clock error can be determined after removing the position-dependent relativistic terms from the clock comparison.
Let us consider UTC(k) at a given location on the Earth.

The lunar clock with proper time
\tau_{\mathrm{l}}

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{l}}+[\tau-\mathcal{T}^{\ddagger}]_{(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{l}}^{\ddagger},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}^{\ddagger})}

Whether their magnitude will be significant with respect to the other sources of uncertainties in the clock comparisons is out of the scope of this paper.

It is foreseen that in the future there will be autonomous clocks on the Moon. For example, ESA should launch in early thirties the NovaMoon lunar in-orbit demonstrator and reference station which will provide a lunar-based local differential, geodetic and timing stations to enhance the accuracy of lunar PNT services to sub-meter levels across the South Pole
[
Ventura-Traveset et al. 2025

To be completely autonomous, these clocks should have a frequency accuracy sufficient for the user needs, and located on a place where the gravity potential is known with a sufficient accuracy so that the gravitational redshift with respect to the reference can be taken into account. These local realizations of the lunar reference time TL could be called TL(k), and if a network of clock is then used in an algorithm ensemble like is done for UTC on the Earth, we propose then to call this ensemble timescale TL(MOON). The traceability to UTC should however still be ensured via regular time transfer links between TL(k) and UTC(k).

6
Discussion and conclusion

Since prehistoric times, the time on the Earth was based on the Earth rotation. The duration of a day was defined as
86\,400\ \mathrm{s}
. As soon as clocks were built, they were designed to tick this
time on the surface of the Earth. When special and general relativity entered into the game, with the need to define a coordinate time associated to the GCRS, it was of course natural to continue with the time rate as it was in use since millennia. For the moon, the situation is completely different as there is currently no clock in use on the Moon, a reference timescale has thus to be defined from scratch. The only obligation is to define a lunar reference timescale based on the TCL which is the coordinate time associated with the LCRS.

Three options were proposed in the previous section: (
1
) using directly TCL, or using a scaled version of TCL so that (
2
) the lunar reference timescale corresponds, on average, to the proper time of a clock on the surface of the Moon, or, (
3
) the lunar reference timescale differs from TT only by periodic variations.

Options (
2
) and (
3
) would require an associated scaling of mass parameters and distances, which would introduce avoidable complexity in every computation, especially for Earth-Moon time and frequency transfers. In addition, because we consider that a similar approach should be used later for Mars and maybe for other planets, this would again ask for additional scalings, with, for each of them, a different numerical value for mass parameters and distances in the solar system. In particular, using option (
3
) in the case of Mars would imply a scaling of the order of
10^{-9}
which, if not correctly implemented, would lead to errors of the oder of few kilometers on the Earth-Mars distance. In the option (
3
), in addition, an accurate clock placed on the Moon surface (or later Mars surface) would not tick the reference time but would have a frequency offset of about
7\times 10^{-10}
for the Moon and
6\times 10^{-9}
for Mars; this frequency offset should be compensated for all applications requiring a higher accuracy. We therefore consider this option as the less convenient one. Aside of the problem of scaling, one advantage of option (
2
) would be that an accurate clock on the Moon surface would tick the reference time TL; however this argument is valid only at the condition that the clock accuracy is better than
10^{-11}
and that the user need is less than
10^{-13}
which is the variation of proper time along the Moon surface due to the topography. For less accurate clocks of more precise user needs, the clocks should be steered on the reference, exactly as if the reference is not defined on the selenoid. In view of this, and considering in addition that option (
2
) would require the definition of a lunar
\mathcal{W}_{0}
which does not exist to date, we consider that the less constraining and natural solution is to use option (
1
), that is to say
\mathrm{TL}=\mathrm{TCL}
, for which only one scaling is required to transform TDB-compatible quantities into TCL/TCB-compatible quantities.

The steering of clocks in the lunar environment should be done from time and frequency transfers with either the Earth clocks, or with lunar navigation satellites.
And the adjusted frequency will be maintained by the clocks for a certain period of time, depending on their intrinsic stability, until the next connection with either the Earth or the satellites. For very accurate clocks, the steering could also be done based on very accurate determination of the orthometric altitude.

For the link between the lunar time and UTC, we have shown that the relationships between the different proper times are complex and can not be uniquely modeled, at accuracy levels well within the expected requirements for applications such as positioning and navigation. The secular trend between Moon time and Earth time, whatever the reference Lunar Time, could however be sufficient to time-tag events in UTC at the sub-microsecond level, which is the magnitude of the periodic terms between TL and TT when working in the Earth-based or Moon-based coordinate systems. We expect UTC to remain the common operational timescale for humans on the Moon, as the contact and coordination with Earth-based operators will be crucial and frequent, and in many cases the synchronization error will be completely negligible for these contacts. For example, it will be less error-prone to synchronize launches for orbital rendez-vous with the control center in UTC than having to use different timescales for the different spacecrafts involved. In such a scenario, errors committed by neglecting the periodic differences of proper time over the short time span of the manoeuvre are negligible with respect to the trajectory corrections that will be necessary anyway for other reasons.

For more precise applications related to communications between the Earth and the Moon, the user will have to consider both the periodic terms of
\mathrm{TL}-\mathrm{TT}
and the travel time of its communication link. We therefore suggest that the periodic terms from the integrals of Eqs. (
27
) and (
28
) are broadcast to the users in a standard way, plus some expression of the scalar products as a function of the latitude and longitude for both the Earth and the Moon. So that it is easy for the timing device to communicate with an Earth station working in UTC, while still keeping its internal timescale reference aligned on TL. These informations could take the form of polynomials, similar to how (
\mathrm{UTC}-\mathrm{GNSS}
timescale) are currently broadcast in navigation messages. The level of complexity of this scheme (degree of the polynomials, their validity period, sensitivity to ephemerides, expected accuracy) has to be studied more in details.

Finally, the different options for a lunar reference timescale proposed here should be reviewed by the space agencies and analysed in the frame of their operational constraints. Defining the coordinate timescale to be used in the cislunar region will be a task for the IAU. As long as there is no accurate clock on the Moon, the UTC(k), being realization of TT, will be used together with the modeling of relativistic effects based on lunar ephemerides, to serve as realization of the lunar timescale reference TL.

We shall introduce a selenographic frame in which clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ is at rest. This frame is rigidly attached to the principal axis of inertia of Moon’s figure and is thus referred to as the “lunar principal axes frame”. The
A
and
C
-axis are directed along the direction of minimal and maximal moments of inertia of the Moon, respectively. The
B
-axis completes the right-handed triad. We shall denote by
(\bm{e}_{A},\bm{e}_{B},\bm{e}_{C})
the three unit-vectors of the lunar principal axis frame. In this frame, the selenocentric position of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ reads as (see also
Kopeikin and Kaplan 2024
for similar constant part)

\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})=\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}\,\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})+\Delta\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})\,,

(49)

where the unit vector
\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}
is defined by

\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})=\bm{e}_{A}(\mathcal{T})\cos\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\cos\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}+\bm{e}_{B}(\mathcal{T})\cos\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\sin\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}+\bm{e}_{C}(\mathcal{T})\sin\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\,.

(50)

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}
is the (mean) radial distance of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ from the center-of-mass of the Moon,
\theta_{\mathrm{l}}
and
\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}
being the (mean) selenographic latitude and longitude of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’, respectively (with
-90^{\circ}\leqslant\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\leqslant 90^{\circ}
and
-180^{\circ}<\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}\leqslant 180^{\circ}
). The term
\Delta\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}
represents the degree 2 lunar surface displacement due to tides raised on Moon by bodies in the solar system (mainly Earth and Sun). This term can be modelled such as (cf.
Petit and Luzum 2010
)

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\left(\frac{m_{\mathrm{A}}}{m_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)^{\!3}\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{\!4}

\displaystyle\times\Bigg\{h_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}\left[P_{2}\left(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})\cdot\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}(\mathcal{T})\right)-\frac{\delta_{\mathrm{A}\mathrm{E}}}{2}(3\cos^{2}\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\cos^{2}\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}-1)\right]\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle+3l_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})\cdot\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}(\mathcal{T}))\big[\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}(\mathcal{T})-(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})\cdot\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}(\mathcal{T}))\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})\big]\Bigg\}\,,

(51)

where
h_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}
(resp.
l_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}
) is the nominal degree 2 Love (resp. Shida) number and
P_{2}
is the Legendre polynomial of degree 2.
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}
and
\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}
are respectively the norm and direction of the selenocentric position vector of body
\mathrm{A}
, namely
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{A}}=\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}\,\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}
with
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}=|\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{A}}|
. Let us emphasize that we have removed the constant radial part of the tidal displacement due to Earth tides since it shall be included in the lunar topography. This was done by introducing
\delta_{\mathrm{A}\mathrm{E}}
which is
\delta_{\mathrm{A}\mathrm{E}}=1
if
\mathrm{A}=\mathrm{E}
(i.e, if the external body is Earth) and
\delta_{\mathrm{A}\mathrm{E}}=0
otherwise. We can approximate the radial displacement
\Delta\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}
as

\Delta\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}\simeq\Delta\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}\,,

(52)

at linear order in
|\Delta\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}|/\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}
. Using, Eq. (
51
), we thus arrive to

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}

\displaystyle=h_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\left(\frac{m_{\mathrm{A}}}{m_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)^{\!3}\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{\!4}

\displaystyle\times\left[P_{2}\left(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})\cdot\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}(\mathcal{T})\right)-\frac{\delta_{\mathrm{A}\mathrm{E}}}{2}(3\cos^{2}\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\cos^{2}\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}-1)\right]\,.

(53)

Let us now focus on tidal displacement raised by the Earth. According to Eq. (
102
) below, we have

\displaystyle P_{2}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{E}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(3\cos^{2}\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\cos^{2}\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}-1)-\frac{3\epsilon_{\mathrm{L}}}{2}\sin 2\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\cos\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}\sin(M_{\mathrm{L}}+\omega_{\mathrm{L}})\,,

(54)

at linear order in
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{\mathrm{L}})
and neglecting
\mathcal{O}(e_{\mathrm{L}})
. In this expression,
\epsilon_{\mathrm{L}}
is the lunar obliquity (
\epsilon_{\mathrm{L}}=0.12\ \mathrm{rad}
),
e_{\mathrm{L}}
is the Moon’s eccentricity (
e_{\mathrm{L}}=0.055
),
M_{\mathrm{L}}
is the mean anomaly of the Moon, and
\omega_{\mathrm{L}}
is its argument of perigee. Because the constant contribution is removed from
P_{2}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}})
in Eq. (
53
), the radial displacement of the lunar crust is varying with a mean synodic month period and an amplitude of the order of

\frac{\Delta\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}\propto\frac{3\epsilon_{\mathrm{L}}h_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}}{2}\left(\frac{m_{\mathrm{E}}}{m_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{a_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{\!3}=4.95\times 10^{-8}\,,

(55)

where
a_{\mathrm{L}}
denotes the semi-major axis of the Moon; we considered
a_{\mathrm{L}}=3.85\times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{km}
and
h_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}=0.03786

[
Folkner et al. 2014
]
. The lunar crust displacement thus amounts to
\pm 8.6\ \mathrm{cm}
. On the other hand, the displacement due to solar tides scales such as

h_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}\left(\frac{m_{\mathrm{S}}}{m_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{a_{\mathrm{E}}}\right)^{\!3}R_{\mathrm{L}}=4.2\ \mathrm{mm}\,,

(56)

where
a_{\mathrm{E}}
denotes the semi-major axis of the Earth. The tidal displacement by Sun is thus one order of magnitude smaller than Earth’s so it will be systematically neglected. Hereafter, the influence of the tidal displacement of the lunar surface in the time difference
[\tau-\mathcal{T}]_{(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})}
will be further investigated through the centrifugal potential and lunar gravity potential.

For later convenience, we shall make use of the ecliptic J2000.0 spatial vector basis, that we shall denote by
(\bm{e}_{x},\bm{e}_{y},\bm{e}_{z})
. This basis, such as the (equator) LCRS basis
(\bm{e}_{X},\bm{e}_{Y},\bm{e}_{Z})
, is non-rotating. The
x
-axis is directed toward the vernal equinox of J2000.0, the
z
-axis points toward the celestial pole of the ecliptic J2000.0, and the
y
-axis completes the right-handed triad. The unit-vectors
(\bm{e}_{x},\bm{e}_{y},\bm{e}_{z})
and
(\bm{e}_{X},\bm{e}_{Y},\bm{e}_{Z})
are related by a rotation of angle
\epsilon
about the direction of the vernal equinox J2000.0:

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{x}

\displaystyle=\bm{e}_{X}\,,

(57a)

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{y}

\displaystyle=\bm{e}_{Y}\cos\epsilon+\bm{e}_{Z}\sin\epsilon\,,

(57b)

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{z}

\displaystyle=-\bm{e}_{Y}\sin\epsilon+\bm{e}_{Z}\cos\epsilon\,,

(57c)

where
\epsilon
is the obliquity of the J2000.0 ecliptic,
\epsilon=23^{\circ}26^{\prime}

[
Petit and Luzum 2010
]
.

The orientation of the lunar principal axis frame is modelled according to the empirical laws of Cassini, namely neglecting the lunar physical librations that shall not exceed
10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad}

[
Newhall and Williams 1996
]
(see also
Kopeikin and Kaplan 2024
for similar approximation) and would only be responsible for introducing periodic terms within the time difference computation. The empirical laws of Cassini state that
[
Eckhardt 1981
]
:

1.

The Moon rotates uniformly about its polar axis
\bm{e}_{C}
with a rotational period equal to
2\pi/n_{\mathrm{L}}
, with
n_{\mathrm{L}}
the mean sideral period of its orbit about the Earth.

2.

The descending node of the lunar equator on the ecliptic precesses in coincidence with
\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}
, the ascending node of the lunar orbit on the ecliptic.

3.

The inclination of the lunar equator on the ecliptic is constant; it is
I_{\mathrm{L}}=0.027\ \mathrm{rad}
.

The second laws implies that the Moon polar axis
\bm{e}_{C}
, its orbit normal, and the celestial pole of the ecliptic
\bm{e}_{z}
are coplanar. Furthermore, in order to maintain a locally-minimum gravitational energy such as is required for stability, the longest axis of the Moon, which coincides with the lowest moment of inertia, have to point Earthward. Among the two unit-vectors that lie within the lunar equator, it is thus
\bm{e}_{A}
which points Earthward whereas
\bm{e}_{B}=\bm{e}_{C}\times\bm{e}_{A}
. The orientation of the principal axes frame with respect to the ecliptic J2000.0, is described by the three following Euler angles:
\phi
,
\theta
, and
\psi
;
\phi
is the longitude of the descending node of the lunar equator on the ecliptic of J2000.0,
\theta
is the inclination of the lunar equator to the ecliptic J2000.0, and
\psi
is the angle between the descending node and
A
-axis. Cassini’s laws thus imply

\phi=\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}\,,\qquad\theta=-I_{\mathrm{L}}\,,\qquad\psi+\phi=L_{\mathrm{L}}+\pi\,,

(58)

where
L_{\mathrm{L}}
is the mean longitude of the Moon. It follows that

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{A}

\displaystyle=\bm{e}_{x}\big[\cos I_{\mathrm{L}}\sin\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}\sin(M_{\mathrm{L}}+\omega_{\mathrm{L}})-\cos\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}\cos(M_{\mathrm{L}}+\omega_{\mathrm{L}})\big]

\displaystyle-\bm{e}_{y}\big[\cos I_{\mathrm{L}}\cos\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}\sin(M_{\mathrm{L}}+\omega_{\mathrm{L}})+\sin\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}\cos(M_{\mathrm{L}}+\omega_{\mathrm{L}})\big]

\displaystyle+\bm{e}_{z}\sin I_{\mathrm{L}}\sin(M_{\mathrm{L}}+\omega_{\mathrm{L}})\,,

(59a)

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{B}

\displaystyle=\bm{e}_{x}\big[\cos I_{\mathrm{L}}\sin\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}\cos(M_{\mathrm{L}}+\omega_{\mathrm{L}})+\cos\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}\sin(M_{\mathrm{L}}+\omega_{\mathrm{L}})\big]

\displaystyle-\bm{e}_{y}\big[\cos I_{\mathrm{L}}\cos\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}\cos(M_{\mathrm{L}}+\omega_{\mathrm{L}})-\sin\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}\sin(M_{\mathrm{L}}+\omega_{\mathrm{L}})\big]

\displaystyle+\bm{e}_{z}\sin I_{\mathrm{L}}\cos(M_{\mathrm{L}}+\omega_{\mathrm{L}})\,,

(59b)

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{C}

\displaystyle=-\bm{e}_{x}\sin I_{\mathrm{L}}\sin\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}+\bm{e}_{y}\sin I_{\mathrm{L}}\cos\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}+\bm{e}_{z}\cos I_{\mathrm{L}}\,.

(59c)

Let us recall that because of perturbations from the Sun and other planets, the lunar orbit does a retrograde precession along the ecliptic in a time period of
18.6\ \mathrm{yr}
, and a prograde precession of the perigee in
8.85\ \mathrm{yr}
(cf. e.g.,
Simon et al. 1994
), which corresponds to mean rates of
\langle\dot{\Omega}_{\mathrm{L}}\rangle=-1.07\times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm{rad}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
and
\langle\dot{\omega}_{\mathrm{L}}\rangle=2.25\times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm{rad}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
, respectively. Therefore, the time derivatives of the unit-vectors of the principal axis frame are given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{e}_{A}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{CL}}(\mathcal{T})\times\bm{e}_{A}(\mathcal{T})\,,

(60a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{e}_{B}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{CL}}(\mathcal{T})\times\bm{e}_{B}(\mathcal{T})\,,

(60b)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{e}_{C}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle=\langle\dot{\Omega}_{\mathrm{L}}\rangle\,\bm{e}_{z}\times\bm{e}_{C}(\mathcal{T})\,.

(60c)

where
\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{CL}}
is the angular velocity of the Moon as predicted by Cassini’s laws:

\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{CL}}(\mathcal{T})=n_{\mathrm{L}}\,\bm{e}_{C}(\mathcal{T})+\langle\dot{\omega}_{\mathrm{L}}\rangle\,\bm{e}_{C}(\mathcal{T})+\langle\dot{\Omega}_{\mathrm{L}}\rangle\,\bm{e}_{z}\,.

(61)

A.2
Centrifugal potential

The LCRS velocity of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ is given by [time differentiation of Eq. (
49
)]

\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})=\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}\,\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{CL}}(\mathcal{T})\times\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})+\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{CL}}(\mathcal{T})\times\Delta\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})+\frac{\mathrm{d}\Delta\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}}\,.

(62)

The second and last terms in the right hand side are due to solid tides which are responsible for displacing the crust of the Moon and hence clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’. These two terms are expected to be (at most) of the order of
|\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{CL}}|\Delta\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}
and
n_{\mathrm{L}}\Delta\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}
, respectively. From Eq. (
61
), we see that they are in fact both proportional to
n_{\mathrm{L}}\Delta\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}
due to the lunar spin orbit resonance 1:1. Let us show that the tidal displacements lead to negligible contributions in the centrifugal potential (i.e., the square of the LCRS velocity of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’). Indeed, the first term in the right hand side of Eq. (
62
) is the dominant one; it returns
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{l}}^{2}\sim n_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}R_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}
, that is to say

\left(\frac{n_{\mathrm{L}}R_{\mathrm{L}}}{c}\right)^{\!2}=2.38\times 10^{-16}\,,

(63)

after being inserted into Eq. (
11
). Therefore, the contribution from tidal displacement generated by Earth [Sun’s is smaller than Earth’s, see discussion below Eq. (
55
)] to the relative frequency difference (through the centrifugal potential) is scaling such as

\left(\frac{n_{\mathrm{L}}R_{\mathrm{L}}}{c}\right)^{\!2}\frac{\Delta\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}\sim 10^{-24}\,.

(64)

It can thus safely be neglected. Only the first term in the right hand side of Eq. (
62
) is considered. Then, considering that

\frac{\langle\dot{\omega}_{\mathrm{L}}\rangle}{n_{\mathrm{L}}}=8.46\times 10^{-3}\,,\qquad\frac{\langle\dot{\Omega}_{\mathrm{L}}\rangle}{n_{\mathrm{L}}}=-4.02\times 10^{-3}\,,

(65)

we keep in Eq. (
62
) only first order terms in
\langle\dot{\omega}_{\mathrm{L}}\rangle/n_{\mathrm{L}}
and
\langle\dot{\Omega}_{\mathrm{L}}\rangle/n_{\mathrm{L}}
. With these simplifications, the expression of the centrifugal potential evaluated at clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ reduces to

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{l}}^{2}=n_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}R_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}\cos^{2}\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\Bigg\{1

\displaystyle+\left[\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{2}-1\right]+\frac{2\langle\dot{\omega}_{\mathrm{L}}\rangle}{n_{\mathrm{L}}}\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{2}+\frac{2\langle\dot{\Omega}_{\mathrm{L}}\rangle}{n_{\mathrm{L}}}\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{2}\cos I_{\mathrm{L}}

\displaystyle-\frac{2\langle\dot{\Omega}_{\mathrm{L}}\rangle}{n_{\mathrm{L}}}\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{2}\tan\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\sin I_{\mathrm{L}}\sin\big(M_{\mathrm{L}}+\omega_{\mathrm{L}}+\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}\big)\Bigg\}\,.

(66)

This relation, once inserted into Eq. (
11
), shows indeed that the main contribution from the centrifugal potential is of the order of (
63
) at the lunar equator. This corresponds to a rate of
-20.5\ \mathrm{ps}/\mathrm{day}
in the time difference
[\tau-\mathcal{T}]_{(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})}
. Let us emphasize that this contribution depends on the selenographic latitude of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ through the trigonometric function
\cos^{2}\theta_{\mathrm{l}}
. It is thus null at the lunar poles, namely on the axis of proper rotation as one could expect for a centrifugal potential term.

The second term in the expression of the centrifugal potential depends on the radial position of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’, it thus changes with the lunar topography. However, as shown in Eq. (
138
), we expect

\mathrm{max}\left|\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{2}-1\right|\sim 10^{-2}\,,

(67)

when
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}=R_{\mathrm{topo}}(\theta_{\mathrm{l}},\varphi_{\mathrm{l}})
with
R_{\mathrm{topo}}
the radial topography of the Moon [cf. Eq. (
137
)]. This means that the contribution from the topography is two orders of magnitude smaller than the dominant one which scales such as in Eq. (
63
); it is thus negligible.

Moreover, since terms proportional
\langle\dot{\omega}_{\mathrm{L}}\rangle/n_{\mathrm{L}}
and
\langle\dot{\Omega}_{\mathrm{L}}\rangle/n_{\mathrm{L}}
represent few percent of the dominant one [see Eqs. (
65
)], all the remaining terms in the centrifugal potential can be neglected. Therefore, at the precision we are working, it is sufficient to model the centrifugal potential at the level of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ such as

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{l}}^{2}=n_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}R_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}\cos^{2}\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\,.

(68)

The estimate in Eq. (
63
) shows that the term
\mathcal{V}^{4}/c^{4}
in Eq. (
11
) is completely negligible when evaluated at clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ (15 orders of magnitude off requirement).

A.3
Lunar gravity potential

According to
Soffel et al. 2003
, the lunar gravity potential at clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ is given by the following expression (neglecting
c^{-2}
terms):

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})

\displaystyle=\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{L}}}{|\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})|}\Bigg\{1+\sum_{\ell=2}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{|\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})|}\right)^{\!\ell}

\displaystyle\times P_{\ell m}(\sin\theta_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T}))\left[C^{\mathrm{L}}_{\ell m}(\mathcal{T})\cos(m\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T}))+S^{\mathrm{L}}_{\ell m}(\mathcal{T})\sin(m\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T}))\right]\Bigg\}\,,

(69)

where
C^{\mathrm{L}}_{\ell m}(\mathcal{T})
and
S^{\mathrm{L}}_{\ell m}(\mathcal{T})
are the lunar Stokes coefficients. The time dependency of Stokes coefficients is due to tides raised on the Moon by bodies in the solar system (mainly Earth and Sun) and to lunar angular velocity variations. The latter will be neglected according to discussion and assumptions in
A.2
. However, we consider time variations due to tides raised by body
\mathrm{A}
on lunar degree 2 coefficients. Then, neglecting the non-elastic response of the Moon to tidal perturbations, we can write (cf. e.g.,
Petit and Luzum 2010
)

\displaystyle C^{\mathrm{L}}_{2m}(\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle=C^{\mathrm{L}}_{2m}+k_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}(2-\delta_{0m})\frac{(2-m)!}{(2+m)!}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\left(\frac{m_{\mathrm{A}}}{m_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left[\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}(\mathcal{T})}\right]^{3}P_{2m}(\sin\theta_{\mathrm{A}}(\mathcal{T}))\cos(m\varphi_{\mathrm{A}}(\mathcal{T}))\,,

(70a)

\displaystyle S^{\mathrm{L}}_{2m}(\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle=S^{\mathrm{L}}_{\ell m}+k_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}(2-\delta_{0m})\frac{(2-m)!}{(2+m)!}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\left(\frac{m_{\mathrm{A}}}{m_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left[\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}(\mathcal{T})}\right]^{3}\,P_{2m}(\sin\theta_{\mathrm{A}}(\mathcal{T}))\sin(m\varphi_{\mathrm{A}}(\mathcal{T}))\,,

(70b)

where
k_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}
is the degree 2 potential Love number of the Moon,
C^{\mathrm{L}}_{2m}
and
S^{\mathrm{L}}_{2m}
(without time dependency) denotes the rigid part of the lunar potential coefficients, and
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{A}}(\mathcal{T})=(\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}(\mathcal{T}),\theta_{\mathrm{A}}(\mathcal{T}),\varphi_{\mathrm{A}}(\mathcal{T}))
are the selenographic coordinates of tides raising body
\mathrm{A}
.

The Moon being highly distorted Earthward, it is convenient to separate in Eq. (
69
) the dominant degree 2 from higher order terms, namely
\ell\geqslant 3
. The lunar gravity potential thus reads as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})=\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{L}}}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}\,\Bigg\{1

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}-1\right)-\frac{\Delta\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}\right)^{\!2}+\frac{J_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}}{2}\!\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}\right)^{\!3}

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}\right)^{\!3}\left[3\left(C_{22}^{\mathrm{L}}\cos 2\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}+S_{22}^{\mathrm{L}}\sin 2\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}\right)\cos^{2}\theta_{\mathrm{l}}-\frac{3J_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}}{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{\ell=3}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}\right)^{\!\ell+1}P_{\ell m}(\sin\theta_{\mathrm{l}})\left[C^{\mathrm{L}}_{\ell m}\cos(m\varphi_{\mathrm{l}})+S^{\mathrm{L}}_{\ell m}\sin(m\varphi_{\mathrm{l}})\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\sum_{m=0}^{2}k_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}(2-\delta_{0m})\left(\frac{m_{\mathrm{A}}}{m_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}\right)^{\!3}\left[\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}(\mathcal{T})}\right]^{3}\frac{(2-m)!}{(2+m)!}

\displaystyle\times P_{2m}(\sin\theta_{\mathrm{l}})P_{2m}(\sin\theta_{\mathrm{A}}(\mathcal{T}))\cos\big[m(\varphi_{\mathrm{A}}(\mathcal{T})-\varphi_{\mathrm{l}})\big]\Bigg\}\,,

(71)

where we included
\Delta\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}
, the effect of the radial displacement of the lunar crust caused by Earth and Sun tides [see Eqs. (
52
)]. We neglected longitudinal and latitudinal displacements by considering that
\theta_{\mathrm{l}}
and
\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}
are constants.

Let us now discuss each contribution in Eq. (
71
) in turn, namely
\ell=0
(i.e., the monopole), the topography (i.e., the second term in curly brackets), the tidal displacement of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ (i.e., the third term in curly brackets),
\ell=2
,
\ell\geqslant 3
, and finally the degree 2 tidal perturbations of the lunar gravity potential.

A.3.1
Monopole contribution.

The monopole term in
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}
is constant in time, we thus expect it to contribute at the level of

\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{L}}}{c^{2}R_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)=3.14\times 10^{-11}\,,

(72)

after being inserted into Eq. (
11
). This corresponds to a rate of about
-2.71\textnormal{\penalty\ $\mu$s}/\mathrm{day}
in the time difference
[\tau-\mathcal{T}]_{(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})}
.

As we shall see below, the monople term in
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}
is the dominant contribution in the scalar potential
\mathcal{W}
. We conclude that the term
\mathcal{W}^{2}/c^{4}
in Eq. (
11
) is thus negligible (it is in fact 6 orders of magnitude smaller than our
10^{-16}
limit). Furthermore, considering that
\mathcal{W}
is five orders of magnitude larger than
\mathcal{V}^{2}
for a clock on the lunar surface, the term
\mathcal{V}^{2}\mathcal{W}^{2}/c^{4}
in Eq. (
11
) is negligible too, being at the level of
10^{-27}
.

A.3.2
Altitude contribution.

Let us now investigate the contribution from the topography of the Moon, namely the second term in Eq. (
71
). This term depends on the location of the clock on the Moon through the ratio
R_{\mathrm{L}}/\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}
. Indeed, for a clock at rest on the lunar surface, we have
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}=R_{\mathrm{topo}}(\varphi_{\mathrm{l}},\theta_{\mathrm{l}})
, where

Tidal displacement contribution.

This oscillating contribution is negligible.

A.3.6
Tidal contribution.

As mentioned earlier, the selenographic coordinates of the Earth are almost constant in time due to spin orbit resonance 1:1 and small lunar obliquity. Hence, the tidal perturbation by Earth, through the variation of the lunar gravity potential coefficients, will induce a constant contribution to the relative frequency difference at the level of

k_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{E}}}{c^{2}R_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{a_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{\!3}=5.63\times 10^{-18}\,,

(79)

once plugged into Eq. (
11
); the numerical value of
k_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}
is
0.024

[
Folkner et al. 2014
]
. This contribution can therefore be neglected in view of our
10^{-16}
threshold. Concerning the Sun, its selenographic coordinates are not constant as for the case of the Earth. Therefore, when plugging the tidal perturbation by Sun into Eq. (
11
), oscillating signature is expected in the relative frequency difference with an amplitude of the order of

k_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{S}}}{c^{2}n_{\mathrm{L}}R_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{a_{\mathrm{E}}}\right)^{\!3}=1.19\times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{ps}\,,

(80)

and a period which is equal to the synodic period of the Moon
(n_{\mathrm{L}}-n_{\mathrm{E}})
, that we approximated to
n_{\mathrm{L}}
. This oscillating contribution being below the picosecond, it can safely be neglected. Therefore, both Earth and Sun tidal distortions of the lunar potential can be omitted.

A.3.7
Summary.

To sufficient approximation, the expression of the contribution of the total lunar gravity potential to
[\tau-\mathcal{T}]_{(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})}
is eventually given by

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{T}_{0}}^{\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{l}}}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle\,\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})=-\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{L}}}{c^{2}R_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\Bigg\{1+\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}-1\right)+\frac{J_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}}{2}

\displaystyle+3\left(C_{22}^{\mathrm{L}}\cos 2\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}+S_{22}^{\mathrm{L}}\sin 2\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}\right)\cos^{2}\theta_{\mathrm{l}}-\frac{3J_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}}{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{\mathrm{l}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\ell=3}^{\ell_{G}}\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}P_{\ell m}(\sin\theta_{\mathrm{l}})\left[C^{\mathrm{L}}_{\ell m}\cos(m\varphi_{\mathrm{l}})+S^{\mathrm{L}}_{\ell m}\sin(m\varphi_{\mathrm{l}})\right]\Bigg\}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{l}}-\mathcal{T}_{0})\,,

(81)

with
\ell_{G}
=150. In this expression, we omitted the topography contribution in terms proportional to the non-spherical part of the lunar gravity potential.

A.4
Tidal potential

According to
Soffel et al. 2003
, at Newtonian order, the tidal potential is given by the following relationship:

\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tidal}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})=w_{\lnot\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}(t,\bm{x}_{\mathrm{L}}+\bm{\mathcal{X}})-w_{\lnot\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}(t,\bm{x}_{\mathrm{L}})-\delta_{ai}\mathcal{X}^{a}\partial_{i}w_{\lnot\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}(t,\bm{x}_{\mathrm{L}})\,,

(82)

where the arbitrary function of time has been fixed to
w_{\lnot\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}(t,\bm{x}_{\mathrm{L}})
for later convenience. In this expression and below,
\bm{x}_{\mathrm{A}}
, the BCRS position of a body
\mathrm{A}
, must be understood as its BCRS trajectory, namely
\bm{x}_{\mathrm{A}}=\bm{x}_{\mathrm{A}}(t)
. In Eq. (
82
), because
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tidal}}
is already multiplied by
c^{-2}
in Eq. (
11
), the 4D relations which are used to pass from BCRS space-time coordinates to LCRS’s, are limited to the Newtonian terms only, that is to say

t=\mathcal{T}\,,\qquad\bm{x}=\bm{x}_{\mathrm{L}}+\bm{\mathcal{X}}\,.

(83)

The (Newtonian) external gravity potential to the Moon is given by

w_{\lnot\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}(t,\bm{x})=G\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\int_{\mathrm{A}}\frac{\rho(t,\bm{x}_{\mathrm{A}}+\bm{X}^{\prime})}{|\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{A}}-\bm{X}^{\prime}|}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}X^{\prime}\,,

(84)

with
\bm{X}^{\prime}
the position vector of the source point taken from the center-of-mass of body
\mathrm{A}
. Typically,
\bm{X}^{\prime}
is spanning a volume enclosing body
\mathrm{A}
. When the external potential is evaluated at the lunar center-of-mass, namely
\bm{x}=\bm{x}_{\mathrm{L}}
, the distance
|\bm{x}_{\mathrm{L}}-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{A}}|
(with
\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}
) is much larger than
|\bm{X}^{\prime}|
, it is thus appropriate to expand the denominator in a Taylor series. Hence, the expression of the Moon external potential reduces to

w_{\lnot\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}(t,\bm{x})=G\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}I_{\mathrm{A}}^{\langle K\rangle}(t)\,\partial_{K}|\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{A}}|^{-1}\,,

(85)

where
I_{\mathrm{A}}^{\langle K\rangle}
denotes the (Newtonian) STF mass multipole moment of body
\mathrm{A}
:

I_{\mathrm{A}}^{\langle L\rangle}(t)=\int_{\mathrm{A}}\rho(t,\bm{x})(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{A}})^{\langle L\rangle}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,.

(86)

Expanding, in Eq. (
82
), the external potential
w_{\lnot\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}(t,\bm{x}_{\mathrm{L}}+\bm{\mathcal{X}})
in Taylor series around
\bm{\mathcal{X}}=0
, and then inserting Eq. (
85
) evaluated at
w_{\lnot\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}(t,\bm{x}_{\mathrm{L}})
, leads to the following expression:

\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tidal}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})=\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tidal}}^{\mathrm{M}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})+\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tidal}}^{\mathrm{NM}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})\,,

(87)

where the tides by the monopole and non-spherical components of body
\mathrm{A}
gravity fields are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tidal}}^{\mathrm{M}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})

\displaystyle=G\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}m_{\mathrm{A}}\sum_{\ell=2}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\ell!}\mathcal{X}^{\langle L\rangle}\partial_{L}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}^{-1}(\mathcal{T})\,,

(88a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tidal}}^{\mathrm{NM}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})

\displaystyle=G\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\sum_{\ell=2}^{+\infty}\sum_{k=2}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\ell!k!}\mathcal{X}^{\langle L\rangle}I_{\mathrm{A}}^{\langle K\rangle}(\mathcal{T})\,\partial_{LK}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}^{-1}(\mathcal{T})\,,

(88b)

respectively. In these expressions,
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}=|\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{A}}|=|\bm{x}_{\mathrm{A}}-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{L}}|
and
\partial_{LK}
is the
(\ell+k)
-th partial derivative relative to components of
\bm{x}_{\mathrm{A}}
, namely

\partial_{LK}\equiv\frac{\partial^{\ell+k}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{A}}^{i_{1}}\ldots\partial x_{\mathrm{A}}^{i_{\ell}}\partial x_{\mathrm{A}}^{j_{1}}\ldots\partial x_{\mathrm{A}}^{j_{k}}}\,.

(89)

We thus infer

\partial_{L}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}^{-1}=(-1)^{\ell}(2\ell-1)!!\,\cfrac{(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{A}})_{\langle L\rangle}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\ell+1}}\,,

(90)

where
\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}=\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{A}}/\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}
.

To derive estimates of the non-spherical components in Eq. (
88b
), let us assume that solar system bodies are axi-symmetric about their polar axis
\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}}
(with
\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}}=1
). According to
Poisson and Will 2014
, the mass multipole moment of body
\mathrm{A}
is thus given by

I_{\mathrm{A}}^{\langle L\rangle}=-m_{\mathrm{A}}R_{\mathrm{A}}^{\ell}J_{\ell}^{\mathrm{A}}s_{\mathrm{A}}^{\langle L\rangle}\,,

(91)

for
\ell\geqslant 2
. In this expression,
J_{\ell}^{\mathrm{A}}
is the mass quadrupole moment of body
\mathrm{A}
and
R_{\mathrm{A}}
is its radius. After inserting it in Eqs. (
88
) and making use of the following STF identities (cf. e.g.,
Hartmann et al. 1994
and
Poisson and Will 2014
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{\langle L\rangle}(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{A}})_{\langle L\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell!}{(2\ell-1)!!}P_{\ell}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}})\,,

(92a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{L}s_{\mathrm{A}}^{K}(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{A}})_{\langle LK\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\ell+k)!}{(2\ell+2k-1)!!}\frac{P_{\ell+k}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}})}{P_{\ell}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}})^{2}-1}

\displaystyle\times\Big\{\big[P_{\ell}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}})P_{\ell+k}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}})-P_{k}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}})\big]P_{\ell}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}})

\displaystyle+\big[P_{\ell}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}})P_{k}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}})-P_{\ell+k}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}})\big]P_{\ell}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}})\Big\}\,,

(92b)

we eventually obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tidal}}^{\mathrm{M}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{A}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\sum_{\ell=2}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)^{\!\ell}P_{\ell}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}})\,,

(93a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tidal}}^{\mathrm{NM}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{A}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\sum_{\ell=2}^{+\infty}\sum_{k=2}^{+\infty}(-1)^{k+1}\frac{(\ell+k)!}{\ell!k!}\,J_{k}^{\mathrm{A}}\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)^{\!\ell}\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{A}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)^{\!k}\frac{P_{\ell+k}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}})}{P_{\ell}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}})^{2}-1}

\displaystyle\times\Big\{\big[P_{\ell}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}})P_{\ell+k}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}})-P_{k}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}})\big]P_{\ell}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}})

\displaystyle+\big[P_{\ell}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}})P_{k}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}})-P_{\ell+k}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}})\big]P_{\ell}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}})\Big\}\,,

(93b)

where
\mathcal{R}=|\bm{\mathcal{X}}|
and
\bm{\mathcal{N}}=\bm{\mathcal{X}}/\mathcal{R}
. In these expressions,
P_{\ell}
denotes the Legendre polynomial of degree
\ell
.

Now let us apply Eq. (
93b
) at the position of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ considering the main term only (i.e.,
\ell=2
and
k=2
). We thus find that the tides raised by the non-spherical component of body
\mathrm{A}
gravity field at the level of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ scales such as

\frac{1}{c^{2}}\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tidal}}^{\mathrm{NM}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})\propto-\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}J_{2}^{\mathrm{A}}\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{A}}}{c^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)^{\!2}\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{A}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)^{\!2}\,.

(94)

For tides raised by the Earth’s and Sun’s quadrupole moments, we find

J_{2}^{\mathrm{E}}\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{E}}}{c^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{E}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{E}}}\right)^{\!2}\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{E}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{E}}}\right)^{\!2}\sim 10^{-23}\,,\qquad J_{2}^{\mathrm{S}}\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{S}}}{c^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{S}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{S}}}\right)^{\!2}\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{S}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{S}}}\right)^{\!2}\sim 10^{-30}\,,

(95)

considering numerical values:
R_{\mathrm{E}}=6\,378\,\mathrm{km}
and
R_{\mathrm{S}}=6.96\times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{km}
for radii, and
J_{2}^{\mathrm{E}}=1.08\times 10^{-3}
and
J_{2}^{\mathrm{S}}=2.11\times 10^{-7}
for quadrupoles (see e.g.,
Folkner et al. 2014
). We conclude that
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tidal}}^{\mathrm{NM}}
is completely negligible.

Let us now focus on the monopole component from external bodies, namely
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tidal}}^{\mathrm{M}}
. According to Eq. (
93a
) this term scales such as

\frac{1}{c^{2}}\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tidal}}^{\mathrm{M}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})\propto\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\sum_{\ell=2}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{A}}}{c^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)^{\!\ell}\,,

(96)

at the level of clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’. For Earth, the quadrupole and hexapole terms (i.e.,
\ell=2
and
\ell=3
) are of the order of

\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{E}}}{c^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{E}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{E}}}\right)^{\!2}=2.37\times 10^{-16}\,,\qquad\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{E}}}{c^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{E}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{E}}}\right)^{\!3}=1.07\times 10^{-18}\,,

(97)

while for Sun, the quadrupole term scales such as

\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{S}}}{c^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{S}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{S}}}\right)^{\!2}=1.32\times 10^{-18}\,.

(98)

Therefore, for what concerns us only the Earth’s quadrupole tidal potential shall be kept, so that to sufficient accuracy, the tidal potential eventually reads as

\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tidal}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})=\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{E}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{E}}}\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{E}}}\right)^{\!2}\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{\!2}P_{2}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{E}})\,.

(99)

Within the lunar principal axis frame,
\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{E}}
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{E}}=

\displaystyle+\bm{e}_{A}\big[\cos\epsilon_{\mathrm{L}}\sin(L_{\mathrm{L}}-\Omega_{\mathrm{L}})\sin(f_{\mathrm{L}}+\omega_{\mathrm{L}})+\cos(L_{\mathrm{L}}-\Omega_{\mathrm{L}})\cos(f_{\mathrm{L}}+\omega_{\mathrm{L}})\big]

\displaystyle+\bm{e}_{B}\big[\cos\epsilon_{\mathrm{L}}\cos(L_{\mathrm{L}}-\Omega_{\mathrm{L}})\sin(f_{\mathrm{L}}+\omega_{\mathrm{L}})-\sin(L_{\mathrm{L}}-\Omega_{\mathrm{L}})\cos(f_{\mathrm{L}}+\omega_{\mathrm{L}})\big]

\displaystyle-\bm{e}_{C}\sin\epsilon_{\mathrm{L}}\sin(f_{\mathrm{L}}+\omega_{\mathrm{L}})\,,

(100)

where
f_{\mathrm{L}}
is the true anomaly of the lunar orbit and where
\epsilon_{\mathrm{L}}
is the lunar obliquity. Given that the Moon orbit is tilted by
\iota_{\mathrm{L}}=0.089\ \mathrm{rad}
relative to the ecliptic and considering, according to Cassini’s laws, that the Moon equator is inclined by
I_{\mathrm{L}}=0.026\ \mathrm{rad}
with respect to the ecliptic, we infer that
\epsilon_{\mathrm{L}}=\iota_{\mathrm{L}}+I_{\mathrm{L}}
, namely
\epsilon_{\mathrm{L}}=0.12\ \mathrm{rad}
.

It is convenient to develop the Legendre polynomial in Eq. (
99
) in a Fourier series of
M_{\mathrm{L}}
, the mean longitude of the Moon. This is achieved after expressing the lunar true anomaly in term of the mean anomaly with the following series expansion

\displaystyle\cos f_{\mathrm{L}}

\displaystyle=-e_{\mathrm{L}}+\frac{2(1-e_{\mathrm{L}}^{2})}{e_{\mathrm{L}}}\sum_{s=1}^{+\infty}\mathrm{J}_{s}(se_{\mathrm{L}})\cos(sM_{\mathrm{L}})\,,

(101a)

\displaystyle\sin f_{\mathrm{L}}

\displaystyle=(1-e_{\mathrm{L}}^{2})^{-1/2}\sum_{s=1}^{+\infty}[\mathrm{J}_{s-1}(se_{\mathrm{L}})-\mathrm{J}_{s+1}(se_{\mathrm{L}})]\sin(sM_{\mathrm{L}})\,,

(101b)

where
\mathrm{J}_{s}(x)
denotes the Bessel function of first kind with
s
-index and
e_{\mathrm{L}}
refers to the eccentricity of the lunar orbit. Only first order in
e_{\mathrm{L}}
is considered in the following discussion since
e_{\mathrm{L}}=0.055
. In addition, we already mentioned that the lunar obliquity is a small angle so only first order in
\epsilon_{\mathrm{L}}
is also considered. Couplings terms of order
\epsilon_{\mathrm{L}}e_{\mathrm{L}}
are neglected for the rest of the discussion. With these simplifications, we find the following expression:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{a_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{E}}}\right)^{\!3}P_{2}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{E}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(3\cos^{2}\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\cos^{2}\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}-1)(1+3e_{\mathrm{L}}\cos M_{\mathrm{L}})

\displaystyle-\frac{3\epsilon_{\mathrm{L}}}{2}\sin 2\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\cos\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}\sin(M_{\mathrm{L}}+\omega_{\mathrm{L}})+3e_{\mathrm{L}}\cos^{2}\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\sin 2\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}\sin M_{\mathrm{L}}\,.

(102)

After plugging it into the expression of the tidal potential and then integrating with respect to time, one gets (i) a term linear in time and (ii) periodic variations (at the lunar orbital frequency about the Earth) in the expression of the time difference
[\tau-\mathcal{T}]_{(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})}
. The term linear in timescales such as

\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{E}}}{c^{2}a_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{a_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{\!2}=2.37\times 10^{-16}\,,

(103)

which represents
7.48\ \mathrm{ns}/\mathrm{yr}
and must thus be considered in the modeling. The periodic variations have amplitudes scaling such as

\epsilon_{\mathrm{L}}\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{E}}}{c^{2}n_{\mathrm{L}}a_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{a_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{\!2}=10.7\ \mathrm{ps}\,,\qquad e_{\mathrm{L}}\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{E}}}{c^{2}n_{\mathrm{L}}a_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{a_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{\!2}=4.90\ \mathrm{ps}\,

(104)

and can then be neglected.

Therefore, to sufficient accuracy, we have the following expression for the tidal potential:

-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{T}_{0}}^{\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{l}}}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\,\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tidal}}(\mathcal{T}^{\prime},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{E}}}{c^{2}a_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{a_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{\!2}(3\cos^{2}\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\cos^{2}\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}-1)(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{l}}-\mathcal{T}_{0})\,.

(105)

It is represented in Fig.
11
. It clearly shows two bulges separated by
180^{\circ}
in longitude, with a maximum effect with an amplitude around
-2.5\times 10^{-16}
at locations
(\theta_{\mathrm{l}},\varphi_{\mathrm{l}})=(0^{\circ},0^{\circ})
and
(0^{\circ},180^{\circ})
.

) from the permanent tides raised on the Moon by the Earth. The

corresponds to null time rate. The origin of the longitude represents lunar prime meridian.

A.5
Negligible potentials

All the terms in Eqs. (
8
) and (
9
) that have not been discussed yet are actually negligible when relative time keeping is required at the level of
10^{-16}
in a cislunar environment. This is what we intent to demonstrate in this section. We have already showed that some of the terms proportional to
c^{-4}
are expected to not contribute in Eq. (
11
) when the observer is at rest on the surface of the Moon (i.e., clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’), and hence, it shall currently reduce to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau_{\mathrm{l}}-\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2c^{2}}\left[\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{l}}^{2}+2\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})\right]+\frac{4}{c^{4}}\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{W}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})\,.

(106)

Let us recall that
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}
and
\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{l}}
refer to the trajectory of the clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’, namely
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}=\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})
and
\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{l}}=\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{l}}(\mathcal{T})
.

We have not discussed the scalar inertial potential
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{iner}}
to date; it shows up at
\mathcal{O}(c^{-2})
. According to
Soffel et al. 2003
, it reads such as

\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{iner}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})=\bm{\mathcal{Q}}(\mathcal{T})\cdot\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}\,,

(107)

where
\bm{\mathcal{Q}}
represents the non-geodesic acceleration of the Moon. At the Newtonian level, the
a
-th component of the non-geodesic acceleration can be written such as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{a}(\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle=G\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\frac{m_{\mathrm{A}}}{m_{\mathrm{L}}}\sum_{\ell=2}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{\ell!}I_{\mathrm{L}}^{\langle L\rangle}(\mathcal{T})\,\partial_{aL}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}^{-1}(\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle+G\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\sum_{\ell=2}^{+\infty}\sum_{k=2}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{\ell!k!}\frac{I_{\mathrm{L}}^{\langle L\rangle}(\mathcal{T})\,I_{\mathrm{A}}^{\langle K\rangle}(\mathcal{T})}{m_{\mathrm{L}}}\partial_{aLK}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}^{-1}(\mathcal{T})\,.

(108)

The dominant term, is the first one in the right-hand side. It corresponds to the couplings of the Moon non-spherical potential to the monopole of external bodies. The second term represents the couplings between non-spherical potentials of the Moon and of external bodies. Let us focus on the first term considering the quadrupole moment of the Moon only. In this condition, the non-inertial potential scales such as

\frac{1}{c^{2}}\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{iner}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})\propto J_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{A}}}{c^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)^{\!3}\,.

(109)

The Earth and Sun contributions are of the order of

J_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{E}}}{c^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{E}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{E}}}\right)^{\!3}=2.18\times 10^{-22}\,,\qquad J_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{S}}}{c^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{S}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{S}}}\right)^{\!3}=3.11\times 10^{-35}\,;

(110)

both are therefore negligible.

Let us now discuss the vector potential contribution in Eq. (
106
), where
\bm{\mathcal{W}}
is given by Eq. (
9
). The vector potential is a combination of three terms whose amplitudes are discussed in turn hereafter. The lunar vector potential
\bm{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}
is given by

\bm{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})=\frac{G}{2}\frac{\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{L}}(\mathcal{T})\times\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}^{3}}\,,

(111)

where
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{L}}
is the spin of Moon, that we approximate by

\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{L}}(\mathcal{T})=\frac{2}{5}\,m_{\mathrm{L}}R_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}\,\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{CL}}(\mathcal{T})\,.

(112)

By making use of Eq. (
61
), we end up with

\frac{4}{c^{4}}\,\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{L}}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})=\frac{4}{5}\left(\frac{n_{\mathrm{L}}R_{\mathrm{L}}}{c}\right)^{\!2}\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{L}}}{c^{2}R_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}\right)\cos^{2}\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\,,

(113)

which scales such as

\left(\frac{n_{\mathrm{L}}R_{\mathrm{L}}}{c}\right)^{\!2}\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{L}}}{c^{2}R_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)=7.47\times 10^{-27}\,.

(114)

and can be neglected.

Then, let us discuss the tidal vector potential. The dominant part of the tidal vector potential can be obtained from
Damour et al. 1991
;
Damour et al. 1992
; we infer the following estimates

\frac{1}{c^{4}}\,\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{tidal}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})\propto\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{A}}}{c^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)\left(\frac{\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{A}}\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{l}}}{c^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)^{\!2}\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{\!2}\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{l}}\,,

(115)

where
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{A}}
is the norm of the LCRS velocity of body
\mathrm{A}
: at the Newtonian level, it is given by
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{A}}=|\bm{v}_{\mathrm{A}}-\bm{v}_{\mathrm{L}}|
, where
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{A}}
and
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{L}}
are the BCRS velocities of body
\mathrm{A}
and Moon, respectively. For the Earth contribution to the vector tidal potential, we find

\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{E}}}{c^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{E}}}\right)\left(\frac{\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{E}}\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{l}}}{c^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{E}}}\right)^{\!2}=1.21\times 10^{-29}\,,

(116)

and for the Sun’s

\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{S}}}{c^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{S}}}\right)\left(\frac{\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{S}}\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{l}}}{c^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{S}}}\right)^{\!2}=1.97\times 10^{-30}\,;

(117)

both contributions are negligible in view of our threshold.

Now let us examine the effect of the inertial vector potential
\bm{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{iner}}
. According to
Soffel et al. 2003
it is given by

\bm{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{iner}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})=-\frac{c^{2}}{4}\,\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{iner}}(\mathcal{T})\times\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}\,,

(118)

which shows from Eq. (
106
), that it contributes to the relative frequency difference as follows:

\frac{4}{c^{4}}\,\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{iner}}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}})=-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\,\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{iner}}(\mathcal{T})\cdot(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}\times\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{l}})\,.

(119)

The angular velocity
\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{iner}}
is a combination of the geodetic, Lense-Thirring, and Thomas precessions:

\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{iner}}=\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{GP}}+\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{LTP}}+\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{TP}}\,.

(120)

At leading order, these are given by

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{GP}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2c^{2}}\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{A}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}^{2}}\right)(3\bm{v}_{\mathrm{L}}-4\bm{v}_{\mathrm{A}})\times\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\,,

(121a)

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{LTP}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\left(\frac{G\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{A}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}^{3}}\right)\Big[3(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}})\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}-\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}}\Big]\,,

(121b)

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{TP}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{3J_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}}{4c^{2}}\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{A}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)^{\!2}\frac{P_{3}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{L}})}{(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{L}})^{2}-1}

\displaystyle\times\Big\{\big[(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{L}})P_{3}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{L}})-P_{2}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{L}})\big]\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}

\displaystyle+\big[(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{L}})P_{2}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{L}})-P_{3}(\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{\!\mathrm{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{\mathrm{L}})\big]\bm{s}_{\mathrm{L}}\Big\}\times\bm{v}_{\mathrm{L}}\,,

(121c)

where
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{A}}
and
\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}}
are respectively the magnitude and direction of
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{A}}
, namely
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{A}}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{A}}\,\bm{s}_{\mathrm{A}}
. In the last expression, the non-geodesic acceleration of the Moon was expressed, at lowest order (i.e., considering the quadrupole moment of the Moon only). Let us emphasize that part of the Lense-Thirring effect (term proportional to
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{A}}
) was inserted into the geodetic precession for conciseness.

According to expression (
119
), we deduce that the geodetic precession contribution to the relative frequency difference scales such as

-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\,\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{GP}}\cdot(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}\times\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{l}})\propto\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\left(\frac{n_{\mathrm{L}}R_{\mathrm{L}}}{c}\right)\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{A}}}{c^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)\left(\frac{|3\bm{v}_{\mathrm{L}}-4\bm{v}_{\mathrm{A}}|}{c}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\,.

(122)

For Earth and Sun contributions, we find
-7.72\times 10^{-26}
and
5.46\times 10^{-25}
, respectively. So the geodetic precession can be omitted considering the
10^{-16}
threshold.

The Lense-Thirring effect enters the time modeling according to Eq. (
119
), that is to say

-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\,\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{LTP}}\cdot(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}\times\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{l}})\propto 2\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\left(\frac{n_{\mathrm{L}}R_{\mathrm{L}}}{c}\right)\left(\frac{G\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{A}}}{c^{3}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\,.

(123)

For Earth and Sun contributions, we find
8.23\times 10^{-30}
and
3.76\times 10^{-30}
, respectively. For the numerical estimate of
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{S}}
we considered
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{S}}=1.909\times 10^{41}\ \mathrm{kg}\,\mathrm{m}^{2}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}

[
Pijpers 1998
]
. Both contributions are negligible.

Finally, let us discuss the Thomas precession. It scales such as

-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\,\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{TP}}\cdot(\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{l}}\times\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{l}})\propto\frac{3J_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}}{4}\sum_{\mathrm{A}\neq\mathrm{L}}\left(\frac{v_{\mathrm{L}}}{c}\right)\left(\frac{n_{\mathrm{L}}R_{\mathrm{L}}}{c}\right)\left(\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{A}}}{c^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)^{\!3}\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{l}}}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\,.

(124)

Then, Earth and Sun contributions are
3.43\times 10^{-34}
and
4.96\times 10^{-39}
, respectively. These contributions are perfectly negligible; they are respectively eighteen and twenty-three orders of magnitude smaller than requirement.

Appendix B
Shape of the selenoid

Let
\bar{\Phi}
be the time average of the equipotential surface representing the sum of the centrifugal potential plus the scalar potential
\mathcal{W}
[cf. Eq. (
16
)]

\bar{\Phi}(\bm{\mathcal{X}})=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}\left[\frac{\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{T})^{2}}{2}+\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{T},\bm{\mathcal{X}})\right]\,,

(125)

where
\tau
represents a characteristic time associated to short period of the motion. For the Moon it is the synodic month period. According to previous computations (see
A
), we obtain the following relation:

\displaystyle\bar{\Phi}(\mathcal{R},\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle=\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}}\Bigg\{1+\frac{J_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}}{2}\!\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}}\right)^{\!2}+\frac{n_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}\mathcal{R}^{3}}{2\,Gm_{\mathrm{L}}}

\displaystyle-\left[\frac{3J_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}}{2}\!\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}}\right)^{\!2}+\frac{n_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}\mathcal{R}^{3}}{2\,Gm_{\mathrm{L}}}\right]\sin^{2}\theta+3\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}}\right)^{\!2}\left(C_{22}^{\mathrm{L}}\cos 2\varphi+S_{22}^{\mathrm{L}}\sin 2\varphi\right)\cos^{2}\theta

\displaystyle+\sum_{\ell=3}^{\ell_{G}}\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathcal{R}}\right)^{\!\ell}P_{\ell m}(\sin\theta)\left[C_{\ell m}^{\mathrm{L}}\cos(m\varphi)+S_{\ell m}^{\mathrm{L}}\sin(m\varphi)\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{m_{\mathrm{E}}}{m_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}}{a_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{\!3}(3\cos^{2}\theta_{\mathrm{l}}\cos^{2}\varphi_{\mathrm{l}}-1)\Bigg\}\,.

(126)

Let us define
\bar{\Phi}_{\mathrm{sel}}
, the numerical value of the (mean) potential of the selenoid, as the equipotential
\bar{\Phi}
evaluated on the equatorial radius and at the intersection between the lunar prime meridian and lunar equator, that is to say at the spatial point of coordinates
\bm{\mathcal{X}}=(R_{\mathrm{L}},0^{\circ},0^{\circ})
. Let us emphasize that this choice is purely arbitrary—a common definition should result from a globally admitted convention instead. We thus have
\bar{\Phi}_{\mathrm{sel}}=\bar{\Phi}(R_{\mathrm{L}},0^{\circ},0^{\circ})
, namely

\displaystyle\bar{\Phi}_{\mathrm{sel}}=\frac{Gm_{\mathrm{L}}}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}\Bigg\{1

\displaystyle+3C_{22}^{\mathrm{L}}+\frac{J_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}}{2}+\frac{n_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}R_{\mathrm{L}}^{3}}{2\,Gm_{\mathrm{L}}}+\frac{m_{\mathrm{E}}}{m_{\mathrm{L}}}\!\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{a_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{\!3}+\sum_{\ell=3}^{\ell_{G}}\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}C_{\ell m}^{\mathrm{L}}P_{\ell m}(0)\Bigg\}\,,

(127)

with numerical value
\bar{\Phi}_{\mathrm{sel}}=2.82\times 10^{6}\ \mathrm{m}^{2}/\mathrm{s}^{2}
. From
\bar{\Phi}_{\mathrm{sel}}
, we can now proceed and solve for
R_{\mathrm{sel}}
, the shape of the selenoid, from the following relation:

\bar{\Phi}(R_{\mathrm{sel}},\theta,\varphi)-\bar{\Phi}_{\mathrm{sel}}=0\,.

(128)

This equation can be solved numerically, up to the desired precision, using for instance a simple Newton-Raphson algorithm with
R_{\mathrm{sel}}=R_{\mathrm{L}}
as first iteration. Instead, hereafter, we provide an approximate algebraic expression.

To this end, let us first recall that the numerical values of the Moon gravity coefficients are

J_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}=2.03\times 10^{-4}\,,\qquad C_{22}^{\mathrm{L}}=2.24\times 10^{-5}\,,\qquad S_{22}^{\mathrm{L}}=5.86\times 10^{-10}\,,

(129)

and (using
GRGM1200A
modeling up to
\ell_{G}=150
)

\sum_{\ell=3}^{\ell_{G}}\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}C_{\ell m}^{\mathrm{L}}P_{\ell m}(0)=-6.06\times 10^{-7}\,.

(130)

Moreover, we also have the following numerical estimates:

\frac{n_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}R_{\mathrm{L}}^{3}}{Gm_{\mathrm{L}}}=7.59\times 10^{-6}\,,\qquad\frac{m_{\mathrm{E}}}{m_{\mathrm{L}}}\!\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{a_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{\!3}=7.54\times 10^{-6}\,.

(131)

These suggest, as a good first approximation, to not consider
S_{22}^{\mathrm{L}}
and
\ell\geqslant 3
terms while solving for
R_{\mathrm{sel}}
from Eq. (
128
). After some algebra we find the first order expression of
R_{\mathrm{sel}}
; it reads such as

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{sel}}(\theta,\varphi)\simeq R_{\mathrm{L}}\Bigg\{1

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{3J_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}}{2}+\frac{n_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}R_{\mathrm{L}}^{3}}{2\,Gm_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\sin^{2}\theta+3C_{22}^{\mathrm{L}}\left(\cos^{2}\theta\cos 2\varphi-1\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{m_{\mathrm{E}}}{m_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{a_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{\!3}\left(\cos^{2}\theta\cos^{2}\varphi-1\right)\Bigg\}\,.

(132)

In Fig.
12
, we represent the altitude of the selenoid with respect to the equatorial radius of the Moon as function of the latitude and longitude on the Moon.

Let us compute
R_{\mathrm{sel}}
at some specific locations. For
\theta=0
and
\varphi=0
(intersection between lunar prime meridian and equator), we find
R_{\mathrm{sel}}(0,0)=R_{\mathrm{L}}=R_{\mathrm{sel}}(0,\pi)
, as it is expected from the definition
\bar{\Phi}_{\mathrm{sel}}=\bar{\Phi}(R_{\mathrm{L}},0,0)
. For
\theta=0
and
\varphi=\pi/4
, we obtain

R_{\mathrm{sel}}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{4})=R_{\mathrm{L}}\left[1-3C_{22}^{\mathrm{L}}-\frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{m_{\mathrm{E}}}{m_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{a_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{\!3}\right]=R_{\mathrm{sel}}(0,\tfrac{3\pi}{4})\,,

(133)

namely
R_{\mathrm{sel}}(0,\pi/4)<R_{\mathrm{L}}
since
C_{22}^{\mathrm{L}}>0
. For
\theta=0
and
\varphi=\pi/2
, we find

R_{\mathrm{sel}}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})=R_{\mathrm{L}}\left[1-6C_{22}^{\mathrm{L}}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{m_{\mathrm{E}}}{m_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{a_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{\!3}\right]=R_{\mathrm{sel}}(0,\tfrac{3\pi}{2})\,,

(134)

so that we eventually have
R_{\mathrm{sel}}(0,\pi/2)<R_{\mathrm{sel}}(0,\pi/4)<R_{\mathrm{L}}
. Let us now compute
R_{\mathrm{sel}}
at the lunar pole, that is to say for
\theta=\pi/2
and
\varphi=0
(notice that
\varphi
is not defined when
\theta=\pi/2
); we obtain

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{sel}}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},0)

\displaystyle=R_{\mathrm{L}}\Bigg[1-\frac{3J_{2}^{\mathrm{L}}}{2}-\frac{n_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}R_{\mathrm{L}}^{3}}{2\,Gm_{\mathrm{L}}}-3C_{22}^{\mathrm{L}}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{m_{\mathrm{E}}}{m_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{L}}}{a_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)^{\!3}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=R_{\mathrm{sel}}(-\tfrac{\pi}{2},0)\,,

(135)

from which we infer
R_{\mathrm{sel}}(\pi/2,0)<R_{\mathrm{sel}}(0,\pi/4)<R_{\mathrm{L}}
. In order to compare
R_{\mathrm{sel}}(\pi/2,0)
and
R_{\mathrm{sel}}(0,\pi/2)
let us substitute the numerical values for the lunar (and Earth) physical parameters. We find

1-\frac{R_{\mathrm{sel}}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}=1.46\times 10^{-4}\,,\qquad 1-\frac{R_{\mathrm{sel}}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},0)}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}=3.87\times 10^{-4}\,,

(136)

so that
R_{\mathrm{sel}}(\pi/2,0)<R_{\mathrm{sel}}(0,\pi/2)
as it is expected from the direction of the mass quadrupole moment. Numerical estimates in Eq. (
136
) represent deficits of
253\ \mathrm{m}
and
672\ \mathrm{m}
with respect to
R_{\mathrm{L}}
, respectively. In other words, the minimal distance between the surface of the selenoid and the center-of-mass of the Moon occurs at the poles. The largest distance happens at the intersection between the lunar prime meridian and equator, but also at the symmetric position with respect to the center-of-mass of the Moon. Within the equatorial plane, the minimal distances are found at
\pm 90^{\circ}
from the lunar prime meridian (see Fig.
12
).

who considered
GRGM1200A

Appendix C
Lunar topography

The topography of the Moon can be assessed from the data obtained by the Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter (LOLA)
[
Ramos-Izquierdo et al. 2009
;
Smith et al. 2010
]
—an instrument onboard the NASA Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter mission. The lunar topography is conveniently reconstructed using
LOLA’s spherical harmonic coefficients

(A_{\ell m}^{\mathrm{L}},B_{\ell m}^{\mathrm{L}})
distributed in the principal axis frame. The radial position of a point on the surface of the Moon is thus given by
\mathcal{R}=R_{\mathrm{topo}}(\theta,\varphi)
, where
R_{\mathrm{topo}}
represents the radial topography of the Moon and is given by the following partial sum

R_{\mathrm{topo}}(\theta,\varphi)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\ell_{\mathrm{topo}}}\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}P_{\ell m}(\sin\theta)\left[A^{\mathrm{L}}_{\ell m}\cos(m\varphi)+B^{\mathrm{L}}_{\ell m}\sin(m\varphi)\right]\,,

(137)

with
A^{\mathrm{L}}_{\ell m}
and
B^{\mathrm{L}}_{\ell m}
having length dimension and
\ell_{\mathrm{topo}}
being the degree to which the series is stopped. In Fig.
13
, we represent the lunar topography (with
\ell_{\mathrm{topo}}=150
) relative to the selenoid
R_{\mathrm{sel}}
derived in Eq. (
132
). The chosen
\ell_{\mathrm{topo}}
is a compromise between computational time and precision of topographic details of the lunar surface. Roughly speaking, the precision of topographic details goes on like
R_{\mathrm{L}}/\ell_{\mathrm{topo}}
, that is to say
12\ \mathrm{km}
for
\ell_{\mathrm{topo}}=150
.

The maximum of the difference between the equatorial radius
R_{\mathrm{L}}
and the topography
R_{\mathrm{topo}}
is of the order of

\mathrm{max}\left|\frac{R_{\mathrm{topo}}}{R_{\mathrm{L}}}-1\right|\sim 5.2\times 10^{-3}\,.

(138)

Appendix D
Resolution of the light travel time equation

Expression (
25
) being implicit in
t_{\mathrm{l}}
, it is usually solved numerically by an iterative process starting with
t_{\mathrm{l}}^{(0)}=t_{\mathrm{e}}
. After
n
iterations, we shall have
t_{\mathrm{l}}=t_{\mathrm{l}}^{(n)}
. This eventually allows one to determine the position of the receiver at time
t_{\mathrm{l}}
.

Another possibility is to perform manually the iterations and then the series expansion in the right-hand side of (
26
) in order to get a closed-form expression of
t_{\mathrm{l}}
up to the desired order in
c^{-1}
. As an illustration, after three iterations, we find

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{l}}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=t_{\mathrm{e}}+\frac{L_{\mathrm{e}\mathrm{l}}}{c}+\frac{L_{\mathrm{e}\mathrm{l}}}{c^{2}}(\bm{l}_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot\bm{v}_{\mathrm{l}})+\frac{L_{\mathrm{e}\mathrm{l}}}{2c^{3}}\Bigg\{2(\bm{l}_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot\bm{v}_{\mathrm{l}})^{2}+L_{\mathrm{e}\mathrm{l}}(\bm{l}_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot\bm{a}_{\mathrm{l}})

\displaystyle+\frac{L_{\mathrm{e}\mathrm{l}}}{|\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}}|}\Bigg[v_{\mathrm{l}}^{2}-\left(\frac{\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}}\cdot\bm{v}_{\mathrm{l}}}{|\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}}|}\right)^{\!2}\Bigg]+L_{\mathrm{e}\mathrm{l}}\,v_{\mathrm{l}}^{a}v_{\mathrm{l}}^{b}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\Delta}{\partial x_{\mathrm{l}}^{a}\partial x_{\mathrm{l}}^{b}}\right]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}}(t_{\mathrm{e}}),\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}(t_{\mathrm{e}}))}\Bigg\}\,,

(139)

where we used
\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}}=\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}(t_{\mathrm{e}})-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}}(t_{\mathrm{e}})
,
\bm{a}_{\mathrm{l}}=\bm{a}_{\mathrm{l}}(t_{\mathrm{e}})
, and
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{l}}=\bm{v}_{\mathrm{l}}(t_{\mathrm{e}})
with
v_{\mathrm{l}}=|\bm{v}_{\mathrm{l}}|
. The instantaneous distance
L_{\mathrm{e}\mathrm{l}}
is defined by

L_{\mathrm{e}\mathrm{l}}=|\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}}|+\Delta(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}}(t_{\mathrm{e}}),\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}(t_{\mathrm{e}}))\,,

(140)

and
\bm{l}_{\mathrm{l}}
, the tangent vector to the ray at position
\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}(t_{\mathrm{e}})
, is given by (see
Le Poncin-Lafitte et al. 2004
and
Bourgoin 2020
)

\bm{l}_{\mathrm{l}}=\frac{\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}}}{|\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}}|}+\left[\frac{\partial\Delta}{\partial\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}}\right]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}}(t_{\mathrm{e}}),\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}(t_{\mathrm{e}}))}\,.

(141)

In most applications, we have
|\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}}|\gg\Delta(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}}(t_{\mathrm{e}}),\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}(t_{\mathrm{e}}))
. As an illustrative example, for the one-way transfer which is considered in this paper,
|\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}}|=3.85\times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m}
while the Shapiro and atmospheric delays are of the order of
7.50\ \mathrm{m}
and around
10\ \mathrm{m}
, respectively. Therefore, it is convenient to apply another series expansion in
\Delta/|\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}}|
around
\Delta/|\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}}|=0
. At zeroth-order, we obtain the following simplified expression (see also
Petit and Wolf 1994
):

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{l}}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=t_{\mathrm{e}}+\frac{|\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}}|}{c}+\frac{\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}}\cdot\bm{v}_{\mathrm{l}}}{c^{2}}+\frac{|\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}}|}{2c^{3}}\Bigg[v_{\mathrm{l}}^{2}+\left(\frac{\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}}\cdot\bm{v}_{\mathrm{l}}}{|\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}}|}\right)^{\!2}+\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}}\cdot\bm{a}_{\mathrm{l}}\Bigg]\,.

(142)

This approximate time of reception can be used eventually to estimate, in the right-hand side of Eq. (
26
), the position of the receiver
\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}=\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}(t_{\mathrm{l}})
with
t_{\mathrm{l}}=t_{\mathrm{l}}^{(3)}
.

Appendix E
Demonstration of range covariance using either TCB or TDB

When relying on TDB (labelled
t^{**}
) rather than TCB (labelled
t
), the light travel time in Eq. (
24
) can be decomposed such as

[t_{\mathrm{l}}-t_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}})}=[t^{**}-t]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}})}+[t^{**}_{\mathrm{l}}-t^{**}_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(t^{**}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}^{**}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}^{**}_{\mathrm{l}})}-[t^{**}-t]_{(t_{\mathrm{l}})}\,,

(143)

where
[t^{**}_{\mathrm{l}}-t^{**}_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(t^{**}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}^{**}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}^{**}_{\mathrm{l}})}
is now the light travel time expressed in TDB. The function
[t^{**}-t]_{(t)}
appearing in Eq. (
143
) represents the difference between TDB and TCG and is given by the right-hand side of Eq. (
29
).

According to the general covariance principle, making use, in Eq. (
24
), of either the left-hand side or the right-hand side of (
143
), shall not affect the proper time difference
\tau_{\mathrm{l}}-\tau_{\mathrm{e}}
. Let us thus demonstrate that the right-hand side of Eq. (
143
) do indeed reduce to the left-hand side.

On one side, according to the definition in Eq. (
29
), we have

[t^{**}-t]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}})}-[t^{**}-t]_{(t_{\mathrm{l}})}=L_{B}(t_{\mathrm{l}}-t_{\mathrm{e}})\,.

(144)

On the other side, the expression for the light travel time expressed in TDB reads

[t^{**}_{\mathrm{l}}-t^{**}_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(t^{**}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}^{**}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}^{**}_{\mathrm{l}})}=\frac{|\bm{x}^{**}_{\mathrm{l}}-\bm{x}^{**}_{\mathrm{e}}|}{c}+\frac{\Delta^{**}(t^{**}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}^{**}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}^{**}_{\mathrm{l}})}{c}\,.

(145)

As discussed in
Klioner 2008
, in order for the light travel time expression to be the same in Eqs. (
26
) and (
145
), the TDB-compatible distances and mass parameters have to be rescaled by the same factor than the one used to rescale TDB in Eq. (
29
), namely
t^{**}=(1-L_{B})t+t^{**}_{0}
, and hence,
\bm{x}^{**}=(1-L_{B})\bm{x}
and
\Delta^{**}=(1-L_{B})\Delta
. Inserting these two last relations into the right-hand side of Eq. (
145
), we find

[t^{**}_{\mathrm{l}}-t^{**}_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(t^{**}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}^{**}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}^{**}_{\mathrm{l}})}=(1-L_{B})(t_{\mathrm{l}}-t_{\mathrm{e}})\,,

(146)

after invoking Eqs. (
26
) and then (
25
). Equation (
143
) is finally recovered by adding together Eqs. (
146
) and (
144
), and then making use of (
25
), which achieves the demonstration.

Appendix F
Demonstration of range covariance using either BCRS or GCRS as intermediate system

In order to show that the proper time difference
\tau_{\mathrm{l}}-\tau_{\mathrm{e}}
is independent of the intermediate system being used for the light travel time computation, we shall demonstrate the equality in Eq. (
34
). Following
Kopeikin and Kaplan 2024
, let us start by rewriting the first term in the left-hand side of Eq. (
34
) as

[\mathcal{T}-T]_{(T_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{l}})}=[\mathcal{T}-t]_{(t_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}})}-[T-t]_{(t_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}})}\,,

(147)

where
[\mathcal{T}-t]_{(t,\bm{x})}
is given by Eq. (
28
) and
[T-t]_{(t,\bm{x})}
by Eq. (
27
). Equation (
34
), thus reduces to

[T_{\mathrm{l}}-T_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{l}})}-[t_{\mathrm{l}}-t_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}})}=[T-t]_{(t_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}})}-[T-t]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}})}\,.

(148)

The equality between the left-hand side—i.e., the difference between the light travel times between clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ and clock ‘
\mathrm{e}
’ in GCRS and BCRS—and the right-hand side—i.e., the difference between the time transformation from TCB to TCG at clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ and at clock ‘
\mathrm{e}
’—can be easily demonstrated at the order
c^{-2}
.

As a matter of fact, the time transfer function expressed in TCG and at second order in
c^{-2}
is given as follows (see
D
for more details):

[T_{\mathrm{l}}-T_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{l}})}=\frac{|\bm{X}_{\mathrm{l}}-\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}}|}{c}+\frac{\bm{V}_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot(\bm{X}_{\mathrm{l}}-\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}})}{c^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(c^{-3})\,,

(149)

where all quantities in the right-hand side are expressed at
T_{\mathrm{e}}
. Let us emphasize that we neglected the delay function for simplicity. Then, according to resolution B1.3 of IAU 2000
[
Soffel et al. 2003
]
, the geocentric and barycentric coordinates of a given space-time event are related, up to second order terms in
c^{-2}
, by

\displaystyle\bm{X}(T)

\displaystyle=\bm{x}(t)-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{E}}(t)+\mathcal{O}(c^{-2})\,,

(150a)

\displaystyle\bm{V}(T)

\displaystyle=\bm{v}(t)-\bm{v}_{\mathrm{E}}(t)+\mathcal{O}(c^{-2})\,.

(150b)

Applying these to Eq. (
149
), we thus deduce

[T_{\mathrm{l}}-T_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(T_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{X}_{\mathrm{l}})}-[t_{\mathrm{l}}-t_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}})}=-\frac{\bm{v}_{\mathrm{E}}\cdot(\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}})}{c^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(c^{-3})\,,

(151)

where
[t_{\mathrm{l}}-t_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}})}
is given, at second order in
c^{-1}
, by

[t_{\mathrm{l}}-t_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}})}=\frac{|\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}}|}{c}+\frac{\bm{v}_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot(\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}})}{c^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(c^{-3})\,,

(152)

where all quantities in the right-hand sides of Eqs. (
151
) and (
152
) are expressed at
t_{\mathrm{e}}
.

Let us now focus on the right-hand side of Eq. (
148
). According to Eq. (
27
), the difference between TCG and TCB at clock ‘
\mathrm{l}
’ is
given by

[T-t]_{(t_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}})}=-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\left\{\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{\mathrm{l}}}\left[\frac{v^{2}_{\mathrm{E}}(t)}{2}+w_{\lnot\mathrm{E}}(\bm{x}_{\mathrm{E}}(t))\right]\mathrm{d}t+\bm{v}_{\mathrm{E}}(t_{\mathrm{l}})\cdot[\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}(t_{\mathrm{l}})-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{E}}(t_{\mathrm{l}})]\right\}+\mathcal{O}(c^{-4})\,.

(153)

Substituting for
t_{\mathrm{l}}
from
t_{\mathrm{l}}=t_{\mathrm{e}}+[t_{\mathrm{l}}-t_{\mathrm{e}}]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}})}
into (
153
), while making use of (
152
), shall return

[T-t]_{(t_{\mathrm{l}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}})}-[T-t]_{(t_{\mathrm{e}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{e}})}=-\frac{\bm{v}_{\mathrm{E}}\cdot(\bm{x}_{\mathrm{l}}-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{E}})}{c^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(c^{-3})\,,

(154)

after Taylor expanding the expression around
t_{\mathrm{l}}=t_{\mathrm{e}}
and after keeping only terms of order
c^{-2}
.

The equality between Eqs. (
151
) and (
154
) verifies the correctness of Eq. (
148
), which achieves the demonstration.
