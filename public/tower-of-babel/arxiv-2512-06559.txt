Tight Universal Bounds for Partially Presorted Pareto Front and Convex Hull
Ivor van der Hoog, Eva Rotenberg, Daniel Rutschmann

Abstract
TimSort is a well-established sorting algorithm whose running time depends on how sorted the input already is. Recently, Eppstein, Goodrich, Illickan, and To designed algorithms inspired by TimSort for Pareto front, planar convex hull, and two other problems. For each of these problems, they define a Range Partition Entropy; a function H H mapping lists I I that store n n points to a number between 0 0 and log ⁡ n \log n . Their algorithms have, for each list of points I I , a running time of O ⁡ ( n ⁡ ( 1 + H ⁡ ( I ) ) ) O(n(1+H(I))) . In this paper, we provide matching lower bounds for the Pareto front and convex hull algorithms by Eppstein, Goodrich, Illickan, and To. In particular, we show that their algorithm does not correspond to TimSort (or related stack-based MergeSort variants) but rather to a variant of QuickSort. From this, we derive an intuitive notion of universal optimality. We show comparison-based lower bounds that prove that the algorithms by Eppstein, Goodrich, Illickan and To are universally optimal under this notion of universal optimality.

In

, whose running time depends on
n
and the output size
k
;

•

order-oblivious algorithms
1
, which fix the input
values
and consider for an algorithm the worst-case running time over all input lists that contain these values;

•

sorting algorithms that adapt to how presorted the input is

,

Entropy.

A powerful tool for proving better-than-worst-case running times is the use of
entropy functions
.
Formally, an entropy function takes as input a list
I
of
n
values and outputs a number between
0
and
\log n
.
A classical example arises in the analysis of TimSort.
For the
sorting
problem, the input is a list
I
of values.
We define an
ordered partition

\Pi=(I_{1},I_{2},\ldots)
as a partition of
I
into
runs
, where each run
I_{i}
is a contiguous sublist of
I
whose values are either non-decreasing or non-increasing.
This induces the
TimSort entropy

\hfill H_{t}(I):=\min_{\text{ordered partitions }\Pi\text{ of }I}\;\sum_{I_{i}\in\Pi}\frac{|I_{i}|}{n}\log\frac{n}{|I_{i}|}.\hfill

It can be shown that TimSort runs in
O(n(1+H_{t}(I)))
time
2
.
There are many stack-based MergeSort algorithms that achieve the same running time, such as the algorithms by Munro and Wild
16
,
Jugé
12
,
Gelling, Nebel, Smith, and Wild
7
,
Buss and Knop
4
,
Takaoka
19
,
and Barbay and Navarro
3
.
More recently, Schou and Wang introduced
PersiSort

17
, which also achieves this running time.

Afshani, Barbay, and Chan
1
study better-than-worst-case performance in a geometric setting.
They revisit the output-sensitive planar convex hull algorithm of Kirkpatrick, Seidel, and McQueen
13
.
Given a planar point set
P
in general position, presented as a list
I
, one may define the output-sensitive entropy

H_{o}(I)
as
\log k
, where
k
is the number of points on the convex hull of
P
.
Kirkpatrick, Seidel, and McQueen show that their algorithm runs in
O(n(1+H_{o}(I)))
time.
Afshani, Barbay, and Chan refine this running time by introducing the
structural entropy
of
P
.
A
structural partition
is a list

\Pi=((I_{1},R_{1}),(I_{2},R_{2}),\ldots)

of pairs
(I_{i},R_{i})
, where
I_{i}
is an arbitrary subset of
I
and
R_{i}
is a triangle containing
I_{i}
such that
(i) the sets
I_{1},I_{2},\ldots
form a partition of
I
, and
(ii) each triangle
R_{i}
is contained in the closure of the convex hull of
P
.
This induces the
structural entropy

1

\hfill H_{s}(I):=\min_{\text{structural partitions }\Pi\text{ of }I}\sum_{(I_{i},R_{i})\in\Pi}\frac{|I_{i}|}{n}\log\frac{n}{|I_{i}|}.\hfill

Note that, although we define
H_{s}(I)
based on the input list
I
, its value depends only on
P
.
Structural entropy captures the intrinsic difficulty of computing the convex hull of
P
in a manner that is more fine-grained than output sensitivity.
Consider, for example, partitioning the unit circle into
\sqrt{n}
arcs.
For each arc, construct a triangle whose vertices lie on that arc, and place three points of
P
at the vertices.
For one of these triangles, place the remaining
\Theta(n)
points of
P
arbitrarily inside it.
For any list
I
containing
P
, the output-sensitive analysis gives the running time
O(n\log n)
for the algorithm of
13
, whereas the structural-entropy analysis yields a much smaller bound:

\hfill H_{s}(I)\leq 1\cdot\log 2\;+\;\sum_{i=1}^{\sqrt{n}-1}\left(\frac{3}{n}\log n\right)\;\Rightarrow\;O(n(1+H_{s}(I)))\subseteq O(n).\hfill

Inspired by these two entropy measures, Eppstein, Goodrich, Illickan, and To
6

define entropy functions for a range of geometric problems, including
Pareto front, planar convex hull, 3D convex hull, the lower envelope of simple polygonal
chains, and the visibility polygon of a point inside a convex polygon with convex holes.
Although their entropy definitions differ from problem to problem, they share a common
structure.
We illustrate their entropy function for constructing the planar convex hull of a size-
n
point set
P
given as a list
I
.

Let
I
be a list storing
P
.
A
respectful partition
is a list

\Pi=((I_{1},R_{1}),\ldots)

of pairs
(I_{i},R_{i})
, where
I_{i}
is a subsequence of
I
and
R_{i}
is a triangle containing
I_{i}
such that
(i) the sets
I_{1},I_{2},\ldots
form a partition of
I
,
(ii) either
I_{i}
is sorted (e.g., by
x
-coordinate) or
R_{i}
is contained in the convex hull of
P
, and
(iii) if
R_{i}
is not contained in the convex hull of
P
(so
I_{i}
is sorted), then
R_{i}
is disjoint from all other triangles
R_{j}
.
This induces the
range partition entropy

6

\hfill H(I):=\min_{\text{respectful partitions }\Pi\text{ of }I}\;\sum_{(I_{i},R_{i})\in\Pi}\frac{|I_{i}|}{n}\log\frac{n}{|I_{i}|}.\hfill

Eppstein, Goodrich, Illickan, and To adapt the algorithm of
13

to compute the convex hull of
P
in

O(n(1+H(I)))
time.
Every structural partition is also a respectful partition, and hence for all inputs
I
we have

H(I)\leq H_{s}(I)
.
These two quantities can be separated: for example, if all points of
P
lie on the convex hull but
I
lists all points of
P
by increasing
x
-coordinate, then
H(I)=0
, and their algorithm uses linear time whilst
1
uses
O(n\log n)
.

Universal optimality.

Entropy functions are appealing because they allow us to upper-bound the running time of an algorithm in a more fine-grained manner.
Their true value, however, emerges when we can also provide matching lower bounds.
A variety of frameworks have been developed for proving such better-than-worst-case lower bounds. Recently
Haeupler, Wajc, and Zuzic
[
10
, STOC’21]

introduced
universal optimality
as an umbrella notion for these analyses, which has gained substantial traction
24
,
25
,
10
,
9
,
8
,
11
,
23
,
22
,
20
.

Consider an algorithmic problem where
\mathcal{U}_{n}
denotes the universe of all possible inputs of size
n
.
The usual worst-case running time of an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is

\max_{I\in\mathcal{U}_{n}}\texttt{Runtime}(\mathcal{A},I)
.
The core idea is to consider smaller universes

U_{1},U_{2},\ldots
whose union covers
\mathcal{U}_{n}
.
For a fixed universe
U_{j}
and algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, its
universal running time
is the worst-case running time over all inputs in
U_{j}
.
The universal lower bound is the best possible universal running time:

\texttt{Universal}(\mathcal{A},U_{j}):=\max_{I\in U_{j}}\texttt{Runtime}(\mathcal{A},I)\text{ and }\texttt{Universal-LB}(U_{j}):=\min_{\mathcal{A}}\texttt{Universal}(U_{j},\mathcal{A})

An algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is
universally optimal
if for all
U_{j}
,
\texttt{Universal}(\mathcal{A},U_{j})\in O(\texttt{Universal-LB}(U_{j}))
.

As a concrete example, the notion of optimality for planar convex hull construction
introduced by Afshani, Barbay, and Chan
1
fits naturally into this framework.
For each size-
n
planar point set
P
, define the universe
U_{P}
to consist of all lists
I
that store
P
.
Clearly, the union of all such universes
U_{P}
covers the universe of all size-
n
inputs for planar convex hull.
For a fixed point set
P
, the universal running time is the worst-case running time of an algorithm over all possible orders in which the points of
P
may be presented.
Afshani, Barbay, and Chan prove that the algorithm of
13
is universally optimal under this interpretation.

For sorting, one can define a universe
U
by fixing a sequence of sizes
(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots)
that together sum up to
n
and assigning to it all inputs
I
for which there exists an ordered partition
\Pi
of
I
where each part
I_{i}\in\Pi
has size
s_{i}
.
The better-than-worst-case optimality of TimSort corresponds to universal optimality under this interpretation.

Contribution.

This paper revisits the analyses of Eppstein, Goodrich, Illickan, and To
6
for Pareto front and planar convex hulls.
Consider the family
\mathcal{I}_{n}
of size-
n
lists in which all points lie on the top-right arc of a circle.
For inputs
I\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
, computing the front or hull reduces to sorting
I
by
x
-coordinate.
We observe that, when the input is restricted to
\mathcal{I}_{n}
, the range-partition entropy
H(I)
is incomparable to the bounds from TimSort.

We further observe that the sorting algorithm implicitly induced by the framework of
6
is not a MergeSort variant but rather a deterministic variant of QuickSort, in which a recursive call terminates whenever its input is already sorted.
We then derive a natural notion of universal optimality that related to the mechanisms of this algorithm, and we prove that the QuickSort variant induced by
6
is universally optimal under this interpretation.

At present, two universal lower bounds are known for planar convex hulls
1
,
20
; both works define a universe by fixing a size-
n
point set
P
and assigning to it all lists
I
containing
P
.
Afshani, Barbay, and Chan
1
restrict the model of computation to algebraic decision trees in which test functions have constant complexity.
Within this model, they show, for each point set
P
, a universal lower bound matching the universal running time of
13
.
Van der Hoog, Rotenberg, and Rutschmann
20
give a different argument: instead of restricting the computational model, they restrict the
output
.
They require the output to consist of the convex hull in cyclic order and, for every index
i
with
I[i]
not on the hull, a
witness
—a triple
(a,b,c)
such that
I[i]
lies inside the triangle
\Delta(I[a],I[b],I[c])
.
With these witnesses in place, the output can be linear-time verified, similar to how one can verify sorting.
Under this output assumption, they show that any comparison-based model of computation admits, for each fixed point set
P
, a universal lower bound matching the universal running time of
13
.

In this paper, we follow
20
and require that output consist of the front (or hull) of the input together with, for every non-front (resp., non-hull) point, a corresponding witness.
We define a sufficiently refined universe and prove that the algorithms from
6
are universally optimal.
Perhaps surprisingly, we do not make use of the entropy function from
6
, as we use an alternative method to express the runtime of their algorithm instead.

2
Entropy and Universal Optimality

We begin by formally and thoroughly discussing the notion of universal optimality.
Throughout this discussion, we use the sorting problem as a running example: the input is a list of values
I
, and the goal is to output
I
in sorted order.
We then define the three notions of universal optimality that correspond to the algorithms in
6
for the sorting problem, the Pareto front problem, and the planar convex hull problem, respectively.

Universal optimality considers a family of meaningful universes
U_{j}
, where each universe
U_{j}
is a set of algorithmic inputs of size
n
.
These universes are not required to be disjoint, but their union must cover the set of all inputs.
For a fixed universe
U_{j}
, an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
has
universal running time

\texttt{Universal}(\mathcal{A},U_{j}):=\max_{I\in U_{j}}\texttt{Runtime}(\mathcal{A},I)
.
Each universe
U_{j}
also induces a
universal lower bound
, defined as the minimum universal running time over all algorithms
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
.
An algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is
universally optimal
if, for
every
universe
U_{j}
, the quantity
\texttt{Universal}(\mathcal{A},U_{j})
asymptotically matches the corresponding universal lower bound.

As the universes become smaller, it becomes increasingly difficult to design a universally optimal algorithm.
Intuitively, this is because we must construct a
single
algorithm that, for every universe
U_{j}
, competes against an algorithm that may be tailor-made for
U_{j}
.

Consider the following two examples for the sorting problem.
First, define a single universe
U
containing all lists of size
n
.
Its universal lower bound is
\Omega(n\log n)
, and any
O(n\log n)
-time sorting algorithm is universally optimal under this interpretation.

Next, define for each size-
n
permutation
\pi_{j}
a universe
U_{j}
consisting of all lists
I
such that applying
\pi_{j}
to
I
yields a sorted list.
The family
\{U_{j}\}
clearly covers all inputs, but in this setting
no
universally optimal algorithm can exist.
Indeed, for each
j
, consider a tailor-made algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{j}
that, given an input
I
, checks in linear time whether applying
\pi_{j}
to
I
produces a sorted list; if not,
\mathcal{A}_{j}
spends quadratic time sorting
I
.
Individually, each
\mathcal{A}_{j}
has terrible worst-case performance.
However, for every universe
U_{j}
, the corresponding algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{j}
runs in linear time on
all
inputs in
U_{j}
.
Thus, a sorting algorithm
\mathcal{A}
would be universally optimal under this interpretation if and only if its worst-case running time were linear!

Universal lower bounds.

In a comparison-based model of computation, one can derive a universal lower bound for a fixed universe
U_{j}
as follows.
Let
O_{j}
denote the set of distinct outputs produced on inputs
I\in U_{j}
.
Any algorithm
\mathcal{A}
operating on
U_{j}
induces a decision tree whose leaves correspond to these outputs.
Hence, there exists at least one input
I\in U_{j}
for which the running time of
\mathcal{A}
is in
\Omega\bigl(\log|O_{j}|\bigr)

since it must distinguish among
|O_{j}|
possible outputs.
Thus,
\Omega\bigl(\log|O_{j}|\bigr)
is an information-theoretic lower bound for
U_{j}
.

An entropy, based only on the input values.

The structural entropy of
1
depends only on the point set
P
and not on the order
I
that
P
is received in.
We can create an entropy-measure for sorting that only depends on the input values and not on the input order, if we allow the input list to contain duplicates.
Let
I
be a list storing a multiset
M
.
We can define the
multiset partition

\Pi
of
I
is the partition into maximal parts
I_{1},I_{2},\ldots
of equal values.
We define
multiset entropy

H_{m}(I):=\sum_{I_{i}\in\Pi}\frac{|I_{i}|}{n}\log\!\frac{n}{|I_{i}|}.

To obtain a matching lower bound, we define a universe by a sequence of sizes
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
and assign to
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
all inputs
I
whose multiset partition has
|I_{i}|=s_{i}
for all
i
.
For each fixed
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
, the universal lower bound is

\Omega\!\left(n+\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{i}\log\!\frac{n}{s_{i}}\right)

(the lower bound for multiset sorting
15
).
For any input
I
in the universe of
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
, the multiset entropy is:
H_{m}(I)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{s_{i}}{n}\log\!\frac{n}{s_{i}}
.
It follows that an
O(n(1+H_{m}(I)))
-time algorithm is universally optimal for this choice of universes.

TimSort and QuickSort.

For TimSort, we define a universe by a sequence of sizes
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
summing to
n
, and assign to it all inputs
I
whose sublists
I[s_{1}+\cdots+s_{\ell-1}:s_{1}+\cdots+s_{\ell}]
are each sorted (increasing or decreasing).
For a fixed universe
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
, counting all possible outputs gives a straightforward universal lower bound of
\Omega\!\left(n+\sum_{\ell}s_{\ell}\log(n/s_{\ell})\right)
.
Note that for an input
I
in the universe
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
, its TimSort entropy
H_{t}(I)
may be significantly smaller than
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{s_{i}}{n}\log\!\frac{n}{s_{i}}
(e.g., when
I
is entirely sorted).
However, it may never be asymptotically larger; thus, the TimSort algorithm is universally optimal.

The sorting algorithm implicitly induced by Eppstein, Goodrich, Illickan, and To
6
is a form of deterministic QuickSort: it finds the median in linear time, partitions around it, recurses on both halves, and stops early whenever a recursive input is already sorted.
This produces complementary behavior.
For example, let
I
list all even numbers in increasing order followed by all odd numbers; here TimSort runs in linear time.
Formally, if we fix the universe
(s_{1},s_{2})=(\frac{n}{2},\frac{n}{2})
then the universal lower bound is linear.
Yet, this QuickSort variant takes
O(n\log n)
time.
Conversely, for the list with
I[i]=i
for even
i
and
I[i]=n+i
for odd
i
, TimSort requires
O(n\log n)
time and every universe containing
I
has an
\Omega(n\log n)
lower bound, while the deterministic QuickSort runs in linear time.
Deterministic QuickSort is thus incomparable to the universal bounds related to TimSort.
It matches the multiset sorting lower bounds, but its behavior is considerably more fine-grained.
This illustrates the need for a different, complementary family of universes to establish universal optimality.

2.1
Defining our problems, entropies and universes

We define entropy measures for the QuickSort implied by
6
, and for the Pareto-front and convex-hull algorithms described in
6
, overloading the notation
H
for all three. For each problem, we also define the families of universes used to establish universal optimality.

QuickSort.

Let the input be a list
I
, and let
R=[1,n]\cap\mathbb{Z}
be the list of all ranks.
A
respectful partition

(R_{1},R_{2},\ldots)
of
R
consists of contiguous sublists of
R
such that, for each interval
R_{i}
, the elements of
I
whose ranks lie in
R_{i}
appear in
I
in increasing (or decreasing) order.
For example, if
R_{i}=[2,4]
, then the second smallest element in
I
must appear before the third smallest, which appears before the fourth smallest.

\text{This yields the \emph{quicksort entropy}:}\hfill H(I):=\min_{\text{respectful }\Pi}\sum_{R_{i}\in\Pi}\frac{|R_{i}|}{n}\log\!\frac{n}{|R_{i}|}.\hfill

Since duplicates share a rank,
H(I)
is always at most the multiset entropy.
We define a universe by a sequence
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
with
\sum s_{i}=n
, assigning to it all inputs with a respectful partition
(R_{1},\ldots,R_{k})
with
|R_{i}|=s_{i}
for all
i
.
From these universes, we obtain:

Theorem 1
.

The sorting algorithm
\mathcal{A}
induced by
6
runs in
O(n(1+H(I)))
time and
\exists c>0
such that
\forall(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
,
\texttt{Universal}(\mathcal{A},(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}))\;\leq\;c\cdot\texttt{Universal-LB}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
.

Pareto front.

A point
p
dominates
q
if
p_{x}\geq q_{x}
and
p_{y}\geq q_{y}
.
The
Pareto front
consists of points not dominated by others in linear order.
The input is a list
I
storing a point set
P
in general position.
The output are two lists. The front list contains the Pareto front.
The witness lists contains for each index
i
where
I[i]
is not on the Pareto front an index
j
such that
I[j]
dominates
I[i]
.
A
respectful partition
is a list
\Pi=((I_{1},R_{1}),\ldots)
where each
I_{i}
is a subsequence of
I
, and each
R_{i}
contains
I_{i}
, such that
(i) the
I_{i}
form a partition of
I
,
(ii) either
I_{i}
is sorted (e.g. by
x
) or the top-right corner of
R_{i}
is dominated by a point of
P
, and
(iii) if the top-right corner of
R_{i}
is not dominated, then
R_{i}
is disjoint from all other
R_{j}
.

\text{This yields the entropy~\cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{eppstein_entropy-bounded_2025}{\@@citephrase{[}}{\@@citephrase{]}}}:}\hfill H(I):=\min_{\text{respectful }\Pi}\sum\nolimits_{(I_{i},R_{i})\in\Pi}\frac{|I_{i}|}{n}\log\!\frac{n}{|I_{i}|}.\hfill

As mentioned, we do not use this entropy in our analysis.
However, we do define a universe with similar characteristics to this entropy.
For the Pareto front, we define a universe by a point set
P
and disjoint axis-aligned rectangles
R
; it contains all inputs
I
storing
P
such that, for each
r\in R
, the subsequence
I\cap r
is monotone in
x
or
y
.

Convex hull.

For convex hulls, the input is again a list
I
storing
P
.
The output consists of the hull list containing the convex hull and the witness lists containing for each index
i
where
I[i]
is not on the convex hull three indices
(a,b,c)
such that
I[i]
is contained in the triangle
(I[a],I[b],I[c])
.
A
respectful partition
is a list
\Pi=((I_{1},R_{1}),\ldots)
where each
I_{i}
is a subsequence of
I
, and each
R_{i}
contains
I_{i}
, such that
(i) the
I_{i}
form a partition of
I
,
(ii) either
I_{i}
is sorted (by
x
or
y
) or contained in a triangle
R_{i}
inside the convex hull of
P
, and
(iii) if
R_{i}
is not inside the convex hull, then
R_{i}
is disjoint from the other triangles.

\text{This yields the entropy~\cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{eppstein_entropy-bounded_2025}{\@@citephrase{[}}{\@@citephrase{]}}}: }\hfill H(I):=\min_{\text{respectful }\Pi}\sum\nolimits_{(I_{i},R_{i})\in\Pi}\frac{|I_{i}|}{n}\log\!\frac{n}{|I_{i}|}.\hfill

Again, we do not use this entropy in our analysis but define a universe using similar characteristics.
A universe is defined by fixing
P
and a set of disjoint triangles
R
;
it contains all lists storing
P
such that, for each
r\in R
, the subsequence
I\cap r
is monotone in
x
or
y
.

3
Sorting

If we apply the Pareto front or convex hull algorithms from
6
to inputs that reduce from sorting, it results in the
truncated quicksort
depicted in
\cref
algo:quicksort.

noend 1

truncated-quicksort
(I^{\prime})
.

0:

4
Pareto Front

For any integer
n
, we denote by
\mathbb{P}_{n}
all sets of
n
points in the plane that lie in general position,
that is, no two points have the same x- or y-coordinate and no three points lie on a line.
We denote by
\pf(P)
the set of points that lie on the Pareto front of
P
in their linear order.
We denote by
\mathbb{I}_{P}
all arrays of length
n
that contain
P
in some order.

The Output.

We require the algorithm to output two lists: The
front list

encodes
\pf(P)
, and the
witness list
encodes a witness for every point in
P-\pf(P)
.
More precisely, a
front list

F=(f_{1},\dots,f_{k})
is a sequence of integers such that

I[f_{1}],\dots,I[f_{k}]
are precisely the points in
\pf(P)
, ordered by increasing x-coordinate.
A
witness list

W=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
is a sequence of
n
integers such that
w_{i}=-1
if
I[i]\in\pf(P)
,
and
I[w_{i}]
dominates
I[i]
otherwise.

Sorted Regions.

Eppstein et al.
6
introduce
sorted regions
.
For computing the Pareto front, they define a
region set

R
to be a set
of disjoint axis-aligned rectangles in the plane. Let
P
be a point set. An input
I\in\mathbb{I}_{P}

respects
R
if
for each region
r\in R
, the points in
r\cap P
appear in
I
in increasing or decreasing order, by x-coordinate or y-coordinate.
We define a universe
(P,R)
by fixing a point set
P
and a region set
R
, and we assign to it the set
\mathbb{I}_{P,R}
of all inputs
I\in\mathbb{I}_{P}
that respect
R
.
Then for
(P,R)
, the
universal running time
is defined as

\Universal(\mathcal{A},(P,R))=\max\limits_{I\in\mathbb{I}_{P,R}}\Runtime(\mathcal{A},I).

An algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is
universally optimal

if there is a constant
c>0
such that, for all universes
(P,R)
, and all algorithms
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
,
we have
\Universal(\mathcal{A},P,R)\leq c\cdot\Universal(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},P,R)
.

4.1
Universal Lower Bounds

The information theoretic lower bound states that, if an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, across some family of inputs,
produces
k
distinct outputs, then the maximum running time of
\mathcal{A}
over this family of inputs is
\geq\log k
.
In our setting, the outputs consists of both a front list and a witness list.
We will apply the information theoretic lower bound to each of these separately.

Fix a universe
(P,R)
.
Observe that, for every input
I\in\mathbb{I}_{P,R}
, there is one front list.
We denote by
\mathbb{F}_{P,R}
the set of all front lists across all inputs
I\in\mathbb{I}_{P,R}
, then the information theoretic lower bound is
\log|\mathbb{F}_{P,R}|
.
Witness lists are more complicated. There can be multiple witness lists that are valid for a single input. To obtain a lower bound, we count the number of
inputs
for which a given lists is a witness lists:

Definition 4
.

Fix a universe
(P,R)
and a list
W\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
of
n
integers. We define:

V(P,R,W)=\left\{I\in\mathbb{I}_{P,R}\mid W\text{ is a witness list for }I\right\}\text{ and }V_{\max}(P,R)=\max_{W\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}|V(P,R,W)|.

Theorem 5
.

Fix a universe
(P,R)
. Then, for any algorithm
\mathcal{A}
,

\Universal(\mathcal{A},(P,R))\in\Omega\Big(n+\log|\mathbb{F}_{P,R}|+\log\frac{|\mathbb{I}_{P,R}|}{V_{\max}(P,R)}\Big).

Proof.

Any algorithm needs
\Omega(n)
time to read the input, so
\Universal(\mathcal{A},(P,R))\in\Omega(n)
.
Since every input
I\in\mathbb{I}_{P,R}
has one front list,
the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
needs to output each different front list in
|\mathbb{F}_{P,R}|
at least once
over all inputs in
\mathbb{I}_{P,R}
.
The information theoretic lower bound shows
\Universal(\mathcal{A},(P,R))\in\Omega(\log|\mathbb{F}_{P,R}|)
.
Finally, we consider the witnesses.
A list of integers
W\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is a witness for at most
V_{\max}(P,R)
different inputs in
\mathbb{I}_{P,R}
.
Thus, the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
needs to output at least
\lceil\frac{|\mathbb{I}_{P,R}|}{V_{\max}(P,R)}\rceil

different lists
W
across all inputs in
\mathbb{I}_{P,R}
.
The information theoretic lower bound shows
\Universal(\mathcal{A},(P,R))\in\Omega(\log\frac{|\mathbb{I}_{P,R}|}{V_{\max}(P,R)})
.
∎

4.2
Ordered Normalized Downdrafts

We estimate the quantity
\frac{|\mathbb{I}_{P,R}|}{V_{\max}(P,R)}
through a combinatorial objects that we call ordered normalized downdrafts.
These are technical, but intuitively capture for a fixed universe
(P,R)
all ways a

Downdrafts.

Let
P
be a point set. A

21
is a map
\varphi:P-\pf(P)\to P

such that for each
p\in P-\pf(P)

\varphi(p)
dominates

.

Definition 6
.

Let

be a region set.
A

Formally, it is a map

\kappa:R\to\{\text{``increasing by $x$''},\;\text{``decreasing by $x$''},\;\text{``increasing by $y$''},\;\text{``decreasing by $y$''}\}.

We write

for the set of all compass functions on

Lemma 7
.

Let
P
be a point set,

\kappa\in\CF(R)
.
Then there exists a linear order

on

•

.

under

.

If
r\in R

\kappa
and
p,q\in P\cap r

, then

.

(It does not matter whether

Proof.

Let
G
be the directed graph on the vertex set
P
with edges defined as follows:

•

For any
p,q\in P

dominating

.

•

If
r\in R
is
x
-sorted under

light blue

.

•

If
r\in R
is

An edge is

If, for any

, there is both a red edge

and a blue edge
p\to q
, then we delete the blue edge
and only keep the red edge.
If

is a DAG, then an arbitrary topological ordering of

yields the linear order

.
What remains is to show that

is acyclic.

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that

is not acyclic and let

If both

are red edges, then

contradicting the minimality of

We may hence assume that

is even and that
p_{1}p_{2},p_{3}p_{4},\dots
are red edges,
and
p_{2}p_{3},p_{4}p_{5},\dots
are blue edges.
By minimality of

.
Without loss of generality, suppose that

is a light blue edge. See
\cref

Since
p_{3}
has no incoming red edges, there are no points in the bottom-left quadrant
Q_{1}
centered at
p_{3}
.
Similarly, since
p_{2}
has no outgoing red edges, there are no points in the top-right quadrant
Q_{2}
centered at
p_{2}
.
On
C
, let
p_{i}
be the next point after
p_{3}
with
p_{i}.x<p_{3}.x
. Then
p_{i-1}.x>p_{3}.x
, so
p_{i-1}p_{i}
is a dark blue edge.
But then, the region in
R
that contains
p_{2}p_{3}
and the region in

that contains

intersect.
This contradiction shows that

Let

Theorem 11
.

The running time of
\cref

O\Big(n+\log\frac{|\mathbb{I}_{P,R}|}{\OND_{\max}(P,R)}\Big).

Proof.

By
\cref
lemm:pf_running_time, the running time is bounded by
O\Big(n+\sum\limits_{p\in P}\depth_{Q(P,R)}\left(u(p)\right)\Big)
.

We apply
\cref
lemm:quadrant_tree_ond and obtain:

\sum_{p\in P}\depth_{Q(P,R)}u(p)\leq\log\Big(\frac{n^{n}}{\OND_{\max}(P,R^{\prime})}\cdot\prod_{r\in R^{\prime}}\frac{1}{|r\cap P|!}\Big).

Since
R^{\prime}
is a refinement of

, we have
\OND_{\max}(P,R^{\prime})\geq\OND_{\max}(P,R)
.
We apply an algebraic argument identical to the last step of
\cref

\prod_{r\in R^{\prime}}\frac{1}{|r\cap P|!}\leq 2^{O(n)}\cdot\prod_{r\in R}\frac{1}{|r\cap P|!}.\hfill\text{ By Stirling's approximation, $n^{n}\leq n!\cdot 2^{O(n)}$.
Therefore:}

\sum_{p\in P}\depth_{Q(P,R)}u(p)\leq\log\Big(\frac{1}{\OND_{\max}(P,R)}\cdot\frac{n!}{\prod_{r\in R}|r\cap P|!}\Big)+O(n)

Finally,
\mathbb{I}_{P,R}
contains all inputs for which the points in every region are sorted by increasing x-coordinate, thus

\frac{n!}{\prod\limits_{r\in R}|r\cap P|!}\leq|\mathbb{I}_{P,R}|
which shows the lemma.
∎

What remains is to show that
O\Big(n+\log\frac{|\mathbb{I}_{P,R}|}{\OND_{\max}(P,R)}\Big)
matches
\Omega\Big(n+\log|\mathbb{F}_{P,R}|+\log\frac{|\mathbb{I}_{P,R}|}{V_{\max}(P,R)}\Big)
.

Corollary 13
.

be a point set and

Then

5
Convex Hull

Recall that, for any integer
n
,
\mathbb{P}_{n}
denotes all sets of
n
points in the plane that lie in general position.
For a point set
P
, the convex hull is the smallest convex region that contains
P
.
We denote by
\ch(P)
the set of point in
P
that lie on the convex hull, in cyclic order.
Recall that
\mathbb{I}_{P}
denotes all arrays of length
n
that contain
P
in some order.
Also recall the notions of a region set
R
(which, for convex hulls, are triangles), the set
I_{P,R}
of inputs that respect
R
,
and the definition of universal running time in this setting.

The Output.

We again require the algorithm to output two lists: The
hull list
encodes
\ch(P)
,
and the
witness list
encodes a witness for every point in
P-\ch(P)
.
More precisely, a
hull lists

H=(h_{1},\dots,h_{k})
is a sequence of integers such that
I[h_{1}],\dots,I[h_{k}]
are precisely the points in
\ch(P)
, in cyclic order. A
witness list

W=((a_{1},b_{1},c_{1}),\dots,(a_{n},b_{n},c_{n}))
is a sequence of
n\times 3
integers such that
a_{i}=b_{i}=c_{i}=-1
if
I[i]\in\ch(P)
,
and otherwise,
I[i]
lies inside the triangle
\Delta(I[a_{i}],I[b_{i}],I[c_{i}])
.

Universal Lower Bounds.

Our approach follows a similar approach to Section
4
where we provide a universal lower bound by counting how many inputs fit a specific witness list. Fix a universe
(P,R)
.
For every input
I\in\mathbb{I}_{P,R}
, there is one hull list. We define

V(P,R,W)=\left\{I\in\mathbb{I}_{P,R}\mid W\text{ is a witness list for }I\right\}\text{ and }V_{\max}(P,R)=\max_{W\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times 3}}|V(P,R,W)|.

Theorem 15
.

Fix a universe
(P,R)
. Then, for any convex hull algorithm
\mathcal{A}
,

\Universal(\mathcal{A},(P,R))\in\Omega\Big(n+\log|\mathbb{H}_{P,R}|+\log\frac{|\mathbb{I}_{P,R}|}{V_{\max}(P,R)}\Big).

Proof.

Any algorithm needs
\Omega(n)
time to read the input, so
\Universal(\mathcal{A},(P,R))\in\Omega(n)
.
Since every input
I\in\mathbb{I}_{P,R}
has one hull list,
the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
needs to output each different hull list in
|\mathbb{H}_{P,R}|
at least once
over all inputs in
\mathbb{I}_{P,R}
.
The information theoretic lower bound shows
\Universal(\mathcal{A},(P,R))\in\Omega(\log|\mathbb{H}_{P,R}|)
.
Finally, we consider the witnesses.
A list of integers
W\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times 3}
is a witness for at most
V_{\max}(P,R)
different inputs in
\mathbb{I}_{P,R}
.
Thus, the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
needs to output at least
\lceil\frac{|\mathbb{I}_{P,R}|}{V_{\max}(P,R)}\rceil

different lists
W
across all inputs in
\mathbb{I}_{P,R}
.
The information theoretic lower bound shows
\Universal(\mathcal{A},(P,R))\in\Omega(\log\frac{|\mathbb{I}_{P,R}|}{V_{\max}(P,R)})
.
∎

5.1
Quadrangle Tree and Ordered Downdrafts

For the Pareto front, there was a natural partial order on
P
given by domination.
We then used this partial order to define downdrafts.
Moreover,
\cref
lemm:pareto_linear_order shows that this partial order goes hand-in-hand with regions that are sorted b x- or y-coordinate.
For the convex hull problem, we can use the quadrangle tree

to get a partial order on

.
However, we will need to permute this partial order to make it compatible with how regions are sorted.
In particular, the definition of a downdraft becomes a lot less geometric.

(

(

.

Normalized Downdrafts

Let
P
be a point set,
R
a region set, and
\kappa\in\CF(R)
a compass function. Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a (truncated) quadrangle tree.
A
downdraft
is a map
\varphi:P-\ch(P)\to P
such that for each
p\in P-\ch(P)
, we have
p\prec_{\mathcal{Q},\kappa}\varphi(p)
. A
normalized downdraft
is a downdraft
\psi
such that for all regions
r\in R
and points
p,q\in P\cap r
with
p<_{\mathcal{Q},\kappa}q
, we have
\psi(p)<_{\mathcal{Q},\kappa}\psi(q)
.

Ordered Normalized Downdrafts.

Given any normalized downdraft
\psi
and point
q\in P
, the
fiber

\psi^{-1}(\{q\})
is the set of all points
p\in P
that get mapped to
q
.
We define the
ordered normalized downdraft

\overline{\psi}=(\psi,\prec_{\overline{\psi}})
as a normalized downdraft
\psi
plus
a total order
\prec_{\overline{\psi}}
on each fiber
\psi^{-1}(\{p\})
,
such that for all regions
r\in R
and points
p,q\in P\cap r
with
\psi(p)=\psi(q)
,
we have
p<_{\prec_{\mathcal{Q},\kappa}}q\Leftrightarrow p\prec_{\overline{\psi}}q
.
Similarly to how we count witness sets, we denote by
\OND(P,R,\mathcal{Q},\kappa)
the set of all ordered normalized downdrafts and
\OND_{\max}(P,R,\mathcal{Q})=\max\limits_{\kappa\in\CF(P,R)}|\OND(P,R,\mathcal{Q},\kappa)|
.

5.4
A Generalization of “Sorted by x- or y-coordinate”

In
\cref
sect:pareto, it was it was important that the compass function sorts points by x-coordinate or y-coordinate. Indeed,
\cref
lemm:pareto_linear_order constructs a linear order that both compatible with the domition partial order, and with the compass function.
In contrast to this, the proofs in
\cref
sect:hull work for a wide range of compass functions. Indeed, the permutation
\rho
in
\cref
lemm:quadrangle_order can deal with an arbitrary compass function.
Thus, we may consider a bigger family of compass functions that just “sorted by x- or y-coordinate”.

For
\cref
sect:hull, a family of compass functions really only needs to satisfy three properties:

[(1)]

1.

We need to decide in linear time whether there exists a compass function according to which
I^{\prime}
is sorted.

2.

If
I^{\prime}
is sorted via some compass function, then we need an algorithm that computes the convex hull of
I^{\prime}
in linear time.

3.

The number of distinct compass functions should be
2^{O(n)}
.

Indeed, property (1) is used on line 1 of
\cref
algo:convex_hull, and property (2) is used in line 2 of
\cref
algo:convex_hull. Property (3) is needed for the analysis, since the number of compass functions shows up in
\cref
coro:hull_counting. (Also, if all points in
R
lie on the convex hull, then property (2) implies property (3) via an information theoretic lower bound.)

Generalized Compass Functions

A
generalized compass function

\kappa
assigns every region
r\in R

an order
p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{k}
of the points in
r\cap P
, such that
p_{1}p_{2}\dots p_{k}
is a simple polygonal curve. In other words,
p_{1}p_{2}\dots p_{k}
is a (directed) spanning path of the point set
r\cap P
.
This generalizes the compass functions from
\cref
def:compass: If
p_{1}.x<p_{2}.x<\dots<p_{k}.x
,
then
p_{1}p_{2}\dots p_{k}
is a simple polygonal curve. This also holds for sorting by y-coordinate and/or in decreasing order.

Let us check that this definition satisfies the conditions (1)-(3):

[(1)]

1.

Deciding whether the points in
I^{\prime}
form a simple polygonal curve can be done in
O(|I^{\prime}|)
time via Chazelle’s polygon triangulation algorithm (second application in Section 5 of
5
).

2.

The convex hull of a simple polygonal curve can be computed in linear time via a variant of Graham scan
14
.

3.

Any planar point set of size
k
has
O(86.81^{k})
spanning paths
18
1
1

1

The abstract of
18
only mentions spanning cycles, but at the end of Section 5.1 in
18
, the authors note that their bound also applies to spanning paths.
. Let
c>0
be a constant so that this bound is
\leq c\cdot 86.81^{k}
for every integer
k\geq 0
. Then, for a single region
r\in R
, there are
\leq c\cdot 86.81^{|r\cap P|}
generalized compass functions.
By taking the product over all regions that contain at least one point of
P
, the total number of generalized compass functions is
\leq c^{n}\cdot 86.81^{n}\in 2^{O(n)}
.

6
Concluding remarks

We established universal optimality for the Pareto front and Convex hull algorithms from
6
.
In Section
5.4
we recall that the algorithms in
6
have an early-termination criterion where they terminate if they encounter a sorted subproblem. We briefly remark that the definition of sortedness (by
x
-coordinate,
y
-coordinate, or even saying that
P
is sorted when its sequence defines a simple polyline) does not affect the asymptotic running time and universal optimality.
In
6
, the running times of the algorithms are upper bounded by an entropy function. In this paper, we do not make use of that function, nor do we show that it can be squeezed between our upper and lower bounds. We believe that this should be possible — that these entropy bounds in
6
do not overestimate their running times— and we consider this an interesting open problem. We also note that we do not provide matching lower bounds for their 3D convex hull, lower envelope, or visibility polygon algorithms. After some initial exploration, these appear to behave quite differently mathematically, and it remains unclear whether these algorithms are universally optimal under any natural notion of universes.
