Risk factors for immediate severe pain in patients with thoracolumbar junction fracture treated with percutaneous pedicle screw fixation: a retrospective study of 598 patients
Authors: Kun Wang, Xin Xu, Ang Li, Junlin Han, Ruoxian Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPercutaneous pedicle screw fixation (PPSF) is widely used for thoracolumbar fractures because it is minimally invasive and facilitates early recovery. However, some patients experience marked pain in the ultra-early postoperative period, which may hinder early mobilization and compromise enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS). This study aimed to identify factors associated with immediate severe pain within 0–8 h after PPSF and to evaluate the discriminatory performance of a multivariable model.MethodsWe retrospectively included 598 consecutive patients with thoracolumbar fractures treated with PPSF at our institution from December 2022 to December 2024. Immediate severe pain was defined as a maximum visual analog scale (VAS) score > 7.0 within 0–8 postoperative hours. Demographic, perioperative, and imaging variables were collected. All patients received a standardized baseline postoperative analgesic regimen, and the analyzed VAS score was recorded before administration of additional rescue analgesics. Standardized preoperative pain scores were not routinely available in this retrospective cohort. Time from injury to surgery was prespecified and retained in the multivariable model as a clinically relevant injury-course variable; however, it was not considered a substitute for baseline pain assessment. Variables with P < 0.10 in univariable analyses and clinically important factors were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC); the 95% confidence interval (CI) for AUC was estimated using bootstrap resampling.ResultsOf 598 patients, 244 (40.8%) developed immediate severe pain. In multivariable analysis, older age (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.076 per 1 year, 95% CI 1.052–1.100), higher body mass index (BMI) (aOR 1.071 per 1 kg/m2, 95% CI 1.025–1.119), greater vertebral compression ratio (aOR 1.554 per 0.1, 95% CI 1.313–1.839), and facet joint violation (FJV) (aOR 1.744, 95% CI 1.131–2.691) were associated with higher odds of immediate severe pain, whereas higher injured vertebral Hounsfield units (HU) were inversely associated with the outcome (aOR 0.901 per 10 HU, 95% CI 0.852–0.952). The model showed good discrimination (AUC = 0.839, 95% CI 0.805–0.873).ConclusionIn this PPSF-treated cohort of thoracolumbar junction fractures, immediate severe pain was associated with age, BMI, vertebral compression ratio, and FJV, while higher HU values were inversely associated with the outcome. These factors may support perioperative risk stratification and individualized optimization of screw placement strategy and analgesia pathways; however, the findings should be interpreted with caution given the retrospective design and the potential for residual confounding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-06939-5.Keywords: Percutaneous pedicle screw fixation, Thoracolumbar junction fracture, Immediate severe pain, Logistic regression, Receiver operating characteristic curve

Introduction

The thoracolumbar junction (T10–L2) lies between the relatively rigid thoracic spine and the more mobile lumbar spine, forming a biomechanical transition zone where stress concentrates and fractures are common [1]. Treatment aims to restore/maintain stability, re-establish sagittal alignment, enable timely ambulation, and reduce complications [2]. With the evolution of minimally invasive and image-guided techniques, percutaneous pedicle screw fixation (PPSF) has become widely used for posterior stabilization of thoracolumbar fractures [3]. Compared with open posterior instrumentation, PPSF limits paraspinal muscle dissection and soft-tissue trauma, reduces blood loss and infection risk, and may shorten hospital stay and facilitate functional recovery [4].

As enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways are increasingly adopted in spine surgery, perioperative analgesia and postoperative activity are central to rapid functional restoration [5, 6]. Evidence suggests that earlier postoperative activity is associated with fewer respiratory and thromboembolic complications and a shorter length of stay, whereas delayed activity may increase complication risk [7]. Therefore, pain control affects not only patient-reported experience and satisfaction but also the ability to turn in bed, ambulate, perform respiratory exercises, and progress through rehabilitation. Nonetheless, in clinical practice, some PPSF patients still develop pronounced “ultra-early” pain (0–8 h), delaying first turning and ambulation and potentially triggering a cascade of anxiety, kinesiophobia, and increased analgesic demand.

Severe immediate pain after PPSF is likely multifactorial (e.g., injury severity, bone quality, adequacy of reduction, and fixation stability). Increasing attention has focused on iatrogenic facet injury, particularly cranial facet joint violation (FJV), a common imaging-detected event influenced by entry point, screw-head position, trajectory planning, and anatomical variation [8]. Prior studies have reported a higher risk of cranial FJV with percutaneous versus open techniques [9], whereas a meta-analysis found comparable rates, suggesting that surgeon experience, imaging guidance, and assessment criteria may contribute to inconsistent findings [10]. Importantly, FJV may alter adjacent-segment biomechanics and has been associated with suboptimal postoperative pain improvement and adjacent-segment degeneration/disease-related outcomes [11, 12]. However, evidence specifically addressing severe “immediate” pain within 0–8 h after PPSF remains limited, and large-sample data clarifying the independent role of FJV and other imaging factors are lacking. Accordingly, using a cohort of 598 PPSF patients, we developed a multivariable logistic regression model to identify factors associated with severe immediate postoperative pain and to evaluate model discrimination, with the goal of informing risk stratification, tailored analgesia, and refinement of surgical strategy within this PPSF-treated population.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study consecutively included patients with thoracolumbar fractures who underwent percutaneous pedicle screw fixation (PPSF) at our institution between December 2022 and December 2024. Participants were identified from the electronic medical record system and the picture archiving and communication system (PACS). The study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE statement [13].

At our institution, treatment decisions for thoracolumbar junction fractures are made by the spine trauma team based on clinical presentation and imaging assessment. Posterior PPSF is generally selected when the fracture is considered mechanically unstable and/or when early mobilization is prioritized, such as in cases with marked vertebral body collapse, progressive kyphotic tendency, suspected posterior ligamentous complex injury, or pain that precludes early mobilization despite appropriate analgesia. Non-operative management (e.g., bracing and analgesia with mobilization as tolerated) is typically chosen for fractures judged to be stable without progressive deformity and with acceptable pain control. Accordingly, the PPSF-treated cohort in the present study may represent a clinically selected population with greater instability and/or higher baseline risk of pain than non-operatively managed patients. The present study was therefore designed to identify factors associated with immediate severe postoperative pain within this PPSF-treated cohort, rather than to compare PPSF with other treatment strategies.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) imaging-confirmed thoracolumbar (T10–L2) fracture; (2) treated with posterior PPSF; and (3) complete perioperative data, with complete VAS records within 0–8 h postoperatively.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) severe neurological deficit requiring emergent decompression or other emergency management (e.g., AIS A/B or clinically equivalent severe impairment) that could preclude reliable pain outcome assessment; (2) severe polytrauma, severe traumatic brain injury, impaired consciousness, or other conditions compromising subjective pain assessment; (3) history of prior spine surgery, confirmed chronic pain syndrome, or long-term analgesic use that could substantially alter baseline pain; and (4) missing key variables that could not be retrieved.

Surgical technique

Posterior PPSF was performed under general anesthesia using a standardized minimally invasive approach. All patients were treated via bilateral paracentral (paramedian) stab incisions (i.e., no midline incision was used in this cohort). Pedicle screw insertion and rod placement were performed under C-arm fluoroscopy guidance according to our institutional routine; navigation/robotic assistance was not used in this series. Indirect reduction was routinely attempted using postural reduction/ligamentotaxis, with gentle distraction–compression through the screw–rod construct under fluoroscopic confirmation as needed to restore alignment and vertebral body height. Because this was a retrospective cohort, the presence/extent of additional targeted reduction maneuvers and quantitative reduction quality were not documented in a standardized manner across all cases and were therefore not analyzed.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was severe immediate postoperative pain, defined as a maximum visual analog scale (VAS) score > 7.0 within 0–8 h after surgery (outcome = 1); otherwise, patients were categorized as non-severe immediate pain (outcome = 0). Postoperative pain was evaluated using a 0–10 visual analog scale (VAS). Assessments were routinely performed and documented by the responsible ward nurses as part of standard ward pain monitoring, with additional evaluations as clinically indicated. In this cohort, the severe pain event of interest referred to a sudden, intolerable low back pain episode occurring after the patient returned to the ward despite routine baseline analgesia, typically accompanied by a strong request for additional analgesic medication. The VAS score used for analysis was recorded at that observation point, before the administration of any additional rescue analgesics beyond the standard postoperative analgesic regimen. The highest such VAS value recorded within 0–8 h after surgery was used for analysis [14]. All patients received standardized baseline perioperative analgesia, under which VAS was assessed during the 0–8 h window. VAS reflected overall postoperative pain intensity and was not site-specific; pain localization (fracture-related pain vs surgical wound/soft-tissue pain) was not systematically documented in this retrospective cohort. However, because the study population consisted of thoracolumbar fracture patients treated with posterior PPSF and severe neurological deficit cases were excluded, the recorded pain in the 0–8 h period was considered to predominantly reflect acute postoperative low back pain in the operative/injured region rather than lower-extremity radicular symptoms.

Perioperative analgesic management

To minimize analgesia-related variability, all patients received the same routine baseline analgesic regimen according to our institutional protocol, including local wound infiltration after skin closure (“cocktail” infiltration) and postoperative patient-controlled analgesia (PCA). Specifically, postoperative PCA was delivered using an analgesic pump with a standardized formulation of sufentanil citrate (2.5 μg/kg) plus ondansetron (16 mg). Importantly, the VAS score used for the primary outcome was recorded before administration of any additional rescue analgesics beyond this standard regimen. Thus, the analyzed severe pain event represented breakthrough intolerable low back pain despite standardized baseline analgesia rather than pain measured after heterogeneous rescue analgesic interventions. Detailed PCA pump settings (e.g., basal rate, bolus dose, lockout interval), as well as exact rescue analgesic use and total analgesic consumption during the first 0–8 h, were not consistently available for standardized retrospective extraction and therefore were not analyzed.

Standardized preoperative pain intensity (e.g., baseline VAS) was not consistently documented in the electronic medical records during the study period and therefore could not be analyzed or included as a covariate. Nevertheless, this study specifically focused on immediate postoperative low back pain rather than radicular lower-extremity pain. Most included patients were managed with preoperative bed rest, and routine clinical observation suggested relatively mild low back pain before surgery in the majority of cases, which may have reduced baseline heterogeneity to some extent, although it did not replace formal preoperative pain measurement.

Imaging measurements and interobserver reliability

All imaging measurements were performed using our institutional PACS. Two observers underwent standardized training and independently completed all measurements while blinded to clinical outcomes. Discrepancies were resolved by joint review until consensus was reached. Interobserver reliability for continuous imaging parameters was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) based on a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement (ICC(2,1)), and ICC estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported [16, 17]. For categorical assessments (FJV presence and FJV grade), agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa with 95% CIs. A schematic illustration of imaging measurements is shown in Fig. 1.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using the independent-samples t test. Categorical variables are presented as counts (%) and were compared using the chi-square test. Severe immediate pain (yes/no) was used as the dependent variable. Univariate logistic regression was first performed to estimate unadjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. Clinically important variables and those with P < 0.10 in univariate analyses were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model, and adjusted ORs with 95% CIs were reported [21]. Because standardized preoperative pain data were unavailable, time from injury to surgery was forced into the multivariable model regardless of its univariable P value as a clinically relevant injury-course variable. It was retained to account for potential differences in peri-injury and perioperative timing, but it was not considered a substitute for standardized preoperative pain assessment. As a sensitivity analysis to partially reduce heterogeneity related to fracture severity and potentially unmeasured baseline pain, the multivariable logistic regression was repeated in a restricted cohort including only patients with less severe fracture morphology (AO Spine type A1–A2), excluding A3 fractures.

Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). AUC estimation and comparison followed the nonparametric method proposed by DeLong et al. [22], and interpretation of AUC was guided by the classic ROC methodology described by Hanley and McNeil [23]. The 95% CI for AUC was estimated using bootstrap resampling [24]. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics

This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee. Given the retrospective design and anonymization of all data prior to analysis, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [25].

Baseline characteristics by immediate severe pain status

Compared with the non-immediate severe pain group, patients with immediate severe pain were older (55.66 ± 9.95 vs 45.66 ± 9.24 years, P < 0.001) and had higher BMI (25.69 ± 4.69 vs 22.89 ± 4.83 kg/m2, P < 0.001). Intraoperative blood loss was slightly higher in the immediate severe pain group (140.67 ± 87.19 vs 126.18 ± 72.78 mL, P = 0.028). There were no significant between-group differences in time from injury to surgery, number of instrumented levels, or operative time (all P > 0.05).

Regarding imaging parameters, the immediate severe pain group showed more unfavorable radiographic features, including higher vertebral compression ratio (0.31 ± 0.13 vs 0.20 ± 0.12, P < 0.001), lower injured vertebral HU (162.34 ± 38.93 vs 192.35 ± 36.55, P < 0.001), and a larger screw–endplate distance (6.89 ± 2.57 vs 6.31 ± 2.39 mm, P = 0.006). The proportion of FJV was also higher in the immediate severe pain group (42.9% vs 23.9%, P < 0.001). Categorical variables including sex, smoking status, major medical comorbidity, injury mechanism, and education level did not differ significantly between groups (all P > 0.05). Fracture morphology according to the AO Spine classification (A1–A3) was also comparable between patients with and without immediate severe pain (χ2 test, P = 0.083).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 598 patients undergoing PPSF, we systematically evaluated factors associated with severe immediate postoperative pain within 0–8 h and developed a multivariable logistic regression model with good discrimination (AUC = 0.839). The main findings were as follows: (1) increasing age and BMI were associated with higher odds of severe immediate pain; (2) greater vertebral compression severity was associated with higher odds of severe immediate pain; (3) lower HU value of the fractured vertebra was associated with higher odds of the outcome, whereas higher HU was inversely associated with immediate severe pain; and (4) facet joint violation (FJV) was associated with severe immediate pain. These findings should be interpreted as associations observed in a retrospective PPSF-treated cohort rather than as evidence of strict causality. Collectively, these results suggest that differences in patient susceptibility, injury severity, bone quality, and technique-related factors may help explain heterogeneity in ultra-early pain within a PPSF-treated cohort and highlight potentially identifiable perioperative risk points. However, because the study population was restricted to patients selected for PPSF in routine clinical practice, these associations should be interpreted in the context of a clinically selected surgical cohort rather than as evidence that PPSF itself determines the occurrence of immediate severe pain.

Baseline pain intensity could plausibly influence pain perception in the ultra-early postoperative window, and we agree that the absence of standardized preoperative pain scores is an important limitation of this retrospective study. The present study, however, focused specifically on severe immediate postoperative low back pain rather than lower-extremity radicular symptoms. To improve comparability of pain assessment, we excluded patients with severe neurological deficits requiring emergent decompression and other conditions that could compromise reliable subjective pain reporting. In routine clinical practice, most included patients were maintained on preoperative bed rest, and low back pain before surgery was generally mild in daily clinical observation, which may have reduced baseline heterogeneity to some extent, although it did not replace formal preoperative pain measurement.

In addition, most cases in the present cohort were mild fractures and some were moderate fractures. Fracture reduction may have influenced early postoperative pain to some degree. However, PPSF in this cohort mainly relied on indirect reduction, which was generally limited in magnitude, and marked anterior column distraction sufficient to induce excessive stretching pain of the anterior longitudinal ligament or annulus fibrosus was considered less likely. Because standardized baseline pain scores, pain localization, and reduction-related parameters were not consistently recorded in this retrospective dataset, these factors could not be formally adjusted for in the multivariable analysis. Time from injury to surgery was retained in the model as a clinically relevant injury-course variable; however, it should not be interpreted as a surrogate for baseline pain intensity, because it may also reflect non-pain-related factors such as surgical scheduling and preoperative preparation. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as associations observed within a relatively homogeneous PPSF-treated cohort rather than as evidence of strict causality. Future prospective studies should incorporate standardized preoperative pain assessment, site-specific pain recording, and reduction-related variables to further validate these findings.

Alignment of ultra-early pain with ERAS goals

With the increasing adoption of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) concepts in spine surgery, perioperative analgesia and early functional restoration are considered central to improving recovery trajectories. The ERAS Society has issued consensus recommendations for perioperative care in lumbar spinal fusion, emphasizing multidisciplinary pathways, opioid-sparing strategies, and early functional recovery as key elements [26]. Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses in adult spine surgery have consistently suggested potential benefits of ERAS pathways in shortening length of stay, improving perioperative recovery, and reducing selected complications [27, 28].

In the present study, we focused on the 0–8 h ultra-early window, during which pain intensity may directly determine the feasibility of first turning, first ambulation, and respiratory training, thereby shaping early rehabilitation pace and inpatient experience. Identifying modifiable factors associated with ultra-early severe pain may facilitate risk-stratified analgesic and rehabilitation pathway management, enabling a practical early cycle of “turning—ambulation—training.” From a perioperative nursing perspective, emerging evidence also suggests that earlier postoperative activity may be associated with lower risks of adverse outcomes such as surgical site infection [29], and evidence syntheses regarding early mobilization after lumbar fusion continue to evolve [30].

Age and BMI: susceptibility and load/inflammation-related contributors

Older age was associated with higher odds of severe immediate pain, potentially reflecting decreased reparative capacity, differences in pain sensitivity, higher comorbidity burden, and age-related bone quality changes. Higher BMI was also associated with higher odds of the outcome, which may indicate greater mechanical loading, increased technical difficulty and soft-tissue stress during surgery, and variability in inflammatory responses. In older and higher-BMI populations, perioperative management may benefit from more proactive preventive analgesia, structured turning/positioning protocols, and feasibility-focused early mobilization planning to mitigate the negative impact of ultra-early severe pain on rehabilitation.

Injury severity: vertebral compression severity and ultra-early severe pain

We found a significant positive association between vertebral compression severity and severe immediate pain, highlighting the role of injury severity and structural involvement in ultra-early pain. Greater compression severity may reflect higher-energy trauma, more extensive vertebral comminution and endplate involvement, and/or a higher likelihood of posterior element injury. Quantitative imaging assessment has been emphasized for thoracolumbar fracture characterization and clinical decision support [18]. During the early postoperative period, mechanical load changes during turning, coughing, and respiratory training may more readily provoke pain in patients with more severe compression. Clinically, incorporating compression severity into preoperative risk assessment may help guide proactive adjustments in reduction strategy, fixation planning, and analgesic intensity, thereby reducing barriers to early rehabilitation initiation.

Bone quality: HU value and screw–bone interface stability

HU value, as a CT-based surrogate for bone quality, has been widely used to evaluate instrumentation-related risks [19, 20]. In our cohort, lower HU values were associated with higher odds of severe immediate pain. Recent systematic reviews/meta-analyses have summarized the prognostic value of HU for predicting spinal surgical outcomes, suggesting that low HU is associated with increased risks of multiple adverse events [31]. Additional evidence syntheses focusing on interbody fusion have supported HU-based prediction of cage subsidence and pedicle screw loosening, further reinforcing HU as a practical tool for preoperative risk stratification [32]. In the ultra-early postoperative phase, low HU may amplify pain responses through micro-motion at the screw–bone interface, reduced capacity to maintain reduction, and focal stress concentration. For patients with low HU, optimization of screw parameters (length/diameter/trajectory) and fixation strategy—while ensuring safety—combined with proactive multimodal analgesia may improve early pain control and facilitate functional restoration.

FJV as a potentially preventable technique-related risk factor

FJV is a common imaging-detected adverse event in percutaneous pedicle screw placement and is closely related to entry point selection, screw-head position, trajectory planning, and inter-individual anatomic variation. However, evidence comparing cranial facet violation rates between percutaneous and open techniques remains inconsistent across studies and meta-analyses, implying that surgeon experience, imaging guidance, and assessment criteria may influence reported rates [8–10]. A recent scoping review further summarized the prevalence of FJV, its potential biomechanical consequences, and its associations with adjacent-segment pathology, underscoring the need for “identification—prevention—standardized assessment” [33].

An expanding body of studies and reviews indicates that facet violation may alter adjacent-segment biomechanics and is associated with delayed pain trajectories and adjacent-segment degeneration/disease-related outcomes [12, 33]. In thoracolumbar trauma populations, PPSF-related FJV has also been linked to progression of facet osteoarthritis [34]. Importantly, our endpoint differs from most prior studies because we focused on the ultra-early postoperative window (0–8 h), rather than mid- or long-term pain. During this period, pain likely represents a mixed phenotype that includes residual injury-related nociception and early peri-incisional/deep soft-tissue irritation. Within this context, the observed association between FJV and ultra-early severe pain suggests that, beyond its longer-term biomechanical sequelae, FJV may also aggravate immediate pain through acute facet capsular/synovial irritation, local inflammatory responses, and mechanical provocation during early turning and mobilization. Notably, we did not assess pain beyond the 0–8 h window; therefore, we cannot infer whether FJV predicts persistent or delayed pain, which should be evaluated in dedicated prospective follow-up studies.

From an interventional standpoint, reducing FJV depends on meticulous trajectory planning, appropriate entry point selection and angulation control, and rational use of intraoperative imaging assistance. In thoracolumbar trauma, navigation-assisted percutaneous screw placement has been reported to reduce FJV compared with conventional fluoroscopy [11]. Evidence is also accumulating across advanced guidance technologies (robotics, navigation, augmented reality) regarding mitigation of screw-related imaging adverse events including FJV. Systematic reviews support that robot-assisted percutaneous pedicle screw placement may reduce FJV and improve radiographic outcomes compared with fluoroscopy-guided techniques [35], and comparative studies have reported lower cranial FJV rates with robotic versus navigation-assisted instrumentation (with possible variability across spinal levels) [36]. Therefore, for high-risk individuals identified in this study, “FJV avoidance” can be set as a priori intraoperative optimization target, with tailored entry point selection, deliberate facet-sparing screw-head placement, and appropriate guidance strategies to maximize feasibility and potential benefit.

Clinical implications: risk stratification and “tailored analgesia + fixation strategy optimization”

With an AUC of 0.839, the model demonstrated good discriminative ability and may support perioperative risk identification and decision-making. From a modifiable-factor perspective, minimizing FJV should be considered a key target in screw-placement strategy optimization. In parallel, integrating ERAS pathways and multimodal analgesia may help facilitate early functional restoration [26–28].

Evidence for regional anesthesia in postoperative spine analgesia has also expanded. Meta-analyses in spinal fusion surgery suggest that the erector spinae plane block (ESPB) can reduce early pain scores and opioid/analgesic requirements, offering a potential tool to strengthen analgesia during the ultra-early window [37]. Systematic reviews focusing on patient-centered outcomes (e.g., quality of recovery) also indicate that ESPB may improve recovery quality while reducing postoperative nausea/vomiting and opioid consumption [38]. Moreover, randomized controlled evidence has evaluated ESPB in minimally invasive posterior transpedicular stabilization for vertebral fractures, supporting its potential to reduce postoperative analgesic needs in trauma-related minimally invasive settings [39]. Therefore, for patients at higher risk (e.g., high compression severity, low HU, high BMI, or advanced age), a closed-loop strategy of “risk stratification—tailored analgesia—facilitated ambulation” may be particularly important.

Limitations and future directions

This study has inherent limitations related to its retrospective single-center design, including potential selection bias. In addition, because all included patients underwent PPSF and treatment allocation was based on routine clinical judgment rather than randomization, confounding by indication cannot be excluded. Patients selected for PPSF may have had greater fracture instability, stronger need for early mobilization, or more severe symptoms than those treated non-operatively. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted within the context of a clinically selected PPSF-treated cohort; they may not be directly generalizable to conservatively managed thoracolumbar junction fractures, and extrapolation to open instrumentation or other fixation strategies requires further validation. Additionally, because patients with severe neurological deficits requiring emergent decompression (e.g., AIS A/B) were excluded, our findings may not be generalizable to thoracolumbar junction fractures with complete or near-complete neurological injury. Some confounders—particularly standardized baseline (preoperative) pain intensity and perioperative analgesic exposure—could not be fully controlled. Although severe neurological deficit cases were excluded and the included patients were relatively comparable in routine preoperative bed-rest status, formal preoperative VAS scores were unavailable, and pain localization was not recorded in a standardized manner. In addition, although all patients received the same routine baseline postoperative analgesia, including wound cocktail infiltration and PCA, and the index VAS score for the primary outcome was recorded before administration of additional rescue analgesics, detailed PCA settings and exact data on rescue analgesic use and total analgesic consumption during the first 0–8 h were not consistently available for standardized retrospective extraction. Therefore, we could not fully distinguish the potential contributions of pre-existing fracture-related pain, surgical wound/soft-tissue pain, possible reduction-related mechanical pain, and unmeasured variability in analgesic exposure to the 0–8 h postoperative pain outcome. Moreover, because pain localization was not recorded in a standardized manner, we could not distinguish whether the reported 0–8 h pain primarily originated from fracture-related nociception or from the surgical wound/soft-tissue irritation. In addition, intraoperative reduction maneuvers and the degree/quality of fracture reduction were not captured as standardized variables in the retrospective records; therefore, we could not evaluate their adjusted association with ultra-early (0–8 h) severe pain. Because most fractures in this cohort were mild and some were moderate, and PPSF mainly relied on indirect reduction with limited magnitude, marked anterior column distraction-related overstretching pain was considered less likely; nevertheless, residual confounding related to reduction cannot be excluded. To partially mitigate this concern, we excluded patients with confirmed chronic pain syndrome or long-term analgesic use and retained time from injury to surgery in the multivariable model as a clinically relevant injury-course variable; however, it was not considered a substitute for standardized preoperative pain assessment. In addition, to partially reduce heterogeneity related to fracture severity and potentially unmeasured baseline pain, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to patients with less severe fractures (AO Spine type A1–A2), and the main adjusted associations remained broadly consistent with those of the primary analysis. Nevertheless, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Additionally, we did not evaluate mid- or long-term pain outcomes; therefore, the present findings specifically address ultra-early (0–8 h) severe pain and should not be extrapolated to delayed pain trajectories or adjacent-segment outcomes. Nevertheless, the large sample size and inclusion of key imaging variables (FJV, compression severity, and HU) provide a relatively comprehensive assessment of factors associated with ultra-early severe pain after PPSF. Future work should include multicenter external validation and prospective studies to confirm generalizability, and to evaluate whether navigation/robot-assisted optimization and risk-stratified analgesic pathways can meaningfully improve ultra-early pain outcomes.

Conclusion

In patients with thoracolumbar junction fractures treated by PPSF, age, BMI, vertebral compression ratio, and FJV were associated with immediate severe pain within 0–8 postoperative hours, whereas higher injured vertebral HU values were inversely associated with the outcome. These factors may facilitate perioperative risk stratification and inform targeted analgesia and screw placement strategy optimization. However, given the retrospective design and residual confounding related to baseline pain status and analgesic exposure, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and require prospective validation.
