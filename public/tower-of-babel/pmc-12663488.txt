Reply
Authors: Jehangir Ali Shah, Musa Karim, Abdul Hakeem, RIVAWAR investigators
Published: 2025

We sincerely appreciate the insightful comments by Dr Abdelnabi and colleagues on the RIVAWAR (Rivaroxaban versus Warfarin in Acute Left Ventricular Thrombus Following Myocardial Infarction) trial.1 The first concern relates to the implied high incidence of left ventricular thrombus (LVT), raising questions about generalizability. During the study period (June 2021 to December 2023), the annual volume of primary percutaneous coronary interventions (PCIs) and early invasive PCI at our center was 9,215 and 2,913 in 2023; 8,991 and 2,414 in 2022; and 8,110 and 2,363 in 2021, respectively.2 Over the trial duration, the total PCI volume was 28,770 (adjusting for half of 2021). Based on this denominator, the estimated incidence of acute LVT was ≈1.2% (331/28,770), which is well within the reported range for post-myocardial infarction LVT. This suggests that the RIVAWAR cohort is well representative of typical post-MI patients rather than a uniquely high-risk group.

Concerning the use of 2:1 randomization and per-protocol analysis, we acknowledge that single-center design and unbalanced randomization can limit generalizability and statistical power particularly for infrequent safety outcomes. The 2:1 randomization was primarily undertaken because of challenges associated with warfarin use, including frequent international normalized ratio testing and patient compliance issues. In addition, this approach reflects a real-world pragmatic trial design aimed at enhancing recruitment and adherence. To address the issue of power, a post hoc analysis indicated approximately 80.9% power based on observed resolution rates, providing confidence in the primary efficacy findings. We have explicitly acknowledged these limitations in the manuscript.1

We agree that the use of noncontrast transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) may limit sensitivity compared with contrast-enhanced transthoracic echocardiography or cardiac magnetic resonance. These limitations, along with the relatively short follow-up, were highlighted in the manuscript.1 However, it is important to note that outcome assessments were conducted in a blinded manner, thereby reducing the risk of detection bias. Furthermore, RIVAWAR’s pragmatic trial design seeks to inform real world practice where access to contrast echo and cardiac magnetic resonance is often limited.

Finally, regarding the high (>95%) thrombus resolution rate at 3 months, we believe this can be explained by the acute nature of the thrombi in our cohort. Acutely formed thrombi are typically fibrin-rich and less organized, making them more responsive to anticoagulation compared with chronic thrombi. This is consistent with previous reports demonstrating high resolution rates (>93%) in similar populations.3,4

In conclusion, despite the acknowledged limitations, RIVAWAR trial contributes valuable data in this understudied area providing a compelling foundation for future large scale, multicenter studies incorporating advanced imaging modalities and longer follow-up.
