A Quantum-Centric Super-Krylov Diagonalization Method
Adam Byrne, William Kirby, Kirk M. Soodhalter, Sergiy Zhuk

Abstract
The problem of estimating the ground-state energy of a quantum system is ubiquitous in chemistry and condensed matter physics. Krylov quantum diagonalization (KQD) methods have emerged as a promising approach for this task, although many existing methods rely on subroutines – particularly the Hadamard test – that are challenging to implement on near-term quantum computers. We present a KQD method that uses only real-time evolutions and recovery probabilities, making it very well adapted for existing quantum hardware. Additionally, we propose a classical post-processing derivative estimation algorithm. Under assumptions on the spectrum of the Hamiltonian, we prove that our algorithm converges exponentially quickly to the ground-state energy. Finally, we provide classical numerical simulations for the transverse-field Ising model on 100 qubits.



Introduction.—
One of the major computational challenges in chemistry and physics is approximating the ground-state energy of many-body quantum systems. In general, this task is exponentially hard on classical computers, due to the cost of classically representing vectors in the Hilbert space
Aspuru-Guzik et al. 2005
;
Kandala et al. 2017
;
Su et al. 2021
. There are many quantum algorithms that avoid this storage problem by encoding the system’s Hilbert space in the exponentially-sized Hilbert space of a register of qubits
Kitaev 1995
;
Peruzzo et al. 2014
;
Urbanek et al. 2020
;
Cohn et al. 2021
;
Seki and Yunoki 2021
;
Baker 2021
;
Stair et al. 2020
;
Motta et al. 2019
;
Klymko et al. 2022
;
Shen et al. 2023a
;
Epperly et al. 2022
;
Kirby et al. 2023
;
Tkachenko et al. 2024
;
Yoshioka et al. 2024
;
Dong et al. 2022
;
Lin and Tong 2022
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Robledo-Moreno et al. 2024
;
Shen et al. 2023b
;
Ding et al. 2024
. The most widely-known algorithm, quantum phase estimation
Kitaev 1995
, requires a fault-tolerant device to perform deep quantum circuits. Another famous algorithm, the variational quantum eigensolver
Peruzzo et al. 2014
, uses shorter-depth circuits, but is limited to small system sizes due to highly non-convex optimization landscapes and barren plateaus
Bittel and Kliesch 2021
;
McClean et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2021
.

Krylov quantum diagonalization (KQD) methods have emerged as a promising alternative for pre-fault-tolerant quantum computers
Stair et al. 2020
;
Motta et al. 2019
;
Klymko et al. 2022
;
Shen et al. 2023a
;
Yoshioka et al. 2024
;
Kirby 2024
;
Parrish and McMahon 2019
. These methods employ the Rayleigh-Ritz procedure, which we outline as follows: Define the Hamiltonian
H
acting on
n
-qubits, with eigenvalue equation

H\ket{u}=\lambda\ket{u}

(1)

and denote by
\mathcal{H}
the corresponding Hilbert space of
N=2^{n}
dimensional complex vectors, equipped with inner product

\langle a,b\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\coloneqq a^{\dagger}b,\quad\forall a,b\in\mathcal{H}.

(2)

Consider an
m
-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{H}
:

\mathcal{K}=\text{span}\,\big\{\ket{\phi_0},...,\ket{\phi_{m-1}}\big\}

(3)

for some basis states
\ket{\phi_j}
. We seek an approximate eigenpair
\big(\widetilde{\lambda},\widetilde{u}\big)\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathcal{K}
satisfying

\left(H\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{\lambda}\widetilde{u}\right)\perp\mathcal{K}.

(4)

The constraint in (
4
) yields the projected
m\times m
eigenvalue problem

\mathbf{H}c=\widetilde{\lambda}\,\mathbf{S}c

(5)

where

\begin{split}\mathbf{H}_{jk}=\bra{\phi_j}H\ket{\phi_k},\quad\mathbf{S}_{jk}=\bra{\phi_j}\ket{\phi_k}.\end{split}

(6)

In practice, the matrices
\mathbf{H}
and
\mathbf{S}
are constructed element-wise on a quantum computer. The resulting eigenvalue problem (
5
) is then solved using classical methods
Saad 2011
and the lowest eigenvalue
\widetilde{\lambda}_{0}
serves as an estimate of the true ground energy
\lambda_{0}
.

Various options have been proposed for the basis states
\{\ket{\phi_j}\}_{j}

Parrish and McMahon 2019
;
Stair et al. 2020
;
Cohn et al. 2021
;
Klymko et al. 2022
;
Motta et al. 2019
;
Seki and Yunoki 2021
;
Kirby et al. 2023
. The real time evolution
U(t)=e^{-\imath Ht}
can be efficiently approximated by existing quantum computers, and so the most suitable choice in the near term is the Krylov basis

\ket{\phi_j(t)}=U^{j}(t)\ket{v},\quad j=0,...,m-1,

(7)

where
t
is some fixed time and
\ket{v}
is some initial state with
{|\bra{u_0}\ket{v}|\neq 0}
. With this choice of basis, the projected matrices in (
6
) become

\begin{split}\mathbf{H}_{jk}(t)&=\bra{v}U^{-j}(t)HU^{k}(t)\ket{v},\\
\mathbf{S}_{jk}(t)&=\bra{v}U^{-j}(t)U^{k}(t)\ket{v}.\end{split}

(8)

The standard method for estimating complex overlaps of the form (
8
) on a quantum device is
the Hadamard test. However, unless the problem has some additional structure
Cortes and Gray 2022
;
Yoshioka et al. 2024
;
Yu et al. 2025
, this involves the implementation of controlled unitary operations, which is a prohibitively difficult task for near-term quantum computers
Preskill 2018
;
Lau et al. 2022
;
Mitarai and Fujii 2019
. Motivated by this problem, we propose the
super
-Krylov method.

The Super-Krylov method.—
Instead of performing the Rayleigh-Ritz procedure on the Hamiltonian
H
, we perform the Rayleigh-Ritz procedure on the
super
-operator

\begin{split}\mathcal{J}:\mathcal{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C)}&\to\mathcal{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C)}\\
\cdot&\mapsto\imath[H,\cdot]\end{split}

(9)

where
\mathcal{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})=\mathcal{M}_{N}
is the Hilbert space of
N\times N
complex matrices, equipped with the trace inner product

\bigl(A,B\bigr)_{\mathcal{M}_{N}}\coloneqq\text{Tr}\left(A^{\dagger}B\right),\quad\forall A,B\in\mathcal{M}_{N}.

(10)

Thus, we declare this a
super
-Krylov method. This approach has two key advantages. Firstly, the method does not require Hadamard tests and uses only quantum probabilities, making it very well adapted for existing quantum devices. Secondly, this method can be applied to estimate the ground energy of a wide class of Hamiltonians. To see this, note that
\mathcal{J}
has eigenvalue equation

\mathcal{J}\outerproduct{u_p}{u_q}=\imath\,(\lambda_{p}-\lambda_{q})\outerproduct{u_p}{u_q}

(11)

for
p,q=0,...,N-1
, where
\imath(\lambda_{p}-\lambda_{q})\in\imath\mathbb{R}
. Denote by
{\Delta_{0}\coloneqq\imath(\lambda_{0}-\lambda_{N-1}})
the eigenvalue with smallest imaginary part, with corresponding eigenoperator
{X_{0}\coloneqq\ket{u_0}\bra{u_{N-1}}}
. Given an estimate
\widetilde{\Delta}_{0}
of
\Delta_{0}
, we can estimate the ground energy of two classes of Hamiltonians, where:

1.

WHW^{\dagger}=-H
for some (anti)unitary
W
, or

2.

\lambda_{N-1}
is known or is ‘easily computable’.

If
H
belongs to the first class, then
\lambda_{0}=-\lambda_{N-1}
and the ground energy is estimated as
\lambda_{0}\approx\widetilde{\Delta}_{0}/2\imath
. If
H
belongs to the second class, then the ground energy is estimated as
\lambda_{0}\approx\widetilde{\Delta}_{0}/\imath+\lambda_{N-1}
. As a concrete example, consider the Heisenberg models

H=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}(A_{ij}\sigma_{i}^{\tau}\sigma_{j}^{\tau}+B_{ij}\sigma_{i}^{\mu}\sigma_{j}^{\mu})+\sum_{0\leq k\leq n-1}C_{k}\sigma_{k}^{\nu}

(12)

for some set of interactions
E
and couplings
A_{ij},B_{ij},C_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
. Here
\tau,\mu\in\chi=\{x,y,z\}
for
\tau\neq\mu
and
\nu\in\chi/\{\tau,\mu\}
, where
\sigma_{i}^{x},\sigma_{i}^{y},\sigma_{i}^{z}
are Pauli operators acting on the
i
th site. It can be shown that the models in (
12
) belong to the first class by taking
W=\prod_{(i,j)\in E}\sigma_{i}^{\tau}\sigma_{j}^{\mu}
. For the second class, consider the antiferromagnetic Heisenberg model

H=\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq n-1}J_{ij}(\sigma_{i}^{x}\sigma_{j}^{x}+\sigma_{i}^{y}\sigma_{j}^{y}+\sigma_{i}^{z}\sigma_{j}^{z})

(13)

with couplings
J_{ij}\geq 0
. The most excited energy of this Hamiltonian can be ‘easily computed’ as
\lambda_{N-1}=\sum_{i,j}J_{ij}
.

While we focus on ground energy estimation in this work, we point out that the super-Krylov method can also be used to estimate the spectral range of an arbitrary Hamiltonian
H
(provided
\ket{w}\mapsto e^{-\imath Hdt}\ket{w}
admits an efficient implementation on a quantum device), since
\lambda_{N-1}-\lambda_{0}\approx\imath\,\widetilde{\Delta}_{0}
. Moreover, if the ground energy of the Hamiltonian is known, then the most excited energy can be estimated as
\lambda_{N-1}\approx\imath\,\widetilde{\Delta}_{0}+\lambda_{0}
.

We proceed with the Rayleigh Ritz procedure for the operator
\mathcal{J}
. Consider the following space
\mathcal{T}
spanned by a set of density matrices
\rho_{k}=\outerproduct{w_k}{w_k}
for
\ket{w_k}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}&\coloneqq\text{span}\,\big\{\rho_{0},...,\rho_{m-1}\big\}\\
&=\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\alpha_{j}\rho_{j}\,\in\mathcal{M}_{N}:\,\,\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\right\}\end{split}

(14)

for some integer parameter
m>0
. We seek an approximate eigenpair
(\widetilde{\Delta},\widetilde{X})\in\imath\,\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{T}
satisfying

\left(\mathcal{J}\widetilde{X}-\widetilde{\Delta}\widetilde{X}\right)\perp\mathcal{T}

(15)

where orthogonality is defined with respect to
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathcal{M}_{N}}
. The condition (
15
) yields the projected
m\times m
eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}a=\widetilde{\Delta}\,\mathbf{R}a

(16)

where

\begin{split}\mathbf{J}_{jk}=\imath\,\text{Tr}(\rho_{j}\,[\rho_{k},H]),\quad\mathbf{R}_{jk}=\text{Tr}(\rho_{j}\rho_{k}).\end{split}

(17)

Since
\mathbf{J}
is skew-symmetric, then
\widetilde{\Delta}\in\imath\mathbb{R}
. The eigenvalue
\widetilde{\Delta}_{0}
of (
16
) with lowest imaginary part serves as an approximation of
\Delta_{0}
.

We have just to choose the generating states
{\left\{\rho_{j}\right\}_{j=0}^{m-1}}
in (
14
). Motivated by the subspace selection in standard KQD methods, which is known to yield rapid convergence for the extremal eigenvalues of
H
, we choose the space generated by powers of the time evolution operator generated by the super-operator
\mathcal{J}
. That is, we take the states

\rho_{j}(t)=U^{j}(t)\rho\,U^{-j}(t),\quad j=0,...,m-1,

(18)

where
\rho=\outerproduct{w}{w}
is some initial state with

\begin{split}0\neq\gamma_{0}\coloneqq\text{Tr}(\rho X_{0}).\end{split}

(19)

Note that
\text{Tr}(\rho X_{0})=\innerproduct{w}{u_0}\innerproduct{u_{N-1}}{w}
, so the condition (
19
) is equivalent to requiring
\innerproduct{w}{u_0},\innerproduct{u_{N-1}}{w}\neq 0
. We will be able to show that this Krylov space yields rapid convergence of the extremal eigenvalues of
\mathcal{J}
, just as for
H
in standard KQD.
Under the choice (
18
), the projected matrices in (
17
) become

\begin{split}\mathbf{J}_{jk}(t)&=\imath\,\text{Tr}(\rho_{j}(t)\,[\rho_{k}(t),H]),\\
\mathbf{R}_{jk}(t)&=\text{Tr}(\rho_{j}(t)\rho_{k}(t))\end{split}

(20)

The time parameter is taken as some fixed value
t=dt
, and we solve the resulting problem

(\mathbf{J},\mathbf{R})\equiv\bigl(\mathbf{J}(dt),\mathbf{R}(dt)\bigr).

(21)

The optimal value of
dt
is provided later in
Theorem

2
.

The following lemma motivates our algorithm for computing
(\mathbf{J},\mathbf{R})
in (
21
).

Lemma 1
.

The entries of
\mathbf{R}(t)
have the equivalent representation

\mathbf{R}_{jk}(t)=|\langle w|U^{-j}(t)U^{k}(t)|w\rangle|^{2}\in[0,1].

(22)

Furthermore, the entries of
\mathbf{J}(t)
satisfy

\mathbf{J}_{jk}(t)=\frac{1}{(j-k)}\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{R}_{jk}(t).

(23)

The proof of
Lemma

1
is provided in the Supplemental Material
sup
. The quantity (
22
) is the recovery probability of
\ket{w}
in the time-evolved state
U^{-j}(t)U^{k}(t)\ket{w}
. This can be estimated by preparing
\ket{w}
, time-evolving via
U^{-j}(t)U^{k}(t)
, then unpreparing
\ket{w}
and estimating the probability of
|00...0\rangle
by repeated measurements. This does not require Hadamard tests, and can be efficiently performed on existing and near-term quantum computers. Then using the relation (
23
), the entries of
\mathbf{J}(t)
can be obtained directly from the entries of
\mathbf{R}(t)
by estimating the quantity
\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{R}_{jk}(t)
. Motivated by these facts, we propose the following algorithm to construct an approximation
(\widehat{\mathbf{J}},\widehat{\mathbf{R}})
of
(\mathbf{J},\mathbf{R})=(\mathbf{J}(dt),\mathbf{R}(dt))
using a quantum device and a classical post-processing function estimation method.

Define
z:[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
by

z(t)\coloneqq|\langle w|U(t)|w\rangle|^{2}.

(24)

Notice that
z\big((k-j)t\big)=\mathbf{R}_{jk}(t)
. The function
z
is measured on a quantum computer at
D
equally spaced timepoints

0=t_{0}\leq t_{1}\leq...\leq t_{D-1}=m\,dt.

(25)

Given these noisy measurements of
z
, we construct
classical
smooth function estimates
\widehat{z}(t)
and
\widehat{\frac{d}{dt}z}(t)
of
z(t)
and
\frac{d}{dt}z(t)
respectively, over
t\in[0,m\,dt]
. These estimates are then used to (classically) approximate the first row of
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{J}}
via

\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{0k}=\left.\widehat{z}(t)\right|_{t=k\,dt},\quad\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{0k}=-\frac{1}{k}\left.\widehat{\frac{d}{dt}z}(t)\right|_{t=k\,dt},

(26)

for
k=1,...,m-1
, and the diagonal elements are computed exactly as
{\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{00}=1}
,
{\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{00}=0}
. Since
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{J}
are Toeplitz-symmetric and Toeplitz skew-symmetric respectively, the remaining entries of
\widehat{\mathbf{J}}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
are determined from the first row of each matrix.

Note that the time evolution
\ket{w}\to U(t)\ket{w}
is not performed exactly on a quantum device, and instead is approximated using some discretization technique (e.g., the Trotter-Suzuki decomposition). The measurement of the quantum probabilities (
22
) will also incur some sampling errors. In this case, the matrices
\mathbf{R}
and
\imath\,\mathbf{J}
will not be Toeplitz-symmetric, and so the approximation
(\widehat{\mathbf{J}},\widehat{\mathbf{R}})
obtained by enforcing this structure will represent the true matrices corrupted by the discretization and sampling errors
Epperly et al. 2022
.

Function estimation.—
We propose a state-space
Milanese and Tempo 1985
;
Milanese and Vicino 1996
approach to construct the function estimates in (
26
). Denote the measurements of
z
by

y_{s}\coloneqq z(t_{s})+\eta_{s},\quad s=0,...,D-1,

(27)

for some noise
\eta_{s}
, which is due to finite shot sampling and hardware noise in the device. We make no assumption about the statistical distribution of this noise, and so our estimate can be applied regardless of the noise description induced by the quantum computer. Define the integer parameter
M\geq 2
which controls the ‘smoothness’ of our function estimates.

Before proceeding, we introduce the following function spaces. For
\Omega\coloneqq[0,m\,dt]
, define the Sobolev space
\mathcal{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
of all functions
{f\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega)}
with first weak derivative in
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega)
. Take
\mathcal{V}(\Omega)
as the space of functions
\vec{a}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
such that
{\vec{a}_{0}(t),...,\vec{a}_{M-1}(t)\in\mathcal{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)}
, with corresponding norm
{\|\vec{a}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{V}}\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\vec{a}^{T}(s)\vec{a}(s)ds}
. For brevity, we use the notation
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}(\Omega)
.

Define the
state vector

\vec{x}\in\mathcal{V}
(note: not a quantum state) by

\vec{x}_{0}(t)\coloneqq z(t),\quad\vec{x}_{l}(t)\coloneqq\frac{d}{dt}\vec{x}_{l-1}(t),

(28)

for
l=1,...,M-1
, and initial condition

\displaystyle\vec{x}_{in}\coloneqq\vec{x}(0).

(29)

We construct an estimate
\widehat{\vec{x}}
of the state vector
\vec{x}
. The components
\widehat{\vec{x}}_{l}(t)
will each serve as function estimates of
{\vec{x}_{l}(t)=\frac{d^{l}}{dt^{l}}z(t)}
, for
t\in\Omega
. First, we write (
27
) and (
28
) as operator equations for
\vec{x}
.
Eq.

27
can be written equivalently as

\vec{y}=C\vec{x}+\vec{\eta}

(30)

where
{C:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}}
is the point-evaluation operator

C\vec{w}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\vec{w}_{0}(t_{0})\\
\vdots\\
\vec{w}_{0}(t_{D-1})\end{pmatrix},\quad\vec{w}\in\mathcal{V}.

(31)

Eq.

28
can be written as

L\vec{x}=\vec{f}

(32)

where
L:\mathcal{D}(L)\subset\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
is the differential operator defined for
\vec{w}\in\mathcal{D}(L)
by

L\vec{w}\coloneqq\frac{d}{dt}\vec{w}(t)-A\vec{w}(t),\quad\vec{w}(0)=\vec{x}_{in},

(33)

with

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&\ldots&0\\
0&0&1&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ldots&1\\
0&0&0&\ldots&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\vec{f}(t)\coloneqq\dfrac{d^{M-1}}{dt^{M-1}}\vec{x}_{0}(t)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\vdots\\
0\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(34)

Eqs.

32
and
30
constitute the
state-space representation
of
\vec{x}
.

The estimate
\widehat{\vec{x}}\in\mathcal{D}(L)
of
\vec{x}
is obtained as the solution of the minimization problem:

\displaystyle\widehat{\vec{x}}\coloneqq\argmin_{\vec{w}\in\mathcal{D}(L)}\biggl(\|\vec{y}-C\vec{w}\|^{2}_{2}+\theta\|L\vec{w}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{V}}\biggr)

(35)

for some regularization parameter
\theta>0
. The problem (
35
) admits the following interpretation. For small
\theta
, the data term
\|\vec{y}-C\widehat{\vec{x}}\|_{2}^{2}
dominates the argument of (
35
), ensuring a better fit of
\widehat{\vec{x}}
to the datapoints
y
. In the limit
\theta\to 0
, this would result in over-fitting. For large
\theta
, the regularization term
\|L\vec{w}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{V}}
dominates, ensuring a smoother estimate
\widehat{\vec{x}}
. In the limit
\theta\to\infty
, this would result in under-fitting.

The following theorem provides a method for selecting the parameter
\theta
in (
35
). Under this selection, we show that there is an analytical expression for the estimate
\widehat{\vec{x}}
and for the ‘worst-case’ estimation error.

Theorem 1
.

Let the regularization parameter in (
35
) take the form
\theta=\theta_{1}/\theta_{2}
where
{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}>0}
satisfy

\theta_{2}\,\|\vec{\eta}\,\|^{2}_{2}+\theta_{1}\,\big\|\vec{f}\,\big\|^{2}_{\mathcal{V}}\leq 1

(36)

for
\vec{\eta}
and
\vec{f}
as given in (
30
) and (
32
) respectively. Then the minimisation problem (
35
) can be written equivalently as a minimax optimisation problem over a suitable bounding set
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V})
. The corresponding minimax solution
\widehat{\vec{x}}
and ‘minimax error’
\max_{\mathcal{G}}|\widehat{\vec{x}}_{j}(t)-\vec{x}_{j}(t)|
can each be expressed analytically as the solution of a coupled differential equation. Furthermore, in the limits
\|\vec{\eta}\,\|_{2}\to 0
and
D\to\infty
, the solution converges in the sense

\big\|\widehat{\vec{x}}-\vec{x}\big\|_{\mathcal{V}}\to 0.

(37)

The proof of
Theorem

1
can be found in the Supplemental Material
sup
. Notice that
\big\|\vec{f}\,\big\|_{\mathcal{V}}=\int_{\Omega}\big(\frac{d^{M-1}}{ds^{M-1}}z(s)\big)^{2}ds<\infty
since, by definition (
24
),
z
is analytic over the bounded domain
\Omega
. Thus, it is always possible to choose
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}>0
satisfying (
36
). The result (
37
) proves that we recover the exact function
\vec{x}
in the limits of zero noise and infinitely many datapoints.

In the Supplemental Material
sup
, we show that the derivative of
z
can be upper bounded as

\left|\frac{d}{dt}z(t)\right|\leq 2m\,|\lambda_{N-1}|,\quad\forall t\in\Omega.

(38)

For the example Hamiltonians in (
12
), (
13
), we have
|\lambda_{N-1}|\leq O(|E|+n)\leq O(n^{2})
, and so the number of measurements required to obtain an accurate estimate of
\frac{d}{dt}z
has worst-case scaling
O(m\,n^{2})
.

Krylov error analysis.—
For the Krylov error, we look to the analysis in
Epperly et al. 2022
;
Kirby 2024
, where the authors provide error bounds on the ground energy estimates obtained from the standard KQD method in the presence of noise.
Theorem

2
is adapted from Theorem 3.1. in
Epperly et al. 2022
, and provides an upper bound on the error of the minimum eigenvalue
\widehat{\Delta}_{0}
of the pair
(\widehat{\mathbf{J}},\widehat{\mathbf{R}})
.
Theorem

2
assumes that “eigenvalue thresholding” is used to regularize the Krylov matrix pair
\big(\widehat{\mathbf{J}},\widehat{\mathbf{R}}\big)
before it is solved. This is required because the ideal matrix
\mathbf{R}
is ill-conditioned, both in general and in practice, and so the noisy matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
can be singular
Epperly et al. 2022
;
Kirby 2024
. Eigenvalue thresholding avoids ill-conditioning of the problem
\big(\widehat{\mathbf{J}},\widehat{\mathbf{R}}\big)
by projecting out the eigenspaces of the Gram matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
whose eigenvalues are below some threshold
\epsilon>0
, thus enforcing that
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
is positive definite.

Theorem 2
.

Take
dt=\pi/2(\lambda_{N-1}-\lambda_{0})
and let

\omega\coloneqq\|\widehat{\mathbf{R}}-\mathbf{R}\,\|_{2}+\frac{\|\widehat{\mathbf{J}}-\mathbf{J}\,\|_{2}}{|\Delta_{0}|}

(39)

be a measure of the noise. Set
\epsilon=O(\omega^{2/3})
and assume that the initial overlap (
19
) satisfies

|\gamma_{0}|>2(\epsilon+\|\widehat{\mathbf{R}}-\mathbf{R}\|_{2}).

(40)

Furthermore, assume that the vectors
U^{j}\ket{w}
in the generating states (
18
) are mutually non-orthogonal. Then the lowest eigenvalue
\widehat{\Delta}_{0}
of the
\epsilon
-thresholded problem
(\widehat{\mathbf{J}},\widehat{\mathbf{R}})
satisfies

\frac{\left|\widehat{\Delta}_{0}-\Delta_{0}\right|}{|\Delta_{0}|}\leq O\left(\frac{\omega^{2/3}+\left(1+\frac{\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{0}}{|\Delta_{0}|}\right)^{-2m}}{|\gamma_{0}|^{2}}\right)

(41)

where
\Delta_{1}
is the eigenvalue of
\mathcal{J}
with second lowest imaginary part.

The proof of
Theorem

2
is deferred to the Supplemental Material
sup
. By (
41
), the estimate
\widehat{\Delta}_{0}
converges exponentially quickly with respect to the Krylov dimension
m
.

Numerical experiments.—

Conclusions.—
In this letter, we presented a new KQD algorithm that uses real-time evolutions and recovery probabilities from a quantum device, in conjunction with a classical post-processing function estimation method, to approximate the ground-state energy of a wide range of Hamiltonians. We proved that the algorithm converges exponentially quickly with respect to the Krylov dimension and performed classical numerical simulations for the Ising Hamiltonian on 100 qubits. One clear direction for follow-up work would be to perform the super-Krylov algorithm on a quantum device for a large system size to demonstrate the compatibility of the method with existing quantum processors. On the theoretical side, it would be fruitful to derive an expression for the error in the state-space estimate in terms of the sampling errors and the number of datapoints. This would allow for an end-to-end error analysis of the estimate
\widehat{\Delta}_{0}
. One could also consider adaptive sampling strategies for the datapoints in (
25
) to reduce the number of measurements required from the quantum device.

I
The Super-Krylov method

In this section, we prove
Lemma

2
, which is stated in the main text and motivates our algorithm for computing the projected matrices in the super-Krylov method. Furthermore, we introduce and prove
Lemma

3
, which provides the upper bound on the derivative
\left|\frac{d}{dt}z(t)\right|
, as discussed in the main text.

We begin by recalling the super-Krylov framework. Define the Hamiltonian
H
acting on
n
-qubits, with eigenvalue equation

H\ket{u}=\lambda\ket{u}.

(S43)

Set
N=2^{n}
and define
\mathcal{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})=\mathcal{M}_{N}
as the Hilbert space of
N\times N
complex matrices, equipped with the trace inner product

\bigl(A,B\bigr)_{\mathcal{M}_{N}}\coloneqq\text{Tr}\left(A^{\dagger}B\right),\quad\forall A,B\in{\mathcal{M}_{N}}.

(S44)

The super-Krylov method employs the Rayleigh-Ritz procedure find the minimum eigenvalue of the super-operator

\begin{split}\mathcal{J}:{\mathcal{M}_{N}}&\to{\mathcal{M}_{N}}\\
X&\mapsto\imath[H,X]\,.\end{split}

(S45)

Recall that
\mathcal{J}
has eigenvalue equation

\mathcal{J}\outerproduct{u_p}{u_q}=\imath(\lambda_{p}-\lambda_{q})\outerproduct{u_p}{u_q}

(S46)

for
p,q=0,...,N-1
. Denote the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{J}
by
\Delta_{j}\in\{\imath(\lambda_{p}-\lambda_{q})\}_{p,q=0}^{N-1}
, indexed in increasing order (with respect to the imaginary part), and the corresponding eigenoperators
X_{j}\in\{\outerproduct{u_p}{u_q}\}_{p,q=0}^{N-1}
. In particular, the eigenvalue of
\mathcal{J}
with smallest imaginary part and corresponding eigenoperator are given respectively by

\Delta_{0}\coloneqq\imath(\lambda_{0}-\lambda_{N-1}),\qquad X_{0}\coloneqq\ket{u_0}\bra{u_{N-1}}.

(S47)

Recall
the projection space
\mathcal{T}
spanned by a set of density matrices
\rho_{k}=\outerproduct{w_k}{w_k}
for some
\ket{w_k}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}&\coloneqq\text{span}\big\{\rho_{0},...,\rho_{m-1}\big\}\\
&=\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\alpha_{j}\rho_{j}\,\in{\mathcal{M}_{N}}:\,\,\,\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\right\}\end{split}

(S48)

We call
\{\rho_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m-1}
the
generating states
. We seek an approximate eigenpair
(\widetilde{\Delta},\widetilde{X})\in\imath\,\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{T}
of
\mathcal{J}
satisfying

\left(\mathcal{J}\widetilde{X}-\widetilde{\Delta}\widetilde{X}\right)\perp\mathcal{T}

(S49)

where orthogonality is defined with respect to
(\cdot,\cdot)_{{\mathcal{M}_{N}}}
. The constraint in (
S49
) is equivalent to setting

\text{Tr}\bigg(\big(\mathcal{J}\widetilde{X}-\widetilde{\Delta}\widetilde{X}\big)^{\dagger}\rho_{k}\bigg)=0

(S50)

for
k=0,...,m-1
. For
\widetilde{X}\in\mathcal{T}
we can write
\widetilde{X}=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}a_{j}\rho_{j}
for some
a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then (
S50
) becomes

\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\text{Tr}\bigg(\big(\mathcal{J}\rho_{j}\big)\rho_{k}\bigg)a_{j}=\widetilde{\Delta}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\text{Tr}\big(\rho_{j}\rho_{k}\big)\,a_{j}

(S51)

where we have used the fact that the operator
\mathcal{J}\widetilde{X}
is Hermitian. Expanding (
S51
) using the definition (
S45
) of
\mathcal{J}
, we obtain the
m\times m
projected eigenvalue problem

\mathbf{J}a=\widetilde{\Delta}\,\mathbf{R}a

(S52)

where

\begin{split}\mathbf{J}_{jk}=\imath\,\text{Tr}\big(\rho_{j}\,[H,\rho_{k}]\big),\quad\mathbf{R}_{jk}=\text{Tr}\big(\rho_{j}\rho_{k}\big).\end{split}

(S53)

The eigenvalue of (
S52
) with smallest imaginary part,
\widetilde{\Delta}_{0}
, serves as an estimate of
\Delta_{0}
.

We have just to choose the generating states
{\big\{\rho_{j}\big\}_{j=0}^{m-1}}
in (
S48
). Motivated by the subspace selection in standard KQD methods, which has been shown to yield rapid convergence for the extremal eigenvalues of
H
, we choose the states produced by powers of the time evolution operator
U(t)\coloneqq e^{-\imath Ht}
acting on some initial state. That is, we take the density matrices

\rho_{j}(t)=U^{j}(t)\rho\,U^{-j}(t),\quad j=0,...,m-1,

(S54)

where
\rho=\outerproduct{w}{w}
for some
\ket{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
, with

\begin{split}0\neq\gamma_{0}\coloneqq\text{Tr}(\rho X_{0})\end{split}

(S55)

or equivalently
\innerproduct{w}{u_0},\innerproduct{w}{u_N}\neq 0
. Under the choice (
S54
), the projected eigenvalue problem in (
S52
) becomes

\mathbf{J}(t)a=\widetilde{\Delta}\,\mathbf{R}(t)a

(S56)

where

\begin{split}\mathbf{J}_{jk}(t)&=\imath\,\text{Tr}\big(\rho_{j}(t)\,[H,\rho_{k}(t)]\big),\\
\mathbf{R}_{jk}(t)&=\text{Tr}\big(\rho_{j}(t)\rho_{k}(t)\big).\end{split}

(S57)

As with standard KQD methods, we fix the time parameter at some ‘optimal’ value
t=dt
and solve the resulting problem
(\mathbf{J},\mathbf{R})\coloneqq\bigl(\mathbf{J}(dt),\mathbf{R}(dt)\bigr)
.

Lemma 2
.

The entries of
\mathbf{R}(t)
have the equivalent representation

\mathbf{R}_{jk}(t)=|\langle w|U^{-j}(t)U^{k}(t)|w\rangle|^{2}\in[0,1].

(S58)

Furthermore, the entries of
\mathbf{J}(t)
satisfy

\mathbf{J}_{jk}(t)=\frac{1}{(j-k)}\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{R}_{jk}(t).

(S59)

Proof.

By the definition (
S54
) of
\rho_{j}(t)
and the cyclic property of the trace, the entries
\mathbf{R}_{jk}(t)
in (
S57
) can be written as

\begin{split}\mathbf{R}_{jk}(t)&=\text{Tr}\left(\rho_{j}(t)\rho_{k}(t)\right)\\
&=\text{Tr}\biggl(U^{j}(t)\outerproduct{w}{w}U^{-j}U^{k}(t)\outerproduct{w}{w}U^{-k}\biggr)\\
&=\bra{w}U^{-j}U^{k}(t)\ket{w}\overline{\bra{w}U^{-j}U^{k}(t)\ket{w}}\\
&=\left|\bra{w}U^{-j}U^{k}(t)\ket{w}\right|^{2}.\end{split}

(S60)

To obtain the relation in (
S59
), we directly differentiate the expression (
S57
) for
\mathbf{R}_{jk}(t)
. The product rule yields

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{R}_{jk}(t)=\imath\,\text{Tr}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{j}(t)\rho_{k}(t)\right)+\imath\,\text{Tr}\left(\rho_{j}(t)\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{k}(t)\right).

(S61)

The density matrices
\rho_{j}(t)
from (
S54
) satisfy the Von Neumann equation:

\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{j}(t)=-\imath j[H,\rho_{j}(t)].

(S62)

Inserting the expression (
S62
) back into (
S61
), we get

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{R}_{jk}(t)&=-\imath j\,\text{Tr}\left([H,\rho_{j}(t)]\rho_{k}(t)\right)-\imath k\,\text{Tr}\left(\rho_{j}(t)[H,\rho_{k}(t)]\right).\end{split}

(S63)

Notice that the first term in (
S63
) can be rearranged as

\textup{Tr}([H,\rho_{j}(t)]\rho_{k}(t))=-\textup{Tr}(\rho_{j}(t)[H,\rho_{k}(t)]).

(S64)

Thus (
S63
) becomes

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{R}_{jk}(t)=\imath(j-k)\,\text{Tr}\left(\rho_{j}(t)[H,\rho_{k}(t)]\right).

(S65)

Finally, we recall the expression (
S57
) for
\mathbf{J}_{jk}(t)
to obtain the desired result.
∎

Recall the definition of the function
z:[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
,

\begin{split}z(t)&\coloneqq|\langle w|U(t)|w\rangle|^{2}\\
&=|\langle w|e^{-\imath Ht}|w\rangle|^{2}.\end{split}

(S66)

We proceed to derive an upper bound on
\left|\frac{d}{dt}z(t)\right|
with respect to the system size
n
and the total number of qubit interactions, as per the main text.

Lemma 3
.

For any Hamiltonian
H
and for
z
as defined in (
S66
), we have

\left|\frac{d}{dt}z(t)\right|\leq 2m|\lambda_{N-1}|,\qquad\forall t\in\Omega.

(S67)

Furthermore, suppose the Hamiltonian
H
takes the form

H=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}(A_{ij}\sigma_{i}^{\tau}\sigma_{j}^{\tau}+B_{ij}\sigma_{i}^{\mu}\sigma_{j}^{\mu}+D_{ij}\sigma_{i}^{\alpha}\sigma_{j}^{\alpha})+\sum_{k\in V}C_{k}\sigma_{k}^{\nu}

(S68)

for some set of interactions (edges)
E
on the vertices
V
, and
\tau,\mu,\alpha,\nu\in\{x,y,z\}
. If
|A_{ij}|,|B_{ij}|,|D_{ij}|,|C_{k}|\leq 1
, then the most excited energy
\lambda_{N-1}
of
H
satisfies

|\lambda_{N-1}|\leq O(|E|+n).

(S69)

Proof.

From the definition of
z
in (
S66
), we have

z\big((k-j)t\big)=\mathbf{R}_{jk}(t),\qquad\frac{d}{dt}z(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{R}_{0k}\left(t/k\right).

(S70)

Using the expression (
S59
) for
\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{R}_{jk}(t)
, we can write the second equation in (
S70
) as

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}z(t)&=-\imath k\,\text{Tr}\big(\rho_{0}(t)\,[H,\rho_{k}(t)]\big)\\
&=-\imath k\,\text{Tr}\big(\rho\,[H,\rho_{k}(t)]\big),\end{split}

(S71)

where the second line follows from the fact that
\rho_{0}(t)=U^{0}(t)\rho\,U^{0}(t)=\rho
. A simple algebra gives

\begin{split}\left|\frac{d}{dt}z(t)\right|&=\bigg|-\imath k\,\text{Tr}\big(\rho\,[H,\rho_{k}(t)]\big)\bigg|\\
&=k\,\bigg|\text{Tr}\big(\rho\,[H,\rho_{k}(t)]\big)\bigg|\\
&=k\,\bigg|\text{Tr}\big([\rho,\rho_{k}(t)]\,H\big)\bigg|.\end{split}

(S72)

Define the
p
-Schatten norm of
A\in{\mathcal{M}_{N}}
by

\begin{split}\|A\|_{S_{p}}&\coloneqq\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\varsigma_{i}(A)^{p}\right)^{1/p}\quad\text{for}\quad p\in[1,\infty);\\
\|A\|_{S_{\infty}}&\coloneqq\varsigma_{N-1}(A),\end{split}

(S73)

where
\varsigma_{i}(A)
denotes the
i
-th lowest singular value of
A
. The Schatten norm satisfies the following Hölder-type inequality: For all indices
p,q\in[0,\infty]
with
1/p+1/q=1
,

\left|\text{Tr}(A^{\dagger}B)\right|\leq\|A\|_{S_{p}}\,\|B\|_{S_{q}},\qquad\forall A,B\in{\mathcal{M}_{N}}.

(S74)

Applying the inequality (
S74
) to (
S72
), we get

\begin{split}\left|\frac{d}{dt}z(t)\right|&\leq k\,\|[\rho,\rho_{k}(t)]\|_{S_{1}}\,\|H\|_{S_{\infty}}.\end{split}

(S75)

By the triangle inequality and sub-multiplicative property for
\|\cdot\|_{S_{p}}
, we can bound the first term in (
S75
) by

\begin{split}\|[\rho,\rho_{k}(t)]\|_{S_{1}}&=\|\rho\,\rho_{k}(t)-\rho_{k}(t)\,\rho\|_{S_{1}}\\
&\leq\|\rho\,\rho_{k}(t)\|_{S_{1}}+\|\rho_{k}(t)\,\rho\|_{S_{1}}\\
&\leq\|\rho\|_{S_{1}}\,\|\rho_{k}(t)\|_{S_{1}}+\|\rho_{k}(t)\|_{S_{1}}\,\|\rho\|_{S_{1}}.\end{split}

(S76)

Let
\lambda_{i}(\cdot)
denote the
i
-th lowest eigenvalue of
(\cdot)
. Then
\varsigma_{i}(\rho_{k}(t))=|\lambda_{i}(\rho_{k}(t))|
, since
\rho_{k}(t)
are Hermitian. Furthermore,
\rho_{k}(t)
are pure (rank-1) density matrices, so

\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}|\lambda_{i}(\rho_{k}(t))|=|\lambda_{N-1}(\rho_{k}(t))|=1,

(S77)

where the second equality follows from Tr
(\rho_{k}(t))=1
. Then, by the definition (
S73
) of
\|\cdot\|_{S_{1}}
,

\begin{split}\|\rho_{k}(t)\|_{S_{1}}=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\varsigma_{i}(\rho_{k}(t))=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}|\lambda_{i}(\rho_{k}(t))|=1.\end{split}

(S78)

Inserting the upper bound (
S78
) for
\|\rho_{k}(t)\|_{S_{1}}
back into (
S76
) yields

\begin{split}\|[\rho,\rho_{k}(t)]\|_{S_{1}}&\leq 2.\end{split}

(S79)

Thus, (
S75
) can be bounded by

\begin{split}\left|\frac{d}{dt}z(t)\right|&\leq 2k\,\|H\|_{S_{\infty}}\\
&\leq 2m\,|\lambda_{N-1}(H)|\end{split}

(S80)

where the second inequality follows from
\|H\|_{S_{\infty}}=\varsigma_{N-1}(H)=|\lambda_{N-1}|
, since
H
is Hermitian, and
k\leq m
.

Now, suppose the Hamiltonian
H
takes the form in (
S68
), i.e.,

H=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}(A_{ij}\sigma_{i}^{\tau}\sigma_{j}^{\tau}+B_{ij}\sigma_{i}^{\mu}\sigma_{j}^{\mu}+D_{ij}\sigma_{i}^{\alpha}\sigma_{j}^{\alpha})+\sum_{k\in V}C_{k}\sigma_{k}^{\nu}

(S81)

for
\tau,\mu,\alpha,\nu\in\{x,y,z\}
, where
\sigma^{x}_{i},\sigma^{y}_{i},\sigma^{z}_{i}
are the Pauli matrices acting on the
i
th site. By the triangle inequality and sub-multiplicative property for
\|\cdot\|_{S_{p}}
, the most excited energy
\lambda_{N-1}
of
H
in (
S81
) can be upper bounded as

\begin{split}|\lambda_{N-1}|&=\|H\|_{S_{\infty}}\\
&\leq\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\bigg(|A_{ij}|\,\|\sigma_{i}^{\tau}\|_{S_{\infty}}\|\sigma_{j}^{\tau}\|_{S_{\infty}}+|B_{ij}|\,\|\sigma_{i}^{\mu}\|_{S_{\infty}}\|\sigma_{j}^{\mu}\|_{S_{\infty}}+|D_{ij}|\,\|\sigma_{i}^{\alpha}\|_{S_{\infty}}\|\sigma_{j}^{\alpha}\|_{S_{\infty}}\bigg)+\sum_{k\in V}|C_{k}|\,\|\sigma_{k}^{\nu}\|_{S_{\infty}}.\end{split}

(S82)

The (Hermitian) Pauli matrices each have eigenvalues
\pm 1
, so
\|\sigma_{j}^{\nu}\|_{S_{\infty}}=|\lambda_{N-1}(\sigma_{j}^{\nu})|=1
. Then (
S82
) becomes

\|H\|_{S_{\infty}}\leq\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\bigg(|A_{ij}|+|B_{ij}|+|D_{ij}|\bigg)+\sum_{k\in V}|C_{k}|.

(S83)

Assume
|A_{ij}|,|B_{ij}|,|D_{ij}|,|C_{k}|\leq 1

\begin{split}\|H\|_{S_{\infty}}&\leq\sum_{(i,j)\in E}3+\sum_{k\in V}1\\
&=3|E|+|V|.\end{split}

(S84)

Recalling

S69

∎

\vec{w}\in\mathcal{V}
by

C\vec{w}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}(\phi_{0}\vec{e}_{0},\vec{w})_{\mathcal{V}}\\
\vdots\\
(\phi_{D-1}\vec{e}_{0},\vec{w})_{\mathcal{V}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\vec{w}_{0}(t_{0})\\
\vdots\\
\vec{w}_{0}(t_{D-1})\end{pmatrix}

(S93)

where the second equality follows since

(\phi_{s}\vec{e}_{0},\vec{w})_{\mathcal{V}}=\int_{\Omega}\delta(t-t_{s})\vec{e}_{0}^{T}\,\,\vec{w}(t)dt=\int_{\Omega}\delta(t-t_{s})\vec{w}_{0}(t)dt=\vec{w}_{0}(t_{s})

(S94)

for
s=0,...,D-1
. The measurements in (
S87
) can then be written as an operator equation for
\vec{x}
:

\vec{y}=C\vec{x}+\vec{\eta}

(S95)

where
\vec{\eta}\coloneqq(\eta_{0},...,\eta_{D-1})^{T}
. Thus, we have derived the state-space representation of
\vec{x}
:

\begin{split}L\vec{x}&=\vec{f}\\
\vec{y}&=C\vec{x}+\vec{\eta}\\
\vec{x}(0)&=\vec{x}_{in}.\end{split}

(S96)

We obtain an estimate
\widehat{\vec{x}}
of
\vec{x}
as the solution of the minimization problem:

\widehat{\vec{x}}\coloneqq\argmin_{\vec{w}\in\mathcal{D}(L)}\biggl(\|\vec{y}-C\vec{w}\|^{2}_{2}+\theta\|L\vec{w}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{V}}\biggr)

(S97)

for some regularization parameter
\theta>0
. The first component
\widehat{\vec{x}}_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
will serve as a function estimate of
\vec{x}_{0}\coloneqq z
and the second component
\widehat{\vec{x}}_{1}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
will serve as a function estimate of
\vec{x}_{1}(t)\coloneqq\frac{d}{dt}z(t)
.

The solution
\widehat{\vec{x}}
of (
S97
) admits the following interpretation. By definition of
C
and
L
in (
S93
) and (
S90
) respectively, the argument of the minimization problem (
S97
) can be expanded (at
\vec{w}=\widehat{\vec{x}}\,
) as

\displaystyle\big\|\vec{y}-C\widehat{\vec{x}}\,\big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{D-1}\big(\vec{y}_{s}-\widehat{\vec{x}}_{0}(t_{s})\big)^{2},

(S98)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\big\|L\widehat{\vec{x}}\,\big\|_{\mathcal{V}}^{2}&=\sum_{l=0}^{M-2}\int_{0}^{\tau}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\widehat{\vec{x}}_{l}(t)-\widehat{\vec{x}}_{l+1}(t)\right)^{2}dt+\int_{0}^{\tau}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\widehat{\vec{x}}_{M-1}(t)\right)^{2}dt.\end{split}

(S99)

For small
\theta
, the term (
S98
) dominates the argument of the minimization problem in (
S97
), and so we get
\big|y_{s}-\widehat{\vec{x}}_{0}(t_{s})\big|\approx 0
. This leads to a better fit of
\widehat{\vec{x}}
to the datapoints
\vec{y}
. In the limit
\theta\to 0
, this would result in over-fitting. For large
\theta
, the term (
S99
) dominates, giving us

\begin{split}\bigg\|\frac{d}{dt}\widehat{\vec{x}}_{l}(t)-\widehat{\vec{x}}_{l+1}(t)\bigg\|_{\mathcal{V}}\approx 0,\qquad\bigg\|\frac{d}{dt}\widehat{\vec{x}}_{M-1}(t)\bigg\|_{\mathcal{V}}\approx 0.\end{split}

(S100)

Informally, this ensures that the structure of
\widehat{\vec{x}}
loosely resembles that of
\vec{x}
in (
S88
). In the limit
\theta\to\infty
, this gives

\widehat{\vec{x}}_{l+1}(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\widehat{\vec{x}}_{l}(t)=\frac{d^{l}}{dt^{l}}\widehat{\vec{x}}_{0}(t)\,\,\Rightarrow\,\,\bigg\|\frac{d}{dt}\widehat{\vec{x}}_{M-1}(t)\bigg\|_{\mathcal{V}}=\bigg\|\frac{d^{M-1}}{dt^{M-1}}\widehat{\vec{x}}_{0}(t)\bigg\|_{\mathcal{V}}\to 0.

(S101)

Thus, the
(M-1)
-th derivative of
\widehat{\vec{x}}_{0}
is penalised, resulting in a smoother, under-fitted function estimate. In this sense, the parameter
M
controls the ‘smoothness’ of the function estimate
\widehat{\vec{x}}_{l}
.

Theorem

3
, which appears in the main text, provides a strategy for selecting the regularization parameter
\theta
. This allows us to compute an analytical expression for the resulting solution
\widehat{\vec{x}}
of (
S97
), and admits the notion of a worst-case estimation error on
\widehat{\vec{x}}
.

Theorem 3
.

Let the regularization parameter in (
S97
) take the form
\theta=\theta_{1}/\theta_{2}
where
{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}>0}
satisfy

\theta_{2}\|\vec{\eta}\,\|^{2}_{2}+\theta_{1}\big\|\vec{f}\,\big\|^{2}_{\mathcal{V}}\leq 1

(S102)

for
\vec{\eta}
and
\vec{f}
as given in (
S96
). Then the minimisation problem (
S97
) can be written equivalently as a minimax optimisation problem over a suitable bounding set
\mathcal{G}\subset\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V})
. The corresponding minimax solution
\widehat{\vec{x}}
and ‘minimax error’
\max_{\mathcal{G}}|\widehat{\vec{x}}_{j}(t)-\vec{x}_{j}(t)|
can each be expressed analytically as the solution of a coupled differential equation. Furthermore, in the limits
\|\vec{\eta}\,\|_{2}\to 0
and
D\to\infty
, the solution converges in the sense

\big\|\widehat{\vec{x}}-\vec{x}\big\|_{\mathcal{V}}\to 0.

(S103)

The proof of
Theorem

3
is structured as follows. For expository reasons, we start in
Section

II.1
by proving the convergence behaviour in (
S103
). In
Section

II.2
, we use the analysis in
Zhuk 2010
to show that, with the choice of
\theta
in (
S102
), the solution of the minimization problem (
S97
) can be derived as the solution of a ‘minimax’ optimization problem. Furthermore, we prove that the corresponding minimax solution and minimax error (i.e., the ‘worst-case error’) can each be expressed as the solution of a coupled differential equation. This provides us with a method of computing an analytical expression for each of the function estimates
\widehat{\vec{x}}_{0}(t)
and
\widehat{\vec{x}}_{1}(t)
, as well as the corresponding worst-case estimation errors.

II.1
Convergence of the function estimates

This subsection focuses on the proof of the convergence result (
S103
) in
Theorem

3
. Define the functional
Q:\mathcal{D}(L)\subseteq\mathcal{V}\to\,\mathbb{R}
by

Q(\vec{w})\coloneqq\|\vec{y}-C\vec{w}\|^{2}_{2}+\theta\|L\vec{w}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{V}}

(S104)

for
\theta>0
. By the definition (
S90
) of
L
, we can write the second term in (
S104
) as

L\vec{w}=\frac{d}{dt}\vec{w}(t)-A\vec{w}(t),\qquad\vec{w}(0)=\vec{x}_{in},

(S105)

for
\vec{w}\in\mathcal{D}(L)
. Define
\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}\in\mathcal{V}
as the ‘right-hand side’ of the operator equation (
S105
),

\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}\coloneqq L\vec{w}.

(S106)

Under the notation (
S106
), the relation (
S92
) for the state vector
\vec{x}
can be written as

L\vec{x}=\vec{f}_{\vec{x}}.

(S107)

Eq.

S106
has analytical solution

\vec{w}(t)=e^{At}\vec{x}_{in}+\int_{\Omega}e^{A(t-s)}\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}(s)ds.

(S108)

Inserting the expression (
S108
) for
\vec{w}
into (
S104
) yields

\begin{split}Q\big(\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}\big)&=\left\|\vec{y}-C\left(e^{At}\vec{x}_{in}\right)+C\left(\int_{\Omega}e^{A(t-s)}\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}(s)ds\right)\right\|^{2}_{2}+\theta\big\|\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}\big\|^{2}_{\mathcal{V}}.\end{split}

(S109)

Simplifying (
S109
), the
j
-th component of
C\left(\int_{\Omega}e^{A(t-s)}\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}(s)ds\right)
can be written as

\begin{split}C\left(\int_{\Omega}e^{A(t-s)}\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}(s)ds\right)_{j}&=\left(\phi_{j}(t)\vec{e}_{0},\int_{\Omega}e^{A(t-s)}f(s)ds\right)_{\mathcal{V}}\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\chi_{j}(s)\vec{e}_{0}^{\,T}e^{A(t_{j}-s)}f(s)ds\end{split}

(S110)

where
\chi_{j}(t)
is the shifted Heaviside function

\chi_{j}(t)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if}\,\,t\leq t_{j}\\
0\quad\text{if}\,\,t>t_{j}\end{cases}.

(S111)

Set

\vec{k}^{(j)}(t)\coloneqq\chi_{j}(t){e^{A^{T}(t_{j}-t)}}\vec{e}_{0}.

(S112)

Then (
S110
) can be written as

\begin{split}C\left(\int_{\Omega}e^{A(t-s)}\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}(s)ds\right)_{j}&=\int_{\Omega}\vec{k}^{(j)}(s)\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}(s)ds\\
&=\left(\vec{k}^{(j)},\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}\right)_{\mathcal{V}}.\end{split}

(S113)

Inserting (
S113
) back into (
S109
) and expanding out the norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
, we get

\begin{split}Q\big(\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}\big)&=\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}\left(\vec{y}_{j}-C\left(e^{At}\vec{x}_{in}\right)_{j}+\left(\vec{k}^{(j)},\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}\right)_{\mathcal{V}}\right)^{2}+\theta\big\|\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}\big\|^{2}_{\mathcal{V}}.\end{split}

(S114)

Analogous to (
S106
), define
\vec{f}_{\widehat{\vec{x}}}\coloneqq L\widehat{\vec{x}}
as the ‘right-hand side’ corresponding to the estimate
\widehat{\vec{x}}
. From (
S97
),
\widehat{\vec{x}}=\argmin_{\vec{w}\in\mathcal{D}(L)}Q(\vec{w})
, and so
\vec{f}_{\widehat{\vec{x}}}
can be obtained as the solution of the minimisation problem:

\vec{f}_{\widehat{\vec{x}}}\coloneqq\argmin_{\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}\in\mathcal{V}}Q\big(\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}\big).

(S115)

Minimising (
S114
) via direct differentiation,

\begin{split}0=\frac{d}{d\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}}Q\big(\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}\big)=-2\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}\left(\,\vec{y}_{j}-C\left(e^{At}\vec{x}_{in}\right)_{j}-\big(\vec{k}^{(j)},\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}\big)_{\mathcal{V}}\,\right)\vec{k}^{(j)}+2\theta\,\vec{f}_{\vec{w}}.\end{split}

(S116)

To keep our calculations concise, we set

\widetilde{\vec{y}}_{j}\coloneqq\vec{y}_{j}-C\left(e^{At}\vec{x}_{in}\right)_{j}.

(S117)

Inserting the definition (
S117
) into (
S116
) and rearranging,

\theta\vec{f}_{\widehat{\vec{x}}}=\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}\left(\,\widetilde{\vec{y}}_{j}-\big(\vec{k}^{(j)},\vec{f}_{\widehat{\vec{x}}}\big)_{\mathcal{V}}\,\right)\vec{k}^{(j)}.

(S118)

We have just to solve the problem (
S118
) for
\vec{f}_{\widehat{\vec{x}}}
. Consider the ansatz

\widehat{\vec{g}}_{\widehat{\vec{x}}}=\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}\beta_{j}\vec{k}^{(j)}

(S119)

for some
\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
. Using this ansatz, (
S118
) becomes

\theta\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}\beta_{j}\vec{k}^{(j)}=\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}\sum_{i=0}^{D-1}\left(\,\widetilde{\vec{y}}_{j}-\beta_{i}\big(\vec{k}^{(j)},\vec{k}^{(i)}\big)_{\mathcal{V}}\,\right)\vec{k}^{(j)}.

(S120)

Equating the coefficients of
\vec{k}^{(j)}
in (
S120
), we have

\theta\beta_{j}\vec{k}^{(j)}=\widetilde{\vec{y}}_{j}-\sum_{i=0}^{D-1}\beta_{i}\big(\vec{k}^{(j)},\vec{k}^{(i)}\big)_{\mathcal{V}}

(S121)

or equivalently, in matrix notation,

\left(\theta I+\mathcal{K}\right)\vec{\beta}=\widetilde{\vec{y}}

(S122)

where
\mathcal{K}
is the
D\times D
Gram matrix with entries
\mathcal{K}_{ij}\coloneqq\big(\vec{k}^{(i)},\vec{k}^{(j)}\big)_{\mathcal{V}}
and
\vec{\beta}\coloneqq(\beta_{0},...,\beta_{D-1})^{T}
. Recalling the expression (
S117
) for
\widetilde{\vec{y}}
, (
S122
) becomes

\begin{split}\left(\theta I+\mathcal{K}\right)\vec{\beta}&=\vec{y}-C\left(e^{At}\vec{x}_{in}\right)\\
&=C\vec{x}+\vec{\eta}-C\left(e^{At}\vec{x}_{in}\right)\\
&=C\left(\vec{x}-e^{At}\vec{x}_{in}\right)+\vec{\eta}.\end{split}

(S123)

Using the expression (
S108
) for
\vec{x}
and the relation (
S113
), we wrote (
S123
) as

\begin{split}\left(\theta I+\mathcal{K}\right)\vec{\beta}&=C\left(\int_{\Omega}e^{A(t-s)}\vec{f}_{\vec{x}}(s)ds\right)+\vec{\eta}\\
&=\bigg(\big(\vec{k}^{(0)},\vec{f}_{\vec{x}}\big)_{\mathcal{V}},...,\big(\vec{k}^{(D-1)},\vec{f}_{\vec{x}}\big)_{\mathcal{V}}\bigg)^{T}+\vec{\eta}\end{split}

(S124)

where
\vec{f}_{\vec{x}}
is the ‘true’ right-hand side in (
S107
). Consider the limit
\|\vec{\eta}\,\|_{2}\to 0
. By the definition (
S102
) of
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
, we can take
\theta_{2}\to\infty
provided
\theta_{2}\|\vec{\eta}\,\|_{2}^{2}\to 0
. Fix
\theta_{1}>0
such that (
S102
) is satisfied. Then
\theta=\theta_{1}/\theta_{2}\to 0
and (
S124
) becomes

\mathcal{K}\vec{\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}\left(\vec{k}^{(0)},\vec{f}_{\vec{x}}\right)_{\mathcal{V}}\\
\vdots\\
\left(\vec{k}^{(D-1)},\vec{f}_{\vec{x}}\right)_{\mathcal{V}}\end{pmatrix}.

(S125)

One can show via direct calculation that
\vec{\beta}
in (
S125
) can be written equivalently as the solution of the minimization problem

\vec{\beta}=\argmin_{\vec{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}\alpha_{j}\vec{k}^{(j)}-\vec{f}_{\vec{x}}\,\right\|_{\mathcal{V}}^{2}.

(S126)

Then, in the limit
D\to\infty
, since
\vec{k}^{(j)}
are linearly independent, the solution
\vec{f}_{\widehat{\vec{x}}}=\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}\beta_{j}\vec{k}^{(j)}
of (
S115
) satisfies

\left\|\vec{f}_{\widehat{\vec{x}}}-\vec{f}_{\vec{x}}\,\right\|_{\mathcal{V}}=\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\beta_{j}\vec{k}^{(j)}-\vec{f}_{\vec{x}}\,\right\|_{\mathcal{V}}\to 0.

(S127)

Thus,
\vec{f}_{\widehat{\vec{x}}}
converges to the ‘true’ right-hand side
\vec{f}_{\vec{x}}
as
\|\vec{\eta}\|_{2}\to 0
and
D\to\infty
. We have just to show that the corresponding estimate
\widehat{\vec{x}}
in
\vec{f}_{\widehat{\vec{x}}}=L\widehat{\vec{x}}
converges to the ‘true’ function
\vec{x}
. By (
S108
), the solution of
\vec{f}_{\widehat{\vec{x}}}=L\widehat{\vec{x}}
takes the form

\widehat{\vec{x}}(t)=e^{At}\vec{x}_{in}+\int_{\Omega}e^{A(t-s)}\vec{f}_{\widehat{\vec{x}}}(s)\,ds.

(S128)

Using (
S128
), the estimation error
\big\|\vec{x}-\widehat{\vec{x}}\big\|_{\mathcal{V}}
can be written as

\begin{split}\big\|\vec{x}-\widehat{\vec{x}}\big\|_{\mathcal{V}}&=\left\|e^{At}\vec{x}_{in}+\int_{\Omega}e^{A(t-s)}\vec{f}_{\vec{x}}(s)\,ds-e^{At}\vec{x}_{in}-\int_{\Omega}e^{A(t-s)}\vec{f}_{\widehat{\vec{x}}}(s)\,ds\right\|_{\mathcal{V}}\\
&=\left\|\int_{\Omega}e^{A(t-s)}\big(\vec{f}_{\vec{x}}(s)-\vec{f}_{\widehat{\vec{x}}}(s)\big)ds\right\|_{\mathcal{V}}.\end{split}

(S129)

For a measurable function
p:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and convex function
q:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, Jensen’s inequality states that

q\left(\int_{\Omega}p(s)ds\right)\leq\int_{\Omega}q\big(p(s)\big)ds.

(S130)

Note
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{V}}
is convex, so we apply Jensen’s inequality to (
S129
) to obtain

\begin{split}\big\|\vec{x}-\widehat{\vec{x}}\big\|_{\mathcal{V}}&\leq\int_{\Omega}\left\|e^{A(t-s)}\big(\vec{f}_{\vec{x}}(s)-\vec{f}_{\widehat{\vec{x}}}(s)\big)\right\|_{\mathcal{V}}\,\,ds\\
&\leq\int_{\Omega}\left\|e^{A(t-s)}\right\|_{\mathcal{V}}\,\,\left\|\vec{f}_{\vec{x}}-\vec{f}_{\widehat{\vec{x}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{V}}ds.\end{split}

(S131)

Take
D\to\infty
. By (
S127
),
\left\|\vec{f}_{\widehat{\vec{x}}}-\vec{f}_{\vec{x}}\,\right\|_{\mathcal{V}}\to 0
. Then, since
\left\|e^{A(t-s)}\right\|_{\mathcal{V}}<\infty
, we get

\big\|\vec{x}-\widehat{\vec{x}}\big\|_{\mathcal{V}}\to 0.

(S132)

Thus, we have obtained the desired result in (
S103
).

II.2
Minimax optimization problem

In this subsection, we prove that the solution of the minimisation problem (
S97
) can be written equivalently as the solution of a minimax problem over some suitable bounding set
\mathcal{G}\subset\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V})
, as per
Theorem

3
. We then introduce and prove
Theorem

3
, which shows that the minimax solution and corresponding minimax error can each be written as the solution of a coupled differential equation. This provides us with a means of computing an analytical expression for each of these quantities.

To keep our calculations concise, we use the notation
\vec{a}\mapsto a
if
\vec{a}\in\mathcal{V}
or
\vec{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
. For example,

\vec{x}\mapsto x,\quad\vec{y}\mapsto y,\quad\vec{f}\mapsto f,\quad\vec{\eta}\mapsto\eta,

(S133)

and the state-space equations in (
S96
) become

\begin{split}Lx&=f\\
y&=Cx+\eta\end{split}

(S134)

with initial condition
x_{in}=x(0)
.

As per
Theorem

3
, we assume a bound of the form

\theta_{2}\,\|{\eta}\|^{2}_{2}+\theta_{1}\,\big\|{f}\big\|^{2}_{\mathcal{V}}\leq 1

(S135)

for some
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}>0
. From (
S89
), we have

\big\|\vec{f}\,\big\|_{\mathcal{V}}=\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{d^{M-1}}{ds^{M-1}}z(s)\bigg)^{2}ds<\infty

(S136)

where the inequality follows since, by definition (
S85
),
z
is analytic over the bounded domain
\Omega=[0,m\,dt]
. We manifestly have
\|\eta\,\|_{2}<\infty
in practice, and so it is always possible to choose
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}>0
satisfying (
S135
). By (
S134
), the bound (
S135
) has equivalent representation

\theta_{2}\,\|y-C{x}\|^{2}_{2}+\theta_{1}\,\big\|Lx\big\|^{2}_{\mathcal{V}}\leq 1.

(S137)

Take
\mathcal{G}_{y}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
of all functions satisfying (
S137
) in the sense:

\mathcal{G}_{y}\coloneqq\bigg\{{w}\in\mathcal{V}:\,\,\theta_{2}\,\|y-C{w}\|^{2}_{2}+\theta_{1}\,\|L{w}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{V}}\leq 1\bigg\}.

(S138)

The space
\mathcal{G}_{y}\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V})
serves as the bounding space for the following minimax optimization problem. The element
\widehat{{x}}_{*}
is called a
minimax estimate
of
{x}
if, for all
{l}\in\mathcal{V}
,

\max_{{w}\in\mathcal{G}_{y}}\left|\big({l},\widehat{{x}}_{*}-{w}\big)_{\mathcal{V}}\right|=\min_{{q}\in\mathcal{G}_{y}}\max_{{w}\in\mathcal{G}_{y}}\left|\big({l},{q}-{w}\big)_{\mathcal{V}}\right|

(S139)

with corresponding
minimax error

\Upsilon_{\widehat{x}_{*}}({l}\,)\coloneqq\max_{w\in\mathcal{G}_{y}}\left|(\,{l},\widehat{x}-w\,)_{\mathcal{V}}\right|.

(S140)

The authors in
Zhuk 2010
showed that the solution
\widehat{x}_{*}
of (
S139
) can be written equivalently as

\widehat{x}_{*}=\argmin_{w\in\mathcal{D}(L)}\bigg(\theta_{2}\,\|y-C{w}\|^{2}_{2}+\theta_{1}\,\big\|Lw\big\|^{2}_{\mathcal{V}}\bigg).

(S141)

Since
\theta_{2}>0
, then (
S141
) has the equivalent representation

\widehat{x}_{*}=\argmin_{w\in\mathcal{D}(L)}\bigg(\|y-C{w}\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{\theta_{1}}{\theta_{2}}\,\big\|Lw\big\|^{2}_{\mathcal{V}}\bigg).

(S142)

Eq.

S142
implies
\widehat{x}_{*}=\widehat{x}
where
\widehat{x}
is the solution of (
S97
) with regularization parameter
\theta=\theta_{1}/\theta_{2}
.

The advantage of writing the solution
\widehat{x}
of the minimization problem (
S97
) as the solution
\widehat{x}_{*}
of the minimax problem (
S139
) is two-fold. Firstly, the minimax solution
\widehat{x}_{*}
can be written analytically as the solution of a coupled differential equation, thus providing a means of computing
\widehat{x}=\widehat{x}_{*}
. Secondly, the solution
\widehat{x}_{*}
admits a minimax error
\Upsilon_{\widehat{x}_{*}}
, which allows us to upper bound the worst-case error in the estimate
\widehat{x}=\widehat{x}_{*}
. Again, this minimax error can be expressed analytically as the solution of a coupled differential equation. These facts are encapsulated by the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

The minimax estimate
\widehat{{x}}_{*}
in (
S139
) forms part of the solution
(\widehat{x}_{*},b)\in\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}
of

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\widehat{x}_{*}(t)&=A\widehat{x}_{*}(t)+\frac{1}{\theta_{1}}e_{M-1}e_{M-1}^{T}b(t),\\
\frac{d}{dt}b(t)&=-A^{T}b(t)-\theta_{2}\sum_{s=0}^{D-1}\phi_{s}(t)e_{0}\left(y_{s}-(\phi_{s}e_{0},\widehat{x}_{*})_{\mathcal{V}}\right),\end{split}

(S143)

for
\widehat{{x}}_{*}(0)=x_{in}
and
{b}(T)=0
. Furthermore, for
l\in\mathcal{V}
, the corresponding minimax error (
S140
) can be written as

\Upsilon_{\widehat{x}_{*}}(l)=\sqrt{\left(l,\widehat{p}\right)_{\mathcal{V}}}

(S144)

where
\widehat{p}
forms part of the solution
(\widehat{p},g)\in\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}
of

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\widehat{p}(t)&=A\widehat{p}(t)+\frac{1}{q}{e}_{M-1}e_{M-1}^{T}g(t),\\
\frac{d}{dt}g(t)&=-A^{T}g(t)-l(t)+\theta_{2}\sum_{s=0}^{D-1}(\phi_{s}e_{0},\widehat{p})_{\mathcal{V}}\,\phi_{s}(t)\,e_{0},\end{split}

(S145)

for
\widehat{p}(0)=0
and
g(T)=0
.

The remainder of this subsection focuses on the proof of
Theorem

3
. By the definition (
S90
) of
L
, the first equation of (
S134
) can be expanded as

\frac{d}{dt}x(t)=Ax(t)+f(t).

(S146)

Let
\widetilde{x}\in\mathcal{V}
solve

\frac{d}{dt}\widetilde{x}(t)=A\widetilde{x}(t),\quad\widetilde{x}(0)=x_{in},

(S147)

and let
\overline{x}\in\mathcal{V}
solve

\frac{d}{dt}\overline{x}(t)=A\overline{x}(t)+f(t),\quad\overline{x}(0)=0.

(S148)

Then, by (
S146
),
x
can be written as

x=\widetilde{x}+\overline{x}.

(S149)

Finally denote the ‘measurements’ of
\overline{x}
by

\overline{y}\coloneqq C\overline{x}+\eta.

(S150)

The proof of
Theorem

3
is organised as follows. We first construct a minimax estimate
\widehat{\varphi}
of the state
\overline{x}
in (
S148
), which call we call the ‘homogeneous’ minimax estimate, since
\overline{x}(0)=0
. In
Lemmas

4
and
5
, we show that the estimate

\frac{|\widehat{\Delta}_{0}-\Delta_{0}|}{|\Delta_{0}|}\leq O\left(\frac{\omega^{2/3}+\left(1+\frac{\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{0}}{|\Delta_{0}|}\right)^{-2m}}{|\gamma_{0}|^{2}}\right)

(S199)

where
\Delta_{1}
is the eigenvalue of
\mathcal{J}
with second lowest imaginary part.

Before proceeding, we point out an important requirement for the convergence of the super-Krylov method. Suppose that the generating states
\{\rho_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m-1}
in (
S48
) are mutually orthogonal. That is, for all
j,k=0,...,m-1
,

\textup{Tr}(\rho_{j}\rho_{k})=0\quad\iff\quad\innerproduct{w_j}{w_k}=0.

(S200)

The entries of the projected matrix
\mathbf{J}
then become

\begin{split}\mathbf{J}_{jk}&=\imath\,\text{Tr}\big(\rho_{j}\,[H,\rho_{k}]\big)\\
&=2\,\textup{Im}\big(\underbrace{\bra{w_j}\ket{w_k}}_{=0}\bra{w_k}H\ket{w_j}\big)\\
&=0.\end{split}

(S201)

By the Courant-Fisher Theorem
Saad 2011
, the lowest eigenvalue
\widetilde{\Delta}_{0}
of
(\widetilde{\mathbf{J}},\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})
can be written as

\widetilde{\Delta}_{0}=\min_{a\in\mathbb{C}^{m}}\frac{a^{\dagger}\mathbf{J}a}{a^{\dagger}\mathbf{R}a}.

(S202)

But, if
\mathbf{J}=0_{m}
as in (
S201
), then (
S202
) becomes

\widetilde{\Delta}_{0}=\min_{a\in\mathbb{C}^{m}}\frac{a^{\dagger}0_{m}a}{a^{\dagger}\mathbf{R}a}=0.

(S203)

If the generating states
\rho_{j}
are non-orthogonal, then the Rayleigh quotient (
S202
) is non-trivial and the corresponding estimate
\widetilde{\Delta}_{0}
will converge to
\Delta_{0}
with respect to the Krylov
m
, as we will show. The following analysis assumes that the generating states are mutually
non-orthogonal
.

We show that projected matrices
(\mathbf{J},\mathbf{R})
can be obtained via a real-time KQD algorithm. This will allow us to call upon the existing error analysis for real-time KQD methods
Epperly et al. 2022
;
Kirby 2024
in order to derive the error bound in (
S199
). First, we provide some useful properties of the Kronecker product in the following lemma (see e.g.,
Horn and Johnson 1991
).

Lemma 7
.

Take
A,B,C,D\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
and define the column-major vectorisation operator
\text{vec}:\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}\to\mathbb{C}^{N^{2}}
,

\textup{vec}(A)\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}|j\rangle\otimes A|j\rangle.

(S204)

Also, define the Kronecker sum

A\oplus B\coloneqq A\otimes I+I\otimes B.

(S205)

Then:

1.

(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)=AC\otimes BD
.

2.

spec
(A\otimes B)=\left\{\lambda_{A}\lambda_{B}:\,\lambda_{A}\in\textup{spec}(A),\,\lambda_{B}\in\textup{spec}(B)\right\}
.

3.

\textup{vec}(ACB)=(B^{T}\otimes A)\,\textup{vec}(C)
.

4.

\textup{Tr}(A^{\dagger}B)=\textup{vec}(A)^{\dagger}\,\textup{vec}(B)
.

5.

For
\ket{x},\ket{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
,
vec
(|x\rangle\langle y|)=\overline{|y\rangle}\otimes|x\rangle
.

6.

e^{A}\otimes e^{B}=e^{A\oplus B}
.

We use the vec
(\cdot)
operator to map the super-Krylov projection method to a real-time KQD-type projection method. Recall the projection space
\mathcal{T}
from (
S48
),

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}&=\text{span}\big\{\rho_{0},...,\rho_{m-1}\big\}\\
&=\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\alpha_{j}\rho_{j}\,\in{\mathcal{M}_{N}}:\,\,\,\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\right\}\end{split}

(S206)

and recall the selection for the density matrices
\rho_{j}
in (
S54
),

\rho_{j}(t)=U^{j}(t)\rho\,U^{-j}(t),\quad j=0,...,m-1,

(S207)

where
\rho=\outerproduct{w}{w}
for some
\ket{w}
, with
\gamma_{0}\coloneqq\text{Tr}(\rho X_{0})\neq 0
. Define the vectorised projection space

T=\textup{vec}\,\mathcal{T}\coloneqq\textup{span }\biggl\{\textup{vec}\big(\rho_{j}(t)\big)\biggr\},\quad j=0,...,m-1.

(S208)

By
Lemma

7
, property 3, the vectors in the spanning set in (

\begin{split}\textup{vec}\big(\rho_{j}(t)\big)&=\textup{vec}\big(U^{j}(t)\rho\,U^{-j}(t)\big)\\
&=\bigg(\big(U^{-j}(t)\big)^{T}\otimes U^{j}(t)\bigg)\text{vec}(\rho)\\
&=\bigg(e^{ij\overline{H}t}\otimes e^{-ijHt}\bigg)\text{vec}(\rho)\end{split}

(S209)

where the last line uses the fact that
\big(U^{-j}(t)\big)^{T}=e^{\imath jH^{T}t}
and
H^{T}=\overline{H}
, since
H
is Hermitian. By
Lemma

7
, property 6, (
S209
) becomes

\begin{split}\textup{vec}\big(\rho_{j}(t)\big)=e^{ij\left(\overline{H}\oplus(-H)\right)t}\,\text{vec}(\rho).\end{split}

(S210)

Eq.

S210
implies that the vectorised projection space

in (
S208

\begin{split}J&\coloneqq\overline{H}\oplus(-H)\\
&=\overline{H}\otimes I-I\otimes H\end{split}

(S211)

applied to some initial state vec
(\rho)
. Looking closer at the operator

, we have the associated eigenvalue equation:

J\overline{|u_{q}\rangle}\otimes|u_{p}\rangle=(\lambda_{q}-\lambda_{p})\overline{|u_{q}\rangle}\otimes|u_{p}\rangle

(S212)

for
p,q=0,...,N-1
. Denote the eigenpairs of

by
(E,\ket{E})

\overline{|u_{q}\rangle}\otimes|u_{p}\rangle=\text{vec}(|u_{p}\rangle\langle u_{q}|).

(S213)

Thus, given an eigenvector estimate of

, the corresponding eigenoperator of
\mathcal{J}
can be estimated via (
S213
). The following Lemma connects the super-Krylov projection method for

to a projection method for the operator

.

Lemma 8
.

Recall the definitions of
J
and
T
from (
S211
) and (
S208
) respectively. Then, the Rayleigh-Ritz method applied to

with projection space

yields the

\imath\,\mathbf{J}(t)a=\widetilde{E}\,\mathbf{R}(t)a,

(S214)

where
\mathbf{J}(t)
and
\mathbf{R}(t)
are as given in (

Proof.

Denote

\ket{\varrho}\coloneqq\text{vec}(\rho)

(S215)

where

S207

Following the Rayleigh-Ritz procedure for

\left(J\widetilde{\ket{E}}-\widetilde{E}\widetilde{\ket{E}}\right)\perp T.

(S217)

Similar to standard real-time KQD methods

\check{\mathbf{J}}(t)b=\widetilde{E}\,\check{\mathbf{R}}(t)b

\begin{split}\check{\mathbf{J}}_{jk}(t)&=\bra{\varrho}e^{-\imath jJt}Je^{\imath kJt}\ket{\varrho},\\
\check{\mathbf{R}}_{jk}(t)&=\bra{\varrho}e^{-\imath jJt}e^{\imath kJt}\ket{\varrho}.\end{split}

We show that
\bigl(\check{\mathbf{J}}(t),\check{\mathbf{R}}(t)\bigr)=\bigl(\imath\,\mathbf{J}(t),\mathbf{R}(t)\bigr)
, where
\mathbf{J}(t)
and

are as given in (

First, focusing on
\check{\mathbf{R}}(t)
, since

\begin{split}e^{\imath kJt}&=e^{\imath k\left(\overline{H}\oplus(-H)\right)t}\\

&=\overline{U}^{k}(t)\otimes U^{k}(t).\end{split}

Then

\check{\mathbf{R}}_{jk}(t)=\bra{\varrho}\biggl(\overline{U}^{-j}(t)\otimes U^{-j}(t)\biggr)\biggl(\overline{U}^{k}(t)\otimes U^{k}(t)\biggr)\ket{\varrho}

Inserting the expression (
