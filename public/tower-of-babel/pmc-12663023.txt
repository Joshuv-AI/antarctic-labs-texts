Application of plasma cell-free metagenomic next-generation sequencing for the identification of Aspergillus fumigatus donor-derived infections among solid organ transplant recipients
Authors: Jordan K Mah, John I Hogan, Sonya Kothadia, Jeffrey E Keenan, Johnathan Berger, Manuela Carugati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA cluster of Aspergillus fumigatus donor-derived infections (DDI) was rapidly diagnosed using plasma metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) among solid organ transplant recipients. The heart recipient, experiencing marginal hemodynamics, underwent an endomyocardial biopsy, which was concerning for a fungal infection on histopathology. Plasma mNGS was performed, identifying A. fumigatus two days prior to conventional diagnostics. This timely diagnosis enabled prompt nephrectomies in the kidney recipients, who survived. This report represents the first published use of mNGS in the diagnosis of Aspergillus fumigatus DDI, highlighting the utility of this novel, underutilized assay for early diagnosis of donor-derived infections.Keywords: Metagenomic next-generation sequencing, Donor-derived infections, Solid organ transplantation, Invasive fungal infection, Aspergillus spp

Introduction

Solid organ transplantation (SOT) is a life-saving procedure for patients with organ failure; however, long transplant wait-times remain given organ scarcity [1]. Efforts to increase the donor pool include utilization of organs from “high-risk” donors, which carry potential risk for donor-derived infections (DDI) [2]. The benefits often outweigh this risk, especially for critically ill patients in dire need of transplantation [2]. Despite enhanced screening practices, DDI remain a significant threat to transplantation [2]. More specifically, fungal DDI are often underrecognized at time of organ procurement and associated with high morbidity and mortality [3]. Early diagnosis of fungal DDI is critical to inform appropriate management; however, conventional diagnostics are limited by modest sensitivity or long turn-around times [4]. We describe the novel application of plasma cell-free metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) for early detection of Aspergillus fumigatus DDI in SOT recipients from a donor with A. fumigatus endocarditis.

Case presentation

Donor

The donor was a 36-year-old undomiciled man with active substance use disorder, diabetes mellitus, and emphysema. He had no history of iatrogenic immunosuppression. He was found unresponsive in public and transported to hospital where imaging demonstrated multiple skull fractures, extensive intracranial hemorrhage, and trans-tentorial herniation. Echocardiography revealed normal heart valves and function. A thoracic computed tomography (CT) scan showed emphysematous changes, two small (≤5 mm) lung nodules, faint left lower lobe ground-glass opacities and tree-in-bud nodularities. Abdominal and pelvic imaging identified nonspecific, diffusely increased echogenicity of the kidneys, hepatomegaly, and heterogeneous appearance of the hepatic parenchyma. Renal biopsies demonstrated marked acute tubular injury. The patient developed transient fevers. Blood and urine cultures were negative, and broncho-alveolar lavage culture grew methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and Candida albicans. No fungal or mycobacterial cultures of the broncho-alveolar lavage were obtained. No gross abnormalities were noted during procurement of the heart and kidneys.

Heart transplant recipient

The heart transplant recipient was a 64-year-old male of Caucasian descent with type 2 diabetes and ischemic cardiomyopathy. He was hospitalized due to cardiogenic shock requiring mechanical circulatory support. His heart transplant surgery was uncomplicated; however, he required continued inotropic support post-surgery. On post-operative day (POD)#6, an endomyocardial biopsy was performed to rule out rejection. This revealed acute on a background of chronic neutrophilic inflammation with visible fungal elements (Fig. 1A/B). This suggested fungal endo-myocarditis. The organ procurement organization (OPO) was notified. The patient reported blurry vision and generalized fatigue. A CT head showed multifocal acute/subacute infarcts. A presumptive diagnosis of fungal endo-myocarditis with cerebral embolization was suspected. Amphotericin B 5 mg/kg IV Q24H and voriconazole 200 mg PO BID were started. The patient later developed severe mitral and tricuspid regurgitation, requiring deployment of veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation on POD#8. This was complicated by a nosocomial pneumonia caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa. On POD#9, a plasma sample was submitted for mNGS (Karius™) testing, resulting on POD#13 with detection of Aspergillus fumigatus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. This was shared with the OPO. On POD#15, blood cultures were positive for A. fumigatus (Fig. 1C/D). Serum galactomannan and (1,3)-β-D-glucan were also positive. Broad range fungal sequencing (28S rDNA and ITS rDNA) performed on the formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded endomyocardial biopsy revealed A. fumigatus. Details of microbiology testing are reported in Table 1. The patient developed bowel ischemia, possibly secondary to septic emboli, and passed away on POD#19.

Renal transplant recipient #1

The right kidney recipient was a 78-year-old man of African descent with membranous glomerulopathy, requiring dialysis before transplantation. His postoperative course was notable for delayed graft function (DGF), lingering abdominal discomfort, and persistent fatigue. Learning from the OPO of the cardiac recipient's probable fungal infection, the patient began micafungin treatment on POD#9. MRI imaging demonstrated innumerable micro-abscesses involving of the renal graft (Fig. 2A). Serum (1,3)-β-D-glucan was >500 pg/ml and serum galactomannan was 2.74. On POD#15, the patient underwent placement of an external iliac stent graft and resection of the renal allograft. Frank purulence was identified in the operative bed. Gross section of the allograft demonstrated innumerable micro-abscesses (Fig. 2B). Histopathology confirmed the presence of fungal elements (Fig. 2C/D). Cultures of all operative specimens grew A. fumigatus. The right kidney recipient was treated with micafungin 150 mg IV Q24H and voriconazole 200 mg PO BID, then isavuconazole 200 mg PO DIE to complete a 3-months course.

Renal transplant recipient #2

The left kidney recipient was a 50-year-old man of Hispanic descent with a familial cardiomyopathy and ESRD requiring hemodialysis. Post-operative course included DGF and surgical wound dehiscence requiring re-admission on POD#11. When clinicians were notified of a probable fungal infection in the donor, broad-spectrum antibiotics, including micafungin and isavuconazole were started. CT angiogram demonstrated a mycotic aneurysm of the renal allograft arterial anastomosis and a bilobed pseudoaneurysm along the graft's arterial vasculature (Fig. 3A/B). Serum (1,3)-β-D-glucan was >500 pg/ml and serum galactomannan was >5.83. On POD#13, the patient underwent placement of an external iliac stent graft and renal allograft explantation. Gross pathology demonstrated innumerable micro-abscesses (Fig. 3C). Histopathology confirmed innumerable fungal elements within the allograft. Multiple operative specimens grew A. fumigatus. Given strong epidemiological link with the A. fumigatus infection diagnosed in the heart recipient, no formal DNA fingerprinting of the A. fumigatus strains recovered in the kidney recipients were performed. The patient completed 4-weeks of micafungin 150 mg IV Q24H and isavuconazole 200 mg PO DIE and subsequently continued isavuconazole. The patient required additional surgeries for vascular complications with A. fumigatus growing from operative specimens. Ultimately, the patient recovered and was continued on lifelong isavuconazole.

Discussion

Fungal DDI are rare yet serious complications of SOT, with Aspergillus DDI associated with high rates of graft failure [3]. Our report describes an unusual A. fumigatus DDI and sheds light on several points critical for the transplant community. First the occurrence of invasive aspergillosis in an apparently immunocompetent donor. Second the application of plasma cell-free metagenomic next-generation sequencing for the identification of A. fumigatus in the recipient.

Invasive aspergillosis in an apparently immunocompetent donor

Invasive aspergillosis is typically encountered among immunocompromised hosts, especially in the setting of neutropenia and glucocorticoids use, and rarely among immunocompetent hosts, when challenged with severe viral lower respiratory tract illnesses [5]. While our donor lacked classic risk factors for invasive aspergillosis, his medical history was notable for pulmonary emphysema, a condition that has been increasingly associated with the occurrence of invasive aspergillosis [6]. We could hypothesize that the presence of diabetes mellitus and substance abuse disorder may have further augmented the risk for invasive aspergillosis of our donor, leading to a disseminated infection. Other routes of acquisition of A. fumigatus, for example contamination of the surgical field at the time of organs procurement or of the preservation fluid used for organs transport are possible. However, these seem unlikely in light of the background healing seen on endomyocardial biopsy, suggesting that the infection and injury predated the transplant.

Application of plasma cell-free mNGS for the identification of A. fumigatus DDI

We describe the first case of A. fumigatus DDI identified using mNGS. A search of English-language publications in Ovid MEDLINE using the terms “Aspergillus,” “Donor-derived infection,” and “Metagenomic next-generation sequencing” yielded no comparable cases.

Given the high morbidity associated with fungal DDI and the potential consequences for multiple organ recipients, mNGS was pursued for our heart transplant recipient. Its rapid turn-around time enabled an early diagnosis of A. fumigatus infection. Of note, identification of A. fumigatus via mNGS preceded identification of A. fumigatus in blood cultures by 48 hours (blood cultures are neither reliable nor recommended for the diagnosis of aspergillosis) and identification in paraffin-embedded endomyocardial biopsy samples by 96 hours. This timely identification allowed appropriate treatment of the heart recipient and contributing to the ultimate survival of the kidney recipients.

Early recognition and prompt treatment of fungal DDI are critical to improving transplant outcomes. However, conventional diagnostics (cytologic, histopathologic and culture-based techniques) suffer from poor sensitivity, lengthy turnaround times, and often require invasive sampling [4,7]. Histopathology alone may not provide accurate species-level identification, critical for selecting appropriate antifungal therapy [8]. Non-invasive fungal biomarkers, like serum galactomannan, demonstrate poor sensitivity (22–41 %) in SOT recipients and have cross-reactivity with multiple fungi [9].

Clinical mNGS is a hypothesis-free approach becoming increasingly available, which sequences all DNA and/or RNA in a clinical sample to detect potential pathogens. Studies of invasive fungal infections (IFI) in immunocompromised populations have shown mNGS enables early, non-invasive diagnosis and species level fungal identification [[10], [11], [12]]. Despite its high specificity, particularly for non-Aspergillus IFI and COVID-19 associated aspergillosis, mNGS has low sensitivity (44 %), limiting its use as a “rule-out” test [[12], [13], [14]].

Assessing the real-world performance of mNGS for infectious disease testing is challenging due to study heterogeneity, diverse clinical contexts of its application, and detection of pathogens missed by standard of-care testing [15]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that mNGS improves diagnostic yield over standard testing, positively influencing clinical management [15]. However, mNGS cannot reliably distinguish colonization from active infection and interpreting results in a clinical context can be difficult. Despite these limitations, several centers report the successful use of mNGS for difficult to diagnosis DDI, including vaccine-derived Yellow Fever virus and Scedosporium, where conventional diagnostics have failed [16,17].

This report highlights the potential value of mNGS in diagnosing fungal DDI. Prospective studies are needed to better define its role in early diagnosis of DDI and its impact on transplant outcomes.
