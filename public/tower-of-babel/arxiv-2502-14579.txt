Non-Hermitian linear perturbation to a Hamiltonian with a constant electromagnetic field and Hall conductivity Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Jorge A. Lizarraga, Kenan Uriostegui

Abstract
The stationary Schrödinger equation for an electron in a constant electromagnetic field with a non-Hermitian linear perturbation is studied. The wave function and the spectrum of the system are derived analytically, with the spectrum consisting of Landau levels modified by an additional term associated with the linear Stark effect, proportional to a complex constant λ \lambda . It is shown that this constant arises from an operator Π ^ \hat{\Pi} that commutes with the Hamiltonian, i.e., Π ^ \hat{\Pi} is a symmetry of the system. Finally, the Hall conductivity for the lowest Landau level is calculated, showing that it remains exactly equal to the inverse of Klitzing’s constant despite the perturbation.



I
Introduction

An important feature of quantum mechanic is the Hermiticity property of the Hamiltonian operator, since it guarantees the spectrum to be real value and also the conservation of probability, thus, it can be physically interpreted. This condition can be mathematically read as
\hat{H}=\hat{H}^{\dagger}
, meaning that the operator is self-adjoint. It is also known as Dirac Hermiticity condition. However, the Hermiticity condition is not the only one that leads to positive spectrum of the Hamiltonian, as discussed by Carl M. Bender and Stefan Boettcher, the so called parity-time symmetry (
{\cal PT}
-symmetry) also leads to a real spectrum of the system, regardless the Hermiticity of the Hamiltonian
Bender and Boettcher 1998
;
Bender 2005
;
Bender 2007
and time-dependent
{\cal PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics emerged shortly afterward, significantly expanding the scope of research in the field
Faria and Fring 2007
;
Gong and Wang 2013
;
Zhang, Wang, and Gong 2019
.
{\cal PT}
-symmetry is not the only interesting symmetry of non-Hermitian systems, in fact a complete study of symmetries for these systems has been made by Kawabata, Shiozaki, Ueda and Sato
Kawabata et al. 2019
showing that there are 38 symmetries in total which describe intrinsic non-Hermitian
topological phases, and recently three similarities shared among them have been pointed out
Montag and Kunst 2024
. Undoubtedly, quantum mechanics is enriched by the consideration of non-Hermitian Hamiltonians.

Experimentally, photonic systems with optical gains and loss of energy
Bender and Hook 2024
;
Ashida, Gong, and Ueda 2020
have shown to be of particular importance, as stated: "the capability to manipulate photonic eigenstates through optical gain and loss and non-reciprocal couplings provides a powerful toolbox for tailoring non-Hermitian Hamiltonians"
Feng et al. 2025
. In fact, the paraxial equation of electromagnetic wave propagation has the same mathematical form than Schrödinger equation with a complex potential
Feng, El-Ganainy, and Ge 2017
and a similar connection exists with the Helmholtz equation for optical cavities
Cao and Wiersig 2015
. Hence, photonics opened an avenue to explore non-Hermitian systems in both ways, experimentally and theoretically
Özdemir et al. 2019
;
Meng, Ang, and Lee 2024
. On the other hand, the use of non-Hermitian Hamiltonian has proven to be a useful tool to numerically analyze non-equilibrium systems, such as the transport properties of a chain with
{\cal PT}
-symmetry
Ortega et al. 2020
where is was shown that in states with broken
{\cal PT}
-symmetry density either accumulates within the system or the system becomes completely depleted, depending on whether the inflow or outflow exceeds the other. Even more, non-equilibrium Green functions method which relies on the non-Hermiticity of the Hamiltonian
Stegmann 2021
, is an excelent method to unviel the electronic transport properties of two dimensional nanosystems as graphene
García et al. 2024
;
Sánchez-Sánchez et al. 2024
or phosphorene
Betancur-Ocampo, Leyvraz, and Stegmann 2019
;
Betancur-Ocampo, Paredes-Rocha, and Stegmann 2020
. Another important feature of non-Hermitian systems is the emergence of exceptional points, which are degeneracies where the eigenvalues and their corresponding eigenvectors coalesce
Heiss 2004
. These points are of particular interest in the design of sensor devices due to their ability to tailor the response to perturbations
Wiersig 2020
and recently a new mathematical method to analyze the physics of non-Hermitian systems beyond exceptional points has been developed
Schomerus 2024
;
Bid and Schomerus 2024
.

These rapid advancements described make it worthwhile to analyze the effect of non-Hermiticity in well-known phenomena like the Integer quantum Hall effect (IQHE). IQHE is a phenomena that occurs when a perpendicular magnetic field is applied to a silicon
field-effect transistor forcing electron in the bulk of the sample to form closed orbits whereas electrons at the edge bounce
Klitzing, Dorda, and Pepper 1980
;
von Klitzing et al. 2020
. The Schrödinger equation of a particle in a magnetic field was solved by Landau and the spectrum of the system is called Landau’s levels
Landau and Lifshitz 2013
. The number of filled Landau levels determines the Hall conductivity, which has been proven to be quantized as
iq^{2}/h
where
i
is an integer number and
h/q^{2}
is the Klitzing’s constant such that
q
is the electron charge and
h
is the Plank’s constant. This phenomena is incredible robust to perturbations
Jeckelmann, Jeanneret, and Inglis 1997
, and it is attributed to the fact that the quantization of conductivity is topological protected since the integer
i
is a topological invariant, now known as Thouless-Kohmoto-Nightingale–den Nijs (TKNN) invariant which is analogous to Chern number
Thouless et al. 1982
. This mathematical analogy inspire the development of topological band theory for Hermitian Hamiltonians
Hasan and Kane 2010
;
Qi and Zhang 2011
;
Bernevig 2013
. However, attempts to generalize this theory to non-Hermitian Hamiltonian has been made
San-Jose et al. 2016
;
Lieu 2018
;
Zyuzin and Zyuzin 2018
;
Yao, Song, and Wang 2018
;
Zhou et al. 2018
;
Shen, Zhen, and Fu 2018
;
Yokomizo and Murakami 2019
and induced non-Hermitian perturbations on Weyl Hamiltonians has proven to preserved the topological charge and shown that the addition of gain and loss could lead to a new class of topological phase transition
Cerjan et al. 2018
. In contrast, the effects of non-Hermiticity on Hall conductivity have been studied by Philip, Hirsbrunner, and Gilbert, who proved that in the particular case of the non-Hermitian quantum anomalous Hall insulator, the Hall conductivity is no longer quantized despite of being categorized as a non-Hermitian Chern insulator
Philip, Hirsbrunner, and Gilbert 2018
. Hence, the effect of the non-Hermiticity on the Hall conductivity is not trivial to predict.

In this work an electron under a constant electromagnetic field with an imaginary linear perturbation is investigated. It is shown that is possible to solve the stationary Schrödinger equation analytically. The eigenvalues are, as expected, complex values such that they are proportional to the Landau’s levels plus an extra term which describes the linear Stark effect
Stark 1913
times a complex constant
\lambda
. Finally, for the ground state, the expected value of the system’s conductivity is calculated, showing that it is exactly equal to the inverse of Klitzing’s constant, implying that, for this state, the non-Hermitian linear perturbation has no effect on the conductivity.

II
Math and Equations

The unperturbed Hamiltonian is given in centimeter-gram-second (CGS) units by the expression

\hat{H}_{0}=\frac{1}{2m}\left(\hat{P}-\frac{q}{c}\vec{A}\right)^{2}+qU

(1)

where
m
and
q
are the electron mass and charge respectively,
c
is the speed of light,
\hat{P}=(\hat{p}_{x},\hat{p}_{y},\hat{p}_{z})=-i\hbar\nabla
is the linear momentum,
\vec{A}=(A_{x},A_{y},A_{z})
is the magnetic vector potential such that the magnetic field is given by its curl,
\vec{B}=\nabla\times\vec{A}
and
U=U(x,y,z)
is the electric field potential such that the electric field is given by
\vec{E}=-\nabla U
. Then the magnetic field is chosen to be described by the symmetric gauge

\vec{A}(x,y,z)=\frac{B}{2}(-y,x,0),

(2)

where
B
is the magnetic field intensity, and the electric field is chosen to be perpendicular to it and along the
x
direction, that is

U(x)=-q{\cal E}x,

(3)

where
{\cal E}
is the electric field intensity. Hence, substituting the expressions for the potentials in the Hamiltonian (
1
), expanding the squared terms and introducing the cyclotron frequency
\omega_{c}=qB/mc
, one can write the following expression

\hat{H}_{0}=\frac{1}{2m}\left(\hat{p}_{x}^{2}+\hat{p}_{y}^{2}-m\omega_{c}\hat{L}_{z}+\frac{m^{2}\hbar^{2}}{4}(x^{2}+y^{2})\right)-q{\cal E}x,

(4)

where the angular momentum
\hat{L}_{z}=x\hat{p}_{y}-y\hat{p}_{x}
was defined and for simplicity the analysis was restricted to the
(x,y)
plane. The above Hamiltonian will be perturbed by the potential
\hat{V}
such that

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{V}

(5)

where the potential is non-Hermitian and has a linear dependence respect the
y
coordinate

\hat{V}(y)=iq{\cal E}y.

(6)

Therefore, the system to be analyzed is written as

\hat{H}=\frac{1}{2m}\left(\hat{p}_{x}^{2}+\hat{p}_{y}^{2}-m\omega_{c}\hat{L}_{z}+\frac{m^{2}\hbar^{2}}{4}(x^{2}+y^{2})\right)-q{\cal E}(x-iy).

(7)

This perturbation makes the Hamiltonian to loss its Hermiticity property. To address this system, is useful to begin by changing the coordinates to the complex plane, such that
z=x+iy
y
z^{*}=x-iy
, and the Hamiltonian operator adopts the following form

\hat{H}=\frac{1}{2m}\left(-\hbar^{2}4\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{*}\partial z}-m\omega_{c}\hbar\left(z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-z^{*}\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{*}}\right)+\frac{m^{2}\omega_{c}^{2}}{4}zz^{*}\right)-q{\cal E}z^{*}.

(8)

Writing down the eigenvalue equation
\hat{H}\psi=E\psi
where
\psi=\psi(z,z^{*})
, and multiplying by
2m/\hbar^{2}

-4\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial z^{*}\partial z}-\frac{m\omega_{c}}{\hbar}\left(z\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z}-z^{*}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z^{*}}\right)+\frac{m^{2}\omega_{c}^{2}}{4\hbar^{2}}zz^{*}\psi-\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}q{\cal E}z^{*}\psi=\frac{2mE}{\hbar^{2}}\psi.

(9)

This equation can be simplified using a gaussian factor in the wave function

\psi(z,z^{*})=e^{-\alpha zz^{*}}\phi(z,z^{*}),\quad\alpha=\frac{m\omega_{c}}{4\hbar},

(10)

substituting in the equation (
9
), the following expression is obtained

-\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial z^{*}\partial z}+2\alpha z^{*}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z^{*}}-\frac{m}{2\hbar^{2}}q{\cal E}z^{*}\phi=\left(\frac{mE}{2\hbar^{2}}-\alpha\right)\phi.

(11)

The variables in this expression can be separated by proposing the function
\phi
to be the product of two functions wich depends only of their respective variable, that is,
\phi(z,z^{*})=f(z)g(z^{*})
, dividing the resulting expression by the factor
f\frac{\partial g}{\partial z^{*}}
and reordening, the following equality is obtained

\frac{1}{f}\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}=2\alpha z^{*}-\frac{m}{2\hbar^{2}}q{\cal E}z^{*}\frac{g}{\frac{\partial g}{\partial z^{*}}}-\left(\frac{mE}{2\hbar^{2}}-\alpha\right)\frac{g}{\frac{\partial g}{\partial z^{*}}}.

(12)

Since the functions
f
and
g
and its respective derivatives have domain and image in the complex plane, the above equality will be satisfied when it is equal to a complex constant
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Hence the following equations are obtained

\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}=\lambda f,

(13)

and,

(2\alpha z^{*}-\lambda)\frac{\partial g}{\partial z^{*}}-\frac{m}{2\hbar^{2}}q{\cal E}z^{*}g=\left(\frac{mE}{2\hbar^{2}}-\alpha\right)g.

(14)

Both equations are solvable analytically, the equation (
13
) gives the function

f(z)=e^{\lambda z},

(15)

and the equation (
14
) gives the solution

g(z^{*})=\exp\left(\frac{m}{2\hbar^{2}}\frac{q{\cal E}}{(2\alpha)^{2}}(2\alpha z^{*}-\lambda)\right)(2\alpha z^{*}-\lambda)^{\beta},

(16)

where
\beta
was defined as

\beta=\frac{m}{2\hbar^{2}}\frac{q{\cal E}}{(2\alpha)^{2}}\lambda+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\left(\frac{mE}{2\hbar^{2}}-\alpha\right).

(17)

The eigenvalues of the system are also obtainable from the the equation (
14
), by expressing the function
g(z^{*})
as

g(z^{*})=\exp\left(\frac{m}{2\hbar^{2}}\frac{q{\cal E}}{(2\alpha)^{2}}(2\alpha z^{*}-\lambda)\right)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}(z^{*})^{n}.

(18)

where
a_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
are constants, substituting it and rearranging, one obtain the equality

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(-\lambda\frac{m}{2\hbar^{2}}\frac{q{\cal E}}{2\alpha}a_{n}+2\alpha na_{n}-\lambda(n+1)a_{n+1}-\left(\frac{mE}{2\hbar^{2}}-\alpha\right)a_{n}\right)(z^{*})^{n}=0.

(19)

Hence, the coefficients
a_{n+1}
can be written in terms of its predecessor
a_{n}

a_{n+1}=-\frac{\frac{mE}{2\hbar^{2}}+\lambda\frac{m}{2\hbar^{2}}\frac{q{\cal E}}{2\alpha}-2\alpha n-\alpha}{\lambda(n+1)}a_{n}.

(20)

The asyntotic behavior of the coeficient is such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_{n}}=\frac{2\alpha}{\lambda},

(21)

so, for large
n
it will diverge. Cutting the serie for a given
n
, it leads to the energies of the form

E_{n}=\hbar\omega_{c}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)-2\frac{\hbar}{m\omega_{c}}q{\cal E}\lambda,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(22)

The first term of the above spectrum are the Landau’s levels
Landau and Lifshitz 2013
, the second term has a dependence on the complex constant
\lambda
and also a linear dependence on the electric field, therefore, the real part of this term causes the spectral lines splitting in a linear way, i.e., the real part of the second term represent the linear Stark effect
Stark 1913
. The eigenfunctions in cartesian coordinates are

\psi_{n}(x,y)=A\exp\left(-\alpha(x^{2}+y^{2})+\lambda(x+iy)+\frac{m}{2\hbar^{2}}\frac{q{\cal E}}{(2\alpha)^{2}}(2\alpha(x-iy)-\lambda)\right)(2\alpha(x-iy)-\lambda)^{n}

(23)

where
A
is the normalization constant. Before to continue with the calculation of the normalization constant, one must mention that the constant
\lambda
is a consequence of a symmetry of the system. Defining the operator

\hat{\Pi}=i(\hat{p}_{x}-\alpha y)+\hat{p}_{y}+\alpha x,

(24)

is straighforward to notice that it conmutes with the Hamiltonian (
7
),

[\hat{\Pi},\hat{H}]=0,

(25)

therefore, applying it to expression (
23
) one could see that an eigenvalue equation is satisfied such that
\lambda
are eigenvalues of the operator (
24
)

\hat{\Pi}\psi_{n}=\lambda\psi_{n}.

(26)

As usual, the normalization constant is calculated such that the inner product of the wave function is equal to the unity. The Hilbert space is the set of square-integrable functions on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, that is
\psi_{n}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, hence, the inner product conditions reads as

\braket{\psi_{n}|\psi_{n}}=\iint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\psi_{n}|^{2}dxdy=1.

(27)

Therefore, substituting the wave functions in the above definition and using the following equality

\left(x+iy-\frac{\lambda^{*}}{2\alpha}\right)\left(x-iy-\frac{\lambda}{2\alpha}\right)=\left(x-\frac{\text{Re}(\lambda)}{2\alpha}\right)^{2}+\left(y+\frac{\text{Im}(\lambda)}{2\alpha}\right)^{2}

(28)

such that
\lambda=\text{Re}(\lambda)+i\text{Im}(\lambda)
and simplify the phase one gets

\begin{split}1=&|A|^{2}(2\alpha)^{2n}\exp\left(\frac{|\lambda|^{2}}{2\alpha}\right)\times\\
&\iint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\exp\left(-2\alpha\left(x-\frac{\text{Re}(\lambda)}{2\alpha}\right)^{2}-2\alpha\left(y+\frac{\text{Im}(\lambda)}{2\alpha}\right)^{2}+\frac{mq{\cal E}}{2\alpha\hbar^{2}}\left(x-\frac{\text{Re}(\lambda)}{2\alpha}\right)\right)\left(\left(x-\frac{\text{Re}(\lambda)}{2\alpha}\right)^{2}+\left(y+\frac{\text{Im}(\lambda)}{2\alpha}\right)^{2}\right)^{n}dxdy.\end{split}

(29)

Then, changing the variables to

\xi_{x}=\sqrt{2\alpha}\left(x-\frac{\text{Re}(\lambda)}{2\alpha}\right)

(30)

and

\xi_{y}=\sqrt{2\alpha}\left(y+\frac{\text{Im}(\lambda)}{2\alpha}\right)

(31)

the above integral simplifies to

1=|A|^{2}(2\alpha)^{n-1}\exp\left(\frac{|\lambda|^{2}}{2\alpha}\right)\iint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\exp\left(-\xi_{x}^{2}-\xi_{y}^{2}+\frac{mq{\cal E}}{(2\alpha)^{3/2}\hbar^{2}}\xi_{x}\right)\left(\xi_{x}^{2}+\xi_{y}^{2}\right)^{n}d\xi_{x}d\xi_{y}.

(32)

Expressing the binomial factor as a sum

\left(a+b\right)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}a^{n-k}b^{k},

(33)

where the binomial coefficient is defined as

\binom{n}{k}=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!},

(34)

using the definition of the gamma function,

\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\xi^{2}}\xi^{k}d\xi=\frac{1}{2}((-1)^{k}+1)\Gamma\left(\frac{k+1}{2}\right),\quad\text{Re}(k)>-1

(35)

hence, the integral can be rewritten as

1=|A|^{2}(2\alpha)^{n-1}\exp\left(\frac{|\lambda|^{2}}{2\alpha}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\Gamma\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right)\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\xi_{x}^{2}+\frac{mq{\cal E}}{(2\alpha)^{3/2}\hbar^{2}}\xi_{x}\right)\xi_{x}^{2(n-k)}d\xi_{x}.

(36)

The exponential can be simplified by completing the square respect the variable
\xi_{x}

1=|A|^{2}(2\alpha)^{n-1}\exp\left(\frac{|\lambda|^{2}}{2\alpha}+\left(\frac{mq{\cal E}}{2(2\alpha)^{3/2}\hbar^{2}}\right)^{2}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\Gamma\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right)\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\left(\xi_{x}-\frac{mq{\cal E}}{2(2\alpha)^{3/2}\hbar^{2}}\right)^{2}\right)\xi_{x}^{2(n-k)}d\xi_{x}.

(37)

Once again, defining the new variable

\overline{\xi}_{x}=\xi_{x}-\frac{mq{\cal E}}{2(2\alpha)^{3/2}\hbar^{2}},

(38)

and using the expression Eq.(
33
) and the definition Eq.(
35
), the following expression is obtained

\begin{split}1=&|A|^{2}\frac{(2\alpha)^{n-1}}{2}\exp\left(\frac{|\lambda|^{2}}{2\alpha}+\left(\frac{mq{\cal E}}{2(2\alpha)^{3/2}\hbar^{2}}\right)^{2}\right)\times\\
&\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{2(n-k)}((-1)^{k^{\prime}}+1)\binom{n}{k}\binom{2(n-k)}{k^{\prime}}\left(\frac{mq{\cal E}}{2(2\alpha)^{3/2}\hbar^{2}}\right)^{k^{\prime}}\Gamma\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(n-k-\frac{k^{\prime}-1}{2}\right).\end{split}

(39)

Is important to note that the above expression is only well defined when
k^{\prime}
has an even value. Hence, the normalization constant,
A=A_{n}
, is

A_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{2\exp\left(-\frac{|\lambda|^{2}}{2\alpha}-\left(\frac{mq{\cal E}}{2(2\alpha)^{3/2}\hbar^{2}}\right)^{2}\right)}{(2\alpha)^{n-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{2(n-k)}((-1)^{k^{\prime}}+1)\binom{n}{k}\binom{2(n-k)}{k^{\prime}}\left(\frac{mq{\cal E}}{2(2\alpha)^{3/2}\hbar^{2}}\right)^{k^{\prime}}\Gamma\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(n-k-\frac{k^{\prime}-1}{2}\right)}},\quad k^{\prime}=\text{even number}.

(40)

III
Electric current of the ground state

The definition of the electric current for a given state,
\psi_{n}
, is

{\bf J}_{e}=\frac{iq\hbar}{2m}\left(\psi_{n}\nabla\psi_{n}^{*}-\psi_{n}^{*}\nabla\psi_{n}\right)-\frac{q^{2}}{mc}\vec{A}|\psi_{n}|^{2}.

(41)

Here, we are interested in the electric current due to a single particle in the lowest Landau level. The wave function for the ground state can be obtained by evaluating the expressions Eq.(
23
) and Eq.(
40
) at
n=0
,

\psi_{0}(x,y)=A_{0}\exp\left(-\alpha(x^{2}+y^{2})+\text{Re}(\lambda)x-\text{Im}(\lambda)y+\frac{mq{\cal E}}{4\hbar^{2}\alpha}\left(x-\frac{\text{Re}(\lambda)}{2\alpha}\right)+i\bigg\{\text{Re}(\lambda)y+\text{Im}(\lambda)x-\frac{mq{\cal E}}{4\hbar^{2}\alpha}\left(y+\frac{\text{Im}(\lambda)}{2\alpha}\right)\bigg\}\right),

(42)

where

A_{0}=\sqrt{\frac{2\alpha}{\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{|\lambda|^{2}}{2\alpha}-\left(\frac{mq{\cal E}}{4(2\alpha)^{3/2}\hbar^{2}}\right)^{2}\right).

(43)

This is the polar form of the wave function
\psi_{0}(r,\theta)=re^{i\theta}
, such that the radius and the angle are defined as

r(x,y)=A_{0}\exp\left(-\alpha(x^{2}+y^{2})+\text{Re}(\lambda)x-\text{Im}(\lambda)y+\frac{mq{\cal E}}{4\hbar^{2}\alpha}\left(x-\frac{\text{Re}(\lambda)}{2\alpha}\right)\right),

(44)

and

\theta(x,y)=\text{Re}(\lambda)y+\text{Im}(\lambda)x-\frac{mq{\cal E}}{4\hbar^{2}\alpha}\left(y+\frac{\text{Im}(\lambda)}{2\alpha}\right).

(45)

This particular form of the wave function lead to an expression for the electric current in terms of the angle, substituting the in the expression Eq.(
41
)

{\bf J}_{e}=\left(\frac{q\hbar}{m}\nabla\theta-\frac{q^{2}}{mc}\vec{A}\right)|\psi_{0}|^{2}.

(46)

Then, the electric current vector can be calculated to be

{\bf J}_{e}=\left(\frac{q\hbar}{m}\text{Im}(\lambda)+\frac{q\omega_{c}}{2}y,\frac{q\hbar}{m}\text{Re}(\lambda)-\frac{q^{2}{\cal E}}{m\omega_{c}}-\frac{q\omega_{c}}{2}x\right)|\psi_{0}|^{2}.

(47)

The expected value of the above vector is

\bra{\psi_{0}}{\bf J}_{e}\ket{\psi_{0}}=\left(\frac{q\hbar}{m}\text{Im}(\lambda)\braket{\psi_{0}^{2}|\psi_{0}^{2}}+\frac{q\omega_{c}}{2}\bra{\psi_{0}^{2}}y\ket{\psi_{0}^{2}},\frac{q\hbar}{m}\text{Re}(\lambda)\braket{\psi_{0}^{2}|\psi_{0}^{2}}-\frac{q^{2}{\cal E}}{m\omega_{c}}\braket{\psi_{0}^{2}|\psi_{0}^{2}}-\frac{q\omega_{c}}{2}\bra{\psi_{0}^{2}}x\ket{\psi_{0}^{2}}\right).

(48)

To solve the respective inner products, is useful to write down the expression for the probability density of the particles, using the previous change of variable, Eq.(
38
) and Eq.(
31
), one can write

\big|\psi_{0}(\xi_{x},\xi_{y})\big|^{2}=\frac{2\alpha}{\pi}\exp\left(-\xi_{x}^{2}-\xi_{y}^{2}\right),

(49)

which is a Gaussian wave function. Then, the integrals are straightforward solvable

\braket{\psi_{0}^{2}|\psi_{0}^{2}}=\iint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\psi_{n}|^{4}dxdy=\frac{\alpha}{\pi},

(50)

\bra{\psi_{0}^{2}}x\ket{\psi_{0}^{2}}=\iint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}x|\psi_{n}|^{4}dxdy=\frac{\text{Re}(\lambda)}{2\pi}+\frac{q{\cal E}}{2\pi\omega_{c}\hbar},

(51)

\bra{\psi_{0}^{2}}y\ket{\psi_{0}^{2}}=\iint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}y|\psi_{n}|^{4}dxdy=-\frac{\text{Im}(\lambda)}{2\pi},

(52)

substituting in the equality (
48
) and redefining Plank’s constant as
h=2\pi\hbar
, and knowing that the conductivity is defined as the electric current per unit of electric field,
\sigma={\bf J}_{e}/{\cal E}
, the expected value of the conductivity vector is

\braket{\sigma}=\bra{\psi_{0}}\frac{{\bf J}_{e}}{{\cal E}}\ket{\psi_{0}}=\left(0,-\frac{q^{2}}{h}\right).

(53)

The first coordinate of this vector is the longitudinal resistivity and the second one is the Hall conductivity. The above result reflects exactly what is expected to happen in the IQHE experiment, while the longitudinal resistivity vanishes the Hall conductivity is expected to be quantized in multiples of the inverse of the Klitzing’s constant, specifically for the ground state is exactly equal to
q^{2}/h
. The minus sign only indicate the directions where the electron is commuting.

Conclusions

The stationary Schrödinger equation for an electron in a constant perpendicular electromagnetic field, perturbed by a non-Hermitian linear potential, has been solved analytically. The spectrum of the system was found to match the Landau levels, with an additional term identifiable as the linear Stark effect having a complex factor
\lambda
which reflects the existence of a symmetry of the system. This factor corresponds to the eigenvalues of the non-Hermitian operator
\hat{\Pi}
, which commutes with the perturbed Hamiltonian. Furthermore, the Hall conductivity for the ground state was calculated, revealing that it remains exactly equal to inverse of the Klitzing’s constant, despite the non-Hermitian nature of the perturbation. These results emphasize the resilience of the Hall effect in the presence of non-Hermitian linear perturbation and open new possibilities for investigating the effects of non-Hermitian potentials in such systems.
