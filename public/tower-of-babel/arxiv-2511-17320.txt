Superconformal topological recursion
Nezhla Aghaei, Reinier Kramer, Nicolas Orantin, Kento Osuga

Abstract
. We investigate a supersymmetric generalisation of topological recursion from two perspectives: algebraic and geometric. The algebraic side concerns a recursive structure encoded in modules of a super Virasoro algebra, and the geometric counterpart is what we call superconformal topological recursion defined on a super Riemann surface. Superconformal topological recursion indicates that odd holomorphic one-forms on a super Riemann surface are related to zero modes of copies of the Clifford algebras, and it also provides a tool to study deformation of non-split super Riemann surfaces, e.g. certain families of super Riemann surfaces carrying odd parameters. On a super Riemann surface over a non-reduced base, the formalism is recursive not only in terms of pants-decomposition but also in terms of odd parameters in a suitable sense.



1.
Introduction

The main aim of the present article is to introduce the formalism of
superconformal topological recursion
and investigate its properties. The setting is fully superconformal in the sense that the initial data and the outputs are both geometric objects on a super Riemann surface, which by definition is a
1|1
-dimensional space with a superconformal structure. Superconformal topological recursion is closely related to modules of the
\mathcal{N}=1
super Virasoro algebra, providing a connection between two notions of superconformality, geometric and algebraic.

1.1.
Motivation and backgrounds

Topological recursion

[
EO07
]
is a universal recursive formalism which has applications in a variety of subjects in mathematics and physics. The set of initial data, called a
spectral curve

\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma_{0}}
, consists of a Riemann surface
\Sigma_{0}
and a few more geometric objects. The output is a sequence of multidifferentials
(\omega_{g,n})
labelled by a pair of two non-negative integers
(g,n)
, each of which is a symmetric meromorphic section of
K_{\Sigma_{0}}^{\boxtimes n}
. The defining formula of
\omega_{g,n}
admits an interpretation in terms of pants-decomposition, and several generalisations have been considered, e.g.
[
BE13
,
BS17
]
.

The range of applications is remarkably broad, well beyond the original scope in terms of matrix models
[
CE06
]
, and it is yet increasing over time. To give a few, topological recursion is strongly linked to intersection theory on moduli spaces of stable curves and cohomological field theories
[
NS14
,
DOSS14
,
BCGS25
]
, including a notable achievement known as
remodeling theorem
conjectured by
[
BKMP09
,
BKMP10
]
and proved in
[
EO15
,
FLZ20
]
. It also describes a recursive structure of Hurwitz numbers and integrable hierarchies
[
EMS11
,
ACEH20
]
recently recaptured in great generality by
[
ABDKS24
,
ABDKS24a
]
. Furthermore, topological recursion has been used as a tool of quantisation
[
BE17
,
Iwa20
,
EGMO24
,
BBKN25
]
creating a bridge with exact WKB analysis and associated Donaldson–Thomas invariants
[
IK22
]
.

In 2017, the notion of
Airy structures

[
KS18
,
ABCO24
]
was introduced. This can be thought of as an algebraic reformulation (and generalisation) of topological recursion. For an index set
I
and a vector space
V_{0}
, the formalism starts with an
Airy ideal

\mathcal{I}=(H_{i})_{i\in I}
, which is a set of differential operators
H_{i}
satisfying certain conditions, and it gives rise to a unique sequence of functions
F_{g,n}
on
V_{0}^{\otimes n}
. It is a reformulation in the sense that given a spectral curve
\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma_{0}}
, one can construct the corresponding Airy ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{S}}
, and for this Airy ideal, multidifferentials
\omega_{g,n}
on
\Sigma_{0}^{n}
are related to functions
F_{g,n}
on
V_{0}^{\otimes n}
by an explicit formula. We note that the converse is not always true, i.e. not all Airy ideals come from topological recursion.

A crucial point is that the Airy ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{S}}
related to a certain spectral curve
\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma_{0}}
can be derived also in terms of so-called twisted modules of the Virasoro algebra. In particular, differential operators
H_{i}
of the associated Airy ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{S}}
induce Virasoro constraints on the generating function of the functions
F_{g,n}
. As a consequence, in terms of Airy structures, one can understand a structural correspondence between topological recursion on a spectral curve
\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma_{0}}
and twisted modules of the Virasoro algebra. The perspective of Airy structures has triggered a series of programmes (e.g.
[
BBCCN18
,
BBCC24
,
BKS23
]
) connecting topological recursion with modules of
\mathcal{W}(\mathfrak{gl}_{r})
-algebras in general — it reduces to the Virasoro algebra when
r=2
. Furthermore, the technique of Airy structures has led to a different proof of the correspondence between topological recursion and certain Hurwitz numbers
[
CDO24
]
.

Besides
\mathcal{W}(\mathfrak{gl}_{r})
-algebras, the Virasoro algebra enjoys another algebraically natural extension, supersymmetric Virasoro algebras, which play a central role in superstring theory. Motivated by developments in topological recursion and Airy structures, one may ask the following questions:

•

Can we incorporate the
\mathcal{N}=1
super Virasoro algebra into topological recursion, defined on some super spectral curve?

•

Is such a supersymmetric recursion related to enumerative invariants of moduli spaces of super Riemann surfaces?

The first idea was put forward by a series of works
[
BO18
,
Bou+20
,
BO21
,
Osu22
]
. Building on their previous attempts, the present article brings major progress towards the first point, though the second point is to be investigated and we leave it to future work.

1.2.
Main results

Our aim poses significant problems. Topological recursion in the ‘ordinary’, i.e. non-super, setting relies on a lot of standard theory of Riemann surfaces, as developed over the last 160 or so years, from the original descriptions by Riemann as extensions of solutions of equations, Mittag–Leffler’s reconstruction of functions/forms from principal parts and the theory of homology and (de Rham/Betti) cohomology to theta functions and prime forms of
[
Fay73
,
Mum83
,
Mum84
]
. While many of these constructions have been adapted to super Riemann surfaces, in many cases this was done in a setting (namely purely Neveu–Schwarz super Riemann surfaces) that is too restrictive for our needs. The more general setting of super Riemann surfaces with Ramond divisor, which we require, has only gained more attention recently
[
Wit15
,
DP15
,
Wit19
,
DO23a
]
, and hence many tools have not yet been developed (or had not when we started this project).

Therefore, we take time and space to develop the theory of super Riemann surfaces we need. Most of these results were developed before, but this part of the paper does also contain new results. In the current phase of the research of super Riemann surfaces, giving a consistent definition itself can be considered as a part of our results. Among such, objects that deserve attentions are the super Givental pairing in
proposition

2.26
and the super quadratic Casimir operators defined in
definitions

2.29
and
2.31
, both defined on a super Riemann surface
\Sigma
. The former is necessary to establish a clear relation to the free fermion algebra. The latter shows how to construct a super analogue of quadratic differential from a super analogue of a differential on
\Sigma
– it is too naive to take a square in the super setting.

We then turn to the algebraic side and focus on combining two notions, super Airy structures due to
[
Bou+20
]
and partial Airy structures due to
[
BCJ22
]
, into what we call
partial super Airy structures
. The concept mostly remains similar to the ordinary setting, and there exists a sequence of associated functions
F_{g,n}
(unique up to some choices) on a super vector space
V
. Major differences are that the associated vector space
V
as well as differential operators are
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded in the supersymmetric sense, and that one needs extra data for uniqueness for the functions
F_{g,n}
. Because it involves new notions and new definitions which can be stated without ambiguities only in the main text we shall not precisely state our main theorem in this introduction. Nonetheless, one of our main theorems (
theorem

4.10
) can be briefly summarised as below.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists certain partial super Airy ideals
\mathcal{I}_{\rm STR}^{\,r}
whose generators are constructed by
r
copies of twisted modules of the
\mathcal{N}=1
super Virasoro algebra. Furthermore, the generating function of the functions
F_{g,n}
obey super Virasoro constraints.

Our next task is to translate algebraic objects
\mathcal{I}_{\rm STR}^{\,r}
and
F_{g,n}
into geometric ones on a super Riemann surface. To be more concrete, we define a
super spectral curve

\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}
which consists of a super Riemann surface
\Sigma
with a few more geometric objects carrying information about the partial super Airy structure
\mathcal{I}_{\rm STR}^{\,r}
. Then, we would like to define multidifferentials
\omega_{g,n}
on
\Sigma
from which one can recover the functions
F_{g,n}
of
\mathcal{I}_{\rm STR}^{\,r}
in a suitable manner. Again, a precise statement about our second main result (
theorem

5.9
) is hard to write here as it involves new notions, but it can be stated as below to address our main idea:

Theorem 1.2
.

Given a partial super Airy structure
\mathcal{I}_{\rm STR}^{\,r}
, there exists an associated super spectral curve
\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}
and multidifferentials
\omega_{g,n}
on
\Sigma
that encode information about the functions
F_{g,n}
associated to
\mathcal{I}_{\rm STR}^{\,r}
. Furthermore, the differentials
\omega_{g,n}
satisfy a set of constraints called
super loop equations
which are geometric counterparts of super Virasoro constraints.

Although
theorem

1.1
and
theorem

1.2
guarantee existence of such functions
F_{g,n}
or equivalently multidifferentials
\omega_{g,n}
, they do not say how to find them in practice. Indeed, topological recursion of
[
EO07
]
can be thought of as a way of solving ordinary loop equations. Thus, our final task is to provide a way of solving super loop equations by using the geometry of a super spectral curve
\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}
. To this end, it turns out that it is more convenient to introduce a new parameter
\gamma
which intuitively measures the degree of functions in odd parameters. As long as there are only finitely many independent odd parameters (which is our setting), we can expand
\omega_{g,n}
with respect to
\gamma
as
\omega_{g,n}=\sum_{k}\gamma^{k}\omega_{g,n}^{(k)}
– this truncates at finite
k
. Then, our third main statement (
theorem

6.5
) can be summarised as below:

Theorem 1.3
.

There exists an explicit formula to solve super loop equations for
\omega_{g,n}^{(k)}
. It is a recursion not only in terms of
2g-2+n
, but also, for fixed
(g,n)
, it is a recursion with respect to
k
. We call such a formalism that recursively constructs
\omega_{g,n}
the superconformal topological recursion.

Perhaps surprisingly, the recursion formula in
theorem

6.5
exhibits a similar structure to blobbed topological recursion
[
BS17
]
rather than the ordinary one. There is actually an intriguing reason why we encounter such a phenomenon. Algebraically, it originates from a generator
\Gamma_{0}
(called a zero mode) of the Clifford (free fermion) algebra satisfying
[\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{0}]=\hslash^{2}
where
[\cdot,\cdot]
refers to a super commutator. Geometrically, it is inherited from an odd element
d\vartheta_{0}
in
H^{1}(\Sigma)
satisfying
\langle d\vartheta_{0},d\vartheta_{0}\rangle=1
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the super Givental pairing. Such a self-conjugate element does not exist in the ordinary setting, and this causes us to take care of such holomorphic contributions upon solving super loop equations.

1.3.
Future directions

Since topological recursion has relations to many different areas of mathematics and several of these also have super analogues, a general question is whether these super analogues are ‘the same’ as ours, i.e. if the relations in the non-super setting also have analogues in the super setting. Let us give a few explicit cases of this as potential future directions.

1

There is a very extensive literature on different aspects on supersymmetry. We do not claim expertise most of these aspects, and as such may have missed relevant references.

•

The moduli spaces of super Riemann surfaces have been studied in e.g.

The super Weil–Petersson volumes of moduli spaces of super Riemann surfaces were studied via

(and

1.4.
Outline

In
section

2
, we introduce super Riemann surfaces with Ramond divisor. Most of the material in this section is not new, but we expect it not to be known to most of our audience. We tailor our presentation with applications to super topological recursion in mind, which means that we also deal with certain less standard concepts, such as symmetric multidifferentials. This section does contain some original material, e.g. the super Givental pairing in
proposition

2.26
and the (twisted) super quadratic Casimirs in
definitions

2.29
and
2.31
as mentioned above.

In
section

3
, we investigate a particular object that is essential for (super) topological recursion: the fundamental bidifferential of the second type. On an ordinary Riemann surface, this object encodes all holomorphic one-forms and their periods, and also yields information on meromorphic forms. An analogous story holds in the super setting, but the extension is not straightforward. We will review such subtleties and study its properties.

Section

4
recalls super Airy structures and generalises this theory. For this purpose, we will highlight differences from ordinary Airy structures to motivate the necessity of defining partial super Airy structures. We then show (
theorem

4.10
) that partial super Airy structures of our interests can be obtained by twisted modules of the
\mathcal{N}=1
super Virasoro algebra. We will also explore new phenomena that only appear in the super setting, e.g. relaxing constraints due to nilpotent parameters.

In
section

5
, we translate the algebraic theory of partial super Airy structures to the geometric counterpart, i.e. loop equations among multidifferentials on a super spectral curve. To be more concrete, we derive loop equations from the associated super Virasoro constraints in
theorem

5.9
. We find that compact super spectral curves yield extra global constraints which imply that the corresponding Airy ideal is no longer just generated by direct sums of the Airy ideals at their ramification loci. We overcome this issue by finding a set of extra algebraic constraints that correctly captures such a global geometric feature.

Section

6
contains the actual formula of superconformal topological recursion, i.e. a recursive way to solve super loop equations. Due to the indeterminacy of the partial Airy structures we started with, we require some choice of holomorphic parts, unlike the ordinary topological recursion but rather analogous to blobbed topological recursion. We also find that both the naive and the elegant super analogue of the derivation of topological recursion do not give rise to a useful formula, so we introduce an
ad hoc
formula instead to make it work with a secondary recursion.

Section

7
contains two examples of super spectral curves, namely the super versions of the Airy and Weber curves. These examples are explained in some detail, and they are used to highlight interesting features of superconformal topological recursion. We present a piece of evidence that the variational formula also holds in the super setting, which by itself is an interesting observation in terms of deformation of super Riemann surfaces.

Notation

•

Since this paper is concerned with a super analogue of non-super structures, we will frequently use the word ‘ordinary’ to refer to the non-super version.

•

We use the standard notations for super algebra and super spaces. For an algebraic superobject
V
(a super vector space, superring, &c.), we write
V^{0}
for its even and
V^{1}
for its odd part. In order to avoid confusion with other meanings of even and odd (e.g. even and odd integers), we occasionally use another common terminology:

•

Following
[

]
, we will consider the super grading and the cohomological grading to be independent. That is, forms
\omega,\tau
have a
parity
, a
\mathbbm{Z}_{2}
-valued super grading
|\omega|
, as well as a
\mathbbm{Z}
-valued cohomological grading
\deg\omega
, and if we take their wedge product,

(1.1)

\omega\wedge\tau=(-1)^{\deg\omega\deg\tau+|\omega||\tau|}\tau\wedge\omega\,.

For instance,
dz\cdot\vartheta=\vartheta\cdot dz
while
d\vartheta\cdot\vartheta=-\vartheta\cdot d\vartheta
for even

and odd
\vartheta
, and
d\vartheta\wedge d\vartheta
is symmetric and non-zero.

•

Derivatives act from the left, so
\partial(ab)=(\partial a)b+(-1)^{|\partial||a|}a(\partial b)
. The contraction between a form
da

\partial
is given via
da(\partial)=(-1)^{|\partial||a|}(\partial a)
. This is different from
[

]
.

•

When considering forms on powers of a space, as usual in the topological recursion formalism, we consider the cohomological grading in each copy of the space to be independent, as well as independent of the single super grading. That is, forms on a space
\Sigma^{n}
have a grading in
\mathbbm{Z}^{n}\times\mathbbm{Z}/2\mathbbm{Z}
. See
section

2.3.5
for the meaning of symmetric forms with these gradings.

•

When we consider a Lie bracket
[\cdot,\cdot]
, it is always a super Lie bracket. That is, it is either commutator or anti-commutator, depending on the parities of arguments.

•

We will often denote superconformal coordinates, e.g.
(x\,|\,\varphi)
, by blackboard bold letters such as
\mathbbm{x}
. The superconformal analogue of non-super objects may receive the same treatment, e.g. the superconformal fundamental bidifferential will be denoted

•

For a set
I=\{i_{1},...,i_{n}\}
and objects

, we write their tuple as

2.
Super Riemann surfaces

In this section, we review and introduce geometric objects in the super setting. Our primary interests are in differentials and multidifferentials that play fundamental roles in superconformal topological recursion. For a good introduction into super Riemann surfaces, which we used as a basis for this section, see
[
Wit19
]
.

2.1.
Super Riemann surfaces

We work in the framework of supergeometry, i.e. locally ringed spaces whose local models are open subspaces of

(2.1)

\mathbbm{C}^{p|q}\coloneqq(\mathbbm{C}^{p},\bigwedge\mathcal{O}^{q}_{\mathbbm{C}^{p}})\,,

with transition maps preserving
parity
, i.e. the
\mathbbm{Z}_{2}
grading induced by putting
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbbm{C}^{p}}^{q}
in degree
1
. We may take either the analytic or the Zariski topology. We will not indicate which choice we make unless it affects the results given. For a thorough introduction into superalgebra and supergeometry, see
[
Ber87
]
. For a recent introduction into supergeometry in the algebro-geometric setting, see
[
BHP23
]
. For a modern introduction in the differential-geometric setting focused more on super Riemann surfaces, see
[
Keß19
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
super Riemann surface

\Sigma
over a base complex supermanifold
T
is a
(1|1)
-dimensional complex supermanifold over
T
, together with the additional structure of a completely non-integrable distribution
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma/T}
, i.e. there is a short exact sequence

(2.2)

0\rightarrow\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma/T}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}^{2}\rightarrow 0\,.

This means that for any non-vanishing local section
\partial
of
\mathcal{D}
,
[\partial,\partial]
is linearly independent of
\partial
at any point. The distribution
\mathcal{D}
is called the
superconformal structure
of
\Sigma

Every super Riemann surface
\Sigma
admits an underlying reduced Riemann surface, which we often denote by
\Sigma_{\textup{red}}
, and which is naturally a subspace of
\Sigma
(although of course not a sub-super Riemann surface). Note that not every
(1|1)
-dimensional supermanifold is a super Riemann surface, unlike the ordinary case. This makes the study of super Riemann surfaces and their moduli interesting and at the same time complicated.

To understand super Riemann surfaces, or super geometry in general, it is sometimes useful to first start from the reduced underlying space, and a natural question is how to extend. For this, we need the following definition.

Definition 2.2
.

A super space
S
over another super base
T
is
projected
if there is a projection map
S\rightarrow S_{\textup{red}}
which is left inverse to the inclusion
S_{\textup{red}}\rightarrow S
.

Such a super space
S/T
is
split
if it is projected, and
S
is isomorphic to the total space of a parity-reversed vector bundle over
S_{\textup{red}}
.

In the smooth category, every super manifold is equivalent to a split one
[
Bat79
]
. However, this is not true in the algebraic category if the base
T
is not equal to its reduced subspace, and this is in particular also the case for super Riemann surfaces: moduli spaces of super Riemann surfaces are not projected for
g\geq 5

[
DW15
]
(and not for
g\geq 5r+1\geq 6
in presence of Ramond punctures, introduced below
[
DO23
]
). Therefore, it is important to explicitly develop the relative theory (i.e. theory over a base
T
), because certain relevant super phenomena only occur in this setting. In practice, working over a base
T
amounts to considering families of super Riemann surfaces. We shall always consider super Riemann surfaces over a base
T
. In order to shorten the notations, however, this will be implicit in the following of this article.

On such a super Riemann surface, there exist specific coordinates called superconformal coordinates.

Definition 2.3
.

A pair of coordinates
\mathbbm{z}=(z|\vartheta)
is said to be
superconformal
if

(2.3)

D_{\vartheta}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\vartheta}+\vartheta\frac{\partial}{\partial z}

is a local generator of
\mathcal{D}
.

One can easily check that in superconformal coordinates
[D_{\vartheta},D_{\vartheta}]=\partial_{z}
, which is linearly independent of
D_{\vartheta}
.

Dually, we may consider the short exact sequence

(2.4)

0\rightarrow\mathcal{D}^{-2}\rightarrow\Omega^{1}_{\Sigma}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}^{-1}\rightarrow 0\,.

In superconformal coordinates, the subbundle of
\Omega^{1}_{\Sigma}
isomorphic to
\mathcal{D}^{-2}
is generated by
\varpi_{\vartheta}=dz-d\vartheta\cdot\vartheta
where one can check, with care on sign conventions, that
\varpi_{\vartheta}(D_{\vartheta})=0
. Using this notation, we see that the exterior derivative on
\Sigma
can be written as

(2.5)

d=dz\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+d\vartheta\frac{\partial}{\partial\vartheta}=\varpi_{\vartheta}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+d\vartheta D_{\vartheta}=\varpi_{\vartheta}D_{\vartheta}^{2}+d\vartheta D_{\vartheta}\,.

Example 2.4
.

The simplest compact super Riemann surface is
\mathbbm{P}^{1|1}
, which is the quotient of
\mathbbm{C}^{2|1}\setminus\{(0,0|\varepsilon)\,,\;\varepsilon\in\mathbb{C}^{0|1}\}
under the
\mathbbm{C}^{*}
-action
c\cdot(u,v|\varepsilon)=(cu,cv|c\varepsilon)
. Outside the locus
v=0
, the coordinates
(z=\frac{u}{v}|\vartheta=\frac{\varepsilon}{v})
are well-defined. Furthermore, there is a natural anti-symmetric bilinear form on
\mathbbm{C}^{2|1}
(unique up to
\mathop{\textup{PGL}}(2|1)
-action):

(2.6)

\langle(u_{1},v_{1}|\varepsilon_{1}),(u_{2},v_{2}|\varepsilon_{2})\rangle=u_{1}v_{2}-u_{2}v_{1}-\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}\,.

This bilinear form induces a one-form
\varpi_{\vartheta}
by specialisation
(u_{1},v_{1}|\varepsilon_{1})=(u_{2},v_{2}|\varepsilon_{2})=(z,1|\vartheta)
:

(2.7)

\varpi_{\vartheta}\coloneqq d_{1}\langle(u_{1},v_{1}|\varepsilon_{1}),(u_{2},v_{2}|\varepsilon_{2})\rangle|=dz-d\vartheta\cdot\vartheta\,.

This defines a (dual) superconformal structure
\mathcal{D}^{-2}
on
\mathbbm{P}^{1|1}
making the coordinates
\mathbbm{z}=(z|\vartheta)
superconformal.

It turns out that a superconformal structure is a severe constraint. E.g. a ramified cover of a super Riemann surface cannot be given a compatible superconformal structure in general. Thus, we need a more general setup, allowing for the superconformality to break down in a controlled manner. A reasonable idea is to introduce a(n even) divisor
2
2

2

An even divisor is of codimension
1|0
.

\mathcal{R}\subset\Sigma
, called the Ramond divisor, along which the superconformal structure degenerates.

Definition 2.5
.

A
super Riemann surface
\Sigma
with a Ramond divisor
\mathcal{R}
is a
(1|1)
-dimensional complex supermanifold together with a subbundle
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}
such that there is a short exact sequence

(2.8)

0\rightarrow\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}^{2}(\mathcal{R})\rightarrow 0\,.

Generally,
\mathcal{R}
is assumed to be reduced, i.e. all components have multiplicity one. Its components are called
Ramond punctures
(R). Points outside the support of
\mathcal{R}
may be called
Neveu–Schwarz points
(NS).

The idea behind this definition is that if
\partial
is a local generator of
\mathcal{D}
, then
[\partial,\partial]
is proportional to
\partial
along
\mathcal{R}
, which implies the breakdown of the complete non-integrability along
\mathcal{R}
. As a consequence,
\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}/\mathcal{D}
is actually generated by
[\partial,\partial]/z
, where
z

2.2.

2.3.1.
Berezinian

The first important geometric structure on
\Sigma

Berezinian bundle

\omega_{\Sigma/T}=\Ber(\Sigma/T)=\Ber(\Omega^{1}_{\Sigma/T})
, which is the super analogue of the canonical bundle – the Berezinian being the super analogue of the determinant. For readers unfamiliar with this notions, let us recall that for a linear automorphism
L
of a super vector space
V

L
as a block matrix with respect to the grading of
V
as

(2.32)

L=\begin{pmatrix}A&\Gamma\\
\Delta&B\end{pmatrix}

and

(2.33)

\Ber L=\det A\det(B-\Delta A^{-1}\Gamma)^{-1}\,.

Given local superconformal coordinates
\mathbbm{z}=(z\,|\,\vartheta)
, the Berezinian has a local section
[dz|d\vartheta]
; given other superconformal coordinates
\mathbbm{w}=(w\,|\,\psi)
, the transition function is

(2.34)

[dw|d\psi]=[dz|d\vartheta]\Ber\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{z}w&\partial_{z}\psi\\
\partial_{\vartheta}w&\partial_{\vartheta}\psi\end{pmatrix}\,=[dz|d\vartheta](D_{\vartheta}\psi).

The second equality explicitly shows that, for a SRS, there is an isomorphism
\Ber(\Sigma)\cong\mathcal{D}^{-1}
, which is given in local coordinates by
[dz|d\vartheta]\mapstochar\rightarrow[d\vartheta]
.

In the presence of a Ramond divisor, however, the Berezinian becomes
\omega_{\Sigma/T}\cong\mathcal{D}^{-1}(-\mathcal{R})\cong\mathcal{Q}(-\mathcal{R})
, with the isomorphism given locally by
[dz|d\vartheta]\mapstochar\rightarrow[d\vartheta]z
. One can check this by explicitly computing the Berezinian for R-R superconformal transformation, or see
[
Wit15
]
for more details.

2.3.2.
Closed forms

One thing to note regarding differential forms on super Riemann surfaces is that there exist non-trivial forms of arbitrary order, unlike the ordinary case. Namely, if
\vartheta
is a local odd coordinate, then
(d\vartheta)^{k}
is non-vanishing for any

. In particular, this means that one-forms are not necessarily closed. For our application, we are interested in closed holomorphic/meromorphic one-forms, as we want to calculate periods and residues.

There are different ways to think about closed one-forms on super Riemann surfaces. One is the extended Berezinian; this is the point of view taken in
[

]

where the first map is an inclusion while the last map is restriction (and

in

2.3.4.
Meromorphic differentials and super-Givental pairing

Let us give a local description of the space of residueless meromorphic

Proposition 2.26
.

Consider the algebra
V=\mathbbm{C}(\!(z)\!)[\vartheta]
of formal Laurent series, and
\Omega_{V}
its Kähler differentials. Let

be

Then, the image of

, admits a super symplectic form, analogous to Givental’s pairing, defined by

(2.52)

\langle[df],[dg]\rangle=\Res_{z=0}[df\cdot g]\,.

.

Proof.

For the exact sequence, note that

is not in the image of

Let us see what this pairing looks like in practice. We first need that

(2.53)

[d\vartheta\,z^{l}]=[d(\vartheta z^{l})-dz\,lz^{l-1}\vartheta]=[d(z^{l}\vartheta)]\,,

[dz\,\vartheta]=[d\vartheta\,z^{a}\vartheta^{2}]=0

(2.54)

\langle[dz\,z^{k-1}],[dz\,z^{l-1}]\rangle=\frac{\delta_{k,-l}}{k}\,,\qquad\langle[dz\,z^{k-1}],[d\vartheta\,z^{l}]\rangle=0\,,\qquad\langle[d\vartheta\,z^{k}],[d\vartheta\,z^{l}]\rangle=\delta_{k+l-a+1}\,.

The pairing is well-defined because adding a constant to

∎

2.3.5.
Multidifferentials

For our applications, we are interested in symmetric multidifferentials in the super setting.

Definition 2.27
.

An
n
-differential
on a super Riemann surface
\Sigma
is a meromorphic section of
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\boxtimes n}\rightarrow\Sigma^{n}
. We often call it a multidifferential when
n
is not specified. A
2
-differential is also called a bidifferential.

Let us focus on the case with
n=2
and clarify the notion of symmetric bidifferentials. For an ordinary Riemann surface
\Sigma_{0}
, one can naturally consider the involution
\tau\colon\Sigma_{0}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}\times\Sigma_{0}
that exchanges the first and second copy. Strictly speaking, for
(p_{1},p_{2})\in\Sigma_{0}\times\Sigma_{0}
and for a bidifferential
\omega
, one cannot naively compare
\omega(p_{1},p_{2})
with
\omega(\tau(p_{1},p_{2}))=\omega(p_{2},p_{1})
as they live in different vector spaces (fibres of the vector bundle). However, the generator
dz_{1}\otimes dz_{2}
can be canonically identified with
dz_{2}\otimes dz_{1}
as fibres are one-dimensional, hence the notion of symmetric bidifferentials is clear.

Fibres become
1|1
-dimensional in the super setting, so we need to introduce another involution to define symmetric differentials in a consistent way. More concretely, let us define a bundle morphism
\iota\colon\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\boxtimes 2}\rightarrow\tau^{*}\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\boxtimes 2}
whose action on the generators of bidifferentials is given by

(2.55)

\iota\colon[dz_{1}][dz_{2}]\mapstochar\rightarrow[dz_{2}][dz_{1}]\,,\quad[dz_{1}][d\vartheta_{2}]\mapstochar\rightarrow[d\vartheta_{2}][dz_{1}]\,,\quad[d\vartheta_{1}][d\vartheta_{2}]\mapstochar\rightarrow-[d\vartheta_{2}][d\vartheta_{1}]\,.

This morphism
\iota
is an involution, in the sense that
\tau^{*}(\iota)\circ\iota=\mathord{\mathrm{Id}}
.
The first action is a consequence of the last action i.e.

\iota([dz_{1}][dz_{2}])=\iota([d\vartheta_{1}][d\vartheta_{2}]\vartheta_{2}\vartheta_{1})=-[d\vartheta_{2}][d\vartheta_{1}]\vartheta_{2}\vartheta_{1}=[dz_{2}][dz_{1}]
in superconformal coordinates. One can similarly check the consistency in Ramond coordinates.

Definition 2.28
.

A bidifferential
\omega
is said to be

if it satisfies
\tau^{*}\omega=\iota(\omega)
.

In general, a symmetric bidifferential in local coordinates
\mathbbm{z}=(z\,|\,\vartheta)
is given by

\displaystyle\omega(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2})

\displaystyle=[d\vartheta_{1}][d\vartheta_{2}](-\vartheta_{1}\vartheta_{2}s(z_{1},z_{2})-\vartheta_{1}\alpha(z_{1},z_{2})+\vartheta_{2}\alpha(z_{2},z_{1})+a(z_{1},z_{2}))

(2.56)

\displaystyle=\frac{[dz_{1}][dz_{2}]}{z_{1}^{k}z_{2}^{k}}s(z_{1},z_{2})+\frac{[dz_{1}][d\vartheta_{2}]}{z_{1}^{k}}\alpha(z_{1},z_{2})+\frac{[d\vartheta_{1}][dz_{2}]}{z_{2}^{k}}\alpha(z_{2},z_{1})+[d\vartheta_{1}][d\vartheta_{2}]a(z_{1},z_{2})

for
s(z_{1},z_{2})=s(z_{2},z_{1})
and
a(z_{1},z_{2})=-a(z_{2},z_{1})
, and
k=0
if NS and
k=1
if R.

We extend the notion to symmetric
n
-differentials for any
n
in an obvious way.

2.3.6.
Super quadratic Casimirs

In the ordinary case, a quadratic differential is a section of
\Omega^{\otimes 2}
, the square of the canonical sheaf
\Omega
on a Riemann surface. Quadratic differentials play important roles in algebraic geometry and mathematical physics. In particular in the context of topological recursion, they are related to quadratic loop equations and Virasoro constraints. It turns out that the super analogue of a quadratic differential should be a section of
\mathcal{Q}^{\otimes 3}
.

In the ordinary case, any quadratic differential
q
can be (non-uniquely) constructed by a bidifferential
\omega
of
\Omega^{\boxtimes 2}
and specialising to the diagonal, assuming that
\omega
has no poles there. Indeed, if
q(z)=f(z)dz^{\otimes 2}
in some local coordinates
z
, then any bidifferential
\omega=dz_{1}\otimes dz_{2}\,g(z_{1},z_{2})
satisfying
g(z,z)=f(z)
gives
q(z)=\omega(z,z)
. This perspective is crucial to define loop equations in the ordinary topological recursion. In the super setting, however, specialisation is problematic.

Imagine a natural lift of a bidifferential
\omega
to a super Riemann surface, i.e., in local coordinates
\mathbbm{z}=(z|\vartheta)
, consider
\omega(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2})=[dz_{1}][dz_{2}]g(z_{1},z_{2})
. A problem arises upon specialisation, which gives
[dz][dz]=[d\vartheta]\vartheta[d\vartheta]\vartheta=0
because
\vartheta^{2}=0
. Therefore, specialisation of a bidifferential in the ordinary setting gives a quadratic differential while it vanishes in the super setting. A similar problem also occurs upon specialisation of any multidifferential.

How can one construct a section of
\mathcal{Q}^{\otimes 3}
from a bidifferential in the super setting, in particular, in such a way that it is compatible with super Virasoro constraints? We will show that the following definition captures all desired properties:

Definition 2.29
.

We define the
super quadratic Casimir operator

\mathcal{C}\colon\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\boxtimes 2}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\otimes 3}
by the action on a symmetric bidifferential
\omega
in any coordinates
\mathbbm{z}=(z|\vartheta)
as follows:

(2.57)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\colon\omega(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2})=[d\vartheta_{1}][d\vartheta_{2}]g(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2})\mapstochar\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(\omega)(\mathbbm{z})\coloneqq[d\vartheta]^{3}\begin{cases}D_{\vartheta_{1}}g(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2})\big|_{\mathbbm{z}_{1}=\mathbbm{z}_{2}=\mathbbm{z}}&\text{NS}\\
D^{*}_{\vartheta_{1}}g(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2})\big|_{\mathbbm{z}_{1}=\mathbbm{z}_{2}=\mathbbm{z}}&\text{R}\end{cases}

Proposition 2.30
.

The super quadratic Casimir operator is well-defined. That is, it is independent of the choice of coordinates.

Proof.

Let us take the same notation as
proposition

2.12
and consider an NS-NS transformation. Recall that
D_{\vartheta}=(D_{\vartheta}\varphi)D_{\varphi}
and
[d\varphi]=[d\vartheta](D_{\vartheta}\varphi)
, hence we have

(2.58)

\displaystyle\omega(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2})=[d\varphi_{1}][d\varphi_{2}](D_{\vartheta_{1}}\varphi_{1})^{-1}(D_{\vartheta_{2}}\varphi_{2})^{-1}g(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2})\eqqcolon[d\varphi_{1}][d\varphi_{2}]\tilde{g}(\mathbbm{x}_{1},\mathbbm{x}_{2}).

Then,

(2.59)

\displaystyle D_{\varphi_{1}}\tilde{g}(\mathbbm{x}_{1},\mathbbm{x}_{2})=(D_{\vartheta_{1}}\varphi_{1})^{-1}(D_{\vartheta_{2}}\varphi_{2})^{-1}\left((D_{\vartheta_{1}}(D_{\vartheta_{1}}\varphi_{1})^{-1})g(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2})+(D_{\vartheta_{1}}\varphi_{1})^{-1}(D_{\vartheta_{1}}g(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2}))\right)

Since
g(\mathbbm{z},\mathbbm{z})=0
for a symmetric bidifferential, we have:

(2.60)

[d\varphi]^{3}D_{\varphi_{1}}\tilde{g}(\mathbbm{x}_{1},\mathbbm{x}_{2})\Big|_{\mathbbm{x}_{1}=\mathbbm{x}_{2}=\mathbbm{x}}=[d\varphi]^{3}(D_{\vartheta}\varphi)^{-3}D_{\vartheta}g(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2})\Big|_{\mathbbm{z}_{1}=\mathbbm{z}_{2}=\mathbbm{z}}=[d\vartheta]^{3}D_{\vartheta}g(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2})\Big|_{\mathbbm{z}_{1}=\mathbbm{z}_{2}=\mathbbm{z}}.

Thus,
\mathcal{C}(\omega)(\mathbbm{z})=\mathcal{C}(\omega)(\mathbbm{x})
as claimed.

Superconformal transformation of other types can be similarly shown by replacing
D_{\vartheta}
with
D_{\vartheta}^{*}
and/or
D_{\varphi}
with
D_{\varphi}^{*}
.
∎

Let us justify why we consider the super quadratic Casimir operator. Given an even differential
\omega=y[dx]+\lambda[d\varphi]
in superconformal coordinates
\mathbbm{x}=(x\,|\,\varphi)
, one can construct a bidifferential by doubling it, i.e.,
\omega^{\boxtimes 2}(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2})\coloneqq\omega(\mathbbm{z}_{1})\otimes\omega(\mathbbm{z}_{2})
. Then, the super quadratic Casimir operator
\mathcal{C}
gives:

(2.61)

\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\boxtimes 2})(\mathbbm{x})=[d\varphi]^{3}(y\lambda+\varphi(y^{2}+\lambda^{\prime}\lambda))\eqqcolon[d\varphi]^{3}(C_{1}+\varphi C_{0}).

If one takes
y
as a free boson and
\lambda
as a free fermion in the context of
\mathcal{N}=1
superconformal field theory in two dimensions, then the
\varphi
-dependent part
C_{0}=y^{2}+\lambda^{\prime}\lambda
and the
\varphi
-independent part
C_{1}=y\lambda
respectively correspond to the so-called
energy-momentum tensor
and
supercurrent
. Thus, after quantisation,
C_{0}
and
C_{1}
generate super Virasoro operators, which is the property we would like to have. We also note that under the superconformal rescaling
(x\,|\,\varphi)\mapstochar\rightarrow(cx\,|\,\sqrt{c}\varphi)
, we have
C_{0}\mapstochar\rightarrow c^{-2}C_{0}
and
C_{1}\mapstochar\rightarrow c^{-\frac{3}{2}}C_{1}
, and this shows that their conformal weights are
2
and
\frac{3}{2}
respectively, which is consistent with the physics expectation.

3.
Super fundamental bidifferential and periods

In this section, we focus on the so-called fundamental bidifferential in the super setting. This object encodes geometric information of
\Sigma
. Let us start by reviewing properties of the fundamental bidifferential in the ordinary setting.

3.1.
Fundamental bidifferential

In the context of Eynard–Orantin topological recursion, one piece of the data of a spectral curve is a symmetric bidifferential with double pole on the diagonal,
B
. On
\mathbbm{P}^{1}
, this has the canonical form

(3.1)

B(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{dz_{1}\,dz_{2}}{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}}=d_{1}d_{2}\log(z_{1}-z_{2})\,.

It is a very important classical object, intimately related to the function theory of Riemann surfaces, and a super analogue is available in the literature. Let us start by recalling some basic function theory of Riemann surfaces, cf. e.g.
[
Fay73
,
Mum83
,
Mum84
]
, along with its superconformal analogues from
[
Wit15
,
DP15
,
Wit19
]
.

For an ordinary compact Riemann surface
\Sigma_{0}
of genus
g
,
H_{1}(\Sigma_{0};\mathbbm{Z})
is a symplectic lattice of rank
2g
, and we will pick a symplectic basis (also called Torelli marking)
\{A_{j},B_{j}\}_{j\in[g]}
such that
A_{j}\cap A_{k}=B_{j}\cap B_{k}=0
and
A_{j}\cap B_{k}=\delta_{jk}
. By the integration pairing, we can consider
H_{1}(\Sigma_{0};\mathbbm{Z})\hookrightarrow H^{0}(\Sigma_{0};\Omega^{1})^{*}
. Then, we call the map

(3.2)

P_{e}\colon H^{0}(\Sigma_{0};\Omega^{1})\rightarrow H^{1}(\Sigma_{0};\mathbb{C})\colon\omega\mapstochar\rightarrow\Big(\gamma\mapstochar\rightarrow\int_{\gamma}\omega\Big)

the
(even) period map
. The
g
-dimensional space
H^{0}(\Sigma_{0};\Omega^{1})
has a unique basis
\{\omega_{j}\}_{j=1}^{g}
normalised on
A
-cycles:
\int_{A_{j}}\omega_{k}=\delta_{jk}
. Moreover, the
period matrix

\Omega_{jk}\coloneqq\int_{B_{j}}\omega_{k}
is symmetric with positive definite imaginary part.

The
fundamental bidifferential
(
of the second kind
)
B
is a symmetric bidifferential on
\Sigma_{0}^{2}
, uniquely characterised by

•

Its only pole is a double one along the diagonal, with principal part
d_{1}d_{2}\log(z_{1}-z_{2})
;

•

\int_{A_{j}}B=0
.

It satisfies
\int_{B_{j}}B=\omega_{j}
, and on the universal cover of
\Sigma_{0}
, we can define the third kind differential with poles only at
p
and

by
\omega^{p-q}(x)=\int_{y=q}^{p}B(x,y)
. We note that for a non-compact Riemann surface, there is no unique fundamental bidifferential as

is not a symplectic lattice any more.

In the ordinary setting,
B
is constructed as follows: to a Riemann surface of genus
g
, we associate its Jacobian
\Jac(\Sigma_{0})
, i.e. the abelian variety of dimension
g
parametrising the degree zero divisor classes on
\Sigma_{0}
. Alternatively,
\Jac(\Sigma_{0})\cong H^{0}(\Sigma_{0};\Omega^{1})^{*}/H_{1}(\Sigma_{0};\mathbbm{Z})
, with the map given by

3.4.

Remark 5.3
.

We restrict to cases with simple ramifications, because the non-simply ramified case poses significant extra difficulties. Firstly, this case is already a lot more complicated in the non-super context, both in writing down the formulas and in proving that they are actually well-posed. Secondly, the super context provides two more complications: whether a component of the ramification locus is Neveu–Schwarz or Ramond depends on the parity of the ramification index by

in the reduced setting, but we will give an exposition closer to

We consider a local super spectral curve as in

We define the constants

Similarly, we define the constants

&\quad+\sum_{k,l\geq 0}[d\vartheta_{1}][d\vartheta_{2}]\left(1-\frac{\delta_{k,0}}{2}-\frac{\delta_{l,0}}{2}\right)V_{kl}z_{1}^{k}z_{2}^{l}\,,\end{split}

The symmetry of
\mathbbm{B}
implies that the constants satisfy the conditions (
4.27
). This implies that the dilaton shift
T
and the polarisation
\Phi
determines
\omega_{0,1}
and
\mathbbm{B}
and vice versa. This is why we use the same letters

The space
Q^{\prime}
has a natural maximally isotropic subspace, namely the space of holomorphic sections

. The fundamental super bidifferential determines a nearly complementary maximally isotropic subspace

, together with a one-dimensional rest
Q^{0}
, together called a
polarisation
, as follows.

In the domain
|z_{1}|<|z_{2}|
, we can rewrite
\mathbbm{B}
in the following way:

(5.11)

\mathbbm{B}(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2})=\sum_{k>0}^{\infty}kd\xi_{k}(\mathbbm{z}_{1})d\xi_{-k}(\mathbbm{z}_{2})+\frac{1}{2}d\eta_{0}(\mathbbm{z}_{1})d\eta_{0}(\mathbbm{z}_{2})+\sum_{k>0}^{\infty}d\eta_{k}(\mathbbm{z}_{1})d\eta_{-k}(\mathbbm{z}_{2})

where for
k>0
, we define, recalling the assumption (
4.27
),

(5.12)

\displaystyle d\xi_{k}(\mathbbm{z})

\displaystyle=[dz]z^{k-1}

(5.13)

\displaystyle d\eta_{k}(\mathbbm{z})

\displaystyle=[d\vartheta]z^{k}

(5.14)

\displaystyle d\xi_{-k}(\mathbbm{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{[dz]}{z^{k+1}}+\sum_{l\geq 0}\Big([dz]\frac{2B_{kl}+\varphi_{l0}\varphi_{k0}}{2k}z^{l-1}+[d\vartheta]\frac{2\varphi_{kl}+V_{0l}\varphi_{k0}}{2k}z^{l}\Big)

(5.15)

\displaystyle d\eta_{0}(\mathbbm{z})

\displaystyle=[d\vartheta]+\sum_{l>0}\Big([dz]\varphi_{l0}z^{l-1}+[d\vartheta]V_{0l}z^{l}\Big)

(5.16)

\displaystyle d\eta_{-k}(\mathbbm{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{[d\vartheta]}{z^{k}}+\sum_{l\geq 0}\Big([dz]\frac{2\varphi_{lk}+V_{k0}\varphi_{l0}}{2}z^{l-1}+[d\vartheta]\frac{2V_{kl}+V_{k0}V_{0l}}{2}z^{l}\Big)\,.

The sets
\{d\xi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbbm{Z}^{*}}
and
\{d\eta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbbm{Z}}
form a super symplectic basis of
Q^{\prime}
. The subset
\{d\xi_{k},d\eta_{k}\}_{k>0}
span

, and we define

and
Q^{0}=\mathop{\textup{Span}}\{d\eta_{0}\}
to get a polarisation. Indeed, one can show by using the conditions (
4.27
) that these forms satisfy

(5.17)

\langle d\xi_{k},d\xi_{l}\rangle=\frac{\delta_{k,-l}}{k}\,,\quad\langle d\xi_{k},d\eta_{l}\rangle=0\,,\quad\langle d\eta_{k},d\eta_{l}\rangle=\delta_{k,-l}

and therefore the projection operator

(5.18)

\mathcal{P}\colon Q^{\prime}\rightarrow Q^{\prime}\colon\omega(\mathbbm{z})\mapstochar\rightarrow\langle\mathbbm{B}(\mathbbm{z},\mathbbm{z}^{\prime}),\omega(\mathbbm{z}^{\prime})\rangle_{\mathbbm{z}^{\prime}}

has eigenspaces
Q^{+}
,
Q^{0}
, and
Q^{-}
, with respective eigenvalues
0
,
\frac{1}{2}
, and
1
.

Remark 5.4
.

Unlike the case without any polarisation discussed in
section

3.3.5
, for a residueless local meromorphic section
\omega(\mathbbm{z})=[dz]\sum_{k\in\mathbbm{Z}}u_{k}z^{k-1}+[d\vartheta]\sum_{k\in\mathbbm{Z}}\upsilon_{k}z^{k}
of

, we find

(5.19)

\langle\mathbbm{B}(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2}),\omega(\mathbbm{z}_{2})\rangle=\frac{1}{2}d\eta_{0}(\mathbbm{z}_{1})\left(\upsilon_{0}+\sum_{l<0}\frac{\varphi_{-l,0}u_{l-1}}{l-1}+V_{0,-l}\upsilon_{l}\right)+\sum_{k<0}d\xi_{k}(\mathbbm{z}_{1})u_{k}+d\eta_{k}(\mathbbm{z}_{1})\upsilon_{k}\,.

The first term comes with extra coefficients because in the expansion (

),

contains positive powers of

which couples with negative power terms of
\omega(\mathbbm{z}_{2})
. This motivates us to consider the following regularised projection to

:

(5.20)

\begin{split}\mathbbm{B}^{\textup{reg}}(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2})&\coloneqq\mathbbm{B}(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2})-\frac{1}{2}d\eta_{0}(\mathbbm{z}_{1})d\eta_{0}(\mathbbm{z}_{2}),\\
\langle\mathbbm{B}^{\textup{reg}}(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2}),\omega(\mathbbm{z}_{2})\rangle&=\sum_{k<0}d\xi_{k}(\mathbbm{z}_{1})u_{k}+d\eta_{k}(\mathbbm{z}_{1})\upsilon_{k}\,\end{split}

We will encounter another role of

in

.

With these definitions and
proposition

4.8
, we have a super Airy structure, and therefore a partition function. We will translate this partition function back into principal parts of symmetric multidifferentials
\omega_{g,n}
. To do this, we first need to translate the Airy structure into more geometric terms. We define differentials
J
and
\mathcal{J}
as

(5.21)

J(\mathbbm{z})\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbbm{Z}}[dz]\frac{J_{k}}{z^{k+1}}+[d\vartheta]\frac{\Gamma_{k}}{z^{k}}=\sum_{k\in\mathbbm{Z}}\frac{[d\vartheta]}{z^{k}}\Big(\vartheta J_{k}+\Gamma_{k}\Big),\quad\mathcal{J}(\mathbbm{z})\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbbm{Z}}d\xi_{-k}(\mathbbm{z})J_{k}+d\eta_{-k}(\mathbbm{z})\Gamma_{k}.

Note that
J
corresponds to a pair of free boson and free fermion (in the context of the
\mathcal{N}=1
super Virasoro vertex operator algebra) in the monomial basis. On the other hand, one can show that
\mathcal{J}
is related to
J
by conjugation by the polarisation as below:

Lemma 5.5
.

The dilaton shift and the change of polarisation act on the differential
J
by

(5.22)

\displaystyle TJ(\mathbbm{z})T^{-1}

\displaystyle=J(\mathbbm{z})+\omega_{0,1}(\mathbbm{z})\,,

(5.23)

\displaystyle\Phi TJ(\mathbbm{z})T^{-1}\Phi^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}(\mathbbm{z})+\omega_{0,1}(\mathbbm{z})\,.

Proof.

The conjugation by
T
shifts
J_{-k}
by
t_{k}
and
\Gamma_{-k}
by
\tau_{k}
, and these shifts combine exactly into
\omega_{0,1}(\mathbbm{z})
.

For the conjugation by
\Phi
, we see that

(5.24)

\displaystyle\ad_{\log\Phi}J_{-k}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{k>0}\sum_{l\geq 0}\frac{B_{kl}}{l}J_{l}+\varphi_{kl}\Gamma_{l}\,,

\displaystyle\ad_{\log\Phi}\Gamma_{-k}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{k\geq 0}\sum_{l\geq 0}\frac{\varphi_{lk}}{l}J_{l}+V_{lk}\Gamma_{l}\,,

(5.25)

\displaystyle\ad_{\log\Phi}^{2}J_{-k}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{k>0}\varphi_{k0}\sum_{l>0}\frac{\varphi_{l0}}{l}J_{l}+V_{l0}\Gamma_{l}\,,

\displaystyle\ad_{\log\Phi}^{2}\Gamma_{-k}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{k>0}V_{0k}\sum_{l>0}\frac{\varphi_{l0}}{l}J_{l}+V_{l0}\Gamma_{l}\,,

(5.26)

\displaystyle\ad_{\log\Phi}^{n}J_{-k}

\displaystyle=\ad_{\log\Phi}^{n}\Gamma_{-k}=0\,,\qquad n>2\,.

The quadratic adjunction being non-zero is a consequence of the zero mode
\Gamma_{0}
. So we get

(5.27)

\begin{split}&\Phi J(\mathbbm{z})\Phi^{-1}\\
&=\Big(\mathord{\mathrm{Id}}+\ad_{\log\Phi}+\frac{1}{2}\ad_{\log\Phi}^{2})J(\mathbbm{z})\\
&=\sum_{k\in\mathbbm{Z}}[dz]\frac{J_{k}}{z^{k+1}}+[d\vartheta]\frac{\Gamma_{k}}{z^{k}}+[dz]\sum_{k>0}\Big(\sum_{l\geq 0}\frac{B_{kl}}{l}J_{l}+\varphi_{kl}\Gamma_{l}\Big)z^{k-1}+[d\vartheta]\sum_{k\geq 0}\Big(\sum_{l\geq 0}\frac{\varphi_{lk}}{l}J_{l}+V_{lk}\Gamma_{l}\Big)z^{k}\\
&\qquad+\frac{1}{2}[dz]\sum_{k,l>0}\varphi_{k0}\Big(\frac{\varphi_{l0}}{l}J_{l}+V_{l0}\Gamma_{l}\Big)z^{k-1}+\frac{1}{2}[d\vartheta]\sum_{k,l>0}V_{0k}\Big(\frac{\varphi_{l0}}{l}J_{l}+V_{l0}\Gamma_{l}\Big)z^{k}\\
&=\sum_{k<0}[dz]\frac{J_{k}}{z^{k+1}}+[d\vartheta]\frac{\Gamma_{k}}{z^{k}}+\Big([d\vartheta]+\sum_{l>0}[dz]\varphi_{l0}z^{l-1}+[d\vartheta]V_{0l}z^{l}\Big)\Gamma_{0}\\
&\qquad+\sum_{k>0}\Big(\frac{[dz]}{z^{k+1}}+\sum_{l>0}[dz]\frac{B_{lk}}{k}z^{l-1}+\sum_{l\geq 0}\big([d\vartheta]\frac{\varphi_{kl}}{k}z^{l}+[dz]\frac{\varphi_{l0}\varphi_{k0}}{2k}z^{l-1}+[d\vartheta]\frac{V_{0l}\varphi_{k0}}{2k}z^{l}\big)\Big)J_{k}\\
&\qquad+\sum_{k>0}\Big(\frac{[d\vartheta]}{z^{k}}+\sum_{l>0}[dz]\varphi_{lk}z^{l-1}+\sum_{l\geq 0}\big([d\vartheta]V_{kl}z^{l}+[dz]\frac{V_{k0}\varphi_{l0}}{2}z^{l-1}+[d\vartheta]\frac{V_{k0}V_{0l}}{2}z^{l}\big)\Big)\Gamma_{k}\\
&=\sum_{k<0}[dz]\frac{J_{k}}{z^{k+1}}+[d\vartheta]\frac{\Gamma_{k}}{z^{k}}+\Big([d\vartheta]+\sum_{l>0}[dz]\varphi_{l0}z^{l-1}+[d\vartheta]V_{0l}z^{l}\Big)\Gamma_{0}\\
&\qquad+\sum_{k>0}\Big(\frac{[dz]}{z^{k+1}}+\sum_{l\geq 0}\big([dz]\frac{2B_{lk}+\varphi_{l0}\varphi_{k0}}{2k}z^{l-1}+[d\vartheta]\frac{2\varphi_{kl}+V_{0l}\varphi_{k0}}{2k}z^{l}\big)\Big)J_{k}\\
&\qquad+\sum_{k>0}\Big(\frac{[d\vartheta]}{z^{k}}+\sum_{l\geq 0}\big([dz]\frac{2\varphi_{lk}+V_{k0}\varphi_{l0}}{2}z^{l-1}+[d\vartheta]\frac{2V_{kl}+V_{k0}V_{0l}}{2}z^{l}\big)\Big)\Gamma_{k}\\
&=\sum_{k>0}\Big(d\xi_{k}(\mathbbm{z})J_{-k}+d\eta_{k}(\mathbbm{z})\Gamma_{-k}\Big)+d\eta_{0}(\mathbbm{z})\Gamma_{0}+\sum_{k>0}\Big(d\xi_{-k}(\mathbbm{z})J_{k}+d\eta_{-k}(\mathbbm{z})\Gamma_{k}\Big)\,.\end{split}

Note that
\Phi
does not act directly on forms, and hence does not affect
\omega_{0,1}
.
∎

We then define the
Hamiltonians

as:

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}^{1}(\mathbbm{x})

\displaystyle\coloneqq[d\varphi]\sum_{j\in\mathbbm{Z}}\varphi\frac{\Phi TJ_{2j}T^{-1}\Phi^{-1}}{x^{j+1}}+\frac{\Phi T\Gamma_{2j+1}T^{-1}\Phi^{-1}}{x^{j+1}}

(5.29)

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}^{2}(\mathbbm{x})

\displaystyle\coloneqq[d\varphi]^{3}\sum_{j\in\mathbbm{Z}}\Big(\frac{\varphi\tilde{L}_{2j}}{4x^{j+2}}-\frac{\tilde{G}_{2j+1}}{2\sqrt{2}x^{j+2}}\Big)

The quadratic Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}^{2}
is closely related to the twisted super quadratic Casimir operator
\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}
in
definition

2.31
. More concretely, we have the following:

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\sigma\colon\mathbbm{z}=(z\,|\,\vartheta)\mapstochar\rightarrow(-z\,|-\vartheta)
. Then, the Hamiltonians
\mathbbm{H}^{i}
can be written as follows:

(5.30)

\displaystyle\begin{split}2\mathbbm{H}^{1}(\mathbbm{x})&=\mathcal{J}(\mathbbm{z})+\mathcal{J}(-\mathbbm{z})+\hslash\omega_{0,1}(\mathbbm{z})+\hslash\omega_{0,1}(-\mathbbm{z})\,;\end{split}

(5.31)

\displaystyle\begin{split}2\mathbbm{H}^{2}(\mathbbm{x})&=-\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}(\hslash^{2}\mathbbm{B}^{\textup{R}}\;+:(\mathcal{J}+\omega_{0,1})\otimes(\mathcal{J}+\omega_{0,1}):)(\mathbbm{z})\end{split}

Proof.

The linear Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}^{1}
can be obtained by taking only the
\sigma
-invariant part of
J
– achieved by symmetrising – and then conjugating by
\Phi T
. The statement immediately follows from
lemma

5.5
.

For the quadratic Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}^{2}
, notice that the conjugation by
\Phi
and
T
commutes with the operation of the twisted super quadratic Casimir operator
\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}
. This is because the twisted super quadratic Casimir operator
\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}
is a geometric operation in terms of coordinates
\mathbbm{z}
while the conjugation is algebraic in terms of the modes
J_{k},\Gamma_{k}
, and they are independent of each other. Therefore, it is sufficient to prove (
5.31
) when
T=\Phi=1
.

The term coming from
\mathbbm{B}^{\textup{R}}
gives the central term
-\frac{\hslash^{2}}{2}\delta_{n,0}
in
-2L_{2n}
as below:

(5.32)

\begin{split}\hslash^{2}\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}(\mathbbm{B}^{\textup{R}})(\mathbbm{z})&=\frac{1}{2}\hslash^{2}[d\vartheta]^{3}D_{\vartheta_{1}}^{*}\Big(\frac{\vartheta_{2}\vartheta_{1}z_{1}z_{2}}{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}}-\frac{z_{1}+z_{2}}{2(z_{1}-z_{2})}\Big)\Big|_{\mathbbm{z}_{1}=-\mathbbm{z}_{2}=\mathbbm{z}}+(\mathbbm{z}\leftrightarrow\sigma(\mathbbm{z}))\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\hslash^{2}[d\vartheta]^{3}\Big(-\frac{\vartheta_{2}z_{1}z_{2}}{(z_{1}-z)^{2}}+\frac{\vartheta_{1}z_{1}z_{2}}{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}}\Big)\Big|_{\mathbbm{z}_{1}=-\mathbbm{z}_{2}=\mathbbm{z}}+(\mathbbm{z}\leftrightarrow\sigma(\mathbbm{z}))\\
&=-\hslash^{2}\frac{[d\vartheta]^{3}\vartheta}{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\hslash^{2}[d\varphi]^{3}\frac{\varphi}{4x^{2}},\end{split}

where we used
[d\vartheta]=\frac{[d\varphi]}{\sqrt{2}z}
. The computations for other terms closely follow a combination of
[
Bou+20
, Proposition 4.14]
and
[
BO21
, Theorem 4.4]
:

(5.33)

\begin{split}\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}(\vcentcolon\mathrel{\mspace{2mu}J\otimes J\mspace{2mu}}\vcentcolon)(\mathbbm{z})&=\frac{1}{2}[d\vartheta]^{3}\vcentcolon\mathrel{\mspace{2mu}\Big(D_{\vartheta}^{*}\Big(\sum_{l\in\mathbbm{Z}}\vartheta\frac{J_{l}}{(z)^{l}}+\frac{\Gamma_{l}}{(z)^{l}}\Big)\sum_{k\in\mathbbm{Z}}\frac{-\vartheta J_{k}}{(-z)^{k}}+\frac{[\Gamma_{k}]}{(-z)^{k}}\Big)\mspace{2mu}}\vcentcolon+(\mathbbm{z}\leftrightarrow\sigma(\mathbbm{z}))\\
&=\frac{1}{2}[d\vartheta]^{3}\vcentcolon\mathrel{\mspace{2mu}\Big(\Big(\sum_{l\in\mathbbm{Z}}\frac{J_{l}}{(z)^{l}}-\vartheta\frac{l\Gamma_{l}}{(z)^{l}}\Big)\sum_{k\in\mathbbm{Z}}\frac{-\vartheta J_{k}}{(-z)^{k}}+\frac{[\Gamma_{k}]}{(-z)^{k}}\Big)\mspace{2mu}}\vcentcolon+(\mathbbm{z}\leftrightarrow\sigma(\mathbbm{z}))\\
&=\frac{1}{2}[d\vartheta]^{3}\sum_{k,l\in\mathbbm{Z}}\frac{\vartheta(-1)^{k}}{z^{k+l}}\vcentcolon\mathrel{\mspace{2mu}-J_{l}J_{k}-l\Gamma_{l}\Gamma_{k}\mspace{2mu}}\vcentcolon+\frac{(-1)^{k}}{z^{k+l}}\vcentcolon\mathrel{\mspace{2mu}J_{l}\Gamma_{k}\mspace{2mu}}\vcentcolon+(\mathbbm{z}\leftrightarrow\sigma(\mathbbm{z}))\\
&=[d\vartheta]^{3}\sum_{n,k\in\mathbbm{Z}}\frac{\vartheta(-1)^{k}}{z^{2n}}\vcentcolon\mathrel{\mspace{2mu}-J_{2n-k}J_{k}-(2n-k)\Gamma_{2n-k}\Gamma_{k}\mspace{2mu}}\vcentcolon+\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{z^{2n+1}}\vcentcolon\mathrel{\mspace{2mu}J_{k}\Gamma_{2n+1-k}\mspace{2mu}}\vcentcolon\,,\end{split}

which after addition of the central term becomes

(5.34)

-2[d\vartheta]^{3}\sum_{n\in\mathbbm{Z}}\frac{\vartheta L_{2n}}{z^{2n}}-\frac{G_{2n+1}}{z^{2n+1}}=-2[d\varphi]^{3}\sum_{n\in\mathbbm{Z}}\frac{\varphi L_{2n}}{4x^{n+2}}-\frac{G_{2n+1}}{2\sqrt{2}x^{n+2}}\,.

This proves
lemma

5.6
.
∎

Now we want to see what this means to the partition function of the Airy structure , which we write as

(5.35)

Z=\exp\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g,n\geq 0\\
n>0\\
2g-2+n\geq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\hslash^{2g-2+n}}{n!}\sum_{i_{1},\dotsc i_{n}\in{A}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}x^{i_{k}}F_{g,n}[i_{1},\dotsc,i_{n}]\Big)\,,

where

. Note that the parity of coefficients

can be odd while the parity of

is even. As a consequence, the order of variables

and coefficients

matters due to possible signs, and our results below hold in this specific ordering.

This partition function is annihilated by
\{J_{2j},\Gamma_{2j-1},\tilde{L}_{2j-s-1},\tilde{G}_{2j-s}\}
for all

. By

G^{i}(\mathbbm{x})\coloneqq Z^{-1}\mathbbm{H}^{i}(\mathbbm{x})Z=[d\varphi]^{2i-1}\mathcal{O}\big(x^{\frac{(s-3)(i-1)}{2}}\big)

We decompose

in homogeneous terms with respect to the
\hslash

and
\vartheta

G^{i}(\mathbbm{x})\eqqcolon\sum_{g,n}\frac{\hslash^{2g-2+n}}{n!}G^{i}_{g,n+1}(\mathbbm{x})\,,

and define

Furthermore, for
k\in\mathbbm{Z}
, let us define

(5.39)

d\xi_{(B,k)}\coloneqq d\xi_{k}\,,\qquad d\xi_{(F,k)}\coloneqq d\eta_{k}\,.

So
\{d\xi_{k}\}_{k\in A}=\{d\xi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbbm{Z}^{*}}\cup\{d\eta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbbm{Z}}

Let
\mathbbm{S}
be a local super spectral curve, and let

be the partition function of the associated partial Airy structure as in

Then, we arrive at one of the main results:

Proposition 5.8
.

The
\mathcal{E}^{i}_{g,n}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{1}_{g,n}(\mathbbm{x};\mathbbm{z}_{[n]})

\displaystyle=\omega_{g,n+1}(\mathbbm{z},\mathbbm{z}_{[n]})+\omega_{g,n+1}(-\mathbbm{z},\mathbbm{z}_{[n]})

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{2}_{g,n}(\mathbbm{x};\mathbbm{z}_{[n]})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}(\omega_{g-1,n+2})(\mathbbm{z};\mathbbm{z}_{[n]})+\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g_{1}+g_{2}=g\\

Furthermore, they satisfy

\mathcal{E}^{i}_{g,n}(\mathbbm{x};\mathbbm{z}_{[n]})=[d\varphi]^{2i-1}\mathcal{O}\big(x^{\frac{(s-3)(i-1)}{2}}\big)\,.

Proof.

First, we note that
\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}

We also note that although each summand

The proof is standard, see e.g.

5.3.

We will now consider several ramification loci by considering a local spectral curve which consists of

6.
Super Topological Recursion

Since
theorem

5.9
shows that there exists a solution of super loop equations, and
section

5.3.1
shows the solution space is very constrained for global spectral curves, the next question is: how do we compute such solutions? Can we find a similar formula to the one of Eynard–Orantin
[
EO07
]
? It turns out that the recursive formula is more complicated and at the same time richer due to supersymmetric effects.

6.1.
Blobbed topological recursion

Let us first recall how to solve loop equations in the ordinary setting following
[
BS17
]
. The formula, called
blobbed topological recursion
, is more general than the Eynard–Orantin topological recursion
[
EO07
]
.

Let
V^{\pm}=\textup{Span}(d\xi_{k})_{k\in A_{r}^{\pm}}
. In the ordinary setting,
A_{r}^{+}
and
A^{-}_{r}
partition
A_{r}
, hence there exist natural projections
\mathcal{H}\colon V\rightarrow V^{+}
and
\mathcal{P}\colon V\rightarrow V^{-}
which give the holomorphic and principal part of a differential
\omega
in a chosen basis
\{d\xi_{B,a,k}\}
. One can naturally extend such projections to multidifferentials
\omega_{n}
in an obvious way. Recall from
section

5.1
that the principal projection is realised by local residue computations, whereas the holomorphic part is precisely the kernel of the residue operation.
[
BS17
]
shows that the most general solution of abstract loop equations (of rank
2
, as first introduced in
[
BEO15
]
) is given as below:

Proposition 6.1

(
[
BS17
]
)
.

For each
(g,n)
with
2g-2+n\geq 1
, fix
\varphi_{g,n}\in(V^{+})^{n}
. Then, if a solution
\{\omega_{g,n}\}
of ordinary abstract linear and quadratic loop with purely holomorphic parts equal to the
\varphi_{g,n}
exists, it is constructed as below:

(6.1)

\omega_{g,n+1}(z_{0},z_{[n]})=\mathcal{H}_{0}\omega_{g,n+1}(z_{0},z_{[n]})+\mathcal{P}_{0}\omega_{g,n+1}(z_{0},z_{[n]}),

where

(6.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}\omega_{g,n+1}(z_{0},z_{[n]})

\displaystyle=\varphi_{g,n+1}(z_{0},z_{[n]})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A^{\prime}\sqcup B=[n]\\
B\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\mathcal{H}_{{0}\sqcup A^{\prime}}\mathcal{P}_{B}\omega_{g,n}(z_{0},z_{[n]}),

(6.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{0}\omega_{g,n+1}(z_{0},z_{[n]})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in R}\Res_{z=a}\frac{\int^{z}_{a}\omega_{0,2}(z_{0},\cdot)}{\Delta_{a}\omega_{0,1}(z)}{\rm Rec}^{a}_{g,n+1}(z,z_{[n]}),

and moreover
\Delta_{a}\omega_{0,1}(z)=\omega_{0,1}(z)-\omega_{0,1}(\sigma_{a}(z))
, and for

), we define for each

{\rm Rec}^{a}_{g,n+1}(z;z_{[n]})=\mathcal{Q}_{g,n}(z;z_{[n]})|_{\sigma=\sigma_{a}}+\big(\Delta_{a}\omega_{0,1}(z)\big)\omega_{g,n+1}(z,z_{[n]})\,.

. Note that (

is a symmetric differential, and hence as long as

) in

[

and references therein.

Let us briefly review how to derive blobbed topological recursion from abstract loop equations in the ordinary setting.
The quadratic loop equations (
5.2
) together with the linear loop equations (
5.1
) imply that

(6.5)

-\Delta_{a}\omega_{0,1}(z)\Delta_{a}\omega_{g,n+1}(z,z_{[n]})+{\rm Rec}^{a}_{g,n+1}(z;z_{[n]})=(dz)^{2}\mathcal{O}(z^{s-1}).

A crucial point is that the right-hand-side has the same order of zero as
\Delta_{a}\omega_{0,1}
at
z=0
, hence dividing by it, we get:

(6.6)

-\omega_{g,n+1}(z,z_{\llbracket n\rrbracket})+\frac{{\rm Rec}^{a}_{g,n+1}(z;z_{[n]})}{\Delta_{a}\omega_{0,1}(z)}=dz\,\mathcal{O}(1)\,.

Therefore, the projection operator kills the unknown right side, and

(6.7)

\begin{split}0&=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{R}}\Res_{z=a}\int^{z}_{a}\omega_{0,2}(z_{0},\cdot)\;\;dz\mathcal{O}(1)\\
&=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{R}}\Res_{z=a}\int^{z}_{a}\omega_{0,2}(z_{0},\cdot)\;\left(-\omega_{g,n+1}(z,z_{[n]})+\frac{{\rm Rec}^{a}_{g,n+1}(z,z_{[n]})}{\Delta_{a}\omega_{0,1}(z)}\right)\\
&=-\mathcal{P}_{0}\omega_{g,n}(z_{0},z_{\llbracket n\rrbracket})+\sum_{a\in\mathcal{R}}\Res_{z=a}\int^{z}_{a}\omega_{0,2}(z_{0},\cdot)\;\frac{{\rm Rec}^{a}_{g,n+1}(z,z_{[n]})}{\Delta_{a}\omega_{0,1}(z)}\,.\end{split}

The power of the (blobbed) topological recursion formula is that although we do not know
a priori
what the right-hand sides of loop equations are, we can kill the unknown right-hand side contributions by taking residues at every ramification point.

It turns out that super topological recursion admits a similar structure to blobbed topological recursion, even without blobs. This is essentially due to the existence of zero modes inducing
\{d\eta_{i}\}_{i\in\hat{A}_{r}}
which are holomorphic along the Ramond divisor
\mathcal{R}
, (see remark
4.11
)

This motivates us to introduce two more new notions,
simple super spectral curves
, and
regularised fundamental bidifferential
. Let us consider them one by one.

6.2.
Simple super spectral curves

In order to motivate the notion of simple spectral curve which we shall introduce in this section, we focus on a degree one Ramond divisor

for brevity, because it is straightforward to generalise the arguments below to a higher degree. In this example, for brevity of notation, we drop

A constructive approach to solve super loop equations (

where

and taking a residue is not defined. This is a clear contrast to the ordinary case (

A next reasonable attempt is to consider the inverse of
\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}

\Delta\omega_{0,1}=[dx]\Delta y+[d\varphi]\Delta\lambda
let us define a differential operator

(6.9)

\mathcal{G}\coloneqq(\Delta y+\varphi\Delta\lambda^{\prime})+(\Delta\lambda+\varphi\Delta y)D_{\varphi}

Then for
\Delta\omega_{g,n+1}=[d\varphi](\varphi\Delta Y+\Delta\Lambda)

(6.10)

[d\varphi]^{3}\mathcal{G}(\varphi\Delta Y+\Delta\Lambda)=\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}(\Delta\omega_{0,1}\boxtimes\Delta\omega_{g,n+1}+\Delta\omega_{g,n+1}\boxtimes\Delta\omega_{0,1}).

It turns out that
\mathcal{G}
admits an inverse
\mathcal{G}^{-1}
which can be written explicitly as an differential operator with respect to
D_{\varphi}
whose coefficients are rational functions of

. Can one use
\mathcal{G}^{-1}
to solve loop equations because this is an analogous operation to ‘dividing by
\omega_{0,1}

\lambda
,
[d\varphi]\mathcal{G}^{-1}(\mathcal{O}(x^{\frac{s-3}{2}}))
becomes singular along the Ramond divisor, and as a consequence, one cannot kill the right-hand side of loop equations by taking residues.

At the moment, we do not know any elegant way of solving super loop equations. Nonetheless, we provide one way which works in a slightly more restricted setting:

Definition 6.2
.

We say that a super spectral curve is
simple
if, at every Ramond puncture, the expansion of
\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}\coloneqq\Delta_{a}y+\frac{\Delta_{a}\lambda^{\prime}\Delta_{a}\lambda}{2\Delta_{a}y}
in any local Ramond coordinate is of order
\mathcal{O}(z^{s-2})
with invertible leading coefficient.

Let us motivate why we consider simple super spectral curves. Using linear loop equations, we can write the quadratic loop equation
\mathcal{E}^{2}_{0,0}(\mathbbm{x})
in terms of
\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}
and
\Delta_{a}\lambda
as

(6.11)

(\Delta_{a}\tilde{y})^{2}=\mathcal{O}(x^{\frac{s-3}{2}}),\quad\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}\Delta_{a}\lambda=\mathcal{O}(x^{\frac{s-3}{2}}).

By definition of
s
, the condition for simple spectral curves is always satisfied when
s=1
. On the other hand, when
s=3
, for instance, we can have a scenario with odd parameters
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}
:

(6.12)

\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}=\frac{\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}{2z}+z+\mathcal{O}(z^{3}),\quad(\Delta_{a}\tilde{y})^{2}=\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}+\mathcal{O}(x)

where we have chosen the local coordinate
z:=x^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

Thus,
\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}
has a pole with nilpotent coefficients, and in general,
\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}
may have arbitrary higher order poles with more nilpotent coefficients as long as those coefficients are fine-tuned such that
(\Delta_{a}\tilde{y})^{2}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. The condition of being simple imposes that
\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}
does not admit such poles and it rather has a zero, i.e. the same behaviour as
\Delta_{a}y
in the ordinary setting
14
14

14

\Delta_{a}y
itself may still have a pole because we can inversely write
\Delta_{a}y=\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}-\frac{\Delta_{a}\lambda^{\prime}\Delta_{a}\lambda}{2\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}}
.
. Therefore, if one divides quadratic loop equations
\mathcal{E}^{2}_{g,n}
by
[d\varphi]^{2}\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}
, we have

(6.13)

\frac{\mathcal{E}^{2}_{g,n}}{[d\varphi]^{2}\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}}=\frac{[d\varphi]^{3}\mathcal{O}(x^{\frac{s-3}{2}})}{[d\varphi]^{2}\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}}=[d\vartheta]\mathcal{O}(1).

which is a similar behaviour to the ordinary setting (
6.6
). This explains why we introduced the notion of simple super spectral curves. We will shortly present how to solve super loop equations with
\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}
.

6.3.

Recall from

5.4

\mathbbm{B}^{\textup{reg}}
. Let us show another instance where
\mathbbm{B}^{\textup{reg}}
plays an important role.

Due to the anti-invariance with respect to
\sigma_{a}
, let us write the right-hand side of (

\frac{[d\varphi]^{3}\mathcal{O}(x^{\frac{s-3}{2}})}{[d\varphi]^{2}\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}}=[d\vartheta](\rho_{0}+\rho_{1}\vartheta z+\cdots)

where

but multidifferential in

\Res_{z=0}\int_{0}^{\mathbbm{z}}\mathbbm{B}(\mathbbm{z}_{0},\cdot)\frac{[d\varphi]^{3}\mathcal{O}(x^{\frac{s-3}{2}})}{[d\varphi]^{2}\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}}=\frac{1}{2}d\eta_{0}(\mathbbm{z}_{0})\rho_{0}

\mathbbm{B}^{\textup{reg}}(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2})=\mathbbm{B}(\mathbbm{z}_{1},\mathbbm{z}_{2})-\frac{1}{2}d\eta_{0}(\mathbbm{z}_{1})d\eta_{0}(\mathbbm{z}_{2}).

That is,

(6.17)

\Res_{\mathbbm{z}=0}\int_{0}^{\mathbbm{z}}\mathbbm{B}^{\textup{reg}}(\mathbbm{z}_{0}\cdot)d\vartheta\,\mathcal{O}(1)=0.

We note that for a Ramond divisor of degree

Remark 6.3

On the other hand, recall that

6.4.
Recursion formula

At the cost of having a good behaviour at the Ramond divisor as described in

Let us rescale
\lambda
by a formal even parameter
\gamma
while keeping

,
\varphi
,
\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}
and
\mathbbm{B}
unchanged. This means that
\Delta_{a}y
is a quadratic polynomial in

\Delta_{a}\omega_{g,n+1}=[d\varphi](\varphi\Delta_{a}Y+\Delta_{a}\Lambda)

\frac{1}{2}(2-\varphi D_{\varphi})\mathcal{C}^{\sigma_{a}}(\Delta_{a}\omega_{0,1}\boxtimes\Delta_{a}\omega_{g,n+1}+\Delta_{a}\omega_{g,n+1}\boxtimes\Delta_{a}\omega_{0,1})=-[d\varphi]^{2}\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}\cdot\Delta_{a}\omega_{g,n+1}\\
-\frac{\gamma}{2}[d\varphi]^{3}\Bigg(\varphi\bigg(\Delta_{a}\lambda^{\prime}(z)\Delta_{a}\Lambda+\Delta_{a}\Lambda^{\prime}\Delta_{a}\lambda(z)-\gamma\frac{(\Delta_{a}\lambda^{\prime})(\Delta_{a}\lambda)}{\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}(z)}\Delta_{a}Y\bigg)-\gamma\frac{(\Delta_{a}\lambda^{\prime})(\Delta_{a}\lambda)}{\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}(z)}\Delta_{a}\Lambda+2\Delta_{a}Y\Delta_{a}\lambda(z)\Bigg)\,,

on sections of

The strange looking

\varphi
-dependent and
\varphi

.

Suppose we have a simple super spectral curve with rescaled
\Delta\lambda
by a formal parameter

where

(6.24)

\mathcal{H}_{0}\omega_{g,n+1}(\mathbbm{z}_{0},\mathbbm{z}_{[n]})=\psi_{g,n+1}(\mathbbm{z}_{0},\mathbbm{z}_{[n]})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A^{\prime}\sqcup B=[n]\\
B\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\mathcal{H}_{{0}\sqcup A^{\prime}}\mathcal{P}_{B}\omega_{g,n+1}(\mathbbm{z}_{0},\mathbbm{z}_{[n]}),

\mathcal{P}_{0}\omega_{g,n+1}(\mathbbm{z}_{0},\mathbbm{z}_{[n]})=\sum_{p_{a}\in\mathcal{R}}\Res_{z=p_{a}}\frac{\int^{z}_{p_{a}}\mathbbm{B}_{0,2}^{\textup{reg}}(\mathbbm{z}_{0},\cdot)}{[d\varphi]^{2}\Delta_{a}\tilde{y}(z)}\textup{Rec}^{a}_{g,n+1}(\mathbbm{z};\mathbbm{z}_{[n]}),

and for any multidifferential

for each

Proof.

We will drop the

Due to linear loop equations, we have:

Therefore, quadratic super loop equations (

Then,

Since

7.1.1.
Towards the variational formula

In the context of topological recursion, we often consider not just a spectral curve but a family of spectral curves over a base

Recall from

Similarly, let us consider a weighted residue:

Therefore, one can think of
