Analysis of toxicity and mechanism of xylazine on testis with network toxicology and molecular docking strategy
Authors: Shanyong Yi, Jiahui Li, Xianxian Zhang, Shuquan Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Xylazine, an alpha-2 adrenergic agonist originally developed as a veterinary sedative, has increasingly been detected as an adulterant in illicit drug supplies, particularly in combination with opioids such as fentanyl. This emerging pattern of misuse poses significant health risks and imposes a growing social burden, underscoring the urgent need to develop a rapid and comprehensive research strategy to investigate the potential toxicological mechanisms of xylazine. The present study aimed to comprehensively assess the toxicity of xylazine on the testis and its potential molecular mechanism using network toxicology and molecular docking analyses. By utilizing ChEMBL, STITCH, GeneCards, OMIM, TTD, and Drugbank databases, we predicted 126 xylazine-associated targets related to testis injury. Through further screening with STRING and Cytoscape software, 18 core targets in the testicular injury network, including steroid receptor coactivator (SRC) and Heat shock protein 90 alpha family class A member 1 (HSP90AA1), were obtained. GO and KEGG pathway analyses conducted through the DAVID database suggested that the core targets of xylazine-induced testicular toxicity may be enriched mainly in neuroactive ligand receptor interaction pathway, cancer pathways, apoptosis signaling pathways, and calcium signaling pathways. Molecular docking via AutoDock confirmed the strong binding between xylazine and the core targets. Together, these findings suggest that xylazine may be involved in regulating cell proliferation, cell apoptosis, and inflammation, which could potentially lead to testis injury. This study provides a theoretical basis for understanding the molecular mechanism of xylazine-induced testicular toxicity. In addition, network toxicology combined with molecular docking has provided new directions for the elucidation of the toxicity and mechanism of action of novel drugs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55771-8.

Introduction

As an α2-adrenergic receptor agonist, xylazine has anticonvulsant, sedative, and strong analgesic effects and is especially suitable for large animals1. Owing to its low price and easy availability, xylazine is becoming an illicit substitute and adulterant in the recreational drug market2. As of October 2022, drug abuse with xylazine has spread throughout the United States3. In April 2023, the United States designated xylazine-doped stimulants as “new national threats” because they damage human tissues and cause 3,000 deaths4. Earlier in May 2022, there were also deaths in Europe due to inhalation of stimulants mixed with xylazine5. At present, the toxicological characteristics of xylazine are not fully understood; however, the consumption of xylazine is increasing, and the severity of social consequences is also increasing2,6. Therefore, the potential toxicological mechanism of xylazine needs to be fully understood, and a new integrated health and safety risk assessment strategy needs to be developed.

Traditional toxicological assays have not been able to rapidly and comprehensively characterize the complex biological effects of xylazine; therefore, innovative approaches are needed to comprehensively and effectively evaluate the potential health risks of xylazine to provide prevention and treatment strategies. Network toxicology can construct a relationship network between compounds, toxicity, and targets on the basis of online databases7,8. As an emerging technology to aid drug design, molecular docking can be used to detect the interaction and affinity between compounds and targets9. The combination of these two methods can be used to analyze the target proteins of compounds systematically and predict the molecular mechanism of the compounds’ effects. Therefore, the research method based on network toxicology combined with molecular docking is a promising strategy.

In the past 60 years, the prevalence of human infertility has continued to rise, from 7% − 8% in the early 1960s to 15% at present10. Decreased sperm count, abnormal sperm morphology, and poor sperm motility are common causes of fertility problems in men11,12. As the gonad dominates these processes, the important association of testicular injury and dysfunction with human infertility cannot be ignored. Given the established reproductive toxic effects of various stimulants13, it is reasonable to hypothesize that xylazine may also have reproductive toxic effects. Therefore, the present study aimed to explore the potential testicular toxicity of xylazine and the underlying molecular mechanism with network toxicology and molecular docking. This study provides preliminary computational insights to guide future experimental validation for prevention and control strategies for evaluating the reproductive toxicity of emerging stimulants such as xylazine and a basis for future research on diagnosing diseases associated with these stimulants.

Materials and methods

Preliminary network analysis of xylazine toxicity

The standard structure of xylazine and the canonical SMILES code were determined by querying “xylazine” in the PubChem database. Using ProTox 3.0, ADMETlab 2.0, and Vnn-ADMET as preliminary screening tools, we obtained basic information regarding xylazine toxicity.

Collection of xylazine targets

The ChEMBL and STITCH databases were used to identify xylazine targets. The species was set to “Homo sapiens”, and the collected target names were converted into gene names with the UniProt database for consistency.

Selection of testis toxicity-related targets

Based on published literature, the GeneCards, OMIM, TTD, and Drugbank databases, relevant targets were screened using the keywords “testicular injury”, “testicular toxicity”, and “testicular dysfunction”. Venny was used to identify common targets between xylazine target genes and those related to testicular injury, testicular toxicity, and testicular dysfunction.

Construction of the protein interaction network and screening of core targets

The intersecting target genes were imported into the STRING database. The analysis parameters were set as follows: species limited to “Homo sapiens”, “Minimum required interaction score” set to “high confidence (score > 0.7)”, and all isolated protein nodes hidden. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks for xylazine-related testicular toxicity were subsequently constructed.

The data were imported into Cytoscape 3.10.2, and topological properties were calculated using the Network Analyzer tool. Three topological parameters were computed via the cytoNCA plug-in: Betweenness Centrality (BC), Closeness Centrality (CC), and Degree Centrality (DC). Targets satisfying the following conditions were selected as core targets for xylazine-induced testicular toxicity: BC > median, CC > median, and DC greater than twice the median.

Gene function and pathway enrichment analysis of target proteins

To investigate the biological function of potential targets in xylazine-induced testicular toxicity, the DAVID database was used to perform Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis14,15. For GO analysis, biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) were evaluated to clarify the main biological functions. KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted to identify significant pathways associated with potential targets of xylazine-induced testicular toxicity, with an FDR threshold < 0.05. Using the core targets, we further investigated pathways associated with testicular injury to clarify important signaling pathways involved in biological processes. Finally, visual analysis was performed to interpret the GO and KEGG results.

Molecular docking for xylazine and core targets

Protein structure preparation

The three-dimensional structures of the seven target proteins were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB, https://www.rcsb.org/): SRC (PDB: 2SRC, resolution: 1.5 Å), TNF (PDB: 1TNF, resolution: 2.2 Å), KRAS (PDB: 4OBE, resolution: 1.95 Å), HSP90AA1 (PDB: 1UY6, resolution: 1.9 Å), BCL2 (PDB: 2O22, resolution: 2.0 Å), CASP3 (PDB: 1CP3, resolution: 2.3 Å), and ESR1 (PDB: 1ERR, resolution: 1.9 Å). The selection criteria included: (i) resolution ≤ 2.5 Å, (ii) presence of a co-crystallized ligand in the binding pocket for docking validation, and (iii) human protein source. Water molecules were removed, missing hydrogen atoms were added, and non-standard amino acids were corrected using UCSF Chimera v1.15 (or PyMOL v2.5). The protonation states of ionizable residues were assigned at pH 7.4 using the H + + server (http://biophysics.cs.vt.edu/). The prepared protein structures were saved in PDB format for subsequent processing.

Ligand preparation

The chemical structure of xylazine (CAS: 7361-61-7, molecular formula: C12H16N2S, molecular weight: 220.33 g/mol, SMILES: Cc1ccccc1C (= N) Nc2cccs2) was obtained from the PubChem database (CID: 5707). The three-dimensional structure was generated and energy-minimized using Avogadro v1.2.0 with the MMFF94 force field (convergence criterion: 10–6 kcal/mol/Å, maximum iterations: 1000). Open Babel v3.1.1 was used to convert the optimized structure to PDBQT format, with the following settings: (i) addition of Gasteiger partial charges, (ii) detection of aromaticity, (iii) assignment of rotatable bonds (torsion number: 5), and (iv) protonation at pH 7.4. The protonation state of the guanidine-like nitrogen (pKa ~ 10.5) was confirmed to be positively charged under physiological conditions.

Binding site definition

For each target protein, the docking grid box was centered on the co-crystallized ligand binding site. The grid dimensions were set to 60 × 60 × 60 Å with a spacing of 0.375 Å, ensuring complete coverage of the active/binding pocket. The specific grid center coordinates were determined based on the geometric center of the co-crystallized ligand in each structure displayed in Supplement Table 1.

Molecular docking protocol

Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina v1.2.0 (Trott & Olson, 2010). The docking parameters were set as follows: exhaustiveness = 32 (default: 8), number of output poses = 20, energy range = 4 kcal/mol. Each docking run was repeated three times with different random seeds to ensure reproducibility of the results. The Lamarckian genetic algorithm was employed for conformational search with the following settings: population size = 150, maximum number of evaluations = 2,500,000, mutation rate = 0.02, crossover rate = 0.8. The docking calculations were performed on a workstation running Ubuntu 20.04 (CPU: Intel Xeon E5-2680, RAM: 64 GB). The binding energies reported represent the estimated free energy of binding relative to the unbound state, with more negative values indicating more favorable interactions. It is important to note that in the context of this study, docking scores were used primarily as a ranking tool for virtual screening and target prioritization within the network pharmacology framework, rather than as quantitative predictors of absolute binding affinity.

Pose selection and interaction analysis

For each target, the top-ranked docking pose (lowest binding energy) was selected for further analysis. PyMOL v2.5 and LigPlot+ v2.2 were used for visualization and interaction mapping. Hydrogen bonds were identified using the following geometric criteria: donor-acceptor distance ≤ 3.5 Å, angle ≥ 120°. Hydrophobic interactions were defined as non-polar atom pairs (C-C) within 4.0 Å. π-π stacking interactions were identified when the distance between aromatic ring centroids was ≤ 5.0 Å. The hydrogen bond force values reported in Table 3 represent the calculated interaction energies (kcal/mol) for each identified hydrogen bond, as estimated by the AutoDock Vina scoring function decomposition.

Animals

Adult male Sprague-Dawley rats aged 8 weeks (260 ± 20 g) were procured from Beijing Vital River laboratory animal company. The rats were housed under controlled conditions, including a 12-hour light/dark cycle, ambient temperature maintained at 21 ± 2 °C, and relative humidity of 50 ± 5%, for 7 days. The rats were randomly divided into four groups: control group (Control, 0.9% saline), low-dose xylazine group (Xylazine-L, 10 mg/kg xylazine), medium‐dose xylazine group (Xylazine-M, 20 mg/kg xylazine), and high‐dose xylazine group (Xylazine-H, 40 mg/kg xylazine). The rats were injected intraperitoneally with saline or xylazine (CAS: 7361-61-7, Sigma-Aldrich, vehicle: 0.9%saline) once daily for 28 consecutive days. One day after the final injection, the rats were anesthetized in a Plexiglas induction chamber with 3% isoflurane in 100% oxygen at 1.5 L/min until loss of consciousness, then euthanized by cervical dislocation. Testis tissue was collected for further study. This study was conducted in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0, and all experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Xinxiang Medical University (Approval No.: XYLL-20240711), in accordance with the ethical principles for animal studies in China.

RNA extraction and qRT‑PCR

This study detected mRNA level changes of seven core targets. The primer sequences for these seven genes were obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (Table 1). Total RNA was extracted from testis tissue, and reverse transcription was performed using the SweScript All-in‐One RT SuperMix for qPCR (One‐Step gDNA Remover) (G3337‐50, Servicebio). The PCR reaction mixture consisted of 7.5 µL of 2 × qPCR Mix, 1.5 µL of 2.5 µM gene primer, 2.0 µL of reverse transcription product, and 4.0 µL of ddH2O. The reaction was performed under the following conditions: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s, followed by a melt curve analysis from 65 °C to 95 °C. Relative gene expression was normalized to β-actin and calculated using the ΔΔCT method.

Enzyme‑Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

ELISA kits for proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA, USEA591Mi), B-cell leukemia/lymphoma 2 (Bcl2, USEA778Ra), and Bcl2-associated X protein (Bax, USEB343Ra) were purchased from USCN KIT INC. ELISA kits for interleukin-1β (IL-1β, D731007), IL-6 (D731010), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α, D731168) were purchased from Sangon Biotech. Testis tissue was homogenized, and the supernatant was collected for cytokine level measurement. All samples were analyzed in triplicate, and the mean value was used for analysis. Standard curves were established using provided standards. Biotinylated antibodies, HRP conjugates, substrate reagents, and stop solution were added sequentially according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Multiskan MS microplate reader (type 352, Labsystems).

Oxidative stress assessment

Oxidative stress status was evaluated by measuring malondialdehyde (MDA) levels and the enzymatic activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), following previously established protocols. Tissue samples from the testis were homogenized in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatants were collected. Total protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit (P0010, Beyotime, Wuhan, China).

MDA level was measured using a thiobarbituric acid (TBA)-based colorimetric kit (A003-1-2, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). Briefly, 100 µL of supernatant was mixed with 100 µL of TBA reagent, heated at 95 °C for 40 min, and cooled to room temperature. After centrifugation at 1,000 × g for 10 min, the absorbance of the supernatant was read at 532 nm using a Multiskan MS microplate reader (type 352, Thermo Fisher, MA, USA).

SOD activity was assayed according to a previously described method. Supernatant samples were added to a reaction mixture containing EDTA (100 mM), riboflavin (2 mM), nitro blue tetrazolium (75 mM), methionine (13 mM), and sodium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.8). Absorbance was measured at 560 nm using a Multiskan MS microplate reader (type 352, Thermo Fisher, MA, USA), and activity values were subsequently calculated.

CAT activity was assessed using commercially available kits (A007-1-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). Following the manufacturer’s instructions, supernatant samples were mixed with the provided reagents, and absorbance was recorded at 405 nm using a Multiskan MS microplate reader (type 352, Thermo Fisher, MA, USA).

Statistical analysis

The results are presented as mean ± SEM and were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using Stata 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Identification of targets for xylazine-induced testicular toxicity

In this study, 4822 targets associated with testicular toxicity were retrieved from databases including GeneCards, OMIM, and TTD, and 249 xylazine-related targets were identified from SwissTargetPrediction, PharmMapper, and STITCH. These targets were intersected and deduplicated to obtain 126 overlapping genes as potential targets associated with xylazine-induced testicular toxicity (Fig. 1B).

The size and color of nodes in the PPI network correspond to their respective degree values (larger and darker nodes indicate greater degree values). Among these hub genes, SRC, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS), HSP90AA1, and apoptosis regulator Bcl-2 (BCL2) exhibited the highest degree values, as indicated by their larger node sizes and darker colors.

We visually represented the top 20 KEGG signaling pathways in reverse order of FDR values, and generated a statistical bubble plot and classification histogram (Fig. 4).

Molecular docking for xylazine and core target proteins

Molecular docking analysis was used to analyze the interaction between xylazine and 7 core target genes (SRC, TNF, KRAS, HSP90AA1, BCL2, caspase-3 (CASP3), and estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1)). The binding energies of 7 core targets with xylazine were all negative, indicating energetically favorable predicted interactions (Table 3). The molecular docking results for xylazine against the seven predicted targets are summarized in Table 3. The binding energies ranged from − 4.8 to − 7.1 kcal/mol. In virtual screening studies, binding energies below − 5.0 kcal/mol are commonly considered to indicate favorable binding (Morris & Lim-Wilby, 2008). Based on this criterion, five targets (SRC, KRAS, HSP90AA1, BCL2, and ESR1) exhibited favorable binding, while TNF and CASP3 were at the borderline. Notably, KRAS displayed the most favorable binding energy (− 7.1 kcal/mol), followed by ESR1 (− 6.6 kcal/mol) and HSP90AA1 (− 6.5 kcal/mol).

The hydrogen bond forces, ranging from 3.0 to 4.7, showed a general correlation with binding affinity. ESR1, with the strongest H-bond interaction (4.7), also exhibited a highly favorable binding energy (− 6.6 kcal/mol). Conversely, TNF and CASP3, with the weakest H-bond forces (3.0 and 3.4, respectively), showed the least favorable binding energies (− 4.8 kcal/mol each). This internal consistency between binding energy and H-bond strength supports the physical relevance of the predicted poses. In summary, the results of molecular docking analysis suggest that these 7 core targets may be involved in the molecular mechanism of xylazine-induced testicular toxicity (Fig. 5).

The effects of xylazine injection on the mRNA levels of the core targets

Validation results are shown in Fig. 6. Compared with the control group, SRC and BCL2 mRNA levels were significantly decreased in the 20 and 40 mg/kg xylazine groups (*p < 0.05), TNF-α and ESR1 were significantly increased in all xylazine groups in a dose-dependent manner (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01), KRAS was significantly increased in the 40 mg/kg xylazine group (*p < 0.05), HSP90AA1 was significantly decreased in all xylazine groups (*p < 0.05), and CASP3 was significantly increased in the 20 and 40 mg/kg xylazine groups (*p < 0.05). These findings support the involvement of these core targets in the proposed mechanisms of xylazine-induced testicular toxicity.

The effects of xylazine injection on cell proliferation, cell apoptosis, inflammatory response, and oxidative stress

To evaluate the effects of xylazine on cell proliferation, cell apoptosis, inflammatory response, and oxidative stress, this study detected the levels of PCNA, BCL2, BAX, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and MDA and the activities of SOD and CAT. PCNA was significantly decreased in all xylazine groups in a dose-dependent manner (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01), BCL2 was significantly decreased in the 20 and 40 mg/kg xylazine groups (*p < 0.05), and BAX, IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and MDA were significantly increased in all xylazine groups in a dose-dependent manner (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01). SOD activity was significantly decreased in the 20 and 40 mg/kg xylazine groups (*p < 0.05), and CAT activity was significantly decreased in all xylazine groups (*p < 0.05) (Fig. 7). These results support that xylazine may affect cell proliferation, apoptosis, inflammation, and oxidative stress….

Discussion

Despite increasing consumption and the social consequences, xylazine is often used as an adjunct to opioids such as fentanyl16 so xylazine-centered studies are lacking. Network toxicology and molecular docking can be used to analyze the potential mechanisms of compounds regulating diseases via multiple targets and multiple pathways at the molecular level, revealing drug‒target‒disease interaction relationships and providing research directions for pharmacological studies on novel drugs17. Given this, network toxicology and molecular docking serve as valuable tools for generating preliminary predictions to explore and analyze the potential effects of xylazine on the human body in a relatively short time.

This study screened 126 potential targets associated with testicular toxicity potentially related to xylazine exposure via online databases. We then established interaction networks of these potential targets and extracted 18 hub genes. These hub genes include steroid receptor coactivator (SRC), tumor necrosis factor (TNF), kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS), heat shock protein 90 alpha family class A member 1 (HSP90AA1), apoptosis regulator Bcl-2 (BCL2), estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1), and caspase-3 (CASP3), which represent potential key nodes within the predicted interaction network associated with xylazine-related testicular toxicity. SRCs are among the most extensively studied coregulatory factors18. Many studies have shown that the diverse post-transcriptional modifications of SRCs can regulate the expression of multiple genes, thus playing a crucial role in reproduction, metabolism, immunity, and tumors19,20. Current research indicates that SRC is involved in the formation of the blood-testis barrier, the renewal of spermatogonial stem cells, and testicular development21. TNF-α is a cytokine that has various bioactive functions in the lymphocyte-macrophage system22. Recent studies have shown that the testis can secrete TNF-α, which is involved in cell proliferation and differentiation, cell apoptosis, immune regulation, inflammation mediation, anti-tumor, cytotoxicity, and other complex biological activities22,23. Increased expression of TNF-α can significantly inhibit spermatogenesis and increase cell apoptosis24, and disrupt the blood-testis barrier25. In addition, TNF-α is associated with autoimmune orchitis26. KRAS plays an important regulatory role in the processes of tumor growth and angiogenesis, and has an important influence on cell growth, survival, differentiation, and other functions27. The normal KRAS gene can inhibit the growth of tumor cells, but once mutated, it will continue to stimulate cell growth and disrupt the growth rules, resulting in the occurrence of tumors28. Current research indicates that the activation of KRAS can promote the development of testicular granulosa cell tumors29. As a heat shock protein, HSP90AA1 plays a crucial role in protein folding and cell survival. Notably, HSP90AA1 is associated with idiopathic male infertility in the Chinese population30. As a group of proteins closely related to apoptosis regulation, the Bcl-2 protein family mainly plays a role in the mitochondrial membrane of cells. Proteins in this family determine cell survival and death by controlling the permeability of the outer membrane of mitochondria31. BCL2 maintains the homeostasis and function of the testis by balancing pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic signals in the spermatogenic microenvironment of the testis32. ESR1 mediates gene transcriptional activation through binding to estrogen and is crucial for maintaining male reproductive function33. The ESR1 gene encodes the production of estrogen receptor α (ERα). ERα knockout mice exhibited dilation of the seminiferous tubules and loss of spermatogenic function34,35. The down-regulation of ERα level is involved in the functional decline of testicular supporting cells and the destruction of the blood-testis barrier in rats36,37. CASP3, as a key protease for apoptosis execution, is involved in the process of testicular spermatogenic cell injury and testicular injury38. Molecular docking results suggested that these hub genes may interact with xylazine with favorable binding affinities (binding energy < 0 kcal/mol), indicating their potential involvement in xylazine-associated testicular injury. This study also detected the mRNA levels of the above hub genes in the testis of rats with repeated xylazine exposure. The results showed that xylazine significantly reduced the mRNA levels of SRC, HSP90AA1, and BCL2 and upregulated the mRNA levels of TNF-α, KRAS, CASP3, and ESR1, especially at medium and high doses. These results suggest that xylazine may be involved in pathophysiological processes such as testicular cell proliferation and differentiation, apoptosis and anti-apoptosis, and the maintenance of reproductive function by regulating the above core targets. However, the detailed mechanism still requires further in-depth experimental validation.

Based on the GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of all potential targets, particularly the hub genes, the results revealed that these targets were mainly enriched in cancer pathways, neuroactive ligand receptor interaction pathways, apoptosis signaling pathways, and calcium signaling pathways. Further investigation indicated that the identified targets were primarily associated with cell proliferation, cell apoptosis, and inflammation. Therefore, we hypothesize that xylazine may contribute to the occurrence and development of testicular injury by regulating cell proliferation, cell apoptosis, inflammation, and oxidative stress. This study further investigated the levels of cell proliferation, cell apoptosis, inflammatory response, and oxidative stress in the testis of rats treated with xylazine. Proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) plays a key role in DNA synthesis and cell proliferation of eukaryotic cells, and its stable expression makes it a good indicator of cell proliferation status39. B-cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2) is an anti-apoptotic protein, while Bcl-2-associated X protein (BAX) is a pro-apoptotic protein. The ratio of BAX/BCL-2 is a key determinant of the apoptotic threshold40. Inflammatory cytokines, particularly TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, play major roles in inflammatory responses41. Oxidative stress results from an imbalance between the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the capacity of the endogenous antioxidant defense system, leading to cellular damage. Malondialdehyde (MDA), the final product of lipid peroxidation, is a widely used biomarker of oxidative damage to cell membranes, whereas superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) are key enzymatic antioxidants that protect cells against ROS-mediated injury42. The results showed that, compared with the control group, the levels of BAX, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and MDA in the testis of xylazine-treated rats were significantly increased, whereas the levels of PCNA and Bcl-2 and the activities of SOD and CAT were significantly decreased. These results suggest that xylazine inhibited cell proliferation and promoted apoptosis, inflammatory responses, and oxidative stress in the testis. Spermatogenesis is a highly orchestrated process in which spermatogonial stem cells (SSCs) undergo sequential phases of self-renewal, differentiation, meiosis, and spermiogenesis. Octamer-binding transcription factor 4 (OCT4) maintains the undifferentiated state and self-renewal capacity of SSCs, thereby preserving the germline stem cell pool43. Stimulated by retinoic acid gene 8 (STRA8), which is expressed in premeiotic germ cells upon retinoic acid stimulation, serves as a master switch that drives the transition from mitotic proliferation to meiotic initiation44. Deleted in azoospermia-like (DAZL) is a germ cell-specific RNA-binding protein essential for multiple stages of gametogenesis, including the expansion and differentiation of spermatogonial progenitors45. Following meiotic initiation, synaptonemal complex protein 3 (SCP3) is a key structural component of the synaptonemal complex that facilitates homologous chromosome pairing and recombination during meiotic prophase46. Finally, during spermiogenesis, transition protein 2 (TNP2) replaces histones to initiate chromatin condensation, facilitating the dramatic nuclear reshaping required for mature sperm formation47. The results showed that, compared with the control group, the mRNA levels of OCT4, STRA8, DAZL, SCP3, and TNP2 in the testis of xylazine-treated rats were significantly decreased. These results suggest that xylazine disrupts spermatogenesis at multiple levels, including stem cell maintenance, meiotic initiation, homologous chromosome pairing, and chromatin condensation, potentially leading to impaired male fertility. These findings imply potentially serious consequences of xylazine exposure and provide a preliminary perspective on the potential mechanisms underlying xylazine-associated testicular toxicity.

The present study provides preliminary insights into potential mechanisms and targets for xylazine-associated testicular toxicity, and the analytical strategy employed may also be applied to rapidly screen other new stimulants. Compared with traditional toxicology studies, which rely primarily on animal models and conventional experimental techniques to identify toxicity targets, the present approach leverages biomedical and biological big data analyses to rapidly and comprehensively generate hypotheses regarding the potential mechanisms of pathophysiological changes induced by novel substances. This strategy may facilitate rapid and preliminary toxicity assessments for emerging compounds. Network toxicology offers several advantages as a complementary tool. First, it can reduce initial reliance on laboratory animals by enabling more targeted experimental designs based on computationally predicted pathways. Second, direct analysis of the interaction network between substances and human targets may help bridge gaps arising from species differences between experimental animals and humans, although it cannot fully replace cross-species validation. Finally, the comprehensive and holistic analysis enabled by network toxicology may help reduce potential bias associated with single-endpoint pharmacological studies. Molecular docking, as employed in this study, remains a critical and widely used in silico method for exploring substance action mechanisms at the molecular level. The integration of network toxicology with molecular docking can enhance the efficiency and predictive capacity of preliminary toxicology research, providing a reference framework for the future evaluation of understudied novel compounds. Nevertheless, these computational approaches serve as hypothesis-generating tools that complement, rather than replace, rigorous experimental validation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although network toxicology and molecular docking provided valuable preliminary predictions, the computational results remain hypothesis-generating and require further experimental validation, including gene knockout or overexpression studies, to establish causal relationships between the identified hub genes and xylazine-associated testicular toxicity. Second, our animal experiments were limited to male Sprague-Dawley rats with a single route of administration (intraperitoneal injection) and a 28-day exposure period; therefore, the findings may not fully translate to other species, routes, or durations of xylazine exposure. Third, phenotypic assessments of testicular toxicity, such as sperm count, motility, morphology, and histopathological examination of testicular tissue, were not included in the current study, which limits the comprehensive evaluation of reproductive impairment at the functional and structural levels. Fourth, molecular docking analyses lacked comparison with known ligands or positive controls, precluding a definitive quantitative assessment of binding affinity strength relative to established interactions. Finally, the mechanistic exploration was primarily confined to transcriptional and protein level changes, without investigating downstream signaling cascades, post-translational modifications, or functional rescue experiments. Future studies should address these limitations to more definitively elucidate the testicular toxicity and underlying molecular mechanisms of xylazine.

Conclusion

Overall, the present study employed an integrated strategy combining network toxicology and molecular docking to conduct a preliminary investigation of the potential testicular toxicity of xylazine. Our results provide preliminary evidence suggesting that xylazine may contribute to testicular injury by inhibiting cell proliferation and promoting cell apoptosis and inflammatory response. This study preliminarily explored the potential molecular mechanisms underlying xylazine-associated testicular toxicity and identified candidate targets that warrant further experimental validation. These findings may provide a foundation for future studies aimed at preventing and treating reproductive damage potentially caused by xylazine.
