On the Complexity of Horizontal Unitracks.
Ivan Molodyk

Abstract
This paper concerns the geometry of bicycle tracks. We model bicycle as an oriented segment of a fixed length that is moving in the Euclidean plane so that the trajectory of the rear point is tangent to the segment at all times. The trajectories of front and back points of the segment are called bicycle tracks, and one asks if it is possible that the front track is contained in the rear track (other than when they are straight lines). Such curves are called unitracks or unicycle tracks. In 2002 D.Finn proposed a construction of unitracks that are obtained as a union of a sequence of curves. Numerical evidence suggested that these curves behave expansively and that various numerical characteristics of the curves grow quickly in the sequence. In this paper we prove that the curves that form a unitrack in Finn’s construction cannot remain graphs of functions, unless they are straight lines. We conclude that the horizontal amplitude of the curves has a linear growth rate between 1 1 and 2 2 .

Introduction

The bicycle tracks geometry is a rich and fascinating subject, and it attracted much attention lately.

One studies a simple model of a bicycle as an oriented segment of fixed length moving in the Euclidean plane. Let the front and the rear points of the segment represent the touching points of the front and back wheels of the bicycle with the ground. Unlike the front wheel, the rear wheel of the bike is fixed on the frame, so its trajectory is tangent to the moving segment at all times. This is a non-holonomic constraint on the motion that defines a completely non-integrable distribution on the 3-dimensional configuration space of directed segments of a fixed length.
The same model describes the motion of hatchet planimeter, see
[
7
,
8
]
.

To put things into perspective, we briefly mention the main directions of study of this bicycle model.

The non-holonomic “bicycle” constraint can be expressed as a first order differential equation that, in appropriate coordinates, is a Riccati equation. The monodromy of this equation is a Möbius transformation depending on the front track. If the front track is a closed curve, the monodromy map assigns the final position of the bicycle to its initial position. The bicycle path is closed if the monodromy has a fixed point. An old conjecture, due to an engineer Menzin, is that if the bicycle segment is unit, the front track is a closed convex curve that bounds area greater than
\pi
, then the monodromy is hyperbolic, that is, has two fixed points. See
[
10
]
for a proof. Another paper on this topic is
[
3
]
. Also see
[
9
]
for a version in the spherical and hyperbolic geometries.

Two front tracks that share the same closed rear track traversed in the opposite directions are said to be in the bicycle correspondence. This correspondence is completely integrable and it is closely related with the planar filament equation, a completely integrable partial differential equation describing evolution of plane curves; see
[
4
,
12
]
.

A question that was explored in
[
6
]
is as follows: if the bicycle tracks were two closed curves on the plane – would we be able to tell what the direction of motion was? This question relates bicycle dynamics to a different geometrical problem concerning bodies floating in equilibrium. The connection and certain properties of such bodies were studied in
[
14
]
.

One can consider the bicycle motion as a horizontal curve in the configuration space, that is, a curve that is tangent to the distribution describing the non-holonomic constraint. This distribution has a natural metric, and one wants to describe its geodesics. In this metric, the length of the bicycle path is the length of the front track. See
[
1
,
2
,
11
]
for results in this direction.

Still another question is whether one can ride a bicycle in such a way that the tracks of the rear and the front wheels coincide (other than along a straight line)? The solutions to this problem are called unicycle tracks, or unitracks. D. Finn have proposed a construction of a unitrack in
[
5
]
. We should also mention a different approach to constructing a unitrack due to S. Wagon
[
13
]
. However, this paper concerns some of the properties of Finn’s construction, briefly explained below.

Consider a smooth curve connecting
(0,0)
to
(1,0)
on the plane, such that it is infinitely flat with respect to the horizontal direction at the endpoints. We assume that this curve
\gamma_{0}
is a segment of the rear track of a bicycle of length
1
. Then we can reconstruct the corresponding segment of the front track — we call it
\gamma_{1}
, and it will connect points
(1,0)
and
(2,0)
. Due to the specific conditions of ”flatness” at the endpoints,
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{1}
can be ”glued” together smoothly at the point
(1,0)
, therefore we can assume
\gamma_{1}
is the continuation of the rear track that started as
\gamma_{0}
. In the same way we can proceed to construct curves
\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}
, and so on. The union of this sequence of curves forms a smooth rear track that contains the front track as a subset.

•

tends to infinity with
n

•

n

•

Theorem D
: The number of ‘zeros’ of the curves

strictly grows with
n
.

•

Conjecture E
: The curves
\gamma_{n}
develop self-intersections.

•

: The ‘vertical amplitude’ of
\gamma_{n}
grows with
n
.

•

Conjecture G
: The ‘vertical amplitude’ of
\gamma_{n}

.

•

\gamma_{n}

•

Theorems A, C, and D are reasonably simple to prove statements. Theorem F is not particularly complicated either, although, the proof is quite technical. On the other hand, Theorems B, H, and I are significantly more complicated. In Section
5
of this paper we will provide a proof of Theorem I by contradiction. We will assume that in fact there is a curve
\gamma_{0}
that will produce a sequence of curves
\gamma_{n}
, where all the curves will be graphs of functions, and we will trace the horizontal movement of points on curves within a bounded interval. Then we will analyze the limiting distribution of the points along the horizontal line, and obtain a bound on the length of the initial curve. When we conclude that the length of the initial curve was no greater than
1
, and therefore
\gamma_{0}
must be a straight line, the proof of Theorem I will be completed. Theorem H follows from Theorem I (the proof is in Section
6
), and Theorem B follows from Theorem H immediately.

2
Basic Properties

Let
X
be the set of all infinitely smooth curves, parametrized by closed intervals, and immersed in the Euclidean plane. We define the map:

\varphi\colon X\to X;\hskip 11.74988pt\varphi\colon\gamma\mapsto\gamma+\frac{\dot{\gamma}}{\|\dot{\gamma}\|},

where the addition is pointwise, i.e.,
\varphi(\gamma)(t)=\gamma(t)+\frac{\dot{\gamma}(t)}{\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|}
as vectors in the Euclidean plane.

Figure 2:
The curve
\varphi(\gamma)
is the front track of a bicycle, where
\gamma
is the back track.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
\gamma\in X
, then
\mathop{\rm Len\mskip 1.0mu}(\varphi(\gamma))\geqslant\mathop{\rm Len\mskip 1.0mu}(\gamma)
, where
\mathop{\rm Len\mskip 1.0mu}
is length.

Proof.

Let
s
be a natural parameter on
\gamma
. Then:

\varphi(\gamma)=\gamma+\dot{\gamma};\hskip 11.74988pt\dot{\varphi(\gamma)}=\dot{\gamma}+\ddot{\gamma}.

Since
s
is a natural parametrization of
\gamma
, we conclude that
\ddot{\gamma}\perp\dot{\gamma}
. Therefore:

\mathop{\rm Len\mskip 1.0mu}(\varphi(\gamma))=\int\limits_{0}^{\mathop{\rm Len\mskip 1.0mu}(\gamma)}\!\!\|\dot{\varphi(\gamma)}\|ds=\int\limits_{0}^{\mathop{\rm Len\mskip 1.0mu}(\gamma)}\!\!\!\|\dot{\gamma}+\ddot{\gamma}\|\,ds=\int\limits_{0}^{\mathop{\rm Len\mskip 1.0mu}(\gamma)}\!\!\sqrt{\|\dot{\gamma}\|^{2}+\|\ddot{\gamma}\|^{2}}\,ds=\\
=\int\limits_{0}^{\mathop{\rm Len\mskip 1.0mu}(\gamma)}\sqrt{1+k_{\gamma}^{2}(s)}\,ds\geqslant\int\limits_{0}^{\mathop{\rm Len\mskip 1.0mu}(\gamma)}\!\!1\,ds=\mathop{\rm Len\mskip 1.0mu}(\gamma),

where
k_{\gamma}(s)=\|\ddot{\gamma}(s)\|
is the modulus of the curvature of
\gamma
. Not only we proved that the length of the curve doesn’t decrease under the transformation
\varphi
, but also that it strictly increases unless its curvature is constant zero, meaning
\gamma
is a straight line.
∎

The following proposition features some ‘expansive’ properties of the map
\varphi
:

Proposition 2.2
.

Consider a smooth curve
\gamma=(x(t),y(t))\colon[a,b]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and let
\varphi(\gamma)=(u(t),v(t))
. Assume that
\gamma
is not a horizontal segment, meaning
y(t)\neq const
. Then assuming that the maximum value of
y
is reached in the interior of
[a,b]
, the maximum value of
v
is bigger than the maximum value of
y
.

The proof of this proposition consists of a somewhat tedious page-long calculation. Since we do not use this result in the paper, we will not include the proof here.

3
Horizontal Unitrack

We are going to study a variation of the unicycle construction, proposed by D.Finn, and presented in the introduction. We will work with a smaller set of curves, defined below.

Let
Y\subset X
be the space of immersed curves
\gamma\colon[a,b]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
such that:

•

\gamma(a)=(0,0)
and
\gamma(b)=(1,0)
;

•

\dot{\gamma}(a)=\dot{\gamma}(b)=(1,0)
in the natural parameter, or,
\dot{\gamma}(a)
and
\dot{\gamma}(b)
are horizontal vectors oriented to the right;

•

\forall n\geqslant 2\,\,\gamma^{(n)}(a)=\gamma^{(n)}(b)=(0,0)
.

In other words,
Y
is the set of smooth curves immersed in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, starting at the origin, finishing in
(1,0)
, and infinitely flat with respect to the horizontal direction at the endpoints.

Figure 3:
Curve
\gamma_{0}
defines the next curve
\gamma_{1}

Recall that in Finn’s construction of a unicycle track we start with a curve
\gamma_{0}\in Y
, and produce the sequence of curves
\gamma_{n}=\varphi^{n}(\gamma_{0})
. Due to the conditions defining
Y\subset X
, the union of the images of these curves forms an infinitely smooth curve. If a bicycle starts riding with its rear wheel along this curve, starting at the beginning of
\gamma_{0}
, the front wheel will be riding along the same curve, starting at the beginning of
\gamma_{1}
.

For convenience we will introduce the modification of the initial mapping
\varphi
, but abusing notation, we will refer to it by the same letter
\varphi
throughout the paper:

\displaystyle\varphi\colon Y\to Y;\hskip 11.74988pt\varphi(\gamma)=\gamma+\frac{\dot{\gamma}}{\|\dot{\gamma}\|}-(1,0).

(1)

Proposition 3.1
.

The new map
\varphi
is well defined, i.e., for all
\gamma\in Y
its image
\varphi(\gamma)
also belongs to
Y
.

Proof.

We need to check that the three conditions from the definition of
Y
are satisfied by
\varphi(\gamma)
:

•

\varphi(\gamma)(a)=\gamma(a)+\frac{\dot{\gamma}(a)}{\|\dot{\gamma}(a)\|}-(1,0)=(0,0)+\frac{(1,0)}{1}-(1,0)=(0,0)
,

\varphi(\gamma)(b)=\gamma(b)+\frac{\dot{\gamma}(b)}{\|\dot{\gamma}(b)\|}-(1,0)=(1,0)+\frac{(1,0)}{1}-(1,0)=(1,0)

•

Consider the arc length parameter on a curve
\gamma
. Then

\dot{\varphi(\gamma)}(b)=\dot{\varphi(\gamma)}(a)=\left(\gamma(a)+\dot{\gamma}(a)-(1,0)\right)\dot{}=\dot{\gamma}(a)+\ddot{\gamma}(a)-(0,0)=(1,0)+(0,0)=(1,0)

•

For all
n>2
the following holds in the arc length parameter:

\varphi(\gamma)^{(n)}=\left(\gamma(a)+\dot{\gamma}(a)-(1,0)\right)^{(n)}=\gamma^{(n)}(a)+\gamma^{(n+1)}(a)-(0,0)=(0,0).

Therefore, the curve
\varphi(\gamma)
is an element of
Y
.

∎

4
Horizontal Unitrack Properties

Denote the projections of
\gamma_{n}
on the horizontal and vertical axes by
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
— two functions from the parameter interval to
{\mathbb{R}}
. Then the following holds:

Proposition 4.1
.

The number of ‘zeros’ of
\gamma_{n}
increases with
n
at least by one. More precisely, the number of solutions of the equation
y_{n+1}=0
is at least one bigger than the number of the solutions of
y_{n}=0
, if this number if finite.

The proof can be found in
[
10
]
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume that for every
n\geqslant 0
the curve
\gamma_{n}
with a parameter
t
satisfies
y_{n}(t)=f_{n}(x_{n}(t))
for some smooth function
f_{n}\colon[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
, i.e.,
\gamma_{n}
is a graph of a smooth function. Then for all
n
the function
f_{n}(x)
is a constant zero function. In other words, unless the unitrack is trivial, after some
n
the curves
\gamma_{n}
will fail to remain graphs of functions.

The next section of this paper will be devoted to the proof of this theorem. Before that, we introduce more definitions and present some conjectures.

Define the
horizontal amplitude
of the curve
\gamma(t)=(x(t),y(t))
with a parameter
t\in[a,b]
as:

H(\gamma)=\max\limits_{t\in[a,b]}{x(t)}-\min\limits_{t\in[a,b]}{x(t)}.

Similarly, the
vertical amplitude
of a curve
\gamma
is:

V(\gamma)=\max\limits_{t\in[a,b]}{y(t)}-\min\limits_{t\in[a,b]}{y(t)}.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assuming that
\gamma_{0}
is not trivial, the sequence
H(\gamma_{n})
is unbounded and non-decreasing. Moreover, there exist
c_{1},c_{2}>0
depending on
\gamma_{0}
, such that:

n-c_{1}\leqslant H(\gamma_{n})\leqslant 2n-c_{2}

This theorem follows from Theorem
4.2
, and the proof will be provided in the 6-th section of the paper.

Conjecture 4.4
.

The sequence
V(\gamma_{n})
is unbounded.

One may notice that Theorem
4.3
and Conjecture
4.4
are very similar. However, there is a very significant difference between horizontal and vertical directions in the horizontal unitrack construction. In the modified version of
\varphi
it is reflected in the last term of the formula (
1
).

Remark:
the fact that
V(\gamma_{n})
is an increasing sequence, follows from the Proposition
2.2
, as both global maximum and global minimum of

are greater and smaller than global maximum and minimum of

respectively.

Conjecture 4.5
.

If
\gamma_{0}
is not trivial, then there exists

, such that
\gamma_{n}
has self-intersections.

There is also a weaker variation of this conjecture, that is still open.

Conjecture 4.6
.

If
\gamma_{0}
is not trivial, then the unitrack consisting of the curves

, where

Proof of Theorem

Plan of the proof

•

We assume the opposite — that there exists a non-trivial curve
\gamma_{0}(t)
, such that all the curves

\gamma_{0}(t)=(t,f_{0}(t));\hskip 11.74988pt\gamma_{n+1}(t)=\gamma_{n}(t)+\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{n}(t)}{\|\dot{\gamma}_{n}(t)\|}-(1,0)\hskip 11.74988pt\text{for all}\,\,n\geqslant 0.

•

We introduce the function

that traces the action of

on horizontal coordinates of points on the curves (see Figure
4
). This function

relates the horizontal components of the curves in the following way:

•

From the initial assumptions we analyze the sequence of functions

, and obtain a pointwise limit

, that inherits some, but not all of the properties of

. For example,

is not necessarily continuous apriori, which causes some complications.

•

Take a closed segment

and consider a very large

. The segment of the curve
\gamma_{n}
, defined by the parameter interval

, is ”hanging above” a closed interval very close to

. Denote its length by
\delta=L(b)-L(a)
. We obtain an upper bound on the slope of the function

in terms of

. We do so by bounding the function

from above by

. Using Lemma

, we are able to obtain the estimate on

, restricted to an interval

\mathop{\rm Len\mskip 1.0mu}(\gamma_{0}|_{[a,b]})\leqslant\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}

A sketch with notations for the proof of Theorem

.

If
L
was continuous, in fact uniformly continuous, we would be able to estimate
s_{n}
from above by an arbitrarily small number. However, we cannot assume continuity of
L
, so we have to take an extra step.

•

We partition the segment of

into

\mathop{\rm Len\mskip 1.0mu}(\gamma_{0}|_{[a,b]})\leqslant\frac{\delta}{1-\frac{\delta}{m}}

We apply this estimate for all positive integers

\mathop{\rm Len\mskip 1.0mu}(\gamma_{0}|_{[a,b]})\leqslant\delta
.

•

It follows from injectivity and other properties of

that its image is a collection of intervals. Because of the estimate above the length of

is no greater than the sum of lengths of those intervals. Therefore the length of
\gamma_{0}
is not greater than

. But the curve connecting
(0,0)
and

has length at least

In the above notations for all
x\in[x_{n}(a),x_{n}(b)]
and for all
n>N
the following holds:

s_{n}(x)<d+\varepsilon-c=\delta+\varepsilon.

Proof.

Recall, that the function
s_{n}(x)=1-\cos\arctan{f_{n}^{\prime}(x)}
tracks how much
\varphi
moves each point to the left.

The sequence
x_{n}(t)
is a decreasing sequence with limit
L(t)
. From Proposition
5.1
also follows that
L(t)=t-\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}s_{k}(x_{k}(t))
with non-negative terms
s_{n}
. For
t\in[a,b]
we have

L(a)\leqslant L(t)\leqslant x_{n}(t)\leqslant d+\varepsilon,\hskip 11.74988pt\text{therefore}\hskip 11.74988pts_{n}(x_{n}(t))\leqslant x_{n}(t)-L(t)\leqslant\delta+\varepsilon.

∎

We will use Corollaries
5.4
and
5.5
to approximate the length of some segments of
\gamma_{0}
. To do that we will need the following obvious lemma:

Lemma 5.6
.

Consider a smooth function
h\colon[p,q]\to{\mathbb{R}}
with a bounded derivative
|h^{\prime}(x)|<c
. Then the length of its graph
\Gamma_{h}
satisfies the following inequality:

Len(\Gamma_{h})<|q-p|\cdot\left(\cos{\arctan{c}}\right)^{-1}=|q-p|\cdot\sqrt{c^{2}+1}.

Note, that the bound on the absolute value of the derivative
f_{n}^{\prime}
is equivalent to a certain bound on
-\cos\arctan f_{n}^{\prime}
, since the function
\cos\arctan(s)
has a global maximum at zero, and it is monotonously increasing on
(-\infty,0)
, and monotonously increasing on
(0,+\infty)
. In particular, the equivalent form of the same statement is as follows:

Lemma 5.7
.

Consider a function
h\colon[p,q]\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\cos\arctan h^{\prime}>C
. Then the length of its graph
\Gamma_{h}
satisfies the following inequality:

Len(\Gamma_{h})<\frac{|q-p|}{C}.

For any
[c,d]\subset S=\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits L
recall that
\delta=d-c
and
I_{C}^{D}=L^{-1}([c,d])
.

Proposition 5.8
.

For any closed interval
[a,b]\subset I_{C}^{D}
the following inequality holds:

Len\left(\gamma_{0}|_{[a,b]}\right)\leqslant\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}.

Proof.

We can apply Lemma
5.7
to:

h=f_{n}\colon[x_{n}(a),x_{n}(b)]\to{\mathbb{R}}\,\,\text{with}\,\,\cos{\arctan{f_{n}^{\prime}}}=1-s_{n}>1-\delta-\varepsilon,

and obtain that

Len(\gamma_{n}|_{[a,b]})\leqslant|\delta+\varepsilon|\cdot\frac{1}{1-\delta-\varepsilon}

According to Lemma
2.1
this inequality yields
Len(\gamma_{0}|_{[a,b]})\leqslant\frac{\delta+\varepsilon}{1-(\delta+\varepsilon)}
. Since this estimate holds for all
\varepsilon>0
, we conclude that,

Len(\gamma_{0}|_{[a,b]})\leqslant\frac{\delta}{1-\delta},

as needed.
∎

However, this is not a sharp estimate. Here’s an improved version of the previous proposition:

Proposition 5.9
.

In the notation of Proposition
5.8
the following inequality holds:

Len\left(\gamma_{0}|_{[a,b]}\right)\leqslant\delta

Proof.

Consider a partitioning of
[c,d]
into
m
equal closed intervals
K_{j}
of length
\frac{\delta}{m}
, where
1\leqslant j\leqslant m
. Define
I_{j}=L^{-1}(K_{j})\cap[a,b]
. Note, that
\{I_{j}\}
does not necessarily define a partition of
[a,b]
, since a preimage of a point could be a whole interval. However,
I_{j}
do cover
[a,b]
by definition.

Apply Proposition
5.8
to each of the segments
K_{j}
. We have the following estimates for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant m
:

Len(\gamma_{0}|_{I_{j}})\leqslant\frac{\frac{\delta}{m}}{1-\frac{\delta}{m}},

Len(\gamma_{0}|_{[a,b]})\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{m}Len(\gamma_{0}|_{I_{j}})\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\frac{\delta}{m}}{1-\frac{\delta}{m}}=m\cdot\frac{\frac{\delta}{m}}{1-\frac{\delta}{m}}=\frac{\delta}{1-\frac{\delta}{m}}.

Since this estimate holds for all natural
m
, we conclude that
Len(\gamma_{0}|_{[a,b]})\leqslant\delta
.
∎

5.5
Final step

Fix a connected component of
S=\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits L
, an interval
J
of length
\delta_{J}
, and denote by
I_{J}=L^{-1}(J)
its preimage under the map
L
.
Since the preimage of every closed segment
[c,d]\subset J
is a closed segment
[a,b]\subset I_{J}
, we can apply Proposition
5.9
to conclude that
Len(\gamma_{0}|_{[a,b]})\leqslant\delta_{J}
. This estimate holds for all closed segments
[a,b]\subset I_{J}
, so it is also true that
Len(\gamma_{0}|_{I_{J}})\leqslant\delta_{J}
.

The following observation gives us the global estimate on the length of
\gamma_{0}
. Since
[0,1]=\bigcup\limits_{J\subset S}I_{J}
, we conclude:

Len(\gamma_{0})\leqslant\sum_{J\subset S}Len(\gamma_{0}|_{I_{J}})\leqslant\sum_{J\subset S}\delta_{J}\leqslant 1.

A curve that connects points
(0,0)
and
(1,0)
has length at least
1
, with the equality only for a straight segment. Therefore,
\gamma_{0}
is indeed a straight segment, and
f_{n}\equiv 0
for all positive integers
n
, as wanted.
\blacksquare

6.1
Supplementary definitions

Most of the definitions make sense without the assumption that the curves
\gamma_{n}
are graphs of functions, so we will use the same notations as in the previous sections.

Let’s fix an arbitrary parameter
t\in[0,1]
on
\gamma_{0}
and use the same parameter on all the curves
\gamma_{n}
, as we did it earlier:

\gamma_{n+1}(t)=\gamma_{n}(t)+\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{n}(t)}{\|\dot{\gamma}_{n}(t)\|}-(1,0).

Recall that we define the functions
x_{n}(t)
and
y_{n}(t)
as cartesian coordinates of the curve
\gamma_{n}
. In other words,

\gamma_{n}(t)=(x_{n}(t),y_{n}(t)).

Let us introduce a new notation — the horizontal coordinate of the leftmost point of a curve
\gamma_{n}
:

l_{n}=\min\limits_{t\in[0,1]}x_{n}(t).

Along with the horizontal coordinate of the rightmost point of
\gamma_{n}
, this notation will help us analyze the horizontal amplitude
H_{n}
of the curves, which is defined in Section
4
. However, we will not need a separate notation for the horizontal coordinate of the rightmost points of the curves, since the following proposition holds:

Proposition 6.1
.

Define
r_{n}=\max\limits_{t\in[0,1]}x_{n}(t)
. Then for all positive integers
n
the double inequality holds:

\displaystyle 1\leqslant r_{n}\leqslant r_{0}.

(3)

Proof.

The functions
s_{n}
, defined in Subsection
5.2
, make sense in general, even without the assumption that all the curves
\gamma_{n}
are graphs of functions. We take the parameter
t
on the curve
\gamma_{n}
as the argument of
s_{n}
. Since for all
n
and for all
t
we have
s_{n}(t)\geqslant 0
, no point can move to the right as we apply
\varphi
, according to Proposition
5.1
. Therefore, the sequence
r_{n}
is non-increasing. At the same time,
\gamma_{n}(1)=(1,0)
for all
n
. Thus, the inequality (
3
) holds.
∎

By the definition of
H_{n}=H(\gamma_{n})
we conclude that
H_{n}=r_{n}-l_{n}\in\left[|l_{n}-r_{0}|,|l_{n}-1|\right]
. Hence, it is enough to analyze the asymptotic growth of the sequence
\{l_{n}\}
, since
\{r_{n}\}
is a bounded sequence.

6.2
Vertical Tangencies

It follows from Theorem
4.2
, that there exists a natural number
N
, such that
\gamma_{N}
is not a graph of a function
y=f(x)
. Since it is still a smooth curve connecting the points
(0,0)
and
(1,0)

t_{v}

\dot{\gamma}_{N}(t_{v})
is vertical.

For all
n>N
fix a parameter value

, such that
x_{n}(t_{n})=l_{n}
. As the following proposition claims, all the leftmost points have vertical tangencies.

Proposition 6.2
.

In above notations for all
n>N
the vector
\dot{\gamma}_{n}(t_{n})
is vertical.

Proof.

Here is the proof by induction starting from
N+1
.

Base
:
\dot{\gamma}_{N+1}(t_{N+1})
is vertical.

Assume that
x_{N}(t_{v})\geqslant 1
. In particular, there might be parameters
t_{r}\in(0,1)
such that
x_{N}(t_{r})\geqslant 1
. In this case recall that
\dot{\gamma}_{N}(1)=(1,0)
, which means that there exist parameters
t_{l}
arbitrarily close to

x_{N}(t_{l})<1
. Denote by
t_{v}^{\prime}
the parameter value at which

reaches minimum on a segment

x_{N}(t_{l})<1

x_{N}(t_{v}^{\prime})<1
. Since
\gamma_{N}
is smooth in both directions at

, we conclude that the tangent vector to
\gamma_{N}(t_{v}^{\prime})
is vertical. Therefore, without loss of generality we can pick the value of
t_{v}
such that
x_{N}(t_{v})<1
.

The image of a neighbourhood of a vertical tangency under

.

Since
x_{N}(t_{v})<1
, we conclude that:

x_{N+1}(t_{v})=x_{N}(t_{v})-1<0\,\,\Rightarrow\,\,l_{N+1}=x_{N+1}(t_{N+1})<0.

This inequality implies that

\dot{\gamma}_{N+1}(t_{N+1})

Step

6.3
Estimates

The latter proposition has a corollary that gives us an estimate on the asymptotics of the sequence
\{l_{n}\}

Corollary 6.3
.

In the above notations for all

l_{n+1}\leqslant l_{n}-1.

Proof.

The fact that
\gamma_{n}

x_{n+1}(t_{n})=x_{n}(t_{n})-1=l_{n}-1\,\,\Rightarrow\,\,l_{n+1}=x_{n+1}(t_{n+1})\leqslant x_{n+1}(t_{n})=l_{n}-1,

as needed.

On the other hand, the following observation follows from the general properties of the mapping

Proposition 6.4
.

For all

.

Proof.

Indeed, by definition
s_{n}(t)<2
for all

to the left under the action of
\varphi
.
∎

Combine the results of the Corollary

\displaystyle 1\leqslant l_{n}-l_{n+1}\leqslant 2

(4)

The following chain of inequalities follows from the formula (

l_{N}-k\geqslant l_{N+k}\geqslant l_{N}-2k;
