Baryogenesis from a Majorana Fermion Coupled to Quarks
Shrihari Gopalakrishna, Rakesh Tibrewala

Abstract
In the theory with a Majorana fermion ( 𝒳 {\cal X} ) coupled to quark-like fermions ( Q Q ) via a dimension-six four-fermion vector-vector interaction, we have computed in an earlier work the baryon asymmetry generated in the decay and scattering processes of the 𝒳 {\cal X} with Q Q . In this work we consider such processes in the expanding early Universe, set up the Boltzmann equations governing the 𝒳 {\cal X} and net baryon number densities, and numerically solve them in example benchmark points, taking the thermally averaged decay and scattering rates and their temperature dependence from the earlier study. We find that starting from a baryon symmetric Universe at early time, the presently observed baryon asymmetry of the Universe (BAU) can be explained in this theory over a wide range of mass scales, M χ ∈ ( 10 4 , 10 16 ) M_{\chi}\in(10^{4},10^{16}) GeV for appropriately chosen couplings. We find that scattering processes play a crucial role in generating the baryon asymmetry in this theory. We present our results in a general manner that should be useful not just in our theory, but also in other related theories that share the essential ingredients. Our results should help guide promising ways to probe such new physics in terrestrial experiments. For instance, in regions of parameter space that yield the observed BAU, we present the rate for neutron-antineutron ( n − n ¯ n\!-\!\bar{n} ) oscillation and discuss the prospects for observing this in upcoming experiments.



1
Introduction

Observationally, it is apparent that we live in a Universe in which there is more matter than antimatter at the present epoch,
the so-called baryon asymmetry of the Universe (BAU).
If the Big-Bang produced matter and antimatter in equal amounts, from a particle physics perspective,
how at late time the BAU emerges remains an open question.
For the asymmetry to develop, the three Sakharov conditions
[
1
]
must be satisfied in any candidate theory,
namely,
C
and
CP
violation, baryon number
B
violation, and departure from thermal equilibrium.
Although the standard model (SM) of particle physics has these necessary ingredients for producing an asymmetry,
it is commonly believed that it falls short in explaining the observed BAU by many orders of magnitude.
Various theories beyond the standard model (BSM) have been put forth to explain the observed BAU (for reviews,
see for example Refs.
[
2
,
3
]
).

In Ref.
[
4
]
we put forward one such proposal containing a Majorana fermion pair
with indefinite baryon number coupled to quark-like fermions, and showed that the three Sakharov conditions
can be satisfied in the theory, enabling the generation of the BAU.
In Ref.
[
5
]
we identify processes
with tree and loop contributions
for which the decay or scattering rate is different for the process and conjugate process carrying opposite
B
,
and compute the rate difference.

Our study here is focused on setting up and solving the Boltzmann equation
for the
{\cal X}
and baryon number densities in the high-temperature expanding Universe,
with the above rates appearing as inputs in the collision terms.
Starting in a baryon symmetric state and evolving through the epoch when the
{\cal X}
departs from thermal equilibrium,
we compute the asymptotic value of the baryon asymmetry,
and identify viable parameter regions that result in the observed BAU.
Although we are motivated by the above theory, we present our results in a manner that is applicable to other related theories.
We study the VV interaction, while in the literature the scalar-scalar (SS) interaction is studied;
for a more comprehensive list of references related to our work, see
Refs.
[
4
,
5
]
.
Furthermore, baryon asymmetry generation in decays is usually studied,
while we include both decay and scattering processes and consider baryon asymmetry generation directly into quarks.
Ref.
[
6
]
includes the multiple operator scattering contribution,
but with the SS interaction.

The paper is organized as follows:
In Sec.
2
we briefly review the theoretical framework that our work is based on.
In Sec.
3
we describe the cosmological setting and
develop the Boltzmann equations governing the
{\cal X}
and baryon number densities in the early Universe. In Appendix
A
we review thermodynamics applicable in the early Universe,
and,
in Appendix
B
we specify the way we handle the case when the
{\cal X}
is not thermalized at any epoch.
In Sec.
4
we present our numerical results,
comparing the decay and scattering rates and the Hubble expansion rate in Sec.
4.1
,
showing the solutions of the Boltzmann equations and the BAU we obtain in our theory in Sec.
4.2
,
and in Sec.
4.3
show the rates for neutron-antineutron oscillation in viable regions of parameter space.
In Appendix
C
we give a compilation from Ref.
[
5
]
of
the decay and scattering rates and the functional form for their temperature dependence that are needed above
as the collision terms in the Boltzmann equations.
In Sec.
5
we briefly discuss the possibility of a partial wash-out of the generated baryon number
from other baryon number violating processes such as the sphalerons.
In Sec.
6
we offer our conclusions.

2
The Theory for Baryon Asymmetry Generation

In this section we review the theoretical framework of Refs.
[
4
,
5
]

that our work is based on.
The theory extends the SM with a new electromagnetic (EM) charge neutral Dirac fermion
\chi
which
couples to an up-type color-triplet quark-like fermion
{U}
with EM charge
+2/3
,
and to two color-triplet down-type quark-like fermions
{D}
with EM charge
-1/3
,
via a dimension-six four-fermion vector-vector (VV) effective operator

{\cal L}^{\rm VV}_{\rm int}=\frac{1}{2\Lambda^{2}}\,[\overline{{D}^{c}_{b}}\,\gamma^{\mu}{D}_{a}]\ [\bar{\chi}\,\gamma_{\mu}\left(g_{L}P_{L}+g_{R}P_{R}\right){U}_{c}]\,\epsilon^{abc}+H.c.\ ,

(1)

where

\Lambda
is the cut-off scale,
the subscripts
a,b,c
on the fields are the color indices, and
D^{c}
is the charge conjugate field.
With the usual SM quark-like baryon number (
B
) assignments of
B({U})\!=\!B({D})\!=\!+1/3
,
from this interaction we assign
B(\chi)\!=\!+1
.

The masses for the vector-like fermions
{U},{D},\chi
are given by

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\rm Dirac\,mass}=-M_{{D}}\overline{{D}}{D}-M_{{U}}\overline{{U}}{U}-M_{\chi}\overline{\chi}\chi\ ,

(2)

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\rm Maj\,mass}=-\frac{1}{2}\overline{\chi^{c}}(\tilde{M}_{L}P_{L}+\tilde{M}_{R}P_{R})\,\chi+h.c.\ ,

(3)

where
M_{U},M_{D},M_{\chi}
are Dirac masses.
We also include Majorana masses
\tilde{M}_{L,R}
for the
\chi
that break baryon number,
which are the only sources of baryon number violation in our theory,
and therefore all baryon number rate asymmetries must be proportional to either of these.
The
\tilde{M}_{L,R}
splits the Dirac fermion
\chi
into two Majorana fermions
{\cal X}_{n}
, with indefinite baryon number.
We diagonalize the
\chi
mass matrix and work in the mass basis in which the VV interaction is
[
4
]

{\cal L}^{\rm VV}_{\rm int}=\frac{\epsilon^{abc}}{2\Lambda^{2}}\left[\widebar{{D}^{c}_{b}}\,\gamma^{\mu}{D}_{a}\right]\ \left[\bar{{\cal X}}_{n}\,G^{n}_{V\mu}{U}_{c}\right]+h.c.\ ,

(4)

where the Lorentz structure of the coupling
G_{V}
is detailed in Ref.
[
4
]
,
and is written in terms of
g_{\scriptscriptstyle L,R}
and the elements of the unitary matrix
{\cal U}
that diagonalizes the mass matrix,
with combination being denoted as
\hat{g}_{\scriptscriptstyle L,R}
.
The physical phases in the coupling and
{\cal U}
lead to
C
and
CP
violation.

In Ref.
[
4
]
we give many examples for how our effective theory could
be completed into a renormalizable UV-complete theory.
We argued there that from this perspective,
in addition to the effective interaction term in Eq. (
4
),
other effective operators could also be generated, namely,

{\cal L}\supset-\frac{1}{\Lambda^{2}}\,[\bar{{U}}_{c}\bar{G}^{n}_{V\mu}{\cal X}_{n}]\,[\overline{{\cal X}}_{m}{G^{m}_{V}}^{\mu}{U}_{c}]-\frac{1}{2\Lambda^{2}}(\delta^{aa^{\prime}}\delta^{bb^{\prime}}-\delta^{ab^{\prime}}\delta^{ba^{\prime}})[\overline{{D}}^{c}_{b}\,\tilde{g}\gamma^{\mu}{D}_{a}]\,[\bar{{D}}_{a^{\prime}}\,\tilde{g}\gamma_{\mu}{D}^{c}_{b^{\prime}}]\ .

(5)

In this work, we also include these operators.

The EM charges of the
{U},{D}
allow couplings with the SM
u,d
quarks.
For instance, in the simple case of the
{U},{D}
being
SU(2)_{L}
singlets, we can write interaction terms
[
4
]

{\cal L}_{\rm Yuk}\supset-\tilde{y}_{d}^{i}\,\bar{D}H^{\dagger}q_{L}^{i}+\tilde{y}_{u}^{i}\,\bar{U}H\!\cdot\!q_{L}^{i}+{\rm h.c.}\ ,

(6)

where
H
is the SM Higgs doublet,
the
q_{L}^{i}
are the three generation SM quark doublets with
i=\{1,2,3\}
being the SM generation index.
Electroweak symmetry breaking due to
\langle H\rangle=(0\ v/\sqrt{2})^{T}
generates mass mixing between

{U}\leftrightarrow u^{i}
and
{D}\leftrightarrow d^{i}
,
and combining this with the
u,d
SM mass terms, we can write in terms of

(u^{i}\ {U})^{T}
and
(d^{i}\ {D})^{T}
the
4\!\times\!4
up and down type mass matrices respectively.
These can be reduced to a block diagonal form by unitary rotations with (the sine of the) mixing angles

s^{\scriptscriptstyle uU}_{i}
and
s^{\scriptscriptstyle dD}_{i}
respectively (see, for example, Refs.
[
7
,
8
]
for details),
with the upper
3\!\times\!3
block identified with the three generation SM quark
u^{i},d^{i}
sectors.
The diagonalization of the SM quark sector then proceeds as in the SM to obtain the Cabibbo-Kobayashi-Maskawa (CKM) matrix as usual.
We can infer that the VV interaction of Eq. (
4
), due to this mixing, induces the operator with SM quarks

{\cal L}\supset\frac{\epsilon^{abc}}{2\Lambda^{2}}\,\hat{g}_{\scriptscriptstyle L,R}s_{\rm eff}^{ijk}\,\left[\widebar{d^{c}_{b}}^{i}\,\gamma^{\mu}d^{j}_{a}\right]\,\left[\bar{{\cal X}}\,\gamma_{\mu}P_{\scriptscriptstyle L,R}u^{k}_{c}\right]\ ,

(7)

where
the
i,j,k
are SM generation indices,
and,

s_{\rm eff}^{ijk}\equiv(s^{\scriptscriptstyle dD})^{2}s^{\scriptscriptstyle uU}V_{ijk}
, the
V_{ijk}
being combinations of the CKM rotation matrices.

Our focus in this work is on how the
{\cal X}
dynamics generates net baryon number in the early Universe.
The
u^{i}\leftrightarrow{U}
and
d^{i}\leftrightarrow{D}
mixings above are baryon number conserving
and only distribute the net baryon number across the SM flavors.
However, the CKM phase may have an effect on the amount of CP violation in the processes that are at play.
For a study on this, albeit in a different theoretical setting, see for example, Ref.
[
9
]
.
When the
{\cal X}
is integrated out, the operator in Eq. (
7
) with
i\!=\!j\!=\!k\!=\!1

induces neutron-antineutron oscillation.
Other choices of
i,j,k
induce flavor changing observables, which can be searched for in experiments.
We discuss these further in Sec.
4.3
.

In Ref.
[
5
]
, we identify loop contributions in
{\cal X}
decay and scattering processes
that generate a baryon asymmetry,
and compute the rate difference between the various processes and their corresponding conjugate processes,
and show their dependence on the couplings, phases, and masses in the theory
in the pseudo-Dirac limit
(M_{n}-M_{\chi})/M_{\chi}\!\ll\!1
.
Two classes of loop contributions are identified there, namely,
the single-operator (with only the operator in Eq. (
4
))
and the multiple-operator contributions (with the operators in Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
)),
that yield a rate difference between process and conjugate process,
which is computed for sample parameter space points – benchmark points BP-A and BP-B respectively.
In addition to these, in this study, we also include another benchmark point, BP-C, as explained in Sec.
4
.

The
{\cal X}
decay and scattering processes that generate the baryon asymmetry are set in the high-temperature early Universe.
The
{\cal X}
starts out being in thermal equilibrium (TE) and eventually decouples as the temperature falls,
and we track its number density using the Boltzmann equation (cf. Sec.
3.1
).
For the relevant decay and scattering processes,
in Ref.
[
5
]

we compute the decay width and scattering cross section as a function of initial state momenta
and average them over the thermal distribution functions to obtain the thermally averaged rates.
We summarize those findings in Sec.
4.1
and Appendix
C
.

For our study here,
we import from Ref.
[
5
]
the thermally averaged decay and scattering rates,
keeping their temperature dependence, but making the following simplifying assumptions.
It is seen there that the rate difference is largely generated in the decay and scattering of the
{\cal X}_{2}
,
and we therefore keep only this state in our analysis here, denoting it simply as
{\cal X}
with mass
M
.
Furthermore, we do not wish to repeat here how the rates are affected by the coupling structure and phases in
G_{V}

as it has already been elucidated in that study, and therefore denote its coupling simply as
g
,
but retain how the decay and scattering rates scale with
g
and
\Lambda
.
Keeping track of the distinction between the
{U},{D}
is also not important for our analysis here,
and we denote them collectively as
Q
with mass
M_{Q}
.
Thus, we abstract from Ref.
[
5
]
the thermally averaged rates,

\langle\Gamma\rangle
,
\langle\Gamma^{(\sigma)}\rangle
and
\langle\Gamma^{\prime}\rangle
,
for the decay process
{\cal X}\rightarrow QQQ
, the scattering process
{\cal X}Q^{c}\rightarrow QQ
,
and the
\Delta B\!=\!2
scattering process
QQQ\rightarrow Q^{c}Q^{c}Q^{c}
, respectively,
along with the rates for their conjugate and inverse processes, with
Q\!=\!\{{U},{D}\}
appropriately chosen.
In this work, we consider these processes in the early Universe,
and compute the BAU from the knowledge of these thermally averaged decay and scattering rates.
We turn next to a discussion of how the BAU comes about in our theory.

3
The Cosmological Setting

In this section we place the microscopic processes reviewed in Sec.
2
in a cosmological setting,
and develop the Boltzmann equation (BE) for the net baryon number in the early Universe to determine the BAU.

The BAU today can be expressed as the ratio

\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\equiv\frac{n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}}{n_{\gamma}}=\frac{(n_{b}-n_{\bar{b}})}{n_{\gamma}}\ ,{\rm\ or,}\quad Y_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\!\equiv\!\frac{(n_{b}-n_{\bar{b}})}{s}\,\ ,

(8)

with
n_{b}
(
n_{\bar{b}}
) the number density of baryons (anti-baryons),
n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
the net baryon number density,
n_{\gamma}
is the number density of photons today,
s
is the entropy density,
and
Y_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
is the baryon number
yield
(see Appendix
A
for a review of thermodynamics applicable in the expanding early Universe).
We can write
\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\!\approx\!6\times 10^{-10}\,\times(\Omega_{b}h^{2}/0.022)
and taking the CMB observations
[
10
]
of
\Omega_{b}h^{2}\!\approx\!0.022

we have
\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle B}^{\rm obs}\!\approx\!6\times 10^{-10}
,
which agrees well with the predictions from BBN
[
11
]

The observed BAU can equivalently be written as
Y_{\scriptscriptstyle B}^{\rm obs}\approx 0.85\times 10^{-10}

In Ref.
[
5

{\cal X}

\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle\chi}\!=\!0

As explained there,
with
n_{\scriptscriptstyle\Delta{U}}\!=\!n_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}-n_{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{U}}
,
n_{\scriptscriptstyle\Delta{D}}\!=\!n_{\scriptscriptstyle{D}}-n_{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{D}}
,
the form of the VV interaction with antisymmetry in color

n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\!=\!(n_{\scriptscriptstyle\Delta{U}}+2n_{\scriptscriptstyle\Delta{D}})/3

There, we present the BEs for the evolution of the number densities of the
{\cal X},{U},{D}

in the expanding Universe as a function of temperature

and the BE for

in terms of the BEs for

In this work we drop the distinction between the
Q=\{{U},{D}\}

and denote by
Q

either
of the

In this limit we have

,
n_{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{Q}}\!=\!n_{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{U}}+2n_{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{D}}
, and,

.
The equilibrium number densities are
n_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}\!=\!n_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm(eq)}\!=\!n_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}e^{\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}/T}
and
n_{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{Q}}\!=\!n_{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{Q}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm(eq)}\!=\!n_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}e^{\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{Q}}/T}
,
where
n_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
is the number density with zero chemical potential.
Due to the QED processes in equilibrium, we have
\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{Q}}\!=\!-\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}
.
We write here the BE for
n_{\scriptscriptstyle{\cal X}},n_{\scriptscriptstyle Q},n_{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{Q}}
,
and obtain the BE for
n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
by taking the difference between the BEs for
n_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}
and
n_{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{Q}}
.
The
{\cal X}
, however, being EM neutral and coupled via the VV interaction,
could behave quite differently depending on how the interaction rate
compares to the Hubble expansion rate.
If the
{\cal X}
were to be in TE, the Majorana nature of
{\cal X}
implies
\mu_{\chi}\!=\!0

and its number density would be
n_{\scriptscriptstyle{\cal X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
,
and when it departs from TE its number density

We start in a baryon-antibaryon symmetric early Universe, i.e.
Y_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(T\!\gg\!M_{\chi})\!=\!0
,
at a temperature much above the electroweak symmetry breaking scale of the order of a TeV.
As
T
falls in the expanding Universe,
it is the
{\cal X}
that has a chance to deviate from TE first, i.e. to decouple from the thermal plasma,
due to its weaker effective coupling,
when the
{\cal X}
decay and scattering rates and inverse rates become smaller than the Hubble expansion rate
H(T)
(cf. Sec.

We expect the
{\cal X}
to eventually decouple for some

, where
x\equiv M_{\chi}/T
,
since the interaction rates
of the
{\cal X}
with
Q

,
due to its dependence on
n_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}
(cf. Sec.

)
with
n_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}=n_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}^{(eq)}\sim e^{-x}
being exponentially suppressed.

Given the

3.1
The Boltzmann Equations

Here we derive the Boltzmann Equation (BE) for the
{\cal X}
number density
n_{\chi}
,
and for the net baryon number density
n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
in the (expanding) early Universe
(for a review, see for example, Ref.
[
2
]
, and Appendix
A
).
The amount of the BAU generated in our theory depends on the parameters of our theory reviewed in Sec
2
, namely,
the mass scale
M_{\chi}
, the cutoff scale
\Lambda
, and, the coupling
g
.

We list next the relevant reaction rates that are the collision terms on the rhs of the BE.
For the decay process
{\cal X}\rightarrow QQQ
and its conjugate process
{\cal X}\rightarrow Q^{c}Q^{c}Q^{c}

with the zero-
T
rest-frame decay widths
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{c}
and the decay width difference
\Delta\Gamma=\Gamma-\Gamma^{c}
,
we obtain the thermally averaged decay rate and decay rate difference as
[
5
]

\langle\Gamma\rangle=f_{TD}\Gamma\ ;\ \langle\Gamma^{c}\rangle=f_{TD}\Gamma^{c}\ ,\ \langle\Delta\Gamma\rangle=f_{TD}\Delta\Gamma\ ,\ \ {\rm where}\ f_{TD}(x)\equiv\frac{M}{\left<E(p)\right>}=\frac{K_{1}(\hat{M}x)}{K_{2}(\hat{M}x)}\ ,

(9)

where

x=M_{\chi}/T
,
\hat{M}=M/M_{\chi}
,
and,

K_{1,2}
are the modified Bessel functions of the second kind.

f_{TD}
is nothing but the thermal average of the (inverse of the) time-dilation factor.
We use
\bar{Q}
and
Q^{c}
, or
\bar{\Gamma}
and
\Gamma^{c}
interchangeably as the distinction is unimportant when dealing with spin-summed rates.
The total decay rate is
\langle\Gamma_{\!\!{\cal X}}\rangle=\langle\Gamma\rangle+\langle\bar{\Gamma}\rangle
.
Thus, given the zero-temperature decay rate
\Gamma
and rate difference
\Delta\Gamma
, for instance from our study in Ref.
[
5
]
,
we obtain their thermal averages
\langle\Gamma\rangle,\penalty\ \langle\Delta\Gamma\rangle
by simply multiplying by the
f_{TD}(x)
factor above.
The inverse-decay channels are

QQQ\rightarrow{\cal X}
and
Q^{c}Q^{c}Q^{c}\rightarrow{\cal X}
respectively.

The thermally averaged scattering rate is

\langle\Gamma^{(\sigma)}\rangle=\left<\sigma_{{\cal X}\bar{Q}}v\right>n_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
, given in terms of the scattering cross section,
and
for the conjugate process is
\langle\bar{\Gamma}^{(\sigma)}\rangle=\left<\sigma_{{\cal X}Q}v\right>n_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
.
The relevant scattering cross sections are

\sigma_{{\cal X}\bar{Q}}\equiv\sigma({\cal X}Q^{c}\rightarrow QQ)
,

\sigma_{{\cal X}Q}\equiv\sigma({\cal X}Q\rightarrow Q^{c}Q^{c})
,
with their inverse scattering channel cross sections being

\sigma_{QQ}\equiv\sigma(QQ\rightarrow{\cal X}Q^{c})
,

\sigma_{\bar{Q}\bar{Q}}\equiv\sigma(Q^{c}Q^{c}\rightarrow{\cal X}Q)
respectively,
and the difference in scattering rates is

\langle\Delta\Gamma^{(\sigma)}\rangle=\langle\Delta\sigma\,v\rangle n_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
with
\Delta\sigma=\sigma_{{\cal X}\bar{Q}}-\sigma_{{\cal X}Q}
.
We denote the thermally averaged LO scattering rate of the
\Delta B\!=\!2
process

QQQ\rightarrow Q^{c}Q^{c}Q^{c}
as

4.3
Terrestrial Probes: Neutron-Antineutron (
n\!-\!\bar{n}
) Oscillation

Having identified the mass scale
M_{\chi}
and the corresponding
g
,
M_{\chi}/\Lambda
values for which
the observed BAU is obtained in our theory,
we contemplate here terrestrial experiments that could help us test this new physics.

Proton decay is forbidden in our theory at the perturbative level because Lorentz invariance requires at least one final state fermion,
either a lepton or antilepton as they are the only SM fermions lighter than the proton,
and this is not allowed in our theory since
L
is a good symmetry.

The
{\cal X}
induces
\Delta B\!=\!2
processes,
for instance, the neutron-antineutron (
n\!-\!\bar{n}
) oscillation;
see Refs.
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
, Refs.
[
18
,
19
]
for reviews,
and Refs.
[
20
,
21
,
22

23
]
for some recent studies.
The
n\!-\!\bar{n}
oscillation is being searched for in experiments but no positive signal has been detected to date.
Currently, among the strongest bounds on the oscillation time is
from the Super-Kamiokande (SK) experiment, which is
\hat{\tau}_{n\bar{n}}\geq 4.7\times 10^{8}
s
[
24
]
at the 90% confidence limit,
which implies
({\Delta\hat{m}}_{n\bar{n}})_{\rm SK}=1/\hat{\tau}_{n\bar{n}}\leq 10^{-34}
GeV.
Ref.
[
25
]
summarizes the theoretical landscape and the prospects in future experiments;
for instance, the HIBEAM/NNBAR experiment at the European Spallation Source (ESS)
anticipates a three orders of magnitude improvement
[
26
]
in the sensitivity.

In our theory, starting from Eq. (
7
) and integrating out the
{\cal X}
we obtain
an effective operator
[
4

Q_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle VV}\equiv\epsilon^{abc}\epsilon^{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}c^{\prime}}(\widebar{u^{c}_{c^{\prime}}}\gamma^{\nu}\gamma^{\mu}P_{\scriptscriptstyle L,R}\,u_{c})\,(\widebar{d^{c}_{b}}\gamma_{\mu}d_{a})\,(\widebar{d^{c}_{b^{\prime}}}\gamma_{\nu}d_{a^{\prime}})\ ,

where the subscript on the fields and on the
\epsilon
tensor denote color indices,
the superscript
c
on the fields denotes charge-conjugation,
and we call attention to the vector nature of this operator.
Clearly, this operator induces neutron-antineutron oscillations.
The
n\!-\!\bar{n}
oscillation rate in our theory is discussed in detail in Ref.
[
4
]
and can be written as

{\Delta m}_{n\bar{n}}=\frac{g^{2}s_{\rm eff}^{2}}{\Lambda^{4}M_{\chi}}\left<\bar{n}|Q_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle VV}|n\right>\ ,

(18)

where

is as discussed in Sec.
2

The matrix elements
\left<\bar{n}|Q_{i}|n\right>\equiv\Lambda_{\rm QCD}^{6}\langle\hat{Q}_{i}\rangle
are most reliably computed using lattice gauge theory (LGT) techniques.
Since, to the best of our knowledge, there is no LGT computation available for vector operators as appropriate for us,
we Fierz rearrange the
Q_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle VV}
operator as explained in Ref.
[
4
]
to obtain related scalar operators (plus other vector and tensor operators),
whose matrix elements have been computed in LGT, for example, in Ref.
[
27
]
.
In the basis of scalar operators there, the

are relevant to the Fierz transformed

numerically the largest,
being
\langle\hat{Q}_{3}\rangle=-13
in our notation, for
\Lambda_{\rm QCD}=0.18
GeV.
Till such time that LGT results for the vector operator matrix elements become available,
we content ourselves to presenting our results by estimating the oscillation rate
taking this scalar operator matrix element obtained in the Fierz rearrangement.

We wish to derive next the
n\!-\!\bar{n}
oscillation bound on our theory from SK,
and also infer the future prospects in upcoming experiments.
For this, we define the improvement factor

10^{\kappa_{n\bar{n}}}\equiv\frac{s_{\rm eff}^{2}}{e_{n\bar{n}}}=2.5\times 10^{-31}\left(\frac{13}{\langle\hat{Q}\rangle}\right)\left(\frac{M_{\chi}}{1\penalty\ {\rm GeV}}\right)^{5}\left[\frac{1}{g\,(M_{\chi}/\Lambda)^{2}}\right]^{2}\ .

(19)

5
Partial wash-out and the final
n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}

In our theory, we have computed in Sec.
4
,
the baryon number yield that is generated by our baryogenesis mechanism and
has stabilized to its asymptotic value
n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(t_{e})\!\equiv\!{n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle e}

at the time
t_{e}
corresponding to
x_{e}\!=\!M_{\chi}/T_{e}
.
We have included in our work the effect of
decay and scattering processes, including
\Delta B\!=\!\pm 2
scattering processes.

Independent of our baryon number generating mechanism,
if some other mechanism such as leptogenesis
[
12
]
has, in addition, generated a nonzero lepton number density
n_{L}(t_{e})\!\equiv\!{n_{\scriptscriptstyle L}}_{\scriptscriptstyle e}
,
we have
n_{\scriptscriptstyle B-L}(t_{e})\!=\!{n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle e}-{n_{\scriptscriptstyle L}}_{\scriptscriptstyle e}
and
n_{\scriptscriptstyle B+L}(t_{e})\!=\!{n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle e}+{n_{\scriptscriptstyle L}}_{\scriptscriptstyle e}
.
At
t_{e}
, the baryon number generated in our mechanism is carried by the
{U},{D}
.
As discussed in Ref.
[
4
]
and reviewed in Sec.
2
, if the
{U},{D}
mix with the SM quarks,
they eventually decay and faithfully transfer this baryon number to SM quarks and leptons
via
B
and
L
conserving interactions,
and
the above
n_{\scriptscriptstyle B-L}
and
n_{\scriptscriptstyle B+L}
values persist even after their decay, now carried by the SM quarks and leptons.

If other
B
violating processes occur following this,
it may cause a partial wash-out of the generated
B
, leading to the survival fraction
w\!<\!1
.
One such effect is the anomalous non-conservation of baryon number
B

[
29
]

due to electroweak sphalerons
[
30
]
, which violate
B+L
but conserve
B-L
,
and is operative at the scale of the electroweak phase transition.
Ref.
[
31
]
claims that if sphalerons are unsuppressed, they drive
n_{\scriptscriptstyle B+L}\!\rightarrow\!0
,
but in a detailed analysis, Ref.
[
32
]
argues that
B+L
reduces but stabilizes at a nonzero value,
and if lepton number violation is also present, for example as Majorana masses of neutrinos,
the requirement that a nonzero baryon number survives places nontrivial constraints on the neutrino masses and the scale of
B-L
violation.
We therefore allow for a nonzero terminal value of
B+L
and take the surviving fraction to be not zero but
\alpha_{w}n_{\scriptscriptstyle B+L}
, with
0\!<\!\alpha_{w}\!<\!1
.
Thus, if the sphalerons are operative
after
time
t_{e}
, following its action,
and after antiparticles have annihilated away leaving only particles, at time
t_{s}
,
we have
n_{\scriptscriptstyle B+L}(t_{s})\!=\!\alpha_{w}n_{\scriptscriptstyle B+L}(t_{e})
, and,

n_{\scriptscriptstyle B-L}(t_{s})\!=\!n_{\scriptscriptstyle B-L}(t_{e})
is unchanged since sphalerons conserve
B-L
.
Denoting
{n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle s}
and
{n_{\scriptscriptstyle L}}_{\scriptscriptstyle s}
,
This implies

n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(t_{s})\!\equiv\!{n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle s}\!=\!(1/2)[(1\!+\!\alpha_{w}){n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle e}\!-\!(1\!-\!\alpha_{w}){n_{\scriptscriptstyle L}}_{\scriptscriptstyle e}]
and

n_{L}(t_{s})\!\equiv\!{n_{\scriptscriptstyle L}}_{\scriptscriptstyle s}\!=\!-(1/2)[(1\!-\!\alpha_{w}){n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle e}\!-\!(1\!+\!\alpha_{w}){n_{\scriptscriptstyle L}}_{\scriptscriptstyle e}]
.

Following such processes, after antiparticles have annihilated away,
we have a thermal bath of photons, protons, neutrons, electrons, neutrinos, and antineutrinos,
with number densities
n_{\gamma}
,
n_{p}
,
n_{n}
,
n_{e}
,
n_{\nu}
,
n_{\bar{\nu}}
respectively,
where we allow for the possibility of a nonzero
n_{\bar{\nu}}
.
EM charge conservation ensures that
p^{+}
and
e^{-}
are in equal numbers, i.e.
n_{p}(t_{s})\!=\!n_{e}(t_{s})
,
consistent with the EM charge neutral Universe we observe today.
We have

n_{p}(t_{s})+n_{n}(t_{s})\!=\!{n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle s}\
and
n_{e}(t_{s})+n_{\Delta\nu}(t_{s})\!=\!{n_{\scriptscriptstyle L}}_{\scriptscriptstyle s}
where
n_{\Delta\nu}\!=\!n_{\nu}-n_{\bar{\nu}}
.
Defining
r_{np}\!\equiv\!n_{n}/n_{p}
, we have
{n_{p}}_{s}\!=\!{n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle s}/(1+r_{np})
and
{n_{n}}_{s}\!=\!{n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle s}r_{np}/(1+r_{np})
.
Thus, given the
{n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle e}
and
{n_{\scriptscriptstyle L}}_{\scriptscriptstyle e}
, and the
\alpha_{w}
and
r_{np}
obtained from the sphaleron dynamics,
all the number densities are determined in terms of these.
If we assume the isospin symmetric limit
n_{p}(t_{s})\!=\!n_{n}(t_{s})
, i.e.
r_{np}\!=\!1
,
we obtain

{n_{p}}_{s}\!=\!{n_{n}}_{s}\!=\!{n_{e}}_{s}\!=\!(1/4)[(1\!+\!\alpha_{w}){n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle e}\!-\!(1\!-\!\alpha_{w}){n_{\scriptscriptstyle L}}_{\scriptscriptstyle e}]
and

{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\Delta\nu}}_{s}\!=\!-(3/4)[(1\!-\!\alpha_{w}/3){n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle e}\!-\!(1\!+\!\alpha_{w}/3){n_{\scriptscriptstyle L}}_{\scriptscriptstyle e}]
.
In the limit of
\alpha_{w}\!\rightarrow\!0
and
{n_{\scriptscriptstyle L}}_{\scriptscriptstyle e}\!\rightarrow\!0
, we have a wash-out factor for
n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
of
w_{\rm sph}\!=\!1/2
.

The total wash-out factor for
n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
, namely
w_{\rm tot}
, is the product of the wash-out factors due to each such effect (if there are more),
and the baryon number density following such baryon number violating processes
is
n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(t_{s})\!=\!w_{\rm tot}n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(t_{e})
, which is to be matched to the observed BAU.
For a given
w_{\rm tot}
, the parameter space point that leads to the observed BAU can be inferred from our results in
Sec.
4
, in particular, Figs.
4
,
5
,
by reading off the parameters corresponding not to the
Y_{\scriptscriptstyle B}/Y_{\scriptscriptstyle B}^{\rm obs}\!=\!1
contour as we would have done ignoring wash-out,
but rather to the
1/w_{\rm tot}
contour.

Our effective theory is to be UV-completed at around the
\Lambda
scale,
and many completions are discussed in Ref.
[
4
]
(see also Appendix
B
),
including, for instance, that the VV effective interaction is resolved by a vector state
\xi^{c}_{\mu}
.
Also, a simple example for the origin of the Majorana mass term from a baryon number conserving UV completion
is realized by adding a scalar field
\Phi_{B}
with
B(\Phi_{B})=-2
,
whose nonzero vacuum expectation value (VEV) leads to the
\chi
Majorana mass, breaking baryon number
(see Ref.
[
4
]
, and Appendix
B

In the early Universe, at temperature

Conclusions

Our focus here has been the baryon asymmetry generated in the early Universe in our theory in Ref.
[
4
]

involving a Majorana fermion pair
{\cal X}
coupled to quark-like fermions via a dimension-six four-fermion vector-vector (VV) interaction
of the form
(g/\Lambda^{2})(\overline{{D}^{c}}\,\gamma^{\mu}{D})\ (\bar{{\cal X}}\gamma_{\mu}{U})

Here we collectively denote as
Q\!=\!\{{U},{D}\}
with baryon number
B(Q)\!=\!1/3
,
and

is then directly related to
n_{\scriptscriptstyle Q}-n_{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{Q}}
, the difference in the
Q
and the
Q^{c}
number densities.

We set up the Boltzmann equations for the
{\cal X}
yield
Y_{\cal X}(x)\!=\!n_{\scriptscriptstyle{\cal X}}/s
and the baryon number yield

,
as a function of
x\!=\!M_{\chi}/T
in the early Universe,
where
n_{\scriptscriptstyle{\cal X}},n_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
are the
{\cal X}
and baryon number densities respectively, and
s
is the entropy density.
We include in the collision terms of the Boltzmann equations
the rates for the decay process
{\cal X}\rightarrow QQQ
,
the scattering process
{\cal X}Q^{c}\rightarrow QQ
,
and,
\Delta B\!=\!2
scattering process
QQQ\rightarrow Q^{c}Q^{c}Q^{c}
,
along with their conjugate and inverse processes and compute this asymmetry.
We take the thermally averaged decay and scattering rates with their temperature dependence (i.e.
x
dependence) from Ref.
[
5
]

for the single-operator and multiple-operator contributions in benchmark points BP-A and BP-B respectively.
In addition, we define BP-C here to explore the possibility of the baryon asymmetry generated in
{\cal X}
decay.
We thus have nine benchmark points BP-nX for n={1,2,3} and X={A,B,C} having the

as in Ref.
[
5

,
while BP-C is with the same

the decay contribution generating the baryon asymmetry. Such a situation may arise in other theories.
This allows us to explore the various aspects of the baryon asymmetry generation in a fairly general way
for a wide range of mass scale
M_{\chi}
.

Starting from an initially baryon symmetric Universe with
Y_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(x_{b})\!=\!0
,
we solve the Boltzmann equations numerically and
find the regions of parameter space
M_{\chi},g,M_{\chi}/\Lambda
of our new physics theory
for which

The baryon asymmetry is generated mainly in scattering processes in BP-A and BP-B,
and including these scattering rates in the Boltzmann equations is important.
Most other studies in the literature usually ignore scattering and include only decays.
In BP-C we find that the asymmetry is generated mainly in decay processes.
We also present our results model independently by varying directly the
\Delta\Gamma

\Delta\sigma\,v

Our most important finding here is that

and
M_{\chi}\!\sim\!(10^{4},10^{16})

We present the

values.
Upcoming experiments will improve the mass reach, potentially uncovering such physics.
High-

Appendix B
Out of thermal equilibrium
{\cal X}
and UV completion

If the
{\cal X}

x_{b}

Of all the BPs considered, this scenario is encountered only in BP-1B (cf. Sec.

This signals that the generation of baryon asymmetry becomes dependent on the UV completion details.
We consider an example UV completion, and abstract from this a parametrization
that is applicable to other UV completions also.

Consider an example UV completion (cf. Ref
[
4
]
),
in which
B
is conserved, but only spontaneously broken.
We have

B(\Phi_{B})=-2

M_{\Phi}
, and
\Phi^{\prime}
another scalar with
B(\Phi^{\prime})=0

M_{\Phi^{\prime}}
.
The potential is such that they acquire VEVs
\left<\Phi_{B}\right>=v_{\Phi}/\sqrt{2}

{\cal X}

,

\left<\Phi^{\prime}\right>=v^{\prime}_{\Phi}/\sqrt{2}

{\cal X}

M_{\chi}=y^{\prime}v^{\prime}_{\Phi}

We are in the pseudo-Dirac limit if
M_{\chi}\approx\tilde{M}
.
The VV effective operators we have been working with in this study are generated by integrating out other states at the cutoff scale
\Lambda

The coupling of the
\Phi_{B}
,
\Phi^{\prime}

In contrast, there are no renormalizable operators directly coupling the
{\cal X}

It is perhaps worth clarifying that the one operator which could have done this, namely,
\bar{\cal X}L\cdot H

{\cal X}
carries nonzero
B

If
\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}

\Phi_{B},\Phi^{\prime}

\Phi
either of the

\Phi\rightarrow{\cal X}{\cal X}

(29)
