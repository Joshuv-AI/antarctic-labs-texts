The effect of avatar identity on level-2 visual perspective taking in individuals with schizophrenia
Authors: Pei Xie, ChaoZheng Huang, Li Lu, HanBin Sang, AiBao Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study investigated whether individuals with schizophrenia (SZ) exhibit impairments in Level-2 visual perspective-taking (VPT)—the ability to understand how a scene appears from another’s viewpoint—and if using a self-represented avatar could mitigate these deficits.MethodsTwo experiments were conducted. Experiment 1 (N = 63) used a digit inversion task to assess Level-2 VPT based on object appearance. Experiment 2 (N = 62) used a round-table task to assess it based on spatial location. Participants with SZ and healthy controls (HC) performed both tasks under two conditions: using a ‘self-avatar’ and an ‘other-avatar’.ResultsIndividuals with SZ demonstrated significant Level-2 VPT impairments in both experiments. Compared to HC, the SZ group showed significantly lower accuracy and a stronger egocentric bias, particularly in perspective-incongruent conditions. Critically, the self-avatar identity did not produce a selective performance benefit in the SZ group.ConclusionsThe findings confirm pervasive Level-2 VPT deficits in schizophrenia across different task domains. The absence of a schizophrenia-specific self-avatar benefit suggests that an instruction-based self-avatar manipulation alone may be insufficient to selectively improve Level-2 VPT performance in schizophrenia. The cognitive mechanisms underlying this pattern remain to be established.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Visual perspective taking (VPT) enables individuals to understand their own and others’ visual content during social interactions, helping them better infer others’ mental states. VPT is divided into Level-1 VPT, which involves understanding whether another person can see an object, and Level-2 VPT, which involves judging an object’s location and features from another person’s perspective [22]. Previous research has shown that individuals with schizophrenia exhibit impairments in visual and cognitive perspective-taking [19], as well as reduced spontaneous perspective-taking [18]. These abnormalities may further affect their ability to infer others’ mental states. This study aimed to explore factors influencing Level-2 VPT abnormalities in individuals with schizophrenia.

Given these impairments in schizophrenia, it is crucial to understand the underlying mechanisms of Level-2 VPT, which involves more complex cognitive processes than Level-1 VPT. According to previous studies, it encompasses two distinct representational processes: one based on the appearance of the object (i.e., how the object stimulus appears from another person’s viewpoint) [39], and another based on the spatial location of the object (i.e., where the object stimulus is situated from another person’s perspective) [5]. Both processes are influenced by the angular disparity between the observer and perspective-taking positions [34]. The primary strategy employed by individuals to complete Level-2 VPT tasks is embodied self-rotation [27]. This strategy requires mentally rotating one’s own body schema to the position of another observer before making judgments [24]. This process requires mental rotation and embodied cognition. This appears to be challenging for individuals with schizophrenia.

Previous studies have found that, in mental rotation tasks, individuals with schizophrenia exhibit patterns like those of control participants, with performance influenced by orientation, condition, and laterality. However, individuals with schizophrenia exhibited slower responses and reduced accuracy [6]. To investigate the bodily rotation abilities of patients with schizophrenia, researchers have employed hand rotation tasks. In these tasks, participants must determine, as quickly and accurately as possible, whether a hand stimulus presented on the screen in various orientations is in their left or right hand. When the hands were randomly presented in six different orientations (0°, 60°, 120°, 180°, 240°, and 300°), the patients exhibited slower reaction times and lower accuracy in behavioral performance [21]. This evidence suggests that impaired mental rotation in individuals with schizophrenia affects their Level-2 VPT performance. Moreover, some studies have found virtual avatars as another influencing factor in Level-2 VPT [40] and proposed that the identity of the virtual avatar, a key factor influencing Level-2 VPT, may modulate the efficiency of employing a mental rotation strategy [17, 26], particularly in populations with cognitive challenges such as schizophrenia.

Amorim et al. [1] proposed that mental rotation might be linked to embodied cognition, suggesting that it is facilitated, at least when processing body-related stimuli, by mapping body-relevant spatial reference frames onto embodied representations. Furthermore, the appearance of these embodied representations influences the mental rotational performance. Previous studies that substituted a human avatar in the digit inversion task (Level-2 VPT) with a robot avatar have investigated the appearance of perspective-taking from the robot’s viewpoint. These studies found that although participants spontaneously adopted the robot’s perspective, the rate of perspective taking was lower than that for a human avatar [40]. This indicates that the type of agent is crucial for individual mentalization. According to Ford et al. [9], the relationship between a virtual avatar’s identity and a participant influences the participant’s VPT. Participants exhibited greater ease in achieving Level-2 VPT with avatars similar to them in age or gender. Consequently, we posit that when an avatar’s identity represents its own self (i.e., the most familiar self), individuals with schizophrenia might find it easier to perform Level-2 VPT. Although most healthy individuals utilize a self-based spatial rotation strategy for Level-2 VPT, this process requires them to mentally simulate their own spatial framework on the avatar. Some studies instructed participants to adopt a self-body rotation strategy, imagining themselves rotating behind an avatar [15]. For individuals with schizophrenia, mentally simulating a secondary self-represented avatar for rotation undoubtedly imposes a greater cognitive load. However, if the avatar in the scene is imagined as itself, the participants may find direct mental rotation considerably easier. Our previous study found that when the avatar in the scene represented their own self, it facilitated spontaneous VPT at Level 1 in individuals with schizophrenia [37].

In summary, to explore the influence of avatar identity on Level-2 VPT in individuals with schizophrenia, the present study employed two sub-studies. These sub-studies separately examined participants’ performance based on the appearance and spatial location of the object, utilizing the digit inversion task [40] and round-table task [9], respectively. Within sub-study in each Experiment, participants were randomly assigned to complete either the baseline condition (where they were instructed to imagine the avatar as a stranger) or the self-represented avatar (where they were instructed to imagine the avatar as themselves) on the first day, followed by the other condition on the following day. Based on the aforementioned evidence, we hypothesized that individuals with schizophrenia would perform better in the self-represented avatar task than in the baseline task. The present study represents a systematic investigation of how a specifically self-represented avatar identity modulates distinct dimensions of Level-2 VPT in schizophrenia. These findings are expected to provide novel insights into the mechanisms underlying social cognitive deficits in individuals with schizophrenia and inform the development of avatar-based interventions for social cognitive training.

Experiment 1 The effect of avatar identity on Level-2 VPT based on object appearance

Methods

Participants

An a priori power analysis (G*Power 3.1.9.2) established that 28 participants were required to achieve 80% power for detecting a medium effect size (f = 0.2) at α = 0.05, based on the planned 2 × 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVA. To avoid an insufficient sample size due to participants abandoning the experiment, 30 individuals with schizophrenia in the clinical stable phase were recruited from the Tianshui Psychiatric Hospital, Gansu Province, China. 33 healthy controls were recruited from the local community (Table 1).

Patients fulfilled the diagnostic criteria provided in the International Classification of Diseases-10 and were diagnosed by two experienced attending doctors. The exclusion criteria were substance abuse, a history of severe brain trauma, other physical diseases, acute psychotic episodes, and significant agitation or disorganization. All patients received antipsychotic medication (mean chlorpromazine equivalent: 245.20 mg) during the experiment. The exclusion criteria for healthy controls were substance abuse, smoking, drinking problems, history of psychotic disorders, history of severe brain trauma, and other physical diseases. Healthy controls who completed the entire procedure received 30 CNY as compensation for their participation. The study was approved by the Ethics Board of the Northwest Normal University (2023161). Informed consent was obtained from all the participants. This study was conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Stimuli

According to Elekes et al. [7], the stimuli feature top-down images of a virtual avatar (male or female) seated at a table oriented either congruently or incongruently with the participant. The table comprises a gray rectangle with black borders (5 cm × 8 cm) centrally displaying a black number in a 32-point font. Critical stimuli were the numbers “6” and “9”, which are rotationally symmetric, when viewed from opposite sides of the table, they appear as each other. The number “0”, which is rotationally symmetric and appears identical from both perspectives, was used as a filler for counterbalancing. A neutral male or female voice presented auditory numbers at 70 dB for 750 ms, with the voice gender matched to both the avatar and the participant’s sex.

Design and procedure

All participants completed two experimental tasks: a Level-2 VPT with another avatar and a Level-2 VPT with their own avatar. On the first day of the experiment, each participant was randomly assigned to complete one of the two tasks, followed by another on the second day.

The participants completed the task in the laboratory using a program developed in E-prime 2.0. Stimuli were presented on a 15.6-inch LCD monitor (resolution: 1920 × 1080; refresh rate: 60 Hz) that was placed flat on the desk. The participants were seated approximately 20 cm from the table edge. Each trial began with a central black fixation cross (“+”) presented for 750 ms. This was followed by a 750 ms auditory stimulus, during which a gender-matched neutral voice pronounced a number (0, 6, or 9) against a blank screen. Subsequently, the numerical stimulus appeared and remained on the screen until the participant made a response. After the participant responded, a 500 ms blank screen preceded the next trial. Participants indicated “yes” or “no” responses by pressing the “F” or “J” key as quickly and accurately as possible, with response keys counterbalanced across participants. The perspective consistency manipulation was defined as follows: In consistent trials, the number the participant saw matched the number the avatar saw (e.g., both saw “6”). In inconsistent trials, the number the participant saw differed from the number the avatar saw due to the rotational symmetry of “6”, and “9” (e.g., the participant saw “6” while the avatar saw “9”). Participants were required to judge whether the number seen by the avatar matched an auditory number presented prior to the visual stimulus. Thus, correct responses required inhibiting one’s own egocentric perspective and accurately computing the avatar’s visual experience. In the perspective-consistent condition, there were 30 “yes” trials, in which the numbers 6 and 9 were each presented 15 times, and 30 “no” trials, totaling 60 trials. Similarly, the perspective-inconsistent condition comprised 30 “yes” trials, in which the numbers 6 and 9 were each presented 15 times, and 30 “no” trials, totaling 60 trials. Thus, there were 120 critical trials. An additional 30 filler trials involving the number “0” were included for counterbalancing. To prevent three consecutive trials with the same perspective condition (consistent or inconsistent), all trials were presented in a pseudorandom order. Twelve practice trials were included, resulting in a total of 162 trials. The experiment consisted of two blocks of 75 trials each (excluding practice trials), with a break provided between the blocks. During the practice phase, the experimenter provided detailed verbal instructions and confirmed that each participant understood whether the avatar represented themselves or a stranger. Practice continued until full comprehension was achieved, and participants who failed to understand the task were excluded from the study. The formal experiment lasted approximately 12–16 min (see Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

Accuracy was analyzed using JASP 0.18.3 with a 2 (group: schizophrenia, healthy controls) × 2 (perspective consistency: consistent, inconsistent) × 2 (avatar: other, self) repeated-measures ANCOVA. Age and years of education were standardized to z scores across the full sample and simultaneously included as continuous covariates to place the two covariates on a comparable scale. Perspective consistency and avatar were included as within-subject factors, and group was included as a between-subject factor. Egocentric response rates were analyzed using a 2 (group: schizophrenia, healthy controls) × 2 (avatar: other, self) repeated-measures ANCOVA, with the standardized age and years of education variables simultaneously included as covariates.

Frequentist analyses were treated as the primary inferential analyses. Bayesian repeated-measures analyses were conducted as supplementary analyses, and inclusion Bayes factors (BF_incl, computed across all models) were reported for effects relevant to the primary hypotheses. Bonferroni corrections were applied to post hoc comparisons.

Results

Accuracy

The accuracy results (see Fig. 2A) showed a significant main effect of consistency, F(1, 59) = 34.281, p < .001, η² = 0.152, and group, F(1, 59) = 24.653, p < .001, η² = 0.131, with individuals with schizophrenia showing lower overall accuracy than healthy controls. The main effect of avatar was not significant, F(1, 59) = 0.011, p = .916, η² < 0.001.

Accuracy (left panel) and egocentric response rate (right panel) in the experiment. Blue bars represent the other-avatar, and orange bars represent self-avatar. Error bars indicate standard error. (***, p < .001)

The consistency × group interaction was significant, F(1, 59) = 15.548, p < .001, η² = 0.069. Post hoc comparisons showed that individuals with schizophrenia were more accurate in the consistent condition than in the inconsistent condition, t(59) = 6.644, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.683. In contrast, no significant consistency effect was observed in healthy controls, t(59) = 0.906, p = .369, Cohen’s d = 0.217. Thus, the interaction was primarily driven by a pronounced consistency effect in the schizophrenia group. None of the avatar-related interactions were significant: avatar × consistency, F(1, 59) = 1.348, p = .250, η² < 0.001; avatar × group, F(1, 59) = 0.004, p = .948, η² < 0.001; and avatar × consistency × group, F(1, 59) = 1.436, p = .236, η² < 0.001. These results indicate that the consistency effect was not reliably modulated by avatar identity and that this pattern did not differ between the schizophrenia and healthy-control groups.

Neither the covariate age, F(1, 59) = 0.389, p = .535, η² = 0.002, nor years of education, F(1, 59) = 1.582, p = .213, η² = 0.008, was significant. Interactions involving age were also nonsignificant: avatar × age, F(1, 59) = 0.419, p = .520, η² < 0.001; consistency × age, F(1, 59) = 0.464, p = .499, η² = 0.002; and avatar × consistency × age, F(1, 59) = 1.933, p = .170, η² < 0.001. Likewise, interactions involving years of education were nonsignificant: avatar × education, F(1, 59) = 0.254, p = .616, η² < 0.001; consistency × education, F(1, 59) = 0.038, p = .847, η² < 0.001; and avatar × consistency × education, F(1, 59) = 0.034, p = .855, η² < 0.001.

Bayesian repeated-measures analyses were conducted as supplementary evidence, and inclusion Bayes factors (BF_incl, computed across all models) are reported for effects relevant to the primary hypotheses. The analysis provided decisive evidence for the group effect, the consistency effect, and the consistency × group interaction (all BF_incl > 100), corroborating the frequentist evidence for overall group differences and group-dependent consistency effects. In contrast, the data favored models excluding the main effect of avatar, BF_incl = 0.075, the avatar × consistency interaction, BF_incl = 0.097, the avatar × group interaction, BF_incl = 0.093, and particularly the avatar × consistency × group interaction, BF_incl = 0.045. Overall, the Bayesian results strongly supported the group effect and the group-dependent consistency effect but provided little evidence for reliable avatar-related modulation.

Egocentric response rates

The egocentric response rate results (Fig. 2B) showed a significant main effect of group, F(1, 59) = 23.395, p < .001, η² = 0.262, with individuals with schizophrenia showing higher egocentric response rates than healthy controls. The main effect of avatar was not significant, F(1, 59) = 0.437, p = .511, η² < 0.001. The avatar × group interaction was also not significant, F(1, 59) = 0.619, p = .435, η² < 0.001, indicating that the group difference in egocentric response rates did not vary reliably between avatar conditions.

Neither age, F(1, 59) = 0.043, p = .837, η² < 0.001, nor years of education, F(1, 59) = 0.488, p = .487, η² = 0.005, was significant. The avatar × age interaction, F(1, 59) = 1.197, p = .278, η² = 0.001, and the avatar × education interaction, F(1, 59) = 0.138, p = .712, η² < 0.001, were also nonsignificant.

Bayesian analyses further provided decisive evidence for the group effect, BF_incl > 100, consistent with the higher egocentric response rates in individuals with schizophrenia. In contrast, the data favored models excluding the main effect of avatar, BF_incl = 0.183, and the avatar × group interaction, BF_incl = 0.189. Evidence for age (BF_incl = 0.639) and years of education (BF_incl = 0.715) was also weak and did not support their inclusion. Overall, the Bayesian results strongly supported the group effect but provided little evidence for reliable avatar-related modulation of egocentric response rates.

Discussion

In Experiment 1, a digit inversion task was employed to investigate Level-2 VPT based on object appearance in individuals with schizophrenia and the influence of avatar identity. Individuals with schizophrenia showed lower overall accuracy and higher egocentric response rates than healthy controls. A significant perspective consistency × group interaction was also observed: individuals with schizophrenia were less accurate in the inconsistent condition than in the consistent condition, whereas no significant consistency effect was found in healthy controls. No significant main effect or interaction involving avatar identity was observed for accuracy, and avatar identity did not significantly affect egocentric response rates.

These results indicate that individuals with schizophrenia exhibit impairments in Level-2 VPT based on object appearance. When their own perspective conflicted with that of the avatar, they experienced greater difficulty suppressing their self-perspective and adopting the avatar’s viewpoint, as reflected in the pronounced consistency effect and higher egocentric response rates. This pattern is consistent with previous findings on Level-1 VPT in schizophrenia [18]. However, the present results did not provide evidence that self-avatar identity modulated Level-2 VPT performance or selectively benefited the schizophrenia group. Possible explanations include difficulties in implementing strategic processes and disruptions in self-recognition associated with schizophrenia [25].

Stimuli

According to Böffel and Müsseler [4] and Janczyk [15], a stimulus scene features a top-down view of a virtual avatar (male or female) seated at a circular table. The table measured 10 cm in diameter with a gray surface and featured a central black dot (0.5 cm diameter). The avatar’s hands rested parallel to the table surface. A blue dot (1 cm in diameter) appears immediately above the avatar’s left or right hand. Avatars appeared at six positions relative to the participants’ face directions: 0°, 60°, 120°, 180°, 240°, and 300°. The avatars at 0°, 60°, and 300° maintained a perspective-consistent orientation with the participant. The avatars at 120, 180, and 240° represented perspective-inconsistent orientations.

Design and procedure

All participants completed two experimental tasks: a Level-2 VPT with another avatar and a Level-2 VPT with their own avatar. On the first day of the experiment, each participant was randomly assigned to complete one of the two tasks, followed by another on the second day.

The participants completed the task in the laboratory using a program developed in E-Prime 2.0. Stimuli were displayed on a 15.6-inch LCD monitor (resolution: 1920 × 1080; refresh rate: 60 Hz) that was placed flat on the desk. The participants were seated approximately 20 cm from the table edge, maintaining central fixation while resting their hands comfortably on the keyboard. Each trial began with a 750 ms central black fixation cross (“+”), followed by the stimulus image. The stimulus image remained on the screen until the participant responded. After the participants responded, a 500 ms blank screen preceded the next trial. Participants indicated whether the blue dot appeared above the avatar’s left or right hand by pressing the ‘F’ or ‘J’ key as quickly and accurately as possible, with response keys counterbalanced across participants. In the perspective-consistent condition (the participant’s view matched that of the avatar), stimuli were presented 30 times per angular position (0°, 60°, and 300°), totaling 90 trials. In the perspective-inconsistent condition, stimuli were presented 30 times per angular position (120°, 180°, and 240°) for a total of 90 trials. This resulted in 180 experimental trials. To prevent three consecutive trials with the same perspective (consistent or inconsistent), all trials were presented in a pseudorandom order. 12 practice trials were conducted yielding 192 trials in total. The experiment comprised two blocks of 90 trials each (excluding practice) with a break between blocks. During the practice phase, the experimenter provided detailed verbal instructions and confirmed that each participant understood whether the avatar represented themselves or a stranger. Practice continued until full comprehension was achieved, and participants who failed to understand the task were excluded from the study. The formal experiment lasted for 8–10 min (see Fig. 3).

Statistical analysis

The accuracy and reaction time data were analyzed using JASP 0.18.3 with a 2 (group: schizophrenia, healthy controls) × 6 (stimulus orientation: 0°, 60°, 120°, 180°, 240°, 300°) × 2 (avatar: other, self) repeated-measures ANCOVA. Age and years of education were standardized to z scores across the full sample and simultaneously included as continuous covariates to place the two covariates on a comparable scale. Stimulus orientation and avatar were included as within-subject factors, and group was included as a between-subject factor. Four participants with schizophrenia who had 0% accuracy in certain stimulus orientations (e.g., 180°) were excluded from the reaction time analysis.

Frequentist analyses were treated as the primary inferential analyses. Bayesian repeated-measures analyses were conducted as supplementary analyses, and inclusion Bayes factors (BF_incl, computed across all models) were reported for effects relevant to the primary hypotheses. Bonferroni corrections were applied to post hoc comparisons.

The stimulus orientation × group interaction was significant, F(1.655, 95.989) = 5.047, p = .012, η² = 0.023, Greenhouse–Geisser ε = 0.331, indicating that the effect of stimulus orientation on accuracy differed between groups. Post hoc comparisons showed no significant orientation differences within the healthy control group (ts < 1.0, ps > 0.05). In contrast, within the schizophrenia group, accuracy at 0° differed from 120°, 180°, 240°, and 300°; 60° differed from 120°, 180°, and 240°; 180° differed from 120°, 240°, and 300°; and 240° differed from 300° (ts > 3.41, ps < 0.05). Follow-up simple-effects comparisons between groups, averaged across avatar conditions, further showed that the healthy control group had significantly higher accuracy than the schizophrenia group at 120°, t(58) = 2.499, p = .015, Cohen’s d = 0.583; at 180°, t(58) = 2.550, p = .013, Cohen’s d = 1.112; and at 240°, t(58) = 2.184, p = .033, Cohen’s d = 0.614. No significant between-group differences were observed at 0°, 60°, or 300° (ts < 1.67, ps > 0.10). Thus, the significant stimulus orientation × group interaction was primarily driven by orientation-related changes in accuracy in the schizophrenia group, whereas accuracy remained relatively stable across orientations in the healthy control group. None of the interactions involving avatar were significant: avatar × group, F(1, 58) = 0.001, p = .974, η² < 0.001; avatar × stimulus orientation, F(2.232, 129.457) = 2.695, p = .065, η² = 0.002, Greenhouse–Geisser ε = 0.446; and avatar × stimulus orientation × group, F(2.232, 129.457) = 0.227, p = .820, η² < 0.001, Greenhouse–Geisser ε = 0.446. Thus, accuracy was not reliably modulated by avatar identity, and this effect did not differ between groups or across stimulus orientations.

Neither age, F(1, 58) = 1.930, p = .170, η² = 0.016, nor years of education, F(1, 58) = 0.241, p = .625, η² = 0.002, was significant. Interactions involving age were also nonsignificant: avatar × age, F(1, 58) = 0.149, p = .701, η² < 0.001; stimulus orientation × age, F(1.655, 95.989) = 0.413, p = .624, η² = 0.002; and avatar × stimulus orientation × age, F(2.232, 129.457) = 0.588, p = .575, η² < 0.001. Similarly, interactions involving years of education were nonsignificant: avatar × education, F(1, 58) = 0.080, p = .778, η² < 0.001; stimulus orientation × education, F(1.655, 95.989) = 0.212, p = .767, η² < 0.001; and avatar × stimulus orientation × education, F(2.232, 129.457) = 0.278, p = .782, η² < 0.001.

Bayesian analyses provided decisive evidence for the stimulus orientation effect, the group effect, and the stimulus orientation × group interaction (all BF_incl > 100), corroborating the frequentist evidence for group differences and group-dependent orientation effects. For avatar-related effects, the data favored models excluding the main effect of avatar, BF_incl = 0.201, the avatar × group interaction, BF_incl = 0.216, and especially the avatar × stimulus orientation × group interaction, BF_incl = 0.012. Evidence for the avatar × stimulus orientation interaction was weaker, BF_incl = 0.477, indicating that the data did not support its inclusion. Overall, the Bayesian results supported the group-dependent orientation effect but provided little evidence for reliable avatar-related modulation of accuracy.

Discussion

In Experiment 2, a round-table task was employed to investigate Level-2 VPT based on spatial location in individuals with schizophrenia and the influence of avatar identity. Between-group comparisons averaged across avatar conditions showed that individuals with schizophrenia had lower accuracy than healthy controls at the perspective-incongruent orientations of 120°, 180°, and 240°. At these orientations, participants responded faster when the avatar represented themselves than when it represented another person.

The accuracy results showed no significant orientation differences within the healthy control group, likely reflecting a ceiling effect and the simplified response design. By contrast, reaction time varied significantly across stimulus orientations. Inspection of the condition means showed that reaction times descriptively increased as the stimulus orientation approached 180° and decreased thereafter, with the longest mean reaction time at 180° in both groups (Table 3; Fig. 4). This descriptive pattern is consistent with previous spatial perspective-taking research, in which alignment effects are often more evident in response latencies than in accuracy [12], and with proposals that angular disparity imposes sensorimotor and orientation-dependent spatial transformation costs [16, 20]. However, the present data do not establish a stronger or qualitatively different orientation-related reaction-time cost in the schizophrenia group. Rather, individuals with schizophrenia were slower overall and showed orientation-related reductions in accuracy, whereas the self-avatar produced faster overall responses without selectively improving performance in the schizophrenia group. The generalized slowing in schizophrenia may reflect broad inefficiency in cognitive task performance [29].

General discussion

This study aimed to investigate whether individuals with schizophrenia exhibit impairments in Level-2 VPT based on object appearance and spatial location, and whether avatar identity modulates their performance. The hypotheses were partially supported. Individuals with schizophrenia showed impairments in both types of perspective-taking. Across the two experiments, accuracy was generally comparable between the self-avatar and other-avatar conditions, suggesting that representing the avatar as the self did not improve the accuracy of perspective judgments. In Experiment 2, however, participants responded more rapidly when the avatar represented themselves. This response-speed advantage showed a comparable pattern in individuals with schizophrenia and healthy controls.

The Level-2 VPT requires both social interaction abilities and visuospatial capacity [19, 34]. In Level-2 VPT tasks, participants were required to imagine another person’s viewpoint from a different position and report what the scene would look like from the observer’s perspective. In other words, the task requires mental representation of a view that is not visually available while resisting interference from perceptual input, that is, what participants see from their own perspective. Participants had to process another’s perspective while inhibiting interference from their own egocentric viewpoints. In this study, individuals with schizophrenia exhibited impairments in both object-characteristic and spatial location-based processing. When an avatar’s perspective is inconsistent with their own, they struggle to accurately judge the avatar’s viewpoint and display more egocentric errors, which is consistent with previous findings on VPT in schizophrenia. For instance, Kronbichler et al. [18] found that individuals with schizophrenia were influenced by their own perspectives, even during Level-1 VPT.

The abnormalities observed in individuals with schizophrenia when adopting an avatar perspective may be related to their inability to overcome egocentric impairments. According to the attribution-self-representation cycle model, the emergence of paranoid symptoms in individuals with mental disorders stems from their tendency to attribute negative events to others to avoid activating negative self-beliefs [3]. This external attribution helps reduce the discrepancy between their actual self-representation and the ideal self, yet it readily fosters the formation of a paranoid, self-centered perspective [3]. Consequently, when attempting to adopt others’ perspectives, individuals remain influenced by their self-referential biases. This leads to prolonged judgment times for others’ perspectives under incongruent conditions, as processing self-perspective interferes. Self-perspective remains more salient than others’ perspectives and requires greater cognitive effort from participants when judging others’ perspectives than when judging their own [32].

Furthermore, Level-2 VPT relies on an individual’s mental rotation ability, which requires mental representation of stimuli observed by another agent [19]. Healthy participants typically employ two mental rotation strategies to complete the Level-2 VPT: embodied self-rotation, which involves mentally transforming one’s self to a different location in space. Currently, a growing body of research indicates that during Level-2 VPT, individuals use motor representations to imagine an “embodied” avatar at a new physical location [17]. In contrast, object-based mental rotation does not involve motor representations of one’s own body but rather a mental rotation of the stimulus scene itself. This strategy involves mentally rotating the scene to the corresponding orientation before making a judgment. Based on the current findings and previous research, individuals with schizophrenia exhibit abnormalities when employing mental rotation strategies for the Level-2 VPT. Mental rotation is fundamentally a cognitive ability that requires individuals to mentally rotate a presented figure back to its original orientation to determine whether it matches a target figure or, alternatively, to imagine how a two- or three-dimensional object would appear when rotated to a specific degree [30]. Early studies found that individuals with schizophrenia demonstrate slower responses and reduced accuracy in mental rotation tasks [6]. Embodied self-rotation is considered as the primary strategy for Level-2 VPT [27]. However, this process places high demand on the embodied representation of one’s own body. Numerous studies have observed abnormal body imagery in individuals with schizophrenia, who report not only delusions of external control over their motor actions, but also question the conscious origin of their own thoughts [10, 33].

However, object-based mental rotation refers to the ability to mentally rotate objects in one’s mind [36] and is associated with spatial perception abilities [13]. One study employed a two-dimensional mental rotation task to investigate the spatial processing capabilities of individuals with schizophrenia and bipolar disorder. The results indicated that both patient groups performed worse than the control group in terms of accuracy, suggesting potential deficits in spatial processing [35]. Another study used a spatial discrimination task to examine the visuospatial perceptions in individuals with schizophrenia. Participants were required to complete 30 trials involving the matching of two stimulus lines to two lines with identical angles and orientations within an array of 11 choices. The findings revealed impaired spatial perception abilities in individuals with schizophrenia compared with healthy controls [11]. The abnormalities observed in both self- and other-avatar perspective-taking among individuals with schizophrenia in Experiments 1 and 2 may indicate that their Level-2 VPT impairments are associated with fundamental cognitive deficits.

Contrary to our hypothesis, the self-avatar condition did not produce a schizophrenia-specific improvement in Level-2 VPT performance. Nevertheless, the significant main effect of avatar on reaction time in Experiment 2 showed that the self-avatar facilitated response speed across both groups. This finding indicates that the general response-time benefit associated with the self-avatar was also present in individuals with schizophrenia. However, this facilitation did not extend to accuracy and was not stronger in the schizophrenia group, suggesting that it was insufficient to selectively compensate for their Level-2 VPT impairments. Several factors may account for the absence of an accuracy-based or schizophrenia-specific benefit.

First, from a phenomenological perspective, the deficit or distortion of the self in schizophrenia is fundamentally understood as “ipseity disturbance,” describing a disruption in the basic sense of selfhood or “I-ness” that most people experience [14]. From a neuroscientific standpoint, this phenomenon is interpreted as the brain’s failure to “recognize the self in action,” leading to errors in information processing and the misattribution of one’s own experiences, thoughts, and actions to others, whether real or imagined [23]. In summary, a core manifestation of self-disorder in schizophrenia may be a fundamental, automatic disruption of coordination between the self, others, and the surrounding environment [25]. Scharfetter, [28] suggested that individuals with schizophrenia might partially or completely lose the basic aspects of self-experience and self-awareness that are naturally present in healthy individuals. These include: awareness of being alive; actively receiving information; existing as a coherent psychophysical unity capable of responsive and conscious action; distinguishing the self from non-self “objects” and thereby engaging in communication; and inhabiting various social roles (understanding whom one cares about, and being aware of one’s own psychology, body, appearance, gender, family role, etc.). Thus, individuals with schizophrenia may struggle to maintain a clear boundary between the self and the external world. This difficulty is compounded in Level-2 VPT, which requires simulating a virtual avatar as the self and subsequently performing mental rotation, a process that demands considerable cognitive effort.

Second, during Level 2 VPT, when the outcome of one’s own perspective conflicts with the avatar’s perspective under inconsistent conditions, participants must inhibit their own perspective to adopt that of the avatar. However, individuals with schizophrenia have been shown to exhibit deficits in perceptual inhibition [38]. A study examining response inhibition in children with schizophrenia, children with autism, and typically developing children across visual, auditory, and verbal domains found that both clinical groups demonstrated impaired response inhibition in all three modalities. Children with schizophrenia showed the slowest responses in visual response inhibition tasks, and in auditory inhibition tasks, their response inhibition ability was even weaker than that of children with autism [31]. Such impairments in inhibitory control may limit the extent to which the general response-time benefit associated with the self-avatar translates into improved accuracy or a schizophrenia-specific advantage. When the self-perspective conflicts with the avatar’s perspective, individuals with schizophrenia may struggle to inhibit their own perspective to resolve the conflict arising from mental rotation, thereby exhibiting egocentric biases.

Finally, the physical similarity between the self-avatar and the other-avatar stimuli may have limited the magnitude or specificity of the self-avatar effect. This may help explain why the self-avatar produced a general response-time benefit but did not result in improved accuracy or a stronger benefit in individuals with schizophrenia. This may be particularly relevant for those experiencing self-disorders. However, according to previous studies [15, 37], avatar embodiment inherently requires mental simulation, as there are no actual physical cues that can truly place an individual in another’s position. In our experiment, the computer screen was placed flat on the laboratory table to align the participant’s bodily position with that of the avatar as closely as possible. Nevertheless, future studies could adopt more explicit self cuing methods, such as personalized avatars or features resembling the participant, to investigate whether such cues can facilitate Level-2 VPT in individuals with schizophrenia.

Limitations

First, although the self-avatar was associated with faster overall responses in Experiment 2, this benefit did not extend to accuracy and was not specific to individuals with schizophrenia. One possible explanation for the absence of an accuracy-based or schizophrenia-specific benefit is that individuals with schizophrenia may experience difficulty employing mental-rotation strategies, which may be a prerequisite for performing Level-2 VPT. A previous study showed that instructing children with autism on how to perform embodied self-rotation and object-based mental rotation significantly improved their Level-2 VPT ability [24]. Based on this, future studies should first investigate whether individuals with schizophrenia are affected by strategy use and, on that basis, further examine the role of self-identity in the Level-2 VPT. Second, considering the potential impact of antipsychotic medication on reaction time and cognitive performance in individuals with schizophrenia, participants with schizophrenia were receiving atypical antipsychotics at stable, prescribed doses. No patient had undergone medication changes or received adjunctive treatments within the month prior to testing, and the experimental sessions began at least two hours after medication intake. Recent evidence suggests that antipsychotic medications generally provide limited cognitive benefits, although their effects on specific cognitive domains may vary [8]. Maintaining stable medication regimens across the testing period helped minimize acute medication-related fluctuations in performance. Nevertheless, we cannot entirely disentangle medication effects from illness-related cognitive impairments. Future studies should consider including unmedicated patients or first-episode samples to better isolate the influence of pharmacological treatment. Third, there were significant group differences in age and education. These disparities largely stem from illness-related educational disruption in patients and the inherent challenges of recruiting perfectly matched clinical samples. Notably, age and years of education were therefore included as covariates in the analyses. Across the two experiments, ages ranged from 20 to 45 years in healthy controls and from 19 to 49 years in individuals with schizophrenia. Previous research suggests that age-related variation in executive functions is relatively limited within this adult age range [2]. Future research should implement stricter demographic matching to further validate the current findings. Finally, due to the relatively small sample size and restricted range of symptom scores in this patient sample, we were unable to examine whether specific symptom dimensions such as persecutory delusions, hallucinations, or passivity symptoms differentially predict Level-2 VPT performance or responsiveness to self-avatar manipulations. Future studies with larger and more symptomatically diverse samples should address this question.

Conclusion

This study systematically demonstrated impairments in Level-2 VPT among individuals with schizophrenia across both the object-characteristic and spatial-location processing domains. Individuals with schizophrenia exhibit pronounced difficulties in adopting another’s viewpoint under perspective-incongruent conditions, displaying a stronger egocentric bias. Although the self-avatar was associated with faster overall responses in Experiment 2, this facilitation was not specific to individuals with schizophrenia. Thus, the present findings do not support a selective self-avatar benefit for Level-2 VPT deficits in schizophrenia. The observed deficits likely stem from multifaceted cognitive abnormalities, including potential impairments in implementing mental rotation strategies (both embodied self-rotation and object-based rotation) essential for Level-2 VPT. The generalized slowing of reaction times further underscores the broad cognitive inefficiencies in schizophrenia. These findings are consistent with broad difficulties in the cognitive processes supporting Level-2 VPT in schizophrenia. However, because mental-rotation strategies, inhibitory control, and self-identification with the avatar were not directly measured, the mechanisms underlying these deficits require further investigation.
