Comparative evaluation of vortex agitation and ultrasonication for recovering strongly attached Salmonella enterica and Listeria monocytogenes from Roma tomatoes
Authors: Ramón García-Frutos, Nanci E. Martínez-Gonzáles, Liliana Martínez-Chávez, Georgina Ocampo-Mariscal, Carla G. Mendoza-Cuevas, Regina Reynoso-Pérez, Porfirio Gutiérrez-González
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reliable recovery of bacterial cells attached to produce surfaces is essential for evaluating pathogen persistence and detachment procedures. This study compares vortex agitation and ultrasonication and evaluated the effect of exposure time on recovery of strongly attached (SA) cells of Salmonella enterica and Listeria monocytogenes from Roma tomato epicarp during storage. Loosely attached (LA) cells were removed by a standardized rinse, after which SA cells were recovered by mechanical treatment for 1, 5 and 10 min. The procedures were evaluated using tomatoes stored at 25 and 35°C for 0, 3, and 6 days. Loosely attached populations decreased during storage, with no significant differences between temperatures at each storage day (P > 0.05). Vortex agitation recovered higher (P < 0.05) SA-cell counts than ultrasonication in most comparisons, ranging from 3.7 to 6.2 versus 3.3 to 5.3 log CFU/tomato for S. enterica, and from 4.8 to 7.0 versus 2.7 to 6.2 log CFU/tomato for L. monocytogenes, respectively. Higher exposure times increased SA-cell recovery for both pathogens. Epicarp fissures were observed in softened tomatoes only after vortex agitation. Under evaluated conditions, recovery depended on procedure and exposure time; vortex agitation yielded generally higher SA-cell counts, whereas ultrasonication better preserved tomato surface integrity.

Background

Fresh tomatoes have been implicated as vehicles for the transmission of Salmonella enterica [1,2], and their surfaces can support the attachment and survival of both S. enterica and Listeria monocytogenes during storage [3]. The recovery of bacterial cells from the fruit epicarp may be influenced by surface characteristics, including roughness and hydrophobicity, as well as by inoculum level, relative humidity, storage time, temperature, fruit maturity, and bacterial strain [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. These factors can affect the quantitative estimation of viable bacterial populations associated with the fruit surface.

For experimental purposes, bacterial cells associated with produce surfaces may be operationally classified as loosely attached (LA) or strongly attached (SA), depending on the recovery procedure used. Loosely attached cells are removed by a gentle rinsing, whereas SA cells remain associated with the epicarp and require an additional mechanical treatment for detachment. Recovery of these remaining cells is relevant in quantitative studies of bacterial attachment, persistence, survival, transfer, and decontamination efficacy. Studies on fresh produce have quantified attached bacterial populations to evaluate attachment and survival on produce surfaces, transfer to edible tissues, and the efficacy of decontamination treatments [[7], [8], [9]]. Other studies have quantified LA and SA populations separately when evaluating decontamination treatments because cells remaining associated with the produce surface after rinsing may affect the estimated microbial reduction achieved by the treatment [10,11]. In Roma tomatoes, initial SA populations of 5.1 log CFU/tomato for S. enterica and 5.6 log CFU/tomato for L. monocytogenes were recovered after removal of LA cells, and the populations of both pathogens changed significantly under different storage conditions [3]. Therefore, excluding SA cells may lead to underestimation of viable surface-associated populations in quantitative studies, although their recovery is not necessarily required for qualitative pathogen detection.

Ultrasonication has been used to recover Salmonella from raw produce surfaces [12] and to detach SA cells of S. enterica and L. monocytogenes from the epicarp of Roma tomatoes [3]. Both ultrasonication and vortex agitation generate mechanical forces that may facilitate the detachment of cells that remain associated with the produce surface after rinsing. However, few studies have directly compared these procedures under equivalent experimental conditions.

Therefore, this study compared vortex agitation and ultrasonication as procedures for recovering SA cells of S. enterica and L. monocytogenes from the epicarp of Roma tomatoes after removal of LA cells. Inoculated tomatoes were stored at 25 or 35°C for up to 6 days. These temperatures were selected from those previously evaluated by Cabrera-Díaz et al. [3] to compare recovery procedures in tomatoes exposed to two non-refrigerated storage conditions that were expected to produce different degrees of ripening and softening. Storage at 5°C, under which lower survival of attached cells had previously been observed, was not included because the study was not designed to evaluate the full range temperatures relevant to tomato storage. Strongly attached cells were subsequently recovered by either procedure for 1, 5, or 10 min. The effects of the recovery procedure, treatment time, and storage condition were evaluated, along with changes in the pH and temperature of the recovery liquid and visible alterations to the Roma tomato epicarp.

Method details

Selection of fruits

Roma tomatoes (Solanum lycopersicum) of uniform size (100 ± 10 g), pink-red color, unwaxed, and free of visible damage were purchased from a local market in Guadalajara, Mexico. The fruits were transported within 1 h to the Food Microbiology and Safety Laboratory at the University of Guadalajara (FMSL-UDG) in the original packer’s box at room temperature (25 ± 5°C). Each independent experiment was performed using tomatoes from a different batch supplied by the same vendor. Upon arrival at the laboratory, tomatoes were placed on stainless-steel stands previously sanitized with 70% ethanol.

Bacterial strains

Six rifampicin-resistant (Rif⁺) strains of S. enterica and six Rif⁺ strains of L. monocytogenes [13] were used in this study. The S. enterica strains included S. Typhimurium (Sm-R-035) isolated from tomato epicarp obtained from retail outlets, S. Typhimurium (Sm-R-037) isolated from human feces, S. Poona (Sm-R-041) isolated from avocado epicarp collected at an orchard, S. Bardo (Sm-R-048) recovered from compost used in an avocado orchard, and two S. Bareilly (Sm-R-559 and Sm-R-743) strains isolated from the epicarp of avocados purchased at retail markets. Five of these S. enterica strains were obtained from the FMSL-UDG collection, whereas the S. Typhimurium strain isolated from the tomato epicarp was provided by Dr. Montserrat Iturriaga of the Autonomous University of Queretaro (ECMF-AUQ).

Similarly, five L. monocytogenes strains were obtained from the FMSL-UDG collection, including one strain (Lm-R-018) isolated from agricultural soil in avocado orchards, one Scott A strain (serotype 4b; ATCC 15313) from a clinical case, and three strains (Lm-R-251, Lm-R-336 and Lm-R-357) recovered from the epicarp of avocados purchased at retail markets. A sixth strain, designated N4, was isolated from commercial guacamole and provided by ECMF-AUQ.

All strains were preserved in tryptic soy broth (TSB; Bioxon, Becton Dickinson, U.S.A.) supplemented with 20% glycerol at −80°C. Working cultures of both pathogens were prepared on tryptic soy agar supplemented with 0.6% yeast extract (TSAYE; Bioxon, Becton Dickinson, U.S.A.) slants and stored at 5°C for no more than 30 days before use. These working cultures were used to prepare the bacterial inocula.

Inoculum preparation and Roma tomato inoculation

The procedures for inoculum preparation and tomato inoculation followed the methodology described by Cabrera-Díaz et al. [3]. An aliquot from each working strain of S. enterica and L. monocytogenes, maintained on TSAYE slants, was transferred with a nichrome loop into 10 mL of TSB supplemented with 0.6% yeast extract (TSBYE) and incubated at 35°C for 18 h. Subsequently, 100 µL of each overnight culture was individually transferred to 10 mL of fresh TSBYE and incubated under the same conditions for 6 h. Then, 100 µL of each culture was inoculated into a sterile 50-mL conical centrifuge tube (Falcon, BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, U.S.A.) containing 35 mL of fresh TSBYE and incubated at 35°C for 18 h.

The resulting cultures were washed twice with 35 mL of sterile saline solution (SS, 0.85% NaCl; Productos Químicos Monterrey, Monterrey, Mexico) by centrifugation (Durafuge 300R, Precision, Winchester, VA, U.S.A.) at 4507 × g and 5°C for 10 min. After the second wash, the supernatant was discarded, and each bacterial pellet was resuspended in 17.5 mL of SS. For each pathogen, the six strain suspensions were combined in a sterile 710-mL Whirl-Pak® plastic bag (Whirl-Pak, Nasco, Ft. Atkinson, WI, U.S.A.) to obtain separate S. enterica and L. monocytogenes inoculum cocktails, each with a final volume of 105 mL. The cocktails were manually homogenized for 30 s.

To estimate the concentration of each inoculum, appropriate serial decimal dilutions were prepared in tubes containing 9 mL of 0.1% peptone water (PW) and 0.1 mL aliquots of each dilution were plated onto TSAYE supplemented with 100 mg/L rifampicin (TSAYE-Rif; GoldBio, St. Louis, MO, U.S.A.). Plates were incubated at 35°C for 24 h for S. enterica and 48 h for L. monocytogenes before colony counting.

For inoculation, each tomato was immersed in either the S. enterica or L. monocytogenes cocktail and manually mixed for 1 min to ensure full contact with the inoculum. This procedure simulated a contamination scenario during tomato washing in bulk tanks. The tomatoes were then aseptically removed from the inoculum and placed on cylindrical stainless-steel stands previously disinfected with 70% ethanol. They were allowed to drain and dry in a biosafety cabinet (Telstar, Bio II Advance Plus, Pennsylvania, U.S.A.) at room temperature for 1 h to promote bacterial attachment to the tomato epicarp.

Storage conditions and recovery of attached cells

After drying, the inoculated tomatoes were placed in sanitized polyethylene baskets (50 × 30 × 6 cm), maintaining approximately 2 cm between fruits to allow air circulation, and stored in individual chambers at 25 or 35°C for 0, 3, or 6 days. At each sampling time, three tomatoes were randomly selected for the enumeration of attached cells. Loosely attached (LA) cells were first recovered from each fruit by manual rinsing. This was followed by the recovery of the remaining strongly attached (SA) cells using either vortexing agitation or ultrasonication. For each recovery method, the same suspension was sampled sequentially for 1, 5, and 10 min. Thus, the exposure times represented repeated measurements from the same experimental unit rather than independent tomato samples. Tomatoes removed from the chambers on day 3 were replaced with non-inoculated fruit to maintain storage conditions until day 6. Three independent trials were conducted for each storage condition, with three tomatoes serving as biological replicates per recovery method and exposure time within each trial (n=9).

Recovery of attached bacterial cells from tomato epicarp by vortex agitation or ultrasonication

Previously inoculated and air-dried tomatoes were randomly assigned to one of two recovery methods, vortex agitation or ultrasonication, to compare their efficiency in recovering SA S. enterica and L. monocytogenes cells from the tomato epicarp. Before applying either recovery method, LA cells were removed by placing each tomato in a sterile Whirl-Pak® bag containing 100 mL of buffered peptone water (BPW; Difco, Franklin Lakes, NJ, U.S.A.). For the ultrasonication treatment, after LA cell removal, each tomato was transferred to a new sterile Whirl-Pak® bag containing 100 mL of BPW and immersed in an ultrasonic bath (Fisher Scientific, model 15337418, U.S.A.) operating at 300 W and 40 kHz. Each tomato was subjected to ultrasonication for up to 10 min. Aseptically, 2 mL aliquots of the same suspension were collected after 1 and 5 min of treatment, without interrupting the procedure except for sample collection. Consequently, measurements at 1, 5, and 10 min were obtained sequentially from the same tomato rinse liquid. Care was taken to ensure that each tomato remained fully submerged in the ultrasonic bath water throughout the procedure to ensure exposure to acoustic cavitation. For the vortex agitation, after LA cell removal, each tomato was transferred to a new sterile Whirl-Pak® bag containing 100 mL of BPW. The bag was carefully sealed to prevent leakage and vigorously agitated using a vortex mixer equipped with a 3-inch platform (Vortex-Genie® 2, Scientific Industries, model SI-0236, U.S.A.) at 3200 rpm. Each tomato was vortex-agitated for up to 10 min. Aliquots of 2 mL were aseptically collected from the same suspension after 1 and 5 min of treatment. Therefore, as with the ultrasonication, measurements at each exposure time were obtained sequentially from the same experimental unit rather than from independent tomato samples, following the same approach used for the ultrasonication treatment. During vortex agitation, the operator held the Whirl-Pak® bag containing the tomato firmly above the vortex platform to maintain continuous contact and consistent agitation. At each sampling point (1 and 5 min), agitation was briefly interrupted to allow aseptic sample collection before resuming vortexing.

Populations of LA and SA S. enterica and L. monocytogenes cells were enumerated from the initial rinse suspension and from the aliquots collected after 1, 5 and 10 min, respectively. Serial decimal dilutions were prepared in 0.1% peptone water (PW). Aliquots of 0.25 mL of the direct rinse liquid and 0.1 mL of each dilution were spread onto TSAYE-Rif plates and incubated at 35°C for 24 h for S. enterica and 48 h for L. monocytogenes. After incubation, typical colonies of the corresponding pathogen were enumerated on each plate, and two representative colonies were subjected to biochemical tests for confirmation of pathogen identity. For S. enterica, confirmation included lactose/glucose fermentation and H₂S production in triple sugar iron agar, lysine decarboxylation and H₂S production in lysine iron agar, and urease activity in urea broth [1]. For L. monocytogenes, confirmation was based on xylose, rhamnose, and mannitol fermentation tests [14].

Temperature and pH measurement of the rinsing liquid during vortex agitation and ultrasonication

The temperature and pH of the rinsing liquid were monitored during vortex agitation and ultrasonication to assess potential changes in these parameters and their possible influence on the recovery of strongly attached (SA) S. enterica and L. monocytogenes cells from the tomato epicarp. For these measurements, uninoculated tomatoes stored under the same conditions as the inoculated fruit (25 or 35°C for 0, 3, or 6 days) were subjected to the same vortex and ultrasonication protocols used for SA cell detachment.

Each tomato was placed in a sterile Whirl-Pak® bag containing 100 mL of BPW and treated as previously described for SA cell recovery in the section Recovery of attached bacterial cells from tomato epicarp by vortex agitation or ultrasonication. Temperature was measured using a type-K thermocouple (Thomas Scientific, Swedesboro, NJ, U.S.A.) connected to a digital thermometer (Amprobe TMD-56, Seattle, WA, U.S.A.). The thermocouple was inserted into the bag containing the tomato and rinsing liquid (Fig. 2) and remained fully submerged throughout treatment. Additionally, uninoculated tomatoes were used to visually monitor changes in the epicarp during storage under the different experimental conditions. Photographs of the fruit were taken at each storage time and temperature.

Data analysis

Counts of loosely attached (LA) and strongly attached (SA) cells for both pathogens were expressed as colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL) and multiplied by 100 mL, which corresponds to the total rinsing volume used per tomato. The resulting values, expressed as CFU per tomato, were converted to logarithmic form (log CFU/tomato) before statistical analysis. For statistical analyses, samples with bacterial counts below the detection limit were assigned a value of 2.3 log CFU/tomato, representing the midpoint between zero and the counting method's minimum detection limit (2.6 log CFU/tomato).

Separate one-way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were performed for each storage day (days 0, 3, and 6) to determine the effect of storage temperature (25 or 35°C) on the counts of LA S. enterica and L. monocytogenes cells recovered from Roma tomatoes. Subsequently, a multifactorial ANOVA was conducted to evaluate the effects of recovery method (vortex agitation or ultrasonication), exposure time (1, 5, and 10 min), storage temperature (25 or 35°C), and storage time (0, 3, and 6 days) on the recovery of SA cells of S. enterica and L. monocytogenes. In addition, separate one-way ANOVAs were performed for each recovery method within each combination of storage temperature and storage time to evaluate the effect of exposure time on SA-cell recovery. Separate one-way ANOVAs were also performed for each combination of storage temperature and storage time to compare the SA-cell recovery between vortex agitation and ultrasonication during each exposure time.

To determine whether differences in SA-cell recovery between recovery methods could be explained by changes in the physicochemical properties of the rinse liquid, pH and temperature were analyzed separately using one-way ANOVA to compare vortex agitation and ultrasonication at each storage condition and exposure time. When significant differences were detected, means were separated using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test (P < 0.05) for each determination. All statistical analyses were performed using Statgraphics Centurion XVIII software (Statpoint Technologies, Inc., Warrenton, VA, U.S.A.).

Method validation

The initial rinse was performed before mechanical treatment to remove and quantify cells that were readily detachable from the tomato epicarp. Cells recovered during this step were operationally designated as loosely attached (LA). In contrast, cells remaining on the fruit surface after rinsing and subsequently recovered by vortex agitation or ultrasonication were designated as strongly attached (SA). Therefore, LA counts were used to document the population removed before mechanical treatment and were not included in comparisons of SA-cell recovery between vortex agitation and ultrasonication.

Vortex agitation recovered significantly higher SA counts than ultrasonication in most comparisons (P < 0.05). For S. enterica, vortex agitation recovered 3.7–6.2 log CFU/tomato, whereas ultrasonication yielded 3.3–5.3 log CFU/tomato. No significant differences between procedures were detected at 35°C after 3 days of storage across the three exposure times (P > 0.05). For L. monocytogenes, vortex agitation yielded 4.8–7.0 log CFU/tomato and ultrasonication yielded 2.7–6.2 log CFU/tomato; vortex agitation produced significantly higher counts under all evaluated conditions (P < 0.05). These results demonstrate that the procedure could detect condition-dependent differences in SA-cell recovery between the two mechanical treatments.

The procedure also detected differences associated with exposure time. For S. enterica, the overall SA counts increased from 4.2 log CFU/tomato after 1 min to 4.6 and 4.8 log CFU/tomato after 5 and 10 min, respectively, with significant differences (P < 0.05) among all three exposure times. For L. monocytogenes, the corresponding overall means were 4.4, 4.7, and 4.9 log CFU/tomato; the counts obtained after 5 and 10 min were significantly higher (P < 0.05) than those obtained after 1 min, whereas the difference between 5 and 10 min was not significant (P > 0.05). Because the effects of exposure time varied among storage conditions and recovery procedures, the condition-specific comparisons presented in Table 1 provide the most appropriate assessment of treatment duration.

To evaluate whether changes in the physicochemical properties of the rinse liquid were associated with differences in SA cell recovery between recovery procedures, pH and temperature were monitored during each treatment (Table 2). Across storage conditions and exposure times, pH values remained relatively stable, ranging from 7.08 to 7.42, with no significant differences between vortex agitation and ultrasonication (P > 0.05). The temperature of the rinse liquid increased slightly with exposure time, particularly during vortex agitation; however, values remained within a relatively narrow range (29.5 to 34.2°C). Although significant temperature differences between recovery procedures were observed under some conditions, mainly at longer exposure times, these changes did not indicate substantial heating of the rinse liquid. In addition, during sample processing, visible epicarp fissures were observed in some tomatoes subjected to vortex agitation, particularly after storage at 35°C for 3 days and at both temperatures after 6 days, whereas such fissures were not observed after ultrasonication. These observations suggest that softened fruit may be more susceptible to surface damage during vortex agitation. The sequential procedure separated two operationally defined bacterial populations: LA cells, removed by the initial rinse, and SA cells, recovered afterward by vortexing or ultrasonication. Under the evaluated conditions, vortexing yielded higher SA populations of S. enterica and L. monocytogenes in most comparisons but caused visible epicarp fissures in some softened tomatoes. No such damage was observed with ultrasonication. Thus, vortexing may be preferred to maximize SA-cell recovery, whereas ultrasonication may be selected when preserving fruit-surface integrity is a priority.

Limitations

The methods were evaluated only in Roma tomatoes under the storage conditions included in this study; therefore, its application to other tomato cultivars, fresh produce commodities, or different storage conditions requires further studies. In addition, only vortex agitation and ultrasonication were compared as procedures for recovering strongly attached (SA) cells. Although vortex agitation generally yielded higher SA cell recovery for S. enterica and L. monocytogenes, visible epicarp fissures were observed in softened tomatoes. Thus, the use of vortex agitation should be balanced against the need to preserve sample integrity, particularly in mature or softened fruit.
