On Anti-Confinement Estimates for Self-Repelling Random Walks
Tobias Schmidt, Mark Sellke

Abstract
. We study a class of d d -dimensional random walks, including the two-dimensional simple random walk, reweighted by a self-repelling Gibbsian pair potential. We prove lower bounds on the diffusion constant for short-range interactions, and superdiffusive behavior in case the interaction is sufficiently long-range. Finally, we show that in the superdiffusive regime, faster temporal decay can be compensated by stronger spatial repulsion and vice-versa. Our technique combines GKS-based correlation inequalities on path space with recursive multi-scale estimates.

We assume that

Taking these definitions, it holds that

With this notation at hand, we are interested in properties of the probability measure

(1.1)

\begin{split}\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}({\mathrm{d}}x)&=\frac{1}{Z_{\alpha,T}}\exp\left(\alpha\sum\limits_{0\leq i<j\leq T}W(x_{j}-x_{i},j-i)\right){\mathbb{P}}({\mathrm{d}}x)\\

Next, we take a closer look at the time-decay

Finally, let us note that on a heuristic level, there is monotonicity in the time-decay: consider once again an interaction of product form

One can also ask what happens if

Following this line of thought, one is led to believe that a time-decay resulting in superdiffusive behavior depends on the spatial interaction in the sense that “stronger” spatial repulsion allows for faster time-decay. We will show this for the special class of

As previously alluded to, our second result concerns long-range interactions. We are interested in finding a regime of superdiffusive behavior. In order to do this, we will study the following path measures.
Define for

the measure

(1.2)

\begin{split}\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}({\mathrm{d}}x)&=\frac{1}{Z_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}\exp\left(\alpha\sum\limits_{0\leq i<j\leq T}\frac{\|x_{j}-x_{i}\|^{\gamma}}{|j-i|^{\xi}}\right){\mathbb{P}}({\mathrm{d}}x).\end{split}

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\gamma\in 2{\mathbb{N}}
and let

Put in words, we show superdiffusive behavior for fixed

, where

. Setting
\gamma=2
and considering the simple random walk in

Let us consider the case

and write

x_{n}=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\phi_{m},\qquad\phi_{m}\in\{\pm 1\},

so that for

x_{i+t}-x_{i}=\sum_{m=i+1}^{i+t}\phi_{m}=:S_{i,t}.

For the exponent
\gamma=2k
the self–interaction of the path is therefore built out of the terms
S_{i,t}^{2k}
. We now consider only

, because long–range interactions are expected to determine the behavior of the system.
Expanding
S_{0,t}^{2k}=(\sum_{m=1}^{t}\phi_{m})^{2k}
and using
\phi_{m}^{2}=1
, the coefficient of a fixed two–body interaction
\phi_{a}\phi_{b}
(

) is obtained by counting monomials in which

and
b
appear an odd number of times and all other indices appear an even number of times. The leading contribution comes from choosing

shows that the pair coefficient scales as

, meaning

(1.3)

S_{0,t}^{2k}\;=\;A_{k}(t)\;+\;C_{k}\,t^{k-1}\!\sum_{1\leq a<b\leq t}\phi_{a}\phi_{b}\;+\;\text{(higher--body spin terms)}\;+\;O(t^{k-2})\!\sum_{a<b}\phi_{a}\phi_{b},

for constants
C_{k}>0
(and some scalar term
A_{k}(t)
) independent of
t
.

Fix two spins
\phi_{m},\phi_{n}
at distance
r=|m-n|
. For each
t\geq r
, the number of blocks of length
t
that contain both

and
n
is asymptotically
(t-r)
; hence, the induced coupling at distance
r
behaves as

(1.4)

\sum_{t\geq r}(t-r)\,\frac{t^{k-1}}{t^{\xi}}\;\asymp\;\sum_{t\geq r}t^{k-\xi}\;\asymp\;r^{-(\xi-k-1)},

whenever
\xi>k+1
.
Thus, at the level of two body interactions, the system behaves like a one–dimensional long–range ferromagnet with tail
\sim r^{-s}
, where
s=\xi-\frac{\gamma}{2}-1
. In the quadratic case
\gamma=2
this reproduces the well-known Dyson model and yields the expected critical threshold
\xi_{c}=4

[
26
]
. One therefore expects that superdiffusive behavior occurs when the effective exponent satisfies
s\leq 2
, i.e.

\xi<3+\frac{\gamma}{2}

at sufficiently low temperature. For
\xi>\xi_{c}(\gamma)
the induced long–range coupling is expected to be too weak to induce long–range alignment of spins, and diffusive behavior is expected (possibly with a renormalized diffusion constant).
Our approach can therefore verify the correct scaling of
\xi
in terms of
\gamma
, but is slightly off on the exact threshold for which superdiffusivity is expected to occur.

In order to understand why our results are most probably not tight, let us briefly sketch our approach. First, we may write

x_{T}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{T/2}\phi_{i}+\sum\limits_{i=T/2+1}^{T}\phi_{i}=:\sigma_{\mathrm{L}}+\sigma_{\mathrm{R}}.

Next, decompose the Gibbs interaction into two disjoint blocks and all interactions between those blocks, i.e.

\begin{split}\sum\limits_{0\leq i<j\leq T}W(x_{j}-x_{i},j-i)&=\sum\limits_{0\leq i<j\leq T/2}W(x_{j}-x_{i},j-i)+\sum\limits_{T/2+1\leq i<j\leq T}W(x_{j}-x_{i},j-i)\\
&+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i\leq T/2\\
T/2+1\leq j\leq T\end{subarray}}W(x_{j}-x_{i},j-i).\end{split}

By GKS, we may replace
the third term by
W(x_{T},T)
, omitting all other interactions between the left and the right block.
Specializing to
W(z,T)=\|z\|^{\gamma}/T^{\gamma/2+c}
for
c>0
, one can deduce that
W(x_{T},T)
is more strongly repulsive (in the GKS sense) than
(\sigma_{\mathrm{L}}+\sigma_{\mathrm{R}})^{2}/T^{1+c}
(see Lemma
5.4
).
By ignoring all other interactions between the left block and the right block,
\sigma_{\mathrm{L}}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{R}}
are independent besides the single interaction between them. This makes it tractable on how much the Gibbs interaction
(\sigma_{\mathrm{L}}+\sigma_{\mathrm{R}})^{2}/T^{1+c}
improves the variance of
x_{T}
compared to the case where
\sigma_{\mathrm{L}}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{R}}
are independent; we refer to Lemma
5.1
for the details. The price to pay for this independence is that we ignore a lot of interactions between the blocks, which likely accounts for the discrepancy between the previously stated heuristics and our Theorem
1.7
.

2.
Related Work

The model we study in this paper is essentially a
self-interacting random walk
, also known in statistical mechanics as a polymer measure. In these models, a random walk is reweighted by an interaction term depending on its local time or its increments. A classical question is the existence and qualitative behavior of Gibbs measures relative to a free walk or Brownian motion. Early works such as
[
9
,
8
]
established existence criteria and proved central limit theorems for perturbations with sufficiently fast temporal decay. The work
[
15
]
identified a diffusive phase for weakly self-repelling walks in
d>2
, showing that the perturbation does not destroy Gaussian behavior at large scales. In the context of self-attracting interactions, Donsker–Varadhan
[
17
]
computed the free energy in strong-coupling asymptotics for the Fröhlich polaron using their large deviation techniques. In
[
31
]
, the diffusion constant of the reweighted process was heuristically connected to the effective mass of a quantum particle coupled to a scalar quantum field; the follow-up work
[
19
]
made this rigorous. More recently, a large body of work has investigated quantitative properties of the diffusion constant, including
[
4
,
28
,
6
,
7
,
3
]
via probabilistic methods. Due to the connection discovered in
[
31
]
, the results from
[
13
,
14
]
can also be interpreted as bounds on the diffusion constant.

If the temporal decay of the pair potential
W
is compactly supported or exponential, the walk remains diffusive: the mean-square displacement satisfies

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}}[\|x_{T}\|^{2}]\approx\sigma^{2}dT,\qquad\sigma^{2}\neq 1,

with
\sigma^{2}<1
in case of an attracting potential and
>1
in case the potential is repulsive. In this regime, the interaction produces only a finite renormalization of the underlying random walk.
For
attractive
interactions, especially in one or two dimensions, localization (or collapse of the walk) can occur even for short-range potentials. In
[
11
]
it is proved that a two-dimensional random walk with an attractive potential behaves sub-diffusively. In
[
10
]
, Bolthausen and Schmock analyzed a general class of
d
-dimensional self-attracting walks; in
d=1
and under sufficiently strong attraction, the walk clusters in a small region, and
x_{T}
converges (when properly rescaled) to a non-trivial random limit. This indicates that in low dimensions there is effectively no finite critical coupling separating diffusive and localized phases: sufficiently strong attraction enforces subdiffusivity or full trapping. This behavior mirrors the classical “polymer collapse” phenomenon in statistical mechanics and is conceptually connected to binding in quantum field models such as the Fröhlich polaron
[
30
]
in dimension
d\leq 2
. We want to note, however, that all techniques that are used to rigorously study behavior of self-interacting random walks discussed so far are based on the large deviation toolbox, meaning that the time decay is either constant or mean–field related.

One interesting feature of our model is the presence of a
long-range temporal interaction

W(z,t)=\frac{\|z\|^{\gamma}}{|t|^{\xi}},

which decays slowly in the time separation
|t-s|
and is not constant. Long-range memory can deeply affect path behavior and is reminiscent of Dyson’s one-dimensional Ising model with power-law interactions
[
20
,
2
]
, where sufficiently slow decay leads to a genuine phase transition. In hierarchical versions of such spin systems, quantities related to susceptibility satisfy recursive relations across dyadic scales, reflecting the accumulation of correlations over multiple length scales. While our model is not hierarchical by construction, a similar dyadic variance recursion emerges at the level of the analysis, driven by long-range temporal interactions (see Lemma
5.2
). Rigorous results for long-range
attractive
interactions of Brownian paths were recently obtained by the authors in
[
7
]
, confirming earlier physical predictions of pinning transitions for long-range self-interacting path measures
[
30
]
.

While attractive interactions lead to collapse, the
repulsive
interactions considered in the present work reward expansion of the walk. This parallels the physics of self-avoiding polymers, where avoidance of previously visited sites leads to superdiffusive or even ballistic behavior. In one dimension, even weak repulsion can induce superdiffusivity: Tóth
[
35
]
proved that the “true” self-avoiding walk (TSAW) on
\mathbb{Z}
scales like
t^{2/3}

and various reinforced or repulsive random walks exhibit similar persistent drift.
In
[
26
]
, the authors study the model defined in equation (
1.2
) with
\gamma=2
and
\xi\in(3,4)
in two dimensions. They deduce that the process is diffusive for high enough temperature, and ballistic for low enough temperature. This implies that temperature is a much more delicate parameter in the discrete attractive setting compared to the continuous model studied in
[
7
]
.
More generally, self–repellent walks on
{\mathbb{Z}}
as in
[
35
]
are defined via a Gibbs measure depending on the local time of the process
[
1
,
33
]
. The conjectured limiting object for a lot of random self–repellent motions is the “true“ self–repelling motion, constructed in
[
34
]
. The convergence of TSAW to the true self–repelling motion was verified recently in
[
23
]
. Other self-repelling models include
[
24
,
18
]
, where the authors are interested in the solution of an SDE which yields a self–repulsive process. Quantities of interest are the scaling behavior or the mean–square displacement. Results on the mean–square displacement can also be found in
[
32
]
. The work
[
22
]
discusses connections between TSAW and the continuous model. Moreover, scaling limits are provided for both models in
d\geq 3
. In the recent work
[
16
]
, an invariance principle is derived in
d=2
, providing evidence that the mean–square displacement is diffusive times a logarithmic divergence with power
1/2
.

In the present model, we show superdiffusive behavior whenever

\xi<\frac{\gamma}{2}+c_{\mathrm{crit}},\qquad c_{\mathrm{crit}}\approx 1.

That is, for sufficiently slow temporal decay of the repulsive potential, the energetic incentive to keep increments large overcomes the entropic cost, and the mean-square displacement grows strictly faster than linearly:

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}}[\|x_{T}\|^{2}]\gg T.

This work follows a general methodology that was recently developed and applied in the continuous polaron model with self-attracting pair potential. The main idea is that one can use correlation inequalities (previously the Gaussian correlation inequality) to compare the full measure
\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}
to a simpler path measure, say
\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}
. Two central simplifications are replacing complicated interactions by simpler ones, and omitting interactions between different areas, recovering a Markov property. For more details, see e.g.
[
28
, Corollary 2.2]
and Lemma
3.4
.
Compared to the previous work
[
7
]
, we are not able to use the previously developed techniques based on Gaussian domination. While they are powerful and apply to a broad class of problems, they crucially rely on the underlying path measure being Gaussian and the interaction to be self-attracting.
The discrete-time setting and the repulsive long-range interactions studied in this work therefore require a different approach, and the present work extends the range of techniques available for analyzing long-range effects in self-interacting random walks.

3.
Correlation inequalities and change of measure

The following is the specific GKS inequality that is used for the remainder of this paper. As soon as it is justified that showing (
3.1
) is sufficient to prove the claim, we simply follow the argument in
[
29
]
.
Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{0}
be the class of functions with

f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{0}\iff f:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}},f\text{ is increasing and non-negative on $(0,\infty)$ and even or odd.}

By
{\mathcal{F}}
we denote positive linear combinations of functions in
{\mathcal{F}}_{0}
. Note that
{\mathcal{F}}
is closed under taking products, which is a tremendous advantage compared to increasing functions in the FKG sense. We also define the function class

{\mathcal{F}}_{n}:=\Big\{f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}},f(x_{1},...,x_{n})=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x_{i}),f_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}\Big\}.

In other words,
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
lifts the space
{\mathcal{F}}
to
n
dimensions by taking products of coordinates.
In particular, p-spin interactions of the form

F(\phi)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\phi_{t_{k}}^{i_{k}}

are contained in
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
. Here, as before,

\phi_{t_{k}}=(\phi_{t_{k}}^{1},...,\phi_{t_{k}}^{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
29
, Theorem 7.1]
)
.

Let
f,g\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
be functions that take only coordinates of one–step increments as input. I.e.,

f(x)=f_{1}(\phi_{1}^{1})f_{2}(\phi_{1}^{2})\dots f_{dT}(\phi_{T}^{d})
with
f_{j}\in{\mathcal{F}}
.
Then, it holds that

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}}[fg]\geq{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}}[f]{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}}[g].

Proof.

By linearity of the integral we may assume that
f_{j}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{0}
for all
j\leq dT
. Note that

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}}[fg]-{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}}[f]{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}}[g]=\frac{1}{2}\iint\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}({\mathrm{d}}x)\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}({\mathrm{d}}y)\big(f(x)-f(y)\big)\big(g(x)-g(y)\big).

Recall that
Z_{\alpha,T}
denotes the partition function of
\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}
. Moreover, take
\Xi({\mathrm{d}}\phi):=\prod_{1\leq j\leq T}\eta({\mathrm{d}}\phi_{j})
. As a random walk contains the same information as its time steps at each time point, there exists
\tilde{f}:\phi\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\tilde{f}(\phi)=f(x),

if
x
is the random walk generated by the steps
\phi
. Finally, abbreviating

F(\phi,\varphi)=\big(\tilde{f}(\phi)-\tilde{f}(\varphi)\big)\big(\tilde{g}(\phi)-\tilde{g}(\varphi)\big)

and

\phi_{[s+1,t]}=\sum\limits_{i=s+1}^{t}\phi_{i},

it holds that

\begin{split}&2Z_{\alpha,T}^{2}\left({\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}}[fg]-{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}}[f]{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}}[g]\right)\\
&=\iint\Xi\left({\mathrm{d}}\phi\right)\Xi\left({\mathrm{d}}\varphi\right)F(\phi,\varphi)\exp\left(\alpha\!\!\!\!\sum\limits_{1\leq s<t\leq T}\!\!\!W\left(\sum\limits_{i=s+1}^{t}\phi_{i},t-s\right)\!+\!W\left(\sum\limits_{i=s+1}^{t}\varphi_{i},t-s\right)\!\right)\\
&=\iint\Xi\left({\mathrm{d}}\phi\right)\Xi\left({\mathrm{d}}\varphi\right)F(\phi,\varphi)\exp\left(\!\alpha\!\!\ \sum\limits_{s<t}\!\!\!\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n(t-s)}\!\!c_{i,t-s}\!\!\left(\!\!\left(\sum\limits_{p=1}^{d}(\phi_{[s+1,t]}^{p})^{q}\right)^{i}\!\!\!+\!\!\left(\sum\limits_{p=1}^{d}(\varphi_{[s+1,t]}^{p})^{q}\!\!\right)^{i}\right)\!\!\right).\\
\end{split}

It thus suffices to show

(3.1)

\iint\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}\big(F_{i}(\phi)+\epsilon_{i}F_{i}(\varphi)\big)\Xi({\mathrm{d}}\phi)\Xi({\mathrm{d}}\varphi)\geq 0

with
F_{i}
being a product of
{\mathcal{F}}_{0}
functions and
\epsilon_{i}=\pm 1
. This follows from expanding the exponentials into a series (note that products of coordinates of any power are contained in
{\mathcal{F}}
). The main point now is that

ab\pm a^{\prime}b^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}(a+a^{\prime})(b\pm b^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2}(a-a^{\prime})(b\mp b^{\prime}).

That is, (
3.1
) is shown if we can show the claim for
F_{i}=f_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{1}
and
\Xi
replaced by an even measure
\lambda
on
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Since
f_{i}
is even or odd,

f_{i}(\sigma x)=\sigma^{\pi(i)}f_{i}(x),

with
\sigma=\pm 1
and
\pi(i)=2
if
f_{i}
is even and
1
if
f_{i}
is odd. Since
\lambda
is even we find that (
3.1
) can be written as

\begin{split}\eqref{eq:gks_2_objective}&=\sum\limits_{\sigma_{1}=\pm 1,\sigma_{2}=\pm 1}\int_{x\geq 0,y\geq 0}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(f_{i}(\sigma_{1}x)+\epsilon_{i}f_{i}(\sigma_{2}y)\right)\lambda({\mathrm{d}}x)\lambda({\mathrm{d}}y)\\
&=\sum\limits_{\sigma_{3}=\pm 1}\big[1+(-1)^{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\pi(i)}\big]\int_{x,y\geq 0}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}\big(f_{i}(x)+\epsilon_{i}\sigma_{3}^{\pi(i)}f_{i}(y)\big)\lambda({\mathrm{d}}x)\lambda({\mathrm{d}}y).\end{split}

In the last equality we consider the cases
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}
and
\sigma_{1}\neq\sigma_{2}
with
\sigma_{3}=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}
. The conclusion is that it suffices to show the claim integrating over
x,y\geq 0
. This is just
1+\prod_{i=1}^{m}\epsilon_{i}

times the integral over
x\geq y\geq 0
. But over this domain all functions we integrate are non-negative (monotone and non-negative by assumption).
∎

In a first step we use (GKS) to argue that it suffices to treat the case
d=1
. Here, the assumption that the random walk evolves independently in each coordinate is a central ingredient. Indeed, it is possible to treat every coordinate as independent spin, meaning that it is possible to only keep interactions acting on the same dimension.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
{\mathbb{P}}
be a product random walk with increment distribution
\eta
and coordinate distribution
\nu
. Denote the one–dimensional reweighted random walk with increments
\nu
by

\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}^{1}_{\alpha,T}
. Then,

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}}[\|x_{T}\|^{2}]\geq d{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}^{1}_{\alpha,T}}[x_{T}^{2}].

Proof.

Recall by Assumption
1.1
that

W(z,t)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n(t)}c_{i,t}\left(\sum\limits_{p=1}^{d}(z^{p})^{q}\right)^{i}.

By the binomial Theorem

W(z,t)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n(t)}c_{i,t}\sum\limits_{p=1}^{d}(z^{p})^{qi}+(\text{remainder}),

where it is easily verified that the remainder term is contained in
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
for some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
due to the fact that it is a (non–negative) linear combination of products of monomials. By (GKS), this term can be omitted, showing the claim.
∎

Corollary
3.2
allows us to work with random walks in
1
dimension, which we will do without mentioning it again.

Remark 3.3
.

We will frequently use Theorem
3.1
in the following way: take

(3.2)

\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}({\mathrm{d}}x)\propto\exp\left(\alpha\sum\limits_{i=1}^{T-2}W(x_{i+2}-x_{i},2)\right){\mathbb{P}}({\mathrm{d}}x).

Since we can expand

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}}\propto\exp\left(\alpha\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<j\leq T\\
j-i\neq 2\end{subarray}}W(x_{j}-x_{i},|j-i|)\right)

into a series and by interchanging integral and summation, we find that this part of the self-interaction is a uniformly bounded limit of positive linear polynomials of single steps. That is, we find for
f(x)=f_{1}(\phi_{1})f_{2}(\phi_{2})...
with
f_{j}\in{\mathcal{F}}
that

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}}[f]\geq{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}}[f].

Of course, there is nothing special about this specific grouping of self-interactions. The point is that one can omit certain self-interaction terms if one only wants to lower bound an average for specific functions
f
.

The argument provided in the last Remark is formalized in more generality in the following Lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

Fix
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and let
\phi=(\phi_{1},...,\phi_{N})
be i.i.d. spins with symmetric and compact law. For every
I\subseteq[N]
let

H_{I}
be a non-negative combination of monomials and

\phi_{I}=\sum\limits_{i\in I}\phi_{i}.

Let
M\subseteq 2^{[N]}
and define

{\mathbb{P}}_{M}({\mathrm{d}}\phi)\propto\exp\left(\sum\limits_{I\in M}H_{I}(\phi_{I})\right){\mathbb{P}}({\mathrm{d}}\phi),

where
{\mathbb{P}}
is the product measure with corresponding single site distribution.
Then, for any
f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{N}
taking only
\phi_{1},...,\phi_{N}
as input and
M\supseteq M^{\prime}
,

{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{M}}[f]\geq{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{M^{\prime}}}[f].

Proof.

It is easily seen that

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}{\mathbb{P}}_{M}}{{\mathrm{d}}{\mathbb{P}}_{M^{\prime}}}=\frac{1}{{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{M^{\prime}}}\Big[\exp\Big(\sum_{I\in M\backslash M^{\prime}}H_{I}(\phi_{I})\Big)\Big]}\exp\left(\sum\limits_{I\in M\backslash M^{\prime}}H_{I}(\phi_{I})\right).

It therefore suffices to show for
f\in{\mathcal{F}}
as given that

{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{M^{\prime}}}\left[f\exp\left(\sum\limits_{I\in M\backslash M^{\prime}}H_{I}(\phi_{I})\right)\right]\geq{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{M^{\prime}}}\left[f\right]{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{M^{\prime}}}\left[\exp\left(\sum\limits_{I\in M\backslash M^{\prime}}H_{I}(\phi_{I})\right)\right].

The result follows by expanding the exponential into a series, exchanging summation and integration and applying Theorem
3.1
. The fact that integration and summation can be exchanged follows by Tonelli’s Theorem.
∎

Let us also consider another simplification that we already discussed. As our random walk is assumed to be symmetric and the interaction between any two spins is of product form, we are in the position to apply Wells’ inequality.

See the Appendix of

Remark 4.3
.

Let us briefly discuss how tight the above result is. In order to do this, let us consider the measure
\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\beta,T}
with
W(z,2)=z^{2}
, which corresponds to the classical one-dimensional Ising model
with nearest-neighbor interaction and zero external field,

H(\sigma)=-\beta\sum_{i=1}^{T-1}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1},\qquad\sigma_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}.

The associated
2\times 2
transfer matrix is

A=\begin{pmatrix}e^{\beta}&e^{-\beta}\\
e^{-\beta}&e^{\beta}\end{pmatrix},

whose eigenvalues are explicitly

\lambda_{0}=2\cosh(\beta),\qquad\lambda_{1}=2\sinh(\beta).

Hence,

\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{0}}=\tanh(\beta)=:t
with

0<t<1
.
In this model the two–point function can be evaluated explicitly to be

\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle=t^{|i-j|}.

Define the susceptibility

\chi(\beta):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\langle\sigma_{0}\sigma_{k}\rangle=1+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}t^{k}=\frac{1+t}{1-t}.

As
\beta\to\infty
,

t=\tanh(\beta)=1-2e^{-2\beta}+o(e^{-2\beta}),

and therefore

\chi(\beta)=\frac{1+t}{1-t}\approx\exp(2\beta).

Consequently, the double sum of two–point functions satisfies

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i,j\leq T\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle=\chi(\beta)\,T+o(T),

and the prefactor grows like
\exp(2\beta)
. Note that similar arguments can be made in case the pair potential has compact support in time (and is non-quadratic) by applying generalized transfer matrix techniques. Therefore,
this reflects that our approach captures the correct first-order behavior of the diffusion constant (note that in our model there is an additional factor of
2
in front of
\beta
).

Proof of Proposition
1.6

By an application of (GKS) it is seen that

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T}}[\phi_{j}]\geq{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{j}}[\phi_{j}],

(5.4)

\begin{split}2{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}\left[\left(T^{-1/2}x_{T}\right)^{2}\right]\;&\geq\;{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta,2}}[(\sigma^{1}_{T/2}+\sigma^{2}_{T/2})^{2}]\\
\;&=\;{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta,2}}[(\sigma^{1}_{T/2})^{2}]+{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta,2}}[(\sigma^{2}_{T/2})^{2}]+2{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta,2}}[\sigma^{1}_{T/2}\sigma^{2}_{T/2}].\end{split}

Symmetry and the construction of
{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta,2}
ensure that

{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta,2}}[(\sigma^{1}_{T/2})^{2}]={\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta,2}}[(\sigma^{2}_{T/2})^{2}]\geq V
, so the crucial quantity
to bound from below is the covariance term
{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta,2}}[\sigma^{1}_{T/2}\sigma^{2}_{T/2}]
.

When
\beta=0
we recover the trivial bound

{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta,2}}[\sigma^{1}_{T/2}\sigma^{2}_{T/2}]=0
, so (
5.4
) simply returns the
variance
V
at the previous scale.
For
\beta>0
we expect
{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta,2}}[\sigma^{1}_{T/2}\sigma^{2}_{T/2}]>0
and hence
a genuine increase in variance.
The following Lemma provides a quantitative lower bound that depends only on
the variance
V
and not on any further features of
\theta
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\theta
be a symmetric probability measure on
{\mathbb{R}}
with
variance
V
supported on a finite set
\Omega
, and let
{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta,2}
be the tilted product measure
defined in (
5.3
). Suppose that
\beta V\leq 2
.
Then

{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta,2}}[yz]\;\geq\;V\,\tanh(\beta V).

As far as we can tell, the condition
\beta V\leq 2
is artificial and only of technical nature. On the other hand, we can assume it to be the case by an application of Lemma
3.4
.
The proof of Lemma
5.1
will be given in Section
6
.

Putting all of this together, we find

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}\left[\left(T^{-1/2}x_{T}\right)^{2}\right]\geq\big(1+\tanh(\min\{2,\alpha V_{T/2}/T^{c}\})\big)V_{T/2}.

We can now iterate: by exactly the same argument, it is possible to lower bound
V_{T/2}
in terms of
V_{T/4}
, corresponding to the random variables

\sigma^{11}_{T/4}=\sqrt{\frac{4}{T}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{T/4}\phi_{j},

\sigma^{12}_{T/4}=\sqrt{\frac{4}{T}}\sum\limits_{j=T/4+1}^{T/2}\phi_{j}

and similar definitions for

\sigma^{21}_{T/4}
and
\sigma^{22}_{T/4}
. Writing the recursion in terms of an iteration variable
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the question about superdiffusivity can be phrased in terms of whether
V_{n}
tends to infinity. Recall

(5.5)

c_{\mathrm{crit}}=\log_{2}\bigl(1+\tanh 2\bigr)\in(0,1).

Lemma 5.2
.

Fix parameters
\alpha>0
and
c<c_{\mathrm{crit}}
, and take the recursion

(5.6)

V_{1}=1,\qquad V_{n+1}=\bigl(1+\tanh(\min(\alpha 2^{-cn}V_{n},2))\bigr)\,V_{n},\quad n\geq 1.

Then, there exists
\alpha_{*}(c)>0
such that
for
\alpha\in(\alpha_{*}(c),\infty)
,
V_{n}\to\infty
.

Proof.

Fix
c<c_{\mathrm{crit}}
, and define the rescaled variable

y_{n}:=\alpha\,2^{-cn}V_{n}.

Then the recursion becomes

y_{n+1}=2^{-c}\big(1+\tanh(\min(y_{n},2))\big)y_{n}.

Define

F_{c}(y):=2^{-c}(1+\tanh(\min(y,2)))\,y,\qquad g_{c}(y):=\frac{F_{c}(y)}{y}=2^{-c}(1+\tanh(\min(y,2))).

Then
y_{n+1}=F_{c}(y_{n})=g_{c}(y_{n})\,y_{n}
. Since
\tanh
is increasing and bounded by 1,
g_{c}(y)
is increasing in
y
with

g_{c}(0^{+})=2^{-c},\quad g_{c}(y)\nearrow g_{c}^{\mathrm{sat}}:=2^{-c}(1+\tanh 2).

By definition of
c_{\mathrm{crit}}
, we have
g_{c}^{\mathrm{sat}}>1
.
Define the critical value

\alpha_{*}(c):=2^{c}\cdot\operatorname{arctanh}(2^{c}-1),

which is the unique value such that

g_{c}\big(\alpha_{*}2^{-c}\big)=1.

By assumption
\alpha>\alpha_{*}(c)
, which implies

y_{1}>\alpha_{*}2^{-c}
, so
g_{c}(y_{1})>1
and
y_{2}>y_{1}
. The sequence
y_{n}
increases until it exceeds 2, after which

y_{n+1}=g_{c}^{\mathrm{sat}}y_{n},

with
g_{c}^{\mathrm{sat}}>1
. So
y_{n}=C(g_{c}^{\mathrm{sat}})^{n}
for some constant
C>0
, and
V_{n}=\alpha^{-1}2^{cn}y_{n}
.
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\Phi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}\phi_{j}
. Then, for
\gamma=2k
with
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\Phi^{\gamma}-\Phi^{2}
is a nonnegative linear combination of even spin monomials of the spins
\phi_{j}
(
1\leq j\leq N
).

Proof.

Abbreviate
S=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\phi_{j}
.
Note that
\Phi^{\gamma}
is already a sum of functions that we care about. Therefore, it suffices to show that this sum contains
\frac{1}{N}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}
for
i\neq j
.
This is clear by writing

\Phi^{\gamma}=\frac{1}{N^{k}}\underbrace{S^{2}\cdot...\cdot S^{2}}_{k-1\text{ times}}\sum\limits_{i,j}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}.

Since
S^{2}=N+\sum_{a\neq b}\phi_{a}\phi_{b}
, the constant term of
(S^{2})^{k-1}

is
N^{k-1}
. Hence, when multiplying
S^{2k-2}
by

\sum_{i,j}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}
, the coefficient of each
\phi_{i}\phi_{j}
(with
i\neq j
)
is at least
N^{k-1}
. As all other terms have non-negative coefficients no cancellations can occur. This shows the claim.
∎

The previous Proposition allows us to switch from general
\gamma
to
\gamma=2
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\gamma=2k
with
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Moreover, let
\xi=\gamma/2+c
with
c>0
. Then,

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}[x_{T}^{2}]\geq{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,2,1+c}}[x_{T}^{2}].

Proof.

Note that the Radon-Nikodym derivative

{\mathrm{d}}\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}/{\mathrm{d}}\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,2,1+c}
is proportional to the exponential of sums of the form

\beta\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum\limits_{j=\ell}^{\ell+n}\phi_{j}\right)^{\gamma}-\beta\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum\limits_{j=\ell}^{\ell+n}\phi_{j}\right)^{2}.

Here,
\ell,n\leq T
such that
\ell+n\leq T
.
By Proposition
5.3
this difference is the sum of products of
\phi_{j}
for
j\in\ell,...,\ell+n
. The result follows from the GKS inequality by using Lemma
3.4
.
∎

The proof of Theorem
1.7
follows by putting the previous three Lemmas together and implementing the previously described strategy.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

By the previous Lemma we find

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}\left[\left(T^{-1/2}x_{T}\right)^{2}\right]\geq{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,2,1+c}}\left[\left(T^{-1/2}x_{T}\right)^{2}\right].

Next, an application of (GKS) yields

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,\gamma,\xi}}\left[\left(T^{-1/2}x_{T}\right)^{2}\right]\geq{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T/2,2,1+c}}\left[(\sigma_{T/2}^{1})^{2}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,2,1+c}}\left[\sigma_{T/2}^{1}\sigma_{T/2}^{2}\right].

Take

\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\mathrm{split,T}}({\mathrm{d}}x)\propto\exp\left(\alpha\sum\limits_{i<j,j\leq T/2}\frac{(x_{j}-x_{i})^{2}}{|j-i|^{1+c}}+\alpha\sum\limits_{i<j,i\geq T/2+1}\frac{(x_{j}-x_{i})^{2}}{|j-i|^{1+c}}+\frac{\alpha(\sigma_{T/2}^{2}+\sigma_{T/2}^{1})^{2}}{T^{c}}\right){\mathbb{P}}({\mathrm{d}}x).

Let
V:={\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,2,1+c}}[(\sigma_{T/2}^{1})^{2}]
.
Apply Lemma
5.1
to find that

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T,2,1+c}}\left[\sigma_{T/2}^{1}\sigma_{T/2}^{2}\right]\geq{\mathbb{E}}^{\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\mathrm{split,T}}}[\sigma_{T/2}^{1}\sigma_{T/2}^{2}]\geq V\tanh\left(\frac{\alpha}{T^{c}}V\right).

In order to calculate
V
, we again write

{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T/2,2,1+c}}[(\sigma_{T/2}^{1})^{2}]\geq{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T/4,2,1+c}}[(\sigma_{T/4}^{11})^{2}]+{\mathbb{E}}^{\hat{{\mathbb{P}}}_{\alpha,T/2,2,1+c}}[(\sigma_{T/4}^{11})(\sigma_{T/4}^{12})].

Similar to before, we again apply Lemma
5.1
to the measure which treats both r.v. as independent with an exponential tilt. Recursing this is exactly what is studied in Lemma
5.2
, yielding the result.
∎

6.
Symmetric laws under a one–variable tilt

The goal of this Section is to prove Lemma
5.1
.
Recall that in the recursive scheme of Section
5
,
we encountered a
symmetric
random variable

S=xy
with variance
V^{2}
(our random variables were symmetric i.i.d. with variance
V
)
and we wish to understand the behavior of the expectation

\mathbb{E}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta}}[S],\qquad\beta>0,

under the exponentially tilted probability measure

(6.1)

\mathbb{P}_{\beta}(\mathrm{d}s)\propto e^{\beta s}\,\mathbb{P}(S\in\mathrm{d}s).

Our goal is to find a
universal lower bound
on
\mathbb{E}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta}}[S]

that depends only on the variance
V^{2}
and holds for
every
symmetric law of variance
V^{2}
.

Even if
S
is supported on a finite, symmetric set
\Omega
, this problem is high–dimensional in principle, since
\Omega
might be large and the law of
S
arbitrary.
We now show that the extremal structure of the feasible set allows one to reduce the analysis to a
four–point
measure, i.e. a measure with
4
atoms at most.

In all that follows, we work on the space of probability measures
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}(\Omega)
on the discrete, finite set
\Omega
so that
x\in\Omega\iff-x\in\Omega
. Next, take

\mathcal{S}_{V^{2}}:=\Bigl\{\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{1}(\Omega)\text{ is symmetric and}\int x^{2}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=V^{2}\Bigr\}.

This is a weakly compact and convex set.
For
\mu\in\mathcal{S}_{V^{2}}
define the affine functionals

L_{1}(\mu):=\int x\,e^{\beta x}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x),\qquad L_{2}(\mu):=\int e^{\beta x}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x),

so that

\mathbb{E}^{\mu_{\beta}}[S]=\frac{L_{1}(\mu)}{L_{2}(\mu)}.

The following Lemma is classical. We provide a proof for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
K
be a nonempty compact convex subset of a locally convex topological
vector space, and let
L_{1},L_{2}:K\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous affine maps with

L_{2}(x)>0
for all
x\in K
. Set

F(x):=\frac{L_{1}(x)}{L_{2}(x)},\qquad x\in K.

Then
F
attains its minimum on
K
at an extreme point of
K
.

Proof.

Since
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are continuous on the compact set
K
and
L_{2}>0
on
K
,
the functional
F
is continuous on
K
. Hence there exists
x_{0}\in K
such that

F(x_{0})=\min_{x\in K}F(x)=:m.

We first show that if
x_{0}
is a minimiser and can be written as a nontrivial
convex combination
x_{0}=tx_{1}+(1-t)x_{2}
with
x_{1},x_{2}\in K
,
t\in(0,1)
,
then both
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are also minimisers, i.e.
F(x_{1})=F(x_{2})=m
.

Because
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are affine, we have

L_{i}(x_{0})=tL_{i}(x_{1})+(1-t)L_{i}(x_{2}),\qquad i=1,2.

Since
F(x_{0})=m
, this is equivalent to

L_{1}(x_{0})=mL_{2}(x_{0}).

Suppose for contradiction that
F(x_{1})>m
. Then there exists
\varepsilon>0

such that

F(x_{1})\geq m+\varepsilon\quad\Longrightarrow\quad L_{1}(x_{1})\geq(m+\varepsilon)L_{2}(x_{1}).

On the other hand, by minimality of
x_{0}
we have
F(x_{2})\geq m
, hence

L_{1}(x_{2})\geq mL_{2}(x_{2}).

Combining these,

\displaystyle L_{1}(x_{0})

\displaystyle=tL_{1}(x_{1})+(1-t)L_{1}(x_{2})

\displaystyle\geq t(m+\varepsilon)L_{2}(x_{1})+(1-t)mL_{2}(x_{2})

\displaystyle=m\bigl(tL_{2}(x_{1})+(1-t)L_{2}(x_{2})\bigr)+t\varepsilon L_{2}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=mL_{2}(x_{0})+t\varepsilon L_{2}(x_{1}).

Since
L_{2}(x_{1})>0
, the last term is strictly positive, so we obtain

L_{1}(x_{0})>mL_{2}(x_{0}),

which contradicts
L_{1}(x_{0})=mL_{2}(x_{0})
. Thus
F(x_{1})\leq m
. Together with

F(x_{1})\geq m
(by minimality of
m
), we get
F(x_{1})=m
. The same argument with

x_{1}
and
x_{2}
interchanged shows
F(x_{2})=m
as well.

Now let

M:=\{x\in K:F(x)=m\}

denote the set of minimisers. Note that
M
is convex by the fact that
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are affine. The set
M
is nonempty (it contains

x_{0}
) and compact (as a closed subset of
K
).

By the Krein–Milman theorem,
M
has at least one extreme point
x^{\ast}
,
and every extreme point of
M
is also an extreme point of
K
.
Finally, let
x^{*}
be an extreme point of
M
such that

x^{*}=ty+(1-t)z
for
y,z\in K
and
t\in(0,1)
. By what we have just shown,
y,z\in M
. By assumption
x^{*}
is an extreme point, meaning that
y=z=x^{*}
. Therefore, an extreme point of
M
is an extreme point of
K
. This shows the claim.
∎

Specializing the previous Lemma to our situation, we find that

\min_{\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}_{V^{2}}}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mu_{\beta}}[S]

is attained at an extreme point of
{\mathcal{S}}_{V^{2}}
. Note that
{\mathcal{S}}_{V^{2}}
can be identified with the probability simplex over
N+1
dimensions, where
2N+1:=|\Omega|
and two linear constraints, i.e.

{\mathcal{S}}_{V^{2}}\simeq\left\{w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N+1}:w_{j}\geq 0,\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N}w_{i}=1,\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{2}w_{i}=V^{2}\right\}.

Here,
w=(w_{0},\dots,w_{N})
corresponds to the probability measure

w_{0}\delta_{0}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\frac{w_{i}}{2}(\delta_{-x_{i}}+\delta_{x_{i}}).

Proposition 6.2
.

The extreme points of
{\mathcal{S}}_{V^{2}}
have at most four atoms. I.e., at most two weights
w_{j}
are greater than zero.

Proof.

Let
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N+1}
and assume that there exists three coordinates (which we take to be
1,2,3
for simplicity) so that
w_{j}>0
for
0<j\leq 3
. We show that
w
is the midpoint of two other weights vectors
w^{+},w^{-}
in
{\mathcal{S}}_{V^{2}}
. Indeed, the linear system of two equations

w_{1}+w_{2}+w_{3}=1-w_{0}-\sum\limits_{i=4}^{N}w_{i}

w_{1}x_{1}^{2}+w_{2}x_{2}^{2}+w_{3}x_{3}^{2}=V^{2}-\sum\limits_{i=4}^{N}w_{i}x_{i}^{2}

can be interpreted as a system of three unknowns
w_{1},w_{2}
and
w_{3}
. Therefore, it is easily verified that the solution manifold is a line in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. Since
w_{j}>0
for
0<j\leq 3
, there exists a direction
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and
\epsilon>0
such that

w^{\pm}=(w_{1}\pm\epsilon h_{1},w_{2}\pm\epsilon h_{2},w_{3}\pm\epsilon h_{3},w_{4},...,w_{N})\in{\mathcal{S}}_{V^{2}}.

In particular,
w=(w^{+}+w^{-})/2
, which by definition means that
w
is no extreme point.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

While we do not need a more general statement, we want to note that this proof can possibly be extended to the setting in which
\Omega
is at least a compact subset of e.g.
{\mathbb{R}}
. The fact that
n
affine conditions on a simplex of probability measures reduce the extreme points to (at most)
n+1
atomic measures is a classical result
[
36
]
.

Our conclusion is that an extreme point of
{\mathcal{S}}_{V^{2}}
is at most four atomic, where the atoms come either in pairs or the minimizer is three-atomic with an atom at
0
. Let
S
be a random variable with such a law, i.e.

S\in\{\pm a,\pm b\}.

A short computation yields

(6.2)

\displaystyle Z(a,b,p;\beta)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{\beta S}\bigr]=p\cosh(\beta a)+(1-p)\cosh(\beta b),

(6.3)

\displaystyle N(a,b,p;\beta)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\bigl[S\,e^{\beta S}\bigr]=p\,a\,\sinh(\beta a)+(1-p)\,b\,\sinh(\beta b).

Therefore

(6.4)

\mathbb{E}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta}}[S]=\frac{N(a,b,p;\beta)}{Z(a,b,p;\beta)}.

Therefore, for fixed
V>0
and
\beta>0
,

(6.5)

\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{S}_{V^{2}}}\mathbb{E}^{\mu_{\beta}}[S]=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<a\leq b,\;p\in[0,1]\\
pa^{2}+(1-p)b^{2}=V^{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{N(a,b,p;\beta)}{Z(a,b,p;\beta)}.

This problem is still non-trivial to solve. In order to make progress, we now rely on the assumption
\beta V\leq 2
.

Proposition 6.4
.

The minimizer of the problem in (
6.5
) is given by the symmetric measure

\mu=\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{V}+\delta_{-V}).

In other words, the optimal measure is two-atomic with atoms at
\pm V
.

Proof.

Let
S
be concentrated on the four points
\{\pm a,\pm b\}
so that its variance is
V^{2}
and

F_{\beta}(a,b,p):={\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta}}[S]
is given by the fraction in (
6.4
). Define the function

k(x):=x\sinh(\beta x)-V\tanh(V\beta)\cosh(\beta x).

A simple calculation shows that

F_{\beta}(a,b,p)\geq V\tanh(V\beta)\iff{\mathcal{N}}(a,b,p):=pk(a)+(1-p)k(b)\geq 0.

Our goal will be to show that
\varphi(t):=k(\sqrt{t})
is convex on
[0,\infty)
, which will yield the claim. Differentiate
k
two times to find

\displaystyle k^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=(1-\beta V\tanh(\beta V))\sinh(\beta x)+\beta x\cosh(\beta x),

\displaystyle k^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\beta\cosh(\beta x)\bigl(2-\beta V\tanh(\beta V)\bigr)+\beta^{2}x\sinh(\beta x).

Since
\beta V<2
implies
\beta V\tanh(\beta V)<2
, we have

2-\beta V\tanh(\beta V)>0,

so
k^{\prime\prime}(x)>0
for all
x>0
, showing convexity.
Next, compute

\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{4t^{3/2}}\bigl(xk^{\prime\prime}(x)-k^{\prime}(x)\bigr),\qquad x=\sqrt{t}

and take
h(x):=xk^{\prime\prime}(x)-k^{\prime}(x)
. Differentiating once we find

h^{\prime}(x)=xk^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)
.
Since

k^{\prime\prime}(x)=\beta\cosh(\beta x)\bigl(2-\beta V\tanh(\beta V)\bigr)+\beta^{2}x\sinh(\beta x),

one checks

k^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)=\beta^{2}\sinh(\beta x)\bigl(3-\beta V\tanh(\beta V)\bigr)+\beta^{3}x\cosh(\beta x),

which is strictly positive for all
x>0
because

\beta V\tanh(\beta V)<2
implies

3-\beta V\tanh(\beta V)>1
.
Thus
h^{\prime}(x)>0
, and since
h(0)=0

h(x)>0\quad\text{for all }x>0.

Hence
\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)>0
for all
t>0
, i.e.
\varphi
is strictly convex.
Let
Y=S^{2}
, which means that

\mathbb{P}(Y=a^{2})=p,\qquad\mathbb{P}(Y=b^{2})=1-p.

By the variance constraint on
S
it holds that

\mathbb{E}[Y]=pa^{2}+(1-p)b^{2}=V^{2}
.
Note that

\mathcal{N}(a,b,p)=\mathbb{E}[k(\sqrt{Y})]=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(Y)].

Since
\varphi
is strictly convex, an application of Jensen’s inequality yields

\mathbb{E}[\varphi(Y)]\geq\varphi(\mathbb{E}[Y])=\varphi(V^{2})=k(V)=0.

Strictness holds unless
Y
is a.s. constant, i.e.
a^{2}=b^{2}=V^{2}
.
Therefore
\mathcal{N}(a,b,p)\geq 0
, with equality only in the two–point case.
Equivalently,

F_{\beta}(a,b,p)\geq V\tanh(\beta V),

with equality if and only if
a=b=V
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

Clearly

{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta,2}}[yz]\geq\inf\limits_{\theta\in{\mathcal{S}}_{V^{2}}}{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta,\theta}}[yz],

where

{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta,\theta}({\mathrm{d}}y{\mathrm{d}}z)\propto\exp\left(\beta yz\right)\theta({\mathrm{d}}y)\theta({\mathrm{d}}z).

By Proposition
6.4
we find that the infimum on the rhs is obtained by taking
\theta=(\delta_{V}+\delta_{-V})/2
. The claim follows by the identity (
6.4
).
∎
