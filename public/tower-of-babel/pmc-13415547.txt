An integrated approach to mining layout design for compound-hazards mines with coordinated rockburst and water inrush control
Authors: Jinzheng Bai, Linming Dou, Siyuan Gong, Jiancheng Jin, Haoze Li, Jingyi Mao, Zhiwen Liu, Fuhong Li, Guofeng Wang, Chao Li, Leigang Miao, Hongjun Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMines susceptible to compound hazards are often characterized by highly water-rich confined aquifers and hard, thick sandstone roofs. When the goaf reaches a certain scale, the instability of overlying strata is accompanied by a sharp increase in water inflow, posing dual risks of water inrush and rockburst. Integrating theoretical analysis, similarity simulation, and numerical modeling, this study explores rational mining layout schemes under the synergistic objective of rockburst prevention and water control. First, based on overburden movement characteristics and microseismic event distribution patterns, the stability of the high-position confined aquifer key stratum was assessed, revealing the risk of fracturing and instability in the water-rich stratum under current mining scales. Subsequently, reasonable ranges for key parameters of the mining layout were determined through theoretical calculations, and the stress field, energy evolution, and plastic zone development of different schemes were compared and analyzed. Finally, the Support Vector Machine (SVM) method was applied to quantitatively evaluate the synergistic effectiveness of rockburst prevention and water control across different schemes, leading to the identification of an optimal mining layout for compound hazards mines. This provides theoretical guidance for the coordinated prevention and control of rockburst and water inrush hazards.Keywords: Compound hazards, Rockburst prevention and control, Water inrush control, Mining layout scheme, Support vector machine (SVM)Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Hydrology, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

With increasing mining depth, the spatial distribution patterns of strata and the hydrogeological and engineering geological conditions become more complex. Hazards such as coal and gas outbursts, rockburst, water inrush, fires, coal spontaneous combustion, and high ground temperature are gradually shifting from single-mode disaster occurrences to a pattern where multiple hazards simultaneously incubate and trigger one another1–4. On one hand, these hazards interact and compound each other, resulting in more complex disaster initiation mechanisms in response to mining disturbances. These mechanisms often involve the coupling of multiple fields, including the stress field, vibration field, energy field, and seepage field5–8. On the other hand, these hazards are characterized by suddenness, rapid development, and high intensity, frequently causing severe damage to underground openings and significant casualties. This greatly increases the difficulty of monitoring, early warning, prevention, and hazard mitigation9–15. In recent years, a number of 10-million-tonne capacity mines have been planned and constructed in major coal production regions of Northwest China. In this area, coal seams are generally buried at great depths, typically exceeding 800 m and reaching over 1000 m at maximum. The overlying strata universally contain extremely thick and hard key layers, primarily composed of coarse-grained sandstone and medium-grained sandstone with uniaxial compressive strengths greater than 60 MPa. The total thickness of these layers ranges from 200 to 300 m, reaching up to approximately 600 m. The hydrogeological and engineering geological conditions are complex. The overall water yield of the aquifers ranges from moderate to strong, with specific capacity exceeding 1.0 L/(s·m) and transmissivity exceeding 100 m²/d. The maximum water pressure in confined aquifers approaches 7 MPa. Under such complex mining conditions characterized by great depth, extremely thick key layers, high confined water pressure, and high in-situ stress, water inflow and rockburst occurrences in multiple mine panels have been relatively severe. Particularly after the mined-out area expands, the movement of the overlying strata intensifies, its vertical influence range increases, and the destructive potential of compound hazards is amplified, posing a serious threat to workers’ lives and the safe, efficient production of the mines16–21. Therefore, it is necessary to design rational mining layout schemes for mines facing compound hazards, based on the synergistic goal of rockburst prevention and water control. This approach aims to mitigate hazards at their source and ensure safe production.

From the perspective of rockburst prevention, efforts should be made to avoid the formation of static load stress concentration within the excavation space as much as possible, while simultaneously minimizing the dynamic load disturbance generated during the mining process. From the perspective of water inrush control, the aim is to minimize the height of mining-induced overburden failure and prevent mining-induced fractures from connecting with confined aquifers, thereby reducing water inflow into the excavation space. Scholars have conducted extensive research on the design principles and optimization strategies for mine layout schemes based on the goals of rockburst prevention and water inflow control22,23.

Zhang et al. compared and analyzed the characteristic differences in the stress field, fracture field, and seepage field of coal-rock masses under three mining methods—pillarless mining, top-coal caving, and protective layer mining—by means of a series of coal-rock mining dynamics experiments. The findings contribute to the optimization of mining layouts24,25. Hamid et al. developed a geometric computational model for calculating the extent of the excavation damage zone above roadways influenced by longwall panels26. They also proposed a computational method for roadway stability analysis and the selection of optimal coal pillar widths. Wang et al. analyzed the surface subsidence patterns of longwall backfill mining under different mining heights and panel widths by employing FLAC3D numerical simulation and physical similarity modeling experiments27. Chen et al. conducted field monitoring of roadway support and stress changes in the roof and coal pillars during the mining of a 10-meter-thick coal seam28. They determined the vertical and horizontal influence zones induced by mining, thereby optimizing the width of the section pillars. Wang et al. conducted a numerical study on the dynamic mechanical state and rockburst risk of section pillars, revealing areas prone to pillar-type rockburst around longwall panels29. Yin et al. investigated the influence mechanisms of factors such as high in-situ stress, panel length, panel width, aquiclude mechanical properties, and groundwater infiltration on the water flow paths in the coal seam floor during mining30. This research can be used to predict the timing and location of potential water inrush events. Zhang et al. employed theoretical analysis to derive an overall criterion for the impact instability of the T-shaped structure in overburden strata backfilled with ultra-high water content material. They also applied limit equilibrium theory to establish an assessment criterion for water seepage in coal pillars31–34. Li et al. proposed a comprehensive method for controlling water inrush from a thick aquiclude floor. The method involves grouting to seal fractures in the Ordovician limestone roof strata, reducing the mining width and implementing roof pre-splitting for pressure relief to mitigate static and dynamic load disturbances, while employing confined water drainage and depressurization techniques to lower water pressure and cut off water sources. Ultimately, this achieves effective prevention and control of water inrush from thick aquiclude floors35–37.

Although existing research has conducted relatively comprehensive studies on the design principles and optimization strategies for mining layout schemes based on rockburst prevention and water control, the focus has predominantly been limited to specific individual working faces. A significant research gap remains in the design of regional mining schemes at the mine panel scale. Particularly, when the design requirements for rockburst prevention and water control conflict, the criteria for selecting key technical parameters in mining layout schemes remain unclear. Therefore, taking a kilometer-deep mine in the Ningzheng mining area of Gansu Province, China as the engineering background, this study employs an integrated methodology of theoretical analysis, similarity simulation, and numerical simulation. It investigates the reasonable ranges of key parameters for a compound mine layout scheme based on the synergistic goal of rockburst prevention and water control, with the ultimate aim of designing an optimal mining layout.

Engineering background

Engineering geological conditions

This study focuses on a newly constructed coal mine in the Ningzheng mining area of the Longdong Basin, Gansu Province. The mine primarily extracts Coal Seam 8, which has a burial depth ranging from 650 m to 1250 m and a variable thickness of 0 to 27 m, with an average thickness of 8.5 m.

The roof strata above the coal seam, from oldest to youngest, consist of the Yan’an Formation, Zhiluo Formation, Anding Formation, Yijun Formation, Luohe Formation, Huachi Formation, and the Quaternary System. A comprehensive stratigraphic column of the mine field is presented in Fig. 1. Coal Seam 8 and its floor exhibit a weak bursting tendency, while the roof shows no bursting tendency (GB/T 25217.2–2010). Both Coal Seam 8 and the first mining panel possess a moderate rockburst risk.

According to the current mining sequence, the first working face to be extracted in this panel is Working Face 1802, with Working Faces 1801 and 1809 designated as standby faces. The fully mechanized top-coal caving mining method is employed, and the goaf is managed using the total caving method.

The results indicate that the Luohe Formation, dominated by sandstone, possesses great thickness and high strength. It controls the movement of overlying strata during panel extraction and constitutes the high-position primary key stratum structure in the roof. On one hand, this structure will lead to an increase in static load stress within the stope. On the other hand, after coal seam extraction, the fracture of this high-position primary key stratum will inevitably induce the coordinated fracturing of multiple strata, including lower-position sub-key strata, releasing high-magnitude dynamic loads and posing a substantial rockburst risk to the mine.

Hydrogeology conditions

Based on the classification of the groundwater system within the Longdong Basin, along with characteristics such as stratigraphic distribution and hydraulic connections, the groundwater system in the mining area can be divided into three major subsystems from top to bottom: the Loess and Valley Phreatic Water System (Q), the Lower Cretaceous Zhidan Group Groundwater System (K1zh), and the Jurassic Groundwater System (J), as shown in Fig. 5.

Profile diagram of water-bearing rock formation in Ningzheng Mining Area.

Within the mine field, the water-bearing and aquiclude layers can be classified into four aquifers and four aquicludes based on their water yield property, types of water-bearing medium, and hydraulic characteristics. Among them, the sandstone pore-fissure aquifer of the Luohe Formation is the third aquifer in the mine field. The composite confined aquifer in the sandstones of the Middle Jurassic Zhiluo Formation and the upper-middle part of the Yan’an Formation (above the roof of Coal Seam 8) constitutes the fourth aquifer. The upper section of the Luohe Formation sandstone pore-fissure aquifer contains thick, coarse-grained sandstone with relatively low average porosity, providing favorable water storage space. It possesses abundant static storage, receives lateral runoff recharge from subsurface rivers, and has a specific capacity of 0.70 ~ 1.22 L/(s·m), a transmissivity of 158 ~ 297 m²/d, and a maximum water pressure of 7.0 MPa, indicating moderate to strong water yield. This aquifer has an average vertical distance of 110 m from Coal Seam 8. After coal seam extraction, it will become a major water-filling source, leading to increased water inflow in the stope and posing a certain risk of water inrush.

Status of compound disasters

Rockburst

As of now, Working Face 1802 has advanced to approximately 1000 m. To assess the impact of mining activities on overburden stability, the profile distribution of microseismic events with magnitudes above 3 recorded during the panel extraction is presented in Fig. 6.

Profile distribution of microseismic events during the extraction of working face 1802.

As shown in Fig. 6a, during the initial stage of extraction with a small mined-out area, overburden activity remains relatively stable. Microseismic events are primarily concentrated near the coal seam, while the number of events in the roof zone is limited. This indicates that the mining disturbance had not yet reached the level of the Luohe Formation at this stage. It can be seen from Fig. 6b,c that as the mined-out area further expands, the frequency of microseismic events increases significantly and progressively evolves towards higher-position strata in the roof. By this point, the zone of roof fracturing has approached the base of the Luohe Formation. As depicted in Fig. 6d, when the mined-out area continues to enlarge, the microseismic distribution develops in height and stabilizes within the middle-lower section of the extremely thick key stratum of the Luohe Formation. Mining-induced fractures gradually increase. Under the influence of the goaf behind the working face, the load on the extremely thick key stratum continuously rises while its stability gradually decreases, resulting in a certain amount of deformation and subsidence.

During the extraction of Working Face 1802, Survey Lines I-I and II-II were established along the strike and dip directions of the panel, respectively, to monitor overburden subsidence. The results are presented in Fig. 7. As shown, the maximum subsidence along the dip direction is approximately 24 mm, while along the strike direction it is about 73 mm. The corresponding maximum subsidence factor is only 0.006, which is significantly lower than the typical range of 0.4 ~ 0.6 for similar burial depths without the presence of a thick, hard stratum. This indicates that, under the specific geological conditions of Working Face 1802 where an extremely thick key stratum exists, the subsidence factor is markedly smaller, and the overburden has not fully subsided. Considering the currently limited extent of the mined-out area, there is a possibility of continued subsidence, or even sudden fracture, as mining activities proceed. Therefore, from the perspective of rockburst prevention, efforts should be made to minimize both the rate and the peak value of overburden fracture-induced subsidence, in order to avoid high-energy mine tremors triggered by the sudden and violent fracture of the extremely thick key stratum, which could induce rockburst.

Water inrush

Figure 8 illustrates the correlation between the total water inflow and the cumulative advance of Working Face 1802 from the start of extraction to the present. As shown, a strong positive correlation exists between water inflow and the advance distance. During the initial extraction stage, water inflow was relatively low, remaining around 50 m³/h. According to the mine water inflow records, only minor roof dripping was observed locally within the area corresponding to Support Units 70 to 80 at this initial stage. As extraction continued to approximately 400 m, water inflow increased significantly to about 120 m³/h, and the observed water outflow zone on site expanded to the area corresponding to Support Units 60 to 90. Subsequently, when the face advanced to around 600 m, the water inflow exhibited a step-like increase, rising to approximately 200 m³/h. As the face approached the 800 m mark, the water inflow generally stabilized. The water source was primarily from the goaf, with no dripping observed in front of or between the support units. Finally, when extraction reached about 1000 m, the water inflow increased sharply again, reaching a maximum of approximately 350 m³/h, representing the period of highest water inrush risk. Therefore, from the perspective of water hazard control, it is crucial to minimize the development height of the water-conducting fracture zone induced by mining activities. This is to avoid intersecting the lower section of the Luohe Formation, which has strong water yield capacity, thereby preventing a sharp increase in face water inflow and mitigating the risk of water inrush.

Relationship between water inflow and cumulative advance during the extraction.

In summary, the mine faces the compound risk of both rockburst and water inrush due to factors such as the extremely thick key stratum and the highly water-rich aquifer within the Luohe Formation, which severely impacts its safe and efficient production. Considering that the mine is still in its early production stage, there is an urgent need to reasonably adjust the spatiotemporal mining layout based on the synergistic goal of rockburst prevention and water control. This adjustment aims to mitigate the risks of rockburst and water inrush while avoiding any negative impact on production capacity.

For conventional mines prone to rockburst, the mining layout commonly employs a “large panel size” paired with “small section pillars,” implementing sequential extraction within each panel. Regarding panel dimensions, a single panel typically accommodates more than three longwall faces, each with a dip length generally exceeding 150 m. This configuration allows for the creation of a relatively large extraction space along the seam. The resulting mining-induced stress concentration becomes more distributed, with less mutual interference between concentrated stress zones, and facilitates energy release. Concerning the section pillars within a panel, a pillar width-to-height ratio (W/H) between 5 and 10 is unfavorable for preventing pillar-type rockburst. To improve resource recovery, mines typically employ small yield pillars, i.e., pillars with a width around 6 to 8 m or less.

However, the aforementioned “large panel + small pillar” scheme is unsuitable for the mine in question. On one hand, under large-panel conditions, the goaf undergoes large-scale, integral caving, triggering severe fracture and movement of the overlying strata. This leads to a significantly greater development height of the water-conducting fracture zone, which is detrimental to water control efforts. On the other hand, with small pillars (< 6–8 m), these yield pillars are prone to water leakage. Fractures in the overburden of adjacent goafs become interconnected more easily, allowing accumulated water from old goafs to flow into the active working face. This causes a sudden surge in water inflow, posing a water inrush threat.

Therefore, for compound hazard mines under similar complex conditions characterized by great depth, extremely thick key strata, and highly water-rich aquifers, a different layout strategy is required. To ensure the safe extraction of subsequent faces in the mining sequence and prevent major potential disasters, the mining layout should be adapted in line with the selected mining method and the capabilities of the face equipment. A strategy analogous to ‘small panel size’ paired with ‘large section pillars’ should be adopted. This approach aims to avoid both excessive stress concentration and the risk of water inrush.

Theoretical framework for mining layout design

During the preliminary design of the panel mining layout scheme, reasonable ranges for the basic geometric parameters of both working faces and panels should be determined based on the relevant theories of rockburst prevention and water control, respectively. Key design parameters primarily include: face width, critical panel dimensions, critical dimensions of protective pillars for the panel, etc. Based on these, the preliminary design of the panel mining layout scheme is conducted to provide sufficient data support for subsequent comparative evaluation and optimization of different schemes. Considering that the mine’s extraction activities will be concentrated within the first panel for a considerable period, the optimization design of the mining layout is focused on the currently delineated first panel. This approach aims to provide both a theoretical and an engineering basis for the scientific design of the “small panel with large pillars” model under similar conditions in the future.

Design of critical safe dimensions for the panel

Rockburst is a particular manifestation of mine pressure. The root cause of all ground pressure behaviors in a stope lies in the strata movement induced by mining, and the fracturing movement of the overlying strata is the primary reason for major mine disasters such as roof collapse, mine tremors, and rockburst. Therefore, regarding the panel extraction dimensions, the most unfavorable scenario—namely, the fracturing of the high-position, extremely thick sandstone key stratum that induces high-energy mine tremors—must be considered. After the successive extraction of working faces within a panel, the overburden above the goafs on both sides of the section pillars will collapse. Design of anti-rockburst safety dimensions for panel isolation pillars

Due to the action of lateral abutment pressure from the goaf, stress concentration will develop above the panel isolation pillar. Under high abutment pressure, the pillar may undergo deformation and failure, potentially leading to a pillar-type rockburst. Once the pillar fails, it loses its load-bearing capacity, triggering a chain reaction of movement in the overlying strata structure and consequently inducing new ground pressure manifestations. Therefore, for the panel isolation pillar, it is necessary to consider not only its static failure but also to satisfy the requirement for core zone stability. Preliminary design of the panel mining layout scheme

Based on the foregoing conclusions, it is established that the requirements for both rockburst prevention and water control can be simultaneously satisfied when the working face width falls within 200 ~ 300 m, the panel extraction dimension is less than 857 m, and the pillar size exceeds 147 m. In light of the current mining status of the first panel and employing an orthogonal design methodology, a total of 42 different mining layout schemes have been designed. These schemes comprise six working face widths (200 m, 220 m, 240 m, 260 m, 280 m, and 300 m) and seven panel isolation pillar widths (150 m, 175 m, 200 m, 225 m, 250 m, 275 m, and 300 m). While all these schemes meet the panel design parameter requirements derived from theoretical analysis, the method for determining the optimal design remains unclear. Therefore, a subsequent comparative evaluation of these distinct design schemes will be conducted. This evaluation will employ numerical and similarity simulation methods to construct a multidimensional assessment framework incorporating indicators such as stress distribution and the extent of the water-conducting fracture zone. Through this targeted comparison, the optimal panel mining layout scheme will be identified.

Mining-induced stress distribution under various mining layouts

Varying barrier pillar widths

Varying barrier pillar widths

To investigate the controlling mechanism of barrier pillar width on the evolution of the mining-induced stress field, a base condition of 600 m mining dimensions for the panels was established. A comparative analysis was conducted using the planar stress nephograms (Fig. 13) corresponding to different barrier pillar widths (150 m, 175 m, 200 m, 225 m, 250 m, 275 m, and 300 m). The internal relationship between pillar width and the degree, extent, and stability of stress concentration was analyzed from three perspectives: horizontal distribution, vertical transfer, and quantitative characteristics. This provides a theoretical basis at the stress-field level for the safe dimension design of barrier pillars in mining panels.

Mining-induced stress distribution in plain view for varying barrier pillar widths.

As shown in Fig. 13, the width of the panel isolation coal pillar determines the planar concentration pattern and intensity of vertical stress within the pillar region, as well as the depth of stress transmission into the roof. When the pillar width is relatively small (e.g., 150 m), a single through‑going high‑stress concentration zone forms in plan view, with a peak vertical stress approaching 65 MPa, exceeding three times the uniaxial compressive strength of the coal mass. In cross‑section, the high‑stress zone extends upward into the roof above the coal seam, creating a conical stress concentration zone that penetrates both the coal seam and the roof. Under such conditions, the lateral abutment stresses from the two adjacent goafs continuously accumulate within the pillar, leading to a “stress saturation” state. The pillar is unable to sustain the transmitted vertical stress, which can easily induce roof breakage and is extremely unfavorable for burst prevention. As the pillar width increases (e.g., from 150 m to less than 200 m), the single high‑stress zone in plan view gradually evolves into a dual‑core stress concentration pattern. The peak vertical stress decreases by about 17%, and the affected area shrinks; a clear stress buffer zone forms inside the pillar, with stress concentration confined to the pillar edges. In cross‑section, the stress concentration core gradually retreats into the coal seam, and the depth of stress influence on the roof significantly decreases, markedly improving overall stability. When the pillar width exceeds 200 m, the planar stress concentration pattern stabilizes; both the peak stress and the affected area remain nearly unchanged, indicating that the lateral abutment stress fields from the two goafs are completely independent without any superposition effect. In cross‑section, the high‑stress zone is fully confined within the coal seam, greatly reducing the risk of roof disturbance. Under such conditions, the regulating effect of pillar width on mining‑induced stress becomes significantly weakened.

This provides a theoretical basis at the stress-field level for the safe design of mining panel dimensions.

Stress distribution for varying mining panel dimensions.

As shown in Fig. 14a–c, as the mining panel dimensions increase from 540 m to 840 m, the high-stress zones formed on both sides of the goafs adjacent to the barrier pillar progressively expand and encroach towards the pillar’s central region, eventually extending into its core. This indicates that large-scale panel mining leads to the deep transfer of lateral abutment pressure into the interior of the pillar.

Consequently, the extensive goaf area resulting from increased mining dimensions intensifies stress disturbance to the roof strata, thereby elevating the risk of roof fracturing. This scenario is unfavorable for rockburst prevention.

Strain energy evolution for various mining layouts

The elastic strain energy density reflects the accumulation and release of energy during the mining process of a working face. Analyzing its aggregation status allows for the inference of rockburst risk during extraction. Within the surrounding rock system of the working face, elastic energy is continuously input and released as mining advances. The dominant factor in this process is the triaxial stress level, meaning that the degree of stress concentration induced by mining determines the extent of elastic energy accumulation.

Varying barrier pillar widths

Varying barrier pillar widths

To accurately assess the patterns of energy accumulation and release in surrounding rock under varying barrier pillar widths, this study utilized the built-in FISH programming language in FLAC3D. Based on the formula for elastic strain energy density, planar and cross-sectional energy distribution nephograms, as well as energy variation curves along the dip direction, were extracted for seven barrier pillar width schemes (150 m, 175 m, 200 m, 225 m, 250 m, 275 m, and 300 m). From three dimensions—the scope and intensity of energy aggregation, along with its vertical transfer characteristics—the research reveals the controlling mechanism of different barrier pillar widths on the evolution of elastic strain energy. This provides a quantitative basis for assessing rockburst risk in mining layout design.

As shown in Fig. 15, when the barrier pillar width is 150 m, a “continuous energy accumulation core” forms across the entire width of the pillar. This high-energy zone covers over 90% of the pillar’s width and extends approximately 30 m into the edges of the adjacent goafs on both sides. The peak elastic strain energy density reaches 3.0 × 10⁵ J/m3, the highest among all evaluated schemes. This indicates that a narrow barrier pillar cannot effectively dissipate the lateral energy from the goafs, leading to continuous superposition of elastic strain energy within the pillar. This condition establishes a substantial foundation for rockburst energy, which is highly unfavorable for prevention. As the pillar width gradually increases, the distribution characteristics of elastic strain energy stabilize. The high-energy zone remains confined to a range of 20–30 m from the outer edges of the barrier pillar on both sides. Once the barrier pillar width exceeds 225 m, the sensitivity of both the intensity and extent of energy accumulation to changes in pillar width decreases markedly, and the energy distribution enters a “stable interval.”

Elastic strain energy distribution in planar view for varying barrier pillar widths.

Figure 16 presents the cross-sectional distribution of strain energy under different barrier pillar widths, focusing on the vertical transfer and accumulation depth of energy. Its evolutionary pattern aligns closely with the vertical extension trend of the stress concentration core. Specifically: when the barrier pillar width is less than 175 m, the accumulation of elastic strain energy exhibits a “vertically penetrating” characteristic, with the high-value energy zone extending from the coal seam floor upward into the roof strata for a range of 15–20 m. When the barrier pillar width exceeds 225 m, both the accumulation depth and intensity of elastic strain energy in the vertical direction stabilize, minimizing disturbance to the surrounding rock. This scenario is favorable for rockburst prevention.

(2)Varying mining panel dimensions

Varying mining panel dimensions

To elucidate the controlling mechanism of mining panel dimensions on the evolution of elastic strain energy, and considering space constraints, a base condition of a 250 m wide barrier pillar was adopted. A comparative analysis was performed using the planar strain energy distribution nephograms (Fig. 17) corresponding to different mining panel dimensions (540 m, 600 m, 660 m, 720 m, 780 m, and 840 m). The analysis focused on three dimensions: the scope and intensity of elastic strain energy aggregation, along with its vertical transfer characteristics. This reveals the controlling mechanism of different panel dimensions on elastic strain energy evolution, thereby providing a quantitative basis for assessing rockburst risk in mining layout design.

Strain energy distribution for varying mining panel dimensions.

As shown in Fig. 17, when the barrier pillar width is 250 m, the distribution of elastic strain energy is uniform. The extent of the high-energy zone is further reduced and remains largely unchanged with variations in mining dimensions. When the mining panel dimensions were 540 m, the high-energy zone was confined to approximately 20 m from the edges on both sides of the pillar, with a peak energy of about 1.2 × 10⁶ J. The energy value in the central region of the barrier pillar remained stable between 0.6 ~ 0.8 × 10⁶ J. When the panel dimensions increased to 720 m, the extent of the high-energy zone did not expand, and the peak energy slightly rose to 1.4 × 10⁶ J. At the maximum panel dimensions of 840 m, the peak energy reached only 1.6 × 10⁶ J, and the area of the high-energy zone was essentially the same as that at 540 m. Overall, employing a 250 m wide barrier pillar results in the lowest intensity of energy accumulation and shows no pronounced energy concentration trend, which is favorable for rockburst prevention.

Fractured zone height development for various mining layouts

FLAC3D finite element numerical simulation software exhibits a high correlation in calculating and analyzing the distribution of plastic zones in the surrounding rock of goafs and the development height of water-conducting fracture zones. Therefore, studying the distribution of plastic zones and the fracture development in the overlying strata of the coal seam can serve as an equivalent assessment of the development of water-conducting fracture zones behind the working face. After the extraction of the working face, the overlying strata of the coal seam are divided, based on their degree of failure, into the following zones from bottom to top: the tensile failure zone, tensile fracture zone, shear failure zone, and undisturbed zone. The tensile failure zone corresponds to the caved zone, the tensile fracture zone corresponds to the fracture development zone, and the shear failure zone corresponds to the bed separation development zone.

Varying barrier pillar widths

Varying barrier pillar widths

Considering space constraints, Fig. 18 presents the distribution of roof damage plastic zones under different barrier pillar widths (150 m, 200 m, 250 m, and 300 m). This can be used to assess the height of fracture development, thereby determining the extent of the water-conducting fracture zone.

Fracture zone height for varying barrier pillar widths.

As shown in Fig. 18a, when the barrier pillar width is 150 m, the height of the water-conducting fracture zone is approximately 252 m, and the plastic zone within the pillar exhibits a significantly fully connected state. As shown in Fig. 18b–d, As the barrier pillar width increases, the height of the water-conducting fracture zone gradually decreases, and the phenomenon of a fully connected plastic zone gradually diminishes. Specifically, when the pillar width is 200 m, the fracture zone height is about 244 m, and the fully connected state of the plastic zone in the pillar area weakens further. At a pillar width of 250 m, the fracture zone height is approximately 240 m. When the pillar width reaches 300 m, the height decreases to 225 m. These results indicate that increasing the width of the barrier pillar helps to moderately reduce the height of the water-conducting fracture zone, which is favorable for water hazard prevention.

(2)Varying mining panel dimensions

Varying mining panel dimensions

Considering space constraints and based on a fixed barrier pillar width of 250 m, Fig. 19 presents the distribution of the roof plastic zone under different mining panel dimensions (540 m, 600 m, 660 m, 720 m, 780 m, and 840 m). This can be used to assess the height of fracture development, thereby determining the extent of the water-conducting fracture zone.

Fracture zone height for varying mining panel dimensions.

As shown in Fig. 19, with a fixed barrier pillar width of 250 m, the development height of the water-conducting fracture zone increases from 177 m (for a mining panel dimension of 540 m) to 258 m (for a dimension of 840 m). The fractures develop only locally without forming a pronounced connected network. Furthermore, with this 250 m pillar, as the mining panel dimensions increase, the distribution of the plastic zone within the barrier pillar becomes progressively more extensive until it becomes fully connected. Additionally, compared to scenarios with barrier pillar widths of 150 m and 200 m, the fully connected state of the plastic zone is the weakest under identical mining panel dimensions. The findings indicate that, for a given mining panel dimension, appropriately increasing the width of the barrier pillar can effectively enhance its water-resisting capacity. Conversely, for a fixed barrier pillar width, larger mining panel dimensions are less favorable for water hazard prevention. These conclusions provide theoretical support for further optimization of mining layout schemes.

SVM-based design of mining layout schemes

As previously established through numerical simulations, stress distribution patterns, energy evolution characteristics, and the development height of water-conducting fracture zones have been obtained for 42 distinct mining layout schemes. This section employs a method integrating Support Vector Regression (SVR) with Bayesian hyperparameter optimization to conduct the optimal design of mining layout schemes, aiming for the synergistic goal of rockburst prevention and water hazard control.

Support Vector Regression (SVR) is a specific application of Support Vector Machines (SVM) to regression analysis. Unlike conventional regression methods, SVR does not solely focus on minimizing the error between predicted and actual values. Instead, it emphasizes finding an optimal hyperplane in a high-dimensional space that can accommodate a predetermined margin of error. The core idea of SVR is to identify an optimal hyperplane within a high-dimensional feature space, such that most data points lie within an ε-insensitive tube around this plane, while simultaneously maximizing the width of this tube. SVR aims to minimize the norm ||w|| to maximize the margin, while allowing some data points to lie outside the margin zone by introducing slack variables to handle these violations. This method employs a data-driven modeling approach, grounded in the principle of structural risk minimization. It utilizes kernel functions to map low-dimensional nonlinear problems into high-dimensional linear space, thereby reducing the strong dependence on data volume typical of traditional regression models (e.g., polynomial regression). This enables quantitative analysis of complex geomechanical relationships and provides a scientific basis for the design of coal mine panel layout schemes.

As shown in Fig. 20, based on the fitting curve derived from Support Vector Regression, the influence of mining panel dimensions and barrier pillar width on the development height of the plastic zone, as well as on stress and energy, is obtained. From Sect. 2.2, it is known that, considering the stronger water-richness of the upper section of the Luohe Formation at this level, it can be concluded that a higher water hazard threat exists when the water-conducting fracture zone develops into the middle-upper section of this formation. This critical distance is the sum of the minimum distance from the Luohe Formation to the #8 coal seam and half of the minimum thickness of the Luohe Formation, namely 226 m. When the development height of the plastic zone is at or below 226 m, the requirement for water hazard control is satisfied.

Multi-parameter evaluation index for dual-hazard prevention.

Under the premise of ensuring safe production, an excessively large barrier pillar design would lead to a certain degree of coal resource wastage. Therefore, by integrating production economics and safety considerations, the minimum barrier pillar width obtained below the 226 m contour line of plastic zone development height is approximately 185 m. At this width, the corresponding maximum mining panel dimension is 755 m. This scheme exhibits a relatively low degree of stress concentration, weak energy accumulation, and high pillar safety. It can, therefore, be adopted as the final mining layout scheme determined based on the requirements for both rockburst prevention and water hazard control.

Universal methodology for mining layout design in compound-hazard mines

In summary, a universal design methodology for mining layouts in compound-hazard mines characterized by thick hard roofs and highly water-rich confined aquifers can be further distilled. This methodology involves four steps, which are summarized as follows: (1) identifying key strata and water-rich layers and characterizing their coupled failure‑water hazard; (2) theoretically calculating safe panel dimensions and barrier pillar widths based on dual objectives of rockburst prevention and water inrush control; (3) performing multi‑scheme numerical simulations to evaluate stress, energy, and failure fields against quantified control targets; and (4) applying support vector regression (SVR) to build surrogate models and identify optimal parameters that balance resource recovery and production efficiency under the two constraints. This methodology provides transferable guidance for synergistic source‑control of rockburst and water inrush hazards under similar geological conditions. Conclusions

This study, based on the geological background of a newly constructed mine with a highly water-rich, thick, and hard key stratum roof, employed a multi-method approach integrating theoretical analysis, numerical simulation, and machine learning algorithms. The research designed the panel mining layout scheme to meet the synergistic requirements of rockburst prevention and water hazard control, analyzing from multiple perspectives including stress, energy, and the development characteristics of the water-conducting fracture zone. The main conclusions are as follows:

An assessment of the stability of the high-position pressurized water-bearing key stratum was conducted based on strata movement patterns and the spatial distribution characteristics of microseismic activity. The assessment indicates a potential risk of fracture and instability in the high-position water-rich stratum under the current mining dimensions.

An assessment of the stability of the high-position pressurized water-bearing key stratum was conducted based on strata movement patterns and the spatial distribution characteristics of microseismic activity. The assessment indicates a potential risk of fracture and instability in the high-position water-rich stratum under the current mining dimensions.

Using theoretical analysis grounded in the principles of rockburst prevention and water hazard control respectively, the reasonable value ranges for the fundamental geometric parameters of the panel layout design were determined. These parameters include working face width, the critical safe dimension of mining panels, and the rockburst-prevention safety size of barrier pillars. Based on these ranges, 42 preliminary mining layout schemes were designed.

Based on these ranges, 42 preliminary mining layout schemes were designed.
