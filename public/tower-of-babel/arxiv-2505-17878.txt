Introduction and Main Results

Throughout this paper, we denote the set of all holomorphic functions on a domain
D\subseteq\mathbb{C}

\mathcal{H}(D)

\mathcal{M}(D)

Let
D\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be a domain. A family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(D)
is said to be

A family

\{f|_{U}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,f\in\mathcal{F}\}

Definition A

For our final results, we will regard

Lemma 1.3

Let

be a non-constant, meromorphic function and

. Then

\displaystyle S_{k}(f)={\sum}_{\mathclap{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})\,\in\,\Lambda}}\,\,\,\,\,\,\Bigg(\frac{-k\cdot k!}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}(-k\cdot j!)^{n_{j}}\cdot n_{j}!}\cdot\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}\Bigg(\bigg(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\bigg)^{(j-1)}\Bigg)^{n_{j}}\Bigg),

\displaystyle S_{k}(f)=\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k^{k-1}}\cdot g^{k}+g^{(k-1)}+P[g]

This form is reminiscent of the well known condition
{af^{n}(z)+f^{(m)}(z)\neq b}

Theorem 1.4

\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D})
be a family of locally univalent functions,

and
{b\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{D})}
be a meromorphic function, such that every

satisfies

\displaystyle S_{k}(f)(z)\neq b(z)\qquad\text{for all}\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

(11)

Then
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}/\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\coloneq\{f^{\prime\prime}/f^{\prime}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,f\in\mathcal{F}\}
is a normal family.

Note that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}/\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
, unlike
\mathcal{H}_{k,M}^{f^{\prime\prime}/f^{\prime}}

\mathcal{F}
does not need to be quasi-normal, as demonstrated by the family
\{z\mapsto e^{nz}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

However, if - in addition to (
11

\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}/\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is pointwise bounded in a single point, then it follows that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}/\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is locally uniformly bounded. Now, using formula (

S_{k}(\mathcal{F})\coloneq\{S_{k}(f)\,\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,f\in\mathcal{F}\}
is locally uniformly bounded as well. This, in turn, allows us to apply Theorem
1.1
to
\mathcal{F}
. Thus, we can regard this as a “self-improving result”, where we require that:

•

S_{k}(\mathcal{F})
omits a function.

•

\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}/\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is bounded in a single point.

and obtain that:

•

S_{k}(\mathcal{F})
is locally uniformly bounded.

•

\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}/\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is locally uniformly bounded.

•

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}/\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
are normal, while
\mathcal{F}
is quasi-normal.

We should note that Theorem
1.4
can not be extended to families of meromorphic functions. To see this, consider
f_{n}(z)\coloneq((2z)^{n}-1)^{-1}
for

and

\displaystyle f_{n}^{\prime}(z)=-\frac{n\,2^{n}\,z^{n-1}}{\big((2z)^{n}-1\big)^{2}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{n}^{\prime\prime}(z)=\frac{n\,2^{n}\,z^{n-2}\big(n-1+(n+1)(2z)^{n}\big)}{\big((2z)^{n}-1\big)^{3}}.

(12)

Clearly, each

has a pole of order

(f_{n}^{\prime\prime}/f_{n}^{\prime})_{n}

On the other hand, we can use the fact that the classical Schwarzian derivative is invariant under Möbius transformations, and calculate

\displaystyle S_{2}(f_{n})(z)=S_{2}(z^{n})=\frac{1-n^{2}}{2\,z^{2}}.

(13)

Thus,
\big(S_{k}(f_{n})\big)_{n\geq 2}

Still, there is a corresponding value distribution result for entire functions. Similarly to Theorem

Corollary 1.5

Let

\displaystyle S_{k}(f)(z)\neq 0\quad\text{ and }\quad S_{k}(f)(z)\neq\infty\qquad\text{for all }z\in\mathbb{C}.

\displaystyle S_{2}(f)(z)=-\frac{e^{2cz}}{2}-\frac{c^{2}}{2},

Likewise, Corollary

{f\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C})}

First, we show that non-constant functions with an analytic generalized Schwarzian derivative have non-vanishing derivatives.

Proposition 2.1

If

is non-constant and

has a zero in

has a

-fold pole in

Proof.

\displaystyle S_{k,n}(f)(z)=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{h(z)}{(z-z_0)^{k-1}}$}}\quad\text{for some analytic }h\text{ in a neighborhood }U\text{ of }z_{0}.

\displaystyle S_{k+1,n}(f)(z)=\frac{1}{(z-z_{0})^{k}}\bigg(\Big(1-k-\frac{m}{n}\Big)h(z_{0})+\ldots\bigg)\qquad\text{for }z\in U,

f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D})
are linearly independent solutions of the differential equation
y^{\prime\prime}+p_{0}\cdot y=0
, then
{f\coloneq f_{1}/f_{2}}
satisfies
S_{2}(f)=p_{0}/2
(cf.
[
15
, Theorem 6.1]
). As a consequence, any linear combination
g=c_{1}f_{1}+c_{2}f_{2}
vanishes at a point
z_{0}
if and only if
{f(z_{0})=-c_{2}/c_{1}}
. Therefore,
f
attains some value
n
times if and only if there exists a non-trivial solution of
y^{\prime\prime}+p_{0}\cdot y=0
with
n
zeros.

This observation is due to Z. Nehari and his paper on the Schwarzian derivative and univalence (see
[
19
, p. 546]
). Since then, numerous results provided alternative criteria for disconjugacy. One such result is the following theorem. However, with minor modifications, Theorem
F
and Lemma
2.2
could also be derived from
[
10
, Theorem 2]
or
[
18
, p. 328]
.

Theorem F

(
[
14
, p. 723]
)

Let
D\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be a convex domain with
\delta\coloneq\text{diam}(D)<\infty
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
p_{0}\in\mathcal{H}(D)
be a holomorphic function with

\displaystyle|p_{0}(z)|<\frac{k!}{\delta^{k}}\qquad\text{ for all }\qquad z\in D.

(18)

Then the differential equation
y^{(k)}(z)+p_{0}(z)y(z)=0
is disconjugate in
D
.

Based on this result, we can show the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
M\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
, such that all
f\in\mathcal{M}_{k,M}
and all
f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{k,M}^{\prime}
have at most
N
poles (CM).

Proof.

For
f\in\mathcal{M}_{k,M}
, it suffices to show that
f^{\prime}
has a limited number of poles.
The case
M=0
was already established in Theorem
[
4
, Lemma 4]
, so we assume
M>0
. Due to Theorem
B
, we can write
f^{\prime}=1/h^{k}
, where
h\not\equiv 0
is a solution of the differential equation

\displaystyle y^{(k)}(z)+\frac{S_{k}(f)(z)}{k}\cdot y(z)=0.

(19)

Now, we choose
\delta>0
with
\delta<\Big(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{k\cdot k!}{M}$}}\Big)^{1/k}
. This implies
{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\Vert S_k(f)\Vert_\infty}{k}$}}\leq\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{M}{k}$}}<\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{k!}{\delta^k}$}}}
,
so Theorem
F
shows that (
19
) is disconjugate in every convex set
C_{\delta}\subseteq\mathbb{D}
with diameter
\delta
. Consequentially,
h
can have at most
k-1
zeros in
C_{\delta}
. By covering the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
with
\tilde{N}
of such convex sets, we obtain the bound
N\coloneq\tilde{N}(k-1)
for the number of zeros of
h
(CM).
∎

For the reader’s convenience, we also restate the main results of
[
7
]
with modified notation, to prevent conflicts with the notation used in this paper.

Theorem G

(
[
7
, Theorem 3]
)

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D})
,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{1\}
. Let
a,b,a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{D})
with
a\not\equiv 0
, and suppose that all poles of
a
have multiplicity at most
k-1
.

Consider a differential polynomial of the form

\displaystyle P[u]=\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{N}a_{\mu}\cdot\prod\limits_{j=1}^{s_{\mu}}u^{(\omega_{\mu,j})},

(20)

where
s_{\mu},\omega_{\mu,j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
satisfy the inequality

\displaystyle(k-1)\cdot\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s_{\mu}}\omega_{\mu,j}+\ell\cdot s_{\mu}\leq\ell\cdot k

(5)

for all
\mu=1,\ldots,N
, where equality can only hold if
2\leq s_{\mu}\leq k-1
.

Suppose that for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
and every
z\in\mathbb{D}
, the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle a(z)\cdot f^{k}(z)+f^{(\ell)}(z)+P[f](z)\neq b(z).

(6)

Then the family
\mathcal{F}
is normal.

Theorem H

(
[
7
, Theorem 4]
)

Let
f\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{C})
,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{1\}
. Let
a,a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\in\mathbb{C}
with
a\neq 0
and consider a differential polynomial

\displaystyle P[u]=\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{N}a_{\mu}\cdot\prod\limits_{j=1}^{s_{\mu}}u^{(\omega_{\mu,j})},

(7)

where
2\leq s_{\mu}\leq k-1
and
\,\sum_{j=1}^{s_{\mu}}\omega_{\mu,j}\neq 0
holds for all
\mu=1,\ldots,N
.

If the function
a\cdot f^{k}+f^{(\ell)}+P[f]
does not vanish, then
f
must be constant.

We conclude this section by proving the claim stated at the end of Section
1
.

Proposition 2.3

There exists
f\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C})
such that
f
is not of the form
f(z)=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{a e^{\alpha z} +b}{ce^{\alpha z} +d}$}}
for any choice of
a,b,c,d,\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, while
S_{2}(f)
omits the values
0
and
\infty
.

Proof.

We consider the Bessel functions defined by (cf.
[
1
, 9.1.12 and 9.1.13]
)

\displaystyle J_{0}(z)

\displaystyle\coloneq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(k!)^{2}}\Big(\frac{z}{2}\Big)^{2k}\qquad\qquad\text{and}

(8)

\displaystyle Y_{0}(z)

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{2}{\pi}\big(\log\frac{z}{2}+\gamma\big)J_{0}(z)+\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}H_{k}}{(k!)^{2}}\Big(\frac{z}{2}\Big)^{2k},

(9)

where
H_{k}
denotes the
k
-th harmonic number and
\gamma
is Euler’s constant. These are linearly independent solutions of the Bessel differential equation (cf.
[
1
, 9.1.1]
)

\displaystyle z^{2}y^{\prime\prime}(z)+zy^{\prime}(z)+z^{2}y(z)=0.

(10)

Next, we define

\displaystyle f_{1}(z)\coloneq J_{0}(e^{z/2})\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{2}(z)\coloneq Y_{0}(e^{z/2}).

(11)

Due to the logarithmic singularity of
Y_{0}
, we initially restrict our consideration of
f_{2}
to the horizontal strips

\displaystyle S_{n}\coloneq\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,(4n-2)\pi<\text{Im}(z)<(4n+2)\pi\}.

(12)

Within each
S_{n}
, a direct computation yields

\displaystyle f_{2}^{\prime\prime}(z)+\frac{e^{z}}{4}\,f_{2}(z)=\frac{e^{z}}{4}\,Y_{0}^{\prime\prime}(e^{z/2})+\frac{e^{z/2}}{4}\,Y_{0}^{\prime}(e^{z/2})+\frac{e^{z}}{4}\,Y_{0}(e^{z/2})=0,

(13)

which implies that
f_{2}
satisfies a second-order linear differential equation with entire coefficients. Hence,
f_{2}
is analytically extensible to an entire function (
cf.
[
12
, Satz 3.2]
).
Similarly,
f_{1}
satisfies

\displaystyle f_{1}^{\prime\prime}(z)+\frac{e^{z}}{4}\,f_{1}(z)=0.

(14)

Now, a classical result about the Schwarzian derivative (see
[
15
, Theorem 6.1]
) implies that the function
f\coloneq f_{1}/f_{2}
fulfills
S_{2}(f)(z)=e^{z}/2
. In particular,
S_{2}(f)
omits the values
0
and
\infty
.

Next, we suppose that
f(z)=\frac{ae^{\alpha z}+b}{ce^{\alpha z}+d}
for some choice of
a,b,c,d,\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
. Then the poles and zeros of
f
must be periodic. However, let
j_{0,n}
and
y_{0,n}
denote the
n
-th positive real zero of
J_{0}
and
Y_{0}
respectively. Then asymptotically (cf.
[
1
, 9.5.12]
)

\displaystyle j_{0,n}\sim\Big(n-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\pi\qquad\text{and}\qquad y_{0,n}\sim\Big(n-\frac{3}{4}\Big)\pi.

(15)

Thus, the positive real zeros of
J_{0}
and
Y_{0}
are almost equidistant, which shows that the zeros and poles of
f
are not periodic. Hence,
f
can not be expressed as a composition of a Möbius transformation and an exponential function.
∎

3.
Proofs of the Main Results

Throughout this section, we consider
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
M\in\mathbb{R}
fixed.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

From Theorem
B
we know that the derivative of every
f\in\mathcal{H}_{k,M}
can be written as
f^{\prime}=1/h^{k}
for some zero-free function
{h\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D})}
. Additionally we have
{S_{k}(f)=-k\,h^{(k)}/h}
. Therefore, the family
{\{h^{(k)}/h\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D})\,\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,f^{\prime}=1/h^{k}\text{ for some }f\in\mathcal{H}_{k,M}\}}
is locally uniformly bounded. Applying Theorem
C
shows that
{\{h^{\prime}/h\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D})\,\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,f^{\prime}=1/h^{k}\text{ for some }f\in\mathcal{H}_{k,M}\}}
is locally uniformly bounded as well. Since
f^{\prime\prime}/f^{\prime}=-k\,h^{\prime}/h
, it follows that the family
\mathcal{H}_{k,M}^{f^{\prime\prime}/f^{\prime}}
is locally uniformly bounded.

Next, we consider a sequence
(f_{n})_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{k,M}
. Because
(f_{n}^{\prime\prime}/f_{n}^{\prime})_{n}
is locally uniformly bounded and due to Proposition
2.1
, we are able to apply Lemma
D
to a subsequence of
(f_{n})_{n}
with
E=\emptyset
. This implies that
\mathcal{H}_{k,M}
is quasi-normal, that
\mathcal{H}_{k,M}^{f^{\prime}/f}
is normal, and that no sequence in
\mathcal{H}_{k,M}^{f^{\prime}/f}
converges to
\infty
.

For the final claim, we use the inequality
1+x<2\,(1+x^{2})
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
to obtain

\displaystyle(f^{\prime})^{\#}(z)=\frac{|f^{\prime\prime}(z)|}{1+|f^{\prime}(z)|^{2}}<\frac{2\,|f^{\prime\prime}(z)|}{1+|f^{\prime}(z)|}<2\,\bigg|\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}\bigg|

(16)

for all
f\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{D})
and
z\in\mathbb{D}
. Since
\mathcal{H}_{k,M}^{f^{\prime\prime}/f^{\prime}}
is locally uniformly bounded, it follows that the spherical derivatives of the functions in
\mathcal{H}_{k,M}^{\prime}
are locally uniformly bounded. Now, Marty’s theorem implies that
\mathcal{H}_{k,M}^{\prime}
is normal.

\square

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We consider a sequence
(f_{n})_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{k,M}
. By Lemma
2.2
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
, such that each
f_{n}
has at most
N
poles. Therefore, we can find a subsequence
(f_{n_{k}})_{n_{k}}
and a set
E\subseteq\mathbb{D}
consisting of at most
N
points, such that for every
z\in\mathbb{D}\backslash E
there exists a neighborhood
U
of
z
in which almost all
f_{n}
are analytic. Applying Theorem
1.1
locally on
\mathbb{D}\backslash E
implies that there is a subsequence
(f_{n_{\ell}}^{\prime\prime}/f_{n_{\ell}}^{\prime})_{n_{\ell}}\subseteq(f_{n_{k}}^{\prime\prime}/f_{n_{k}}^{\prime})_{n_{k}}
that converges locally uniformly on
\mathbb{D}\backslash E
to some
F\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D}\backslash E)
. Since
E
has no accumulation point, we conclude that
\mathcal{M}_{k,M}^{f^{\prime\prime}/f^{\prime}}
is quasi-normal and that no sequence in
\mathcal{M}_{k,M}^{f^{\prime\prime}/f^{\prime}}
converges to
\infty
.

Analogously to the proof of Theorem
1.1
, applying Lemma
D
to
(f_{n_{\ell}})_{n_{\ell}}
shows that both
\mathcal{M}_{k,M}
and
\mathcal{M}_{k,M}^{f^{\prime}/f}
are quasi-normal, and that no subsequence of
(f_{n_{k}}^{\prime}/f_{n_{k}})_{n_{k}}
converges to
\infty
.

Finally, the convergence of
(f_{n_{\ell}}^{\prime\prime}/f_{n_{\ell}}^{\prime})_{n_{\ell}}
on
\mathbb{D}\backslash E
to
F\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D}\backslash E)
together with inequality (
16
) show that
((f_{n_{\ell}}^{\prime})^{\#})_{n_{\ell}}
is locally uniformly bounded on
\mathbb{D}\backslash E
. Therefore,
\mathcal{M}_{k,M}^{\prime}
is quasi-normal by Marty’s theorem.

\square

Proof of Lemma
1.3
.

Since
f
is non-constant, there exists a neighborhood
U\subseteq\mathbb{D}
such that
{S_{k}(f)|_{U}\in\mathcal{H}(U)}
. By Theorem
B
, there exists a zero-free
{h\in\mathcal{H}(U)}
with
f^{\prime}=1/h^{k}
on
U
. Furthermore, we can find a logarithm
L\in\mathcal{H}(U)
of
h
, i.e.
h=\text{e}^{L}
. Using Faà di Bruno’s formula (cf.
[
21
, Chapter 2.8]
), we have that

\displaystyle h^{(k)}=\big(\text{e}^{L}\big)^{(k)}={\sum}_{\mathclap{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})\,\in\,\Lambda}}\,\,\,\,\,\,\Bigg(\frac{k!}{n_{1}!\cdot\ldots\cdot n_{k}!}\,e^{L(z)}\,\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}\bigg(\frac{L^{(j)}(z)}{j!}\bigg)^{n_{j}}\Bigg),

(17)

where
\Lambda
is the set of all natural tuples
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})
with
\sum_{j=1}^{k}j\cdot n_{j}=k
. Now, using Theorem
B
and
L^{\prime}=h^{\prime}/h=-f^{\prime\prime}/(k\cdot f^{\prime\prime})
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{k}(f)=-k\,\frac{h^{(k)}}{h}&={\sum}_{\mathclap{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})\,\in\,\Lambda}}\,\,\,\,\,\,\Bigg(\frac{-k\cdot k!}{n_{1}!\cdot\ldots\cdot n_{k}!}\,\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}\bigg(\frac{L^{(j)}(z)}{j!}\bigg)^{n_{j}}\Bigg)\\
&={\sum}_{\mathclap{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})\,\in\,\Lambda}}\,\,\,\,\,\,\Bigg(\frac{-k\cdot k!}{n_{1}!\cdot\ldots\cdot n_{k}!}\,\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}\Bigg(\frac{1}{-k\cdot j!}\cdot\bigg(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\bigg)^{(j-1)}\Bigg)^{n_{j}}\Bigg)\\
&={\sum}_{\mathclap{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})\,\in\,\Lambda}}\,\,\,\,\,\,\Bigg(\frac{-k\cdot k!}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}\big((-k)\cdot(j!)\big)^{n_{j}}\cdot n_{j}!}\,\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}\Bigg(\bigg(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\bigg)^{(j-1)}\Bigg)^{n_{j}}\Bigg).\end{split}

(18)

Finally, by the identity theorem, this equality extends from
U
to
\mathbb{D}
.

\square

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
\Lambda
be defined as in Lemma
1.3
, and set
{\tilde{\Lambda}\coloneq\Lambda\backslash\{(k,0,\ldots,0),(0,\ldots,0,1)\}}
. Next, we fix an enumeration
\mu
of
\tilde{\Lambda}
, denote
N\coloneq|\tilde{\Lambda}|
and define for each multi-index
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})\in\tilde{\Lambda}
that

\displaystyle a_{\mu^{-1}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{-k\cdot k!}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}\big((-k)\cdot(j!)\big)^{n_{j}}\cdot n_{j}!}.

(19)

In addition, for
\nu=\mu^{-1}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})
we set:

\displaystyle s_{\nu}

\displaystyle\coloneq\sum_{r=1}^{k}n_{r}

and for

\displaystyle j=1,\ldots,s_{\nu},\qquad\text{we define}

(20)

\displaystyle\omega_{\nu,j}

\displaystyle\coloneq r-1

whenever

\displaystyle n_{1}+\ldots,n_{r-1}<j\leq n_{1}+\ldots+n_{r}.

(21)

Using Lemma
1.3
, we get for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
that

\displaystyle S_{k}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k^{k-1}}\,\bigg(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\bigg)^{k}+\bigg(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\bigg)^{(k-1)}\!+\,\,\,{\sum}_{\mathclap{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})\,\in\,\tilde{\Lambda}}}\,\,a_{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}\,\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}\Bigg(\bigg(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\bigg)^{(j-1)}\Bigg)^{n_{j}}

(22)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k^{k-1}}\,\bigg(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\bigg)^{k}+\bigg(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\bigg)^{(k-1)}+\underbrace{\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}a_{\mu}\,\prod\limits_{j=1}^{s_{\mu}}\bigg(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\bigg)^{(\omega_{\mu,j})}}_{\eqcolon P[f^{\prime\prime}/f^{\prime}]}.

(23)

Now, we want to apply Theorem
G
with
\ell\coloneq k-1
. Note that
f^{\prime\prime}/f^{\prime}
is analytic, since
f
is locally univalent. Furthermore, condition (
5
) holds for
\mu=1,\ldots,N
, because
\sum_{r=1}^{k}n_{r}\cdot r=k
for
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})\in\tilde{\Lambda}
and therefore we get

\displaystyle(k-1)\cdot\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s_{\mu}}\omega_{\mu,j}+\ell\cdot s_{\mu}=(k-1)\sum\limits_{r=1}^{k}n_{r}\,(r-1)+(k-1)\,\sum_{r=1}^{k}n_{r}=\ell\cdot k

(24)

independently of
\mu
.
Additionally, we have
2\leq s_{\mu}\leq k-1
for all
\mu=1,\ldots,N
, because we removed the multi-indices
(k,0,\ldots,0)
and
(0,\ldots,0,1)
from
\tilde{\Lambda}
. Hence, Theorem
G
applies and we conclude that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}/\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is a normal family.

\square

Proof of Corollary
1.5
.

We reuse the notation used in the proof of Theorem
1.4
and write
S_{k}(f)
as in equation (
23
). By Proposition
2.1
,
f^{\prime}
does not vanish and therefore
f^{\prime\prime}/f^{\prime}
is an entire function. Since
2\leq s_{\mu}\leq k-1
and
\,\sum_{j=1}^{s_{\mu}}\omega_{\mu,j}\neq 0
holds for all
\mu=1,\ldots,N
, we are able to apply Theorem
H
to
f^{\prime\prime}/f^{\prime}
. Thus,
f^{\prime\prime}/f^{\prime}\equiv b
for some constant
b\in\mathbb{C}
. Notice that
b\neq 0
, since
S_{k}(f)
omits the value
0
. Therefore,
f
must be of the form
f(z)=ae^{bz}+c
for some
a,b,c\in\mathbb{C}
with
a,b\neq 0
.

\square
