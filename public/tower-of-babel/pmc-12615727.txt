Hemodynamic determinants of postoperative neurocognitive impairment using Random Forest analysis and partial dependence plots
Authors: Faruk Sanberk Kiziltas, Ozhan Ozkan, Fatih Toptan, Ibrahim Kara, Zuhal Dogan, Kadir Gokmen, Esra Gundogdu Eryilmaz, Ali Fuat Erdem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effect of hemodynamic data during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) on neurocognitive impairment in patients undergoing coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery using machine learning algorithms. Twenty-eight CABG patients were included in the study. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure, pulse (PLS), and SpO2 were recorded at 2.4-second intervals, yielding 730,870 data points (450,548 after artefact removal). Data were analyzed using Random Forest classification and Partial Dependence Plots (PDPs) to evaluate the collective and individual effects of parameters on neurocognitive outcomes. Separate analyses were conducted for the surgery period, bypass, and cross-clamp periods. Cognitive impairment was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), with a decrease of ≥ 2 points considered indicative of impairment. The highest classification accuracy (86%) was achieved during the bypass period when all parameters were analyzed together. Parameter importance varied by surgical phase: PLS was most significant during the surgery period and bypass periods. PDPs revealed specific optimal parameter ranges for each surgical phase. This study highlights the complex and dynamic role of hemodynamic parameters in preserving neurocognitive function during CABG surgery. Findings suggest that monitoring specific combinations of parameters during different surgical phases could help reduce neurocognitive risks. The significant importance of PLS data and its relationship with mean arterial pressure (MAP) values indicates that pulsatile flow may play a crucial role in neurocognitive protection. Future research should validate these findings in larger cohorts and develop phase-specific monitoring strategies for clinical implementation.Keywords: Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), Coronary artery bypass graft surgery (CABG), Montreal cognitive assessment (MoCA) test, Neurocognitive, Partial dependence plot (PDP), Random ForestSubject terms: Cardiology, Engineering

Introduction

In Cardiopulmonary Bypass (CPB) operations, the function of the heart is bypassed and circulation is provided through a heart-lung machine.

Although advancements in surgical and anesthesia techniques and medical device technologies have increased the success of CPB operations, studies have revealed that the incidence of neurocognitive impairment after complex operations like CPB is very high (24% − 79%)1–10.

The etiology of neurocognitive impairment after CPB operations remains unresolved, with studies indicating it is multifactorial10,11.

Most research on the causes of post-CPB neurocognitive impairment has focused on Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP). However, no consensus has been established regarding the optimal level at which MAP should be maintained11–15.

The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) test is one of many neuropsychological tests used to detect neurocognitive impairment. MoCA evaluates various cognitive domains including memory, naming, delayed recall, and visual-spatial abilities, with a maximum possible score of 3016,17.

Machine learning approaches like Random Forest have gained significant adoption in healthcare applications due to their capacity to manage complex datasets and their resilience against overfitting. These algorithms have demonstrated particular value in biomedical prediction tasks where multiple variables interact in non-linear ways18,19. This makes Random Forest especially suitable for analyzing how various intraoperative hemodynamic measurements might collectively impact post-surgical cognitive outcomes.

To address the interpretability challenges inherent in complex machine learning models, visualization techniques such as Partial Dependence Plots offer valuable insights into the relationships between predictors and outcomes20,21. By illustrating how changes in specific hemodynamic variables affect predicted neurocognitive status while controlling for other factors, these plots help translate abstract algorithmic predictions into clinically meaningful information that can inform medical decision-making.

In this research, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of hemodynamic parameters in patients undergoing CABG surgeries. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on isolated MAP values, our novel approach examines the combined effects of Systolic-Diastolic Pressure, Pulse (PLS), and SpO2 values on neurocognitive outcomes11–15. Using Random Forest algorithms combined with Partial Dependence Plots (PDPs), we investigated the complex interactions between these hemodynamic parameters across distinct surgical phases - overall surgery duration, bypass period, and cross-clamp period. This multifaceted analysis enabled us to identify optimal parameter combinations and ranges specific to each surgical phase, providing insights into how these hemodynamic factors collectively influence neurocognitive outcomes following CABG procedures.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Sakarya University Faculty of Medicine on February 12, 2023, with approval number 220,294 and conducted with 28 patients. All patients undergoing on-pump CABG surgery were included in the study. Patients with depression, aged 80 years or older, undergoing aortic or mitral valve surgery, having diabetes mellitus, cerebrovascular disease, psychiatric disorders or with an ejection fraction (EF) < 50% were excluded from the study. Prior to conducting any analysis, anonymization was implemented to protect patient confidentiality. The research dataset was meticulously stripped of any personal identifiers, with only clinically relevant numerical and categorical variables retained. This approach ensured strict adherence to ethical guidelines and data protection standards, safeguarding patient privacy throughout the research process.

Although a score of 26 and above is recommended for evaluation of the MoCA test, this is debated in the literature when considering the patient’s education level, age, and cultural background22,23. In our study, based on information from the literature, patients who showed a decrease of 2 or more points in the MoCA score were considered to have developed neurocognitive impairment24,25.

Patients scheduled for CPB surgery underwent the MoCA test preoperatively, administered by Anesthesiology and Reanimation Specialists. One month postoperatively, the test was conducted by Cardiovascular Surgery Specialists at Sakarya Training and Research Hospital. The Turkish version 8.1 of the MoCA test was used in this study. A total of 28 patients were evaluated, and the MoCA test was administered. The patients were divided into two groups, and a cognitive impairment was considered present if the second MoCA test showed a decrease of 2 or more points.

To ensure clarity in terminology, we define the following surgical phases based on consultation with cardiovascular surgeons:

Cross-clamp period: The duration of aortic cross-clamping, corresponding to complete cardiac arrest. This period is nested within the bypass phase (n = 38,452 data points).

Cross-clamp period: The duration of aortic cross-clamping, corresponding to complete cardiac arrest. This period is nested within the bypass phase (n = 38,452 data points).

These phases represent nested intervals: cross-clamp period ⊆ bypass period ⊆ surgery period. Hemodynamic monitoring was continuous throughout all phases using the same monitoring equipment, allowing for phase-specific analysis of the relationship between hemodynamic parameters and neurocognitive outcomes.

Partial dependence plots (PDPs)

PDPs are effective visualization tools designed to enhance the interpretability of complex machine learning models. They illustrate the relationship between a selected subset of input features and the model’s predictions by keeping all other features constant. This allows for an analysis of how variations in one or two features influence the model’s output. PDPs are particularly valuable for understanding intricate models like Random Forests and gradient-boosted trees, as they highlight which features are most significant and how they impact predictions. By providing a clearer perspective on model behavior, PDPs help demystify “black-box” models and promote transparency in the decision-making process. These plots enable a deeper analysis by visually demonstrating the effects of specific features on the outcomes20.

PDPs provide a graphical representation of how the prediction function varies with respect to specific input variables. Here, x represents the complete set of variables influencing . PDPs calculate the average effect of a specific feature on the model’s predictions. This is achieved by averaging the model’s predictions across all possible values of the other features, effectively isolating the contribution of the selected feature while accounting for the overall data distribution.

Discussion

This study reveals the complex and dynamic role of hemodynamic parameters in preserving neurocognitive functions during CABG surgery. Our research contributes to the literature with four significant findings: First, we demonstrated that neurocognitive protection depends not on a single hemodynamic parameter, but on optimal combinations of PLS, SpO2, and MAP that vary according to surgical phases. Second, our analysis using the Random Forest algorithm revealed parameter importance degrees that vary by surgical phase - while PLS had the highest importance throughout the surgery and during the bypass phase, the absence of PLS value during the cross-clamp phase and the decreased classification performance during this period emphasize the critical role of PLS in neurocognitive protection. Third, through PDPs, we provided concrete targets that can be used in clinical practice by defining specific, optimal parameter ranges for all surgical phases. Fourth, our findings emphasize the importance of surgical phase-specific, personalized hemodynamic management strategies to prevent neurocognitive impairment during CABG surgery.

Heart rate (HR) directly influences cerebral perfusion through its role in cardiac output (CO), as expressed by the equation CO = HR × SV (stroke volume). Our findings reveal optimal heart rate ranges that vary by surgical phase: 65–75 or 115–120 bpm during surgery period, and 65–93 or 110–115 bpm during bypass. This bimodal distribution suggests two distinct physiological states where cerebral perfusion is optimized through different stroke volume compensations.

Our data reveal that severe bradycardia (< 60 bpm) is associated with higher neurocognitive risk, while two distinct optimal ranges show protective effects: Severe Bradycardia (< 60 bpm): Inadequate cardiac output despite compensatory stroke volume increase, insufficient cerebral perfusion pressure, and poor adaptation to CPB demands. Lower Optimal Range (65–93 bpm): Enhanced diastolic filling time, improved coronary perfusion, and optimal stroke volume-heart rate balance. Higher Optimal Range (110–115 bpm): Compensatory mechanism maintaining cardiac output when stroke volume is compromised, reflecting appropriate physiological response to surgical stress.

Our key finding is that the optimal MAP value appears to shift depending on the heart rate range. Specifically: When HR was in the 70–80 bpm range, neurocognitive risk was lowest with MAP values around 70 mmHg, When HR was in the 115–120 bpm range, the optimal MAP shifted upward to approximately 90 mmHg. This reflects fundamental cardiovascular physiology where MAP = (HR × SV) × SVR. Higher heart rates may require elevated MAP to overcome increased vascular resistance and maintain adequate diastolic pressure for cerebral perfusion.

Previous studies examining optimal MAP targets during CPB have yielded conflicting results, which our findings help to reconcile by demonstrating the critical importance of heart rate-MAP interactions and the particular dangers of severe bradycardia.

Studies Supporting Moderate MAP Targets: Newman et al. found that MAP values below 50 mmHg were associated with cognitive impairment, particularly in elderly patients, but concluded that MAP alone was not a fundamental factor in cognitive decline11. This finding aligns with our results showing that MAP effectiveness depends critically on concurrent heart rate values. When heart rate drops below 60 bpm in our study, even moderate MAP values (around 48 mmHg) become associated with maximum risk scores, supporting Newman’s observation that isolated MAP targets are insufficient.

Studies Favoring Higher MAP Targets: Siepe et al. demonstrated that higher systemic perfusion pressure (80–90 mmHg vs. 60–70 mmHg) was associated with reduced early postoperative cognitive impairment and delirium13. Gold et al. found that high MAP values (80–100 mmHg) led to lower rates of neurological complications14. Our findings provide mechanistic insight into these observations: higher MAP targets may be particularly beneficial when heart rate is suboptimal, serving as a compensatory mechanism to maintain cerebral perfusion pressure when cardiac output is compromised.

Studies with Neutral Findings: Vedel et al. found no significant difference between low (40–50 mmHg) and high (70–80 mmHg) MAP targets, while Charlson et al. found no difference between high and individualized pressure targets12,15. These neutral findings may reflect the studies’ failure to account for heart rate interactions. Our data suggest that a wide range of MAP values can be protective (45–75 mmHg during bypass) provided heart rate is maintained in optimal ranges and severe bradycardia (< 60 bpm) is avoided.

Integration with Microembolic and Oxygenation Studies: While Pugsley et al. emphasized the role of microembolic events and Hu et al. highlighted cerebral oxygenation monitoring27,28, our study suggests that maintaining adequate heart rate (> 60 bpm) and optimal hemodynamic parameter combinations may reduce both microembolic risk and improve cerebral oxygenation simultaneously. The severe risk associated with bradycardia may work through multiple pathways including reduced embolic clearance and compromised oxygen delivery.

The practical application of our findings suggests a paradigm shift toward integrated, phase-specific hemodynamic management with particular emphasis on preventing severe bradycardia during CABG surgery. Our most clinically actionable finding is the critical importance of maintaining heart rate above 60 bpm throughout all surgical phases. This threshold should be considered a “red line” requiring immediate intervention. Clinical protocols should include: Continuous heart rate monitoring with alarms set at 65 bpm to provide early warning. Readily available chronotropic agents (atropine, isoproterenol) for immediate bradycardia management. Consideration of temporary pacing when pharmacological interventions are insufficient. Proactive management of factors that predispose to bradycardia (hypothermia, high-dose opioids, beta-blocker effects).

Beyond bradycardia prevention, our results support phase-specific hemodynamic targets. During the bypass phase, targeting heart rates in the 65–93 bpm range with MAP 45–75 mmHg and SpO2 91–100% provides optimal neuroprotection. During the overall surgical period, heart rates of 65–75 bpm or 115–120 bpm with corresponding MAP adjustments (70–90 mmHg) minimize neurocognitive risk.

Rather than managing individual parameters in isolation, our findings support an integrated approach where heart rate, MAP, and oxygenation are optimized as synergistic combinations. For example, when heart rate is maintained in the 65–93 bpm range during bypass, MAP can be managed within the broader 45–75 mmHg range while ensuring SpO2 remains 91–100%.

Our identification of maximum risk combinations provides clear targets for avoidance. Beyond severe bradycardia, combinations of low SpO2 (< 85), low MAP (< 50 mmHg), and suboptimal heart rates create particularly dangerous scenarios that require immediate correction.

Several limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting our findings. The single-center design and relatively small sample size (n = 28) limit the generalizability of our results, although the large number of data points (450,548 after artefact removal) provides substantial analytical power for machine learning applications. The absence of postoperative outcome data beyond MoCA scores represents a significant limitation, as factors such as extubation time, ICU length of stay, and postoperative complications could influence neurocognitive outcomes. Future prospective studies should systematically control these perioperative variables.

While our identification of 60 bpm as a critical threshold is physiologically plausible and statistically robust within our dataset, this finding requires validation in larger, diverse patient populations. Future studies should specifically examine whether this threshold holds across different patient demographics, surgical complexities, and institutional practices.

Future research should investigate the underlying mechanisms by which severe bradycardia increases neurocognitive risk. Studies incorporating cerebral blood flow measurements, regional cerebral oxygenation monitoring, and neuroimaging could provide deeper insights into the pathophysiology of bradycardia-induced cognitive impairment.

Prospective studies evaluating the clinical implementation of bradycardia prevention protocols and integrated hemodynamic management strategies are needed to demonstrate their practical effectiveness in reducing postoperative neurocognitive impairment. These studies should include cost-effectiveness analyses and assessment of implementation barriers.

Extended follow-up studies with MoCA assessments at 3, 6, and 12 months post-surgery would provide insight into whether the acute neurocognitive effects of suboptimal hemodynamics translate into persistent cognitive impairment or represent transient phenomena.

While our PDP visualizations effectively demonstrate pairwise parameter interactions, future research could benefit from advanced interpretability methods such as Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) to explore three-way parameter interactions more comprehensively. SHAP analysis could provide deeper insights into how PLS, SpO2, and MAP collectively contribute to neurocognitive risk prediction.

Conclusion

This study provides novel insights into the critical role of hemodynamic parameters, particularly heart rate, in neurocognitive outcomes during CABG surgery. Our most significant finding is the identification of severe bradycardia (PLS < 60 bpm) as a primary, preventable risk factor for neurocognitive impairment, representing a clear, actionable target for clinical intervention.

By integrating physiological knowledge with explainable machine learning models trained on a large dataset of over 450,000 data points, we identified phase-specific, dynamic interactions among PLS, MAP, and SpO2 that are crucial for perioperative neuroprotection. These parameter combinations provide practical targets for individualized hemodynamic management beyond single-parameter optimization.

The use of partial dependence plots allowed complex machine learning outputs to be translated into clinically interpretable parameter ranges, supporting real-time intraoperative decision-making. This approach enables protocol-based yet personalized strategies, such as rapid correction of bradycardia and optimization of PLS-MAP matching during critical surgical phases.

While validation in larger cohorts is essential, our results offer a robust framework for developing evidence-based, integrated hemodynamic management protocols aimed at reducing postoperative neurocognitive impairment. Future clinical protocols should prioritize prevention of severe bradycardia and adopt phase-specific, integrated strategies reflecting the dynamic interplay of key hemodynamic parameters identified in this study.
