Medial plantar artery flap for reconstruction of heel and foot defects in malignancy- a retrospective analysis
Authors: Anand Raja, Chandra Kumar Krishnan, Shalini Shree Krishnamurthy, Viswamadesh Ramachandran, Gurushankari Balakrishnan, Kanuj Malik, Kathiresan Narayanaswamy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReconstruction of the soft tissue defects of the heel has been an area of challenge over the years for plastic and reconstructive surgeons. Background

Soft tissue defects of the heel and the lateral aspect of the foot are difficult areas to reconstruct and have been a challenge for plastic and reconstructive surgeons over the years [1]. The ideal flap must be able to withstand (a) the high stress of body weight, (b) Sheer forces, and (c) must be sensate to prevent pressure ulcerations.

The medial plantar artery flap described by Harrison and Morgan in 1981 [2] fulfils all the above criteria and is an ideal replacement for soft tissue defects of the heel. This flap is based on the medial plantar artery and nerve and uses skin and fascia from the non-weight-bearing area of the instep of the sole (the donor area is covered by a skin graft). This can cover defects of the heel, the lateral aspect of the foot, and the distal Achilles area [3].

In our subcontinent, patients presenting with skin cancers around the heel are quite common, possibly due to barefoot walking. The medial plantar artery flap is a preferred flap of choice for reconstruction of the weight-bearing area as it is a robust and sensate flap. An insensate flap can lead to trophic ulcers or pressure ulcers, adding to the morbidity. Being a pedicled flap, there is no requirement for microvascular anastomosis. This flap can come in handy for surgeons who perform reconstructions as well. This obviates the need for microvascular free flap and is thus simple, reliable and reproducible. The use of this flap for an oncological resection is very limited. The postoperative morbidity and flap outcomes in such a setting are also limited. Ours being one of the largest series meeting both the requirements can aid the reader in performing this flap in their practice.

We present in this article the relevant anatomy, technique, and our clinical experience and collate these results with those in world literature.

Methods

A retrospective analysis of patients who underwent reconstruction of defects of the heel and sole at our Institution between January 1, 2007, and December 31, 2018, was performed. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC), and informed consent was obtained from all the patients.

The records were reviewed for demographic, clinicopathological, postoperative morbidity and postoperative outcomes, especially in terms of flap failure and outcomes. Early flap failures were those that occurred within 3 months, and any failure > 3 months was considered a late failure. Two-point discrimination was assessed for all patients using callipers during their follow-up and was recorded. Additionally, a monofilament test was performed on all patients to differentiate between pain sensation and protective sensation.

Anatomy

The posterior tibial artery branches into the medial and lateral plantar arteries between the medial malleolus and medial tubercle of the calcaneum [4]. The medial plantar artery (MPA) supplies the abductor halluces and the flexor digitorum brevis, with branches that supplies the skin and ends by anastomosing at the hallux. The medial plantar nerve, a major branch of the tibial nerve, accompanies the MPA, providing sensory innervation to the hallux and dividing into digital nerves. Branches of this nerve make the MPA flap sensate. The plantar aponeurosis consists of five bands supporting the toes and, along with the neurovascular bundle, is involved in flap raising procedures requiring division of the aponeurosis.

Procedure

Preoperative presence of plantar sensation and patency of dorsalis pedis is confirmed. The posterior tibial artery is located by palpation, and adequacy of the MPA vascularity is confirmed by Doppler. The procedure is performed under tourniquet control. The tumor is resected (Figs. 1 and 2A), the defect is measured (Fig. 2B), and an exact area is marked on the instep with the artery in the middle (Fig. 2C). Sometimes with larger flaps, the vessel may lie medially along the flap. The flap is confined to the instep at all times, and no part of the flap is harvested from the weight-bearing area. The distal margin of the flap is 1 cm proximal to the head of the metatarsal.

We start dissection along the medial margin of the flap, and the incision is deepened up to the abductor halluces. The flap is then raised in the sub-fascial plane till the septum between the abductor halluces and the flexor digitorum brevis is encountered (Figs. 2D, E). This septum is not breached, and the abductor hallucis is retracted to identify the neurovascular bundle that runs in the septum. As the flap has to be rotated to the heel, the vessels are traced proximally to the division of the posterior tibial vessels. To secure an adequate length of the flap without tension, the abductor halluces muscle, and or the lateral plantar artery may be divided. The medial branch of the MPA (supplying the great toe) is carefully divided, preserving the lateral branch to the instep skin. The distal margin is incised up to the plantar aponeurosis. The lateral aspect of the flap is then incised up to the plantar aponeurosis, the flexor digitorum brevis is exposed, and the flap is raised sub-fascial in the septum between the abductor halluces and the flexor digitorum brevis. The flap is finally freed by dividing the proximal part, and the neurovascular pedicle is clearly identified on the flap (Fig. 2F).

The flap is then transposed to the recipient area and sutured in a single layer with 2 − 0 nylon or a double layer with subcutaneous and clips over a corrugated rubber drain (Fig. 3). The donor area is covered with a split-thickness skin graft. The limb is immobilised in a POP slab with a window in the flap area for monitoring. The flap is monitored postoperatively on an hourly basis for the first 24 h for colour, turgor, and bleeding on prick. All sutures are removed on day 14. Non-weight-bearing mobilisation is started at the end of the first week, partial weight-bearing at the 6th week, and if no wound complications are encountered, weight is gradually increased to full weight-bearing within two weeks of starting mobilisation. The patients are mobilised with silicone or microcellular rubber footwear. The flap is assessed every two to three weeks for viability, infection, necrosis, and sensation. Results

A total of 41 patients underwent MPA flap reconstruction. The mean age was 47 years (range 37–54). Male: Female was 31:10. The indication for the primary resections was melanoma in 30 patients and squamous cell carcinoma in 11 patients. The mean duration of follow-up was 82.42 months (range 14–123 months). No patient had any history of pre-existing neurological deficit, and sensory perception was present over the instep in all patients. All defects were in the weight-bearing area of the foot. Lesions over the heel and the lateral aspect of the foot were present in 25 and 16 patients, respectively. The tumour bed was formed by the tendons over the plantar aspect of the foot and the calcaneum. Bone was not resected in any patient. The size of the defect ranged from 3 × 2 cm to a maximum of 10 × 7 cm. All flaps were island fasciocutaneous flaps, in that the plantar aponeurosis along with the overlying fat, skin, and neurovascular pedicle was raised en bloc. All patients had donor defects covered with a split-thickness skin graft.

Minimal marginal flap necrosis was seen in three patients and pus discharge in 2 patients. Hematoma, wound dehiscence, and total flap necrosis were seen in one patient each. All patients were managed conservatively except for the patients who developed (a) hematoma - underwent clot evacuation (exploration did not identify a source of bleed) - and (b) total flap necrosis - this was identified on day 3. Venous thrombus led to flap failure. She underwent flap excision with split-thickness skin grafting.

All patients had two-point discrimination to touch and pain sensation tested during follow-up with a mean value of 30 mm. All patients had sensate flaps which refers to restoration of protective sensation (confirmed by a monofilament test). The time to full weight-bearing mobilization was between 8 and 10 weeks after surgery. There are no late flap failures at the last follow-up. There was no donor site morbidity.

Discussion

Different methods have been described to reconstruct the weight-bearing areas of the heel. Each has its own merits and demerits. The goal of reconstruction should be to withstand weight-bearing and provide sensation.

The different techniques described to reconstruct the heel include the V-Y advancement flap as described by Colen [5], reverse sural artery flaps, and neurovascular island flaps from the large second and third toes to resurface plantar defects [6].

The first flap based on the MPA, a pedicled inset flap, was described by Shanahan and Gingrass et al. in 1979 [7]. Reiffel and McCarthy [8] performed cadaveric studies of the plantar skin, fascia, and the neurovascular supply and concluded that dissection deep to the plantar fascia allows the inclusion of the medial or lateral plantar nerve and artery with the flap, thereby allowing considerable mobility while preserving vascularity and sensibility. Several variations of the flap were reported. An instep island neurovascular flap based on the MPA was described by Harrison and Morgan in 1981 [2]. Morrison et al. [9] in 1983 described the use of an instep island fasciocutaneous flap based on either the medial or lateral plantar neurovascular bundle or both as an island and free flap. Hidalgo and Shaw [10] in 1986 showed that the heel was innervated by the medial calcaneal nerves and that blood supply to the instep area is predominantly from the proximal plantar subcutaneous plexus and that contribution from the medial plantar artery was not significant. They advised medial flap rotation to preserve calcaneal nerves and dissected in the supra-fascial plane above the plantar fascia. However, this supra-fascial rotation or transposition may not reach the posterior-most area of the weight-bearing surface [1]. In contradiction to the technique described in literature [7, 9], where the flap dissection is started distally (proximal to the metatarsal head) and moves proximally, we commence our dissection medially. This has the advantage of early identification and preservation of the neurovascular pedicle in the septum between the abductor halluces and the flexor digitorum brevis.

Almost the entire instep area can be included in raising the flap. The largest flap that was harvested in the current series was 10 × 7 cm, which is consistent with that reported in literature [1]. The mobility of the flap can be increased by (a) making an island flap, (b) dividing the lateral plantar vessels at their origin from the posterior tibial vessels [1], or (c) dividing the abductor hallucis muscle. Once adequately mobilised, it can reach almost any part of the weight-bearing area of the heel.

The medial plantar artery flap is robust and reliable, as it closely replaces “like tissue with like”. The replaced skin tissue has fibrous septa running from the plantar aponeurosis to the skin with thick subcutaneous fat, which helps to resist pressure and shear forces. Sensation helps in preventing trophic ulcers. Morrison et al. [9] concluded that this innate mechanical property makes the flap durable.

Much available literature on MPA flaps has described their use mainly in the management of diabetic foot, neuropathic ulcers, leprosy, trauma, and burn contractures. Only a few case reports and series are available on the use in malignant lesions. To the authors’ knowledge, ours is the largest series employing MPA flaps in malignancy. When done for neuropathic ulcers, recurrent ulcers could be of concern, but this is obviated when done for malignant lesions. This flap allows for early mobilisation, which results in the early achievement of a better quality of life in such patients. Our results emphasise that amputation is not the only choice for patients with malignant lesions over the heel and that the limbs can be successfully salvaged with the MPA flaps. When in doubt about the margins, the flap can be done as a staged procedure once the definite biopsy report is reviewed, but with a risk of increased infection rates.

Minor complications such as marginal necrosis, infection, and wound dehiscence with delayed healing have been reported in a minority of patients, which is consistent with that reported in literature [2].

The study by Gravem [11] reported that only 1 of 14 patients developed recurrent ulceration. Benito-Ruiz [12] did not report any recurrent ulcers with 1–2 years of follow-up. Similarly, Woo et al. also did not report any ulcerations or wound dehiscence [13]. Schwartz [2] reported that 7 of 50 patients with insensate flaps on long-term follow-up developed recurrent ulcers. In the present study, all flaps are healthy with no flap loss to date.

If due diligence and care are taken to preserve the medial plantar nerve, sensation can be preserved in all patients (provided they did not have a preoperative sensory deficit). In the current study, all patients had preservation of nerve functions, as was also reported by Schwarz [2]. Miyamoto et al. [14] reported that on long-term follow-up, 7 of 13 patients had hypo- or hyperaesthesia of the feet. A few authors have also reported hyperkeratosis along with paraesthesia [13].

A few authors [14] have included a part of the flexor digitorum muscle in the flap. We have never included the muscle in any flap. Other authors [2, 11] have also found this unnecessary. These flaps have traditionally been used for the reconstruction of trophic ulcers over the heel. This is one of the largest studies that has used this flap for the reconstruction of defects after resection for malignancy.

Conclusions

The medial plantar artery provides one of the best reconstructive options for defects over the weight-bearing part of the heel. Satisfactory results were obtained with a good color and texture match for heel repair and a good sensory recovery. No functional deficit was found at the donor site. It is a durable and reliable flap and can even be used to cover large defects.
