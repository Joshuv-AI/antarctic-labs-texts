Social support and psychological distress among family caregivers of palliative patients at two rural hospitals in Malawi: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ian Matandika, Modai Mnenula, Prosper Lutala
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCaring for palliative patients imposes a significant burden, as caregivers must provide physical, psychological, social, and spiritual support. Identifying ways to support informal caregivers is essential for improving their quality of life as well as that of the patients. This study aims to assess the prevalence and determinants of social support and psychological distress, and to explore their relationship among family caregivers of patients receiving palliative care at Nkhoma Mission Hospital and Neno District Hospital in rural Malawi.MethodsA total of 308 adult caregivers of adult palliative patients attending services at Nkhoma Mission Hospital and Neno District Hospital were recruited between November 2022 and February 2023. Data were collected using the Self-Reporting Questionnaire (SRQ) and the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS). Categorical data were analyzed using frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized using mean and standard deviation. Logistic regression was used to determine significant associations.ResultsOf the 308 participants, 176 (57.1%) reported receiving social support, and 129 (41.9%) experienced psychological distress. Caregivers at Nkhoma were over six times more likely to report receiving social support compared to those at Neno (odds ratio [OR] 6.7; 95% CI: 3.1–14.4; p < 0.001). Female caregivers were more than twice as likely to report receiving social support (relative risk [RR] 2.2; 95% CI: 1.2–4.4; p = 0.016). Higher levels of psychological distress were more likely to be reported among caregivers who received support from friends (OR 2.2; 95% CI: 1.3–3.8; p = 0.004) or significant others (OR 1.9; 95% CI: 1.1–3.4; p = 0.023), compared to those supported by family members (OR 1.2; 95% CI: 0.7–2.0; p = 0.562).ConclusionsThis study reveals a substantial burden of psychological distress among informal caregivers of palliative patients in rural Malawi, with notable differences in social support based on gender and geographic location. To better support these caregivers, community-based programs that promote social connectedness are recommended, alongside the routine screening for psychological distress within palliative care services.

Background

Palliative care is a holistic approach aimed at enhancing the quality of life for patients with life-threatening illnesses and their families by addressing physical, psychological, social, and spiritual needs through integrated, person-centered care [1]. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) like Malawi, the delivery of palliative care often relies heavily on informal caregivers due to limited healthcare resources and infrastructure [2].

Informal caregivers, typically family members or close friends, provide unpaid care that extends beyond customary social support, encompassing tasks such as medication management, assistance with daily activities, emotional support, and coordination with healthcare providers [3]. Their involvement is crucial in ensuring continuity of care, especially in home-based settings where professional healthcare services may be scarce [4]. In palliative care, patients can receive supported from informal care givers such as family members, friends, groups of well-wishers, or any other caregiver who has not received professional training to take care of palliative patients. Support may include moral encouragement, material assistance and financial resources.

Despite their indispensable role, informal caregivers frequently experience significant burdens, including physical strain, emotional stress, social isolation, and financial hardship [5]. These challenges are often exacerbated by a lack of formal support systems, training, and respite services, which are more commonly available in high-income countries [6]. In LMICs, the absence of such support structures can lead to caregiver burnout, negatively impacting both the caregiver’s well-being and the quality of patient care [7].

Evidence has established that a positive relation between patients and caregivers—characterized by shared positivity, recognition of adversity, and adaptation to evolving roles—has a positive impact on overall well-being. Furthermore, McCauley et al. [8] find the connection between emotional connectedness and well-being of caregivers through shared decision-making, emotional support and structured care on caregivers. In the same way, protagonists of support of advanced cancers patients emphasized the importance of social and emotional support for informal caregivers [9]. However, most of this evidence emanates from high-resource settings. In contrast, there is limited research examining how these processes unfold in LMICs like Malawi, where cultural expectations and health system constraints may shape caregiving experiences in different ways.

The provision of palliative care services in Malawi is mainly done through outpatient departments of government health facilities. These services are provided under the coordination of the Ministry of Health and with support from the government and externally funded Nongovernmental organizations [10] such as Ndi Moyo in Salima district and Abwenzi Pa Za Umoyo in Neno district. Yet, there is an enormous lack of data regarding the experience and supportive needs of informal caregivers in this setting. It is necessary to understand these needs in order to design targeted interventions to alleviate caregiver burden and improve the overall quality of palliative care services.

The present research seeks to describe the experiences, challenges, and support needs of informal caregivers who are delivering palliative care in Malawi. By understanding their experiences, the research aspires to guide the formulation of viable support system guides in palliative care and in the long term contribute to policy formulation at the national level.

Methods

Aim

The aim of the study was to assess the prevalence and determinants of social support and psychological distress, and to explore their relationship among family caregivers of patients receiving palliative care at Nkhoma Mission Hospital and Neno District Hospital in rural Malawi.

Study design

This was a descriptive cross-sectional study with correlational analysis. This allowed us to quantify the prevalence of social support and psychological distress and their relationship using validated questionnaires across a larger sample within a short timeframe. We were also able to produce figures which are useful in informing policy decisions and securing funding. However, it does not establish causality, is limited by potential confounding factors, self-report bias and may not provide in-depth perspectives from the informant.

Sample and setting

The study included caregivers of adult palliative patients from Neno District Hospital and Nkhoma Mission Hospital. Nkhoma Mission Hospital, affiliated with the Nkhoma Synod of the Church of Central Africa Presbyterian, operates under the Christian Health Association of Malawi (CHAM). According to 2021 public health office records, the hospital serves a population of approximately 75,513 individuals in the southeastern part of Lilongwe District. It receives referrals from surrounding health centers in Lilongwe and Dedza, acting as a bridge between peripheral clinics and referral hospitals such as Bwaila and Kamuzu Central Hospitals.

Nkhoma hospital has a palliative care department which receives referrals from clinicians working in both inpatient wards and outpatient clinics. The department also conducts regular home-based services to patients enrolled into care. A multidisciplinary team comprising a clinician, nurse, pastor, social worker, and driver conducts biweekly home visits to monitor patient well-being. Follow-up frequency varies—patients are typically reviewed every three or six months, depending on staff and transport availability. This system reduces loss to follow-up and promotes adherence to treatment for patients receiving palliative care for various conditions. Since most palliative patients are cared for at home, these visits naturally involve informal caregivers. At the time of the study, 87 patients were enrolled in palliative care at Nkhoma.

Neno District Hospital serves a catchment population of 153,132 [11]. It borders Ntcheu and Balaka to the north, Zomba to the east, Blantyre to the east and southeast, Mwanza to the south, and Mozambique to the west. Opened in 2009, the hospital was initially a health center supported by Partners in Health organization (Abwenzi Pa Za Umoyo – APZU) since 2007. The organization has been instrumental in helping the district to achieve universal health coverage and supports programs targeting HIV, non-communicable diseases, and maternal and child health [12].

An Integrated Chronic Care Clinic (IC3) was established by APZU, where patients with chronic illnesses—including those requiring palliative care—are reviewed on weekdays. These patients are referred from other departments within the hospital. A multidisciplinary team consisting of nurses, social workers, patient attendants, and occasionally a clinician conducts home visits every three months to promote treatment adherence. This model also encourages collaboration between informal caregivers and trained professionals. At the time of data collection, Neno District Hospital had 461 adult patients enrolled in palliative care.

Sample size calculation

A stratified approach was used to determine sample size due to the population size differences between the two study sites. In the first step, the sample size for an infinite population was adults calculated using the formula:

N = (Z² × P × (1 − P))/E²,

at a 5% significance level, assuming a prevalence (P) of 46% [13] and a margin of error (E) of 5%, yielding 382 participants.

In the second step, finite population correction was applied using the formula:

n = N/[1 + {(N − 1)/Npop}],

where Npop represents the finite population. This gave 71 participants for Nkhoma and 209 for Neno. After adjusting for a 10% non-response rate, the final sample sizes were 78 for Nkhoma and 230 for Neno, totalling 308 participants.

Participants and data collection

Palliative patients were accompanied to the palliative care clinics by guardians who also cared for them at home. Participants were selected using systematic sampling until the sample size was reached. Every third caregiver was recruited during routine weekly home visits or when they accompanied patients to the palliative care clinics. The questionnaires were administered by the principal investigator and trained research assistants—community health workers experienced in data collection at both the community and health facility levels. In addition to their research experience, an induction of two days was organized and facilitated by the principal investigator. The aim was to familiarize the assistant researchers with the protocol, operating procedures, and ethical requirements of the research. A mix of PowerPoint presentations, demonstrations and practical were done; allowing the team to go through questionnaires, use of the phones and capturing data, introduction and closure of the interview, and a good way to answer potential questions that may arise from participants. A test was done at the end to cross-check their understanding of the content learnt and time to time mentorship was there to support the team throughout the data collection period.

Questionnaires were pre-tested on a single day with caregivers of palliative patients to assess the feasibility of study procedures. On average, it took about 10 min to complete all questionnaires and participants were recruited after providing informed consent. Feedback from participants and researchers were used to identify confusing and unclear items. Minor revisions were made to improve the wording and responses. This process helped to validate the survey by making the content easier to understand and more relevant the target population.

Data collection occurred from October 2022 to February 2023 on weekdays during palliative care clinic days and home visits. Interviews were conducted in either English or Chichewa, and responses were recorded electronically using KoBo Toolbox, a mobile data collection platform [14]. All participants reviewed and confirmed their responses on the tablets before submission to the online database. Using the participant’s language of choice enhanced the reliability of responses and the use of tablets facilitated the security of data management. However, some participants required guidance with navigation of the tablets, and occasional technical issues called for constant supervision by researchers.

Data collection tools

Sociodemographic details

This questionnaire consisted of 12 questions about sociodemographic characteristics of the caregivers.

Self-Reporting questionnaire

In this study, the Chichewa version of the Self-Reporting Questionnaire (SRQ) was used. The questionnaire was translated to the local language, Chichewa, and validated for use in Malawi by Stewart et al. [15]. It has a high level of internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.85) and good diagnostic performance (AUROC = 0.856 for major disorders and 0.826 for minor disorders) [16]. It has 20 questions with responses yes/no and is used to assess anxiety, depression and somatic symptoms which are non-specific. The scores are aggregated, and a mean score is obtained. An SRQ mean score of ≥ 8 is considered positive psychological distress.

Multidimensional scale of perceived social support

The Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) measures the perception of adequate social support received among respondents from 3 sources namely significant other, family and friends [17]. The original MSPSS has 12 items with 7 responses each having a scale of 0–6 scores. There is a maximum score of 72 and a higher score indicates perceived social support. The MSPSS was translated, validate and adapted for use in Malawi [18] and was used in this study. The Malawian version has 5 possible responses on a scale of 0–4 giving a maximum score out of 48 and a higher score indicating social support. Responses were requested in a Likert scale, with 0–1 representing low social support, 2–3 as moderate, and 3–4 as high levels of social support. We considered everyone with a higher score as having social support. For the subgroups of family, friend, or significant other, a higher cut off score of 3–4 indicated social support in each of the subgroups. The significant other referred to a person who is outside of family or friendship bond such as a spiritual advisor, volunteer, caregiver, mentor or spouse [18]. A response card of the MSPSS was available to assist participants when filling in the responses. It featured faces ranging from very sad to very happy, representing the participants’ level of agreement with each item.

Data analysis

The Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) v23 was used to analyze the data. Socio-demographic characteristics of participants are presented in percentages and frequencies for the categorical data and all continuous data are summarized as mean and standard deviation. Prevalence of psychological distress is presented from the SRQs average score cut off. Perceived social support is presented as three domains of significant other, family, and friends. Chis-square was used to examine the association between social support and sociodemographic characteristics of the study population. The relationship between social support and psychological distress was established using logistic regression where all variables were included for analysis regardless of the p-value on Chi-square analysis. Statistical significance was tested at a p-value of < 0.005.

Independent associations between social support and psychological distress

When subjected to binary logistic regression to test for independent associations, participants receiving any source of social support were three times more likely to report psychological distress [OR = 3.6 (95% CI 2.1–5.9; p < 0.001)] and this association was positive. When the different sources of social support were subjected to multivariate model, caregivers who reported receiving social support from a friend [OR = 2.2 (95% CI 1.3–3.8; p < 0.004)] were two times more likely to report psychological distress; and caregivers who reported receiving social support from a significant other [OR = 1.9 (95% CI 1.1–3.4; p < 0.023)] were almost twice as likely to experience psychological distress. Caregivers who reported receiving social support from family were less likely to report psychological distress [OR = 1.2 (95% CI 0.7–2.1; p < 0.562)]. Discussion

The study shows that 57.1% of participants had social support, with a higher proportion—84.6%—of those receiving primary care from Nkhoma Mission Hospital compared to those receiving care from Neno District Hospital. This disparity may be explained by the easier road access to Nkhoma Mission Hospital and its accessible catchment area, unlike Neno’s poor geographic terrain, which makes it a hard-to-reach area. As such, social support may be more readily provided to patients and family caregivers who can be physically visited with ease. Nkhoma Mission Hospital also has a well-developed and functional palliative care department that offers social support, home-based care, and follow-up for palliative patients and those with chronic or acute-on-chronic conditions and low socioeconomic status. This support can be classified as coming from a “significant other,” especially in cases where patients and guardians are visited regularly by the same palliative care team member(s) with whom they may feel emotionally connected.

Despite reporting a lower rate of social support at 47.8%, Neno District Hospital runs an in- and out-of-hospital palliative care program through collaboration with the Ministry of Health and APZU [19]. It is important to note that our study did not explicitly measure social support provided by these palliative care programs; instead, we assessed the perceptions of social support from family, friend and significant other. To better understand the reasons behind the low rates of reported social support—beyond the geographic challenges—qualitative studies should be conducted, although this was beyond the scope of the current study.

We expected higher social support from family, given the extended family culture prevalent in Malawi, which has also been observed in other studies [19, 20]. However, our study demonstrated that social support was shared almost equally among family (39.6%), friends (39.9%) and significant other (31.8%). This suggests that despite families remaining central to caregiving, they may be overburdened and unable to meet the needs for the palliative patients. Therefore, social connectedness should be encouraged within the families and the community to help reinforce existing support systems and ensure reliable caregiving networks.

Female caregivers were more likely to receive social support compared to their male counterparts. While some studies have shown higher rates of social support among men, others found no gender difference [21]. However, our finding aligns with existing evidence that women tend to value social connectedness and intimate relationships more than men [22, 23]. Their inclination toward forming and maintaining social bonds may make them more likely to seek social support.

We observed high rates of psychological distress (42.9%) among the study population. This figure falls within the range reported in previous studies, which link caregiver burden to poor psychological outcomes, including depression and anxiety rates of 12% to 59% [24–26]. While not all psychological distress leads to clinical depression or anxiety—an area not explored further in this study—the presence of psychological distress alone warrants attention. It is essential to address the psychological needs of informal caregivers to prevent further mental health deterioration. Palliative care providers should consider using screening tools such as the Self-Reporting Questionnaire (SRQ) to identify patients and guardians in need of psychological evaluation and care.

Our study established a positive association between social support and psychological distress, a finding that contradicts existing literature. A meta-analysis by Harandi et al. [27], has reported that social support acts as a protective factor against psychological distress. In contrast, we found that psychological distress was twice as likely to be reported among caregivers who received support from friends and nearly twice as likely among those receiving support from a significant other than those receiving from family. This could be explained by unmeasured caregiver biopsychosocial characteristics—such as trauma history, resilience, and other life changes—which may have confounded our findings. It is also possible that caregivers experiencing greater psychological distress sought more social support. These findings suggest possible a bidirectional relationship between social support and psychological distress highlighting the need for further research.

Respite care should be considered for caregivers who report low family support, as evidence shows that such services can reduce psychological and physical burdens [28, 29]. In low-income settings where respite care may not be feasible, community health workers may serve as a viable alternative, as suggested by Barnett et al. [30]. However, we need advocate for such formal services as they can help relieve socio-economic pressure among caregivers who may lack consistent support.

Our findings reveal a complex relationship between social support and mental health among caregivers of palliative patients. Contrary to existing studies, our results point toward a bidirectional relationship [30, 31]. This suggests that while social support is considered protective, some caregivers may experience psychological distress despite having social support. It may also suggest that caregivers with psychological distress may actively seek for more social support. Further research is needed to explore potential confounders of psychological distress in caregivers who report receiving social support.

Both instrumental (practical) and emotional support can help lessen the negative effects of caregiving on mental health, particularly in cases of bereavement and ongoing caregiver burden [32, 33]. Family doctors and primary care providers play a crucial role in identifying carer distress early and facilitating support systems, which can reduce caregiver stress and improve psychological outcomes [33]. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that online and community-based interventions improve the resilience and general well-being of caregivers [34]. In palliative care settings, informal caregivers’ psychological distress and quality of life can be considerably improved by strengthening social support networks through professional, community, or family-based interventions.

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study exploring the association between perceived social support and psychological distress among informal caregivers of palliative patients in primary care settings in Malawi. We established this relationship using standardized questionnaires validated for use in Malawi. Our findings raise important questions about factors influencing caregivers’ mental health beyond social support.

However, our study has limitations. Being cross-sectional, it is subject to recall bias and does not allow for conclusions about causality [35]. Surveys relied on self-reported measures of social support and psychological distress, which may be influenced by social desirability or participants’ interpretations of the questions. Although participants understood the categories of support (family, friends, significant other), these classifications may not capture the full complexity or quality of support received. Data collection was conducted in either English or Chichewa by trained research assistants, which likely reduced but may not have eliminated language or interviewer-related bias. Additionally, our study did not measure support provided directly by palliative care programs, which limits the ability to attribute reported support to specific sources. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insight into the complex relationship between social support and psychological distress among informal caregivers in rural Malawi.

We recommend longitudinal cohort studies to examine whether caregivers’ psychological distress improves over time after receiving psychosocial support. In addition, qualitative studies could explore why psychological distress persists among caregivers despite the presence of social support.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the significant burden of psychological distress among family caregivers of palliative patients in rural Malawi and highlights the variations in social support based on gender and geographic location. While family networks are often central to caregiving, our results show a complex relationship where caregivers receiving support from family reported lower psychological distress compared with those supported primarily by friends or significant others. These findings suggest that family support may play a particularly protective role in mitigating caregiver distress emphasizing the role of family networks in caregiving. The study also highlights the importance of targeted interventions to address psychological challenges among caregivers of palliative patients regardless of whether they are getting social support or not.

Given the high rates of psychological distress among informal caregivers, palliative care services should incorporate regular mental health screening to enable early referral and psychological support. Furthermore, we recommend community initiatives that promote social connectedness to enhance caregiver well-being.
