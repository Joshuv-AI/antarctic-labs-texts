Rheumatology 2050: How our specialty is changing
Authors: Mariam Omar, Katie Bechman, Mark Russell, James Galloway
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRheumatology has undergone unprecedented transformation over the past quarter century, evolving from broad-spectrum immunosuppression to precision therapeutics targeting specific immune pathways. As the clinical, biological and economic landscape continues to evolve, understanding emerging trends is crucial for shaping the future of the specialty. In this narrative review, we will examine contemporary themes that will define rheumatology by 2050, spanning key areas of rheumatological practice, including precision medicine, genomic discoveries, disease prevention, cellular therapies and healthcare economics.Multiple transformative trends are currently reshaping rheumatology. Precision medicine is advancing beyond single-pathway targeting towards molecular stratification integrating serology, genomics and synovial pathotyping. Whole-exome sequencing has revealed new disease entities such as vacuoles, E1 enzyme, X-linked, autoinflammatory, somatic (VEXAS) syndrome, while disease interception strategies show promise for preventing established autoimmunity, with trials demonstrating potential relative risk reduction in rheumatoid arthritis development using abatacept in at-risk individuals. Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy has achieved complete clinical remission in severe systemic lupus erythematosus, while immune checkpoint inhibitor-induced rheumatic diseases present new diagnostic challenges. Environmental pressures, including air pollution and occupational exposures, are increasingly recognised as disease drivers. In the UK, national audit data show disparities in access to advanced therapies even within a universal healthcare system. Artificial intelligence integration promises enhanced diagnostics and monitoring, while economic pressures necessitate innovative funding models, with biosimilars demonstrating potential for expanding treatment access.By 2050, rheumatology will be characterised by molecularly stratified diseases, more affordable and accessible targeted therapies, curative cellular interventions and digital clinical ecosystems. Success will depend not only on technological advances but on ensuring equitable access, affordability, and preservation of patient-centred care.Keywords: Rheumatology, Precision medicine, Disease interception, CAR-T therapy, Artificial intelligence, Health equity, Biosimilars, VEXAS syndrome

Introduction

During the past quarter century, rheumatology has undergone a transformation that is hard to equal in any other branch of internal medicine, perhaps matched only by oncology, which has likewise witnessed extraordinary advances in precision medicine. We have been a vanguard of precision therapeutics, matching highly targeted agents to clearly delineated immune pathways and, in so doing, delivering unprecedented improvements in disease control while simultaneously reducing systemic toxicity. Yet the clinical, biological and economic landscape continues to evolve. Our patients are older and more multimorbid; environmental and lifestyle pressures are shifting; and an ever-expanding armamentarium of drugs is accompanied by legitimate concerns about long-term safety and affordability. This review is deliberately selective. Its aim is to provoke thought by drawing together nine contemporary themes that, in the author’s view, will shape rheumatology by the year 2050.

The reality of precision medicine

The first licensed biological agent for rheumatoid arthritis (RA) arrived in the late 1990s with tumour necrosis factor (TNF) inhibition.1 Over the subsequent 2 decades, we have moved from blunt, non-specific agents such as corticosteroids and methotrexate to a toolkit of monoclonal antibodies and small molecules that target single cytokines, lineage-specific cells or intracellular kinases. The impact has been profound: wheelchairs have disappeared from clinic waiting rooms, small-joint arthroplasty is now rare due to effective early disease control, and trainees are far less familiar with the stigmata of long-term steroid use than their predecessors.2

Yet precision medicine has also revealed its limits. Progress towards true personalisation remains slow: predictive biomarkers of therapeutic response are largely absent, and around 40% of patients fail to achieve an adequate clinical response. The MATURA-STRAP trial, which stratified patients by synovial B-cell density to test whether B-cell-rich tissue predicted benefit from rituximab, did not confirm a biomarker effect.3 This underscores that biological heterogeneity extends beyond single histological features and highlights the need for integrated molecular signatures to guide treatment selection.

Precision medicine has shown that not every pathway is shared by every disease. Interleukin 17 blockade is transformative in psoriatic arthritis and axial spondyloarthritis but disappoints in RA, whereas interleukin 23 inhibition is highly effective in psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis yet offers no benefit in spondyloarthritides.4 Even within a single disease there is heterogeneity: seropositive RA responds more robustly to B-cell depletion than seronegative disease, reflecting distinct pathogenic mechanisms.5 Deeper molecular stratification integrating serology, genomics, transcriptomics and synovial pathotyping will be required if the promise of ‘the right drug first time’ is to be realised. This molecular understanding is being further refined by discoveries that challenge our traditional disease boundaries and reveal entirely new pathogenic mechanisms.

Yet not all immune-mediated manifestations have shared equally in these advances. Autoimmune-related interstitial lung disease remains a major unmet need: anti-fibrotics slow decline but rarely reverse damage, and biologics that transformed articular disease show limited pulmonary benefit.6 Heterogeneous immune-fibrotic pathways – from MUC5B-driven epithelial injury to interferon signatures – demand molecularly defined endotypes and targeted antifibrotic strategies (Fig. 1).7

Emerging diseases in the genomic era: VEXAS and beyond

Whole-exome sequencing has recently uncovered a series of late-onset autoinflammatory syndromes driven by somatic mutations. The archetype is VEXAS (vacuoles, E1 enzyme, X-linked, autoinflammatory, somatic) syndrome caused by post-zygotic variants in ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme 1 (UBA1). A systematic review encompassing 720 patients characterised the striking multi-system phenotype, with many features reminiscent of autoimmune disease: fevers, weight loss, neutrophilic dermatoses, cutaneous vasculitis, pulmonary infiltrates, joint pains, ocular inflammation, chondritis and bone marrow disease. Mechanistically, loss of cytoplasmic UBA1 isoform impairs ubiquitin signalling, triggering type I interferon-driven inflammation.8,9 VEXAS may be the first of many ‘genetically cryptic’ disorders revealed by increasingly accessible somatic sequencing and serves as a reminder that clinicopathological entities will continue to splinter as technology exposes their molecular underpinnings.

These molecular insights are more than academic curiosities; they are driving revolutionary approaches to treatment that may fundamentally alter how we conceptualise therapeutic intervention (Fig. 2).

Disease interception – closing the stable door before the horse bolts

For all their efficacy, current drugs rarely induce permanent tolerance: tapering studies consistently show relapse in a majority of patients.10 The logical extension is to intervene earlier before immunological self-organisation becomes entrenched. In RA, this strategy targets the pre-RA stage individuals who experience joint pain (arthralgia) without clinical arthritis (joint swelling), but carry RA-associated autoantibodies such as anti-citrullinated peptide antibodies (ACPA). These patients are at high risk of progression to inflammatory arthritis.

The APIPPRA trial tested this concept by randomising 213 anti-citrullinated peptide antibody (ACPA)-positive individuals with arthralgia to abatacept or placebo for 12 months. Progression to RA at 3 years was 29% versus 42% (hazard ratio 0.6), equating to a 22% relative risk reduction.11 The ARIAA trial showed a similar effect using MRI criteria and a shorter 6-month course of abatacept, also reporting reduced RA development over time.12 An accompanying Lancet editorial argued that abatacept’s modest effect is nonetheless a proof of principle: immunological interception is feasible, but true prevention will require combination strategies, perhaps pairing short-course biologics with tolerogenic vaccination or microbiome modulation.13 Data from the TREAT EARLIER trial further support this trajectory, showing that even short-term methotrexate therapy in at-risk individuals can reduce symptom burden and functional decline.14

In contrast, recent hydroxychloroquine trials in pre-clinical RA and systemic lupus erythematosus did not prevent disease progression, highlighting that the optimal window for immunological interception remains uncertain.15,16 Such preventive strategies will inevitably rely on sophisticated technological platforms to identify at-risk individuals, monitor intervention responses and optimise care delivery, capabilities that artificial intelligence is uniquely positioned to provide.

Advanced cellular therapies – from CAR T to CAR NK and beyond

A decade ago, RA dominated the rheumatology pages of the high-impact journals; today, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) claims that crown. Nowhere is this shift clearer than in the burgeoning field of chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR T) therapy. The first multi-centre series of CD19-targeted CAR T in severe, refractory SLE reported complete clinical remission in all 15 treated patients with only grade 1–2 cytokine release syndrome (CRS)17. Comparable responses have since been described in myositis and systemic sclerosis, fuelling excitement that durable, drug-free remission may finally be within reach.17, 18, 19

Limitations, however, are non-trivial: manufacturing requires autologous T-cell harvest and lymphodepletion; patients remain in hospital for 9–12 days; CRS and neurotoxicity, though usually mild in autoimmunity, demand specialist oversight; and relapse may necessitate repeat infusion after 12–24 months.19 Yet CAR T penetrates sanctuary sites and depletes tissue-resident B cells beyond the reach of monoclonal antibodies, offering a mechanistic rationale for longer-lasting responses.20

To overcome these barriers, other chimeric antigen receptor strategies (eg NK cells or T reg cells) are being developed. By retaining targeted cytotoxicity while reducing the risk of CRS and graft-versus-host disease, and enabling off-the-shelf manufacture from healthy donor cells, CAR-NK therapies could offer a safer and more accessible alternative.21 In parallel, bispecific antibodies such as CD3-CD20 ByTes may deliver comparable efficacy without lymphodepletion or inpatient stay and could supersede CAR T approaches before 2050.22 Dose de-escalation studies and dual antigen constructs will determine whether these cellular therapies can move beyond specialist centres and into broader clinical use. Could we move from disease treatment to disease cure: rather than waiting for established disease to develop, might we intervene even earlier in the disease trajectory?

Immune checkpoint inhibitor-induced rheumatic disease – cancer-autoimmunity paradox

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed the landscape of oncology, offering durable remissions in malignancies previously associated with poor prognoses, such as metastatic melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, renal cell carcinoma and Hodgkin lymphoma.23 By targeting inhibitory immune pathways such as CTLA-4 and PD-1/PD-L1, ICIs enhance T-cell-mediated antitumour responses. Their indications are rapidly expanding and are now being used earlier in the disease course, including adjuvant settings in stage II–III melanoma.24 Their indications continue to expand across a growing spectrum of cancers, including triple-negative breast cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and microsatellite instability-high colorectal cancer.25

While cancer typically develops in an immunologically permissive environment, characterised by tumour-mediated immune evasion and impaired immune recognition, ICIs deliberately enhance immune surveillance to restore and amplify anti-tumour activity by reactivating T cells through blockade of inhibitory pathways such as PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4.26 This fundamental shift from immune suppression to immune activation can disrupt mechanisms of immune tolerance, leading to a spectrum of immune-related adverse events (irAEs), with rheumatic manifestations representing a particularly challenging subset.

Emerging evidence indicates that rheumatic irAEs encompass a broader spectrum than previously recognised, including seronegative inflammatory arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, myositis, myalgia, skin sclerosis, nephritis and vasculitis, often accompanied by increased autoantibody production.27 Importantly, recent studies suggest that ICS may not only trigger de novo autoimmunity, but also unmask or accelerate the evolution of subclinical, autoantibody-positive disease that might otherwise have remained latent.27,28

As ICIs are increasingly integrated into the treatment of a broader range of tumour types,29 rheumatology must embrace this new clinical paradigm. This shift requires rheumatologists to become comfortable with the complexities of managing patients with active malignancy, including the need for close collaboration with oncology teams to navigate dynamic treatment priorities.

Environmental and lifestyle pressures – a changing world

No discussion of future rheumatology can ignore the external milieu. Environmental exposures are increasingly recognised as major drivers of increasing autoimmune disease prevalence. Occupational exposures represent a well-documented environmental threat, particularly in industrial settings. Substantial evidence links occupational dust and chemical exposure through mining, stonemasonry, building and other related trades to an increased risk of various systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases including RA and systemic sclerosis. Swedish population-based studies demonstrate dose-dependent relationships for silica exposure across multiple autoimmune phenotypes.30

Emerging environmental threats present new challenges. Chronic exposure to particulate matter has been linked to increases the odds of connective tissue disease, inflammatory bowel disease and RA by around 10% for every 10 µg/m³ increase in ambient concentration.31 Contemporary research reveals concerning associations between pesticide exposure and RA among agricultural workers, while microplastics and bisphenol A exposure show potential to aggravate inflammatory processes through cellular mechanisms affecting fibroblast proliferation and migration.32

Metabolic and lifestyle-related factors add to this environmental burden. Rising rates of obesity and its downstream sequelae – osteoarthritis, psoriatic arthritis and metabolic syndrome – highlight the growing interface between adiposity and inflammation.33 The STEP 9 randomised trial demonstrated that weekly 2.4 mg semaglutide led to a 14% weight reduction and a 42-point fall in WOMAC knee pain at 68 weeks, compared with 3% and 28 points with lifestyle advice alone.34 Weight-loss pharmacotherapy therefore represents a credible disease-modifying adjunct for osteoarthritis and potentially inflammatory arthritis, in which adipokine and cytokine signalling drive local and systemic inflammation. Recent evidence further suggests that GLP-1 agonists may exert direct vasculoprotective, antithrombotic and anti-inflammatory effects independent of weight loss, broadening their potential relevance to rheumatology.35

Equity in access to advanced therapies

Access to advanced therapies is often assumed to be equitable within universal healthcare systems, but recent data challenge this assumption. A national cohort analysis study using the National Early Inflammatory Arthritis Audit (NEIAA), a UK-wide audit established by the British Society for Rheumatology in 2014, found that between 2018 and 2023, Asian patients, particularly women, were significantly less likely to receive biologic or targeted synthetic DMARDs within 12 months of RA diagnosis compared to White individuals, even after adjusting for socioeconomic status, comorbidities, disease severity and treatment response.36 Conversely, Black individuals were more likely to receive advanced therapies, largely due to higher baseline disease activity. Age disparities were also striking, patients under 40 were over twice as likely to be initiated on biologics as those over 65. These findings suggest that access is shaped not only by clinical indicators but also by sociocultural beliefs, communication gaps and clinician bias. Ensuring truly equitable prescribing will require culturally tailored education, shared decision-making frameworks, and ongoing scrutiny of real-world data to detect and address gaps in care.

Artificial intelligence – promise and pitfalls

The final disruptor is artificial intelligence (AI). Large language model-driven tools already draft clinic letters and triage patient queries.37 Algorithmic surveillance of laboratory results could automate methotrexate and biologic safety monitoring, flagging abnormalities before annual review.38 Digitally literate patients may prefer conversational chat bots for disease education and self-management support, particularly those in Generation Alpha, who have grown up interacting with screens. Conversely, older individuals may continue to value face to face explanation from a clinician. Augmented diagnostic support – real-time ultrasound interpretation, automated MRI cartilage quantification, phenotypic clustering of electronic health record data – is close to reality.39 The profession must therefore define the tasks best delegated to machines, the ethical boundaries of algorithmic decision making, and the skills that future trainees will require.

However, technological sophistication means little without addressing the fundamental economic constraints that increasingly determine which treatments patients can access.

The price of care

Hand in hand with therapeutic innovation has come spiralling cost. Biologicals accounted for just 9% of the English NHS rheumatology medicines budget in 2004; by 2018 they consumed over 60%. The Russell Lancet analysis of national prescribing data offers a salutary lesson in how that trajectory can be bent. Introduction of biosimilar adalimumab in 2018 reduced annual spend on the molecule by approximately £130 million, freeing sufficient funds to increase overall biological utilisation by 62% without extra cost to the health service.40 Similar savings were observed after infliximab and etanercept lost exclusivity. Looking forward, pay for performance agreements, adaptive licensing and indication-based pricing are likely to become routine. The profession must remain an active partner in these conversations lest affordability become the primary determinant of care. Yet economic pressures represent only one dimension of the external forces reshaping rheumatological practice – environmental and lifestyle factors present equally compelling challenges that demand urgent attention.

Conclusions

By 2050, rheumatology will be characterised by molecularly stratified diseases, affordable targeted therapies, occasional curative cellular interventions and a digital clinical ecosystem. Artificial intelligence will likely support diagnosis, monitoring and education, while biosensor data and personalised algorithms may tailor treatment in real time. Yet, these advances alone will not define the success of our specialty. Our challenge is to harness these advances while safeguarding equity, affordability and the human touch that remains at the heart of patient care.
