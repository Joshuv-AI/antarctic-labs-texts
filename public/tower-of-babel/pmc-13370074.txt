Molecular screening for active severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 infections among non-human primates in Indonesia
Authors: Ni Luh Ayu MEGASARI, Krisnoadi RAHARDJO, Boedi SETIAWAN, Abhirama Zabdika PRASETYA, I Wayan Andama SINDHURANU, Maria Inge LUSIDA, Yasuko MORI, Rima Ratnanggana PRASETYA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSevere acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) poses an ongoing public health concern, and its zoonotic nature increases the risk of cross-species transmission between humans and animals. Non-human primates (NHPs) are particularly susceptible because of the high similarity in angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) critical residues compared to those in humans, especially among catarrhine species. Therefore, surveillance of NHP populations is essential, particularly in Indonesia, a megadiverse nation hosting numerous primate species that frequently interact with humans. In this study, 237 NHPs representing multiple species, including long-tailed macaques (Macaca fascicularis), langurs (Trachypithecus spp.), gibbons (Hylobates spp.), and orangutans (Pongo pygmaeus), were sampled from both in-situ and ex-situ conservation settings across five provinces (Bali, East Java, West Java, Central Kalimantan, and West Sumatra). Paired nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal swabs were tested for SARS-CoV-2 RNA using quantitative real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). No molecular evidence of active SARS-CoV-2 infection was detected in any of the individuals sampled. None of the samples showed amplification of either the N1 or N2 gene targets, and no borderline Ct values (all Ct >35 or undetermined) or single-target reactivity was observed. These findings highlight the importance of ongoing surveillance of Indonesian NHP populations by expanding geographical coverage, increasing the sample sizes, incorporating serological testing, and evaluating additional sample types to better assess potential spillover and spillback risks.Keywords: Indonesia, non-human primates, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2, surveillance, zoonosis

INTRODUCTION

The coronavirus disease (COVID-19) outbreak was declared a global pandemic by the World Health Organization on March 11, 2020. Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) was identified as the causative agent, resulting in approximately 776 million confirmed cases and more than seven million deaths worldwide as of September 2024 [35]. The emergence of SARS-CoV-2 is considered to have involved a zoonotic spillover event, with genomic evidence pointing to a likely origin in bat coronaviruses, possibly through one or more intermediate hosts, although its exact origin remains under investigation [3, 21]. Given its zoonotic origin, concerns have arisen regarding the potential for SARS-CoV-2 transmission between humans and animals.

SARS-CoV-2 infection in humans involves angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) as the cell-entry receptor and the serine protease TMPRSS2 for spike (S) protein priming [15]. Critical amino acid residues in ACE2 determine viral binding affinity, and variations in these residues can influence the susceptibility of species to infection [23]. Non-human primates (NHPs) share close evolutionary relationships with humans, with extensive genetic, physiological, and anatomical similarities [34]. Notably, many NHP species, especially catarrhines (apes and Old World monkeys), possess ACE2 critical residues identical to those in humans, raising concerns regarding their susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 and their potential for bidirectional transmission [12]. This includes several species native to Indonesia, such as long-tailed macaques (Macaca fascicularis), langurs (Trachypithecus spp.), gibbons (Hylobates spp.), and orangutans (Pongo pygmaeus), which have been predicted in silico to have a high binding affinity for the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein [23], with some (e.g., Macaca fascicularis) shown to be experimentally susceptible [19, 30].

SARS-CoV-2 has been reported in a wide range of animal species across 36 countries, including several wildlife populations and zoo animals such as gorillas, tigers, and lions [7, 10, 22]. Serological and molecular investigations have detected SARS-CoV-2 in both free-ranging and captive NHPs, including Mico melanurus [26]. Genomic analyses have suggested possible human-to-NHP transmission [26], underscoring the importance of surveillance programs to prevent spillover into vulnerable and endangered primate populations [12].

Indonesia is a megadiverse nation with one of the world’s richest primate assemblages, including prosimians, Old World monkeys, lesser apes, and great apes distributed across the archipelago [14]. The country has reported more than 6.8 million confirmed COVID-19 cases [36], and pre-vaccination seroepidemiological studies have indicated SARS-CoV-2 exposure levels of up to 35.6% in certain regions [1, 24, 29, 37]. Despite the magnitude of the human infection, animal surveillance remains limited. Previous studies in Indonesia have screened pets (dogs, cats, and birds), livestock (pigs and cattle), and traded wildlife, such as bats, for SARS-CoV-2 antibodies detected in several domestic animal species [17, 32]. However, to date, no surveillance has been conducted in the NHP population. This study investigated the presence of active SARS-CoV-2 infection in non-human primates sampled from multiple regions and conservation settings in Indonesia.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design, ethic approval, and sample collection

This cross-sectional study received ethical approval from multiple Indonesian authorities and institutional committees. Permissions for wildlife handling and sampling were obtained from the Directorate General of Natural Resources and Ecosystem Conservation (approval numbers: SK.91/KSDAE/SET.3/KSA.2/5/2021; SK.963/KSDAE/SET.3/KSA.2/12/2021; SK.106/KSDAE/SET.3/KSA.2/5/2023), the National Research and Innovation Agency (LIPI-BRIN) (approval numbers: B-2422/IV/KS.01.04/3/2021,B-7616/IV/KS.01.04/9/2021, and B-4057/IV/KS.00/4/2023), and the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Universitas Airlangga, Surabaya, Indonesia (ethical approval number: 2. KE.075.06.2021).

Samples were collected between 2021 and 2023 across several regions of Indonesia with substantial NHP populations, including Bali, East Java, West Java, Central Kalimantan, and West Sumatra. Sampling was performed at both in-situ and ex-situ conservation sites (Fig. 1). The two in-situ conservation sites were located in Bali and East Java. Two ex-situ conservation sites were located in East Java, one in West Java, and two in Central Kalimantan. The animals were opportunistically sampled during scheduled health checks, translocation events, or routine management activities. All accessible individuals present at the time of the sampling visit were included regardless of clinical signs. The nature and intensity of human contact varied among settings. In ex-situ facilities, NHP caretakers and veterinarians typically have direct contact with animals for approximately 4–8 hr per day. These sites also receive a limited number of visitors under controlled conditions. In contrast, in-situ sites involve minimal direct human contact, with occasional but regulated visitor presence. Regarding potential SARS-CoV-2 exposure among the staff, no respiratory disease outbreaks or confirmed COVID-19 cases were formally reported at any of the sampling sites near the collection date. It should be noted that, in accordance with national health protocols, staff members testing positive for COVID-19 were required to undergo mandatory isolation, thereby reducing the risk of workplace transmission during the study period.

Anesthesia and sample collection

Adult NHPs were anesthetized with intramuscular ketamine hydrochloride at 5–15 mg/kg [27]. Vital parameters including heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and body temperature were continuously monitored. Paired nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal swabs were aseptically collected, and each animal was closely monitored in a quiet, secure environment until full recovery from anesthesia, as evidenced by normal posture, alertness, and voluntary movement. Only then were they released back into their habitats or enclosures. No adverse events related to anesthesia or sampling were observed in any of the 237 individuals. Swabs were immediately placed in viral transport medium (VTM), transported in cool boxes with dry ice, and stored at −80°C until further analysis.

RNA isolation and detection of SARS-CoV-2 infection

Viral RNA was extracted using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Each RNA extraction batch included a negative control (nuclease-free water) and positive control (synthetic SARS-CoV-2 RNA; 2019-nCoV Positive Control, IDT). An exogenous internal control (human RNase P) in a subset of samples was co-amplified to verify the RNA extraction quality and the absence of PCR inhibitors. SARS-CoV-2 RNA was detected using quantitative real-time reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) with the THUNDERBIRDTM Probe One-Step qRT-PCR Kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)-recommended primers targeting the N1 and N2 regions of the nucleocapsid (N) gene [20]. The CDC N1/N2 assay used was specific to SARS-CoV-2, and based on in silico analysis and previous validation, did not exhibit cross-reactivity with known seasonal coronaviruses [20]. Thermal cycling protocol was as follows: reverse transcription at 55°C for 10 min, initial denaturation at 95°C for 2 min, and 45 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 10 sec and annealing/extension at 60°C for 1 min. The Ct ≤35 cut-off was based on the CDC emergency use authorization guidelines for human diagnostics and was conservatively applied to minimize false positives in this wildlife screening context [20]. Samples were considered positive if both N1 and N2 regions amplified with cycle threshold (Ct) value ≤35.

RESULTS

The 237 NHPs represented four genera: long-tailed macaques (Macaca fascicularis), langurs (Trachypithecus spp.), gibbons (Hylobates spp.), and orangutans (Pongo pygmaeus) (Table 1). Macaca fascicularis samples were collected from two in-situ conservations and one ex-situ conservation site in East Java. Trachypithecus spp. samples were collected from two ex-situ conservation sites in East Java and West Java, and Hylobates spp. samples were collected from two ex-situ conservation sites in West Java and Central Kalimantan. Pongo pygmaeus samples were collected from one ex-situ conservation site in Central Kalimantan.

Sample collection was heavily skewed toward captive/rehabilitated NHPs in ex-situ conservation (186/237; 78.48%), considering the higher probability of contracting COVID-19 through human contact. None of the samples showed amplification of either N1 or N2 targets, with no borderline Ct values (all Ct >35 or undetermined) or single-target reactivity, indicating no active infection.

DISCUSSION

The coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic poses a threat to both humans and wildlife, raising concerns about the potential spillover and spillback of SARS-CoV-2 [2]. To our knowledge, this is the first molecular screening for SARS-CoV-2 in multiple NHP in Indonesia. Most samples were collected from NHPs with a frequent history of human contact, mainly from captive/rehabilitated animals living in ex-situ conservation areas (186/237; 78.48%). This situation likely increases the potential exposure to SARS-CoV-2 through human interactions [13]; however, none of the samples tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 RNA, providing no molecular evidence of active infection.

Our negative findings align with those of several other surveillance studies on captive and wild NHPs that reported no active infections during similar periods [25, 28, 39]. It is important to note, however, that PCR-based surveillance is inherently limited to detecting current infections and does not reflect past exposure or subclinical infection. Therefore, these negative results do not exclude the possibility of SARS-CoV-2 transmission in these populations. Consequently, serological testing remains essential to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the SARS-CoV-2 exposure history and transmission dynamics in NHPs. Additionally, we provided an estimate of the maximum prevalence that was compatible with our data. To quantify the surveillance effort, using the rule of three for zero positives in 237 samples, the upper bound of the 95% confidence interval for the prevalence of active infection in this sampled population is estimated to be ≤1.26%. This quantitative estimate strengthens the interpretation of negative findings and clarifies the level of circulation that may have been missed.

Similar studies have reported negative findings in captive and wild NHPs, including Macaca mulatta from the California National Primate Research Center [39], Brazilian NHPs (Callithrix, Callicebus, and Alouatta) [2, 28], and wild gorillas (Gorilla gorilla), and chimpanzees (Pan troglodytes troglodytes) in Gabon, Central Africa [25].

Two genera of apes, Hylobates and Pongo, and two genera of Old-World monkeys, Macaca and Trachypithecus, were included. In silico studies investigating the binding affinity of various NHP ACE2 receptors to the SARS-CoV-2 spike receptor-binding domain (S-RBD) predicted high susceptibility to COVID-19 in catarrhines (apes and Old-World monkeys), including Pongo abelii, Hylobates moloch, and Macaca fascicularis. Amino acid residues at the critical binding sites for SARS-CoV-2 S-RBD are notably conserved among catarrhines [23]. Laboratory studies confirmed that several NHP species could be successfully infected with SARS-CoV-2, including Macaca fascicularis, Macaca mulatta, Chlorocebus sabaeus, and Papio hamadryas [19, 30, 31, 38]. These studies indicated the possibility that various NHPs species may act as SARS-CoV-2 reservoirs. Despite the inclusion of four genera within the catarrhine parvorder, this study found no evidence of SARS-CoV-2 RNA in the samples.

Infection dynamics may vary among NHPs species. Initial research suggested lower SARS-CoV-2 susceptibility in platyrrhines than in catarrhines, but a recent report showed natural infection in a common woolly monkey (Lagothrix lagothricha) from a wildlife rehabilitation center in Ecuador [9]. New World monkeys are also predicted to have low susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 [23]. Experimental studies have confirmed that Callithrix jacchus, a New World monkey species, exhibits lower susceptibility to infection, as characterized by mild symptoms and rapid clearance of viral RNA [19, 31]. In contrast, naturally occurring SARS-CoV-2 infections have been reported in black-tailed marmosets (Mico melanurus) from an urban area of Brazil [26]. Animal-to-human transmission has also been suggested in brown-headed spider monkeys (Ateles fusciceps) at a wildlife rescue center in Ecuador [5]. Further studies in more NHP species are needed to investigate SARS-CoV-2 infection dynamics in different NHP species.

The results of this study suggest that no active SARS-CoV-2 infection was detected in the NHPs enrolled during the study period. However, the possibility of past infection could not be excluded and could be determined by serological tests to detect the presence of SARS-CoV-2-related immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies. The detection of anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike (S) or nucleocapsid (N) IgG antibodies provides information regarding previous infection [11, 18, 24]. A study in Spain identified antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 N and S proteins in two captive gorillas [4]; therefore, the detection of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), virus neutralization test (VNT), or plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT) should be considered [4, 13].

The absence of detectable infections in this study may reflect the effectiveness of biosafety and biosecurity protocols implemented at the sampled conservation sites. During the pandemic, many Indonesian wildlife centers enforced enhanced protocols, including the mandatory use of personal protective equipment (PPE) such as masks and gloves by staff, hand sanitization, visitor restrictions, health screening for animal caretakers, and quarantine procedures for newly arrived animals. Furthermore, during field sampling, all research personnel were required to provide negative SARS-CoV-2 PCR test results and undergo a 10-day quarantine period before entering any facility or engaging in animal handling. Collectively, these measures, designed to safeguard endangered species, are likely to play a crucial role in mitigating the risk of human-to-NHP transmission, even in settings with regular human contact, and should be documented and encouraged as part of One Health strategies.

In our study, all paired nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal swabs were consistently negative, suggesting no apparent advantage of one sample type over the other in asymptomatic animals. However, collecting both swab sample types remains a prudent and recommended strategy to maximize detection sensitivity, especially if viral shedding patterns differ between the respiratory compartments.

However, molecular detection has several limitations. Mutations in the target region may interfere with PCR results, and viral loads lower than the detection limit may produce false-negative results [16]. A study of captive and semi-captive Cebus unicolor in the Peruvian Amazon demonstrated negative PCR results; however, the SARS-CoV-2 genome sequence was successfully detected using a metagenomic sequencing approach [33]. A serological study found approximately 1.6% positive samples [4]; therefore, increasing the number of samples for SARS-CoV-2 screening may also be beneficial. SARS-CoV-2 has also been detected using various samples [5, 9, 26, 33], including stool and tissue samples. Assessing various sample types may also enhance SARS-CoV-2 screening in NHPs.

Our study was inherently limited to detecting active infections at a single time point. The high proportion of samples from the ex-situ setting (78.5%) suggests that our findings may not fully represent the risk in wholly wild, inaccessible populations where human contact is rare; however, spillover from other wildlife or environmental sources could occur. Serological surveys are needed to uncover past exposure.

Considering Indonesia’s vast biodiversity, the sample collection in this study was concentrated in five provinces (Bali, East Java, West Java, Central Kalimantan, and West Sumatra) out of thirty-eight. NHPs in other regions may exhibit different epidemiological profiles that could influence SARS-CoV-2 infectivity. The findings of this study contribute to SARS-CoV-2 surveillance in Indonesian NHPs species, especially in those that are endangered, such as orangutan [8]. Approximately 30 NHPs species in Indonesia are projected to become extinct by 2050 due to climate change [6], highlighting the importance of disease prevention, including COVID-19, to preserve their survival. Continuous surveillance of infection in NHPs remains critical because of the high mutation rate and capacity of the virus to adapt to new hosts. Expanding the surveillance regions, increasing sample sizes, testing diverse sample types, and incorporating both serological and molecular techniques are recommended.

Conclusion

This study found no molecular evidence of active SARS-CoV-2 infection in Indonesian NHPs, despite their predicted susceptibility and frequent human contact in ex-situ settings. Continued surveillance—expanded across regions, sample types, and incorporating serological approaches—remains essential to clarify transmission dynamics and safeguard endangered species.
