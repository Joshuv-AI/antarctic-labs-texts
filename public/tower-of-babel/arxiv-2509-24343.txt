Abstract
We study the parametrized complexity of fundamental relations between multidimensional subshifts, such as equality, conjugacy, inclusion, and embedding, for subshifts of finite type (SFTs) and effective subshifts. We build on previous work of E. Jeandel and P. Vanier on the complexity of these relations as two-input problems, by fixing one subshift as parameter and taking the other subshift as input. We study the impact of various dynamical properties related to periodicity, minimality, finite type, etc. on the computational properties of the parameter subshift, which reveals interesting differences and asymmetries. Among other notable results, we find choices of parameter that reach the maximum difficulty for each problem; we find nontrivial decidable problems for multidimensional SFT, where most properties are undecidable; and we find connections with recent work relating having computable language and being minimal for some property, showing in particular that this property may not always be chosen conjugacy-invariant.

Introduction

A subshift or shift space is a collection of colorings of the
d
-dimensional grid
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(
d\geq 1
) by a finite set of colors and avoiding a set of forbidden patterns. We are interested in subshifts of finite type (SFT) and effective subshifts, which can be defined by a set of forbidden patterns which is finite or recursively enumerable, respectively.

Subshifts, in particular SFT, have received a lot of interest as a source of a great variety of natural undecidable problems, starting from the Domino problem. In
[
11
]
, Jeandel and Vanier described the computational difficult of a number of relations between subshifts: equality, conjugacy, inclusion, and embedding. We are interested in the parametrized version of the same problems, that is when one subshift is fixed and the other is given as input.

Conventions.
We denote by
Y
the parameter subshift, which is fixed beforehand, and by
X
the input subshift, which ranges over the class of SFTs or the class of effective subshifts. The integer
d
always refer to the dimension.

In this paper, we study the following problems:

X=Y

’

X\simeq Y

’

X\subseteq Y

’

X\hookrightarrow Y

’

Y\subseteq X

’

Y\hookrightarrow X

’

where the symbol
\simeq
indicates the existence of a topological conjugacy, and the symbol
\hookrightarrow
indicates the existence of a topological embedding.

One motivation for this approach is to understand the relationship between the computational difficulty of these problems, seen as properties of
Y
, and its dynamical and combinatorial properties. While the two-input version of each problem has a single difficulty,
some choices of parameter
Y
make the problem as difficult as possible (as the two-input version) or, conversely, make the problem easier, and we manage to describe and sometimes characterise which properties of
Y
impact which problem. Strikingly, these properties vary a lot depending on the considered problem: periodicity, minimality, finiteness, being of finite type, etc. For example, we find a connection between our results on
X\simeq Y
and the recent work
[
1
]
, which links computability and minimality properties of
Y
(see
Section

8
). This new finer perspective provides more information on the relationship between dynamical and computational properties of shifts.

A second motivation to study these parametrized problems is their connection to Rice-like theorems for properties of SFTs
[
8
,
9
,
12
,
7
]
, which prove that most relevant properties of SFTs are undecidable, as expressed in the well-known metaphor “swamp of undecidability” from
[
13
]
. While most properties that we consider can be easily shown to be undecidable thanks to these results, we do find some nontrivial decidable problems for SFT inputs (
Y\subseteq X
and
Y\hookrightarrow X
for some choices of
Y
), highlighting limits of these generic Rice-type theorems. Providing a complete characterisation of decidable problems for SFT seems a worthy long-term goal.

We have chosen to omit sofic subshifts from our results for space reasons, though we expect that all results on effective subshifts on dimension
d=1
are also valid for sofic subshifts on dimension
d\geq 2
. This follows from classical simulation results
[
3
,
10
]
which are explained in detail in
[
11
, Section 1.3]
. We also expect that many of our arguments can be extended to subshifts over more general finitely generated groups with decidable word problem (and in some cases, undecidable domino problem), but we have chosen to restrict our study to
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
as more tools are available.

After providing some basic background and notations in
Section

2
, we give some general upper and lower bounds on the problems that are either direct or follow from existing results in
Section

3
. In
Section

4
we study the problem
Y\subset X
, and characterize its complexity in terms of the Turing degree of
\mathcal{L}(Y)
. In
Section

5
we study the problem
Y\hookrightarrow X
, which is also decidable for some
Y
, but our conditions are somewhat technical and a full characterization seem more difficult. In
Section

6
we study the problems
X\subset Y
and
X\hookrightarrow Y
where finite type plays a role. In
Section

7
we study the equality problem
X=Y
, and in
Section

8
we study the conjugacy problem
X\simeq Y
and its relationship with
[
1
]
. Finally, in
Section

9
we make a few observations about the one-dimensional case, its relationship to the open problem of conjugacy, and lifts to higher dimensional subshifts.

Preliminaries

2.1
Subshifts

Let
d\geq 1
and let
\mathcal{A}
be a finite alphabet. We endow
\mathcal{A}
with the discrete topology and
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}=\prod_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{A}=\{x\colon\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathcal{A}\}

\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
are called
configurations
. We also consider the continuous left action
\mathbb{Z}^{d}\curvearrowright\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
by
shift translations
. Given

and

we define its shift or translation

\sigma^{u}(x)(v)=x(v+u)
,

.

A

is a function
p\colon S\to A
, where

is finite. We say that

appears
or

such that
\sigma^{u}(x)|_{S}=p
.
A configuration

is

if its orbit
\{\sigma^{u}(x):u\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
is finite; intuitively,

can be described by a finite pattern repeated periodically following
d
linearly independant period vectors.

A

is a (possibly empty) subset of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
which is topologically closed and invariant by shift translations. The subshift
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
is called the
fullshift
on alphabet
\mathcal{A}
.
Alternatively, any subshift can be defined by forbidding a set of patterns
\mathcal{F}
: we define

X_{\mathcal{F}}=\{x\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}:\text{no pattern from $\mathcal{F}$ appears in $x$}\}.

A subshift

is said to have

(
SFT
for short) if there is a finite set
\mathcal{F}
such that
X=X_{\mathcal{F}}
. A subshift is
effective
when
X=X_{\mathcal{F}}
for a
\Sigma_{1}^{0}
set
\mathcal{F}
. The class of SFTs is properly contained in the class of effective subshifts.

A subshift is
minimal
if it contains no nonempty subshifts other than itself.

The
language

\mathcal{L}(X)
of a subshift
X
is the set of all patterns that appear in some configuration in
X
, and
\mathcal{L}^{c}(X)
is its complement. We remark that
X
is effective exactly when
\mathcal{L}(X)
is a
\Pi_{1}^{0}
set.
We also define

\mathcal{L}_{n}(X)\subseteq\mathcal{L}(X)
as the subset of patterns with support
\{0,\dots,n-1\}^{d}
.

2.2
Embeddings and conjugacies

A function

\forall u\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},\ F(x)(u)=f((\sigma^{u}(x))|_{D}).

(1)

The

The

An important property is that

whenever
X\subseteq Y
of when
X
embeds into

We say that

when it has positive entropy and any proper subsystem has strictly lower entropy.

3
General upper and lower bounds

The goal of this section is to review general upper and lower bounds for the parametrized problems of interest. By identifying simple cases, where the complexity of the parametrized problem can be derived from general facts, we also find more interesting cases that we handle in future sections.

We start by reviewing hardness results. From
[
7
]
we have some general tools to prove hardness results for dynamical properties of SFTs in dimension
d\geq 2
.

Definition 3.1
.

A property
\mathcal{P}
of SFTs is a
Berger
property if there are two SFTs
X_{-}
and
X_{+}
such that

1.

X_{+}
satisfies
\mathcal{P}
;

2.

Every SFT that factors on
X_{-}
does not satisfy
\mathcal{P}
;

3.

There is a morphism
X_{+}\to X_{-}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
d\geq 2
.
Every Berger property of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-SFTs is
\Sigma_{1}^{0}
-hard.

This result was stated in
[
7
]
under the assumption that the property is preserved by conjugacy, but this assumption is in fact not necessary. We provide a proof of this fact in
Appendix

A
.

For effective subshifts we have a stronger result on dimension
d\geq 1
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
d\geq 1
. Every nontrivial property
\mathcal{P}
of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-effective subshifts is undecidable. More precisely,
\mathcal{P}
is
\Sigma_{1}^{0}
-hard if the empty subshift satisfies
\mathcal{P}
and
\Pi_{1}^{0}
-hard otherwise.

Proof.

See Appendix
B
.
∎

In the case of SFT inputs,
Theorem

3.2
provides a lower bound for four out the six parametrized problems mentioned in the introduction. Remark that the next result puts no assumptions on the parameter subshift (such as being SFT or effective).

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
d\geq 2
. Let
Y
be a
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-subshift. The following problems with SFT inputs are either
\Sigma_{1}^{0}
-hard, or trivial.

X=Y

’

X\simeq Y

’

X\subseteq Y

’

X\hookrightarrow Y

’

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the alphabet for
Y
. For
X\subseteq Y
and
X\hookrightarrow Y
we choose
X_{+}=\emptyset
and
X_{-}
the full shift on
|\mathcal{A}|+1
symbols. For
X=Y
and
X\simeq Y
when
Y
is not SFT, note that no SFT
X
satisfy the property, so the property is trivial. If
Y
is an SFT, then we choose
X_{+}=Y
and
X_{-}
the direct product of
Y
with a fullshift on two symbols. In all four cases, basic properties of topological entropy for subshifts ensure that the conditions from the definition of Berger property are met, and therefore the hardness claim follows from
Theorem

3.2
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Let
Y
be a subshift which is not of finite type, and consider the problem

on input an SFT
X
. In this case the answer is “no” on every input, so the problem is decidable. This often occurs when the parameter subshift does not belong to the class of input subshifts, so it is important to exclude these trivial cases from undecidability results.

As mentioned in the introduction, hardness results for
Y\subseteq X
and
Y\hookrightarrow X
with SFT inputs cannot be obtained with the same direct arguments. We study these problems in
Section

4
and
Section

5
.

When the input subshift ranges over effective subshifts the situation is different, and there are no longer non-trivial decidable cases. The following is a direct consequence of
Theorem

.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
d\geq 1
and let
Y
be a
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-subshift. The following problems with effective inputs are either trivial,
\Sigma_{1}^{0}
-hard, or
\Pi_{1}^{0}
-hard.

X=Y

’

X\simeq Y

’

X\subseteq Y

’

X\hookrightarrow Y

’

Y\subseteq X

’

Y\hookrightarrow X

’

This lower bound is not optimal in general, we are interested in this document in understanding the exact complexity of these problems and how it depends on the parameter

.

4
The inclusion problem
\mathbf{Y\subseteq X}

As we mentioned before, Rice-like theorems can not be applied to prove general lower bounds for
Y\subseteq X
with SFT inputs, and in fact this problem can be decidable for some choices of
Y
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
Y
be a
subshift. Then the problem
Y\subseteq X
on SFT inputs is Turing-equivalent to
\mathcal{L}(Y)
.

Proof.

Recall that every set is Turing-equivalent to its complement; thus we work with
\mathcal{L}^{c}(Y)
. In order to see that
\mathcal{L}^{c}(Y)\leq_{m}(Y\subseteq X)
, note that a pattern
p
does not appear in
Y
if and only if
Y\subseteq X_{\{p\}}
. For the other reduction, let
X
be a
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-SFT given as a finite set of forbidden patterns
\mathcal{F}
. Then
Y\subseteq X
if and only if all patterns in
\mathcal{F}
belong to
\mathcal{L}^{c}(Y)
(the second reduction is not a many-one reduction, but a “truth-table” reduction).
∎

This result generalizes
[
7
, Propoisition 6.2]
which proves that the problem
Y\subseteq X
for SFT inputs is decidable exactly when
Y
has computable language.

In the next result we are able to classify the possible difficulties of this problem when
Y
is a
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-SFT,
d\geq 2
. Namely, this is the class of all computably enumerable Turing degrees (all Turing degrees of
\Sigma_{1}^{0}
subsets of
\mathbb{N}
).

Theorem 4.2
.

The class of Turing degrees of languages of SFTs on dimension
d\geq 2
equals the class of computably enumerable Turing degrees.

Proof.

We fix
d=2
. Since the language of a
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-SFT is always a
\Pi_{1}^{0}
set, its Turing degree is a computably enumerable degree. Therefore to prove our claim it suffices to show that for every
\Pi_{1}^{0}
set
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
we can find a
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-SFT
Y_{S}
such that
\mathcal{L}(Y_{S})
and
S
are Turing-equivalent. Consider the subshift
X_{S}\subset\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
defined by the set of forbidden patterns
\{01^{n}0:n\notin S\}
. Then
X_{S}
is effective and
\mathcal{L}(X_{S})
is Turing-equivalent to
S
. By
[
3
]
, there is a
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-SFT
Y_{S}
that simulates
X_{S}
; without stating their result formally, we only stress that there is a computable surjection
\mathcal{L}(X_{S})\to\mathcal{L}(Y_{S})
(projective subdynamics) and, as shown in
[
5
, Section 5]
, there is a computable
reconstruction function
that allows one to compute
\mathcal{L}(Y_{S})
from
\mathcal{L}(X_{S})
, so that
\mathcal{L}(Y_{S})
and
\mathcal{L}(X_{S})
are Turing-equivalent.
∎

In the previous proofs we are not able to obtain a many-one equivalence, so we ask the following question.

Question 4.3
.

Which many-one degrees are achieved by the problem

with SFT inputs?

For effective inputs, the upper bound is reached.
We are indebted to Mathieu Hoyrup (personal communication) for the following proof.

Theorem 4.4
.

There exists an SFT

such that the problem

-complete.

A number

be an computable enumeration of all left-c.e. numbers.

First we prove that

is
\Pi^{0}_{2}
-hard. We reduce the problem

. Given

a Turing machine, let

the Turing machine which simulate

, then

, and so on. Let

be such that

where

halts in the first

steps of the computation of

. It is clear that

.

Next we prove that

\Pi^{0}_{2}
-hard, reducing

. Given

, we can compute

and

(

[

, Definition 3.2.28]
). Then we compute

. One has

.

Finally, let

be the SFT which performs the following computation as in

as input, compute the successive approximations

of
\Omega
and check that for all

We prove that

is
\Pi^{0}_{2}
-hard reducing the set

. For any

, let

be the subshift of

5
The embedding problem
\mathbf{Y\hookrightarrow X}

We are mainly interested in the case of SFT inputs, as this problem has a nontrivial boundary between the decidable and undecidable cases (with an upper bound of
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
by
Theorem

3.7
), while it is always undecidable (
\Pi^{0}_{1}
-hard) for effective inputs by
Theorem

. We are not able to characterize which properties of

makes this problem decidable, but exhibit sufficient conditions for decidability and undecidability.

We start with sufficient conditions for
Y\hookrightarrow X
to be undecidable.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assume that
Y
contains a configuration that is not strongly periodic, but is strongly periodic outside of a finite region. Then
Y\hookrightarrow X
for SFT inputs is
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-hard.

Proof.

We reduce the halting problem on empty input. Let
M
be a Turing machine given as input for the halting problem. Let
y_{p}\in Y
be the configuration given by the hypothesis: there is a finite set
C
such that
y_{p}
is strongly periodic outside
C
with period
p\in\mathcal{A}^{n\times n}
.

X
is the following SFT with
|\mathcal{A}|^{n^{2}}+1
layers:

•

the first layer is a configuration in
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
;

•

each extra layer

is associated with one pattern

. When

appears on the first layer with support
[a,a+n-1]\times[b,b+n-1]
, a computation starts at position
(a,b)
on layer

(to allow for pattern

overlapping with itself).
The computation is halted if another occurrence of
p_{i}
appears in the computing area.

Any configuration

on the first layer can be extended to a configuration

by starting computations wherever required and, on each extra layer, leaving all cells with no computation empty. Any configuration of
X
is obtained in this manner with possible additional computations with no starting point (coming from infinity).

Suppose first that
M
halts, and prove that
Y\hookrightarrow X
for SFT inputs. Define

by

which is clearly injective. This function is local: if

halts in
t

can determine which computation occurs on every layer from the value of

in a

Suppose now that
Y\hookrightarrow X
for SFT inputs and prove that

halts. Let

be local and injective. Because

is local,

is strongly periodic of periods

and
(0,n)
when far away from

; and because

is injective,

is not strongly periodic. Note that if the first layer of

was strongly periodic, then

would be either strongly periodic or not even strongly periodic outside of a finite region (because of computations coming from infinity); therefore the first layer of

cannot be strongly periodic. Therefore there is some pattern in the first layer that occurs in

and not in the strongly periodic region. This pattern has a rightmost uppermost occurrence that triggers a computation which is never truncated. Since

is strongly periodic away from

∎

It is remarkable that the previous result makes no use of any global property of

, but only of the presence of a single configuration. However, this periodic structure is not needed, as the following example has no such configuation.

Theorem 5.2
.

The problem
Rob\hookrightarrow X
for SFT inputs is
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-complete, where

is the Robinson SFT without computation (see
[

]
).

Proof.

Let

be the Robinson SFT with a second layer on alphabet

. Each computation macrotile is cut diagonally into a northwest red half and a southeast blue half; it is possible to do this in an SFT manner, by checking that each southwest and northeast corner are the ends of a red/blue diagonal border, and because the diagonal of a macrotile crosses sub-tiles along the diagonal. Clearly there is no morphism

since, for any

and

, you can find positions

such that
x_{i+\llbracket-r,r\rrbracket^{2}}=x_{j+\llbracket-r,r\rrbracket^{2}}
, but

and

are in the northwest, resp. southeast, halves of their macrotiles.

We reduce the problem
Halt
. Given a Turing machine
M
as input, let
X_{M}
be the Robinson SFT in which we simulate
M
as in
[
17
]
, except that the computation is allowed to halt and the computation zone after halting remains empty.

also contains a red/blue layer as in

, except that the layer contains an empty symbol

when the cell contains no computation.

Suppose that
M
halts in

that, to each

. Conversely, if

.

For the next result, we recall that a subshift is finite if and only if all configurations are strongly periodic
[
4

.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let

be a finite
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-subshift (

). Then

for SFT inputs is decidable.

Proof.

Since

is finite, we find a common set of linearly independent periods
v_{1},\dots,v_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for all configurations; that is,
\sigma^{v}(y)=y
for every

and all

. Given as input an SFT

, we add finitely many forbidden patterns to build the (finite) SFT

Then

embeds into

if and only if

embeds into

, and the last question is decidable as both systems are finite.
∎

We might expect that

is always more difficult than the corresponding inclusion problem

\mathcal{L}(Y)

Theorem 5.4

There exists an effective
\mathbb{Z}
-subshift

is not computable (and therefore

Let

Theorem 6.1

Let

\Sigma_{1}^{0}
-complete for SFT/effective inputs.

The upper bound follows from

Therefore we shall be interested in taking a parameter subshift which is not an SFT. If the parameter

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
Y
be an effective subshift which is not an SFT. Then the problem

on effective inputs is
\Pi_{2}^{0}
-complete.

Proof.

Let

be the alphabet for

We reduce the problem
Total
. Given a Turing machine

, we describe a new Turing machine

as follows. On input

simulates

if and only if

Given a Turing machine

, consider the effective subshift

on alphabet

.

The maximal complexity for inclusion can be achieved even for SFT inputs. Notice that the same problem is
\Pi^{0}_{1}

Let

Theorem 6.4
.

In dimension

, there exists an effective subshift
Y
such that

on SFT inputs is
\Sigma^{0}_{3}
-complete.

Proof.

Fix the injective function

.
Let
Y
be an SFT that simulates a computation, as in Figure
1
, of a fixed Turing machine

. On an input of the form

written in unary (except the

(where

is, by an abuse of notation, the code of the Turing machine), writes its value in unary with a special symbol, and then simulates

. The computation halts if the input is finite and malformed, and the halting symbol is forbidden. We distinguish input configurations (containing an input), finite-input configurations, and inputless configurations.
All configurations have three other layers (black, red, green) each containing at most one vertical line, and finite-input configurations have one black, red and green line at positions
0,o(M,n)
and
2o(M,n)
, respectively, from the start of the input.
The subshift

is effective; unicity of each line is enforced by ”left” and ”right” symbols, positions of the black and red line are enforced in an SFT manner (using the special symbol written by

), and the only non-SFT constraint is to enforce the position of the green line.

We reduce the problem
COF
. Given a Turing machine

, let

be as

except that the input is forced to start with

and there is no green line.

is an SFT.

Suppose that

. There exists only finitely many finite-input configurations in

. We define an embedding

which adds a green line as the symmetric to the black line relative to the red line at distance at most

, where

is the longest halting input of

, and is the identity everywhere else.

If

, suppose for a contradiction that there exist an embedding

of radius

. If

is an input configuration with no unneeded lines, then

is
(0,1)
-periodic everywhere except on the upper-right quarterplane, so

Because

is injective, we find arbitrarily large inputs

Theorem

3.4
shows that the equality problem for SFT inputs is
\Sigma_{1}^{0}
-complete as long as
Y
is an SFT (otherwise, output “no” for every input). Therefore we are interested in the equality problem with effective inputs. We also assume that the parameter
Y
is nonempty; determining whether an effective subshift is empty is a
\Sigma_{1}^{0}
-complete problem.

Recall from
Theorem

3.9
that the equality problem with two effective inputs is
\Pi_{2}^{0}
-complete, so this is the general upper bound for the parametrized problem. In the next result we show that
D(\Sigma_{1}^{0})
is always a lower bound.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
Y
be a nonempty effective subshift. Then the equality problem
X=Y
for effective inputs is
D(\Sigma^{0}_{1})
-hard.

Proof.

Let
DP
be the Domino problem for
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-effective subshifts. It is well-known that
DP
is
\Pi_{1}^{0}
-complete in any dimension

. This implies that

is
D(\Sigma_{1}^{0})
-complete.

Given a nonempty effective subshift

, we proceed by reduction to the problem
DP^{c}\times DP
. Let
(Z_{1},Z_{2})
be a pair of effective subshifts. Define

, where

is a fresh symbol, which is an effective subshift. Let

be the projection to the second coordinate, and put

. Since effective subshifts are closed by factor maps
[
2
]
,

is an effective subshift. This process is computable in the sense that one can computably enumerate a set of forbidden patterns for

from

. Therefore:

X=\begin{cases}Y\ &Z_{1}=\emptyset,\ Z_{2}\neq\emptyset\\
\{\#\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\ &Z_{1}\neq\emptyset,\ Z_{2}=\emptyset\\
\emptyset\ &Z_{1}=\emptyset,\ Z_{2}=\emptyset\\
Y\cup\{\#\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\ &Z_{1}\neq\emptyset,\ Z_{2}\neq\emptyset\end{cases}

In particular
X=Y
if and only if
Z_{1}=\emptyset
and
Z_{2}\neq\emptyset
.
∎

Recall from

that the conjugacy problem with two effective inputs is
\Sigma_{3}^{0}

Theorem 8.1
.

Let

. There is a
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-SFT

for effective inputs is
\Sigma^{0}_{3}
-complete.

Proof.

We prove the result for

. Let

\{20,21\}

either belong to
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
, to
\{2\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
, or are a sequence of symbols in
\{0,1\}
followed by infinitely many

with effective inputs is
\Sigma_{3}^{0}
-complete. using a reduction to the problem
COF

Given a Turing machine
M
we compute below a Turing machine

such that:

1.

M\in\textbf{COF}\Leftrightarrow M^{\prime}\in\textbf{COF}
.

2.

When

then the cardinality of
\{n\in\mathbb{N}:M^{\prime}(n)\uparrow\}
is a power of two.

3.

When

then
\{n\in\mathbb{N}:M^{\prime}(n)\uparrow\}
contains no arithmetic progression.

Let
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a computable enumeration of all finite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
,
A=\{2^{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
(or any infinite computable set that does not contain

nor any infinite arithmetic progression), and

the increasing computable bijection. Define

as follows:

•

if
n\not\in A
, then
M^{\prime}(n)\downarrow
;

•

if
n\in A
, then
M^{\prime}(n)
halts if and only if

for some

.

In this manner
\{n\in\mathbb{N}:M^{\prime}(n)\uparrow\}
is a subset of
A
and is in bijection with the finite subsets of
\{x\in\mathbb{N}:M(x)\uparrow\}
; in particular, if it is finite, its size is a power of two. Thus
M^{\prime}
satisfies the desired properties.

Given a Turing machine
M
we compute an effective subshift
X_{M}\subset\{0,1,2\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
defined by the set of forbidden words
\{20,21\}\cup\{01^{n}2:M^{\prime}(n)\downarrow\}
. Every configuration in
X_{M}
can be written as either
{}^{\omega}\{0,1\}^{\omega}
;
{}^{\omega}2^{\omega}
;
{}^{\omega}12^{\omega}
; or
{}^{\omega}\{0,1\}01^{n}2^{\omega}
for some
n
with
M^{\prime}(n)\uparrow
. Note that, for all
n\geq 0
,
01^{n}2\in\mathcal{L}(X_{M})
if and only if
M^{\prime}(n)\uparrow
.

We now prove that
M\in\textbf{COF}
if and only if
X_{M}\simeq Y
.

First, assume that
M\not\in\textbf{COF}
. Since
M^{\prime}\not\in\textbf{COF}
, we have
01^{n}2\in\mathcal{L}(X_{M})
for infinitely many values of
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume that
X_{M}
is an SFT to reach a contradiction. Since the language of an
\mathbb{Z}
-SFT is regular
[
14
]
, we use the pumping lemma for regular languages and find an infinite arithmetic progression
P\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that
01^{n}2\in\mathcal{L}(X_{M})
for all
n\in P
. This implies that
P\subset\{n\in\mathbb{N}:M^{\prime}(n)\uparrow\}
, which is a contradiction with Property 2. Therefore
X_{M}
is not SFT and cannot be conjugate to
Y
.

Now assume that
M\in\textbf{COF}
. Then
\{n\in\mathbb{N}:M^{\prime}(n)\uparrow\}
is finite, and its cardinality is a power of two, say,
2^{\ell}
. Enumerate this set in increasing order as
\{n_{1},\dots,n_{2^{\ell}}\}
.
Let
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{2^{\ell}}\}
be the enumeration of all words in
\{0,1\}^{\ell}
in lexicographic order. Define a map
F\colon X_{M}\to Y
as follows: if
x\in X_{M}
contains
01^{n_{i}}2
for some
i=1,\dots,2^{\ell}
, then
F(x)
is the same configuration where
01^{n_{i}}2^{\ell}
is replaced by
w_{i}2^{1+n_{i}}
(which has the same length); otherwise,
F(x)=x
. The map
F
is well-defined because every
x\in X_{M}
contains at most one occurrence of a word
01^{n_{i}}2
for
i=1,\dots,2^{\ell}
, and it is immediate that
F
is a conjugacy.
∎

We provide a lower bound for SFT parameters.

Theorem 8.2
.

Let
Y
be a nonempty SFT. Then the conjugacy problem
X\simeq Y
with effective inputs is
D(\Sigma_{1}^{0})
-hard.

Proof.

Let

be a
D(\Sigma_{1}^{0})
-complete set, and
B
and

be
\Sigma_{1}^{0}
-sets such that
C\subseteq B
and
A=B\smallsetminus C
.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
X_{n}
on alphabet
\{0,1\}
as follows: if
n\in B
, we forbid the symbol

, and if
n\in C
, we forbid the symbol

. This set of forbidden patterns is recursively enumerable from

, so
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a computable sequence of effective subshifts.

X_{n}=\begin{cases}\{1\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}&\ n\in B,\ n\notin C\\
\emptyset&\ n\in B,\ n\in C\\
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}&\ n\notin B,\ n\notin C\end{cases}

Notice that
Y\simeq Y\times X_{n}
exactly when
|X_{n}|=1
(as
Y\times X_{n}
is otherwise empty or has too much entropy). This is equivalent to

, which is a
D(\Sigma_{1}^{0})
-complete problem.
∎

We now examine dynamical conditions that may reduce the complexity of the problem.

Theorem 8.3
.

Let
Y
be an SFT that is
P
-minimal for inclusion for a
\Pi^{0}_{1}
, monotonous, conjugacy-invariant SFT property

. In other words:

•

If an SFT

satisfies

then any SFT conjugate to
X
or containing

satisfies
P
;

•

Y
satisfies
P
, and any strict subshift
X\subsetneq Y
does not satisfy
P
;

•

There is an algorithm that, given a finite set of forbidden patterns, halts if and only if the associated SFT does not satisfy
P
.

Then the problem
X\simeq Y
with effective inputs is
D(\Sigma_{1}^{0})
.

Proof.

Let
Y
be an SFT as in the statement, and let
X
be an effective subshift given as input. For all
n
, let
X_{n}
be the SFT obtained by forbidding all the forbidden patterns of
X
that we can computably enumerate in time
n
. We claim that
X
and
Y
are conjugate if and only if
(\exists n\ X_{n}\simeq Y)\land(\forall m,\ P(X_{m}))
.

Indeed, if
X
and

are conjugate then
X
is an SFT and so
\exists n\ X\simeq X_{n}
. Since
X\subseteq X_{m}
for all

and

is monotonous, so

holds for all

.

Conversely, suppose that the formula above holds, and fix

large enough so that

is conjugate to

. Since

is conjugacy-invariant,

satisfies

and every proper subshift of

fails to satisfy

. If

, then

is a proper subshift of

for

large enough, so

fails to satisfy

.
∎

(

]
)
.

We find a relationship between

and results in

9
Conjugacy and embedding in dimension
1

Given two
\mathbb{Z}
-SFTs as inputs, their equality and inclusion problems are easily seen to be decidable. In contrast, the decidability status of the conjugacy problem is a famous open question, and the embedding problem is probably very difficult as well (see
Theorem

9.4
). Therefore we cannot hope to find a
\mathbb{Z}
-SFT whose conjugacy or embedding problems for SFT inputs are undecidable. Still, obtaining decidability results for one-dimensional subshifts is useful, particularly for the
Y\hookrightarrow X
problem, because they yield decidability results for higher-dimensional subshifts obtained by taking lifts, as the next result shows.

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
Y^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
be a
\mathbb{Z}
-subshift. Let
\pi\colon\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{Z}
be any surjective group homomorphism, and consider the lift
Y=\{(y(\pi(g))_{g\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}:y\in Y^{\prime}\}
.

The problem
Y\hookrightarrow X
with SFT (resp. effective) inputs is many-one equivalent to
Y^{\prime}\hookrightarrow X
with SFT (resp. effective) inputs.
The same result holds for
Y\subseteq X
.

Proof.

Given a subshift
X
, consider its periodic part
X_{p}=\{x\in X:gx=x\ \forall g\in\ker(\pi)\}
.
X_{p}
is a subshift which is effective, resp. SFT, when
X
is. Notice that
Y_{p}=Y
. Furthermore,
X_{p}
is the lift of the subshift
W_{X}=\{y\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}:(y(\pi(g))_{g\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\in X\}

which is, again, effective/SFT when
X
is.
Since the periods of a configuration are preserved by an embedding, it follows that
Y\hookrightarrow X
if and only if
Y\hookrightarrow X_{p}
, which is equivalent to
Y^{\prime}\hookrightarrow W_{X}
. A similar argument proves the other direction, and holds for
\subseteq
.
∎

Remark 9.2
.

The previous proof fails for
X\hookrightarrow Y
(and other problems) because
X\hookrightarrow Y
if and only if
X_{p}\hookrightarrow Y
and
X=X_{p}
; however, the second condition is undecidable in general.

Lifts of
\mathbb{Z}
-SFTs with decidable embedding problem form a class of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-SFTs with decidable embedding problem. Unfortunately, we are not able to answer the following question.

Question 9.3
.

For which
\mathbb{Z}
-SFTs
Y
is the problem
Y\hookrightarrow X
with SFT inputs decidable?

This problem is decidable for NMC SFTs, defined in
[
16
]
. This is a large class in that it is dense in the space of
\mathbb{Z}
-subshifts for a suitable topology
[
16
, Theorem 3.6]
.

We expect
9.3
to be difficult, as it can be reduced to the (non-uniform) conjugacy problem for SFTs in some cases.

Theorem 9.4
.

Let
Y
be a nonempty mixing
\mathbb{Z}
-SFT. The conjugacy problem
Y\simeq X
with SFT inputs admits a many-one reduction to the embedding problem
Y\hookrightarrow X
with SFT inputs.

Proof.

Let
Y
be a mixing
\mathbb{Z}
-SFT and let
X
be an SFT given as input for the problem
X\simeq Y
. We check that
X
is mixing, which is a decidable property (see
[
14
]
), and answer negatively if it is not. If it is, then by Krieger’s embedding theorem we have
Y\simeq X
if and only if
h_{top}(Y)=h_{top}(X)
and
Y\hookrightarrow X
. Therefore we only need to check if
h_{top}(Y)=h_{top}(X)
, which is a decidable question, and finally check whether
Y\hookrightarrow X
.
∎

Remark 9.5
.

The same proof holds for
X\hookrightarrow Y
. We do not get a many-one equivalence since the answer to the problem
Y\hookrightarrow X
is not immediate when
X
is not mixing.

To conclude, we provide one last decidability result which goes beyond the class of SFTs. Recall that the sunny side up is an effective (in fact sofic) subshift which is not SFT.

Theorem 9.6
.

Let
Y=\{x\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}:|x^{-1}\{1\}|\leq 1\}
be the sunny side up subshift.
Then
Y\hookrightarrow X
for SFT inputs is decidable.

Proof.

Check that
X
contains one configuration of the form
{}^{\omega}awa^{\omega}
, where
a
is a letter and
w
a word. This is decidable for
\mathbb{Z}
-SFTs.
∎

Remark 9.7
.

Notice that the same problem is undecidable for the two-dimensional sunny side up by
Theorem

5.1
. More generally, we do not know of any subshift
Y
in dimension
2
with a decidable embedding problem
Y\hookrightarrow X
with SFT inputs, except for lifts of
\mathbb{Z}
-SFTs or a union or slight generalization thereof.

10
Conclusion

We conclude this article by a direction for future work. For a factor
F
, the subshift
F(Y)
is simpler than
Y
from many points of view. It is natural to expect that a problem associated to
F(Y)
is always easier than the same problem associated to
Y
. All our characterizations and examples are consistent with this belief, but we did not find a reduction or a systematic argument for arbitrary
Y
and
F
. The same question can be asked for other dynamical operators such as total simulations, as introduced in
[
12
]
.

Appendix D
Technical point on
[
1
, Corollary 13]

Corollary 13 in
[
1
]
states that a subshift has strong computable type – in the case of SFT, this is equivalent to having computable language – when it is minimal for a
\Sigma^{0}_{2}
property. While not explicitely stated, this is equivalent to being minimal for a
\Pi^{0}_{1}
property.

By definition, a
\Sigma^{0}_{2}
property is a computable union of
\Pi^{0}_{1}
properties. Therefore a
\Pi^{0}_{1}
property is
\Sigma^{0}_{2}
. For the other direction, it is enough to see that being minimal for a union of properties is equivalent to being minimal for one of the properties.
