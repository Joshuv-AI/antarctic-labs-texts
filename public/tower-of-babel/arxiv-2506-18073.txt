Reducible Iterated Graph Systems: multiscale-freeness and multifractals
Nero Ziyu Li, Frank Xin Hu, Thomas Britz

Abstract
Iterated Graph Systems (IGS) aims to transplant ideas from fractal geometry into graph theory. Building on this framework, we extend Edge IGS from the primitive to the reducible setting. Within this broader context, we formulate rigorous definitions of multifractality and multiscale-freeness for graph fractals, and we establish conditions that are equivalent to the occurrence of these two phenomena. We further determine the corresponding fractal and degree spectra, proving that both are finite and discrete. These results complete the foundational theory of Edge IGS by filling the gap left by the primitive case studied in [ 1 , 2 ] .

1
Introduction

We aim to investigate the fundamental properties of graphs that exhibit fractal characteristics,
and to this end we introduce the Iterated Graph Systems (IGS) model.
More generally, one may view an IGS as a form of discrete fractal structure,
an object whose antecedents have in fact been present in the literature for a long time.

1.1
Background

The term fractal lattice was introduced by physicists around 1970 to describe self-similar networks
on which renormalisation techniques could be applied
[
3
,
4
]
.
These lattices were soon used to study a wide range of statistical physics models,
including the Ising and Potts models
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
,
percolation and cluster formation
[
8
,
9
]
,
random walks and anomalous diffusion
[
10
,
11
]
,
as well as diffusion-limited reactions and dynamical processes
[
10
,
12
]
.
Although fractal lattices have been extensively explored from a physical perspective,
much of that progress is empirical or heuristic and thus falls short of a general and rigorous framework.

Fractal networks describe large empirical graphs in complex-network science
and emerged in the early 2000s
[
13
]
.
Alongside the well-known small-world property of short paths with high clustering
[
14
]

and the scale-free power-law degree distribution
[
15
]
,
many natural networks were later shown – via box-covering analysis –
to be fractal and self-similar across scales
[
13
]
.
Further studies of hierarchical modularity and epidemic spreading confirmed that
small-world, scale-free and fractal structures now form the canonical triad of
complex-network properties
[
16
,
17
]
.

The expression fractal graph is less common in mathematics,
yet one can trace it back to the 1980s
when Barlow and Bass rigorously constructed Brownian motion on the Sierpiński gasket and carpet,
establishing sub-Gaussian heat kernel bounds and foundational results in analysis
on fractal graphs
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
.
Around the same time,
Kigami introduced a framework for defining Laplacian via graph approximations
and developed the theory of
post-critically finite self-similar sets
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
.
Ben Hambly investigated the spectral asymptotics of such graphs,
relating eigenvalue counting functions to local geometry
and developing the notion of spectral dimension
[
24
,
25
]
.
These ideas were later extended to more general metric–measure spaces and random walks,
producing a robust framework for heat-kernel estimates and
functional inequalities
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
.

While much is known about analysis on specific fractal graphs, a general and flexible framework for constructing and studying such structures remains largely undeveloped.
Edge IGS attempt to fill that gap by producing generic fractal graphs by given general iterations.
The underlying idea is hardly new, and its traces can be found in many physical models and mathematical constructions.
A first prototype of the edge-substitution approach already appears in Xi et al.
[
30
,
31
]
, yet the notion was not formally defined and named until
[
2
]
.
Since then a series of papers has continued to develop the framework and its applications
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
.

A systematic study of IGS provides a natural vantage point
from which to examine the geometry and analysis of fractal graphs.
Moreover, any results obtained for IGS feed directly back into
statistical physics, complex networks, and the broader theory of fractal graphs.
For all these reasons, we believe that developing the theory of IGS is timely.

In the primitive setting, the fractality and scale-freeness of Edge IGS have already been analyzed
in
[
1
,
2
]
.
By contrast, extending those results from the primitive case to the reducible case remains completely unexplored.
The next two examples show that such an extension is necessary, meaningful, and non-trivial.

Starting from an initial graph, one proceeds step by step:
at each step, every edge is replaced, independently and according to its color,

Example 1.2
.

Figure

the so-called diamond hierarchical lattice (DHL).
If we have only
R_{1}
with only one color,
then it turns out to be the classical DHL,
which is a very much-studied example
whose limit graph has integer Minkowski dimension
2
and is scale-free with degree dimension

.
See, for instance,

[

,

]
for detailed analyses.

Our picture, however, uses the full reducible rule pair
(R_{1},R_{2})

and therefore produces what one may call a splendor diamond hierarchical lattice.
The splendor DHL is an instructive example:
it satisfies both multifractality and multiscale-freeness,
and, remarkably, its fractal spectrum coincides with its degree spectrum.

One can readily imagine that estimating the growth behavior of degrees is already a delicate task;
assessing distances is even more complicated, particularly for an arbitrary reducible IGS.
Many natural questions therefore arise:

•

How large is the limiting diameter?

•

What is the fractal dimension?

•

Does the scale-free property survive in any case?

Once these questions have been answered, one can hope to investigate further models on such graphs.

The main theorems of this paper,
reproduced in the subsection below, answer these questions
in the wider setting of arbitrary Reducible Iterated Graph Systems.

1.3
Main theorems

Theorem
3.10
.

Let
\mathscr{R}=(\Xi_{\iota}^{0},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{S})
be any

(reducible)
Edge Iterated Graph Systems and let

(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}})
be its Gromov-Hausdorff scaling limit.
Then
\Xi_{\iota}
fails to satisfy the

balanced-distance and divergent-mass (BDDM)
condition
if and only if

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(\Xi_{\iota})=\dim_{\mathrm{B}}(\Xi_{\iota})=\frac{\displaystyle\log\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{M}}\circ\mathfrak{b}_{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}(\ell)}(\mathbf{M})\big)}{\displaystyle\log\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)}\,.

Theorem
3.12
.

Let
\mathscr{R}=(\Xi_{\iota}^{0},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{S})
be any (reducible) EIGS
and let
(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}})
be its Gromov-Hausdorff scaling limit.
Then
\Xi_{\iota}
satisfies the BDDM condition if and only if

\Xi_{\iota}
is multifractal with a
finite discrete spectrum

\mathscr{S}(\Xi_{\iota})=\bigg\{s_{k}:=\frac{\log\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{M})\big)}{\log\displaystyle\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)\Big)}\ \bigg|\ k\in\mathscr{K}_{\mathrm{BDDM}}(\iota)\bigg\}\,,

and
1<\lvert\mathscr{S}(\Xi_{\iota})\rvert<\infty
.
Moreover,

\dim_{\mathrm{B}}\big(\{x:\dim_{\mathrm{loc}}(x)=s_{k}\}\big)=s_{k}\,.

Theorem
4.5
.

Let
\mathscr{R}=(\Xi_{\iota}^{0},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{S})
be a reducible iterated graph system and
let
(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\deg}_{\Xi_{\iota}})
be its degree scaling limit.
If

\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{N})\big)>1
,
then
\Xi_{\iota}

does not
satisfy the BEDM condition if and only if

\Xi_{\iota}
is scale-free and its degree dimension is

\dim_{D}(\Xi_{\iota})=\frac{\displaystyle\log\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{M}}\circ\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{N}}^{-1}(k)}(\mathbf{M})\big)}{\log\displaystyle\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{N})\big)}\,.

Theorem
4.8
.

Let
\mathscr{R}=(\Xi_{\iota}^{0},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{S})
be
a reducible IGS with degree scaling limit
(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\deg}_{\Xi})
.
If

\max_{j\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{j}(\mathbf{N})\big)>1
,
then
\Xi_{\iota}
satisfies the BEDM condition
if and only if it is finite discrete multiscale‐free with degree spectrum

\mathscr{D}(\Xi_{\iota})=\bigg\{\frac{\log\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{M})\big)}{\log\displaystyle\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{N})\big)}\ \bigg|\ k\in\mathscr{K}_{\mathrm{BEDM}}(\iota)\bigg\},\qquad 1<\big|\mathscr{D}(\Xi_{\iota})\big|<\infty\,.

Although a reducible IGS is a natural extension of an irreducible one,
the step from irreducible to reducible turns out to be indispensable and far from trivial.

In forthcoming work, we shall introduce a broader framework called General Iterated Graph Systems,
which in particular generates many classical objects, such as the Sierpiński gasket graph,
in an entirely transparent way.
Most of those classical fractals rely crucially on
reducible
substitutions;
without a firm understanding of reducible IGS behavior,
we cannot hope to build a general theory of fractal graphs, such as fractal lattices or fractal networks.
Hence, although parts of this paper appear technical,
the analysis carried out here is both necessary and unavoidable.

2
Reducible Iterated Graphs Systems

In this section, we introduce reducible iterated graph systems and establish their basic properties,
thereby setting the stage for the sections that follow.

2.1
Construction of reducible IGS

In this subsection, we introduce the definitions of reducible IGS.
Since some of these definitions can be found in
[
1
,
2
]
,
we aim to keep them as concise as possible without sacrificing readability.

Notation 2.1
.

For each positive integer
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
,
define
[N]:=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
.
In this paper, the variables
i
,
j
,
k
,
m
, and
n
represent integers,
with
n
specifically denoting the number of iterations.

Let
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
be the standard basis for
\mathbb{R}^{1\times m}
,
where each basis vector
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}
has
1
in its
i
-th entry and
0
in all other entry.
For any vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{1\times m}
,
denote each
i
-th entry of
\mathbf{v}
as
[\mathbf{v}]_{i}:=\mathbf{v}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}^{\top}
.

For any matrices
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
,
let
[\mathbf{A}]_{ij}:=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j}^{\top}
be the entry in row
i
and column
j
of
\mathbf{A}
.
Let
\mathbf{v}\leq\mathbf{w}
denote that
[\mathbf{v}]_{i}\leq[\mathbf{w}]_{i}
for all
i\in[m]
and
let
\mathbf{A}\leq\mathbf{B}
denote that
[\mathbf{A}]_{ij}\leq[\mathbf{B}]_{ij}
for all
i,j\in[m]
.

Definition 2.2
.

An Edge Iterated Graph System (EIGS)
\mathscr{R}=(\Xi_{\iota}^{0},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{S})
is a triple where,
for some positive number of colors
K
,

\Xi_{\iota}^{0}

is a given
\iota
-colored edge where
\iota\in[K]
.
When the subscript
\iota
is omitted,
\Xi^{0}
may denote an arbitrary directed finite graph.
In particular, we denote the two vertices in
\Xi_{\iota}^{0}
by
\mathfrak{v}^{+}
and
\mathfrak{v}^{-}
.
In this paper,
\iota
always represents the initial color;

\mathcal{R}

=\{R_{i}\}_{i\in[K]}
is a family of directed graphs,
called rule or seed graphs.
Each rule graph must contain two planting vertices
\{\alpha_{i}^{+},\alpha_{i}^{-}\}\subset V(R_{i})
.

The color function
\mathscr{C}:E(G)\to[K]
indicates the color of each arc in
E(G)
.
The substitution operator is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

:e=(a,b)\mapsto R_{\mathscr{C}(e)}

fixing
a=\alpha_{\mathscr{C}(e)}^{+},\;b=\alpha_{\mathscr{C}(e)}^{-}
.

That is, we substitute the arc
(a,b)
by
R_{\mathscr{C}(e)}
such that

a
and
b
coincide with
\alpha^{+}
and
\alpha^{-}
.

We now define a discrete time
n\in\mathbb{N}
dynamical systems by

\Xi_{\iota}^{n+1}:=\mathcal{S}(\Xi_{\iota}^{n}):=\bigcup_{e\in E(\Xi_{\iota}^{n})}\mathcal{S}(e),

satisfying
\mathcal{S}^{0}(\Xi_{\iota}^{0})=\Xi_{\iota}^{0}

and
\mathcal{S}^{n+m}(\Xi_{\iota}^{0})=\mathcal{S}^{n}\big(\mathcal{S}^{m}(\Xi_{\iota}^{0})\big)
,
for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\Xi_{\iota}
be the Gromov-Hausdorff scaling limit defined in
[
2
]
)
of
(\Xi_{\iota}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
(see Theorem
3.6
below).

We here provide the definition of reducibility,
where the definitions of
\mathbf{M}
,
\mathbf{N}
, and
\mathcal{D}
will be given in the subsequent subsection.
An iterated graph system is said to be mass-reducible if
\mathbf{M}
is reducible;
it is said to be metric-reducible if
\mathcal{D}_{\min}
is reducible;
and it is said to be degree-reducible if
\mathbf{N}
is reducible.

Relevant definitions

In this subsection, we will provide the definitions of
\mathbf{M}
,
\mathbf{N}
, and
\mathcal{D}
.

Notation 2.3
.

For a directed graph
G
, we define the vector
\boldsymbol{\chi}(G)\in\mathbb{N}^{1\times K}
by

[\boldsymbol{\chi}(G)]_{i}:=\big|\{e\in E(G):\mathscr{C}(e)=i\}\big|\,,

where
\boldsymbol{\chi}(G)
counts the number of edges of each color in the entire graph
G
.
Specifically, the
i
-th component
[\boldsymbol{\chi}(G)]_{i}
represents
the total number of edges in
G
that have color
i
.

For each vertex
v\in V(G)
,
the vector
\boldsymbol{\kappa}(v)\in\mathbb{N}^{1\times 2K}
is defined as a vector capturing the local edge distribution around
v
:

\begin{array}[]{l@{\;}l@{\;}ll}[\boldsymbol{\kappa}(v)]_{2i-1}&:=\big|\{e\text{ is an outgoing edge}&:\mathscr{C}(e)=i\}\big|&\text{for }i\in[K]\,;\\[5.69054pt]
[\boldsymbol{\kappa}(v)]_{2i}&:=\big|\{e\text{ is an incoming edge}&:\mathscr{C}(e)=i\}\big|&\text{for }i\in[K]\,.\end{array}

Here,
\boldsymbol{\kappa}(v)
encodes both the out-degree and in-degree of vertex
v
for each color
i
.

Definition 2.4
.

The matrices
\mathbf{N}\in\mathbb{N}^{2K\times 2K}
and
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{N}^{K\times K}
are constructed as follows:

\mathbf{N}:=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\kappa}(R_{1}:\alpha_{1}^{+})\\
\boldsymbol{\kappa}(R_{1}:\alpha_{1}^{-})\\[-2.84526pt]
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\kappa}(R_{k}:\alpha_{K}^{+})\\
\boldsymbol{\kappa}(R_{k}:\alpha_{K}^{-})\end{pmatrix}\qquad\mathbf{M}:=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\chi}(R_{1})\\[-2.84526pt]
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\chi}(R_{K})\end{pmatrix}.

The matrix
\mathbf{N}
systematically organises
the local connectivity information of all planting vertices across all rule graphs in the edge iterated graph system.

The matrix
\mathbf{M}
captures the total number of edges of each color in each rule graph
R_{i}
.
Specifically, the entry
[\mathbf{M}]_{ij}
represents the number of edges in
R_{i}
that have color
j
.
This matrix provides a concise summary of the color distribution across all seed graphs in the iterated graph system.

We now begin to analyse the growth rate of path lengths in an EIGS.
When discussing metrics, we transform all directed graphs into their underlying undirected graphs.
Throughout this paper, distances are always defined with respect to these undirected graphs.
That is, when considering distances, we will always ignore the direction of edges.

Definition 2.5
.

In this paper, paths have no repeating vertices.
Denote one path between vertices
\alpha_{i}^{+}
and
\alpha_{i}^{-}
in underlying (undirected)
R_{i}
by
P_{i}(\alpha_{i}^{+},\alpha_{i}^{-})
, and define

\mathcal{P}_{i}:=\big\{P_{i}(\alpha^{+},\alpha^{-}):P_{i}(\alpha^{+},\alpha^{-})\subset R_{i}\big\}\,.

Consider the Cartesian product

\mathcal{P}:=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathcal{P}_{j}\,.

Each element, or choice,

\mathcal{C}=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{k})\in\mathcal{P}
is a vector of length
K

each of whose entries
P_{i}(\alpha^{+},\alpha^{-})
is in
\mathcal{P}_{i}
.

For each
i\in[K]
, define

\mathcal{V}_{i}:=\big\{\boldsymbol{\chi}\big(P_{i}(\alpha^{+},\alpha^{-})\big):P_{i}(\alpha^{+},\alpha^{-})\in\mathcal{P}_{i}\big\}.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be the set of all matrices

\mathbf{D}_{\mathcal{C}}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{d}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{d}_{K}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\chi}([\mathcal{C}]_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\chi}([\mathcal{C}]_{K})\end{pmatrix}

where
\mathbf{d}_{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{i}
for each
i\in[K]
or,
equivalently,
[\mathcal{C}]_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{i}
.

Let
\mathbf{D}_{\min}
denote a matrix in
\mathcal{D}
that attains the minimum value

\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)\,.

In other words,
\mathbf{D}_{\min}
is any matrix in
\mathcal{D}

whose largest reachable block spectral radius is minimal among all matrices in the family.
Symbols that have not yet been defined here will be introduced in the next subsection.

2.3
Reducible forms

Definition 2.6
.

A non-negative matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
is

reducible
if there exists a permutation matrix
\mathbf{P}

and an integer
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbf{X}}\geq 2
such that

\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{P}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{B}_{1}(\mathbf{X})&\mathbf{X}_{12}&\cdots&\mathbf{X}_{1\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbf{X}}}\\
0&\mathbf{B}_{2}(\mathbf{X})&\cdots&\mathbf{X}_{2\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbf{X}}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\mathbf{B}_{\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbf{X}}}(\mathbf{X})\end{pmatrix},

where each diagonal block

In this paper, we utilize a significant extension of the Perron-Frobenius Theorem

We summarise the theorem, which appears relatively straightforward but has a non-trivial proof.
We will present this result using the notation defined previously.

Theorem 2.8

(Lustig-Uyanik Theorem
[
38
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
be
a primitive-Frobenius matrix written in upper-block-triangular Frobenius normal form,
and fix a color
\iota\in[K]
with block index
\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{X}}(\iota)\in[\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbf{X}}]
.
Let

\displaystyle\varkappa(\iota):=\max\Big\{\Big|\big\{j\leq L:\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k_{j}}(\mathbf{X})\big)=\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{X}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{X})\big)\big\}\Big|:\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{X}}(\iota)=k_{0}\rightsquigarrow_{\mathfrak{B}}\cdots\rightsquigarrow_{\mathfrak{B}}k_{L}\Big\}.

There exist positive constants
c_{1},c_{2}
depending only on
\mathbf{X}

such that, for every integer
n\geq 1
,

c_{1}n^{\varkappa(\iota)-1}\Big(\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{X}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{X})\big)\Big)^{n}\leq\Big\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\iota}\mathbf{X}^{n}\Big\|_{1}\leq c_{2}n^{\varkappa(\iota)-1}\Big(\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{X}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{X})\big)\Big)^{n}.

(1)

Moreover,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\iota}\mathbf{X}^{n}}{\big\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\iota}\mathbf{X}^{n}\big\|_{1}}=\mathbf{v}_{\iota}\neq\mathbf{0}\,.

(2)

The current literature including
[
38
]
does not
explicitly establish the existence of the norm limit for reducible non-negative matrices.
In particular, equations (
1
) and (
2
) only provide
two-sided estimates for the length of the iterates and guarantee convergence of their direction;
they do not identify the actual limit of the length itself.
Since our argument relies on that limit,
we present the following lemma, which states its existence in precise form.

We write
f\overset{x\to x_{0}}{\asymp}g
if
\lim_{x\to x_{0}}f(x)/g(x)=c
.

Lemma 2.9
.

\displaystyle\big\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\iota}\mathbf{X}^{n}\big\|_{1}\asymp n^{\varkappa(\iota)-1}\Big(\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{X}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{X})\big)\Big)^{n}
.

Proof.

(i)

For every primitive non-negative matrix

A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
the Perron-Frobenius Theorem yields the power convergence

\bigg(\frac{\mathbf{A}}{\rho(\mathbf{A})}\bigg)^{n}\overset{n\to\infty}{\to}\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{A}}\,.

Hence,

\mathbf{A}^{n}=\rho(\mathbf{A})^{n}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{A}}+\mathbf{E}_{n}),\qquad\|\mathbf{E}_{n}\|\leq c\bigg(\frac{\rho_{2}(\mathbf{A})}{\rho(\mathbf{A})}\bigg)^{n},

where
\rho_{2}(\mathbf{A})<\rho(\mathbf{A})
is the second-largest
eigenvalue.
For every non-negative
row vector
\mathbf{u}\neq\mathbf{0}
we obtain

\big\|\mathbf{u}\mathbf{A}^{n}\big\|_{1}=\rho(\mathbf{A})^{n}\Big\|\mathbf{u}\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{A}}+O\Big(\Big(\frac{\rho_{2}(\mathbf{A})}{\rho(\mathbf{A})}\Big)^{n}\Big)\Big\|_{1}\asymp\rho(\mathbf{A})^{n}\,.

(ii)

Consider the block matrix

\mathbf{X}=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{B}\\
0&\mathbf{C}\end{smallmatrix}\big)

with
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{C}
primitive,
\mathbf{B}\geq\mathbf{0}
,
and
\rho(\mathbf{A})=\rho(\mathbf{C})
.
By the usual upper-triangular power formula,

\mathbf{X}^{n}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}^{n}&\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbf{A}^{n-1-k}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}^{k}\\
0&\mathbf{C}^{n}\end{pmatrix}.

Divide by
\rho(\mathbf{A})^{n}
and substitute the expression from
(i) for
\mathbf{A}^{m}
and
\mathbf{C}^{k}
:

\rho(\mathbf{A})^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbf{A}^{n-1-k}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}^{k}=\frac{1}{\rho(\mathbf{A})}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{A}}+\mathbf{E}_{n-1-k})\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{C}}+\mathbf{F}_{k})\,.

Expanding the right-hand side gives

\frac{n}{\rho(\mathbf{A})}\,\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{C}}+O(1),

since

\|\mathbf{E}_{m}\|\leq c_{3}\big(\rho_{2}(\mathbf{A})/\rho(\mathbf{A})\big)^{m}

and

\|\mathbf{F}_{k}\|\leq c_{4}\big(\rho_{2}(\mathbf{C})/\rho(\mathbf{C})\big)^{k}
,
and since the geometric series are summable.
Consequently,

\big\|\mathbf{u}\mathbf{M}^{n}\big\|_{1}\asymp n\rho(\mathbf{A})^{n}\qquad\text{for all}\quad\mathbf{u}\geq\mathbf{0},\ \mathbf{u}\neq\mathbf{0}.

(iii)

Write the Frobenius normal form of
\mathbf{X}
as a
block-upper-triangular matrix with diagonal blocks

\mathbf{B}_{1}(\mathbf{X}),\dots,\mathbf{B}_{\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbf{X}}}(\mathbf{X})
.
Scan these blocks from top to bottom.

Whenever the current block has spectral radius
strictly smaller

than the maximal spectral radius encountered so far, its contribution
is exponentially dominated and does not increase the polynomial power.

Whenever the current block is primitive and its spectral radius equals
the current maximal one, it attaches on top of an existing
same-radius chain; by (ii) this produces an additional factor
n
.

After the scan finishes, the total polynomial power accumulated is
precisely the integer
\varkappa(\iota)-1
,
because there are
\varkappa(\iota)-1
consecutive diagonal blocks
reachable from
\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{X}}(\iota)
whose
spectral radius equals
\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{X}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{X})\big)
.

By (i)–(iii),

\big\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\iota}\mathbf{X}^{n}\big\|_{1}\asymp n^{\varkappa(\iota)-1}\Big(\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{X}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{X})\big)\Big)^{n},

which is the desired statement.
∎

3
), it follows that

\rho(\mathbf{D}^{\prime})<\max_{j}\frac{[\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{D}^{*}}]_{j}}{[\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{D}^{*}}]_{j}}\leq\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D}^{*})\big)=\rho(\mathbf{D}^{*}).

But this contradicts the minimality condition in the choice of
\mathbf{D}^{*}
,
where we had set its dominant block to achieve the minimal possible value of
\max_{\ell}\rho(\mathbf{B}_{\ell})
,
ranging over all
\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}
.

Therefore, our assumption is false,
and so, for every
\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}
and for every
i\in\mathfrak{r}_{\mathbf{D}}(\iota)
,

\frac{[\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{D}^{*}}]_{i}}{[\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{D}^{*}}]_{i}}\geq\rho(\mathbf{D}^{*})=\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D}^{*})\big)\,.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\bigg\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\iota}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{D_{k_{i}}}\bigg\|_{1}

\displaystyle\succeq\bigg\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\iota}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{D_{k_{i}}}\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{D}^{*}}\bigg\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\iota}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{D_{k_{i}}}\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{D}^{*}}

\displaystyle\succeq\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\iota}\max_{\ell}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{D}_{k_{i}})\big)\prod_{i=2}^{n}\mathbf{D_{k_{i}}}\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{D}^{*}}

\displaystyle\succeq\cdots\succeq\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\iota}\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{D}^{*}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbf{D}_{k_{i}})}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D}_{k_{i}})\big)

\displaystyle\succeq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D}_{k_{i}})\big)

\displaystyle\succeq\Big(\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)\Big)^{n}\,.\qed

Theorem
3.3
provides a lower bound on distance growth
that is governed by the matrix family
\mathcal{D}
.
This explicit control is very useful: it tells us precisely how fast distances increase.
Having that rate in hand allows us to study further distance properties of the graph, such as its diameter.

Lemma 3.4
.

\displaystyle\rm{diam}(\Xi^{n}_{\iota})\asymp n^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\Big(\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)\Big)^{n}
.

Proof.

In previous work
[
2
]
,
a detailed analysis of shortest path growth behavior in deterministic irreducible iterated graph systems was given.
By introducing uniform paths, it was shown that, in each graph iteration,
the length of the shortest path can be expressed recursively through a matrix operation,
where each matrix
\mathbf{D}_{k_{i}}
represents the substitution rule applied at each iteration step.
Using this framework, it was proved that the growth of the shortest path length follows
an exponential growth behavior, with the rate controlled by the spectral radius of the matrices.

In this paper, we extend this framework to study reducible IGS.
Although the reducibility of the matrices introduces a decoupling of the graph into smaller subgraphs,
the construction of uniform paths and the recursive representation of shortest paths remain unchanged.
Therefore, by adapting the methods from the previous work in
[
2
]
,
we prove the relationship between the shortest path length and the diameter of the graph,
and we derive the associated results in the form of the following results.

We begin by recalling the framework from previous work,
where the shortest path distance between vertices
\mathfrak{v}^{+}
and
\mathfrak{v}^{-}

in the graph
\Xi_{\iota}^{n}
was represented as a matrix norm:

\rm{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}^{n}}(\mathfrak{v}^{+},\mathfrak{v}^{-})=\left\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\iota}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{D}_{k_{i}}\right\|_{1}

In this representation,

\mathbf{D}_{k_{i}}
denotes the matrix corresponding to the substitution rule applied in the
i
-th iteration,
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\iota}
is the initial vector associated with the vertices
\mathfrak{v}^{+}
and
\mathfrak{v}^{-}
.
By recursively applying the substitution rules, e
ach step introduces a new matrix
\mathbf{D}_{k_{i}}
,
and the length of the path is computed via the product of these matrices.

This representation directly follows the framework introduced in the previous work
for deterministic irreducible IGS.
Therefore, we can extend the same reasoning to reducible IGS,
where despite the reducibility of the matrices,
the uniform path concept and the recursive path length representation remain applicable.

Next, we consider the diameter of the graph
\Xi_{\iota}^{n}
.
It was shown in
[
2
]
that the diameter and the shortest path distance are comparable,
and, in fact, we can estimate their relationship with a constant factor:

\rm{diam}(\Xi^{n}_{\iota})\asymp\rm{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}^{n}}(\mathfrak{v}^{+},\mathfrak{v}^{-})\,.

This shows that the growth of the shortest path length is asymptotically the same as the growth of the graph’s diameter.

Finally, we turn to the result from Theorem
3.3
,
which provides an important bound on the growth of the matrix product.
The following theorem states that for any reducible matrix sequence

\mathbf{D}_{k_{1}},\mathbf{D}_{k_{2}},\dots,\mathbf{D}_{k_{n}}
,
the product grows exponentially in the following manner:

\bigg\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\iota}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{D}_{k_{i}}\bigg\|_{1}\succeq\Big(\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D}))\Big)^{n}\,.

This result provides a lower bound for the growth rate of the shortest path lengths in terms of
the spectral radius of the matrices
\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})
,
controlling the exponential growth rate of the path lengths.

However, although Theorem
3.3
guarantees an exponential lower bound,
it does not yet pin down the true growth rate.
A sharper picture follows once we combine that result with
the reducible Lustig-Uyanik theorem and Lemma
2.9
, yielding

\big\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\iota}(\mathbf{D}^{*})^{n}\big\|_{1}\asymp n^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\Big(\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)\Big)^{n}.

Since every other product has an exponential rate at least

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)
,
we obtain

\displaystyle n^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)^{n}

\displaystyle\asymp\min_{\mathbf{D}_{k_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{D}_{k_{n}}}\Big\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\iota}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{D}_{k_{i}}\Big\|_{1}

\displaystyle\succeq\Big\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\iota}(\mathbf{D}^{*})^{n}\Big\|_{1}

\displaystyle\succeq\min_{\mathbf{D}_{k_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{D}_{k_{n}}}\Big\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\iota}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{D}_{k_{i}}\Big\|_{1}

\displaystyle\asymp n^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)^{n}.

The leftmost inequality is immediate from the Lemma
2.9
,
while the rightmost one follows because every product that attains
the minimal exponential rate must consist solely of copies of
\mathbf{D}^{*}
,
and such a choice necessarily carries the same polynomial factor.

Thus, combining all of these results, we conclude that:

\rm{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}^{n}}(\alpha_{\iota}^{+},\alpha_{\iota}^{-})\asymp\rm{diam}(\Xi^{n}_{\iota})\asymp n^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\Big(\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)\Big)^{n}\,.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

3.2
Scaling limit

To analyse the system’s fractal nature, we first specify the notion of a limit graph.
We then prove that the metric induced by this construction exists and is well-defined.

Definition 3.5

(
[
2
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(G^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of finite connected graphs.
For each
n
, every edge of
G^{n}
is scaled by the common factor
1/\rm{diam}(G^{n})
,
giving the compact metric space

\big(G^{n},\hat{\rm}{d}_{G^{n}}\big),\quad\text{where}\quad\hat{\rm}{d}_{G^{n}}:=\frac{\rm{d}_{G^{n}}}{\rm{diam}(G^{n})}\,.

The graph sequence
\mathcal{G}
possesses a
Gromov-Hausdorff scaling limit

if the sequence of metric spaces
(G^{n},\hat{\rm}{d}_{G^{n}})
converges,
regarding the Gromov-Hausdorff topology, to some compact metric space

(G^{n},\hat{\rm}{d}_{G^{n}})
.
In that case, we call
(G,\hat{\rm}{d}_{G})
the Gromov-Hausdorff scaling limit of
\mathcal{G}
.

Theorem 3.6
.

If
\displaystyle\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)>1
,
then the Gromov-Hausdorff scaling limit
\big(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}}\big)
exists
for every initial color
\iota\in[K]
.

Proof.

Since the vertex sets form an increasing chain
V(\Xi_{\iota}^{0})\subset V(\Xi_{\iota}^{1})\subset\dots
,
we can without loss of generality restrict attention to two vertices
x,y\in V(\Xi_{\iota}^{k})

that are joined in
\Xi_{\iota}^{k}
where
k\leq n
by
a single edge of color
\iota^{*}\in\mathfrak{r}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)
.
Lemma
2.9
then gives

\hat{\rm{d}}_{\Xi_{\iota}}(x,y):=\frac{\rm{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}^{n}}(x,y)}{\rm{diam}\big(\Xi_{\iota}^{n}\big)}\asymp\frac{\displaystyle(n-k)^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota^{*})-1}\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota^{*})}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)^{n-k}}{\displaystyle n^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota^{*})-1}\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)^{n}}\,.

Two scenarios can occur:

If

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota^{*})}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)=\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)
,
then the
\iota^{*}
-edges and the
\iota
-edges coexist
in the Gromov-Hausdorff scaling limit with a fixed equilibrium ratio.

If

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota^{*})}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)<\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)
,
then color
\iota^{*}
is suppressed:
every edge of that color is rescaled to length
0
and disappears in the limit.

Hence, for any pair of vertices
x,y
,
we may define the rescaled distance

\hat{\rm{d}}_{\Xi_{\iota}}(x,y):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{\rm{d}}_{\Xi_{\iota}^{n}}(x,y)\in[0,1]\,.

If
\hat{\rm{d}}_{\Xi_{\iota}}(x,y)=0
yet
x\neq y
,
then the entire subgraph connecting
x
and
y
has collapsed;
we declare
x\sim y
and form the metric quotient

\big(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\rm{d}}_{\Xi_{\iota}}\big):=\big(V(\Xi_{\iota})/\sim,\hat{\rm{d}}_{\Xi_{\iota}}\big)\,.

Now fix a root vertex
x_{\#}\in V(\Xi_{\iota}^{0})
and, for each
n
, take two labelled copies

V(\Xi_{\iota}^{n})^{(1)}:=\{v^{(1)}:v\in V(\Xi_{\iota}^{n})\},\quad V(\Xi_{\iota}^{n})^{(2)}:=\{v^{(2)}:v\in V(\Xi_{\iota}^{n})\}\,.

Set

e_{n}(v):=\big|\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}^{n}}(x_{\#},v)-\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}}(x_{\#},v)\big|+\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)^{-n}\,.

Define a metric

\rm{d}_{n}^{\#}:V(\Xi_{\iota}^{n})^{(1)}\sqcup V(\Xi_{\iota}^{n})^{(2)}\times V(\Xi_{\iota}^{n})^{(1)}\sqcup V(\Xi_{\iota}^{n})^{(2)}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

by

\rm{d}_{n}^{\#}\big(v^{(i)},w^{(j)}\big):=\begin{cases}\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}^{n}}(v,w)&,\;i=j=1\,;\\[4.0pt]
\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}}(v,w)&,\;i=j=2\,;\\[4.0pt]
\displaystyle\min_{\begin{smallmatrix}v\in V(\Xi_{\iota}^{n})^{(1)},\\
u\in V(\Xi_{\iota}^{n})^{(2)}\end{smallmatrix}}\big(\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}^{n}}(v,u)+e_{n}(u)+\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}}(u,w)\big)&,\,i\neq j\,.\end{cases}

Since
e_{n}(u)\geq\big|\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}^{n}}(x_{\#},u)-\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}}(x_{\#},u)\big|
,

\rm{d}_{n}^{\#}
satisfies the triangle inequality; symmetry and
positive definiteness are clear,
so it is a real metric gluing the two copies isometrically.
For every
v\in V(\Xi_{\iota}^{n})

\rm{d}_{n}^{\#}\big(v^{(1)},v^{(2)}\big)=e_{n}(v)\preceq n^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)^{-n}\,.

Hence, the Hausdorff distance inside

(V(\Xi_{\iota}^{n})^{(1)}\sqcup V(\Xi_{\iota}^{n})^{(2)},\rm{d}_{n}^{\#})

satisfies

\rm{d}_{\mathrm{H}}^{\#}\big(V(\Xi_{\iota}^{n})^{(1)},V(\Xi_{\iota}^{n})^{(2)}\big)\preceq n^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)^{-n}\,.

Therefore,

\rm{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}\big((\Xi_{\iota}^{n},\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}^{n}}),(\Xi_{\iota}^{n},\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}})\big)\preceq n^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)^{-n}\,.

Since
\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}^{n}}
extends to
\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}}
,
the natural embedding yields

\rm{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}\big((\Xi^{n},\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi}),(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}})\big)=\hat{\rm}{d}_{\mathrm{H}}\big(V(\Xi_{\iota}^{n}),V(\Xi_{\iota})\big).

Lemma
3.4
shows that every vertex added at level

n+1
is within

\displaystyle cn^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)^{-n}

of the previous level, whence inductively

\rm{d}_{\mathrm{H}}\big(V(\Xi_{\iota}^{n}),V(\Xi_{\iota})\big)\preceq n^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)^{-n}\,.

By the triangle inequality for
\rm{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}
,

\displaystyle\quad\;\rm{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}\Big((\Xi_{\iota}^{n},\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi^{n}}),(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}})\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\rm{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}\Big((\Xi_{\iota}^{n},\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}^{n}}),(\Xi_{\iota}^{n},\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}})\Big)+\rm{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}\Big((\Xi_{\iota}^{n},\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}}),(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}})\Big)

\displaystyle\preceq n^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)^{-n}+n^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)^{-n}

\displaystyle\preceq n^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)^{-n}\overset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0\,.

Given
\varepsilon>0
,
choose
N
with
\displaystyle N^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)^{-N}<\varepsilon
.
The finite set
V(\Xi_{\iota}^{n})
then forms an
\varepsilon
-net in
\Xi_{\iota}
.
Since
\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}}
is a pointwise limit of complete metrics,
any Cauchy sequence in
\Xi_{\iota}
converges.
Total boundedness and completeness imply that

(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}})
is compact, completing the proof.
∎

Definition 3.7
.

Let
(F,\rm{d})
be a non-empty compact metric space.
We say that
F
is
quasi-self-similar
if there exist
constants
a>0
and
r_{0}>0
such that for every non-empty
subset
U\subseteq F
with

\operatorname{diam}(U)\leq r_{0}
there is a map

g:F\cap U\to F
satisfying

a\operatorname{diam}(U)^{-1}\rm{d}(x,y)\leq\rm{d}\big(g(x),g(y)\big),\quad\text{for all }x,y\in F\cap U\,.

Theorem 3.8
.

For every initial color
\iota\in[K]
the Gromov-Hausdorff scaling
limit
(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}})
is quasi-self-similar in the sense of Definition
3.7
.

Proof.

For simplicity let
\Lambda:=\displaystyle\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)
throughout this proof.

Lemma
3.4
gives the precise diameter growth

\rm{diam}\big(\Xi_{\iota}^{n}\big)\asymp n^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\Lambda^{n}\,.

Set

r_{0}:=\Lambda^{-1},\qquad a:=\frac{\displaystyle\min_{i\in[K]}\rm{d}_{R_{i}}\big(\alpha_{i}^{+},\alpha_{i}^{-}\big)}{\displaystyle\max_{i\in[K]}\rm{d}_{R_{i}}\big(\alpha_{i}^{+},\alpha_{i}^{-}\big)}\in(0,1]\,.

Let
U\subset\Xi_{\iota}
be non-empty with

\operatorname{diam}(U)\leq r_{0}
.
Choose the minimal
k\geq 1
such that

k^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\Lambda^{-k}<\operatorname{diam}(U)\Lambda\,.

At level
k
each rule-graph copy (“tile”)
has diameter
\asymp k^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\Lambda^{-k}
;
different tiles are
\Lambda^{-k}
-separated even after collapsing
slow parts.
Hence,
U
is contained in a
unique
level-
k
tile; denote this

\Xi_{\iota^{\prime}}^{(e)}
, where
e
is the generating seed

In particular,

\rm{diam}(\Xi_{\iota^{\prime}}^{(e)})\asymp k^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota^{\prime})-1}\Lambda^{-k}\,.

Let

\pi_{k}:\Xi_{\iota^{\prime}}^{(e)}\to R_{i_{k+1}}

be the inverse of the
k
successive substitution maps; it is
L
-Lipschitz with

\rm{d}\big(\pi_{k}(x),\pi_{k}(y)\big)\asymp k^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota^{\prime})-1}\Lambda^{k}\rm{d}(x,y)\,.

Embed the seed graph
R_{i_{k+1}}
into the limit space by the natural inclusion
\jmath
.
Define

g:=\jmath\circ\pi_{k}:U\longrightarrow\Xi_{\iota}\,.

For all
x,y\in U
,

\rm{d}\big(g(x),g(y)\big)\asymp k^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota^{\prime})-1}\Lambda^{k}\rm{d}(x,y)\,.

\operatorname{diam}(U)\preceq\big(\max_{i\in[K]}\rm{d}_{R_{i}}(\alpha_{i}^{+},\alpha_{i}^{-})\big)k^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\Lambda^{-k}\,.

Combining the two displays and absorbing absolute constants yields

a\operatorname{diam}(U)^{-1}\rm{d}(x,y)\leq\rm{d}\big(g(x),g(y)\big)

for
x,y\in U
and
with the universal constants

a
and

fixed above.

Since the construction is valid for every non-empty

with

\operatorname{diam}(U)\leq r_{0}
, the limit space

(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}})

satisfies Definition

and is therefore

∎

Definition 3.9
.

The limit space
(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}})
is said to satisfy the

balanced-distance and divergent-mass condition

(BDDM condition) if there exist two indices

k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)
such that

\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k_{1}}(\mathbf{D}_{\min})\big)=\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k_{2}}(\mathbf{D}_{\min})\big),\quad\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota,k_{1})=\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota,k_{2}),

while in the mass matrix
\mathbf{M}
we have

\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime}_{1}}(\mathbf{M})\big)\neq\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime}_{2}}(\mathbf{M})\big).

Write

\mathscr{K}_{\mathrm{BDDM}}(\iota):=\big\{k\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota):\text{$k$ participates in the BDDM pair(s)}\big\}\,.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
\mathscr{R}=(\Xi_{\iota}^{0},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{S})
be a

reducible
iterated-graph system and let

(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}})
be its Gromov-Hausdorff scaling limit.
Then
\Xi_{\iota}
fails to satisfy the

balanced-distance and divergent-mass (BDDM)
condition
if and only if

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(\Xi_{\iota})=\dim_{\mathrm{B}}(\Xi_{\iota})=\frac{\displaystyle\log\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{M}}\circ\mathfrak{b}_{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}(\ell)}(\mathbf{M})\big)}{\displaystyle\log\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)}\,.

Proof.

Set

\Lambda_{\mathcal{D}}:=\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big),\qquad\Lambda_{\mathbf{M}}:=\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{M}}\circ\mathfrak{b}_{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}(\ell^{*})}(\mathbf{M})\big),

Suppose that the BDDM condition is not satisfied.
Then, for every primitive block
k\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)
that attains the maximal
distance exponent and height,

\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathcal{D})\big)=\Lambda_{\mathcal{D}},\quad\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota,k)=\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)\,,

the same exponent-height pair is realized in the mass matrix
\mathbf{M}
:
in particular,

\rho(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{M}))=\Lambda_{\mathbf{M}}

and

\varkappa_{\mathbf{M}}(\iota,k)=\varkappa_{\mathbf{M}}(\iota)
.
Consequently, every sub-graph that survives in the limit
carries distance growth

n^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\Lambda_{\mathcal{D}}^{n}

and mass growth

n^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{M}}(\iota)-1}\Lambda_{\mathbf{M}}^{n}
.
Thus,
\Xi_{\iota}
possesses a single geometric scale.

Conversely, if such universal distance-mass pair fails to exist,
then there are two blocks with the same distance parameters but different mass parameters:
the BDDM condition is then satisfied.

Fix
n\geq 1
and set
r_{n}:=\Lambda_{\mathcal{D}}^{-(n-1)}
.
Take the level-
(n-1)
tiling;
each tile has diameter
\asymp(n-1)^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\Lambda_{\mathcal{D}}^{-(n-1)}\preceq r_{n}
.
There are
N^{\textup{cov}}_{n}\asymp(n-1)^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{M}}}\Lambda_{\mathbf{M}}^{n-1}
such tiles;
hence, the whole space is covered by that many
r_{n}
–balls.
Therefore,

\dim_{\mathrm{B}}(\Xi_{\iota})\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log N^{\textup{cov}}_{n}}{-\log r_{n}}\preceq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{M}}(\iota)-1}\log\Lambda_{\mathbf{M}}}{n^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\log\Lambda_{\mathcal{D}}}=\frac{\log\Lambda_{\mathbf{M}}}{\log\Lambda_{\mathcal{D}}}\,,

the polynomial factors
(n-1)^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min},\mathbf{M}}}
being absorbed by
\log
.

Choose the same level
n-1
tiles and place in each tile one centre.
Half-radius balls of size
r_{n}/2
are disjoint because
different tiles are
\Lambda_{\mathcal{D}}^{-(n-1)}
separated after normalisation.
Hence, the maximal packing number satisfies
N^{\textup{pack}}_{n}\asymp N^{\textup{cov}}_{n}
,
and

\dim_{\mathrm{B}}(\Xi_{\iota})\geq\dim_{\mathrm{P}}(\Xi_{\iota})\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log N^{\textup{pack}}_{n}}{-\log(r_{n}/2)}\succeq\frac{\log\Lambda_{\mathbf{M}}}{\log\Lambda_{\mathcal{D}}}\,.

By quasi-self-similarity of
\Xi_{\iota}

[
40
]
,
one has
\dim_{\mathrm{H}}=\dim_{\mathrm{P}}=\dim_{\mathrm{B}}
.
Combining them together yields the claimed result

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(\Xi_{\iota})=\dim_{\mathrm{B}}(\Xi_{\iota})=\log\Lambda_{\mathbf{M}}/\log\Lambda_{\mathcal{D}}
.

If the BDDM condition holds,
then we can repeat the covering/packing argument separately for each distinct mass exponent,
obtaining at least two different limit slopes;
Minkowski and Hausdorff dimensions therefore cannot collapse to the single ratio above.
It follows that equality of dimensions implies that the BDDM is not satisfied.
∎

Definition 3.11
.

Let
(G,\rm{d})
be a non-empty compact metric space induced by a graph
G
.
Whenever the limit exists,
the
local dimension at
x\in F
is defined as

\dim_{\mathrm{loc}}(x)=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\log\Big|E\big(B(x,r)\big)\Big|}{\log r}\,.

The set of all attained local dimensions is called the

fractal dimension spectrum

\mathscr{S}(G,\rm{d})=\Big\{\dim_{\mathrm{loc}}(x):x\in G,\dim_{\mathrm{loc}}(x)\text{ exists}\Big\}\subset[0,\infty)\,.

The metric space
(G,\rm{d})
is said to be
multifractal with a finite discrete fractal spectrum

if
\mathscr{S}(G,\rm{d})
satisfies

1<\lvert\mathscr{S}(G,\rm{d})\rvert<\infty\,.

Equivalently, the spectrum consists of a finite set of distinct real numbers,
so that local dimensions assume more than one but only finitely many values across
G
.

Theorem 3.12
.

Let
\mathscr{R}=(\Xi_{\iota}^{0},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{S})
be any (reducible) EIGS
and let
\Xi_{\iota}
be its Gromov-Hausdorff scaling limit.
Then
\Xi_{\iota}
satisfies the BDDM condition if and only if

\Xi_{\iota}
is multifractal with a
finite discrete spectrum

\mathscr{S}(\Xi_{\iota})=\bigg\{s_{k}:=\frac{\log\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{M})\big)}{\log\displaystyle\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)\Big)}\ \bigg|\ k\in\mathscr{K}_{\mathrm{BDDM}}(\iota)\bigg\}\,,

and
1<\lvert\mathscr{S}(\Xi_{\iota})\rvert<\infty
.
Moreover,

\dim_{\mathrm{B}}\big(\{x:\dim_{\mathrm{loc}}(x)=s_{k}\}\big)=s_{k}\,.

Proof.

Assume that the Gromov–Hausdorff limit
\Xi
satisfies the BDDM condition.
By Theorem
3.6
,
every edge whose color is not

\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}^{-1}\big(\mathscr{K}_{\mathrm{BDDM}}(\iota)\big)
is collapsed to a point in the limit.
Hence, it suffices to consider the subgraph generated by those edges whose colors lie in the set

\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}^{-1}\big(\mathscr{K}_{\mathrm{BDDM}}(\iota)\big)
.

Non-degeneracy.

The BDDM condition is equivalent to the existence of at least two colors, say
\iota_{1}
and
\iota_{2}
,
whose mass grows at different rates in
(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\rm}{d}_{\Xi_{\iota}})
.
Applying Theorem
3.10
to these colors yields

\dim_{\mathrm{B}}(\Xi_{\iota_{1}})\neq\dim_{\mathrm{B}}(\Xi_{\iota_{2}}),

and therefore
|\mathscr{S}(G,\rm{d})|>1
.

Finiteness.

In an EIGS, the number of colors is finite,
so the set
\mathscr{K}_{\mathrm{BDDM}}(\iota)
is finite as well.
From the proof of Theorem
3.6
,
we know that, around any vertex
v\in V(\Xi)
,
the incident edges can only carry colors from the subset

\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}^{-1}\big(\mathscr{K}_{\mathrm{BDDM}}(\iota)\big)
.
Using the stability of the Minkowski (box-counting) dimension
[
41
]
,
there exists a color
\iota^{*}\in[K]
such that

\dim_{\mathrm{loc}}(x)=\max\big\{\dim_{\mathrm{B}}(\Xi_{\iota}):\iota\in[K]\big\}=\dim_{\mathrm{B}}(\Xi_{\iota^{*}})\,.

Consequently,

|\mathscr{S}(G,\rm{d})|<|[K]|<\infty\,.

Fractal spectrum.

Let
E_{k}:=\{x:\dim_{\mathrm{l}oc}(x)=s_{k}\}
.
For every generation
n
, we have the crude counting estimate

|E_{k}|\leq 2\,\big|\big\{e\in\Xi^{n}:\dim_{\mathrm{B}}\big(\Xi_{\mathscr{C}(e)}\big)=s_{k}\big\}\big|\,.

Indeed, the local dimension of any vertex is bounded above by the
Minkowski dimension of the limit graph generated by its incident edges,
so the number of vertices with local dimension
s_{k}
cannot
exceed twice the number of edges whose color yields that dimension.

Since the edge colors belong to finitely many substitution rules,
there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\big|\{e\in\Xi^{n}:\dim_{B}(\Xi_{\mathscr{C}(e)})=s_{k}\}\big|\leq n^{c}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{M}}\circ\mathfrak{b}_{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}(\ell^{*})}(\mathbf{M})\big)^{n}\,.

Consequently,

\dim_{B}(E_{k})\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log n^{c}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{M}}\circ\mathfrak{b}_{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}(\ell^{*})}(\mathbf{M})\big)^{n}}{\log n^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{D}_{\min}}(\iota)-1}\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{D})\big)^{n}}=s_{k}\,.

Conversely, pick
k^{*}\in\mathscr{K}_{\mathrm{BDDM}}(\iota)
and let

\iota^{*}\in\mathfrak{b}_{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}(k^{*})
be its color.
By Theorem
3.10
,

\dim_{\mathrm{B}}\big(\Xi_{\iota^{*}}\big)=s_{k}
, and, by the primitive-Frobenius property,
no other Minkowski dimension occurs inside
\Xi_{\iota^{*}}
.
Using the stability of the Minkowski dimension
[
41
]
,
we obtain

\dim_{\mathrm{B}}(E_{k})\geq\dim_{\mathrm{B}}\big(\Xi_{\iota^{*}}\big)=s_{k}\,.

Combining the two bounds above yields
s_{k}\leq\dim_{\mathrm{B}}(E_{k})\leq s_{k}
.
Hence,

\dim_{\mathrm{B}}\big(\{x:\dim_{\mathrm{l}oc}(x)=s_{k}\}\big)=s_{k}\,.

Now, suppose that
\Xi_{\iota}
is multi-fractal.
By Theorem
3.10
,
if the limit graph
\Xi_{\iota}
displays at least two distinct Minkowski dimensions,
then there exist colors
\iota_{1},\iota_{2}
such that

\dim_{\mathrm{B}}\big(\Xi_{\iota_{1}}\big)\neq\dim_{\mathrm{B}}\big(\Xi_{\iota_{2}}\big)
.
Meanwhile, Theorem
3.6
states that every component of the Gromov-Hausdorff limit shares
a common exponential distance growth rate.
Hence, the two different Minkowski dimensions must arise from different polynomial exponents
attached to the same exponential rate;
that is,
\Xi_{\iota}
satisfies the BDDM condition.
∎

4
Multiscale-freeness and degree spectrum

In this section, we introduce the definition of multiscale-freeness,
establish conditions equivalent to its occurrence, and present the corresponding degree spectrum.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\Delta(G):=\max_{w\in V(G)}\deg(w)
.
The scaling degree of any vertex
v\in V(G)
is

\hat{\deg}(v):=\frac{\deg(v)}{\Delta(G)}\,.

The graph sequence
\mathcal{G}=(G^{0},G^{1},G^{2},\ldots)

has a degree scaling limit
(G,\hat{\deg}_{G})
, where

\hat{\deg}_{G}(v):=\frac{\deg_{G^{n}}(v)}{\Delta(G^{n})}

if, for any vertex
v\in G^{n}
,
the limit
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{\deg}_{G^{n}}(v)
exists.

Theorem 4.2
.

If

\displaystyle\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota)}\!\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{N})\big)>1
,
then the degree scaling limit
\big(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\deg}_{\Xi_{\iota}}\big)
exists.

Proof.

For any
v_{\iota^{\prime}}^{m,n}
, Lemma
2.11
implies that
\hat{\deg}_{\Xi}(v^{m,n})
equals

\displaystyle\quad\;\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{\deg}_{\Xi^{n}}(v^{m,n})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\big\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}(v^{m,m})\mathbf{N}^{n-m}\big\|_{1}}{\displaystyle\max_{v^{0,0}\in V(\Xi_{\iota}^{0})}\big\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}(v^{0,0})\mathbf{N}^{n}\big\|_{1}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{c_{1}(n-m)^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota^{\prime})-1}\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}(\iota^{\prime})}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{N})\big)^{n-m}}{c_{2}n^{\displaystyle\varkappa_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota)-1}\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{N})\big)^{n}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\;\displaystyle\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}(\iota^{\prime})}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{N})\big)<\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{N})\big)\,;\\
\displaystyle\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{N})\big)^{-m}&,\;\displaystyle\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}(\iota^{\prime})}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{N})\big)=\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{N})\big)\,.\end{cases}

where the constant
c_{1},c_{2}>0
are independent of
n
.
The limit therefore always exists.
∎

Definition 4.3
.

For any positive real number
\ell>0
, define

P_{G}(\ell)=\big|\{v\in V(G):\hat{\deg}_{G}(v)=\ell\}\big|\,.

(4)

Let
G
be a degree scaling limit of a graph sequence
\mathcal{G}
.
The graph
G
is scale-free if and only if,
taking the limit
\ell\to 0
for all
\ell>0
such that
P_{G}(\ell)>0
,

\dim_{D}(G)=\lim_{\ell\to 0}\frac{\log P_{G}(\ell)}{-\log\ell}

(5)

exists and is positive, in which case this limit is the degree dimension of
G
.

Definition 4.4
.

A reducible IGS satisfies the

balanced-degree and divergent-mass
(BEDM) condition if there exist two indices

k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota)
such that

\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k_{1}}(\mathbf{N})\big)=\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k_{2}}(\mathbf{N})\big)\,,\qquad\varkappa_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota,k_{1})=\varkappa_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota,k_{2})\,,

while the corresponding blocks in the mass matrix
\mathbf{M}

satisfy

\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime}_{1}}(\mathbf{M})\big)\neq\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime}_{2}}(\mathbf{M})\big)\,.

Here,

\varkappa_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota,k_{1})
(resp.
\varkappa_{\mathbf{M}}(\iota,k_{1})
)
stands for the number of successive diagonal blocks,
counted along the block chain of
\mathbf{N}
(resp.
\mathbf{M}
) that starts at
\mathfrak{b}(\iota)
,
whose spectral radii all coincide with the maximal spectral radius attained on that chain,
as specified in the Lustig-Uyanik Theorem.

Condition (BE) says the two blocks have identical degree exponent and highest polynomial order,
while (DM) says their mass growth diverges; together, these constitute the BEDM condition.

Write

\mathscr{K}_{\mathrm{BEDM}}(\iota):=\big\{k\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota):\text{$k$ participates in the BEDM pair(s)}\big\}\,.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\mathscr{R}=(\Xi_{\iota}^{0},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{S})
be a reducible iterated graph system and
let
(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\deg}_{\Xi_{\iota}})
be its degree scaling limit.
If

\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{N})\big)>1
,
then
\Xi_{\iota}

does not
satisfy the BEDM condition if and only if

\Xi_{\iota}
is scale-free and its degree dimension is

\dim_{D}(\Xi_{\iota})=\frac{\displaystyle\log\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{M}}\circ\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{N}}^{-1}(k)}(\mathbf{M})\big)}{\log\displaystyle\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{N})\big)}\,.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.2
,
the degree-scaling limit
\big(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\deg}_{\Xi_{\iota}}\big)
exists whenever

\max_{\ell\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\ell}(\mathbf{N})\big)>1
.

Assume that the reducible IGS fails the BEDM condition.
Then, as shown in the proof of Theorem
4.2
,
the limit graph enjoys exactly one non–vanishing degree exponential growth rate;
all other potential rates are driven to
0
in the scaling limit.

For each sufficiently small
\ell
with

\displaystyle 0<\ell<\Big(\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{N})\big)\Big)^{-1}
,
define the integer depth
m=m(\ell)\geq 1
by

\Big(\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{N})\big)\Big)^{-(m-1)}\leq\ell<\Big(\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{N})\big)\Big)^{-m}\,.

We now estimate the tail function
P_{\ell}\big(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\deg}_{\Xi_{\iota}}\big)
.
Since
\ell
has been chosen so that
P_{\ell}>0
,
Lemma
2.11
implies that there are at least

m^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{M}}(\iota)-1}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{M}}\circ\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{N}}^{-1}(k^{*})}(\mathbf{M})\big)^{m}

vertices with scaled degree exceeding
\ell
,
where

\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{M}}\circ\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{N}}^{-1}(k^{*})

denotes the block of
\mathbf{M}
that contains the color achieving the global degree growth rate.
Note that Proposition
2.13
shows that all such colors lie in the same block of
\mathbf{M}
.
Thus,

P_{(\Xi_{\iota},\hat{\deg}_{\Xi_{\iota}})}(\ell)\succeq m^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{M}}(\iota)-1}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{M}}\circ\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{N}}^{-1}(k^{*})}(\mathbf{M})\big)^{m}\,.

Lemma
2.11
implies that every vertex satisfies

\hat{\deg}_{G}(v)=c_{v}\Big(\max_{k^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime}}(\mathbf{N})\big)\Big)^{-t},\qquad t\in\mathbb{N}\,,

with constants
0<c_{\min}\leq c_{v}\leq c_{\max}<\infty
independent of
v
.
If
\hat{\deg}_{G}(v)=\ell
, then

c_{\min}\Big(\max_{k^{\prime}}\rho(\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime}}(\mathbf{N}))\Big)^{-t}\leq\Big(\max_{k^{\prime}}\rho(\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime}}(\mathbf{N}))\Big)^{-m}\leq c_{\max}\Big(\max_{k^{\prime}}\rho(\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime}}(\mathbf{N}))\Big)^{-t}.

Taking

\displaystyle\max_{k^{\prime}}\rho(\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime}}(\mathbf{N}))

logarithms gives

m-\Big\lceil\log_{\max_{k^{\prime}}\rho(\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime}}(\mathbf{N}))}c_{\max}\Big\rceil\leq t\leq m+\Big\lceil\log_{\max_{k^{\prime}}\rho(\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime}}(\mathbf{N}))}c_{\min}^{-1}\Big\rceil\,.

Hence, there exist fixed integers
c_{1},c_{2}\geq 0
(independent of
m
) such that
every vertex with
\hat{\deg}_{G}(v)=\ell
is born in one of the generations
t\in\{m-c_{1},\dots,m+c_{2}\}
.
For any such generation
t
,

\big|\{v\in\Xi^{t}:\hat{\deg}_{G}(v)=\ell\}\big|\leq|V(\Xi^{t})|\leq m^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{M}}(\iota)-1}c_{4}\Big(\max_{k^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime}}(\mathbf{M})\big)\Big)^{t}\,,

where
c_{4}>0
is a constant independent of
t
.
Summing over the admissible generations yields

\displaystyle P_{G}(\ell)

\displaystyle\leq c_{4}\sum_{t=m-c_{1}}^{m+c_{2}}\Big(\max_{k^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{M}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime}}(\mathbf{M})\big)\Big)^{t}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{4}}{\max_{k^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{M}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime}}(\mathbf{M})\big)-1}\Big(\max_{k^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{M}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime}}(\mathbf{M})\big)\Big)^{m+c_{2}+1}

\displaystyle\preceq m^{\varkappa_{\mathbf{M}}(\iota)-1}\Big(\max_{k^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{M}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k^{\prime}}(\mathbf{M})\big)\Big)^{m}

Together, the above inequalities give

\frac{\log\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{M}}\circ\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{N}}^{-1}(k^{*})}(\mathbf{M})\big)}{\log\displaystyle\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{N})\big)}\leq\dim_{\mathrm{D}}(\Xi_{\iota})\leq\frac{\log\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{M}}\circ\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{N}}^{-1}(k^{*})}(\mathbf{M})\big)}{\log\displaystyle\max_{k\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathbf{N}}(\iota)}\rho\big(\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{N})\big)}\,.

Conversely, suppose that the BEDM condition is not satisfied.

\mathbf{N}
but exhibit different

in
\mathbf{M}
,

.
By contraposition, scale-free-ness with a single degree dimension

∎

Definition 4.7
.

Let
