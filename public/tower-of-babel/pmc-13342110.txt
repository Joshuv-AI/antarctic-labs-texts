Chiral Brønsted acid-catalyzed kinetic resolution of radical additions
Authors: Cheng Luo, Xin Li, Fangfang Lu, Shanshan Cao, Xiaowei Zhao, Zhiyong Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Kinetic resolution has been a cornerstone for accessing enantioenriched molecules, but its application in radical chemistry has remained elusive due to the high reactivity of radical intermediates. Here, we present a new approach enabling precise Kinetic resolution in radical addition processes, yielding enantioenriched products and recovered starting materials with high efficiency. Two examples are provided: the Kinetic resolution of Minisci reactions between N-heterobiaryls or biaryls and glycine-derived redox-active esters under visible light irradiation with a chiral Brønsted acid catalyst, achieving high yields and enantioselectivities. The second example involves the reductive coupling of aldehydes with N-heterobiaryl-based olefins, enabling efficient synthesis of axially chiral heterobiaryls featuring both axial and remote central chirality. This work represents a conceptual breakthrough in asymmetric radical reactions, inspiring future developments in radical transformations using accessible racemic feedstocks.

Introduction

Kinetic resolution (KR)1–4 stands as a cornerstone methodology in asymmetric catalytic synthesis, renowned for its distinctive capacity to concurrently furnish both enantiomerically enriched starting materials and products—thereby delivering two valuable chiral entities from a single transformation (Fig. 1a). Notably, the synthetic advantages of KR also include the direct utilization of racemic and readily available starting materials, which often pose challenges in stereocenter manipulation to produce key prochiral intermediates. Moreover, in scenarios where efficient asymmetric catalytic routes to high-value targets remain undeveloped, KR emerges as a strategic alternative, enabling direct access to enantioenriched compounds from their racemic forms—which can themselves be prepared through maximally expedient synthetic pathways. These compelling attributes have driven sustained interest in developing innovative and highly selective KR systems. However, while enantioselective radical addition reactions have been successfully established in the realm of chiral photochemistry5–20, the implementation of KR processes based on this mechanistic paradigm to construct important molecular architectures remains conspicuously unexplored.Fig. 1Research backgrounds and present work.a Profile and synthetic advantages of kinetic resolution; b Putative challenges of kinetic resolution in radical addition reactions; c Present work.

In recent years, radical chemistry has experienced rapid development, driven by the establishment of sustainable and environmentally friendly platforms for generating radical species, such as visible-light-driven photochemical reactions21,22. The exceptional reactivity of radicals enables the direct transformation of simple feedstock chemicals under mild reaction conditions with excellent functional group tolerance. However, this important and inherent property conversely poses a significant challenge to chiral catalysts in efficiently differentiating between the two enantiomers of olefin substrates (i.e., SubS and SubR) during radical addition (Fig. 1b)12–20. Moreover, it can lead to racemic background reactions, resulting in the formation of racemic products, which further compromises the efficiency of KR. In addition to affecting the selectivity factor (s-factor), radicals are prone to undergoing various side transformations in reaction systems, such as disproportionation, homocoupling, redox reactions, and hydrogen atom transfer (HAT), thereby reducing the yields of the targeted products18. Despite these formidable challenges, which have led many chemists to consider KR for radical addition reactions as an impossible task, we remain motivated to explore its feasibility. This approach would enable the direct use of diverse racemic substrates whose stereocenters cannot be ablated through various photochemical methods. Herein, we report a significant breakthrough in the field of KR, specifically in its application to radical addition reactions (Fig. 1c).

Results

Two representative reaction types involving radical addition to either azaarenes (i.e., the Minisci reaction)13–18 or vinylazaarenes (i.e., addition to olefins)19,20 have been successfully demonstrated to undergo efficient KR in the presence of a chiral Brønsted acid catalyst, with or without a photosensitizer. In the first reaction, racemic N-heterobiaryls with stable axial chirality were treated under photochemical conditions with glycine-derived redox-active esters (RAEs). This approach not only resulted in the formation of Minisci-type products but also enabled the recovery of the corresponding biaryl substrates with impressive yields and enantioselectivities (ees). Furthermore, by incorporating azaarenes as auxiliary groups during this KR transformation, we developed an efficient route for accessing both enantiomers of axially chiral molecules with exceptional outcomes. The robust capacity of KR in this crucial radical addition process is further demonstrated by its successful application in the synthesis of planar chiral molecules23. Notably, KR was also applied to the reductive coupling between racemic N-heterobiaryl-based olefins and aldehydes, yielding successful results. Beyond further validating the feasibility of KR in radical addition reactions, this methodology enables the efficient establishment of chiral secondary alcohols at distal positions relative to axial chiral centers, a task that remains highly desirable yet challenging in asymmetric synthesis24–29. This opens up a pathway for the synthesis of complex and valuable atropisomers containing multiple stereogenic elements.

The Minisci reaction represents a classical radical addition approach for the direct synthesis of azaarene derivatives from simple imine-containing azaarene precursors7. Recent advancements in this field have been significantly propelled by the emergence of the sustainable visible light-driven photoredox catalysis, which has facilitated the development of several enantioselective pathways13–18. In this context, we selected this reaction as a platform to investigate the possibility for KR, utilizing racemic 8-(2-methoxynaphthalen-1-yl)quinoline (±)-1a as a model substrate. This choice is underscored by the pivotal role that axially chiral N-heterobiaryls play in asymmetric catalysis and pharmaceuticals30–44. A series of glycine-derived RAEs45 were evaluated for their reactivity with (±)-1a in the presence of various photosensitizers and chiral Brønsted acid catalysts. In the preliminary studies, it was discovered that the transformation could proceed without requiring a photosensitizer. Additionally, chiral phosphoric acids (CPAs) emerged as promising catalysts. Consequently, subsequent investigations focused on optimizing the reaction parameters, encompassing the selection of the most suitable CPA, solvent, and temperature (refer to Table S1 in Supplementary Information (SI)).

Encouragingly, when employing 10 mol% of the 1′-spirobiindane-7,7′-diol (SPINOL)-based CPA C1, along with 0.2 equivalent of bis(1-adamantyl)-butyl-phosphane (nBuPAd2) and 1.0 equivalent of NaH2PO4 as additives, the reaction between (±)-1a and RAE-1—featuring a pivaloyl (piv) N-protecting group—afforded the corresponding Minisci-type product 2a in 48% yield with 90% ee (entry 1, Table 1). Meanwhile, the quinoline-based substrate could be recovered in 43% yield with 97% ee (i.e., 1a). The selectivity was found to be highly sensitive to the substituents at the 6,6′-positions of SPINOL; for instance, catalysts C2 and C3 resulted in lower yields, even though approximately 50% of (±)-1a was consumed (entries 2 and 3). Notably, while the enantioselectivity for 1a was moderate, 2a produced in the presence of C3 exhibited 88% ee. Further investigations involved testing other RAEs with varying electron-withdrawing groups at the N-substituent positions, including acetyl (RAE-2) and tert-butyloxycarbonyl (Boc, RAE-3). However, these modifications did not yield improved results (entries 4 and 5). It is noteworthy that the reaction did not proceed in the absence of the CPA C1 (entry 6). The inclusion of NaH2PO4 as an additive was found to enhance selectivity, likely due to its influence on the hydrogen bonding interactions between C1 and the quinoline ring (entry 7). When the transformation was attempted without nBuPAd2, no reaction was observed (entry 8). We hypothesized that nBuPAd2 might act as a mild base, facilitating the deprotonation necessary for aromatization, a critical step following the radical addition. To explore this, we tested 1.0 equivalent of NaHCO3 in place of nBuPAd2, yet no reaction occurred (entry 9). We speculated that the lack of reactivity could be attributed to the poor solubility of NaHCO3. Consequently, we added an additional 10 equivalents of H2O (entry 10). This adjustment resulted in the formation of 2a in 19% yield with 91% ee, while the recovery of 1a was 77% with only 27% ee. Extending the reaction time to 90 h improves chemical conversion (entry 11). Entry 12 shows that water addition only improves the solubility of the inorganic salt in the reaction medium. These results indicate the KR effect is sensitive to the system’s basicity, which affects the hydrogen-bonding interaction between the substrate and C1, thereby influencing EDA complex formation (vide infra) and subsequent radical processes. Control experiments further underscored the necessity of an oxygen-free environment and a suitable light source for the transformation to occur (entries 13 and 14).

With the optimal reaction conditions established, we explored the scope of this chiral Brønsted acid-catalyzed KR platform using a variety of racemic N-heterobiaryls (±)-1 (Fig. 2). We discovered that the naphthyl rings, when substituted by diverse aryl and alkyl groups, were highly compatible, yielding enantioenriched amine-functionalized products 2a–v with yields ranging from 31 to 50% and ee values between 84 and 93%. Furthermore, the desired enantiomerically pure substrates 1a–v were obtained in yields of 34 to 52%, with ee values from 81 to 99.7%. Remarkably, (±)-1 demonstrated versatility, as it tolerated various substituents on the naphthalene moiety, including fused aromatic ((±)-1g) and heteroaromatic rings ((±)-1o-p), as well as the attractive borate ((±)-1t) and phosphine ((±)-1u-v) groups, achieving satisfactory results. We then evaluated a range of intriguing substrates featuring diverse electron-rich polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, such as dibenzo[b,d]thiophene, 9H-carbazole, and dibenzo[b,d]furan, as alternatives to naphthalene for connection with quinoline. We were pleased to observe that products 2y-zb were obtained in yields of 41 to 42% with 87 to 90% ee, while enantiomeric substrates 1y-zb were recovered in 42–47% yields with 80–86% ee. The substrate (±)-1zc, representing a non-fused aromatic ring capable of generating atropisomerism, also demonstrated good tolerance, yielding satisfactory selectivity for the KR event (s = 48). Finally, we introduced substituents on the quinoline (e.g., (±)-1zd) or replaced it with phenanthridine ((±)-1ze), and the remarkable selectivity was disclosed.

Subsequently, we tested the compatibility of this KR tool using azaarenes as auxiliary groups. Notably, the axially chiral molecule [1,1′-binaphthalen]-2-amine was functionalized with an 8-quinolyl group on its amine moiety (i.e., (±)-3a, Fig. 3a). The pronounced spatial separation between the axial chirality and the site of radical addition presented significant challenges for the chiral acid catalyst in achieving precise KR. However, thorough investigation revealed that the reaction of (±)-3a with RAE−1 yielded product 4a in 43% yield with 87% ee under conditions involving 1.0 mol% of a dicyanopyrazine-derived chromophore (DPZ)12, 10 mol% of SPINOL-based CPA C4, 0.2 equivalents of nBuPAd2, and a temperature of −60 °C. The recovered substrate (i.e., 3a) was obtained in 46% yield with 88% ee. Control experiments indicated no reaction occurred in the absence of DPZ (Table S2 in the SI). Subsequently, we explored a series of [1,1′-binaphthalen]-2-amines with various substituents on the naphthyl ring, which led to products 4b–e being produced in yields ranging from 35 to 45% with ee values between 83 and 91%. Other axially chiral amines were also examined, achieving satisfactory selectivity (s = 28–49) and yielding corresponding products 4f–h in 32 to 36% with ee values of 84 to 88%. The starting substrates 3f–h were recovered with yields of 36 to 44% with 80 to 93% ee. We also investigated the compatibility of other N-azaaryl substituents. Notably, when employing 5-methoxy-8-quinolyl as the protective group for the nitrogen atom, product 4i was obtained in 43% yield with 90% ee. The azaaryl group of 4i can be readily cleaved utilizing ceric ammonium nitrate (CAN), resulting in axially chiral [1,1′-binaphthalen]-2-amine 5i in 57% yield with 81% ee (Fig. 3b). Furthermore, the ee of the recovered substrate 3i was slightly improved to 93% after recrystallization. The application of CAN also provided product ent-5i, the enantiomer of 5i, in 63% yield with 87% ee. [2.2]Paracyclophane derivatives represent a class of typical planar chiral molecules that have garnered significant attention due to their diverse applications, particularly in asymmetric catalysis23. In this context, we synthesized single quinoline-functionalized variants to evaluate KR of these compounds via asymmetric photoredox catalysis (Fig. 3c). Utilizing SPINOL-derived CPA C5 as the catalyst and DPZ as the photosensitizer, we observed that RAE−1 reacted efficiently with the representative substrates (±)-6a‒(±)-6d. The results are remarkable, showcasing not only high yields but also impressive ee values for the resulting products 7a–d, as well as for the recovered substrates 6a–d. This highlights the robustness and versatility of our KR platform, despite the highly reactive nature of the radical addition process.

Subsequently, the transformation of (±)-1a with RAE-1 was selected as a representative reaction for investigating the plausible mechanism. We measured the UV/vis spectra of various species and their distinct combinations within the reaction system (left picture, Fig. 4a). The results clearly indicated the remarkable formation of an electron donor-acceptor (EDA) complex48 (purple line) between (±)-1a (grey line) and chiral catalyst C1 (dark red line). We hypothesize that the two enantiomers of (±)-1a, namely 1a and ent-1a, exhibit different abilities to interact with C1, due to their opposing steric effects. To further explore this, we surveyed the UV/vis spectra of ent-1a in the presence of C1 and ent-C1 (middle picture, Fig. 4a). A slight red shift was observed when ent-1a interacted with ent-C1. In contrast, ent-1a can form an EDA complex with C1. We attribute this difference in EDA complex formation to distinct thermodynamic interactions between C1 and the two enantiomeric pairs, as confirmed by 1H-NMR titration experiments. DFT calculations were also performed to explore the underlying thermodynamic factors (Supplementary Data 1 for electronic structure calculations, and Supplementary Data 2 for Cartesian coordinates). Additionally, the UV/vis spectra illustrated in right picture (Fig. 4a) demonstrated that nBuPAd2 adversely affects the formation of the EDA complex between (±)-1a and C1. Emission quenching experiments showed that no fluorescence quenching of (±)-1a, C1, or RAE-1 was observed upon addition of nBuPAd2, regardless of the presence or absence of C1. From the results, it is evident that KR can arise from two potential mechanisms: first, the differing tendencies of the two enantiomers of (±)-1a to form EDA complexes with C1, suggesting that KR is facilitated by a thermodynamic difference during the formation of these complexes49; second, the distinct capacity of C1 to activate both 1a and ent-1a for addition by α-amino radicals derived from REA-1. In this context, 1a with 90% ee was subjected to reaction with RAE-1 under established conditions (Fig. 4b). The product ent−2a was isolated in 18% yield with 4% ee, while 1a was recovered in 71% yield with 99% ee. Notably, when ent-C1 was employed instead of C1, ent-2a was obtained in 75% yield with 97% ee, and the surplus of 1a was recovered in 11% yield with 60% ee. We then explored the reaction between (±)-1a and RAE-1 under standard conditions but utilized two 3 W blue LEDs with a laser line filter, emitting wavelengths above 490 nm (Fig. 4c). As a result, no reaction occurred, suggesting that the EDA complex cannot be activated with such long-wavelength photons. In this context, we introduced 1.0 mol% DPZ as a photosensitizer, which facilitated the transformation, yielding 2a in 36% yield with 91% ee, while 1a was recovered in 45% yield with 82% ee. The selectivity observed under these conditions was comparable to that obtained in the initial experiments (entry 1, Table 1), further underscoring the pivotal role of the chiral catalyst in activating azaarenes through H-bonding interactions. This strategy leverages the thermodynamic differences in the chiral catalyst’s interactions with the two enantiomers, thereby facilitating an efficient pathway for achieving precise KR during the radical addition process.

Based on the preceding findings, we propose a plausible mechanism for the KR in the chiral Brønsted acid-catalyzed Minisci-type reaction, as illustrated in Fig. 4d. The reaction initiates with the formation of an EDA complex 8 between ent-1a and the chiral catalyst C1. Upon irradiation, a single electron transfer occurs between ent-1a and C1, generating radicals 9 and 10. The formation of 10 is confirmed by a control experiment using 2,6-di-tert-butyl-4-methylphenol (BHT) as a radical-trapping reagent. Radical 10 is subsequently oxidized by RAE-1, resulting in the release of CO2, the regeneration of ent-1a, and the formation of anion 12. Concurrently, the α-amino radical 11 derived from RAE-1 adds to complex 8, driven by the more favorable interaction of C1 with ent-1a, resulting in radical cation 13. The α-amino radical, subsequently generated from 13 with the deprotonation of nBuPAd250, which exhibits mild basicity, undergoes single-electron oxidation by radical 9 to yield the corresponding product 2a. Meanwhile, the resulting phosphonium cation is regenerated to nBuPAd2 in the presence of anion 12, concomitantly affording isoindoline-1,3-dione 14 as a by-product.

In contrast, due to the diminished interaction of 1a with C1, its reactivity is significantly lower than that of ent-1a, allowing for the successful accumulation of 1a. This mechanistic insight emphasizes the critical role of the chiral catalyst in governing both the selectivity and efficiency of the reaction. Notably, extending the reaction time can compromise the ee of the products. In this context, selectivity may deteriorate as the concentration of the enantiomer (e.g., ent-1a), which is predominantly activated by the chiral catalyst, gradually decreases.

Inspired by this success, we undertook an investigation into the feasibility of KR for new radical addition reaction types, thereby enhancing the reliability of the proof-of-concept51. Consequently, we explored the reductive coupling reaction between racemic N-heterobiaryl-based olefin ((±)-13) and benzaldehyde (14a) (Fig. 5a). To our satisfaction, in the presence of CPA C14 as the chiral catalyst and diethyl 2,6-dimethyl-1,4-dihydropyridine−3,5-dicarboxylate (HEH) as the terminal reductant at −30 °C in CH2Cl2 solvent, product 15a, containing an axially chiral center and a remote chiral secondary alcohol, was obtained in 44% yield with 98% ee and 6:1 dr. Simultaneously, enantioenriched substrate 13a was achieved in 48% yield and 90% ee. These results robustly demonstrated the viability of directly accessing such attractive axially chiral olefins via this KR approach. To enhance the synthetic utility of this method by efficiently producing complex atropisomer products featuring intriguing double stereogenic elements, we conducted the reaction using 2.5 equivalents of (±)-13. As a result, the yield and dr of 15a were improved to 85% and 10:1, respectively. In this context, a range of aldehydes and olefins were evaluated to preliminarily delineate the potential substrate scope (Fig. 5b). It was observed that aldehydes bearing electron-withdrawing or electron-donating groups on the aromatic rings of aryl substituents, as well as heteroaromatic rings as substituents, were highly compatible, yielding products 15b–g in 76–87% yields with 95–98% ee and 9:1–10:1 dr. Discussion

In summary, the incorporation of a chiral Brønsted acid catalyst in conjunction with visible light irradiation enables successful KR for radical addition to both azaarenes and vinylazaarenes. This is achieved despite the presence of several inherent challenges in attaining precise chemo- and enantioselectivity within the reaction system. Such a robust proof-of-concept for asymmetric radical chemistry inspires further investigation into kinetic resolution for more elusive radical-based processes. Ultimately, this research paves the way for asymmetric photocatalysis to become an even more powerful tool for the precise synthesis of valuable enantioenriched compounds.
