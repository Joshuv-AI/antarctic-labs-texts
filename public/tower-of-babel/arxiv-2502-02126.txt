On a Brain Tumor Growth Model with Lactate Metabolism, Viscoelastic Effects, and Tissue Damage


Abstract
In this paper, we study a nonlinearly coupled initial-boundary value problem describing the evolution of brain tumor growth including lactate metabolism. In our modeling approach, we also take into account the viscoelastic properties of the tissues as well as the reversible damage effects that could occur, possibly caused by surgery. After introducing the PDE system, coupling a Fischer–Kolmogorov type equation for the tumor phase with a reaction-diffusion equation for the lactate, a quasi-static momentum balance with nonlinear elasticity and viscosity matrices, and a nonlinear differential inclusion for the damage, we prove the existence of global in time weak solutions under reasonable assumptions on the involved functions and data. Strengthening these assumptions, we subsequently prove further regularity properties of the solutions as well as their continuous dependence with respect to the data, entailing the well-posedness of the Cauchy problem associated with the nonlinear PDE system. Key words: nonlinear initial-boundary value problem, reaction-diffusion equation, Fischer–Kolmogorov type equation, well-posedness, regularity results, tumor growth models. AMS (MOS) Subject Classification: 35K61, 35K57, 35D30, 35Q92, 35B65, 92C50.

Introduction

In this paper, we study an initial-boundary value problem describing the dynamics of a brain tumor including lactate metabolism, viscoelastic effects of the tissues as well as their possible damage.
Four nonlinearly coupled PDEs describe the evolution of the concentrations of tumor denoted by
\varphi
, the intracellular lactate

\sigma
, the “small” displacement
\boldsymbol{u}
, and the damage parameter
z
. The equation for
\sigma
is based on the derivation done in
[
Aub+05
]
and in
[
Gui+11
]
. Actually, in the first brain lactate kinetics models in the literature (cf.
[
Che+22
]
,
[
Gui+18
]
and references therein) the authors dealt with the evolution of both capillary and intracellular lactate concentrations. However, since
here we had in mind to include the displacement and damage evolution in the model, we neglect the capillary lactate concentration in order to simplify the PDE system, which, in our case, turns out to be the following one:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi-\Delta\varphi=p(\sigma,z)\varphi\left(1-\frac{\varphi}{N}\right)-\varphi g(\sigma,z),

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma-\Delta\sigma+\frac{k_{1}(\varphi,z)\sigma}{k_{2}(\varphi,z)+\sigma}=J(\varphi,z),

(1.1b)

\displaystyle-\diver\left[\mathcal{A}(\varphi,z)\varepsilon(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u})+\mathcal{B}(\varphi,z)\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{u})\right]=\boldsymbol{f},

(1.1c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}z-\Delta z+\beta(z)+\pi(z)\ni w-\Psi(\varphi,\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{u})),

(1.1d)

posed in
Q\coloneqq\Omega\times(0,T)
, where
\Omega
is a bounded
C^{2}
domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n=2,3
and
T>0
is a fixed time. Regarding the boundary conditions, we assume the system is isolated from the exterior, so we prescribe no-flux conditions for
\varphi
and
z
. Regarding the lactate
\sigma
, we allow more general
Robin conditions, with the physical constants set to 1 for simplicity. Since our model is specifically designed for brain tumors, the domain is confined by a rigid boundary, the cranium, which prevents displacement at the boundary. Consequently, it is a natural choice to impose homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions for the displacement
\boldsymbol{u}
. Therefore, we
couple the previous system with the following boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\varphi=\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}z=0,

(1.2a)

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\sigma=\sigma_{\Gamma}-\sigma,

(1.2b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}=\mathbf{0}\,.

(1.2c)

Finally, we consider the following usual initial conditions:

\varphi(0)=\varphi_{0},\quad\sigma(0)=\sigma_{0},\quad\boldsymbol{u}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\quad z(0)=z_{0}\,.

(1.3)

The coupling between equations (
1.1a
) and (
1.1b
) was studied in
[
Che+22
]
, while models including the effects of the stress (reducing the proliferation of the tumor) were already introduced and studied in
[
GLS21
]
, where a Cahn–Hilliard type dynamics for the tumor concentration was used in place of the Fischer–Kolmogorov type equation (
1.1a
).
Finally, let us mention the recent work
[
Cav24
]
, by one of the authors of the present contribution, where a system coupling tumor growth dynamics of Cahn–Hilliard type together with displacement and damage was first analyzed and the existence of weak solutions was proved for the corresponding initial-boundary value problem.
In our setting, the Fischer–Kolmogorov type equation (
1.1a
) (cf.
[
Fis37
]
,
[
KPP37
]
) describes the evolution of tumor cell concentration, denoted by
\varphi
, which takes values in the interval
[0,N]
. The constant
N
represents the carrying capacity, which is defined as the maximum number of cells per unit volume. The balance between proliferation and
apoptosis is taken into account through the rate
p
, while we account for necrosis through the rate
g
, where both
p
and
g
possibly depend on the lactate proportion
\sigma
and the damage parameter
z
.

The reaction-diffusion equation (
1.1b
) represents the evolution of intracellular lactate production
\sigma
. Since lactate diffusion from cells to capillaries is coupled with the transport of hydrogen ions
H+
, in symport
terms we have positive functions
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
, representing the ratios between a Michaelis–Menten
constant and the concentration of
H+
in the intracellular space and in the capillary
compartment, respectively.
The positive function
J
collects the production of lactate in cells by glycolysis, the consumption of lactate by metabolism, and the diffusion of lactate in neighboring regions.
Moreover, we assume that
k_{1}
,
k_{2}
, and
J
are possibly dependent on the other variables
\varphi
and
z
.

The main novelty of this contribution relies on the fact that here we include the mechanics in the model by assuming a viscoelastic behavior of biological tissues.
It is indeed well known that solid stress can aﬀect tumor growth (see e.g.
[
Urc+22
]
) and, at the same time, tumor growth increases mechanical stress. Due to the complexity of the problem, as a first approach here we
assume infinitesimal displacements, so we work in the case of linear elasticity (cf. also
[
GLS21
]
and
[
GLS21a
]
for similar derivations).
The evolution of the small displacement
\boldsymbol{u}
is then ruled by the vectorial quasi-stationary balance law (
1.1c
), where the two tensors
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
describe the elastic and viscous effects. They may depend on the tumor and damage variables, as well as on
\sigma
in a non-degenerating way (cf. assumptions (
2.11
) later on). For a more detailed discussion of this dependency, we refer to the following
Remark

3.4
. Finally,
\boldsymbol{f}
represents a given volume force.
Let us notice that the study of the mechanical effects in tumor growth models results particularly important in the case of brain (glioma) tumors, where the evolution strongly depends on the tissues (see e.g.,
[
Alf+17
]
and references therein).

Finally, following the ideas developed in
[
Cav24
]
, we consider, through equation (
1.1d
), the possible damage effects that could occur for example in case of surgery that causes lesions which, in turn, affect
the proliferation of tumor cells. Following the derivation in Frémond and Nedjar (cf.
[
FN95
]
,
[
FN96
]
,
[
Fré02
]
), the evolution of the damage parameter
z\in[0,1]
(
z=0
means the tissue is completely damaged and
z=1
means completely safe) is ruled by the evolution inclusion (
1.1d
), where the maximal monotone graph
\beta
having bounded domain (in
[0,1]
) forces the variable
z
to assume the physically meaningful values in between
0
and
1
. A simple choice for
\beta
could be the subdifferential of the indicator function of the set
[0,1]
, which takes value
0
in
[0,1]
and is
+\infty
elsewhere. The function
\pi
denotes a regular, possibly non-monotone function,
w
represents an energy threshold for initiation of damage, while
\Psi
describes the coupling between the damage and the displacement along with the tumor concentration. Usually, in damage models (cf., e.g.,
[
HK11
]
,
[
RR14
]
,
[
HR15
]
,
[
MRZ10
]
,
[
TM10
]
) the dependence of
\Psi
on
\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{u})
is quadratic because it comes from the derivative of the elastic part of the energy with respect to the damage variable
z
. However, here we cannot handle a quadratic dependence and so we assume a Lipschitz-continuity dependence of
\Psi
with respect to both
\varphi
and
\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{u})
(cf. assumption
7
later on), cf. also
[
KS06
]
.

The main mathematical difficulties arise here from here on the nonlinear coupling between equations and, in particular, on the dependence of the elasticity and viscosity matrices in (
1.1c
) by
\varphi
and
z
.

The technique used in the proof relies on a suitable maximum principle coupled with Moser estimates to prove the boundedness of
\varphi
and
\sigma
, as well as a fixed-point argument, suitable a priori estimates and compactness results entailing on the sufficient weak, weak-star and strong convergences in the approximating sequences in order to pass to the limit and identify the nonlinearities.

Hence, the plan of the paper is the following: in the next Section
2
we set the assumptions on the data and we state our main results concerning the global (in time) existence of weak solutions, the regularity results under more restrictive assumptions on the data, as well as the continuous dependence of solutions with respect to the data. In Subsection
3.1
we prove the first result through a Schauder fixed-point argument including a maximum principle and a Moser argument in order to prove the constraints on the variables
\varphi
and
\sigma
as well as a time discretization of the displacement equation (
1.1c
) and a regularization of the graph
\beta
by means of its Yosida approximation
\beta_{\lambda}
. The proof is then concluded by passing to the limit in both the time step
\tau
and the regularization parameter
\lambda
.
In Section
3.2
we prove a higher regularity result which is then needed in order to get uniqueness and continuous dependence of solutions with respect to the data (cf. Section
3.3
).

Many relevant issues arise from this problem and will be the subject of further investigations, like the optimal control problem that is of particular importance when administrating drugs or antiangiogenic therapies to the patient. In this direction, we refer to
[
Che+22
]
and
[
Gui+18
]
, where damage and mechanical effects are not taken into account. For a different perspective, we also refer to
[
GLS21a
]
, where the proposed system, unlike ours, is not specifically tailored for brain tumors and again, it does not include tissue damage.

Theorem 3.6

The proof of

3.1.1
The approximate system

The approximate problem is obtained by replacing the maximal monotone operator
\beta
with its Yosida approximation
\beta_{\lambda}\coloneqq({\widehat{\beta}_{\lambda}})^{\prime}
, where
\lambda\in(0,\lambda^{*})
is intended to go to
0
in the limit. Moreover, we introduce the truncation function

\alpha(\varphi)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\varphi\left(1-\frac{\varphi}{N}\right)&\text{ if }0\leq\varphi\leq N\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\\
\end{cases}

(3.2)

and we use it in the (

to be sure that it does not vanish. This way, we get the approximate PDE system:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi-\Delta\varphi=p(\sigma,z)\alpha(\varphi)-\varphi g(\sigma,z),

(3.3a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma-\Delta\sigma+\frac{k_{1}(\varphi,z)\sigma}{k_{2}(\varphi,z)+|\sigma|}=J(\varphi,z),

(3.3b)

\displaystyle-\diver\left[\mathcal{A}(\varphi,z)\varepsilon(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u})+\mathcal{B}(\varphi,z)\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{u})\right]=\boldsymbol{f},

(3.3c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}z-\Delta z+\beta_{\lambda}(z)+\pi(z)=w-\Psi(\varphi,\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{u})).

(3.3d)

\displaystyle\varphi(0)=\varphi_{0},\quad\sigma(0)=\sigma_{0},\quad\boldsymbol{u}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\quad z(0)=z_{0}\quad\text{a.e. in }\Omega

a.e. in
(0,T)
, for every

Proof.

The proof is based on the Schauder fixed-point argument (see e.g.
[
Bré11
, p. 179]
). We introduce the Banach space

\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\{(\sigma,z)\in L^{2}(0,T;H)\times L^{2}(0,T;L^{\infty})\}

endowed with the standard norm

\lVert(\sigma,z)\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}\coloneq\lVert\sigma\rVert_{L^{2}(H)}+\lVert z\rVert_{L^{2}(L^{\infty})}.

In what follows, we construct an operator
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
, to which we intend to apply the Schauder fixed-point argument.

Step 1.
Starting from
(\overline{\sigma},\overline{z})\in\mathcal{X}
, we find
\varphi\in H^{1}(0,T;H)\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;V)\cap L^{2}(0,T;W)
as the unique solution of the semilinear parabolic equation with Lipschitz continuous nonlinearity

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\varphi-\Delta\varphi=p(\overline{\sigma},\overline{z})\alpha(\varphi)-\varphi g(\overline{\sigma},\overline{z})&\text{ in }Q,\\
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\varphi=0&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
\varphi(0)=\varphi_{0}&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{cases}

(3.11)

exploiting that
g(\overline{\sigma},\overline{z})\in L^{\infty}(Q)
and
p(\overline{\sigma},\overline{z})\in L^{\infty}(Q)
. The well-posedness of this system can be proved in several classical ways, such as using Galerkin discretization (see, e.g.,
[
Trö10
, Lemma 5.3, p. 373]
) or semigroup theory (see, e.g.,
[
Pat19
, Chapter 20]
). The regularity can be shown by standard results for linear parabolic equations (see
[
DL92
,
Lio61
]
), from which we obtain the estimate

\lVert\varphi\rVert_{H^{1}(H)\cap L^{\infty}(V)\cap L^{2}(W)}\leq C,

(3.12)

for a certain positive constant
C
independent from
\lambda
and
(\overline{\sigma},\overline{z})
. Next, we aim to prove that

0\leq\varphi\leq N.

(3.13)

To do so, we test the first equation of the system (
3.11
) with
(\varphi-N)^{+}
and we integrate over
\Omega
. Thanks to the boundary condition, we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\textrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t\,}\int_{\Omega}|(\varphi-N)^{+}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\textrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t\,}\int_{\Omega}|(\varphi-N)^{+}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla[(\varphi-N)^{+}]|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}p(\overline{\varphi},\overline{z})\alpha(\varphi)(\varphi-N)^{+}\,\mathrm{d}x\,-\int_{\Omega}\varphi g(\overline{\sigma},\overline{z})(\varphi-N)^{+}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\leq 0,

where the last inequality holds because where
\varphi\geq N
by definition
\alpha(\varphi)=0
(so the first addend is equal to 0) and
\varphi
is trivially positive (so the second addend is non-positive). Integrating in time, it follows that

\int_{\Omega}|(\varphi-N)^{+}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\leq\int_{\Omega}|(\varphi_{0}-N)^{+}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,=0,

employing the Hypothesis
9
according to which
\varphi_{0}\leq N
. As a consequence,
\varphi\leq N
a.e. in
Q
. In a very similar way, we test the same equation by
-\varphi^{-}
and integrate over
\Omega
, obtaining:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\textrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t\,}\int_{\Omega}|\varphi^{-}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\textrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t\,}\int_{\Omega}|\varphi^{-}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi^{-}|\,\mathrm{d}x\,

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}p(\overline{\sigma},\overline{z})\alpha(\varphi)\varphi^{-}\,\mathrm{d}x\,-\int_{\Omega}|\varphi^{-}|^{2}g(\overline{\sigma},\overline{z})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\leq 0.

Integrating in time, it follows that

\int_{\Omega}|\varphi^{-}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\leq\int_{\Omega}|\varphi_{0}^{-}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,=0,

since
\varphi_{0}\geq 0
by Hypothesis
9
, so
\varphi\geq 0
a.e. in
Q
. Notice that we can consequentially remove the truncation
\alpha
.

Step 2.
Starting from
(\overline{\sigma},\overline{z})
and
\varphi
, we find
\sigma\in H^{1}(0,T;V^{\prime})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;H)\cap L^{2}(0,T;V)
as the unique solution of the following linear parabolic equation

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma-\Delta\sigma+\frac{k_{1}(\varphi,\overline{z})\sigma}{k_{2}(\varphi,\overline{z})+|\overline{\sigma}|}=J(\varphi,\overline{z})&\text{in }Q,\\
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\sigma+\sigma-\sigma_{\Gamma}=0&\text{on }\Sigma,\\
\sigma(0)=\sigma_{0}&\text{in }\Omega.\end{cases}

(3.14)

Using Hypothesis
2
and standard regularity results (see, e.g.,
[
Lio61
]
), we also have that

\lVert\sigma\rVert_{H^{1}(V^{\prime})\cap L^{\infty}(H)\cap L^{2}(V)}\leq C,

(3.15)

for a positive constant
C
that does not depend on
\lambda
,
(\overline{\sigma},\overline{z})
and
\varphi
. Now we want to prove that there exists a positive constant
M
that depends only on
M_{0}
,
J^{*}
and
T
such that

0\leq\sigma\leq M

(3.16)

almost everywhere in
Q
.
Multiplying the first equation in (
3.14
) with
-\sigma^{-}
, integrating over
\Omega
and employing the boundary condition, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t\,}\int_{\Omega}|\sigma^{-}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\text{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t\,}\int_{\Omega}|\sigma^{-}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\sigma^{-}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,+\int_{\Gamma}|\sigma^{-}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}S\,+\int_{\Omega}\frac{k_{1}(\varphi,\overline{z})|\sigma^{-}|^{2}}{k_{2}(\varphi,\overline{z})+|\overline{\sigma}|}\,\mathrm{d}x\,

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}J(\varphi,\overline{z})\sigma^{-}\,\mathrm{d}x\,-\int_{\Gamma}\sigma_{\Gamma}\sigma^{-}\,\mathrm{d}S\,\leq 0.

Integrating in time over
(0,t)
, we have

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\sigma^{-}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\sigma_{0}^{-}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,=0,

where the last equality stands because of Hypothesis
9
. As a consequence,
\sigma\geq 0
a.e. in
Q
. Finally, we employ a standard Moser–Alikakos technique to prove that
\sigma\leq M
for a certain
M>0
yet to be found. We multiply the first equation in (
3.14
) by
q\sigma^{q-1}
with
q>2
and integrating in space over
\Omega
. Notice that, in order to be sure that all the integrals are well-defined, one should introduce a truncation of
\sigma
,

\sigma_{k}\coloneq\begin{cases}\sigma&\text{ if }\sigma\leq k\\
k&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, multiply the previous equation by
q(\sigma_{k})^{q-1}
and proceed as we will do. In the end, having obtained an estimate that does not depend on
k
, one should pass to the limit as

and recover the thesis. We will not do it in this rigorous way to avoid overloading the notation and we proceed formally testing the first equation in (
3.14
) by
q\sigma^{q-1}
, obtaining

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t\,}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x\,+q(q-1)\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{q-2}|\nabla\sigma|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,+q\int_{\Gamma}\sigma^{q}\,\mathrm{d}S\,+q\int_{\Omega}\frac{k_{1}(\varphi,\overline{z})\sigma^{q}}{k_{2}(\varphi,\overline{z})+|\overline{\sigma}|}\,\mathrm{d}x\,

\displaystyle=q\int_{\Gamma}\sigma_{\Gamma}\sigma^{q-1}\,\mathrm{d}S\,+q\int_{\Omega}J(\varphi,\overline{z})\sigma^{q-1}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\leq qM_{0}\int_{\Gamma}\sigma^{q-1}\,\mathrm{d}S\,+qJ^{*}\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{q-1}\,\mathrm{d}x\,

\displaystyle\leq(q-1)\int_{\Gamma}\sigma^{q}\,\mathrm{d}S\,+(M_{0})^{q}|\Gamma|+(q-1)\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x\,+(J^{*})^{q}|\Omega|

having used the boundedness of
\sigma_{\Gamma}
and
J
from Hypotheses
8
and
2
and then the Young inequality with exponents
q/(q-1)
and
q
. Doing the obvious simplification in the previous inequality and employing the fact that
\sigma
is non-negative, we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t\,}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x\,

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\text{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t\,}\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x\,+q(q-1)\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{q-2}|\nabla\sigma|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,+\int_{\Gamma}\sigma^{q}\,\mathrm{d}S\,+q\int_{\Omega}\frac{k_{1}(\varphi,\overline{z})\sigma^{q}}{k_{2}(\varphi,\overline{z})+|\overline{\sigma}|}\,\mathrm{d}x\,

\displaystyle\leq(M_{0})^{q}|\Gamma|+(q-1)\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x\,+(J^{*})^{q}|\Omega|.

Integrating with respect to time over
(0,t)
, knowing that by Hypothesis
9

\sigma_{0}\leq M_{0}
, we find

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{q}(t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\sigma_{0}^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x\,+(M_{0})^{q}|\Gamma|T+(J^{*})^{q}|\Omega|T+(q-1)\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\,\mathrm{d}t\,

\displaystyle\leq(M_{0})^{q}(|\Omega|+|\Gamma|T)+(J^{*})^{q}|\Omega|T+(q-1)\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\,\mathrm{d}t\,,

from which we deduce

\lVert\sigma(t)\rVert_{L^{q}}\leq\left[M_{0}(|\Omega|+|\Gamma|T)^{\frac{1}{q}}+J^{*}|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{q}}T^{\frac{1}{q}}\right]e^{\frac{q-1}{q}T}\eqcolon C_{q}

using Gronwall inequality and then taking the
q^{th}
-root of both sides of the inequality. Passing to the limit as
q\to+\infty
, observing that by easy calculations
C_{q}\to M_{0}e^{T}
or
C_{q}\to J^{*}e^{T}
, we have

\lVert\sigma(t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq M\coloneq\max\{M_{0},J^{*}\}e^{T}

for a.e.

.

Step 3.
Starting from
\varphi
and
\overline{z}
, we find
\boldsymbol{u}\in W^{1,\infty}(0,T;V_{0})
as the unique solution of

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left[\mathcal{A}(\varphi,\overline{z})\varepsilon(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u})+\mathcal{B}(\varphi,\overline{z})\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{u})\right]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}x\,=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x\,&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in V_{0},\\

To do so, we proceed by time discretization. We consider a uniform partition of

and equidistant nodes

I_{\tau}^{k}\coloneqq\begin{cases}[0,\tau]&\text{ if }k=1,\\
(t_{\tau}^{k-1},\ t_{\tau}^{k}]&\text{ if }k=2\dots K_{\tau}.\end{cases}

We approximate

,

, and

with their local means, i.e., we define

\boldsymbol{f}_{\tau}^{k}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t_{\tau}^{k-1}}^{t_{\tau}^{k}}\boldsymbol{f}\,\mathrm{d}s,\qquad\varphi_{\tau}^{k}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t_{\tau}^{k-1}}^{t_{\tau}^{k}}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}s,\qquad z_{\tau}^{k}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t_{\tau}^{k-1}}^{t_{\tau}^{k}}\overline{z}\,\mathrm{d}s,

for every

\mathcal{A}_{\tau}^{k}\coloneqq\mathcal{A}(\varphi_{\tau}^{k},z_{\tau}^{k}),\qquad\mathcal{B}_{\tau}^{k}\coloneqq\mathcal{B}(\varphi_{\tau}^{k},z_{\tau}^{k}).

Remark 3.9
.

It is obvious that, since
\varphi,\overline{z}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;V)
and
\boldsymbol{f}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H)
, then

\varphi_{\tau}^{k},z_{\tau}^{k}\in V
and
\boldsymbol{f}_{\tau}^{k}\in H
with

\lVert\varphi_{\tau}^{k}\rVert_{V}\leq\lVert\varphi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(V)},\quad\lVert z_{\tau}^{k}\rVert_{V}\leq\lVert\overline{z}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(V)},\quad\lVert\boldsymbol{f}_{\tau}^{k}\rVert_{H}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\infty}(H)},

(3.18)

for every
k=1,\dots,K_{\tau}
.

Starting from
\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{\tau}=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in V_{0}
, we solve recursively the following time-discrete problem:

\begin{cases}\text{Given }\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}^{k-1}\in V_{0},\text{ find }\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}^{k}\in V_{0}\text{ s.t. for all }\boldsymbol{v}\in V_{0}\\
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{A}_{\tau}^{k}\varepsilon\bigg(\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}^{k}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}^{k-1}}{\tau}\bigg)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}x\,+\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{B}_{\tau}^{k}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}^{k})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}x\,=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}_{\tau}^{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x\,.\end{cases}

(3.19)

Since the equation in (

\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{A}_{\tau}^{k}+\tau\mathcal{B}_{\tau}^{k})\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}^{k})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}x\,=\int_{\Omega}\tau\boldsymbol{f}_{\tau}^{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\mathcal{A}_{\tau}^{k}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}^{k-1})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}x\,

where
\mathcal{A}_{\tau}^{k}+\tau\mathcal{B}_{\tau}^{k}
is strictly positive definite and the right-hand side yields a linear bounded functional on

, the existence follows from Lax–Milgram Theorem. Now our aim is to pass to the limit in the discrete equation, recovering a solution to the original problem. For the sake of brevity, we introduce the shorter notation

\boldsymbol{v}_{\tau}^{k}\coloneqq\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}^{k}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}^{k-1}}{\tau}

to denote the discrete velocity. Taking
\boldsymbol{v}_{\tau}^{k}
as a test function in equation (

), it is easy to prove that

\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{\tau}^{k}\rVert_{V_{0}}\leq C\left(\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(H)}+\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\rVert_{V_{0}}\right),

(3.21)

where the constant
C
depends on
T
but non on

,
\lambda
,
\varphi_{\tau}^{k}
, and

.
As a consequence, since

\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}^{k}=\tau\sum_{j=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{v}_{\tau}^{j}+\boldsymbol{u}_{0},

we also have that

\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}^{k}\rVert_{V_{0}}\leq C

(3.22)

where, again
C
depends on
\boldsymbol{f}
,
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
and
T
but not on
k
,
\lambda
,
\varphi_{\tau}^{k}
, and
z_{\tau}^{k}
.
Given any sequence of scalar, vector-valued or tensor-value functions
\{w_{\tau}^{k}\}_{k=0}^{K_{\tau}}
defined over
\Omega
, we introduce the piecewise constant interpolations
w_{\tau}
and the piecewise linear interpolation
\widehat{w}_{\tau}
over the time interval
[0,T]
as

w_{\tau}(t)\coloneqq w_{\tau}^{k},\qquad\widehat{w}_{\tau}(t)\coloneqq\frac{t-t_{\tau}^{k-1}}{\tau}w_{\tau}^{k}+\frac{t_{\tau}^{k}-t}{\tau}w_{\tau}^{k-1}

for every

\partial_{t}\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\tau}=\boldsymbol{v}_{\tau}
and estimates (

\lVert\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\tau}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}(V_{0})}+\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(V_{0})}\leq C.

By standard compactness results, we deduce that there exists a
\boldsymbol{u}\in W^{1,\infty}(0,T;V_{0})
such that

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\tau}\to\boldsymbol{u}\qquad\text{weakly-}\ast

\displaystyle\qquad\text{in }W^{1,\infty}(0,T;V_{0}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}\to\boldsymbol{u}\qquad\text{weakly-}\ast

\boldsymbol{f}_{\tau}\to\boldsymbol{f}\qquad\text{strongly}\qquad\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;H)

\varphi_{\tau}\to\varphi,\,\,z_{\tau}\to\overline{z}\qquad\text{a.e.}\qquad\text{in }Q.

+C_{\eta}\bigg(\int_{\Omega}|\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,+\int_{\Omega}|z|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\bigg),\end{split}
