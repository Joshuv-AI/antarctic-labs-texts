The Noisy Quantitative Group Testing Problem
Tenghao Li, Neha Sangwan, Xiaxin Li, Arya Mazumdar

Abstract
In this paper, we study the problem of quantitative group testing (QGT) and analyze the performance of three models: the noiseless model, the additive Gaussian noise model, and the noisy Z-channel model. For each model, we analyze two algorithmic approaches: a linear estimator based on correlation scores, and a least squares estimator (LSE). We derive upper bounds on the number of tests required for exact recovery with vanishing error probability, and complement these results with information-theoretic lower bounds. In the additive Gaussian noise setting, our lower and upper bounds match in order.



1
Introduction

Quantitative group testing (QGT)
[
3
]
[
4
]
[
9
]
generalizes classical group testing by observing, for each pooled test, the
number
of defectives in the pool.
Compared with binary outcomes, quantitative measurements can substantially reduce the number of tests required for exact support recovery and can enable simple and efficient decoding algorithms.
In this work, we study QGT under a non-adaptive random testing design in the sublinear regime, where the number of defectives grows sublinearly with the total number of items, and we focus on three canonical observation models.

1.1
Noiseless Model

In the
noiseless
QGT model, each test returns the exact number of defective items included in the pool.
This setting serves as a clean baseline and highlights the fundamental advantages of quantitative measurements in the absence of uncertainty.
The noiseless model is well studied in the literature.
In the dense regime, where the fraction of defectives is constant, sharp information-theoretic thresholds were characterized by Alaoui
et al.

[
1
]
and Scarlett and Cevher
[
16
]
.
These results were later extended to sparse and sublinear regimes by Gebhard
et al.

[
4
]
, who derived sharp thresholds for high-probability recovery. Feige and Lellouche
[
3
]
showed that many polynomial-time algorithms require significantly more tests than information-theoretic limits and demonstrated improved recovery via reductions to structured subset selection problems. On the algorithmic side, a variety of non-adaptive decoding strategies have been proposed.
Gebhard
et al.

[
4
]
introduced a greedy reconstruction algorithm, while coding-theoretic constructions based on sparse graph codes were developed by Karimi
et al.

[
9
]
.
More recently, Hahn-Klimroth
et al.

[
7
]
proposed a polynomial-time algorithm achieving near optimal number of tests.

Overall, in the absence of noise, it is widely accepted that
\Theta(k\log(n/k)/\log k)

non-adaptive tests are both necessary and sufficient for exact recovery.
[
2
]
.

1.2
Additive Gaussian Noise Model

In many practical settings, pooled measurements are corrupted by continuous noise, motivating the
additive Gaussian noise
QGT model
[
7
,
18
]
.
In this model, each test outcome is perturbed by independent Gaussian noise, as commonly encountered in analog sensing and aggregated physical measurements. Dedicated studies of additive Gaussian noise in QGT have emerged only recently.
Hahn-Klimroth and Kaaser
[
6
]
analyzed a greedy correlation-based decoder and characterized regimes of noise variance under which exact recovery is possible with high probability. Their result shows that for moderate noise levels, recovery is achievable with on the order of
\Theta(k\log n)
tests.

However, no prior work has established tight information-theoretic achievability and converse bounds for Gaussian QGT.
Our work addresses this gap by deriving matching upper and lower bounds on the sample complexity and by comparing the performance of practical decoders to these fundamental limits.

1.3
Noisy Z-Channel

The
noisy Z-channel
model captures asymmetric item-level unreliability.
In this setting, each defective item included in a test may independently fail to contribute to the observed count, leading to false negatives but no false positives.

Hahn-Klimroth and Kaaser
[
6
]
studied a related noisy channel model in which each item’s state flips with a certain probability prior to pooling.
This framework includes symmetric flip noise and, as a special case, one-sided error models.
They showed that a greedy decoding algorithm can exactly recover the defective set with high probability up to a critical noise level, beyond which errors become unavoidable.

Techniques from noisy binary group testing also provide useful insights.
For example, Price
et al.

[
14
]
developed fast splitting algorithms that achieve near-optimal test complexity and runtime under stochastic test errors, although their analysis focuses on Boolean outcomes.

1.4
Other Noise Models

Beyond the mentioned models, a few works have explored adversarial or structured corruption models.
In particular, Li and Wang
[
12
]
studied combinatorial QGT under bounded adversarial noise, where test outcomes may be perturbed arbitrarily within fixed limits.
In this setting, exact recovery is generally impossible, and the goal shifts to partial recovery.
They established lower bounds on the order of
\Omega(n\log n)
tests and proposed deterministic test designs with efficient decoding that achieve these bounds up to constant factors.

1.5
Our Contributions

Table
1
compares our results with existing work.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We provide a unified analysis of three quantitative group testing models—noiseless, additive Gaussian noise, and noisy Z-channel—under a common non-adaptive random design framework.
This contrasts with prior work, which typically focuses on individual noise models or specific problem formulations.

•

We provide both achievability and converse bounds for the two noisy models.
In particular, for the additive Gaussian noise model, we establish matching achievability and converse bounds on the number of tests required for exact recovery.
To the best of our knowledge, this yields the first tight characterization of the sample complexity for Gaussian QGT up to constant factors.

•

We analyze two decoding approaches across all models:
a computationally efficient correlation-based linear estimator and a statistically optimal (but generally combinatorial) least squares estimator (LSE).
This allows us to explicitly quantify the performance gap between polynomial-time algorithms and information-theoretically optimal decoding under different noise regimes.

•

While simple correlation-based linear estimators for quantitative group testing were previously studied in both the noiseless setting
[
4
]
and noisy settings
[
6
]
, our results provide sharper guarantees.
In particular, prior formulations allow multi-set defectives within pooled tests, whereas we restrict attention to standard binary testing matrices, aligning more closely with the classical definition of QGT.
Despite this stronger modeling restriction, our bounds achieve equal or improved sample complexity in all three models, and we derive explicit non-asymptotic guarantees with concrete absolute constants.

Basic Notations

For a matrix
A
, we use
a_{i,j}
to denote its
(i,j)
-th entry.
A_{i}
denotes the
i^{\text{th}}
row of
A
.
|A_{i}|
denotes the number of 1s in
A_{i}
. For a vector
v
, we use
v_{i}
to denote its

2.2
Quantitative Group Testing

Let
n
denote the
total number of items
, and let
k
denote the
number of defective items
. Let
x^{*}
be an
n
-dimensional binary vector with Hamming weight
k
. i.e.
\|x^{*}\|_{0}=k
. We denote the support of
x^{*}
by

D\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathrm{supp}(x^{*})\subset[n],\qquad|D|=k.

We remark that, although our algorithms will only consider input vectors
x^{*}
with Hamming weight exactly
k
for convenience, these can be generalized to the case
\|x^{*}\|_{0}\leq k
without order-wise change in algorithm complexities.

Let
m
be the
number of tests
, and let
A
be a
m\times n
binary matrix which we call the
test matrix
.
Let
y
be the
test outcome vector
. This vector will be defind differently in different models, and will be specified in the next section.

Unless stated otherwise, we assume a random Bernoulli design for test matrix:

a_{i,j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Ber}(1/2).

The goal of QGT is to recover the support
D^{*}
(equivalently, the vector
x^{*}
) from the test outcomes
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, using as few tests
m
as possible, with vanishing error probability as
n\to\infty
. Denote
\widehat{x}
as the outcome of the decoding algorithm. In this paper, for all cases, we aim for
exact recovery
, i.e.
\widehat{x}=x^{*}
.

2.3
Observation Models

Noiseless model

In the noiseless setting, each test returns the exact number of defectives in the pool:

y=Ax^{*},\qquad y_{i}=\sum_{j\in D}a_{i,j}.

(1)

Additive Gaussian noise model

In this model, the pooled counts are corrupted by independent additive Gaussian noise:

y=Ax^{*}+N,\qquad N_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

(2)

Noisy Z-channel model

In the noisy Z-channel model, each defective item included in a test may independently fail to contribute to the observed count.
Formally, for each test
i
and item
j
, let

z_{i,j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Ber}(1-p),

where
p\in(0,1)
is the false-negative probability.
The observation is

y_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{i,j}\,x_{j}^{*}\,z_{i,j}.

(3)

2.4
Decoding methods

We analyze two decoding strategies that are common across all three models.

2.4.1
Linear estimator (correlation-based decoder)

The linear estimator assigns each item a score based on its correlation with the observed outcomes, which is widely used in literature
[
6
,
4
,
13
]
.
Define the score vector

S\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=A^{\top}y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad S_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i,j}y_{i}.

(4)

The decoder outputs the indices of the
k
largest coordinates of
S
:

\widehat{D}_{\mathrm{lin}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathrm{TopK}(S,k),\qquad

(5)

Intuitively, defective items tend to participate in tests with larger observed counts and therefore accumulate larger scores.
This decoder is computationally efficient and runs in polynomial time.

2.4.2
Least Squares Estimator

We also study a decoder motivated by least squares.
In all three models, the expected observation conditioned on
A
and a candidate signal
x
takes the form

\mathbb{E}[y\mid A,x]=\begin{cases}Ax,&\text{noiseless},\\
Ax,&\text{Gaussian},\\
(1-p)Ax,&\text{Z-channel}.\end{cases}

This motivates the least-squares objective

\mathcal{L}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\|y-\mathbb{E}[y\mid A,x]\|_{2}^{2}.

The LSE decoder is defined as

\widehat{x}^{\mathrm{LSE}}\in\arg\min_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n},\ \|x\|_{0}=k}\|y-\mathbb{E}[y\mid A,x]\|_{2}^{2}.

(6)

Concretely:

•

In the noiseless and Gaussian models,
\mathbb{E}[y\mid A,x]=Ax
, leading to the objective
\|y-Ax\|_{2}^{2}
.

•

In the noisy Z-channel model,
\mathbb{E}[y\mid A,x]=(1-p)Ax
, yielding the objective
\|y-(1-p)Ax\|_{2}^{2}
.

While solving (
6
) exactly is generally combinatorial and not computationally efficient, the LSE serves as a statistically optimal benchmark.

3
Main Results

3.1
Noiseless quantitative group testing

Our first theorem establishes that a simple linear estimator based on correlation scores suffices for exact recovery with high probability.

Theorem 1

(Noiseless QGT: Linear Estimator)
.

Let
n,k,A,x^{*},y
be as defined in Section
2
.
In the noiseless model
y=Ax^{*}
, consider the linear decoder that outputs indices of the
k
largest coordinates of
A^{\top}y
,
the number of tests required to recover
x^{*}
with vanishing error probability satisfies

m\geq\bigl(\frac{16}{\ln 3}k+8-\frac{8}{\ln 3}\bigr)\log\bigl(k(n-k)\bigr)

3.2
Additive Gaussian noise model

In this setting, we show that least-squares maximum-likelihood decoding achieves the optimal sample complexity, and we complement this with a matching information-theoretic converse.

Theorem 2

(Additive Gaussian QGT)
.

Let
n,k,A,x^{*},y
be as defined in Section
2
and let
k=n^{\theta}
for some
\theta\in(0,1)
. In the additive Gaussian noise model
y=Ax^{*}+N
, where
N
has i.i.d. Gaussian entries with variance
\sigma^{2}
, we have:

•

For the LSE decoder

\widehat{x}=\arg\min_{x}\|y-Ax\|_{2}^{2},

the number of tests required to recover
x^{*}
with vanishing error probability satisfies

m=O\!\left(\frac{k\log(n/k)}{\log(1+k/\sigma^{2})}\right).

•

Any decoder with any test design must satisfy

m=\Omega\!\left(\frac{k\log(n/k)}{\log(1+k/\sigma^{2})}\right).

•

For the linear decoder,
the number of tests required to recover
x^{*}
with vanishing error probability satisfies

m\geq\bigl(\frac{16}{\ln 3}k+32\sigma^{2}+8-\frac{8}{\ln 3}\bigr)\log\bigl(k(n-k)\bigr)

3.3
Noisy Z-channel model

In this asymmetric noise setting, we provide achievability guarantees and information-theoretic limits.

Theorem 3

(Noisy Z-channel QGT)
.

Let
n,k,A,x^{*},y
be as defined in Section
2
. In the noisy Z-channel model with false-negative probability
p
, we have:

•

For the LSE decoder

\widehat{x}=\arg\min_{x}\|y-(1-p)Ax\|_{2}^{2},

the number of tests required to recover
x^{*}
with vanishing error probability satisfies

m=O\!\left(\frac{k\log(n/k)}{\log(1+2C_{p})}\right)

tests, where
C_{p}
depends explicitly on the channel parameter

C_{p}=\frac{(1-p)^{2}\ln\frac{p}{1-p}}{2p-1}

•

If
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{\max\{A_{i}x,1\}}\right]\leq\frac{c}{k}
, for some absolute constant
c
, then any decoder with any test design must satisfy

m=\Omega\!\left(\frac{k\log(n/k)}{\log(\frac{1-k/n+kp/n}{p})}\right),

The assumption is satisfied if every entry of test matrix
A
is i.i.d
\mathrm{Ber}(1/2)
.

•

For the linear decoder,
the number of tests required to recover
x^{*}
with vanishing error probability satisfies

m\geq\Bigl(\frac{16p}{(1-p)^{2}\ln\frac{1+p}{1-p}}+\frac{8(2k-1)(1+p)}{(1-p)^{2}\ln\frac{3+p}{1-p}}\Bigr)\log\bigl(k(n-k)\bigr).

4.1
Proof of Theorem
1

Proof.

Write
D\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathrm{supp}(x^{*})
,
|D|=k
, and for each
i\in[n]
define the score

S_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(A^{\top}y)_{i}=\sum_{t=1}^{m}a_{t,i}\,y_{t},\qquad y_{t}=\sum_{s\in D}a_{t,s}.

For
i\in D
and a fixed row
t
,

a_{t,i}y_{t}=a_{t,i}\sum_{s\in D}a_{t,s}=\begin{cases}0,&a_{t,i}=0,\\[3.0pt]
1+\sum_{s\in D\setminus\{i\}}a_{t,s},&a_{t,i}=1,\end{cases}

where the
a_{t,s}
for
s\in D\setminus\{i\}
are i.i.d.
\mathrm{Ber}(1/2)
and
independent of
a_{t,i}
. Hence

\mathbb{E}[a_{t,i}y_{t}]=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(1+\frac{k-1}{2}\Bigr)=\frac{k+1}{4},\qquad\mathbb{E}[S_{i}]=m\,\frac{k+1}{4},

For
j\notin D
,
a_{t,j}
is independent of
y_{t}
, and

\mathbb{E}[y_{t}]=k/2
, so

\mathbb{E}[a_{t,j}y_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[a_{t,j}]\,\mathbb{E}[y_{t}]=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{k}{2}=\frac{k}{4},\qquad\mathbb{E}[S_{j}]=m\,\frac{k}{4},

Thus, for every
i\in D
and
j\notin D
,

\mathbb{E}[S_{i}-S_{j}]=\frac{m}{4}.

Fix
i\in D
and
j\notin D
and write

S_{i}-S_{j}=\sum_{t=1}^{m}z_{t},\qquad z_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=a_{t,i}\sum_{s\in D}a_{t,s}-a_{t,j}\sum_{s\in D}a_{t,s}.

Each summand admits the decomposition

z_{t}=a_{t,i}^{2}+\sum_{s\in D\setminus\{i\}}a_{t,i}a_{t,s}-\sum_{s\in D}a_{t,j}a_{t,s},

where
a_{t,i}^{2}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(1/2)
and every product
a_{t,i}a_{t,s}
or

a_{t,j}a_{t,s}
is
\mathrm{Ber}(1/4)
.
Using the optimal subgaussian variance proxy for centered Bernoulli
variables
[
10
]
,

\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{opt}}(p)=\frac{1-2p}{2\ln\frac{1-p}{p}}

and summing over the independent Bernoulli components of
z_{t}
, we obtain the variance proxy

\|z_{t}-\mathbb{E}z_{t}\|^{2}_{\text{vp}}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{4}+\frac{2k-1}{4\ln 3},

uniformly in
t
.

Denote
\mu\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{E}[S_{i}-S_{j}]=m/4
.
The standard subgaussian tail bound gives

\Pr(S_{i}<S_{j})=\Pr(S_{i}-S_{j}\leq 0)=\Pr\Bigl(\sum_{t=1}^{m}(z_{t}-\mathbb{E}z_{t})\leq-\mu\Bigr)\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\mu^{2}}{2m\|z_{t}-\mathbb{E}z_{t}\|^{2}_{\text{vp}}}\Bigr)\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{m}{32(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{2k-1}{4\ln 3})}\Bigr).

An error occurs if
S_{i}\leq S_{j}
for some
i\in D
,
j\notin D
.
By the union bound,

\Pr(\text{Error})\;\leq\;\sum_{i\in D}\sum_{j\notin D}\Pr(S_{i}<S_{j})\;\leq\;k(n-k)\exp\Bigl(-\frac{m}{32(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{2k-1}{4\ln 3})}\Bigr)

Therefore, to recover
x^{*}
with vanishing error probability, we need

m\geq\bigl(\frac{16}{\ln 3}k+8-\frac{8}{\ln 3}\bigr)\log\bigl(k(n-k)\bigr)

∎

Proof of Theorem
2

4.2.1
LSE achievability

Proof.

Minimizing over
x
is equivalent to maximizing

f(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}[2y_{i}A_{i}x-(A_{i}x)^{2}],

so that the decoder is
\widehat{x}=\arg\max_{x}f(x)
.

For any
x\neq x^{*}
, define the difference

f(x)-f(x^{*})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta_{i},\quad\text{where }\Delta_{i}=2(A_{i}x-A_{i}x^{*})(y_{i}-A_{i}x^{*})-(A_{i}x-A_{i}x^{*})^{2}.

Substituting
y_{i}=A_{i}x^{*}+N_{i}
gives

\Delta_{i}=2(A_{i}x-A_{i}x^{*})N_{i}-(A_{i}x-A_{i}x^{*})^{2}.

Let
b\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=Ax-Ax^{*}
. Then each
\Delta_{i}=2b_{i}N_{i}-b_{i}^{2}
, and the error event is

\Pr(\text{error})=\Pr\!\left(\exists x\neq x^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}f(x)\geq f(x^{*})\right)=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{\|x-x^{*}\|_{H}=2l}\Pr(f(x)\geq f(x^{*})).

Conditioning on the values of
b^{\prime}=(b_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,b_{m}^{\prime})
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr(f(x)\geq f(x^{*}))

\displaystyle=\Pr\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}2b_{i}N_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b^{\prime}}\Pr\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}2b_{i}^{\prime}N_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}^{\prime 2}\right)\Pr(b^{\prime}=A(x-x^{*})).

Since each
b_{i}N_{i}
is a mean-zero sub-Gaussian variable with parameter
\|b_{i}N_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}=\frac{8}{3}b_{i}^{2}\sigma^{2}
, and the terms are independent, by the standard Hoeffding bound (Theorem 2.6.2 in
[
19
]
), for some absolute constant
c>0
we have

\Pr\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}2b_{i}N_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}^{2}\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-c\frac{(\sum_{i}b_{i}^{2})^{2}}{\sum_{i}b_{i}^{2}\sigma^{2}}\right)=2\exp\!\left(-c\frac{\sum_{i}b_{i}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right).

Each test row
A_{i}
is independent and has i.i.d. entries
\mathrm{Ber}(1/2)
.
For fixed
x
and
x^{*}
differing in
2l
positions, the random variable
b_{i}=A_{i}(x-x^{*})
is the difference of two independent
\mathrm{Bin}(l,1/2)
variables.
By Lemma
3
,

\Pr(b_{i}=t)=4^{-l}{2l\choose l+|t|}\leq\frac{e}{\pi\sqrt{2l}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2l}\right).

As the rows
A_{i}
are independent,

\Pr(b^{\prime}=A(x-x^{*}))=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\Pr(b_{i}^{\prime}=b_{i})\leq\left(\frac{e}{\pi\sqrt{2l}}\right)^{m}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\sum_{i}b_{i}^{\prime 2}}{2l}\right).

Combining the two bounds,

\displaystyle\Pr(f(x)\geq f(x^{*}))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{b^{\prime}}2\exp\!\left(-c\frac{\sum_{i}b_{i}^{\prime 2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{e}{\pi\sqrt{2l}}\right)^{m}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\sum_{i}b_{i}^{\prime 2}}{2l}\right)

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{e}{\pi\sqrt{2l}}\right)^{m}\sum_{b^{\prime}}\exp\!\left(-(\frac{c}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{2l})\sum_{i}b_{i}^{\prime 2}\right).

Since each coordinate
b_{i}^{\prime}\in[-l,l]
and they are independent,

\sum_{b^{\prime}}\exp\!\left(-(\frac{c}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{2l})\sum_{i}b_{i}^{\prime 2}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{t=-l}^{l}\exp\!\left(-(\frac{c}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{2l})t^{2}\right).

Bounding the discrete sum by an integral,

\sum_{t=-l}^{l}e^{-\alpha t^{2}}\leq 2\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha t^{2}}dt=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{\alpha}},\quad\alpha=\frac{c}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{2l}.

Hence,

\Pr(f(x)\geq f(x^{*}))\leq\left(\frac{e}{\pi\sqrt{2l}}\right)^{m}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{\frac{c}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{2l}}}\right)^{m}=\left(\frac{c^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\frac{l}{\sigma^{2}}+1}}\right)^{m},

for some absolute constant
c^{\prime}>0
.
For a given Hamming distance
2l
, the number of possible
x
differing from
x^{*}
in those coordinates is at most
{k\choose l}{n-k\choose l}\leq{k\choose l}{n\choose l}
.
Applying Lemma
1
gives

{k\choose l}{n\choose l}\leq\left(\frac{e^{2}kn}{l^{2}}\right)^{l}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\Pr(\text{error})

\displaystyle\leq k\max_{1\leq l\leq k}{k\choose l}{n\choose l}\left(\frac{c^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\frac{l}{\sigma^{2}}+1}}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle\leq k\max_{1\leq l\leq k}\left(\frac{e^{2}kn}{l^{2}}\right)^{l}\left(\frac{c^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\frac{l}{\sigma^{2}}+1}}\right)^{m}.

To make the total probability vanish as
n\to\infty
, it suffices that the exponent of the largest term be negative, yielding

m\geq\max_{1\leq l\leq k}\frac{l\log(\frac{kn}{l^{2}})}{\log(\frac{l}{\sigma^{2}}+1)}.

Let

T\ \mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\ \frac{k\log(n/k)}{\log\!\big(1+\tfrac{k}{\sigma^{2}}\big)}\ =\ \frac{k(1-\theta)\log n}{\log\!\big(1+\tfrac{k}{\sigma^{2}}\big)}.

Now we aim to show that for any integer
l\in[1,k]
,

F(l)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{l\log(\frac{kn}{l^{2}})}{\log(\frac{l}{\sigma^{2}}+1)}\leq(1+o(1))\ T

Case 1:
1\leq l\leq k^{1-\varepsilon}
.

Using
k=n^{\theta}
and
l\leq k^{1-\varepsilon}
, we have

\log\!\Big(\frac{kn}{l^{2}}\Big)\ \leq\ \log(kn)=(1+\theta)\log n,

and
\log(1+\tfrac{l}{\sigma^{2}})\geq\log(1+\tfrac{1}{\sigma^{2}})
. Hence

F(l)\ \leq\ \frac{k^{1-\varepsilon}(1+\theta)\log n}{\log\!\big(1+\tfrac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\big)}.

Then

\frac{F(l)}{T}\ \leq\ \frac{1+\theta}{1-\theta}\cdot\frac{1}{k^{\varepsilon}}\cdot\frac{\log\!\big(1+\tfrac{k}{\sigma^{2}}\big)}{\log\!\big(1+\tfrac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\big)}.

For any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, the factor
k^{-\varepsilon}
dominates the at-most polylogarithmic growth of
\log(1+\tfrac{k}{\sigma^{2}})
, so
\frac{F(l)}{T}\to 0
. Therefore, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\max_{1\leq l\leq k^{1-\varepsilon}}F(l)\ \leq\ o(1)\cdot T

Case 2:
k^{1-\varepsilon}<l\leq k
.

Set
g(l)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=l\log\!\big(\tfrac{kn}{l^{2}}\big)
. Then

g^{\prime}(l)\ =\ \log(kn)-2\log l-2,\qquad g^{\prime\prime}(l)\ =\ -\frac{2}{l}<0,

so
g
is concave. When
n/k>e^{2}
, we have
g^{\prime}(k)=\log(n/k)-2>0
, and concavity implies
g
attains its maximum over
[1,k]
at the boundary
l=k
. Hence, for all sufficiently large
n
,

l\log\!\Big(\frac{kn}{l^{2}}\Big)\ \leq\ k\log\!\Big(\frac{n}{k}\Big)

For the denominator, we have

\log\!\Big(1+\frac{l}{\sigma^{2}}\Big)\ \geq\ \log\!\Big(1+\frac{k^{1-\varepsilon}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big)\ =\ (1-\varepsilon)\log\!\Big(1+\frac{k}{\sigma^{2}}\Big)\ +\ O(1),

Therefore,

F(l)\ \leq\ \frac{k\log(n/k)}{(1-\varepsilon)\log\!\big(1+\tfrac{k}{\sigma^{2}}\big)+O(1)}\ =\ (1+o(1))\,\frac{k\log(n/k)}{\log\!\big(1+\tfrac{k}{\sigma^{2}}\big)}.

∎

4.2.2
Converse Bound

Proof.

Let
X
denote the random support (uniform on
\binom{n}{k}
),
Y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})
the observations, and condition on any fixed matrix
A
.
By Fano’s inequality,

P_{e}\;\geq\;1-\frac{I(X;Y\,|\,A)+\log 2}{\log\binom{n}{k}},

so that achieving
P_{e}\to 0
requires

I(X;Y\,|\,A)\ \geq\ (1-o(1))\,\log\binom{n}{k}.

For each row
A_{i}
, define the noiseless measurement

S_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\langle A_{i},X\rangle

Then
y_{i}=S_{i}+N_{i}
with
N_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
independent of
S_{i}
.
Because the channel is memoryless conditional on
A
, the chain rule gives

I(X;Y\,|\,A)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}I(X;y_{i}\,|\,A,y_{<i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}I(S_{i};y_{i}\,|\,A_{i}),

For each test, we have

I(S_{i};y_{i}\,|\,A_{i})=h(y_{i}\,|\,A_{i})-h(N_{i}).

\mathrm{Var}(y_{i}\,|\,A_{i})=\mathrm{Var}(S_{i}\,|\,A_{i})+\sigma^{2}

For any random variable
Z
with variance
v
, the differential entropy satisfies

h(Z)\ \leq\ \tfrac{1}{2}\log(2\pi ev),

with equality if and only if
Z
is Gaussian.
Hence, we obtain

h(y_{i}\,|\,A_{i})\;\leq\;\tfrac{1}{2}\log\bigl(2\pi e(\mathrm{Var}(S_{i}\,|\,A_{i})+\sigma^{2})\bigr),\qquad h(N_{i})=\tfrac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e\sigma^{2}).

Therefore,

I(S_{i};y_{i}\,|\,A_{i})\;\leq\;\tfrac{1}{2}\log\Bigl(1+\frac{\mathrm{Var}(S_{i}\,|\,A_{i})}{\sigma^{2}}\Bigr).

Given
A_{i}
and the uniform random support
X
,
S_{i}=\langle A_{i},X\rangle
follows a hypergeometric distribution with parameters
(n,k,w_{i})
, where
w_{i}=\|A_{i}\|_{0}
. Its variance satisfies

\mathrm{Var}(S_{i}\,|\,A_{i})=k\frac{w_{i}}{n}\Bigl(1-\frac{w_{i}}{n}\Bigr)\frac{n-k}{n-1}\leq\frac{k}{4},

the upper bound being attained when
w_{i}=n/2
.

Consequently,

I(S_{i};y_{i}\,|\,A_{i})\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\log\!\Bigl(1+\tfrac{k}{4\sigma^{2}}\Bigr).

Summing over all tests gives

I(X;Y\,|\,A)\leq m\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}\log\!\Bigl(1+\tfrac{k}{4\sigma^{2}}\Bigr).

Combining this with the Fano requirement
I(X;Y\,|\,A)\geq(1-o(1))\log\binom{n}{k}
yields

m\;\geq\;(1-o(1))\,\frac{\log\binom{n}{k}}{\tfrac{1}{2}\log(1+\tfrac{k}{4\sigma^{2}})}.

Finally, using
\log\binom{n}{k}=k\log(n/k)+O(k)
gives the desired bound.
∎

Proof.

Minimizing

For

k

\|y_{i}-\mathbb{E}y_{i}\|_{\mathrm{vp}}\;\leq\;k\,\frac{2p-1}{2\ln\ \frac{p}{1-p}}.

Applying Corollary 1.7 of
[
15
]

\Pr\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}2b_{i}(y_{i}-\mathbb{E}y_{i})\geq(1-p)\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}^{2}\right)\leq\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{(1-p)^{2}\sum_{i}b_{i}^{2}}{k\cdot\frac{2p-1}{\ln\frac{p}{1-p}}}\right).

Each test row
A_{i}
is independent and has i.i.d. entries
\mathrm{Ber}(1/2)
.
For fixed
x

x^{*}

2l

\mathrm{Bin}(l,1/2)

By Lemma

\Pr(b_{i}=t)=4^{-l}{2l\choose l+|t|}\leq\frac{e}{\pi\sqrt{2l}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2l}\right).

As the rows

\Pr(b=A(x-x^{*}))=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\Pr(b_{i})\leq\left(\frac{e}{\pi\sqrt{2l}}\right)^{m}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\sum_{i}b_{i}^{2}}{2l}\right).

Combining the two bounds,

\displaystyle\Pr(f(x)\geq f(x^{*}))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{b}\exp(-\frac{(1-p)^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}^{2}}{k\cdot\frac{2p-1}{\ln\frac{p}{1-p}}})\left(\frac{e}{\pi\sqrt{2l}}\right)^{m}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\sum_{i}b_{i}^{2}}{2l}\right)

Hence,

\Pr(f(x)\geq f(x^{*}))\leq\left(\frac{e}{\pi\sqrt{2l}}\right)^{m}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)^{m}=\left(\frac{e/\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{\frac{(1-p)^{2}\ln\frac{p}{1-p}}{2p-1}\frac{2l}{k}+1}}\right)^{m}

For a given Hamming distance

\displaystyle\Pr(\text{error})

\Pr(S_{i}=s|A=a)=\frac{\binom{|a_{i}|}{s}\binom{n-|a_{i}|}{k-s}}{\binom{n}{k}}

Suppose,

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[S_{i}p(1-p)]+\text{Var}[S_{i}(1-p)]

\displaystyle=p(1-p)\mathbb{E}[S_{i}]+(1-p)^{2}\text{Var}[S_{i}]

\displaystyle=p(1-p)\mathbb{E}[S_{i}]+(1-p)^{2}\text{Var}[S_{i}]

\displaystyle=(1-p)k\frac{|a_{i}|}{n}\left(1-(1-p)\frac{k}{n}\right)

]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\bigl(2\pi e(k(1-p)(1-\frac{k}{n}(1-p))+\frac{1}{12})\bigr)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{2}\log\bigl(4\pi e(k(1-p)(1-\frac{k}{n}(1-p)))\bigr)

\displaystyle-H(Y_{i}\mid X,A_{i})

5
Future Directions

Several directions remain open for future work.
First, while our analysis focuses on a simple correlation-based linear estimator and a least squares benchmark, it would be interesting to design and analyze other computationally efficient algorithms that further narrow the gap to information-theoretic limits.
In particular, iterative schemes that leverage quantitative measurements may achieve improved noise robustness while retaining polynomial-time complexity.

Second, our results cover additive Gaussian noise and Z-channel noise, but many practical settings involve more complex or heterogeneous corruption mechanisms.
Extending the current framework to other noise models would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the robustness limits of noisy quantitative group testing.

Using the inequality
1-x\leq e^{-x}
for all real
x
, we obtain

\frac{{2l\choose l+b}}{{2l\choose l}}\leq\exp\!\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{b}\frac{2i-1}{l+i}\right).

Since
l+i\leq 2l
for all
i\leq b
, it follows that

\sum_{i=1}^{b}\frac{2i-1}{l+i}\geq\frac{1}{2l}\sum_{i=1}^{b}(2i-1)=\frac{b^{2}}{2l}.

By Lemma
2
,

4^{-l}{2l\choose l+b}\leq 4^{-l}{2l\choose l}\exp\!\left(-\frac{b^{2}}{2l}\right)\leq\frac{e}{\pi\sqrt{2l}}\cdot\exp\!\left(-\frac{b^{2}}{2l}\right)

∎
