Matrix Thermodynamic Uncertainty Relation for Non-Abelian Charge Transport
Domingos S. P. Salazar

Abstract
Thermodynamic uncertainty relations (TURs) bound the precision of currents by entropy production, but quantum transport of noncommuting (non-Abelian) charges challenges standard formulations because different charge components cannot be monitored within a single classical frame. We derive a process-level matrix TUR starting from the operational entropy production Σ = D ( ρ S ​ E ′ ∥ ρ S ′ ⊗ ρ E ) \Sigma=D(\rho^{\prime}_{SE}\|\rho^{\prime}_{S}\!\otimes\!\rho_{E}) . Isolating the experimentally accessible bath divergence D bath = D ( ρ E ′ ∥ ρ E ) D_{\mathrm{bath}}=D(\rho^{\prime}_{E}\|\rho_{E}) , we prove a fully nonlinear, saturable lower bound valid for arbitrary current vectors Δ ​ q \Delta q : D bath ≥ B ⁡ ( Δ ​ q , V , V ′ ) D_{\mathrm{bath}}\geq B(\Delta q,V,V^{\prime}) , where the bound depends only on the transported-charge signal Δ ​ q \Delta q and the pre/post collision covariance matrices V V and V ′ V^{\prime} . In the small-fluctuation regime D bath ≥ 1 2 ​ Δ ​ q 𝖳 ​ V − 1 ​ Δ ​ q + O ⁡ ( ‖ Δ ​ q ‖ 4 ) D_{\mathrm{bath}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta q^{\mathsf{T}}V^{-1}\Delta q+O(\|\Delta q\|^{4}) , while beyond linear response it remains accurate. Numerical strong-coupling qubit collisions illustrate the bound and demonstrate near-saturation across broad parameter ranges using only local measurements on the bath probe.

A core conceptual obstacle is that “entropy production” in open quantum dynamics is not naturally
a divergence between a state and a time-reversed state. A particularly clean microscopic definition
is the process-level entropy production (EP)
Esposito et al. 2010

\Sigma:=D\!\left(\rho^{\prime}_{SE}\,\big\|\,\rho^{\prime}_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}\right),

(1)

where
D(\rho\|\sigma)=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho(\ln\rho-\ln\sigma))
is the quantum relative entropy and the initial product state
\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}
evolves unitarily to
\rho^{\prime}_{SE}
and

\rho^{\prime}_{S}:=\mathrm{Tr}_{E}\rho^{\prime}_{SE}

1

I(S\!:\!E):=D(\rho_{SE}^{\prime}\|\rho_{S}^{\prime}\otimes\rho_{E}^{\prime})
plus the bath divergence
D_{\mathrm{bath}}:=D(\rho^{\prime}_{E}\|\rho_{E})

\Sigma=I(S\!:\!E)+D_{\mathrm{bath}}\ \geq\ D_{\mathrm{bath}}.

(2)

This definition is well-suited to non-Abelian transport because it requires no choice of reversed dynamics.

We consider one collision between a system

(i) Integral representation in terms of

The bath relative entropy admits an exact integral representation

(ii) From a scalar witness bound to the matrix form.

Discussion

Onsager limit.

In a near-fixed-point regime one has
V^{\prime}\approx V
(hence
V_{\lambda}\approx V
and
s_{\lambda}\approx\Delta q^{\mathsf{T}}V^{-1}\Delta q
),
so Eq. (
10
) reduces to the quadratic Onsager form stated in Eq. (
4
),
while remaining valid far beyond linear response and often near-saturated in our numerics.

Symmetric-covariance regime
V^{\prime}=V
.

In the special case where the covariance is unchanged by the collision,
V^{\prime}=V
, the interpolation
(
6
) becomes
\lambda
-independent,
V_{\lambda}=V
, and hence
s_{\lambda}
is constant:

s_{\lambda}\equiv s:=\Delta q^{\mathsf{T}}V^{-1}\Delta q.

(13)

The bound (
10
) then collapses to a one-parameter function which admits the closed form

D_{\mathrm{bath}}\ \geq F(s)=2\sqrt{\frac{s}{s+4}}\;\operatorname{artanh}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{s}{s+4}}\right).

(14)

Physically,
V^{\prime}=V
is feasible whenever the probe state is (approximately) stationary under the collision at the level of
second moments of the measured charges. This occurs, for example, (a) in a near-fixed-point/linear-response regime where

\rho_{E}^{\prime}\approx\rho_{E}
so that covariances change only at higher order in the current, (b) in weak-coupling or large-probe limits
where a single collision perturbs the probe by
O(1/N)
and its covariance self-averages. In these regimes the full matrix bound reduces to the universal curve
F(s)
, while deviations from
V^{\prime}=V
quantify how far the
process operates from this symmetric-covariance idealization.

Matrix TUR in the symmetric-covariance regime (
V^{\prime}=V
).

We have seen above that when
V^{\prime}=V
the bound reduces to
D_{\mathrm{bath}}\geq F(s)
with
s=\Delta q^{\mathsf{T}}V^{-1}\Delta q

[Eqs. (
13
)–(
14
)]. Since
F(s)
is strictly increasing for
s\geq 0
, it can be inverted to yield

s\ \leq\ G(D_{\mathrm{bath}}),\qquad G:=F^{-1}.

(15)

Introducing the same auxiliary inverse function as in the classic (commuting) case
Timpanaro et al. 2019b
,
namely
g(x)
defined as the inverse of
x\mapsto x\tanh x
, one convenient closed form is obtained by writing

D_{\mathrm{bath}}=2y\tanh y
with
y=g(D_{\mathrm{bath}}/2)
, which gives
G(D)=4\sinh^{2}\!\left(g\!\left(\frac{D}{2}\right)\right)
. We define

f(D):=\frac{1}{G(D)}=\frac{1}{4}\,\csch^{2}\!\left(g\!\left(\frac{D}{2}\right)\right).

(16)

Then the scalar inequality (
15
) is equivalent to the
Matrix Symmetric Quantum TUR
, which is our second main result valid for
V=V^{\prime}
,

V-f(D_{\mathrm{bath}})\,\Delta q\,\Delta q^{\mathsf{T}}\ \succeq\ 0,

(17)

i.e., the left-hand side is positive semidefinite. Equation (
17
) has the same
formal
structure a classical matrix exchange TUR
(e.g. Ref.
Timpanaro et al. 2019b
), but its content is intrinsically quantum. When the charges commute, we recover the classical matrix TUR, where
V
reduces to the classical covariance matrix.

Here the current is a
vector of expectation-value changes of noncommuting charges
(for instance, in our simulation,

Q_{X}=\sigma_{X}/2
and
Q_{Z}=\sigma_{Z}/2
with
[Q_{X},Q_{Z}]\neq 0
), so there is no underlying joint distribution of
simultaneously measurable observables from which
V
could be interpreted as a classical covariance matrix.
Rather,
V
is a symmetrized quantum covariance geometry, reconstructible operationally from repeated preparations
with different measurement settings.
The symmetric-covariance condition
V^{\prime}=V
should therefore be viewed as an idealized regime in which the collision
preserves second moments of the chosen non-Abelian charges as already discussed.

Application: strong-coupling collision simulation

Collision/repeated-interaction architectures—sequential strong system–ancilla couplings with ancilla re-preparation—are now experimentally routine across photonic platforms, trapped-ion open-system simulators, superconducting circuits with fast ancilla reset, and even gate-based quantum hardware
Uriri et al. 2020
;
So et al. 2025
;
Andersen et al. 2019
;
Cattaneo et al. 2023
;
Haroche 2013
;
Cuevas et al. 2019
. We model a
single-shot
nonequilibrium transport collision simulation. here a system qubit
S

collides once with a fresh probe qubit
E
(the “bath probe”).
Each run starts from a product preparation
\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}
, undergoes a tunable

strong
entangling interaction
U_{SE}
, and ends with reduced output states

\rho^{\prime}_{S}
and
\rho^{\prime}_{E}
.
The process-level entropy production is
\Sigma
in Eq. (
1
), and the directly accessible bath relative entropy is
D_{\mathrm{bath}}
[Eq. (
2
)].

The probe qubit
E
is prepared in a full-rank state, conveniently parameterized by a Bloch vector,

\rho_{E}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mathbb{I}+r\,\hat{n}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\Big),\qquad 0<r<1,\ \ \hat{n}\in S^{2},

(18)

so
\rho_{E}
can be a polarized “thermal-like” qubit along direction
\hat{n}
with polarization
r
. A
fresh
copy of
E
is used for each run (collision model).

The system qubit is prepared independently.
To scan both near-equilibrium and far-from-equilibrium regimes, we use either:
(i) a small coherent rotation away from
\rho_{E}
(near-fixed-point regime), or
(ii) a generic state with different Bloch direction (far-from-equilibrium).
In all cases the protocol uses only the prepared
\rho_{S}
and
\rho_{E}
and the measured output
\rho^{\prime}_{E}
. The collision is implemented by a tunable partial SWAP, realizable for instance via an exchange/Heisenberg
interaction,

U_{\mathrm{swap}}(\phi)=e^{-i\phi\,\mathrm{SWAP}}=\cos(\phi)\,\mathbb{I}_{SE}-i\sin(\phi)\,\mathrm{SWAP},

(19)

\phi\in(0,\pi/2]
(details in the SM). We take the transported charges to be two spin components on the probe qubit, chosen explicitly as

q=\big(q_{1},q_{2}\big),\quad q_{1}\equiv Q_{X}:=\frac{1}{2}\,\sigma_{X},\quad q_{2}\equiv Q_{Z}:=\frac{1}{2}\,\sigma_{Z},

(20)

so that
m=2
and
[Q_{X},Q_{Z}]\neq 0
.
This choice provides the minimal non-Abelian setting while keeping all quantities directly accessible in the simulation. We define the measured current on the bath probe as the change in charge expectation values:

\Delta q_{u}:=\mathrm{Tr}\!\big[(\rho^{\prime}_{E}-\rho_{E})\,Q_{u}\big],\qquad\Delta q=\big(\Delta q_{u}\big)_{u=1}^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

(21)

Each
\Delta q_{u}
is obtained from repeated runs by measuring
Q_{u}
on
E

before the collision (state
\rho_{E}
) and after the collision (state
\rho^{\prime}_{E}
).
Because the
Q_{u}
do not commute, different components are estimated from separate measurement settings
(rotating the measurement basis between runs).

We also estimate the charge covariances on
E
before and after the collision using (
8
-
9
). Operationally,
V
and

are reconstructed from the same repeated measurements used to estimate means:
one collects outcome statistics for each measurement basis and forms the empirical covariance matrix

We use the Frobenius (Hilbert–Schmidt) norm
\|A\|:=\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(A^{\dagger}A)}
,
so the relative covariance drift is reported as
\|V-V^{\prime}\|/\|V\|
. We also evaluate
D_{\mathrm{bath}}=D(\rho^{\prime}_{E}\|\rho_{E})
,
which only involves states of the probe qubit.
Experimentally,
D_{\mathrm{bath}}
can be obtained by single-qubit tomography (or any equivalent
state-reconstruction method such as classical shadows), followed by classical post-processing to compute the
quantum relative entropy. From
(\Delta q,V,V^{\prime})
we form

V_{\lambda}
and

from (
6
)
and the bound

where

In this sense,

V\mapsto V^{\prime}
quantifies the associated covariance geometry.
Our bound applies in this incompatible-charge setting and is often near-saturated in our numerics.

Outlook and Conclusions

We developed a
matrix
thermodynamic uncertainty relation for transport of
noncommuting

charges, starting from the process-level entropy production

\Sigma=D(\rho^{\prime}_{SE}\|\rho^{\prime}_{S}\!\otimes\!\rho_{E})
and isolating the experimentally accessible bath
relative entropy
D_{\mathrm{bath}}=D(\rho^{\prime}_{E}\|\rho_{E})
.
Our central result is a fully nonlinear,
saturable
bound

D_{\mathrm{bath}}\ \geq\ B(\Delta q,V,V^{\prime}),

(25)

whose only inputs are the probe-side current vector
\Delta q
and the pre/post charge-covariance matrices

(V,V^{\prime})
.
In the small-current regime this reduces to the Onsager quadratic form, while beyond linear response the

covariance drift

V\!\to\!V^{\prime}
quantifies the breakdown of any single-covariance description.
Crucially, non-Abelianity enters operationally through incompatibility: for noncommuting charges there is no
single joint measurement frame, so one must combine statistics from distinct settings (captured in our
incompatibility indicator
C
).
Our witness resolves this core obstruction: it certifies dissipation without weak-coupling, Markovian, or
time-reversal assumptions, using only first/second moments accessible from local measurements (or
tomography/shadows) on the bath probe alone. In our collision-model numerics the bound is often near-saturated
across broad parameter ranges, including strongly coupled regimes.

Several directions now look ripe.
(i) Extend the one-shot witness to multi-collision and continuous-time dynamics, making explicit how the
resulting non-Abelian covariance geometry governs finite-time precision–dissipation tradeoffs and mixing.
(ii) Characterize equality and near-equality conditions, turning the TUR into a
design principle
for
optimal non-Abelian transport protocols and minimal-dissipation gates.
(iii) Deploy the framework in platforms where incompatible components are natural and measurable (e.g. SU(2)
spin transport, cold atoms, and superconducting-qubit collision experiments), where both
C
and the drift

V\!\to\!V^{\prime}
provide directly testable signatures of genuinely quantum nonequilibrium transport.
Finally, the same logic extends beyond KL via the
\chi^{2}_{\lambda}
decomposition of Petz
f
-divergences,
suggesting a family of tunable “geometric” TURs matched to experimental constraints and noise models.

f(t)=t\log t
yields the quantum relative entropy

D(\rho\|\sigma):=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\rho(\log\rho-\log\sigma)\right].

(S4)

S-III
One collision step, entropy production, mutual information split, and bath relative entropy

Consider a single collision with initial product state
\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}
and unitary
U
on
S\otimes E
:

\rho^{\prime}_{SE}=U(\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E})U^{\dagger},\qquad\rho^{\prime}_{S}=\mathrm{Tr}_{E}(\rho^{\prime}_{SE}),\qquad\rho^{\prime}_{E}=\mathrm{Tr}_{S}(\rho^{\prime}_{SE}).

(S5)

We define the (KL) entropy production as the relative entropy to the nonequilibrium reference
\rho^{\prime}_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}
,

\Sigma:=D\!\left(\rho^{\prime}_{SE}\,\middle\|\,\rho^{\prime}_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}\right).

(S6)

Using standard identities for relative entropy of bipartite states,
\Sigma
splits into a nonnegative mutual-information term and the bath relative entropy:

\Sigma=I(S{:}E)_{\rho^{\prime}_{SE}}+D(\rho^{\prime}_{E}\|\rho_{E}),\qquad I(S{:}E)_{\rho^{\prime}_{SE}}:=D\!\left(\rho^{\prime}_{SE}\,\middle\|\,\rho^{\prime}_{S}\otimes\rho^{\prime}_{E}\right)\geq 0.

(S7)

In the numerical plots labeled “Dbath”, we focus on the bath relative entropy alone,

D_{\mathrm{bath}}:=D(\rho^{\prime}_{E}\|\rho_{E}),

(S8)

which removes the (positive) mutual information contribution and is therefore a sharper target for saturation.

S-IV
Quadratic contrasts:
\chi^{2}_{\lambda}
and integral representations

For
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
\sigma>0
, define the super operator

K_{\lambda,\rho,\sigma}:=(1-\lambda)L_{\rho}+\lambda R_{\sigma}.

(S9)

The
\chi^{2}_{\lambda}
divergence is the quadratic form

\chi^{2}_{\lambda}(\rho\|\sigma):=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[(\rho-\sigma)\,K_{\lambda,\rho,\sigma}^{-1}(\rho-\sigma)\right].

(S10)

A key input is that operator-convex
f
-divergences admit a decomposition into
\chi^{2}_{\lambda}
modes: there exists a nonnegative weight function
w_{f}(\lambda)
(depending on
f
) such that

D_{f}(\rho\|\sigma)=\int_{0}^{1}w_{f}(\lambda)\,\chi^{2}_{\lambda}(\rho\|\sigma)\,d\lambda.

(S11)

For KL, the weight is
w_{\mathrm{KL}}(\lambda)=\lambda
, giving

D(\rho\|\sigma)=\int_{0}^{1}\lambda\,\chi^{2}_{\lambda}(\rho\|\sigma)\,d\lambda.

(S12)

S-V
Currents, covariance matrices, and a one-dimensional witness

Let
\vec{Q}=(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{m})
be a list of (possibly noncommuting) reservoir charges on
E
. Define the one-step current vector

\Delta\vec{q}:=\Big(\mathrm{Tr}\big[(\rho^{\prime}_{E}-\rho_{E})Q_{1}\big],\dots,\mathrm{Tr}\big[(\rho^{\prime}_{E}-\rho_{E})Q_{m}\big]\Big)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

(S13)

For a real vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, define the scalar charge witness and its transported current

Q_{u}:=u\cdot\vec{Q}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}Q_{i},\qquad x(u):=\mathrm{Tr}\big[(\rho^{\prime}_{E}-\rho_{E})Q_{u}\big]=u\cdot\Delta\vec{q}.

(S14)

We use the symmetrized covariance matrix in state
\tau
,

\mathrm{Cov}_{\tau}(Q_{i},Q_{j}):=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\tau\,\{\Delta_{\tau}Q_{i},\Delta_{\tau}Q_{j}\}\right],\qquad\Delta_{\tau}Q_{i}:=Q_{i}-\mathrm{Tr}(\tau Q_{i})\,\mathbb{I}.

(S15)

Define the pre- and post-collision covariance matrices

V_{ij}:=\mathrm{Cov}_{\rho_{E}}(Q_{i},Q_{j}),\qquad V^{\prime}_{ij}:=\mathrm{Cov}_{\rho^{\prime}_{E}}(Q_{i},Q_{j}).

(S16)

Then
\mathrm{Var}_{\rho_{E}}(Q_{u})=u^{\top}Vu
and
\mathrm{Var}_{\rho^{\prime}_{E}}(Q_{u})=u^{\top}V^{\prime}u
.

S-VI
TUR from Chapman–Robbins at fixed
\lambda
: general
f
and KL corollary

For
\lambda\in(0,1)
define the tight Chapman–Robbins contrast

h_{\lambda}(x,y,z):=\frac{x^{2}}{(1-\lambda)y+\lambda z+\lambda(1-\lambda)x^{2}}.

(S17)

S-VI.1
Theorem: scalar TUR for a witness observable

Theorem 1

(Witness TUR for
D_{f}(\rho^{\prime}_{E}\|\rho_{E})
)
.

For any operator-convex
f
with integral weight
w_{f}(\lambda)\geq 0
as in (
S11
), and any
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

D_{f}(\rho^{\prime}_{E}\|\rho_{E})\geq\int_{0}^{1}w_{f}(\lambda)\,h_{\lambda}\!\Big(x(u),\,\mathrm{Var}_{\rho^{\prime}_{E}}(Q_{u}),\,\mathrm{Var}_{\rho_{E}}(Q_{u})\Big)\,d\lambda.

(S18)

Proof.

Apply (
S11
) with
(\rho,\sigma)=(\rho^{\prime}_{E},\rho_{E})
, and use the tight Chapman–Robbins lower bound for each
\chi^{2}_{\lambda}(\rho^{\prime}_{E}\|\rho_{E})
with witness
Q_{u}
, which yields

\chi^{2}_{\lambda}(\rho^{\prime}_{E}\|\rho_{E})\geq h_{\lambda}\!\Big(x(u),\,\mathrm{Var}_{\rho^{\prime}_{E}}(Q_{u}),\,\mathrm{Var}_{\rho_{E}}(Q_{u})\Big).

(S19)

Integrating (
S19
) against
w_{f}(\lambda)\,d\lambda
gives (
S18
).
∎

Corollary 1

(KL witness bound)
.

For KL,
w_{\mathrm{KL}}(\lambda)=\lambda
and therefore

D_{\mathrm{bath}}=D(\rho^{\prime}_{E}\|\rho_{E})\geq\int_{0}^{1}\lambda\,\frac{(u\cdot\Delta\vec{q})^{2}}{(1-\lambda)\,u^{\top}V^{\prime}u+\lambda\,u^{\top}Vu+\lambda(1-\lambda)(u\cdot\Delta\vec{q})^{2}}\,d\lambda.

(S20)

S-VI.2
Theorem: matrix TUR via optimizing over
u

For each
\lambda\in(0,1)
define the convex combination and the curvature factor

M_{\lambda}:=(1-\lambda)V^{\prime}+\lambda V,\qquad\alpha_{\lambda}:=\lambda(1-\lambda).

(S21)

Assume
M_{\lambda}
is invertible on the support of
\Delta\vec{q}
. Define the scalar

s_{\lambda}:=\Delta\vec{q}^{\top}M_{\lambda}^{-1}\Delta\vec{q}.

(S22)

Lemma 1

(Optimization identity at fixed
\lambda
)
.

For fixed
\lambda\in(0,1)
,

\sup_{u\neq 0}\;\frac{(u\cdot\Delta\vec{q})^{2}}{u^{\top}M_{\lambda}u+\alpha_{\lambda}(u\cdot\Delta\vec{q})^{2}}=\frac{s_{\lambda}}{1+\alpha_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}},

(S23)

and an optimizer is
u\propto M_{\lambda}^{-1}\Delta\vec{q}
.

Proof.

Write the denominator as
u^{\top}M_{\lambda}u+\alpha_{\lambda}(u^{\top}\Delta\vec{q})^{2}
.
Let
v:=M_{\lambda}^{1/2}u
and
a:=M_{\lambda}^{-1/2}\Delta\vec{q}
. Then

\frac{(u^{\top}\Delta\vec{q})^{2}}{u^{\top}M_{\lambda}u+\alpha_{\lambda}(u^{\top}\Delta\vec{q})^{2}}=\frac{(v^{\top}a)^{2}}{\|v\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha_{\lambda}(v^{\top}a)^{2}}.

(S24)

For fixed
a
, the quantity
(v^{\top}a)^{2}/\|v\|_{2}^{2}
is maximized by
v\parallel a
(Cauchy–Schwarz), with maximum
\|a\|_{2}^{2}=a^{\top}a=\Delta\vec{q}^{\top}M_{\lambda}^{-1}\Delta\vec{q}=s_{\lambda}
. Substituting yields (
S23
). The maximizing direction
v\parallel a
corresponds to
u\propto M_{\lambda}^{-1}\Delta\vec{q}
.
∎

Theorem 2

(Matrix TUR for the bath relative entropy)
.

The KL bath relative entropy satisfies the fully optimized integral bound

D_{\mathrm{bath}}\geq\int_{0}^{1}\lambda\,\frac{s_{\lambda}}{1+\lambda(1-\lambda)s_{\lambda}}\,d\lambda,\qquad s_{\lambda}=\Delta\vec{q}^{\top}\big((1-\lambda)V^{\prime}+\lambda V\big)^{-1}\Delta\vec{q}.

(S25)

Proof.

Start from the KL witness bound (
S20
). For each fixed
\lambda
, the integrand is exactly the objective in Lemma
1
with
M_{\lambda}
and
\alpha_{\lambda}
. Optimizing over
u
at fixed
\lambda
yields
\frac{s_{\lambda}}{1+\alpha_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}}
, and integrating over
\lambda
gives (
S25
).
∎

Remark on Quantum f-Divergences

Our main result can also be stated for general Quantum Petz f-divergences. Using the integral representation,

D_{\mathrm{f}}(\rho^{\prime}_{E}\|\rho_{E})=\int_{0}^{1}d\lambda\;w_{\mathrm{f}}(\lambda)\chi^{2}_{\lambda}(\rho^{\prime}_{E}\|\rho_{E}),

(S26)

for positive weights
w_{f}(\lambda)
. It results in

D_{\mathrm{f}}(\rho_{E}^{\prime}\|\rho_{E})\geq B_{\mathrm{f}}(\Delta q,V,V^{\prime}),

(S27)

where
B_{\mathrm{f}}(\Delta q,V,V^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{1}d\lambda\,w_{\mathrm{f}}(\lambda)s_{\lambda}/(1+\lambda(1-\lambda)s_{\lambda})
. The entropy production is the specific Kullback-Leibler case
w_{KL}(\lambda)=\lambda
. Another notable example is the affinity/Bures–Hellinger family
H^{2}(\rho,\sigma):=1-\mathrm{Tr}(\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{\sigma})
, where
w_{\mathrm{f}}(\lambda)=(1/\pi)\sqrt{\lambda(1-\lambda)}
.

S-VII
Small-current limit: quadratic form and relation to Onsager geometry

In a near-fixed-point regime,
\rho^{\prime}_{E}=\rho_{E}+O(\varepsilon)
, implying

V^{\prime}=V+O(\varepsilon),\qquad\Delta\vec{q}=O(\varepsilon).

(S28)

Then
s_{\lambda}=s+O(\varepsilon^{3})
with

s:=\Delta\vec{q}^{\top}V^{-1}\Delta\vec{q}.

(S29)

Substituting
V^{\prime}=V
into (
S25
) yields a closed form

D_{\mathrm{bath}}\geq F(s),\qquad F(s):=\int_{0}^{1}\lambda\,\frac{s}{1+\lambda(1-\lambda)s}\,d\lambda=2\sqrt{\frac{s}{s+4}}\;\mathrm{artanh}\!\sqrt{\frac{s}{s+4}}.

(S30)

Expanding at small
s
gives

F(s)=\frac{s}{2}-\frac{s^{2}}{12}+O(s^{3}),\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad D_{\mathrm{bath}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta\vec{q}^{\top}V^{-1}\Delta\vec{q}+O(\|\Delta\vec{q}\|^{4}).

(S31)

It is useful to relate this to the matrix used in standard linear-response notation. Define

(C_{\mathrm{eff}})_{ij}:=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\rho_{E}\,\{\Delta_{\rho_{E}}Q_{i},\Delta_{\rho_{E}}Q_{j}\}\right].

(S32)

By (
S15
)–(
S16
), we have
C_{\mathrm{eff}}=2V
, hence the leading quadratic bound can also be written as

D_{\mathrm{bath}}\geq\Delta\vec{q}^{\top}C_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}\Delta\vec{q}+O(\|\Delta\vec{q}\|^{4}).

(S33)

Comparison with Kubo–Mori.

The Hessian of
D(\rho_{E}+\delta\rho\|\rho_{E})
at
\delta\rho=0
defines the Kubo–Mori (Bogoliubov) inner product and its associated covariance matrix
V^{\mathrm{KM}}
(a linear-response object). Replacing
V^{\mathrm{KM}}
by the symmetrized covariance
V
typically yields a looser quadratic bound, but the advantage is that
V
is directly a noise (fluctuation) matrix and is exactly what is estimated in the simulation.

Applications: strong-coupling qubit collision model (numerical experiment)

Goal.

We numerically demonstrate that the bath relative entropy

D_{\mathrm{bath}}:=D(\rho^{\prime}_{E}\|\rho_{E})
generated by a
single strong-coupling collision

satisfies the matrix TUR lower bound

D_{\mathrm{bath}}\geq B(\Delta q,V,V^{\prime})
from Eq. (
10
),
and we map out when the bound becomes nearly saturated.
The simulation implements the microscopic definitions in
Eqs. (
1
)–(
2
) and the bound in
Eqs. (
6
)–(
10
).

Microscopic setup: one-shot collision at strong coupling

Systems.

Both the system
S
and bath probe
E
are qubits (
\dim S=\dim E=2
).
A
single collision
starts from a product input

\rho_{SE}=\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E},

(S34)

and evolves under a joint unitary
U_{SE}
to

\rho^{\prime}_{SE}=U_{SE}(\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E})U_{SE}^{\dagger},\qquad\rho^{\prime}_{S}=\mathrm{Tr}_{E}\rho^{\prime}_{SE},\qquad\rho^{\prime}_{E}=\mathrm{Tr}_{S}\rho^{\prime}_{SE}.

(S35)

The entropy production
\Sigma
is computed from the process-level definition
(Eq. (
1
)) and obeys
\Sigma\geq D_{\mathrm{bath}}
(Eq. (
2
)).

Why this is
strong coupling
.

We do
not
assume weak interaction, a Markovian generator, or a perturbative GKLS limit.
Instead, we directly apply a generic two-qubit unitary
U_{SE}
with a tunable interaction angle

\phi
that can be
O(1)
(including values close to
\pi/2
), so a
single collision

can create large correlations
I(S\!:\!E)_{\rho^{\prime}}
and non-negligible bath relative entropy

D(\rho^{\prime}_{E}\|\rho_{E})
.
This is the nonperturbative regime in which linear-response approximations are not expected to hold
a priori, and where saturation/non-saturation of the fully nonlinear integrand in
Eq. (
10
) is genuinely informative.

State preparation: generic bath state and controlled system deviation

Bath reference state
\rho_{E}
.

We sample
\rho_{E}
as a generic full-rank qubit state via its Bloch representation

\rho_{E}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mathbb{I}+r\,\hat{n}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\Big),\qquad 0<r<1,\ \ \hat{n}\in S^{2},

(S36)

where
\vec{\sigma}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})
.
The Bloch direction
\hat{n}
is Haar-uniform on the sphere, and the radius
r
is drawn from a
mixture distribution designed to cover both moderately mixed and near-pure regimes, subject to
configurable cutoffs

r\in[r_{\min},r_{\max}],\qquad r_{\min}=0.1,\ \ r_{\max}=0.95\ \ (\text{default}).

(S37)

This produces a broad range of bath purities
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{E}^{2})=(1+r^{2})/2
and eigenvalue ratios, which
stress-tests numerical stability of
D(\rho^{\prime}_{E}\|\rho_{E})
and the covariance inversions below.

System input state
\rho_{S}
.

To probe both near-equilibrium and far-from-equilibrium behavior while retaining a clean control
parameter, we sample
\rho_{S}

relative to

\rho_{E}
using three modes:

•

Haar isospectral (default):

\rho_{S}=U\rho_{E}U^{\dagger}
with
U\in\mathrm{SU}(2)
Haar-random.
This keeps
\rho_{S}
and
\rho_{E}
isospectral (same eigenvalues) but generically misaligned
(equivalently, it injects coherence relative to the bath eigenbasis).

•

Small isospectral (near-fixed-point control):

\rho_{S}=U(\varepsilon)\rho_{E}U(\varepsilon)^{\dagger}
where

U(\varepsilon)=e^{-i\varepsilon(\hat{a}\cdot\vec{\sigma})/2}
rotates around a random axis
\hat{a}
,
with
\varepsilon\in[\varepsilon_{\min},\varepsilon_{\max}]
drawn log-uniformly.
This targets the regime where
V^{\prime}\approx V
and the Onsager/small-fluctuation expansion becomes visible.

•

Independent random:

\rho_{S}
is an independent Bloch-sampled qubit state, to explore fully generic nonequilibrium inputs.

Interaction unitary: partial swap + bath-aware fixed-point block

Two knobs for strong-coupling mixing.

We use a family of two-qubit unitaries with two independent sources of noncommuting dynamics.

(i) Partial swap.

Define the SWAP operator on
S\otimes E
by
\mathrm{SWAP}\,\ket{ij}=\ket{ji}
.
The partial-swap unitary is

U_{\mathrm{swap}}(\phi):=e^{-i\phi\,\mathrm{SWAP}}=\cos(\phi)\,\mathbb{I}_{SE}-i\sin(\phi)\,\mathrm{SWAP},\qquad\phi\in[\phi_{\min},\phi_{\max}],

(S38)

with default sampling range
\phi\in[0.05,1.57]
.
This is a canonical collision-model interaction: it continuously interpolates between identity and
(full) SWAP, and can strongly exchange information/charges between
S
and
E
in a single shot.

(ii)
\rho_{E}
-aware fixed-point unitary.

We additionally include a
bath-adapted
unitary
U_{\mathrm{fp}}
constructed in the eigenbasis of
\rho_{E}
.
Let
\rho_{E}=W\mathrm{diag}(p_{0},p_{1})W^{\dagger}
. In the basis where
\rho_{E}\otimes\rho_{E}
is diagonal, define

U_{\mathrm{fp}}^{(\mathrm{eig})}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\alpha}&0&0&0\\
0&&&0\\
0&&U_{2\times 2}&0\\
0&0&0&e^{i\beta}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\alpha,\beta\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,2\pi),\ \ U_{2\times 2}\sim\mathrm{Haar\ on}\ U(2),

(S39)

acting as arbitrary mixing in the
\{\ket{01},\ket{10}\}
subspace while only phasing
\ket{00}
and
\ket{11}
.
Transforming back,

U_{\mathrm{fp}}=(W\otimes W)\,U_{\mathrm{fp}}^{(\mathrm{eig})}\,(W\otimes W)^{\dagger},

(S40)

which
exactly preserves

\rho_{E}\otimes\rho_{E}
as a fixed point, i.e.,

U_{\mathrm{fp}}(\rho_{E}\otimes\rho_{E})U_{\mathrm{fp}}^{\dagger}=\rho_{E}\otimes\rho_{E}
.

(iii) Composed strong-coupling unitary (default).

Our default interaction is the composition

U_{SE}=U_{\mathrm{swap}}(\phi)\,U_{\mathrm{fp}},

(S41)

which provides two independent noncommuting “knobs”:
the swap angle
\phi
controlling direct exchange strength, and the bath-eigenbasis block
controlling additional coherent mixing that is
adapted to the chosen

\rho_{E}
.

Currents and non-Abelian charge geometry (what we measure)

Charge observables.

We consider
m=k=2
charge observables
q=(q_{u})_{u}
represented by qubit operators

q_{u}\equiv Q_{u}=\frac{1}{2}\,\hat{e}_{u}\cdot\vec{\sigma}.

(S42)

To avoid privileged axes, we often sample a
random charge frame
by drawing a random rotation

R\in\mathrm{SO}(3)
and setting
\{\hat{e}_{u}\}
to be (a subset of) the rotated orthonormal axes:

•

k=2
: choose
\hat{e}_{1}=R\hat{x}
and
\hat{e}_{2}=R\hat{z}
(two noncommuting charges).

This implements a genuinely non-Abelian setting at the level of observables: for
k\geq 2
the
Q_{u}

do not commute and define a matrix-valued covariance geometry.

Currents.

In the collision model, one can evaluate transported charge either on
S
or on
E
(depending on the
operational convention). In the numerics we use the bath-side change

\Delta q_{u}:=\mathrm{Tr}\!\big[(\rho^{\prime}_{E}-\rho_{E})\,Q_{u}\big],\qquad\Delta q=\big(\Delta q_{u}\big)_{u=1}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},

(S43)

which is directly computable from
\rho^{\prime}_{E}
and has the same status as the current vector
introduced in Eq. (
3
).

Covariances.

We compute the (real, symmetric) covariance matrix of the charges in the initial and final bath states:

V_{uv}:=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\rho_{E}\{\Delta Q_{u},\Delta Q_{v}\}\right],\qquad V^{\prime}_{uv}:=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\rho^{\prime}_{E}\{\Delta Q^{\prime}_{u},\Delta Q^{\prime}_{v}\}\right],

(S44)

where
\Delta Q_{u}=Q_{u}-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{E}Q_{u})\,\mathbb{I}
and similarly for
\Delta Q^{\prime}_{u}
with
\rho^{\prime}_{E}
.
These are the
V
and
V^{\prime}
entering Eq. (
6
).

Step-by-step simulation pipeline

For each Monte Carlo sample, we perform:

1.

Sample the bath state.

Draw
(r,\hat{n})
and set
\rho_{E}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{I}+r\,\hat{n}\cdot\vec{\sigma})
.

2.

Choose the charge vector.

Either fix
\{Q_{u}\}
to Pauli directions (legacy) or sample a random frame
R
and define

Q_{u}=\frac{1}{2}(R\hat{e}_{u})\cdot\vec{\sigma}
for
k=2
.

3.

Sample the system state.

Construct
\rho_{S}
using one of the modes above (typically Haar-isospectral relative to
\rho_{E}
).

4.

Sample the strong-coupling unitary.

Draw
\phi\in[\phi_{\min},\phi_{\max}]
, and draw
(\alpha,\beta,U_{2\times 2})
to build
U_{\mathrm{fp}}
,
then set
U_{SE}=U_{\mathrm{swap}}(\phi)U_{\mathrm{fp}}
.

5.

Evolve and reduce.

Compute

\rho^{\prime}_{SE}=U_{SE}(\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E})U_{SE}^{\dagger}
and partial traces

\rho^{\prime}_{S}=\mathrm{Tr}_{E}\rho^{\prime}_{SE}
,
\rho^{\prime}_{E}=\mathrm{Tr}_{S}\rho^{\prime}_{SE}
.

6.

Compute EP and bath relative entropy.

Compute

\Sigma=D\!\left(\rho^{\prime}_{SE}\,\big\|\,\rho^{\prime}_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}\right),\qquad D_{\mathrm{bath}}=D(\rho^{\prime}_{E}\|\rho_{E}),

(S45)

using spectral matrix logarithms (eigendecomposition with a small eigenvalue floor for stability).

7.

Compute currents and covariances.

Compute
\Delta q_{u}=\mathrm{Tr}[(\rho^{\prime}_{E}-\rho_{E})Q_{u}]
and the matrices
V,V^{\prime}
as above.
We also record diagnostics such as the relative covariance drift

\|V^{\prime}-V\|/\|V\|
(using Frobenius).

8.

Evaluate the bound.

For
\lambda\in(0,1)
, define the interpolated covariance
V_{\lambda}
and the scalar

s_{\lambda}=\Delta q^{\mathsf{T}}V_{\lambda}^{-1}\Delta q,

(S46)

and compute the integral in Eq. (
10
) by Gauss–Legendre quadrature on
[0,1]
.
Numerically, we regularize the inversion when
V_{\lambda}
becomes ill-conditioned.

Sampling strategies: how we find saturation

A purely uniform Monte Carlo can miss the near-saturation “manifold” when it occupies a small
volume in parameter space. We therefore use three strategies:

•

Monte Carlo:
unbiased baseline over all parameters.

•

Stratified sampling:
bin points by a chosen x-axis variable (e.g.
s
or the full bound)
to ensure uniform coverage across decades.

•

Saturation hunt:
start from a stratified baseline and then preferentially keep/refine the
smallest-gap samples per bin, yielding clouds that visibly approach the diagonal

D_{\mathrm{bath}}=B(\Delta q,V,V^{\prime})
in the master plot.

Small current regime and the inset theory curve

When
V^{\prime}\approx V
(small covariance drift, typical of the small-isospectral mode), the full integral bound
collapses to a one-parameter function of the simple signal-to-noise ratio

s:=\Delta q^{\mathsf{T}}V^{-1}\Delta q
,

D_{\mathrm{bath}}\approx F(s),\qquad F(s)=2\sqrt{\frac{s}{s+4}}\;\mathrm{artanh}\!\sqrt{\frac{s}{s+4}},

(S47)

with small-
s
expansion
F(s)=\frac{s}{2}-\frac{s^{2}}{12}+O(s^{3})
.
We overlay
F(s)
and its quadratic truncation in the inset to show explicitly how the
Onsager-type quadratic form emerges as the small-fluctuation limit while the main panel
displays the fully nonlinear strong-coupling behavior.
