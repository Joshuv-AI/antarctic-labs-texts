Factors associated with multiple sexual partnerships among men in South Africa
Authors: Disebo Maine, Karabo Mhele
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultiple sexual partnerships are associated with a higher risk of sexually transmitted infections. This study aims to determine the prevalence and predictors of multiple sexual partnerships among men in South Africa.MethodsThis is a cross-sectional study using data from the 2016 South African Demographic and Health Survey, with a sample of 3,616 males aged 15–59 years.ResultsThe prevalence rate of multiple sexual partnerships among men was 15,6%. Logistic regression results showed that the rate decreased with age: 25–34 years (AOR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.34– 0.70), 35–44 years (AOR = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.16–0.42), and 45–59 years (AOR = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.07–0.25) using age 15–24 as reference category, but higher in rural areas (AOR = 1.80, 95% CI:1.23–2.62); and among men who had sexual debut before 16 years (AOR = 2.28, 95% CI: 1.32–3.94); men who had heard about STIs (AOR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.15–2.19), transactional sex (AOR = 3.59, 95% CI: 2.20–5.86), coming from middle (AOR = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.07–2.30), and rich wealth index households (AOR = 1.75, 95% CI: 1.08–2.84), and higher when the recent sexual partner was an acquaintance (AOR = 1.97, 95% CI: 1.37–2.84).ConclusionIntervention should be provided at all societal levels. Information and protective measures should be intensified for young men, alongside campaigns aimed at discouraging early sexual debut at a young age.

Background

Studies have noted numerous negative outcomes associated with multiple sexual partnerships (MSPs), with a key concern being the increase in sexually transmitted infections (STIs), as well as engaging in other risky behaviors such as drug and alcohol use [[1, 2] as well as among psychoactive substance users [3] and unintended pregnancy among women [4] and more likely to occur among certain populations such as same sex male partners [5]. The prevalence of MSP is in Sub-Saharan African countries where prevalence of more than 30% was reported in some countries [6, 7].

Research indicates that men are at a higher risk than women of engaging in MSP, which increases their likelihood of contracting and spreading STIs [8]. This trend is particularly prominent in South Africa, where studies have consistently shown that men are more likely to participate in MSP compared to women [9–11]. A recent study has shown a rising trend in MSP among men, highlighting the urgency for enhanced focus and intervention strategies in this area [12]. Furthermore, a separate study revealed that men continue to engage in sexual relationships beyond the age of 50 years [13]. These issues underscore the significance of understanding factors driving this behavior among men, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, where the prevalence is higher. Nonetheless, there are few studies investigating MSP among men in general, as most research primarily targets young individuals, especially young women [10, 14] One notable study examining MSP among men in South Africa was limited to only a few provinces [10]. Consequently, there is a significant lack of comprehensive information regarding the predictors of MSP among men of all ages across South Africa. Therefore, this study intends to fill this gap by exploring the levels and predictors of MSP among men. A study of this nature can aid in the development of interventions aimed at reducing HIV and AIDS and assist in attaining of Sustainable Development Goal number 3 which deals with health and wellbeing. Such interventions would be particularly relevant in South Africa, which has the highest infection rates, with 20% of the population affected (Hodes, 2018) [15].

Numerous studies have identified various sociodemographic factors associated with the phenomenon of MSP. Younger individuals, particularly those aged from 15 to 24 years, are more likely to engage in MSP [16, 17]. Moreover, the phenomenon varies geographically throughout the country, highlighting the importance of incorporating provinces into the analysis when conducting such studies. A study by Mangold et al. [18] for instance, found that participants from the Free State province in South Africa demonstrated a markedly higher propensity to engage in MSP compared to their counterparts in the Western Cape. Similar findings have been reported in other countries, underscoring regional variation in the occurrence of MSP [19]. This observation suggests that socio-economic factors and cultural differences across different areas may contribute to this disparity.

Additionally, marital status significantly influences rates of MSP. Specifically, individuals who have previously been married exhibit a higher susceptibility to engaging in MSP [20, 21]. However, the higher prevalence of MSP among these individuals may be a contributing factor to the dissolution of their previous marriages, rather than a consequence. Conversely, it could also be that not being in a committed marriage increases one’s vulnerability to certain sexual behaviors, specifically MSP. Thus, the relationship between marital history and sexual behavior is complex and warrants further exploration to fully understand the contributing factors at play.

The relationship between MSP and educational attainment has been inconsistent. While one study found a positive correlation between higher education levels and MSP [12], other research reported that individuals with no education had a higher likelihood of engaging in MSP [21]. Additionally, a study conducted among men in Lesotho indicated that education was significant only at the secondary and higher levels [22]. Therefore, one can assume that the influence of education on MSP differs depending on the contextual factors in each country. Moreover, individuals who engage in sexual activity at an earlier age tend to have a greater number of sexual partners throughout their lives compared to those who delay their first sexual experience. This pattern suggests a correlation between MSP and engaging in other risky sexual behaviors, which may increase the likelihood of contracting HIV/AIDS [21]. Furthermore, transactional sex, which involves exchanging gifts, favors, or money for sex, has consistently been associated with multiple sexual partnerships [23].

Theoretical framework

This study employs the Sociological Ecological Model to investigate the various factors influencing MSP across different levels. The model underscores that social behaviors are shaped by a complex interplay of factors at the individual, interpersonal, community, and structural or societal levels.

At the individual level, personal characteristics such as age, educational level, and beliefs can have varying effects on MSP among individuals. At the interpersonal level, the nature of a person’s relationship networks can significantly influence their engagement in MSP. For instance, married men typically exhibit lower rates of MSP due to their commitment to their spouses, while single men may be more prone to higher-risk behaviors, including increased MSP.

At the community level, different cultural norms prevalent among racial groups contribute to variations in MSP behaviors. As a result, we would expect notable differences in MSP rates among men from diverse racial backgrounds. Furthermore, geographical factors—such as whether an area is rural or urban, as well as the specific provinces in which individuals reside—also play a crucial role in shaping MSP rates.

Consequently, we can expect distinct rates of MSP among men from diverse racial backgrounds. Additionally, geographical factors—such as whether an area is rural or urban, as well as the specific provinces where individuals live—also significantly impact MSP rates.

Data and methods

This is a cross-sectional analytical study based on the 2016 South African Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data collected by Statistics South Africa. Technical assistance was provided by ICF International, with funding from the United States Agency for International Development. The respondents consisted of weighted males from South Africa aged 15–59 years who were sexually active. The sampling weight variable was calculated from the men’s file using (mv005/100000). Furthermore, the survey design effects were adjusted using the primary sampling unit (PSU) (mv021) and stratified variable (mv023).

The survey employed a stratified two-stage cluster sampling design. In the first stage, Enumeration Areas (EAs) were selected using systematic random sampling with probability proportionate to size. In the second stage, a sample of households was drawn from an updated list of households within each selected EA. A total of 750 EAs were chosen in the first stage, and from each EA, 20 households were randomly selected in the second stage through systematic sampling. Ultimately, 11,083 households participated in the survey, comprising a nationally representative sample of over 15,000 residential units. The study has a total of 3616 respondents.

Exclusion criteria

Respondents were excluded from the analysis when they were not sexually active in the past 12 months before the survey.

Data analysis

The study used STATA version 17 (Stata Corp Inc, College Station, TX, USA) for data analysis. The analysis involved different methods. In the first stage of the analysis, descriptive statistics were employed to characterize the study population. The second stage involved bivariate analysis using a chi-square test to determine if there is a statistically significant association between MSP and the identified determinants. Furthermore, binary logistic regression analysis was used to model the relationship between the variables in the model and the dependent variable. The statistical significance level for all analyses was set at 0.05, meaning that results with a probability of occurring by chance of less than 5% were deemed statistically significant. Additionally, all confidence intervals were established at 95%, indicating that the true population value is expected to fall within the confidence interval 95% of the time.

Additionally, multicollinearity was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF). All variables had VIF values of less than 2.1, with a mean VIF value of 1.38, indicating no collinearity. The model was also tested using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test for Goodness-of-fit, and the results showed that the model fits reasonably well, with p > 0.05 (0.92).

Dependent variable

For this study, MSP refers to respondents having two or more sexual partners in the year before the study. This variable was derived from a question on the number of |sexual partners, including a spouse, in the last 12 months. The variable was coded “0” if the person reported less than two sexual partners and “1” if they had two or more sexual partners in a year before the survey.

Predictor variables

Predictor variables were the age of the respondent at the time of the survey, residence, province, level of education, racial population group, marital status, age at sexual debut, and recent sexual partner. These variables were categorized as follows: age of respondent at time of survey (15–24 years, 25–34 years, 35–44 years, and 45–59 years), residence (rural or urban); and the province of residence (Western Cape, Eastern Cape, Northern Cape, Free State, KwaZulu-Natal, North West, Gauteng, Mpumalanga, or Limpopo); level of education was (no education, primary, secondary, or tertiary); racial groups (Black, White, Colored or Asian); marital status (never in a union, currently in a union, and formerly in union); age of sexual debut (15 years and younger, 16–17 years, 18–19 years, and 20 years and older); recent sexual partner (girlfriend, spouse, living, partner, an acquaintance, and others). Other questions posed were “ever heard about STIs (yes, no); ever paid someone in exchange for sex (yes, no); sexually active in past 4 weeks (yes, no); smokes cigarette (No, don’t smoke, smokes every day, smokes some days); employment status (worked in the past year, not in the past year; currently working); household wealth index (poorest, poorer, middle, rich, and richest) and frequency of internet use last month (not at all, less than once a week, at least once a week, and almost every day).

Most participants (29.9%) had initiated sexual activity between the ages of 16 and 17 years, while 24.2% did so before turning 16 years of age. Sociodemographic characteristics of participants and MSP prevalence rate

MSP prevalence

Information in Table 1 illustrates that 15.6% of participants reported having two or more sexual partners in the year preceding the survey. The relationship between MSP and all variables in the study was statistically significant at the 5% level. The prevalence of MSP decreased with each subsequent age group, with those aged 15–24 years showing the highest rate at 20.7%, followed by 19.5% among individuals aged 25–34 years, and the lowest rate of 5.2% was among those aged 40–49 years.

Geographically, the highest prevalence was in rural areas, where 17.9% of participants engaged in MSP compared to 14.5% in urban areas. When examining the provinces, Free State and Limpopo had the highest prevalence rates at 21.9% and 21.6%, respectively, while the Northern Cape had the lowest at 7.5%. Additionally, prevalence increased with educational level, reflecting about a 12-percentage-point difference between those with no education (7.4%) and those with a tertiary education (19.8%). Also, the highest percentages were among participants who had previously been in a union (23.9%) and those who had never been in one (19.5%).

Among racial groups, Blacks had the highest percentage (17.1%) of those engaging in MSP, which is more than double the rate of other racial groups. For instance, the next highest rates were found among Coloreds and Whites, who exhibited prevalence rates of 6.3% and 5.2%, respectively. Participants who initiated sexual activity at ages younger than 16 years of age and between 16 and 17 years demonstrated the highest percentages of MSP engagement, with 26.3% and 20.4%, respectively, while those who postponed sexual debut until the age of 20 years or older showed the lowest percentage of 6.1%. Moreover, the MSP rate was highest when the recent sexual partner was merely an acquaintance, with over one-third (35.0%) having engaged in MSP, followed by those in relationships where the partner was defined as a “girlfriend,” showing a prevalence of 24.7%. Additionally, men who had heard about STIs (18.2%) engaged in MSP compared to only 8.9% of those who had not. MSP was most prevalent among participants from households with “richer” and “middle” wealth indices, with 18.2% and 18.1%, respectively, engaging with multiple partners. Furthermore, nearly half (43.7%) of those who had paid someone for sex reported being in a relationship with MSP, contrasting with only 14.3% of those who had not paid someone for sex.

Likewise, men who were sexually active in the four weeks before data collection had a higher prevalence rate of 21.4%, compared to just 7.3% for those who were not sexually active. Additionally, 26.9% of individuals employed in the past year were engaged in MSP, while only 15.8% of those not working reported engaging in MSP. Furthermore, internet use correlated with a higher prevalence rate; individuals who used the internet daily in the past month had the highest rate at 21.5%, compared to just 11.0% of those who did not use it at all. Smoking a cigarette was significantly associated with MSP. Individuals who smoked every day (17,6%) and some days (20.8%) had the highest percentage of those who reported engaging in MSP compared to only 13.9% of those who did not smoke.

Although the educational level was only statistically significant on the crude model showing that individuals with secondary (OR = 2.49, 95% CI:1.23–5.03) and tertiary (COR = 3.08, 95% CI: 1.43–6.63) levels were more likely to have engaged in MSP. Likewise, the race was only significant in the crude model showing high odds ratios for blacks (COR = 3.09, 95% CI: 1.43–6.69) while other racial groups were not statistically significant. Similarly, being in a union was associated with lower odds ratio (COR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.31–0.63) in the crude model but the variable was not statistically significant in the adjusted model. Furthermore, the number of children born to a participant was associated with higher odds ratios (AOR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.13–1.31) for each additional child born by the participant. The results also demonstrated that different sexual behaviors were significantly associated with MSP. Those who initiated sexual activity before age 15 or below had the highest odds ratios (AOR = 2.28, 95% CI: 1.32–3.94), followed by those who initiated sex between 16 and 17 years of age (AOR 1.82, 95% CI: 1.08–3.06). Moreover, if the last sexual partner was reported to be a “spouse,” the odds of engaging in MSP dropped by 78% (AOR = 0.22, 95% CI: 0.11–0.46). Conversely, if the partner was an “acquaintance,” the likelihood of MSP increased almost two-fold (AOR = 1.97, 95% CI: 1.37–2.84). Those who were sexually active in the past four weeks were 2.75 times more likely to have engaged in MSP (AOR = 2.75, 95% CI: 1.94–3.90). Additionally, individuals who paid someone for sex were more than three times as likely to have engaged in MSP (AOR = 3.59, 95% CI: 2.20–5.86). Interestingly, those who had heard about STIs were more likely to have engaged in MSP (AOR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.15–2.19) compared to those who had not. Lastly, smoking tobacco was not statistically significantly associated with MSP in the adjusted model, although the variable was significant in the crude model indicating that it increased for those who smoked every day (COR = 1.32, CI:102 − 1.71) and those who did so not every day (COR = 1.63, CI:1.08–2.46).

Furthermore, being in employment a year before the survey was associated with a higher likelihood of engaging in MSP (AOR = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.02–2.63). Additionally, compared to those who came from households classified as “poorest’’ in the wealth index, the odds of having MSP increased by 57% (AOR = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.07–2.30) and 75% (AOR = 1.75, 95% CI: 1.08–2.83) among those coming from “middle” and “richer” households, respectively. The use of the internet was significantly associated with MSP, but only for those who used it almost every day (AOR = 1.43, CI:1.03–2.00).

In summary, the variables that remained significant in the multivariate analysis were age groups, residing in rural areas, the additional number of children, early initiation of sexual activity, having engaged in sexual activity in the past four months, and the type of recent sexual partner.

Discussion

In the analysis, the study explored the factors associated with MSP among men in South Africa, using the Social Ecological Model. This model emphasizes the impact of individual, interpersonal, community, and structural factors on MSP. The results revealed that MSP is common in the country, with 15.6% of respondents reporting that they had engaged in sexual relations with more than one partner in the year preceding the survey. The findings revealed that these various factors at different levels are interconnected and collectively influence the occurrence of MSP. These factors are discussed in the section below.

Individual-Level factors

The study identified personal characteristics that were critical for the facilitation of MSP at the individual level, showing that age, age of sexual debut, transactional sex, and knowledge about STIs were significantly associated with MSP at the individual level. Regarding age, younger men aged 15–24 years were more likely to engage in MSP than their older counterparts. Similar studies conducted in South Africa and Ghana also supported this finding [2, 12, 22]. Hormonal changes during puberty can increase young men’s desire for sexual exploration, often leading them to experiment with different partners. This phase of experimentation is not only about physical attraction; it also reflects a broader search for social validation [24]. Most young men move away from home to work or study when they reach the adolescent stage. This provides a necessary environment for social experimentation, as they have limited parental control when they are away.

Moreover, MSP increased significantly with a decrease in the age of sexual debut. The finding is corroborated by other studies conducted elsewhere [25, 26]. The study further showed that this problem is endemic in the country, with more than half of the respondents (54.1%) reporting initiating sexual contact before the age of 18.

One of the surprising findings was that having knowledge about other STIs was associated with an increased risk of MSP, implying that knowledge does not automatically translate into safe practice. Other studies have also made similar findings, where those who perceive HIV as a high risk also have a higher tendency for MSP [10, 27]. A different study also found that individuals who knew someone who died from HIV-related diseases were more prone to having MSP [11]. This indicates that understanding the negative consequences of MSP does not necessarily deter someone from engaging in risky behavior, highlighting the need for a more in-depth exploration of this phenomenon. On the other hand, it might mean that people in risky relationships are at risk of contracting different STIs, thus increasing their knowledge level of these diseases.

Furthermore, individuals who paid for sex were found to have a higher likelihood of engaging in MSP. This may be interpreted as a personal behavioral choice reflecting one’s attitudes towards sexuality and risk. One study discovered that young men often engage in MSP primarily to seek social approval from their peers [28] and therefore are likely to use material means to entice potential sexual partners. One study found that this behavior posed the highest risk of HIV infection to sexual partners of the perpetrators [29].

Interpersonal factors

Among the interpersonal factors analyzed, the type of recent sexual partner emerged as a significant determinant of MSP, even after controlling for other variables. When the recent sexual partner was identified as a “spouse,” the risk of MSP decreased by over 70%. Conversely, having a relationship with an individual with whom the respondent had only a casual acquaintance resulted in more than a twofold increase in risk. This finding underscores the importance of interpersonal dynamics, as the nature of these relationships is shaped by varying social expectations and levels of commitment.

Community factors

The findings also revealed significant differences in place of residence and provinces. A study conducted in Uganda found that contextual factors, such as living in specific neighborhoods, were linked to a higher likelihood of engaging in MSP [3]. Supporting these findings, research in Ethiopia also showed significant regional variations in MSP [30]. These variations may be attributed to different cultural practices prevalent in various regions of a country. For example, participants from the province of Limpopo – one of the provinces with the most polygamous marriages – had the highest likelihood of engaging in MSP [31]. This suggests that MPS is also driven by cultural factors. Furthermore, differences in income levels in different geographical areas can create a suitable condition for MSP. Men from areas with better incomes may take advantage of poor women, who typically have limited options for improving their material circumstances [32]. This would explain why the risk is also higher in provinces such as Gauteng and Western Cape, which are economically more advanced than the rest of the provinces. Furthermore, higher migration levels in some areas may increase the risk of MSP as individual migrants may become lonely and interact with different people.

The household wealth index emerged as a significant factor indicating that men from middle- to higher-income households are more likely to engage in MSP, as corroborated in a different study [12]. Men from relatively affluent economic backgrounds possess the resources to attract women, particularly those from financially disadvantaged circumstances. Research indicates that women involved in MSP frequently originate from economically vulnerable backgrounds, suggesting that they may pursue relationships with men capable of providing essential support for their survival [2].

Conclusion and recommendations

The study revealed that factors influencing MSP operate at multiple levels, including individual, interpersonal, and societal dimensions. The findings suggest that interventions targeting only one of these levels may be inadequate to address this issue. It is crucial to consider broader structural factors, such as social inequality and gender norms, as they may significantly impact sexual behaviors related to MSP.

The study highlighted the significant prevalence of MSP, especially among younger men. The study therefore recommends implementing public health intervention programs and services specifically aimed at this demographic and high-risk areas. These programs should ensure an adequate supply of condoms, along with awareness initiatives to promote safe practices to help in the prevention of HIV.

Postponing the age of first sexual intercourse is identified as a protective factor against MSP. The study therefore recommends that awareness initiatives on this issue should be introduced to younger people before they know about sex. The findings suggest potential implications for HIV transmission rates within the population, as a lower prevalence of MSP is correlated with reduced rates of HIV and AIDS. Community-level strategies should address provincial disparities through equitable access to information and services. Also, HIV prevention programs should combine individual-focused education with structural interventions that addresses addressing underlying social determinants.

Future studies should consider an in-depth study to investigate the reasons why men who are aware of other STIs tend to engage in MSP. This exploration could provide valuable insights into the factors influencing their behavior.

Study limitations and strengths

The study used the most current and representative data in South Africa and therefore has the potential to update the current information that can be used by policymakers in the country. However, the study relies on self-reported data, which introduces the possibility of under-reporting or over-reporting regarding the number of sexual partners. Some questions might be perceived to be sensitive by the participants, especially those concerning the MSP. On the other hand, respondents may provide answers that are socially desirable. This has the potential to distort the association between MPS and some predictors, leading to misdirection of resources at the policy level. Additionally, the data only reflects the number of sexual partners in the past 12 months and does not account for previous sexual partners or overall sexual history. Additionally, the data did not collect information on drug use, which is one of the important behavioral variables associated with MSP.
