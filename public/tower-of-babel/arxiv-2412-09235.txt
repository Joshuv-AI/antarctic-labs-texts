Abstract
. We study stability of optimizers and convergence of Sinkhorn’s algorithm for the entropic optimal transport problem. In the special case of the quadratic cost, our stability bounds imply that if one of the two entropic potentials is semiconcave, then the relative entropy between optimal plans is controlled by the squared Wasserstein distance between their marginals. When employed in the analysis of Sinkhorn’s algorithm, this result gives a natural sufficient condition for its exponential convergence, which does not require the ground cost to be bounded. By controlling from above the Hessians of Sinkhorn potentials in examples of interest, we obtain new exponential convergence results. For instance, for the first time we obtain exponential convergence for log-concave marginals and quadratic costs for all values of the regularization parameter, based on semiconcavity propagation results. Moreover, the convergence rate has a linear dependence on the regularization: this behavior is sharp and had only been previously obtained for compact distributions [ CDV25 ] . These optimal rates are also established in situations where one of the two marginals does not have subgaussian tails. Other interesting new applications include subspace elastic costs, weakly log-concave marginals, marginals with light tails (where, under reinforced assumptions, we manage to improve the rates obtained in [ Eck25 ] ), the case of Lipschitz costs with bounded Hessian, and compact Riemannian manifolds. Keywords: Entropic Optimal Transport, Sinkhorn’s algorithm, Entropic stability, Semiconcavity propagation

1.
Introduction

Given two Polish spaces
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
, marginal probability distributions
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}),\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
, and a cost function
c:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the entropic optimal transport problem (EOT) reads as

(EOT)

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\rho,\nu)}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}c\,\mathrm{d}\pi+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi|\rho\otimes\nu),

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the set of couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
,
\mathrm{KL}
denotes the Kullback-Leibler divergence (also known as relative entropy), and
\varepsilon>0
is a regularization parameter.
The study of EOT has greatly intensified since the observation
[
Cut13
]
that adding an entropic penalty in the objective function of the Monge-Kantorovich problem (corresponding to
\varepsilon=0
in (
EOT
)) leads to a more convex, more regular, and numerically more tractable optimization task,
thus opening new perspectives for the computation of transport distances in machine learning and beyond, see
[
PC19
]
. Much of the success of entropic regularization techniques in applications can be attributed to the fact that EOT can be solved by means of an exponentially-fast matrix scaling algorithm, Sinkhorn’s algorithm, and to the fact that EOT is more stable than the Monge-Kantorovich problem with respect to variations in the cost or marginals. Because of this, considerable efforts have been made over the last decade to turn these intuitions into sound mathematical statements. This has produced many important contributions which we shall discuss in more detail below, nonetheless, several open questions remain. For example, exponential convergence of Sinkhorn algorithm is not well understood when both the marginals’ support and the ground cost are unbounded, as it is the case in the landmark example of the quadratic cost with Gaussian marginals. This article aims at showing that semiconcavity bounds for entropic and Sinkhorn potentials play a key role in answering some of these questions. We now provide the reader with some background on EOT, and then proceed to describe our main contributions.

1.1.
Background on EOT and Sinkhorn algorithm

The entropic optimal transport problem is a regularized version of the Monge-Kantorovich problem

(MK)

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\rho,\nu)}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}c\,\mathrm{d}\pi\,.

In the case when
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}
is endowed with a distance
{\mathbf{d}}
and
c(x,y)={\mathbf{d}}(x,y)^{2}
, the optimal value of (
MK
) coincides with the squared Wasserstein distance of order two, which we denote by
\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)
.
It is known
[
Nut21
, Thm 4.2]
that under mild integrability conditions on the cost
c
, there exist two functions
\varphi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}},\psi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon}:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
with
\overline{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, called entropic potentials, such that the unique optimal plan
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}
for (
EOT
) admits the Radon–Nikodým density

(1.1)

\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}}{\mathrm{d}(\rho\otimes\nu)}(x,y)=\exp\biggl(-\frac{c(x,y)+\varphi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon}(x)+\psi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon}(y)}{\varepsilon}\biggr)\,,\quad\rho\otimes\nu\,\text{a.e.}

For any measurable
\varphi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
and
\psi:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
we set

(1.2)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\rho}_{0}(\varphi)(y)

\displaystyle:=-\varepsilon\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}\exp\biggl(-\frac{c(x,y)+\varphi(x)}{\varepsilon}\biggr)\rho(\mathrm{d}x)\,,

\displaystyle\Psi^{\nu}_{0}(\psi)(x)

\displaystyle:=-\varepsilon\log\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\exp\biggl(-\frac{c(x,y)+\psi(y)}{\varepsilon}\biggr)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\,.

Imposing that a probability measure of the form (
1.1
) belongs to
\Pi(\rho,\nu)
yields the following non-linear system, also known as Schrödinger system,

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \varphi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon}&=-\Psi_{0}^{\nu}(\psi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon})\,,\\
\psi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon}&=-\Phi^{\rho}_{0}(\varphi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon})\,.\end{aligned}\right.

Note that a priori the identities (
1.3
) are valid only
\rho
-almost surely and
\nu
-almost surely respectively. However, since
\Psi_{0}^{\nu}
and
\Phi^{\rho}_{0}
are well-defined even outside the supports, we obtain extensions of
\varphi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon}
,
\psi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon}
taking values in
\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
such that (
1.3
)
holds everywhere on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
.
Sinkhorn’s algorithm solves (
1.3
) as a fixed point problem,
i.e.
, it constructs two sequences of potentials
(\varphi^{n}_{\varepsilon},\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon})
defined through the iterations

(1.4)

\begin{cases}\varphi^{n+1}_{\varepsilon}&=-\Psi_{0}^{\nu}(\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon})\,,\\
\psi^{n+1}_{\varepsilon}&=-\Phi^{\rho}_{0}(\varphi^{n+1}_{\varepsilon})\,.\end{cases}

Typically, the initialization is
\varphi^{0}_{\varepsilon}=0
, but other choices are possible.

1.2.
Entropic stability for optimal plans

In this article, we consider a broad setting in which we require the target space
(\mathcal{Y},\mathfrak{g})
to be a (possibly unbounded) connected Riemannian manifold without boundary endowed with its Riemannian metric
\mathfrak{g}
whose associated Riemannian distance we denote
{\mathbf{d}}
. To fix ideas,
one can simply take
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with the standard Euclidean metric. We will often consider this setting when applying the abstract results to concrete examples in
Section

1.4
. To state our main results, we need to introduce a notion of
\Lambda
-semiconcavity by saying that a differentiable function
f:(\mathcal{Y},\mathfrak{g})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
\Lambda
-semiconcave if for all
z,y\in\mathcal{Y}
and for any geodesic
(\gamma_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
such that
\gamma_{0}=y
and
\gamma_{1}=z
we have

(1.5)

f(z)-f(y)\leq\langle\nabla f(y),\,\dot{\gamma_{0}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}+\frac{\Lambda}{2}\,{\mathbf{d}}(z,y)^{2}\,.

In the Euclidean setting, where geodesics are straight lines, this is equivalent to requiring that for all
z,y\in\mathcal{Y}
it holds

f(z)-f(y)\leq\langle\nabla f(y),\,z-y\rangle+\frac{\Lambda}{2}\,{\mathbf{d}}(z,y)^{2}\,.

The first contribution of this article is an abstract
\mathrm{KL}
-stability bound for optimal plans associated with different marginal distributions.
For this result to hold, we require to be able to differentiate under the integral sign in (
1.3
), thus being able to write the gradient of
\psi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon}
as a conditional mean.

Assumption 1
.

Assume
c(x,\cdot)
to be
\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathcal{Y})
and that we can differentiate under the integral sign in (
1.3
). That is, the formula

(1.6)

\nabla\psi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon}(y)=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\nabla_{2}c(x,y)\,\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}(\mathrm{d}x|y)\,,

holds in the sense of weak derivatives
\nu
-a.e.

The identity (
1.6
) is well-known and often used the EOT literature. Particularly, it holds (even in a strong sense) in a variety of examples including the ones we are going to discuss in section
Section

1.4
. For readers’ convenience we show at
Lemma

A.1
in
Appendix

A
that
Assumption

1
holds under an integrability condition involving
\rho
, the cost
c
and its first derivative w.r.t.
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.

Theorem 1.1

(KL stability of optimal plans)
.

Let
Assumption

1
hold and
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu},\,\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\mu}
be the unique optimizers in (
EOT
) for the set of marginals
(\rho,\nu)
and
(\rho,\mu)
. If there exists
\Lambda>0
such that

y\mapsto c(x,y)+\psi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon}(y)

is
\Lambda
-semiconcave uniformly in
x\in\supp(\rho)
, then

\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\mu}|\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu})\leq\mathrm{KL}(\mu|\nu)+\frac{\Lambda}{2{\varepsilon}}\,\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)\,.

The key step in the proof of
Theorem

1.1
is the observation, made at Lemma
2.1
, that the semiconcavity assumption implies the following
\mathrm{KL}
-bound between conditional distributions

\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}(\cdot|y)|\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}(\cdot|z))\leq\frac{\Lambda}{2\varepsilon}\,{\mathbf{d}}^{2}(y,z)\,.

The desired conclusion is then obtained using the gluing method, which is often employed in works on the stability of both entropic and classical optimal transport, see
[
EN22
,
DNWP24
,
LM24
]
for example.

Let us also mention here that our proof strategy is robust and easily generalizes to settings where we consider different notions of semiconcavity. In particular, given any non-negative function
\omega\colon\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
we say that a differentiable function
f:(\mathcal{Y},\mathfrak{g})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
(\Lambda,\omega)
-semiconcave if for all
z,y\in\mathcal{Y}
and for any geodesic
(\gamma_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
such that
\gamma_{0}=y
and
\gamma_{1}=z
we have

f(z)-f(y)\leq\langle\nabla f(y),\,\dot{\gamma_{0}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}+\frac{\Lambda}{2}\,\omega(z,y)\,.

Then, if
\mathbf{W}_{\omega}
denotes the generalized Wasserstein functional induced by
\omega
,
i.e.
, for any
\mu,\,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})

\mathbf{W}_{\omega}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}}\omega(z,y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(z,y)\,,

the entropic stability result stated in
Theorem

1.1
immediately generalizes to the following

Theorem 1.2

(KL stability of optimal plans generalized)
.

Let
Assumption

1
hold and
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu},\,\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\mu}
be the the unique optimizers in (
EOT
) for the set of marginals
(\rho,\nu)
and
(\rho,\mu)
. If there exists
\Lambda>0
and a non-negative function
\omega\colon\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

y\mapsto c(x,y)+\psi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon}(y)

is
(\Lambda,\omega)
-semiconcave uniformly in
x\in\supp(\rho)
, then

\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\mu}|\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu})\leq\mathrm{KL}(\mu|\nu)+\frac{\Lambda}{2{\varepsilon}}\,\mathbf{W}_{\omega}(\mu,\nu)\,.

This general result covers a diverse range of marginals, whose log-densities might not decay as Gaussians. For instance, it applies to the quadratic cost
c(x,y)=|x-y|^{2}/2
with marginals
\rho(\mathrm{d}x)\propto\exp(-|x|^{q})\mathrm{d}x
and
\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\propto\exp(-\sum_{i=1}^{d}|y_{i}|^{p}_{p})\mathrm{d}y
with
q\geq 2
and
p\in(1,2)
(with
\omega(z,y)=|z-y|^{1+\frac{p}{q}}
, cf.
[
GS25
]
).

Literature review: stability

Many recent works study the stability for entropic optimal transport with respect to variations in the marginal inputs.
In
[
CCGT23
]
, we obtain entropic stability bounds in a negative Sobolev norm, for a general class of problems with costs induced by diffusions on Riemannian manifolds with Ricci curvature bounded from below (which includes the quadratic cost on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
). The article
[
EN22
]

obtains a quantitative Hölder estimate between the Wasserstein distance of optimal plans and that of their marginals. This result applies to the quadratic cost, and to more general costs. Among the assumptions required, there is a transport-entropy inequality for marginals, which relates to Talagrand’s inequality, see

TI

(
τ
)

below. A more qualitative stability is proven under mild hypothesis in
[
GNB22
]
.
On the dual side, Carlier and Laborde show in
[
CL20
]

\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
-Lipschitz bounds for potentials for multimarginal problems in a bounded setting. The authors of
[
DdBD24
]
succeed in controlling the
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
-norm of the difference of entropic potentials with the Wasserstein distance of order one between marginals using an approach based on Hilbert’s metric. Among their assumptions, there is boundedness of the cost function and compactness of the marginals’ supports. Subsequently,
[
CCL24
]
bounds the difference of potentials with the Wasserstein distance of order two of the respective marginals. The norm used to express these bounds depends on the smoothness and boundedness of the cost. For example, the authors show an
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
-bound between the gradient of entropic potentials provided the cost is bounded with two bounded derivatives. For the quadratic cost, gradients of entropic potentials provide with good approximations of optimal transport maps (see
[
Gre24
,
MS23
]
in unbounded settings and
[
PNW21
]
in semidiscrete settings), thus justifying the growing interest around the stability of
EOT
.
[
DNWP24
]
establishes Lipschitz bounds between the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-norm of the difference of the gradients of entropic potentials and the Wasserstein distance of the marginals. Here, the dependence of the Lipschitz constant on the regularization parameter is polynomial, thus improving on earlier results,
and marginals may have unbounded support. All these results are obtained using a functional inequality for tilt-stable probability measures, see
[
CE22
]
and
[
BBD24
, Lemma 3.21]
. Their main assumption is that both entropic potentials have a bounded Hessian. For this reason, these stability results are the closest in spirit to ours and partially inspired our work. Nonetheless, there are some important differences: we only require a bound on the Hessian of one of the two potentials, and the scope of
Theorem

1.1
is not restricted to the quadratic cost, but covers general semiconcave costs. Finally, we mention the works
[
GMT22
,
LM24
]
since, even if they study the stability problem for unregularized optimal transport, they are somewhat connected to this work. In
[
GMT22
, Prop. 8]
the authors establish a link between the optimality gap and the distance from the optimal plan, provided that the optimal transport map is generated by a
c
-concave potential. In
[
LM24
]
covariance inequalities are employed to bound the distance between transport maps. As we shall see in the sequel of this article, and as already understood in
[
FGP20
,
CP23
,
Con24
]
, these inequalities are valid tools for estimating the concavity properties of entropic potentials, see also
[
GS25
]
for very recent results in this direction.

1.3.
Exponential convergence of Sinkhorn’s algorithm

Sinkhorn’s algorithm admits a primal interpretation as the iterated Bregman projection algorithm for the relative entropy functional, see
[
BCC+15
]
. To explain this, we introduce two sequences of plans, called Sinkhorn plans, as follows:

(1.7)

\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi^{n,n}}{\mathrm{d}(\rho\otimes\nu)}=\exp\Big(-\frac{c+\varphi^{n}_{\varepsilon}\oplus\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\Big)\,,\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi^{n+1,n}}{\mathrm{d}(\rho\otimes\nu)}=\exp\Big(-\frac{c+\varphi^{n+1}_{\varepsilon}\oplus\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\Big)\,.

In the above, and in the rest of the paper, for given potentials
\varphi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
\psi:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we set
\varphi\oplus\psi(x,y)=\varphi(x)+\psi(y)
.
Then,
\pi^{n+1,n}
may be viewed as the entropic projection of
\pi^{n,n}
in the sense of Csiszár
[
Csi75
]
on the set of plans having first marginal
\rho
. Likewise,
\pi^{n+1,n+1}
is the entropic projection of
\pi^{n+1,n}
on the set of plans having second marginal
\nu
. The second marginal
\nu^{n+1,n}
of
\pi^{n+1,n}
and the first marginal
\rho^{n,n}
of
\pi^{n,n}
are then given by

(1.8)

\mathrm{d}\nu^{n+1,n}\coloneqq e^{-\nicefrac{{(\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}-\psi^{n+1}_{\varepsilon})}}{{\varepsilon}}}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,,\quad\mathrm{d}\rho^{n,n}\coloneqq e^{-\nicefrac{{(\varphi^{n}_{\varepsilon}-\varphi^{n+1}_{\varepsilon})}}{{\varepsilon}}}\,\mathrm{d}\rho\,.

The second general result of this article is a sufficient condition for the exponential convergence of Sinkhorn plans to the optimal plan
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}
. To state it, let us recall that a probability measure
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
is said to satisfy a Talagrand’s inequality with constant
\tau
,

TI

(
τ
)

for short, if

(
\mathrm{TI}(\tau)
)

\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)\leq 2\tau\,\mathrm{KL}(\mu|\nu)\,,\quad\forall\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})\,.

Theorem 1.3

(Exponential convergence of Sinkhorn’s algorithm)
.

Let
c(x,\cdot)
be
\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathcal{Y})
and assume that there exist
\Lambda\in(0,+\infty)
and
N\geq 2
such that

y\mapsto c(x,y)+\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}(y)

is
\Lambda
-semiconcave uniformly in
x\in\supp(\rho)
and
n\geq N-1
.

(i)

If there exists
\tau\in(0,+\infty)
such that
\nu
satisfies

TI

(
τ
)

, then for all
n\geq N-1

\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}|\pi^{n+1,n+1})\leq\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}|\pi^{n+1,n})\leq\bigg(1-\frac{\min\{\tau\Lambda,\,\varepsilon\}}{\min\{\tau\Lambda,\,\varepsilon\}+\tau\Lambda}\bigg)^{(n-N+2)}\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}|\pi^{0,0})\,.

(ii)

If there exists
\tau\in(0,+\infty)
such that
\nu^{n,n-1}
satisfies

TI

(
τ
)

for all
n\geq N
, then

\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}|\pi^{n+1,n+1})\leq\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}|\pi^{n+1,n})\leq\bigg(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon+\tau\Lambda}\bigg)^{(n-N+1)}\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}|\pi^{0,0})

holds for all
n\geq N
.

Examples of measures satisfying the Talagrand transport inequality

(
τ
)

can be found for instance in the monograph
[

]
and in
[
GRS11
]
. This inequality interpolates between a Log-Sobolev inequality and a Poincaré inequality (see e.g.
[

, Theorem 9.6.1 and Proposition 9.6.2]
, see also

]
for its connection with log-Sobolev inequalities). Let us just mention here that this class includes log-concave measures and weakly log-concave measures
[

, Remark 1.7]
, and it is stable under products
[
BGL13
, Proposition 9.2.4]
and under bounded perturbations
[
GRS11
, Corollary 1.7]
.

1.4.
Examples of application

We now discuss several applications of Theorem
1.3
. We emphasize that this result provides with a general sufficient condition for the exponential convergence of Sinkhorn’s algorithm: the examples we present here do not represent its full range of applicability, which deserves to be further explored.
Finally, at the end of the current section we analyse a different broad range of examples where the standard
\Lambda
-semiconcavity approach fails, but that can yet be treated with the the more abstract
(\Lambda,\omega)
-semiconcavity strategy,
i.e.
, with
Theorem

1.2
and
Theorem

1.4
.

•

If we write that
“Sinkhorn’s algorithm converges exponentially fast and for
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{m}
(explicitly given) the rate is
1-\kappa(\varepsilon)
”
we always mean that for any
\varepsilon>0
there is
\kappa^{\prime}\in(0,1)
such that for all
n\geq 1
we have

(1.10)

\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}|\pi^{n+1,n+1})\leq(1-\kappa^{\prime})^{n}\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}|\pi^{0,0})

and that if
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{m}
, then
\kappa^{\prime}
can be taken to be equal to
\kappa(\varepsilon)
. The requirement that
\varepsilon
is small enough is just to have
\min\{\tau\Lambda,\varepsilon\}=\varepsilon
when applying Theorem
1.3
. In all examples, this hypothesis could be dropped, and the convergence rate is always explicit even for large
\varepsilon
, but we prefer to keep it for readability.

•

If we write that
“Sinkhorn’s algorithm converges exponentially fast and for
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{m}
(explicitly given) the
asymptotic
rate is
1-\kappa(\varepsilon)
”
we mean that for any
\varepsilon>0
(
1.10
) holds for some
\kappa^{\prime}\in(0,1)
and all

. Moreover, if
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{m}
, for all
\delta>0
there exists

such that (
1.10
) holds for
n\geq N_{\delta}
and
\kappa^{\prime}=\kappa(\varepsilon)-\delta

We start our gallery of examples with the case of (weakly) log-concave marginals. To state the next proposition, we introduce the notation

Proposition 1.5

(Anisotropic quadratic costs and (weakly) log-concave marginals)
.

Let

\mathcal{X},\,\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be two open (connected, possibly unbounded) domains of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
endowed with the Euclidean metric
and assume that
c(x,y)\coloneqq\langle x-y,\,\Sigma(x-y)\rangle/2

. Further assume that
\nu
satisfies

TI

(
τ
)

.

(i)

If
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\rho(\mathrm{d}x)=e^{-U_{\rho}(x)}\mathrm{d}x

(1.11)

\nabla^{2}U_{\rho}(x)\succeq\alpha_{\rho},\quad\nabla^{2}U_{\nu}(y)\preceq\beta_{\nu}\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

1-\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon+\tau\|\Sigma\|_{2}(\nicefrac{{\beta_{\nu}}}{{\alpha_{\rho}}})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\,.

Otherwise, for any general initialization

(ii)

If
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}

the rate is

(1.13)

1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\,\alpha_{\rho}}{\varepsilon^{2}\,\alpha_{\rho}+\tau\,\|\Sigma\|_{2}^{2}}\,.

(iii)

In the quadratic cost case

Remark 1.6
.

In fact, in

The previous result applies to the fundamental example of the Euclidean quadratic cost

If we consider a marginal

Here and below, for a function

Proposition 1.7

(
\rho

.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open (connected, possibly unbounded) domain of

(1.16)

\nabla^{2}U_{\rho}(x)\succeq\begin{cases}C\,|x|^{\delta}\quad&\text{ for }|x|>R\,,\\
-L\quad&\text{ for }|x|\leq R\,.\end{cases}

Further assume that
\nu
satisfies

TI

(
τ
)

. Then we distinguish two cases.

(i)

If

(1.17)

\nabla_{1}^{2}c(x,y),\nabla^{2}_{2}c(x,y)\succeq h(c)\quad\text{ and }\quad\left\|\nabla_{1}\nabla_{2}c(x,y)\right\|_{2}\vee\left\|\nabla_{1}^{2}c(x,y)\right\|_{2}\leq H(c)\,

and

1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2+\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}}}{\varepsilon^{2+\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}}+\varepsilon^{1+\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}}\tau\delta_{H}+2\tau H(c)^{2}\,(\varepsilon^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}}+(L+2)^{2}\,C^{-\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}}(\varepsilon+2\delta_{H})^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}})}

1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}+2\,\tau\,\|\Sigma\|_{2}^{2}\,\Bigl(1+(L+1)^{2}\bigl[R^{2}\vee{C^{-\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}}}\bigr]\Bigr)}\,.

The class of probability measures satisfying the light-tails condition (

U_{\rho}(x)=C_{0}(1+|x|^{2+\delta})
for which

and similarly

For Lipschitz costs with bounded Hessian, we deduce the following convergence result.

Proposition 1.8

(Lipschitz costs)
.

Let
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be two open (connected, possibly unbounded) domains of

(1.18)

Further assume that
\nu
satisfies

TI

(
τ
)

. We distinguish two cases.

(i)

Assume that the cost is Lipschitz in

(1.19)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|c(x,y)-c(x,\hat{y})|\leq\mathrm{Lip}_{\infty,2}(c)\,|y-\hat{y}|\,\quad\forall y,\hat{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Then, Sinkhorn’s algorithm converges exponentially fast and for
\varepsilon
small enough (as in (
2.31
)) the rate is

1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}+\tau\varepsilon(H(c)-h(c))+\tau\mathrm{Lip}^{2}_{\infty,2}(c)}\,.

(ii)

Assume that the cost is Lipschitz in
x
, uniformly in
y

(1.20)

\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|c(x,y)-c(\hat{x},y)|\leq\mathrm{Lip}_{1,\infty}(c)\,|x-\hat{x}|\ \quad\forall x,\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

\left\|\nabla_{12}^{2}c(x,y)\right\|_{2}\leq H(c)

(

Assume that

(

Lastly, we discuss the convergence of Sinkhorn’s algorithm on Riemannian manifolds, starting from the
d

\mathbb{S}^{d}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
endowed with the angular metric and corresponding angular distance between two points, that is defined for any

On
{\mathbb{S}^{d}}
we will consider the

c(x,y)=1-\cos({\mathbf{d}}(x,y))=1-\langle x,y\rangle\,,\quad\text{ and }\quad c_{\delta}(x,y)=\arccos(\delta\langle x,y\rangle)^{2}

which avoids the singularity the Hessian of

faces when considering antipodal points

. For this class of problems we can prove the following.

Proposition 1.10

(Riemannian sphere)
.

Assume that
\nu
satisfies

TI

(
τ
)

. We distinguish two cases.

(i)

[

,

(Heat kernel cost)

1.5.
Literature review: Sinkhorn’s algorithm

Bounded costs.

Sinkhorn’s algorithm has a long history, going back at least to the works of Sinkhorn
[
Sin64
]
and Sinkhorn and Knopp
[
SK67
]
in the discrete setting. Here, it is employed as an algorithm to construct a matrix with prescribed rows and columns sums. Other important early contributions include
[
FL89
,
BLN94
]
. In particular,
[
FL89
]
introduced Hilbert’s metric as a tool to prove exponential convergence. The realization
[
Cut13
]
that
EOT
provides with a numerically more tractable version of the Monge-Kantorovich problem triggered an explosion of interest on the subject. For bounded costs,
[
CGP16
]
obtained the first exponential convergence results in the continuous setting using the Hilbert metric approach and
[
DMG20
]
establishes qualitative
\mathrm{L}^{p}
convergence and regularity estimates for Sinkhorn iterates using an optimal transport approach; in particular these results apply to the multimarginal case as well. The exponential convergence of the algorithm in the multimarginal setting is a result of Carlier
[
Car22
]
. A more probabilistic viewpoint is introduced in
[
GNCD23
]
, where contraction estimates are obtained by means of coupling arguments. The work
[
CDV25
]
shows that exponential convergence takes place for semiconcave bounded costs, under various sets of hypotheses on the marginals.
The main innovation of this work consists in showing that the exponential rate of convergence deteriorates polynomially in
\varepsilon
. This is in contrast with previous works that exhibited a rate of convergence that decays exponentially in
\varepsilon
.

Unbounded costs.

For unbounded costs and marginals, Rüschendorf
[
Rüs95
]
establishes qualitative convergence for Sinkhorn plans in relative entropy. These results are improved in
[
NW22
]
where the authors manage to show qualitative convergence on both the primal and dual side under mild assumptions on the cost and marginals. The work by Léger
[
Lég21
]
provides with a insightful interpretation of Sinkhorn’s algorithm as a block-coordinate descent algorithm on the dual problem, see also
[
AFKL22
,
LAF23
]
. From this interpretation, it follows that the speed of convergence is at least
n^{-1}
under minimal assumptions. Polynomial rates of convergence are also established in
[
EN22
]
as a consequence of the above mentioned stability results and subsequently improved in
[
GN22
]
, exploiting a symmetrized version of the KL-divergence. It is only very recently that the first exponential convergence results for unbounded costs and marginals have appeared. To the best of our knowledge, the first article containing such results is
[
CDG23
]
, which studies the quadratic cost. The main result is that if the marginals are weakly log-concave and
\varepsilon
is large enough, exponential convergence of
\nabla\varphi^{n}
to
\nabla\varphi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon}
takes place. This kind of convergence is particularly useful as

[

PNW21
]
. The proof follows a geometric approach that highlights that semiconvexity and semiconcavity bounds on Sinkhorn potentials can be leveraged to obtain exponential convergence. Subsequently, the article
[
Eck25
]
succeeds in constructing versions of the Hilbert metric that are contractive for general unbounded costs. In contrast with
[
CDG23
]
, exponential convergence is shown for all values of
\varepsilon
. Roughly speaking, the main assumption is that there exist some

such that

grows no faster than

and the tails of both

\nu

for some

. When applied to the quadratic cost, this assumptions does not completely cover log-concave distributions and their perturbations, leaving out Gaussian marginals for example. Over the past few years, a number of relevant contributions focused on different asymptotic properties of Sinkhorn’s algorithm than the speed of convergence. It would be impossible to account for all of them here. Let us just mention
[
Ber20
]
for the relation with Monge-Ampère equation,
[
DKPS23
]
for the construction of Wasserstein mirror gradient flows, and
[

for construction of a Transformer variant inspired by Sinkhorn’s algorithm.

\nabla_{2}^{2}(c(x,y)+\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}(y))=\Sigma+\nabla^{2}\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}(y)\overset{\eqref{eq:hess:hess:cov:psieot}}{=}\varepsilon^{-1}\,\mathrm{Cov}_{X\sim\pi^{n,n}(\cdot|y)}(\Sigma(y-X))\,.

Therefore in this section, in order to study the semiconcavity of
y\mapsto c(x,y)+\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}(y)
, it is enough to control the conditional covariance matrices.

2.3.1.
Log-concavity of
\rho

We start with the proof of (ii).
From (
2.3
) (applied along Sinkhorn’s algorithm) it follows
\nabla^{2}\varphi^{n}_{\varepsilon}(x)\succeq-\Sigma
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, which combined with (
1.7
) further implies that for all
y

\nabla^{2}_{1}\big(-\log\pi^{n,n}(x|y)\big)\succeq\nabla^{2}U_{\rho}(x)\succeq\alpha_{\rho}\,,

where we wrote
\pi^{n,n}(x|y)
for the density of
\pi^{n,n}(\mathrm{d}x|y)
with respect to the Lebesgue measure.
This guarantees that, uniformly in
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the conditional measure
\pi^{n,n}(\mathrm{d}x|y)
satisfies the Poincaré inequality
\mathrm{PI}(\alpha_{\rho}^{-1})
(cf.
[
BGL13
, Corollaries 4.8.2]
).

Then, from (
2.11
) we deduce that for any unit vector
v
it holds

\varepsilon\,\langle v,\,\nabla_{2}^{2}(c(x,y)+\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}(y))v\rangle=\mathrm{Var}_{X\sim\pi^{n,n}(\cdot|y)}(\langle v,\,\Sigma X\rangle)\leq\alpha_{\rho}^{-1}\,\|\Sigma v\|^{2}\leq\alpha_{\rho}^{-1}\,\|\Sigma\|_{2}^{2}\,.

This means that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and uniformly in
x\in\supp(\rho)
the map
y\mapsto c(x,y)+\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}(y)
is
\Lambda
-semiconcave on
\supp(\nu)
with
\Lambda=(\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho})^{-1}\|\Sigma\|_{2}^{2}
. This combined with
Theorem

1.3
-
(i)
proves the exponential proves convergence of Sinkhorn’s algorithm. If
\varepsilon\leq\|\Sigma\|_{2}\,\sqrt{\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\alpha_{\rho}}}}
, then
\varepsilon\leq\tau\Lambda
and the rate takes the form (
1.13
).

2.3.2.
Log-concavity of
\rho
and log-semiconvexity of
\nu

We now discuss (i). Strengthening our assumption on the marginals, we can improve on the convergence rate.
As a starter, we show the following convexity/concavity result that generalizes what is already known for the Euclidean quadratic cost in
[
CP23
]
and
[
CDG23
, Theorem 10]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume
\Sigma\succ 0
,
\nabla^{2}U_{\rho}\succeq\alpha_{\rho}
and
\nabla^{2}U_{\nu}\preceq\beta_{\nu}
for some
\alpha_{\rho}>0
and
\beta_{\nu}\in(0,+\infty)
. If
\nabla^{2}\varphi^{0}_{\varepsilon}\succeq-\Sigma+A_{0}\,\Sigma
for some matrix
A_{0}\succeq 0
commuting with
\Sigma
, then for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(2.12)

\nabla^{2}\varphi^{n}_{\varepsilon}\succeq-\Sigma+A_{n}\,\Sigma\quad\text{ and }\quad\nabla^{2}\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}\preceq-\Sigma+B_{n}\,\Sigma\,,

where
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are two sequences of positive semidefinite symmetric matrices iteratively defined via

(2.13)

\begin{cases}B_{n}=\Sigma(A_{n}\Sigma+\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho})^{-1}\,,\\
A_{n+1}=\Sigma(B_{n}\Sigma+\varepsilon\beta_{\nu})^{-1}\,,\end{cases}

and converging to

(2.14)

\displaystyle A_{\infty}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}}{2}\Sigma^{-1}+\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\alpha_{\rho}^{2}}{4}\Sigma^{-2}+\frac{\alpha_{\rho}}{\beta_{\nu}}\biggr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\,,

\displaystyle B_{\infty}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon\beta_{\nu}}{2}\Sigma^{-1}+\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\beta_{\nu}^{2}}{4}\Sigma^{-2}+\frac{\beta_{\nu}}{\alpha_{\rho}}\biggr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\,.

Let us briefly notice before the proof that usually Sinkhorn’s algorithm is initialized at
\varphi^{0}_{\varepsilon}=0
, for which the assumption
\nabla^{2}\varphi^{0}_{\varepsilon}\succeq-\Sigma
holds with
A_{0}=0
. Nonetheless, as for any
n\geq 1
,
\nabla^{2}\varphi^{n}_{\varepsilon}\succeq-\Sigma
by (
2.3
), one can get a similar conclusion for any initialization and all
n\geq 1
by replacing (
2.12
) with
\nabla^{2}\varphi^{n}_{\varepsilon}\succeq-\Sigma+A_{n-1}\,\Sigma
and
\nabla^{2}\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}\preceq-\Sigma+B_{n-1}\,\Sigma
, where
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfy (
2.13
).

Proof.

The idea of the proof is to mimic the iterative proof given in
[
Con24
,
CDG23
]
combined with covariance bounds as in
[
CP23
]
. We will proceed by induction. The base case holds true with
A_{0}\succeq 0
, by assumption. Next, let us assume that
\nabla^{2}\varphi^{n}_{\varepsilon}\succeq-\Sigma+A_{n}\Sigma
for some
A_{n}\succeq 0
. This implies that the conditional measure
\pi^{n,n}(\cdot|y)
is log-concave since

\nabla^{2}_{1}(-\log\pi^{n,n}(x|y))=\varepsilon^{-1}(\Sigma+\nabla^{2}\varphi^{n}_{\varepsilon}(x))+\nabla^{2}U_{\rho}(x)\succeq\varepsilon^{-1}A_{n}\Sigma+\alpha_{\rho}\,.

Then, from the Brascamp–-Lieb inequality (see for instance
[
CP23
, Lemma 2]
), we can deduce that

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}(y)\overset{\eqref{eq:hess:hess:cov:psieot}}{=}-\Sigma+\varepsilon^{-1}\mathrm{Cov}_{X\sim\pi^{n,n}(\cdot|y)}(\Sigma X)=-\Sigma+\varepsilon^{-1}\Sigma\,\mathrm{Cov}_{X\sim\pi^{n,n}(\cdot|y)}(X)\Sigma

\displaystyle\preceq-\Sigma+\Sigma(A_{n}\Sigma+\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho})^{-1}\Sigma\,,

which proves
\nabla^{2}\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}\preceq-\Sigma+B_{n}\Sigma
. This further implies that the conditional probability measure
\pi^{n+1,n}(\cdot|x)
is log-convex uniformly in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
since

\nabla^{2}_{2}(-\log\pi^{n+1,n}(y|x))=\varepsilon^{-1}(\Sigma+\nabla^{2}\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}(y))+\nabla^{2}U_{\nu}(y)\preceq\varepsilon^{-1}\,B_{n}\Sigma+\beta_{\nu}\,.

This, combined with the Cramér–Rao inequality (see for instance
[
CP23
, Lemma 2]
) gives uniformly in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\varphi^{n+1}_{\varepsilon}(x)\overset{\eqref{eq:hess:hess:cov:phieot}}{=}-\Sigma+\varepsilon^{-1}\mathrm{Cov}_{Y\sim\pi^{n+1,n}(\cdot|x)}(\Sigma Y)=-\Sigma+\varepsilon^{-1}\,\Sigma\,\mathrm{Cov}_{Y\sim\pi^{n+1,n}(\cdot|x)}(Y)\Sigma

\displaystyle\succeq-\Sigma+(B_{n}\Sigma+\varepsilon\beta_{\nu})^{-1}\Sigma\,,

which proves
\nabla^{2}\varphi^{n+1}_{\varepsilon}\succeq-\Sigma+A_{n+1}\Sigma
. This shows the validity of (
2.12
) with the sequences defined at (
2.13
). Moreover, the same induction argument shows that whenever
\Sigma\succ 0
we are guaranteed for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
that
A_{n}\succ 0
,
B_{n}\succ 0
and both matrices commute with
\Sigma
. The rest of the proof is devoted to showing that the sequence
(A_{n},B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converge to the fixed point of (
2.13
), so that (
2.14
) follows from the validity of (
2.13
) in such limit points.
In view of that, let us preliminary notice that since
A_{0}
and
\Sigma
commute, both are jointly diagonalizable over a basis
\{v_{1},\dots,\,v_{d}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover since
\Sigma
is non-singular, notice that (
2.13
) can be rewritten as

(2.15)

B_{n}=(A_{n}+\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}\Sigma^{-1})^{-1}\,\quad\text{ and }\quad A_{n+1}=(B_{n}+\varepsilon\beta_{\nu}\Sigma^{-1})^{-1}\,.

From this we immediately deduce that with respect to the same basis
\{v_{1},\dots,\,v_{d}\}
the matrices
A_{n}
and
B_{n}
are diagonal as well for each
n\geq 0
and their eigenvalues (respectively
\{a_{n}^{1},\dots,\,a_{n}^{d}\}
and
\{b_{n}^{1},\dots,\,b_{n}^{d}\}
) satisfy

(2.16)

b_{n}^{k}=(a_{n}^{k}+\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}\Sigma_{kk}^{-1})^{-1}\,\quad\text{ and }\quad a_{n+1}^{k}=(b_{n}^{k}+\varepsilon\beta_{\nu}\Sigma_{kk}^{-1})^{-1}\,\quad\forall\,k=1,\dots,\,d\,,

where
\Sigma_{kk}>0
denotes the
k^{th}
diagonal entry of
\Sigma
over the basis
\{v_{1},\cdots,\,v_{d}\}
. For each
k=1,\dots,\,d
we will show that
(a_{n}^{k})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is and
(b_{n}^{k})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are converging to positive limits.

Firstly, assume that
a^{k}_{1}<a^{k}_{0}
. Then
(a_{n}^{k})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is monotone decreasing. Indeed, if we assume that
a_{n}^{k}>a_{n-1}^{k}
then we have

\displaystyle b_{n}^{k}-b_{n-1}^{k}=\frac{1}{a_{n}^{k}+\frac{\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}}{\Sigma^{-1}_{kk}}}-\frac{1}{a_{n-1}^{k}+\frac{\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}}{\Sigma^{-1}_{kk}}}<0\,,

which further implies that

a_{n+1}^{k}-a_{n}^{k}=\frac{1}{b_{n}^{k}+\frac{\varepsilon\beta_{\nu}}{\Sigma_{kk}}}-\frac{1}{b_{n-1}^{k}+\frac{\varepsilon\beta_{\nu}}{\Sigma_{kk}}}>0\,.

Therefore by induction we have shown that if
a^{k}_{1}<a^{k}_{0}
then
(a_{n}^{k})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is monotone decreasing. On the other hand, if
a^{k}_{1}\geq a^{k}_{0}
, then we can prove that
(a_{n}^{k})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is monotone non-decreasing and
(b_{n}^{k})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is monotone non-increasing, by the same argument. Hence either one between
(a_{n}^{k})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(b_{n}^{k})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is monotone non-increasing and lower-bounded (since for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
that
A_{n}\succ 0
,
B_{n}\succ 0
are positive definite). From this we may thus deduce the convergence of either one between
(a_{n}^{k})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(b_{n}^{k})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, which implies the convergence of the other one via (
2.16
). In conclusion we have shown that for each
k=1,\dots,\,d
the sequences
(a_{n}^{k})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(b_{n}^{k})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converge to some positive limit points.

In particular, this shows that the sequence
(B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to a positive semidefinite matrix
B_{\infty}
. Moreover, from (
2.15
) we immediately see that the limit-matrix solves

(2.17)

B_{\infty}=[(B_{\infty}+\varepsilon\beta_{\nu}\Sigma^{-1})^{-1}+\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}\Sigma^{-1}]^{-1}\,,

Observe that, since
B_{\infty}
is positive semidefinite, the right hand side in the above identity dominates a positive definite matrix, whence
B_{\infty}
is positive definite and invertible. Moreover, since
B_{\infty}
and
\Sigma
commute, we can rewrite (
2.17
) as

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\alpha_{\rho}\,B_{\infty}^{2}+\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}\beta_{\nu}B_{\infty}\Sigma^{-1}-\beta_{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{\rho}\,\biggl(B_{\infty}+\frac{\varepsilon\beta_{\nu}}{2}\Sigma^{-1}+\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\beta_{\nu}^{2}}{4}\Sigma^{-2}+\frac{\beta_{\nu}}{\alpha_{\rho}}\biggr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\biggr)\biggl(B_{\infty}+\frac{\varepsilon\beta_{\nu}}{2}\Sigma^{-1}-\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\beta_{\nu}^{2}}{4}\Sigma^{-2}+\frac{\beta_{\nu}}{\alpha_{\rho}}\biggr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\biggr)\,,

where the square root has to be understood as the square root of a positive semidefinite matrix. Since
B_{\infty}
ought to be positive definite we conclude that

B_{\infty}=-\frac{\varepsilon\beta_{\nu}}{2}\Sigma^{-1}+\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\beta_{\nu}^{2}}{4}\Sigma^{-2}+\frac{\beta_{\nu}}{\alpha_{\rho}}\biggr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\,.

The convergence of
(B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to
B_{\infty}
implies the convergence of
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
towards the limit matrix

A_{\infty}=(B_{\infty}+\varepsilon\beta_{\nu}\Sigma^{-1})^{-1}=-\frac{\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}}{2}\Sigma^{-1}+\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\alpha_{\rho}^{2}}{4}\Sigma^{-2}+\frac{\alpha_{\rho}}{\beta_{\nu}}\biggr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\,.

∎

If in the previous lemma we consider as Sinkhorn’s iterates the constant iterates
\varphi^{n}_{\varepsilon}=\varphi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon}
and
\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}=\psi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon}
(
i.e.
we start the algorithm already in the fixed point
(\varphi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon},\psi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon})
), then we immediately deduce the following.

Corollary 2.3
.

When the two marginals satisfy (
1.11
) and
\Sigma\succ 0
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\varphi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\succeq-\Sigma-\frac{\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}}{2}+\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\alpha_{\rho}^{2}}{4}+\frac{\alpha_{\rho}}{\beta_{\nu}}\,\Sigma^{2}\biggr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\,,

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\psi^{\nu}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\preceq-\Sigma-\frac{\varepsilon\beta_{\nu}}{2}+\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\beta_{\nu}^{2}}{4}+\frac{\beta_{\nu}}{\alpha_{\rho}}\,\Sigma^{2}\biggr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\,.

Let us also highlight here a regularizing property that can be immediately deduced from
Lemma

2.2
.

Corollary 2.4
.

When the two marginals satisfy (
1.11
) and
\Sigma\succ 0
, if
A_{0}\succeq A_{\infty}
(defined at (
2.14
)) then
B_{n}\preceq B_{\infty}
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Equivalently, if we start Sinkhorn’s algorithm in
\varphi^{0}_{\varepsilon}
such that

(2.18)

\nabla^{2}\varphi^{0}_{\varepsilon}\succeq-\Sigma-\frac{\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}}{2}+\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\alpha_{\rho}^{2}}{4}+\frac{\alpha_{\rho}}{\beta_{\nu}}\,\Sigma^{2}\biggr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\,,

then for any
n\in\mathbb{N}

(2.19)

\nabla^{2}\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}\preceq-\Sigma-\frac{\varepsilon\beta_{\nu}}{2}+\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\beta_{\nu}^{2}}{4}+\frac{\beta_{\nu}}{\alpha_{\rho}}\,\Sigma^{2}\biggr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\,.

Proof.

Let us start by showing that if
A_{0}\succeq A_{\infty}
, then
B_{n}\preceq B_{\infty}
. In view of this, it suffices to show by induction that
A_{n}-A_{\infty}\succeq 0
and
B_{n}-B_{\infty}\preceq 0
. The base case is the standing assumption. Next, assume
A_{n}-A_{\infty}\succeq 0
. From (
2.13
) (and by recalling that
(A_{\infty},B_{\infty})
is a fixed point solution of the latter) we immediately deduce that

B_{n}-B_{\infty}=(A_{n}+\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}\Sigma^{-1})^{-1}-(A_{\infty}+\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}\Sigma^{-1})^{-1}\preceq 0\,,

since our inductive step implies
(A_{n}+\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}\Sigma^{-1})-(A_{\infty}+\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}\Sigma^{-1})\succeq 0
. This shows
B_{n}-B_{\infty}\preceq 0
, which further implies (same reasoning from (
2.13
))
A_{n+1}-A_{\infty}\succeq 0
, which concludes our inductive proof.

Finally, if (
2.18
) holds, then we can take
A_{0}\succeq A_{\infty}
in
Lemma

2.2
, hence the above discussion yields
B_{n}\preceq B_{\infty}
which implies (
2.19
).
∎

We are now able to conclude our analysis of the semiconcavity of
y\mapsto c(x,y)+\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}(y)
.
For a generic initial condition, we know by Lemma
2.2
that
\nabla\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}\preceq-\Sigma+B_{n}\Sigma
. Therefore for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
the map

is
\left\|B_{n}\,\Sigma\right\|_{2}
-semiconcave. Since
B_{n}
converges to
B_{\infty}
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|B_{n}\,\Sigma\right\|_{2}=\left\|B_{\infty}\Sigma\right\|_{2}=\left\|-\frac{\varepsilon\beta_{\nu}}{2}+\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\beta_{\nu}^{2}}{4}+\frac{\beta_{\nu}}{\alpha_{\rho}}\Sigma^{2}\biggr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\varepsilon\beta_{\nu}}{2}+\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\beta_{\nu}^{2}}{4}+\frac{\beta_{\nu}}{\alpha_{\rho}}\left\|\Sigma\right\|_{2}^{2}\biggr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\leq\sqrt{\nicefrac{{\beta_{\nu}}}{{\alpha_{\rho}}}}\,\left\|\Sigma\right\|_{2}\,,

and therefore
Theorem

1.3
-
(i)
implies the exponential convergence of Sinkhorn’s algorithm and for
\varepsilon\leq\tau\,\sqrt{\nicefrac{{\beta_{\nu}}}{{\alpha_{\rho}}}}\,\left\|\Sigma\right\|_{2}
the asymptotic rate is

(2.20)

1-\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon+\tau\|\Sigma\|_{2}(\nicefrac{{\beta_{\nu}}}{{\alpha_{\rho}}})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\,.

Finally, if we start Sinkhorn’s algorithm with
\varphi^{0}_{\varepsilon}
satisfying (
2.18
), then
Corollary

2.4
implies the validity of (
2.19
) for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus,
y\mapsto c(x,y)+\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}(y)
is
\Lambda
-semiconcave for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
\Lambda=\left\|B_{\infty}\Sigma\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\nicefrac{{\beta_{\nu}}}{{\alpha_{\rho}}}}\|\Sigma\|_{2}
. Therefore in this case
Theorem

1.3
-
(i)
gives the exponential convergence of Sinkhorn’s algorithm with rate given by (
2.20
) if
\varepsilon\leq\tau\,\sqrt{\nicefrac{{\beta_{\nu}}}{{\alpha_{\rho}}}}\,\left\|\Sigma\right\|_{2}
. In fact, we have actually proven that the exponential convergence with rate (

) holds under the weaker assumption
\nabla^{2}\varphi^{\varepsilon}_{0}\succeq-\Sigma-\varepsilon\frac{\alpha_{\rho}}{2}+\Big(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\alpha^{2}_{\rho}}{4}+\frac{\alpha_{\rho}}{\beta_{\nu}}\Sigma^{2}\Big)^{1/2}
but we have preferred to keep the stronger condition in the statement of
Proposition

1.5
-
(i)
for better readability.

2.3.3.
Marginal
\rho
weakly log-concave

Here we restrict to the quadratic cost setting (
i.e.
,
\Sigma=\Id
) and relax the convexity assumption of
U_{\rho}
by simply requiring
\rho
to be weakly log-concave (cf. (
1.14
)), that is, we assume

(2.21)

\langle\nabla U_{\rho}(\hat{x})-\nabla U_{\rho}(x),\hat{x}-x\rangle\geq\alpha_{\rho}|\hat{x}-x|^{2}-|\hat{x}-x|f_{L}(|\hat{x}-x|)\,,\quad\forall x,\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where for any
L\geq 0
,
r>0
,
f_{L}(r)=2L^{1/2}\tanh\big((L^{1/2}r)/2\big)
. We now reformulate this condition introducing the convexity profile (this is a classical notion in the coupling literature, see
[
Ebe16
]
for example) of a given
U
as the function
\kappa_{U}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by

(2.22)

\kappa_{U}(r)\coloneqq\inf\biggl\{\frac{\langle\nabla U(\hat{x})-\nabla U(x),\,\hat{x}-x\rangle}{|x-\hat{x}|^{2}}\quad\colon\,|x-\hat{x}|=r\biggr\}\,.

For ease of notation we also set
\kappa_{\rho}\coloneqq\kappa_{U_{\rho}}
. With this notation at hand, (
2.21
) rewrites as

(2.23)

\kappa_{\rho}(r)\geq\alpha_{\rho}-r^{-1}f_{L}(r)\quad\forall r>0\,.

It follows from (
2.3
) that, since
\nabla^{2}_{1}c=\Id
,

(2.24)

\kappa_{(\varepsilon^{-1}\varphi^{n}_{\varepsilon}+U_{\rho})}(r)\geq\alpha_{\rho}-\varepsilon^{-1}-r^{-1}f_{L}(r)\,\quad\forall r>0,n\in\mathbb{N}\,.

At this point, we can invoke
[
Con24
, Lemma 3.1]
(see
Appendix

C
for a statement of this result using the current notation) to conclude that

\kappa_{\Phi^{\rho}_{0}(\varphi^{n}_{\varepsilon})}(r)\geq\frac{\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}-1}{\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}}-\frac{L}{\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}^{2}}\,,

which gives

\nabla^{2}\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}=-\nabla^{2}\Phi^{\rho}_{0}(\varphi^{n}_{\varepsilon})\preceq-\frac{\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}-1}{\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}}+\frac{L}{\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}^{2}}=-1+\frac{1}{\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}}+\frac{L}{\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}^{2}}\,.

From this we immediately conclude that uniformly in
x\in\supp(\rho)
, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the map
y\mapsto c(x,y)+\psi^{n}_{\varepsilon}(y)
is
\Lambda
-semiconcave
with
\Lambda=(\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho})^{-1}+\nicefrac{{L}}{{\varepsilon\alpha_{\rho}^{2}}}
. We can thus invoke
Theorem

1.3
-
(i)
which gives exponential convergence of Sinkhorn’s algorithm. Moreover, if
\varepsilon\leq\alpha_{\rho}^{-1}\sqrt{\tau(\alpha_{\rho}+L)}
, then the convergence rate is

1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\alpha_{\rho}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}\alpha_{\rho}^{2}+\tau(\alpha_{\rho}+L)}\,.

\square

2.4.
Proof of Proposition
1.7

Assume that the cost satisfies

\nabla_{1}^{2}c(x,y)\,,\nabla^{2}_{2}c(x,y)\succeq h(c)\quad\text{ and }\quad\left\|\nabla_{12}^{2}c(x,y)\right\|_{2}\vee\left\|\nabla_{1}^{2}c(x,y)\right\|_{2}\leq H(c)\,,

uniformly in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. Moreover assume that
\rho
has light tails in the following sense

\nabla^{2}U_{\rho}(x)\succeq\begin{cases}C\,|x|^{\delta}\quad&\text{ for }|x|>R\,,\\
-L\quad&\text{ for }|x|\leq R\,,\end{cases}

for some positive
\delta,\,C>0
and
L,\,R\geq 0
. To estimate the conditional covariances, we shall use the following abstract result.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\pi=e^{-U}
,
\alpha>0,L,R\geq 0
be such that

(2.25)

\nabla^{2}U(x)\succeq\begin{cases}-L,\quad&{|x|<R}\,,\\
\alpha,\quad&|x|\geq R\,.\end{cases}

Moreover, let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be Lipschitz . Then we have

\mathrm{Cov}_{X\sim\pi}(f(X))\preceq 2\mathrm{Lip}(f)^{2}\big(\alpha^{-1}+\alpha^{-2}(L+\alpha)^{2}R^{2}\big)\,.

Proof.

We define the function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

given by

\phi(x)=\frac{L+\alpha}{2}|x|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{|x|<R\}}+\Big((L+\alpha)R(|x|-R)+\frac{(L+\alpha)R^{2}}{2}\Big)\mathbf{1}_{\{|x|\geq R\}}\,.

Then, the function
\phi
is convex and
(L+\alpha)R
-Lipschitz. Moreover,
\nabla^{2}\phi(x)\succeq(L+\alpha)
for
|x|<R
. But then, we deduce from (
2.25
) that
\bar{\pi}(\mathrm{d}x)\propto\exp(-U(x)-\phi(x))\mathrm{d}x
is
\alpha
-log-concave and in particular satisfies
\mathrm{PI}(\alpha^{-1})
. Now, let
(X,\bar{X})
be a coupling between
\pi
and
\bar{\pi}
. Then, following the argument given in
[
BP24
, Lemma 2.1]
, we find that for all
v
such that
|v|=1

(2.26)

\displaystyle\langle v,\,\mathrm{Cov}_{X\sim\pi}(f(X))v\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}_{X\sim\pi}(\langle f(X),v\rangle)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathrm{Var}_{\bar{X}\sim\bar{\pi}}(\langle f(\bar{X}),v\rangle)+2\mathbb{E}[|\langle f(X),v\rangle-\langle f(\bar{X}),v\rangle|^{2}]\,.

Using that the function
x\mapsto\langle f(x),v\rangle
is
\mathrm{Lip}(f)
-Lipschitz, we can use that
\bar{\pi}
satisfies
\mathrm{PI}(\alpha^{-1})
to obtain

\mathrm{Var}_{\bar{X}\sim\bar{\pi}}(\langle f(\bar{X}),v\rangle)\leq\mathrm{Lip}(f)^{2}\alpha^{-1}\,.

Moreover, if
(X,\bar{X})
is optimal for
\mathbf{W}_{2}(\pi,\bar{\pi})
we obtain

\mathbb{E}[|\langle f(X),v\rangle-\langle f(\bar{X}),v\rangle|^{2}]\leq\mathrm{Lip}(f)^{2}\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{d}}(X,\bar{X})^{2}]=\mathrm{Lip}(f)^{2}\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}(\pi,\hat{\pi})\,.

Plugging the last two bounds back into (
2.26
) and invoking first TI(
\alpha^{-1}
) then LSI(
\alpha^{-1}
) for
\bar{\pi}
we obtain

\langle v,\,\mathrm{Cov}_{f(X)\sim\pi}(f(X)),v\rangle\leq 2\mathrm{Lip}(f)^{2}\big(\alpha^{-1}+\alpha^{-2}\mathrm{Lip}(\phi)^{2}\big)\,.

Since
\mathrm{Lip}(\phi)\leq(L+\alpha)R
, the conclusion follows.
∎

With this result at hand, we obtain the following.

Lemma 2.6
.

Assume that
c
satisfies (
1.17
) and that
\rho
satisfies (
1.16
). Then for any
\alpha>0
we have

(2.27)

\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left\|\mathrm{Cov}_{X\sim\pi^{n,n}(\cdot|y)}(\nabla_{2}c(X,y))\right\|_{2}\leq 2H(c)^{2}\,\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}+\biggl(\frac{L\varepsilon}{\alpha}+\frac{\alpha+\delta_{H}}{\alpha}\biggr)^{2}\,\biggl[R^{2}\vee\biggl(\frac{\alpha+\delta_{H}}{\varepsilon\,C}\biggr)^{\frac{2}{\delta}}\biggr]\biggr)\,,

where we have set

. As a consequence of this we have

\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left\|\mathrm{Cov}_{X\sim\pi^{n,n}(\cdot|y)}(\nabla_{2}c(X,y))\right\|_{2}\leq 2H(c)^{2}\,\biggl(1+(L+2)^{2}\biggl[R^{2}\vee{C^{-\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}}}\biggl(1+2\frac{\delta_{H}}{\varepsilon}\biggr)^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}}\biggr]\biggr)\,.

Proof.

Notice that the upper bound on the Hessian of the cost guarantees the semiconvexity of
\varphi^{n}_{\varepsilon}
, since we recall from (

) that

\nabla^{2}\varphi^{n}_{\varepsilon}(x)=-\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\nabla_{1}^{2}c(x,y)\,\pi^{n,n-1}(\mathrm{d}y|x)+\varepsilon^{-1}\,\mathrm{Cov}_{Y\sim\pi^{n,n-1}(\cdot|x)}(\nabla_{1}c(x,Y))\succeq-H(c)\,.

By using this estimate we deduce that uniformly in

\nabla^{2}_{1}(-\log\pi^{n,n}(x|y))\succeq\begin{cases}C\,|x|^{\delta}-\frac{\delta_{H}}{\varepsilon}\quad&\text{ for }|x|>R\,,\\
-L-\frac{\delta_{H}}{\varepsilon}\quad&\text{ for }|x|\leq R\,.\end{cases}

we find

\nabla^{2}_{1}(-\log\pi^{n,n}(x|y))\succeq\begin{cases}\frac{\alpha}{\varepsilon}\quad&\text{ for }|x|>R(\alpha,\varepsilon)\,,\\

\mathrm{Cov}_{X\sim\pi^{n,n}(\cdot|y)}(\nabla_{2}c(X,y))\preceq 2H(c)^{2}\,\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}+\biggl(\frac{L\varepsilon+\delta_{H}}{\alpha}+1\biggr)^{2}\,\biggl[R^{2}\vee\biggl(\frac{\alpha+\delta_{H}}{\varepsilon\,C}\biggr)^{\frac{2}{\delta}}\biggr]\biggr)\,.

We have thus shown the first bound. The second one can be obtained by considering the specific choice

∎

Assume that
c
satisfies (

) and that

Otherwise, if

(and set

From

This proves that

is
\Lambda
-semiconcave uniformly in

and in

Let us briefly discuss how the gradient and the Hessian of any function

given by
\gamma_{t}=\exp_{y}(tv)
, explicitly given by

\gamma_{t}=\exp_{y}(tv)=\cos(t\|v\|)\,y+\sin(t\|v\|)\frac{v}{\|v\|}\,.

To determine

{\mathbb{S}^{d}}

{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d+1}

.

\displaystyle\max\biggl\{\mathrm{KL}(\nu|\nu^{N,N-1})^{1-\gamma},\,\frac{\tau\Lambda}{2\varepsilon}(1+\mathrm{KL}(\nu^{N,N-1}|\nu)^{1-\gamma})\biggr\}(\mathrm{KL}(\rho|\rho^{n,n})^{\gamma}+\mathrm{KL}(\nu|\nu^{n,n-1})^{\gamma})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M(\mathrm{KL}(\rho|\rho^{n,n})+\mathrm{KL}(\nu|\nu^{n,n-1}))^{\gamma}\,,

where we have set
M\coloneqq 2^{1-\gamma}\max\biggl\{\mathrm{KL}(\nu|\nu^{N,N-1})^{1-\gamma},\,\frac{\tau\Lambda}{2\varepsilon}(1+\mathrm{KL}(\nu^{N,N-1}|\nu)^{1-\gamma})\biggr\}
for notation’s sake.
Using this result in (
2.7
) gives

(B.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}|\pi^{n+1,n})-\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}|\pi^{n,n-1})=-(\mathrm{KL}(\rho|\rho^{n,n})+\mathrm{KL}(\nu|\nu^{n,n-1}))\leq-\frac{\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}|\pi^{n+1,n})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\gamma}}}}{M^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\gamma}}}}\,.

This simple recursion in particular implies (
Lemma

B.2
below) that for all
n\geq N

\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}|\pi^{n+1,n})\leq(n-N+1)^{-\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}}\biggl(\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma M^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}+\frac{1}{(n-N+1)\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}|\pi^{N,N-1})^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}}\biggr)^{-\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}}\,.

∎

Below we state and prove the technical lemma used in order to conclude the proof of
Theorem

B.1
(there we set
\alpha=\gamma^{-1}>1
,
a_{n}=\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\rho\nu}|\pi^{n+1,n})
and
C=M^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}
).

Lemma B.2
.

Let
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of non-negative reals and suppose for any
n\geq N
it satisfies

a_{n}-a_{n-1}\leq-\frac{a_{n}^{\alpha}}{C}\,,

for some positive constant
C>0
and some
\alpha>1
. Then for any
n\geq N
we have

a_{n}\leq(n-N)^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\,\biggl(\frac{\alpha-1}{C}+\frac{1}{(n-N)a_{N}^{\alpha-1}}\biggr)^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\,.

Proof.

Firstly, notice that the sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq N}
is non-increasing since we have
a_{n-1}\geq a_{n}+C^{-1}a_{n}^{\alpha}\geq a_{n}
and therefore the limit exists (since it is non-negative). Next, introduce the convex function
f(x)=x^{-(\alpha-1)}
and let
b_{n}\coloneqq f(a_{n})
. From the convexity of
f
we then have for any
n\geq N

b_{n}-b_{n-1}=f(a_{n})-f(a_{n-1})\geq(\alpha-1)a_{n-1}^{-\alpha}(a_{n-1}-a_{n})\geq\frac{\alpha-1}{C}\,.

From this we see that for any
n\geq N

b_{n}=b_{N}+\sum_{k=N}^{n-1}b_{k+1}-b_{k}\geq a_{N}^{-(\alpha-1)}+(n-N)\frac{\alpha-1}{C}\,,

and hence that

a_{n}=b_{n}^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\leq\biggl((n-N)\frac{\alpha-1}{C}+a_{N}^{-(\alpha-1)}\biggr)^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\,,

from which our thesis follows.
∎

Appendix D
Proof of Lemma

As a starting point, recall Hamilton’s gradient estimate
[
Ham93
]
: this ensures that if
u
is a positive solution to the heat equation
\partial_{t}u=\Delta u
with
u(0,\cdot)\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(M)
and
{\textrm{Ric}}_{M}\succeq\kappa\mathfrak{g}
globally on
M
for some
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}

(D.1)

t|\nabla_{M}\log u(t,\cdot)|^{2}\leq(1+2\kappa^{-}t)\log\left(\frac{\|u(0,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}}{u(t,\cdot)}\right),\qquad\forall t>0\,.

The desired bound (
2.38
) will follow by applying the inequality above to
u(s,y)=\mathrm{p}_{t+s}(x,y)
, where
t>0
and
x\in M
are fixed. To this end, we need to verify that
u(0,\cdot)=\mathrm{p}_{t}(x,\cdot)\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(M)
, since by the compactness of
M
we already know that
{\textrm{Ric}}\geq\kappa\Id
for some
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}

2.41

\begin{split}\|\mathrm{p}_{t}(x,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}&=\sup_{y\in M}\mathrm{p}_{t}(x,y)\leq\frac{C_{2}}{{\textrm{vol}}(B_{\sqrt{t}}(x))}\,,\\
\inf_{x}\mathrm{p}_{2t}(x,y)&\geq\frac{C_{0}e^{-C_{1}\frac{{\textrm{diam}}(M)^{2}}{t}}}{{\textrm{vol}}(B_{\sqrt{2t}}(x))}>0\,.\end{split}

M
(see for instance
[
Pet06
]

C_{3}>0

(D.2)

{\textrm{vol}}(B_{\sqrt{2t}}(x))\leq C_{3}{\textrm{vol}}(B_{\sqrt{t}}(x))\,,\qquad\forall x\in M,\,\forall t>0\,,
