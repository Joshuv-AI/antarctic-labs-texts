Identification of dysregulated gene clusters and pathways driving ocular surface squamous neoplasia progression
Authors: Kartik Goel, Shruti Rathore, Prisha Warikoo, Mehak Sapra, Shirali Gokharu, Rajnish Kumar, Dheerendra Kumar, Arpan Gandhi, Virender Singh Sangwan, Sima Das, Anil Tiwari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ocular Surface Squamous Neoplasia (OSSN) represents a spectrum of ocular malignancies that threaten vision and ocular integrity. To unravel the molecular mechanisms underlying OSSN progression, we conducted RNA-sequencing on conjunctival tissues from healthy individuals and patients with OSSN. Our analysis revealed marked alterations in the expression of genes implicated in inflammation, immune dysregulation, cell cycle regulation, and cellular stress responses. Notably, genes such as TP53, CXCL9, CXCL11, IL6, TNFα, MMP7, MMP9, GSTM1, IFNα, and IL1β showed significant dysregulation in OSSN samples compared to controls. Pathway enrichment analysis highlighted the activation of Interferon-α, Interferon-γ, and IL6/JAK-STAT3 signaling, alongside pathways regulating inflammatory response, p53 signaling, G2M checkpoint, and apical surface integrity. Together, these findings indicate that OSSN is characterized by a pro-inflammatory and proliferative transcriptomic profile driven by chronic immune signaling and disrupted cell cycle control. These findings provide novel insights into the transcriptional landscape of OSSN and identify key pathways that may be targeted for improved diagnosis and therapy. The molecular insights provided by this study can potentially inform stratified management approaches and aid in the development of novel treatments for this challenging ocular surface malignancy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29063-6.

Introduction

Ocular Surface Squamous Neoplasia (OSSN), one of the most common ocular surface malignancies, encompasses a spectrum of diseases characterized by the abnormal growth of dysplastic squamous epithelial cells in the cornea, limbus, and conjunctiva1,2. Epithelial homeostasis in the ocular surface relies on a balance between basal cell proliferation and supra-basal cell differentiation, and its disruption leads to OSSN3,4. The disease progresses from benign lesions like papilloma and pseudotheliomatous hyperplasia to pre-invasive stages such as conjunctival/corneal intraepithelial neoplasia and, ultimately, to invasive Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC), which can metastasize to the lymph nodes, lungs, bones, and parotid glands, necessitating aggressive treatment2,5,6. The global incidence of OSSN varies based on geography, environmental exposure, and population characteristics, with the highest prevalence in equatorial regions like sub-Saharan Africa, where UV radiation is a key etiological factor7,8. In India, OSSN accounts for 0.02%–0.1% of ophthalmic malignancies9. Other risk factors include ocular surface injury, smoking, genetic predisposition, and immunosuppression10–13.

Recent studies have identified key genetic and epigenetic factors in OSSN, particularly in genes like TP53, MSH6, and ATM among others, which disrupt cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, and genomic stability, enabling uncontrolled proliferation of the tumor cells14,15. This genomic instability could act as a hallmark of cancer progression, enabling pre-invasive lesions to transition to invasive stages16,17. Alongside genetic alterations, dysregulation of local immune responses plays a critical role, as chronic inflammation, creates a tumor-promoting microenvironment by enhancing proliferation, inhibiting apoptosis, and facilitating immune evasion8,14,18–20.

While previous studies have identified molecular factors associated with OSSN, the specific pathways governing its initiation and progression remain incompletely mapped19,21–25. Despite advances in molecular research, a detailed transcriptomic expression profile of OSSN is yet to be established. The molecular signatures driving the disease remain elusive, emphasizing the need for further investigation to identify specific biomarkers or gene expression patterns that could reliably classify OSSN severity and aid in early diagnosis and targeted treatment26–33.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to identify the clusters of genes and their associated pathways involved in OSSN as well as to investigate the transcriptomic shifts occurring from healthy to diseased states and correlate these molecular signatures with the clinical stages of disease severity.

The current study explores the transcriptomic landscape of OSSN tumor tissue and investigates some of the highly dysregulated genes associated with integral biological functions and pathways including Interferon alpha(IFN-α), Interferon Gamma(IFN-γ) pathways, Interlukin-6 (IL6), JAK-STAT3 signaling, hallmark apical surface, p35 pathway, and G2M Checkpoint pathways. These findings underscore the complex interplay between molecular alterations and pathological features in OSSN, highlighting the importance of cell cycle and immune dysregulation, loss of epithelial polarity, and chronic inflammation in disease pathology. By unraveling the dysregulated genes and their associated pathways, this study provides critical insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying OSSN pathogenesis. Furthermore, these results pave the way for identifying potential biomarkers for early diagnosis, disease stratification, and therapeutic targeting of OSSN.

Results

Clinical demographics of the patients

Among the 12 patients analyzed, 67% (8/12) were classified as invasive OSSN, while 33% (4/12) were non-invasive. Systemic diseases were present in 75% of invasive cases compared to 50% (2/4) of non-invasive cases, with invasive OSSN demonstrating diabetes (25%) and hypertension (25%). In contrast, non-invasive OSSN was primarily associated with diabetes (50%) and coronary artery disease (25%). AS-OCT imaging showed that invasive cases exhibited stromal invasion, disrupted basement membrane, and heterogeneous stromal reflectivity, while non-invasive cases showed sharply demarcated epithelial hyperreflectivity and intact basement layer (Fig. 1). Histopathology confirmed these findings, with all non-invasive cases displaying epithelial dysplasia confined above an intact basement membrane, whereas of invasive cases demonstrated basement membrane breaches and stromal infiltration (Fig. 1). The combination of AS-OCT and histopathology proved highly effective in distinguishing invasive from non-invasive disease, supporting the need for comprehensive evaluations in managing OSSN. Using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) we identified several enriched pathways in OSSN samples including IFNα and IFNƴ signaling, IL6 JAK STAT3 signaling, p53 pathway, and inflammatory response pathways among others (Fig. 3C).

The comprehensive GSEA analysis identified key pathways involved in the pathogenesis of OSSN, including those related to cell cycle regulation, ECM remodeling, inflammation, and cancer-associated signaling pinpointing specific genes and molecular interactions that are central to its tumorigenesis.

These enriched pathways were associated with cell cycle regulation, cell division, cell proliferation, cell signaling, immune cell activation, epithelial remodeling, and inflammation.

Gene validation using qPCR

To validate these pathways, we compared key gene expressions between non-invasive and invasive OSSN. While some comparisons lacked statistical significance due to sample size, consistent trends supported their biological relevance in tumors.

The expression of TP53 was found to be significantly upregulated (p value = 0.03) in OSSN tumors in comparison to the healthy conjunctiva (Fig. 5). This upregulation could indicate an active response to cellular stress or early tumorigenic signals. Similarly, DNA repair genes like GSTM1 (p value = 0.01) and ATM (p value = 0.009) were found to be significantly upregulated in OSSN tumors compared to the controls. (Fig. 5). Downregulated genes included KRT4 (p value = 0.03) and SLC6A14 (p value = 0.01) that were found to have a reduced expression in comparison to healthy controls (Fig. 5) which may signify a loss of normal epithelial function and integrity, reduced control over extracellular remodeling, and changes in cellular homeostasis (Fig. 5).

To highlight the role of immune and inflammatory responses in OSSN pathogenesis, we checked the expression of significant chemokines – CXCL9 and CXCL11, and pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL6, IFNα, and IL1β. The upregulation of CXCL9 (p value = 0.0002) and CXCL11 (p value = 0.0121) in OSSN samples in comparison to control, suggests an active immune response targeting the tumor cells (Fig. 5). IL6, a key pro-inflammatory cytokine, was significantly elevated in OSSN (p value = 0.02), suggesting its role in a pro-tumorigenic environment (Fig. 5). Similarly, IFNα (p value = 0.009) and IL1β (p value = 0.009) were both found to be significantly overexpressed in OSSN tumors compared to the healthy conjunctival tissues. (Fig. 5). These markers collectively suggest a dynamic tumor-immune interaction in OSSN.

With respect to the previously discussed data and GSEA, we analyzed the expression of genes associated with cellular stress and the subsequent degradation of the ECM (Fig. 5). To evaluate the impact of oxidative stress caused by cellular stress in OSSN tumors, we observed that S100A11 (p value = 0.009) gene expression was significantly elevated in OSSN compared to control conjunctiva (Fig. 5). Similarly, MYH9, which is an important marker of cytoskeletal organization and cellular stress had higher expression (p value = 0.009) in OSSN compared to healthy conjunctiva (Fig. 5). Aquaporin 3 (AQP3) on the other hand, which helps regulate cellular antioxidant defenses during oxidative stress was found to be significantly downregulated (p value = 0.01) in OSSN compared to healthy conjunctiva (Fig. 5).

PRDX1 and PRDX2 are two important antioxidant enzymes that neutralize ROS, protecting cells from oxidative stress. Expression levels of both the genes were found to be upregulated (PRDX1 p value = 0.03, PRDX4 p value = 0.009) compared to controls, which aligns with the oxidative stress present in the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 5). In addition, oxidative stress and hypoxic conditions can result in the activation of Matrix Metalloproteinases (MMPs), which serve as proteolytic enzymes that degrade ECM components, facilitating tissue remodeling and clearing pathways for tumor cell invasion34. Higher expression levels of MMP2 (p value = 0.02) and MMP9 (p value = 0.009) were detected in OSSN tumors than healthy conjunctiva, highlighting ECM degradation (Fig. 5). Similarly, the expression of Tenascin C (TNC) (p value = 0.0004) and Collagen (Col1A1) (p value = 0.01) increased gradually in OSSN compared to the healthy controls, which presented lower expression (Fig. 5). Although not all pairwise comparisons were statistically significant, the expression trends were consistent and support the role of oxidative stress and ECM changes in OSSN. These results collectively suggest that OSSN tumors exhibit elevated cellular stress and a loss of epithelial polarity, reflecting disrupted homeostatic and structural integrity of the ocular surface epithelium.

Discussion

OSSN is a complex ocular surface malignancy with diverse histopathological and clinical manifestations, ranging from benign dysplasia to invasive carcinoma8,10,15. Despite its rising incidence, its molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood8. In this study, we employed RNA-sequencing to investigate the gene expression landscape of OSSN, uncovering key dysregulated genes and pathways associated with cell cycle dysregulation, cell signaling, inflammation, and apoptosis.

Pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified apoptosis, cell cycle regulation, interferon-gamma signaling, inflammation, and ECM degradation, as key enriched pathways. The upregulation of cell cycle and DNA damage repair pathways suggests an active cellular response to tumor proliferation, balancing genomic stability with rapid growth. These findings highlight the involvement of key cancer-associated pathways, particularly those regulating cell cycle repair and apoptosis, suggesting that the tumor cells are experiencing cellular stress as they attempt to control growth and survival. This enrichment aligns with the understanding that cancer-associated DNA damage triggers cellular mechanisms for DNA repair and replication. ECM remodeling emerged as a crucial factor in OSSN, with altered ECM dynamics facilitating invasion, migration, and proliferation. Additionally, interferon-gamma signaling and immune-mediated cell death pathways underscore the inflammatory response in OSSN, which may influence tumor immune escape or progression21,27,35.

TP53, the “guardian of the genome,” was significantly upregulated in OSSN suggesting a weakening of its tumor suppressor functions, allowing uncontrolled proliferation and thereby progression14. Similarly, upregulation of GSTM1 and ATM in OSSN indicates an adaptive response to cellular stress and DNA damage, enhancing antioxidant defenses for tumor survival8.

KRT4 downregulation in OSSN, suggests a loss of epithelial integrity and a shift toward a more aggressive, undifferentiated state. The progressive loss of SLC6A14 highlights an altered ion homeostasis, contributing to dysregulated tumor metabolism. CXCL9 and CXCL11, IFN-γ induced chemokines, were elevated in OSSN, indicating increased immune cell recruitment in response to tumor progression. Overexpression of IL6 in OSSN suggests a strong inflammatory response, while IFNα upregulation indicates an anti-tumor immune response modulated by disease stage24,30. IL1β was highly expressed in these tumors, potentially initiating an early immune response, which suggests tumor adaptation to evade immune signals (Fig. 636.

ROS generation and hypoxia-driven signaling activate ECM-degrading enzymes, weakening tissue barriers and promoting invasion37. S100A1, involved in calcium signaling, was elevated in OSSN, suggesting a role in oxidative stress adaptation. Similarly, MYH9, crucial for cytoskeletal organization and cell motility, was upregulated in OSSN, supporting migration through the altered ECM in response to hypoxic stress. AQP3, a water and glycerol channel, was downregulated in OSSN, implying a reduced ability to manage oxidative stress via hydration and ROS neutralization Fig. 6.

While our study provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying OSSN pathophysiology, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size poses a challenge to the generalizability of our findings. OSSN is a comparatively rare ocular malignancy, and in many regions of India, low awareness and limited access to healthcare facilities further reduce the number of diagnosed and reported cases. Secondly, our analysis does not account for the potential impact of other contributing factors, such as genetic predisposition, co-existing infections, or environmental influences, that might influence the presentation and progression of OSSN. Hence, our future studies aim to include larger, more diverse cohorts to strengthen statistical power and improve the generalizability of results. Moreover, targeting additional molecular markers beyond those tested, particularly those upstream or downstream in the identified pathways, will provide a deeper understanding of OSSN progression. Functional experiments, such as knockdown or overexpression studies, and protein validation would be crucial to validate the roles of key genes identified. Additionally, integrating multi-omics approaches, including epigenomics and proteomics, could provide a more holistic understanding of OSSN biology. Longitudinal studies tracking molecular changes during disease progression and treatment response would also be valuable for identifying dynamic biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets. Finally, community-based awareness programs could help improve early detection and reporting of OSSN, ultimately enhancing research opportunities and patient outcomes.

Limitations and future direction

OSSN is a rare disease which our study included only a small number of samples. We could not fully link the gene expression findings to clinical and pathological features because of the limited sample size. In addition, we have not yet confirmed these gene changes at the protein level or tested their function in the lab. In the future, we plan to include more samples, perform protein-level and functional studies, and identify important genes involved in OSSN development and progression. These steps will help in finding better ways to diagnose and treat this rare disease.

Conclusion

Our study highlights the intricate molecular landscape of OSSN, revealing key dysregulated genes and pathways that contribute to its progression. By analyzing the gene expression profiles of OSSN tumor tissues in comparison with those of healthy conjunctiva, we identified critical molecular alterations linked to cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, cell signaling, inflammation, and ECM remodeling. The differential expression patterns observed in genes such as TP53, GSTM1, CXCL9, IL6, TNFα, MMPs, and PRDX1/2 emphasize the interplay of tumor-promoting factors and microenvironmental changes that drive this disease. Despite the limitations of sample size, these findings represent a significant step toward understanding the molecular underpinnings of OSSN. This knowledge lays the groundwork for future studies aimed at developing targeted diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic strategies, ultimately improving patient outcomes in this rare but impactful ocular malignancy.

Materials and methods

Study cohort and ethics approval

The study cohort comprised patients diagnosed with ocular surface squamous neoplasia (OSSN) based on clinical presentation and histopathological evaluation (H&E grading). Patients were recruited from the Ocular Oncology Centre at Dr. Shroff’s Charity Eye Hospital, a tertiary care facility in North India. Tumor tissue samples were obtained via incisional or excisional biopsies. Additionally, five age- and gender-matched healthy conjunctival tissues were collected from patients undergoing cataract surgery, serving as controls. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection, including consent for the publication of any identifying information, images, or clinical details. The study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Dr. Shroff’s Charity Eye Hospital, New Delhi. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations as set forth by the Declaration of Helsinki and the policies outlined by the Institutional Review Board. Demographic and clinical data—including age at presentation, gender, and ocular and systemic history—were recorded at the time of enrollment. (Supplementary Table 1).

Clinical presentation and histological grading

Patients with suspected OSSN were classified as either non-invasive or invasive based on clinical presentation and AS-OCT and ultrasound bio microscopy (UBM) imaging and histopathological findings. Non-invasive cases exhibited lesions confined to the epithelium, whereas invasive cases showed evidence of stromal invasion This classification provided a comprehensive framework for correlating clinical and pathological features of OSSN.

RNA isolation

Tumor tissues from OSSN patients and healthy conjunctival tissues were collected and preserved in Invitrogen™ RNAlater™ Stabilization Solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA no. AM7024). The preserved tissues were manually ground using a mortar and pestle for effective tissue lysis. The lysed samples were then processed with TRIzol™ Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA Catalogue No. 15596026) for RNA extraction, following the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration and quality of the extracted RNA (A260/280 ratio) were measured using a Thermo Scientific™ NanoDrop™ Lite Spectrophotometer (catalog No. NDNDLUSCAN) to ensure suitability for downstream applications.

Transcriptomic profiling

RNA was extracted from OSSN samples (n = 3) and controls (n = 3) for sequencing. Libraries were prepared, and sequencing was performed on NovaSeq 6000, generating 60 million reads per sample. The FastQC (version 0.12.1) and Cutadapt (version 4.6) were used for quality check and trimming filtering out adapter sequences. The reads were then aligned to the human reference genome GRCh37 using the HISAT2. The StringTie (version 2.2.1) was used for assembling mapped reads into transcripts. StringTie merge was run with all assembled transcript files (in GTF/GFF format), reference annotation file (gencode.v32.annotation.gtf). Differential gene expression analysis between OSSN and control samples was carried out using DESeq2 (version 1.34.0)38. Finally, differentially expressed genes were subjected to downstream functional analysis to explore their biological roles in OSSN.

Pathway enrichment analysis

Based on the differentially expressed features identified by DESeq238, the DEGs were subjected to databases including Reactome (https://reactome.org/), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, https://www.genome.jp/kegg/), and Gene Ontology (GO, https://geneontology.org/) to identify the top biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions associated with OSSN. Further, normalized enrichment scores were obtained using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) to identify specific biological pathways associated with OSSN.

Gene validation using qRT-PCR

To validate the expression levels of selected differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified through RNA sequencing, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis was performed. Total RNA was extracted and quantified using the previously described protocol from 9 confirmed cases of OSSN and 4 healthy controls. After extraction, cDNA was synthesized using the Thermo Scientific™ RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA catalog no. K1622) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Genes selected for qRT-PCR validation were chosen based on their involvement in the most upregulated pathways identified in our transcriptomic analysis, relevance in existing literature, and known roles in cancer-related pathways.

Gene-specific primers were designed for the selected target genes, including KRT4, GSTM1, TP53,SLC6A14, ATM, COL1A1, AQP3, S100A11, CXCL9, CXCL11, IL6, IFNα, IL1β, MYH9, PRDX1, PRDX4, MMP2, MMP9, TNC, and CoL1A1, and qRT‒PCR was conducted using PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix for qPCR (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA catalog no. A25743) on the Azure Cielo Real-Time PCR System (Azure Cielo 6, Azure Biosystems, Dublin, CA, USA). Each reaction was performed in duplicate to ensure reproducibility, with cycle settings of 95 °C for 10 s followed by 60 °C for 20 s; this process was repeated for 40 cycles.

The expression levels of the target genes were normalized to those of the housekeeping gene EFE1A1 to control for variations in RNA input and cDNA synthesis efficiency. Relative gene expression levels were calculated via 2^(-ΔΔCt) method, and fold-change values were compared with RNA-Seq data to assess concordance between the two methods.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of the qRT‒PCR gene expression data was performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.0. To assess the significance of differences in gene expression among the three groups, we applied a non-parametric test using the Kruskal-Wallis test. This approach was chosen due to its robustness in handling non-normally distributed data. The test allowed for comparisons across multiple groups, providing insights into statistically significant variations in gene expression levels. The results with p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
