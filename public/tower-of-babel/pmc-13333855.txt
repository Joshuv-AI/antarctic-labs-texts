Sustainable biosynthesis of NiO-carbon nanocomposites with enhanced charge dynamics for high-performance electrochemical pesticide detection in agricultural applications and photocatalytic remediation
Authors: Syed Khasim, M. Rashad, Taymour A. Hamdalla, Chellasamy Panneerselvam, Shams A. M. Issa, Syed G. Dastager, Humaira Parveen, Meshari M. Aljohani, Zia Ul Haq Khan, Basavaraj Angadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bio-derived NiO-Carbon nanocomposites were prepared using Neem (Azadirachta indica) leaf extract through a green combustion approach. Structural analysis confirmed the formation of face-centered cubic NiO with crystallite sizes in the 25–50 nm range. The optimized 1:1 NiO: C composite demonstrated a limit of detection calculated via the 3σ/slope method in the millimolar range, along with a linear sensing response between 1 and 6 mM for glyphosate. In addition, the material achieved 98.78% degradation of Congo Red (CR dye) under UV irradiation (365 nm, 15 mW/cm²) while maintaining strong reusability and stability. The improved performance is attributed to the presence of conductive carbon, which facilitates charge transport, reduces electron–hole recombination, and increases the availability of active surface sites. Electrochemical impedance analysis further confirmed reduced charge-transfer resistance in the optimized composite, supporting its enhanced interfacial kinetics. The scalable and cost-effective synthesis, combined with stability in acidic media, highlights the potential of this composite as a multifunctional platform for pesticide monitoring and wastewater remediation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51109-6.

Introduction

The rapid urbanization and population growth has led to increased demand for the food production from agricultural sector. To achieve high productivity and to produce economically profitable crops extensive use of pesticides and agrochemicals in higher concentrations has become noticeably increasing. The extensive use of these chemically hazardous pesticides in agricultural sector has significant impact on the human health and environment1. Over 3.3 million tons of pesticides are used globally each year (2023)1. The excessive usage of pesticide leading to 4–13% of ground water bodies exceeds safety limits, 1–2% food products exceeds legal safety limits and nearly 385 million cases of poisning reported worldwide with few thousand deaths annually1. The most commonly used pesticides in agricultural sector are generally characterized to have high bio accumulative capacity, low biodegradability due to their physiochemical properties2–4. Apart from that these hazardous pesticides have a long -life ranging from 5 to 15 years with increasing toxicity that adversely affects the humans and other animals. Glyphosate (N-(phosphonomethyl) glycine) commonly referred as Glyp, one of the most widely used herbicide in agriculture and urban areas across the world2, that was first reported by the Swiss chemist Henri Martin in 19503 as a potential pharmaceutical compound. Glyphosate is extensively used in agriculture due to its ability towards controlling the perennial weed species, tubers and rhizomes5–7. There are several reports indicates the human exposure of glyphosate leading to chronic diseases such as hyperactivity disorder, kidney failure, Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases. Some of the literatures also indicate the exposure of glyphosate may lead to cancer in human beings4. Glyphosate has been detected in 40% of surface waters in agricultural regions makes the problem real, measurable, and pressing. During the first year of its commercialization in 1974, the Glyphosate consumption was about 3000 tons8. Since then the annual consumption of Glyphosate has increased exponentially from 56 thousand tons in 1994 to nearly 600,000 to 750,000 tons in 2021 which could reach 920,000 tons by 20258. This estimate of exponential growth indicates significant increase in worldwide Glyphosate consumption that may reach few million tons by 2030. Further, in view of mammalian toxicity, the LD50 (Lethal dose, 50%) of Glyphosate is 5037 mgkg−1, which is in the border line of 5000 mgkg−1 for the least toxic chemicals of garde IV9. The glyphosate exposure in low concentrations for prolonged duration may lead to adverse consequences on human health and other animals, hence detecting glyphosate in low concentrations is need of the hour.

In the recent past, various analytical techniques have been employed towards detection of glyphosate, such as chromatographic techniques, fluorescence spectroscopy, electrophoresis, enzymatic immunosorbent assays2. All these analytical methods have advantages and limitations due to the complex nature of detection methods some of them involving enzymatic techniques. In the recent past, electrochemical sensors have emerged as promising alternates to traditional complex and expensive analytical techniques. The electrochemical sensors have advantage as they are cost-effective, simple operation procedures, high sensitivity, specific selectivity and long-term stability. As glyphosate exhibit inherent electrochemical activity, its detection needs novel electrode materials modified with specific properties towards its accurate detection. Various materials have been used in the past towards the detection of glyphosate, such as gold nanoparticles, chitosan, metal-organic frameworks, zeolites, carbon nanotubes and other carbon-based electrodes10. Compared to conventional materials such as gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) and titanium dioxide (TiO₂), NiO-C composites offer a combination of high surface area, tunable electrochemical activity, and enhanced charge transport due to the synergistic interaction between nickel oxide and carbon. While AuNPs exhibit higer sensitivity in sensing applications, they are costly and less stable under harsh conditions. TiO₂, widely used for photocatalytic degradation (PD), suffers from limited visible light activity and rapid recombination of electron–hole pairs. In contrast, NiO-C composites not only show promising electrochemical sensing performance due to NiO’s redox activity and carbon’s conductivity, but also demonstrate improved photocatalytic efficiency under visible light, making them a cost-effective and multifunctional alternative for environmental monitoring and remediation.The metal oxide nanoparticles supported by carbon are emerging as promising candidates in electrochemical sensors due to their properties such as large surface area, abundant active sites for electrochemical activity, improved diffusion at the electrode surface.

Recently, Nanoscience and nanotechnology has witnessed tremendous developments towards the synthesis of novel metal oxides for variety of technological applications. The biogenic synthesis of metal oxide nanoparticles using plant extracts has emerged as a promising eco-safe and scalable techniques in the recent years11. In comparison to porous layered nanostructures, metal oxide nanoparticles synthesized via green chemistry process the advantages of highly porous structure with large surface area, random distribution of semiconducting oxide particles with great coercivity10. NiO has a cubic lattice structure and is a significant transition metal oxide. Due to its potential usage in many different applications, including gas sensors, electrochromic films, catalysis, battery cathodes, and magnetic materials, it has gained more and more attention. Among various Metal oxide nanoparticles, NiO NPs have been investigated intensively due to their low-cost synthesis by biological means and improved electrochemical activity. Recent comparative studies suggest that the approach of Ni dopants and a variety of heterostructure formation with Ni improves the electrochemical sensor metrics and photocatalytic dye degradation12. The Ni incorporation induces an higher balance of sensitivity and stability for electrochemical sensors as well as catalytic response towards dye degradation. Recently electrochemical sensors based on bimetallic Cu/Ce-MOF combined with Ni-doped ZnO nanoparticles shows high sensitivity for detection of ciprofloxacin compared to many Co- or Fe-based doped ZnO analogues, that indicates the benefit of Ni doping enhancing the electron transfer and catalytic activity12. Ni/N-doped graphene oxide was used to detect hydroquinone and catechol, Ni/N-GO composite showed improved performance than many other single-dopant N- or GO-only based sensors13. Heterostructure formation, such as NiO/Bi₂O₃14 or ZnO/NiO composites15, enables efficient electron-hole separation and improved redox potentials. Carbon-based NiO composites, including NiO/rGO, NiO/bio-carbon, and NiO–g-C₃N₄/CC, demonstrate higher activity due to the conductive carbon network, which enhances charge mobility and prevents NiO aggregation and improves the photocatalytic performance16,17.

NiO NPs were extensively investigated as it can be easily form Ni2+/Ni3+ redox couple during electrochemical activity in alkaline solution. Due to the poor conductivity of NiO NPs, their potential usage in technological applications is limited. Hence, modification of NiO NPs by intercalation with carbon-based materials could prove to improve its electrochemical performance and enhance the detection limit18. The preparation of the NiO-Carbon based composites lies in the simplicity of their preparation methods and fabrication of electrochemical sensors. The electrodes prepared using oxide nanoparticles and carbon-based composites proved to exhibit higher electrochemical sensing responses18.

Recently, some of the researc studies explored the synthesis of carbon based composite as a multifunctional material using medicinal plants19. The Silver nanoparticles synthesized from extracts of medicinal neem plant, had a spherical morphology, were crystalline, and had an average size of 20 to 25 nm20. In another study NiO NPs were synthesized from Eucomis Azadirachta indica, an Indian plant known for lowering fever21. Extensive therapeutic benefits of medicinal Neem plant has caught the attention of researchers all over the world in recent years to synthesize biocompatible nanoparticles from it20. There are several reports of preparing the NiO NPs and NiO based composites from plant extracts, but the synthesis of NiO and Corbon simultaneously using neem plant extract and formation their composite is not reported so far. Moreover, there are few reports about NiO NPs towards sensing pesticides, but the electrochemical sensing of glyphosate using biosynthesized NiO-C composite from Neem plant extract are not reported so far. Further, Congo-red dye is one of the widely used dye in many industries in particular the textile industry. The presence of Congo-red dye in most of the water bodies causing a serious threat to human and animal lives. Hence, removal of these organic dyes from the water through PD using novel nano materials is of great priority22–24.

Carbon based materials, in particular carbon-based metal nanostructures, have emerged as potential materials for catalysis under visible-light owing to their interesting properties and great tunability that can be tailored by turning their morphological features25. Recently, a number of carbon-based photocatalysts with different structures and morphologies have been synthesized via well-designed manufacture approaches. The combination of carbon with metal nanoparticles can significantly decrease the charge recombination of the photogenerated electron-hole pairs, so that the photocatalytic performance of the nanocomposite system can be improved25. Hence, Carbon has been considered as a potential material for the design and combination of novel metal-based photocatalysts, and their distinctive properties have been manufactured through various strategies25. There are several carbon-based nanoparticles are reported in the literature to tune their photocatalytic performance, but the nanocomposites based on Carbon and NiO NPs for PD are not reported henceforth.

The novelty of this study does not rely solely on green synthesis, as bio derived NiO systems have been previously reported. In this work, the approach is to bring the rational integration of bio-derived carbon and NiO through a combustion-driven in-situ formation mechanism, yielding a structurally coherent NiO-Carbon composite with enhanced interfacial charge dynamics. Unlike conventional post-mixing approaches (such as NiO/rGO or NiO-biochar), the current synthesis ensures synergetic interactions between NiO crystallites and carbon domains, resulting in improved electron mobility and electrochemical stability under acidic environments. Additionally, this work demonstrates a dual-functional environmental platform, enabling both electrochemical glyphosate sensing and photocatalytic dye degradation using the same optimized material, thereby integrating agrochemical monitoring and wastewater remediation applications. Such multifunctional stability-oriented design has not been systematically demonstrated in Neem-derived NiO-Carbon based system. To demonstrate the comparison of present work within the existing literature, a comparative summary of previously reported NiO-based systems is presented in Table 1. Moreover, the study provides the evidence that carbon incorporation stabilizes NiO against acidic degradation, opening new directions for robust sensor design. This study opens new avenues towards eco-friendly low-cost synthesis of NiO-C composite (from Neem plant) as a multifunctional material for technological applications.

Materials and methods

Plant identification

The source of Neem (Azadirachta indica) was collected from University of Tabuk Campus, Tabuk, Saudi Arabia. It was authenticated by a botanist, from the Department of Biology, University of Tabuk, Tabuk, Saudi Arabia. The voucher specimens were deposited in the Herbarium of the Entomology and Toxicology Laboratory, University of Tabuk, Saudi Arabia with voucher no UT/AI/2024/Tabuk/KSA. The collection of A. indica plant materials was performed according to institutional, national, and international guidelines. Moreover, the plant used in this study is not rare or endangered species, rather a common plant available across Tabuk region, Saudi Arabia.

Synthesis of NiO NPs from neem plant extract

The neem plant (Azadirachta indica) was identified by the co-author CP (Details have been incorporated in supplementary section) and its fresh leaves were collected, purified and processed. Using neem plant leaf extract as a fuel, the NiO NPs were produced by the green solution combustion method. Freshly matured Neem leaves (10 g finely chopped) dissolved in 100 ml DI water ( 10% W/v) in 250 ml flask heated between 60 and 80 °C for 30 min with gentle stirring. The mixture is centrifuged at 3000 rpm, allowed to cool at room temperature, filtered using whatman (No 1) to remove the particulates. Final extract measuring pH 6 is freezed for further use. 1 ml of neem plant extract and 1 g of [Ni(NO3)2ˑ6H2O] were placed in a muffle furnace heated at 320 ± 10 °C. A claer-through gel was produced upon boiling, and subsequently, the container was filled with white foam as it expands. Then, the mixture burned quickly, producing a white byproduct with a very porous structure with the combustion process lasts for in less than 5 min. The neem leaf extract plays multiple mechanistic roles in the green combustion synthesis. Its organic constituents, including polyphenols, flavonoids, and carbohydrates, act as fuel during combustion, promoting rapid heat generation and oxide formation. In addition, functional groups present in the extract facilitate complexation with nickel precursor ions, aiding homogeneous nucleation of NiO nanoparticles. Upon thermal treatment, partial carbonization of these organic compounds leads to the in-situ formation of a conductive carbon phase, resulting in the NiO–carbon composite structure. During the combustion process, the organic constituents of the neem extract undergo partial carbonization under the applied thermal conditions, leading to the formation of an in-situ carbon phase. The combustion temperature plays a critical role in determining the extent of carbonization and structural ordering. Under the present synthesis conditions, the resulting carbon is expected to be predominantly amorphous in nature. Quantitative carbon yield was not independently measured, as carbon formation occurred simultaneously with oxide formation during the self-propagating combustion process.

Preparation of NiO-carbon composite

In a reaction vessel, 1:1 ratio of NiO and carbon powder (obtained by carbonization of neem leaves at high temperature) were added, and the combination was thoroughly mixed by adding 5 ml of distilled water. Further, the mixture was subjected to sonication for a duration of 30 min. The sonication method made use of a 13-mm diameter high-grade titanium alloy ultrasonic probe that was powered by a 20 kHz microprocessor unit (Model PRO-500, 500 W). At a high-power dissipation of 100 W, the powders undergo high-quality dispersion. The NiO-Carbon composite precursor was calcined at 450 °C for 2 h under a nitrogen atmosphere (flow rate 100 mL/min) to promote NiO formation while minimizing carbon oxidation in a furnace. This NiO-carbon composite is designated as A (1:1), whereas the composites B (1:2) and C (2:1), were prepared in the same method but varied the carbon and NiO concentrations [as shown in Scheme 1].

Preparation of carbon paste electrode for sensor study

After grinding the composite product for about 30 min in agate mortor, a mixture of NiO-C Composite, graphite powder, and silicon oil in a ratio of 15:70:15 were mixed to obtain a paste. The carbon paste was then poured into a Teflon-lined hollow tube having diameter 5 mm and the exterior of the carbon paste electrode was smoothened up.

Electrochemical detection of glyphosate

Electrochemical measurements towards glyphosate detection were carried out using a three-electrode system connected to an electrochemical workstation (e.g., CHI660E). A glassy carbon electrode (GCE) modified with the NiO–Carbon composite served as the working electrode, a platinum wire as the counter electrode, and Ag/AgCl as the reference electrode. Prior to modification, the glassy carbon electrode (diameter 5 mm) was polished sequentially with 1.0, 0.3, and 0.05 μm alumina slurry, rinsed thoroughly with deionized water, sonicated in ethanol and DI water for 5 min each, and then dried under nitrogen. It is important to distinguish the intrinsic Ni²⁺/Ni³⁺ surface redox transitions (pseudocapacitive behavior) from the analyte-induced faradaic current response associated with glyphosate detection.This ensures a clean and reproducible electrode surface for composite deposition. The active surface area of the NiO-C working electrode (for 5 mm diameter) was estimated to be 0.196 cm2. Although regulatory limits for glyphosate in drinking water are typically in the µM or sub-µM range, localized concentrations in agricultural runoff, pesticide storage areas, and industrial discharge streams can be substantially higher due to over-application, accidental spills, or concentrated formulations. Therefore, the 1–6 mM detection range of the present NiO-carbon composite is particularly relevant for monitoring elevated contamination scenarios and preliminary on-site screening rather than trace-level potable water analysis.

Photocatalytic measurements

To study the degradation of the Cango Red (CR) dye, the produced A, B, and C nanocomposites were subjected to a PD experiment under the influence of ultraviolet (UV) light. After a series of experimental trials with varying concentrations of dye and catalyst, the optimal level was determined to be 20 mg/L of CR with 60 mg of photocatalyst. Experimental setup used to study the photocatlytical degradation under UV light follows the procedure reported earlier29. Photocatalytic experiments were performed under a UV lamp with a peak wavelength of 365 nm and an intensity of 15 mW/cm² measured at the sample surface using a radiometer. The distance between the lamp and the reaction vessel was maintained at 10 cm throughout the experiments. The reaction mixture was magnetically stirred and temperature was kept constant at 25 ± 2 °C.

Materials characterizations

An X-ray diffractometer (Shimadzu) with the following parameters: V-50 kV, I-20 mA, λ-1.541 Å, and a scan rate of 2o min−1 is used to conduct PXRD analyses of the A, B, and C nanocomposites. The surface morphology was examined using a Hitachi tabletop scanning electron microscope (Model TM 3000) and the data was acquired by SEM using JSM-6510 system (JPN). The TEM analysis was conducted using a JEOL JEM-2100 electron microscope. A Perkin Elmer Spectrometer (Spectrum 1000) equipped with KBr pellets was used for the FT-IR analysis. The samples were subjected to DRS analyses in 200–800 nm range using a Shimadzu UV-2600 spectrophotometer. The Perkin-Elmer TG/DTA-6300 apparatus was used to conduct the TGA and DTA under a nitrogen environment at temperatures between 25 and 750 °C, at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1. For spectral analysis, Fluor Essence™ was utilized. Cyclic Voltametry (CV) measurements were conducted on a CHI604E potentiostat using a tri-electrode setup. The working electrode was a NiO–C powder, the counter electrode was a platinum wire, and the reference electrode was Ag/AgCl with 0.1 N HCl and 0.1 N KOH as an electrolyte (details related to electrolyte preparation are discussed in supplementary information). The voltage variation was between − 0.2 and 0.6 V relative to the Ag/AgCl electrode, and the scan rates were 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 mV/s. Furthermore, EIS measurements were also performed using an AC amplitude of 5 mV in a frequency range of 1 Hz to 1 MHz.

All three samples exhibit a comparable and pure cubic structure with the characteristic NiO peaks observed at (002), (111), (200), (222), (220), (311), and (222) crystal planes. The characteristic peaks observed in the PXRD are in well agreement with the JCPDS card number 47-1049. The prepared sample is well-crystalline due to the presence of sharp, intense peaks. Carbon ions were successfully Interfacial interaction into the host lattice of NiO sites, according to PXRD data. The average crystallite size (D) was estimated from Scherrer’s equation D = Kλ/ βcosθ and was found to be in the range 25–30 nm. The PXRD intensity drops and shifts slightly towards the lower angle side as the dopant concentration rises. This is mainly due to the tensile stress caused by the firmly capping of dopant ions into the host lattice, causes the unit cell volume to grow. The results indicate the formation of a NiO-carbon composite rather than substitutional carbon doping within the NiO lattice. Carbon is expected to exist as a separate conductive phase interacting at the interface with NiO particles, and no direct evidence of lattice incorporation is claimed in this study.

Fourier-transform infra-red spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis

The FT-IR spectra of A, B, and C nanocomposites are illustrated in Fig. 1d. A narrow and strong band at 3634 cm−1 corresponding to the (OH) stretching vibration, which is characteristic feature of NiO-C composite.The intense and wide band at 3419 cm−1 is attributed to the O-H stretching vibration of the water molecules in the interlayer and the OH group that forms a bonding with hydrogen. For a transition to be considered “allowed,” whether it’s direct or indirect, the momentum matrix element defining it must be non-zero. Thus, the transition can be guaranteed to take place provided that the particle (e.g., an electron) participating in the process receives enough energy. Any transition, whether direct or indirect, is considered “forbidden” if the corresponding element of the momentum matrix is zero. Even with enough energy, the transformation will not be able to hold. A previously disallowed transition, nonetheless, can occasionally become permissible. In certain cases, a transition that is prohibited in first order perturbation theory may become permissible in second order perturbation theory.

The Eg of the synthesized NiO-C nanocomposites were determined to be 3 to 4.5 eV when the line (F(R∞)hϋ)2 was extrapolated to zero, since NiO is a material with a direct band gap (Fig. 4b). The observed variation in band gap values (3-4.5 eV) is attributed to size-dependent quantum effects, defect levels, and interfacial interactions between NiO and the incorporated carbon phase, which can slightly modify the absorption edge without changing the intrinsic direct band-gap nature of NiO. Similar variations have been reported in NiO nanostructures due to structural defects and surface states38. It is further clarified that the photocatalytic experiments in the present study were performed under UV irradiation (365 nm), and no visible-light activity is claimed. The variation in the calculated band gap values among the NiO-C composites is mainly attributed to particle size differences and defect states introduced through carbon incorporation, which can slightly shift the optical absorption edge.

The TGA and DTA studies

To further understand the thermal stability of the synthesized nanocomposites, the TGA/DTA study was conducted. Figure 4c–e displays the thermogram indicating the degradation of electrode material. When NiO-C Compositeis heated under oxygen from 0 to 800 °C, the TGA-DTA curves show the thermal transition. The TGA curve indicates that NiO-C Compositedecomposes in two stages, with weight loss happening between 50 and 280 °C and 350 and 500 °C. As expected, the NiO-C Compositedegradation caused a weight loss of around 8% in the first temperature area39. The CR dye’s PD by the NiO-C composite occurs through a synergistic mechanism involving various steps such as absorption of light, generation of electron–hole pairs, transfer of charge, and radical formation. In the presence of ultraviolet light, NiO-C composite can undergo photoelectrons and hole generation in its conduction and valence bands49. The carbon matrix in the composite provides higher electron conductivity and a high surface area, acting as an electron acceptor and transport channel. The photogenerated electrons migrate from NiO to the carbon support, which prevents rapid recombination of electron–hole pairs. This extends the lifetime of charge carriers and enhances photocatalytic efficiency. Further, the reactive oxygen species are formed as the electrons in conduction band reduce adsorbed oxygen molecules on the carbon surface to facilitate the formation of superoxide radicals. Whereas, holes in valence band oxidize the water molecules or hydroxyl ions to generate highly reactive hydroxyl radicals. The ROS species (⋅OH and ⋅O2−​) generated are strong oxidizing agents. Further, these radicals can attack the azo groups (–N = N–) and aromatic rings present in CR dye molecules, breaking them into smaller intermediate compounds. Through these successive oxidation steps, the CR dye molecules are eventually mineralized into non-toxic end products such as CO₂, H₂O, and inorganic ions resulting in complete degradation. The enhanced photocatalytic performance of Composite A (1:1 ratio) can be attributed to the ideal combination of semiconducting activity from NiO and conductive/adsorptive properties of carbon. The synegetic interactions between them leads to efficient degradation of pollutants through improved charge dynamics and surface interactions. The balance between NiO and carbon provides, efficient charge separation, adequate light absorption, enhanced exposure of catalytic sites, and improved adsorption of dye molecules. At low carbon content (e.g., 2:1 NiO: C): The composite contains a higher proportion of NiO, which contributes to photocatalytic activity through its inherent semiconducting properties. However, insufficient carbon limits conductivity and charge separation, leading to faster electron-hole recombination and reduced efficiency.

Under the irradiation of UV, NiO absorbs photons and generate electron-hole (e⁻/h⁺) pairs in the composite system. Due to the synergetic interaction between the phases of NiO and Carbon, the photogenerated electrons are transferred to the conductive carbon network, which acts as an electron transport pathway. This process decreases the electron-hole recombination and improves the efficiency of charge separation. These transferred electrons react with dissolved oxygen to produce superoxide radicals (·O₂−), while the holes oxidize water or hydroxide ions to form hydroxyl radicals (•OH). These reactive oxygen species (ROS) subsequently attack the azo bonds (-N = N-) and aromatic structures of Congo Red dye, leading to significant degradation and eventual mineralization. The improved performance of the 1:1 composite is attributed to balanced interfacial contact between NiO and Carbon, which optimizes charge transport and surface-active sites without affecting the photocatalytic centers.

In comparison with conventional metal oxides such as gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) titanium dioxide (TiO₂) and zinc oxide (ZnO), the NiO-C composite demonstrates high performance in both glyphosate detection and dye degradation. While TiO₂ and ZnO are widely used photocatalysts, their efficiency is often limited under visible light due to wide band gaps and fast electron–hole recombination. In contrast, the NiO-C composite benefits from the synergistic interaction between NiO’s redox-active sites and carbon’s high electrical conductivity, enabling efficient charge separation and enhanced activity under visible light conditions. Additionally, the NiO-C composite exhibits notable sensitivity and selectivity toward glyphosate, whereas TiO₂ and ZnO are not commonly employed for electrochemical pesticide sensing due to their lower conductivity and poor surface interaction with target molecules. Furthermore, the incorporation of carbon enhances the material’s environmental sustainability by improving reusability, reducing the need for noble metals, and enabling low-temperature synthesis routes. Importantly, the NiO-C composite offers dual functionality serving effectively in both environmental remediation (dye degradation) and agrochemical monitoring (glyphosate detection) which positions it as a cost-effective, multifunctional, and environmentally friendly alternative to traditional single-purpose photocatalysts or sensors. It is well known that NiO in some conditions is highly unstable causing leaching behavior resulting in adverse toxic impacts51. Nevertheless, some studies demonstrate that, formation of composite with carbonaceous materials reduces the toxicity effects as carbon reduces the metal ion leaching provides stability and improve environmental compatibility52–54. While the present study establishes the glyphosate sensing and photocatalytic dye degradation efficiency of NiO–Carbon composites, detailed assessments of biocompatibility, leaching behavior, and long-term environmental safety were beyond the scope of this work. Such studies will be essential in future investigations to ensure safe and sustainable application of these composites in agricultural and environments.

Conclusions

In this study, a bio-derived NiO-carbon composite was successfully synthesized via a green combustion approach using neem leaf extract. Structural characterization confirmed the formation of face-centered cubic NiO with crystallite sizes in the range of 25–50 nm, while morphological and electrochemical analyses demonstrated effective interfacial coupling between NiO and the carbon phase. The optimized 1:1 NiO: C composite exhibited a diffusion-controlled electrochemical response with a linear detection range of 1–6 mM and a limit of detection calculated using the 3σ/slope method in the millimolar range. In addition, the composite achieved 98.78% degradation of CR under UV irradiation, maintaining high reusability and stable performance over repeated cycle. Further, the NiO-C composite electrode shows glyphosate detection stability in acidic conditions which is relevent for practical applications in scenarios where the sensor could be exposed to acidic conditions. The photocatalytic analysis using optimised NiO-C composite indicates an efficiency of 98.78% of CR dye degradation when exposed to UV radiations. In conclusion, these findings highlight the versatility and cost-effective synthesis of NiO-C composite as a promising multifunctional material for glyphosate sensing and PD of CR dye that could facilitate the agricultural sector and industrial waste water treatment technologies.
