The second Trump presidency: Health care themes, policies, and impacts in his first year back in office
Authors: Thomas Rice, Lynn Y Unruh, Andrew J Barnes, Erica Richardson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•This article reviews the Trump administration’s health actions during the first year in office.•Trump has focused on reducing the size of government, cutting programs, and staff.•Particularly deep cuts are being made to Medicaid and the ACA marketplaces.•Cuts to NIH, CDC, and other agencies will reduce data collection and analysis.•Health status may decline both domestically and internationally.Keywords: Donald Trump, Health care, Quality, Regulation, NIH, CDC, USAID

Research in Context

What is already known about the topic?

The topic is new as Donald Trump has just completed his first year of his second term as President of the United States.

What does the study add to the literature?

The study summarizes policies implemented during the first year of Trump’s administration and presents future policies under discussion. His administration has enacted many policies aimed at reducing the role of government in health care. It has also substantially changed the federal government's guidance on issues such as vaccination policy and nutrition.

What are the policy implications?

Millions of Americans will lose their health insurance coverage. Future scientific research, particularly that carried out by universities, is in jeopardy. This, in turn, will stymie medical and pharmaceutical innovation and compromise the delivery of high-quality care. Falling vaccination rates will result in greater illnesses from contagious diseases. Cuts to global health have endangered lives in many of the world's poorest countries, while the destabilization of global trade has limited the scope for European countries to fill the gap.

President Trump came into office with an agenda to lower government spending by reining in government programs, and he acted quickly to realize that agenda, dramatically reducing federal employment and funding for programs. In this article, we discuss the health initiatives of his administration during its first year in office, which ended in January 2026, focusing separately on both domestic and international initiatives. While some previous research examines the first 50 or 100 days of this presidency in the health domain, we now know far more about the administration’s policies, particularly in areas such as vaccination and nutrition [1,2].

Broad overview of the Trump agenda

Project 2025

In 1981, the Heritage Foundation, a conservative think tank, published its first "Mandate for Leadership”, which provided recommendations for the new administration of President Ronald Reagan. In 2023, it published its ninth edition, anticipating the incoming (second) Trump administration. The 2023 document has provided a blueprint for much of what President Trump has sought to accomplish in the first year of his second presidency.

In addition to the Mandate for Leadership document, Project 2025 provides a database of allied personnel who can aid implementation (e.g., through federal appointments) and a “playbook” for drafting suggested transition plans for the new administration, allowing it “to go to work on Day One to deconstruct the Administrative State”. As of March 2025, over 30 individuals associated with Project 2025 had been nominated or appointed to roles in the Trump administration [3].

Broadly, the document prioritises four areas: (1) focus on family, (2) “dismantle the administrative state and return self-governance to the American people”, (3) defend U.S. sovereignty and borders, and (4) secure individual rights [4]. This article focuses on (2), as it encompasses most of the agenda in health care, particularly the downsizing or elimination of health care programs and research and ending what has been termed a “woke” agenda. As stated in the document, “Conservatives desire a smaller government not for its own sake, but for the sake of human flourishing”, and moreover, a conservative President has the tools to “defang and defund the woke culture warriors who have infiltrated every last institution in America” [5].

Smaller government

Reducing the size of the federal government and its regulatory authority has been a Republican Party goal for decades. It can be traced back at least to the 1920s, with President Calvin Coolidge saying, “the chief business of the American people is business.” [6]. Republicans opposed much of Franklin D. Roosevelt’s New Deal during the Great Depression. The administration of Ronald Reagan solidified this ethos; he is quoted as saying, “Government is not the solution to our problem, government is the problem” [7].

Upon his inauguration, President Trump immediately established a Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE). DOGE is not an official branch of government, as it was not established by Congress. However, it took on tremendous power over both the federal workforce and programs funded by the federal government. Foremost was closing or drastically paring down agencies and programs that are not consistent with the administration’s goals.

The Republican Congress passed H.R. 1, often referred to as “One Big Beautiful Bill” (OBBB), in July 2025. It called for deep cuts in federal funding over 10 years, not only to shrink government but to pay for some of his other initiatives, including continuation of large tax cuts implemented in Trump’s first administration, as well as additional spending on defense and border protection. As discussed below, Congress did not go along with many of his proposals to reduce biomedical research, but others - including drastic cuts to the Medicaid program and to the subsidies that make individually purchased health insurance more affordable – are being implemented.

Undoing the “woke” agenda

The term “woke” became part of the lexicon in the mid-2010s in the Black community as a way of staying alert to police brutality, although the term dates much further back in time. It has since morphed, with those on the left viewing it as a call for social justice, while those on the right use it as a synonym for political correctness [8]. It reached its apex in the 2024 Presidential campaign, when President Trump ran for office, campaigning on the ideas that stressed social issues, stating that “Our country will be woke no longer.” [9]

In its first year, the administration has implemented aggressive retrenchments in equity-related policies and regulations. Targets include diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) programs at universities and medical care for transgender individuals, particularly children. The Department of Education has led these efforts, suing a number of universities, accusing them of favoring pro-Palestinian protesters at the expense of Jewish students and employees, and illegally favoring racial minorities in admissions [10].

Specific health initiatives in the first year

We discuss five specific areas of health care that have been implemented or are currently being implemented.

Reducing research and support for research

National Institutes of Health

The United States National Institutes of Health (NIH) is the world's largest funder of science and innovation ($47 billion in 2024), dedicated to improving human health and longevity. On 27 January 2025, the Office of Management and Budget released a memorandum temporarily pausing funding and requiring all Federal agencies to identify and review the funding of all programs for compliance with a series of executive orders signed by President Trump [11]. Executive orders are presidential directives to federal agencies based on the President's constitutional authority; that is, they do not require Congressional assent [12].

About one week later, the NIH released supplemental guidance stating that the indirect rate, essentially an add-on to cover overhead costs for the institutions conducting the research, would be set at 15 %, substantially lower than the 50 % or higher indirect rate many top-tier research universities have negotiated [13]. Within days, several legal cases were filed across nearly half of the US states to challenge the revised cap on indirect rates. On 5 March, a federal judge issued a nationwide temporary injunction of the proposed 15 % NIH cap on the indirect rate as court cases work their way through the legal system [14].

In the initial months of President Trump's second term, the NIH also rescinded funding for at least 400 research grants, targeting research related to health equity and environmental justice, as well as nearly $500 million for identifying and developing antiviral drugs to combat viruses with pandemic potential. The president's proposed budget for the Department of Health and Human Services (DHHS) called for a 40 % reduction in NIH funding and the consolidation of the current 27 institutes and centers, which would dramatically reshape the NIH and its priorities [15]. Congress ultimately did not agree, approving a small increase in NIH funding for fiscal year 2026 without changes in NIH’s internal structure [16].

Centers for disease control and prevention

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the US agency dedicated to protecting public health, has faced massive layoffs during Trump's second term. About 20 %, or nearly 2400 employees, were terminated in the first months of 2025. In addition to personnel losses, DOGE requested that the CDC cut $2.9 billion (35 %) from its contracts [17]. This is in addition to the $11.4 billion in CDC grants to states that DHHS has already cut. CDC funds account for a significant portion of state health departments’ annual budgets, which are used to vaccinate people, track disease outbreaks, and monitor clean water. Nevertheless, Congress’s 2026 budget kept CDC funding at a similar level as in previous years [18].

Health services and health policy research

Two federal agencies, the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) and the Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation (ASPE), conduct much of the federal government's research on health services and health policy research. AHRQ stopped funding new grants for which funding was already appropriated beginning 1 April 2025, and staffing has been cut by at least 65 % [19]. These cuts are likely to affect the federal government's ability to gather data on causes of hospitalization, patient safety, and patient engagement, and to publish guidance on delivering better, safer health care. Cuts at ASPE include former staff who set the federal government's annual poverty guidelines, which delineate income eligibility for food and health insurance benefits, including the nearly 80 million American families covered by Medicaid [20].

Reductions in health insurance coverage

Medicaid

Medicaid and the Children's Health Insurance Program (CHIP) cover over 80 million Americans, or 1 in 5 Americans. These public insurance programs provide health benefits for low-income children, adults, and families. Federal dollars are used to match state dollars to finance Medicaid and CHIP coverage. Although there is some uniformity in the federally required scope, breadth, and depth of coverage for these programs, states have the flexibility to provide more generous benefits and extend eligibility to additional populations while still receiving federal matching dollars. Under the Obama Administration, Congress passed the Affordable Care Act (ACA), allowing states to increase the income eligibility limit for Medicaid. Since then, 41 states, including the District of Columbia (Washington, DC), have expanded Medicaid, and 10 have not [21].

During Trump’s first year, CMS informed states that they can no longer use federal matching funds to support certain non-medical services for Medicaid enrollees, including housing and nutrition support. H.R. 1 called for reducing federal revenues by $4.5 trillion and cutting spending by $1.1 trillion. Nearly all (95 %) of these cuts will come from reductions in federal health spending, including over $900 billion in cuts to Medicaid and other health care coverage programs [22]. As a result,10 million people are expected to lose coverage by 2034 [23]. The details on how these historic reductions in federal spending on coverage for low-income families would be achieved include: 1) implementing a requirement that adults eligible for Medicaid expansion work, look for work, or engage in other qualifying activities (e.g., community service) for 80 h per month to stay enrolled (those who are not in school, a caregiver of a child 13 or younger, or medically frail are exempt); 2) limiting states' ability to use provider taxes as revenue for states' share of Medicaid spending, 3) reducing the amount Medicaid can pay certain providers by restricting state-directed payments, and 4) increasing administrative frictions by making it more difficult to enroll in coverage and requiring more frequent eligibility redeterminations.

Georgia’s 2023 work requirements reduced program enrollment by far more than expected. Over 100,000 Georgians applied, but two years later, only 8000 were enrolled. According to the Kaiser Family Foundation, “The program has cost more than $100 million, with only $26 million spent on health benefits and more than $20 million allocated to marketing contracts.” [24]. Other costs include processing additional paperwork and implementing new technologies to verify and track employment, other qualifying activities, and exemptions to the enrollment requirements.

ACA premium subsidies

ACA marketplaces for individual insurance purchases were implemented in 2014. They were established to allow individuals to purchase coverage without underwriting restrictions (e.g., everyone was eligible regardless of their health) and to make such coverage affordable for Americans without employer coverage, Medicare, or Medicaid. 85 % of purchasers received premium subsidies based on an income-related scale [25]. Subsidies were available to individuals and families with incomes below 400 % of the federal poverty level (in 2025, the poverty level was $ 15,060 for an individual and $31,200 for a family of four). During the Biden Administration, legislation was passed that substantially increased these premium subsidies, effective in 2021, but only through calendar year 2025. The enhanced subsidies ensured that no one would have to spend >8.5 % of their income on individual-market insurance plans. As a result, marketplace enrollment doubled from 2021 to 2025, from 12 million to 24 million people, with an estimated 93 % of enrollees receiving subsidies [26].

The Republican-led Congress chose not to extend the enhanced subsidies beyond December 2025, despite Democrats' efforts to keep them, which led to the longest federal government shutdown in US history – 43 days. Without the enhanced subsidies, out-of-pocket premiums will rise dramatically. During the open enrollment period that began in November 2025, it was estimated that consumers saw an average premium increase of 115 % over the previous year (part of this was due to a 26 % average increase in amounts charged by insurance companies, with the rest was due to the expiration of the enhanced premium subsidies). The nonpartisan Congressional Budget Office estimates that continuing these subsidy enhancements would have cost $350 billion over a 10-year period; the failure to extend them beyond 2025 will increase the number of Americans without health insurance by an estimated 3.8 million [27]. Other Trump administration efforts to reduce ACA enrollment include shortening the annual open enrollment period and cutting outreach spending by 90 % [28].

Make America healthy again

President Trump established the Make America Healthy Again (MAHA) Commission by an executive order in February 2025. The Commission, headed by DHHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, Jr., aims to lower chronic disease rates, overhaul the CDC and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to sever ties with pharmaceutical companies, and lower pharmaceutical prices [29].

Chronic illness (make our children healthy again)

The MAHA Commission report on making children healthy identifies numerous policy areas (such as reforming dietary guidelines, defining ultra-processed foods, and enhancing food labeling), but few have been implemented to date. New dietary guidelines appeared in January 2026 [30]. The guidelines place greater emphasis on consuming more protein and full-fat dairy products. More traditionally, they advise eating more vegetables and fruits, and cutting back on processed foods and sugar.

The MAHA list of policies tends to focus on what individuals can do to improve their health through participation in various nutritional and fitness programs. Another individual-level policy that will take effect in January 2026 is to deny “junk food” (undefined by the end of 2025) purchases paid for by the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) [31].

Some MAHA policies target change at both the institutional and industry levels, such as improving school food options and raising standards for infant formula, eliminating harmful chemicals from the food supply, and strengthening oversight and enforcement of laws on direct-to-consumer prescription drug advertising. These institutional and industrial programs, too, remain largely unimplemented. However, two policies to remove perceived harmful chemicals from the food and water supply have emerged: removing red food dye from the food supply and fluoride from the water supply. While the first is a positive step, the second is not supported by scientists or public health advocates [32]. Finally, none of the policies address the social determinants of ill health, which include the environment, poverty, and systemic racism and discrimination.

Moreover, the MAHA Commission works hand in hand with the Trump administration, which is cutting staff in federal agencies and removing regulations in the food and agriculture industries. For example, changes are being made to the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) (which has lost over 15,000 staff since January 2025) [33] that lower food safety standards, speed up slaughter lines at meatpacking houses, and ban speech about agricultural pollution [34].

The Trump administration is also counteracting positive programs aimed at reducing chronic disease by weakening climate change regulations. An estimated one-third of the staff at the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has been cut, and the agency has eliminated its research group, which is responsible for building knowledge about the factors that affect environmental toxins and for developing regulatory strategies. The EPA is also encouraging the use of more coal and reconsidering mercury standards for coal-fired power plants, the mandatory Greenhouse Gas Reporting System, wastewater regulations, and regulations “throttling the oil and gas industry” [35,36].

Infectious diseases and vaccinations

Changes in the staffing and research of DHHS agencies (such as the CDC and FDA) are affecting vaccine access and utilization. Kennedy fired the entire CDC advisory committee on vaccines (ACIP) and replaced members with vaccine skeptics [37]. In March 2025, >40 research studies examining vaccine hesitancy and efforts to improve immunization levels were cut [38]. In May, DHHS revoked a $600 million contract to Moderna to develop a vaccine against bird flu, while in August, nearly $500 million in grants and contracts for developing mRNA vaccines were canceled [39]. Grants to state and local health departments for COVID testing, vaccinations, and training health workers have been terminated. Federal grants to further COVID-19 research and development of new drugs have been terminated, and the DHHS Office of Long COVID Research and Practice has been closed [40].

In contrast, DHHS is funding research on treatments other than vaccines, such as vitamins for measles, cod liver oil, and other supplements - treatments that have already been studied and found to have little effect [41].

Kennedy publicly questions the efficacy and safety of vaccines and promotes doubt in the public’s mind about the safety of childhood vaccines. In particular, he links vaccines to autism, despite overwhelming evidence and scientific consensus that vaccines are not responsible for the increases seen in childhood autism and that recommended childhood vaccines are safe and effective [[42], [43], [44]]. Kennedy ordered the CDC to replace its website statement that there is no link between autism and vaccines with the new website now stating that “Studies supporting a link have been ignored by health authorities.” The CDC website does not list the “ignored” studies [45].

Due to alleged safety concerns, childhood vaccination schedules are being revised. Childhood combination vaccines for measles, mumps, rubella, and chickenpox are being separated into the first three, then months later, the last [46]. Increasing the number of times children need to obtain vaccines is likely to reduce immunization rates. In December 2025, the CDC stopped recommending hepatitis B vaccination for newborns [47]. In January 2026, ACIP went further, reducing the number of recommended childhood vaccines from 17 to 11 by excluding Hepatitis A and B, rotavirus, and flu, recommendations that were subsequently rejected by the American Academy of Pediatrics, calling them “dangerous and unnecessary” [48].

COVID-19 vaccine recommendations are also changing. In October, the CDC approved the ACIP’s decision not to recommend the COVID-19 vaccine for individuals of any age. ACIP states that individuals under the age of 65 should consider the risk-benefit of vaccination, which is more favorable if they are at risk for severe COVID-19 [49]. Further, the administration announced that it will prohibit federal funds from going to school districts with COVID-19 vaccine mandates [50].

All states currently require children to have specified vaccines as a condition for school attendance. However, one state—Florida—is preparing to eliminate the school mandate [51]. Other conservative states may follow.

Paracetamol for pregnant women and children

While admitting that the evidence that links paracetamol (more commonly known by its brand name Tylenol in the US) to autism is weak, Kennedy recommends that paracetamol be used with caution in pregnant women and young children. His administration is urging manufacturers to add warning labels to bottles [52]. If followed, these recommendations would eliminate one of the few treatments for fever and pain that is safe to take during pregnancy and childhood.

Drug safety, conflict of interest, and drug prices

Like what happened at the CDC, Kennedy’s DHHS dismissed numerous FDA employees and cut funding by over 11 %. Prior to the cuts, FDA staffing and volume of inspections were insufficient, and 73 % of drug approvals did not meet the criteria for “adequate and well-controlled studies” [53]. The 2025 staffing cuts further reduced the number of critical staff, including those involved in facility inspection programs and those who test pharmaceutical ingredients for their impact on disease [54]. In place of the funding cuts, the FDA is increasing industry user fees to raise the revenue to run the department. User fees are paid by drug companies in exchange for expedited drug review with decreased regulatory standards [55].

Under Kennedy, the FDA has also reduced the number of advisory committees that include experts on scientific, technical, and policy issues, and has begun using handpicked “expert panels”. While the advisory committees include independent experts, prior to 2025, many members had ties to the pharmaceutical industry, and this potential for conflict of interest continues under Kennedy. The revolving door also continues. The head of the FDA has made it clear that the prohibition against senior agency staff going directly into the pharmaceutical industry is non-binding [56].

Under the Trump administration, pharmaceutical prices for 688 individually purchased prescription medicines increased in 2025 [57]. However, in late December, it negotiated lower prices on select drugs from several companies in exchange for a three-year period of tariff-free treatment for those drugs [58].

Adding privatized preauthorization to traditional Medicare

In January 2026, Medicare will launch a demonstration program in six states using an AI-driven preauthorization system, known as the Wasteful and Inappropriate Service Reduction (WISeR) model [59]. The preauthorizations will be run by private, for-profit companies, chosen in part based on their success in the Medicare Advantage program, which is known for its high prior authorization denial rate. Companies will be paid in part based on the amount of care they deny [60]. The model will begin with 17 procedures that require WISeR preauthorization. This is a significant change, as it is the first time in the 60-year history of traditional Medicare that private, for-profit companies will decide on seniors’ care.

Impacts on health and health system

Access

The Commonwealth Fund reports that the proposed Medicaid cuts would put one in five children at risk of losing health insurance [61]. The lower hospital revenue and higher uncompensated care resulting from these cuts will likely contribute to the closures of rural and critical-access hospitals [62]. The loss of ACA premium subsidies, combined with premium increases, will result in a significant decline in ACA enrollment, which in turn will lead to higher uninsurance rates. If health plans cut benefits to offset premium hikes, underinsurance rates are likely to increase as well.

Quality

The NIH cuts will affect research on new treatments and products, while cuts at AHRQ will affect health care quality assessments and research on ways to improve quality. The direction of research will also change to reflect the priorities of the Trump administration.

Federal cuts to Medicaid and the ACA, along with the increase in uncompensated care resulting from these changes, will likely affect quality. These changes will put pressure on the financial viability of health care providers and will likely lead to staff and service reductions [63].

The quality of healthcare will also be affected by cuts to agencies that regulate medical devices and pharmaceuticals, such as the FDA. FDA cuts have included top scientists overseeing drug approvals (including vaccines). Cuts and staff replacements will likely affect the safety and efficacy of future drugs, food safety, food additives, tobacco regulation, and medical devices [64].

Public health: chronic disease

With an emphasis on individual efforts to improve health, important institutional, industrial, and environmental reforms and regulations are being neglected. Although Kennedy advocates for improving the food served in schools, no policies have been implemented. The food and agribusiness industries continue to produce their unhealthy products. Americans are exhorted to follow nutrition guidelines and eat healthier, and people in poverty are denied access to “junk food” when there is no improvement in the food supply. Chronic disease will not be reduced by turning back the clock on environmental standards.

Public health: infectious disease

Slashing research funding and public health staff, and replacing leaders with those who encourage vaccine skepticism, anti-vaccine research, and lax public health guidelines, is affecting the ability to prevent, detect, and respond effectively to disease outbreaks. Vaccination hesitancy and anti-vaccination sentiment in the past few years have already led to lower vaccination uptake [65]. Under MAHA, this is increasing. MAHA changes in vaccination schedules will also lead to fewer vaccinations and more disease outbreaks. Preventable diseases that can have lethal complications, such as measles, could become endemic once more. Touted as a “historic public health achievement,” measles was eliminated from the U.S. in 2000, meaning that there were no new domestic cases. As of December 2025, there were 1912 cases and three deaths [66]. In addition to the lower vaccination rate, reduced disease outbreak monitoring could hinder the public health response to this ongoing measles outbreak.

Disparities

Health care disparities are increasing for several reasons. First, executive orders issued in January 2025 ended federal DEI programs in all federal agencies. Staff working in these areas were fired, and DEI training was halted. In the private sector, the policy led to the removal of DEI programs, the elimination of DEI training for employees, and the monitoring of external communications.

Second, funding cuts to Medicaid and changes to ACA plans will lead to more under- and uninsured individuals in the population. This will be borne disproportionately by racial/ethnic minorities and women [67].

Third, health care, including gender-affirming care for transgender and non-binary populations, is undergoing transformation due to mandates to end non-binary identification in documents such as passports. This may spill over into the health care sector. Already, NIH grants addressing the health of sexual and gender minority groups have been canceled. Gender literacy and competence training of providers may come to an end. Non-binary language and categories for health care identification purposes may also end [68].

International cooperation and aid

Potentially the most consequential change globally so far has been the virtual elimination of the United States Agency for International Development (USAID) program, reducing staffing from approximately 10,000 to just the 15 positions required by law, and moving its remaining staff and programs to the Department of State [69]. Secretary of State Marco Rubio announced that 5200 of 6200 (about 83 %) of current USAID projects have been canceled [70]. The White House has stated that the reason for the cuts was that foreign aid is “not aligned with American interests and in many cases antithetical to American values” [71].

The defunding of USAID – a focus of DOGE from the outset – will disproportionately affect the lowest-income countries. The impact on care for those with HIV/AIDS is likely to be especially acute because the US provides more than half of foreign assistance for the disease, mainly through the United States President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) program. It is estimated that PEPFAR has saved 25 million lives, and, until recent cuts, was serving 20 million people with HIV/AIDS, providing medications for over 200,000 people daily, and testing for a similar number [72,73].

Simulations show that the removal of PEPFAR funding in South Africa alone would result in a predicted 600,000 deaths over ten years unless alternative funding is found [58]. Simulations from another study focusing on sub-Saharan Africa conclude that “without continued PEPFAR programmes, models predict that by 2030, an additional 1 million children will become infected with HIV, 0.5 million additional children will die of AIDS, and 2.8 million children will additionally become orphaned by AIDS.” [74]

Other cancelled projects include those aimed at: immunizations, clean water in refugee camps, tuberculosis medications, Ebola response, domestic violence shelters, malnutrition, and pre- and post-natal care [75]. Moreover, the Malaria Atlas estimates that the USAID-based President’s Malaria Initiative would have averted 15 million cases and 100,000 deaths across over two dozen African countries in 2025, if it had not been cut [76].

From December 2026, the US has announced a series of bilateral agreements with governments in fourteen African countries to restore some of the funding lost through the USAID cuts, but the structure of this assistance reflects a decisive shift away from traditional aid toward co-investment with national governments and is less than half the previous level of assistance [77].

Implications for Europe

Although a significant portion of PEPFAR and USAID assistance is directed toward low-income countries in sub-Saharan Africa, lower- and middle-income countries within the WHO European Region have also received substantial support, notably Ukraine after the escalation of the Russian invasion in 2022. In 2023, Ukraine was the largest recipient country of USAID funding ($16 billion of a total $72 billion budget) and was the only European country with a program under PEPFAR [78]. The implication for Europe is that the EU and countries working bilaterally will need to step up rapidly to cover shortfalls caused by deep cuts to USAID budgets.

The cuts to USAID bilateral assistance have been accompanied by cuts to US support for the global health infrastructure and multilateral programmes, withdrawing from the World Health Organization (WHO) on the first day of the second Donald Trump presidency (20 January 2025), but subsequently also ending support for other UN agencies that play an important role in global health, such as UNICEF, UNAIDS, and UNHCR in February 2025. In 2019–2022, the US was the largest individual donor, contributing 13 % of total WHO revenues (about $500 million annually) through both assessed and voluntary contributions. However, US spending on global health programmes – bilaterally and through multilateral projects and core funding for multilateral organizations – was around $13.4 billion annually in 2019–2022, the equivalent of about 45 out of every 100 dollars spent [79]. This dependence on a single payer made the global health ecosystem extremely vulnerable to changes in the domestic politics of one country. Diversifying revenues for global health is therefore important for building greater resilience in the system, but the speed of defunding for long-established programmes has left no time for such adjustments.

After the US, the largest funders of global health programs are in Europe; however, the increased need for international development aid is occurring while public budgets in Europe are under strain. Part of Trump’s economic strategy is the extension of tariffs to reduce trade imbalances; new, near-universal US tariffs were announced on 2 April 2025. In response, global stock and bond markets tumbled, prompting Trump to pause the introduction of tariffs for 90 days from 9 April 2025. As of October 2025, the implementation of certain agreements has alleviated some trade tensions; however, significant uncertainty persists regarding the future direction and stability of the global economy. Projections indicate that global economic growth will decelerate from 3.3 % in 2024 to 3.2 % in 2025, and further to 3.1 % in 2026 [80]. These tensions flared again on 17 January 2026, when Trump announced plans to impose additional tariffs (10 % on 1 February, rising to 25 % on 1 June) if European countries did not support the sale of Greenland to the United States, although later he retracted the threat [81]. The European Union was proactive in understanding both the implications of a second Trump presidential term and the potential introduction of tariffs on global trade [82]. However, lower-than-anticipated economic growth across Europe will be difficult to mitigate.

Shifting security alliances and the war in Ukraine have also prompted European countries to significantly increase their defense spending, at the expense of international development aid (as seen in the UK) or by increased borrowing (as in Estonia and Germany) [83]. Trade wars, inflation, and the need for increased defense spending put pressure on public budgets, but previous experience shows the importance of maintaining health and welfare spending to ensure that this pressure is not borne by the most vulnerable in Europe [84].

Domestic US policy is also likely to have repercussions in Europe and globally, particularly in the pharmaceuticals sector. NIH research funding for pharmaceutical R&D previously dwarfed spending by other national governments and the EU. Cuts to NIH spending would reshape the global innovation ecosystem for pharmaceutical companies and the innovation model that centres R&D in the US. Major pharmaceutical companies earn most of their revenue in the US, but successful drug price cuts to match other wealthy nations will lower their profits. Companies may reconsider launch schedules for new products that have traditionally prioritized the US market [85]. Cutting FDA capacity will also slow the pace at which new treatments reach the US and global markets.

The FDA is also responsible for developing internationally harmonized standards for medicines. Cuts to the FDA budget may affect its capacity to fulfill its role in setting and monitoring global standards. Depending on how planned changes to the approvals process for medicines are implemented in the US, these countries may look to HTA bodies in Europe, such as the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and national HTA agencies like NICE in the UK, for more rigorous guidance. In this respect, the timing of the new EU HTA Regulation, which was launched in January 2025, is fortuitous [86].

Critical medicines and medical supplies are increasingly being understood as a national security issue. In the context of cuts to international development aid and the challenges of accessing medicines and medical supplies during the COVID-19 pandemic, many countries in the global South are now focusing on developing domestic and local supply chains for these essential goods [87]. In March 2025, the European Commission put forward the Critical Medicines Act to ensure a stable supply of essential medicines across all EU countries, regardless of market size [88]. On April 1, 2025, the US Secretary of Commerce initiated an investigation under Section 2.3.2 of the Trade Expansion Act (19 U.S.C. § 1862) to determine the effects on national security of imports of pharmaceuticals, pharmaceutical ingredients, and their derivative products [89]. While ensuring that global supply chains for essential medicines and medical goods are robust is a good thing, the dominance of the US in the global pharmaceutical market means that there is a risk that protectionist ‘America first’ policies encouraged by a ‘health sovereignty’ agenda could lead companies to concentrate R&D and production in the US. New, more aggressive US tariffs and security policies have enabled the US to pressure governments by linking issues outside of health. For example, the 25 % price increase for new prescription pharmaceuticals in the UK was linked to the wider US-UK Economic Prosperity Deal, which seeks to minimize tariffs on UK exports to the US [[90], [91]].

Conclusion

Health care played a large role in the first year of Donald Trump’s second term as US President. Significant reductions in health care staff, projects, and budgets have occurred, despite ongoing legal challenges. A central theme is to make government smaller, but there are other themes as well: reducing government regulations; curtailing DEI initiatives; cutting health aid to other countries, with the remaining aid aligning with the administration’s interests; and relying less on the conclusions of the scientific community. Because nearly all of these changes have been brought about by executive orders rather than legislation, they do not require congressional assent – potentially a key strategy, as his party may lose control of one or both houses of Congress in the 2026 midterm elections in November 2026.
