Immunosenescence as a modifiable driver of neurodegenerative diseases: Pathways and multidimensional emerging strategies for Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases
Authors: Jingyu Zhao, Linzhuo Qu, Hongjian Guan, Huiying Che, Fei Shi, Yang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Immunosenescence represents a core upstream driver of Alzheimer's disease (AD) and Parkinson's disease (PD), exerting its pathological effects through four mechanistically interconnected cascades: chronic inflammatory imbalance mediated by the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), oxidative stress arising from immune-metabolic reprogramming, protein homeostasis dysfunction caused by autophagy-lysosomal failure, and disrupted neuro-immune crosstalk secondary to breakdown of central nervous system (CNS) immune privilege. Current clinical practice remains largely constrained to neurotransmitter-based symptomatic management, which can neither arrest upstream pathological progression nor restore lost neuronal populations. Emerging intervention strategies targeting immunosenescence, including senolytic agents, antioxidant and metabolic modulators, autophagy restoration, and precision-targeted nanocarrier delivery, have demonstrated substantial capacity to delay disease progression in preclinical and early clinical investigations. These strategies act through selective clearance of senescent immune cells, remodeling of the neuroinflammatory microenvironment, and restoration of cellular quality-control mechanisms. The multidimensional treatment strategy approaches from the perspectives of artificial intelligence (AI) guided target discovery, nanocarrier engineering, and gut microbiota intervention to simultaneously address multiple nodes in the immunosenescence network, thereby overcoming the inherent limitations of single-target strategies. This review consolidates current mechanistic evidence and translational data to establish immunosenescence as an actionable therapeutic target and to provide a conceptual framework for precision disease-modifying therapy in AD and PD.

Introduction

The accelerated aging of the global population has made neurodegenerative diseases one of the most decisive biomedical challenges of the 21st century. Alzheimer's disease (AD) and Parkinson's disease (PD) alone affect millions of patients worldwide and bring enormous economic and social burdens [1]. At present, drug therapy still focuses on neurotransmitter correction. AD mainly relies on acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, while PD relies on levodopa and its adjuvant drugs. Although these therapies can alleviate early symptoms of the disease, they cannot fundamentally block disease progression [2,3]. Recently, in the Phase III CLARITY-AD trial, monoclonal antibody leqembi was found to specifically bind to soluble oligomers of β-amyloid protein, effectively clearing Aβ pathological protein from the brain and altering the clinical trajectory of AD [4]. However, its therapeutic effect is limited to clearing the downstream-formed Aβ pathological products themselves and does not address the upstream pathogenic mechanisms of neuroinflammation and cellular aging. It is currently unclear whether the drug has a synergistic effect in the treatment of PD. Therefore, elucidating the cascade regulatory mechanisms behind AD and PD is a crucial step in effectively preventing disease progression.

Growing evidence has suggested that immunosenescence refers to a series of degenerative and systemic functional remodelling and dysregulation processes that occur in the immune system as the body ages. This transformation is mainly driven by four pathological cascades that are structurally different but functionally interrelated [5]. Aging immune cells release chronic SASP, maintaining polarisation of M1-like microglia and activation of constitutive NLRP3 inflammasomes, creating a self-sustaining neuroinflammatory microenvironment [6,7]. Aging immune cells also transition from oxidative phosphorylation to aerobic glycolysis, activating NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2) and producing reactive oxygen species (ROS), directly promoting the aggregation and neurotoxicity of amyloid beta (Aβ) and alpha-synuclein (α-syn) [8]. The paralysis of autophagy lysosome pathway in aging microglia leads to impaired phagocytic function, transforming immune cells from scavengers to a source of pathological protein re-inoculation [9]. The age-related loss of blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity allows peripheral T lymphocyte infiltration and drives the destruction of autoimmune-like neurons within the CNS parenchyma [10]. These cascading reactions together form a self-reinforcing pathological network termed inflammaging, which is the basis for the progression of AD and PD.

This review systematically describes four pathological pathways driven by immunosenescence and the mechanistic contributions to AD and PD. Four emerging single-target strategies-immune regulation, antioxidant and metabolic regulation, protein homeostasis repair, and precise targeted delivery were evaluated for translational evidence [[11], [12], [13]]. We also discuss a multidimensional therapeutic strategy that integrates intelligent nanocarrier systems, artificial intelligence-guided drug design and gut-brain axis intervention, and we explicitly appraise the strength of the supporting evidence; in particular, gut microbiota-directed approaches (FMT and probiotics) are supported mainly by animal studies, isolated case reports and small pilot trials. Core Pathological Pathways Driven by Immunosenescence

Immunosenescence encompasses the multidimensional remodeling of immune system homeostasis that accumulates with age. Through overlapping functional impairments, it drives AD and PD progression via four cascading and mutually reinforcing pathological pathways, including SASP-driven chronic inflammatory imbalance, oxidative stress exacerbated by immunometabolic reprogramming, protein homeostasis dysfunction arising from autophagy–lysosomal failure and neuroimmune crosstalk disruption from CNS immune barrier breakdown [14,15].

Chronic inflammatory imbalance: SASP-mediated microenvironment remodeling

Inflammatory dysregulation is the most direct mechanism through which immunosenescence accelerates neurodegeneration. SASP factors released by senescent immune cells continuously signal to the CNS, triggering irreversible remodeling of central innate immunity. Unlike transient inflammatory responses, SASP-driven inflammation induces sustained M1 microglial polarisation and constitutive NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby establishing a persistent neuroinflammatory microenvironment [16].

AD and PD share a pathological substrate characterised by a maladaptive coupling between impaired immune surveillance and neurotoxic injury. Prolonged exposure to aging signals such as IFN-γ and TNF-α cause microglia to lose their capacity to clear Aβ plaques and α-syn aggregates while becoming hyperactive producers of ROS and excitotoxic glutamate, damaging surrounding cholinergic neurons in AD and dopaminergic neurons in PD [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. In both disorders, neuronal loss emerges when the central immune system shifts from neuroprotection to disease acceleration.

Oxidative stress and immune-metabolic reprogramming

Immunosenescence extends beyond simple functional decline to a deep metabolic reorganisation of immune cells. Under normal circumstances, activated microglia transition to aerobic glycolysis to support rapid cytokine synthesis, and this shift can be resolved once the activation signal is cleared. In contrast, upregulation of CD38 causes aging microglia to continuously shift from oxidative phosphorylation to aerobic glycolysis [21]. This metabolic state creates a self-circulating cycle of energy deficiency and oxidative damage, affecting the progression of neurodegenerative diseases. Insufficient ATP production can prevent immune cells from maintaining autophagy, leading to failure of pathological protein clearance. Excessive ROS modifies Aβ/α-syn through carbonylation, making these proteins more prone to aggregation and more neurotoxic [22,23]. Recently, Wei et al. [24] found that lactate accumulation in aging microglia activates NF-κB signaling through H3K18 lactylation, thereby upregulating an IL-6- and CXCL1-driven senescence-associated secretory phenotype and forming a positive feedback loop. Knockdown of PKM2, a key glycolytic enzyme, can effectively improve cognitive dysfunction in AD model mice [25]. These findings confirm that glycolytic reprogramming in aging microglia is a stable pathological state that differs in mechanism and treatment from the transient metabolic transition of activated microglia (Table 1). The latest preclinical experiments have shown that 78c, a CD38 inhibitor, can specifically eliminate aging microglia and protect the immune function of normal microglia [26,27]. Protein homeostasis dysfunction: autophagy-lysosomal failure

In addition to inflammation and oxidation, the aging immune system can also impair the protein quality control of the brain. Immune cells typically maintain surveillance of the nervous system by actively engulfing misfolded proteins, but immunosenescence disrupts this function through the autophagy lysosome pathway. Aging microglia exhibit insufficient lysosomal acidification and decreased tissue protease activity, leading to a state of digestive overload [29].

In AD and PD, this dysfunction manifests as immune cells recognize and engulf Aβ plaques or α-syn aggregates, but are unable to completely degrade them. The accumulation of undegraded substances in lysosomes leads to cellular metabolic overload, ultimately resulting in the rupture or necrosis of immune cells and the release of intact toxic proteins into the microenvironment. This not only maintains the accumulation of pathological proteins, but also accelerates their spread throughout the brain [32].

Disrupted neuro-immune crosstalk: CNS immune privilege breakdown

Immunosenescence disrupts the physiological immune privilege of the central nervous system (CNS), converting a barrier that normally excludes peripheral immune cells into a conduit for their entry [33]. The intact blood-brain barrier (BBB), combined with low expression of MHC in nerve cells, can limit the access of peripheral lymphocytes under normal circumstances and the presentation of antigens in the brain parenchyma, thereby protecting neurons from adaptive immune attacks [[34], [35], [36]]. As age increases, antigen-specific CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and Th17 cells infiltrate the brain parenchyma in AD and PD, while SASP-derived MMP-3, MMP-9, TNF-α, and NOX2 jointly disrupt BBB integrity and enhance its permeability to peripheral aging white blood cells [37,38]. These two processes reinforce each other, forming a pathological feedforward loop, and infiltrating lymphocytes further disrupt the barriers they cross, gradually deepening the interaction disturbance of neural immunity [39]. Once these infiltrating lymphocytes enter the parenchyma, they induce neuronal death through the death receptor pathway or the release of perforin/granzyme, directly driving nerve damage and shifting neurodegenerative diseases from innate immune inflammation to autoimmune attack [40,41]. This feed-forward loop provides the mechanistic rationale for combining senolytic therapy with strategies that restore BBB integrity.

These four pathways converge into a pathological network. SASP-driven microglial polarisation and intrinsic NLRP3 activation establish the initiated chronic inflammatory state. These misfolded proteins would further accumulation, hindering the normal function of autophagy lysosomes, causing lysosome rupture and accelerating their diffusion in the brain. The breakdown of the BBB allows peripheral lymphocytes to invade the central nervous system, transforming protein and inflammatory burdens into targets for direct autoimmune neuron attacks. Clinical Intervention Strategies and Current Limitations

At present, the drug treatment of AD still mainly relies on acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) and the NMDA receptor antagonist memantine [2]. Anti-Aβ monoclonal antibodies lecanemab and donanemab slow clinical decline in selected patients with early symptomatic AD, but amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) are important treatment-associated risks. In CLARITY-AD, lecanemab was associated with ARIA-E in 12.6% and ARIA-H in 17.3% of treated participants [[4]. In TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2, any ARIA occurred in 36.8% of donanemab-treated participants [42]. Unfortunately, the antibodies gosuranemab and semorinemab targeting Tau were not successful in phase II trials [43,44].

Although long-term use of levodopa may lead to movement disorders in patients, it remains the gold standard for symptomatic treatment of PD [45]. In recent years, dopamine receptor agonists, MAO-B inhibitors and COMT inhibitors have been used as adjuvants or first-line drugs, showing significant therapeutic effects in PD with advanced levodopa deficiency [46]. Anti-α-syn immunotherapy has not yet reached the endpoint in Phase II trials [47], while gene-targeted therapies targeting LRRK2 or GBA mutations have only shown progress in specific populations. NLRP3 inhibitors are continuously advancing from preclinical studies to clinical research [48] (Table 3). Emerging Single-Target Strategies Directed at Immunosenescence

Immunosenescence offers rich intervention targets, including accumulation of aging immune cells, chronic inflammatory imbalance, oxidative-metabolic dysregulation, and progressive protein homeostasis failure. Precision strategies aimed at key nodes in this pathological network have been shown to meaningfully delay AD and PD progression by remodeling the neuroimmune microenvironment and protecting neuronal function. The four emerging strategies below provide translational evidence from preclinical models and selected clinical studies.

Immune regulation strategies

The immune regulatory strategies in neurodegenerative diseases mainly eliminate aging immune cells through senolytics, block pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, and rebuild immune tolerance through regulatory T cells.

The classic senolytic combination of dasatinib and quercetin (DQ) has made progress in AD models. In elderly mice, intraperitoneal injection of DQ selectively cleared p16INK4a-positive aging microglia in the brain, reduced Aβ plaque deposition in AD model mice by 42%, and improved spatial learning in the Morris water maze [30]. Longitudinal studies have found that the proportion of senescent immune cells in peripheral blood significantly decreases after treatment with DQ combined with fisetin DQF. The addition of DQF helps to counteract the epigenetic aging clock acceleration caused solely by DQ and has a certain level of safety [31].

In interventions targeting cytokines, the anti-TNF-α monoclonal antibody Infliximab has shown local anti-inflammatory efficacy in PD studies. In rats with portal vein shunting-induced hepatic encephalopathy, intravenous Infliximab normalized IL-1β- and TNF-α-positive cells in the cerebellum, restored motor coordination, and improved Y-maze learning [49]; it remains in the early candidate stage in AD models. Intravenous injection of Tregs into the spleen of healthy mice increased the survival rate of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, reduced the α-syn aggregation rate, and significantly increased peripheral IL-10 after four weeks, suggesting that Tregs can alleviate neuroinflammatory damage by enhancing CNS immune tolerance [50].

Although these approaches improve pathological and behavioral outcomes in animal models, substantial limitations remain at the clinical translation stage. Future work should prioritise cross-disease mechanistic studies with clear translational relevance and rigorous clinical evaluation.

Antioxidant and metabolic regulation strategies

The antioxidant and metabolic regulation strategies aim to delay immune cell aging and protect neuronal function by supplementing key metabolic substrates, clearing ROS, and reshaping cellular metabolic pathways. Traditional methods targeting downstream inflammatory effectors on inflammation output may impair essential functions of microglia, including synaptic pruning, Aβ phagocytosis, and myelin debris clearance [51]. In contrast, immunosenescence specific strategies act on upstream aging mediators that are only expressed in aging cells, including p16INK4a/p21 positive aging cell populations, the CD38-NAD+ depletion axis, and LXR-mediated cholesterol aging amplification [28], thereby limiting the inflammatory output of aging cells at the source rather than simply antagonizing their downstream products.

In terms of supplementing NAD+ precursors, NMN showed neuroprotective properties as a direct precursor. After oral administration of 500 mg/kg/day NMN to AD mice, NAD+ levels in the brain increased, LXR/CD38 mediated macrophage aging was reversed, and inflammatory cell infiltration around Aβ plaques decreased [52]. However, there is uncertainty regarding the conversion of these rodent doses to the human body [53]. The permeability of NAD+ across the blood-brain barrier and the species differences in the human NMN intestinal transporter SLC12A8 further increase the complexity of dose conversion [54]. It is worth noting that the constitutive upregulation of CD38 in aging macrophages and microglia competitively depletes NAD+ precursors, reducing their bioavailability in the brain, providing a theoretical basis for their combined use [28]. At present, the N-DOSE trial (NCT05589766) is testing the dose of nicotinamide nucleoside to evaluate its effect on increasing midbrain NAD+ levels in Parkinson's disease patients.

In terms of metabolic pathway regulation, the GLP-1 receptor agonist semaglutide inhibited α-synthesis induced activation of microglia in MPTP-induced PD mouse models by activating the GLP-1R-dependent Nrf2 signaling pathway, improving motor scores and survival of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra [55]. However, in human EVOKE Phase III trials, the drug did not show clear cognitive benefits [56]. In terms of antioxidant combination therapy, treatment with N-acetylcysteine (NAC) combined with vitamin E in AD rat models showed that systemic metabolic regulation can significantly reduce immunosenescence markers in the brain [57].

In summary, these treatment strategies have shown consistent neuroprotective effects in rodent models, but their translation into humans still has limitations and more rigorous research and cross-species validation are needed in the future.

Protein homeostasis repair strategies

The protein homeostasis repair strategy addresses the damaged autophagosome and ubiquitin proteasome system (UPS) caused by immunosenescence, with the aim of activating autophagic flux, restoring molecular chaperone function, and clearing misfolded proteins.

For autophagy activation, studies have found that in AD models, microinjection of the rapamycin derivative everolimus into the mouse brain can activate neuronal autophagy, reduce Aβ oligomer levels, and significantly inhibit the pro-inflammatory response of microglia to abnormal proteins [58]. In an MPTP mouse model of PD, rapamycin increased LC3 immunoreactivity, reduced α-synuclein immunoreactivity and preserved nigral dopaminergic neurons, supporting autophagy activation as a preclinical strategy [59]. In the 24-month phase II ADAMANT trial, the active tau vaccine AADvac1 was safe and immunogenic, but it did not significantly improve cognitive or functional outcomes in the overall mild-AD population [60]. Subsequent biomarker-defined post hoc analyses are hypothesis-generating and should not be interpreted as confirmatory efficacy evidence. Preclinical studies of the alpha-syn vaccine PD01A showed a decrease in alpha-syn aggregation in the mouse brain, an increase in the survival rate of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, and no significant autoimmune response [61].

Their long-term efficacy and safety should be evaluated in adequately powered Phase III trials, together with combination approaches designed to restore protein-homeostasis networks more comprehensively.

Targeted delivery strategies

Accurately targeting cells using the BBB still poses challenges at the clinical translational level. Targeted delivery strategies can effectively address this issue through nanocarrier engineering and alternative drug delivery pathways.

The TREM2 antibody-modified liposome carrier showed good targeting performance in AD models, delivering rapamycin at high concentrations to activated brain microglia, which can improve Aβ clearance efficiency and reduce systemic immunosuppressive side effects [62]. Meanwhile, TREM2-targeted polymer nanoparticles can selectively bind to TREM2-positive microglia, increasing the concentration of local anti-inflammatory factors, which is significantly better than non-targeted delivery [63]. In the PD model, silica nanoparticles loaded with anti-TNF-α monoclonal antibodies and modified with transferrin receptor ligands can achieve a 5.8-fold BBB penetration rate, reduce brain TNF-α and improve motor function [64].

Recently, after four weeks of nasal administration, the concentration of NMN in the brain of AD model mice significantly increased, the burden of Aβ plaques decreased by 47%, and olfactory memory was significantly improved [52]. Consistently, the high-impact nasal lipid nanoparticle delivery model showed significant efficacy in PD [65]. The targeted delivery strategy combining nanocarrier engineering with nasal alternative drug delivery can improve the efficiency of drug crossing the BBB and the concentration of drug enrichment in the brain, while reducing systemic side effects. This provides a feasible transformation pathway for improving pathological clearance and functional recovery in AD and PD. Multidimensional Combination Immunotherapy

The four immunosenescence-driven pathological pathways constitute an interconnected network, providing a strong rationale for multidimensional combination strategies in neurodegenerative diseases.

Intelligent nanocarrier delivery systems

Accurate delivery of nanocarriers not only relies on the intrinsic efficacy of the drug itself, but also overcomes the weaknesses of traditional therapies by improving its attainability to target cells in the central nervous system. By utilizing modular engineering methods, BBB penetration ligands, immune checkpoint modulators, and pathological protein neutralizers are integrated into the same carrier, thereby simultaneously acting on multiple pathological cascade links at the same lesion site.

In AD, dual target immune checkpoint regulated nanosystems have demonstrated outstanding advantages. TREM2 antibody-modified lipid nanoparticles co-loaded with PD-L1 Fc fusion protein and Aβ-targeted nanoparticles serve as a pathway for crossing the BBB and preferentially enriching microglia activation in the inflammatory microenvironment of the brain. Among them, PD-L1 inhibits self-reactive T cell activation and promotes Treg proliferation to rebuild immune tolerance by binding to PD-1, while Aβ-targeting nanobodies neutralise Aβ oligomers and block the transformation of microglia to a pro-inflammatory phenotype [66]. Under the combined action, it significantly reduces Aβ plaque deposition and inflammatory cytokine burden in AD mouse models, and can improve cognitive function. It was found that engineered dendritic cell (DC) nano gel conjugates can reach key brain immune regions, block PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitory signals between microglia and T cells, trigger DC-mediated presentation of Aβ antigen, and enhance Aβ-specific T cell response [67]. Preclinical experiments have shown that this strategy has a stronger inhibitory effect on neuroinflammation compared to free antibodies, and no significant systemic immune suppression has been observed.

In PD, research focuses more on the combination of biomimetic cell membrane carriers and cell homing mechanisms. A multifunctional biomimetic nanocarrier system encapsulates PLGA nanoparticles onto the membrane of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) modified with anti-α-synthetic antibodies and PD-L1 single-chain antibodies, and internally loads GDNF [68]. Another innovative study found that T cells were anchored to the surface of Ly6c + monocytes using DNA tetrahedral nanocarriers, and the brain homing properties of monocytes were utilized to precisely deliver T cells to inflammatory lesions [69]. However, these multifunctional carriers still face long-term repeated administration conditions when promoted to large animal or non-human primate models. Lipid- or PEG-stabilized carriers may induce the production of anti-carrier antibodies, thereby accelerating clearance after re-administration and weakening the conversion barrier of therapeutic efficacy. Regulatory evaluation standards for multifunctional nanocarriers are still in the development stage, which brings significant uncertainty to their clinical translation pathways.

AI-Guided Combination Therapy

AI-guided target identification and nanocarrier engineering

Artificial intelligence-driven single-cell and spatial transcriptome analysis can accurately identify immunosenescence-specific pro-inflammatory targets (CD33, NLRP3, TYROBP), avoiding systemic immune suppression caused by pan-inflammatory interventions [70].

In AD, deep learning analyzed approximately 700,000 human AD brain tissue snRNA-seq data and identified different subtypes of microglia cells [71]. Further analysis of spatial transcriptomics revealed spatial heterogeneity in the reprogramming of immune metabolism in microglia, which is correlated with plaque distance [72]. Based on this, AI computing processes that DRIAD compare the negative correlation between drug transcription profiles and characteristic genes of aging microglia and discover that FDA-approved COX inhibitors are potential AD repositioning drugs; the database analysis confirmed that the AD incidence rate of its users was significantly reduced, providing population-level evidence for this strategy [71].

In PD, research has found that candidate drugs such as cyproheptadine have been identified for drug repositioning, which can inhibit the activation of the MAPK/NF-κB pathway in microglia, reduce the release of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6, and alleviate chronic damage to dopaminergic neurons caused by neuroinflammation. The emerging single-cell basic model still mainly achieves cross-disease migration by fine-tuning on AD/PD combined datasets, and there are limitations to independent validation evidence for PD specificity [73].

AI-driven single-cell and spatial transcriptome analysis has been fully developed in AD, and in the future, larger-scale PD-specific single-cell profiling and clinical database analysis will be needed.

AI-guided mitochondrial targeting for oxidative stress

For the oxidative stress pathway, AI algorithms integrate PET-ROS imaging with mitochondrial metabolome data to model the spatiotemporal distribution of brain oxidative stress, guiding the design of biomimetic triphenylphosphonium-functionalised polylactic acid nanoparticles loaded with edaravone, ergothioneine, or AI-screened antioxidant compounds. Serban et al. [73] provided direct empirical precedent for this AI-optimised screening approach using multi-omics integration. These nanoparticles target neuronal mitochondria directly, clearing excess ROS and restoring electron transport chain function. An AI-designed antioxidant peptide, validated through DPPH radical scavenging assays and docking studies, confirmed binding to the Keap 1-Nrf2 interface. Degradable inorganic nanoenzymes such as cerium dioxide (CeO2) can provide sustained antioxidant activity, overcoming the low bioavailability that limits traditional antioxidants [74]. This integrated solution path effectively overcomes the bottleneck of low bioavailability of traditional antioxidants.

AI conformational analysis integrated with PROTAC and extracellular vesicle delivery

AI deep learning of the conformational and aggregation characteristics of pathogenic proteins, including Aβ, tau, and α-syn, guides the design of high-affinity PROTAC bifunctional ligands that recruit E3 ubiquitin ligase to pathogenic proteins, achieving precise intracellular degradation via the UPS [75]. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from MSCs and modified with rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG) peptides to enhance brain targeting and BBB penetration serve as delivery vehicles for molecular chaperones such as HSP70 and HSP90, or for specific siRNA, repairing endoplasmic reticulum folding capacity and inhibiting pathogenic protein misfolding [76]. The natural biocompatibility of EVs enables combined intracellular and extracellular regulation, restoring protein homeostasis from multiple angles.

AI multi-omics network analysis for neuro-immune crosstalk

AI integration of transcriptomic, immunohistochemical, and neurometabolomic data maps interaction networks governing core pathways, including CX3CL1-CX3CR1 and TLR4-MyD88, to identify non-redundant synergistic intervention targets. Dual-functional nanocarriers modified with both microglia- and neuron-targeting ligands co-deliver a CX3CR1 antagonist and BDNF, suppressing microglial cytotoxic activation while promoting neuronal survival to repair impaired neuro-immune communication [77]. Some strategies additionally use AI-designed photosensitive promoters for optogenetic control, achieving spatiotemporally specific expression of key neuro-immune interaction factors and enabling precise, light-controlled intervention [78].

Intestinal Microbiota Intervention: targeting the gut-immune-brain axis

The gut microbiome plays an important regulatory role in the neuroimmune axis and is a potential target for peripheral interventions in central immune failure. Preclinical studies have shown that fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) can restore the diversity of gut microbiota and may limit the spread of peripheral inflammation to the central nervous system by activating gut GPR41/43 receptors, promoting Treg proliferation, and upregulating tight junction protein expression in the BBB [79]. In addition, adding PD-L1 antibody therapy to FMT can have a synergistic effect, but this is still a mechanistic hypothesis that has not been tested in patients. According to reports, the probiotic combination of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus can downregulate peripheral IL-6 expression and increase antigen-specific antibodies, which may improve peripheral immune function. It is unclear whether these effects translate into benefits for central immune aging in AD or PD [80].

In PD, animal studies have reported that FMT can restore gut microbiota and secondary bile acid production, and activate central TGR5 to inhibit alpha-syn aggregation and reduce neuroinflammation [81]. It has been proposed that reprogramming probiotics expressing IGF2 can inhibit the activation of Th17 cells in the PD gut, promote the synthesis of 5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP) by intestinal chromaffin cells, and reduce peripheral metabolic loss of levodopa when used in combination with levodopa [82]. In the MPTP-induced Parkinson's disease mouse model, supplementation with Lactobacillus rhamnosus E9 for 15 days can alleviate motor deficits and restore intestinal barrier proteins [83]. So far, the mechanism of FMT plus PD-L1, secondary bile acids, and IGF2 reprogramming probiotics has only been validated in animal models.

The clinical evidence of FMT and probiotic intervention in AD and PD is still in the preliminary stage. In AD, it is limited to an uncontrolled case report of a patient receiving treatment for Clostridium difficile infection [84]. In PD, it consists of two small-scale randomised FMT trials and a short-term (4-week) probiotic trial conducted in 72–120 patients with a primary outcome of constipation rather than disease progression (Table 3). These studies did not design or provide evidence to demonstrate disease changes, nor did they evaluate immune aging-related endpoints, and the follow-up time was relatively short compared to the course of neurodegeneration. Challenges and Future Directions

Despite substantial preclinical progress in immunosenescence-targeted strategies, the path to widespread clinical translation still faces substantive unresolved obstacles.

The primary issue lies in the immune heterogeneity among patients, and it is urgent to adjust the dosage in real-time based on biomarkers in clinical practice. The plasma p16INK4a mRNA and circulating SASP factors can serve as peripheral indicators reflecting systemic aging cell load, while CSF biomarkers can more directly reflect microglial aging and neuroinflammatory activity. Integrating the two into the experimental design of adaptive drug titration is the most rigorous path to establish an effective and safe treatment window (Table 5). At the same time, immunosenescence weakens the innate and adaptive immune defenses of AD/PD patients, increases susceptibility to infection, and may trigger reactivation of latent viruses. This review systematically demonstrates the mechanistic position of immunosenescence as a common upstream driving factor for AD and PD, and decomposes it into four interrelated pathological pathways: senescent cell clearance, metabolic reprogramming, protein homeostasis repair, and targeted delivery. Furthermore, the clinical treatment strategy for neurodegenerative diseases will be repositioned from symptomatic treatment to systemic level immune network reprogramming.

Existing research has confirmed that every node in the immunosenescence network has pharmacological accessibility, but its efficacy is highly dependent on precise matching of intervention methods and target mechanisms. Clearing senescent cells is superior to blocking downstream mediators in inhibiting neuroinflammation; Correcting metabolic defects in aging immune cells can reverse SASP and phagocytic dysfunction. The restoration of autophagy and active immune clearance act on the upstream links of pathological protein aggregation formation. At the same time, the development of targeted delivery technology has gradually reshaped the feasibility boundaries of these therapies, enabling precise interventions to break through the limitations of the BBB.

The integration of artificial intelligence-driven patient stratification, multi-target collaborative therapies, and next-generation nano-delivery platforms signals a paradigm shift from single-target approaches to networked, multi-node collaborative treatment models. Advances in single-cell and multi-omics analyses now allow for the identification of preclinical immunosenescence characteristics, opening up intervention windows before irreversible nerve damage occurs. This enables targeted immunosenescence therapies to more effectively halt disease progression. Among innovative strategies, gut microbiota-directed interventions (such as FMT and probiotics) present a promising peripheral entry point into central immune aging. However, current evidence is primarily derived from animal studies, isolated case reports, and small pilot trials, leaving their clinical applicability unproven. Robust, randomised, placebo-controlled longitudinal clinical trials with predefined immune, microbiota, and clinical endpoints are essential before these approaches can be adopted in clinical practice; thus, the present conclusions should be seen as hypothesis-generating. The mechanistic insights and therapeutic progress outlined in this review establish a conceptual framework for this paradigm shift and provide a theoretical basis for advancing precision medicine in neurodegenerative diseases from passive neuroprotection to active immune remodelling.
