Lack of Detection of Norwalk Virus in Saliva Samples From a Controlled Human Infection Model
Authors: Robert L Atmar, Frederick H Neill, Nicole M Hayes, Antone R Opekun, David Y Graham, Mary K Estes, Sasirekha Ramani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollowing recent reports of norovirus replication in salivary gland cells, we examined whether the prototype norovirus strain, Norwalk virus (GI.1), could be detected in the saliva of 21 experimentally infected persons. Viral RNA was not detected in saliva 2 and 7 days after challenge despite high levels being present in feces.Keywords: challenge, detection, norovirus, RT-qPCR, saliva

Human noroviruses (HuNoVs) are an important cause of acute gastroenteritis, with 19–21 million cases estimated to occur annually in the United States [1]. They are classified into genogroups and genotypes based on phylogenetic analysis of their capsid sequence, with genogroup II (GII) strains being much more common than GI strains or those of other genogroups [2]. Only within the past 10 years have HuNoVs been able to be propagated in cell culture, and there are still no small-animal disease models for this pathogen [3]. Thus, the controlled human infection model (CHIM) is used to assess various questions related to HuNoV pathogenesis.

Human noroviruses are spread predominantly by the fecal-oral route, with contaminated food, water, and fomites being potential sources of infection along with person-to-person transmission [4]. There have also been reports of potential airborne transmission after vomiting events [5, 6]. A limited number of prior reports have noted detection by reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) of HuNoV in oral samples, particularly in persons who reported a prior episode of vomiting [7–9]. A recent report noted the presence of murine norovirus in the saliva of infected mice and the ability of a GII.4 HuNoV to replicate in ductal and acinar salivary gland cell lines, suggesting the potential for salivary transmission [10]. We performed the current study to evaluate whether Norwalk virus could be detected in salivary samples collected as part of a previously conducted CHIM study [11].

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Virus

A fecal sample (#551-07) collected following experimental infection with Norwalk virus/68 [12] and containing 4.8 × 109 genomic equivalents (GE) per milliliter was used in standardization experiments to spike saliva samples.

Saliva Samples for Assay Development

Saliva samples were collected by the drool and spit method from 5 healthy adults. The healthy adult samples were spiked as described below.

Seeding Experiments

Two hundred fifty–microliter aliquots of saliva from 5 healthy persons or an equivalent volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were seeded with 5 μL or 10 μL of virus containing 4.8 ×109 GE/mL, 4.8 × 108 GE/mL (1:10), or 4.8 × 107 GE/mL (1:100), mixed and frozen at −80 °C until analyzed.

Clinical Study

Saliva samples were collected by the drool and spit method from 5 healthy adults and from persons experimentally infected with the 42399 challenge inoculum for Norwalk virus/68 [11], as described previously [13]. In brief, a CHIM was used to determine the human infectious dose of Norwalk virus/68. Healthy adult participants were administered different dosages of the Norwalk virus/68 42399 challenge inoculum and monitored for signs and symptoms of Norwalk virus–associated acute gastroenteritis. Infection status was determined by the identification of virus in fecal samples and by evidence of serological responses to the virus. Results of the study have been reported previously, including details of participants’ infection status and virus shedding [11, 14]. Saliva samples were collected during screening and on study days 2 and 7 after virus inoculation (performed on study day 0). Saliva samples were mixed with protease inhibitors prior to storing at −80 °C. The study was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board at Baylor College of Medicine, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT00138476).

Viral RNA Extraction and Amplification

Fifty microliters of saliva was extracted using a MagMax-96 viral RNA isolation kit using a KingFisher Flex purification system, resulting in a 100-μL extract. Norwalk virus RNA was amplified on a StepOnePlus thermocycler by real-time RT-PCR using 3 μL of the extract added to the qScript XLT One-Step RT-qPCR ToughMix reagent with ROX reference dye (Quanta Biosciences) and the NIFG1F/NV1LCR/NIFG1P primer pair and probe [15]. Seeded samples were run in triplicate, and samples from the challenge study were amplified in single wells.

Statistical Methods

Samples with a cycle threshold value of <40 were considered positive. Cycle threshold values from triplicate wells were averaged, and those with no amplification were assigned a cycle threshold value of 41. A saliva sample was considered positive for virus if at least 2 of the 3 wells tested in an experiment had virus detected. Differences between samples were analyzed by 2-way analysis of variance using GraphPad Prism 10 software for Windows (version 10.2.2).

RESULTS

Norovirus Detection in Seeded Saliva Samples

Extracted saliva contained 9.2 × 104, 9.2 × 105, and 9.2 × 106 GE of Norwalk virus per analyzed volume (50 μL). Norwalk virus was consistently detected at the 2 higher concentrations of virus evaluated; all but 1 seeded saliva sample containing 9.28 × 105 GEs was positive (Figure 1). In contrast, Norwalk virus was detected only 60% of the time at the lowest concentration; virus was identified in the seeded samples in 0, 1, 2, 3, and 3 of the 3 independent experiments using saliva samples obtained from the 5 healthy persons. The PBS samples containing the lowest Norwalk virus concentration also were positive in 2 of the 3 experiments. Similar results were observed in a single experiment using 2-fold less virus to seed the samples.

Norovirus Detection in Saliva From Infected Persons

Twenty-one persons were infected in the study, as reported previously, and samples from these individuals were assessed for the presence of virus in the saliva samples collected prior to challenge (study day 0) and on study days 2 and 7 postchallenge. None of the saliva samples had Norwalk virus detected. In contrast, virus was present in fecal samples at high titers in 81% of participants on study day 2 by the time of the saliva collection and in all 8 persons who vomited prior to saliva collection (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

GII.4 HuNoVs have recently been reported to replicate in a salivary cell line, and murine noroviruses similarly were able to replicate in salivary gland tissues [10]. These observations led us to explore whether we could find evidence of HuNoVs in samples we previously collected during a Norwalk virus GI.1 CHIM. We first demonstrated that virus was detected in >50% of samples seeded with approximately 2 × 106 GEs/mL of saliva, which corresponds to approximately 100–1000 human infectious dose 50% per milliliter [11]. However, we did not detect virus in any salivary samples of infected persons on days 2 and 7 following challenge, periods of time when much higher amounts of virus were present in fecal samples and virus was detected in vomitus.

A limitation of our study is that the limit of detection of virus with the methods we used was approximately 1 million GE/mL. A prior report that identified HuNoVs in saliva samples found them at a concentration between 102.5 and 103.7 GE/mL [9], so it is possible that we could have missed the presence of low levels of virus in saliva. Virus degradation with loss of viral RNA is another possibility. Although the Norwalk virus particles are resistant to proteolytic degradation [16], we evaluated salivary samples stored with a protease inhibitor cocktail to decrease the likelihood that a negative result might be due to loss of virus integrity. Another limitation is that we examined only a single HuNoV genotype, and biological differences between genotypes have been reported [17]. Further, the HuNoVs detected in saliva samples to date have been GII strains [7, 9], although 1 report described detection of several GI genotypes in nasopharyngeal swabs [18]. In the latter report, saliva samples were not specifically evaluated for the presence of HuNoVs. We studied a GI strain, which may not be important if the virus is present in the oral cavity as a result of vomiting. However, it could be important if a different mechanism is involved. A third consideration is that we studied healthy, non-immunocompromised adults, and it is possible that findings could be different in young children. For example, HuNoV RNA has been reported in sera from young children with HuNoV gastroenteritis [19–21] and in immunocompromised adults [8, 22], but we are unaware of any such reports in non-immunocompromised adults [23].

In summary, we failed to detect Norwalk virus in the saliva of experimentally infected adults at times when high levels of virus were present in their stool and moderate levels were present in vomitus. These data indicate that if Norwalk virus replication occurs in salivary glands and is shed in the saliva, then it either occurs at very low levels or on a different time course relative to replication in the small intestine. The data also suggest that the saliva of infected adults is unlikely to be an important route for viral transmission.
