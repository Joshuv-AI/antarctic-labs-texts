Prosecutor politics: The impact of election cycles on criminal sentencing in the era of rising incarceration
Authors: Chika O Okafor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExploiting variation in the timing of district attorney elections across nearly 40 states during the steepest rise in U.S. incarceration (roughly 1986–2006), this paper shows that election years increased per capita admissions and months sentenced to state prisons, with admissions rising 3 percent—approximately 63,000 additional prison admissions over the period. These effects were concentrated in the South, were stronger in Republican counties, and declined as public opinion softened, largely disappearing after the era ended. Results suggest that sentencing outcomes respond to voter preferences, implying that the politics underlying rising incarceration may be as important to understand as the policies themselves.Keywords: Criminal justice, Criminal legal system, Inequality, Mass incarceration, Politics, Prosecutors

Introduction

State and federal prisoners in the U.S. increased by over 600 percent between 1970 and 2000—from roughly 200,000 to 1.3 million. As of 2020, the U.S. had the highest total number of incarcerated individuals in the world (with more than 2.1 million people in jail or prison) and the highest rate of incarceration (at approximately 600 people per 100,000).

Leading scholars attest that the unprecedented rise in incarceration rates is in no small part attributable to public policy choices (Travis et al., 2014). For example, in the 1960s and 1970s, the use of incarceration was expanded in a multitude of ways, including: prison time being increasingly required for lesser offenses; sentences being lengthened for violent crimes and repeat offenders; and drug crimes becoming more severely punished. In the 1980s, more legislative changes were adopted, such as mandatory minimums for various offenses. And in the 1990s, Congress and more than half of states adopted “three strikes and you’re out” laws that mandated minimum sentences of 25 years or longer for relevant offenders. Scholars attest that public policy choices represent the “best single proximate explanation” for the unprecedented rise in incarceration (Travis et al., 2014), yet it remains unknown whether these legislative choices are the prime cause or, instead, simply a proximate but intermediate one.

Public policy choices are not made in a vacuum; they can reflect political moments encouraged by the public will. To understand how the public’s preferences may have impacted rising incarceration, there is perhaps no better elected official to research than local prosecutors (frequently called district attorneys or DAs). Many legal practitioners hold that it is DAs who guide who gets incarcerated and for how long—more so than any other actor in the criminal legal system. The decision on whether to charge an individual, what charges to file, whether to drop a case, whether to offer a plea bargain, and the specific terms of the plea offer remains an unreviewable power of DAs. Significantly, how district attorneys arrive at these [sometimes literally] life-and-death decisions remains under-explored. Furthermore, given the importance of initial charging decisions and the extremely high prevalence of plea bargaining—a process mostly negotiated by the district attorney—many believe sentencing outcomes are significantly linked to the decisions and discretion of the respective district attorney. In recent times, plea bargaining has been the mode of conviction for over 95 percent of convicted felons in counties, meaning individual sentences were strongly influenced by negotiations with the district attorney, and not by the outcomes of judge or jury trials.1 Lastly, the vast majority of DAs are elected officials, which suggests their decisions might be influenced by politics (e.g., by considerations for re-election or for viability as a candidate for higher office later).

How do political incentives and pressures affect the decisions of district attorneys? During the era of rising incarceration, the political landscape may have incentivized elected officials to enact harsher punishments. The 1980s witnessed the expansion of the War on Drugs, with the coinciding crack-cocaine epidemic2 and the doubling of handgun-related homicides between 1985 and 1990. The 1990s saw public and political mobilization in favor of harsher punishments culminating in the landmark 1994 Crime Bill, the largest crime bill in U.S. history. And throughout this period, many politicians sought to be viewed as “tough-on-crime.”

Past research has found that voters may weigh performance in election years more than in non-election years (see, e.g., Healy and Lenz, 2014). As such, this paper studies the impact of DA election cycles on criminal sentencing outcomes. I compile a new comprehensive dataset on the election cycles and offices sought for all district attorneys in office during the steepest rise in incarceration in U.S. history (roughly 1986–2006). Exploiting variation in the timing of DA elections, I employ a dynamic differences-in-differences specification to find that being in a DA election year increases total admissions per capita to state prisons and total months sentenced per capita. In assessing the magnitude of the findings, I estimate that the election year effects on admissions per capita rose by approximately 3 percent—roughly 63,000 additional prison admissions over the time period covered by this project. Similarly, the increase in months sentenced per capita in election years is roughly 3 percent.

Various empirical checks in this paper reinforce the findings. For one, this paper provides evidence that the treatment effects in DA election years are not attributable to other relevant elections (e.g., judicial, mayoral, or sheriff elections). Furthermore, this paper demonstrates that the increased level of criminal sentencing during DA election years does not reflect similar cyclical changes in overall crime levels and arrest rates. Lastly, this paper finds that the average delay between case filings and prison admissions is relatively short, meaning election year increases in the prison admissions rate can plausibly be linked to DA decision-making in anticipation of elections.

The data this paper leverages capture prosecutorial discretion at its most consequential margin—commitment to state prison. However, the paper does not directly capture many of the earlier decisions DAs make—from declinations to charge reductions to jail sentences—that never produce a prison admission. To the extent that electoral incentives also influence these unobserved margins, the estimates in this paper understate the full behavioral response of district attorneys to political incentives.

What are the implications of these findings for understanding key drivers behind the unprecedented rise in incarceration? First, the findings support the notion that voter preferences influence incarceration above and beyond legislative or public policy choices, since the empirical strategy controls for differences in laws across place and time (among other controls). Second, after dispelling alternative explanations such as differences in arrest or crime rates, this paper provides evidence that political incentives have at least a short-term cyclical impact on DA behavior—and, in turn, on the punitiveness of the criminal legal system. Lastly, I explore various mechanisms and find further evidence that sentencing outcomes are associated with public sentiment: (1) election effects are higher in the South; (2) election effects depend on county political ideology, being higher in Republican counties; (3) election effects declined in the era of rising incarceration, closely coinciding with softening public opinion on punishment as measured through the General Social Survey (a longitudinal survey administered yearly since the 1970s); and (4) election effects largely disappeared at the national level after the era of rising incarceration ended. Taken together, these findings suggest that DA behavior and sentencing outcomes may respond to voter preferences—particularly preferences regarding the harshness of courts.

This paper makes four main contributions. First, this paper compiles a new comprehensive dataset on the election cycles of district attorneys. I compile information on over 4200 DAs—every district attorney who was in office between roughly 1986–2006. Second, this paper provides causal evidence that DA election years increase the severity of certain criminal sentencing outcomes. The empirical analysis covers approximately 40 states and spans over 20 years at the height of the rise in U.S. incarceration levels. No other study has been found to date that compares election dynamics of district attorneys between U.S. regions and across time. Third, I explore key mechanisms behind the election year effects along spatial, political, and temporal lines—and find that district attorney election effects decline over the period 1986–2006, in tandem with U.S. public opinion softening regarding criminal punishment. No prior studies have been found that evaluate the evolution in DA election year effects over time and compare them to trends in public opinion.

In short, district attorneys appear to respond to political incentives from the local electorate, which is important given the public’s greater desire for punitiveness toward those convicted of crimes during the era of rising incarceration. Other elected officials, including some of the legislators who enacted legislative changes to the criminal legal system from the 1960s through the 1990s, may similarly have been responding to public sentiment. If so, then the public policy choices during that era, in fact, represent an intermediate—rather than a prime—cause of the unprecedented rise in incarceration. Consequently, collective approaches to transforming U.S. public opinion, and not simply technocratic approaches to designing public policy, may be instrumental in curbing historic levels of incarceration.

Related Literature.

Research has more broadly explored the role of prosecutorial discretion in sentencing outcomes (Reinganum, 1988; Bjerk, 2005; Piehl and Bushway, 2007), bias (Didwania, 2022; Harrington and Shaffer, 2025), and its relevance to perpetuating sentencing disparities in light of reforms in determinate sentencing (Miethe, 1987; Kessler and Piehl, 1998; Stemen et al., 2005). A couple of studies have explored the effects of district attorney election cycles in individual states, such as Florida (Nadel et al., 2017) and North Carolina (Dyke, 2007). Yet none of these studies has employed a dynamic difference-in-differences approach, which allows more granular comparisons of election years to the years immediately preceding and following the election. This identification strategy provides more compelling evidence for the existence (or absence) of election year effects. Among other findings, this paper provides new insight into heterogeneity in DA election year effects over time, by geographic region, and by the political ideology of the corresponding district.3

Another contribution of this paper is its scope, which spans the majority of U.S. states over a 20-year time period, thereby providing greater insights into the role of election cycles in national trends in incarceration and more easily controlling for idiosyncrasies within individual states. Furthermore, the analysis of national incarceration trends cannot be performed solely by relying on the extant literature on judges, mainly because the vast majority of criminal sentences result from plea bargaining, a process heavily dependent on the influence of the district attorneys.

Past research suggests that appointed district attorneys tend to have lower conviction rates than their elected counterparts, who might be more concerned with appearing competent to their voters (Rasmusen et al., 2009). The literature also suggests that those DAs who are in office for longer terms tend to prosecute fewer cases, which might be a result of the fact that they do not have to worry about re-election quite as much as DAs who only hold office for four-year terms. Bandyopadhyay and McCannon (2014) compares data from North Carolina DA elections and appointed DA states to find that re-election incentives lead to a 10% increase in the proportion of cases taken to trial; the presence of a challenger leads to an additional 15% increase. The paper finds that when re-election pressures increase, DAs tend to take more cases to trial and they tend to offer fewer plea bargains, perhaps in order to boost their re-election probability. Pfaff (2011) suggests that the convex increase in incarceration figures in the U.S. in the 1980s and 1990s is connected to an increase in the total number of felony filings per arrest (a discretionary power of the DA), as opposed to increases in arrest rates (decided by the police), increased time served per sentence (sometimes decided by a judge in the absence of a plea deal when the case goes to trial), and increased crime rates (often connected to much larger social, political, and economic factors). Neal and Rick (2016) provides a contrasting perspective, finding that much of the growth in prison populations can be attributed to changes in sentencing policies. Krumholz (2019) investigates the impact of district attorney political affiliation, finding that a Republican DA leads to an increase in prison admissions and newly sentenced months per capita in the four years following their election, driven by higher incarceration for drug offenses. In contrast, this paper focuses on the effects of public sentiment, comparing outcomes in predominantly Republican and non-Republican counties (among other things).

The sparse research on district attorneys does relate to a larger literature on how judges respond to political pressures4—yet district attorneys and judges occupy distinct roles in the criminal legal system so may face different incentives. Huber and Gordon (2004) mentions that voters may be more likely to perceive instances of underpunishment by judges than overpunishment, which plausibly also applies to the prosecutorial discretion that district attorneys exercise. In addition, many judges themselves previously served as district attorneys, introducing another source of similarity through selection.

This paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 provides institutional background on the role of the district attorney. Section 3 describes the data collected for this study. Section 4 explains the empirical strategy. Section 5 describes the main results. Section 6 explores heterogeneity and mechanisms behind the results related to public sentiment, and Section 7 concludes.

Institutional background

Description of the district attorney’s role

The district attorney is the most common term for the chief local prosecutor, who can also be referred to as a county prosecutor, a city prosecutor, a state prosecutor, a state attorney, a prosecuting attorney, a Commonwealth’s attorney, a circuit solicitor, an attorney general, or a district attorney general, depending on the state. Table 1 shows various characteristics of DAs by state. During the time period of this study, there were approximately 2300 DA jurisdictions, each served by its own unique DA. In most states, an individual DA jurisdiction corresponds with a particular county, though there are exceptions in which a single DA jurisdiction may span several counties. As such, DAs can be the representative for a single county, numerous counties, or an entire state in legal matters involving the government. While DAs have complete authority to prosecute within their districts, the direct day-to-day tasks involved in the prosecution of felonies are performed by their assistant district attorneys (ADAs) and/or deputy district attorneys (DDAs). Yet, DAs are often responsible for the actual hiring and promotion of ADAs and DDAs in their offices. Furthermore, DAs decide the charging and sentencing policies for their office (within the discretion allowed by state law): just as they can decide to follow more punitive policies in their district, they can also decline to prosecute some crimes, they can narrow the range of sentencing dictated by the law for certain crimes, and they can choose rehabilitative sentences over incarceration. For example, on March 20, 2020, it was reported that Kim Foxx, the DA in Cook County, IL, decided that her office would not prosecute low-level drug offenses during the COVID-19 pandemic. In short, in their respective offices, district attorneys can set the tone of prosecution, the culture of the office, and the decision-making policies.

Selection process

Currently, chief local prosecutors are elected in every U.S. state/area but four—Alaska, Connecticut, New Jersey, and Washington DC, where they are appointed by their Attorney General, a commission, or the governor. After being elected or appointed, DAs in most states serve four-year terms—those in Alabama, Kentucky, and Louisiana serve six-year terms, DAs in Tennessee serve eight-year terms, and DAs in New Hampshire serve two-year terms. During the period studied here, prosecuting attorneys in Arkansas (until the 2002 election) and district attorneys in Wisconsin (until the 2008 election) also served two-year terms. In every state except Colorado, there is no limit to the number of terms that one can hold the office of district attorney. There is also no equal distribution of the population that a given DA district serves—the DA’s office in Los Angeles oversees over 10 million people, while the office in Alpine County, California, has jurisdiction over just below 1000 people (Hessick and Morse, 2020).

Electoral incentives without electoral competition

A basic question arises from this paper’s setting: why would electoral pressures arise for district attorneys even absent direct competition? I propose three complementary reasons why. First, career concerns: district attorneys are concerned not only about the next election, but also about their next office. And performance during electoral periods is inherently more salient, creating signals for parties and donors when pursuing higher office. This channel is consistent with canonical career concern models in which current performance affects future prospects (Holmström, 1999). Second, deterrence of challengers: even if a district attorney runs unopposed in a given election, they still face the threat of future competition. Especially in climates with higher voter appetite for punitiveness, establishing a reputation for “toughness” near elections can discourage future challengers for the office. This dynamic is consistent with evidence from courts that shows that the potential for competition—and not just actual competition itself—shifts behavior toward more punitive decisions (Huber and Gordon, 2004; Gordon and Huber, 2007; Berdejó and Yuchtman, 2013). Third, heightened visibility: election periods increase media attention on those campaigning for office, including district attorneys. As a result, high-profile failures near elections can both damage career trajectories and encourage future challengers for the office (Snyder and Strömberg, 2010). Importantly, all three of these mechanisms operate in a context where DA elections are rarely contested, so political incentives may operate through signaling, deterrence, and visibility rather than just head-to-head competition. The heterogeneity results presented in Section 6 are consistent with these channels. Namely, election year effects are greater in Republican counties and in the South, areas where tough-on-crime signaling may have carried more career value during this era. Also, election year effects declined over time as public opinion shifted, reducing the benefits of punitiveness.

Data

Sentencing data

National Directory of Prosecuting Attorneys—

I compiled the names of all district attorneys in the United States using the National Directory of Prosecuting Attorneys (administered by the National District Attorneys Association) for all years in which the directory could be retrieved: 1987, 1989, 1992, 1994, 1998, 1999, 2000, 2003, and 2005.5 For the core empirical analysis of this paper, I include the full terms in office for the district attorneys identified through this process. Since election years tend to fall in even-numbered years, the core empirical analysis thus spans roughly 1986–2006. The advantages of analyzing this time period are numerous; two are: (1) the time period corresponds with the largest recorded increase in total U.S. prison population (see Fig. 1); and (2) the period corresponds with the greatest state participation in sentencing outcome data from the National Corrections Reporting Program (as described immediately below).

National Corrections Reporting Program (NCRP)—

This represents the primary source for sentencing data used in this study. NCRP collects offender-level data on admissions and releases from state and federal prisons. The NCRP records include offender-level information such as BJS offense category, state offense category, total sentence, race, gender, age, prior felony status, county, and state where sentence was imposed. The analysis includes the full universe of reported records on prison admissions. The National Corrections Reporting Program (NCRP) relies on data submitted voluntarily by individual states.6 Sentenced months corresponds to the longest length of time as stated by the court that the offender could be required to serve for all offenses of which they have been convicted.7 All per capita sentencing outcomes included in this paper are calculated using U.S. Census estimates for the corresponding year. The NCRP documents admissions to state prisons, which are the final stage of the criminal process. However, most cases in which district attorneys exercise discretion do not result in prison admissions. Roughly 40 percent of state felony convictions result in a sentence to state prison, with the remainder largely resulting in jail or probation—and many felony arrests do not result in convictions at all.8 The estimates in this paper therefore isolate electoral effects on the imprisonment margin of prosecutorial discretion.

Election data & summary statistics

Election data were compiled from various sources. Election information for district attorneys was compiled from states, counties, and other sources (see dataset indicated in Hessick and Morse (2020)). The Database on Ideology, Money in Politics, and Elections (DIME) contains over 130 million political contributions made by individuals and organizations for local, state, and federal elections between 1979 and 2014; it also includes information on candidates and committees for state and federal elections (Bonica, 2015).

Both the election year and election month variables described in Section 4.1 are derived from the date of the general election. Yet some incumbents face no challenger in the general election, and in heavily partisan districts, electoral pressure may subside once the primary election concludes—months before the general election. Two features of the empirical design nonetheless limit the resulting mismeasurement. First, primaries still fall within the calendar year of the general election. This means electoral pressure around the primaries still falls within both the election year t=0 of Fig. 2 and the twelve-month pre-election window of Fig. 3. Second, even if a district lacks meaningful competition in a given general election, the time period is still coded as an election year. Although electoral incentives can operate even absent direct competition (Section 2.3), to the extent that such districts experience less electoral pressure, their inclusion in the treatment group would attenuate the estimates toward zero.

Empirical strategy

Research design

In this paper, I adopt a quasi-experimental research design to estimate the effects of election cycles on the decision-making of district attorneys. County-level variation in the timing of district attorney elections results in substantial variation across counties which I exploit using a series of dynamic difference-in-differences specifications:

The variable Ycdt corresponds to one of two outcome variables—(1) admissions per capita or (2) months sentenced per capita—evaluated for county c in district d in time period t.9 Time period t is evaluated in either years or months. On the right-hand side, let Rct=t−Ec denote the “relative time”—the number of periods relative to the nearest election period for the district attorney in county c in time period t. T equals half the length of a district attorney’s term in office.10 γd are district fixed effects, λt are year fixed effects, and Γcdt is a vector of controls.11 The standard errors are corrected for correlation across district attorneys and over time in a given county by clustering at the district level.

The coefficients of interest are βk, which reflect the criminal sentencing outcomes relative to the reference time period X. In each section below, I indicate which election cycle time period corresponds to the normalized time period X. For example, if I normalize such that X=−1, that would mean that, for a district attorney serving in county c in time period t, all βk coefficients would estimate the sentencing outcomes relative to the sentencing outcomes when Rct=−1 (the period immediately preceding the election period). I measure the dependent variable in logs, which yields regression estimates that can approximate the percentage difference in sentencing outcomes.12

I first estimate these models using the NCRP data for which I also have data on the corresponding prosecutor from the National Directory of Prosecuting Attorneys.13 The NCRP data contain offender-level information, such as BJS offense category, total sentence, county, and state where sentence was imposed. The sample for each year consists of all states that reported data and had four-year election cycles.14

The key identifying assumption for difference-in-differences estimation strategies is the common trends assumption, which in this case is that any differential change in outcomes in district attorney election years is the result of the district attorney election. Perhaps the greatest challenge to this identifying assumption are the confounding effects of other relevant elections with the same timing as DA election cycles—for example, other offices related to law enforcement (e.g., sheriff elections). These alternative elections may have direct (and indirect) effects on sentencing outcomes, which are explored in Section 5.4.

Case processing timelines and within-year effects

This paper’s empirical design raises a key question: since criminal cases take time to process, can election year effects on prison admissions be attributable to prosecutorial behavior during the same election year? Below, I provide evidence that the answer is yes.

First, data suggest that most felony cases are processed quickly enough to support within-year effects. According to State Court Processing Statistics (SCPS)—a dataset that includes felony defendants in large urban districts during a time period mostly overlapping with this study—the median time from arrest to adjudication was 2.79 months from 1990–2006 (United States Department of Justice, 1990–2009). Furthermore, 78.2% of cases were adjudicated within six months and 91.6% within nine months, with the interquartile range (25th to 75th percentile) being 1.08 to 5.49 months. This speed in processing is largely attributable to plea bargaining, which accounts for more than 95% of felony convictions.

Second, even accounting for the additional time between adjudication and actual prison admissions, the timeline supports within-year effects. The median lag from arrest to adjudication is 2–3 months, with less than 1–2 months afterward until admission. This means that arrests that occur from November through August prior to an election would typically result in prison admissions by election day in November. This timeline is consistent with the monthly analysis in Fig. 3, which shows elevated levels of criminal sentencing beginning early in election years.

Third, prosecutors can greatly impact the timing of cases. District attorneys can accelerate the resolution of cases in many ways: (1) filing more severe charges that encourage defendants to plead quickly; (2) changing terms in plea offers to encourage acceptance by threatening longer sentences at trial; and (3) prioritizing the processing of certain categories of cases over others. Conversely, DAs can slow the resolution of cases (e.g., by postponing charging decisions until after elections). The discretion DAs have over the timing of cases suggests that election year patterns can plausibly reflect intentional rather than incidental timing.

Results

Election effects on criminal sentencing – outcomes by year

Fig. 2 graphically depicts estimates of the effects of election cycles on two separate criminal sentencing outcomes across all offenses: (1) admissions per 1000 population; and (2) total sentenced months per 1000 population. These graphs follow the regression specification from Eq. (1). All estimates are calculated relative to the year immediately preceding the election year (X=−1), which is normalized to 0. The graphs provide evidence that election years have effects on the admissions rate per 1000 population and months sentenced per 1000 population. The estimated effects of an election year on the admissions rate are statistically significant and positive—approximately 4.3% higher than in the year immediately preceding the election. The estimated effects of an election year on months sentenced are also positive and statistically significant—approximately 4.0% higher.15

Election effects on criminal sentencing – outcomes by month

This subsection presents another analysis of Eq. (1), this time with the time period observed in months and all coefficients measured relative to thirteen months before the election month (X=−13). Fig. 3 illustrates monthly estimates for total admissions (Panel A) and months sentenced (Panel B) across offense subcategories during the entire 4-year election cycle, with adjustments made for seasonality in criminal justice outcomes.

This analysis provides evidence that similar positive election year effects persist across offense subcategories, with the impact relative to adjacent years being most pronounced for property offenses. Though the election year estimates are positive across categories, the observed heterogeneity of election year effects across crime categories could plausibly be affected by various factors, including differences in political risk associated with lenient behavior and varying levels of discretionary power that DAs possess for different crime categories. First, violent cases may be the least cyclical. There is arguably greater political risk associated with being lenient in a high-profile murder case than in a lower-level property offense, regardless of timing in the election cycle. Second, more severe crimes may be resolved the most slowly due to their complexity, pushing their outcomes past the election window even if the cases arise during the election period. As a result, even if significant political pressure persists for violent crime, district attorneys may be more responsive in a given election cycle in their property caseload.

Fig. 3 shows an upward shift in the level of criminal sentencing in the months leading up to the election. A specification comparing the twelve months preceding the election (the shaded window in Fig. 3) with the remaining months of the four-year cycle demonstrates this point: for all offenses, admissions are 1.5 log points higher (s.e. 0.6) in the pre-election window, and months sentenced are 1.8 log points higher (s.e. 0.6). Furthermore, the period of criminal sentencing right before the election is higher than at any other period during the entire 4-year cycle for all offenses aggregated together, as well as for property offenses and drug offenses, though drug offense trends appear noisier. As documented in Section 4.2, with median time from arrest to admission of just over 2–3 months, a significant majority of the prosecutorial decisions made within a year of the election would manifest as prison admissions in the months leading up to the November elections. The fact that we generally observe elevated levels of criminal sentencing beginning in January (t=−10) and staying elevated through the election period aligns with this timeline. Furthermore, the lag between prosecutorial decisions and ultimate adjudication may help explain why there are not perfectly consistent declines in the admissions rates and sentenced months from t=0 to t=1.

Though both the district attorneys themselves—along with the corresponding county characteristics—may be the same across consecutive months in the same jurisdiction, individual criminal cases are generally unrelated to each other. Thus, the fact that the dots are close together and higher in the period immediately preceding a general election suggests that there is, in fact, a higher level of criminal sentencing during this time period. One can clearly see that all the estimates in the ten months leading up to the election month are of higher magnitude than in any other ten-month period in the plot for the admissions rate outcome across all offense subcategories—as well as in the months sentenced outcome across each offense subcategory except violent offenses. The clearest observation of this upward trend occurs in two plots: all offenses aggregated together and property offenses taken individually. Furthermore, the maximum monthly estimate across the entire election cycle in Panel A occurs during this pre-election period. In Panel B, the maximum estimate similarly occurs in this pre-election period across all offense subcategories, except for drug offenses.17

Lastly, the increase in criminal sentencing during the pre-election period appears more pronounced for property offenses than for violent offenses (and, to a lesser degree, for drug offenses), especially in Panel B. This finding is consistent with both annual estimates by offense category in Appendix Figure C.1, and the discussion above.

Magnitude of election year effects

Static difference-in-differences estimates (comparing the election year to all pooled non-election years) imply that election years increase prison admissions per capita by 2.8 log points (s.e. 0.8)—3 percent—with a comparable effect on total months sentenced per capita (2.7 log points, s.e. 0.7). The estimates from the dynamic specification comparing periods t=0 with t=−1 are larger (4.2 log points; see Fig. 2). I use the static estimates to describe magnitudes below so that they draw on the full election cycle—not just adjacent years.

These static estimates are more conservative than the raw differences in Panel B of Table 2. According to the summary statistics, population-weighted admissions levels in election years are 6.5 percent higher than in mid-cycle years. Compared with the static estimate of approximately 3 percent, this means nearly half of the population-weighted difference can be attributed to electoral timing itself. The remainder likely reflects differences in the composition of districts and calendar years observed across points in the election cycle.

These static estimates suggest that approximately 63,000 additional prison admissions are attributable to electoral timing alone across the election cycles in the sample. This reduces to roughly 2600 per election year.18 These magnitudes reflect only the cyclical component of electoral incentives—the within-district timing variation that the research design isolates. The magnitudes do not reflect level effects of electoral incentives that might cascade throughout the entire election cycle. As such, these magnitudes do not reflect the total contribution of prosecutorial elections or broader electoral incentives to incarceration growth during this period. Instead, they provide evidence of one mechanism through which, as Section 6 shows, public punitiveness during the era of rising incarceration could translate into heightened levels of criminal sentencing.

Robustness checks

Alternative weighting for two-way fixed effects (TWFE) estimator

Recent research has highlighted that in settings with variation in treatment timing across units—such as in election cycles—the estimated coefficients in two-way fixed effects regressions can be contaminated by effects from other periods (De Chaisemartin and d’Haultfoeuille, 2020; Sun and Abraham, 2021). Given this potential problem in the estimation of regression coefficients in the two-way fixed effects model, I adapt an alternative estimator following a similar logic to Sun and Abraham (2021) (which was adapted for this setting given differences from the event-study setting they consider).19 This alternative estimator is robust to treatment effect heterogeneity. Under this alternative two-way fixed effects estimator, estimates remain positive across all outcomes and offense subcategories; magnitudes are close to the baseline two-way fixed effects estimates for both outcomes (Appendix B).

Common trends assumption – conceptual introduction

The key identifying assumption for the difference-in-differences empirical approach used throughout this Section is common trends. Given the cyclical nature of elections, the common trends assumption in this context is different from standard settings with an absorbing treatment (i.e., where units remain treated once initially treated). In particular, for the common trends assumption to hold in this paper’s setting, this requires that in the absence of treatment (i.e., without being in a DA election time period), the expectation of the outcome (i.e., per capita admissions rate and per capita months sentenced) follows the same evolution over time in every county (De Chaisemartin and d’Haultfoeuille, 2020).

Common trends assumption – empirical analyses of other elections

Perhaps the greatest threat to the common trends assumption is potential confounding effects from other elections—in other words, whether there are other local and state elections systematically occurring at the same time as DA elections that meaningfully impact sentencing outcomes. For one, judicial elections may prove particularly relevant. Judges often exercise meaningful discretion that could impact prosecutor behavior whether within or outside of plea bargaining—negotiations that are conducted in the shadow of the trial. The analysis below thus addresses the concern empirically rather than definitionally. In Table 3 and Appendix Table C.24, I provide empirical evidence that the common trends assumption holds by testing for potential confounding effects of synchronous judicial, mayoral, and sheriff elections.

Common trends assumption – empirical analyses on crime rates and arrest levels

Another threat to the common trends assumption is that the election year increase in criminal sentencing levels could simply reflect cyclical trends in the crime rate or volume of arrests—not anything linked to DA behavior. Fig. 4 provides evidence invalidating this threat by graphically depicting estimates of the effects of DA election cycles on per capita arrest rates and per capita crime rates,21 via the regression specification from Eq. (1). The plots show there is no statistically significant election year effect on the volume of arrests and crimes. There appears to be no statistically significant political cycle for either the arrest rate or the crime rate. Hence, Fig. 4 does not support the notion that the main election effects on criminal sentencing outcomes can be explained by changes in the volume of arrests or crimes around elections. In other words, this analysis undercuts a perspective that election-year increases in the admissions rate or total months sentenced might be attributable to election-year increases in arrests or crime. Thus, it appears that DA election effects are not attributable to changes in criminal behavior; this evidence suggests criminal activity does not spike in or near DA election years. Given the aforementioned relationship between mayoral/sheriff elections, police presence, and arrest rates—and since arrest rates are not increasing in DA election years—Fig. 4 suggests the relative non-synchronicity of DA elections with those of mayors and sheriffs, and/or the relatively low impact of mayoral and sheriff elections on the election effects found in this study when they are synchronous. Appendix Table C.6 re-estimates the main specification with outcomes scaled to arrests rather than population, which shows an even greater magnitude of election year effects on admissions per arrest of approximately +3.8 log points (s.e. 1.7). This provides further evidence that the electoral response operates through the conversion of arrests into prison admissions—the area most directly impacted by prosecutorial discretion.

Heterogeneity analysis and mechanisms of public sentiment

The results thus far document that the admissions rate per capita and the rate of total months sentenced per capita increase in the lead-up to general elections during an era of rising incarceration (roughly 1986 to 2006). In this section, I perform additional analysis to shed light on various mechanisms that underlie the relationship between DA election year sentencing outcomes and public sentiment. The analysis uncovers several key facts, described in more detail below: (1) DA election effects vary by region and are concentrated in the South Central United States; (2) DA election effects depend on county political ideology, being higher in Republican counties; (3) DA election effects declined in the era of rising incarceration, coinciding with softening public opinion on punishment; and (4) DA election effects disappeared on average at the national level after the era of rising incarceration ended. Taken together, these facts provide evidence of a nexus between public sentiment and sentencing outcomes.

DA election effects track public punitiveness: larger across tough-on-crime era; lower after

The change in election year effects over time in the top plot of Fig. 8 is consistent with the view that evolution in public sentiment over time impacted DA behavior. In the 1980s and 1990s, anecdotally there was a lot more discussion about—and perhaps perceived benefit from—being a politician who was viewed as being “tough-on-crime.” This was the period of the spike in crack-related drug convictions, the passing of the 1994 crime bill, and the establishment of minimum sentencing laws around the country. The GSS shows that, in fact, the vast majority of the population believed courts were not harsh enough. The bottom plot of Fig. 8 shows that only after 1994 did this fraction of the population begin to decline. In contrast, those who believed courts were too harsh or about right have become a growing share of the population since 1994. In short, public sentiment toward punishment has been shifting over the last 25 years or so. This shift coincides with lower election year effects over time, and has plausibly impacted the political incentives faced by district attorneys.

The post-2005 period in Fig. 8 shows no consistent electoral cycle. Admissions rate estimates for 2006–2009 and 2014–2016 do not statistically differ from zero, consistent with gradual rather than abrupt attenuation as public punitiveness receded. Though 2010–2013 does have a positive estimate, this is explained by the enactment of a major state policy (rather than electoral timing)—specifically, the implementation of California’s 2011 Public Safety Realignment.27 As nearly all California district attorney elections in this bin occurred in 2010, this 2011 policy shock sharply reduced prison admissions across California’s non-election years within that bin, mechanically inflating the election year estimate. Appendix Table C.17 corroborates this point, showing that excluding California makes the 2010–2013 estimate statistically indistinguishable from zero, consistent with the null estimates in the other post-era bins.28

There is plausibly a nexus between (1) changes in public opinion over time, and (2) changes in the salience of criminal activity over time. In other words, the public may adopt a more lenient stance toward criminal punishment in periods in which crime is less prominent. The guiding hypothesis is clear: the salience of crime is plausibly more impactful on public sentiment—and in turn the political incentives district attorneys face—than the actual crime rate, since salience more proximately influences voter perceptions than the crime levels themselves. Under this hypothesis—and based on the public sentiment trends described in Fig. 8—one would expect that the softening of public opinion toward punishment would coincide with (and potentially be caused by) a declining salience of criminal activity in the public eye. The GSS figure at the bottom of Fig. 8 illustrates a clear shift in the public opinion trend line after the year 1994. Now, compare that shift in trends with Fig. 9. Fig. 9 shows the incidence of top local newspaper articles that include various crime-related keywords; in short, it can be viewed as a proxy for the salience of crime in the public eye. Strikingly, again we see a clear break in the trend line around the year 1994, after which the incidence of articles mentioning various crime-related language begins to decline. This general decline occurs for articles that include the words “murder,” “crime,” “assault,” and “arrest.” Yet, the downward trend is most apparent for articles that contain the word “murder.” Murders may possess the greatest nexus with public sentiments toward criminal punishment (given the extreme severity and grotesqueness of the offense). Hence, the sharp decline in local newspaper coverage of murder (and other crime-related terms) after 1994 is consistent with the perspective that the softening of public sentiment toward punishment is in response to the declining salience of criminal activity in the public eye.

Conclusion

For this paper, I compiled a new comprehensive dataset on the election cycles and offices sought for all district attorneys in office during the steepest rise in incarceration in U.S. history (roughly 1986–2006). Using quasi-experimental methods, I found that being in a DA election year increased total admissions per capita to state prisons and total months sentenced per capita. I found evidence that sentencing outcomes are associated with public sentiment: (1) election effects are higher in the South; (2) election effects depend on county political ideology, being higher in Republican counties; (3) election effects declined in the era of rising incarceration, coinciding with softening of public opinion on punishment as measured through the General Social Survey; and (4) election effects disappeared at the national level after the era of rising incarceration ended.

If sentencing outcomes were solely determined by the DA’s personal philosophy toward punishment, one might not expect there to be much cyclicality in the severity of outcomes. One would simply expect the districts with DAs having harsher theories of punishment to have consistently more severe sentences. Instead, we see greater election-year effects in Republican counties, which is consistent with the perspective that DA behavior incorporates political incentives, such as perceived voter preferences for harshness. Future analysis might explore “progressive prosecutors”—based on the finding that political incentives for the level of sentencing differ markedly between liberal and conservative districts, this may translate into different behaviors in the run-up to elections for various DA offices. Other legal scholars have already touted the potential for progressive prosecutors to help reform the system (Hessick and Morse, 2020). One would expect not only that areas with progressive prosecutors would have lower overall sentences, but based on this study, one would also expect there to be no election year effects—or perhaps election year effects that are less pronounced—for districts where progressive prosecutors are politically viable.

There are at least two obvious interpretations of the election year effects this study finds: (1) that the election year level of sentencing is too high, based on prevailing theories of justice; (2) that the election year level of sentencing is adequate, and instead the level for non-election years is too low. Given the fact that the United States has the highest incarceration rate in the world, many might find the second interpretation less plausible than the first one. Furthermore, past research has found that initial felony convictions that occurred within six months of district attorney elections had a greater likelihood of being overturned in appellate court, providing evidence that election dynamics may yield more inaccurate sentences, wrongful convictions, and deviations from justice (McCannon, 2013). Hence, under the first interpretation that the election year level of sentencing is too high, politics may have encouraged overly severe sentencing outcomes. Thus, the argument for reducing the political incentives of DAs becomes more salient—particularly when the public’s desire for harshness diverges from what may be considered “fair” or socially optimal.

Accordingly, the findings relate to broader legal debates on the efficacy of “democratizing” the criminal legal system (see, e.g., Rappaport, 2020). On one side of the argument are those who attribute many problems with the criminal legal system to an ill-informed (or particularly vengeful) American public; this side tends to call for the system to be more greatly influenced by professionals and experts. On the other side of the argument are those who attribute the problems with the criminal legal system to bureaucratic attitudes and other institutional/structural problems. This side tends to call for more control of the system from local communities, with greater responsiveness to the lay public. For those skeptical of democratizing the criminal legal system, a potential corrective response to the election year effects could be to transition the DA selection process from an electoral system to an appointive one. Several states/areas already operate under this model, such as New Jersey, Alaska, Connecticut, and Washington, DC.

Some legal scholars, however, question the political feasibility of accomplishing this shift (Kress, 1976; Wright, 2014). For those who support increased democratization of the criminal legal system, appropriate corrective responses to the election year effects found in this paper are less clear. According to some, even if criminal justice actors were made more accountable to local communities, it remains unclear whether the lay public in the local communities most afflicted by crime would necessarily encourage more leniency (Rappaport, 2020). There could be unintended consequences, with more democratization potentially yielding political incentives to increase the severity of sentences. Similarly, proposals to increase the competitiveness of DA elections may exacerbate the impact of election cycles on sentencing outcomes (Hessick and Morse, 2020). Ultimately, the myriad consequences of further democratization in the DA selection process may remain an empirical question—one that I leave for future research.

Of note, this paper only evaluates the short-term cyclical impact of being in an election year on sentencing outcomes. It does not capture potential longer-term political considerations that might impact how district attorneys approach their work at all periods in the election cycle. This means the full impact of political incentives on the criminal legal system and the unprecedented rise in incarceration may be larger than what is found in this paper alone: not only (1) might DAs be responding to political incentives in ways above and beyond what can be captured through this paper’s empirical strategy alone, but also (2) the findings may signal that other elected officials, particularly legislators, were also responding to public sentiment in making legislative choices during the era of rising incarceration. So much work on criminal justice reform to date has focused on public policy improvements to the system itself and to the laws that govern it. If the political incentives of those entrusted to administer justice are shaped by public sentiment regarding criminal punishment, then this paper carries two implications. First, for political economy: electoral accountability in the criminal legal system may operate even absent competitive elections, through career concerns and anticipatory behavior that respond to voter preferences. Second, for public policy: focusing on “hearts and minds”—on shifting public opinion toward punishment independent of shifts to laws or to the structure of the criminal legal system itself—may prove not only beneficial but also instrumental in improving sentencing outcomes and stemming historic levels of incarceration.
