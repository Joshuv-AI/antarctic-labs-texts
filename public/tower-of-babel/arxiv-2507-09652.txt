Machine-Precision Prediction of Low-Dimensional Chaotic Systems
Christof Schötz, Niklas Boers

Abstract
Low-dimensional chaotic systems such as the Lorenz-63 model are commonly used to benchmark system-agnostic methods for learning dynamics from data. Here we show that learning from noise-free observations in such systems can be achieved up to machine precision: using ordinary least squares regression on high-degree polynomial features with 512-bit arithmetic, our method exceeds the accuracy of standard 64-bit numerical ODE solvers of the true underlying dynamical systems. Depending on the configuration, we obtain valid prediction times of 32 to 105 Lyapunov times for the Lorenz-63 system, dramatically outperforming prior work that reaches 13 Lyapunov times at most. We further validate our results on Thomas’ Cyclically Symmetric Attractor, a non-polynomial chaotic system that is considerably more complex than the Lorenz-63 model, and show that similar results extend also to higher dimensions using the spatiotemporally chaotic Lorenz-96 model. Our findings suggest that learning low-dimensional chaotic systems from noise-free data is a solved problem.

Chaotic dynamical systems represent a wide range of complex phenomena across many natural systems and scientific fields
[

,

Here, we present a conceptually simple method executed at high numerical precision that vastly outperforms previous approaches for predicting chaotic systems. It achieves VPT values up to 105 Lyapunov times depending on the specific configuration (
Table

2
), with an example shown in
Figure

1
. We accomplish this by estimating the system’s propagator—the mapping from the current state,
(x(t),y(t),z(t))
, to the state after a fixed time step,
(x(t+\Delta\!t),y(t+\Delta\!t),z(t+\Delta\!t))
, using ordinary least squares (OLS) linear regression on high-degree polynomial features (up to degree 16), constructed from the current state vector.

While our approach is conceptually simple, executing it for high polynomial degrees presents a numerical challenge as we need to solve systems of linear equations given by ill-conditioned matrices. We address this issue through appropriate data normalization and high precision arithmetic. Specifically, we use 512-bit arithmetic and matrix operations provided by the MPLAPACK
[
Nak22
]
and MPFR
[
Fou+07
]
libraries. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application of such high precision arithmetic in a machine learning context for dynamical systems.

Polynomial regression for learning dynamical systems has recently been employed by
[
Gau+21
]
. These authors focus on time delay embedding with relatively low-degree polynomials and use Ridge regression (i.e., Tikhonov regularization), whereas we emphasize high-degree polynomials without time delay embedding and achieve our results without regularization, i.e., using OLS. Note that regularization is typically used to reduce variance of parameters estimated from noisy data at the cost of an induced bias; in our case the data is noise-free and indeed we have found that using regularization makes the results worse due to the introduced bias. A predecessor to our method appears in the simulation study
[
Sch+25
]
(called
LinPo6
therein), but it is limited to degree
6
polynomials and standard 64-bit precision.

The backbone of our method, OLS with polynomial features, can be traced back to the 19th century
[
Ger16
,
Sti74
]
. Machine learning for L63 was first explored in the early 90s
[
ET92
]
.
Given that 64-bit computing architectures became widely available in the mid-2000s, it is remarkable that—even though the standard-precision version of our method (VPT of
21
Lyapunov times) was both conceptually and technologically available—it has not been discovered and presented in the literature for nearly two decades.

Table

Since the L63 system represents a physical process, it is appropriate to use the most accurate available approximation to the exact solution as the default. In our case, this corresponds to the output of a high-precision (512-bit) 4th-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) ODE solver. To reflect typical computational practice, we assume that data is stored in double precision (64 bit), which is then used to train the forecasting model. Given that our polynomial propagator is not computationally intensive, there is no reason to artificially limit its computational precision. Consequently, in our default configuration, we solve the system at 512-bit precision, store data at 64-bit precision, and predict at 512-bit precision. Under this setting, our system-agnostic method achieves higher accuracy (VPT of
35.6
Lyapunov times) than a state-of-the-art 64-bit ODE solver using the true dynamics (VPT of
32.1
Lyapunov times).

Additionally, we present results with different components of the data pipeline operating at single (32-bit), double (64-bit) or multi (512-bit) precision. Even when data is generated using a 64-bit ODE solver, we demonstrate substantial improvements over previous state-of-the-art methods by achieving a VPT of 32 Lyapunov times. When data is calculated and stored at 512 bit throughout, we obtain a VPT of 105 Lyapunov times using a degree 16 polynomial, which can likely be further improved using even higher degrees for the polynomial. A 32-bit version of our method performs much worse (VPT between 6 and 7 Lyapunov times). Note that Graphical Processing Units (GPUs) commonly use such single precision arithmetic as default and achieving results comparable to the ones we present below would not be straightforward with existing GPU hardware and common drivers and software.

Our method is extremely data efficient, achieving the abilities of a 64-bit ODE solver with about
10^{3}
observations in an optimal setting. A polynomial
\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
of degree
p
has
\frac{1}{2}(p+1)(p+2)(p+3)
coefficients. Thus, our polynomials of degree
\leq 16
have less than
3\cdot 10^{3}
parameters, which is much less than commonly employed ML methods, which typically exceed
10^{5}
parameters
[
MG25
, Table 1]
. Furthermore, both training and inference are fast—together taking approximately 10 seconds on a modern CPU to produce the result shown in
Figure

1
. This efficiency stems from the method relying on only a few matrix operations and not requiring a gradient descent-based training procedure. It is worth noting, however, that high-precision calculations generally take significantly more time than standard precision.

The L63 system is defined by polynomial dynamics, see (
1
). However, the discrete propagator, which we estimate using polynomial regression, is not analytically a polynomial function. But numerically, the ground truth trajectories—generated using a fourth-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) integrator—do correspond to a polynomial propagator. Yet its polynomial degree is extremely high, since we apply the RK4 solver at a smaller time step than the interval between training data points. Specifically, we prove that the polynomial degree of the propagator of a
k
-sub-sampled (take every
k
th element) RK4-L63 time series is
F_{2+4k}
, where
F_{i}
is the
i
-th Fibonacci number. Thus, taking every second data point of the ODE solver output yields a degree
F_{10}=55
polynomial propagator. Consequently, even our high-degree polynomial models do not reach the complexity necessary to fully replicate the RK4 algorithm exactly.

To confirm that our findings are not artifacts of the numerical setup, we successfully replicate our experiments on Thomas’ Cyclically Symmetric Attractor
[
Tho99
]
, a chaotic system governed by non-polynomial dynamics given by

\dot{x}=\sin(y)-bx\,,\qquad\dot{y}=\sin(z)-by\,,\qquad\dot{z}=\sin(x)-bz\,,

where we set
b=0.208
. For this system, we also achieve VPT values exceeding those of a double precision ODE solver, but this requires a polynomial degree of
25
.

We explore how our method’s performance depends on the dimension of the dynamical system using the spatiotemporally chaotic Lorenz-96 model
[
Lor95
]
,

\dot{x}_{i}=(x_{i+1}-x_{i-2})x_{i-1}-x_{i}+8\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,\dots,d\quad\text{with}\quad x_{-1}=x_{d-1},x_{0}=x_{d},x_{d+1}=x_{1}\,.

We demonstrate that our method achieves machine precision at least in dimensions
d=5,6,7,8,9
.

Our results indicate that, given sufficient noise-free data and computational resources, chaotic systems can be predicted to arbitrary accuracy—and thus arbitrary valid times. We have demonstrated this for systems with dimensionality
d<10
, and expect that similar results are attainable also for higher dimensions, provided adequate computational effort. However, for systems with
d\geq 100
, achieving machine-precision prediction appears computationally infeasible without adapting the method.

Overall, our findings suggest that learning low-dimensional chaotic systems from noise-free data is a solved problem using OLS polynomial regression, indicating that benchmarks using such a setup may be too simple for effectively evaluating ML methods designed for learning dynamical systems.

Methods

2.1
General Setup

Consider a dynamical system described by a
d
-dimensional, first order, autonomous ordinary differential equation

\dot{u}(t)=f(u(t))\,,

(2)

where
u\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
solution
and
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the vector field describing the dynamics of the system. Given initial conditions
u(0)=u_{0}
for
u_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and assuming
f
is sufficiently smooth (e.g., globally Lipschitz continuous), the solution
u
of (
2
) exists and is unique.

We make
n
noise-free observations
y_{i}=u(t_{i})
,
i=1,\dots,n
, of the trajectory
u
at time points
t_{i}
. The observation times start at a negative value and extend up to
t_{n}=0
. These times are assumed to be equally spaced. This means
t_{i}:=(i-n)\Delta\!t
for a fixed step size
\Delta\!t>0
.

For the prediction task, we assume
f
and
u
to be unknown, but we have access to the observation time series
(y_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n}
and the step size
\Delta\!t
. Our goal is to predict the future values
z_{j}:=u(j\Delta\!t)
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
given
z_{0}=y_{n}
. In this case the training and test data are
sequential
. Alternatively, we also consider the
random
test mode, in which we set
z_{j}:=\tilde{u}(j\Delta\!t)
for
j\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
for a new solution
\tilde{u}
of (
2
), where
\tilde{u}(0)=z_{0}
is chosen randomly from the systems’ attractor. We emphasize that, after training on
(y_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n}
, only a single state
z_{0}
is available to initialize the prediction in the
random
test mode.

To evaluate a given forecast
(\hat{z}_{j})_{j=1,\dots,m}
, we define the Valid Prediction Time (VPT) as the maximum time duration over which the normalized Euclidean distance between the prediction and the ground truth remains below a given threshold
\varepsilon>0
. Formally,

\mathsf{VPT}_{\varepsilon}\!\left((z_{j})_{j=1,\dots,m},(\hat{z}_{j})_{j=1,\dots,m}\right):=\Delta\!t\max\left\{J\in\{1,\dots,m\}\,\kern-1.2pt\mathopen{}\left|\vphantom{J\in\{1,\dots,m\}}\,\forall j\in\{1,\dots,J\}\colon\frac{\left\|z_{j}-\hat{z}_{j}\right\|_{2}}{\sigma}\leq\varepsilon\right.\mathclose{}\kern-1.2pt\right\}\,,

where
\sigma
is the standard deviation of the system, defined as

\sigma:=\sqrt{\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}\left\|u(t)-\mu\right\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t}\,,\qquad\text{with}\qquad\mu:=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}u(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

In practice,
\sigma
and
\mu
are approximated empirically from a long ground truth trajectory,

\bar{\sigma}:=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\left\|z_{j}-\bar{\mu}\right\|_{2}^{2}}\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bar{\mu}:=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}z_{j}\,.

Note that the VPT in our definition is translation and scale invariant.

If the system dynamics are recreated well enough, the forecast error roughly increases by a factor of Euler’s number each Lyapunov time. We can use this to translate between VPT values for different thresholds:
Assuming all VPT values are given in units of Lyapunov time, we have

\mathsf{VPT}_{\varepsilon_{2}}=\mathsf{VPT}_{\varepsilon_{1}}+\log\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{\varepsilon_{1}}\right)\,,

where
\log
is the natural logarithm and
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\in(0,1]
. Empirically, in our simulation study, this formula is a rough but valid approximation (showing at least on significant digit to be correctly approximated for well performing systems; see also the slope in
Figure

5
).

We study three chaotic dynamical systems: the Lorenz-63 system (L63)

]
, Thomas’ cyclically symmetric attractor (TCSA)

, and the Lorenz-96 system (L96)
[

]
, see

. For the L63 we choose the standard parameters, so that the dynamics are described by the vector field

u_{2}\\

u_{1}(28-u_{3})-u_{2}\\
u_{1}u_{2}-\frac{8}{3}u_{3}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(3)

For TCSA, we have

\sin(u_{3})-bu_{2}\\

(4)

where we choose

For L96, we use the standard forcing value

u_{i}\\

\vdots\\

\end{bmatrix}\,.

(5)

5,6,7,8

2.3
Polynomial Propagator Forecast

To create a forecast
(\hat{z}_{j})_{j=1,\dots,m}
from the observations
(y_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n}
, we first estimate the propagator
\Phi_{\Delta\!t}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\,u(t)\mapsto u(t+\Delta\!t)
. Then we apply the learned propagator repeatedly starting from a given state
z_{0}
, which is either the last observed state
z_{0}=y_{n}
(sequential test mode) or a new randomly chosen state on the attractor of the system (random test mode).

The propagator is estimated using ordinary least squares (OLS) linear regression with multivariate monomials as features. That is, for a given state vector
s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a fixed maximal degree
p\in\mathbb{N}
, we create a feature vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
using the feature function
\xi_{p}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
such that

x=\xi_{p}(s):=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{d}s_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}\ \Bigg|\ \alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d}\in\{0,\dots,p\},\,\sum_{k=1}^{d}\alpha_{k}\leq p\right)\,.

(6)

For example,

\xi_{2}(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})=\left(1,s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{1}s_{2},s_{2}s_{3},s_{1}s_{3},s_{1}^{2},s_{2}^{2},s_{3}^{2}\right)\,.

(7)

The number of features is

D=\binom{d+p}{d}=\frac{(d+p)!}{d!\,p!}\,.

(8)

For example, in
d=3
dimensions we obtain
D=165
features using maximal degree
p=8
and
D=969
using
p=16
.
We apply the feature function to the observations
y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to obtain
x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Then we apply OLS linear regression to the data
(x_{i},y_{i+1})_{i=1,\dots,n-1}
to obtain the regression coefficients
\hat{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times d}
.
By stacking the feature vectors and targets row-wise into matrices
X\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times D}
and
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times d}
, respectively, we can express the coefficients as

\hat{\beta}=(X^{\!\top}\!X)^{-1}X^{\!\top}\!Y

(9)

assuming the matrix
X^{\!\top}\!X\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
is invertible. The estimated propagator is

\hat{\Phi}_{\Delta\!t}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\,s\mapsto\hat{\beta}^{\!\top}\!\xi_{p}(s)\,.

(10)

Our forecast
(\hat{z}_{j})_{j=1,\dots,m}
is defined by

\hat{z}_{j}:=\hat{\Phi}_{\Delta\!t}(\hat{z}_{j-1})

(11)

with a known initial state
\hat{z}_{0}:=z_{0}
.

2.4
Numerical Considerations

Executing the procedures described above numerically is necessarily an approximation to the mathematically exact calculations. Its precision is influenced by implementation choices, such as data normalization, the algorithm used for solving linear equations, and the amount of information used for storing a single number.

On a standard machine with standard operations, a number is represented with 64 bit, which roughly translates to 15 significant digits. By using the C libraries MPLAPACK and MPFR, we represent numbers with 512 bit of information, which allows for more than 150 significant digits. We run different experiments where different processing steps are executed with either single precision (32 bit), double precision (64 bit) or multi precision (512 bit).

We first use a Runge Kutta ODE solver of order 4 (RK4) with a solver time step of
\Delta\!t_{0}=2^{-10}\approx 0.001
for L63 and L96 to create a long trajectory of the system. For TCSA, we use
\Delta\!t_{0}=2^{-6}\approx 0.016
as it has a larger characteristic timescale. We then randomly choose a starting point on the long trajectory and do temporal sub-sampling (taking every
k
-th element of the trajectory) to create the training and test data with the desired step size
\Delta\!t
, number of observations
n
, and a large enough number
m
of states in the test set.

When using single or double precision for the polynomial forecaster, our results depend on whether and how we normalize our data. We may first transform the training data
y_{i}
to
\tilde{y}_{i}
by normalizing it to mean
0
and covariance matrix equal to the identity matrix, i.e.,

\tilde{y}_{i}=\hat{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(y_{i}-\hat{\mu})\,,\qquad\hat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-\hat{\mu})(y_{i}-\hat{\mu})^{\!\top}\!\,,\qquad\hat{\mu}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\,,

(12)

where
\hat{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}
is the matrix square root of the symmetric positive definite matrix
\hat{\Sigma}
and
\hat{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is its inverse.
As this
full
normalization is not common in the literature, we also compare it with the more common
diagonal
normalization, where each dimension is scaled by the inverse of its standard deviation individually without changing correlations between variables, i.e.,

\tilde{y}_{i}=\hat{S}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(y_{i}-\hat{\mu})\,,\qquad\hat{S}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\hat{\sigma}_{d}^{2}\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad\hat{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-\hat{\mu}\right)^{2}\,.

(13)

When normalizing the training data, the output of the estimated propagator has to be scaled back to the original scale before comparing it with the test data. When using multi-precision arithmetic (512 bit), we do not normalize the data as we do not suffer from numerical instability in this case.

We use the R programming language
[
R C24
]

4
Discussion

We have shown that a simple, data-driven approach based on polynomial fitting can match—and in some cases outperform—standard ODE solvers. We have explicitly demonstrated this result for the 3-dimensional polynomial L63 system, the 3-dimensional non-polynomial TCSA, and the
d
-dimensional polynomial L96 system with
d<10
. These findings suggest that our method generalizes to a broad class of low-dimensional dynamical systems governed by autonomous first-order ODEs. From this perspective, the problem of learning such systems from noise-free data may be considered effectively solved. In fact, the widely used noise-free L63 model and similar low-dimensional chaotic system benchmarks may no longer be adequate, as they prove too easy under these conditions.

4.1
Limitations of Existing Methods

We now consider potential reasons why previously proposed methods did not achieve results comparable to ours. Specifically, we examine three widely used approaches for forecasting low-dimensional chaotic dynamical systems: Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamical Systems (SINDy)
[
BPK16
]
, Long Short-Term Memory networks (LSTMs)
[
HS97
]
, and Reservoir Computers
[
Jae01
]
.

To predict a system
\dot{u}=f(u)
, the SINDy algorithm uses estimates of the derivative
\dot{u}(t)
to fit the model function
f
and then uses an ODE solver to create a forecast. The model function
f
is assumed to be a sparse linear combination of a predefined set of features. E.g., in the standard version of SINDy
[
BPK16
]
, this set of features are the monomials up to degree
5
. Thus, the algorithm is not fully system agnostic, as some information about the target system must be encoded in the feature set. (In contrast, our method fits the typically non-polynomial propagator
\Phi_{\Delta\!t}
, rather than fitting the model function
f
, and is effective for any sufficiently smooth ODE.) SINDy achieves a VPT of up to 13 Lyapunov times for L63 (
Table

1
), but the standard version of SINDy would not be able to accurately recover systems such as TCSA, where
f
includes non-polynomial components like sine functions. Furthermore, the necessity of estimating derivatives from data introduces error (increasing with larger time steps
\Delta\!t
). Even if the feature set is sufficiently expressive, these derivative estimation errors prevent SINDy from achieving forecasts at or near machine precision.

LSTMs, a class of recurrent neural networks, are trained using gradient-based optimization to minimize prediction error over sequences of data. While they can, in principle, approximate complex temporal dynamics, matching the precision of ODE solvers would require their parameters to be tuned with near-exact accuracy—a level of precision that is unrealistic for gradient descent methods, which are inherently approximate and prone to local minima, saddle points, and vanishing gradients. Accordingly, the best VPT that has been achieved with LSTMs for the L63 system is 6 Lyapunov times (
Table

1
).

Unlike LSTMs, most Reservoir Computers, such as the Echo State Network (ESN)
[
Jae01
]
, are typically trained by solving a linear system of equations, which allows for more precise weight estimation. Similar to LSTMs, Reservoir Computers predict the next state
u(t+\Delta\!t)
based on the current state
u(t)
and an internal memory state
r(t)
, known as the reservoir. Up to 13 Lyapunov times VPT have been achieved with Reservoir Computers (
Table

1
). However, in our setup, where the system evolves deterministically as
u(t+\Delta\!t)=\Phi_{\Delta\!t}(u(t))
, the use of an auxiliary memory is unnecessary and complicates the learning task.
Moreover, RCs commonly use regularization during training to reduce variance and improve numerical stability. While beneficial with noisy data, regularization introduces bias that limits forecasting accuracy, preventing performance at machine precision. Finally, some RCs such as the ESN rely on randomly initialized neural networks to construct features. Compared to structured polynomial bases, this random approach is less efficient for precise function approximation and, without regularization, would lead to numerical instability when the resulting features are nearly linearly dependent.

4.2
Factors Enabling Machine Precision Forecasting

Two key ingredients underpin our success: multi-precision arithmetic and high-degree polynomials.

Multi-precision arithmetic plays a critical role by overcoming numerical limitations in solving the linear systems involved in polynomial fitting. This becomes especially important when working with noise-free data, where it is necessary to exploit the full range of significant digits provided. Standard double-precision solvers of linear equations often fail to preserve these digits due to numerical instability, particularly when high-degree polynomials are used. Note that modern machine learning frameworks, such as PyTorch and TensorFlow, do not support numerical precision beyond double precision (64 bit) on either CPU or GPU. Moreover, they are typically optimized for single precision (32 bit) on GPU, rendering them unsuitable for the multi-precision (512-bit) experiments conducted in this study.

While high-degree polynomials are traditionally viewed with skepticism in machine learning—often associated with overfitting—we have shown that, in the absence of noise, this concern does not apply here. In our simulation study, polynomials of degree up to 25 are used effectively. Since the training data is noise-free, the classical notion of overfitting is irrelevant: there is no noise to overfit to.

That said, our simulation setup is clearly artificial. In practical scenarios, it is rare to have noise-free observations with 15-digit precision spanning the full range of 64-bit floating-point numbers—if such data exists at all. More often, measurements are noisy, and even the leading digit may be unreliable. In such settings, neither multi-precision arithmetic nor high-degree polynomials are helpful. Indeed, as shown in
[
Sch+25
]
, polynomial propagator methods—and most other techniques—perform poorly when noise is present. Because noise is inherent in most real-world data, we argue that future research on learning dynamical systems should primarily focus on methods that are robust to noisy observations.

Appendix E
Random Test Mode

As shown in
Figure

13
, there is virtually no difference between testing on a time series that continues from the training data and testing with randomly chosen initial conditions on the attractor. From a theoretical standpoint, this is expected: the system’s propagator, which we estimate, is a function
\Phi_{\Delta\!t}
satisfying
u(t+\Delta\!t)=\Phi_{\Delta\!t}(u(t))
, meaning it only requires the current state to predict the next one.

As seen in
Table

1
, Reservoir Computers and Recurrent Neural Networks (such as LSTMs) are commonly used in the field. These methods effectively implement a function
\Psi_{\Delta\!t}
of the form
[u(t+\Delta\!t),r(t+\Delta\!t)]=\Psi_{\Delta\!t}(u(t),r(t))
, where
r
represents the reservoir or memory. While the initial state
u(0)
is typically given, there is no straightforward way to set
r(0)
. In sequential test settings,
r
is often initialized using its final value from the training phase. In random test settings starting from an arbitrary initial state, it is usually assumed that a longer segment in the immediate past of the test data is available to ”warm up” the reservoir.

In contrast, the polynomial propagator method does not require such a warm-up period. It performs optimally using only a single state vector as the initial condition.

Appendix F
Effect of Data Normalization

We compare different data normalization schemes in
Figure

14
. See
Section

2.4
for a description of the different normalization schemes. Full normalization—where data is linearly transformed to obtain an identity covariance matrix—yields the best performance. Since the underlying function being approximated remains the same, normalization affects only the numerical stability of the linear system solved during the least squares fitting of the propagator. Given that numerical accuracy appears to be the primary limiting factor in this study, the benefits of improved stability through better normalization are clearly reflected in the results. However, when using multi-precision arithmetic with double-precision input data, the increased numerical accuracy inherently stabilizes the computations, making normalization unnecessary.

Appendix G
Polynomial ODE Solution

In this section, we show that applying an RK4 ODE solver to the L63 system for
k
steps yields a polynomial of degree
F_{4k+2}
in the initial state, where
F_{\ell}
is the
\ell
-th Fibonacci number. In particular, one RK4 step is a degree
F_{6}=8
polynomial and two steps result in degree
F_{10}=55
.

Define a single step in the the 4th order Runge–Kutta procedure
\mathsf{RK4}\!\left(f,u_{0},h,1\right)
for an ODE of the form
\dot{u}=f(u)
with initial conditions
u_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and time step
h:=\Delta\!t\in\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle w_{1}

\displaystyle=f(u_{0}),

\displaystyle w_{2}

\displaystyle=f\!\left(u_{0}+\frac{1}{2}hw_{1}\right),

\displaystyle w_{3}

\displaystyle=f\!\left(u_{0}+\frac{1}{2}hw_{2}\right),

\displaystyle w_{4}

\displaystyle=f\!\left(u_{0}+hw_{3}\right),

\displaystyle\mathsf{RK4}\!\left(f,u_{0},h,1\right)

\displaystyle=u_{0}+\frac{1}{6}h(w_{1}+2w_{2}+2w_{3}+w_{4})\,.

The
k
-th step in the RK4 procedure is defined recursively by
\mathsf{RK4}\!\left(f,u_{0},h,0\right)=u_{0}
and

\mathsf{RK4}\!\left(f,u_{0},h,k\right)=\mathsf{RK4}\!\left(f,\mathsf{RK4}\!\left(f,u_{0},h,k-1\right),h,1\right)\,.

The L63 system is described by the ODE
\dot{u}=f_{\mathsf{L63}}(u)
, where

f_{\mathsf{L63}}\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{bmatrix}\right):=\begin{bmatrix}a(y-x)\\
x(b-z)-y\\
xy-cz\end{bmatrix}

with
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}
. The default parameter values are
a=10
,
b=28
,
c=\frac{8}{3}
.

The following theorem presents explicit expressions for the leading terms of the polynomial obtained by applying one RK4 step to the L63 system.

Theorem G.1
.

Let

\begin{bmatrix}\zeta_{1}\\
\zeta_{2}\\
\zeta_{3}\end{bmatrix}:=\mathsf{RK4}\!\left(f_{\mathsf{L63}},\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{bmatrix},h,1\right)\,.

Define
\alpha:=2h^{-1}a^{-1}-1
.
Then

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{h^{7}a^{3}}{192}x^{2}z^{2}(y+\alpha x)+q_{1}(x,y,z)\,,

\displaystyle\zeta_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{11}a^{4}}{1536}x^{3}y^{2}z(y+\alpha x)^{2}+q_{2}(x,y,z)\,,

\displaystyle\zeta_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{11}a^{4}}{1536}x^{3}yz^{2}(y+\alpha x)^{2}+q_{3}(x,y,z)\,,

where
q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}
are polynomials with
\deg(q_{1})\leq 4
and
\deg(q_{2}),\deg(q_{3})\leq 7
.

Proof.

We calculate the polynomial results of each intermediate step of the RK4 procedure. We use
P_{\ell}
in an expression for an arbitrary polynomial in
x,y,z
of degree at most
\ell
. Each occurrence of an expression
P_{\ell}
may refer to a different polynomial. Set
u_{0}:=(x,y,z)
. We calculate:

w_{1}=f_{\mathsf{L63}}(u_{0})=\begin{bmatrix}a(y-x)\\
x(b-z)-y\\
xy-cz\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a(y-x)\\
-xz+P_{1}\\
xy+P_{1}\end{bmatrix}\,,

w_{2}=f_{\mathsf{L63}}\!\left(u_{0}+\frac{1}{2}hw_{1}\right)=f_{\mathsf{L63}}\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}2^{-1}ha(y+\alpha x)\\
-2^{-1}hxz+P_{1}\\
2^{-1}hxy+P_{1}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}-2^{-1}haxz+P_{1}\\
-2^{-2}h^{2}axy(y+\alpha x)+P_{2}\\
-2^{-2}h^{2}axz(y+\alpha x)+P_{2}\end{bmatrix}\,,

w_{3}=f_{\mathsf{L63}}\!\left(u_{0}+\frac{1}{2}hw_{2}\right)=f_{\mathsf{L63}}\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}-2^{-2}h^{2}axz+P_{1}\\
-2^{-3}h^{3}axy(y+\alpha x)+P_{2}\\
-2^{-3}h^{3}axz(y+\alpha x)+P_{2}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}-2^{-3}h^{3}a^{2}xy(y+\alpha x)+P_{2}\\
-2^{-5}h^{5}a^{2}x^{2}z^{2}(y+\alpha x)+P_{3}\\
2^{-5}h^{5}a^{2}x^{2}yz(y+\alpha x)+P_{3}\end{bmatrix}\,,

w_{4}=f_{\mathsf{L63}}\!\left(u_{0}+hw_{3}\right)=f_{\mathsf{L63}}\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}-2^{-3}h^{4}a^{2}xy(y+\alpha x)+P_{2}\\
-2^{-5}h^{6}a^{2}x^{2}z^{2}(y+\alpha x)+P_{3}\\
2^{-5}h^{6}a^{2}x^{2}yz(y+\alpha x)+P_{3}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}-2^{-5}h^{6}a^{3}x^{2}z^{2}(y+\alpha x)+P_{4}\\
2^{-8}h^{10}a^{4}x^{3}y^{2}z(y+\alpha x)^{2}+P_{7}\\
2^{-8}h^{10}a^{4}x^{3}yz^{2}(y+\alpha x)^{2}+P_{7}\end{bmatrix}\,,

\mathsf{RK4}\!\left(f_{\mathsf{L63}},u_{0},h,1\right)=u_{0}+\frac{1}{6}h(w_{1}+2w_{2}+2w_{3}+w_{4})=\frac{1}{6}h\begin{bmatrix}-2^{-5}h^{6}a^{3}x^{2}z^{2}(y+\alpha x)+P_{4}\\
2^{-8}h^{10}a^{4}x^{3}y^{2}z(y+\alpha x)^{2}+P_{7}\\
2^{-8}h^{10}a^{4}x^{3}yz^{2}(y+\alpha x)^{2}+P_{7}\end{bmatrix}\,.

With
2^{5}\cdot 6=192
and
2^{8}\cdot 6=1536
, we have shown the theorem.
∎

Using
Theorem

G.1
, we can give explicit expressions in terms of Fibonacci numbers for the degree of the polynomial obtained by applying
k
RK4 steps to the L63 equations.
Denote the Fibonacci sequence as
(F_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, where
F_{k+1}=F_{k}+F_{k-1}
with
F_{0}=0
and
F_{1}=1
.

Theorem G.2
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let

\begin{bmatrix}\zeta_{1}\\
\zeta_{2}\\
\zeta_{3}\end{bmatrix}:=\mathsf{RK4}\!\left(f_{\mathsf{L63}},\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{bmatrix},h,k\right)\,.

Assume
a,h\neq 0
.
Then

\deg(\zeta_{1})=F_{4k+1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\deg(\zeta_{2})=\deg(\zeta_{3})=F_{4k+2}\,.

Proof.

First note that, by
Theorem

G.1
, one RK4 step has the form

\mathsf{RK4}\!\left(f_{\mathsf{L63}},\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{bmatrix},h,1\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{1}x^{2}yz^{2}+q_{1}(x,y,z)\\
\gamma_{2}x^{3}y^{4}z+q_{2}(x,y,z)\\
\gamma_{3}x^{3}y^{3}z^{2}+q_{3}(x,y,z)\end{bmatrix}

with
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
and polynomials
q_{1}
,
q_{2}
,
q_{3}
that cannot cancel their respective first term as they contain different monomials. As we assume
h,a\neq 0
, we have
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}\neq 0
.
Now, we prove the statement of the theorem by induction over
k
:
The induction base with
k=1
follows directly from
Theorem

G.1
with the arguments given above:

\displaystyle\deg(\gamma_{1}x^{2}yz^{2}+q_{1}(x,y,z))

\displaystyle=5=F_{5}\,,

\displaystyle\deg(\gamma_{2}x^{3}y^{4}z+q_{2}(x,y,z))

\displaystyle=8=F_{6}\,,

\displaystyle\deg(\gamma_{3}x^{3}y^{3}z^{2}+q_{3}(x,y,z))

\displaystyle=8=F_{6}\,.

For the induction step, let

\begin{bmatrix}\zeta_{k,1}\\
\zeta_{k,2}\\
\zeta_{k,3}\end{bmatrix}:=\mathsf{RK4}\!\left(f_{\mathsf{L63}},\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{bmatrix},h,k\right)

and assume
\deg(\zeta_{k-1,1})=F_{4(k-1)+1}
and
\deg(\zeta_{k-1,2})=\deg(\zeta_{k-1,3})=F_{4(k-1)+2}
.
Applying
Theorem

G.1
to the
k
-th RK4 solver step yields

\mathsf{RK4}\!\left(f_{\mathsf{L63}},\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{bmatrix},h,k\right)=\mathsf{RK4}\!\left(f_{\mathsf{L63}},\begin{bmatrix}\zeta_{k-1,1}\\
\zeta_{k-1,2}\\
\zeta_{k-1,3}\end{bmatrix},h,1\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{1}\zeta_{k-1,1}^{2}\zeta_{k-1,2}\zeta_{k-1,3}^{2}+\tilde{q}_{1}(x,y,z)\\
\gamma_{2}\zeta_{k-1,1}^{3}\zeta_{k-1,2}^{4}\zeta_{k-1,3}+\tilde{q}_{2}(x,y,z)\\
\gamma_{3}\zeta_{k-1,1}^{3}\zeta_{k-1,2}^{3}\zeta_{k-1,3}^{2}+\tilde{q}_{3}(x,y,z)\end{bmatrix}\,.

The polynomials
\tilde{q}_{1},\tilde{q}_{2},\tilde{q}_{3}
are of lower degree than their respective
\zeta
-terms and, thus, cannot cancel the leading monomials in the
\zeta
-terms.
Therefore, using the induction hypothesis,

\displaystyle\deg(\zeta_{k,1})

\displaystyle=2\deg(\zeta_{k-1,1})+\deg(\zeta_{k-1,2})+2\deg(\zeta_{k-1,3})

\displaystyle=2F_{4(k-1)+1}+3F_{4(k-1)+2}

\displaystyle=2F_{4(k-1)+3}+F_{4(k-1)+2}

\displaystyle=F_{4(k-1)+3}+F_{4(k-1)+4}

\displaystyle=F_{4k+1}

and

\displaystyle\deg(\zeta_{k,2})

\displaystyle=3\deg(\zeta_{k-1,1})+4\deg(\zeta_{k-1,2})+\deg(\zeta_{k-1,3})

\displaystyle=3F_{4(k-1)+1}+5F_{4(k-1)+2}

\displaystyle=3F_{4(k-1)+3}+2F_{4(k-1)+2}

\displaystyle=F_{4(k-1)+3}+2F_{4(k-1)+4}

\displaystyle=F_{4(k-1)+4}+F_{4(k-1)+5}

\displaystyle=F_{4k+2}\,.

The calculation for
\deg(\zeta_{k,3})=F_{4k+2}
is almost the same as for
\deg(\zeta_{k,2})
.
∎
