Early Suture Thread for Port Closure in Robotic Surgery
Authors: Yusuke Goto, Yasunori Matsumoto, Ken Wakai, Ryuji Oka, Hiroto Kato, Yukio Naya
Published: 2026

Abbreviation

robot‐assisted radical prostatectomy

Port‐site hernia after robot‐assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP) is rare, with an incidence rate of 0.4%–0.66% [1]. However, several case reports have emphasized the importance of secure port closure to prevent port‐site hernia, which can be a life‐threatening comorbidity [2, 3]. Although EndoClose is widely used for the closure, it can be difficult to advance through the abdominal wall and retrieve the suture at the end of surgery, particularly when the specimen bag lies close to the abdominal wall or when surgeons experience fatigue (Figure 1a). In this report, we describe a simple method to reduce these challenges by placing the suture thread at the beginning of the surgery.

Early suture thread for closure of the AirSeal port. (a) Conventional method of closing the AirSeal port. Closure at the end of the surgery is often difficult when the specimen bag is close to the abdominal wall. The blue arrow indicates the port. (b) Novel method: The suture thread is placed immediately after the insertion of the port. After placing the robotic ports, the 12‐mm AirSeal port is pushed through the abdominal wall. (c) AirSeal port is removed immediately. (d) EndoClose is inserted through the same tract created by the AirSeal port and advanced to the depth of the peritoneum. (e, f) The suture thread is pulled through cranial and caudal sides using 2‐0 Vicryl, without tying, leaving the end of the suture outside the patient's body. (g) The AirSeal port is reinserted through the same tract again. (h) After completion of RARP, the AirSeal port is removed and the 2‐0 Vicryl is sutured. Closure is confirmed by directly observing the port (blue arrow).

We use the daVinci X surgical system (Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) with four robotic 8‐mm ports. For the assistant port, a 12‐mm AirSeal Access Port (CONMED Corporation, Largo, FL, USA) is placed on the right side of the abdominal wall, along with a 5‐mm VersaOne optical trocar (Medtronic, Ireland). After inserting the robotic ports, the 12‐mm AirSeal port is advanced through the abdominal wall and then removed immediately (Figure 1b,c). EndoClose is then inserted through the same access point and advanced to the depth of the peritoneum (Figure 1d). Using EndoClose, a 2‐0 Vicryl thread is passed through the peritoneum from the cranial side and retrieved from the caudal side, leaving both ends of the thread outside the patient's body without tying (Figure 1e,f). The AirSeal port is then reinserted through the same tract again, and the surgery is performed (Figure 1g). After completing RARP, the third and left‐hand instruments are removed, and the robot is undocked to provide an unobstructed view of the AirSeal port site. Following the removal of the AirSeal port, we suture the 2‐0 Vicryl and confirm port‐site closure under direct visualization (Figure 1h). Port‐site closure is further verified by inserting a finger into the tract, after which the fascia is closed.

Between March 2025 and November 2025, we performed 41 RARP procedures using this technique for patients with prostate cancer. The procedures involved five console surgeons and six assistant surgeons. The median time for suture placement at the beginning of surgery was 92 s (range: 31–200 s), and the median time for port closure at the end of the surgery was 29 s (range: 15–72 s). The total additional time to close the AirSeal port with this technique was 120 s (range: 62–240 s). Procedural time varies according to surgical proficiency. Although complications such as instrument interference, contamination, or entanglement could theoretically occur, none were observed in this study. We also applied this technique in four cases undergoing robot‐assisted radical cystectomy (RARC) in this period. This approach is particularly useful when operative time is prolonged, and the surgeons experience fatigue, as is often the case in RARC with intracorporeal urinary diversion.

The need for port closure after RARP remains controversial. In particular, port‐site hernia occurring at laterally positioned ports is reported to be less common than at centrally located ports, as the multiple layers of the lateral flank musculature tend to close upon removal of the trocar [3]. However, the risk increases with larger ports. A systematic review recommends closure of ports larger than 10 mm to prevent port‐site hernia [4]. The 12‐mm AirSeal port is useful for maintaining air pressure and removing surgical mist during robotic surgery; however, caution is needed because its outer diameter is 16 mm, exceeding the inner diameter of 13.3 mm. Similarly, most 12‐mm trocars have outer diameters of approximately 15 mm (range, 14.4–16.8 mm) [5]. A previous report described robotic peritoneal closure by switching the fourth robotic arm to the master controller [6]. In comparison, our early thread technique offers a simple and feasible method for port closure and is potentially applicable across a range of robotic surgeries. Furthermore, several case reports have documented port‐site hernias occurring at 8‐mm port sites [7]. Although the need for closure of 8‐mm ports also remains controversial, this technique can be applied to close such ports. A limitation of this study is that the long‐term effectiveness of this technique in preventing port‐site hernias remains unclear. Given the relatively short median follow‐up period of 8.3 months, the potential for late‐onset hernias cannot be ruled out. Further studies with longer follow‐up periods are warranted to confirm the long‐term efficacy of this technique in preventing port‐site hernias. In conclusion, our method provides a feasible option for port closure.
