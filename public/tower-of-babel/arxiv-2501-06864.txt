Fisher’s Randomization Test for Causality with General Types of Treatments
Zhen Zhong, Shan Huang, Donald B. Rubin

Abstract
Researchers has long been focusing on causal inference with binary or categorical treatments, where causal estimands are well understood and inference tools are rich. However, causal problems involving continuous treatments are common in practice, yet a formal framework is scarce in the literature. We extend classic Fisher’s randomization test to address an initial question: does a treatment have effect on the outcome of interest conditional on a set of covariates. Our theory starts from randomized experiments and generalizes to observational studies. Inference tools are developed to establish causal relationships and to verify underlying assumptions. Keywords Hypothesis testing, unconfoundedness, Bayes factors, sensitivity analysis



1
Introduction

Fisher’s randomization test (FRT,
Fisher 1935
) is exact under the sharp null hypothesis. Because it does not rely on any distributional assumptions, but only on how the experiment is carried out. The sharp null hypothesis, however, is restrictive and has been dismissed as ”uninteresting and academic”
(
Neyman et al. 1935
)
. As a result, recent literature
(
Wu & Ding 2021
;
Caughey et al. 2023
, e.g.)
has focused on carefully designed test statistics to extend the usefulness of Fisher’s randomization tests for meaningful hypotheses in finite-population settings.

In this paper, we recast FRT in a perspective that combines sampling units from a population and randomizing general types of treatments, e.g., a continuous one. When units are sampled from a population, we don’t impose distributional assumptions such as independent and identical sampling. We then introduce a hypothesis on conditional independence between observed outcomes and treatments, and show that FRT is exact under such a hypothesis. We build a framework for both randomized experiments and observational studies and introduce a weak unconfoundedness assumption, under which conditional independence provides a more fundamental statement on the absence of causality, and rejecting it is a meaningful causal statement.

A critical issue in FRT is the choice of test statistics.
Fisher 1947
has noted that the same data may contradict the hypothesis in different ways using different test statistics. He makes no specific suggestion since

… (the experimenter) is aware of what observational discrepancy it is which interests him, and which he thinks may be statistically significant, before he inquires what test of significance, if any, is available appropriate to his needs.

In this paper, we consider a scenario where experimenter’s causality of interest is expressed by a family of parametric models. Based on
Jeffreys 1935
’s Bayesian hypothesis testing framework, we define the prior and conditional probabilities of competing hypotheses. The choice of test statistics is then devoted to maximizing the percent increase in probability of establishing such causality. We conclude with a recommendation model-based test statistics.

Unconfoundedness is an untestable assumption in observational studies
(
Imbens & Rubin 2015
)
. Since
Cornfield et al. 1959
, sensitivity analysis has become a unique tool to examine how conclusions may change when unconfoundedness is violated at different levels.
Rosenbaum 1995
describes sensitivity analysis for
p
-values by bounding the difference between two estimated propensity odds ratios.
Imbens & Rubin 2015
modifies
Rosenbaum 1995
’s approach to a Cornfield-type analysis for Fisher’s
p
-value.
VanderWeele & Ding 2017
extend
Cornfield et al. 1959
’s analysis by introducing E-value as the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the treatment and outcome, conditional on the measured covariates, to fully explain away a specific treatment–outcome association. These approaches are partially or fully limited to binary outcomes, treatments and confounders. In our framework, sensitivity analysis is carried out with no such limitation. We revisit
Imbens et al. 2001
’s example to show how it works.

Related Literature.
Model-based FRT have been used to test Fisher’s sharp null hypothesis with noncompliance
(
Rubin 1998
)
and to test Neyman’s weak null hypothesis in observational studies
(
Ding & Guo 2023
)
. There lacks a frequentist theory in these approaches. Another related literature is about conditional permutation tests
(
Barber & Candès 2015
;
Candes et al. 2018
;
Berrett et al. 2020
;
Łazȩcka et al. 2023
)
. These tests are shown to be valid in testing conditional independence in non-causal settings. However, what implications of conditional independence to causal inference remain unexplored.

Causal Problems

Consider a sample of
n
units indexed by
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. Let
\mathcal{W}
denote a treatment space and
w\in\mathcal{W}
a specific version of treatment. In treatment-versus-control experiments,
\mathcal{W}=\{0,1\}
and
w
can be
0
or
1
. In factorial experiments with
k
factors,
\mathcal{W}=\{0,1\}^{k}
and
w
can be any
k
-dimensional treatment vector of
0
’s and
1
’s. Also,
\mathcal{W}
can be an interval of continuous dose of a drug, in which
w
represents a specific dose.

Throughout this paper, potential outcomes are subject to a generalized version of Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA,
Rubin 1980
). First, for each unit, the potential outcomes do not vary with the treatments assigned to other units. Second, given an outcome space
\mathcal{Y}
, the outcome of unit
i
is the result of a potential outcome function that maps
w\in\mathcal{W}
to
Y_{i}(w)\in\mathcal{Y}
.

2.2
Sampling and Assignment Mechanisms

Rubin 1978
characterizes two sources of uncertainty in a randomized experiments for categorical treatments. Firstly, the
n
units are usually sampled from a population
\mathcal{P}
of interest, which can be a pool of
N\geq n
units, or an imaginary super-population of infinite units. Secondly, treatment assignment to the
n
units, if not fully determined by rules, contributes to another source of uncertainty. Consider a joint space
\mathcal{P}^{n}\times\mathcal{W}^{n}
, in which each point represents
n
unit-treatment pairs. We equip the space with a
\sigma
-algebra and a probability measure to turn it into a probability space. We then introduce random variables defined on this probability space.

For fixed
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{W}^{n}
, let
\boldsymbol{Y}(\boldsymbol{w})
denote the potential outcome vector under
\boldsymbol{w}
, with the
i
th element being
Y_{i}(w_{i})
. Once sampled from a population, covariates and potential outcomes are fixed, so that uncertainty in
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}(\boldsymbol{w})
comes solely from repeated sampling from the population.

Let
\boldsymbol{W}
denote the treatment vector where the
i
th element
W_{i}
is the treatment assigned to unit
i
in a randomized experiment. At the design stage, given observed covariates, an assignment mechanism is usually created with a specification of the density function
f(\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{X})
. A Bernoulli design, e.g., assigns each unit with an independent Bernoulli trial, so that

f(\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{X})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{W_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{(1-W_{i})}.

Two features make the assignment mechanism important. First, it is known explicitly in a randomized experiment. Second, it is conditionally independent of the (unknown) sampling mechanism given covariates, so we can write
f(\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{Y}(\boldsymbol{w}),\boldsymbol{X})=f(\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{X})
for any fixed
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{W}^{n}
.

Let
\boldsymbol{Y}
denote the observed outcome vector with the
i
-th element being
Y_{i}=Y_{i}(W_{i})
under the generalized SUTVA. Uncertainty in
\boldsymbol{Y}
comes from both repeated sampling from the population and repeated assignment.

2.3
Hypotheses

Fisher 1935
introduces the sharp null hypothesis without using potential outcomes to carry out an exact test. For a general type of treatment, the hypothesis can be stated as follows.

Hypothesis 1

(
No Individual Treatment Effects Under SUTVA
)
.

For
i=1,\ldots,n
,
Y_{i}(w_{i})=Y_{i}(w_{i}^{\prime})
for all
w_{i},w_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}
.

A test statistic is defined as a real-valued measurable function

T:\mathcal{Y}^{n}\times\mathcal{W}^{n}\times\mathcal{X}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}.

Let
\boldsymbol{W}^{\text{rep}}
denote an assignment vector drawn from
f(\boldsymbol{W}^{\text{rep}}\mid\boldsymbol{X})
. A Fisher’s
p
-value is defined as

p=\Pr\left(T(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{W}^{\text{rep}},\boldsymbol{X})\geq T(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{X})\mid\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{X}\right),

(1)

where the probability is taken over repeated draw of
\boldsymbol{W}^{\text{rep}}
. The
p
-value is dominated by a uniform distribution under Hypothesis
1
, meaning that for fixed
\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{X}
, the probability of
p\leq\alpha
under repeated draw of
\boldsymbol{W}
does not exceed
\alpha
for any
\alpha\in[0,1]
. However, as Hypothesis
1
is to sharp to be plausible in practice,
Fisher 1935
tends to view the exact test as a measure of discrepancy of experimental results from this hypothesis.

A key limitation in
Fisher 1935
’s approach is that the causal estimand is defined at the sample level. We instead focus on a hypothesis at the population level, stating that observed outcomes are conditional independent of treatments given covariates.

Hypothesis 2
.

\boldsymbol{Y}\perp\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{X}
.

In a randomized experiment, Hypothesis
2
is implied by Hypothesis
1
, but not vice versa unless the population of interest is the sample at hand. A counter example can be given as follows. For each unit
i
, if
Y_{i}(1)
and
Y_{i}(0)
follow i.i.d. standard normal distributions, the observed outcomes follows the same distribution regardless of treatment assignment, yet
\Pr(Y_{i}(1)=Y_{i}(0))=0
.

The next lemma tells that Fisher’s
p
-value under Hypothesis
2
is also dominated by a uniform distribution, which does not require a known sampling mechanism.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that, under repeated sampling and assignment, the joint distribution of
(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{X})
admits a density function. Under Hypothesis
2
, the Fisher’s
p
-value defined in (
1
) satisfies

\Pr(p\leq\alpha)\leq\alpha

for any
\alpha\in[0,1]
, where the probability is taken over the joint distribution of
(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{X})
.

Special forms of Lemma
1
have been obtained by
Barber & Candès 2015
;
Candes et al. 2018
;
Berrett et al. 2020
for i.i.d. variables, and
Łazȩcka et al. 2023
for discrete variables. Our result is more general since we make milder restrictions on the joint distribution of sampling and assignment mechanisms.

3
Weak Unconfoundedness and its Implications

In observational studies, there is no simple way to separate assignment mechanisms from sampling mechanisms. As a consequence, Simpson’s paradox
(
Simpson 1951
)
arises because it’s possible to have
Y_{i}\perp W_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
without
Y_{i}\perp W_{i}
, and conversely
(
Dawid 1979
)
, which makes Hypothesis
2
meaningless to reject without specifying a proper set of conditioning covariates.

For a binary treatment,
Rosenbaum & Rubin 1983
introduce the strong ignorability assumption as a reasonable basis for causal inference, which consists of two parts:

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{Y}(0),\boldsymbol{Y}(1))\perp\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{X}\text{ (unconfoundedness)},

(2)

\displaystyle 0<f(\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{X})<1\text{ (overlap)}.

(3)

As
Rosenbaum & Rubin 1983
’s unconfoundedness is not adequate for general types of treatments,
Hirano & Imbens 2004
provide a weak unconfoundedness assumption: an assignment mechanism is weakly unconfounded given
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
if
Y_{i}(w)\perp W_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
for all
w\in\mathcal{W}
. This assumption, however, is imposed at the unit level, which is often accompanied with additional assumptions such as i.i.d. units. We propose a weak unconfoundedness assumption at the sample level, allowing for general sampling mechanisms.

Assumption 1
.

For any fixed
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{W}^{n}
,
\boldsymbol{Y}(\boldsymbol{w})\perp\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{X}
.

It can be verified that Assumption
1
holds automatically in randomized experiments with controlled assignment mechanisms, because the only source of uncertainty in
\boldsymbol{W}
comes from repeated assignment given
\boldsymbol{X}
, which is independent of the repeated sampling of
\boldsymbol{Y}(\boldsymbol{w})
. The next theorem provides an alternative explanation to Hypothesis
2
, generalizing it from randomized experiments to observational studies with weakly unconfounded assignment mechanism.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that, for any fixed
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{W}^{n}
, the joint distribution of
(\boldsymbol{Y}(\boldsymbol{w}),\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{X})
admits a density function.
Under Assumption
1
, the following two statements are equivalent:

1.

\boldsymbol{Y}\perp\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{X}
.

2.

For any fixed
\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}^{n}
, if
f(\boldsymbol{w}\mid\boldsymbol{X})\cdot f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\mid\boldsymbol{X})>0
, then

\boldsymbol{Y}(\boldsymbol{w})\mid\boldsymbol{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\boldsymbol{Y}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})\mid\boldsymbol{X},

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
means that two distributions are identical.

For each unit
i
, the second statement in Theorem
1
implies no conditional average treatment effect:

\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(w_{i})-Y_{i}(w_{i}^{\prime})\mid\boldsymbol{X}_{i}]=0.

(4)

Rejecting
\boldsymbol{Y}\perp\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{X}
does not necessarily mean rejecting (
4
) for some pair of treatments. Because two distributions may differ in other ways, such as conditional variances. In the next section, we develop a Bayesian framework for demonstrating the causality of interest in a systematic way.

4
Choice of Test Statistics

Neyman & Pearson 1933
introduce errors of type II as the frequency of falsely accepting the null hypothesis under true alternative hypotheses.
Fisher 1955
criticizes this concept, arguing that errors of type II depend not only on the frequency with which alternative hypotheses are in fact true, but also greatly on how closely they resemble the null hypothesis. As for detailed choice of tests that may be sensitive to different alternatives, Fisher would rather leave it a subjective decision because an experienced experimenter knows what is the appropriates
(
Lehmann 1993
)
. We develop a new concept to replace the error of type II for the choice of test statistics in FRT. We firstly introduce the concept through a general Bayesian hypothesis testing problem and then focus on FRT as an important special case.

4.1
Probabilities of Hypotheses

Bayesian hypothesis testing, originally introduced by
Jeffreys 1935
, is a methodology of quantifying the evidence in favor of a scientific theory
(
Kass & Raftery 1995
)
. Suppose that
f(z\mid\theta)
is a density function defined on a parameter space
\Theta
. Let
\Theta_{0}\subset\Theta
denote a subspace of parameters and
\Theta_{1}=\Theta\backslash\Theta_{0}
denote its complement. We then have two competing hypotheses
H_{0}:\theta\in\Theta_{0}
versus
H_{1}:\theta\in\Theta_{1}
.

To conduct Bayesian hypothesis testing,
Jeffreys 1935
firstly specifies prior probabilities for true
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
as
\Pr(H_{0})
and
\Pr(H_{1})
, respectively. Given prior measures
\pi_{0}
on
\Theta_{0}
and
\pi_{1}
on
\Theta_{1}
, he then calculates the prior predictive densities of observed data given
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
respectively as

f(z\mid H_{0})=\int_{\Theta_{0}}f\left(z\mid\theta\right)\pi_{0}(d\theta);\quad f(z\mid H_{1})=\int_{\Theta_{1}}f\left(z\mid\theta\right)\pi_{1}(d\theta).

(5)

With observed data
Z
drawn from one of two competing hypotheses, he obtains a ratio of evidence as

\frac{\Pr(H_{1}\mid Z)}{\Pr(H_{0}\mid Z)}=\frac{f(Z\mid H_{1})}{f(Z\mid H_{0})}\frac{\Pr(H_{1})}{\Pr(H_{0})},

in which
f(Z\mid H_{1})/f(Z\mid H_{0})
is commonly known as the Bayes factor.

Jeffreys 1961
and
Kass & Raftery 1995
provide thresholds for drawing conclusions from Bayes factors. On the other hand, frequentists would like to incorporate Bayes factors with repeated sampling process to avoid subjective decisions and reconcile Bayes/non-Bayes way of thinking
(
Good 1992
;
Berger 2003
;
Robnik & Seljak 2022
;
Fowlie 2023
, e.g.)
. Let
\delta(z)
denote a randomized decision rule, which is a measurable function defined on the sample space
\mathcal{Z}
and taking values in
[0,1]
. Had we observed the data
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
, we would reject
H_{0}
if a Bernoulli trial with probability
\delta(Z)
turns out to be
1
. A Bayesian error for falsely rejecting
H_{0}
can be defined as

\Pr(\delta=1\mid H_{0})=\int\Pr(\delta(z)=1)f(z\mid H_{0})dz.

When
H_{0}
is a point null hypothesis,
\Pr(\delta=1\mid H_{0})
reduces to frequentist error of falsely rejecting
H_{0}
. Similarly, a Bayesian error for correctly accepting
H_{1}
can be defined as

\Pr(\delta=1\mid H_{1})=\int\Pr(\delta(z)=1)f(z\mid H_{1})dz.

This quantity, however, is irrelevant to practice unless
f(z\mid H_{1})
describes the true data generating process.

We instead consider the posterior probability of
H_{1}
conditional on rejecting
H_{0}
, i.e.,

\Pr(H_{1}\mid\delta=1)=\frac{\Pr(\delta=1\mid H_{1})\Pr(H_{1})}{\Pr(\delta=1)},

where
\Pr(\delta=1)
is the prior probability of rejecting
H_{0}
:

\Pr(\delta=1)=\Pr(\delta=1\mid H_{0})\Pr(H_{0})+\Pr(\delta=1\mid H_{1})\Pr(H_{1}).

By comparing
\Pr(H_{1}\mid\delta=1)
with the prior probability of
H_{1}
, we introduce the percent increase in probability (PIP) of
H_{1}
as

100\%*\frac{\Pr(H_{1}\mid\delta=1)-\Pr(H_{1})}{\Pr(H_{1})}.

PIP is a measure of the rationale of
\delta(z)
given prior and model specifications for competing hypotheses. It indicates how much increase in our belief of
H_{1}
after observing evidence extracted from a decision rule.

4.2
FRT Using Bayes Factors as Test Statistics

Consider a family of outcome models defined by the conditional density function
f(\boldsymbol{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{X};\theta),\theta\in\Theta
. Let
\Theta_{0}\subset\Theta
denote the subspace on which Hypothesis
2
holds, and
\Theta_{1}=\Theta\backslash\Theta_{0}
denote its complement on which the causality of interest exists. Given prior probabilities
\Pr(\theta\in\Theta_{0})
and
\Pr(\theta\in\Theta_{1})
, our objective is to maximize the PIP of
\theta\in\Theta_{1}
while controlling the frequency of falsely rejecting Hypothesis
2
. We set prior measures
\pi_{0}
on
\Theta_{0}
and
\pi_{1}
on
\Theta_{1}
, and calculate the Bayes factor between models in
\Theta_{1}
and
\Theta_{0}
as

T_{\text{BF}}(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{X})=\frac{f(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{X}\mid\theta\in\Theta_{1})}{f(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{X}\mid\theta\in\Theta_{0})}=\frac{f(\boldsymbol{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{X};\theta\in\Theta_{1})}{f(\boldsymbol{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{X};\theta\in\Theta_{0})}.

While a naive decision rule can be established by comparing
T_{\text{BF}}
with a pre-determined threshold, concerns may be raised due to its reliance on distributional assumptions and the threshold.

We instead build FRT with the test statistic chosen as
T_{\text{BF}}
. Lemma
1
implies that the frequency of FRT for falsely rejecting Hypothesis
2
does not exceed
\alpha
, which holds regardless of the outcome model specification or the choice of test statistics. When the causality of interest is expressed by a family of outcome models, FRT using
T_{\text{BF}}
is optimal among all choice of test statistics for establishing such causality, which we formalize in the next theorem.

Theorem 2
.

If
T_{\text{BF}}
is a real-valued measurable function, then the PIP of
\theta\in\Theta_{1}
is maximized by
T_{\text{BF}}
among all test statistics for FRT at a fixed significance level.

A famous quotes, initially made by George Box (
Box 1976
), is “All models are wrong but some are useful”. In the context of FRT, models parameterized by
\theta\in\Theta
are not necessarily correct, but they formulate the causality of interest and guide the construction of test statistics. In practice, a useful simplification is to pick a model parameterized by
\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}
and calculate

T_{\text{Pos}}(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{X})=-\operatorname{log}\left(f(\theta=\theta_{0}\mid\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{X})\right),

(6)

The next proposition tells that it is equivalent to using
T_{\text{BF}}
or
T_{\text{Pos}}
as a test statistic for FRT.

Proposition 1
.

If both
T_{\text{BF}}
and
T_{\text{Pos}}
are real-valued measurable functions, then Fisher’s
p
-value remains invariant using either
T_{\text{BF}}
or
T_{\text{Pos}}
as a test statistic.

When the analytic form of
f(\theta=\theta_{0}\mid\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{X})
is absent, we could replace it in (
6
) with its Laplace’s approximation. As an alternative choice of test statistics, it doesn’t affect the validity of FRT, but allows a majority class of outcome models to be utilized.

5
Modeling Assignment Mechanisms and Sensitivity Analysis

As exact forms of assignment mechanisms are unknown in observational studies, we need to calculate Fisher’s
p
-values using those modeled by observed data. Consider a class of assignment models with conditional density functions given by
f(\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{X};\psi),\psi\in\Psi
. Let
\widehat{\psi}
be estimated, e.g., from maximum likelihood. Let
\widehat{p}
denote Fisher’s
p
-value with probability taken over
\boldsymbol{W}^{\text{rep}}
drawn from
f(\boldsymbol{W}^{\text{rep}}\mid\boldsymbol{X};\widehat{\psi})
. The next theorem tells that a consistently estimated assignment model is needed to guarantee the asymptotic validity of FRT.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that
f(\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{X};\psi)
is continuous in
\Psi
. If
\widehat{\psi}
converges in probability to
\psi
, then under Hypothesis
2
,

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Pr(\widehat{p}\leq\alpha)\leq\alpha

for any
\alpha\in[0,1]
, where the probability is taken over
f(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{X};\psi)=f(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{X})f(\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{X};\psi)
.

Following
Ding & Guo 2023
, we can also use the posterior distribution
f(\psi\mid\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{X})
for estimating
\psi
, which turns
\widehat{p}
into a posterior predictive
p
-value
(
Meng 1994
)
. Combining Theorem
3
with the Bernstein-von Mises theorem, we know that in large samples, the Bayesian variant of
\widehat{p}
behaves similarly to that with
\psi
estimated by maximum likelihood. In small samples, however, the Bayesian variant should be preferred.

One source of systematic bias in
\widehat{p}
comes from misspecification of the assignment model. Another one comes from possible violation of Assumption
1
, which leads to omitted variable bias in the assignment model. We recommend conducting sensitivity analysis in observational studies to validate the robustness of FRT towards different model specifications and levels of confounding strength. For each unit
i
, suppose that

W_{i}=h(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},E_{i};\psi),\psi\in\Psi,

where
h
is a link function and
E_{i}
is a random error. Let
\boldsymbol{E}
denote the
n
-dimensional vector of random errors for all units, which is assumed to be normally distributed under a certain link function. We decompose
\boldsymbol{E}
as a sum of two uncorrelated normal random variables
\boldsymbol{U}
and
\boldsymbol{V}
that have proportionate covariance matrices. The squared correlation coefficient
\rho^{2}
between
E_{i}
and
U_{i}
serves as a metric of confounding strength.

For a binary confounder and a binary treatment, this metric is a model-based counterpart of
Cornfield et al. 1959
’s relative risk. Let
I(A)
denote the indicator function for an event
A
, which takes
1
on
A
and
0
otherwise. Consider binary
W_{i}
and
D_{i}
that are jointly modeled by

W_{i}=I(a_{w}+E_{i}>0),\quad D_{i}=I(a_{u}+U_{i}>0),

where

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}E_{i}\\
U_{i}\end{pmatrix}\sim N\left(\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\
\rho&1\end{pmatrix}\right).

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\rho\geq 0
, since otherwise we can use
1-D_{i}
instead of
D_{i}
as the confounder.

Cornfield et al. 1959
describe the strength of
D_{i}
using the following relative risk

\frac{\Pr(D_{i}=1\mid W_{i}=1)}{\Pr(D_{i}=1\mid W_{i}=0)}.

Since
\Pr(D_{i}=1\mid W_{i}=1)
is monotonously increasing in
\rho
, and
\Pr(D_{i}=1\mid W_{i}=0)
is monotonously decreasing in
\rho
, we know that the relative risk is monotonously increasing in
\rho^{2}
.

Let
\boldsymbol{E}^{\text{rep}}
denote a repeated draw of the error term conditional on
\boldsymbol{U}
. Because
\boldsymbol{E}^{\text{rep}}
and
\boldsymbol{E}
are both correlated with
\boldsymbol{U}
by a squared multiple correlation coefficient
\rho^{2}
, we know that
\boldsymbol{E}^{\text{rep}}=|\rho|\boldsymbol{E}+\boldsymbol{V}^{\text{rep}}
,
where
\boldsymbol{V}^{\text{rep}}
is uncorrelated with
\boldsymbol{E}
and has a covariance matrix proportionate to that of
\boldsymbol{E}
by
1-\rho^{2}
. Let
E_{i}^{\text{rep}}
denote the
i
-th element of
\boldsymbol{E}^{\text{rep}}
. Conditional on a posterior draw of
\psi^{\text{pos}}
, a repeated draw of the assignment vector
\boldsymbol{W}^{\text{rep}}
can be constructed with the
i
-th element given by

W_{i}^{\text{rep}}=h(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},E_{i}^{\text{rep}};\psi^{\text{pos}}),\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

(7)

Let
\widehat{p}(\rho^{2})
denote Fisher’s
p
-value with probability taken over
\boldsymbol{W}^{\text{rep}}
drawn from (
7
). When
\rho^{2}=0
, unobserved confounding is ignored in (
7
), and
\widehat{p}(0)
reduces to
\widehat{p}
derived under Assumption
1
. When
\rho^{2}=1
, there is no randomness in treatment assignment for unveiling causal relationships. We plot
\widehat{p}(\rho^{2})
against
\rho^{2}
as a sensitivity curve. A minimal
\rho^{2}_{\alpha}
for
\widehat{p}(\rho^{2}_{\alpha})
to exceed a pre-specified significance level
\alpha
indicates the minimal strength that an unobserved confounder could overturn a significant causal relationship. As an analytical form of
\widehat{p}(\rho^{2})
is almost intractable, we provide a simulation-based approach through the following example.

6
A Realistic Example

Imbens et al. 2001
use a survey of lottery players to analyze the effects of unearned income on labor earnings, savings and consumption. The dataset consists of “nonwinners” and “winners” samples, with the latter contains a subsample of “big winners”. The nonwinners receive no yearly prize, although they actually won at least one small, one-time prize, ranging from
\$100
to
\$5,000
. The winners have won a yearly prize paid out over
20
years, with the total prize ranging from
\$22,000
to
\$9,696,000
, and
\$2,000,000
to
\$9,696,000
for the big winners.

Imbens et al. 2001
specify a linear outcome model over the yearly prize
W_{i}
and additional covariates:

Y_{ij}=a+bW_{i}+\boldsymbol{d}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}+\varepsilon_{ij},

(8)

where
a,b,\boldsymbol{d}
are constants and
\varepsilon_{ij}
is a random error. The index
i,j
indicates unit
i
in the
j
-th post-lottery year, with
j
ranging from
0
to
6
. The coefficient
b
is a product of two parameters in the original model, which are not separated in estimation.
Imbens et al. 2001
use standard ordinary least square (OLS) estimators to investigate the effect of
W_{i}
on
Y_{ij}
. The critical assumption is that the magnitude of the prize is randomly assigned. However, the chance for winning a major prize appears to depend on covariates like age and years of schooling, which is likely due to the difference between season ticket holders and single ticket buyers. Another source of unobserved confounding comes from non-respondents, who are discarded in this study.

We apply FRT accompanied with sensitivity analysis to verify the conclusions drawn by
Imbens et al. 2001
. Assuming
\varepsilon_{ij}\sim N(0,\sigma_{j}^{2})
in the outcome model, we specify a non-informative prior as
\pi(a,b,\boldsymbol{d},\sigma_{j})\propto\sigma_{j}^{-2}
. Let
\boldsymbol{Y}_{j}
denote the outcome vector in period
j
with the
i
-th position being
Y_{ij}
. To test whether
b=0
, we calculate
T_{\text{Pos}}
using data in period
j
as

\displaystyle T_{\text{Pos},j}

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{log}(f(b=0\mid\boldsymbol{Y}_{j},\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{X}))

(9)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{log}(\widehat{\sigma}_{j}^{2})+\frac{n}{2}\operatorname{log}\left(1+\widehat{b}_{j}^{2}/\widehat{\sigma}_{j}^{2}\right)+\text{constant},

where
\widehat{b}_{j},\widehat{\sigma}_{j}^{2}
are homoscedastic OLS estimators for
b,\sigma_{j}^{2}
using data in period
j
, respectively, and the constant term is not affected by
\boldsymbol{W}
.

Let
K_{i}
be a binary indicator for winning the yearly prize
(K_{i}=1)
or not
(K_{i}=0)
. We build an assignment model for each unit
i
as

\displaystyle K_{i}

\displaystyle=I(a_{k}+\boldsymbol{d}_{k}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}+\xi_{i}>0),

(10)

\displaystyle\operatorname{log}(W_{i})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}a_{l}+\boldsymbol{d}_{l}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}+\nu_{i},\quad\text{if }K_{i}=1\\
-\infty,\quad\text{if }K_{i}=0,\end{cases}

where
a_{k},a_{w},\boldsymbol{d}_{k},\boldsymbol{d}_{w}
are constants,
\xi_{i}\sim N(0,1)
, and
\nu_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2})
. Assuming non-informative priors
\pi(a_{k},\boldsymbol{d}_{k})\propto 1
and
\pi(a_{w},\boldsymbol{d}_{w},\sigma^{2})\propto\sigma^{-2}
, we use a Gibbs sampling algorithm or an analytic form of the posterior distribution to obtain posterior draws of parameters as
a_{k}^{\text{pos}},\boldsymbol{d}_{k}^{\text{pos}},a_{w}^{\text{pos}},\boldsymbol{d}_{w}^{\text{pos}},\sigma^{\text{pos}}
. In addition, we obtain posterior draws of residuals as

\displaystyle\xi_{i}^{\text{pos}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\xi_{i}\mid\xi_{i}>0,\quad\text{if }K_{i}=1\\
\xi_{i}\mid\xi_{i}\leq 0,\quad\text{if }K_{i}=0\end{cases}\quad\text{for }\xi_{i}\sim N(-a_{k}^{\text{pos}}-\boldsymbol{d}_{k}^{\text{pos}}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top},1),

\displaystyle\nu_{i}^{\text{pos}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{log}(W_{i})-a_{l}^{\text{pos}}-\boldsymbol{d}_{l}^{\text{pos}}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top},\quad\text{if }K_{i}=1.

We conduct sensitivity analysis by specifying an unobserved confounding matrix
\boldsymbol{U}
, which is an
n\times 2
dimensional normal random matrix, with
U_{i1}
in the
i
-th row correlated with
\xi_{i}
and
U_{i2}
correlated with
\nu_{i}
. We set
\rho^{2}=\operatorname{var}(U_{i1})/\operatorname{var}(\xi_{i})=\operatorname{var}(U_{i2})/\operatorname{var}(\nu_{i})
, so that the error terms uncorrelated with
\boldsymbol{U}
are
\widetilde{\xi}_{i}\sim N(0,1-\rho^{2})
and
\widetilde{\nu}_{i}\sim N(0,(1-\rho^{2})(\sigma^{\text{pos}})^{2})
. A repeated draw of treatment given observed data comes from

\displaystyle K_{i}^{\text{rep}}

\displaystyle=I\left(a_{k}^{\text{pos}}+\boldsymbol{d}_{k}^{\text{pos}}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}+\rho^{2}\xi_{i}^{\text{pos}}+(1-\rho^{2})\widetilde{\xi}_{i}>0\right),

(11)

\displaystyle\operatorname{log}(W_{i}^{\text{rep}})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}a_{l}^{\text{pos}}+\boldsymbol{d}^{\text{pos}}_{l}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}+\rho^{2}\nu_{i}^{\text{pos}}+(1-\rho^{2})\widetilde{\nu}_{i},\quad\text{if }K_{i}^{\text{rep}}=1\\
-\infty,\quad\text{if }K_{i}^{\text{rep}}=0.\end{cases}

Note that the conditional distribution of
\boldsymbol{W}^{\text{rep}}
is continuous for
\rho^{2}\in[0,1)
, and is discontinuous at
\rho^{2}=1
because it shrinks to a point mass distribution. Applying Scheffé’s theorem
(
Scheffé 1947
)
, we know that
\widehat{p}(\rho^{2})
is a continuous function for
\rho^{2}\in[0,1)
. To estimate
\widehat{p}(\rho^{2})
, we partition
[0,1)
into
M
equal subintervals with endpoints:
\rho^{2}_{1}=0,\ldots,\rho^{2}_{M}=1-1/M,\rho^{2}_{M+1}=1
. At every
m
-th endpoint (
m=1,\ldots,M
), we draw a replicated sample of
T_{\text{Pos},j}^{\text{rep},m}
given
\rho^{2}_{m}
, and compare it with the original
T_{\text{Pos},j}
. We then fit
I\left(T_{\text{Pos},j}^{\text{rep},m}\geq T_{\text{Pos},j}\right)
against
\rho^{2}_{m}
using kernel regression with the bandwidth satisfying
h\rightarrow 0
and
Mh\rightarrow\infty
. Applying the Lindeberg-Feller Theorem
(
Ferguson 1996
, p. 27)
, the fitted curve converges in probability to the underlying sensitivity curve for
\rho^{2}\in[0,1)
as
M\rightarrow\infty
.

Our results are presented as follows. We use Specification IV in Table 4 of
Imbens et al. 2001
as the outcome model, which is fitted with an expanded set of covariates and a complete set of units. Figure

shows sensitivity curves using post-lottery earnings in the
j
-th year as outcomes, where

ranges from
0
to
6
. It can be seen that causal relationships are robust to unobserved confounding from the first year to the fourth year. Figure

shows the minimal strength
\rho^{2}_{0.05}
to overturn causal relationships in Figure
1
, which has an inverted U-shaped curve against time, indicating that unobserved confounding, if exists, decays more fast than the causal relationship.

7
Conclusion

FRT is a design-based inference strategy to test conditional independence between outcomes and treatments in any randomized experiment, which naturally extends to any observational study with unconfounded assignment mechanism. A unique advantage of FRT is that the roles of assignment mechanisms and outcome models are separated. A controlled assignment mechanism guarantees exactness of FRT, while an outcome model, without necessarily being correct, expresses the causality of interest and leads to the test statistic that maximizes percent increase in probability of establishing such causality.

To verify the underlying weak unconfoundedness assumption in an observational study, we propose to draw a sensitivity curve to visualize how strong unobserved confounders could overturn a significant causal relationship. The minimal strength provides an alternative angle besides Fisher’s
p
-value to build and validate causality.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

If
\boldsymbol{Y}\perp\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{X}
, the probability of rejecting
p\leq\alpha
under repeated sampling and randomization can be calculated according to

\displaystyle\int I(p\leq\alpha)f(\boldsymbol{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{X})f(\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{X})f(\boldsymbol{X})d\boldsymbol{Y}d\boldsymbol{W}d\boldsymbol{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\int I(p\leq\alpha)f(\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{X})d\boldsymbol{W}f(\boldsymbol{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{X})f(\boldsymbol{X})d\boldsymbol{Y}d\boldsymbol{X}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int\alpha f(\boldsymbol{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{X})f(\boldsymbol{X})d\boldsymbol{Y}d\boldsymbol{X}=\alpha.

where the inequality is because
p
is dominated by a uniform distribution under repeated randomization for fixed
\boldsymbol{Y}
and
\boldsymbol{X}
.
\square

Proof of Theorem
1
.

We first show that statement 1 implies 2. If
f(\boldsymbol{w}\mid\boldsymbol{X})\cdot f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\mid\boldsymbol{X})>0
, statement 1 implies
f(\boldsymbol{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{W}=\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{X})=f(\boldsymbol{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{W}=\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{X})

(the same for

\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}

We then show that statement 2 implies 1. For fixed

. If

, we have

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{X})

\displaystyle=\int f(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{w}\mid\boldsymbol{X})d\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle=\int f(\boldsymbol{Y}(\boldsymbol{w})\mid\boldsymbol{X})f(\boldsymbol{w}\mid\boldsymbol{X})d\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{Y}(\boldsymbol{w})\mid\boldsymbol{X})\int f(\boldsymbol{w}\mid\boldsymbol{X})d\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{Y}(\boldsymbol{w})\mid\boldsymbol{X}),

Proof of Theorem
2
.
Given
\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{X}
, let
f(\boldsymbol{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{X};\alpha)
denote
(1-\alpha)
-th quantile of the distribution of

drawn from
f(\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{X})

By definition, the probability of

\alpha
under randomization in
\boldsymbol{W}

\alpha f(\boldsymbol{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{X};\alpha)\leq\int f(\boldsymbol{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{X},\theta\in\Theta_{1})f(\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{X})d\boldsymbol{W}.

Because the right hand side is a conditional density function, we know that
\int f(\boldsymbol{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{X};\alpha)d\boldsymbol{Y}=C_{\alpha}\leq\infty

For
\alpha\in[0,1]
, let

denote the space of all FRTs at the significance level

q,\text{ if }p=\alpha^{+}\\
0,\text{ if }p>\alpha^{+}\end{cases},

(12)

where

is determined with

such that

\Pr(\delta=1\mid H_{0})=\int\Pr(\delta=1\mid\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{X})f(\boldsymbol{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{X},H_{0})=\alpha.

Let

\square
