Pediatric resting EEG collection, preprocessing, and analysis: A systematic review
Authors: Kathryn N Gray, Haleigh M Brown, Agostina Mannara, George A Buzzell, Santiago Morales, Sonya V Troller-Renfree
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractResting electroencephalography (EEG) has been posited as a biomarker for children’s cognitive and social development. However, substantial heterogeneity in current EEG data collection, preprocessing, and analytic approaches lowers interpretability and reproducibility across studies. This systematic review aims to identify existing methodological variability and consensus in the recent developmental EEG literature and explore whether any differences can be explained by the age of the sample, year of publication, or the resting paradigm. Reviewed articles were drawn from six journals notable for publishing pediatric EEG research between 2011 and 2023. We screened over 650 articles, resulting in 56 articles for further data extraction. Extracted information included: sample characteristics (e.g. age), data collection paradigm (e.g., resting paradigm), preprocessing and signal analysis steps (e.g., power spectra transformations, frequency band boundaries), and statistical analysis methods (e.g., multiple comparison corrections). Our results highlight substantial differences in data collection, preprocessing, and analytic steps that are largely not explained by sample age, publication year, or resting paradigm. Collectively, our findings emphasize the need for increased standardization in data collection, preprocessing, and analysis methods to ensure future developmental EEG research is reproducible and reliable. We provide a suggested checklist for researchers detailing what to report when publishing resting EEG research and suggest collaborative efforts to establish consensus.Keywords: EEG, Resting, Baseline, Theta, Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Resting State

Introduction

Electroencephalography (EEG) measured when the brain is not explicitly engaged in a task or is otherwise at rest, referred to here as “resting EEG”, has been posited as a useful biomarker for children’s cognitive and social development (e.g., Brito et al., 2019; Troller-Renfree et al., 2022). Resting EEG is a relatively inexpensive, direct, and non-invasive method to measure brain activity (Bell and Cuevas, 2012). EEG is particularly well-suited for developmental research, as the hardware, processing steps, and many of the tasks can remain relatively similar across the lifespan (Buzzell et al., 2023). EEG can be collected in a variety of settings, including in laboratories (Brito et al., 2020), participants’ homes (Bhavnani et al., 2022, Troller-Renfree et al., 2021), and schools (Xu and Zhong, 2018), making EEG an ideal neuroimaging technique for both basic experimental and applied research. Despite these advantages, the lack of standardized procedures for collecting, processing, and analyzing resting EEG data makes it difficult to compare results across samples and challenges reproducibility.

Differences in EEG data collection, preprocessing, and analysis stem from a variety of sources. First, laboratories have varying amounts of computational resources available, such as the amount of storage space to save data and processing power to analyze it at scale. Greater computational resources enable longer paradigms and higher sampling rates, which can be especially beneficial for researchers using independent component analysis. Second, expanding knowledge in the field leads to continual changes in data collection, preprocessing, and analytic approaches. For example, manually inspecting EEG data was once the gold standard method for cleaning data and removing artifacts. Now, with the creation of automated pipelines (Debnath et al., 2020, Lopez et al., 2022), preprocessing EEG has become more consistent and replicable, though less comparable to manual approaches. Third, differences in how EEG data are collected and processed can be an innate part of developmental research. The appropriate resting paradigm for an infant, who cannot focus on a fixation cross for a long period of time or open and close their eyes upon request differs from the ideal resting paradigm for an adolescent (Hervé et al., 2022). Additionally, as children age, the predominant frequency transitions from the theta band to the alpha band (Cellier et al., 2021, Perone et al., 2018) and children’s bodies and heads develop (e.g., the fontanelle closes; Troller-Renfree et al., 2024), which likely necessitate changes to the definition of frequency bands. Researchers handle this complexity in various ways: some papers will have multiple frequency ranges for participants of different ages (e.g., Xie et al., 2022), while others find a range that is suitable enough for all of their participants (e.g., Smit et al., 2012). Other researchers adjust the frequency ranges for every participant depending on their individual spectra (e.g., Perone et al., 2018), though this is currently less common in the literature. Thus, some of the common factors that create heterogeneity in developmental EEG research may be inescapable due to differences in laboratory resources, study design, and child development.

Heterogeneity in the field may also be attributable to differences in decisions regarding data collection. Specifically, there seems to be inconsistency in how “resting” is defined in the field, which may have an impact on measured power values. For instance, Fatori et al. (2021) described infants watching videos of abstract shapes as a “quasi-resting-state condition”, whereas another paper described this same paradigm as just “resting” (Bryant and Cuevas, 2019). Even minor differences across resting paradigms are likely to change the shape of EEG power spectra. Resting alpha and theta power have been shown to be lower in social conditions than in nonsocial conditions within some regions (St. John et al., 2016), and “eyes closed” protocols show increased power at lower frequencies and decreased power at higher frequencies than during “eyes open” (Isler et al., 2023). Similarly, in their Supplementary Materials, MacNeill and colleagues (2018) suggest that alpha power measurements may vary by paradigm, and these power measurements could reflect infants’ visual attention abilities. Despite variations in power values, findings from resting EEG paradigms are still synthesized, even though this may amount to “comparing apples to oranges.”

The difficulty in comparing resting EEG study findings is further complicated by the differing types of EEG power used for hypothesis testing. EEG power values are commonly examined in terms of either “absolute power” (e.g., MacNeill et al., 2018), “relative power” (e.g., Brito et al., 2022), or both (e.g., Troller-Renfree et al., 2020). Absolute power represents the total amount of power (energy) within a given frequency band, whereas relative power reflects the proportion of total power within one frequency band relative to the total power across all frequency bands (Marshall et al., 2002). One possible benefit of using relative power is that it is thought to control for individual differences, such as skull thickness and hair type, which can bias absolute power values (Benninger et al., 1984, Clarke et al., 2001, Marshall et al., 2002, Troller-Renfree et al., 2023). However, a recent investigation demonstrated that relative power may not help control for these individual differences as previously believed (Sandre & Troller-Renfree, 2026). Moreover, a primary weakness of relative power is that, by virtue of being a proportion, its values can be driven by differences in the numerator or denominator. That is, findings in one band (e.g., a relative increase in beta) necessitate a relative decrease in another band (e.g., a relative decrease in theta). Furthermore, due to their proportional nature, relative power measures can actually become negatively associated with their absolute counterparts purely for mathematical reasons (see Troller-Renfree et al., 2025 supplemental materials for further discussion). These dynamics can make it challenging to parse the drivers of changes in relative power across development (e.g., an increase in relative beta and a decrease in relative theta may be two separable effects or two sides of the same coin). Most importantly, this highlights the necessity of carefully considering the type of EEG power used in analyses when interpreting and synthesizing results, which can only be done when power is clearly reported in the methods.

Additional variability in analytic steps and preprocessing throughout the literature has furthered difficulties in drawing general conclusions across papers. Some of this variability may be driven by specific hypotheses, such as papers that only examine whole-brain power or regional power. Specific hypotheses may also drive which frequency bands are included in analyses or if frequency bands are further split (e.g., low-gamma and high-gamma). This variability in reporting power may impede the field both from making precise hypotheses and from drawing meaningful general conclusions. Moreover, it seems likely that some preprocessing methods and analytic choices are not based on hypotheses, such as different ways of log-transforming the power spectra. This variability in methods is particularly troublesome when methods are not clearly reported, inhibiting researchers’ ability to reproduce findings. Consistency in preprocessing, signal analysis steps, and statistical decisions would strengthen the generalizability and reproducibility of developmental EEG literature.

Whether findings are interpreted as statistically significant is directly affected by decisions surrounding corrections for multiple comparisons. Statistical alpha levels are commonly adjusted for families of multiple tests (Schubert et al., 2025), but it is unclear what constitutes a “family” of tests in resting EEG work. It could be argued that a family should be at the whole-brain level (e.g., grouping absolute power models and relative power models separately), at the band level (e.g., grouping theta, alpha, beta, and gamma models separately), or at the regional level (e.g., grouping frontal, central, parietal, etc. models separately). This ambiguity makes it difficult to interpret findings that have been corrected for multiple comparisons, as the family-wise error rate that researchers use would greatly impact which findings survive corrections. Thus, if there is vast heterogeneity in how corrections for multiple comparisons are being conducted, comparability across studies would be further decreased.

To better understand heterogeneity in the field, a recent study surveyed experts in developmental EEG (Troller-Renfree et al., 2024). Experts were given a hypothetical study design, with identical resources and participants, and asked to answer various questions on how they would pre-register their methods and preprocessing. None of the experts answered with exactly the same pre-registration details. Areas that had particularly little consensus included allowances for bad channels, thresholds for artifact removal, transformations of power spectra, analyses on relative versus absolute power, and data minima for inclusion. This lack of consensus across expert analysts in the field highlights the variability in decisions that experts make regarding how resting EEG is processed and analyzed, although it is unclear if these differences are also reflected in the broader literature.

Beyond the specific decisions that researchers make, there is a broader issue of whether these decisions are reported in the methods or supplementary materials of published articles. Underreporting of demographic data has previously been noted as an issue (Morales et al., 2025), as well as underreporting of data collection and preprocessing procedures in event-related potential research (Clayson et al., 2019). Xu et al. (2024) recently published the Reliability, Effect size, And Data quality in EEG (READIE) Toolbox to facilitate the reporting of data quality metrics, but this does not cover methodological/processing decisions. Details of what to report have been suggested in a variety of other scholarly works, such as guidelines for reporting event-related potential research (Keil et al., 2014, Picton et al., 2000) and in clinically-focused EEG work (Ewen et al., 2025). The EQUATOR Network has also published several checklists for various aspects of health research, including the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT; Hopewell et al., 2025) and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA; Page et al., 2021). However, trends in the reporting of pediatric resting EEG parameters are unknown and should be elucidated to guide future pipelines and reporting checklists, especially given that there is not currently a checklist specifically for pediatric resting EEG.

This systematic review aims to identify areas of commonality and differences in data collection, preprocessing, and analysis that currently exist in the developmental EEG literature. Specifically, we explore reporting standards of and the frequencies of data collection paradigms, preprocessing steps, and statistical analysis methods. We examine whether these methodologies and reporting of parameters differ by sample age, publication year, and resting paradigm. Finally, we use these findings to provide a checklist for researchers to encourage consistent reporting and make explicit recommendations for future research. Our ultimate goal is to make developmental EEG research more interpretable, reliable, and reproducible.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

Manuscripts for examination are consistent with those identified by a recent systematic review on the generalizability of developmental EEG (Morales et al., 2025). First, they identified notable journals for pediatric EEG research between 2011 and 2021. To identify these journals, they used PubMed to record all publications that included relevant keywords (e.g., “EEG,” “infant,” “child,” “adolescent,” and “pediatric”) in the title, abstract, or manuscript text. Clinical journals using EEG for clinical diagnoses were excluded. Two journals with the most published developmental EEG papers were selected from each of the following three categories: developmental neuroscience, general development, and general science. This process yielded the following journals: Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience, Developmental Psychobiology, Developmental Science, Child Development, PLoS One, and Scientific Reports. Using PubMed, articles that were published in these journals and matching the previously mentioned keywords were identified, and subsequent identification included articles from 2022 to 2023. All of these articles (i.e., articles from 2011 to 2023) were imported to Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, 2024) for screening and extraction.

Paper screening protocol

Paper inclusion, review, and coding took place in three steps: title and abstract screening, full text review, and data extraction. Primary coders reviewed articles separately and were blind to each other's responses. When conflicts arose, the first two authors would discuss and come to a consensus. If a third party was needed, the senior author was consulted.

At the title and abstract screening stage, we used the following eligibility criteria. Studies were included only if they were empirical EEG studies with children (aged 0–18). Studies with neurotypical and/or unique populations (e.g., populations with neurodevelopmental disorders, such as ASD) were included. The paper needed to include analyses on band power during a resting/baseline EEG paradigm, which included a variety of protocols including eyes open/closed, watching an experimenter demonstration, watching a video, and/or sleeping. Exclusion criteria included studies only using animal or adult populations, task-based EEG, non-EEG (e.g., fNIRS or fMRI) measures, only aEEG, iEEG, TMS & TEP, ENMG, or reviews or meta-analyses. At the full text stage, we used the same criteria as above, while also eliminating studies that did not include classic measures of band power (e.g., analyses were only multiscale entropy, connectivity, coherence, sleep spindles, or FOOOF/Specparam). Similarly, studies were excluded if they only included derivatives of band power, such as examining the differences in power during a task, measures of asymmetry, or three-dimensional distributions of the spectral density.

Complete consensus from two coders was required for title and abstract screening. For full text review, one of two coders reviewed each article. Manuscript inclusion/exclusion was conducted and reported in a PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). Initially, 659 publications were imported for screening. After removing two duplicates, 657 publications remained. For title and abstract screening, 607 publications were agreed on (92.4%; for either inclusion or exclusion), and 50 disagreements were resolved through consensus. After the consensus process, 150 publications were forwarded for full text review, where two coders reviewed 35 articles to become reliable (κ = 0.83). After becoming reliable, one coder completed the full text review stage, and 56 publications were included for data extraction. We included 9 publications from Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience, 10 from Developmental Psychobiology, 10 from Developmental Science, 3 from Child Development, 15 from PLoS One, and 9 from Scientific Reports.

PRISMA flow diagram showing the number of studies excluded and kept at each stage.

Data extraction was conducted in Covidence. This allowed a team of four coders to answer questions about the articles in a quantitative, comparable way. Two independent coders completed data extraction for each article, and the first author would review any discrepancies in their answers and resolve them. A third independent coder checked all extracted data after this process to ensure accuracy. Coders also reviewed supplemental materials that accompanied articles if they were available. Extracted data and the extraction codebook are available at https://osf.io/u7q32/overview.

Data analysis

Our results section is organized into three separate categories: collection procedures, preprocessing parameters, and analysis parameters. We first summarized the parameters that were included in this investigation. Next, we examined differences in each parameter by the age of the sample, year of publication, and whether the paradigm was awake or sleeping.

Since some papers included multiple groups/samples in their analyses, we coded each sample as independent to allow for differing preprocessing steps across groups, resulting in 111 samples in the full dataset. Papers had 1–9 samples (M = 1.98), with a median of 1 sample. We used this full dataset, clustered at the paper level (i.e., paper was a random effect), with samples coded separately to examine differences by sample age, publication year, and awake/sleep paradigms. Samples were only coded separately when they differed by age (e.g., treatment and control groups were represented as one sample). For all descriptives on papers, we used a condensed dataset, where each paper was only represented once.

For binary outcomes (i.e., resting paradigms, EEG system, power transformations, power examinations, whole-brain/regional analyses, and multiple comparison corrections), we dummy-coded each level of the outcome. For example, the conduction of an eyes open/closed paradigm was coded as 1, and no eyes open/closed was coded as 0. For the following analyses, only levels that were used in 10 or more papers were used as outcomes. We ran generalized linear models with a logit link and a binomial distribution to test whether the age of the sample, year of publication, or awake/sleep paradigm were predictors of the binary outcomes listed above, while accounting for the fact that some studies have multiple samples. Publication year was centered and scaled (divided by its standard deviation) in every model to aid convergence. The model that predicted the use of Electrical Geodesic and the model on reporting multiple comparison corrections both failed to converge when awake/sleep paradigm was included, so we tested awake/sleep paradigm in chi-square models instead of including it in the generalized linear models.

For some of the continuous outcomes (i.e., recording minutes, epoch length, and band boundaries), we ran linear mixed models to test whether the age of the sample, year of publication, or awake/sleep paradigm were predictors, while accounting for the fact that some studies have multiple samples. Publication year and each continuous outcome, except for band boundaries, were centered and scaled (divided by their standard deviation) to aid convergence. For continuous outcomes that had fewer than 10 observations for the awake/sleep paradigm variable, we ran linear mixed models to test only whether the age of the sample and the year of publication were predictors, while accounting for the fact that some studies have multiple samples. This included the following outcomes: epoch overlap, bad channel threshold, allowance for bad channels, data minima, and average number of epochs. Again, publication year and each continuous outcome were centered and scaled (divided by their standard deviation) to aid convergence. Additionally, we ran a Pearson correlation to examine whether the minimum data needed for inclusion was associated with recording length.

Finally, because many parameters are frequently unreported in papers, we analyzed whether the reporting of parameters (i.e., missingness) was predicted by the age of the sample, year of publication, or awake/sleep paradigm. To test this, we ran generalized linear models with a logit link and a binomial distribution to test whether these predictors were associated with the reporting of the following parameters: recording minutes, epoch length, epoch overlap, the average number of epochs, the minimum data needed for inclusion, the method used for bad channels, and the percent of bad channels allowed. Due to scaling issues with the model for epoch length, the awake/sleep paradigm was tested in a chi-square model instead of including it in the generalized linear model. The reporting of power transformations and corrections for multiple comparisons were tested as part of the previously stated analyses, given that “None” and “No correction”, respectively, were options during extraction. For power transformations, “None” means that researchers did not report using any log-transformation, though it is unclear if they truly did not use a transformation or if omitted it from their methods section/supplemental materials.

In the reported results, we round percentages to whole numbers. However, the full percentages can be viewed on the visualizations. We present traditional p-values alongside FDR-corrected p-values. The families for these tests were grouped in the same way that the paper is organized (i.e., collection, procedures, preprocessing parameters, and statistical analysis parameters), with an additional family for analyses examining reporting. Each family was further grouped by the predictors. For example, the p-values for the mean age predictor for all collection procedure analyses were grouped together.

Collection procedures

Resting paradigms

First, we calculated the frequency of the resting paradigms used in the literature. We created five categories of resting paradigms: eyes open/closed (e.g., lights on/off or looking at a fixation cross), watching an experimenter demonstration (e.g., blowing bubbles or manipulating toys), watching a non-salient video (e.g., a video of abstract shapes), watching an emotionally salient video, and/or sleeping. If a paper had more than one resting paradigm, we counted these paradigms separately; that is, each paradigm was considered as a separate binary outcome per sample.

As shown in Fig. 2, we found that 45% of studies used eyes open/closed, 14% used an experimenter demonstration, 23% played a non-salient video, 5% played an emotionally salient video, and 25% were recorded while participants slept. For the following analyses, only eyes open/closed, non-salient video, and sleeping were used as outcomes due to the small number of cases for experimenter demonstration and non-salient videos.

Resting paradigms throughout papers (N = 56). If a paper included multiple paradigms, each paradigm was counted separately. Hence, the total is greater than 100%. Eye Open/Closed also comprises lights on/off paradigms.

The age of the sample was a significant predictor for two of the paradigms. Researchers were more likely to ask older children to participate in an eyes open/closed or lights on/off paradigm (B = 2.87, SE = 0.75, z = 3.81, p < .001, padj <.001) and less likely to ask older children to watch a non-salient video (B = −1.08, SE = 0.51, z = −2.11, p = .035, padj = .044). The age of the sample was not significantly associated with whether children were asked to sleep (p > .05). Additionally, publication year did not significantly predict whether eyes open/closed, non-salient videos, or sleeping was used as the resting paradigm (p > .05).

EEG system

The frequency of which EEG systems are used in the literature was calculated. 98% of papers reported which EEG system they used. We found that Electrical Geodesic was used most often (50%), as shown in Fig. 3.

EEG systems throughout papers (N = 56).

Whether Electrical Geodesic was used was significantly predicted by the age of the sample (B = −1.30, SE = 0.57, z = −2.30, p = .022, padj =.036), such that when researchers had younger samples, they were more likely to use Electrical Geodesic. The use of Electrical Geodesic was not significantly associated with publication year (p > .05) nor by the resting paradigm awake/sleep (p > .05).

Length of recording

Recording length was coded as the total number of minutes of the resting EEG protocol per paper. If a range was provided (e.g., 3–4 min), we coded the middle of the range (e.g., 3.5 min). Recording length was not reported in 10 papers (18%). Two studies were excluded as outliers, because they had recording lengths that were greater than three standard deviations of the mean (i.e., 37 h and 40.5 h). An additional outlier was removed after visual inspection of the data, as it had a recording length of 9.333 h, while the rest of our data had recording lengths equal to or less than 60 min. After removing these outliers, the mean recording length was 8.31 min, the median was 4 min, and the range was 40 s to 60 min.

Recording length and the age of the sample were significantly associated with older children completing longer paradigms on average (B = −0.003, SE = 0.001, t(50.10) = −5.12, p < .001, padj < .001). Additionally, whether participants were in an awake paradigm was significantly associated with the length of recording (B = −2.24, SE = 0.29, t(39) = −7.66, p < .001, padj < .001), such that being asked to sleep predicted longer recording lengths compared to awake paradigms. Recording length and publication year were not significantly associated (p > .05). Whether authors reported recording length was not significantly associated with sample age (p > .05), publication year (p > .05), or awake/sleep paradigm (p > .05).

Preprocessing and signal analysis parameters

Epoch length

Epoch length was coded as the total number of seconds per epoch. Four papers did not report the epoch length (7%). For the 52 remaining papers, epochs had a mean length of 11.99 s, a median of 2 s, and a mode of 1 s. However, these descriptives are heavily swayed by resting EEG recorded during “sleep,” which was significantly associated with a longer epoch length as compared to awake protocols (B = −1.89, SE = 0.23, t(1.93) = −8.22, p = .016). For sleeping protocols, the median epoch length was 30 s, with a standard deviation of 17, and a range of 10–60 s. For awake protocols, the median epoch length was 1 s, with a standard deviation of 15.21, and a range of 0.5–90 s. Epoch length was not significantly associated with the age of the sample (p > .05) nor year of publication (p > .05). Additionally, whether authors reported epoch length was not significantly associated with sample age (p > .05), publication year (p > .05), or awake/sleep paradigm (p > .05).

Epoch overlap

Epoch overlap was coded in percentages. As shown in Table 1, epoch overlap information was missing for 29 papers (52%). In the remaining 27 papers, epoch overlap ranged from 0% to 80%, and 50% was the most common epoch overlap used. Epoch overlap was not significantly predicted by the age of the sample (p > .05) nor year of publication (p > .05). Interestingly, whether epoch overlap was reported was significantly associated with awake/sleep paradigm (B = −23.86, SE = 5.51, z = −4.33, p < .001, padj <.001), such that reporting epoch overlap was more likely for studies using awake paradigms. Whether researchers reported epoch overlap was not significantly predicted by sample age (p > .05) or publication year (p > .05).

Threshold to reject bad channels

We coded the threshold authors stated they would use to reject bad channels. In 33 papers, this information was either unreported (N = 14) or a different method, such as using the standard deviation of the threshold or manual inspection, was used to reject channels (N = 19). As shown in Table 1, in the 23 remaining papers, 100 μV was the most common threshold for bad channels, with a range of 75 μV to 300 μV. The threshold for bad channels was not significantly associated with sample age (p > .05) nor publication year (p > .05). Moreover, whether researchers reported the method used to reject bad channel was not significantly predicted by sample age (p > .05), publication year (p > .05), or awake/sleep paradigm (p > .05).

Allowance for bad channels

We coded the percentage of channels that could be excluded from analyses per participant, before the entire participant was excluded. When authors reported the number of channels that could be bad and the total number of channels, we calculated the percentage of channels that could be bad. Information on the allowance for bad channels was not reported in 38 papers (68%). As shown in Table 1, most commonly, researchers allowed 15% of a participant’s channels to be bad before excluding that participant, though this allowance ranged from 3.13% of channels to 80%. The percentage of channels that were allowed to be bad was not significantly predicted by the age of the sample (p > .05) nor year of publication (p > .05). Additionally, whether the authors reported allowance for bad channels was not significantly predicted by the age of the sample (p > .05), year of publication (p > .05), or awake/sleep paradigm (p > .05).

Statistical analysis parameters

Power transformations

First, we calculated the frequency of the types of transformations used in the literature. As shown in Fig. 4, natural log (LN) was utilized in 27% of the papers, common log (Log10) in 16% of the papers, an unspecified log transformation in 13% of the papers, and 45% of papers did not report whether they used any transformation in either their main manuscript or supplemental materials.

Power transformations throughout papers (N = 56).

Transforming the power spectra using natural log was not significantly associated with the age of the sample (p > .05), publication year (p > .05), nor awake/sleep paradigms (p > .05). However, not reporting any transformation of the power spectra was significantly associated with publication year. Surprisingly, newer studies were more likely to not report any transformation than older studies (B = 13.84, SE = 5.17, z = 2.68, p = .007, padj =.067), though this finding is marginally significant after corrections for multiple comparisons. Not reporting any transformation was not significantly associated with the age of the sample (p > .05) nor awake/sleep paradigms (p > .05).

Band definition

We extracted the band boundaries that researchers used if they used bands to quantify their power spectrum. In Fig. 5, band boundaries used in papers are displayed. Papers that did not use set band boundaries, such as those who used individual alpha frequency (IAF), are not included in this figure.

Band boundaries defined for each sample used in papers. Dashed lines indicate the age of the sample, such that the mean age in each study below the dashed line is equal to or less than the labeled age.

We examined whether any of the upper band boundaries or lower band boundaries (for bands used in 10 or more papers) were predicted by the age of the sample, year of publication, or awake/sleep paradigms. Based on this criterion, the bands analyzed were delta, theta, alpha, beta, and gamma. The age of the sample significantly predicted the upper boundary of theta (B = 0.02, SE = 0.01, t(28.39) = 2.06, p = .049, padj = .267) and the lower boundary of alpha (B = 0.03, SE = 0.01, t(41.14) = 2.90, p = .006, padj = .065), such that both of these boundaries increased with sample age. Note that these analyses did not survive corrections for multiple comparisons. The upper and lower boundaries of delta, beta, and gamma, lower boundary of theta, and upper boundary of alpha, were not significantly associated with the age of the sample (p > .05). Publication year did not significantly predict any of the upper or lower band boundaries (p > .05). Interestingly, whether the paradigm was awake or sleep significantly predicted the upper boundary of theta (B = −0.88, SE = 0.40, t(30.94) = −2.19, p = .037, padj = .146) and the lower boundary of alpha (B = −1.14, SE = 0.36, t(23.42) = −3.17, p = .004, padj = .021), such that these boundaries were lower for awake paradigms than for sleep paradigms. Again, note that the upper boundary of theta did not survive multiple comparison corrections, though the lower boundary of alpha did. The upper and lower boundaries of delta, beta, and gamma, lower boundary of theta, and upper boundary of alpha, were not significantly associated with awake/sleep paradigms (p > .05).

Data minima and adjustments

The minimum data needed for inclusion in analyses was reported in 18 papers (32%). There was a large range in how much data researchers required participants to have to be included in analyses (i.e., 10 s to 300 s). The median was 45 s, and the mode was 120 s. The minimum data needed for inclusion was not significantly associated with the age of the sample (p > .05) nor the year of publication (p > .05). Additionally, the minimum data needed for inclusion was not significantly associated with the length of recording (p > .05). Whether authors reported the minimum data needed for inclusion in analyses was not significantly predicted by sample age (p > .05), publication year (p > .05), or awake/sleep paradigm (p > .05).

We also coded the average number of usable epochs reported in papers, which was only reported in 17 papers (30%). The median was 77 epochs, with a range of 3.5 epochs to 652.5 epochs. The average number of epochs was not significantly predicted by sample age (p > .05) nor publication year (p > .05). Moreover, whether authors reported the average number of usable epochs was not significantly predicted by sample age (p > .05), publication year (p > .05), or awake/sleep paradigm (p > .05).

Absolute versus relative power

As shown in Fig. 6, 70% of papers included absolute power findings in their manuscript, 45% included relative power findings in their manuscript, 2% included absolute power findings in only the supplemental materials, 0% included relative power findings in only the supplemental materials, and 4% stated that they examined absolute or relative power but do not include those findings in the manuscript.

Power examinations across papers (N = 56). If a paper examined more than one type of power, they were counted separately. Hence, the total is greater than 100%.

Including absolute power in the main manuscript was not significantly predicted by the age of the sample (p > .05), publication year (p > .05), nor awake/sleep paradigms (p > .05).

Including relative power in the main manuscript was significantly predicted by the age of the sample (B = 2.12, SE = 0.64, z = 3.33, p < .001, padj =.019), such that relative power was more likely to be used with older samples than with younger samples. Publication year also significantly predicted whether relative power was included in the main manuscript (B = 24.33, SE = 7.26, z= 3.35, p < .001, padj =.011), such that more papers included relative power results in recent years. Awake/sleep paradigm did not significantly predict whether relative power was included in the main manuscript (p > .05).

Whole-brain and regional analyses

We coded whether researchers had whole-brain analyses and any regional analyses. If researchers used region as a within-subjects factor in an ANOVA, we coded this as a whole-brain analysis, along with all of the included regions. We also collapsed left and right regions (e.g., left temporal and right temporal would be coded as temporal). As shown in Fig. 7, 48% of papers included whole-brain analyses, 63% included frontal analyses, 9% included frontal polar analyses, 4% included medial frontal analyses, 4% included lateral frontal analyses, 39% included central analyses, 4% included fronto-central analyses, 39% included parietal analyses, 23% included temporal analyses, 4% included temporoparietal analyses, 43% included occipital analyses, 14% included posterior analyses, and 4% were at the electrode level.

Regions examined throughout papers (N = 56). If a paper examined more than one region, they were counted separately. Hence, the total is greater than 100%.

The inclusion of whole-brain power significantly differed by sample age (B = 1.14, SE = 0.45, z = 2.55, p = .011, padj =.079), publication year (B = 8.46, SE = 2.57, z = 3.29, p =.001, padj =.011), and awake/sleep paradigms (B = 29.64, SE = 9.95, z = 2.98, p = .003, padj =.019). Thus, whole-brain power is more likely to be included in analyses with older samples versus younger samples, in newer papers rather than older papers, and for awake paradigms rather than sleep paradigms. However, it should be noted that the association between sample age and the inclusion of whole-brain power did not survive corrections for multiple comparisons. Findings for the differences in sample age, publication year, and awake/sleep paradigms for the inclusion of each regional analysis are shown in Table 1 of the Supplementary Materials.

Corrections for multiple comparisons

As shown in Fig. 8, 23% of papers used Bonferroni, 16% used False Discovery Rate (FDR), 7% used Holm-Bonferroni, 5% used a Tukey test, 2% used a randomization test, 2% did not need to correct for multiple comparisons, and 45% did not report whether they corrected for multiple comparisons.

Procedures used for corrections for multiple comparisons throughout papers (N = 56). If a paper used more than one procedure across hypotheses, they were counted separately. Hence, the total is greater than 100%.

Promisingly, publication year significantly predicted whether researchers reported using any correction (B = −13.71, SE = 6.93, z = −1.98, p = .048, padj =.215), such that reporting a correction was more likely in recent years, though this did not survive corrections for multiple comparisons. Not reporting any correction was not significantly predicted by the age of the sample (p > .05) nor awake/sleep paradigm (p > .05). The use of Bonferroni corrections was not significantly predicted by the age of the sample (p > .05), publication year (p > .05), nor awake/sleep paradigms (p > .05).

Discussion

This systematic review provides an overview of the state of pediatric resting EEG collection, preprocessing, and analytic strategies. We reviewed articles that were published in six notable pediatric EEG journals during 2011–2023, and we provided the reporting standards of and the frequencies of resting EEG methods. We also explored whether these methods and their reporting differed by sample age, publication year, and resting paradigm. We found that while some methods were fairly similar across papers or could be explained by differences in sample age, publication year, or awake/sleep paradigms, field-level efforts are needed to establish a consensus regarding the methods to best support the interpretability and reliability of pediatric resting EEG.

Insights regarding data collection

As expected, we saw that resting paradigms partly varied by sample age, though there was great variability not attributable to sample age. We were surprised that younger kids were not more likely to have a sleeping paradigm as young infants can be difficult to keep awake. Furthermore, we were surprised to see infant samples were asked to participate in every type of paradigm. It is unclear what factors are guiding researchers when choosing resting paradigms. Given that these differences are not due to core developmental research questions, it is likely the literature may reflect unnecessary variability due to the stimuli and procedures being used at rest. Although researchers are trying to home in on the same construct of resting brain activity, theory and research suggest that different paradigms likely impact the shape of the power spectrum (Isler et al., 2023, MacNeill et al., 2018, St. John et al., 2016). Thus, methodological work is needed to understand whether these patterns of brain activity should all be grouped together under the term “resting” brain activity; moreover, greater consensus in resting stimuli is needed.

Due to the variability within categories of resting paradigms, we could not statistically examine differences within all categories of resting paradigms. For example, “watching a non-salient video” is common, but there is a lot of variety in these videos. Some are described as abstract screensavers, while other videos are more likely to grab children’s attention. Some videos have audio while others do not. Similarly, during “eyes closed”, researchers sometimes requested that children use their imaginations (Perone et al., 2018), though it is unclear if these prompts may affect power values. For replicability, researchers should include a link to the video and/or task that children watched during the recording. Moreover, thorough reporting of resting paradigms would allow for future work to run meta-analyses on resting paradigms and power values, so that we could have a better understanding of the impact that variety in resting paradigms may have on EEG power.

Perhaps unsurprisingly given the variability in paradigm, the length of resting EEG recordings had a large range. In general, longer recordings were more common for sleep, with awake recordings having a median of 3.75 min and sleep recordings having a median of 60 min. We also found that older children were more likely to have longer recording lengths. Paradigm length, of course, is likely to impact patterns of brain activity, states of arousal, and motor artifact. In addition, longer paradigms may allow for children to reach required data minima, though recording length was not associated with higher data minima (p > .05). Moreover, following empirical reports that only 15–30 s of clean data produce reliable estimates of neural activity (Leach et al., 2020, Sandre et al., 2024, Troller-Renfree et al., 2021), it is likely that very long recording times are unnecessary. As a field, we should come to a consensus on recording lengths to ensure that we are not unnecessarily burdening older children, while still collecting high quality data.

Finally, perhaps unsurprisingly, we also identified that the most common EEG system used in these papers was Electrical Geodesic. Researchers were more likely to use Electrical Geodesic with younger samples, which suggests that researchers find quick application saline systems preferable for EEG recordings with younger children.

Insights about preprocessing and signal analysis

When examining preprocessing steps, we found that epoch length varied widely for both awake and sleeping protocols. Resting EEG recorded during sleep paradigms had longer epochs than awake paradigms. Longer epoch lengths have the advantages of better frequency resolution and the ability to resolve lower frequency oscillations, though they require less motor artifact for epochs to be included in analyses. Thus, it follows that researchers using awake paradigms would use shorter epoch lengths, due to awake children moving more. There is a delicate balance in choosing epoch lengths for data quality, particularly given that longer lengths may disproportionately exclude children with higher motor activity, perhaps creating bias in which participants are included in analyses. This bias could be particularly egregious for certain research questions (e.g., studies exploring attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder or autism), as children who may be essential to the research question are more likely to be removed from analyses.

Prime examples of preprocessing parameters that require further consensus are the threshold used to reject bad channels and the percentage of channels that researchers allowed to be excluded before excluding the entire participant. When reported, there was a large range in the threshold used (75–300 μV) and in the allowance for bad channels (3.13%–80%), but neither of these choices were related to sample age or publication year. It is unclear what factors influence these decisions, which is troublesome given that excluding data may alter the significance of findings. Without consensus in the field or pre-registrations detailing preprocessing parameters, this threshold could easily be changed during analyses to alter inclusion or significance. Nuances in the process of excluding bad channels add additional sources of variability. For example, researchers may reject the entire epoch if any channel exceeds the threshold, interpolate only the channel that exceeds the threshold, visually inspect channels instead of using a set threshold, etc. To ensure consistency and reliability in developmental EEG research, preprocessing parameters—such as the process and threshold for bad channel rejection—should be standardized or given a guiding framework.

Insights concerning data analysis

Vast heterogeneity in band boundaries limits the comparability of EEG findings. While there were a couple of boundaries that varied by sample age, most boundaries had little consensus by sample age (e.g., delta ranges were very inconsistent in infant research). This inconsistency hinders readers’ ability to generalize findings across studies. Additionally, readers may not notice these small, key differences in methods, so findings may be compared regardless, leading to a flawed generalization. To add another layer of complexity, IAF is sometimes used to determine boundaries instead of setting specific boundaries. This technique addresses interindividual variability, though there is also inconsistency in implementing IAF (Corcoran et al., 2018). Thus, the variety in band boundaries impedes researchers’ ability to easily interpret and generalize findings from EEG studies, even when accounting for developmental differences. In addition to various frequencies used for band boundaries, nuances in the labeling of band boundaries further complicate their interpretability. Bands are sometimes split into “high” and “low”, “fast” and “slow”, or “1” and “2”, though it is not clear why these smaller categories are created in some papers. When researchers choose to examine uncommon bands, peer reviewers and the broader field should ask for justification for these choices. Moreover, researchers should carefully consider if straying from traditional bands is essential for their research questions, given that inconsistent terminology makes it difficult to interpret findings and compare across studies. Similarly, researchers occasionally refer to their frequency ranges as the quantified ranges (e.g., 3–5 Hz) throughout their paper, without ever naming them. While this may be a strength in an individual paper, across the field, it can make synthesizing findings difficult, especially given that band definitions should change across developmental ages. It is important that researchers are able to compare and synthesize findings, so the field should consider standardizing the way that band boundaries are named based on methodological work.

Just as smaller categories are sometimes created for band boundaries, regions are sometimes divided in unique ways. Splitting regions makes synthesizing results across papers challenging and begs the question: how are regions selected for analysis? Typically, researchers can analyze all regions. Moreover, the definition of regions may differ in terms of which electrodes were selected across systems, montages, and studies. When deviating from traditional regions or only including some regions, these choices should be justified (e.g., the cap only has frontal electrodes) and, optimally, preregistered. In cases where a researcher has a specific regional hypothesis, best practice may be to include analyses on all regions in supplementary materials. This would allow for more accurate coding for meta-analyses, ultimately leading the field to a better understanding of regional brain power.

Relative power seems to be more common in the literature in recent years. However, while relative power is useful in controlling for individual differences such as skull thickness (Benninger et al., 1984, Clarke et al., 2001, Troller-Renfree et al., 2023), it has its own limitations. The meaning of relative power in the literature seems inconsistent—specifically, the definition of relative power varies by which bands researchers choose to include or if they include the whole power spectrum (e.g., total power from 2 to 45 Hz). For example, when gamma is not included when calculating relative power for theta, alpha, and beta, it may be more accurate to refer to these calculations as their own ratio. Similarly, delta is often excluded from relative power calculations, though it is implied that “whole-brain” power is in the denominator. Primarily, this highlights the importance for researchers to specify how relative power was calculated, as well as the importance for readers to consider relative power calculations when interpreting and comparing results.

Though we are seeing an uptick in the use of multiple comparison corrections in recent years, a statistical technique often employed to reduce the presence of false positives, corrections for multiple comparisons are being conducted in various ways. This variety is problematic: these corrections alter the significance of findings. Given that this is an analytical decision, there is no reason to not have a guiding framework on how corrections for multiple comparisons should be conducted. However, there are several questions that need to be considered while coming to a consensus. Most broadly, what should be considered a “family” in a multiple comparison test with EEG? Should we correct at the whole-brain level (relative and absolute), at the band level, or at the regional level? How should this be modified for studies that have different numbers of analyses? Moreover, multiple researchers used non-EEG related aspects of their research as their family (e.g., sample age). How should this be taken into account? These questions also lead into the question of whether exploratory work should be corrected for multiple comparisons. As we are encouraging authors to report several contrasts (e.g., including analyzing all regions and including results in the supplement), correcting for multiple comparisons in exploratory work may hinder significance for hypothesized results. Despite all these nuances and complicated issues in deciding on corrections for multiple comparisons, it is possible and necessary for the field to come to a consensus on how corrections should be implemented for pediatric EEG research.

Insights regarding reporting

In addition to standardizing and pre-registering preprocessing parameters, these parameters need to be fully reported in either the main manuscript or supplementary materials. In the literature we reviewed, parameters were often unreported (see Table 1 for information on how many studies reported each parameter). Some parameters were not reported due to researchers citing the preprocessing pipeline that they used instead of including details in their manuscript, even though most pipelines allow parameters to be edited. Most unreported parameters were fully omitted from both the manuscript and its supplementary materials without an explanation. Unreported parameters hinder the reproducibility of studies and limit the interpretability and generalizability of findings. While preprocessing steps were often not reported, there was even more data missing for analytic steps, suggesting more care to report methods for the sake of reproducibility compared to data showing reliability. To attempt to address this issue, we included a direct analysis of missingness, which showed that neither sample age nor publication year significantly predicted whether researchers reported several parameters such as recording length, epoch length, epoch overlap, bad-channel methods, data-inclusion minima, or the average number of usable epochs. Further discussion on addressing this issue as a field can be found in the “Future Directions and Next Steps” section.

Limitations

There are several limitations of this review. First, we used only six notable pediatric EEG journals, and we limited the timeframe of our search, both for the sake of feasibility and to capture common trends that may have the most readership from pediatric EEG researchers. While these decisions strengthened our review by using the most updated and popular papers, we did not have as holistic of a view of pediatric resting EEG methodologies as clinical neurophysiology journals and broader neuroscience outlets were not included. Similarly, only papers with classic band power measures were included to ensure that additional heterogeneity was not added due to differences in decisions based on the EEG metric, given that our cell sizes would be too small to reliably test if processing decisions differed across metrics. Second, we limited our scope in which methodologies we extracted from papers, thus likely underestimating all of the potential sources of variability in resting EEG analyses. For example, we did not code which electrodes authors selected to define regions, though the variability in these selections may inhibit accurate synthesizations of findings. To attempt to address this issue, we included a direct analysis of reporting (i.e., missingness), which showed that neither sample age nor publication year significantly predicted whether researchers reported several parameters such as recording length, epoch length, epoch overlap, bad-channel methods, data-inclusion minima, or the average number of usable epochs. Third, if any information on data collection, preprocessing, or statistical analyses was missing, we chose to continue with this missing data and not to reach out to authors, as we wanted to focus on what was reported and for the sake of consistency. Due to the low reporting of some parameters throughout the literature, especially as missing data may be nonrandom, it is difficult to interpret null results. Namely, less than half of the papers reported information on the threshold to reject bad channels, the allowance for bad channels, data minima, and the average number of usable epochs. We strongly advise future manuscripts to include this information and for reviewers to check this information is included in future manuscripts. Finally, some of the characteristics of articles were moderately skewed. Sample age was positively skewed (skew = 0.86) and publication year was negatively skewed (skew = ‐0.86), though these were within acceptable ranges (Bulmer, 2012). Most studies had awake paradigms (76%) rather than sleep paradigms.

Future directions and next steps

As the field increasingly leverages resting EEG as a biomarker for developmental outcomes, it is critical to move beyond documenting existing heterogeneity and towards empirically addressing it. Our review confirms what is largely known by the field, that substantial variability exists, but also confirms another less-known trend: these methodological inconsistencies and underreporting gaps are largely unexplained and have not significantly improved over the last 12 years. This suggests that existing reporting guidelines (e.g., Styles et al., 2021), standardized pipelines, and consensus efforts have not yet successfully standardized practice within the pediatric resting EEG community.

To bridge this gap, we provide the Reporting and Evaluation Checklist for Analysis of Pediatric EEG (RECAP-EEG) (see Fig. 9). This interactive checklist is directly derived from the most frequently omitted parameters identified in our review—such as bad channel rejection thresholds, data inclusion minima, and epoch overlap. We propose that this checklist be integrated into formal submission and peer-review processes to ensure that future studies provide the transparency necessary for replication. By adopting these standards, journals and reviewers can enforce a level of detail that our data shows are currently missing from the literature. The interactive PDF can be downloaded at https://osf.io/u7q32/overview.

The Reporting and Evaluation Checklist for Analysis of Pediatric EEG (RECAP-EEG), which is intended for authors and peer reviewers to ensure that papers provide the necessary information for transparency and replication of pediatric resting EEG research.

Furthermore, our findings highlight areas that need more core methodological work, such as interrogating how variability in processing changes power values and their associations with outcomes of interest. For instance, the timing of transformations during preprocessing (e.g., log-transform the power spectrum before or after computing relative power) remains inconsistent (Troller-Renfree et al., 2024) and may change the relative rank of participants’ power values (Sandre & Troller-Renfree, 2026) – this likely has real implications for the interpretation of research findings and could be interrogated empirically now that the unpredictable field-level heterogeneity has been documented. Ultimately, we call for a blend of field consensus efforts and methodological papers that specifically test the effects of various methodological decisions (e.g., epoch lengths, referencing schemes, and artifact-rejection thresholds) to make pediatric resting EEG a replicable biomarker.

Conclusion

The agglomeration of research findings is what allows researchers to truly leverage EEG as a biomarker for policy work, clinical practices, and educational applications. It is essential that developmental EEG researchers come to a consensus on decisions regarding data collection, preprocessing, and analytic steps, so that findings can be accurately compared and synthesized. This systematic review describes current methodologies and reporting practices in pediatric EEG research and highlights differences in methods that cannot be explained by sample age, publication year, or awake/sleep paradigms. Moreover, to ensure that EEG findings are reliable and interpretable, methods need to be reported clearly and with greater transparency. When possible, preprocessing and analytic plans should be preregistered, and scripts and data should be shared. Given the importance of EEG as a developmental biomarker, it is essential that the field takes these steps.
