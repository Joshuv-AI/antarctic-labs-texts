Maternal methionine supplementation in low-protein diets during late gestation and lactation increases performance and alters fecal microbiota of sows and their offspring
Authors: Mengliao Wan, Zhiwei Kong, Yuan Gao, Yulong Yin, Fengna Li, Yehui Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Methionine (Met) is known to enhance antioxidant defense and reproductive efficiency in sows, but its role under low-protein feeding conditions is unclear. This study evaluated the effects of Met supplementation on lactation performance, antioxidant status, and gut microbiota of sows and their offspring. Sixty-six multiparous sows with two parity and similar body weight (244.1 ± 1.73 kg) were assigned to a normal protein diet (18% crude protein [CP]) or low-protein diets (15% CP) containing graded standardized ileal digestible (SID) Met levels (0.23%, 0.30%, 0.37%, 0.44%, and 0.51%, respectively) from d 107 of gestation to d 21 of lactation, with 11 replicates in each group and one sow per replicate. The results showed that sows fed low-protein diets supplemented with 0.30% Met achieved milk yields and piglet growth comparable to those of the normal protein group (P > 0.05). In addition, 0.30% Met supplementation reduced serum urea nitrogen content, enhanced the concentrations of milk immunoglobulin M, glutathione, and taurine, and increased the activities of serum total superoxide dismutase in sows and glutathione peroxidase in piglets when compared with the normal protein diet (P < 0.05). Microbiome analysis indicated higher relative abundance of Christensenellaceae_R-7_group in piglets from the 0.30% Met group, and this relative abundance was positively correlated with growth traits. These findings suggest that supplementation with 0.30% Met within the 15% CP diet supports sow performance and piglet development comparable to normal protein feeding, via enhanced antioxidant capacity and favorable modulation of gut microbiota.

Introduction

To satisfy the elevated nutritional demands of modern high-yielding sows, elevating the crude protein (CP) level in diets has long been regarded as a straightforward nutritional strategy (Strathe et al., 2017). However, excessive dietary CP intake triggers a persistent influx of amino acids (AAs) into the liver, thereby perturbing the systemic energy and nitrogen homeostasis (Luczkowska et al., 2024). This subsequently impairs nitrogen utilization efficiency, leading to increased urinary nitrogen excretion, which in turn exacerbating environmental pollution (Pedersen et al., 2018). Apart from nitrogen excretion into environment, feeding excessive dietary protein to lactating sows also reduces milk yield by impairing energy utilization of sows (Pedersen et al., 2019; Strathe et al., 2020). Therefore, it is necessary to match protein supply with actual AA requirements of the sow. Existing literature data have shown that low-protein diets exert beneficial roles in lactating sows (Han, 2024; Huber et al., 2018, 2016, 2015; Wang et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2024b). Currently, to facilitate the formulation of low-protein diets in swine production, the following five crystalline essential AAs (EAAs), including lysine (Lys), methionine (Met), tryptophan (Trp), threonine (Thr), and valine (Val), are commonly supplemented (Rocha et al., 2022). However, despite studies on AA supplementation in low-protein diets for lactating sows (Huber et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2019), the restrictive sequence of these five EAAs remains poorly understood. Thus, a key challenge in advancing low-protein diets for lactating sows is to determine this restrictive sequence, which is essential for accurately predicting EAA requirements during lactation.

In this context, previous studies have dedicated considerable effort to addressing the aforementioned questions. Through the AA partial deduction method, this study first identified that the restrictive order of EAAs in low‑protein diets for sows during late gestation (d 107 of gestation) and lactation is Lys ≈ Trp ≈ Met > Thr ≈ Val, based on sow weight loss (Han et al., 2025). Then, the research investigated the Lys requirements in low-protein diets for sows during late gestation and lactation, and indicated that optimal reproductive performance of sows can be maintained with a standardized ileal digestible (SID) Lys level ranging from 0.81% to 0.90% (Han, 2024), which is slightly higher than the recommended values (0.78%) in NRC (2012). Therefore, considering ongoing improvements in sow productivity, genetic potential, breed variations, and genotype–environment interactions, the current AA requirements established by the NRC (2012) may warrant re-evaluation under modern low-protein feeding strategies.

Based on Met's critical role in methylation reactions, glutathione (GSH) synthesis, and antioxidant defense, its requirement likely differs under low-protein feeding (Wan et al., 2025). However, there is limited information on how varying Met levels in low-protein diets influence antioxidant status, nitrogen metabolism, and lactational performance of sows. While studies based on the NRC (2012)-recommended dietary protein level (17.7%) have suggested an optimal Met level of approximately 0.48% during late gestation and lactation (Azad et al., 2018; Bin et al., 2018), this recommendation may not translate directly to low-protein formulations. This is particularly critical given that excessive Met intake can lead to elevated homocysteine (Hcy), a metabolic byproduct associated with increased fetal mortality (Yang et al., 2019), and that a substantial rise in Hcy concentration has been observed in sows during late gestation (Xia et al., 2021). Hence, further exploration of the optimal Met level in low-protein diets for sows during late gestation and lactation is urgently needed. In this study, a ‘low-protein’ diet refers to a diet with CP reduced by approximately 3 percentage points from the conventional level (18%), supplemented with crystalline EAA to meet requirements, representing a practical strategy for nitrogen reduction (Wang et al., 2018). On this basis, the current study investigated that optimal Met supplementation in low-protein diets would enhance sow reproductive and lactation performance and piglet growth by improving antioxidant capacity and nitrogen utilization.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

Animal procedures in this study were carried out under the guidelines established by the Animal Care Committee of the Institute of Subtropical Agriculture, Chinese Academy of Sciences, and were conformed to Chinese guidelines on the ethical use of animals (ISA-2023–0002; 2023–03-13).

Animal and experimental design

A total of 66 multiparous Landrace × Large White sows (parity = 2; average body weight = 244.1 ± 1.73 kg) at d 107 of gestation were used. Sows were stratified by body weight and parity and randomly assigned to six dietary treatments (n = 11 sows per treatment). Each sow was considered an experimental unit. The six diets included one normal protein control (18% CP, 0.27% SID Met) and five low-protein diets (15% CP) supplemented with different SID Met levels (0.23%, 0.30%, 0.37%, 0.44%, and 0.51%) until the piglets were weaned (d 21 of lactation). This dietary Met dosage regimen was formulated with the aim of avoiding severe impairment of animal performance, while specifically aiming to identify the optimal supplementation range and evaluate the effects of Met excess (Azad et al., 2018; Han et al., 2025). All diets contained 0.80% SID Lys based on the previous experimental results (Han, 2024), and the other SID AAs and net energy levels were formulated to meet or exceed the nutrient requirements for lactating sows (≥2 parities, litter average daily gain [ADG], approximately 230 g/d) as recommended by the NRC (2012), with all AAs (other than Met) maintained at non-limiting levels (Han et al., 2025) (Table 1). The DL-Met (purity 99%) was purchased from Fufeng Group Co., Ltd. (Linyi, Shandong, China). Sows were housed individually in farrowing crates under controlled temperature (22–28 °C) with ad libitum access to water. Feed allowance increased gradually after farrowing until maximum voluntary intake was reached. Sample and data collection

Sow body weight was measured on d 107 of gestation and d 21 of lactation, while daily feed intake was monitored throughout. Litter weights were measured on lactation d 3, 14, and 21.

On d 14 of lactation, milk samples were collected from the anterior, middle, and posterior teats during natural milk letdown. Fresh fecal samples were obtained rectally three days prior to weaning. On the weaning day, after an 8-h fast, blood was drawn from the sow's marginal ear vein (with at least eight replicates per group). Post-weaning estrus onset was recorded (Schwarz et al., 2021). On the weaning day, eight litters per replicate group were randomly selected. From each litter, one piglet with a body weight closest to the litter average was chosen. These piglets were fasted for 8 h before blood collection via the anterior vena cava and subsequent euthanasia for colon content collection. All milk, fecal, and colon content samples were immediately stored at −80 °C for subsequent analyses. Blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain serum, which was then stored at −80 °C until analyses.

Chemical composition analysis for diets

Crude protein was analyzed by Kjeldahl method (GB/T 6432–2018) (China National Standard, 2018b) using a Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (Model Kjeltec 9, FOSS Analytical A/S, Hillerød, Denmark). Crude fiber was analyzed according to the filtration method (GB/T 6434–2022) (China National Standard, 2022) with a fiber analyzer (Model FT12, C. Gerhardt GmbH & Co. KG, Königswinter, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany). The AA contents were analyzed in accordance with GB/T 18246-2019 (China National Standard, 2019) using an automatic AA analyzer (Model L8800, Hitachi High-Technologies Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Crude ash content was determined by combusting samples in a muffle furnace (Model 5 E-MF6000, Changsha Kaiyuan Instrument Co., Ltd., Changsha, Hunan, China) at 550 ± 20 °C to a constant weight according to GB/T 6438-2007 (China National Standard, 2007). Dry matter was determined by samples in a constant-temperature oven at 105 ± 2 °C to a constant weight in line with GB/T 6435-2014 (China National Standard, 2014). Organic matter content was calculated as the difference between dry matter and crude ash contents. Calcium content and total phosphorus were analyzed in accordance with GB/T 6436-2018 (China National Standard, 2018a) and GB/T 6437–2018 (China National Standard, 2018c) using an inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES 5100, Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). Digestible energy, net energy, and available phosphorus were calculated according to China Feed Database (2021).

Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) analysis

The BUN (C013-3-1) levels in lactating sows were measured using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Co., Ltd., Nanjing, Jiangsu, China).

Milk nutrient composition analysis

The total milk yield per sow during lactation was calculated from litter size and the ADG of piglets, following the method described by Miao et al. (2019).

The composition of milk (fat, protein, lactose, fat-free dry matter, and total solid) and the milk urea nitrogen (MUN) levels were determined by a milk composition analyzer (, MilkoScan FT+200 Type 76150, FOSS Analytical A/S, Hillerød, Denmark) as previously reported (Wei et al., 2019).

Milk AA profiles were analyzed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatograph (ACQUITY UPLC, Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) equipped with a photodiode array (PDA) detector (ACQUITY UPLC PDA Detector, Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). Briefly, 0.5 mL of milk was mixed with an equal volume of hexane (C6H14) and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove lipids. Then, 0.3 mL of the lower phase was combined with 0.3 mL of 10% sulfosalicylic acid and allowed to stand for 1 h. After centrifugation under the same conditions, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane for analysis.

Milk fatty acid composition was measured using the Agilent 6890 Gas Chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). In brief, 1 mL of milk was mixed with 0.7 mL of KOH solution (56 g KOH dissolved and diluted to 100 mL with distilled water) and 5.3 mL of methanol. The mixture was incubated in a 55 °C water bath for 1.5 h with vortexing every 20 min. After cooling, 0.58 mL of dilute sulfuric acid (prepared by mixing 65.22 mL of concentrated H2SO4 with 34.78 mL of distilled water) was added, and the reaction was continued under the same conditions. Following the reaction, 3 mL of hexane was added, and the mixture was vortexed, centrifuged at 1500 × g for 5 min, and the hexane layer was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane prior to GC injection.

The concentrations of secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA; CSB-E12063p), immunoglobulin M (IgM; CSB-E06805p), and immunoglobulin G (IgG; CSB-E06804p) in milk were assayed using commercially available ELISA kits (Wuhan Huamei Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China).

Serum and milk Met metabolites analysis

The contents of GSH (A006-2-1) and Hcy (JL-T1120) in serum and milk of lactating sows and piglet's serum were analyzed with kits purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Co., Ltd. China (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) and Shanghai, Jianglai Co., Ltd., China (Shanghai, China), respectively; taurine (Tau) was analyzed following the same ultra-high-performance liquid chromatograph method as described for the AA profiles.

Serum oxidative stress parameters analysis

The serum levels of malondialdehyde (MDA; A003-1-2) and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC; A015-2-1), and the activities of total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD; A001-3-2), catalase (CAT; A007-1-1), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px; A005-2-1) in lactating sows and piglets were measured using their corresponding commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Co., Ltd., Nanjing, Jiangsu, China).

Gut microbiota analysis

The gut microbiota in fecal samples from lactating sows and in colon content from piglets was analyzed following the protocol described in the previous study (Yu et al., 2024). Briefly, microbial DNA was extracted using the Fast Pure Stool DNA Isolation Kit (Magnetic bead) (Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. DNA quality was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, while DNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The V3–V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR using the primer pair 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806 R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). Finally, libraries were constructed following Illumina's standard library preparation protocols and sequencing was performed on the Illumina PE300 platform (MiSeq PE300, Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) analysis

Short-chain fatty acids in fecal samples from lactating sows and colon content from piglets were determined by Agilent 6890 A gas chrom-atography (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) as previously described (Duan et al., 2019).

Statistical analysis

The experiment data was analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and the results were presented as means and standard error of the mean (SEM). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for the six groups uses the following model:Xij=μ+ai+eij,where Xij represents the dependent variable; μ represents the overall mean; ai represents the effect of dietary treatment; and eij represents the random error. Linear and quadratic relationships were established among the five low-protein groups. Given that the data exhibited a significant quadratic response, a quadratic regression model was employed to determine the optimal dietary Met supplementation level. For variables affected by litter size, further analysis of covariance (ANCOVA)—with litter size included as a covariate—was performed when statistically significant differences were detected via ANOVA. Statistical differences between the treatment's means were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Reproduction performance of sows

As shown in Table 3, parameters related to reproduction performance (specifically, number of piglets per litter, litter weight of piglets, piglets' weaning weight, 3–21 d ADG of piglets, 3–21 d milk yield, weaning 7-d estrus interval, and weaning 7-d estrus rate) were not significantly different among the 18% CP group and the 15% CP + 0.30% Met or 0.37% Met groups (P > 0.05). However, compared to the normal protein group, 0.44% and 0.51% Met supplementation significantly prolonged the 7-d post-weaning estrus interval (P = 0.014). Moreover, compared to the normal protein group, 0.51% Met supplementation significantly reduced the ADG of piglets and the sow's milk yield from d 3 to 21 and the piglets' weaning weight (P < 0.05). Discussion

Previous studies have demonstrated the benefits of low-protein, AA-supplemented diets for nursery pigs (Norgaard and Fernandez, 2009), weaned piglets (Li et al., 2024b, 2017), and growing-finishing pigs (Li et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2023a). However, little is known about the effects of such diets on sows during late gestation and lactation. The current study evaluated the impact of low-protein diets supplemented with Met on the performance, antioxidant status, and gut microbiota of lactating sows and their offspring.

During lactation, maternal milk yield is strongly correlated with suckling piglet growth (Qi et al., 2023). The results demonstrated that the peak milk yield was achieved in the 0.30% Met supplementation group, a response independent of litter size, which was consistent with the trends observed for piglet ADG. Similarly, a previous study reported that in sows fed a normal-protein (17.7%) diet, supplementation with 0.48% Met from d 90 of gestation to d 21 of lactation significantly increased piglet ADG and weaning weight, whereas 0.60% Met did not provide further improvement (Azad et al., 2018). Although the precise mechanism remains unclear, the decline in reproductive performance associated with elevated Hcy levels in sow serum and milk may explain the reduced efficacy at higher Met levels (Wan et al., 2025). In contrast to previous studies investigating Lys requirements (Han, 2024), sow reproductive performance in this study did not follow a gradual plateau with increasing dietary Met levels, but rather exhibited a significant quadratic response. To accurately characterize this relationship, the current study consequently performed quadratic regression analyses. These analyses revealed that both milk yield and piglet ADG initially increased and then decreased as dietary Met levels rose, with peak performance estimated at 0.341% and 0.355% SID Met, respectively. The decline in production performance at the highest Met level (0.51%) indicates that the optimal range was exceeded, thereby entering a phase of diminishing returns or potential metabolic toxicity, a phenomenon likely mediated by elevated Hcy accumulation (Audet et al., 2015; Zhou, 2024). Collectively, these results suggest that under the conditions of this study, the optimal SID Met supplementation level in low-protein lactating sow diets lies within the range of 0.34%–0.35%.

Lower BUN and MUN levels reflect improved nitrogen utilization efficiency, which may contribute to enhanced milk production. Further investigations into nitrogen balance and whole-body protein turnover would help clarify the metabolic adaptations to low-protein diets (Naveed-ul-Haque et al., 2018). The present results support this perspective, as sows fed the low-protein diet with 0.30% and 0.37% Met exhibited lower BUN and MUN levels compared with the 18% CP diets. Beyond milk yield, higher milk lactose and IgM concentrations in sows fed 0.30% Met within the low-protein diets likely further promoted piglet growth, as milk lactose is the main energy source for suckling piglets (Sun et al., 2019) and improved milk components likely contributed to the increased growth rate of suckling piglets (Jin et al., 2025a,b). Taken together, these data demonstrate that appropriate Met supplementation (0.30%) in low-protein diets exerts beneficial effects on sow lactation performance and piglet growth.

Interestingly, improved lactation performance has been linked to elevated levels of milk AAs (Tan et al., 2018). Historically, AAs have been viewed primarily as building blocks for protein synthesis; however, they are now recognized to play key functional roles in metabolism (Wu, 2010, 2013). For instance, as the second most abundant AAs in milk from d 8 to 29 of lactation (Wu and Knabe, 1994), Lys is required for maintaining intestinal mucosal integrity, particularly under stress conditions such as weaning, thereby promoting piglet health (He et al., 2013). Moreover, available evidence indicates that low-protein diets supplemented with crystalline AAs can improve Lys utilization efficiency for milk production (Zhang et al., 2024a). Asp, a major metabolic substrate in the small intestine (Qi et al., 2020), also contributes to intestinal integrity and immune function during inflammation (Li et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2017). Similar to Asp, Glu serves as an important energy source for intestinal cells and is crucial for the development of intestinal mucosal morphology (Cruzat et al., 2018; Li et al., 2024b). In the present study, the concentrations of Lys and Asp in sow milk were significantly higher in the 0.30% Met group than in the 18% CP group, accompanied by a tendency toward higher Glu levels. These results suggest that the improved lactation performance induced by 0.30% Met supplementation in low-protein diets may be associated with increased concentrations of key AAs in milk.

Apart from milk AAs, milk fatty acids, especially PUFAs, play an important role in early neonatal development, contributing to cognitive function, vision, mucosal integrity, and immune responses (Metzler-Zebeli, 2021). Higher intake of n-3 PUFAs through sow milk is correlated with increased incorporation of these fatty acids into piglet enteric tissues and cell membranes, where they exert bioactive effects on immune regulation and epithelial barrier function (Lauridsen, 2020). Moreover, maternal higher intake of n-3 PUFA has been shown to increase ADG by approximately 11% (Chen et al., 2017). Consistently, the current data showed that low-protein 0.30% Met diets significantly increased milk C18:3n3 concentrations to levels comparable to normal protein diets, which corresponded with higher piglet weaning weight and ADG. Thus, under low-protein feeding conditions, appropriate maternal Met supplementation (0.30%) may serve as an effective strategy to enhance n-3 PUFA intake in pre-weaning piglets. Notably, before weaning, piglets obtain more n-3 PUFAs from milk than via placental transfer (Vodolazska and Lauridsen, 2020; Zentek et al., 2012), further supporting the rationale for optimizing sow diets during late gestation and lactation to boost milk n-3 PUFA content.

Given the improved milk composition observed in this study, further investigation was conducted to determine whether redox status was also enhanced, since oxidative stress can negatively affect milk components in lactating sows (Su et al., 2017). The MDA content is a biomarker positively associated with oxidative damage, and higher MDA concentrations indicate greater oxidative stress (He et al., 2023). However, antioxidant enzymes such as T-SOD, T-AOC, and GSH-Px could help mitigate the levels of toxic reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Galasso et al., 2021). In this study, maternal low-protein 0.30% Met diets increased serum T-SOD activity in sows, as well as T-AOC and GSH-Px activities in piglets, to levels comparable to or higher than those in the normal-protein group. This was accompanied by reduced MDA concentrations in both sows and piglets compared with the 0.51% group, indicating improved systemic antioxidant status. Furthermore, elevated Tau, a methionine metabolite, was detected in both milk and piglet serum following maternal 0.30% Met supplementation, which may further contribute to enhanced antioxidant defense, as a causal relationship between Tau and the body's antioxidant system has been established previously (Han et al., 2020). Furthermore, plasma antioxidant enzyme levels have been positively correlated with improvements in milk composition and piglet growth performance (Jin et al., 2025a,b; Liu et al., 2023b). Therefore, the enhanced redox status observed in sows and piglets fed low-protein diets with 0.30% Met likely contributed to the optimized milk composition in sows and the improved growth performance of their offspring.

Besides lactation performance, the weaning-to-estrus interval is a key indicator of sow reproductive efficiency (Schwarz et al., 2021). Prolonged intervals and lower post-weaning estrus rates reduce sow longevity, leading to higher culling rates and increased production costs (Rempel et al., 2017). In the present study, low-protein diets supplemented with 0.30% Met shortened the weaning-to-estrus interval to a level similar to that of the normal-protein group. These beneficial effects may be due to an enhanced antioxidant capacity observed in sows, as increased oxidative stress has been linked to longer estrus intervals (Hu et al., 2019). Conversely, supplementation with 0.51% Met prolonged the weaning-to-estrus interval, which may be related to increased serum Hcy levels in sows. Hcy accumulation can exacerbate oxidative stress by promoting ROS generation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production (Ding et al., 2014; Wu et al., 2022).

Interestingly, maternal gut microbiota can influence offspring development, in part through vertical transmission via milk (Macpherson et al., 2017). Therefore, the current study examined changes in the gut microbiota of sows following Met supplementation. At the phylum level, low-protein diets with 0.30% Met increased the relative abundance of Firmicutes, a phylum linked to improved nutrient utilization and lactation performance (Jami et al., 2014; Leblois et al., 2018). At the genus level, the 0.30% Met group showed significantly lower Terrisporobacter abundance and higher Norank_f_Muribaculaceae abundance compared with the 0.51% group, and these values were comparable to those observed in the 18% CP group. Although Terrisporobacter is often described as an acetate-producer, recent studies in infants suggest that its elevated abundance may exacerbate oxidative stress (Cai et al., 2019; Li et al., 2024a). Consistently, the correlation analyses revealed negative associations between Terrisporobacter abundance and key performance parameters, including piglet weaning weight, ADG from d 3 to 21, and milk yield. In contrast, Norank_f_Muribaculaceae was positively correlated with milk yield and is generally regarded as a beneficial genus due to its anti-inflammatory properties (Li et al., 2024c). Thus, low-protein diets with 0.30% Met appear to promote a more favorable gut microbiota structure in sows. Moreover, sows fed low-protein diets with 0.30% Met exhibited higher fecal concentrations of acetic acid and butyric acid, similar to those in the normal-protein group. Acetic acid possesses anti-inflammatory activity and contributes to intestinal homeostasis (Wei et al., 2022). Butyric acid enhances intestinal barrier function by upregulating tight-junction protein expression (Huang et al., 2015; Wei et al., 2022). Collectively, these findings suggest that supplementing low-protein diets with 0.30% Met helps restore sow intestinal microbiota and SCFA profiles to a state comparable to that of normal-protein diets, which may in turn support offspring growth.

Given that the offspring's intestinal microbiota can be influenced by the maternal microbial composition (Tancredi et al., 2025), the current study analyzed the colonic microbiota of piglets. The results showed that maternal low-protein diets supplemented with 0.30% Met significantly reduced the abundance of Firmicutes and increased that of Bacteroidota in piglet colons, consistent with previous findings (Azad et al., 2018). At the genus level, the rise in Bacteroidota was primarily driven by higher Christensenellaceae_R-7_group relative abundances. Although the overall Firmicutes abundance decreased, specific beneficial genera within this phylum—such as Lactobacillus and Phascolarctobacterium—were enriched to levels comparable to those in the 18% CP group. Lactobacillus, a well-characterized probiotic, improves growth performance, feed efficiency, nutrient utilization, and immune function (Donsbough et al., 2010). Similarly, Christensenellaceae_R-7_group and Phascolarctobacterium contribute to intestinal homeostasis through the production of SCFAs, including acetate, butyrate, and propionate acid (Guo et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2021). Furthermore, spearman correlation analyses showed that the relative abundances of Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, Bacteroides, Lactobacillus, and Phascolarctobacterium in piglet's colonic contents were positively correlated with piglet ADG from d 3 to 21, suggesting the altered microbiota structure may support enhanced growth of piglets. However, these beneficial microbial changes were absent in the 0.51% Met group. Instead, maternal supplementation with 0.51% Met increased the relative abundance of Actinobacteriota in piglet colons, a phylum also elevated in sows and previously associated with diarrheal conditions in pigs (Wang et al., 2023). The parallel shifts in microbial profiles between sows and their offspring suggest potential vertical transmission, although strain-level sequencing would be needed to confirm this route. Furthermore, based on the changes in SCFA-producing bacteria, SCFA concentrations were measured in piglet colonic contents. Maternal low-protein diets with 0.30% Met significantly increased acetic acid and butyric acid levels, further indicating an improved intestinal environment in piglets.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provides the first evidence that protein-restricted diets (15%) with optimal Met supplementation (0.30%) can enhance sow lactation performance and support suckling piglet growth to an extent comparable to normal-protein diets (18% CP) (Fig. S3). These benefits appear to be mediated through improvements in milk yield and composition, systemic redox status, and gut microbiota structure. Elucidating the molecular mechanisms by which optimal maternal Met supplementation promotes offspring growth will advance the understanding of how protein-restricted diets can serve as an effective nutritional strategy for sows during late gestation and lactation, particularly in the context of soybean meal reduction and substitution. However, this study also has several limitations. For instance, the mechanism through which elevated Hcy levels impair sow reproductive performance, as well as the origin of harmful bacteria in the piglet intestines (specifically, whether they are transmitted from sows), warrant further investigation.
