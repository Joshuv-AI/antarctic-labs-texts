High‐Flow Nasal Cannula and Continuous Positive Airway Pressure in Infants With Acute Bronchiolitis—Trends of Use in Norway
Authors: Knut Øymar, Joel Selvakumar, Maren Våland Øymar, Ingvild Dalen, Ken Erik Klaseie, Ujinthini Sivanantham, Kasper Øvsthus, Inger Heimdal, Kari Holte, Kari Risnes, Ketil Størdal, Claus Klingenberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimHigh‐flow nasal cannula (HFNC) and continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) are options for respiratory support in infants with bronchiolitis. The aim of this study was to evaluate the use of HFNC and CPAP in infants with bronchiolitis at five hospitals in Norway to study time trends over seasons and differences in use between hospitals.MethodsIn this multicentre retrospective study, we compared data from 1186 infants < 12 months of age hospitalized for bronchiolitis at five Norwegian hospitals during the winter seasons 2017–2018, 2018–2019, and 2021–2022.ResultsOver the three seasons, HFNC was provided to 29%–33% and CPAP to 8%–18% of all infants, but with a substantial difference between hospitals for both modalities. The change between seasons was less prominent but also differed between hospitals. The length of stay was longer, and the age and weight were lower in those receiving any non‐invasive respiratory support, but this did not differ between infants treated with HFNC or CPAP.ConclusionIn Norwegian hospitals there was a substantial variation in the use of HFNC and CPAP for infants with bronchiolitis. This calls for unified and evidence‐based guidelines for the use of non‐invasive respiratory support for this patient group.

Introduction

Both in Norway and worldwide, 2%–3% of infants are hospitalized for bronchiolitis during their first year of life, leading to a significant seasonal workload burden for paediatric departments [1, 2]. According to recent guidelines, the basis for treatment is minimal handling, with oxygen, respiratory, and nutritional support only when needed [1, 3, 4]. Hypoxemia is common, and standard oxygen therapy (SOT) (unhumidified oxygen via nasal cannula or mask) is used both in high‐ and low‐income countries for those with mild or moderate respiratory distress to achieve a satisfactory oxygen saturation [1]. For children failing to respond to SOT, continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) has been used for decades as rescue therapy when available. This may have contributed to fewer children with bronchiolitis requiring mechanical ventilation [5]. Though CPAP may be used at ordinary paediatric wards [6], it is not always well tolerated by the child; it requires close surveillance and care and thus is often provided in a paediatric intensive care unit (PICU) setting [5].

High‐flow nasal cannula (HFNC) has over the last decades been introduced as respiratory support in several paediatric conditions with respiratory distress. HFNC delivers an adjustable flow of humidified gas with the ability to adjust oxygen concentration relative to the patient's demand [7, 8, 9]. Compared to CPAP, HFNC is better tolerated by the patients, more easily available in low‐resource settings, and has fewer reported side effects [10]. In many paediatric wards, HFNC has replaced CPAP as the most common respiratory support when escalating treatment beyond SOT for infants with bronchiolitis [9, 10].

The use of non‐invasive respiratory support (HFNC or CPAP) could potentially reduce respiratory distress, the need for mechanical ventilation, and reduce the length of hospital stay. However, both CPAP and HFNC are resource demanding compared to SOT, and a concern for overuse has been discussed [11, 12]. To ensure a balance between resources used and patient outcomes, clear guidelines for when HFNC or CPAP is recommended would be optimal and help ensure that treatment is more consistent between different paediatric departments [13].

The process of incorporating new treatment modalities such as HFNC may vary substantially between hospitals [14, 15]. The aim of this study was to evaluate the use of HFNC and CPAP in infants with bronchiolitis at five hospitals in Norway, to study time trends over seasons, and differences in use between hospitals. Such benchmarking may, together with recommendations from the literature, facilitate optimal use of HFNC and CPAP for infants with bronchiolitis.

Methods

Setting and Ethics

The study included data from five Norwegian hospitals: Østfold Hospital, Drammen Hospital, St. Olav's University Hospital (Trondheim), Stavanger University Hospital, and University Hospital of North‐Norway (Tromsø). Three seasons were included: 2017–2018 and 2018–2019 before the Covid‐19 pandemic and the post‐pandemic 2021–2022 season. For the two first seasons, the study period was November 1st to April 30; for the last season, the study period was extended to include the period from August 1st to April 30 to accommodate the early incidence of seasonal bronchiolitis that year. The study was assessed by the Regional Ethics Committee (number 2019/126) as a quality project and not subject to formal submission, with a waiver of individual consent and confidentiality. The data protection officers at respective participating hospitals approved the study.

Data Collection and Flow

We included infants < 12 months of age hospitalized for bronchiolitis. Eligible patients were identified retrospectively using the primary and secondary diagnosis given at discharge. Infants with a comorbidity possibly affecting pulmonary function, such as bronchopulmonary dysplasia and cystic fibrosis, were excluded [16]. Infants who were intubated or transferred to a tertiary hospital for intensive care were registered but not included in analyses. Infants admitted to the hospitals > 6 h were considered hospitalized.

Data were extracted from the hospital's electronic medical records. We included clinical characteristics, diagnostic tests, and treatment modalities with a focus on the mode of respiratory support. The annual number of children born (2017, 2018, and 2021) in the catchment area of the respective hospitals was extracted from the Medical Birth Registry of Norway. Data were registered and stored in a common secure server for research (Services for sensitive data; University of Oslo).

Statistics and Analyses

Results are presented as numbers and proportions (%) or as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), as appropriate. Differences between hospitals at each time period, time trends, and differences in time trends between hospitals in the use of HFNC/CPAP were analysed by multilevel logistic regression, both unadjusted and adjusted for infant's sex, age at hospitalization, prematurity (gestational age < 37 weeks), and weight, and allowing for clustering of observations within hospitals by including a random intercepts term. Differences between children treated with HFNC/CPAP versus not treated were analysed by Chi‐square tests for categorical data and Mann–Whitney tests for continuous data. Analyses were performed with SPSS Statistics 30 and Stata 17, and p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

The use of HFNC differed significantly between hospitals for all seasons but was for most hospitals relatively stable throughout the period (Table 1 and Figure 2). An exception was Østfold Hospital where HFNC was not used during the first season and used in a lower proportion of infants compared to the other hospitals. Altogether, the use of HFNC was stable during the period, but the seasonal variation differed significantly between hospitals (Table 2).

The use of CPAP also differed significantly between hospitals for all seasons. In four hospitals, the use increased from the first to the second period and was stable thereafter (Table 1 and Figure 2). Altogether, in unadjusted analysis, the use of CPAP increased over the seasons, with a statistically significant difference in time trends between hospitals (Table 2).

Any non‐invasive respiratory support doubled over the period at one hospital (Østfold) but was more stable for the other hospitals (Figure 2 and Table 1). Whereas Trondheim and Tromsø mainly used HFNC and less CPAP, this was the opposite for Østfold. For all hospitals combined, the change between seasons was non‐significant, but there was a significant difference in time trend between hospitals after adjustment (Table 2).

The use of HFNC, CPAP, or any non‐invasive respiratory support did not differ between groups of different gestational age (Table 3). However, infants receiving any non‐invasive respiratory support were younger and had lower weight than those without support. The length of stay was longer in those receiving any non‐invasive respiratory support but did not differ between HFNC and CPAP. Readmission rates were similar between groups.

Discussion

In this retrospective study including 1186 infants with bronchiolitis covering three seasons, we show a high use of HFNC and CPAP in Norway, but also a marked variation between five Norwegian hospitals. However, except for one hospital, there were minor changes in the use over the three seasons. The large practice variations within a country with only around 55.000 annual births is striking and warrants harmonization of national clinical practice guidelines.

When comparing data from studies on the use of non‐invasive respiratory support for infants with bronchiolitis, the population studied must be considered. Infants in our study were all below 1 year of age; they were all hospitalized, and consequently, most cases would be defined as moderate–severe bronchiolitis [1].

The effect of SOT, HFNC, and CPAP in infants with bronchiolitis has been studied in high‐quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and systematic reviews [5, 8, 9]. Most children with mild to moderate respiratory distress seem to be managed well on SOT. However, HFNC reduces the risk of treatment escalation by half compared to SOT, and children failing to SOT often respond well to HFNC [5, 10]. A systematic review and meta‐analysis of RCTs comparing HFNC vs. CPAP reported that among infants < 12 months, HFNC showed a higher risk of treatment failure [5]. Similar results were shown in a recent meta‐analysis from Laiho et al.; however, that study included children up to 24 months of age [18].

Based on the above studies, SOT is suggested as the first‐line treatment for hypoxia in bronchiolitis in clinical guidelines [11]. If escalation is needed, it is suggested that HFNC is the first choice for rescue therapy because it is safe, better tolerated than CPAP, and with less or no need for sedation [19]. CPAP is an option when treatment with HFNC fails [10, 11, 19]. However, the level of symptoms for when to initiate or escalate treatment is often not clearly specified in the existing European and US bronchiolitis guidelines [11, 12, 19]. This may be one reason for the substantial practice variation in our study, which is not unique to Norway. A Dutch study also showed a large variation between hospitals in both initiation and weaning from HFNC [14]. In a large US study, there were large variations in the use of HFNC both outside and in PICUs [15]. A study from Australia and New Zealand reported that nurses would choose to initiate HFNC more frequently than doctors, even in non‐hypoxic children [20]. Currently, > 50% of children with mild to moderate bronchiolitis admitted in the USA and Australia are receiving HFNC therapy [21, 22], in line with our study. Overall, variations in use suggest there is a need for better guidelines for the initiation of HFNC in bronchiolitis.

In our study, the use of non‐invasive respiratory support was associated with longer hospitalization. This may reflect confounding by indication with infants receiving non‐invasive respiratory support being more ill, supported by the finding that they were also younger. Some studies have shown a reduction in duration of hospitalization and length of oxygen therapy by using HFNC [9]. This has raised the question whether HFNC should be used as primary rather than rescue therapy [10]. Others argue that there is an overuse of HFNC in bronchiolitis [11, 12]. Three RCTs with a total of 2000 participants did not show any beneficial outcome with HFNC compared to SOT, with similar oxygen duration, duration of hospitalization, rates of transfer to PICU and intubation [23, 24, 25]. Moreover, the substantial increase in the use of both HFNC and CPAP during recent years has been paralleled by an increase in PICU admissions and costs, and in some studies duration of hospitalization [11]. Cost‐effectiveness is an important aspect when deciding treatment level [10].

Increased use of resources and costs associated with non‐invasive respiratory support and possibly also increased length of stay have resulted in an increasing tendency for reducing respiratory support beyond SOT, especially HFNC [11, 26]. In a US quality improvement project, a standardized HFNC protocol for initiation and weaning was associated with less time on HFNC and decreased length of stay both in the PICU and the hospital, without a change in adverse events [27]. A similar program including 250 hospitals in the USA reported a reduction in both initiation and duration of HFNC but no change in length of stay [28]. The authors concluded that reduced utilization was due to intervention effects rather than temporal trends.

Some new guidelines recommend a stricter indication for using HFNC in infants with mild to moderate bronchiolitis. The guidelines from The Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne state that HFNC should be reserved for those where a trial of SOT fails to keep the oxygen saturation above 90% [29]. Implementing an Italian 2023 guideline which clearly states that HFNC is a second‐line option after SOT failure only significantly reduced HFNC use from 43.5% to 5.5% [13]. A recent extensive review concluded that HFNC should only be initiated for refractory hypoxemia or impeding respiratory failure [30].

We have not found similar surveillance studies as ours from the other Nordic countries reporting the actual use of respiratory support for infants with bronchiolitis. The results from some Nordic studies may, however, indicate a comparable trend with increasing HFNC use and variable practices between hospitals [31, 32, 33]. Both in Norway and the other Nordic countries, the national guidelines on bronchiolitis seem to lack specific recommendations on when to initiate or wean from HFNC and CPAP.

The rather large number of participants, the inclusion of five hospitals and three seasons are strengths of this study. A weakness is that we did not explicitly register the use of SOT; therefore we cannot report how many children were given SOT without escalation to HFNC and/or CPAP.

In conclusion, we have shown a substantial variation between Norwegian hospitals in the use of HFNC and CPAP for infants with bronchiolitis. There is a worldwide tendency to initiate deimplementation programs to address and reduce overuse of HFNC in bronchiolitis. Our results call for similar programs and updated guidelines both in Norway and other Nordic countries with clear advice when to initiate non‐invasive respiratory support and which modality to choose.
