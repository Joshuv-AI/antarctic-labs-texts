A new approach to (3+1)-dimensional TQFTs via topological modular forms
Sergei Gukov, Vyacheslav Krushkal, Lennart Meier, Du Pei

Abstract
. In this paper, we present a construction toward a new type of TQFTs at the crossroads of low-dimensional topology, algebraic geometry, physics, and homotopy theory. It assigns TMF-modules to closed 3 3 -manifolds and maps of TMF-modules to 4 4 -dimensional cobordisms. This is a mathematical proposal for one of the simplest examples in a family of π ∗ ​ ( TMF ) {\pi}_{*}({\rm TMF}) -valued invariants of 4 4 -manifolds which are expected to arise from 6 6 -dimensional superconformal field theories. As part of the construction, we define TMF-modules associated with symmetric bilinear forms, using (spectral) derived algebraic geometry. The invariant of unimodular bilinear forms takes values in π ∗ ​ ( TMF ) {\pi}_{*}({\rm TMF}) , conjecturally generalizing the theta function of a lattice. We discuss gluing properties of the invariants. We also demonstrate some interesting physics applications of the TMF-modules such as distinguishing phases of quantum field theories in various dimensions.

2.2.
4d TQFT over TMF

Defining and computing (even at the level of physics) the new invariants when the 6d theory is no longer a free theory appears to be a daunting task. Our approach
to overcoming this difficulty is to utilize a novel type of 4d TQFT structure not over the usual field of complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
, but over TMF. In other words, we now associate to a 3-manifold a TMF-module,

M_{3}\quad\leadsto\quad\mathcal{Z}(M_{3})\in\text{TMF-mod},

and to a 4d cobordism
W
from
M_{3}
to
M^{\prime}_{3}
a morphism of modules

W\quad\leadsto\quad\mathcal{Z}(W):\;\mathcal{Z}(M_{3})\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}(M_{3}^{\prime}).

When
M_{3}=M_{3}^{\prime}=\emptyset
, one has
\mathcal{Z}(M_{3})=\mathcal{Z}(M_{3}^{\prime})=
TMF, and taking the image of 1 under
\mathcal{Z}(W)
in the homotopy group allows one to interpret
\mathcal{Z}(W)\in\pi_{*}
TMF.

The existence of such TQFT follows from considering the compactification of the 6d theory on open 4-manifolds. If
M_{4}
has a boundary
\partial M_{4}=M_{3}
, then, after compactifying the 6d theory on it, instead of having a stand-alone theory
T[M_{4}]
, it is now a boundary condition for a three-dimensional theory
T[M_{3}]
. In the set-up considered in
[
GPPV21
]
,
T[M_{3}]
has 3d
\mathcal{N}=1
supersymmetry, again the minimal amount in 3d. Then, as we will discuss in greater detail and generality in Section
10
, there is a natural TMF-module associated with it, which in physics is interpreted as the space of 2d
(0,1)
boundary conditions. And
T[M_{4}]
precisely takes value in this TMF-module as it is a specific boundary condition. When
W
is a cobordism from
M_{3}
to
M_{3}^{\prime}
,
T[W]
is now an interface between
T[M_{3}]
and
T[M_{3}^{\prime}]
, and similarly it gives a map between two TMF-modules.

The 4d TQFT that we are going to build corresponds to taking the 6d theory
T
to be the “free tensor multiplet.” Although, being a free theory, it is not expected to lead to powerful invariants of four-manifolds that can detect smooth structures, it will serve as a testing ground for the existence and functionality for such TQFTs from more general 6d theories.

Furthermore, the computation of the TMF-modules are also of interest from the quantum field theory point of view. In this simplest case,
T[M_{3}]
will be given by an abelian gauge theory, possibly with Chern–Simons levels, and, as we will discuss in Section
10
, the TMF-modules constructed are often compatible with our knowledge about the physics of such 3d theories, sometimes leading to new insights and new predictions.

3.
Background on topological modular forms

3.1.
Modular forms and moduli of elliptic curves

A
(weakly holomorphic) modular form of weight
k
is a holomorphic function
f
on the upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}
such that

f\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\right)=(c\tau+d)^{k}f(\tau)

for all
\tau\in\mathbb{H}
and all
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, having at most a pole at
\infty
. Equivalently, a modular form of weight
k
is a holomorphic section of the line bundle
\omega_{\mathbb{C}}^{\otimes k}
on the orbifold
[\mathbb{H}/\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})]
, where
\omega_{\mathbb{C}}
by definition descends from
\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{C}
via the
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-action

\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\cdot(\tau,z)=\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},(c\tau+d)z\right).

The orbifold
[\mathbb{H}/\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})]
classifies complex elliptic curves (which are all of the form
\mathbb{C}/(\tau\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z})
for some
\tau\in\mathbb{H}
)
[
Hai08
]
. In algebraic geometry, we can take elliptic curves over arbitrary commutative rings (and even more generally) and thus it makes sense to replace
[\mathbb{H}/\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})]
by the moduli stack of elliptic curves
\mathcal{M}
defined over the integers. We will also denote
[\mathbb{H}/\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})]
by
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}
as it agrees with the complex orbifold associated to the complexification of
\mathcal{M}
. A map
\varphi\colon X\to\mathcal{M}
corresponds exactly to an elliptic curve over
X
. More precisely,
\mathcal{M}
carries the universal elliptic curve
\mathcal{E}
and
\varphi
corresponds to the elliptic curve
\varphi^{*}\mathcal{E}
. Over the complex numbers,
\mathcal{E}
can be constructed as
[\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{C}/(\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\ltimes\mathbb{Z}^{2})]
.

As every elliptic curve,
\mathcal{E}
comes with a “neutral element” of its group structure; since it is an elliptic curve over
\mathcal{M}
, this takes the form of a section
e\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{E}
of the structure map
p\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{M}
. (The section
e
defines a closed immersion and more precisely a relative Cartier divisor; we will sometimes identify
e
with this divisor.) We obtain a line bundle
\omega=e^{*}\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{E}/\mathcal{M}}
as the pullback of the sheaf of differentials. Using that
dz
defines a trivialization of the sheaf of differentials of a complex elliptic curve
\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{Z}+\tau\mathbb{Z}
, one produces an isomorphism of the complexification of
\omega
with
\omega_{\mathbb{C}}
.

The space
M_{k}=H^{0}(\mathcal{M};\omega^{\otimes k})
of global sections of
\omega^{\otimes k}
on
\mathcal{M}
is called the space of
integral modular forms of weight
k
. By the
q
-expansion principle, the map
M_{k}\to M_{k}^{\mathbb{C}}
into complex modular forms identifies
M_{k}
with the complex modular forms with integral
q
-expansion. For more details and background see e.g.
[
MO20
, Appendix A]
and the references therein.

3.2.
Topological modular forms

Topological modular forms
\TMF
are a refinement of classical modular forms. They are a multiplicative cohomology theory whose coefficients
\TMF^{*}(\pt)
resemble the ring of integral modular forms, but incorporate torsion. More precisely, there is a homomorphism
\TMF^{-2k}(\pt)\to M_{k}
, which becomes an isomorphism upon inverting
6
. Variants of topological modular forms are also known under the name of elliptic cohomology. The theory of topological modular forms was built by Hopkins together with Goerss, Miller and Mahowald and later revamped by Lurie. We refer to
[
DFHH14
,
Beh20
,
Hop02
]
and
[
Lur09
]
for details, background and references, or to
[
GPPV21
, Appendix A]
for another short overview.

Both for the construction of
\TMF
and for our purposes, it is important to view
\TMF
not just as a multiplicative cohomology theory but as an
E_{\infty}
-ring spectrum. These are spectra with a highly-coherent commutative multiplication. Many frameworks exist to make this precise: for an
\infty
-categorical treatment see
[
Lur12
]
or the more succinct
[
Gep20
]
. Alternatively, they can e.g. be modeled as commutative monoids in symmetric or orthogonal spectra; see e.g.
[

]

[

[

•

-ring spectra

\pi_{*}R
. They moreover represent a multiplicative cohomology theory such that
R^{*}(\pt)\cong\pi_{-*}R
.

•

E_{\infty}
-ring spectra behave in many ways like usual commutative rings (or commutative differential graded algebras). In particular, they have an
\infty

Like modular forms arise as the global sections of a sheaf on

of

and

whose associated edge homomorphism

4.
Looijenga line bundles

As in the last section, let us denote by
\mathcal{M}
the moduli stack of elliptic curves and by
\mathcal{E}
the universal elliptic curve over
\mathcal{M}
. Let
b
be an integral symmetric bilinear form, i.e. a symmetric bilinear map
b\colon\mathbb{Z}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{Z}
. (For brevity, we will assume from now on that all our bilinear forms are integral and symmetric.) Following Looijenga
[
Loo77
]
, one can associate to
b
a line bundle on
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\times_{\mathcal{M}}d}
. We will discuss this below, as well as a novel extension to derived line bundles, yielding
\TMF
-modules upon taking global sections.

4.1.
The complex picture

Throughout this section, we will view the complex universal elliptic curve
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}
as the orbifold
[\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{C}/(\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\ltimes\mathbb{Z}^{2})]
. Correspondingly, we view the fiber product
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\times_{\mathcal{M}}d}
as the orbifold
[\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{C}^{d}/\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\ltimes(\mathbb{Z}^{2})^{d}]
. Given an element
(\tau,z)\in\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{C}^{d}
and elements
A\in\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
(m_{1},m_{2})\in(\mathbb{Z}^{2})^{d}
, the action is given by

\displaystyle A\cdot(\tau,z)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\,,\,(c\tau+d)^{-1}z\right)

\displaystyle(m_{1},m_{2})\cdot(\tau,z)

\displaystyle=(\tau,z+m_{1}\tau+m_{2}).

Following
[
Rez20
]
we define:

Definition 4.1
.

Let
b
be a bilinear form on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and denote its
\mathbb{C}
-bilinear extension
\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
by the same symbol. We define the
complex Looijenga line bundle

\mathcal{L}_{b}^{\mathbb{C}}
on
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\times_{\mathcal{M}}d}
by defining an action of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\ltimes(\mathbb{Z}^{2})^{d}
on
\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{C}^{d}\times\mathbb{C}
as follows (using notation as above):

\displaystyle A\cdot(\tau,z,y)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},(c\tau+d)^{-1}z,e^{\pi i(c(c\tau+d)^{-1}b(z,z))}y\right)

\displaystyle(m_{1},m_{2})\cdot(\tau,z,y)

\displaystyle=\left(\tau,z+m_{1}\tau+m_{2},e^{-2\pi i(b(z,m_{1})+\frac{1}{2}b(m_{1},m_{1})\tau)}y\right).

In the case that
b
is positive definite and
even
(i.e.
b(v,v)\in 2\mathbb{Z}
for all
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
), global sections
f
of
\mathcal{L}_{b}^{\mathbb{C}}\otimes\omega_{\mathbb{C}}^{\otimes k}
are called a
Jacobi form of weight
k
and index
b
in
[
BS23
, Definition 1.3]
(see also
[
Kri96
]
) if their Fourier expansion is well-behaved, i.e. if the pullback of
f
to a function on
\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{C}^{d}
can be written as

(4.2)

f(\tau,z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}q^{n}\sum_{r\in L^{\vee}:b(r,r)\leq 2n}c(n,r)e^{2\pi ib(z,r)}.

Here,
q=e^{2\pi i\tau}
and
L^{\vee}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\,:\,b(z,m)\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ for all }m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
}.

The precise formulae are closely related to the transformation behavior of theta functions. If
b
is positive definite, even and
unimodular
(i.e. defining an isomorphism from
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
to its dual), we define

(4.3)

\theta_{b}\colon\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C},\qquad(\tau,z)\mapsto\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}e^{\pi i\tau b(v,v)+2\pi ib(v,z)}.

The function
\theta_{b}
is an example of a Jacobi form of weight
\frac{d}{2}
with respect to
b
(see e.g.
[
Kri96
, Section 4]
or
[
KP84
, Proposition 3.4, 3.5]
). Of course, the theory of theta functions is much older than these sources, going back to the 19th century, and was invented and developed by Jacobi, Rosenhain, Riemann etc.

From the formulae in
4.1
, we directly deduce the following properties:

(1)

(Additivity)
\mathcal{L}_{b+b^{\prime}}^{\mathbb{C}}\cong\mathcal{L}_{b}^{\mathbb{C}}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{b^{\prime}}^{\mathbb{C}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is the structure sheaf.

(2)

(Functoriality) A morphism
\mathbb{Z}^{d_{1}}\to\mathbb{Z}^{d_{2}}
corresponds to a matrix
A
, which induces a morphism
\mathcal{E}^{A}\colon\mathcal{E}^{\times_{\mathcal{M}}d_{1}}_{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathcal{E}^{\times_{\mathcal{M}}d_{2}}_{\mathbb{C}}
. Moreover, given a bilinear form
b\in\Bil(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
with matrix
B
, we can define a new bilinear form
A^{*}b\in\Bil(\mathbb{Z}^{e})
with matrix
A^{T}BA
. With this notation, there is an isomorphism
\mathcal{L}_{A^{*}b}^{\mathbb{C}}\cong(\mathcal{E}^{A})^{*}\mathcal{L}(b)^{\mathbb{C}}
.

Moreover, there is an isomorphism
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{C}}_{(1)}
with
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathbb{C}}(e)\otimes\omega_{\mathbb{C}}

[
BM25
]
. Here and in the following, we will often identify a bilinear form with its defining matrix; thus, e.g.,
(1)
stands for the bilinear form
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
, sending
(v,w)
to
vw
.

Beyond the transformation behavior of theta functions, there is also a second motivation for the definition of the Looijenga line bundles: there is essentially no other choice with the properties above. (A third motivation from algebraic topology is given in
[
Rez20
]
.)

Proposition 4.4
.

Suppose that for every bilinear form
b\in\Bil(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
, there is a line bundle
\mathcal{K}_{b}
on
\mathcal{E}^{\times_{\mathcal{M}}d}_{\mathbb{C}}
, satisfying additivity and functoriality as above. Assume moreover
\mathcal{K}_{(1)}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathbb{C}}(e)\otimes\omega_{\mathbb{C}}
. Then
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{C}}_{b}\cong\mathcal{K}_{b}
for all
b\in\Bil(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
.

Proof.

Every symmetric integer matrix is a sum of symmetric matrices with at most two nonzero entries, which equal
\pm 1
. Thus, every bilinear form on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is a sum of bilinear forms pulled back along a projection
\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{Z}^{2}
or
\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{Z}
from
h=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
or
(1)
, respectively, or their negatives. By additivity and functoriality, it remains to show that
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{C}}_{h}\cong\mathcal{K}_{h}
.

Note that
h\oplus(1)\cong(1)\oplus(-1)\oplus(1)
. Since pullback along the first two factors of
h\oplus(1)
is
h
again and
\mathcal{L}_{(-1)}^{\mathbb{C}}\cong\left(\mathcal{L}_{(1)}^{\mathbb{C}}\right)^{-1}
by additivity (and likewise for
\mathcal{K}
), the result follows. (See
Remark

5.9
for more details.)
∎

This implies that every approach to Looijenga line bundles satisfying the same formal properties and normalization will yield (up to isomorphism) the same line bundles.

For us, a

To associate to a

(1)

(Additivity)
\mathcal{L}_{b+b^{\prime}}\simeq\mathcal{L}_{b}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{b^{\prime}}

(2)

(Functoriality) A morphism
\mathbb{Z}^{e}\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}

\mathcal{E}^{A}\colon\mathcal{E}^{\times_{\mathcal{M}}e}\to\mathcal{E}^{\times_{\mathcal{M}}d}

with matrix

(3)

(Normalization)
\mathcal{L}_{(1)}\simeq\mathcal{O}^{\mathrm{top}}_{\mathcal{E}}(e)[-2]

\mathcal{O}^{\mathrm{top}}_{\mathcal{E}}(e)

Analogously to

, one shows that the three properties above define the derived and integral Looijenga line bundles uniquely up to equivalence/isomorphism, but a priori these conditions might overdetermine the (derived) line bundle.

To be more precise, let
\Lat
be the category of lattices, i.e. finitely generated free abelian groups. (Equivalently, its objects might be viewed as

for

\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathbb{Z}^{d}=\mathcal{E}^{\times_{\mathcal{M}}d}

9

9

More precisely, the
(2,1)

Consider the three functors

\displaystyle\Bil\colon\Lat^{\op}\to\AbGroup,

\displaystyle\qquad\Lambda\mapsto\Bil(\Lambda),

\mathbb{Z}[(-)^{\vee}]

\Lambda

Crucial Observation
.

The map
\mathbb{Z}[()^{\vee}]\to\Bil
is a surjection with kernel generated by three explicit relations. Thus, defining integral and derived Looijenga line bundles reduces to checking these three relations.

In our situation, these three relations boil down to the following:

(1)

The pullbacks of
\mathcal{L}_{(1)}\simeq\mathcal{O}^{\mathrm{top}}_{\mathcal{E}}(e)[-2]

(2)

The relationship to anomalies and Lurie’s work on

To a quadratic form

Definition 6.1

6.1.
Duality

Our main goal in this section is to show that

and
L_{b}
are dual up to a shift. Recall that

are the global sections of the derived line bundle

on

is the dual of the global sections of the dual

As a warm-up, we consider instead of a derived line bundle on a stack a usual line bundle

on a smooth projective variety

We need to upgrade this in two ways: first, we do not have a variety over a field but a “relative variety” over the stack
\mathcal{M}
. Secondly, we are not working in classical algebraic geometry, but in spectral algebraic geometry. Such a generalization is exactly the topic of

as in
[
Lur18c
]
. The change of cohomological degree in Serre duality will translate in our setting into a shift by the relative dimension (namely

) and the identification of the dualizing sheaf will result in another shift (by

Instead of just having a result of the global sections of

\mathcal{M}
(since derived global sections on

10.
TMF-modules and quantum field theories

In this section, we will explain in more detail how TMF-modules arise from quantum field theory in three dimensions, how various results in previous sections can be understood from the physics point of view, and how TMF-modules can be a useful tool in the study of quantum field theories. We do not aim for mathematical rigor in this section, use physical language freely, and allow ourselves to speculate.

10.1.
TMF-modules and phases.

Beside mathematical applications, we also expect that the TMF-module invariants of 3d supersymmetric theories can be useful in the study of the structure of phases and dynamics of such theories. We now take a physics detour to discuss this in further details.

This new invariant is expected to detect finer structure then the more familiar invariants such as the Witten index
[
Wit82a
]
. For example, during a phases transition, the Witten index can remain constant while the TMF-module can change. This is because the Witten index for a 3d supersymmetric theory is ultimately the index of its KK-reduction on
T^{2}
, and different phases in 3d can become connected once reduced to 1d. For example, the Fayet–Iliopoulos (FI) parameter
[
FI74
]
for a 3d
\mathcal{N}=2
theory is real, and there can be walls separating different phases, but it will become complexified once we reduce the theory to 2d, allowing one to circumvent the transition point by turning on the imaginary part of the FI parameter. Other more familiar invariants (such as the algebra of BPS line operators) are often also subject to the same weakness. On the other hand, the TMF-module is a genuine invariant of 3d theories, and could better distinguish phases. We now give an example of this.

Example 10.4
.

Consider a family of 3d
\mathcal{N}=2
theory given by

[

and
[

for more details. The Witten index in either phase is

, associated with the unreduced TMF homology of

Phase transitions often happen when the spectrum of the theory becomes massless. At the phase boundary, the Witten index is ill-defined, but one can still consider the space of boundary conditions as a TMF-module. Denote the space at the phase transition as
\mathcal{B}_{0}
, and these on the two sides as
\mathcal{B}_{+}

\mathcal{B}_{-}

\mathcal{B}_{-}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}_{0}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}_{+}

but maybe not in the opposite directions due to the emergence of new massless degrees of freedom at the phase transition point allowing special boundary conditions that won’t exist away from this point.

What happens at the phase boundary? One can get some idea by compactifying and consider the space of boundary conditions for 1d
\mathcal{N}=2
supersymmetric theories. The space of boundary condition in a 1d theory is almost the same as its space of quantum states. If one considers supersymmetric boundary conditions, then one gets the space of supersymmetric (a.k.a. BPS) states. The TMF-module and its rank is respectively a refinement of the BPS Hilbert space and the Witten index. In the 1d case, there are two kinds of phase transitions. One is often referred to as “wall-crossing” which is when the spectrum of the theory become continuous. Another is when pairs of bosonic and fermionic states enter or leave the ground state. Describing and classifying the behavior of TMF and KO-module at phase boundary in a similar fashion should shed much light on the physics of phase transition in two and three dimensions. It would be interesting to understand whether there are additional constraints of these spaces and maps between them, and, furthermore, whether homotopy theory can be effectively used to classify phase transitions of 3d theories.

Remark 10.5
.

One can also use TMF-modules to tackle higher-dimensional theories, and there are different ways of achieving this. For example, one can consider the space of 2d
(0,1)
surface operators in higher-dimensional theories, which will again be TMF-modules. Another strategy is to first compactify the

(0,1)

This can lead to more interesting structures, as there are often more ways to compactify to 3d, such as considering different manifolds to compactify on and different backgrounds (e.g. holonomy and fluxes of global symmetries). Yet another — which is in a sense more natural from the TQFT perspective — method is to associate TMF-linear categories to 4d theories and higher categories to theories in even higher dimensions. We will give more comments about this generalization toward the end of this section. It would be interesting to see whether these approaches can be helpful in the study of phases of higher-dimensional theories.

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{1}}

For
i=0,1
consider the free and torsion subgroups of the first homology of
Y_{i}
,
H_{1}(Y_{i})\cong F_{i}\oplus T_{i}
. The goal is to relate the linking forms on
T_{0},T_{1}
and the intersection pairing on the cobordism
W
. To this end, consider
a_{0},b_{0}\in\tau H_{1}(Y_{0})
, and let
x,y
be elements in
H_{2}(W,Y_{0}\cup Y_{1})
with
\alpha_{0}(x)=a_{0},\alpha_{0}(y)=b_{0}
.
Denote
a_{1}:={\alpha}_{1}(x),b_{1}:={\alpha}_{1}(y)
. A priori, there are three cases: (1)
a_{1},b_{1}\in\tau H_{1}(Y_{1})
, (2) one of
a_{1},b_{1}
is a torsion elements and the other one is in
F_{1}
, and (3)
a_{1},b_{1}\in F_{1}
, for any
x\in{\alpha_{0}}^{-1}(a_{0})
and
y\in{\alpha_{0}}^{-1}(b_{0})
. However, it follows from the non-degeneracy of the torsion linking pairing

\tau H_{1}(W)\times\tau H_{2}(W,Y_{0}\cup Y_{1})\longrightarrow\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}

that the case (3) is possible only when
\lambda(a_{0},b_{0})=0
. Therefore we focus on computing the torsion linking pairing in the first two cases.

(1)

Suppose
a_{1},b_{1}\in\tau H_{1}(Y_{1})
.
Consider
\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\Delta a_{i}=\Delta b_{i}=0.
For
i=0,1
consider relative cocycle representatives
X,Y
of
x,y
in
(W,Y_{0}\cup Y_{1})
and cocycle representatives
A_{i},B_{i}
of
a_{i},b_{i}
in
Y_{i}
. Let
U_{i},V_{i}
be cochains in
Y_{i}
such that
\partial U_{i}=\Delta A_{i}
,
\partial V_{i}=\Delta B_{i}
. It follows that

\widetilde{X}:=\Delta X-U_{0}-U_{1},\;\widetilde{Y}:=\Delta Y-V_{0}-V_{1}

are cocycles in
W
. A geometric argument, outlined below and similar to that in
[
FK25
, Lemma 2.1]
) implies a relation in
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
between the intersection pairing
H_{2}(W)\times H_{2}(W)\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}
and the linking forms:

(A.7)

\lambda(a_{0},b_{0})-\lambda(a_{1},b_{1})\equiv-\frac{[\widetilde{X}]\cdot[\widetilde{Y}]}{\Delta^{2}}\;\,({\rm mod}\;1).

Indeed,

\lambda(a_{i},b_{i})\equiv\frac{U_{i}\cdot B_{i}}{\Delta}\;\,({\rm mod}\;1).

Geometrically,
\widetilde{X}
consists of
\Delta X\subset W
capped off by
U_{0}
in
Y_{0}
and
U_{1}
in
Y_{1}
, with the analogous description of
\widetilde{Y}
. Push
\widetilde{X}
by an isotopy slightly into the interior of
W
. Then transverse intersections
\widetilde{X}\cap\widetilde{Y}
fall into three categories: intersections
\Delta X\cap\Delta Y
, and
U_{i}\cap\Delta Y
,
i=0,1
. The intersection number
\Delta X\cdot\Delta Y
is a multiple of
\Delta^{2}
, so it does not contribute to
Equation

A.7
(mod 1). The other two terms,
U_{0}\cdot\Delta Y
and
U_{1}\cdot\Delta Y
are precisely

\lambda(a_{0},b_{0})-\lambda(a_{1},b_{1})
with an additional factor
\Delta
since the intersections are counted with
\Delta Y
.

(2)

Suppose
a_{1}\in\tau H_{1}(Y_{1})
and
b_{1}\in F_{1}
. Using the notation as above, consider a cocycle
\widetilde{X}:=\Delta X-U_{0}-U_{1}
in
W
and a relative cocycle
\widetilde{Y}:=\Delta Y-V_{0}
in
(W,Y_{1})
. The analogue of
Equation

A.7
reads

\lambda(a_{0},b_{0})\equiv-\frac{[\widetilde{X}]\cdot[\widetilde{Y}]}{\Delta^{2}}\;\,({\rm mod}\;1).

The right-hand side refers to the intersection pairing
{H_{2}(W)\times H_{2}(W,Y_{1})\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}}
.
