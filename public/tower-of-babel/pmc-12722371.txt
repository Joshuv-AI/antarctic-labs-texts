Serum and cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers as predictors of cognitive impairment in multiple sclerosis: a systematic review of longitudinal studies
Authors: Giovanni Restuccia, Carla Susinna, Giulia Marafioti, Carmela Rifici, Giangaetano D’Aleo, Edoardo Sessa, Serena Silvestro, Angelo Quartarone, Viviana Lo Buono
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsMultiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune disease of the central nervous system, characterized by inflammation, demyelination, gliosis, and neuronal loss. Cognitive disorders are part of the clinical presentation, as well as in the early stages, with a prevalence rate ranging from 40 to 65% and are strictly associated with poor quality of life. In recent years, advances in the field of molecular biomarkers have contributed to improving the accuracy of cognitive diagnosis and to identifying subjects at risk of developing cognitive disorders. By focusing on longitudinal studies, this review aims to elucidate the temporal dynamics and potential predictive value of CSF and serum biomarkers in identifying cognitive decline and monitoring disease progression in MS.MethodsA systematic review was conducted using the PRISMA guidelines. 827 studies were identified by searching PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science between July and August 2025. After screening, 8 studies fulfilled the eligibility criteria and were included.ResultsNfL showed heterogeneous results: some studies identified it as a feasible biomarker able to predict cognitive decline, while others did not. GFAP did not show significant correlations. Parvalbumin was longitudinally associated with poorer cognition and greater fatigue. Specific microRNAs (miR-126.3p, miR-9p) were associated with processing speed decline. Higher BDNF levels were linked to cognitive improvement. Other biomarkers (OPN, IL-6, CHI3L1, CXCL13) provided insufficient evidence.ConclusionsFindings add interesting contributions to the complex picture of the pathophysiology underpinning cognitive decline in MS. The most promising direction is the shift toward blood-based biomarkers, due to their minimally invasive nature and potential for clinical applicability, which may enable clinicians to anticipate, monitor, and potentially modify the cognitive trajectory of people living with MS.Keywords: Multiple sclerosis, Molecular biomarkers, Cognition, Cognitive impairment, Neurofilament light chain, Systematic review

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune disease of the central nervous system (CNS), characterized by inflammation, demyelination, gliosis, and neuronal loss [1]. The mean age of onset ranges from 20 to 30 years and commonly affects women (female to male sex ratio is approximately 3:1) [2]. Worldwide, MS active cases are roughly 3 million, and the incidence rate is 2.1 per 100,000 persons/year; according to this rate, it is possible to estimate that someone in the world is diagnosed with MS every 5 min [3]. Neurological signs and symptoms are closely related to the localization of lesions within the CNS, with autonomic, visual, motor, and sensory deficits representing the most common clinical features. The onset and progression of these manifestations are primarily driven by inflammatory and neurodegenerative processes, alongside genetic, environmental, and immunological factors, which together contribute to the heterogeneity of the disease. Recent research suggests that inflammation and neurodegeneration are not distinct phases but rather constitute a single disease continuum that coexists from disease onset [4]. This evolving perspective on MS pathophysiology has significant implications for its clinical characterization, particularly regarding cognitive dysfunction. Cognitive impairment has gained recognition as an integral and early manifestation of the disease, reflecting the widespread and early involvement of both inflammatory and degenerative mechanisms within the CNS. Cognitive disorders are highly prevalent in MS, even from the early stages of the disease, with estimates ranging from 40 to 65% [5]. Neuropsychological disorders are closely associated with functional status, often leading to reduced participation in social life, increased difficulty with daily activities, and a higher risk of unemployment [6].

Cognitive impairment can also be associated with depressive symptoms, the most rapid disease progression, and increased disability in general, thus being responsible for poor Quality of Life (QoL) in subjects with MS [5]. Cognitive domains typically affected in MS include episodic and working memory, attention, conceptual reasoning, executive function (EF), information processing speed (IPS), orientation, and verbal fluency [7]. However, the neurobiological basis of cognitive functioning in MS is still not fully understood with both neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration possibly playing a crucial role in the onset of cognitive dysfunction [8, 9]. In recent years, advances in molecular biomarker research have improved diagnostic accuracy and contributed to the early identification of patients at risk of developing cognitive deficits across the disease course [10].

This research field has already achieved robust results in the context of other neurodegenerative diseases. For example, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) Tau and Beta-amyloid (Aβ42) are regularly used in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [11], and the use of blood biomarkers in Parkinson’s disease is currently being explored [12]. Nevertheless, there is still a paucity of literature in MS, and this is possibly due to the lack of harmonization in the methodological choices adopted by the available studies [10]. This systematic review aims to evaluate longitudinal studies examining the relationship between serum and CSF biomarkers and cognitive performance in MS, to clarify their temporal dynamics and potential for predicting cognitive decline and tracking disease progression.

Materials and methods

A systematic review was conducted using the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [13] to investigate the existing literature about the longitudinal relationship between serum and CSF biomarkers and cognitive performance in MS. A protocol for this systematic review was registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with ID: CRD420251143923.

Search strategy

According to the aim of this systematic review, a search strategy was developed. The studies were identified by searching in PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science databases from July to August 2025. The search combined the following terms in PubMed: (“multiple sclerosis”[All Fields] OR “MS”[All Fields]) AND (“biological biomarker”[All Fields] OR “molecular biomarkers”[All Fields] OR “BDNF”[All Fields] OR “NfL”[All Fields]) AND (“cognition”[MeSH Terms] OR “cognition”[All Fields] OR “cognitions”[All Fields] OR “cognitive”[All Fields] OR “cognitively”[All Fields] OR “cognitives”[All Fields] OR “cognitive impairment”[All Fields]). No specific search filters were applied.

Study selection

After removing the duplicates using Zotero, a screening for title and abstract was performed independently by two researchers (G.R. and C.S.). At this stage, reviews, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, letters to the editor, short communications, and non-English articles were excluded. G.R and C.S. read the full-text articles and made sure they met the following criteria: (a) participants were subjects with a diagnosis of MS; (b) the study assessed serum and/or CSF biomarkers; (c) the study design was longitudinal; and (d) papers included a standardized cognitive assessment. On the other hand, studies were excluded if they met the following criteria: (a) cross-sectional study design; (b) studies focusing exclusively on genetic polymorphisms; (c) studies without a standardized cognitive assessment; and (d) articles not available as full text. After an accurate evaluation, 8 articles fulfilled the chosen criteria. For the qualitative synthesis, the included studies were grouped according to the investigated biomarker in order to enhance the comparability of findings and to facilitate the identification of consistent associations between specific biomarkers and cognitive outcomes.

Data extraction

Following the full-text selection, data were extracted independently by two reviewers (G.R. and C.S) and reported in a table using Microsoft Excel. For each eligible study, they collected information on: (i) general study characteristics (authors, year of publication, sample size, follow-up duration); (ii) population characteristics (multiple sclerosis phenotype when available); (iii) biomarkers assessed (type, biological source, serum or cerebrospinal fluid, detection methods); (iv) cognitive outcomes (neuropsychological tests or batteries employed, definition of cognitive impairment when available); and (v) main findings regarding the association between biomarkers and cognitive performance or decline. Any doubt throughout the data extraction process between the two reviewers was resolved through discussion and, when required, consultation with a third reviewer (V.L.B.).

Assessing the quality of included studies—risk of bias

G.R. and C.S. independently evaluated the risk of bias in selected studies. Any disagreements throughout this process, as well as in earlier stages, were settled by consulting V.L.B., who provided the final decision. All eligible studies were examined using the Cochrane tool for non-randomized controlled studies— of exposures (ROBINS-E) tool [14], which encompasses seven domains: (i) bias due to confounding, (ii) bias arising from measurement of the exposure, (iii) bias in selection of participants into the study (or into the analysis), (iv) bias due to post-exposure interventions, (v) bias due to missing data, (vi) bias arising from measurement of the outcome, and (vii) bias in selection of reported result.

Results

Study selection

A total number of 827 search entries were generated by searching on PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases (Fig. 1). After the removal of duplicates, 579 studies were screened by title and abstract. Following the screening, 34 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Finally, 8 studies were included in the systematic review.

PRISMA flow chart

Study characteristics

A total of 457 subjects with MS were included in this systematic review. All the included studies were longitudinal and explored the relationship between serum and CSF biomarkers and cognitive performance in MS. The most frequently investigated biomarker was neurofilament light chain (NfL; n = 7), followed by glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP; n = 2). Among the 7 studies evaluating NfL concentrations, 6 measured serum NfL (n = 1 by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, ELISA; n = 5 by single-molecule array, SIMOA), while 1 study assessed CSF NfL using an ELISA kit. Regarding cognitive assessment, 4 studies exclusively administered the Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT), whereas 1 study used the processing speed test (PST), both evaluating IPS. Neurofilament light chain (NfL)

NfL was the most assessed biomarker in the studies’ selection (n = 7); however, they showed heterogeneous results in terms of predicting cognitive decline. A study reported that higher serum NfL (sNfL) concentrations at baseline were associated with higher future cognitive decline measured with SDMT over a median of 3.1-year observation period [15]. A similar result has been found by Bhan et al. [16], who showed that baseline sNfL levels were associated with a decrease in SDMT score during a 10-year observation period. Despite this association, after performing longitudinal analyses, neither sNfL nor CSF NfL (cNfL) showed a significant association with longitudinal change in SDMT. Another study observed an association between higher sNfL levels at screening and lower California Verbal Learning Test—second edition (CVLT-II) scores at year 1 on a 9-year follow-up period [18]. Tiu et al. [20] built a prediction model based on subjects that showed a significant cognitive decline on Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) or SDMT scores throughout a 1-year follow-up. Specifically, they demonstrated that sNfL, cNfL and Aβ42, combined with a clinical score (Bayesian Risk Estimate for MS at Onset, BREMSO), can predict an early cognitive decline for RRMS subjects at the time of diagnosis. On the other hand, some studies found no correlations between NfL and SDMT performance [19] or PST score [17], as well as in a study in which subjects underwent a more comprehensive neuropsychological assessment with the Brief Repeatable Battery of Neuropsychological Tests for MS (BRB-N) [22]. Although no significant association with cognitive performance was detected, a study reported the presence of higher levels of sNfL at the baseline in subjects characterized by a more severe disease course based on an increase in the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score, the presence of relapse, and self-reported judgment of decline in health [19]. A similar result was also reported by the aforementioned study [15] in which the predictive value of sNfL in detecting cognitive decline was more evident in subjects with acute inflammatory disease activity.

Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)

Only two studies in our systematic review assessed GFAP concentrations; however, no significant correlations with decline over time in PST [19] or SDMT performance [15] were reported.

Osteopontin (OPN) and interleukin-6 (IL-6)

A marginal negative correlation of OPN level with SDMT performance at the baseline assessment was observed in a single study [19]. IL-6 association with cognitive performance was explored in a recent 2-year follow-up study included in this work; however, after correcting for multiple comparisons, no significant correlation with SDMT performance was observed [19].

Chitinase-3-like 1-protein (CHI3L1), C–X–C motif chemokine ligand 13 (CXCL13)

Serum concentrations of Chitinase-3-like 1-protein (CHI3L1, also known as YKL-40) and C–X–C motif chemokine ligand 13 (CXCL13) were correlated with SDMT score neither at baseline nor at 2-year follow-up assessment [19].

MicroRNAs

Among different microRNAs evaluated in a recent 1-year longitudinal study, miR-126.3p and miR-9.5p showed significant results [17]. MiR-126.3p correlated with EDSS and IPS performance, assessed with PST, at baseline, and miR-126.3p and miR-9.5p correlated with change in PST score at 1 year.

Parvalbumin (PVALB)

CSF levels of parvalbumin (PVALB) have longitudinally been correlated with cognitive impairment in a 4-year follow-up study [22]. Subjects were clustered into ‘cognitively normal’ (CN, 0 failed subtests), ‘mildly cognitive impaired’ (mCI, one or 2 failed subtests), and ‘severely cognitive impaired’ (sCI, 3 or more failed subtests), based on their performance at BRB-N. A global cognitive functioning index and domain-specific cognitive functioning indices were also calculated. Subjects with severe cognitive impairment at 4-year follow-up had significantly higher CSF PVALB levels at diagnosis, compared with those ones with normal cognitive functioning. Higher PVALB levels at baseline were significantly associated with worse global cognitive and memory functioning at the end of the follow-up time. Specifically, higher PVALB levels at diagnosis were associated with a worse performance in verbal learning and phonemic fluencies at the end of the follow-up period. Notably, a preliminary clinical threshold for PVALB concentrations at diagnosis was proposed (2.57 ng/mL), which maximizes the risk of showing cognitive impairment at follow-up, with a sensitivity of 91% (specificity 30%). Furthermore, subjects with higher levels of PVALB at diagnosis showed higher cognitive and global fatigue at follow-up.

Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)

Higher BDNF levels in CSF at baseline were found in those subjects who experienced cognitive improvement (based on z-score change for each neuropsychological test), while subjects with disability improvement (EDSS) had higher serum BDNF levels at baseline [21]. Secondary analysis demonstrated significant correlations between serum BDNF and EDSS change, CSF BDNF and average z-score change and CSF BDNF and number of cognitive tests with improvement.

Beta-amyloid (Aβ42)

Aβ42 role in predicting cognitive impairment in MS was explored in only one of the selected studies, demonstrating that Aβ42, when combined with sNfL, cNfL and a clinical score (BREMSO), can predict an early cognitive decline for RRMS subjects at the moment of diagnosis [20]

Risk of bias

The Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (ROBINS-E) [23] was used to assess the risk of bias of the articles included in this review. Figure 2 shows the summary of the risk of bias assessment. Most of the included studies (n = 6) were reported as having “some concerns” in terms of risk of bias [15, 17–20, 22]. This was commonly due to concerns about inadequate adjustment for confounding variables, selection of participants, measurement of the outcome (e.g. only SDMT was administered, practice effect), and in the selection of reported results (e.g. significant results were emphasized). Two studies [16, 21] were rated as having a “high risk” of bias due to concerns about missing data (subjects drop out during follow-up).

Discussion

This systematic review synthesizes current evidence on serum and CSF biomarkers as predictors of future cognitive decline in MS subjects.

Among the biomarkers analysed, NfL was the most extensively studied. NfL is a protein in the neuronal cytoskeleton and is particularly expressed at the axonal level. NfL is released into CSF and blood under normal conditions, but its concentrations proportionally increase following neuronal damage and axonal lesions [24]. Since neuronal and axonal damage are involved in the pathophysiology of MS, these processes seem to play a crucial role in the onset of cognitive dysfunction. Therefore, NfL may be a useful biomarker reflecting the degeneration of brain regions that are critical for cognitive functioning, especially in the presence of inflammatory activity. Previous works are in line with this hypothesis, demonstrating that higher NfL levels in subjects with MS are associated with brain and spinal cord volume loss, with a poor long-term outcome and cognitive impairment [25–27]. However, evidence resulting from the longitudinal studies showed controversial results. Some studies identified NfL as a feasible biomarker able to predict a future cognitive decline [15, 16, 18, 20], while other studies did not, as well as in long follow-up periods [17, 19, 22]. Notably, subjects with higher sNfL levels seem to have a greater risk of experiencing an episodic verbal memory decline throughout the course of the disease and are more likely to experience a more severe disease course [18]. Furthermore, NfL levels in serum appear to be a better informative measure of cognitive decline than concentrations in CSF and this could be attributed to the different assay methodologies adopted in the studies. Beyond NfL, emerging biomarkers showed encouraging signals. PVALB is a calcium-binding protein expressed by fast-spiking GABAergic interneurons [28], which constitute a significant portion of cortical myelin and thus are particularly vulnerable to demyelination in MS [29]. Coherently with this statement, CSF PVALB concentrations reflect cortical atrophy in newly diagnosed subjects, also confirmed in postmortem observations [29]. Recent evidence indicates that CSF levels of PVALB can predict future cognitive deterioration in MS, showing longitudinal associations with poorer global cognition, impaired verbal memory, and greater fatigue. Importantly, a preliminary clinical threshold was also proposed by authors, suggesting a potential role in patient stratification [22]. Similarly, specific microRNAs (miR-126.3p, miR-9.5p) were significantly associated with processing speed and its decline over 1 year in MS subjects [17]. MicroRNAs are post-transcriptional regulators involved in inflammatory and neurodegenerative pathways. MiR-126-3p is mainly expressed in endothelial cells, where it regulates vascular homeostasis and angiogenesis. Through these mechanisms, it contributes to maintaining endothelial integrity and modulating neuroinflammation [30, 31], whereas miR-9-5p plays an important role in the differentiation of neural stem cells, axonal growth, and synaptic plasticity. It is also involved in neuroimmune regulation, as it modulates microglial activation and cytokine production [32, 33]. The exact role of these regulators in MS pathophysiology is still underexplored; however, the findings presented in this work support the potential role of specific microRNAs as prognostic biomarkers of cognitive impairment in MS. On the other hand, BDNF—a key molecule involved in remyelination, neuroplasticity and memory [34]—showed an opposite trend, with higher CSF levels linked to cognitive improvement [21]. Therefore, BDNF may play an important role in neurological and cognitive improvement acting as a neuroplasticity mediator in MS. Conversely, GFAP unexpectedly did not manage to achieve significant results [15, 19]. GFAP is a structural protein found in the cytoskeleton of astrocytes, which is released in high concentrations into the CSF and bloodstream during the process of astrogliosis. Previous studies described its use in MS and reported correlations with disease severity, the extent of neuroinflammation and disease progression [35, 36]. Furthermore, GFAP is currently being explored also in PD, where elevated plasma concentrations have shown a promising role not only in identifying subjects with cognitive impairment but also in predicting those who subsequently developed full-blown dementia [37]. However, inconclusive studies analyzed may arise from the scarce number of studies included and the different assay methodologies used. Furthermore, astrocytic reactivity (i.e. GFAP) may not be the correct pathological process underpinning cognitive decline in MS. In addition, even predominantly inflammatory biomarkers such as OPN, IL-6, CHI3L1, and CXCL13 provided insufficient longitudinal evidence on cognition [19]. While these molecules remain valuable for characterizing disease activity [38–41], their predictive role for cognitive outcomes may emerge only when combined with other biomarkers.

The main strength of our work lies in the exclusive inclusion of longitudinal studies, which are relevant for monitoring the relationship between biomarkers and cognitive performance over time. However, several limitations must be considered: the number of eligible studies was exiguous, and considerable methodological heterogeneity—including variability in cognitive assessment tools, biomarker assay methodologies (e.g., ELISA vs. SIMOA), and biological sources (CSF vs. blood)—represents a major limitation for direct comparison across studies. Moreover, most studies relied exclusively on SDMT or PST as measures of cognitive functioning. While these tools are well-established gold standards for a brief cognitive assessment in MS, their stand-alone use likely underestimates deficits in other domains, such as memory and executive functioning. Thus, this limitation highlights the importance of employing more comprehensive neuropsychological batteries in future research. Another limitation that should be emphasized is the lack of conversion of sNfL raw values into age-adjusted percentiles or z-scores based on a healthy control reference database. Converting raw sNfL concentrations into such standardized metrics provides a biologically normalized measure, allowing direct comparison across individuals and cohorts [42]. Unlike conventional statistical covariate adjustment, z-scores integrate demographic corrections at the biomarker level, improving interpretability and cross-study comparability. In our systematic review only two [18, 20] out of eight studies applied this type of standardization.

In conclusion, no single biomarker fully explains cognitive decline in MS. Nevertheless, individual biomarkers provide important insights into the complex pathophysiological processes underlying cognitive dysfunction. In our view, the most promising direction for future research is the development of integrated biomarker panels that could also add significant contributions to the clinical field. In this context, with the advent of more advanced and highly sensitive assay technologies such as SIMOA [43], blood-based biomarkers should be emphasized. Compared to CSF sampling, blood work is minimally invasive and easier to employ in routine clinical practice. Ultimately, the predictive value of these biomarkers will be critical for the early identification of subjects at risk of cognitive decline, enabling clinicians to anticipate, monitor, and potentially modify the cognitive trajectory of people living with MS.
