Port-Access Robotic Repair of Barlow Mitral Annular Disjunction and Severe Mitral Valve Regurgitation
Authors: Shazil Mahmood, Mohamed AbdalAziz, Zin Tarakji, Parvathy Sankar, Richard Bloomingdale, George Comas, Rakesh M. Suri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mitral annular disjunction (MAD) is associated with malignant ventricular arrhythmias and progressive mitral regurgitation. We describe a 35-year-old man with a history of sudden cardiac death who presented with palpitations and recurrent ventricular arrhythmias. Remote transthoracic echocardiography showed no structural abnormality, with repeated transthoracic echocardiography demonstrating only mild mitral regurgitation. During this admission, transesophageal echocardiography revealed severe mitral regurgitation from Barlow disease with profound MAD. Port-access robotic mitral valve repair eliminated mitral regurgitation and corrected MAD. This case underscores that ventricular arrhythmias may precede valve disease, warranting high suspicion for MAD in young patients with arrhythmias and mild mitral regurgitation.

Mitral annular disjunction (MAD) is a structural abnormality in which the hinge point of the posterior mitral leaflet is displaced away from the left ventricular myocardium. This structural abnormality can disrupt normal atrioventricular coupling and cause fatal arrhythmias. We herein describe a concise surgical technique for directly bridging the annular-myocardial gap and restoring native annular dynamics in MAD.

We present a case of a 35-year-old man with a history of remote cardiac arrest at the age of 16 years followed by refractory sustained ventricular arrhythmias of unclear cause despite ablation. Progressive severe mitral regurgitation later developed secondary to Barlow disease with profound MAD.

Five years after the ablation, he presented with palpitations, acute sharp chest pain, and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea. He was vitally stable, with signs of hypervolemia. Echocardiography demonstrated sinus rhythm with rightward axis deviation and incomplete left bundle branch block; implantable cardioverter-defibrillator interrogation showed no sustained arrhythmias. He was admitted for intravenous diuresis with symptomatic improvement. Remote transthoracic echocardiography did not show any valvular disease. However, repeat transthoracic echocardiogram identified moderate mitral regurgitation. Transesophageal echocardiography confirmed ejection fraction of 55%,severe mitral regurgitation, and 18-mm MAD as seen previously on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (Video), confirming profound myxomatous degeneration with extensive bileaflet prolapse consistent with Barlow's disease (most marked at the P2 scallop) and multiple regurgitant jets.

Robotic mitral valve repair was performed through 8-mm right thoracic ports without rib spreading or thoracotomy incisions, with peripheral near-percutaneous cardiopulmonary bypass (Video). After aortic cross-clamping and cardioplegic arrest, left atriotomy revealed extensive bileaflet prolapse typical of Barlow's disease with severe MAD, particularly prominent over the posteromedial annulus. There was a tall, redundant P2; redundant P1; diminutive P3; and multiple clefts (Figure A). A large triangular resection of P2 was performed with running 2-layer Prolene reconstruction using a leaflet shortening technique. Following the large triangular resection, and reconstruction additional clefts became apparent which were corrected with interrupted Prolene stitches.

The anterolateral commissure was plicated to correct prolapse and to prevent systolic anterior motion; 2 posteromedial clefts were closed. MAD was eliminated by repositioning the atrialized “false” annulus to the “true” ventricular annulus during implantation of a 28-mm SimuPlus (Medtronic) annuloplasty band, reinforced with additional pledgeted sutures and secured between left and right fibrous trigones (Figure). Pledgets were employed to distribute the tension of the resuspension sutures, to provide additional support at the level of the ulcerated and weakened annular tissue, to ensure durable fixation of the annuloplasty ring to the true anatomic annulus (Figure B).Figure.Surgeon’s view of mitral annular disjunction (A) before and (B) after repair.

Postrepair transesophageal echocardiography showed no residual mitral regurgitation and a mean transmitral gradient of 1 mm Hg with complete elimination of MAD. The patient was extubated in the operating room; he was walking the night of surgery and was dismissed home on postoperative day 3. At follow-up, the patient has not experienced repeated palpitations. The 90-day follow-up transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrated stable repair and no recurrent mitral regurgitation.

Comment

MAD is characterized by atrial displacement of the mitral leaflet hinge point of the mitral annulus from the ventricular myocardium. It is most frequently associated with myxomatous degeneration, Barlow disease, and bileaflet mitral valve prolapse. This constellation is present in more than one-third of patients with mitral valve prolapse.1

MAD leads to excessive annular and leaflet motion, promoting myocardial stretch, fibrosis, and arrhythmogenesis.2 This phenotype is linked to malignant ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death; risk markers include syncope, repolarization abnormalities, and scar on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging.2 Ablation can reduce arrhythmia burden but does not correct the underlying mechanical substrate if MAD persists.3

Surgical correction of MAD during mitral valve repair offers the dual benefit of restoring leaflet coaptation and reestablishing annular-ventricular continuity.4 In Barlow's disease, repositioning the atrialized portion of the annulus to the true ventricular annulus, supplemented with pledgeted plication sutures, can effectively eliminate the atrialized segment in >90% of cases.4 The anatomic restoration of mitral annulus-left ventricular dynamic continuity has been associated with a reduction in ventricular arrhythmias in a subset of patients.2 In a prospective study of patients with Barlow disease, one-third presented with significant preoperative arrhythmias; approximately half had marked arrhythmia reduction after repair, with MAD more prevalent in those who improved.5

The surgical approach described utilized previously reported repair maneuvers, including reduction of tall posterior leaflet tissue6 (ie, large triangular resection with leaflet shortening reconstruction), commissural plication to address localized prolapse and maneuvers to prevent systolic anterior motion, closure of clefts, and flexible posterior annuloplasty to protect the leaflet repair.4,5 When MAD is broad, targeted annular plication during band implantation reinforces ventricularization of the annulus, particularly in areas where tissue ulceration is identified.4 A port-access robot-assisted approach provides enhanced visualization of leaflet disease and facilitates precise leaflet and annular repair with favorable recovery profiles in experienced centers.5

In our case, the patient’s severe bileaflet prolapse, tall and redundant P2 segment, multiple clefts, and severe MAD were addressed with large triangular P2 resection and leaflet shortening, anterolateral commissural plication to mitigate systolic anterior motion, cleft closures, and annular plication with annuloplasty band secured at the fibrous trigones for repair durability. The current strategy was employed through 8-mm muscle-sparing ports without rib spreading thoracotomy, allowing effective elimination of mitral regurgitation, and elimination of MAD, restoring annular-ventricular continuity to potentially reduce arrhythmic risk.2,4

In conclusion, this case highlights the importance of maintaining high suspicion for MAD in young patients presenting with ventricular arrhythmias, even when preliminary transthoracic imaging suggests the absence of structural abnormality or significant mitral regurgitation. The identification of ventricular arrhythmias may precede the discovery of significant mitral valve regurgitation in the presence of MAD, and careful surveillance with advanced imaging is warranted. Transesophageal echocardiogram and MRI should be considered early in the assessment of these patients. When MAD and significant mitral valve regurgitation are identified, even complex multiscallop mitral valve prolapse, port-access robotic mitral valve repair can simultaneously restore valve competence, eliminate MAD, and potentially reduce arrhythmic risk, underscoring the value of timely recognition and comprehensive surgical correction in this high-risk phenotype.
