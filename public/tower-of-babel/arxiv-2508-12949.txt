Symmetric orthogonalization and probabilistic weights in resource quantification
Gökhan Torun

Abstract
Transforming non-orthogonal bases into orthogonal ones often sacrifices essential properties or physical meaning in quantum systems. Here, we demonstrate that Löwdin symmetric orthogonalization (LSO) outperforms the widely used Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization (GSO) in characterizing and quantifying quantum resources, with particular emphasis on coherence and superposition. We employ LSO both to construct an orthogonal basis from a non-orthogonal one and to obtain a non-orthogonal basis from an orthogonal set, thereby avoiding any ambiguity related to the basis choice for quantum coherence. Unlike GSO, which depends on the ordering of input states, LSO applies a symmetric transformation that treats all vectors equally and minimizes deviation from the original basis. This approach generates basis sets with enhanced stability and physical relevance, facilitating the analysis of superpositions in non-orthogonal quantum states. Building on LSO, we also introduce Löwdin weights—probabilistic weights for non-orthogonal representations that provide a consistent measure of resource content. These weights further enable basis-independent quantification of coherence and state delocalization through information-theoretic measures such as entropy and participation ratios. Our theoretical and numerical analyses confirm LSO’s superior preservation of quantum state symmetry and resource characteristics, underscoring the critical role of orthogonalization methods and Löwdin weights in resource theory frameworks.

2.1  Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization

As one of the most well-known orthogonalization techniques, the Gram-Schmidt process is a staple of linear algebra due to its straightforward iterative structure
[
1
]
. Owing to its broad familiarity, historical significance, and extensive applications in mathematics, physics, and engineering, it serves as a natural reference point in discussions of basis transformations. For these reasons, we give it brief consideration here and compare it with the LSO, as shown in Table
1
.

Let
\left\{\ket{c_{k}}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}
be a linearly independent set of non-orthogonal vectors in an inner product space. The orthonormal set
\left\{\ket{{e}_{k}^{\text{GS}}}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}
with the same span is constructed via the Gram-Schmidt process, henceforth referred to as the Gram-Schmidt basis, as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{1}

\displaystyle=\ket{c_{1}},\quad\ket{{e}_{1}^{\text{GS}}}=\frac{\mathbf{v}_{1}}{\|\mathbf{v}_{1}\|};

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{2}

\displaystyle=\ket{c_{2}}-\langle{c_{2}}|{{e}_{1}^{\text{GS}}}\rangle\ket{{e}_{1}^{\text{GS}}},\quad\ket{{e}_{2}^{\text{GS}}}=\frac{\mathbf{v}_{2}}{\|\mathbf{v}_{2}\|};

(2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{j}

\displaystyle=\ket{c_{j}}-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\langle{c_{j}}|{{e}_{i}^{\text{GS}}}\rangle\ket{{e}_{i}^{\text{GS}}},\quad\ket{{e}_{j}^{\text{GS}}}=\frac{\mathbf{v}_{j}}{\|\mathbf{v}_{j}\|}\quad\text{for }j=3,\ldots,d.

(3)

Here
\mathbf{v}_{j}
in Eq. (
3
) is the orthogonalized (but unnormalized, in general) vector at step
j
, and
\|\mathbf{\cdot}\|
is the inner product norm. The GSO process ensures orthogonality by subtracting, at each step, the components of
\ket{{c}_{j}}
along the prior orthonormal vectors
\left\{\ket{{e}_{i}^{\text{GS}}}\right\}_{i=1}^{j-1}
, ensuring orthogonality. Once normalized, the orthogonalized
\mathbf{v}_{k}
form unit vectors, completing an orthonormal basis
\left\{\ket{{e}_{k}^{\text{GS}}}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}
.

On the one hand, the GSO approach offers algorithmic simplicity, allowing for efficient sequential vector processing. On the other hand, its most critical drawback is arguably order dependence, since the resulting orthogonal basis is highly sensitive to the input vector sequence (see Figure
1
). This introduces undesirable arbitrariness in applications where basis symmetry is physically meaningful, compromising the reliability of the orthogonalized representation. Consequently, there is indeed a well-motivated need to explore alternative orthogonalization methods that mitigate this limitation while preserving computational tractability.

2.2  Löwdin’s orthogonalization methods

Although less widely known than GSO in general, Löwdin’s orthogonalization method proves especially powerful in applications where symmetry preservation is critical
[
5
,
6
,
4
]
. Let us return to the origin of the problem and consider the given set of linearly independent but non-orthogonal basis vectors
\left\{\ket{c_{k}}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}
in a Hilbert space. The non-orthogonality is characterized by the overlap matrix
\mathbf{O}_{d}
(also called the Gram matrix):

\mathbf{O}_{d}=\begin{pmatrix}\langle c_{1}|c_{1}\rangle&\langle c_{1}|c_{2}\rangle&\cdots&\langle c_{1}|c_{d}\rangle\\
\langle c_{2}|c_{1}\rangle&\langle c_{2}|c_{2}\rangle&\cdots&\langle c_{2}|c_{d}\rangle\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\langle c_{d}|c_{1}\rangle&\langle c_{d}|c_{2}\rangle&\cdots&\langle c_{d}|c_{d}\rangle\end{pmatrix},

(4)

where
O_{ij}(\equiv\left[\mathbf{O}_{d}\right]_{ij})=\langle c_{i}|c_{j}\rangle
explicitly represents the
(i,j)
-th element of matrix
\mathbf{O}_{d}
. The overlap matrix
\mathbf{O}_{d}
is Hermitian (
\mathbf{O}_{d}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{O}_{d}
) and positive-definite. It should be emphasized that, when employing non-orthogonal basis sets, the overlap matrix plays a crucial role in preserving the correct geometric and physical structure of the system. Through the use of the overlap matrix, we construct the orthonormal basis states
\left\{\ket{{e}_{k}^{\text{L}}}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}
, commonly referred to as the Löwdin basis.

Now consider the case where the sets
\left\{\ket{c_{k}}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}
and
\left\{\ket{{e}_{k}^{\text{L}}}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}
span the same subspace of the Hilbert space. In this case, the orthogonal states
\ket{{e}_{i}^{\text{L}}}
can be expressed as linear combinations of the original non-orthogonal states
\left\{\ket{c_{k}}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}
through the (linear) transformation

\ket{{e}_{i}^{\text{L}}}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}T_{ki}\ket{c_{k}},\quad i=1,\ldots,d,

(5)

where the coefficients
T_{ki}\equiv\left[\mathbf{T}\right]_{ki}
form a
d\times d
transformation matrix
\mathbf{T}
. The problem is then formulated as determining the transformation coefficients
\left\{T_{ki}\right\}
via the overlap matrix
\mathbf{O}_{d}
given by Eq. (
4
). Per-Olov Löwdin introduced two closely related but distinct approaches
[
5
,
6
]
for constructing orthonormal bases from non-orthogonal ones, namely, symmetric orthogonalization (SO) and canonical orthogonalization (CO).

2.2.1
Symmetric orthogonalization

This method produces an orthonormal basis
\left\{\ket{{e}_{i}^{\text{L}}}_{\mathrm{sym}}\right\}_{i=1}^{d}
that is the “closest” to the original basis in a least-squares sense. One achieves this through the following three steps:

1.

Diagonalize the overlap matrix
: Solve the eigenvalue problem
\mathbf{O}_{d}\mathbf{u}_{k}=\lambda_{k}\mathbf{u}_{k}
for
k=1,\ldots,d
, where
\lambda_{k}>0
(since
\mathbf{O}_{d}
is positive definite) and
\{\mathbf{u}_{k}\}
form an orthonormal set.

2.

Construct the inverse square root
: Form the eigenvector matrix
\mathbf{U}=(\mathbf{u}_{1}\ldots\mathbf{u}_{d})
and diagonal eigenvalue matrix
\mathbf{D}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})
. Then compute

\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{sym}}=\mathbf{O}_{d}^{-1/2}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}=\text{diag}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}},\ldots,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{d}}}\right).

(6)

3.

Apply the transformation
: The (symmetric) orthonormal basis vectors are then given by

\ket{{e}_{i}^{\text{L}}}=\ket{{e}_{i}^{\text{L}}}_{\mathrm{sym}}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left[\mathbf{O}_{d}^{-1/2}\right]_{ki}\ket{c_{k}},\quad i=1,\ldots,d.

(7)

The (Löwdin) SO process described by Eq. (
7
) stands out as the preferred basis transformation because it uniquely meets three essential criteria. First, it exactly minimizes the Frobenius norm
[
5
,
6
]

\|{\mathbf{E}}-{\mathbf{C}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\sqrt{\sum_{k}\|{e}_{k}^{\text{L}}-c_{k}\|^{2}},

(8)

ensuring the orthogonalized basis
{\mathbf{E}}=\left\{\ket{{e}_{k}^{\text{L}}}_{\mathrm{sym}}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}
preserves maximal geometric similarity to the initial non-orthogonal basis
{\mathbf{C}}=\left\{\ket{c_{k}}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}
(see Figure
2
). Second, through the transformation (
7
), SO conserves all inherent symmetries of
{\mathbf{C}}
(a critical feature for quantum chemistry and condensed matter applications where symmetry-adapted bases are essential). Third, in contrast to sequential methods, which are sensitive to the choice of the first vector (e.g., Gram-Schmidt), SO preserves the equal status of basis states via the symmetric form of
\mathbf{O}_{d}^{-1/2}
, which applies a uniform transformation to all vectors in
{\mathbf{C}}
. We might conclude that this triad of properties—minimal distortion, symmetry preservation, and basis democracy—establishes SO as one of the optimal orthogonalization processes for quantum mechanical bases. In molecular systems, for instance, Löwdin symmetrically-orthogonalized orbitals retain maximal correspondence with atomic orbitals while satisfying orthogonality constraints
[
5
,
6
]
.

Geometric illustration of Löwdin SO for a two-dimensional system. (a) The original non-orthogonal vectors
\ket{c_{1}}(=c_{1})
and
\ket{c_{2}}(=c_{2})

\theta\in(0,\pi/2)

\beta

\ket{{e}_{1}^{\text{L}}}_{\mathrm{sym}}(=\bar{c}_{1})
and
\ket{{e}_{2}^{\text{L}}}_{\mathrm{sym}}(=\bar{c}_{2})
, where
\theta+2\beta=\pi/2
ensures mutual orthogonality. The same applies in higher dimensions. All vectors are assumed unit-norm.

2.2.2
Canonical orthogonalization

This method produces an orthonormal basis
\left\{\ket{{e}_{i}^{\text{L}}}_{\mathrm{can}}\right\}_{i=1}^{d}
and it is particularly useful when some eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
are small (indicating near-linear dependence, i.e., ill-conditioned systems). One achieves this through the following three steps:

1.

Diagonalize the overlap matrix
: Obtain the eigenvector matrix
\mathbf{U}=(\mathbf{u}_{1}\ldots\mathbf{u}_{d})
and diagonal eigenvalue matrix
\mathbf{D}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})
from the eigenvalue problem,
\mathbf{O}_{d}\mathbf{u}_{k}=\lambda_{k}\mathbf{u}_{k}
.

2.

Form the transformation matrix
: Construct
\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{can}}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}
. Notice that this differs from SO by simply omitting
\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}
.

3.

Generate the new basis
: The (canonical) orthonormal vectors are then given by

\ket{{e}_{i}^{\text{L}}}=\ket{{e}_{i}^{\text{L}}}_{\mathrm{can}}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{U_{ki}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}\ket{c_{k}},\quad i=1,\ldots,d.

(9)

The (Löwdin) CO process described by Eq. (
9
) directly aligns with the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{O}_{d}
, making it preferable for systems with near-linear dependence (i.e., small eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
). As it seen, SO requires the full matrix product
\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{sym}}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}
, whereas CO only needs the transformation
\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{can}}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}
. These yield identical results when
\lambda_{k}\approx 1
, but diverge when
\mathbf{O}_{d}
contains small eigenvalues or strong non-orthogonalities. In summary, the symmetric approach (
7
) preserves the original basis relationships, making it ideal for quantum mechanical applications where physical interpretability is important. In contrast, the canonical method (
9
) offers inherent stability with near-singular overlap matrices, making it better suited for numerical applications that require robust eigenvector alignment. Accordingly, LSO serves as a key tool in our calculations.

2.3  Fixing the Löwdin basis as the computational basis

As demonstrated, the SO process transforms a non-orthogonal basis
{\mathbf{C}}
into an orthogonal basis
{\mathbf{E}}
via the transformation given in Eq. (
7
). Alternatively, the transformation (
7
) can also be expressed as

\mathbf{E}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{O}_{d}^{-1/2}.

(10)

Since
\mathbf{O}_{d}
is Hermitian,
\mathbf{O}_{d}^{-1/2}
is also Hermitian. The transformation (
10
) guarantees orthonormality of
\mathbf{E}
. Note that the matrix
\mathbf{O}_{d}^{-1/2}
is invertible due to the positive definiteness of
\mathbf{O}_{d}
(
\lambda_{k}>0
). The inverse square root of
\mathbf{O}_{d}
multiplied by its square root yields the identity matrix,
\mathbf{O}_{d}^{-1/2}\mathbf{O}_{d}^{1/2}=\mathbf{I}_{d}
. The inverse transformation is therefore well-defined:

\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{E}\mathbf{O}_{d}^{1/2}.

(11)

This framework is valid for any finite-dimensional basis in physical quantum systems, where
\mathbf{O}_{d}
is always positive definite with strictly positive eigenvalues.

Expressing the relation (
11
) in terms of individual basis vectors, we obtain

\ket{c_{i}}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left[\mathbf{O}_{d}^{1/2}\right]_{ki}\ket{e_{k}^{\text{L}}}_{\mathrm{sym}},\quad i=1,\ldots,d,

(12)

where
\left\{\ket{e_{k}^{\text{L}}}_{\mathrm{sym}}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}
denotes the Löwdin (symmetrically orthogonalized) basis. For computational convenience, we may represent the orthogonalized basis
{\mathbf{E}}
in terms of a standard computational basis:

\left\{\ket{e_{k}^{\text{L}}}_{\mathrm{sym}}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}=\left\{\ket{1},\ket{2},\ldots,\ket{d}\right\},

(13)

where
\{\ket{k}\}_{k=1}^{d}
are orthonormal states spanning the
d
-dimensional Hilbert space. This choice, without loss of generality, not only simplifies computations but also facilitates the study of quantum resources across different basis representations. Specifically, by treating the computational basis as the original orthonormal set and applying LSO inversely to generate symmetrically non-orthogonalized vectors (Eq. (
11
)), one can probe superposition more deeply, extending coherence theory to a broader framework. In the next section, we bring together all the information presented above to derive novel results beyond what has been previously established for coherence
[
18
]
and superposition
[
10
]
.

3  Quantum Resources in Relation to Reference Bases

Quantum resource theories (QRTs) have come to be a study of how the quantum properties could lead to an operational advantage in quantum information processing, and broadly speaking, concerned with their quantification, characterization, and manipulation under physical restrictions
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
. A resource theory is composed of two elements: the free states (whose set is denoted by
{\mathcal{F}}
) and the free operations (
{\mathcal{O}}
). All states which are not free regarded as (quantum) resources. The free operations are physical transformations which do not create any resources, that is, they must transform free states into free ones and allow for the resource to be manipulated but not freely created. For extended discussion of these themes, see Refs.
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
.

The primary focus of our work is the
superposition
of linearly independent vectors. Depending on whether the preferred basis vectors are orthogonal or non-orthogonal, different QRTs can be formulated. In this context, two closely related QRTs have been developed: the resource theory of coherence (RTC)
[
18
]
, defined with respect to an orthogonal basis, and the resource theory of superposition (RTS)
[
10
]
, which generalizes the former to non-orthogonal basis sets. While both theories quantify quantum resources arising from superpositions, the non-orthogonal case requires additional considerations due to the nonvanishing overlaps between basis states.

Quantum coherence
[
18
]
is inherently a basis-dependent property. Whether a quantum state exhibits coherence depends on the choice of the orthonormal basis with respect to which coherence is defined. To illustrate this, consider two orthonormal basis sets in a two-dimensional Hilbert space: the computational basis
\left\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\right\}
and the Hadamard basis
\left\{\ket{+},\ket{-}\right\}
, where
\ket{\pm}=\left(\ket{0}\pm\ket{1}\right)/{\sqrt{2}}
.
Then, the coherence property of the state
\ket{\chi}=\left(\ket{0}+\ket{1}\right)/{\sqrt{2}}\>(\equiv\ket{+})
depends on the basis chosen. Obviously, the state
\ket{\chi}
exhibits coherence in the computational basis but is incoherent in the Hadamard basis. Specifically, a state that is a superposition in one basis may be an eigenstate (and hence incoherent) in another. Consequently, any rigorous discussion of quantum coherence requires the explicit specification of the reference basis. Following our discussion of the computational basis (equivalently, Löwdin basis) in Section
2.3
, we formally adopt this as the fixed reference basis for all subsequent coherence and superposition analysis.

The RTS
[
10
]
employs a set of non-orthogonal yet linearly independent basis states
\left\{\ket{c_{k}}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}
for a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{d}
. Within this framework, superposition-free states are precisely those diagonal in this basis:
\rho=\sum_{k}\rho_{k}\outerproduct{c_{k}}{c_{k}}
, where
\rho_{k}\geq 0
forms a probability distribution. The set of resource states (those exhibiting superposition) consists of all density operators outside
\mathcal{F}
. Pure superposition states are characterized by linear combinations of the form
a_{1}\ket{c_{1}}+a_{2}\ket{c_{2}}+\dots+a_{n}\ket{c_{n}}
(with
a_{i}\neq 0
for
i=1,2,\dots,n\in(2,d]
), subject to the normalization condition
\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{i}^{\ast}O_{ij}a_{j}=1
. Despite their apparent similarity, coherence and superposition exhibit an obvious distinction—the former is formulated in an orthogonal basis, the latter in a non-orthogonal basis. As mentioned, this implies that superposition theory constitutes a generalization of coherence theory
[
10
]
.

As established in
[
4
]
, the Löwdin SO method serves as an effective tool for characterizing pure superposition states. As discussed in Section
2.2.1
, a defining feature of SO is its ability to maximally preserve the structure and symmetry of the original non-orthogonal basis states. This property leads to an investigation of Löwdin SO’s role in determining the hierarchical relationships among resource states. Crucially, as shown in
[
4
]
, states of maximal superposition
[
22
]
are mapped to maximally coherent states under SO, and vice versa—a consequence of the invertible nature of LSO. Thus, maximally coherent states and states with maximal superposition are identified as equivalent under the action of Löwdin’s SO. To make this important and interesting result more concrete, let us go through the case where
d=2
. Following the steps from Section
2.2.1
, the Löwdin basis states can be obtained in the following form:

\displaystyle\ket{e_{2}^{\text{L}}}_{\mathrm{sym}}\equiv\ket{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{2}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}\right)\ket{c_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{2}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}\right)\ket{c_{2}},

To address this, we analyze a two-dimensional system, comparing three distinct sets of non-orthogonal bases. As an illustrative example, consider the following normalized superposition state:

(21)

\displaystyle S^{(2)}

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle S^{(3)}

Crucially, this insight underscores a broader principle: fixation on explicit representations, when overlaps are known, can introduce unnecessary computational overhead. Having clarified this point, we now address a deeper and equally critical question: What is the degree of coherence in a given superposition state? This inquiry lies at the heart of our study, as coherence quantifies quantum behavior in such systems where non-orthogonal basis states are constructed from the Löwdin basis (specifically, the computational basis in our case). In the following sections, we resolve this question by integrating analytical reasoning with explicit examples.

3.1  Löwdin weights

Measurement probabilities of a quantum state are determined by the modulus squared of its expansion coefficients in an orthonormal basis
[
24
,
25
]
. However, in the presence of non-orthogonal bases, such as atomic orbitals in quantum chemistry or coherent states in quantum optics (i.e., superposition states), a more careful treatment is required. Importantly, the coefficients in such expansions are not directly interpretable as probabilities due to interference effects and overlap between basis states. We explore this problem through the use of Löwdin’s SO process. This construction defines a new class of quantities, namely the Löwdin weights, which form a probability distribution over the symmetrically orthogonalized basis
[
26
]
.

Consider a normalized (pure) quantum superposition state
\ket{\alpha}\in\mathrm{span}\left\{\ket{c_{k}}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}
, expressed as

\displaystyle\ket{\alpha}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}\ket{c_{k}},\quad a_{k}\in\mathds{C}.

(24)

The LSO process constructs an orthonormal basis
\left\{\ket{e_{k}^{\text{L}}}_{\mathrm{sym}}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}
from the original non-orthogonal set by applying (
7
). To express
\ket{\alpha}
in this orthonormal basis, we define the coefficient vector
\mathbf{a}=\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathds{C}^{d}
. The representation of (
24
) in the Löwdin basis (i.e., computational basis) is then given by

\displaystyle\ket{\alpha}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}b_{k}\ket{e_{k}^{\text{L}}}_{\mathrm{sym}}\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{d}b_{k}\ket{k},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{b}=\left(b_{1},\dots,b_{d}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}=\mathbf{O}_{d}^{1/2}\mathbf{a}.

(25)

Once the vector
\mathbf{b}
is obtained (physically, the coefficients
\left\{b_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}
in the Löwdin basis can be seen as renormalized contributions of the original non-orthogonal basis states), the weight contribution of the
k
-th orthogonalized basis vector to the state
\ket{\alpha}
is computed as follows:

\displaystyle w_{k}^{\text{L}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left[\mathbf{O}_{d}^{1/2}\right]_{ki}a_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left[\mathbf{O}_{d}^{1/2}\right]_{kj}a_{j}\right)

(26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|b_{k}|^{2}=\left|\left[\mathbf{O}_{d}^{1/2}\mathbf{a}\right]_{k}\right|^{2}=\left|\langle{k}|{\alpha}\rangle\right|^{2},\quad k=1,\dots,d.

These weights form a probability distribution since
w_{k}^{\text{L}}(\alpha)\geq 0
and
\sum_{k=1}^{d}w_{k}^{\text{L}}(\alpha)=1
. They capture the
effective contribution
of each non-orthogonal basis vector to the physical state, interpreted through its orthonormal Löwdin image. More specifically, Eq. (
26
) shows how the original coefficients
\left\{a_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}
, along with the pairwise overlaps
\langle{c_{i}}|{c_{j}}\rangle
(
i\neq j
), determine the distribution of the state
\ket{\alpha}
over the Löwdin basis. From another perspective, these weights quantify how strongly the state
\ket{\alpha}
aligns with the orthonormal directions
\ket{k}
, providing a consistent generalization of probabilistic weights to the non-orthogonal setting, while preserving geometric and physical interpretability.

We can extend the preceding analysis from a pure superposition state
\ket{\alpha}
, given in Eq. (
24
), to a general density operator
\rho
supported on the span of the non-orthogonal basis
\{\ket{c_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{d}
. For a density operator
\rho=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\rho_{ij}\ket{c_{i}}\bra{c_{j}}
(Hermitian, normalized, and positive semi-definite), the Löwdin-transformed state
\rho_{\text{L}}
encodes the statistical weights in the Löwdin basis:

\displaystyle w_{k}^{\text{L}}(\rho)=\left[\rho_{\text{L}}\right]_{kk}=\left[\frac{\mathbf{O}_{d}^{1/2}{\rho}\mathbf{O}_{d}^{1/2}}{\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathbf{O}_{d}^{1/2}{\rho}\mathbf{O}_{d}^{1/2}\right)}\right]_{kk}.

(27)

These weights (
27
) generalize the pure-state case, preserving key properties:

•

Non-negativity
:
w_{k}^{\text{L}}(\rho)\geq 0
and
\sum_{k}w_{k}^{\text{L}}(\rho)=1
(from
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{\text{L}})=1
).

•

Consistency
: For
\rho=\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha}
,
w_{k}^{\text{L}}(\rho)=w_{k}^{\text{L}}(\alpha)=\left|\langle{k}|{\alpha}\rangle\right|^{2}
.

•

Basis Invariance
: The Löwdin weights are uniquely determined by the initial non-orthogonal basis. Unlike other orthogonalization schemes, the LSO approach eliminates arbitrary phase and rotation freedoms, thereby yielding a natural orthonormal reference frame. Consequently, the resulting weights are independent of auxiliary choices in the orthogonalization process.

The Löwdin weights
w_{k}^{\text{L}}(\rho)
given in Eq. (
27
) thus provide a natural probabilistic interpretation for non-orthogonal expansions, reducing to Born-rule probabilities
[
24
,
25
]
when basis set
\{\ket{c_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{d}
is orthogonal, that is,
\mathbf{O}_{d}=\mathbf{I}_{d}
.

The Löwdin weights
[
26
]
play important roles in various areas of quantum theory and related disciplines. Their theoretical and practical significance can be summarized in the following aspects:

(1)

Superposition Quantification in Non-Orthogonal Bases
: In standard formulations, coherence
[
18
]
is defined with respect to a fixed orthonormal basis and quantified through multiple approaches
[
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
23
,
31

,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
]

[
10
]

This procedure recovers a well-defined probability distribution, enabling a consistent extension of coherence measures to non-orthogonal contexts, while preserving the geometric and physical integrity of the original representation.

(2)

Resolution of Decomposition Ambiguity
: A basic complication arising from non-orthogonal expansions is the non-uniqueness of the decomposition: a given state can be represented with respect to infinitely many non-orthogonal bases, even when the overlaps and the coefficient vector are fixed (this issue is carefully addressed above for the state in Eq. (3)

As a consequence, the Löwdin weights retain interpretative continuity with the original expansion while enabling rigorous probabilistic treatment. This property would be particularly advantageous in applications requiring minimal distortion, such as variational analysis or physically motivated basis optimization.

These features establish the Löwdin weights (

Consider a two-dimensional non-orthogonal basis set
\{\ket{c_{1}},\ket{c_{2}}\}

\displaystyle\langle c_{1}|c_{2}\rangle=s=\frac{1}{2},\quad\mathbf{O}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\

Based on this overlap value and the associated overlap matrix, illustrative examples are presented and their results analyzed. First, let the density operator

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{3}{5}\ket{c_{1}}\bra{c_{1}}+\frac{1}{5}\ket{c_{1}}\bra{c_{2}}+\frac{1}{5}\ket{c_{2}}\bra{c_{1}}+\frac{2}{5}\ket{c_{2}}\bra{c_{2}}=\begin{pmatrix}0.6&0.2\\

\displaystyle\mathbf{O}_{2}^{1/2}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\lambda_{2}}+\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}&\sqrt{\lambda_{2}}-\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\\

The Löwdin weights, defined as the diagonal elements of

Next, we consider the case where

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{3}{5}\ket{c_{1}}\bra{c_{1}}+\frac{2}{5}\ket{c_{2}}\bra{c_{2}}=\begin{pmatrix}0.6&0\\

For our third example, we now take the maximally mixed (superposition) state to compare it with the previous example, given in dimension two by

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{1}{2}\ket{c_{1}}\bra{c_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\ket{c_{2}}\bra{c_{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\

In fact, such examples can be readily generalized. Consider a general density operator in dimension two, expressed as

\displaystyle\rho=p\ket{c_{1}}\bra{c_{1}}+q\ket{c_{1}}\bra{c_{2}}+q\ket{c_{2}}\bra{c_{1}}+(1-p)\ket{c_{2}}\bra{c_{2}}=\begin{pmatrix}p&q\\

From this matrix, the Löwdin weights are given by

\displaystyle w_{1}^{\text{L}}(\rho)=\left[\rho_{\text{L}}\right]_{11}=\frac{1+\left(2p-1\right)\sqrt{1-s^{2}}+2qs}{2+4qs},

Hence, the Löwdin transformation provides crucial insights for quantum state characterization, which we summarize as follows::

•

The Löwdin weights yield a consistent probability distribution that properly accounts for basis overlap, serving as unambiguous indicators of classical mixing.

•

The off-diagonal elements in the Löwdin basis contain two contributions:

3.2  Probabilistic interpretation of Löwdin weights and coherence quantification

In this section, we focus on understanding the conceptual roles of Löwdin weights and on quantifying coherence in superposition states. Now, consider again a superposition state expanded in a non-orthogonal basis
\{\ket{c_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{d}
as in Eq. (
24
). As discussed, applying the LSO to this basis maps it to an orthonormal one, in which the superposition state
\ket{\alpha}
is associated with a unique probability distribution
\mathbf{w}=\big(w_{1}^{\text{L}}(\alpha),\dots,w_{d}^{\text{L}}(\alpha)\big)
. This distribution admits a direct information-theoretic interpretation: the Shannon entropy of the weights,

H(\mathbf{w})=-\sum_{k=1}^{d}w_{k}^{\text{L}}(\alpha)\log_{2}w_{k}^{\text{L}}(\alpha),

(43)

quantifies the effective delocalization of the state over the Löwdin-orthonormalized basis. Importantly, large entropy values correspond to states that are broadly spread across the basis (high coherence), whereas smaller values indicate localization
[
39
,
40
]
on fewer basis components (low coherence). This extends the standard coherence quantification in orthonormal settings
[
18
,
23
]
to general non-orthogonal scenarios without ambiguity.

Beyond the Shannon entropy (
43
), one can also quantify delocalization using the participation ratio (PR)
[
41
]
, where Löwdin weights naturally serve as the primary and foundational element in this context. In terms of Löwdin weights, the PR is defined as:

\text{PR}(\mathbf{w})=\frac{1}{\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(w_{k}^{\text{L}}(\alpha)\right)^{2}}.

(44)

The PR estimates the effective number of orthonormalized basis states over which
\ket{\alpha}
is delocalized. Complementarily, the inverse participation ratio (IPR)
[
39
,
42
,
43
]

\text{IPR}(\mathbf{w})=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(w_{k}^{\text{L}}(\alpha)\right)^{2}

(45)

quantifies localization: higher IPR indicates concentration on fewer basis states, with PR and IPR related by
\text{PR}=1/\text{IPR}
. Both measures inherit the probabilistic and basis-independent properties of Löwdin weights and provide a geometric perspective complementary to (
43
). Quantifying coherence in superposition states is a fundamental problem of broad relevance; here, we emphasize the potential role of Löwdin weights in addressing this issue and present a first step toward its solution.

Before concluding this section, we consider two-dimensional systems as illustrative examples, where a pure state
\ket{\beta}
is expanded in a non-orthogonal basis
\{\ket{c_{1}},\ket{c_{2}}\}
as follows:

\displaystyle\ket{\beta}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\gamma^{2}+2\gamma s}}\big(\ket{c_{1}}+\gamma\ket{c_{2}}\big).

(46)

The basis states are normalized, and their non-orthogonality is characterized by a finite overlap
\langle c_{1}|c_{2}\rangle=s
, where
s
quantifies the degree of linear dependence between the states. The superposition state
\ket{\beta}
is constructed as a weighted combination of
\ket{c_{1}}
and
\ket{c_{2}}
, with the parameter
\gamma
controlling the relative contribution of each basis state. The normalization factor in the expression for
\ket{\beta}
explicitly accounts for both the superposition weights and the non-orthogonality of the basis, ensuring the state remains properly normalized.

For the specific case where
\gamma=0.6
and the overlap
s=0.4
, Eq. (
26
) yields the Löwdin weights
w_{1}^{\text{L}}(\beta)\approx 0.66
and
w_{2}^{\text{L}}(\beta)\approx 0.34
. The Shannon entropy
H(\mathbf{w})
, computed using these weights via Eq. (
43
), attains a value of approximately
0.925
. This entropy value reflects a balanced degree of coherence, indicating that the state
\ket{\beta}
is neither fully localized nor perfectly delocalized over the orthonormalized basis. The reduction in entropy from its maximum value (which is 1 for
d=2
) arises from the interplay between the non-orthogonality of the original basis (
s=0.4
) and the asymmetry in the superposition coefficients (
\gamma=0.6
).

To further elucidate the role of basis non-orthogonality, we reduce the overlap to
s=0.1
while keeping
\gamma=0.6
. This modification results in a more pronounced imbalance in the Löwdin weights, with
w_{1}^{\text{L}}(\beta)\approx 0.715
and
w_{2}^{\text{L}}(\beta)\approx 0.285
. Then, the Shannon entropy decreases to
H(\mathbf{w})\approx 0.862
, signaling a higher degree of state localization compared to the
s=0.4
case. This trend demonstrates that as the overlap between the original basis states diminishes, the state
\ket{\beta}
