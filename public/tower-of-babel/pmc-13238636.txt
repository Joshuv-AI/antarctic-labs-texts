Landscape of Immune Remodeling and NAP1L1‐Driven Epithelial Reprogramming in Gastric Cancer Metastasis
Authors: Min Sun, Yuanzhi Wang, Yuqing Zhang, Xiaojuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMetastasis is a major driver of treatment failure and mortality for gastric cancer, and the detailed cellular and molecular patterns driving tumor cells from the primary tumor to metastatic lesions remain poorly characterized.MethodsWe analyzed single‐cell RNA sequencing data from 131 samples, including 27 normal gastric tissue (NC), 48 primary gastric tumors (PT), and 56 metastatic lesions (lymph node metastasis, LNM [sample number = 2]; liver metastasis, LM [sample number = 9]; ovary metastasis, OM [sample number = 3]; peritoneum metastasis, PM [sample number = 42]), and finally generated a high‐resolution cellular atlas.ResultsUsing all cells, we identified 11 major cell types and characterized remodeling of T cell or natural killer cell, myeloid, and epithelial cells across primary and metastatic lesions. The depletion of cytotoxic CD8.Teff and NK cells in LNM, concomitant with enrichment of exhausted CD4.Tex cells, were observed. CD4.Treg cells were found enriched in PT but reduced in metastatic lesions. Comparison of gene expression between primary and metastasis for epithelial cells identified NAP1L1 as a consistently upregulated gene across all four distinct metastatic sites (LNM, LM, OM, and PM). In silico knockout of NAP1L1 in epithelial cells indicated the perturbation of focal adhesion and cell‐substrate junction pathways and high expression of prometastatic genes, including Jun proto‐oncogene, AP‐1 transcription factor subunit, JunB proto‐oncogene, AP‐1 transcription factor subunit, and activating transcription factor 3.ConclusionOur study deciphered the immune and epithelial cell dynamics of metastasis and identified NAP1L1 as a novel regulator of epithelial cell reprogramming and metastatic progression.Keywords: gastric cancer, metastasis, NAP1L1, single‐cell RNA sequencing, tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) was one of the most prevalent and lethal malignant tumors worldwide [1, 2], and its metastasis was the primary cause of treatment failure and mortality in advanced patients [3]. The metastatic process for cancer cells was a complex process driven by diverse factors, including tumor microenvironment (TME) [4, 5], immune cell dysregulation, and epithelial cell reprogramming in metastasis lesions [6, 7, 8, 9]. However, the cellular and molecular changes underlying the transition from primary to distant metastatic lesions (e.g., lymph node, liver, ovary, and peritoneum) remained poorly elucidated at single‐cell resolution.

As a powerful tool to decipher cellular heterogeneity at single‐cell levels [10, 11], single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) enabled us to characterize the immune and parenchymal cell dynamics during tumor progression and metastasis [12, 13]. Also, recent studies used scRNA‐seq to decode the cellular atlas of primary GC and metastasis [14, 15], yet few studies focused on conserved metastasis‐associated genes from primary lesions to multiple metastasis lesions. As a key component of TME, immune cells played a dual role in antitumor or immunosuppressive [16, 17]. T or NK cell exhaustion and myeloid cell polarization were associated with facilitating tumor immune evasion, but their lesion‐specific dynamics in distinct GC metastases remained uncharacterized. Epithelial cell transcriptional reprogramming was crucial for GC metastasis, yet the core genes regulating this process still need to be identified. NAP1L1 was reported to play a role in chromatin remodeling and cell cycle regulation in several malignancies [18, 19, 20], which suggested its potential involvement in tumor progression and aggressiveness. However, its expression pattern and function in GC metastasis, especially across multiple metastatic sites, have not been investigated.

Here, we performed scRNA data analysis on clinical samples spanning normal gastric tissue, primary GC, and four major metastatic lesions (lymph node metastasis [LNM], liver metastasis [LM], ovary metastasis [OM], and peritoneum metastasis [PM]), constructing a comprehensive high‐resolution cellular atlas of GC and its metastases. We systematically characterized the lineage‐specific remodeling of T cell or natural killer cell (T/NK) cells and myeloid cells across distinct pathological states, and identified conserved transcriptional changes in metastatic epithelial cells. We aim to unravel the cellular and molecular basis of GC metastasis, identify core regulatory genes and pathways, and provide novel potential therapeutic targets for inhibiting GC metastatic progression.

Methods

Data sets

The GC scRNA‐seq expression matrix and clinical information were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo, accession numbers GSE163558, GSE183904, GSE228598, and GSE239676). GSE163558 contained three primary GC, one normal control, two LNM, one OM, one PM, and two LM samples. GSE183904 contained 4 PM, 26 primary GC, and 10 normal control samples. GSE228598 contained 28 PM samples. GSE239676 contained 7 LM, 16 normal control, 2 OM, 10 PM, and 19 primary GC samples.

Single‐Cell RNA Data Analysis

The RNA expression matrix quality control, cell filter, cluster, and analysis were performed by Seurat (v.5.0.0, https://satijalab.org/seurat/) following its official guideline [21]. The percentage of mitochondrial and ribosome genes was calculated for each sample. Quality control was applied to cells based on three metrics step by step: the unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts, the number of genes, and the proportion of mitochondrial gene counts per cell. Specifically, the combination of UMIs (< 25,000), gene counts (500 < genes < 5000), and mitochondrial genes (percentage < 20) was used for raw gene‐cell‐barcode matrix filtering [22, 23]. After removing low‐quality cells from the matrix, a global‐scaling normalization method, “LogNormalize,” which normalizes the feature expression measurements for each cell by the total expression, multiplies this by a scale factor (10,000). The FindVariableFeatures function was used to identify genes with the most obvious cell‐to‐cell differences, and a consensus list of 2000 variable genes was obtained. Then, a linear transformation was performed by variable genes (excluding all ribosomal and mitochondrial genes) with the greatest recovery rates across samples. The RunPCA function was used to compute the principal components (PCs). The JackStraw and PCElbowPlot functions of Seurat were used to select a subset of significant PCs.

Batch effect correction among samples was performed by the Harmony (v.1.2.0) algorithm [24]. The PCA matrix was generated with 50 PCs from selected genes, and the analysis was performed with a resolution of 0.3 using the FindClusters function. The differentially expressed genes were analyzed by the FindMarkers function in Seurat with the following parameters (min.pct = 0.2 and logfc.threshold = 0.2). Differential gene expression (DGE) was defined by Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests with default regularization parameters in Seurat. The log2‐transformed fold change > 1 or < −1 with adjusted p < 0.05 was considered a significant change.

In Silico Gene Knockout in Epithelial Cells Using scTenifoldKnk

In silico knockout of NAP1L1 was performed in GC epithelial cells using the scTenifoldKnk (v1.0.3) package in R language [25]. Raw count matrices were normalized by LogNormalize (scale.factor = 10,000), and genes expressed in ≥ 10% of epithelial cells were retained. The parameters were set following its official default guides: cell number was set to 2000, Net number was set to 20, Comp number was set to 3, and core number was set to 20. Differentially expressed genes were defined as adjusted p < 0.05 and log 2FC > 1 or < −1.

Pathways Analysis

Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes analyses were performed by clusterProfiler (v.4.0.5) [26]. The top 10 most enriched pathways (ranked by adjusted p value) were filtered for further visualization.

Statistics

Statistical analysis of all data was performed using R (v4.2.1). Student's t tests were conducted for normally distributed data, and Mann–Whitney test was used for data with unknown distribution.

Results

Single‐Cell Atlas Construction of Normal Gastric Tissue, Primary GC, and Metastatic Lesions

To characterize the heterogeneity of gastric and metastasis lesions, we integrated the scRNA data set, performed analysis, and finally generated a high‐resolution atlas (Figure 1A). We enrolled 27 samples from normal gastric control (NC, cell number = 58,502), two samples from LNM (cell number = 11,105), 48 samples from primary gastric tumor (PT, cell number = 157,000), nine samples from LM (cell number = 33,755), three samples from OM (cell number = 4811), and 42 samples from PM (cell number = 148,226; Figure 1A,B).

After quality control, data normalization, dimensionality reduction, and manual annotation by canonical marker, we identified 11 major cell types that were confirmed by classical markers (Figure 1C,D). Specifically, the lymphoid lineages T cells were featured with CD3D and CD3E; B cells with MS4A1 and CD79A; plasmocytes with MZB1; myeloid monocytes with Fc gamma receptor 3A (FCGR3A), CD14, and LYZ; macrophages with CD163 and CD68; mast cells with proto‐oncogene receptor tyrosine kinase (KIT), tryptase alpha/beta 1 (TPSAB1), TPSB2, and MS4A2; epithelial cells with EPCAM, KRT8, and KRT18; fibroblasts with COL1A1, COL3A1, and DCN; and endothelial cells with CLDN5, CDH5, and VWF (Figure 1D).

Besides, cell type distribution was highly consistent across all groups, which indicated no extreme data or samples (Figure 1E). Further percentage calculation revealed the dominance of T cells among all groups (Figure 1F). Observation‐to‐expectation analysis further demonstrated significant enrichment or depletion trends for each group (Figure 1G), which indicated a cellular remodeling in the microenvironment during GC progression and metastasis.

Lesion‐Specific Remodeling of T/NK Cell Subpopulations in GC Metastasis

Given the predominance of T/NK cells in the GC microenvironment, we extracted this lineage for deep subclustering and functional annotation. Cell counts of T/NK cells ranged from 2644 to 66,621 across groups, confirming marked variation in lineage abundance (Figure 2A,B). Unsupervised clustering identified eight functionally distinct T/NK subpopulations, including CD4.Naive, CD4.Memory, CD4.Treg, CD4.Tex, CD8.Naive, CD8.Memory, CD8.Teff, and NK cells (Figure 2C). Canonical marker gene expression profiling validated the functional identity of each subpopulation (Figure 2D): CD4.Treg cells exhibited specific upregulation of immunosuppressive markers forkhead box P3 and cytotoxic T‐lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA4); Cytotoxic CD8.Teff cells expressed high levels of the pro‐lytic gene granzyme A; CD4.Tex cells displayed an exhausted phenotype characterized by CTLA4 and programmed cell death 1 upregulation; Naive T‐cell subsets (CD4.Naive and CD8.Naive) showed robust expression of homing and quiescence markers C‐C motif chemokine receptor 7 (CCR7) and selectin L; NK cells were defined by the expression of innate cytotoxicity genes TYRO protein tyrosine kinase binding protein and natural killer cell granule protein 7.

Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) visualization revealed an overall similar spatial distribution of T/NK subpopulations across groups (LM, LNM, NC, OM, PM, and PT), yet distinct group‐specific changes in abundance and distribution were evident (Figure 2E). Further quantified analysis revealed a dysregulated percentage of Cytotoxic T/NK cells (Figure 2F). CD8.Teff and NK cells showed markedly reduced trends in LNM, which indicated the loss of antitumor cytotoxic immunity in the lymph node. Whereas CD4.Tex showed the most significant upregulation in LNM, which represented an exhausted T‐cell status in the lymph node lesion. CD4.Treg cells were enriched in PT, yet depleted in LM, NC, OM, and PM, which indicated a lesion‐specific immunosuppressive status (Figure 2G).

Together, these results demonstrated diversity niches among distinct GC metastatic and primary gastric, characterized by cytotoxic cell loss and T‐cell exhaustion in LNM, and Treg‐mediated immunosuppression between primary and metastatic lesions.

Myeloid Cell Polarization Toward Niche‐Adaptive Immunosuppressive Phenotypes in GC Metastasis

Considering that myeloid cells are central to innate immune regulation and tissue homeostasis [27], we therefore extracted monocyte and macrophage, then performed subclustering (Figure 3A,B), and identified nine subsets, including Mast cells (featured with KIT, TPSAB1, and carboxypeptidase A3), cycling monocytes (featured with marker of proliferation Ki‐67), CD16+ monocytes (featured with FCGR3A, leukocyte specific transcript 1, and leukocyte immunoglobulin like receptor B2), complement C1q C chain (C1QC)+ macrophage (featured with C1QC, complement C1q A chain, and apolipoprotein E), NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3)+ macrophage (featured with NLRP3, epiregulin, and interleukin 1 beta [IL1B]), three conventional dendritic cell (C‐type lectin domain containing 9A (CLEC9A) positive dendritic cell [CLEC9A + DC] featured with CLEC9A, FMS like tyrosine kinase 3, and indoleamine 2,3‐dioxygenase 1; CD1c molecule (CD1C) positive dendritic cell [CD1C + DC] featured with CD1C, AFc gamma receptor 1A, and major histocompatibility complex, class II, DQ alpha 1; lysosomal associated membrane protein 3 (LAMP3) positive dendritic cell [LAMP3 + DC] featured with LAMP3, CCR7, and fascin actin‐bundling protein 1), and leukocyte immunoglobulin like receptor A4 positive (LILRA4+) plasmacytoid dendritic cell (featured with LILRA4, granzyme B, and interleukin 3 receptor subunit alpha; Figure 3C,D) [28, 29].

Further expression analysis of inflammatory factor revealed distinct patterns between primary and metastatic lesions; some cells exhibited high expression of inflammatory factor genes, such as C‐X‐C motif Chemokine Ligand, C‐C motif Chemokine Ligand series, and IL1B (Figure 3E). By grouping all cells according to different primary and metastatic lesions, it was found that there were significant differences in cell distribution (Figure 3F). Further statistical analysis of the percentage of cells in each group revealed that C1QC+ macrophages were higher in LM, NC, OM, and PM than in gastric primary lesions (Figure 3G). The observation‐to‐expectation analysis results indicate that LILRA4 + plasmacytoid dendritic cell (pDC), LAMP3 + conventional dendritic cell (cDC), and CLEC9A + cDC are enriched in LNM (Figure 3H). These findings identify niche‐adaptive polarization of the myeloid compartment as a hallmark of GC metastasis, with distant metastases favoring immunosuppressive macrophage accumulation and LNM exhibiting selective enrichment of DC subsets.

NAP1L1 as a Conserved Metastasis‐Associated Gene Driving Epithelial Cell Transcriptional Reprogramming in GC

Epithelial cells represent the malignant parenchymal component of GC, and their transcriptional reprogramming is a key driver of metastatic progression [30]. We extracted epithelial cells from all groups for UMAP visualization, while marked variation in epithelial cell count was observed, spatial distribution was consistent across groups (Figure 4A,B). To identify transcriptional programs underlying GC metastasis, we performed DGE analysis of epithelial cells between metastatic lesions (LM, LNM, OM, and PM) and PT (served as the primary tumor control), with volcano plots revealing extensive transcriptional reprogramming in all metastatic subsets (Figure 4C). To identify common factors that promote metastasis, we overlapped all DGEs and finally focused on NAP1L1 (Figure 4D), whose expression levels were significantly higher in each metastatic lesion than in the primary lesion, and significantly higher in the tumor primary lesion than in the normal gastric epithelial control (Figure 4E). Compared with the primary gastric lesion, PM, OM, LM, and LNM were upregulated by 1.11–2.52 folds, respectively (Figure 4F). These data establish NAP1L1 as a conserved, metastasis‐associated gene whose expression correlates with GC progression from normal tissue to primary tumor to metastatic lesions, suggesting a functional role in driving epithelial cell reprogramming during GC metastasis.

Gene expression analysis of the NAP1L1 knockout signature identified a panel of significantly dysregulated genes that exhibited the most robust upregulation following virtual NAP1L1 depletion (Figure 5C,D). These genes are implicated in key cellular processes, including transcription factor, immediate stress response, and cell cycle regulation, which contribute to metastatic progression. Together, these in silico perturbation data demonstrated that NAP1L1 modulated the expression of prometastatic genes and regulated critical pathways governing cancer cell invasion and metastasis, establishing it as a functional regulator of epithelial cell reprogramming in GC.

Discussion

Tumor metastasis was a complex process driven by diverse factors in the TME [31]. However, the detailed mechanisms that govern this process remain incompletely understood. In this study, we generated a high‐resolution single‐cell atlas comprising normal gastric tissue, primary GC, and metastatic lesions (LNM, LM, OM, and PM), uncovered lesion‐specific immune remodeling, and identified NAP1L1 as a novel gene that could be regarded as a conserved regulator of epithelial cell reprogramming and metastatic progression.

Lesion‐Specific T/NK Cells Shape Anti‐Tumor Immunity in GC Metastasis

Our study identified a lesion‐specific pattern of T/NK cells among all tissues, including marked depletion of cytotoxic CD8.Teff and NK cells, and robust upregulation of exhausted CD4.Tex cells in the lymph node. This result was consistent with immune evasion in LNM, where chronic antigen stimulation drove T‐cell exhaustion, loss of cytotoxic function, and ultimately enabled malignant cell seeding and outgrowth in the lymph node [32, 33]. The co‐occurrence of exhausted CD4 + T cells and depletion of cytotoxic CD8+ effector T cells in metastatic lymph nodes suggested that the failure of CD4 + T cells may underlie the compromised CD8 + T‐cell cytotoxicity in the microenvironment. The CD4.Treg cells were found to be enriched in primary tumors and depleted in metastases, which further highlighted the immunosuppressive plasticity and facilitated the malignant cells to escape from immunity [34]. These data indicated that GC metastatic lesions drove adaptive immune remodeling and that targeting T‐cell exhaustion and restoring cytotoxic immunity, particularly in LNM, may represent a promising strategy for inhibiting LNM in GC.

Myeloid Cell Polarization Toward Niche‐Adaptive Phenotypes Drives Innate Immune Suppression in GC Metastasis

Our study uncovered two core myeloid remodeling events in GC metastasis, including enrichment of immunosuppressive C1QC+ macrophages in distant metastases (LM, OM, and PM) and selective enrichment of DC subsets in LNM. C1QC+ macrophages were an M2‐like, anti‐inflammatory subset characterized by the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines (e.g., interleukin 10 and transforming growth factor β) and proangiogenic factors—features that promote cancer cell survival, invasion, and metastatic seeding in distant organs [35]. Their enrichment in LM, OM, and PM identified immunosuppressive macrophage polarization as a conserved feature of distant GC metastasis. In contrast, the enrichment of pDC in LNM revealed a unique innate immune status in the lymph node. This indicates the increased antigen presentation in the lymph node microenvironment, or a compensatory response to the loss of cytotoxic T/NK cells.

NAP1L1 as a Potential Regulator of Epithelial Cell Reprogramming and GC Metastasis

In epithelial malignant cells, we compared epithelial cells from all metastasis lesions with those from primary lesions and identified NAP1L1 as a conserved, metastasis‐associated gene with expression that correlates with GC progression (normal < PT < metastasis). NAP1L1 was a member of the nucleosome assembly protein family [18, 19], with known roles in chromatin remodeling and cell cycle regulation [20]; however, its function in cancer metastasis has not been previously characterized. Our in silico NAP1L1 knockout analysis revealed that depletion of this gene perturbs focal adhesion and cell‐substrate junction pathways. Both pathways were critical cellular processes governing cancer cell invasion, migration, and metastatic seeding. These results suggested a role of NAP1L1 in modulating cell‐matrix interactions and cellular motility. Since these pathways were closely associated with cellular invasiveness instead of the canonical epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) program, it implied that NAP1L1 may facilitate metastatic potential through a distinct adhesion‐dependent mechanism rather than a broad EMT‐like process. These data established NAP1L1 as a functional regulator of epithelial cell reprogramming in GC, with its upregulation driving the expression of pro‐metastatic transcriptional programs and modulating pathways critical for metastatic progression.

Study Limitations

First, this is a cross‐sectional study of the GC metastatic microenvironment; longitudinal studies tracking GC progression from primary tumor to metastatic lesion are needed. Second, the NAP1L1 knockout analysis indicates its role in epithelial cell reprogramming; the in vitro and in vivo functional validation (e.g., overexpression, knockout, and GC metastasis animal models) or independent cohorts are still needed. Third, the samples for OM lesions (n = 3) and LMN (n = 2) are limited; more metastatic samples will enable more robust characterization of niche‐specific remodeling events. Fourth, this study was based on cross‐sectional, unpaired primary and metastatic samples from different individuals rather than paired specimens from the same patients. This may influence the results, and interindividual heterogeneity cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

Our scRNA‐seq study deciphers the cellular and molecular landscape of GC metastasis, reveals lesion‐specific remodeling of the adaptive and innate immune microenvironment, and identifies NAP1L1 as a novel and conserved regulator of epithelial cell reprogramming and metastatic progression. Our findings established that GC metastatic niches drive divergent immune adaptation and that NAP1L1 modulates prometastatic pathways critical for cancer cell invasion and migration. We provided a comprehensive single‐cell resource for the GC research community and identified new therapeutic targets to inhibit GC metastasis.
