CORSTITCH - A free, open source software for stitching and georeferencing underwater coral reef videos
Julian Christopher L. Maypa, Johnenn R. Manalang, Maricor N. Soriano

Abstract
CorStitch is an open-source software developed to automate the creation of accurate georeferenced reef mosaics from video transects obtained through Automated Rapid Reef Assessment System (ARRAS) surveys. We utilized a Fourier-based image correlation algorithm to stitch sequential video frames, aligning them with synchronized GNSS timestamps. The resulting compressed Keyhole Markup Language (KMZ) files, compatible with geographic information systems such as Google Earth, enable detailed spatial analysis. Validation through comparative analysis of mosaics from two temporally distinct surveys of the same reef demonstrated the software’s consistent and reliable performance.

The latest stitching and georeferencing software of ARRAS, known as KikoMaan, was licensed by UP Diliman in 2021 to Antipara Exploration
[
10

To promote the wider use of the ARRAS method we developed CorStitch, an open-source stitching software designed to generate georeferenced mosaics from underwater belt transect videos. Unlike Kiko
\&
Stitch which stitches and blends full image frames, CorStitch stitches horizontal or vertical bands (depending on the tow direction) taken from the center of video. Unlike KikoMaan, georeferencing is already built-in in CorStitch providing a turnkey solution for translating underwater videos and GPS tracks into georeferenced mosaics usable in geographic information systems. The CorStitch-generated mosaics were validated by comparing two belt transects of the same fringing reef with nearly identical GPS tracks. Comparison of the mosaics demonstrated the software’s consistent and reliable performance, as evidenced by the identical coral colonies appearing in overlapping and adjacent mosaics.

We hope that by providing CorStitch as free software more communities and local agencies can perform mapping, validation, and natural capital accounting of benthic habitats in their municipal waters.

2.1
Data Collection and Preprocessing

The data collection method is inspired by an ARRAS-type survey. In this survey, we utilized a GoPro Hero 4 camera and a Humminbird 899ci HD SI combo echosounder. The camera was set to capture a wide-angle video with a resolution of 1080p and a frame rate of 30. Both of these instruments were attached to a towfish so that both the camera and the echosounder’s transducer were facing the seafloor. The experimental setup is shown in Figure
2
.

We collected video and echosounder data along a 4.5 km belt transect in Brgy. Gulod, Calatagan, Batangas by towing the towfish at a rate of 5 km/h. The survey area contained sandy patches and coral reefs at a depth of approximately 5 m - 15 m. This range of depth was chosen because it was observed that video detail was lost at depths greater than 15 m due to the water’s light attenuating property.

2.2.1
Image correlation

In the process of converting the video data to mosaics, we employ a stitching algorithm that correlates two successive video frames. If two image functions
f
and
g
correspond to two adjacent frames that are related by a planar shift, then we write
g
as

g(x,y)=f(x+\Delta x,\,y+\Delta y)

(1)

where
\Delta x
and
\Delta y
correspond to the horizontal and vertical shifts, respectively. The cross-correlation between the two functions is

CC=(f\star g)(x,y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy^{\prime}f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})g(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime}).

(2)

This expression is related to the convolution integral given by

CC=(f\star g)(x,y)=f(-x,-y)^{\ast}\circledast g(x,y),

(3)

where
∗
denotes the complex conjugate of the function. Due to the presence of the two indefinite integrals in the convolution, it would be computationally extensive to calculate the cross-correlation between each pair of frames of high-resolution videos. Thus, to efficiently compute the cross-correlation, we apply a Fourier transform to eq. (
3
) to convert the convolution to a Hadamard product given by

CC=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{F(u,v)^{\ast}\circ G(u,v)\right\},

(4)

where
F
and
G
are the Fourier transforms of
f
and
g

[
12
]
, respectively, and
\circ
is the Hadamard product or the entry-wise product. The maximum value, or the peak, of
CC
is the point at which the two images overlap the most with respect to the center of the image. This means that, given the relation in
1
, the correlation peak of
f
and
g
is displaced from the center by
\Delta x
horizontally and
\Delta y
vertically. By overlapping
f
and
g
based on where they correlate the most, the two images can be stitched together to create a seemingly continuous image.

Calculating eq. (
4
) by means of Fast Fourier Transform is already computationally efficient. However, the location of the correlation peak is influenced by both the phase and magnitude of
F
and
G
. Thus, high brightness areas will give a relatively high correlation value even if most of the features of
f
and
g
do not match. To remove the weight of the image’s brightness in computing the cross-correlation, the Fourier transform can be normalized. This normalization will not affect the true location of the correlation peak because the Fourier transform of the spatial shift between
f
and
g
is the phase difference between
F
and
G
. Therefore, the normalization of the Fourier transform can be approximated by

PC=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{\frac{F(u,v)^{\ast}\circ G(u,v)}{\left|F(u,v)^{\ast}\circ G(u,v)\right|}\right\}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{\exp(-i(u\Delta x+v\Delta y)\right\},

(5)

where
PC
is the phase correlation of the two images.

Using these concepts of cross-correlation, CorStitch can stitch succeeding frames together to create a mosaic. In the context of Corstitch’s data, the program is designed to process underwater videos primarily containing marine benthos. This makes the frames either predominantly green or blue due to the water’s high attenuation to longer wavelengths of light. With this, for correlating
f
and
g
, CorStitch will only use the green channel to get the correlation peak.

Another important factor to consider when correlating the frames is magnification. Due to the planar shift of the camera, an object in
f
will have a different magnification in
g
due to the changing object-to-camera distance. This difference in magnification, which is a parallax error, can affect the location of the correlation peak if the camera is towed at high speeds. To combat this effect, we minimize the angular field of view by only using the horizontal central strip of each frame. Minimizing the angular field of view approximates the image to a telecentric image, thus reducing the parallax errors.

During experimentation, we noticed that
CC
fails to correlate images with large patches of sand. This is because sand patches have high brightness, thus giving inaccurately high correlation values.
PC
on the other hand had no problems in getting the correct correlation peaks in sandy patches. However,
PC
fails when the features are distorted when the data contains motion blur while
CC
does not. This could be due to the varying brightness of the features which aids in the correlation of
CC
. With this, CorStitch uses both
CC
and
PC
to correlate
f
and
g
, and it will use the correlation peak with the least displacement from the center. We use the peak with the least displacement because succeeding video frames should have small displacements, this is especially true for videos with a high frame rate.

A negative value of

f
and let

be the frame after it. We repeat this process until
g

CorStitch compiles the frames into multiple mosaics, with each mosaic containing
n

n
is equal to

n=\text{frames per second}\times\text{mosaic time}.

We set the mosaic time to be

2.2.2
Georeferencing

By synchronizing the video time with the local time, we were able to match the mosaics with their corresponding GPS data, which was collected by the echosounder. However, based on the experimental setup, the down-looking echosounder only collected GPS data along the center vertical of the mosaics. To get the GPS coordinates of the corners of the mosaic, we used the following formulas:

\displaystyle\mathrm{lat_{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{lat_{1}}+\frac{\Delta l_{h}}{r_{E}}\cdot\frac{180^{\circ}}{\pi},

(7)

\displaystyle\mathrm{lon_{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{lon_{1}}+\frac{\Delta l_{v}}{r_{E}}\cdot\frac{180^{\circ}}{\pi}\cdot\frac{1}{\cos(\mathrm{lat_{1}})},

(8)

where
lat_{1}
and
lon_{1}
are the latitude and longitude measured by the echosounder,
\Delta l_{h}
and
\Delta l_{v}
are the horizontal and vertical shift, respectively, in meters, and
lat_{2}
and
lon_{2}
are the new latitude and longitude after applying the shifts. Once we have the GPS coordinates of the corners, we then georeference these images by using the SimpleKML python library. Using the library, we export every 100 mosaics into one compressed Keyhole Markup Language (KMZ) file.

3
Results and Discussion

We tested CorStitch using a 40-minute video of a video transect. The mosaics contain 5 seconds’ worth of video frames with a resolution of 854
px
by 480
px
. The height of each central strip is 95
px
, which is approximately 20
\%
of the frame’s original height.

3.1
Mosaics

CorStitch processed the videos into mosaics using the cross-correlation algorithm. The resulting mosaics are shown in Figure
4
. It can be observed that in deeper regions, CorStitch correaltes the frames more effectively, enabling seamless stitching of successive frames.

Figure 4:
Sample mosaics from Calatagan, Batangas made by CorStitch.

However, correlation errors occur in shallower areas. The correlation error is most apparent on large coral mounds, such as the one in Figure
4
c. Because the coral mound is closer to the camera than its surroundings, the camera’s focus automatically shifts to the mound, resulting in blurriness in the surrounding deeper regions. This blurriness makes it difficult to correlate objects outside the camera’s depth of field, leading to errors in the location of the correlation peak.

Another source of error is caused by the camera’s motion. Motion blur along the tow direction tends to increase with depth. This is because, even though the camera is towed at a constant speed, the pixel shift of objects at greater depths is smaller than the pixel shift of objects at shallower depths. Consequently, the mound appears blurry, which displaces the location of the correlation peak. Additionally, errors are caused by the camera’s angular motion, specifically its roll angle. This roll angle, induced by waves colliding with the banca as the camera is towed, manifests as sideways motion in the mosaic.

It is important to note that georeferencing requires knowing the actual width of the mosaic, or assigning each pixel to its corresponding coordinate. Since this type of data cannot be obtained using a single echosounder, CorStitch sets the width to 3 meters for visualization purposes. This width was chosesn because it was observed that the features in the mosaics were preserved; they were neither stretched nor comprossed in this setting.

To test the consistency of the georeferenced images, we used another dataset collected at a different time along the same belt transect.

Figure

By matching the coral colonies in the two mosaics, it can be seen that the overlapping mosaics depict the same coral colonies. This demonstrates that CorStitch can consistently create and georeference the mosaics.

It is worth noting that the corals do not completely overlap. This is due to differences in tides and GPS accuracy during the data collection periods. However, the degree of overlap between the two mosaics and the matching coral colonies indicates that CorStitch successfully and consistently georeferenced the mosaics.

4
Conclusions

By testing CorStitch with multiple belt transect videos, the software has shown that it can stitch together video frames to create georeferenced mosaics. The overlapping georeferenced mosaics from data sets taken along the same belt transect but at different times showed the same coral colonies, which shows that CorStitch is a reliable tool in processing data from ARRAS surveys.

The presence of stitching errors in the mosaics can be minimized through better data collection methods and better correlation algorithms. Starting with the collection methods, we advise that the towing of the camera must be slow and steady. The tow speed should be around
2
to
4~\mathrm{m/s}
, and the survey is best conducted in the absence of large waves.

For the correlation algorithm, other algorithms can be explored such as Phase-Correlation and take into account the angular displacement of the two succeeding frames.
