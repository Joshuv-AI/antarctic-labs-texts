Central Message

An extended VV to left atrial anastomosis offers a safe, extra-cardiac salvage option for mixed TAPVR, avoiding posterior dissection but requiring vigilance for coronary anomalies.

Although simple forms of total anomalous pulmonary venous return (TAPVR) can be characterized as less complicated repairs with good outcomes, mixed TAPVR variants present with anatomic complexity and require different approaches. In these mixed TAPVR variants, a dominant, unobstructed venous confluence establishes a path of least resistance, which can hemodynamically mask critical pathology in solitary, remote veins during initial hemodynamic assessment. Consequently, mixed TAPVR is an independent risk factor for postoperative pulmonary venous obstruction (PVO), with mortality rates significantly higher than isolated subtypes.1,2

To standardize management, mixed TAPVR variants are divided into 2 distinct groups: category 1, or “2 + 2” patterns, where venous return is split symmetrically; and category II, or “3 + 1” patterns, where a major confluence drains 3 veins while a solitary vein drains remotely.3 Category II variants carry significantly higher surgical risks due to the difficulty of recruiting widely separated veins into the left atrium (LA) without tension.1 Standard techniques for the solitary vein, such as ligation or incorporation, carry inherent risks of lobar infarction, thrombosis, and stenosis. To address these challenges, we present a repair technique for a “3 + 1” variant where the patient's specific anatomy, an isolated left upper pulmonary vein (LUPV) draining to a vertical vein (VV), which also entered the existing confluence, fortuitously allowed for an extra-cardiac repair. This approach avoided reentry into the LA thereby avoiding potential mitral valve (MV) injury and dissection of native pulmonary veins (PVs) but highlighted a peril regarding coronary anatomy supplying the LA.

Case Presentation

A 5-month-old male infant was diagnosed postnatally with a “3 + 1” unobstructed TAPVR variant consisting of 3 veins draining to the coronary sinus (CS) and an LUPV draining to the innominate vein Figure 1. At 2 months of age, he underwent primary repair consisting of CS unroofing and atrial septal defect (ASD) closure. The interatrial incision was re-endothelialized with running Prolene sutures. The LUPV was unobstructed and left in situ. Over the following 2 months, a progressive increase in the gradient to 12 mm Hg prompted further imaging in anticipation of reoperation. Computed tomography revealed a critical stenosis (0.42 cm) at the junction of the confluence with the LA and massive prestenotic dilation (1.18 cm) of the VV to its entry point into the confluence, which had dilated due to downstream obstruction at the confluence (Figure E1). Written informed consent was obtained from the patient's parent/legal guardian for the publication.

Operative Technique

Reoperation was performed via median sternotomy with cardiopulmonary bypass and cooling to 22 °C. After standard aortic and bicaval cannulation, the heart was elevated to dissect the PV confluence. The LA was opened through the septum to clarify the anatomy, and a coronary probe confirmed the location of the VV entering into the confluence. The VV was ligated and divided at its junction with the innominate vein, and it was widely spatulated. This unique anatomic configuration allowed a direct long side-to-side anastomosis between the left atrial appendage (LAA) and the VV/LUPV (Figures E2 and E3). To optimize exposure, we used intermittent deep hypothermic circulatory arrest during the repair.

After completion of the anastomosis, significant bleeding was noted near the left atrioventricular groove, but away from the left circumflex artery itself, and easily controlled with delicate 8-0 sutures. The transient ST-segment changes observed on separation from cardiopulmonary bypass, in the absence of wall motion abnormalities, suggest that a small, anomalous atrial branch of the circumflex supplying the LAA may have been impacted.

Postoperative Course

Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated appropriate hemodynamics with no gradient through the PVs. The patient was extubated on postoperative day 1, weaned to room air, and discharged home on postoperative day 4. Echocardiography at 6 weeks postoperatively revealed a patent pulmonary venous anastomosis to the LA with a mean gradient of 4 mm Hg, which is not hemodynamically significant, at the same site as the initial repair. The patient recovered remarkably well and has required only routine postoperative surveillance, which included a chest radiograph and serial echocardiograms. Because the patient remains asymptomatic and thriving, a postrepair computed tomography was not clinically indicated. At his 9-month well-child check (3.5 months postsurgery), the patient remained clinically well, demonstrating normal growth and achieving all age-appropriate developmental milestones.

Discussion

This case demonstrates the complexities inherent in mixed TAPVR and offers critical insights for the congenital surgeon regarding timing, technique, and anatomic pitfalls.

Although the initial residual gradient at the first operation was concerning, leaving the VV intact was a deliberate strategy. We elected not to address the isolated anomalous LUPV during the operation, because infant repair of an isolated PV can increase the risk of restenosis. Our mental model was that in the absence of suture lines (ie, just the unroofing of the CS and ASD closure), the gradient would potentially not progress. Typically, an isolated anomalous PV does not require early repair and can be addressed in later childhood. In this case, leaving the VV intact provided a vital egress and fortuitously presented an opportunity to conduct the re-repair without risking injury to the MV, which was very close to the unroofed CS, and avoided dissection of the extensive scar tissue that was present at the ASD patch. This approach would also mitigate the need to further dissect the native PVs, which were not obstructed. We were able to perform a standard repair using the LAA and the VV/LUPV connection. Although this approach leveraged fortuitous dilation, surgeons must carefully evaluate preoperative morphology to identify unfavorable anatomies. For instance, in infracardiac TAPVC where the VV is in a less accessible area, this approach likely would not be useful. High-risk predictors of postoperative obstruction include smaller confluence-to-total PV area ratios and the presence of a single PV orifice.4 To avoid anastomotic distortion, surgeons should prioritize wide, tension-free connections. In most cases of postoperative PVO, a sutureless repair is preferred when intracardiac unroofing is not required.5 Additionally, extending left atrial incisions directly into the orifices of the veins themselves can further mitigate geometric distortion.

Perhaps the most critical lesson is the risk of coronary injury. There are many variations of vascular supply to the LA, and surgeons using this approach must be cognizant of these variants. Furthermore, extreme care must be taken to avoid injury to the left circumflex artery, which, although usually constant in its anatomic location within the left atrioventricular groove, remains vulnerable during dissection.

Conclusions

For very rare cases of “3 + 1” mixed TAPVR in which there is an isolated anomalous LUPV draining to a VV that maintains a connection to the LA, an extended anastomosis between the VV and the LAA provides a superior salvage option for postrepair PVO. It avoids the hazards of posterior mediastinal dissection and protects the MV. However, surgeons must navigate the atrioventricular groove with vigilance, anticipating the varied coronary anatomy supplying the LA in these complex variants.
