Forgotten by donors: a call to action by persons with disabilities to resource disability justice within sexual and reproductive health rights funding
Authors: Lisa Adams
Published: 2023

The persistence of ableism and other forms of oppression means that persons with disabilities around the globe, and in particular, women, trans, non-binary and gender non-conforming persons with disabilities, do not have control or choice over their sexuality or reproduction. Ableism is a system of beliefs that are based on discrimination and oppression of persons with disabilities. Deeply rooted in ableism is the belief that persons with disabilities need to be “fixed” or “cured” in order to participate and be included, even when these treatments cause pain and trauma. Ableism creates a system that favours people without disabilities, built upon beliefs and stigma about persons with disabilities that dehumanise them. Ableism produces a web of barriers to access services including education, employment, housing, health care among others, as well as causing severe isolation and exclusion through segregated systems such as institutionalisation and forced incarceration.*

Relentless reproductive injustices experienced by persons with disabilities globally, through ableist laws, policies and practices, have major impacts on their sexual and reproductive health. These reproductive oppressions include a lack of access to culturally competent and accessible sexual and reproductive health services and information, the denial of persons with disabilities as sexual beings and systemic violence such as forced and coerced sterilisation and contraception.1 These multiple and intersecting forms of oppression are far-reaching and extend to the removal of decision-making by persons with disabilities to consent to sex or make autonomous choices about their bodies and their lives. Denial of decision-making can be formal through the legal system to place a person under a substitute decision-making mechanism such as guardianship or conservatorship, formally removing their legal capacity. Decision-making can also be removed informally in which a person’s legal capacity is “de-facto” removed by family members or caregivers who make decisions on their behalf without their consent.2 Persons with disabilities, impacted not only by ableism but racism, sexism, homotransphobia, classism, casteism or other forms of oppression, face even more barriers to achieving sexual and reproductive justice.3

These reproductive justice† issues should be well known in human rights philanthropy yet work at the intersections of SRHR and disability rights is woefully under-funded. Data on global human rights philanthropy by private foundations reveal that the majority of funding is siloed, addressing only one issue or population and that support for intersectional movement building is limited. Data on private foundation funding on human rights presented in this paper are global, including funding to organisations in both the global North and South. This funding has largely failed to support work that cuts across movements and issues supporting more intersectional advocacy, and this is equally true for funding that addresses SRHR and disability in both the global North and South.

Feminist disability activists around the world point to the lack of attention and prioritisation given to disability justice within SRHR movements as well as by donors funding SRHR work. They highlight that the lack of representation of women with disabilities as well as persons with disabilities of diverse sexual orientation, gender identity and expression and sex characteristics (SOGIESC) within the leadership of either movement, means their issues are not prioritised by rights movements globally. They also highlight that the lack of representation of women with disabilities within the leadership of either movement, means their issues have also not been prioritised by rights movements globally.

Under-representation of disability issues within SRHR has also been highlighted by both SRHR and disability activists within the Nairobi Principles working group, a working group composed of disability activists from different countries working at the intersection of disability and SRHR. During three years of online discussions on the intersectional priorities of persons with disabilities in exercising their SRHR, activists continuously address exclusion of these issues in both SRHR and disability movements.‡

The paper uses the term diverse SOGIESC and this refers to people who have been marginalised on the basis of their sexual orientation, gender identity or gender expression such as non-binary, trans or gender non-conforming, as well as sex characteristics including intersex persons. In some contexts Lesbian, Gay, Bi-sexual, Trans, Queer, Intersex plus (LGBTQI+) terminology is more commonly used.

This paper analyses human rights funding data from both private foundations and bilateral donors on SRHR and disability.4 The majority of data analysed in this paper focused on private foundations. The available data on bilateral donor funding for SRHR were more opaque and therefore it was harder to determine amounts spent at the intersection of disability and SRHR. The paper also examines available data on human rights funding by private foundations for intersectional cross-movement advocacy at these intersections. It is important to note that private foundation funding amounts include funding to organisations in the global North as well as the global South.

The paper also centres the voices and perspectives of disability activists, largely from the global South, on how donor funding can better support reproductive justice for persons with disabilities. The recommendations to the donor community are grounded in reproductive and disability justice frameworks and lived experiences of persons with disabilities.

Almost all of the activists interviewed for this paper are people the author has known for a long time through collaboration on cross-movement feminist advocacy as well as through previous work at a disability rights funding organisation. It should be noted that the author worked with one of the interviewees and was overseeing their work at the Disability Rights Fund/Disability Rights Advocacy Fund several years ago and before this article was written. The author had an established working relationship with all but one interviewee. The author identified and reached out to Simone Aspis through a recommendation from a colleague based on their intersectional work and specific focus on advocacy led by persons with intellectual disabilities. All the interviewees were selected based on their unique expertise in SRHR and disability. The author sought to bring together a diversity of perspectives on the basis of geography, disability constituency and advocacy experiences. While there is a good representation of diverse perspectives in this paper, the author recognises that many other factors are not included in this sample, such as the perspectives of trans, non-binary or intersex persons with disabilities, Deafblind persons with disabilities, persons with albinism, Indigenous persons with disabilities, persons with multiple disabilities and those groups of persons with disabilities marginalised by other factors such as religious minority status, ethnic minority status, migration status, etc.

The activists interviewed include Robinah Alambuya, founder of Triumph Uganda, an organisation of persons with psychosocial disabilities in Jinja, Uganda; Dwi Ariyani, a disability activist in Indonesia and Regional Head of Programs in Asia for the Disability Rights Fund and the Disability Rights Advocacy Fund; Agness Chindimba, a Deaf activist and founder of Deaf Women Included, an organisation of women with disabilities working to advance their priorities within the wider women’s movement; Simone Aspis, a grassroots activist and founder of Changing Perspectives, an organisation campaigning to end institutionalisation, forced sterilisation and promote the SRHR of persons with disabilities in the United Kingdom. Simone identifies as a woman with learning disabilities and believes in the power of self-advocates speaking on their own behalf rather than parent-led organising; Phylis Mbeke Ndolo, Executive Director of WomenSpaces Africa in Kenya, a feminist grassroots organisation of women with disabilities focused on SRHR advocacy. These five leaders shared their priorities on SRHR funding and recommendations for how donors can support stronger, more intersectional advocacy and movement building. All interviewees gave their consent for their names to be used in this paper.

Data on funding for SRHR and disability rights

Based on research by the Human Rights Funders Network in 2019 on human rights grant-making by private foundations, funding allocated to persons with disabilities globally is significantly smaller than for other populations, making up 3% of all human rights grant-making in 2019. Funding on disability by multilateral and bilateral donors made up 4% of the total OECD human rights funding to lower-and-middle income countries in 2019. Global funding by private foundations to support SRHR in 2019 made up 10% of all human rights funding. Similarly, SRHR made up 10% of OECD human rights funding by multilateral and bilateral donors in 2019 to low-and middle-income countries.4 Digging deeper into the data, the majority of human rights grant-making by private foundations focuses on one population or issue (i.e. women and girls, indigenous peoples, gender-based violence).5 While there isn’t clear data on how multilateral and bilateral donors address intersectionality in their funding, according to the Guttmacher Institute report on SRHR funding to low-and middle-income countries by multilateral and bilateral donors, persons with disabilities are one of the groups whose needs are overlooked within overall SRHR programmes.6 The implications of this data, particularly for organisations in the Global South, show that both private foundations and multilateral and bilateral donors spend very little on disability and the absence of intersectional funding models perpetuates disability exclusion on key issues such as SRHR.

Funding by human rights foundations plummets when looking at more intersectional identities. Out of 26,000 human rights grants awarded by foundations in 2019, only 28% were intended to benefit more than one population and only 7% address more than three groups (for example, indigenous persons with disabilities who identify as LGBTQI). This suggests that the majority of human rights funding by foundations is failing to consider intersectional identities, the interconnectedness of oppression and the way in which struggles for liberation are intertwined.4 Most critically, donors are not supporting critical cross-movement work.

Grants for LGBTQI people and persons with disabilities are the most siloed funding streams within human rights philanthropy. For example, 77% of LGBTQI grants and 73% of grants for persons with disabilities do not reference any other populations, thus there is very little overlap with other identities and issues. This could be either because funders are not collecting this in their data, or they are leaving out the depth and diversity of identities persons with disabilities have.5 The homogenous or narrowed focus of foundation funding on disability limits the potential for activists with disabilities to address multiple rights issues including SRHR.

Some areas of human rights funding are more intersectional, such as women’s and girls’ rights, with 16% of funding intersecting with disability. SRHR funding has high overlap with other rights issues with 81% of SRHR grants addressing two or more issues.5 However, within SRHR funding by foundations, only 3% addressed persons with disabilities.4 The marginalisation of disability issues within SRHR funding and, conversely, the lack of attention to other issues within disability funding, are extremely limiting to organisations of persons with disabilities (OPDs), led by women with disabilities and persons with disabilities of diverse SOGIESC, to advance their priorities within SRHR.

Disability justice activists outline their priorities for change: reimagining how funding can support reproductive justice for all

The pervasive injustices persons with disabilities face in exercising their SRHR and the sheer lack of funding at these intersections underscore an urgent need for more donor investments and better quality funding on disability and reproductive justice. Based on the World Health Organization’s estimate that persons with disabilities represent 16% of the global population,7 significantly more funding must be allocated to women with disabilities and persons with disabilities of diverse SOGIESC and their representative organisations to lead reproductive justice programming and have visible leadership in SRHR priority setting at the international level.

Eliminate barriers to funding for persons with disabilities

As a first step, donors must eliminate barriers persons with disabilities face in accessing funding including access to information on funding opportunities. One of the first hurdles activists face is accessing donor networks and information about funding. Many activists with disabilities are not part of the social networks of power and influence that their non-disabled peers are. These social capital privileges can help enable connections to donors and funding opportunities.8 Donors can take an active role in facilitating these connections by creating networking platforms that encourage participation from diverse organisations.

Of equal importance, donors need to reassess their funding requirements and application processes to ensure they are accessible, flexible and feasible for small NGOs. Eligibility requirements for funding can be a barrier for small NGOs and requirements for due diligence and auditing can be burdensome for small organisations with limited staffing. Application processes themselves can be inaccessible to organisations of persons with disabilities and reflect an ableist bias. For example, written application requirements in which the western voice is dominant require global South organisations to be literate in a more Northern voice.9 For persons with disabilities who have been systematically excluded from international funding participation and mainstream education, adopting donor language can be a major barrier. Donors also have an obligation to reassess their funding mechanisms to enable more flexible funding opportunities that allow for dialogue with applicants to discuss the priorities and the areas of work applicants feel are most important.

Support critical disability and reproductive justice issues

Along with increased funding on disability and SRHR, interviewees underscored the importance of funding what the movement’s priorities are based on a global South perspective. As a priority, activists highlighted the need for reforms to protect the autonomy, decision-making and consent of persons with disabilities in exercising their SRHR. One of the most critical SRHR violations persons with disabilities face is the denial of decision-making (either formally through the removal of an individual’s legal capacity and being placed under guardianship, or informally when people make decisions on behalf of persons with disabilities without their consent). When decision-making and control is taken from them, they cannot consent to sexual and reproductive health services of their choice. This also enables harmful SRHR decisions to be made without their consent, such as forced or coerced sterilisation, contraception or abortion. These gross human rights violations should serve as a rallying call to the SRHR movement and to donors. This was highlighted as a priority issue for persons with psychosocial disabilities in particular by Robinah Alambuya.10

Donors can also make a significant impact by supporting SRHR community services (for all aspects of sexual and reproductive health including family planning, prenatal and postnatal care, contraception, testing for sexually transmitted diseases, information and communication on sexual and reproductive health, etc.) that are accessible, culturally competent and inclusive to persons with disabilities, and that could serve as an example for other communities to replicate.10 In the current climate where sexual and reproductive health services are highly inaccessible, where persons with disabilities face widespread discrimination by service providers, and community-based support services are not widespread, donors need to understand the importance of building community-based networks of care to support persons with disabilities. Community care alternatives are particularly critical to prevent persons with disabilities from being institutionalised, imprisoned in mental health wards, or deprived of their liberty by being detained in other informal settings including by being detained in their homes by families and caregivers.

In addition to accessible SRHR community services, there is an urgent need for comprehensive sexuality education (CSE) and information that is accessible and inclusive.11 As Agness Chindimba explains, access to information is a critical SRHR priority for Deaf women because the majority of CSE is inaccessible or unavailable in Deaf schools. Agness explains that funding is needed to also support Deaf literacy (learning national Sign Languages) so that Deaf women and girls who have not had access to formal education can get information and communicate with SRHR providers.12

Embed disability and reproductive justice frameworks to guide funding and advocacy

Equally critical to shift the funding ecosystem is for donors to embed disability justice and reproductive justice frameworks in their grant-making. The narrative on SRHR and disability must move beyond rights violations and vulnerability to address the complexity of intersecting oppressions. In Indonesia, ableist assumptions that persons with disabilities are not sexual and will not become parents are widespread. These discriminatory and stigmatising beliefs are part of the reason that disability is not seen as a cross-cutting issue within the sexual and reproductive health rights agenda.13 When disability is addressed by SRHR actors, it is often limited to accessibility of services, rights violations and vulnerability and does not address the complexity of issues or the diversity of the disability community. SRHR conversations should also consider the rights of persons with disabilities to pleasure, intimacy and sexual relationships as well as becoming a parent, promoting a “sex positive” approach advancing reproduction and sexuality of persons with disabilities.14 Donors can support this shift of paradigm by embedding disability justice frameworks in their funding streams and setting funding goals for transformative change.

Centre the leadership and wisdom of persons with disabilities

Funding must also centre the leadership of persons with disabilities. Donors can set a standard for ensuring work on SRHR and disability is led by persons with disabilities themselves and their representative organisations, elevating their wisdom based on their lived experiences and supporting research done by persons with disabilities. Donors must be intentional about learning from persons with disabilities so that their expertise informs and guides funding strategies and priority setting. Donors can play a critical role in transforming reproductive justice work by centring the leadership of persons with disabilities and investing in the wisdom of persons with disabilities to articulate their lived realities and priorities for SRHR.10,13 Robinah explains, “If you don’t understand the lived realities of structurally excluded groups of persons with disabilities, you can’t effectively plan programmes.” This knowledge is critical for building community-based sexual and reproductive health services that are accessible and reflect the needs and priorities of persons with disabilities.10 Without this understanding, there is a risk that donor funding will not effectively dismantle compounding injustices within SRHR and could even reproduce disability exclusion and stigmatising practices. In that case, ableism will persist in the struggle to access SRHR funding.

Just as important, donors can support research led by persons with disabilities on SRHR and invest in their wisdom and expertise. Interviewees underlined that research and data on SRHR and disability are missing in most countries. Research designed and executed by persons with disabilities will help bring more nuanced learning at these intersections. Data and statistics are also critical for influencing donors and bringing more visibility to policy makers on SRHR and disability issues.

Fund cross-movement advocacy and capacity building on SRHR and disability justice

Interconnected to leadership building, all of the interviewees underlined the importance of funding persons with disabilities to lead SRHR advocacy. Disability-led activism is critical for amplifying SRHR demands based on lived realities. Simone highlighted that activism led by persons with intellectual disabilities needs more attention and support from donors, particularly on issues of bodily autonomy, choice and consent.15 There are many obstacles persons with intellectual disabilities face in accessing advocacy spaces and in building stronger self-advocacy movements. This can be especially problematic around reproductive justice issues when parents and caregivers speak on their behalf. There is a great need for more funding for self-advocates to have conversations on SRHR and to determine their priorities themselves.

To do this effectively, funding must be allocated to support spaces and platforms for collective visioning by persons with disabilities to outline their SRHR advocacy priorities. These collective spaces are critical to those who have been structurally excluded, such as persons with learning disabilities, autistic persons, Deafblind persons and persons with multiple disabilities, so that they have a space to articulate their priorities. This is particularly important around reproductive rights advocacy where, historically, non-disabled people have spoken on their behalf and their opinions and perspectives have been sidelined or excluded.15

Linked to this, donors can play a critical role in building cross-movement connections and understanding on SRHR and disability. There have been some bridges built between SRHR actors and disability activists in Indonesia, for example, but Dwi Ariyani feels that the voices and perspectives of women with disabilities need to be better integrated and valued so that they are seen as equal partners within the SRHR movement.13 This also requires the SRHR movement to ensure these spaces are accessible. In Kenya, ableism persists in other rights spaces and one of the major barriers is lack of accessibility. Women with disabilities don’t have the SRHR awareness and empowerment their peers have in part because they haven’t been able to access these spaces.8 Simone believes that one way to tackle this is for donors to fund activists to think through SRHR and disability advocacy across movements and support disability activists to be able to engage more effectively on intersectional advocacy. Donors can play a critical role in intentionally funding at these intersections and purposefully supporting more cross-movement work and nuanced collaboration on SRHR and disability.

Towards more intersectional and transformative change

Donors must commit to supporting work that is intersectional and can bring movements together to help enable more transformative change. Donors have the power to support intersectional SRHR advocacy and dialogues that centre the leadership and wisdom of persons with disabilities. This intersectional work can give voice, decision-making power and agency to persons with disabilities to shape the global SRHR agenda. Funding that supports the nexus of SRHR and disability through cross-movement dialogues can help shape the human rights landscape so that activists in all sectors better understand that disability rights are reproductive rights and that the interconnected struggle for justice includes persons with disabilities.

To truly shift the funding towards disability justice, funders must also look inward to address disability representation within their own organisations and structures and recruit persons with disabilities as staff and appoint them to their boards. A more inclusive and accessible organisation with expertise in both disability rights and SRHR will be better positioned to understand how disability justice and reproductive justice are interconnected.
