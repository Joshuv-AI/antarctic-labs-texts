Understanding delivery type as a mediator in the relationship between insurance type and permanent contraception fulfillment: a mixed-methods multi-site study
Authors: Brooke W. Bullington, Kristen A. Berg, Suzanna Larkin, Kari White, Margaret Boozer, Tania Serna, Emily S. Miller, Jennifer L. Bailit, Kavita Shah Arora
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMany patients who desire postpartum permanent contraception cannot obtain it, with barriers linked to insurance type – particularly Medicaid’s 30-day consent policy – and delivery type (cesarean vs. vaginal). This study uses a mixed-methods approach to examine whether delivery type mediates the relationship between insurance type and permanent contraception fulfillment.MethodsWe conducted a concurrent mixed-methods analysis of data collected from three US hospitals. We analyzed quantitative data from 2,794 patients (2018–2019) using mediation analysis to assess the indirect effect of insurance type on contraception fulfillment via delivery type. We also thematically analyzed qualitative data from semi-structured interviews with 67 patients and 54 obstetrician-gynecologists (2022–2023) to explore how delivery type influences contraceptive decision-making and service provision.ResultsMediation analysis showed a significant indirect effect of insurance type on contraception fulfillment through delivery type (average causal mediation effect: -0.06; 95% confidence interval [CI]: -0.10, -0.04). Medicaid patients had higher odds of delivering vaginally (odds ratio [OR]:1.78; 95% CI: 1.44, 2.21), and vaginal delivery was strongly associated with reduced fulfillment (OR: 0.05; 95% CI: 0.04, 0.07).Qualitative findings reinforced these results. Patients and providers described how Medicaid’s consent policy delayed access, while cesarean delivery made permanent contraception easier. Providers more often discussed permanent contraception with patients already undergoing a cesarean delivery, and patients who delivered vaginally faced logistical challenges with follow-up care.DiscussionDelivery type is an important mediator in the relationship between insurance type and postpartum permanent contraception fulfillment. Addressing policy restrictions, provider counseling disparities, and institutional constraints is critical to ensuring equitable contraceptive access.

Introduction

Female permanent contraception (formerly known as sterilization) is one of the most common contraceptive methods among reproductive-aged women in the United States (US) [1]. Permanent contraception procedures are commonly performed after childbirth, with approximately half of all procedures occurring during the postpartum period. Postpartum permanent contraception procedures follow an estimated 8–9% of live births in the US, but prior studies show that many more delivering patients desire the procedure but are unable to obtain it [2–5]. Those with unfulfilled permanent contraception requests are more likely to have a subsequent pregnancy in the following year, resulting in short interpregnancy intervals, which have been associated with poor maternal and neonatal outcomes [6, 7].

Prior research has explored barriers to postpartum permanent contraception fulfillment, with considerable focus on the role of insurance type [4, 8–12]. This focus is a result of the history of pervasive forced and coerced sterilization in the 20th century that targeted people of color, low-income people, and people with disabilities [3]. This history led to a federally mandated sterilization consent policy in 1978 that requires patients with Medicaid insurance to sign a consent form and undergo a 30-day waiting period prior to receiving permanent contraception. The federal consent form and waiting period do not apply to patients with private insurance. Studies have documented that patients with Medicaid are less likely to receive a desired postpartum permanent contraception procedure compared to those with private insurance and many hypothesize this is an unintended consequence of the 1978 legislation [4, 8–10].

Research has also explored the role that delivery type – whether a patient has a vaginal or cesarean delivery – plays in fulfillment of permanent contraception [11, 13]. In studies exploring the relationship between insurance type and permanent contraception fulfillment, delivery type is one of the strongest predictors of fulfillment; patients with cesarean deliveries are more likely to undergo the permanent contraception procedure compared to those who have a vaginal delivery [12, 14]. This is likely because cesarean deliveries remove some barriers to permanent contraception fulfillment, including scheduling as well as healthcare professional and operating room availability [14]. Additionally, performing the procedure during a cesarean delivery provides technical ease for clinicians and convenience for patients, and is therefore considered “ideal,” according to the American College of Obstetrics and Gynecology [15].

Previous literature has documented a relationship between insurance type and route of delivery, finding that those with private insurance are more likely to undergo cesarean deliveries compared to those with Medicaid [16, 17], likely due to payment and reimbursement mechanisms. Among the specific subsample of patients who desire permanent contraception, this relationship may be particularly strong [4], possibly because cesarean delivery offers a convenient opportunity to perform the procedure. Insurance therefore may influence route of delivery, both in the general population and among those with desire for permanent contraception [4, 12]. Understanding whether route of delivery mediates the relationship between insurance type and permanent contraception fulfillment can help reveal when and how patients encounter barriers to fulfillment, and inform targeted improvements to ensure that people can receive their desired contraceptive method. Yet, prior research has not conceptualized delivery type as a mediator in the relationship between insurance type and permanent contraception fulfillment. A mediator is an intermediate variable between an exposure (in this case, insurance type) and an outcome (fulfillment of permanent contraception) that is influenced by the exposure and the outcome [18].

In this study, we evaluate whether delivery type functions as a mediator in the link between insurance type and permanent contraception fulfillment and examine this relationship through a mixed-methods, multi-site study design. Our mixed-methods approach allows us to quantify the extent that route of delivery explains the relationship between insurance type and permanent contraception fulfillment and to explore how route of delivery influences clinicians’ and patients’ permanent contraception service delivery and decision-making. We hypothesized that route of delivery would partially mediate the relationship between insurance type and permanent contraception fulfillment and that qualitative data would corroborate pathways along the mediation model.

Methods

We employed a concurrent, equal status mixed-methods design, where quantitative and qualitative data held equivalent importance (QUANT + QUAL) and were integrated during analysis and interpretation [19]. All data were collected from patients and their delivering obstetrician-gynecologist (OBGYN) at one of four hospitals across the US (University of California San Francisco in San Francisco, California; Northwestern Memorial Hospital in Chicago, Illinois; MetroHealth Medical Center in Cleveland, Ohio; or University of Alabama at Birmingham in Birmingham, Alabama). All patients included had a documented desire for permanent contraception in their medical chart during their delivery hospitalization. For quantitative and qualitative analyses, we excluded patients from the University of California San Francisco because California state policy requires all patients, regardless of insurance type, to complete a sterilization consent form with an associated waiting period.

Quantitative sample and data

Data source

Quantitative data were collected during 2018 and 2019 through a retrospective review of the electronic medical record at each of the four aforementioned hospitals. Patients who delivered at or beyond 20 weeks’ gestation and had documentation regarding desire for postpartum permanent contraception during their inpatient hospitalization were eligible for inclusion. We abstracted demographic and clinical information, including documentation of contraceptive plan and fulfillment of permanent contraception, from linked inpatient and outpatient electronic medical records. At each of the four hospitals, one trained research assistant reviewed notes and abstracted relevant information. 1% of study records were audited by the site’s principal investigator to ensure accuracy. Research assistants across sites communicated weekly to ensure consistency. Further methodological details have been previously published [4].

Quantitative measures

Our primary outcome was fulfillment of postpartum permanent contraception before hospital discharge, which was operationalized as a binary variable (fulfilled vs. unfulfilled). Our primary exposure was insurance type, which was abstracted from billing records and operationalized as a binary variable (Medicaid vs. private insurance). Patients with Medicaid and another insurance plan were categorized as having Medicaid insurance, given that the federally mandated sterilization consent policy applies to secondary insurance holders. Route of delivery, our mediator of interest, was abstracted from medical charts and operationalized as a binary variable (cesarean vs. vaginal delivery).

Covariates of interest included maternal age at delivery (continuous; in years), parity prior to delivery (binary; less than two vs. two or more), weeks of gestation at delivery (continuous), marital status (binary; married vs. unmarried), adequacy of prenatal care based on the Kotelchuck Index (binary; adequate vs. inadequate), race and ethnicity (categorical; non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic white, Hispanic, and other/unknown) and body mass index (BMI; continuous). We chose these covariates because they are associated with delivery type and permanent contraception fulfillment [14, 20].

Quantitative analysis

We conducted a mediation analysis to examine the direct and indirect effects of insurance type on fulfillment of permanent contraception within one year postpartum, with delivery type as the mediator. Using the R ‘mediation’ package, we estimated the average causal mediation effect, average direct effect, and total effect of insurance type on permanent contraception [21]. We employed nonparametric bootstrapping to compute 95% confidence intervals with 10,000 simulations to assess the robustness of the indirect pathway. Proportion mediated was calculated to assess the relative contribution of delivery type in explaining the association between insurance type and permanent contraception fulfillment. We utilized logistic regression for both the mediator and outcome models due to the binary nature of the variables. The mediator model (predicting delivery type) adjusted for maternal age at delivery, parity prior to delivery, weeks of gestation at delivery, marital status, adequacy of prenatal care, and BMI. The outcome model (predicting fulfillment of postpartum permanent contraception) adjusted for the same set of covariates, as well as delivery type.

Qualitative sample and data

Data source

Qualitative data were collected during 2022 and 2023 from a separate sample. The same eligibility criteria were used as for the quantitative analytic sample though, in addition, participants in the qualitative study were required to speak either English or Spanish for participation. We recruited eligible patients, including those who did and did not receive postpartum permanent contraception, to participate in in-depth, phenomenologically informed semi-structured interviews. We interviewed patients six-to-ten weeks postpartum, employing the “neutral but interested” phenomenological approach [22], balancing the minimization of recall bias with allowing time for patients to have undergone an interval postpartum permanent contraception procedure following their hospital discharge. We purposively sampled participants based on insurance type, fulfillment of permanent contraception, parity, and route of delivery to enhance inclusivity of experiences across all sites.

After an eligible patient agreed to participate, we invited their delivering OBGYN to participate in a separate phenomenologically informed semi-structured interview. Though we acknowledge that a multi-disciplinary team delivers pregnancy care, often including certified nurse midwives, we chose to interview OBGYNs given their central role in decision-making and procedural access. If an OBGYN delivered more than one included patient, they were interviewed about each of their included patients. For five included patients, their delivering OBGYN could not be reached; these five patients are still included in the qualitative analysis.

Using the same phenomenological approach as with patient interviews, we developed in-depth interview guides to explore patients’ experiences seeking postpartum permanent contraception and OBGYNs’ perspectives on navigating the process with their patients. Interview guides were initially developed based on existing literature [23–26] and later refined after conducting six pilot interviews at each site (three with patients, three with OBGYNs).

We contacted eligible patients and OBGYNs by phone and email and re-contacted them up to three times if they did not respond. Four trained female research assistants (2 white, 1 Black, 1 Asian) with graduate-level training in qualitative research conducted interviews on Zoom. All interviews were audio recorded and transcribed for analysis, and all participants were compensated with a $100 gift card for their participation.

We aimed to achieve thematic saturation across all sites, which was determined based on an ongoing review of interview transcripts and thematic summaries as they were conducted, in addition to team discussions.

Qualitative analysis

We employed a two-phase codebook thematic analysis, utilizing both deductive and inductive approaches [27]. First, we developed a comprehensive initial codebook using relevant literature and our interview guides. Four trained research assistants (MT, JH, SM, DD) and one co-investigator first used the initial codebook to code a common set of transcripts to ensure consistency. Then, after demonstrating reliable application of the codebook, the team coded all remaining transcripts. Throughout this process, the team met weekly to compare coding, discuss questions, and resolve discrepancies. A random sample of 10% of transcripts were double-coded to ensure consistency.

Second, for the current study’s specific focus on delivery type as a key mediator of how patients’ insurance type may matter to their fulfillment of permanent postpartum contraception, we conducted a targeted secondary qualitative analysis. Using the indexed data from the team’s initial coding, authors BB and KB inductively analyzed the coded content to specifically examine how patients’ and providers’ perspectives aligned with, and helped explain, the hypothesized mediation pathways. For example, when providers discussed how cesarean deliveries facilitated easier permanent contraception procedures, we created a code such as ‘delivery type facilitates PC.’ To comprehensively document potential pathways of effect, we developed additional codes to capture variables that may potentially influence the fulfillment of permanent contraception (e.g., hospital staff availability), as well as variables that may potentially modify those relationships (e.g., BMI conditioning the relationship between delivery type and permanent contraception). All transcripts, during both phases of analysis, were coded using NVivo software.

Mixed-methods analysis

We integrated quantitative and qualitative strands to expand understanding of how and why insurance type may influence permanent contraception fulfillment, with particular attention to the mediating role of delivery type. This integration occurred following the completion of separate quantitative and qualitative analyses, during which we synthesized findings to generate comprehensive insights about the pathways and mechanisms influencing postpartum permanent contraception. Building on our previous work identifying route of delivery as crucial for permanent contraception fulfillment [4], we utilized a concurrent mixed-methods design guided by a directed acyclic graph (DAG; see Fig. 1). This conceptual model specified hypothesized pathways between insurance type, route of delivery, and permanent contraception fulfillment. The DAG informed both our quantitative mediation analysis and our qualitative investigation. For the quantitative component, we estimated the direct and indirect effects between focal variables. Concurrently, we analyzed qualitative data to examine whether and how patients and providers discussed these same pathways. We then integrated findings using the DAG as an organizing framework, with quantitative results establishing the magnitude and significance of associations while qualitative findings illuminated the mechanisms underlying those relationships.

Quantitative results

Mediation analysis showed a statistically significant indirect effect of insurance type on fulfillment of postpartum permanent contraception through delivery type (average causal mediation effect = −0.06, 95% CI [−0.10, −0.04], p <.001). Specifically, compared to patients with private insurance, patients with Medicaid insurance had a significantly higher likelihood of delivering vaginally which, in turn, was associated with a significantly lower likelihood of achieving permanent contraception. The direct effect of insurance type on fulfillment of permanent contraception was also significant (average direct effect = −0.08, 95% CI [−0.12, −0.04], p <.001), in that patients with Medicaid, compared to private, insurance experienced a significantly lower likelihood of achieving permanent contraception. The total effect of insurance type on permanent contraception fulfillment was − 0.14 (95% CI [−0.20, −0.10], p <.001). The average proportion mediated was 0.45 (95% CI [0.32, 0.68]), indicating that approximately 45% of the association between insurance type and permanent contraception fulfillment was explained by delivery type. Qualitative findings

The qualitative data from patients and OBGYNs corroborated two key pathways: the relationship between insurance type and permanent contraception fulfillment and the relationship between route of delivery and permanent contraception fulfillment. Our qualitative analysis also identified other determinants of delivery type and permanent contraception fulfillment, and potential moderators of the relationship between delivery type and fulfillment, which we display in Fig. 1.

Insurance type and permanent contraception fulfillment

During interviews, both patients and OBGYNs discussed how insurance type – and the federally mandated Medicaid sterilization policy in particular – directly impacted whether patients could have their desired permanent contraception procedure fulfilled.

One clinician explained how the 30-day waiting period for Medicaid may impact whether a patient can get permanent contraception. They said, “For patients where suddenly…they’re inside of that 30-day window…I do tell them that, ‘Unfortunately, because of this waiting period, we may not be able to perform [the permanent contraception procedure.]’…I don’t always…tell them that if they have private insurance they wouldn’t need this form because I don’t think pointing that out to them is going to be helpful in any way.”

Multiple patients also explained how the Medicaid policy impacted their permanent contraception fulfillment. One patient who had not completed the 30-day waiting period before labor said, “Then, at the hospital, since I ended up going—I went into labor, she told me that I couldn’t get [permanent contraception]. Medicaid wouldn’t pay for it because it’ll have to be on file for 30 days. It was just filled out like two weeks before.” Another patient described a similar story: “Then [the doctor was] like, ‘Okay, but then you signed the form right now, so you don’t have the time. There’s not enough time to do the sterilization.’”.

Our qualitative analysis therefore identifies that both patients and clinicians perceive insurance type, and more specifically the Medicaid sterilization policy, to influence whether patients can receive their desired postpartum permanent contraception procedure.

Route of delivery and permanent contraception fulfillment

Both patients and OBGYNs discussed how route of delivery impacted permanent contraception decision-making and fulfillment.

Many clinicians discussed the ease of performing the permanent contraception procedure during a cesarean delivery. When discussing a specific patient, one clinician reflected, “With [this patient], it was easy. She’s got a C-section. She’s got an open belly. It’s easy to take the tubes out.”

Some clinicians also brought up that performing a tubal ligation during a cesarean does not add much risk compared to doing the procedure at another time. One said, “The tubes are right there. It doesn’t add much to the operating time. It doesn’t add much risk, as opposed to doing it later when it would add another anesthesia and operative risk.”

Finally, some clinicians discussed how route of delivery influences their counseling around permanent contraception. In particular, clinicians noted that they were more likely to discuss permanent contraception if a patient was already having a cesarean delivery. A clinician asserted, “I very rarely bring [permanent contraception] up, except for like, ‘Hey, if you’d have to have a C-section, would you want your tubes tied?”

Patients also explained how their route of delivery impacted their permanent contraception decision-making. Similar to providers, patients noted that having the procedure at the same time as their cesarean made the process easier. One patient said, “’cause since they’re already cutting, I’m gonna go under the knife, so I was like, ‘I might as well if I’m gonna do it ’cause I don’t wanna do it twice.’”.

Other patients discussed how they did not want to experience additional pain after a vaginal delivery. Speaking of her vaginal delivery, one patient explained her motivation for seeking permanent contraception, indicating her desire to avoid another painful delivery experience: “I was supposed to get [an epidural]. […] [My baby] was coming too fast, so it ended up being natural. I felt all of that pain, and I definitely didn’t want to feel anything like that again.”

A few patients who underwent cesarean deliveries discussed how they would not have received the permanent contraception procedure if they had undergone vaginal delivery. One patient said, “If I would have had to [give birth] vaginally and had to go back for the [permanent contraception] procedure, I don’t think I would have done it. […] One of the factors why I did it is because of the way it was gonna be done right there when I had the C-section. I wouldn’t have had to return to get it done.”

Factors conditioning the link between route of delivery and permanent contraception fulfillment

While both clinicians and patients described how route of delivery influenced their permanent contraception fulfillment, their reflections sometimes included conditional language revealing how various factors modify that relationship. Participants described moderating factors at multiple levels, from individual patient characteristics to institutional constrains. For example, clinicians emphasized how patient clinical factors could affect the feasibility of performing permanent contraception procedures, particularly after vaginal deliveries. As one clinician explained, “Things like maternal weight or multiple prior C-sections, so they’re probably not the greatest candidates for the procedure after a [vaginal] delivery.” Hospital resource constraints were additionally impactful, as another clinician noted: “Another consideration with after a vaginal delivery for timing of a tubal ligation is the availability of the surgeon and anesthesia and being able to time that, because our operating rooms are also accommodating urgent and more time-sensitive procedures that have to happen for maternal or fetal safety.”

Patient responses also revealed additional moderating factors. For example, some patients explicitly made contingency plans based on their delivery type and their partner’s willingness to undergo permanent contraception (or vasectomy). As one patient described, “My plan was that if I will go through cesarean section, please have my tubes tied. Otherwise, if I will deliver my baby vaginally, then my husband will go through vasectomy.” Beyond partner involvement, broader life circumstances could also determine permanent contraception fulfillment. One patient explained how transportation barriers blocked her access to an interval procedure: “Right after my son was delivered, they came in and—asked me if I wanted to do it right after, but my levels were low, so the next day, they came in and was like, ‘We would like you to come back in six weeks for the sterilization.’ I wasn’t able to make that because of our car being broke down.”

Mixed methods results

Meta-inferences from the integration of quantitative and qualitative findings reveal key insights about how delivery type mediates the relationship between insurance type and permanent postpartum contraception fulfillment. First, delivery type emerged as an essential mechanism, accounting for approximately 45% of the effect of having Medicaid insurance on permanent contraception fulfillment, despite a modest overall effect size (average causal mediation effect = −0.06, p <.001). This quantitative finding comports with the qualitative data in that both patients and providers emphasized the importance of delivery type in postpartum permanent contraception decision-making and access, but did not discuss any direct relationship between insurance type and delivery type. This convergence suggests that while the mediation effect through delivery type is small in absolute terms, it captures a crucial pathway in the lived experiences of patients and providers.

Second, qualitative data illuminated specific mechanisms underlying two of the three pathways in our mediation model. Insurance type primarily affected permanent postpartum contraception through Medicaid’s mandatory 30-day waiting period policy, while delivery type influenced permanent contraception fulfillment through both clinical and patient-centered factors. Clinicians reported being more apt to counsel patients about permanent postpartum contraception if patients were expected to undergo cesarean delivery due to the procedure’s relative ease and minimal additional risk. Patients similarly expressed strong preferences for combining permanent postpartum contraception with cesarean delivery to avoid multiple surgeries, with some explicitly stating they would not have pursued the procedure if they had delivered vaginally. Third, the qualitative data also reveal important moderating factors not specified in the quantitative analysis, including hospital-level constraints (e.g., operating room availability), patient clinical factors (e.g., BMI, comorbidities), and social circumstances (e.g., transportation access, partner preferences regarding permanent contraception). Finally, qualitative interviews notably did not elicit any direct discussion of how insurance type might influence delivery type, suggesting this pathway may operate through mechanisms not readily apparent to patients or providers.

Discussion

Using a mixed-methods study design, we quantitatively and qualitatively explored delivery type as a mediator between insurance type and postpartum permanent contraception fulfillment. Our quantitative findings reveal that delivery type is an important mediator in the relationship between insurance type and permanent contraception fulfillment. Compared to patients with private insurance, those with Medicaid insurance had a higher likelihood of delivering vaginally which, in turn, was associated with a lower likelihood of achieving desired permanent contraception. Our qualitative findings highlighted key mechanisms in pathways between role of delivery type and permanent contraception fulfillment. These included the role of the Medicaid sterilization consent policy and the impact of delivery type on patient and clinician decision-making and service delivery. This mixed-methods approach reveals not only the significance of route of delivery as a mediator, but also how it may shape access to permanent contraception.

Our quantitative findings suggest that insurance type has a dual effect on postpartum permanent contraception: first, insurance (via the Medicaid sterilization consent policy) is indirectly associated with permanent contraception fulfillment via delivery type, and, second, is directly associated with permanent contraception fulfillment even after accounting for delivery type. While existing studies have documented how the Medicaid sterilization consent policy creates barriers to access [4, 8–10], our findings suggest one way in which this policy may operate in practice. In line with research showing that patients with private insurance have higher cesarean rates than those with Medicaid [16, 17], our data suggest that this pattern holds among patients seeking permanent contraception [13]. We find that privately insured patients desiring permanent contraception are more likely to undergo cesarean deliveries, possibly because providers recognize the procedural advantages of combining permanent contraception with cesarean delivery. This suggests a complex relationship where insurance status may affect both delivery decisions and permanent contraception access, potentially creating compounded disadvantages for Medicaid patients seeking permanent contraception.

Prior studies have quantitatively shown higher rates of permanent contraception fulfillment among those with cesarean compared to vaginal deliveries [4, 12]; our work qualitatively reveals the multiple pathways through which delivery type operates, including provider counseling, patient preferences, and institutional constraints, and how these pathways may interact. For example, clinicians in our study discussed how their counseling around permanent contraception is shaped by expectations around a patient’s delivery type. Some clinicians explicitly stated that they more often counsel about permanent contraception if a patient is already planning a cesarean delivery. The technical ease of performing the permanent contraception procedure therefore may, in addition to reducing institutional barriers to the procedure like operating room availability, influence whether a patient is presented with the option of undergoing the procedure altogether [13].

While prior research has emphasized clinical and institutional factors that render cesarean deliveries more conducive to permanent contraception [14, 15], our findings highlight how patients’ lived experiences also shape this relationship. Patients’ narratives revealed how practical constraints of recovery, transportation, and partner support made combining permanent contraception with cesarean delivery not just clinically expedient, but personally essential. Some patients explicitly stated they would forgo sterilization entirely if it required a separate procedure after vaginal delivery, pointing to how their complex social circumstances—not just medical considerations—influence reproductive healthcare access. Although early access to outpatient care is often considered essential given the Medicaid sterilization waiting period, our finding that adequacy of prenatal care was not associated with permanent contraception fulfillment underscores the complexity of social determinants of health and individual decision-making in reproductive health outcomes. Thus, the link between delivery type and permanent contraception access must be understood through both clinical and patient-centered lenses, and patient preferences should be elicited and respected throughout the permanent contraception service delivery process.

Our data did not reflect any direct discussion from clinicians or patients on the relationship between insurance type and delivery type. Consistent with our phenomenologically informed approach that prioritized participants’ own framings of their experiences, we did not explicitly probe this relationship in ways that might have elicited in more direct reflection. We theorize that this absence of discussion reflects that this pathway may operate through mechanisms not directly apparent to patients, or not at the forefront of clinicians’ daily practice or conscious decision-making, as these patterns may emerge from systemic factors in care delivery rather than explicit clinical decisions. Patients often only know about their own experiences navigating their delivery care and likely do not conceptualize the counterfactual of what would have happened if they, for example, had a different type of insurance. This study’s convergence of quantitative evidence for this pathway’s existence with qualitative evidence for this pathway’s invisibility to participants offers crucial insight to the structural versus experiential nature of insurance-related barriers to permanent contraception access. Future research probing this specific pathway may further clarify delivery type as a mediator between insurance type and fulfillment.

Our study’s findings should be considered in light of strengths and limitations. A key strength is our comprehensive methodological approach, combining data from multiple healthcare sites across regions to provide broader insights into permanent contraception access in U.S. healthcare settings. The mixed-methods design enabled us to both measure the role of delivery type as a mediator and understand nuances of the underlying mechanisms driving this relationship. Additionally, our integration of clinical records with patient narratives offers fuller insight into how permanent contraception decisions and outcomes unfold in practice. However, certain limitations affect the interpretation of our results. Given the study’s retrospective nature, data were lacking on patients who may have received permanent contraception at other institutions or whose partners chose permanent contraception instead. Although we conducted qualitative interviews relatively soon after delivery (6–10 weeks postpartum) to optimize recall, participants’ memories of their experiences may still be imperfect. Furthermore, despite our intention to include diverse perspectives, our interview sample included only English-speaking participants, potentially missing important insights from Spanish-speaking patients and those from other cultural backgrounds.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that route of delivery serves as a mediator between insurance type and postpartum permanent contraception fulfillment. Through mixed-methods analysis, we identified specific mechanisms by which delivery type shapes both clinical practice and patient decision-making. These findings have important implications for research, clinical care, and policy; researchers should consider route of delivery as a mediator rather than a confounder when examining insurance-based disparities in permanent contraception access. Clinicians should be cognizant of how route of delivery influences their counseling practices, consider standardized counseling guides, advocate for adequate operating room access for postpartum permanent contraception, and understand its impact on patient preferences and access. Finally, policymakers should consider how the interaction between insurance, delivery type, and institutional constraints creates barriers to reproductive healthcare access, especially when considering revision to the Medicaid sterilization policy. Addressing these intersecting factors is essential for improving equitable access to postpartum permanent contraception.
