Decreased amyloid-related structure–function coupling in preclinical Alzheimer’s disease

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStructural pathways of the brain facilitate functional communication, and their disruption in preclinical Alzheimer’s disease may reflect network vulnerability and compensatory brain maintenance. However, it remains unclear how early amyloid-β affects structure-function alignment, whether effects are explained by functional network organisation, how they relate to cognition, and which biological processes contribute to their development.MethodsWe included 460 older adults without dementia from AMYPAD-PNHS with functional MRI, diffusion MRI, and amyloid-β PET. Structure-function coupling was quantified using the structural-decoupling index (SDI) at global, sub-network, and regional scales. Linear models investigated the effect of amyloid-β burden on SDI. Mediation analyses evaluated whether functional graph topology explained amyloid-associated SDI effects and whether SDI mediated the relationship between amyloid-β burden and cognition. Regional gene expression data were integrated to assess transcriptomic determinants of amyloid-related structure-function coupling.ResultsAmyloid-positive individuals exhibit higher global SDI, driven by visual cortices. Mediation analyses demonstrate that amyloid-related SDI alterations are explained by reductions in local clustering, indicating less segregated processing. Despite higher SDI in amyloid-positive individuals, elevated SDI in visual regions mitigates the negative effect of amyloid-β burden on cognition. Amyloid-related SDI changes correlate with genes associated with amyloid-β metabolism, microglial activation, and synaptic remodelling.ConclusionsEarly amyloid-β pathology is associated with decoupling of brain structure and function, primarily in visual cortices, mediated by network reconfiguration and shaped by regional molecular architecture. These findings suggest that lower structure-function coupling may represent a compensatory mechanism in preclinical Alzheimer’s disease and highlight SDI as a biomarker for stratification and monitoring in prevention trials.

Introduction

Amyloid-beta (Aβ) pathology, detectable up to 20 years before Alzheimer’s disease symptom onset1,2, has been linked to synaptic dysfunction3,4, neuronal hyperactivity5,6, white matter disruption7,8. These early alterations affect large-scale brain network organisation and communication9–11, suggesting that Aβ may alter brain connectivity from the earliest stages of the disease. With 10-44% of cognitively normal older adults already showing measurable Aβ deposition12, and recent advances in disease-modifying anti-Aβ therapies demonstrating the greatest clinical efficacy when initiated in early stages, there is a need to decipher Aβ-related neurobiological alterations in asymptomatic individuals13,14. Brain network dysfunction is recognised in Alzheimer’s disease as a downstream consequence of pathology15,16 and as a driver of disease progression17,18. Because of this, Alzheimer’s disease has been conceptualised as a disorder of large-scale brain networks19,20. Yet, prior studies have largely examined brain structural and functional alterations in isolation21,22, overlooking that the human brain is inherently organised as a hierarchical network, whose functional activity is constrained by its underlying structure23,24. Investigating how early Aβ accumulation perturbs this structure–function relationship before overt cognitive decline may yield novel insights into mechanisms of disease progression and inform the development of biomarkers and therapeutic targets aimed at mitigating the effects of early Aβ pathology on the brain and cognition.

Structure–function coupling (SFC) reflects the degree to which functional communication patterns follow anatomical connections, and its disruption may signal early network vulnerability or compensatory brain maintenance25. SFC is region-specific, closely tracking the unimodal to transmodal principal gradient of functional cortical organisation26,27. The distribution of SFC follows the hierarchical and cytoarchitectural principles of the human brain28, shaped by differences in microstructural properties29, underlying myelination30 and excitation-inhibition balance31. Structure and function in primary sensory and motor regions are typically closely aligned or coupled, enabling efficient information transmission, while transmodal regions like the default mode network (DMN) tend to be more liberal or decoupled23,32. The spatial pattern seems to be a result of the rapid evolutionary expansion of the transmodal cortices33, allowing complex cognitive flexibility and adaptation34,35.

A reduction in SFC has been associated with ageing36–38, and there is emerging evidence of an imbalance of this typical hierarchical organisation in various neurological conditions39–44, including Alzheimer’s disease45–50. However, most previous studies focused on late-stage clinical Alzheimer’s disease and lacked biomarker integration, and, in addition, showed mixed results. Studies looking at global SFC in cognitively impaired individuals generally report an Aβ-dependent stage-wise increase in overall SFC from mild cognitive impairment to Alzheimer’s disease46,51. Results from regional exploration suggest an increase in SFC of the higher-order regions, impacting brain communication46. A recent study also showed an SFC increase in Alzheimer’s disease-related medial temporal regions accompanied by an SFC decrease in visual regions in response to Aβ pathology52, possibly reflecting subnetwork-specific dysfunction or an adaptive brain maintenance mechanism (i.e., processes supporting the preservation of cognitive function despite accumulating pathology)53. These studies suggest a dynamic trend, and studying this relationship could help identify the time point when individuals switch from resilience to impairment.

In this study, we investigate how Aβ burden relates to SFC across global, sub-network, and regional scales in a large cohort of older adults without dementia using multimodal imaging data. We examine which functional network changes drive Aβ-related changes in the structure-function relationship by testing whether specific graph-theoretical features mediate the association between Aβ burden and regional SFC. We then assess whether these SFC alterations mediate the impact of Aβ on cognitive function and whether regional vulnerability to Aβ-related SFC changes is shaped by underlying gene-expression patterns. We demonstrate that higher Aβ burden is associated with decreased SFC, particularly in visual cortices, driven by reduced network segregation. This decoupling mitigates the negative association between Aβ and cognition, suggesting a potential compensatory mechanism. Regional effects align with molecular signatures related to amyloid metabolism, microglial activation, and synaptic remodelling.

Materials and Methods

Study data

This study utilised multimodal neuroimaging and clinical data from the European Amyloid Imaging to Prevent Alzheimer’s Disease (AMYPAD, https://amypad.eu; EudraCT 2018-002277-22) Prognostic and Natural History Study (PNHS) of the AMYPAD consortium, which was initiated in 2016 as part of the Innovative Medicine Initiative-ADDI54–56. The AMYPAD-PNHS is a multicentre European study designed to investigate early Alzheimer’s disease-related pathology using Aβ-PET imaging and multimodal biomarker data. Participants were recruited through ten parent cohorts across Europe that contributed harmonised clinical, neuroimaging, and biomarker data. Inclusion criteria were as follows: the participant (1) should not have dementia (i.e., Clinical Dementia Rating global score (CDR) < 1), (2) be older than 50 years of age, (3) be able to undergo MRI and PET acquisition, and (4) be active or previously enroled in a sponsor-approved parent cohort. The AMYPAD PNHS study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Review Committee (Medisch Ethische Toetsingscommissie; METC) of Amsterdam UMC under application number 2018.357. In addition, the parent cohorts contributing data to the present analyses obtained approval from their respective local ethics committees or institutional review boards (Comité de protection des personnes Sud-Méditerranée II; the Comité de Ética de la Investigación con medicamentos del Parc de Salut MAR). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1975, revised 2008), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All analyses were performed using de-identified data obtained through the AMYPAD consortium under existing ethical approvals, and no additional local IRB approval was required for the present secondary analyses. The dataset was obtained from the AMYPAD PNHS dataset version v20230657.

For the present analyses, we included 460 participants with available diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI), and Aβ PET imaging data from the same session, and APOE carriership status. MRI acquisition protocols are detailed elsewhere58, with the specific parameters of fMRI data used in this study summarised in Table S1. APOE genotyping was performed according to each cohort’s established laboratory procedures, and APOE-ε4 carriers were defined as participants with at least ε4 allele. Cognitive outcomes were selected according to cross-cohort availability and conceptual comparability of neuropsychological assessments across participating AMYPAD cohorts. Given its early vulnerability in pathological ageing and its consistent availability across cohorts, we focused on the memory domain. Memory performance was assessed using measures of immediate and delayed verbal recall and visual memory. Individual test scores were standardised within each parent cohort and combined into composite z-scores. Because the present analysis combines participants from multiple cohorts with different underlying reference distributions, the resulting pooled sample does not necessarily have a mean of zero for all composite scores. Additional details on cognitive assessments and harmonisation procedures are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Imaging data acquisition & processing

Resting-state fMRI preprocessing and functional connectome computation

Rs-fMRI images were pre-processed using fMRIPrep v23.0.159. Briefly, the pipeline included estimation of a BOLD reference image, head-motion parameter extraction, and slice-timing correction where applicable. Distortion correction was carried out using a fieldmap-less approach based on nonlinear registration of the BOLD reference to the participant’s T1-weighted image using symmetric normalisation in advanced normalisation tools (ANTs)60. Functional images were then aligned to the T1w image using FreeSurfer’s boundary-based registration algorithm61 and subsequently normalised to ICBM 152 Nonlinear Asymmetrical (MNI152NLin2009cAsym) standard space using the same ANTs framework. Following fMRIPrep, we performed removal of initial non-steady-state volumes, spatial smoothing (4 mm FWHM), nuisance regression (6 head motion parameters, average cerebrospinal fluid, and white matter signal), linear detrending, and temporal band-pass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz), consistent with the original and other SDI studies23,49,50,52. Spatial smoothing was applied to be consistent with other standardised preprocessing pipeline outputs from fMRIPrep. To assess the impact of this step, SDI was additionally computed without spatial smoothing (Supplementary Fig. S6). The same smoothing parameters were applied across all cohorts to ensure consistent preprocessing in this multi-dataset analysis. The brain was parcellated into 100 parcels using the Schaefer atlas, which subdivides the cortex into spatially contiguous parcels organised across Yeo’s 7 canonical functional networks: visual (VIS), somatomotor (SMN), dorsal attention (DAN), salience/ventral attention (VAN), limbic (LIM), frontoparietal control (FPN), and DMN62. This atlas combines information on local gradients and global similarity of functional MRI signals to produce a biologically consistent and functionally coherent organisation of the cortex62. The 100-parcel resolution was selected as it provides an optimal trade-off between spatial detail and signal reliability, aligning well with the resolution of the current dataset (Supplementary Table S1). A coarser parcellation improves the stability of regional time series and derived network measures by increasing signal-to-noise ratio, while maintaining representation of large-scale functional networks, particularly in multi-cohort datasets with heterogeneous acquisition parameters. To assess robustness to parcellation resolution, the main analyses were repeated using a higher-resolution Schaefer-200 atlas. For each parcel, mean BOLD time series were extracted and used to compute pairwise Pearson correlations, yielding individual functional connectivity matrices for subsequent graph-theoretical analyses.

Diffusion-weighted imaging preprocessing and structural connectome computation

Diffusion-weighted MRI (DWI) data were preprocessed using QSIprep v0.1963. Initial steps included denoising and correction for B1 field inhomogeneities, implemented through MRtrix364. Motion and eddy-current artefacts were corrected using FSL tools65. Geometric distortions were addressed using a fieldmap-less approach based on symmetric normalisation in ANTs, which generated the deformation field required to unwarp the b0 image and enhance subsequent alignment between the DWI data and the corresponding T1-w scan. For downstream modelling, the DWI series were resampled into T1w space, where single-shell three-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution was performed to estimate fibre orientation distributions (FODs)66. FODs were then intensity-normalised across tissues. Whole-brain probabilistic tractography was conducted in MRtrix3 using the iFOD2 algorithm67, incorporating anatomically informed constraints derived from a hybrid surface–volume segmentation approach68. Tractography was performed within an anatomically constrained tractography framework with backtracking enabled and streamlines terminated at the grey matter–white matter interface. Streamlines were generated using dynamic seeding throughout the white matter, with a target of 10 million streamlines per participant, and standard MRtrix3 tractography parameters (default step size and curvature constraints; https://mrtrix.readthedocs.io/en/latest/reference/commands/tckgen.html), with streamline lengths constrained between 30 and 250 mm. Streamline filtering and weighting were performed using the SIFT2 algorithm69, which assigns a cross-sectional weighting coefficient to each streamline to better match the fibre density estimated from the diffusion model. Structural connectomes were generated in subject-specific native space. The Schaefer 100-region cortical parcellation, originally defined in MNI space, was transformed into each participant’s T1-weighted anatomical space using the nonlinear MNI152NLin2009cAsym to T1-weighted transformation produced during QSIPrep preprocessing and subsequently aligned to the diffusion data to assign streamline endpoints to parcels. Spatial transformations were performed using ANTs as implemented in QSIPrep. Parcel-to-parcel connectivity weights were then computed by summing the SIFT2-weighted streamlines connecting each pair of regions in the Schaefer 100-region parcellation. This resulted in an individual 100 × 100 weighted structural connectivity matrix for each participant.

Amyloid PET acquisition and quantification

The acquisition and standardisation procedures for Aβ-PET imaging in AMYPAD have been described in prior methodological publications54,70. In brief, scans were acquired 90–110 min following administration of either 185 MBq (±10%) of [¹⁸F]Flutemetamol or 350 MBq (±20%) of [¹⁸F]Florbetaben, using the tracer-specific standard protocol consisting of four 5-minute frames71,72. PET data were processed using a fully automated pipeline developed by IXICO plc. Individual frames were motion-corrected, averaged, and co-registered to each participant’s structural MRI. Standardised uptake value ratio (SUVr) images were generated using the whole cerebellum as the reference region in native space73. Global Aβ burden was quantified by converting SUVr values to Centiloid (CL) units based on the Global Alzheimer’s Association Interactive Network (GAAIN) standard target region74.

Structural-decoupling index (SDI) computation

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the Structural-Decoupling Index (SDI) was computed to quantify the alignment between regional fMRI activity and the underlying structural connectome, employing the graph signal processing pipeline described by Preti et al23. Briefly, a group-average SC was derived from cognitively unimpaired Aβ− individuals (CDR = 0), normalised, and converted into a graph Laplacian. As the structural basis is shared across participants, SDI, in our case, reflects the degree to which functional activity aligns with, or deviates from, a common structural reference architecture. SDI estimates using subject-specific SC were highly consistent with the group-average approach (r = 0.963; Supplementary Fig. S5), confirming robustness and justifying the use of a shared structural basis. Spectral decomposition yielded structural eigenmodes (spatial harmonics), reflecting the spatial patterns of structural connectivity across the cortex. After temporal z-scoring of time courses, each participant’s atlased fMRI pattern at each timepoint was projected onto the structural eigenmodes using the graph Fourier transform, which breaks down brain activity into components that reflect how signals spread across the structural connections. In other words, this produced a spatial frequency-domain representation of regional brain activity aligned with structural topology. Then, the cumulative power spectrum was used to determine the cut-off frequency at 50% of spectral power, separating structurally coupled (low-frequency) from decoupled (high-frequency) components. Regional SDI was defined as the ratio of the norm of decoupled to coupled components. Lower SDI values indicate greater SFC, and higher values reflect weaker SFC. For each participant, SDI values were calculated for each cortical region, and then summarised at three scales: global (mean across all cortical regions of interests; ROIs); network-level (mean within Yeo 7-networks); and regional (100 cortical ROIs defined by the Schaefer-100 atlas). As acquisition parameters can influence the frequency content of the functional signal (Supplementary Table S1), all statistical models accounted for differences in fMRI acquisition protocols by including repetition time (TR), number of fMRI timepoints (nTimepoints) as covariates, while cohort was included as a random intercept in linear mixed-effects (LMM) analyses to further account for site-level variability (Supplementary Fig. S1). Graph metrics and network topology analysis

To capture different dimensions of topological organisation, we calculated three node-level graph metrics derived from the subject-level functional connectivity matrices, using the Brain Connectivity Toolbox implemented in Python (BCTpy v0.6.0; https://pypi.org/project/bctpy)75. We used clustering coefficient as a proxy for functional segregation76, betweenness centrality as a measure of functional importance or hubness77, and participation coefficient for functional integration78. All graph metrics were computed on weighted, undirected functional connectomes thresholded at a fixed network density, retaining the top 20% of strongest absolute connections, to reduce the influence of weak and potentially spurious edges while ensuring comparability across subjects79,80. To ensure compatibility with graph metrics requiring non-negative weights, correlation values were converted to absolute values81, resulting in unsigned weighted networks. Diagonal elements were set to zero prior to analysis. For shortest-path–based measures such as betweenness centrality and efficiency, edge weights were converted to connection lengths using the inverse weight transformation implemented in the Brain Connectivity Toolbox. The participation coefficient was calculated relative to network communities detected using the Louvain modularity algorithm, applied separately to each subject’s functional connectome. Graph measures were z-scored across all subjects before entering statistical models to facilitate interpretation and comparison of effect sizes.

Statistics and reproducibility

A total of 460 older adults without dementia, with a mean age of 66.14 ± 7.43 years, were included in the study. Summary statistics for demographic, clinical, and cognitive variables were calculated for the overall cohort and stratified by Aβ status (Aβ– vs. Aβ + ; CL > 24.1)82. Group comparisons were performed using Wilcoxon rank sum tests for continuous variables and Pearson’s Chi-squared tests for categorical variables. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation or as count (percentage). All P-values were two-sided, with a significance threshold of P ≤ 0.05, and false-discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied where noted. Exact P-values are reported throughout where available (up to four decimal places), while extremely small values are reported using scientific notation where appropriate. Statistical analyses were carried out using R (v4.3.1), unless otherwise specified. As an additional sensitivity analysis, participants with high head motion (mean FD > 0.5 mm) were excluded, and all analyses were repeated (Supplementary materials).

Association of amyloid with structure–function coupling

To assess the relationship between Aβ burden and SFC, we performed a series of regression models using SDI at different scales as the outcome variable and global Aβ burden (CL) as the main predictor of interest. All models were adjusted for age, sex, APOE-ε4 carriership, global CDR score, fMRI TR, nTimepoints, and meanFD as covariates. To further account for potential cohort-level differences in acquisition protocols, models were estimated using LMMs with cohort included as a random intercept. Each model was estimated using the lmer function from the lme4 package in R (v4.3.1), and assumptions of linearity and normality of residuals were visually checked. For the network- and regional-level analyses, Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons, with associations considered significant at P ≤ 0.0583. As a sensitivity analysis, the network-level models were re-estimated in the subset of participants with CDR = 0 to verify that observed associations were not driven by individuals with subtle clinical impairment.

Network properties linking amyloid to structure–function coupling

To explore the network properties underlying Aβ-related changes in SDI, we performed mediation analyses using structural equation modelling, inspecting whether graph properties derived from the functional connectome described above mediate the association between Aβ burden and regional SDI. Based on the results of the regional Aβ–SDI regression analyses, we selected the nine cortical ROIs that exhibited the strongest positive associations between CL values and SDI across the cortex (hereafter referred to as Aβ–SDI associated regions). This data-driven exploratory approach allowed us to focus on regions most sensitive to early Aβ-related SFC changes. Mediation models were constructed to test whether a given graph metric (mediator, M) statistically accounted for the relationship between Aβ burden (independent variable, X) and SDI (dependent variable, Y). To assess their joint contribution to SDI, we constructed multi-mediator models with all three graph metrics included simultaneously as parallel mediators (M₁, M₂, M₃). The mediation percentage was calculated by dividing the indirect effect by the total effect and multiplying by 100. All mediation models were performed using the “lavaan” R package (v0.6-17). Models were fit separately for each Aβ–SDI associated regions, and indirect effects (ab path) were FDR-corrected across ROIs using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Covariates included age, sex, APOE-ε4 carriership, CDR, fMRI TR, nTimepoints, and meanFD, with cohort included as a random intercept. Robustness of the findings was tested across alternative density thresholds (10% and 30%).

Cognitive correlates of amyloid-related structure–function coupling

To evaluate the cognitive relevance of SDI in the context of Aβ pathology, we next tested whether SDI mediates the effect of Aβ burden on cognitive performance in Aβ+ individuals. To reduce dimensionality and capture the shared variance in Aβ-sensitive regions, we first applied Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) to the SDI values of the Aβ–SDI associated regions belonging to the VIS network. CFA was also performed using the “lavaan” package, specifying a single latent factor model. Model fit was assessed using standard indices, including the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardised Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). The resulting latent factor (LF) was used as a unidimensional mediator in subsequent mediation models. Available cognitive outcomes were included: global cognition (MMSE) and memory performance (immediate recall, delayed recall, and visual memory). These analyses were exploratory and aimed to provide an initial assessment of the behavioural relevance of SDI. Models were adjusted for the same covariates as above.

Transcriptomic determinants of amyloid-related structure–function coupling

To explore whether regional patterns of gene expression contribute to the spatial distribution of Aβ-related alterations in SFC, we used transcriptomic data from the publicly available Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA; http://human.brain-map.org). The AHBA provides microarray-based expression profiles from six neurotypical adult post-mortem brains (one female; age range 24–57 years, mean 42.5 ± 13.4 years). From this dataset, we extracted a set of genes previously implicated in Alzheimer’s disease and related neurodegenerative or cerebrovascular processes, informed by recent genome-wide association studies, including those focused on Alzheimer’s disease84, white matter hyperintensities (WMH)85, cerebrovascular disease86, limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy (LATE)87, posterior cortical atrophy (PCA)88. A detailed rationale and the full gene list are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Data 1). Gene-expression values for each of the 100 Schaefer-parcellated cortical regions were obtained using the abagen toolkit (https://github.com/rmarkello/abagen), applying standard preprocessing steps and default settings89. For each gene, we quantified the spatial correspondence between its regional expression profile and the cortical pattern of Aβ–SDI associations by correlating expression values with the ROI-wise β estimates derived from the linear models. Because spatial brain maps exhibit inherent autocorrelation, the statistical significance of these correlations was evaluated using a null-model framework that accounts for spatial dependencies in cortical data90. Finally, to gain insight into biological pathways linked to genes showing significant spatial associations with Aβ-related SFC patterns, we conducted gene over-representation analysis (ORA). Increased spatial gradient of SDI from sensory-motor to higher-order cognitive regions

The spatial distribution of SDI across canonical networks followed established large-scale cortical hierarchies in cognitively unimpaired Aβ− individuals (Fig. 2a). The VIS network showed the lowest SDI (strongest SFC), followed by DAN and SMN. Intermediate SDI values were observed in the FPN, LIM, and VAN, whereas the DMN exhibited the highest SDI (weakest SFC). Regionally, high coupling was localised to unimodal sensory and motor cortices, with progressively lower coupling observed in transmodal association regions, consistent with previously reported literature (Fig. 2b). These spatial patterns were largely consistent using a more fine-grained parcellation (Supplementary Fig. S11).

Amyloid burden is associated with lower structure-function coupling across scales

Aβ+ individuals exhibited higher global SDI compared to Aβ– participants (P = 0.013; Supplementary Fig. S2). Global SDI correlated with amyloid-β levels in the Aβ+ group (β = 0.056, P = 0.0231; Fig. 3a), while no relationship was observed in Aβ– individuals (β = 0.022, P = 0.5298). These findings suggest that Aβ pathology is associated with lower SFC in the Aβ+ group.

To probe regional specificity, we modelled the association between CL and SDI in the seven canonical functional networks. Significant effects were observed after FDR-correction in the VIS (β = 0.005, P = 0.0003) and VAN (β = 0.004, P = 0.0394) networks (Fig. 3b). The strongest regional SDI increases were localised bilaterally to the occipital regions and paracentral lobules (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Data 2), and showed comparable spatial distributions when using a higher-resolution parcellation (Supplementary Fig. S11) and excluding high-motion participants (Supplementary Fig. S7). When the analysis was restricted to the CDR 0 group, effects persisted exclusively within the VIS network (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Network topology explains amyloid-related structure–function coupling changes

In the single mediator model, we examined the paracentral lobule in the left hemisphere (LH, Frontal Medial 1), where the Aβ–SDI associated regions showed the strongest positive association in the primary analysis. In this region, the clustering coefficient mediated the effect of Aβ burden on SDI (standardised indirect effect: ab = 0.159, P = 0.0009), accounting for 78% of the total effect (Supplementary Fig. S4a). Aβ burden was associated with reduced local clustering (β = –0.207, P = 0.0007), and lower clustering in turn predicted increased SDI (β = –0.770, P = 2.2 × 10⁻16). The direct effect was attenuated and no longer significant, suggesting full mediation. The mediation effect remained significant across eight out of nine significant Aβ-SDI regions after FDR-correction (Supplementary Fig. S4b).

In the multi-mediator model, we observed a contribution of all three topological metrics across all the Aβ–SDI associated regions. For the paracentral lobule in the left hemisphere (LH, Frontal Medial 1), clustering and betweenness centrality both showed indirect effects (ab1 = 0.150, P = 0.0009 and ab2 = 0.029, P = 0.0462, respectively), while there was no effect via participation coefficient (ab3 = −0.009, P = 0.45). After FDR-correction, only clustering mediation effects remained significant. Together, these mediators explained 83% of the total effect of Aβ on SDI, with clustering again emerging as the strongest contributor (Fig. 4a). The total mediation effect remained significant across eight out of nine significant Aβ-SDI regions after FDR-correction (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Table S2). The total mediation effect results were robust across alternative density thresholds (Supplementary Fig. S12) and comparable after excluding high-motion participants (Supplementary Fig. S8).

Lower structure-function coupling attenuates the effect of amyloid on cognition

The resulting LF derived from CFA of the significant visual ROIs captured shared SDI variance in a subset of posterior regions, with strong loadings in the bilateral visual cortex and surrounding areas (Fig. 5, top).

The SDI LF was positively associated with global cognition and memory outcomes. Greater global Aβ burden was associated with higher SDI latent scores across all models, including MMSE (β = 0.385, P = 0.0003), immediate recall (β = 0.386, P = 0.0003), delayed recall (β = 0.387, P = 0.0003), and visual memory (β = 0.406, P = 0.0022). In turn, higher SDI LF (lower SFC) was associated with better MMSE performance (β = 0.316, P = 0.0016), immediate recall (β = 0.300, P = 0.0012), delayed recall (β = 0.385, P = 4.414 × 10⁻⁵), and visual memory (β = 0.353, P = 0.0008; Fig. 5).

For MMSE, Aβ burden was negatively associated with performance (direct effect: c’ = –0.447, P = 3.557 × 10⁻⁵), with SDI LF mediating 38% of this relationship (indirect effect: ab = 0.122, P = 0.0179). For immediate recall, the indirect effect was stronger, with SDI LF mediating 58% of the association (ab = 0.116, P = 0.016). The largest indirect effect was observed for delayed recall, with SDI LF significantly mediating the Aβ-cognition relationship (ab = 0.149, P = 0.0069). Visual memory also showed a relatively smaller mediation effect, with SDI accounting for 29% of the association (ab = 0.144, P = 0.0235) (Fig. 5). The total mediation effect results remained comparable after excluding high-motion participants (Supplementary Fig. S9).

Regional molecular underpinnings of amyloid-related coupling changes

Across the 249 Alzheimer’s disease and neurodegeneration-related genes selected from recent GWAS, 20 genes showed significant spatial correlations (Fig. 6a).

The 20 significant genes included established Alzheimer’s disease risk genes such as BIN1, CLU, and MAF, as well as genes implicated in Aβ metabolism (USP6NL, TREM2), immune and microglial activation (TNIP1, SLC24A4RG, SIGLEC11), and cytoskeletal organisation (SPDY3, NCK2, JAZF1). Absolute spatial correlation coefficients ranged from 0.25 to 0.42, with USP6NL showing the strongest association (Fig. 6a). Scatterplots for representative genes (Fig. 6b) illustrate the directionality of these associations. For example, higher expression of USP6NL and TNIP1 correlated with higher SDI-Aβ effect (weaker Aβ-related SFC), whereas CLU and MAF showed negative associations.

Gene ORA identified biological processes central to Alzheimer’s disease pathophysiology, including Aβ-beta clearance, Aβ fibril formation, complement activation, microglial cell activation, regulation of apoptotic signalling, and positive regulation of endocytosis (Fig. 6c). These pathways converge on Aβ processing, neuroinflammatory responses, and synaptic remodelling, indicating that regions with higher expression of these genes may be intrinsically more vulnerable to Aβ-related network changes. These results remained comparable after excluding high-motion participants (Supplementary Fig. S10).

Discussion

In this large, well-characterised cohort of older adults without dementia, we investigated how cortical Aβ burden is associated with brain structure–function relationships. We found that increased Aβ burden was associated with weaker SFC. This relationship was strongest in the visual cortices, typically characterised by higher structure-function alignment. This pattern suggests an early and spatially selective vulnerability of large-scale connectivity in the preclinical phase of Alzheimer’s disease. We demonstrated that these early alterations were partly explained by intermediate functional network mechanisms, which mediated the effect of Aβ burden on SDI. Specifically, lower local clustering mediated this association, suggesting a shift toward less locally constrained functional signal processing. We observed that lower SFC attenuated the adverse association between early Aβ pathology and cognitive performance, a pattern consistent with preserved functional organisation despite amyloid burden, which may reflect processes related to brain maintenance or resilience. We found that this specific change in SFC pattern was driven by transcriptomic factors closely linked to Alzheimer’s disease pathophysiology.

Our findings expand on prior literature, which has largely focused on late clinical Alzheimer’s disease stages, reporting an increase in SFC driven by the DMN46,49,50. Here, we show that early Aβ deposition is instead linked to a decrease in SFC, predominantly of the occipital cortices. Given that the visual regions are typically highly coupled, this effect may not reflect isolated dysfunction but rather an early shift in how functional signals are expressed relative to the structural connectome, which may first become observable in systems that are most tightly constrained by structure. This effect of Aβ on the visual SFC has also been reported in a recent study by Chen et al., where they focused on cognitively impaired individuals, suggesting it starts with early pathology and persists throughout the disease continuum52. The observed decrease in SFC of visual cortices in our study aligns with earlier findings of increased VIS-DMN connectivity in Aβ+ but non-demented individuals11, possibly reflecting changes in large-scale functional communication patterns. This is characteristic of the visual system, as it is amongst the most redundant large-scale networks, with multiple structural and functional pathways, providing greater flexibility in how functional signals are expressed across the structural network96. A recent large-scale study also found relatively more structural connections between occipital regions and temporal regions in individuals with Alzheimer’s disease pathology97. Animal studies support this interpretation, showing a pattern of neuronal hyperactivity in the primary visual cortex and brain regions highly connected to it in response to early Aβ, which eventually leads to synaptic failure98,99. The increased engagement of the visual and DMN networks has also been seen in other disorders such as Parkinson’s disease, where it is related to the pathophysiology of visual hallucinations and predicts white matter damage and cognitive decline100,101. These observations suggest that weaker visual SFC may reflect early functional network-level adaptation relative to the underlying structural network architecture in response to Aβ toxicity in Alzheimer’s disease hub regions102,103.

We further demonstrated that the effect of Aβ burden on SFC was partly mediated by local functional network properties, particularly clustering104,105. This suggests a shift toward less locally segregated functional organisation that may accompany early Aβ-related network alterations. These findings indicate that early Aβ-related SFC changes may involve alterations in functional network topology that modify the balance between locally constrained and more distributed patterns of signal expression across the structural network102,103.

By linking spatial patterns of Aβ-related SFC changes to regional gene expression profiles, we further show that intrinsic molecular architecture likely contributes to these observations. The identified genes included well-established Alzheimer’s disease risk genes (BIN1, CLU, TREM2, MAF, USP6NL)106,107 as well as genes implicated in Aβ metabolism108, microglial activation109, endocytosis110, and cytoskeletal organisation. Gene ontology enrichment revealed biological processes central to Alzheimer’s disease pathophysiology, including Aβ formation and clearance111, complement activation112, microglial cell activation113,114, apoptotic signalling115, and regulation of synaptic and protein complex assembly116. Our findings suggest that regions enriched in these gene expression patterns may be intrinsically more susceptible to early Aβ-related SFC changes, even before clinical symptoms emerge. Together with the network analysis, these results support the existence of a regional vulnerability gradient shaped by both connectivity profiles and molecular predisposition. This provides a mechanistic link between genetic susceptibility and functional network vulnerability, highlighting potential targets for early intervention in individuals at risk for Alzheimer’s disease117.

There is growing evidence that specific patterns of SFC influence cognitive performance during both development38,118 and neurodegeneration52,119. In the context of this study, we operationalise brain maintenance as the relative preservation of functional organisation and cognitive performance in individuals who already exhibit amyloid pathology. We found that lower SFC within visual regions suppressed the relationship between Aβ burden and cognitive performance across multiple domains. This pattern is consistent with the possibility that early SFC changes reflect adaptive functional reorganisation that helps preserve cognition in the presence of early pathology53, preserving cognitive function until later disease stages, when compensatory capacity may collapse as pathology overloads in the transmodal Aβ hub regions, leading to loss of neural flexibility, and eventual coupling of these regions. Similar early-stage functional alterations in VIS and VAN networks, despite minimal structural damage, have also been reported in asymptomatic MAPT mutation carriers, where they were interpreted as compensatory network reconfigurations that later give way to structural degeneration as the disease advances120. Given the cross-sectional nature of this study, it remains to be determined whether the adaptive response we found precedes Aβ accumulation, persists over time or ultimately collapses as pathology progresses. Moreover, because the present analyses are cross-sectional, this pattern does not uniquely support compensatory or maintenance interpretations and could also reflect other unmodelled factors.

From a translational perspective, identifying cognitively normal individuals who already exhibit early SDI-based SFC changes could help enrich prevention trials with participants at greatest risk for disease progression. Because SDI provides a network-level, non-invasive measure of structure–function reconfiguration, it could serve as a sensitive biomarker to monitor target engagement and efficacy of anti-Aβ or neuroprotective interventions. Quantifying the spatial and network-specific profile of SFC, particularly in regions such as the visual system, where early compensatory functional network reconfiguration may initially occur while cognition remains preserved, may aid in stratifying patients for individualised therapeutic approaches, particularly in the critical preclinical window when brain maintenance mechanisms are still active121.

Some limitations of the study should be considered. Differences in acquisition parameters across and within sites, including TR, TE, field strength, slice thickness, and scan duration, may influence the frequency content and signal characteristics of the fMRI data, thereby affecting SDI estimation (Supplementary Table S1). To account for these differences and ensure valid comparisons across datasets, we modelled fMRI TR, nTimepoints, and meanFD as covariates and included cohort as a random intercept in mixed-effects models to account for cohort- and protocol-related variability. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference regarding whether these adaptations persist or collapse over time. Although we found persistently lower visual network SFC in the CDR 0 subgroup, in the absence of tau PET data, we can only speculate that this effect may occur independently of substantial tau pathology. The cross-sectional design also limits causal interpretation of the mediation analyses, as temporal ordering between variables cannot be established in this setting122. In addition, the mediators (functional network properties) and the outcome variable (SDI) were derived from the same rsfMRI time series, which may introduce statistical dependencies between measures and inflate indirect effects. Consequently, the mediation results should be interpreted as exploratory associations rather than evidence for mechanistic pathways. Future longitudinal studies incorporating tau and vascular markers will be required to disentangle the contributions of different pathologies to structural–functional reconfiguration and to determine whether changes in functional network topology precede or follow alterations in SFC. Similarly, the cross-sectional nature of the cognitive mediation analyses limits inference about compensatory or maintenance processes, which will require longitudinal studies to determine whether preserved SFC predicts resistance to cognitive decline. The transcriptomic analyses relied on gene expression data from the AHBA, which includes only six donors and primarily reflects gene expression patterns from relatively young, neurologically healthy individuals. Consequently, these data capture normative spatial gradients of gene expression rather than transcriptional changes associated with ageing or AD pathology. The observed associations should therefore be interpreted as reflecting regional molecular architecture that may predispose certain brain regions to amyloid-related SFC alterations, rather than gene expression changes occurring during disease progression. Future studies incorporating larger transcriptomic datasets and age-matched or disease-stage–specific gene expression profiles will be important for validating and extending these findings. Graph metrics were computed at a fixed network density to ensure comparability across subjects, although robustness analyses across alternative thresholds demonstrated consistent results (Supplementary Fig. S12), the choice of threshold may still influence the exact values of graph measures. Future work could further explore robustness across alternative graph constructions. We used the Schaefer cortical parcellation for SDI computation, which provides a robust and widely used representation of large-scale cortical networks. Analyses across both 100- and 200-parcel resolutions yielded consistent results, although future studies with higher-quality data may leverage finer parcellations to potentially reveal additional spatial detail. This approach does not capture subcortical contributions, which may be relevant given their role in network integration and early Alzheimer’s disease pathology. Future work could employ complementary approaches to approximate SFC that do include subcortical structures where early and regionally specific vulnerability has been reported. Given the evidence for distinct Aβ PET phenotypes and their association with differential network patterns123, it would be of interest to investigate whether regional Aβ metrics further refine the prediction of SDI alterations. Future longitudinal, multimodal imaging studies will be critical to determine whether the early SFC alterations observed here are sustained compensatory mechanisms or transient adaptations that eventually fail with disease progression.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that SFC alterations may represent an early and spatially selective reconfiguration of functional activity relative to the structural connectome in response to Aβ deposition. Characterising these early events at the systems level and integrating them with molecular and cognitive markers could enable precision targeting of interventions in the critical preclinical window of Alzheimer’s disease.
