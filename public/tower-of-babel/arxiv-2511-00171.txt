CompAgent: An Agentic Framework for Visual Compliance Verification
Rahul Ghosh, Baishali Chaudhury, Hari Prasanna Das, Meghana Ashok

Abstract
Visual compliance verification is a critical yet underexplored problem in computer vision, especially in domains such as media, entertainment, and advertising where content must adhere to complex and evolving policy rules. Existing methods often rely on task-specific deep learning models trained on manually labeled datasets, which are costly to build and limited in generalizability. While recent Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) offer broad real-world knowledge and policy understanding, they struggle to reason over fine-grained visual details and apply structured compliance rules effectively on their own. In this paper, we propose CompAgent, the first agentic framework for visual compliance verification. CompAgent augments MLLMs with a suite of visual tools—such as object detectors, face analyzers, NSFW detectors, and captioning models—and introduces a planning agent that dynamically selects appropriate tools based on the compliance policy. A compliance verification agent then integrates image, tool outputs, and policy context to perform multimodal reasoning. Experiments on public benchmarks show that CompAgent outperforms specialized classifiers, direct MLLM prompting, and curated routing baselines, achieving up to 76 76 % F1 score and a 10 10 % improvement over the state-of-the-art on the UnsafeBench dataset. Our results demonstrate the effectiveness of agentic planning and robust tool-augmented reasoning for scalable, accurate, and adaptable visual compliance verification.

Introduction

Ensuring visual content compliance has become a critical imperative in our increasingly digital world, fundamentally serving to protect and empower all members of society. Regulatory frameworks—ranging from global guidelines like GDPR
Voigt and von dem Bussche 2017
to region-specific broadcasting codes like Ofcom
Ofcom 2020
—establish standards that safeguard vulnerable populations and promote inclusive access to digital content. These regulations play a vital role in creating a more equitable and safer digital environment, particularly benefiting children and diverse populations who rely on accessible content through accommodations like closed captions and appropriate warnings. Beyond individual protections, streaming platforms face severe consequences for compliance breaches, with recent violations incurring penalties up to $23 million
American Veterinary Medical Association 2024
. The rapid digital expansion and evolving threats underscore the urgent need for automated, accurate, and scalable visual compliance verification systems.

Visual compliance verification is inherently complex due to the evolving nature of compliance policies, which vary across regions, cultures, and industries. A policy may involve multifaceted considerations, such as detecting harmful objects, inappropriate gestures, explicit content, or region-specific cultural sensitivities. Manual review, while precise, is prohibitively slow and labor-intensive for the scale of modern media pipelines, where thousands of images and videos are processed daily. Consequently, the research community has recently turned attention to AI-driven solutions for automated compliance verification

;
Franco et al. 2025
;
Gandhi et al. 2020
;
Palla et al. 2025
;
AlDahoul et al. 2024

Existing approaches generally fall into two categories. The first relies on training dedicated deep learning classifiers to identify violations for predefined categories (e.g., nudity, violence, or hate symbols)
Gandhi et al. 2020
;
Yousaf and Nawaz 2022
. The second utilizes multimodal large language models (MLLMs), either by crafting compliance-oriented prompts or by fine-tuning them for specific compliance rules
Franco et al. 2025
;
Palla et al. 2025
;
AlDahoul et al. 2024
;
Jha et al. 2024
. While these methods have shown promise, they face key limitations. Training domain-specific models is costly and impractical due to (a) the lack of labeled datasets for highly specialized compliance scenarios and (b) the rapid evolution of compliance policies, which renders static models obsolete. On the other hand, directly applying MLLMs often struggles to achieve optimal performance because (a) rules are often intricate and interdependent, making it challenging for a model to interpret them holistically, and (b) understanding fine-grained visual details—such as contextual cues or nuanced cultural indicators—requires structured guidance beyond naive prompting. Consequently, current solutions either incur prohibitive labeling costs or deliver suboptimal accuracy.

To address these limitations, we introduce
CompAgent
, an agentic framework for automated visual compliance verification in images (see Fig.
1
). Our approach departs from monolithic models or pure prompt engineering by introducing a tool-augmented, agent-based architecture that decomposes the compliance verification task into modular steps. Specifically, our contributions are as follows:

•

Agentic Compliance Framework: We design the first ever (as per our knowledge at the time this work is done) agent-based system for visual compliance verification that dynamically orchestrates specialized vision tools (e.g., face detection, object detection, and more) according to compliance rules.

•

Policy-Aware Agents: We develop agents that parse compliance policies to identify applicable rules, plan and select the most relevant tools, eliminating the need for manual mapping and finally perform verification using the image, policy, and tool outputs to jointly reason about compliance, improving both precision and interpretability.

•

Training-Free and Cost-Efficient: Our approach requires no labeled data or fine-tuning, making it highly adaptable to evolving compliance policies and practical for real-world deployment.

We evaluate
CompAgent
on two public datasets under diverse compliance policies and demonstrate that it significantly outperforms baselines and state-of-the-art methods, including direct MLLM prompting and specialized classifiers. Comprehensive ablation studies validate the effectiveness of our design choices, and qualitative analyses illustrate the explainability of our framework. Although our current work focuses on single-image verification, the modular and agentic design of
CompAgent
lays a practical foundation for future extensions to video compliance verification, highlighting its strong potential to generalize to video applications.

2
Related Works

Figure 2
:
The architecture of

Early image content moderation relied heavily on rule-based systems and traditional machine learning classifiers. These methods often focus on predefined heuristics, statistical models, or textual cues associated with images rather than complex visual analysis. The approaches evolve from simple keyword filtering and metadata analysis using rule-based systems
Kaur et al. 2021
to more sophisticated methods using Machine Learning. In this section, we examine the related works in image content moderation techniques using machine learning models through two primary approaches: traditional computer vision frameworks and state-of-the-art Large Language Models (LLMs).

2.1
Traditional Computer Vision Frameworks

Historically, methods such as Support Vector Machines (SVMs) and Random Forests were used with handcrafted features to classify images as per content preferences. These systems relied on features such as color histograms, texture patterns, and edge detection to categorize content. Recent works using traditional computer vision often employ Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for analyzing visual features, and learning their pattern with predefined safety policies, such as nudity, violence, or hate speech. Tools for unsafe image classification, such as NudeNet
NotAI-tech 2019
and NSFW-Nets
Falconsai 2024
are built on CNN architectures. Leveraging CNNs, several Contrastive Language–Image Pre-training (CLIP) based
Radford et al. 2021
approaches have been developed for image content moderation, such as
Nichol et al. 2021
;
Schramowski et al. 2022
. Notably,
Poppi et al. 2025
introduces HySAC, which innovatively uses CLIP embeddings in hyperbolic space to model content safety hierarchies, while maintaining awareness of unsafe concepts and enabling flexible moderation. Similarly, authors in
Poppi et al. 2024
propose Safe-CLIP, which fine-tunes a CLIP backbone on synthetic safe/unsafe image-text pairs to “unlearn” links between NSFW concepts and the embedding space. In the realm of specialized detection, CNN-based approaches such as CUE-Net
Senadeera et al. 2024
have shown particular effectiveness in video violence detection, combining spatial cropping with transformer architectures to achieve state-of-the-art accuracy on standard benchmarks like RWF-2000
Cheng et al. 2021
and Real Life Violence Situations Dataset
Soliman et al. 2019
. More comprehensive frameworks have also emerged, such as ICM Assistant
Wu et al. 2025
, which implements a rule-based system for transparent content filtering with structured outputs.

2.2
Large Language Model based Frameworks

LLM-based approaches have significantly influenced unsafe image detection by integrating multimodal capabilities, enabling more nuanced and context-aware moderation compared to traditional computer vision methods. These models, often extend into vision-language models (VLMs), combine visual understanding with natural language processing to assess images for violations such as nudity, violence, or hate content, providing not just binary classifications but also detailed rationales and policy-compliant evaluations.
Liu et al. 2023
introduces LLaVA, one of the first large VLMs with instruction-tuning capabilities, which becomes a crucial baseline for subsequent safety-focused research. LlavaGuard
Helff et al. 2025
introduces an open VLM-based framework for assessing image safety, offering customizable taxonomies, safety ratings, categories, and rationales to evaluate compliance with diverse policies, outperforming traditional safeguards in accuracy and flexibility. Authors in
Gou et al. 2024
propose ECSO, a training-free defense mechanism that leverages the inherent safety mechanisms of text-only LLM backbones by transforming images into textual descriptions when unsafe content is detected, showing substantial improvements in harmless rates.
Xu et al. 2025
introduces SafeVision, an image moderation system using vision-LLMs that combines human-like understanding with guardrails for harmful content detection. Research in
Luo et al. 2025
involves the use of cross-modal co-embeddings in LLMs, and zero-shot learning. Another avenue of research focuses on supervised fine-tuning to adapt LLMs for specific content moderation tasks, and authors in
Ma et al. 2023
delve into the pitfalls of fine-tuning for content moderation.

2.3
Agentic AI based Solutions

Recent advances in LLMs have catalyzed the emergence of agentic systems across diverse application domains. In spatial reasoning and 3D understanding, VADAR
Marsili et al. 2025
demonstrates how agentic frameworks can dynamically generate APIs to decompose complex visual queries into executable programs, achieving superior performance on benchmarks requiring multi-step grounding and inference. Similarly, in video understanding, Agent-of-Thoughts Distillation
Shi et al. 2025
employs specialized agents for temporal grounding, object detection, and action recognition to construct reasoning chains for VideoQA tasks. The information retrieval domain has witnessed the rise of Agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation (Agentic RAG)
Singh et al. 2025
, where autonomous agents dynamically manage retrieval strategies and iteratively refine contextual understanding through reflection, planning, and tool use. More recently, Agentic Deep Research systems
Zhang et al. 2025
integrate reasoning agents with search capabilities to tackle complex, multi-step information synthesis tasks. Even in structured database interactions, agentic workflows have proven effective, with divide-and-conquer strategies and verification steps enhancing Text-to-SQL generation
Guo et al. 2025
.

Despite this proliferation of agentic approaches, the application of autonomous agents to visual compliance verification remains entirely unexplored. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to introduce an agentic framework specifically designed for automated visual compliance verification, addressing the unique challenges of decomposing compliance criteria, orchestrating evidence gathering, and providing interpretable verification traces.

3.1
Planning Agent

The
Planning Agent
serves as the strategic orchestrator of the compliance verification process, implemented through a ReAct (Reasoning and Acting)
Yao et al. 2023
framework with carefully designed prompts that encode complete policy specifications and structured instructions for systematic analysis. When presented with an image and compliance policies, the
Planning Agent
maintains the current reasoning state
s_{t}
at time
t
and systematically analyzes policy requirements to determine the next action
a_{t}
.

At each step, the
Planning Agent
operates on the current state
s_{t}=\{I,P,E_{t}\}
, which contains the input image
I
, compliance policy
P
, and accumulated evidence
E_{t}
from previously executed tools. Based on this state and the available
Tool Suite

T
, the agent strategically selects the next tool to invoke or decides to conclude the verification process. The agent updates the state after each tool execution:
s_{t+1}\leftarrow
UpdateState(
s_{t}
, ExecuteTool(
a_{t},I
)), integrating new evidence into the reasoning chain. When the
Planning Agent
determines that sufficient evidence has been gathered to make an informed compliance decision, it selects the terminal action
a_{t}=
CONCLUDE, triggering the transition to the verification phase handled by
CVAgent
.

The agent’s decision-making follows an explicit reasoning chain that decomposes complex compliance requirements into specific verification tasks, identifying which tools from the
Tool Suite
are most appropriate for gathering relevant evidence. The
Planning Agent
’s tool selection strategy is guided by multiple factors: the policy requirements that need verification, the agent’s understanding of each tool’s capabilities and limitations through comprehensive tool descriptions, the evidence already gathered from previous tool invocations, and the current context of the analysis. Rather than applying every tool exhaustively, the
Planning Agent
selectively invokes specific tools based on necessity, ensuring thorough yet efficient verification. For instance, if the policy concerns age-restricted content, the agent may prioritize face detection tools; if text-based violations are relevant, Text Detection becomes critical.

3.2
Tool Suite

The
Tool Suite

T
comprises a carefully curated collection of specialized tools that the
Planning Agent
can invoke during the compliance verification process. Each tool in the
Tool Suite
is defined by two key components: (1) a
tool description
that includes comprehensive information about its input arguments, return format, usage guidelines, and its specific capabilities, optimal usage scenarios, and known limitations—these detailed descriptions are critical for enabling the
Planning Agent
’s intelligent tool selection strategy described in the previous section, and (2) an
invoke function
that executes the tool on the input image and returns structured results. When the
Planning Agent
selects a tool
a_{t}
from the
Tool Suite
, it calls ExecuteTool(
a_{t},I
) which triggers the tool’s invoke function with the input image
I
, producing outputs in a standardized format containing detection results, confidence measures, and structured metadata. This standardization facilitates seamless integration of evidence during state updates and final compliance assessment.

The
Tool Suite
is organized into three categories, focusing on efficiency and accuracy rather than exhaustive tool application.

Summarization Tools

bda
form the foundation of our analysis pipeline, providing high-level content understanding and serving as the primary mechanism for establishing overall context. They leverage multiple visual models to generate comprehensive natural language descriptions that capture both obvious and subtle content elements.

Content Detection Tools
.
This category comprises fundamental analysis tools that extract specific content elements.
Face Detection

rek
processes facial attributes while maintaining privacy.
Object Detection

rek
identifies and localizes objects within images with confidence scores.

Text Detection

rek
employs Optical Character Recognition capabilities to extract and process textual content from images, performing word-level detection.

Content Moderation

bda
identifies potentially unsafe content categories. It processes images through multiple moderation models and returns structured labels indicating the presence and severity of policy-relevant content.

Specialized Compliance Tools
.
These tools are specifically designed for compliance verification tasks.
LlavaGuard Classification

Helff et al. 2025
functions as an expert system for compliance verification, providing comprehensive assessments including safety ratings, violation categories, and detailed rationales. It operates as a specialized system trained specifically on compliance policies and verification tasks.
Safe-CLIP

Zhao et al. 2025
performs zero-shot detection of toxic content across seven predefined categories including explicit content, violence, and prohibited substances.
ICM Assistant

Wu et al. 2025
employs template-based verification to provide structured, explainable safety assessments. It matches visual content against predefined violation templates and generates detailed explanations linking specific visual elements to policy violations, supporting transparent and auditable verification decisions.

3.3
Compliance Verification Agent

The
CVAgent
performs the final compliance assessment after the
Planning Agent
concludes evidence gathering. Implemented as an MLLM, the
CVAgent
receives the final state
s_{t}=\{I,P,E_{t}\}
containing the input image
I
, compliance policy
P
, and all accumulated evidence
E_{t}
from executed tools. Through step-by-step reasoning over the state, image, and policy, the
CVAgent
integrates these multiple information streams through structured multimodal reasoning to produce a comprehensive safety assessment
A=\{rating,category,rationale\}
, where
rating
is a binary classification (Safe/Unsafe),
category
identifies the specific policy violation if any, and
rationale
provides a detailed explanation of the decision.

The MLLM-based
CVAgent
synthesizes evidence by analyzing the image content, examining tool outputs with their confidence measures, interpreting the policy specification, and tracing the
Planning Agent
’s reasoning chain in
E_{t}
. This step-by-step analysis enables cross-validation of findings, conflict resolution, and evidence weighting based on tool confidence and policy relevance. The agent maintains transparency by generating detailed rationales that explicitly link visual evidence to policy requirements, explaining which content elements triggered violations and why.

4.1
Datasets

For our experiments, we use two open-source benchmark datasets, named LlavaGuard, and UnsafeBench.
LlavaGuard

Helff et al. 2025
is a comprehensive content moderation dataset of 1,290 test images (758 safe, 532 unsafe) labeled across nine safety categories (Hate/Humiliation/Harassment, Violence/Harm/Cruelty, Sexual Content, Nudity Content, Criminal Planning, Weapons/Substance Abuse, Self-Harm, Animal Cruelty, and Disasters/Emergencies).
UnsafeBench

Qu et al. 2024
dataset has both real-world and AI-generated content. For our experimentation, we use 2,037 (1,260 safe, 777 unsafe) images across 11 categories (Shocking, Sexual, Self-harm, Hate. Violence, Harassment, Illegal Activity, Political, Deception, Spam, and Public and personal health).

4.2
Evaluation Metrics

We formulate the task as a binary classification problem for visual compliance verification. To account for class imbalance, we primarily report precision, recall, and F1 score, focusing on the “Unsafe” class as the positive class of interest, with the “Unsafe” F1 as the single final metric. In addition, we provide overall accuracy for a broader assessment of model performance. To capture balanced performance across categories, we also report the macro F1 score - average of the F1 scores for the “Unsafe” and “Safe” classes.

5.1
Quantitative Analysis

We evaluate
CompAgent
, which employs the Claude Sonnet 3.5 v2 LLM as its
Planning Agent
, against state-of-the-art baselines on two benchmark datasets: LlavaGuard and UnsafeBench. As shown in Table
1
,
CompAgent
significantly outperforms existing approaches across all metrics, demonstrating the effectiveness of our agentic framework for visual compliance verification.

In the zero-shot setting, we observe that even advanced MLLMs struggle with direct compliance verification. Among the tested models, Llama 4 Maverick achieves the best zero-shot performance with Unsafe F1 scores of 0.55 and 0.71 on LlavaGuard and UnsafeBench respectively. While these results are promising, they fall short of specialized solutions, indicating that raw MLLM capabilities alone are insufficient for robust compliance verification. The performance gap is particularly evident on LlavaGuard, where complex policy rules require more structured analysis than simple prompting can provide.

Fine-tuned models show stronger performance but with notable limitations. LlavaGuard, when using dataset-specific policies, achieves impressive results on its native dataset (0.91 F1) but shows significant performance degradation on UnsafeBench (0.66 F1), highlighting challenges in cross-dataset generalization. Similarly, SafeVision reports strong performance on LlavaGuard (0.81 F1) but struggles with UnsafeBench (0.50 F1). These results underscore two key limitations of fine-tuned approaches: first, they require extensive labeled data and retraining to adapt to new policies or domains; second, their performance is heavily tied to the specific dataset and policy framework they were trained on, making them less practical for real-world scenarios where content and compliance requirements can vary significantly. This dataset dependency suggests that while fine-tuning can achieve strong results in controlled settings, it may not provide the flexibility and generalization needed for broad deployment across diverse compliance scenarios.

The category-based routing baseline, which employs predefined tool clusters, shows moderate improvement over zero-shot approaches with Unsafe F1 scores of 0.61 and 0.63 on LlavaGuard and UnsafeBench respectively. While this demonstrates the value of specialized tools, the rigid routing structure limits its ability to handle complex or evolving compliance requirements.

In contrast,
CompAgent
achieves state-of-the-art performance with Unsafe F1 scores of 0.93 on LlavaGuard and 0.76 on UnsafeBench. We also observe that
CompAgent
’s decision trajectories reveals significant adaptability in its approach to compliance verification. The framework demonstrates 95 distinct tool usage patterns on the LlavaGuard dataset and 147 different trajectories on UnsafeBench. This superior performance stems from three key advantages: (1) dynamic tool selection that adapts to specific policy requirements, (2) structured multimodal reasoning that effectively integrates tool outputs with policy context, and (3) training-free operation enabling seamless adaptation to new compliance policies without retraining.

6
Conclusion

We presented
CompAgent
, the first agentic framework for visual compliance verification.
CompAgent
achievges state-of-the-art performance by several key ways: (1) it performs continuous reasoning to automatically generate detailed verification plans while interacting with compliance-aware tools; (2) it introduces a comprehensive tool suite tailored for both compliance policy understanding and image-level analysis; and (3) it demonstrates strong generalizability across diverse compliance policies and datasets.
CompAgent
with its extensible tool suite makes decades of computer vision research accessible to visual compliance verification, enabling robust moderation that can evolve with emerging policies and societal needs.

Routing Architecture

We illustrate our routing architecture in Fig.
4
. A routing prompt drives Claude Sonnet 3.5 v2 to select and activate either category-based or policy-based nodes, which specialize in content-specific or policy-specific rules, respectively. Metadata produced by the activated nodes is then consolidated by a metadata fusion module. Finally, a multi-modal compliance analyzer (Claude Sonnet 3.5 v2) integrates the fused metadata with the compliance policy and input image to generate the final decision. In our experiments, we adopt the category-based routing configuration.
