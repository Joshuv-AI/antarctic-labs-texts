Cities as Atmospheric Infrastructures: An Analysis of Activist Responses to Respiratory Illness in the Czech Republic
Authors: Susanna Trnka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCities and the activities urban infrastructures enable have long been noted for making demonstrable impacts on the surrounding atmosphere. Cities also provide unique social and political infrastructures, promoting particular forms of cohesion and fracture, including opportunities for promoting political consciousness and inspiring collective activism. This paper introduces the concept of ‘atmospheric infrastructures’ to examine both of these facets of urban health and the dynamic relationship between them. It builds on a growing literature on ‘affective atmospheres’ that explores how collectively experienced emotions and senses are linked to specific locales and an emerging line of scholarship that foregrounds the duality of atmospheres as both affective spaces and constitutive of the air we breathe. Frequently underplayed, however, in analyses of affective atmospheres is the role of (human and nonhuman) agency. I propose viewing urban spaces as atmospheric infrastructures in order to highlight issues of collective responsibility in the constitution of both kinds of (meteorological and affective/political) atmospheres. My analysis is situated in responses to air pollution in the Czech Republic, where social activists frame respiratory health as a citizenship issue, fighting for the health of the city (rather than of individuals), and value civic education as much as protesting against pollution.

Introduction

Whether it be industrial pollution intensifying levels of PM2.5 and PM10 or urban greening programmes mitigating rising temperatures, cities and the activities urban infrastructures enable have long been noted for making demonstrable impacts on the surrounding atmosphere. Cities also act as unique social and political infrastructures, promoting particular forms of cohesion and fracture as well as opportunities for producing political consciousness and inspiring collective activism. This paper introduces the concept of ‘atmospheric infrastructures’ as a means of examining both of these facets of urban health and the dynamic relationship between them.

A growing literature in sociology, anthropology and geography on ‘affective atmospheres’ highlights how collectively experienced emotions, affects and senses are linked to particular locales (i.e., how certain spaces are seen as producing a ‘joyful’, ‘electric’ or ‘threatening atmosphere’ or feel restrictive or, conversely, create an atmosphere in which ‘one can breathe’). An important strand of this work emphasises the political dimensions of atmospheric affects, noting how place‐based, shared emotions and senses often (implicitly or explicitly) undergird political activism (Panagia 2009; Trnka 2012; Stephens 2015), as well as feelings of inclusivity (Kuruoğlu and Woodward 2021). At the same time, a small number of scholars have begun to explore the duality of atmospheres as, on the one hand, affective spaces and, on the other, constitutive of the air we breathe. Geographer Peter Adey, for example, notes that atmospheres are ‘simultaneously meteorological and affective’ and calls for a research agenda that ‘comprehends megacity air/atmospheres in their vitality, corporeality and expressiveness’ (2013, 293; see also McCormack 2008). How might attentiveness to this ‘atmospheric duality’ help us understand urban respiratory health?

Drawing together these emergent strands of analysis, I propose that viewing urban spaces as ‘atmospheric infrastructures’ foregrounds the role that human and nonhuman agency plays in the constitution of both meteorological atmospheres (the air we breathe) and the affective atmospheres (the ‘feeling in the air’) that urban sites inspire, enabling a better understanding of the social and political factors that underpin environmental health. This approach is premised on a sociological understanding of urban sites as composed of not only the built environment but also politics, most notably the forms of civil society, local government and social engagement that cities promote (Sennett 1994).

My analysis develops out of an ethnographic examination of activist responses to air pollution and respiratory health in the Czech Republic, specifically in the cities of Prague and Ostrava. Respiratory health, in particular the impact of air pollution on children, has been the focus of intensive social activism across a number of Czech cities. Concerns were first publicly raised in Prague and in northern urban areas of Bohemia in 1989 as part of the lead‐up to the 1989 Velvet Revolution (Vaněk 1996). In the early 2000s, national (and international) attention shifted to the Moravian city of Ostrava, where specific neighbourhoods were claimed by Czech scientists as having the highest rates of childhood asthma in the world (Sram et al. 2013). Today, more broad‐scale environmental efforts occur in cities across the nation. Although it is difficult to gauge levels of public concern, public discourse is permeated with accounts of urban infrastructures, most notably industrial works and automobile networks, as damaging to respiratory health. Czech medical professionals, moreover, frequently prescribe leaving the city for protracted periods as a countermeasure to respiratory (and other) illnesses, provoking governmental debates over the scientific validity of the nation's rurally located ‘health spa’ system (lázeňství).

Many Czech social activists frame respiratory health as a citizenship issue, focusing on the collective responsibility of urban residents to engage in activism that transcends their personal or familial needs, fighting for the health of the city, rather than its individuals. Activist efforts underscore the ways that urban infrastructures both enable activities that contribute to pollution levels and provide effective means for publicising and countering them. Cities emerge as ‘atmospheric infrastructures’ that both undergird the gases, liquids and particles that compose the atmosphere and the affective and political grounds for determining them. Made up of various smaller infrastructures (e.g., industrial facilities, communications networks, parks and greenery, transport systems and educational facilities), they act as a unit to mould both politics and the constitution of the air above.

From Atmospheric Turns to Atmospheric Infrastructures

Recently, social scientists have begun retheorising what we mean by ‘atmospheres’, taking part in what has been described as a scholarly ‘atmospheric turn’ (Gandy 2017; McDowell 2024). Much of this work has come from theorists interested in affect theory and, more specifically, relationships between affect and place. Geography has witnessed increasing efforts to outline the social dynamics generated by and generative of ‘urban atmospheres’ (Gandy 2017), as well as, more generally, to examine how affect is ‘grounded in the environment itself’, so much so that ‘a deserted beach feels “lonely,” a rumbling sea is “threatening,” a ruined church “ominous”’ (S. D. Brown et al. 2019, 6). Sociology has begun to pay more attention to the atmospheric qualities of built environments, examining various kinds of feeling states that are produced through the ‘socio‐material arrangements’ of multiethnic neighbourhoods (Kuruoğlu and Woodward 2021), museums (Dekel and Vinitzky‐Seroussi 2017), urban ruins and other previously abandoned sites (Göbel 2015, 2020). Anthropologists have likewise started to utilise ‘atmospheres’ to understand phenomena as specific as the emotion of loneliness (Schnegg 2025) or the affective impact of city lighting (Bille and Schwabe 2023) and, more broadly, to describe ‘how forces come to reside in experiences, conditions, things, dreams, landscapes, imaginaries and lived sensory movements’ (Steward 2011).

Several scholars have focused on the political dimensions of affective atmospheres, urging researchers not to ‘neglect the roles of emotions, affects, and feelings in political life’ (Stephens 2015, 101). This includes highlighting, for example, how the designing of specific affective atmospheres can be conducive to political engagement (Osler and Szanto 2021) or how a ‘visceral urban politics’ may include exclusionary practices grounded in sensorial experiences of disgust and uncleanliness (Pow 2017, 260). Whether it be a public piazza (Panagia 2009), a hospital or clinic (Street 2011; McDowell 2026), or migrant housing located along a railway track (Elinoff 2021), places have been shown to incite political discussion, community‐building and fracture.

With works aiming to describe ‘affective atmospheres’ in terms of their ‘presence and absence, materiality and ideality, definite and indefinite, singularity and generality’ (Anderson 2009, 80), there is demonstrably a good deal of slippage as to what actually constitutes an atmosphere. But, for many, this is also an explicit part of the concept's attractiveness. Often traced back to the work of philosopher of science Gernot Böhme (who, in turn, credits his conceptualisation of atmospheres to the inspiration of neo‐phenomenologist Hermann Schmitz), this scholarly approach frequently emphasises that atmospheres should be understood as ‘fleeting, ambiguous, and vague’ (Bille et al. 2015, 33). As geographer Matthew Gandy suggests, ‘The word ‘atmosphere’ can simultaneously evoke both a body of air, or even simply a space, along with its prevailing affective characteristics. … The meaning can oscillate between, for example, the latent atmosphere of a room or the complex interplay between multiple subjects in crowd‐like situations’ (2017, 355). Arguably, the concept is usually used to refer to a mood or sensibility that ‘permeates the air’, with a focus on the spatial, environmental and sometimes social elements that are thought to generate it. As one set of proponents argues, the salience of the concept resides in its acknowledgement that ‘wind and sea’ as ‘actors in their own right … combine with the living and the dead communities to co‐produce a palpable sense of place’ (S. D. Brown et al. 2019, 6).

At the same time, inspired by the dual meaning in English (and several European languages) of the word atmosphere, scholars have begun to probe the connection between such affective spaces and (literally) the air we breathe (Ingold 2012, 2024; Ford 2022). Directly echoing geographer Derek P. McCormack's conceptualisation of atmospheres ‘in terms that are simultaneously meteorological and affective’ (2008, 414), Adey calls for examinations of megacities (which I suggest could also be extended to comparatively smaller urban areas) that consider how air and affective atmosphere are intertwined (2013, 293). For Adey, who aims to understand how ‘the qualities of the city … seep and imbue its material and biological fabric with affect’ (2013, 291), a key issue is what differences in air quality indicate about class and other forms of social cleavage; as Adey argues, ‘the testimony of pollutants and choking effluvium’ goes far to reveal ‘who belongs and who does not, who is [deemed] deserving and who is not’ (2013, 291). In health‐focused research, similar approaches are promoted by anthropologists McDowell (2024), who examines lived experiences of tuberculosis in rural India by means of tracing the ‘atmospheric entanglements’ between breath, microbes, air, dust and inequality, and Eli Elinoff, who documents the interrelated ways in which, during a time of acute social unrest in Thailand (2019–2023), ‘air pollution, the COVID‐19 pandemic, and government restrictions on speech transformed how Thai citizens breathed and how they related to the monarchy’ (2024, 357).

Notably, all four scholars (Adey, Elinoff, McCormack and McDowell) address a facet of analysis that is often missing or underplayed in the broader literature on affective atmospheres, namely, the role that humans play in creating the environments that impact upon them. This is a necessary corrective if we wish to be able to view cities as both human‐built environments and as generative of specific kinds of atmospheres.

By drawing on the concept of infrastructure as part of examining the atmosphere, I aim to highlight how (affective, political and gaseous) atmospheres are created and sustained. Focusing on infrastructural relations between cities and atmospheres, I suggest, foregrounds the question: Who is responsible for the air?

Cities as Infrastructures

Although work on atmospheres is currently experiencing a heyday in the social sciences, the concept of infrastructures has been popular among sociologists, anthropologists, geographers and science and technology (STS) scholars since at least the late 1990s and has a long association with urban studies (Campo et al. 2025, 124). My intention in pairing together novel perspectives on atmospheres with more established scholarship on infrastructures is to enable us to further probe the relationships between human actors, cities and the air we breathe.

Although there is a variety of ways that infrastructures have been conceptualised, they are most often thought of as material or conceptual undergirding or ‘substrate supporting something else’ (Campo et al. 2025, 117). In his widely cited definition of infrastructure, anthropologist Brian Larkin recommends envisaging infrastructures as ‘material forms that allow for the possibility of exchange over space, … physical networks through which goods, ideas, waste, power, people, and finance are trafficked’ (2013, 327).

Scholars, including Larkin himself, have extended this definition beyond material forms to include cybernetics and ideational infrastructures (e.g., Jackson et al. 2007; Edwards et al. 2009). They have furthermore underscored the political and affective dimensions of infrastructures, revealing how infrastructures can come to be imbued with ‘desire and promise’ (Larkin 2013, 327), acting as ‘sites of popular imagination and anticipation’ (Reeves 2016, 2; see also Starr 1999; Street 2011; Zampieri 2025; McDowell 2026).

Infrastructures, moreover, act not only on people but also on surrounding environments (Corchia and Borghini 2025). Notably, there has been sustained social science inquiry into what infrastructures might reveal about human and nonhuman agency, in particular with respect to how infrastructures are designed, built or, in some cases, ‘grown’ (Starr 1999; Edwards et al. 2009), as well as how they might come to be agentive in their own right (Starr 1999; Hansen and Schulze 2023; Zampieri 2025; see also Winner 1980).

Perhaps surprisingly, given their power, infrastructures regularly come in and out of public focus (Starr 1999; Larkin 2013). Indeed, some scholars contend that when infrastructures surface into visibility, it is generally because things have gone wrong—an incorrectly connected pipe or a breakdown in communication capabilities (Starr 1999; Lea and Pholeros 2010; Jensen 2016). Larkin, however, warns not to hold onto the characteristic of invisibility too tightly, lest it impede understandings of infrastructures' intimate engagements in the political: ‘invisibility is certainly one aspect of infrastructure, but it is only one and at the extreme edge of a range of visibilities that move from unseen to grand spectacles and everything in between’ (2013, 336). Indeed, the heightened visibility of infrastructure has primarily been noted in contexts of nation‐building and modernisation efforts (Humphrey 2005; Larkin 2013; Jensen 2016; Reeves 2016), as well as other contexts where infrastructural projects become the focus of public debate, such as industrial works that generate air pollution.

As intentional, built environments, ostensibly intended to provide hospitable, or at least survivable, living conditions for their residents, cities inspire—among scholars and the residents who live in them—questions about not only livability but also agency, causation and intentionality. Unlike other kinds of spaces that may be popularly interpreted as ‘naturally’ dangerous or ‘naturally’ healthy, cities are usually seen as intentionally constituted by human agents, even if they are also viewed as taking on ‘a life of their own’, sparking debate over who is responsible for their perceived harms. Recognising cities as infrastructures enables us to think of them as the spaces of everyday life, while simultaneously noting how they act as the substrata for political, affective, environmental and health conditions.

Both ‘infrastructures’ and ‘atmospheres’ have generated rich areas of scholarly inquiry that have much to offer to understandings of urban health. By bringing them together, the concept of ‘atmospheric infrastructures’ provides an analytical lens for examining how cities produce and support the gases, liquids and particles that compose the atmosphere as well as the affective and political grounds that help determine them. Foregrounding issues of human and nonhuman agency, ‘atmospheric infrastructures’ refocuses analytical attention on how meteorological and affective/political atmospheres are created and thus the collective, and individual, responsibilities we hold for them.

Methods

My analysis develops out of ethnographic research I undertook on therapeutic approaches to childhood respiratory illnesses in the Czech Republic. The research, which received ethics approval from the University of Auckland, was conducted through short, intensive fieldwork visits of 1–3 months that took place over six years (2010–2016), later supplemented by one month of follow‐up fieldwork in 2025.

The 2010–2016 research involved face‐to‐face site visits, interviews and extended discussions with members of six ecological organisations based in Prague, Ostrava and Brno: Vzduch, Čisté nebe, Arnika, Pražské matky, a local branch of Greenpeace and Ekologický Institut Veronica. It also included interviews with a variety of medical professionals who work with children and adults with respiratory disorders (family physicians, respiratory specialists, nurses, physiotherapists and medical spa directors), discussions with public health officials engaged in environmental health, meetings with environmental health scientists from Prague's Ústav experimentální medicíny AV ČR (Institute of Experimental Medicine), interviews with representatives from the country's major health insurance providers and discussions with lawyers and legal activists engaged in environmental health litigation. I made trips to half a dozen Czech health spas that focus on treating respiratory illnesses. I also had repeated discussions with civil engineers about the expansion of Prague's roadway infrastructure. In 2014, I interviewed representatives of the steel company ArcelorMittal Ostrava, which was then involved in highly publicised legal disputes over pollution emissions and their impacts on respiratory health, and undertook a site visit of the Ostrava steel plant. Throughout 2010–2016, I conducted formal and informal interviews with adults and children with chronic respiratory conditions (usually asthma), repeatedly lived with a family of a child with asthma, and spent extended time with other families whose children had respiratory health issues 1 .

Follow‐up discussions with environmental health activists were conducted in October 2025 over Zoom and email and face‐to‐face in Prague during November–December 2025. Fieldwork in Prague in 2025 included discussion with Arnika representatives, participant observation at activist events, meetings with ecologists and roadway engineers and discussions with Prague residents about the environment and their respiratory health.

As is commonplace in anthropological research, I undertook an iterative, grounded theory approach, allowing themes to emerge from fieldwork encounters and following up on topics that my interlocutors viewed as significant. Most notably, although my initial project was intended to focus on biomedical treatments of childhood asthma, upon the urging of medical professionals, families of children with asthma and respiratory scientists, I expanded to examining activist approaches to environmental factors, specifically urban air pollution. Three key discourses emerged: residents' visceral knowledge of how urban infrastructures cause respiratory harm; the use of urban infrastructures to educate the general public about their personal responsibilities for air pollution; and the valorisation and cultivation of nonurban, ‘natural’ atmospheres, which often rely on urban infrastructures to be sustained.

Do You Feel It in the Air? Cities as Industrial Infrastructures

It was a gorgeously clear and bright sunny afternoon in May 2014 when I met with one of the founders of Ostrava's activist groups, Čisté nebe (Clean Sky), in a small beer garden in the neighbourhood of Radvanice, which is situated downwind from the steelworks and is widely acknowledged to have some of the worst air quality in Europe (Pokorná et al. 2016). I had taken the bus from central Ostrava and found that, when I stepped off, the air felt noticeably ‘heavier’, and my eyes had begun to itch. By the time I finished the 5‐minute walk to the beer garden, I was coughing a little. After he greeted me, Ivo (pseud.) took note of my cough, probing, ‘Do you feel it? You can smell it, can't you?’ He explained how, even on a clear day when pollution measurements were not particularly alarming, it could still have a visible, physical impact. Although I was embarrassed to feel my breathing hindered by what I knew were comparatively mild conditions, for Ivo it was important that I stop and articulate my embodied response to the air he breathes every day.

Nicknamed ‘Black Ostrava’ because of its visible air pollution, Ostrava is nationally and internationally notorious for its often smoggy atmosphere (Viviano 1994; Budinský 2014). People are quick to point out the visceral qualities of breathing in pollution and noting its visible presence in the city. When I received my company‐approved, individualised tour of the ArcelorMittal steel plant, the tour guide was clearly delighted that I not only spoke Czech but was also open to hearing his philosophical musings over the politics of industrial production (Trnka 2017a). Veering off script, he made sure I noticed the ash on the ground of the compound. ‘The part they don't tell me to show you is the black stuff coating the ground. … But without dust, it wouldn't be Ostrava’, he noted sardonically.

Industrialism and the pollution that it produces are viewed as part of the identity of the city. Inhaled by its residents, pollution also comes to constitute part of their bodies and identities, a prominent example of how one's sense of self can be shaped by the infrastructures that mould our living conditions (Corchia and Borghini 2025). Ostrava's residents, for example, popularly depict their identities as emerging out of the city's industrial infrastructure, comparing the rugged, ‘industrialized’ Ostravan or Ostravák (resident of Ostrava) to the more polished and ‘cosmopolitan’ Pražan or Pražák (Praguer) (Budinský 2014).

Although the damage caused by Ostrava's industrial infrastructure captured public attention in the early 2000s, there had long been concerns about air quality in other parts of the Czech Republic. During 41 years of rule by the Communist Party (1948–1989), it was prohibited to publicly suggest the state was neglecting the environment, much less that such neglect had a negative impact on the citizenry (Vaněk 1996; Jehlicka 2001). However, days before the November 1989 Velvet Revolution, which ended Communist Party rule, widespread antigovernment protests broke out in northern industrial towns such as Teplice, where residents expressed outrage over poor respiratory health thought to be caused by intense air pollution (Vaněk 1996).

Despite their profound political impact, Czech historian Miroslav Vaněk, who grew up in Teplice, emphatically asserted to me that the protests must be understood as motivated by community suffering and not political ideology:The pollution was so bad, so many children would get very sick every year. And everyone could see how sick they were. You would go visit the doctor and the waiting room was full of sick children with stuffed noses, coughs, their eyes red and so swollen that they could not shut them … This was not an ideological struggle like the dissidents' arguments about human rights, but it was something everyone in that area was living through.

In some ways, Vaněk's analysis of environmental conditions alone compelling citizens to act is echoed by one of the founders of an early Prague‐based environmental organisation, Pražské matky (Prague Mothers). A few months before the Teplice protests, in May 1989, a group of women activists publicly called on the government to improve Prague's air quality in the name of protecting their children's health. Explaining how Pražské matky came to take over a downtown street with their baby prams in tow, Jarmila Johnová, one of the group's founders, recounted: ‘You could see it in the air. On the bad days, my son … went gray and had no energy. It was my own intuition that his listlessness was linked to the air quality, as we weren't doctors. But we knew there was dust in the air, and we couldn't bear to see our children like this. If it were made public, we imagined the government would need to start caring about the environment’.

But Johnová was also very clear that although the protests were spurred by the widely felt health impacts of poor environmental conditions, they cannot be attributed simply to the atmosphere provoking residents to spontaneously take over the streets. Indeed, she outlined the strategic planning it took to execute a public protest during the Communist period, explaining how the seemingly apolitical name of Pražské matky was suggested to her by a male dissident as a ruse to confuse the authorities that theirs was not a political group but ‘just mothers’ worried about their children. The strategy worked, as historians Kilburn and Vaněk (2004, 9) note, ‘The parade of prams … left bystanders supportive and security forces completely disarmed’. According to Johnová, although the impacts of air pollution might be so compelling that a parent does not need to be a doctor to ‘intuit’ that it is the cause of their child's suffering, there is still the need for careful organisational strategising. During the Communist regime, this involved tempering the political significance of one's demands. In the years after the 1989 revolution, it involved intensive educational campaigns about the damage caused by air pollution and the urban infrastructures that produce it.

But although Prague and Teplice were some of the first places where protests broke out, arguably the most prominent site of contention during the immediate post‐communist period was the industrial heartland of Ostrava. The end of the Communist regime saw the widespread easing of media censorship, leading to extensive coverage of the region's environmental damage. At the same time, the post‐1989 widespread closures of coal mines and slowing of steel production caused a notable, rapid improvement in local air quality.

Subsequently, however, some of the steelworks, most notably Ostrava's state‐owned Nová Huť Klementa Gottwalda (NHKG) steel plant, which had been in operation since 1951, were bought out by foreign investors. Refurbished and rebranded ‘ArcelorMittal Ostrava’, the former NHKG steelworks became the Czech Republic's largest steel producer, capable of generating 3.6 million tons of crude steel per year (Liberty, 2019). The plant was also found to regularly exceed EU emissions standards, leading to widely publicised public protests and lawsuits. Data from 2013, for example, when public protests were in their fifth year, revealed levels of PM10, which can lead to aggravated asthma, chronic bronchitis and lung cancer, that exceeded EU limits on 129/365 days (Czech Hydrometeorological Institute 2014).

The plight of Ostrava's approximately 300,000 residents (along with another 500,000 people residing in the surrounding area) became a focal point of national and, at times, international concern (e.g., Stewart 2008). In addition to dramatic public protests, during which residents, including young children, took to the streets while wearing gas masks, there were debates in Parliament, fronted by well‐known scientists and respiratory physicians, a petition signed by over 2000 people demanding that pollution regulations stop being breached and a series of legal cases, most notably when the city of Ostrava unsuccessfully sued the Czech government for not enforcing pollution regulations and ensuring a ‘favourable environment’ for its citizens (TV Nova 2013).

Throughout, ArcelorMittal denied being the primary source of the pollution, invoking a range of other possible contributors, including other (smaller) local steel plants, a local heating plant, and automobile and home‐heating emissions (Trnka 2017a). Despite ongoing legal cases over the health impacts that Ostrava's poor air quality is alleged to have caused (Lazarová 2019), local protests against ArcelorMittal have dissipated due to improved industrial de‐dusting measures and the fact that the steelworks were sold on to Liberty Steel, which later filed for bankruptcy and closed the primary production facilities in late 2023 (with new owners taking over the plant in late 2025).

Today, Czech environmental activists’ focus on industrial pollution has been notably eclipsed by other environmental health concerns, for example, widespread calls for minimising traffic in congested urban areas. Pražské matky renamed itself Pěšky městem (loosely translated as ‘walking through the city’) in 2020 and now focuses its efforts on making Prague more pedestrian‐friendly. This includes calls to restrict car emissions, most notably because of the ’10,000 people in Czech Republic (who) die because of air pollution every year’ (Pěšky městem, n.d.). A similar initiative, 30 Pro Prahu (30 for Prague), was spearheaded by the activist group Arnika in 2023 with the aim of limiting speeds in the centre of the city to 30 km/hour. Arnika and allied groups promote pedestrian and bicycle transport in Prague while also monitoring urban planning in order to ensure the existence of green spaces. Activists are also engaged in lawsuits against improperly licensed public works and lobbying against other major roadworks deemed detrimental to local ecologies. Notably, in addition to focusing on specific infrastructures (steel plants, motorways), current activism suggests a broadening of scope, highlighting concerns over how urban living as a whole impacts on health and well‐being.

But as Johnová suggested, there is more to environmental activists’ work than responding to the ills of urban living. Although their efforts are targeted at achieving specific environmental outcomes, just as significantly, Czech activists see their role as educating and bringing together residents and thus laying the foundation for an active civil society that determines its city’s future. Here, the city is envisioned as not just the infrastructure for air pollution but also the infrastructure for its solutions.

Cities as Civic Infrastructures: Citizenship, Responsibility and the Needs of the City

In Radvanice, Ostrava, Čisté nebe activist Ivo spent 2 hours with me discussing Ostrava’s air pollution and his assessment of its probable cause, which he attributed solely to the illegal emission levels from the nearby ArcelorMittal steel plant. Two weeks before our meeting, I had seen him interviewed in a British documentary about the widespread galvanisation of public protest in Ostrava, which made me think he would steer our discussion towards the street protests, court cases and residents’ petition that demanded the government enforce emissions controls on ArcelorMittal. Instead, he spent most of the interview evoking his passion for citizen science, in particular, Čisté nebe’s role in the development and distribution of apps that make local air quality measurements which are then collected and collated with data from across the entire Czech Republic.

It quickly became clear that his organisation’s purpose was twofold: to pressure city officials into enforcing stricter emissions controls on ArcelorMittal and to educate residents on how to measure the air quality outside of their homes. As the English‐language version of Čisté nebe’s 2025 website states: ‘Our mission is to spread complex information about air quality related issues and therefore raise a social responsibility for our environment in people, especially in politicians’.

Activism to ‘raise social responsibility … in people’ over environmental issues, and in particular over environmental health, has a long history in the Czech Republic. In their public explanations of who or what is responsible for the pollution levels in Ostrava, most (but not all) environmental activists drew a singular line of causation back to ArcelorMittal. They also, however, assumed that calling on ArcelorMittal to change its practices would likely be ineffective; as one activist explained to me, there is no point in appealing to a corporate conscience when industry is always ‘beyond reason’. Instead, going down the chain of responsibility and asking who is responsible for holding the company to account, they took to the streets and courts, demanding a response from city officials and/or the national government, while simultaneously pronouncing that it is Ostrava’s residents who are ultimately accountable for the city’s pollution levels.

Asking, ‘Who is responsible for holding the government in check?’, activists made concerted efforts to compel local residents to demand action. In contrast to scholarly critiques of neoliberal assumptions of personal responsibility as leading individuals to focus only on themselves (Trnka and Trundle 2017), taking personal responsibility was depicted in activist discourses as the catalyst for propelling community action. Cast as a city‐wide concern that needs to be dealt with on an infrastructural level, respiratory illness became the personal responsibility of all of Ostrava’s residents.

As one politically active public health specialist told me, civic apathy towards air pollution is just as big a problem as the industries that emit it, and the only credible solution for improving respiratory health is to get more residents involved in demanding clean air. ‘What’s the answer to stopping asthma?’ she rhetorically asked before laughingly replying, ‘Don’t live next to a smoke stack!’ and then immediately launching into an explanation of how such single‐handed solutions were not going to solve the issue. ‘What we need is a change in society’, she asserted. Scathingly critical of individualised solutions, she suggested the underlying problem with combatting respiratory illness was the general public’s passivity, lack of education and general ‘learnedness’ (vzdělaný). Rather than viewing respiratory illness as an affliction suffered by some bodies and not others, local residents, she argued, need to educate themselves and demand collective solutions:The crisis in respiratory health … is due to lack of education. People prioritise their finances over their health because they are not educated. They don’t form groups and get active. Instead they go play football and don’t worry about the environment. Babies get put on inhalers, people get put on corticosteroids, and everyone is passive.

Taking personal responsibility here becomes the first step in recognising one’s collective responsibility. In parallel with Ivo’s promotion of apps to measure local air quality in Ostrava, activists from Pražské matky explained that they have long focused on engaging primary school children in measuring environmental damage not only so that they can learn about the damage air pollution causes but so that they also learn that it is their responsibility to mitigate it.

Anthropologist Kristina Alda encountered similar views among the urban activists she worked with in Ostrava, who told her that their gardening activities were intended not only to add some green space to the city but also ‘to change people’s relationship to public space and to get them to take on some level of responsibility. … We could tell people specifically what to do, but it wouldn’t be sustainable. That desire and sense of responsibility needs to be in the people’ (Alda 2023, 184).

Rather than disruption, protest or anger, much of Czech environmental activism adopts the tone of respectful, considered education campaigns. Urban infrastructures play a privileged role in such efforts. Activists are very aware of how cities themselves act as conduits for social activism, providing visible sites of public protest and mass audiences. Pavements and sometimes emptied streets offer up ready‐made staging grounds for protests, whereas transport systems easily enable protesters to gather together. Just as significantly, cities are widely viewed as promoting an ethos of informed discussion and otherwise providing the basis of civic life. As sites of universities, theatres and cinemas, bookshops, cafes and activists, as well as government headquarters, urban infrastructures play a unique role in spreading public consciousness, knowledge and ‘learnedness’ (vzdělaný), as well as providing hubs for both the creation and communication of scientific and political information. Cities are thus seen as enabling the infrastructures not only for environmental damage but also for bringing together residents to attempt to mitigate it.

Cities, as noted, also create the foundations of collective identities, linking together the historical and present‐day circumstances of residents and the spaces in which they live, as well as their outlook on what ‘their’ city might become in the future (Jacobs [1961] 1993; Sennett 1994). In some activists’ reckonings, not only should the members of a city act collectively, they should act for the well‐being of the city, meaning both for the collective of its current residents and for the city as an entity in its own right.

The latter sentiment is exemplified in Green Party representative Matěj Michalk Žaloudek's public statement outlining the reasoning behind 30 Pro Prahu (30 for Prague), an initiative which demands a city‐wide 30 km/h speed limit in order to curb emissions and ensure pedestrian safety:We want to negotiate with the city on how to contribute to the goals to which the city has committed itself. We believe that politicians are serious about their proclamations that people's safety and health are a priority for them. We are open to dialogue on the parameters of our proposal because we realize that the city is a complex organism in which all interests need to be balanced.(Arnika Press Release, 2023, my translation)

We believe that politicians are serious about their proclamations that people's safety and health are a priority for them. We are open to dialogue on the parameters of our proposal because we realize that the city is a complex organism in which all interests need to be balanced.

Clearly, ‘the city’ with whom Žaloudek envisages negotiating is the members of the city government. It is, however, significant that Žaloudek describes the city as an organic unit, ‘a complex organism’ with its own goals and commitments.

Considering the city as ‘a complex organism’ evokes a mutuality between the ‘life of the city’ and that of its current residents, one that nevertheless requires making a distinction between them (Sennett 1994), evoking a tension between whose needs are to be addressed. On the one hand, people are ultimately deemed responsible for creating the environments in which they live (both urban and, to some extent, rural, as discussed below), and on the other, cities emerge as actors who, if they are to remain sustainable into the future, have their own needs that must be met. As sociologist Luca Corchia and philosopher Andrea Borghini suggest, cities are arguably exemplary of how infrastructures ‘embody sedimented practices while simultaneously shaping and informing new ones’ (2025, 127). As much as people construct and shape the city and are responsible for how it functions, the city shapes both them and future residents.

By invoking the needs of the city, activists, moreover, deflect notions that their proposals are antithetical to the needs of ‘modern’, urban life. Statements such as Žaloudek's offset criticisms that environmental activists are out of touch with the needs of ordinary residents. (A Prague civil engineer, for example, complained to me that whenever a form of protected wildlife is discovered on a proposed roadway, environmental activists try and scupper the whole building project, ‘as if they don't care that we won't be able to drive to work, just to save one frog’.) By invoking images of the city as ‘a complex organism’, environmental activists suggest they too are aware of the need to protect the city's own ‘vitality’ and do not aim to unilaterally promote the therapeutic benefits of ‘nature’.

Valorisation and (Urban) Cultivation of ‘Natural’ Atmospheres

Just as environmental protests have a long history, so too does the widespread public perception of urban infrastructures as damaging to respiratory health. One of the most popular public solutions to respiratory illness is lengthy sojourns out of urban areas. It is also a solution officially prescribed by state healthcare services.

The Czech Republic enjoys a comparatively high level of biomedical care, ranking 14th out of 35 EU nations in the Euro Health Consumer Index of 2018 (Björnberg and Phang 2019). Despite the emphasis on biomedicine, ‘climate therapy’ remains a popular, governmentally funded mode of care for respiratory illnesses 3 . The country is criss‐crossed with over 30 health spas that focus on providing immersion in a ‘healthy’ climate (Pohůdka 2025). Many have been in operation since their establishment in the 18th–20th centuries, with some notable spas founded during the 12th and 14th centuries.

As part of the publicly funded health system, health spas are premised on the notion that alongside their famed balneotherapy, or therapeutic use of local mineral waters, breathing in rural air and other forms of outdoor exposure are necessary correctives to the physical as well as social and psychic damages incurred by city living (Trnka 2017b). Multiweek spa treatments are popular among urban residents wishing to depart polluted cities. But they are just as popular among residents of villages or rural areas who are prescribed spa cures in mountainous areas where specific microclimates, alongside lower levels of dust and other air pollutants, are thought to improve respiratory function and increase viral immunity. After the end of the Communist regime in 1989, there was wide‐scale revision of the public healthcare system, which has led to continued debates over the extent of the government's current responsibilities to provide citizens with healthcare (Heitlinger and Trnka 1998; Read 2007; Heitlinger 2009; Stöckelová et al. 2023). This has included both public debates and legal mandates to ensure Czech citizens' access to therapeutic spa care (Trnka 2017b).

Alongside the respite offered by spa care and other sojourns into ‘natural surroundings’, there is widespread emphasis on improving the air quality of one's immediate, domestic surroundings. In my previous research on childhood asthma, I found that although the Czech Republic and New Zealand had similar guidelines on mitigating asthma by eliminating allergens and other environmental triggers, in the Czech Republic both respiratory physicians and families of patients paid comparatively greater attention to environmental solutions (Trnka 2017a). In urban domiciles, occupants will often engage in daily rituals of opening all the windows in a house or apartment to ventilate its rooms (even when the surrounding streets may be clogged with traffic and therefore have questionable air quality) and engage in extensive sweeping and mopping to remove dust. Urban residents are also often very aware of differences in air quality across specific neighbourhoods and sometimes, even street by street. While there are concerted efforts to improve respiratory health that directly target patients’ bodies, creating healthy living spaces is a widespread focal point of therapeutic concern for individuals, families, as well as the biomedical establishment.

These views draw on long‐established cultural distinctions between cities and ‘nature’ (příroda) in which the rural is often viewed as inherently healthy because it has not been damaged by industrial production (Trnka 2020). I first realised the strength of these associations during my initial months of researching how Czech scientists, respiratory physicians and families viewed childhood asthma. Repeatedly, I was told that asthma is caused by air pollution and that air pollution stems from industrial activity. Medical professionals and laypeople were generally taken aback when I explained that my interest in this topic was spurred by New Zealand's high childhood asthma rate: Affecting an estimated 20%–25% of New Zealand children, the nation has some of the highest childhood asthma rates in the developed world (OECD 2015). ‘But New Zealand doesn't have high levels of industrial air pollution, so how can it have so much asthma?’ was a refrain I heard from physicians, scientists and families alike.

The association between respiratory illness and industrial air pollution is so deeply rooted in cultural perspectives that a family physician prefaced an account of a young boy who lived in a rural area and found that his asthma improved when he moved into the city (and away from the allergens that triggered his breathing difficulties) with the declaration that this case would undoubtedly ‘shock and surprise’ me. As this example demonstrates, the emphasis on industrial causes leads to other factors of respiratory problems, such as natural allergens, including natural air pollutants, being minimised or politicised to the extent that they disappear from public consciousness and debate. As one researcher employed at the Institute of Experimental Medicine complained to me, it is nearly impossible for him to publish a paper on how young people's smoking habits contribute to their rate of respiratory illnesses, given the centre's focus on demonstrating the connections between respiratory disease and industrial pollution.

Although ‘nature’ is culturally imbued with inherent therapeutic qualities, there is also an awareness that ‘nature’ can and should be humanly cultivated. In cities, urban environmental activists engage in greenifying urban spaces by planting herb gardens along the pavements in downtown Ostrava (Alda 2023) and promoting community gardens in Prague. But rural spaces are also seen as in need of human care, be it by planting trees to minimise dust or introducing bees to keep plants flowering. Even though the promotion of ‘clean’ atmospheres in mountains and other rural areas is often attributed to nature itself being inherently ‘clean’, human action and citizen responsibility are seen as integral to the preservation of ‘natural’ atmospheres just as they are the basis of producing urban ones.

Cities continue to play a vital role here, with their unique organisational abilities often extended to nearby, rural areas. Arnika, for example, is headquartered in Prague but coordinates a tree planting programme in Silesia, a river basin biodiversity programme in Moravia and a widespread campaign to delimit the impact of waste incinerators around the nation. Ostrava's Čisté nebe was involved in a multination European project, funded by Erasmus+, to raise awareness in rural areas about air pollution. Running for 2 years (2018–2019), the Clean Air project supplied teachers in rural schools with educational material about air pollution and was explicitly envisioned as an urban effort to educate rural communities. As explained on the English‐language version of Čisté nebe's (n.d.) website:The project focuses on rural areas due to the fact that public awareness of the problem is much lower among rural communities compared with inhabitants of larger cities. Therefore, the project responds to awareness and education gap that is the most serious within rural areas.

Although rurally organised movements play their own roles, it is not uncommon for urban activists to extend their stewardship of educational projects beyond the city's parameters.

Conclusion

Czech environmental activists are keen to ask who or what is responsible for the atmosphere. Notably, this includes the atmosphere of political affect, in particular, what motivates people to engage in collective political struggle. As the recent literature on affective atmospheres has demonstrated, places can create emotions, sensations and other affects. Places also create the air or atmospheres that envelop and permeate them. Czech environmental activists emphasise both their activities implicitly and explicitly, promoting collective identities and responsibilities, with urban infrastructures emerging as not only focal points of concern but also participants in providing the groundwork for political activism. Moreover, with the recent shift away from targeted campaigns against industrial pollution, the whole mode of urban living has become an activist focus, highlighting the city as the infrastructure for a range of modalities of well‐being.

Globally, there has been increasing interest in examining the role of human activity in creating what were previously taken to be (in many Western epistemologies) ‘natural phenomena’ (Latour [1991] 1993). This is reflected in wide‐ranging scholarship on the Anthropocene, climate change, human impacts on weather systems (e.g., Naylor 2024) and even the perceived division between the earth and the sky (Ingold 2007). Such concerns are mirrored in popular discourse in debates over government control of natural disasters and heated exchanges between climate change activists and climate change deniers. At the same time, we are increasingly aware of the agency of nonhuman actors in shaping environmental conditions. One area of increasing public focus is how urban infrastructure impacts the atmosphere. As cities expand to house growing populations, today providing shelter for 55% of the world’s population but with this percentage expected to rise to 70% by 2050 (United Nations Statistics Division 2023), scholarly and public interest in cities' roles in producing atmospheres will increase.

A well‐established area of sociology, anthropology and geography, the field of environmental health provides productive avenues for examining the (often contested) health impacts of environmental degradation (Catton and Dunlap 1978; Phillips 2015; Fox and Alldred 2016). In the area of respiratory health, this includes research on air pollution, climate change and pesticides, as well as on community organising and social activism in response to them (e.g., Harper 2004; P. Brown 2007; S. D. Brown et al. 2012; Fortun et al. 2014; Gabrys 2020; Vallée 2023). Focusing on cities as atmospheric infrastructures has the analytical benefit of foregrounding the human‐made qualities of air, prompting questions of accountability and possibilities for change. It compels us to move beyond consideration of atmospheres as something ephemeral and hard to pin down that currently permeates much of the literature on affective atmospheres, towards highlighting and questioning how atmospheres are created—both the affective atmospheres that enable politics and those that supply the air we breathe—and the interrelation between them.

Although air pollution constitutes a ‘planetary health’ problem, people's sense of their lived environments and engagements in political activism frequently foreground ‘their city’ as a recognised place, site of political struggle (Gabrys 2020) and the basis of a unique, shared social identity. Highlighting the collective unit of the city thus allows us to assess the challenges of air pollution on a scale with which many people readily identify, be they Praguers or Ostravans, Delhiites or New Yorkers. Rather than viewing respiratory health as individualised, population‐specific or on a global‐environmental scale, it recasts attention to the urban infrastructures that create and ameliorate polluted environments. As urban spaces become even more widespread, considering cities as atmospheric infrastructures thus contributes a new approach to urban sustainability that addresses not only human and ecological well‐being within, and beyond, the city but also how cities themselves can act as viable forms of life.
