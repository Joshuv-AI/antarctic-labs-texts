The asymmetric effect of social connection and the cumulative damage of comorbidities on physical and mental health of older Virginians
Authors: Newton Ochoki, Lynn Wiles, Peijia Zha, Vince Falco, Carielle Joy Rio, Jonathan Romero, Leorey N Saligan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study explored relationships of social connection with physical and mental health in older adults, specifically examining the effects of comorbidities on these relationships and the influence of sociodemographic variables on the strength of these associations.MethodsThis cross-sectional study enrolled 174 community-dwelling adults aged 60 years and older residing in Hampton Roads, Virginia, USA. Data were collected from April to August 2025. Hierarchical linear regression analyses examined the associations of social connection with physical and mental health. Moderated mediation analysis examined if comorbidities mediated these relationships and whether sociodemographic factors moderate the strengths of these associations.ResultsIn hierarchical linear regression models, comorbidity burden was significantly associated with poorer mental and physical health, explaining an additional 3% and 10% of the variance, respectively, beyond sociodemographic factors. Moderated mediation analysis revealed social connection had an indirect effect on mental health, where social connection was associated with higher comorbidities, which in turn was associated with poorer mental health, among Black relative to White participants (b = −0.80, 95% CI = −1.85, −0.02).ConclusionA conditional indirect pathway linked social connection to mental health through comorbidity burden among Black older adults, though the incremental contribution of social connection was minimal.Keywords: Social connection, Biological aging, Health disparities, Comorbidities

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates the global population of older adults aged 60 and above at around 1 billion, with the United States (US) accounting for approximately 54.1 million (WHO, 2021). In the US, life expectancy is projected to increase from 78.3 years in 2022 to 80.4 years in 2050 (Mokdad et al., 2024). This demographic shift underscores an urgent need to reorient healthcare systems toward meeting the complex needs of older adults (Gianfredi et al., 2025).

As people age, physiological changes increase vulnerability to physical and mental health challenges, significantly impacting daily functioning and quality of life (Hemmeter and Ngamsri, 2022). Common physical challenges include sensory impairments along with chronic conditions such as diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and musculoskeletal conditions, which may lead to reduced mobility and endurance (Aïdoud et al., 2023; Sourtzi et al., 2019). Older adults also commonly experience mental health challenges such as depression, anxiety, and cognitive decline (Raihan et al., 2024). These mental health issues are further compounded by aging, as the risk of neurodegenerative diseases such as dementia, particularly Alzheimer's disease, increases significantly with advancing age (Manly et al., 2022).

There is a growing body of literature linking social isolation to poor physical function and mental health among older adults (Barnes et al., 2022, Philip et al., 2020). The prevalence of social isolation among older adults varies, but it has been reported at about 33% in one meta-analysis that included countries such as the USA, Germany, and Japan (Ran et al., 2024). Recent literature suggests that health outcomes associated with social isolation are greatly influenced by transitional changes into and out of social isolation, as well as by persistent isolation (Kim and Hwang, 2024). In the 12 year longitudinal study by Kim and Hwang (2024) with a two year transitional period into and out of isolation, older adults who transitioned out of social isolation,which is akin to social connection, exhibited very minimal cognitive decline compared to those who transitioned into social isolation. Similarly, in another 17 year longitudinal study by Kim et al. (2026), transitioning out of social isolation did not significantly affect the frailty status of older adults. The transitional period in this study was also two years. Persistent isolation, on the other hand, in the study by (Kim et al., 2026) led to a three times likelihood of developing frailty, while another study by Nakagomi et al. (2023) found that poor interaction with friends over three years was associated with higher odds of developing functional disability and being hopeless. These studies not only demonstrate that social isolation is not static but also provide evidence explaining why years of biological injury lead to harmful health outcomes. This principle is referred as “asymmetric effect,” and it explains why, once biological injury has occurred, current social connections are usually insufficient to reverse the damage (Kim et al., 2026; Kim and Hwang, 2024).

Since current social connections can make it difficult to assess meaningful health outcomes, it is important to understand the mechanisms by which social isolation leads to poor health outcomes. Kwon and Kim (2026) report that isolation leads to poor health outcomes through comorbidity burden that is caused by biological aging. This happens when isolation dysregulates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, thereby amplifying stress, as evidenced by inflammatory markers such as cortisol and interleukin-6 (IL-6). This stress triggers behavioral and psychological changes in the body, such as poor sleep, depressive symptoms, physical inactivity, and even smoking or alcohol consumption, all of which accelerate biological aging and cause comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, and kidney diseases (Cudjoe et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2026).

Studies are now also revealing that inequities in sociodemographic factors moderate the influence of comorbidities on health outcomes via the degree of biological injury they impose (Esemezie et al., 2026). Inequities such as illiteracy, racism, low income, and unemployment not only lead to a lack of access to healthcare, healthy food, and safe housing, but they equally contribute to biological aging through chronic stress, which triggers further development of comorbidities (Bell and Ferraro, 2025; Kamis et al., 2025).

Owing to observed differences in health outcomes associated with the transitional nature of social isolation, it is important to understand the relationship between different transitional isolation states and physical and mental health. Specifically, this study examines the relationship between social connection and these health outcomes. Guided by the cumulative disadvantage theory (Dannefer, 2003), it also explores the mechanisms linking social connection to physical and mental health by investigating whether comorbidities mediate these relationships and if sociodemographic factors moderate them.

Methods

Study design and sample

This is an institutional review board-approved cross-sectional study (Protocol number: 2233290–3). The study ensured protection of human subjects through maintaining their safety and privacy.

Data were collected from April to August 2025. Eligible participants included adults aged 60 years or older, residing in Hampton Roads, Virginia, USA, and able to provide written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were a recent psychiatric crisis related to major depression and alcohol/drug dependence requiring hospitalization. The sample size was 174 older adults.

Measures

Physical and mental health were measured using the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS®) Global Health short form version 1.0. In this tool, the raw sum of physical and mental health scores is converted to T-scores. The T-scores range from 16.2 to 67.7, with higher scores indicating better physical and mental health (Broderick et al., 2013).

The National Institutes of Health Biomedical Research Informatics Computing System (BRICS) Social Connection and Social Isolation instrument (BRICS, 2025) measured self- reported scores of social connection and social isolation along a single continuum. Scores ranged from 0 to 4, with higher scores indicating greater social connection and lower scores indicating lower social connection. Comorbidity burden was measured using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), a validated measure of disease burden based on the presence and severity of chronic conditions (Charlson et al., 2022). The CCI score ranged from 0, indicating low comorbidity burden, to 5 or greater, indicating high comorbidity burden.

Sociodemographic variables included age, sex, race, income, education level, and insurance status (insured/not insured).

Theoretical framework: cumulative disadvantage theory

This study adopted the cumulative disadvantage theory (Dannefer, 2003) to explore whether comorbidities mediate the relationship between social connection and physical and mental health, and to determine if sociodemographic factors moderate these relationships. The theory posits that the disadvantageous accumulation of risk shapes life-course trajectories. We conceptualized the outcomes of physical and mental health as life-course trajectories. The disadvantageous accumulated risk was conceptualized as comorbidity, which served as a mediator between physical and mental health in the study. Comorbidity arises from the accumulation of pathophysiologic processes over the course of life. The theory also includes the concept of interindividual divergence, whereby people born around the same time experience different social systems, sociodemographic factors, and even structural differences over the course of life, ultimately affecting their life course trajectories as well. We therefore conceptualized social connection as the predictor variable, while various sociodemographic factors were treated as moderating variables capable of producing different outcomes in different people. Our hypothesis is that social connection affects the physical and mental health of older adults through comorbidities, but this association differs by sociodemographic factors.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the study variables. Bivariate analyses were conducted to examine associations between study variables and physical and mental health outcomes using Pearson's correlation coefficient, independent-samples t-tests, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate. Hierarchical linear regression analyses were conducted to examine whether the addition of social connection contributed to the prediction of mental and physical health beyond sociodemographic factors and comorbidity. More specifically, study variables were entered into models. Model 1 included sociodemographic factors only, Model 2 added comorbidity, while the final model (Model 3) further included social connection. This approach allowed us to examine the incremental contribution of each block to explaining variance in physical and mental health outcomes.

To examine whether social connection was indirectly associated with physical and mental health through comorbidity, and whether this indirect relationship differed across sociodemographic factors, we conducted moderated mediation analysis using Hayes (2018) PROCESS macro (Model 14). Although social connection did not contribute additional variance in the regression models, we proceeded with moderated mediation because contemporary mediation analysis does not require a significant total or direct effect to test indirect effects, opposing pathways or conditional effects may suppress the overall association (Hayes, 2018). Model 14 tests second stage of moderation, where sociodemographic factors moderate the path from comorbidity (mediator) to physical and mental health outcomes, consistent with the cumulative disadvantage theory (Dannefer, 2003) which posits that sociodemographic factors condition health outcomes. Bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals (10,000 resamples) were used to evaluate indirect effects at different levels of the moderator(s) (±1 SD from the mean, or specific values for categorical moderators. Statistical significance for all analyses was set at p < .05. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS software, version 31.

Comorbidity score and several sociodemographic factors were associated with the mental and physical health scores of the older adults in the bivariate analysis. Mental health scores were significantly associated with race (p = .04), income (p = .01), insurance status (p < .001), education level (p < .001), and comorbidity score (p = .01); whereas physical health scores were significantly associated with age (p = .04), sex(p = .01), race (p = .02), income (p < .001), education level (p < .001), and comorbidity score (p < .001). Since these variables demonstrated statistically significant associations, they were subsequently included in multivariable hierarchical linear regression models.

Hierarchical regressions predicting older adults mental and physical health in Hampton roads, Virginia (data collected: April–august 2025)

Hierarchical linear regression analyses were carried out to determine whether social connection predicted mental and physical health scores beyond sociodemographic factors and comorbidity.

The addition of comorbidities in Model 2 resulted in a statistically significant increase in explained variance (ΔR2 = 0.03, ΔF = 6.90, p < .001), indicating that comorbidity accounted for an additional 3% of the variance in mental health beyond sociodemographic factors. However, including social connection in Model 3 did not significantly improve the prediction of mental health. Additionally in Model 3, which was the final model, participants with an annual income of $50,000–$99,999 reported mental health scores that were 3.05 points lower than those earning $100,000 or more (b = −3.05, p = .04). Further, insured participants had mental health scores that were 5.43 points higher than uninsured participants (b = 5.43, p = .05). Similarly, participants who had high school education and below had mental health scores that were 4.12 points lower than those who had an undergraduate education (b = −4.12, p = .01). In addition, each additional comorbidity was associated with a 0.82-point decrease in mental health score (b = −0.82, p = .01). For physical health on the other hand, the addition of comorbidities in Model 2 also led to a statistically significant increase in explained variance (ΔR2 = 0.10, ΔF = 21.33, p < .001), indicating that comorbidities explained an additional 10% of the variance in physical health beyond sociodemographic factors. Adding social connection to Model 3 did not significantly improve the model. In the final model (Model 3), each additional comorbidity was associated with a 1.54-point decline in physical health score (b = −1.54, p < .001). Detailed results for all models are presented in Table 2.

Moderated mediation of social connection on older adults mental and physical health in Hampton roads, Virginia (data collected: April–august 2025)

Moderated mediation analyses were conducted to examine whether comorbidity burden mediated the association of social connection with mental and physical health, and whether this indirect pathway varied across key sociodemographic factors (race, income, insurance status, and education). Separate models were estimated for each sociodemographic moderator. Social connection was not directly associated with mental health scores (p = .17). However, a significant interaction between comorbidity burden and Black race indicated that the negative association between comorbidity burden and mental health was stronger among Black participants compared with White participants (b = −1.26, 95% CI = −2.51, −0.02). As shown in Table 3 and Fig. 1, mental health scores declined with increasing comorbidity across racial groups, with the steepest decline observed among Black participants. Consistent with this pattern, a significant moderated indirect effect was observed, such that greater social connection was associated with greater comorbidity, which in turn was associated with poorer mental health among Black participants relative to White participants (b = −0.80, 95% CI = −1.85, −0.02). In contrast, the moderated mediation analysis for physical health did not reveal any significant interactions between comorbidity and sociodemographic factors. Although comorbidity burden was independently associated with poorer physical health (p = .04), social connection did not show a significant direct or indirect association with physical health.

Discussion

We observed no direct association between social connection and mental or physical health. However, moderated mediation revealed a significant indirect path: social connection was associated with higher comorbidity burden, which in turn predicted poorer mental health, particularly among Black older adults compared with White participants. The significant indirect effect coexists despite the absence of incremental predictive value of social connection to health outcomes in our regression models because it was conditional and specific to Black participants, representing a subgroup mechanism rather than a broad predictive contribution. Supporting cumulative disadvantage theory (Dannefer, 2003), present social ties in underserved minority communities cannot fully buffer against systemic health inequities, financial strain, disease stigma, or healthcare barriers (Bazargan et al., 2023); Wu et al. (2025). Although, older adults report positive social connections through family, friends, neighbors, healthcare providers, and trusted institutions like churches (Clair et al., 2025), the quality of this support is often insufficient to overcome the broader effects of social and health inequities (Gnan et al., 2023).

Since our cross-sectional measure captured social connection at a single point, it could not account for years of accumulated pathophysiologic damage from past social isolation. This aligns with the “asymmetric effect,” wherein negative social ties and historical isolation exert a far stronger detrimental impact on health than present positive relationships can remediate (Kim and Hwang, 2024; Labianca and Brass, 2006). Biologically, past social isolation triggers persistent inflammatory cascades (e.g., IL-6, CRP), accelerating biological aging and chronic disease progression through psychological and behavioral pathways (Zhao et al., 2026). Consistent with prior research linking multimorbidity to physical decline and adverse mental health outcomes in late life (Zheng et al., 2025), these findings demonstrate that late-life support alone is insufficient to undo longstanding physiological damage.

Furthermore, sociodemographic factors significantly shaped these health trajectories. In hierarchical regression analyses, lower educational attainment (high school or less) predicted poorer mental health, whereas higher income and insurance coverage were protective. These results align with broader evidence linking lower education and income inequality to poorer mental health outcomes in older populations (Jester et al., 2023).

To address these complex disparities, interventions must adopt a dual approach. At the clinical and structural levels, efforts should prioritize expanding healthcare access, enhancing health literacy, and enforcing insurance parity in minority communities (Jimenez et al., 2022) alongside individual lifestyle-based risk modifications (Xie et al., 2022, CDC,2026). Additionally, innovative delivery models, such as telerehabilitation, offer cost-effective ways to reduce physical access barriers for older adults (Veras et al. (2025). To be effective, such initiatives must be culturally congruent, economically viable, gender-responsive, and broadly accessible (Birnbaum, 2023; Veras et al., 2025).

The study has several limitations. First, the BRICS Social Connection and Social Isolation instrument is not specific to older adults and may lack sensitivity to fully capture social isolation, as individual items were not significantly associated with health outcomes. Second, the small, single-region sample (N = 174) limits generalizability and statistical power to detect moderated mediation effects. This is particularly relevant given the small incremental variance explained by social connection in the regression models, which may reflect insufficient power to detect subgroup-specific effects. Third, a cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and longitudinal tracking of dynamic disease progression. For future research, as emerging technologies, such as artificial intelligence, become increasingly integrated into social interactions, future research should explore whether inclusion of artificial intelligence influence social interactions and health outcomes in older populations.

Conclusion

In this exploratory study, we observed a conditional indirect effect whereby social connection was indirectly associated with mental health through higher comorbidity burden, which was linked to poorer mental health among Black participants compared with White participants. However, the minimal incremental variance explained by social connection in the regression models suggests that, while statistically significant, this pathway may make a limited contribution to overall health outcomes. These findings highlight that the relationship between social connection and later-life health is complex and shaped by interacting factors, including sociodemographic conditions and chronic disease. Proactive efforts to promote meaningful social connection early in life, especially in disadvantaged communities, are vital to preventing the long-term health impacts of isolation.
