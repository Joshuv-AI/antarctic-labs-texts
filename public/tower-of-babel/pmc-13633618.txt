Coronary Sinus Electrogram Changes After Cavotricuspid Isthmus Ablation for Typical Atrial Flutter: What Is the Mechanism?
Authors: Shun Nakamura, Takashi Nakashima, Takahisa Ido, Shigekiyo Takahashi, Takuma Aoyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Typical atrial flutter was terminated and sinus rhythm was restored. We discussed the mechanism of changes in the coronary sinus atrial electrograms.

Case Presentation

A 60‐year‐old female with systemic scleroderma and interstitial pneumonia presented with a 2‐month history of persistent dyspnea and atrial flutter (AFL). Echocardiography showed a reduced left ventricular ejection fraction of 39% with mild mitral and tricuspid regurgitation.

The baseline rhythm was AFL with an atrial tachycardia‐cycle length of 256 ms. The catheter positions are shown in Figure 1A. Entrainment mapping and three‐dimensional electroanatomical mapping (CARTO, Biosense Webster Inc., Diamond Bar, CA, USA) confirmed typical counterclockwise AFL (Figure 1B,C). Following cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI) linear ablation, the AFL terminated and sinus rhythm was restored (Figure 2). Bidirectional CTI block was achieved. Discussion

The present case demonstrates an epicardial connection between the low right atrium (RA) and the CS musculature, as elucidated by detailed EGM analysis (Figure 3). During sinus rhythm post‐AFL termination, double potentials were recorded along the CS electrodes. The first component, characterized by dull, low‐amplitude signals, exhibited a proximal‐to‐distal activation sequence, representing far‐field endocardial left atrial (LA) potentials (arrowheads in Figure 3A). The second component consisted of sharp, high‐amplitude signals with a distal‐to‐proximal activation sequence, representing near‐field epicardial CS musculature potential (open arrowheads in Figure 3A). These two EGMs merged in the distal CS, suggesting an endocardial LA and CS musculature connection distally, but not proximally. However, these two components were superimposed during AFL (arrow in Figure 3B). During entrainment pacing from mid‐CS 7/8, the double potentials separated, and their activation sequence was reversed from that during sinus rhythm. The first component was near‐field epicardial CS musculature potential with a proximal‐to‐distal sequence (arrowheads in Figure 3C), while the second was far‐field endocardial LA potentials with a distal‐to‐proximal sequence (open arrowheads in Figure 3C).

Mechanistic insights into CS EGMs changes. (A) Intracardiac EGMs during sinus rhythm. (B) Intracardiac EGMs during AFL. (C) Entrainment mapping from CS 7/8. Abbreviations as in Figures 1 and 2.

Another critical mechanistic insight was obtained when premature atrial contractions (PACs) were mechanically induced in the RA by the OCTARAY mapping catheter during AFL (Figure 4). During these PACs, the double potentials became separated, with the near‐field CS musculature EGM (open arrowheads in Figure 4) preceding the far‐field LA EGM (arrowheads in Figure 4). These observations confirm that the PACs conducted directly from the RA to the CS musculature via an epicardial bridge. Given that the double‐potential intervals and the activation sequence of the far‐field LA EGMs varied during mechanical PACs, multiple alternative pathways likely existed between the CS musculature and the LA.

Intracardiac EGMs of mechanical PACs induced by the OCTARAY mapping catheter during AFL. Abbreviations as in Figures 1 and 2.

Based on these findings, we concluded that the LA was connected to the CS musculature at the distal CS, and that an epicardial connection existed between the RA and the CS musculature. CTI ablation eliminated this low RA‐to‐CS musculature connection. Several limitations should be noted in this case. First, because the baseline rhythm was AFL, CS EGMs could not be recorded during sinus rhythm prior to ablation. Second, although endocardial bidirectional block across the CTI line was confirmed, whether the epicardial connection between the RA and the CS musculature was blocked remained unverified. Third, the precise insertion site (epicardial‐to‐endocardial or endocardial‐to‐epicardial) was not fully clarified. Nonetheless, we believe that these limitations do not undermine the validity of our interpretation.

While RA‐to‐CS epicardial connections have been documented in animal models, clinical electrophysiological observations in humans remain rare [1].

Furthermore, reports describing this connection through the analysis of intracardiac EGMs within the CS have seldom been reported. Our case is noteworthy because the presence of a low RA‐to‐CS epicardial bridge was clearly demonstrated by evaluating EGM morphologies and activation patterns during both sinus rhythm and typical AFL.
