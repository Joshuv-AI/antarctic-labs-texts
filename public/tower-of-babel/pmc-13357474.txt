C/EBPγ interacts with C/EBPα and activates the β-catenin pathway in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
Authors: Xiaoyue Pan, Cong Xia, Yulong Gan, Ying Cao, Man Zhao, Huibing Liu, Yanlin Zhou, Yuqi Wang, Zhongzheng Li, Bin Li, Lan Wang, Guoying Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIdiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a progressive and fatal interstitial lung disease characterized by an abnormal epithelial‒mesenchymal transition (EMT) and fibroblast activation, although the molecular mechanisms driving these processes remain unclear. Here, we report that the expression of CCAAT enhancer binding protein γ (C/EBPγ), a transcription factor, is significantly upregulated in lung tissues from patients with IPF and the lungs of mice with bleomycin-induced fibrosis. In A549 epithelial cells, C/EBPγ overexpression promoted the EMT, as indicated by decreased E-cadherin expression and increased N-cadherin and vimentin expression. In MRC-5 fibroblasts, C/EBPγ overexpression increased cell migration and upregulated the expression of fibrotic markers, including collagen I, fibronectin, and α-SMA. Mechanistically, C/EBPγ activated the β-catenin pathway by stabilizing β-catenin through the transcriptional repression of AXIN1, a key component of the degradation complex. This repression occurred via an interaction with C/EBPα, antagonizing its promotion of AXIN1 expression, as confirmed by ChIP‒qPCR, luciferase assays, coimmunoprecipitation, immunofluorescence staining, and rescue experiments. In vivo, adeno-associated virus-mediated C/EBPγ overexpression aggravated bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis in mice, increasing collagen deposition, inflammation, and β-catenin expression, whereas C/EBPγ knockdown alleviated these changes. Collectively, these findings suggest that the downregulation of C/EBPγ expression attenuates fibrosis progression through the C/EBPα–AXIN1–β-catenin axis, underscoring its involvement in β-catenin pathway regulation and advancing the understanding of IPF pathogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06245-7.Keywords: Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, C/EBPγ, β-catenin signaling, AXIN1, C/EBPα

Introduction

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic interstitial lung disorder characterized by persistent alveolar injury and dysregulated tissue repair, leading to progressive fibrosis and a decline in pulmonary function [1, 2]. IPF predominantly affects middle-aged and elderly individuals, particularly men over 65 years of age, with its incidence steadily increasing worldwide [3, 4]. This disease is associated with an unfavorable prognosis, with a median survival of approximately 3–5 years following diagnosis [5], severely impairing patients’ quality of life and imposing a significant burden on health care systems [3]. While the etiology is multifactorial—involving environmental exposure, genetic predisposition, and immune dysregulation [6]—current treatments, such as pirfenidone and nintedanib, primarily aim to slow progression, although their efficacy varies and no curative options exist [7, 8]. Recently, the oral preferential PDE4B inhibitor nerandomilast (JASCAYD) has been approved in China, as supported by phase II and III clinical data from patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis showing an attenuation of lung function decline alongside anti-inflammatory/antifibrotic effects [9], with gastrointestinal adverse events remaining key tolerability consideration typical of PDE4 inhibition [10]. Nevertheless, longer-term safety data and real-world effectiveness remain to be further established. However, further elucidation of the underlying mechanisms and development of targeted therapies are essential to improve long-term outcomes.

Multiple signaling cascades, such as the TGF-β/Smad, Wnt/β-catenin, Notch, and inflammation-associated pathways, are disrupted during the pathogenesis of IPF [11]. These signaling cascades play vital roles in controlling cellular differentiation, proliferation, apoptosis, and the balance of extracellular matrix (ECM) production and degradation, with notable crosstalk, such as that involving TGF-β, enhancing Wnt/β-catenin signaling to promote ECM deposition and fibroblast activation and contributing to IPF progression [12]. Among these pathways, Wnt/β-catenin signaling is particularly notable because of its pivotal role in promoting fibrosis progression. Wnt signaling stabilizes β-catenin, facilitating its nuclear accumulation and activation of downstream genes encoding ECM components and profibrotic factors [13]. The upregulation of multiple components of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway has been observed in the lungs of patients with IPF and is correlated with disease severity [14]. Targeting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway shows therapeutic promise; for instance, the inhibitor ICG-001 reduces collagen deposition by up to 50% and improves lung function in bleomycin-induced models [15]. Our recent work demonstrated that TRIOBP modulates miR-29b expression to influence the β-catenin pathway, underscoring its critical role in IPF pathogenesis [16]. These findings support the central role of β-catenin in IPF and suggest that it is a viable therapeutic target. In studies of β-catenin signaling, HLY78 is commonly used as a pharmacological activator of canonical Wnt signaling. This small-molecule agonist enhances β-catenin activity by promoting the interaction between Dishevelled (Dvl) and LRP6, and is widely applied in mechanistic studies to investigate pathway activation and downstream biological effects [17, 18].

CCAAT enhancer binding protein γ (C/EBPγ), a relatively understudied member of the C/EBP family, is the smallest and simplest family member and comprises only a leucine zipper and basic region without activation domains (ADs) or negative regulatory domains (RDs) [19]. This minimal structure has long been thought to constrain its functional potential. However, emerging evidence has revealed the involvement of C/EBPγ in the pathogenesis of various diseases, highlighting its broader significance [20–23]. Its role in fibrotic disorders, however, remains poorly defined. In skin fibrosis, C/EBPγ indirectly promotes fibroblast activation and matrix remodeling [24]. While direct evidence in pulmonary fibrosis is lacking, previous work has shown that C/EBPγ is upregulated by IL-1β in LPS- or immunoglobulin-mediated lung inflammation models, conferring protection through decreased vascular permeability, restricted neutrophil infiltration, and reduced inflammatory mediator release [25]. Furthermore, C/EBPγ overexpression has been shown to attenuate inflammatory responses by antagonizing the transcriptional activity of other family members, such as C/EBPβ and C/EBPδ [25] and downregulating cytokines, such as IL-6 [26]. Consequently, C/EBPγ is often viewed as an anti-inflammatory regulator, prompting speculation that it might mitigate fibrosis via immunomodulation. However, this hypothesis has not been experimentally validated in pulmonary fibrosis, and critical gaps remain regarding its functional role in lung fibroblasts and its potential interactions with core fibrogenic signaling cascades. Importantly, fibrosis differs from acute inflammation; although inflammation can trigger remodeling, fibrotic progression involves sustained transcriptional changes, an aberrant epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and extracellular matrix accumulation. Thus, whether C/EBPγ acts protectively or paradoxically to drive fibrosis in chronic injury settings remains a key unresolved question with clinical implications.

This study investigated the potential involvement of C/EBPγ in pulmonary fibrosis, as its role in this process remains unknown. Through immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence analyses, we found that C/EBPγ expression was markedly increased in the lungs of IPF patients and similarly increased in the lungs of mice with bleomycin-induced fibrosis. We performed loss- and gain-of-function experiments in A549 and MRC-5 cells to clarify the function and molecular mechanisms of C/EBPγ in pulmonary fibrosis. C/EBPγ overexpression enhanced the EMT in A549 cells and fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition (FMT) in MRC-5 cells. Mechanistically, C/EBPγ enhanced β-catenin signaling by stabilizing the protein and promoting its nuclear accumulation. Rather than directly regulating β-catenin transcription, C/EBPγ interacted with C/EBPα to repress AXIN1 expression, thereby limiting β-catenin phosphorylation and subsequent degradation. Consistent with these observations, adeno-associated virus-driven C/EBPγ overexpression exacerbated bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis in vivo, whereas C/EBPγ silencing mitigated the fibrotic response.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

The human alveolar epithelial cell line A549 and the lung fibroblast cell line MRC-5 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). A549 cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12 medium, MRC-5 cells in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM), and HEK293T cells in high-glucose DMEM, each supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. All cells were maintained under humidified conditions at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. All cell lines were regularly examined and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

When cultures reached nearly 80% confluence, cells were detached with 0.25% trypsin–EDTA and reseeded into six-well plates (Corning, NY, USA) at a density of 1.0 × 10⁵ cells per well. Transfection was carried out once cultures reached about 70–80% confluence to ensure both high efficiency and good cell survival. For stimulation assays, cells were deprived of serum for 24 h before being exposed to specific agents, such as recombinant human transforming growth factor-β1 (rhTGF-β1) or bleomycin (BLM), depending on the experimental setup.

Plasmids and transfection

Human expression plasmids pcDNA3.1-C/EBPγ, pcDNA3.1-C/EBPα, and the truncation mutant pcDNA3.1-C/EBPγ ΔbZIP, as well as FLAG-tagged AXIN1 expression plasmids and their corresponding empty vectors, were transfected into A549 and MRC-5 cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

For gene silencing experiments, small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting human C/EBPγ was purchased from RiboBio Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). Transfections were carried out using the corresponding siRNA transfection reagent provided by the same manufacturer, following the recommended protocol.

Western blot analysis

Lung tissues and cultured cells were lysed in SDS buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein levels in the supernatant were quantified with a BCA assay kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Equal protein samples were resolved on 8–12% SDS–PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Immunoreactive bands were detected using the Odyssey Fc Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA). The following antibodies were used: C/EBPγ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #PA5-101128), β-actin (Affinity, #T0022), α-SMA (Cell Signaling Technology, #19245), COL1A1 (Cell Signaling Technology, #72026), E-cadherin (Cell Signaling Technology, 14472 S), N-cadherin (Cell Signaling Technology, #13116), Vimentin (Proteintech, 10366-1-AP), Fibronectin (Cell Signaling Technology, #26836), β-catenin (Proteintech, #51067-2), phos-β-catenin (T41 + S45) (Abcam, #ab81305), C/EBPα (Proteintech, #29388-1), AXIN1 (Proteintech, #16541-1-AP), GSK3β (Proteintech, #51065-1-AP), Phospho-GSK3A (Tyr279)/GSK3B (Tyr216) (Proteintech, #29125-1-AP), CSNK1A1 (Proteintech, #55192-1-AP).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT–PCR)

For transcriptional analysis of tissue and cultured cells, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Takara, Dalian, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol and previously published methods [27]. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was generated using the GoScript™ Reverse Transcription System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT–PCR) was carried out on a LightCycler 480 instrument (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) with SYBR Green Master Mix (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and cDNA as the template. Relative mRNA expression were determined using the 2^−ΔΔCt approach. Primer sequences applied in this study are provided in Table 1.

Collagen gel contraction assay

The contractile ability of MRC-5 fibroblasts was assessed using a collagen gel contraction model based on rat tail collagen type I (Corning, Corning Inc., NY, USA). MRC-5 cells were subjected to the corresponding transfection or pharmacological treatments prior to the assay.

Collagen mixtures were prepared on ice by combining rat tail Collagen I (3 mg/mL), 10× PBS, serum-free medium, and 0.1 M NaOH to adjust the pH to approximately 7.4. The concentration of collagen was finally adjusted to 1.5 mg/mL. Equal volumes of the collagen mixture and cell suspension (5 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were gently mixed and dispensed into 24-well plates (0.5 mL per well). Gels were allowed to polymerize at 37 °C for 30–60 min, then covered with 0.5 mL of complete medium containing 10% FBS. After incubation for 24–48 h, the gels were gently released from the well edges using a sterile pipette tip to permit contraction. Images were taken at 0 h and again at 24–48 h with an inverted microscope, and gel areas were measured using ImageJ (v1.52q). Contraction was quantified as the percentage reduction in area, calculated by (initial area − final area) / initial area × 100%, where a higher percentage reflected greater contractile strength.

Wound healing assay

A sterile marker was used to draw reference lines on the bottom of a sterile six-well plate. A549 and MRC-5 cells were seeded into the wells and cultured overnight. After transfection with either C/EBPγ plasmid or siRNA, and once the cells reached 90–100% confluence, a straight wound was made across the cell monolayer with a sterile pipette tip. The wells were gently rinsed with PBS to clear away detached cells and debris. Images of the scratched area were taken at 0, 24, and 48 h using an inverted phase-contrast microscope.

Luciferase reporter assay

MRC-5 cells were co-transfected with the appropriate plasmids and the corresponding luciferase reporter plasmids (TOPFlash-CP rapid-degradation luciferase reporter plasmid, Beyotime, Shanghai, China, the AXIN1 promoter luciferase reporter plasmid). After 48 h of incubation, luciferase activity was determined using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. In brief, cells underwent one wash with PBS before being lysed in 1× Passive Lysis Buffer (PLB, 100 µL per well in a 24-well plate) for 15 min at room temperature with gentle shaking. The lysates were then collected, centrifuged at 4 °C to remove debris, and the collected supernatants were then transferred to 96-well plates for luminescence measurement.

Luciferase Assay Reagent II (LAR II) was prepared by reconstituting the lyophilized substrate in Luciferase Assay Buffer II and stored at − 80 °C until use. Before measurement, both LAR II and cell lysates were equilibrated to room temperature. For each sample, 20 µL of cell lysate was mixed with 100 µL of LAR II solution, and luminescence was recorded immediately using a microplate luminometer. Relative light units (RLU) representing luciferase activity were adjusted according to total protein levels quantified via the BCA assay. Non-transfected controls (NTC) were included to adjust for background signals. All steps were carried out under low-light conditions to minimize nonspecific luminescence interference.

ChIP-qPCR

ChIP–qPCR was performed as described [16]. Approximately 1 × 10⁷ treated MRC-5 cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min. The reaction was quenched by adding 125 mM glycine for 10 min, followed by two washes with ice-cold PBS. The cell pellets were then resuspended in 1 mL of cytolysis buffer, mixed gently, and incubated on ice for 10–15 min with occasional inversion every 2 min. Cells were centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 5 min at 4 ℃, the supernatant was discarded, and the nuclear pellet was resuspended in 500 µL of nuclear lysis buffer (1% SDS, 10 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.1, containing 1×protease inhibitor cocktail). The chromatin fragments were sheared to an average size of approximately 250 bp or smaller. Soluble chromatin was collected by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 10 min and diluted 1:10 with dilution buffer (150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.1, 2 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail). Chromatin was incubated with Protein A/G beads at 4 ℃ overnight. Rabbit IgG served as a negative control. The immune complexes were sequentially washed twice with low-salt buffer (150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.1, 2 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS), twice with high-salt buffer (500 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.1, 2 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS), twice with LiCl buffer (250 mM LiCl, 1% NP-40, 1% deoxycholate, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.1), and twice with TE buffer (pH 8.0). Beads were eluted twice with 150 µL elution buffer (0.1 M NaHCO₃, 1% SDS) by vortexing at 70 ℃ and 1000 rpm for 10 min using a Thermo Mixer C (Eppendorf). The eluates were combined and incubated at 65 ℃ overnight to reverse the cross-linking. DNA fragments were purified using QIAquick Spin Columns and quantified by Nanodrop. Antibodies used were anti-C/EBPα (Proteintech, #29388-1) and anti-IgG (Beyotime, A7016). Primers used for ChIP–qPCR are listed in Table 2.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunocytochemistry (ICC)

Lung samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sliced into 4 μm sections. After deparaffinization and rehydration, the slices were incubated with an endogenous peroxidase blocking solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 10 min to quench peroxidase activity. Antigen retrieval was carried out in Tris-EDTA buffer (Beyotime) at 100 °C for 10 min. After PBS rinsing, samples were blocked with blocking buffer at 37 °C for 30 min, then incubated with primary antibodies against C/EBPγ at room temperature. Following additional PBS washes, biotin-conjugated secondary antibodies (Beyotime) were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Staining was developed using a DAB substrate, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Images were obtained with a light microscope.

For immunocytochemistry, cells were seeded onto poly-L-lysine–coated coverslips, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, and subsequently permeabilized using 0.03% Triton X-100 for 5 min. After three PBS rinses, they were blocked with 5% goat serum for 30 min and incubated with primary antibodies targeting β-catenin, HA, or C/EBPα overnight at 4 °C. The following day, cells were exposed to Alexa Fluor 488– (green) or Alexa Fluor 594– (red) conjugated secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 1 h. Nuclei were stained with DAPI, and fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence or confocal microscope (LSM 700, Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

Bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis and human IPF samples

All animal procedures were performed according to the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) guidelines of Henan Normal University (Approval No. SMKX-2118BS1018) and adhered to national regulations and the principles of the Association for the Study of Animal Behavior. Male C57BL/6 N mice (8–10 weeks old) were obtained from Charles River Laboratories (Beijing, China) maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions.

In the bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis model, mice were administered a single intratracheal dose of 50 µL bleomycin (1.5 U/kg; Kyorin Pharmaceutical, Tokyo, Japan) dissolved in PBS, or PBS alone as a vehicle control. Mice were euthanized at 21 days post-bleomycin administration via intraperitoneal injection of urethane, and lung tissues were harvested for further analysis.

For AAV administration in vivo, male C57BL/6 N mice were acclimatized for 3 days prior to experimentation and randomly assigned to four groups (n = 8 per group): saline, AAV+saline, BLM-induced pulmonary fibrotic, and AAV + BLM. Recombinant AAV2/9 vectors carrying shRNA targeting C/EBPγ (AAV-shC/EBPγ) or C/EBPγ overexpression constructs (AAV-C/EBPγ) were administered via intratracheal instillation at a dose of 2.5 × 10¹¹ viral genomes (v.g.) per mouse in a total volume of 50 µL. Seven days after AAV delivery, mice received a single intratracheal instillation of bleomycin (1.5 U/kg). Body weight was monitored every other day throughout the experimental period. Mice were euthanized 21 days after bleomycin administration, and lung tissues were harvested for subsequent analyses.

Lung tissue specimens obtained from patients diagnosed with IPF and non-fibrotic controls were sourced from Henan Provincial Chest Hospital, in accordance with the ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT clinical practice guidelines. The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Henan Provincial Chest Hospital (Approval No. 2019-05-07), and all participants provided written informed consent prior to surgery. All procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed data, whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed data. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by appropriate post hoc multiple-comparisons tests (Tukey or Dunnett, as applicable). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The specific statistical tests used are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

C/EBPγ is upregulated in the lungs of IPF patients and mice with bleomycin-induced fibrosis

The expression of C/EBPγ in lung tissues from patients and bleomycin-induced mouse models was examined to determine its potential involvement in IPF. Immunohistochemical staining revealed a pronounced increase in C/EBPγ expression in lung tissues from IPF patients compared with non-IPF control subjects (Fig. 1A). Consistently, elevated C/EBPγ expression was also observed in lung sections from bleomycin-treated mice (Fig. 1B). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed the upregulation of C/EBPγ, which strongly colocalized with α-SMA-positive regions in lung tissues from patients with IPF (Fig. 1C). Similarly, immunofluorescence staining showed a significant increase in C/EBPγ expression in the lungs of mice with bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis (Fig. 1D).

C/EBPγ promotes the EMT and activates fibrosis in vitro

Given the observed upregulation of C/EBPγ in fibrotic lung tissues, we next sought to clarify its functional role in pulmonary fibrosis. C/EBPγ siRNA-mediated knockdown experiments were conducted in A549 cells. Western blots showed that silencing C/EBPγ in A549 cells led to an increase in E-cadherin expression, which was accompanied by decreased expression of N-cadherin and vimentin (Fig. 2A). Conversely, C/EBPγ overexpression led to a decrease in E-cadherin expression and a concomitant upregulation of N-cadherin and vimentin expression at both the mRNA (Fig. 2B) and protein levels (Fig. 2C). In MRC-5 cells, C/EBPγ overexpression also increased the expression of the fibrotic markers fibronectin and α-SMA, as shown by Western blotting (Fig. 2D), and collagen contraction assays further confirmed that C/EBPγ overexpression increased the contractile capacity of MRC-5 cells (Fig. 2E).

Consistent with these molecular changes, wound healing assays demonstrated that C/EBPγ knockdown markedly reduced wound closure (Fig. 2F), whereas C/EBPγ overexpression significantly increased the migration of A549 cells (Fig. 2G) and MRC-5 cells (Fig. 2H). Taken together, these findings indicate that C/EBPγ promotes the EMT in A549 cells and FMT in MRC-5 cells, potentially contributing to the progression of pulmonary fibrosis.

C/EBPγ promotes the EMT and FMT through the β-catenin pathway

Based on the preceding findings, we speculated that C/EBPγ might exacerbate fibrosis progression. We examined the potential mechanisms by which C/EBPγ regulates fibrosis by analyzing alterations in fibrosis-related signaling pathways under both knockdown and overexpression conditions to further test this hypothesis. Western blots showed that β-catenin protein levels changed in parallel with C/EBPγ expression (Fig. 3A). Consistently, luciferase reporter assays confirmed that β-catenin signaling was enhanced following C/EBPγ overexpression (Fig. 3B).

Given that β-catenin plays a pivotal role as a profibrotic mediator, we next investigated whether C/EBPγ exerts its effects through this pathway. C/EBPγ was silenced or overexpressed in A549 and MRC-5 cells, followed by treatment with β-catenin agonists or inhibitors. HLY78, a β-catenin pathway agonist that binds the DIX domain of Axin and enhances the Axin–LRP6 interaction, was used to activate β-catenin signaling. In A549 cells, the administration of HLY78 suppressed E-cadherin expression but increased the expression of N-cadherin and vimentin, and silencing C/EBPγ partially reversed these changes (Fig. 3C). Conversely, treatment with the β-catenin inhibitor MSAB effectively restored the changes in the expression of EMT markers induced by C/EBPγ overexpression (Fig. 3D).

The FMT was also observed in MRC-5 cells, and C/EBPγ knockdown attenuated the upregulation of the expression of fibrotic markers induced by HLY78, including fibronectin, collagen I, and α-SMA (Fig. 3E). In contrast, MSAB treatment reversed the increases in the expression of these markers caused by C/EBPγ overexpression (Fig. 3F). Consistent results were obtained from the collagen gel contraction assay, in which MSAB reduced the increase in collagen contractility induced by C/EBPγ overexpression (Fig. 3G). Collectively, these findings suggest that C/EBPγ promotes fibrosis progression by modulating the β-catenin signaling pathway and that the activation or inhibition of this pathway can partially offset the effects of C/EBPγ knockdown or overexpression on FMT and EMT-related marker expression.

In addition to altering β-catenin protein levels, C/EBPγ also increased the nuclear translocation of β-catenin. Immunofluorescence staining of both A549 (Fig. 3H) and MRC-5 (Fig. 3I) cells showed increased β-catenin accumulation predominantly within the nuclei of C/EBPγ-positive (HA-tag-positive) cells following C/EBPγ overexpression. Since nuclear β-catenin accumulation and subsequent target gene activation constitute major downstream events of Wnt signaling, these results further confirm that C/EBPγ enhances β-catenin signaling activity, thereby contributing to fibrosis progression.

C/EBPγ regulates β-catenin stability through the transcriptional repression of AXIN1

We next investigated the mechanism by which C/EBPγ regulates β-catenin. Given that C/EBPγ functions as a transcription factor, we initially hypothesized that it might directly influence β-catenin transcription. However, the qPCR analysis revealed that C/EBPγ overexpression did not affect β-catenin mRNA levels (data not shown), suggesting that its regulatory effect occurs posttranscriptionally.

We assessed whether C/EBPγ affects β-catenin stability by measuring its degradation rate in MRC-5 cells. Following C/EBPγ overexpression, cycloheximide (CHX) treatment was applied to block protein synthesis, and β-catenin expression was monitored at different time points. Western blot analysis demonstrated that C/EBPγ markedly slowed β-catenin degradation (Fig. 4A).

Because β-catenin is targeted for degradation through a destruction complex consisting of AXIN1, APC, GSK3β, and CK1, we next examined these upstream regulators. qPCR confirmed that the AXIN1 mRNA level decreased following C/EBPγ overexpression (Fig. 4B). Consistently, luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that C/EBPγ overexpression significantly suppressed AXIN1 promoter activity, indicating that C/EBPγ negatively regulates AXIN1 transcription (Fig. 4C). Western blotting further validated the suppressive effect of C/EBPγ on AXIN1 protein expression (Fig. 4D).

Rescue experiments were performed by coexpressing AXIN1 with C/EBPγ in MRC-5 cells to validate this mechanism. Western blot analysis showed that AXIN1 restoration markedly reversed the upregulation of fibrosis markers induced by C/EBPγ overexpression (Fig. 4E). Similarly, AXIN1 coexpression partially reduced β-catenin protein levels and induced β-catenin phosphorylation at Thr41 and Ser45 (Fig. 4F). Collagen gel contraction assays showed that C/EBPγ overexpression increased the contractile ability of MRC-5 cells, whereas AXIN1 restoration mitigated this effect (Fig. 4G). Luciferase reporter assays further indicated that C/EBPγ significantly activated the β-catenin signaling pathway, whereas the restoration of AXIN1 expression attenuated this activity (Fig. 4H). Together, these data indicate that C/EBPγ increases β-catenin activity by transcriptionally repressing AXIN1, thereby promoting β-catenin stability and downstream fibrotic responses.

C/EBPγ suppresses AXIN1 by interacting with C/EBPα

We next investigated the mechanism by which C/EBPγ suppresses AXIN1 expression. C/EBPγ represents the smallest and structurally simplest member of its family and lacks the regulatory domain required for transcriptional activation. It cannot form homodimers but can heterodimerize with other family members, generating “functionally deficient” complexes that typically attenuate the transcriptional activity of their partners’ target genes. Based on these properties and our previous findings, we hypothesized that C/EBPγ represses AXIN1 transcription through an interaction with other family members, thereby indirectly activating the β-catenin pathway. Among these family members, C/EBPα has been shown to play a protective role in both pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis by reducing the expression of α-SMA, fibronectin, and collagen I, thus alleviating fibrosis progression. These findings prompted us to investigate whether C/EBPγ modulates fibrosis through an interaction with C/EBPα. C/EBPα overexpression in MRC-5 cells markedly decreased β-catenin protein levels but increased AXIN1 expression (Fig. 5A). Immunofluorescence staining showed that C/EBPα overexpression markedly attenuated the HLY78-induced increases in collagen I, fibronectin and α-SMA levels in MRC-5 cells (Fig. S1A). qPCR analysis demonstrated that C/EBPα overexpression markedly increased AXIN1 transcription, whereas the coexpression of C/EBPγ partially suppressed this increase (Fig. 5B). No significant alterations were observed in Wnt3a, Wnt5a, or other components of the β-catenin destruction complex. The qPCR analysis revealed no significant differences in APC, Wnt3a, Wnt5a, CK1 or GSK3β transcription (Figure S2A), and western blot analyses showed no obvious changes in CK1, total GSK3β, or phospho-GSK3β (Tyr216) levels (Figure S2B). These results indicate that the regulation of AXIN1 likely contributes to the C/EBPγ and C/EBPα-mediated activation of β-catenin signaling. An AXIN1 promoter-driven luciferase reporter was constructed and transfected into MRC-5 cells to further determine whether AXIN1 is transcriptionally regulated by C/EBPα and C/EBPγ. Luciferase assays revealed that C/EBPα significantly increased AXIN1 promoter activity, whereas C/EBPγ suppressed this transactivation (Fig. 5C), suggesting that C/EBPα and C/EBPγ exert opposing regulatory effects on AXIN1 at the transcriptional level. When C/EBPα and C/EBPγ were overexpressed individually or together, Western blots showed that C/EBPα partially reversed the upregulation of fibrosis markers induced by C/EBPγ (Fig. 5D). Similarly, AXIN1 and phosphorylated β-catenin (Thr41 + Ser45) levels increased in parallel: C/EBPα increased AXIN1 and β-catenin phosphorylation, while C/EBPγ antagonized these effects (Fig. 5E). Luciferase reporter assays further revealed that C/EBPα and C/EBPγ exerted opposing effects on the activation of the β-catenin pathway, suggesting functional antagonism (Fig. 5F). Collagen gel contraction assays confirmed that the opposing effects of C/EBPα and C/EBPγ on cell contraction were strongly associated with β-catenin signaling activity (Fig. 5G).

Coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) experiments were performed in HEK293T cells to investigate whether this regulatory interaction involves direct protein binding, and the results revealed a physical interaction between C/EBP and C/EBP (Fig. 5H). We assessed the functional requirement of this interaction by generating a human C/EBP bZIP truncation construct through the deletion of the C-terminal basic leucine zipper (bZIP) domain responsible for dimerization (amino acids 101–150) (Figure S3A) and the fusion of an HA tag to the C-terminus for detection. Co-IP analysis confirmed that the bZIP truncation mutant failed to interact with C/EBP (Fig. 5I) and had no effect on the fibronectin or -SMA levels in MRC-5 cells under either basal conditions or upon stimulation with the -catenin agonist HLY78 (Figure S3B). Immunofluorescence staining of MRC-5 cells further showed the nuclear colocalization of C/EBP (full length) and C/EBP (Fig. 5J). Three primer pairs spanning the AXIN1 promoter region were designed for a ChIP–qPCR analysis to further determine whether this interaction affects C/EBP occupancy at the AXIN1 promoter. The overexpression of C/EBP in MRC-5 cells significantly reduced C/EBP occupancy at the AXIN1 promoter, whereas the bZIP truncation mutant did not reduce C/EBP promoter occupancy (Fig. 5K). Collectively, these findings indicate that C/EBP physically interacts with C/EBP via its bZIP domain and attenuates C/EBP-mediated transcriptional activation of AXIN1 by reducing C/EBP occupancy at the AXIN1 promoter.

Compared with BLM-treated mice, C/EBPγ-overexpressing mice exhibited markedly aggravated fibrotic changes. Western blot analysis demonstrated the substantial upregulation of α-SMA, collagen I, and fibronectin expression (Fig. 6B). AAV-mediated C/EBPγ knockdown attenuates bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis in mice

Mice were administered an adeno-associated virus carrying an shRNA targeting C/EBPγ (AAV-shC/EBPγ) to achieve gene knockdown, followed by bleomycin (BLM) treatment to induce pulmonary fibrosis (Fig. 7A). Western blotting showed that α-SMA, collagen I, and FN protein levels were markedly decreased (Fig. 7B). Immunofluorescence staining showed that β-catenin levels in mouse lung tissues were reduced in parallel with the decreased expression of C/EBPγ (Fig. 7C). Micro-CT imaging revealed an improved alveolar architecture and reduced fibrotic lesions (Fig. 7D), and both the lung-to-body weight ratio (Fig. 7E) and the hydroxyproline content (Fig. 7F) decreased. BALF leukocyte counts were reduced (Fig. 7G), indicating decreased inflammation. Histological analyses further confirmed these findings, as H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining revealed markedly reduced alveolar damage and collagen deposition, whereas α-SMA immunohistochemistry showed decreased myofibroblast activation in C/EBPγ knockdown mice compared with controls (Fig. 7I). Collectively, these in vivo findings indicate that the suppression of C/EBPγ expression effectively mitigates fibrosis progression.

Discussion

IPF is a progressive interstitial lung disease of uncertain etiology characterized by a declining respiratory capacity and poor clinical outcomes [28]. The primary pathological characteristics of IPF are abnormal fibroblast activation and the excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) in lung tissue [1, 29]. While antifibrotic agents such as pirfenidone and nintedanib modestly delay disease progression, their therapeutic effects are limited, with median survival still limited to 3–5 years. Thus, new mechanisms and targeted therapies are urgently needed [30]. In recent years, the role of transcription factors in regulating the pathogenesis of IPF has garnered increasing attention [31–34]. This study revealed that C/EBPγ was significantly upregulated in lung tissues from both IPF patients and a mouse model of bleomycin-induced fibrosis. Functional experiments revealed that C/EBPγ knockdown suppressed the fibrotic phenotype, whereas C/EBPγ overexpression promoted the EMT, fibroblast activation, and ECM deposition. Mechanistic investigations showed that C/EBPγ exerts its profibrotic effect by downregulating AXIN1, thereby stabilizing β-catenin, promoting its nuclear translocation, and activating downstream signaling pathways. Additionally, C/EBPγ forms a heterodimer with C/EBPα, which reduces AXIN1 transcriptional activity and regulates the AXIN1–β-catenin axis. In an AAV-transduced mouse model, the overexpression of C/EBPγ exacerbated pulmonary fibrosis, while its knockdown alleviated the disease. These results not only underscore the profibrotic role of C/EBPγ in IPF but also elucidate the regulatory network of transcription factor families (Fig. 8).

Schematic illustrating the mechanism of C/EBPγ in pulmonary fibrosis. (A) Left panel: When C/EBPγ expression is low, C/EBPα exerts its anti-fibrotic effects by promoting AXIN1 transcription, leading to β-catenin phosphorylation and degradation, thereby inhibiting β-catenin signaling activation, reducing epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and fibroblast activation, and alleviating pulmonary fibrosis. Right panel: When C/EBPγ expression is high, it forms heterodimers with C/EBPα to suppress AXIN1 transcription, resulting in β-catenin dephosphorylation, stabilization, and nuclear translocation, activating β-catenin signaling, promoting EMT and fibroblast activation, and exacerbating pulmonary fibrosis. (B) Left panel: C/EBPα forms homodimers that occupy the AXIN1 promoter and activate its transcription, leading to increased AXIN1 expression. Elevated AXIN1 promotes assembly of the β-catenin destruction complex, thereby facilitating β-catenin degradation. Right panel: C/EBPγ forms heterodimers with C/EBPα, which reduces C/EBPα occupancy at the AXIN1 promoter and suppresses AXIN1 transcription. Decreased AXIN1 expression attenuates destruction complex recruitment, resulting in β-catenin accumulation

Wnt/β-catenin signaling was identified in 2003 as a major profibrotic signaling cascade involved in IPF and serves as a pivotal mechanism underlying the progression of pulmonary fibrosis. Chilosi et al. first reported the abnormal nuclear accumulation of β-catenin in the lung tissues of IPF patients, demonstrating that the activation of this pathway is linked to the abnormal proliferation of lung epithelial cells and fibrosis [14]. Subsequent studies have confirmed that Wnt/β-catenin signaling is functionally upregulated in lung fibroblasts from IPF patients, promoting their proliferation and ECM deposition [35]. In experimental models, the suppression of the β-catenin pathway markedly attenuated bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis in mice, as evidenced by reduced inflammation and ECM accumulation [15, 36]. The Wnt coreceptor LRP5 has been identified as an important regulator of IPF pathogenesis, with clinical studies reporting that elevated LRP5 expression correlates with rapid disease progression [37]. The experimental evidence suggests that LRP5 may contribute to fibrogenesis through epithelial- and macrophage-associated β-catenin activation to promote extracellular matrix deposition [38]. At the mechanistic level, β-catenin acts in synergy with TGF-β signaling, which promotes β-catenin activation and drives the differentiation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts [39–42]. Moreover, reduced expression of Wnt inhibitors such as DKK1 relieves the suppression of LRP5/6-mediated Wnt signaling, leading to β-catenin stabilization and increased transcription of profibrotic target genes, thereby exacerbating fibrotic responses [13, 43]. Collectively, these findings underscore that β-catenin acts not only as an “executor” of fibrosis but also as a key node integrating multiple signaling pathways. Existing studies agree that β-catenin remains continuously activated in the lungs of IPF patients rather than being involved in a transient stress response. Therefore, investigating the upstream factors or regulatory mechanisms of this pathway is crucial. In our study, we identified and characterized the transcription factor C/EBPγ as a key regulator of β-catenin stability. Specifically, C/EBPγ increases β-catenin stability by downregulating AXIN1 expression, thereby promoting its nuclear translocation and activating downstream fibrotic signaling. This mechanism is consistent with the classical regulatory pattern of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and reveals a potential new intervention point for the transcription factor family within this signaling axis. Notably, in addition to being a component of the β-catenin degradation complex, AXIN1 contains its own NES sequence, and it functions as a molecular chaperone to promote the nuclear export of β-catenin [44, 45]. Thus, the C/EBPγ-mediated downregulation of AXIN1 expression not only impairs the function of the degradation complex but also restricts the nuclear export of β-catenin, further increasing its nuclear accumulation. This finding is consistent with the observation of β-catenin localization in the nucleus following C/EBPγ overexpression, as shown by immunofluorescence staining. This mechanism supports the functional loop of the C/EBPγ–AXIN1–β-catenin axis at the cellular level, providing additional evidence for enhanced β-catenin signaling and fibrotic effects. This discovery not only broadens the regulatory network of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in IPF but also provides a new perspective for understanding its pathogenesis and developing more targeted therapeutic strategies.

Members of the C/EBP family have been identified as critical regulators of fibrosis in different organs. For instance, in liver fibrosis, they regulate the expression of inflammatory factors and the activation of hepatic stellate cells [46, 47]; in renal fibrosis, they influence the EMT, FMT and ECM deposition; and in cardiac fibrosis, they are involved in cardiomyocyte reprogramming and collagen synthesis. Current research indicates that the involvement of this family in IPF is diverse and context dependent. In addition to C/EBPγ, other family members have been widely studied. For example, C/EBPα primarily plays a protective role in epithelial cells by inhibiting TGF-β signaling and upregulating cell cycle-related genes, thereby reducing the EMT and fibroblast activation [48, 49]. In fibroblasts and macrophages, C/EBPβ activates the NF-κB pathway, promoting the expression of proinflammatory factors (such as IL-6 and TNF-α) and ECM synthesis, thus exacerbating the fibrotic process [50, 51]. C/EBPδ plays a region- and cell type-specific dual role in pulmonary fibrosis by promoting fibrosis in alveolar epithelial cells and macrophages through the induction of IL-6 and MCP-1 expression [52, 53], while suppressing fibrosis in Clara cells through the upregulation of the protective proteins CCSP and SCGB3A2 [54–57]. C/EBPζ (CHOP) serves as an essential regulator of endoplasmic reticulum stress, driving type II alveolar epithelial cell apoptosis via an IRE1α/PERK-dependent pathway; its loss markedly alleviates fibrosis induced by hypoxia or repeated epithelial injury [58, 59]. These results underscore the multifaceted functions of the C/EBP family in various cell types involved in IPF, such as lung epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and immune cells, and their reported associations with multiple signaling pathways, including those involving TGF-β, NF-κB, and HIF-1α. These insights are crucial for understanding the regulatory network of fibrosis. In our study, we found that elevated expression of C/EBPγ stimulated β-catenin signaling, which in turn promoted fibrosis progression. While some existing studies support the protective role of C/EBPγ in inflammation and aging, our experimental results provide novel insight into the role of C/EBPγ in fibrosis. In this study, we employed several methods to investigate the effect of C/EBPγ on the β-catenin pathway. We observed that the overexpression of C/EBPγ in both A549 and MRC-5 cells resulted in increased β-catenin protein levels and its significant nuclear translocation, thereby verifying β-catenin activation. The use of agonists and inhibitors further validated the dependence of this effect on the β-catenin pathway. Additionally, the regulation of AXIN1 by C/EBPα and the interaction between C/EBPγ and C/EBPα strengthened this evidence. Mechanistically, AXIN1 promoter–driven luciferase assays first demonstrated that C/EBPα and C/EBPγ exert opposing regulatory effects on AXIN1 transcription, indicating functional competition at the promoter level. Consistently, ChIP–qPCR analyses (using both full-length and truncated C/EBPγ constructs) revealed that C/EBPγ overexpression reduces C/EBPα occupancy at the AXIN1 promoter, supporting a direct transcriptional regulatory effect of C/EBPγ on AXIN1. Importantly, we did not observe significant alterations in the levels of upstream components of the Wnt signaling pathway, such as CK1, total or phosphorylated GSK3β protein levels, or the expression of Wnt3a, Wnt5a, or APC. These findings suggest that C/EBPγ primarily modulates β-catenin signaling through transcriptional regulation of AXIN1 rather than by altering upstream ligand availability or core destruction complex components. Although changes in BALF leukocyte counts were observed in association with altered C/EBPγ expression in vivo, this parameter primarily reflects overall disease severity rather than serving as evidence for a direct regulatory role of C/EBPγ in inflammation; our study focuses on the function of C/EBPγ in structural lung cells within the fibrotic context rather than in immune cell compartments. We believe that these findings do not contradict existing studies but rather improve our understanding of the diverse roles of C/EBP family members. For instance, C/EBPβ, a member of this family, is predominantly associated with proinflammatory and profibrotic effects [50, 51], although some studies have also shown its inhibitory role in the β-catenin pathway [60]. Transcription factors typically regulate multiple target genes, resulting in the formation of cell type-dependent multidimensional regulatory networks. These networks can modulate both protective and pathogenic pathways under various physiological and pathological conditions, suggesting that their functions may be bidirectional. The structure and function of C/EBPγ distinguish it from other transcription factors in terms of its mode of transcriptional regulation. It interacts with other family members to fine-tune their transcriptional efficiency [20, 61]. In addition, C/EBPγ can interact with other bZIP-containing transcription factors, such as ATF4 [22]. This broadens the scope of the role of C/EBPγ, potentially by altering gene expression governed by both family-related and external transcription factors. Furthermore, given the essential roles of C/EBPα in epithelial differentiation, cell cycle control, and maintenance of alveolar homeostas [62, 63], potential risks should be considered when interventions that affect C/EBPα-dependent transcriptional programs are proposed. Although reducing C/EBPγ activity under fibrotic conditions may relieve its inhibitory effect on C/EBPα and thereby be beneficial, C/EBP family members operate within tightly regulated transcriptional networks. Thus, excessive or systemic modulation could inadvertently disturb epithelial homeostasis and the repair capacity, underscoring the importance of context- and stage-dependent regulation. Collectively, investigations of the diverse functions of C/EBPγ under various conditions not only broaden our understanding of the C/EBP family but also deepen our knowledge of disease mechanisms and may aid in the development of more targeted therapeutic strategies.

Despite systematically demonstrating the profibrotic role of C/EBPγ in IPF and its underlying mechanisms in both in vitro and in vivo models, several limitations should be noted. First, this study largely relied on a bleomycin-induced murine model and classical in vitro systems, such as A549 and MRC-5 cells. While these models are widely used and experimentally tractable, they do not fully capture the heterogeneity, chronic progression, and multifactorial interactions characteristic of IPF in humans, including patient-specific variability. Second, our data reveal that the C/EBPγ–C/EBPα heterodimer is a critical regulator of the AXIN1–β-catenin axis and underscore the central role of β-catenin signaling in the C/EBPγ-mediated profibrotic effects. However, the broader effects of disrupting this heterodimer on pathways beyond β-catenin warrant further investigation, as well as its potential interactions with other family members (such as C/EBPδ) or additional coregulatory factors, including epigenetic modifiers. This inherent complexity may constrain a comprehensive understanding of the dynamic regulatory networks orchestrated by the C/EBP family. Third, although AAV-mediated modulation of C/EBPγ in vivo confirmed its functions, questions regarding long-term safety, dose optimization, and potential off-target effects remain unresolved. Moreover, intratracheal AAV delivery results in whole-lung expression, and given the multicellular nature of IPF and the context-dependent role of C/EBPγ, contributions from epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and immune cells may coexist. While our in vitro data support a direct profibrotic role of C/EBPγ in structural lung cells via the C/EBPγ–C/EBPα–AXIN1–β-catenin axis, future studies with stage-specific or cell type-restricted models are needed to dissect these contributions in vivo. These limitations highlight important directions for future research, including validation in patient cohorts and integrative multiomics profiling, which will be crucial for advancing our understanding of the transcriptional regulatory landscape in IPF.

This study reveals a profibrotic role of C/EBPγ in bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis: C/EBPγ forms a transcriptionally repressive heterodimer with C/EBPα that suppresses AXIN1 expression, leading to the activation of the β-catenin pathway and promoting the EMT, fibroblast activation, and ECM deposition. These findings not only contribute significantly to the understanding of the role of the C/EBP family in fibrotic networks but also provide new insights into IPF pathogenesis. Collectively, our findings identify the C/EBPγ-C/EBPα–AXIN1–β-catenin axis as a critical regulatory module in IPF and highlight pathway-level modulation, such as AXIN1 stabilization or increased β-catenin turnover, as a potential direction for future translational investigation.
