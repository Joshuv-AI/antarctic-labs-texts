The Busemann Process and Steep Highways in Directed First Passage Percolation
Sam McKeown

Abstract
We consider the Busemann process in planar directed first passage percolation. We extend existing techniques to establish the existence of the process in our setting and determine its distribution in a number of integrable models. As examples of their utility, we show how these explicit distributions may be used to quantify the semi-infinite geodesics passing through thin rectangles, and the clustering phenomenon observed in competition interface angles. There is a natural connection with various particle systems, and in particular we obtain the multi-class invariant distributions for discrete-time TASEP with parallel updates.

Introduction

First passage percolation (FPP) is a natural model of a random metric on a graph, which assigns distances, or
passage times
, between vertices as the minimal sum of weights along paths connecting those points. We will restrict ourselves to the graph
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. One tool in understanding the long geodesics (minimising paths), and thus the large scale behaviour of the model, is the
Busemann process
. Writing
L(x,y)
for the (random) passage time between vertices
x,\,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, this is the collection of (random) limits

B^{\xi}(x,y)=\lim_{v\to\xi\cdot\infty}L(x,v)-L(y,v),

(1.1)

where by
v\to\xi\cdot\infty
we mean that
\lvert v\rvert\to\infty
and
v/\lvert v\rvert\to\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Such limits were introduced by Newman
[
New95
]
and the program continued in
[
LN96
,
Hof08
]
, wherein they were used to study the large-scale geometry of FPP and derive many properties of (semi-)infinite geodesic rays, such as their existence and limiting directions, under certain unproved assumptions.

The full, unconditional existence of the limits in (
1.1
) is available only under a directedness assumption on the geodesics and was proven in the case of
last passage percolation
(LPP) in
[
GRS17a
]
. So-called “generalised Busemann functions” have been shown to exist in broad generality by
[
GJR23
]
, serving much the same purpose as the Busemann process and in particular giving the existence of geodesic rays in fixed directions. Their construction as weak limits, however, makes questions such as ergodicity much harder, despite being immediate when we start with the representation in (
1.1
).

Apart from trying to understand the general picture, it is possible also to pin down the exact distribution of the Busemann process in a handful of
solvable
(or
integrable
) models, which has yielded a much finer picture of their geometry. In particular, the distribution of the process has been found for exponential LPP in
[
FS20
]
and for the inverse-gamma directed polymer — its positive temperature relative — in
[
BFS25
]
. This understanding has also been extended, not without technical difficulty, to their various scaling limits. See
[
SS23
,
Bus24
,
BSS24
,
Gro+25
]
.

We give the existence of the Busemann limits in the same terms as
[
GRS17a
]
, but in the context of a more flexible
edge-weight
FPP. We then determine their joint distributions for certain models with Bernoulli-exponential and Bernoulli-geometric weights, of the type considered in
[
Mar09
,
MP10
]
. Our description relies, as does much prior work, on variations of the
multi-line process
developed by Ferrari and Martin in their influential series of papers
[
FM07
,
FM06
,
FM09
]
.

We give an application to the
highways and byways problem
of Hammersley and Welsh
[
HW65
]
. This is the problem of determining the limiting expected density of semi-infinite geodesics within the tree of geodesics, and has been resolved in its original formulation by
[
AHH22
]
, then quantitatively in
[
DEP24
]
. We give what appears to be the first almost sure result in this direction, albeit under very specific assumptions on the weights and the subsets through which we take the limit.

Finally, we find the asymptotic density of the
convoys
of our model. We must defer the definitions until after we have properly introduced the model, but readers may be familiar with the phenomenon by the same name which has been observed in TASEP. See
[
AAV11
]
.

2
Definitions and main results

2.1
The model

Our setting throughout is the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Write
e_{1}
for the horizontal edge emanating from the origin, or interchangeably as the point
(1,0)
, and similarly for
e_{2}=(0,1)
. For each pair
x\leq y\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
(where
(x_{1},x_{2})\leq(y_{1},y_{2})
if
x_{1}\leq y_{1}
and
x_{2}\leq y_{2}
), let
\Pi(x,y)
be the set of directed nearest neighbour paths connecting
x
to
y
. A path
\pi\in\Pi(x,y)
is represented by a sequence of vertices
x=\pi^{0}\leq\pi^{1}\cdots\leq\pi^{n}=y
, where
\pi^{i}-\pi^{i-1}\in\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
,
1\leq i\leq n
. To lighten the notational load we also consider
\pi
to consist of the edges
(\pi^{i-1},\pi^{i})
.

The random environment consists of weights
\{W({e})\}_{e\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})}
living on the edges of the lattice. These weights are used to assign to each path
\pi\in\Pi(x,y)
a
passage time

L(\pi)
, by summing the weights along the edges taken:

L(\pi)=\sum_{e\in\pi}W({e}).

(2.1)

The minimal passage time among
\Pi(x,y)
is the point-to-point passage time
L(x,y)
, and the path attaining this minimal passage time is called a
geodesic
. Observe that when the weight distribution is continuous and i.i.d, the geodesic is almost surely unique. Even when it is not, we can adopt the convention of always taking the right-most of the available geodesics. We write
\gamma_{x,y}
for
the
geodesic connecting
x
to
y
chosen according to this rule, and abbreviate
\gamma_{x}=\gamma_{0,x}
,

Overloading notation to write
0\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
for the origin, we will abbreviate
\Pi(x)=\Pi(0,x)
and
L(x)=L(0,x)
. Also abbreviate
W({(x-e_{i},x)})=W(x;i)
. Write
\lambda
for the joint distribution of
(W(0;1),W(0;2))
and
\lambda_{i}
for the marginal of
W(0;i)
. The following blanket assumption will be implicit in the sequel.

Assumption 2.1
.

Assume that the pairs
\{(W(x;1),W(x;2))\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
are i.i.d and non-constant, and that
\mathbb{E}\lvert W(0;i)\rvert^{2+\epsilon}<\infty
for
i\in\{1,2\}
and some
\epsilon>0
. Assume also that
(0,0)\in\operatorname{supp}(\lambda)
.

Remark 2.2
.

Insisting that
(0,0)
lie in the support of our weights is no restriction in the directed setting. A directed path connecting
x
to
y
has exactly
\langle y-x,e_{1}\rangle
horizontal edges and
\langle y-x,e_{2}\rangle
vertical edges. Shifting the distribution of the weights by a constant affects the passage times by a linear shift and is of no consequence.

Those specialisations of the model for which there is only one random weight per vertex often admit a queueing or interacting particle representation. Taking
W({x;1})=W({x;2})
gives vertex-weight FPP (or the familiar LPP, after swapping signs), with its well-known correspondence to the G/G/1 queue and to TASEP (see for example
[
DMO05
]
). If we take instead
W({x;2})=0
and insist that the remaining horizontal weights are non-negative, then we call the model
strict-weak first passage percolation
(SWFPP). SWFPP with Bernoulli weights has been studied under the name of the Seppäläinen-Johansson model. An illustration of this model is provided in
Fig.

Namely, one looks at all up-right directed paths which connect two vertices and sums over the horizontal weights collected, taking the path which minimises this quantity. Observe that there is a pronounced asymmetry between the directions, which does not appear in the more familiar last passage percolation.

SWFPP can be seen as a degenerate case of what we will call the
Seppäläinen-Johansson-Ransford
(SJR)
model
, introduced in
[
Ran24a
]
. Here we associate to the vertices
x
i.i.d Bernoulli switching variables
S(x)
, and set
W({x;1})=S(x)\widetilde{W}(x;1)
,
W({x;2})=(1-S(x))\widetilde{W}(x;2)
. Here
(\widetilde{W}(x;1),\widetilde{W}(x;2))
are weights satisfying
2.1
. In words, we choose independently at each vertex one of the horizontal or vertical incoming weights to be random, and the other to be zero. The SJR model may be seen as a random directed metric on a tree of coalescing random walks, in the sense described in
[
VV23
]
.

We mention an undirected relative of SWFPP, where vertical edges are given a shared constant weight and horizontal weights remain random. This model was introduced in
[
Has25
]
and we will refer to results on the limit shape established for this model.

2.1.1
The store model

A useful perspective in analysing SWFPP is that of a discrete-time queueing model. So as to avoid confusion with the better known G/G/1 queue, we will follow

(W_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
of attempted outputs, or
service
. The formulas remain valid if these are taken to be real-valued, but for the purpose of our analogy we imagine them to be non-negative. At time
k
, our bin receives an input
I_{k}
, which is added to the store, and subsequently attempts to output
W_{k}
. If there is not enough material in the store, the entire contents of the store are output. The situation is depicted in

2.1.2
The limit shape

We call a function
\ell:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
the
time constant
, or by conflating it with its level sets, the
limit shape
, if we have

\ell(s,t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{L(\lfloor ns\rfloor,\lfloor nt\rfloor)}{n}\ \mathrm{a.s}.

(2.3)

Such limit shapes exist under much weaker assumptions than we take here. It will be useful for this convergence to be uniform across all directions. A result of this kind was proved for vertex-weight last passage percolation by Martin in
[
Mar04
]
. It may be of independent interest to note that such convergence holds in our setup too.

Theorem 2.3
.

The limit shape
\ell
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 0}
, and almost surely,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\max_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq}^{2},\lvert x\rvert=n}\lvert L(x)-\ell(x)\rvert=0.

(2.4)

We once again abbreviate
\ell(0,x)=\ell(x)
.

In fact, Martin’s results go further in the planar case to give universal asymptotics for the limit shape near the axis
[
Mar04
, Theorem 2.4]
. An extension of his argument yields the same asymptotics for edge weights (or indeed on the directed triangular lattice), albeit with a subtlety when weights in one direction are taken to be constant. Such a discussion would lead us too far astray, however.

2.2
Existence of the Busemann process

Our first set of results extend those of
[
GRS17a
]
to our setting. The theorem below summarises our knowledge of the Busemann process when
\ell
is assumed to be differentiable and strictly convex. This remains unproven in any level of generality, but can be directly verified for the models we treat in the remainder of the paper, where explicit representations exist. The most general statement, and the attendant notation, are given in
Appendix

B
.

In the following,
\mathscr{U}=\{(t,1-t):t\in(0,1)\}
is the set of directions into the first quadrant. We order these angles by their first component, so that larger angles are those closer to horizontal. Let
\Theta_{z}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
be the shift of the environment by
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. That is,
\Theta_{z}(W){x}=W({x-z})
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Suppose the limit shape
\ell
is differentiable and strictly convex. With probability
1
, there is a (random) subset
\mathscr{U}_{0}\subseteq\mathscr{U}
with countable complement, such that for a fixed
\xi
we have
\mathbb{P}(\xi\in\mathscr{U}_{0})=1
, and for all
\xi\in\mathscr{U}_{0}
the limits

B^{\xi}(x,y)=\lim_{v_{n}\to\xi\cdot\infty}L(x,v_{n})-L(y,v_{n})

(2.5)

exist for all
x,\,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, where
\lparen v_{n}\rparen\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is a sequence with
\lvert v_{n}\rvert\to\infty
and
v_{n}/\lvert v_{n}\rvert\to\xi
. Moreover, for all
\xi\in\mathscr{U}
, the limits

B^{\xi^{+}}(x,y)=\lim_{{\begin{subarray}{c}\zeta\to\xi^{+}\\
\zeta\in\mathscr{U}_{0}\end{subarray}}}B^{\zeta}(x,y),\qquad B^{\xi^{-}}(x,y)=\lim_{{\begin{subarray}{c}\zeta\to\xi^{-}\\
\zeta\in\mathscr{U}_{0}\end{subarray}}}B^{\zeta}(x,y)

(2.6)

exist.

Let
x,\,y,\,z\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be arbitrary vertices. The random functions
B^{\xi\pm}(x,y)
satisfy the following properties:

(i)

(Cocycle) We have the identity

B^{\xi\pm}(x,y)=B^{\xi\pm}(x,z)+B^{\xi\pm}(z,y).

(2.7)

(ii)

(Ergodicity) The
\mathbb{R}^{4}
-valued process
\{\psi_{x}^{\xi\pm}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},\xi\in\mathscr{U}}
defined by

\psi_{x}^{\xi\pm}=(W({x;1}),W({x;2}),B^{\xi\pm}(x,x+e_{1}),B^{\xi\pm}(x,x+e_{2}))

(2.8)

is stationary under translations
\Theta_{z}
, and moreover is
jointly ergodic
under the group of such translations.

(iii)

(Measurability) The values
B^{\xi+}(x,y),\,B^{\xi-}(x,y)
are measurable with respect to
\{W(z):z\geq x\text{ or }z\geq y\}
, and hence independent of all other weights.

(iv)

(Adaptedness) We have

\min_{i\in\{1,2\}}\Big\lparen W(x,x+e_{i})-B^{\xi\pm}(x,x+e_{i})\Big\rparen=0.

(2.9)

(v)

(Monotonicity) If
\xi\cdot e_{1}<\zeta\cdot e_{1}
, then

B^{\xi-}(x,x+e_{1})\leq B^{\xi+}(x,x+e_{1})\leq B^{\zeta-}(x,x+e_{1})

(2.10)

and

B^{\xi-}(x,x+e_{2})\geq B^{\xi+}(x,x+e_{2})\geq B^{\zeta-}(x,x+e_{2}).

(2.11)

(vi)

(One-sided continuity) If
\zeta_{n}\cdot e_{1}\searrow\xi\cdot e_{1}
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}B^{\zeta_{n}\pm}(x,y)=B^{\xi+}(x,y).

(2.12)

Similarly, if
\zeta_{n}\cdot e_{1}\nearrow\xi\cdot e_{1}
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}B^{\zeta_{n}\pm}(x,y)=B^{\xi-}(x,y).

(2.13)

When
\xi\in\mathscr{U}_{0}
, these limits coincide and the map
\xi\mapsto B^{\xi}(x,y)
is continuous here.

(vii)

(Distinct means) The mean vectors
h(\xi\pm)=h(B^{\xi\pm})
defined by

h(\xi\pm)\cdot e_{i}=\mathbb{E}[B^{\xi\pm}(0,e_{i})]

(2.14)

satisfy

h(\xi\pm)=\nabla\ell(\xi).

(2.15)

In particular, Busemann limits in two distinct directions can not have the same expectation.

From the cocycle property in (
2.7
), we see that it is enough to consider nearest neighbour Busemann functions. We abbreviate
B^{\xi}_{i}(x)=B^{\xi}(x,x+e_{i})
.

Remark 2.5
.

For a fixed
\xi\in\mathscr{U}
, the above theorem tells us that the Busemann limit exists almost surely and we may construct
Busemann geodesics
by the following procedure. Starting from any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we take
i_{1}\in\{1,2\}
such that
B^{\xi}(x_{0},x_{0}+e_{i_{1}})=W(x_{0},x_{0}+e_{i_{1}})
(although such a choice may not be unique). Then we repeat this process at
x_{1}=x_{0}+e_{i_{1}}
, and so on. The resulting infinite path is a semi-infinite geodesic in direction
\xi
. This is the standard method of producing geodesics from the Busemann process, but for completeness we include a proof in
Appendix

B
. See also
[
ADH17
, Section 5.3]
and
[
Ras18
, Section 8]
. Even when the limit does not exist, we may carry this process out using
B^{\xi+}
or
B^{\xi-}
, producing two distinct geodesics.

There is a pleasing simplification in SWFPP: to produce a geodesic from
B^{\xi}
, we need only look at
B^{\xi}(x,x+e_{2})
. If it’s zero, we can take an up step, and otherwise go right. That the decision may be done without reference to the horizontal increments or the environment of weights allows us to perform direct computations which would be quite unwieldy in exponential LPP. Some results of this type are in
Sections

2.6
and
2.7
.

Remark 2.6
.

The functions defined in
Theorem

2.4
are
north-east
Busemann functions, in the sense that the sequence of points
v_{n}
in the definition eventually lie in the top-right quadrant. We may equivalently consider
south-west
Busemann functions, of the form

B^{\xi}(x,y)=\lim_{v_{n}\to-\xi\cdot\infty}L(v_{n},x)-L(v_{n},y).

(2.16)

In fact, it is these functions whose joint distribution we identify in
Theorem

2.8
. The distribution of the north-east Busemann process is then the result of applying a reflection about the origin.

2.3
Exact distributions for Bernoulli-exponential weights

In
[
FS20
]
, the joint distributions of the Busemann functions in exponential LPP are characterised as the unique distribution invariant under a vector-valued M/M/1 queue. This is then identified as the result of a certain recursive process, which we will choose to call a
multi-line
distribution. Such constructions were introduced by Ferrari and Martin in the context of multi-class TASEP
[
FM07
]
, and as we mention in the introduction, have been successfully adapted to treat many other models. We follow the same route, although now with a slightly expanded set of weight distributions, namely
Bernoulli-exponential
(and Bernoulli-geometric) variables.

A Bernoulli-exponential variable is simply the product of independent Bernoulli and exponential variables. By
W\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p)\mathrm{Exp}(\tau)
, we mean that

\mathbb{P}(W\geq x)=(1-p)\mathbbm{1}(x=0)+pe^{-\tau x},\quad x\geq 0.

(2.17)

These variables arise naturally when working with exponentials and max-plus operations. If, for example,
X\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\tau)
and
Y\geq 0
independent, then
(X-Y)^{+}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p)\mathrm{Exp}(\tau)
for some
p
. Taking
p=1
gives us an exponential variable.

We denote by
\mathrm{Geom}_{+}(\alpha)
a geometric variable with positive support, and by
\mathrm{Geom}_{0}(\alpha)
one with non-negative support. That is, if
X_{0}\sim\mathrm{Geom}_{0}(\alpha)
, then

\mathbb{P}(X_{0}=k)=(1-\alpha)^{k}\alpha,\quad k\geq 0,

(2.18)

and
X_{0}+1\sim\mathrm{Geom}_{+}(\alpha)
. The Bernoulli-geometric distribution is defined in the obvious way, as
\mathrm{Ber}(p)\mathrm{Geom}_{+}(\alpha)
. Observe that
p=1
gives a
\mathrm{Geom}_{+}(\alpha)
variable and
p=1-\alpha
gives a
\mathrm{Geom}_{0}(\alpha)
variable.

For concreteness, let us consider only
\mathrm{Ber}(p)\mathrm{Exp}(1)
weights,
0<p\leq 1
, and let
\lambda
be the distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}}
whose components are i.i.d
\mathrm{Ber}(p)\mathrm{Exp}(1)
distributed. We may equally consider
\mathrm{Ber}(p)\mathrm{Geom}_{+}(\alpha)
or
\mathrm{Ber}(p)
distributions, with little modification.

For
\rho_{1}<p
, let
\nu_{H}^{\rho_{1}}
have instead i.i.d
\mathrm{Ber}(q)\mathrm{Exp}(\tau)
components, where
q,\,\tau
satisfy

\rho_{1}=\frac{q}{\tau},\qquad\frac{q\tau}{1-q}=\frac{p}{1-p}.

(2.19)

When
p=1
, we simply fix
q=1
. These equations can be solved to give rather complicated expressions:

q=\frac{2\sqrt{p\rho_{1}}}{\sqrt{p\rho_{1}}+\sqrt{4(1-p)+p\rho_{1}}},\quad\tau=\frac{q}{\rho_{1}}.

(2.20)

Observe that
\rho_{1}
is the mean of
\nu_{H}^{\rho_{1}}
. Varying
\rho_{1}\in(0,p)
gives us parameters
q
lying in
(0,p)
and
\tau
in
(1,\infty)
.

Now let
\rho=(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{n})
be a vector of means with
p>\rho_{1}>\cdots>\rho_{n}
, and write
\nu_{H}^{\rho}=\nu_{H}^{\rho_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\nu_{H}^{\rho_{n}}
. Take
(I^{1},\dots,I^{n})\sim\nu_{H}^{\rho}
and consider the following procedure. Set
J^{1}=I^{1}
, and for
k\geq 2
let
J^{k}=H(I^{k},J^{k-1})
. Finally, write
\mu_{H}^{\rho}
for the distribution of
(J^{1},\dots,J^{n})
. We call the coupling resulting from this iterative procedure a
multi-line
distribution. Martin and Prabhakar
[
MP10
]
have shown the following for store output map
H
.

Theorem 2.7

(Theorem 7.1 of
[
MP10
]
)
.

Take
W\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p)\mathrm{Exp}(1)
and
(J^{1},\dots,J^{n})\sim\mu_{H}^{\rho}
, independent. Then:

(i)

The marginal distributions are
J^{k}\sim\nu_{H}^{\rho_{k}}
.

(ii)

We have component-wise monotonicity:
J^{1}\geq J^{2}\geq\cdots\geq J^{n}
.

(iii)

The distribution
\mu_{H}^{\rho}
is jointly invariant under
H
:

(H(J^{1},W),\dots,H(J^{n},W))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(J^{1},\dots,J^{n}).

(2.21)

When the parameters are suitably adjusted, the same construction produces the multi-class (or vector-valued) invariant measures for two further maps arising from our store model. For the moment let us just say that the
H
map captures the relationship between the Busemann functions on a horizontal line and on the line directly above it. Similarly, we may introduce maps
A
and
V
, capturing this evolution on antidiagonal and vertical lines. These are introduced properly in
Section

3.1
.

Given a mean
\rho_{1}>0
, let
\nu_{A}^{\rho_{1}}
and
\nu_{V}^{\rho_{1}}
be distributions on
\mathbb{Z}
-indexed sequences whose entries have i.i.d Bernoulli-Exponential distribution with mean
\rho_{1}
, the parameters explicit functions of
\rho_{1}
. The formulas for these parameters are involved and uninformative. Later we will consult
Table

1
and choose the distribution corresponding to our weights and with the desired mean.

As before, define
\nu_{A}^{\rho}=\nu_{A}^{\rho_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\nu_{A}^{\rho_{n}}
, and
\nu_{V}^{\rho}
analogously. Let
\mu_{A}^{\rho}
(resp.
\mu_{V}^{\rho}
) be the distributions after iteratively applying
H
to
\nu_{A}^{\rho}
(resp.
\nu_{V}^{\rho}
), as in the definition of
\mu_{H}^{\rho}
. We will prove in
Section

4
that these distributions satisfy the conclusions of
Theorem

2.7
, now with maps
A
and
V
. It is curious to note that the maps
A
and
V
do not appear in the constructions of their respective multi-line distributions.

This detour into the fixed points of these maps is ultimately with the purpose of identifying the distribution of the Busemann process along horizontal, antidiagonal and vertical lines.

Theorem 2.8
.

In SWFPP, let our weights follow a Bernoulli-exponential, Bernoulli-geometric, or Bernoulli distribution. Set
e_{H}=e_{1}
,
e_{A}=e_{1}-e_{2}
and
e_{V}=-e_{2}
and take
U\in\{H,A,V\}
. Let
\xi_{1}>\cdots>\xi_{n}
be a sequence of directions with
\mathbb{E}[B^{\xi_{i}}(0,e_{U})]=\rho_{i}
, where here our Busemann functions are directed
south-west
. Then

\big\lparen B^{\xi_{1}}(ke_{U},(k+1)e_{U}),\dots,B^{\xi_{n}}(ke_{U},(k+1)e_{U})\big\rparen_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sim\mu_{U}^{\rho}.

(2.22)

A surprising but straightforward fact is that when
p=1
and our weights are simply rate
1
exponential, the distributions
\mu_{H}^{\rho}
and
\mu_{A}^{\rho}
coincide when the choice of
\rho
is valid for each. Less straightforwardly, these are also related to the distributions
\mu^{\rho}
described by Fan and Seppäläinen in
[
FS20
]
, there in the context of exponential LPP.

Proposition 2.9
.

Denote by
B
the
south-west
Busemann process for exponential SWFPP, and by
\overline{B}
the
north-east
Busemann process for exponential LPP. For a parameter
0<\rho<1
, let
\xi(\rho)
be the angle such that
\mathbb{E}[B^{\xi(\rho)}(e_{2},e_{1})]=\rho^{-1}
and
\overline{\xi}(\rho)
the angle such that
\mathbb{E}[\overline{B}^{\overline{\xi}(\rho)}(e_{2},0)]=\rho^{-1}
. Then

\{B^{\xi(\rho)\pm}(k(e_{1}-e_{2}),(k+1)(e_{1}-e_{2})):k\in\mathbb{Z},0<\rho<1\}\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\{\overline{B}^{\overline{\xi}(\rho)\pm}((k+1)e_{2},ke_{2}):k\in\mathbb{Z},0<\rho<1\}

(2.23)

Much is known about the Busemann functions in exponential LPP, and we can, for example, observe that the results of
[
SS23
,
Bus24
]
imply convergence of our SWFPP Busemann process to the stationary horizon when
p=1
. One expects that the stationary horizon appears as a limit of the Busemann process for all choices of
p>0
, though we avoid the necessary verifications here. Indeed, this convergence has already been shown for Bernoulli weights in
[
BSS23
]
.

We remark that whereas the results of
[
FS20
]
capture the distribution of the Busemann process along horizontal and (equivalently) vertical lines in exponential LPP, we are additionally able to describe the distribution along the antidiagonal for our model.

2.4
Parallel TASEP

As a byproduct of identifying the multi-class invariant distributions for the maps
A
and
V
, we can also answer a question found in
[
FM07
,
MS11
]
concerning the
parallel TASEP
. We will be more explicit with our definition in
Section

3.1
, but informally, this is a discrete-time TASEP wherein all of the particles moving at a given time step move simultaneously. If a particle is to move forward on a given time step, then the space in front of the particle must be vacant before that time step. The multi-line distribution in the following statement should be constructed with Bernoulli-geometric variables, rather than Bernoulli-exponential.

Corollary 2.10
.

The multi-class distribution on
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
whose inter-particle distances are given by
\mu_{A}^{\rho}
is invariant under the dynamics of parallel TASEP with Bernoulli-geometric jumps.

2.5
The SJR model

We may also consider the Busemann process of the SJR model. As it turns out, the distribution in this case largely reduces to the question for SWFPP.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let the switching variables of our SJR model be
\mathrm{Ber}(\alpha)
-distributed and let
\xi^{*}=(1-\alpha,\alpha)
be the percolation angle. Let
\xi_{1}>\cdots>\xi_{n}>\xi^{*}
be a sequence of angles and let
(B^{\xi_{1}},\dots,B^{\xi_{n}})
be the associated Busemann functions. Consider now
(B_{0}^{\xi_{1}},\dots,B_{0}^{\xi_{n}})
, the Busemann functions for the SWFPP model whose horizontal weights are identical to those of our original model, but whose vertical weights are all zero. Then

(B^{\xi_{1}},\dots,B^{\xi_{n}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(B_{0}^{\xi_{1}},\dots,B_{0}^{\xi_{n}}).

(2.24)

This gives a near-complete description of the Busemann process when the weights are Bernoulli-exponential, although we still cannot say much about the joint distribution of
(B^{\xi_{1}},B^{\xi_{2}})
, when
\xi_{1}>\xi^{*}>\xi_{2}
. Two special cases are the river delta model
[
BR19
]
with exponential weights, and the degenerate model wherein each vertex has weight
1
on a random incoming edge and
0
on the other.

The connection between the Busemann process and the limit shape contained in
2.4.(vii)
gives a decomposition in terms of two SWFPP limit shapes.

Corollary 2.12

(Theorem 1 of
[
Ran24a
]
)
.

Let
\ell
be the time constant for an SJR model, and
\ell_{H}
(resp.
\ell_{V}
) be the time constant for the SWFPP whose horizontal (resp. vertical) weights are unchanged but whose vertical (resp. horizontal) weights are constant zero. Then

\ell=\ell_{H}\vee\ell_{V}.

(2.25)

Ransford’s original proof is considerably more direct and avoids the Busemann machinery, but both Ransford’s proof and our own proof of
Theorem

2.11
have as their essential ingredient the observation contained in Lemma 2 of
[
Ran24a
]
, which implies that SJR and an appropriate SWFPP are indistinguishable in stationarity.

2.6
Highways and byways

The immediate purpose of Newman’s introduction of Busemann functions to FPP
[
New95
]
was to study the existence and structure of semi-infinite geodesics. The existence of the Busemann limits and their ability to produce semi-infinite geodesics in a given direction immediately implies the following general theorem. This is a direct analogue of Theorem 2.1 of
[
GRS17
]
in our slightly more general setting, and is largely contained in Theorem 5.8 of
[
GJR23
]
.

Corollary 2.13
.

Return to the general setting of
2.1
and consider directions
\xi\in\mathscr{U}
at which the limit shape is differentiable and strictly convex. Then

(i)

Almost surely, simultaneously for all such
\xi
, there is at least one geodesic ray from the origin in direction
\xi
.

(ii)

For a fixed
\xi
, almost surely the geodesic ray from the origin is unique.

(iii)

For fixed
\xi
, almost surely all geodesic rays (and not just those from the origin) in direction
\xi
coalesce.

With the explicit descriptions of the Busemann process for the Bernoulli-exponential SWFPP described above, we may hope for finer information on the nature of the semi-infinite geodesics. Let
\Gamma
be the tree of (right-)geodesics rooted at
0
and let
\Gamma_{\infty}
be the subtree formed by its infinite branches. We identify
\Gamma_{\infty}
with its set of vertices. The subtree trivially contains the axes, and from
Corollary

2.13
we see that it contains a continuum of topological ends.

We call the edges (and by abuse of language, also the vertices) of
\Gamma_{\infty}

highways
. The underlying analogy is that one expects a geodesic between distant points to first travel to seek out a path of highly favourable edges going in the desired direction, travel with this path for most of the journey, and then turn off only when close to its destination. These highly favourable edges form the semi-infinite geodesics. The highways-and-byways problem mentioned in the introduction may be understood as determining the asymptotics of

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\big\lvert\Gamma_{\infty}\cap([0,n]\times[0,n])\big\rvert}{n^{2}}\right],

(2.26)

which is the expected proportion of vertices inside an
n\times n
square footed at the origin which lie on a highway from the origin. The specific question found in
[
HW65
]
is whether this proportion goes to zero. While this has been answered affirmatively in the harder setting of undirected FPP by
[
AHH22
,
DEP24
]
, one may ask: rather than look at the limit in expectation, is there anything to be said of the almost sure limit? That the expected proportion in (
2.26
) goes to zero immediately implies by Fatou’s lemma that the limit infimum of the proportion is zero almost surely, but doesn’t rule out
a priori
some intermittent explosive branching in the tree producing large clusters of highways.

We don’t provide an answer to the question outlined above, but can say something in this spirit. Namely, we consider the intersection of
\Gamma_{\infty}
not with a square but with a thin rectangle, and find that there is nontrivial behaviour. The following results are stated for SWFPP with rate 1 exponential weights.

Theorem 2.14
.

Write
A_{k}(n)
for the cardinality of
\Gamma_{\infty}\cap(\{k\}\times[0,n])
. Then for any
k\geq 1
, we have

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{{A_{k}(n)}}{n}=0,\qquad\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{{A_{k}(n)}}{n}=1\mathrm{\;{a.s.}}

(2.27)

Theorem 2.15
.

Write
B(n)
for the number of branches of
\Gamma_{\infty}
along the vertical axis up to height
n
— that is, the number of connected components of
\Gamma_{\infty}\cap(\{1\}\times[0,n])
. Then there exist constants
0<C_{1}\leq C_{2}<1
, such that for all
n
large enough we have

C_{1}\log n\leq B(n)\leq C_{2}\log n.

(2.28)

The strict bound
C_{2}<1
is conditional on a claimed result in
[
MS11
]
concerning collisions in a discrete-time TASEP speed process, for which the authors do not provide a proof. Without this input, our statement still holds, except weakened to have
C_{2}\leq 1
.

2.7
The competition interface

The geodesic connecting the origin to
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}
, if it is unique, must go through exactly one of
e_{1}
or
e_{2}
. Write
\mathcal{R}_{1}=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}:x\neq 0,\,e_{1}\in\gamma_{x}\}
, and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
the same with
e_{2}
in place of
e_{1}
. These sets are disjoint and the overlap of their boundaries is called the
competition interface
for the tree rooted at the origin. The interface is known to be asymptotically linear in LPP under mild assumptions on the limit shape
[
GRS17
, Theorem 2.6]
. To state the analogous result here, let
r_{n}=\max\{r\geq 0:(r,n)\in\mathcal{R}_{1}\}
be the horizontal position of the interface at height
n
.

Proposition 2.16
.

In the general setup of
2.1
, suppose our weights follow a continuous distribution and that
\ell
is differentiable everywhere. Then the limit

\xi^{*}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{r_{n}}{r_{n}+n}

(2.29)

exists almost surely, and
\ell
has an exposed point in direction
\xi^{*}
.

This
\xi^{*}
is the
critical angle
at the origin. We may define
\xi^{*}(x)
in the same way, using the tree of geodesics rooted at
x
.

The critical angle is intimately related to the discontinuity points of the Busemann process. The following may be seen by considering the procedure of
Remark

2.5
. Recall that we order angles by saying that an angle closer to the horizontal is larger.

Proposition 2.17
.

In SWFPP and under the assumptions of
Proposition

2.16
, we may alternatively define the critical angle as

\xi^{*}=\sup\{\xi:B^{\xi}(0,e_{2})=0\}.

(2.30)

In particular, by monotonicity,

\mathbb{P}(\xi^{*}\geq\xi)=\mathbb{P}(B^{\xi}(0,e_{2})=0).

(2.31)

The joint distributions of critical angles along a line are, morally, related to the distribution of speeds in a corresponding speed process. For exponential LPP, this is the TASEP speed process
[
AAV11
]
, while the integrable models in SWFPP are closely related to the discrete-time particle systems considered in
[
MS11
]
. There is some nontrivial correlation between the speeds of each particle, and the formation of “convoys” is observed. These are sets of particles sharing the same speed, which after a long time eventually meet and travel together. This is surprising in light of the fact that the speeds have continuous marginal distributions. A description of a single convoy is found in
[
AAV11
, Theorem 1.8]
, and here we give the analogue in SWFPP. For the following, recall that a renewal process can be defined as a point set in
\mathbb{R}
whose increments (
holding times
) are independent and identically distributed.

Theorem 2.18
.

Consider Bernoulli or Bernoulli-Exponential weights.

(i)

The ordering of adjacent competition interfaces has

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi^{*}(0)>\xi^{*}(e_{2}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3},

(2.32)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi^{*}(0)=\xi^{*}(e_{2}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6},

(2.33)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi^{*}(0)<\xi^{*}(e_{2}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}.

(2.34)

(ii)

For fixed
\xi_{1}\in\mathscr{U}
, the set
\{k:\xi^{*}(ke_{2})\geq\xi_{1}\}
is a Bernoulli process with density
p_{1}=\mathbb{P}(B^{\xi_{1}}(0,e_{2})=0)
. If
\xi_{2}>\xi_{1}
, then the set
\{k:\xi_{1}\leq\xi^{*}(ke_{2})\leq\xi_{2}\}
is a renewal process. whose holding times have mean
(p_{1}-p_{2})^{-1}
.

(iii)

Write
\alpha=\xi^{*}(0)
. Then
C_{\alpha}=\{k:\xi^{*}(ke_{2})=\alpha\}
is a renewal process whose holding times have infinite mean, and asymptotically satisfies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\lvert C_{\alpha}\cap[0,n]\rvert}{\sqrt{n}}=c_{\alpha}\ \mathrm{a.s},

(2.35)

where
c_{\alpha}
is an explicit constant depending on
\alpha
. In particular, almost surely every convoy along the vertical axis is infinite.

Of these,
2.18.(i)
is a straightforward computation using the multi-line distributions, and we refer to
[
AAV11
, Theorem 1.7]
for an example. To our knowledge, the asymptotic density for a convoy in the TASEP speed process (in the sense of (
2.35
)) has not appeared explicitly, but our calculation may be followed with little modification to produce the leading order term. The explicit constant
c_{\alpha}
for exponential weights is given in
Proposition

5.25
.

3
Update maps and their fixed points

As well as point-to-point passage times
L(x,y)
, we may also consider
line-to-point
passage times
L^{\Gamma}(x)
from a path
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. We must specify a function
g:\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
representing passage times on the path, and then define

L^{\Gamma}(x)=\min_{p\in\Gamma}g(p)+L(p,x).

(3.1)

Observe that if
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}
is a
down-right
path (one consisting only of steps
e_{1}
and
-e_{2}
), then by setting
g(p)=L(p)
, we have that the passage times
L(x)
and
L^{\Gamma}(x)
are equal whenever both are defined.

Shifting
g
by a constant will merely shift the resulting passage times
L^{\Gamma}(x)
by the same constant, so we may consider the passage times to be instead defined, up to a constant, by the increments
I=\Delta g=\{g(p_{i+1})-g(p_{i}):i\in\mathbb{Z},\,(p_{i},p_{i+1})\in\Gamma\}
. In this indexing, the points of our path are ordered from top-left to bottom-right.

Suppose we translate our path forward to
\Gamma+e_{1}
and look at
g^{\prime}:\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
given by
g^{\prime}(p)=L^{\Gamma}(p+e_{1})
. The resulting map on the increments taking
\Delta g=I\mapsto I^{\prime}=\Delta g^{\prime}
is called an
update map
. Since this map depends on the weights on edges above and to the right of vertices of
\Gamma
, we denote this set of weights by
W
and write
I^{\prime}=U^{\Gamma}(I,W,e_{1})
. We can of course replace
e_{1}
by any vector in
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}
.

This map is not well-defined for arbitrary sequences
I,\,W
, so for the time being let us call these sequences
admissible
if the map makes sense. We will give precise constraints when we consider specific examples of update maps below.

A useful property that holds in complete generality is that update maps are monotone in the first two arguments.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be an arbitrary down-right path and take
v\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}
. If
I,\,I^{\prime}
are sequences of increments along
\Gamma
and
W,\,W^{\prime}
are sets of weights adjacent to
\Gamma
, all admissible, then
I\geq I^{\prime}
and
W\geq W^{\prime}
implies

U^{\Gamma}(I,W,v)\geq U^{\Gamma}(I^{\prime},W^{\prime},v).

(3.2)

Here the comparisons are made component-wise.

Our interest is less so in the update maps themselves, but rather in their fixed points. We call a distribution
\rho
a
fixed point
of the update map if when
I\sim\rho
and
W\sim\lambda
, we get
U^{\Gamma}(I,W,v)\sim\rho
(either for some fixed
v
or all
v\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}
— clearly having this for all
v\in\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
is enough to imply the latter). The Busemann process provides a source of particularly strong fixed points. In the statement below, it is important to consider south-west Busemann functions.

Proposition 3.2
.

In the general setting of
2.1
, let
\xi_{1}>\cdots>\xi_{n}\in\mathscr{U}
be a sequence of directions and consider
I^{k}=\{B^{\xi_{k}}(p_{i+1},p_{i}):i\in\mathbb{Z},\,(p_{i},p_{i+1})\in\Gamma\}
. Then if
W
is the set of weights adjacent to
\Gamma
,

U^{\Gamma}(I^{k},W,v)=(I^{\prime})^{k},

(3.3)

where the right sequence consists of the Busemann functions evaluated along the shifted path
\Gamma+v
.

In particular

\big\lparen U^{\Gamma}(I^{1},W,v),\dots,U^{\Gamma}(I^{n},W,v)\big\rparen\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(I^{1},\dots,I^{n}).

(3.4)

and the distributions
(I^{1},\dots,I^{n})
are jointly ergodic under applications of the update map.

The first claim may be seen directly from the definition of the Busemann limits in (
2.16
). The second claim follows from the stationarity in
2.4.(ii)
.

3.1
Update maps in SWFPP

As one might expect, the update maps associated with the horizontal and vertical lines, as well as the antidiagonal, have especially simple explicit expressions and are quite tractable. This is even more true when we restrict ourselves to SWFPP. For each choice of path, let us define the maps in terms of increments and weights, and see how they may be recast in terms of the store model introduced in
Section

2.1
and as the dynamics of various interacting particle systems.

We quickly note that passage times in SWFPP possess a monotonicity not found in general edge-weight FPP. Recall that in SWFPP, horizontal edges are non-negative and vertical edges are zero.

Lemma 3.3
.

Take
y\leq x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}
and suppose we are in the setting of SWFPP. Then

L(y,x+e_{2})\leq L(y,x)\leq L(y,x+e_{1}).

(3.5)

If the horizontal weights satisfy

It will be convenient in this context to write

3.1.2
The vertical update

Now let

\mathcal{S}^{V}=\Big\{(X_{k})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathbb{Z}}:\lim\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=k}^{k+n-1}X_{j}=\mu,\text{ for some }\mu>0\text{ and all }k\in\mathbb{Z}\Big\}.

Remark 3.5
.

TASEP enjoys a symmetry under exchanging particles and holes: as particles in TASEP move rightwards, the holes move leftwards under the same dynamics. This symmetry breaks down in discrete-time TASEP, but there remains some particle-hole duality. If we consider sequential TASEP with Bernoulli jumps, then the holes undergo parallel TASEP with geometric jumps. Using this duality, we may write passage times under Bernoulli weights as a function of passage times under a coupled geometric-weight model, and vice versa. In this sense, it is natural that both Bernoulli and geometric / exponential weights give rise to solvable SWFPP models, and that interpolations in the form of Bernoulli-geometric weights remain tractable.

3.2.2
Extension to SJR

The SJR model is a modest generalisation of SWFPP: instead of weights on horizontals and zeros on verticals, we allow the roles to swap independently on each vertex. It might then be unsurprising that the store and particle system pictures valid in SWFPP can be adapted to accommodate this generalisation. In the store picture, the correct thing to do is to allow both

and

material, one annihilating the other. The positive material behaves as before, but the negative material obeys different dynamics.
2
2

2

The generalisation of the particle picture is less natural: particles may have some random width and we allow some form of limited overtaking.

As before, we consider a
\mathbb{Z}
-indexed tandem of storage bins which push material forward, simultaneously at each time step. We take a sequence

of real-valued quantities, and real-valued weights

More importantly, it is apparent from the description that positive material and negative material are separately conserved, unless they meet in a single bin. In that case, the amount of each material decreases. This suggests that only one type of material may exist in stationarity.

We adjust the notation for the next proof. Consider a double-indexed collection of weights

Remark 3.15

Nothing stops us from defining SWFPP and SJR in higher dimensions. For the former, we fix a direction and the edges in all other directions are constrained to have zero weight. One direction is easy - an invariant measure for SWFPP is also invariant for SJR. Our proof in the other direction for two dimensions relied on a representation in terms of positive and negative material moving through stores, which doesn’t appear to be available in general.

For ease of presentation, we prove the lower and upper bounds separately, in
Proposition

5.4
and
Proposition

5.8
.

5.1.1
A lower bound on jumps

Proposition 5.4
.

There is
C_{1}>0
such that

\lvert\mathcal{B}\cap[0,N]\rvert\geq C_{1}\log N

(5.13)

for all
N
large enough, almost surely.

The argument goes through our first coupled process, which is just the sum of certain independent increments. Here and in the remainder of the section, we set
t_{k}=1-2^{-k}
.

Lemma 5.5
.

There is a coupling of our process
\{b(t)\}
and a process
\{\overline{b}(t)\}
such that
b(t_{k})\leq\overline{b}(t_{k})
for all
k\geq 0
, where
\overline{b}(t)
may be represented as

\overline{b}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor-\log_{2}(1-t)\rfloor}\bigg[1+\sum^{k}_{j=1}z_{k,j}\bigg],

(5.14)

with
z_{k,j}\sim\mathrm{Geom}_{0}(2^{-j})
and the
z_{k,j}
all mutually independent.

Proof.

Consider
b(t_{1}),b(t_{2}),\dots
, whose joint distribution we may access from the multi-line process. Take
I^{k}\sim\nu^{k}
to be the independent inputs into the multi-line process and
\widetilde{I}^{k}
the associated outputs. As in the proof of
Proposition

5.3
, we describe how the values of
b(t_{k})
depend on the inputs
I^{k}
.

Observe that for us to have
b(t_{k})\neq b(t_{k-1})
, the store with inputs
I^{k}
and service
\widetilde{I}^{k-1}
must have no output at time
b(t_{k-1})
. Let
E_{k}(b(t_{k-1}))
be the event that the store with input
I^{k}
is empty immediately before the input at time
b(t_{k-1})
. Since
\widetilde{I}^{k-1}_{j}=0
for
0\leq j<b(t_{k-1})
, it must be that the store is empty before the input at time
0
, and subsequently
I^{k}_{j}=0
for
0\leq j\leq b(t_{k-1})
. This probability may be computed as

\mathbb{P}(E_{k}(b(t_{k-1})))=\frac{1-t_{k}}{1-t_{k-1}}t_{k}^{b(t_{k-1})}=\frac{1}{2}(1-2^{-k})^{b(t_{k-1})}.

(5.15)

Again we use the distributions given in
Table

1
. We will have
b(t_{k})\neq b(t_{k-1})
if
E_{k}(b(t_{k-1}))
occurs along with having
I^{k}_{b(t_{k-1})}=0
, for a total probability of

p_{k}=\frac{1}{2}(1-2^{-k})^{b(t_{k-1})}.

(5.16)

though we do not need this expression in proving this lemma, it will be useful later on.

Now, let us look at the value of
b(t_{k})
. If
E_{k}(b(t_{k-1}))
does not occur, then
b(t_{k})=b(t_{k-1})
. If it does, then the new value will be the time of the first output for the store with input
I^{k}
and service
\widetilde{I}^{k-1}
. This must be after the next nonzero input to the store, say at time
y_{k,k}=b(t_{k-1})+n_{k}(b(t_{k-1}))
, and thereafter occurs at the first nonzero service. Here
n_{k}(b)=\min\{n\geq 0:I^{k}_{n+b}\neq 0\}
, and
n_{k}(b)\sim\mathrm{Geom}_{0}(1-t_{k})
.

The sequence of services,
\widetilde{I}^{k-1}
, is itself the output of a store, with input
I^{k-1}
and service
\widetilde{I}^{k-2}
. Let
E_{k-1}(y_{k,k})
be the event that this store is empty before input at time
y_{k,k}
. On this event, we must now wait for the first subsequent nonzero input from
I^{k-1}
, and then for the first nonzero service. If the store is not empty, then we need only wait for the first nonzero service. We then wait time

y_{k,k-1}=\mathbbm{1}_{E_{k-1}(z_{k,k})}n_{k-1}(y_{k,k})

(5.17)

for the input, and for the service we look at the store with input
I^{k-2}
and service
\widetilde{I}^{k-3}
, and so on. Ultimately,

\displaystyle b(t_{k})

\displaystyle=b(t_{k-1})+\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{k,j},

(5.18)

\displaystyle=b(t_{k-1})+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbbm{1}_{E_{j}(y_{k,j+1})}n_{j}(y_{k,j+1}).

(5.19)

For this to work at
j=1
we should define
y_{k,k+1}=b(t_{k-1})
. Observe that the quantities
n_{j}(y_{k,j+1})
are all independent of each other.

The only troublesome point in (
5.18
) is the dependency on the event
E_{j}(y_{k,j+1})
. We define the process
\overline{b}(t)
to ignore this dependence. Define
\overline{b}(t_{0})=b(t_{0})=0
, and thereafter define

\overline{b}(t_{k})=\overline{b}(t_{k-1})+1+\sum_{j=1}^{k}z_{k,j},

(5.20)

where
z_{k,j}
is defined recursively by
z_{k,k+1}=\overline{b}(t_{k-1})
and

z_{k,j}=n_{j}(z_{k,j+1}).

(5.21)

The additional
1
in the sum is merely to ensure that
\overline{b}(t_{k})\neq\overline{b}(t_{k-1})
. The collection
\{z_{k,j}:k\geq 1,\,1\leq j\leq k\}
consists of independent geometric variables. Since both
\{b(t_{k})\}
and
\{\overline{b}(t_{k})\}
are defined through the same input variables
I^{k}
, and since for
\overline{b}(t_{k})
we always overestimate the time we wait to see output in a queue, we ensure that
b(t_{k})\leq\overline{b}(t_{k})
.
∎

We are interested in the number of values taken by
\{b(t)\}
before it hits a level
N
. Let
T_{N}=\inf\{t:b(t)\geq N\}
, and let
V_{N}=\lvert\{b(t):0\leq t<T_{N}\}\rvert
. Similarly, let
\overline{T}_{N}
and
\overline{V}_{N}
be the corresponding quantities for
\{\overline{b}(t)\}
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Almost surely, we have the asymptotics

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{-\log(1-T_{N})}{\log N}=1

(5.22)

and

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\overline{V}_{N}}{\log_{2}N}=1.

(5.23)

Proof.

Find
K
such that
2^{K}\leq N<2^{K+1}
. The first limit is a very coarse estimate which only uses the marginals of
b(t)
, namely that they are geometric with mean
(1-t)^{-1}
. We again use the dyadic times
t_{k}=1-2^{-k}
. We know

\mathbb{P}(T_{N}\geq t_{k})=\mathbb{P}(b(t_{k})\leq N)=1-(1-2^{-k})^{N}\leq 1-(1-2^{-k})^{2^{K+1}}.

(5.24)

So

\mathbb{P}(T_{N}\geq t_{K(1+\epsilon)})\leq 1-(1-2^{-K(1+\epsilon)})^{2^{K}}\leq C(1-e^{-2^{-K\epsilon}}).

(5.25)

This upper bound decays very quickly and in particular is summable. We may thus have
T_{N}\geq t_{K(1+\epsilon)}
only finitely often. Thus

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{-\log_{2}(1-T_{N})}{\log_{2}N}\leq 1+\epsilon

(5.26)

almost surely. Taking
\epsilon
to zero and finding the matching lower bound (similar to what we do below), gives the limit.

For the second part, note that since
\overline{b}(t)
is constant between its jumps at times
t_{k}
, we may write
\overline{V}_{N}=-\log_{2}(1-\overline{T}_{N})
. We must show that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{-\log_{2}(1-\overline{T}_{N})}{\log_{2}N}=1.

(5.27)

A short calculation gives
\mathbb{P}(z_{k,j}\geq 2^{j(1+\epsilon)})\leq e^{-2^{j\epsilon}}
, and therefore there will be some (random)
C>0
such that
z_{k,j}\leq C2^{j(1+\epsilon)}
, for all
k,\,j
. Hence

\displaystyle\overline{b}(t_{k})

\displaystyle\leq k+\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{l}C2^{j(1+\epsilon)}

(5.28)

\displaystyle\leq k+C\sum_{l=1}^{k}2^{(l+1)(1+\epsilon)}

(5.29)

\displaystyle\leq k+C2^{(k+2)(1+\epsilon)}

(5.30)

\displaystyle\leq C2^{k(1+\epsilon)},

(5.31)

where in the last line we enlarge
C
to absorb the other terms. Some manipulation gives a bound on the hitting time: if
\overline{b}(t_{k})\geq 2^{K}
, then

k\geq(1+\epsilon)^{-1}(K-\log_{2}C).

(5.32)

So since
\epsilon>0
may be chosen arbitrarily,

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{-\log_{2}(1-\overline{T}_{N})}{\log_{2}N}\geq 1.

(5.33)

The same procedure, with a lower bound of
c2^{j(1-\epsilon)}
on the
z_{k,j}
, gives the matching upper bound, and we conclude that in fact

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{-\log_{2}(1-\overline{T}_{N})}{\log_{2}N}=1,

(5.34)

which is equivalent to what we want.
∎

We now know how many jumps to expect before
\{\overline{b}(t)\}
reaches a given level. We will get a lower bound on the jumps of
\{b(t)\}
by showing that the two processes jump “together” at least a positive proportion of the time. This will follow easily after a technical lemma.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
A=\{k\geq 0:\overline{b}(t_{k})\leq 2^{k}\}
. There is a deterministic
p>0
such that

\lim_{K\to\infty}\frac{\lvert A\cap[0,K]\rvert}{K}=p\ \mathrm{a.s}.

(5.35)

Proof.

We relate
\overline{b}(t)
to an
\mathbb{R}
-valued ergodic process, for which the statement is immediate. Let
Y_{k}=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{k}2^{j-k}y_{k,j}
, where
y_{k,j}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1)
and these are mutually independent across all
k,\,j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Consider the process
R_{k}=\sum_{l=-\infty}^{k}2^{l-k}Y_{l}
, which is quite clearly stationary and ergodic under shifts of the index. The support of
R_{k}
is
[0,\infty)
, so for each
\alpha
we can say

\lim_{K\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbbm{1}\{R_{k}\leq\alpha\}}{K}=\mathbb{P}(R_{k}\leq\alpha)\ \mathrm{a.s}.

(5.36)

Now observe that
\lfloor 2^{j}y_{k,j}\rfloor\sim\mathrm{Geom}_{0}(2^{-j})
. We may couple our
R_{k}
with
\{\overline{b}(t)\}
such that

\overline{b}(t_{k})=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\bigg[1+\sum_{j=1}^{l}2^{j}\lfloor y_{l,j}\rfloor\bigg].

(5.37)

Under this coupling, the typical difference between
2^{k}R_{k}
and
\overline{b}(t_{k})
is quite small:

\displaystyle 2^{k}R_{k}-\overline{b}(t_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=-\infty}^{k}\sum_{j=-\infty}^{l}2^{j}y_{l,j}-\sum_{l=1}^{k}\big[1+\sum_{j=1}^{l}2^{j}\lfloor y_{l,j}\rfloor\big]

(5.38)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=-\infty}^{0}\sum_{j=-\infty}^{l}2^{j}y_{l,j}+\sum_{l=0}^{k}\sum_{j=-\infty}^{0}2^{j}y_{l,j}+\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{l}(2^{j}y_{l,j}-\lfloor 2^{j}y_{l,j}\rfloor)-k.

(5.39)

Write
S^{1}_{k}
,
S^{2}_{k}
,
S^{3}_{k}
for the three sums. Among these,
S^{1}_{k}
doesn’t actually depend on
k
, so let us write
H^{1}=S^{1}_{k}
. For
S^{2}_{k}
, an application of the Borel-Cantelli lemma to the
y_{k,j}
shows there is a random
H^{2}
such that
S^{2}_{k}\leq H^{2}k
for all
k\geq 0
. Lastly, the terms of
S^{3}_{k}
are individually bounded by
1
, so trivially
S^{3}_{k}\leq k^{2}
. Using the triangle inequality in (
5.38
) and plugging in these bounds,

\lvert 2^{k}R_{k}-\overline{b}(t_{k})\rvert\leq H^{1}+(H^{2}+1)k+k^{2}\leq Hk^{2},

(5.40)

for all
k\geq 1
(and
H
chosen large enough).

Now to prove the lemma,

\displaystyle\{k\geq 0:\overline{b}(t_{k})\leq 2^{k}\}

\displaystyle\subseteq\{k\geq 0:2^{k}R_{k}-Hk^{2}\leq 2^{k}\}

(5.41)

\displaystyle=\{k\geq 0:R_{k}\leq 1+H2^{-k}k^{2}\}.

(5.42)

With
\epsilon>0
arbitrary, let
k_{\epsilon}
be large enough that
H2^{-k_{\epsilon}}k_{\epsilon}^{2}<\epsilon
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{K\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{K}\mathbbm{1}\{\overline{b}(t_{k})\leq 2^{k}\}}{K}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{K\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{K}\mathbbm{1}\{R_{k}\leq 1+H2^{-k}k^{2}\}}{K}

(5.43)

\displaystyle=\lim_{K\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=k_{\epsilon}}^{K}\mathbbm{1}\{R_{k}\leq 1+H2^{-k}k^{2}\}}{K}

(5.44)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{K\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{K}\mathbbm{1}\{R_{k}\leq 1+\epsilon\}}{K}

(5.45)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(R_{0}\leq 1+\epsilon).

(5.46)

Taking
\epsilon\to 0
, we can replace the last bound by
\mathbb{P}(R_{0}\leq 1)
. Repeating this calculation with a matching lower bound leaves us with

\lim_{K\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{K}\mathbbm{1}\{\overline{b}(t_{k})\leq 2^{k}\}}{K}=\mathbb{P}(R_{0}\leq 1)>0.

(5.47)

∎

Proof of
Proposition

5.4
.

Recall the expression for
p_{k}
from (

), which gives the probability that
b(t_{k})\neq b(t_{k-1})
and hence there is a jump point in

makes clear that whether this happens is a function of fresh, independent inputs whose only dependence on the trajectory thus far is through the value of

p_{k}=\frac{1}{2}(1-2^{-k})^{b(t_{k-1})}\geq\frac{1}{2}(1-2^{-k})^{\overline{b}(t_{k-1})}.

(5.48)

If
k\in A
, the set from
Lemma

, then we have a further estimate

p_{k}\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{-(1+\epsilon)}>0,

(5.49)

uniformly for all
k
large enough. Then
Lemma

5.7
shows that this estimate holds a positive proportion of the
k
, and thus there must be a jump for
b
, within
(t_{k-1},t_{k}]
for a positive proportion of the

.
Lemma

5.6
says that
T_{N}
will typically be around
1-N^{-1}
, so there will be of order

such dyadic intervals before we hit

∎

5.1.2
An upper bound on jumps

For the upper bound, we wish to show that the number of jumps of the discretised process is a good proxy for the total number in the original process, and then give an upper bound on the former. The dyadic spacing may no longer be sufficient, so we now choose
\alpha>1
and look at times
t_{k}=1-\alpha^{-k}
. Taking
\alpha\searrow 1
improves the accuracy of our discretisation.

Proposition 5.8
.

There is
C_{2}<1
such that

\lvert\mathcal{B}\cap[0,N]\rvert\leq C_{2}\log N

(5.50)

for all
N
large enough, almost surely.

Remark 5.9
.

We can prove (
5.50
) for general continuous weights if we allow
C_{2}=1
, and in particular we don’t need the power of
Theorem

5.2
. Observe that the geodesic
\gamma_{(1,k)}
will branch off the vertical axis at the height
0\leq j\leq k
such that
W({(0,j)})
is minimised, and so
k
will fail to be in
\mathcal{B}
unless
W({(0,k)})
sets a new minimum for the weights along the vertical axis. The behaviour of such minima is discussed in
[
Dur19
, Example 2.3.10]
, where it is shown that up to height
N
, there will asymptotically be
\log N
minima.

Lemma 5.10
.

There is a coupling of
\{b(t)\}
and the process
\{X(t)\}
defined above, such that if
\mathcal{D}(b)=(d_{0},d_{1},\dots)
and
\mathcal{D}(X)=(d^{\prime}_{0},d^{\prime}_{1},\dots)
are the jump points for the processes, we have

\frac{1-d_{k+1}}{1-d_{k}}\leq\frac{1-d^{\prime}_{k+1}}{1-d^{\prime}_{k}}

(5.51)

for all
k\geq 0
. That is, on a logarithmic scale, the times
\{1-d_{k}\}
are farther separated than are
\{1-d^{\prime}_{k}\}
.

Proof.

Recall that we define
d_{0}=d^{\prime}_{0}=0
, and have
b(0)=X(0)=0
. For both processes, the first jump is chosen uniformly on
[0,1]
, so we may let
d_{1}=d^{\prime}_{1}
. Subsequently, suppose we have sampled the first
k
points such that (
5.51
) holds and wish to do the same with the
k+1
-th. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1-d_{k+1}}{1-d_{k}}\geq r\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(d_{k+1}\leq 1-(1-d_{k})r\right)

(5.52)

\displaystyle=(1-(1-d_{k})r)^{b(d_{k})}(1-r)

(5.53)

\displaystyle\leq 1-r

(5.54)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1-d^{\prime}_{k+1}}{1-d^{\prime}_{k}}\geq r\right),

(5.55)

so we may sample
d_{k+1}
and
d^{\prime}_{k+1}
such the relation
Eq.

5.51
continues to hold. ∎

Lemma 5.11
.

With
t_{k}=1-\alpha^{-k}
as above, let
D_{k}=\{\mathcal{D}(b)\cap[t_{k},t_{k+1}]\neq\emptyset\}=\{b(t_{k})\neq b(t_{k+1})\}
. Then we have

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{l=0}^{k}\mathbbm{1}_{D_{l}}}{\lvert\mathcal{D}(b)\cap[0,1-t_{k}]\rvert}\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}.

(5.56)

That is, in lieu of counting the actual number of zeroes up to
t_{k+1}
, we may count only the number of times that
b(t_{l})\neq b(t_{l+1})
,
0\leq l\leq k
, and in doing so we miss at most a portion of
\alpha^{-1}
of the total.

Proof.

Consider the jumps of the process
\{X(t)\}
, which we again write as
\mathcal{D}(X)=(d^{\prime}_{0},d^{\prime}_{1},\dots)
. Suppose
d^{\prime}_{k}
lies in
[t_{l},t_{l+1}]
. Conditional on
d^{\prime}_{k}
,
1-d^{\prime}_{k+1}
is distributed uniformly in
[0,1-d^{\prime}_{k}]
. It will certainly lie beyond
t_{l+1}
if

\frac{1-d^{\prime}_{k+1}}{1-d^{\prime}_{k}}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}.

(5.57)

This happens with probability
\alpha^{-1}
, and the ratios in (
5.57
) are independent across different values of
k
. This implies the statement of the lemma for the points
d^{\prime}_{k}
.

As before, write
\mathcal{D}(b)=(d_{0},d_{1},\dots)
for the jumps of
\{b(t)\}
. The coupling from
Lemma

5.10
gives us

\frac{1-d^{\prime}_{k+1}}{1-d^{\prime}_{k}}\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}\Rightarrow\frac{1-d_{k+1}}{1-d_{k}}\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}.

(5.58)

We see then that the statement must also hold for the
d_{k}
.
∎

At this point, we may notice that since
\mathbb{P}(b(t_{k+1})\neq b(t_{k}))=(1-\alpha^{-1})t_{k+1}^{b(t_{k})}\leq(1-\alpha^{-1})
, and since these jumps capture at least a portion of
\alpha^{-1}
of the total, we have found the following asymptotic bound for the number of jumps up until time

:

\alpha\cdot(1-\alpha^{-1})\cdot\log_{\alpha}(1-t)=\frac{(\alpha-1)\log(1-t)}{\log\alpha}

(5.59)

Taking
\alpha\searrow 1
brings this upper bound to simply
\log(1-t)
. Along with
Lemma

5.6
, would give the desired upper bound in
Proposition

5.8
with constant
C_{2}=1
. That we can take
C<1
means that the factor of

which we discarded does play a role.

Lemma 5.12
.

For
\epsilon>0
arbitrary and

large enough, we have that

\mathbb{P}(b(t_{k})\geq\alpha^{k}\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}b(t_{k})\neq b(t_{k-1}))\geq e^{-(1+\epsilon)}.

(5.60)

Proof.

Recall our description of the jump from

to

from the proof of

. While waiting for the first output of the relevant store, we must at least wait for the first non-zero input from the sequence

\mathrm{Geom}_{+}(\alpha^{k})

∎

Proof of
Proposition

5.8
.

Let
D=\{k\geq 0:b(t_{k})\neq b(t_{k-1})\}
be those indices where we see jumps in
\{b(t)\}
. Consider also
D_{\mathrm{ind}}
, where each
k\geq 0
is a member independently with probability
1-\alpha^{-1}
. We couple
D
and
D_{\mathrm{ind}}
in an obvious way. Take an i.i.d collection
\{U_{k}:k\geq 1\}
of
\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
random variables and put
0
in both sets. Assume that we have determined the membership for all
j\leq k
, and moreover we have a sample of
\{b(t):t\leq t_{k}\}
such that this process has jumps in the intervals indicated by
D
. We then add
k+1
to the sets according to the following rule:

\displaystyle U_{k+1}\leq 1-\alpha^{-1}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow k+1\in D_{\mathrm{ind}},

(5.61)

\displaystyle U_{k+1}\leq(1-\alpha^{-1})t_{k+1}^{b(t_{k})}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow k+1\in D.

(5.62)

We will try to show that for the correct choice of
\alpha>1
, we have

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\lvert D\cap[0,k]\rvert}{k}\leq C_{2}\log\alpha,

(5.63)

with
C_{2}<1
. We will compare it to
D_{\mathrm{ind}}
, for which we have trivially that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\lvert D_{\mathrm{ind}}\cap[0,k]\rvert}{k}=1-\alpha^{-1}=\log\alpha+o(\alpha-1).

(5.64)

Thus if we can show that a positive proportion of elements of
D_{\mathrm{ind}}
fail to lie in
D
, then (
5.63
) will follow from taking
\alpha\approx 1
in (
5.64
).

Rearranging (
5.61
) gives

\mathbb{P}(k+1\in D\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}k+1\in D_{\mathrm{ind}})=t_{k+1}^{b(t_{k})},

(5.65)

and also that conditional on the value of
b(t_{k})
, this event is independent of
\{b(t):t<t_{k}\}
. Suppose
k\in D
. By
Lemma

5.12
,

\mathbb{P}(b(t_{k})\geq\alpha^{k})\geq e^{-(1+\epsilon)},

(5.66)

for
\epsilon>0
arbitrary and
k
large enough. Let
Z=\min\{l\geq 1:k+l\in D_{\mathrm{ind}}\}
, the gap until the next element of

. Then

, independent of everything else. For
\alpha

,
\mathbb{P}(Z\leq\alpha/(\alpha-1))\geq 1-e^{-(1-\epsilon)}
. Using (
5.66

Proposition 5.14

5.3
Convoys on the vertical axis

For concreteness, let us prove
Theorem

2.18
(at least, the latter two claims) for exponential rate
1
weights. The techniques we use apply equally to Bernoulli and Bernoulli-geometric weights, except that the precise expressions and resulting constants will necessarily differ.

Also, rather than looking at the critical angle
\xi^{*}
, it will be helpful to look at the critical parameter
\rho^{*}\in(0,\infty)
with
B^{\xi^{*}}_{2}(0)\sim\mathrm{Ber}((1+\rho^{*})^{-1})\mathrm{Exp}(\rho^{*})
. This
\rho^{*}
is a decreasing function of
\xi^{*}
, so the various inequalities in
Theorem

2.18
should be reversed.

It will be useful to define some probabilities, whose meaning will become apparent when we introduce our random walk below:

1.

Let
q(\rho)=(1+\rho)^{-1}
be the probability that
B^{\rho}_{2}(ke_{2})\neq 0
.

2.

Let
p^{\uparrow}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=(1-q(\rho_{1}))q(\rho_{2})+q(\rho_{1})q(\rho_{2})\rho_{1}(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})^{-1}
,
p^{\rightarrow}=(1-q(\rho_{1}))(1-q(\rho_{2}))
and
p^{\downarrow}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=1-p^{\uparrow}-p^{\rightarrow}
be the probabilities of going up, flat, and down, respectively, in the random walk.

3.

Let
q^{\uparrow}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=p^{\uparrow}(1-p^{\downarrow})^{-1}
and
q^{\rightarrow}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=p^{\rightarrow}(1-p^{\downarrow})^{-1}
be the probabilities when we condition on the step being up or flat.

Proposition 5.15
.

For a fixed
\rho>0
, the set
\{k:\rho^{*}(ke_{2})\geq\rho\}
is a Bernoulli process with density
q(\rho)=(1+\rho)^{-1}
.

Proof.

We have
\rho^{*}(ke_{2})\geq\rho
if and only if
B^{\rho}_{2}(ke_{2})\neq 0
, which happens with probability
q(\rho)
. Moreover, the variables
\{B^{\rho}_{2}(ke_{2}):k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
are all independent.
∎

Proposition 5.16
.

For fixed
0<\rho_{1}<\rho_{2}
, the set
C(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=\{k:\rho_{1}\leq\rho^{*}(ke_{2})\leq\rho_{2}\}
is a renewal process whose holding times may be represented in terms of stopping times in a particular random walk. Specifically, consider
S_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}X_{j}
, where
X_{j}=X^{2}_{j}-X^{1}_{j}
, and

X^{1}_{j}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(q(\rho_{1}))\mathrm{Exp}(\rho_{1}),\quad X^{2}_{j}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(q(\rho_{2}))\mathrm{Exp}(\rho_{2})

(5.100)

are i.i.d. Define
\tau=\min\{k\geq 1:S_{k}<S_{k-1}\leq\min_{0\leq j\leq k-1}S_{k}\}
, and let
\tau^{1},\,\tau^{2},\dots
be independent copies of
\tau
. Let
p^{\downarrow\downarrow}
be the probability defined below and take
Z\sim\mathrm{Geom}_{+}(p^{\downarrow\downarrow})
. Then the increments between elements of
C(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
are independent and equal in distribution to
\sum_{n=1}^{Z}\tau^{n}
.

Proof.

We look at our multi-line representation and identify this walk
S_{k}
. Suppose
0\in C(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
, and let
I^{1},\,I^{2}
be the inputs into our multi-line process. The distribution of the sequence
\{(B^{\rho_{1}}_{2}(ke_{2}),B^{\rho_{2}}_{2}(ke_{2})):k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is that of
(I^{1},H(I^{2},I^{1}))
. Write
\widetilde{I}^{2}=H(I^{2},I^{1})
. That
0
belongs to
C(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
means that
I^{1}_{0}>0
and
\widetilde{I}^{2}_{0}=0
. The store had nonzero service at time
0
but zero output, meaning that the store was empty before this time and that
I^{2}_{0}=0
.

We will have another element of
C(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
the next time the store is empty and subsequently receives zero input and nonzero service. Consider
S_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(I^{2}_{j}-I^{1}_{j})
. An increase in
S_{k}
means that the input was greater than the available service and thus the quantity in the store has increased. A decrease in
S_{k}
means that the store has decreased (possibly to zero). The store is empty exactly when
S_{k}
attains its (weak) running minimum. If
S_{k-1}
is a running minimum, then the store is empty at the beginning of time step
k
. If the value of the walk decreases at
S_{k}
, then the store had nonzero service and possibly zero input. Conditional on a down step, the probability the input was zero is

p^{\downarrow\downarrow}=\frac{(1-q(\rho_{2}))q(\rho_{1})}{p^{\downarrow}}=\frac{\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}}{1+\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}}.

(5.101)

In this case we have found our next element of
C(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
. Otherwise, the store is again empty and we begin the wait once more.
∎

To make the dependence on the parameters more explicit, denote by
\tau(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
the stopping time in
Proposition

5.16
. So long as
\rho_{1}<\rho_{2}
,
\tau(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
has finite mean and moreover, exponential decay (as the return time of a biased random walk). From ergodicity considerations, we know that the density of
C(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
must be
q(\rho_{1})-q(\rho_{2})
, and so we must have

\mathbb{E}[\tau(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})]=\frac{(1+\rho_{1})(1+\rho_{2})}{\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}}.

(5.102)

We could also derive this through Wald’s identities, and we will take this approach later on.

The more interesting case is when we look at
C(\rho)=\{k:\rho^{*}(ke_{2})=\rho\}
. Since
\rho^{*}
is continuously distributed, this set must be empty almost surely. But if we condition on it being nonempty, then we get the same description as above, now with
\tau(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
replaced by
\tau(\rho)=\tau(\rho,\rho)
.

Proposition 5.17
.

Let
\rho=\rho^{*}(0)
. Let
\tau^{1},\tau^{2},\dots
be independent copies of
\tau(\rho)
and let
Z\sim\mathrm{Geom}_{+}(p^{\downarrow\downarrow})
, where now
p^{\downarrow\downarrow}=2\rho(1+2\rho)^{-1}
. Then the convoy for

.

Proof.

We have in mind the same situation as in
Proposition

5.16
, now taking
\rho_{2}\searrow\rho_{1}
. We are left with two sets of independent inputs
I^{1},\,I^{2}
, both with
\mathrm{Ber}(q(\rho))\mathrm{Exp}(\rho)
marginals. In general there may be difficulties defining
H(I^{2},I^{1})
, but our conditioning means that we may assume the store is empty before and after service at time
0
. Now we repeat the argument from
Proposition

5.16
linking elements of our set to
\tau(\rho)
.
∎

We turn to finding the number of vertices in our convey below a given height, or equivalently the number of renewals before a given time. If the mean of the holding time were finite then this would just be an application of the renewal theorem. However, the mean here is infinite, as seen from taking
\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}
in (
5.102
), or by ergodicity considerations. We use an extension of the standard renewal theorem to heavy-tailed distributions:

Theorem 5.18

(Theorem 5 of
[
Eri70
]
)
.

Let
F
be the distribution for our holding times and suppose
1-F(x)=Lx^{-\alpha}+o(x^{-\alpha})
, for some
0<\alpha<1
. Let
U(n)
be the number of renewal times in
[0,n]
(where for simplicity we may condition on a renewal at time

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{U(n)}{n^{\alpha}}=\frac{1-\alpha}{L\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\ \mathrm{a.s}.

(5.103)

The full statement found in
[

]
is quite a bit more general, but this version will suffice. The theorem suggests that we should study the tails of our holding time distribution, but it will be easier to instead study tails of hitting times of our walk and relate the two.

Lemma 5.19
.

The stopping time
\tau
admits a further decomposition into a sum of geometrically many hitting times. Let
\sigma=\min\{k\geq 1:S_{k}\leq 0\mid S_{1}\geq 0\}
, or the first time the walk reaches zero conditioned on taking a non-negative first step. Then if
\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2},\dots
are independent copies of
\sigma
, we have
\tau\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}1+\sum_{j=1}^{Y}\sigma^{j}
, where
Y\sim\mathrm{Geom}_{0}(p^{\downarrow})
.

Proof.

Recall that
\tau
records the first time that we reach a strict running minimum, where the previous time is itself a running minimum. Initially the walk is at a running minimum, so we will get
\tau=1
if the first step is down, with probability
p^{\downarrow}
. Otherwise, the run takes a non-negative step, and we must wait for the walk to reach its prior low point. This time is
\sigma_{1}
. After time
\sigma_{1}
, we are again at a running minimum, and will have
\tau=1+\sigma_{1}
if we subsequently take a down step, and this situation happens with probability
(1-p^{\downarrow})p^{\downarrow}
. Iterating the argument gives the claimed decomposition.
∎

Random walks with Bernoulli or exponential increments are special in that their hitting times are particularly amenable to analysis through Wald’s identities. Still, the time we care about, the sum in
Proposition

5.16
, is rather complicated. It is necessary to first understand
\sigma
and go through the decomposition from the last lemma.

Lemma 5.20
.

Suppose
\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}=\rho
and set
\beta=\rho^{2}(1+\rho)^{-2}
. Let
\widetilde{\sigma}=(\sigma-1)\mid\{\sigma\geq 1\}
, just the value
\sigma-1
conditioned on having it being non-negative. Then
\widetilde{\sigma}
has characteristic function

\widetilde{\phi}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[e^{i\theta\widetilde{\sigma}}]=\frac{\sqrt{1-\beta e^{i\theta}}-\sqrt{1-e^{i\theta}}}{\sqrt{1-\beta e^{i\theta}}+\sqrt{1-e^{i\theta}}}.

(5.104)

Further, the generating function of
\widetilde{p}_{k}=\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\sigma}=k)
is

\widetilde{f}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{p}_{k}z^{k}=\frac{\sqrt{1-\beta z}-\sqrt{1-z}}{\sqrt{1-\beta z}+\sqrt{1-z}}.

(5.105)

Proof.

The characteristic function of our steps
X^{2}-X^{1}
is easily seen to be

\displaystyle M(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[e^{it(X^{2}-X^{1})}]

(5.106)

\displaystyle=\left\lparen(1-q(\rho))+\frac{q(\rho)}{1-it/\rho}\right\rparen\left\lparen(1-q(\rho))+\frac{q(\rho)}{1+it/\rho}\right\rparen

(5.107)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho^{2}((1+\rho)^{2}+t^{2})}{(1+\rho^{2})(\rho^{2}+t^{2})}.

(5.108)

Consider
\sigma^{a}=\min\{k\geq 0:S_{k}\leq-a\}
, the hitting time for
-a<0
. This
\sigma^{a}
is equal in distribution to
\widetilde{\sigma}
conditioned on
S_{1}=a
. Wald’s third identity applied here tells us that

1=\mathbb{E}\left[M(t)^{-\sigma^{a}}e^{itS_{\sigma^{a}}}\right].

(5.109)

Memorylessness means that
S_{\sigma^{a}}\sim-a-\mathrm{Exp}(\rho)
and is independent of
\sigma^{a}
. So we factor the expectation and get

1=\mathbb{E}\left[M(t)^{-\sigma^{a}}\right]e^{-ait}\mathbb{E}[e^{it(S_{\sigma^{a}}+a)}].

(5.110)

Plugging in the characteristic function for an exponential and rearranging, this becomes

e^{ait}(1+it/\rho)=\mathbb{E}\left[M(t)^{-\sigma^{a}}\right].

(5.111)

Now with

and taking an expectation over

(we condition it to be positive):

\frac{1+it/\rho}{1-it/\rho}=\mathbb{E}\left[M(t)^{-\widetilde{\sigma}}\right].

Now let

solve

. We would have

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{i\theta\widetilde{\sigma}}\right].=\frac{1+it(\theta)/\rho}{1-it(\theta)/\rho}.

(5.113)

One can solve this equation to find

t(\theta)=i\frac{\rho(1+\rho)\sqrt{1-e^{i\theta}}}{\sqrt{(1+\rho)^{2}-\rho^{2}e^{i\theta}}}.

(5.114)

Now plugging in to (
5.113
) gives the desired expression (
5.104
).

For the second claim, observe that
\widetilde{\phi}(\theta)=\widetilde{f}(e^{i\theta})
, and
\widetilde{\phi}
may be seen as the restriction of
\widetilde{f}
to the unit circle. Moreover,
\widetilde{f}
is analytic on the unit disc. The inversion formula for characteristic functions reduces here to the statement that Fourier coefficients of
\widetilde{\phi}
are the probabilities
\widetilde{p}_{k}
. The Fourier coefficients of
\widetilde{\phi}
are in turn the coefficients of the power series expansion of its extension

about the origin. This is the substance of (

).
∎

To recover the distribution of

, and so of

, or at least understand the asymptotic behaviour of its coefficients. Fortunately, we can simply borrow standard tools from the theory of analytic combinatorics, which exist to answer such questions.

Proposition 5.21
.

The probabilities
\widetilde{p}_{k}
have

\widetilde{p}_{k}=k^{-3/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(1-\beta)}}+o(k^{-3/2}).

Hence

has
p_{0}=q^{\rightarrow}
, and for

p_{k}=k^{-3/2}\frac{q^{\uparrow}}{\sqrt{\pi(1-\beta)}}+o(k^{-3/2}).

(5.116)

Proof.

The function of interest,

, may be written in a less symmetric but more approachable form as

\widetilde{f}(z)=\frac{1}{(1-\beta)z}\big\lparen 2-(1+\beta)z-2\sqrt{1-\beta z}\sqrt{1-z}\big\rparen.

(5.117)

Dividing by
z
just shifts the series coefficients downward, and the

term only affects the first two coefficients. For the asymptotic behaviour of the coefficients, it is enough to look at

-\frac{2}{1-\beta}\sqrt{1-\beta z}\sqrt{1-z}.

(5.118)

We look to
[
FS09
, Theorem VII.8]
, which states that it is enough to understand the function near

, then
[
FS09
, Theorem VI.1]
to see that the coefficients are approximately

-\frac{2}{1-\beta}\cdot\sqrt{1-\beta}\cdot\frac{-k^{-3/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}=k^{-3/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(1-\beta)}}.

(5.119)

In fact, the theorems we quoted may be used to find the asymptotic expansion up to arbitrary order, but we will not use anything beyond the leading term.
∎

Remark 5.22
.

The order of the

being

can be expected from what is known generally about return times of random walks

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\widetilde{U}(n)}{\sqrt{n}}=\mathbb{E}[Z]\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{U^{s}(n)}{\sqrt{n}}=\frac{p^{\downarrow}\sqrt{1-\beta}}{(1-p^{\downarrow})q^{\uparrow}\sqrt{\pi}}.

Remark B.2
.

The generalised Busemann functions are related to the underlying environment only through the adaptedness property. Thus, any proof which takes the above as its starting point and produces these objects as functions of the environment must use adaptedness in an essential way. The utility of this property was identified in
[
GRS16
]
, where it is connected to maximisers of variational formulas for the limit shape. It is also interesting to note that the adaptedness here is exactly the notion of max-plus harmonicity described in
[
AGW09
]
, the graph here being the integer lattice with up-right directed edges.

The property has been called

in the context of vertex-weight LPP
[
Sep18
]
, referring to the fact that one can completely recover the weight configuration from the collection of Busemann functions in a fixed direction. In edge-weight FPP we have only this weaker statement: if
\mathscr{U}_{0}
is dense, then almost surely the weight configuration is determined by the full collection of generalised Busemann functions.

We are interested in showing that these functions arise as limiting differences of the passage times, and moreover that they extend to a full-fledged
Busemann process
indexed by
\mathscr{U}
. Specifically, for a direction

and

(B.6)

is a sequence of vertices with limiting direction

and
\lvert v_{n}\rvert\to\infty
.

This task has been carried out in
[
JR20
]
for planar vertex-weight models. The extension to the remaining directions relies on the “path crossing trick” to give monotonicity, after which limits can be taken to extend the process from

Lemma B.3

Suppose

(B.8)

(B.9)

But the finite variance of the weights is enough to ensure that this term too goes to zero almost surely. This gives the lower bound

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\lvert v_{n}\rvert_{1}^{-1}L^{\mathrm{NE}}_{v_{n}-e_{1}\in\pi}(0,v_{n})\geq(s+t)^{-1}\max_{0\leq\tau\leq s}\{\ell(s-\tau,t)+\alpha\tau\}.

(B.42)

The argument for the upper bound is completely identical to the one in

]

∎

B.2
Construction of semi-infinite geodesics

Lastly, we prove that the procedure of
Remark

2.5
does indeed produce geodesics with the correct direction. We follow Section 8 of
[
Ras18
]
. Recall that we have an angle
\xi\in\mathscr{U}
and a procedure that produces a path
\pi=(x_{0},x_{1},\dots)
, such that
x_{k+1}-x_{k}\in\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
and such that
B^{\xi}(x_{k},x_{k+1})=W(x_{k},x_{k+1})
. For our purposes, we may assume that the limit shape
\ell
is differentiable and strictly convex at
\xi
.

Lemma B.11
.

The path
\pi
is a semi-infinite geodesic.

Proof.

Consider a truncated path
\pi^{(n)}=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
. The cocycle property of the Busemann functions and our construction mean that

B^{\xi}(x_{0},x_{n})=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}B^{\xi}(x_{k},x_{k+1})=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}W(x_{k},x_{k+1})=L(\pi^{(n)}).

(B.54)

On the other hand, if
\tilde{\pi}^{(n)}=(\tilde{x}_{0},\dots,\tilde{x}_{n})
is another path with the same endpoints,
\tilde{x}_{0}=x_{0}
and
\tilde{x}_{n}=x_{n}
, then we may also write

B^{\xi}(x_{0},x_{n})=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}B^{\xi}(\tilde{x}_{k},\tilde{x}_{k+1}).

(B.55)

However now, the adaptedness property means that
B^{\xi}(\tilde{x}_{k},\tilde{x}_{k+1})\leq W(\tilde{x}_{k},\tilde{x}_{k+1})
. Hence

L(\pi^{(n)})=B^{\xi}(x_{0},x_{n})=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}B^{\xi}(\tilde{x}_{k},\tilde{x}_{k+1})\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}W(\tilde{x}_{k},\tilde{x}_{k+1})=L(\tilde{\pi}^{(n)}).

(B.56)

Taking a minimum over the possible
\tilde{\pi}^{(n)}
shows that
L(\pi^{(n)})=L(x_{0},x_{n})
, and this segment is a geodesic. We may do this for any
n
.
∎

Lemma B.12
.

Suppose the limit shape
\ell
is differentiable and strictly convex at
\xi
, and take another direction
\zeta\in\mathscr{U}
. Almost surely, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{B^{\xi}(0,\lfloor n\zeta\rfloor)}{n}=\nabla\ell(\xi)\cdot\zeta.

(B.57)

Proof.

The assumption on the limit shape means that the Busemann increments
B^{\xi}_{i}(x)
are ergodic with respect to shifts. Let
\lfloor n\zeta\rfloor=(p_{n},q_{n})
and use the cocycle property to write

B^{\xi}(0,\lfloor n\zeta\rfloor)=\sum_{k=0}^{p_{n}-1}B^{\xi}((k,0),(k+1,0))+\sum_{l=0}^{q_{n}-1}B^{\xi}((p_{n},l),(p_{n},l+1)).

(B.58)

By the ergodic theorem, these sums have

\frac{1}{p_{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{p_{n}-1}B^{\xi}((k,0),(k+1,0))\to\mathbb{E}[B^{\xi}(0,e_{1})],\text{ and }\frac{1}{q_{n}}\sum_{l=0}^{q_{n}-1}B^{\xi}((p_{n},l),(p_{n},l+1))\to\mathbb{E}[B^{\xi}(0,e_{2})],

(B.59)

almost surely. Now note that
p_{n}/n\to\zeta\cdot e_{1}
and
q_{n}/n\to\zeta\cdot e_{2}
, and recall from
B.1.(iii)
that

(\mathbb{E}[B^{\xi}(0,e_{1})],\mathbb{E}[B^{\xi}(0,e_{2})])=\nabla\ell(\xi).

(B.60)

We put everything together to conclude that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{B^{\xi}(0,\lfloor n\zeta\rfloor)}{n}=\mathbb{E}[B^{\xi}(0,e_{1})](\zeta\cdot e_{1})+\mathbb{E}[B^{\xi}(0,e_{2})](\zeta\cdot e_{2})=\nabla\ell(\xi)\cdot\zeta.

(B.61)

∎

Proposition B.13
.

Suppose the limit shape
\ell
is differentiable and strictly convex at
\xi
. Then almost surely
\pi
is a semi-infinite geodesic with direction
\xi
(that is,
x_{n}/n\to\xi
).

Proof.

We have already shown that it is a geodesic in
Lemma

B.11
. For the directedness, note that by compactness the sequence
\{x_{n}/n\}
does have limit points. Suppose that we have a subsequence
x_{n_{i}}
such that
x_{n_{i}}/n_{i}\to\zeta
. We would like to show
\zeta=\xi
. From
Lemma

B.12
we see that

\frac{B(x_{0},x_{n_{i}})}{n_{i}}\to\nabla\ell(\xi)\cdot\zeta.

(B.62)

But our argument in
Lemma

B.11
shows that
B(x_{0},x_{n_{i}})=L(x_{0},x_{n_{i}})
. Thus also

\frac{B(x_{0},x_{n_{i}})}{n_{i}}\to\ell(\zeta),

(B.63)

thus showing that
\ell(\zeta)=\zeta\cdot\nabla\ell(\xi)
.

At this point we observe that differentiating the scaling relation
\ell(t\xi)=t\ell(\xi)
shows that
\ell(\xi)=\xi\cdot\nabla\ell(\xi)
. Subtracting this from the equation derived in the previous paragraph,

\ell(\zeta)-\ell(\xi)=(\zeta-\xi)\cdot\nabla\ell(\xi).

(B.64)

Consider the convex function

f(t)=\ell(t\xi+(1-t)\zeta)-(t\xi+(1-t)\zeta)\nabla\ell(\xi)-\ell(\xi)+\xi\cdot\nabla\ell(\xi).

(B.65)

This
f
satisfies
f(0)=f(1)=0
and
f^{\prime}(1-)=0
. Hence
f
is identically zero on
[0,1]
, and

\ell(t\xi+(1-t)\zeta)=\ell(\xi)+(1-t)(\zeta-\xi)\cdot\nabla\ell(\xi).

(B.66)

Thus
\ell
is affine along the line connecting
\zeta
and
\xi
. We have assumed
\ell
is strictly convex at
\xi
, and so the only possibility is
\zeta=\xi
.
∎
