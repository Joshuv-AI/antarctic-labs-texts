Association between estimated glucose disposal rate and testosterone deficiency in male patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Wenmiao Pan, Jianyong Zhao, Jing Xu, Liang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsulin resistance (IR) is intricately associated with the reduction or deficiency of testosterone (TD) in males. Estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) has emerged as a novel marker for assessing IR. This study aims to examine the association between eGDR and total testosterone, as well as the risk of TD, in male patients diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).MethodsThis cross-sectional study evaluated 957 male patients with T2DM admitted to the Second Affiliated Hospital and Yuying Children’s Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University between 2019 and 2022. The study explored the association between eGDR and testosterone, as well as the risk of TD, utilizing multivariable linear and logistic regression analyses. Additionally, the study elucidated the mediating roles of AIP and SII in the effect of eGDR on TD through the mediation analysis.ResultsAfter adjusting for potential confounding variables, eGDR demonstrates a significant negative linear association with the risk of TD (OR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.77, 0.89) and a significant positive linear association with total testosterone (β = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.10, 0.19). The subgroup analyses showed that this association was stronger in individuals younger than 60. Additionally, AUC for eGDR was significantly higher (AUC = 0.656, 95% CI: 0.618, 0.695) compared to waist circumference, HbA1c, and TyG. Mediation analysis identified that AIP and SII accounted for 14.9% and 8.8% of the association between eGDR and TD, respectively.ConclusionThe study demonstrates that, in adult patients with T2DM, an elevated eGDR is positively associated with testosterone and inversely associated with the risk of TD.Clinical trialNot applicable.Keywords: eGDR, Testosterone deficiency, Insulin resistance, Diabetes mellitus, Obesity

Introduction

Testosterone, a principal androgen in male reproductive physiology, is integral to the development of sexual desire, erectile function, and secondary sexual characteristics [1, 2]. It is vital for maintaining bone density, promoting muscle growth, supporting the brain and nervous system, and ensuring cognitive health by sustaining normal testosterone levels in males [3, 4]. Studies indicate that low testosterone levels are common among males with metabolic syndrome (MetS), including conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [5–8]. Hypogonadism is observed in approximately one-third of males with T2DM [9, 10]. An expanding body of evidence indicates that males with low testosterone levels have reduced survival rates and increased mortality rates from all causes, including cardiovascular, cancer, and respiratory conditions, compared to males with normal or elevated testosterone levels [11, 12]. Therefore, it is crucial to monitor testosterone levels in patients with T2DM.

IR is a critical determinant in the onset and progression of cardiometabolic diseases. In this context, numerous studies underscore the association between IR and TD. Souteiro et al. [13] identified IR as the principal risk factor for TD in obese males, noting that some males with TD exhibit a higher IR than individuals with mild DM. A separate pilot study demonstrates that Metformin, a pharmacological agent used in the management of DM, has the potential to restore testosterone in male patients with T2DM by ameliorating IR [14]. Moreover, in cases of late-onset hypogonadism and functional hypogonadism, the association between TD and metabolic disorders has been established as bidirectional [15]. Consequently, the association between IR and male testosterone is emerging as a focal point of contemporary research.

The complex and lengthy nature, as well as the limited scope, of traditional IR evaluations such as HOMA-IR, have driven the search for other methods. eGDR, integrated with readily accessible clinical parameters such as hypertension, WC, and HbA1c, is considered a simple and effective surrogate marker for IR in individuals with T1DM [16]. Prior studies have expanded the application of eGDR to encompass patients with T2DM, acute ischemic stroke, and non-diabetic cohorts. These investigations have revealed a significant association between eGDR and a range of outcomes, such as diabetic complications, CVD, and all-cause mortality [17–20]. Nonetheless, the association between eGDR and TD has yet to be explored.

Therefore, this study intends to explore the association between eGDR, testosterone, and TD in male patients with T2DM. Additionally, it aims to assess the predictive capability of eGDR in identifying TD. Through this research, the study aspires to provide new insights into the reproductive health of male patients with T2DM. We hypothesized that lower eGDR, reflecting greater IR, would be independently associated with a higher prevalence of TD in this population (chines).

Materials and methods

Subjects and research design

In this cross-sectional study, a cohort of 957 patients diagnosed with T2DM, admitted to the Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University from January 2019 to August 2022, were enrolled. Inclusion criteria included a diagnosis of T2DM according to WHO standards [21], being 18 years old or older, and having complete biochemical and clinical data available. Exclusion criteria included preexisting conditions such as hypogonadism, chronic renal failure, hypopituitarism, exposure to radiation therapy, chronic alcoholism, or chronic liver disease.

Biochemical measurements

Upon admission, the following data were collected: diabetes duration (DD), diabetes medical treatment, history of hypertension, smoking status, blood pressure, drinking status, height, WC, and weight. Hyperlipidemia was defined by any of the following: TG ≥ 1.7mmol/L, TC ≥ 5.18 mmol/L, current use of hypolipidemic medications.

Following an overnight fast, serum samples were first taken between 7:30 a.m. and 9:30 a.m. The analysis included measurements of LDL-C, FPG, ALT, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), estradiol (E2), serum uric acid (UA), TC, luteinizing hormone (LH), TG, albumin, sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), creatinine, HDL-C, total testosterone, aspartate transaminase (AST), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, and platelet count, following previously established methodologies [22]. To measure the total serum testosterone levels, a competitive electrochemiluminescence immunoassay was utilized on the Elecsys automated analyzer to determine the serum testosterone levels of these samples, with a minimum linear limit of 0.02 ng/ml. According to the guidelines set forth by the American Urological Association [23], TD is characterized by total testosteron of less than 3 ng/ml.

Measurement of eGDR

eGDR = 21.158 - (3.407×hypertension) - (WC× 0.09) - (0.551×HbA1C [%]), where hypertension was coded as 0 for absence and 1 for presence [24, 25].

Ascertainment of AIP, SII, and TyG

AIP = log10 [TG/ HDL-C] [26].

SII = platelet count*neutrophil count/lymphocyte count [27].

TyG = Ln[FPG (mg/dL) ×TG (mg/dL) /2] [28].

Statistical analysis

Patients were stratified into quartiles based on their eGDR (Q1: <4.99; Q2: 4.99–6.53; Q3: 6.53–8.24; Q4: >8.24). Two regression analyses were performed: (1) a multivariate linear regression analysis to explore the link between eGDR and total testosterone, with results presented as β (95% CI). (2) A multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine the link between eGDR and TD, with outcomes presented as OR (95% CI). Variables demonstrating clinical significance and statistical significance in the univariate analysis (P < 0.05) were incorporated into the multivariate analyses. In Model 1, there were no adjustments for covariates. Model 2 was accounted for age and BMI. Model 3 was further adjusted by Model 2 including ALT, AST, GGT, TC, SHBG, insulin use, albumin, creatinine, LH, FSH, E2, smoking, TG, FPG, drinking, and metformin use, uric acid, HDL-C, SBP, and DBP.

Subgroup analysis was employed to stratify patients based on BMI, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, hyperuricemia, HbA1c levels, and age. To explore the potential nonlinear association between eGDR and testosterone, as well as the prevalence of TD, restricted cubic spline regression was utilized. ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of eGDR, HbA1c, WC, and TyG in detecting TD. All statistical analyses were conducted with R software (Version 4.4.2).

Association of eGDR with testosterone level and TD

Among patients with T2DM and TD, eGDR was significantly reduced, as illustrated in Fig. 1. To elucidate the association between total testosterone, eGDR, and the risk of TD, comprehensive multivariate regression analyses were performed, the findings of which are detailed in Table 2. In Model 3, linear regression analysis notably showed a steady positive link between eGDR and testosterone (β = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.10, 0.19). Similarly, when continuous eGDR was split into quartiles, the positive association stayed statistically significant in all models. Subsequently, logistic regression analysis was used to explore the link between eGDR and the risk of TD (Table 3). Each of the three models showed a negative link between eGDR and the risk of TD, with Model 3 showing an OR of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.77, 0.89). Clinically, our findings suggest that for every 1-unit increase in eGDR, the odds of TD decrease by approximately 17%. This implies that improving insulin sensitivity, as reflected by higher eGDR values, may be beneficial in maintaining normal testosterone levels in male patients with T2DM.

Subgroup analysis

Figure 3 illustrates the outcomes of subgroup analyses stratified by age group, BMI, HbA1c, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and hyperuricemia. Except for age subgroups, the majority of other subgroups exhibited a significant positive association between eGDR and total testosterone (all p for interaction > 0.05). The association between eGDR and testosterone was more pronounced in patients with T2DM who were younger than 60 (β = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.09, 0.22). Similarly, except for age subgroup, the inverse association between eGDR and the risk of TD was uniform across all other subgroups. Within the age subgroups, the association between eGDR and the risk of TD was more pronounced in participants younger than 60 (OR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.73–0.89).

Discussion

The findings indicate that elevated eGDR in male patients with T2DM is positively associated with serum testosterone and inversely associated with the risk of TD. Subgroup analyses and interaction tests verified the consistency of these association across various subgroups. Mediation analysis further revealed that AIP and SII partially mediated the association between eGDR and TD. Additionally, ROC analysis demonstrated that eGDR possesses superior predictive capabilities for TD compared to WC, HbA1c, and TyG.

The prevalent risk factors contributing to TD encompass advanced obesity, age, DM, and hyperlipidemia. IR represents a pivotal physiological mechanism in metabolic disorders and is regarded as a crucial independent predictor of TD. Furthermore, the association between IR and TD is established as bidirectional. IR can lead to a decrease in testosterone, which subsequently exacerbates obesity and IR itself [15]. Tsai et al. [29] conducted a study measuring testosterone in 221 middle-aged males without DM, finding a significant negative association between testosterone and both C-peptide and insulin levels through multivariate analysis. Similarly, a study involving 2,361 Chinese males aged 20 to 73 demonstrated a persistent negative association between testosterone and metabolic syndrome [30]. Currently, HIEC is widely recognized as the gold standard for evaluating IR [31]. However, the necessity for frequent arterial blood glucose measurements limits its practical application [32, 33]. Therefore, there is an imperative need for a more straightforward and feasible method for assessing IR.

eGDR, along with easily obtainable clinical parameters such as WC, HbA1c, and hypertension, has been suggested as a simple substitute marker for IR in patients with T1DM [16]. Previous research has demonstrated that this alternative method can achieve a high degree of accuracy compared to established gold standard measures [16, 34]. Furthermore, eGDR has been identified as an independent predictor of coronary artery disease [35], depression [24], all-cause mortality [36], and peripheral vascular diseases [37] in patients with T1DM. Recent research has broadened the investigation of eGDR’s applicability to encompass non-diabetic individuals [38], patients with T2DM [34, 39], and those undergoing acute ischemic stroke [34, 40]. Considering the shared risk factors between CVD and TD [41], it is reasonable to hypothesize a potential association between eGDR and TD. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the inaugural exploration of this association.

Furthermore, the study demonstrated that AUC for eGDR was significantly greater than that of other IR indicators, such as TyG, as well as surrogate markers for obesity, such as WC, indicating the potential clinical utility of eGDR in future applications. These findings are consistent with prior research. Peng et al. conducted a comparative evaluation of various indicators of insulin sensitivity, including HOMA-IR and TyG, and found that eGDR exhibits a stronger association with IR. This is primarily because TyG is susceptible to short-term fluctuations in a participant’s fasting glucose and TG, which can affect the results [42]. In contrast, eGDR has been shown to be less sensitive to short-term variations in blood glucose levels, thereby offering a more comprehensive assessment of insulin sensitivity [42, 43].

The findings from subgroup analyses further highlight the intricate association between eGDR, testosterone, and the prevalence of TD. The analysis revealed that the inverse association between eGDR and TD was more pronounced in individuals under 60. It is widely acknowledged that younger individuals demonstrate a heightened susceptibility to insulin [44, 45], which consequently leads to reduced eGDR within this population [34, 46, 47]. Furthermore, it is plausible that with advancing age, individuals may encounter an increased number of risk factors for metabolic diseases. Consequently, the influence of IR on the prevalence of TD may be relatively limited among older patients with T2DM. In contrast, within younger populations, who are generally regarded as healthier and lacking other potential risk factors, the association between IR and TD is expected to be more pronounced.

The results suggest that SII and AIP partially mediate the association between IR, as quantified by eGDR, and T2DM, underscoring the critical importance of monitoring inflammation and lipid levels in patients with low eGDR. IR is strongly associated with elevated plasma pro-inflammatory cytokines [48, 49], and related research has demonstrated that pro-inflammatory cytokines are significantly higher in patients with T2DM compared to healthy individuals [50]. IR notably elevates the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, and TNFα, which subsequently stimulate hepatic production of additional cytokines [51]. Furthermore, chronic inflammation is considered the main reason for low testosterone levels in the obese population because in a state of inflammation, inflammatory cytokines are mainly derived from adipose tissue, which can reduce testosterone production by converting testosterone to estradiol [52]. Consequently, the modulation of SII may increase the risk of TD in patients with T2DM who exhibit low eGDR. Moreover, contemporary medical research indicates that individuals with dyslipidemia frequently present with reduced testosterone levels, and AIP, as an emerging lipid biomarker, can effectively reflect the characteristics and severity of dyslipidemic processes [53]. These findings contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the intricate interactions among IR, inflammation, dyslipidemia, and TD.

The association between eGDR and the reduction in male testosterone is not yet fully understood. This association may be elucidated by considering the following factors: Firstly, eGDR is a composite index encompassing blood pressure, glucose metabolism, and obesity status. An abnormal eGDR value frequently indicates the presence of hypertension, hyperglycemia, and obesity. Research indicates that insulin plays a crucial role in sustaining normal testosterone levels by stimulating the expression of GnRH in hypothalamic neurons, thereby enhancing GnRH secretion [54]. Conversely, hyperglycemia has been associated with a reduction in the expression of mitochondrial acetylase 3, which impairs mitochondrial function and the activity of insulin receptors in hypothalamic neurons. This impairment subsequently leads to a decreased expression of GnRH genes and proteins, ultimately inhibiting GnRH neuronal activity and resulting in lowered testosterone levels [55]. Furthermore, hyperglycemia can directly lead to a reduction in testosterone production by Leydig cells through the activation of the TLR4-mediated oxidative stress pathway [56]. Moreover, aromatase, found in fat cells, mainly converts testosterone into estradiol in peripheral tissues. In obese individuals, the higher number of adipocytes leads to increased aromatase expression, which boosts the peripheral conversion of testosterone [57]. Thirdly, the reduction in testosterone levels may be associated with an increase in arterial stiffness within the hypertensive population [58]. Finally, mechanisms related to obesity-induced leptin resistance, along with inflammatory factors, mediate inflammatory responses that suppress the function of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis in obese males, resulting in decreased testosterone [59, 60].

Considering that the eGDR can be determined using readily accessible clinical parameters—namely, hypertension status, WC, and HbA1c—it provides a cost-effective and accessible method for assessing IR and identifying patients at increased risk for TD. This is particularly advantageous in primary care or metabolic disease settings, where direct hormonal testing may be delayed or unavailable. Early identification of these patients enables clinicians to implement targeted interventions, such as lifestyle modifications or pharmacological therapies, aimed at enhancing insulin sensitivity and potentially mitigating TD.

This study is subject to certain limitations. Primarily, the cross-sectional design of this study precludes the establishment of a causal relationship between eGDR and TD. Although it is possible that reduced eGDR contributes to TD, it is equally plausible that low testosterone levels may promote IR. This bidirectional relationship warrants cautious interpretation, intervention experiments, and longitudinal studies are needed to clarify causality. Secondly, the study’s focus on male patients with T2DM from a single hospital may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population. Thirdly, the study could not gather data on testicular volume, preventing its use as a covariate in the analysis. Fourthly, the study is limited by the absence of data on free testosterone, which constrains the comprehensiveness of our findings. Fifthly, the diagnosis of hypogonadism was based exclusively on testosterone levels, without incorporating clinical evaluations of the patients. Finally, we employed the electrochemiluminescence immunoassay method to measure testosterone levels, a technique extensively utilized in epidemiological research [61, 62]. However, this assay may yield slightly less precise measurements compared to chromatography, potentially resulting in inaccurate classifications of TD. Consequently, future investigations aiming to validate these findings should contemplate the use of chromatography-based techniques for more precise quantification of testosterone levels.

This study presents a significant advantage in that it thoroughly characterizes subjects based on a large population, employing subgroup analysis to assess potential differences in eGDR and testosterone across diverse populations, thereby enhancing the reliability of the findings.

Conclusion

This study identified a positive association between eGDR and testosterone, as well as a negative association between eGDR and the risk of TD in male patients with T2DM. These findings indicate that eGDR may serve as a valuable biomarker for assessing testicular health and for identifying the risk of TD in male patients with T2DM, thereby facilitating early intervention and detection.
