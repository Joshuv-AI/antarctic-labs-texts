Hemorrhagic shock revealing a spontaneous rupture of a splenic artery aneurysm in a young patient without prior medical history: A rare case and literature review
Authors: Hicham Benramdane, Hajar Errahal, Adnane Boubacar, Hamid Ziani, Siham Nasri, Imane Kamaoui, Imane Skiker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Splenic artery aneurysm (SAA) is a rare condition, and its spontaneous rupture is life-threatening. It usually occurs in patients with known risk factors such as pregnancy or portal hypertension.We report the case of a 29-year-old male with no prior medical history who presented with sudden abdominal pain and hemorrhagic shock. Contrast-enhanced CT angiography revealed massive hemoperitoneum due to spontaneous rupture of a splenic artery aneurysm. Despite emergency surgical management, the patient died.This case is important because it demonstrates that splenic artery aneurysm rupture can occur in young patients without classic risk factors, highlighting the need to consider this diagnosis in cases of unexplained hemorrhagic shock.

Introduction

Splenic artery aneurysms are a rare vascular entity but particularly significant due to their risk of rupture [1]. Their prevalence in the general population is estimated at <1%, although most remain asymptomatic and undiagnosed. They account for approximately 60% of visceral artery aneurysms and are more frequently observed in women, particularly multiparous women, with a peak incidence in the fifth to sixth decades of life [2].

Major risk factors include pregnancy, portal hypertension, liver cirrhosis, systemic hypertension, atherosclerosis, connective tissue disorders, and trauma. Spontaneous rupture of a splenic artery aneurysm is a serious surgical emergency with a high mortality rate. The risk of rupture is increased in symptomatic patients, during pregnancy, and in aneurysms larger than 2 cm, which is commonly used as a threshold for intervention [2].

Imaging, particularly contrast-enhanced CT, plays a crucial role in the early diagnosis and management. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography (CTA) is the diagnostic modality of choice, as it allows precise localization of the aneurysm, assessment of its size, and detection of active bleeding, while digital subtraction angiography remains the gold standard and enables simultaneous therapeutic embolization [3]. Treatment can be endovascular (embolization) or surgical (ligation, resection ± splenectomy). The choice of strategy mainly depends on the patient’s hemodynamic status and the size of the aneurysm [4].

Case presentation

The patient is a 29-year-old male with no significant past medical history and no known cardiovascular or hepatic risk factors. There was no history of recent trauma, persistent fever, jaundice, or constitutional symptoms prior to the acute episode.

He presented to the emergency department with several hours of diffuse abdominal pain associated with uncontrollable vomiting.

Initial physical examination revealed frank hypovolemic shock. The patient was tachycardic at 120 beats per minute and hypotensive, with a blood pressure of 104/45 mmHg, resulting in a wide pulse pressure of 59 mmHg. Clinical signs of peripheral hypoperfusion were present, including mucocutaneous pallor and cold extremities. Abdominal examination showed diffuse tenderness with guarding, highly suggestive of acute hemoperitoneum.

Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST) performed on admission revealed abundant free intraperitoneal fluid.

Urgent laboratory tests confirmed severe acute anemia (hemoglobin: 7.9 g/dL), mild coagulopathy (prothrombin time: 68%), leukocytosis (12,000/mm³), and elevated inflammatory markers (CRP: 129 mg/L). Serum lactate was markedly elevated at 6.8 mmol/L, confirming severe tissue hypoperfusion.

Immediate resuscitation was initiated with rapid infusion of crystalloids, followed by activation of a massive transfusion protocol. The patient received 6 units of packed red blood cells, 4 units of fresh frozen plasma, and 1 platelet concentrate prior to surgery. Despite aggressive resuscitation, he remained hemodynamically unstable.

The patient was transiently stabilized to allow urgent contrast-enhanced CT angiography, performed approximately 40 minutes after ED admission. He was transferred to the operating room within 25 minutes following imaging.

Abdominopelvic CT angiography confirmed rupture of a fusiform aneurysm measuring 22 mm in diameter, located in the distal third of the splenic artery, in close contact with the pancreatic tail. Imaging demonstrated a large peri‑gastric hematoma (135 × 45 mm) compressing the stomach, extensive hemoperitoneum, and infiltration of the peri‑splenic and peri‑pancreatic fat. Features of the CT hypoperfusion complex were also present.

Given the confirmed ruptured aneurysm and persistent hemorrhagic shock, the patient underwent emergency surgical exploration. A hemostatic splenectomy with ligation of the splenic artery was performed.

A distal (caudal) pancreatectomy was required due to direct involvement and contusion of the pancreatic tail by the expanding hematoma and difficulty achieving secure vascular control. Partial left adrenalectomy was performed because of adjacent hemorrhagic infiltration compromising hemostasis. The pancreatic stump was reinforced with stapling.

Postoperatively, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit with persistent hemodynamic instability (BP: 100/40 mmHg; HR: 120 bpm). Despite continued transfusion support and vasopressor therapy, his course was complicated by refractory hemorrhagic shock associated with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and subsequent multi-organ failure.

The patient died in the immediate postoperative period.

Discussion

Spontaneous rupture of a splenic artery aneurysm is a rare but potentially catastrophic event. It most commonly occurs in multiparous women, pregnant patients, or individuals with portal hypertension, trauma, or inflammatory diseases. However, cases have been reported in young adults with no medical history, as in our observation, highlighting that rupture can occur even in the absence of classic risk factors [5].

A particular clinical feature described in splenic artery aneurysm rupture is the “double rupture phenomenon,” characterized by an initial contained bleeding within the lesser sac followed by secondary free intraperitoneal rupture, leading to sudden hemodynamic collapse. This mechanism may explain transient stabilization before rapid deterioration [5].

The typical clinical presentation includes acute abdominal pain, hemodynamic instability, and signs of hypovolemic shock. These manifestations are often non-specific, making the diagnosis challenging. Imaging, particularly contrast-enhanced abdominal CT (CT angiography), is the examination of choice. It allows visualization of the aneurysm, localization of the rupture, and assessment of the extent of intraperitoneal hemorrhage, thereby guiding urgent therapeutic decisions. Several series report that CT angiography enables rapid preoperative diagnosis in over 90% of cases [6].

Treatment of splenic artery aneurysms relies on 2 main approaches: endovascular (embolization) and open surgery. Current recommendations generally consider a size threshold of 2 cm as an indication for treatment, even in asymptomatic patients, due to increased rupture risk. Aneurysms larger than 2-2.5 cm, symptomatic lesions, and those occurring during pregnancy carry a significantly higher risk of rupture. Endovascular embolization is less invasive and suitable for hemodynamically stable patients, though it may be complicated by splenic infarction or reperfusion requiring re-intervention. Open surgery (ligation or resection ± splenectomy) is preferred in cases of hemodynamic instability or massive rupture [3].

Our patient, young and without prior medical history, illustrates the rarity and severity of this condition. There was no significant diagnostic delay; however, the massive hemoperitoneum and extensive peri‑pancreatic hematoma observed on CT indicate that the severity of hemorrhage was the primary determinant of outcome. Despite rapid surgical management, the outcome was fatal, reflecting the high mortality rate reported in the literature for spontaneous ruptures, which can reach 25%-40%.

Additionally, the presence of CT hypoperfusion complex signs suggested profound systemic shock and may represent a poor prognostic indicator, reflecting severe and prolonged tissue hypoperfusion.

This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high clinical suspicion, even in patients considered low-risk, and the necessity of early diagnosis and aggressive management.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our observation illustrates the severity of a spontaneous rupture of a splenic artery in a young adult with no prior medical history and highlights the central role of imaging and rapid intervention in patient survival.
