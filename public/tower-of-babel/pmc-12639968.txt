Triple network disruption in medication overuse headache: functional signatures and clinical impact
Authors: Greta Demichelis, Davide Fedeli, Giuseppe Ciullo, Jean Paul Medina Carrion, Domenico D’Amico, Alessandra Erbetta, Ruben Gianeri, Stefania Ferraro, Danilo Antonio Montisano, Erika Guastafierro, Maria Grazia Bruzzone, Marina Grisoli, Alberto Raggi, Licia Grazzi, Anna Nigri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic migraine with medication overuse headache (CM-MOH) is a disabling neurological condition. CM-MOH is associated with functional impairment and high prevalence of psychiatric comorbidities. Neuroimaging research showed alterations in large-scale brain networks in chronic pain disorders. Among these, the so-called “triple network model” -Default Mode Network (DMN), Salience Network (SN), and Central Executive Network (CEN)- has garnered attention for its critical role in cognitive and emotional regulation. Dysregulation within and between these networks has been implicated in various chronic pain, substance abuse, and affective disorders. However, the role of triple network functional connectivity alterations in CM-MOH remains poorly understood. This study aims to investigate alterations in functional connectivity (FC) both within and between the triple networks and whole brain, and to explore how these neural changes relate to the clinical and psychiatric characteristics of CM-MOH.MethodsThis case-control study, 91 patients with CM-MOH and 35 healthy controls underwent clinical, neuropsychological, and resting-state fMRI assessments as part of the CM-MIND study. Patients were classified by type of medication overuse (triptans, NSAIDs, polyabusers). FC within and between the DMN, SN, and CEN, as well as whole-brain FC, was analyzed. Associations with clinical and psychiatric features (including allodynia, medication intake, anxiety, and depression) were examined using between-group comparisons and regression analyses.ResultsCompared to healthy controls, CM-MOH patients exhibited increased extra-network DMN and SN FC with sensorimotor regions, most pronounced in triptan abusers who also showed CEN-motor alterations. Moreover, SN nodes were associated with reduced global and local efficiency. Allodynia severity was associated with increased SN-sensorimotor and decreased DMN intra-network FC, while higher anxiety correlated with reduced DMN-amygdala/putamen FC. Migraine chronification and higher medication intake were linked to increased DMN and SN FC with temporal-occipital regions. Notably, polyabusers showed SN-CEN disconnection and concurrently enhanced coupling of both networks with temporo-occipital regions as an effect of medication intake.ConclusionsOur results suggest that CM-MOH involves maladaptive connectivity of the triple network model. Alterations in FC, mainly in SN and DMN, and in CEN to a lesser extent, might be associated with migraine chronification and pain embodiment, while altered CEN interactions might be associated with medication overuse.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-025-02207-9.

Introduction

Migraine is a prevalent neurological disorder affecting ~ 15.2% of the global population [1], particularly women of reproductive age. It represents a leading cause of disability, characterized by severe, recurrent headaches often accompanied by nausea, photophobia, and phonophobia. Despite its high prevalence, it frequently remains underdiagnosed and undertreated.

Chronic migraine (CM), defined by ≥ 15 headache days/month, may evolve into medication overuse headache (MOH), a specific form induced by excessive use of acute medication [2, 3]. The global prevalence of CM who develop MOH (CM-MOH) ranges from 0.6 to 7.1% [3–5]. CM-MOH severely impacts quality of life [6, 7] and is frequently comorbid with pain and psychiatric disorders [8, 9]. The COMOESTAS study [10] reported depression and anxiety in up to 40% and 27.7% of CM-MOH patients, respectively [9, 10]. Moreover, allodynia - an increased sensitivity to stimuli that leads to discomfort during routine activities (e.g., hair combing, showering) - affects ~ 60–70% of migraineurs [11–14].

CM-MOH commonly involves overuse of triptans, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and combination analgesics [15]. Triptans, target trigemino-cortical pathways, central to migraine pathophysiology [16–18], whereas NSAIDs, broadly inhibit prostaglandin synthesis [17, 18]. Combination analgesics, often mixing caffeine, acetaminophen, and other compounds, can also lead to MOH when overused (polyabuser patients) [19].

The pathophysiology of migraine and MOH has been extensively studied using advanced neuroimaging techniques [20], with particular emphasis on resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI). rs-fMRI has been instrumental in elucidating how brain functional connectivity (FC) adapts to continuous pain processing and modulation [21, 22]. Research involving patients with CM and CM-MOH has reported abnormal functional coactivation within three key networks: the default mode network (DMN), salience network (SN), and central executive network (CEN) [23–27]. These networks collectively form the “triple network model” [28]. The DMN, which includes the medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, and parts of the parietal lobe, is typically active during mind-wandering, self-referential processing, and when the brain is not focused on externally goal-directed tasks (“internal awareness”) [29]. By contrast, the CEN, involving the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and the posterior parietal cortex - also known as the fronto-parietal network - is activated during high-level cognitive operations, such as attention, working memory, problem-solving, and decision-making (“external awareness”) [30, 31]. The SN, comprising the anterior insula and the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex [30], plays a critical role in detecting salient external (sensory) and internal (autonomic and emotional) stimuli [30, 32] and in modulating the balance between the DMN and the CEN. Dysregulation in the interplay and balance both within and between these networks has been implicated in chronic pain, potentially contributing to the persistence of pain as well as the associated abnormal cognitive and emotional processes [28, 33–35]. Additionally, aberrant functioning of these networks has been linked to various psychopathological conditions, including depression and anxiety [36–38] as well as substance-use disorders [39, 40]. While prior studies examined FC alterations of these networks in CM and CM-MOH [23–27], few explored associations with drug overuse profiles, clinical features, and psychiatric symptoms.

This study aims to (i) characterize FC alterations across the triple network model (DMN, SN, and CEN) and whole brain, and (ii) investigate associations between FC patterns and CM-MOH clinical features, including medication overuse profiles, allodynia, depression, and anxiety.

Materials and methods

Subjects

CM-MOH patients were consecutively recruited at the Fondazione IRCCS Istituto Neurologico C. Besta (Milan) as part of the MIND-CM project [41]. All patients met CM and MOH diagnostic criteria (codes 1.3 and 8.2) according to the International Headache Society guidelines, 3rd edition [2, 42]. Diagnosis was appointed by neurologists with specific expertise in headache disorders on occasion of a structured withdrawal treatment at our headache center, either in ward or in day-hospital. The threshold for overuse is defined based on the ICHD-3 criteria for MOH, i.e. intake of medication for acute migraine treatment in more than 10 or 15 days per month, for three consecutive months: the threshold is set to 15 intakes for paracetamol and NSAIDs, and it is set at 10 intakes for all the other classes, namely ergotamine, triptans, opioids, combination-analgesics, and for multiple classes not individually overused. Exclusion criteria were other primary or secondary headache disorders, aura, pregnancy, neurological or psychiatric conditions, or missing MRI. Age- and sex-matched healthy participants (3:1 ratio) with no history of primary headaches, chronic pain, or neurological/psychiatric disorders were recruited. The study was approved by the Istituto C. Besta ethics committee (no. 51/2018), and all participants provided informed consent in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. The study followed the recommended strong consensus standards for fMRI research in migraine [43].

Behavioural and clinical assessment

Patients completed self-report questionnaires assessing headache impact (Headache Impact Test - HIT-6) [44], disability (WHO Disability Assessment Schedule - WHODAS-12) [45], cutaneous allodynia (Allodynia Symptoms Checklist - ASC-12) [11], and depression (Beck Depression Inventory – BDI-II) [46] and anxiety (State-Trait Anxiety Inventory – STAI-Y1, STAY-Y2) [47]. Clinical data included attack frequency and medication intake in the last 3 months, migraine duration (age at enrolment minus age at migraine onset), CM duration.

MRI assessment

All participants underwent MRI acquisition using a 3T scanner (Achieva-dStream, Philips) with a 32-head channel coil, during the interictal interval as assessed by a clinical interview, before detoxification. As part of the MRI protocol, a high-resolution structural 3D T1-weighted (T1w) image (repetition time [TR] = 8.11 s, echo time [TE] = 3.71 ms, field-of-view [FOV] = 240 × 240 mm, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3, flip angle = 8°, 185 sagittal slices) and a rs-fMRI sequence (gradient echo-planar imaging sequence, eyes opened, TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, FOV = 80 × 80 mm, voxel size = 3 × 3 × 3.2 mm3, slice gap = 0.4 mm, flip angle = 80°, 34 axial slices, 450 volumes, Phase Encoding direction = posterior/anterior) were acquired. All images were visually inspected by an expert neuroradiologist (A.E.) to exclude brain abnormalities or artifacts affecting the exam quality.

rs-fMRI pre-processing

Data quality was assessed using the MRIQC software [48]. CONN toolbox (v. 20.b; Matlab 2020b) [49] was used for rs-fMRI analysis with the “Default MNI” pipeline. For each participant, rs-fMRI images were realigned to the first volume, unwarped, and a slice-time correction was applied. Outlier volumes were identified with the ART toolbox (framewise displacement > 1.1 mm; global BOLD signal changes > 5 SD) [49]. T1w images were segmented to generate white matter and cerebrospinal fluid components, and both rs-fMRI and T1w images were then normalized to the MNI space. Functional images were smoothed with a 6 mm3 full width at half maximum (FWHM) gaussian kernel. Rs-fMRI data denoising was performed with the anatomical component-based noise correction (aCompCor denoising) to extract noise signals from white matter and cerebrospinal fluid components [49, 50]. Additionally, outlier volumes scrubbing parameters, realignment parameters, and ramping “effect of rest” (initial magnetization transient effects) were entered as nuisance regressors. Finally, a 0.008–0.1 Hz bandpass filter was applied.

Statistical analyses

Clinical data analysis

CM-MOH patients were classified into drug abuse profiles: triptan abusers, NSAIDs abusers, polyabusers. Medication intake was defined as the total dose count of triptans and NSAIDs per trimester. To determine whether CM-MOH patients, considered as a whole group or categorized by drug abuse profiles, differed from healthy controls or from each other in terms of demographic variables (age and sex) and clinical variables (attack frequency, medication intake, migraine duration, CM duration, ASC-12, STAI-Y1 and STAY-Y2, BDI-II), Kruskal–Wallis test, Mann-Whitney test, or Chi-square tests were employed using R 4.2.1, after verifying the normality of the data with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test.

rs-fMRI between-group analyses

Functional connectivity analyses were conducted on DMN (nodes: medial prefrontal cortex [MPFC], posterior cingulate cortex [PCC], bilateral inferior parietal lobules [LP]), SN (nodes: anterior cingulate cortex [ACC], bilateral anterior insula [AIns], rostral prefrontal cortex [RPFC], and supramarginal gyrus [SMG]), and CEN (nodes: bilateral lateral prefrontal cortex [LPFC], posterior parietal cortex [PPC]) using the CONN network atlas.

Two complementary approaches were used: (i) seed-to-voxel analyses to assess whole-brain connectivity patterns of each network, and (ii) ROI-to-ROI analyses to evaluate intra- and inter-network alterations within the triple network model.

In the seed-based analyses, for each network, the mean time series across all nodes of that network (i.e. seed) was obtained. This time course was used to compute Pearson’s correlation coefficients with the time series of each brain voxel. A Fisher R-to-Z transform was used to convert the correlation coefficients to Z scores. Seed-to-voxel connectivity maps were generated and entered into a second-level general linear model to investigate FC differences of DMN, SN, and CEN between CM-MOH patients and healthy controls and between patients of each drug abuse profile and healthy controls. Age and sex were considered as nuisance variables in the model. Results were Family-wise Error Rate (FWE) corrected at p < 0.05 at the cluster level and p < 0.001 uncorrected at the voxel level (default setting). For clusters showing significant differences between CM-MOH patients and healthy controls, functional connectivity values were extracted and compared across medication overuse subgroups and healthy controls using the Mann-Whitney test. In the ROI-to-ROI analyses, 15 × 15 connectivity matrices were generated for each subject by computing Pearson’s correlations between the time series of each node and all the other nodes of the DMN, SN, and CEN. Correlation values were transformed into Z scores using Fisher’s R-to-Z transform. These matrices were entered into a second-level GLM to test group differences (i.e., CM-MOH patients vs. healthy controls and patients of each drug abuse profile vs. healthy controls), including age and sex as covariates. Results were corrected for multiple comparisons at the cluster level using FDR (p < 0.05) and thresholded at the connection level with an uncorrected p < 0.05 (default settings).

rs-fMRI regression analyses

Separate regression analyses investigated the relationship between FC of DMN, SN and CEN and clinical variables in CM-MOH patients with both seed-based and ROI-ROI approaches.

Across the entire sample, attack frequency, migraine duration, and CM duration were evaluated in separate models. A distinct model assessed the effects of allodynia (measured using the ASC-12), state anxiety (measured using the STAI-Y1), and depression (measured using the BDI-II) on FC, testing each variable individually while controlling for the others.

Additionally, the impact of medication intake was evaluated through four separate regression analyses: one considering the entire sample and three considering subsamples stratified by drug abuse profiles. Significant results were FWE corrected at p < 0.05 at the cluster level and p < 0.001 uncorrected threshold at the voxel level for seed-based analyses, and FDR corrected at p < 0.05 cluster-level and p < 0.05 uncorrected at the connection level for ROI-ROI analyses.

Graph theory analyses

To further characterize the global and local efficiency within the triple network model organization, graph theory metrics were extracted from 15 × 15 connectivity matrices. Global efficiency reflects the overall integration of the connectome, including DMN, SN and CEN; higher values indicating more effective communication across distant brain regions. Local efficiency, instead, measures how well information is exchanged within a node’s immediate neighborhood; higher values indicate strong local integration. Between-group (CM-MOH patients vs. healthy controls) graph analyses were performed. Processing steps are reported in detail in supplementary materials.

Results

Demographic and clinical data

Of 94 eligible patients, one was excluded for imaging artifacts and 2 for excessive motion (volume outliers > 150 out of 450 volumes), yielding 91 CM-MOH patients (80 women; mean age = 46.5 ± 10.8 years) and 35 healthy participants (30 women; mean age = 43.5 ± 11.8 years). CM-MOH patients included 28 triptan abusers, 40 NSAIDs abusers, and 23 polyabusers. Patients exhibited moderate allodynia (ASC-12 score = 7 ± 4.4), mild depression (BDI-II score = 18 ± 10.5), and mild anxiety (STAI-Y1 score = 59 ± 11.81; STAI-Y2 score = 56 ± 12.21). No significant differences were reported between healthy participants and CM-MOH patients for age (T(124)= -1.11, p = 0.27) and sex (X2 = 0.001; p = 0.97), nor among healthy participants and patients grouped by drug abuse profile (p > 0.05) (Table 1). Clinical variables did not significantly differ across abuse profile (i.e. triptans, NSAIDs, polyabusers) (Table 1).

Among abuse profiles, only triptan abusers, compared to healthy controls, showed significant FC alterations of DMN and SN with bilateral sensorimotor areas resembling those observed in the overall CM-MOH group but with more extensive patterns. They also displayed significant CEN connectivity alterations with the same sensorimotor regions. Notably, in this group, FC alterations between DMN and somatosensory regions extended further on the right side, toward the lateral portion corresponding to the somatotopic representation of the face (Fig. 1A). No significant differences were observed between other drug abuse profiles and healthy controls. Notably, unthresholded FC maps and FC of clusters showing significant differences between CM-MOH patients and healthy controls, suggest that NSAID overusers and polyabusers exhibit a similar, albeit subthreshold and less pronounced pattern of alterations (see eFigures 1 and eFigure 2).

Moreover, ROI-ROI analyses revealed specific inter-network connectivity disruptions within the triple network model. When comparing CM-MOH patients with healthy controls, no between-group differences among DMN, SN, and CEN connectivity emerged. Among abuse profiles, only polyabusers, compared to healthy controls, showed decreased inter-network connectivity between the SN and CEN, involving adjacent frontal and parietal nodes. Specifically, reduced connectivity was observed between the right RPFC of the SN and the right LPFC of the CEN, the right LPFC of the CEN and the right SMG of the SN, as well as the right SMG of the SN and the right PPC of the CEN (see Table 2; Fig. 2).

Discussion

Our research explored the resting-state FC of the triple network model (DMN, SN, and CEN) and their interactions with brain regions in a large sample of CM-MOH patients (N = 91) compared to healthy controls. Taken together, our findings indicate that CM-MOH patients exhibit alterations across three complementary levels of connectivity: interactions of the triple network model with extra-network regions, inter-network connectivity within the triple network model, and intra-network efficiency of specific nodes. In CM-MOH patients, the DMN, which supports self-referential thinking, and the SN, crucial for detecting relevant sensory and emotional stimuli, both exhibited increased FC with primary sensorimotor areas. These alterations were particularly pronounced in triptan abusers. Moreover, several nodes of the SN showed reduced global and local efficiency, suggesting a loss of integration and specialization within the triple network model. The CEN, responsible for high-cognitive tasks, also showed connectivity changes with motor regions in triptan abusers, while NSAID abusers and polyabusers exhibited a similar but less pronounced subthreshold pattern. With respect to clinical variables, greater allodynia scores were associated with increased SN-sensorimotor connectivity and with decreased DMN intra-network connectivity in parietal nodes, while higher anxiety scores correlated with decreased DMN-amygdala-putamen connectivity. Increased medication intake and migraine chronification were linked to enhanced FC of both DMN and SN with multisensory regions, including temporal and occipital areas. Notably, polyabusers, showed inter-network connectivity disruptions between the SN and CEN, in frontal and parietal nodes and concurrently enhanced coupling of both networks with temporo-occipital regions as an effect of medication intake.

Intra- and inter-network alterations within the triple network model

Previous studies have reported both intra-network (DMN, SN, CEN) [23–25, 27] and inter-network [26, 27] FC alterations in chronic migraine, with more pronounced SN and CEN intra-network disconnection in CM-MOH [23, 25]. In line with these findings, our results in CM-MOH show intra-network disruption in the SN and DMN and extend current evidence by highlighting possible inter-network SN–CEN alterations that seem to be specific to polyabusers. Notably, these patients are at higher risk of developing CM-MOH compared with single-drug users (NSAIDs or triptans) [15] and may be more prone to an abuse-like profile. The altered SN–CEN inter-network connectivity resembles patterns previously reported in substance abuse and addiction studies within the triple network model [39, 40]. This finding suggests a possible role of the CEN in drug abuse behaviour, whose impaired executive control functions may reduce top-down regulation of the SN, thereby facilitating maladaptive salience attribution to medication-related cues.

Interplay among networks of the triple model and sensorimotor regions

Our results revealed aberrant FC of DMN and SN with somatosensory regions in CM-MOH patients compared to healthy controls, consistent with findings in other chronic pain conditions, such as fibromyalgia and low back pain, which show increased inter-network connectivity among DMN, SN, and the sensorimotor network [34, 51–55]. These alterations may impair the discrimination, perception, and processing of painful stimuli [22]. Notably, enhanced FC between the DMN, related to internal awareness, and somatosensory regions may contribute to the embodiment of chronic pain, integrating it into self-perception [35, 54]. This hypothesis is supported by previous evidence showing that FC between DMN and primary somatosensory cortex intensifies in relation to pain persistence [33]. On the other side, increased FC between the SN, encoding sensory, autonomic, and emotional information, and the somatosensory cortex may amplify the unpleasant experience during emotional pain processing in CM-MOH patients [33]. Importantly, SN-sensorimotor connectivity is positively associated with allodynia symptoms, a known risk factor for migraine chronification [14]. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing increased allodynia severity linked to enhanced resting-state FC of SN in CM patients [24] as well as increased FC of the secondary somatosensory cortices in episodic migraine patients [56].

Functional characterization by abuse profile

Our results suggest that the altered FC pattern observed across the entire sample is primarily driven by triptan abusers. Compared to healthy participants, these patients exhibited a broader pattern of increased DMN and SN connectivity with sensorimotor regions along with altered CEN-Supplementary Motor Area (SMA) connectivity. Notably, these alterations were also present in NSAIDs abusers and polyabusers, albeit with a lower statistical threshold, suggesting a common pattern along abuse profiles, that is more pronounced in triptans abusers.

These results could be related with chronic pain that may enhance DMN connectivity in the neural regions representing the pain-affected face, aligning with a somatotopically-dependent pattern of pain representation. This is consistent with studies showing region-specific DMN and SN connectivity changes in chronic pain patients based on pain localization [55, 57], although in our study such alterations were mainly observed for the DMN and only appeared in the unthresholded maps for the SN.

In addition, alterations within the CEN have been previously reported in both chronic [24] and episodic migraine patients [22, 58, 59]. This heightened CEN-SMA coupling may potentially reflect altered goal-directed behaviour and motor defensive strategies, and potentially impair higher-order cognitive functions [23].

Among abuse profiles, triptan abusers exhibited the most marked FC alterations that might likely reflect the distinct mechanisms of action and prescription indications of drugs of abuse [17, 18]. Triptans specifically target migraine pathways, are generally prescribed as second-line treatments for severe attacks, and carry a higher and faster risk of CM-MOH development [60–62], factors that together may contribute to the widespread FC alterations observed across all networks of the triple model. Conversely, NSAIDs exert a more generalized, non-specific effect, are recommended as first-line treatment for acute migraine [60–62], and these features may account for the subtler FC abnormalities.

Impact of migraine chronification and drug intake on functional connectivity

Several demographic and clinical factors increase the risk of migraine chronification [2, 14, 24] and drug overuse [63]. We found that CM duration was positively associated with increased SN and DMN connectivity with the occipital cortex, while drug intake correlated with increased DMN connectivity with temporal regions. These patterns suggest that repeated drug intake and longer CM duration may enhance sensory processing and plasticity in multisensory regions, promoting drug-related cue representation, similar to other substance abuse disorders [64–66]. Moreover, CM duration often includes remission phases, highlighting its cyclical nature and relapse tendency, which may reinforce altered network interactions and drug-seeking behaviors. Indeed, previous studies have implicated occipital pole FC and excitability in distinguishing chronic migraines from episodic migraine [67], as well as in remission of CM to episodic ones [68]. In line with this, visual cortex dysfunction is considered a key feature of migraine, both during and between attacks [69]. Our observations might suggest that persistent high-frequency migraine attacks, along with higher drug intake, may disrupt DMN and SN interactions with visual and temporal regions, potentially worsening migraine chronification. On the other hand, repeated drug use may sensitize mesocorticolimbic systems [62, 70], which closely overlap with the SN [71]. In polyabusers, increased drug intake is associated with more widespread FC alterations, particularly between the SN and CEN and bilateral temporo-insular regions. Over time, this sensitization could increase the salience of medication-related cues, triggering physiological arousal and attentional biases that drive drug-seeking behaviour [62, 65]. Consistent with these functional modulations, the inter-network SN–CEN disconnection observed in polyabusers may reflect reduced executive control over salience processing, a mechanism that implicated in substance abuse disorders and potentially contributing to compulsive medication-seeking in MOH.

Association with psychiatric symptoms

State anxiety was negatively associated with DMN connectivity to a subcortical cluster encompassing the right amygdala, known for its role in emotional regulation, and part of the right putamen. In patients with sustained anxiety, the amygdala negatively couples with DMN FC [33, 72, 73]. We can speculate that when pain becomes embodied, leading to abnormal DMN connectivity with sensory processing regions, it may trigger anxiety, or conversely, anxiety may exacerbate pain perception and disrupt DMN connectivity. This maladaptive modulation can impair emotional regulation, further worsening patients’ well-being and mental health.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has some limitations. First, the absence of a CM patient group prevents us from determining whether the observed characteristics are specific to MOH. However, it should be noted that previous studies observed similar patterns of intra-network FC alterations in CM and CM-MOH patients, albeit with more pronounced dysconnectivity in SN and CEN in CM-MOH [23–25]. Additionally, as a tertiary centre, our population predominantly consists of MOH cases, limiting generalizability. Second, the cross-sectional design and the chronic nature of the disease preclude causal inference on pain-related connectivity changes. Third, we did not collect data on the frequency of specific headache types (e.g., migraine vs. tension-type), which may differ across drug abuse profiles. A further limitation is the absence of specific questionnaires assessing substance abuse, which would have provided a more direct characterization of addiction-related behaviours in MOH patients. Future studies should validate these findings in episodic migraine and CM patients to clarify specific features related to chronicity and MOH.

Nonetheless, our study has notable strengths. The large sample size enhances statistical power and robustness of our conclusions, while detailed pharmacological characterization provides insights into drug effects. Similar depression and anxiety scores across abuse profiles ensure comparability of psychiatric variables. Importantly, our investigation encompasses not only intra-network connectivity but also inter-network and whole-brain connectivity analyses, providing a comprehensive perspective.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study provides novel insights into FC alterations in the triple network model in CM-MOH. The SN may represent a key contributor to both pain chronification and medication overuse. We hypothesize that during the process of migraine chronification, the SN may become hyperconnected with sensorimotor regions, a maladaptive reorganization associated with increased allodynia, and coupled with a loss of efficiency within the network. This process seems to be followed by increased connectivity between the DMN and sensorimotor regions, fostering the embodiment of chronic pain through a somatotopic representation and impairing emotional regulation (i.e., anxiety). Drug-specific effects indicate that changes in CEN connectivity with sensorimotor regions may emerge subsequent to changes observed in SN and DMN. This interpretation is in line with De Ridder’s triple network model of chronic pain, which proposes that an initial “suffering” response mediated by the SN evolves into chronicity and “embodiment” through the DMN, ultimately affecting executive functioning via the CEN in response to persistent sensory pain signals.

On the other hand, we suggest that the CEN, through its interaction with the SN, may contribute to medication overuse. In polyabusers, this is reflected in the disconnection between the two networks, along with enhanced connectivity of CEN and SN with regions outside the triple network model associated with the level of medication intake. This pattern suggests that impaired executive control may weaken the regulation of salience processing, potentially reinforcing drug-abuse behaviour.

Overall, these results point to the critical importance of pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions targeting dysfunctional connectivity of this model as a promising strategy to improve management of chronic pain and medication overuse headache.
