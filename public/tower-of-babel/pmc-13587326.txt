The role of traction in the treatment of severe idiopathic scoliosis: non-traction vs. traction
Authors: Zhiyue Shi, Jie Xiao, Tao Li, Jingming Xie, Yingsong Wang, Zhi Zhao, Li Zhang, Yayi Pu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTreatment planning for severe idiopathic scoliosis (SIS) requires consideration of both deformity correction and the appropriate grade of osteotomy. This study aimed to compare radiographic and clinical outcomes between patients who underwent surgical correction with or without preoperative skull-femoral traction.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed patients with SIS who had a main thoracic curve greater than 90°and flexibility less than 30% and underwent surgery at our institution between 2014 and 2018. Patients were grouped according to whether they received preoperative skull-femoral traction (SFT). The two groups were compared in terms of age at surgery, body mass index, preoperative and postoperative radiographic parameters (main scoliosis, kyphosis and their flexibilities, deformity angular ratios, CSVL-C7PL, SVA, etc.), SRS-Schwab osteotomy grade, operative time, blood loss, complications, and 3-year follow-up outcomes.ResultsTwenty-two SIS cases were involved (traction vs. non-traction: 12 cases vs. 10 cases). There were no significant differences in age at surgery or in most preoperative radiographic parameters, except for CSVL-C7PL (4.1 cm vs. 1.7 cm, P < 0.05). Compared with the non-traction group, the traction group had a significantly lower mean SRS-Schwab osteotomy grade (2.8 vs. 4.8, P < 0.01), along with shorter operative time, less blood loss, and fewer complications. The final corrective effects of the two groups were similar at 3-year follow-up (P > 0.05).ConclusionsPreoperative skull-femoral traction may help balance deformity correction and surgical invasiveness in SIS by reducing the need for higher-grade osteotomy, operative burden, and complications while maintaining comparable correction outcomes.

Background

Advances in surgical techniques, particularly spinal osteotomy, have substantially improved the correction of severe idiopathic scoliosis (SIS) over recent decades. According to different resected ranges, spinal osteotomies are systematically classified into 6 different types and the higher-grade can create increased flexibility and space to achieve more powerful correction of spinal deformity. However, higher-grade osteotomy, particularly three-column osteotomy (3CO), is technically demanding and associated with greater surgical invasiveness and higher risks [1]. In the cases undergoing 3COs, the incidences of neurological complications related to pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO) were 7–14%, whereas it was increased to 27% in posterior vertebral column resection (PVCR) with 18–40% incidences of major non-neurological complications [2]. Moreover, many SIS-related adverse patient-specific factors also have to be faced such as poor basic status, difficult surgical process, vulnerable neurological tolerance, etc. For our much earlier cases before 2014, PVCR was the first choice to directly correct SIS without any other assistance. At the same time of achieving wonderful correction, it was always a headache problem to have to consider about a series of surgical risks and complications related to the more aggressive process. So the success of SIS treatment is not only based on how hard the skills being mastered by a surgeon.

The 4-year from 2014 to 2018 was a transition period of our institution, and we started to consider more about how to balance the relationship between the maximal clinical outcomes and the minimal negative effects in the treatment of severe spinal deformity. During this period, we revisited the significance of traction -- the oldest way being applied to treat scoliosis, and started to treat more patients under the assistance of traction. Traction as an important adjunctive measure of SIS correction, which can improve major curve, increase its flexibility and reduce neurologic risks through gradually correcting a contorted spine [3]. The significance of traction in SIS treatment has been explored by some previous studies but most of them were lack of control. The aim of this retrospective case-control comparative study was to explore the real value of traction to optimize the treatment of SIS.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This was a retrospective, controlled comparative study conducted at a single institution. In accordance with the ethical standards of the Clinical Research Ethics Committee, SIS patients being operated by a single senior spinal surgeon (Y.S.W.) were reviewed. To minimize potential selection bias related to differences in surgical experience over time, we included patients treated during the same period, from April 2014 to April 2018. This four-year period represented a transitional phase in our institutional treatment strategy for severe spinal deformity. During this period, preoperative skull-femoral traction was gradually introduced as an adjunct to surgical correction. Consequently, some patients underwent traction-assisted surgical correction, whereas others underwent direct surgical correction without traction. To avoid heterogeneity caused by different traction methods, only patients who received preoperative skull-femoral traction were included in the traction group. All enrolled patients were screened using the same predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were: (1) right main thoracic scoliosis; (2) scoliotic Cobb angle more than 90˚; (3) scoliotic flexibility less than 30%; (4) only female patients included; (5) a minimum follow-up period of 3 years. Patients with (1) pre-existing neurological dysfunction; (2) any neurological deterioration by traction; (3) non-idiopathic etiology such as neuromuscular, congenital scoliosis, etc.; (4) intraspinal malformation such as Chiari’s malformation, syringomyelia, split cord, tethered cord etc.; (5) undergoing revision spine surgery were excluded. All consecutive patients who met the predefined eligibility criteria during the study period were included in the final analysis. After eligibility was confirmed, no patient was selectively excluded from either group on the basis of surgical invasiveness, radiographic correction, operative time, blood loss, osteotomy grade, or postoperative complications.

Grouping

Patients were divided into the traction and non-traction groups according to whether preoperative SFT was used.

Traction protocol

SFT was initiated approximately 2 weeks before surgery [4]. The initial traction weight was 2–3 kg on skull and bilateral femurs respectively and daily increment of traction weight was 1–2 kg for skull and each femur. Maximum traction was expected to about 50% of the patient’s basic body weight. Physical examination of neurological function was done twice a day routinely. Careful nursing care, respiratory and limb exercises, and nutritional support were provided during traction.

At the beginning of surgery, intraoperative SFT was reduced to 50% of preoperative weight of traction, which would be maintained in whole surgical process under close monitoring of intraoperative neuromonitoring (INM).

Data collection

The reviewed data of each patient included age at surgery, body mass index (BMI) and imaging characteristics in detail before surgery. According to standard standing radiographs, configuration of main curve was demonstrated by main scoliotic Cobb angle, kyphosis, coronal/sagittal flexibilities, deformity angular ratios (DARs). DAR was generated by dividing the maximum Cobb angle by the number of levels spanning the curve, which included coronal, sagittal and total DARs [5]. Coronal trunk balance was evaluated by the distance between the center sacral vertical line and the C7 plumbline (CSVL-C7PL) and sagittal balance was evaluated by the distance from the C7 plumbline to the posterior edge of the upper sacral endplate surface (SVA). About surgical procedure, applied SRS-Schwab osteotomy grade [6], instrumental and fusion levels, operating room (OR) time, blood loss were recorded. Intraoperative and postoperative occurrence of complications were reviewed too. Postoperatively and at 3-year follow-up, the coronal and sagittal correction rates, CSVL-C7PL and SVA were collected.

Statistical analysis

The items for comparison between the 2 groups included age at operation, BMI, preop radiographic indicators (main scoliotic Cobb angle, kyphosis, coronal/sagittal flexibilities, DARs, CSVL-C7PL, SVA), applied SRS-Schwab osteotomy grade, surgical damage (OR time, blood loss, complications), final correction, etc. All quantitative data were calculated as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between two groups were assessed by independent t test. Statistically significant differences were accepted at P < 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA).

Results

Patient population and surgical outcomes

During the study period, all surgically treated patients with SIS were screened. Finally, 22 eligible patients were included: 12 in the traction group and 10 in the non-traction group. The mean age of them was 18.0 (range, 10–34) years and mean BMI, 18.1 (range, 10.4–25.5) kg/m2. In the coronal plane, the mean scoliotic Cobb angle before operation was 106.4˚ (range, 92.0˚-120.0˚), mean flexibility was 13.1% (range, 1.0%-24.7%), and mean CSVL-C7PL was 3.0 ± 2.1 (range, 0-8.5) cm. In the sagittal plane, the mean kyphosis was 68.6˚ ± 24.0˚ (range, 26.0˚-103.0˚), mean flexibility was 17.6% (range, 10.4%-32.0%), and mean SVA was 3.1 (range, 0-8.5) cm. The mean coronal DAR was 15.2 (range, 9.3–26.8) ˚/level, sagittal DAR 9.4 (range, 2.9–21.5) ˚/level and total DAR was 24.5 (range, 15.4–48.3) ˚/level.

In them, 12 cases underwent preoperative SFT. After preoperative 2-week traction, the mean scoliotic Cobb angle was decreased to 74.2˚ (range, 57˚-90˚) from 106.8˚ (range, 93˚-117˚) (P < 0.01); and the mean kyphosis was decreased to 45.9˚ (range, 22˚-87˚) from 64.5˚ (range, 37˚-101˚) (P < 0.01). The mean coronal and sagittal corrective rates by traction were 30.2% (range, 10.0%-46.7%) and 29.7% (range, 13.0%-45.0%).

For all the 22 patients, the mean grade of applied SRS-Schwab osteotomy was 3.7: 7 cases underwent grades 1–2 osteotomies, 3 underwent grades 3–4 osteotomies, and 12 underwent grades 5–6 osteotomies. The mean fusion range was 15.1 (range, 13–17) levels, mean OR time was 577 (range, 360–750) minutes, and mean blood loss was 1700 (400–3600) ml. Postoperatively, the mean coronal correction rate was 60.5% (range, 37.3%-75.6%), mean sagittal correction rate was 44.5% (range, 17.1%-69.5%), mean CSVL-C7PL was 1.5 (range, 0.5–3.3) cm, and mean SVA was 2.1 (range, 0.5–4.7) cm. At 3 years’ follow-up, the mean coronal correction rate was 58.2% (range, 31.0%-74.5%), mean sagittal correction rate was 41.1% (range, 14.0%-69.2%), mean CSVL-C7PL was 1.5 (range, 0-4.5) cm, and mean SVA was 2.7 (range, 0-7.1) cm (Table 1).

Comparisons between traction and non-traction groups

All the 22 patients were divided into two groups: 12 in the Traction group and 10 in the Non-traction group. The two groups were comparable in mean ages (20.0 years vs. 15.5 years) and BMIs (16.7 kg/m2 vs. 16.9 kg/m2) (P > 0.05). Preoperatively, there was no difference between the basic radiographic parameters of the two groups including scoliotic Cobb angle, kyphosis, coronal and sagittal flexibilities (P > 0.05). As the morphological parameters of main curve, coronal, sagittal and total DARs were similar in the two groups (P > 0.05). Regarding to the trunk balance, the mean CSVL-C7PL was significantly greater in the traction group than in the non-traction group (4.1 cm vs. 1.7 cm, P = 0.0045), indicating worse baseline coronal imbalance in the traction group. No significant difference was observed in preoperative sagittal balance, as reflected by SVA (3.7 cm vs. 2.3 cm, P = 0.1503).

About surgical process, the mean grade of osteotomy in the Non-traction group was 4.8 (range, 3–6): none underwent grade 1 or 2 osteotomy; all the 10 cases underwent 3COs (100%): grades 3–4 osteotomies in 2 and grades 5–6 in 8 (Fig. 1A-B). In the Traction group, the mean grade of applied osteotomy was 2.8 (range, 1–5): 7 cases (58.3%) underwent grades 1–2 osteotomies (Fig. 2A-B); 5 (41.7%) underwent 3COs: grade 4 in 1 and grade 5 in 4. The mean SRS-Schwab grade of applied osteotomies of the Traction group was significantly reduced to 2.8 from 4.8 of the Non-traction group (P = 0.0045) (Fig. 3). Moreover, the mean OR time and blood loss in the Traction group were decreased significantly (P < 0.01) with similar fusion levels (P > 0.05).

Complications

In the Traction group, during the preoperative 2-week traction, 4 patients (33.3%) had pin loosening at skull or femoral site. None experienced pin site fracture or infection. In surgical process, 4 patients had pleural tear: 1 in the Traction group and 3 in the Non-traction group (25.0% vs. 75.0%). One patient of the Non-traction group experienced a decline of SSEP amplitude more than 50% and a complete disappearance of MEP in corrective process after undergoing grade 5 osteotomy, which were gradually recovered by improving spinal subluxation and decreasing spinal cord tension. Then final Stagnara wake-up test demonstrated her complete neurological function. In the Traction group, none experienced positive neurological incidence in surgical process.

The main postoperative complications of the 22 patients included superficial surgical site infection in 5 cases, hydrothorax in 5, pneumonia in 3. For all the 13 complications, only 3 occurred in the Traction group but another 10 in the Non-traction group (23.1% vs. 76.9%). None experienced postoperative neurological deficits. During follow-up, 1 patient of the Non-traction group experienced deep surgical site infection at about 1.5 year after undergoing grade 5 osteotomy, who was cured by repeated debridement without implant removal.

Discussion

The challenges in correcting SIS include a large main curve, poor flexibility, and a high potential risk of neurological complications. Spinal osteotomies as a series of main surgical measures to correct SIS, which can create changed space and flexibility by resecting different ranges of spinal structures. Morphological characteristics, size, and rigidity of main curve are the main factors in determining the required grade of spinal osteotomy. The much severer and stiffer the curve, the more space and flexibility needed to be created, and the required grade of spinal osteotomy would be increased. Higher-grade osteotomy can create more space, flexibility and freedom from posterior to anterior columns, but which also has to face “upgraded” damages and risks related to the more aggressive process [7, 8]. How to balance the relationship between maximal clinical outcomes and minimal surgical damage is the key to success in the treatment of SIS. To reduce risks and damage as much as possible, surgeons have attempted to add traction to obtain a partial correction as well as to release potential flexibility of major curve before operation [3, 9]. The other achievements of traction in severe spinal deformity also aims to help trunk rebalance, improve the poor nutritional status and pulmonary function, avoid or facilitate 3COs, etc [10].

Previous studies have reported that preoperative traction can achieve approximately 27%-66% correction of severe curved spine in both the coronal and sagittal planes [11, 12]. In a retrospective study from Mehrpour et al., they performed preoperative traction on 23 patients with severe scoliosis and their mean Cobb angle was improved from 99.9˚ preoperatively to 75.5˚ after traction and finally to 49.5˚ after one-stage posterior surgical correction [13]. Iyer et al. used halo-gravity traction in 30 pediatric patients with severe early onset spinal deformity and 80% of them had SIS. Following the application of traction, the mean coronal curvature was improved to 77˚ from preoperative 112˚ and the mean sagittal curvature was improved to 73˚ from preoperative 106˚ [14]. In a recent study by Hwang et al. involved 59 patients of severe scoliosis with different etiologies, and the mean preoperative main scoliosis and flexibility were 96.9˚ and 24.8% respectively. After applying preoperative traction in a mean duration of 3.1 weeks, the mean scoliotic Cobb angle was corrected to 63˚ and the mean traction-related correction rate was 34.4% [15]. In this study including 22 SIS patients, their preoperative mean main scoliosis and kyphosis were 106.4 ˚and 68.6˚, and the coronal and sagittal flexibilities were 13.1% and 17.6%, respectively. For correcting these severe and stiff curves, 3COs would not be avoided if without any help from other measures. Preoperative 2-week SFT was used in 12 cases of them, and the mean scoliotic Cobb angle was decreased to 74.2˚ from 106.8˚; the mean sagittal angle was also decreased to 45.9˚ from 64.5˚. And the mean coronal and sagittal traction-related correction rates were 30.2% and 29.7%, which were similar as previous studies. After undergoing surgical correction, 57.4% and 44.3% of the mean coronal and sagittal correction rates were achieved in these 12 patients, so more than one half of the final correction was achieved by preoperative traction.

Potential flexibility release and/or space creation are two basic conditions of SIS correction, which means the more potential flexibility being released and the less space being needed to be created. The significance of adding traction in SIS treatment is to “break up the whole into parts”, which divides the final correction into two main parts: one part is obtained by traction before operation; the other is achieved through further surgical process. Moreover, traction itself could not create additional space for correction, but can maximize the release of tissue viscoelasticity and allow surgeon to have a further understanding of the “true appearance” of the curve before surgery: how much flexibility does the deformity itself really have? How much space and flexibility do we really need to create by spinal osteotomy? Is it essential to perform a higher-grade osteotomy? Traction can bring more unexpected achievements in the treatment of SIS.

Sponseller et al. demonstrated that the frequency of using a vertebral column resection was less in the patient with traction [16]. Nemani et al. presented their experiences of using preoperative traction in a series of 24 patients and decreased the need for 3COs following traction [17]. Iyer et al. presents their retrospective study about the use of traction in 96 cases with an average scoliosis and an average kyphosis more than 120˚. In these cases, 57.3% of curves were idiopathic and 75% was biplanar deformities. Through their use of traction, the planned rate of 3COs dropped from 91% to 38% [14]. In the present case-control study, compared with the Non-traction group, the cases in the Traction group had comparable ages, BMIs, preoperative scoliotic main curves, flexibilities and DARs but with more obvious coronal imbalances. However, the mean grade of applied osteotomies in the Traction group was 2.8 and only 41.7% of them underwent 3COs, which was significantly lower than grade 4.8 of the patients without traction. The drops of spinal osteotomies in these similar cases resulted in obviously reduced OR time, blood loss and surgical damage. Moreover, the incidence of postoperative complications of them was three times lower than the patients without traction. Therefore, the combined use of traction is beneficial to reduce the use of higher-grade osteotomies especially 3COs as well as related complications. One important issue to consider when interpreting these findings is the baseline difference in coronal trunk balance between the two groups. The traction group had a significantly larger preoperative CSVL–C7PL distance than the non-traction group, indicating more severe coronal imbalance before treatment. This difference may partly reflect residual selection bias related to the retrospective, non-randomized study design, because preoperative SFT may have been more frequently used in patients with more pronounced trunk imbalance during the institutional transition period. Despite this baseline imbalance, the traction group achieved postoperative and 3-year coronal balance outcomes comparable to those of the non-traction group, while requiring a lower osteotomy grade and showing a reduced operative burden. Therefore, the main value of preoperative SFT in this cohort was not superior final correction, but comparable correction with reduced surgical invasiveness.

In addition, traction may help reduce neurological risk during the treatment of SIS [18]. Using traction can gradually corrects the spinal alignment while allowing the clinician to monitor neurological complications in awake status [6, 19]. Garabekyan et al. believed that preoperative traction could achieve partial and gradual correction of the deformity and could reduce or even avoid neurological risks and complications related to surgical correction [20]. Zhang et al. had reported their successful experience of using posterior-only surgical correction with heavy halo-femoral traction to correct severe rigid congenital scoliosis associated with split cord malformation in 13 cases without any spine-shortening osteotomy. They believed that the preoperative traction may gradually increase the tolerance of spinal cord to stretch and ischemia from spinal correction [21]. In this study, there was no postoperative neurological deficit occurrence in all the 22 cases, but INM positive event was detected in one case without traction. Therefore, traction could be used as an effective measure to reduce surgery-related neurological risks in the treatment of SIS.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small. Severe idiopathic scoliosis with a main thoracic curve greater than 90° and flexibility less than 30% is uncommon, and the strict eligibility criteria further limited the number of patients available for analysis. Nevertheless, all consecutive patients who met the predefined eligibility criteria during the study period were included, and both groups were evaluated using the same criteria. Second, because this was a retrospective, non-randomized, single-center study, residual selection bias could not be completely excluded. In particular, the traction group showed greater preoperative coronal imbalance, as indicated by a larger CSVL–C7PL distance. This finding suggests that preoperative skull-femoral traction may have been more frequently used in patients with more pronounced trunk imbalance during the institutional transition period. Therefore, this baseline discrepancy should be considered when interpreting the coronal balance outcomes. Third, to reduce etiological heterogeneity, this study included only patients with idiopathic scoliosis. Accordingly, the findings may not be directly generalizable to patients with other types of severe spinal deformity. Future prospective multicenter studies with larger cohorts and standardized indications for traction are warranted to further validate these findings.

Conclusions

In summary, preoperative skull-femoral traction may help reduce the need for higher-grade spinal osteotomy in the treatment of severe idiopathic scoliosis. By achieving partial correction before surgery, traction may allow comparable final correction with a lower-grade osteotomy, while reducing operative burden and complications. The combined use of traction and surgery may therefore provide a better balance between deformity correction and surgical invasiveness.
