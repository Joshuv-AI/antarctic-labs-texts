The mediating effect of blood biomarkers in the associations between inflammatory bowel disease and incident psychiatric disorders: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Jing Feng, Yanjun Wu, Meijun Meng, Ruijie Zeng, Yuying Ma, Dongling Luo, Lijun Zhang, Yajie Zhang, Yang Li, Wentao Huang, Felix W Leung, Chongyang Duan, Weihong Sha, Hao Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Despite the growing evidence of an association between inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and psychiatric disorders, there has been limited research exploring the underlying mediating role of blood biomarkers on the gut-brain axis. This study aimed to examine the association between IBD and the risk of incident psychiatric disorders and investigate whether and how blood biomarkers mediate this association.Methods:This prospective cohort study using data from the UK Biobank included participants without psychiatric diagnoses at baseline. The case-cohort consisted of participants with a hospital-based diagnosis of IBD at baseline. The primary outcome was all psychiatric disorders. Secondary outcomes included 11 major psychiatric disorders. Cox regression models estimated adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) for psychiatric outcomes. Causal mediation models investigated the potential mediation effects of blood biomarkers.Results:Among 491 131 participants, patients with IBD exhibited higher risks of overall psychiatric disorders (HR 1.23 [95% CI: 1.13–1.33]), substance misuse (1.23 [1.09–1.38]), depression (1.36 [1.22–1.52]), anxiety (1.15 [1.01–1.30]) and post-traumatic stress disorder (1.87 [1.00–3.51]) compared with non-IBD participants. The association with incident substance misuse was only among patients with Crohn’s disease (CD, 1.47 [1.23–1.76]), but not ulcerative colitis (UC, 1.01 [0.84–1.21]). Mediation analysis revealed 16, 14, 15, and 6 biomarkers partially mediated the associations for all psychiatric disorders, substance misuse, depression, and anxiety, respectively. Six blood markers showed the strongest mediating effects: neutrophil count (proportion mediated: 12.04%), C-reactive protein (10.29%), systemic immune-inflammatory index (8.94%), erythrocyte distribution width (16.51%), erythrocyte count (9.76%), and albumin (9.15%). Moreover, several blood mediators of CD identified in association with incident substance misuse may explain the risk discrepancy between IBD subtypes.Conclusion:The blood biomarkers of inflammation, blood oxygen-carrying capacity, and metabolism mediate the effect of IBD on the risk of psychiatric outcomes and could be considered as a therapeutic target.Keywords: gut-brain axis, inflammatory bowel disease, mental health, psychiatric disorders

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), with Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) as its subtype, is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory disorder of the gastrointestinal tract1. Its global prevalence is rising, particularly in industrialized nations2. Existing evidence indicates that patients with IBD are more likely to experience psychiatric disorders than the general population3–7. Recent meta-analyses revealed a high prevalence of anxiety (32.1%) and depressive symptoms (25.2%) among IBD patients, with significantly elevated odds ratios (OR 2.6 for anxiety, OR 1.9 for depression) compared to healthy controls8. The exact mechanism remains unknown but may be related to a complex interaction between the gut and the brain, known as the ‘gut-brain axis’9,10.

Research on the relationship between IBD and psychiatric disorders is extensive, but several key issues still need clarification. First, existing research reports inconsistent findings. While a meta-analysis suggested an increased risk of anxiety [hazard ratios (HR) 1.48] and depression (HR 1.55) after an IBD diagnosis11, a recent Mendelian randomization study failed to establish a causal link12. Second, past studies have focused on anxiety or depression in IBD patients, with limited research on other significant psychiatric categories. Small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, and inadequate adjustments for covariates also affected the accuracy and generalizability of the results13–15. In addition, preclinical studies have identified potential mechanisms in the IBD-psychiatric disorders link9,16,17. However, the mediating role of specific blood markers, indicating inflammation and pathophysiological changes in different disease states, remains unproven in large population-based cohorts. Investigating these blood markers’ potential mediating effects could enhance our comprehension of the relationship between IBD and psychiatric disorders, providing a basis for future more precise interventions.

Therefore, this study makes up for the lack of psychiatric outcomes by containing 11 major psychiatric subtypes to determine the long-term effects of IBD on psychiatric disorders using data from a large-scale cohort of UK Biobank. Furthermore, in order to explore the underlying mechanism, mediation analyses were adopted to quantify whether and how blood markers mediated the association of IBD with the incidence of psychiatric disorders.

Methods

Data

The UK Biobank is a prospective cohort study comprising 502 368 participants aged 37–73 years. Participants were recruited from 22 assessment centers located in England, Scotland, and Wales between March 2006 and December 201018. The baseline assessment involved questionnaires, face-to-face interviews, biological samples, and physical and genetic parameters. All participants provided informed consent, and ethical approval was obtained from the North West Multi-Center Research Ethics Committee (Approval Numbers: 11/NW/0382, 16/NW/0274, and 21/NW/0157). The current study has received approval within the UK Biobank project framework under code 83339.

In this study, individuals with prior psychiatric diagnoses at baseline and those lacking complete blood biomarker data were excluded from the analysis. The specific criteria for participant selection in each analysis conducted are outlined in the flowchart (Supplementary eFigure 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C809). The work has been reported in line with the strengthening the reporting of cohort, cross-sectional, and case–control studies in surgery (STROCSS) criteria19 (Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C810).

Exposure

Participants diagnosed with IBD were identified using International Classification of Disease-10 (ICD-10) codes. CD was identified using the code K50, while UC was identified using the code K51. The diagnosis of IBD was based on hospital admission data. Individuals with a diagnosis of IBD before the baseline were considered as the exposure group (IBD group), while those without a diagnosis of IBD before the baseline, regardless of subsequent IBD development during the follow-up period, were considered as the nonexposure group (non-IBD group). Additionally, participants with diagnoses of both CD and UC (n=528) were excluded from the IBD subtype analysis but still included in the IBD group analysis.

Outcomes

Our primary outcome was all psychiatric disorders (ICD-10 F10-F99). Secondary outcomes included substance misuse (F10-F19), schizophrenia (F20-F29), bipolar disorder (F30, F31), depression (F32, F33), anxiety disorders (F40, F41), post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD, F43.1), behavioral syndromes (F50-F59), personality disorders (F60-F69), intellectual disability (F70-F79), developmental disorders (F80-F88), and autism spectrum disorders (F84). Outcomes were classified using ICD-10 codes and based on hospital psychiatric disorder diagnosis. Participants who had psychiatric disorders diagnosed before baseline were excluded.

Mediators

Potential blood biomarkers (including inflammatory, erythrocyte-related, and metabolic markers) mediating the association between IBD and psychiatric disorders, were selected based on our knowledge of potential pathways as well as previous literature9,16,20. These blood samples were obtained from baseline hematological assays at the time of recruitment of UK Biobank participants. Inflammatory markers included C-reactive protein (CRP), white blood cell count, neutrophil count, platelet count, and composite measures such as the systemic immune-inflammatory index (SII, neutrophils × platelets/lymphocytes), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR). In addition, erythrocyte-related markers included erythrocyte count (RBC), red cell distribution width (RDW), hematocrit percentage (HCT), reticulocyte counts, and hemoglobin concentration (Hb). For metabolites, albumin, glucose, lipids, and fatty acids were evaluated.

Covariates

The directed acyclic graph (DAG) showed the main relationships among IBD (exposure), mediators, psychiatric disorders (outcome), and covariates (Supplementary eFigure 2, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C809)21–25. Sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, and ethnicity), socioeconomic status [education, income, and the Townsend deprivation index (TDI)], lifestyle factors (smoking status, alcohol intake frequency, physical activity, dietary habits, sleeping duration, and BMI), comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, coronary heart disease, and neurological and cerebrovascular disorders) and family history of psychiatric disorders were collected at baseline. Details about covariates are available in Supplementary eMethods (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C809).

Statistical analysis

We used the Kaplan–Meier method to estimate the cumulative incidence of overall psychiatric disorders. Multivariable Cox regression models were used to estimate the HR and 95% CI of the risk of overall and specific psychiatric disorders associated with IBD and its subtypes. All the previously mentioned covariates were included in the models. The assumption of proportional hazards was examined through Schoenfeld residuals or Kaplan–Meier method and no obvious violation was found. All participants were followed up until the occurrence of any psychiatric outcome, death, or until the end of follow-up evaluation for the UK Biobank cohort of 31 October 2022, whichever came first. In addition, subgroup analysis and tests of multiplicative interaction were also performed to identify whether the effect of IBD on psychiatric disorders would be different in specific populations.

To further explain the underlying mechanisms, we evaluated a series of blood biomarkers for mediation analysis in the relationship between IBD and incident psychiatric disorders. Three steps should be satisfied for mediation analysis26: step 1, the association of IBD with incident psychiatric disorders; step 2, the association of IBD with blood biomarkers; and step 3, the association of biomarkers with incident psychiatric disorders. If all three associations are confirmed, the causal mediation analysis can be performed utilizing the ‘mediation’ package in R (with 1000 bootstrap iterations). By combining the mediator and outcome models and adjusting for all covariates, indirect effects, direct effects, total effects, and mediator proportions for each mediator were calculated.

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the stability of the findings in primary analysis. Firstly, additional covariates, including anxiolytic medication use, antidepressant medication use, antipsychotic medication use, IBD medication use, and bowel resection, were added to the model for further adjustment. Secondly, participants diagnosed with IBD after baseline were excluded. Thirdly, to avoid possible bias from reverse causality, participants with psychiatric disorders diagnosed within 1 year of baseline were further excluded, following the exclusion of those diagnosed with IBD after the baseline. Fourth, the analyses were repeated by adjusting for the minimal sufficient adjustment set of covariates (MSAS) to minimize confounding bias. The MSAS was determined using the directed acyclic graph, which included sociodemographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, and lifestyle factors (Supplementary eFigure 2, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C809). Fifth, we utilized Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPTW) from propensity scores (PS) to mitigate confounding bias27,28. After the IPTW analysis to balance covariates, we further conducted Cox regression analysis to assess the association between IBD and psychiatric disorders. If the Standardized Mean Differences (SMD) of covariates after IPTW weighting is less than 0.1, it indicates satisfactory balance among covariates, thus obviating the need for further adjustment. Sixth, in consideration of potential interactions among multiple mediator biomarkers, we conducted additional mediation analysis for multiple causal mechanisms. We fitted multiple mediator models and tested the assumption of homogeneous interaction29. Details of this analysis were presented in Supplementary eMethods (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C809).

Covariate data were available for almost all participants, with few missing values observed for select variables. The percentage of missing for all covariates was calculated and covariates with proportions less than 20% were included. We imputed the fraction of missing data using the multiple imputation by chained equations approach (Supplementary eTable 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C809). A significance level of two-sided P<0.05 was used, and in cases of multiple testing, false-discovery rate (FDR) adjusted P-values were applied. Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using R software (version 4.2.1) and GraphPad Prism (version 6.00).

Effect of IBD and subtype on the risk of psychiatric disorders

In the multivariable Cox regression model, patients with IBD exhibited a 23% higher risk of incident overall psychiatric disorders [adjusted HR (aHR) 1.23, 95% CI: 1.13–1.33, P<0.001] compared with non-IBD participants (Fig. 2). Similar results were observed for risk of incident substance misuse (aHR 1.23, 95% CI: 1.09–1.38, P<0.001), depression (aHR 1.36, 95% CI: 1.22–1.52, P<0.001), anxiety (aHR 1.15, 95% CI: 1.01–1.30, P=0.031), and PTSD (aHR 1.87, 95% CI: 1.00–3.51, P=0.047). The cumulative incidence of all psychiatric disorders over time was significantly higher in participants with IBD than in non-IBD group (log-rank, P<0.001, Supplementary eFigure 3, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C809).

Both CD and UC had significantly increased risks of incident overall psychiatric disorders and depression (Fig. 2). Notably, patients with CD (aHR 1.47, 95% CI: 1.23–1.76, P<0.001), but not UC (aHR 1.01, 95% CI: 0.84–1.21, P=0.901), was significantly associated with an increased risk of substance misuse compared with non-IBD participants. Further comparison of the levels of blood markers between CD and UC was conducted to explore the potential causes for the risk discrepancy (Supplementary eFigure 4, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C809). The cumulative incidence of all psychiatric disorders was significantly higher in participants with CD and UC than in non-IBD group (log-rank, P<0.001, Supplementary eFigure 3, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C809).

Mediation analysis of blood markers on associations of IBD with incident psychiatric disorders

Following the initial identification of psychiatric outcomes significantly associated with IBD (step 1), the assessment continued to explore potential mediating biomarkers associated with IBD (step 2, Supplementary eTable 2, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C809) and these outcomes (step 3, Supplementary eTable 3, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C809), including all psychiatric disorders, substance misuse, depression, anxiety, and PTSD. It should be noted that we did not identify any biomarkers that were associated with both IBD and PTSD. Therefore, for the outcome of PTSD, we did not conduct further mediation analysis.

A range of biomarkers, significantly associated with both IBD and each outcome, were selected for mediation analyses. The associations of IBD with incident all psychiatric disorders, substance misuse, depression, and anxiety were mediated by 16, 14, 15, and 6 biomarkers, respectively (Fig. 3, Supplementary eTable 4, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C809). Among these mediating blood markers, six key blood markers exhibited the strongest mediating effects. Specifically, neutrophil count (proportion mediated: 12.04%), CRP (10.29%), and SII (8.94%) in inflammatory markers; RDW (16.51%) and RBC (9.76%) in erythrocyte-related indicators; and albumin (9.15%) in metabolic markers. For different mental outcomes, the specific blood markers exerting the most significant mediation effects varied: RBC (9.76%), albumin (9.15%), and SII (8.94%) for all psychiatric disorders; RDW (16.51%), neutrophil count (12.04%), and CRP (10.29%) for substance misuse; RBC (8.50%), Hb (7.30%), and SII (5.71%) for depression; and RBC (7.44%), platelet count (4.16%), and HCT (3.36%) for anxiety.

For CD and UC, similar blood markers mediated the association of CD and UC with overall psychiatric disorders and depression (Supplementary eTables 5–7, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C809). For overall psychiatric outcome, the strongest mediators were albumin (9.44%) in CD and RBC (10.8%) in UC. Notably, for depression, the proportions of the strongest mediators were approximately twice as high in CD as in UC: RBC (13.51% vs. 6.16%), Hb (12.07% vs. 4.75%), and HCT (10.11% vs. 3.52%). Furthermore, triglycerides and monounsaturated fatty acids only mediated the relationship between CD and overall psychiatric disorders and depression, not UC. Additionally, in the association between CD and the risk of substance misuse, 14 blood markers were identified as partial mediators, with the strongest mediating proportion for RDW (8.22%).

Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses

Figure 4 shows the associations with overall psychiatric disorders by population subgroups. Significant effect modification was observed for different age, ethnicity, alcohol consumption, and BMI groups. The association between IBD and psychiatric disorders was generally consistent in all subgroups but was more prominent among younger (aHR 1.31, 95% CI: 1.18–1.46), non-white (aHR 1.84, 95% CI: 1.29–2.62), and nonobese populations (aHR 1.28, 95% CI: 1.17–1.40).

The results were robust in the sensitivity analyses (Supplementary eFigures 5–9, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C809, Supplementary eTables 8–12, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C809). Notably, IPTW successfully balanced baseline covariates among the groups, since SMD after IPTW were <0.1 for all covariates. The association between IBD or IBD subtype and overall psychiatric disorders was assessed using the Cox regression models in the above IPTW cohort. In weighted analyses, exposure to IBD was significantly associated with increased risk of all psychiatric disorders [IPTW-weighted HR (wHR) 1.27, 95% CI: 1.16–1.39], substance misuse (wHR 1.18, 95% CI: 1.03–1.34), depression (wHR 1.45, 95% CI: 1.28–1.64), anxiety disorders (wHR 1.22, 95% CI: 1.05–1.41), and PTSD (wHR 2.14, 95% CI: 1.07–4.31), consistent with the main analysis. Subtypes of IBD were associated with an increased risk of overall psychiatric disorders, mirroring the findings of the main analysis mentioned previously (Supplementary eFigure 9, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C809). Supplementary eTable 12 (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C809) displays the mediation effects within the multiple mediator models. Even after accounting for the interaction effects of other biomarkers, our primary analysis results remained robust, with the strongest mediation effects for overall psychiatric disorders generally consistent and even stronger mediation proportion found for substance misuse outcomes.

Discussion

In our study, we observed a significant association between IBD and an increased risk of psychiatric disorders. Across IBD subtype, patients with CD have a higher overall risk of psychiatric morbidity, especially substance misuse, than patients with UC. More importantly, this study represented the mediating role played by blood-cell-based biomarkers in the relationship between IBD and the risk of psychiatric disorders. Among them, six mediating variables have the strongest mediating effect: RDW, neutrophil count, CRP, albumin, RBC, and SII. Furthermore, we identified several blood mediators of CD in association with incident substance misuse, potentially explaining the risk discrepancy between IBD subtypes.

Our findings confirm prior research that IBD increases the risk for various psychiatric disorders, primarily by increasing the incidence of substance misuse disorders, depression disorder, anxiety disorder, and PTSD5,8,30,31. Despite prior research by Bernstein et al. and Marrie et al.7,32, which utilized incidence rate ratios to demonstrate an increased incidence of various psychiatric disorders in the IBD population, our study did not find a significant association between IBD and an elevated risk of bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, or ASD, etc., when examined through survival analysis. This is largely consistent with the findings of Ludvigsson et al.’s and Butwicka et al.’s research4,6. This discrepancy may be attributed to variations in the demographic characteristics of the populations included or the sources of data utilized. Moreover, as IBD subtype, both CD and UC were significantly associated with the risk of depression disorder, which is similar to the results of most previous studies13,14. In addition, our study observed an elevated risk of substance misuse with CD, but not UC.

Blood-based parameters not only serve as convenient tests across numerous fields and routine clinical assessments but also offer accurate and reproducible insights into chronic diseases. We further explored whether and how blood markers mediated the established connection between IBD and psychiatric disorders. In recent years, the gut-brain axis theory has shed light on the bidirectional communication between the gut and the brain10,33–36. Various pathways, such as immune and inflammatory, metabolic and endocrine, and neuronal pathways, play a critical role in this complex interaction. From the mediation analysis, we observed that a variety of blood biomarkers played certain mediating roles in the association between IBD and psychiatric disorders. Firstly, our research suggests that the heightened incidence of psychiatric disorders among IBD patients may be closely tied to inflammation37, particularly neutrophil count, CRP, and SII17,38. In line with the gut-brain axis theory, the induction of inflammation could alter the integrity of the blood-brain barrier and increase inflammatory mediators in the central nervous system, subsequently affecting neurotransmitters in brain regions responsible for mood regulation39,40. Secondly, our study also identified erythrocyte-associated biomarkers, such as RDW and RBC, with significant strong mediating effects. Disease activity and malabsorption of nutrients within the intestine may impact the oxygen supply to various parts of the body, particularly the brain41–43. Furthermore, we found changes in metabolomics also mediated the IBD-psychiatric disorders link, which may be related to oxidative stress or altered mineral levels induced by abnormal albumin and lipid metabolism in patients with IBD44,45.

Additionally, upon comparison, similar blood indicators were found to significantly mediate the heightened risk of depression in both CD and UC. Among these, erythrocyte-related measures, such as RBC and Hb, exhibited the most pronounced mediating effect. Moreover, we were intrigued by the variation in substance misuse risk between UC and CD, leading us to explore potential mediators of their distinct effects. We found 14 blood biomarkers mediated the association between CD and substance misuse, and of these, levels of 11 markers (including neutrophil count, neutrophil percentage, lymphocyte percentage, CRP, PLT, SII, NLR, RBC, RDW, albumin, and triglycerides) differed significantly between CD and UC. These findings suggest CD may present with a more severe condition and imply hematological markers could serve as early predictive tools for substance misuse among IBD patients.

Our discussion’s strength lies in several key aspects. Firstly, to our knowledge, this is one of the pioneering and most extensive studies that has endeavored to quantify the mediating effects of multiple biomarkers on the association between IBD and psychiatric disorders in a population-based cohort. It not only attempts to establish the relationship between IBD and multiple psychiatric disorders but also allows for the correlation between mediators. Secondly, the robustness of our study is significantly enhanced by the use of the representative UK Biobank database population. This broad and diverse data source lends a high degree of generalizability to our findings. Thirdly, our meticulous approach to accounting for various socioeconomic, lifestyle and dietary confounding factors further enhances the reliability and validity of our results. Lastly, our study acknowledges the potential impact of blood biomarkers on psychiatric disorders in the context of IBD. Attention to the degree and specificity of the effects of blood biomarkers aims to better characterize the association between IBD and psychiatric disorders, which helps guide the prevention and intervention of psychiatric comorbidities in IBD patients.

The study also has limitations that deserve consideration. Firstly, we used biomarker measurements from a single time point in the UK Biobank database, and the blood samples obtained may not have been collected at the closest time point to the survey information46. Secondly, inherent inspection errors in biological biomarker measurements may exist, despite generally high-quality validation with coefficients of variation below 9%47–49. Thirdly, we acknowledge that our study did not consider a few potential lifestyle-related factors, leading to some residual confounding. The recording of family history of psychiatric disorders in the UK Biobank was limited, providing data solely on severe depression in family members. Lastly, the lack of clinical and endoscopic disease activity indices of IBD also limited the direct analysis of IBD severity impact. To address this, we explored the mediating role of inflammatory factors as indirect indicators of IBD activity and adjusted for bowel resection and IBD-related medications as supplementary covariates to minimize potential treatment influence on the study.

Conclusions

Our study reveals the mediating role of inflammatory, erythrocyte-related, and metabolic blood biomarkers in the association between IBD and incident psychiatric disorders, providing new insights into the relationship between IBD and mental health. This finding may provide a basis for future more precise interventions. Future research could involve a wider range of biomarkers reflecting inflammation, metabolism, and other states, and further clarify whether there is a causal relationship between biomarkers and the development of psychiatric disorders in patients with IBD.
