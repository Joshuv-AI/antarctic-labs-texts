Clinical course and outcomes of patients with anti-interferon-gamma autoantibody-associated adult-onset immunodeficiency: an observational cohort study
Authors: Nasikarn Angkasekwinai, Ekkarat Wongsawat, Nutchavadee Vorasan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnti-interferon-gamma autoantibody (anti-IFN-γ-auto-Abs)-associated adult-onset immunodeficiency (AOID) syndrome presents with variable clinical manifestations, and long-term outcome data and natural history remain limited.Materials and methodsThis single-center study utilized a disease registry of AOID patients (≥18 years) at Siriraj Hospital, Bangkok, Thailand. Clinical outcomes (survival, death, loss to follow-up) as of November 20, 2024 and disease status (remission, non-remission) at each visit were analyzed.ResultsA total of 65 patients were recorded over an eight-year period between 2017 and 2024. The mean age of all patients was 56.4 years, and 66% were female. At initial presentation, 95 opportunistic infections were isolated from 61 patients. M. abscessus (44.2%) was the predominant pathogens, followed by M. avium complex (13.7%), salmonellosis (9.5%) and M. tuberculosis (8.4%). Among 53 patients with at least one year of follow-up, 12 (22.6%) achieved remission and 41 (77.4%) remained in non-remission. Antibody levels exhibited a significant decline over time (p < 0.05) across all patients. However, no significant difference in antibody levels was observed between remission and non-remission group (p > 0.05). Notably, median baseline and latest antibody concentration were significantly lower in the remission group compared to the non-remission group, respectively (baseline level: 3.5, IQR 2.8–3.8 vs. 4.0, IQR 3.5–4.2, p = 0.030; latest level: 2.1, IQR 1.8–2.4 vs. 3.0, IQR 2.1–3.6, p = 0.044).ConclusionThis study noted a slight shift in pathogen distribution, even though rapidly growing mycobacteria remain the most common infection. Most patients experienced non-remission, necessitating prolonged antimicrobial therapy. Our findings indicate a declining trend in antibody levels over time. Given the high rates of non-remission and the associated need for extended treatment, immunomodulatory agents should be considered, especially for non-remission patients, to potentially reduce antibody levels and the risk of infection recurrence.Keywords: Nontuberculous mycobacteria, anti-interferon gamma autoantibody, adult-onset immunodeficiency syndrome

Introduction

Anti-interferon-gamma autoantibody (anti-IFN-γ-auto-Abs)-associated immunodeficiency syndrome has emerged as adult-onset immunodeficiency (AOID) in various Asian countries including Thailand, since 2004 [1–3]. These patients exhibit increase susceptibility to disseminated infections caused by intracellular pathogen, such as non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM), Salmonella spp., Talaromyces spp and Varicella-Zoster [1,4,5]. The clinical presentation and natural history of this condition can vary significantly. While approximately one-fifth of patients may be able to discontinue antimicrobial treatment, the majority of patients experience persistent or recurrent infections despite prolonged antimicrobial therapy, necessitating long-term follow-up [6]. Currently, there is a paucity of data on the long-term outcomes of these patients. To address this critical knowledge gap, a research team has established a study registry for patients with anti-IFN-γ-auto-Abs-associated AOID syndrome. Long-term data collection from this registry will assist in gaining a more comprehensive understanding of the clinical course and outcome of this challenging condition.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This study utilized data from a single-center, disease registry of patients with anti-IFN-γ-auto-Abs-associated AOID syndrome. This registry was established in July 2019 to collect data on the occurrence and types of infection, disease activity, auotoantibody level, antimicrobial treatment, and clinical outcomes in these patients. Eligible participants were adult patients (≥18 years) diagnosed with anti-IFN-γ-auto-Abs–associated AOID at Siriraj Hospital, Bangkok, Thailand, between January 2017 and November 2024. Retrospective data, collected from patient diagnosed between January 2017 and July 2019, excluded those diagnosed before December 2016, as their data was previously published [6]. Prospective data were collected from new patients diagnosed between July 2019 and November 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Siriraj Institutional Review Board (COA no. Si 398/2019). This study followed the Declaration of Helsinki Ethical Principles for Medical Research involving human subjects. Written inform consent was obtained from participants in the prospective cohort and was waived for the retrospective cohort approved by the Siriraj Institutional Review Board, Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University.

Medical records were retrospectively reviewed for patients diagnosed before July 2019. Antimicrobial therapy was determined by the attending physician based on the identified pathogens, infection site, and patient tolerance. Data included infection characteristics and associated reactive skin diseases. Final clinical outcomes, categorized as survive, death or loss to follow-up (LTF) were assessed as of November 20, 2024. Disease status at each visit, including the last visit before loss to follow-up or at the end of study was categorized as drug-free remission, (no antibiotic requirement) or non-remission (stable disease requiring oral antibiotics), or active disease requiring intravenous antibiotics within the past year. Serum anti-IFN-γ auto-Abs concentrations were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), with results expressed as the optical density (OD) relative to a healthy control. An OD of ≥ 1 was considered positive [6].

Statistical analysis

Study data were collected and managed using research electronic data capture (REDCap) tools hosted at the Research Data Management Unit (RDMU), Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University [7]. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation, https://www.r-project.org). The Fisher’s exact test and chi-squared test were used to compare baseline categorical variables between remission and non-remission groups. We compared the mean age difference between the two groups using a t-test, while other numeric variables were analyzed for differences between groups using the Mann-Whitney U test. A linear mixed-effects model was used to assess the effect of time, disease status, and their interaction on antibody levels. Patient was included as a random effect, using the lmer function from the lme4 package in R. A survival analysis of the time to the death was performed using the ‘survminer’ R package. Statistical significance was considered at an alpha level of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05).

Results

Over an eight-year period (2017–2024), the registry recorded 65 patients with anti-IFN-γ-auto-Abs-associated AOID, with a median follow-up time of 29 months. The mean age of all patients was 56.4 years, 66.2% were female and 30.8% had comorbidities. All patients were detected sporadically. Their birthplace are distributed across all regions of Thailand, and no cluster of familial cases have been identified. At initial presentation, 95 opportunistic infections (OIs) were isolated from 61 patients. Non-tuberculous mycobacteria accounted for approximately 70% of isolated pathogen, with rapidly growing mycobacteria (RGM) being the most common (47.4%), followed by slowly growing mycobacteria (SGM) (15.8%). Specifically, M. abscessus was the most frequently isolated pathogen (44.2%), followed by M. avium complex (13.7%), salmonellosis (9.5%) and M. tuberculosis (8.4%) (Table 1). Over the follow-up period, 135 proven OIs were identified in 61 patients. Of which, 27 patients (44.3%) experienced a single OI, 17 patients (27.9%) had two OIs, 6 patients (9.8%) had three OIs, 5 patients (8.2%) had four OIs, 3 patients (4.9%) had five OIs, 2 patients (3.3%) had six OIs, and 1 patient (1.6%) had nine OIs. These 135 OIs originated from 158 separate sites. Notably, 14 patients (21.5%) had infections at multiple site with the same pathogen during a single infection event. The most common isolation site was the lymph node (32.2%), followed by bloodstream (19%), lungs (15.8%) and tissue (14.6%) (Figure 1). M. abscessus was frequently isolated from lymph nodes, blood and tissues, M. intracellulare was isolated from tissue and blood, while Salmonella spp. was frequently isolated from blood specimen. At the end of follow-up, 29 patients (44.6%) were alive, 4 patients (6.2%) had died and 32 patients (49.2%) were lost to follow up (LTF). Among the 29 surviving patients, 22 (75.9%) continued to be in non-remission, whereas 7 (24.1%) achieved remission and ceased antibiotic treatment for a median duration of 53 months. The four patients who died were all in non-remission states, with a median time to death of 29 months post diagnosis. Of the 32 patients lost to follow up, the majority (82.8%) were in non-remission status, with a median time to LTF of 22.5 months post diagnosis (Table 1).

Of the 53 patients followed up for at least one year, 12 (22.6%) achieved remission, while 41 (77.4%) remained in non-remission at the last visit. Table 2 compares the clinical characteristic of these two group. The non-remission group exhibited a higher frequency of fungal infections, a greater occurrence of reactive skin disease, and a higher incidence of concomitant OIs at initial presentation compared to the remission group. However, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in age, gender, the initial OI, the total number of OIs, the presence of reactive skin disease, white blood cell count, or C-reactive protein level. Notably, the median baseline antibody concentration was significantly lower in the remission group than in the non-remission group, respectively (3.5, IQR 2.8–3.8 vs. 4.0, IQR 3.5–4.2, p = 0.030). Similarly, the median latest antibody concentration was also significantly lower in the remission group (2.1, IQR 1.8–2.4 vs. 3.0, IQR 2.1–3.6, p = 0.044). Survival curve analysis showed no significant difference in mortality between the two group (p = 0.13).

Among the 53 patients, antibody levels significantly decreased over time compared to baseline (Supplementary Table S1). The estimated average decline in antibody levels was 0.73 in year 1, 0.93 in year 2, 0.84 in year 3, 1.09 in year 4, and 1.23 in year 5. This demonstrated a significant effect of time on antibody levels (p < 0.05), indicating a consistent decline throughout the study period. However, there was no significant difference in antibody levels between patients in remission and non-remission group (p > 0.05), and the interaction between time and disease status was not significant (Supplementary Table S1). Figure 3 illustrates the antibody level changes over time, revealing that no patients achieved remission within the first two years after diagnosis. Although antibody levels generally decreases, fluctuations and temporary increases were observed. Furthermore, disease status remain dynamic, with patients transitioning between remission (allowing for medication cessation) states and non-remission (requiring new treatment) states.

Discussion

This study describes the clinical characteristics and outcomes of patients with anti-IFN-γ-auto-Abs-associated AOID syndrome over an eight-year period (2017–2024). Compared to our previous cohort of 80 patients with data collected until the 2016 [6], the current cohort exhibited a higher number of initial OIs (95 OIs in 65 patients vs. 91 OIs in 80 patients). Furthermore, the distribution of common pathogen differed. The proportion of NTM infection was slightly higher in the current cohort (70%) compared to the previous cohort (52%). Although RGM remained the most common pathogen, slow growing mycobacteria particularly MAC were more common in the current cohort compared to the previous cohort (16% vs. 6%) and higher than observed in a Thai cohort from Srinagarind Hospital in northeastern Thailand (data collected from 2010 to 2018) [5]. Additionally, the occurrence of salmonellosis and Varicella-Zoster virus was lower in the current cohort compared to the previous cohort (10% vs. 20% and 0% vs. 5–10%, respectively). The epidemiology of infections seems to have shifted compared to that observed nearly a decade ago. Long-term monitoring, including data from other hospitals, is crucial for a comprehensive understanding of these changes.

Rapid-growing mycobacteria (RGM) were frequently identified from lymph node and bloodstream samples, while slow-growing mycobacteria (SGM) particularly MAC, were commonly identified from tissue such as bone and joint. These finding align with previous studies [5,8]. Given that 19% of the isolates were obtained from blood cultures and 32% from lymph node biopsies, performing blood culture for mycobacteria while awaiting lymph nodes or tissue biopsy result could potentially enhance pathogen identification.

The majority of patients either under follow-up, or lost to follow-up, experienced non-remission. Only 24% of patients were able to achieve remission and discontinue antibiotic for more than 4 years. This remission rate is slightly higher than the 12% reported in a previous review of 111 patients across 35 studies conducted between 2004 and 2011 [8]. However, another study demonstrated that up to 80% of patients achieved infection clearance after a long-term follow-up period of 6–8 years [5]. The mortality rate in this cohort (4 out of 65 patients) was comparable to our previous cohort (4 out of 63 patients) [6]. However, approximately half of the patients were also lost to follow-up.

A significant proportion of patients continued to experience recurrence or new infections, requiring long-term antimycobacterial therapy. Previous study reported median time to infection clearance of 3, 4 and 5 years for patients treated with antibiotics alone, antibiotics with rituximab and antibiotics with cyclophosphamide, respectively [5]. Currently, data on the optimal duration of antimycobacterial therapy is limited due to the persistent and recurrent nature of these infection. Our study found that no patient discontinued antibiotic within the first two years. These findings support the 2023 Infectious Diseases Society of Thailand guidance for treating non-tuberculous mycobacterial infection in patients in adult-onset immunodeficiency syndrome due to anti-interferon gamma autoantibody, which recommends antimicrobial treatment for at least 2–4 years, with the absence of superficial lymphadenopathy, resolved imaging abnormalities, and negative culture for the same pathogen from any site. However, recurrence or relapse can occur even after treatment cessation [8–10].

Antibody levels are associated with neutralizing activity and immunodeficiency [1,8]. Although the interaction between follow-up time and disease status did not reach statistically significance (p > 0.05), the estimated coefficients suggested a trend of decreasing antibody levels over time in both groups. Notably, this decline appeared more pronounced in the remission group, though without statistical significance. This trend may reflect underlying biological or clinical processes with potential clinical implications. Further investigation in a larger cohort with balanced group sizes is warranted to confirm this finding.

Both baseline and latest antibody concentration were lower in the remission group compared to the non-remission group. This observation may inform decisions regarding antibiotic discontinuation in patients with low antibody levels. However, data on specific antibody levels thresholds to prevent recurrent infection is limited. Larger prospective studies are needed to establish these thresholds. Antibody levels exhibited fluctuations over time, with potential increases following decreases. This dynamic behavior mirrors the fluctuating nature of disease status, which can transition between remission and non-remission states. Therefore, in patients experiencing new symptoms or recurrent infection after antibiotic discontinuation, immunosuppressive therapy may be considered to reduce antibody levels. While several immunomodulatory agents such as rituximab [11,12], cyclophosphamide [13,14], corticosteroid [15], bortezomib [16,17] and daratumumab [18], have demonstrated reduction in antibody level and infection recurrence, infection clearance times appear prolonged in those receiving rituximab and cyclophosphamide. Close long-term follow-up for recurrent or new infections is crucial in these patients.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the relatively small sample size of 65 patients over an eight-year period reflects the rarity of this specific disease. Secondly, as a single-center study conducted at a university hospital in Bangkok, Thailand, the observed infections spectrum may not be representative of other regions within Thailand or other countries, thus limiting the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, variation in antibody level measurement methods across different laboratories could introduce inconsistencies. Lastly, the anti-IFN-γ auto-antibody concentrations in this study only measures the binding of autoantibodies to interferon-gamma. While diluting the serum and measuring titers can be a more sensitive method for detecting changes in binding antibody levels [16], we did not perform this procedure. Despite this, the observed decrease in OD over time probably suggests that neutralizing antibody levels are also declining. However, we did not perform functional assays to directly measure the neutralizing activity. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the clinical manifestations and disease course of patients with anti-IFN-γ-auto-Abs-associated immunodeficiency syndrome. Further long-term studies involving larger multi-center cohorts are warranted.

Conclusions

Rapidly growing mycobacteria remain the most common infectious agents, although a slight shift in pathogen distribution was observed. The majority of patients experienced persistent non-remission state, requiring prolonged antimicrobial therapy. Antibody levels demonstrated a general declining trend over time, but also exhibited fluctuations. Immunomodulatory agents should be considered, particularly in patients with non-remission, to potentially decrease antibody level and reduce the risk of infection recurrence. Further long-term studies involving larger multi-center cohorts are warranted.
