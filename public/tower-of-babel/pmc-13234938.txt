Introduction

Widespread use of AI in healthcare could exacerbate privacy risks and longitudinal trends of declining patient trust in healthcare.1-3 Opacity around AI use, increasingly publicized data sharing, and limited communication with patients may undermine patient trust and comfort with the technology.4 Recognition of these potential challenges has led to calls for more specific, patient-centered AI policy and practice in addition to state-level legislative activity focused on disclosure and patient protections.5,6 However, it is possible that threats to patient trust may be tempered by increased exposure to AI in daily life through non-healthcare applications like navigation systems and large language models (eg, ChatGPT). If this kind of general exposure to AI translates to comfort with the technology in healthcare, the need for redesigned health privacy policies or extensive overhaul of health system governance approaches for AI transparency may not be as urgent.1 This raises a key question—is public comfort with AI in healthcare different from comfort with AI in other domains? The answer has critical implications for whether and how practice and policy may need to evolve.

Existing policy treats issues of privacy, security, data ownership, and access profoundly differently for healthcare compared with other domains. For example, healthcare data is largely defined by its user (ie, predominantly health systems and insurers) and is not readily accessible by employers or other entities that do not serve a role in patient care. At the same time, identifiable data can be freely exchanged under HIPAA regulations for the purposes of treatment, payment, and operations that extends the scope of data sharing to third-party trusted business associates that support these functions7,8 like a vendor providing an ambient scribe or AI-enabled scheduling application. As AI becomes increasingly visible to patients through their direct interactions with these kinds of tools, many stakeholders and experts have focused on policy and practice development for consent, notification, and engagement.9,10 Policymakers are engaged in these discussions via debate on proposed legislation related to data protection and AI disclosures in Pennsylvania House Bill 210011 or the implementation of California's recently enacted AB489 requiring clear labeling of AI-generated content in healthcare.12 In addition to these policy developments, privacy policy debates that gained traction during the big data era continue regarding HIPAA's limitations in the current health data ecosystem.7,13 To design patient-centered AI in healthcare and inform these policy debates, it is necessary to empirically understand how patients perceive AI and to what extent healthcare represents an exceptional case.

If the public does not see AI in healthcare as fundamentally distinct from AI in other domains, patient trust and comfort with healthcare AI may not warrant major policy intervention or fundamental changes to AI governance in care delivery organizations. Overhauls of existing privacy policies and data sharing practices may not be warranted, thus reducing the considerable effort and related cost required to modernize these systems. However, evidence of healthcare exceptionalism may indicate that inaction regarding patient engagement is insufficient to safeguard patient trust as these technologies shape healthcare. The objective of this study was to empirically examine the question of healthcare exceptionalism among the US adult public for AI and consider the implications for AI use and policy in healthcare.

Data and methods

Cross-sectional survey data was analyzed from a nationally representative sample of US adults. The online survey was fielded by the National Opinion Research Center (NORC) from June to July 2023. Survey respondents viewed a brief explanatory video describing how AI can be applied in the healthcare system that was tested through focus groups and reviewed by national experts.14,15 The survey instrument underwent cognitive testing and piloting prior to fielding to minimize response bias and ensure participant comprehension. Population weights, calculated using the Current Population survey, were applied to produce national estimates. The NORC and University of Michigan IRBs determined the study to be exempt.

Respondents indicated their comfort with a broad range of AI applications across non-healthcare domains on a four-point Likert scale (1 = not comfortable, 4 = very comfortable). The full survey question text is available in the Supplement. AI applications (Table 1) were presented in randomized order to minimize order bias. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with an oblique promax rotation was used to identify underlying factors of comfort with AI. Two factors were retained. Factor loadings of at least 0.4 were used to determine whether a variable loaded on a particular factor.16 Cronbach's alpha (>0.7) was used to confirm internal consistency of each factor. Composite scores of comfort with AI were calculated as the respondent's average score across all survey measures comprising that factor, which were then compared with respondents’ comfort with AI in healthcare using the Wilcoxon signed rank test.

Respondents indicated their comfort with healthcare AI on a four-point Likert scale (1 = not true, 4 = very true) in response to the statement “I am comfortable with AI tools being used in my healthcare”. Respondents whose comfort scores exceeded the mean were coded as high comfort (binary variable set to 1) and those at or below the mean were coded as low comfort to indicate relative comfort. Multivariable logistic regressions of each binary comfort score (Factor 1, Factor 2, healthcare) on respondents’ self-reported demographics, health literacy, self-perception of tech-savviness, and health insurance status were conducted.

Results

The survey was completed by 2039 respondents. Missing data for 84 of these respondents resulted in an analytic sample of 1955. The EFA of comfort with non-healthcare AI applications indicated that variables loading on Factor 1 were largely utility-focused and had been publicly available for a longer period than the variables that loaded on Factor 2. Factor 1 included GPS, smart watches, weather forecasting, driving safety alerts, bank fraud alerts, and digital assistants like Alexa or Siri (Table 1). This factor is labeled “familiar and personally useful AI”. Factor 2 included newer (eg, self-driving cars, ChatGPT) and less directly or personally useful tools including online shopping recommendations, facial recognition software, targeted advertising on social media, and video streaming recommendations. Factor 2 is labeled “novel and marketing AI”. The Wilcoxon signed rank test indicated that respondents perceived healthcare AI differently than Factor 1 (P < 0.001) but not Factor 2 (P = 0.6) (Figure 1).

Weighted multivariable logistic regression of the binary indicators of comfort indicated that female respondents were less comfortable with healthcare AI (OR 0.74, P = 0.019) and Factor 1 (OR 0.77, P = 0.016) than male respondents (Table 2). Self-identified tech-savviness was a consistent positive predictor of comfort across the three AI categories, while education and racial identity did not have significant relationships with comfort. Self-reported willingness to take risks was a significant predictor of comfort with health AI and Factor 2 but not Factor 1.

This study has limitations as a cross-sectional analysis of survey data. Due to the relative novelty of AI for the public, longitudinal analysis of any changes in comfort over time will be necessary to further inform trustworthy decision-making about AI implementation by providing insight into changes in public perceptions over time. Qualitative analysis will also be important to understand why patients may feel differently about healthcare AI (eg, risk, lack of personal benefit, privacy expectations).

Discussion

This analysis identifies that public comfort with healthcare AI is more closely aligned with perceptions of newer and riskier (eg, self-driving cars) AI than other AI types with which the public may be more familiar or more likely to perceive potential personal benefit (eg, fraud alerts). It is possible that increasing familiarity with AI could shift public comfort over time, especially for those who see themselves as tech-savvy or comfortable with risk. However, given indicators of lower confidence and acceptance of AI-generated advice among patients17 and concerns about the state of AI oversight,18 public comfort requires additional attention and action in the healthcare system.

Perception of personal benefit or usefulness could be an important element of public comfort. In addition to relative ubiquity, many of the Factor 1 applications provide direct value to the user (eg, GPS, bank fraud alerts, personal assistants). On the other hand, Factor 2 applications may offer less obvious personal benefit or seem misaligned with an individual's interests (eg, targeted advertising, facial recognition). Our findings of higher comfort with Factor 1 may reflect higher perceived utility or expectation of benefit. The finding that comfort with healthcare AI is significantly lower than Factor 1 applications may reflect that the public has yet to identify personal value in healthcare AI applications. Policy and AI development that focus on specifically addressing patient priorities like reducing medical debt or increasing access to care may ameliorate some of this discomfort.

These results also indicate that perceptions of healthcare AI may mirror shifts in public comfort with novel or riskier applications as opposed to AI in general. Given the alignment between healthcare AI and newer or riskier AI identified in this analysis, comfort may only increase over the long term and if the public perceives that appropriate safeguards like AI evaluation and FDA oversight are in place.19 This will require intervention and more rigorous AI monitoring,20 as health systems are not considered trustworthy AI users by most US adults.21 In the meantime, health systems, industry stakeholders, and policymakers can address public sensitivities regarding healthcare AI by promoting notification, privacy, and transparency, which are critical ethical safeguards of the public's trust.9

Conclusion

Analysis of this cross-sectional, nationally representative survey of the US adult public identified evidence of healthcare exceptionalism in that public comfort with healthcare AI was significantly lower than comfort with more familiar AI applications. Comfort with AI in healthcare was more closely aligned with perceptions of newer or riskier (eg, self-driving cars) AI than other AI types with which the public may be more familiar or more likely to perceive potential personal benefit (eg, fraud alerts). Stakeholders including health systems and policymakers should prioritize rigorous evaluation, privacy, communication about AI use, and patient value to ensure that implementation does not violate patient comfort or undermine trust.
