College students physical activity and subjective wellbeing with the mediating role of social support self esteem and resilience
Authors: Haijin Pan, Hongguo Guan, Zhenlei Lv, Henan Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Physical activity has become a vital means for psychological adjustment. This study aims to explore the relationship between physical activity and subjective well-being among college students, as well as the mediating roles of perceived social support, self-esteem, and psychological resilience. Researchers randomly selected 846 college students for this study, utilizing scales for physical activity, social support, psychological resilience, self-esteem, and subjective well-being to survey the current status of these variables among the student population. The results indicate that physical activity significantly positively affects perceived social support, self-esteem, and psychological resilience within the college student group. Both perceived social support and self-esteem also significantly positively impact subjective well-being. However, the direct effects of physical activity and psychological resilience on subjective well-being are not significant. Moreover, perceived social support and self-esteem mediate the impact of physical activity on subjective well-being, while psychological resilience does not serve a mediating role. Finally, this study presents relevant theoretical and practical implications concerning physical activity and subjective well-being, providing a theoretical foundation to promote physical activity and enhance subjective well-being among college students.

Introduction

As societal competition intensifies, people’s psychological stress continues to escalate, making physical activity (PA) one of the essential methods for regulating psychological states1. Participation in PA has been consistently related to both physical vitality and emotional steadiness in the college student population, while its relevance to subjective well-being (SW) has also been widely acknowledged in empirical research2. SW is usually understood as referring to a person’s subjective assessment of their quality of life, encompassing emotional experiences and mental states, including both positive and negative experiences3. The presence of subjective SW among college students has been consistently linked with diverse areas of functioning, spanning psychological health, scholastic achievements, social connectedness, and expectations for professional development4. Therefore, understanding and enhancing the SW of college students can not only improve their psychological health and social status but also promote academic success, having significant implications for fostering high-quality development among college students.

Current research on SW primarily focuses on three aspects. Firstly, researchers have explored the internal and external factors affecting individual SW. Internal factors include self-acceptance5 and psychological resilience (PR)6, while external factors involve the quality of social relationships7 and job satisfaction8. Notably, PA has been proven to significantly enhance college students’ SW9. For instance, the study by KundziA and Grants10 found that PA could improve the balance of brain chemicals, such as increasing the release of endorphins, which are associated with positive sensations and directly affect SW. Second, cross-cultural research has highlighted the critical role of cultural values in shaping SW11. From a cross-cultural perspective, Western societies tend to emphasize personal achievement, self-actualization, and individual freedom, whereas Eastern cultures place greater importance on interpersonal relationships, social responsibility, and collective belonging. In the Chinese context, college students’ SW is influenced not only by academic performance but also by family expectations, peer comparisons, and prevailing social norms12,13. Meanwhile, Chinese society is undergoing a period of rapid transformation, during which individuals’ values and psychological needs reflect a blend of traditional and modern elements. This cultural shift has intensified the tension college students experience between personal development and external expectations14. Moreover, the combined pressures of higher education expansion and a competitive job market present unique challenges for Chinese college students in both academic and career adjustment15–18. These contextual factors may contribute to differences in how PA promotes SW compared to Western contexts. Lastly, intervention studies on SW have also garnered significant attention, with research exploring methods like cognitive-behavioral therapy19 and mindfulness meditation20 to enhance individuals’ SW.

Existing literature exploring the interaction between PA and SW, as well as its potential influencing factors, still exhibits significant research gaps, especially within the university environment. The academic pressures faced by students may cause the effects of these factors to differ from those in the general adult population. Much of the current literature addresses physical activity (PA) and subjective well-being (SW) as a separate dyad, emphasizing their one-to-one linkage without integrating other contextual factors21,22, overlooking the comprehensive effects of potential mediating variables such as PSS, SE, and PR. This research focuses on bridging the gap by investigating how mediating factors shape the connection between PA and SW in the context of college students.

To summarize, the present research addresses the association between PA and SW among college students and considers the intermediary roles of PSS, SE, and PR. These findings contribute fresh conceptual insights and empirical support for understanding college students’ SW. The results may provide meaningful strategies and practical references for supporting college students’ overall health, emotional condition, and subjective well-being.

Literature review

Theoretical framework

Self-Determination Theory (SDT), proposed by Deci and Ryan23, explains how individuals foster motivation, personality integration, and psychological well-being by satisfying basic psychological needs. According to the theory, autonomy, competence, and relatedness are regarded as core psychological needs that support intrinsic motivation and are closely associated with SW24. In the context of PA among college students, engaging in PA can fulfill the need for competence (e.g., physical improvement and a sense of achievement), while team-based interactions help satisfy relatedness (e.g., social connection and belonging). These experiences stimulate intrinsic motivation and contribute to higher levels of SW2,9 In this study, PSS, SE, and PR are conceptualized as key psychological responses following the satisfaction of basic needs through PA. Specifically, PSS reflects the fulfillment of relatedness; SE, as a positive evaluation of self-worth, aligns closely with competence; and PR demonstrates autonomy and self-regulation when facing stress. Theoretically, these three constructs form parallel mediating pathways through which PA is associated with SW. While conceptually independent, they jointly convey the positive psychological effects of PA.

Positive Psychology Theory, developed by Seligman and Csikszentmihalyi25, emphasizes the identification and development of individual strengths, positive experiences, and psychological resources to maximize well-being and mental health. From this viewpoint, SW can be described as a multidimensional concept that encompasses positive emotions, engagement, interpersonal connections, life meaning, and accomplishment, which together form the PERMA model26. PA, regarded as a constructive behavioral pattern, has frequently been linked to the strengthening of SE, PR, and PSS. These resources are considered core mechanisms for promoting SW within the framework of positive psychology9,27–29. Moreover, the theory underscores the malleability of positive resources, thereby supporting the use of PA interventions to enhance college students’ SW and mental health. This theoretical lens thus provides integrative insight and conceptual grounding for the positive variables included in this study.

Taken together, SDT and Positive Psychology Theory offer complementary perspectives for the current research model. SDT explains how PA fosters intrinsic motivation and positive psychological states through the satisfaction of basic needs, while positive psychology highlights how the accumulation and activation of psychological resources, such as SE, PSS, and PR, contribute to SW. By integrating these two frameworks, this study establishes a comprehensive model illustrating how PA may influence college students’ SW through three parallel psychological mediators, thereby offering both theoretical justification and a practical framework for well-being interventions.

Physical activity

PA refers to activities that maintain physical health and enhance fitness through systematic, repetitive, and purposeful physical exercises. Such activities promote the coordinated operation of various body systems, effectively improving individual health levels30. PA significantly impacts an individual’s physical health, psychological state, and daily life, and has become a significant research field. In this domain, studies primarily address the effects of PA on individual physiological, psychological, and lifestyle aspects31–35. Firstly, PA markedly affects physiological health. Research has shown that PA significantly enhances cardiopulmonary functions34, regulates weight, and improves metabolic functions31, with regular participation in physical activities significantly reducing the probability of persistent medical diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and obesity35. Secondly, PA positively influences psychological health. It can enhance individuals’ self-efficacy36, improve mood33, reduce stress33, and increase SW29. Lastly, PA significantly affects social interactions37. For instance, Zhu, et al.38 demonstrated that participating in PA not only enhances physical vitality but also helps to increase the frequency of social interactions and build stronger social support networks. Additionally, Engagement in PA is frequently related to stronger scholastic achievements, as students maintaining consistent activity levels are commonly observed to attain comparatively better academic results39.

Perceived social support

PSS denotes a person’s subjective understanding of the assistance offered by those around them40. Studies indicate that PSS profoundly affects various aspects of an individual’s life. Specifically, PSS can significantly improve students’ academic performance41. Social support not only helps students succeed in the school environment but also facilitates their adaptation to university life42. For instance, Tinajero, et al.42 discovered that PSS enhances students’ academic self-efficacy and motivation, thereby improving their academic performance. Additionally, adequate social support provides emotional and practical assistance, alleviating academic stress and enhancing learning efficiency and outcomes. Furthermore, PSS is closely linked to college students’ psychological health. Numerous studies have shown that increased PSS is linked to reduced anxiety and depression43. Meanwhile, perceived social support is often strongly associated with individual levels of SW44. Finally research on the effects of PSS on PA, such as the study by Roh and Moon27, indicates that individuals experiencing substantial social support have a greater tendency to enjoy physical activities, thus enhancing their enthusiasm and persistence in participation. This positive engagement in physical activities, in turn, promotes social interactions and the building of social networks. Expanding evidence has linked the diversity and depth of social support systems to how individuals perceive their social embeddedness and connection with others45.

Self-esteem

SE denotes a person’s general assessment or perception of their own value, involving cognitive and emotional experiences related to self-identity and self-value46. SE is commonly regarded as a key factor associated with individuals’ psychological well-being and capacity for social adjustment. Current research on SE primarily focuses on two aspects: Firstly, the impact of PA on SE has received considerable attention. Participation in physical activities allows individuals to experience enhanced physical capabilities and improved appearance, which typically directly boosts their self-value47. Secondly, SE significantly influences people’s overall mental state. Fluctuations in SE have been repeatedly connected with emotional conditions such as depressive symptoms and anxiety, according to findings from prior empirical work48. Individuals with higher levels of SE are more capable of employing effective approaches to handle obstacles and setbacks, thereby reducing the production of negative emotions. Conversely, people with reduced SE are more likely to engage in negative self-attribution49, which further increases the chances of suffering from depression and anxiety. SE not only affects individuals’ emotional states but also significantly contributes to long-term psychological health and social adaptation.

Psychological resilience

PR refers to an individual’s capacity to adapt and recover psychologically when facing life challenges and stressors32. It is widely recognized as a key psychological resource that contributes to mental health and SW.

Existing research suggests that PA is positively associated with the enhancement of PR9,33,50. Individuals who regularly engage in PA tend to demonstrate stronger coping abilities and are more likely to maintain emotional stability and mental flexibility under stressful conditions9. Engagement in PA has been linked to better physical condition and stronger beliefs in personal capability, while its connection to PR may stem from its role in fostering uplifting emotional states and promoting integrated mind–body regulation33. Moreover, PR is closely linked to SW9,51. Empirical evidence suggests that individuals with greater resilience tend to develop stronger internal coping resources and report more frequent positive emotional experiences, which are often associated with increased life satisfaction and well-being33.

Hypotheses

Physical activity and subjective Well-Being

Evidence from contemporary research has connected PA to enhanced physical and mental well-being, as well as to elevated levels of SW2. Studies suggest that PA promotes SW through various physiological and psychological mechanisms. Physiologically, PA improves the balance of brain chemicals. For instance, research by KundziA and Grants10 found that PA increases the release of endorphins, chemicals associated with positive emotions, thereby directly enhancing individual SW. Additionally, PA enhances cardiopulmonary function and metabolic function, thus improving overall health35, which in turn fosters psychological well-being10. From a psychological perspective, PA is frequently associated with improved mental health outcomes. By engaging in physical activities, individuals experience enhanced physical capabilities and appearance improvements, typically boosting their SE directly36. To illustrate, Zhang, et al.2 investigated data from 1204 college students found a significant positive impact of PA on SW. Similarly, research by Lucchini, et al.29, examining families, also confirmed the significant positive effect of PA on SW. Drawing upon the preceding theoretical analysis, this research formulates the following set of hypotheses:

H1

PA significantly positively affects college students’ SW.

Physical activity, perceived social support, and subjective well-being

Participating in team sports or fitness activities allows individuals to feel support and encouragement from peers, which is crucial for enhancing emotional states and self-worth27. Research by Roh and Moon27 indicates that when people experience strong social support in physical activities, they are more likely to experience enjoyment and satisfaction. Gizem, et al.45 further suggest that positive feedback and support in social interactions can boost individuals’ confidence in their abilities, a key factor in enhancing SW. Studies show that social interactions and support promoted by PA not only enhance the positive effects of the activity itself but also indirectly increase SW2. Additionally, PA creates an environment conducive to developing and maintaining positive social networks. These networks provide emotional support and increase individuals’ sense of belonging and social participation44. For instance, research by Gizem, et al.45 investigating the relationship between college students’ PA and SW revealed that PA not only significantly positively impacts their SW but also indirectly enhances happiness through enhanced social interactions. Therefore, in this study, PSS acts as a crucial psychological and social resource mediating between PA and SW. Building on the previous discussion, the following hypotheses are proposed in this study:

H2

PA significantly positively affects college students’ PSS.

H3

College students’ PSS significantly positively affects their SW.

H4

PSS mediates the relationship between PA and SW.

Physical activity, psychological resilience, and subjective well-being

PA serves as an effective approach to enhance PR52. Regular participation in physical activities can increase individuals’ mental and physical endurance and enhance their PR, enabling better emotional regulation and stress management capabilities when facing adversities, thereby helping them cope better with life’s pressures and challenges33. Research by Cai, et al.9 suggests that PR, as a mediating variable, explains how PA enhances individual SW through psychological pathways. Individuals with high PR are more effective in using the positive psychological changes brought by PA, such as increased confidence and social interactions, thus experiencing higher life satisfaction and happiness. Additionally, research by Xiao-Qiang and Fen50 found that PR affects individuals’ experiences and feelings in physical activities, thus differently impacting their SW. Study points out that PR is vital to transform the positive experiences of PA into SW51. Individuals with high PR are better at handling negative emotions and stress in physical activities, thereby more fully experiencing the positive effects of PA33. Therefore, this study emphasizes enhancing PR in health interventions to maximize the positive effects of PA. Informed by the prior theoretical and empirical review, this research articulates the following hypotheses:

H5

PA significantly positively affects college students’ PR.

H6

College students’ PR significantly positively affects their SW.

H7

PR mediates the relationship between PA and SW.

Physical activity, self-esteem, and subjective well-being

PA has a direct positive effect on individuals’ SE. By participating in physical activities, individuals experience a sense of achievement and progress, which typically directly enhances their self-worth28. According to Pérez-Fuentes, et al.28, those who maintain regular physical activity often report a heightened sense of pride and achievement, patterns that correspond with elevated SE. Research by Zhang, et al.53 demonstrates that those with higher SE have a greater tendency to gain positive experiences such as pleasure and satisfaction, which further enhance their life satisfaction and SW. Additionally, as a significant psychological resource, SE helps individuals maintain a positive attitude and emotional state when facing challenges and stress54. People with high SE typically have a greater capacity to feel positive emotions, reducing the impact of negative emotions, which helps enhance their overall well-being55. Based on the preceding discussion, this study proposes that SE may serve as a mediating variable linking PA and SW in the context of college students. Engagement in physical activity among college students has been linked to elevated self-esteem and a stronger sense of personal achievement, patterns that correspond with increased subjective happiness. Therefore, this study proposes the following hypotheses:

H8

PA significantly positively affects college students’ SE.

H9

College students’ SE significantly positively affects their SW.

H10

SE mediates the relationship between PA and SW.

Materials and methods

Participants

Between November 2024 and January 2025, this study employed an online survey platform (https://www.wjx.cn/) to collect data for testing the proposed hypothesized model. Before commencing data collection, participants received clear information regarding the study’s objectives and were assured that their responses would remain confidential and solely serve academic research purposes. Participation was entirely voluntary. Using a simple random sampling method, the study selected college students from three colleges in China to ensure that the sample accurately reflected the target population and optimized balance and diversity among the participants. These included a comprehensive university, a science and engineering university, and a normal university. This sampling strategy was adopted to ensure a balanced representation of different academic backgrounds and institutional types, thereby enhancing the generalizability and diversity of the sample. After removing invalid responses, questionnaires with completion times between 78 and 780 s were retained, resulting in a total of 846 valid responses included in the analysis. The participants included 404 males (47.75%) and 442 females (52.25%), with the majority aged between 18 and 22 years old, totaling 729 individuals (86.17%). The following illustrates the distribution of participants across academic years: 454 freshmen (53.66%), 209sophomores (24.70%), 86 juniors (10.17%), and 97 seniors (11.47%). Measurement instruments

Physical Self- Description Questionnaire (PSDQ)

The PSDQ was used to assess the PA status of college students56. This study selected two subscales, PA and sport competence, comprising a total of 12 items. For example, one item is, “I am good at most sports.” Items 2 and 3 were reverse-scored. This scale uses a 5-point Likert scoring system, where 1 means “strongly disagree” and 5 means “strongly agree.” Higher scores indicate greater perceived competence in PA. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the PSDQ was 0.953, indicating high reliability.

Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS)

The MSPSS was used to assess the PSS levels among college students57. It includes 12 items covering three dimensions: family, friends, and significant others. For instance, one item is, “I have friends with whom I can share my emotions.” This scale uses a 7-point scoring system, where 1 means “strongly disagree” and 7 means “strongly agree”. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the MSPSS was 0.952, indicating high reliability.

Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC)

The study employed the abbreviated CD-RISC to evaluate PR58. This scale measures an individual’s problem-solving abilities, positive thinking, emotional control, and resilience when faced with stress, changes, illness, or failure. It consists of 10 items rated on a Likert 5-point scale, where 1 represents “strongly disagree” and 5 represents “strongly agree”. Higher scores indicate greater PR. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the CD‐RISC was 0.941, indicating high reliability.

Self-esteem Scale (SES)

The SES was used to measure the SE of college students59.This scale comprises 7 items rated on a Likert 5-point scale, where “1” means “strongly disagree” and “5” means “strongly agree.” Higher total scores indicate higher levels of SE. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the CD-SES was 0.885, indicating high reliability.

The Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS)

The SWLS was used to assess the SW of college students60. This scale contains 5 items scored on a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 7 (“strongly agree”). Higher overall scores represent greater SW. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the SWLS was 0.882, indicating high reliability.

A translation and back-translation procedure was utilized in this study, aiming to maintain semantic consistency and cultural suitability in the questionnaire content61. The initial version of the translation was separately produced by two bilingual professionals, one specializing in psychology and the other in linguistics. A unified draft was developed by reconciling differences between the two versions through a comparative evaluation process. A third expert, who had not participated in the initial translation, conducted the back-translation. To address possible semantic inconsistencies, the back-translated text was carefully examined against the original version, and revisions were made to eliminate ambiguity or mismatched content. To examine whether the questionnaire items were clearly understood and culturally suitable, a preliminary test was administered to a sample of 40 college students in China. Based on their feedback, we revised specific wordings to improve comprehensibility. The finalized Chinese version was then subjected to exploratory factor analysis (EFA) using SPSS. Using principal axis factoring, the procedure involved extracting one predetermined factor, with the rotation option deliberately omitted. The results showed that all subscales demonstrated good factorability. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) values ranged from 0.775 to 0.874, and Bartlett’s tests of sphericity were all significant (p < 0.001). Most items had communalities above 0.50, and no items were removed. Although a few subscales showed slight tendencies toward a two-factor structure, the principal components of each subscale independently explained more than 50% of the total variance (SW: 53.46%; SE: 51.22%; PA: 63.80%; PR: 66.44%; PSS: 50.84%). These findings indicate that the questionnaire meets the requirements for structural validity, linguistic clarity, and cultural adaptation, making it appropriate for use in formal data collection.

Statistical processing

Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) was utilized as the primary analytical approach to evaluate the structural relationships and validate the proposed framework. PLS-SEM presents a range of significant benefits: first, it is a data analysis approach focused on prediction62; second, it proves effective with limited sample sizes and retains its robustness even when the data fails to conform to the normality assumption62; third, it effectively handles complex model structures63. Given these advantages, PLS-SEM was deemed suitable for this study. Compared to other approaches, covariance-based structural equation modeling (CB-SEM) is typically preferred in theory-driven studies featuring relatively simple model structures and data that closely follow a normal distribution. However, the model constructed in this study involves multiple mediating variables and latent pathways. The primary aim is to explore underlying mechanisms rather than to achieve optimal model fit. Preliminary tests of data normality also revealed that several variables exhibited skewed distributions. Given these considerations, PLS-SEM was better suited to the theoretical objectives and data characteristics of this study. Therefore, CB-SEM was not used for model analysis.

Structural model analysis

For the analysis of the structural model in this study, several indicators can be used, including tests for multicollinearity and significance testing of path coefficients. These indicators help assess the reliability and explanatory power of the model.

Consistent with the recommendations of Hair Jr, et al.62, a multicollinearity assessment was carried out to evaluate whether any such issues exist in the model. Based on the empirical guideline, the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) for each variable should stay below 3.362. As shown in Table 6, VIF values for all variables range between 1.000 and 2.152, suggesting that multicollinearity does not pose a problem in the model.

Mediation analysis

To examine the mediating influence of PR, PSS, and SE on the relationship between PA and SW, mediation analysis was carried out. A bootstrap-based mediation analysis was performed using PLS-SEM, as suggested by69. Table 8 summarizes the results of the mediation analysis. Initially, the direct effect of PA on SW was not significant (β = 0.070; t = 1.960; p > 0.05), and the indirect effect through PR was also not significant (β = 0.034; t = 1.474; p > 0.05), indicating that PR does not mediate this relationship. Conversely, the indirect effect of PA on SW through PSS was significant (β = 0.109; t = 6.599; p < 0.001), with a marginally significant direct effect (β = 0.070; t = 1.960; p > 0.05), suggesting a complementary partial mediation. Similarly, the indirect effect of PA on SW through SE was significant (β = 0.121; t = 5.252; p < 0.001), and the direct effect (β = 0.070; t = 1.960; p > 0.05) also indicates a complementary partial mediation. These results reveal the key mediating roles of PSS and SE between PA and SW, while PR does not serve as a mediator. These findings provide theoretical support and empirical evidence for enhancing SW through strengthened social support and SE.

Discussion

This study explored the associations between PA and SW among college students, with particular attention to the potential mediating roles of PSS, SE, and PR. The analysis, conducted using Smart PLS software, supported most of the study hypotheses. Specifically, findings indicated that PA significantly positively influences PSS, SE, and PR within college students. PSS and SE were positively associated with SW, while the direct effects of PA and PR on SW were not statistically significant. Mediation analysis further indicated that PSS and SE fully mediated the link between PA and SW, whereas PR did not exhibit a mediating role in this relationship.

PA did not have a significant impact on SW, which is inconsistent with the findings of KundziA and Grants10. Their research indicated that PA could alter the balance of brain chemicals, such as enhancing the release of endorphins related to positive emotions, thereby directly boosting an individual’s SW. The reasons for this inconsistency might include high employment pressure and academic burdens that college students face, which could have a greater impact on SW, overshadowing the positive effects of PA. Additionally, the diverse lifestyles of college students, including socializing and entertainment, might dilute the direct impact of PA on SW.

PA significantly positively impacted PSS, aligning with the findings of Li and Ren70, who confirmed that physical activities predict positive outcomes in PSS among 797 left-behind children in rural areas. This suggests that PA not only enhances physical fitness but also fosters positive interactions with peers and strengthens emotional connections within teams. This emotional resonance helps build deeper social support, thereby improving individuals’ perceptions of social support levels45.

PSS significantly positively affected SW, in line with the results of Gorgol, et al.71, who found that PSS significantly influenced SW among 1067 adults. In this study, PSS substantially enhanced SW among college students, possibly because social support provided an essential emotional outlet and psychological comfort, helping them face academic pressures and life challenges. Moreover, PSS not only offered emotional solace but also provided practical help, thus improving their life satisfaction and happiness71.

PSS significantly mediated the impact of PA on SW, and PSS significantly positively affected SW, in line with the results of Gorgol, et al.71, who found that PSS significantly influenced SW among 1067 adults. Zhang, et al.2, who found that PSS fully mediated the effect of PA on SW. In this study, college students, through engaging in PA, received more social support, which significantly enhanced their SW. The reasons for this result may include enhanced social interactions and teamwork through PA, not only improving individuals’ sense of social belonging but also enhancing their social support networks and thereby increasing their SW2. Additionally, PA provided opportunities for shared goals and collective activities, allowing students to build strong friendships and mutual support relationships, further enhancing their SW2.

PA significantly positively influenced PR, in line with the results of Cai, et al.9, which showed that PA significantly enhances individuals’ PR. In this study, college students significantly increased their PR through PA, likely because the activity required students to learn how to handle failures and setbacks competitively and to set and achieve personal goals. These experiences strengthened their courage to face and resolve difficulties, thereby enhancing their PR.

PR did not significantly affect SW, which is inconsistent with the findings of Dong-Quan72, who found that PR significantly impacts SW among college students. The discrepancy in findings may be due to little variability in the resilience levels among the sample, which might have precluded a full examination of the actual effect of PR on SW. For instance, Cohen73 noted that homogeneity in a sample’s resilience levels could limit the significance of the associations between variables. As a nuanced and layered psychological condition, SW has been linked to diverse influences across psychological processes, biological mechanisms, and social contexts74. Although PR is an important psychological resource, its effect might be overshadowed by other more potent or direct factors. It is also worth noting that the instrument used to assess PR in this study, the CD-RISC, was originally developed within a Western cultural context. While the measurement model assessment confirmed strong internal consistency and construct validity of the scale in the present sample, and its reliability and validity were thoroughly examined, potential cultural differences may nonetheless influence the scale’s sensitivity. Therefore, future research may further explore cross-cultural adaptation and localized revision of the scale to more comprehensively assess the mechanisms of PR among Chinese college students.

PR did not exhibit a significant mediating effect in the relationship between PA and SW among college students. This finding contrasts with the results reported by Cai, et al.9, whose study focused on college students under prolonged isolation during the COVID-19 pandemic. In that high-stress and uncertain context, PR was more likely to be activated as a coping resource. In contrast, the participants in the present study were Chinese college students living in a relatively stable environment, where academic and daily life conditions were more normalized. Under such circumstances, the adversity faced by students may have been limited, which could have restricted the activation and effectiveness of PR as a key adaptive resource, thereby weakening its role in enhancing SW. Moreover, the main sources of stress for college students today often stem from academic burden and concerns about future employment. These types of stress may be more effectively moderated by variables such as perceived stress or SE, rather than by deeper psychological recovery mechanisms. It is also possible that unmeasured confounding factors, such as socioeconomic status, physical health, or recent life events, may have masked the potential effects of resilience on SW. For instance, Diener et al. (2003) noted that socioeconomic status has a direct impact on SW, which may reduce the relative influence of other psychological traits, such as resilience.

PA significantly positively influenced SE, in line with the results of Jeong47, who observed significant effects of PA on SE among nursing students. In this study, college students significantly enhanced their SE through participation in physical activities. This might be because engaging in physical activities improves individuals’ physical condition, capabilities, and social status, typically leading to increased feelings of achievement and self-worth. This positive experience is a crucial factor in building SE47.

SE significantly positively influenced SW, in line with the results of Fei75, who explored the relationship between SE and SW among impoverished students, finding that SE significantly predicts SW. The findings indicate that elevated SE in college students is associated with higher levels of SW. This may reflect a greater tendency among individuals with strong SE to interpret life events more positively and experience pleasant emotions, which can lead to more frequent involvement in activities like artistic expression, sports, or other personally meaningful actions that serve as important sources of well-being75.

SE served as a significant mediator in the relationship between PA and SW, in line with the results of Zhang, et al.53, who found that individuals with higher SE are more likely to gain more positive experiences, such as pleasure and satisfaction, which further enhance the favorable influence of PA on SW. In this study, college students significantly enhanced their SE through participation in physical exercises, and this enhanced SE, in turn, raised their SW. This might be because engaging in physical activities can enhance individuals’ confidence, significantly improve their SE, and simultaneously affect their SW28.

Impact

This research examines the influence of PA on SW within college students, along with the multiple mediating roles of PSS, PR, and SE, revealing key factors and their interrelationships. The outcomes of this study contribute meaningfully to theoretical understanding and provide actionable guidance for real-world applications.

Theoretically, this research delves deeply into the factors influencing college students’ SW, including PA, PSS, PR, and SE. It thoroughly reveals the mechanisms behind these variables. Through examining the interplay among variables, this study emphasizes the potential mediating functions of PSS, SE, and PR in the relationship between PA and SW. This research contributes to existing scholarship on the association between PA and college students’ SW, while offering further insights into the various factors related to SW. These findings offer fresh theoretical perspectives by underscoring the relevance of PA to psychological health and addressing existing gaps in related theoretical work.

Practically, this study underscores the significant positive impact of PA on college students’ PSS and SE. College students should actively seek more opportunities and resources for PA, thereby enhancing their levels of SE and PSS, and subsequently, their life satisfaction. Additionally, for schools and educators, the findings highlight the key role of PA in enhancing students’ well-being, providing a scientific basis for formulating physical education policies. Specifically, schools may incorporate elements of mental health education into physical education curricula and draw on existing intervention studies to develop actionable design principles. For example, mindfulness training or emotion regulation exercises can be integrated with aerobic activities to help students manage stress more effectively. Group-based physical activities can be used to foster peer support and emotional communication. Additionally, course objectives may include modules aimed at enhancing PR and SE, thereby promoting both mental and physical development. Implementing these strategies is likely to strengthen the practical value of physical education, inform the design of evidence-based interventions, and promote the development of an educational atmosphere characterized by well-being, positivity, and mutual support.

Limitations and future research

Despite its contributions, the present study is subject to certain limitations that highlight directions for further investigation. First, the use of self-report questionnaires may introduce expectancy effects and subjective bias, potentially influencing the accuracy of the collected data. Subsequent investigations are encouraged to adopt more empirically grounded assessment approaches, including behavioral PA tracking and biological metrics, in order to obtain more accurate measurements and ensure greater methodological rigor. Second, the narrow demographic characteristics of the participants may limit the extent to which the findings can be generalized to broader populations. Given that the current sample consisted solely of Chinese college students, subsequent research could broaden its scope to include individuals from various cultural and regional backgrounds, thereby facilitating comparative analyses of how different factors relate to SW across cultures. Additionally, key covariates such as academic stress and socioeconomic status were not accounted for in the current analysis. These factors have been widely shown to significantly influence SW and may have introduced some degree of bias in the estimation of the mediating effects. Future studies are encouraged to include these critical covariates in the analytical model to improve the reliability and interpretive depth of the results. Furthermore, the sample in this study included a relatively high proportion of first-year students, while the response rate among senior students, particularly those in their final year who were engaged in internships or job-seeking activities, was comparatively low. This imbalance may have introduced potential bias, as students’ psychological states may vary across academic years. Future research is recommended to achieve a more balanced distribution of participants across different grade levels to improve the representativeness and generalizability of the results. Finally, the cross-sectional nature of this study constrains the ability to infer causal relationships. Future investigations may adopt a longitudinal approach to examine how PA, PR, SE, social support, and SW interact and evolve over time.

Conclusion

This study examines the factors influencing SW among college students and the mediating roles of PSS, PR, and SE between PA and SW. The results show that in the college student population, PA significantly positively affects PSS, SE, and PR. Both PSS and SE significantly positively influence SW. However, the impacts of PA and PR on SW are not significant. PSS and SE fully mediate the relationship between PA and SW, while PR does not act as a mediator. This study provides a theoretical foundation for enhancing the SW of college students and offers references for the practice of college mental health education.
