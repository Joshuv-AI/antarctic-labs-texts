Rab26 promotes hypoxia-induced hyperproliferation of PASMCs by modulating the AT1R-STAT3-YAP axis
Authors: Zaichun You, Qiuhong Huang, Li Zeng, Xin Tian, Wen Zhang, Dongqin Wei, Juan Fu, Liling Xu, Li Ren, Yan Zhao, Hang Qian, Guansong Wang, Xunmei Zhou, Binfeng He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe hyper-proliferation of pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) significantly contributes to pulmonary vascular remodeling (PVR) in chronic hypoxic pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), leading to right ventricular dysfunction and increased mortality. Despite the critical role of this process, the mechanisms underlying hypoxia-induced PASMCs hyper-proliferation remain largely unexplored. In this study, hypoxia was found to upregulate Rab26 expression in rat PASMCs (rPASMCs) and lung tissue in a PAH mouse model. Silencing Rab26 reduced rPASMCs hyper-proliferation. Rab26 interacted with angiotensin II Type 1 receptor (AT1R) and modulated its transport to the cell surface, thereby activating Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3), upregulating Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1) expression, and promoting YAP1 nuclear translocation. Moreover, hypoxia enhanced the interaction between YAP and STAT3, with STAT3 also facilitating YAP nuclear translocation. Rab26 deficiency downregulates the levels of pSTAT3 and YAP1 in lung tissue and smooth muscle arteries and attenuated the hypoxia-induced PVR. Rab26 deficiency results in decreased expression of pSTAT3 and YAP1 in lung tissue and pulmonary smooth muscle arteries, while also attenuating hypoxia-induced PVR. Furthermore, pharmacological inhibition of AT1R and pSTAT3 further reduces pSTAT3 and YAP1 expression both in wild-type (WT) and Rab26−/− PAH mice. In summary, our findings demonstrate that Rab26 facilitates PASMC hyper-proliferation and pulmonary vascular remodeling by modulating AT1R transport and activating the downstream STAT3-YAP pathway under hypoxic conditions. These results suggest that targeting Rab26 may be a promising therapeutic strategy for PVR and PAH.Graphical abstract Keywords: Rab26, AT1R, STAT3, YAP1, PVR

Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a severe, life-threatening disease, which progressive increased pulmonary artery remodeling and pulmonary vascular resistance (mean pulmonary artery pressure >20 mm Hg, a wedged PAP (PAWP) ≤ 15 mmHg and a PVR ≥ 2 Wood units), resulting in irreversible right heart failure and even death [1]. Considered a characteristic of PH, PVR involves excessive proliferation of PASMCs [2], dysfunction of the endothelial barrier [3], increased extracellular matrix deposition [4], and immune-inflammatory disruption [5]. Badesch DB and colleagues revealed that the incidence of PH is 2.0 cases per 1 million per year and a prevalence of 10.6 cases per 1 million adults [6]. Exposure of pulmonary vessels to long-term hypoxia is considered a major cause of pulmonary artery remodeling [7, 8]. In addition to high-altitude exposure, chronic lung diseases (CLD) such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), interstitial lung disease (ILD), and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) contribute significantly to hypoxia in pulmonary blood vessels [7, 9]. Research conducted by Abby D. Poms and colleagues revealed that CLD-associated PH leads to a worse outcome than that in idiopathic PAH (group I PH) [10]. Apart from long-term oxygen treatment (LTOT), there are no effective therapies for CLD-PH. Therefore, it is imperative to further investigate the novel mechanism of hypoxia-induced PH to explore potential therapeutic approaches.

The AT1R is a critical G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) in the Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) [11]. Increasing evidence demonstrates that hyperactivation of AT1R promotes excessive proliferation and migration of PASMCs, leading to abnormal vascular remodeling in PH [12–15]. Further studies have demonstrated that the function of AT1R depends on its cellular sub-localization, specifically its presence on the surface of vascular SMCs [16–18]. Previous research has shown that hypoxia increases the expression of AT1R on the cell surface of rPASMCs, triggering phenotypic modulation through downstream STAT3 signaling and leading to the hyperproliferation of rPASMCs [17]. However, hypoxia has been found not to impact the total expression of AT1R in the heart and blood vessels [19]. Additionally, another study revealed that the activation of AT1R by TRV023, a β-arrestin–biased AT1R agonist, stimulates the migration and proliferation of PASMCs by modulating the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway [20]. Nevertheless, there are still substantial gaps in our understanding of the regulation of AT1R at the cell surface and the AT1R-mediated signaling pathway for PVR.

Rab Guanosine Triphosphatase (GTPase) belongs to the small GTPase protein family of the Ras superfamily, and more than 60 Rab GTPases are localized to various intracellular membranes, serving to regulate cargo trafficking between organelles in eukaryotic cells [21]. Dysfunction of Rab GTPases has been linked to abnormal physiological processes and diseases such as cancer [22], neurodegenerative diseases [23], immune disorders [24], and PVR [17]. Recent studies have highlighted the involvement of Rab26 in the post-Golgi transport of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) [25], playing critical roles in regulating disease progression. Specifically, Rab26 deficiency has been found to promote insulin secretion [26], induce endothelial barrier hyperpermeability [27], impair phagocytosis and apoptosis of macrophages [28], and enhance airway inflammation responses induced by cigarette smoking [29]. Moreover, Rab26 has been shown to promote the proliferation of lung cancer [30] and mediate cisplatin resistance [31]. However, the role of Rab26 in hypoxia-induced excessive proliferation of PASMCs and PVR remains unknown.

In this study, we demonstrated that Rab26 was upregulated in hypoxia-induced animal models of PAH and in rPASMCs. Knockout or silencing of Rab26 protected against hypoxia-induced PVR in vivo and in vitro. Additionally, we found that Rab26 mediated the transport of AT1R from the cytoplasm to the cell surface. Moreover, we established that AT1R-mediated STAT3-YAP1 signaling played a critical role in the pathobiological mechanisms that regulate the excessive proliferation of PASMCs and subsequent PVR in PAH. In conclusion, these findings support the potential of Rab26 as a therapeutic target for the treatment of PAH.

Material & method

Material and reagent

Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) (#11965092), Lipofectamine™ 3000 (#L3000015), and Opti-MEM™ I medium (#31985070) were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. Fetal bovine serum (FBS) (#Z7010FBS-500) was acquired from ZETA LIFE Inc. Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (#11202ES08), Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (#40203ES80) and 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EDU) Imaging Kit (#40275ES60) were purchased from Yeasen Biotechnology. 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was obtained from Sangon Biotech (#E607303-0002). Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (#HY-K0202A), Sugen 5416(#HY-10374), Losartan (#HY-17512) and Stattic (#HY-13818) were purchased from MedChemExpress (MCE). Angiotensin II (Ang II) (#T7040) was obtained from TargetMol Chemicals Inc. RNAiso Plus (TRIzol) regents (#T9108) and Prime Script™ RT reagent Kit (#RR037A) was purchased from Takara Biomedical Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd.

The antibodies used were as follows: rabbit anti-AT1R (#25343-1-AP), Rab26 polyclonal antibody (#14284-1-AP), mouse anti-GOLGA2 antibody (#66662-1-Ig), mouse anti-DYKDDDDK-tag (Flag-tag) (66008-4-Ig), rabbit anti-Na-K-ATPase (#14418-1-AP) were purchased from Proteintech Group, Inc. Rabbit anti-YAP1 (#14074), rabbit anti-STAT3 (#12640), rabbit anti-phospho-STAT3 (pSTAT3)(Tyr705) (#9145), rabbit anti-HA-tag (#3724), rabbit anti-Flag-tag (#14793), rabbit anti-PCNA (#13110), Anti-rabbit IgG (#7074), Anti-α-SMA (#19245) was obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (CST), Inc. Rabbit anti-β-actin (#ab7817) was purchased from Abcam. lnc.

Cell culture

Rat PASMCs (rPASMCs) was harvested according to our previous study [17]. rPASMCs were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS. When the cells grew to 90% confluence, trypsinization and counting were performed before each experiment.

Bioinformatics analysis

The bioinformatics analysis was based on the NCBI GEO database (GSE15197), which 13 healthy lung tissue (control) and 18 lung tissue with PAH patients. These transcripts in this database were used to construct the co-expression network by using the “WGCNA” R package, and identification of key modules associated with PAH occurrence, the correlation between the Module eigengene (ME) and the phenotypic traits (PAH occurrence) was calculated by using the Pearson correlation coefficient to assess the relationship between each gene co-expression module and PAH occurrence. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis were performed by the “clusterProfiler” R package [32].

Transfection of SiRNA and plasmid

Rab26 siRNA (sense: 5’-UUAUUCCGGAAGUCGAUGCTT, antisense: GCAUCGACUUCCGGAAUAATT-3’), STAT3 siRNA (sense: 5’-GCGUGUGCAGGAUCUAGAATT, antisense: UUCUAGAUCCUGCACACGCTT-3’) and Negative control (NC) (sense:5’- UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3’, antisense: 5’-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3’) was from GenePharma (Shanghai, China).

The plasmids, Rab26-HA and AT1R-GFP, was gifted from prof. Guangyu Wu (Department of Pharmacology and Toxicology, Georgia Regents University). The plasmid Flag-YAP1 was obtained from YouBio Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd.

siRNA or plasmids were diluted in Opti-MEM (in the absence of antibiotics or fungicides) and transfected into rPASMCs cells using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent, respectively. The medium was replaced, and fresh complete DMEM was added to the cell dishes to continue cell culture.

Hypoxia exposure of rPASMCs

The hypoxic culture conditions were established by exposing the cells to 1% O2 using an oxygen-regulated, airtight hypoxic chamber incubator (Thermo, USA). rPASMCs were serum-starved for 24 h in DMEM containing only 0.5% FBS, after which they were incubated under hypoxic conditions for indicated time-points.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

The vability of rPASMCs was determined using the CCK-8 assay, following the manufacturer’s instructions. rPASMCs cells were cultured in 96-well plates, 5 replica Wells in each experimental group and each well (0.5 × 104 cells), incubate under specified conditions. Then, mix 10 µL of CCK-8 solution with 100 µL of DMEM complete medium, then add it to a 96-well plate and incubate it at 37 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, the optical density (OD) value at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Bio-Tek instruments, MOX200, USA). Finally, statistical analysis and graph generation were performed with GraphPad Prism 9.0 software.

EdU cell proliferation assay

The cell proliferation assay was performed using the EdU assay, following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, 2 × 105 cells were seeded in 12-well plates and incubated overnight under the specified conditions, followed by the addition of EdU reagent for a 2-hour incubation period. Subsequently, the samples were fixed with a 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sangon, Shanghai) for an additional 15 min and washed 1 to 2 times with a cleaning solution. Following this, the samples were incubated with the click-reaction reagent for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Hoechst 33,342 reagent was again used to counterstain the nuclei. The stained samples were examined using a LEICA DMI8 fluorescence microscope system, and the data were captured with LASX software. Image J was utilized to process the images, including overlapping the photographs taken in the two fluorescence channels and counting the number of cells. Finally, GraphPad Prism 9.0 was employed for graphical representation.

Immunofluorescence

To evaluate the cellular colocalization of Rab26 and AT1R, rPASMCs were plated on glass-bottom imaging dishes and transiently co-transfected with plasmids HA-Rab26 and AT1R-GFP for 24 h using Lipofectamine 3000. Following transfection, cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and incubated with HA-tag antibody and secondary antibody. DAPI was utilized to stain the nuclei. The representative immunofluorescent images were captured using a Zeiss LSM710 confocal microscope.

For subcellular localization analysis of AT1R or YAP1, fixed rPASMCs (4% PFA) were incubated with primary antibodies (rabbit anti-AT1R, anti-YAP1, or mouse anti-GOLGA2) followed by species-matched secondary antibodies. To analyze YAP1-pSTAT3 co-localization, rPASMCs were transfected with flag-YAP1 plasmid for 24 h. Fixed cells were dually stained with mouse anti-Flag and rabbit anti-pSTAT3 antibodies.

Predicting protein-protein interactions

The interaction between Rab26 and AT1R, STAT3 and YAP1 was predicted using the molecular docking method, as documented in previous studies [33]. The prediction results were visualized using the PyMol tool (http://pymol.org). Moreover, the interaction between STAT3 and YAP1 was also forecasted, and the prediction results were displayed using online tools available on the String website (http://string-db.org).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and western blotting

The plasmids Rab26-HA or Flag-YAP1 were co-transfected into rPASMCs cells using lipofectamine 3000, respectively. After 24 h of transfection, the cells were washed with cold PBS and then lysed using cell lysis buffer. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000g for 15 min at 4 °C and collect protein. The Protein A/G Magnetic Beads were incubated with HA-tag or Flag-tag antibody for 2 h at 4 °C and then washed. 400 ng of total protein was added to the mixture and incubated overnight at 4 °C in a refrigerator. The next day, the beads were washed four times with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (PBST, pH 7.4) (Sangon Biotech, #A100777-0500) and collected, then samples were detected using western blot.

Cells and tissues were lysed using lysis buffer consisting of RIPA (Beyotime, #P0013B), a protease inhibitor mixture (Beyotime, #P1046-1), a phosphatase inhibitor mixture (Beyotime, #P1046-2), 0.05 M EDTA, pH 8.0 (Beyotime, #P1046-3) and centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 15 min at 4 ℃. Protein samples were subjected to PAGE gel (EpiZyme, #PG113) electrophoresis and transferred to PVDF membranes (Cytiva, #10600023). The membranes were blocked with 5% no-fat milk (BD, #232100), incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 ℃, and then incubated with secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Bands were visualized with ECL chemiluminescent substrate (Biosharp, #BL523B) by the ChemiScopeS6 SE imaging system (CLINX, China). Densitometric analysis was conducted using Image Lab software.

In addition, the membrane protein, nuclear protein, and total protein were isolated using the Mem-PER Plus Membrane Protein Extraction Kit (Thermo Scientific, #89842) and the Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime, # P0027).

RT-qPCR analysis of gene expression

The RNA from treated rPASMCs was isolated using TRIzol reagents and then reverse transcribed into cDNA using the Prime Script™ RT reagent Kit. Subsequently, qPCR was performed using Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix on the BIO-RAD CFX96 platform, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The 2−ΔΔCt method was employed to determine the relative expression of the target gene. The primer sequences are as follows: Rab26 forward: 5’-TGTGGATGGCATGAAGGTGAAGC-3’, reverse: 5’-TTGGTGATGTCGTAGAGCAGCAAC-3’. β-actin direct purchase from Sangon Biotech (#B662302).

Chronic hypoxia induced pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) mice model

Mice wild-type (WT) littermate mice (C57BL/6J) were purchased from Spiff Biosciences (Beijing, China). Rab26 knockout (KO) mouse (Rab26−/−) on a C57/BL6J background were generated according to our previous study [27, 34]. The animal experiments were approved and carried out in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Army Medical University (AMUWEC20188101).

8–10 weeks male wild-type (WT) and Rab26−/− mice, were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5 per genotype per group, respectively). Hypoxia/Su group: WT and Rab26−/− mice were placed in a ventilated chamber (Tawang Technology, #Ox-100-M) under hypoxic conditions (10% ± 0.5% O2) for 5 weeks. These mice were also injected subcutaneously with SU5416 at a dose of 20 mg/kg body weight once weekly [35]. Hypoxia/Su/Losartan group: WT and Rab26−/− mice underwent identical treatment protocols as the Hypoxia/Su group, with Losartan supplemented via drinking water at a final concentration of 0.6 g/L. To prevent drug accumulation or degradation over time, fresh Losartan solutions were prepared twice weekly [36, 37]; Hypoxia/Su/Static group: WT and Rab26−/− mice were received treatment identical to the Hypoxia/Su group but additionally administered Static at a dose of 10 mg/kg body weight pre doses, three times weekly for the full duration of the hypoxic intervention [38]. Normoxia group: WT and Rab26−/− mice were maintained under standard room air conditions throughout the experiment.

Echocardiography was performed using the Vevo 2100 high-resolution imaging system (VisualSonics, Toronto, Canada) five weeks after hypoxia induction [39]. The pulmonary acceleration time (PAT) and the ratio of PAT to ejection time (ET) have been recorded. Subsequently, the mice were sacrificed, and lung and heart tissues were used for H&E staining, IHC, and western blotting as well as heart tissues were used to evaluate Fulton index.

The heart was collected and thoroughly rinsed with 1 mL PBS to remove residual blood. After removing both atria, surface liquid was blotted with filter paper before recording the total heart weight. The ventricles were then dissected into right ventricle (RV) and left ventricle plus interventricular septum (LV + S), which were weighed separately. The right ventricular hypertrophy index (RVHI) was calculated as follows [40]: RVHI (%) = [RV/(LV + S)] × 100.

Pulmonary vascular tunica media was marked through IHC labeling with anti-α-SMA. Subsequently, images were captured using an EasyScanner scanner and analyzed with the MoticDSM v3.0.6.0 system. For each mouse, 8–10 vessels were analyzed, and the percentage of medial thickness (% MT) was calculated as the ratio of the difference between the external and internal diameters to the external diameter, multiplied by 100, as follows: %MT = (External Diameter - Internal Diameter)/External Diameter × 100.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and the experiments were carried out with at least three replicas. Statistical analysis involved use of a two-tailed Student’s t-test for experiments with two groups, and significant differences were assessed using one-way analysis of variance for experiments with more than two groups. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Rab26 was upregulated in rPASMCs induced by hypoxia and in experimental rodent PAH models

To investigate the potential role of Rab26 in pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), we first analyzed its expression in lung tissues from PAH patients and healthy controls using bioinformatics approaches. Significantly higher Rab26 levels were observed in PAH samples compared to controls (p < 0.05, Fig. 1A). Next, we performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to identify key modules associated with PAH pathogenesis (Fig. 1B). Rab26 was clustered within the red module, which exhibited a strong positive correlation with PAH occurrence (r = 0.65, p < 0.05) (Fig. 1C). These findings collectively suggest that Rab26 may play a functional role in the development of PAH.

We investigated the expression of Rab26 in rat rPASMCs under hypoxic conditions, rPASMCs were exposed to 1% O2 for 48 h. The data showed that hypoxia induced hyper-proliferation of rPASMCs (Figs. 1D and E). The expression levels of Rab26 mRNA and protein were increased in the hypoxia group compared to the normoxia group (p < 0.05) in a time-dependent manner (Figs. 1F and G). Furthermore, PAH mice models were established and confirmed in this study (Fig. 1H). Rab26 expression was significantly upregulated in the lung vascular and lung tissue of the PAH mouse model (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1J and I). These findings suggest that Rab26 may play a role in hypoxia-induced hyper-proliferation of rPASMCs.

Silencing Rab26 attenuates hyper-proliferation of rPASMCs induced by hypoxia

To evaluate the role of Rab26 in hypoxia-induced hyper-proliferation of rPASMCs, Rab26 siRNA was transfected into rPASMCs. The data showed that the levels of Rab26 and PCNA were notably reduced after transfecting rPASMCs with Rab26 siRNA for 48 h compared to the NC group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2A and B). As illustrated in Fig. 2C and D, silencing Rab26 suppressed the proliferation of rPASMCs under normoxic conditions. Similarly, Rab26 knockdown attenuated hypoxia-induced hyper-proliferation of rPASMCs (Fig. 2E and F). Following transfection of rPASMCs with Rab26-HA plasmid for overexpression, we observed that Rab26 overexpression significantly enhanced cellular proliferation and upregulated PCNA expression in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 2G-I). Collectively, these data indicate that Rab26 critically regulates hypoxia-induced hyper-proliferation in rPASMCs.

Rab26 regulates the cell surface transport and subcellular localization of AT1R

As a member of the small G protein superfamily, Rab26 modulates GPCR transport from the Golgi to the cytoplasm [41]. In our study, we investigated whether Rab26 regulates the levels of AT1R at the surface of rPASMCs, as AT1R serves as a key receptor for PASMC proliferation following extracellular stimulation, such as hypoxia and Ang II17. The data illustrated that hypoxia up-regulated AT1R protein levels on the cell surface in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 3A). Silencing Rab26 decreased AT1R protein levels at the cell surface (Fig. 3B and C). In contrast, overexpression of Rab26 increased AT1R protein levels at the cell surface (Fig. 3D). These findings suggested that Rab26 might modulate the transport of AT1R to the cell surface. Next, we predicted protein-protein interactions and found that Rab26 interacts with AT1R (Fig. 3E). This was confirmed by co-IP assays (Fig. 3F) and immunostaining for co-localization of Rab26 and AT1R (Fig. 3G). Furthermore, the down-regulation of Rab26 mediated AT1R acculturation in Golgi body (Fig. 3H). These results demonstrate that Rab26 regulates the trafficking of AT1R from the Golgi apparatus to the cell surface.

Rab26 promoted the proliferation of rPASMCs through AT1R activation

Based on the above results, we speculated that Rab26 may promote the proliferation of rPASMCs through the AT1R signaling pathway. To confirm this hypothesis, we evaluated the proliferation ability of rPASMCs following Rab26 siRNA treatment in the presence of either an AT1R agonist or antagonist. The data showed that silencing Rab26, in the absence or presence of Losartan (an AT1R antagonist), suppressed the proliferation of rPASMCs; the inhibitory effect in these groups was stronger than that of the Losartan-only treatment group (Fig. 4A-D). Similarly, results shown that rPASMCs exposed to hypoxic conditions exhibited comparable trends (Fig. 4E-H). Furthermore, the knockdown of Rab26 partially blocked the proliferation of rPASMCs induced by the AT1R agonist Ang II (Figs. 4I-J). These data demonstrate that Rab26 promotes the proliferation of rPASMCs through the AT1R signaling pathway.

Rab26 promotes YAP1 expression and nuclear translocation by AT1R activation

Increasing evidence indicates that YAP1 plays a crucial role in hypoxia-induced proliferation of rPASMCs through YAP1 nuclear translocation [42, 43]. Furthermore, Ang II also induces YAP1 nuclear translocation in vascular endothelium [44]. Thus, we investigated whether Rab26 may promote YAP1 expression and nuclear translocation dependent on AT1R activation. KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment of gene sets associated with the Hippo signaling pathway and JAK-STAT signaling pathway in the PAH group (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, Rab26 was specifically enriched within these two pathways (Fig. 5B).

The data demonstrated that the levels of total and nuclear YAP1 protein were increased in rPASMCs exposed to hypoxic conditions (Fig. 5C and D). Overexpression of Rab26 significantly upregulated total and nuclear YAP1 protein levels (Fig. 5E), whereas Rab26 knockdown markedly decreased YAP1 protein abundance in both total and nuclear fractions (Fig. 5F). Inhibition of AT1R by Losartan and Rab26 silencing partially blocked hypoxia-induced YAP1 expression in rPASMCs (Figs. 5G and H). Furthermore, we found that Ang II induced YAP1 expression and knocking down Rab26 diminished this effect on YAP1 (Fig. 5I). These data suggest that Rab26 promotes YAP1 expression and nuclear translocation by activating the AT1R signaling pathway.

Rab26-mediated promotion of YAP1 nuclear translocation requires STAT3 activation

Previous studies have shown that YAP1 nuclear translocation is essential for STAT3 signaling [45]. Therefore, we investigated whether STAT3 is involved in Rab26-AT1R axis-mediated YAP1 nuclear translocation in rPASMCs. First, we observed that hypoxia induces STAT3 activation (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, Rab26 silencing partially inhibited STAT3 activation under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions (Fig. 6B and C), as well as during Ang II-induced AT1R stimulation (Fig. 6D). Additionally, antagonism of AT1R activation by Losartan also inactivated STAT3 under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, we found that YAP1 may interact with STAT3 based on predictions from protein-protein interaction analyses (Figs. 6F and G) and confirmed this using co-IP assays and immunostaining (Figs. 6H). The co-localization regions and fluorescence intensity of YAP1 and phosphorylated STAT3 (pSTAT3) increased after PASMCs were exposed to hypoxic conditions (Fig. 6I). Silencing STAT3 reduces YAP1 levels in the nucleus after PASMCs are exposed to hypoxic conditions (Fig. 6J and K). These findings demonstrate that Rab26 facilitates YAP1 nuclear translocation, which in turn is essential for STAT3 activation mediated by the AT1R signaling pathway.

Rab26 deficiency attenuates hypoxia-induced pulmonary vascular remodeling

The above findings demonstrate that Rab26 critically regulates hypoxia-induced rPASMCs. To further investigate its role in disease progression, we examined the impact of Rab26 deficiency on hypoxia-induced PVR and PAH in a murine model. The data demonstrate that Rab26 deficiency attenuates PVR (Fig. 7A) and decreases the MT% and RVHI compared to the WT model group (Figs. 7B and C). Pulsed-wave Doppler imaging was performed to assess pulmonary artery flow in all animals, with high-quality images enabling consistent measurement of RV systolic time intervals (Fig. 7D). Under normoxic conditions, both WT and Rab26−/− mice exhibited symmetrical pulmonary systolic flow patterns. In contrast, hypoxia-induced PAH led to a distinct leftward shift of the Doppler flow peak toward early systole. Quantitative analysis revealed that the PAT/ET ratio was significantly lower in hypoxic mice of both genotypes compared to normoxic controls (p < 0.05). Notably, Rab26−/− PAH mice showed a partial restoration of the PAT/ET ratio relative to WT PAH mice (Fig. 7E). The levels of pSTAT3 and YAP1 were elevated in the lung tissue of the PAH mouse model (Fig. 7F), and Rab26 deficiency partially inactivated STAT3 and reduced YAP1 expression in the lung tissue and pulmonary vascular of PAH mouse model (Figs. 7G, H and I). Furthermore, pharmacological inhibition of AT1R and pSTAT3 further attenuated pSTAT3 and YAP1 expression in PAH of both WT and Rab26−/−mice (Fig. 7J). Collectively, these results demonstrate that Rab26 drives hypoxia-induced PVR and PAH progression by activating the AT1R-STAT3-YAP1 signaling axis.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that hypoxia upregulates Rab26 expression, which facilitates AT1R trafficking to the plasma membrane. This process activates STAT3 and promotes its interaction with YAP1, ultimately driving YAP1 nuclear translocation and inducing hyper-proliferation of rPASMCs. Furthermore, Rab26 deficiency downregulates pSTAT3 and YAP1 levels in lung tissue and pulmonary arteries, thereby attenuating hypoxia-induced PVR and PAH.

An increasing number of studies have focused on the significant role of Rab GTPases in regulating the function of vascular smooth muscle cells. A previous study demonstrated that Rab1 modulates AT1R transport to the cell surface, thereby regulating the phenotypic switching of rPASMCs and proliferation under hypoxic conditions [17]. Similarly, Rab5a and Rab7 also play important roles in promoting the proliferation of smooth muscle cells [46, 47]. Our results indicate that hypoxia induces the expression of Rab26 in vitro, and silencing Rab26 attenuates hypoxia-induced hyper-proliferation by reducing AT1R levels at the cell surface, followed by the inactivation of STAT3 and a reduction in YAP1 nuclear translocation. Consequently, Rab26 deficiency mitigates hypoxia induced PVR and PAH in mice. These data suggest a fundamental pro-proliferation role for physiological levels of Rab26 in rPASMCs.

Rab26 is a member of the small GTPase protein family, and its primary role is to modulate target proteins, such as GPCRs, during post-Golgi transport [25, 41]. Li C and colleagues found that Rab26 directly interacts with α2-adrenergic receptors and transports them to the cell surface from the Golgi [41]. Consistent with this report, we also found that Rab26 directly interacts with AT1R and modulates its post-Golgi transport to the cell surface under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions, facilitating the activation of STAT3 and YAP1 nuclear translocation. This suggests that AT1R may be a downstream effector of Rab2625,41. Given that Rab GTPases coordinates vesicular transport through their GTP/GDP exchange cycles [21, 48], we speculated that the GTP/GDP cycle of Rab26-coordinated transport vesicles plays an essential role in controlling the transport of AT1R to the cell surface [41]. Furthermore, our previous study revealed that the upregulation of Rab1 enhances nascent AT1R transport from the endoplasmic reticulum to the Golgi when rPASMCs are exposed to hypoxic conditions [17]. These data indicate that the multistep intercellular trafficking of plasma membrane proteins involves several Rab GTPase proteins [49] and that targeting multiple Rab GTPase proteins may be a more effective approach to control the levels of plasma membrane proteins at the cell surface and their downstream signaling in target cells under pathological conditions, thereby contributing to the treatment of diseases such as PAH.

Another important finding presented in this manuscript is that Rab26-mediated AT1R activation promotes YAP1 nuclear translocation, leading to the hyper- proliferation of rPASMCs, which requires STAT3 activation and its interaction with YAP1. YAP1, a member of the Hippo pathway, serves as a critical transcriptional regulator when localized in the nucleus, controlling the proliferation of PASMCs both in vitro and in vivo [50–53]. This suggests that YAP1 nuclear translocation is a critical event in hypoxia-induced hyper-proliferation of rPASMCs. Several studies have shown that YAP1 nuclear translocation can be promoted by force transmission [54], PDLIM7 [55], IFN-γ [56], and circular RNA circHelz [57]. Our study demonstrates that YAP1 nuclear translocation is facilitated by STAT3 activation through AT1R. Recent research has revealed a potential reciprocal regulatory relationship between YAP1 and STAT3. On one hand, STAT3 is required for YAP1 nuclear translocation [45], which is consistent with our findings. On the other hand, YAP1 can enhance IL-6 production, thereby activating STAT3, which ultimately facilitates the proliferation of cancer cells [58, 59]. Therefore, we speculate that nuclear YAP may promote IL-6 expression following STAT3 activation, which in turn promotes YAP1 nuclear translocation and IL-6 production, establishing a positive regulatory feedback loop between STAT3 and YAP1.

This study has several limitations. First, our findings require validation in Rab26 knockout mice with a specific knockout in vascular smooth muscle. Second, the novel mechanisms by which AT1R regulates STAT3 activation must be investigated. Third, further research is necessary to elucidate how YAP1 functions in regulating the proliferation of rPASMCs in the nucleus.

Conclusions

In summary, hypoxia induces the expression of Rab26, which facilitates the transport of AT1R to the cell surface, thereby enhancing AT1R-mediated YAP nuclear translocation that requires interaction with STAT3. This process leads to hypoxia induced hyper-proliferation of rPASMCs and PVR. Therefore, silencing Rab26 may represent a novel approach for PAH therapy.
