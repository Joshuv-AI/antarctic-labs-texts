USB-Rec: An Effective Framework for Improving Conversational Recommendation Capability of Large Language Model CCS: Information systems Recommender systems
Jianyu Wen, Jingyun Wang, Cilin Yan, Jiayin Cai, Xiaolong Jiang, Ying Zhang

Abstract
. Recently, Large Language Models (LLMs) have been widely employed in Conversational Recommender Systems (CRSs). Unlike traditional language model approaches that focus on training, all existing LLMs-based approaches are mainly centered around how to leverage the summarization and analysis capabilities of LLMs while ignoring the issue of training. Therefore, in this work, we propose an integrated training-inference framework, User-Simulator-Based framework (USB-Rec), for improving the performance of LLMs in conversational recommendation at the model level. Firstly, we design a LLM-based Preference Optimization (PO) dataset construction strategy for RL training, which helps the LLMs understand the strategies and methods in conversational recommendation. Secondly, we propose a Self-Enhancement Strategy (SES) at the inference stage to further exploit the conversational recommendation potential obtained from RL training. Extensive experiments on various datasets demonstrate that our method consistently outperforms previous state-of-the-art methods. Codes are available at https://github.com/John-Wendell/USB_Rec .



1.
Introduction

Conversational recommendation aims to achieve users’ desired outcomes from continuous interaction between users and CRSs.
The CRS
(
Li et al. 2018
;
Sun and Zhang 2018
;
Zhou et al. 2020b
;
Jannach et al. 2021
)
continuously adjusts the recommended content according to users’ feedback, thus eliciting users’ preferences and making more accurate recommendations.
However, conventional CRSs
(
Wang et al. 2023b
;
Bang et al. 2023
;
Qin et al. 2023
)
are prone to overfitting the training set, which consequently leads to suboptimal performance on large industrial datasets.

Over the past few years, Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated general applicability across various domains
(
Abbasiantaeb et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. [n. d.]
;
Dubey et al. 2024
;
GLM et al. 2024
)
.
Recent works
(
Zhao et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024b
;
Wu et al. 2024
)
exploit LLMs as the core component of CRSs.
These methods predominantly focus on developing intricate pipelines or sophisticated prompts to optimize response quality of LLMs.
And, current research efforts have neglected training-centric approaches for enhancing LLMs intrinsic capabilities in conversational recommendation at the model level.
Endowing LLMs with intrinsic conversational recommendation capability at the model level could eliminate lots of complex pipelines and make the CRSs more streamlined and efficient.
However, fine-tuning LLMs faces substantial technical hurdles.
Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) methods heavily rely on the training datasets, which is prone to induce overfitting for LLMs.
In addition, conversational inconsistencies among different recommenders during data collection introduce significant noise, which adversely affects the model’s learning efficacy.
While Reinforcement Learning (RL) strategies enhance LLMs generalization and ensure stylistic consistency in responses, they relies on a costly and time-consuming manual scoring process.

In this work, we propose a User-Simulator-Based framework (USB-Rec) to exploit LLMs’ conversational recommendation ability through synergistic integration of model-intrinsic optimization during both training and inference stages.
Firstly, we propose an LLM-based Preference Optimization dataset construction method for RL training.
We adopt a simulated user to evaluate the responses generated by LLM and subsequently replace the original labels with those of higher scores, thus constructing a preference pair dataset for further RL training.
After training with RL based on the preference pair dataset, the output distribution of LLM tends to converge closer to the target than that achieved with SFT.
Consequently, we propose SES at the inference stage to find the most appropriate response.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

(1) We design an automatic and LLM-based preference data construction strategy for RL training, which reduces the noise by using a user simulator to score the generated conversation.

(2) We propose a Self-Enhancement Strategy (SES) at the inference stage to further exploit the conversational recommendation potential obtained from RL stage.

(3) Extensive experiments on various datasets demonstrate that our method consistently outperforms previous state-of-the-art methods.

3.
Methodology

In this section, we propose a User-Simulator-Based framework (USB-Rec) for LLM-based conversational recommendation.
Firstly, we design a Preference Optimization dataset collection strategy (Sec.
3.1
), which leverages LLM-based simulated user agents to score generated responses to realize human-free and efficient RL training.
Secondly, we propose a Self-Enhancement Strategy (SES) at the inference stage (Sec.
3.2
) to further exploit the conversational recommendation potential obtained from the RL stage.

3.1.
PO Dataset Construction Strategy (PODCS)

We propose a method for scoring the responses of a recommender system by a user model to construct a high-quality preference dataset, to be used for RL optimizing LLMs.

LLM-based User Simulation

Following iEvaLM
(
Wang et al. 2023b
)
, we employ an LLM as a user simulator, which assigns scores to the generated recommendations.
Provided with labels, the user simulator engages in simulated conversations with the recommender system, utilizing sampled conversations from the dataset as seeds.
After a predetermined number of conversational rounds, the user simulator assigns a score to the recommender system as follows:

(1)

s=\left\{\begin{aligned} &2,\,\text{prediction}\geq\text{label}\\
&1,\,\text{prediction}\approx\text{label}\\
&0,\,\text{prediction}<\text{label}\\
\end{aligned}\right.\\

When the recommendation of of CRS aligns with the label, or when the user simulator considers the recommended items as superior to the label, we assign a score of 2.
When the user simulator perceives the recommended items to be comparable to the label, we assign a score of 1.
However, we assign a score of 0 if the user simulator assesses the recommendation results as inferior to the label.

We utilize the user simulator for both preference dataset construction at the training stage and the evaluation at the inference stage.

Preference Dataset Construction

SFT training heavily relies on the quality of training datasets.
However, existing datasets are extremely noisy, which substantially limits the performance.
Therefore, we design a new preference dataset construction scheme for conversation recommendation for RL training.

Specifically, we employ the optimal model from the SFT stage as the recommender, while the original model acts as the user in the simulated conversation.
An LLM-based user simulator is utilized to provide feedback.
Then, as shown in Algorithm
1
, we collect the preference pairs for preference dataset construction.
For each sample from the training dataset, we execute the conversation simulator
\mathcal{S}(\theta,\phi;h_{i})
for
k
times, with high-temperature settings.
The conversation simulator will generate a sequence of 2n-turns, comprising the user simulator’s utterances
\mathcal{U}=\{u_{0},u_{1},\cdot\cdot\cdot,u_{n}\}
, recommender’s responses
\mathcal{R}=\{r_{0},r_{1},\cdot\cdot\cdot,r_{n}\}
, and the final score
s
.
We select responses with high scores as acceptable completions and those with low scores as rejected completions.
For straightforward examples, it is common to reach a situation with all scores as 2.
We randomly select one as preferred data, while treating the original labels in the dataset as dispreferred data.
However, numerous challenging samples remain suboptimal or receive low scores even after undergoing SFT, which may adversely affect subsequent RL training.
Therefore, we set the preferred completion as ground truth, while the dispreferred completion as the low-scored response.
To make the preference pair reliable, we adopt a majority voting strategy.
Finally,
r_{w}

(2)

(3)

3.2.
Self-Enhancement Strategy (SES)

After the training stage, the LLM is equipped with the potential for multi-round conversation recommendation, but such potential cannot be fully utilized due to the noise in the training dataset and the inherent noise in RL training.
Although the distribution of the output is close to the target, it remains dispersed.
Therefore, we proposed SES to further exploit the potential of the fine-tuned LLM for multi-round conversational recommendation.
SES induces high-quality responses from an LLM by multiple times sampling with a high-temperature setting.
With an internal scoring mechanism, SES facilitates to capture the most appropriate recommendation.

Figure
2
primarily describes the workflow of SES with a sample.
Figure
2
(a) illustrates the functions of the modules used in SES, as well as their corresponding role-specific prompts.
When a certain round of conversation in Figure
2
(b) is in SES, the user preference summarizer in Figure
2
(c) will first summarize previous dialogues into a user preference profile.
This profile is then used to construct an internal user simulator.
The yellow-highlighted section in Figure
2
(c) represents internal simulated conversation: The recommender LLM samples multiple responses to the user’s current utterance in Figure
2
(b), each of which subsequently engages in extended simulated conversation with the internal user simulator until termination, with their final scores obtained through the majority voting.
The tree search strategy further extends this process by having the recommender LLM iteratively sample multiple responses at each turn of the internal simulated conversation, each branching into new simulated dialogues with the internal user simulator.
Ultimately, the CRS returns the response from the initial sampling batch whose sub-nodes achieve the highest aggregated scores to the user simulator for evaluation in Figure
2
(b).

User Preference Summarizer

Aiming to assign a score to the responses sampled from LLM, we design an internal user model to filter the response preferences.
Though without access to labels, the internal user simulator model is expected to act as closely as possible with the external user simulator model, which has label access, thus filtering consistent response preferences with the external user simulator model.
Therefore, it is essential to capture an accurate and functional user profile.
Instead of simply passing the user’s historical conversation information to the internal user simulator, we leverage an LLM, which summarizes the user preferences from historical conversations and generates a fixed-format user profile.
The user profile is then fed into the internal user model along with the historical conversations as a prompt.
The expression of the user preference summarizer is

(4)

p=\mathcal{F}^{ups}(h^{e}),

where the
h^{e}
means the conversation history in the external evaluation process,
\mathcal{F}^{ups}
is the LLM function with a fixed input prompt template, and
p
is the user profile.

Internal User Simulator

With the user’s preference profile and historical conversation information, the internal user simulator assigns scores to the recommendation and explanation of the recommender system after a specific number of rounds.
The internal user’s response process can be expressed as

(5)

r_{i}=\mathcal{F}^{ius}(h^{s},p),

where

(6)

h^{s}=h^{e}\parallel h^{i}.

where
h^{i}
is the dialogue history between the recommender system and the internal user simulator,
\parallel
represents the concatenation operation,
r_{i}
means the response of the internal user simulator in the i-th round in within the simulated conversation, and
\mathcal{F}^{ius}
is function of internal user simulator LLM with a fixed prompt template.

Search Strategy

To explore the potential of fine-tuned LLMs’ multi-round conversational recommendation, we design a search strategy to identify the best possible responses from diverse outcomes.
First, we increase the temperature of the LLM at each round of interaction with the external user simulator to encourage the generation of more diverse responses.
Then, each sample is scored using a tree search strategy combined with majority voting, based on the independent interactions between the internal user and the recommender system.

Tree search strategy is designed to to mitigate the bias in scoring due to the internal simulator’s lack of awareness of true labels.
Specifically, with the tree search strategy, SES iteratively samples multiple responses at each turn.
Each sampled response further branches into a new simulated dialogue with the internal user simulator.
Ultimately, the CRS returns the initial response whose sub-nodes achieve the highest aggregated scores.
As a result, the tree search strategy mitigates the potential bias in scoring.
Thanks to this search strategy and majority voting, the quality of the scoring is ensured.

Datasets

We experiment on two popular real-world conversational recommendation datasets:
1) ReDial
(
Li et al. 2018

is a conversational movie recommendation dataset created by 504 crowd-sourcing workers on Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT) with 10,006 dialogues and 182,150 utterances, including 51,699 related movies.
2) OpenDialkg
(
Moon et al. 2019
)
is a cross-domain conversational recommendation dataset with 6,429 dialogues about movies and 5,891 dialogues about books, including 37,838 and 34,035 utterances respectively. We follow iEvaLM
(
Wang et al. 2023b
)
to split the training, validation, and testing sub-datasets for both datasets.

Implementation Details

For both SFT and PO stages, we deploy LoRA to finetune the LLMs based on the LLaMa-Factory
(
Zheng et al. 2024
)
platform.
We employ LoRA fine-tuning to reduce the trainable parameters.
We set the rank of LoRA to 8, batch size to 128, and search learning rate from 1e-5 to 1e-8.
On the SFT stage, we set loss to cross-entropy and learning rate to 2e-7 and 5e-7 for ReDial and OpenDialkg dataset, respectively.
In the RL stage, we adopt SimPO
(
Meng et al. 2024
)
and set
\beta
to 0.5.
When collecting preference data following Algorithm
1
, we set the temperature of recommender LLM in the first round of
\mathcal{S}(\theta,\phi;h_{i})
to 0.5.
What’s more, since the final scoring is discrete, even if the temperature is tuned up, it is infeasible to avoid a large number of score repetitions for the same sample, especially when confronting extreme examples such as the extremely difficult or simple ones, while the time and computational consumption of sampling is high, so we chose
k=2
.

Evaluation Metrics

Traditional recommendation evaluation metrics, like Recall@1, is proved that they tend to lead to unfair evaluation for CRS with LLM to a strong preference for the traditional recommender systems that overfit the items in the datasets
(
Wang et al. 2023b
;
Bang et al. 2023
;
Qin et al. 2023
)
.
Therefore, following Section
3.1
, we not only utilize Recall@1, but we also leverage iEval to evaluate those methods on the test dataset as our evaluation metric.

For iEval, we set the number of simulated dialogue rounds to 3, and in the last round we let user simulator give the recommendations of the LLM a score of 0, 1, or 2.
To ensure the consistency in evaluating and scoring different models, we choose Llama3.1-8B as the external user simulator to evaluate the three models mentioned before.
Besides, we find that the evaluation results are very unstable when the number of test samples is less than 200, and gradually converge after the number larger than 400, so finally we choose 512 samples, considering the time consumption.
For Recall@1, we test them on all data in test dataset.

To validate the effectiveness and generalizability of our method for improving the multi-round conversation recommendation capability of large models, we conduct experiments on several well-known LLMs, Llama3.1-8B-instruct
(
Dubey et al. 2024
)
, ChatGLM3-6B
(
GLM et al. 2024
)
and Qwen2.5-7B-instruct
(
Yang et al. 2024b
)

Main Results

Performance Comparison

Table
1
presents the Recall@1 and iEval results of different methods on the ReDial and OpenDialkg datasets.
Our method enhances the conversational recommendation potential of LLMs by RL training based on user simulator and employs a self-enhancement strategy to further improve performance during test time.
As shown in Table
1
, the proposed method, USB-Rec, outperforms all baselines on both datasets across most metrics. Specifically, USB-Rec achieves the highest iEval scores on both ReDial (1.29) and OpenDialKG (1.40), indicating superior recommendation quality.
In terms of Recall@1, USB-Rec attains competitive performance, getting the sub-optimal result on ReDial dataset, the same as UniCRS (0.050), and achieving a near-top score on OpenDialKG (0.300), closely following BARCOR (0.312) and UniCRS (0.308).
Furthermore, compared to ReFICR, our method exhibits a marginal disadvantage in Recall@1 but demonstrates a significant improvement in iEval.
This indicates that while RAG can enhance model accuracy within existing datasets, it may constrain the exploration of potentially superior recommendations.
These results demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of USB-Rec across diverse conversational recommendation scenarios.

Generalizability Study

In order to investigate the generalizability of our framework for training and inference, we evaluated the recommendation ability of three LLMs in Table
2
.
The results show that LLMs have gained significant improvement in recommendation ability after applying our framework.

Specifically, Llama has a certain recommendation potential on its own, so the value of the metric is significantly improved with SES applied to the Llama that was not fine-tuned.
Qwen and ChatGLM lacked the potential in conversational recommendation, and thus the effect of SES is slightly for them, being raised by 0.05 and 0.03 on average, respectively, without our fine-tuning framework.
However, after going through our training framework, SES can fully utilize their conversational recommendation capabilities, improving the value evaluation metric by more than 0.1, even if the gains are not evident after training alone, which are all less than 0.05.
This phenomenon illustrates that although the improvement of our fine-tuning method is not significant due to the noise in the dataset, the model could learn the potential of conversational recommendation ability, i.e., the output distribution of the model is approaching that of a recommendation expert, but the low probability makes it difficult to express.
And we demonstrate that our framework not only gives LLMs which lack conversation recommendation capabilities the potential to recommend, but brings this potential to full utilization.

In order to investigate the effect of each hyper-parameter of SES on the improvement of the recommendation ability of the large model, we choose Llama3.1-8b as a baseline to study and analyze.

Effect of Search Sampling Temperature

We have conducted a detailed study of the temperatures of the LLMs responses with 3 simulation rounds, as shown in Figure

For the first response of the recommender LLM during the internal search, when the temperature is too low the LLM cannot explore more diversity with only 1.31 score, and when the temperature is too high the LLM tends to deviate from the goal bring the score down to 1.31. Effect of Tree Search Depth in SES

As shown in the Table
4

Effect of Tree Search Width in SES

In Table
5
, we examine the effect on performance of different number of simulation rounds and the number of response samples taken in the first round of SES, with fixed number of majority voting, 10.
As the number of simulation rounds increases, the LLMs’ recommendation become more effective. The performance is improved noticeably as the number of simulated rounds increases from 3 to 4, with an average increase of evaluation metric about 0.06, however, after 4 rounds the improvement is gradually insignificant, with an average gain of only 0.02 from 4 to 5 rounds.
This means that more and deeper interactions can provide more valid information for LLMs to make better recommendations, however there is also an upper limit to how much that can help the LLMs, which is constrained by the summarization and reasoning ability of the LLMs themselves for we choose a small version of those LLMs due to the limited computational resources.

What’s more, we find that the performance does not get better the more times the responses are sampled. Instead, the metric values start to decline when the number of sampling is more than 4.
The results from 5 sampling times are on average 0.02 or 0.03 lower than those from 2 sampling times, and an average of 0.04 to 0.06 lower than those from 3 sampling times.
This is mainly due to the fact that the internal model doesn’t know the real labels, and when faced with too many choices, it can easily be misled, leading to deteriorating performance.

5.
CONCLUSION

In this work, we propose USB-Rec, a training-inference integrated framework for improving LLMs’ conversational recommendation capability at the model level.
At training stage, an automatic preference pair dataset construction method is proposed for RL training.
At inference stage, We design a search strategy that can deeply explore the conversational recommendation capabilities of LLMs that acquired from training stage but not be fully expressed.
Extensive experiment results show that USB-Rec outperforms previous traditional NLP-model-based and LLM-based methods, demonstrating its effectiveness and the generalizability for different base LLMs.
