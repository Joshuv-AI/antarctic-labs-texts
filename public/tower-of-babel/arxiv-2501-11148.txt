Exponents in the local properties problem for difference sets have a gap at 2
Sanjana Das

Abstract
. We study the local properties problem for difference sets: If we have a large set of real numbers and know that every small subset has many distinct differences, to what extent must the entire set have many distinct differences? More precisely, we define g ⁡ ( n , k , ℓ ) g(n,k,\ell) to be the minimum number of differences in an n n -element set with the ‘local property’ that every k k -element subset has at least ℓ \ell differences; we study the asymptotic behavior of g ⁡ ( n , k , ℓ ) g(n,k,\ell) as k k and ℓ \ell are fixed and n → ∞ n\to\infty . The quadratic threshold is the smallest ℓ \ell (as a function of k k ) for which g ⁡ ( n , k , ℓ ) = Ω ⁡ ( n 2 ) g(n,k,\ell)=\Omega(n^{2}) ; its value is known when k k is even. In this paper, we show that for k k even, when ℓ \ell is one below the quadratic threshold, we have g ⁡ ( n , k , ℓ ) = O ⁡ ( n c ) g(n,k,\ell)=O(n^{c}) for an absolute constant c < 2 c<2 — i.e., at the quadratic threshold, the ‘exponent of n n in g ⁡ ( n , k , ℓ ) g(n,k,\ell) ’ jumps by a constant independent of k k .



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background on local properties problems

There is a long history of studying problems with the following form: If we have a large object and we know that every small piece of it is ‘unstructured’ in some sense, how unstructured must the
entire
object be? In other words, to what extent can we go from a
local
property about lack of structure to a
global
one?

Erdős and Shelah
[
5
, Section V]
initiated the study of a local properties problem for graphs. Here, the large object is an edge-coloring of a complete graph, and the small pieces are constant-sized induced subgraphs; we think of an edge-coloring as unstructured if it contains many distinct colors. To formalize this, we define
f(n,k,\ell)
to be the minimum number of colors needed to edge-color
K_{n}
such that every induced subgraph
K_{k}
contains at least
\ell
colors. This can be viewed as a generalization of Ramsey numbers, which correspond to the case
\ell=2
— the
t
-color Ramsey number
r_{t}(k)
is the smallest
n
for which it is not possible to edge-color
K_{n}
with
t
colors such that every
K_{k}
contains at least two colors, i.e., the smallest
n
for which
f(n,k,2)>t
.

When studying
f(n,k,\ell)
, we typically think of
k
and
\ell
as constants, and study the asymptotic behavior of
f(n,k,\ell)
as
n\to\infty
. One direction to approach this problem from is to search for thresholds: If we fix
k
and increase
\ell
from
1
to
\binom{k}{2}
, then
f(n,k,\ell)
will increase from
1
to
\binom{n}{2}
, and we can ask for the value of
\ell
at which
f(n,k,\ell)
begins to exhibit a certain behavior, which we call the
threshold
for that behavior. In
[
7
]
, Erdős and Gyárfás introduced the following thresholds.

•

The
polynomial threshold
is the smallest
\ell
(as a function of
k
) for which
f(n,k,\ell)=\Omega(n^{\varepsilon})
for some
\varepsilon>0
(possibly depending on
k
).

•

The
linear threshold
is the smallest
\ell
for which
f(n,k,\ell)=\Omega(n)
.

•

The
superlinear threshold
is the smallest
\ell
for which
f(n,k,\ell)=\omega(n)
.

•

The
quadratic threshold
is the smallest
\ell
for which
f(n,k,\ell)=\Omega(n^{2})
.

Erdős and Gyárfás
[
7
]
exactly determined the linear and quadratic thresholds — they showed that the linear threshold is
\binom{k}{2}-k+3
and the quadratic threshold is
\binom{k}{2}-\lfloor k/2\rfloor+2
. They also showed that the polynomial threshold is at most
k
; Conlon, Fox, Lee, and Sudakov
[
3
]
later proved that this is tight. Sárközy and Selkow
[
13
]
showed that the superlinear threshold is at most
\binom{k}{2}-k+\lceil\log k\rceil+3
.

Erdős and Gyárfás
[
7
]
also proved a general upper bound on
f(n,k,\ell)
using a random construction; works including
[
12
,
9
,
1
]
have proved several families of lower bounds, which nearly match this upper bound in many cases.

Erdős
[
6
]
also posed a similar local properties problem for distinct distances. Here, the large object is a set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and we think of a set of points as unstructured if it spans many distinct distances. So we define
\phi(n,k,\ell)
as the minimum number of distinct distances that
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
can span, given that every
k
points span at least
\ell
distinct distances.

We always have
\phi(n,k,\ell)\geq f(n,k,\ell)
— we can convert any configuration of points into a graph on those points where the color of an edge represents the distance between its two endpoints — and in many regimes, this is the best known lower bound for
\phi(n,k,\ell)
. However, there are a few cases where we are able to make use of the geometric structure to prove better lower bounds: Fox, Pach, and Suk
[
10
]
proved that
\phi(n,k,\binom{k}{2}-k+6)=\Omega(n^{8/7-o(1)})
. (Meanwhile, we only know that
f(n,k,\binom{k}{2}-k+6)=\Omega(n)
.)

It is also possible to approach this problem in terms of thresholds (in particular, Fox, Pach, and Suk comment on the linear and quadratic thresholds in
[
10
, Section 1]
), but such thresholds are much farther from being understood than those for
f(n,k,\ell)
, at least for general values of
k
. Erdős also posed several problems about determining
\phi(n,k,\ell)
for small values of
k
and
\ell
; in particular, in
[
6
]
he asked whether
g(n,4,5)=\Omega(n^{2})
. Recently Tao
[
15
]
proved that the answer is no, which means that when
k=4
, the quadratic threshold is
6
. See
[
14
, Section 7]
for more about this problem.

1.2.
A local properties problem for difference sets

We study an arithmetic local properties problem, first described in
[
12
]
. Here, our large object is a set of numbers, and we think of a set of numbers as unstructured if it contains many distinct differences. More formally, for a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we define the
difference set
of
A
to be

A-A=\{\left\lvert a-b\right\rvert\mid a,b\in A,\,a\neq b\}.

We only include positive differences in
A-A
; this is nonstandard but more natural for this problem.

We define
g(n,k,\ell)
to be the minimum value of
\left\lvert A-A\right\rvert
over all
n
-element sets
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with the property that every
k
-element subset
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
satisfies
\lvert A^{\prime}-A^{\prime}\rvert\geq\ell
, which we refer to as the
(k,\ell)
-local property
. We think of
k
and
\ell
as constants, and study the asymptotic behavior of
g(n,k,\ell)
as
n\to\infty
. (We use standard asymptotic notation throughout this paper; all asymptotic notation is as
n\to\infty
, and the implicit constants may depend on
k
,
\ell
, and any other relevant parameters.)

This problem can be viewed as a one-dimensional version of the local properties problem for distinct distances — in particular, we have

(1.1)

g(n,k,\ell)\geq\phi(n,k,\ell)\geq f(n,k,\ell)

(since any collection of real numbers
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
can be viewed as a set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
).

Note that any
m
-element set
A
satisfies
m-1\leq\left\lvert A-A\right\rvert\leq\binom{m}{2}
. This means that the problem is only interesting when
k-1\leq\ell\leq\binom{k}{2}
(the
(k,\ell)
-local property is vacuous for smaller values of
\ell
, and impossible to satisfy for larger values of
\ell
). Also, if we increase
\ell
from
k-1
to
\binom{k}{2}
, then
g(n,k,\ell)
will increase from
n-1
to
\binom{n}{2}
(as long as
k\geq 4
). So the most natural thresholds to consider are the superlinear and quadratic threshold, both introduced by Li
[
11
]
(defined analogously to the corresponding thresholds for
f
).

•

The
superlinear threshold
is the smallest
\ell
(as a function of
k
) for which
g(n,k,\ell)=\omega(n)
.

•

The
quadratic threshold
is the smallest
\ell
for which
g(n,k,\ell)=\Omega(n^{2})
.

1.3.
Previous work

Because of (
1.1
), all lower bounds for
f
or
\phi
are also lower bounds for
g
. However, it turns out that we know significantly stronger lower bounds for
g
than for
f
or
\phi
. (In the opposite direction, apart from a few cases with
k\leq 4
, nearly all the nontrivial
upper
bounds we know on
\phi
come from upper bounds on
g
, while Erdős and Gyárfás’s random construction in
[
7
]
gives much better upper bounds on
f
.)

The first lower bounds specific to
g
were proven by Fish, Pohoata, and Sheffer
[
9
]
, who showed that for all
r\geq 2
and
k
divisible by
2r
, we have

(1.2)

g\left(n,k,\binom{k}{2}-\frac{(r-1)(r+2)}{2}\binom{k/r}{2}+1\right)=\Omega(n^{\frac{r}{r-1}\cdot\frac{k-2r}{k}}).

For example, when
r=2
, this states that for
4\mid k
we have
g(n,k,k^{2}/4+1)=\Omega(n^{2-8/k})
. More generally, if we think of
r
as fixed and
k
as reasonably large, (
1.2
) gives a family of lower bounds on
g(n,k,\ell)
for values of
\ell
roughly between
7k^{2}/32
and
k^{2}/4
. (For comparison, such values of
\ell
are well below the linear threshold for
f
, which occurs at
\binom{k}{2}-k+3
.)

Fish, Pohoata, and Sheffer also proved an upper bound for
g(n,k,\ell)
for small values of
\ell
: They showed that for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
a>0
such that for all sufficiently large
k
, we have

(1.3)

g(n,k,ak(\log k)^{1/4-\varepsilon})=n\cdot 2^{O(\sqrt{\log n})}=n^{1+o(1)}.

This comes from a result in additive combinatorics that for such values of
\ell
, any
k
-element set with fewer than
\ell
differences must contain a
3
-AP (in this paper, we use ‘
k
-AP’ as an abbreviation for ‘
k
-term arithmetic progression’). So any
3
-AP-free set satisfies the
(k,\ell)
-local property; the bound then follows by using the construction of
3
-AP-free sets due to Behrend
[
2
]
.

Fish, Lund, and Sheffer
[
8
]
then proved the upper bound

g\left(n,k,\frac{k^{\log_{2}3}-1}{2}\right)=O(n^{\log_{2}3}).

Their construction was roughly an affine
t
-cube (a set of the form
\{a+\varepsilon_{1}d_{1}+\cdots+\varepsilon_{t}d_{t}\mid\varepsilon_{i}\in\{0,1\}\}
for fixed
a
,
d_{1}
, …,
d_{t}
) with
t\approx\log_{2}n
.

Li
[
11
]
then introduced and studied the superlinear and quadratic thresholds. She proved that the superlinear threshold is exactly
k
; the only
k
-element subsets that violate the
(k,k)
-local property are
k
-APs, so this result states that any set with only linearly many differences must contain a
k
-AP.

Li also proved that if
k
is even, then

(1.4)

g\left(n,k,\frac{3k^{2}}{8}-\frac{3k}{4}+2\right)=\Omega(n^{2}),

which means the quadratic threshold is at most
3k^{2}/8-3k/4+2
. The idea of the proof is that a set with few distinct differences must contain a difference repeated many times; then if we take
k/2
pairs of numbers with that difference, they form a
k
-element subset with at most
3k^{2}/8-3k/4+1
differences, violating the
(k,\ell)
-local property for
\ell=3k^{2}/8-3k/4+2
.

Li also proved several ‘intermediate’ lower bounds for
g(n,k,\ell)
— in particular, she showed that if
k
is divisible by
8
, then

(1.5)

g\left(n,k,\frac{9k^{2}}{32}-\frac{9k}{16}+5\right)=\Omega(n^{4/3}),

and if
k\geq 8
is a power of
2
, then

(1.6)

g\left(n,k,\frac{k^{\log_{2}3}+1}{2}\right)=\Omega(n^{1+\frac{2}{k-2}}).

The proofs of both bounds can be interpreted as showing that a set with few distinct differences must contain many congruent affine
t
-cubes, and a
k
-element set consisting of
k/2^{t}
congruent affine
t
-cubes violates the
(k,\ell)
-local property for the chosen values of
\ell
. (For (
1.5
) we take
t=2
, and for (
1.6
) we take
t=\log_{2}k-1
; we can view the proof of (
1.4
) as the same argument with
t=1
.)

Finally, Li also proved an upper bound for small values of
\ell
, using a random construction: She showed that for every
a\geq 2
, for all sufficiently large
k
we have

(1.7)

g(n,k,ak+1)=O(n^{1+\frac{a^{2}+1}{k}}).

In
[
4
]
, the author further studied the quadratic threshold. For
k
even, they determined it exactly — they showed that we have

(1.8)

\displaystyle g\left(n,k,\frac{k^{2}}{4}+1\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega(n^{2}),

(1.9)

\displaystyle g\left(n,k,\frac{k^{2}}{4}\right)

\displaystyle=o(n^{2}),

which means the quadratic threshold is exactly
k^{2}/4
. For
k
odd, they determined the quadratic threshold up to a constant-length window — they showed that

(1.10)

\displaystyle g\left(n,k,\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{4}\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega(n^{2}),

(1.11)

\displaystyle g\left(n,k,\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{4}-4\right)

\displaystyle=o(n^{2}).

The lower bounds (
1.8
) and (
1.10
) come from showing that a set with few distinct differences must contain a
sum
repeated many times, and a
k
-element set formed by taking many pairs with the same sum violates the
(k,\ell)
-local property. The upper bounds (
1.9
) and (
1.11
) come from a random construction.

In
[
4
]
, the author also proved a family of ‘intermediate’ lower and upper bounds — they showed that

(1.12)

g\left(n,k,\frac{3^{t-1}}{4^{t}}k^{2}+\frac{3^{t-1}+1}{2}\right)=\Omega(n^{1+\frac{1}{2^{t}-1}})

for all integers
t\geq 1
, and

(1.13)

g\left(n,k,\left\lceil\frac{(c-1)(k-1)}{c}\right\rceil^{2}\right)=o(n^{c})

for all
1<c\leq 2
. The proof of the lower bound (
1.12
) combines ideas from Li’s proof of (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) with ones from the proof of (
1.8
) — a set with few distinct differences must contain
k/2^{t-1}
disjoint affine
(t-1)
-cubes whose centers form pairs with equal sums. The proof of the upper bound (
1.13
) again comes from a random construction.

If we think of
t
and
c
as fixed and
k
as reasonably large, the values of
\ell
in both (
1.12
) and (
1.13
) are quadratic in
k
, and the exponents of
n
are constants greater than
1
. So one interpretation of these bounds is that for all
1<c\leq 2
, the
threshold for
\Omega(n^{c})
— i.e., the smallest
\ell
for which
g(n,k,\ell)=\Omega(n^{c})
— is ‘quadratic in
k
.’ (More precisely, for each
1<c\leq 2
, there exist
a_{1},a_{2}>0
such that for all large
k
, the threshold for
\Omega(n^{c})
is between
a_{1}k^{2}
and
a_{2}k^{2}
. Furthermore, these constants can be chosen such that as
c\to 1
, we have
a_{1},a_{2}\to 0
.)

1.4.
Our question and result

Once we understand a threshold for
g
, it is natural to consider what the transition in the behavior of
g
‘looks like’ at that threshold. For example, Li
[
11
]
showed that the superlinear threshold is
k
, so it is natural to ask how
much
faster than linear
g(n,k,\ell)
grows once
\ell
crosses this threshold. Behrend’s construction of
3
-AP-free (and therefore
k
-AP-free) sets with
n^{1+o(1)}
differences shows that for any
k\geq 3
, we have
g(n,k,k)=n^{1+o(1)}
. So when we cross the superlinear threshold,
g(n,k,\ell)
changes from a function which is linear in
n
to one which is not linear, but still only
n^{1+o(1)}
. Furthermore, (
1.3
) means that when
k
is large,
g(n,k,\ell)
remains
n^{1+o(1)}
for a reasonably large range of
\ell
. (This question was also the motivation behind Li’s upper bound (
1.7
).)

In this paper, we study this question for the quadratic threshold. When
k
is even, the quadratic threshold is
k^{2}/4+1
, so it is natural to ask what the behavior of
g(n,k,k^{2}/4)
looks like — we know that it is subquadratic, but
how
far from quadratic is it? So far, the best lower bound we know on
g(n,k,k^{2}/4)
(for reasonably large
k
) comes from the bound (
1.2
) of Fish, Pohoata, and Sheffer
[
9
]
(with
r=3
), which gives

(1.14)

g\left(n,k,\frac{k^{2}}{4}\right)=\Omega(n^{3/2-9/k}).

Meanwhile, a more careful analysis of the proof of (
1.9
) from
[
4
]
would give the quantitative bound

(1.15)

g\left(n,k,\frac{k^{2}}{4}\right)=O(n^{2-2/k+o(1)}).

In fact, this bound can be deduced directly from (
1.13
) — if we plug any
c>2-2/k
into (
1.13
), the value of
\ell
it gives is still
k^{2}/4
.

For any
fixed

k
, the exponent of
n
in (
1.15
) is bounded away from
2
; so it is not the case that
g(n,k,k^{2}/4)=n^{2-o(1)}
. However, the behaviors of the lower and upper bounds (
1.14
) and (
1.15
) are qualitatively very different as
k
becomes large. In (
1.14
) the exponent of
n
is at most
3/2
, which is bounded away from
2
; in (
1.15
) it is at least
2-2/k
, which becomes arbitrarily close to
2
as
k
grows. So it is natural to ask which behavior is correct.

In this paper, we show that the qualitative behavior of the lower bound (
1.14
) is correct — the exponent of
n
in
g(n,k,k^{2}/4)
is bounded away from
2
by a constant independent of
k
. (One can formalize the notion of the ‘exponent of
n
in
g(n,k,\ell)
’ by considering
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\log_{n}g(n,k,\ell)
or
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\log_{n}g(n,k,\ell)
.)

Theorem 1.1
.

There is an absolute constant
c<2
such that for all even
k
, we have

g\left(n,k,\frac{k^{2}}{4}\right)=O(n^{c}).

Our proof obtains the constant
c=2-2^{-29}
. (We did not optimize this.)

.

There is an absolute constant
c<2
such that for all odd
k
, we have

g\left(n,k,\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{4}-4\right)=O(n^{c}).

Unlike for even
k
, we do not know the exact quadratic threshold for odd
k
— we know that
g(n,k,(k+1)^{2}/4)=\Omega(n^{2})
, but we do not understand the behavior of
g(n,k,\ell)
when
\ell
is between these two values. However, Proposition
1.2
does guarantee that the exponent of
n
in
g(n,k,\ell)
jumps by a constant independent of
k
when we decrease
\ell
from the quadratic threshold to
four
below it (for even
k
, we could make the same statement with one in place of four).

1.5.
Overview

We now give an overview of the ideas of the proof of Theorem
1.1
. (The proof of Proposition
1.2
uses the same ideas.)

First, it is often useful to think about whether a set satisfies the
(k,\ell)
-local property in terms of the ‘configurations of equal differences’ formed by its
k
-element subsets. More precisely, given
k
numbers, we can write down a (minimal) system of equations that keeps track of which differences among them are equal — for example,
\{1,2,5,6,9\}
corresponds to the system
\{x_{1}-x_{2}=x_{3}-x_{4},\,x_{1}-x_{3}=x_{3}-x_{5}\}
. We refer to such a system of equations as a
k
-configuration
. We can figure out the number of distinct differences among
k
numbers just by looking at the
k
-configuration they form; so a set satisfies the
(k,\ell)
-local property if and only if it avoids all
k
-configurations which have fewer than
\ell
distinct differences.

We will first discuss the ideas behind the proof of (
1.9
) (the weaker bound
g(n,k,k^{2}/4)=o(n^{2})
) in
[
4
]
, which the proof of Theorem
1.1
builds on. Our goal is to obtain a set
A
with
\left\lvert A\right\rvert=n
and
\left\lvert A-A\right\rvert=o(n^{2})
which satisfies the
(k,k^{2}/4)
-local property. We do so via a random construction. Roughly, we start by taking a random subset of
\{1,2,\ldots,o(n^{2})\}
of size a bit bigger than
n
. Some
k
-configurations are expected to appear very few times in this random subset, so we can eliminate all their appearances using the alteration method. We refer to
k
-configurations that we can avoid in this way as
2
-bad
, and the remaining ones as
2
-good
. (We are suppressing a few technical details here — the actual construction and the description of
2
-good and
2
-bad
k
-configurations are slightly more complicated — but this is the main idea.)

It then suffices to show that every
2
-good
k
-configuration has at least
k^{2}/4
distinct differences. The idea is that the number of times a
k
-configuration is expected to appear in our random subset is controlled by the number of times it appears in the ground set
\{1,2,\ldots,o(n^{2})\}
, which is controlled by the number of linearly independent equations it has. So a
2
-good
k
-configuration cannot have too many independent equations, and we can use this to show that it cannot have too many ‘repeated’ differences (so it must have many distinct differences). The value
k^{2}/4
is tight — the
k
-configuration

\{x_{1}+x_{2}=x_{3}+x_{4}=\cdots=x_{k-1}+x_{k}\},

which we call a
star of size
k
, is
2
-good and has exactly
k^{2}/4
distinct differences.

In order to prove Theorem
1.1
, we wish to improve the bound of
o(n^{2})
to
O(n^{c})
; so we now take a random subset of
\{1,2,\ldots,O(n^{c})\}
. We refer to the
k
-configurations that we can avoid using the alteration method as
c
-bad
, and the remaining ones as
c
-good
. Being
c
-good is a somewhat weaker condition than being
2
-good. However, it turns out that we can still show that every
c
-good
k
-configuration has at least
k^{2}/4
distinct differences — i.e., the star of size
k
is still the one with the fewest differences. Proving this statement is the bulk of the argument. For this, we use a stability-type argument, which consists of three steps.

(1)

First, by directly reusing the ideas from the argument that every
2
-good
k
-configuration has at least
k^{2}/4
distinct differences, we obtain a slightly weaker bound for
c
-good
k
-configurations (one that differs from
k^{2}/4
by a constant factor depending on
2-c
).

(2)

Next, we show that any
k
-configuration whose number of distinct differences is close to this weaker bound must be close to a star of size
k
— more precisely, any
k
-configuration within a
(1-\varepsilon)
-factor of the weaker bound must contain a star of size
(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})k
(where
\varepsilon^{\prime}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
).

If
2-c
is sufficiently small with respect to
\varepsilon
, then any
k
-configuration which is not within a
(1-\varepsilon)
-factor of the weaker bound will have fewer than
k^{2}/4
distinct differences. So it now suffices to consider
k
-configurations which contain a huge star.

(3)

Finally, we show that among
c
-good
k
-configurations which contain a huge star, the one with the fewest differences is the star of size
k
. Intuitively, we can imagine constructing such a
k
-configuration by starting with a huge star and adding a few extra equations. Requiring the
k
-configuration to be
c
-good
a priori
allows us to add a few more equations than we could if we required it to be
2
-good. However, we show roughly that even with these extra equations, there is nothing better to do with the remaining variables (i.e., the ones not in the huge star we started with) than adding them to the star; and once our star has all
k
variables, we show that we cannot add any more equations on top of it without violating
c
-goodness.

The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we flesh out the concept of
k
-configurations and define which ones are
c
-good and
c
-bad. In Section
3
, we describe the random construction and show that it avoids all
c
-bad
k
-configurations. (This random construction is almost the same as the one in
[
4
]
, but we use a slightly stronger definition of
c
-goodness here for technical reasons, so we need to tweak the construction a bit.) In Section
4
, we present the ‘backbone’ of the argument that every
c
-good
k
-configuration has at least
k^{2}/4
distinct differences; this argument involves a handful of lemmas whose proofs are fairly technical, and we present these proofs in Sections
5
–
8
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
; in Section
9
, we explain how to adapt the argument to prove Proposition
1.2
.

Setup

In this section, we formalize the notion of
k
-configurations and define which
k

2.1.
Conventions

Throughout this paper, we will work with systems of linear equations in
k
variables
x_{1}
, …,
x_{k}
; we first fix a few conventions regarding such systems.

We always work over the field
\mathbb{Q}
. We consider linear equations to be defined up to rearrangement, but not scaling. For example, we consider
x_{1}-x_{2}=x_{3}-x_{4}
and
x_{1}+x_{4}=x_{2}+x_{3}
to be the same equation, but
x_{1}-x_{2}=0
and
2x_{1}-2x_{2}=0
to be different equations.

Given a linear equation
(*)
, we define its
content
to be an expression
*
such that
(*)
is the equation
{*}=0
. (For any nontrivial equation
(*)
— i.e., one that is not the equation
0=0
— there are two ways to define its content; we choose one arbitrarily.) For example, the content of the equation
x_{1}-x_{2}=x_{3}-x_{4}
is either
x_{1}-x_{2}-x_{3}+x_{4}
or
-x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}-x_{4}
.

We say linear equations
(*_{1})
, …,
(*_{t})
are
independent
if their contents
*_{1}
, …,
*_{t}
are linearly independent. We say a collection of linear equations
\mathcal{T}=\{(*_{1}),\ldots,(*_{t})\}

implies
an equation
(*)
if
(*)
can be written as a linear combination of
(*_{1})
, …,
(*_{t})
, i.e.,

(2.1)

{*}=c_{1}{*_{1}}+\cdots+c_{t}{*_{t}}

for some
c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}\in\mathbb{Q}
. We say
\mathcal{T}

minimally implies

(*)
if
\mathcal{T}
implies
(*)
but no proper subset of
\mathcal{T}
does; equivalently,
\mathcal{T}
minimally implies
(*)
if
\mathcal{T}
is independent and the coefficients
c_{1}
, …,
c_{t}
in (

) are nonzero.

2.2.
Definition of
k
-configurations

We now define
k
-configurations and formalize some of the notions informally referred to in the proof overview in Subsection
1.5
(e.g., what it means for a
k
-configuration to ‘have’ a certain number of distinct differences).

We define a
difference equality
to be a nontrivial equation of the form

x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{2}}=x_{i_{3}}-x_{i_{4}}

for some (not necessarily distinct) indices
i_{1},\ldots,i_{4}\in[k]
. We define a
k
-configuration
to be a system of linear equations in
x_{1}
, …,
x_{k}
where each equation is a difference equality. For convenience, we consider two
k
-configurations to be the same if they produce equivalent systems of equations — for example, we consider

\{x_{1}-x_{2}=x_{3}-x_{4},\,x_{1}-x_{2}=x_{5}-x_{6}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\{x_{1}-x_{2}=x_{3}-x_{4},\,x_{3}-x_{4}=x_{5}-x_{6}\}

to be the same, and we will typically write this
k
-configuration as
\{x_{1}-x_{2}=x_{3}-x_{4}=x_{5}-x_{6}\}
.

We define the
k
-configuration
formed
by
k
numbers
a_{1}
, …,
a_{k}
to be the
k
-configuration consisting of all the difference equalities that
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
satisfies. For example, the
k
-configuration formed by
1
,
2
,
4
,
5
,
9
,
10
(given in that order) is
\{x_{1}-x_{2}=x_{3}-x_{4}=x_{5}-x_{6},\,x_{1}-2x_{4}+x_{5}=0\}
.

We will only be interested in
k
-configurations formed by
k
distinct numbers. So we say a
k
-configuration
\mathcal{C}
is
invalid
if it implies
x_{i}=x_{j}
for some
i\neq j
, and
valid
otherwise. If
a_{1}
, …,
a_{k}
are distinct, then the
k
-configuration they form must be valid.

For a pair
(i,j)\in[k]^{2}
with
i>j
, we say a
k
-configuration
\mathcal{C}

certifies

(i,j)
if
\mathcal{C}
implies

x_{i}-x_{j}=x_{i^{\prime}}-x_{j^{\prime}}

for some
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in[k]^{2}
where either
i^{\prime},j^{\prime}<i
, or
j^{\prime}=i
and
i^{\prime}<j
.

The reason for this definition is as follows: Suppose that we want to understand the number of distinct differences among
k
distinct numbers
a_{1}
, …,
a_{k}
. Then we can imagine going through all possible differences
\left\lvert a_{2}-a_{1}\right\rvert
,
\left\lvert a_{3}-a_{1}\right\rvert
,
\left\lvert a_{3}-a_{2}\right\rvert
,
\left\lvert a_{4}-a_{1}\right\rvert
,
\left\lvert a_{4}-a_{2}\right\rvert
,
\left\lvert a_{4}-a_{3}\right\rvert
, …, in that order, and recording whether each difference is repeated or new (i.e., whether we have already seen it or not). If we let
\mathcal{C}
be the
k
-configuration formed by
a_{1}
, …,
a_{k}
, then a difference
\left\lvert a_{i}-a_{j}\right\rvert
is repeated if and only if
\mathcal{C}
certifies
(i,j)
. So the number of distinct differences among
a_{1}
, …,
a_{k}
is the number of pairs
(i,j)
that
\mathcal{C}
does
not
certify, or
\binom{k}{2}
minus the number of pairs that it
does
certify.

In particular, this means that if
\mathcal{C}
is a valid
k
-configuration, then permuting the indices of its variables does not affect the number of pairs it certifies (we can always find
k
distinct numbers
a_{1}
, …,
a_{k}
that form
\mathcal{C}
; permuting indices in
\mathcal{C}
corresponds to reordering these numbers, which of course does not affect the number of distinct differences among them).

We will now define which

, so the same is true of any equation implied by a

, we say

); otherwise we say
\mathcal{C}
is

.

\mathcal{C}

if it is valid, collinearity-free, and

\mathcal{C}
is

-bad
. Note that if
\mathcal{C}
is

-good, then every difference equality implied by

contains four distinct variables (a difference equality containing two variables would be invalid, and one containing three variables would be collinearity-inducing).

Lemma 2.3
.

Fix any
1<c\leq 2
. For every

, there is a set

with
\left\lvert A\right\rvert=n
and
\left\lvert A-A\right\rvert=O(n^{c})
such that for all distinct
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in A
, the
k
-configuration that they form is
c
-good.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose that
c
is sufficiently close to
2
and that
k
is even. Then every
c
-good
k
-configuration certifies at most
(k^{2}-2k)/4
pairs.

(Note that equality holds for a star of size
k
, as seen in Example

.)

Together, Lemmas
2.3
and

immediately imply Theorem

— if we take

to be the set given by Lemma

, then the number of distinct differences in any

-element subset

is

minus the number of pairs certified by the

-configuration that the elements of

form. And Lemma
2.3
guarantees that this

-configuration is

-good, so by Lemma
2.4
this number of differences is at least
\binom{k}{2}-(k^{2}-2k)/4=k^{2}/4
.

3.
The random construction

In this section, we prove Lemma
2.3
— the statement that we can construct
n
-element sets
A
with
O(n^{c})
differences which avoid all
c
-bad
k
-configurations — using a random construction. (We say
A

avoids
a
k
-configuration
\mathcal{C}
if there do not exist distinct
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in A
such that
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
forms
\mathcal{C}
.)

In order to avoid collinearity-inducing
k
-configurations, we need the following fact.

Lemma 3.1
.

Fix
\kappa>0
. Then for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
of size
\left\lvert S\right\rvert=n^{1-o(1)}
(where the asymptotic notation is as
\kappa
is fixed and
n\to\infty
) such that for all nonzero
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}
of magnitude at most
\kappa
, there do not exist distinct
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\in S
with
\alpha s_{1}+\beta s_{2}+\gamma s_{3}=0
.

The construction for Lemma
3.1
is a direct adaptation of the construction of
3
-AP-free sets by Behrend
[
2
]
(avoiding
3
-APs corresponds to taking
\kappa=2
, but Behrend’s construction actually works for any constant
\kappa
, with the same proof), so we defer it to Appendix
A
.

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

Assume that
n
is sufficiently large (with respect to
k
and
c
); we will then construct
A
with the desired properties such that
\left\lvert A\right\rvert=n
and
\left\lvert A-A\right\rvert\leq n^{c}
.

We will first
deterministically
avoid all collinearity-inducing
k
-configurations using Lemma
3.1
. By definition, for every collinearity-inducing
k
-configuration
\mathcal{C}
, there exist nonzero
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{Q}
with
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=0
such that
\mathcal{C}
implies an equation of the form

\alpha x_{i_{1}}+\beta x_{i_{2}}+\gamma x_{i_{3}}=0.

There are only finitely many
k
-configurations, so by clearing denominators and taking a maximum over all collinearity-inducing
k
-configurations, we can find a constant
\kappa
(depending on
k
) such that for every collinearity-inducing
k
-configuration
\mathcal{C}
, there exist such
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}
with magnitude at most
\kappa
.

Now let
S
be the set given by Lemma
3.1
for this value of
\kappa
and with
n
replaced by
\lfloor n^{c}\rfloor
. Then
S
avoids all collinearity-inducing
k
-configurations, and we have
\left\lvert S\right\rvert=n^{c-o(1)}
and
\left\lvert S-S\right\rvert\leq n^{c}
.

We will now use randomness to find some
A\subseteq S
which avoids all
c
-heavy
k
-configurations. Let

\rho=\frac{2n}{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}=n^{1-c+o(1)}.

Define
B
to be a
\rho
-random subset of
S
(where we include each element of
S
independently with probability
\rho
), so that
\mathbb{E}[\left\lvert B\right\rvert]=2n
. Our goal is to delete a small number of elements from
B
such that the resulting set avoids all
c
-heavy
k
-configurations.

For this, first consider a
specific

c
-heavy
k
-configuration
\mathcal{C}
which we wish to avoid. Since
\mathcal{C}
is
c
-heavy, it implies some collection
\mathcal{T}
of
t
independent equations on
v
variables, for some
v<ct+1
. Then it suffices to look at all solutions to
\mathcal{T}
in
B
with distinct entries (looking at only the
v
variables present in
\mathcal{T}
) and delete one element from each — if
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\in B
are distinct and form
\mathcal{C}
, then the
v
numbers
b_{i}
corresponding to variables
x_{i}
in
\mathcal{T}
must form a solution to
\mathcal{T}
.

To bound the expected number of elements this causes us to delete, first note that the number of solutions to
\mathcal{T}
in
S
is at most
\left\lvert S\right\rvert^{v-t}
. One way to see this is to imagine putting
\mathcal{T}
into row echelon form; then we have
v-t
free variables, each of whose values can be chosen in
\left\lvert S\right\rvert
ways, and the values of the remaining variables are determined in terms of the values of the free variables.

Meanwhile, for any such solution with distinct entries, the probability that all
v
of its entries are placed into
B
is
\rho^{v}
. So the expected number of solutions to
\mathcal{T}
in
B
with distinct entries is at most

\rho^{v}\left\lvert S\right\rvert^{v-t}=n^{v(1-c)+o(1)}\cdot n^{c(v-t)-o(1)}=n^{v-ct+o(1)}=o(n).

(Note that
v
and
c
are at most
k
and
v-ct<1
, so we can bound
v-ct
away from
1
by a constant only depending on
k
.)

Now let
B^{\prime}
be the set obtained by taking
B
and performing such deletions for
every

c
-heavy
k
-configuration
\mathcal{C}
. Each
c
-heavy
k
-configuration
\mathcal{C}
causes us to delete
o(n)
elements in expectation, and the total number of
k
-configurations is a constant only depending on
k
, so the
total
number of elements we delete is also
o(n)
in expectation; this means

\mathbb{E}[\left\lvert B^{\prime}\right\rvert]=\mathbb{E}[\left\lvert B\right\rvert]-o(n)=2n-o(n)\geq n

(assuming that
n
is sufficiently large). Finally, this means there is some outcome of the randomness under which
\left\lvert B^{\prime}\right\rvert\geq n
, and we can obtain
A
from
B^{\prime}
by deleting elements arbitrarily until it has size exactly
n
.
∎

Throughout the argument, we will have two parameters

•

We use

Setting

c\geq 2-\min\{\varepsilon^{2}/32,2/k_{0}\}
works; this allows us to get
c=2-2^{-29}
.

4.1.
The technical lemmas

In this subsection, we collect the statements of the lemmas whose proofs we defer. First, for Step
1
of the outline (the baseline bound), we need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Fix
i\in[k]
, and let
\mathcal{S}
be a
c
-good collection of independent difference equalities on
x_{1}
, …,
x_{i}
which all contain
x_{i}
, with
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert\leq s
. Then
\mathcal{S}
certifies at most
2s
pairs
(i,\bullet)
.

When we say that
\mathcal{S}
certifies at most
2s
pairs
(i,\bullet)
, we mean there are at most
2s
indices
j<i
for which
\mathcal{S}
certifies
(i,j)
.

The intuition behind Lemma
4.1
is that a
single
difference equality containing
x_{i}
certifies two pairs
(i,\bullet)
— for example,
x_{i}-x_{1}=x_{2}-x_{3}
certifies
(i,1)
and
(i,2)
. So Lemma
4.1
would be immediate if
\mathcal{S}
did not imply any difference equalities containing
x_{i}
other than the ones already in
\mathcal{S}
. The difficulty comes from the fact that
\mathcal{S}

can
imply additional difference equalities, which could potentially certify extra pairs
(i,\bullet)
. However, it turns out that when this happens, some of the difference equalities in
\mathcal{S}
must certify the
same
pairs
(i,\bullet)
, and this overlap compensates for those extra pairs.

For Step
2
of the outline (the stability argument), we need the following two lemmas.

Lemma 4.2
.

Fix
i\in[k]
and
s\geq 1/\varepsilon
, and let
\mathcal{S}
be a
c
-good collection of independent difference equalities on
x_{1}
, …,
x_{i}
which all contain
x_{i}
, with
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert\leq s
. Suppose that
\mathcal{S}
certifies at least
2(1-\varepsilon)s
pairs
(i,\bullet)
and does not imply any star of size at least
2(1-16\varepsilon)s
. Then
\mathcal{S}
certifies at most
(1-2\varepsilon)s^{2}
pairs
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in[i-1]^{2}
.

The intuition behind Lemma
4.2
is that if
\mathcal{S}
consisted of
s
equations defining a star of size
2(s+1)
(e.g.,
\mathcal{S}=\{x_{i}+x_{1}=x_{2}+x_{3},\,x_{i}+x_{1}=x_{4}+x_{5},\,\ldots,\,x_{i}+x_{1}=x_{2s}+x_{2s+1}\}
), then
\mathcal{S}
would certify exactly
2s
pairs
(i,\bullet)
and exactly
s(s-1)
pairs
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in[i-1]^{2}
. Lemma
4.1
states that
2s
is the maximum possible number of pairs
(i,\bullet)
that
any
collection
\mathcal{S}
of size at most
s
could certify; and Lemma
4.2
states that if
\mathcal{S}
is close to this maximum, then either it must be similar to a star of size
2(s+1)
(in that it must imply a star of nearly this size), or the number of pairs
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in[i-1]^{2}
that it certifies must be substantially smaller.

Lemma 4.3
.

Fix
i\in[k]
, and let
\mathcal{S}
be a collection of difference equalities containing
x_{i}
and
\mathcal{R}
a collection of difference equalities not containing
x_{i}
such that the following conditions hold.

•

\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{S}
is
c
-good and linearly independent.

•

Every difference equality containing
x_{i}
that is implied by
\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{S}
is actually implied by
\mathcal{S}
alone.

Then the number of pairs certified by
\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{S}
but not
\mathcal{S}
is at most
2048\left\lvert\mathcal{R}\right\rvert^{2}
.

When we apply Lemma
4.3
,
\mathcal{S}
will be as in Lemma
4.2
, and
\mathcal{R}
will be much smaller than
\mathcal{S}
(i.e., we will have
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert\leq s
and
\left\lvert\mathcal{R}\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon s
). Lemma
4.2
gives us a bound slightly smaller than
s^{2}
for the number of pairs certified by
\mathcal{S}
alone; the intuition behind Lemma
4.3
is that it means taking
\mathcal{R}
into account will have very little effect on this bound (it will only give us
2048\varepsilon^{2}s^{2}
extra pairs, which is much smaller than
s^{2}
).

Finally, for Step
3
, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{x_{1}+x_{2}=x_{3}+x_{4},\,x_{1}+x_{2}=x_{5}+x_{6},\,\ldots,\,x_{1}+x_{2}=x_{2p-1}+x_{2p}\}
, and fix
i>2p
. Let
\mathcal{S}
be a collection of difference equalities containing
x_{i}
such that
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{P}
is independent and
c
-good and
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{P}
does not imply
x_{1}+x_{2}=x_{i}+x_{j}
for any
j>2p
. Then
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{P}
certifies at most
6\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert
pairs
(i,\bullet)
.

The intuition behind Lemma
4.4
is that it describes what happens when we start with a huge star and try to add a small number of equations to it (when we apply Lemma
4.4
,
\mathcal{S}
will be very small). Specifically, it states that if we have a variable
x_{i}
and try to introduce it into our picture without making it part of the huge star, then we can only certify very few pairs
(i,\bullet)
.

In the remainder of this section, we will prove Lemma
2.4
assuming these lemmas. In Section
5
, we will present some preliminary observations which will be useful for the proofs of several of these lemmas. We will then prove Lemmas
4.1
and
4.2
in Section
6
, Lemma
4.3
in Section
7
, and Lemma
4.4
in Section
8
.

4.2.
A baseline bound

As described in Step
1
, the first step of the argument is to prove a slightly weaker bound on the number of pairs that
\mathcal{C}
certifies.

Lemma 4.5
.

If the solution space of
\mathcal{C}
has dimension
d
, then
\mathcal{C}
certifies at most
(k-d)(k-d+1)
pairs.

Proof.

First, imagine that we put
\mathcal{C}
into row echelon form. This splits the variables
x_{1}
, …,
x_{k}
into sets of free and non-free variables, such that in order to construct a solution to
\mathcal{C}
, we can choose the values of the free variables arbitrarily, and these values uniquely determine the values of the non-free variables. In particular, the number of free variables must be exactly
d
; so by renaming variables, we can assume without loss of generality that the free variables are precisely
x_{1}
, …,
x_{d}
.

Then
\mathcal{C}
cannot imply any difference equalities on
x_{1}
, …,
x_{d}
, so for each
i\leq d
, the number of pairs
(i,\bullet)
certified by
\mathcal{C}
is simply
0
.

Meanwhile, for each
i>d
, we can bound the number of pairs
(i,\bullet)
certified by
\mathcal{C}
as follows: Fix
i
, and let
\mathcal{S}
be a maximal linearly independent set of difference equalities on
x_{1}
, …,
x_{i}
involving
x_{i}
that are implied by
\mathcal{C}
. This means every difference equality on
x_{1}
, …,
x_{i}
involving
x_{i}
that is implied by
\mathcal{C}
is in fact implied by
\mathcal{S}
(otherwise we could add it to
\mathcal{S}
while preserving independence); so every pair
(i,\bullet)
certified by
\mathcal{C}
is in fact certified by
\mathcal{S}
. But we must have
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert\leq i-d
— this is because
x_{1}
, …,
x_{d}
are all free variables, so
\mathcal{C}
cannot imply more than
i-d
linearly independent equations on
x_{1}
, …,
x_{i}
. Then Lemma
4.1
means that
\mathcal{S}
(and therefore
\mathcal{C}
) certifies at most
2(i-d)
pairs
(i,\bullet)
.

Finally, summing over all
i
, the total number of pairs that
\mathcal{C}
certifies is at most

2\cdot 1+2\cdot 2+\cdots+2\cdot(k-d)=(k-d)(k-d+1).\qed

Recall that our ultimate goal is to prove that
\mathcal{C}
certifies at most
(k^{2}-2k)/4
pairs (this is the number of pairs certified by a star of size
k
; a star of size
k
achieves equality in Lemma
4.5
with
d=k/2-1
).

To see how Lemma
4.5
compares to the desired bound, note that if the dimension of the solution space of
\mathcal{C}
is
d
, then
\mathcal{C}
implies some collection of
k-d
linearly independent equations (which contain at most
k
variables); since
\mathcal{C}
is
c
-good, this means

k\geq c(k-d)+1,

which rearranges to
k-d\leq(k-1)/c
. If
k
is small with respect to
2-c
, then we have
(k-1)/c<k/2
, so
k-d\leq k/2-1
. Then Lemma
4.5
immediately gives the desired bound, and we are done.

So for the remainder of the argument, we can assume that
k
is large (more precisely, that
k\geq k_{0}
— we defined parameters so that
2-c
is small with respect to
k_{0}
). Then Lemma
4.5
gives a bound of

(k-d)(k-d+1)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{k-1}{c}\right\rfloor\left(\left\lfloor\frac{k-1}{c}\right\rfloor+1\right)\leq\frac{k^{2}}{c^{2}}.

This differs from the desired bound of
(k^{2}-2k)/4
by a tiny constant factor (which we can make arbitrarily small by making
c
sufficiently close to
2
).

4.3.
A stability statement

The next step of the argument, as described in Step
2
, is to prove a stability-type statement — that if
\mathcal{C}
is even
close
to the weaker bound of
k^{2}/c^{2}
that we get from Lemma
4.5
, then it must be similar to a star of size
k
(in that it must imply a star of size nearly
k
).

Lemma 4.6
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{C}
does not imply any star of size at least
(1-17\varepsilon)k
. Then the number of pairs that it certifies is at most

\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8}\right)\cdot\frac{k^{2}}{c^{2}}.

Proof.

Let
d
be the dimension of the solution space of
\mathcal{C}
(which satisfies
d\leq(k-1)/c
). As in the proof of Lemma
4.5
, imagine that we put
\mathcal{C}
into row echelon form and rename variables so that
x_{1}
, …,
x_{d}
are free (i.e., in order to construct a solution to
\mathcal{C}
, we can choose the values of
x_{1}
, …,
x_{d}
arbitrarily, and these determine the values of the remaining variables). Then
\mathcal{C}
cannot certify any pairs
(i,\bullet)
with
i\leq d
, and it certifies at most
2(i-d)
pairs
(i,\bullet)
for each
i>d
(by the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
4.5
). We say an index
i>d
is
near-saturated
if
\mathcal{C}
certifies at least
2(1-\varepsilon)(i-d)
pairs
(i,\bullet)
.

Case 1
(The largest near-saturated index
i
satisfies
i-d\geq(1-\varepsilon)k/2
).
Here, the main idea is that we will use Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
to save a
(1-\varepsilon)
-factor on the number of pairs
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in[i]^{2}
that
\mathcal{C}
certifies, compared to the bound of
2\cdot 1+\cdots+2\cdot(i-d)=(i-d)(i-d+1)
from Lemma
4.5
. Meanwhile, we will use the fact that no indices past
i
are near-saturated to individually save a
(1-\varepsilon)
-factor on the number of pairs
(i^{\prime},\bullet)
that
\mathcal{C}
certifies for each
i^{\prime}>i
. Combining these will allow us to save a
(1-\varepsilon)
-factor compared to the bound from Lemma
4.5
.

First let
\mathcal{S}
be a maximal collection of independent difference equalities on
x_{1}
, …,
x_{i}
involving
x_{i}
that are implied by
\mathcal{C}
. Then let
\mathcal{R}
be a maximal collection of difference equalities on
x_{1}
, …,
x_{i-1}
implied by
\mathcal{C}
such that
\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{S}
is independent.

•

The maximality of
\mathcal{S}
means that every difference equality on
x_{1}
, …,
x_{i}
involving
x_{i}
that is implied by
\mathcal{C}
is in fact implied by
\mathcal{S}
(in particular, all pairs
(i,\bullet)
certified by
\mathcal{C}
are actually certified by
\mathcal{S}
).

•

The maximality of
\mathcal{R}
means that every difference equality on
x_{1}
, …,
x_{i-1}
that is implied by
\mathcal{C}
is implied by
\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{S}
(in particular, all pairs
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in[i-1]^{2}
certified by
\mathcal{C}
are certified by
\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{S}
).

First, since
x_{1}
, …,
x_{d}
are free variables,
\mathcal{C}
can imply at most
i-d
independent equations on
x_{1}
, …,
x_{i}
. So letting
s=i-d
, we have
\left\lvert\mathcal{R}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert\leq s
. Furthermore, since
i
is near-saturated,
\mathcal{S}
certifies at least
2(1-\varepsilon)s
pairs
(i,\bullet)
, so Lemma
4.1
means that
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert\geq(1-\varepsilon)s
, and therefore
\left\lvert\mathcal{R}\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon s
.

Then
\mathcal{S}
satisfies the conditions for Lemma
4.2
— the assumption that
k\geq k_{0}
(where
k_{0}
is large with respect to
\varepsilon
) means that
s\geq 1/\varepsilon
, and the assumption that
\mathcal{C}
does not imply a star of size at least
(1-17\varepsilon)k
means that
\mathcal{S}
does not imply a star of size at least
2(1-16\varepsilon)s
, since

2(1-16\varepsilon)s\geq 2(1-16\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{(1-\varepsilon)k}{2}\geq(1-17\varepsilon)k.

So Lemma
4.2
means that
\mathcal{S}
certifies at most
(1-2\varepsilon)s^{2}
pairs
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in[i-1]^{2}
. Meanwhile,
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{S}
satisfy the conditions for Lemma
4.3
, which gives that
\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{S}
certifies at most

2048\left\lvert\mathcal{R}\right\rvert^{2}\leq 2048\varepsilon^{2}s^{2}

additional pairs
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in[i-1]^{2}
(i.e., ones not already certified by
\mathcal{S}
). Finally,
\mathcal{S}
certifies at most
2s
pairs
(i,\bullet)
. So in total, the number of pairs
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in[i]^{2}
certified by
\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{S}
(and therefore by
\mathcal{C}
) is at most

(1-2\varepsilon)s^{2}+2048\varepsilon^{2}s^{2}+2s\leq(1-\varepsilon)s(s+1)

(this inequality again uses the fact that
\varepsilon
is small and
s
is large with respect to
\varepsilon
). Recall that
s=i-d
; so we have indeed saved a
(1-\varepsilon)
-factor on the number of pairs
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in[i]^{2}
that
\mathcal{C}
certifies compared to the bound of
(i-d)(i-d+1)
from Lemma
4.5
.

Finally, for each
i^{\prime}>d
, the fact that
i^{\prime}
is not near-saturated means that
\mathcal{C}
certifies at most
2(1-\varepsilon)(i^{\prime}-d)
pairs
(i^{\prime},\bullet)
. So in total, the number of pairs that
\mathcal{C}
certifies is at most

(1-\varepsilon)(i-d)(i-d+1)+2(1-\varepsilon)((i-d+1)+\cdots+(k-d))=(1-\varepsilon)(k-d)(k-d+1).

Since
k-d\leq(k-1)/c
, this is at most
(1-\varepsilon)k^{2}/c^{2}
, which is smaller than the desired bound.

Case 2
(No indices
i
with
i-d\geq(1-\varepsilon)k/2
are near-saturated).
Here, the idea is that we can save a substantial amount on the number of pairs
(i,\bullet)
that
\mathcal{C}
certifies for every index
i
with
i-d\geq(1-\varepsilon)k/2
, compared to the bound of
2(i-d)
from Lemma
4.5
. And there are reasonably many such indices, so this will give a substantial saving on the total number of pairs that
\mathcal{C}
certifies.

To formalize this, imagine that we compare the two sums

(\text{I})=\sum_{i>d}\#(\text{pairs $(i,\bullet)$ certified by $\mathcal{C}$})\quad\text{and}\quad(\text{II})=2\cdot 1+2\cdot 2+\cdots+2\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{k-1}{c}\right\rfloor.

The first is the number of pairs actually certified by
\mathcal{C}
, and the second is the bound we get from Lemma
4.5
after using the fact that
k-d\leq(k-1)/c
. (In particular, we have
(\text{II})\leq k^{2}/c^{2}
.)

We will compare
(\text{I})
and
(\text{II})
termwise. (It is possible that
(\text{II})
has more terms than
(\text{I})
; in that case, we imagine appending
0
’s to the end of
(\text{I})
.) Every term in
(\text{I})
is at most the corresponding term in
(\text{II})
. For all
(1-\varepsilon)k/2\leq j\leq(k-1)/c
, the
j
th term in
(\text{II})
is
2j
, while the
j
th term in
(\text{I})
is at most

2(1-\varepsilon)j\leq 2j-\frac{\varepsilon k}{2}.

(If
j+d\leq k
, then this follows from the assumption that
i=j+d
is not near-saturated; otherwise, the
j
th term in
(\text{I})
is
0
.) The number of such
j
is at least

\frac{k-1}{c}-\frac{(1-\varepsilon)k}{2}-1\geq\frac{\varepsilon k}{4}

(since
c\leq 2
and we assumed
k\geq k_{0}
, i.e., that
k
is large with respect to
\varepsilon
). So we get that

(\text{I})\leq(\text{II})-\frac{\varepsilon k}{2}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon k}{4}\leq\frac{k^{2}}{c^{2}}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}k^{2}}{8}\leq\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8}\right)\frac{k^{2}}{c^{2}}.\qed

In order to prove Lemma
2.4
, we want to show that
\mathcal{C}
certifies at most
(k^{2}-2k)/4
pairs. Since we chose
2-c
to be small (and assumed
k
is large) with respect to
\varepsilon
, we have

\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8}\right)\cdot\frac{k^{2}}{c^{2}}\leq\frac{k^{2}-2k}{4}.

So if
\mathcal{C}
does not imply a star of size at least
(1-17\varepsilon)k
, then we are done; it remains to consider the case where
\mathcal{C}
does implies a star of this size.

4.4.
The huge-star case

Finally, we handle the case where
\mathcal{C}
implies a huge star, corresponding to Step
3
of the outline. The proof in this case has two components. The first is that
\mathcal{C}
certifies very few pairs
(i,\bullet)
for variables
x_{i}
outside the star; this is captured by Lemma
4.4
. The second is that
\mathcal{C}
cannot certify any extra pairs
within
the star; this is captured by the following lemma.

Lemma 4.7
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{C}
implies a star
\{x_{1}+x_{2}=x_{3}+x_{4}=\cdots=x_{2p-1}+x_{2p}\}
. Then
\mathcal{C}
cannot imply any difference equality on
x_{1}
, …,
x_{2p}
other than the ones implied by this star.

Proof.

We call the variables
x_{2i-1}
and
x_{2i}

opposites
in our star (for
1\leq i\leq p
). Assume for contradiction that
\mathcal{C}
does imply a difference equality
(*)
on
x_{1}
, …,
x_{2p}
which is not implied by our star, and consider the set of variables consisting of the four variables in
(*)
and their opposites. This set contains
2t
variables for some
2\leq t\leq 4
(where
t
depends on how many pairs of opposites appear in
(*)
).

But the star implies
t-1
independent equations on these
2t
variables, namely the equations stating that each pair of opposites has equal sum. (For example, if
(*)
is the equation
x_{1}-x_{5}=x_{9}-x_{7}
, then we would take the
8
variables
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
,
x_{5}
,
x_{6}
,
x_{7}
,
x_{8}
,
x_{9}
,
x_{10}
and the
3
equations
x_{1}+x_{2}=x_{5}+x_{6}
,
x_{1}+x_{2}=x_{7}+x_{8}
, and
x_{1}+x_{2}=x_{9}+x_{10}
.) And
(*)
provides another independent equation on these variables, so this means
\mathcal{C}
implies
t
independent equations on
2t
variables. Since
t\leq 4
and
c
is close to
2
, we have
2t<ct+1
, so this contradicts the fact that
\mathcal{C}
is
c
-good.
∎

By combining Lemmas
4.4
and
4.7
, we obtain the following bound for the huge-star case.

Lemma 4.8
.

Suppose that the largest star implied by
\mathcal{C}
has size
2p
for some
(1-17\varepsilon)k\leq 2p\leq k
. Then
\mathcal{C}
certifies at most
p(k/2-1)
pairs.

Proof.

By renaming variables, we can assume that the largest star implied by
\mathcal{C}
is

\{x_{1}+x_{2}=x_{3}+x_{4}=\cdots=x_{2p-1}+x_{2p}\}.

Then by Lemma
4.7
, the number of pairs
(i,j)\in[2p]^{2}
certified by
\mathcal{C}
is exactly the number of pairs certified by this star, which is
p^{2}-p
(as computed in Example
2.1
).

Meanwhile, for each
i>2p
, we bound the number of pairs
(i,\bullet)
that
\mathcal{C}
certifies using Lemma
4.4
. Let

\mathcal{P}=\{x_{1}+x_{2}=x_{3}+x_{4},\,x_{1}+x_{2}=x_{5}+x_{6},\,\ldots,x_{1}+x_{2}=x_{2p-1}+x_{2p}\}

be as in the statement of Lemma
4.4
, and let
\mathcal{S}
be a maximal collection of difference equalities involving
x_{i}
implied by
\mathcal{C}
such that
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{P}
is linearly independent. The maximality of
\mathcal{S}
means that every pair
(i,\bullet)
certified by
\mathcal{C}
is certified by
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{P}
. Meanwhile, since
\mathcal{C}
is
c
-good and
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{P}
is linearly independent and contains at most
k
variables, we have

\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mathcal{P}\right\rvert\leq\frac{k-1}{c},

and since
\left\lvert\mathcal{P}\right\rvert=p-1\geq(1-17\varepsilon)k/2-1
, this means

\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert\leq\frac{k-1}{c}-\frac{(1-17\varepsilon)k}{2}+1\leq 10\varepsilon k\leq 24\varepsilon p

(since
2-c
is small with respect to
\varepsilon
, and we assumed
k
is large with respect to
\varepsilon
).

We say a

is a collection of independent difference equalities
\{(*_{1}),\ldots,(*_{t})\}
which minimally implies a difference equality
(*)
, and we say
(*)
is
produced
by this minimal implication. In all our technical lemmas, we start with a collection of difference equalities with certain properties, and we want to understand the pairs that this collection certifies; this means we want to understand what other difference equalities it implies. We will typically do so by looking at minimal implications, and we will often use the results of this section to make claims about the structure of those minimal implications.

The first observation is that if we have a
constant-sized
collection of difference equalities which we know is
c
-good, then we can actually say it is
2
-good.

Claim 5.1
.

If
\mathcal{T}
is a set of difference equalities with
\left\lvert\mathcal{T}\right\rvert<1/(2-c)
and
\mathcal{T}
is
c
-good, then
\mathcal{T}
is
2
-good.

Proof.

By definition,
\mathcal{T}
is
c
-light if and only if every
t
linearly independent equations implied by
\mathcal{T}
together contain at least
ct+1
variables. But if
\mathcal{T}
implies
t
linearly independent equations, we must have
t\leq\left\lvert\mathcal{T}\right\rvert
. And for all such values of
t
, we have
ct+1>2t
, which means that requiring a collection of
t
equations to contain at least
ct+1
variables is equivalent to requiring it to contain at least
2t+1
variables.

So if
\mathcal{T}
is
c
-light then it is also
2
-light; and the remaining conditions in the definition of being
c
-good (being valid and collinearity-free) do not depend on
c
.
∎

and
m=\lfloor e^{\sqrt{\log n}}/16\kappa\rfloor
.

First, for any
\vec{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})\in[m]^{d}
, we have
v_{1}^{2}+\cdots+v_{d}^{2}\leq dm^{2}
. So we can choose some
1\leq r\leq dm^{2}
for which the set

\mathbb{S}=\{\vec{v}\in[m]^{d}\mid v_{1}^{2}+\cdots+v_{d}^{2}=r\}

has size at least
m^{d}/dm^{2}=m^{d-2}/d
. Now let
\varphi\colon\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{Z}
be the map

\vec{v}\mapsto v_{1}+(16\kappa m)v_{2}+(16\kappa m)^{2}v_{3}+\cdots+(16\kappa m)^{d-1}v_{d}.

Then
\varphi
is injective on
[m]^{d}
(by the uniqueness of base-
16\kappa m
expansion), and for all
\vec{v}\in[m]^{d}
, we have

1\leq\varphi(\vec{v})\leq(16\kappa m)^{d}\leq e^{\sqrt{\log n}\cdot\sqrt{\log n}}=n.

Now take
S
to be
\varphi(\mathbb{S})
. First we will show that
S
does avoid the linear patterns that we wish to avoid. Assume not; this means there exist distinct points
\vec{u},\vec{v},\vec{w}\in\mathbb{S}
such that

\alpha\varphi(\vec{u})+\beta\varphi(\vec{v})+\gamma\varphi(\vec{w})=0

for some nonzero
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}
of magnitude at most
\kappa
with sum
0
. Plugging in the definition of
\varphi
, this means

\sum_{i=1}^{d}(16\kappa m)^{i-1}(\alpha u_{i}+\beta v_{i}+\gamma w_{i})=0.

But we have
-3\kappa m\leq\alpha u_{i}+\beta v_{i}+\gamma w_{i}\leq 3\kappa m
for each
i
, so by the uniqueness of base-
16\kappa m
expansion, this means we must have

\alpha u_{i}+\beta v_{i}+\gamma w_{i}=0

for all
i
. But this means
\vec{u}
,
\vec{v}
, and
\vec{w}
are collinear, which is impossible because
\vec{u}
,
\vec{v}
, and
\vec{w}
lie on a sphere (and a line can only intersect a given sphere at most twice).

So we have shown that
S
indeed avoids the forbidden linear patterns; it remains to show that
\left\lvert S\right\rvert=n^{1-o(1)}
, or equivalently that
\log\left\lvert S\right\rvert=(1-o(1))\log n
. For this, we have
\left\lvert S\right\rvert=\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert\geq m^{d-2}/d
, which means

\log\left\lvert S\right\rvert\geq(d-2)\log m-\log d.

Since
d\geq\sqrt{\log n}-1
and
m\geq e^{\sqrt{\log n}}/32\kappa
, we can write
(d+1)\log(32\kappa m)\geq\sqrt{\log n}\cdot\sqrt{\log n}=\log n
, so

\displaystyle\log\left\lvert S\right\rvert

\displaystyle\geq(d+1)\log(32\kappa m)-(d+1)\log 32\kappa-3\log m-\log d

\displaystyle\geq\log n-(d+1)\log(32\kappa)-3\log m-\log d.

Each of the terms being subtracted is
O(\sqrt{\log n})
(with the implicit constant depending on
\kappa
), so
\log\left\lvert S\right\rvert=\log n-O(\sqrt{\log n})=(1-o(1))\log n
, as desired.
