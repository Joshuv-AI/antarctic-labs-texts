Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome following severe postpartum hemorrhage: An atypical diffuse MRI pattern of brain involvement
Authors: Said Abdi Mohamed, Abdisalam Ismail Hassan, Abdullahi Ali Roble, Marian Ali Abdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPosterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) is a rare neurological complication that may occur in the setting of severe postpartum hemorrhage (PPH). We report the case of a 28-year-old grand multiparous woman with a term twin pregnancy who developed severe PPH during an emergency cesarean section, complicated by hemodynamic instability and metabolic acidosis. Postoperatively, she developed seizures, aphasia, and generalized weakness. Brain magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated bilateral diffuse cortical and subcortical hyperintense signal abnormalities involving the frontoparietal and occipital lobes on FLAIR and T2-weighted sequences, with mild associated diffusion restriction, findings consistent with an atypical diffuse pattern of PRES and associated hypoxic-ischemic brain injury. The patient required prolonged intensive care, including ventilatory support, blood transfusions, antiepileptic therapy, and management of cerebral edema. Gradual neurological improvement was observed following multidisciplinary care and rehabilitation. This case highlights the importance of early recognition, prompt neuroimaging, and timely multidisciplinary management of neurological complications following severe PPH to improve outcomes.Keywords: Postpartum hemorrhage, Twin pregnancy, Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome, Quadriparesis, Case report

Introduction

Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome is a clinic-radiological disorder characterized by acute neurological symptoms, including seizures, altered mental status, visual disturbances, and focal neurological deficits, accompanied by characteristic neuroimaging findings [1]. The condition is most commonly associated with hypertensive emergencies, pre-eclampsia/eclampsia, renal dysfunction, immunosuppressive therapy, and systemic endothelial injury [2]. Although uncommon, PRES may also occur following severe postpartum hemorrhage, likely as a consequence of hemodynamic instability, cerebral autoregulatory dysfunction, and endothelial damage. Magnetic resonance imaging is the imaging modality of choice for diagnosing posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome. Typical MRI findings include bilateral symmetrical vasogenic edema appearing as hyperintense signal abnormalities on T2-weighted and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery sequences, predominantly involving the parieto-occipital regions [3]. However, atypical imaging patterns have been increasingly recognized and may demonstrate diffuse cerebral involvement, diffusion restriction, hemorrhage, or contrast enhancement, which can complicate diagnosis and broaden the differential considerations.

We present the case of a 28-year-old grand multiparous woman with a term twin pregnancy who developed severe PPH following an emergency cesarean section. Brain MRI demonstrated bilateral diffuse cortical and subcortical hyperintense signal abnormalities involving the frontoparietal and occipital lobes on FLAIR and T2-weighted sequences, with corresponding mild diffusion restriction on apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps, consistent with an atypical diffuse pattern of PRES. This case highlights the important role of MRI in identifying atypical manifestations of PRES and facilitating timely diagnosis and management in patients with neurological deterioration following severe postpartum hemorrhage.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old woman, gravida 7 para 6, at 37 weeks of gestation with a confirmed twin pregnancy, was referred to our center with a 2-day history of labor pain. At presentation, her vital signs were notable for a blood pressure of 156/83 mmHg, a pulse rate of 87 beats per minute, a temperature of 37.5°C, and an oxygen saturation of 97% on room air. She had not attended any antenatal care visits, as she came from a rural area with limited access to primary healthcare services.

On admission, she was conscious but disoriented, with impaired awareness of time, place, and person, and had a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 14/15. Clinical examination revealed a term uterus consistent with twin gestation. Obstetric ultrasound confirmed a viable twin pregnancy at 37 weeks, with the first twin in cephalic presentation and the second in breech presentation. Both fetuses demonstrated positive fetal heart activity, and vaginal examination indicated advanced labor.

Preoperative laboratory evaluation revealed severe anemia (hemoglobin 7 g/dL), leukocytosis (white blood cell count 18.9 × 10³/mm³), a platelet count of 107 × 10³/mm³, and an elevated C-reactive protein level (19 mg/L). Renal function tests and coagulation parameters were within normal limits. Given prolonged labor, twin gestation, and grand multiparity, the patient underwent an emergency cesarean section under general anesthesia. Intraoperative laboratory investigations revealed worsening anemia (hemoglobin 5.4 g/dL), moderate thrombocytopenia (platelet count 90 × 10³/mm³), leukocytosis (white blood cell count 20.75 × 10³/mm³), and elevated inflammatory markers. Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated severe metabolic acidosis with associated hypoxemia. The intraoperative course was complicated by severe postpartum hemorrhage requiring resuscitative measures, blood product transfusion, correction of metabolic derangements, and management of hypertension. Two live neonates were delivered successfully with good Apgar scores. Postoperatively, the patient remained intubated and was transferred to the intensive care unit for close monitoring. Despite supportive management, she remained clinically unstable. On the second postoperative day, she developed acute neurological deficits, including aphasia, generalized weakness, and a seizure episode. A neurology consultation was requested, and brain MRI was performed for further evaluation. Brain MRI, including FLAIR and T2-weighted sequences, demonstrated bilateral symmetrical cortical and subcortical hyperintense signal abnormalities predominantly involving the frontoparietal and occipital lobes without significant mass effect (Fig. 1). Corresponding regions showed mild diffusion restriction, suggesting predominantly vasogenic edema with focal cytotoxic components. In correlation with the clinical presentation and characteristic imaging findings, the features were considered consistent with posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome. Subsequent neurological examination demonstrated quadriparesis, with muscle power graded 0/5 in all 4 limbs. Muscle tone was preserved, and deep tendon reflexes were exaggerated bilaterally. Cranial nerve examination revealed pupils of normal size and reactive to light, suggesting preserved brainstem function. Sensory examination and assessment of higher cortical functions could not be reliably performed because of the patient's impaired level of consciousness and limited cooperation.

Axial brain MRI (A and B: FLAIR; C: T2-weighted) demonstrates bilateral symmetric diffuse cortical and subcortical hyperintense signal abnormalities predominantly affecting the frontoparietal and occipital lobes of both cerebral hemispheres (blue arrows). (D) ADC (apparent diffusion coefficient) map shows corresponding areas of mild diffusion restriction (blue arrows). The imaging findings are consistent with an atypical diffuse pattern of posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome.

The patient remained in the intensive care unit, where she received multidisciplinary supportive care, close neurological monitoring, and rehabilitation interventions. Following a 33-day intensive care unit stay, she was transferred to the neurology ward. At the time of transfer, she remained quadriparetic with muscle strength graded 1/5 but was conscious, able to feed orally, and actively participating in rehabilitation therapy. After 10 days in the neurology ward, she was discharged with continued physiotherapy and outpatient neurological follow-up. At follow-up, she was fully awake and responsive. Neurological assessment demonstrated improvement in upper-limb muscle strength (3/5), while lower-limb strength remained unchanged. Ongoing rehabilitation and neurological follow-up were recommended.

Discussion

Postpartum hemorrhage remains a leading cause of maternal morbidity and mortality worldwide, particularly in low-resource settings where access to continuous antenatal care and emergency obstetric services is limited. Uterine atony is the most frequent cause of PPH, accounting for up to 70%-80% of cases, and is especially common in women with grand multiparity, multiple gestations, prolonged labor, and anemia [4]. In this case, the combination of twin pregnancy, grand multiparity, and lack of antenatal care contributed to increased risk for uterine overdistension, ineffective myometrial contraction, and substantial postpartum blood loss. Twin gestations are associated with increased rates of cesarean delivery and greater need for transfusion compared with singleton pregnancies, likely secondary to both mechanical and physiological challenges in achieving uterine contraction and hemostasis [5]. Severe PPH can compromise systemic perfusion and lead to end-organ ischemia. Hypotension from acute blood loss reduces cerebral perfusion pressure, and prolonged or repeated episodes of hypoperfusion can result in hypoxic-ischemic brain injury. Although hypoxic-ischemic injury is commonly described in the context of neonatal asphyxia, it may also occur in adults following sustained or recurrent systemic hypotension [6]. In the present case, the patient developed diffuse hypoxic-ischemic injury involving bilateral cerebral hemispheres, which was evident on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed after acute neurological deterioration. Magnetic resonance imaging plays a pivotal role in the diagnosis of posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome. While the classic imaging appearance consists of symmetrical vasogenic edema predominantly affecting the parieto-occipital regions, atypical presentations involving more extensive cerebral regions have been increasingly reported. In our patient, brain MRI revealed diffuse bilateral cortical and subcortical hyperintense signal abnormalities involving the frontal, parietal, and occipital lobes on T2-weighted and FLAIR sequences. Mild areas of diffusion restriction were also identified on diffusion-weighted imaging with corresponding low ADC values. These findings were consistent with an atypical diffuse pattern of posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome. Recognition of such atypical MRI manifestations is essential, as they may mimic other neurological conditions and potentially delay diagnosis. This case underscores the value of MRI in establishing the diagnosis of PRES in patients who develop acute neurological deterioration following severe postpartum hemorrhage [7]. Although the precise pathophysiology of Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome remains incompletely understood, endothelial dysfunction and disruptions in cerebral autoregulation are central mechanisms. In circumstances of acute hypertension or rapid blood pressure fluctuations, hyperperfusion and breakdown of the blood-brain barrier may cause extravasation of plasma into the interstitium, producing vasogenic edema [8]. In this patient, systemic stress from hemorrhage, hemodynamic instability, and possible preexisting hypertensive tendencies likely contributed to endothelial injury and the development of bilateral PRES. While Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome is considered a potentially reversible condition with timely intervention, it can coexist with cytotoxic edema and permanent tissue damage in the setting of significant hypoxia and hemodynamic compromise [9].

The neurological manifestations of posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome commonly include seizures, altered levels of consciousness, visual disturbances, and focal neurological deficits. These symptoms often necessitate prompt neuroimaging, particularly magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which typically demonstrates vasogenic edema predominantly affecting the parieto-occipital regions, although other brain areas may also be involved. In some cases, PRES may coexist with hypoxic-ischemic brain injury, especially in the context of severe systemic compromise [10]. Severe neurological deterioration may require advanced supportive measures, including endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation for airway protection. Management of such patients generally requires a multidisciplinary approach involving obstetricians, intensivists, neurologists, and rehabilitation specialists.

In cases of postpartum hemorrhage, initial management focuses on hemodynamic stabilization and correction of underlying causes. This often includes oxygen supplementation, intravenous fluid resuscitation, and transfusion of blood products such as packed red blood cells and fresh frozen plasma. In the intensive care setting, supportive care may include infection control with broad-spectrum antibiotics, measures aimed at reducing cerebral edema; such as osmotic agents, and antiepileptic medications for seizure control [11]. Antibiotics were prescribed as part of routine intensive care management in view of leukocytosis and elevated inflammatory markers; however, there was no definitive evidence of systemic or central nervous system infection. Therefore, infection was not considered the primary cause of PRES. Although severe anemia may have contributed to reduced cerebral oxygen delivery and aggravated the patient's neurological condition, it is not a well-established independent cause of PRES. In contrast, the patient experienced severe postpartum hemorrhage complicated by profound blood loss, hemodynamic instability, hypoxemia, and metabolic acidosis immediately preceding the onset of neurological symptoms. These factors are known to cause endothelial dysfunction, impaired cerebral autoregulation, and cerebral hypoperfusion, which are recognized mechanisms in the development of PRES. Accordingly, severe postpartum hemorrhage was considered the principal precipitating factor for PRES in this case, while anemia and empirical antibiotic therapy represented secondary clinical considerations rather than primary etiological factor.

Early initiation of physiotherapy, including passive range-of-motion exercises, plays an important role in preventing complications such as joint stiffness and muscle contractures.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the potential for severe postpartum hemorrhage in high-risk obstetric settings particularly in grand multiparity and twin gestation to precipitate complex neurological complications, including posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome and hypoxic-ischemic brain injury. It underscores the importance of early recognition of risk factors, prompt hemodynamic stabilization, and timely multidisciplinary management. Furthermore, early neuroimaging and structured rehabilitation play a critical role in improving neurological outcomes. Strengthening access to antenatal care and emergency obstetric services remains essential to reduce the burden of such preventable complications.
