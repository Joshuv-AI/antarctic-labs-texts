Mindfulness, psychological resilience, and cognitive reappraisal on athletic performance: a predictive model based on psychological mechanisms
Authors: Cheng Xing, Zainal Abidin N E, Aman M S
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMindfulness is increasingly viewed as a psychological resource for athletic performance, yet the sequential mechanism through which mindful awareness is translated into performance through cognitive reappraisal and psychological resilience remains insufficiently examined. This study constructed and tested a predictive model linking mindfulness, cognitive reappraisal, psychological resilience, and perceived athletic performance among competitive university athletes.MethodsA cross-sectional questionnaire survey was conducted with 482 competitive university athletes recruited from 19 universities in Sichuan Province, China. Validated scales were used to measure mindfulness, cognitive reappraisal, psychological resilience, and perceived athletic performance. Structural equation modeling was used to examine the hypothesized direct paths, and bias-corrected bootstrapping was applied to test the mediating and sequential mediating effects.ResultsMindfulness was positively associated with athletic performance, cognitive reappraisal, and psychological resilience. Cognitive reappraisal and psychological resilience also positively predicted athletic performance, while cognitive reappraisal positively predicted psychological resilience. Bootstrapping supported the indirect effects of mindfulness on athletic performance through cognitive reappraisal and psychological resilience. The sequential mediation pathway from mindfulness to cognitive reappraisal, psychological resilience, and athletic performance was significant but modest, indicating a multi-stage self-regulatory process.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that mindfulness may enhance athletic performance not only directly but also through emotion-regulation and resilience-building mechanisms. By clarifying the pathway from mindful awareness to cognitive reappraisal, psychological resilience, and performance, this study extends the Mindfulness-to-Meaning perspective to competitive sport and provides evidence for staged psychological training programmes that integrate mindfulness, cognitive reframing, and resilience development.Keywords: Mindfulness, Cognitive reappraisal, Psychological resilience, Athletic performance, Structural equation modeling

Introduction

Achieving optimal athletic performance requires more than physical conditioning and technical proficiency. In recent years, growing empirical attention has shifted toward understanding how psychological mechanisms influence athletes' capacity to perform under pressure, adapt to adversity, and maintain consistency in high-stakes environments [43]. This shift reflects not only the increasing recognition of mental health in elite sport, but also the evolving need for evidence-based psychological frameworks that address the complexity of performance optimization beyond conventional physical training paradigms [23].

Among various psychological constructs, mindfulness has emerged as a key contributor to athletes’ mental adaptability and performance consistency. Mindfulness, characterized by an awareness of the present moment combined with an accepting and non-evaluative mindset, has been associated with improvements in emotional regulation, focused attention, and the ability to manage stress in demanding performance settings. [31, 54]. It is increasingly applied in sport psychology to buffer against performance anxiety and to foster psychological flexibility during competition [3].

In parallel, psychological resilience—the capacity to recover and maintain functioning after adversity—has gained prominence as a core protective factor for athletes navigating intensive training and competitive stressors [24, 46]. Resilience enables athletes to adaptively interpret setbacks, sustain effort, and maintain motivation under fluctuating performance conditions [15]. However, despite the recognized benefits of both mindfulness and resilience, their interdependence in shaping athletic outcomes remains conceptually underdeveloped and underexplored in empirical research.

Cognitive reappraisal has also gained growing attention as a key adaptive strategy for regulating emotions, involving the reinterpretation of emotionally charged situations to alter their psychological impact [55]. Reappraisal has demonstrated efficacy in reducing sport-related anxiety and preserving performance under pressure [10, 52]. Notably, mindfulness may act as a meta-cognitive precursor to reappraisal, facilitating athletes’ ability to flexibly reinterpret stress-inducing scenarios through enhanced emotional awareness and cognitive decentering [14].

Although mindfulness, cognitive reappraisal, and psychological resilience have each been associated with improved performance outcomes, prior studies have tended to examine these constructs separately or through single-mediator models. It therefore remains unclear whether cognitive reappraisal and psychological resilience function as parallel mediators or form a sequential pathway through which mindfulness is translated into athletic performance. Prior studies have largely examined these factors in isolation, leaving a critical gap in understanding how they interact to support psychological functioning and performance optimization in athletic contexts.

To bridge this research gap, the current study introduces a predictive model in which cognitive reappraisal and psychological resilience are conceptualized as mediating variables linking mindfulness to athletic performance. Drawing upon third-wave cognitive-behavioral theories and mindfulness-acceptance-commitment frameworks, this research aims to clarify the underlying regulatory mechanisms that translate mindfulness into tangible performance benefits [22]. Specifically, the study investigates:

How does mindfulness influence cognitive reappraisal, psychological resilience, and athletic performance?

In what ways do cognitive reappraisal and psychological resilience function as mediators in the relationship between mindfulness and athletic performance?

Through the development and empirical validation of this model, the study enhances the nuanced understanding of psychological regulation in athletic contexts and provides practical guidance for designing interventions aimed at strengthening athletes’ mental preparedness, emotional flexibility, and performance under pressure.

Theoretical background and hypotheses

Theoretical foundation

The theoretical foundation of this study is built on an integrated self-regulatory perspective that explains how mindfulness may be translated into athletic performance through cognitive and resilience-related mechanisms. Mindfulness-related sport psychology provides the general basis for understanding mindfulness as a psychological resource that enables athletes to remain aware, accepting, and task-focused under competitive pressure [4]. The Mindfulness-Acceptance-Commitment framework further supports the application of mindfulness in sport by emphasizing experiential acceptance and psychological flexibility as key processes through which athletes can remain engaged with performance tasks despite stress or setbacks [16]. Attentional Control Theory also helps explain why mindfulness is relevant to performance, as mindful awareness may reduce task-irrelevant thoughts and support more stable attentional allocation in high-pressure situations [26]. These perspectives together justify the position of mindfulness as the upstream psychological resource in the proposed model.

The pathway from mindfulness to athletic performance is further clarified through the Mindfulness-to-Meaning Theory, which argues that mindful awareness creates psychological distance from automatic emotional reactions and thereby facilitates constructive reappraisal of stressful experiences [17]. In competitive sport, this means that athletes with higher mindfulness may be better able to reinterpret mistakes, pressure, or temporary setbacks as manageable challenges rather than fixed threats. Cognitive reappraisal can then contribute to psychological resilience because repeated constructive reinterpretation may reduce the perceived threat of stressful events, strengthen athletes’ sense of controllability, and help them accumulate adaptive coping resources. Over time, this process supports emotional stability, recovery from adversity, and sustained effort under changing performance conditions. [49]. From an SDT-informed perspective, this self-regulatory process may also strengthen athletes’ perceived competence and autonomous control when coping with competitive demands [5]. Therefore, these theories are integrated in this study not as separate explanatory frameworks, but as complementary components of a staged mechanism: mindfulness supports attentional stability and cognitive distance, cognitive reappraisal transforms stressful experiences into adaptive meanings, psychological resilience reflects the accumulation of adaptive coping capacity, and athletic performance represents the behavioral outcome of this self-regulatory process.

Mindfulness as a psychological resource in athletic performance

Mindfulness is understood as the intentional and self-directed ability to focus on the present moment while maintaining an open, accepting, and non-judgmental perspective [30]. Within the field of sport, mindfulness has gained growing recognition as a psychological resource because athletes are required to sustain attention, regulate emotional responses, and cope with pressure during training and competition [4]. Competitive performance often involves uncertainty, mistakes, evaluation pressure, physical fatigue, and rapidly changing situational cues. In such contexts, mindfulness may help athletes notice internal experiences without immediately becoming dominated by anxiety, rumination, or automatic emotional reactions. This non-reactive awareness is important because athletes who can remain attentive and composed under pressure are more likely to maintain task engagement and execute performance-relevant skills effectively [16].

Beyond its direct relevance to performance, mindfulness may also create the psychological conditions necessary for adaptive cognitive and emotional regulation. By enhancing present-moment awareness and metacognitive monitoring, mindfulness allows athletes to observe stressful experiences with greater cognitive distance, which may support the reinterpretation of pressure, mistakes, or temporary performance decline in a more constructive manner [14]. This process is closely related to cognitive reappraisal, as reappraisal requires individuals to modify the meaning assigned to emotionally charged situations before negative emotions become fully intensified [17]. In athletic settings, the ability to reinterpret competitive stressors as manageable challenges rather than fixed threats may further help athletes preserve motivation, regain emotional balance, and sustain effort after setbacks.

Empirical studies also support the role of mindfulness in sport-related psychological functioning. Mindfulness-based interventions have been shown to improve athletes’ sport-specific mindfulness, emotion regulation, and self-rated athletic performance [28]. Research on mindfulness and psychological health further suggests that mindfulness is associated with adaptive coping and reduced maladaptive emotional reactions, both of which are closely connected to resilience development [32]. In competitive sport, where athletes must repeatedly face losses, training demands, injury risk, and performance fluctuations, such regulatory capacities are particularly important for maintaining psychological resilience and consistent functioning [13]. Accordingly, mindfulness can be conceptualized as an upstream psychological resource that may contribute to athletic performance while also supporting cognitive reappraisal and psychological resilience. Athletes’ use of cognitive reappraisal can significantly impact their emotional regulation and, subsequently, their performance [45]. Cognitive reappraisal represents an emotion regulation strategy that operates prior to the full activation of an emotional response, targeting the interpretation of the triggering event, which allows individuals to change their interpretation of stress-inducing events and mitigate negative emotional responses [6]. Within the realm of sport psychology, athletes who engage in cognitive reappraisal tend to exhibit greater emotional stability and remain composed during high-pressure situations,adjust their thinking during setbacks, and remain focused on performance tasks [11]. For instance, Josefsson et al. [28] showed that athletes who practiced mindfulness-based strategies—of which reappraisal is a core mechanism—demonstrated superior attentional control and competitive performance. Further, Noetel et al. [40] identified cognitive reappraisal as a critical mediating factor between mindfulness and athletic performance, indicating that reframing stressful experiences contributes to enhanced outcomes in competitive settings.

Psychological resilience denotes an individual’s capacity to bounce back from difficulties, manage emotional responses effectively, and respond constructively to demanding circumstances [13]. In athletic settings, individuals with higher resilience are generally more capable of handling both the physical strain and mental pressures associated with rigorous training and competitive performance. Fletcher and Sarkar [13] indicated that mentally tough athletes, characterized by higher resilience, showed better coping capacity and emotional stability in the face of athletic stress. According to Appleton et al. [1], athletes exhibiting greater psychological resilience are more likely to achieve higher levels of performance and maintain consistency when facing competitive pressure. Lai et al. [33] further observed a positive correlation between psychological resilience and athletes’ self-efficacy, as well as their overall success in sports performance. Together, these findings suggest that resilience is an essential psychological trait contributing to optimal athletic functioning.

Meanwhile, research has consistently demonstrated that cognitive reappraisal plays a crucial role in fostering psychological resilience. Tugade and Fredrickson [49] found that individuals who frequently apply cognitive reappraisal tend to experience more positive emotions, gradually accumulate psychological resources, and develop effective coping mechanisms over time. Keng et al. [32] emphasized that mindfulness-based interventions promote reappraisal processes, which subsequently lead to enhanced resilience when dealing with emotional challenges. In the sports context, Campoamor-Olegario et al. [7] showed that athletes who scored higher in hope and reappraisal also exhibited better stress-coping capabilities—attributes closely linked with psychological resilience. The evidence indicates that athletes capable of reframing challenging experiences through cognitive reappraisal are more likely to cultivate and sustain resilient mental states. Mindfulness has a positive effect on athletic performance by enhancing emotional regulation, attentional control, and metacognitive awareness [36]. Cognitive reappraisal serves as a central pathway by which mindfulness generates positive outcomes, enabling individuals to reframe stress-inducing experiences in ways that alleviate emotional strain [17]. Mindfulness-to-Meaning Theory reveals the mindfulness promotes decentering and metacognitive insight, which in turn foster reappraisal processes that lead to more adaptive emotional responses. Keng et al. [32] emphasized that mindfulness interrupts automatic cognitive reactions such as rumination and facilitates the use of reappraisal to reinterpret competition-related stress in more constructive ways. Noetel et al. [40] provided evidence that cognitive reappraisal played a significant mediating role in linking mindfulness-based interventions to athletes’ performance improvements. Similarly, Moore et al. [38] found that athletes who adopted reappraisal as a strategy in response to performance anxiety were more likely to maintain focus and achieve competitive goals. These results imply that cognitive reappraisal could function as a vital psychological mechanism through which mindfulness contributes to enhanced athletic performance.

In addition to performance, Mindfulness has likewise been linked to psychological resilience, defined as the capacity to cope successfully with stress and rebound from adversity [41]. According to the Mindfulness-to-Meaning Theory, mindfulness promotes ongoing cycles of constructive cognitive reappraisal, which over time contribute to the development of emotional steadiness and greater psychological resilience [18]. Reappraisal enables individuals to reconstruct adverse experiences as opportunities for learning and growth, which contributes to the development of resilient traits [44]. In sport contexts, Lelek-Kratiuk and Szczygieł [34] showed that athletes who were better at reframing stressors using reappraisal demonstrated stronger resilience and stress-coping abilities. Mindfulness practice, by promoting a non-reactive and accepting stance toward experiences, enhances reappraisal tendencies, which in turn improve athletes’ ability to handle adversity with greater composure and psychological strength [58]. Both theoretical frameworks and empirical evidence affirm the mediating function of cognitive reappraisal in linking mindfulness to psychological resilience. Among these, psychological resilience has emerged as a crucial mediator that connects mindfulness to performance outcomes. The Mindfulness-Acceptance-Commitment (MAC) model posits that mindfulness enhances psychological flexibility and fosters acceptance—two critical components for building resilience in demanding competitive settings [28]. Fletcher and Sarkar [13] observed that athletes with higher resilience tend to rebound from challenges more rapidly, preserve self-confidence, and consistently perform at a high level. Tugade and Fredrickson [50] further theorized that mindfulness indirectly enhances well-being through positive emotion regulation processes that build resilience over time. Zhang et al. Materials and methods

Data collection

Mixing the convenience sampling and purposive sampling strategy was used to recruit athletes from 19 universities across Sichuan Province, China. Eligible participants were student-athletes with confirmed experience in provincial or national competitions. Participants were drawn from both team-based sports (such as football, basketball, and volleyball) and individual disciplines (including track and field, swimming, gymnastics, martial arts, tennis, and table tennis). Out of 535 questionnaires administered via both online platforms and printed formats, 482 were deemed valid after removing 53 incomplete or invalid responses, resulting in a final response rate of 90.1%. Prior to initiating data collection, coaches and institutional coordinators were briefed on the purpose of the study and collaborated in selecting eligible athlete participants. Participants were provided with a written summary outlining the study’s purpose, gave informed consent, and were clearly informed of their right to withdraw at any stage. Data collection was conducted anonymously, with all information treated in strict confidence.

Instrument

The questionnaire consisted of four validated scales corresponding to the core constructs: mindfulness, cognitive reappraisal, psychological resilience, and perceived athletic performance.

To measure mindfulness, the study employed the State Mindfulness Scale for Physical Activity (SMS-PA-2) [51], comprising 19 items distributed across four distinct subscales: These subdimensions include awareness of mental processes, bodily sensations, acceptance of thoughts, and acceptance of physical experiences. Participants responded using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 4 (“very much”). This instrument has shown strong internal consistency and has been validated for use in physical activity contexts [2]. The 25-item Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) was utilized to assess psychological resilience, encompassing five dimensions: personal competence, instinctual trust, adaptability to change, sense of control, and spiritual orientation [9]. The Chinese version has shown good psychometric performance in athlete populations [56]. Cognitive reappraisal was measured through a six-item subscale from the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ), which was localized and psychometrically validated in the Chinese context by Wang et al. [53]. Participants rated their responses on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 7 (“strongly agree”). The scale has exhibited strong reliability, with a test–retest coefficient of 0.82 and a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85. To evaluate athletic performance, the study employed the Perceived Performance in Sport Questionnaire (PPSQ), originally created by Charbonneau (2001) and later adapted by Lourenço et al. [35]. This instrument comprises five items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”), where higher scores reflect greater perceived performance. The scale has demonstrated high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values consistently between 0.88 and 0.91.

Pilot testing

Before administering the survey on a larger scale, a preliminary test involving 30 athletes was carried out to assess the translated questionnaires in terms of clarity, contextual relevance, and cultural suitability. Feedback was solicited on potential ambiguities and revised accordingly. The reliability assessment showed that each subscale attained a Cronbach’s alpha exceeding 0.80, indicating strong internal consistency and justifying the instruments’ applicability in the main research phase.

Data processing and analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0 alongside AMOS version 24.0 for structural modeling procedures. Preliminary analyses involved computing descriptive statistics and examining the dataset for normal distribution, missing data, and potential outliers. To evaluate the measurement model, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted, examining factor loadings, composite reliability (CR), average variance extracted (AVE), and various model fit indices. Following this, structural equation modeling (SEM) was applied to test the proposed direct effects, mediation paths, and sequential mediation processes. Model fit was evaluated using a range of indices, including χ2/df, RMSEA, GFI, AGFI, CFI, and TLI. To examine both mediating and sequential mediation effects involving cognitive reappraisal and psychological resilience, a bootstrap procedure with 5,000 resamples and bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals was employed.

Results

Descriptive analysis

A total of 482 individuals participated in the study, shown in Table 1, with a gender distribution slightly skewed toward males (56%) compared to females (44%). In terms of academic standing, the majority of respondents were in their third year (40%), followed by seniors (21.8%), sophomores (19.9%), freshmen (10%), and a smaller group of postgraduates (8.3%). Regarding sport type, 55.2% were engaged in team sports, while 44.8% participated in individual sports. The most commonly reported specific disciplines were basketball (12.7%) and football (12%), closely followed by volleyball (10.8%) and martial arts (10.2%). Notably, 16.8% of participants reported being involved in sports not listed under the main categories. Regarding competitive background, most participants had considerable experience, with 38.2% indicating 4 to 6 years and 34.6% reporting over 7 years of involvement. Only a small fraction (4.4%) had been involved for less than a year. The competition level also reflected this experience, with 62.7% having competed at the provincial level, and 24.9% reaching national-level events. A small elite group (4.6%) had international-level experience. Finally, training frequency was relatively high across the board, with over half (51%) training five or more times a week, and another 35.1% training three to four times weekly. Only 13.9% trained less frequently, at one to two sessions per week. Overall, the data suggest a well-experienced and competitively active sample, balanced in gender and with diverse sports representation.

This form of validity determines whether each construct is empirically separate from the others and is typically assessed using the Fornell–Larcker criterion [8]. Under the Fornell–Larcker criterion, discriminant validity is confirmed when the square root of a construct’s AVE (positioned on the diagonal) exceeds its correlations with all other constructs (represented by the off-diagonal values in the same row and column). This criterion verifies that each latent construct explains more variance in its own observed indicators than it shares with any other construct within the model.

Discussion and conclusion

Interpretation of key findings

Mindfulness positively enhances cognitive reappraisal, psychological resilience, and athletic performance

The direct effects suggest that mindfulness functions as an upstream self-regulatory resource in athletic contexts. Rather than operating only as a general psychological trait, mindfulness appears to create favorable conditions for attentional stability, emotional awareness, and adaptive responses to competitive pressure. The relatively stronger association between mindfulness and cognitive reappraisal is theoretically meaningful because reappraisal requires individuals to notice emotional reactions before becoming fully absorbed by them. In competitive sport, mindful awareness may provide the cognitive distance needed for athletes to reinterpret mistakes, pressure, or temporary performance decline as manageable challenges rather than fixed threats.This interpretation is especially relevant in sport because athletes often have limited time to regulate emotional reactions during competition. Mindfulness may therefore support reappraisal not by eliminating pressure, but by helping athletes change their relationship to pressure while remaining engaged with the performance task. The association between mindfulness and psychological resilience suggests that mindful athletes may not necessarily experience fewer stressors, but may respond to unavoidable setbacks with less avoidance, self-criticism, or emotional overreaction. In this sense, resilience can be understood as a functional outcome of repeated mindful regulation under competitive demands. This finding is particularly relevant in competitive sports environments where resilience is often a differentiator between consistent and inconsistent performance.

The direct link between mindfulness and athletic performance should be interpreted with caution. It does not imply that mindfulness automatically produces superior performance; rather, it suggests that present-focused awareness may support the psychological conditions under which athletes can execute skills more consistently under pressure. This relationship supports the increasing inclusion of mindfulness training in athletic programs aimed at optimizing focus, reducing performance anxiety, and enhancing execution under pressure. The positive roles of cognitive reappraisal and psychological resilience further indicate that performance-related outcomes may depend on athletes’ ability to interpret pressure adaptively and maintain functioning after setbacks. Because athletic performance in this study was self-perceived, these findings should be understood as evidence of perceived performance functioning rather than objective competitive superiority. The relationship between cognitive reappraisal and resilience further suggests that emotion regulation may not only support immediate performance functioning but also contribute to the development of more stable adaptive capacity under competitive adversity. Collectively, the direct pathways suggest that mindfulness should be understood as part of a broader self-regulatory system rather than as an isolated trait. Its relevance to sport appears to lie in its capacity to support attention, cognitive reframing, and resilience-related adaptation under competitive pressure.

Cognitive reappraisal and psychological resilience play as significant mediation roles

Beyond the direct pathways, the indirect effects suggest that mindfulness may influence athletic performance through a staged regulatory process rather than through a single psychological route. The mediation through cognitive reappraisal appears to be the most immediate pathway because reappraisal is closely tied to how athletes interpret pressure during performance. Mindfulness may help athletes notice stress without automatic judgment, while reappraisal allows them to transform that stress into a more manageable performance cue. This suggests that part of the reason mindfulness contributes to better performance lies in its ability to enhance athletes’ capacity for cognitive reframing. This does not mean that mindfulness removes competitive stress; rather, it may help athletes change the meaning assigned to stress, thereby reducing its disruptive effect on attention and emotional control.

The role of cognitive reappraisal in the mindfulness-resilience link suggests that resilience may partly develop through repeated meaning reconstruction. When athletes repeatedly reinterpret setbacks as controllable or temporary, they may gradually build a stronger capacity to recover and continue effort under pressure. This interpretation extends the Mindfulness-to-Meaning perspective by specifying psychological resilience as a downstream adaptive resource that may emerge from repeated cycles of mindful awareness and cognitive reappraisal.This implies that the resilient mindset observed in mindful individuals may not stem directly from mindfulness alone, but also from the enhanced cognitive strategies that mindfulness fosters. These findings point to a layered psychological process, where mindfulness supports reappraisal, and reappraisal builds the psychological stamina athletes need.

The smaller mediation effect through psychological resilience alone may indicate that resilience is less immediate than cognitive reappraisal in performance situations. Resilience reflects accumulated adaptive capacity, and its influence may be more visible across repeated training and competition experiences than in a single cross-sectional assessment. This suggests that the ability to recover from adversity may partially account for how mindfulness improves outcomes, albeit less strongly than cognitive mechanisms. The pathway from cognitive reappraisal through resilience to performance further suggests that cognitive reframing may become performance-relevant when it contributes to more stable coping capacity. In this sense, reappraisal may not only regulate immediate emotional reactions but also support longer-term resilience-building.

The sequential mediation pathway was statistically significant but modest in magnitude. This finding should therefore be interpreted cautiously, as the full pathway from mindfulness to cognitive reappraisal, psychological resilience, and performance is unlikely to represent the dominant route through which mindfulness operates. The small effect size is theoretically understandable because serial mediation involves the transmission of influence across multiple psychological stages, where the original effect is gradually attenuated. Its substantive value lies in showing that mindfulness may not only operate through immediate cognitive or performance-related pathways, but may also contribute to a slower process of meaning reconstruction and resilience accumulation. In practical terms, this finding suggests that mindfulness-based training should not be expected to produce large immediate performance gains through this full chain alone. Instead, mindfulness training may be more useful when combined with explicit cognitive reframing exercises and resilience-building strategies across repeated training and competition experiences. Overall, the mediation findings provide a differentiated account of how mindful awareness, cognitive meaning reconstruction, and resilience-related adaptation jointly contribute to perceived performance in competitive sport.

Theoretical contributions

This study advances the field of performance-focused sport psychology by empirically validating a multi-layered predictive model that explains how mindfulness facilitates athletic performance through two interrelated psychological mechanisms: cognitive reappraisal and psychological resilience. While previous studies have largely investigated the direct effects of mindfulness or isolated single mediators, this research offers a novel contribution by integrating both parallel and sequential mediation pathways into a unified structural model [28]. In doing so, it provides a more ecologically valid understanding of how attentional, cognitive, and emotional self-regulation processes interact dynamically under competitive pressure.

A key theoretical advancement lies in the empirical extension and refinement of the Mindfulness-to-Meaning Theory within the sport performance domain [18]. This study explicitly models and tests the sequential relationship whereby mindfulness enhances cognitive reappraisal, which in turn fosters psychological resilience, ultimately leading to improved performance outcomes. Previous applications of the theory have often posited a general link between mindfulness and positive outcomes via emotion regulation, yet without specifying the temporal and hierarchical ordering of regulatory processes. More specifically, this study deepens the Mindfulness-to-Meaning framework by clarifying how cognitive reappraisal may become a resilience-building mechanism in competitive sport. Reappraisal does not simply reduce immediate negative emotion,it changes the meaning assigned to pressure, mistakes, and setbacks. When athletes repeatedly reinterpret these experiences as controllable, temporary, or informative rather than as fixed threats, they may gradually develop stronger perceived controllability, coping confidence, and emotional stability. In this sense, psychological resilience is conceptualized not merely as a stable trait, but as an adaptive capacity that can be accumulated through repeated cycles of mindful awareness and constructive meaning reconstruction. By confirming a cognition-to-emotion regulatory cascade, this study fills a theoretical gap and supports the existence of a staged self-regulation mechanism in high-performance athletic settings.

Additionally, the findings contribute to Attentional Control Theory by illustrating how mindfulness may reduce task-irrelevant cognitive intrusions—such as rumination or evaluative anxiety—thereby enabling athletes to allocate attention more efficiently to performance-relevant cues [12]. This attentional refinement serves as a precursor to cognitive reframing, thereby operationalizing how metacognitive awareness can initiate broader emotional regulatory benefits.

From the perspective of Self-Determination Theory (SDT), the study reinforces the role of mindfulness in supporting psychological autonomy and competence, which are expressed through enhanced internal regulation of thoughts and emotions [5]. By demonstrating that mindfulness leads to better emotion-focused coping strategies (e.g., reappraisal) and fosters resilient states conducive to persistence, this model offers a nuanced SDT-based interpretation of motivated self-regulation in sport. The study also contributes conceptually to the Mindfulness-Acceptance-Commitment (MAC) model by clarifying the cognitive-affective pathways through which experiential acceptance (a core MAC tenet) translates into psychological performance gains. Specifically, it positions cognitive reappraisal as a key mediator that bridges mindful awareness with adaptive coping, extending MAC from a process framework into a validated structural model.

A further theoretical contribution involves the reconceptualization of psychological resilience. Rather than treating resilience as a fixed trait or moderator, as common in earlier works, this study empirically establishes resilience as a state-like, malleable outcome that emerges from higher-order cognitive regulation [21, 25]. This shift aligns with developmental sport psychology and underscores the trainability of resilience through upstream interventions targeting mindful awareness and reframing skills.

Finally, the identification of varying effect sizes across mediation paths shows that the cognitive reappraisal path had a stronger effect (β = 0.226) than the psychological resilience path (β = 0.095). This result suggests that top-down cognitive mechanisms may exert a more immediate influence on athletic performance than bottom-up affective processes. It also provides a basis for future theoretical refinement, as regulatory pathways may differ in their functional immediacy, plasticity, and role within mental skills interventions.

In sum, this study makes three primary theoretical contributions: (1) it formalizes and validates a dual-path and sequential mediation model linking mindfulness to athletic performance, (2) it reframes psychological resilience as a dynamic psychological outcome shaped by cognitive-emotional processes, and (3) it integrates attentional, cognitive, and emotional regulation into a staged framework for understanding performance enhancement in sport. These contributions significantly extend the conceptual boundaries of existing theories and offer a scaffold for future research and intervention design.

Practical implications

The findings provide practical guidance for universities and sport training institutions in developing psychological support systems for competitive student-athletes. Since mindfulness was associated with athletic performance both directly and indirectly through cognitive reappraisal and psychological resilience, athlete development should not rely solely on physical conditioning and technical instruction. Universities and training institutions can incorporate mindfulness-based modules into regular athletic programs to help athletes strengthen present-moment awareness, attentional stability, and emotional self-regulation under pressure. This is particularly relevant for student-athletes who must manage academic demands, intensive training schedules, performance evaluation, and repeated competitive stress.

For coaches and sport training teams, the results highlight the importance of integrating cognitive reappraisal into daily training and competition preparation. Athletes often experience mistakes, temporary performance decline, opponent pressure, and emotional frustration during competition. Coaches can help athletes reinterpret these experiences as feedback, challenges, or opportunities for tactical adjustment rather than as signs of failure. In this sense, cognitive reappraisal can be translated into practical coaching strategies through post-competition review, guided reflection, scenario-based training, and pre-competition psychological preparation. Such practices may help athletes maintain task focus, emotional control, and adaptive decision-making in high-pressure situations.

For sport psychologists, counseling centers, and student-athletes themselves, the sequential mediation pathway suggests a staged intervention logic. Psychological support may begin with mindfulness-based awareness training, then progress to cognitive reframing exercises, and further incorporate resilience-building strategies such as coping planning, recovery routines, and confidence rebuilding after setbacks. This pathway is especially useful for athletes who experience performance anxiety, unstable confidence, or difficulty recovering from failure. Overall, the study suggests that mindfulness, cognitive reappraisal, and psychological resilience can be integrated into a coordinated athlete development framework, in which institutions provide support platforms, coaches translate psychological skills into training routines, professionals design structured interventions, and athletes apply these skills in repeated competitive practice.

Limitations and directions for future research

This study constructed and empirically validated a structural model to examine the influence of mindfulness on athletic performance through two key psychological mechanisms: cognitive reappraisal and psychological resilience. However, there are several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although the sample included 482 competitive university athletes from 19 universities in Sichuan Province, the use of convenience and purposive sampling may limit the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. Because participants were recruited through accessible institutions, coaches, and institutional coordinators, the sample may overrepresent athletes who were easier to contact, more willing to participate, or more embedded in organized university sport systems. Therefore, the findings should be generalized with caution to athletes from other provinces, professional teams, non-university sport systems, or recreational sport populations. Future studies should consider probability sampling, stratified multi-province recruitment, or longitudinal and mixed-method designs to strengthen external validity and contextual interpretation. Although the sample size was adequate for SEM, the non-probability sampling strategy and regional focus may limit external validity. Future research could benefit from expanding the sample size, employing more sophisticated sampling techniques, and integrating experimental and qualitative approaches, such as interviews, to enrich data depth and contextual understanding.From a theoretical standpoint, the study primarily drew upon established psychological frameworks, including the Mindfulness-Acceptance-Commitment model, Self-Determination Theory (SDT), Mindfulness-to-Meaning Theory, and Attentional Control Theory. While these offer a solid foundation, they are predominantly rooted in psychology. Future investigations could take an interdisciplinary approach, incorporating theories from sociology, neuroscience, education, or behavioral economics, and explore a broader range of internal and external variables. This could help move beyond the boundaries of a purely psychological model and offer more comprehensive insights.

Lastly, although the data analysis methods met the study’s objectives, they relied on traditional statistical techniques. To enhance the analytical depth and visual interpretability of future findings, researchers may consider adopting machine learning methods and leveraging advanced programming languages such as R or Python. These tools can facilitate more dynamic data visualization and offer greater flexibility in uncovering complex patterns, ultimately contributing to a richer and more accessible presentation of research outcomes. Although the mediation effects were tested using 5,000 bootstrap resamples and all confidence intervals excluded zero, future studies may use larger bootstrap resampling procedures or multi-group analyses to further examine the stability of the mediation model across different athlete subgroups.

In conclusion, this study offers a theoretically grounded and practically relevant framework that integrates attentional, cognitive, and emotional pathways to explain how mindfulness contributes to athletic performance. Despite certain methodological constraints, it provides a solid foundation for future research and intervention development in applied sport psychology. As the demand for mental optimization in sport continues to grow, this model may serve as a valuable guide for designing psychologically informed training systems that elevate both performance and well-being in competitive athletic contexts.

Conclusion

This study validated a predictive model to explore how mindfulness influences athletic performance through the mediating roles of cognitive reappraisal and psychological resilience.

The findings provide clear empirical evidence that mindfulness not only has direct effects on performance, but also exerts its influence through key psychological pathways. Cognitive reappraisal emerged as both a direct enhancer of performance and a bridge between mindfulness and resilience, while resilience itself contributed significantly to performance outcomes. These results highlight the importance of cultivating mental awareness, emotional flexibility, and adaptive strength in athletes, offering valuable guidance for the design of psychological training interventions. Theoretically, this study extends the application of mindfulness-related constructs within the domain of sport psychology and provides empirical support for a layered psychological model grounded in frameworks such as Self-Determination Theory and the Mindfulness-to-Meaning Theory. At the same time, it opens the door for future interdisciplinary expansion beyond traditional psychological models. Practically, the research offers actionable insights for educators, coaches, training teams, and mental health professionals, suggesting that integrating mindfulness-based strategies into athlete development programs may significantly enhance both psychological readiness and competitive performance.
