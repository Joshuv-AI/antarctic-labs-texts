Spurious Fingertip Oxygen Desaturation During the 6‐Minute Walk Test in a Patient With NSIP and Raynaud Phenomenon: The Value of Forehead Pulse Oximetry and Transcutaneous Oxygen Tension
Authors: Yusuke Nakamura, Masayoshi Takamori, Honoka Sunaga, Kosuke Natsume, Yuri Nomura, Yuto Goto, Nobuhiko Tsukada, Azusa Tsukada, Nobuhiko Uchida, Nana Yazawa, Hiromi Hoshi, Yasuo Shimizu, Seiji Niho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTReduced digital perfusion associated with Raynaud phenomenon may cause spuriously low fingertip pulse oximetry readings during the 6‐minute walk test (6MWT), potentially leading to overestimation of exercise‐induced hypoxaemia in patients with interstitial lung disease. We report a 39‐year‐old woman with non‐specific interstitial pneumonia and Raynaud phenomenon who underwent repeated 6MWTs. Fingertip SpO2 progressively decreased from 96% to 82%, whereas forehead SpO2 remained stable (93%–98%). Simultaneously, transcutaneous oxygen tension (TcPO2) measured on the volar forearm remained unchanged. Progressive finger cooling and digital pallor were also observed. Fingertip SpO2 rapidly returned to baseline during recovery. These findings suggested that the apparent desaturation reflected reduced digital perfusion rather than true systemic hypoxaemia. This case highlights the potential limitations of fingertip pulse oximetry during exercise in patients with Raynaud phenomenon. Alternative methods of assessing oxygenation, such as forehead pulse oximetry or TcPO2, may be useful during the 6MWT when fingertip SpO2 readings are discordant with the clinical presentation.Keywords: 6‐min walk test, interstitial lung disease, pulse oximetry, Raynaud phenomenon, spurious desaturation

Introduction

The 6‐minute walk test (6MWT) is routinely used to evaluate exercise capacity in patients with interstitial lung disease (ILD) [1]. Oxygen desaturation during the test provides clinically important information and is widely used to assess exertional hypoxaemia and determine the need for ambulatory oxygen therapy [1, 2]. Reliable assessment of oxygen saturation is therefore essential.

Raynaud phenomenon is characterised by transient impairment of digital perfusion while systemic oxygenation remains unchanged [3]. Because fingertip pulse oximetry depends on adequate pulsatile blood flow, impaired digital perfusion may produce spuriously low oxygen saturation readings. Forehead pulse oximetry has been reported to produce fewer poor‐quality signals than fingertip pulse oximetry during the 6MWT [4]. It is often difficult to determine whether apparent exercise‐induced desaturation in patients with Raynaud phenomenon reflects true hypoxaemia or reduced digital perfusion.

We describe a patient with non‐specific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) in whom simultaneous assessment using fingertip and forehead pulse oximetry together with transcutaneous oxygen tension (TcPO2) suggested that marked exercise‐induced fingertip desaturation reflected reduced digital perfusion rather than true systemic hypoxaemia.

Case Report

A 39‐year‐old woman with scoliosis and Raynaud phenomenon was referred to our hospital because of progressive exertional dyspnoea, a dry cough and abnormal chest radiographic findings. She was 161.3 cm, 48.4 kg and had a BMI of 18.6 kg/m2. She had experienced easy fatigability since giving birth 2.5 years earlier, followed by worsening dyspnoea and dry cough over the preceding 6 months. She had been treated with inhaled corticosteroids and bronchodilators for presumed asthma without improvement.

She had no known drug allergies, had never smoked, was not taking regular medications and had no history of exposure to birds or feather products. Oxygen saturation was 98% while breathing room air. Her Hugh–Jones classification was Grade I, modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnoea scale was Grade 1 and Borg dyspnoea scores were 1 at rest and 4 during exertion.

During the initial 6MWT, fingertip pulse oximetry showed progressive desaturation to 88% after 4 min, prompting termination of the test.

Arterial blood gas analysis on room air showed a pH of 7.424, PaCO2 of 38.1 mmHg, PaO2 of 101.5 mmHg and HCO3 − of 24.4 mmol/L. Autoimmune serological testing showed a low‐titre antinuclear antibody (ANA) at 1:20 with a speckled pattern. Rheumatoid factor, anti‐cyclic citrullinated peptide, anti‐DNA, anti‐RNP, anti‐SS‐A/Ro, anti‐SS‐B/La, anti‐Scl‐70, anti‐centromere, anti‐aminoacyl‐tRNA synthetase, anti‐MDA5, anti‐TIF1‐γ antibodies, proteinase 3‐ANCA and myeloperoxidase‐ANCA were negative. Anti‐avian and anti‐Trichosporon antibodies were negative. Given the presence of Raynaud phenomenon and an NSIP pattern, the patient underwent rheumatological evaluation. No additional clinical features suggestive of systemic sclerosis or another connective tissue disease, including sclerodactyly, digital ulcers, inflammatory arthritis or muscle weakness, were identified. Therefore, no definable connective tissue disease was diagnosed at the time of evaluation.

Chest computed tomography (CT) demonstrated subpleural consolidation with traction bronchiectasis in the right upper lobe together with relatively homogeneous ground‐glass opacities and subpleural reticulation in both lower lobes (Figure 1). Transbronchial lung cryobiopsy from the right B9b demonstrated relatively uniform interstitial fibrosis with lymphoid follicles and focal fibroblastic foci. Although no definable connective tissue disease was identified after rheumatological evaluation, the combination of Raynaud phenomenon and an NSIP pattern fulfilled the research classification criteria for interstitial pneumonia with autoimmune features (IPAF). Following multidisciplinary discussion, idiopathic NSIP was considered the most likely clinical diagnosis, recognising that IPAF represents a research classification rather than a specific clinical diagnosis. The patient remains under clinical follow‐up for the potential development of a definable connective tissue disease.

Chest CT comparison at initial presentation and after treatment. At initial presentation, chest CT showed subpleural consolidation with traction bronchiectasis in the right upper lobe, together with relatively homogeneous ground‐glass opacities and subpleural reticulation in both lower lobes (A–C). Two weeks after corticosteroid treatment, these abnormalities had partially resolved (D–F).

Treatment with intravenous methylprednisolone (500 mg/day for 3 days) was followed by oral prednisolone (50 mg/day), which was tapered to 15 mg/day over 1 month. Mycophenolate mofetil was initiated 2 weeks later and titrated to 1000 mg/day according to therapeutic drug monitoring. Two weeks after treatment initiation, chest CT showed partial resolution of the right upper lobe consolidation and mild improvement of the bilateral ground‐glass opacities (Figure 1). Percent‐predicted forced vital capacity improved from 55.4% to 58.2%. Her Hugh–Jones grade and mMRC score both improved to 0 and her Borg scores improved to 0 at rest and 1 during exertion.

Despite these clinical, radiological and physiological improvements, a repeat 6MWT demonstrated rapid fingertip desaturation to approximately 85% within 2 min, without dyspnoea or other symptoms. The finding was reproducible. During the apparent desaturation, the pulse waveform became unstable and progressive finger cooling was observed.

To investigate this discrepancy, 6MWT was performed with simultaneous monitoring using fingertip pulse oximetry, forehead pulse oximetry and TcPO2. During exercise, fingertip SpO2 progressively decreased after approximately 1 min and was accompanied by a fall in fingertip temperature (palmar aspect of the left fourth finger) from 36.4°C to 34.8°C. In contrast, forehead SpO2 and TcPO2 remained essentially unchanged and forehead temperature was stable (Figure 2). The skin over the proximal interphalangeal joints became pale during exercise, whereas the distal fingertips retained their normal colour (Figure 2). Ultrasonography demonstrated minimal change in radial artery diameter before and during exercise (2.2 vs. 2.1 mm). Taken together, these findings suggested that the apparent desaturation reflected reduced digital perfusion rather than true systemic hypoxaemia.

Discussion

This case suggests that marked fingertip oxygen desaturation during the 6MWT does not necessarily reflect true exercise‐induced hypoxaemia in patients with Raynaud phenomenon. Despite marked improvement in symptoms, pulmonary function and chest CT findings after treatment, our patient repeatedly exhibited profound fingertip desaturation without dyspnoea. In contrast, forehead SpO2 and TcPO2 remained stable throughout exercise. These findings suggest that the apparent desaturation reflected reduced digital perfusion rather than true systemic hypoxaemia.

Raynaud phenomenon is characterised by episodic vasospasm of the digital circulation, resulting in transient reduction of peripheral perfusion while systemic oxygenation is preserved [3]. Because pulse oximetry depends on an adequate pulsatile arterial signal, reduced digital perfusion may produce unstable or spuriously low oxygen saturation measurements. In our patient, fingertip desaturation was accompanied by waveform instability, finger cooling and digital pallor, all of which are consistent with impaired digital perfusion associated with Raynaud phenomenon. Sympathetic activation during exercise may have contributed to the reduction in digital perfusion [5].

Previous studies have demonstrated limitations of finger pulse oximetry in patients with systemic sclerosis, in whom Raynaud phenomenon is common [6, 7]. Wilsher et al. demonstrated that forehead oximetry provides reliable oxygen saturation measurements during the 6MWT in patients with systemic sclerosis [6], while Elkjær et al. more recently showed that finger pulse oximetry may be inaccurate and less reliable in this population [7]. Our case extends these observations beyond systemic sclerosis by showing that exercise‐associated reductions in digital perfusion can mimic marked systemic oxygen desaturation, as demonstrated by simultaneous fingertip and forehead SpO2 monitoring, TcPO2 measurement and serial assessment of finger temperature and colour. These findings emphasise that the limitations of fingertip pulse oximetry should also be considered in patients with Raynaud phenomenon, regardless of whether systemic sclerosis or another underlying disease is present.

Oxygen desaturation during the 6MWT is widely used to assess exertional hypoxaemia and determine the need for ambulatory oxygen therapy in patients with ILD [2]. Falsely low fingertip SpO2 readings may therefore lead to overestimation of disease severity and influence subsequent clinical management. Our findings suggest that reduced digital perfusion should be considered when interpreting fingertip SpO2 desaturation during the 6MWT in patients with Raynaud phenomenon. Alternative methods of assessing oxygenation, such as forehead pulse oximetry or TcPO2, may therefore be useful during the 6MWT when fingertip SpO2 readings are discordant with symptoms or the overall clinical course.
