Acute cholecystitis in older adults with multimorbidity: does surgery offer better outcomes?
Authors: Anaïs Tribolet, Sophie Chopinet
Published: 2025

The study by Rachael C. Acker, published in JAMA Surgery in April 2025 (1), aimed to compare the benefits of operative treatment by cholecystectomy versus non-operative treatment based on antibiotherapy, with or without percutaneous gallbladder drainage, in elderly patients with multiple comorbidities hospitalized for acute lithiasis cholecystitis (ALC).

This was a retrospective cohort study using Medicare administrative data, conducted in the United States between April 2016 and December 2018. The authors included patients aged 65.5 years or older. Multimorbidity was identified using validated comorbidity sets within the Medicare database. A propensity score matching approach was used to balance characteristics between the two groups, and an instrumental variable analysis was performed, using the probabilities of a hospital to operate to account for unmeasured confounders. The primary outcome was 30- and 90-day mortality in both groups. Secondary outcomes included readmission rates, emergency department (ED) visits, and overall healthcare costs.

A total of 32,527 patients aged over 65.5 years who were admitted to ED in the United States for ALC between 2016 and 2018 were included in the study. Among them, 21,728 patients (66.8%) underwent a cholecystectomy (operative group), while 10,799 patients (33.2%) had a non-operative treatment. The median age of the patients included was 78.8 years.

In the operative group, 89.4% of patients underwent laparoscopic cholecystectomy. In the non-operative group, 3,462 patients (32.1%) underwent percutaneous gallbladder drainage, and 28% of patients ultimately underwent cholecystectomy within 180 days.

After propensity score adjustment, 30-day mortality was 6% in the non-operative group compared to 3% in the operative group, representing an absolute risk difference reduction of 3% (P<0.01). At 90 days, mortality was 10% in the non-operative group versus 6% in the operative group, corresponding to an absolute risk difference reduction of 4% (P<0.01). Readmissions and ED visits were significantly lower in the operative group. Initial costs were higher in the operative group, but total 90-day costs were lower compared to the non-operative group. The results before propensity score matching were similar.

The study focused on a subgroup of patients in clinical equipoise—situations where uncertainty about the best treatment approach leads to variability in clinical decision-making. These patients were identified through an instrumental variable analysis based on hospitals’ likelihood of performing early surgery in patients with similar age and multimorbidity, but who were treated differently solely due to institutional practice patterns. In this subgroup, no significant difference in mortality at 30 or 90 days was observed between operative and non-operative subgroups. However, the operative subgroup showed fewer readmissions, fewer ED visits, and lower total hospitalization costs at 90 days.

The authors therefore conclude that in the context of uncertainty regarding the best treatment strategy for an elderly and multimorbid patient hospitalized with ALC, operative treatment should be strongly considered.

ALC is a frequent cause of ED visits, especially in older adults (2). The management of ALC is well established, with early laparoscopic cholecystectomy considered the standard treatment (3,4). However, in elderly patients with multiple comorbidities and high operative risk, non-operative treatment, including antibiotic therapy with or without percutaneous gallbladder drainage, is often preferred. Non-operative treatment is associated with a higher risk of recurrence, and current guidelines for the management of gallstone disease in older patients with comorbidities remain lacking in the literature (5).

The authors report a significantly lower mortality rate in the group undergoing cholecystectomy, with an absolute risk reduction of 3% at 30 days and 4% at 90 days compared to the non-operative group treated with antibiotics alone or combined with percutaneous drainage. Additionally, patients in the operative group had fewer readmissions and fewer ED visits. While operative treatment leads to higher initial costs, total 90-day costs were lower, likely due to fewer complications and hospitalizations. These findings support the benefits of operative treatment, even in elderly patients with multiple comorbidities, provided they are considered suitable candidates for surgery.

In the subgroup of patients in “clinical equipoise”—corresponding as situations where the decision to operate or not is not straightforward—no significant difference in mortality was observed at 30 or 90 days. This suggests that for high-risk, multimorbid elderly patients, non-operative treatment may be an acceptable alternative. However, even within this subgroup, those who underwent surgery had fewer readmissions, fewer ED visits, and lower overall costs at 90 days compared to those treated non-surgically.

The study’s methodology is robust, combining propensity score adjustment for measured confounders with instrumental variable analysis to correct for unmeasured biases (related to the surgeon, hospital setting, or clinical practice variability). The higher severity of ALC in patients in the non-operative group could have overestimated the benefit of surgery in crude analyses. Moreover, the large cohort ensures strong statistical power, while the subgroup analysis of patients in clinical equipoise focuses on a population in whom surgical decision-making is especially complex.

The latest World Society of Emergency Surgery (WSES) consensus guidelines recommend early cholecystectomy for the treatment of ALC, including in elderly patients (5,6). However, there is a lack of high-quality evidence in this population. Most available studies involve small cohorts (7,8) or report conflicting results regarding optimal treatment strategies (9,10). A recent Delphi international consensus, conducted by Antonio Pesce, reaffirms that laparoscopic cholecystectomy remains the gold standard for ALC, while recognizing that percutaneous gallbladder drainage is a valuable option in high-surgical-risk patients with severe clinical presentation; however, the consensus does not focus exclusively on elderly patients (11).

The value of Rachael C. Acker’s study lies in its ability to provide compelling evidence in favor of operative treatment in older patients. However, the study has limitations. Major patient characteristics such as American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status Classification System (ASA-PS) score, the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) and nutritional status are not reported. The severity of ALC is not detailed, with no biological, radiological, or clinical data beyond the presence of sepsis. The Tokyo classification is not provided. Although propensity score adjustment was applied, the absence of key variables may have altered the outcomes. Furthermore, decompensation of comorbid conditions are not described. Non-operative treatment consisted of antibiotics, with or without percutaneous drainage, but details on the type and duration of antibiotics, the indication for drainage, and its management (e.g., clamping) are lacking. Likewise, postoperative complications, including bile duct injuries, which have significant long-term implications, are not specifically reported. The study does not account for the role of patient and family preferences in treatment decisions, which is an important factor in clinical management. Quality of life outcomes were also not evaluated, despite being a major concern for elderly patients. Finally, long-term outcomes for both primary and secondary endpoints would have added further value to the study.

In conclusion, this study provides strong evidence in favor of operative treatment by cholecystectomy for ALC in elderly patients with multiple comorbidities who are suitable candidates for surgery. The operative treatment was associated with a lower 30- and 90-day mortality, fewer ED visits, fewer readmissions, and lower overall costs. While the findings are promising, the absence of detailed patient data, ALC severity, and long-term follow-up limits the study’s strength.
