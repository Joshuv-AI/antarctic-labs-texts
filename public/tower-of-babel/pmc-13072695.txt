Immune cells in keloids: mechanisms and potential treatments
Authors: Yuqi Wu, Yuting Huang, Hao Zhang, Chen Dong, Xiaodong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKeloids are a prevalent pathological fibrotic and proliferative disorder characterized by persistent inflammation and immune dysregulation. In recent years, the critical role of immune cells in keloid development and progression has attracted increasing attention.ObjectiveTo summarize the immunoregulatory mechanisms and immune dysfunction involved in keloids and to discuss therapeutic strategies targeting these pathways.MethodsRelevant evidence on immune cell populations and immunoregulatory pathways implicated in keloid pathogenesis was reviewed, with emphasis on regulatory T cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, CD8+ T cells, and natural killer cells.ResultsRegulatory immune cells, particularly regulatory T lymphocytes (Tregs), exert important effects on keloid formation. Tregs secrete cytokines such as transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) and interleukin-10 (IL-10), which suppress inflammation while promoting fibroblast hyperproliferation. M2 macrophages are the predominant macrophage subtype in keloid tissue and contribute to extracellular matrix deposition through the secretion of anti-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic factors. In addition, keloids may exhibit an immune milieu characterized by enrichment of canonical immunoregulatory populations, including regulatory T cells, tolerogenic dendritic cells, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, together with impaired cytotoxic immune activity involving dysfunctional or exhausted CD8+ T cells and natural killer cells. These alterations collectively support an anti-inflammatory yet pro-fibrotic microenvironment that favors keloid persistence and progression.ConclusionImmunoregulatory mechanisms and cytotoxic immune dysfunction are central to keloid pathogenesis. Targeting these pathways may provide a basis for the development of novel immunomodulatory strategies to improve keloid outcomes.

Introduction

Keloid is a pathological fibrotic condition marked by aberrant tissue repair and persistent fibrosis, leading to compromised appearance and function [1,2]. While the precise mechanisms underlying keloid formation remain complex and not entirely understood, research has identified several contributing factors, including trauma, genetic predisposition, inflammatory responses, and dysregulated extracellular matrix (ECM) metabolism [3–6]. Based on studies of the keloid microenvironment, accumulating evidence indicates that multiple immunoregulatory programs contribute to keloid persistence. These include regulatory T cells (Tregs), CD8+ T cells with regulatory-like features, M2-like macrophages, tolerogenic dendritic cell (DC) states, MDSC-like suppressive myeloid signatures, and cytotoxic lymphocytes (CD8+ T cells and NK cells) that display checkpoint-associated functional restraint in lesional niches [7,8]. Under normal conditions, such immune cells help maintain tissue homeostasis by limiting excessive immune activation; however, in keloid tissue, their aberrant activity paradoxically drives persistent fibrosis (Table 1).

Current studies on immune cells involved in keloids.

Among the various immune cells implicated in keloid formation, Tregs have drawn special attention. Tregs produce cytokines with anti-inflammatory properties, such as IL-10 and TGF-β, primarily to modulate immune responses and limit inflammation [9,10]. Paradoxically, however, these molecules may also promote fibroblast activation and excessive collagen deposition, fueling fibrosis and pathological scar tissue formation [11,12]. However, in keloids, these protective mechanisms sometimes tip the scales toward fibrosis rather than healthy tissue regeneration. In a similar vein, M2 macrophages produce cytokines that stimulate ECM build up [13,14], while certain dendritic cell subsets tend to assume a tolerogenic stance, further intensifying the fibrotic environment. Importantly, NK cells and CD8+ T cells are not ‘immunosuppressive’ by lineage; instead, they often exhibit checkpoint-associated dysfunction that blunts cytotoxic surveillance and may permit fibroblast overgrowth. MDSCs, recognized for their strong suppressive myeloid functions in chronic inflammation, can also amplify pro-fibrotic signals.

Keloids often exhibit an apparent paradox in which overt inflammatory features are minimal, yet excessive scarring and fibrosis continue to progress. This phenomenon does not indicate immune quiescence; rather, it likely reflects a shift toward an altered immune microenvironment. Accumulating evidence suggests that keloid lesions are characterized by Th2-skewed immune programs, enrichment of IL-10– and TGF-β–dominated signaling, and activation of immune checkpoint and inhibitory pathways, including PD-1/PD-L1 and NKG2A-mediated regulation [15,16]. These mechanisms may attenuate classical pro-inflammatory readouts while permitting persistent fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix deposition. Importantly, this immune landscape is not uniform across all keloid lesions. Actively growing regions, particularly at the lesion margins, tend to display increased immune cell infiltration and enhanced fibrovascular and immune–stromal crosstalk. In contrast, long-standing or clinically stable keloids often appear to be entrenched in an extracellular matrix–dominated state, in which immune-restrained circuits contribute to the maintenance of fibrosis rather than its resolution.

In this review, we integrate evidence from human studies, including recent single-cell transcriptomic analyses that distinguish active from inactive keloid lesions, to delineate the key immune regulatory mechanisms operating within the keloid microenvironment. We further discuss how these pathways may be therapeutically targeted to rebalance immune–fibrotic interactions and potentially restore tissue homeostasis.

The pathogenesis of keloids

Keloid is a pathological scar caused by abnormalities in wound healing regulation, characterized by persistent activation of fibroblasts, excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM), and abnormal angiogenesis. Immunological factors play an important role. TGF-β, a key pro-fibrotic cytokine, activates downstream Smad proteins and promotes fibroblast proliferation, and leads to the overproduction of collagen (especially type I and III collagen). The elevated levels of TGF-β in keloids are positively correlated with scar severity [17,18], highlighting its critical role in fibrosis progression. Unlike the network arrangement of collagen fibers in normal skin, the collagen fibers in keloid tissue form a dense parallel bundle arrangement, contributing to the stiffness and abnormal texture of the scar. In addition to collagen, ECM components such as fibronectin and hyaluronic acid also over accumulate, further aggravating the fibrotic process [19,20]. Angiogenesis plays a key role in maintaining keloid growth, as studies have shown that the micro vessel density in keloid tissue is significantly higher than in normal scars. This is driven by the continuous activation of endothelial cells and the overexpression of angiogenic factors, forming an abnormal vascular network that provides oxygen and nutrients while sustaining the inflammatory microenvironment, which serves as the basis of keloid pathology [21]. Hyperactivation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is associated with endothelial to mesenchymal transition (EndMT) [22], during which endothelial cells acquire fibroblast-like properties that further promote scar growth. Shim et al. identified the interplay between fibroblasts and vascular endothelial cells as a driver of sustained fibrosis [23]. Their spatial transcriptomic analyses revealed that fibroblasts near blood vessels receive pro-fibrotic signals, intensifying collagen deposition and scar growth, suggesting that targeting these fibrovascular interactions could be a potential therapeutic strategy.

The immune response modulation in keloids

Wound healing is tightly modulated by immune responses involving both innate and adaptive pathways. Following cutaneous injury, various immune cell populations, such as neutrophils, macrophages, DCs, NK cells, and CD8+ T lymphocytes—are promptly activated and secrete a range of cytokines and chemokines [24,25]. These factors facilitate the clearance of necrotic tissue and recruitment of additional immune cells. Macrophages play dual roles: M1 macrophages drive initial inflammation, while M2 macrophages promote later-stage tissue repair and fibrosis regulation. Tregs play a pivotal role by releasing anti-inflammatory mediators, including IL-10 and TGF-β, thereby attenuating exaggerated immune responses, shaping the inflammatory milieu, and limiting fibroblast hyperactivity and aberrant collagen deposition. Dysregulated immune responses, such as imbalanced macrophage polarization or impaired Treg function, result in chronic inflammation, fibroblast hyperactivation, and pathological collagen deposition associated with keloid formation [26]. Moreover, proinflammatory cytokines like interleukin-6 (IL-6) trigger the activation of the NF-κB signaling cascade, thereby intensifying localized immune activity and promoting fibrotic processes. Moreover, DCs activate the Th2-IL-4/IL-13 pathway by secreting chemo/cytokines such as CXCL9/10/11 and TSLP-OX40L-IL-33, and direct cross-linking of collagen with FXIIIa, which dually drive persistent inflammation and collagen over deposition [27]. NK cells and CD8+ T cells are functionally restrained by inhibitory signals such as NKG2A/CD94 and lose their killing and inhibitory role in removing aberrant fibroblasts, which drives persistent inflammation and collagen over deposition. MDSCs can exacerbate fibrosis by suppressing T cell-mediated antifibrotic responses and facilitating chronic inflammatory conditions.

Among these cells, immune cells contribute to the formation and remodeling of keloid tissue by suppressing the inflammatory response and modulating the immune microenvironment (Figure 1). Single-cell (and spatial) transcriptomic landscape of immune microenvironment in keloids

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics have substantially refined our understanding of the immune microenvironment in keloids. Beyond delineating cellular composition, these approaches enable the identification of distinct functional states and activation programs within individual cell populations, while simultaneously resolving their spatial organization within the tissue. This integrated cellular and spatial information is particularly critical in keloids, where the localization and context dependent behavior of immune cells profoundly influence disease initiation, persistence, and progression. Several human studies now go a step further by splitting samples based on clinical activity (active vs. inactive) and/or where the biopsy came from (the growing edge/periphery vs. the center), often with mature scar or normal skin/scar controls for comparison [28]. That kind of setup helps explain the familiar paradox in keloids: inflammation can look toned down, yet fibrosis keeps moving forward. The inflammatory signals may be quieter, but immune-inhibitory or pro-resolution programs can stay switched on and still support fibroblast activation, vascular changes, and ongoing extracellular matrix (ECM) buildup in specific tissue niches. Notably, beyond immune lineages, recent single-cell studies also highlight Schwann cells as a niche-defining stromal population with reinforced ECM communication, providing a neuro–stromal layer that may indirectly support local immune restraint [29].

Across these single-cell and spatial transcriptomic atlases, the fibroblast and vascular compartments consistently emerge as the principal structural and regulatory drivers of keloid pathology. Mesenchymal-like fibroblast programs are markedly expanded in keloids, and the integration of spatial information further reveals intensified fibrovascular interactions that likely support aberrant matrix deposition and tissue remodeling. Importantly, the immune compartment is not absent but appears locally constrained. Recurrent transcriptional signatures point to enriched regulatory T cell activity, M2-like macrophage polarization, and antigen-presenting cells adopting more tolerogenic states, accompanied by suppressive myeloid programs [30]. In parallel, cytotoxic lymphocytes, including CD8+ T cells and NK cells, are frequently present yet exhibit transcriptional features consistent with functional attenuation, potentially mediated by inhibitory immune checkpoint pathways [31]. Thus, it’s not a globally quiet immune landscape, just one that’s skewed in a particular direction. In the sections that follow, we use this single-cell and spatial evidence as a guide to walk through each cell population and its regulatory circuits, while keeping activity status and sampling region in mind as key factors shaping cell proportions, state shifts, and pro-fibrotic effects (Table 2).

The role of Tregs in keloids

Tregs are key immunomodulators that help maintain immune homeostasis and restrain excessive inflammation. In keloids, human single-cell RNA-seq studies consistently detect Tregs within the CD45+ compartment, with relative enrichment compared with normal skin or mature scar controls. In activity-stratified datasets, Treg abundance and suppressive programs (e.g. FOXP3, IL10, CTLA4) are more evident in inactive lesions or centrally sampled regions than at the actively expanding periphery, supporting a role in maintaining local immune restraint (Table 2).

Characteristics and functions of Tregs

Tregs represent a crucial immune cell subset dedicated to preserving immunological equilibrium [32,33] and mitigating the risk of autoimmune pathology by restraining overactive immune responses. Originating in the thymus, Tregs are defined by the expression of specific markers, notably the transcription factor Foxp3, along with surface proteins CD4 and CD25 [34]. Foxp3 is essential for the functional differentiation and inhibitory capacity of Tregs [35]. Tregs mediate their immunoregulatory functions through multiple pathways, most notably by secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β. These mediators suppress the activation and function of effector T cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages, thereby dampening excessive inflammatory responses [32]. Tregs also regulate the function of antigen-presenting cells through direct intercellular contact, for example, by the expression of cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4). Murao et al. confirmed that Tregs play a key role in reducing the collagen synthesis of keloid fibroblasts, indicating that Tregs effectively alleviate fibrosis by inhibiting the excessive activation of fibroblasts [36]. Tregs are not only crucial in the self-regulation of the immune system, but also play an important role in tissue repair and wound healing, by inhibiting the excessive immune response, reducing tissue damage, and promoting regenerative repair. Defective Treg function is associated with multiple pathological conditions, including autoimmune diseases, chronic inflammatory diseases, and cancer, and thus Tregs have become potential therapeutic targets for immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory therapies [37,38].

Immunoregulatory and antifibrotic roles of Tregs in keloid formation

During keloid formation, Tregs, as key immunomodulatory cells, play an important role in preventing excessive fibrosis by regulating the local immune microenvironment (Figure 2). Although fibrosis is a necessary process for wound healing, uncontrolled fibrosis can lead to keloid formation. Peng et al. found that Tregs inhibit the fibrotic process by suppressing fibroblast activity and reducing the secretion of fibroblast cytokines (e.g. FGF-9), which reduces collagen over-deposition and regulates extracellular matrix metabolism, thus maintaining a balance of tissue repair [39]. Tregs regulate inflammation and fibrosis through the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β [40]. However, during the process of keloid formation, due to the dysregulation of these anti-inflammatory pathways, fibroblasts remain highly active, leading to an abnormal increase in collagen synthesis, which drives continued keloid growth. Tregs play a dual role in suppressing excessive immune responses and preventing uncontrolled fibrosis. Their dysfunction is a key immune mechanism in keloid formation.

Tregs-related signaling pathways and molecular mechanisms in fibrosis

The functional regulation of Tregs depends on multiple signaling pathways and molecular mechanisms, the most critical signaling pathway is the TGF-β/Smad pathway, which play a central role in the development, differentiation, and maintenance of immune regulatory functions [41]. TGF-β is not only essential for Treg differentiation but also regulates the repressive function of Treg by binding to the downstream Smad 2/3 proteins. In multiple fibrotic diseases, TGF-β/Smad signaling plays a key role in regulating Treg-mediated fibrosis and suppression of immune response through TGF-β mediated pathway [42,43]. Tregs play anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effects during tissue repair while maintaining immune balance. The IL-2/STAT5 signaling pathway is also a key factor in maintaining Treg survival and function. IL-2 binds to STAT5 to promote the expansion and activity maintenance of Treg while inhibiting the hyperactivation of effector T cells [44], which may provide the necessary protective role in the immunomodulation of keloids. Moreover, FOXP3 is a transcription factor unique to Tregs and the molecular basis of Treg function [45,46], and its expression is regulated by various signaling pathways (especially TGF-β and IL-2).

Suzanne et al. demonstrated that the mTOR signaling pathway affects the activity and stability of Treg by regulating cellular metabolism. Inhibition of mTOR enhances the immunosuppressive function of Treg, while activation of mTOR impaired its regulatory capacity [47]. Wnt/β-catenin signaling also plays a role in regulating Treg function, and studies suggest that Wnt signaling affects Treg differentiation and function by regulating β-catenin stability, and inhibition of this pathway may enhance the ability of Tregs to negatively regulate the immune response [48]. Cheng et al. showed that inhibiting Kv 1.3 channels can effectively inhibit the proliferation of Treg and regulate the immune response in the process of fibrosis [49]. The function of Tregs is precisely modulated by multiple signaling pathways, which may aggravate the fibrotic response in keloid, thus targeting these key signaling pathways provides potential therapeutic targets for immunotherapy of keloid.

The role of macrophages and M2-skewed programs in keloids

Macrophages are a major immune component in keloid lesions and show marked state plasticity across human datasets. Rather than a simple ‘high inflammation’ picture, recent single-cell transcriptomic studies suggest that macrophages in keloids frequently adopt inflammation-dampened yet pro-remodeling programs, aligning immune restraint with persistent extracellular-matrix (ECM) accumulation [50]. In particular, mononuclear phagocyte compartments (including macrophages and cDC2) can represent predominant immune populations in keloid tissue [51], and keloid macrophages often display M2-like features with suppressed inflammatory pathways alongside upregulated EMT/TGF-β–related signaling (Table 2).

Polarization drive and ‘multipronged’ pro-fibrotic effect of M2 macrophages

Human single-cell atlases consistently place macrophages among the major CD45+ populations in keloids, although their relative abundance varies with lesion activity and sampling region. Functionally, keloid macrophages frequently show a shift toward inflammation-dampened, tissue-remodeling programs: for example, macrophage signatures can be reduced in inflammatory response pathways (e.g. TNF-α/NF-κB, IL6/JAK/STAT signaling) while being enriched for EMT- and TGF-β–related programs, consistent with an M2-like, pro-fibrotic polarization state [52].

Mechanistically, stromal- and T cell–derived cues (e.g. CSF1/M-CSF, IL-4/IL-13–STAT6 signaling) are plausible upstream drivers that reinforce alternative activation and fibrovascular remodeling within keloid niches [53]. Notably, while hypoxia/HIF signaling has been proposed in keloid biology, recent human evidence using established hypoxia markers suggests that overt hypoxia at the growing edge may be limited [52], indicating that macrophage polarization is more likely shaped by combined cytokine, vascular, and matrix cues rather than hypoxia alone [54,55].

M2 macrophages secreted large amounts of TGF-β1 and PDGF-BB, which directly induced keloid fibroblasts to express α-SMA and synthesize type I/III collagen; at the same time, they released MMP-9 and MMP-12 and upregulated TIMP-1/2, which disrupted the balance of matrix turnover, contributing to collagen deposition [56,57]. High levels of IL-10 and surface PD-L1 inhibit effector T cells and stabilize Foxp3+ Treg through a positive feedback loop, while VEGFA and FGF-2 support the complex microvascular network and provide sustained nutrients for lesion expansion [58,59]. In vitro co-culture and xenograft models showed that blockade of TGF-β or CSF1R rapidly impaired collagen production, supporting a central contribution of M2-skewed macrophage programs to keloid fibrosis.

Macrophage-centered immunoregulatory circuitry in keloids

In the keloid microenvironment, M2-skewed macrophage programs may function as central hubs that couple immune restraint to fibrotic persistence through coordinated interactions with multiple immune populations. A recurrent axis involves macrophage–Treg reinforcement: M2-like macrophages can produce immunoregulatory mediators, including TGF-β1 and IL-10, which support the differentiation, expansion, and maintenance of FOXP3+ Tregs [60]. In turn, Tregs further amplify an IL-10–dominant milieu, forming a reinforcing circuit that can dampen overt inflammatory outputs while sustaining fibroblast activation and collagen deposition. This macrophage–Treg loop provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for the ‘muted inflammation yet progressive fibrosis’ phenotype observed in many keloids [61,62].

Beyond regulatory T cells, M2-like macrophages may play a pivotal role in shaping antigen presentation toward immune tolerance within keloid lesions. Specifically, macrophage-derived immunoregulatory signals can condition dendritic cells (DCs), including cDC2-like subsets, toward an IL-10–enriched, tolerogenic phenotype [63]. This reprogramming dampens Th1- and Th17-associated inflammatory responses and contributes to the stabilization of an immune-restrained microenvironment that favors persistent fibrosis. At the same time, M2-like macrophages may contribute to impaired cytotoxic surveillance. Through a combination of inhibitory ligand–receptor signaling (e.g. PD-L1–PD-1) and immunoregulatory cytokines (including IL-10), macrophage-centered networks can reduce NK- and CD8+ T-cell effector activity, potentially allowing persistence of activated fibroblast states and ongoing extracellular matrix remodeling [64,65].

Collectively, these multidimensional cellular interactions are consistent with a macrophage-centered immunoregulatory network that favors continued fibroblast activation and pathological fibrosis. From a translational perspective, key nodes within these immunoregulatory circuits, including macrophage polarization states, cytokine signaling pathways centered on IL-10 and TGF-β, and inhibitory immune checkpoint pathways, representing rational targets for therapeutic modulation in keloids. These potential intervention strategies are discussed in greater detail in the treatment-focused section of this review. Such macrophage-centered circuits likely operate within a broader niche architecture supported by stromal and neuroglial cues, including Schwann cell–associated programs that reinforce ECM remodeling and may stabilize immunoregulatory microdomains.

Clinicopathological significance of M2 macrophages in keloids

Histologically, keloid lesions exhibit significantly increased numbers of CD68+CD163+ or CD68+CD206+ M2-like macrophages compared with normal skin or hypertrophic scars [66]. These cells tend to localize to proliferative regions and invasive margins, correlating with denser collagen bundles, abundant myofibroblast proliferation, and extensive microvascularization. Key profibrotic factors secreted by M2-like macrophages, including TGF-β1, PDGF-BB, and VEGF, directly promote excessive fibroblast activation, collagen deposition, and angiogenesis [67]. Clinically, elevated infiltration of M2-like macrophages and a more pronounced M2-skewed macrophage profile have been associated with greater lesional activity and more prominent fibrotic features in keloids.

Importantly, higher-quality longitudinal human data are beginning to link macrophage states to treatment response. In a randomized controlled setting with pre- and post-treatment biopsies, keloids that respond to intralesional therapy show measurable remodeling of the immune compartment, including shifts in macrophage polarization states alongside changes in regulatory T-cell signatures. These observations provide clinically anchored support that macrophage programs are not merely correlative but can track with (and potentially contribute to) therapeutic outcomes, strengthening the rationale for macrophage-targeted or macrophage-rebalancing strategies in keloids [55].

Collectively, M2-like macrophage abundance and activation status not only drive characteristic keloid pathology like excessive fibrosis, aberrant angiogenesis, and immune suppression, but also reliably predict clinical severity, recurrence risk, and systemic immune alterations.

CD8+ T cells and cytotoxic dysfunction in keloids

The cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells is essential for immune surveillance and the elimination of aberrant cells. In tumor settings, CD8+ T lymphocytes can rapidly mediate target-cell killing and their abundance is often inversely associated with stromal fibroblast–dominant microenvironments, highlighting the importance of cytotoxic competence in shaping tissue outcomes [68]. In keloids, however, emerging human transcriptomic evidence suggests that the key issue is not simply whether CD8+ T cells are present, but how their functional state is reprogrammed within a fibrotic niche [69]. Notably, an activity- and region-aware scRNA-seq study stratifying lesions as active vs. inactive and sampling periphery vs. center indicates stage- and location-dependent immune restraint, in which cytotoxic lymphocyte programs appear more constrained in inactive/central regions than at the expanding edge [70]. Consistently, integrative analyses combining single-cell signals with bulk immune signatures further support altered cytotoxic T-cell related programs in keloid immune landscapes (Table 1), while a treatment-anchored single-cell study of intralesional TAC+ 5-FU suggests that therapy can remodel immune-cell states, including cytotoxic lymphocyte–associated programs, alongside broader microenvironmental rewiring (Table 2). Collectively, these studies converge on a common message: CD8+ T cells are a reproducible component of the keloid CD45+ compartment, yet they often exhibit checkpoint-engaged/functional impairment–leaning states rather than sustained, robust cytotoxic activation, supporting the concept of cytotoxic restraint within keloid lesions.

At the cellular-subset level, keloid CD8+ T cells also appear heterogeneous. Wang et al. reported that the proportion of CD28+CD8+ T cells was higher in keloid patients than in controls, and in vitro assays suggested that CD8+ T cells can modulate fibroblast activity under both direct and indirect exposure conditions [71]. Importantly, the expansion of CD8+CD28- subsets observed in some contexts may reflect a more differentiated and potentially regulatory-leaning state; in certain patients, this shift could contribute to an immune-restrained microenvironment rather than effective cytotoxic surveillance. These observations underscore that CD8+ T cells should not be treated as a single functional entity in keloids; instead, subset composition and state transitions are likely to determine whether CD8+ T cells exert anti-fibrotic restraint or become functionally constrained within the lesion [72].

Although several studies describe an overall reduction of CD8+ T cells in keloid tissue and/or circulation, more recent flow-cytometric analyses have identified increased effector memory CD8+ T cells and CD103+CD8+ tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM) within keloid lesions. Given the long-lived nature of TRM cells, persistent TRM populations may provide a local immune memory that facilitates recurrence after excision; however, whether TRM cells directly drive relapse through sustained cytokine output or act indirectly by shaping local immune–stromal interactions will require longitudinal, treatment-anchored validation [73]. Overall, current evidence supports a model in which CD8+ T cells are present but frequently functionally reprogrammed in keloids, with inhibitory/checkpoint engagement and resident-memory features emerging as clinically relevant axes for future investigation.

The role of dendritic cells in keloids

In the dermis, conventional dendritic cells (cDCs) are resident immune cells critical for pathogen defense and tissue homeostasis. Emerging evidence indicates that dendritic cell subsets significantly influence the immunopathology of keloids. Compared to normal skin, keloid tissues exhibit markedly reduced infiltration of CD4+ effector T cells, MDSCs, and both activated and immature dendritic cells [52]. A detailed analysis of the mononuclear phagocyte system in keloid lesions reveals the presence of conventional type I dendritic cells (cDC1s), conventional type II dendritic cells (cDC2s), and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), with cDC2s and macrophages being the dominant populations [74].

cDC2s represent the predominant dendritic cell subset found in human peripheral blood, as well as in various tissues and lymphoid organs, and are distinguished by their broad immunomodulatory capabilities. Notably, cDC2s secrete elevated levels of IL-12 and other cytokines, including IL-8, IL-10, and TNF-α, thereby facilitating robust activation of diverse T cell populations such as Th1, Th2, Th17, and CD8+ T cells [75,76]. Previous research underscores the pathological enrichment of factor XIIIa (FXIIIa)-expressing dendritic cells within keloid tissues relative to hypertrophic and mature scars [77]. These FXIIIa-positive dendritic cells, functioning as potent antigen-presenting cells (APCs), drive pathogenic interactions between epidermal and dermal layers, contributing significantly to persistent fibrosis. Critically, dendritic cell-derived TGF-β promotes Treg differentiation, further establishing an altered immune microenvironment conducive to chronic fibrosis. Concurrently, cDC2-driven activation of Th2 cells enhances secretion of profibrotic cytokines, such as IL-4 and IL-13 [78], which potentiate ECM synthesis by fibroblasts. Therapeutically targeting cDC2s and downstream Th2 signaling pathways thus represents a promising approach to mitigate excessive ECM deposition and attenuate keloid formation, opening novel avenues for antifibrotic therapies.

Natural killer cells and restrained cytotoxicity in keloids

Natural killer (NK) cells are innate cytotoxic lymphocytes that contribute to immune surveillance through the production of IFN-γ and TNF-α and through direct cytolytic activity. In keloids, current evidence suggests that the key issue is not simply NK-cell abundance, but the presence of a functionally restrained cytotoxic program within the lesional immune microenvironment. In particular, the NKG2A/CD94–HLA-E axis identified in keloids provides a plausible inhibitory mechanism linking reduced cytotoxic immune surveillance to persistent fibroproliferation [79,80]. Although the role of NK cells in keloids remains less well defined than that of macrophages or Tregs, available evidence supports the concept that inhibitory signaling and immune-checkpoint engagement may limit effective cytotoxic responses in lesional niches. In parallel, amphiregulin (AREG), a known profibrotic mediator in skin fibrosis, may represent an additional pathway favoring fibroblast survival and matrix remodeling, although direct evidence defining NK cells as the dominant source of AREG in keloids is still lacking [81]. Moreover, TGF-β-rich fibrotic microenvironments are known to suppress NK-cell cytotoxicity, including inhibition of IFN-γ production and downregulation of activating receptors, providing a mechanistic framework for the functionally restrained NK-cell state observed in keloid lesions [11]. Together, these findings suggest that impaired NK-cell cytotoxicity may contribute to immune evasion and persistent fibrosis in keloids, and that restoration of cytotoxic immune surveillance may offer a potential therapeutic avenue [11,79,80].

The role of myeloid-derived suppressor cells in keloids

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) constitute a heterogeneous population of immature myeloid progenitor cells originating from the bone marrow, exhibiting strong immunosuppressive capacities and serving as precursors to dendritic cells, macrophages, and granulocytes. Recent studies have highlighted the pivotal role of MDSCs in tissue remodeling and wound healing through the secretion of TGF-β and arginase-1 (ARG1), which significantly enhance collagen synthesis by fibroblasts, thus facilitating accelerated tissue repair [82]. Additionally, the production of nitric oxide (NO) by MDSCs through inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) plays a significant role in wound healing. This process involves multiple mechanisms, including the modulation of vascular responses and the stimulation of fibroblast proliferation [83].

Keloids, characterized by excessive scar formation, demonstrate features consistent with a Th2-skewed immune response. Recent findings indicate significant correlations between gene expression patterns and immune cell populations within keloids. Specifically, FOXF1 and LPAR1 expression negatively correlates with the abundance of monocytes and follicular helper T cells (Tfh), while positively correlating with MDSC infiltration. Meanwhile, in the keloid immune microenvironment, SASPs (CXCL1, CXCL2, GM-CSF, M-CSF, etc.) released by SRP-fibroblasts are highly efficient in recruiting MDSCs to create an immunosuppressive niche; MDSCs create an immune-restrained niche [84,85], through inhibition of NK cells, CD8+ T cells, and promotion of M2-macrophage polarization, further weakening the immune surveillance that removes senescent/abnormal fibroblasts and maintaining the chronic proliferative state of the keloid, while potentially increasing resistance to surgical or pharmacological treatments.

Schwann cells as neuro–immune modulators in keloids

Beyond immune-cell–centric suppression, recent single-cell work has brought peripheral-nerve–associated stromal programs into the keloid narrative, with Schwann cells (SCs) emerging as a reproducible, lesion-enriched population rather than a negligible bystander. In a human scRNA-seq comparison of keloids vs. normal scars, SCs were expanded in keloids (reported as ∼4.13-fold higher than normal scar), and keloid SCs showed markedly increased IGFBP5 expression (reported as ∼8.02-fold higher than SCs in normal scars), together with enrichment of ECM-related pathways, supporting a pro-fibrotic, mesenchymal-leaning SC state in keloid tissue [86].

Mechanistically, these data suggest that Schwann cells can engage the canonical fibroblast–vascular remodeling axis through the release of growth-permissive signals. Recombinant IGFBP5 increased pro-fibrotic readouts, including the induction of TGF-β, promoted collagen synthesis and angiogenic behavior in vitro, and exacerbated scar growth in vivo, accompanied by elevated TGF-β signaling and collagen deposition. Pathway analyses further linked IGFBP5 driven fibroblast proliferation to modulation of the p53–p21–Cyclin D1 axis [87]. In line with clinically prioritized symptoms such as pain and pruritus, integrated single-cell RNA sequencing analyses across multiple human datasets identified an expanded IGFBP5+ Schwann cell subpopulation positioned in closer proximity to dominant extracellular matrix–producing fibroblasts. This interaction was characterized by selective activation of Midkine (MDK) signaling in fibroblast–Schwann cell communication. Notably, MDK expression correlated with symptom severity, and MDK stimulation promoted Schwann cell proliferation and a repair-associated phenotype that enhanced neuronal axonogenesis and neuropeptide release, including substance P. Conversely, MDK inhibition reduced aberrant Schwann cell expansion and downstream neuropeptide output [88].

Taken together, these findings argue that SCs provide a neuro–fibrotic bridge in keloids: SC expansion and state remodeling (e.g. IGFBP5-high, repair-like programs) may simultaneously reinforce fibroblast proliferation/ECM deposition and shape the symptomatic neuroinflammatory tone through fibroblast-derived mediators such as MDK. This framework provides a practical extension of the concept of low overt inflammation with progressive fibrosis in keloids, in which immune effector outputs appear attenuated while stromal–neural signaling circuits remain highly active. It further motivates therapeutic considerations aimed at disrupting fibroblast–Schwann cell interactions, as MDK- and IGFBP5 associated signaling axes, together with the modulation of immune checkpoint pathways and suppressive myeloid programs discussed above (Table 2).

Clinical applications and future prospects

With the deepening of research, the application of immune cells in keloid treatment has become an increasing concern and shows good prospects in clinical research through cell transplantation and in vitro expansion, these cells show great potential in regulating local immune response, inhibit fibrosis, and promote tissue repair. Optimizing cell therapy strategies to ensure long-term stability and safety will be a key area of future research, making immune cells a promising approach for the treatment of keloids and other fibrotic diseases.

Landscape of reported therapies and clinical evidence

Keloid management remains predominantly local and multimodal, which is consistent with a biology in which fibroblast activation and ECM persistence are maintained within stage- and region-dependent niches rather than by uniformly high-grade inflammation [89]. Recent reviews generally support combination approaches over monotherapy, and the therapeutic pipeline is gradually expanding toward immunomodulatory strategies that can be evaluated in prospective clinical settings.

Intralesional injection therapies (most commonly corticosteroid-based regimens, often in combination) remain a mainstay, but recent double-blinded RCT–linked biopsy studies refine what ‘response’ represents at the tissue level. In pre-/post-treatment biopsies sampled from the active border, clinical responders show coordinated immune remodeling: anti-inflammatory macrophage programs become more prominent, whereas Treg-associated signals diminish in the superficial dermis, consistent with a shift along the macrophage–Treg axis discussed in Sections 4 and 5 [55]. A complementary RCT-derived analysis further argues against hypoxia as a dominant feature at the keloid growth edge (using HIF-1α and CA9 as markers), and instead links response to dynamic vascular/lymphatic remodeling in the mid-dermis during treatment [90]. Together, these controlled datasets connect clinical improvement to measurable immune–vascular rewiring rather than nonspecific suppression of inflammation [55,90].

Beyond established local approaches, therapies that intersect immune–fibrotic signaling are beginning to enter prospective evaluation. IL-4Rα blockade (dupilumab) has progressed beyond anecdotal reports into clinical testing, including a randomized, placebo-controlled study in keloids (NCT04988022; 52-week treatment phase) and an open-label proof-of-concept study (NCT05128383) with a defined completion timeline. Conceptually, targeting type 2–skewed programs is compatible with the observation that Th2-leaning immunoregulatory signals can coexist with progressive fibrosis even when overt inflammation appears muted. In addition, botulinum toxin A has been explored as an adjuvant after excision, with a recent clinical study in ear keloids reporting improved cosmetic outcomes with immediate post-surgical injection, supporting its use in anatomically constrained sites where minimizing tension and modifying scar biology are both relevant [91,92]. Although BTX-A is not an immune drug, it can be integrated into an immune–mechanical–fibrotic coupling framework, in which reduced tension-associated profibrotic signaling may indirectly shift local cytokine tone.

Two practical implications emerge from recent biopsy-linked clinical evidence. First, clinical improvement is associated with remodeling of immune and stromal states, including changes in macrophage polarization balance, regulatory T cell dynamics, and vascular and lymphatic architecture, rather than a uniform suppression of inflammatory activity. Second, these response-linked signatures reinforce the importance of stage- and region-aware sampling (active border vs. center; active vs. inactive lesions) when interpreting immune proportions and checkpoint-associated restraint signals, aligning with the activity-stratified single-cell evidence summarized earlier (Table 2). Despite incremental progress, mechanism-aligned, adequately powered, blinded RCTs remain limited [93]. Near-term priorities include harmonized outcome measures (flattening/recurrence plus symptom endpoints), pre-specified tissue biomarker panels anchored to macrophage–Treg–checkpoint axes, and enrollment stratification by lesion stage/region to reduce biological heterogeneity; ongoing and recently completed dupilumab trials provide a workable template for immune-pathway targeting that can be extended to other immunoregulatory nodes highlighted in this review.

Therapeutic strategies for keloid based on Tregs

Naoki Murao et al. conducted co-culture experiments involving Tregs and keloid fibroblasts, demonstrating that Treg-enriched CD4+ T cells diminished the presence of CD4+ T cells within the co-culture, subsequently reducing the expression of type I collagen and TGF-β mRNA in keloid fibroblasts (KFs) [36]. Additionally, rapamycin was shown to inhibit effector T cell proliferation, thus facilitating the expansion of Tregs. Furthermore, rapamycin reduced both collagen synthesis and α-SMA expression in KFs [94,95]. These findings suggest that topical application of rapamycin could influence keloid formation by modulating Treg populations and directly impacting fibroblast activity.

Therapeutic strategies for keloid based on CD8+ T cells

Recent scRNA-seq studies have further confirmed that CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) are markedly reduced in both the peripheral blood and lesion sites of keloid patients, whereas the NKG2A/CD94 complex is significantly upregulated. Given that CD8+ T cells are critical for suppressing fibroblast proliferation and activation, the overexpression of NKG2A/CD94 within keloid lesions may further diminish CTL function, indirectly enhancing aberrant fibroblast activity and excessive extracellular matrix deposition.

Building on the latest advances in tumor immunotherapy, the monoclonal antibody monalizumab specifically blocks the NKG2A/CD94 pathway, thereby restoring the effector functions of NK cells and CD8+ T cells [93,96]. Notably, when combined with the blockade of the programmed cell death (PD) axis, monalizumab has been shown to significantly boost antitumor immune responses [97]. However, monalizumab has been shown to have some side effects that await more clinical exploration. Translating this approach to keloid management, inhibiting NKG2A/CD94 may reinvigorate CD8+ T cell functionality, thereby reinstating their suppressive effect on fibroblast proliferation and activation. As a result, it holds the potential to limit excessive extracellular matrix deposition and mitigate keloid progression.

Challenges and future prospects of immune cells in keloid

Immune cells, including Tregs, M2 macrophages, DCs, NK cells, and CD8+ T cells, play crucial roles in modulating immune responses and maintaining tissue homeostasis. However, in keloid pathogenesis, these cells exhibit dysregulated activity, contributing to the formation of an altered immune microenvironment that promotes excessive fibroblast proliferation and ECM deposition. Additionally, DCs, which are pivotal in antigen presentation and initiating adaptive immune responses, often adopt a tolerogenic phenotype in keloids, contributing to immune evasion and persistence of fibroproliferative activity. Similarly, NK cells, which play a critical role in cytotoxicity against aberrant cells, show impaired functionality in keloid tissues, limiting their ability to regulate abnormal fibroblast proliferation. CD8+ T cells, which typically mediate cytotoxic responses against pathogenic cells, exhibit functional exhaustion in the keloid microenvironment, characterized by reduced cytokine production and impaired cytolytic function. This immune imbalance, where pro-inflammatory responses are suppressed while pro-fibrotic signals dominate, highlights the complexity of keloid pathogenesis and presents a significant therapeutic challenge.

Future research directions should focus on restoring balanced immune regulation and restoring DC and NK cell functionality to enhance immune surveillance. Strategies aimed at reversing CD8+ T cell exhaustion through immune checkpoint inhibitors or cytokine modulation may help restore immune control over fibroblast proliferation. Additionally, targeted immunotherapies, gene editing technologies, and personalized approaches based on immune profiling hold promise for mitigating fibrosis while preserving normal wound healing. Identifying reliable biomarkers to predict treatment responses will further aid in the development of effective, patient-specific therapeutic strategies for keloid management.

Conclusion

Keloid is a pathological fibrotic disorder with complex pathogenesis. Accumulating evidence indicates that keloid lesions are shaped by stage- and region-dependent immune regulation rather than uniformly high-grade inflammation. In particular, immunoregulatory circuits involving Tregs, M2-like macrophages, tolerogenic antigen-presenting states, and checkpoint-associated restraint of cytotoxic lymphocytes can dampen overt inflammatory outputs while permitting persistent fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition. Therefore, therapeutically rebalancing key immune–fibrotic circuits—especially those supported by human single-cell and biopsy-linked clinical evidence—may complement established local treatments and help improve keloid outcomes. This innovative therapeutic approach offers new therapeutic options for keloid patients by co-modulating the immune microenvironment and the fibrotic process. In the future, with continued optimization of cell preparation technologies and expansion of clinical studies, improving the efficacy, safety, and long-term stability of immune-based therapies will remain a major research priority. Beyond their therapeutic potential in keloids, these strategies may also offer broader insights into the integration of immune regulation and regenerative medicine.
