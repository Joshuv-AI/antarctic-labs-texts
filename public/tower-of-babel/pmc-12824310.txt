An integrated and sustainable process for selective separation of zinc from arsenic-bearing lead smelting residue by solvent extraction
Authors: Mohammad Hossein Davoudi, Hossein Kamran Haghighi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study focuses on the development of a solvent–extraction–based purification route for the selective separation of zinc and arsenic from the acidic leach solution generated by a lead smelting furnace residue in a metallurgical facility in Zanjan, Iran. The residue contained high levels of zinc (14.75%) alongside lead (7.50%), arsenic (4.00%), and chloride (9.00%), making it a critical target for resource recovery and environmental remediation. This research aims to develop an effective hydrometallurgical route for the selective recovery of zinc and the removal of arsenic from lead smelting furnace residue. The process includes chloride elimination followed by sulfuric acid leaching to obtain an acidic zinc-bearing solution as the feed for downstream purification. Two independent purification approaches were briefly examined to provide process context. A precipitation route using iron-based oxidizing agents enhanced arsenic removal but could not reduce the final concentration to electrowinning-acceptable levels. The primary focus of this study is a D2EHPA-based solvent extraction process applied to the same leach solution in a separate treatment pathway. This method involved four extraction stages, followed by two scrubbing steps and two counter-current stripping stages. The solvent extraction system achieved deep arsenic removal (0.33 mg/L in the final electrolyte) and simultaneously enriched zinc to 51,790 mg/L, fully meeting the requirements for electrowinning. The results demonstrate that solvent extraction provides a highly efficient and technically robust purification route for producing high-purity zinc from complex leach solutions derived from lead smelting furnace residue.Keywords: Zinc, Solvent extraction, D2EHPA, Arsenic, RecyclingSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Metal recycling has gained significant importance for environmental and economic reasons, with zinc among the most widely recycled metals globally1–3. As high-grade ore deposits diminish, there is an increasing trend toward recovering zinc from secondary sources, particularly industrial waste4,5. Pyrometallurgical waste from lead production represents a substantial source of recoverable zinc, particularly the residues generated in lead smelting furnaces, which contain significant amounts of zinc along with environmentally hazardous elements such as arsenic6,7. The presence of arsenic poses environmental challenges due to its toxicity and persistence, necessitating effective removal strategies before zinc recovery8. Despite its importance, the processing of lead smelting furnace residues has received almost no attention in the scientific literature, and the available knowledge is limited compared to other metallurgical by-products. In particular, no detailed studies have examined lead smelting furnace residues with the complex and unusual mineralogical characteristics observed in the present waste material. This lack of research highlights the need for a systematic investigation of zinc recovery and arsenic separation from such chemically challenging residues. Precipitation remains one of the most widely applied and technically reliable approaches for arsenic removal in hydrometallurgical systems, and its effectiveness has been demonstrated in numerous studies9–11. However, our preliminary tests showed that under the unusually high arsenic concentration and simultaneous presence of zinc in the leach solution, the precipitation route became highly sensitive to pH. Achieving the pH required for complete arsenic removal increased the risk of zinc co-precipitation, whereas operating at slightly lower pH values resulted in incomplete arsenic separation. Due to the strong pH dependence of the specific chemistry of the present residue, precipitation was evaluated only as an initial reference method, while the main focus of this work was on solvent extraction using D2EHPA. Based on extensive hydrometallurgical studies on zinc purification, solvent extraction with D2EHPA has been widely recognized as a robust method for separating zinc from dissolved impurities in acidic media12,13. After our previous work on arsenic removal by precipitation from the same leach solution did not fully achieve the required residual arsenic level for electrowinning, D2EHPA was selected as an alternative route for zinc purification. In typical zinc systems, D2EHPA is used at concentrations of about 10–30 vol% in kerosene, at equilibrium pH values in the range 2–5 and moderate aqueous-to-organic phase ratios (A/O ≈ 1–2), conditions under which zinc is efficiently transferred to the organic phase in the form of Zn2+–D2EHPA complexes13,14. A cation-exchange reaction governs the extraction mechanism whereby divalent zinc cations are selectively loaded into the organic phase. In contrast, arsenic in acidic sulfate solutions is present predominantly as oxyanion species (H2AsO4−, HAsO42−), which are not extracted by acidic organophosphorus extractants such as D2EHPA15. This fundamental difference in speciation allows zinc to be readily extracted, while arsenic remains in the raffinate, making D2EHPA an inherently suitable extractant for Zn/As separation. The present study focuses explicitly on the selective behavior of zinc and arsenic in a complex leach solution derived from lead smelting furnace residue. In the proposed flowsheet, arsenic is distributed between the aqueous raffinate and the solid leach residue. Both streams can be treated through established industrial methods such as ferric arsenate precipitation or lime–ferric neutralization9,16,17. In parallel, the authors are investigating selective solvent extraction of arsenic from the raffinate as a potential recovery route18,19. Together, these options offer a comprehensive and practical strategy for managing arsenic generated during the process.

Materials and methods

Material

In this study, the sample was obtained from the pyrometallurgical waste generated by a lead smelting furnace at a factory in Zanjan, Iran. The dust emissions from the furnace atmosphere pass through two main filtration stages after entering the chimney system. The first stage is dry filtration, followed by wet filtration. In dry filtration, the dust is collected in bag filters. During wet filtration, the dust, after cooling, enters the aqueous phase via water. In slurry form, it enters a thickener where the solid and liquid phases are separated. The solid phase, which collects at the bottom of the thickener, is fed into a filter press and is identified as “Cake 1.” Cake 1 contains high levels of lead and is recharged into the furnace along with the input feed. The thickener solution is sent from the top of the thickener to neutralization tanks, where it is neutralized using lime. As the pH increases to approximately 10, all metals and elements, including zinc, arsenic, and chloride, are removed from the solution phase and separated from the system using a filter press. The resulting cake, known as “Cake 2,” was used in the experiments of this research (Fig. 1).

Reagents and equipment

The experimental work utilized both analytical and industrial-grade chemicals. Sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 98%, Merck) served as the leaching agent. Arsenic precipitation was achieved using potassium permanganate (KMnO4) and ferrous sulfate (FeSO4)—the solvent extraction process employed di-2-ethylhexyl phosphoric acid (D2EHPA) from PayaShimi Co., diluted in kerosene. EDTA and xylene orange were used for zinc titration. Hydrated lime (Zanjan Zinc Industry Co.) was used to regulate pH. The equipment included a Dragon Lab OS20-Pro mechanical stirrer, a hot plate stirrer, a Schott CG 825 pH meter, an Agilent 200 Series atomic absorption spectrometer, separator funnels, and a vacuum filtration apparatus.

Methods

This section outlines the sequential experimental procedures employed for zinc recovery from lead smelting furnace waste. The methodology follows a process chain beginning with pretreatment to remove chloride contaminants, followed by mild acid leaching to solubilize zinc and arsenic. The resulting leach solution was then processed through selective arsenic precipitation followed by solvent extraction to recover zinc. This paper provides a brief overview of the technique, with a primary focus on the solvent extraction process, detailing its extraction, scrubbing, and stripping steps. The following subsections describe each stage of the overall recovery process.

Pretreatment process

Before the acid leaching stage, the Cake 2 underwent necessary preparation, including crushing and sizing to achieve an appropriate particle distribution. The material was ground and passed through a 150-mesh sieve (equivalent to 105 μm), ensuring optimal surface area for the subsequent leaching process. After that, a water washing procedure was implemented to minimize the chloride content, which could potentially interfere with subsequent recovery processes. The samples were treated with deionized water at ambient temperature and gentle agitation to facilitate the removal of chloride. In lead smelting operations, chloride commonly originates from flux additives and auxiliary materials used during furnace processing. These chloride species typically form highly water-soluble salts that do not bind strongly to the solid matrix. During the washing step, these soluble chloride compounds readily dissolved into the wash water and were separated from the solid residue through filtration20,21. Upon heating the chloride-rich wash water during drying, visible chloride crystals were formed, confirming that the removed chloride existed predominantly as water-soluble species. After washing, the samples were filtered and dried at 105 °C for 24 h before proceeding to the acid leaching step. The chloride content was analyzed before and after pretreatment using the Mohr titration method.

Acid leaching

Due to its proven effectiveness in hydrometallurgical applications, sulfuric acid was selected as the primary leaching agent in experiments. Several parameters influence leaching efficiency: acid concentration, temperature, reaction time, and liquid-to-solid ratio. Each parameter was varied independently while maintaining the others constant to identify the most significant parameters affecting zinc extraction. Following each leaching experiment, the pulp was filtered to separate the pregnant leach solution (PLS) from the solid residue. The leached cake was thoroughly washed, dried in a laboratory oven at 105 °C until it reached a constant weight, and then preserved for further analysis. Both the leach solutions and dried residues were analyzed using atomic absorption spectroscopy to determine elemental compositions. Precipitation method

To reduce arsenic concentration in the PLS, precipitation was carried out using ferrous sulfate in combination with oxidizing agents, including potassium permanganate (KMnO4), manganese dioxide (MnO2), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). The effects of oxidant type, temperature, reaction time, and pH were evaluated. Optimal conditions were selected based on arsenic removal efficiency, measured by analyzing residual arsenic in the treated solution.

Solvent extraction process

Zinc separation was further enhanced using solvent extraction with a 30% D2EHPA and 70% kerosene mixture. The process involved three sub-steps:

Extraction: Various aqueous-to-organic (A:O) phase ratios were tested to transfer zinc from the PLS into the organic phase without pH adjustment. Equilibrated phases were separated and analyzed for zinc content22.

Extraction: Various aqueous-to-organic (A:O) phase ratios were tested to transfer zinc from the PLS into the organic phase without pH adjustment. Equilibrated phases were separated and analyzed for zinc content22.

Scrubbing: To remove co-extracted impurities, a two-stage scrubbing sequence was applied. The loaded organic phase was first washed with water (O:A = 4:1), followed by scrubbing with a solution containing 8500 mg/l Zn and 14,000 mg/l H2SO4 (O:A = 10:1). This ensured the selective removal of impurities with minimal zinc loss.

The loaded organic phase was first washed with water (O:A = 4:1), followed by scrubbing with a solution containing 8500 mg/l Zn and 14,000 mg/l H2SO4 (O:A = 10:1). This ensured the selective removal of impurities with minimal zinc loss.

Stripping: Zinc was stripped from the organic phase using 20,000 mg/l Zn and 140,000 mg/l H2SO4. The process was optimized at an O:A ratio of 4:1 in a two-stage counter-current mode. The resulting zinc-rich aqueous solution was suitable for final recovery.

Results and discussion

Characterization

Mineralogical composition

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the waste sample is shown in Fig. 2. The analysis indicates a complex mineralogy dominated by various hydrated calcium sulfate phases, including basanite (CaSO4·0.5 H2O), gypsum (CaSO4·2 H2O), and hemihydrate calcium sulfate (CaSO4·0.67 H2O). The detection of calcite (CaCO3) confirms the use of lime in the neutralization process. Additionally, the presence of zinc hydroxide nitrate ammonia [Zn5(OH)8(NO3)2·1.3NH3·0.7 H2O] suggests that a significant portion of zinc precipitated during treatment. The identification of lead arsenate hydroxide [Pb5(AsO4) 3OH] further confirms the co-precipitation of toxic elements, such as lead and arsenic. Overall, the observed mineral phases reflect the multi-elemental nature of the pyrometallurgical waste and the effectiveness of the neutralization process in stabilizing hazardous components. However, the presence of zinc- and arsenic-bearing compounds suggests the potential for the selective recovery of both valuable and hazardous elements.

Microstructural and elemental analysis

Figure 3a shows the BSE micrograph of the as-received residue at 500× magnification. The bright regions in the BSE image correspond to zones containing higher average atomic number elements, while darker areas reflect lighter oxide–silicate components. This contrast pattern indicates the coexistence of multiple metal-bearing phases within a single particle. Elemental distribution was examined using EDX mapping on the same region. The Zn map in Fig. 3b shows a dense and widespread signal throughout the particle, indicating that zinc-bearing phases are finely distributed rather than present as isolated clusters. This uniform dispersion is consistent with zinc being incorporated into mixed oxide phases generated during high-temperature processing. Similarly, the Pb map in Fig. 3c displays an almost identical widespread distribution pattern, confirming that lead is also dispersed throughout the particle rather than concentrated in discrete separate grains. The matched distribution of Zn and Pb suggests that these metals coexist within mixed heavy-oxide domains, consistent with the BSE contrast observed in Fig. 3a. The chlorine map in Fig. 3d reveals a much weaker and scattered signal compared with Zn and Pb. This fine, dot-like distribution indicates that Cl is present mainly as surface-associated, water-soluble chloride species rather than as well-defined crystalline chloride phases.

Arsenic precipitation

Arsenic precipitation using Fe2+/oxidant systems was investigated to purify the leach solution9,10. Five parameters were optimized: Fe2+–oxidant ratio, reaction time, temperature, pH, and oxidant type. Among potassium permanganate, manganese dioxide, and hydrogen peroxide, KMnO4 exhibited the highest performance, removing up to 99.94% of arsenic and lowering its concentration from 2000 mg/L to < 20 mg/L, consistent with prior studies27. Its superior efficiency arises from strong oxidation of arsenite to arsenate (Eq. 2) 28 and concurrent oxidation of Fe2+ to Fe3+ (Eq. 3) 29, which then reacts with arsenate to form insoluble ferric arsenate dehydrate (Eq. 4) 28.

The process performed best at 25 °C, where efficiency reached 99.96%, offering energy savings. pH strongly affected selectivity: removal increased from pH 2–5.5, peaking at 99.99% at pH 5.5, but zinc co-precipitation rose (30–45%) above pH 5 30. Thermodynamic modeling using Medusa software confirmed that manganese begins to co-precipitate with arsenic as Mn3(AsO4)2·8 H2O at pH values above 4. At the same time, zinc co-precipitation as Zn3(AsO4)2·2.5 H2O becomes significant at pH values above 5.5 (Fig. 5A and B). Therefore, pH 4.5 was identified as the optimal condition, providing maximum arsenic removal while maintaining both zinc and manganese in solution for subsequent recovery.

Extraction stage

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of the solvent extraction process and establish the optimal conditions for efficient separation, systematic experiments were conducted using a wide range of aqueous-to-organic (A:O) phase ratios. The objective was to determine the ideal number of extraction stages and the most effective phase ratio to maximize extraction efficiency while maintaining selectivity31. In many conventional solvent-extraction studies, the equilibrium pH is externally controlled and typically maintained at about 2–2.5, which is considered optimal for zinc extraction. In the present work, however, external pH control was not applied. Instead, the neutralized leachate, initially at pH around 4–5, experienced a rapid spontaneous decrease in pH to below 2 upon contact with the acidic organic phase (30% v/v D2EHPA), owing to proton release from the extractant. This rapid drop limited the time during which zinc could be extracted under favorable pH conditions. During this transition, zinc and manganese competed for extraction sites. Under these acidic conditions, D2EHPA efficiently extracts Zn(II) (a cationic species). In contrast, arsenic remains predominantly as oxyanions (H2AsO4−/HAsO42−) that are not extracted by acidic organophosphorus extractants and therefore stay in the aqueous phase18. Among the common metal ions present, iron(III) exhibits the highest affinity for D2EHPA, followed by zinc(II), whereas manganese(II) and the remaining divalent cations display progressively weaker extraction tendencies32,33, Zinc was extracted preferentially over manganese. This behavior highlights that the spontaneous pH-drop approach favors zinc recovery and restricts manganese co-extraction, which would otherwise be more pronounced in classical pH-controlled systems22,31. The investigated A:O Ratios included 4:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, 1:4, and 1:10, covering a broad spectrum of operational scenarios with varying organic loadings. These experiments provided critical data for understanding the mass transfer behavior and phase equilibrium under different mixing conditions. The detailed outcomes of these tests are presented in Table 6, which highlights the impact of phase ratio variations on extraction performance. The choice of fixed parameters such as pH, extractant concentration, and contact time was guided by insights from previous laboratory and pilot-scale studies22,31.

Extraction isotherms and McCabe-Thiele diagrams were constructed to interpret the experimental data further and model the system’s behavior. These graphical tools are essential for visualizing equilibrium relationships and estimating the number of theoretical stages required to achieve a target separation level. The extraction isotherms provide insight into the distribution of metal ions between the two phases. At the same time, the McCabe–Thiele approach enables a clear understanding of the stage-wise progression in counter-current extraction systems34. All experiments were performed at a constant temperature of 25 °C to ensure the consistency and reliability of the data. The corresponding diagrams, illustrated in Fig. 6, demonstrate the system’s behavior under these controlled conditions and serve as a basis for process optimization.

Analysis of these diagrams indicates that under conditions where the organic to aqueous phase ratio (O:A) is 1:1, the extraction stages were found at four stages. Thus, with an input solution containing 12,180 mg/l Zinc, a final concentration of 2800 mg/l was obtained after these four counter-current extraction stages, corresponding to a zinc recovery efficiency of 80.0%. A batch simulation of solvent extraction was performed to apply this four-stage design. As observed, zinc concentrations in the Raffinate solutions (aqueous phase) and in the organic solution after each extraction cycle were measured and recorded in Table 7. In this regard, 34 experiments were designed and executed under identical operating conditions. Aqueous and organic solutions were separated in each experiment using a decanter after the extraction stages. These experiments were conducted under uniform conditions: An O:A phase ratio of 1:1, a temperature of 25 °C, and a contact time of 15 min. The solvent extraction of zinc using D2EHPA proceeds via a cation exchange mechanism in which zinc ions from the aqueous phase are transferred into the organic phase. When zinc is extracted into the organic phase, protons are returned to the aqueous phase, lowering the pH. This behavior necessitates careful pH control during multistage extraction to maintain optimal efficiency. Under the experimental conditions used here, the results show a consistent decrease in zinc content in the aqueous phase at each stage, confirming the progressive extraction. In contrast, only a very minor transfer of arsenic to the organic phase was observed (< 5% of the initial concentration, ~ 55 mg/L), compared with more than 9000 mg/L Zn. This indicates that D2EHPA exhibits very high selectivity toward zinc under the applied conditions. The low affinity of D2EHPA for arsenic is consistent with the predominant anionic speciation of arsenic (H2AsO4−, HAsO42−) in the tested pH range, which prevents efficient extraction by an acidic extractant. This is attributed to the fact that under the pH conditions used (typically \~2–5), arsenic exists mainly in anionic forms (e.g., H2AsO4−, HAsO42−). D2EHPA, an acidic extractant, lacks the basic functional groups required to form extractable ion-pairs with these negatively charged arsenic species. Therefore, arsenic remains in the aqueous Raffinate and is effectively separated from zinc. This selective extraction behavior is particularly advantageous for purifying zinc-containing leachates with arsenic impurities. The zinc and arsenic extraction isotherms also support the observed extraction behavior. Zinc shows strong partitioning into the organic phase as its concentration increases, resulting in a characteristic isotherm. In contrast, arsenic shows negligible transfer to the organic phase, resulting in a near-flat isotherm. Furthermore, zinc extraction efficiency increases significantly as pH rises from \~2.5 to \~5.0, while arsenic remains unaffected over this pH range. This difference results in an approximate equilibrium pH shift (ΔpHeq) of about 3.5–4.0 between the two species.

Stripping stage

The stripping stage in the solvent extraction process represents a critical and final step for zinc metal recovery, functioning as the reverse of the initial extraction process. This crucial phase occurs after the scrubbing stage has effectively removed significant impurities from the organic phase. The mechanism involves the transfer of zinc metal ions from the organic phase back into an acidic aqueous solution, typically achieved using high-concentration sulfuric acid that produces zinc sulfate solution. The stripping efficiency has significant economic implications, directly affecting operational costs and overall zinc recovery yield. A key challenge in the stripping process involves maintaining organic solvent effectiveness after multiple extraction and stripping cycles, as gradual solvent degradation can negatively impact process efficiency. This stage ultimately determines the final purity of recovered zinc and the reusability of the organic solvent in subsequent extraction cycles, making it a cornerstone of the entire recovery operation. For the stripping operation, a solution containing 20,000 mg/l of zinc and 140,000 mg/l of sulfuric acid was used31,36. The high sulfuric acid concentration provides the acidic conditions necessary for effective zinc stripping from the organic phase. Experiments were conducted with various organic-to-aqueous phase ratios to optimize and better understand the process. These experiments aimed to collect the necessary data for plotting the stripping isotherm and the McCabe-Thiele diagram. The results of these experiments are fully presented in Table 11. This data provides valuable information about how zinc distribution changes between phases under different conditions.

Proposed flowsheet

The flowchart (Fig. 8) illustrates the process for treating and recovering valuable metals, particularly zinc, from lead smelting furnace residue. Initially, the residue, containing 14.75% Zinc, 7.50% Pb, 4.00% As, and 9.00% Cl, undergoes a pretreatment step involving water washing, aiming to remove chloride impurities. This washing step was optimized to achieve 94% chlorine removal using pure water at 85 °C for 120 min, reducing the chlorine content from 9.00% to approximately 0.50% in the pretreated residue. Pure water proved to be as effective as alkaline solutions and more economical and environmentally friendly. After washing, the solid residue composition improves to 17.00% Zinc, 5.30% As, and 0.50% Cl. Subsequently, the pretreated residue is subjected to sulfuric acid leaching using a solution with a concentration of 0.75 M H2SO4 at 75 °C for 60 min with a solid-to-liquid ratio (L/S) of 6. This leaching step facilitates the dissolution of zinc and arsenic into the liquid phase. After the solid-liquid separation, the solid residue primarily contains 0.7% Zinc, 14.56% Pb, 3.3% As, and 0.3% Cl, while the pregnant leach solution contains 13,880 mg/l Zinc and 1500 mg/l As. In the solvent extraction pathway, the solution without arsenic removal was treated utilizing D2EHPA as the extractant in a multi-step procedure (extraction, scrubbing, and stripping). This results in a final solution with a zinc concentration of 51,790 mg/l and an arsenic concentration of 0.33 mg/l, achieving significant arsenic removal.

Process flow diagram for the recovery of zinc from lead smelting furnace residue.

In the proposed integrated process, the arsenic-bearing leach residue from the primary leaching stage is combined with the arsenic-containing raffinate returned from the zinc solvent extraction circuit (using D2EHPA extractant). This combined stream is then subjected to acidic leaching using sulfuric acid, which effectively dissolves arsenic into solution while maintaining a closed-loop acid management system. The resulting pregnant leach solution undergoes solvent extraction using amine-based extractants, Cyanex 923 or similar reagents, to extract and concentrate arsenic from the aqueous phase selectively19,37. The loaded organic phase, now enriched in arsenic, is subsequently stripped to produce a concentrated arsenic solution. This concentrated stream serves as the feedstock for synthesizing various arsenic compounds, including Scorodite (FeAsO4·2 H2O) and alternative arsenic salts, such as calcium arsenates and other stable arsenate minerals28,30,38. Scorodite, in particular, offers dual advantages as it can be safely and permanently disposed of through engineered storage due to its low solubility and high stability, or it can serve as a precursor for conversion to other arsenate compounds depending on specific project requirements and market conditions39.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed an efficient hydrometallurgical process for zinc recovery from lead smelting furnace waste containing 14.75% zinc, 7.50% lead, 4.00% arsenic, and 9.00% chloride. Following chloride pre-treatment, acid leaching with 0.75 M H2SO4 at 75 °C for 60 min achieved over 96% zinc extraction efficiency and 58% arsenic dissolution. Two purification approaches were compared to address high arsenic contamination in the leach solution. Arsenic precipitation using ferrous sulfate and potassium permanganate achieved 99.94% arsenic removal but left a residual arsenic concentration of 6 mg/L, exceeding electrowinning requirements (< 1 mg/L). Solvent extraction with D2EHPA (30% in kerosene) proved superior, employing four extraction stages, two scrubbing steps, and two stripping stages. This process reduced arsenic to 0.33 mg/l and concentrated zinc to 51,790 mg/l in the final electrolyte, meeting electrowinning specifications. This is the first comprehensive study to investigate and compare both precipitation and solvent extraction techniques for treating residues. The developed process provides a technically robust, environmentally compliant solution for recovering high-purity zinc from challenging metallurgical wastes, offering significant potential for industrial applications in resource recovery and environmental remediation. Beyond its high efficiency, the solvent extraction route also provides a clear arsenic-management advantage: arsenic rejected to the raffinate can be directed to a dedicated stabilization or recovery circuit. In contrast, the undissolved arsenic remaining in the leach residue can be safely handled or reprocessed in subsequent treatment steps. Overall, the D2EHPA-based solvent extraction system offers a technically robust and environmentally compliant purification pathway, outperforming precipitation-based treatment and enabling the production of high-purity zinc electrolyte from complex lead smelting furnace residues.
