An integrated AI pipeline for automated cytogenetic analysis of bone marrow karyograms in hematological malignancies: A Pix2Pix enhancement and deep learning detection approach
Authors: Lamia Alsubaie, Sarah Alsobaie, Nawaf Alhammad, Luluh Aldhubayi, Yazeed Al-shaikh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConventional cytogenetic analysis remains central to the diagnosis and risk stratification of hematological malignancies but is constrained by labor-intensive workflows, inter-observer variability, and sensitivity to image quality. Although artificial intelligence (AI) approaches have been proposed for individual analytical tasks, clinically integrated, end-to-end pipelines aligned with reporting standards remain limited.MethodsWe developed and evaluated a clinically oriented, AI-assisted cytogenetic analysis pipeline integrating image enhancement, chromosome detection, numerical and targeted structural abnormality assessment, and standardized reporting. Image quality was enhanced using a Pix2Pix-based generative model, followed by chromosome localization with a YOLOv8 detector and structural classification using a Siamese ResNet-18 architecture. Outputs were translated into ISCN-formatted reports aligned with College of American Pathologists (CAP) requirements. The system was evaluated retrospectively on clinical bone marrow karyogram datasets, with performance assessed using image-level fidelity metrics, analytical performance, and system-level feasibility under expert oversight.ResultsImage enhancement demonstrated high structural fidelity (mean SSIM >0.98). Within a gated, quality-controlled pipeline, chromosome detection achieved robust performance, and the structural classifier showed strong discriminative ability for the targeted abnormality t(9;22). At the case level, primary concordance with expert interpretation was 85.8%, increasing to 92.35% following adjudication of clinically acceptable reporting differences. Automated generation of CAP-aligned ISCN reports was achieved under predefined quality-control constraints with mandatory expert validation.ConclusionsThis study presents a clinically grounded, proof-of-concept AI-assisted cytogenetic decision-support pipeline integrating enhancement, gated analysis, abnormality assessment, and structured reporting. While demonstrating encouraging system-level performance, the framework is intended as a decision-support tool and remains at the stage of controlled feasibility evaluation. Further prospective validation, expanded structural coverage, and deployment-level governance will be required before clinical implementation.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Chromosomal abnormalities, Cytogenetics, Image enhancement, Generative adversarial networks, Hematological malignancies, Karyotype analysis, Deep learning

Introduction

Cytogenetic analysis remains a cornerstone in the diagnosis, prognostic stratification, and therapeutic decision-making of hematological malignancies. Chromosomal abnormalities such as numerical gains or losses and recurrent structural rearrangements play a critical role in disease classification and risk assessment across myeloid and lymphoid neoplasms.1, 2, 3 Conventional karyotype analysis provides genome-wide structural information but is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and dependent on expert interpretation, resulting in substantial inter-observer variability and prolonged turnaround times (TATs) in routine clinical practice.4, 5

Standard cytogenetic workflows require high-quality metaphase preparation, manual chromosome identification, and accurate interpretation using the International System for Human Cytogenomic Nomenclature (ISCN), which provides a standardized framework for describing chromosomal findings, all of which demand extensive expertise.6 Despite ongoing advances in molecular techniques, karyotyping remains indispensable for detecting balanced rearrangements, complex karyotypes, and clonal evolution, particularly in hematological malignancies where cytogenetic findings directly influence diagnostic classification and treatment selection.7, 8

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and deep learning have demonstrated significant potential in automating image-based medical tasks, including object detection, segmentation, and pattern recognition.9, 10, 11 In cytogenetics, AI-driven approaches have been explored for chromosome segmentation, karyotype reconstruction, and abnormality detection, with promising performance reported in controlled settings.12, 13, 14, 15, 16 However, many existing approaches focus on isolated tasks, rely on curated datasets, or lack integration into clinically realistic workflows, limiting their translational applicability.17, 18

Image quality is a critical determinant of performance in automated cytogenetic analysis. Variability in staining, image resolution, background noise, and chromosome overlap can substantially affect downstream detection and classification performance.16, 19 Generative models, particularly conditional generative adversarial networks (GANs) such as Pix2Pix, have been successfully applied to image enhancement tasks in medical imaging, offering the potential to improve contrast and structural clarity while preserving underlying morphology.13, 20 Nevertheless, the clinical value of image enhancement depends on its integration with downstream analytical models rather than its use as a standalone output.

In parallel, object detection frameworks such as You Only Look Once (YOLO) have demonstrated robust performance in real-time detection tasks and have been adapted for biomedical image analysis, including chromosome localization and enumeration.15 When combined with deep feature-based classifiers, these approaches enable multi-level analysis pipelines capable of addressing both numerical and structural abnormalities. Despite these advances, relatively few studies have evaluated fully end-to-end AI systems that integrate image enhancement, chromosome detection, abnormality classification, and standardized reporting within a single clinically oriented framework.17

Furthermore, the deployment of AI in cytogenetics raises important considerations regarding interpretability, validation, and clinical responsibility.21, 22 Regulatory and professional bodies, including the College of American Pathologists (CAP), emphasize that AI tools should function as decision-support systems rather than autonomous diagnostic agents, with final interpretation retained by qualified personnel.23 Accordingly, robust evaluation strategies, transparent error analysis, and clear delineation of system boundaries are essential for safe clinical adoption.24

This study presents a clinically grounded, modular, proof-of-concept cytogenetic decision-support pipeline designed to address these challenges. The proposed system integrates Pix2Pix-based image enhancement with chromosome detection, numerical abnormality assessment, and a targeted structural analysis module focused on t(9;22) as a representative use case, as well as an automated generation of CAP-aligned cytogenetic reports using ISCN nomenclature. Performance is evaluated using frozen models, defined as models whose architectures, parameters, and hyperparameters were fixed after training and not updated during evaluation, on an independent clinical cohort to prevent data leakage and post hoc optimization. Detailed analyses of concordance, discordant cases, and workflow impact were subsequently performed. By emphasizing clinical feasibility, transparency, and expert oversight, this work aims to demonstrate the practical role of AI in supporting—rather than replacing—cytogenetic expertise.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This retrospective study was approved by the local Institutional Review Board and conducted using archived cytogenetic data obtained from a CAP-accredited clinical cytogenetics lab. The dataset comprised conventional G-banded karyogram images and corresponding expert cytogenetic interpretations generated as part of routine clinical care. All data were fully de-identified before analysis. The overall study rationale, conceptual framework, and integrated clinical-computational workflow are summarized in Fig. 1, which outlines clinical inputs, AI-assisted analysis, and structured cytogenetic reporting with expert oversight.

Data sources and patient cohort

Data were sourced from a single cytogenetics lab performing routine bone marrow karyotyping for suspected or established hematological malignancies. The study cohort comprised 183 cases encompassing 3190 karyogram images, reflecting routine diagnostic prevalence. Normal karyotypes were included to support calibration of false-positive rates. The cohort structure and analytical workflow are summarized in Fig. 2, which illustrates patient-level dataset partitioning, preprocessing steps, model development using an internal cohort (45 cases), and independent system-level evaluation on a separate clinical cohort (183 cases) using frozen models without retraining to prevent data leakage.

End-to-end model development and evaluation strategy. (A) Data preprocessing, annotation, and dataset partitioning. (B) Model development using an internal cohort (45 cases) incorporating image enhancement, chromosome detection, numerical and structural analysis, and ISCN-aligned reporting. (C) System-level evaluation on an independent cohort (183 cases) using frozen models without retraining to prevent data leakage.

All specimens underwent conventional metaphase preparation, including cell culture, mitotic arrest, hypotonic swelling, fixation, and standard trypsin–Giemsa (GTG) G-banding. Band-level resolution was not explicitly recorded for individual images, reflecting the retrospective nature of the dataset and routine clinical documentation practices. All available karyograms deemed analyzable in routine reporting were included, without exclusion based on predefined band-resolution thresholds.

Images were obtained from MetaSystems automated microscopy platforms equipped with 12–16-bit monochrome sensors and exported as 24-bit RGB PNG files via the MetaSystems Ikaros karyotyping platform. All images were acquired under standardized lab conditions using a single imaging platform, contributing to reduced variability in image characteristics.

Karyograms with severe technical limitations—such as extensive chromosome overlap, fragmentation, or poor banding clarity—were not excluded at acquisition but were instead identified through the analyzability assessment module and routed for mandatory expert evaluation, which may affect downstream detection and structural classification performance.

Data management and quality control

All potential patient identifiers were removed at the source, and file names were systematically randomized before analysis. Data access was governed by a role-based access control (RBAC) framework adhering to the principle of least privilege. Image data were encrypted at rest using AES-256 standards, and secure transmission was ensured via Transport Layer Security version 1.2 or higher.

To prevent data leakage, the dataset was partitioned strictly at the patient level, such that all karyogram images from a given patient were assigned exclusively to a single data split (training, validation, or test). Image-level processing and batching were applied only within the training set for model development and optimization and did not involve cross-patient data splitting. Duplicate images were identified and removed. A standardized data dictionary was maintained using Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) controlled vocabularies to ensure consistent and structured representation of clinical and phenotypic features across cases. Although HPO is more commonly applied in germline and rare disease settings, it was used here to provide a unified and reproducible framework for annotating heterogeneous clinical data. Cytogenetic findings and diagnostic interpretations remained aligned with ISCN nomenclature and established disease-specific clinical frameworks.

In addition to data security and quality control, data governance considerations were incorporated to support transparency and reproducibility. Data lineage was maintained through structured dataset organization and consistent preprocessing procedures, enabling traceability of images and associated annotations throughout the analytical pipeline. Intermediate and final outputs were retained to support auditability and facilitate expert validation when required.

The system was designed within a human-in-the-loop framework, whereby automated outputs are subject to mandatory expert verification before final interpretation. Drift-adjacent quality control checks and corresponding mitigation strategies applied during retrospective evaluation are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Whereas a fully operational clinical governance infrastructure—such as continuous monitoring, model updating, and deployment-level oversight—was beyond the scope of this study, these elements represent important considerations for future clinical implementation.

To further contextualize the governance framework, the present study reflects a research-grade data management approach with core safeguards including de-identification, RBAC under a least-privilege model, encryption at rest and in transit, and strict patient-level data partitioning to prevent leakage. All outputs were subject to mandatory expert review before interpretation.

For clinical deployment, additional governance layers would be required to support end-to-end traceability and accountability. These include formal identity and access management with audit logging, dataset and annotation versioning with lineage tracking, model version control and registry systems, and explicit documentation of model training and validation history. Furthermore, clinically deployed systems would require mechanisms for output provenance tracking, structured override logging for expert corrections, and continuous performance monitoring to detect model drift over time. Cross-site data governance, regulatory compliance workflows, and incident response frameworks would also be necessary to ensure safe integration into clinical lab environments. Accordingly, the present work establishes a secure and controlled research framework while outlining key governance considerations for future translation into clinically deployable cytogenetic AI systems.

AI pipeline architecture

The AI-assisted cytogenetic system was implemented as a modular, proof-of-concept, end-to-end analytical pipeline comprising two primary phases: image enhancement (Phase 1) and karyogram analysis (Phase 2) (Fig. 3). The pipeline was designed to operate on conventional G-banded karyogram images and to support downstream cytogenetic interpretation through structured, auditable outputs rather than autonomous diagnosis. The pipeline is intended to support structured cytogenetic analysis and reporting within a decision-support framework rather than to function as an autonomous diagnostic system.

Phase 1: Image enhancement

Image enhancement was performed using a Pix2Pix conditional generative adversarial network (cGAN)13 designed to stabilize contrast and improve G-band delineation while preserving chromosomal morphology (Fig. 3A). The architecture employed a U-Net generator25 with a symmetric encoder–decoder structure and skip connections, paired with a PatchGAN discriminator. The model was trained for 3000 epochs using paired low- and high-quality images generated through controlled degradations, including blur, noise, compression artifacts, and illumination drift applied to high-quality source images to emulate routine clinical variability (Supplementary Fig. S1). Training employed a composite loss function incorporating adversarial loss, L1 loss for pixel-level fidelity, and structural similarity preservation. Enhancement performance was quantitatively assessed using peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and the structural similarity index measure (SSIM), as summarized at the image level in Fig. 5. Complementary qualitative examples illustrating enhancement effects at the karyogram level are provided in Fig. 6.

Phase 2: Karyogram analysis

Phase 2 comprised four interconnected modules forming a multi-step analysis pipeline (Fig. 3B), addressing numerical and targeted structural abnormality assessment, case-level diagnosis, and report generation.

Module 1: Chromosome detection, classification, and enumeration

Module 1 served as the initial gatekeeping and detection stage of the pipeline, performing chromosome localization, gross karyogram quality assessment, and numerical enumeration (Fig. 3B). A fine-tuned YOLO-based object detector with an anchor-free head and integrated feature pyramid network and path aggregation network was used to localize chromosome instances within each karyogram.26

In parallel, the detection outputs were used to perform a gross analyzability assessment. Karyograms exhibiting severe technical limitations—such as extensive chromosome overlap, fragmentation, truncation, or insufficient detectable metaphases—were automatically flagged as grossly unanalyzable and routed for specialist evaluation rather than being passed downstream. This gating step prevents propagation of low-quality inputs into subsequent analytical stages, ensuring that such cases are redirected for expert evaluation.

For numerical analysis, two independently trained YOLOv8 models were used in combination to improve robustness. The first model focused on whole-karyogram chromosome detection, whereas a second model (Version 2) was trained for 24-class chromosome classification (chromosomes 1–22, X, and Y) using 266 annotated karyogram images (∼6384 individual chromosomes). Outputs from both models were reconciled using a consensus-based enumeration strategy, enabling reliable estimation of chromosome counts and sex karyotype inference while reducing single-model bias.

The final output of Module 1 consisted of: (i) localized chromosome bounding boxes, (ii) a binary analyzability flag (analyzable vs. grossly unanalyzable), and (iii) detection-based chromosome counts forwarded to downstream numerical and structural analysis modules. Representative intermediate outputs from area-based chromosome detection, analyzability screening, and numerical abnormality identification are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2.

The detection models were developed using transfer learning and task-specific data augmentation strategies to improve generalization from a relatively limited dataset. The system operates on structured karyogram images rather than raw metaphase spreads, reducing variability in chromosome layout and background complexity. In addition, all data were derived from a single lab using standardized imaging protocols, resulting in relatively consistent acquisition conditions. These factors contribute to model stability within the current dataset, although broader validation across diverse lab settings remains necessary.

Module 2: Structural abnormality classification

A Siamese ResNet-18 architecture11 augmented with squeeze-and-excitation (SE) blocks27 was trained to identify the t(9;22) translocation, the hallmark cytogenetic abnormality in chronic myeloid leukemia. The model was trained on 532 karyogram images (266 normal, 266 abnormal) using a stratified 70/15/15 train/validation/test split. For each case, corresponding chromosome 9 and chromosome 22 pairs were isolated, spatially normalized, and resized to 256 × 256 pixels before being processed as paired inputs by the Siamese network to enable direct structural comparison. This structural classification component is limited to detection of t(9;22) as a targeted proof-of-concept and does not represent comprehensive structural abnormality detection.

Module 3: Case-level diagnosis

A rule-guided multi-modal fusion layer integrated outputs from chromosome detection, numerical enumeration, and structural classification modules to enable generation of case-level cytogenetic interpretations (Fig. 4). The aggregated outputs were passed to a deterministic expert system implementing ISCN rules,28 ensuring standardized, auditable construction of diagnostic karyotype strings.

Dual decision logic for numerical and structural abnormality detection. (A) Numerical analysis pathway: chromosome detection and enumeration for gain/loss assessment and sex karyotype inference. (B) Structural analysis pathway: selection and canonical preprocessing of chromosomes 9 and 22 followed by Siamese ResNet-18–based classification of t(9;22). Outputs are fused for case-level diagnosis.

Module 4: ISCN-guided report generation

The final module aggregated outputs from all preceding analytical stages and applied deterministic ISCN rules to assist in the generation of clinically aligned, human-readable cytogenetic reports in accordance with CAP reporting standards.4

To ensure transparency in report construction, report elements were explicitly partitioned into three categories: (i) rule-based components, generated using deterministic logic (e.g., ISCN structure, chromosome enumeration, and internal consistency checks); (ii) model-derived outputs, including chromosome detection, classification, and inferred structural abnormalities (e.g., t(9;22)), which were incorporated as decision-support inputs; and (iii) mandatory expert-completed fields, including clinical interpretation, case contextualization, and final report validation and sign-out.

Report generation was subject to predefined quality-control criteria, including a requirement for a minimum of five analyzable karyograms per case. Cases that did not meet this threshold were withheld from automated report generation and flagged for personnel review. The system does not permit fully automated report release without human validation, ensuring that final interpretation and report approval remained under certified cytogenetic oversight. Automated report-level validation rules and safety checks applied before ISCN report generation are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

Model selection rationale

Model architecture was selected based on task suitability, clinical robustness, and prior validation in biomedical imaging contexts. Pix2Pix was chosen for image enhancement due to its ability to perform paired image-to-image translation while preserving structural fidelity, which is critical for maintaining G-band morphology. YOLO-based detectors were selected for chromosome localization and classification because of their strong performance in dense object detection tasks and suitability for high-resolution cytogenetic images. A Siamese ResNet architecture was employed for structural abnormality detection to enable direct pairwise comparison of homologous chromosomes, supporting sensitive detection of subtle structural differences relevant to recurrent translocations.

Training procedures and hyperparameters

All models were trained using NVIDIA GPUs with CUDA acceleration. Hyperparameters were optimized using structured grid searches with factorial sweeps. Training stability was improved through gradient clipping to mitigate exploding gradients and the use of exponential moving average (EMA) updates to smooth parameter trajectories.

Early stopping was applied with a patience of 20–30 epochs, using task-specific validation metrics to guide convergence: structural similarity index measure (SSIM) for image enhancement, mean average precision (mAP) for chromosome detection, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) for structural classification. Optimization was performed using Adam or AdamW optimizers with cosine annealing learning-rate schedules for enhancement and classification tasks, and a one-cycle learning-rate policy for detection. Mixed-precision (FP16) training was employed to improve computational efficiency and memory utilization. Image quality assessment

Baseline image quality was assessed using three established no-reference image quality assessment (NR-IQA) metrics: natural image quality evaluator (NIQE), perception-based image quality evaluator (PIQE), and blind/referenceless image spatial quality evaluator (BRISQUE).19, 29 Enhancement model performance was evaluated using full-reference metrics including PSNR and SSIM,30 comparing enhanced outputs to high-quality ground truth images.

Evaluation strategy and statistical analysis

All analysis methods, performance thresholds, and aggregation rules were predefined before evaluation on the external test cohort. A one-touch evaluation policy was enforced; whereby model architectures and hyperparameters were frozen following training to prevent post hoc optimization. Dataset partitioning was performed at the patient level to eliminate data leakage. External validation was conducted using an independent clinical dataset from a partner laboratory to assess generalizability.

Statistical analysis employed case-level cluster bootstrap resampling to estimate 95% confidence intervals while accounting for within-patient correlations. Model calibration was evaluated using expected calibration error (ECE) and Brier scores. Performance was additionally examined across image quality strata defined by NIQE-based thresholds.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was concordance between AI-assisted interpretations and reference cytogenetic assessments at the case level. Secondary outcomes included characterization of discordant cases, identification of system boundary conditions, and qualitative assessment of image enhancement fidelity and interpretability. Performance was evaluated within a clinical decision support under expert oversight, rather than standalone algorithmic accuracy.

Results

Image enhancement model performance

Performance of the Pix2Pix image enhancement model was evaluated using full-reference metrics at both the image and case levels. Across the full dataset (N = 3190 karyogram images), enhanced outputs demonstrated high structural fidelity, with a mean SSIM exceeding 0.98 and a PSNR of 35.07 ± 1.45 dB. Pixel-level error remained minimal, with a mean absolute error (MAE) of 1.78 ± 0.17 and a mean squared error (MSE) of 23.48 ± 7.90, indicating preservation of chromosomal morphology following enhancement (Fig. 5). These results reflect technical consistency and reconstruction fidelity of the enhancement process rather than direct clinical validation. Accordingly, interpretation of these metrics is limited to assessment of image-level fidelity. The potential clinical relevance of enhancement is evaluated indirectly through its effect on downstream analytical performance, including chromosome detection, numerical assessment, and structural classification, as reported in subsequent sections. Whereas beyond the scope of the current study, formal ablation analyses and reader-based evaluations are currently underway in follow-up work to quantify the incremental clinical contribution of the enhancement module.

Case-level enhancement consistency and visual fidelity

To assess robustness under clinically realistic conditions, enhancement performance was further evaluated at the case level by aggregating results across all karyograms within each clinical case (N = 183). Case-level analysis demonstrated stable enhancement behavior, with an average PSNR of 35.11 dB and an average SSIM of 0.9823, confirming that enhancement quality was maintained when applied across multi-image patient cases. Fig. 6A illustrates the consistency of enhancement quality across the clinical cohort, with no evidence of case-level degradation.

Qualitative visual assessment of representative cases demonstrated improved contrast and G-band delineation while preserving chromosomal structure and relative band positioning. No visually apparent enhancement-induced artifacts—such as band drift, halo effects, or structural distortion—were observed on qualitative inspection. Corresponding absolute difference maps showed minimal pixel-wise deviation between enhanced and reference images, supporting the quantitative findings (Fig. 6B).

System performance on chromosome detection and analysis

Following enhancement, the integrated pipeline localized chromosomes, performed gross analyzability screening, and supported numerical and targeted structural assessment (t(9;22)). Automated gross karyogram quality classification achieved an AUC of 0.82, indicating moderate performance and preliminary feasibility for distinguishing analyzable from unanalyzable images (Fig. 7A). At the operational threshold (recall = 0.75, false positive rate (FPR) = 0.15), the system conservatively flagged technically limited karyograms—including those with overlapping metaphases, fragmentation, or insufficient banding— and directed them to specialist assessment instead of automated processing. This gating behavior prevents propagation of low-quality inputs and established a controlled boundary for automated analysis before case-level interpretation. To contextualize the role of image enhancement within the overall pipeline, downstream analytical performance was evaluated using enhanced karyogram images to assess whether key features required for chromosome detection, enumeration, and structural classification were preserved. Despite this gating strategy, residual errors in upstream chromosome detection and localization may propagate to downstream numerical and structural analysis, particularly in cases with suboptimal banding quality or partial segmentation ambiguity. The selected operating point (recall = 0.75; FPR = 0.15) was defined empirically and is not linked to a formal decision-analytic framework.

Clinical reporting workflow and decision-support integration

The final module integrates outputs from all preceding analytical stages within a decision-support platform to assist in generating clinically aligned cytogenetic reports. Report fields were explicitly labeled according to their source as rule-based, model-derived, or expert-completed to ensure transparency and appropriate clinical interpretation. Rule-based deterministic aggregation logic is applied to construct ISCN-formatted karyotype nomenclature and standardized report elements in accordance with CAP reporting standards.

Model-derived outputs, including detection and classification results, are incorporated as decision-support inputs and require expert verification before final reporting. Automated quality-control checks ensure that reports are generated only when minimum analyzability criteria are met; cases failing these criteria are withheld from automated reporting and referred for expert assessment before final reporting.

Additional safeguards include automated flagging of incomplete ISCN fields and required verification when discordant findings, including sex chromosome assignment discrepancies, are detected. As illustrated in Fig. 9, final interpretation, validation, and report authorization remain the responsibility of certified cytogenetic personnel, and fully automated report release is not permitted. Reports are generated in a CAP-aligned PDF format suitable for laboratory information system (LIS) integration. The reporting interface integrates AI-derived outputs with rule-based ISCN formatting, with final interpretation and approval performed by certified personnel.

Clinical concordance between AI-assisted interpretation and expert review

Across the evaluation cohort (N = 183 cases), AI-assisted case-level interpretations were compared against final expert cytogenetic diagnoses to assess clinical concordance (Fig. 10A). Perfect agreement (raw concordance) was observed in 157 cases (85.8%), whereas 26 cases (14.2%) demonstrated discordance requiring further review. Primary concordance (raw agreement with expert diagnosis) was treated as the main endpoint, whereas adjudicated accuracy following review of discordant cases was considered a secondary analysis (Fig. 10; see Fig. 11 for detailed discordance characterization).

Analysis of discordant cases revealed that most did not represent true analytical failures, but rather differences in biological interpretation, clonal prioritization, and clinical reporting emphasis. Discrepancies were primarily attributable to variation in clonal ranking and reporting priority, as well as image quality limitations affecting interpretability (Fig. 11). True algorithmic errors accounted for a minority of cases (3.83%, 7/183), encompassing both numerical and structural misclassifications.

To further characterize discordance, a structured error analysis was performed using predefined categorization criteria (Fig. 11). Discordant cases were classified into four primary groups: true errors, quality-limited cases, clonal ranking differences, and ground truth discrepancies, with an additional unclassified category for cases not assignable to predefined groups. True errors were observed in 7 cases (3.83%), including numerical (n = 3) and structural (n = 4) misclassifications. Quality-limited cases (n = 2, 1.09%) were associated with severe image quality issues and were appropriately flagged as non-analyzable. The largest proportion of discordance was attributable to clonal ranking differences (n = 12, 6.56%), reflecting variation in prioritization or reporting order rather than failure to detect clinically relevant abnormalities. Ground truth discrepancies (n = 3, 1.64%) represented differences between AI output and initial manual reporting, whereas two cases (7.7% of discordant cases) were classified as unclassified. This categorization provides a descriptive breakdown of discordant cases without influencing primary performance metrics.

To better contextualize these findings, an adjudicated concordance analysis was performed, accounting for clinically acceptable reporting differences in which the AI correctly identified relevant abnormalities but differed in clonal prioritization or reporting emphasis. Under this framework, concordance increased to 92.35% (169/183) (Fig. 10B). This secondary analysis provides additional interpretive context but does not replace raw concordance as the primary performance endpoint. A summary of case-level concordance outcomes is provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Clinical feasibility and decision-support impact

To contextualize the potential clinical value of AI-assisted cytogenetic decision support, baseline TAT for conventional manual cytogenetic workflows was assessed as part of a retrospective laboratory process review conducted during data collection. This analysis reflects routine clinical practice and serves as a reference for workflow burden rather than a direct performance comparison.

Across analyzable cases (n = 202), the total TAT from sample receipt to finalized cytogenetic report demonstrated substantial variability (Fig. 12). The median TAT was 30.5 days (IQR: 23–44), with a 90th percentile of 72.3 days, indicating prolonged delays in a subset of cases. Notably, these values exceed commonly referenced reporting benchmarks, including the CAP recommendation of 14 days for cytogenetic results. This analysis provides operational context for the current workflow rather than a direct measure of AI-assisted improvement. The observed variability establishes a baseline against which potential workflow optimization strategies, including AI-assisted analysis, may be evaluated in future studies.

No-reference image quality assessment before and after enhancement

NR-IQA metrics were evaluated before and after Pix2Pix-based enhancement using NIQE, PIQE, and BRISQUE. Across all three metrics, post-enhancement scores demonstrated an upward shift, indicating increased deviation from natural-scene statistics (Supplementary Fig. S4). This behavior is expected for GAN-based enhancement models that emphasize edge sharpening and contrast normalization, which NR-IQA algorithms may flag as apparent distortion. Notably, these changes were not associated with reduced cytogenetic interpretability and were interpreted in conjunction with full-reference metrics (PSNR, SSIM) and downstream analytical performance to confirm preservation of chromosomal structure.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study presents a clinically oriented proof-of-concept for an integrated AI-assisted cytogenetic decision-support system designed to operate within routine bone marrow karyotype workflows. The key findings demonstrate that: (i) Pix2Pix-based image enhancement preserves chromosomal morphology and banding structure while improving visual consistency; (ii) downstream chromosome detection and structural classification for (t(9;22)) can be performed reliably within a gated, quality-controlled pipeline; (iii) case-level AI-expert concordance exceeds predefined feasibility thresholds when clinically acceptable reporting differences are considered; and (iv) automated ISCN-aligned report generation can be integrated into CAP-compliant workflows under expert oversight. Collectively, these findings support the feasibility of AI as a supportive tool in cytogenetic practice within supervised clinical workflows. These findings should be interpreted within the scope of a carefully engineered proof-of-concept system demonstrating feasibility of an integrated cytogenetic decision-support pipeline, rather than as evidence of readiness for clinical deployment.

Role of image enhancement in cytogenetic decision support

Image quality remains a critical determinant of performance in automated cytogenetic analysis, as subtle G-band patterns directly inform chromosome identification and structural interpretation.3, 14 In this study, Pix2Pix-based enhancement achieved high structural fidelity (SSIM ≈ 0.98) while maintaining pixel-level consistency (PSNR ≈ 35 dB). These findings demonstrate technical fidelity of the enhancement process; however, direct clinical benefit was not formally evaluated in this study. Consistent with this, downstream analytical performance remained stable across detection, enumeration, and structural classification tasks, supporting the interpretation that enhancement preserves diagnostically relevant features without independently establishing clinical utility. Establishing clinical utility will require dedicated reader studies assessing diagnostic confidence, as well as ablation analyses to quantify the contribution of enhancement to downstream detection and classification performance. These results align with prior work suggest that generative models can improve perceptual clarity without compromising underlying anatomical structure when carefully constrained.13, 20

Enhancement benefits were not uniform across all images. Performance gains were more pronounced in lower-quality inputs, consistent with ceiling effects observed in image quality assessment literature.2 This supports the use of no-reference image quality metrics as triage tools rather than absolute quality indicators, guiding selective enhancement and reducing unnecessary processing of already optimal images. Consistent with these findings, downstream chromosome detection demonstrated stable performance while conservatively flagging grossly unanalyzable karyograms for expert review, reinforcing the system's role as a quality-aware decision-support tool rather than a fully autonomous diagnostic engine. The absence of visually apparent artifacts, including band drift or hallucinated structures, confirmed through difference-map inspection, addresses a key concern surrounding generative models in medical imaging.2

Clinical integration and human-in-the-loop design

A central design principle of the proposed system is explicit human oversight. Rather than maximizing standalone algorithmic accuracy, the pipeline incorporates multiple guardrails, including gross analyzability screening, confidence-based routing, and expert interpretation for flagged cases. This approach aligns with established recommendations that clinical AI systems should function as decision-support tools, preserving clinician authority and accountability.12, 16, 22

The observed AI-expert concordance reflects this design philosophy. Whereas perfect agreement was achieved in the majority of cases, most discordant outputs did not represent true analytical failures but rather differences in clonal prioritization, reporting emphasis, or image quality constraints. After accounting for clinically acceptable reporting variation, adjudicated concordance exceeded 90%, supporting feasibility within a decision-support context. Raw concordance (85.8%) represents the primary endpoint, whereas adjudicated concordance provides secondary interpretive context.

Whereas many discordant cases reflected differences in clonal prioritization or reporting emphasis, a subset involved more complex discrepancies, including substantial numerical and structural deviations under challenging image conditions (see Supplementary Table S3 for representative examples). In particular, several cases demonstrated marked divergence in predicted karyotype structure compared with expert interpretation, reflecting limitations in handling highly complex abnormalities or suboptimal image quality. For example, certain cases demonstrated marked divergence in predicted karyotype structure compared with expert interpretation, reflecting limitations in handling highly complex abnormalities and cases with suboptimal image quality.

Crucially, true algorithmic errors were uncommon and were systematically surfaced rather than concealed, reinforcing the system's safety-oriented design. These findings should be interpreted as evidence of early feasibility rather than robust, clinically validated confidence-based triage. Whereas the results support the feasibility of automated quality gating, performance does not yet support deployment as an independent triage system and requires further validation in clinically representative cohorts. Further work is needed in calibration, refinement, and integration with decision-analytic threshold selection. Whereas the current study incorporates core data protection and access controls, full clinical deployment would require expanded governance infrastructure, including auditability, model lifecycle management, and continuous monitoring within regulated lab environments.

The multi-stage design of the pipeline introduces inherent dependencies between modules, whereby errors in upstream chromosome detection and localization may influence downstream numerical and structural analysis. Although analyzability gating mitigates the impact of severely degraded inputs, residual inaccuracies—particularly in cases with borderline image quality or ambiguous chromosome segmentation—may propagate through subsequent stages. These effects were observed in boundary cases, including missed structural abnormalities associated with suboptimal banding quality.

Future work should incorporate systematic evaluation of cross-module robustness, including sensitivity analyses to perturbations in detection outputs and confidence-stratified performance assessment, to better characterize error propagation and strengthen reliability across the pipeline.

Beyond serving as a safety mechanism, expert cytogenetic interpretation plays a central role in clinical decision-making that extends beyond image-based abnormality detection. Whereas the AI system performs structured detection and aggregation of cytogenetic features, human experts contribute biological, clonal, and clinical synthesis, including interpretation of clonal hierarchy, integration with clinical context, and prioritization of findings in a diagnostically meaningful manner. In this context, observed discrepancies often reflect differences in interpretation and prioritization rather than simple errors, underscoring the complementary roles of AI-assisted analysis and expert judgment.

Reporting automation and workflow implications

Automated generation of ISCN-formatted, CAP-aligned cytogenetic reports represents a meaningful translational contribution. Prior AI studies in cytogenetics have largely focused on isolated tasks—such as segmentation or classification—without addressing downstream reporting requirements.15, 16 By embedding deterministic ISCN logic and quality-control rules within the reporting module, the proposed system bridges a key gap between algorithmic output and lab-ready, clinically interpretable documentation.

The retrospective TAT analysis highlights the operational burden of conventional cytogenetic workflows, with substantial variability and frequent delays beyond recommended reporting windows.4 TAT analysis provides operational context for the current workflow rather than a direct measure of AI-assisted improvement. The observed TAT reflects multiple contributing factors, including culture growth duration, image acquisition processes, and workflow-related constraints such as case volume and reporting workload. This variability highlights existing workflow limitations and establishes a baseline against which potential AI-assisted optimization strategies may be evaluated. Simulation-based modeling of AI-assisted workflow impact represents an important next step for translating these findings into measurable operational benefit.

This study does not directly evaluate AI-assisted TAT reduction; rather, these findings provide baseline operational context. The proposed system is positioned to assist in triage, standardization, and report preparation, which may contribute to improved workflow efficiency in scenarios where reporting and interpretation represent limiting steps. However, any efficiency gains must be balanced against the need for mandatory expert validation and appropriate safeguards to prevent automation-related errors.22

In this context, the present study does not provide a closed-loop evaluation of AI-assisted TAT. A formal assessment would require prospective measurement or simulation-based modeling incorporating task-level time decomposition and the proportion of cases requiring expert escalation. In particular, the need for expert interpretation in discordant or low-quality cases introduces a trade-off between automation efficiency and review burden. Future work should include time-motion analyses or simulation frameworks to quantify the net impact of AI integration on lab throughput.

Comparison with prior work

Compared with previously reported systems such as ChromoEnhancer and other deep learning–based cytogenetic tools, this study emphasizes end-to-end clinical integration, including reporting feasibility and concordance analysis, rather than component-level optimization alone.3, 8, 15, 16 In contrast to prior approaches that emphasize algorithmic performance in isolation, the present work evaluates AI performance within a clinically constrained decision-support framework that explicitly incorporates reporting, validation, and expert interpretation within a structured clinical context. The combination of image enhancement, gated analysis, structured ISCN aggregation, and CAP-aligned reporting distinguishes this work from prior efforts that terminate at image- or chromosome-level outputs. To our knowledge, few published systems have evaluated AI-assisted cytogenetic analysis through the lens of clinical concordance, decision-support boundaries, and reporting feasibility within accredited lab workflows. Importantly, this system is presented as a proof-of-concept decision-support pipeline rather than a fully autonomous diagnostic system.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cohort size is modest by deep learning standards, particularly for structural abnormality detection, and the analysis is restricted to a single recurrent translocation (t(9;22)) as a targeted proof-of-concept. Second, the retrospective study design does not fully capture challenges associated with prospective clinical deployment, including real-time processing constraints, user interaction, and workflow integration. Third, generalizability across laboratories with differing imaging platforms, staining protocols, and case mixes remains to be established through future multi-center validation studies.

Structural errors, particularly missed detection of clinically significant abnormalities such as t(9;22), represent an important limitation. These errors are more likely to occur in cases with suboptimal banding quality, reduced breakpoint visibility, or ambiguous chromosome segmentation, where diagnostic interpretation is inherently challenging. Given the high clinical impact of such abnormalities, these findings underscore the importance of specialist evaluation in a clinically supervised setting. Accordingly, the proposed system is intended to function as a decision-support tool that assists, rather than replaces, expert cytogenetic interpretation, with safeguards designed to ensure that uncertain or high-risk cases are appropriately escalated for review.

In addition, the dataset used for chromosome detection and structural classification is limited in size and derived from a single-center source, which may constrain generalizability. Future work should incorporate multi-center datasets to evaluate performance across diverse imaging conditions and lab workflows. Future work will include confusion matrix-based evaluation of classification performance across expanded abnormality categories.

Future directions

Future work should expand coverage of structural abnormalities beyond recurrent translocations, incorporate larger multi-institutional datasets, and explore federated or other privacy-preserving learning strategies to address data-sharing constraints.21 Prospective clinical studies evaluating user interaction, reporting efficiency, and diagnostic consistency will be essential to support real-world translation. In addition, integration with complementary cytogenetic and genomic modalities—such as fluorescence in situ hybridization, chromosomal microarray analysis, and sequencing—represents a logical next step toward comprehensive, multimodal decision support.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the feasibility of a clinically grounded, proof-of-concept AI-assisted cytogenetic decision-support system, emphasizing integration, safety, and interpretability rather than standalone diagnostic performance.

By integrating image enhancement, gated analysis, structured ISCN reporting, and expert-informed workflows, the proposed framework outlines a practical and responsible pathway for AI adoption in routine cytogenetic practice. Together, these findings establish a foundation for continued development and prospective validation of clinically deployable systems that support diagnostic consistency and workflow efficiency while preserving expert judgment. Whereas the system demonstrates encouraging system-level performance, it remains at the stage of a controlled proof-of-concept evaluation and is not yet ready for clinical deployment. Broader structural coverage and prospective validation will be required before clinical deployment.
