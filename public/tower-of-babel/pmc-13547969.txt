Analysis of Genetic Factors in a Family With Short Stature
Authors: Siqing Zhang, Tian Zuo, Youping Deng, Dongchi Zhao, Lihong Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTo elucidate the genetic underpinnings of short stature in a familial cohort of five individuals.MethodsA family with a history of short stature from Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University was the subject of this study. Peripheral blood samples were collected from family members for whole exome sequencing and Sanger sequencing to identify genetic anomalies.ResultsThe male proband, aged 3 years and 10 months, had significant growth retardation, with a height of 91 cm (< 3rd percentile) and a weight of 13 kg (< 3rd percentile). Whole exome sequencing identified a missense mutation in the COL1A2 gene (c.577G>A, p.Gly193Ser) with maternal inheritance. Sanger sequencing confirmed this mutation in the mother and half‐sister. According to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines, this variant was classified as likely pathogenic. Additionally, a heterozygous mutation in the GH1 gene (c.291+1G>A) was detected in the father and grandfather, contributing to the familial short stature phenotype.ConclusionIn this family, we identified that variants in the COL1A2 and the GH1 can each cause short stature. This reflects both the genetic consistency and complexity of short stature, which is highly dependent on comprehensive genetic testing.Keywords: COL1A2, GH1, short stature, whole exome sequencing

Introduction

Short stature is a condition in which an individual's height is more than two standard deviations (SD) below the average height of their peers of the same race, gender, and age under similar living conditions, or falls below the 3rd percentile (< 3rd). The causes of short stature are numerous and complex, and are closely related to genetics, nutrition, environmental factors, inborn errors of metabolism, and endocrine conditions (Baron et al. 2015; Collett‐Solberg, Jorge, et al. 2019; Growth Hormone Research 2000; Johannsson et al. 2018; Scalco et al. 2026). There are numerous conditions associated with short stature, with the five most common ones in the Chinese population being Growth Hormone Deficiency (GHD), Idiopathic Short Stature (ISS), Constitutional Delay of Growth and Puberty (CDGP), Familial Short Stature (FSS), and Congenital Hypothyroidism (CH) (Fang et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2009). Owing to the characteristics of multiple diseases, complex clinical manifestations, phenotypic overlap among diseases, and high genetic heterogeneity, prior research shows that routine comprehensive screening has limited ability to identify the cause in short children with no other obvious clinical features. Moreover, without chromosomal karyotype analysis or genetic detection, it is easy to miss or misdiagnose short stature caused by hereditary diseases (Adanur Saglam et al. 2025; Collett‐Solberg, Ambler, et al. 2019; Jee et al. 2018). Therefore, karyotyping and genetic testing can assist in identifying the cause of the disease and formulating an individualized treatment plan, enabling accurate assessment of the benefits and risks of treatment (Scalco et al. 2026).

Osteogenesis imperfecta (OI), also referred to as brittle bone disease, is a type of bone dysplasia characterized by strong clinical and genetic heterogeneity (Goddard et al. 2025; Tournis and Dede 2018), with an incidence ranging from 1 in 20,000 to 1 in 10,000. According to the current genetic classification, there are 19 types of osteogenesis imperfecta. Type I osteogenesis imperfecta, caused by mutations in the COL1A1 or COL1A2 genes, accounts for 90% of all OI cases. These mutations are typically inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern and result in either reduced synthesis of normal collagen or structural defects of the collagen triple helix. Clinical manifestations may comprise short stature, blue sclera, hearing impairment, recurrent fractures, laxity of ligaments and joints, and dentin dysplasia, often involving multiple connective tissues (Byrd et al. 2024; Forlino and Marini 2016; Marini et al. 2017).

OI exhibits remarkable clinical and genetic heterogeneity. While autosomal dominant mutations in COL1A1 and COL1A2 account for most cases, recessive forms result from mutations in genes involved in Type I collagen post‐translational modification (e.g., CRTAP, P3H1, PPIB), folding and crosslinking (e.g., SERPINH1, FKBP10, PLOD2), osteoblast differentiation (e.g., SP7, WNT1), and bone mineralization (e.g., IFITM5) (Marom et al. 2020). Moreover, the same gene can produce vastly different phenotypes; for example, specific COL1A1 mutations causing haploinsufficiency typically yield mild Type I OI, whereas substitutions of glycine residues in the triple helix often lead to moderate or severe forms (Forlino and Marini 2016; Marom et al. 2020). This allelic heterogeneity, coupled with variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance in some families, makes precise genotype–phenotype correlation challenging and necessitates individualized clinical evaluation and genetic counseling (Marom et al. 2020; Van Dijk and Sillence 2014).

Congenital growth hormone deficiency (GHD), which is a cause of short stature, occurs in 1 in 4000 to 1 in 10,000 individuals. It can be attributed to genetic factors or structural changes in the hypothalamus and pituitary gland (Alatzoglou et al. 2014; Arzilli et al. 2025; Oswiecimska et al. 2016). Childhood GHD can manifest as an isolated deficiency, such as isolated GHD (IGHD), or in combination with one or more other pituitary hormone deficiencies, known as combined pituitary hormone deficiency. Defects in the growth hormone 1 (GH1) or growth hormone releasing hormone receptor (GHRHR) gene, which are involved in the regulation of growth hormone (GH) secretion, frequently lead to IGHD (Alatzoglou et al. 2014; Haris et al. 2024).

In this study, we discovered a complex family with short stature spanning three generations and involving five family members. Through genetic testing, we identified two pathogenic gene mutations within the family: COL1A2 and GH1, both of which are inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, providing a clear diagnosis and theoretical basis for subsequent treatment.

Results

Case Presentation

Medical History

The proband was a boy aged 3 years and 10 months who had been seeking medical treatment for more than 3 years because of growth retardation. He was the second child, delivered by cesarean section at 37 weeks of gestation and weighed 1.9 kg. The body length at birth was not known. The child was formula‐fed after birth. Developmental milestones were normal. He could say “mama” at 11 months. At that time, he had age‐appropriate language development and cognitive abilities, normal mental status, and normal gross and fine motor skills. There was no history of drug or food allergies, but he had a history of tibia and fibula fractures at 1 year old. The patient's father, mother, sister, grandfather, grandmother, and half‐sister (same mother) were all of short stature (Table 1). Furthermore, his mother had a history of fracture in 2018 (due to a fall while cycling) and his sister had a history of shoulder dislocation.

Physical Examination

The patient's height was 91 cm (< 3rd percentile), weight was 13.0 kg (< 3rd percentile), and body mass index was 15.69 kg/m2 (50th–75th percentile). The sclera and teeth were normal (Figure 1A), but the patient had short fingers (Figure 1B). The skin was normal. No abnormal findings were observed in the heart and lungs. The liver and spleen were not palpable below the ribs. The liver was 1 cm below the costal margin, and the spleen was not palpable. The external genitalia were normal.

Laboratory and Imaging Studies

The growth hormone stimulation test indicated a peak growth hormone level of 16.9 μg/L at 60 min. Insulin‐like growth factor‐1 (IGF‐1) was 70.1 ng/mL (−0.7SD) and insulin‐like growth factor binding protein‐3 (IGFBP‐3) was 3.38 ug/mL. Liver and kidney function, electrolyte levels, glucose metabolism, thyroid function, adrenocorticotropic hormone, and cortisol were all within normal limits. Peripheral blood karyotyping analysis did not show any significant abnormalities. Echocardiography indicated multiple thin left‐to‐right shunts at the atrial level, but there was no abnormality in electrocardiogram (ECG). Bone age assessment indicated that the bone age of Tanner‐Whitehouse 3 Radius, Ulna, and Short bones (TW3‐RUS) was 3 years and 6 months, and TW3‐Carpal was 2 years and 8 months (Figure 2A). Pituitary Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) demonstrated a sagittal pituitary height of 4 mm, within the normal reference range (3.9 ± 0.3 mm) for age‐matched young children (Figure 2B). Full‐length lateral spine X‐ray examination did not show any significant abnormalities.

Genetic Testing

Peripheral blood karyotype analysis indicated that the patient had a 46, XY karyotype. Whole exome sequencing of three family members, including the proband, confirmed the presence of the COL1A2 variant (NM_000089.4): c.577G>A (p.Gly193Ser). The mutation originated from his mother. Sanger sequencing analysis revealed that his mother and sister carried the mutation. According to the ACMG guidelines, this variant is classified as likely pathogenic (PM2_Supporting, PP3_Strong, PS4_Supporting, PP1_Supporting), with the following evidence: This variant was not found in the normal control population in the NHLBI Exome Sequencing Project (ESP) database, the 1000 Genomes Project database, and the Exome Aggregation Consortium (ExAC) database (PM2_Supporting). Various statistical methods, including conservative predictions, evolutionary predictions, and splicing site supporting evidence effects, predicted harmful effects on genes or gene products (PP3_Strong). The variant was observed in several individuals with osteogenesis imperfecta (PS4_Supporting) (Gentile et al. 2012; Marini et al. 2007; Venturi et al. 2006). The mutation was carried by both the mother and sister, which was consistent with family cosegregation (PP1_Supporting). We identified the genetic cause of short stature in the patient, his mother, and his sister (Figure 3).

Sanger sequencing results of family members. COL1A2:c.577G>A variant was detected in the samples of the proband, mother, and sister. GH1:c.291+1G>A variant was detected in the samples of the father and grandfather.

At the same time, we unexpectedly found GH1 (NM_000515.5): c.291+1G>A in the whole exome sequencing data of the patient's father. Sanger sequencing was also performed on his family and confirmed the same results in a peripheral blood sample from the patient's grandfather (Figure 3). This variant site is also classified according to ACMG guidelines as a pathogenic variant (PS3, PS4_Supporting, PM2_Supporting, PM6, PP1_Strong). This variant disrupts the splice donor site of intron 3 (IVS3), resulting in exon 3 skipping and deletion of amino acids 32–71 in the mutant GH mRNA (PS3) (Binder et al. 2007; Cogan et al. 1995; Hamid et al. 2009).

Discussion

In recent years, genetic studies on familial short stature (FSS) have increasingly unveiled its complex molecular basis. Accumulating evidence suggests that a subset of FSS cases actually have a monogenic etiology. In a systematic analysis, Plachy et al. (2023) pointed out that a considerable proportion of children clinically diagnosed with FSS harbor definitive monogenic pathogenic variants, indicating that FSS is not merely a polygenic condition but should be considered a broad‐spectrum concept encompassing monogenic causes. Meanwhile, Lin et al. (2017), by evaluating 34 height‐related SNPs in a Han Chinese population with FSS, identified 13 GWAS‐recognized loci that were independently associated with FSS risk and exhibited cumulative effects, involving genes such as ZBTB38 and LCORL, thereby confirming the important role of polygenic minor additive contributions in FSS. Adding further complexity, Genevieve et al. reported a family carrying pathogenic variants in three genes—SHOX, PDE4D, and ACAN—in which individuals carrying two variants simultaneously presented with a more severe short stature phenotype, suggesting possible additive or modifying effects among different genetic variants (Comel et al. 2025). Collectively, these studies outline the complete genetic spectrum of FSS, ranging from polygenic additive effects to monogenic dominant inheritance and even oligogenic additive effects. The finding in our family that COL1A2 and GH1 variants each lead to isolated short stature serves as a typical example of two independent monogenic drivers within this spectrum, further enriching the genetic heterogeneity map of FSS.

In the proband, a COL1A2 variant (c.577G>A) was detected, resulting in the substitution of glycine with serine at position 193 in the protein. This disrupts the glycine residue within the Gly‐Xaa‐Yaa repeat sequence of the triple helical domain, which is known to be crucial for the structure and stability of collagen (Bella et al. 1994; Long et al. 1993; Shoulders and Raines 2009). This variant has been observed in multiple patients with osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) (Gentile et al. 2012; Lindahl et al. 2015; Maioli et al. 2019; Marini et al. 2007; Venturi et al. 2006). Intriguingly, short stature was noted in one of the patients (Venturi et al. 2006), while no similar phenotypes were observed in the other cases. According to the clinical classification of OI proposed by Sillence et al. in Sillence et al. 1979, the clinical phenotypes of these patients range from Type I to Type IV (Gentile et al. 2012; Lindahl et al. 2015; Marini et al. 2007; Venturi et al. 2006).

Several other variants at the same amino acid position have also been documented (c.577G>C, c.577G>T). It has been demonstrated that these variants similarly disrupt normal protein function (Kanno et al. 2018; Maioli et al. 2019; Mrosk et al. 2018). One patient reported by Kanno harbored the c.577G>C variant and manifested reduced bone density, blue sclera, and dentinogenesis imperfecta (Kanno et al. 2018). In a case described by Mrosk, a patient with the c.577G>T variant exhibited the typical symptoms of OI Type I (Mrosk et al. 2018).

In our report, the proband, his mother, and half‐sister all exhibited short stature without typical OI features. None of them had blue sclera, dentinogenesis imperfecta, or recurrent bone fractures. Only the proband had a history of tibia and fibula fractures at the age of 1. To date, there are no obvious abnormalities in bone development. The phenotypes of all these family members are consistent with Type IV OI.

Another splicing variant in a different gene, GH1 (c.291+1G>A), was detected in the father and grandfather who also had short stature. This variant was initially reported by Cogan. It leads to isolated growth hormone deficiency (IGHD) by disrupting the GH1 IVS3 donor splice site, resulting in the skipping of exon 3 and the loss of amino acids 32 to 71 of the mature GH peptide (Cogan et al. 1995).

Our study has several limitations. First, the COL1A2 variant exhibited an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern in the affected probands. However, due to the dispersed residence of family members, we were unable to perform systematic follow‐up on all carriers, precluding assessment of the penetrance of the “isolated short stature” phenotype among variant carriers. Second, this family consists of two independent genetic lineages, and currently no family member carries both the COL1A2 and GH1 variants, preventing evaluation of potential additive phenotypic effects when both variants coexist. Future studies should further elucidate the pathogenic mechanisms of these variants through in vitro functional experiments, and explore oligogenic inheritance patterns of short stature in larger family‐based cohorts.

Although five family members display short stature, the implicated genes are distinct. Through trio‐based whole exome sequencing, we identified pathogenic variants in different genes, COL1A2 and GH1, in affected family members, thereby providing a clear genetic diagnosis for this family. This discovery informs clinical treatment options and may have positive implications for genetic counseling and prenatal genetic diagnosis for this family in the future.

Methods

Whole Exome Sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using “QIAamp Blood genomic DNA extraction kit (Cat No./ID: 51104)”, and DNA concentration and purity were detected by NanoDrop2000. The Covaris M220 DNA ultrasonic crusher was used to fragment DNA to a length range of 150–300 bp. The fragment DNA was amplified after terminal repair and A reaction. The amplified libraries were targeted using the xGen Exome Research Panel v1.0 kit (IDT). PE150 sequencing was performed using Illumina Novaseq 6000. Data is analyzed through internal processes and pipelines.

Sanger Testing

According to the location information of the site to be verified, the sequence of the corresponding region is downloaded from UCSC, and the reference genome version that is consistent with the required physical location of the gene (GRCh37) is selected. Primer Premier 5 or Primer 3 Plus software was used to design specific primers for the amplification area, and after design, synthetic primers were ordered in Shenggong Bioengineering (Shanghai) Co. LTD. The TaKaRa LA PCR Kit Ver.2.1 was used for PCR amplification. The PCR products qualified by electrophoresis were sent to Shenggong Bioengineering (Shanghai) Co. Ltd. for sanger sequencing. After the sequencing data was returned, the sequencing data was analyzed using the BioEdit software.
