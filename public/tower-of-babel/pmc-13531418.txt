A biomimetic glycosylated peptide sensor with enhanced assaying accuracy in complex biofluids
Authors: Yanxin Li, Yinan Zhan, Jie Yang, Yudong Wang, Rui Han, Gao-Chao Fan, Jason J. Davis, Xiliang Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The application of electrochemical biosensing platforms in complex biological matrices is hindered by the combined effects of biofouling and enzymatic degradation of receptive or supporting interfacial elements. Here, bioinspired by the vascular endothelial glycocalyx, we introduce a β-d-glucose (β-d-Glc) moiety at the C-terminus of sensor-integrated peptide sequences to markedly enhance peptide–water interactions, reduce nonspecific adsorption, and protect the peptide backbone from proteolytic cleavage. Integrated experimental and all-atom molecular dynamic (MD) numerical investigations demonstrate that incubations in clinical human serum are associated with much higher levels of resistance. Interfacial aptamer co-immobilization then supports highly effective specific target recognition. The so-generated glycosylated peptide (GP) sensors display high detection accuracy (compared to hospital standards) and promising clinical applicability, over and above that possible with analogous non-glycosylated peptide (non-GP) analogues. This work, then, establishes a new, versatile and highly-accessible strategy to support highly challenging and diagnostically-impactful molecular detection.

Introduction

The highly sensitive and scalable electrochemical detection of disease biomarkers in complex biological fluids (e.g., whole blood, serum, interstitial fluid (ISF), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)) can offer a great deal to early disease diagnosis and treatment monitoring.1–6 Nevertheless, the nonspecific adsorption of interfering substances from these matrices poses a formidable challenge that results from false positive/negative signals, decreased assay accuracy, general failures and, ultimately, compromised clinical decisions.7–10 The development of robust antifouling interfaces with high stability remains, then, a central real-world challenge.11

A broad range of antifouling materials have been developed to address this issue. Polyethylene glycol (PEG) and its derivatives represent widely used hydrophilic coatings,12,13 while zwitterionic polymers exhibit a strong hydration-mediated resistance to nonspecific adsorption.14–16 Peptide interfaces have also emerged as promising alternatives with high levels of structural tunability.17,18 To enhance the protease resistance of these in clinical human serum, researchers have implemented a number of approaches, including the incorporation of non-natural amino acids (e.g., d-amino acids),19–21 peptide cyclization,22–25 PEG grafting,12,26 and hydrocarbon stapling.24,27–29

The glycocalyx, a dense and complex carbohydrate network, forms a villous or brush-like structure on the surface of vascular endothelial cells. It is rich in highly glycosylated components, primarily composed of proteoglycans, glycoproteins, and glycolipids.30,31 Among these, O-glycosylation, the covalent attachment of monosaccharides to the hydroxyl groups of serine (S) or threonine (T) residues—has generated significant attention.32–37 Saccharides, ubiquitous in nature, possess abundant polar hydroxyl groups that confer high levels of hydrophilicity and further functionalization. Using a biomimetic glycosylation approach, the Jin group have, for example, successfully developed highly water-soluble polyhydroxy thioalkyl iron–cobalt derivatives, demonstrating the potential of this strategy for enhancing the performance of energy storage materials.38 The Jacobsen group have reported a glycosylation reaction mediated by a small-molecule catalyst, achieving high regioselectivity and stereoselectivity, thus providing a more direct and efficient route for the synthesis of complex oligosaccharides and glycoconjugates.39 Simultaneously, the Pashkuleva team have demonstrated the O-glycosylation of tripeptides in inhibiting peptide aggregation and significantly enhancing the thermal stability of generated assemblies.34

Inspired by the vascular endothelial glycocalyx, we generated novel glycosylated peptides (GPs) in order to present high levels of hydrophilicity and protease resistance. Drawing on previous work with non-glycosylated peptides (non-GP),40–42 we rationally introduced a β-d-glucose (β-d-Glc) linkage at its specific C-terminal site.33,34,43–45 The peptide glycosylation process, along with its comparative (to non-GP) performance, is presented in Scheme 1.

Results and discussion

Design rationale of the GP

Inspired by the structure and function of the vascular endothelial glycocalyx, this design leverages the distinct three-dimensional conformation of the β-d-Glc. Spatially, conjugating β-d-Glc to the terminus of the d-amino acid configured linear zwitterionic peptide (ZP) introduces significant steric hindrance. This effectively shields the electrode surface, minimizing direct contact with nonspecific biomolecules, thereby reducing biofouling and enhancing the interface's stability against enzymatic degradation in complex biofluids. Physiochemically, both β-d-Glc and the d-amino acid configured ZP exhibit excellent hydrophilicity and hydration capacity. Specifically, the abundant hydroxyl groups in β-d-Glc form strong hydrogen bonds (H-bonds) with water molecules, while the d-amino acid configured ZP establishes a dense hydration layer via strong electrostatic interactions between its oppositely charged groups and water. The glycosylation of the d-amino acid configured ZP with β-d-Glc thus constructs a highly hydrated barrier at the electrode interface, effectively suppressing nonspecific protein adsorption and proteolytic degradation.

It is noteworthy that the synergistic action of β-d-Glc and the d-amino acid configured ZP is anticipated to surpass that of the d-amino acid configured ZP alone. This composite modification integrates steric repulsion, a hydration layer protection mechanism, and a charge-neutral surface. Consequently, the sensor interface modified with β-d-Glc glycosylated d-amino acid configured ZP is expected to maintain high sensitivity for target recognition while possessing superior anti-biofouling properties. This will significantly enhance detection accuracy, long-term stability, and reliability in complex biological clinical samples.

Validation of antifouling and protease-resistant properties

In order to assess the potentially productive effects of glycosylation in increasing the resistance to both protease degradation and antifouling. We first assessed the antifouling performance of the base interfaces (PEDOT/GCE and AuNPs/PEDOT/GCE) in various complex biofluids (Fig. S7A). Subsequently, the constructed GP and non-GP interfaces were exposed to a range of undiluted complex biofluids before and after exopeptidase (carboxypeptidase Y (CPY)) and endopeptidase (trypsin (Try)) treatment. As shown in Fig. 1C and S7B, the GP interface exhibited markedly greater antifouling (compared to analogous non-GP interfaces) across all tested fluids (pink vs. yellow) and maintained this after 4 h of continued exposure to CPY and Try (blue vs. magenta). This is largely attributable to the excellent hydrophilicity and hydration capacity of both β-d-Glc and the d-amino acid-configured ZP, whose combination creates a highly hydrated barrier at the electrode interface, effectively suppressing nonspecific protein adsorption and protease degradation. These observations are fully consistent with both good antifouling characteristics and the β-d-Glc moiety being active in shielding against the protease.

To further investigate the cause of the signal decrease, we examined the antifouling performance of GP and non-GP in human blood and serum under three conditions: without CPY/Try, with inactivated CPY/Try, and after CPY/Try degradation. As shown in Fig. S8 and S9, we concluded that a small portion of the signal decrease was likely attributable to nonspecific adsorption of CPY/Try, while the primary cause should still be the loss of antifouling performance due to enzymatic degradation by CPY/Try, leading to the signal decrease.

Subsequently, we individually evaluated the antifouling performance of the PEG and ZP (Fig. 1Cvs. S7C). Compared with these two standard antifouling materials (PEG and ZP), the GP still exhibited markedly superior low-fouling adsorption, thereby demonstrating that glycosylation has a pronounced effect on enhancing peptide hydration and antifouling performance.

The impact of glycosylation on fouling was further analyzed through incubation experiments with labelled bovine serum albumin (FITC-BSA), myoglobin (Cy3-Mb), and lysozyme (Cy5-Lys) using confocal fluorescence imaging. Notably (Fig. 1D), the GP interface again exhibited markedly reduced/undetectable levels of fouling. The observations again attribute to the high hydrophilicity and the strong steric hindrance presented by its three-dimensional structure (see supporting simulations of the Section “MD Analysis of Nonspecific Fouling Interactions”). The retention of these characteristics over prolonged periods (45 days) is striking (Fig. 2B–D and S10–S12).

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation of hydration

To generate a more robust mechanistic understanding of these observations, we first examined hydration structures at the GP and non-GP interfaces (Fig. S13). The two-dimensional number density (2D-ND) distribution of water molecules (N nm−3, N being the molecular number of water molecules), projected along the XZ and YZ planes, was calculated to visualize the hydration-layer distribution. Hydration at the non-GP surfaces is relatively sparse (Fig. 2E and G) compared to the dense hydration at the glycated analogue (Fig. 2F and H), where water density is notably higher than bulk water (indicative of strong association with the peptides).

A more detailed analysis reveals the following: (i) comparative hydration at the peptide headgroups (blue-colored in Fig. S1). To delineate the structural origin of the distinct hydration behaviors, we examined hydration at the peptide headgroups (the sole structural difference between GP and non-GP), focusing on the 8–9 nm region along the Z-axis (Fig. 2E–H). The GP headgroup exhibits a markedly denser, more continuous, and more uniformly distributed hydration shell (higher number densities, deeper color regions) with it comparatively more diffuse and discontinuous in the non-GP headgroup regions.

(ii) Quantification of peptide–water hydrogen bonding (H-bonding). To quantify the hydration strength of GP and non-GP, we calculated the total H-bonds and their angle/distance distributions between each peptide and surrounding water molecules. As shown in Fig. 3A, GP forms significantly more H-bonds with water (∼4650) than non-GP (∼4250), due to the hydroxyl groups the β-d-Glc moiety. The H-bond angle (∼11.5°) and distance (∼2.8 Å) distributions for both peptides are nearly identical due to their similar backbone structures, with difference expected to arise primarily from the β-d-Glc glycosylated headgroups (Fig. 3B and C). On examining the associated H-bonding in this region, we resolve the GP headgroup forming ∼750 H-bonds, far exceeding the ∼260 H-bonds formed by non-GP headgroups (Fig. 3D). Moreover, GP headgroup H-bonds exhibit smaller angles (11.5°) and shorter distances (2.77 Å), compared to non-GP's 16.5° and 2.87 Å (Fig. 3E and F). These shorter, more linear H-bonds reflect stronger and more directional interactions. Collectively, these results support the potent effects of glycosylation in enhancing headgroup hydration, yielding a denser, more continuous, and structurally well-defined hydration layer around GP. Further analyses (Fig. 3G) indicate that the imposed effect on water by the glycosylated moiety additionally manifests in terms of prolonged H-bonds lifetimes. (iii) Peptide–hydration layer interactions: a quantitative analysis of spatial distribution. To further characterize the hydration environment surrounding the peptide headgroups, we calculated the radial distribution function (RDF) of water molecules centered on the oxygen atoms in the headgroups. As shown in Fig. S14, the first RDF peak for GP occurs at ∼1.86 Å, slightly closer than the ∼1.92 Å peak observed for non-GP. The higher peak intensity for GP indicates a greater local water density within the first hydration shell. These results align with the H-bond analysis and further demonstrate the role of interfacial glycation in supporting a more ordered and continuous hydration layer. In order to evaluate the non-covalent interactions more comprehensively, we further quantified the total electrostatic (coulombic) and van der Waals (vdW) interaction energies at these interfaces. As shown in Fig. 3H and I, the GP interfaces were found to consistently present stronger interactions. If one isolates the electrostatic interactions between charged Glu (E) (–COOH) and Lys (K) (–NH2) or polar (e.g., the –OH on serine (S)) peptide residues and water permanent dipoles, these are also notably stronger for the GP derivative (approximately −1.60 × 105 kJ mol−1 compared to −1.42 × 105 kJ mol−1). Detailed energy decompositions are provided in Fig. S15. Collectively, these analyses demonstrate the potent role that glycosylation has in promoting strong interactions (quantified H-bonding, vdW forces, and electrostatic interactions) with water molecules leading to the formation of a denser, more continuous, and structurally more stable hydration layer.

MD analysis of nonspecific fouling interactions

Following the above hydration analyses, we next investigate the broad-spectrum antifouling through a panel of model proteins with distinct net charges at physiological pH—positively charged lysozyme (Lys, pl: 10.5–11.2), negatively charged calmodulin (CaM, pl: 4.0–4.3), and nearly neutral myoglobin (Mb, pl: 6.8–7.3) with GP and non-GP interfaces. These comparisons allow an evaluation of the respective contributions of vdW versus electrostatics interactions. Subsequently, three geometric descriptors were evaluated between the Lys, CaM, Mb and the non-GP or GP interfaces: the minimum contact distance (MCD), number of contact atoms (NCA), and contact surface area (CSA). As shown in Fig. 4A, all three foul proteins rapidly approached and stably contacts with the non-GP interface, with the MCD stabilizing below 0.3 nm after ∼23 ns (Lys), ∼88 ns (CaM), and ∼6 ns (Mb), indicating successful adsorption. In contrast, MCD values for GP fluctuated and remained above 1.0 nm for most of the simulation, suggesting no stable protein–interface binding. For non-GP interface, the NCA and CSA values reached ∼326 and 8.97 nm2 for Lys, ∼202 and 6.26 nm2 for CaM, and ∼349 and 10.22 nm2 for Mb, confirming extensive foul protein–interface contacts, contrasting strongly with resolved negligible NCA and CSA at the glycated surface (demonstrating minimal or no direct contact (Fig. 4B and C)). Fig. S16 reveals that all three proteins form intermolecular H-bonds with the non-GP: Lys and Mb each form approximately 8 H-bonds, CaM forms roughly 4, indicating relatively weaker interactions. Strikingly, none of the proteins establish persistent intermolecular H-bonds with GP interface.

To further support the above picture, we quantified binding energies. At the non-GP interface, vdW interaction energies increase with time then plateaued (at 100 ns) at −365 kJ mol−1, −307 kJ mol−1, and −449 kJ mol−1 for Lys, CaM, and Mb, respectively (Fig. 4D). Fig. 4E further reveals the electrostatic interactions differ markedly. Lys showed strong attraction (−1439 kJ mol−1, dominant driving force), Mb exhibits a moderate electrostatic interaction (−318 kJ mol−1, mixed vdW and electrostatic), and CaM predominantly positive (repulsion, adsorption driven by vdW). None of these fouling proteins displayed positive association with GP, with interaction energies extremely weak (∼0 kJ mol−1), consistent with other analyses within this work (refer to DPV and fluorescence data). The distinct interaction trends reflect the intrinsic differences in interface charge distribution and protein structural features.

Based on the aforementioned mechanisms, we employed the MM/PBSA method to analyze the macroscopic change in thermodynamic binding free energy (ΔGbind), with more negative values representative of stronger interactions. Fig. 4F revealed strong interactions between non-GP and Lys (ΔGbind ≈ −34 kcal mol−1) and Mb (ΔGbind ≈ −21 kcal mol−1). In contrast, non-GP interactions with CaM are highly variable (ΔGbind ≈ −40 to 30 kcal mol−1) due to electrostatic repulsion and weaker vdW interactions. Nevertheless, trajectory snapshots (Fig. 4G; see corresponding dynamic processes in Videos 1–6) and contact analysis confirmed that CaM maintains an intermittent but persistent contact with non-GP, while Lys and Mb establish stable and lasting contact, consistent with their low MCD values and experimental analyses (refer to DPV and fluorescence) see further details in Fig. S17. Strikingly, GP showed negligible binding (ΔGbind ≈ 0 kcal mol−1) with any of the three fouling proteins, even after CaM contacted with GP, it immediately rebounds at the next moment, reflecting no stable interfacial binding.

Collectively, these observations are self-consistent and entirely supportive of the experimental (zeta potential, water contact angle, DPV signal stability and confocal imaging) observations.

Analytical performance of the GP-biosensor

Prior to evaluating sensor (see SI for sensor fabrication detail) performance, the relative concentrations of GP and the NSE-Apt in prepared films were optimized (Fig. S18). As presented in Fig. 5A and B, the sensors exhibit nearly linear DPV responses versus target NSE concentrations in both PBS and fetal bovine serum (FBS), outperforming most reported studies (Table S1). Although our sensor exhibits notable advantages in antifouling and long-term reliability in complex clinical biofluids, it still has some limitations compared with state-of-the-art NSE sensors (Table S2), and breakthroughs in sensitivity are still needed. It is worth noting that the non-GP sensors showed significant differences between the GP sensors in FBS (Fig. S19A and B vs.5A and B). Subsequently, to further highlight the advantage of the GP in sensing, we compared its sensing performance with that of the most widely adopted standard material PEG (Fig. 5Avs. S20). Compared with sensing in PBS, PEG exhibited a greater change in FBS and was more significantly affected by interferents than the GP, thus indicating that the GP offers stronger practical application advantages.

(B) Corresponding DPV responses (a) PBS and (b) FBS. (C) Selectivity analysis for NSE (0.1 µg mL−1) against various potentially interfering proteins (10 µg mL−1) and their mixture (Mix). In the presence of NSE concentrations at clinically relevant positive 0.1 µg mL−1, signals remained negligible with a signal interference ratio ((ΔIMix − ΔINSE)/ΔINSE) of merely 3.50% even after adding 10 µg mL−1 of interferents such as cortisol (Cort), SARS-CoV-2 spike glycoprotein receptor binding domain (RBD), C-reactive protein (CRP), aminopeptidase N (APN), vancomycin (Vanc), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA) (Fig. 5C). This retained assaying selectivity in real-world relevant fluids is a critical part of impactful sensing. Across 5 independently-prepared GP sensors (and two clinically relevant spiked NSE concentrations), response relative standard deviations (RSDs%) were 2.92% and 1.36%, respectively (Fig. 5D). The regenerability of the GP sensors were evaluated by immersing the electrode in 1.0 M NaOH solution at 4 °C for 10 min after NSE detection, followed by reuse for NSE detection (100 ng mL−1). Fig. S21 presents the current responses of the sensor after 10 regeneration cycles, showing only a slight decrease in current, indicating that the GP sensors can be readily regenerated.

Clinical performance evaluation

In assaying for NSE in 20 real clinical samples, including 10 negative (healthy) and 10 positive (small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) patients) (Fig. 6A), a notable discrepancy is observed for quantifications at non-GP sensors and hospital standard analyses (Fig. 6B and C). All experiments were performed in accordance with the Guidelines of the Qingdao Central Hospital (Qingdao, China) and approved by the ethics committee at Qingdao university of science and technology. Informed consents were obtained from human participants of this study. A Bland–Altman analysis (Fig. 6D) reveals a mean difference of 1.14 ng mL−1, with only 90% of points within a 95% limit of agreement, and a coefficient of variation (CV) of 57.7%. In contrast, quantifications at GP sensors demonstrated a much better agreement across the same analyses (Fig. 6E and F), with corresponding Bland–Altman analyses (Fig. 6G) showing a mean difference of only 0.6 ng mL−1, 95% of data points within a 95% limit, and a substantially lower CV of 7.9%. Moreover, as shown in the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) plot (Fig. S22), the GP sensors yielded R2 = 0.9948, whereas the non-GP sensors gave R2 = 0.7795, indicating that the GP sensors exhibit high correlated with the hospital detection method. These observations, of course, underscore the valuable role of interfacial glycosylation in retaining clinically relevant assaying accuracy.

Conclusions

In conclusion, inspired by the structure and function of the vascular endothelial glycocalyx, we have developed a C-terminal glycosylation strategy for a peptide-based interfacial sensing platform to detect specific NSE in serum, effectively addressing persistent issues of biofouling and enzymatic degradation typically prohibitively problematic in complex clinical biofluids. Combined MD simulations and experimental validations demonstrate the key role of glycated hydration at conductive interfaces thereafter able to support high levels of analytical accuracy with real patient samples. We believe the approaches herein are, then, tractable and well-suited for real-world biomedical applications.
