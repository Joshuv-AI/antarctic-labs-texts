Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry reveals known and novel (NatC, BCS1L) antinuclear antibodies in rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Jonas De Leeuw, Birthe Michiels, Rita Derua, Jean-Baptiste Vulsteke, Doreen Dillaerts, Maaike Cockx, Patrick Verschueren, Xavier Bossuyt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIn a fraction of patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA), antinuclear antibodies (ANA) are present. However, in most ANA-positive samples from patients with RA, no autoantigens can be identified. We aimed to characterise the ANA landscape in RA and to identify known and novel autoantigens using an unbiased immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) approach.MethodsSera from 51 ANA-positive, DMARD-naïve patients with RA were analysed by IP-MS using HeLa nuclear or cytoplasmic extracts (depending on the ANA staining pattern). Immunoprecipitated proteins were digested and identified using liquid chromatography coupled with MS. The prevalence of new or unfamiliar autoantigens was assessed in 43 healthy individuals and 88 ANA-positive individuals with a systemic rheumatic disease (systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjögren’s disease, systemic sclerosis and idiopathic inflammatory myopathy).ResultsAutoantigens were detected in 10 of 51 RA samples (in 2/29 with titre 1/160, in 4/12 with titre 1/320 and in 4/10 with titre ≥1/640). Autoantigens identified included SSA-Ro60, SSB-La, Ku, fibrillarin, components of the mitochondrial multienzyme complex, protein argonaute 1, 2, 3 and T-cell restricted intracellular antigen 1 and TIA-1-related protein. Two novel autoantigens were discovered: the N-terminal acetyltransferase C (NatC) complex and mitochondrial chaperone BCS1. Neither NatC nor BCS1 was detected in healthy individuals or individuals with other systemic rheumatic diseases.ConclusionIP-MS revealed a diverse spectrum of autoantigens in patients with ANA-positive RA, including previously unreported targets. These findings expand our knowledge of RA-associated autoimmunity and highlight antibodies to NatC and BCS1 as potential novel biomarkers. Further studies are needed to assess their clinical relevance.Keywords: Autoantibodies, Rheumatoid Arthritis, Autoimmunity, Biomarkers

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic autoimmune disease characterised by chronic joint inflammation potentially resulting in progressive cartilage and bone destruction. In two-thirds of patients with early RA, rheumatoid factor (RF) and/or anticitrullinated protein antibodies (ACPAs) are present. While these antibodies are well-established markers of RA, antinuclear antibodies (ANAs) are found in 25%–77% of patients with RA, which is higher than the ANA prevalence in the general population.1 2 However, in most ANA-positive samples from patients with RA, no specific autoantigen can be identified. A recently developed unbiased proteomic approach based on immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry (IP-MS)3 enables comprehensive identification of autoantigens, including previously unrecognised autoantigens. This approach has recently been applied in systemic autoimmune diseases, revealing novel antigenic landscapes and expanding our understanding of disease-specific autoimmunity.3,7 Systematic characterisation of ANA-associated autoantigens in RA using IP-MS has not been performed. In this study, we aimed to expand our knowledge on the ANA landscape in RA by applying the recently described3 unbiased IP-MS approach, which allows identification of known and unknown autoantigens in patients with RA.

Materials and methods

IP-MS workflow

Identification of autoantibodies by IP-MS was performed as described by Vulsteke et al.3 In short, immunoprecipitation was performed using patient sera cross-linked to A/G magnetic beads and incubated with HeLa nuclear or cytoplasmic extracts as a protein source, depending on the human epithelial type 2 (HEp-2) indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) ANA pattern. Precipitated proteins were digested and analysed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry on a Waters Xevo G3 Quadrupole Time-Of-Flight machine. Protein identification and relative protein quantification using label-free Hi3 were done using ProteinLynx Global Server software. Samples in which autoantigens were identified were retested in an independent IP-MS experiment in order to confirm the results.

RA patient samples, healthy and disease controls

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Hospital Leuven (Belgium) (S65091). Fifty-one patients with RA were included for IP-MS analysis. Patients were selected as detailed below.

The patients were selected out of 187 DMARD-naïve patients with RA based on ANA positivity. The 187 patients were from (i) the CareRA study cohort (n=82), an early RA cohort with patient inclusion based on the 1987 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) classification criteria for RA,8 (ii) the ReCoRD registry (a prospective registry that includes patients with a new RA diagnosis established by a consensus clinical evaluation performed by a team of five experienced rheumatologists at the University Hospitals Leuven) (n=64) and (iii) patients (n=41) with a clinical diagnosis based on established diagnostic standards for whom leftover material was available at the Department of Laboratory Medicine, University Hospitals Leuven (secondary use of material).9

All 187 patients had been screened for ANA by HEp-2 IIF at the University Hospitals Leuven, Belgium. Sixty-two patient samples were HEp-2 IIF ANA positive with titre ≥1/160. For 51 of the 62 HEp-2 IIF ANA-positive individuals, serum was available for IP-MS analysis. The patients for which serum was available were notified. Forty-three healthy controls (28 females and 15 males with a median age of 42 years (age range: 21–64 years)) with an unknown ANA status were included (Ethics Committee approval UZ Leuven S63708). These samples were analysed one or multiple times for a total of 82 analyses with cytoplasmic extract and 127 analyses with nuclear extract as protein source for immunoprecipitation.

A previously reported3 ANA-positive disease control group was included (Ethical approval S60347) consisting of 88 samples: (i) 12 patients with anti-Jo1-positive inflammatory myopathy (IIM) (nine females and three males with a median age of 50 years (age range: 26–71 years)); (ii) 12 patients with anti-Ro60-positive and anti-SSB-positive Sjögren’s disease (11 females and one male with a median age of 54 years (age range: 23–78 years)); (iii) 36 patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) (30 females and six males with a median age of 44 years (age range: 13–72 years)) and (iv) 28 patients with antitopoisomerase I-positive, anticentromere-positive or anti-RNA polymerase III-positive systemic sclerosis (SSc) (21 females and seven males with a median age of 58 years (age range: 35–81 years)). In addition, and not included in our previous work, 14 patients with antisynthetase antibodies (six males and eight females with a median age of 56 years (age range: 20–67 years)) were included (with a total of 18 MS runs).

Results and discussion

In order to identify the target antigens of ANA in RA, we tested 51 ANA-positive samples (titre ≥1:160) obtained from individuals with RA by IP-MS. Autoantigens were identified in 10 of the 51 samples (in two out of 29 samples with titre 1/160, in four out of 12 samples with titre 1/320 and in four out of 10 samples with titre ≥1/640). The patients in whom an autoantigen was identified were all classified as RA according to the 2010 ACR/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology classification criteria.10 Eight previously described autoantibodies and two unreported autoantibodies were found. Anti-SSA-Ro60 and anti-SSB-La antibodies were identified in RA#1. Anti-Ro60 is commonly detected in primary Sjögren’s disease (pSjD) and SLE, and although less frequently, in other connective tissue diseases, including RA.11 12 Notably, this patient developed mild sicca symptoms 4 years after the initial RA diagnosis. Anti-Ro60/La positivity had been identified by routine laboratory analysis, supporting a diagnosis of (secondary) Sjögren’s syndrome overlapping with RA.

Anti-Ku antibodies were identified in RA#2. Anti-Ku is classically associated with overlap syndromes and may be present in patients with SSc, SLE or IIM.13 In this case, the patient presented solely with symmetrical polyarthritis and exhibited no clinical features of systemic autoimmune disease. Of note, anti-Ku were also identified by routine immunological analysis.

Antifibrillarin antibodies were identified in RA#3. Antifibrillarin antibodies exhibit high specificity for SSc, particularly the diffuse cutaneous subtype, and are only infrequently observed in overlap syndromes.14 At present, case #3 does not manifest clinical features indicative of SSc. Of note, antifibrillarin antibodies were also reported by routine immunological analysis.

Antibodies to protein argonaute (AGO) 1, 2 and 3 were identified in RA#5 and RA#6. Anti-AGO2 (anti-Su) has been reported in rheumatic and neurological diseases and in apparently healthy individuals at low prevalence, with a reported prevalence varying across populations and different studies.15 16 In RA#5, routine laboratory analysis revealed anti-SSA-Ro60 which was not observed by IP-MS.

Antibodies to T-cell intracytoplasmic antigen 1 (TIA-1) and TIA-1-related protein (TIAR) were found in RA#8 (in experiments using cytoplasmic as well as nuclear extract). TIA-1 and TIAR are messenger RNA (mRNA) binding proteins involved in post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression.17 Using recombinant proteins and western blot analysis, Jimenez-Boj et al18 reported autoantibodies to TIA-1 and/or TIAR in 61% of individuals with SLE, 42% of individuals with SSc, 15%–31% of individuals with other rheumatic diseases and 6% of healthy controls. In our IP-MS database, we did not identify TIA-1 and/or TIAR as an autoantigen in 43 healthy controls (analysed in 82 runs with cytoplasmic extract and 127 runs with nuclear extract as protein source for IP) and in 102 inflammatory controls, including individuals with antisynthetase syndrome (n=14 with 18 separate MS runs), other IIM (n=12), pSjD (n=12), SLE (n=36) and SSc (n=28). It needs to be further investigated whether autoantibodies to TIA-1/TIAR detected by IP are distinct from those detected by western blot analysis using recombinant proteins.

In three individuals (RA#4, RA#7, RA#10), components of mitochondrial multienzyme complexes were identified in the immunoprecipitate: (dihydrolipoyllysine-residue acetyltransferase E2 component of pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC) (DLAT)) and pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 component subunit alpha/beta (PDHA/PDHB) subunits of PDC. In all three cases, a mitochondrial-like staining (AC21 pattern) was found in HEp-2 IIF analysis. Of note, Jearn and Kim reported antimitochondrial antibodies (detected by IIF) in 33 of 927 patients with RA (3.6%).19 In addition to DLAT and PDHB, mitochondrial chaperone BCS1 (gene name BCS1L) was found in the immunoprecipitate of RA#10 (online supplemental table 1). BCS1 is a mitochondrial chaperone protein involved in the assembly of respiratory chain complex III.20 BCS1 has not yet been reported as a mitochondrial autoantigen and we did not find it in the immunoprecipitate of a cytoplasmic extract of 43 healthy adults (tested in 82 runs) and 14 patients (tested in 18 runs) with antisynthetase antibodies.

The N-terminal acetyltransferase C (NatC) complex was identified in the immunoprecipitate of case RA#9, having a cytoplasmic staining pattern on IIF (online supplemental table 2). The NatC complex is engaged in N-terminal protein acetylation of methionine residues (a post-translational modification) and protects proteins from ubiquitination and degradation.17 NatC is a novel autoantigen and we did not identify it in the immunoprecipitate (using a cytoplasmic extract) of 43 healthy adults (analysed in 82 runs) and 14 patients with antisynthetase antibodies (analysed in 18 runs).

The clinical relevance of autoantibodies against BCS1 and the NatC complex is currently unknown. As the new autoantibodies were only identified in one patient, specific clinical associations could not be studied. As such, these findings should be considered exploratory and primarily demonstrate the ability of the IP-MS approach to uncover previously unrecognised autoantigens in RA.

Conclusion

IP-MS identified autoantigens in 10/51 ANA-positive samples. A multitude of autoantigenic targets was revealed, including antimitochondrial antibodies and antibodies to AGO proteins. Further studies are needed to explore the potential clinical relevance of autoantibodies against the new autoantigens of the NatC complex and BCS1.
