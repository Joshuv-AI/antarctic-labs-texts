Exploring LLMs for Automated Pre-Testing of Cross-Cultural Surveys Conference: ; ; CCS: Human-centered computing Empirical studies in HCI
Divya Mani Adhikari, Vikram Kamath Cannanure, Alexander Hartland, Ingmar Weber

Abstract
. Designing culturally relevant questionnaires for ICTD research is challenging, particularly when adapting surveys for populations to non-western contexts. Prior work adapted questionnaires through expert reviews and pilot studies, which are resource-intensive and time-consuming. To address these challenges, we propose using large language models (LLMs) to automate the questionnaire pretesting process in cross-cultural settings. Our study used LLMs to adapt a U.S.-focused climate opinion survey for a South African audience. We then tested the adapted questionnaire with 116 South African participants via Prolific, asking them to provide feedback on both versions. Participants perceived the LLM-adapted questions as slightly more favorable than the traditional version. Our note opens discussions on the potential role of LLMs in adapting surveys and facilitating cross-cultural questionnaire design.



1.
Introduction

Surveys are a useful methodology in ICTD research for collecting essential data, such as assessing technology adoption and user needs and evaluating the impact of interventions
(
Earl et al. 2013
)
. However, designing relevant questionnaires for the ICTD population is challenging as it requires resources and careful cultural consideration to adapt standardized questionnaires to non-western contexts such as South Africa
(
Toyama 2018
;
Dell and Kumar 2016
)
. Additionally, developing new questionnaires poses challenges as it requires specialized domain experts
(
Synodinos 2003
)
. After a questionnaire is drafted, thorough pre-testing

The feedback is then used to refine the questions before their final distribution.
(

)
is essential, for example, through pilot studies

)
. Pretesting adds utility to the survey pipeline, particularly in cross-cultural settings where language nuances may lead to misunderstandings
(
Gjersing et al. 2010
;
Beaton et al. 2000
)
. However, finding participants for pilot studies could be difficult due to the lack of availability of participants or adequate funding
(
Joseph et al. 2016
)
. Our work proposes a novel workflow using large language models (LLMs) to facilitate automated questionnaire pre-testing for cross-cultural settings. While current LLMs can generally perform well in most contexts, they often lack the cultural nuance needed for accurate representation in non-Western contexts, largely due to their training on data from predominantly Western and high-resource settings
(
Ochieng et al. 2024
)
. Even within high-resource contexts, LLM outputs exhibit various biases
(
Navigli et al. 2023
)
. This limitation poses challenges for generating culturally accurate questionnaires across diverse settings, a gap our proposed approach aims to address.

Traditional pre-testing of a questionnaire draft requires feedback from stakeholders, which is used to finalize the draft. If feasible, domain experts can provide feedback on the initial draft through expert review
(
Ikart 2019
)
or behavior coding
(
Blair et al. 2007
)
. Alternatively, feedback can come from participants from the target audience through cognitive interviews
(
Willis and Artino 2013
)
or pilot studies
(
Ismail et al. 2018
)
. Often, finding the appropriate users and extracting feedback from stakeholders before the survey can be time-consuming and resource-intensive
(
Stahura 2005
)
. Thus, prior work has used a semi-automated version of pre-testing (called web-probing)
(
Lenzner and Neuert 2017
;
Behr et al. 2024
;
Neuert 2023
)
where users are asked follow-up questions to gain a more detailed insight into their reasoning. Recent advancements with Large Language Models (LLMs) have opened further opportunities to automate this pipeline. For example,
Olivos and Liu 2024
;
Sarstedt et al. 2024
show LLMs can be used in pretesting to solicit feedback and suggest necessary changes to improve it.
Haavisto and Welsch 2024
show how LLM-based evaluation and suggestions can help adapt surveys in cross-cultural contexts.

LLMs have shown some potential in pre-testing, as shown by
Olivos and Liu 2024
; however, simply asking an LLM to review a questionnaire could lead to biased suggestions. Furthermore, the ability of LLMs to take on different personas could be useful in simulating automated pre-testing with fictional participants
(
Sarstedt et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
and getting responses from different perspectives that could potentially mitigate the bias shown by the LLM. Existing research has demonstrated that LLMs show human-like behavior
(
Jansen et al. 2023
;
Kim and Lee 2023
;
Argyle et al. 2023
;
Schramowski et al. 2022
;
Horton 2023
;
Hämäläinen et al. 2023
)
which suggests that responses from LLMs could act as proxies of human responses during pretesting.

Our work expands on the literature on LLMs for questionnaire pretesting in a cross-cultural setting. We used LLMs to adapt a standardized questionnaire focused on user climate opinions of US
(
Marlon et al. 2022
)
audiences to a South African audience. We conducted a study with 116 participants to explore the utility of the LLMs in survey research. Through our work, we answer the following research questions:

(1)

RQ1
: Can LLMs pre-test a standardized questionnaire to improve relevance to a target audience?

(2)

RQ2
: How does LLM-generated pre-test content compare with standardized questionnaires?

We gathered participants through Prolific

Participants were requested to rate the questions for both sets of questions on four main criteria: clarity, bias, relevance, and specificity. Our initial findings show that participants perceived the LLM-generated content as less biased and clearer than the standardized questionnaire.

2.1.
Automated Questionnaire Pretesting

The questionnaire was pretested by simulating a real-world pilot study using multiple LLM instances. We initialized one of the LLMs as the interviewer and multiple LLMs as the participants based on the previously generated list of personas. The interviewer LLM then asked questions from the extracted questionnaire, and the simulated participant LLMs responded accordingly based on their persona descriptions. After each question, the interviewer LLM asked follow-up questions dynamically generated based on the previous responses of the participant. The generated follow-up questions followed the cognitive interviewing technique to determine the participants’ thought processes. The conversation history (interview transcript) between the interviewer LLM and the participant LLMs was stored for further analysis. This pipeline is shown in figure

3.
Experiment and Results

We conducted a study on Prolific where we asked crowdsourced participants from South Africa to rate questions from the original and LLM-pretested questionnaires. Participants were asked to rate the questions on four criteria: clarity, bias, relevance, and specificity. The study procedures were approved by Saarland University’s ethical review board (ID:[24-09-6]). The study included 116 participants with at least a bachelor’s degree in Climate Science, Psychology, Sociology, and Social work. Participants were within the 18-54 years range, 75% of whom were Female. About 90% of the participants mentioned having at least a fair experience with surveys. See the Study design in the Appendix
B
for details on the experiment design.

3.1.
RQ1: LLM Pre-testing showed comparable results to standardized survey.

Participants felt the original questions were generally clear, relevant, slightly biased, and specific. Figure
1
B shows the mean score for each criterion after merging related statements using reverse coding for the original and the pretested questions. After pretesting, the updated questionnaire still retains its original qualities, as shown in the graph. Pretesting slightly improves upon clarity and has reduced bias, suggesting that the participants found the pretested questionnaire suitable for the South African audience. Additionally, participants were asked to pick the favored questions for each set. Participants picked the pre-tested question for 4 out of 5 in this task, indicating that most pretested questions were also favorable.

3.2.
RQ2: LLM-based pre-testing improved clarity and slightly reduced bias.

We computed the difference between the mean score of the original and the pretested questions for all metrics, i.e., clarity, bias, relevance, and specificity. We found a slight improvement (1%-3%) in clarity and relevance in the pretested questions. For specificity and bias, the score for the pretested question decreased. We also computed the related paired t-test on the ratings for the four criteria. We found statistically significant results (p<0.05) for clarity and marginally significant results for bias. 4.
Discussion

This work showed a pipeline for using LLMs to generate and test survey questionnaires. The LLM’s knowledge base allows questionnaire analysis from different perspectives (e.g., from a different cultural standpoint) and enables them to adapt existing questionnaires for cross-cultural studies. For example, in question 4 (Appendix
D
), the original question asks, “
Do you think Congress should be doing more or less to address global warming?
”. The LLM understood that “Congress” doesn’t apply to South Africa and thus suggests changes to the question. Additionally, the results from participant feedback indicate improvements in clarity and reduction in bias, which suggests promise for future research. However, we request researchers to approach this area with cautious optimism.
Rozado 2024
have shown that current LLMs generally have a center-left leaning that must be considered when working with political surveys. Furthermore,
Wang et al. 2024
argue that LLMs cannot portray identity groups as they tend to misportray and flatten their representation. Similarly, LLMs could fail in the low-resource context
(
Ochieng et al. 2024
)
. Therefore, future work could extend our work for these contextual limitations.

Nevertheless, our work could motivate the application of LLMs in other aspects of survey research. Future studies could test our pipeline on other domains to determine if LLMs exhibit utility beyond our initial study. Moreover, expert reviews of the LLM suggestions could help validate the results obtained from this study.

Appendix A
Questionnaire Generation using LLMs

Each Questionnaire has a research question that the researcher wants to have answered through the survey. We write a detailed description of the research question that includes the main research idea, the number of questions in the questionnaire, the language, the target audience details, specific topics that the questions should relate to, and so on. This description is included in the generation prompt passed to the LLM. The system initialization and the generation prompt can be seen in the Appendix
C
. Next, we use the Exa API
4
4

4

https://exa.ai/
to get five relevant news articles based on the given research question and generate a summary of the articles. This summary is also passed to LLM as contextual information to make the generated questionnaire more relevant to the current affairs and political climate. Another external source we use is a database of questions downloaded from the Survey Quality Predictor 3.0
(
W.E. Saris 2022
)
. It is a collection of crowdsourced multilingual and multi-domain questions. We use an LLM to select the appropriate filter criteria for language and domain based on the given research question and then use FAISS
(
Johnson et al. 2019
)
to retrieve the most relevant questions from the filtered set. Based on this contextual information, the LLM generates a questionnaire relevant to the research question using RAG
(
Lewis et al. 2020
)
. The researcher can then further modify the questions or add new questions to improve the questionnaire.

Using the same information, we generate a list of Personas to simulate the participants in the Pilot study. A Persona is a description of a fictional person. Each Persona contains basic demographic information such as Name, Age, Gender, Education, etc., and dynamic preferences based on the given research question. This pipeline is shown in figure
2
.

Appendix C
Prompts

C.1.
Persona generation prompt

Generate a list of persona based on the following information:

<question>What are the beliefs, perceptions of risk, policy preferences, and behaviors of the South African public regarding climate change and global warming?</question>

C.2.
Interviewer prompt

You are a survey interviewer tasked with conducting a study.
Your role is to follow a predetermined questionnaire diligently, ensuring that each question is asked exactly as written without any deviations.
Once the questionnaire is completed, conclude the conversation appropriately. After ending the conversation, just reply with "END" without quotes.

C.3.
Follow-up Question generation prompt

You will be given a chat history with a participant and you generate a probing question to probe the participant.
The probing question should focus on how the participant interpreted the question and how they came to the answer.

Some examples of the cognitive probing questions are:

- What does the term <term> mean to you?

- How did you come up with your answer?

- Can you repeat the question I just asked you in your own words?

- How sure are you that <answer>?

- How do you remember that you have <answer>?

- Why do you say that you think it is <answer>?

- Was that easy or hard to answer?

- I see you have <answer>. Can you tell me more about that?

- What do you think the purpose of this question is?

- I noticed that you hesitated before answering. Tell me what you were thinking.

- Tell me more about that.

Use above template along with the chat history to generate specific probing questions.
Detect the appropriate language to use based on the context of the chat history. Otherwise, use English.
The output should be just the question without any numbering or additional text.

C.4.
Participant prompt

You are a participant in a survey interview for a study. Following are your details:

Name: Thabo Dlamini

Age: 35

Gender: Male

Race: Black

Location: Johannesburg, South Africa

Occupation: Environmental Scientist

Education: Masters in Environmental Science

Beliefs: Strong believer in climate change and its impacts

Perceptions of Risk: High concern about air pollution and water scarcity

Policy Preferences: Supports government regulations on carbon emissions

Behaviors: Actively recycles and uses public transportation

You will be asked a series of questions based on the survey questionnaire.
Please answer them strictly following the details above and the chat history so far below.
If the question has options strictly pick just the option. For open-ended questions, answer in maximum 2-3 sentences.
If the question is in a language other than English, please answer in that respective language.
Please stick strictly to the details provided above and do not deviate from them.

C.5.
Reviewer prompt

As a reviewer, you have been provided with the transcripts of a survey pilot study interviews, along with the original research question.
Your task is to evaluate the survey questionnaire based on several criteria, including clarity, comprehension, and sensitivity.
Select a question from the interview transcript where the participant encountered difficulties or misunderstandings.
Check for any contradictions or inconsistencies in the participant’s responses.

Some of the possible issues that you need to look for are:

double questions

ambiguous questions

ambiguous word meanings

loaded or leading questions or phrase

level of question difficulty

lopsided response categories

missing response categories

missing questions

necessity and relevance of individual questions

discriminating questions (between certain groups within the target group)

non-response rates

effect of ordinal position of multiple responses

perceptions of pictures

degree of attention

Provide a detailed review of the survey questionnaire, highlighting any areas that need improvement or revision as shown in following example.
Then, suggest the corrected questions as well.
Consider only the main questions when suggesting improvements and ignore follow up questions when suggesting corrections.
Think carefully about the participant’s responses to the questions and how they could be improved.
When describing issues, mention the question as well as the participant number (e.g. Persona 1) and what problem you found with it.

Example output:

Question: Question from the survey questionnaire.

Problem: Describe the problem with the question. Relate it to the above points.

Sources: Mention the participant number and the transcript where the issue was observed.

Suggestion: Suggest a corrected version of the question.

Question: Question from the survey questionnaire.

Problem: Describe the problem with the question. Relate it to the above points.

Sources: Mention the participant number and the transcript where the issue was observed.

Suggestion: Suggest a corrected version of the question.

Assuming global warming is happening, do you think it is…?

Assuming global warming is happening, what do you believe is the primary cause?

Options: Caused mostly by human activities, Caused mostly by natural changes in the environment, Don’t know, Other (please specify)

This question can be problematic because it includes an option ("None of the above because global warming isn’t happening") that contradicts the assumption made in the question. This might confuse the respondents or lead to inconsistencies in their answers. It’s important that the question aligns with the premise. Also, the option "Other" seems vague, which can lead to confusion about what respondents should include under this category.
