Multi-omics study of molecular and genetic bases of orthostatic hypotension
Authors: Elena Zelenova, Veronika Daniel, Maria Bruttan, Lilya Artemieva, Ekaterina Spektor, Aleksandra Mamchur, Daria Kashtanova, Mikhail Ivanov, Gerel Abushinova, Lorena Matkava, Antonina Rumyantseva, Aleksey Ivashechkin, Pavel Grebnev, Liliya Golubnikova, Sergey Mitrofanov, Anna Akopyan, Olga Beloshevskaya, Tatiana Saliyeva, Irina Tarasova, Irina Strazhesko, Vladimir Yudin, Valentin Makarov, Anton Keskinov, Olga Tkacheva, Sergey Yudin, Veronika Skvortsova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Orthostatic hypotension is a sharp decrease in blood pressure when an individual transitions from a supine to an upright position. OH affects at least 30% of older adults. It is attributed to the dysfunction of the autonomic innervation and decreased vascular bed capacity. Genomic (n = 2526), methylomic (n = 910), and transcriptomic (n = 391) data from centenarians aged 90 years and older were used to examine molecular and genetic factors for OH. No statistically significant genetic predictors of OH were identified. However, the study revealed numerous epigenetic markers of OH indicative of general aging, such as DNA hypomethylation. The predictive DNA methylation-based model for orthostatic hypotension demonstrated an average accuracy of 79%. The transcriptome analyses highlighted associations between OH and inflammation pathways, as well as other age-related biological processes. Integrated omics and clinical data have identified six key mechanisms associated with orthostatic hypotension: metabolic dysregulation, impaired muscle tone, altered cell proliferation, inflammation, humoral regulation, and neural regulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-025-02019-3.

Introduction

Orthostatic hypotension (OH) is a sharp drop in systolic blood pressure of ≥ 20 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure of ≥ 10 mmHg when an individual changes from a horizontal to an upright position [1]. This condition affects over 30% of older adults [2]. It is believed to arise from impaired autonomic innervation and decreased vascular bed capacity [3]. The transition from a lying to a standing position naturally causes blood to redistribute, resulting in its accumulation in the veins of the lower extremities and a reduction in venous return to the heart. As a result, there is a decline in stroke volume, cardiac output, and arterial blood pressure. Baroreceptors located in the aortic arch and carotid sinuses detect these changes and reflexively trigger the sympathetic nervous system [4].

OH can be neurogenic or non-neurogenic [5]. Neurogenic OH is a clinical manifestation of an impaired baroreflex due to damage along any of these pathways. These include suprasegmental pathways, which are affected by central autonomic nervous system dysfunctions, such as autonomic dysfunctions observed in Parkinson’s disease [6] or multiple system [7], and peripheral pathways, which may be compromised by damaged peripheral autonomic nerves, commonly seen in diabetic autonomic neuropathy. Where there is no structural nerve damage, OH may arise as a side effect of certain medications, including nitrates, diuretics, calcium channel blockers, alpha-blockers, dopaminergic and anticholinergic drugs, and tricyclic antidepressants, or it may have a cardiogenic origin [4, 5].

Safarpour et al. showed that OH is more prevalent in women and is associated with increasing age [8]. No data on OH prevalence in centenarians were identified in our search, though the estimated prevalence in the cohort aged 60 years and older is 22% [9]. The clinical significance of OH in elderly stems from its established association with cerebral hypoperfusion [10, 11], an increased risk of falls [12], fractures [10], syncopal episodes [13], and all-cause mortality [10]. Hence, examining the underlying mechanisms of this geriatric syndrome is a high priority within clinical medicine, especially for centenarians, who are more vulnerable to these clinically relevant outcomes.

Several molecular genetic markers of OH have been identified, which may facilitate target identification in drug discovery. Johanson et al. [14] found a link between OH and inflammatory markers, including midkine, a protein also known as the heparin-binding growth factor, immunoglobulin-like transcript 3 (ILT-3) involved in immune response modulation, regenerating islet-derived protein 4 (REG-4), and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase type 5 (TRAP). The authors concluded that OH and cardiovascular autonomic dysfunction represent a complex phenomenon influenced by both hemodynamics and inflammatory mechanisms. In a subsequent study, the authors [15] conducted proteomic analysis to examine OH biomarkers and identified associations between OH and numerous proteins, including matrix metalloproteinase-7 (MMP-7), t-cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain 1 (TIM-1), thrombomodulin (TM), myoglobin (MB), caspase-8 (CASP-8), CXC motif chemokine ligand-1 (CXCL-1), Dickkopf-related protein 1 (DKK-1), lectin-type oxidized LDL receptor 1 (LOX-1), placental growth factor (PIGF), proteinase-activated receptor 1 (PAR-1), and monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1).

Many of these proteins have also been described as biomarkers of atherosclerosis and inflammation. OH has also been associated with increased levels of endostatin, a biologically active peptide formed as a result of collagen XVIII breakdown in the extracellular matrix [16], with the association remaining even after clinical and demographic adjustments. Endostatin has been reported as a marker of heart failure and a predictor of mortality from CVDs [17]. Thus, OH, along with inflammation and vascular dysfunction from a complex cluster, which highlights its complex pathophysiology. This complex cluster should be thoroughly studied from the molecular and genetic standpoints to develop more effective diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Numerous large-cohort studies have examined genetic variants involved in the regulation of both systolic and diastolic blood pressure [18–21], also attempting to establish the relationship between the detected variants and OH. Their results, however, were inconclusive and lacked statistical significance [22]. In a population-based genome-wide association study of OH in two Korean cohorts, none of the 333,651 SNPs met the genome-wide significance threshold. However, eight SNPs were identified in both cohorts and had p values of less than 1*10–5 in the meta-analysis. Four of them were located in the intergenic regions on chromosome 2, near the CTNNA2 gene, which is crucial for dendritic spine stabilization on hippocampal neurons in rodents. The authors conclude that hippocampal neurons can influence OH by interacting with the autonomic nervous system [23].

Genetic studies failed to provide the explanation of blood pressure variability and OH development. Consequently, researchers turned to analysis of methylation sites associated with blood pressure levels. Irvin et al. summarized data on differentially methylated sites in participants with OH and results of whole-epigenomic association studies (EWASs) of blood pressure [24]. Participants with OH had differentially methylated sites in the RAAS genes (the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) [25, 26], which was further confirmed in vivo and in vitro experiments [27]. The above studies have provided data on the potential relationship between blood pressure levels and their deviations with various epigenetic markers, but no such data have been obtained specifically for OH. Thus, epigenetic studies may be extremely beneficial for examining the causes of cardiovascular diseases and may complement the existing methodologies in highlighting the molecular mechanisms that underlie them.

A combination of various ‘omics’ technologies enables the simultaneous analysis of both genetic and epigenetic markers. With the emergence of new technologies, ‘omics’ studies have gained traction and have been utilized to investigate a wide range of conditions [28]. This study aimed to explore the common mechanisms of OH across etiologies in centenarians—the most vulnerable to OH-related complication age group—through integrated multi-omics analysis encompassing genomic, epigenomic (DNA methylation), and transcriptomic domains.

Methods

Participant recruitment

In 2019–2023, participants aged 90 years and older were recruited through the social and geriatric services of Moscow and the Moscow Region. All participants provided informed consent for the collection of blood and biomaterials, as well as for two visits by a physician. Each participant underwent a comprehensive geriatric assessment for 15 geriatric syndromes in accordance with the clinical guidelines of the Ministry of Health of the Russian Federation [29]. OH was assessed following the standard diagnostic procedure [30]. During the orthostatic test, blood pressure was measured using a validated sphygmomanometer that had undergone metrological certification. Baseline blood pressure and heart rate were recorded after 7–10 min of rest in the supine position, followed by measurements on the same arm at 1 and 3 min after transitioning to the standing position—or sitting, if the patient was unable to stand. Orthostatic hypotension was defined as a reduction in systolic blood pressure (SBP) by ≥ 20 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) by ≥ 10 mmHg at any measurement after standing, or a reduction in SBP by ≥ 15 mmHg and/or DBP by ≥ 7 mmHg after transitioning to the sitting position. The test was performed twice, and the diagnosis of orthostatic hypotension was confirmed only if the diagnostic criteria were met in both tests. Detailed information on the geriatric assessment and related procedures can be found in our previous publication [31].

DNA extraction and methylation analysis

DNA was extracted from whole blood samples using the MagAttract HMW DNA Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Sample libraries were prepared using the Illumina DNA Prep reagent kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Document # 1000000025416 v10, Illumina, USA) and IDT for IDT for Illumina Nextera DNA UD Indexes A and B to prevent cross-contamination. The Infinite F Nano + was used for library quantification.

The D1000 Reagents and 4200 TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, USA) were used for library sizing. The automated Freedom EVO platform (Tecan, Switzerland) was used for library pooling. The Agilent HS D1000 ScreenTape Reagents and the 4200 TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, USA). The NovaSeq 6000 was used for whole-genome sequencing with the S4 Reagent Kit (300 cycles) (Illumina, USA) for 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads.

For methylome analysis, 1000 ng of genomic DNA was used in each sample. The Zymo EZ DNA Methylation Kit (Zymo Research) was for bisulfite treatment of DNA. Subsequent sample preparation steps were performed using the Tecan Freedom EVO Workstation and the Illumina Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip Kit (Illumina, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions as outlined in the Infinium HD Methylation Assay Protocol Guide (15019519 v07). The methylation BeadChips were scanned using the Illumina iScan System compatible with the Autoloader 2.× for automated loading and unloading.

Bioinformatic analysis of genomic data

To obtain FASTQ files, sequenced data in BCL format were demultiplexed using the bcl2fastq2 Conversion Software v.2.20.0.422 (Illumina). Sequencing quality was checked using Sequencing Analysis Viewer v.2.4.7 (Illumina). The quality of FASTQ files was checked using FastQC v.0.11.9. Reads were aligned to the reference genome (GRCh38.d1.vd1) using the Illumina DRAGEN Bio-IT Platform (v.07.021.510.3.5.7). The quality of alignment of BAM files was checked using DRAGEN FastQC v.0.11.9 and mosdepth v0.3.1. All samples were checked for duplicates, unmapped reads, and other quality metrics. The mean sequencing coverage was 30 × for all samples.

Strelka2 v2.9.10 (Illumina) with default settings was used for small variant calling (of up to 50 b.p.). CrosscheckFingerprints (Picard), containing a haplotype map file, was used to check for duplicates. Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP v99.2) was used for variant annotation.

Genome-wide association study (GWAS)

To account for population structure, the first 10 principal components from the principal component analysis (PCA) of the genomes were used as covariates in the GWAS.

Variants violating the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (p value < 10–6); variants with an allele frequency (AF) of more than 0.98; multiallelic variants; and variants with a minor allele frequency (MAF) of less than 0.01 were removed.

RNA extraction and sequencing

The Tempus Spin RNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used for total RNA extraction from blood samples collected in Tempus Blood RNA Tubes with an RNA stabilizer, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

The Illumina TruSeq Stranded Total RNA reagent kit and TruSeq RNA UD Indexes were used for transcriptome sample preparation. The Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used for library quantification. The Agilent HS D1000 ScreenTape Reagents and the 4200 TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, USA) were used for library sizing. The NovaSeq 6000 was used for whole-genome sequencing with the S4 Reagent Kit (200 cycles) (Illumina, USA) for 2 × 100 bp paired-end reads.

Bioinformatics analysis of transcriptomic data

Salmon v.1.10.1 [32] was used for transcript-level quantification from RNA-seq data. Genome assembly GRCh38.p13 (human) and its annotation from Gencode v.41 were used as the reference. FastQC v. 0.11.9 and MultiQC v. 1.13 were used for sequencing quality assessment.

Differential expression assessment

Differential expression differences between sample sets were assessed in R using the DESeq2 software package [33]. The padj score, i.e., p values of differences adjusted for multiple testing with the Benjamini–Hochberg correction, was used as the significance level. In each group, genes with non-zero expression levels in less than 80% of the total number of samples were excluded from the analysis. No batch effect correction was performed prior to the analysis; however, the batch number, i.e., the cell number, was input as a covariate.

Functional analysis

Functional analysis of differentially expressed genes was carried out via overrepresentation analysis (ORA) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). Both analyses were based on a list of significant genes, applying a threshold of a multiple testing-adjusted p value (padj) of less than 0.05. The log2 fold change (log2FC) values representing the differences in gene expression levels between the two groups served as the variable comparison operator.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Python version 3.9.12. The data processing was carried out with the pandas library, and statistical tests were executed using SciPy. Continuous variables were reported as medians and interquartile ranges, while categorical variables are reported as absolute and relative numbers (%) of individuals exhibiting a particular trait.

To compare the case (participants with OH) and control (participants without OH) groups, regression analysis (logistic regression) was carried out to adjust for sex and age. The target variable was OH, with p values and logistic regression coefficients reported for each feature.

Differentially methylated sites (significant results)

Expression levels of genes containing sites significantly associated with OH were compared between the case and control groups. Only the KIF13A gene showed statistically significant differences between the groups (Fig. 2B). Notably, this gene contains the site that exhibited the strongest association between its methylation and OH (Table 3; Fig. 2C and D).

The locations of statistically significant differentially methylated CpG sites in the genome were analyzed, and their positions relative to CpG islands were visualized as a proportion of these sites on the MethylationEPIC beadchip (Fig. 3B). The observed distribution pattern of significant differentially methylated CpG sites, particularly the high proportion, can be attributed to the methodology of assessing DNA methylation and should not be interpreted as indicative of their biological implications.

A large proportion of significant CpG sites were located in intergenic regions, and almost all of them were hypomethylated in older adults with OH (Table 3), while the genes, including introns and promoter regions, contained fewer significant sites than a beadchip provides for.

Predictive models

Both logistic regression and SVC predictive models showed fairly high predictive capacities, with an average ROC AUC of 0.76 and SVC 0.79, respectively. Figure 4A shows ROC AUC values across all cross-validation runs. Since the SVC model had a higher ROC AUC, it was used to identify the importance of individual features. Figure 4B provides the permutation importance of the features included in the SVC model.

Fig. 4Predictive models. A ROC-AUC across 20 runs of cross-validation of the logistic regression and SVC models. B Permutation importance of features included in the SVC model

Predictive models. A ROC-AUC across 20 runs of cross-validation of the logistic regression and SVC models. B Permutation importance of features included in the SVC model

The cg00321709 site in the CYP2E1 gene exhibited the highest permutation importance in the SVC model. The methylation level of this site in the EWAS was positively associated with OH, i.e., it had a positive regression coefficient. The next in importance was the intergenic site cg10831324, with a negative regression coefficient in the EWAS. This shows that the model considers both risk and protective factors with a high level of importance.

Transcriptome analysis

Transcriplomic data were available for 391 participants. The analysis of differentially expressed genes aimed at identifying transcriptomic markers of OH revealed 713 statistically significant genes after the multiple testing adjustment (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Table S2). The results were interpreted via over-representation analysis (ORA) (Fig. 5B) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) (Fig. 5C). In the GSEA, most of the significant functional pathways were associated with inflammatory processes, and the genes involved in these pathways were predominantly overexpressed in older adults with OH.

Discussion

Overall fındıngs

This study aimed at examining the mechanisms underlying orthostatic hypotension in older adults by integrating molecular and genetic data derived from their blood samples (Fig. 6), with particular emphasis on the processes involved in the dysregulation of vascular tone and the adaptability of vascular beds to changes in gravity. However, no statistically significant genetic associations were identified. Moreover, the analysis of marginally sub-threshold genetic variants did not reveal any potential contributors to alterations in blood pressure.

Scheme of the study results annotations

None of the marginally sub-threshold genetic variants were associated with blood pressure regulation. Notably, intronic variants in the DIPK1A (rs200137283), LINC02511 (rs144052797, rs149142272), KCNQ1 (rs72844290 and rs12284372), and GINS1 genes (rs188473560), as well as the intergenic rs17088118 variant, have not been previously linked to the examined phenotype. The DIPK1A gene encodes an FAM69 protein. FAM69 is a family of cysteine-rich type II transmembrane proteins localized in the endoplasmic reticulum with unclear functions [34]. The GINS1 gene encodes a protein, which is part of the GINS1 complex, a core component of the CMG helicase, involved in DNA replication [35]. The potassium channel encoded by the KCNQ1 gene is involved in the normal functioning of the cardiovascular system and the maintenance of water and salt homeostasis [36]; however, the specific contributions of the detected variants to OH have yet to be determined.

The absence of statistically significant findings may be attributed to the fact that OH can arise from a range of other conditions, which often do not share common genetic bases. Moreover, OH can be induced by a whole range of drugs. Additionally, the observed epigenetic and expression changes may be adaptive and compensatory mechanisms due to impaired hemodynamic responses to the transition from lying to standing position and may be unrelated to the underlying causes of OH. Therefore, the transcriptomic and epigenomic characteristics observed in OH patients may be of great significance. In this study, we obtained findings on both non-specific changes and specific significant findings on differential methylation and gene expression levels that affect the cardiovascular and nervous systems; the condition of the blood vessel wall, including inflammation, lipid metabolism, and the functioning of the intercellular matrix; and the blood system. Hence, many of the findings represent a single cluster of pathological or compensatory processes that affect all functional pathways involved in blood pressure regulation.

METABOLISM

Participants with OH showed approximately twice as frerquent malnutrition syndrome, along with lower glucose levels and higher total and LDL-cholesterol values. On the one hand, poor nutritional status leads to hypovolemia through both reduced fluid intake and decreased plasma oncotic pressure—the latter being supported by lower total protein levels in the OH group. On the other hand, markers of metabolic dysfunction such as hypercholesterolemia reflect greater cardiovascular comorbidity burden, where many standard medications (calcium channel blockers, diuretics, etc.) are known to induce orthostatic hypotension. Dehydration and hypovolemia also initiate a series of physiological responses, the effects of which we observed in our study. For instance, we observed differential methylation of the SERPINF2 and PDGFC genes. The former encodes a serpin protein, a major plasmin inhibitor, that is crucial for the regulation of normal hemostasis [37]; the latter encodes the platelet-derived growth factor, PDGFC [38]. This may be associated with hemoconcentration, a common phenomenon that occurs when the circulating blood volume decreases, resulting in a compensatory increase in procoagulant activity [39, 40]. Hypercoagulation is frequently linked to various inflammatory processes [41], the biomarkers of which we identified in our study. These will be discussed below in greater detail.

Adequate inotropy and chronotropy ensure that the myocardium can adapt to changing hemodynamic conditions. The observed epigenetic changes in our study may also reflect OH-induced alteration in the myocardium. For example, the RYR2 gene encodes isoform 2 of the ryanodine receptor RyR2, primarily expressed in cardiomyocytes, which, like other ryanodine receptors, is a regulator of cardiac excitation–contraction coupling [42]. Moreover, the FHOD3 gene, abundantly expressed in cardiomyocytes, encodes a protein that acts as a regulator of actin filament assembly in sarcomeres within cardiomyocytes, thus playing an important role in maintaining normal myocardial contractility [43]. A number of mutations in FHOD3 have been shown to contribute to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [44, 45]. The differential methylation of RYR2 and FHOD3 possibly reflects compensatory or abnormal changes in cardiac function in OH.

MUSCLE TONE

Centenarians with orthostatic hypotension were less physically active and more frequently presented with frailty syndrome. On one hand, reduced physical activity exacerbates orthostatic dysfunction through impaired muscle pump activity and decreased venous return. On the other hand, orthostatic hypotension itself leads to decreased physical activity, thereby creating a vicious cycle. This physical inactivity is further reflected in metabolic markers of reduced energy metabolism, particularly through the observed downregulation of genes related to oxidative phosphorylation. Some of the differentially methylated sites were located in genes also responsible for catabolism, such as ALOX15 and LOXHD1, which encode lipoxygenases, enzymes catalyzing the conversion of polyunsaturated fatty acids into bioactive lipid mediators [46]. PRDM16 is involved in body weight regulation, glucose and lipid metabolism, as well as energy homeostasis, and is one of the therapeutic targets for type 2 diabetes [47]. Orthostatic hypotension is a known complication of diabetic autonomic neuropathy; however, we found no differences in the prevalence of type 2 diabetes or its complications between the study groups (Table 1). This may suggest that the metabolic changes that lead to autonomic neuropathy may be underlain by other, more specific mechanisms, which are only partially associated with the manifestation of type 2 diabetes. The CYP2E1 gene encodes a protein from the cytochrome P450 enzyme superfamily. Cytochrome P450 proteins are monooxygenases that catalyze many reactions involved in drug metabolism and the synthesis of cholesterol, steroids, and other lipids. The protein is localized in the endoplasmic reticulum, and its expression is induced by ethanol, the diabetic state, and starvation [48]. The PRRX1 gene encodes a protein from the family of peroxiredoxins, an antioxidant enzyme responsible for the reduction of nearly 100% of cytoplasmic and up to 90% of mitochondrial H2O2 [49]. These findings suggest that patients with OH exhibit metabolic vulnerability and inadequate compensation for oxidative stress, indicating that the effects of OH may extend beyond hemodynamics and hypoperfusion.

Cell proloferatıon and ınflammatıon

The vascular wall plays an important role in hemodynamics. A recent systematic review [50] showed that arterial stiffness, common in older adults, reduces the buffering effect of the descending aorta and leads to an early return of pressure waves, contributing to OH. We observed indirect signs of intercellular matrix remodeling and inflammation, indicated by differential methylation in collagen-encoding genes COL27A1 and COL5A1 and genes encoding immunoglobulin superfamily proteins IGSF3 and IGSF23, as well as the RAET1E gene, respectively. Our findings are consistent with the current understanding of the nature of arterial stiffness [51]. Most of the functional pathways identified in the transcriptomic analysis were associated with inflammatory processes. Moreover, older adults with OH showed increased expression of genes involved in inflammatory pathways. The RAET1E gene, which we found to be differentially methylated, has been associated with atherosclerosis in mice, and the encoded protein has been linked to the release of inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines [52]. The association between inflammatory markers and OH has been reported in a number of small-cohort studies [14, 15], but their authors did not hypothesize about the role of inflammation in OH. In addition, the observed changes in methylation of genes involved in cytoskeleton regulation, such as PHACTR3, and cell adhesion, such as NTM and GRP123 [53, 54]. This may indicate the adaptation of vascular smooth muscle cells to changes in the intracellular matrix, leading to vascular remodeling and increased vascular stiffness [55].

Neural regulatıon

The CNS plays a key role in the regulation of blood pressure, ensuring the integration of afferent signals and the coordination of effector responses of the autonomic nervous system, mainly the innervation of vascular smooth muscle cells and cardiomyocytes and the regulation of water-salt homeostasis. Therefore, the detection of differentially methylated genes that are associated with the normal functioning of the CNS is to be expected. However, we cannot ascertain that the markers detected in blood reach the CNS. Moreover, there may be a bidirectional effect, whereby the CNS becomes damaged due to hypoperfusion, as demonstrated by the FAB and MMSE test results in the study participants (Table 1). For example, the TENM2 gene encodes teneurin transmembrane protein 2, which is involved in axon guidance and synapse formation, as well as intercellular communication during embryonic development [59]. The CBLN1 gene, which regulates axon growth in various parts of the brain, is also associated with axon guidance [60]. The DEAF1 gene encodes autoregulatory epidermal factor 1, which is abundantly expressed in the CNS during embryonic development [61]. The GFRA1 gene encodes the GFRα1 receptor from the family of glial neurotrophic factors that regulate the survival, migration, and growth of neurons in the spinal cord, the brain, and the peripheral nervous system [62].

The KCTD8 gene encodes a protein promoting the expression of GABAB receptors in axons [56], thereby regulating inhibitory processes in the CNS. Although the ANKK1 gene encodes a serine-threonine protein kinase, it shares the locus with the dopamine receptor gene DRD2 and has been associated with neuropsychiatric disorders characterized by nigrostriatal dopamine depletion [57]. The TH gene, also associated with dopamine secretion, encodes tyrosine hydroxylase (TG), an enzyme responsible for catalyzing the conversion of L-tyrosine into the precursor to dopamine—L-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine (L-DOPA). TG deficiency in the striatum is a hallmark of Parkinson’s disease [58], which is often associated with autonomic dysfunction, including OH. The CAMTA1 gene is also associated with neurodegenerative diseases [59]. The NPBWR1 gene, abundantly expressed by Schwann cells, is associated with neuropathic pain [60]. Neuropathic pain is a common manifestation of polyneuropathy, one of the origins of OH. Orthostatic hypotension is a known complication of diabetic autonomic neuropathy; however, we found no differences in the prevalence of type 2 diabetes or its complications between the study groups (Table 1). This may suggest that the metabolic changes that lead to autonomic neuropathy may be underlain by other, more specific mechanisms, which are only partially associated with the manifestation of type 2 diabetes.

Humoral regulatıon

In hypovolemia, humoral mechanisms—primarily, the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS)—are activated to restore circulating blood volume and support vascular resistance, especially when neurogenic regulation is compromised. Additionally, changes in thyroid hormone levels can impact the cardiac muscle, leading to increased heart rate and myocardial contractility [61], and we observed significantly higher levels of free tyroxine (and lower TSH levels) in OH patients compared to controls. Differential methylation in two genes involved in the normal functioning of the thyroid gland, DUOXA2 and TRHDE [62–64] possibly reflects the changes in thyroid status, which is compensatory for OH potentially through modulation of cardiac pump function. Moreover, the functional analysis revealed the involvement of the secretory pathway, suggesting altered endocrine and paracrine regulation in OH. Moreover, genes involved in exocytosis and endocytosis also showed differentially methylated sites. Notably, differential methylation was detected in the MICAL3 gene, which encodes a cytoplasmic protein associated with increased exocytosis through cytoskeletal reorganization [65], and the KIF13A gene, which encodes a protein that coordinates the positioning of endosomes [66].

Lımıtatıons & unresolved questıons

In addition to the findings that are consistent with current understanding of the causes and effects of OH, we identified changes that are difficult to interpret conclusively. For example, several genes with differentially methylated sites are involved in the regulation of embryonic development of the cardiovascular system, such as ISL1, SLIT3, HAND1, and SOSBRS2, respectively [67–69]. Their roles have been established for the early stages of ontogenesis; therefore, changes in their methylation at an older age are difficult to interpret. We also found differentially methylated sites in a number of cancer-associated genes, such as EVA1B, NAALADL2, SORBS2, GDNF, XRRA1, EGFR-AS1, EPB41L3, TCHH, FAT3, and PDGFC. These findings are supported by the observed increase in the expression of genes involved in the myogenesis pathways, indicating the proliferative activity of some cells and epithelial-mesenchymal transition pathways. Furthermore, a considerable number of identified methylation sites are located within intergenic regions, making it difficult to establish a direct relationship between their methylation and gene expression.

Our study was exploratory in nature and did not aim to demonstrate the mechanistic role of the identified epigenetic markers in the development of orthostatic hypotension. Despite our study did not establish the exact cause-and-effect molecular mechanisms underlying OH, its findings provide a new perspective on this complex condition.

It is important to note that methylation-based model developed during the study cannot be used as a tool for predicting the long-term risk of developing orthostatic hypotension, since DNA methylation reflects the current state of the organism. The ability to construct such a model with relatively high performance metrics indicates that the identified methylation sites associated with orthostatic hypotension form a distinct DNA methylation pattern, which can be described using our machine learning model. Furthermore, the fact that the SVM-based model outperformed logistic regression suggests the presence of nonlinear interactions among the factors serving as predictors in this case. Further clinical application of the developed model for risk stratification or as a supplement to classical diagnostic tools will require additional validation, which will certainly be the focus of our future research.

Conclusion

In this study, we did not identify any statistically significant genetic predictors of orthostatic hypotension, but participants with OH exhibited multiple epigenetic markers that appear to reflect both the pathogenic mechanisms contributing to OH development (including metabolic disturbances and impaired neural regulation) and compensatory adaptations (such as vascular wall remodeling through cellular proliferation, thyroid status modifications, and cardiac function adjustments). Taken together, these alterations formed the basis of a predictive model demonstrating 79% accuracy in OH risk prediction.
