Early Prone Positioning After Acute Type A Aortic Dissection Surgery for Moderate-to-Severe Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome Is Safe and Shortens Duration of Mechanical Ventilation
Authors: Huilong Chen, Chuang Wu, Yunwei Chen, Bin Chen, Junhao Xie, Shuang Zhou, Feng Chen, Juxiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IMPORTANCE:Evidence for prone positioning in post-cardiac surgery acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), especially after acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) repair, is extremely limited. Clinicians remain uncertain about its safety, feasibility, and the optimal timing of initiation in this hemodynamically vulnerable population.OBJECTIVES:To evaluate the effectiveness and safety of prone positioning for moderate-to-severe ARDS (MS-ARDS) after ATAAD repair, and to determine whether early initiation (≤ 48 hr) provides additional clinical benefit.DESIGN, SETTING, AND PARTICIPANTS:A retrospective, single-center cohort study conducted in a tertiary cardiovascular center. Seventy-eight adults with MS-ARDS after ATAAD surgery were included: 58 received prone positioning and 20 remained supine.MAIN OUTCOMES AND MEASURES:Primary outcomes were duration of mechanical ventilation (MV) and postoperative ICU length of stay (LOS). Secondary outcomes included hospital LOS, hospitalization cost, and 28- and 90-day mortality. Adverse events were systematically captured using prespecified hemodynamic and respiratory categories.RESULTS:Preoperative and perioperative characteristics were comparable between groups. In the prone cohort, the Pao2/Fio2 improved from 92.67 ± 21.04 to 152.45 ± 64.28 mm Hg at 4 hours (p < 0.001), demonstrating rapid oxygenation gain. Patients were further stratified by timing of intervention: early prone positioning (EPP: ≤ 48 hr from ARDS onset) and delayed prone positioning (DPP). EPP was associated with a significantly shorter duration of MV 5.03 ± 1.87 days compared with DPP 8.32 ± 4.73 days and the supine group 7.51 ± 4.08 days (p = 0.002). No increase in adverse events was observed, and no episodes of malignant arrhythmia or cardiac arrest occurred.CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE:Prone positioning for MS-ARDS after ATAAD repair was feasible, safe, and rapidly improved oxygenation. Initiation within 48 hours was associated with a clinically meaningful reduction in ventilation duration. These findings support early, protocolized prone positioning in selected postoperative ATAAD patients and justify further evaluation in prospective trials.

KEY POINTS

Question: Is prone positioning safe and beneficial for moderate-to-severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (MS-ARDS) after acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) surgery, and does early initiation improve outcomes?

Findings: Prone positioning was feasible and was not associated with major adverse events when implemented within a standardized ICU protocol. Early initiation (≤ 48 hr after ARDS onset) was associated with improved oxygenation and shorter durations of mechanical ventilation and ICU stay compared with delayed initiation.

Meaning: Early, protocolized prone positioning may be beneficial in patients with MS-ARDS after ATAAD surgery and support further evaluation in prospective randomized trials.

Cardiopulmonary interactions render patients after cardiac surgery vulnerable to pulmonary complications that prolong intensive care and increase costs. Common complications including atelectasis, pleural effusion, pneumonia, phrenic nerve dysfunction, pneumothorax, and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) contribute to hypoxemia and adverse outcomes (1). ARDS is characterized by diffuse alveolar-capillary inflammation with increased permeability and loss of aerated lung tissue in gravity-dependent regions (1, 2). Its incidence appears higher after open repair of acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) than after elective cardiac procedures, and severe ARDS carries substantial risks of multiple organ failure and death (3, 4). Although several adjuncts are used in ARDS including lung-protective ventilation, neuromuscular blockade, prone positioning, inhaled vasodilators, and extracorporeal support, not all are feasible or routinely adopted early in the immediate post-cardiac surgery period (5, 6).

Prone positioning improves oxygenation and outcomes in general ARDS cohorts, yet evidence specific to postoperative cardiac surgery, and particularly ATAAD repair, remains limited and often extrapolated from noncardiac populations (7). At our tertiary cardiovascular center, severe hypoxemia has persisted despite advances in surgical techniques, prompting the implementation of prone positioning in 2020 for selected postoperative patients.

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of adults with moderate-to-severe ARDS (MS-ARDS) following ATAAD repair to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of prone positioning in routine practice and to explore whether earlier initiation (≤ 48 hr from ARDS onset) is associated with improved ventilatory outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Population

This retrospective study was conducted at a tertiary cardiovascular hospital between January 2020, when prone positioning was first introduced for postoperative ATAAD patients and June 2022. All patients received standardized postoperative care, including goal-directed hemodynamic optimization and protocolized intensive therapy. Because prone positioning was implemented selectively for patients with MS-ARDS after surgery, group assignment reflected real world clinical decision making. Patients managed without prone positioning comprised the supine group and receiving standard therapy including conservative fluid management and lung-protective ventilation. Those in the prone group received prone positioning in addition to standard care, according to clinical judgment and safety assessment. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Xiamen Cardiovascular Hospital, Xiamen University approved the study titled as “prone positioning in patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome following acute type A aortic dissection surgery” before the first participant (Approval No. 25, Medical Ethics Department, 2020, IRB No. KY2020-026-05). All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation and in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1875. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for cohort studies.

Patient Enrollment

We screened consecutive adults with ATAAD who underwent Sun’s procedure (total arch replacement with implantation of a stented elephant trunk in the distal aorta). ARDS was diagnosed and graded according to the Berlin definition; patients with MS hypoxemia were included for analysis, operationalized as Pao2/Fio2 (P/F) less than or equal to 150 mm Hg under positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) greater than or equal to 5 cm H2O. Exclusion criteria were: age younger than 18 years; hereditary connective tissue disorders (e.g., Marfan syndrome); untreated or ineffectively treated malperfusion of myocardial, cerebral, or mesenteric territories; continuous lumbar drainage for spinal cord injury; and death within 48 hours after surgery.

ARDS and Hemodynamic Management

Differential diagnoses and corresponding therapeutic strategies were guided by the Berlin definition (7). Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) was employed for ARDS confirmation (8) and lung recruitability assessment (9), characterization of cardiac and extracardiac structures, and evaluation of cardiac functional and hemodynamic guidance as part of a restrictive fluid management (10). The POCUS protocol used for assessing hypoxemia is detailed in Figure-S1 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B637). In cases of cardiac dysfunction, the underlying causes such as pericardial tamponade or coronary ischemia were promptly investigated and managed accordingly. If systolic dysfunction was impaired, dobutamine is the preferred inotrope, whereas norepinephrine should be prioritized when vascular tone is reduced. Volume status was assessed using the passive leg raising (PLR) test, interpreted by ultrasound derived velocity time integral or pulse pressure variation (11). When the PLR response was negative, a negative fluid balance strategy was applied until the test became positive. Subsequently, 300 mL of fluid was infused over 30 minutes to achieve the target volume state. Conversely, when the PLR response was positive, fluids were administered until the response converted to negative, which was defined as the target volume state. Inferior vena cava diameter and its respiratory variability were used as hemodynamic indicators once the target volume state was achieved. Hemodynamic instability prompted immediate reassessment, whereas in clinically stable patients, evaluations were performed bid. Mechanical ventilation (MV) was administered in a volume-controlled mode with a constant inspiratory flow, targeting a tidal volume of 6–8 mL/kg of predicted body weight while maintaining PEEP greater than 5 mm Hg.

Protocol for Prone Positioning

Patients assigned to the prone group were positioned prone for greater than or equal to 16 consecutive hours per 24-hour cycle, typically 16:00–08:00 on successive days. Ventilation targets during prone positioning were an end-inspiratory plateau pressure less than or equal to 30 cm H2O and Pao2 greater than or equal to 80 mm Hg under lung-protective settings. Criteria for discontinuation of prone positioning were predefined as follows: sustained oxygenation improvement, defined as P/F ratio greater than or equal to 150 mm Hg with PEEP greater than or equal to 5 cm H2O, maintained for greater than or equal to 8 hours in the supine position after the most recent prone session; or safety triggers, including cardiac arrest, artificial airway obstruction, clinically significant hemodynamic instability, sustained malignant arrhythmias, or the treating clinician’s judgment to terminate therapy.

Measurements and blood gas analysis were performed at four points: PPpre was recorded right before the initiation of prone positioning. PP4h and PP12h were recorded at 4 and 12 hours after the initiation of prone positioning. PPpost was recorded at 2 hours after returning to the supine position. Additional arterial blood gases and monitoring were obtained at clinician discretion. In the supine group, measurements were collected at time points aligned with the prone protocol. Sedative and analgesic drugs were administered routinely. Sedation depth was assessed every 6 hours, targeting a Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) –1 to –3 while supine and RASS –3 to –5 during prone sessions. Neuromuscular blocking agents were reserved for persistent ventilator dyssynchrony despite optimization of sedation and ventilator settings (12).

Outcome Measures

The main outcomes were duration of MV and postoperative ICU length of stay (LOS). Readiness for weaning was assessed under the following criteria: Spo2 greater than 90% and/or Pao2 greater than 60 mm Hg, respiratory rate less than 35 breaths/min, pressure support less than or equal to 10 cm H2O, PEEP less than or equal to 5 cm H2O, Fio2 less than or equal to 0.50, and tidal volume greater than 4 mL/kg of predicted body weight. Extubation was attempted when these criteria were sustained. MV duration was defined by the time of final successful liberation from invasive ventilation. Postoperative ICU LOS was calculated from the end of surgery to final discharge from the ICU. The secondary endpoints included hospital LOS, total hospitalization cost, and mortality at 28 and 90 days.

Adverse outcomes were prespecified and classified into two categories. One was prone positioning associated events, including arrhythmia, pressure ulcer, endotracheal tube obstruction, unplanned extubation, accidental tube or catheter disconnection, oxygen desaturation (pulse oximetry < 85% sustained > 5 min), significant heart rate decrease (heart rate < 30 beats/min for > 1 min), and hypotension (mean blood pressure < 60 mm Hg sustained > 5 min). The other was the complications that cannot be clearly attributed to prone positioning, including poor wound healing, acute kidney injury requiring continuous renal replacement therapy, delirium, tracheotomy requirement, and all-cause mortality.

Statistical Analysis

The preoperative and perioperative data, ventilator settings, arterial blood gas value, organ function, and outcome related data were recorded. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and were compared using the chi-square test or adjusted chi-square test. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± sd or median with interquartile ranges, depending on distribution. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. For nonnormally distributed variables, the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis H test was applied.

For oxygenation analyses, P/F ratio values at predefined time points were compared across groups, and 95% CIs were estimated using t-distribution-based methods when applicable. The prone cohort was further stratified according to the timing of prone positioning initiation into an early prone positioning (EPP) subgroup (≤ 48 hr from ARDS onset) and a delayed prone positioning (DPP) subgroup (> 48 hr). Oxygenation trajectories and clinical outcomes were compared among the supine, EPP, and DPP groups.

There were no missing values in the baseline covariates, exposure, or outcomes used in this study. Consequently, all analyses were conducted on a complete dataset, and no imputation or sensitivity analyses for missing data were necessary. All statistical analyses were performed using R (Version 4.0.3; R Core Team, 2020; Vienna, Austria) and SPSS (Version 25.0; IBM, Armonk, NY). A two-tailed p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

During the study period, 407 patients with ATAAD underwent open surgery and were admitted to the ICU, of whom 371 developed hypoxemias. After excluding 137 patients (due to continuous lumbar drainage, ineffectively treated malperfusion syndrome, cardiogenic/non-ARDS pulmonary edema, or other prespecified conditions), 234 were screened for eligibility. Of these, 82 patients met the predefined diagnostic criteria for MS-ARDS (P/F ≤ 150 mm Hg with PEEP ≥ 5 cm H2O). Four were subsequently excluded because of severe acute pancreatitis, acute myocardial infarction, or pulmonary embolism, leaving 78 patients included in the final cohort. Of these, 20 patients were managed in the supine group and 58 received prone positioning (Fig. 1).

Preoperative and Perioperative Characteristics of Participants

Preoperative characteristics were broadly comparable between two groups. Although overweight and hypertension were common among the enrolled patients, only one patient had type 2 diabetes and none had pulmonary disease, while approximately 75% had a history of smoking. The mean P/F ratio was around 200 mm Hg, indicating substantial preexisting impairment in oxygenation. Nearly half of the cohort presented with elevated lactate levels. Serum creatinine was mildly increased, although left ventricular ejection fraction remained largely preserved before surgery.

Perioperative indices were also similar between groups. Surgical procedures for ATAAD were prolonged, with a mean operative duration of approximately 9 hours. Cardiopulmonary bypass time exceeded 4 hours, and the aortic cross clamp time surpassed 3 hours. Significant intraoperative bleeding occurred, with a mean blood loss of 1600 mL, necessitating routine platelet transfusion. Allogeneic transfusion requirements were minimized through the use of autologous blood salvage. Detailed preoperative and perioperative characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Ventilator Settings, Organ Function, and Adjunctive Therapies at Enrollment

Following classification as MS-ARDS, patients underwent ventilator optimization and POCUS-guided hemodynamic management. The delivered tidal volume was slightly higher than the set value, while plateau pressure remained less than 30 cm H2O, albeit modestly higher in the prone cohort. Disease severity and the vasoactive-inotropic score on postoperative day 1 were comparable between groups. Thoracentesis with pleural drainage was performed in seven of 20 supine patients (35%) and 24 of 58 prone patients (41.4%) 1–5 days after enrollment. Postoperative blood transfusion was required in greater than 90% of the cohort, and approximately 50% of the prone group received neuromuscular blocking agents. No patients required extracorporeal membrane oxygenation or inhaled nitric oxide. Detailed data are provided in Table S1 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B637).

Hemodynamics and Fluid Balance

Average fluid intake was limited to approximately 3000 mL per day, resulting in a negative fluid balance, particularly within the first four postoperative days. Although no significant differences were observed between the supine, EPP, and DPP groups, additional negative fluid balance was pursued in the supine group to reduce lung water and improve oxygenation. However, by postoperative day 5, maintaining a negative balance became difficult in the supine group, whereas the prone groups consistently remained in negative fluid balance. Detailed data are provided in Table S2 and Figure S-2 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B637).

Oxygenation Function and the Effect of Prone Positioning

The lowest P/F ratio recorded across the cohort was 83.79 ± 20.81 mm Hg. In the prone cohort, a total of 135 prone sessions were delivered, each lasting 16 hours per protocol. During the first prone session, oxygenation improved from PPpre 92.67 ± 21.04 mm Hg to PP4h 152.45 ± 64.28 mm Hg, and further to PP12h 178.86 ± 75.26 mm Hg. Thirty-nine patients required additional prone sessions beyond the first. A characteristic pattern was observed: the P/F ratio increased during prone positioning and fell after returning supine, while demonstrating an overall upward trajectory over time. The within-patient P/F fluctuations across sessions are illustrated in Figure S-3 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B637).

Primary and Secondary Outcomes

The duration of MV was numerically shorter in the prone cohort than in the supine cohort (6.39 ± 3.70 vs. 7.51 ± 4.08 d; p = 0.258). ICU LOS favored the prone cohort (11.22 ± 6.24 vs. 16.50 ± 13.61 d; p = 0.022). Both hospital LOS and hospitalization cost were also lower in prone group. Two patients in the supine group died due to intracerebral hemorrhage and massive cerebral infarction. Overall mortality was low at 2.56%, with no significant difference between groups.

Adverse Events

The pressure ulcer rate in the prone group was 8.63%. Wound-healing complications occurred in four of 78 patients (5.13%), no cases of sternal wound infection or sternal dehiscence were observed. No episodes of arrhythmia, endotracheal tube obstruction, unplanned extubation, or accidental tube/catheter dislodgement were observed in either group, and no clinically significant respiratory or hemodynamic instability occurred during positioning. Tracheostomy was performed in three patients. Detailed outcomes are presented in Table 2.

Subgroup Analysis

The prone cohort initiated their first prone positioning session at a mean of 50.16 ± 28.91 hours after enrollment. Thirty-four patients were stratified into the EPP group and 24 into the DPP group. Oxygenation improved more rapidly and to a greater extent in the EPP group, particularly by postoperative day 3, during which a larger proportion of EPP patients achieved successful ventilator weaning within the first postoperative week (Fig. 2). The duration of MV differed significantly among the three groups: EPP 5.03 ± 1.87 days, compared with DPP 8.32 ± 4.73 days and supine group 7.51 ± 4.08 days (p = 0.002). Similarly, the ICU and hospital LOS were substantially shorter in the EPP than in both the DPP and supine groups (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

This retrospective cohort study demonstrated that, among patients with MS-ARDS following open repair surgery for ATAAD, prone positioning was associated with rapid improvements in P/F and shorter durations of MV and ICU stay, particularly when initiated within 48 hours of ARDS onset. No severe adverse events related to prone positioning were observed, and no significant fluctuations in respiratory or circulatory parameters were observed. These findings are directionally consistent with established adult ARDS practice, in which prone positioning is typically implemented for severe hypoxemia, for example, P/F less than 150 mm Hg as part of a lung-protective ventilation strategy.

ARDS is a well-recognized postoperative complication after cardiac surgery and has been associated with substantial mortality in selected cohorts (13). In the context of ATAAD, prolonged operative duration, extended cardiopulmonary bypass and cross clamp time, and exposure to allogeneic blood products promotes endothelial and epithelial injury, creating a cumulative multihit environment that accelerates the progression to ARDS (14). Aortic dissection may also involve bronchial arteries, compromising pulmonary nutritional blood flow and contributing to additional lung ischemia (15).

For severe ARDS, prone positioning is a cornerstone of adult management and improves outcomes when applied early and for prolonged sessions (5). Notably, postoperative cardiac surgery populations, particularly patients after ATAAD repair have not been included in randomized trials (5, 16). Our study provides cohort level evidence in this specific setting and helps fill an important knowledge gap in postoperative ATAAD-related MS-ARDS, demonstrating that prone positioning is safe, with greater benefits observed when initiated early rather than delayed. This timing effect likely reflects key advantages of EPP mitigating severe hypoxemia and reducing ventilator-induced lung injury, particularly in patients requiring higher Fio2 levels (17). By contrast, the combination of prolonged hypoxemia and more intensive ventilatory support may explain the lack of significant benefit observed with delayed prone therapy (17).

Prone positioning in postoperative ATAAD patients presents a unique and delicate risk-benefit balance, particularly when applied early. The Sun’s procedure for ATAAD involves median sternotomy, extended cardiopulmonary bypass and cross-clamp time, hypothermic circulatory arrest with selective cerebral perfusion, total aortic arch replacement, and deployment of a stented elephant trunk into the descending aorta (18, 19). Following such extensive surgery, patients face unique vulnerabilities, including hemodynamic fragility, anterior chest wall instability after sternotomy, the potential risk of graft compression, accidental displacement of airway devices, and concerns regarding the security of central lines and mediastinal/chest drains. These factors collectively make EPP potentially more hazardous despite its respiratory benefits. Therefore, prone positioning must be individualized, performed by an experienced team, and supported by meticulous hemodynamic and device monitoring.

Patients treated at our center frequently develop postoperative fluid accumulation, as ATAAD surgery is performed emergently and requires strict preoperative control of blood pressure and cardiac workload together with prolonged and complex operative procedures. Although all patients postoperative patients in our cohort received a restrictive fluid management strategy due to MS-hypoxemia, differences emerged between groups. Patients in the prone positioning group were generally able to achieve and maintain a negative fluid balance throughout the first postoperative week. In contrast, patients in the supine cohort required a more aggressive negative fluid balance during the first several postoperative days; however, by postoperative days 5–7, achieving and sustaining a negative balance became increasingly difficult. Given concerns about hemodynamic fluctuations after cardiac surgery, our center used a slightly lower PEEP than that reported by Guérin et al (5).

Notably, in the present study, complication rates associated with prone positioning remained low. Two factors likely contributed to the favorable outcomes, the establishment of specialized aortic teams at our institution since 2018 (18), and the implementation of perioperative, POCUS-guided, goal-directed therapy. As the largest regional aortic center in southwestern China, our staff undergo standardized training, and we perform approximately 200 ATAAD repairs (Sun’s procedure) annually, consistent with a high volume center (20). An optimized strategy for ATAAD with malperfusion syndrome has yielded an in-hospital mortality near 4% (18). We introduced prone positioning for post-cardiac surgery ARDS in January 2020 and established a dedicated ICU area with team training and simulations to ensure safety and effectiveness (Video 1). Lung ultrasound supported diagnosis and guided therapy (e.g., identifying recruitable regions) (21, 22), and a restrictive fluid strategy helped maintain cardiopulmonary stability during prone positioning (23), thereby guiding the safe implementation of the maneuver. Because we excluded deaths within 48 hours postoperatively and patients with untreated malperfusion syndrome, the enrolled cohort had low mortality (24, 25), which differs from some prior reports and explains our focus on MV duration and ICU LOS rather than mortality as primary endpoints.

Taken together, this retrospective investigation possesses several notable strengths. First, it focuses on ARDS following ATAAD repair, an underrepresented subgroup in prior randomized evidence, and evaluates prone positioning in the immediate postoperative setting (26). Second, prone positioning was implemented within a protocolized ICU workflow supporting internal consistency, feasibility, and safety after major cardiothoracic surgery. Third, prespecified stratification by early vs. delayed initiation provides clinically actionable insight into timing effects. Above all, when prone positioning can be safely performed, it may reduce the overall need for venovenous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation in this population. However, patients who are hemodynamically unstable, have malperfusion syndrome, or have other contraindications to prone positioning are unlikely to tolerate the maneuver; in such cases, extracorporeal life support remains the preferred escalation strategy.

Several limitations merit consideration. This was a single-center observational study with clinician determined treatment allocation; thus, causal inference is limited and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Selection and survivorship bias may have been introduced by exclusion of early postoperative deaths and patients with untreated malperfusion, limiting mortality generalizability and justifying MV duration and ICU LOS as primary outcomes. In addition, detailed physiologic measures (e.g., compliance, driving pressure, ultrasound-based recruitability) were incompletely available and constrained mechanistic interpretation. The higher use of neuromuscular blockade in the prone positioning group may act as a confounder and should be considered when interpreting prone related outcomes. Finally, as the study was conducted in a high-volume aortic center with specialized teams, generalizability to other settings may be limited. To address these limitations, a prospective randomized controlled trial has been initiated (ChiCTR2500098016).

CONCLUSIONS

In this retrospective cohort, prone positioning was feasible, safe, and associated with meaningful clinical benefit in patients who developed MS-ARDS after ATAAD surgery when applied within a standardized, protocol-driven ICU framework. Early initiation, particularly within 48 hours of ARDS onset, was associated with improved oxygenation and shorter durations of MV and ICU stay. These findings support the consideration of early, protocolized prone positioning as part of postoperative lung-protective management in selected ATAAD patients and warrant confirmation in prospective randomized trials.
