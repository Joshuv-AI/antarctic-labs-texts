Distinct central and peripheral airway features in bronchiolitis obliterans and COPD: an observational study
Authors: Mariko Kogo, Naoya Tanabe, Hisako Matsumoto, Tomoki Maetani, Yi Zhang, Shinya Tsukamoto, Toyofumi Fengshi Chen-Yoshikawa, Akihiko Yoshizawa, Akihiro Ohsumi, Daisuke Nakajima, Hiroshi Date, Toyohiro Hirai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBronchiolitis obliterans (BO) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are both characterized by small airway obstruction. However, their pathogenesis and clinical presentations differ, and the underlying mechanisms of BO remain unclear. This study investigated disease-specific alterations in the small (peripheral) and large (central) airways in BO compared with COPD.MethodsThis retrospective single-center study included patients with BO and COPD who underwent lung transplantation and patients with normal spirometry who underwent lobectomy for lung cancer (controls). Central airway tree morphology and peripheral airway pathology, including epithelial composition, were assessed using computed tomography (CT) and histology of resected lung tissue, respectively.ResultsPatients with BO, COPD, and controls (n = 43, 13, and 17, respectively) exhibited median ages of 24, 52, and 59 years and median % predicted forced expiratory volume in 1 s of 22%, 17%, and 103%, respectively. The airway tree-to-lung volume ratio on CT and epithelial erosion in peripheral airways were greater in patients with BO than in those with COPD and controls, whereas FOXJ1-positive cells, indicating ciliated cells, were fewer. The number of p40-positive cells was greater in COPD than in the other groups. PAS score, representing mucus-producing cells, was lower in the BO group than in the COPD group.ConclusionsCentral airway volume and peripheral airway epithelial erosion were greater, with fewer FOXJ1-positive and PAS-positive cells in BO than COPD, despite similar lung function. Central airway dilation and peripheral airway obstruction associated with epithelial erosion and ciliated cell reduction may be key pathological features of BO.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04297-w.

Background

Bronchiolitis obliterans (BO) is characterized by irreversible airflow obstruction that occurs following injury to the small airways from various causes [1–4]. BO has emerged as a major complication after hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation (HSCT) and lung transplantation, being a leading cause of death beyond the first-year post-transplantation [2, 3]. Patients with BO experience a severe trajectory; however, due to its unclear pathogenesis, there is no confirmed treatment or prevention.

Recent studies have highlighted the crucial role of airway epithelial cells in airway diseases [5]. The airway epithelium mainly consists of ciliated, basal, and secretory cells. The depletion and dysfunction of these cells following epithelial injury are pivotal in hindering epithelial repair, thereby contributing to the progression of BO [6, 7]. However, these insights are largely derived from experimental studies, and the manifestations in patients with BO remain poorly understood. Furthermore, it remains unclear how the nature and clinical presentation of BO differ from those of other airway diseases, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), which is also characterized by small airway obstruction. A better understanding of the underlying mechanisms of these differences is warranted for more disease-specific management.

With advancements in computed tomography (CT) image analysis software, morphological features of the central airway tree can now be assessed. CT imaging enables the quantification of total airway count (TAC), airway dimensional changes, airway fractal dimension (AFD), and the presence of mucus plugs in patients with chronic airway diseases, including COPD [8–12]. Although previous studies have suggested morphological changes in the central airways of patients with BO [13–15], the detailed characteristics remain largely unexplored. Our previous study showed an association between central airway dilation and BO progression [14]. Building on these findings, we hypothesized that central airway features may reflect pathological changes in the small (peripheral) airways, including airway epithelial abnormalities in BO, and that these features differ from those in COPD. This study aimed to characterize central and peripheral airway lesions, with particular focus on epithelial changes, in patients with BO compared to those with COPD.

Methods

Study design and subjects

This retrospective study included patients with BO and COPD who underwent lung transplantation at Kyoto University Hospital between 2009 and 2021. BO was pathologically diagnosed using surgical specimens at the time of lung transplantation. BO was defined by the presence of one or more bronchioles exhibiting concentric luminal narrowing due to subepithelial inflammation and fibrosis on histopathological examination [1]. Clinical, physiological, and radiological data of patients with BO and COPD at the time of lung transplantation registration were retrieved from clinical records. Additionally, patients with normal pulmonary function who underwent lung lobectomy for lung cancer at Kyoto University Hospital were included as controls.

CT analysis

CT images with slice thicknesses of 0.5 and 1 mm were acquired at the time of registration using an Aquilion 64 scanner (Toshiba, Tokyo, Japan). The scanning conditions included 0.5-mm collimation, 500-millisecond scan time, 120 peak kilovoltage, and auto-exposure control. Reconstruction was performed using a high-spatial-frequency algorithm (FC51). CT scans were acquired at full inspiration, with patients instructed to inhale deeply and hold their breath under the supervision of a radiologic technologist. For the CT analysis, we excluded patients with pneumothorax at registration or a history of lung resection or transplantation. Two pulmonologists independently scored airway mucus plugs based on bronchopulmonary segment anatomy, and the scores were averaged [16, 17]. Additionally, CT scans of patients aged ≥ 18 years were analyzed for airway tree morphologies, including TAC, AFD, and the ratio of airway to lung volume, using the SYNAPSE VINCENT ver 5.3 (FUJIFILM Medical, Tokyo, Japan) [14, 18].

Pathological analysis for the assessment of peripheral airways

For histological assessment of the lung tissues collected during lung transplantation, the lungs were inflated by instilling formalin through the major bronchus until full inflation was visually confirmed, although the precise inflation pressure was not documented. Following formalin fixation, the tissues were processed into paraffin blocks, and sliced into 4-µm thick sections following standard surgical pathology protocols [19]. The diagnosis of BO was pathologically confirmed using hematoxylin and eosin and elastica van Gieson staining. Digital images of small airways were captured in two sections from different lobes using the BZ-X800 E3 (Keyence, Osaka, Japan) and analyzed using ImageJ software (Media Cybernetics). Relatively circular bronchioles (long axis/short axis < 3) smaller than 2 mm in the long axis without complete obstruction were evaluated [20]. The following parameters were evaluated and averaged for each patient: (1) luminal occlusion, measured as the percentage of luminal content relative to the maximal luminal area [20]; (2) epithelial erosion, calculated as the percentage of epithelial detachment relative to the airway circumference; (3) alveolar attachments, determined by the number of alveolar attachments to the outer wall of bronchioles, adjusted by the perimeter of the basement membrane, which is defined as an elastic layer of the bronchiole; and (4) cell compositions of the epithelium, including: (i) ciliated cells (FOXJ1-positive cells [invitrogen, 14-9965082]), (ii) basal cells (p40 positive cells [Roche 518-112981]), and (iii) mucosecretory cells (Periodic acid-Schiff stain [PAS] positive cells). The proportions of FOXJ1 and p40 positive cells were expressed as the cell count of positive cells adjusted by the length of the remaining epithelium. The proportion of PAS-positive cells was scored by two pulmonologists and averaged as follows: 0 (no staining), 1 (less than half the length of the epithelium), 2 (more than half the length of the epithelium), and 3 (multilayered) (Figure S1 [see Additional file 1]). For the control patients, one section of a resected lung without cancer was assessed. Pathological assessment was performed blinded to clinical and radiological information.

Statistical analysis

Data were compared among patients with BO, COPD, and control subjects. Continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s test for multiple comparison. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Interobserver agreements of mucus plug and PAS scores were evaluated using weighted Cohen’s kappa, and reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient. Correlations between central airway variables on CT and peripheral airway variables on pathology were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients. A two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP 17 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Data are presented as median values with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables and as percentages for categorical variables.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

A total of 43 patients with BO, 13 with COPD, and 17 controls were included in this study. Figure 1 illustrates representative CT images of central airway morphology and mucus plugs, along with corresponding peripheral airway pathology in BO and COPD. The clinical characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. The median ages were 24, 52, and 59 years in the BO, COPD, and control groups, respectively. The underlying etiology of BO included post-lung transplantation complications in four cases and post-HSCT complications in the remaining cases. Among patients with available pulmonary function data at the time of lung transplantation, % of predicted FEV1 (%FEV1) was comparable between the BO and COPD groups.

Analysis on central airways on CT

CT analysis was performed in 31 patients with BO, 9 with COPD, and 17 controls (Fig. 2). Interobserver agreement for the mucus plug score was weighted κ = 0.63 and ICC = 0.47. The mucus plug score was significantly higher in patients with BO compared to the control group, while it was similar to that observed in patients with COPD (Fig. 3). In the analysis of the airway tree, 13 patients with BO who were under the age of 18 were excluded. The remaining study cohort comprised 18 patients with BO, 9 with COPD, and 17 controls. Among 18 patients with BO included in airway tree analysis, age (median [IQR] 30 [24–45] years, p < 0.01), height (164 [158–170] cm, p < 0.01), and weight (43 [38–51] kg, p < 0.01) were significantly greater than in excluded patients, while pulmonary function was comparable (%FVC: 53 [43–67]%, p = 0.06; %FEV1: 22 [19–25]%, p = 0.26). All were never-smokers. TAC was significantly lower in patients with COPD compared to the other groups. The airway-to-lung volume ratio was highest in patients with BO and lowest in those with COPD (Fig. 3).

Analysis of peripheral airways on pathology

Among the 73 patients, the number of bronchioles examined per section and their mean basement membrane length were consistent across the BO, COPD, and control groups (Table 1). As illustrated in Fig. 4, the percentage of luminal occlusion was significantly higher in patients with BO compared to controls, although the difference between the BO and COPD groups was not significant. In patients with BO, epithelial erosion was observed regardless of the presence of BO lesions in the peripheral airways. The percentage of epithelial erosion was significantly higher in patients with BO than in those with COPD or controls. Conversely, the number of alveolar attachments per basement membrane length was significantly lower in patients with COPD than in those with BO or controls. Regarding epithelial cell composition, all three cell types tended to decrease in the remaining epithelium of patients with BO compared to controls. The number of FOXJ1-positive cells (ciliated cells) was significantly lower in patients with BO than in those with COPD or controls (Fig. 5). In contrast, the number of p40-positive cells (basal cells) was significantly higher in patients with COPD than in those with BO or controls. The PAS score, representing mucus-producing cells, was lower in patients with BO than in those with COPD. Interobserver agreement of PAS score was weighted κ = 0.93 and ICC = 0.93. There was no significant association between CT variables and pathological findings in patients with BO, except for a positive correlation between TAC and alveolar attachments (rho = 0.53, p = 0.02).

Discussion

Our study revealed that patients with BO and COPD exhibited higher mucus plug scores in the central airways on CT compared to controls. Notably, unlike patients with COPD, those with BO demonstrated an increased airway-to-lung volume ratio. Pathological examination of the peripheral airways in patients with BO revealed epithelial erosion and a reduction in FOXJ1-positive ciliated cells, which contrasted with the increased number of p40-positive basal cells observed in patients with COPD. This study underscores disease-specific alterations in both the central and peripheral airways of patients with BO.

Both COPD and BO are characterized by small airway dysfunction and central airway abnormalities [8–13, 15]. CT findings of airway morphology in COPD include loss of proximal airways (reduced TAC) [8, 21], decreased airway-to-lung volume ratio [10], and diminished AFD [22]. These changes may reflect underlying small airway dysfunction, as demonstrated in Kirby’s analysis of airway tree morphology [23] and Tamura’s report on mucus scores [24]. While CT-based airway morphological analyses have gained attention, morphological changes in BO remain poorly understood. Therefore, our findings are clinically important, as they demonstrate greater mucus plugs and an elevated airway-to-lung volume ratio in patients with BO, distinguishing this condition from COPD.

Epithelial erosion was more severe in patients with BO than in those with COPD and controls. Particularly, the reduction of FOXJ1-positive cells in the remaining epithelium of peripheral airways was significant in BO. Previous studies have shown that subepithelial fibrosis, concentric narrowing, and obliteration of bronchioles are the major pathological features of BO [1, 4], and that airway epithelial damage may be both the initial trigger and perpetuating factor in disease progression [25, 26]. Moreover, impaired epithelial repair may be induced by loss of basal cells or secretory club cells [6, 27] as well as dysfunction and depletion of ciliated cells [7, 28]. Our findings substantially extend those observations through direct evaluation of peripheral airway pathology in resected lung tissue from patients with BO as a complication of HSCT or lung transplantation.

The observed higher number of P40-positive basal cells in patients with COPD than in those with BO is consistent with previous findings [29] and suggests that the differences in epithelial composition between COPD and BO may be associated with the contrasting CT findings of increased and decreased airway volumes in patients with BO and COPD, respectively. While central airway dimensions decrease as airway remodeling progresses in COPD [30], the mechanism underlying central airway enlargement in BO remains unclear. Our previous study demonstrated a positive association between the prevalence of BO lesions on pathology and airway dilation on CT [14]. Together with the observed reduction in FOXJ1-positive ciliated cells in this study, these findings suggest that ciliated cell reduction may be associated with central airway enlargement in BO. Damaged ciliated cells are associated with increased Th17-mediated inflammation and subsequent neutrophil influx [31–33], which may promote airway destruction and disease progression in BO [34–36]. Furthermore, the observed lack of significant correlation between central airway CT findings and peripheral airway pathology in patients with BO may reflect our exclusive inclusion of severe cases requiring lung transplantation.

The PAS score was significantly higher in patients with COPD than in those with BO. This is consistent with a previous finding that increased mucinous cells are a major pathological feature of COPD [5]. The lower PAS scores in BO, together with reduced FOXJ1-positive cells on pathology, despite comparable mucus scores on CT between BO and COPD, suggest that impaired mucociliary clearance due to ciliated cell reduction, rather than increased number of mucus-producing cells, may be more closely associated with mucus plug formation in the central airways visible on CT in patients with BO. Enlarged central airway observed in the patients with BO might additionally be associated with different distribution of mucus plug from those in patients with COPD who have reduced central airway tree visibility due to luminal narrowing.

The reason for the positive correlation between TAC and the number of alveolar attachments in patients with BO observed in our study remains unclear. Given that both the number of alveolar attachments and TAC were comparable to those in the control group, factors other than disease progression itself, such as a history of recurrent respiratory infection, may contribute to this association [37]. Further research is needed to elucidate the clinical significance of the relationship between TAC and alveolar attachments in patients with BO.

Given the impact of lung volume on airway dimensions, this study assessed airway volume while adjusting for lung volume. Nevertheless, the airway-to-lung volume ratio can be affected by hyperinflation and emphysema, particularly in COPD. Despite this consideration, the increased airway-to-lung volume ratio observed in BO compared to controls indicates the presence of airway enlargement in BO. Furthermore, patients with BO and COPD exhibited similar pulmonary function in our study. Consequently, the variation in the airway-to-lung volume ratio may more accurately reflect the distinct pathophysiological differences between BO and COPD.

Considering the heterogeneous distribution of BO and COPD lesions within the lung, our study assessed samples from two sites in patients with BO and COPD. Conversely, for control patients, only one section without tumor involvement from resected lung tissue was available. Nonetheless, normal lungs are generally regarded as histologically uniform, and similar findings are anticipated irrespective of the region examined. Furthermore, despite the variation in the number of sections evaluated, the number of bronchioles analyzed was comparable across the groups. Therefore, the impact of evaluating a different number of sections should be minimal, although potential sampling bias may still exist.

We excluded bronchioles with complete obstruction from the pathological analysis because epithelial structures could not be identified, which might have caused underestimation of the true extent of bronchiolar occlusion. Because our previous study clarified the association between pathological BO lesions, including complete occlusion, and CT airway dilation [14], this study aimed to characterize airway epithelial composition rather than to quantify the prevalence of bronchiolar obstruction. Consequently, our data revealed distinct epithelial structural characteristics in the remaining bronchioles in BO.

We acknowledge that the relatively young age of patients with BO could be a potential confounder in our results. Since ciliary function in ciliated cells may decline with age [38], the number of FOXJ1-positive cells may also decrease with age. However, our finding that patients with BO who had the youngest population showed the lowest number of FOXJ1-positive cells suggests that age differences are unlikely to alter our interpretation that a reduced number of FOXJ1-positive cells represents a feature of BO.

This study has several limitations. First, to compare radiological and pathological differences between BO and COPD in patients with similar pulmonary function, we included patients who underwent lung transplantation. Because the study population consisted of end-stage patients, potential selection bias should be considered. This limits our findings’ generalizability to milder stages of the diseases. Additionally, given the retrospective nature of our study, several confounders, including unknown differences in lung inflation at the time of pathological sample collection, as well as residual selection bias could have influenced our results. Moreover, clinical factors such as age, smoking history, and transplant-related variables may influence airway morphology and epithelial phenotype. However, despite these limitations, our study provides important novel insight into the unique structural changes in both large and small airways in BO. Secondly, the small sample size from a single center limits our ability to fully interpret the results, as it may have reduced the statistical power to detect differences between groups, especially when comparing BO with COPD. Nonetheless, given our findings for COPD are consistent with previous reports [8, 10, 21, 22, 29], the inclusion of additional COPD cases would likely further delineate the differences from BO rather than alter the observed trends. Therefore, the impact on our overall conclusions is minimal.

Conclusions

Despite similar %FEV1, patients with BO demonstrated a larger central airway tree volume, greater epithelial erosion, and fewer FOXJ1-positive ciliated cells and PAS-positive mucus-producing cells in the peripheral airways compared to those with COPD. These findings suggest that central airway dilation coupled with peripheral airway obstruction, which are associated with epithelial erosion and reductions in ciliated and mucus-producing cells, may be key pathological features of BO. Our observations provide new insights into the BO-specific structural and epithelial features, potentially enhancing our understanding of its pathogenesis and addressing the urgent clinical need for future effective BO therapeutic strategy.
