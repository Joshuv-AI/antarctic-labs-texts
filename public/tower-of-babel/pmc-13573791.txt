Jingtong Granules for Cervicogenic Headache: Clinical Efficacy, Mechanisms, and Translational Potential
Authors: Wei Wei Liu, Ming Xin, Hongqi Ge, Qingwen Jia, Xinyong Liu, Yalin Wang, Shengda Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeadache is a highly prevalent neurological symptom worldwide. Among headache subtypes, cervicogenic headache (CEH) has attracted growing research attention owing to its rising incidence, younger‑onset trend, and substantial adverse impacts on patients’ quality of life. However, conventional pharmacological therapies for CEH may be associated with notable adverse effects. Jingtong Granule, a Chinese patent medicine with the functions of promoting blood circulation to remove blood stasis and regulating qi to relieve pain, is widely applied for cervical spondylotic radiculopathy in clinical practice. In recent years, its potential therapeutic value for various headache disorders, particularly CEH, has drawn increasing interest, yet a comprehensive systematic review on this topic remains absent. Based on recent domestic and international studies, this review systematically summarizes the pathogenesis of CEH (especially the relationship between CEH and cervical lesions), the formula characteristics and pharmacological effects of Jingtong Granule, its clinical applications (used either alone or combined with other interventions) for headache management, and its safety profile. Current limitations of available evidence and directions for future research are also highlighted. Notably, existing clinical and mechanistic evidence regarding Jingtong Granule for CEH remains preliminary, and high‑quality, large‑scale clinical trials are still required to support its standardized clinical application. This review aims to provide preliminary references for future clinical use and related investigations of Jingtong Granule in headache treatment.Keywords: Jingtong granule, cervicogenic headache, traditional Chinese medicine, analgesic effect, neuroinflammation

Introduction

Cervicogenic headache (CEH) is defined as a secondary headache originating from cervical spine pathology, characterized by unilateral pain that may radiate from the neck to the head and imposes a heavy global clinical burden. It is a common condition, yet often underdiagnosed, with a prevalence of up to 18.9% in some populations and an incidence of 2.2% in the healthy population.1 CEH is associated with significant disability, seriously impairing patients’ physical and mental health as well as their quality of life. Epidemiological studies indicate that CEH is more prevalent in individuals with cervical spine disorders, such as cervical spondylosis and herniated discs, and it is frequently accompanied by neck pain and other musculoskeletal complaints.2 Despite its high incidence and substantial burden, unmet clinical needs for CEH treatment persist, highlighting an urgent demand for safe and effective therapeutic strategies.

At present, conventional pharmacological medications are the main clinical treatment for CEH, among which non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and muscle relaxants are the most commonly used.3 However, these conventional pharmacological medications have obvious limitations: they are prone to adverse reactions such as gastrointestinal discomfort, and long-term use may lead to drug dependence.4 More importantly, they only relieve symptoms temporarily without addressing the root cause of the disease, resulting in high recurrence rates and failure to meet long-term clinical treatment needs. In contrast, Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) has unique advantages for treating chronic diseases, including multi‑target regulation, good long‑term safety, and a focus on addressing underlying causes.5

Jingtong Granule (JTKL) is a Chinese patent medicine included in the National Medical Insurance Category B and the Pharmacopoeia of the People’s Republic of China.6 Its main components include Panax notoginseng, Ligusticum chuanxiong, Corydalis yanhusuo, Pueraria lobata, Paeonia lactiflora, Clematis chinensis, and Notopterygium incisum, with the effects of promoting blood circulation to remove blood stasis and regulating qi to relieve pain. Clinically, JTKL has been widely used in the treatment of cervical spondylotic radiculopathy,7 and its application value in CEH has gradually gained attention in recent years. However, the specific therapeutic efficacy of JTKL in CEH treatment, its potential pharmacological mechanisms, and its translational value in clinical practice remain unclear, which has become an important scientific question to be solved.

Although several published reviews have summarized TCMs interventions for CEH, most of these articles broadly covered multiple herbal formulas and acupuncture therapies. To date, few targeted reviews have specially focused on JTKL. Moreover, existing publications have mainly discussed its clinical effectiveness, without a comprehensive integration of its pharmacological mechanisms, action pathways, safety evidence, and future translational prospects. Accordingly, this review was conducted to fill this literature gap, by systematically synthesizing both clinical and pre‑clinical evidence for JTKL in CEH treatment and pointing out remaining challenges for future high‑quality research.

Therefore, this review systematically combs through the relevant domestic and international studies over the past decade, aiming to clarify the clinical value and mechanism of JTKL in CEH treatment, and provide a reference for its standardized application and subsequent in-depth research.

Composition and Pharmacology of JTKL

JTKL, a formulation derived from TCM, typically contains a variety of herbal ingredients known for their therapeutic effects on cervical pain and related musculoskeletal conditions. Specifically, JTKL is composed of seven traditional Chinese herbal ingredients: Sanqi (Radix Notoginseng), Chuanxiong (Rhizoma Ligustici), Yanhusuo (Rhizoma Corydalis), Bai Shao (Radix Paeoniae Alba), Wei Ling Xian (Radix Clematidis), Gegen (Radix Puerariae), and Qiang Huo (Rhizoma Notopterygii) (Table 1).

Sanqi is rich in active ingredients such as panax notoginseng saponins, flavonoids, polysaccharides, amino acids, and trace elements, among which panax notoginseng saponins serve as the core material basis for its efficacy.8 Notably, Sanqi possesses the unique effect of “stopping bleeding without leaving stasis and resolving stasis without damaging healthy qi”.9 Similarly, Chuanxiong mainly contains volatile oils, alkaloids, organic acids, and polysaccharides, and it is clinically applied in the treatment of various common diseases, including cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, nervous system diseases, and respiratory system diseases.10

The lactones levistolide A (47), tokinolide B, chuanxiongnolide R1 (56), and chuanxiongdiolide R2 (57), isolated from Ligusticum chuanxiong, exert anti‑inflammatory effects.11 Regarding Yanhusuo, its main chemical components include alkaloids, organic acids, nucleosides, and steroids, with alkaloids being the material basis for its efficacy. Several compounds, namely dehydrocorydaline, 13‑methyl‑palmatrubine and saulatine, have been identified as analgesic‑active ingredients in Corydalis yanhusuo.11 Specifically, Yanhusuo exerts effects in regulating the central nervous system (such as analgesia and sedation), regulating the cardiovascular system (such as vasodilation), as well as exhibiting various other pharmacological activities including anti-tumor, anti-inflammation, hypoglycemia, and hypolipidemia.11 Bai Shao mainly contains chemical components such as terpenoids, tannins, volatile oils, flavonoids, steroids, polysaccharides, and phenylethanoid glycosides, and it has multiple pharmacological effects, including liver protection, anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects, anti-depression, immune regulation, anti-oxidation, neuroprotection, and improvement of glucose metabolism.12 Among them, total glucosides of paeony, an active constituent extracted from Paeonia lactiflora, exerts anti‑inflammatory, antioxidant and immunomodulatory effects.12 Wei Ling Xian mainly consists of various chemical components such as saponins, flavonoids, lignans, alkaloids, terpenoids, and phenols, among which triterpenoid saponins are considered the main material basis for its pharmacological activities such as anti-inflammation.13 Gegen is chemically characterized by isoflavones, saponins, polysaccharides, alkaloids and coumarins. Isoflavones, its major active constituents, exert antioxidant effects by scavenging free radicals, anti‑inflammatory effects via regulating inflammatory pathways, hypoglycemic effects through modulating glucose‑metabolizing‑enzyme activity, and are capable of protecting vascular endothelium and improving blood circulation.14 In terms of Qiang Huo, studies have shown that nearly 300 volatile oils and terpenoids, 43 coumarins, 24 sugars and glycosides, 47 organic acids and phenols, and 20 polyacetylenes have been isolated from it. The bio-active constituents isolated from Qiang Huo, namely 4-methyl-3-trans-hexenylferulate, (-)-bornylferulate, 4-methoxyphenethylferulate and phenethyl-ferulate, can markedly inhibit nitric-oxide (NO) secretion and exhibit anti-inflammatory activity.15

Nevertheless, it remains difficult to pinpoint either a single or a small subset of active moieties responsible for the overall efficacy of JTKL. As a multi-component compound Chinese herbal preparation, its therapeutic effects are presumed to rely on synergistic interactions among multiple bioactive components. Further well-designed pharmacological experiments are therefore required to identify its core effective substances.

Pathogenesis of CEH

CEH is a multifactorial secondary headache characterized by unilateral head pain radiating from the neck, with cervical spine lesions as the primary initiating cause. Its pathophysiology involves coordinated disorders of the cervical local structure, cerebral blood supply, neuroinflammation, and central pain regulation,16 while TCM attributes it to qi stagnation, blood stasis and collateral obstruction.17 Clarifying these mechanisms is essential to illustrate the therapeutic targets and efficacy rationale of JTKL for CEH. This section systematically elaborates the modern medical and TCM pathogenesis of CEH, laying a solid theoretical foundation for subsequent analysis of JTKL’s pharmacological effects and clinical application.

Peripheral Cervical Structural Lesion Pathway

The cervical spine forms the fundamental anatomical basis of CEH, which, unlike other headache subtypes, originates from cervical‑spine structural abnormalities.19 The upper cervical segments (C1‑C3) share close neural‑functional connections with the cranial nociceptive system.20 Accordingly, pathological changes in cervical tissues may directly or indirectly trigger head pain. This feature differentiates CEH from primary headaches including migraine and tension‑type headache, and underscores the key role of peripheral cervical lesions in its pathogenesis.21

Multiple predisposing factors drive cervical structural‑lesion development, chiefly chronic mechanical strain, prolonged poor posture, age‑related degeneration, and acute cervical trauma.22 Chronic strain from long‑term desk‑work or abnormal neck posture combined with age‑related degenerative changes represents the most frequent clinical trigger, while acute injuries such as cervical sprain may also produce rapid structural damage.23 Together, these risk factors result in irreversible pathological alterations: cervical disc herniation, facet‑joint hypertrophy, persistent paravertebral‑muscle spasm, and ligament calcification‑fibrosis.24 These structural abnormalities compress, stretch, or chemically irritate upper cervical nerve roots (C1‑C3) and the nearby sympathetic plexus, activating peripheral nociceptive afferent fibres.25 Pain signals ascend along sensory pathways and converge within the brain‑stem trigeminocervical complex (trigeminal nucleus caudalis), the key centre for cranio‑cervical pain integration. This process produces referred head pain and constitutes the principal pathological mechanism underlying acute CEH episodes.26

In addition to direct nerve compression, soft‑tissue injury secondary to cervical structural lesions triggers sustained sterile inflammation, a critical driver of the transition from acute to chronic CEH.27 Damaged tissues release multiple inflammatory mediators such as prostaglandins, bradykinin and histamine, which activate peripheral cervical and cephalic nociceptors.28 These mediators lower nociceptor‑activation thresholds and induce peripheral pain sensitization, whereby mild mechanical or chemical stimuli can evoke robust pain signals.2 Such sensitization worsens headache severity and persistence, facilitates recurrent episodes and chronic progression of CEH, and creates a self‑sustaining vicious cycle among structural injury, inflammation and persistent head pain.29

Vertebrobasilar Insufficiency Pathway

Arising from the subclavian artery, the vertebral artery supplies the posterior cerebral circulation, brainstem and cerebellum,30 and its close anatomical association with cervical soft‑tissue structures renders it vulnerable to compression and injury induced by cervical structural disorders.31 This anatomical relationship may lead to vertebrobasilar insufficiency, a secondary pathological pathway that synergizes with peripheral cervical lesions to aggravate CEH symptoms.32

Degeneration‑, strain‑ or trauma‑related cervical structural lesions directly compress the vertebral artery.33 Pathological alterations including disc herniation, facet‑joint hyperplasia, osteophytes and paravertebral‑muscle spasm may produce persistent mechanical pressure, particularly on the vertebral‑artery segment running through the upper‑cervical transverse foramina.34 Sustained compression causes vertebral‑artery luminal stenosis, elevated vascular resistance and reduced vertebrobasilar blood‑flow velocity.35 Decreased blood‑flow further impairs posterior‑circulation perfusion and disturbs the balance of cerebral blood supply and demand.36

Insufficient posterior‑circulation perfusion secondary to vertebrobasilar stenosis induces cerebral hypoxia and metabolic disturbance.37 Hypoxia and metabolic disorders stimulate intracranial pain‑sensitive structures such as the meninges, intracranial vessels and intracranial peripheral nerves.38 Distinct from referred pain triggered by direct nerve compression, headache produced via this pathway frequently presents clinically specific concomitant symptoms.39 This mechanism is strongly linked to CEH marked by prominent dizziness, persistent neck stiffness and occipital distending pain, and these accompanying symptoms often correlate with headache severity.40

Numerous clinical studies support an association between vertebrobasilar insufficiency and CEH.41,42 Doppler‑ultrasound imaging has demonstrated that CEH patients exhibit significantly reduced vertebral‑artery systolic‑peak and end‑diastolic flow velocities, alongside elevated vascular resistance‑index values, compared with healthy controls.43 Furthermore, clinical‑observation data indicate that headache relief is strongly positively correlated with improved cerebral‑perfusion parameters.44 Resolution of vertebrobasilar blood‑flow abnormalities is accompanied by marked improvement in occipital distending pain and dizziness, further validating this pathogenic pathway and supporting targeted therapies aimed at enhancing cerebral circulation.45

Neuroinflammation and Neurohumoral Imbalance Pathway

Neuroinflammation acts as the key pathological driver of chronic progression and frequent recurrence in CEH, converting transient acute headache into intractable chronic pain.46 Distinct from acute mechanical compression or transient ischemia, neuroinflammation triggers long‑term neural remodeling and persistent pain hypersensitivity, generating a self‑sustaining inflammatory loop independent of the original cervical structural injury.47 This pathway accounts for persistent CEH symptoms and is associated with poor responses to conventional symptomatic therapy and high recurrence rates, representing a major focus of current basic and clinical CEH research.48

Local cervical injury caused by structural degeneration, mechanical compression or soft tissue damage serves as the initial trigger for neuroinflammation in CEH.49 Such injury activates microglia and astrocytes in the spinal dorsal horn and trigeminal nervous system, which are central immune cells critically involved in pain modulation.50 Upon activation, glial cells undergo phenotypic transformation and robustly produce multiple pro‑inflammatory mediators, including interleukin‑6 (IL‑6), tumor necrosis factor‑α (TNF‑α), interleukin‑1β (IL‑1β), nitric oxide (NO), and endothelin‑1 (ET‑1).51–53 These bioactive substances synergistically exacerbate cervical nerve edema, impair blood‑nerve barrier integrity, and sustain the activation of nociceptive afferents. This process induces and maintains central pain sensitization, ultimately transforming acute intermittent headache into intractable chronic pain.54

Beyond inducing pain sensitization, neuroinflammation‑mediated vasoactive mediator imbalance further aggravates CEH symptoms.55 Abnormal NO/ET‑1 ratios impair cerebral vascular motility and disrupt cerebral microcirculation, which synergizes with vertebrobasilar insufficiency to exacerbate headache, dizziness and occipital distending pain.56 Recent mechanistic studies have identified dorsal root ganglion and spinal cord GDAP1 and HMGB1 as key regulators of CEH neuroinflammation.57 These molecules modulate glial activation and inflammatory responses, and are recognized as critical targets for the anti‑inflammatory and analgesic effects of JTKL. This evidence provides a solid molecular basis for the therapeutic mechanism of this herbal formula in CEH management.7,58

TCM Pathogenesis

In the theoretical system of TCM, CEH is not directly classified under a single specific disease name but is categorized under syndromes such as “headache”, “head wind”, and “neck bi”.59 From a TCM perspective, the head is regarded as the “meeting point of all yang meridians” and the “seat of the sea of marrow”, relying on the nourishment of qi and blood for normal physiological functions.60 The neck, as a crucial hub connecting the skull and the trunk, serves as the core pathway for the circulation of qi and blood and the upward transportation of visceral essence to nourish the brain.5 Any pathological disorder in the cervical meridians and collaterals can easily disrupt the nourishment of the brain marrow and induce head pain, which is the core pathological logic of CEH from a TCM viewpoint and lays the theoretical foundation for its syndrome differentiation and treatment.61

The core pathogenesis of CEH in TCM is qi stagnation and blood stasis with obstructed collaterals, which is referred to as “pain due to obstruction”. This is the core pathological link running through the occurrence and progression of the disease.62 TCM theory holds that various factors, including invasion of external wind-cold, chronic strain caused by long-term desk work, acute cervical trauma, as well as emotional stagnation and impaired liver qi dispersion, can block the circulation of qi and blood in the cervical meridians and collaterals.62 The stagnation of qi and blood stasis in the cervical collaterals not only leads to disharmony of the cervical collaterals and symptoms such as neck stiffness and spasm but also hinders the upward nourishment of qi and blood to the brain, resulting in malnutrition of the brain marrow and subsequent headache. Clinically, acute attacks or chronic protraction of CEH is induced, where “qi stagnation” and “blood stasis” interact and influence each other, forming a complex pathological state.63

In clinical practice, the typical symptoms of CEH patients are highly consistent with the TCM pathogenesis of “qi stagnation and blood stasis”, serving as the core basis for syndrome differentiation. Patients often present with persistent neck stiffness and limited mobility, with head pain radiating from the neck to the face.64 The pain is significantly aggravated when the neck performs movements such as flexion, extension, and rotation.65 Some patients may also be accompanied by local tenderness in the neck and dizziness. These clinical manifestations fully confirm the pathological state of blocked cervical collaterals and impaired qi and blood circulation, which is completely consistent with the core pathogenesis of “qi stagnation and blood stasis with obstructed collaterals”. It also provides an objective basis for TCM clinical syndrome differentiation and the establishment of therapeutic principles. Correspondingly, the core TCM therapeutic principle for CEH is to promote blood circulation, remove blood stasis, regulate qi, and relieve pain, aiming to unblock the stagnated cervical collaterals (Figure 2), harmonize the circulation of qi and blood, and fundamentally eliminate the pathological basis of “pain due to obstruction” to achieve a therapeutic effect that addresses both the symptoms and the root cause.66

The formula composition and efficacy of JTKL are highly consistent with the core TCM pathogenesis and therapeutic principles of CEH, and the rationality of its clinical application is fully verified by the integrated interpretation of modern medical and TCM pathogenesis.67 JTKL takes TCMs for promoting blood circulation and removing blood stasis as the core, combined with herbs for regulating qi, unblocking collaterals, soothing the liver and relieving pain.68 It precisely targets the key pathological link of “qi stagnation and blood stasis with obstructed collaterals”, which can effectively unblock the stagnated cervical collaterals, regulate the circulation of qi and blood, not only relieve local symptoms such as neck stiffness and pain but also improve the state of malnutrition of the brain marrow.69,70 This provides theoretical support for the use of JTKL in the treatment of CEH from the perspective of traditional Chinese medicine. Materials and Methods

Literature Search Strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted to retrieve eligible domestic and international studies published between January 2010 and December 2026. Both English and Chinese databases were searched to ensure comprehensiveness. English databases included PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library, while Chinese databases covered China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data, VIP Chinese Science and Technology Periodical Database, and China Biology Medicine Database (CBM). Boolean operators (AND, OR) were applied to combine search terms accurately. The English search terms included “Jingtong Granule”, “Jingtong Keli”, “cervicogenic headache”, “CEH”, “pharmacological mechanism”, “clinical efficacy”, “safety” and “analgesia”. Corresponding Chinese search terms were “颈痛颗粒”, “颈源性头痛”, “药理作用”, “临床疗效”, “安全性” and “镇痛”. Furthermore, reference lists of all included studies and relevant high-quality publications were manually screened to supplement potentially overlooked eligible literature.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

Two trained researchers independently performed literature screening and standardized data extraction to ensure objectivity and avoid bias. Any discrepancies during the process were resolved through discussion and consultation with a third senior researcher. Core extracted data covered four dimensions: (1) Basic study information: author, publication year, study design, sample size, intervention regimen, and treatment duration; (2) Efficacy indicators: Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) pain score, headache attack frequency, headache duration, Recorded cervical range of motion (ROM) and clinical total effective rate; (3) Safety indicators: type and incidence of adverse events, and corresponding adverse reaction management measures; (4) Mechanism-related indicators: key inflammatory factors, neurohumoral regulatory factors, pain signaling pathway proteins, and core molecular targets associated with the therapeutic effects of JTKL.

Standardized tools were adopted for methodological quality assessment. The Cochrane Risk-of-Bias Tool for randomized controlled trials (RoB 1.0) was used to evaluate all included RCTs.71,72 Quality assessment was completed independently by the same two researchers. Studies were not excluded merely on the basis of individual high-risk bias items. Risk-of-bias evaluation was used to interpret outcome reliability and overall evidence quality, so as to ensure the credibility and persuasiveness of the review conclusions. Risk-of-bias assessment for the included RCTs was completed (Supplementary Figure 1).

Clinical Efficacy and Safety of JTKL for CEH

Several RCTs carried out in China have yielded preliminary evidence suggesting the promising clinical efficacy and safety of JTKL for CEH.73 These encouraging findings may reflect stable pharmacological effects of JTKL and indicate its potential applicability across different patient subgroups with CEH.73,74

The bulk of available clinical evidence supporting the efficacy of JTKL comes from clinical trials conducted in China. These studies have assessed its therapeutic effects from multiple dimensions and commonly addressed three key clinical outcomes: rapid pain relief, improvement of cervical mobility, and long‑term control of headache symptoms. By evaluating changes in headache Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores, cervical range of motion, and headache‑attack frequency, these studies suggest potential clinical benefits of JTKL for chronic and recurrent CEH. Since most of these trials reflect routine real‑world clinical practice, their findings may be applicable to patients with long‑term, recurrent CEH.

Beyond its primary therapeutic effects, JTKL may offer unique benefits when combined with conventional medicines and rehabilitation interventions. Preliminary clinical evidence suggests that JTKL used alongside non‑steroidal anti‑inflammatory drugs, muscle relaxants, or physical therapy could produce synergistic effects, potentially improving clinical response rates and prolonging symptom remission. Such combined regimens may be helpful for patients with refractory CEH who respond poorly to monotherapy, possibly improving therapeutic outcomes and lowering the risk of adverse events from long‑term conventional drug treatment. These findings indicate that JTKL has potential value as an adjuvant intervention, which may expand therapeutic choices for complicated CEH cases.73

In terms of safety, the available data from included RCTs suggest that JTKL shows a favourable safety profile with good clinical tolerance. Adverse events associated with JTKL are rare and mostly mild, mainly presenting as transient gastrointestinal discomforts such as mild nausea or loose stools.6 These minor reactions are usually self‑limiting and may not require treatment discontinuation, which indicates the potential suitability of JTKL for long‑term use among patients with chronic CEH. Theoretically, given the well‑documented therapeutic effects of JTKL for cervical spondylosis,75 beneficial outcomes might also be expected when it is applied to cervicogenic headache originating from cervical lesions. Therefore, JTKL may serve as a reasonable supplementary treatment option for patients with cervicogenic headache and concomitant cervical spondylosis who obtain unsatisfactory responses to physical therapy.76 Nevertheless, large‑scale clinical trials are still required to further verify this hypothesis.

A prospective‑randomized study recruited 90 patients with recurrent cervicogenic headache of qi‑stagnation and blood‑stasis syndrome, allocated to three equal‑sized groups: Jingtong Granules (JTKL), radiofrequency ablation, and combined JTKL‑radiofrequency therapy. Safety Profile

JTKL presents a favorable and reliable safety profile in routine clinical practice, with a low incidence of adverse reactions and no reports of severe adverse events in all included studies. This high safety profile makes JTKL highly suitable for long-term administration in the management of chronic CEH, a condition requiring persistent treatment.7

According to the drug instructions, patients with allergic constitution may experience skin rash and itching during medication. These symptoms will gradually subside after drug withdrawal and generally do not require special treatment. In clinical practice, adverse reactions are mainly allergic reactions such as skin rash, occasionally accompanied by gastrointestinal discomfort, all of which are reversible. According to literature research, only one case of elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT) has been reported, which returned to normal after drug withdrawal.80 Data from the National Center for ADR Monitoring show that a total of 2 cases of gastric discomfort were collected from January 2015 to December 2019 in patients taking JTKL, and the symptoms were relieved after drug discontinuation.

Improvement of Cerebral and Cervical Circulation

Vertebrobasilar insufficiency represents one of the most important pathological pathways underlying CEH, and is closely associated with a series of typical clinical manifestations including dizziness, occipital distending pain, and neck stiffness in CEH patients.20,21 Cervical structural disorders such as intervertebral disc herniation, facet joint hyperplasia, and muscle spasm may directly compress the vertebral artery, leading to vascular stenosis, elevated peripheral resistance, and reduced blood flow velocity. These alterations consequently result in insufficient posterior cerebral perfusion, cerebral hypoxia, and metabolic disturbances, which further activate intracranial pain‑sensitive structures and exacerbate headache symptoms.21 Therefore, improving vertebrobasilar blood supply has been recognized as a critical therapeutic strategy for CEH patients presenting with circulatory dysfunction.21

JTKL exerts a significant regulatory effect on cerebral and cervical blood circulation, directly targeting the vertebrobasilar insufficiency pathway of CEH to improve abnormal blood perfusion.6 Related pharmacological and clinical studies have demonstrated that JTKL can effectively dilate the vertebral artery and basilar artery, relax vascular smooth muscle, reduce peripheral vascular resistance, and significantly accelerate the peak systolic velocity and end-diastolic velocity of the vertebral artery, thereby reversing the cerebral hypoperfusion state induced by cervical structural lesions and restoring the normal blood supply and oxygen supply balance of the posterior circulation, brainstem and cerebellum.6

In addition to its potential anti‑inflammatory effects, JTKL may modulate the dynamic balance of key pro‑inflammatory mediators in the body, which could represent one possible mechanism underlying improved neuro‑inflammatory function. It might down‑regulate the over‑expression of interleukin‑6 (IL‑6), tumor necrosis factor‑α (TNF‑α) and interleukin‑1β (IL‑1β), thereby correcting the disturbed inflammatory cytokine profile among patients with CEH.73 By restoring the balance of these pro‑inflammatory mediators, JTKL may reduce neuroinflammation in cerebral and cervical tissues, ameliorate microcirculatory disturbance, and relieve headache‑dizziness symptoms induced by inflammatory injury and insufficient perfusion.73

Anti-Inflammatory and Analgesic Effects

Neuroinflammation has been widely recognized as the core pathological mechanism that drives the chronic progression, recurrent attacks and refractoriness of CEH.73 Unlike acute nociceptive pain induced by simple mechanical compression, chronic neuroinflammation mediates long-term glial cell activation, peripheral and central pain sensitization, and neural circuit remodeling, which forms a self-sustaining pathological loop independent of the initial cervical injury, making CEH symptoms difficult to relieve with conventional single-target analgesics.21 This persistent inflammatory microenvironment is the key reason for the transition from acute intermittent headache to chronic persistent CEH,21,22 and also the core target for JTKL to exert its therapeutic effect.

JTKL, a multi-component traditional Chinese patent medicine, exerts its prominent anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects mainly by regulating the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway, a pivotal intracellular pathway that mediates inflammatory response and pain signal amplification.11 The NF-κB pathway is abnormally activated in CEH patients, promoting the transcription and expression of a large number of pro-inflammatory genes, and further aggravating the inflammatory cascade reaction and pain signal transmission.84,85 JTKL targets this key pathway to inhibit its overactivation, blocking the upstream initiation link of inflammatory response and laying a foundation for subsequent inflammatory inhibition and pain relief.11

A large number of in vivo and in vitro pharmacological studies have verified that JTKL combined treatment group significantly downregulates the gene and protein expression of key pro-inflammatory cytokines in serum, cervical local soft tissues, spinal dorsal horn and trigeminal nervous system, including interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β).73 These cytokines are the main mediators of peripheral nerve edema, nociceptor sensitization and abnormal pain signal transmission. By reducing the release of these inflammatory mediators, JTKL effectively alleviates edema of upper cervical nerve roots, repairs the damaged blood-nerve barrier, blocks the ectopic discharge of nociceptive afferent fibers, and cuts off the transmission pathway of abnormal pain signals from the periphery to the center.73

Neuromuscular Modulation

Abnormal neuromuscular function is a crucial initiating and aggravating factor of CEH, especially for patients with chronic CEH induced by long-term poor posture and chronic strain.73 Long-term cervical muscle tension, persistent spasm and increased muscle tone can not only aggravate the mechanical load of cervical vertebrae and promote the progression of structural lesions, but also directly compress and stimulate upper cervical nerve roots and peripheral nerves, inducing ectopic pain signals and forming a vicious circle between muscle spasm and headache. Regulating neuromuscular function is an important link to block the initial trigger of CEH.73

JTKL exerts a targeted regulatory effect on neuromuscular conduction and muscle function, effectively relieving abnormal tension and persistent spasm of paravertebral muscles.73 It can regulate the signal transmission at the neuromuscular junction, inhibit the excessive excitability of motor nerve endings, reduce the abnormal contraction of cervical skeletal muscle, and restore the normal tone and flexibility of neck muscles.6,7,73 By relieving muscle spasm, JTKL directly reduces the continuous mechanical compression and chemical stimulation on cervical nerve roots, blood vessels and surrounding soft tissues, cutting off the peripheral trigger link of headache induced by neuromuscular abnormalities.73,74

Furthermore, the chief herbal component of JTKL, Chuanxiong, modulates the levels of key neurotransmitters related to pain regulation and neuromuscular control, including 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) and norepinephrine (NE), which are critical for inhibiting abnormal pain signal transmission and maintaining normal neuromuscular function.86 JTKL regulates the synthesis, release and reuptake of these neurotransmitters, restoring their dynamic balance in the central and peripheral nervous systems,86 inhibiting the excessive transmission and amplification of pain signals in the trigeminocervical nucleus, the core integration center of head and neck pain.87

Translational Potential and Future Directions

JTKL, a well-validated Chinese patent medicine with reliable efficacy and high safety in the clinical management of CEH, embodies distinctive translational value and broad clinical promotion prospects in the field of analgesic therapy.7 Different from single-target analgesic and muscle relaxant drugs that are mostly limited to short-term symptomatic relief and accompanied by potential adverse reactions with long-term use, JTKL relies on its multi-component, multi-target synergistic regulatory characteristics, matching the complex and multifactorial pathogenesis of CEH, which fills the clinical gap of long-term and safe maintenance drugs.73 With the increasing integration of TCM and modern neurology, the translational research of JTKL for CEH has gradually become a focus of cross-disciplinary research, and its clinical application value is gradually recognized by domestic and foreign scholars.

Nevertheless, despite the accumulated preliminary basic and clinical evidence, several critical research gaps and bottlenecks still exist in the process of promoting the international clinical translation and standardized application of JTKL. Most of the existing research is limited to domestic single-center small-sample studies, lacking high-level international clinical evidence and systematic basic pharmacological verification, which restricts its global promotion and inclusion in international CEH diagnosis and treatment guidelines. Therefore, sorting out the current translational value, existing challenges and future research directions of JTKL is of great significance to improve its research system, optimize clinical application and realize international recognition.

Clinical Translational Value

JTKL shows unique clinical advantages and extensive adaptability in the translational treatment of CEH. Clinical practice has confirmed that JTKL is highly suitable for adjuvant therapy of acute exacerbation of CEH and long-term maintenance treatment of chronic recurrent CEH, and its therapeutic value is particularly prominent for patients with poor tolerance to NSAIDs, muscle relaxants and other conventional pharmacological medications, patients with concurrent cervical spondylosis, cervical muscle strain and other comorbidities, and elderly patients with multiple underlying diseases who require long-term medication.73,74

The core translational value of JTKL lies in its dual advantages of multi-target pharmacological efficacy and low toxicity safety profile, which perfectly fit the clinical needs of long-term management of chronic headache. Unlike conventional pharmacological medications that often act on a single signaling pathway or target, JTKL simultaneously intervenes in multiple core pathological pathways of CEH, including neuroinflammation, vertebrobasilar insufficiency, neuromuscular dysfunction and TCM qi stagnation and blood stasis, achieving a holistic therapeutic effect that addresses both symptoms and root causes.6,7,58 Meanwhile, its mild and transient adverse reactions mainly limited to gastrointestinal tract, no obvious liver and kidney toxicity, make it an ideal choice for long-term maintenance treatment.7,78,80

Therefore, JTKL has broad application prospects in the hierarchical diagnosis and treatment of chronic headache, being suitable for both specialist pain clinics, neurology clinics in tertiary hospitals and primary medical institutions. Its convenient administration form (granule) and standardized dosage form are conducive to clinical promotion and improvement of patient compliance, enabling it to serve as a first-line adjuvant drug for the primary screening and treatment of CEH, thereby reducing the medical pressure on tertiary hospitals. In addition, according to the Chinese expert consensus, JTKL, as a Chinese patent medicine specifically indicated for cervical spondylotic radiculopathy (CSR), exerts effects of promoting blood circulation, removing blood stasis and relieving pain. Cervical nerve root pain is a typical symptom in patients with CSR, and JTKL has been shown to have favorable clinical efficacy and safety; collectively, these characteristics endow JTKL with high social and economic benefits.6,7

Current Research Challenges

Although JTKL has achieved certain application results in domestic clinical practice, it still faces multiple key bottlenecks that restrict its international clinical translation and standardized application, the most prominent of which is the lack of high-level international clinical evidence. At present, almost all existing clinical research evidence of JTKL for CEH comes from domestic single-center or small-sample multi-center studies, lacking large-sample, international multi-center, double-blind, RCTs that meet international medical standards. This single research region and limited sample size lead to low international recognition of its clinical efficacy, making it difficult to be included in international CEH diagnosis and treatment guidelines and accepted by clinicians.

The lack of long-term follow-up clinical data is another critical challenge restricting its clinical application. Most existing studies only focus on short-term efficacy observation within 1–3 months, lacking rigorous follow-up data for more than 6 months, especially long-term data on sustained efficacy, recurrence rate, and long-term safety.73 For chronic recurrent CEH, long-term maintenance efficacy and recurrence control are the core indicators to evaluate drug value, and the current incomplete long-term data makes it impossible to fully clarify the long-term clinical value of JTKL, and also limits its application in chronic disease long-term management.

The systematic basic pharmacological research of JTKL is relatively lagging behind, and its material basis and molecular mechanism have not been fully elucidated. As a multi-component Chinese patent medicine, its specific effective components, core pharmacological targets, and “component-target-pathway” regulatory network corresponding to CEH treatment have not been systematically confirmed, and most studies only stay in the overall efficacy observation of the drug, lacking in-depth exploration of its effective substances and molecular mechanism. This unclear mechanism research is not conducive to the international recognition of its pharmacological effects, and also restricts the optimization and secondary development of its prescription.

Additionally, there is a lack of standardized clinical application regimens and unified dosage guidelines for JTKL in the treatment of CEH. At present, the clinical dosage, treatment course and administration mode of JTKL mostly rely on empirical application, lacking targeted precise medication regimens for patients with different disease severity, different age groups and different TCM syndrome types, and no unified clinical application specification or expert consensus has been formed. This non-standardized application mode leads to uneven efficacy in different clinical practices, and also increases the difficulty of its standardized promotion and international translation.

Future Research Directions

To break through the current research bottlenecks and promote the international clinical translation of JTKL, the top priority is to carry out high-level clinical research to upgrade the level of clinical evidence. It is necessary to design and implement large-sample, international multi-center, double-blind, RCTs covering different regions and ethnic groups, strictly following the CONSORT statement standards for clinical trial design and result reporting, and supplementing complete long-term follow-up data of 6–12 months, focusing on long-term efficacy, recurrence rate, quality of life improvement and long-term safety indicators, so as to provide high-level evidence support for its international promotion and inclusion in global guidelines.

The second key direction is to conduct systematic basic research to clarify the pharmacological material basis and molecular mechanisms of JTKL. By combining cutting-edge technologies including network pharmacology, molecular docking, metabolomics and proteomics with in-vivo animal and in-vitro cell experiments, researchers may screen and identify the core active components of JTKL against CEH, and verify its major pharmacological targets and signalling pathways (such as the NF-κB-mediated inflammatory pathway and the IL-6/TNF-α/IL-1β inflammatory regulatory pathway).73 A complete “component-target-pathway” regulatory network could then be established to explore the scientific connotation of its multi-target synergistic effects, which may provide a theoretical foundation for mechanistic explanations.

Precision and standardized clinical application research is also an important future direction, aiming to formulate unified clinical guidelines and medication regimens. It is necessary to carry out stratified clinical research based on the age, disease severity, TCM syndrome types and comorbidities of CEH patients, explore the optimal dosage, treatment course and administration mode for different patient groups, and formulate standardized clinical application guidelines and expert consensus for JTKL in the treatment of CEH, so as to realize precise medication and standardized application, improve clinical efficacy consistency and reduce unnecessary empirical medication.

Finally, it is essential to optimize the combined therapeutic protocols of JTKL and explore synergistic treatment modes with higher efficacy and higher safety. Focus on the combined application of JTKL with conventional pharmacological medications (such as NSAIDs, muscle relaxants) and rehabilitation therapies (such as cervical traction, massage, physical therapy), clarify the synergistic mechanism between different treatment methods, screen the optimal combined regimen with reduced conventional pharmacological medications dosage, enhanced efficacy and reduced adverse reactions, and build a comprehensive treatment system integrating TCM, conventional pharmacological medications and rehabilitation, to provide more optimized and efficient treatment options for refractory CEH patients.

Discussion

This review systematically synthesizes and analyzes the clinical efficacy, safety profile, underlying pharmacological mechanisms, and translational potential of JTKL in the intervention of CEH. Its core objective is to clarify the unique clinical advantages of JTKL and bridge the existing research gaps of TCM in the field of secondary headache disorders. The key findings from this comprehensive analysis demonstrate the distinct therapeutic merits of JTKL in treating CEH, laying a robust theoretical and clinical foundation for its standardized clinical application, widespread promotion, and further in-depth translational research.

In terms of safety, JTKL presents a favorable and reliable safety profile that is highly suitable for long-term clinical application, a critical advantage over conventional therapeutic agents for CEH. Existing clinical data confirm that the incidence of adverse reactions associated with JTKL is extremely low, and the few reported adverse events are limited to mild, transient gastrointestinal discomforts such as slight dry mouth or loose stool, which resolve spontaneously without drug discontinuation or targeted intervention.80 No severe adverse events related to hepatic or renal toxicity, cardiovascular damage or drug dependence have been documented in relevant studies, in sharp contrast to the risks of gastrointestinal irritation, renal function impairment and potential dependence associated with long-term use of NSAIDs and muscle relaxants. This superior safety profile makes JTKL an optimal candidate for long-term maintenance therapy of chronic recurrent CEH, addressing the unmet clinical need for safe long-term management of this chronic disorder.

As a classic multi-component TCM patent medicine, JTKL exerts multi-target and multi-pathway therapeutic effects that are highly consistent with the complex, multifactorial pathogenesis of CEH, embodying the holistic therapeutic concept of TCM and aligning with the international research frontier of complex drug mechanisms. It intervenes simultaneously in multiple core pathological pathways of CEH, including inhibiting neuroinflammation via regulating NF-κB signaling pathway and downstream pro-inflammatory cytokines, improving vertebrobasilar blood circulation and correcting vasoactive substance imbalance, and regulating neuromuscular conduction to relieve muscle spasm and block central pain sensitization.6,7,73 This multi-dimensional regulatory mode not only targets the local pathological lesions of the cervical spine, but also modulates the overall neural and circulatory disorders, which is highly compatible with the characteristics of complex chronic diseases.

Despite the encouraging clinical outcomes reported in many included studies, several important methodological limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the available evidence for JTKL in CEH. Most of the published clinical trials recruited relatively small sample sizes and were carried out at single medical centres. A considerable number of studies did not adopt rigorous randomised controlled designs, blinding procedures, or placebo‑controlled groups, which may increase the risk of performance bias and detection bias. In addition, inconsistent outcome indicators and variable follow‑up durations across trials have created heterogeneity among the reported results. Positive findings are more likely to be published than neutral or negative outcomes, suggesting potential publication bias within this research field. The overall quality and strength of current evidence remain limited. We therefore should interpret the therapeutic benefits of JTKL cautiously, and large‑sample, multi‑centre, high‑quality randomised controlled trials are urgently required to draw more robust conclusions.

Conclusion

Taken together, existing evidence suggests that JTKL may serve as a potentially safe, effective and multi‑target therapeutic option for CEH, with promising clinical application value and translational prospects. It should be noted that current relevant research remains limited by small sample sizes, single‑center designs and incomplete long‑term follow‑up data, which restricts the strength of our conclusions. At present, firm recommendations for its widespread global use cannot be made. Future standardized international multi‑center randomized controlled trials, in‑depth basic research on pharmacological material basis and molecular targets, and unified clinical guidelines are urgently required to further validate its efficacy and safety before it can be considered as a long‑term adjuvant therapy for CEH.
