Beyond Single Biomarkers: A Multidimensional Risk Score for Improved Post-Transplant Prognosis Prediction in Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Xiao-yong Ye, Simeng Lei, Guang Jia, Qiang He, Zhili Ji, Jun Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionInflammation- and nutrition-related biomarkers are associated with prognosis in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), but most studies have focused on single markers. This study aimed to develop and internally validate a multidimensional inflammatory-nutritional risk score (RS) for predicting post-transplant outcomes in HCC patients.Materials and methodsThis single-center retrospective cohort included 239 patients with pathologically confirmed HCC who underwent liver transplantation between April 2013 and July 2023. Fourteen preoperative inflammation- and nutrition-related markers were calculated from laboratory data obtained within one week before transplantation. A bootstrap LASSO-Cox framework with 2000 resampling iterations was used to identify stable prognostic markers, and those selected in >70% of iterations were retained for the primary RS. Overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) were assessed. Model performance was evaluated using Cox regression, C-index, time-dependent ROC analysis, calibration, decision curve analysis, and bootstrap optimism-corrected internal validation.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 53 months, 83 OS events and 89 RFS events occurred. Four markers, including fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio, gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase-to-prealbumin ratio, gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase-to-albumin ratio, and aspartate aminotransferase-to-neutrophil ratio index, were selected to construct the primary RS. Patients with high RS had significantly worse OS and RFS than those with low RS, and RS remained independently associated with both outcomes in multivariable Cox analyses. Adding RS improved prognostic performance beyond Milan criteria, Hangzhou criteria, UCSF criteria, and clinicopathological models incorporating alpha-fetoprotein, tumor burden, portal vein tumor thrombus, and microvascular invasion. The final RS-plus clinicopathological model achieved apparent C-indices of 0.766 for OS and 0.759 for RFS, with bootstrap optimism-corrected C-indices of 0.756 and 0.749, respectively.ConclusionA four-marker inflammatory-nutritional RS based on routinely available preoperative biomarkers was independently associated with OS and RFS after liver transplantation for HCC and provided incremental prognostic information beyond established criteria and clinicopathological factors. Because the RS-plus model incorporates microvascular invasion, it should be interpreted as a postoperative prognostic tool. External validation is required before clinical application.

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains one of the most common malignancies and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with a persistently high disease burden despite advances in surveillance and treatment.1 For selected patients with HCC, liver transplantation (LT) offers the unique advantage of removing both the tumor and the underlying cirrhotic liver, thereby representing a potentially curative treatment strategy.2 However, post-transplant tumor recurrence continues to be a major determinant of long-term survival. Although conventional selection systems, such as the Milan, Hangzhou, and UCSF criteria, have improved candidate selection and risk stratification, considerable prognostic heterogeneity remains among patients who undergo LT. Therefore, there is a continuing need for reliable, easily accessible, and clinically interpretable biomarkers that can refine post-transplant prognostic assessment beyond established tumor burden-based criteria.

Systemic inflammation, nutritional status, coagulation activation, cholestasis, and hepatic functional reserve are closely involved in the biological behavior of HCC and may influence recurrence and survival after LT. Inflammation contributes to tumor progression by promoting immune dysregulation, angiogenesis, invasion, and metastatic dissemination.3,4 Meanwhile, impaired nutritional status and poor hepatic functional reserve may reflect both tumor-host interaction and reduced physiological resilience, which can adversely affect postoperative recovery and long-term outcomes. Accordingly, a variety of inflammation- and nutrition-related biomarkers have been investigated in HCC, including the fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio (FAR),5 neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR),6 platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR),7 monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR),8 systemic immune-inflammation index (SII),9 prognostic inflammatory index (PII),10 aspartate aminotransferase-to-neutrophil ratio index (ANRI),11 albumin-to-globulin ratio (AGR),12 gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase-to-albumin ratio (GAR),13 gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase-to-prealbumin ratio (GPAR),14 albumin-to-alkaline phosphatase ratio (AAPR),15 prognostic nutritional index (PNI),16 albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) grade,17 and platelet-albumin-bilirubin (PALBI) grade.18

We previously reported that FAR was an independent prognostic factor in HCC patients after LT using the same institutional cohort.5 However, that study focused on a single biomarker and did not determine whether a multidimensional combination of inflammatory and nutritional markers could provide more stable and incremental prognostic information. Indeed, most existing studies have evaluated individual biomarkers separately, which may not fully capture the complex interplay among inflammation, coagulation, nutrition, cholestasis, and liver functional reserve. Moreover, the optimal combination of these markers and their added value beyond established transplant criteria and clinicopathological risk factors remain insufficiently defined.

In the present study, we systematically evaluated a panel of preoperative inflammation- and nutrition-related biomarkers in HCC patients undergoing LT. Using a bootstrap LASSO-Cox framework, we aimed to identify the most stable prognostic markers and develop a multidimensional inflammatory-nutritional risk score (RS). We further assessed whether this RS could improve prediction of overall survival and recurrence-free survival beyond conventional transplant criteria and clinicopathological models. By integrating complementary biological dimensions into a clinically accessible scoring system, this study sought to provide a robust and interpretable tool for individualized post-transplant risk stratification in patients with HCC.

Methods

Patient Selection

This retrospective study analyzed patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) who underwent liver transplantation at Beijing Chaoyang Hospital, Capital Medical University, from April 2013 to July 2023. All cases were pathologically confirmed as HCC by postoperative histology. The Ethics Committee of Beijing Chaoyang Hospital approved the retrieval and analysis of patient clinical data (No. 2020-D.-303, approved 28 May 2020).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: histopathologically confirmed primary HCC, no concurrent malignancies or extrahepatic metastatic lesions, available follow-up information, complete clinical and pathological records, and comprehensive preoperative blood test results obtained within one week before surgery. Patients were excluded if they had concurrent malignancies or metastatic lesions, died during the perioperative period, or lacked critical clinical, pathological, follow-up, or laboratory data. A total of 260 patients were initially screened, and 239 patients were finally included in the analysis. Data Collection

Patient data were obtained from the hospital information system for individuals who underwent liver transplantation for HCC. Laboratory results were cross-verified using the electronic medical record system. An Excel database was used for systematic data entry, checking, and management. The collected variables included sex, age, hepatitis B surface antigen status, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), preoperative hematological and biochemical parameters, tumor-related pathological variables, and follow-up information. Laboratory variables included albumin, globulin, prealbumin, total bilirubin, fibrinogen, alkaline phosphatase, gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase, aspartate aminotransferase, white blood cell count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, and platelet count. Tumor-related variables included T stage, N stage, TNM stage, number of intrahepatic tumors, maximum tumor diameter, total intrahepatic tumor diameter, gross tumor morphology, histological type, tumor differentiation grade, macrovascular invasion, microvascular invasion (MVI), and portal vein tumor thrombus (PVTT). Transplant criteria, including Milan, Hangzhou, and UCSF criteria, were also recorded.

Missing values were checked across all clinical, pathological, laboratory, and follow-up variables. Complete case analysis was performed, and patients with missing data in key variables required for model construction or survival analysis were excluded.

Follow-Up

All transplant recipients were followed regularly after surgery. The median follow-up duration was 53 months, with a range of 4–134 months. Follow-up visits were scheduled monthly during the first six postoperative months. Between six months and two years after transplantation, follow-up was generally performed every three to six months. After two years, follow-up was continued at regular intervals according to clinical condition.

Routine surveillance included serum tumor markers and contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Imaging examinations were generally performed every six months. When local recurrence or distant metastasis was suspected, additional targeted imaging examinations, including CT, MRI, bone scanning, or positron emission tomography-computed tomography, were performed according to clinical indications.

Overall survival (OS) was calculated from the date of liver transplantation to death from any cause or the last confirmed follow-up. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) was calculated from the date of liver transplantation to the first documented tumor recurrence, death, or last follow-up, whichever occurred first. Patients without recurrence or death were censored at the date of last follow-up.

Definition of Inflammatory and Nutritional Related Markers

Inflammatory and nutritional related markers were calculated using laboratory test results obtained within one week before liver transplantation. The following markers were evaluated:

FAR = fibrinogen (g/L) / serum albumin (g/L)

NLR = neutrophil count (109/L) / lymphocyte count (109/L)

PLR = platelet count (109/L) / lymphocyte count (109/L)

MLR = monocyte count (109/L) / lymphocyte count (109/L)

SII = neutrophil count (109/L) × platelet count (109/L) / lymphocyte count (109/L)

PII = neutrophil count (109/L) × monocyte count (109/L) / lymphocyte count (109/L)

ANRI = aspartate aminotransferase (U/L) / neutrophil count (109/L)

AGR = serum albumin (g/L) / serum globulin (g/L)

GAR = gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (U/L) / serum albumin (g/L)

GPAR = gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (U/L) / serum prealbumin (g/L)

AAPR = serum albumin (g/L) / alkaline phosphatase (U/L)

PNI = serum albumin (g/L) + 5 × lymphocyte count (109/L)

ALBI = log10 total bilirubin (μmol/L) × 0.66 − albumin (g/L) × 0.085

PALBI = 2.02 × log10 total bilirubin (μmol/L) − 0.37 × [log10 total bilirubin (μmol/L)]2 − 0.04 × serum albumin (g/L) − 3.48 × log10 platelet count (109/L) + 1.01 × [log10 platelet count (109/L)]2

Development of the Risk Scoring System

To improve the robustness of variable selection and reduce instability caused by single biomarker screening, bootstrap resampling combined with LASSO-Cox regression was used to identify stable inflammatory-nutritional predictors. Fourteen inflammatory and nutritional indicators were included as candidate markers: FAR, NLR, PLR, MLR, SII, PII, ANRI, AGR, GAR, GPAR, AAPR, PNI, ALBI, and PALBI.

Bootstrap resampling was performed for 2000 iterations. In each bootstrap sample, the sample size was the same as that of the original analysis cohort, and patients were sampled with replacement. Candidate variables were standardized using Z-scores before LASSO-Cox regression. LASSO-Cox regression was performed with α set to 1, and the optimal λ.min was determined by 10-fold cross-validation. Variables with non-zero coefficients in each bootstrap iteration were recorded. The selection frequency of each marker was then calculated as the proportion of bootstrap iterations in which the marker was retained.

Markers selected in more than 70% of bootstrap iterations were retained to construct the primary risk score (RS). This threshold was selected to balance variable stability and model parsimony. To further examine the stability of the selection strategy, additional RS models were constructed using alternative selection-frequency thresholds of >50%, >60%, and >80%. These models were compared with the primary >70% RS in sensitivity analyses.

For survival modeling, the RS was analyzed as a continuous variable. For survival curve visualization and subgroup analyses, the optimal cutoff for RS was identified using maximally selected rank statistics. Patients were then stratified into high-RS and low-RS groups according to this cutoff.

Model Construction and Validation

The final RS-plus clinicopathological model was constructed using Cox proportional hazards regression. The model incorporated RS, maximum tumor size, tumor number, PVTT, and MVI. These variables were selected based on clinical relevance, pathological importance, non-overlapping prognostic information, and model parsimony.

Nomograms were constructed to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS and RFS. Model discrimination was evaluated using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curves. Time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) values were calculated at 1, 3, and 5 years. Calibration curves were generated to assess the agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities. Decision curve analysis was used to evaluate the clinical net benefit of the RS-plus model compared with the clinicopathological model without RS, the treat-all strategy, and the treat-none strategy.

The incremental prognostic value of RS was evaluated by adding RS to established transplant criteria and clinicopathological models, including Milan criteria, Hangzhou criteria, UCSF criteria, AFP plus tumor burden, AFP plus tumor burden plus PVTT, and AFP plus tumor burden plus PVTT plus MVI. Tumor burden was defined by maximum tumor size and tumor number. Incremental value was assessed using changes in C-index, likelihood-ratio tests, AIC, and bootstrap 95% confidence intervals for ΔC-index.

Sensitivity Analyses

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the primary RS. First, alternative RS models were constructed using different bootstrap selection-frequency thresholds, including >50%, >60%, >70%, and >80%. Second, marker-redundancy analyses were performed by removing GAR, GPAR, or both GAR and GPAR from the primary RS. Third, endpoint-specific derivation analyses were conducted by deriving RS models using OS and RFS separately and then cross-applying them to both endpoints.

Internal validation of the final RS-plus clinicopathological model was performed using 1000 bootstrap resamples. The optimism-corrected C-index was calculated by subtracting the mean optimism from the apparent C-index.

Exploratory subgroup analyses were also performed to evaluate whether the prognostic association of high RS was consistent across clinically relevant subgroups, including age, sex, AFP level, maximum tumor size, tumor number, total tumor size, TNM stage, PVTT, MVI, pathological differentiation, and Milan, Hangzhou, and UCSF criteria. Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals were estimated for high RS versus low RS within each subgroup. P for interaction was used to assess heterogeneity across subgroup strata.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.4.2. Normally distributed continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation and were compared between groups using independent samples t-tests. Non-normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as median with interquartile range and were compared using Kruskal–Wallis tests. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and were compared using χ2-tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. When multiple pairwise comparisons were performed, P values were adjusted using the Bonferroni method.

Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were compared using the Log rank test. Prognostic factors were evaluated using univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Events per variable were calculated for the primary multivariable Cox models. Model discrimination was assessed using C-index and time-dependent AUC. Calibration curves and decision curve analysis were used to evaluate model calibration and clinical utility. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Potential nonlinear associations between RS and survival outcomes were assessed using restricted cubic spline Cox regression adjusted for maximum tumor size, tumor number, PVTT, and MVI. The nonlinearity test was based on the nonlinear component of the spline model.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 239 patients with HCC who underwent LT were included in the final analysis. According to the optimal RS cutoff, 148 patients were assigned to the low-RS group and 91 patients to the high-RS group. Compared with the low-RS group, patients in the high-RS group had more aggressive tumor characteristics. The high-RS group had higher proportions of advanced TNM stage, MVI, multiple tumors, maximum tumor size >5 cm, total tumor size >8 cm, poor or undifferentiated pathological differentiation, AFP >400 ng/mL, PVTT, and patients beyond Milan, Hangzhou, and UCSF criteria. Incremental Prognostic Value of RS

Adding RS improved the prognostic performance of established transplant criteria and clinicopathological models.

For OS, adding RS increased the C-index of the Milan criteria model from 0.654 to 0.730, with a ΔC-index of 0.076. The C-index of the Hangzhou criteria model increased from 0.621 to 0.732, with a ΔC-index of 0.110. The C-index of the UCSF criteria model increased from 0.675 to 0.741, with a ΔC-index of 0.066. Adding RS also improved clinicopathological models. The C-index increased from 0.682 to 0.737 for AFP plus tumor burden, from 0.705 to 0.736 for AFP plus tumor burden plus PVTT, and from 0.743 to 0.767 for AFP plus tumor burden plus PVTT plus MVI. All likelihood-ratio tests were significant, and AIC decreased after adding RS (Figure 4D and Table S5).

Similar improvements were observed for RFS. Adding RS increased the C-index of the Milan criteria model from 0.657 to 0.724, with a ΔC-index of 0.067. The C-index increased from 0.614 to 0.719 for the Hangzhou criteria model and from 0.678 to 0.736 for the UCSF criteria model. For clinicopathological models, adding RS increased the C-index from 0.675 to 0.730 for AFP plus tumor burden, from 0.704 to 0.735 for AFP plus tumor burden plus PVTT, and from 0.743 to 0.759 for AFP plus tumor burden plus PVTT plus MVI. These findings indicate that RS provided additional prognostic information beyond established transplant criteria and conventional clinicopathological predictors (Figure 4D and Table S5).

Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the primary RS. In the selection-threshold analysis, the >70% model retained four markers, including FAR, GPAR, GAR, and ANRI, and achieved strong discrimination with fewer variables. For OS, the >70% primary RS achieved a C-index of 0.718 and 1-, 3-, and 5-year AUCs of 0.804, 0.720, and 0.761, respectively. For RFS, the corresponding C-index was 0.705, with 1-, 3-, and 5-year AUCs of 0.747, 0.733, and 0.748 (Table S6).

The >60% model showed similar performance but required six variables, whereas the >50% model incorporated eight variables without improving discrimination. The >80% model retained only FAR and GPAR and showed reduced performance. These findings supported the >70% selection-frequency model as the primary RS because it achieved a favorable balance between stability, parsimony, and discrimination (Table S5).

Marker-redundancy analyses showed that removing either GAR or GPAR resulted in only modest performance attenuation. For OS, the C-index decreased from 0.718 to 0.711 after removing GAR and to 0.708 after removing GPAR. For RFS, the corresponding C-index decreased from 0.705 to 0.700 and 0.698. However, simultaneous removal of both GPAR and GAR led to a more evident decrease in discrimination, with C-indices of 0.678 for OS and 0.673 for RFS. These results suggest that GPAR and GAR contributed complementary prognostic information despite partial overlap (Table S6).

Endpoint-specific derivation analyses showed highly consistent performance. The OS-derived RS achieved C-indices of 0.718 for OS and 0.705 for RFS. The RFS-derived RS achieved C-indices of 0.704 for RFS and 0.716 for OS. These findings indicate that the RS was not dependent on a single endpoint definition (Table S6).

Bootstrap internal validation demonstrated limited optimism. The final RS-plus clinicopathological model had an apparent C-index of 0.766 for OS and 0.759 for RFS. After 1000 bootstrap resamples, the optimism-corrected C-indices were 0.756 for OS and 0.749 for RFS, indicating stable internal model performance (Table S7).

Exploratory Subgroup Analyses

Exploratory subgroup analyses showed that the adverse prognostic association of high RS was generally consistent across clinically relevant subgroups.

For OS, high RS was associated with increased mortality risk in the overall cohort, with an HR of 4.09. The direction of association was broadly consistent across age, sex, AFP level, maximum tumor size, tumor number, total tumor size, TNM stage, PVTT, MVI, pathological differentiation, and transplant criteria. No significant interaction was observed across these subgroup strata (Figure S1).

For RFS, high RS was also associated with increased risk of recurrence or death in the overall cohort, with an HR of 4.01. The adverse association of high RS remained directionally consistent across major clinical subgroups, and no significant interaction was observed. These findings suggest that the prognostic effect of RS was not strongly modified by major clinicopathological characteristics. However, given the limited sample size in some subgroups, these analyses should be interpreted cautiously (Figure S2).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of patients with HCC undergoing LT, we developed and internally validated a multidimensional inflammatory-nutritional risk score based on routinely available preoperative laboratory markers. The primary RS consisted of four markers, including FAR, GPAR, GAR, and ANRI, which were selected using a bootstrap LASSO-Cox framework with a prespecified 70% selection-frequency threshold. The RS was significantly associated with both OS and RFS and remained independently prognostic after adjustment for established clinicopathological factors. More importantly, adding RS improved the discriminative performance of conventional transplant criteria and clinicopathological models, including Milan, Hangzhou, UCSF, AFP plus tumor burden, and models incorporating PVTT and MVI.19–21 These findings suggest that the preoperative inflammatory-nutritional state contains clinically meaningful prognostic information that is not fully captured by tumor morphology or pathological features alone.

The selection of FAR, GPAR, GAR, and ANRI is biologically plausible. FAR integrates fibrinogen and albumin, thereby reflecting both coagulation activation and nutritional reserve. Elevated fibrinogen may promote tumor progression through inflammation, angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, and metastatic potential,22,23 whereas reduced albumin reflects impaired nutritional status, systemic inflammation, and compromised hepatic functional reserve. We previously reported the prognostic value of FAR in HCC patients after LT; however, a single biomarker is unlikely to capture the complex host-tumor interaction that determines post-transplant outcomes. The present study extends our previous observation by embedding FAR into a multidimensional score that also includes markers related to cholestasis, hepatic injury, nutritional reserve, and systemic inflammatory status.

GPAR and GAR both incorporate gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase, a marker closely related to cholestasis, oxidative stress, hepatic inflammation, and aggressive tumor biology.24 Their denominators, prealbumin and albumin, represent different but complementary aspects of nutritional and hepatic synthetic function.25 Prealbumin has a shorter half-life than albumin and may be more sensitive to acute changes in nutritional and inflammatory status, whereas albumin reflects more stable hepatic reserve and systemic protein metabolism. Although GPAR and GAR partly share the same numerator, our marker-redundancy sensitivity analyses showed that removal of either marker resulted in only modest performance attenuation, whereas simultaneous removal of both led to a more evident decrease in discrimination. This finding suggests that GPAR and GAR are not merely redundant variables but may capture complementary pathophysiological information. ANRI, which combines aspartate aminotransferase and neutrophil count, may reflect liver injury, inflammatory activation, and tumor-associated host response. Together, the four components of the RS represent a compact but multidimensional signature of coagulation, nutrition, cholestasis, hepatic injury, and inflammation.

A major strength of this study is the use of bootstrap LASSO-Cox selection rather than a single data-driven screening procedure.26 In biomarker studies, variable selection may be unstable, especially when multiple correlated inflammatory and nutritional indices are evaluated simultaneously. To reduce this instability, we used repeated bootstrap resampling to estimate marker selection frequency and selected markers that consistently appeared across iterations. The sensitivity analysis across selection thresholds further supported the choice of the >70% model. The >70% RS retained only four markers but achieved discrimination comparable to the >60% model and better than the >50% and >80% models. Therefore, the primary RS achieved a favorable balance between marker stability, parsimony, and predictive performance. The endpoint-specific sensitivity analysis also demonstrated that OS-derived and RFS-derived RS models showed highly consistent performance, supporting the robustness of the selected marker combination across survival endpoints.

The RS also provided incremental prognostic value beyond established transplant criteria. The Milan, Hangzhou, and UCSF criteria primarily rely on tumor burden and related morphological features, and they have played an important role in candidate selection for LT. However, these criteria do not directly incorporate systemic inflammation, nutritional reserve, coagulation activation, or cholestatic injury. In our study, adding RS improved the C-index of all three transplant criteria for both OS and RFS. Similar improvements were observed when RS was added to clinicopathological models incorporating AFP, tumor burden, PVTT, and MVI. These results indicate that RS provides prognostic information complementary to conventional tumor-centered variables. Clinically, this may be particularly relevant because patients with similar tumor burden can still have heterogeneous post-transplant outcomes due to differences in host inflammatory-nutritional status and hepatic functional reserve.

The final RS-plus clinicopathological model incorporated RS, maximum tumor size, tumor number, PVTT, and MVI. This model achieved favorable discrimination, with apparent C-indices of 0.766 for OS and 0.759 for RFS, and bootstrap-corrected C-indices of 0.756 and 0.749, respectively. The low optimism observed in bootstrap validation suggests that the model was not excessively overfitted despite being derived from a single-center cohort. Calibration analysis showed acceptable agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities, and decision curve analysis demonstrated favorable net benefit across clinically relevant threshold probability ranges. These results suggest that the RS-plus model may provide additional prognostic information for postoperative risk stratification. However, the present study did not test RS-guided surveillance, immunosuppression adjustment, or treatment strategies. Therefore, its clinical use should be considered hypothesis-generating and requires external validation and prospective evaluation.

The exploratory subgroup analyses suggested a generally similar direction of association across clinically relevant strata within the study cohort. The adverse prognostic association of higher RS was directionally consistent across clinically relevant subgroups, including age, sex, AFP level, tumor burden, TNM stage, PVTT, MVI, pathological differentiation, and transplant criteria. Most interaction tests were non-significant, suggesting no strong evidence that the prognostic effect of RS was limited to a specific subgroup. However, these subgroup analyses should be interpreted cautiously because several strata, such as the female subgroup and PVTT-positive subgroup, had limited sample sizes and wide confidence intervals. Therefore, subgroup findings should be considered exploratory rather than confirmatory.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective single-center study, and selection bias cannot be fully excluded. Although bootstrap internal validation showed limited optimism, external validation in independent cohorts is still required before broader clinical application. Second, all inflammatory and nutritional markers were derived from preoperative laboratory tests, and dynamic postoperative changes were not incorporated. Longitudinal changes in inflammation, nutrition, liver function, and immunosuppression may provide additional prognostic information. Third, although we evaluated multiple routinely available inflammatory-nutritional indices, the study did not include molecular, radiological, or immune microenvironmental features. Integration of RS with imaging, pathological, and molecular data may further improve risk stratification. Fourth, the RS was developed using laboratory data from a specific transplant population, and its applicability to patients undergoing hepatectomy, locoregional therapy, or systemic therapy requires separate evaluation. Fifth, Several transplant-specific factors were not fully captured in the current retrospective dataset and may have influenced OS and RFS. These include bridging or downstaging therapy before LT, waiting time, immunosuppressive regimens, antiviral therapy, donor characteristics, early graft function, postoperative complications, treatment after recurrence, and changes in transplant selection criteria or perioperative management during the 10-year study period. Finally, although the RS is biologically interpretable, the mechanistic relationship between the selected markers and post-transplant recurrence remains to be clarified in future translational studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study developed a multidimensional inflammatory-nutritional RS based on routinely available preoperative laboratory biomarkers, including FAR, GPAR, GAR, and ANRI, for patients with HCC undergoing LT. The RS was independently associated with OS and RFS and provided incremental prognostic information beyond established transplant criteria and conventional clinicopathological factors. The preoperative RS may help characterize host inflammatory-nutritional status, whereas the RS-plus clinicopathological model incorporating clinicopathological predictors should be interpreted as a postoperative prognostic model because clinicopathological predictors are generally determined from explant pathology. Although the RS-plus model showed favorable discrimination, calibration, decision-curve performance, and bootstrap internal validation, external validation in independent multicenter cohorts is required before clinical use.
