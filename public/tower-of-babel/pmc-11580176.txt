Revisiting Pyrimidine-Embedded Molecular Frameworks to Probe the Unexplored Chemical Space for Protein–Protein Interactions
Authors: Jeong
Yeon Yoo, Yoona Choi, Heejun Kim, Seung Bum Park
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ConspectusProtein–protein interactions (PPIs) are essential in numerous biological processes and diseases, making them attractive yet challenging drug targets. While many advances have been made in traditional drug discovery, targeting PPIs has been difficult due to a lack of specialized chemical libraries designed to modulate these interactions. Current libraries mainly focus on conventional target proteins like enzymes or receptors as substrate analogs rather than small-molecule modulators targeting PPIs. These traditional drug targets behave differently from PPIs. Conventional druggable targets have relatively small surfaces and binding pockets that have allowed them to be targeted with current libraries, but PPIs behave differently than these traditional drug targets. As a result, there is an urgent need for an innovative approach to expand the druggable space.To address this, we developed a privileged substructure-based diversity-oriented synthesis (pDOS) strategy, aimed at creating maximal skeletal diversity to explore broader biochemical space. Pyrimidine serves as the privileged substructure in our approach, which employs several strategies: (i) silver-catalyzed or iodine-mediated tandem cyclizations to generate pyrimidine-embedded polyheterocycles; (ii) diverse pairing strategies to produce pyrimidodiazepine-containing polyheterocyclic skeletons with enhanced scaffold saturation; (iii) skeletal transformation to develop pyrimidine-fused medium-sized azacycles via chemoselective cleavages or migrations of N–N or C–N bond; (iv) design of small-molecule peptidomimetics that systematically mimic three pivotal protein secondary structures using pyrimidodiazepine-based scaffolds; and (v) identification of pyrimidodiazepine-based small-molecules that allosterically inhibits the interaction between human ACE2 and the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein to block viral entry into host cells.Through these approaches, we generated 39 distinct pyrimidine-embedded frameworks, demonstrating significant molecular diversity validated by chemoinformatic analyses such as Tanimoto similarity and principal moment of inertia (PMI) analysis. This molecular diversity extends pyrimidine structures beyond traditional linear or bicyclic forms, creating polyheterocycles with enhanced 3D structural diversity. These novel frameworks overcome the limitation of simpler privileged scaffolds, offering promising tools for modulating PPIs.Our pDOS approach highlights how privileged structure-embedded polyheterocycles, particularly those based on pyrimidine, can effectively target previously undruggable PPIs. This strategy provides a new direction for drug discovery, allowing for the development of small molecules that operate beyond traditional drug-like rules. In addition to expanding the chemical space for PPI modulation, our pDOS strategy enables the creation of scaffolds that are particularly suited for targeting complex and dynamic protein interfaces. This innovation could significantly impact therapeutic development, offering solutions for previously intractable drug targets. By expanding the scope of pyrimidine-based scaffolds, we have opened up new possibilities for targeting PPIs and advancing chemical biology.This perspective demonstrates the potential outlines of our pDOS strategy in creating structurally diverse frameworks, offering a platform for the discovery of PPI modulators and facilitating the exploration of untapped biochemical spaces in drug development, potentially transforming the way we approach these complex biological interactions.

Introduction

Identifying novel bioactive small molecules is a cornerstone in medicinal chemistry and chemical biology. Protein–protein interactions (PPIs) have gained prominence in the research for new drug candidates and as tools for exploring previously uncharted biological processes due to their critical roles in regulating biological systems and their involvement in diseases such as cancers, metabolic disorders, and neurodegenerative conditions.5 Historically, PPIs were deemed “undruggable” because of their complex networks and characteristics, such as large and flat surfaces. As a result, early efforts relied heavily on peptides or antibodies to modulate PPIs, but these agents suffer from poor stability against proteolysis and limited cell permeability.6

Despite over 650,000 PPIs documented in databases, fewer than 60 PPIs have been utilized as drug targets.7 The FDA approval of small-molecule PPI drugs such as Venetoclax (2016) and Tafamidis (2019) highlights the potential for small molecules in PPI modulation.8 While traditional approaches, such as peptidomimetics and computational strategies, have shown promise, they are often hampered by the lack of structural data for disease-related PPIs, creating challenges in drug discovery.7,8

Innovative approaches such as targeted protein degradation (TPD) and targeted protein upregulation (TPU) have gained prominence in response to these challenges. TPD, exemplified by PROTAC (Proteolysis TArgeting Chimeras) technology, promotes the interaction between a protein of interest and its E3 ubiquitin ligase, leading to its ubiquitination and subsequent proteasome-mediated degradation of the target protein.9 This groundbreaking technology leverages the cell’s natural recycling mechanisms to eliminate disease-causing proteins selectively. On the other hand, TPU, pioneered by our group, selectively upregulates disease-fighting proteins by inhibiting the specific PPI between the protein of interest and its E3 ligase.4 The success of PROTAC and similar proximity-induced regulations hinges on a deep understanding of PPIs’ dynamic and context-dependent nature, which regulate protein homeostasis and provide novel approaches for influencing cellular processes.10 Nevertheless, a universal design strategy for modulating uncharted PPIs remains elusive.

Our strategy for discovering novel PPI modulators focuses on the unbiased, high-throughput evaluation of small-molecule libraries with maximized skeletal diversity. Small-molecule collections rich in three-dimensional (3D) skeletal diversity are invaluable for identifying novel chemical probes and therapeutic agents, especially targeting PPIs with unknown structures.7,11,12 To address this, diversity-oriented synthesis (DOS) has been developed, offering molecular frameworks with maximized skeletal and stereochemical diversity, thus facilitating comprehensive compound screening.13−15

To further enhance both structural diversity and biological relevance, we developed a privileged substructure-based DOS (pDOS) strategy. The approach emphasizes the innovative construction of polyheterocycles containing privileged substructures, expanding the bioactive chemical spaces and enabling the discovery of novel modulators for previously uncharacterized PPIs.16 Despite an overall increase in FDA-approved drugs, the proportion of sp3 carbons has not risen substantially, likely due to the slow progress and inherent challenges in sp3 synthetic chemistry.31−33 Our pDOS strategy offers an efficient solution for generating spatially diverse structures—from flat architectures to those with high sp3 character—centered around biorelevant core structures.

Over the past decade, the pDOS strategy has proven effective in identifying novel small-molecule modulators across a range of biological activities, including antineuroinflammatory effects,17,18 chondrogenesis-inducing activity,19 inhibition of protein–miRNA interaction,20,21 and antiviral activities.3 We have focused on pyrimidine as a privileged structural motif due to its critical role in drug discovery, particularly as a nucleoside analog for kinase inhibitors and adenosine receptor modulators.22

Previous pyrimidine-containing compounds have been predominantly mono- and bicyclic with linear connections, limiting their structural diversity.23−25 By contrast, our approach leverages pyrimidine to generate discrete polyheterocycles, which offer enhanced skeletal diversity and potential for selective PPI modulation. This account highlights our ongoing efforts to design divergent synthesis strategies for creating unprecedented pyrimidine-embedded molecular frameworks to discover novel PPI modulators (Figure 1).

Design and Synthesis of Pyrimidine-Containing Novel Scaffolds

pDOS Strategy for Pyrimidine-Embedded Polyheterocycles

We hypothesized that pyrimidine-embedded polyheterocycles with more than three fused rings would provide a larger and more complex surface, enhancing their potential to selectively modulate PPIs compared to traditional mono- and bicyclic pyrimidine-based molecules. This approach aims to move beyond simply targeting the active site of a single protein and opens up the possibility of engaging broader protein interfaces involved in PPI modulation. Our pDOS strategy was designed to efficiently and divergently synthesize these polyheterocycles (Figure 2), starting from ortho-alkynylpyrimidine carboxaldehyde 1, which could be converted to corresponding imines or hydrazones. pDOS Pathway for Diverse Pyrimidodiazepine-Embedded Polyheterocycles

To further increase 3D spatial diversity, we devised a pDOS strategy to synthesize pyrimidodiazepine-embedded structures. Transition-metal-catalyzed cross-coupling reactions have been pivotal for C–C bond formation in pharmaceutical synthesis.27 While recent focus has shifted toward Csp3–Csp3 coupling, aryl–aryl coupling remains a reliable method.28−31 However, many libraries generated in this way exhibit structural flatness, as indicated by FDA-approved drugs lacking sp3 carbons.32,33 To address this, we incorporated saturated ring systems to pyrimidine structures, focusing on pyrimidodiazepine, which offers greater conformational diversity through its 7-membered diazepine ring.34 This structure is considered privileged in bioactive small molecules, particularly benzodiazepine-based drugs.35 Benzodiazepine, analogous to pyrimidodiazepine, has been extensively studied due to its ability to structurally mimic protein secondary structures, serving as crucial recognition elements in PPIs.36−38

We designed a multifunctionalized pyrimidodiazepine intermediate 3 containing five reactive sites (A–E), which facilitates the synthesis of 16 distinct pyrimidine-embedded scaffolds with high skeletal diversity through selective functional group pairing (Figure 3). For example, A–B pairing produced tetracyclic scaffolds (VI-1 and VI-2) via an intramolecular substitution reaction with the pyrimidyl nitrogen as an internal nucleophile, while B–C pairing with ring-closing metathesis generated tricyclic pyrimidodiazepine-fused scaffolds (VII-1–VII-3) and a unique [7.4.1]-bridged scaffold VIII. The C pairing exploited the dual functionality of the imine moiety as both a nucleophile and an electrophile. Diastereoselective rhodium-catalyzed oxygenative [2 + 2] cycloaddition of intermediates 3 generated the β-lactam-containing scaffold IX-1, and its intramolecular ring closure produced the 5-membered imidazolidinone-fused scaffold IX-2. Additionally, in situ [2,3]-sigmatropic rearrangement enabled the formation of the ring-expanded scaffold X in the (E)-selective manner. In C–D pairing, intramolecular cyclization yielded the rigid [4.2.1]-bridged scaffold XI and heterocycle-fused tricyclic scaffolds XIII (oxazolidinone in XIII-1, aziridine in XIII-2, lactam in XIII-3, and sultam in XIII-4). The synthetically challenging diazocane-fused scaffold XII was also obtained via nucleophilic ring expansion, while D–E pairing resulted in a conformationally restricted [4.3.1]-bridged scaffold XIV via ring-closing metathesis.

This pDOS pathway enabled the efficient synthesis of 16 unprecedented pyrimidine-embedded polyheterocycles with increased structural novelty. Notably, each final compound was prepared in relatively short steps (average 2.2 steps from intermediate 3) and exhibited a higher sp3 carbon content than previously reported flat pyrimidine-containing molecules. The structural novelty of these scaffolds offers significant potential for discovering new bioactive compounds as modulators of unmet therapeutic targets.

To assess the ability of the pyrimidine-based pDOS library with enhanced scaffold saturation to modulate challenging biological targets, such as PPIs, we conducted high-throughput screening (HTS) using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Our screening identified a novel leucyl-tRNA synthetase (LRS)–RagD PPI inhibitor as an anticancer agent from the aziridine-fused pyrimidodiazepine scaffold XIII-2, generated via C–D pairing. This finding underscores the potential of the pyrimidine-embedded pDOS library for selectively modulating previously undruggable targets, which highlights the distinct and diverse nature of pyrimidine-embedded pDOS scaffolds despite sharing a common pyrimidodiazepine structure.

pDOS Pathway for Pyrimidine-Embedded Medium and Bridged Azacycles

We aimed to incorporate medium-sized rings into the pyrimidine-based pDOS library to enhance scaffold saturation and introduce unique 3D structural rigidity. Medium-sized rings (8–11 members), although common in bioactive natural products,39,40 are underrepresented in synthetic drugs due to challenges in synthesis.41,42 Their inherent conformational constraints offer significant pharmacological advantages,43 including improved binding affinity, oral bioavailability, and target selectivity, especially for PPIs.39 Inspired by bioactive molecules like heliannuol A (allelopathic), brazilone (anticoagulant), and kurzichalcone (anticancer), we developed a pDOS strategy to create pyrimidine-embedded medium-sized ring scaffolds, leveraging pyrimidine’s additional heteroatom provides for enhanced noncovalent interaction with biological targets.

Traditional medium-sized ring cyclizations of linear precursors often face entropic and enthalpic barriers.41 To overcome this, we developed an alternative strategy, employing ring expansion reaction of accessible 5- or 6-membered rings via selective bond cleavage or migration.44,45 This approach enabled the preparation of 14 distinct pyrimidine-embedded azacycles, starting from key intermediate 4, which was designed through selective N-quaternization of azacyclic precursors to activate the N–N bridge for divergent synthesis (Figure 4).

We aimed to achieve the synthesis of bridged/medium-sized ring skeletons, which was difficult to access due to the limited synthetic accessibility, enabling PPI modulations via unique 3D constraints in the PPI interfaces. Through four distinct pathways, we successfully generated medium ring-fused scaffolds with diverse structural features. Pathway I used selective N–N bond cleavage to create 8- to 10-membered scaffolds (XV-1–XV-4) through base-promoted iminium formation and hydride-mediated neutralization. Scaffold XV-2 was further diversified into conformationally restricted scaffolds XVI and XVII through intramolecular substitution reactions. Cleavage of the central N–N bond and subsequent intramolecular aminal formation generated tetracyclic scaffold XVIII (pathway II) or unique [4.3.1]-bridged scaffold XIX-1 and [6.3.1]-bridged scaffold XIX-2 (pathway III). Additional N-quaternization of scaffold XIX-1 and its bridge elongation produced [4.3.2]-bridged scaffold XX. In pathway IV, internal nucleophile-triggered dissociation of the C–N bond generated 8- to 9-membered oxadiazacycle-containing scaffolds XXI-1 and XXI-2.

This pDOS pathway enabled the efficient synthesis of 14 synthetically challenging yet biologically relevant medium-sized, conformationally rigid azacycles through ring expansion strategies involving chemoselective N–N bond cleavage or rearrangement reactions in short steps (average 1.9 steps from intermediate 4). The key intermediate 4 was readily accessible via kinetically favorable five-membered ring formation. The chemical library of medium-sized and/or bridged azacycles holds significant potential as novel therapeutic agents and small-molecule PPI modulators due to their unique structural features and 3D conformational rigidity.

Molecular Diversity of Unprecedented Pyrimidine-Embedded Core Skeletons

We conducted chemoinformatics analyses to assess the molecular diversity of our pyrimidine-embedded polyheterocycle library, derived from three distinct pDOS pathways. Using Tanimoto similarity analysis, which measures structural similarity between compounds (score range: 0.00 to 1.00 as 0.00 indicating no similarity and 1.00 indicating identical structures),46,47 we calculated pairwise similarity coefficients for 39 scaffolds with extended connectivity fingerprints (ECFP_6).48 Despite sharing a common pyrimidine core, the scaffolds displayed significant structural diversity, with an average Tanimoto score of 0.17 (Figure 5A). The Tanimoto similarity matrix also revealed that the scaffolds generated through similar synthetic routes (e.g., II-1 to II-4, IV-1 to IV-2, VI-1 to VI-2) had relatively high structural differences. Interestingly, scaffold XII from the pyrimidodiazepine-based pDOS library and scaffold XV-1 from the pyrimidine-embedded medium-sized ring library were structurally identical despite arising from different synthetic routes, demonstrating the versatility of our pDOS strategy. Lower scores across our scaffold set demonstrate the significant molecular diversity achieved in our pDOS approach.

(A) Tanimoto similarity analysis for unprecedented pyrimidine-embedded core skeletons derived from pDOS pathways. (B) List of known pyrimidine-containing compound reference sets and principal moment of inertia (PMI) analysis of our new pyrimidine-embedded polyheterocycles compared with reference sets.

This molecular diversity of our scaffolds was further highlighted by PMI (principal moment of inertia) analysis. Briefly, PMI analysis uses three moments of inertia, and the normalized ratio is obtained by dividing two lower moments of inertia values by the highest, which its three corners of the triangle can define as (0,1), (0.5,0.5), (1,1). At the ends of the three vertices are the representatives 2-butyne (0,1; rod), benzene (0.5,0.5; disc), and adamantane (1,1; sphere).49 For PMI analysis, the energy-minimized conformer of each compound was determined using quantum mechanical calculations with density functional theory (DFT) at the B3LYP/6-31G* level (in silico deprotection of protecting groups was applied as required). Normalized PMI ratios were calculated and plotted on a triangular graph with vertices representing a perfect rod, disc, and sphere. PMI analysis confirmed the 3D shape diversity of our pDOS-based molecules compared to FDA-approved pyrimidine-containing drugs and bioactive pyrimidine compounds as reference sets (Figure 5B, left).49,50

As shown in Figure 5B, most reference compounds were confined to rod- and disc-like regions, indicating their three-dimensional flatness. In contrast, our pDOS scaffolds occupied a broader 3D space with wide distribution in the PMI plot, extending into more spherical regions. These results confirm that our pyrimidine-embedded pDOS compounds exhibit excellent molecular shape diversity, making them distinct and complementary to known pyrimidine-based drugs. Collectively, the Tanimoto and PMI analyses underscore the potential of our pyrimidine-embedded pDOS libraries to effectively modulate underrepresented biological targets, particularly proteins in protein–protein interactions.

Rational Design of Small-Molecule Peptidomimetics Using Pyrimidodiazepines

Our development of pDOS strategies for pyrimidine-containing polyheterocycles aims to expand molecular diversity and facilitate the discovery of novel compounds with unique modes of action, particularly for unexplored PPIs. Pyrimidodiazepine-based scaffolds have shown promise in modulating various biological pathways, which led us to explore their potential in the rational design of peptidomimetics.51 By leveraging the geometric features of pyrimidine-containing polyheterocycles as skeletal templates, we aimed to enhance interprotein recognition for PPI modulation.

PPIs are often mediated by protein secondary structures such as α-helix, β-strand, β-turn, which are critical for PPI interfaces.52 Peptidomimetics seeks to mimic these structures by aligning the backbone α-carbon (Cα) and properly orienting side chains.53,54 Since each secondary structure has distinct geometry with different criteria, developing mimetic scaffolds for different secondary structures has proven challenging,54−56 with early efforts such as Hamilton’s terphenyl mimetic of the α-helix setting a foundation.57−65 However, creating structurally consistent peptidomimetic scaffolds for systematic comparison remains difficult, hindering the evaluation of physiological outcomes across different mimetic designs. To address this, we developed three distinct peptidomimetic scaffolds with similar molecular frameworks to mimic the α-helix, β-strand, and β-turn.

Recent advancements in drug discovery have highlighted the use of macrocyclic peptides, like Merck’s MK-0616, which binds PCSK9 and inhibits its interaction with the low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) to lower LDL cholesterol in hypercholesterolemia treatment.66 After significant structure–activity relationship (SAR) studies to improve the pharmacokinetic properties of the macrocyclic peptides, this pioneering PPI drug MK-0616 successfully proceeded to the Phase 3 clinical stage. Despite its success, MK-0616 still suffers from low oral bioavailability (2%). In contrast, small nonpeptide molecules that mimic protein secondary structures could offer better pharmacokinetic properties, such as higher oral bioavailability. With this in mind, we revisited pDOS strategies using the pyrimidodiazepine moiety, a privileged heterocycle containing both a flat aromatic pyrimidine ring and a flexible sp3-carbon-rich diazepine ring. Our previous work1 showed that modifying the geometrically flexible diazepine ring can significantly alter the molecular structure, making pyrimidodiazepine a valuable scaffold for small-molecule peptidomimetics.

Given that PPI interfaces are hydrophobic7,67 and consist of structurally defined recognition motifs called the hot spot,53 we designed tricyclic pyrimidodiazepine-containing scaffolds with conformational rigidity to reduce entropic penalties during protein binding. These frameworks were tailored to replicate the three major protein secondary structures, with high predictability of their conformation based on computer-aided molecular design. To address the hydrophobic nature of PPI interfaces, our peptidomimetic scaffolds accommodate hydrophobic amino acid side chains, while the nitrogen-rich pyrimidodiazepine core mitigates overall hydrophobicity, preserving drug-like properties.

We virtually generated 41 tricyclic scaffolds based on pyrimidodiazepine, varying in ring size, regiochemistry, and stereochemistry (Figure 6A). These scaffolds were evaluated using computer-aided algorithms to match key structural criteria for α-helices, β-strands, and β-turns by measuring (1) distances between Cα equivalents on mimetic cores and (2) root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) values between Cα of representative peptide secondary structures and their equivalents on mimetic cores. The Cα–Cα distances, which vary among secondary structures, serve as a first-pass criterion for peptidomimetics.53 RMSD values indicate the 3D spatial similarity of Cα equivalents when superimposed with peptide crystal structures, with RMSD values below 1.0 Å indicating structural solid similarity to peptide crystal structures.65,68,69 From the 41 virtual scaffolds, we selected those that met the criteria for structural rigidity, PPI drug-likeness, and synthetic accessibility, incorporating ethylene diamine analogs to enhance structural diversity.

To synthesize the peptidomimetic scaffolds XXII–XXIV, we prepared three building blocks: ethylene diamine for β-turn mimetics and orthogonally protected novel chiral cyclic triamines for β-strand and α-helix mimetics, starting from pyrimidine. The synthetic route involved nucleophilic aromatic substitution of 4-chloropyrimidine-5-carboxaldehyde, followed by intramolecular reductive amination and decoration with amino acid residues (Figure 6B–C). Single crystal X-ray crystallography confirmed that the synthesized scaffolds (XXII–XXIV) adopted the desired conformation that computer-aided in silico analysis predicted.

These scaffolds, with varying 3D spatial coverage, occupy different types of bioactive chemical space. We validated their functionality by testing the same amino acid combinations on three peptidomimetic scaffolds across two phenotypes: lipid-droplet (LD) accumulation and Tau aggregation. Despite the identical amino acid combinations, the β-strand mimetic scaffold only affected LD accumulation, while the α-helix mimetic scaffold only influenced Tau aggregation. These findings demonstrated how distinct peptidomimetic scaffolds can lead to different biological activities, making them valuable tools for investigating uncharted PPIs.

Pyrimidodiazepine-Containing Antiviral PPI Modulators

To validate the broader applicability of our developed scaffolds for modulating other PPIs beyond LRS–RagD and to access their potential as effective drug candidates, we conducted an ELISA-based screening of our pDOS library for inhibitors for the RBD–ACE2 interaction. The RBD–ACE2 interaction plays a critical role in the entry of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2) into host cells, a virus declared a pandemic by the WHO in March 2020. The rapid spread of SARS-CoV-2 created a global public health emergency. Virus infections begin when viral particles bind to cellular receptors on host cells, and inhibiting this initial recognition is a key strategy to viral infection and its transmission.70

SARS-CoV-2 infects cells that express the surface receptor angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) and the transmembrane protease serine 2 (TMPRSS2). ACE2 serves as the receptor for the viral spike protein, while TMPRSS2 facilitates viral entry. Once inside, the host cells undergo pyroptosis, releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as ASC oligomer, IL-1, and ATP, which induce an immune response and can trigger a cytokine storm, leading to multiorgan damage.71

In our search for PPI modulators of the RBD–ACE2 interaction, we screened our in-house polyheterocyclic pDOS library and identified an initial hit compound based on scaffold XXIII, while other pyrimidodiazepine-based scaffolds showed no significant effect on this PPI. To enhance the potency of the initial hit compound, we conducted two rounds of SAR studies (Figure 7A). In the first SAR study, SB27012, which replaced a tert-butyloxycarbonyl group with a cyclopropyl amide, exhibited improved potency with a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) value of 7.7 μM in an ELISA experiment (Figure 7B). Although Remdesivir (RDV), one of the most promising treatments for SARS-CoV-2, showed a slightly better IC50 value (6.0 μM) under the same conditions, our SB27012 demonstrated superior inhibitory activity at a lower concentration (3.13 μM).

(B) Targeting ACE2 with small molecules to suppress the entry of ancestral and variant SARS-CoV-2 into the host cells, without impairing the enzymatic activity of ACE2. The chemical structures of SB27012, SB27041, and SB27047. Reproduced from ref (3). Copyright 2022 Wiley-VCH GmbH.

Despite SB27012’s intense activity in viral infection assays, the emergence of highly mutated SARS-CoV-2 variants, such as alpha, beta, gamma, delta, and omicron, could affect its efficacy due to their enhanced rates of cell transmission.72 To address this, we performed a second round of SAR studies focusing on the R1 and R3 positions, resulting in SB27041 and SB27047, which showed improved potency against these variants (Figure 7).3 While RDV alone, even at high concentrations (25–50 μM), did not fully inhibit viral activity, its synergistic effect was observed when our SB27047 was used at low concentrations (1.5–3.5 μM), leading to complete virus inhibition.

While the exact binding site of the RBD–ACE2 interaction remains to be determined, this study demonstrates that pyrimidodiazepine-based scaffolds from our pDOS library can effectively modulate PPIs. These findings highlight the potential of our molecules as a chemical navigator to identify drug-like PPI modulators and provide a foundation for further research into SARS-CoV-2 and similar viral pathogens.

Concluding Remarks and Future Perspective

Protein–protein interactions (PPIs) are invaluable targets in chemical biology and biomedical research for discovering new therapeutic agents and exploring previously uncharted biological processes. However, the discovery of PPI modulators has been challenging due to the limitations of existing chemical libraries, which are typically biased toward traditional drug-like small molecules designed as substrate analogs targeting active sites of druggable proteins. While our pDOS strategy offers a promising solution, potential limitations need to be addressed, such as generating fewer final compounds compared to combinatorial chemistry, which typically produces large libraries by combining different building blocks on limited scaffolds. Additionally, there is a possibility of multiple hits for a single biological target due to the prevalence of common privileged structures.

To address these challenges, we developed pDOS strategies that construct unique chemical libraries with maximized skeletal diversity and biological relevance, enabling the discovery of novel small molecules targeting PPIs. Our focus has been on enhancing the skeletal diversity of polyheterocycles rather than merely increasing the number of final compounds, as these can be expanded during SAR studies once hit compounds are identified through screening campaigns. Over two decades of research, various strategies have been introduced to construct pDOS libraries, demonstrating that molecules sharing the same privileged structure can still operate effectively across diverse biological contexts.

This article provides an overview and a future perspective on diverse pDOS strategies utilizing pyrimidine as a privileged substructure to target various PPIs. Despite the unique characteristics of PPIs, challenges remain in modulating these interactions. Our pDOS strategies can help address these challenges by focusing on the following:

Despite ongoing efforts, no universal principles for designing small-molecule PPI modulators exist due to the limited understanding of the innate small-molecule ligands regulating PPIs. With over 650,000 documented PPIs, only a tiny fraction has been thoroughly investigated. We proposed a rational design strategy to address this issue, utilizing three differentiated pyrimidodiazepine-based scaffolds to mimic three major protein secondary structures (α-helix, β-strand, and β-turn).

These results highlight strategies for maximizing molecular diversity and demonstrate the potential of pyrimidine-containing scaffolds as versatile PPI modulators. This approach opens new possibilities for designing small molecules with pharmacokinetic stability and improved accessibility, which are capable of modulating PPIs. We believe that our pDOS strategy and the chemical library will significantly contribute to expanding the PPI-relevant chemical space, ultimately allowing these molecules to play critical roles in various biological processes.

In conclusion, the pDOS strategy significantly advances the discovery of novel small molecules that target PPIs. By expanding chemical space and enhancing the biological relevance of these compounds, our approach holds promise for uncovering new therapeutic agents and deepening our understanding of complex biological processes. The future of PPI modulation is bright, and our pyrimidine-based chemical library is well-positioned to play a crucial role in this exciting area of research.
