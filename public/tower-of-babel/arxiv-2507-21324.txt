Abstract
We report the discovery of activity emanating from (18916) 2000 OG 44 (alternately designated 1977 SD), a minor planet previously reported to be both an extinct comet or an ACO . We observed 2000 OG 44 with a thin tail oriented towards the coincident anti-solar and anti-motion vectors (as projected on the sky) in images we acquired on UT 2023 July 24 and 26 with the APO 3.5 m ARC telescope (New Mexico, USA). We also include observations made in Arizona with the VATT at the MGIO and the Lowell Observatory LDT near Happy Jack. We performed dynamical simulations that reveal 2000 OG 44 most likely originated in the Oort cloud, arriving within the last 4 Myr. We find 2000 OG 44 , which crosses the orbits of both Jupiter and Mars, is at present on an orbit consistent with a JFC . We carried out thermodynamical modeling that informed our broader diagnosis that the observed activity is most likely due to volatile sublimation.



I
Introduction

Comets are not expected to exhibit tails or comae at all times. Most commonly, comets will return to a quiescent state when too distant from the Sun for sufficient energy to induce sublimation for the involved ice species. These same objects may reactivate when enough energy is available, when they are closer to the Sun. Some objects on orbits consistent with comet-class objects, including short-period comets like the
JFC
, have never been observed to be active, despite extensive observational efforts. Some of these objects,
the asteroids on cometary orbits (
ACO
;
Y. R. Fernández et al. 2005
;
J. Licandro et al. 2006
),
are thought to be asteroids that were perturbed onto cometary orbits, while others, such as
dormant comets
(
Q.-Z. Ye et al. 2016
)
,
extinct comets
(
Y. R. Fernández et al. 2001
)
,
dead comets or cometary nuclei
(
P. L. Lamy et al. 2004
)
,
dark comets
(
D. Z. Seligman et al. 2023
;
A. G. Taylor et al. 2024
)
,
or
Manx comets
(
K. J. Meech et al. 2014
)
,
are thought to have started out as comets but are no longer active. Inactivity may be permanent, for example resulting from volatile depletion, or temporary, such as insulation and suffocation by dust or other materials that cover volatiles, thereby quenching the process by reducing available energy
(
E. Kührt & H. U. Keller 1994
)
.

Some inactive bodies have later been found to display activity. Late activity detection may be the result of any combination of improved observing geometry (e.g., geocentric and heliocentric distance), enhanced observing circumstances (e.g., larger aperture telescope), or onset of activity (due to, for example, perihelion passage), such as (551023) 2012 UQ

MMR
(

(

Both the

(
K. Volk & R. Malhotra 2008
;
V. V. Emel’yanenko et al. 2013
)
, are dynamically unstable populations with lifetimes on the order of tens of thousands of years
(
H. F. Levison & M. J. Duncan 1997
)
. To date, roughly 700
JFC
and 20 active quasi-Hildas have been identified. Study of volatile-bearing minor planets helps us understand the origins of terrestrial water
(
A. Morbidelli et al. 2000

H. H. Hsieh & D. Jewitt 2006
;
P. Hartogh et al. 2011
;
C. O. Chandler 2022

and informs planetary defense

It is thought that only a handful of Oort cloud comets could have been captured

As mentioned in Section

Orbital parameters (Table
2
) and measured properties (Table
3
) are provided in the Appendix.
At the time 2000 OG
44
was discovered (UT 2000 July 30), it was at a heliocentric distance
r_{H}=2.109
au, inbound to perihelion with a
\nu=292^{\circ}
true anomaly angle.

One parameter especially significant for our calculations and activity assessment is the rotation period of 2000 OG
44
.
G. M. Szabó et al. 2020
acquired 670 frames of 2000 OG
44
with the Kepler spacecraft as part of K2 Campaign C14, with observations spanning UT 2017 June 25 to July 12, when 2000 OG
44
was at heliocentric distance
4.196<r_{H}<4.271
au, and true anomaly angle
132.6^{\circ}<\nu<134.1^{\circ}
.
They produced a phase-folded lightcurve and found 2000 OG

\sim

IV
Thermodynamical Modeling

We conducted thermodynamical modeling to inform our activity mechanism analysis and assess prior posits that 2000 OG
44
should be inactive due to desiccation. Our model
(
C. O. Chandler et al. 2021
)
follows
H. H. Hsieh et al. 2015
in describing the temperature of an airless body on an orbit comparable to 2000 OG
44
. Here we utilize the measured geometric albedo (
A=0.045\pm 0.041
;
A. K. Mainzer et al. 2019
) to compute an estimated Bond albedo (
A_{\mathrm{B}}\approx\frac{2}{3}A\approx 0.03
) in our calculations, but consider a broad range of rotation scenarios despite the previously reported 22-day period.

The modeled temperatures are bound by the thermophysical extremes:
\chi=1
(solid line) the “flat slab” case, and
\chi=4
(dashed line), the isothermal approximation. 2000 OG
44
has a reported period of
\sim
22 days, thus temperatures are more likely to be near the
\chi=1
line.

We iteratively solve the equations of
C. O. Chandler et al. 2020
and input a range of temperatures that, in turn, return corresponding distances for a given equilibrium temperature. We then produce an inverse function that provides temperature as a function of heliocentric distance for a given set of parameters. As the albedo for 2000 OG
44
has been measured (
A\approx 0.05
; Table
3
), we hold this value constant. We compute temperatures corresponding to the thermophysical extremes of
\chi=1
and
\chi=4
, as shown in Figure
2
. While
G. M. Szabó et al. 2020
found the rotation period of 2000 OG
44
to be over 22 days, so
\chi=1
(a “flat slab”) is a more apt value, we include the full range of temperatures here in case the rotation period is revised in the future.

We combine our thermodynamical modeling results in an “observability” plot (Figure
2
) that helps us find correlated events and identify potential observational biases. For example, activity is most likely detected when the object is brightest, i.e., at opposition and perihelion, with the caveat the object must have been visible at night from a given observatory. For sublimation-driven activity, observing an object at perihelion, where the temperature is warmest, helps maximize the likelihood of activity detection when the activity is caused by volatile sublimation. Figure
2
shows the previous two perihelion passages (2015, 2008) took place during sub-optimal observing conditions (i.e., observable hours from either hemisphere, fainter apparent magnitude). However, when 2000 OG
44
was discovered, it was both bright and observable. Thus, the absence of observed activity suggests the object was quiescent during that apparition.

Thermal fracture is another potential activity mechanism, whereby extreme temperatures or temperature swings cause a body to crack, releasing material in the process. This is thought to be a possible mechanism responsible for the Geminid meteor stream parent (3200) Phaethon, which experiences temperatures from
\sim
195 K to over 800 K
(
K. Ohtsuka et al. 2009
;
J. Li & D. Jewitt 2013
)
. However, 2000 OG
44
reaches
\sim 310
K at perihelion for the prescribed
\chi=1
, which, along with the relatively mild temperature swings (
\sim
100 K), suggests that thermal fracture is not responsible for the activity we observed.

Throughout its present-day orbit, 2000 OG
44
remains warmer than 145 K, the gigayear-timescale surface water ice survival threshold for small solar system bodies
(
N. Schorghofer 2008
;
C. Snodgrass et al. 2017
)
. In Section
V
we learn 2000 OG
44
is not stable for such a long period. Even if 2000 OG
44
was stable for such a period, we cannot rule out sublimation because it is still possible for subsurface ice as shallow as a few cm to survive
(
N. Schorghofer 2008
;
D. Prialnik & E. D. Rosenberg 2009
)
which, in turn, can be excavated or exposed by another event, such as an impact, discussed further in Section
VII
.

V
Dynamical Study

Exploring the dynamical history of 2000 OG
44
can further our understanding of the body’s potential composition, as well as inform our diagnosis of the underlying activity mechanism. Previously,
R. Gil-Hutton & E. Garcia-Migani 2016
found 2000 OG
44
had an orbit consistent with a Centaur prior to -10.2 kyr, and was in the
TNO
(
TNO
) region prior to -27 kyr, at
a>300
au
(
E. A. García Migani 2019
)
. Moreover, they determined 2000 OG
44
could have been in an orbit with a perihelion distance under 1 au for
429\pm 21
years, which they describe as significant because objects with
q<1
au for
>1
kyr are likely to be dormant/desiccated, and that 2000 OG
44
is itself likely depleted. However, the
R. Gil-Hutton & E. Garcia-Migani 2016
study was carried out nearly a decade ago, so we conducted a fresh dynamical investigation that incorporated the most up-to-date orbital elements known for 2000 OG
44
, as provided by JPL Horizons
(
J. D. Giorgini et al. 1996
)
.

We carried out dynamical simulations with the
Rebound
package
(
H. Rein & S.-F. Liu 2012
)
and the
Trace

(
T. Lu et al. 2024
)
integrator. We included the Sun and all of the planets (except for Mercury), and generated 500 orbital clones based on a Gaussian distribution of the orbital uncertainties (Table
2
) as provided by JPL Horizons
(
J. D. Giorgini et al. 1996
)
. We ran our simulations for
\pm 4
Myr with a nominal 0.02 yr timestep and, crucially, variable timestep capability to properly account for close encounters.

Key results are provided in Figure
3
, starting with general orbital configuration of 2000 OG
44
(Figure
3
a) as viewed from above the plane of the solar system. Figure
3
b shows 2000 OG
44
in the Jupiter co-rotating reference frame, a technique we employ to help distinguish between
QHA
and
JFC

(
C. O. Chandler et al. 2022
;
W. J. Oldroyd et al. 2023a
, e.g.,)
. In Figure
3
c and b 2000 OG
44
can be seen periodically crossing the outer orbit of Mars. Notably, while 2000 OG
44
fully crosses the orbit of Jupiter (Figure
3
panels a – c), at present (
\pm
200 yr) the minor planet does not typically experience close encounters with the gas giant, where it normally stays exterior to one Jupiter Hill radius (Figure
3
d).

Figure
3
e highlights 2000 OG
44
’s longitude of perihelion precession on a roughly 20 kyr cycle. This indicates 2000 OG
44
undergoes a period of close encounters with Jupiter that takes place roughly every 40 kyr. The next window of such encounters with Jupiter will be in about 35 kyr.

Despite these periods of Jovian encounters, when we consider the dynamical history of 2000 OG
44
going back 4 Myr (Figure
3
f), we find that the vast majority (99.2%) of clones originate from the Oort cloud (the remaining 0.8% were
JFC
). We also see that 2000 OG
44
most likely transitioned to the
JFC
regime some 750 kyr ago, with 100% of the clones belonging to the
JFC
at -160 kyr.

Our results differ substantially from those of
R. Gil-Hutton & E. Garcia-Migani 2016
(especially their Figure 1, which shows the semimajor axis, perihelion, and aphelion evolution). We attribute these discrepancies to several factors, including a significant difference in orbital elements (e.g., the current value of
a
is
>0.01
au further out than their value). Moreover, their Bulirsch-Stoer
(
R. Bulirsch & J. Stoer 1966
)
integrator was configured for a fixed one-day timestep, which may be inadequate for the close Jovian encounters encountered by 2000 OG
44
.

2000 OG
44

VI
Dynamical Classification

A commonly employed tool to distinguish between dynamical classes is the Tisserand Parameter with respect to Jupiter, which relates the influence of Jupiter over a body’s orbit by

T_{\mathrm{J}}=\frac{a_{\mathrm{J}}}{a}+2\cos\left(i\right)\sqrt{\frac{a\left(1-e^{2}\right)}{a_{\mathrm{J}}}},

(1)

where
a
and
a_{\mathrm{J}}
are the semi-major axes of 2000 OG
44
and Jupiter, respectively,
e
is the orbital eccentricity of 2000 OG
44
, and
i
is the orbital inclination of 2000 OG
44
. Cometary bodies typically have
T_{\mathrm{J}}<3

(
H. F. Levison 1996
)
, though the boundary is inexact
(
H. H. Hsieh & N. Haghighipour 2016
)
. Relevant here is the ambiguity between the
JFC
and the quasi-Hilda objects — bodies that have orbits similar to asteroid (153) Hilda but are not themselves bound within the stable interior 3:2
MMR
with Jupiter. Dynamical modeling (Figure
3
b) is needed to distinguish between the two, as we describe in
C. O. Chandler et al. 2022
and
W. J. Oldroyd et al. 2023b
.
2000 OG
44
has previously been reported as a
QHC
candidate
(
R. Gil-Hutton & E. Garcia-Migani 2016
;
J. Correa-Otto et al. 2023
)
and a Hilda asteroid in 3:2
MMR
with Jupiter
P. Kankiewicz & I. Włodarczyk 2006
.

To explore the quasi-Hilda membership of 2000 OG
44
we consider here first the
I. Toth 2006
excitation parameter

\mathcal{E}=\sqrt{e^{2}+\sin^{2}(i)},

(2)

where
e
and
i
are the orbital eccentricity and inclination.
I. Toth 2006
defines the quasi-Hildas as
2.90<T_{\mathrm{J}}<3.04
and
0.18<\mathcal{E}<0.36
. The present-day values are
e=0.584
and
i=7.396^{\circ}
, which results in an
\mathcal{E}=0.598
. For historic values we make use of the eccentricity and inclination reported in (1) the
MPEC
(
MPEC
) 2000-S65
e=0.5808864
,
i=7.32620^{\circ}

(
G. Hahn et al. 2000
)
, and (2)
R. Gil-Hutton & E. Garcia-Migani 2016
(
e=0.586
,
i=7.42^{\circ}
), which result in
\mathcal{E}=0.595
and
\mathcal{E}=0.600
, respectively. While both values fall outside the mean
\mathcal{E}=0.27\pm 0.09
quasi-Hilda excitement range described in
I. Toth 2006
, they point out notable outlier members, such as 77P/Longmore (
\mathcal{E}=0.547
) and 246P/NEAT (
\mathcal{E}=0.398
). Thus, while 2000 OG
44
does not have a typical
QHC
excitation
\mathcal{E}
metric, this property alone cannot rule out membership in the population, but 2000 OG
44
also has
T_{\mathrm{J}}=2.735
, well outside the
I. Toth 2006
prescribed range.

A second method for identifying quasi-Hildas proposed by
I. Toth 2006
(also adopted by
R. Gil-Hutton & E. Garcia-Migani 2016
) defines
QHC
by their radial distance from a point in Lagrangian
(k,h)
space, described by

k=e\cos\left(\bar{\omega}-\bar{\omega}_{\mathrm{J}}\right)\ \mathrm{and}\ h=e\sin\left(\bar{\omega}-\bar{\omega}_{\mathrm{J}}\right),

(3)

where
e
is the orbital eccentricity of the body, and
\bar{\omega}
and
\bar{\omega_{\mathrm{J}}}
are the longitude of perihelion for the object and Jupiter, respectively. We calculated the
(k,h)
distance of 2000 OG
44
to be 0.518, well outside the
I. Toth 2006
prescribed range of an object being within a circle of radius 0.24 centered at
(k,h)=(0.075,0)
. Thus, by the
(k,h)
metric, 2000 OG
44
does not qualify as a
QHC
.

2000 OG
44
has
T_{\mathrm{J}}=2.735
(primarily due to its high eccentricity), which, along with our dynamical modeling, leads us to classify the object primarily as a
JFC
. Moreover, with a perihelion distance
q=1.593
au, interior to Mars’ aphelion (
Q_{\mathrm{M}}=1.666
au) but exterior to Mars’ semi-major axis (
a_{\mathrm{M}}=1.530
au), 2000 OG
44
is a Mars-crossing body of the “outer grazer” sub-type.

VII
Summary and Discussion

We observed 2000 OG
44
, an object that had previously been scrutinized for over 20 years in searches for cometary activity, yet none was found until this work. We found the object to be exhibiting a distinct, thin comet tail in the coincident anti-solar and anti-motion directions (as projected on the sky) in observations we carried out with
ARCTIC
on the 3.5 m
ARC
at
APO
on UT 2023 July 24 and 26 (Section
II
). At that time, 2000 OG
44
was at a heliocentric distance
r_{H}=1.649
au, inbound (true anomaly angle
\nu=338^{\circ}
) to its perihelion passage (
q=1.604
au).

Our dynamical modeling (Section
V
) indicates 2000 OG
44
originated in the Oort cloud and transitioned to a
JFC
orbit at least 100 kyr ago. We also saw that 2000 OG
44
regularly enters periods of close encounters with Jupiter, with the next cycle due in approximately 35 kyr. 2000 OG
44
has experienced on the order of 100,000 perihelion passages since it arrived in its present orbit (Figure
3
f), far in excess of the more typical
JFC
limit of
N_{q}=1600
(equating to about 12,000 years;
H. F. Levison & M. J. Duncan 1997
).

We carried out thermodynamical modeling (Section
IV
), informed by past ground- and space-based measurements found in the literature, and estimate that 2000 OG
44
experiences temperatures ranging from approximately 100 K to 300 K. Considering the observed 22-day rotation period
(
G. M. Szabó et al. 2020
)
, at all times 2000 OG
44
is at an equilibrium temperature above the 145 K surface water ice gigayear survival threshold
(
N. Schorghofer 2008
;
C. Snodgrass et al. 2017
)
. However, sublimation is still possible because
(1) it is probable that 2000 OG
44
arrived much more recently than this timespan (Figure
3
f) due to its dynamical behavior,
(2) subsurface water ice could still be present, even on longer timescales
(
N. Schorghofer 2008
;
D. Prialnik & E. D. Rosenberg 2009
)
, and
(3) if 2000 OG
44
has a faster rotation rate, the object spends a significant fraction of its time in a region below 145 K (Figure
2
), in which case water ice would survive.
Regardless of the rotation rate, the temperature range and maximum temperature are unlikely to cause thermal fracture akin to that experienced by active bodies such as (3200) Phaethon
(
K. Ohtsuka et al. 2009
;
J. Li & D. Jewitt 2013
)
. Moreover, rotational disruption is highly unlikely to be a viable activity mechanism given its reported long rotation period.

Another source of cometary activity is impact events, suggested in cases such as e.g., (596) Scheila
(
F. Moreno et al. 2011
)
and, notably, definitively caused in the case of Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) target Dimorphos, the moon of (65803) Didymos
(
R. T. Daly et al. 2023
)
. It is possible, but very unlikely (once every
\sim
300,000 years for a 100 m impactor;
C. G. Díaz & R. Gil-Hutton 2008
) that 2000 OG
44
experienced an impact event that caused the activity we observe. We find it more likely that volatile sublimation is the primary activity driver given the apparent onset of activity near its perihelion passage, and additional observations of activity in the future would lend further support to this diagnosis. Moreover, 2000 OG
44
has been classified as a D-type object (Table
3
;
J. Licandro et al. 2018
), which are thought to be primordial bodies that migrated inwards from the Kuiper belt
(
H. F. Levison et al. 2009
)
, and thus would likely contain ices.

2000 OG
44
has previously been dynamically classified as a comet and quasi-Hilda object, and qualifies by the definitions of
I. Toth 2006
and
R. Gil-Hutton & E. Garcia-Migani 2016
. Our analysis (Section
VI
) suggests that the object is, at present, on an orbit consistent with a member of the
JFC
.

We encourage follow-up observations to help characterize the activity circumstances and morphology, noting that, if cometary activity is detectable again during this apparition, this will be the last opportunity until the object approaches perihelion again in late-2030. We observed 2000 OG
44
active during the inbound portion of its orbit, so we anticipate it will again be active again at
\nu\gtrsim 330^{\circ}
.
