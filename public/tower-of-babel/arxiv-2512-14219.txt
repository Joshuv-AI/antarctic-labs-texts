Analysis of a finite element method for second order uniformly elliptic PDEs in non-divergence form
Weifeng Qiu

Abstract
. We propose one finite element method for both second order linear uniformly elliptic PDE in non-divergence form and the elliptic Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation. For the linear elliptic PDE in non-divergence form, we consider two scenarios of the matrix coefficient matrix A A . One is A A is uniformly continuous. The other is A A is discontinuous but γ ​ A \gamma A is dominated by I d I_{d} where γ \gamma is a positive weight function. We prove that optimal convergence in discrete W 2 , p W^{2,p} -norm of the numerical approximation to the strong solution for 1 < p ≤ 2 1<p\leq 2 on convex polyhedra in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} ( d = 2 , 3 d=2,3 ). If the domain is a two dimensional non-convex polygon, p p is valid in a neighbourhood of 4 3 \frac{4}{3} . We also prove the well-posedness of strong solution in W 2 , p ​ ( Ω ) W^{2,p}(\Omega) for both linear elliptic PDE in non-divergence form and the HJB equation for 1 < p ≤ 2 1<p\leq 2 on convex polyhedra in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} ( d = 2 , 3 d=2,3 ) and for p p in an open interval starting from 1 1 and including 4 3 \frac{4}{3} on two dimensional non-convex polygon. Furthermore, we relax the assumptions on the continuity of coefficients of the HJB equation, which have been widely used in literature.

Introduction

We mainly consider optimal convergence to the strong solution in
W_{h}^{2,p}(\Omega)
-norm
(see (
7
)) of a finite element method for both second
order linear uniformly elliptic PDE in non-divergence form (
2
) and the elliptic
Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation (
3
) on Lipschitz polyhedral domains in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

(
d=2,3
). We consider the well-posedness of the strong solution in
W^{2,p}(\Omega)
of
(
2
) and (
3
) as well. The ranges of
p
are described below:

(1a)

\displaystyle\text{(PDE analysis) }p\in(1,2]\text{ if }\Omega\text{ is a convex polyhedra }(d=2,3),

\displaystyle\qquad\text{ while }p\in(1,\frac{4}{3}+\epsilon_{2})\text{ if }\Omega\text{ is a non-convex polygon in }\mathbb{R}^{2};

(1b)

\displaystyle\text{(Numerical analysis) }p\in(1,2]\text{ if }\Omega\text{ is a convex polyhedra }(d=2,3),

\displaystyle\qquad\text{ while }p\in(\frac{4}{3}-\epsilon_{1},\frac{4}{3}+\epsilon_{2})\text{ if }\Omega\text{ is a non-convex polygon in }\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Here
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
are two positive numbers up to the domain
\Omega

(see Lemma
2.1
).

The linear elliptic PDE in non-divergence form is find the solution
u
satisfying

(2)

\displaystyle A:D^{2}u+\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla u+cu=f\text{ in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega.

We assume that
A=A(\boldsymbol{x})\in[L^{\infty}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}
is a symmetric and uniformly positive definite
matrix-valued function on
\Omega
,
\boldsymbol{b}\in[L^{\infty}(\Omega)]^{d}
, and
c\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)

with
c\leq 0
.

The elliptic Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation is to find
the solution
u
satisfying

(3)

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha\in\Lambda}[A^{\alpha}:D^{2}u+\boldsymbol{b}^{\alpha}\cdot\nabla u+c^{\alpha}u-f^{\alpha}]=0\text{ in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega.

Here the non-empty set
\Lambda
is called index set.
We assume that there are positive constants
\nu\leq\overline{\nu}
such that
for any
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
\alpha\in\Lambda
,

(4)

\displaystyle\nu|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{2}\leq\Sigma_{1\leq i,j\leq d}a_{ij}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x})\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\leq\overline{\nu}|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{2}\text{ and }c^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq 0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega\text{ almost everywhere};

(5)

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha\in\Lambda}\|\boldsymbol{b}^{\alpha}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\sup_{\alpha\in\Lambda}\|c^{\alpha}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}<+\infty.

We further assume that there is a subset
E_{0}\subset\Omega
with zero

d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure such that

(6)

\displaystyle\text{there is a countable subset }\tilde{\Lambda}\subset\Lambda\text{ satisfying that}

\displaystyle\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega\setminus E_{0}\text{ and }\forall\alpha\in\Lambda\text{ and }\forall\epsilon>0,\text{there is }\tilde{\alpha}\in\tilde{\Lambda}\text{ such that}

\displaystyle\qquad|A^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x})-A^{\tilde{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{x})|+|\boldsymbol{b}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{b}^{\tilde{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{x})|+|c^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x})-c^{\tilde{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{x})|+|f^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x})-f^{\tilde{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{x})|<\epsilon.

Here, we denote by
|B|=\left(\Sigma_{1\leq i,j\leq d}|b_{ij}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

for any matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

The linear elliptic PDE in non-divergence form (
2
) is the starting point to
study the elliptic HJB equation (
3
), because (
3
) becomes
(
2
) if the index set
\Lambda
contains a single element.
In addition, (
2
)appears in the linearization and numerical methods of fully non-linear
second order PDEs
[
5
,
16
,
31
]
.
The HJB equation (
3

associated with stochastic control problems, which have widespread applications across engineering, physics,
economics, and finance
[

,
27

1.1.
Literature review

Unlike partial differential equations expressed in divergence form, the theory governing linear equations in non-divergence
form, and more broadly, fully nonlinear PDEs, relies on alternative solution frameworks, including classical solutions,
viscosity solutions, and strong solutions.

There are several papers

[
1
,
3
,
6
,
8
,
12
,
15
,
19
,
27
,
29
,
33
,
32
,
37
]

focusing on convergence to the viscosity solutions.
In the Barles–Souganidis framework
[
1
]
, monotonicity serves as a fundamental principle underpinning
numerical schemes to ensure convergence to viscosity solutions of fully nonlinear partial differential equations.
This concept is also applicable to the monotone finite difference methods for the HJB equation
[
3
,
19
]
.
A monotone finite element like scheme for the HJB equation was proposed in
[
6
]
.
In
[
32
]
, Nochetto and Zhang introduced a two-scale method based on the integro-differential
formulation of (
2
), where a discrete Alexandroff–Bakelman–Pucci estimate
was established. This two-scale method
[
32
]
was extended to the Isaacs equation
[
37
]
.
However, up to our knowledge, all these numerical methods for convergence to the viscosity are low
order. In addition, most of them have stencils which increase
under mesh refinement, such that the corresponding linear system may not be sparse.

With respect to numerical methods for the convergence to the strong solutions,
there are tree main categories.

(I)

A
can be discontinuous but
\gamma A
dominated by
I_{d}
where

\gamma
is a positive weight function
.
In
[
39
]
, authors introduced and analyzed a Discontinuous Galerkin
(DG) method for (
2
). The main assumptions are
the coefficients
A
is dominated by
I_{d}
after being multiplied by a
positive weight function
\gamma
(Cordès condition) and the domain
\Omega
is convex.
The stability and optimal rate of convergence in discrete
H^{2}
-norm were
proven in
[
39
]
. Following this direction, there are several papers

[
2
,
21
,
43
]
. The technique in
[
39
]

has been extended to the HJB equation (
3
) in

[
40
,
22
,
43
]

but with assumption that
A^{\alpha}\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega}\times\Lambda)
and the index set
\Lambda
is a compact metric space.
It seems that numerical methods along this direction
may only be able to obtain optimal rate of convergence with respect to
discrete
H^{2}
-type norm (
p=2
).

(II)

A
is globally continuous
. In
[
14
]
, authors
introduced and analyzed a
C^{0}
-conforming DG method for
(
2
). The main assumptions are
A\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})

and the domain
\Omega
has
C^{1,1}
boundary. They followed the approach in

[
24
, Chapter
9
]
to prove that their numerical
method could achieve stability and optimal rate of convergence

-norm for any

However, this numerical method
and its variant - a DG method in
[
17
]
can not deal with discontinuous

along mesh interfaces
(
u_{h}
is the numerical solution). Therefore, the numerical methods in

[
14
,
17

can not be applied for the HJB equation (
3
).

(III)

Residual minimization methods
.
The main advantage of residual minimization methods
[
35
,
41
,
23
]

is that they can easily inherit the a priori estimate of PDE operators of (
2
)
and (
3
). However, the minimal requirements of the regularity of
true solution

for these methods are highly
affected by the choice of the norm to measure the residual in their designs.
It is not clear which norm to choose for the two scenarios mentioned above.

A natural question is whether it is possible to design one numerical method for both
linear elliptic PDE in non-divergence form and the elliptic HJB equation on Lipschitz
polyhedra in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d=2,3
) and achieve optimal convergence in discrete

-norm to the strong solution with a wide range of

Mesh and space notation

We denote by

Let

(d-1)
-dimensional boundary mesh faces in

\mathcal{F}_{h}:=\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I}\cup\mathcal{F}_{h}^{B}

(d-1)
-dimensional mesh faces in

Let

Without loss of generality, we assume that the global labelling number of
K^{+}
is larger than that of
K^{-}

We introduce the jump and average of a scalar or vector valued function

\displaystyle[v]|_{F}:=v^{+}-v^{-},\qquad\{v\}|_{F}=\frac{1}{2}\left(v^{+}+v^{-}\right),

where

On a boundary mesh face

,
we define

.

We define

and

with integer

. We also define the piecewise Sobolev space with respect to the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
:

\displaystyle W^{s,p}(\mathcal{T}_{h}):=\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}W^{s,p}(K),\qquad W_{h}^{p}:=W^{2,p}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\cap W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega),\qquad W_{h}^{s,p}(D):=W^{s,p}(\mathcal{T}_{h})|_{D}.

For a given subdomain
D\subset\Omega
. we define
V_{h}(D)\subset V_{h}
and

as the subspaces which vanish outside of
D
by

\displaystyle V_{h}(D):=\{v\in V_{h}:v_{h}|_{\Omega\setminus D}=0\},\qquad W_{h}^{p}(D):=\{v\in W_{h}^{p}:v|_{\Omega\setminus D}=0\}.

Associated with
D\subset\Omega
, we define a semi-norm on
W_{h}^{2,p}(D)
for

:

(7)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{W_{h}^{2,p}(D)}=\|D_{h}^{2}v\|_{L^{p}(D)}+\left(\Sigma_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I}}h_{F}^{1-p}\|[\nabla v]\|_{L^{p}(F\cap\overline{D})}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

\displaystyle\|v\|_{W_{h}^{2,p}(\Omega)}=\|D_{h}^{2}v\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+\left(\Sigma_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I}}h_{F}^{1-p}\|[\nabla v]\|_{L^{p}(F)}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

For any subdomain

whose inner radius bigger than
2h
and any

, we
introduce the following mesh-dependent semi-norm

\displaystyle\|w\|_{L_{h}^{p}(\Omega)}:=\sup_{v_{h}\in V_{h}(D)}\dfrac{(w,v_{h})_{D}}{\|v_{h}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(D)}},

where

For any

.

Definition 2.1
.

We denote by

2.2.
Finite element method for linear elliptic PDE in non-divergence form and HJB equation

2.2.1.
FEM for linear elliptic PDE in non-divergence form

We define a function
\gamma
on
\Omega
to be

(12)

\displaystyle\gamma(\boldsymbol{x})=\dfrac{\text{Tr}A(\boldsymbol{x})}{|A(\boldsymbol{x})|^{2}}=\dfrac{\Sigma_{1\leq i\leq d}a_{ii}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\Sigma_{1\leq i,j\leq d}(a_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}},\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega.

Obviously, the linear elliptic PDE in non-divergence form (
2
) is equivalent to

(13)

\displaystyle\tilde{A}:D^{2}u+\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}\cdot\nabla u+\tilde{c}u=\tilde{f}\text{ in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega,

where
\tilde{A}=\gamma A
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}=\gamma\boldsymbol{b}
,
\tilde{c}=\gamma c
and
\tilde{f}=\gamma f
.

The finite element method for linear elliptic PDE in non-divergence form (
2
) is to find

u_{h}\in V_{h}
satisfying

(14)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\tilde{A}:\overline{\nabla}_{h}(\nabla u_{h})+\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}\cdot\nabla u_{h}+\tilde{c}u_{h})v_{h}d\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\gamma(\tilde{A}:\overline{\nabla}_{h}(\nabla u_{h})+\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}\cdot\nabla u_{h}+\tilde{c}u_{h})v_{h}d\boldsymbol{x}=\int_{\Omega}\gamma fv_{h}d\boldsymbol{x}=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{f}v_{h}d\boldsymbol{x},\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}.

2.2.2.
FEM for the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation

For any
\alpha\in\Lambda
, we define a function
\gamma^{\alpha}

on

Lemma 3.1
.

(Uniqueness of linear elliptic PDE in non-divergence form)
Let
A\in[C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})]^{3\times 3}
uniformly elliptic,
\boldsymbol{b}\in[L^{\infty}(\Omega)]^{3}
,
c\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
with
c\leq 0
,
and
\Omega
is an open bounded convex domain in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Let
1<p\leq\frac{3}{2}
.
If
\phi\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle A:D^{2}\phi+\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla\phi+c\phi=0\text{ in }\Omega,

then
\phi=0
in
\Omega
.

Remark 3.1
.

Though it is inspired by that of
[
24
, Lemma
9.16
]
,
the proof of Lemma
3.1
avoids using the common technique of “flattening”

\partial\Omega
, which is critical in
[
24
, Lemma
9.16
]
. Thus Lemma
3.1

is valid on convex polyhedra in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Proof.

We choose
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\overline{\Omega}
arbitrarily. For any
R>0
, we denote by
\eta
the cut-off function to be

1
inside of
\mathcal{B}_{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}):=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0}|<R\}
and
to be
0
outside of
\mathcal{B}_{2R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}):=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0}|<2R\}
.

We define
v=\eta\phi
. We notice that in
\Omega
,

\displaystyle A:D^{2}v=A:D^{2}(\eta\phi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta A:D^{2}\phi+2A:(\nabla\eta\otimes\nabla\phi)+\phi A:D^{2}\eta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\eta\left(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla\phi+c\phi\right)+2A:(\nabla\eta\otimes\nabla\phi)+\phi A:D^{2}\eta,

since
A:D^{2}\phi+\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla\phi+c\phi=0
in
\Omega
.
Therefore, by Sobolev’s embedding inequality, we have that

(21)

\displaystyle A:D^{2}v\in L^{q}(\Omega)\text{ with }q:=\min(\dfrac{3p}{3-p},2)>\frac{3}{2}.

Obviously,
1<p<q
.

We define
A_{0}=A(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
.
We define the linear mapping
M:L^{\tilde{q}}(\Omega)\rightarrow W^{2,\tilde{q}}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,\tilde{q}}(\Omega)
by

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\left(A_{0}\nabla M(f)\right)=f,\qquad\forall f\in L^{\tilde{q}}(\Omega),\forall 1<\tilde{q}\leq q.

By Lemma
2.1
,
the mapping
M
is not only well-defined but also continuous
for any
1<\tilde{q}\leq q
.

It is easy to see that in
\Omega

\displaystyle A_{0}:D^{2}v=(A_{0}-A):D^{2}v+A:D^{2}v.

Since
1<p<q
, we can
apply the mapping
M
on both sides of the above equation, to obtain that

\displaystyle v=M\left((A_{0}-A):D^{2}v\right)+M\left(A:D^{2}v\right)\text{ in }\Omega.

We define
B_{2R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}):=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega:|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0}|<2R\}=\mathcal{B}_{2R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\cap\Omega
. We define a linear mapping

T:W^{2,\tilde{q}}(B_{2R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}))\rightarrow W^{2,\tilde{q}}(B_{2R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}))
by

\displaystyle Tw=\left(M\tilde{w}\right)|_{B_{2R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}

where
w\in W^{2,\tilde{q}}(B_{2R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}))
and

\displaystyle\tilde{w}=(A_{0}-A):D^{2}w\text{ in }B_{2R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}),\qquad\tilde{w}=0\text{ in }\Omega\setminus\overline{B_{2R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}.

Here
1<\tilde{q}\leq q
.
Therefore, for any
1<\tilde{q}\leq q
, we have that

\displaystyle\|Tw\|_{W^{2,\tilde{q}}(B_{2R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}))}\leq C_{\tilde{q}}\|\tilde{w}\|_{L^{\tilde{q}}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{\tilde{q}}\|(A_{0}-A):D^{2}w\|_{L^{\tilde{q}}(B_{2R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{\tilde{q}}\|A_{0}-A\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}))}\|w\|_{W^{2,\tilde{q}}(B_{2R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}))},\qquad\forall w\in W^{2,\tilde{q}}(B_{2R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})).

The constant
C_{\tilde{q}}
is due to Lemma
2.1
.

Since
A\in[C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})]^{3\times 3}
,
there is a
R_{0}>0
such that
\|T\|\leq\frac{1}{2}
from
W^{2,\tilde{q}}(B_{2R_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}))
to

W^{2,\tilde{q}}(B_{2R_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}))
for all
p\leq\tilde{q}\leq q
.
By (
21
) and Lemma
2.1
,

M\left(A:D^{2}v\right)\in L^{\tilde{q}}(\Omega)

for any
1<\tilde{q}\leq q
.
Therefore for any
p\leq\tilde{q}\leq q
, there is a unique
w\in W^{2,\tilde{q}}(B_{2R_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}))
such that

\displaystyle w=Tw+M\left(A:D^{2}v\right)|_{B_{2R_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}}\text{ in }B_{2R_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}.

On the other hand, since
v=0
in
\Omega\setminus\overline{B_{2R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}

and
v\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
(
1<p<q
), we have that

\displaystyle v|_{B_{2R_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}=T(v|_{B_{2R_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})})+M\left(A:D^{2}v\right)|_{B_{2R_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}\text{ in }B_{2R_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}).

Therefore we have that
\phi|_{B_{R_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}\in W^{2,q}(B_{R_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}))
.

Since
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\overline{\Omega}
is chosen arbitrarily,
we can conclude that
\phi\in W^{2,q}(\Omega)

where
q=\min(\dfrac{3p}{3-p},2)>\frac{3}{2}
. So
\phi\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
. Furthermore, by the interior estimate in

[
24
, Lemma
9.16
]
, we have

since

A:D^{2}\phi+\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla\phi+c\phi=0
in
\Omega
.
Therefore, by the ABP estimate
[
24
, Theorem
9.1
]
, we can conclude
that
\phi=0

in

\Omega
.
∎
