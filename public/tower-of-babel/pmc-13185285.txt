Dietary fiber-driven shifts in mycobiota and short-chain fatty acid landscape in porcine hindgut
Authors: Jianwei Qin, Hua Li, Xiaofeng Deng, Shuangmei Huang, Jun He, Aimin Wu, Zhaolai Dai, Jingyi Cai, Gang Tian, Xiangbing Mao, Bing Yu, Ping Zheng, Junqiu Luo, Hui Yan, Jiayong Tang, Yuheng Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study employed a porcine model to investigate the dynamic effects of inulin (INU) and microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) on the profiles of intestinal short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and the composition of rectal fungal communities. Twenty-four healthy growing pigs were randomly allocated to three groups: a control group (CON), a 5% INU-supplemented group, and a 5% MCC-supplemented group. We hypothesized that soluble, rapidly fermentable INU and insoluble, slowly fermentable MCC would elicit distinct temporal dynamics in the fungal community, and that specific fungal taxa would correlate with SCFA profiles, reflecting a functional link between fungal succession and fiber fermentation. Gas chromatography analysis confirmed that dietary treatments induced distinct SCFA patterns in a time-dependent manner. On day 3, the concentrations of isobutyric acid (IBA) and isovaleric acid (IVA) were higher in the INU group than in the MCC group (P < 0.05). Conversely, on day 6, the concentrations of acetic acid (AA), propionic acid (PA), and total SCFA in the INU group were lower than those in both the CON and MCC groups (P < 0.05). ITS sequencing revealed that both INU and MCC markedly restructured rectal fungal communities, triggering temporal fluctuations in the relative abundances of Ascomycota and Basidiomycota, while selectively enriching functionally diverse genera. Specifically, the INU group exhibited enrichment of Vishniacozyma and Plectosphaerella at day 6, whereas the MCC group was characterized by elevated abundances of Trichosporon (day 6), Alternaria (day 10), Fusariella (day 10) and Paracremonium (day 10). Correlation analysis identified significant associations between key fungal genera and SCFA profiles. On day 3, the abundance of Aspergillus was negatively correlated with IVA and IBA concentrations. In line with this, pigs in INU group displayed reduced Aspergillus concomitant with increased IVA and IBA. On day 6, Vishniacozyma abundance was negatively associated with PA and total SCFAs, which agreed with the observation that the INU group had higher Vishniacozyma but lower PA to total SCFAs ratio relative to the CON and MCC groups (P < 0.05). Collectively, these findings highlight the functional involvement of intestinal fungi in dietary fiber degradation and offer novel insights into fungi-mediated dietary fiber digestion in the host gut.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42523-026-00563-8.

Introduction

Research has demonstrated a robust association between dietary fibers (DFs) and health [1, 2]. DF is a naturally-occurring complex blend of soluble (SDF) and insoluble (IDF) fibers. DFs are predominantly metabolized selectively by intestinal microorganisms. They play a pivotal role in shaping the interactions between microbiota and host [3, 4]. DF can be classified into soluble dietary fiber (SDF) or insoluble dietary fiber (IDF) according to its water-solubility. Studies have indicated that inulin, a common SDF and prebiotic, can stimulate the growth and activity of beneficial gut bacteria and induce alterations in the gut microbial composition, thus conferring benefits to host health [5]. Moreover, inulin offers multiple health advantages, such as enhancing intestinal barrier function and modulating inflammatory responses [6]. Cellulose is a representative type of IDF. It is widely present in the cell walls of plants and is a linear high-molecular polymer linked by β-1,4-glucosidic bonds [7, 8]. Microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) is a product obtained after natural cellulose undergoes partial depolymerization through physical and chemical treatments and the removal of the non-crystalline part [9]. It can be used as a DF to improve the physiological functions of the intestine [10]. DFs cannot be digested by the human body. However, they are either partially or completely fermented by microorganisms upon entering the large intestine, which further leads to the production of SCFAs [11, 12]. Gut microbes play a crucial role in the utilization of plant polysaccharides. Swine exhibit relatively high similarities to humans in terms of physiology, the gastrointestinal microbial ecosystems, and the carbohydrate fermentation activity of gut microbes [13]. Consequently, swine are regarded as ideal animal models for investigating the relationship between gut microbes and nutrient (e.g. carbohydrates) utilization of host.

Currently, the majority of studies on the gut microbiota of humans and animals focus on pathogenic or symbiotic bacteria, while there are extremely few studies on the nutritional functions of intestinal fungi. The results of our study using weaned piglets as a model indicate that specific fungal genera in the pig colon, including Saccharomycopsis, Mrakia, Wallemia, Cantharellus, Eurotium, Solicoccozyma, and Penicillium, exhibit an immediate response to the pattern of carbohydrate combinations, and their abundances are related to the concentrations of glucose and fructose in the chyme as well as the activity of β-glucosidase [14]. These findings provide direct evidence for the relationship between complex carbohydrates in the diet and the fungal community in the hindgut of pigs. Therefore, although the number of gut fungi in pigs is relatively small, they are likely to play an important role in the process of fiber fermentation and utilization.

Although we have previously confirmed that the composition of the gut fungi in pigs is related to the composition of dietary carbohydrates, the real-time dynamic changes in the composition of gut fungi in pigs when they consume different types of dietary fibers remain unclear. Therefore, in this study, growing pigs with a fully developed gut microbiota were selected as the research objects. They were fed diets containing inulin or MCC respectively, to investigate the dynamic response patterns of the gut fungal flora in pigs to different types of DFs. We also hypothesized that different types of dietary fiber can induce distinct temporal dynamic succession patterns in the porcine hindgut fungal community, and that changes in specific fungal taxa are closely associated with SCFA profiles, thereby revealing the intrinsic link between fungal community succession and fiber fermentation function. Based on this, the present study aims to systematically investigate the dynamic response patterns of the porcine hindgut fungal community to inulin and microcrystalline cellulose, and to elucidate the relationship between fungal community dynamics and short-chain fatty acid production, so as to provide new insights into the mechanism of fungal-mediated dietary fiber digestion in the host gut.

Materials and methods

Animals and treatments

This trial was performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and was approved by Sichuan Agricultural University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (DKYB20131704). Twenty-four healthy DLY growing pigs with an initial body weight of approximately 71.44 ± 1.75 kg were selected. Employing a one-way randomized block experimental design and following the principle of equal numbers of male and female pigs, they were randomly assigned into three groups, the control group (CON), the 5% inulin group (INU), and the 5% MCC group (MCC). Each group consisted of eight replicates, with one pig per replicate, and all pigs were individually housed in single cages. The formal experimental period spanned 20 days.

The diets were formulated in accordance with the NRC (2012) [15] feeding standards (Table S1 in the Supplementary Material). All diets were devoid of antibiotics, growth promoters, and fungal additives. The inulin was purchased from Billing Ingredients (Guangzhou, China), and the MCC was purchased from Shandong Xinda Biotechnology Co.

On days 0, 3, 6, 10, and 20 of the experiment, approximately 5 g of rectal contents were collected from each pig and immediately stored at -80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Measurement of SCFA concentrations

Concentrations of acetate (AA), propionate (PA), butyrate (BA), isobutyrate (IBA), valerate (VA), and isovalerate (IVA) in the rectal contents were measured using a gas chromatograph (Varian CP-3800). The sum of the concentrations of these SCFAs was defined as the total SCFA concentration. The entire testing procedure was conducted on ice, following the method described in reference [16].

DNA extraction of rectal contents

Frozen rectal content samples were retrieved and thawed on ice. Subsequently, genomic DNA was extracted from the rectal content samples using the Zymo Research BIOMICS DNA Microprep Kit (Item No. D4301), strictly following the protocol provided in the kit instructions. The integrity of the DNA samples was assessed by electrophoresis on a 0.8% agarose gel. Nucleic acid concentration and purity were then determined using a microspectrophotometer (NanoDrop 2000 C, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Construction of amplicon library and bioinformatic analysis

According to the target sequencing region, specific primers containing index sequences were synthesized to amplify the ITS2 region of the samples. The primer sequences were as follows: ITS1F (5’-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3’) and ITS2R (5’-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3’). The PCR amplification program was set as follows: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min (1 cycle); followed by 25–30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 20 s, annealing at 54 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s; final extension at 72 °C for 5 min (1 cycle); and a final hold at 4 °C. Three technical replicates were performed for each sample in the PCR amplification, and equal amounts of PCR products in the linear amplification phase were pooled for subsequent library construction. The PCR products were mixed with 6× loading buffer, and the target fragments were detected by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel. Qualified samples were subjected to target band recovery using the Zymoclean Gel Recovery Kit (D4008). The recovered products were quantified using a Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific), and equal molar amounts of the qualified products were pooled. Library construction was carried out using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB #E7645L) from New England BioLabs. Paired-end sequencing (PE250) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 SP Reagent Kit V1.5. Paired-end sequences were assembled using the FLASH software. Raw reads were demultiplexed into sample-specific sequences based on barcodes using the Sabre tool, and the barcode sequences were trimmed thereafter. Quality control of the demultiplexed sequences was performed using QIIME2 software with the following criteria: sequences with an average quality score lower than 30 were filtered out; sequences shorter than 200 bp were removed; and sequences containing more than 0 ambiguous bases (N) were discarded.

Statistical analysis

Data regarding SCFA concentrations and other relevant data were initially organized using Excel 2019. Subsequently, one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) was performed, followed by Duncan’s multiple range test for multiple comparisons. The results are presented as mean ± standard error (SEM). Differences were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05.

Results

Concentrations of SCFAs in rectal contents of the pigs

The results of gas chromatography analysis (Fig. 1) indicated that on day 6, the contents of AA, PA and total SCFA were higher in the CON and MCC groups compared to the INU group (P < 0.05). Specifically, the concentration of BA was higher in the CON group than in the INU group on day 6 (P < 0.05). On day 3 of the experiment, the contents of IBA and IVA were higher in the INU group than in the MCC group (P < 0.05). On day 20, the IBA and IVA concentrations were higher in the CON group than in the INU group (P < 0.05). The total SCFA concentration in all groups exhibited a pattern of first increasing and then decreasing, with day 6 serving as the inflection point. Notably, on day 6, the total SCFA content of the MCC group was higher than that of the INU group (P < 0.05).

Temporal variations in β-diversity of fungal communities within rectal contents

The results of Principal Co-ordinates Analysis (PCoA) indicated that at the commencement of the experiment (day 0, Fig. 2A), samples from the rectal contents of the three groups of pigs did not exhibit distinct clustering. On day 3 and day 6 (Fig. 2B, C), samples from the INU group clustered coherently with those from the MCC group and were distinguishable from those of the CON group. By day 10 and day 20 (Fig. 2D, E), samples from the CON group clustered uniformly with those from the INU group and were clearly segregated from those from the MCC group.

Comparison of composition of fungal community in rectal contents of pigs fed different DFs containing diets at the same time points

At the phylum level, fungi in the rectal contents of pigs were predominantly composed of Ascomycota and Basidiomycota (Fig. 3A). Specifically, on day 3, the relative abundance of Ascomycota in the CON group was higher than in the INU and MCC groups (P < 0.05), while the abundance of Basidiomycota in the INU and MCC groups was higher than that in the CON group (P < 0.05, Fig. 3B).

In light of the PCoA plots, the responses of fungal community to different DFs were mainly concentrated on days 3, 6, and 10. Thus, we further illustrated the changes at the genus level in each group at these time points (Fig. 3C-E). Specifically, Aspergillus, Trichosporon, Cladosporium, Acremonium and Microascus all exhibited higher relative abundances on days 3, 6, and 10 (Fig. 3C-E). The results of linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) indicated a greater number of differential fungal taxa on days 6 and 10 (Fig. 3G, H). Comparative analysis of differential fungi using an LDA score threshold of 3.5 revealed fewer differential abundant taxa on days 3 and 20 compared to days 6 and 10 (Fig. 3F, S1). Among the top 15 most abundant fungal genera, significant differences in relative abundance were observed between groups. The CON group demonstrated significantly higher relative abundances of Penicillium on day 3 (P < 0.05, Fig. 3F), Acremonium, Kazachstania, and Talaromyces on day 6 (P < 0.05, Fig. 3G), and Perenniporia and Cladosporium on day 10 (P < 0.05, Fig. 3H). Conversely, the INU group exhibited increased abundances of Vishniacozyma and Plectosphaerella specifically on day 6 (P < 0.05, Fig. 3G), while the MCC group showed elevated abundances of Trichosporon on day 6 (P < 0.05, Fig. 3G), followed by Alternaria, Paracremonium, and Fusariella on day 10 (P < 0.05, Fig. 3H).

Comparison of composition of fungal community in rectal contents of pigs fed same DF containing diet at different time points

At the phylum level, the INU and MCC groups exhibited similar trends of change at different time points (Fig. 4A, C). In the two groups, the abundance of Ascomycota on day 20 was significantly higher than that on days 3 and 6 (P < 0.05). Regarding Basidiomycota, in the INU group, its abundance on day 3 was higher than that on days 0 and 20 (P < 0.05), and it was higher on day 6 than that on day 20 (P < 0.05, Fig. 4B). In the MCC group, the abundance of Basidiomycota on day 20 was lower than that on days 3 and 6 (P < 0.05, Fig. 4D). Over time, the INU and MCC groups gave rise to different genera of fungi (Fig. 4E-H). Specifically, the ITS sequences of the INU group identified 10 differential fungal genera, whereas the MCC group yielded 13 differential fungal genera (Fig. 4F, H). In the INU group, dynamic fluctuations in fungal genus-level abundances were observed across different time points (Fig. 4F). On day 3, Cladosporium, Plectosphaerella, and Phlebia exhibited marked increases in relative abundances, whereas Aspergillus demonstrated a significant reduction (P < 0.05). Filobasidium and Wallemia reached peak abundances on day 6 (P < 0.05), while Penicillium showed a progressive increase starting from day 6 onwards (P < 0.05). Subsequently, Fusariella displayed elevated relative abundances on days 10 and 20 (P < 0.05), and Kazachstania and Acremonium exhibited enrichment on day 20 (P < 0.05). Consistent with these patterns, the MCC group also displayed temporal variations in fungal community composition (Fig. 4H). Plectosphaerella and Aspergillus dominated initially, with their highest relative abundances observed on day 0, followed by gradual declines over time. Trichosporon demonstrated a bloom on day 3 (P < 0.05), while Penicillium increased markedly on day 6 (P < 0.05). By day 10, Alternaria, Fusariella, and Holtermanniella became enriched (P < 0.05). Notably, on day 20, Kazachstania, Acremonium, Cephalotrichiella, Nigrospora, and Pestalotiopsis showed elevated abundances (P < 0.05), whereas Wallemia decreased persistently from day 10 onward (P < 0.05).

Correlation analysis between intestinal fungal groups and the concentrations of SCFAs in the rectal contents of the pigs

On day 3 (Fig. 5A), the majority of fungal genera exhibited weak correlations with the concentrations of SCFAs. Nevertheless, the relative abundance of certain fungal genera, such as Acremonium and Aspergillus, were negatively correlated with the concentrations of IBA and IVA (P < 0.05). Moreover, Aspergillus and Penicillium demonstrated negative correlations with the concentration of AA (P < 0.05). On day 6 (Fig. 5B), the relative abundance of Aspergillus was positively correlated with the content of IBA (P < 0.05), and Talaromyces showed positive correlation with the concentrations of total SCFA and AA (P < 0.05). On the contrary, Fusarium was negatively correlated with the concentration of IVA (P < 0.05), and Plectosphaerella was negatively correlated with the concentrations of IVA and IBA (P < 0.05). Additionally, Vishniacozyma was found to be negatively correlated with the contents of VA, Total SCFA, PA, and BA (P < 0.05), and Wallemia was negatively correlated with the concentrations of PA and AA (P < 0.05). On day 10 (Fig. 5C), most fungal genera displayed weak correlations with SCFAs contents. However, a particular fungal genus, Exophiala, was significantly correlated with SCFAs, showing negative correlation with both the concentrations of total SCFA and AA (P < 0.05).

Discussion

SCFAs can adopt either straight-chain or branched-chain conformations. They are generated through the anaerobic fermentation of indigestible carbohydrates by intestinal microorganisms [17, 18]. SCFAs are absorbed in the large intestine and are influenced by food intake patterns and diet-mediated changes in the gut microbiota, exerting physiological effects by modulating the intestinal environment and host signaling mechanisms [19]. In the hindgut of monogastric animals such as humans and pigs, SCFAs are generally considered the main metabolic products of bacterial fermentation of carbohydrates [20, 21], but there is currently no consensus on whether gut fungi also produce SCFAs. It is well-known that inulin is a fructan linked by β-2,1-glycosidic bonds, it can be rapidly fermented by intestinal microbiota as a typical SDF, predominantly in the pig’s cecum [22]. In contrast, MCC, a typical IDF with a β-1,4-glucan structure, necessitates a more intricate enzymatic process and undergoes slower fermentation [23, 24]. Given that the concentration of SCFAs can, to some extent, reflect the extent of microbial fermentation [25], our results suggest that between days 3 to 6 after the introduction of a fiber-containing diet, microbial fermentation in the hindgut of growing pigs may become more active compared to earlier time points. It is important to note that variations in dosage, experimental duration, physiological status of the subjects, and sampling location within the gastrointestinal tract may lead to differing effects of inulin on SCFA concentrations. Such factors could contribute to inconsistencies across studies. For instance, inulin has been reported to increase intestinal SCFA levels in individuals who are overweight or obese [26]. In contrast, our previous research demonstrated that inulin supplementation reduced the concentrations of AA, BA, and total SCFAs in the colonic digesta of growing pigs [27], further supporting the current observation that AA, PA, and total SCFA levels in the hindgut were lower in the INU group compared to the other groups.

Furthermore, SCFA production is mainly regulated by the intestinal microbiota. Thus, a question arises: Would the addition of inulin and MCC enrich different microbiota? Our previous research on mice can partially answer this question, that is, bacteria in the colon do indeed respond specifically to different types of DFs [16]. Inulin has been demonstrated to significantly modify the intestinal microbiota and augment the abundance of probiotics such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus [28]. Additionally, after the administration of inulin and MCC, the bacterial communities in the ileum and colon of pigs exhibit markedly distinct clustering patterns [29]. However, there remains substantial scope for further investigation into the impact of inulin and MCC on the composition of fungal communities. In the current study, we found that at the phylum level, fungi in the hindgut of the pigs were predominantly composed of Ascomycota and Basidiomycota, which is in accordance with previous research [30]. At the baseline (day 0), no significant clustering was observed among the rectal content samples from the three groups of pigs. This indicates that the fungal community structures of the three groups were similar at the commencement of the experiment, which confirms the correctness of selecting day 0 as the baseline time point. By days 3 and 6, the fungal community in the rectal content samples from pigs fed inulin and MCC containing diets clustered consistently and were clearly distinguishable from the control. This indicates that the fungal community in the hindgut of pigs can rapidly adapt to an SDF-containing diet within a short period of time. This rapid response of gut fungal communities to DFs is similar to that of bacteria. It has been shown that the shaping of bacterial community in the gut of humans by diet can occur within 24 h, and this change reaches stability after 10 days [31]. Interestingly, we observed that by days 10 and 20, samples from the pigs in CON group clustered consistently with those from the pigs fed inulin containing diet and were clearly separated from those from the pigs ingesting MCC containing diet. This shift may reflect the adaptive responses of gut fungal communities to different DFs and the dynamic nature of these changes over time. This aligns with previous findings that diet-induced changes in the gut microbiota are often temporary [32], and extends this idea by suggesting that the hindgut fungal community in pigs may exhibit a more sustained adaptive response to MCC.

The results also revealed that on day 3, the relative abundance of Ascomycota was higher in the gut of pigs in CON group than in the pigs fed DFs containing diets, with the decreased abundance of Basidiomycota. Longitudinal analysis demonstrated that in the pigs fed inulin and MCC, Ascomycota underwent a process of first decreasing and then increasing, while Basidiomycota first increased and then decreased. Moreover, the abundance of Basidiomycota and Ascomycota returned to their initial levels as of day 10. In nature, most Basidiomycota taxa possess a greater capacity to degrade cellulose and hemicellulose than Ascomycota [33]. Notably, while both phyla break down lignocellulose through the secretion of extracellular enzymes, the enzymes produced by Ascomycota are generally more active under acidic conditions [34–36]. Therefore, the dynamic alterations in the abundance of Basidiomycota within the pig gut might mirror the real-time utilization of DFs by this phylum.

Previous studies have shown that Trichosporon, which was enriched in the MCC group on day 6, can produce cellulase to degrade cellulose under conditions of different carbon sources and insoluble cellulose [37, 38], this may be the reason for the enrichment of Trichosporon in the hindgut of pigs fed MCC. Moreover, certain species within Trichosporon are capable of degrading mycotoxins in addition to assimilating inulin [39]. On day 10, pigs fed MCC containing diet was predominantly enriched in Alternaria, Fusariella, and Paracremonium. Most Alternaria species are common saprophytic fungi that obtain energy through cellulolytic activities [40]. There exist reports suggesting that certain species within the Alternaria genus exhibit high cellulase-producing capabilities. For instance, Alternaria alternata, a well-known plant pathogen, demonstrates the capacity to utilize a diverse range of carbon sources, among which cellulose is included [41]. It is noteworthy that we did not identify any fungal genera associated with cellulose utilization that have been publicly reported in the hindgut of pigs fed inulin. This comprehensively indicates that the distinct alterations in fungal composition in the gut of pigs fed MCC might represent a specific response to cellulose.

Despite the limited number of studies on intestinal fungi in monogastric animals, existing literature a clear bidirectional interaction between SCFAs and fungi. To some extent, fluctuations in SCFA concentrations can reflect both fungal abundance and the activity of microbial fermentation [42–44]. In the current study, at days 3 and 10, relatively few fungal genera exhibited significant correlations with SCFAs. On day 3, the abundance of Aspergillus and Acremonium was negatively correlated with the levels of IVA and IBA. Among them, the impact of Aspergillus was likely more substantial due to its higher relative abundance and greater variability. IVA and IBA are classified as branched-chain fatty acids, which are mainly derived from the microbial fermentation of branched-chain amino acids such as leucine, isoleucine, and valine in the gut [45]. These fermentation processes are generally slow, and the main products are branched-chain fatty acids with one less carbon atom than the original amino acids [46, 47]. Bacteria have the ability to synthesize all 20 proteogenic amino acids, including the nine essential amino acids necessary for mammalian growth [48]. Conversely, Aspergillus, for example, the model strain Aspergillus nidulans, contains enzymes involved in encoding the biosynthesis of branched-chain amino acids [49]. In the present study, in the hindgut of pigs fed inulin, the relative abundance of Aspergillus decreased, yet the levels of IVA and IBA increased. This result appears to furnish evidence for the trade-off between the abundance of Aspergillus and the concentrations of branched-chain fatty acids. However, its potential association with specific bacterial groups remains to be investigated. Given the intimate relationship between Aspergillus and polysaccharide utilization [50, 51], the correlation between its abundance and branched-chain fatty acids might also mirror its connection to the fermentation of carbohydrates in pig hindgut. Furthermore, the abundances of eight additional fungal genera exhibited significant correlations with the concentrations of AA, PA, and branched-chain fatty acids, suggesting that specific fungal species in the pig hindgut are likely either directly or indirectly engaged in microbial fermentation. Owing to the dearth of relevant research regarding the functions of these fungal genera in degrading DFs, whether this correlation represents a cause or an effect still necessitates research for verification.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that inulin and MCC differentially shape the dynamics of SCFAs and fungal communities in the pig hindgut. SCFA concentrations followed a temporal pattern, peaking on day 6, with total SCFAs significantly higher in the MCC group than in the INU group. The fungal community, dominated by Ascomycota and Basidiomycota, responded rapidly to dietary intervention within 3–6 days. Notably, INU induced transient fluctuations in the fungal community (within 6 days), whereas MCC had more sustained effects (lasting 10–20 days). Specific taxa were associated with each fiber type: Vishniacozyma and Plectosphaerella with INU degradation, and Trichosporon and Alternaria with cellulose degradation. Furthermore, Aspergillus and other genera correlated with branched-chain fatty acid levels. These results underscore a strong link between fiber structure, fungal composition, and SCFA metabolism.

Collectively, our findings support the active role of fungi in structure-dependent fiber degradation and their interaction with SCFA metabolism, suggesting a dynamic “dietary fiber structure–fungal succession–SCFA axis”. However, as this study establishes correlation rather than causation, the functional contributions of key fungal taxa and the mechanisms of fungal-bacterial synergy in fiber fermentation require further investigation.
