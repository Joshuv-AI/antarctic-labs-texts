The relationship between caring stress management and quality of working life in Iranian nurses: the mediating role of hope
Authors: Mozhgan Taebi, Sharipova Gulnihol, Leila Poursoleiman, Alexey Yumashev, Samad Karkhah, Gafforov Khudoyor, Mamatqulov Bakhramjon, Soheyla Kalantari, Azadeh Emami, Reza Salehi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNurses, as central providers of healthcare, face considerable occupational stress that threatens both their well-being and the quality of patient care. This study investigates the relationship between caring stress management and quality of working life (QWL) among Iranian nurses, with a focus on the mediating role of hope.MethodsUsing a cross-sectional design, 300 nurses from two Tehran hospitals completed validated instruments measuring Caring stress management, QWL, and hope. Data were analyzed with SPSS and AMOS, employing structural equation modeling and bootstrapping methods.ResultsStructural equation modeling revealed that caring stress management significantly predicted hope (β = 0.65, p < 0.001), and hope significantly predicted quality of working life (β = 0.45, p < 0.001). The direct effect of caring stress management on quality of working life was significant (β = 0.38, p < 0.001), and the indirect effect through hope was also significant (β = 0.29, 95% CI [0.23, 0.36], p < 0.001), confirming hope’s partial mediating role. The model demonstrated good fit (χ²/df = 1.95, CFI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.05).ConclusionFindings highlight that caring stress management is not only directly associated with quality of working life but is also indirectly associated with it through higher levels of hope. These results underscore the importance of implementing caring stress management and hope-promoting strategies to support nurses’ psychological resilience, which is linked to better quality of working life and healthcare outcomes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Caring stress management, Quality of working life, Hope, Nurse

Introduction

Nursing is recognized globally as a highly stressful profession, with nurses experiencing significant psychological and occupational strain that can lead to burnout, emotional exhaustion, reduced productivity, and compromised patient safety [1–3]. In Iran, these challenges are further intensified by nurse shortages and limited healthcare resources, resulting in an estimated 37.5% prevalence of high stress among nurses (95% CI: 28.5–47.4) [4, 5]. Such conditions underscore the necessity of developing effective strategies to manage occupational stress and promote nurses’ psychological well-being.

Caring stress management is a key approach to reducing the negative psychological and behavioral effects of stress in nursing, helping nurses regulate emotions, enhance coping, and maintain performance [6–8]. These strategies are closely linked to nurses’ Quality of Working Life (QWL), which includes job satisfaction, work–life balance, well-being, and growth opportunities [9].

Previous studies have reported that unmanaged stress significantly diminishes QWL, increases absenteeism, and contributes to workforce turnover [10]. Therefore, identifying psychological mechanisms that strengthen the impact of Caring stress management on QWL is essential for improving both nurse well-being and healthcare outcomes [9, 10].

In recent years, growing attention has been paid to the role of positive psychological resources in promoting resilience and occupational well-being among healthcare providers. Among these, hope has emerged as a central construct within positive psychology [11]. According to Snyder’s Hope Theory, hope consists of two cognitive components—agency thinking (goal-directed determination) and pathways thinking (the perceived ability to generate strategies to achieve goals) [12]. High levels of hope have been associated with better emotional regulation, increased resilience to workplace adversity, and more adaptive coping behaviors among nurses and other health professionals [12]. Within the nursing context, hope may empower individuals to interpret stressors more constructively, sustain motivation, and maintain engagement in patient care even under challenging conditions [3].

Moreover, hope is closely intertwined with psychological resilience, as it enhances the capacity to recover from workplace adversity, and aligns with principles of positive psychology by fostering optimism, personal growth, and adaptive coping. This interconnectedness suggests that hope may serve as a key mediating mechanism through which positive psychological interventions, including caring stress management, translate into improved quality of working life for nurses.

Despite the extensive literature on stress and QWL, few studies have examined the psychological mechanisms that explain how Caring stress management interventions influence nurses’ quality of working life, particularly in non-Western contexts [13]. While prior evidence suggests that Caring stress management improves well-being [14], the mediating role of hope remains theoretically plausible but empirically underexplored. Understanding this mechanism is essential for developing more effective, psychologically informed interventions targeting nurses’ occupational health [15].

Several studies have addressed psychological mediators such as mindfulness [16], self-compassion and benefit finding [17], and self-regulation [18], but none has explicitly examined hope as a mediator in the stress–QWL relationship.

By clarifying the mediating role of hope, this study is expected to contribute to theory by advancing the understanding of psychological pathways linking stress-management strategies to nurses’ occupational well-being. Practically, the findings may inform the development of more targeted interventions that strengthen hope as a means to enhance quality of working life. Furthermore, identifying this mechanism may guide future research toward designing and testing positive-psychology–based models in nursing contexts.

Therefore, the present study aims to fill this gap by examining whether hope mediates the relationship between Caring stress management and QWL among nurses. Grounded in Hope Theory and stress–well-being frameworks, we propose that Caring stress management interventions strengthen nurses’ sense of hope—by improving their perceived ability to cope with challenges—which subsequently enhances their QWL.

Research hypotheses

H1

Caring stress management is positively associated with hope among nurses.

H2

Hope is positively associated with nurses’ quality of working life (QWL).

H3

Hope mediates the relationship between Caring stress management and QWL, such that higher levels of Caring stress management increase hope, which in turn enhances QWL.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study aimed to examine the associations between caring stress management and nurses’ quality of working life, with hope examined as a potential mediator. The cross-sectional design enabled efficient data collection from a large sample at a single point in time, allowing analysis of direct and indirect relationships while providing a foundation for future longitudinal or experimental research to explore causal effects.

Participants and sampling

The statistical population included all nurses working in two major hospitals in Tehran, across various departments (ICU, emergency, surgery, internal medicine, pediatrics, and general wards). Convenience sampling was used. Efforts were made to recruit participants with diverse demographic and professional characteristics (age, gender, education, work experience, and hospital unit) to enhance representativeness.

Sample size determination

Based on power analysis for Structural Equation Modeling (SEM), considering three latent variables (Caring stress management, QWL, and hope), a significance level of 0.05, statistical power of 80%, and effect size of 0.2, the required sample size was determined to be 300 nurses. The calculation was performed using G*Power for multiple regression as a proxy for SEM, which is commonly recommended when estimating sample size for models with a limited number of latent variables. The assumed SEM model included 23 observed indicators across the three latent constructs. This meets the commonly cited participant-to-parameter ratio of at least 10:1 (Kline, 2016), supporting the adequacy of the sample size. This calculation assumed multivariate normality of the observed variables, linear relationships between latent constructs, and adequate communality of indicators. Normality of observed variables was additionally checked using skewness and kurtosis values to ensure compliance with SEM assumptions.

Dropout considerations

Although 326 questionnaires were distributed, an anticipated dropout/non-response rate of approximately 8–10% was considered. Ultimately, 300 fully completed questionnaires were collected, yielding a response rate of 92%.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected face-to-face during work breaks in the hospitals between March and May 2025. Participants were provided with detailed explanations about the study purpose and assured of confidentiality and voluntary participation. The questionnaires were administered and collected by the research team and trained support staff. To maximize response rates, participants were reminded verbally and reassured about anonymity and the importance of their contributions. Out of 326 distributed questionnaires, 300 were fully completed (response rate = 92%).

Handling missing data

Missing responses were minimal (< 2%) and listwise deletion was applied. No systematic patterns of missingness were detected, minimizing potential bias.

Measurement scales

All scales underwent linguistic translation and cultural adaptation for the Iranian nursing context. Psychometric properties, including internal consistency and construct validity, were confirmed for the current sample, ensuring that the instruments were both reliable and culturally appropriate.

Demographic information

Collected data included age, gender, marital status, education, work experience, and current work unit.

Caring stress management scale

To our knowledge, the Nurses’ Caring Stress Management Scale was developed and psychometrically validated for the first time within the Iranian nursing context and comprises 23 items distributed across four subscales. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale: Completely Agree (5), Agree (4), No Opinion (3), Disagree (2), and Completely Disagree (1). Total scores range from 23 to 115 [19], with higher scores indicating greater Caring stress management by nurses.

The validity and reliability of this scale were initially assessed and confirmed in Iran. In the present study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86.

Quality of working life

In the present study, the WRQoL-2 Questionnaire was used to assess the quality of working life. This scale employs 7 main components to measure employees’ quality of working life: satisfaction with profession and job, working conditions, general comfort and well-being, work-home interface, stress at work, control at work, and participation and involvement at work [20, 21].

The WRQoL-2 questionnaire consists of 32 questions using a Likert scale (1 = completely disagree, 5 = completely agree). Factor analysis of the questionnaire’s questions indicated that 31 items of the questionnaire could be summarized into 6 factors. In fact, these six factors can explain 62% of the variance of the variables. Furthermore, its reliability has been confirmed, and the questionnaire was culturally adapted for Iran using a forward–backward translation process to ensure its validity [20]. In the current study, the reliability of this instrument was confirmed among nurses (α = 0.79).

Snyder’s hope scale (SHS)

The Snyder’s Hope Scale (SHS), developed by Snyder, Irving, and Anderson [22], is a 12-item instrument designed to measure hope. Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale. The scale includes 4 items assessing Agency Thinking, 4 items assessing Pathways Thinking, and 4 filler items, with items 3, 7, and 11 reverse-scored. Total scores range from 12 to 60.

The scale has demonstrated robust reliability and validity internationally (α = 0.74–0.84, test-retest reliability = 0.80) and has been psychometrically confirmed in Iran. In this study, internal consistency among nurses was α = 0.82 [23].

Data analysis

The data collected in this research were analyzed using the SPSS software (version 25) and the AMOS software for testing the conceptual model. First, descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage, were used to describe the sample’s demographic characteristics and the main research variables. Then, regression analysis was used to examine the initial relationships between the independent, mediating, and dependent variables.

The main goal of the data analysis was to test the proposed mediation model. To this end, path analysis using the AMOS software was employed to evaluate the direct and indirect effects of Caring stress management on the quality of working life, considering the mediating role of hope. In this analysis, direct relationships between variables were examined first. Then, to test the indirect effects, the bootstrapping method with 5,000 resamples was used, which is considered a standard method in mediation studies due to its high accuracy in calculating indirect effects. The model fit was evaluated using well-known indices such as the Chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio, Comparative Fit Indices (CFI and GFI), and the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA).

Results

Out of approximately 360 eligible nurses, 326 were approached, and 300 completed the questionnaires (response rate = 92%). Among these participants, the majority were women (85%), with a mean age of 32.5 ± 6.8 years; more than half (62%) were married, and most held a bachelor’s degree (74%). The average clinical work experience was 7.1 ± 5.3 years. The findings are based on data from the two hospitals included in this study and pertain to the tested structural model of associations.

Preliminary data screening was conducted prior to hypothesis testing. Missing data were minimal (< 2%), and listwise deletion was applied, resulting in a final sample of 300 nurses. Comparisons between cases with complete and incomplete data showed no significant differences in key demographic variables (p > 0.05), indicating that listwise deletion was unlikely to bias the results. Assumptions of normality were assessed using skewness and kurtosis statistics. All variables fell within acceptable ranges (|skewness| < 1, |kurtosis| < 2). Outlier analyses based on standardized z-scores and Mahalanobis distance identified no influential univariate or multivariate outliers.

Potential common method bias was examined using Harman’s single-factor test; the first factor accounted for less than 40% of the total variance, suggesting that common method bias was not a significant concern. Multicollinearity diagnostics indicated acceptable VIF values (< 3). Overall, the data met the assumptions required for regression and structural equation modeling.

Preliminary regression analyses

Prior to testing the full mediation model, preliminary bivariate regression analyses were conducted to examine the direct relationships between the key variables. As shown in Table 1, caring stress management was positively associated with hope (β = 0.65, t = 12.80, p < 0.001), indicating that higher levels of caring stress management were linked to greater hope among nurses. In turn, hope showed a significant positive association with quality of working life (β = 0.45, t = 16.15, p < 0.001). These significant bivariate associations provided empirical justification for testing the proposed mediation model using structural equation modeling.

Structural equation modeling

Path analysis using AMOS software was conducted to examine the proposed mediation model. Unlike the preliminary bivariate regressions, SEM estimates all relationships simultaneously while accounting for measurement error and intercorrelations among variables, providing more accurate and conservative path coefficients.

Prior to testing the structural model, the measurement model was evaluated using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), and all latent constructs demonstrated acceptable factor loadings and fit indices, supporting the adequacy of the measurement model for subsequent structural analysis.

The structural equation model demonstrated good fit to the data (χ² = 428.10, df = 219, χ²/df = 1.95), with fit indices within acceptable ranges: CFI = 0.96, NFI = 0.94, and RMSEA = 0.05. The R² values for the endogenous variables were Hope (R² = 0.42) and Quality of Working Life (R² = 0.55), indicating that the model explained a substantial proportion of variance in these outcomes.

In the full SEM, caring stress management showed a significant direct association with hope (β = 0.65, 95% CI [0.57, 0.73], p < 0.001), and hope showed a significant direct association with quality of working life (β = 0.45, 95% CI [0.39, 0.51], p < 0.001). The direct effect of caring stress management on quality of working life was also significant (β = 0.38, 95% CI [0.28, 0.47], p < 0.001).

Discussion

The results of this study showed that both caring stress management in caregiving and hope had significant direct and positive associations with nurses’ quality of work life. The confirmation of the partial mediating role of hope indicates that caring stress management not only enhances QWL directly but also contributes indirectly by strengthening nurses’ sense of hope. This pattern aligns with psychological frameworks such as Snyder’s Hope Theory and positive coping models, which emphasize the role of cognitive–emotional resources in shaping occupational outcomes.

These findings align with a broad body of international literature highlighting the importance of psychological determinants in managing occupational stress and improving well-being among healthcare workers. Importantly, some prior studies were conducted specifically among nurses, while others involved patients or the general population, providing context for the generalizability of these findings. For instance, studies in Pakistan and Turkey examined constructs such as hope, resilience, and self-efficacy among nurses, whereas studies on Iranian cancer patients focused on social support and quality of life. These differences provide context for the generalizability of our findings to nursing populations [6, 24, 25]. Although these studies examined different variables, they similarly suggest that positive psychological resources play a universal protective role across diverse cultural contexts [26, 27]. This broader global evidence complements Iranian findings, such as the study on cancer patients where social support mediated the relationship between fear of recurrence and quality of life [24]. reinforcing the idea that psychological and social factors can mitigate stress-related outcomes. Together, these studies reinforce the idea that psychological and social factors can mitigate stress-related outcomes, supporting the relevance of hope as a key mechanism. The mediating role of hope observed in this study is also supported by national research demonstrating that hope and spirituality mediate the relationship between attachment and life satisfaction among Iranian adults [28]. At the same time, some international studies report that in highly demanding work environments, psychological resources may not fully counterbalance the effects of severe organizational stressors. These inconsistencies suggest that the effectiveness of hope as a mediator may depend on contextual factors such as workload intensity, institutional support, and cultural expectations.

It is also important to acknowledge that previous literature has reported conflicting findings regarding the relationships between stress management, hope, and quality of working life. Some studies suggest that other psychological resources, such as resilience and social support, may also play significant mediating roles, potentially explaining variations in outcomes across different contexts [29–31]. These discrepancies highlight the need to consider additional variables and contextual factors when interpreting the impact of caring stress management on nurses’ well-being.

Theoretical and practical implications

The strong direct relationship between caring stress management and hope (β = 0.65) highlights the importance of effective coping strategies in fostering future-oriented and constructive attitudes among nurses. Nurses with well-developed stress management skills are less prone to negative cognitive patterns and more able to formulate achievable goals, thereby enhancing their sense of hope.

These findings refine theoretical frameworks by positioning hope as an active, dynamic mechanism that facilitates adaptation to occupational stress rather than merely a passive outcome. Building on this, our findings can be interpreted in light of occupational stress and QWL models, including Karasek’s demand–control model, Siegrist’s effort–reward imbalance model, and burnout frameworks. This highlights the theoretical contribution of identifying hope as a mediating mechanism in nursing contexts. This perspective aligns with Snyder’s Hope Theory, Broaden-and-Build Theory, and broader positive psychology frameworks, including Psychological Capital (PsyCap), which emphasize that positive cognitive–emotional states broaden coping resources, promote resilience, and improve occupational outcomes. Importantly, these results have direct implications for health management and psychological counseling, suggesting that interventions fostering hope and stress management can be strategically integrated into workplace design to enhance nurses’ well-being, productivity, and overall quality of care.

From a practical standpoint, the results underscore the need for structured training programs in caring stress management techniques, including mindfulness, problem-solving, and time management. Given hope’s mediating role, interventions targeting the enhancement of hope—such as goal-setting workshops, cognitive reframing exercises, and peer support initiatives—should be integrated into professional development programs in Iran. These practical recommendations, while derived from two Tehran hospitals, can be customized for different healthcare institutions outside Iran, for various nursing groups, or for specific risk groups, such as new nurses, ICU staff, or those with high workload. These approaches should extend beyond the two hospitals studied and can potentially be implemented at national or regional scales, particularly in healthcare systems facing staff shortages and elevated stress levels.

At the organizational level, addressing systemic stressors, including workload imbalance, insufficient staffing, and inadequate supervision, is crucial. Improving these conditions can foster an environment that naturally nurtures hope, enhancing nurses’ overall quality of work life. At the policy level, these findings may guide broader initiatives aimed at promoting psychological well-being among healthcare personnel.

Limitations and suggestions

Despite its strengths, this study has some limitations that should be considered;

First, the cross-sectional design captures associations at a single point in time, which limits the ability to infer causality. While the mediation model provides insights into potential pathways, the temporal sequence of caring stress management, hope, and quality of working life cannot be definitively established. This limitation suggests that observed effects might vary if measured longitudinally.

Second, the study relied on self-report instruments, which could introduce social desirability bias. Participants may have overestimated their stress management abilities or hope levels, potentially inflating the strength of associations observed. Nevertheless, validated scales and anonymity measures were employed to reduce such bias.

Third, the sample was limited to nurses from two hospitals in Tehran, which may constrain generalizability. Differences in institutional policies, resources, and patient populations in other regions could lead to variations in the relationships between stress management, hope, and quality of working life. Although efforts were made to recruit participants from various clinical departments and with diverse demographic and professional characteristics (e.g., age, gender, education level, and work experience) to enhance representativeness, the findings may still not be fully generalizable to all nurses in other cities in Iran or to nurses working in different organizational settings. Therefore, caution is warranted when applying these findings to broader nursing populations or international contexts. Future studies should consider using stratified or random sampling methods to improve generalizability.

While the proposed mediation pattern is theoretically grounded and supported by the data, it should be interpreted with reasonable caution. Because this study used a cross-sectional design, the findings represent statistical associations rather than confirmed temporal or directional effects. Likewise, although convenience sampling provided access to a diverse clinical sample, it naturally limits assumptions about the uniformity of these patterns across all nursing settings. Importantly, these methodological considerations do not diminish the relevance of the findings; rather, they help clarify the conditions under which the results are valid and emphasize the need for future longitudinal or experimental research to further strengthen evidence for the proposed model.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that both Caring stress management in caregiving and hope have significant direct and positive effects on nurses’ quality of work life. This positive relationship means that the better nurses are at managing their stress and the higher their levels of hope, the more favorable their quality of work life will be. The most important outcome of this research was the confirmation of the partial mediating role of hope in this relationship. This finding suggests that caring stress management is not only directly associated with quality of work life but is also indirectly associated with it through higher levels of hope. In other words, hope acts as a psychological mechanism that explains part of the impact of Caring stress management on quality of work life.

Furthermore, this study makes a theoretical contribution by clarifying the psychological pathways through which stress management influences occupational well-being, aligning with frameworks in positive psychology, workplace well-being theories, and nursing management. By identifying hope as a key mediating mechanism, the results provide evidence for integrating positive psychological resources into interventions aimed at enhancing nurses’ resilience, motivation, and overall work experience.

Additionally, the findings have practical implications beyond individual nurses, highlighting potential benefits for the broader healthcare system and society. Effective stress management and promotion of hope may contribute to improved job satisfaction, reduced turnover rates, enhanced quality of care, and better patient outcomes, thereby supporting organizational efficiency and overall healthcare delivery. This contribution may inform both theory development and practical strategies in healthcare settings, supporting evidence-based approaches to improve nurse well-being and patient care outcomes.
