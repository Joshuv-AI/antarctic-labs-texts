The multi-level friendship paradox for sparse random graphs
Rajat Subhra Hazra, Frank den Hollander, Azadeh Parvaneh

Abstract
In [ 13 ] we analysed the friendship paradox for sparse random graphs. For four classes of random graphs we characterised the empirical distribution of the friendship biases between vertices and their neighbours at distance 1 1 , proving convergence as n → ∞ n\to\infty to a limiting distribution, with n n the number of vertices, and identifying moments and tail exponents of the limiting distribution. In the present paper we look at the multi-level friendship bias between vertices and their neighbours at distance k ∈ ℕ k\in\mathbb{N} obtained via a k k -step exploration according to a backtracking or a non-backtracking random walk. We identify the limit of empirical distribution of the multi-level friendship biases as n → ∞ n\to\infty and/or k → ∞ k\to\infty . We show that for non-backtracking exploration the two limits commute for a large class of sparse random graphs, including those that locally converge to a rooted Galton-Watson tree. In particular, we show that the same limit arises when k k depends on n n , i.e., k = k n k=k_{n} , provided lim n → ∞ k n = ∞ \lim_{n\to\infty}k_{n}=\infty under some mild conditions. We exhibit cases where the two limits do not commute and show the relevance of the mixing time of the exploration. Keywords: Sparse random graph, local convergence, multi-level friendship bias, exploration, mixing. MSC2020: 05C80, 60C05, 60F15, 60J80, 60G50. 1
Introduction and outline

1.1
The friendship paradox

In 1991, the American sociologist Scott Feld discovered a phenomenon called the
friendship paradox
, which he summarised by saying that ‘your friends have more friends than you do’: within any social network of individuals with mutual friendships the difference between the average number of friends of friends and the average number of friends is always non-negative
[
12
]
. Later, many papers explored the friendship paradox from different perspectives, and proposed extensions. A few papers studied the friendship paradox from a
mathematical
perspective, using concepts from graph theory to describe social networks as graphs in which the vertices represent individuals and the edges represent friendships between individuals. The goal of these papers was to
quantify
the friendship paradox for several classes of sparse random graphs.

In
[
17
]
, the implications of the friendship paradox on systematic biases in perception and thought contagion are discussed. This study is based on the idea that social norms are influenced by our perceptions of the people around us. For instance, individuals are more likely to smoke when they have acquaintances who smoke
[
10
]
. In
[
23
]
, a new strategy for predicting election polls is proposed based on the friendship paradox. Moreover, research on the so-called
generalised friendship paradox
investigates how attributes other than popularity can produce similar phenomena. For instance, your co-authors have more citations, more publications and more collaborators than you do
[
11
]
, or your virtual friends receive more viral content than you do
[
14
]
. The behaviour of the friendship paradox on random graphs, such as the Erdős-Rényi random graph, has been explored in
[
8
]
and
[
25
]
. In
[
8
]
, it is argued that the generalised friendship paradox should hold when the attribute in question correlates positively with the number of friends.

A few papers have investigated the friendship paradox from a probabilistic perspective. In
[
9
]
, for instance, it is proved that a randomly chosen friend of a randomly selected person has stochastically more friends than that person has. In
[
13
]
, a new notion called
significant friendship paradox
is introduced for locally tree-like random graphs. First, the
friendship bias
of a given vertex is defined, both for simple graphs and multi-graphs, as the difference between the average degree of the neighbours of the vertex and the degree of the vertex itself. The friendship paradox is defined to be significant for a locally tree-like random graph if the proportion of vertices with non-negative friendship bias converges in probability to a number in
[\tfrac{1}{2},1]
in the limit as the number of vertices tends to infinity. This notion is interesting because, although the friendship paradox holds in all finite graphs without self-loops, the number of vertices with negative friendship bias can vastly outnumber those with non-negative bias. Interestingly,
[
13
]
provides mathematical and numerical evidence that the friendship paradox is significant for several well-known classes of locally tree-like random graphs, namely, the homogeneous Erdős-Rényi random graph, the inhomogeneous Erdős-Rényi random graph, the configuration model and the preferential attachment model. This provides valuable insight into the structure of these random graphs.

1.2
The multi-level friendship paradox

While
[
12
]
and the previously mentioned studies primarily addressed the friendship paradox at the individual level, subsequent research in
[
20
]
extended the concept to higher levels. In
[
20
]
, the concept of
multi-step friendship paradox
was introduced, which considers the friendship paradox not only at the individual level but across multiple levels in the network hierarchy. It explores how the friendship paradox persists and evolves within communities, organisations, or entire populations. Later,
[
5
]
analysed the multi-level friendship paradox in more detail from a probabilistic perspective.

In the present paper we choose the higher levels via
exploration
, i,e., we run a random walk on the graph and register the random vertex that it visits after
k
steps. We focus on two types of exploration: backtracking random walk and non-backtracking random walk, plus a ‘lazy’ version of backtracking random walk where the random walk has a strictly positive probability to stand still. Other choices would be possible as well, but we will not consider them. We look at the short-level and long-level behaviour of the friendship paradox in locally tree-like random graphs, where short level refers to small exploration depths and long-level refers to large exploration depths. We focus on the
empirical friendship-bias distribution

\mu_{n}^{(k)}
at depth
k
when the graph has
n
vertices. We identify the limit of
\mu_{n}^{(k)}
when
n\to\infty
and/or
k\to\infty
. We find that for non-backtracking exploration the two limits commute, while for backtracking exploration they do not when the local limit is a subcritical tree.

In the study of the short-level behaviour of the friendship paradox, we show that
\mu_{n}^{(k)}
converges weakly to some measure
\mu_{\infty}^{(k)}
as
n\to\infty
, for both types of exploration. However, to identify the weak limit of
\mu_{\infty}^{(k)}
as
k\to\infty
we must analyse the behaviour of the exploration on the limiting tree. A non-backtracking random walk on a tree can only move downward. Under the assumption that the offspring distributions are independent, we show that
\mu_{\infty}^{(k)}
converges weakly to some
\mu
as
k\to\infty
. In contrast, a backtracking random walk on a tree can move both upward and downward. We restrict our analysis to instances where the limiting tree is almost surely finite, for which it turns out that
\mu_{\infty}^{(k)}
converges weakly to some
\mu^{\star}
that is different from
\mu
.

In the study of the long-level behaviour of the friendship paradox, when the finite random graph on
n
vertices is also almost surely connected and non-bipartite, the backtracking random walk on it is ergodic, and its stationary distribution is the limit of the
k
-step transition probabilities. Consequently, the
k
-level friendship bias converges to a stationary friendship bias as
k\to\infty
. Using this property, we establish that the weak limit of
\mu_{n}^{(k)}
as
k\to\infty
corresponds to the empirical distribution of the stationary friendship biases almost surely. Moreover, by using properties of local convergence of random graphs, we prove that the limiting law in this setting is the measure
\mu
in probability. This result demonstrates that the limits of
\mu_{n}^{(k)}
as
k\to\infty
and
n\to\infty
do not always commute. For the non-backtracking exploration, we model each edge of the graph as two directed edges in opposite directions and employ the non-backtracking random walk on directed edges. Notably, this approach yields the same result as the backtracking exploration. Since the assumption of almost sure connectedness may be too restrictive, we extend our results to more general random graphs, including bipartite or disconnected graphs, by incorporating a lazy exploration technique.

We also study the limit of the empirical friendship-bias distribution at a depth that depends on
n
, i.e.,
k=k_{n}
, for choices of
k_{n}
satisfying
\lim_{n\to\infty}k_{n}=\infty
. In the case when
k_{n}
is much larger than the mixing time of the exploration we show that, under some mild additional assumptions, the limit is
\mu
for both types of exploration. In the case when the mixing time is much smaller than the mixing time, we focus on the configuration model and non-backtracking exploration, and show that the limit remains
\mu
. Here, we make use of the possibility to couple graph explorations with certain branching processes in the configuration model. Finally, we show that, under certain uniform control conditions on the behaviour of
\mu_{n}^{(k)}
with respect to both
n
and
k
,
\mu_{n}^{(k_{n})}
converges weakly to the measure
\mu
in probability for both types of exploration and any choice of
k_{n}
satisfying
\lim_{n\to\infty}k_{n}=\infty
.

The key tool in our analysis is the notion of
local convergence
, which captures what a random graph looks like when viewed from a vertex that is chosen uniformly at random in the limit as
n\to\infty
. We combine this tool with properties of the local limit and properties of the exploration on the local limit in order to
quantify
the multi-level friendship paradox. An important contribution of our paper is that it gives a
geometric
clarification of how the bias that is inherent in the friendship paradox behaves as a function of the size of the graph and the depth of the exploration, and how it settles down to a limiting bias as both the size and the depth tend to infinity.

2.1
Graphs

Throughout this paper, a graph
G=(V(G),E(G))
is an undirected simple graph or multi-graph. The vertices in
V(G)
represent individuals, the edges in
E(G)
represent mutual friendships. We use two-membered sets in
V(G)
to represent edges, and in the presence of multiple edges
E(G)
is treated as a multi-set. Also a self-loop is represented in
E(G)
by a multi-set. Denote by

\displaystyle(d_{i}^{(G)})_{i\in V(G)},\qquad A^{(G)}=(A_{i,j}^{(G)})_{i,j\in V(G)},

the
degree sequence
, respectively, the
adjacency matrix
of
G
, where
A_{i,j}^{(G)}
is the number of edges connecting
i
and
j
. Each self-loop adds 2 to the degree. The pair
(G,o)
will mean that
G
is a
rooted
graph with a root vertex
o
.

In the sequel, we set the empty sum equal to
0
, and set the empty product equals to
1
. For
r\in\mathbb{Z}
, we abbreviate
\mathbb{N}_{r}=\{r,r+1,\ldots\}
. We use the standard notion for asymptotic order as
n\to\infty
:
a_{n}=\Theta(b_{n})
when
a_{n}
is of the same order as
b_{n}
,
a_{n}=O(b_{n})
when
a_{n}
is at most of order
b_{n}
, and
a_{n}=o(b_{n})
when
a_{n}
is of smaller order than
b_{n}
.

2.2
Non-backtracking exploration

Random graphs.

Let
G_{n}
be a simple random graph with
n\in\mathbb{N}_{3}
vertices labelled by
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
with minimum degree of at least
2
, defined on a probability space
(\Omega_{n},\mathcal{F}_{n},\mathbb{P}_{n})
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{3}
and
\omega\in\Omega_{n}
, we construct a non-backtracking random walk
X_{n}(\omega)=(X_{n,k}(\omega))_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
with state space
[n]
on a common probability space
(\Omega_{n}^{\mathrm{rw}},\mathcal{F}_{n}^{\mathrm{rw}},\mathbb{P}_{n}^{\mathrm{rw}})
as follows. The initial state
X_{n,0}(\omega)
is chosen uniformly at random from
[n]
. At each subsequent step,
X_{n,k+1}(\omega)
is chosen uniformly at random from the neighbours of
X_{n,k}(\omega)
in the graph
G_{n}(\omega)
, with the condition that it does not return to the state visited immediately before.

Formally, if
P_{n}^{(k)}(i,j)
is the probability that the non-backtracking random walk
X_{n}
starting at vertex
i
ends up at vertex
j
after
k
steps, then for
\omega\in\Omega_{n}
,
X_{n}(\omega)=(X_{n,k}(\omega))_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a time-homogeneous stochastic process with initial distribution
\pi_{n,0}
defined as

\displaystyle\pi_{n,0}\big(\{i\}\big)=\frac{1}{n},\qquad i\in[n],

and transition kernels
(P_{n}^{(k)}(\cdot,\cdot)(\omega))_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
with
P_{n}^{(k)}(\cdot,\cdot)(\omega)\colon\,[n]\times 2^{[n]}\rightarrow[0,1]
defined by

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(k)}(i,A)(\omega)=\mathbb{P}_{n}^{\mathrm{rw}}\big\{X_{n,r+k}(\omega)\in A\mid X_{n,r}(\omega)=i\big\}=\sum_{j\in A}P_{n}^{(k)}(i,j)(\omega),\qquad r\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

where
P_{n}^{(0)}(i,j)(\omega)=\delta_{j}(\{i\})
, and
\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac measure at
x
. It is important to note that
(P_{n}^{(k)}(\cdot,\cdot)(\omega))_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
does not satisfy the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation, as
X_{n}(\omega)
is not a Markov chain.

To specify the transition probabilities more precisely, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, define the set of non-backtracking
k
-walks as follows:

\displaystyle W_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\Big\{(i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in[n]^{k+1}:\{i_{l},i_{l+1}\}\in E(G_{n})\text{ for all }0\leq l\leq k-1,

\displaystyle\qquad\text{ and if }k\geq 2,\text{then also }i_{l^{\prime}-1}\neq i_{l^{\prime}+1}\text{ for all }1\leq l^{\prime}\leq k-1\Big\}.

Also, for
k\geq 2
and
i,j\in[n]
, set

\displaystyle W_{n,k}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\Big\{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1})\in[n]^{k-1}\colon\,(i,i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1},j)\in W_{n,k}\Big\}.

Indeed, for
i,j\in[n]
,

P_{n}(i,j)=P_{n}^{(1)}(i,j)=\frac{A_{i,j}^{(G_{n})}}{d_{i}^{(G_{n})}},

(2.1)

and

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(k)}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1})\in W_{n,k}(i,j)}\dfrac{1}{d^{(G_{n})}_{i}\,(d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{1}}-1)\,\cdots\,(d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k-1}}-1)}

(2.2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1})\in[n]^{k-1}}\frac{A^{(G_{n})}_{i,i_{1}}}{d^{(G_{n})}_{i}}\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}\frac{A^{(G_{n})}_{i_{l},i_{l+1}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{i_{l-1}\neq i_{l+1}\}}}{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{l}}-1},\qquad k\geq 2,

where we have taken
i_{0}=i
and
i_{k}=j
in the last line.

To check that the transition probabilities are normalised, note that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[n]}A^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k-2},i_{k-1}}\frac{A^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k-1},j}\mathbbm{1}_{\{i_{k-2}\neq j\}}}{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k-1}}-1}=A^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k-2},i_{k-1}}\frac{\sum_{j\in[n]}A^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k-1},j}\mathbbm{1}_{\{i_{k-2}\neq j\}}}{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k-1}}-A^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k-2},i_{k-1}}}=A^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k-2},i_{k-1}}.

For
k\geq 2
, this gives

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[n]}P_{n}^{(k)}(i,j)=\sum_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1})\in[n]^{k-1}}\frac{A^{(G_{n})}_{i,i_{1}}}{d^{(G_{n})}_{i}}\prod_{l=1}^{k-2}\frac{A^{(G_{n})}_{i_{l},i_{l+1}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{i_{l-1}\neq i_{l+1}\}}}{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{l}}-1},

which for
k\geq 3
equals

\displaystyle\sum_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-2})\in[n]^{k-2}}\frac{A^{(G_{n})}_{i,i_{1}}}{d^{(G_{n})}_{i}}\prod_{l=1}^{k-3}\frac{A^{(G_{n})}_{i_{l},i_{l+1}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{i_{l-1}\neq i_{l+1}\}}}{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{l}}-1}.

Continuing this recursion, we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[n]}P_{n}^{(k)}(i,j)=\sum_{j\in[n]}\frac{A_{i,j}^{(G_{n})}}{d_{i}^{(G_{n})}}=1,\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

Rooted random trees.

Consider an almost surely locally finite infinite rooted random tree
(G_{\infty},\phi)
, defined on a probability space
(\Omega_{\bar{\mu}},\mathcal{F}_{\bar{\mu}},\mathbb{P}_{\bar{\mu}})
, where we write
\bar{\mu}
to denote the law of
(G_{\infty},\phi)
. Let
d_{j}
be the number of
offspring
of vertex
j\in V(G_{\infty})
, and
d^{(G_{\infty})}_{j}
be the
degree
of
j\in V(G_{\infty})
. Note that
d^{(G_{\infty})}_{\phi}=d_{\phi}
and
d^{(G_{\infty})}_{j}=d_{j}+1
for
j\in V(G_{\infty})\setminus\{\phi\}
. For
\omega\in\Omega_{\bar{\mu}}
, we construct a non-backtracking random walk
X(\omega)=(X_{k}(\omega))_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
on the tree
(G_{\infty},\phi)
by setting
X_{0}(\omega)=\phi
and, at level
k+1
, choosing
X_{k+1}(\omega)
uniformly at random among the offspring of
X_{k}(\omega)
. Clearly,
X(\omega)
is a Markov chain. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, take
i_{0}=\phi
and suppose that

\displaystyle I_{k}

\displaystyle=\Big\{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in(V(G_{\infty}))^{k}\colon\,\{i_{l},i_{l+1}\}\in E(G_{\infty})\text{ for all }0\leq l\leq k-1,

\displaystyle\quad\quad\text{ and if }k\geq 2,\text{then also }i_{l^{\prime}-1}\neq i_{l^{\prime}+1}\text{ for all }1\leq l^{\prime}\leq k-1\Big\}.

Considering that
(G_{\infty},\phi)
is an infinite tree, this implies that
I_{k}
is non-empty for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
. For
j\in V(G_{\infty})
, let
P^{k}(\phi,j)
be the probability that the non-backtracking random walk
X
starting at the root
\phi
ends up at vertex
j
of the tree after
k
steps, i.e.,

\displaystyle P^{k}(\phi,j)=\sum_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1}):\,(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1},j)\in I_{k}}\frac{1}{d_{\phi}\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}d_{i_{l}}}=\sum_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1}):\,(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1},j)\in I_{k}}\frac{1}{d_{\phi}\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}(d^{(G_{\infty})}_{i_{l}}-1)}.

Note that
\#\{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1})\colon\,(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1},j)\in I_{k}\}\in\{0,1\}
.

2.3
Backtracking exploration

Random graphs.

Let
G_{n}
be a finite random graph without self-loops and isolated points on
n\in\mathbb{N}_{2}
vertices labelled by
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, defined on a probability space
(\Omega_{n},\mathcal{F}_{n},\mathbb{P}_{n})
. Let us construct a backtracking random walk
X_{n}=(X_{n,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
on the graph
G_{n}
by picking
X_{n,0}
uniformly at random from
[n]
, and at level
k+1
letting
X_{n,k+1}
be chosen uniformly at random from the neighbours of
X_{n,k}
. In other words, for
\omega\in\Omega_{n}
, the stochastic process
X_{n}(\omega)=(X_{n,k}(\omega))_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a time-homogeneous Markov chain on state space
[n]
with initial distribution
\pi_{n,0}
defined as

\displaystyle\pi_{n,0}\big(\{i\}\big)=\frac{1}{n},\qquad i\in[n],

and with transition matrix
P_{n}(\omega)=\big(P_{n}(i,j)(\omega)\big)_{i,j\in[n]}
given by

P_{n}(i,j)(\omega)=\frac{A_{i,j}^{(G_{n})}(\omega)}{d_{i}^{(G_{n})}(\omega)},\qquad i,j\in[n].

(2.3)

We write
P_{n}^{(k)}=P_{n}^{k}
, the
k
-th power of
P_{n}
, to denote the
k
-step transition matrix of the backtracking random walk, which is the same notation as used for the non-backtracking random walk.

Rooted random trees.

Let
(G_{\infty},\phi)
be an almost surely locally finite non-trivial rooted random tree, defined on a probability space
(\Omega_{\bar{\mu}},\mathcal{F}_{\bar{\mu}},\mathbb{P}_{\bar{\mu}})
, where we use the same notations as in Section
2.2
for the tree
(G_{\infty},\phi)
. We construct a backtracking random walk
X=(X_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
on the tree
(G_{\infty},\phi)
as follows. Set
X_{0}=\phi
and, at level
k+1
, select
X_{k+1}
uniformly at random from the neighbours of
X_{k}
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, suppose that

\displaystyle J_{k}=\Big\{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in(V(G_{\infty}))^{k}\colon\,\{i_{j},i_{j+1}\}\in E(G_{\infty})\text{ for all }0\leq j\leq k-1\Big\},

where
i_{0}=\phi
. Since
(G_{\infty},\phi)
is non-trivial,
J_{k}
is non-empty for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
. For
j\in V(G_{\infty})
, let
P^{k}(\phi,j)
be the probability that the backtracking random walk
X
ends up at
j
after
k
steps, i.e.,

\displaystyle P^{k}(\phi,j)=\sum_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1})\colon\,(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1},j)\in J_{k}}\frac{1}{d_{\phi}\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}d^{(G_{\infty})}_{i_{l}}}.

2.4
The multi-level friendship bias

As an extension of the concepts introduced in
[
13
]
, we introduce the following.

Definition 2.1
.

(a) For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in[n]
, the
k
-level friendship bias
for vertex
i
is defined as

\displaystyle\Delta_{i,n}^{(k)}=\sum_{j\in[n]}P_{n}^{(k)}(i,j)\,d_{j}^{(G_{n})}-d_{i}^{(G_{n})}.

(b) The
k
-level friendship bias for the root
\phi
of the tree
(G_{\infty},\phi)
is defined as

\displaystyle\Delta_{\phi}^{(k)}=\sum_{j\in V(G_{\infty})}P^{k}(\phi,j)\,d^{(G_{\infty})}_{j}-d_{\phi}.

Define
\mu^{(k)}_{\infty}\colon\,\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\to[0,1]
to be the law of
\Delta_{\phi}^{(k)}
. Expectation with respect to
(G_{\infty},\phi)
is denoted by
\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}}
.

\spadesuit

Note that the
1
-level friendship bias
\Delta_{i,n}^{(1)}
obtained through non-backtracking exploration and backtracking exploration are identical and coincide with the definition of
\Delta_{i,n}
in
[
13
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

(a) The
k
-level quenched friendship bias empirical distribution

\mu_{n}^{(k)}\colon\,\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\to[0,1]
is defined as

\displaystyle\mu_{n}^{(k)}(\cdot)=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\delta_{\Delta_{i,n}^{(k)}}(\cdot),

where
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbb{R}
.

(b) The
k
-level annealed friendship bias empirical distribution

\tilde{\mu}_{n}^{(k)}\colon\,\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\to[0,1]
is defined as

\tilde{\mu}_{n}^{(k)}(\cdot)=\mathbb{E}_{n}\big[\mu_{n}^{(k)}(\cdot)\big],

where
\mathbb{E}_{n}
denotes the expectation with respect to the random graph
G_{n}
.

(c) The
average
k
-level friendship bias
is defined as

\Delta_{[n]}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\Delta_{i,n}^{(k)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\,\mu_{n}^{(k)}(\mathrm{d}x),

with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n}\big[\Delta_{[n]}^{(k)}\big]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}_{n}\big[\Delta_{i,n}^{(k)}\big]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\,\tilde{\mu}_{n}^{(k)}(\mathrm{d}x).

\spadesuit

Note that, for fixed
\omega\in\Omega_{n}
,

Let

\displaystyle\vec{E}(G_{n})=\Big\{\overset{\rightarrow}{\{i,j\}}:\{i,j\}\in E(G_{n})\Big\}.

6.1
Average multi-level friendship bias

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

First consider the non-backtracking exploration. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, using (
2.1
)–(
2.2
) and symmetrisation we get

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i_{0}\in[n]}\sum_{i_{k}\in[n]}P_{n}^{(k)}(i_{0},i_{k})\,d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2n}\sum_{i_{0}\in[n]}\sum_{i_{k}\in[n]}\Big(P_{n}^{(k)}(i_{0},i_{k})\,d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k}}+P_{n}^{(k)}(i_{k},i_{0})\,d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{0}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2n}\sum_{(i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in W_{n,k}}\bigg(\frac{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k}}}{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{0}}}+\frac{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{0}}}{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k}}}\bigg)\,\frac{1}{\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}(d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{l}}-1)}

and

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i_{0}\in[n]}d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{0}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2n}\sum_{i_{0}\in[n]}\sum_{i_{k}\in[n]}\Big(P_{n}^{(k)}(i_{0},i_{k})\,d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{0}}+P_{n}^{(k)}(i_{k},i_{0})\,d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{(i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in W_{n,k}}\frac{1}{\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}(d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{l}}-1)}.

Hence

\displaystyle\Delta_{[n]}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2n}\sum_{(i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in W_{n,k}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k}}}{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{0}}}}-\sqrt{\frac{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{0}}}{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k}}}}\,\right)^{2}\,\frac{1}{\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}(d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{l}}-1)}\geq 0,

with equality if and only if the degrees of the endpoints of each non-backtracking walk in
W_{n,k}
are the same.

Next consider the backtracking exploration. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, set

V_{n,k}=\Big\{(i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in[n]^{k+1}\colon\,\{i_{j},i_{j+1}\}\in E(G_{n})\text{ for all }0\leq j\leq k-1\Big\}.

Then (
2.3
), along with the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation, implies that

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i_{0}\in[n]}\sum_{i_{k}\in[n]}P_{n}^{(k)}(i_{0},i_{k})\,d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2n}\sum_{i_{0}\in[n]}\sum_{i_{k}\in[n]}\Big(P_{n}^{(k)}(i_{0},i_{k})\,d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k}}+P_{n}^{(k)}(i_{k},i_{0})\,d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{0}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2n}\sum_{(i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in V_{n,k}}\bigg(\frac{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k}}}{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{0}}}+\frac{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{0}}}{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k}}}\bigg)\,\frac{1}{\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{l}}}

and

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i_{0}\in[n]}d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{0}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2n}\sum_{i_{0}\in[n]}\sum_{i_{k}\in[n]}\Big(P_{n}^{(k)}(i_{0},i_{k})\,d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{0}}+P_{n}^{(k)}(i_{k},i_{0})\,d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{(i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in V_{n,k}}\frac{1}{\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{l}}}.

Hence

\displaystyle\Delta_{[n]}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2n}\sum_{(i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in V_{n,k}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k}}}{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{0}}}}-\sqrt{\frac{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{0}}}{d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{k}}}}\,\right)^{2}\,\frac{1}{\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}d^{(G_{n})}_{i_{l}}}\geq 0,

with equality if and only if the degrees of the endpoints of each backtracking walk in
V_{n,k}
are the same.

For odd values of
k
, by traversing
k
times from each edge, it is seen that the equality holds if and only if every connected component of the graph is regular. But for even values of
k
this is equivalent to the case where each connected component of the graph is either regular or bi-regular bipartite. Indeed, in the case when each connected component of the graph is either regular or bi-regular bipartite, and
k
is even, it is clear that the degrees of the endpoints of each backtracking walk in
V_{n,k}
are equal. However, to prove the converse, first note that for any even
l
, equality of endpoint degrees in walks of
V_{n,l}
holds if and only if the same equality holds for walks in
V_{n,2}
. Now, consider an arbitrary connected component of
G_{n}
, say
C_{n}
, and let
k
be an even integer. Suppose that the degrees of the endpoints of each walk in
V_{n,k}
are equal and that
C_{n}
is not a bipartite graph. Since
C_{n}
is not bipartite, there is an odd cycle in
C_{n}
. By traversing the odd cycle as many times as needed, any two vertices of
C_{n}
can be joined by a walk in
V_{n,l}
with some even
l
, which ensures that they must have the same degrees, indicating that
C_{n}
is regular. On the other hand, if the degrees of the endpoints of each walk in
V_{n,k}
are the same and
C_{n}
is a bipartite graph, then any two vertices on the same side can be joined by a walk in
V_{n,l}
with an even
l
. Therefore they must have the same degrees, implying that
C_{n}
is bi-regular bipartite.
∎

6.2
Convergence of the measures

For a rooted graph
(G,o)
, let
B_{r}^{(G)}(o)=((V(B_{r}^{(G)}(o)),E(B_{r}^{(G)}(o))),o)
be a rooted subgraph with root vertex
o
, defined as follows:

\displaystyle V(B_{r}^{(G)}(o))

\displaystyle=\big\{i\in V(G)\colon\,\mathrm{dist}_{G}(o,i)\leq r\big\},

\displaystyle E(B_{r}^{(G)}(o))

\displaystyle=\big\{\{i,j\}\in E(G)\colon\,\max\{\mathrm{dist}_{G}(o,i),\mathrm{dist}_{G}(o,j)\}\leq r\big\},

where
\mathrm{dist}_{G}
represents the graph distance in
G
. We write
G_{1}\simeq G_{2}
to denote two graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are
isomorphic
.

Let
\mathscr{G}
be the set of all connected locally finite rooted graphs equipped with the metric

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\mathscr{G}}\big((G_{1},o_{1}),(G_{2},o_{2})\big)=\frac{1}{1+\sup\{r\geq 0\colon\,B_{r}^{(G_{1})}(o_{1})\simeq B_{r}^{(G_{2})}(o_{2})\}}.

Note that
\mathrm{d}_{\mathscr{G}}
is considered a metric, following the convention that two connected locally finite rooted graphs
(G_{1},o_{1})
and
(G_{2},o_{2})
are identified when
(G_{1},o_{1})\simeq(G_{2},o_{2})
.

6.2.1
Convergence for short levels

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We give the proof for the non-backtracking exploration only. A similar approach can be applied to the backtracking exploration.

Define an everywhere locally finite modification of
(G_{\infty},\phi)
by

\displaystyle(G_{\infty}^{\prime}(\omega),\phi)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(G_{\infty}(\omega),\phi),&\text{if }(G_{\infty}(\omega),\phi)\text{ is locally finite},\\
(H,\phi),&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

where
(H,\phi)
is an arbitrary locally finite infinite deterministic rooted tree (rooted at
\phi
). To simplify the notation, we assume, without loss of generality, that
(G_{\infty},\phi)\equiv(G_{\infty}^{\prime},\phi)
. Moreover, let
\mathscr{C}(G_{n},j)
denote the connected component of vertex
j\in V(G_{n})
in the graph
G_{n}
, viewed as a rooted graph with
j
as the root vertex. When
G_{n}
is connected, we simply have
\mathscr{C}(G_{n},j)=(G_{n},j)
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
U_{n}
be a uniformly chosen vertex from
[n]
. The proof below here follows the lines of the proof of
[
13
, Theorems 2.3–2.4]
, and is a direct consequence of the definition of local convergence of random graphs and weak convergence of measures.

(a) Since
G_{n}
converges locally in probability to
(G_{\infty},\phi)
, for every bounded and continuous function
h\colon\,(\mathscr{G},\mathrm{d}_{\mathscr{G}})\to(\mathbb{R},|\cdot|)
we have

\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}_{n}}\big[h(\mathscr{C}(G_{n},U_{n}))\mid G_{n}\big]=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}h(\mathscr{C}(G_{n},i))\overset{\mathrm{pr.}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}}\big[h((G_{\infty},\phi))\big],\qquad n\to\infty,

(6.3)

where the expectation in the left-hand side is the conditional expected value given
G_{n}
when
(G_{n},U_{n})
has the joint law
\bar{\mu}_{n}
.

Now, let
f\colon\,(\mathbb{R},|\cdot|)\to(\mathbb{R},|\cdot|)
be an arbitrary bounded and continuous function. Define the function
h\colon\,(\mathscr{G},\mathrm{d}_{\mathscr{G}})\to(\mathbb{R},|\cdot|)
as follows:

h((G,o))=(f\circ g)((G,o)),

(6.4)

where, with
i_{0}=o
, the function
g\colon\,(\mathscr{G},\mathrm{d}_{\mathscr{G}})\to(\mathbb{R},|\cdot|)
is defined as

with

\Delta_{o,k}^{(G)}=\sum_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in(V(G))^{k}}\frac{A^{(G)}_{i_{0},i_{1}}}{d^{(G)}_{i_{0}}}\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}\frac{A^{(G)}_{i_{l},i_{l+1}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{i_{l-1}\neq i_{l+1}\}}}{d^{(G)}_{i_{l}}-1}\,d^{(G)}_{i_{k}}-d^{(G)}_{i_{0}}.

(6.5)

Note that the boundedness of
f
implies the boundedness of
h
. Also, for any
(G_{1},o_{1}),(G_{2},o_{2})\in\mathscr{G}
, if
\mathrm{d}_{\mathscr{G}}((G_{1},o_{1}),(G_{2},o_{2}))<\frac{1}{k+1}
, then

\sup\big\{r\geq 0\colon\,B_{r}^{(G_{1})}(o_{1})\simeq B_{r}^{(G_{2})}(o_{2})\big\}>k.

This implies that
B_{r}^{(G_{1})}(o_{1})\simeq B_{r}^{(G_{2})}(o_{2})
for all
r\in\{0,1,\ldots,k\}
. Consequently,
g((G_{1},o_{1}))=g((G_{2},o_{2}))
, which demonstrates that
g
is continuous. Therefore
h
, being a composition of two continuous functions, is also continuous. Furthermore,
h(\mathscr{C}(G_{n},i))=f(\Delta_{i,n}^{(k)})
and
h((G_{\infty},\phi))=f(\Delta_{\phi}^{(k)})
a.s. Inserting this into (
6.3
), we get

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}^{(k)}=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}f(\Delta_{i,n}^{(k)})\overset{\mathrm{pr.}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}}\big[f(\Delta_{\phi}^{(k)})\big]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\mathrm{d}\mu^{(k)}_{\infty},

which completes the proof that
\mu_{n}^{(k)}\Longrightarrow\mu^{(k)}_{\infty}
as
n\to\infty
in probability.

Suppose that
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
, and consider the function
h\colon\,(\mathscr{G},\mathrm{d}_{\mathscr{G}})\to(\mathbb{R},|\cdot|)
defined by

\displaystyle h((G,o))=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\Delta_{o,k}^{(G)}\in A\}},

(6.6)

with
\Delta_{o,k}^{(G)}
defined as in (
6.5
). For any
(G_{1},o_{1}),(G_{2},o_{2})\in\mathscr{G}
, if
\mathrm{d}_{\mathscr{G}}((G_{1},o_{1}),(G_{2},o_{2}))<\frac{1}{k+1}
, then
B_{r}^{(G_{1})}(o_{1})\simeq B_{r}^{(G_{2})}(o_{2})
for
r\in\{0,1,\ldots,k\}
. Hence,
h((G_{1},o_{1}))=h((G_{2},o_{2}))
, which implies that
h
is continuous. In this way, from (
6.3
), we get
\mu_{n}^{(k)}(A)\overset{\mathrm{pr.}}{\longrightarrow}\mu^{(k)}_{\infty}(A)
. Applying the dominated convergence theorem, we also get that
\tilde{\mu}_{n}^{(k)}(A)\to\mu^{(k)}_{\infty}(A)
.

(b) Let
\mu_{i,n}^{(k)}(\cdot):=\mathbb{E}_{n}[\delta_{\Delta_{i,n}^{(k)}}(\cdot)]
, which is the law of
\Delta_{i,n}^{(k)}
. For an arbitrary bounded and continuous function
f\colon\,(\mathbb{R},|\cdot|)\to(\mathbb{R},|\cdot|)
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu}_{n}^{(k)}=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\mathrm{d}\mu_{i,n}^{(k)}=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}_{n}\big[f(\Delta_{i,n}^{(k)})\big].

(6.7)

Let
h
be as in (
6.4
). Since
G_{n}
converges locally weakly to
(G_{\infty},\phi)
, we must have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}_{n}}\big[h(\mathscr{C}(G_{n},U_{n}))\big]\to\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}}\big[h((G_{\infty},\phi))\big],\qquad n\to\infty,

(6.8)

where the expectation in the left-hand side is the expected value when
(G_{n},U_{n})
has the joint law
\bar{\mu}_{n}
. But

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}_{n}}\big[h(\mathscr{C}(G_{n},U_{n}))\big]=\mathbb{E}_{n}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}_{n}}\big[h(\mathscr{C}(G_{n},U_{n}))\mid G_{n}\big]\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{n}\Big[\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}h(\mathscr{C}(G_{n},i))\Big]

(6.9)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}_{n}\big[f(\Delta_{i,n}^{(k)})\big]

(6.10)

and
\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}}[h((G_{\infty},\phi))]=\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}}[f(\Delta_{\phi}^{(k)})]
. Hence, by (
6.7
), (
6.8
) and (

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu}_{n}^{(k)}\to\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}}\big[f(\Delta_{\phi}^{(k)})\big]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\mathrm{d}\mu^{(k)}_{\infty},

which completes the proof that
\tilde{\mu}_{n}^{(k)}\Longrightarrow\mu^{(k)}_{\infty}
as

.

Finally, let
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
. By substituting the bounded and continuous function
h
defined in (
6.6
) into (
6.8
), and using (
6.9
), we conclude that
\tilde{\mu}_{n}^{(k)}(A)\to\mu^{(k)}_{\infty}(A)
.
∎

6.2.2
Identification of the limit for short levels

In this section, we prove Theorem
3.3
. To begin, we shall first state a general lemma that will also be utilised in the proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
(H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of finite non-null random graphs that converges locally weakly to an almost surely locally finite, connected rooted graph
(H,\varphi)
with a deterministic law
\nu
. If the degrees of the neighbours of the root
\varphi
are i.i.d., and independent of
d_{\varphi}^{(H)}
, and if
d_{\varphi}^{(H)}
has a finite first moment, then the degree distribution of the neighbours of
\varphi
is size-biased with respect to the degree distribution of
\varphi
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that
(H,\varphi)
is everywhere locally finite, following a similar approach to the construction in (
6.2.1
). As an extension of
(\mathscr{G},\mathrm{d}_{\mathscr{G}})
, we define
\mathscr{G}_{\star}
as the topological space of isomorphism classes of connected locally finite graphs with an ordered pair of root vertices, equipped with the topology induced by a natural extension of the metric
\mathrm{d}_{\mathscr{G}}
.

Since
(H,\varphi)
is a local weak limit, the probability measure
\nu
is unimodular and obeys
the mass-transport principle
[
4
, Section 3.2]
. Therefore, it is involution invariant
[
2
, Propositon 2.2]
, i.e. for all Borel function
h:\mathscr{G}_{\star}\to[0,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigg[\sum_{w\in\partial_{\varphi}}h\big((H,\varphi,w)\big)\bigg]=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigg[\sum_{w\in\partial_{\varphi}}h\big((H,w,\varphi)\big)\bigg],

where
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}
is the expectation with respect to the random graph
(H,\varphi)
, and
\partial_{\varphi}=V(B_{1}^{(H)}(\varphi))\setminus\{\varphi\}
is the set of neighbours of
\varphi
. In particular, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, by considering the Borel function
h_{k}
on
\mathscr{G}_{\star}
as
h_{k}((G,u,v))=\mathbbm{1}_{\{d_{v}^{(G)}=k\}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigg[\sum_{w\in\partial_{\varphi}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{d_{w}^{(H)}=k\}}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigg[k\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{d_{\varphi}^{(H)}=k\}}\bigg]=k\,\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\big\{d_{\varphi}^{(H)}=k\big\},

where
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
is the probability measure on the underlying space of the random graph
(H,\varphi)
. Noting that the degrees of the neighbours of the root
\varphi
are i.i.d. and independent of
d_{\varphi}^{(H)}
, we conclude that if
w_{0}
is a neighbour of
\varphi
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigg[\sum_{w\in\partial_{\varphi}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{d_{w}^{(H)}=k\}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\Big[\sum_{w\in\partial_{\varphi}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{d_{w}^{(H)}=k\}}\mid d_{\varphi}^{(H)}=j\Big]\,\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\big\{d_{\varphi}^{(H)}=j\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\big[d_{\varphi}^{(H)}\big]\,\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\big\{d_{w_{0}}^{(H)}=k\big\}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\big[d_{\varphi}^{(H)}\big]\,\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\big\{d_{w_{0}}^{(H)}=k\big\}=k\,\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\big\{d_{\varphi}^{(H)}=k\big\}.

In other words, by noting that
0<\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[d_{\varphi}^{(H)}]<\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\big\{d_{w_{0}}^{(H)}=k\big\}=\dfrac{k\,\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\big\{d_{\varphi}^{(H)}=k\big\}}{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[d_{\varphi}^{(H)}]},

which completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

(a) Let us label the vertices of
(G_{\infty},\phi)
excluding the root by
\mathbb{N}
in an arbitrary manner, i.e.,
V(G_{\infty})=\mathbb{N}\cup\{\phi\}
. Let
m_{1}=\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}}[d_{1}]
and
m_{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}}[d_{1}^{2}]
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, set

\chi_{k}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}P^{k}(\phi,j)\,d^{(G_{\infty})}_{j}.

To complete the proof, it suffices to show that
\chi_{k}\overset{\mathrm{pr.}}{\longrightarrow}m_{1}+1
as
k\to\infty
for the non-backtracking exploration. This immediately allows us to conclude, by applying the continuous mapping theorem, that

\Delta_{\phi}^{(k)}=\chi_{k}-d_{\phi}\overset{\mathrm{pr.}}{\longrightarrow}m_{1}+1-d_{\phi},\quad\quad k\to\infty,

which settles the claim by noting that, according to Lemma
6.1
,
m_{1}+1=m^{(2)}/m^{(1)}
.

For the non-backtracking exploration,

\chi_{k}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}P^{k}(\phi,j)\,d_{j}+1.

Using conditions (I) and (II), we have that
d_{j}
and
P^{k}(\phi,j)
are independent for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover,
P^{k}(\phi,j)
is non-zero only if
j
is in the
k
th-generation after
\phi
on the tree, i.e. when
\mathrm{dist}_{G_{\infty}}(\phi,j)=k
. Therefore,
P^{k}(\phi,i)P^{k}(\phi,j)
is non-zero only if both
i
and
j
are in the
k
th-generation, and in this case, if
i\neq j
, we must have that
P^{k}(\phi,i)P^{k}(\phi,j)
is independent of
d_{i}
and
d_{j}
.
Therefore, by the monotone convergence theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}}\big[\chi_{k}\big]=m_{1}+1

(6.11)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}}\bigg[\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\mathbb{N}\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}P^{k}(\phi,i)\,P^{k}(\phi,j)\,d_{i}\,d_{j}\bigg]\leq m_{1}^{2}.

Hence, by the monotone convergence theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}}\big[\chi_{k}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq(m_{1}+1)^{2}+m_{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}}\bigg[\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\big(P^{k}(\phi,i)\big)^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq(m_{1}+1)^{2}+m_{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}}\bigg[\sup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}P^{k}(\phi,i)\bigg].

In the following, we prove that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}}\bigg[\sup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}P^{k}(\phi,i)\bigg]\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad k\to\infty.

(6.12)

Consequently, by applying Chebyshev’s inequality, we can conclude that
\chi_{k}\overset{\mathrm{pr.}}{\longrightarrow}m_{1}+1
as
k\to\infty
.

To prove (
6.12
), note that (
6.3
) holds for every bounded continuous function
h\colon(\mathscr{G},\mathrm{d}_{\mathscr{G}})\to(\mathbb{R},|\cdot|)
. If
h((G,o))
is the indicator function of the event that not all degrees in the graph

are at least 3 within some arbitrary finite graph distance from

, then (similarly as in the proof of the continuity of (
6.6
) in the proof of Theorem
3.1
) it can be shown that
h
is continuous. Since the minimum degree in the graph
G_{n}
is at least 3, applying this function
h
we get that the left-hand side of (
6.3
) is zero, which implies that the right-hand side of (
6.3
) must also be zero. As the distance is arbitrary, it follows that
d_{\phi}\geq 3
and
\inf_{i\in\mathbb{N}}d_{i}\geq 2
almost surely. Therefore
\sup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}P^{k}(\phi,i)<2^{-k}
almost surely, which proves (
6.12
).

(b)
Next consider the backtracking exploration. Let
\Omega^{\prime}\in\Omega_{\bar{\mu}}
be such that for all
\omega\in\Omega^{\prime}
the tree
(G_{\infty}(\omega),\phi)
is finite and non-bipartite. Then the backtracking random walk
X(\omega)
on it is ergodic, and so

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}P^{k}(\phi,j)(\omega)=\frac{d_{j}^{(G_{\infty})}(\omega)}{\sum_{j\in V(G_{\infty}(\omega))}d_{j}^{(G_{\infty})}(\omega)}

for all
j\in V(G_{\infty}(\omega))

[
24
, Theorem 1.8.3]
. Hence, as
k\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\phi}^{(k)}(\omega)=\sum_{j\in V(G_{\infty}(\omega))}P^{k}(\phi,j)(\omega)\,d^{(G_{\infty})}_{j}(\omega)-d_{\phi}(\omega)\to\frac{\sum_{j\in V(G_{\infty}(\omega))}(d_{j}^{(G_{\infty})}(\omega))^{2}}{\sum_{j\in V(G_{\infty}(\omega))}d_{j}^{(G_{\infty})}(\omega)}-d_{\phi}(\omega).

Since
\mathbb{P}_{\bar{\mu}}\{\Omega^{\prime}\}=1
, this completes the proof.
∎

6.2.3
Convergence and identification of the limit for long levels

In this section, we prove Theorem
4.1
. To proceed, we first state a general lemma that will also be used in the proof.

Lemma 6.2
.

If
(H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of finite random graphs with a deterministic vertex set that converges locally in probability to an almost surely locally finite, connected rooted graph
(H,\varphi)
, and if
((d_{i}^{(H_{n})})^{m})_{i\in V(H_{n}),\,n\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly integrable for some

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\#V(H_{n})}\sum_{i\in V(H_{n})}(d_{i}^{(H_{n})})^{j}\overset{\mathrm{pr.}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{E}_{H}\big[(d_{\varphi}^{(H)})^{j}\big],\qquad n\to\infty,

for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}

\mathbb{E}_{H}
denotes the expectation with respect to the randomness of the graph
(H,\varphi)
.

Proof.

Consider
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Without loss of generality, assume that
(H,\varphi)
is everywhere locally finite, in a manner similar to the construction in (

).

For

, define a bounded function

by setting

Then, in a manner analogous to the proof of the continuity of the function in (

is continuous. Therefore, by the local convergence in probability of

to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\#V(H_{n})}\sum_{i\in V(H_{n})}(d_{i}^{(H_{n})})^{j}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{d_{i}^{(H_{n})}\leq M\}}\overset{\mathrm{pr.}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{E}_{H}\Big[(d_{\varphi}^{(H)})^{j}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{d_{\varphi}^{(H)}\leq M\}}\Big],\qquad n\to\infty.

(6.13)

Moreover, by the monotone convergence theorem, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{H}\Big[(d_{\varphi}^{(H)})^{j}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{d_{\varphi}^{(H)}\leq M\}}\Big]\to\mathbb{E}_{H}\big[(d_{\varphi}^{(H)})^{j}\big]\qquad M\to\infty.

\epsilon>0

such that for all

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{H_{n}}\bigg\{\Big|\tfrac{1}{\#V(H_{n})}\sum_{i\in V(H_{n})}(d_{i}^{(H_{n})})^{j}-\mathbb{E}_{H}\big[(d_{\varphi}^{(H)})^{j}\big]\Big|>\epsilon\bigg\}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}_{H_{n}}\bigg\{\tfrac{1}{\#V(H_{n})}\sum_{i\in V(H_{n})}(d_{i}^{(H_{n})})^{j}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{d_{i}^{(H_{n})}>M\}}>\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{H_{n}}\bigg\{\Big|\tfrac{1}{\#V(H_{n})}\sum_{i\in V(H_{n})}(d_{i}^{(H_{n})})^{j}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{d_{i}^{(H_{n})}\leq M\}}-\mathbb{E}_{H}\big[(d_{\varphi}^{(H)})^{j}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{d_{\varphi}^{(H)}\leq M\}}\big]\Big|>\tfrac{\epsilon}{4}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2}{\epsilon}\,\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\,\sup_{i\in V(H_{n})}\mathbb{E}_{H_{n}}\bigg[(d_{i}^{(H_{n})})^{m}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{d_{i}^{(H_{n})}>M\}}\bigg],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n}\bigg[\Big(\sum_{j\in V_{k_{n}}(\mathrm{BP}_{n})}P_{n,\mathrm{BP}_{n}}^{(k_{n})}(\varnothing,j)\,d^{(\mathrm{BP}_{n})}_{j}\Big)^{2}\bigg]\leq M^{2}\,2^{-k_{n}}+\big(\mathbb{E}[D_{n}^{\star}]\big)^{2}\to\big(\mathbb{E}[D^{\star}]\big)^{2},\qquad n\to\infty.

Therefore, by the Chebyshev inequality,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in V_{k_{n}}(\mathrm{BP}_{n})}P_{n,\mathrm{BP}_{n}}^{(k_{n})}(\varnothing,j)\,d^{(\mathrm{BP}_{n})}_{j}\overset{\mathrm{pr.}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{E}[D^{\star}]=\dfrac{\mathbb{E}[D^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[D]},\qquad n\to\infty.

Hence, by the continuous mapping theorem and (
6.28
),

\displaystyle\Delta_{\varnothing,n,\mathrm{BP}_{n}}^{(k_{n})}\Longrightarrow\Delta_{\phi},\qquad n\to\infty,

and, by the continuity of
f
and the dominated convergence theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[f(\Delta_{\varnothing,n,\mathrm{BP}_{n}}^{(k_{n})})\big]\Longrightarrow\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}}\big[f(\Delta_{\phi})\big],\qquad n\to\infty.

(6.29)

Applying the Skorohod representation theorem, we can couple the random variables such that the convergence in (
6.27
) is almost sure. By the uniform continuity of
f
we can therefore conclude that

\displaystyle f(\Delta_{U_{n},n}^{(k_{n})})-f(\Delta_{\varnothing,n,\mathrm{BP}_{n}}^{(k_{n})})\overset{\mathrm{pr.}}{\longrightarrow}0,\qquad n\to\infty,

and so, by the dominated convergence theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}_{n}}\big[f(\Delta_{U_{n},n}^{(k_{n})})\mid G_{n}\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[f(\Delta_{\varnothing,n,\mathrm{BP}_{n}}^{(k_{n})})\big]\overset{\mathrm{pr.}}{\longrightarrow}0,\qquad n\to\infty.

This, together with (
6.29
) and the continuous mapping theorem, implies (
6.24
) and establishes the theorem.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.4
.

Consider a sequence
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying
\lim_{n\to\infty}k_{n}=\infty
. From the assumptions of the theorem and the Skorohod representation theorem, we can couple the random variables on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
such that

\displaystyle\Psi(N)\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}0,\qquad N\to\infty,

and

\displaystyle\mu_{n}^{(\infty)}\Longrightarrow\mu,\qquad n\to\infty\qquad\text{almost surely.}

(6.30)

Here, for simplicity, we have used the same notation for the new random variables as for the original ones. Since weak convergence in Polish spaces is equivalent to convergence in the Prohorov metric
[
6
, Theorem 6.8]
, from (
6.30
) we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{P}(\mu_{n}^{(\infty)},\mu)\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}0,\qquad n\to\infty.

As a result, there exists
\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega
with
\mathbb{P}\{\Omega^{\prime}\}=1
such that for any
\omega\in\Omega^{\prime}
and any
\epsilon>0
, we can find
N_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all
n,N\geq N_{\epsilon}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{P}(\mu_{n}^{(k_{n})},\mu)\leq\mathrm{d}_{P}(\mu_{n}^{(k_{n})},\mu_{n}^{(\infty)})+\mathrm{d}_{P}(\mu_{n}^{(\infty)},\mu)\leq\Psi(N)+\mathrm{d}_{P}(\mu_{n}^{(\infty)},\mu)<\epsilon.

Therefore
\mathrm{d}_{P}(\mu_{n}^{(k_{n})},\mu)\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}0
as
n\to\infty
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, which implies that in the original probability space,
\mu_{n}^{(k_{n})}\Longrightarrow\mu
as
n\to\infty
in probability, which completes the proof of the theorem.
∎
