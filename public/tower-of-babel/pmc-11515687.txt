Low PD-L1 expression, MAP2K2 alterations, and enriched HPV gene signatures characterize brain metastases in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma

ABSTRACT

Background

Brain metastasis (BM) is a rare complication of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) that carries a high rate of morbidity and poor prognosis [1–5]. The median overall survival after BM diagnosis is reported as 2–5 months [3, 6, 7]. Clinical risk factors for BM are not well defined, but the majority of patients who develop BMs present with an advanced stage and typically have metastases at other distant sites [3, 6, 8]. The molecular characteristics and immunogenicity of BMs in HNSCC are still largely unexplored, leaving a critical knowledge gap in this field.

While a few studies have hinted at possible associations between HNSCC BMs and human papillomavirus (HPV)-related oropharyngeal tumors or TP53 mutations, the true significance of these findings remains uncertain due to conflicting results and limited sample sizes [6, 8–10]. The association of TP53 mutation and BMs is further complicated by the fact that all 5 patients with a TP53 mutation in the study by Bulut et al. (2014) were also HPV-negative, raising the question as to whether TP53 mutations are a predictive biomarker for BMs or rather smoking-related disease (which has been previously reported) [11]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the extent of the molecular data that has been described for patients with HNSCC and BMs. We therefore sought to further characterize the genomic features of patients with BMs from HNSCC.

Herein, we present the results of a multi-institutional collaboration, which includes a detailed analysis of the clinical and molecular data for 61 cases of HNSCC with BMs. Our objectives were to (i) identify clinical and demographic data that might predict the development of BMs; (ii) to determine the comparative frequency of oncogenic mutations, copy number alterations, and large chromosomal changes in both primary tumor and BM samples, with a specific emphasis on clinically-targetable alterations; and (iii) to investigate the immunogenicity of HNSCC BMs.

Methods

Patient population

We conducted a pooled analysis of 61 cases of HNSCC-BM from 3 academic institutions [Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (DFCI), n = 11; Mount Sinai Hospital (MSH), n = 8; and University of California San Diego (UCSD), n = 5] and Foundation Medicine Inc. (FMI, n = 37) (Figure S1). Patient characteristics and survival analysis were reported for the cases from academic centers (n = 24) spanning the period of August 2003 to June 2022. A subset of samples from these cases had next-generation sequencing (n = 19), multiple immunofluorescence (n = 16), and proximity ligation sequencing (PLS, n = 2) performed. The FMI HNSCC-BM cohort had next-generation sequencing performed and was compared to an additional 4082 local samples (i.e. samples that were not from distant metastatic sites). The FMI BM samples were collected and analyzed from April 2014 to January 2023; local samples were analyzed from February 2023 to April 2023. Limited patient characteristics were reported for the FMI cohort. HPV status was determined by polymerase chain reaction, next-generation sequencing (where the presence of any HPV viral genome was considered HPV+), or p16 INK4A immunohistochemistry, the latter serving as a clinically validated surrogate for direct HPV testing.

Survival outcomes

Overall survival was defined as the time in months between the date of HNSCC diagnosis or BM diagnosis and death; patients who were alive at the data cutoff were censored. BM-free survival was defined as the time in months between the date of HNSCC diagnosis or relapsed/metastatic (R/M) diagnosis and the diagnosis of BM. Survival was calculated independent of HPV status and then stratified by HPV status.

Next-generation sequencing DFCI/MSH cohort

Next-generation DNA sequencing was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin embedded (FFPE) tissues from primary tumor specimens or BMs. Samples were analyzed using a clinically validated targeted next-generation sequencing assay called OncoPanel [12]. This assay included either 282 or 447 genes with evidence as drivers of cancer biology (depending on the version of OncoPanel). Somatic mutations, copy number alterations, tumor mutational burden (TMB), and mismatch repair status were included. High-TMB was defined as ≥ 10 mutations/megabase (mut/Mb). Mismatch repair deficiency [also called microsatellite instability (MSI)] was evaluated by determining the number of small insertion/deletion events that occur in homopolymer regions within exonic sequence data across all genes on the panel, as previously described [13]. Somatic mutations included known pathogenic alterations (putative driver) and variants of uncertain significance; alterations that have been determined to have no clinical utility were not included in the analysis. Copy number alterations were reported as shallow deletions (heterozygous loss), deep deletions (homozygous loss), and amplifications (defined as greater than 6 copies of a locus).

Next-generation sequencing, PD-L1 staining, and gene enrichment analysis for FMI cohort

Comprehensive genomic profiling at Foundation Medicine was completed using ≥ 50 ng FFPE sections. Either the FoundationOne® or FoundationOne CDx hybrid capture-based sequencing platforms using adaptor ligation-based libraries were used per routine clinical care at the time of sample receipt, and testing may have occurred at any time point within the study period. Base substitutions, short insertions and deletions, copy number changes (amplifications and homozygous deletions) and rearrangements (including gene fusions) were assessed in at least 295 genes, as previously described [14, 15]. TMB was determined on 0.8–1.2 megabases of sequenced DNA, extrapolated to the whole genome, as previously described [16]. TMB-high was defined as ≥ 10 mut/Mb. Assessment of MSI was performed from DNA sequencing interrogated > 1500 mononucleotide repeats, as previously described [17]. HPV status was determined using a competitive alignment of unmapped reads by BLASTn to the NCBI database of known viral sequences, as previously described [18]. Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression was determined using the DAKO 22C3 protocol where negative tumor cell scoring was defined as 0% staining, low positive as 1-49% staining, and high positive as ≥ 50% staining. Pathogenic gene alterations detected in the FMI HNSCC-BM cohort were compared to the cohort of FMI local specimens in a gene enrichment analysis.

Proximity ligation sequencing of HNSCC specimens

PLS uses crosslinked chromatin to capture the frequency of genomic sequence interactions at the time tissue is FFPE. High-throughput sequencing of the pairs of interactions can be used to generate a frequency interaction matrix. Because chromatin interaction frequencies are governed predominantly by distance of sequences along the linear length of the chromosome, chromosome abnormalities are ready identified as deviations from expected interaction frequencies.

Libraries were generated by Phase Genomics using their Proximo Human Hi-C kit adapted for FFPE samples. In brief, chromatin is recovered from two 5 μm curls of FFPE tissue using adaptive focused acoustics (Covaris). Liberated chromatin is immobilized on a magnetic bead solid support and subjected to restriction digestion. Overhangs produced by digest are filled-in with biotinylated nucleotides and subjected to proximity ligation. Chimeric DNA molecules are recovered using streptavidin beads and used to generate an Illumina-compatible, paired-end sequencing library. Libraries are then sequenced to a target depth of 150 M read pairs on a NovaSeq 6000 system in the 2 × 150 bp configuration. PLS data was analyzed on the Phase Genomics OncoTerra cloud platform which utilizes a suite of artificial intelligence-based and bioinformatic tools. Paired-end reads were mapped to the human reference genome (hg38) and evaluated for minor allele frequency to generate a sequence interaction matrix. Read depth, minor allele frequency, and interaction matrix together are used by the OncoTerra platform to identify structural variants.

Multiple immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescent staining was performed for 8 h overnight on 5 μm thick FFPE samples using a BOND Rx autostainer (Leica Biosystems, Inc). Samples were baked at 60 °C for 3 h before being loaded into the instrument. Excess paraffin was removed using dewax solution (Leica Cat. AR9222) and tissue was rehydrated using a graded series of ethanol and deionized water. The instrument then applied a sequential series of antigen retrieval, primary antibody incubation, and fluorophore incubation to each slide. Finally, Spectral DAPI (Akoya Biosciences Cat. FP1490) was applied and incubated for 20 min. Tissue was kept hydrated by the autostainer using deionized water until they were removed from the instrument. Slides were coverslipped using Diamond Anti-fade Mounting medium (Fisher Scientific, Cat. P36961) and kept in the dark at 4ºC until imaging.

Whole slide images were acquired at 20X using Akoya Biosciences PhenoImager HT multispectral imaging platform. Regions of interest were selected by a pathologist (SJR) and subsequently analyzed using a machine learning algorithm within Akoya inForm v2.6. A custom code was written to extract density and percentage from inForm for each biomarker. Cell densities were quantified for intra-tumoral regions and invasive margins (IM). The IM was defined as a 40 μm radius from the intersection of tumor and stromal tissue (Figure S2). Primary antibody and Opal fluorophore information can be found in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics including medians and ranges were reported for patient characteristics. Overall survival and BM-free survival were estimated by the Kaplan-Meier method. Time-to-event points were compared using the log-rank test stratified by HPV status. These analyses were performed using SPSS version 29 software. Statistical analyses performed on the FMI cohort were conducting using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria v3.6.0). Proportions of categorical variables were compared via Fisher’s exact tests using 2 × 2 contingency tables. Fractions of arm-level cytogenetic changes were assessed via two-tailed independent T-tests. False discovery rate was corrected using the Benjamini/Hochberg procedure. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. Data from the FMI HNSCC-BM cohort (n = 37) was only available for age at diagnosis (median 59 years) and sex at birth (78% male). Data from the combined academic centers (n = 24) identified a median age at diagnosis of 58 years. The majority of patients in this cohort identified as White (100%) and male (75%). 55% of patients were current or former tobacco smokers; 75% of patients were current or former consumers of alcohol. Oropharynx was the most common primary site of disease (75%). Most patients with oropharynx primary were HPV + or p16+ (9/10 HPV + by PCR plus 5 additional p16 + without HPV PCR testing, total HPV+/p16 + was 14/16, 88%). Radiation ± chemotherapy was chosen as curative-intent treatment more often than surgery ± adjuvant therapy (71% vs. 25%). Detailed treatment data was available for the 5 patients treated at UCSD and 3 patients treated at DFCI (Table S2). The median number of treatment lines before BM was 4 (range 1–6); all 8 patients received immunotherapy before BM, 7 (88%) received chemotherapy, and 6 (75%) received cetuximab. The median number of treatment lines after BM was 1 (range 0–4) with a median progression-free survival of 2 months (range 1–4 months). All 8 patients (100%) had distant metastasis at the time of BM diagnosis; lung was the most common site (n = 7, 88%).

Survival

After a median follow-up from the time of HNSCC diagnosis of 37.3 months (range 6.2 to 81.0), 22/24 deaths occurred (91.7%). The median overall survival from BM diagnosis was 9.7 months (range 0.0 to 27.0) (Fig. 1A). The median overall survival from HNSCC diagnosis was 39.3 months (range 6.2 to 82.2) (Fig. 1B). The median BM-free survival from R/M diagnosis was 7.6 months (range 0 to 68.0) and from HNSCC diagnosis was 20.1 months (range 4.9 to 77.9) (Fig. 1C-D). The majority of patients developed BM within 2 years of HNSCC diagnosis and within 1 year of R/M diagnosis. There were no statistically significant differences in overall survival or BM-survival when data was stratified by HPV-status (Figure S3A-D). However, there was a trend towards significance in the median overall survival from BM diagnosis, with HPV-positive patients living longer than HPV-negative patients (HPV-positive 10.7 months vs. HPV-negative 4.4 months, p-value 0.08).

Genomic alterations from the DFCI/MSH cohort

Somatic mutations and copy number variations for BM (n = 13), primary tumors from patients with BM (n = 6), and paired BM and primary tumor samples (n = 3) with ≥ 2 alterations are shown in Fig. 2A-C. Most samples were from HPV/p16 + tumors (62%) with a median TMB of 4.56 mut/Mb (range 1.52–21.29); all were microsatellite stable. The most frequently altered genes in BM specimens were ATM (54%), KMT2A (54%), PTEN (46%), RB1 (46%), and TP53 (46%). The most frequently altered genes in primary tumors were ATM (83%), KMT2A (67%), SETD2 (67%), SOX2 (67%), NOTCH1 (50%), and RB1 (50%). A full list of gene alterations can be found in Table S3. Notable BM gene alterations not shown in Fig. 2 include EGFR, FANCA, FANCD2, KRAS, MTOR, RAD52, and TSC1 (all n = 1, 8%); a FGFR3-TACC3 fusion was detected in a primary tumor sample. The paired samples frequently had homologous gene alterations. However, there were examples of acquired gene alterations (ATM, CDH1, RB1, and CDKN2B shallow deletions) and lost gene alterations (CCND1 and VHL shallow deletions and SOX2 and MYB amplifications) in BM samples. Most alterations were shallow deletions; point mutations, amplifications, and deep deletions were also detected. Notably, PIK3CA point mutations and amplifications were detected in 3/13 (23%) BM specimens and 2/6 (33%) primary tumors.

Genomic alterations from the FMI cohort

Pathogenic somatic mutations and copy number variations for the 25 most frequent genes in the FMI HNSCC-BM cohort are shown in Fig. 3A. HPV signature was detected in 51% of specimens. There was one case of MSI-L (3%) and 7/37 (19%) were TMB-high; all TMB-high cases were HPV-negative (7/18, 39%). The most frequently altered genes were CDKN2A (40.5%), TP53 (37.8%), PIK3CA (27.0%), CCND1 (21.6%), FGF19 (21.6%), FGF3 (21.6%), and FGF4 (21.6%). A more comprehensive list of gene alterations can be found in Figure S4A. Notably, EZH2 alterations were detected in 2/37 (5.4%) of cases. Most alterations were short-variants or copy number variations, but rearrangements and multiple alterations were also detected. All the CDKN2A and TP53 alterations occurred in cases that lacked the HPV signature (presumed HPV-negative cases). PIK3CA alterations were found in HPV-negative and HPV-positive specimens.

Next-generation sequencing was also performed on 4082 local HNSCC specimens (Fig. 3B and S4B). HPV signature was present in 26.1% of cases. MSI-L was detected in 2.4% of specimens and MSI-H was detected in 1.0%. The median TMB was 3.75 mut/Mbp (range 0.0 to 313.2), and 17.8% of cases were TMB-high. The most frequently altered genes were TP53 (67.3%), CDKN2A (45.2%), TERT (26.6%), and PIK3CA (21.7%). Most of these alterations were short-variants, and to a lesser extent copy number variations. The majority of TP53, CDKN2A, and TERT alterations occurred in HPV-negative specimens. PIK3CA alterations occurred frequently in both HPV-negative and HPV-positive specimens.

FMI gene alteration enrichment

Pathogenic alterations in the 37 HNSCC-BM FMI specimens were compared with the 4082 FMI HNSCC local specimens. Only 3 differences were statistically significant using the Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment for false discovery rate. Pathogenic mutations in MAP2K2 were enriched in the BM cohort, while pathogenic mutations in TSC1 and chromosome 17p gains were enriched in the local cohort (Fig. 3C). Interestingly, all 20 pathogenic mutations in TSC1 were unique and inactivating in the local cohort. Additionally, the BM cohort was found to be enriched for the HPV signature. Without the correction for false discovery rate, the following genes were also enriched in the BM cohort: ARID2, BCL6, BTK, CSF3R, ABRAXAS1, FGF12, FUBP1, GALNT12, RAD51D, REL, ROS1, RPA1, and ZNF217. Gain of chromosome 1p, 3p, and 17p and loss of chromosome 5q, 19p, and 20q were enriched in local specimens, while gain of chromosome 1q and loss of chromosome 4p, 6p, 11q, and 13q were enriched in BM. There were no significant differences in age, gender, average number of mutations, genomic ancestry, MSI status, TMB score, homologous recombination deficiency (measured by genome-wide loss of heterozygosity), PD-L1 staining, or any of the other 436 genes captured across the FMI solid tumor assays.

Chromosomal rearrangements discovered with PLS

Primary tumor specimens from patients with HNSCC-BM (n = 2) and HNSCC without distant metastasis (n = 2) were subjected to PLS. All four samples were from p16-positive oropharyngeal primary tumors. Chromosomal structural abnormalities were detected in all specimens (Table S4 and Fig. 4). Translocations were the most frequently observed abnormality; the majority were unbalanced.

Chromosomal rearrangements detected in patients with HNSCC-BM have potential clinical implications. For example, a complex inversion was observed on chromosome 8 involving NRG1, CSMD1, and FAM110B (Figure S5A). Additionally, der(7)t(7;22) results in the juxtaposition of MED15 and EGFR genes (Figure S5B). MED15 is frequently overexpressed in HNSCC and may have a strong influence on EGFR expression as a result. Figure S5C shows inv(1), which creates a potential regulatory fusion between ACTL8 and JAK1. The breakpoint in JAK1 results in truncation of the FERM domain, yielding an activated kinase fragment. Notably, none of these changes were detected on commercially available next-generation sequencing.

Immune markers of DFCI/MSH HNSCC-BM

Immune cell and PD1/PD-L1 densities within the tumor and at the IM were calculated for primary and BM HNSCC specimens (Fig. 5). Lower CD8+, CD8 + PD1+, FOXP3+, PD1+, and PD-L1 + cell densities were observed in BM specimens compared with primary specimens both within the tumor and at the IM (Fig. 6A-C). These differences were greater at the IM. Immune cell and PD1/PD-L1 densities were also higher at the IM than within the tumor for both primary and BM specimens.

Only a small fraction of the CD8 + cells were also positive for PD1, and PD-L1 cell density was very low for most specimens tested. This was also reflected with the strikingly low number of specimens with a PD-L1 combined positive score (CPS) ≥ 1% (1/13, 7.7%). The one specimen with a positive PD-L1 CPS (19%) was a BM from an HPV + oropharyngeal cancer (Fig. 6D). This specimen had an average CD8 + cell density within the tumor (mean, 73.1 cells/mm2), and the lowest FOXP3 density in the cohort (mean, 1.6 cells/mm2). The overall survival for this patient was 24.3 months from initial diagnosis and 2.9 months from BM.

Discussion

In this work, we present the largest analysis to date of the clinical, molecular, and immunologic features of HNSCC patients with BM. We show that the clinical features of this population are similar to what has previously been reported in the R/M setting with the following exceptions: (1) 100% of patients at academic centers were White and non-Hispanic (possibly related to the geography of these centers), and (2) this population was enriched for HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancer [19, 20]. We also provide a thorough analysis of the genomic alterations in HNSCC-BM, with notable identification of gene alterations that can be targeted with Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved therapies (e.g. PIK3CA, ATM, EGFR) [21–23]. With multiple immunofluorescence, we identified low levels of immune markers in the BMs and a striking paucity of PD-L1 expression. In fact, only 1/13 (8%) specimens had a positive PD-L1 CPS (defined as ≥ 1%). This is in stark contrast to what has previously been reported in the R/M setting with early and latter lines of therapy (PD-L1 CPS ≥ 1%, 52–86%) [19, 20, 24].

One of the key questions we hoped to address with this study is whether any of the analyzed data could be predictive of BM development for HNSCC. We did not test this directly, but several observations were made. First, for the 8 patients with full treatment data, it was noted that 100% of them had distant metastasis at the time of BM diagnosis and 88% had lung metastasis. This suggests a progressive pattern, where local or locally advanced disease would be unlikely to develop de novo BM in the absence of other distant sites of disease. This is further supported by studies where the rate of distant metastasis at BM diagnosis ranged from 82 to 100% 3,6,8.

Second, most patients developed BM within the first 2 years of HNSCC diagnosis with a median BM-free survival from diagnosis of 20.1 months and a median BM-free survival from R/M disease of 7.1 months. This suggests that the development of BM is not dependent on a prolonged treatment course. We also examined whether HPV/p16 + patients were more likely to get BM due to an improved prognosis with a longer life span (which has previously been demonstrated) [25], but there was no difference in BM-free survival from diagnosis or R/M disease when stratified by HPV/p16.

Using the FMI dataset, we were able to identify statistically significant genomic differences between BM and local specimens. BM specimens were enriched for the HPV signature and pathogenic activating MAP2K2 mutations, while local specimens were enriched for inactivating TSC1 mutations and chromosome 17p gains. Statistical significance of the HPV signature in HNSCC-BM from such a large cohort of patients (n = 37 BM and n = 4082 local specimens) provides further validation to what was previously reported [8, 9]. Whether there is an underlying biologic mechanism connecting HPV infection to BM development warrants additional research, but notable reported differences in HPV-infected tumors versus HPV negative tumors include viral oncogene expression (e.g. E6 and E7), genomic alterations that differ from HPV-negative tumors, and differences in immunogenicity [26, 27]. The significance of MAP2K2 mutations in BM is intriguing, because the gene encodes the MEK2 protein, which is involved in the RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK signaling pathway. Whether these mutations confer a predilection for BM or simply reflect a resistance mechanism to prior therapies (e.g. EGFR-directed therapy) remains unknown. However, mutations in ERK-regulating proteins were identified as a resistance mechanism for non-small cell lung cancer treated with EGFR-directed therapies [28]. There also remains a question as to whether inactivating TSC1 mutations or chromosome 17p gains could be protective against BM. This is supported by the fact that TSC1 loss and/or inactivation can lead to PD-L1 upregulation and improved clinical response to anti-PD1 immune checkpoint inhibitors [29]. Chromosome 17 gains have also been reported as a feature of well differentiated HNSCC, and because the TP53 gene is located on chromosome 17p, gain of this region could be protective against disease progression and/or distant spread [30]. However, further research is needed to substantiate these observations.

We next asked the question of whether our data could be used to inform treatment selection after BM development. Among the most frequently altered genes in HNSCC-BM were targetable alterations, including PIK3CA, ATM, CCND1, and EGFR (all with FDA-approved therapies in solid tumors) [21–23, 31, 32]. The gene list is expanded when considering therapies that are currently in development (e.g. NOTCH, CTNNB1, and TP53) [33–35]. The PLS data also identified large chromosomal changes that are expected to alter the expression and/or function of NRG1, EGFR, and JAK1, all of which have corresponding targeted therapies [22, 36, 37]. Interestingly, these findings were not uncovered on commercially available next-generation sequencing, which suggests that PLS could complement traditional molecular testing and identify additional patients who might benefit from targeted therapies. While further research is needed to establish the efficacy of targeted therapies in HNSCC-BM, it may be worth considering these drugs for patients harboring a targetable alteration, either in clinical trial or with off-label therapy. Immunotherapy, on the other hand, might be less effective in HNSCC-BM due to lower densities of CD8 + cells, PD1 expression, and reduced central nervous system penetration with immune checkpoint inhibitors [38]. Furthermore, all but one case of HNSCC-BM had negative PD-L1 expression (PD-L1 CPS ≤ 1% in 12/13 cases; 92.7%), which is in stark contrast to prior studies in the R/M setting with early and latter lines of therapy (PD-L1 CPS ≤ 1%, 14–48%) [19, 20, 24].

Finally, we wanted to compare our findings in HNSCC with other solid tumors. In 2015, Brastianos et al. published a large molecular analysis of paired primary and BM specimens across a wide range of tumors, discovering genetic divergence of BM from primary tumors and identifying common gene alterations in BM specimens [39]. Our data provides further validation to the model of genetic divergence as we observed both shared and unique gene alterations in our paired sample analysis. Additionally, we identified gene alterations in HNSCC-BM affecting the cell cycle and PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathways, both of which were recognized as common in BMs from different solid malignancies [39, 40]. This provides additional evidence that these pathways may be important for BM development and that genetic divergence from the primary lesion may be necessary.

Our study has limitations that we would like to acknowledge. First, we acknowledge detection of copy number alterations and mutations in TP53 and RB in HPV/p16 + samples. This raises questions as to whether all p16 + cases were HPV-infected or whether p16 overexpression was due to an alternate mechanism (noting that expression of the HPV viral proteins E6 and E7 tends to be mutually exclusive with these alterations due to their effects on p53 and Rb respectively). We investigated this possibility and can report that 5/16 p16 + cases did not have corresponding HPV DNA testing performed to confirm HPV infection. It is also possible that HPV-infected tumors lost expression of the E6 and/or E7 proteins after acquiring an alteration in RB or TP53; this has previously been reported [41].

In addition, although this is one of the largest studies of HNSCC-BM, the sample size was too small to perform a detailed comparative analysis of all the genomic alterations; this was particularly true for the PLS analyses. The data collected from academic centers was also geographically restricted and may not be representative of diverse populations. The FMI data did not have this same sampling bias and would be expected to have broader applicability, particularly for patients residing in the United States. It is also notable that brain imaging is not routine for HNSCC, so the majority of specimens analyzed were from patients with symptomatic lesions. The significance of this in relation to molecular and immune-related alterations is unknown. Additionally, we acknowledge that important gene alterations or changes to the tumor immune microenvironment might have been missed in our analysis since the respective gene and antibody panels did not capture every possible alteration. Despite these limitations, we were able to provide clinical, molecular, and immune profiling of HNSCC-BM, which we hope will serve as a catalyst for future research.

Conclusions

BM is a rare event in HNSCC with a poor prognosis and an uncertain treatment paradigm. Our data provides insight into the molecular changes and modifications to the tumor immune microenvironment that permit BM development in HNSCC. We observed genetic divergence from the primary tumor lesion and identified gene alterations that could be targeted with FDA-approved therapies. We also observed low levels of immune markers in BM lesions with a striking paucity of PD-L1 expression. This raises concern about the effectiveness of anti-PD1/anti-PD-L1 directed therapies for this population. Additionally, MAP2K2 alterations and the HPV gene signature were enriched in BM specimens, findings that warrant further investigation.
