Disrupting the CBFβ-SMMHC-RUNX1 oncogenic protein–protein interaction in inv(16) AML: from fusion biology to targeted therapy
Authors: Emadeldin M Kamel, Sally Mostafa Khadrawy, Mohamed A M Ali, Noha A Ahmed, Saleh Alkhedhairi, Faris F Aba Alkhayl, Al Mokhtar Lamsabhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCore-binding factor (CBF) leukemias, including inv(16) AML, involve RUNX1/CBFβ chromosomal rearrangements that generate oncogenic fusion proteins. In inv(16) AML, the CBFβ–SMMHC fusion (CBFB–MYH11) dominantly perturbs RUNX1 by sequestering it in aberrant, high-affinity complexes. Structural studies reveal that CBFβ–SMMHC binds the RUNX1 Runt domain with higher affinity than wild-type CBFβ, aided by a second RUNX1-binding site in its SMMHC tail. This altered interface underlies the fusion’s dominant-negative disruption of RUNX1 target-gene regulation. Chemical probes have been developed to disrupt this interface; notably, the bivalent inhibitor AI-10-49 selectively binds CBFβ–SMMHC, displacing RUNX1 and restoring RUNX1 transcriptional function. AI-10-49 delays leukemia progression in murine inv(16) models and induces apoptosis in human inv(16) AML cells. Mechanistically, uncoupling RUNX1 from CBFβ–SMMHC liberates RUNX1 to repress oncogenic programs: for example, RUNX1 rebinds distal MYC enhancers and recruits polycomb factors (RING1B) in place of SWI/SNF (BRG1) to silence MYC, triggering leukemia cell apoptosis. These chromatin and transcriptional consequences underscore how CBFβ–SMMHC sustains leukemic transcriptional programs. Importantly, combining CBFβ–SMMHC inhibitors with BET bromodomain inhibitors synergistically eradicates inv(16) leukemia in preclinical models. Together, these insights into the structural basis and functional role of the CBFβ–SMMHC–RUNX1 interface highlight protein–protein interaction disruption as a promising translational strategy in core-binding factor leukemia therapy.

Introduction

Core-binding factor (CBF) is a heterodimeric transcription factor complex essential for blood cell development. It consists of a DNA-binding CBFα subunit (RUNX1, RUNX2, or RUNX3) and a non-DNA-binding CBFβ subunit [1]. CBFβ itself does not contact DNA; instead, it stabilizes the RUNX1 (CBFα) DNA-binding Runt domain on target gene enhancers/promoters and protects RUNX proteins from degradation [1, 2]. RUNX1 (also known as AML1) is particularly critical in hematopoiesis, required for the emergence of definitive hematopoietic stem cells and for the differentiation of multiple lineages [3]. Through its DNA-binding Runt domain, RUNX1 regulates genes controlling stem cell self-renewal, myeloid differentiation, and lymphoid development [3, 4]. In normal cells, the CBFβ–RUNX1 partnership ensures robust transcriptional control: CBFβ binding allosterically optimizes the RUNX1 Runt domain conformation for DNA binding and transcriptional activation [1, 5]. Conversely, loss of RUNX1 or CBFβ function leads to bone marrow failure or developmental lethality, underscoring the complex’s vital role in blood formation [1].

The chromosomal inversion inv(16)(p13q22) in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) disrupts the CBFB gene and fuses it to MYH11, encoding a CBFβ–Smooth Muscle Myosin Heavy Chain chimera (CBFβ-SMMHC) [6, 7]. This fusion oncoprotein underlies the core-binding factor subtype of AML known as inv(16) or FAB M4Eo leukemia, characterized by myelomonocytic blasts with abnormal eosinophils. Clinically, inv(16) AML accounts for ~ 8–10% of adult and pediatric AML cases [8, 9] and is associated with relatively favorable outcomes under intensive chemotherapy[8]. Complete remission is achieved in ~ 90% of cases, although roughly half will eventually relapse [9]. This suggests that while conventional treatment is effective, residual leukemia-initiating cells often persist, motivating the pursuit of more precise therapies. Importantly, CBFβ-SMMHC alone is insufficient for leukemogenesis: it creates a pre-leukemic state (blocking differentiation and expanding progenitors), but cooperating mutations (commonly in kinase signaling genes like KIT or FLT3) are required for progression to full AML [9]. Genetically engineered mice confirm that CBFβ-MYH11 expression causes a differentiation arrest similar to Runx1/CBFβ knockout, and additional mutations accelerate leukemia onset [10]. Thus, the CBFB-MYH11 fusion establishes the foundational block in hematopoiesis that defines this leukemia, providing a unique target for therapy.

It has been proposed that fusion proteins linked to AML that block differentiation might leave the long-term hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) pool largely intact, yet markedly weaken these cells’ ability to repopulate the blood system [11]. In this context, researchers identified a CBFβ-SMMHC–driven preleukemic myeloid progenitor population that is susceptible to transformation into AML [12]. A defining feature of HSCs is their capacity to divide either symmetrically, producing additional HSCs, or asymmetrically, generating progenitors that sustain multilineage hematopoiesis [13, 14]. Disruptions to these division patterns triggered by CBFβ-SMMHC closely resemble those observed when Runx1 is absent. Notably, CBFβ-SMMHC appears not to disrupt the persistence of long-term HSCs (Fig. 1), allowing the fusion to remain in this compartment without obvious effects for extended periods. This aligns with clinical findings in human AML: analyses of inv(16) cases indicate that CD34⁺CD38⁻ HSCs carrying the CBFβ-MYH11 fusion can arise before birth and remain undetected for many years prior to AML development, and they can also be found after diagnosis [15–17]. Moreover, inv(16) patients who achieve remission may still test positive for CBFB-MYH11 even two years following allogeneic bone marrow transplantation [18]. By contrast, the common CBF leukemia fusion involving RUNX1 has been reported to increase HSC and progenitor numbers in both mouse and human systems [19–22]. Together, these observations imply that CBFβ-SMMHC and RUNX1 likely influence distinct biological programs as leukemia evolves.

Unlike kinase oncogenes, CBFβ-SMMHC is an abnormal transcription factor complex that exerts its effects through protein–protein interactions (PPIs) rather than catalytic activity. Traditionally, transcription factor PPIs were viewed as difficult to inhibit with small molecules because their interfaces are typically large and relatively featureless [23]. However, CBFβ-SMMHC presents a compelling case for PPI disruption: the fusion’s leukemogenic activity is critically dependent on binding the RUNX1 Runt domain, which it hijacks to repress normal gene expression. By disrupting the CBFβ-SMMHC–RUNX1 interaction, one can potentially release RUNX1 to resume its normal transcriptional programs, inducing differentiation or apoptosis of leukemia cells. Indeed, proof-of-concept studies have shown that small molecules can inhibit the CBFβ/RUNX1 interaction and selectively kill inv(16) leukemia cells [9, 24]. While wild-type RUNX1–CBFβ binding is essential for normal hematopoiesis, the inv(16) fusion offers unique targetability: the fusion protein forms multimers and creates an altered interface with RUNX1 (including a fusion-specific binding site), which can be exploited for selective inhibitor design [25]. The development of such inhibitors demonstrates that even “undruggable” transcription factor interactions can be targeted with rational design. Therefore, PPI disruption in CBF leukemias represents a paradigm for translating fusion oncoprotein biology into a precision therapy approach (Fig. 2). In this review, we first summarize the molecular and structural basis of CBFβ-SMMHC–RUNX1 dependence and the resulting transcriptional dysregulation. We then discuss chemical and pharmacologic strategies developed to disrupt this interaction, emphasizing determinants of selectivity for the fusion complex. Finally, we synthesize emerging mechanistic insights downstream of RUNX1 release—including transcriptional and epigenetic remodeling—and outline rational combination strategies and translational considerations for precision targeting of inv(16) AML.

The CBFβ-SMMHC fusion as a PPI-driven oncoprotein

Domain architecture of CBFβ-SMMHC and oligomerization features

CBFβ-SMMHC is a chimeric protein combining most of the 182-amino-acid CBFβ with a large fragment of the MYH11 (smooth muscle myosin heavy chain) tail region (Fig. 3) [26]. The fusion retains the RUNX1-binding domain of CBFβ (allowing it to heterodimerize with RUNX1’s Runt domain). Uniquely, the SMMHC portion provides a coiled-coil oligomerization motif (the Assembly Competence Domain, ACD) that enables CBFβ-SMMHC to multimerize or form filaments in cells [27, 28]. In essence, CBFβ-SMMHC can assemble into higher-order complexes, unlike wild-type CBFβ which is monomeric. Oligomerization localizes multiple RUNX1-binding CBFβ domains in close proximity and is required for its leukemic function: knock-in mice expressing a fusion lacking the multimerization region fail to develop leukemia [29]. Another critical element is the High-Affinity Binding Domain (HABD) within the SMMHC tail. This ~ 40-amino-acid segment (identified by NMR mapping) provides a second interface for RUNX1, greatly strengthening the fusion’s grip on the Runt domain [30]. The fusion’s extreme C-terminus (last ~ 95 residues) serves as a transcriptional repression module: it binds co-repressors like Sin3A and class I HDACs (notably HDAC8) [27, 31, 32], and its deletion abrogates the fusion’s ability to cause leukemia [29]. Overall, the domain architecture of CBFβ-SMMHC shows how a PPI-driven mechanism is built into the protein: a RUNX1-binding head plus a multimerizing, corepressor-recruiting tail, all of which cooperate to perturb normal gene regulation.

The RUNX1 interaction: altered affinity/stoichiometry and the role of the SMMHC segment

CBFβ-SMMHC acts as a potent dominant-negative inhibitor of RUNX1 through an altered biophysical interaction with the RUNX1 Runt domain. Quantitative binding studies have demonstrated that the fusion protein binds RUNX1 far tighter than wild-type CBFβ does. Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) measurements showed CBFβ-SMMHC has substantially higher affinity for the isolated RUNX1 Runt domain, and intriguingly, an altered binding stoichiometry compared to normal CBFβ [1, 27, 33]. NMR structural studies provided an explanation: in the CBFβ-SMMHC/RUNX1 complex, the RUNX1 Runt domain makes contacts with both the CBFβ portion and a specific region of the SMMHC tail [6]. In other words, the fusion protein engages RUNX1 at two interfaces simultaneously – one via the native CBFβ–Runt interface and a second via the SMMHC HABD. This bivalent binding mode drastically increases effective affinity and allows the fusion to outcompete normal CBFβ for RUNX1. Deletion of the SMMHC segment identified as the HABD abolishes the extra contact and reduces the fusion’s affinity back to wild-type levels [30]. The result is that CBFβ-SMMHC can sequester RUNX1 far more effectively, potentially capturing RUNX1 even at low concentrations and keeping it away from DNA. The altered stoichiometry observed in vitro suggests that a single CBFβ-SMMHC multimer could bind multiple RUNX1 molecules, or conversely, that one RUNX1 Runt domain might be held in a clamp-like grip by a fusion dimer. These avidity effects underscore how the fusion’s SMMHC-derived elements reshape the binding equilibrium, making RUNX1 a hostage of the fusion oncoprotein.

Notably, the presence of DNA can disrupt this high-affinity interaction. When the RUNX1 Runt domain is bound to its DNA target sequence, the additional SMMHC-mediated contact is sterically/allosterically hindered, and the fusion’s binding affinity reverts closer to normal [33]. This implies that RUNX1 already engaged on DNA is less susceptible to sequestration by CBFβ-SMMHC. However, in leukemic cells, many RUNX1 molecules are not properly DNA-bound due to the fusion’s dominant-negative action [34]. Thus, CBFβ-SMMHC can capture RUNX1 in the nucleoplasm and possibly even extract RUNX1 from DNA at promoters, given its avid binding [1, 3, 27]. The altered affinity and binding mode of CBFβ-SMMHC are central to its pathogenicity: by “soaking up” RUNX1 via an unnatural dual interface, the fusion deprives RUNX1 of its normal gene regulatory functions.

DNA, allostery, and conformational control of the Runt domain interface

The interaction of RUNX1 and CBFβ (normal or fusion) is intimately linked to RUNX1’s DNA-binding conformation. Crystallographic and NMR studies of the RUNX1 Runt domain show that CBFβ binding induces specific structural rearrangements in RUNX1 that mirror those caused by DNA binding [5, 35, 36]. In particular, CBFβ contacts the Runt domain at key loop regions (for example, contacting RUNX1 Tyr113 in the βC–βD loop) and triggers an allosteric change that stabilizes the DNA-binding interface [2, 5, 37]. Several flexible loops in the Runt domain (the βO–βA loop, βC–βD loop, βE–βF loop, and the βG–βG′ “wing”) undergo coordinated shifts upon CBFβ binding, essentially pre-organizing RUNX1 for high-affinity DNA binding [5, 36, 38]. The most dramatic change is an “S-switch” movement in the βG–βG′ loop (named for a conserved serine residue) – this loop swings into a new conformation when CBFβ binds, creating a complementary surface for DNA interaction [5, 38]. CBFβ makes multiple contacts in this region (e.g., with RUNX1 Thr161 in the βG–βG′ loop) and indirectly influences adjacent loops via a network of hydrogen bonds [2, 39]. These allosteric effects illustrate that CBFβ is not just a passive tether for RUNX1; it actively molds the RUNX1 Runt domain into a DNA-ready state [5].In the context of CBFβ-SMMHC, this allosteric interplay gains another layer. The fusion’s HABD binds the RUNX1 Runt domain in a way that introduces an additional (avidity-enhancing) contact beyond the native CBFβ–Runt interface [1, 27]. Consistent with this model, DNA binding is expected to bias RUNX1 toward the DNA-compatible (“S-switch–on”) conformation stabilized by wild-type CBFβ, which would reduce accessibility of the alternative fusion-favored contact surface [1, 5]. We can therefore view the system as an allosteric competition: either RUNX1 is bound to DNA (with CBFβ supporting from one side), or it is bound to the fusion (with CBFβ and SMMHC gripping it in an alternative state) [1, 27]. Thus, DNA and the fusion effectively pull RUNX1 into different structural states. CBFβ-SMMHC may favor a RUNX1 conformation that is less competent for productive DNA engagement and/or proper chromatin occupancy, thereby functionally withdrawing RUNX1 from its target genes [1, 27]. Conversely, pharmacologically releasing RUNX1 from the fusion should restore its DNA-binding conformation and allow it to engage chromatin. This dynamic highlights how allosteric regulation of the Runt domain interface by CBFβ, DNA, and the fusion tail segment dictates the outcome of RUNX1’s activity. Allostery and conformational control are therefore central considerations both for understanding the fusion’s mechanism and for devising strategies to disrupt the fusion–RUNX1 complex without derailing normal RUNX1 function.

Consequences of RUNX1 sequestration: transcriptional repression and leukemogenic programs

By sequestering RUNX1, CBFβ-SMMHC impairs RUNX1-dependent transcription, leading to a cascade of effects that block differentiation and promote leukemogenesis. Early models proposed two, not mutually exclusive, mechanisms: (1) Cytoplasmic sequestration – CBFβ-SMMHC retaining RUNX1 in the cytoplasm via the myosin heavy chain’s association with actin filaments, thereby preventing RUNX1 from reaching the nucleus [40, 41]; and (2) Nuclear corepression – CBFβ-SMMHC binding RUNX1 at gene regulatory sites and actively repressing transcription through recruitment of HDACs and other corepressors [28, 31]. There is evidence for both modes: cytoskeletal colocalization and relocalization of RUNX factors away from the nucleus has been demonstrated in cell systems (and supported in vivo), while fractionation and reporter-based studies support DNA-dependent cooperative repression by RUNX1 plus the inv(16) fusion in the nucleus [28, 31]. In knock-in mice, a single CbfbMYH11 allele causes an embryonic hematopoietic block with profound loss of definitive hematopoiesis, consistent with dominant suppression of core-binding factor function [42]. The result is an accumulation of immature progenitors unable to execute normal differentiation programs [42]. Beyond this dominant-negative effect (loss of RUNX1 function), substantial data support corepressor recruitment as a gain-of-function feature of the fusion. The fusion’s C-terminal repression region associates with mSin3A and HDAC8, and inv(16)-mediated repression is HDAC-inhibitor sensitive [31]. Notably, inv(16) leukemic stem cell biology also involves a CM/HDAC8/p53 axis: p53 activity can be inhibited in inv(16)+ AML via interaction with CBFβ-SMMHC and HDAC8, and HDAC8 inhibition restores p53 acetylation/activity and selectively impacts inv(16)+ AML stem/progenitor cells [43]. Gene-expression work in CbfbMYH11 models further indicates that the fusion can impose a transcriptional program not fully explained by simple CBF loss alone; for example, CbfbMYH11 embryos show aberrant persistence of specific progenitor programs, and a subset of these signatures is detectable in human CBFB-MYH11 leukemia samples [44]. Interestingly, studies of a fusion variant lacking the HABD (residues 179–221) revealed an unexpected twist. In mice, CBFβ-SMMHC Δ179–221 (HABD-defective) reduced RUNX1 binding yet accelerated leukemogenesis relative to full-length fusion [30]. The same work also discusses patient “type I” CBFB-MYH11 fusions lacking HABD and their inefficient RUNX1 binding, supporting the idea that complete RUNX1 sequestration is not strictly required for AML [30]. One interpretation is that CBFβ-SMMHC perturbs multiple gene sets in parallel (RUNX1-dependent and RUNX1-modulatory), so that partial RUNX1 inhibition can still sustain differentiation blockade while permitting additional leukemia-promoting outputs; this “optimal inhibition” concept has been highlighted in mechanistic discussions of ΔHABD phenotypes and therapeutic targeting strategies [30].

Structural and biophysical basis of the target interface

RUNX1 Runt domain–CBFβ interface: “hot spots” and allosteric coupling

Structural studies of the RUNX1–CBFβ complex (in the absence of the fusion) define a reproducible heterodimerization surface that contains energetically important “hot spots” relevant to inhibitor design [45, 46]. The RUNX1 Runt domain adopts an immunoglobulin-like β-barrel fold that engages CBFβ through a contiguous interface, with local contact regions that make disproportionate contributions to binding energy [45, 46]. Consistent with this, mutagenesis of conserved residues on either partner (e.g., RUNX1 Tyr113 and key residues on the complementary CBFβ surface) markedly reduces binding, highlighting residue-level hot spots that can serve as anchors for chemical disruption strategies [47]. Thus, even though the overall interface is extended, it is not energetically uniform, and perturbing a limited subset of contacts can destabilize the complex.

A key challenge is that the Runt–CBFβ interface remains relatively flat and extensive—typical of transcription-factor PPIs—making classical “pocket-centric” drug discovery difficult [48, 49]. Nonetheless, structural models reveal shallow clefts/grooves that become apparent in the bound state and cluster near recurrent energetic nodes (including regions adjacent to the βC–βD contact area), suggesting potential footholds for small molecules [2, 45]. Supporting feasibility, the prototypical tool compound Ro5-3335 was identified via quantitative high-throughput screening for disruptors of RUNX1–CBFβ binding, providing proof of principle that drug-like molecules can modulate this interaction, even if the precise binding mode remains incompletely resolved [50]. As discussed above, DNA binding and CBFβ binding are coupled through conformational dynamics of the Runt domain; this coupling provides an additional route to inhibition beyond simply “blocking” the interface [2, 5, 36]. In this framework, inhibitors can be orthosteric (directly perturbing interface hot spots) or allosteric (binding away from the interface but shifting conformational/dynamic states to weaken docking). Notably, NMR-guided approaches have demonstrated that ligands binding outside the direct interface can nevertheless inhibit heterodimerization by altering CBFβ dynamics and reducing productive RUNX1 engagement, illustrating a viable allosteric strategy [33].

Because the interaction is dynamic, inhibitor design should explicitly consider conformational exchange rather than relying solely on a static structural snapshot. Solution NMR studies (including footprinting of Runt/CBFβ/DNA assemblies and analyses of conformational exchange in interface-proximal regions) emphasize that the complex samples multiple states and that specific regions are particularly sensitive to binding-linked structural/dynamic changes [5, 47]. Practically, this means candidate inhibitors should be evaluated not only for disruption of RUNX1–CBFβ binding, but also for whether they undesirably compromise RUNX1’s productive DNA engagement and broader transcription factor function.

In addition to crystallographic/NMR structures, molecular dynamics (MD) analyses provide a residue-level “hot-spot map” and highlight how DNA occupancy can allosterically tune interface packing [51, 52]. As summarized in Fig. 4, the RD–CBFβ surface can be organized into discrete interaction clusters centered on energetically important residues. One prominent cluster involves RD Tyr113/Glu116/Arg118/Arg139 engaging CBFβ Arg33/Arg35, consistent with the βC–βD region acting as a key recognition element. A second cluster links RD Asp66 and His163 to CBFβ Lys11, and a third captures contacts between RD Thr161 and CBFβ Asn104 (Fig. 4). Comparison of RD–CBFβ binary simulations with RD–CBFβ–DNA ternary simulations indicates that DNA occupancy can subtly reshape loop geometry and packing at these hot spots, supporting the concept of allosteric coupling between the DNA-bound state and CBFβ docking. Collectively, these data reinforce that the interface is not a rigid, featureless plane but a dynamic surface with recurrent residue-level “handles” that can be targeted either directly (orthosteric disruption at hot spots) or indirectly (allosteric stabilization of conformations that disfavor CBFβ binding) (Fig. 4) [51].

Fusion-specific determinants: how SMMHC elements reshape binding

The inv(16) fusion introduces two defining, fusion-specific determinants that distinguish CBFβ-SMMHC from wild-type CBFβ: (i) an additional SMMHC-mediated RUNX1 contact (the HABD-dependent interaction) and (ii) multimerization/avidity driven by SMMHC assembly elements. Here we focus on what these features imply from a structural/druggability standpoint, rather than re-describing the domain architecture. Although biophysical and NMR-based studies support contributions from both the CBFβ core and SMMHC sequences to the strengthened interaction with the RUNX1 Runt domain [27], a high-resolution structure of a full RUNX1–CBFβ–SMMHC assembly is still lacking. Consequently, current models rely on integrated evidence (binding measurements, solution mapping, and fusion-variant behavior) that collectively support an avidity/induced-fit mechanism in which SMMHC-derived surfaces reinforce RUNX1 engagement beyond the canonical RUNX1–CBFβ interface [27, 30]. From a drug-discovery perspective, this structural gap is important: it limits precise definition of whether the HABD creates a discrete “neo-surface” or transient pocket at the Runt domain that could be selectively targeted, and it motivates further structural mapping (e.g., crosslinking/MS, cryo-EM of higher-order assemblies, or expanded NMR/HDX studies) to pinpoint fusion-specific contact geometry.

Likewise, SMMHC-driven multimerization reshapes the binding landscape in ways that are not captured by monomeric RUNX1–CBFβ structures. Assembly-competence elements promote higher-order organization of the fusion complex, and genetic/mapping studies indicate that this multimerization module is tightly linked to repression-associated functions and leukemogenic activity [31, 53, 54]. Importantly, multimerization does more than “increase affinity”: it can generate effective local concentration and stoichiometry effects that enable RUNX1 capture through avidity and may stabilize interaction states that are rare or inaccessible in the wild-type complex.

Implications for inhibitor design: orthosteric vs. allosteric disruption, selectivity challenges

Two main strategies can disrupt the CBFβ-SMMHC–RUNX1 interaction: orthosteric inhibitors that block the Runt–CBFβ interface, and allosteric inhibitors that bind elsewhere and shift conformational/dynamic states to weaken docking [48, 49]. Early tools such as Ro5-3335 exemplify the orthosteric approach, but a key limitation is selectivity, since the same interface is required for normal RUNX1–CBFβ function, raising toxicity concerns [50]. In contrast, allosteric approaches can be more selective, as shown by NMR-guided CBFβ allosteric inhibitors that disrupt heterodimerization without occupying the canonical interface [33]. In inv(16) AML, selectivity may also be achieved by exploiting fusion-specific context, including oligomerization/assembly, which enables bivalent/polyvalent designs that preferentially engage the fusion complex and spare monomeric CBFβ [55]. Another potential selectivity lever is a fusion-only “neo-surface” created by HABD-dependent reinforcement, though its structural basis remains to be defined [30]. Finally, candidates should be tested in functionally relevant assays that capture the RUNX1–CBFβ–DNA ternary biology, prioritizing compounds that release RUNX1 from the fusion while preserving or restoring productive chromatin engagement. Overall, despite persistent selectivity challenges, existing probes support the feasibility of pharmacologically targeting critical fusion interactions in inv(16) AML models [56].

Chemical strategies to disrupt CBFβ-SMMHC PPIs

CBFβ-SMMHC-selective disruption (AI-10-49 class): concept and selectivity logic

The most successful strategy so far for selectively targeting CBFβ-SMMHC involves bivalent small-molecule inhibitors that exploit the fusion’s oligomeric structure. A prime example is AI-10-49, a compound specifically designed to bind the fusion protein with higher affinity than it binds wild-type CBFβ [56]. The concept emerged from an initial small molecule (a “parent” inhibitor) that could bind CBFβ and prevent its interaction with RUNX1 To achieve selectivity, researchers tethered two copies of this inhibitor with an appropriate linker, creating a bivalent molecule (AI-10-49) [55]. Because CBFβ-SMMHC forms multimers (dimers), AI-10-49 can simultaneously engage two fusion monomers in the complex, effectively clamping the multimer together. Wild-type CBFβ is monomeric and presents only a single binding site, so AI-10-49’s two warheads cannot both bind one CBFβ molecule – resulting in much weaker overall binding to wild-type CBFβ [56]. This ingenious use of avidity means AI-10-49 preferentially disrupts the CBFβ-SMMHC–RUNX1 interaction without significantly interfering with normal RUNX1–CBFβ function.

In preclinical tests, AI-10-49 was shown to be highly selective for inv(16) AML cells. It induces cell death in the CBFβ-SMMHC-expressing AML cell line ME-1, but notably did not inhibit growth of Kasumi-1 cells (which express a different fusion, RUNX1-ETO, and rely on RUNX1 but not CBFβ) [57]. Furthermore, AI-10-49 had minimal impact on normal human hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells in culture, suggesting it spares normal RUNX1 functions [58]. In mouse models of inv(16) leukemia, AI-10-49 treatment significantly prolonged survival and reduced leukemic burden, whereas it showed no obvious toxicity in non-leukemic mice [56]. These results validate the selectivity logic: by targeting a unique feature (dimerization) of the fusion protein, the inhibitor achieves a therapeutic window where leukemia cells are sensitive but normal cells are largely unharmed. Mechanistically, AI-10-49 binds to the CBFβ portion of the fusion (at or near the RUNX1 interface) and sterically blocks RUNX1 from binding [59]. The linker likely spans the distance between two CBFβ domains in the fusion dimer, essentially locking the fusion in a RUNX1-unbound state.

Another notable aspect of AI-10-49 is that it works rapidly: within hours of treatment, RUNX1 is released from the fusion and relocates to target gene enhancers [59]. AI-10-49 is therefore an “on-target” inhibitor that engages the fusion protein directly and triggers a defined downstream response (RUNX1 reactivation). Its selectivity for CBFβ-SMMHC sets it apart from earlier compounds. The success of AI-10-49 has also spurred exploration of other bivalent inhibitors or improved analogues. The goal is to optimize pharmacological properties (solubility, stability) while maintaining the bivalent binding mechanism that confers selectivity. In summary, AI-10-49 exemplifies a fusion-tailored PPI inhibitor, converting deep knowledge of CBFβ-SMMHC’s multimeric nature into a therapeutic strategy [59].

Inhibitors of RUNX1–CBFβ as related tools/comparators

Before fusion-selective inhibitors were developed, researchers had identified pan-CBFβ/RUNX1 inhibitors that disrupt the interaction in a more general way. The benzodiazepine derivative Ro5-3335 (Fig. 6) was one of the first small molecules reported to target the RUNX1–CBFβ interaction [50]. Discovered via a chemical library screen, Ro5-3335 binds to both RUNX1 and CBFβ (the exact binding site is not fully resolved) and inhibits RUNX1’s transcriptional activity in cell-based assays [50]. In functional studies, Ro5-3335 showed the ability to reduce the viability of CBFβ-SMMHC–expressing leukemia cells (ME-1) and modestly prolonged survival in an inv(16) mouse model [50]. However, because it binds both components of the heterodimer, Ro5-3335 likely interferes with RUNX1 activity in normal cells as well. Indeed, extended treatment of wild-type mice caused only minor hematologic effects (slight changes in blood counts) and no overt toxicity, indicating that partial inhibition of RUNX1/CBF in normal cells is tolerable, at least in the short term [50]. Notably, biochemical studies with Ro5-3335 yielded puzzling results: in purified protein assays, it blocked CBFβ–Runt binding, but in cell extracts, it did not prevent RUNX1 and CBFβ from co-immunoprecipitating. This suggests Ro5-3335 might not simply displace RUNX1 from CBFβ, but could induce a conformational change that alters RUNX1’s ability to regulate gene expression (for example, changing RUNX1’s DNA-binding conformation or preventing recruitment of co-factors) [5, 50]. In summary, Ro5-3335 provided a valuable proof-of-principle that small molecules can target the RUNX1–CBFβ interface, though its lack of specificity and unclear mechanism limit its therapeutic potential.

Chemical structures of disruptors in this study

Another important comparator is the AI-10-104 series of compounds (Fig. 6). These arose from structure-guided efforts to improve upon early hits like AI-4-88 [56]. AI-10-104 is a “broad-spectrum” RUNX inhibitor, meaning it disrupts RUNX/CBFβ interactions across different contexts (not just CBFβ-SMMHC) [56]. Mechanistically, AI-10-104 binds to CBFβ and blocks its binding to RUNX proteins, leaving RUNX in an auto-inhibited state (the Runt domain has an internal inhibitory sequence that is normally relieved by CBFβ binding). In cancer models outside of leukemia – for example, certain solid tumors or other malignancies where RUNX1 is oncogenic – AI-10-104 has been used as a chemical tool to reduce RUNX activity [60, 61]. However, in inv(16) cells, AI-10-104 is less selective: it will inhibit the fusion, but also wild-type RUNX1-CBFβ (and even other RUNX family members, since RUNX2/3 also use CBFβ). Thus, AI-10-104 can be thought of as a pan-RUNX/CBFβ antagonist, whereas AI-10-49 is an inv(16)-specific antagonist [25]. The broader activity of AI-10-104 could be advantageous in contexts like solid tumors where RUNX1 or RUNX2 drive cancer growth. But in leukemia, especially CBF leukemias, a pan-RUNX inhibitor might carry more side effects due to hitting normal hematopoiesis [61]. Indeed, AI-10-104 and similar first-generation inhibitors required micromolar concentrations for effect and had suboptimal pharmacokinetics. They serve as important reference points and starting scaffolds for medicinal chemistry optimization. AI-10-49 itself was derived by dimerizing an earlier mono-valent inhibitor (similar to AI-10-104), underscoring that the general scaffold had potency that could be enhanced by the bivalent approach [56, 62].

Other related small molecules and approaches have also been explored. For example, heterocyclic compounds identified by Gorczynski et al. were shown to bind CBFβ and disrupt its association with RUNX1, with activity in leukemia cells (these include earlier compounds like Compound 26 in their reports, which predate AI-10-49) [33]. There is also interest in molecules that target RUNX1 directly – for instance, stabilizing an autoinhibitory conformation of RUNX1’s Runt domain so it cannot bind CBFβ. One study described a small molecule (analogous to AI-10-4 series) that leaves RUNX1 in an “off” state [63]. While this would indeed inhibit the fusion’s effects, it is effectively similar to a RUNX1 knockout and could have considerable side effects by shutting down RUNX1 function globally. Therefore, these broad PPI inhibitors (Ro5-3335, AI-10-104, etc.) (Fig. 6) are often viewed as useful research tools and useful prototypes. They confirm that RUNX1–CBFβ is druggable, but they also highlight the need for specificity. For a clinical therapy in inv(16) AML, the preference is to target the fusion oncoprotein selectively, restoring RUNX1 transcriptional activity, rather than inhibiting RUNX1 outright [25].

Alternative intervention points: targeting RUNX1 Runt domain, fusion oligomerization, or cofactors

Disrupting CBFβ-SMMHC’s binding to RUNX1 is the most direct way to neutralize the fusion, but alternative strategies targeting other aspects of the fusion oncoprotein have shown promise as well. One approach is to target the RUNX1 Runt domain itself with molecules that either compete with CBFβ-SMMHC or otherwise modulate RUNX1’s activity [63]. For example, a hypothetical small molecule could bind in the pocket on RUNX1 where CBFβ normally fits [2], thereby preventing both wild-type and fusion interactions. As noted, this is essentially a pan-RUNX inhibitor strategy and could phenocopy RUNX1 loss – not ideal for selectivity. However, there may be more nuanced ways: for instance, interfering with the nuclear localization or post-translational modifications of RUNX1 could selectively impact fusion complexes. Some studies have looked at delivering peptide decoys derived from the RUNX1 Runt domain that can bind CBFβ-SMMHC and block RUNX1 sequestration; these cell-permeable peptides mimic the RUNX1 interface and tie up the fusion protein, freeing endogenous RUNX1. Such peptides showed on-target effects in cell culture but are not yet viable drugs due to stability and delivery challenges [64].

Another strategy is targeting the fusion’s oligomerization and cofactor interactions. The CBFβ-SMMHC multimerization (via the ACD coiled-coil) is crucial for its leukemogenic function [54]. Disrupting the coiled-coil assembly (for example with an α-helix mimetic that wedges into the coiled-coil interface) could render the fusion unable to form the large complexes needed to hijack RUNX1 effectively. While designing coiled-coil disruptors is challenging, there has been some progress in other systems using stapled peptides or small molecules targeting specific heptad repeat interactions. A successful oligomerization inhibitor would effectively lower the local concentration of RUNX1-binding sites and perhaps make the fusion behave more like a monomeric CBFβ (which, as in the ΔHABD case, might alleviate RUNX1 sequestration at least partially).

Additionally, the fusion’s interaction with chromatin modifiers presents druggable vulnerabilities. A striking example is HDAC8, which CBFβ-SMMHC recruits to deacetylate p53 and possibly other substrates [31, 43]. A specialized HDAC8 inhibitor named 22d was shown to selectively kill CBFβ-SMMHC–expressing leukemia cells by reactivating p53 [43]. In fusion-positive cells, 22d caused accumulation of acetylated p53 and triggered apoptosis, while sparing non-inv(16) cells and normal CD34 + hematopoietic cells [43]. This implies that CBFβ-SMMHC creates a unique dependency on HDAC8-mediated repression; blocking that interaction or the enzyme’s activity specifically harms the leukemia cells (a synthetic lethal interaction between the fusion and HDAC8). Targeting other cofactors is also conceivable: for instance, the fusion might engage the corepressor Sin3A or other histone deacetylases (HDAC1/2 via the SIN3 complex) [43]. Broad HDAC inhibitors are already used in leukemia, but they are not selective and have substantial toxicity. A more focused approach could be to disrupt the fusion–corepressor binding interface. If the region of CBFβ-SMMHC that binds Sin3A or N-CoR/SMRT were mapped, one could design peptides or molecules to block those contacts, relieving repression on RUNX1 target genes.

Lastly, targeting the downstream effects of the fusion can be considered an indirect PPI disruption. For instance, as we will discuss in Sect. 5, CBFβ-SMMHC causes aberrant activation of pro-survival programs (like MYC). One could combine a PPI inhibitor with a MYC pathway inhibitor (e.g., BET bromodomain inhibitor) to ensure that even if some RUNX1 remains sequestered, the downstream pathway is hit (this is more of a combination therapy angle. In summary, while the central focus is the RUNX1–CBFβ-SMMHC interface, there are multiple intervention points in the fusion’s network: one can target RUNX1 itself (less ideal systemically), disrupt the fusion’s multimeric scaffold, or hit its recruited accomplices (such as HDACs or other epigenetic enzymes) [43]. Each approach has its own balance of selectivity and efficacy, and some (like HDAC8 inhibition) may be best deployed alongside direct PPI disruption to fully dismantle the oncoprotein’s effects [59].

Pharmacology considerations: stability, cell permeability, on-target biomarkers

Developing a PPI inhibitor into a real drug requires attention to pharmacological properties – a lesson learned from early RUNX1–CBFβ inhibitors [50]. Compounds like Ro5-3335 [50] had moderate potency and needed to be administered frequently to maintain effect, partly due to metabolic instability [50]. AI-10-49, while more potent and selective, is a relatively large bivalent molecule, raising questions about cell permeability and in vivo pharmacokinetics [56]. Encouragingly, AI-10-49 was reported to have favorable pharmacokinetics in mice (likely due to its bivalent binding contributing to a longer target residence time) [65]. Still, further optimization is needed to ensure oral bioavailability or reasonable half-life in humans. Solubility is another concern: early analogues like AI-10-104 had solubility limits that affected their cell activity. Medicinal chemistry efforts focus on refining these molecules to improve solubility without sacrificing binding affinity. The use of linkers in bivalent inhibitors must balance flexibility (to allow both ends to bind their targets) with rigidity (to avoid entropy penalties and off-target binding). A too-flexible linker could allow the compound to interact with unintended proteins, whereas a too-short linker might not span the two sites on the fusion dimer [56].

Cell permeability and nuclear uptake are crucial, since the target is a nuclear protein complex. Designing PPI inhibitors often involves larger molecular weight and polar surface area than typical enzyme inhibitors, which can impair crossing the cell membrane. Some strategies to enhance permeability include prodrug forms (that release the active inhibitor inside cells), adding cell-penetrating moieties, or exploiting active transport mechanisms if known. The case of Ro5-3335 [50] (a benzodiazepine analog) likely benefited from the inherent membrane permeability of that scaffold [50]. For newer analogues, achieving the same may require careful tweaking of lipophilicity [56].

On-target biomarkers are invaluable for demonstrating that the drug engages CBFβ-SMMHC in patients. One straightforward marker is the transcriptional response: as soon as RUNX1 is liberated, we expect upregulation or reactivation of specific RUNX1 target genes [56]. In fact, acute RUNX1 release upon AI-10-49 treatment can be measured by increased RUNX1 occupancy at certain gene enhancers (e.g., distal enhancers of the MYC gene) [59]. A drop in MYC mRNA levels within a few hours of dosing could serve as a pharmacodynamic marker that the fusion’s hold on RUNX1 has been broken [56, 59]. Another potential biomarker is the subcellular localization of RUNX1: if the fusion sequesters some RUNX1 in the cytoplasm, a good inhibitor might cause an increase in RUNX1’s nuclear concentration (though this might be subtle and hard to measure in primary cells). The epigenetic changes provide biomarkers too – for instance, if the drug is working, one might observe a decrease in H3K4me1 and increase in H3K27me3 at fusion-occupied enhancers (as RUNX1 binding recruits Polycomb complexes) [66, 67]. Such chromatin marks can be assessed by ChIP or CUT&RUN techniques in patient samples pre- vs. post-therapy. Finally, simple cell surface or differentiation markers could be monitored: effective RUNX1 restoration might induce partial differentiation of blasts (e.g., increased expression of myeloid maturation markers) or signs of apoptosis (phosphatidylserine exposure, mitochondrial depolarization in blasts). In animal studies, delay of leukemia progression and reduction of leukemic engraftment are ultimate proof of on-target effect [68], and similar logic will apply in human trials – e.g., molecular remission (clearing of CBFB-MYH11 transcripts) could be the endpoint.

Mechanistic outcomes of PPI disruption in inv(16) AML

Rapid RUNX1 release and transcriptional reactivation

Pharmacologic disruption of the CBFβ-SMMHC–RUNX1 interaction produces an immediate reversal of the fusion’s effects at the molecular level [56, 59]. Upon treatment with a selective inhibitor like AI-10-49, RUNX1 is acutely liberated from the fusion within hours [56, 59]. Biochemically, this is evident as free RUNX1 protein that is no longer co-immunoprecipitated with CBFβ-SMMHC and that can diffuse throughout the nucleus [56, 59]. The “free” RUNX1 rapidly re-engages with its target DNA sequences, many of which had been unoccupied or minimally occupied in the presence of the fusion [59, 69]. Genome-wide analyses have captured this phenomenon: for example, chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) experiments show that within 6 h of AI-10-49 treatment, RUNX1 occupancy increases significantly at numerous enhancers and promoters in inv(16) leukemia cells [59, 70]. This indicates that RUNX1 molecules, once released, quickly find their binding motifs in the genome, particularly at genes that were previously repressed by the fusion [70].

The transcriptional consequences of RUNX1 reactivation are profound. RUNX1 is a transcription factor that can act as an activator or repressor depending on context. In the case of inv(16) AML, many RUNX1 target genes that promote differentiation or apoptosis have been suppressed by the fusion. When RUNX1 is freed and returns to these sites, it often triggers transcriptional reactivation [56, 59]. RNA-seq studies confirm that within hours to a day of treatment, a wave of gene expression changes occurs – essentially the inverse of the fusion’s gene signature [71]. Genes involved in myeloid differentiation, cell cycle arrest, and pro-apoptotic pathways are upregulated, reflecting RUNX1’s regained function as a regulator of these programs [56, 59]. Conversely, some genes that were aberrantly activated in the presence of the fusion (due to indirect effects) may be downregulated once RUNX1 is back controlling the transcriptional landscape [72].

A critical aspect of this reactivation is that it recapitulates normal developmental cues. For instance, RUNX1 target genes important for granulocytic maturation (such as CSF3R or CEBPE) see increased expression, suggesting that blasts might be poised to resume differentiation [73]. However, inv(16) cells often undergo apoptosis rather than complete maturation (discussed in 5.4), likely because the cooperating mutations (and the sudden loss of MYC support, see 5.2) push them toward cell death rather than slow differentiation [56, 59].

One noteworthy observation is that RUNX1 can also function as a transcriptional repressor at certain sites – particularly the MYC oncogene enhancers [74]. Thus, “reactivation” of RUNX1’s function can mean turning off some genes that were inappropriately on. In summary, the immediate outcome of PPI disruption is that RUNX1 is restored to its rightful genomic occupancy, which sets off a cascade of transcriptional changes: previously suppressed differentiation/apoptosis genes turn on, and aberrantly active self-renewal/proliferation genes turn off. This rapid reprogramming is the foundational event that leads to the phenotypic outcomes of therapy.

MYC-centered vulnerability: enhancer re-occupancy and chromatin complex switching

A major downstream consequence of disrupting the CBFβ-SMMHC–RUNX1 interaction is repression of the proto-oncogene MYC, a central driver of leukemic growth programs. In inv(16) AML, MYC levels are functionally important for survival, and reducing MYC is sufficient to compromise viability [75]. Consistent with this, pharmacologic release of RUNX1 using the bivalent inhibitor AI-10-49 rapidly restores RUNX1 access to regulatory DNA and initiates a MYC-directed transcriptional collapse [25]. Mechanistically, RUNX1 re-engages three distal MYC enhancers (reported at approximately + 0.18 Mb, + 0.5 Mb, and + 1.7 Mb from the MYC transcription start site), which are part of an enhancer architecture frequently associated with high MYC output in hematologic malignancies [59]. A key feature of the response is not merely “RUNX1 binding,” but a switch in enhancer-associated chromatin machinery: RUNX1 re-occupancy is followed by loss of SWI/SNF (BRG1) occupancy and recruitment of PRC1 activity (RING1B/RNF2), accompanied by increased repressive chromatin features at these enhancers and a marked drop in MYC transcription. Functionally, this MYC repression is causal for cell death: MYC knockdown reduces inv(16) AML viability, and ectopic MYC expression partially rescues AI-10-49–induced apoptosis [76]. Importantly, 3D enhancer–promoter communication appears to persist early after inhibitor exposure, indicating that MYC downregulation can occur through biochemical reprogramming of enhancer output rather than immediate loss of enhancer–promoter proximity (Fig. 8). These observations also suggest rational combinations: because BET proteins support oncogenic enhancer function and MYC expression, pairing fusion-disrupting agents with BET bromodomain inhibitors (e.g., JQ1/I-BET class) is a mechanistically grounded strategy and has shown strong activity in leukemia models.

Epigenetic remodeling signatures (BRG1/RING1B, H3K4me1/H3K27me3)

As hinted above, one of the hallmarks of disrupting the fusion–RUNX1 complex is a wave of epigenetic remodeling at target loci. The MYC enhancer example is particularly well-characterized: AI-10-49 causes loss of BRG1 (SWI/SNF) and gain of RING1B (PRC1) at those enhancers [56, 59]. This is emblematic of a larger pattern In inv(16) cells, CBFβ-SMMHC likely maintains certain genomic regions in an abnormally active state (through recruitment of SWI/SNF or other coactivators) and other regions in a repressed state (through its HDAC interactions) [43, 77]. When RUNX1 is freed, it can re-establish normal repressive marks on oncogenic enhancers and possibly activate some differentiation genes by recruiting co-activators. The net effect observed in experiments is a shift in the balance of chromatin marks: active enhancer marks drop, repressive marks rise on RUNX1-reengaged regions. At the three MYC enhancers, H3K27ac levels (not explicitly mentioned above but indicated by the open/active status) would be expected to fall once PRC1 comes, while H3K4me1 (a mark of primed/enhancer state) falls and the Polycomb-associated mark H3K27me3 increases [78]. These changes are essentially converting an active super-enhancer into a poised or silenced enhancer.

Beyond MYC, there are likely other loci where RUNX1’s return causes epigenetic changes [77]. For instance, genes that should be activated for differentiation might gain H3K4me3 at their promoters once RUNX1 recruits coactivators (CBFβ-SMMHC might have prevented that by either keeping RUNX1 away or recruiting repressors). Although detailed chromatin profiling genome-wide after AI-10-49 is still limited, the evidence points to a general theme: RUNX1 acts as a genome organizer, and releasing it resets the chromatin landscape towards a more normal state [56, 59]. Even the fact that Polycomb (PRC1/PRC2) gets re-invoked is notable – it suggests that in normal progenitors, RUNX1 might help shut off genes like MYC via Polycomb as they differentiate, a process the fusion was subverting [79].

Another layer of epigenetic remodeling involves the fusion’s direct partners like HDAC8 and p53 [43]. When CBFβ-SMMHC is inhibited or removed, the repression of p53 via deacetylation is lifted [43]. That means p53 becomes acetylated and active, which can induce expression of p53 target genes involved in apoptosis and cell cycle arrest [43]. While this is more of a post-translational modification change than a chromatin mark, it is an important signature – effectively an epigenetic outcome on a transcription factor [43]. In experiments, treating inv(16) cells with an HDAC8 inhibitor (or potentially with the PPI inhibitor, indirectly) increases acetylation on p53 lysines, enhancing p53’s ability to drive apoptosis [43]. Thus we see a coordinated picture: RUNX1 reoccupies chromatin and triggers local chromatin changes, and simultaneously, the fusion’s absence means global effects like active p53 come into play [43, 59]. The sum of these epigenetic and signaling changes pushes the leukemic cell towards cell death or differentiation [43, 59].

One can detect these epigenetic changes as biomarkers. For example, an increase in RING1B and H3K27me3 at MYC enhancers after treatment is a highly specific indicator that RUNX1 has done its job of repression [80]. Similarly, one could look at acetyl-p53 levels or the expression of p53 target genes like CDKN1A/p21 as indicators that the fusion’s repression of p53 is relieved [43]. It is worth noting that the involvement of SWI/SNF and Polycomb suggests that large chromatin remodeling machines are downstream of the fusion’s activity [81]. This connects inv(16) AML to a broader theme in leukemia biology where dysregulated transcription factors hijack epigenetic regulators (another example is RUNX1-ETO recruiting HDACs and DNMTs in t(8;21) AML) [82, 83]. Importantly, it means that therapies which further modulate these epigenetic regulators could synergize – for instance, combining a PPI disruptor with a BRD4 inhibitor or a histone methyltransferase inhibitor might amplify the transcriptional reprogramming [84].

Functional phenotypes: apoptosis, differentiation effects, leukemia-initiating activity

Disrupting the CBFβ-SMMHC–RUNX1 complex produces a strong functional response dominated by apoptosis and reduced clonogenic potential. In ME-1 cells and primary inv(16) AML blasts, the CBFβ-SMMHC–selective inhibitor AI-10-49 rapidly increases apoptotic cells (Annexin V/7AAD) within ~ 48 h and suppresses colony-forming capacity, while showing minimal effects on normal-karyotype AML samples and CD34 + cord-blood progenitors, consistent with on-target selectivity [56, 59]. Although some contexts show partial differentiation features, differentiation is not the dominant phenotype: AI-10-49 reduced inv(16) viability without an apparent differentiation effect in patient samples, and MYC suppression in inv(16) AML primarily drives apoptosis rather than increased myeloid maturation markers.

In vivo, AI-10-49 delays disease progression in transplant models, and combining AI-10-49 with BET inhibition (JQ1) further enhances anti-leukemia activity, reducing leukemia burden and extending survival (reported as elimination of inv(16) leukemia in human cells and a mouse model). Since inv(16) AML is sustained by leukemia-initiating/LSC populations and MYC-linked programs support their maintenance, independent work showing that targeting LSC regulators (e.g., the miR-126 → MYC axis) reduces LSC activity reinforces the therapeutic value of RUNX1-reactivation strategies that collapse MYC output [85].

Combination therapies and synthetic lethal directions

BET inhibition (e.g., JQ1) and MYC suppression synergy

Given the central role of MYC in inv(16) AML cell survival (as revealed by RUNX1 reactivation studies), combining CBFβ-SMMHC PPI disruption with therapies that directly or indirectly suppress MYC is a rational strategy [57]. One prominent example is the combination of AI-10-49 [56] with the BET bromodomain inhibitor JQ1 [84, 86]. BET proteins (like BRD4) are epigenetic readers that sustain MYC transcription in many cancers [84, 86]; JQ1 binds BET bromodomains and causes downregulation of MYC expression [84, 86]. In inv(16) cells, AI-10-49 and JQ1 have been shown to work synergistically to kill leukemia cells [59]. AI-10-49 releases RUNX1 to repress MYC, while JQ1 further reduces transcriptional activation of MYC – together they effectively shut off MYC from two angles [87]. This results in enhanced apoptosis compared to either agent alone. In mouse models, the combination produced a significant extension in leukemia-free survival, even achieving elimination of detectable leukemia in some cases. The strong synergy likely comes from the fact that inv(16) AML cells are so exquisitely MYC-dependent that any robust dual hit to MYC tips the balance irreversibly toward cell death [59].

Beyond JQ1, other strategies to attack MYC could also synergize. Although direct MYC inhibitors are not available clinically (MYC is a transcription factor often deemed “undruggable”), one can target pathways that support MYC. For instance, CDK9 inhibitors (which affect transcriptional elongation of MYC) [88], or mTORC1 inhibitors (which can diminish MYC translation) [89], might combine well with RUNX1-releasing therapy. Additionally, the synthetic lethal relationship with MYC suggests that combining AI-10-49 with any drug that creates stress MYC makes worse (like inhibitors of ribosome biogenesis or proteosome inhibitors) could preferentially harm the leukemia cells [59].

The combination with BET inhibitors like JQ1 is particularly appealing because BET inhibitors alone have shown some efficacy in AML by lowering MYC and other pro-leukemic genes, but not enough for sustained responses due to resistance [90, 91]. In an inv(16) context, RUNX1 reactivation hits MYC and presumably a network of other targets (some of which might be compensatory pathways). JQ1 then prevents the cell from rallying a transcriptional compensation via BET proteins [90, 91]. Notably, JQ1 will also suppress other oncogenic drivers beyond MYC (such as BCL2 or CDK6 in some AML contexts) [90], potentially covering escape routes the cells might use. Overall, this suggests that dual therapy could be far more potent and also possibly allow lower dosing of each, reducing toxicity.

Preclinical synergy data has spurred interest in testing such combos in animal models and eventually patients. One could envision a clinical trial where inv(16) AML patients receive a RUNX1–CBFβ inhibitor plus a BET inhibitor [92]. The goal is that even minimal residual disease (MRD) is eradicated, as the combination might eliminate any cells that one agent alone would leave behind. Indeed, Pulikkan et al. (2018) showed that combined AI-10-49 and JQ1 completely cleared inv(16) leukemic blasts in a mouse xenograft model, a result rarely seen with single agents [59]. This provides a strong rationale to pursue such combination clinically. It exemplifies a broader concept: target the oncogenic fusion’s primary function (RUNX1 sequestration) and simultaneously target a key downstream effector or addiction (MYC via BET inhibition) – a combined approach exploiting synthetic lethality for maximal effect [84].

Standard-of-care and rational combinations: chemotherapy and targeted agents

Another important avenue is integrating PPI disruptors with existing standard-of-care therapies for AML [93]. For inv(16) AML, standard treatment is intensive chemotherapy (e.g., cytarabine-based regimens) often followed by high-dose consolidation [93–95]. These therapies cause DNA damage and kill rapidly dividing cells. A CBFβ-SMMHC inhibitor could potentially enhance chemotherapy efficacy or allow for dose reductions by sensitizing leukemia cells to cytotoxic effects. For instance, if RUNX1 reactivation induces differentiation, the formerly quiescent or chemo-resistant blasts might enter the cell cycle or lose some drug-resistance traits, making them more chemosensitive. Moreover, the severe block in differentiation caused by the fusion is partly why blasts persist; lifting that block might reduce the “stemness” of leukemia cells, making them easier to eliminate with chemotherapy.

One rational combination is with DNA hypomethylating agents (like azacitidine or decitabine), which are sometimes used in AML, especially in less fit patients [93]. These agents broadly reactivate silenced genes and can promote differentiation. In an inv(16) context, a hypomethylating agent might complement PPI disruption by activating additional tumor suppressor genes while RUNX1 reactivation is occurring. There’s also interest in venetoclax (a BCL-2 inhibitor) combinations, as venetoclax with low-dose chemotherapy is effective in many AML subtypes [96–98]. If CBFβ-SMMHC inhibition upregulates pro-apoptotic factors (via p53 or others), adding venetoclax could strongly trigger mitochondrial apoptosis. Conversely, venetoclax might help kill any cells that partially resist RUNX1 reactivation by leaning on BCL-2 survival.

Another targeted combination to consider is with tyrosine kinase inhibitors in cases where inv(16) AML has a cooperative KIT mutation, which occurs in ~ 30% of CBF AML [99, 100]. Midostaurin, a FLT3/KIT inhibitor, is already used in AML with FLT3 mutations and has shown benefit in CBF AML in some studies [101]. Targeting the KIT signaling (proliferation driver) while simultaneously restoring RUNX1 (differentiation and apoptosis driver) could cover both the differentiation block and the proliferation stimulus. This approach addresses both “hits” of the two-hit model: one drug reverses the differentiation block, the other curtails uncontrolled proliferation.

Immunotherapies might also be combined. For example, an inhibitor releasing RUNX1 could increase expression of differentiation antigens on blasts (making them easier targets for immunotherapy) or could synergize with immune checkpoint blockade if the dying differentiating cells become more immunogenic. While speculative, it is known that differentiation therapy in APL makes blasts express maturation markers and can attract immune clearance.

It is important in any combination to consider scheduling. For example, giving the PPI inhibitor first might prime cells for chemo by synchronizing them or making them express drug-targetable proteins. Alternatively, one might want to debulk with chemo then use the targeted agent to eradicate MRD. In APL, ATRA is typically given after chemotherapy during consolidation [102].

Preclinical studies can help determine whether simultaneous or sequential administration is ideal.

In terms of rational combos, one should also think about resistance (next section) – combining with other agents may preempt the outgrowth of resistant clones. For instance, if a clone manages to maintain MYC expression despite RUNX1 reactivation (say via some alternate enhancer hijack), a BET inhibitor could still target that [59]. If a clone loses p53 making it less sensitive to RUNX1-mediated apoptosis, combining with another pro-differentiation agent might kill it by forcing differentiation.

Resistance mechanisms and biomarkers to monitor response

As with any targeted therapy, leukemia cells may develop resistance to CBFβ-SMMHC inhibitors [103]. Anticipating these mechanisms is important for designing combination strategies and monitoring tools [103]. One possible resistance mechanism is mutation of the target interface – for example, mutations in the CBFβ portion of the fusion that reduce drug binding but still allow RUNX1 binding [56, 103]. Since AI-10-49 binds to CBFβ (and possibly spans to another subunit), a point mutation in CBFβ’s binding groove might diminish AI-10-49 affinity. If such a mutation did not severely disrupt RUNX1 binding (for instance, if it is at a pocket site not absolutely essential for RUNX1), it could confer resistance [103]. Similarly, a mutation in RUNX1’s Runt domain that weakens drug binding but retains fusion binding could occur [103]. However, because RUNX1 is not oncogenically mutated in these leukemias usually, acquiring a RUNX1 mutation might be less likely (plus it could impair the cell’s fitness). Still, resistant subclones in culture could be selected to see what emerges – common changes might involve the fusion protein altering a residue at the small molecule’s binding site [103].

Another route to resistance is pathway rewire or bypass: the leukemia cell might overcome RUNX1’s restored repression by upregulating alternate pathways [59, 103]. For instance, if MYC is repressed by RUNX1, the cell might mutate or amplify a different gene that can compensate for MYC loss [104]. Or it might mutate something in the enhancer (like the RUNX1 binding sites themselves) so that RUNX1 can no longer bind and repress effectively. Interestingly, experiments deleting the RUNX1 binding motifs at the MYC enhancers did reduce MYC and cell viability, implying that if a leukemia clone were to lose those sites (via mutation or chromosomal alteration), it would actually harm itself baseline [59]. So that particular escape seems disadvantageous to the leukemia unless coupled with some other change. Nonetheless, cells might find other ways: e.g., upregulating anti-apoptotic proteins like BCL2A1 or MCL1 in response to the drug, thereby countering the pro-apoptotic effect [105]. Monitoring expression of such potential escape genes could be a way to catch resistance early [105].

The leukemia microenvironment might also contribute to resistance [106]. Cytokines or stromal signals could potentially dampen RUNX1’s restored activity (by post-translational modifications, etc.), or provide growth signals that lessen dependency on MYC [106]. For example, high levels of IL-3 or GM-CSF might partially rescue differentiation-blocked cells by strong proliferation signals even when RUNX1 is back [106]. Combination with targeted therapies against such signaling (JAK inhibitors if IL-3 is high, etc.) could be considered if observed [106].

To detect emerging resistance, certain biomarkers would be informative: [107] - Molecular residual disease: Because inv(16) AML can be tracked by PCR for CBFB-MYH11 transcripts, one can monitor how well the transcript levels drop with therapy [4, 5, 6]. If a patient’s fusion transcript stops declining or starts rising while on the inhibitor, that’s an early sign of resistance [107, 108]. - RUNX1 target gene expression: one could monitor a set of RUNX1-repressed genes (like MYC itself or some downstream signature). If initially MYC goes down and p21 goes up (for example) but later MYC re-elevates or target genes fail to respond despite drug, that indicates the cells are no longer responding as before [59].- Sequencing for mutations: periodic sequencing of the CBFB-MYH11 fusion gene from blood or marrow could catch mutations in CBFβ or other regions that might mediate resistance [107]. Chromatin marker changes: using newer techniques like ATAC-seq or ChIP in serial samples could show if those enhancer mark changes (H3K27me3 gain at MYC enhancers, etc.) are maintained [109]. If the enhancer chromatin flips back to active state despite continuous therapy, it implies resistance at an epigenetic level [110].

If resistance is detected, combination therapies as discussed become crucial [103]. For example, if a clone acquires a mutation that reduces AI-10-49 binding, adding a different class of inhibitor (like an HDAC8 inhibitor or a PROTAC) might kill those cells [43]. Or if the cells adapt by upregulating BCL-2, adding venetoclax could cut that escape route [108]. This is analogous to what is done in other leukemias where monotherapy often is not curative due to resistant clones, so multi-agent therapy is used to prevent any one clone from surviving [103].

Translational outlook

Next-generation modalities: proteolysis (PROTACs), covalent strategies, macrocycles, peptides

Looking ahead, there are exciting possibilities to refine or replace small-molecule PPI inhibitors with advanced therapeutic modalities. One concept is using PROTACs (proteolysis-targeting chimeras) to target the fusion protein for degradation [111, 112]. A PROTAC would consist of a ligand that binds CBFβ-SMMHC (for example, one half could be derived from AI-10-49 or a similar binder) linked to a ligand for an E3 ubiquitin ligase (Fig. 9). If successful, the PROTAC would cause ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of the fusion oncoprotein [112]. This approach could have two advantages: (1) It would eliminate the fusion entirely, not just disrupt RUNX1 binding, thereby also removing other fusion activities (like HDAC8 recruitment or any RUNX1-independent effects). (2) Catalytic action – a single PROTAC molecule can induce degradation of many target molecules, potentially achieving strong suppression even if binding affinity is modest [112]. Designing a PROTAC for CBFβ-SMMHC is challenging but feasible since we do have ligands that bind the fusion. One must ensure the ligand part is selective (to avoid degrading wild-type CBFβ or other RUNX partners). If achieved, a CBFβ-SMMHC PROTAC could be extremely potent in clearing leukemia cells, essentially reverting the genotype at a protein level. Early-phase research in PROTACs has shown success in targeting transcription factors like ER or AR for degradation, supporting the idea [113–115].

A schematic illustration of using PROTAC as an example to target the fusion protein for degradation

Another next-gen idea is covalent inhibitors [116, 117]. If there’s a nucleophilic pocket or cysteine on CBFβ-SMMHC near the interface, a covalent binder could lock the fusion in an “inactive” conformation irreversibly. Covalent drugs can provide prolonged inhibition with short exposures [116]. One would have to identify a unique cysteine on the fusion (or even on RUNX1, but better on the fusion) that a drug could target without hitting other proteins. For instance, if the fusion’s CBFβ domain has a cysteine not present in wild-type CBFβ (perhaps due to different redox environment or sequence differences after fusion), that could be exploited.

Macrocycles and peptides are also intriguing. The interaction surface for RUNX1–CBFβ is relatively large, and nature often uses peptides (like viral proteins or autoinhibitory loops) to modulate such interactions. A stapled peptide derived from the RUNX1 βC–βD loop or from the SMMHC HABD region could potentially bind and interfere with the complex. Stapled peptides (hydrocarbon-stabilized helices) have been used to inhibit other transcription factor PPIs (e.g., p53-MDM2 disruptors) [118, 119]. For CBFβ-SMMHC, a peptide matching the critical interface of RUNX1 might act as a decoy: it could bind the fusion’s CBFβ pocket and HABD, preventing the actual RUNX1 from binding. Or a peptide from CBFβ’s binding interface could bind to RUNX1’s Runt domain, blocking both fusion and wild-type – unless modified for selectivity. Peptides can be quite specific and potent, but delivering them to cells (especially to the nucleus) is a hurdle [119]. Some cell-penetrating peptide tags or nanoparticle delivery might help. Macrocyclic compounds, which are somewhere between small molecules and peptides, could also be employed to target extended surfaces with high affinity [120].

In addition to classical modalities, one can imagine gene therapy or protein therapy approaches: for example, delivering a miniaturized RUNX1 protein that competes for fusion binding but lacks the ability to bind DNA (so it acts as a sink for the fusion). This is conceptually similar to a decoy peptide but larger. Or using CRISPR-based gene editing to knockout the fusion gene – though delivering CRISPR to all leukemia cells is currently not practical.

Finally, immunotherapies might indirectly target the fusion. A PROTAC-like twist is the idea of “molecular glues” that cause the fusion to be recognized by the immune system.[7] For instance, if a small molecule could induce a neo-epitope or make the fusion aggregate and present peptides on MHC, that could help immune clearance. Vaccines against the unique junction of CBFβ-MYH11 have been contemplated as well [121].

Future perspectives

Looking forward, several future perspectives emerge. Clinically, we anticipate trials in CBF-AML patients to test these inhibitors [56], likely in combination with existing therapies (given synergy with agents like JQ1 [59, 122] and perhaps enabling reduced chemotherapy dosing). Success in inv(16) could encourage analogous strategies in other fusion-driven cancers – for example, drugs that disrupt RUNX1-ETO in t(8;21) AML [123, 124] or other transcription factor fusions (though each has unique challenges). Scientifically, continued refinement of our compounds is underway: next-generation molecules aim to improve pharmacokinetics and oral availability, and innovative approaches like PROTACs [112] could literally destroy the fusion protein rather than just inhibit it. It will also be fascinating to dissect whether long-term inhibition might allow some normal RUNX1 activity to compensate for fusion loss in any surviving pre-leukemic cells, or if resistance mechanisms (like fusion mutations or pathway bypasses) appear in patients – and how to counteract them (possibly with multi-pronged therapies targeting, say, both the fusion and its cofactor dependencies). Moreover, this line of research opens questions about the role of RUNX1 as a tumor suppressor [8]. In inv(16) AML, we see that restoring RUNX1’s function is lethal to cancer cells; could transiently boosting RUNX1 activity be beneficial in other contexts of low RUNX function (like certain MDS or RUNX1-mutated leukemias) [125, 126]? Perhaps not directly with the same drug, but conceptually, transcription factor reactivation emerges as a therapeutic theme.

Recently, recurrent in-frame insertions in exon 3 of CBFB have been described in a small subset of AML and termed “CBFB-GDXY,” reflecting a highly stereotyped replacement at CBFβ Asp87 (D87) that generates either GDSY or GDTY within the N-terminal RUNX-binding domain [127]. Transcriptomically, CBFB-GDXY cases cluster adjacent to canonical CBF AML (e.g., CBFB::MYH11), while showing a more stem-like expression signature with increased HOXA/MEIS1 compared with typical inv(16) AML. Reported co-mutation patterns also differ from CBFB::MYH11 AML, with enrichment of FLT3 (often TKD) and BCOR-family lesions and a relative lack of KIT mutations. Mechanistically, the consequences of these insertions remain to be defined; however, their location within the RUNX-interaction region raises the possibility that they alter CBFβ–RUNX1 affinity, conformation, or cofactor recruitment, thereby generating a CBF-like transcriptional program through a mutation-driven perturbation of the same core complex targeted in inv(16). Clarifying whether CBFB-GDXY rewires the CBFβ–RUNX1 interface in ways that influence vulnerability to PPI-disrupting strategies will be an important direction for future work [127].

Conclusions

Targeting the CBFβ-SMMHC–RUNX1 protein–protein interaction in inv(16) AML illustrates how deep mechanistic understanding of a fusion oncoprotein can be translated into a tractable therapeutic strategy. Rather than inhibiting an enzyme active site, this approach neutralizes leukemogenic function by restoring a corrupted transcription factor network: disrupting the fusion’s grip on RUNX1 re-establishes normal gene control, collapses pro-survival transcriptional programs, and reinstates differentiation and apoptosis pathways that the leukemia depends upon suppressing. The evolution from early, nonselective RUNX1–CBFβ antagonists to fusion-selective bivalent inhibitors underscores a central lesson for “undruggable” targets: specificity can be achieved by exploiting unique structural and organizational features of the oncoprotein itself, not merely by improving potency against a conserved interface.

Across the studies reviewed, a coherent therapeutic logic emerges. inv(16) AML is sustained by RUNX1 sequestration coupled to epigenetic and transcriptional rewiring, creating a state of dependency that can be reversed rapidly when RUNX1 is liberated. The consequent transcriptional reprogramming is not subtle: key oncogenic circuits such as MYC-centered enhancer networks are switched off, chromatin states at critical regulatory elements are remodeled, and fusion-imposed constraints on tumor suppressor pathways are relieved. Importantly, these effects can be achieved with a therapeutic window, as normal hematopoietic cells are comparatively resilient to transient perturbation when RUNX1 function is preserved rather than extinguished.

Taken together, disruption of the CBFβ-SMMHC–RUNX1 interaction represents a blueprint for precision targeting of fusion-driven leukemia: identify the obligate protein–protein interactions that sustain malignant identity, design inhibitors that exploit fusion-specific architecture, and validate on-target pathway collapse using molecular biomarkers of transcriptional and chromatin resetting. If these principles translate clinically, inv(16) AML could shift from a chemotherapy-dependent disease to one managed with mechanism-based therapy that is both more selective and more durable. Beyond inv(16), the broader implication is that transcription factor complexes and oncogenic PPIs need not remain beyond pharmacologic reach; with sufficient structural insight and creative modality design, they can become central nodes for targeted cancer therapy.
