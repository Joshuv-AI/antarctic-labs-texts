The search goes on: optimal trauma triage criteria for older adults remain elusive
Authors: Stepheny Berry, Craig Follette, Jennifer Hartwell
Published: 2025

Mitigating overtriage and undertriage in trauma activation criteria (TAC) has continued to vex trauma surgeons for decades since the earliest criteria were described in the inaugural Optimal Resources for the Injured Patient by the American College of Surgeons (ACS) in 1976.1 Recent studies such as Boulton et al2 evaluated elderly-specific prehospital trauma triage criteria and found limited real-world data on their effectiveness in improving patient care and outcomes. Conversely, in a recent meta-analysis, Wake et al3 investigated various criteria for trauma activation and found low Glasgow Coma Scale, hypotension, abnormal vital signs at emergency department triage, and the presence of multiregion trauma predict the presence of major trauma in adult trauma patients, hypothesizing that these criteria could form a foundation for evidence-based TAC.

The current study by Fakhry et al4 investigates the effectiveness of existing TAC in identifying high-intensity, time-sensitive (HITS) interventions in older versus younger patients. This robust study of over 216 000 patients carefully reviewed various levels of trauma activation (full, partial, or none) and ultimately found that current TAC identifies the need for HITS interventions in both younger and older adults equally and does not improve overtriage or undertriage in either group. Of note, the current study uses systolic blood pressure (SBP) <90 mm Hg and heart rate (HR) >120 beats per minute as criteria for full trauma activation; however, the ACS Best Practices Guidelines for Geriatric Trauma Management recommends an SBP <110 mm Hg and HR >90 beats per minute for geriatric patients. It is our bias that the ACS Best Practices’ more conservative guidelines drive overtriage of the geriatric patient. Knowing several centers have adopted the ACS Best Practices Guidelines into their activation criteria, using the ACS suggested criteria could have changed the results of the study and presents an opportunity for a similar study using the more conservative cut-off points.

Healthcare resources remain limited and precious, so the quest for more precise, efficient, high-yield TAC should continue. This novel article addresses key clinical issues and adds to the ever-mounting evidence of the need for more research in TAC.
