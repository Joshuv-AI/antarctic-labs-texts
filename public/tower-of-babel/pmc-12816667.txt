Selective cognitive effects of multilingualism emerge in visuospatial working memory in later life
Authors: Olivia R Maurice, Caroline Jones, Celia B Harris
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpidemiological literature suggests that speaking multiple languages can delay the onset of neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease. Cognitive and neuroscientific explanations, however, remain inconclusive. While some studies have found behavioural and neural effects of being multilingual (compared to monolingual) for working memory, inhibitory control, and attention across the lifespan, others have found no such evidence. An emerging focus on nuanced, dimensional measures of language experience, direct comparison of tasks within the same experiment, and a direct comparison of stimulus modality (visuospatial versus auditory processing) could potentially resolve these mixed findings. The present study investigated whether multilingual experience, measured continuously via the Language History Questionnaire (LHQ-3), was associated with domain-specific cognitive performance in both auditory and visuospatial modalities in a lifespan sample (N = 94, aged 18–83). Participants completed working memory (N-back), inhibitory control (Simon), and attention (Attention Network and Dichotic Listening) tasks in both auditory and visuospatial formats. Generalised additive models revealed no significant associations between multilingual experience and cognitive performance for auditory and most visuospatial tasks. However, a significant interaction between multilingual language diversity and age was found for visuospatial working memory, with older multilingual adults demonstrating higher accuracy and faster response times. These findings suggest that the cognitive effects of multilingualism may be age-, domain-, and modality-specific rather than universal. Neuroprotective associations observed in epidemiological and neuroimaging studies may therefore reflect mechanisms beyond direct behavioural performance, such as neural reserve or compensation.Keywords: Multilingual, Language experience, Cognitive ageing, Memory, Attention, InhibitionSubject terms: Cognitive ageing, Cognitive neuroscience, Psychology

Introduction

Dementia refers to a group of neurodegenerative disorders that affect over 55 million people globally, with 10 million new cases diagnosed every year1. In response to growing worldwide concern, recent studies have provided evidence for the role of modifiable lifestyle factors in managing overall dementia risk2,3. Lifestyle factors experienced throughout the lifespan have been shown to substantially impact dementia risk; these factors include education, participation in physical and cognitive activities, high quality nutrition, social engagement, and psychological wellbeing2–5. Engaging in both cognitively demanding and social activities has broad benefits for brain health, with studies showing delays in the expression of Alzheimer’s disease-related impairment2,3,6,7. Considering the benefits of both social engagement and cognitive stimulation for cognitive function and dementia risk2,4,5, it is possible that a lifetime of speaking and using multiple languages could integrate social and cognitive factors to deliver such benefits8,9. In light of this possibility, the current study examined the effects of age and multilingualism across ranging cognitive tasks.

Epidemiological research suggests that speaking more than one language is associated with delayed onset of symptoms for a range of neurodegenerative conditions at a population level10–14. This benefit of speaking multiple languages has been observed for various dementia neuropathologies including Alzheimer’s disease, frontotemporal dementia and vascular dementia10, as well as mild cognitive impairment, logopenic variant primary progressive aphasia and post-stroke cognitive decline15–17. Studies indicate that people with multilingual experience show delayed onset of dementia symptoms by up to five years, as compared with their monolingual peers10–14,18,19. Ageing research using categorical designs with bilinguals has similarly documented onset delays in dementia symptoms by approximately 4.1 years, equating to a 47% reduction in dementia prevalence for this population of older people11.

Inconsistent findings in the cognitive literature

Despite promising population data demonstrating the potential benefits of multilingualism for decreased age-related neuropathology and increased resilience to decline, research aiming to identify potential advantages for multilinguals on a range of cognitive tasks has yielded much more mixed results. Proponents of a multilingual cognitive advantage suggest that greater cognitive reserve is responsible for benefits, accruing due to the lifelong demands of managing multiple language systems. These increased demands are thought to particularly affect language selection and conflict monitoring, which is argued to strengthen individuals’ ability to control attention, inhibit distractors, task shift, monitor stimuli and exercise working memory20–23. Over time, these functions are proposed to lead to more efficient controlled processing and therefore, improved attention, inhibitory control, and working memory performance.

Current data with bilingual-specific samples have provided some evidence for domain-specific advantages of speaking more than one language on cognitive functions including working memory, inhibitory control and attention24–29. Behavioural data from measures like the Simon task have shown that bilingual middle-aged and older adults are better at ignoring irrelevant stimuli, suggesting advantages in inhibition and attentional control25. Further evidence using categorical frameworks supports inhibitory advantages for bilingual and multilingual older adults on tasks involving perceptual or response conflict30,31. Research also indicates that multilinguals may have specific strengths in working memory and task shifting, with improvements often seen in spatial working memory, although some studies show that verbal working memory performance may be lower32,33. Meta-analyses by Adesope et al.24 and Grundy and Timmer34 similarly report moderate effects linking bilingualism to cognitive advantages, with larger effects for younger people than older people. Proponents of the multilingual cognitive advantage also report benefits for metalinguistic awareness35, phonetic perception36, and creative thinking37,38. However, these findings appear to contrast with more recent work (e.g., Vivas et al.39, van den Berg et al.40, which have highlighted greater cognitive benefits among older bilinguals. One possible explanation for these inconsistencies lies in a lifespan perspective: The cognitive effects of multilingual experience may accumulate gradually, becoming more pronounced with age as individuals engage in sustained language control and cross-linguistic monitoring over time. Within this framework, the protective or compensatory effects of multilingualism may only emerge when age-related cognitive changes begin to manifest. This cumulative view therefore underscores the importance of considering age as a moderating factor in the relationship between multilingualism and cognition.

However, a significant body of alternative research argues against cognitive benefits associated with multilingualism, with multiple studies showing null results for executive function, attention and inhibition (for a thorough review, see Paap et al.41. Large-scale investigations by de Bruin et al.42,43 and Leivada et al.44 have similarly failed to detect reliable cognitive differences once key demographic and experiential factors were controlled. Critics point to the high proportion of studies reporting null effects and to methodological issues like poorly standardised definitions of what constitutes bilingualism or multilingualism, publication bias, and inadequate sample matching on demographics45,46. Meta-analyses have also produced mixed outcomes: While Adesope et al.24 reported small-to-moderate advantages when aggregating across child and adult samples, Lehtonen et al.47 adopted stricter inclusion criteria focusing on adult participants and task-specific controls, ultimately finding that modest advantages on working memory and inhibition tasks largely disappeared after correcting for publication bias. Overall, both proponents and opponents of a multilingual advantage call for a shift toward more nuanced and dimensional approaches, to more reliably investigate the cognitive effects of multilingualism. Particularly, more nuanced research will help to identify the circumstances and tasks for which advantages of multilingualism might be evident. Future research should aim to clarify the specific contexts and cognitive domains in which multilingual cognitive effects might emerge, using reproducible designs that address current inconsistencies48.

Moving towards multidimensional measures of language experience

One reason for the mixed findings in the extant behavioural literature could be that not all multilinguals are equal. Instead, multilingualism may be better considered as a spectrum where people vary on the extent to which they are able to effectively communicate. Evidence using dichotomous comparisons has shown support for experience-dependent neural plasticity, whereby greater language experience lends itself to greater advantages49. From this, it could be inferred that greater quality experience contributes to these benefits. As a result, it is not only possible that advantages increase across the number of known languages, but that more specific facets of an individual’s language acquisition and usage profile may exert unique benefits.

Emerging data suggests that specific dimensions of language experience may better explain domain-specific effects, providing a conceptualisation of multilingualism as a multi-dimensional individual difference rather than a dichotomous, or “yes/no” categorisation. These dimensions, or individual differences in features of linguistic ability, include the number of languages, types of languages, proficiency, frequency of usage, age of acquisition, and mode of acquisition (sequential versus simultaneous)50,51. With the advancement of research on the effects of language experience, support has grown for more detailed insights into multilinguals’ language profile and how variations on different dimensions may underly any possible advantages of multilingual experience. By acknowledging the inherent heterogeneity of multilingual samples, researchers can better account for experience-related variance which contribute to inconclusive or highly variable findings within the literature27,42,52,53. Despite this, much of the existing literature on these dimensions of language experience uses designs which isolate singular factors such as proficiency (for review, see Linck et al.54. Such research designs limit understanding of language experience to one dimension rather than determining overall language experience by accounting for the varying influences of multiple dimensions.

Multilingual cognition and modality-dependent effects

Mixed findings in previous studies also suggest that the potential effects of multilingualism on cognitive function could be modality- or task-dependent: While multilinguals may perform better than monolinguals on visuospatial tasks, this pattern tends to be reversed on verbal and auditory tasks12,26,33. This means that mixed findings may result from heterogeneity in task selection across different studies. Known as the “weaker links” hypothesis, it has been theorised that modality-dependent performance differences (such as poorer verbal and auditory task performance) may occur due to multilinguals dividing frequency of use between each known language, as opposed to monolinguals, whose time is allocated to speaking a singular known language55–57. These findings, if robust, may suggest that while visuospatial advantages arise via a singular established processing pipeline that is strengthened for multilinguals, verbal and auditory stimuli are likely to be encoded dependent on the relevant language, and thus, these cognitive networks may be comparatively weaker for multilinguals. Differential effects across cognitive domains could also arise because visuospatial working memory draws more heavily on domain-general control mechanisms, such as those engaged during language switching, whereas auditory-verbal domains rely more on frequently exercised representations. Thus, multilingual experience may selectively influence visuospatial tasks through enhanced control processes. In bilingual samples, there is some evidence that these disadvantages on verbal tasks can be extinguished with discontinued second-language use58, suggesting an important interaction with frequency of language use. However, many monolingual-bilingual studies focus on language production performance (e.g. naming) in response to visual stimuli rather than comprehension as an outcome57,59. Cognitive disadvantages arising from competing lexical demands could be better understood by focusing on auditory comprehension across the spectrum of language experience. So far, studies have not examined performance on a full range of cognitive tasks matched across visuospatial and auditory modalities, within the same experiment. Including matched tasks across multiple cognitive domains would facilitate more direct comparison of visuospatial versus auditory performance to determine whether the “weaker links” effect remains consistent.

The current research

While multilingual experience has been linked to enhanced cognitive functioning and resilience against age-related decline at a population level, these effects may not translate into uniform cognitive performance benefits across modality, cognitive domain, as well as the lifespan. According to compensatory and cognitive reserve models, individuals may draw on lifelong experiences—such as managing multiple languages—to offset neural and cognitive ageing. Consequently, the benefits of multilingual language diversity could be more pronounced in older adults, where accumulated experience and adaptive neural mechanisms contribute to preserved performance. This perspective suggests that age could moderate the relationship between multilingual experience and cognitive outcomes, reflecting a cumulative or compensatory effect of lifelong language engagement.

To address mixed findings in the literature, the current study directly manipulated cognitive task and modality in a factorial design, repeating the same tasks on the same participants in a single experiment, using a lifespan sample of people with richly varied language experiences. We aimed to determine whether a lifetime of multilingual experience, when indexed continuously using the Language History Questionnaire (LHQ-3), was associated with superior performance on distinct cognitive domains (working memory, inhibition, and attention) across modalities (auditory and visuospatial) in a lifespan sample. Capturing these nuances in language experience and directly examining the effects of task and stimulus type within a single experiment could provide key insights into the mechanisms driving multilingual effects on brain ageing and help to resolve mixed findings across heterogenous previous research.

We administered a comprehensive continuous language experience measure (LHQ-3) and a series of six behavioural tasks indexing visuospatial and auditory working memory (2-back task), inhibitory control (Simon task), and attention (Attention Network task, Dichotic Listening task). Considering the existing body of literature, it was hypothesised that:

Individuals with higher Multilingual Language Diversity Scores (as measured by the LHQ-3) would exhibit greater accuracy on visuospatial cognitive tasks, with this multilingual advantage being more pronounced in older adults, reflecting a potential protective effect of multilingualism against age-related cognitive decline.

Method

Participants

Participants were 94 adults (range: 18–83 years, M = 43.2, SD = 21.8), who self-identified as 72 women, 21 men, and 1 nonbinary person. All participants resided in Australia, and a wide range of nationalities were reported. Fifty-two participants had Australian origin, 8 were from India, 5 from the United Kingdom, 4 from the Philippines, 3 from New Zealand, 2 from Pakistan, Lebanon and the United States, and 1 each from Cambodia, Canada, China, Fiji, Germany, Greece, Italy, Lithuania, Peru, Poland, Serbia, South Africa, Ukraine, Vietnam, and Zimbabwe respectively. Of those born in Australia, many retrospectively revealed foreign ancestry including Argentina, France, Iran, Indonesia, Japan, Korea, the Netherlands, Papua New Guinea, and Switzerland. Participants were from a diverse range of linguistic backgrounds (see Tables 1, 2, 3 and 4), with 24 monolingual, 38 bilingual, 20 trilingual and 12 quadrilingual individuals. Overall, there was a mean duration of L1 use of 40 years (SD = 23.26), mean duration of L2 use of 19 years (SD = 20.13), mean duration of L3 use of 13 years (SD = 15.34), and mean duration of L4 use of 5 years (SD = 5.27). Mean language proficiency was 0.86 for L1 (SD = 0.18), 0.61 for L2 (SD = 0.29), 0.47 for L3 (SD = 0.26), and 0.41 for L4 (SD = 0.27).

Measures

6-item cognitive impairment test (6CIT; Brooke & Bullock60)

To ensure a cognitively healthy participant cohort, the 6CIT screener was administered prior to participation to screen for existing cognitive impairment. The 6CIT is a 3–4-minute measure and uses an inverse score. Questions are weighted to produce a total out of 28. Normal scores range from 0 to 7, while 8 or more typically indicate the presence of mild to significant cognitive impairment. At the cut-off, the 6CIT demonstrates high sensitivity and specificity for both clinical and neuro-normative samples. Potential participants who scored above the cut-off (n = 2) were not included in the study due to possible cognitive impairment.

Language history questionnaire (LHQ-3.0; Li et al.61)

The LHQ is an assessment tool for measuring linguistic background and proficiency, and remains one of the most comprehensive and widely adopted tools for capturing key dimensions of multilingual experience. Data are collected on individuals’ country of origin, country of residence, educational history, educational background, speaking, listening, reading and writing ability in each language, as well as acquisition mode, frequency and context of use, age of acquisition and cultural background. Aggregated numerical scores were generated for language proficiency, language dominance, and language immersion levels. The LHQ calculates a Multilingual Language Diversity score (MLDS), a Shannon entropy-based metric which reflects both the number of languages reported and the relative balance of proficiency and use across different communicative contexts. Here, entropy refers to relative balance of languages across contexts. Detailed information regarding the formulae used for sub-score calculation is available at https://lhq-blclab.org/static/docs/aggregate-scores.html. The MLDS ranges from 0 to 2 and is calculated such that a monolingual would have a score of 0, while a perfectly balanced and highly proficient multilingual would have a score of 2. Scores between these parameters indicate varying degrees of multilingual experience, with higher scores representing more proficient, balanced language use. This approach captures not only the breadth of multilingual exposure but also the evenness of language engagement, consistent with formulations of language entropy. The LHQ demonstrates robust reliability and validity for use with multilingual samples, such that overall scores significantly correlate with objective measurements of language ability. The dimensions captured by the LHQ align closely with the variables of interest in our study and allow for comparability with previous research, which is essential for situating our findings within the broader literature. The measure has also been recently proposed by field leaders as an industry standard tool for evaluating language experience in similar protocols to the current study62.

N-back task (Kirchner63; Jaeggi et al.64)

To measure visuospatial and auditory working memory, participants completed two versions of the n-back task. Participants were presented with a sequence of stimuli and were asked to indicate using a keyboard press whether the current stimulus was congruent with the stimulus presented n trials prior. The load factor (n) can be adjusted to alter task difficulty, such that 2-n required participants to identify the stimulus presented 2 trials ago. A 2-n load factor was selected to align with the existing body of research (see Dong & Li65. Participants completed both visuospatial and auditory versions of the 2-back working memory task, which respectively prompted participants to remember letters presented visually or verbally (Fig. 1). Both task versions were comprised of 3 blocks of 50 trials each, separated by self-paced breaks to maintain participant engagement. Prior to the actual task, participants completed two blocks of practice to gain familiarity with the task, Consistent with similar behavioural research, stimulus presentation was set to 2000 milliseconds or until a response was given by the participant. The inter-stimulus interval was set to 1000 milliseconds to reduce carryover effects from the preceding trial.

Simon task (Simon & Rudell66)

To measure visuospatial and auditory inhibitory control, participants completed two versions of the Simon task. The Simon task is a choice-reaction task that involves presenting a stimulus in a left or right location, to which a left or right keypress response is to be made. The Simon task assesses the stimulus-response compatibility effect, where participants respond faster when stimulus features and stimulus location are congruent. For the visuospatial version of this task, the words ‘left’ and ‘right’ were visually presented in both congruent and incongruent locations on the screen. For the auditory version of this task, the words ‘left’ and ‘right’ were heard, with a corresponding audio icon presented in a congruent or incongruent location (Figs. 2 and 3). Both the visuospatial and auditory task included 3 blocks of 50 trials each, with self-paced breaks between each block. Prior to the actual task, participants completed two blocks of practice to gain familiarity with the task. Consistent with similar behavioural research, stimulus presentation was set to 2000 milliseconds or until a response was given by the participant. The inter-stimulus interval was set to 1000 milliseconds to reduce carryover effects from the preceding trial.

Attention network task (Fan et al.67)

To measure visuospatial attention, participants completed the Attention Network task, in which participants must identify whether a central arrow flanked by distractors is pointing left or right. The attention network task evaluates participants’ capacity on components of attention including alerting, orienting and shifting, as stimulus presentation is preceded by either no cue, a general cue, or spatial cue (Fig. 4). Here, alerting refers to achieving and maintaining readiness to respond, orienting involves directing attention toward a specific spatial location or stimulus, and shifting refers to exercising executive control to manage interference between competing stimuli or responses. Participants completed 3 blocks of 50 trials, including self-paced breaks. Prior to the actual task, participants completed two blocks of practice to gain familiarity with the task. Consistent with similar behavioural research, stimulus presentation was set to 2000 milliseconds or until a response was given by the participant. The inter-stimulus interval was set to 1000 milliseconds to reduce carryover effects from the preceding trial.

Vignette of the attention network task.

Dichotic listening task (Bryden68; Hugdahl69)

To measure auditory attention, participants completed the Dichotic Listening task, which instructs participants to listen for either a male or female voice while listening to both the target audio and a distractor. Participants must identify whether the digit (number) they say is higher or lower than 5. Participants are evaluated on their ability to alert, orient and shift towards the relevant auditory stimulus (Fig. 5). Participants completed 3 blocks of 50 trials, with scheduled breaks between self-paced blocks. Prior to the actual task, participants completed two blocks of practice to gain familiarity with the task. Consistent with similar behavioural research, stimulus presentation was set to 2000 milliseconds or until a response was given by the participant. The inter-stimulus interval was set to 1000 milliseconds to reduce carryover effects from the preceding trial.

Procedure

Participants signed up for the study online, underwent eligibility screening and, if eligible for the study, were sent the Qualtrics survey link containing participant information sheet and consent forms. Inclusion criteria were participants aged 18 years or over with English proficiency, normal scores on the 6CIT and normal or corrected hearing and vision. Testing was conducted in English using the experiment builder platform Gorilla via videoconference (Zoom) or in-laboratory at the MARCS Institute for Brain, Behaviour and Development, Western Sydney University. Regardless of whether participation was online or face-to-face, the experimenter remained present with the participant throughout, to support participation and answer and questions, and all tasks were completed in a single session. Tasks and the language survey were accessed via a standardised weblink provided to all participants on a lab or personal computer, irrespective of the testing location. The duration of the experiment was approximately 90 min per participant, with breaks available to provide adequate support and reduce any risks of fatigue. Sessions were scheduled for a maximum of two hours to accommodate both set-up and participation, and no participants exceeded this time limit. Breaks were self-paced and were placed between blocks as well as between tasks. The LHQ was administered in the initial stage of the experiment within 20–30 min, with each of the subsequent behavioural tasks being approximately 10–12 min in length. A brief audio check was conducted prior to the onset of auditory behavioural stimuli to ensure that participants had sufficient hearing ability to provide valid data, and auditory settings were calibrated as needed to ensure sufficient volume.

Task order was automatically randomised with a Latin Squares design to control for order effects using the in-built functionality of the Gorilla experimental software. This function automatically counterbalances task order across participants. The Latin Squares randomisation was not explicitly stratified by age; however, age was included as a covariate in all subsequent analyses to test for lifespan effects. For those undertaking the study via videoconference, participants were instructed to complete the study using their personal computer in a quiet room free from distraction. All participants were instructed to use headphones and adjust volume settings appropriately prior to commencing the experiment. Participants were visually monitored by the experimenter via either webcam (if participating online) or physical presence in the testing room (if participating face-to-face) throughout the testing duration to ensure consistency between those completing the study virtually and those participating in the lab. While not every device-specific factor could be controlled, these precautions were designed to minimise variability in stimulus presentation. Task instructions and design emphasised both speed and accuracy equally, and responses were not time-pressured or incentivised in a way that would encourage a compensatory trade-off strategy. Participants recruited as part of their undergraduate psychology requirements received course credit, while participants from the Age Lab Participant Registry were reimbursed with AUD $30 per hour of their time.

Data preprocessing and statistical approach

Data processing to omit missing cases and merge raw LHQ and behavioural data was performed using R version 4.4.2 and Python 3.12. All relevant scripts and data files are available on Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/27yjh/?view_only=67059559c6344882a8dd98f2c2331fde). Prior to conducting analyses, the merged dataset was examined for missing values, outliers, and any erroneous data entries. Independence of observations was checked using visual inspection. Missing data was omitted using listwise deletion, excluding cases with missing values to maintain consistency across paired variables. A nonparametric t-test (Mann-Whitney) was conducted prior to analyses to check for effects of testing location (virtual versus face-to-face). Results indicated that there was no significant effect of testing location (p >.05) on accuracy or reaction time for any task, and therefore analyses proceeded as planned.

We employed Generalised Additive Models (GAMMs) to examine the relationships between multilingual experience (MLDS scores), age, and cognitive task performance across 12 dependent variables: six auditory cognitive tasks (Dichotic Listening Accuracy and RT, Auditory Simon Task Accuracy and RT, Auditory N-Back Accuracy and RT) and six visuospatial cognitive tasks (Attention Network Task Accuracy and RT, Visuospatial Simon Task Accuracy and RT, Visuospatial N-Back Accuracy and RT).

GAMMs were chosen over traditional linear models due to their ability to capture non-linear relationships through smooth functions while maintaining interpretability61,70–72. This approach is particularly suitable for examining age-related changes in cognitive performance, which often exhibit non-linear trajectories across the lifespan. This was also considered appropriate for analysing multilingual experience data, which are also non-linear and typically do not follow a normal distribution72. GAMMs assume that the effects of predictors on the outcome are additive. Observations should be independent, and the response variable must follow a distribution from the exponential family (e.g., Gaussian, binomial, Poisson) with an appropriate link function. Residuals should be well-behaved, with constant variance for Gaussian models, and minimal multicollinearity among predictors. Smoothness of functions is typically selected via cross-validation to balance flexibility and avoid overfitting.

GAMMs were conducted on aggregated participant-level data, using mean accuracy and reaction time values per condition. Aggregation was chosen to ensure model stability given the relatively small number of trials per condition and to facilitate direct comparison across tasks with differing trial structures. The use of accuracy and reaction time further aligns with current recommendations for multilingualism and cognition research (see Pliatsikas et al.62. Therefore, reaction time and accuracy scores on the Attention Network task, Dichotic Listening task, Visuospatial Simon task, Auditory Simon task, Visuospatial N-back task and Auditory N-back task were the dependent variables, while the independent variables were participant age and Multilingual Language Diversity Scores. All tests were two-tailed with a 0.05 level of significance.

Results

Model structure

For each cognitive outcome, we fitted GAMMs structures to examine the individual and joint effects of MLDS and age while controlling for education as a parametric covariate. Interaction-focused models were also computed for the purpose of visualising any potential significant results.

To visualise and quantify the smooth interaction between MLDS and age, we fitted additional GAMMs with tensor product smooths: Cognitive Performance ~ s(MLDS, Age) + Education. These models employed thin plate regression splines to estimate smooth surfaces representing the joint effect of multilingual experience and age on cognitive performance. The tensor product smooth s(MLDS, Age) allows for flexible interaction patterns that may not be captured by simple multiplicative terms. We employed multiple visualisation approaches to characterise the complex relationships within the data: Two-dimensional plots of individual smooth functions for MLDS and age effects, three-dimensional surface plots of MLDS, Age interactions, two-dimensional contour plots of the three-dimensional interaction surfaces, and interactive 3D plots of the fitted surfaces. For each model, we evaluated smooth term significance using p-values from the GAMMs structure, parametric term significance for linear effects, model fit diagnostics including residual analysis and goodness-of-fit measures, and interaction significance for the s(MLDS, Age) tensor product smooth term.

All GAMMS models were fitted using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation method rather than alternatives such as generalised cross-validation (GCV). REML provides a more reliable and stable estimation of smoothing parameters, particularly when models include multiple smooth terms and covariates (Wood73). Unlike GCV, which can sometimes overfit the data, REML balances model fit and smoothness by estimating variance components jointly with the fixed effects, thereby improving generalisability and interpretability of the results. Two GAMMs (controlling for education) revealed significant nonlinear interaction effects between multilingual language diversity (MLDS) and age on performance in the visuospatial N-back task. For accuracy (VNBAC), the smooth interaction term s(MLDS, Age) was statistically significant, F(2.11) = 4.55, edf = 2.06, p =.014, indicating a moderately complex interaction surface. The overall model explained 28% of deviance (-REML = −39.75, adjusted R² = 0.23), suggesting that the joint influence of MLDS and age contributes meaningfully (if modestly) to predicting accuracy. Within the corresponding reaction time model (VNBRT), the MLDS × Age smooth was even more pronounced, with a highly significant effect, F(7.03) = 7.38, edf = 5.46, p <.001, and greater surface complexity, reflecting a more intricate interaction. Instead, MLDS and age interact in a highly nonlinear fashion, suggesting that multilingualism may offer modest, differential and selective effects on cognition throughout the lifespan.

The three-dimensional plots for these interactions appeared curved, and the contours for accuracy scores particularly further emphasise this trend: Increased MLDS appears to buffer against age-related slowing, especially after age 50.

Across the remaining models, for both modes of the inhibition and attention tasks as well as for the auditory working memory task, no significant main effects or interactions were found for MLDS scores on cognitive performance in either accuracy or reaction time tasks (all p >.05). In each of these comparisons, smooth terms for MLDS were non-significant (all p >.05), indicating that MLDS did not exhibit meaningful nonlinear relationships with performance. Similarly, linear MLDS terms in reduced models were not significant (all p >.05), suggesting limited evidence for a direct effect of multilingual experience on task outcomes. GAMMS modelling independent effects of Age while controlling for MLDS and Education similarly found limited support, with significant results only for visuospatial working memory accuracy (p =.032) and reaction time (p <.001).

Additionally, interaction terms between MLDS and Age—whether specified as linear (MLDS, Age) or as smooth terms (s(MLDS, Age))—were consistently non-significant across tasks (all p >.05). These null findings suggest that the influence of multilingualism, at least as operationalised by the MLDS index, does not uniformly extend to other domains of cognitive control or attention beyond visuospatial working memory. While age occasionally showed significant effects in isolation, MLDS did not emerge as a robust predictor in most models, underscoring the potential importance of task domain and cognitive demands in moderating multilingualism effects.

An unexpected pattern emerged regarding education effects, wherein individuals with doctoral-level education and those in the “Other” category (predominantly technical certifications and vocational qualifications) demonstrated more cautious response patterns across several cognitive tasks. Specifically, these groups exhibited significantly lower accuracy in the visuospatial n-back (VNBAC: Doctoral Level: β = −0.50, p <.001; Other: β = −0.20, p =.008) and auditory n-back tasks (ANBAC: Other: β = −0.09, p =.009), alongside significantly slower reaction times (ANBRT: Other: β = 1443.31, p <.001). This pattern could suggest poorer performance, or potentially a more conservative response strategy prioritising accuracy over speed; however, this caution did not translate into improved performance and instead resulted in both slower responses and reduced accuracy. This counterintuitive finding may reflect heightened awareness of task difficulty or increased performance anxiety in these groups, though the small sample sizes in these education categories (particularly for doctoral-level participants) necessitate cautious interpretation. Alternatively, the “other” category’s heterogeneity—encompassing diverse non-traditional educational backgrounds—may obscure meaningful group differences. These education effects were independent of the primary MLDS × Age interactions and appeared most pronounced in demanding working memory tasks rather than in simpler selective or divided attention paradigms.

Given the modest effects, simple robustness checks were conducted following analysis to ensure results were not artefacts of modelling (i.e., analysing correlations alongside GAMMs and rerunning models with log RTs). As initial analysis used Gaussian distributions with identity link functions, robustness checks using beta-logit (accuracy) and gamma-log (reaction time) distributions were also conducted, which yielded patterns consistent with the initial results. Correlation analyses revealed the same pattern of results (p <.05). Log-transformed RTs for the interaction between MLDS and Age revealed that visuospatial n-back effects remained robust to corrections, while reaction times for both the visuospatial (p =.02) and auditory Simon task (p =.002) became significant (Fig. 10). These effects, after modelling, suggested that older, more multilingual participants were slower to respond (Fig. 11). However, considering that auditory task performance was near chance, these results should be interpreted with caution. Given potential sensitivity limitations, we treat these findings as tentative and inconclusive. Further modelling also revealed that visuospatial n-back reaction time demonstrated a weak negative correlation with accuracy, indicating a potential speed-accuracy trade-off (r = -.29, p =.004).

Discussion

Principal findings

This study examined whether multilingual experience, calculated as a continuous score derived from the LHQ-3 Multilingual Language Diversity Score (MLDS), was differentially associated with cognitive performance across the adult lifespan. Specifically, we investigated whether greater language diversity was associated with enhanced accuracy and faster response times across visuospatial and auditory tasks of working memory, attention, and inhibition, and whether these effects varied by age. We hypothesised that individuals with higher MLDS scores would exhibit greater accuracy on visuospatial cognitive tasks, with this multilingual advantage being more pronounced in older adults, reflecting a potential protective effect of multilingualism against age-related cognitive decline (Ha). We also expected that higher Multilingual Language Diversity Scores would be associated with faster reaction times on visuospatial cognitive tasks across ageing (Hb), and that the cognitive performance advantages associated with multilingual experience would be more pronounced in visuospatial tasks compared to auditory tasks, which were expected to show disadvantages (Hc). While no widespread evidence was found for a global multilingual advantage, our results provided partial support for a more nuanced, age- and task-specific cognitive benefit associated with multilingualism.

The most notable finding was a significant interaction between MLDS and age in predicting both accuracy and reaction time on the visuospatial N-back task. Older people with greater levels of multilingual experience demonstrated better accuracy than those with less experience, suggesting that multilingualism may offer domain-specific cognitive resilience in later life. This is consistent with the cognitive reserve hypothesis, which posits that enriching experiences such as multilingual language use may help preserve cognitive function in the face of age-related decline. These findings suggest that visuospatial working memory may be particularly sensitive to the effects of multilingual experience, but that such advantages may only emerge in older age, when cognitive resources are more vulnerable to decline.

Contrary to expectations, no significant disadvantages of multilingual experience were found across auditory cognitive tasks, apart from reaction time on the auditory Simon task after log-transformation. However, performance was near-chance and thus this effect is interpreted with caution. This finding aligns with other research suggesting that benefits may not generalise across all sensory modalities or cognitive domains12,26,33. Additionally, younger adults did not demonstrate any performance benefits associated with higher MLDS, diverging from prior findings indicating that multilingualism may enhance working memory in early adulthood. These results instead suggest that the cognitive benefits of multilingualism may emerge as a compensatory mechanism, becoming more detectable only as age-related changes begin to affect cognitive function.

Age-related performance differences were consistent with prior literature, as visualised by the three-dimensional and surface plots: Older participants generally exhibited slower response times and lower accuracy, specifically on working memory tasks, reflecting typical age-associated decline in processing speed and executive function74,75. Our findings suggest that although longer reaction times and poorer accuracy with age are expected on cognitive domains such as working memory76, some of these effects could be mitigated through cognitively demanding experiences such as multilingual language use.

Taken together, these findings suggest that multilingual experience may not confer broad or universal cognitive advantages, but instead may be selectively protective for visuospatial working memory in older age. This limited but meaningful effect adds to a growing body of literature questioning the robustness of the bilingual cognitive advantage, and highlights the importance of considering age, task domain, and modality specificity in future research. The use of identical tasks across auditory and visuospatial modalities in the present study allowed for a more direct test of these moderating effects, addressing gaps in previous research that lacked such specificity and inter-measure comparability.

Implications for mechanisms driving multilingual brain advantages

Although neuroimaging studies indicate that brain regions linked to abilities such as working memory, inhibition, and attention are engaged during multilinguals’ language comprehension and production77–79, our data suggests that these effects do not directly translate to reliable patterns of cognitive advantages nor disadvantages on behavioural measures, regardless of task type or modality. Multilingual experience was not significantly associated with greater accuracy on any cognitive domain, apart from visuospatial working memory accuracy in older adults, thus providing only partial and limited support for the first hypothesis. Reaction time was also not related to multilingual experience apart from via an interaction with age on visuospatial working memory. However, this interaction showed that participants with greater language experience were slower to respond, indicating that the second hypothesis was not met. In addition, multilingual experience was associated with a performance advantage on only one visuospatial measure, but no auditory measures, thus providing partial support for the third study hypothesis. Previous meta-analysis (see Grundy & Timmer34 has shown that while working memory capacity is demonstrably greater for multilinguals, this effect is especially large in younger people. Our pattern of results and the accompanying visualisations indicated that for visuospatial working memory, older participants demonstrated a comparatively greater multilingual advantage on task accuracy.

The findings broadly contradict previous work supporting cognitive advantages and stimulus modality-dependent effects12,26,33 but align with alternative findings suggesting that the multilingual cognitive advantage could be spurious41,45,46. Despite this, much of the research on multilingual cognitive performance has lacked designs that test for both domain and modality specific effects—few studies have examined these effects concurrently, which may explain mixed effects (see Cockcroft et al.77; Espi-Sanchis & Cockcroft78. To address the shortcomings of prior literature, our study included identical cognitive tasks (working memory, attention, inhibition) administered as visuospatial and auditory versions, enabling direct comparison. This specificity and use of multiple comparisons was designed to identify any nuanced patterns of benefits, to identify for which specific tasks (if any) multilingual cognitive advantages may occur. Results of the current experiment showed that multilingual cognitive advantages exclusively occurred for visuospatial working memory and were more pronounced for older people. The observed multilingual advantage emerging only in the visuospatial domain indicates that task modality is a critical moderating factor and suggests that the cognitive consequences of multilingualism are not uniformly distributed across domains.

The selective behavioural effects observed in this study may help explain why epidemiological and neuroimaging evidence often reveals strong associations between multilingual experience and neural (but not always cognitive) outcomes10–14,18,19,77–79. It is possible that neuroprotective mechanisms, such as neural reserve or compensatory reorganisation, operate at a neural level without necessarily translating into broad behavioural advantages. The present findings contribute to understanding the behavioural–neural gap observed within multilingualism research, whereby robust epidemiological and neuroimaging evidence of protective or reserve-like effects does not always translate into behavioural enhancements. Consistent with cognitive reserve models, multilingual experience may provide compensatory support under conditions of age-related decline, rather than improving baseline cognitive performance across the lifespan. Future longitudinal and larger-scale studies will be essential to clarify whether and how multilingual experience buffers cognitive decline over time.

Similar considerations for specificity and multiple comparisons further informed the use of the LHQ-3 MLDS, which addressed the use of various language measures with varying specificity in previous literature. Traditional classifications of participants into “monolingual” and “bilingual” categories have been increasingly criticised for masking substantial variability in language use, proficiency, and exposure42. The use of the LHQ-3 enabled a more granular view of multilingual experience, allowing for nuanced modelling of its interaction with age along continuous, multidimensional scales. This study contributes to the ongoing epistemological shift in multilingualism research from categorical, group-based comparisons toward experience-based frameworks. This approach frames multilingualism as a complex, graded construct rather than a binary trait, highlighting the value of dimensional measures for understanding both cognitive and neural outcomes. The significant interaction between MLDS and age in predicting visuospatial working memory performance supports the utility of continuous measures in revealing effects that may be obscured in group-level analyses.

The present findings align with the more cautious interpretations emerging in the second wave of bilingualism research45–47. The absence of reliable significant effects of multilingual experience across auditory tasks and within the younger adults suggests that multilingual advantages, where they do occur, are not global and may be moderated by both task characteristics and participant variables. This interpretation is supported by recent critiques arguing that bilingual advantages are more likely to be observed under specific conditions—such as high task demands, ageing populations, or when more ecologically valid or domain-sensitive cognitive tasks are used41. Our results support this view: The only reliable multilingual advantage appeared in the context of visuospatial working memory performance in older adults, a cognitively demanding task likely to be sensitive to age-related decline. Overall, this study contributes to the ongoing paradigm shift toward multidimensional measures of language experience, emphasising the importance of capturing the diversity and complexity of multilingualism through continuous and experience-based metrics rather than binary group comparisons.

Importantly, although the visuospatial N-back used alphabetic stimuli, its core processing demands relied primarily on visuospatial encoding and maintenance rather than phonological or lexical activation. As such, visuospatial tasks may involve cognitive processes that are more engaged during multilingual language switching or monitoring, or that tap into control mechanisms that generalise more robustly to nonverbal contexts12,26,33. These data align with the weaker links hypothesis, which suggests that modality-specific performance differences arise due to multilinguals dividing frequency of use between languages55–57. This division disproportionately affects phonological and lexical representations, meaning that auditory versions of the task, where phonological decoding is required, are more likely to draw on comparatively weaker language-specific networks. In contrast, the visuospatial modality relies on a largely language-independent processing pipeline, reducing reliance on phonological coding and thereby mitigating weaker-links disadvantages. The lack of reliable effects observed in the auditory tasks may also reflect differential task demands that fail to recruit the executive processes hypothesised to benefit from multilingual experience.

Furthermore, the interaction showing comparatively stronger effects for older adults may contrast earlier work suggesting stronger bilingual advantages in youth24,34, but could also reflect a floor effect in cognitive decline in this group—if younger adults are already high-performing on cognitive tasks, multilingual experience may offer limited room for enhancement. Alternatively, any benefits conferred by multilingualism could accumulate over time or may become more apparent only as age-related decline sets in, offering compensatory advantages rather than baseline performance enhancements. This aligns with cognitive reserve theory, suggesting that individuals with multilingual experience may recruit alternative neural networks or different cognitive strategies than monolinguals, allowing them to maintain performance despite underlying age-related changes. This framework helps explain why cross-sectional behavioural measures sometimes fail to capture broad benefits, even when epidemiological and neuroimaging studies indicate neural or protective effects. Future longitudinal research is needed to dissect how these compensatory processes unfold over time and under varying conditions of cognitive challenge. Together, our findings suggest that cognitive effects associated with multilingual experience are selective and context-dependent, varying according to factors such as age, task demands, and modality, rather than reflecting a global advantage.

Limitations and future directions

While this study offers new insight into the potential age- and task-specific effects of multilingual experience, several limitations must be acknowledged. Given the cross-sectional design of this study, interpretations regarding lifespan or age-related changes should be made cautiously, as longitudinal data are needed to robustly establish developmental trajectories. Although the sample spanned a wide age range (18–83 years), the total sample size (N = 94) was relatively small for detecting subtle interaction effects between age and multilingualism. As such, null findings should be interpreted with caution, as limited statistical power may have reduced sensitivity to smaller effects.

While our implementation of the LHQ-3 helped resolve current issues in the field by more comprehensively indexing multilingual experience as a continuous variable rather than a dichotomous one, some issues remain48. Specifically, individuals with a greater number of languages but lower or unbalanced usage may show similar MLDS values to highly engaged bilinguals. This raises the broader question of whether the number of known languages or the quality and frequency of use is more strongly associated with potential cognitive outcomes. Given Australia’s multicultural environment, language exposure often involves high variability in proficiency and use across family, education, and social contexts. This variability may not be fully captured by entropy scores alone. Future research should aim to resolve these concerns through more advanced modelling and potential triangulation using multiple language experience measures.

In addition, although the behavioural measures selected are standardised and widely used within cognitive neuroscience and psychology, there is a mounting concern that traditional cognitive instruments have poor alignment with actual ability80. Thus, such measures may fail to capture facets of cognition which extend beyond laboratory testing environments to apply in day-to-day settings81. That is, differences in cognitive performance attributable to multilingual experience may exist but may not be easily captured by traditional laboratory paradigms. The development of more nuanced and more ecologically valid psychometric and behavioural measures could resolve these barriers to accurately profile cognition in day-to-day life, as recent studies suggest disparities between performance in the lab and performance in more naturalistic settings82.

Relatedly, metacognitive literature indicates that objective laboratory-based cognitive measures do not always demonstrate congruence with self-report evaluations of cognitive function83,84. It is likely that examining individuals’ judgements of their own cognitive function, as well as investigating fluctuations via ecological momentary assessment, may offer new insights into the complex and subtle ways that multilingual experience may impact cognition. Several studies to date have found that metamemory (judgement of one’s own memory capacity) mediates age effects on measures of associative, episodic, and working memory across the lifespan, providing some precedent for additional exploration85–87.

It is important to note that our sample predominantly reflects heritage language use within immigrant contexts in Australia, and findings should be interpreted with consideration of this specific sociolinguistic environment. Anecdotal evidence from the current study indicates that participants have strong beliefs about how specific cognitive abilities are either improved or impaired by their multilingual experience and specific heritage language. For example, one participant recounted that she felt her working memory was improved by her exposure to, and comprehension of, multiple languages, although “I understand the language better when reading or listening compared to speaking.” In contrast, another recalled that “the learning of languages is difficult for me … I can’t interpret accents easily and can’t recall foreign names.” While one participant stated, “I grew up hearing multiple languages in a single sentence” and that this had made them “more attentive”, another expressed that the same experience “created confusion”. Context-dependent language use was also indicated by participants, suggesting preferences for certain languages depending on cognitive operation or emotional state:

''I definitely think in Telugu, but I remember numbers and count using English."

"Afrikaans is the language of my heart and best used to express intense emotion. English is the language of business and used mostly in everyday life.''

Linguistics research provides some basis for speculation, with bilingual and multilingual students demonstrating better metalinguistic awareness than monolinguals regarding their language skills in reading and working memory88. Further research should examine this in greater detail to robustly map out potential effects and understand how changes in metacognition may be associated with language use as well as with long-term trajectories of cognitive ageing.

Conclusions

Overall, the current study found limited but meaningful support for a multilingual effect on cognition, specifically in the domain of visuospatial working memory among older adults. Multilingual language diversity was associated with better accuracy in this domain, while no advantages were observed in auditory tasks or in younger adults. These findings suggest that multilingualism may confer conditional age-specific, domain-selective benefits rather than global enhancements in cognitive performance.

The absence of widespread multilingual advantages in behavioural data underscores the importance of refining both the measurement of cognitive function and the theoretical models linking language experience to brain health. While behavioural performance may not always reveal benefits of multilingualism, cognitive resilience may still be conferred through underlying neural mechanisms. Future studies should continue to refine how cognitive function and language experience are measured, and consider potential moderators such as age, task complexity, and modality. These results should be interpreted within the sociolinguistic ecology of Australia, emphasising heritage language use in immigrant populations, which may influence the observed cognitive effects of multilingual experience.

Overall, this study highlights the value of incorporating both lifespan and task-modality perspectives in research on multilingualism and cognition and points to the need for more ecologically valid, multidimensional, and mechanistically grounded approaches in future work.
