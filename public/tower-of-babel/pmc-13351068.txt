Comparing artificial intelligence and physician performance in predicting IDH mutation status in glioma
Authors: Satoshi Takahashi, Masamichi Takahashi, Manabu Kinoshita, Mototaka Miyake, Risa Kawaguchi, Naoki Shinojima, Akitake Mukasa, Kuniaki Saito, Motoo Nagane, Ryohei Otani, Fumi Higuchi, Shota Tanaka, Nobuhiro Hata, Kaoru Tamura, Kensuke Tateishi, Ryo Nishikawa, Hideyuki Arita, Masahiro Nonaka, Takehiro Uda, Junya Fukai, Yoshiko Okita, Naohiro Tsuyuguchi, Yonehiro Kanemura, Fumiyasu Tsushima, Shingo Kakeda, Toshiaki Akashi, Toshiaki Taoka, Yoshiyuki Watanabe, Kei Yamada, Toshinori Hirai, Minako Azuma, Takashi Yoshiura, Jun Sese, Koichi Ichimura, Yoshitaka Narita, Ryuji Hamamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPredicting isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutations in gliomas using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is clinically important for treatment planning. This study compared two artificial intelligence (AI) models, GliomaDepth-IDH (ResNet34-based) and GliomaVista-IDH (Vision Transformer-based), with 18 physicians (eight neuroradiologists, five neurosurgeons, and five neurosurgery residents) in predicting IDH mutation status. On the Brain Tumor Segmentation Challenge dataset, the GliomaVista-IDH AI model achieved an area under the curve (AUC) value of 0.97, significantly outperforming all physician groups. However, external validation on a Japanese cohort revealed performance degradation: GliomaDepth-IDH declined to an AUC of 0.75 and GliomaVista-IDH to 0.82, with GliomaVista-IDH showing significant calibration issues (Brier score = 0.32). High-performing physicians achieved comparable results (AUC = 0.88) with superior calibration (Brier score = 0.19). Inter-rater reliability analysis revealed substantial variability across physician groups. These findings suggest that AI models can assist many physicians, while experienced practitioners remain competitive with better-calibrated predictions in challenging domains.Subject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience, Oncology

Introduction

Gliomas are one of the most common intracranial neoplasms of the central nervous system1. Their prognosis differs greatly depending on the subtypes, which are diagnosed based on histopathological and molecular features according to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria. Recently, the WHO criteria have been periodically updated, with increasing emphasis on molecular diagnosis2,3. Among the various molecular markers, the mutation status of isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) is widely regarded as the most clinically significant.

IDH mutations play a fundamental role in the biology and classification of glioma. As a truncal mutation in glioma, IDH mutation status profoundly affects patient prognosis4. Gliomas with IDH mutations demonstrate significantly better prognosis and enhanced response to chemotherapy than those with IDH-wild-type status, irrespective of the histological grade5,6. Glioblastoma (GBM), the only grade 4 glioma, has the poorest overall prognosis, and lower-grade gliomas with IDH wild-type status also exhibit equally poor outcomes7. Therefore, identifying IDH mutation status is crucial in clinical decision-making. Currently, the two standard detection methods are immunohistochemistry and deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) sequencing8,9. Although DNA sequencing more accurately detects the IDH mutation status, both methods require pathological specimens obtained through invasive surgical procedures. Despite these challenges, non-invasive methods of detecting IDH mutation status in gliomas represent a significant opportunity for three main reasons. First, surgical procedures, including biopsies, carry inherent risks, such as bleeding and infection10,11. Second, preoperative knowledge of IDH mutation status would substantially influence surgical decision-making. For instance, Beiko et al. evaluated whether resecting non-enhancing tumor regions on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with gadolinium T1 benefits patients and found its association with survival benefits in IDH1 mutant grade 3–4 gliomas but not in IDH1-wild-type high-grade gliomas12. Third, from a long-term perspective, non-invasive methods of detecting IDH mutation status could fundamentally transform the overall treatment strategies. IDH-mutant gliomas are more responsive to chemotherapy and radiotherapy than IDH-wild-type gliomas13,14, a hypothesis supported by in vitro experiments15,16.

Of the various potential non-invasive prediction methods of IDH mutation status, MRI-based approaches have emerged as the most promising owing to the ubiquity of MRI in clinical settings, since hospitals treating gliomas routinely perform these imaging studies. One established MRI-based prediction method for IDH mutation status focuses on T2-weighted (T2), fluid-attenuated inversion recovery mismatch17, which serves as a highly specific imaging biomarker for the IDH mutant, 1p19q non-deleted molecular subtype of glioma. However, this approach requires specific MRI protocols for optimal results18. Radiomics, the use of medical images as quantifiable data rather than merely visual representations, has recently gained significant momentum19. This approach demonstrates remarkable synergy with Artificial Intelligence (AI) technologies, enabling the development of numerous predictive systems with impressive diagnostic capabilities20,21. Furthermore, the integration of deep learning approaches has yielded particularly high prediction accuracy22–24. Notably, the clinical utility of AI-assisted diagnostic workflows has been substantiated by recent randomized controlled trials in other domains of radiology. For instance, AI assistance significantly improved diagnostic sensitivity for pulmonary nodule detection in real-world lung cancer screening settings and outperformed expert radiologists in detecting radiolucent foreign body aspiration on chest CT25,26. These successful implementations demonstrate the potential for human-AI collaboration in medical imaging; however, such integration remains unexplored for IDH mutation prediction in gliomas.

Despite the development of several impressive prediction models, MRI-based predictors of IDH mutation status are yet to be implemented in routine clinical practice27. The gap between research and application is attributed to the following two major factors. First, although a few recent studies have compared the performance of AI models with that of human experts for IDH mutation prediction28,29, these comparisons remain limited in scope. These studies typically involved only one or two radiologists, and the physician cohorts consisted exclusively of neuroradiologists without representation from other specialties involved in glioma management, such as neurosurgeons. For AI implementation to be justified, models must demonstrate clear advantages over a diverse range of physicians who would use them in clinical practice; therefore, a more comprehensive evaluation encompassing different clinical expertise levels and specialties is needed. Second, most proposed AI models have not been adequately validated against domain shift-induced performance degradation. Typically, these models achieve high prediction performance on data from the same domain as their training data. However, their performance usually decreases dramatically when applied to data from different domains30,31—a phenomenon known as the “domain shift problem.” In practice, the training and testing domains (that is, hospital data characteristics) are likely to differ significantly.

Therefore, to address these critical gaps, this study aimed to directly compare AI models to human physicians and explicitly address the domain shift problem.

Results

Dataset characteristics and study population

This study included 544 and 122 cases from the Japanese cohort (JC) and BraTS datasets, respectively. The BraTS dataset is a publicly available international dataset from the Brain Tumor Segmentation 2019 Challenge, whereas the JC dataset is a private collection of imaging data from 951 adult patients with gliomas treated at 10 facilities across Japan between November 1991 and October 2015. This fundamental difference in data sources creates an inherent domain-shift challenge because the BraTS dataset represents a standardized international collection with specific imaging protocols, whereas the JC dataset reflects real-world clinical practice variations across multiple Japanese institutions over an extended period, potentially introducing differences in imaging acquisition parameters, patient populations, and clinical practices.

Table 1 summarizes the key characteristics of the two datasets. Although no significant difference in sex distribution was found between the datasets (53.9% vs. 52.5% male, p = 0.657), patients in the JC dataset were significantly older than those in the BraTS dataset (median age, 60.0 vs. 53.0 years, p < 0.001). Additionally, the BraTS dataset exhibited significantly larger tumor volumes compared to the JC dataset (median volume, 107.26 cm³ vs. 70.02 cm³, p < 0.001), reflecting differences in disease stage or detection patterns between the cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 1). An important distinction between the datasets lies in their histopathological classification system; the JC and BraTS datasets used the WHO 2016 and 2007 criteria, respectively, creating classification discrepancies. The JC dataset contained a significantly higher proportion of GBM cases (59.9% vs. 46.7%, p = 0.007) and a lower percentage of IDH mutations (28.7% vs. 47.5%, p < 0.001) than the BraTS dataset. These demographic and molecular differences are critical for interpreting the performance variations observed in subsequent analyses.

Comparison of performance between AI models and human physicians

We systematically evaluated the performance of AI models in predicting IDH mutation status compared to that of the different physician groups. Figure 1 shows the overall experimental workflow, outlining our approach for comparing the AI model and physician performance in predicting IDH mutation status. Two distinct deep learning models were employed for this comparison: GliomaDepth-IDH, which is based on the ResNet34 architecture and has demonstrated robust performance in previous studies, and GliomaVista-IDH, which utilizes a Vision Transformer (ViT) architecture with attention-based mechanisms. Both models were trained on the same dataset but represented fundamentally different approaches to deep-learning-based image analysis, with the ViT-based model expected to achieve superior performance through its advanced attention mechanisms.

Experimental design for comparing AI models and physicians in IDH mutation status prediction.

The workflow of the study was organized into three phases using two independent datasets. Phase 1—Dataset Preparation: The BraTS dataset (122 cases) was divided into training (92%, 112 cases) and testing (10 cases) subsets. From the training subset, 10 cases were selected for physician training. The JC dataset (544 cases) was randomly split into training (“51%, 275 cases) and test (49%, 269 cases) pool subsets, from which 10 test cases were selected to match the WHO classification distribution of the BraTS test set. Phase 2—Training Phase: Two AI models (GliomaDepth-IDH and GliomaVista-IDH) were developed using 10-fold cross-validation of the BraTS training data. Additionally, both models were retrained on the JC training data (reference training, gray arrows) to evaluate the impact of domain-specific training on the model performance. Eighteen physicians (eight neuroradiologists with >15 years of experience, five neurosurgeons with >10 years of experience, and five neurosurgery residents with <5 years of experience) were trained using the 10 selected cases from the BraTS training data. Phase 3—Evaluation: All trained AI models and physicians predicted the IDH mutation status on both test sets (BraTS: 10 cases; JC: 10 cases), totaling 20 cases. Arrow type: Dashed lines indicate random data splitting, solid black arrows indicate primary training, grey arrows indicate reference training for domain shift analysis, and thin black arrows indicate IDH prediction.

The primary analysis involved models trained on the BraTS training dataset, which were then evaluated on both BraTS and JC testing subsets to assess the cross-domain generalization performance. To quantify the impact of the domain shift, we trained domain-matched versions of both models specifically on the JC training dataset for comparison with the cross-domain BraTS-trained models.

The physician participants comprised three distinct groups with varying levels of expertise: eight neuroradiologists, all recognized as experts in neuroradiology in Japan with >15 years of clinical experience and recruited from different major medical institutions; 10 neurosurgeons with diverse clinical experience levels, including five specialists with >10 years of experience, board certification in neurosurgery, and subspecialty expertise in neuro-oncology; and five neurosurgery residents with <5 years of experience. Before evaluation, all participants received standardized training using 10 randomly selected cases from the BraTS training dataset.

For statistical comparisons, we evaluated the performance of the AI model ensemble (derived from 10-fold cross-validation) against the aggregated predictions of each physician group. This approach ensured that group-level performance metrics were compared rather than individual models or rater predictions, thereby providing a fair comparison of the collective performance of AI systems and physician groups with different levels of expertise. All statistical tests were conducted at the group level to assess differences in diagnostic accuracy between the AI ensemble and each physician cohort.

Figure 2 depicts these comparisons using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Tables 3 and 4 present detailed performance metrics, including the area under the curve (AUC) value, accuracy, precision, recall, F1 scores, and Brier scores for calibration assessment. Statistical comparisons were performed using case-level stratified bootstrap analysis (5000 iterations), and the results are presented in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. Notably, our primary comparisons were conducted at the group level—comparing physician groups against the ensemble of 10-fold cross-validation results from the AI models. Because the DeLong test is designed to compare two single ROC curves derived from the same set of cases, it is not ideally suited for group-level comparisons where multiple individuals or model folds contribute to each group’s performance distribution. Therefore, we employed a stratified bootstrap analysis as the primary statistical approach. Nevertheless, the DeLong test results are provided as a reference in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4 for readers who may find pairwise ROC curve comparisons informative. Cross-fold validation ROC curves for both AI models and individual physician performance across both datasets are presented in Supplementary Figure 2.

Additionally, we performed decision curve analysis to assess the clinical utility of the AI models across a range of threshold probabilities (Supplementary Fig. 3). This analysis evaluated the net benefit of using each model compared to the default strategies of treating all patients or treating none, providing insights into the practical value of model predictions for clinical decision-making. Performance on BraTS dataset

When tested on the BraTS dataset, both AI models outperformed all physician groups in terms of IDH mutation prediction. GliomaDepth-IDH model achieved an AUC of 0.85 [95% confidence interval (CI), 0.78–0.91], accuracy of 0.79 [0.72–0.86], and F1 score of 0.84 [0.78–0.89]. GliomaVista-IDH model achieved an AUC of 0.97 [95% CI, 0.94–1.0], accuracy of 0.9 [0.84–0.96], and F1 score of 0.92 [0.87–0.97] (Fig. 2a; Table 3). Neuroradiologists achieved an AUC of 0.65 [95% CI, 0.56–0.74], neurosurgeons an AUC of 0.59 [0.39–0.79], and neurosurgery residents an AUC of 0.54 [0.33–0.76] (Fig. 2a; Table 3). GliomaVista-IDH significantly outperformed all physician groups (p = 0.002 for all comparisons), whereas GliomaDepth-IDH showed significant differences compared to neurosurgeons (p = 0.02) and neurosurgery residents (p = 0.002), although the difference from neuroradiologists did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.054) (Supplementary Table 1).

Calibration analysis revealed Brier scores of 0.09 [95% CI, 0.04–0.14] for GliomaVista-IDH and 0.18 [0.12–0.23] for GliomaDepth-IDH (Fig. 3; Table 3). Physician groups showed higher Brier scores: neuroradiologists (0.32 [0.23–0.41]), neurosurgeons (0.36 [0.22–0.51]), and neurosurgery residents (0.42 [0.34–0.51]).

Inter-rater reliability analysis using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) revealed the following results(Supplementary Table 5): neuroradiologists (ICC(2,1) = 0.535 [95% CI, 0.300–0.810], ICC(2,k) = 0.902 [0.780–0.970]), neurosurgeons (ICC(2,1) = 0.219 [ − 0.020–0.610], ICC(2,k) = 0.584 [ − 0.100–0.890]), and neurosurgery residents (ICC(2,1) = 0.063 [ − 0.110–0.440], ICC(2,k) = 0.250 [ − 1.030–0.800]).

Performance on external validation (JC dataset)

On the JC dataset, GliomaDepth-IDH achieved an AUC of 0.75 [95% CI, 0.68–0.82] and accuracy of 0.75 [0.68–0.82] (Fig. 2b; Table 4). With Youden index optimization, accuracy improved to 0.80 [95% CI, 0.74–0.86] and F1 score to 0.74 [0.69–0.80] (Table 4). GliomaVista-IDH achieved an AUC of 0.82 [95% CI, 0.77–0.87], with default accuracy of 0.64 [0.59–0.69] and recall of 0.92 [0.84–1.0] (Table 4). After Youden-correction, GliomaVista-IDH achieved accuracy of 0.83 [95% CI, 0.80–0.86], precision of 0.82 [0.72–0.93], and F1 score of 0.79 [0.75–0.83] (Table 4).

Calibration analysis showed Brier scores of 0.21 [95% CI, 0.18–0.25] for GliomaDepth-IDH and 0.32 [0.27–0.36] for GliomaVista-IDH (Fig. 3; Table 4). Among physicians, neuroradiologists achieved a Brier score of 0.25 [0.18–0.32], neurosurgeons 0.35 [0.20–0.50], and neurosurgery residents 0.34 [0.24–0.44].

Neuroradiologists achieved an AUC of 0.75 [95% CI, 0.65–0.85], neurosurgeons 0.60 [0.41–0.79], and neurosurgery residents 0.55 [0.28–0.83] (Table 4). The differences between the AUCs of GliomaDepth-IDH and neuro radiologist (p = 0.929) (p = 0.195) and residents (p = 0.137) were not statistically significant (Supplementary Table 2). Across all 360 physician-case evaluations, the “I have no idea” option was selected once (0.28%), by one neuroradiologist for JC_test_7.

Inter-rater reliability on the JC dataset showed: neuroradiologists (ICC(2,1) = 0.676 [95% CI, 0.450–0.880], ICC(2,k) = 0.943 [0.870–0.980]), neurosurgeons (ICC(2,1) = 0.345 [0.090–0.700], ICC(2,k) = 0.725 [0.330–0.920]), and neurosurgery residents (ICC(2,1) = 0.092 [ − 0.060–0.430], ICC(2,k) = 0.335 [ − 0.400–0.790]) (Supplementary Table 6).

Impact of domain-specific training

To assess the impact of domain-specific training, we evaluated the JC-trained models on both the BraTS (Supplementary Table 7) and JC test datasets (Supplementary Table 8). When applied to the BraTS dataset, the JC-trained models showed a reduced overall performance despite the relatively preserved AUC values, demonstrating bidirectional domain shift effects (Supplementary Table 7). In contrast, the JC-trained models showed improved performance relative to the BraTS-trained versions when tested on the JC dataset (Supplementary Table 8). GliomaDepth-IDH achieved an AUC of 0.78 [95% CI, 0.73–0.82], compared to 0.75 [0.68–0.82] for the BraTS-trained version (Supplementary Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 4a). GliomaVista-IDH achieved an AUC of 0.82 [95% CI, 0.79–0.86], compared to 0.82 [0.77–0.87] for the BraTS-trained version (Supplementary Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 4b). For JC-trained GliomaVista-IDH, the accuracy was 0.73, precision 0.63, F1 score 0.70, and Brier score 0.24, compared with 0.64, 0.53, 0.67, and 0.32, respectively, for the BraTS-trained version (Supplementary Table 8; Table 4).

Impact of combined dataset training

To evaluate whether mixed-domain training improves model generalization, we trained both models on the combined BraTS and JC training datasets and evaluated their performance on each dataset separately (Supplementary Tables 9–10; Supplementary Fig. 5). On the BraTS test dataset, GliomaDepth-IDH achieved an AUC of 0.92 (95% CI, 0.88–0.97), representing an improvement over the BraTS-only trained model (AUC, 0.85). GliomaVista-IDH achieved an AUC of 0.91 (95% CI, 0.86–0.96), which was slightly lower than that of the BraTS-only-trained model (AUC, 0.97), suggesting a modest trade-off in within-domain performance as a result of combined training. On the JC test dataset, GliomaDepth-IDH achieved an AUC of 0.78 (95% CI, 0.72–0.84), and GliomaVista-IDH achieved an AUC of 0.83 (95% CI, 0.77–0.89), comparable to or exceeding the performance of domain-specific JC-trained models (AUCs, 0.78 and 0.82, respectively). These results demonstrate that combined dataset training yielded the most robust performance across both domains, maintaining discriminative ability on the BraTS dataset while improving generalization to the JC cohort.

Analysis of high-performing physicians

A subgroup of high performers—two neuroradiologists and one neurosurgeon who achieved AUCs above 0.85 on the JC dataset—was identified (Fig. 4; Table 4). This group achieved an AUC of 0.88 [95% CI, 0.82–0.95] and F1 score of 0.78 [0.59–0.97] on the JC dataset, compared to that of the GliomaDepth-IDH (AUC = 0.75 [0.68–0.82]) and GliomaVista-IDH (AUC = 0.82 [0.77–0.87]) (Fig. 4b). The high performers achieved a Brier score of 0.19 [95% CI, 0.06–0.32], compared to that of the GliomaDepth-IDH (0.21 [0.18–0.25]) and GliomaVista-IDH (0.32 [0.28–0.36]) (Fig. 3; Table 4).

Misclassification patterns across datasets

To understand how domain-specific characteristics influence diagnostic errors, we analyzed the misclassification patterns across both datasets (Table 5; Figs. 5–6). False negatives (FNs) exceeded false positives (FPs) across all evaluator groups for the BraTS dataset (Table 5a; Fig. 5), indicating a consistent tendency to classify IDH-mutant tumors as wild type. BraTS_test_4 was frequently misclassified as an IDH mutant by nearly all groups, despite being wild type. On the other hand, BraTS_test_5, an IDH mutant case, was consistently misidentified as wild type by most evaluators. Conversely, the FPs outnumbered the FNs for all physician groups (Table 5b; Fig. 6), revealing a stronger tendency to diagnose tumors as IDH-mutant. This pattern was particularly pronounced in the GliomaVista-IDH model, which showed 33 FPs and 3 FNs. The GliomaDepth-IDH model exhibited a more balanced error distribution with 12 FPs and 13 FNs. Among the specific cases, JC_test_3, 5, 6, and 7 were repeatedly misclassified as IDH-mutant by high performers who recorded zero FNs overall.

Discussion

In this study, we compared the performance of AI models with different physician groups (neuroradiologists, neurosurgeons, neurosurgery residents, and high performers) in predicting the IDH mutation status in gliomas.

Our results on the BraTS dataset demonstrate that machine learning models can exceed human expert performance in the radiological assessment of IDH mutation status when trained and tested on data from the same domain. The ViT-based glioma Vista-IDH model achieved near-perfect classification (AUC = 0.97) with superior probability calibration (Brier score = 0.09). Calibration analysis revealed distinct prediction patterns. Physician predictions tended to cluster around moderate probability levels, whereas AI models, particularly GliomaVista-IDH, produced more decisive predictions closer to probability extremes. Interrater reliability analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity across physician groups, with neuroradiologists demonstrating moderate single-measure reliability and neurosurgery residents exhibiting minimal interrater consistency.

The external validation of the JC dataset revealed critical insights into the cross-domain performance and practical limitations of AI deployment. The performance degradation in both AI models demonstrates the impact of the domain shift on model generalization. Several dataset-specific factors likely contributed to this degradation, including significantly smaller tumor volumes in the JC dataset (median: 70.02 cm³ vs. 107.26 cm³, p < 0.001) and heterogeneous slice thicknesses in original JC acquisitions despite subsequent resampling. GliomaVista-IDH significantly over-predicted the IDH-mutant status at default thresholds, achieving high recall (0.92) but poor accuracy (0.64). The Youden-corrected thresholds substantially improved the performance metrics, but such post-hoc calibration is not feasible for real-world clinical applications where the ground truth is unavailable. GliomaDepth-IDH maintained a better default calibration despite its lower discriminative ability, and neuroradiologists achieved comparable calibration, suggesting the theoretical potential for a perfectly calibrated GliomaVista-IDH to outperform neuroradiologists, although achieving such calibration in practice remains challenging.

The domain-specific training results provide important insights into strategies for mitigating the domain-shift effects. While GliomaDepth-IDH showed only modest AUC improvement with JC-specific training (0.75 to 0.78), GliomaVista-IDH demonstrated that equivalent discriminative capability can mask substantial differences in calibration and clinical utility. Despite identical AUC values (0.82), the JC-trained GliomaVista-IDH showed markedly improved accuracy (0.64 to 0.73), precision (0.53 to 0.63), and Brier score (0.32 to 0.24). These improvements indicate that domain-specific training effectively addresses the overprediction tendency observed in the BraTS-trained model, resulting in better calibrated predictions that are more suitable for clinical deployment. This finding underscores that the AUC alone is insufficient for evaluating clinical readiness; a comprehensive assessment of calibration and precision-recall balance is essential.

Furthermore, our combined dataset training experiments provided additional insights into potential strategies for optimizing cross-domain generalization. Models trained on the combined BraTS and JC datasets demonstrated more robust performance across both domains, with GliomaDepth-IDH achieving an AUC of 0.92 on BraTS and 0.78 on JC, and GliomaVista-IDH achieving AUCs of 0.91 and 0.83, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 5; Supplementary Tables 9–10), suggesting that exposure to diverse training data may improve generalizability without substantial sacrifice of within-domain performance. However, even with combined training, performance on the JC dataset remained modest, possibly reflecting the challenges posed by the inherent heterogeneity of multicenter data with variable imaging protocols and acquisition equipment. These findings suggest that standardizing imaging protocols and equipment across institutions may represent a promising long-term strategy for improving AI diagnostic performance; however, further investigations using larger and more diverse datasets are required to confirm this hypothesis.

An important finding of our study was the impact of domain shift on both AI models and human physicians (Tables 3–4; Figs. 1–2). A domain shift occurs when a model or expert trained in one context (domain) is required to perform in a different context with varying characteristics such as imaging protocols, tumor distributions, or patient demographics32,33. Although this phenomenon is widely recognized in AI, our results suggest that human physicians experience similar performance challenges when faced with unfamiliar data domains. All physicians participating in our study practiced in Japan and were more accustomed to imaging characteristics such as those in the JC dataset. Although they received training on a small subset (10 cases) from the BraTS dataset, this limited exposure was likely insufficient to familiarize them with the substantial differences in BraTS data. Consequently, their performance in the BraTS test cases appeared to be affected by domain-related challenges, such as those affecting the AI models. Performance degradation was particularly evident among neurosurgeons and residents, whose AUC scores dropped significantly when evaluating BraTS data compared with JC data. Even neuroradiologists, despite their specialized training in image interpretation, showed variations in performance between datasets, indicating that although human expertise is robust in many aspects, it is not immune to domain-specific variations. Physicians are effectively “trained” by their clinical environment and patient population and may require adaptation when confronting significantly different imaging characteristics or disease presentations. Interestingly, our high-performer group, comprising two neuroradiologists and one neurosurgeon, demonstrated exceptional ability to overcome domain-shift challenges. Their superior performance across both datasets suggested that they possessed adaptable diagnostic strategies that transcended domain-specific limitations. Understanding these adaptable strategies can provide valuable insights into medical education and the development of AI models. This observation is consistent with recent meta-analyses comparing generative AI with physicians, which report that while AI models performed comparably to non-expert physicians, expert physicians significantly outperformed AI systems. These findings collectively suggest that experienced practitioners possess diagnostic capabilities that current AI models cannot fully replicate34.

Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies on human performance in IDH mutation prediction. Previous studies have reported radiologists’ AUCs ranging from 0.743 to 0.864, and our neuroradiologists’ performance on external validation (AUC = 0.75) fell within this range28,29. The high-performance subgroup (AUC = 0.88) achieved a performance at the upper end of previously reported human capabilities, suggesting that exceptional diagnostic expertise exists among certain practitioners regardless of the study setting.

Despite these findings, this study had some limitations. First, our datasets were relatively small (122 and 544 cases from BraTS and JC, respectively), with testing subsets of 10 cases each. This limited test set size may have reduced the statistical power to detect differences between groups, particularly when comparing performance across physician specialties or between models and physicians. This constraint reflects the practical challenge of coordinating evaluations among 18 physicians with demanding clinical responsibilities. We prioritized the breadth of physician representation across specialties over the depth of individual case evaluations to enable meaningful group-level comparisons. To mitigate this limitation, we employed a stratified bootstrap analysis and comprehensive multimetric evaluation to ensure robust statistical inferences within the constraints of the available sample size. Nevertheless, the limited test set size restricted the generalizability of our findings and underscores the need for larger multicenter cohorts in future studies. Second, while the JC dataset was preprocessed to match the spatial resolution of the training data through resampling to 1 mm³ isotropic voxels, the original images were acquired with heterogeneous slice thicknesses across different institutions. This inherent variability in the original acquisition parameters may have introduced subtle image quality differences, contributing to the observed performance degradation in the JC dataset. The resampling process, which is necessary for standardization, cannot fully compensate for information loss or artifacts introduced by nonisotropic acquisition, potentially creating a more challenging domain for model generalization. Third, the sample size and diversity of physicians were limited. All participating physicians practiced in Japan, potentially introducing a geographical bias. Therefore, expanding this study to include physicians from diverse global regions would provide more robust insight into how domain familiarity affects diagnostic performance across different healthcare settings with varying imaging protocols and clinical routines. Fourth, although the AI models used were state-of-the-art at the beginning of this study, they may not represent the most advanced algorithms currently available. Newer deep learning architectures may provide improved accuracy and robustness, particularly for heterogeneous datasets. However, surpassing the accuracy of the top performers on familiar datasets remains challenging, even with advanced techniques, owing to their extensive clinical expertise. Fifth, our study primarily focused on imaging data, whereas physicians typically integrate multiple information sources, including clinical history, laboratory tests, and demographic factors, to reach diagnostic conclusions. A more comprehensive comparison would incorporate these additional data points, providing a more realistic assessment for clinical decision-making. Therefore, future studies should explore how integrated clinical and molecular data influence the diagnostic performance of both human experts and AI models. Finally, we did not investigate whether a collaborative “human + AI” approach might enhance the diagnostic accuracy beyond what each approach achieves individually. This represents a critical direction for future research as the synergy between human expertise and computational analysis can overcome the limitations of each approach.

Returning to our central question of whether it is worthwhile to introduce AI models to predict the IDH mutation status in gliomas using MRI, our findings provide sophisticated answers with important clinical implications. Before addressing these implications, it is important to contextualize why non-invasive IDH prediction methods hold clinical significance. Although histopathological and genetic testing remain the current gold standards for determination of IDH mutation, these methods require invasive surgical procedures that impose a substantial burden on patients. These invasive approaches remain the gold standard, not because they are ideal but because no sufficiently reliable non-invasive alternative currently exists. Implementation of a reliable non-invasive IDH prediction method in clinical practice has the potential to fundamentally transform the treatment paradigm for patients with glioma, enabling preoperative molecular stratification that informs surgical planning, guides treatment selection, and potentially identifies candidates for conservative management strategies rather than for immediate intervention. Based on our results, the AI model outperformed most physicians, except for a small group of high-performing physicians. For most physicians, particularly neurosurgeons and residents, adopting such an AI system provides a clear value for enhancing diagnostic accuracy. In addition, the AI model demonstrated advantages in cases where traditional radiological features were ambiguous or misleading. Even for high-performing physicians, an AI system can provide valuable decision support when encountering cases outside their familiar domain. As demonstrated by our analysis of domain-shift effects, both human experts and AI models face challenges when moving between different clinical contexts. Therefore, a collaborative approach may leverage the complementary strengths of the broad pattern recognition capabilities of AI models and the contextual reasoning of experienced physicians. Notably, the AI model represents one of the many tools used to aid diagnosis rather than a replacement for clinical judgment. In practice, physicians integrate various clinical details under uncertain conditions, considering each patient’s unique circumstances. This comprehensive context-driven approach fundamentally differs from the probability-based output of an AI model. A particularly insightful finding emerged from JC_test_7, in which one neuroradiologist responded, “I have no idea,” instead of making a definitive prediction. This case featured a small lesion with ambiguous imaging characteristics that made diagnosis based on the available MRI sequences challenging. Therefore, recognizing the limitations of the available data and consciously withholding judgment represents a sophisticated level of clinical wisdom, acknowledging uncertainty rather than forcing a potentially incorrect classification. This contrasts with both AI models and other physicians who provided definitive predictions for this case despite its ambiguity. Current AI models typically produce a probability score regardless of the input quality or diagnostic certainty and lack the ability to identify cases in which additional information is necessary for a reliable classification. Therefore, developing AI systems that can recognize “uncertainty thresholds” and request additional data (including spectroscopy, perfusion imaging, and clinical information) before rendering a diagnosis represents the next frontier of medical AI development.

Building on the concept of uncertainty-aware AI systems, we propose that practical clinical implementations should incorporate two complementary strategies: human-in-the-loop frameworks and continuous domain-specific learning. Human-in-the-loop integration would not only allow AI systems to defer to physician judgment in uncertain cases—as exemplified by the neuroradiologist’s appropriate response of “I have no idea” for JC_test_7—but also enable clinicians to provide real-time feedback that could help the system calibrate its uncertainty thresholds over time. Such bidirectional interaction would leverage the complementary strengths identified in our study: the broad pattern recognition capabilities of AI models for straightforward cases and the contextual reasoning of experienced physicians for ambiguous presentations requiring the integration of additional clinical information. Indeed, recent systematic reviews and randomized controlled trials have substantiated the clinical utility of such collaborative workflows. A meta-analysis of AI-assisted skin cancer diagnosis demonstrated improved sensitivity (74.8% to 81.1%) and specificity (81.5% to 86.1%), with the largest benefits observed among non-dermatologists, which aligns with our finding that less exp experienced physicians may benefit most from AI assistance in IDH mutation prediction25,35.

Our findings underscore the critical importance of such adaptive mechanisms. GliomaVista-IDH, despite achieving the highest AUC (0.82) for external validation, exhibited an accuracy of only 0.64 without post-hoc threshold calibration, rendering it impractical for clinical use in its default configuration. Furthermore, implementing continuous learning mechanisms that allow models to adapt to institution-specific imaging characteristics and patient populations may help mitigate the domain-shift effects observed in our external validation. By periodically incorporating locally validated cases with physician confirmed outcomes, such adaptive systems can refine their decision boundaries and improve the calibration for each clinical environment, potentially reducing the systematic overconfidence demonstrated by the GliomaVista-IDH on the JC dataset. However, the continuous learning of machine learning models in clinical settings faces significant regulatory hurdles. Current regulatory frameworks in most jurisdictions require the re-certification of AI medical devices when their algorithms are substantially modified, creating tension between the need for adaptive learning and the requirements for regulatory compliance. Addressing these regulatory challenges while ensuring patient safety is essential for realizing the full potential of continuous-learning and uncertainty-aware medical AI systems.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that AI models for IDH mutation prediction offer conditional rather than universal clinical value and that their utility is strongly dependent on the clinical context and end-user expertise. Within familiar domains, advanced AI architectures can achieve performance comparable to or exceeding that of most physicians, with the notable exception of high-performing specialists who demonstrate superior diagnostic capabilities that current AI systems cannot fully replicate. However, domain shifts substantially degrade AI performance, with challenges extending beyond discriminative ability to include critical calibration failures that limit practical deployment without post hoc adjustments. Importantly, physicians are not immune to domain-shift effects, suggesting that both human experts and AI models require domain-specific adaptation for optimal performance. These nuanced findings indicate that AI models should be introduced into clinical practice as collaborative decision-support tools rather than autonomous diagnostic systems, with their primary value in assisting less experienced practitioners, standardizing assessments, and providing complementary perspectives in ambiguous cases. One promising direction is the development of uncertainty-aware AI systems that explicitly present predictive uncertainty alongside their outputs, as such information could potentially serve as a valuable input for physicians performing contextual clinical judgments that integrate patient-specific backgrounds and clinical circumstances, a domain in which human expertise remains indispensable. We believe that fostering a tripartite collaboration among patients, physicians, and AI systems, each contributing its unique strengths, holds the greatest promise for advancing patient-centered care in neuro-oncology.

Methods

Ethics approval and study design

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the National Cancer Center, Tokyo, Japan (approval ID: 2016-496), and from the ethics committees of all participating institutions that provided the images (Institutional Review Board No. 2013-042). All procedures were carried out in accordance with the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects. Written informed consent for genetic analysis was obtained from all participants, whereas image analysis was conducted on an opt-out basis with the approval of the institutional review boards.

Datasets

This study used the Brain Tumor Segmentation (BraTS) 2019 Challenge dataset, which is publicly available, and a JC dataset, which is a private collection. For inclusion in our analysis, cases from both datasets met the following criteria: (1) availability of all four MRI sequence types: T1-weighted images (T1), T2-weighted images (T2), fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted images (GdT1); (2) the presence of defined Volume of Interest (VOI) demarcating the tumor region; (3) confirmation of diagnosis through surgical resection or biopsy with pathological examination; and (4) documentation of isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutation and 1p/19q co-deletion statuses. All MRI data underwent identical preprocessing procedures to ensure comparability.

The BraTS dataset is publicly available from the BraTS Challenge36–40. For our study, we selected BraTS cases that could be associated with The Cancer Genome Atlas to obtain the corresponding IDH mutation and 1p/19q co-deletion status information. Further details regarding the BraTS dataset structure and acquisition parameters have been described in the literature37,41.

The JC dataset comprises imaging data from 951 adult patients with glioma treated at 10 facilities across Japan between November 1991 and October 2015. Of these, 544 met the four inclusion criteria described above. The IDH mutation status in this dataset was assessed by pyrosequencing, targeting hotspot mutations in IDH1 (R132) and IDH2 (R172). The 1p/19q co-deletion status was determined using one of the following three methods: multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification, microarray-based comparative genomic hybridization, or microsatellite analysis. All tumor VOIs in the JC dataset, including edema regions, were manually delineated by experienced neuroradiologists using specialized in-house tools, as described in our previous studies30,42.

Dataset partitioning

The datasets were strategically classified to ensure robust training and evaluation. From the BraTS dataset, we randomly selected 10 cases to form a testing subset (BraTS-Random-Testing-Subset-10) that was exclusively reserved for evaluating the performance of both the physicians and AI models. The remaining BraTS cases constituted the training domain of the study. From this training portion, we randomly selected 10 cases (BraTS-Random-Training-Subset-10) for physician training.

Similarly, we split the JC dataset into training and testing domains in a 1:1 ratio using random selection. All randomization procedures were conducted using the sample method from the Python Pandas package to ensure unbiased selection. The JC test dataset initially contained 269 cases that were deemed to be largely extensive for physician evaluation. Therefore, a secondary selection process was performed. To maintain consistency in the case distribution, we selected cases from the JC testing dataset to match the distribution of glioblastomas, diffuse gliomas, and oligodendrogliomas found in the BraTS testing dataset. Through this process, we randomly selected 10 cases to form the JC-Random-Testing-Subset-10 using the same randomization approach employed for the BraTS dataset.

Participant recruitment and training

10 neurosurgeons with diverse levels of clinical experience participated in our study. Among these, five were specialists with >10 years of experience, board certification in neurosurgery, and subspecialty expertise in neuro-oncology, while the remaining five were neurosurgery residents with <5 years of experience. All participants underwent training using 10 randomly selected cases from the BraTS training dataset. This training was facilitated by our custom-developed tool, which is available in our GitHub repository.

Eight neuroradiologists participated in this study, all of whom are recognized as experts in neuroradiology in Japan with >15 years of clinical experience. Each specialist was recruited from a different medical institution, enabling the incorporation of diverse clinical expertise from major medical centers across the country. Similar to the neurosurgeons, these specialists also received training on 10 randomly selected cases from the BraTS training dataset using the same in-house tool.

During the evaluation phase, all physicians had access to the complete volumetric MRI data for each case via our in-house viewing tool, allowing them to scroll through all available axial slices across all MRI sequences (T1, T1CE, T2, and FLAIR). This comprehensive access to full imaging data reflects real-world clinical practice, in which physicians routinely review complete imaging studies rather than isolated slices.

Image preparation and processing

All MRI modalities (T1, T2, FLAIR, and contrast-enhanced T1) from the JC dataset were subjected to a standardized preprocessing pipeline to account for variability in slice thickness and spatial resolution across the JC patient cohort acquired from multiple institutions. The original voxel spacing characteristics of the JC dataset before preprocessing are provided in Supplementary Table 11. First, each volume was resampled to a 1.0 mm isotropic resolution (1 × 1 × 1 mm) using cubic B-spline interpolation via the VTK library. This step ensured spatial consistency across all subjects, regardless of the original acquisition parameters.

Subsequently, brain extraction (skull stripping) was performed on the resampled T1-weighted images using the Brain Extraction Tool (BET) in the FSL software package. To ensure anatomical alignment across modalities, the brain mask derived from the T1 sequence was applied to the corresponding T2 and FLAIR sequences for the same subject.

For image denoising, the high-intensity image values, which were in the top 0.1% of each top value, were replaced. This denoising method was applied to T1, FLAIR, and GdT1, but not to T2. This is because the top high-intensity values of T2 represent the expression of fluid, specifically the cerebrospinal fluid. The image values of all MRI scans were scaled from 0 to 1. All MRI volumes were cropped and resized to 176 (W) × 192 (H) × 160 (D).

Before using testing or training for the AI, all MRI was normalized in each volume (e.g., case 1 T2 image or case 99 GdT1, etc.). Next, based on VOI, we selected the axial slice with the largest tumor area including edema. Then, two axial slices—one slice up and one slice down, centered on the largest tumor slice—were selected. For GliomaDepth-IDH, 3 axial slices per MRI volume, resulting in 12 axial slices per case, were selected. The selected images were used as a 176 (W) × 192 (H) × 12 (D) array. On the other hands, in case of GliomaVista-IDH, all images were 0 padded to a size of 224 (W) × 224 (H). Finally, each selected slice was imputed as 4 (image types, D) × 244 (W) × 244 (H) arrays, with each dimension representing the MRI image type (FLAIR, T1, T1CE, and T2). As a result, we obtained three arrays per sample, and the CNN and ViT models showed the same region. In the testing phase, only the slice with the largest tumor area was used.

Deep learning model development

We employed two distinct deep learning models to predict IDH status in gliomas. The first model, GliomaDepth-IDH, was based on the historical ResNet34 architecture, which demonstrated robust performance in a previous study43. The second model, GliomaVista-IDH, utilizes a Vision Transformer (ViT) architecture that employs attention-based mechanisms, and is expected to achieve superior performance44. Both models were trained on the same dataset but represented fundamentally different approaches to deep learning-based image analysis.

Model architecture

The architecture of GliomaDepth-IDH is based on ResNet3443. We used a pretrained model that was trained on ImageNet45. The original ResNet34 is designed for 3-channel images classifying 1000 classes, which is not ideal for our purpose. Therefore, we replaced the first Convolutional layer to handle 12-channel images and modified the final fully connected layer for 2-class classification (IDH wild-type or mutant). We retained the 2-class output configuration for GliomaDepth-IDH because preliminary experiments with single-output regression showed substantially degraded performance with this architecture. However, the architecture of GliomaVista-IDH is based on ViT44. We used a pretrained model that trained on ImageNet-21k and fine-tuned on ImageNet-1k (with additional augmentation and regularization). The input channels were four because four image types (FLAIR, T1, T1CE, and T2). After the input layer, the structure of GliomaVista-IDH adhered to the base model, ultimately obtaining a fully connected layer and performing binary regression. To improve probability calibration, we modified the final output layer of GliomaVista-IDH to produce a single continuous value rather than two-class logits, directly regressing the probability of IDH mutation. Additionally, the loss function employed was cross-entropy for GliomaDepth-IDH and binary cross-entropy for GliomaVista-IDH. Our implementation was based on the Python PyTorch package.

Model training

For GliomaDepth-IDH, the cross entropy loss was used for the loss function, with SGD as the optimizer. The momentum and weight decay were set to 0.1 and 0.0001, respectively. OneCycleLR was selected as the scheduler with a maximum learning rate of 0.005. In the training phase, we used the Python PyTorch and PyTorch Lightning packages. For GliomaVista-IDH, the Adam optimizer was selected, and the learning rate, beta 1, beta 2, eps, and weight decay were set to 0.00005, 0.9, 0.999, 0.00000001, and 0, respectively. The same PyTorch and PyTorch Lightning frameworks were utilized for implementation.

For GliomaDepth-IDH, the cross-entropy loss was used with SGD as the optimizer. The momentum and weight decay were set to 0.1 and 0.0001, respectively. OneCycleLR was selected as the scheduler with a maximum learning rate of 0.005. Training was implemented in Python using PyTorch and PyTorch Lightning. For GliomaVista-IDH, the Adam optimizer was used with a learning rate of 0.00005, β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999, ε = 1e-8, and weight decay = 0, again using PyTorch and PyTorch Lightning.

To ensure a transparent comparison while respecting each model’s convergence behavior, we adopted fixed epoch budgets per model: GliomaDepth-IDH was trained for 100 epochs, and GliomaVista-IDH for 20. The training and validation learning curves for all models are provided in the Extended Data section (Supplementary Fig. 6). We also tested early stopping strategies that monitored either the validation loss or Brier score; however, neither approach matched nor improved the performance obtained with these fixed per-model epoch regimens.

As previously noted, the training and validation sets were created using fully random splits. Under this protocol, we observed fold-to-fold variability. In a subset of random folds, the validation loss remained elevated or failed to decrease, even as the training progressed, suggesting hard validation outliers and/or potential label noise. We explored several additional training configurations beyond the settings described above; however, this behavior did not materially improve, which we attribute to the data heterogeneity intrinsic to the randomly drawn validation subsets rather than to the specific optimizer or scheduler choices.

Cross-validation approach

We categorized the BraTS and JC training datasets into 10 equal portions to implement a robust cross-validation approach. For each iteration, one portion was designated for validation, and the remaining portions were used for training. This process resulted in the development of 10 deep learning models for each dataset, yielding a total of 20 models.

Evaluation methods

Regarding the physician evaluation tool, we developed a custom evaluation tool for clinicians to assess IDH mutation status from MRI images. This tool is publicly available in the GitHub repository. The tool presents all four MRI sequences (T1, T2, FLAIR, and GdT1) simultaneously, allowing clinicians to navigate through the imaging data using a user-friendly interface. Additionally, this tool includes functionality for adjusting the brightness and contrast, as well as synchronous scrolling across all sequences. In the training phase, immediate feedback regarding the correct IDH mutation status was provided after each assessment. However, no feedback was provided to avoid influencing the subsequent evaluations in the testing phase.

We compared the IDH mutation status prediction performance of the AI models and physicians using the following two primary metrics: accuracy and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). For physician assessment, the participants used our custom-designed evaluation tool. The evaluation tool presented MRI sequences of each case and required physicians to select one of the following five options: “IDH wild-type,” “probably IDH wild-type,” “I have no idea,” “probably IDH mutation,” or “IDH mutation.” This five-point scale generated a pseudo-continuous variable that enabled AUC calculation, providing a more nuanced evaluation of diagnostic confidence than binary classification alone. Physicians were given the option to respond “I have no idea” for cases where they felt unable to make a confident prediction. Such responses were conservatively treated as incorrect predictions (assigned a probability of 0.5) in all performance calculations rather than being excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses, including tests for differences between groups, were performed using the ‘SciPy’ package in Python 3.10. Demographic differences between datasets were tested using appropriate statistical tests. Age differences were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test because the data were non-normally distributed. Sex differences were analyzed using the χ² test. Model and physician performances were quantified using accuracy, AUC, precision, recall, and F1-score. Among these, only the AUC was subjected to formal statistical testing for group differences. The other metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, and F1) were reported descriptively as summary statistics. For AUC-based comparisons between the AI models and physician groups, we employed case-level stratified bootstrap resampling, in which positive and negative cases were resampled with replacement within each class for 5000 iterations. In each bootstrap replicate, AUCs were recomputed and averaged for each group. The difference in mean AUC (ΔAUC) across groups was then used to construct the bootstrap distribution. We report the point estimate (ΔAUC), 95% percentile confidence interval (2.5th–97.5th percentiles), and the two-sided p-value calculated against the null hypothesis ΔAUC = 0. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Because DeLong’s test fundamentally compares two correlated single ROC curves for identical samples, it is not directly applicable to our primary group-level comparison. Nevertheless, for reference, DeLong’s results calculated on a representative (consensus) score series, defined as the per-case mean of model folds for AI models and the per-case mean across physicians within each group, are provided in Supplementary Tables 4, 5. These DeLong results were shown only for context and were not used in the primary inferential analysis. The DeLong test implementation followed the method described by DeLong et al., which utilized the mid-rank approach to handle tied predictions. For each comparison, the covariance matrix of the AUC estimates was computed using the structural components of the Mann–Whitney U statistic, and the test statistic was calculated as the difference in AUCs divided by the square root of the variance of the difference. The 95% confidence intervals for individual AUCs were derived from DeLong variance estimates using a normal approximation. Two-sided p-values were computed using a standard normal distribution. Inter-rater reliability among clinicians was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) calculated using the Python Pingouin package. We employed a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement, reporting both ICC(2,1) for single-measure reliability and ICC(2,k) for average-measure reliability across the k raters. ICC(2,1) represents the expected correlation between a single rater’s score and the mean of k randomly selected raters evaluating the same cases, whereas ICC(2,k) represents the reliability of the mean rating across all k raters. The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed using the F-distribution-based methods. ICC analyses were performed separately for each physician subgroup (neuroradiologists, neurosurgeons, neurosurgery residents, and high performers) on both the BraTS and JC datasets (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6).

A decision curve analysis was performed to evaluate the clinical utility of the AI models across a range of threshold probabilities (Supplementary Fig. 3). Net Benefit was calculated as: Net Benefit = (TP/n) − (FP/n) × (pt/(1−pt)), where TP is true positives, FP is false positives, n is the total number of cases, and pt is the threshold probability. This metric quantifies the trade-off between the benefit of correctly identifying the target condition and the cost of false-positive predictions weighted by the clinical consequences at each decision threshold. Importantly, for this analysis, the probability labels were intentionally inverted such that a predicted value of 1 corresponded to the IDH wild-type, whereas 0 corresponded to the IDH mutant. This inversion was performed because, from a clinical standpoint, IDH-wild-type glioma typically represents a more aggressive disease biology and may necessitate surgical or therapeutic intervention when suspected; aligning the positive class with the clinically actionable condition allows the decision curve to directly reflect potential clinical decision thresholds relevant to management. Decision curves were generated for threshold probabilities ranging from 0.01 to 0.99 and compared against two reference strategies: “treat all” and “treat none.” Across both datasets, the decision curves illustrate the range of threshold probabilities at which each model provides a greater net benefit than the default management strategies, highlighting model-dependent variations in the potential clinical utility for identifying patients likely to have IDH-wild-type gliomas.

Model interpretability

To provide interpretable explanations for AI model predictions, we employed Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) for GliomaDepth-IDH and attention map visualization for GliomaVista-IDH.

For the GliomaDepth-IDH, Grad-CAM visualization was generated using the PyTorch-Grad-CAM library. The target layer was set to the final BasicBlock of layer4 (model.layer4[-1]), which represented the deepest convolutional layer of the ResNet34 architecture and captured the high-level semantic features most relevant to the classification decision. This layer consists of two 3×3 convolutional layers with 512 channels, batch normalization, and rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation. Class-specific gradient information was computed by backpropagation from the predicted class output, and the resulting activation maps were weighted by global average-pooled gradients to produce the final Grad-CAM heatmaps. The grayscale activation maps were converted into color heat maps using the JET color map and overlaid on the corresponding T1-weighted contrast-enhanced (T1CE) MR images with 50% transparency for visualization.

For GliomaVista-IDH, attention maps were extracted from the Vision Transformer (ViT-Small) architecture, which employed 16×16 pixel patches as input tokens. The model consists of 12 transformer blocks, each containing multihead self-attention with six attention heads and an embedding dimension of 384. To generate interpretability visualizations, we extract attention weights from the final transformer block by computing query-key-value (QKV) projections from the normalized features. The attention weights across all the heads were averaged, and the resulting attention vector (excluding the classification token) was reshaped into a spatial grid corresponding to the original patch layout. This grid was then resized to match the input image dimensions (224 × 224 pixels) using bilinear interpolation, producing a continuous attention mask that highlighted the regions that the model considered most relevant for IDH mutation prediction. The attention masks were normalized to their maximum values and overlaid on the corresponding MR images with 50% transparency for visualization.

For both models, visualizations were generated using the best-performing fold from the 10-fold cross-validation (fold 5 for GliomaDepth-IDH and Fold 0 for GliomaVista-IDH). Representative cases from both the BraTS test dataset (Fig. 7) and the JC external validation dataset (Fig. 8) were selected to illustrate the interpretability of the model predictions across different data domains.

Hardware environment

All models were trained and evaluated on an NVIDIA DGX-1 system equipped with 8 × Tesla V100 GPUs (32 GB memory per GPU), dual Intel Xeon E5-2698 v4 processors (2.2 GHz, 20 cores each), and 512 GB DDR4-2133 system memory.

Software and computational environment

All analyses were performed using Python. Model training and prediction were performed using Python 3.10.12, PyTorch 2.2.0, PyTorch Lightning 1.8.1, torch metrics 0.11.4, and timm 0.9.16. Data processing, statistical evaluation, and visualization were conducted using Python 3.10.15, NumPy 1.25.2, pandas 2.2.3, scikit-learn 1.5.2, SciPy 1.9.3, nibabel 5.3.2 for neuroimaging data handling, and pytorch-grad-cam 1.5.5 for interpretability analysis.
