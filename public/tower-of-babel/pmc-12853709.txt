Exploring the intrinsic mechanism of growth mindset on the academic performance of female art majors in China
Authors: Linxia Ouyang, Yun Peng, Lin Li, Libo Zhang, Yang Yi, Chun Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe growth mindset proposes that intelligence comprises expandable, malleable, and controllable attributes, and those who embrace this mindset are inclined to view challenges and failures through a more optimistic lens. As a result, it can propel female college art majors towards greater achievements in both academic and professional settings. This study employed empirical analysis on 398 questionnaire responses, utilising Mplus 8.0 software. The research findings reveal the following: (1) There is a positive correlation between the academic performance of college female art majors and their growth mindset; (2) Grit serves as a mediator in the relationship between growth mindset and the academic performance of college female art majors; (3) Learning adaptability mediates the association between growth mindset and the academic performance of college female art majors; (4) Personal growth initiative serves as a moderator in the relationship between growth mindset and the academic performance of college female art majors. These findings not only enrich learning theories but also provide practical insights for improving students’ academic outcomes.Keywords: Growth mindset, Grit, Learning adaptability, Personal growth initiative, Academic performance

Introduction

Art emerges from society and serves as a vital component of higher education [1]. Universities play a key role in nurturing artistic talent. Unlike other disciplines, art majors require students to cultivate professional skills while studying theoretical knowledge [1]. This includes extensive practical exercises that enhance the academic performance of art majors by integrating professional practice and theory [1]. Some scholars have conducted further analysis of the academic performance of male and female college students, finding that female students underperform compared to their male peers in areas such as fundamental knowledge, learning ability, innovative thinking, and creativity [2, 3]. Consequently, this hurts the academic performance of female students [2]. Therefore, universities should accurately understand the characteristics and principles of art education, continually improving the academic performance of female art majors. The concept of growth mindset asserts that abilities are malleable and can be continually improved through effort and training [4–7]. This belief fosters a more positive outlook on challenges and failures, facilitating success in both academic and professional spheres [8–10]. Previous studies have shown that individuals with a growth mindset attribute improvement to effort, thereby boosting students’ motivation to learn and enhancing their subsequent academic performance [11, 12]. Some researchers have not achieved positive results [13–15]. These controversies underscore the need for a re-examination of the role of the growth mindset in the academic performance of female college art majors.

Grit encompasses an individual’s perseverance and passion for long-term goals [16], comprising unwavering effort (perseverance of effort) and sustained interests. “Perseverance of effort” denotes a person’s continual application of effort over time, while “consistency of interest” reflects a person’s steadfast enthusiasm for loftier objectives, which may even amplify in the face of challenges or setbacks, particularly as they age [16]. Previous studies have primarily focused on predicting outcomes using grit, with limited investigation into its influencing factors. Grit is regarded as a vital attribute for contemporary youth, profoundly influencing psychological dynamics and playing a pivotal role in students’ growth and development. Despite widespread acknowledgement of its significance in learning, empirical evidence establishing its correlation with academic performance remains sparse [17], fueling ongoing debates concerning its impact on learning outcomes [18, 19]. For example, a study comprising 832 Chinese high school students revealed a noteworthy positive correlation between grit and academic performance [18]. In contrast, another study reported no significant association between grit and academic achievement [19].

Collie and Martin [20] redefined adaptability as the ability to use specific skills to adjust to novel, changing, and uncertain environments. Holliman et al. [21] defined adaptability as encompassing an individual’s thoughts, actions and influence in management, adjustment, and modification. The application of adaptability to the field of learning has given rise to the concept of learning adaptability. Learning adaptability encompasses the psychological and behavioural processes by which individuals actively adapt their mental and physical states to meet the challenges of ever-changing learning conditions, striving to maintain equilibrium between internal and external learning environments [22]. Learning adaptability reflects students’ capacity to adapt to academic demands, attitudes toward the learning process, participation in learning activities, and their level of effort, directly impacting the efficiency and quality of their learning [23], which may significantly influence students’ academic performance.

A personal growth initiative (PGI) refers to individuals actively and purposefully enhancing and refining themselves through the process of self-improvement [24, 25]. As a new concept from the perspective of positive psychology, PGI has garnered widespread attention in the field. Unlike relatively stable personality traits, PGI is fundamentally a collection of skills aimed at fostering self-improvement [26], most similar to specific proactive characteristics in individuals, containing developmental components that can be improved through intervention [27]. Similar to other developed skills, the level of an individual’s PGI exists on a continuum from low to high [28]. The goal of PGI is to achieve growth and change in life, representing a metacognitive structure through which individuals consciously and actively enhance their cognition and behaviour in life [29]. In the context of learning, personal growth initiatives may play a significant role.

Therefore, this study includes variables such as grit, learning adaptability, and PGI to empirically explore the underlying mechanism of the interaction between growth mindset and academic performance among female art majors. The research aims to address several key gaps in the existing literature. First, while previous studies have shown inconsistent findings regarding the relationship between growth mindset and academic outcomes, few have focused on the unique group of female art students, who encounter both gender-specific learning patterns and the particular demands of practice-based art education. Second, although grit, learning adaptability, and PGI have been individually linked to academic performance, their combined role in the context of higher education in art remains largely unexplored. By integrating these psychological factors within an analytical framework, this study aims to provide a more detailed understanding of the elements that influence academic achievement in this specific student group. The results are expected to offer both theoretical insights into the motivational and self-regulatory processes involved in art education and practical guidance for developing supportive interventions to improve the educational experience and outcomes of female art majors.

Research hypotheses

Growth mindset and college female art majors’ academic performance

A growth mindset is defined by the belief that intelligence and abilities are adaptable, flexible, and can be enhanced continually through dedication and practice [8, 30]. Individuals with a growth mindset derive greater enjoyment from the learning process, as they hold the belief that sustained efforts will ultimately lead to success [31, 32]. Applied to academics, this mindset can enhance learning outcomes. Interventions promoting a growth mindset can transform the current teaching situation, enabling learners to re-evaluate themselves while exploring knowledge, thus improving their learning effectiveness [33]. A growth mindset can help college female art majors overcome adversity and alleviate various negative emotions [34]. When individuals believe in the potential for improvement through practice and training, setbacks or failures do not deter their pursuit of excellence. They are less inclined to give up easily, maintaining a positive attitude toward challenges. Consequently, those with a growth mindset often demonstrate increased resilience in the face of academic obstacles. They see overcoming setbacks as a vital part of self-transformation, embracing challenges with enthusiasm, actively seeking solutions, and persisting until they reach their self-improvement goals. Individuals with a growth mindset exhibit greater academic engagement, making it a significant predictor of academic performance [35]. Based on these considerations, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1: There is a positive correlation between the academic performance of college female art majors and their growth mindset.

H1: There is a positive correlation between the academic performance of college female art majors and their growth mindset.

The mediating role of grit

Numerous empirical studies currently affirm the pivotal role of a growth mindset in fostering grit and subsequent academic success [36–39]. This research suggests that embracing a growth mindset can motivate individuals to establish and persevere in pursuit of lofty, long-term goals [38]. When individuals view their abilities as malleable through effort and practice, they are less likely to become discouraged in the face of setbacks, perceiving these challenges as opportunities for ongoing self-improvement. They actively seek effective problem-solving strategies, persistently advancing toward their objectives [8, 37], thus demonstrating heightened levels of grit. Furthermore, individuals with a growth mindset typically approach learning with a goal-oriented mindset, which further indicates their elevated levels of grit [40]. Consequently, it can be inferred that a positive correlation exists between a growth mindset and grit.

Grit embodies an individual’s enduring commitment and unwavering dedication to long-term objectives, characterised by sustained effort and persevering interest [25, 41, 42], propelling individuals to overcome difficulties and progress towards their goals. According to the theory of character strengths, positive personality traits can significantly influence an individual’s behaviour, emotions, and cognition. Grit significantly and positively predicts an individual’s stress levels and psychological well-being [43, 44]. Higher levels of grit lead to greater psychological flexibility, enabling individuals to make positive self-adjustments when facing adversity and maintain an optimistic attitude. Students with higher levels of grit typically exhibit clear learning motivations, demonstrate a willingness to invest time and effort into their studies, possess greater confidence in their abilities, and consequently, attain higher levels of academic performance and overall happiness [45]. Research has confirmed that grit strongly predicts students’ academic performance [41]. Thus, this study posits the following hypothesis:

H2: Grit serves as a mediator in the relationship between growth mindset and the academic performance of female college art majors.

H2: Grit serves as a mediator in the relationship between growth mindset and the academic performance of female college art majors.

The mediating role of learning adaptability

A growth mindset fosters resilience in disadvantaged students [46], aligning with the concept of adaptability. Research indicates that those with stronger adaptability often exhibit a growth mindset [47], demonstrating greater flexibility [48] and improved adaptation to new environments. Consequently, a growth mindset positively predicts learning adaptability [49].

Adaptability plays a positive role in various aspects of development. For example, vocational adaptability is negatively correlated with vocational anxiety [50] and positively correlated with life satisfaction [51]. Adaptability extends beyond vocational contexts, having also been studied for its positive association with students’ learning performance [52–55]. Research demonstrates a positive association between adaptability and factors like hope and a negative association with anxiety and despair [54]. In other words, learning adaptability can help college female art majors reduce negative emotions associated with learning, develop a positive cognitive understanding of their current learning, and consequently achieve better academic performance. Thus, this study posits the following hypothesis:

H3: Learning adaptability mediates the association between growth mindset and the academic performance of college female art majors.

H3: Learning adaptability mediates the association between growth mindset and the academic performance of college female art majors.

The moderating role of PGI

PGI is a self-directed process where individuals actively pursue personal growth and self-improvement. This initiative fosters a growth mindset, embracing the potential for growth and development of basic abilities and intelligence. Psychodynamic studies have shown that PGI is closely associated with “self-actualisation,” “striving for superiority,” and “psychosocial development” [56]. Research indicates that students with high PGI tend to demonstrate greater dedication and motivation in their studies [57], which in turn results in better academic performance [58, 59]. Additionally, PGI has a significant impact on students’ resilience, fostering a positive effect on their academic performance [60]. In other words, high PGI individuals perceive challenges as learning opportunities, recover effectively from setbacks, and enhance academic efficacy, autonomy, control, and life goals [61]. Finally, PGI can serve as a protective factor against stress, indirectly promoting academic performance by increasing students’ sense of happiness and reducing anxiety that impedes academic success [62]. In contrast, individuals with low PGI may lack autonomy and a sense of purpose, leading to a lack of interest in learning and a tendency to become discouraged, ultimately resulting in lower academic performance [61]. Thus, this study posits the following hypothesis:

H4: PGI serves as a moderator in the relationship between growth mindset and the academic performance of female college art majors.

Methods

Data collection

The questionnaire, translated and refined by a professional agency, consists of two parts. The first part outlines the research’s purpose and significance, assuring respondents that their data will be used solely for scientific research and not for commercial purposes. Participants must be of legal age; minors require written consent from their parents or guardians. The second part includes various variables. Before data collection, the study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the College of Textile and Fashion at Hunan Institute of Engineering, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the “Ethical Review Measures for Scientific and Medical Research Involving Humans” issued by the Chinese government.

Before formal data collection, this study conducted a pre-test and calculated the sample size. A total of 60 questionnaires were distributed in this case, and 54 valid responses were obtained, which met the sample requirements for the pre-test [63]. The analysis of the pre-test questionnaires indicated that the average variance extraction (AVE) of each variable was no less than 0.50, the factor loadings of each item exceeded 0.60, and the composite reliability (CR) of each variable ranged from 0.74 to 0.86. These results strongly suggest that the questionnaire has good reliability and validity, making it highly suitable for formal survey research.

Data collection took place from February to April 2024. The questionnaire was structured into three distinct sections: procedural particulars, confidentiality guarantees, and variable items. To mitigate response bias, the order of the items was randomised. Participants filled out the questionnaire anonymously, and a $2 incentive was provided to enhance the authenticity of their responses. Recruitment was carried out through both offline and online channels. Offline, 220 female art majors were recruited through personal networks, such as colleagues and acquaintances associated with universities. For the online recruitment, questionnaire links were disseminated on social media platforms, which successfully attracted an additional 260 female art majors to participate. In total, 480 questionnaires were distributed, and 424 valid responses were gathered. Concurrently, informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians. After excluding incomplete submissions, 398 valid responses remained. Geographically, 174 participants (43.7%) were from eastern China, 125 (31.4%) from central China, and 99 (24.9%) from western China. The study group was composed of 165 students from rural towns (41.5%) and 233 students from cities (58.5%), with a mean age of 20.4 years (SD = ± 3.1).

Measurement

Measurement of Growth Mindset refers to the Mindset Inventory developed by Dweck [64], which comprises six items, including three positively scored items and three negatively scored items, such as “It’s challenging for me to change my level of ability” and “I can learn new knowledge, but I can’t alter my fundamental level of ability.”

Measurement of Grit, based on Duckworth et al.‘s scale [16], comprises two dimensions: Consistency of Interests and Perseverance of Effort, with 12 items. Examples include " I frequently establish a goal only to later opt for a different pursuit " and “I have accomplished a goal that required years of dedication.”

Measurement of Learning Adaptability, based on Feng et al.‘s scale [65], comprises five dimensions: learning motivation, teaching mode, learning ability, learning attitude, and learning environment, with a total of 44 items. Examples include “I believe that university students not only need to acquire knowledge but also need to develop social skills” and “I feel that I am adapting well to the academic demands of university.”

Measurement of PGI was evaluated via Scale-II (PGIS-II) by Robitschek [29], encompassing four dimensions: planfulness, using resources, intentional behaviour, and readiness for change, with 16 items like “I actively endeavour to enhance myself” and “I am continually evolving into the individual I aspire to become.”

The students were asked to report their GPA to assess their academic performance. This information was then used to rank students within their respective cohorts. The GPAS of students ranked in the top one-seventh received a score of “7”, those in the bottom one-seventh received a score of “1”, and the others were scored from “6” to “2” accordingly.

A 7-point Likert scale was used to measure all items, with one representing “strongly disagree” and seven representing “strongly agree.”

Additionally, age, academic year, and monthly expenditure were deliberately selected as control variables. Specifically, for monthly expenditure, a value of 1 represents less than 1,500 RMB; 2 stands for the range of 1,501-2,000 RMB; 3 corresponds to 2,001–2,500 RMB; 4 indicates 2,501-3,000 RMB; 5 denotes 3,001–3,500 RMB; 6 signifies 3,501- 4,000 RMB; and 7 represents over 4,000 RMB.

Data analysis

In this study, data analysis was carried out using Mplus 8.0 software, which involved the following steps: First, the measurement model was examined through Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). The relationships between latent variables and observed variables were defined in the “MODEL” section. The Maximum Likelihood Estimation method (ESTIMATOR = ML) was employed to calculate factor loadings (with values greater than 0.6) and model fit indices (CFI ≥ 0.90; RMSEA ≤ 0.08), which were used to validate the construct validity. Second, for the mediation model, the paths were set in the “MODEL” section. The indirect effects were declared via the “MODEL INDIRECT” command. The Bootstrap method was used to generate confidence intervals. If the interval did not include zero, it indicated that the mediation effect was significant. Finally, for the moderation model, interaction terms needed to be constructed. The coefficients of the moderation effects were estimated using the Multigroup Structural Equation Modelling (MSEM) approach. The significance of the interaction terms was tested to determine whether the moderation effect was significant.

Sample common method bias test

Building on existing literature [66–69], this study mitigated Common method bias through established procedural and statistical controls. Procedurally, the questionnaire was devised to prevent automatic responses by randomising item order and ensuring anonymity. To further validate these findings, statistical analyses were conducted. Harman’s single-factor test revealed that the first factor accounted for only 38.72% of the variance, which is below the critical 50% threshold. Additionally, including a latent common method factor in the structural equation model improved fit indices, with a reduction in RMSEA (0.01) and SRMR (0.02), and increases in CFI (0.04) and NFI (0.03), collectively indicating that common method bias is not substantial in this study.

The model demonstrated a good fit to the data. The fit indices were as follows: χ²/df = 2.82 (< 3), which measures the discrepancy between the model and data relative to degrees of freedom; CFI = 0.92 (> 0.90) and TLI = 0.94 (> 0.90), which compare the hypothesized model to a baseline model; RMSEA = 0.06 (< 0.08) and SRMR = 0.07 (< 0.08), which quantify approximate error per model degree of freedom and standardized residual variance, respectively; GFI = 0.93 (> 0.90) and AGFI = 0.91 (> 0.90), which assess the proportion of variance-covariance explained by the model; and NFI = 0.93 (> 0.90), which evaluates the model fit relative to a null model. Collectively, these indices support the model’s acceptable fit.

A post hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1. With a significance level of α = 0.05 and a sample size of N = 398, the study revealed that the statistical power exceeded 0.95 for the key observed effects (e.g., the direct and indirect paths). Thus, the findings regarding the direct, indirect, and moderating effects are statistically well-supported.

Discussion

Theoretical contributions

This study examines the internal psychological processes linking a growth mindset to academic performance among Chinese female art students, revealing various mediating and moderating paths. The findings confirm a strong positive link between growth mindset and academic achievement, supporting and expanding previous research [33, 35] by focusing on a specific group. Additionally, the results indicate that grit serves as an important mediating factor, supporting the notion that a growth mindset fosters perseverance in the face of difficulties, which in turn enhances academic outcomes [18].

By integrating the mediating roles of grit and learning adaptability with the moderating role of PGI, this research offers a more comprehensive understanding of the psychological processes involved. Examining these mechanisms specifically among female art majors addresses the influence of individual characteristics and academic context, thereby illuminating the nuanced interplay within this group. This integrated model not only clarifies the internal mechanisms but also establishes a solid foundation for future theoretical development and practical interventions.

Practical implications

First, educational institutions should develop systematic interventions to foster a growth mindset. Given the foundational role of a growth mindset identified in this study, such interventions are essential. This can be achieved by integrating mindset training into the curriculum and fostering a campus culture that supports it. Schools can offer workshops or include content within existing psychology courses to teach students about the science of neuroplasticity and the importance of effort. At the same time, creating an environment that celebrates learning from mistakes through positive messaging can reinforce these concepts.

Second, teaching practices should intentionally promote grit (perseverance of effort). Since grit acts as a key mediator, educators should create opportunities for students to develop and demonstrate perseverance. This includes setting challenging yet attainable long-term projects, clearly guiding students to break down significant goals into manageable steps, and offering supportive feedback that highlights progress and strategies for overcoming obstacles. Valuing sustained effort, rather than only final results, is essential.

Third, proactive support must be provided to improve students’ learning adaptability. The crucial mediating role of learning adaptability indicates that helping students adjust to new academic demands is essential. Specifically for female art majors moving into higher education, instructors can organise orientation programmes and continuous guidance on time management, self-regulated learning, and the use of university resources. Creating a supportive classroom environment where asking questions and seeking help is encouraged can greatly ease this adaptation process.

Fourth, initiatives that promote PGI should be implemented to enhance the advantages of a growth mindset. Since PGI influences the link between mindset and performance, actively empowering students to participate in their own development is crucial. This can be achieved by guiding students to set specific, personal goals in their academic and artistic pursuits, and by offering various growth opportunities, such as joining clubs, participating in exhibitions, and undertaking internships. Encouraging self-reflection and the proactive seeking of feedback can further boost their sense of agency in their own developmental journey.

Limitations and directions for future research

This study has several limitations that also suggest directions for future research. First, although it is based on a strong theoretical foundation, the cross-sectional design cannot determine definitive causal links between the variables. To directly examine the causal effects of a growth mindset, future research should adopt more rigorous methods. In particular, longitudinal studies with multiple assessments (for example, measuring students’ growth mindset, grit, adaptability, and academic performance at entry, mid-term, and graduation) could be carried out to uncover the dynamic developmental relationships among these variables. More significantly, intervention studies employing randomised controlled trials (RCTs) could be designed, where systematic workshops or online modules are used to promote a growth mindset actively, enabling a precise evaluation of its causal influence on academic performance and the proposed mediating mechanisms.

Secondly, the sample of this study mainly consisted of female Chinese art students. Different cultural backgrounds, customs, and group characteristics can influence individuals’ attitudes and behavioural choices. Future research could involve targeted comparisons, such as replicating this study with students from various majors (e.g., STEM, social sciences) or cultural backgrounds (e.g., Eastern vs. Western universities) to assess the model’s broader applicability. Specifically, it would be beneficial to explore how growth mindset interventions interact with the development of creative personality within educational traditions that emphasise technique and discipline.

Thirdly, although this study controlled for age, academic year, and monthly expenditure, the lack of more comprehensive socioeconomic status (SES) measures (such as parental education and occupation) must be recognised. These factors are potential confounders in the relationship between mindset and academic performance. Future research should systematically gather and account for these socioeconomic background variables, for instance, by combining household income, parental education level, and occupational prestige into a single index. Additionally, researchers could examine whether the mechanisms of the growth mindset operate differently across subgroups with diverse SES backgrounds, which would improve the validity of the findings and support more inclusive educational practices.

Finally, this study relied on self-reported data, which may be influenced by social desirability bias or inaccurate self-assessment. To improve the validity of the findings, future research should adopt multi-method approaches. This could include combining objective indicators of academic performance (e.g., course grades, portfolio scores, exhibition records) with behavioural measures (e.g., observing persistence time on challenging tasks) or peer evaluations, thereby creating a multidimensional and more reliable criterion system.
