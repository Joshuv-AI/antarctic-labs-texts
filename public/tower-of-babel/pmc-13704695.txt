Functional Disability Mediates the Association Between Low Back Pain and Sleep Disturbance
Authors: Soya Kawabata, Noriaki Kurita, Takuya Nikaido, Ryoji Tominaga, Yuji Endo, Nobuyuki Fujita, Shin-ichi Konno, Seiji Ohtori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Study Design.Population-based cross-sectional study.Objective.To examine the associations of sleep disturbance with low back pain (LBP) and pain-related disability in a community-based setting in Japan.Summary of Background Data.Sleep disturbance and LBP are highly prevalent and often overlap in ways that impair quality of life. Although their association has been reported, few population-based studies have simultaneously evaluated both LBP status and pain-related disability using validated assessment tools.Methods.Data from 2186 adults aged 20 to 90 years were analyzed. Sleep disturbance was assessed using the Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS; cutoff ≥6), and its association with LBP and pain-related disability [measured by the modified Oswestry Disability Index (mODI)] was examined. Psychological distress was assessed using the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6). Multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors independently associated with sleep disturbance. The Spearman rank correlation coefficients were calculated to examine the correlations between AIS scores and individual mODI items.Results.The prevalence of LBP and sleep disturbance was 14.9% and 29.7%, respectively. Participants with sleep disturbance were more likely to report LBP and had significantly higher mODI and K6 scores. In regression models, chronic LBP was associated with sleep disturbance when mODI was not included; however, this association was no longer significant after inclusion of mODI, which itself remained robustly associated. Higher K6 scores were also independently related to sleep disturbance. Correlation analyses showed that among mODI items, “Pain intensity” (r=0.329, P < 0.001) and “Sitting” (r=0.305, P < 0.001) had the strongest correlations with AIS scores.Conclusion.Disability related to LBP, rather than pain itself, was significantly associated with sleep disturbance, and psychological distress was also independently associated with insomnia.Level of Evidence.Level II.

Sleep disorders are highly prevalent worldwide, with an estimated 30% to 35% of adults experiencing insomnia symptoms, and 4% to 20% meeting the diagnostic criteria.1 Sleep disorders affect both mental and physical health. Insomnia increases the risk of psychiatric disorders, such as depression,2 while chronic sleep deprivation and fragmented sleep are associated with higher risks of hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, obesity, and diabetes.3 Conversely, chronic diseases, including cancer, rheumatoid arthritis, and fibromyalgia, have been suggested to contribute to the onset and persistence of sleep disorders.1 Previous reports have also shown that patients with chronic musculoskeletal pain frequently experience sleep disturbances, including poor sleep quality and difficulty initiating sleep.4,5 In addition, sleep disorders impose major societal burdens, leading to higher health care costs and reduced productivity.3

Low back pain (LBP) is one of the most severe and persistent forms of chronic musculoskeletal pain and is a major determinant of healthy life expectancy.6,7 Like sleep disorders, LBP imposes substantial economic costs through increased health care utilization and reduced work productivity.8 Its etiology is multifactorial, involving organic disorders such as lumbar disc degeneration and osteoporotic vertebral fractures, as well as sarcopenia and postural abnormalities.9,10 In addition, psychological factors are known to contribute to both the onset and persistence of LBP.11 Because LBP involves the trunk, a central structure for physical function, it markedly limits activities of daily living (ADL). The Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) is widely regarded as the gold-standard instrument for assessing functional disability in LBP. In addition to quantifying the pain-related restrictions in daily life, the ODI has been shown to predict clinical outcomes and treatment responses, underscoring its value in both clinical and research settings.12–14 Therefore, a comprehensive evaluation of LBP should encompass not only pain intensity but also functional disability, with ODI serving as a validated and reliable measure.

Several studies have reported an association between LBP and sleep disturbance, indicating that individuals with LBP are more likely to experience sleep problems and vice versa.15,16 However, most prior research has examined only the presence or absence of LBP, with limited attention to the role of pain-related disability in ADL. Furthermore, no population-based study has simultaneously evaluated both LBP status and ADL-related disability to determine their independent associations with sleep disturbance in a community-based setting. To address this gap, the present study investigated the associations of LBP and disability, measured by the ODI, with sleep disturbance assessed by the Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS), using cross-sectional data from a Japanese community-based survey.17

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participant Selection

This study employed the same target population and data collection procedures as reported in previous nationwide surveys.18,19 Using a cross-sectional design, ∼5000 adults aged 20 to 90 years were recruited through stratified two-stage random sampling, stratified by geography and degree of urbanization. Self-administered questionnaires were distributed and collected by trained surveyors between June 17 and July 17, 2023.

Ethics Approval

This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Assessment of Sleep Disturbance

Sleep disturbance was assessed using the AIS, based on previous validation studies.17,20 A total AIS score of ≥6 was classified as sleep disturbance, while a score ≤5 indicated its absence. Participants with missing responses on three or more of the eight AIS items were excluded from the analysis.

Definition of LBP

LBP was defined as pain persisting for at least 24 hours in the posterior trunk region between the 12th rib and the lower gluteal folds. To facilitate accurate identification, participants were provided with a body diagram highlighting the relevant area. LBP was classified as present if participants responded “yes” to the question: “Have you experienced LBP lasting more than 24 hours in the past month?”

Measured Items

The presence and duration of LBP were documented and classified as acute (<1 mo), subacute (1–<3 mo), or chronic (≥3 mo). Functional disability due to LBP was assessed using a modified ODI (mODI), derived from the standard 10-item ODI by removing the “sleeping” item to avoid conceptual overlap with the AIS. If three or more items were unanswered, the mODI score was treated as missing. For valid responses, the raw total score was divided by the maximum possible score (5 points per answered item) and then multiplied by 100 to obtain a standardized percentage score (0–100 scale).

Psychological distress was measured using the six-item Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6). A total score of ≥15 indicated psychological distress. Participants with a missing response on any K6 item were excluded from the analysis.

Additional variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), alcohol consumption, smoking status, and physical activity. Participants were classified as “drinkers” if they consumed alcohol three or more times per week, and as “smokers” if they had smoked within the past month. Physical activity was assessed in two domains: (1) regular light exercise (≥30 min per session, at least twice per week for ≥1 y) and (2) daily walking or equivalent activity (≥1 h per day).

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared between participants with and without sleep disturbance. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean±SD and analyzed using the Student t test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as medians [interquartile ranges (IQR)] and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test.

To identify factors associated with sleep disturbance, multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted with the presence of sleep disturbance as the dependent variable. Three models were constructed: (1) K6 score and LBP duration, (2) K6 score and mODI score, and (3) K6 score, LBP duration, and mODI score. All models were adjusted for age, sex, BMI (included for clinical relevance despite P = 0.10 in univariate analysis), alcohol consumption, and exercise habits, which showed significant differences in univariate analyses. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs and P-values were reported. In addition, the Spearman rank correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the relationship between each mODI item and the total AIS score.

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac, version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Two-tailed P-values<0.05 were considered indicative of statistical significance.

RESULTS

Of the 5000 eligible participants, 2186 were included in the final analysis. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study population. The mean age was 56 years, and slightly more than half of all participants were women. Sleep disturbance (AIS ≥6) was identified in 29.7% of participants. Most (85.1%) reported no LBP; 11.5% had chronic LBP, 2.5% acute LBP, and 1.0% subacute LBP. The median mODI score was 4.4 (IQR: 0.0–12.5), and the median K6 score was 1.0 (IQR: 0.0–4.0). Psychological distress (K6 ≥15) was present in only 2.5% of participants.

DISCUSSION

This large community-based study of Japanese adults demonstrated a significant association of both chronic LBP and pain-related disability with sleep disturbance. However, when both variables were included in the same multivariable model, only disability remained significant. This suggests that the relationship between chronic LBP and sleep disturbance is mediated primarily through functional impairment rather than pain itself. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first population-based study to evaluate the relationship between ODI-assessed disability and sleep disturbance. Correlation analyses further demonstrated that the ODI items of “Pain intensity” and “Sitting” were most strongly correlated with AIS scores, while the remaining domains showed weaker but consistently positive correlations. The prominence of sitting-related disability is noteworthy, as sitting is an essential daily posture. Persistent pain during sitting may produce sustained physical discomfort, thereby contributing directly to insomnia symptoms.

Cross-sectional studies have consistently shown a bidirectional relationship between chronic LBP and sleep problems: individuals with chronic LBP are more likely to experience insomnia or poor sleep quality, and conversely, those with insomnia often report greater back pain severity.21,22 For example, one study comparing 70 patients with chronic LBP to age-matched and sex-matched healthy controls found that 53% of patients reported sleep disturbances, compared with only 3% of controls.21 Similarly, analysis of data from the 2009 to 2010 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) demonstrated a strong association between sleep disorders and increased risk of chronic LBP in the U.S. general population.22 Our findings corroborate this association in a Japanese community-based population and extend previous work by showing that disabilities in ADL, rather than pain status alone, are closely linked with sleep disturbance.

Although few studies have directly compared AIS and ODI, related research using other validated instruments has consistently demonstrated strong associations between insomnia severity and functional disability due to LBP.23,24 For instance, a validation study of the AIS in Japanese patients with chronic pain reported positive correlations between AIS scores and pain intensity, disability, depression, anxiety, and pain catastrophizing.24 Similarly, studies of patients with spinal disorders have found moderate correlations between the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) and ODI scores.23 In those studies, PSQI was an independent predictor of disability alongside pain intensity, with a stronger association with ODI than that observed for radiating leg pain. These findings are consistent with our results, which showed significant correlations between mODI and AIS scores. In our study, because the cohort was drawn from the general population rather than from clinical patients, the overall level of disability was relatively low. Nevertheless, even within this generally low-disability cohort, clear differences in mODI scores were observed according to the presence of sleep disturbance, underscoring the robustness of this association. Notably, our use of the ODI adds clinical value, as it is not only a validated measure of disability but also a well-established predictor of prognosis in LBP. Thus, the observed associations between ODI scores and sleep disturbance may reflect broader links between disability, quality of life, and long-term outcomes. Indeed, the importance of addressing functional outcomes has also been highlighted in the neurosurgical field, both historically and with recent technological advances.25,26 The novelty of our study lies in its large community-based cohort and its analytical approach, which incorporated both ODI scores and LBP status. This design allowed us to demonstrate that the relationship between chronic LBP and sleep disturbance is largely mediated by disability.

Although the cross-sectional design of our study precludes causal inference, longitudinal research suggests that sleep disturbances may contribute to the onset or persistence of LBP and that improving sleep can facilitate recovery from back pain.27–29 In contrast, there is no clear consensus on whether LBP predicts subsequent insomnia: some studies found no such effect, while others reported that recurrent LBP significantly increases the risk of new-onset sleep disturbance.16,29 Collectively, these findings reinforce the concept of a bidirectional relationship between LBP and sleep disturbance.

The neurophysiological and psychological mechanisms underlying the relationship between LBP and sleep disturbance remain unclear. When these conditions coexist, psychological factors appear to play an important mediating role. In patients with LBP, insomnia severity has been shown to correlate with levels of depression and anxiety, and the association between pain and insomnia is partially mediated by psychological distress.24,30 However, the directionality of these effects is uncertain, and some evidence indicates that insomnia, psychological distress, and LBP may exert independent but mutually reinforcing influences.30 Consistent with this notion, our study found that both mODI and K6 scores were independently associated with sleep disturbance, supporting the presence of complex interactions. Beyond psychosocial mechanisms, recent EEG-based studies have revealed altered resting-state brain activity in patients with chronic LBP and comorbid insomnia, suggesting that neurophysiological pathways may also contribute.31 These findings indicate that fully elucidating the link between LBP and sleep disturbance will require an integrated framework that incorporates both psychological and neurobiological factors, a challenge for future research.

The findings of this study have important clinical implications. In managing patients with LBP, systematic assessment of sleep disturbance should be prioritized. Our results indicate that disability, rather than pain itself, is more strongly associated with sleep disturbance. In addition, psychological distress was independently associated with insomnia, suggesting that comprehensive treatment strategies addressing both physical disability and psychological well-being may be beneficial. However, future longitudinal and clinical trial studies are required to determine whether interventions targeting functional or psychological status can actually improve sleep outcomes.

Some limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationship between LBP and sleep disturbance. Second, information regarding therapeutic interventions, such as the use of sleep medications for sleep disorders or analgesics for LBP, was not collected, and these factors may have influenced the results. Third, all data were derived from self-administered questionnaires, which are subject to recall and reporting biases. Fourth, the response rate was relatively low. The COVID-19 pandemic may have affected the data collection process. Of note, declining response rates have been reported even in large-scale national surveys in the United States.32 Fifth, we were unable to account for other potential confounding factors, including medical history, night shift work, and other occupational factors. Despite these limitations, this study provides novel community-based evidence demonstrating a significant association between sleep disturbance and both LBP and pain-related disability among Japanese adults. Future longitudinal studies incorporating therapeutic data and broader covariate adjustment will be needed to clarify causal pathways between sleep disturbance and LBP.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that LBP-related disability, rather than the presence of chronic pain alone, was significantly associated with sleep disturbance. Psychological distress also emerged as an independent predictor of insomnia. These findings underscore the clinical relevance of incorporating functional disability assessment in managing patients with LBP who report sleep problems. However, due to the cross-sectional design of this study, causality cannot be established, and further research is warranted to clarify the directionality of these associations.
