Forecasting US land use through 2067 using multi-method projections applied to seven decades of USDA data
Authors: Nasrin Alamdari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong-term land use projections are essential for food security planning, conservation policy, and sustainable development, yet forecasting frameworks applied to the complete historical record of United States land use remain absent from the literature. Here we present the first comprehensive forecasting analysis of the USDA Economic Research Service (ERS) Major Land Uses (MLU) dataset, which spans 1945 to 2017 across 16 temporal observations for the 48 contiguous states. We develop and compare three forecasting approaches: (1) a Markov chain transition probability model estimated from 15 consecutive period-pairs via constrained least squares; (2) Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)-selected parametric curve fitting among linear, quadratic, logistic, and exponential models; and (3) scenario-modified Markov projections representing business-as-usual (BAU), accelerated urbanization, and conservation pathways. Projections extend 50 years to 2067 with bootstrap-derived uncertainty bounds from 500 iterations. Under BAU, cropland is projected to decline from 20.6% to 17.1% of total land area, forest-use land from 28.0% to 19.3%, while grassland pasture and range increases from 34.8% to 39.8% and special uses from 9.0% to 12.6%. AIC model selection independently identifies logistic saturation curves as the best-fitting model for cropland, forest, and urban land, providing convergent evidence that these major transitions are approaching asymptotic equilibria rather than continuing linearly. Under accelerated urbanization, urban land reaches 6.5% by 2067 with correspondingly greater losses in cropland and forest. State-level Markov models reveal convergence half-lives ranging from 5 to 15 years, demonstrating that the U.S. land use transformation is geographically asynchronous. The proposed framework leverages publicly available census-based tabular data and is designed to complement, rather than replace, remote sensing approaches; it can be readily adapted to any country that maintains periodic census-based or survey-based land use inventories, particularly in contexts where high-frequency national accounting over multi-decadal periods is required.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49479-y.Keywords: Land use forecasting, Markov chain, Transition probability, Steady-state equilibrium, Scenario analysis, USDA Major Land Uses, Cropland loss, Urban expansion, Bootstrap uncertaintySubject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Land use and land cover change (LULCC) is among the most consequential anthropogenic forces reshaping terrestrial ecosystems globally, directly affecting carbon cycling, biodiversity, hydrological systems, food production capacity, and human livelihoods1–3. The United States, encompassing approximately 2.26 billion acres (0.91 billion hectares) of land area, plays a disproportionate role in global land use dynamics owing to its position as the world’s largest agricultural exporter and one of the largest emitters of land-use-related greenhouse gases4,5. Understanding the trajectory and equilibrium state of U.S. land use is therefore of both national and global significance.

The USDA Economic Research Service (ERS) Major Land Uses (MLU) series represents the longest-running, most comprehensive accounting of all major uses of public and private land in the United States6. Initiated in 1945 and updated approximately every five years to coincide with the Census of Agriculture, the series compiles data from the USDA Forest Service, National Agricultural Statistics Service, U.S. Census Bureau, Bureau of Land Management, and other agencies into a consistent state-level framework spanning six major land use categories: cropland, grassland pasture and range, forest-use land, urban areas, special uses, and miscellaneous other land7,8. The most recent release covers 1945 through 2017 across 16 temporal observations9.

Previous analyses of the MLU data have been almost exclusively retrospective in nature. Lubowski et al.10 developed econometric models of land use transitions using the 1982–1997 National Resources Inventory but did not produce long-term forecasts. Bigelow and Borchers8 characterized patterns and trends through 2012 without projecting future trajectories. Mpanga and Koomson11 recently analyzed cropland competition and food security implications using the complete 1945–2017 series, documenting a 13.4% decline in cropland and 393% increase in urban land, but their analysis remained descriptive. More broadly, the land use forecasting literature has relied primarily on spatially explicit models driven by remote sensing data12–14, cellular automata15,16, agent-based simulations17, or econometric approaches18,19, which are well-suited to spatial analysis but operate on different temporal and organizational scales than the census-based, multi-decadal accounting framework used here. Freely available satellite-derived land cover products (e.g., NLCD, ESA CCI) have greatly reduced barriers to remote sensing analysis; the present framework is best understood as complementary, designed for long-term national accounting where consistent 70-year tabular records are available.

A critical gap therefore persists: no study has applied systematic, multi-method forecasting to the complete seven-decade MLU historical record to derive projections with quantified uncertainty. This gap is consequential because policymakers developing agricultural preservation programs, urban growth boundaries, and conservation designations require not only knowledge of historical trajectories but credible estimates of where the land base is heading—and at what pace. The distinction between a system approaching equilibrium versus one undergoing active transformation carries fundamentally different policy implications.

In this study, we address this gap through a comprehensive forecasting framework with the following specific contributions: (i) the first application of Markov chain transition probability models to the complete 1945–2017 MLU series at both national and state levels; (ii) AIC-based model selection among four parametric forms to identify the functional shape of each land use trajectory; (iii) derivation of steady-state equilibrium distributions and convergence dynamics; (iv) scenario analysis examining accelerated urbanization and conservation alternatives; (v) bootstrap uncertainty quantification with 500 iterations; and (vi) historical trend characterization using Shannon entropy, hierarchical clustering, Mann-Kendall tests, and a Cropland-Urban Competition Index to contextualize the forecasts.

Results

Historical land use dynamics (1945–2017)

Over the 72-year study period, the U.S. land base underwent substantial restructuring (Fig. 1; Table 1). Urban land increased by 393.1%, from 14,973 to 73,830 thousand acres, representing the most dramatic absolute category change. Special-use lands nearly doubled (+99.2%, from 85,020 to 169,342 thousand acres), driven primarily by expansion of national parks, wildlife refuges, and wilderness areas. Cropland declined 13.4% (from 450,692 to 390,085 thousand acres), forest-use land declined 12.2% (from 601,717 to 528,605 thousand acres), and miscellaneous other land declined 23.6%. Grassland pasture and range remained comparatively stable (−0.3%).

Mann-Kendall trend tests applied to all 288 state–category combinations revealed that urbanization is the only category exhibiting statistically significant monotonic trends (p < 0.05) in all 48 contiguous states. Significant trends were detected in 35 of 48 states for special uses, 32 for forest, 28 for cropland, 18 for pasture and range, and 13 for miscellaneous other land (Supplementary Fig. S3). Normalized Shannon entropy, computed as a measure of land use diversification, increased significantly over the study period (slope = 0.00062 per year, R² = 0.781, p < 0.001), indicating systematic movement away from agricultural dominance toward a more diversified land use portfolio (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Hierarchical clustering using Ward’s method identified five distinct state transformation archetypes (Supplementary Fig. S2): (1) an Urban Intensification cluster comprising six northeastern states (Connecticut, Delaware, Maryland, Massachusetts, New Jersey, Rhode Island) characterized by extreme urban growth and cropland contraction; (2) a Mixed Transformation cluster; (3–4) two Cropland Decline variants affecting 22 predominantly midwestern and southern states; and (5) a Special Use Expansion cluster of 18 western and plains states where federal land designations drove the dominant trend.

Markov transition probabilities

The estimated 6 × 6 transition probability matrix (Table 2, Fig. 3a) reveals key structural features of U.S. land use dynamics. Cropland exhibited a self-retention probability of 0.608, meaning approximately 61% of the cropland share was maintained between consecutive observation periods. Forest-use land showed the second-lowest self-retention (0.508), with substantial transitions to pasture and range (0.243) and special uses (0.249). Notably, urban land displayed a distinctive profile: its self-retention was only 0.368, but the dominant off-diagonal transition was to special uses (0.545), reflecting the strong co-expansion of urban infrastructure and peri-urban land designations. No category exhibited a substantial reverse transition probability from urban, empirically confirming the widely hypothesized irreversibility of urbanization20,21.

Parametric model selection

AIC-based comparison among linear, quadratic, logistic, and exponential models (Table S1) identified logistic saturation curves as the best-fitting functional form for cropland (AIC = −167.36), forest (−161.06), and urban land (−197.82). Pasture and range, and miscellaneous other land favored quadratic fits, while special uses was best described by a linear model. The logistic selections are substantively important: they indicate that the rates of cropland decline, forest loss, and urban expansion are all decelerating and approaching asymptotic limits, rather than continuing at historical rates. The logistic model predicts cropland stabilization near 20.5% of total land area, urban near 3.9%, and forest near 29.2%. These independently estimated saturation levels provide critical comparison points for the Markov steady-state equilibrium.

National projections through 2067

Under the business-as-usual (BAU) Markov projection (Figs. 2, 4; Table S2), cropland is projected to decline from 20.6% (2017) to 17.1% of total land by 2067, representing an additional loss of approximately 66,000 thousand acres. Forest-use land experiences the largest projected decline, from 28.0% to 19.3% (approximately 165,000 thousand acres). These losses are largely absorbed by pasture and range, which increases from 34.8% to 39.8%, and special uses, which increases from 9.0% to 12.6%. Urban land stabilizes near 4.0%, consistent with the logistic saturation identified independently through AIC model selection.

The Markov steady-state equilibrium—the distribution satisfying π = πT—was computed as the left eigenvector of the transition matrix corresponding to eigenvalue 1 (Table S3). The national system approaches this equilibrium by approximately 2057, at which point the Euclidean distance from steady state falls below 0.001 (Fig. 8a). Bootstrap uncertainty quantification

Bootstrap analysis with 500 iterations (Fig. 5) revealed heterogeneous projection uncertainty across categories. Urban projections exhibited the narrowest 90% confidence intervals (CI width < 1% point at 2067), reflecting high consistency in urban expansion dynamics across historical periods. Cropland and forest showed wider uncertainty bands (90% CI width approximately 4–6% points), reflecting greater sensitivity to which specific periods were included in transition matrix estimation. Pasture and range exhibited the widest confidence intervals, consistent with its documented responsiveness to commodity market dynamics22,23.

Scenario analysis

Three scenarios were constructed by systematically modifying the transition probability matrix while preserving its row-stochastic property (Fig. 6, Table S4). The ±50% scenario modification magnitude was selected to bracket the observed interquartile range of decadal variation in urbanization transition rates across the study period, and is consistent with the scale of urban growth rate changes documented in response to major policy interventions such as the Conservation Reserve Program and Smart Growth legislation. The accelerated urbanization scenario (+50% transition rates to urban) produces urban land of 6.5% by 2067 (vs. 4.0% under BAU), with corresponding additional losses of approximately 11,000 thousand acres of cropland and 19,000 thousand acres of forest-use land beyond BAU levels. Under the conservation scenario (50% reduction in cropland-to-urban and forest-to-urban transitions, redirected to special uses), urban growth is constrained to 3.6%, preserving approximately 8,000 thousand acres of agricultural and forest land relative to BAU. These results demonstrate that even moderate policy interventions altering the rate of land conversion can meaningfully shift long-term trajectories.

Cropland-urban competition

We developed a Cropland-Urban Competition Index (CUCI), defined as the ratio of cumulative urban gain to the absolute value of cumulative cropland loss from a 1945 baseline, expressed as a percentage. This index quantifies the degree to which urban expansion accounts for agricultural land loss (Fig. 7). The CUCI showed substantial temporal variation, with values fluctuating between 68% and 177% over different periods, reflecting the complex interplay of urbanization, agricultural policy, and market forces. Values exceeding 100% indicate periods when urban growth exceeded total cropland loss, implying net conversion from non-cropland sources.

State-level convergence dynamics

State-level Markov models, estimated independently for each of the 48 contiguous states, revealed substantial geographic heterogeneity in transformation velocity (Fig. 8, Supplementary Fig. S4). Convergence half-lives—defined as the years required for a state’s land use distribution to move halfway from its 2017 composition to its steady-state equilibrium—ranged from 5 to 15 years. Twenty-four states exhibited half-lives of 5 years, predominantly in the Urban Intensification and eastern Cropland Decline clusters, indicating these states have already largely completed their major land use transitions. In contrast, western Mountain states exhibited the longest convergence times, with ongoing expansion of special-use lands driven by federal land management policy decisions that may continue for decades24.

Cross-validation

Leave-last-out cross-validation (trained on 1945–2002, tested on 2007, 2012, and 2017) demonstrated that linear extrapolation outperformed the Markov chain for short-term point forecasts (overall RMSE: 0.024 vs. 0.086; Fig. 3b, Table S5). This result is expected: the Markov model captures average transition dynamics across all historical periods rather than extrapolating recent trends, and its primary advantage lies not in point prediction accuracy but in its capacity to derive equilibrium distributions, identify inter-category competition structures, and support counterfactual scenario analysis—capabilities absent from simple extrapolation. For policymakers, the Markov framework’s value lies precisely in these long-run properties: equilibrium analysis reveals where the system is heading under sustained dynamics, while scenario-modified matrices allow quantitative comparison of alternative policy pathways—analyses that are structurally impossible within a linear extrapolation framework.

Discussion

This study presents the first multi-method forecasting framework applied to the complete USDA Major Land Uses series. Several findings merit detailed discussion.

Convergence of independent methods

Perhaps the most robust finding is the convergence between two independent analytical approaches. Logistic models, fitted purely to the temporal shape of each trajectory, predict cropland stabilization near 20.5% of total land. The Markov steady-state equilibrium, derived from transition dynamics between consecutive periods, predicts stabilization at 17.1%. While these differ in magnitude—likely because the logistic model captures only recent deceleration while the Markov model extrapolates average historical dynamics—both are consistent with the hypothesis that cropland decline is decelerating toward an asymptotic limit, though uncertainty bounds remain wide enough that continued linear decline cannot be ruled out. Subject to these uncertainties, the convergence the post-war era of rapid agricultural contraction may be entering a mature phase25,26. Similarly, both approaches are consistent with urban expansion decelerating toward an asymptotic level (logistic: ~3.9%; Markov: ~4.0%), indicating the period of explosive urban growth that characterized 1945–2000 is decelerating27,28.

Food security implications

The projected additional loss of approximately 66,000 thousand acres of cropland under BAU has significant implications for domestic and global food security29,30. However, this loss occurs predominantly through transitions to pasture and range, suggesting an economic optimization as marginal cropland reverts to lower-intensity uses rather than permanent removal from agricultural production. The accelerated urbanization scenario presents a more concerning picture: 6.5% urban land by 2067 would represent irreversible conversion of productive agricultural land to impervious surfaces on a scale equivalent to the entire harvested cropland of a major agricultural state31. The difference between scenarios—approximately 11,000 thousand acres of cropland—provides a quantitative benchmark for evaluating the cumulative impact of urban containment policies32.

The conservation dividend

The conservation scenario demonstrates that halving the rate of agricultural and forest conversion to urban uses preserves approximately 8,000 thousand acres over 50 years. This “conservation dividend” quantifies the potential returns from policies such as conservation easements33, agricultural preservation zoning34, urban growth boundaries35, and transfer of development rights programs36. That the conserved land is redirected primarily into special uses rather than maintained in crop production reflects the complex multi-directional nature of land use competition that Markov frameworks can capture but linear models cannot.

Geographic heterogeneity and policy implications

The wide range of state-level convergence half-lives (5 to 15 years across the contiguous 48 states) carries direct policy implications. States with short half-lives—predominantly in the Northeast—have already largely completed their land use transformations and face management-oriented challenges: maintaining existing land use patterns, managing aging infrastructure, and responding to incremental changes. States with longer half-lives, predominantly in the Mountain West, are still undergoing active transformation driven by federal land management decisions, and policy interventions can more meaningfully alter their trajectories24. The five clustering-derived archetypes further suggest that regionally differentiated policy approaches would be more effective than uniform national standards, consistent with recent calls for place-based conservation and agricultural policy37,38.

Methodological contributions

This study demonstrates that credible, multi-scenario land use projections with uncertainty quantification can be generated from publicly available statistical datasets as a complement to spatially explicit remote sensing approaches12,39. The framework—Markov chains with bootstrap uncertainty, AIC-selected parametric models, and scenario-modified transition matrices—is best suited to nations with consistent multi-decadal census-based or survey-based land inventories; such inventories are more common in developed countries, and in data-limited settings, remote sensing-derived land cover products may provide a more practical alternative40,41. Within its intended scope, the framework offers a tractable, reproducible approach to long-run national land use accounting that complements rather than replaces spatially explicit methods.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. The first-order Markov assumption—that future shares depend only on current shares—ignores potential dependencies on rates of change or external drivers such as commodity prices, population growth, or climate42. These excluded drivers—particularly population dynamics, agricultural commodity markets, and projected climate shifts—could alter transition probabilities in ways the current framework cannot anticipate; future extensions should consider coupling Markov projections with scenario-based socioeconomic and climate drivers to improve policy realism. The five-year temporal resolution prevents detection of sub-decadal dynamics. The limited dataset of 16 observations over 72 years constrains model reliability; bootstrap resampling and leave-last-out cross-validation provide partial mitigation, and sensitivity analysis through temporal subsampling confirmed that projection directions are robust even when early or late observations are withheld. Goodness-of-fit diagnostics for AIC-selected parametric models, including R², RMSE, and residual plots, are provided in Supplementary Tables S2–S3 and Fig. S5; parameter confidence intervals confirm stable estimation for all selected models. Cross-validation showed the Markov model underperforms linear extrapolation for short-term point forecasting, suggesting hybrid approaches may be valuable38. The scenario analysis uses modifications grounded in policy-relevant magnitudes (e.g., USDA Conservation Reserve Program enrollment caps, observed urbanization rate variability across decades) to the transition matrix; coupling with economic models of land rents, population projections, and climate scenarios would further strengthen realism43,44. Finally, state-level analysis cannot capture within-state heterogeneity, which is particularly important for urban fringe dynamics45.

Methods

Data source and processing

We used the USDA ERS Major Land Uses (MLU) data product, which provides state-level estimates for six major land use categories at approximately five-year intervals from 1945 to 20176,9. The six categories—total cropland, grassland pasture and range, forest-use land, land in urban areas, all special uses of land, and miscellaneous other land—together account for all land area in each state. Analysis was restricted to the 48 contiguous states for temporal consistency, as Alaska and Hawaii entered the series only in 1959. All values are reported in units of thousand acres. The dataset comprises 16 temporal observations (1945, 1949, 1954, 1959, 1964, 1969, 1974, 1978, 1982, 1987, 1992, 1997, 2002, 2007, 2012, 2017), yielding 768 state–year observations.

Markov chain transition model

Land use dynamics were modeled as a first-order discrete-time Markov chain in which the land use share vector s(t+1) at time t+1 is a linear transformation of s(t): s(t+1) = s(t) · T, where T is a 6 × 6 row-stochastic transition probability matrix. Element T_ij represents the probability that land in use category i at time t transitions to category j at time t+1. The first-order assumption is adopted because the five-year observation interval and limited dataset of 16 time points do not support reliable estimation of higher-order dependencies; moreover, autocorrelation analysis of the share time series showed no statistically significant lag-2 partial autocorrelation, suggesting that current shares adequately characterize transition dynamics at this temporal resolution. T was estimated from 15 consecutive period-pairs via ordinary least squares: T = (X′X)⁻¹X′Y, where X and Y are matrices of stacked share vectors at times t and t+1 respectively. The raw least-squares solution was projected onto the set of row-stochastic matrices by clipping negative values to zero and row-normalizing46. Sensitivity analysis confirmed that the clipped values were small in magnitude (all clipped entries <0.02), and row-normalization adjustments were correspondingly minor, suggesting negligible bias introduced by this projection step. This procedure was applied at both national and individual state levels.

Steady-state equilibrium and convergence

The steady-state distribution π satisfying π = πT was obtained as the left eigenvector of T corresponding to eigenvalue 1, normalized to sum to unity. The existence and uniqueness of this eigenvector is guaranteed when the Markov chain is irreducible and aperiodic, which was verified for the national transition matrix. Convergence dynamics were assessed by computing the Euclidean distance d(t) = ||π(t) − π*|| between the projected share vector π(t) and the steady-state π* at each time step. A convergence half-life was defined as the number of projected years required for d(t) to fall to 50% of d (2017).

Parametric curve fitting with AIC model selection

For each land use category, four parametric models were fitted to the national share time series using nonlinear least squares47: linear s(t) = at + b (2 parameters); quadratic s(t) = at² + bt + c (3 parameters); exponential s(t) = ae^(b(t−1945)) + c (3 parameters); and logistic s(t) = L/(1 + e^(−k(t−t₀))) + b (4 parameters). The Akaike Information Criterion48 AIC = n·ln(SSE/n) + 2k was used to select the best model, balancing goodness of fit against parsimony.

Scenario analysis

Three scenarios were constructed by modifying the transition matrix: (1) Business-as-usual (BAU), using the estimated T directly; (2) Accelerated urbanization, increasing all transition probabilities to urban (column 4) by 50% with compensating proportional reductions in other off-diagonal elements; (3) Conservation, reducing cropland-to-urban and forest-to-urban transitions by 50% and redirecting to special uses. All modifications preserved the row-stochastic property of the matrix.

Bootstrap uncertainty quantification

To quantify projection uncertainty, 500 bootstrap iterations were performed. In each iteration, the 15 period-pairs were resampled with replacement, T was re-estimated via constrained least squares, and projections were generated through 2067. Percentile-based confidence intervals (5th–95th for 90% CI; 25th–75th for 50% CI) were derived from the bootstrap distribution of projected shares at each future time point.

Cross-validation

Leave-last-out temporal cross-validation was performed: models were trained on 1945–2002 data (12 observations) and used to forecast 2007, 2012, and 2017. Forecast accuracy was assessed via root mean squared error (RMSE) comparing Markov chain and linear extrapolation approaches across all six categories.

Historical trend characterization

Historical patterns were characterized using five complementary methods: (1) normalized Shannon entropy49 H′ = −Σp_i ln(p_i)/ln(n) for land use diversification; (2) Gini coefficients50 for spatial concentration across states; (3) Mann-Kendall trend tests51 with Sen’s slope52 applied to all 288 state–category share time series; (4) agglomerative hierarchical clustering using Ward’s method53 on standardized decadal change rates to identify transformation archetypes; and (5) a Cropland-Urban Competition Index (CUCI) = (cumulative urban gain / |cumulative cropland loss|) × 100, quantifying the proportion of cropland losses attributable to urban expansion.
