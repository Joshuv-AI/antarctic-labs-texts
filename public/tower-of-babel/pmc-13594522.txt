Low-Dose Corticosteroid Combined with Hydroxychloroquine for the Treatment of Generalized Annular Atrophic Lichen Planus: A Case Report
Authors: Jinhui Xu, Li Chai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAnnular atrophic lichen planus (AALP) is a rare subtype of lichen planus. It is distinguished from other variants by the reduction of elastic fibers in the superficial dermis and the presence of annular skin lesions, which are characteristic features of this variant. AALP often exhibits a chronic course and poor response to treatment. The rare case presented here may provide valuable insights for dermatologists.Materials and MethodsA case of a 57-year-old male diagnosed with generalized annular atrophic lichen planus is reported. Based on the clinical features and histopathological examination, a final diagnosis of generalized annular atrophic lichen planus was made. No significant abnormalities were found in the patient’s baseline condition. Following the diagnosis of AALP, the patient was subjected to combination oral therapy with low-dose methylprednisolone (16 mg/day) and hydroxychloroquine (0.4 g/day).ResultsA rapid resolution of the skin rash was observed two weeks after the initiation of treatment. During the clinical follow-up period of six months, no significant recurrence of the rash was noted.ConclusionThis case suggests that oral corticosteroids combined with hydroxychloroquine may be an effective treatment option for generalized annular atrophic lichen planus.

Introduction

Annular atrophic lichen planus (AALP) is a rare variant of lichen planus (LP) characterized by the coexistence of annular and atrophic lesions superimposed on the classic features of LP.1,2 The diagnosis of AALP relies primarily on histopathological examination in addition to clinical presentation. A key histopathological feature is the reduction of elastic fibers in the superficial dermis, which aids in distinguishing it from other variants of LP.1,3 AALP often responds poorly to conventional LP therapies, posing a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge for clinicians.2,4 Herein, we present a case of an exceptionally rare generalized form of annular atrophic lichen planus.

Case Presentation

A 57-year-old male presented to our hospital with generalized violaceous red rashes persisting for over three months. Prior to admission, the patient had been treated with topical mometasone furoate without systemic therapy, yet the rash continued to progress and spread extensively. Upon physical examination, large patches of purplish-red flat papules were observed all over the patient’s body, with wickham striae visible in some areas. Numerous annular erythematous lesions were also noted, and skin atrophy and color fading were observed in the center of some lesions. (Figure 1). Histopathological examination of a skin biopsy showed liquefactive degeneration of the basal cell layer and a reduction of elastic fibers in the superficial dermis (Figure 2). The patient’s history revealed no clear history of medication use prior to the onset of symptoms and no significant underlying diseases. Laboratory investigations yielded the following results: white blood cell count 3.3×109/L, monocyte percentage 12.7%, absolute lymphocyte count 0.71×109/L, low-density lipoprotein 4.39 mmol/L, total cholesterol 5.65 mmol/L, triglycerides 3.18 mmol/L, carcinoembryonic antigen 13.15 ng/mL, squamous cell carcinoma antigen 1.95 ng/mL, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate 7 mm/h. Tests for antinuclear antibodies, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, syphilis, and HIV were negative. The determination of Mycobacterium tuberculosis antibodies was also negative. Gastroscopy and colonoscopy indicated chronic non-atrophic gastritis. No significant abnormalities were detected by renal ultrasound, thyroid ultrasound, hepatobiliary-pancreatic-splenic ultrasound, superficial lymph node ultrasound, and chest CT. There was no relevant family history. Based on the clinical presentation, histopathological findings, and medical history, a final diagnosis of generalized annular atrophic lichen planus was made. Following the diagnosis of generalized annular atrophic lichen planus, the patient showed a poor response to conventional topical corticosteroids and tacrolimus. Considering the extensive involvement of the skin lesions, the patient was initiated on oral methylprednisolone 16 mg/day combined with hydroxychloroquine sulfate 0.4 g daily. After two weeks of regular treatment, significant resolution of the rash was observed. During the subsequent six-month follow-up period, no obvious recurrence of the skin lesions was noted.

Discussion

Lichen planus is a chronic, inflammatory, autoimmune-related disease affecting the skin and mucous membranes. Annular atrophic lichen planus is a rare variant of lichen planus, reported thus far only in adults (21–77 years old), with a male predominance. Generalized annular atrophic lichen planus is even more uncommon.5 Clinically, AALP presents as violaceous papules that expand centrifugally to form atrophic plaques with raised borders. Histopathologically, it exhibits typical features of lichen planus, including vacuolar interface changes, necrotic keratinocytes (Civatte bodies), pigment incontinence, and a band-like lymphocytic infiltrate, along with the characteristic feature of reduced elastic fibers in the superficial dermis.5,6 The etiology of AALP remains unclear. An association between hepatitis C and lichen planus is frequently reported. Furthermore, isolated reports have linked annular lichen planus to factors such as breast augmentation surgery and the use of anti-HER2 antibodies.7–9 In this patient, no strong associations with factors like hepatitis C or specific medications were identified, which is regrettable.

For the treatment of lichen planus, topical corticosteroids constitute the foundational therapeutic approach. However, for annular atrophic lichen planus, the efficacy of topical corticosteroids is often unsatisfactory, with only partial resolution of lesions reported in some cases.5 Topical tacrolimus represents an advancement over corticosteroids and has achieved varying degrees of success in some reported cases.1,6 In the management of this case, initial treatment with topical corticosteroids and tacrolimus was administered, but significant improvement in the skin lesions was not observed. Hydroxychloroquine possesses immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory properties and may inhibit T-cell-mediated inflammatory processes.10 Eyler et al reported a case of generalized lichen planus in 2017. Treatment with methylprednisolone and hydroxychloroquine initially controlled the lesions, but the rash progressed subsequently. Control was ultimately achieved only after the addition of acitretin.2 In the present case, given the extensive involvement of skin lesions and the poor response to topical therapy, the patient was treated with a combination of oral methylprednisolone (16 mg/day) and hydroxychloroquine (0.4 g/day). Marked resolution of the skin lesions was achieved, and no significant recurrence was observed during a six-month follow-up period. The generalized annular atrophic lichen planus reported here is exceedingly rare and was resistant to first-line topical therapies with corticosteroids and tacrolimus. Significant therapeutic efficacy was ultimately obtained with the combination of oral methylprednisolone and hydroxychloroquine. This report has certain limitations, including a small sample size and a relatively short treatment duration. Nevertheless, the combination of low-dose oral corticosteroids and hydroxychloroquine may represent a therapeutic option for patients with generalized annular atrophic lichen planus.
