Perceived Inadequate Neighborhood Food Shopping and Cardiovascular Disease Risk: The Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis
Authors: Amier Haidar, Ghadi Ghanem, Rishi Rikhi, Karol E. Watson, Shreela V. Sharma, Michael D. Shapiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndividuals with access to healthy food have healthier diets, improved cardiometabolic risk profiles, and decreased cardiovascular disease (CVD). However, previous studies have focused on the neighborhood food environment as distance to and density of food retailers. Our study examines the association between an individual’s perceived adequacy of neighborhood food shopping and incident CVD.MethodsWe analyzed data from 6814 participants in the MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis), a prospective cohort study of individuals aged 45 to 84 who were enrolled in the study between 2000 and 2002 and followed longitudinally. Cox proportional hazards models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) for incident CVD, adjusting for sociodemographic, behavioral, and cardiovascular risk factor covariates.ResultsApproximately 20% of participants reported inadequate neighborhood food shopping, with higher prevalence among Black and Hispanic participants, lower income, or educational level of high school or less. In fully adjusted models, perceived inadequate neighborhood food shopping was associated with a 21% increased risk of incident CVD (HR, 1.21 [95% CI, 1.04–1.41]; P=0.013). The CVD incidence rate per 100 person‐years was higher in the inadequate neighborhood food shopping group (1.39 versus 1.19).ConclusionsIn this large, diverse, prospective cohort study, the perceived inadequacy of neighborhood food shopping was associated with a significantly higher risk of incident CVD. These findings add to growing evidence that the neighborhood food environment plays a critical role in shaping cardiovascular health. Future research should evaluate the impact of neighborhood‐level interventions and policies on cardiovascular outcomes.

The neighborhood food environment is shaped by food availability and access, with the price, quality, and relative abundance of healthy foods in a neighborhood directly shaping diet quality. 1 A key component of the neighborhood food environment is the physical retail food environment, which encompasses grocery stores, corner stores, farmer’s markets, and convenience stores. 2 Prior research has demonstrated that individuals with greater access to adequate food shopping tend to have healthier dietary patterns and more favorable cardiometabolic risk profiles. 2 , 3 , 4

In the United States, individuals from racial and ethnic minority groups and those with lower socioeconomic status are more likely to live in neighborhoods with limited access to healthy food options and are disproportionately burdened by unfavorable nutrition environments. 1 These disparities are rooted in historical racial segregation and community disinvestment, which have shaped the built environment and contributed to inequities in access to healthy food. 1 The resulting neighborhood food environment, characterized by limited availability of affordable, nutritious options, influences cardiometabolic health through multiple pathways. In such contexts, individuals may be more likely to consume inexpensive, low‐quality, calorie‐dense foods leading to weight gain and cardiometabolic dysfunction. 5 , 6 Moreover, the chronic stress of inadequate neighborhood food environments may contribute to psychological distress, which may trigger chronic inflammation, result in anxiety and depression, and encourage maladaptive health behaviors, thereby increasing cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk. 5 , 6 , 7

Although prior studies have linked neighborhood food environments to CVD risk, many of these studies used objective measures of neighborhood food environments such as geographic information systems (GIS). 2 , 8 Individual perception of the neighborhood food environment has been associated with cardiometabolic risk factors, with studies demonstrating survey‐based healthy food availability to be better predictors of both hypertension and type 2 diabetes risk when compared with GIS‐based food environment. 3 , 9 , 10 , 11 Very few studies have examined the influence of perceived neighborhood food shopping in large, diverse, longitudinal cohorts. 3 , 9 , 10 Therefore, the objectives of this study are to examine the association between perceived inadequate neighborhood food shopping and incident CVD events in a multiethnic cohort and to assess whether these associations differ by race or ethnicity, education, or income.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design and Population

Data were collected from the MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis), a National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute‐sponsored, population‐based, prospective cohort study of 6814 men and women from 6 field centers across the United States. Baseline data were collected from July 2000 to August 2002 (exam 1). Participants ranged in age from 45 to 84 years and were free of clinically apparent CVD. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards at all field centers. All participants provided written informed consent at the baseline exam, and the participants were invited to complete subsequent exams and questionnaires. Further details of the study design have previously been published. 12

Measurements

Standardized questionnaires were used to gather information on age, sex, race or ethnicity, education and income levels, occupational information, smoking status, medical history, physical activity, and medication use for diabetes, lipid lowering, and hypertension. Height was measured with a stadiometer and body weight with a balance scale to the nearest 0.1 cm and 0.5 kg, respectively. Waist circumference was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm, at the minimum abdominal girth using a steel measuring tape with standard 4‐ounce tension. Seated blood pressure was taken 3 times at 1‐minute intervals following a standardized protocol. The average of the last 2 measurements were used for analysis. Total cholesterol and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and triglycerides were measured at a central laboratory (University of Vermont, Burlington, VT). At the baseline exam, participants completed a 120‐item food‐frequency questionnaire assessing frequency of consumption (times per day, week, or month) and the serving size (small, medium, or large) of each food‐frequency questionnaire item. 13 , 14 The food‐frequency questionnaire was previously validated in non‐Hispanic White, Hispanic, and Black American individuals, and evaluated against plasma lipid concentrations within the MESA cohort. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 Ultra‐processed foods were categorized according to the Nova classification system. 19 Inadequate neighborhood food shopping was measured by the question Lack of Adequate Food Shopping in Neighborhood. The possible responses were (1) very serious problem, (2) somewhat serious problem, (3) minor problem, or (4) not really a problem. The answers were reclassified into 2 categories similarly to prior studies, 20 with those who answered with responses 1 through 3 considered to have inadequate neighborhood food shopping, and those answering with response 4 considered to have adequate neighborhood food shopping. This measure is drawn from the validated MESA Neighborhood Problem Scale, which has demonstrated construct validity in capturing neighborhood conditions relevant to health and has been applied in prior studies of food environments and cardiovascular risk. 21 , 22

Cardiovascular Disease Events

Events and mortality were verified with medical records or interviews by 2 physicians from MESA. Total incident CVD was defined as nonfatal myocardial infarction, resuscitated cardiac arrest, death resulting from coronary heart disease, stroke (not transient ischemic attack), death resulting from stroke, angina, probable angina associated with coronary revascularization, and other atherosclerotic CVD death. Incident CVD events were adjudicated by the MESA mortality and morbidity review committee, with a follow‐up period of 18.5 years. Myocardial infarction required either abnormal cardiac biomarkers (>2 times upper limits of normal) regardless of chest pain or ECG findings; evolving Q waves regardless of chest pain or biomarker findings; or a combination of chest pain, ST‐T evolution or new left bundle‐branch block, and biomarker levels 1 to 2 times upper limits of normal. Resuscitated cardiac arrest was classified when a patient successfully recovered from a full cardiac arrest through cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Fatal CHD required a documented myocardial infarction within the previous 28 days, chest pain within the 72 hours before death, or a history of CHD and required the absence of a known nonatherosclerotic or noncardiac cause of death. Neurologists reviewed and classified stroke as present if there was a focal neurologic deficit lasting 24 hours or until death, with a clinically relevant lesion on brain imaging, and no nonvascular cause.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were described with continuous variables presented as mean±SD or medians (interquartile ranges) based on distribution, and categorical variables presented as frequencies (percentages). Baseline characteristics were compared between individuals with inadequate and adequate neighborhood food shopping using chi‐square tests for categorical variables and ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables. Of the 6814 baseline participants, those with missing exposure or outcome data were excluded. Missing covariates (<5% overall) were handled via listwise deletion. Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the association between inadequate neighborhood food shopping and incident CVD events. Perceived inadequate food shopping was assessed at baseline only and modeled as a fixed exposure. Model 1 was unadjusted; model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, race or ethnicity, educational attainment, and income; and model 3 was adjusted for model 2 covariates plus high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein‐cholesterol, lipid‐lowering medication use, systolic blood pressure, type 2 diabetes, diabetes medication use, tobacco use in pack‐years, moderate and vigorous physical activity, waist‐to‐height ratio, and ultra‐processed food consumption. The survival analyses evaluation period was from the MESA baseline exam to 18.5 years of follow‐up. A model that included interactions for race or ethnicity, sex, and income was assessed and subgroup analyses stratifying the cohort were conducted for significant interactions to evaluate potential effect modification. A conceptual model illustrating the hypothesized relationships among perceived neighborhood food shopping adequacy, sociodemographic factors, behavioral factors, clinical risk factors, and CVD is provided in Figure S1. Sensitivity analyses excluding participants with CVD events occurring within the first year of follow‐up were completed to address potential reverse causation and proportional hazards assumption was tested. In all analyses, P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. STATA 18.5 was used for all analyses.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics for MESA participants are displayed in Table 1. The average age of the sample was 61 years, with 3523 women and 3209 men. The sample included 39% White, 12% Chinese, 27% Black, and 22% Hispanic participants. Approximately 20% of participants reported inadequate food shopping in their neighborhoods. A higher percentage of Hispanic and Black participants (23% each) reported inadequate neighborhood food shopping compared with 18% of White and 12% of Chinese participants. Inadequate neighborhood food shopping was higher in individuals with a high school or less education (21%) compared with those with at least some college (19%). Across income groups, 22% of individuals making $0 to $25 000 a year report inadequate neighborhood food shopping, compared with 21% making $25 000 to $75 000 a year and 20% making $75 000 a year or more. Individuals who reported inadequate neighborhood food shopping also had high waist‐to‐height ratios and body mass index and consumed more servings per day of ultra‐processed foods.

DISCUSSION

This study adds to the literature by examining the relationship between perceived lack of adequate neighborhood food shopping and CVD risk in a large and diverse population. The results of this prospective cohort analysis indicate that individuals who perceived inadequate food shopping in their neighborhoods had a modest but statistically significant increased risk of CVD events. This finding contributes to growing evidence suggesting that perceived features of the neighborhood food environment may have meaningful implications for cardiovascular health.

A key strength of this study is its focus on perceived adequacy of neighborhood food shopping, a construct that is conceptually distinct from both GIS‐based measures of the food environment and household food insecurity. Although GIS‐defined food access assesses the physical presence or proximity of food outlets, the perception that neighborhood food shopping is inadequate may arise from structural issues such as disinvestment, historical segregation, lack of transportation, or low‐quality food outlets. This reflects an individual’s lived experiences, including evaluations of food quality, affordability, cultural relevance, and convenience that are not captured by density and proximity of retailers. 23 Prior studies have suggested that these subjective factors may be more closely tied to actual food purchasing behavior than objective proximity measures alone, 23 and such perceptions may be better predictors of diet quality and associated with more favorable cardiometabolic risk profiles. 2 , 3 , 4 , 11 In a study conducted by Corona et al. both perceived neighborhood accessibility and affordability of healthy foods were associated with reduced odds of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and obesity. 3 Our findings support the value of incorporating this dimension in environmental health research and the increasing evidence that the neighborhood food shopping environment, encompassing grocery stores, corner stores, farmer’s markets, convenience stores, walkable infrastructure, and other physical resources, significantly influences CVD risk. 1 , 2 , 7 , 9

Although perceptions are inherently subjective, they are shaped by and reflective of broader neighborhood and social conditions. The neighborhood food environment influences not only dietary behaviors and cardiovascular risk but also individuals’ ability to obtain sufficient, nutritious food on a consistent basis, making it a key structural determinant of food insecurity. 1 , 7 , 24 Food insecurity is defined as the condition where, at certain times during the year, households are uncertain about their ability to obtain sufficient food to meet the needs of all their members due to inadequate financial or other resources. 25 A growing body of evidence links food insecurity to poorer cardiometabolic health 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 and increased risk of CVD. 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 These associations may explain the findings of the present study, as prior research has shown that better physical access to grocery stores is associated with food security. 24 Notably, the variable in this study assessed individuals’ perception of the adequacy of food shopping in their neighborhoods, and prior studies have demonstrated that such perceptions are closely tied to experiences of food insecurity. 24 Although perceptions are not a proxy for food insecurity, they offer an important lens into how individuals interact with and are affected by their built environment. Therefore, incorporating physical dimensions of neighborhood environments such as food shopping access into cardiovascular research is essential for broadening current prevention and intervention strategies that address the upstream social and structural determinants of CVD. 9 In this way, perceptions can serve as a proxy for how structural determinants manifest at the individual level. Recognizing these perceptions as valid and informative can help bridge the gap between environmental‐level interventions and individual health outcomes.

Our findings provide evidence that inadequate neighborhood food shopping is not only a social concern but also is associated with increased CVD risk. This highlights a need for further prospective studies that might inform policy strategies that improve food retail access, such as incentives for supermarkets and grocery stores in underserved areas, expansion of community‐based food programs, and optimization of federal nutrition assistance programs. 1 , 4 At the clinical level, integrating food environment considerations into cardiovascular risk assessment may help clinicians better understand and address social determinants of health among their patients. 9 More broadly, multi‐level interventions, combining urban planning, public health, and health care approaches, may be necessary to mitigate the cardiovascular impact of inadequate food shopping environments. 9

A key limitation of this study is that perceived adequacy of neighborhood food shopping was assessed at baseline only (2000–2002) and not updated throughout the follow‐up period; changes in neighborhood conditions, gentrification, or relocation over the follow‐up period could affect the accuracy of exposure classification, potentially leading to exposure misclassification. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as reflective of food environments during that period. Although contemporary food access has evolved, extensive longitudinal evidence indicates that the structural features of neighborhood food environments have remained remarkably stable over the past several decades. 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 National data continue to show persistent disparities in physical food access, particularly in Black, Hispanic, and low‐income communities, and many structural determinants of food access such as neighborhood disinvestment, store affordability, food quality, and transportation barriers remain prevalent today. As a result, the mechanisms by which inadequate food shopping environments influence cardiovascular health continue to be relevant despite these broader shifts in retail trends. 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 Nevertheless, the exposure was assessed using a single subjective item; although this limitation should be considered, the longstanding stability of structural food access disparities supports the relevance of our findings for contemporary cardiovascular risk.

This study does have additional limitations that should be considered. Given the observational study design, there is an inability to make causal inferences between lack of adequate neighborhood food shopping and CVD events. The measure was also dichotomized, which introduces the possibility of measurement error and misclassification of the exposure. Individuals may differ in how they interpret or report neighborhood conditions based on personal expectations, cultural preferences, or unmeasured psychosocial factors. Such variability could lead to nondifferential misclassification of perceived food shopping adequacy. This measure has not been validated against objective or geographic indicators in this cohort, limiting our ability to compare subjective and objective assessments of the food environment. However, prior studies have shown that survey‐based measures yielded similar findings to GIS‐derived measures on assessing the influence of food environment on diet quality. 23 Self‐reported perceptions are also susceptible to social desirability and reporting bias, as individuals may interpret or report differently depending on personal expectations, cultural preferences, or experiences unrelated to the actual food environment. Additionally, the exposure variable does not capture the multiple dimensions of the food environment such as affordability, store quality, or variety of healthy options, which may be independently associated with CVD. Lastly, despite adjusting for a wide range of sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical factors, unmeasured confounding may remain, particularly from unmeasured factors such as neighborhood safety, transportation access, or individual food preferences, which could influence both perceptions of the food environment and CVD risk.

Despite these limitations, our findings underscore the potential relevance of individuals’ perceptions of their local food environment to cardiovascular health. Future studies should examine the interplay between perceived and objective food access over time, investigate mechanisms linking perceptions to behavior and disease, and explore how neighborhood change modifies these relationships. Incorporating both subjective and objective assessments will improve the precision of environmental health research and help guide more effective interventions aimed at reducing CVD risk through neighborhood‐level change.

CONCLUSIONS

In this large, diverse, prospective cohort study, the perceived inadequacy of neighborhood food shopping was associated with a significantly higher risk of incident CVD. These findings add to growing evidence that the neighborhood food environment plays a critical role in shaping cardiovascular health. Given the observed associations, efforts to improve the neighborhood food environment, particularly in historically underserved communities, may serve as an important strategy for reducing cardiovascular risk and health disparities. Future research should evaluate the impact of neighborhood‐level interventions and policies on cardiovascular outcomes.
