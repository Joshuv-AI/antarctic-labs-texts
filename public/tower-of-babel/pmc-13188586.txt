Human-animal interactions: starting with pets of children with cerebral palsy
Authors: Marcelo Rosales, Angela Rittler, Jill Heathcock
Published: 2026

Background

Pets are present in over 60% of American homes (American Pet Product Association, 2023), and pets are commonly described as a positive for constant companionship, social support, and increased physical activity [1–3]. Evidence across the lifespan shows that living with pets supports positive health outcomes in childhood, adulthood, and older adulthood [4–7].

Human-animal interaction and Animal-Assisted Interventions (AAI) purposefully include an animal for therapeutic purposes [8] and are often beyond having a pet in the home. These interventions include hippotherapy, service animals, support animals, animal-assisted activity, animal-assisted therapy, and animal-assisted education. These therapies are becoming more commonly integrated into treatment plans for various developmental disabilities, and preliminary findings suggest that they may yield positive results such as improving social communication, interpersonal relationships, and motor development [9–11].

Four percent of children in the United States have one or more disabilities [12]. Cerebral palsy (CP) is one of children’s most common neuromotor disorders [13]. Children with CP present with varying motor, social-emotional, and cognitive impairments that impact participation in everyday activities [14]. AAI is one intervention tool that promotes motor, communication, and psychosocial skills in children with CP [15, 16]. Additionally, having animals (e.g., pets) in the home is associated with positive health outcomes in children with developmental disabilities, such as improved family function, decreased anxiety, physical activity, play interactions, social skills, and emotional well-being [9, 17–19].

While AAI including the presence of pets has demonstrated positive results, further study is needed to understand what specific patients might benefit from AAI, what barriers (e.g., socioeconomic factors) exist to receiving AAI [1, 16], and what is the presence of pets in the homes of children with CP. Understanding these areas will help guide future research on those patients who will benefit the most from AAI and the benefits of human-animal interaction on overall health for children with disabilities. As a start to understanding an aspect of human-animal interactions, the aims were (1) describe the number and type of pets and (2) examine the relationship between the presence of pets and types of homes, socioeconomic status, Gross Motor Function Measure-66 (GMFM-66), Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS level), age, mobility, social/cognitive skills, responsibility, and happiness. This analysis was designed to quantify the occurrence and identify the type of pets in the homes of children with CP and explore factors that may or may not contribute to pets being in the home.

Methods

Participants

One hundred children aged 2–8 with cerebral palsy of all GMFCS levels participated in a randomized control trial (NCT02897024, registered September 7, 2016). Approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board (IRB16-00492) of The Ohio State and Nationwide Children’s Hospital and are in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Children were recruited through clinical follow-up appointments, informational flyers, word of mouth, email blasts, and the ResearchMatch registry. Informed consent was obtained from the parent or legal guardian for all participants. The data reported in this manuscript were collected before any intervention and no treatment effects are presented. Children were included in the study if they were between the ages of 2–8 years of age, diagnosed with a motor delay or CP in GMFCS-level I-V, and available to tolerate 2 h of therapy based on parent report. Exclusion criteria were having uncontrollable seizures or any co-morbid conditions that prevented therapy, participation in another daily treatment program, auditory or visual conditions that prevented treatment, progressive neurological disorder with no potential for improvement, or a recent surgery where participation in the study is contraindicated.

Measures

Participants were assessed using the Gross Motor Function Measure-66 (GMFM-66), and caregivers answered surveys regarding medical history that included whether they had a pet, the type of home the child lived in, and what kinds of mobility devices the child used to ambulate. If caregivers answered that pets were present in the home, the types of pets and number of pets we asked as follow-up questions. In addition, the caregivers answered the Pediatric Evaluation of Disability Inventory (PEDI-CAT), the Pediatric Outcomes Data Collection Instrument (PODCI), and Hollingshead.

GMFM

The Gross Motor Function Measure (GMFM) [20] is a gold standard outcome measure for motor function in children with cerebral palsy. The GMFM has five domains of functional skills: (1) lying and rolling, (2) sitting, (3) crawling and kneeling, (4) standing, and (5) walking, running, and jumping. Each item is administered no more than three times, and the best score is recorded. Scores for each dimension are expressed as a percentage of the maximum score for that dimension, and the total score is obtained by averaging the percentage scores across the five dimensions to assess change in gross motor function in children with cerebral palsy aged 5 months to 16 years of age. This tool measures capacity rather than performance and takes around 45 to 60 min to administer, and no special equipment is required; the therapists can use what is available in the clinic setting [21, 22]. The GMFM-66 score (Range: 0-100) was used where lower scores show worst performance.

Based on the child’s motor performance, a Gross Motor Function Classification System level (GMFCS level) is assigned to the child as a classification of motor functionality. GMFCS level (category range: I-V) was used with lower levels being more function than higher GMFCS levels.

Hollingshead

The Hollingshead four-factor index of socioeconomic status [23] is a survey designed to measure an individual’s social status based on marital status, employment, education, and occupation. This survey computes an index score, where higher scores mean higher SES levels (Hollingshed range: 11–77).

PEDI-CAT

The Pediatric Evaluation of Disability Inventory-Computer Adapted Test (PEDI-CAT) [24] is a parent-reported outcome measure for children with physical, behavioral, and cognitive conditions from birth to 21 years old. This analysis used scoring sections for mobility and transfer (Score ranges 0-100), social and cognitive skills (Score ranges 0-100), and responsibility (Score ranges 0-100) where higher scores mean greater proficiency. Wheelchair use was calculated from the caregiver report in the mobility and transfer section where caregivers state if children use a wheelchair or walker. No participant reported that a walker was used, so walkers were not used as a variable.

PODCI

The Pediatric Outcomes Data Collection Instrument (PODCI) [25] is a questionnaire measuring function and quality of life for children aged 2 to 18 years. In this study, the PODCI was completed by parents of children under 11. The PODCI is a valid and reliable tool for assessing conditions affecting physical and emotional functioning, self-image, and symptom status [26]. It includes five subscales: Upper Extremity Function (UE), Transfers and Basic Mobility (TBM), Sports and Physical Function (SPF), Comfort, Happiness, and Global. The PODCI has been reported in the literature assessing children and adolescents with various musculoskeletal disorders [27–29]. The happiness scoring section was used for the analysis where higher scores mean greater proficiency (Score ranges 0-100).

Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 29). Descriptive statistics were used to describe group statistics and the prevalence of pets in the home. Chi-squared tests, odds ratios, and general linear models were used to examine the association between pets in the home, home type, and wheelchair use. Non-responses to survey questions were omitted from the statistical calculations. When General linear models (GLM) were used, age was added to the model to control the effect of age. GLM’s were used to explore the association between pets in the home, home type, and wheelchair use further. Spearman correlations were used to examine the relationship between the presence of pets and the number of pets with GMFM-66 score (GMFM-66), GMFCS level, age, social/cognitive skills (PEDI-CAT-Cog), mobility and transfer skills (PEDI-CAT-mobility), responsibility scores (PEDI-CAT), and happiness (PODCI). These analyses were used to explore whether pet ownership is associated with increased motor function, social participation, and well-being for children with CP.

Prevalence of pets in the home

59% of participants had a pet in the home, the most common being a dog (present in 76% of homes with pets) and a cat (present in 34% of homes with pets). The total number of pets residing in a participant’s household ranged from 0 to 10 (median:1). Types of pets included dogs (n = 45), cats (n = 20), fish (n = 6), hedgehogs (n = 2), turtles (n = 2), birds (n = 1), guinea pigs (n = 1), ducks (n = 1), bearded dragons (n = 1), chinchillas (n = 1), ferrets (n = 1), and pot-belly pigs (n = 1).

Pets in the home and housing type

Compared to apartments, children who lived in homes were more likely to have pets (X2= 10.94, p < 0.01) (Table 2). Odd ratios showed that children who lived in homes were 6.9 times more likely to have a pet than those in apartments (OR 95% CI: 2.0-23.9). When controlling for age using a general linear model, housing type was still associated with pet (p < 0.001, F = 3.692).

Pets in the home and wheelchair use

Children who used wheelchairs (X2 = 4.07; p = 0.04) were likelier to have pets than those who did not (Table 3). Odd ratios showed that children who used wheelchairs were 2.7 times more likely to have a pet than those who did not (OR 95% CI: 1.0-7.2). However, it was found that age was significantly correlated with wheelchair use. Results from a general linear model assessing the association between pets in the home and wheelchair use, controlling for age; found that wheelchair use was not associated with pets in the home (p = 0.058, F = 12.976).

Pets in the home relationship to clinical measures

No relationship was found between the presence of pets and the number of pets with socioeconomic status, GMFM-66 scores, GMFCS level, age, mobility skills, social/cognitive skills, responsibility, and happiness (all p > 0.05).

Discussion

This analysis revealed several findings related to having a pet. Our results indicate that pets are common for children with CP, with dogs and cats being the most common. Functional limitations, particularly those associated with wheelchair use, did not impact the prevalence of pet ownership. Third, apartment-dwelling children are less likely to have pets, potentially due to apartment restrictions and rules. Lastly, our results found no association between developmental outcomes or social factors, and having pets.

The most common pets were dogs and cats, which is in line with the national pet owners survey [32], reporting similar findings for all Americans. Children with disabilities who have service dogs have been found to have increased psychosocial health and school/work functioning [16, 33]. While this analysis did not specifically look at service dogs, it was found that children who use wheelchairs were more likely to have a pet than those not in wheelchairs, without controlling for age. However, further analysis is needed to examine the relationship between having a pet and using a wheelchair. When age was controlled for, there was a non-significant trend in the association between having a pet in the home and using a wheelchair. Some of the participants in this study were at a young age when wheelchair use is not as prevalent. Therefore, a follow-up study is needed with a larger sample of children with CP with a broader age range, where wheelchair use is more common.

These data are limited because data from a single time point were examined and that pets could play a role in a child’s life span at older ages than our sample contains. While these data show that pets are common in the homes of children with CP, they do not support the idea that pets improve developmental outcomes or that introducing a pet into the home of a child with CP will improve their function. However, these exploratory data give directions for future studies with AAI.

Conclusions

Considerations for future research are vast, including (1) decision-making for pets in the homes of children with disabilities, (2) developing home-based interventions that incorporate pets, and (3) identifying features of AAI that support health and physical development. Another avenue of pet-related research could be examining AAI’s impact, safety, and efficacy for children without pets. National animal-assisted healthcare programs like Paws with a Cause and Pet Partners have been broadly implemented to improve health in the United States. Many aspects of AAI for children with CP need to be studied with rigorous methodologies.
