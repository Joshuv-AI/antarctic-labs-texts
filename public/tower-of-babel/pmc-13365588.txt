Electrorotation as a tool for the dielectric characterization of tumor plasma cells in multiple myeloma
Authors: Grazia Scandura, Samuele Moscato, Ilaria Dulcamare, Enrico La Spina, Caterina Dieni, Francesco Di Raimondo, Maide Bucolo, Massimo Camarda, Alessandra Romano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multiple myeloma (MM) is a hematological malignancy characterized by the clonal expansion of tumor plasma cells. Genetic alterations, metabolic adaptations, stress-response pathway activation, and membrane remodeling promote clinical heterogeneity, which complicates treatment and contributes to variable therapeutic outcomes. Given the central role of the plasma membrane in the electrokinetic properties of cells, electrical parameters such as membrane capacitance (Cm) and electrical impedance have emerged as promising, biomarker-independent indicators for cell characterization. Here, we investigated the dielectric properties of three human MM cell lines (HMCLs) with distinct metabolic profiles and sensitivity to proteasome inhibitors. Using a custom electrorotation (ROT) platform, we were able to discriminate HMCLs with different metabolic profiles, stress-responsepathway activity, and sensitivity to proteasome inhibitors. Overall, this proof-of-concept study highlights the potential of ROT as a label-free biophysical approach that may provide a complementary layer for the functional characterization of MM cells.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49392-4.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a hematologic malignancy that accounts for approximately 1% of all cancers and about 10% of blood malignancies1. It is characterized by the clonal expansion of terminally differentiated plasma cells (PCs) that produce monoclonal immunoglobulins (M proteins), which accumulate in the serum and urine of most patients2,3.

MM exhibits substantial heterogeneity at both genetic and clinical levels, with its molecular profile undergoing notable changes during the transition from precursor conditions, such as monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) or smouldering multiple myeloma (SMM), to symptomatic disease4–8. This biological complexity results in marked clonal diversity and a wide spectrum of clinical outcomes, often leading to heterogeneous responses to therapy6,7.

Recent advances in diagnostics, including SLiM-CRAB criteria, measurable residual disease (MRD) assessment, and whole-genome or single-cell sequencing, have improved early detection, risk stratification, and personalized treatment9. Therapeutic progress with immunomodulatory drugs, proteasome inhibitors, monoclonal antibodies, CAR-T cells, and bispecific antibodies has enhanced disease control in both transplant-eligible and non-eligible patients1,10–12. Nevertheless, MM remains challenging, particularly in relapsed or refractory cases, due to tumor PC adaptations such as metabolic and morphological changes, stress-response pathway activation, and alterations in membrane antigens and transporters13–18.

The plasma membrane also plays a key role in cellular electrical behaviour. Ion channels and pumps establish electrochemical gradients, while transporters mediate the movement of sodium (Na⁺), potassium (K⁺), and calcium (Ca²⁺), generating a transmembrane charge gradient. This is reflected in membrane capacitance (Cm), which depends on the electrical properties of the membrane, including the size, polarity, and charge of its molecules as well as its overall structure19,20.

These factors also affect the electrical properties of malignant cells, including Cm and electrical impedance, which have been shown to differ between malignant cells and their normal counterparts (e.g., leukemic cells vs. T lymphocytes and erythrocytes21–23. Emerging evidence suggests that variations in gene expression and resultant phenotypic states may influence cellular dielectric signatures. In particular, differences in HER2/neu expression levels in breast cancer cell lines have been associated with distinct dielectric profiles measured by electrorotation (ROT)24,25. Similarly, dielectrophoresis (DEP) studies have shown that shifts in cell fate linked to transcriptional programs correlate with unique dielectric responses, supporting the concept that dielectric measurements may reflect underlying molecular phenotypes26.

Advances in microfluidic and on-chip platforms have enabled a broad range of label-free sensing strategies, including optical, electrical, and hydrodynamic methods, for high-precision single-cell and multi-cell analysis26–29. Among these, electrokinetic techniques such as ROT and DEP have proven particularly effective for quantifying the dielectric properties of biological cells.

Under an alternating electric field, if the induced dipole is approximately aligned with the field, the particle experiences a dielectrophoretic force (F_DEP)30. Conversely, when subjected to a rotating electric field, the induced dipole is not aligned with the field direction, generating a rotational torque T(ω) on the particle21.

The rotational behaviour of cells depends on intrinsic electrical properties such as Cm, membrane conductivity, and cytoplasm conductivity, which can be derived from ROT measurements31–36. Moreover, Kramers–Kronig relations describe a direct relationship between the frequency dependence of the ROT torque T(ω) and the DEP force F_DEP, as regions of maximal F_DEP frequency often coincide with ROT maxima37–41 (Supplementary Fig. S1). A detailed theoretical description of ROT principles and equations is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

ROT provides a valuable tool for cell characterization and functional discrimination between cell populations, as it enables non-invasive measurement of cellular electrical properties, such as Cm and cytoplasm conductivity. Compared with conventional MM biomarker detection methods, ROT offers several advantages: unlike flow cytometry, which requires fluorescent labelling, and next-generation or single-cell sequencing, which are time-consuming, costly, and destructive to cells, ROT is a label-free technology capable of probing functional heterogeneity at the single-cell level.

Building on these principles, we applied ROT to characterize the dielectric properties of human myeloma cell lines (HMCLs) with different cellular phenotypes. Specifically, we aimed to (i) assess the dielectric behaviour of HMCLs under rotating electric field conditions and (ii) quantify Cm across representative HMCLs. This strategy provides a label-free functional approach to discriminate between cell lines and complements existing molecular and morphological analyses.

Materials and methods

Cell cultures

Experiments were performed using three immortalized HMCLs: NCI-H929, OPM2, and U266, kindly provided by Amodio’s laboratory. Cell culture conditions were adapted from previously published protocols42. NCI-H929 and OPM2 cells were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 88365) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Gibco™, 10091148), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco™, 15140122), and 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco™, 25030081), whereas U266 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 20% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum and the same antibiotics and L-glutamine concentrations. All cells were incubated in standard growth conditions at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂.

Bortezomib (BTZ; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was used at a concentration of 15 nM for 24 hours.

Sample preparation and HPLC analysis of metabolites

Metabolite extraction and HPLC analysis were performed as previously described43.

For metabolic analysis, 3 × 106 cells were harvested and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. The cells were then deproteinized using ice-cold HPLC-grade acetonitrile (CH₃CN) mixed with 10 mM KH₂PO₄ (pH 7.40) in a 3:1 (v/v) ratio, and agitated for 60 s. After deproteinization, the sample was centrifuged at 12,400 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting clear supernatant was then combined with HPLC-grade chloroform, mixed, and centrifuged again at maximum speed for 5 min. The upper aqueous phase was collected for HPLC analysis.

This analysis included the simultaneous separation and quantification of high-energy phosphates, oxidized and reduced nicotinamide coenzymes, glycosylated UDP-derivatives, nitrite and nitrate, as well as purines and pyrimidines. Amino acids and amino group-containing compounds were separated and quantified using chromatographic conditions established in our laboratory. Both analyses were carried out with an HPLC system consisting of a Spectra System P4000 pump, a Hypersil C-18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm particle size, with a guard column), and a UV6000 LP diode array detector with a 5 cm light path flow cell (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rodano, Milano, Italy).

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR for gene expression analysis

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR were carried out as previously reported14.

mRNA expression levels were measured by quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR). Total RNA was extracted from cells using Trizol® reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen). The extracted RNA was then quantified, and its quality and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ThermoScientific) before proceeding with cDNA synthesis (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Milan, Italy) as the manufacturer’s protocol. RT-qPCR was performed using the 7900HT Fast Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher) with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Life Technologies).

The expression levels of the target genes HSPA5 (Fw: TAGCGTATGGTGCTGCTGTC; Rv: TGACACCTCCCACAGTTTCA), ATF4 (Fw: GCTAAGGCGGGCTCCTCCGA; Rv: ACCCAACAGGGCATCCAAGTCG), HMOX1 (Fw: ACTGCGTTCCTGCTCAACAT; Rv: GGGGCAGAATCTTGCACTTT), TLR4 (Fw: AAGCCGAAAGGTGATTGTTG; Rv: CTGAGCAGGGTCTTCTCCAC); p62/SQSTM1 (Fw: GGGGCGCCCTGAGGAACAGA; Rv: CCTGGTGAGCCAGCCGCCTT); MFN1 (Fw: GGCATCTGTGGCCGAGTT; Rv: ATTATGCTAAGTCTCCGCTCCAA); MFN2 (Fw: GCTCGGAGGCACATGAAAGT; Rv: ATCACGGTGCTCTTCCCATT); OPA1 (Fw: GTGCTGCCCGCCTAGAAA; Rv: TGACAGGCACCCGTACTCAGT); DRP1 (Fw: TGGGCGCCGACATCA; Rv: GCTCTGCGTTCCCACTACGA), FIS1 (Fw: TACGTCCGCGGGTTGCT; Rv: CCAGTTCCTTGGCCTGGTT) were detected by SYBR Green fluorescence. For each sample, the expression levels of the target mRNAs were normalized to the housekeeping gene B2M (Fw: AGCAGCATCATGGAGGTTTG; Rv: AGCCCTCCTAGAGCTACCTG) using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Mitochondrial assessment

Mitochondrial staining was performed using the MitoTracker RED CMXRos probe (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Milan, Italy), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were stained with 200 nM of the MitoTracker RED CMXRos for 30 min at 37 °C. After incubation, cells were washed three times and analysed using a flow cytometry MACSQuant Analyzer 10 (Miltenyi Biotec) and analysed using FlowLogic software (Miltenyi Biotech)30.

Identification of DEGs and transcriptomic analysis

Total RNA was extracted from HMCLs using the ReliaPrep™ RNA Miniprep System (Promega Corporation). RNA quantity, purity, and integrity were assessed using the Agilent 5400 system, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All samples passed quality control, with RNA integrity number (RIN) values ranging from 8.3 to 9.9, indicating high-quality RNA suitable for downstream sequencing.

RNA sequencing was performed on three independent biological replicates per experimental condition. Library preparation and sequencing were carried out by a commercial service provider (Novogene) following standard Illumina protocols.

Raw sequencing reads were quality-checked using FastQC, aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using STAR, and gene-level counts were generated using featureCounts.

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package in R. Raw count data were normalized using the median-of-ratios method implemented in DESeq2. Genes with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and an absolute log₂ fold change > 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

The resulting differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were subjected to over-representation analysis (ORA) for Gene Ontology (GO) terms. A q-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Cell viability assessment

Cells were incubated with complete medium (Control) or ROT buffer (9.5% Sucrose, 0.1 mg/mL Dextrose, 0.1% BSA, 0.1 mM calcium acetate [(CH3COO)2Ca]36,45 at room temperature for 1 and 3 hours. Prior to use, the buffer was sterile-filtered through a 0.22 μm pore-size syringe filter (Millex, Millipore; Biosigma S.p.A., Cona, Italy) and adjusted to a final pH of 7 and a final conductivity of 33 mS/m, as previously described45.

Then, cell viability was assessed using the Annexin V/7-AAD assay kit (BioLegend, Belgium) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were washed with PBS and resuspended in 100 µL of cold binding buffer. Then, 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL of 7-AAD were added to the cell suspension and mixed gently. Cells were incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Finally, 400 µL of binding buffer was added to terminate the staining, and the percentage of viable cells was determined by flow cytometry MACSQuant Analyzer 10 (Miltenyi Biotech).

ROT characterization

To perform ROT experiments, HMCLs were resuspended in a low-conductivity suspension buffer whose composition and physicochemical properties were described above. More specifically, the cells were suspended at a concentration of 500 cells/µL in ROT buffer (9.5% sucrose, 0.1 mg/mL dextrose, 0.1% BSA, 0.1 mM calcium acetate [(CH₃COO)₂Ca]), which was filtered using a Millex Millipore syringe filter with 0.22 μm pores (Biosigma S.p.A., Cona, Italy). The conductivity was adjusted to a final value of 33 mS/m using a 0.1 mM potassium chloride solution, measured with an Electric Conductometer DDS Digital Lab (DDS-307, Changzhou W&J Instrument Co., Ltd., Changzhou, Jiangsu, China)45.

The ROT system developed by the startup STLab srl in collaboration with the Department of Electrical, Electronics, and Computer Science Engineering (DIEEI) of the University of Catania is composed of three key elements: (1) a custom quadrature amplifier (QAMP), (2) a ROT-chip where the suspended cells are injected, and (3) a custom algorithm for the automatic extraction of the cell rotations.

The working principle of the system is illustrated in Fig. 1. A standard, compact, and inexpensive waveform generator, controlled via PC, generates two low-power 90° phase-shifted sinusoidal signals (Fig. 1A). These signals are processed by the QAMP that generates four high-power, quadrature sinusoidal signals (Fig. 1B). The four quadrature signals are connected to the ROT-chip via SMA cables (Fig. 1C), where the rotating electric field is generated on a 400 μm circular area. Mounted on the microscope, the ROT-chip incorporates a rubber O-ring positioned at the center between the electrodes, forming a fluidic chamber for cell suspension during ROT spectra measurements. Using a calibrated pipette, 15 µL of the cell suspension was loaded into the device, filling the O-ring chamber. A cover slide was then placed on top to close the chamber and minimize cell movement. Cell rotation was monitored using a digital camera and recorded as a video for subsequent offline analysis.

The acquisition of a complete ROT spectrum for a single cell typically requires approximately 1 min, and the overall ROT characterization was completed within two hours after cell resuspension.

A MATLAB script running on the PC performs a frequency sweep. For each step of the frequency sweep, a specific number of frames were captured and stored. After the frequency sweep was completed, the collected frames were analysed using a custom algorithm, and the characteristic ROT spectra of the cells was extracted (Fig. 1). Finally, Fig. 1B and C show the experimental setup and the ROT-chip used, respectively.

Membrane capacitance (Cm) calculation

The Cm was calculated from the membrane permittivity (εmem) of each analysed cell line, derived using the single-shell model. This parameter was obtained by fitting the experimental ROT spectra, assuming a membrane thickness of d = 8 nm, which lies within the physiological range of 5–10 nm44–47.

where εo is the vacuum permittivity, εmem represents the estimated membrane permittivity, and d is the membrane thickness. For each cell line, Cm values were derived from the best fit of experimental ROT spectra obtained from approximately 30 individual cells.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 11.00 or RStudio version v2023.06.1. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) unless otherwise stated. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and symbols used to indicate statistical differences are described in the figure legends.

For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or repeated-measures ANOVA was applied, as appropriate, followed by Holm–Šídák or Tukey–Kramer post hoc tests for multiple comparisons.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on metabolite data using the “corrplot,” “FactoMineR,” and “Factoextra” R packages. Explained variance and the quality of variable representation on the factor map, expressed as squared cosines (Cos²), were evaluated. PCA results are presented as biplots with variables represented as color-coded vectors.

For ROT measurements, approximately 30 individual cells were analysed per cell line, reflecting the limited sample size due to the intrinsically low throughput of the ROT setup.

ROT data were summarized using descriptive statistics (mean ± SEM). For the Cm parameter, statistical comparisons between groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by appropriate post hoc tests, as described above.

No formal tests of normality were imposed beyond standard assumptions commonly adopted for exploratory analyses with limited sample sizes.

Results

HMCLs differ in metabolism and stress-response pathways

The in vitro experiments were conducted on three HMCLs: NCI-H929, OPM2, and U266. To assess their metabolic characteristics, individual metabolites were analysed using HPLC followed by PCA. This analysis revealed that PC1 plays a crucial role in distinguishing OPM2 cells from both NCI-H929 and U266 cells (Fig. 2A). Notably, the most significant contributors to PC1 were ADP, ATP, GTP, NAD+, NADH, and the NAD+/NADH ratio, highlighting the impact of energy metabolism on the distinctive metabolic profiles of these cells (Fig. 2B). Consistently, OPM2 cells displayed higher levels of energetic nucleotides (GMP, ADP, GDP, ATP, CTP, GTP, and UTP) and an increased NAD⁺/NADH and NADP⁺/NADPH ratio compared with NCI-H929 and U266 cells, consistent with differences in mitochondrial redox states (Fig. 3A). These findings were further supported by gene expression analysis using RT-qPCR, which showed elevated expression of genes involved in mitochondrial homeostasis, particularly those regulating mitochondrial fusion (MFN1 and MFN2), fission (DRP1 and FIS1), and biogenesis (OPA1) (Fig. 3B). Similarly, Mitotracker staining revealed higher mitochondrial mass in OPM2 cells (Fig. 3C). Additionally, we observed differences in the expression of genes related to autophagy (P62) and stress response (HSPA5, ATF4, HMOX1), which further distinguish OPM2 cells from NCI-H929 and U266 cells (Fig. 3B). Finally, we assessed HMOX1 mRNA levels after treatment with BTZ. BTZ significantly increased HMOX1 expression in all three cell lines, with the highest expression observed in OPM2 compared to the other two cell lines (Fig. 3D). Overall, these results indicate that the OPM2 cells differ significantly from NCI-H929 and U266 cells in mitochondrial function and cellular stress response pathways following BTZ treatment.

HMCLs display distinct transcriptomic signatures enriched for plasma membrane organization

To investigate the dielectric properties of HMCLs, we analysed their transcriptomic profiles. Correlation analysis revealed distinct clustering among the cell lines, with OPM2 displaying a unique transcriptomic profile compared to the other two (Supplementary Fig. S2).

We performed differential expression analysis between NCI-H929 and OPM2, identifying 7,648 significant DEGs (adjusted p-value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change > 1). Similarly, comparison between U266 and OPM2 revealed 7,099 significant DEGs (adjusted p-value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change > 1).

DEGs were then analysed using traditional GO enrichment analysis, which includes three categories: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). ORA revealed enriched GO terms related to plasma membrane organization (GO:0007009) and structure, such as ruffle organization (GO:0031529) and plasma membrane invagination (GO:0099024), as well as transport processes across the plasma membrane. These included terms like inorganic ion import across the plasma membrane (GO:0099587) and voltage-gated calcium channel complex (GO:0005891), which were identified in both cell lines. Additionally, we found GO terms significantly enriched for solute symporter activity (GO:0015370), regulation of transporter activity (GO:0032409), and peptide transport (GO:0015833; Fig. 4; Supplementary Table S1–S2).

ROT buffer has a slight impact on cell viability

Characterization using the ROT technique involves suspending cells in a low-conductivity buffer that allows rotation when an electric field is applied at room temperature.

Since the main factors affecting cell viability and cellular alterations during ROT measurements are the physicochemical properties of the suspension buffer and the duration of exposure, we assessed the impact of the ROT buffer on cell viability in the three HMCLs after incubation at room temperature for 1 and 3 h.

Cell viability was not affected in NCI-H929 cells resuspended in the ROT buffer compared to the control after 1 h of incubation (Fig. 5A). However, a slight reduction of viability was observed in OPM2 and U266 cells compared to the control (mean percentage reduction of 7.55 ± 0.12 SEM for the OPM2 cell line and 4.86 ± 0.79 SEM for the U266 cell line; Fig. 5B, C).

After 3 hours, cell viability remained largely unchanged in NCI-H929 and OPM2 cells relative to control (Fig. 6A, B), whereas U266 cells showed a significant reduction compared with the control (mean percentage reduction of 16.41 ± 4.60 SEM; Fig. 6C). HMCLs show distinct dielectric properties revealed by ROT analysis

To derive the ROT characteristic spectra and assess the dielectric properties of HMCLs under a rotating electric field, the videos acquired during ROT experiments were analysed using the Pixel-Intensity Algorithm (PxI) described in42. The Single-Shell Model41 was then employed to estimate the dielectric parameters of the cells, and a least-squares fitting procedure was applied to minimize the discrepancy between the experimentally measured ROT spectra and the theoretical predictions. ROT spectra of the three representative HMCLs, reported in Fig. 7A, showed a reasonable approximation between experimental results and theoretical predictions.

ROT analysis revealed a frequency shift among the three HMCLs, with a maximum rotation frequency of approximately 250 kHz for NCI-H929 cells, 150 kHz for U266 cells, and 100 kHz for OPM2 cells (Fig. 7A).

Area-specific Cm values derived from the model further highlighted electrical differences among the three cell lines, reflecting variations in plasma membrane electrical properties. OPM2 cells showed the highest Cm value (Cm = 8.97 ± 1.47 SEM) compared to U266 (Cm = 5.83 ± 1.14 SEM) and NCI-H929 cell lines (Cm = 4.67 ± 0.71 SEM; Supplementary Table S3), indicating significant differences from both NCI-H929 and U266 cell lines (Fig. 7B). In contrast, no significant difference was detected between NCI-H929 and U266 cells (p = 0.073), which may reflect the limited sample size rather than the absence of a true biological effect.

Taken together, these results indicate that ROT analysis allows discrimination of HMCLs based on dielectric properties associated with Cm.

Discussion

In MM, adaptive changes in PCs involving cellular metabolism, stress-response pathways, and plasma membrane organization contribute to drug resistance and disease recurrence13–18,48. Relapsed MM patients often respond poorly to subsequent therapies; therefore, achieving minimal residual disease (MRD) negativity, assessed by next-generation flow cytometry (NGF) or next-generation sequencing (NGS), is a key prognostic factor for progression-free and overall survival49–51. However, these approaches are costly, time-consuming, and require large numbers of bone marrow cells51. In addition, variability in membrane antigen expression and the lack of a unique specific marker further limit the standardization of label-based techniques53.

Recently, Musso et al.53 reviewed the use of DEP as a label-free approach for cell manipulation, showing its potential to discriminate cancer cells from healthy ones in peripheral blood samples based on phenotypic properties and membrane capacitance. Building on this concept, our study provides novel insights by demonstrating, for the first time, that HMCLs exhibit distinct dielectric properties when analysed with the ROT technique. These findings reveal functional heterogeneity among malignant PCs and highlight the value of biophysical characterization as a complementary dimension to conventional molecular and immunophenotypic analyses.

Although HMCLs represent a simplified in vitro model of MM, they remain widely used to dissect cellular, molecular, and genetic mechanisms, and to standardize novel experimental approaches54. Here, we selected three HMCLs to characterize their dielectric behaviour with ROT, providing a first detailed assessment of their biophysical diversity. Although limited in number, these cell lines capture biologically distinct MM contexts, offering an initial framework for future extension to primary patient samples.

To reflect patient variability, we first assessed metabolic heterogeneity among HMCLs. Consistent with previous reports of metabolic and stress-related diversity in MM cells14,52,5556,57, the three cell lines displayed distinct profiles. OPM2 cells showed higher levels of energetic nucleotides (GMP, ADP, GDP, ATP, CTP, GTP, UTP), increased mitochondrial activity, and elevated ER stress markers (HSPA5, ATF4), along with glycosylated UDP derivatives, consistent with features previously associated with reduced BTZ sensitivity14. BTZ-induced HO-1 upregulation in OPM2 cells was higher than in the other cell lines, consistent with previous reports linking the TLR4/HO-1/CO axis to drug resistance mechanisms55,56,57.

Transcriptomic analysis confirmed clearly distinct expression signatures between OPM2 and the other two cell lines. In particular, GO enrichment analysis of DEGs highlighted terms related to plasma membrane organization and transport processes, including inorganic ion import, solute symporter activity, and peptide transport, suggesting that these molecular differences may underlie the observed variations in dielectric behaviour19.

Among DEGs, selected ion channels (e.g., KCNN4 and P2RX7), together with cytoskeletal scaffolding proteins (e.g., MYH9 and ANXA1), may contribute to the observed dielectric differences and represent plausible molecular correlates of variations in area-specific Cm, given their known roles in membrane dynamics and structural organization57.

In addition, exploratory correlation analyses suggested associations between stress and metabolic markers (ATF4, P62, HSPA5) and biophysical parameters (Supplementary Table S4-S5), supporting a potential link between intracellular stress states and dielectric properties. This interpretation is consistent with previous evidence indicating that heterogeneous expression and functional reliance on p62/SQSTM1, as well as differences in autophagic activity and vacuole accumulation under stress conditions, can influence cytoplasmic conductivity by modulating ionic mobility and macromolecular crowding41,58,60,65. While these associations remain correlative, they provide a biologically plausible framework linking intracellular stress and membrane organization to dielectric properties, warranting further functional validation. Therefore, future studies will be required to directly measure and disentangle membrane-associated from cytoplasmic contributions.

From a biophysical perspective, at low frequencies, cellular electrical behaviour is largely influenced by structural features of the plasma membrane, including microvilli, folds, and ruffles, as well as by the overall membrane area. Accordingly, Cm primarily reflects the presence and integrity of these superficial structures rather than variations in protein/lipid composition or membrane thickness35,41,59–61. Experimental factors, including excessive membrane charging, non-physiological buffers, Joule heating, shear stress, and electroporation may also influence measurements62. In this study, ROT measurements were performed within electroporation-safe ranges62–64 and under buffer exposure times compatible with preserved cell viability, supporting the interpretation that the observed differences in ROT spectra and Cm values primarily reflect intrinsic cellular properties rather than experimental artifacts.

Under these conditions, each HMCL exhibited a distinct ROT spectrum, with maximum rotation frequencies around 250 kHz for NCI-H929, 150 kHz for U266, and 100 kHz for OPM2, allowing clear discrimination among lines. Area-specific Cm calculations further supported differences in membrane morphology and structural organization between OPM2 cells and the other two cell lines20, whereas no significant difference was observed between NCI-H929 and U266 cells, likely reflecting limited statistical power due to the small sample size rather than the absence of a true biological difference. Notably, NCI-H929 cells displayed a lower Cm value, consistent with previous evidence linking reduced membrane folding and surface features to lower effective Cm65,66.

These intrinsic differences in membrane structure and dielectric properties indicate that ROT captures functional heterogeneity among myeloma cell lines, providing information beyond the mere distinction between malignant and non-malignant cells. Unlike previous studies primarily using electrokinetic techniques for this purpose67, our results position ROT as a rapid, label-free approach to explore phenotypic variability in MM.

While these findings highlight the potential of ROT, several limitations should be considered. The study relied exclusively on established HMCLs, with no primary patient samples or mixed-cell/MRD-relevant experiments. Additionally, U266 cells displayed sensitivity to prolonged buffer exposure, emphasizing the need to optimize experimental conditions. Finally, single-cell ROT throughput, operational standardization, and instrument accessibility remain practical bottlenecks for clinical translation.

Despite these limitations, this study provides proof-of-concept evidence that ROT captures intrinsic differences in membrane dielectric properties and functional states, complementing traditional molecular and immunophenotypic analyses. Combining ROT with high-dimensional approaches such as NGS or NGF could offer a complementary strategy to further investigate MM heterogeneity and inform future translational studies.
