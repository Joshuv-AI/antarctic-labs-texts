MXene-assisted Bi2Te3 pellet-based thermoelectric generator
Authors: Syed Sheraz Ali, Samiya Firdous, Sokhna Dieng, Jhonathan Prieto Rojas, Tawfik A. Saleh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A thermoelectric generator (TEG) was fabricated using n-type and p-type Bi2Te3 pellets mounted on a polyamide/Kapton substrate through low-temperature soldering. Ti3C2Tx MXene was synthesized via selective etching and delamination of the corresponding MAX phase, followed by structural and morphological characterization to confirm the successful formation of layered conductive nanosheets. A physically attached Ti3C2Tx MXene backing layer was introduced to investigate its influence on thermoelectric output performance under externally applied thermal gradients. The fabricated devices exhibited an approximately linear increase in output voltage with increasing temperature difference (ΔT). At a ΔT of 76 °C, the original TEG generated an open-circuit voltage of 19.6 mV, whereas the MXene-assisted configuration achieved 22.3 mV, corresponding to an enhancement of approximately 13.8%. Load-dependent measurements further revealed an increase in maximum output power from 94.08 nW to 124.32 nW under matched load resistance conditions, representing an improvement of approximately 32%. The MXene-assisted TEG also exhibited an increased normalized areal power density compared with the pristine device, further demonstrating the beneficial effect of the MXene interfacial layer. The enhanced thermoelectric performance is attributed to the high electrical conductivity and layered morphology of Ti3C2Tx MXene, which contribute to improved thermal distribution and interfacial heat management within the TEG architecture. These findings demonstrate the potential of MXene-assisted thermoelectric systems for energy harvesting applications.

Introduction

The swift expansion of the Internet of Things (IoT), wearable electronics, and distributed sensor networks has driven a growing demand for reliable, maintenance-free power sources capable of long-term autonomous operation.1–3 Conventional battery-based systems suffer from limited lifetime, frequent replacement requirements, and recycling challenges, particularly in large-scale or inaccessible deployments.4 In response to this demand, thermoelectric generators (TEGs), which directly convert thermal gradients into electrical energy through the Seebeck effect, have gained significant attention as promising candidates for self-powered and maintenance-free energy harvesting systems owing to their solid-state nature, absence of moving components, robust reliability, and noise-free operation.5,6 TEGs are particularly attractive for applications including low-grade heat sources, such as the human body, industrial pipelines, and environmental heat, where continuous and sustainable energy harvesting is required.7–9 However, real-world application of TEGs is often limited by factors including poor thermal contact with heat sources, interfacial losses, and the trade-off between device flexibility and thermoelectric performance.10,11 While thermoelectric devices have been developed to improve conformability to curved surfaces, the use of low thermal conductivity substrates and interfacial inefficiencies can significantly reduce effective temperature gradients and overall device efficiency.12

Thermoelectric generators (TEGs) have attracted considerable attention for sustainable energy harvesting owing to their ability to directly convert waste heat into electricity. Recent advances have expanded their applications from building energy harvesting to wearable electronics, self-powered sensing, electronic skins, and continuous solar energy harvesting, highlighting their broad potential for next-generation energy-autonomous systems.13–17

Among the commercially available thermoelectric materials, Bi2Te3-based compounds are widely recognized for their excellent performance near room temperature, making them the preferred choice for flexible and low-temperature thermoelectric generators.18,19 As a result, Bi2Te3 has been extensively utilized in applications involving wearable electronics and low-grade waste heat harvesting. Despite these advantages, interfacial electrical and thermal losses at the junctions between Bi2Te3 and adjacent components continue to limit the overall energy conversion efficiency of TEG devices.

Recent advances in two-dimensional materials have positioned MXenes as attractive candidates for interface engineering owing to their high electrical conductivity, layered morphology, and tunable surface chemistry.20–25 Typically represented by the general formula Mn+1AXn, MXenes such as Ti3C2Tx are synthesized through selective etching of the A-layer from Ti3AlC2, resulting in layered nanosheets terminated with functional groups (–OH, –O, –F). These surface terminations, combined with their high electrical conductivity and large specific surface area, enable strong interfacial adhesion and efficient charge transport across heterogeneous junctions.26,27 The layered morphology facilitates rapid carrier mobility and conformal coating over irregular surfaces, making MXenes particularly suitable for interfacial engineering. Owing to these properties, MXenes have demonstrated significant potential across a wide range of applications, including energy storage devices, sensors, electrocatalysis, soft actuators, and wearable electronics.28–30

In thermoelectric systems, MXenes have been explored primarily as secondary phases in composite materials and thin-film devices to enhance charge transport and reduce thermal conductivity through interfacial phonon scattering mechanisms.31–35 The incorporation of MXene nanosheets into conventional thermoelectric matrices such as Bi2Te3-, Sb2Te3-, SnSe-, and GeTe-based systems has been shown to enhance electrical transport through carrier injection while simultaneously suppressing lattice thermal conductivity, thereby improving overall thermoelectric performance. Additionally, MXene-based thin films and composite devices have demonstrated improved flexibility and moderate power outputs in the range of nW to µW; however, their relatively low Seebeck coefficient limits their standalone thermoelectric efficiency.36 Owing to their high electrical conductivity and surface functionalities that facilitate efficient interfacial interactions, MXenes are hypothesized to serve as effective conductive interfacial layers that enhance charge transport and minimize contact losses in pellet-based thermoelectric generators. Despite these advancements, the application of MXene as a dedicated interfacial layer in bulk pellet-based thermoelectric generators remains largely unexplored. Unlike composite-based approaches, where excessive MXene content can adversely affect electrical transport, the present work employs MXene as an interfacial layer, enabling improved charge transport without compromising the intrinsic properties of Bi2Te3 pellets. Therefore, leveraging the high conductivity and interfacial compatibility of MXene at junction interfaces presents a promising approach to reduce contact resistance and enhancing overall device performance. Polyimide-based thermoelectric generators (TEGs) enable enhanced flexibility and improved thermal management through vertical placement, facilitating better heat dissipation and conformal contact with heat sources.

Experimental

Material and methods

To synthesize MAX precursors, powders of Ti (99.5%, −325 mesh, Alfa Aesar), Al (99.5%, −325 mesh, Alfa Aesar), C (graphite, 99%, −325 mesh, Alfa Aesar), and TiC (99.5%, typically 2 µm, Alfa Aesar) were utilized. Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 36.5–38%, Fisher Chemical), hydrofluoric acid (HF, 48.5–51%, Acros Organics), lithium chloride (LiCl, 99%, Acros Organics), were used in their pure form for the synthesis, etching, and delamination of MXene. Commercial Bi2Te3 pellets (Goodfellow, UK) were used as the thermoelectric material.

Synthesis of MAX precursor: Ti3AlC2

The precursor powder, in the ratios provided in Table 1 were mixed with 10 mm zirconia balls in a 2 : 1 ratio. The mixtures were placed in plastic containers and ball-milled at 50 rpm for 18 h. Afterward, the powder mixtures were transferred to alumina crucibles and put into a furnace that was heated to a high temperature as indicated in Table 3. Argon was passed through the furnace an hour before heating until the reaction was complete. Both the heating and cooling processes were carried out at a constant rate of 3 °C per minute, with a 2 h holding period. After cooling, the samples were ground using a TiN-coated milling bit and sieved to produce particles smaller than 75 µm.

Synthesis of MXene: Ti3C2Tx

MAX phase powders were etched and delaminated using the previously reported protocols.37–39 An etchant solution was prepared by mixing 2 mL of HF (49% solution), 12 mL of HCl (36% solution), and 6 mL of DI water in a polyethylene bottle. The solution was stirred at room temperature for a few minutes. To prevent overheating from the exothermic reaction, 1 g of MAX phase powder was slowly added over 5 minutes. The temperature was then increased to 35 °C, and the reaction was continuously stirred at 350 rpm for 24 hours. After the etching reaction, the resulting mixture was washed repeatedly with DI water until the pH was close to neutral (pH 6). The collected sediment was added to 20 mL of DI water containing 1 g of LiCl. This mixture was stirred at 350 rpm for 24 hours at a temperature of 35 °C. Following the delamination reaction, the reaction mixture was washed with DI water using centrifugation at 3500 rpm for 10 minutes, discarding the clear supernatant and resuspending the sediment in water until the supernatant became dark. The dark supernatant indicates successful delamination of multilayer into single to few-layer MXene flakes and was freeze-dried for further use. To get the MXene film, the neutral supernatant MXene solution obtained after centrifuging was vacuum filtered and dried. A Ti3C2Tx MXene film measuring 3 cm × 1 cm was cut from the as-prepared film and used as the interfacial layer in the TEG.

Pellet preparation

The bulk Bi2Te3-based thermoelectric pellets, comprising both p-type and n-type materials, were procured from commercial vendors.40,41 These pellets were pre-fabricated into uniform cuboid or cylindrical geometries. Each pellet had dimensions of approximately 1.4 × 1.4 × 1.6 mm3 (L × W × H). This specific geometry was selected to provide an optimized surface area-to-volume ratio, facilitating efficient thermal and electrical contact within the thermoelectric module and thereby enhancing device performance. The compact size of the pellets ensures uniform heat distribution and allows for high packing density, making them suitable for miniaturized TEG configurations. Fabrication of pellet-based TEG and MXene based TEG

The fabrication of the original thermoelectric generator (TEG) begins with designing and printing a serpentine pattern on a copper-coated polyimide sheet. The sheet is cut into a serpentine shape. The unwanted copper is then removed using ferric chloride etching, leaving behind the required conductive pathways that define the TEG structure. The layout consists of 5 thermoelectric (TE) pairs with alternating n-type and p-type configurations.

Following the preparation of the substrate, commercially available Bi2Te3-based pellets (n-type and p-type) are assembled onto the patterned copper tracks. The pellets are placed in an alternating n–p configuration along the structure. The pellets are then soldered using low-temperature solder (such as Sn–Pb or Sn–Ag alloy) and a temperature-controlled soldering iron to establish electrical connections.

Finally, the entire TEG (7 cm × 2 cm) is mounted onto a Kapton tape base to provide mechanical support and electrical insulation. The positive and negative terminals are defined at the ends of the module, allowing measurement of voltage output under varying temperature conditions.

The MXene-based TEG is fabricated using MXene in conjunction with the TEG on Kapton tape to measure the optimized TEG performance. MXene films are cut and pasted to the TEG. A Ti3C2Tx MXene film was positioned at the interface between the thermoelectric module and the heat source and secured using Kapton tape to ensure stable contact during the measurements. No additional conductive adhesive or silver paste was used. The temperature difference (ΔT) was determined from the hot-plate temperature and the ambient room temperature. The electrical output (open-circuit voltage and current) was measured through the positive and negative terminals of both the pristine TEG and the MXene-assisted TEG. The positive and negative terminals are used to measure the electrical values upon variation of temperature.

Material characterization

The XRD pattern were collected from 5° to 80° (2θ) with a step size of 0.04. A D/teX Ultra detector with a Cu Kα emitter (λ ≈ 1.5418 Å) was employed along with an Ultima IV X-ray diffractometer to determine the crystalline phases and record the XRD patterns. The surface morphologies of the materials were studied using FE-SEM QUANTA FEG 250 (FEI, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Electrical conductivity was measured using the four-probe method to be 9000 S cm−1. The electrical conductivity of the synthesized Ti3C2Tx MXene was measured to assess its effectiveness as a conductive interfacial layer in the TEG.

Results and discussion

The fabricated TEG module was evaluated under applied thermal gradients by measuring the generated voltage across the device, followed by power estimation using an external load resistance. The voltage output exhibited a linear dependence on temperature difference, confirming the stable thermoelectric behavior of the device. Subsequently, Ti3C2 MXene, as shown in Fig. 2(a), was introduced as an interfacial coating at the soldered copper junctions, and the measurements were repeated under identical conditions. The MXene-integrated device demonstrated an enhanced voltage output and improved power generation compared to the original configuration. The enhanced performance is attributed to the high electrical conductivity and interfacial properties of MXene, while literature reports suggest that MXene can influence thermal transport and heat distribution at interfaces,43 potentially contributing to the observed improvement. Additionally, the conformal nature of the MXene coating contributes to improved electrical continuity and reduced localized thermal losses, thereby enhancing the overall thermoelectric performance of the TEG.

(a) MXene film, (b) room-temperature XRD patterns for Ti3C2 MXene.

At ambient conditions, XRD was done for Ti3AlC2 MAX powder and its corresponding etched Ti3C2 MXene, as shown in Fig. 2(b). The main peak in the MAX phase at (104) at 2θ = 39° disappears in Ti3C2 MXene, indicating successful extraction of the Al atomic layer from the Ti3AlC2 structure. Also, the characteristic peak corresponding to the (002) crystal plane shifts towards a smaller angle, from 9.48° to 7.27°, and broadens, as shown in the inset, which clearly illustrates the shift. This also confirms the MXene XRD pattern from the previous works.44–46 This shift is attributed to an increase in the c-lattice parameter due to delamination, which is characteristic of the layered structure of the Ti3C2 MXene structure.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed on Ti3AlC2 MAX powder and the corresponding etched Ti3C2 MXene, as shown in Fig. 3 and S1. The surface morphology of the MAX phase (Fig. S1) at a magnification of 50 µm reveals a dense and compact structure composed of irregular, rock-like particles distributed non-uniformly across the surface. This morphology is characteristic of the layered MAX phase before etching.

(a) SEM morphology and (b) cross-section image of SEM film.

Following the etching process, a significant morphological transformation is observed, as shown in Fig. 3(a). The Ti3C2 MXene exhibits a typical two-dimensional (2D) layered structure with sheet-like features and delaminated flakes. This is further confirmed by the cross-sectional SEM image in Fig. 3(b), which shows stacked and exfoliated layers. The MXene sheets appear thin and loosely packed, indicating successful removal of the Al layers and formation of few-layer structures. The presence of visible interlayer spacing and uniformly distributed nanosheets is a defining characteristic of MXene materials. This transition from a dense bulk structure to a layered 2D morphology confirms the effectiveness of the etching process and is consistent with previously reported studies.47,48 Furthermore, the EDX identifies the corresponding elements present in Ti3C2 MXene as shown in Fig. S2. It shows mix terminated MXene that includes O 1s, Cl 2p, and F 1s. The electrical conductivity of the synthesized Ti3C2 MXene was measured to be approximately 8903 S cm−1, indicating its highly conductive nature and suitability for interfacial charge transport applications. This high conductivity supports its role in reducing interfacial resistance in the TEG device.

Commercially available N-type and P-type Bi2Te3 pellets, with their respective thermal conductivity and Seebeck coefficient values listed in Table 2, were used in this study. They were ground to powder, and then the X-ray diffraction patterns of the pellets were measured as shown in Fig. 4(a and b), where the diffraction peaks can be indexed to the standard rhombohedral (hexagonal representation) Bi2Te3 phase, confirming the crystalline nature and phase purity of the materials. The absence of secondary phases indicates that both pellets retain the characteristic structure required for thermoelectric applications. The P-type Bi2Te3 exhibits prominent reflections such as the (015) plane, indicating preferred orientation, while the N-type pellet shows similar phase peaks with slight variations in intensity due to compositional differences.

XRD for (a) N-type Bi2Te3, (b) P-type Bi2Te3, and SEM image for (c) N-type Bi2Te3, (d) P-type Bi2Te3.

The surface morphology of the N-type and P-type Bi2Te3 pellets was analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), as shown in Fig. 4(c and d). The SEM image at a magnification of 2 µm reveals a compact microstructure composed of irregular and plate-like grains distributed across the surface. The grains appear stacked and closely packed, indicating good densification of the pellet. Such morphology is commonly observed in Bi2Te3-based thermoelectric materials and reflects the typical layered structure of the compound.49,50 The presence of well-connected grains suggests favorable electrical pathways within the material. The EDX analysis of the n-type and p-type Bi2Te3 pellet and their respective corresponding quantitative elemental composition has been shown in Fig. S3.

Other than the reported calculation method,51,52 the thermoelectric performance of both device configurations was evaluated using a controlled hot-plate heating setup. The hot-plate temperature was monitored using a K-type thermocouple, and the temperature difference (ΔT) was estimated relative to the ambient room temperature. Initially, the output voltage was recorded at room temperature, where negligible voltage generation was observed due to the absence of an effective temperature difference across the thermoelectric pellets as shown in Fig. 5.

Subsequently, the temperature at the heated side of the TEG was gradually increased from 24 °C to 100 °C, corresponding to an estimated temperature difference (ΔT) of approximately 76 °C based on the hot-plate and ambient temperatures. The temperatures shown in Fig. 5 represent the hot-plate set temperatures The generated open-circuit voltage was recorded across the positive and negative terminals using a digital multimeter, and the electrical measurements for both the original and MXene-assisted TEGs were conducted under identical hot-plate temperature settings. Both the original and MXene-assisted TEGs exhibited an approximately linear increase in output voltage with increasing estimated temperature difference, confirming stable thermoelectric behavior and effective thermal-to-electrical energy conversion.

The original TEG generated an open-circuit voltage of 19.6 mV at ΔT of 76 °C, whereas the MXene-backed TEG produced an enhanced voltage output of 22.3 mV under similar thermal conditions, corresponding to an enhancement of approximately 13.8%. The improved performance of the MXene-assisted configuration is attributed to the high electrical conductivity and layered morphology of Ti3C2Tx MXene, which contribute to improved electrical contact and interfacial charge transport, resulting in enhanced thermoelectric performance. For comparison purposes, the output power discussed above corresponds to an identical external load resistance of 1.0 kΩ for both devices. However, the maximum output power was achieved at different load resistances. The pristine TEG exhibited a maximum power of 94.08 nW at a load resistance of 0.75 kΩ, whereas the MXene-assisted TEG reached a maximum power of 124.32 nW at a load resistance of 1.0 kΩ. According to the maximum power transfer principle, these load resistances approximately represent the effective internal resistance of the respective devices.

The load resistance corresponding to the maximum output power provides an approximation of the effective electrical characteristics of the complete device and should not be interpreted solely in terms of the interfacial resistance. The MXene-assisted TEG consistently exhibited higher output voltage and power than the pristine device over the investigated load resistance range, indicating that the Ti3C2Tx MXene interfacial layer contributed to improved electrical transport within the device. As the load resistance increased, the output power initially increased and attained a maximum value when the load resistance approached the effective internal resistance of the device. Further increases in load resistance resulted in a decrease in power output due to reduced current generation.

The enhanced thermoelectric behavior observed in the MXene-assisted configuration demonstrates the potential of Ti3C2Tx MXene as an effective interfacial engineering material for thermoelectric energy harvesting systems. Additionally, the Bi2Te3 pellets remained securely attached to the Kapton substrate during device fabrication and electrical testing. A detailed investigation of interfacial adhesion and bending reliability will be the subject of future work.

Fig. 9 illustrates the relationship between output power and current for both the original and MXene-assisted TEGs. In both cases, the output power increased initially with increasing current, reached a maximum value, and then gradually decreased, resulting in a characteristic peak-shaped behavior associated with thermoelectric load matching conditions. The original TEG achieved a maximum power output of approximately 96.04 nW at around 9.8 µA, whereas the MXene-assisted TEG exhibited an enhanced peak power of approximately 124.32 nW at around 11.15 µA. Additionally, the MXene-assisted device maintained consistently higher power output over the entire current range compared to the original TEG. This improvement is attributed to the high electrical conductivity and layered morphology of Ti3C2Tx MXene, which improve electrical continuity and reduce interfacial electrical losses within the thermoelectric system.

The enhanced electrical performance of the MXene-assisted TEG is primarily attributed to the highly conductive Ti3C2Tx MXene interfacial layer. Owing to its high electrical conductivity and layered morphology, the MXene layer improves electrical continuity between adjacent components, facilitating more efficient charge transport and reducing interfacial electrical losses. Consequently, the generated thermoelectric voltage is collected more efficiently, resulting in higher open-circuit voltage and output power than those of the pristine TEG. Future work will focus on comprehensive structural and interfacial characterization of the Ti3C2Tx MXene/Bi2Te3 interface to elucidate barrier formation, lattice mismatch, and charge carrier/phonon transport mechanisms, providing deeper insight into the origin of the enhanced thermoelectric performance.

To enable a direct comparison with previously reported thermoelectric generators, the maximum output power was normalized by the total active footprint area of the thermoelectric pellets (0.196 cm2). Based on this normalization, the original and MXene-assisted TEGs achieved maximum areal power densities of approximately 0.480 µW cm−2 and 0.634 µW cm−2, respectively, at ΔT ≈ 76 K as shown in Table 6. The MXene-assisted TEG therefore exhibited an improvement of approximately 32% in normalized power density compared with the pristine device.

As shown in Table 7, previous studies generally improved thermoelectric generator performance through material modification, thin-film optimization, or conductive interface engineering. In contrast, the present work employs a physically attached Ti3C2Tx MXene interfacial film while preserving the commercially available Bi2Te3 pellets without any compositional modification. Therefore, the objective of this study was to investigate the influence of MXene-assisted interfacial engineering on the electrical output characteristics of a pellet-based thermoelectric generator rather than to optimize the intrinsic thermoelectric properties of Bi2Te3 or develop a fully optimized thermoelectric device.

Although several previously reported thermoelectric generators exhibit higher areal power densities than the present work, it should be noted that many of these studies achieved enhanced performance through optimization of the thermoelectric materials, device architecture, or composite fabrication. In contrast, the objective of the present study was not to develop a fully optimized high-power thermoelectric generator prototype, but rather to investigate the influence of a physically attached Ti3C2Tx MXene interfacial film on the electrical output characteristics of a commercial Bi2Te3 pellet-based TEG. Unlike previous studies in which nanomaterials were incorporated directly into the Bi2Te3 matrix, the commercial Bi2Te3 pellets used in this work remained unmodified. The study was carried out to investigate the influence of MXene-assisted interfacial engineering on the electrical output characteristics of a pellet-based thermoelectric generator rather than to optimize the intrinsic thermoelectric properties of Bi2Te3 or develop a fully optimized thermoelectric device.

Conclusion

Ti3C2Tx MXene was successfully synthesized through selective etching of the parent MAX phase and characterized to confirm the formation of layered conductive nanosheets with favorable interfacial properties. A pellet-based thermoelectric generator (TEG) was fabricated using commercially available n-type and p-type Bi2Te3 pellets assembled on a polyamide/Kapton substrate through low-temperature soldering. The introduction of a physically attached MXene backing/interface layer enhanced the thermoelectric output performance under applied thermal gradients. The MXene-assisted TEG exhibited an open-circuit voltage of 22.3 mV at ΔT ≈ 76 K, compared to 19.6 mV for the pristine device, along with an increase in maximum output power from 96.04 nW to 124.32 nW. The improved performance is attributed to the highly conductive layered Ti3C2Tx MXene interfacial layer, which improves electrical continuity and facilitates interfacial charge transport. These results demonstrate the potential of MXene-assisted thermoelectric systems for lightweight energy harvesting applications. The observed enhancement was achieved without modifying the commercial Bi2Te3 pellets, indicating that a simple MXene interfacial layer can effectively improve the electrical output characteristics of a pellet-based thermoelectric generator. Future work will focus on optimizing the uniformity of the MXene coating and enhancing interfacial stability, as well as performing detailed structural and interfacial characterization of the Ti3C2Tx MXene/Bi2Te3 interface to better understand the underlying charge transport mechanisms and further improve device performance.
