Enhanced supercapacitor performance using self-assembled silver nanowire electrodes
Authors: Ahmet Güngör, Büşra Tuğba Çamiç, Süleyman Çelik, Emre Erdem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Using a controlled self-assembly approach, this study demonstrates the successful fabrication of electrodes based on silver nanowires (AgNWs), which are promising materials for flexible energy storage systems. Glass substrates functionalized with (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (APTES) are coated with AgNW films for 4, 8, and 16 h. The morphological, structural, and electrochemical properties of these films are subsequently evaluated. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis reveals high crystalline phase purity and a face-centered cubic (fcc) structure in the synthesized AgNWs. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) characterizations indicate that a uniform nanowire network and optimal surface roughness are achieved with an 8-hour coating time. Four-point probe measurements confirm enhanced electrical conductivity, particularly in the AgNWs@8 h sample. Electrochemical performance is assessed using a two-electrode system in 6 M potassium hydroxide (KOH) electrolyte. The AgNWs@8 h electrodes exhibit the highest specific capacitance (318.84 F/g), energy density (102.03 Wh/kg), and power density (1119.68 W/kg) at 1 A/g in a symmetric two-electrode configuration. These results demonstrate that oriented AgNW structures offer a high-performance, scalable, and low-temperature alternative to conventional supercapacitor electrodes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50472-8.

Introduction

The rising energy consumption, growth of renewable resource systems, and expansion of mobile technologies have fueled the demand for flexible, lightweight, and high-performance energy storage devices — a demand that has in turn driven intensive research into advanced electrode materials for supercapacitors1–3. The limited power density and prolonged charging times of conventional battery systems render them inadequate for some applications. Accordingly, supercapacitors, characterized by their elevated power density, rapid charge-discharge capability, and extended cycle durability, have garnered heightened interest as energy storage technology in recent years4–6. Electrode material is a crucial determinant of supercapacitor performance; thus, advancing next-generation electrode systems is essential for enhancing energy density and cyclic stability7–9.

One-dimensional (1D) nanostructured materials with high surface area, including nanotubes, nanowires, and nanorods, have attracted considerable attention as electrode materials in electrochemical energy storage systems10,11. These materials offer significant advantages by providing shortened diffusion pathways for ions and excitons, thereby facilitating enhanced charge/discharge kinetics12. 1D nanostructures can also enhance performance in electrochemical systems by facilitating ion transport, offering a large specific surface area, and allowing for various surface functionalizations. Carbon nanotubes (CNTs), metal oxide nanorods, and metal nanowire formations are among the most extensively studied categories in this context.

Among various candidates, silver nanowire (AgNWs)-based electrodes have attracted significant attention due to their excellent conductivity, flexibility, and ability to form interconnected networks. In addition to their widespread use in optoelectronic and sensor applications, AgNWs have also been investigated for flexible supercapacitor systems13–15. For instance, Yin et al.15 reported highly conductive PDMS/AgNWs/carbon fiber composite thin films with stable electrochemical performance in flexible supercapacitors, while Liu et al.14 demonstrated ultralong AgNW-based transparent conductive films enabling all-solid-state supercapacitors with excellent flexibility and cycling stability. Despite these advances, critical challenges such as junction resistance, morphology optimization, and long-term stability remain unresolved16,17. The present study addresses these issues by employing a controlled self-assembly strategy and systematic two-electrode characterization18,19.

Various solution based-coating techniques have been developed for the deposition of AgNWs onto substrates, including solution casting20, spray coating21, rod coating22, and self-assembly23 methods. While conventional techniques such as solution casting and spray coating offer simplicity and scalability, they often result in non-uniform distribution and random orientation of nanowires, leading to inefficient network formation and electrical performance24. In contrast, the self-assembly method enables the formation of highly ordered, percolated networks with enhanced connectivity between individual nanowires, resulting in improved electrical conductivity and mechanical stability. Furthermore, this technique allows for precise control over film thickness, morphology and porosity while maintaining the inherent flexibility of AgNWs25. The efficiency of AgNW-based electrodes is considerably influenced by both the characteristics of the nanostructure and the production techniques and coating parameters applied17,26. Poor wire-to-wire contact, irregular morphology, and PVP residue on the surface can adversely affect conductivity and electroactive surface area27,28. As a result, choosing the coating technique that yields a regulated, directed, and uniform network structure is essential for converting AgNWs into high-performance supercapacitor electrodes29,30.

This study explores the potential of AgNWs, fabricated through a controlled self-assembly technique and processed at low temperatures, to achieve low sheet resistance and electrochemical performance in a two-electrode configuration. Previous studies on self-assembled AgNW films have predominantly focused on optoelectronic applications, such as transparent electrodes for organic photovoltaic devices and other flexible optoelectronic systems31–33. The present study adapts the self-assembly approach for the fabrication of supercapacitor electrodes. High-density, well-connected nanowire networks were produced via controlled electrostatic deposition of AgNWs onto APTES-functionalized glass surfaces. The morphological, structural, electrical, and electrochemical characteristics of the prepared films were systematically investigated as a function of deposition time (4, 8, and 16 h), demonstrating the successful application of this technique in energy storage devices, distinct from its prior use in optoelectronics.

A distinctive feature of this research is the deliberate use of a symmetric two-electrode configuration to evaluate the practical device-level performance of the AgNW electrodes. This method more precisely represents the performance of the fabricated electrodes under actual device settings, facilitating a more reliable assessment of the material’s usability.

Compared to conventional slurry-based or composite electrode fabrication routes, the self-assembly strategy adopted in this work offers several notable advantages: (i) it enables binder-free film formation with a highly interconnected metallic nanowire network, ensuring efficient electron transport and minimal interfacial resistance; (ii) it eliminates the need for multiple synthesis or annealing steps, allowing low-temperature and scalable processing; and (iii) it provides structural uniformity and tunable nanowire density through deposition-time control. These features collectively lead to a simplified yet efficient electrode design that demonstrates promising electrochemical performance under symmetric two-electrode device conditions.

The findings suggest that self-assembled AgNW electrodes exhibit good electrical performance under testing conditions and have significant potential for application in flexible energy storage systems. This work presents a practical and effective framework for advancing AgNWs into scalable, low-temperature, fabricable, and high-performance supercapacitor electrodes. Moreover, electrodes based on AgNWs for supercapacitors possess potential uses in wearable electronics, microsensor systems, and flexible battery-integrated circuits. Therefore, the fabrication approach presented in this work provides significant improvements to both fundamental materials science and practical energy technologies.

Experimental

Materials

3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane (APTES), anhydrous ethanol, methanol, and isopropyl alcohol (IPA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Silver nanowires (AgNWs), dispersed in IPA at a concentration of 20 mg/mL, with an average diameter of 100 nm and lengths ranging from 5 to 10 μm, were acquired from ACS Materials. Glass slides (Menzel-Glaser, 15 × 15 mm²) used as a substrate were treated with piranha solution (7:3 H₂SO₄/H₂O₂) at 90 °C for 1 h. Following this, the samples were sonicated in deionized (DI) water for 15 min and subsequently dried under nitrogen (N₂) flow. Potassium hydroxide (KOH, reagent grade, 90%, flakes) was used as an electrolyte (6 M KOH solution) and purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Preparation of AgNWs conductive electrodes

In this study, silver nanowire (AgNW)-based conductive electrodes were successfully fabricated on glass substrates via an electrostatically assisted self-assembly coating method for supercapacitor applications. The formation of the AgNWs network was achieved by electrostatic interactions between the silver nanowires and glass surfaces functionalized with 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane (APTES). APTES was utilized to silanize the glass substrates—a well-established technique for immobilizing metal nanoparticles onto surfaces. This process involves the reaction of alkoxy silane groups with surface silanol groups, resulting in a functionalized interface that promotes strong adhesion34. The amine and thiol functional groups in APTES facilitated strong interactions with the metallic surfaces of the nanowires, enabling the formation of uniform and stable self-assembled AgNW films.

The glass substrates were first functionalized with APTES to introduce amine-terminated groups, generating a positively charged surface favorable for AgNW adhesion. The APTES-modified substrates were subsequently immersed into an isopropyl alcohol (IPA)-based AgNW dispersion using a dip-coating approach. During immersion, AgNWs spontaneously assembled onto the substrate surface, forming a continuous and interconnected nanowire network. This organization arises from the combined effects of electrostatic attraction between the positively charged APTES layer and the negatively charged AgNWs. The resulting films exhibit uniform coverage and strong adhesion to the substrate. This self-assembly mechanism is consistent with previously reported electrostatic self-assembly processes35–37.

For detailed substrate preparation, the glass substrates were immersed in a 3% APTES solution for 3 h, thoroughly rinsed with methanol, and dried under a nitrogen flow, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 1 h. The APTES-functionalized substrates were then immersed in an AgNWs solution (polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), 0.5 mg/ml in isopropanol) for 4, 8, and 16 h. After immersion, the substrates were rinsed with IPA, dried under a nitrogen stream, and finally annealed at 230 °C for 15 min under atmospheric conditions, following the optimization reported in our previous study38(see Table 1).

Characterizations

The surface morphologies of AgNWs conductive films were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) on the JEOL JIB-4601 F FIB-SEM Multi-Beam system. The sheet resistance of the AgNW films were measured using the four-point probe method (Cascade Microtech CP4 4-Point Probe). The average surface roughness (Ra) of the films was measured by atomic force microscopy (AFM) using a Nanomagnetics Instruments ezAFM system. Crystal structure characterization of the samples was carried out using the Bruker D8 Advance X-ray Diffraction (XRD) system.

Comprehensive investigations were conducted to evaluate the electrochemical performance of AgNWs electrodes, explicitly focusing on samples AgNWs@4, AgNWs@8, and AgNWs@16, which were tested with different coating times. A comparative analysis was conducted on the specific capacitance, energy density, and cycling stability of the electrodes to determine the optimal coating period. Electrochemical measurements were conducted utilizing BioLogic VMP-300 potentiostat in an aqueous electrolyte comprising 6 M KOH. Whatman glass microfiber membranes served as dielectric separators within the supercapacitor cells. The AgNWs-based conductive films were incorporated into supercapacitor devices within a two-electrode configuration and assessed for performance. AgNWs electrode-based supercapacitor design

The electrochemical performance of the self-assembled AgNW electrodes was evaluated in a symmetric two-electrode configuration using the as-prepared AgNW-coated glass slides directly, without removing the films from the glass substrate and without any slurry formulation, conductive additive, or polymeric binder. This strategy was adopted to preserve the integrity of the self-assembled AgNW conductive network and to enable direct correlation between the electrical characterization and the electrochemical performance of the same electrode architecture.

For device assembly, two identical AgNW-coated glass slides were used as the opposing electrodes. A glass fiber separator soaked with 6 M KOH electrolyte was placed between the two electrodes to provide ionic conduction and electrical insulation, while copper foil was attached to each electrode only as a current collector to ensure stable electrical contact during the measurements. The use of 6 M KOH was intentionally selected because this electrolyte is widely employed in aqueous supercapacitor studies due to its high ionic conductivity and favorable ion-transport characteristics. In addition, alkaline KOH media are commonly used for evaluating Ag-containing capacitive electrodes, allowing more meaningful comparison with related literature. Acidic and organic electrolytes were not investigated in the present study, as the purpose here was to assess the device-level performance of the self-assembled AgNW electrodes in a simple, reliable, and highly conductive aqueous electrolyte system. In this configuration, the self-assembled nanowire network remained intact throughout device fabrication and testing. For clarity, the actual assembly and current-collection pathway of the symmetric device are illustrated in Scheme 1. The AgNWs were deposited on only one side of each glass substrate, i.e., the APTES-functionalized face. During assembly, the AgNW-coated sides were oriented toward the separator/electrolyte region, while copper foil was attached directly to the AgNW-coated side at the contact region and served solely as the current collector. Therefore, the electrical pathway was established through the AgNW network rather than through the insulating glass substrate.

The electrochemical evaluation was deliberately performed in a symmetric two-electrode configuration because this approach provides a more realistic assessment of supercapacitor device behavior than three-electrode half-cell measurements, which are mainly intended to probe the intrinsic response of an individual electrode. Electrochemical measurements were carried out using a BioLogic VMP-300 multichannel potentiostat. Cyclic voltammetry (CV), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and galvanostatic charge-discharge measurements with potential limitation (GCPL) were performed to evaluate the capacitive behavior, charge-transfer characteristics, and cycling stability of the assembled devices. Results and discussion

Electrical characterization of AgNWs electrodes

In this study, AgNW-based electrodes were fabricated via AgNWs self-assembly, and the correlation between deposition time and sheet resistance was investigated to determine optimal processing conditions for supercapacitor electrode fabrication. Based on optimization results, deposition times of 4, 8, and 16 h were selected for film preparation. Previous research on self-assembled AgNW films for solar cell applications examined deposition times of 1.5, 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 h. Post-thermal treatment measurements revealed that films processed for shorter deposition times (1.5 and 3 h) failed to form a sufficiently conductive network for reliable sheet-resistance measurement, while substrates with extended deposition times demonstrated progressively improved sheet resistance values: 15 Ω sq− 1 (6 h), 6 Ω/ sq− 1 (12 h), and 4 Ω sq− 1 (18 and 24 h)25. Based on these findings, the deposition process was initiated at 4 h rather than 3 h to ensure the formation of a measurable conductive network, and 8 h was selected instead of 6 h to investigate further reductions in sheet resistance for supercapacitor electrodes. Since the decrease in sheet resistance plateaued at 18 h in the previous study, a maximum deposition time of 16 h was considered sufficient for process time efficiency. Considering these factors, deposition times of 4, 8, and 16 h were established for supercapacitor applications, yielding post-annealing sheet resistance values of 681 Ω sq− 1, 12 Ω sq− 1, and 4 Ω sq− 1, respectively (Table 3).

To understand the origin of the sheet resistance changes, the morphology of the films was first examined. In Fig. 1, SEM images of AgNW films deposited for 4, 8, and 16 h show that increasing deposition time leads to a higher nanowire density and the formation of a more continuous and interconnected network. This transition from a sparse to a dense network structure facilitates the formation of continuous electron transport pathways. As a result, the sheet resistance decreases, corresponding to improved electrical conductivity. In Figure S1, EDS elemental mapping confirms the homogeneous distribution of Ag throughout the film surface. SEM-EDS analysis further reveals that the density of nanowires clearly increases with longer deposition times, supporting the formation of a more continuous network with lower sheet resistance. Thermal annealing further decreased the sheet resistance of the AgNW@8 h film by promoting junction formation between individual nanowires. As shown in the tilted SEM images, no junctions are observed before annealing (Fig. 2a), whereas after annealing, the marked regions clearly show interwoven nanowires forming junctions (Fig. 2b). These annealing-induced junctions create a more continuous network, further reducing sheet resistance. AFM images (Figure S2) confirm the formation of this more interconnected network after annealing. Another factor influencing sheet resistance is the PVP layer on the AgNW surface, which acts as an insulating barrier at nanowire junctions. Thermal annealing effectively removes this PVP layer, contributing to the further enhancing electrical connectivity26.

To support the SEM analysis, AFM measurements were conducted to further investigate the surface topography and roughness evolution of the AgNW films. Non-normalized AFM images preserve actual height variations, showing that maximum film height increased from ~ 300 nm (4 h) to 1.5 μm (16 h) as more material accumulated (Figure S2). Normalizing the images would suppress this real morphological difference and potentially conceal finer surface features; hence, the non-normalized AFM maps more accurately represent the true surface morphology. The average roughness (Ra) of electrodes is a key parameter influencing device performance, as surface roughness determines the effective interfacial area between layers. While the un-annealed AgNW electrodes exhibited extremely high surface roughness values of 51.93 nm, 180 nm, and 240 nm, these values decreased to 39.25 nm, 160 nm, and 160 nm for 4 h, 8 h, and 16 h deposition times after annealing, respectively (Fig. 3). Unlike in some electronic device applications, high roughness can positively contribute to device performance in supercapacitor applications when it is properly controlled. In electrochemical capacitors, reactions occur at the electrode–electrolyte interface within a nanometer-scale region. High surface roughness increases the electrode surface area and allows for greater adsorption of electrolyte ions, thereby enhancing energy storage performance39. Moreover, the moderately high roughness level observed for the AgNWs@8 h sample appears to offer a balance between increased electroactive surface area and maintained film integrity, which aligns with its superior electrochemical performance. This correlation suggests that not only the magnitude but also the uniformity and connectivity of the roughened surface contribute to enhanced ion accessibility and charge transfer kinetics.

The crystalline structure of the silver nanowires was identified as face-centered cubic (fcc) based on X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (Fig. 4), confirming their high phase purity. The lattice constant was determined to be 4.104 Å, consistent with the reference data (JCPDS No. 87–0717). As shown in Fig. 4a, AgNW films prepared with different coating durations (4, 8, and 16 h) exhibited nearly identical diffraction patterns, with characteristic peaks corresponding to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) planes of fcc Ag. The absence of peak shifts or broadening indicates that coating duration and the resulting film thickness did not affect the crystallographic structure. To further evaluate the structural stability upon thermal treatment, XRD patterns of unannealed and annealed AgNWs@8 h were compared (Fig. 4b). No additional phases or detectable peak shifts were observed, confirming that annealing preserved the fcc Ag structure. In all cases, the (111) reflection exhibited the highest intensity, indicating a preferred orientation of the nanowires along this crystallographic plane40. This anisotropic alignment favors directional charge transport within the electrode network and is consistent with the improved conductivity observed for AgNWs@8h41. Moreover, the absence of secondary peaks associated with silver oxide phases (such as Ag₂O or AgO) demonstrates that the synthesis and post-treatment conditions effectively prevented surface oxidation42. This structural stability aligns well with the high electrochemical durability observed in long-term GCPL tests, further validating the robustness of the self-assembled AgNW films.

Electrochemical performance of AgNWs electrode-based supercapacitors

The electrochemical impedance characteristics of AgNWs electrodes exhibited significant differences based on the coating time. After evaluating the Nyquist curves displayed in Fig. 5 with the equivalent circuit parameters detailed in Table 4, it can be concluded that deposition time strongly affects the distributed resistive, non-ideal capacitive, and diffusion-related processes within the self-assembled AgNW network/electrolyte interface43. Because these AgNW films are rough, heterogeneous, and percolative rather than compact planar electrodes, the fitted circuit should be interpreted phenomenologically, with the constant phase elements (Q) reflecting non-ideal interfacial capacitance and the W terms describing diffusion-related low-frequency behavior in the interconnected nanowire architecture. The AgNWs@8 h sample demonstrates a distinct slope in the low-frequency region and a constricted semicircular profile in the high-frequency domain, reflecting both minimal charge transfer resistance (R3 = 0.98 Ω) and low Warburg impedance (W2 = 626.16 Ω·s⁰·⁵), signifying rapid ion transport44. This sample also shows the lowest ohmic contribution (R1 = 0.53 Ω), markedly lower than AgNWs@4 h (2.59 Ω) and slightly lower than AgNWs@16 h (0.69 Ω), further confirming the improved electrical continuity of the 8 h network. In addition, the relatively high a₂ value (0.86), corresponding to a more nearly ideal interfacial response, suggests a more nearly ideal interfacial response than that of AgNWs@4 h (0.16), consistent with a more homogeneous and better-connected electrode/electrolyte interface. The low Zre component (< 15 Ω) and the swift saturation of the –Zim curve indicate minimal internal resistance and diffusion limitations of the AgNWs@8 h electrodes45. Conversely, the AgNWs@4 h sample had poor charge transfer and restricted ion permeability, evidenced by its high Zre (~ 230 Ω) and large semicircular diameter. This behavior is also consistent with its high fitted resistance terms, particularly R2 = 225.30 Ω and R3 = 17.57 Ω, together with the largest W2 value (4778 Ω·s⁻⁰·⁵), indicating severely hindered ion/electron transport through a sparse and weakly percolated nanowire network. The AgNWs@16 h sample demonstrated minimal charge transfer resistance; however, the deviation of the diffusion curve from the ideal in the –Zim component suggests a partial limitation to ion transport caused by excessive coating46. Although AgNWs@16 h exhibited an even lower R3 value (0.46 Ω) and the lowest W2 value (176.91 Ω·s⁻⁰·⁵), its much larger W3 value (1425.81 Ω·s⁻⁰·⁵), compared with 162.74 Ω·s⁻⁰·⁵ for AgNWs@8 h, indicates that excessive deposition introduced an additional diffusion-related constraint at low frequencies. Likewise, the high R2 value of 275.20 Ω suggests that the densified network did not translate into the most favorable overall electrochemical response. These behaviors correspond closely with the characterization findings. SEM scans showed a low density of the nanowire network on the AgNWs@4 h electrodes, but the AgNWs@16 h sample had irregular clusters resulting from the high density. The AgNWs@8 h sample displayed a uniform, interconnected, and continuous nanowire network in both SEM and AFM investigations; this configuration corresponded with the low resistance and rapid response characteristics found in the EIS data. Overall, the EIS results indicate that AgNWs@8 h provides the most balanced impedance response, where low ohmic resistance, low polarization resistance, and moderated diffusion impedance are simultaneously achieved through an optimally interconnected self-assembled nanowire architecture. To further support the reliability of the equivalent-circuit fitting, the ZFit-derived χ²/|Z|² and χ/√N values are now reported in Table 4 as quantitative measures of fitting quality for AgNWs@4 h, AgNWs@8 h, and AgNWs@16 h.

To evaluate the structural and electrochemical stability of the self-assembled AgNWs electrodes, impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was additionally performed after the GCPL cycling test. In our standard experimental sequence, the electrochemical measurements were carried out in the following order:

(i) PEIS (EIS) analysis to determine the initial internal resistance and charge-transfer characteristics, (ii) cyclic voltammetry (CV) to assess redox reversibility, and (iii) galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCPL) cycling (5000 cycles) to evaluate long-term capacitance retention. Upon completion of the GCPL test, a second EIS measurement was recorded under identical conditions to examine the post-cycling behavior of the electrodes.

As shown in Figure S4, all AgNWs electrodes exhibit a moderate increase in impedance after cycling, attributed to partial accumulation of interfacial resistance during prolonged operation. Notably, the AgNWs@8 h electrode retains the most stable impedance profile with the smallest change in semicircle diameter, confirming the excellent mechanical integrity and charge-transfer stability of the optimized self-assembled nanowire network47. This post-cycling EIS evaluation, together with the initial/final GCPL profile comparison, supports the electrochemical robustness of the self-assembled AgNW electrodes after extended cycling under the tested conditions. This comprehensive evaluation reveals that deposition time directly influences morphological and electrochemical processes. Exceptionally, AgNWs@8 h electrodes, due to their optimal film thickness and improved wire-to-wire connectivity, provide an ideal balance of charge transfer and ion diffusion, low internal resistance, and high electrochemical efficiency, vital for supercapacitor applications.

The capacitive behavior of AgNWs electrodes was assessed employing the CV method within the 0–0.8 V potential range. The CV curves at a scan rate of 20 mV/s, presented in Fig. 6a, reveal mixed capacitive behavior in all samples, with significant variations in both the enclosed area and the curve profile. At this scan rate, the AgNWs@16 h sample exhibits a relatively high capacitance contribution, whereas the AgNWs@8 h electrode shows the more balanced overall CV response when the entire scan-rate range is considered48. These differences indicate that deposition time strongly affects the electrochemically accessible surface area, charge-storage response, and ion-transport behavior of the self-assembled AgNW networks. The AgNWs@4 h sample possesses the smallest curve area and demonstrates inadequate capacitive performance owing to insufficient network density49. Figure 6b displays the specific capacitance (Cs) values obtained using various scan rates. The AgNWs@8 h electrode reaches a capacitance of 318.84 F/g at low scan rates, implying high ion permeability and a large electroactive surface area50. A decrease in Cs values was observed over all samples with increased scan rate, indicating the diffusion-limited behavior commonly observed in supercapacitor devices51,52. Although AgNWs@16 h exhibits relatively high capacitance at certain individual scan rates, this advantage is not maintained consistently across the full scan-rate window. In contrast, AgNWs@8 h exhibits the most stable overall capacitance trend with increasing scan rate, indicating a more favorable balance between charge storage and rate capability.

The CV curves obtained at scan rates ranging from 1 to 200 mV/s for each sample in Figure S3 distinctly demonstrate this effect. The AgNWs@8 h sample exhibits the most balanced and stable CV response among the investigated samples; however, the visible broad redox-related features indicate that the charge-storage mechanism is not purely electric double-layer capacitive (EDLC), but rather involves mixed capacitive behavior with contributions from both double-layer charging and surface faradaic processes53. Its relatively symmetric profile and lower distortion at increasing scan rates support a highly interconnected conductive network structure that enables more efficient charge propagation and ion transport. The narrowing of the curves and asymmetry at high speeds in the AgNWs@4 h and AgNWs@16 h electrodes suggest that structural anomalies negatively affect the electrochemical response. These results reveal a strong correlation with SEM and AFM analyses. The sparse and disconnected nanowire network determined in the AgNWs@4 h sample corresponds with lower capacitance and limited ion diffusion. The AgNWs@16 h electrode displayed a dense network structure; however, local densification and possible wire-to-wire overlaps identified in the SEM data limited ion mobility, especially at higher scan rates where fast ion transport becomes more critical54. Conversely, the AgNWs@8 h electrode showed the most balanced overall electrochemical response due to its uniform morphology and low impedance values, as consistently supported by the CV, EIS, and morphological analyses. It should be noted that at a scan rate of 20 mV/s, the AgNWs@16 h electrode exhibited a relatively higher capacitance than AgNWs@8 h. This is likely due to the dense nanowire packing in AgNWs@16 h, which allows ions sufficient time to diffuse through the network under slow scan conditions. However, as demonstrated in Figure S3, this advantage diminishes at higher scan rates because ion transport becomes hindered by local agglomerations and restricted pathways. By contrast, the AgNWs@8 h electrode sustains a more stable capacitive response across the entire scan rate range (1–200 mV/s), indicating that its uniform and interconnected morphology provides the most favorable balance between charge storage, ion accessibility, and rate capability. Accordingly, the conclusion that AgNWs@8 h exhibits the best overall rate performance is based on the combined trend observed in Fig. 6b and the lower distortion of the CV profiles in Figure S3 at elevated scan rates, rather than on the capacitance value at a single scan rate alone.

To further quantify the charge-storage kinetics, b-value analysis based on the relationship i = avᵇ was performed using the scan-rate-dependent CV data, and the results are presented in Figure S7. The calculated b-values were 0.45 for AgNWs@4 h (Adj R² = 0.97) and 0.60 for AgNWs@8 h (Adj R² = 0.86). These values indicate that both electrodes exhibit non-ideal mixed charge-storage behavior, while the higher b-value of AgNWs@8 h suggests a greater surface-controlled capacitive contribution and more favorable electrochemical kinetics than AgNWs@4 h. This quantitative result is consistent with the CV profile evolution, impedance behavior, and superior overall electrochemical response observed for the AgNWs@8 h electrode.

The GCPL behavior of AgNWs electrodes has been studied within the potential range of 0–0.8 V at a constant current density of 1 A/g. This current density was intentionally selected to provide a direct and consistent comparison among the AgNWs@4 h, AgNWs@8 h, and AgNWs@16 h symmetric devices under the same full-cell operating condition. In addition to the main comparison at 1 A/g, supplementary GCPL measurements were also carried out at 0.5 and 2.4 A/g, and the corresponding charge–discharge profiles are presented in Figure S6. Furthermore, the energy density–power density relationship derived from these multi-current-density GCPL data is presented in Figure S8 as a Ragone plot. This analysis confirms that the device performance was evaluated beyond a single current density and shows that AgNWs@8 h maintains the most favorable balance between energy density and power density over the investigated operating range. These additional results confirm that the galvanostatic behavior of the electrodes was not evaluated at a single current density only, and they further support the superior overall response of the AgNWs@8 h sample under variable current-load conditions. In the present directly assembled, binder-free AgNW architecture, 1 A/g was sufficient to reveal meaningful differences in discharge behavior, polarization, and cycling stability while preserving stable and interpretable galvanostatic profiles. Moreover, 1 A/g was retained as the principal reporting condition in the main text because it allowed consistent comparison among the present devices and with commonly reported device-level supercapacitor data in the literature. Figure 7a illustrates the specific capacity (mAh/g) curves, displaying the variation in energy storage capacity during the charge-discharge process for each sample55. For clarity, Fig. 7a presents the GCPL results in terms of specific discharge capacity (mAh/g), whereas the specific capacitance values discussed elsewhere were calculated from the same galvanostatic profiles for comparison with standard supercapacitor metrics. The AgNWs@8 h sample is distinguished by its prolonged discharge capacity and longer charge-discharge duration, indicating more efficient charge storage under galvanostatic conditions. It shows an increased number of electroactive sites in its electrode structure and an enhanced charge-carrying ability. The AgNWs@4 h sample demonstrates poor electrochemical performance, characterized by diminished discharge capacity. Figure 7b illustrates a comparison of the potential-time profiles for each sample. The AgNWs@8 h electrode displays approximately symmetrical curves in both charge and discharge phases, indicating a favorable and relatively reversible capacitive response56. This symmetrical configuration verifies that charge transmission between the electrodes transpires consistently and quickly. The more distorted charge-discharge curves in the AgNWs@4 h and AgNWs@16 h samples reveal imbalances, especially in charge transfer and ion mobility. Figure 7c illustrates the long-term cycling stability, and the capacitance retention values after 5000 cycles are compared. The capacitance retention values were calculated by comparing the specific capacitance at the 5000th cycle with that obtained from the 1st cycle under identical GCPL conditions, according to “Retention (%) = (C₅₀₀₀/C₁) × 100”, where C₁ is the specific capacitance obtained from the first charge-discharge cycle and C₅₀₀₀ is that obtained after 5000 cycles. This clarification has now been added to the revised manuscript. After 5000 charge–discharge cycles, the AgNWs@8 h electrode maintained 98.5% of its initial capacitance, confirming its excellent long-term stability. The AgNWs@16 h electrode retained 95.02% of its capacitance, while the AgNWs@4 h electrode preserved 92.7%. These corrected values are consistent with the cycling profiles presented in Fig. 7c and further highlight the superior durability of the AgNWs@8 h electrode. Furthermore, the post-cycling EIS analysis (Figure S4) supports this conclusion, showing only a slight increase in impedance values after 5000 GCPL cycles. To further visualize the stability behavior, the initial and final galvanostatic charge–discharge profiles of all samples are compared in Figure S5. Among the investigated electrodes, AgNWs@8 h exhibits the smallest deviation between the 1st and 5000th cycles, confirming the high preservation of its electrochemical response during prolonged cycling. In contrast, AgNWs@4 h and AgNWs@16 h show more noticeable changes in the charge–discharge profiles after 5000 cycles, indicating comparatively greater structural/electrochemical deterioration. These observations are in good agreement with the capacitance-retention results shown in Fig. 7c.

The data obtained from the GCPL analysis are directly correlated with the SEM and AFM results. The AgNWs@8 h electrode provides substantial energy storage capacity and extended cycle life due to its more uniform and continuous nanowire architecture and low internal resistance values. The elevated specific capacitance noted in cyclic voltammetry tests and the minimal charge transfer resistance identified in electrochemical impedance spectroscopy data corroborate this robust galvanostatic charge-discharge performance.

The data on capacitance, energy density, and power density in Table 5 show the significant impact of deposition time on supercapacitor performance. The AgNWs@8 h electrode demonstrated superior performance, achieving a specific capacitance of 318.84 F/g, an energy density of 102.03 Wh/kg, and a power density of 1119.6 W/kg. This enhanced performance can be attributed to a complicated matrix of factors corresponding to structural and electrochemical characterization data. XRD analyses demonstrated that the AgNWs structure preserved its crystalline nature across all samples; however, morphological characterization data, particularly from SEM and AFM, were more supportive. SEM images demonstrate that the nanowire network exhibited sparsity and dispersion after a 4-hour deposition period.

In contrast, optimal density and uniform distribution were attained after an 8-hour deposition duration and a 16-hour deposition time, which, conversely, hindered the effective diffusion of ions due to wire-to-wire overlaps and irregular regions. AFM analyses confirmed this finding, indicating that the AgNWs@8 h sample exhibited optimal surface roughness, characterized by balanced roughness. The electrical performance data revealed that the electrode with an 8-hour coating time exhibited the lowest surface resistance, demonstrating enhanced charge transport capacity. This finding aligns with the elevated current response and charge-discharge efficiency noted in CV and GCPL data. The low charge transfer resistance (Rct) and high tilt angle observed in PEIS analyses suggest that the AgNWs@8 h sample facilitates rapid and efficient ion diffusion. The AgNWs@4 h sample exhibited deficiencies in specific capacitance and energy density related to its low nanowire density and insufficient interconnect structure. The limited access of ions to active sites and higher internal resistance are primary factors contributing to this poor performance. Conversely, despite the AgNWs@16 h electrode having more nanowires, the observed densification and wire agglomeration in SEM analyses hindered ion access to the micropores, diminishing the effective surface area. This structure resulted in electrons traveling along irregular paths instead of unidirectional and efficient paths, adversely impacting both capacitance and energy density.

As summarized in the revised Table 5, the electrochemical performance of the AgNWs@8 h electrode is comparable to or better than several recently reported AgNW-based supercapacitor systems evaluated under two-electrode conditions, supporting the practical device-level relevance of the present symmetric configuration. Despite using a simple self-assembled AgNW film in a symmetric two-electrode configuration, this work demonstrates competitive device-level performance relative to comparable two-electrode AgNW-based systems. This confirms the structural integrity and intrinsic electrochemical efficiency of the self-assembled AgNW network.

An 8-hour coating time effectively integrated critical parameters, including nanowire density, uniform distribution, suitable surface roughness, elevated conductivity, and minimal internal resistance, resulting in the highest performance values. The improved electrochemical performance can be attributed to the self-assembled architecture that promotes continuous electron percolation pathways and facilitates ion transport across the porous nanowire network. These results demonstrate that the self-assembled AgNWs electrodes developed in this study offer a simple yet highly efficient alternative to complex composite architectures, highlighting their strong potential for scalable, cost-effective supercapacitor fabrication.

Conclusion

This study involved the fabrication and comprehensive characterization of self-assembled silver nanowire (AgNW) electrodes specifically designed for supercapacitor applications. The deposition time was found to directly influence structural and functional parameters such as nanowire connectivity, surface roughness, and electrical conductivity, which in turn governed the electrochemical performance. Among the tested samples, the AgNWs@8 h electrode exhibited the most balanced morphology and the highest electrochemical activity, achieving a specific capacitance of 318.84 F g⁻¹, an energy density of 102.03 Wh kg⁻¹, and a power density of 1119.68 W kg⁻¹ at 1 A/g in a symmetric two-electrode configuration in a two-electrode system.

It should be noted that the present study did not include mechanical bending or flexibility tests, as the primary objective was to elucidate the electrochemical performance of self-assembled AgNW electrodes on rigid substrates. While flexibility is an essential criterion for wearable energy storage devices, such investigations fall beyond the scope of the current work. Nevertheless, the promising electrochemical stability observed here suggests that future studies integrating AgNW electrodes into flexible architectures and performing systematic bending cycle tests would provide further validation of their potential for flexible supercapacitor applications. Although the present study was carried out on glass-supported electrodes, future work will aim to transfer the self-assembled AgNW architecture onto flexible substrates and to assess its mechanical durability and electrochemical stability under repeated bending cycles.

Beyond the quantitative outcomes, this work highlights the significance of a binder-free, low-temperature, and environmentally sustainable fabrication approach for high-performance electrodes. The self-assembly method demonstrated here establishes a scalable and reproducible route for developing conductive networks with tunable morphology and improved charge transport characteristics. The strong correlation observed between structural analyses (XRD, SEM, AFM) and electrochemical behavior (CV, EIS, GCPL) further reinforces the reliability of this strategy for precision electrode engineering.

Overall, this study provides fundamental insights into the structure–property relationships governing AgNW-based electrodes and sets a foundation for their potential translation into next-generation flexible and wearable energy storage systems. The demonstrated synthesis approach offers a sustainable and scalable pathway toward advanced nanostructured electrodes, bridging the gap between laboratory-scale innovation and practical device-level implementation.
