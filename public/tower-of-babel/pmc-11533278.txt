Prediction of recurrence-free survival and risk factors of sinonasal inverted papilloma after surgery by machine learning models
Authors: Siyu Miao, Yang Cheng, Yaqi Li, Xiaodong Chen, Fuquan Chen, Dingjun Zha, Tao Xue
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesOur research aims to construct machine learning prediction models to identify patients proned to recurrence after inverted papilloma (IP) surgery and guide their follow-up treatment.MethodsThis study collected 210 patients underwent IP resection surgery at a university hospital from January 2010 to December 2023. Six machine learning algorithms including ExtraSurvivalTrees (EST), GradientBoostingSurvivalAnalysis (GBSA), RandomSurvivalForest (RSF), SurvivalSVM, Coxnet and Coxph, were used to construct the prediction models. Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) values were used to explain the importance of various features in predicting IP recurrence.ResultsWe found that the recurrence rate of IP patients is 20.00%, with a median recurrence time of 35.5 months. Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified mild or moderate dysplasia as an independent risk factor for recurrence. The EST model performs the best in predicting postoperative recurrence of IP, with C-index of 0.968 and 0.878 in the training and testing sets. SHAP emphasizes five important predictive factors for recurrence, including bone defects, orbital involvement, smoking, no processing of tumor attachment sites and drinking.ConclusionsTo our knowledge, this is the first study to use multiple ML models to predict postoperative recurrence of IP. The EST model has the best predictive performance, with SHAP emphasizing several key predictive factors for IP recurrence. This study emphasizes the practicality of machine learning algorithms in predicting IP clinical outcomes, providing valuable insights into the potential for improving clinical decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-024-02099-6.Keywords: Inverted papilloma, Machine learning, Prediction model, Recurrence

Introduction

Neoplasms remain the main killer worldwide [1, 2]. Among which, inverted papilloma (IP) is a type of benign tumor characterized by epithelial cell proliferation. It is locally invasive, recurrent, and has a propensity to become malignant [3, 4]. The most prevalent clinical symptom of IP is nasal congestion, accompanied by other manifestations such as rhinorrhea, headache, and hearing loss. Commonly affected sites include the ethmoid and maxillary sinuses [5]. These tumors can erode adjacent structures, including the orbit and skull base, leading to severe complications [6]. Furthermore, the recurrence rate of IP ranges from 12.80% to 29.70% [7, 8], and the likelihood of transformation into squamous cell carcinoma is approximately 9% [9]. Current research suggests that the recurrence of IP may be associated with multiple primary infections of Human Papilloma Virus (HPV) and previous surgical interventions, although findings vary across different studies [10].

In recent years, machine learning algorithms have garnered significant interest among researchers due to their proficiency in detecting complex, nonlinear relationships within high-dimensional data and uncovering latent correlations between various factors and diseases. Various interpretable machine learning tools, such as Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) and Variable Importance (VIMP), have been employed to elucidate model interpretations [11, 12]. In nasology, machine learning has been extensively applied to studies on nasal anatomy, rhinosinusitis, and allergic rhinitis [13]. For IP, the current machine learning application focuses on the differential diagnosis based on imaging features. Ramkumar et al. [14] applied machine learning to differentiate between IP and Sinonasal Squamous Cell Carcinoma through MRI images. In addition, Gu et al. [15] applied machine learning to differentiate between IP and malignant sinonasal tumors through MRI images. Subsequently, research has been conducted using convolutional neural networks to classify IP and malignant transformation to squamous cell carcinoma (IP-SCC) from MRI images [16]. In the latest research, automatic machine learning algorithms have been applied to differentiate MRI images of IP and IP-SCC [17]. At the same time, researchers have been also interested in classifying IP and nasal polyps through deep learning [18, 19]. However, machine learning applicated in assessing recurrence post IP surgery remains unexplored. This study aims to evaluate the clinical characteristics and surgical outcomes of IP patients at our center. We utilized different machine learning algorithms to predict the recurrence free survival (RFS) rates of IP patients and to identify potential demographic and clinical predictors of recurrence.

Methods

Study design and population

A retrospective study was conducted on all patients diagnosed with IP at Xijing Hospital from January 2010 to December 2023. Patients who underwent surgical treatment in our hospital and were histopathologically confirmed IP were included in the study. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) incomplete patient information; (2) initial histopathological confirmation of a malignant tumor; (3) follow-up duration of less than 6 months; and (4) histopathological confirmation of tumor recurrence during follow-up.

All patients underwent preoperative nasal endoscopy and computed tomography (CT) of the paranasal sinuses, while magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed for some. The treatment objective for IP was complete tumor resection. All surgeries were conducted by either a deputy chief or chief physician in Otolaryngology Head and Neck Surgery, with meticulous documentation of operation records. Surgical approaches included endoscopic sinus surgery, open surgery, and combined modalities. Specifically, functional endoscopic sinus surgery targeted IP originating from the nasal cavity. For tumors from the lateral, posterior, and superior walls of the maxillary sinus, the natural orifice was enlarged for tumor removal. Tumors from the inferior, anterior, and medial walls of the maxillary sinus, not visible through the natural opening, were addressed via internal diameter sinus surgery using the anterior lacrimal recess approach. Endoscopic sinus surgery was also employed for ethmoid sinus tumors. Partial orbital resection was performed for tumors invading the orbital plate. For sphenoidal sinus tumors, endoscopic sinus surgery utilized the sphenoidal mouth approach. Depending on tumor size, either a Draf IIb or Draf III procedure was conducted for tumors originating from the frontal sinus. Tumors from the pterygopalatine fossa were resected through the anterior lacrimal approach to the posterior wall of the maxillary sinus. In cases where tumors could not be completely resected endoscopically, open surgery was undertaken. Postoperative follow-ups were scheduled at 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and annually thereafter.

Data collection

The baseline characteristics of the patients, including age, sex, smoking, drinking, hypertension, diabetes, IP history, first surgery in our center or outer, nasal operation history, with chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS), Krouse, hyperosteogeny, and bone defects, were determined previous cases information and preoperative imaging examinations. In addition, surgical records were reviewed to ascertain if IP preoperative consideration, the planned IP surgical approach, position, attachment site treatment, base, and the presence of orbital or skull base involvement. Pathological outcomes were derived from histopathological reports. The primary endpoints were IP recurrence and the time to recurrence, defined as the interval between the last operation and the histopathological confirmation of recurrence. It is important to note that the baseline and clinical characteristics were meticulously documented for each treatment episode when a patient underwent multiple surgeries at our hospital due to recurrence.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics Software Version 23 was utilized for statistical analysis. Continuous variables with normal distribution were evaluated using the T test. For binary variables and disordered multicategorical variables, the Chi-square test and Fisher's exact test were applied respectively to assess differences between groups. The Mann–Whitney U test was employed for continuous variables and ordered multicategorical variables that did not follow a normal distribution. Univariate Cox regression was conducted on all variables, while multivariate Cox regression was applied to variables with a p value less than 0.05. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Python version 4.10 was used to generate Kaplan–Meier curves and calculate the RFS.

Machine learning

Machine learning models were developed using Python version 4.10. Initially, the data set was split into an 80% training set (168 samples) and a 20% test set (42 samples), with variable scaling to a 0–1 range implemented via the MinMaxScaler function in the sklearn package. Survival analysis models, including ExtraSurvivalTrees (EST), GradientBoostingSurvivalAnalysis (GBSA), RandomSurvivalForest (RSF), SurvivalSVM, Coxnet and Coxph, were constructed by the sksurv package. In the training set, SMOTE technique is used to resample the recurrent group. Then fivefold cross validation and param grid search was used to determine the optimal hyperparameters for each model and construct the best-performing models. Subsequently, the C-index, time-dependent areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve (time-dependent AUC) and integrated brier score (IBS) for the best-performing model were calculated for both the training and test sets. To estimate the reliability of the performance assessment, a 95% CI was calculated for each performance evaluation by bootstrapping a sample from the training and testing cohort 100 times. Time-dependent ROC curves and time-dependent brier score curves were generated using the sksurv package. Finally, the importance of each feature was ranked using the shap package through SHAP.

Ethics approval

The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Air Force Medical University. Given the retrospective nature of the study, the Ethics Committee waived the requirement for informed consent.

Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to assess the prognostic significance of clinical risk factors for RFS across the study cohort, as detailed in Table 2. Significant risk factors identified include Krouse T4 (p = 0.006, HR = 6.17, 95% CI 1.70–22.36), bone defects (p = 0.001, HR = 2.81, 95% CI 1.57–5.17), involvement of two or more primary disease sites (p = 0.008, HR = 3.77, 95% CI 1.41–10.10), no processing of tumor attachment sites (p = 0.037, HR = 1.99, 95% CI 1.04–3.81), orbital involvement (p = 0.01, HR = 2.41, 95% CI 1.24–4.69), skull base involvement (p = 0.001, HR = 3.26, 95% CI 1.63–6.52), mild or moderate dysplasia (p < 0.001, HR = 4.00, 95% CI 2.07–10.63), and severe dysplasia (p = 0.046, HR = 2.78, 95% CI 1.02–7.59). In addition, mild or moderate dysplasia was identified as an independent risk factor for postoperative recurrence of IP (p = 0.035, HR = 2.93, 95% CI 1.08–7.98).

Model interpretation

SHAP was utilized to visually interpret each model. Figure 4 displays the summary plot and variable importance histograms for the EST, GBSA, and RSF models, organized by variable importance from highest to lowest. In the best-performing EST model, the five most important features were bone defects, orbital involvement, smoking, no processing of tumor attachment sites, and drinking. For the GBSA model, the top features included age, orbital involvement, bone defects, skull base involvement, and IP history. The RSF model highlighted age, no processing of tumor attachment sites, drinking, and Krouse T4 as the five most significant characteristics. Discussion

Main interpretation

In this study, we analyzed a cohort of 210 patients with IP at our center to calculate 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year recurrence free survival rates. We performed Cox regression to identify demographic and clinical features associated with relapse post-IP surgery. Subsequently, we constructed prediction models using various machine learning algorithms and visualized feature importance using SHAP. The bee colony summary map of the EST model revealed that bone defects was the most influential factor for postoperative recurrence. Other significant predictors included orbital involvement, smoking, no processing of tumor attachment sites, drinking, mild or moderate dysplasia, skull base involvement, and Krouse T4. To our knowledge, this study is the first to develop a clinical feature-based interpretable machine learning model for predicting recurrence free survival following IP surgery.

It is well-recognized that IP exhibits a high recurrence rate postoperatively. Previous research indicates that the likelihood of no recurrence follows a bimodal distribution over the duration of follow-up, with specific peaks in recurrence times. In this study, 20.00% of cases experienced a recurrence within an average follow-up period of 44.26 months. The median time to recurrence was 35.50 months. The probabilities of no recurrence at 1, 3, and 5 years were 97.00%, 83.61%, and 75.02%, respectively. These findings are largely in alignment with existing literature [20, 21].

Lifelong follow-up is clinically recommended [10], yet patient compliance is low due to high time and economic costs. Consequently, constructing a predictive model to assess the likelihood of postoperative recurrence is crucial for guiding follow-up strategies. While numerous studies have explored the risk factors for recurrence after IP surgery, few have developed predictive models. Machine learning algorithms, which can identify nonlinear correlations, demonstrate superior performance compared to traditional statistical methods [22]. In addressing survival analysis, many studies classify outcomes at different time points as binary events, which can misrepresent the actual survival outcomes over time [23]. To circumvent these limitations, we utilized the scikit-survive package to construct and validate a prognostic model. Our three models performed consistently well with the training set characteristics and maintained accuracy during the first 55 months of the test set before experiencing a significant drop in predictive effectiveness. Conversely, the other models showed consistent predictive performance for the first 30 months. This divergence likely stems from differing risk factors influencing early versus late recurrence after IP surgery. We hypothesize that early recurrence is predominantly determined by the thoroughness of the surgery, whereas late recurrence may be influenced by factors such as low-risk HPV infection [24, 25]. However, the small number of late recurrence cases and potential increases in machine learning algorithm errors could also reduce the AUC, a hypothesis that warrants verification through larger-scale studies.

To elucidate the importance of variables in machine learning models, we introduced SHAP values, which are instrumental in explaining the interpretability of various models. Although age was not identified as a risk factor in the EST model, SHAP values indicated that younger patients (represented by bluer values) were more prone to relapse in both the GBSA and RSF models. This increased risk in younger individuals may be attributed to more invasive tumors or longer follow-up periods. A German cohort study corroborates this perspective [26]. Regarding lifestyle factors, while different studies present varying conclusions on whether smoking promotes recurrence [21, 27], both smoking and drinking were significant promoters of IP recurrence in our EST model. However, hypertension and diabetes were not identified as potential risk factors. In our study, 50 patients with primary surgery in other hospitals had a slightly higher recurrence rate (26.00%) compared to 18.13% among the other 160 patients with that in our hospital, though the difference was not statistically significant. In addition, neither a history of IP nor prior nasal surgery significantly affected postoperative recurrence. In contrast, a study by Oisín Bugter suggested that a history of nasal surgery could predispose patients to recurrence [28]. Furthermore, research from the University of Pennsylvania highlighted significantly higher inflammation levels in the contralateral nasal cavity in IP, suggesting a link to chronic inflammation [29], although our findings did not support a correlation between chronic sinusitis and postoperative IP recurrence. Lisan et al. found that Krouse T3 paranasal sinus IP posed a higher recurrence risk than T2 [30]. In our data, Krouse T4 was more likely to recur than stage 1, likely due to tumors breaching bone barriers and invading adjacent tissues, complicating surgical removal. The most common sites of origin in our study were the maxillary sinus (40.48%) and the nasal cavity (28.57%), with high recurrence rates observed in tumors originating from multiple sites (70.00%) and the frontal sinus (50.00%). Despite the efficacy of DrafIIb and DrafIII surgeries [31], maintaining the integrity of the frontal sinus proved challenging. A study in South Korea found that the site with the highest recurrence rate after maxillary sinus surgery was the lateral wall [32], which was not thoroughly analyzed in our study. Meanwhile, tumor base, piecemeal resection, and bone defects were not risk factors for postoperative recurrence in IP surgery. Prior research has shown that surgery guided by attachment location significantly reduced recurrence risks [33, 34]. Complete resection during initial surgery is critical for preventing recurrence, as suggested by Healy et al., who found that total removal of bone under the tumor attachment site reduces recurrence rates compared to simpler dissection [35]. In our cohort, not performing special treatment on tumor attachment sites significantly increases the risk of recurrence compared to removing the bone at the attachment site. Patients with bone defects, orbital involvement, or skull base involvement were more susceptible to recurrence. The difficulty in achieving precise resection in the complex anatomy of areas like the pterygopalatine fossa, infratemporal fossa, and surrounding orbital and intracranial tissues often leads to residual tumor tissue, increasing recurrence risks. This conclusion aligns with findings by Wang et al. and supports our observations [36].

Limitations

The study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective study, and the narrow range of sample selection may limit its generalizability. Second, to counter the low incidence of IP and the imbalance between the relapsed and non-relapsed groups, SMOTE resampling and fivefold cross-validation were employed, which might have influenced the model's performance. Future studies should incorporate a large, multicenter data set to validate our conclusions. Third, although the data set was divided into 80% for training and 20% for testing, our model has not undergone external validation. This aspect is crucial and will be addressed in future research.

Conclusion

In this study, the recurrence rate of IP patients was 20.00%, with a median recurrence time of 35.50 months. Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified mild or moderate dysplasia in pathological biopsies as an independent risk factor for IP recurrence. In addition, we employed machine learning algorithms, specifically the EST model within our ML prognosis framework, demonstrating their suitability for clinical research in rare diseases with small sample sizes. The EST model highlighted five key predictors of IP recurrence, including bone defects, orbital involvement, smoking, no processing of tumor attachment sites, drinking.
