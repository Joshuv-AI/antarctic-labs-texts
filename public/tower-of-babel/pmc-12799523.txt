Burden of neural tube defects in the Middle East and North Africa from 1990 to 2021: Findings from the global burden of disease study
Authors: Arash Babaei-Ghazani, Hosna Soleymanzadeh, Hanieh Kariznoee, Hamid Ansari, Faeze Farhadi, Khashayar Danandeh, Homa Seyedmirzaei, Amin Nakhostin-Ansari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveFew studies have examined the burden of neural tube defects (NTDs) in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA), and those that exist are limited in geographic coverage and timeframe. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the burden and epidemiology of NTDs in the MENA region from 1990 to 2021.MethodsWe used Global Burden of Disease (2021) data in our study. We extracted the incidence, death, and Disability-adjusted life years (DALY) rates and age-standardized rates across genders, age groups, MENA countries, and years from 1990 to 2021. Additionally, we extracted the sociodemographic index (SDI) values for each country and MENA from 1990 to 2021.ResultsThe age-standardized DALY rate of NTDs decreased by 72.72 % (95%UI: −83.67, −55.14) from 298.93 (95%UI: 145.34–422) in 1990 to 72.57 (95%UI: 54.89–95.42) per 100000 in 2021 in the region. In 2021, the age-standardized DALY rate of NTDs was the lowest in KSA (11.62 per 100000, 95%UI: 8.47–15.79), Palestine (15.39 per 100000, 95%UI: 11.41–20.28), and Qatar (16.35 per 100000, 95%UI: 12.31–21.4). On the other hand, it was the highest in Afghanistan (185.88 per 100,000, 95 % UI: 104.29–285.14), Yemen (165.46 per 100,000, 95 % UI: 98.46–281.66), and Sudan (150.92 per 100,000, 95 % UI: 99.84–222.41). There was a negative association between SDI and age-standardized DALY rate of NTDs.ConclusionsAlthough the burden of NTDs has declined in the MENA region in recent years, it remains a major public health concern in countries like Afghanistan, Sudan, and Yemen.

Introduction

Neural tube defects (NTDs), congenital structural defects affecting the central nervous system, can cause long-term physical and cognitive disability and mortality in newborns worldwide.1,2 Spina bifida and anencephaly, the most common NTD types, are characterized by distinct features.3,4 NTDs are the second most common congenital malformation (age-standardized rate of 2.1 per 100,000 people worldwide) and therefore are a priority for preventive interventions.2,5,6 However, NTD prevalence considerably varies across countries. The prevalence of spina bifida alone has been reported to be as high as 0.13 % in certain regions, such as Africa.7 The identified risk factors for NTDs include genetic predisposition, maternal nutrition, and environmental factors, all of which can be targeted through preventive measures.8, 9, 10 Strategies for NTD prevention have included raising awareness regarding the importance of folic acid intake, and promoting preconception counseling and care, folic acid supplementation in the periconceptional period, and folic acid fortification.11, 12, 13 Access to appropriate clinical and rehabilitation services is crucial to increase survival rates and long-term quality of life among people with NTDs, yet has substantial direct and indirect healthcare costs for families and healthcare systems.14, 15, 16, 17, 18 However, a 2018 study has indicated that efforts to prevent and address the needs of individuals with NTDs are limited by a lack of accurate prevalence estimates; difficulties in quantifying their overall burden, particularly in resource-limited settings, have hindered effective resource allocation.19

Understanding NTD epidemiology is key to improving clinical management and developing effective prevention programs.20 In line with the World Health Organization's recommended folic acid intake of at least 300 μg/day for women of childbearing age, the widespread use of folic acid, which has been shown to decrease the risk of neural tube defects as much as 75 %, has played a major role in the recent decline in NTD prevalence across developed countries.1,21, 22, 23 However, NTDs remain a public health concern in less developed regions, including Eastern Mediterranean and African countries.24 Most pregnancies affected by NTDs occur in low- and middle-income countries.19 Challenges in folic acid fortification and inadequate access to prenatal care are among the factors contributing to the high NTD prevalence in developing countries.25,26 Additionally, children with NTDs in developing countries often have limited access to essential care.27,28 Therefore, evaluating the epidemiology and NTD burden in these countries is critical for tailoring preventive strategies to communities with high NTD burdens and providing necessary care.

Despite the relatively high NTD prevalence in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA), limited studies on NTDs have been conducted in this region.24 A study on NTD burden in low- and middle-income countries, including most MENA countries, has reported an overall NTD prevalence of approximately 1.67 per 1000 live births.29 A systematic review of 43 studies, including more than 608,384 people across Africa, has indicated an NTD prevalence of 21.42 per 10,000 live births, with particularly high prevalence rates in Algeria and Ethiopia.30 A survey of 13 Arab countries has indicated an NTD prevalence between 0.62 and 13.8 per 1000 births.31 However, no comprehensive analysis of NTD burden in the MENA region has compared burdens across countries and evaluated trends over time. To address these gaps, this study evaluated the NTD burden in the MENA region from 1990 to 2021.

Materials and Methods

Data overview and estimates

We used Global Burden of Disease (2021) data to examine the NTD burden in MENA countries. Details regarding the methods of the GBD 2021 study, which evaluated the burden of 371 diseases and 88 risk factors across 204 countries, have been described elsewhere.32, 33, 34 We analyzed data on NTD burden in the MENA region and its constituent countries, including Afghanistan, Algeria, Bahrain, Egypt, Iran, Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Libya, Morocco, Oman, Palestine, Qatar, KSA, Sudan, Syria, Tunisia, Turkey, United Arab Emirates, and Yemen.

In the GBD 2021 study, NTDs were defined by ICD-10 codes of Q00–Q01.9, Q05–Q05.9, Q07.01, and Q07.03, or ICD-0 codes of 740–741.93 and 742.035. Verbal autopsy, surveillance, and vital registration data were used to estimate the burden of congenital birth defects including NTDs. Verbal autopsy data for individuals older than 5 years were excluded, because of unreliable age patterns. Additionally, data sources from the parent model that included only a subset of congenital birth defects were excluded, to avoid underestimation of the total congenital birth defect burden. Data with suspected systematic underreporting were used only for estimating the burden of subgroups of congenital birth defects. The burden of congenital birth defect subgroups, including NTDs, was estimated with cause-of-death ensemble modeling. For NTDs, the variables tested for the model included in-facility delivery, folic acid intake, folic acid fortification index, healthcare access and quality index, antenatal care coverage (both one and four visits), age-standardized summary exposure values for smoking, household air pollution, fasting plasma glucose, low fruit and vegetable consumption, maternal education, sociodemographic index (SDI), legality of abortion, maternal alcohol consumption during pregnancy, and alcohol consumption per capita, which were selected on the basis of the available literature on factors associated with NTDs and expert opinions. Furthermore, mortality rates were estimated for only age groups younger than 70 years.32,36

Indexes and analyses

We extracted the incidence, death, and disability-adjusted life year (DALY) rates, as well as age-standardized rates, across sexes, age groups, countries, and years from 1990 to 2021. All values are reported per 100,000 population. DALYs were computed as the sum of years of life lost from mortality and years lived with disability (YLDs). In the GBD study, years of life lost from mortality were calculated by multiplication of the number of deaths due to NTDs by the remaining life expectancy at the age of death, whereas YLDs were estimated by multiplication of the prevalence of each condition severity level by its corresponding disability weight.35

We also extracted 95 % uncertainty intervals (95%UIs) for each value. For each estimate in the GBD study, 1000 draws were generated; the mean of these draws represented the final estimate, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles were the 95%UI.32 Additionally, we extracted the SDI values for each country and the entire MENA region from 1990 to 2021. The SDI values ranged from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating more favorable sociodemographic status. Furthermore, the SDI was estimated according to the geometric mean of the fertility rate among individuals younger than 25 years, the average education level of individuals 15 years or older, and lag-distributed income per capita.

R version 4.4.2 and the ggplot2, tidyr, dplyr, tidyverse, stringr, and maps packages were used for the analyses. We used locally estimated scatterplot smoothing regression to evaluate the association between the age-standardized DALY rates for NTDs in MENA countries and the SDI across years.

Results

The age-standardized incidence rate of NTDs decreased by 54.33 % (95%UI: −57.71 to −50.45), from 4.49 (95%UI: 3.77–5.25) in 1990 to 2.05 (95%UI: 1.81–2.3) per 100,000 in 2021 in MENA countries. Furthermore, the age-standardized DALY rate for NTDs decreased by 72.72 % (95%UI: −83.67 to −55.14), from 298.93 (95%UI: 145.34–422) in 1990 to 72.57 (95%UI: 54.89–95.42) per 100,000 in 2021 in the region. Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive overview of NTD burden in the MENA region from 1990 to 2021, to our knowledge. Our findings indicated a significant decline in the age-standardized DALY rate for NTDs during this period. Whereas the age-standardized DALY rate in MENA countries was higher than the global rate in 1990, it fell below the global rate by 2021. However, the NTD burden remained relatively high in countries such as Afghanistan, Yemen, and Sudan. Additionally, we observed a negative association between the SDI and the age-standardized DALY rate for NTDs.

Although the NTD burden was higher in the MENA region than worldwide in 1990, the age-standardized incidence rate of NTDs in MENA countries became similar to the global rate by 2021. However, the age-standardized DALY rate in MENA countries was lower than the global average. Although the global NTD burden has decreased in recent years, the Western, Middle, and Eastern African regions continued to have the highest YLDs worldwide; this phenomenon might have contributed to the observed higher NTD burden worldwide than in the MENA region.37 Both prevention strategies and the availability of surgical treatment for NTDs, particularly in the more developed areas of the region, might have contributed to the decreased DALY rate for NTDs.38,39 However, considerable heterogeneity in NTD burden persisted across the MENA region. Although the recent decreases in NTD burden are promising, the slightly higher age-standardized incidence rate with respect to global rates highlights a need for stronger prevention policies to further decrease regional burdens. One potential solution might involve partnerships with non-governmental organizations to support folic acid supplementation and fortification efforts, particularly in countries such as Afghanistan and Yemen, where the NTD burden is high, and ongoing conflicts might pose additional barriers to such interventions.40,41 Furthermore, given the limited access to prenatal care in rural areas, training community health workers to expand and improve prenatal services in rural areas could enhance prenatal care in the MENA region and contribute to NTD prevention.42,43

Sudan, Yemen, and Afghanistan had the highest NTD burden in the region. Although NTDs have been well studied in Sudan, limited studies on NTDs and their associated factors have been conducted in Yemen and Afghanistan, and future studies must consider the magnitude of the issue in those countries. Several factors might be responsible for the high NTD burden in those countries. A lack of awareness regarding the role of folic acid in preventing NTDs, along with inadequate folic acid intake, are key factors contributing to the high burden and incidence of NTDs in those countries.44 One positive step taken in this regard has been mandatory folic acid fortification in Yemen, which might aid in the primary prevention of NTDs in this country.1,45 Another factor potentially contributing to the high NTD burden in those countries is inadequate prenatal care leading to delayed diagnosis and management of those conditions. A lack of proper prenatal care for many pregnancies in those countries might contribute to the high NTD burden.46, 47, 48, 49 Additionally, ongoing conflicts and wars in those countries further exacerbate limited access to necessary prenatal care.50 War and conflict can increase healthcare costs, even in years following a conflict, thus posing further financial barriers in countries with already limited resources.51 For example, in Yemen, despite improvements in healthcare services before the onset of conflict, the civil war that began in 2014 led to a rapid deterioration in health outcomes, including increased maternal mortality and worsened children's health, and diminished access to healthcare.52 Even after diagnosis, challenges persist in the proper management of NTDs in those countries. In Sudan, and possibly other countries, pregnancies are usually not terminated after the antenatal diagnosis of NTDs.53 This aspect is particularly important, because those countries, like most MENA countries, have predominantly Muslim populations. Islamic regulations on pregnancy termination influence both family decisions and physicians' options for the prenatal management of fetuses diagnosed with NTDs.54 Furthermore, surgical management of NTDs might not be widely available in those countries.55 Given the regional limitations and lack of resources for proper NTD management, the best solution might involve NTD prevention through folic acid fortification and supplementation. International organizations can play crucial roles in supporting those countries by providing both logistical and scientific assistance.56 Given that the prevalence of spina bifida, a major NTD type, has been found to be substantially lower in regions with mandatory folic acid fortification than in regions with voluntary fortification (33.86 vs. 48.35 per 100,000 live births), promoting and supporting the implementation of mandatory fortification programs in those countries might provide an effective and efficient approach to preventing neural tube defects.57

KSA can serve as a model for NTD prevention and management in other countries in the region. KSA not only had the lowest age-standardized DALY rates for NTDs in 2021 but also achieved one of the largest declines in this index from 1990 to 2021. Several factors might have contributed to this success in decreasing the NTD burden in recent years. One key factor is the mandatory folic acid fortification program, which achieved 100 % coverage and was probably crucial in lowering NTD rates across the country.58 Furthermore, although differences exist across studies and samples, Saudi women have generally good knowledge and positive attitudes regarding folic acid intake.58, 59, 60 Furthermore, initiatives such as the King Faisal Specialist Hospital and Research Center NTD registry might have contributed to guiding proper prevention and management of NTD in the country.61 Finally, although physician advice and recommendations remain the primary source of information on the benefits of folic acid supplementation, one study has indicated that more than 30 % of pregnant women in KSA learned about supplementation through social media and awareness campaigns. These findings highlight the important role of social media and public awareness campaigns in increasing knowledge regarding folic acid supplementation for the prevention of NTDs.60

Herein, we observed a negative association between SDI and NTD burden in the MENA region. As described in the methods section, in the GBD study, the SDI consists of three components: the fertility rate among individuals <25 years of age, the average education level of individuals ≥15 years of age, and lag-distributed income per capita. One reason for this association might be how SDI components contribute to NTD burden. For example, previous studies have associated lower maternal education levels with greater risk of having a child with NTDs; this risk might be exacerbated in neighborhoods where most residents have low levels of education.62, 63, 64 Therefore, in countries with higher educational levels, where both community members and pregnant women are more likely to have higher educational levels and to be aware of the benefits of folic acid intake for preventing NTDs, a lower NTD burden can be expected.65 Furthermore, another reason for the negative association between SDI and NTD burden might be that increases in the gross domestic product are associated with lower mortality and YLDs due to NTDs.37

Although this study is the first, to our knowledge, to examine NTD burden in the MENA region, several limitations must be considered. First, this study has the general limitations of GBD studies, including a reliance on estimates, particularly in regions with limited available data. Considerable variations in data collection and reporting methods, data quality, and data coverage of congenital anomalies, including NTDs, exist across regions and countries in the GBD study. This issue is likely to be more pronounced in the MENA region, which includes several developing countries and countries affected by conflict. Limited resources in those countries might result in incomplete surveillance systems, thus ultimately leading to underreporting of the congenital anomalies including NTDs. Although advanced statistical methods have been used to address these issues and heterogeneities, they might still influence the reliability and accuracy of estimates.35 However, importantly, having some form of estimate is better than having no data, and these estimates can serve as a foundation for future research on the epidemiology of NTDs in the MENA region.32 Second, we did not assess how different risk factors might contribute to NTD burden across countries and demographic groups; this topic may be investigated in future studies in the region. Future studies may also focus on the specific barriers contributing to the high NTD burden in countries such as Yemen, Afghanistan, and Sudan. Experiences of success in countries such as KSA, such as the implementation of registries for congenital anomalies and NTDs, could inform strategies in other countries across the region. Third, the cost-effectiveness of various preventive strategies, such as maternal education campaigns and fortification programs, may be further evaluated in future studies. Finally, because we did not evaluate the trends in the burdens of various types of NTDs separately, future studies are needed to provide a more detailed assessment of these trends.

Conclusion

Although the NTD burden has declined in the MENA region in recent years, it remains a major public health concern in countries including Afghanistan, Sudan, and Yemen. The successes of countries such as KSA in effectively decreasing the NTD burden can serve as a model for others in the region to implement policies aimed at further lowering NTD rates. Given the ongoing conflicts and limited resources in these countries, international efforts may be necessary to provide the scientific expertise and resources to strengthen preventive strategies and improve prenatal care in these countries.
