Assessing waste reduction knowledge and practices in the operating room
Authors: Hannah Wavering-Walters, Quetzal A Class, Lopa K Pandya
Published: 2026

Despite recent efforts to decrease our carbon footprint, the United States continues to be a top contributor to greenhouse gas emissions and energy usage worldwide [1]. Healthcare systems are responsible for using a significant proportion of fossil fuels and for producing substantial carbon emissions and solid waste [2]. Of the solid waste produced in the hospital setting, approximately 70% of hospital solid waste is from operating rooms and labor and delivery units [3].

Waste can be broken down into two types: non-hazardous and hazardous. Non-hazardous or general waste is believed to not pose biological, chemical, radioactive, or physical health hazards. Approximately 85% of medical waste can be considered non-hazardous waste and 15% hazardous [4]. A JAMA Surgery review identified reduction and segregation of operating room waste as the greatest opportunity to effectively reduce surgical waste [3]. One study found that 70% of the hazardous waste stream contains nonhazardous waste, indicating frequent misclassification of waste and suggesting that a significant barrier to correct waste allocation was physician knowledge [5], [6]. Despite this, however, few studies assess surgical staff knowledge on recycling and waste allocation in the operating room [5], [6], [7].

To address this gap in knowledge, we performed a two-part observational study that focused on addressing waste allocation and provider knowledge. Our findings shed light on whether providers have knowledge and behave in a way that aligns with proper waste disposal.

Part one was an observational study of waste allocation within the operating room. With the aim of examining possible associations between the amount of waste weight produced and patient and procedure factors, we quantified operating room waste during hysteroscopy and collected patient and procedure characteristics from the electronic medical record. Hazardous and nonhazardous waste from 27 total procedures was weighed, and patient and procedure characteristics were collected. The average total waste produced was 7.8 kg (SD = 2.08) with an average weight of 7.4 kg (SD = 1.52) for nonhazardous waste. Only three (11%) of the 27 cases observed utilized the hazardous receptacle. No patient factors were significantly associated with amount of nonhazardous waste weight produced. However, we did find a trend between having had previous gynecologic surgeries and amount of nonhazardous waste weight was identified (r = 0.35, p = .08). Further research may show that this association indicates that more difficult cases require the use of more instrumentation and supplies.

Part two involved the distribution of an anonymous, online survey focusing on surgical staff attitudes and knowledge of waste allocation and recycling. The survey was adapted with permission from a previously validated multicenter study [7] and included a novel knowledge test that asked participants to select which items were recyclable. The survey was sent to all operating room staff across all departments. A total of 75 survey responses were received (13% response rate). A majority of participants stated that they cared about appropriate waste allocation in operating rooms, would be willing to alter their workflow to accommodate improved waste allocation, and would like to learn more about it. We also found that both respondent interest and care about waste allocation was associated with increased correct number of identified recyclable items; however, self-reported knowledge of recyclable items was not associated with correct identification of recyclable items. Our results support the idea that physician knowledge is a barrier to correct waste allocation and interventions aimed at improving physician or operating room staff knowledge may improve correct waste allocation. Future studies in this area can include investigating operating room staff attitude and response to specific operating room interventions, such as increased signage.

Previous studies suggest that physicians tend to overutilize hazardous waste bins, possibly due to lack of knowledge [6]. While our current findings show that hazardous waste bins were underutilized, our takeaway message underscores that operating room waste is not allocated correctly. Both underutilization and overutilization of hazardous waste pose environmental and institutional problems. Underutilization of hazardous waste bins, such as that noted in this study, can lead to disposal of untreated waste in landfills that can cause water contamination, inadvertent exposure to pathogens, and even cost institutions in the form of financial penalties and lawsuits [8]. Overutilization of the hazardous waste bins leads to unnecessary incineration of waste, which has both environmental [5] and financial impacts. One study concluded that most healthcare workers understandably err on the side of caution and dispose of most waste as hazardous waste, increasing the cost and environmental impact associated with hazardous waste processing [6]. Another study found a 30% reduction in hazardous waste led to an annual costs savings of $694,141 after education was provided on correct allocation of waste to staff [9]. A similar study showed over a $200,000 annual saving in waste removal fees after education and signage was provided to staff [10]. Together, prior studies and our findings from the current multimodal study suggest that both waste allocation and cost savings can be improved with education of surgeons and operating room staff.

As the healthcare sector continues to contribute substantially to environmental harm, addressing operating room waste is no longer optional, but essential. Given a demonstrated gap between provider interest and knowledge, healthcare institutions should prioritize educational interventions, standardized recycling protocols, and provide clear signage to improve waste allocation. Incorporating these interventions may impact operating room sustainability and overall environmental impact from hospitals in a meaningful way. Future studies should evaluate the effectiveness of these interventions across diverse surgical specialties and environments to help establish best practices.
