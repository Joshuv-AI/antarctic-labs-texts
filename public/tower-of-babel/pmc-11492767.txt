Research capacity and culture: a survey of Australian Orthopaedic Association members
Authors: Luke McCarron, Steven Watson, Dave Parsons, Luke Robinson, Lisa O’Brien, Arvind Puri, Annabelle Stabler
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStrong research capacity and culture (RCC) within professional groups predict engagement in high-quality research and the integration of evidence into practice. However, factors affecting RCC participation and development are currently unknown in Australian Orthopaedic Surgeons.Study designCross-sectional survey.MethodsAll Australian Orthopaedic Association members were invited to complete the individual domain of the validated Research Capacity and Culture tool.ResultsOne hundred and sixty-three Australian Orthopaedic Association members (9%) provided survey answers. Survey findings indicated that AOA members scored moderate or high for most RCC domains (14 out of 15). Barriers to the completion of research included a lack of time, other work roles taking priority, and a lack of funds for research.ConclusionThe Australian Orthopaedic Association members surveyed in this study demonstrated adequate research skills and experience, although reported they could benefit from additional support securing funding and navigating specific research tasks. Increasing future collaboration with academic institutions and clinician-researcher roles may enhance the output of high-quality orthopaedic research in Australia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-024-05108-w.

Introduction

Research capacity building focuses on developing the skills and abilities to conduct high-quality, sustainable research [1–3]. This can involve training, infrastructure development, or access to information or technologies that promote continuous learning and growth. Research capacity is closely linked to research culture, which reflects the attitudes of individuals and organisations about completing research tasks and activities [1, 3]. There is a growing body of literature regarding the benefits of a positive research capacity and culture (RCC) in healthcare, highlighting a benefit to the consumer, health professionals, and the wider community [3, 4]. Increased engagement in research activities has been shown to boost overall productivity and job satisfaction, improving consumer outcomes by translating new research into everyday clinical practice [5]. At an individual level, RCC may encompass a researcher’s skills, knowledge, and motivation to conduct high-quality, impactful studies. Researchers who foster a strong individual research culture tend to collaborate, stay current with advancements in their field and are committed to responsibly disseminating findings, thus boosting professional and organisational capacity.

RCC can be measured using the RCC tool, a standardised outcome measure to identify research capacity [1]. The tool requires participants to answer questions about self-reported success or skill, exploring various aspects of perceived barriers and motivators for undertaking research. Several studies have implemented the RCC tool across various health professional groups. Parsons, Ross, Glasgow, Robinson, McCarron and O’Brien [3] used the RCC tool to survey Australian hand therapists, Williams and Lazzarini [6] explored research capacity in podiatrists, and Harper, Taylor, Jepiuh, Mino, Huynh Tran, Tam and Harris [7] investigated barriers and facilitators for occupational therapists at one tertiary hospital in Western Australia. Some consistent barriers identified between groups included a lack of dedicated research time, poor access to additional funding, and the need for research mentoring/support for some clinician-researchers. Conversely, research motivators included increasing career advancement opportunities and developing new skills. Despite this, it is currently unknown whether all health professional groups in allied health or medicine experience the same barriers and facilitators.

The Australian Orthopaedic Association (AOA) is Australia’s peak professional organisation for orthopaedic surgery [8]. The AOA is a not-for-profit organisation that provides high-quality specialist education, training and continuing professional development for Australian orthopaedic surgeons. The AOA has more than 1750 members, mainly consisting of orthopaedic consultants and trainees (registrars) spanning all orthopaedic subspecialties. The AOA supports scientific research and orthopaedic humanitarian initiatives in Australia and overseas. However, it is unknown whether Orthopaedic doctors feel they have the necessary skills to complete research projects to the required standard. As such, this study aims to use the RCC tool to measure AOA members’ current self-rated individual research skills, including barriers and facilitators to research engagement. Findings could help the AOA provide better training and support for AOA members, leading to improved success and impact on their research projects.

Materials and methods

Study design

A cross-sectional online survey using the Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys (CHERRIES) reporting guidelines was conducted [9]. Institutional ethics approval was granted before commencement (HREC/2023/QGC/96775).

Participants

The participants were AOA members, “registered medical practitioners who possess the qualification FRACS(Orth) from the Royal Australasian College of Surgeons or its equivalent and who have been elected a Fellow”, fellows or registrars in training to become an orthopaedic surgeon, or medical practitioners with a special interest in orthopaedic surgery [10].

Procedure

Data were collected using the Qualtrics online survey tool between October 2023 and January 2024. Participants were provided four months to open and complete the survey. All current AOA members (N = 1800) were invited to participate in the study by email, which the AOA distributed as gatekeeper. The AOA sent a second ‘reminder’ email eight weeks after data collection commenced. Survey data were securely stored on Qualtrics.

Study participation was voluntary, with no incentives (financial or other) to participate. AOA members were emailed the Participant Information Form (PIF) and provided an explanatory statement on the survey landing page. Implied consent was obtained when AOA members read the PIF and chose to click the link to commence the survey, which could be paused and resumed later.

Survey tool

The RCC tool is a reliable and valid measure with strong internal consistency and high test-retest reliability [1]. It consists of three domains: (i) the individual, (ii) the professional group (team), and (iii) the organisation [1]. While the tool can measure the three domains, this study sought to specifically investigate the individual research skills of AOA members for the following reasons. It is common for many AOA members to work or complete their research projects in small group settings or isolation, either in private practice or rural/remote settings. It was deemed unfeasible for AOA members to complete all three RCC domains (45–60 min) due to known time constraints, as many of the members are time-poor. As such, by focusing on the individual domain (15–20 min), the likelihood of survey completion was increased.

The survey consisted of two sections: general demographics and the RCC tool (Supplementary Table 1). General demographics included gender, years of experience, workplace setting, employment status, and geographical location. The individual domain of the RCC Tool consists of 15 questions on a 10-point numerical scale, where one is the lowest possible level of skill or success, and ten is the highest possible level of skill or success. Participants could also select the additional option of “unsure.” AOA members were asked about perceived barriers and motivators for performing research using an open-text option. All questions were optional, and answers were anonymous. A back button was provided to allow AOA members to review and change their responses before submission.

Data analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27) was used to clean and analyse data. Participants were included in the analysis if they provided demographic information and at least one response for the RCC Tool, motivators, or barriers. Demographic data was reported using descriptive statistics. Mean and standard deviations were reported for continuous data, median and interquartile range (IQR) for ordinal data (1–10), and frequencies and percentages for categorical data. Results from the RCC tool were analysed as ordinal data and IQR, with median scores reported. The RCC Tool data were interpreted as “low” (median < 4), “moderate” (4-6.9), and “high” (> 7) success [11–13].

Results

Response metrics

A total of 177 AOA members opened the survey, with 163 respondents (approximately 9% of the AOA membership) choosing to complete the descriptive survey questions, and 159 respondents completing the RCC tool.

Participant characteristics

The AOA member demographic data is presented in Table 1. Respondents were predominantly male (83%), with ‘Consultant’ surgeons representing (84%) of the surveyed population. Consultant years of orthopaedic experience ranged from less than 1 to 44 years (median: 18 years, IQR: 23 years). Most respondents worked in a mix of public and private settings (58%). The most common geographical locations of practice were Victoria (36%), Queensland (26%), New South Wales (16%), and Western Australia (12%), with most respondents working in a Metropolitan area (79%). Full-time employment was reported by 100 respondents (61%), followed by ‘self-employed’ for 36 respondents (22%). An Adult patient caseload was most commonly reported (93%), with the most common sub-specialties representing knee (24%), pelvis and hip (19%) and trauma (14%).

Qualifications

Sixty-two respondents (36%) possessed a post-graduate qualification by coursework, and 40 (23%) possessed a postgraduate qualification by research (including honours). Eleven (7%) respondents indicated they were currently enrolled in a higher degree by research, and 57 (35%) respondents were currently or previously working in a research position.

Research activity

Forty-two respondents (26%) indicated that research activities were included in their job description, and 20 respondents (12%) were ‘Unsure’. Research activities completed in the last 12 months included conducting a literature review (99 respondents, 61%), writing or submitting an ethics application or research protocol (77 respondents, 47%), data collection as part of an approved research project (103 respondents, 63%), analysing qualitative research data (59 respondents, 36%), analysing quantitative research data (78 respondents, 48%), writing a manuscript for publication (86 respondents, 49%), presenting research findings at a conference (92 respondents, 56%), applying for research funding (42 respondents, 26%), completing a quality improvement project (38 respondents, 23%), or supervising a student or junior clinician to complete a research project (7 respondents, 4%). Only 29 respondents (18%) indicated that they had not participated in any research activities.

Resource provision

Eighty-one respondents (28%) indicated that ‘No provisions’ were made to complete research as part of their role. Other members reported being provided with ‘Library access’ (22%), ‘Software access’ (11%), ‘Research supervision’ (11%) and ‘Administrative support’ (10%).

Individual and professional motivators and barriers

The most reported motivators (enablers) for individual respondents were to improve patient outcomes (109, 69%), keep the brain stimulated (78, 49%), or desire to prove a theory/hunch (76, 48%) (see Table 2). While the common barriers were identified as a lack of time for doing research (122, 78%), other work roles taking priority (100, 64%), and lack of funds for research (88, 56%), the desire for work/life balance (78, 50%), lack of support from management (69, 44%) and other personal commitments (63, 40%) (see Table 3).

When asked how research culture could be improved or better supported, common responses included the allocation of dedicated research time, more accessible funding or research grants, increased research support or mentorship, stronger collaboration with universities or health professionals with academic experience, assistance with research related tasks such as statistical analysis and ethics applications, higher recognition for doctors with research experience, additional credit or acknowledgment for the completion of higher degrees (PhD, MPhil), and the prioritisation of longer-term studies such as randomised control trials.

RCC tool scores

The highest-rated individual skills were related to finding relevant literature (med = 8, IQR = 7–9) and critically reviewing the literature (med = 8, IQR = 6–9) (see Table 4). Other areas of strength included collecting data (e.g. surveys, interviews) (med = 7, IQR = 5–8), writing a research report (med = 7, IQR = 4–8), writing for publication in peer-reviewed journals (med = 7, IQR = 4–8), and integrating research findings into practice (med = 7, IQR = 5–9). The lowest rated included securing research funding (med = 3, IQR = 1–5) and analysing qualitative research data (med = 4, IQR = 3–7).

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the current RCC of AOA members in Australia using a standardised and validated tool. The AOA members that completed the survey rated their skills highly in several research tasks, such as finding relevant literature, critically reviewing the literature, collecting data, writing a research report, writing for publication in peer-reviewed journals, and integrating research findings into practice. This population rated themselves as moderate or high for 14 out of the 15 research tasks being scored. In contrast, the findings of several previously completed RCC surveys for allied health professionals reported lower confidence levels across the same research tasks [3, 11]. For example, in a recent survey of Australian hand therapists, 8 out of the 15 tasks were reported as low [3]. While it is unclear why the AOA members in this survey reported a higher level of confidence than their allied health colleagues, one explanation may be the requirement to complete research while in training to become an orthopaedic surgeon.

Several barriers and enablers were identified in this study. The enablers identified in this study aligned with the results reported in other studies investigating RCC among allied health professions [3, 6, 11]. The top responses in the individual domain of the RCC tool were related to finding relevant literature, critically reviewing the literature, and integrating that literature into practice. Conversely, respondents felt the least confident advising less experienced researchers, analysing data (quantitative and qualitative), writing an ethics application, and securing research funding, highlighting the need for additional funding and support for clinician-researchers in the AOA. Multiple barriers included a lack of time for doing research, other work roles taking priority, and a lack of funds for research. These were found to be similar to barriers identified by allied health professions, suggesting these barriers may be universally experienced by health practitioners and not unique to the AOA member community [3, 6, 11].

The findings of this survey present an opportunity for RCC growth and improvement. Some strategies and suggestions to improve RCC include increasing research opportunities and funding for junior AOA members to get involved in research, providing mentoring and supervision, or pursuing collaborations with universities or other research institutions. It is recognised that some public and privately funded providers/organisations may have already implemented many of these strategies, resulting in significant and high-quality research output. Despite this, investing resources and funding to support developing clinician-researchers could boost RCC in this population further, promoting improved career satisfaction, retention, and advancement.

Limitations

AOA members were exclusively surveyed, and as such medical practitioners outside of orthopaedics are not represented in this study. Due to the low participation rate (9%), there is a high risk of response bias. This limits the generalisability of the findings and reduced the ability to investigate confounding variables in the respondents’ RCC using advanced statistics. Response rates were, however, similar to other RCC studies of comparable design [3, 4, 6]. Additionally, the study population was derived exclusively from Australia, which may limit the generalisability of results to other countries. Furthermore, while outside the scope of this study, the completion of a focus group or semi-structured interviews would also have been beneficial in gaining a deeper understanding of the barriers and motivators identified in this study.

Conclusion

The AOA members surveyed in this study demonstrated sufficient research skills, experience, and knowledge to complete various research-related tasks. AOA members who responded indicated they may benefit from additional guidance and support when securing research funding or navigating specific research tasks (statistical analysis, ethics submissions). Opportunities for improvement include increasing collaboration with universities or other research institutions and the addition of clinician-researcher roles. However, additional research is required to confirm these findings.
