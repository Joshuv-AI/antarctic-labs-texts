Abstract
We discuss a nondeterministic variant of the recently introduced machine model of deterministic auxiliary depth- k k storage automata (or aux- k k -sda’s) by Yamakami. It was proven that all languages recognized by polynomial-time logarithmic-space aux- k k -sda’s are located between LOGDCFL \mathrm{LOGDCFL} and SC k \mathrm{SC}^{k} (the k k th level of Steve’s class SC). We further propose a new and simple computational model of semi-unbounded fan-in Boolean circuits composed partly of cascading blocks, in which the first few AND gates of unbounded fan-out (called AND (ω) gates) at each layer from the left (where all gates at each layer are indexed from left to right) are linked in a “cascading” manner to their right neighbors though specific AND and OR gates. We use this new circuit model to characterize a nondeterministic variant of the aux- 2 ​ k 2k -sda’s (called aux- 2 ​ k 2k -sna’s) that run in polynomial time using logarithmic work space. By relaxing the requirement for cascading circuits, we also demonstrate how such cascading circuit families characterize the complexity class P \mathrm{P} . This yields an upper bound on the computational complexity of LOG ​ k ​ SNA \mathrm{LOG}k\mathrm{SNA} by P \mathrm{P} . Keywords. auxiliary storage automata, LOG k k SNA, semi-unbounded fan-in circuit, cascading circuit, frozen blank sensitivity, NSC k

2.3
Frozen Blank Sensitivity and Weak Depth Susceptibility

Finally, we introduce another computational model of

(or

We further demand that either the input-tape head or the storage-tape head (or both) must move at any step.

To see the complexity of

-XOR Problem:

This problem can be solvable by an appropriate

-sna, which first reads and writes

Yamakami
[

studied two essentially different restrictions of

-sda’s on the behavior of their input-tape heads and the usage of their inner states while scanning specific storage-tape symbols. In the

and (2) an inner state cannot change while reading the frozen blank symbol

The same paper asserted in
[
21

that depth-susceptible

-sda’s precisely characterize
\mathrm{DCFL}
(i.e., the class of all deterministic content-free languages).

In this work, nevertheless, we wish to explore the potential of another notion for a precise characterization of
\mathrm{DCFL}
as well as
\mathrm{CFL}
.
We say that a
k
-sna is
(frozen) blank sensitive
if its input-tape head must make a stationary move while reading the frozen blank symbol

on a storage tape on any computation path. Notice that any depth-susceptible

-sna is (frozen) blank sensitive. For clarity, the notation
\mathrm{2SDA}_{FBS}
is used for the collection of all languages recognized by (frozen) blank-sensitive 2-sda’s.

Proposition 2.2

2\mathrm{SDA}_{FBS}=\mathrm{DCFL}
.

To prove this proposition, we require the key lemma, Lemma

.
As a slightly weaker notion of depth-susceptibility, here we introduce another notion of weak depth-susceptible.
A

-sna

if, while a storage-tape head is scanning symbols

in
\Gamma^{(k)}

, then an inner state cannot be altered along each computation path of

As an immediate consequence of weak depth-susceptibility, once a

-sna enters a blank region, either it makes a turn or it continues moving deterministically in one direction (as long as an associated computation path terminates).

In our intended simulations between aux-
k
-sna’s and cascading circuits explained in Section
4

O(\log{n})
space-bounded auxiliary (work) tapes.
For this very reason, we introduce another tool that partially describes “configurations”, which are conventionally called

. Since a computation path is a series of configurations, it also induces a series of corresponding surface configurations.
Formally, a surface configuration
C
is of the form
(q,l_{1},l_{2},w,l_{3},a)

C^{\prime}=(q,l_{1},l_{2},w,l_{3},u)

is referred to as an

of

We remark that each surface configuration completely ignores the content
u
of the storage tape except for the
l_{3}
th tape cell. For later convenience, we write
head_{3}(C)
instead of
l_{3}
.
Different from a configuration, such a surface configuration
C
is generally obtained from the information on the surface configuration at the previous step and also the previous content of storage-tape cell
l_{3}
because a tape head modifies the tape cell before it moves away in a specific direction at the next step. When this tape cell is already frozen blank, nevertheless, the latter information is not necessary since the tape cell is frozen forever.
As special surface configurations, we consider the
initial surface configuration

P_{0}
and
accepting surface configurations

P_{acc}
defined as

Slightly abusing the terminology, we succinctly say that a surface configuration

(resp., a

2.3

Let us consider an arbitrary gate of

We inductively follow paths of

(ω)

Since there are polynomially many gates in

denote any cascading semi-circuit of

Hereafter, we will explain how to evaluate each gate in

To help understand the following evaluation procedure, we intend to exemplify each evaluation step for

using the circuit example of Fig.

space-bounded auxiliary tape to store the current gate label and search the encoding
\langle G_{n}\rangle
written on an input tape for its connecting gates. We begin with gate
2
and continue traversing gates
3
and
6

4
on a storage tape and move to its left child, gate 3. This record will be used later to traverse a subcircuit rooted at gate
4

We further record the label of gate 3 together with gate
7
and move to its left child, gate 6.
If we nondeterministically choose gate 23, then we follow a corresponding link to gate 11. Here, we record the label of gate 11 as well as gate
6

From gate 31, we start backtracking upward to gate 3 as follows. We first evaluate gate 23. If its value is “true”, then we revise the record on gates 23 and 6 by erasing the record of gate 6 and move to gate 3. On reaching gate 3, we erase the record on gates

Since the modification of the auxiliary tape is strictly prohibited by the weak depth-susceptibility, we move the storage-tape head to a non-frozen-blank tape cell and start simulating gates

When we reach gate 28, we retrieve the record on gate 28 and the value of gate 31 from the storage tape and evaluate the value of gate 28.
We then backtrack upward to gate 2 to complete the evaluation of the target subcircuit

In what follows, we intend to formalize the above evaluation procedure.

(1) Initially, we set
g
to be the root (i.e., the output gate) of
C_{n}
. As the first move of
M
, we store (the label of) the root onto the initially-blank cell block of the storage tape and move the storage-tape head to the right.

(2) Assume that
g
is an AND gate (of fan-in
2
and fan-out

. Let

be these two gates appearing in

in order from left to right. In Fig.

(a) If this is the first time visiting

onto an initially-blank cell block. We then reset

to be

and move to the right for the next inductive step. This process is intended to evaluate the outcome of a subcircuit rooted at

(b) If we reach

to be

, delete the record on

by replacing it by a series of

so that we will try to evaluate the outcome of a subcircuit rooted at

(3) Assume that

is an OR gate, which is not in any cascading block but is directly connected to input gates, OR gates, AND gates, and AND

(4) Assume that

is an AND
(ω)
gate (of fan-in

\geq 1

It is possible to check whether or not the current AND
(ω)
gate is in a certain cascading block of length
\geq 2

. Thus, there is no need to read any record of an AND

By this moment, the record on a top AND

It is important to note that we should avoid any movement of input-tape and auxiliary-tape heads while reading

Next, we consider the case where

(a) Assume that

(ω)

(b) Assume that

(c) Otherwise, let

(5) Assume that

(6) In the case where the storage-tape head returns to the start cell, we accept if the root of

The above simulation nondeterministically checks all paths of the cascading semi-circuit

from its leaves to the root and evaluates all gates correctly.

By the above construction together with

, we conclude that

is well-defined. Since

has size at most

,

. The space usage of the auxiliary (work) tape of

is

. Notice that the content of each storage-tape cell is modified at most

Next, we will explain how to simulate the cascading circuit

, which may consist of two or more cascading semi-circuits. More precisely, we assume that

is made up of a basis subcircuit, say,

and a number of cascading semi-circuits, say,

of

We first simulate

by running depth-first search using a storage tape by recording the information on the choice of inputs to each AND gate whenever we encounter an AND gate. If we reach a leaf

Since any two cascading semi-circuits are disjoint, after each cascading semi-circuit is simulated and evaluated, we move the storage-tape head away to a new
\Box

.

5
In Connection to P

In Section
4
, we have argued how families of polynomial-size semi-unbounded fan-in
k
-cascading circuits with logarithmic alternations characterize (frozen) blank-sensitive aux-
2k
-sna’s running in polynomial time using only
O(\log{n})
work space. Here, we turn our attention to
\mathrm{P}
and argue how to relate cascading circuit families to
\mathrm{P}
. To generalize cascading circuits, we intend to remove the upper bounds of cascading length and alternations of cascading circuit families. We then obtain a cascading-circuit characterization of
\mathrm{P}
.

Proposition 5.1

The complexity class
\mathrm{P}
coincides with
\mathrm{CCIRcasc,\!alt,\!size}(n^{O(1)},n^{O(1)},n^{O(1)})
.

Proof.
It is well-known that uniform families of polynomial-size Boolean circuits precisely characterize
\mathrm{P}
.
Firstly, we show that
\mathrm{P}\subseteq\mathrm{CCIRcasc,\!alt,\!size}(n^{O(1)},n^{O(1)},n^{O(1)})
. This is obvious because a standard Boolean circuit is also a cascading circuit with no cascading block.

Secondly, we show that
\mathrm{CCIRcasc,\!alt,\!size}(n^{O(1)},n^{O(1)},n^{O(1)})\subseteq\mathrm{P}
. Given a family
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
n^{O(1)}
-cascading circuits of
n^{O(1)}
alternations and
n^{O(1)}
size. We intend to evaluate the outcome of
G_{n}
by simulating the behavior of
G_{n}
on an appropriate DTM
M
using
n^{O(1)}
space and
n^{O(1)}
time.
Since
G_{n}
has at most
p(n)
gates, we list up all these gates onto a work tape of a DTM in order. We start with input gates of
G_{n}
and evaluate
G_{n}
layer by layer. We write their values on a work tape as follows.
Note that AND gates are viewed as a special form of AND
(ω)
gates. We thus evaluate each AND
(ω)
gate to be true if all inputs to this gate are “true”, and we evaluate each OR gate to be true if at least one of inputs to this gate is true. After all gates are evaluated, we check whether the output gate of
G_{n}
is true. Since the above procedure can be carried out in polynomial time,
M
is a polynomial-time DTM simulating
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

\Box

An immediate consequence of Proposition
5.1
is an upper-bound on the computational complexity of
\mathrm{AuxSNA}\mathrm{depth,\!space,\!time}(k,O(\log{n}),n^{O(1)})_{FBS}
.

Corollary 5.2

For any constant
k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,
\mathrm{AuxSNA}\mathrm{depth,\!space,\!time}(k,O(\log{n}),n^{O(1)})_{FBS}\subseteq\mathrm{P}
.

Proof.

For simplicity, we write
{\cal L}(k)
for
\mathrm{AuxSNA}\mathrm{depth,\!space,\!time}(k,O(\log{n}),n^{O(1)})_{FBS}
.
Firstly, we consider the even case
2k
for an arbitrary integer
k\geq 1
. By Proposition
4.5
,
{\cal L}(2k)
coincides with
\mathrm{CCIRcasc,\!alt,\!size}(k,O(\log{n}),n^{O(1)})
. By Proposition
5.1
, we conclude that
{\cal L}(2k)
is included in
\mathrm{P}
. Secondly, we consider the odd case
2k+1
. Since
{\cal L}(2k)\subseteq{\cal L}(2k+1)\subseteq{\cal L}(2k+2)
, it follows that
{\cal L}(2k+1)
is also included in
\mathrm{P}
.

\Box

In the end, we remark that, since
\mathrm{LOG}(k\mathrm{SNA}_{FBS})\subseteq\mathrm{AuxSNA}\mathrm{depth,\!space,\!time}(k,O(\log{n}),n^{O(1)})_{FBS}
holds by a simple simulation, Corollary
5.2
yields an upper bound of the computational complexity of
\mathrm{LOG}(k\mathrm{SNA}_{FBS})
by
\mathrm{P}
.

6
Brief Discussions and Future Research Directions

We have studied aux-
k
-sna’s, which are nondeterministic variants of aux-
k
-sda’s proposed in
[
21
]
, and we have presented its characterization in terms of uniform families of polynomial-size semi-unbounded fan-in Boolean circuits with logarithmic alternations, called
k
-cascading circuits
built up from length-
k
cascading blocks, composed of semi-unbounded fan-in gates together with AND
(ω)
gates of bounded fan-in and unbounded fan-out.

For future research, we wish to list eight key open questions associated with aux-
k
-sna’s and
k
-cascading circuits.

1.

To achieve our characterization, in essence, we have required two specific conditions of aux-
k
-sna’s: weak depth-susceptibility and no frozen blank turn. As shown in Lemma
2.5
, these conditions are immediately induced from another notion of (frozen) blank sensitivity. We now wonder if these conditions can be further relaxed or eliminated completely. If so, the elimination of the conditions further simplifies the model of the aux-
k
-sna’s.

2.

The computational model of cascading circuits is a simple modification of the standard circuit model by supplementing cascading blocks. We dare to ask whether or not there are any “natural” computational models, completely different from cascading circuits, which precisely capture the behaviors of aux-
k
-sna’s.

3.

Our circuit characterization works for
\mathrm{AuxSNA}\mathrm{depth,\!space,\!time}(2k,O(\log{n}),n^{O(1)})_{FBS}
with an arbitrary positive integer
k
. How can we modify the definition of cascading circuits to establish an exact characterization of
\mathrm{AuxSNA}\mathrm{depth,\!space,\!time}(k,O(\log{n}),n^{O(1)})_{FBS}
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
in terms of circuit families?

4.

The probabilistic variant of Hibbard’s limited automata was discussed extensively in
[
19
]
. It is of importance to look into a similar probabilistic variant of
k
-sna’s and explore its characteristic features for a better understanding of storage automata.

5.

In Section
5
, the removal of the upper bounds of cascading length and alternations significantly increases the computational complexity of cascading circuits. If we expand only the cascading length from
k
to
O(\log^{t}{n})
, where
t
is any constant in
\mathbb{N}^{+}
, then what is the computational complexity of
\mathrm{CCIRcasc,\!alt,\!size}(O(\log^{t}{n}),O(\log{n}),n^{O(1)})
?

6.

We have introduced
\mathrm{LOG}k\mathrm{SNA}
based on
k
-sna’s. Is it true that
\mathrm{LOG}k\mathrm{SDA}\neq\mathrm{LOG}k\mathrm{SNA}
and
\mathrm{LOG}k\mathrm{SNA}\neq\mathrm{LOG}(k+1)\mathrm{SNA}
for any number
k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
?

7.

Does
\mathrm{AuxSNA}\mathrm{depth,\!space,\!time}(k,O(\log{n}),n^{O(1)})_{FBS}
coincide with
\mathrm{LOG}k\mathrm{SNA}_{FBS}
? If so, can we expand it to the case of
\mathrm{LOG}k\mathrm{SDA}
?

8.

In
[
21
, Lemma 2.1]
,
\mathrm{DCFL}
is characterized by depth-susceptible 2-sda’s, whereas we characterize
\mathrm{DCFL}
using (frozen) blank-sensitive 2-sda’s in Proposition
2.2
. What is the relationship between these two notions over
k
-sda’s for any
k\geq 3
? More specifically, is it true that
k\mathrm{SDA}_{suseptible}=k\mathrm{SDA}_{FBS}
for any
k\geq 3
? Here, the subscript “susceptible” refers to the condition that underlying
k
-sda’s are all depth-susceptible.

9.

An additional memory device of “counter” is appended to pushdown automata in
[
23
,
24
]
to enhance the computational power of the pushdown automata. Does the use of multiple counters help increase the computational power of
k
-sna’s?
