Associations of left ventricular hypertrophy and poor sleep quality with haemodynamic and cardiometabolic risk factors in adults with hypertension: a cross-sectional study from Pakistan
Authors: Muhammad Saad Nadeem Butt, Hamza Shafique, Amjad Iqbal Burq
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine the associations between sleep quality and left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) and their associations with haemodynamic and cardiometabolic risk factors among adults with hypertension in Pakistan.DesignA cross-sectional analytical study conducted from February to July 2025.SettingConducted in three tertiary care hospitals in Sialkot, Pakistan representing both urban and rural populations.ParticipantsA total of 405 participants aged ≥30 years, diagnosed with hypertension, were enrolled. Patients with primary sleep disorders, psychiatric illness, pregnancy or incomplete data were excluded.Outcome measuresSleep quality was assessed using the Urdu version of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) with a cut-off ≥5. Blood pressure was measured as the average of three seated readings. LVH was determined by echocardiography. Modified Poisson regression with robust SEs was applied to estimate adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) for factors associated with LVH and poor sleep, accounting for clustering by hospital.ResultsLVH was present in 38.3% of participants, and 68.4% had poor sleep quality. Introduction

According to the established medical protocols, a person is labelled hypertensive when an individual’s clinical blood pressure readings consistently reach or exceed a systolic pressure of 140 mm Hg and/or a diastolic pressure of 90 mm Hg.1 In 2010, global data indicate that approximately 1.39 billion adults, accounting for 31.1% of the adult population, were estimated to have hypertension.2 Alarmingly, more than half of the people in these studies were unaware of high hypertension, especially in low-income and middle-income countries where resources and awareness are limited.3 4 Almost 75% of hypertension-related deaths affect low-income and middle-income countries, where the prevalence is high and awareness and control are limited.4 Hypertension is a significant cause of cardiac pathologies and renal failure.5 National data from Pakistan reveal a considerable age-adjusted incidence of hypertension, with similar proportions of pre-existing and newly diagnosed cases.6 Within 6 months, mortality occurred in 14.6% of patients with hypertensive emergency and 4.7% of patients with hypertensive urgency.7

Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) is initially a normal compensatory response but then becomes maladaptive and can lead to heart failure in many pathological ways.8 This pathological change often results from long-term hypertension, which causes remodelling of the heart and is usually linked with concentric hypertrophy of the heart due to chronic pressure overload.9 10 A lack of sleep has become a major health problem because of its association with increased heart risk, which includes artery blockage and endothelial dysfunction.11

Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) is a widespread sleep issue caused by repeated airway obstruction at night, leading to poor sleep quality and a high rate of mortality due to cardiovascular accidents.12 Individuals who slept for less than 6 hours had a small increase in the risk of high blood pressure (1.1%), although this change was not statistically significant.13 14 Individuals suffering from insomnia or short sleep durations have a greater risk of hypertension.15 Poor sleep quality and disorders such as OSA can cause cardiac remodelling, including LVH, due to chronic blood pressure increases and neurohormonal dysregulation.16 17 However, the effects of sleep quality and sleep duration on hypertension are not clear because of different results from different types and methods of studies, such as the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), echocardiography and sleep monitoring.1820 Few studies have explored the associations among sleep quality, hypertension and LVH in tertiary care hospitals, especially in lower middle income countries (LMICs).

Despite this growing evidence, the relationship between poor sleep quality and LVH remains inconsistent, with some studies showing significant associations while others lose significance after adjusting for blood pressure, obesity and duration of hypertension. Most available research comes from high-income countries, and there is a notable scarcity of data from South Asian and other low-income and middle-income settings, where hypertension is common and often poorly controlled, and structural cardiac complications are understudied.

Pakistan faces one of the highest burdens of hypertension in the region, yet little is known about sleep disturbances and their potential association with LVH in adults with hypertension. No published study from Pakistan has simultaneously assessed LVH and sleep quality using standardised echocardiographic measurements and a validated local sleep assessment tool. This highlights an important evidence gap for early detection and risk stratification.

Our study aimed to assess the associations between sleep quality, high blood pressure and LVH. We aimed to identify key risk factors that could help us diagnose hypertension-related cardiac changes early, followed by early intervention strategies.

Objective

Our objectives were to assess the associations between sleep quality, hypertension severity and LVH in a population with high blood pressure via a cross-sectional study. Additionally, this study investigated various cardiometabolic and haemodynamic risk factors that play key roles. Specifically, we aimed to (1) Determine the prevalence of LVH and poor sleep quality, (2) Examine their associations with demographic, haemodynamic and cardiometabolic variables, and (3) Evaluate whether poor sleep quality is independently associated with LVH after adjusting for potential confounders. By achieving these objectives and generating context-specific evidence in a tertiary care setting, we can bridge the knowledge gap and inform targeted interventions tailored to our local population, ultimately optimising hypertension management and prevention strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and settings

We conducted a cross-sectional study during February 2025 to July 2025 at three tertiary care hospitals located in Sialkot, Pakistan, serving both rural and urban populations, ensuring a diverse sample. Because participants were recruited from three separate hospitals, clustering by site was accounted for during statistical analysis using robust SEs.

Sampling strategy

We enrolled all patients with hypertension visiting outpatient departments of hospitals until the required sample size was reached (consecutive sampling). The enrolled adults with hypertension were subsequently screened via the inclusion and exclusion criteria and asked for written informed consent. Only 405 met the criteria, provided informed consent and were involved in the study. This consecutive sampling approach ensured feasibility but may limit generalisability, which is acknowledged as a study limitation.

Sample size and sampling techniques

We calculated the required sample size by using Cochran’s formula,21 which is as follows:

n=Z²p(1 p)/e²,

where we used 95% confidence level (Z=1.96), an assumed prevalence (p) of 50% to ensure maximum variability and a margin of error (e) of 5%.

After calculation, the required sample size to be collected was 384. To account for a potential non-response rate of 5%, the final adjusted sample size was set at 405 participants.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS V.27. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 unless otherwise specified. Because the study included participants from three tertiary hospitals, clustering by hospital was accounted for in all multivariable models using robust (sandwich) variance estimators.

Participant characteristics were summarised using means and SD for continuous variables (eg, PSQI Score) and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables (eg, BMI, comorbidity status). As data were collected directly by trained interviewers and clinicians, no missing values were present in the data set.

Model-building strategy

A structured, multistep modelling strategy was used:

A priori inclusion:

Age and sex were included in all multivariable models regardless of significance based on established clinical relevance.

Bivariate screening

Variables with p<0.20 in bivariate analyses were considered for multivariable entry.

Clinical relevance

Variables known to influence LVH or sleep quality (BMI, smoking, hypertension duration, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, comorbidities) were included irrespective of preliminary significance.

Backward elimination

Sequential removal of non-significant variables was performed while ensuring that removal did not produce >10% change in aPRs of remaining associations (to avoid residual confounding).

Assessment of collinearity

Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with VIF<5 considered acceptable.

Continuous blood pressure variables (systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure) were retained in final models to minimise information loss, while BMI and duration of hypertension remained categorical due to established clinical cut points and interpretability.

Results

Descriptive statistics

We used the data of 405 participants for analysis. The sample was composed of 47.2% men and 52.8% women. Comorbid diabetes was present in 35.8% of the participants, 17.8% of the participants had kidney disease, and 14.3% were diagnosed with both kidney disease and diabetes. In terms of education, 29.1% of the participants had received no formal education, whereas 39.5% had graduated. A total of 7.7% of the participants admitted that they were current smokers, whereas 26.7% were former smokers. A total of 77.3% of the participants (daily and occasionally) were on antihypertensive medication. The majority of participants (73.3%) reported good sleep (very good + fairly good) over the past month. A total of 38.5% of the participants admitted that they had sleep disturbances, whereas 28.1% of the participants reported that they had no sleep disturbances over the past month. Approximately 74.8% of the participants took more than 16 min to fall asleep. The actual sleep duration for 34.1% of the participants was 4−6 hours, whereas for the same number of participants (34.1%), it was less than 4 hours. Many participants (39.5%) slept after 23:00 hours, and approximately 44% woke up before 5:00 hours. Approximately 37.5% of the participants (sometimes + often) reported low daytime enthusiasm. Approximately 55.8% of the participants had a systolic blood pressure between 141 mm Hg and 159 mm Hg, and 52.3% had a diastolic blood pressure between 91 mm Hg and 99 mm Hg. LVH was present in 38.3% of the participants. A total of 68.4% of the participants reported poor sleep. The mean PSQI Score was 7.14 (± 3.659) above the cut-off of 5, with a range from 0 (denoting excellent sleep) to 16 (denoting poor sleep).

Bivariate associations

Determinants of LVH

In unadjusted analyses, several clinical and behavioural characteristics demonstrated strong associations with the presence of LVH. BMI category was significantly associated with LVH (χ²=25.72, p<0.001). Smoking status also exhibited a strong association (χ²=44.02, p<0.001), with the highest LVH prevalence among the heaviest smoking categories. Comorbidity burden showed the most pronounced relationship (χ²=83.72, p<0.001), indicating increasing LVH prevalence with greater multimorbidity.

Indices of blood pressure were consistently associated with LVH. Individuals with elevated systolic blood pressure had substantially higher LVH prevalence (χ²=123.56, p<0.001), paralleled by a similarly strong association with elevated diastolic pressure (χ²=102.37, p<0.001). Duration of hypertension also demonstrated a strong monotonic association (χ²=58.86, p<0.001). Although gender showed a statistically significant association (χ²=7.20, p=0.007), this relationship did not persist following Bonferroni correction, suggesting the effect may be modest or confounded by other variables (see online supplemental table 2)

Determinants of poor sleep quality

Poor sleep quality (PSQI≥5) demonstrated several significant associations with patient characteristics. BMI category (χ²=23.47, p<0.001), smoking status (χ²=20.37, p<0.001) and comorbidity burden (χ²=17.19, p<0.001) were all associated with poorer sleep, suggesting that metabolic and chronic disease burden may contribute to sleep disturbances. Hypertension duration was strongly associated with poor sleep (χ²=86.88, p<0.001) and elevated systolic blood pressure was similarly related (χ²=32.73, p<0.001). In contrast, diastolic blood pressure showed no meaningful association (p=0.738).

Gender was weakly associated with sleep quality (p=0.039); however, this did not withstand correction for multiple comparisons, indicating that the effect is likely minimal (see online supplemental table 3).

Correlation analysis

Spearman’s correlation coefficients revealed several clinically relevant relationships. Age was positively correlated with systolic blood pressure (ρ=0.118), diastolic blood pressure (ρ=0.173) and hypertension duration (ρ=0.319), consistent with expected age-related vascular changes. The PSQI Score exhibited its strongest correlation with hypertension duration (ρ=0.327), suggesting that chronic hypertension may progressively impair sleep quality. Systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure were moderately correlated (ρ=0.297), reflecting physiological coherence between the pressure indices. Notably, the PSQI Score demonstrated minimal correlation with diastolic blood pressure (ρ=0.024), indicating that diastolic pressure is unlikely to influence sleep quality meaningfully (see online supplemental table 4).

Multivariable analyses

Primary modified Poisson model for LVH

In the fully adjusted model incorporating demographic, behavioural and clinical variables, poor sleep quality was not significantly associated with LVH (aPR=1.11; 95% CI 0.81 to 1.54; p=0.512). Several covariates were independently associated with LVH prevalence. Age remained a robust association (aPR=1.32; p<0.001), consistent with cumulative cardiovascular remodelling. Both systolic blood pressure (aPR=1.021 per mm Hg; p<0.001) and diastolic blood pressure (aPR=1.030 per mm Hg; p<0.001) demonstrated independent and additive contributions to LVH risk, highlighting the continuous nature of pressure-related cardiac stress. Longer hypertension duration was similarly associated with increased LVH prevalence (aPR=1.47; p=0.002), reinforcing the effect of chronic exposure.

With respect to BMI, the overweight category showed a protective association relative to normal weight (aPR=0.77; p<0.001) and obesity was also associated with lower LVH prevalence (aPR=0.71; p=0.042). This inverse association, often referred to as the obesity paradox, has been documented in several cardiovascular risk studies and may reflect confounding by muscle mass, treatment patterns or reverse causality.

Comorbidity category 3 increased the prevalence of LVH (aPR=1.10; p=0.009), whereas the highest comorbidity category (category 4) showed a marked protective association (aPR=0.23; p<0.001). This counterintuitive pattern may reflect small category sizes or heterogeneity of conditions within comorbidity strata (see online supplemental table 5)

Sensitivity analyses

Alternative operationalisations of sleep quality were evaluated. When poor sleep was defined as PSQI≥8, the association with LVH reached statistical significance (aPR=1.24; p=0.043). A stricter cut-off (PSQI≥11) yielded a similar association (aPR=1.18; p=0.012). Modelling PSQI as a continuous score revealed a small but statistically significant dose-response relationship, with each 1-point increase associated with a 3.3% higher prevalence of LVH (aPR=1.033; p<0.001). These findings suggest that the relationship between sleep quality and LVH may be detectable only at more severe levels of sleep impairment (see online supplemental table 6).

Backward elimination model

The reduced model identified four major determinants of LVH: age, systolic pressure, diastolic pressure and hypertension duration—all of which remained significant with consistent effect sizes. All smoking categories were inversely associated with LVH at highly significant levels, a finding that may reflect residual confounding, reverse causation or measurement characteristics rather than protective effects of smoking. Such patterns have been previously observed in epidemiological data sets and warrant cautious interpretation (see online supplemental table 7).

Stratified analyses

Participants younger than 60 years

Among individuals <60 years, poor sleep quality was strongly and independently associated with LVH (aPR=3.78; 95% CI 1.34 to 10.63; p=0.012). This finding suggests that in younger adults, sleep disturbance may play a more influential role in cardiac remodelling or that younger individuals with poor sleep may accumulate risk more rapidly. Neither systolic nor diastolic pressure reached statistical significance in this subgroup (see online supplemental table 8).

Participants aged 60 years or older

In contrast, among participants ≥60 years, poor sleep quality demonstrated no association with LVH (p=0.893). In this group, systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure remained strongly associated with LVH, and overweight BMI emerged as a protective factor (aPR=0.61; p=0.006). These observations suggest that in older individuals, haemodynamic factors may overshadow any potential influence of sleep quality on left ventricular structure (see online supplemental table 9).

GEE model accounting for cluster effects

Results from the GEE model (Generalized Estimating Equations) were consistent with the main model. Poor sleep quality was not significantly associated with LVH after accounting for within-cluster correlation (aPR=1.08; p=0.629). Age, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure and hypertension duration all remained significant determinants, providing further evidence that the null association between sleep quality and LVH is robust across model types (see online supplemental table 10).

Influence diagnostics

Evaluation of Cook’s distance values indicated the absence of influential observations. All values (Cook’s D ≤0.02) were well below accepted thresholds, confirming that no single data point disproportionately influenced the model estimates (see online supplemental table 11).

Discussion

Summary of findings

In this cohort of 405 adults with hypertension, as expected, we found that a longer duration of hypertension, overweight, the presence of comorbidities, and increased systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure were associated with LVH. These findings align with the clinical imperative of managing modifiable predictors in the population with high blood pressure.2729

Notably, our study not only validates previous knowledge and well-established associations but also provides important insights by exploring the interplay between multiple clinical variables.

Poor sleep quality (χ2 test) was crudely associated with LVH in the bivariate analysis but lost its significance in the multivariable analysis. These findings suggest that it may exert its influence via indirect pathways such as blood pressure, BMI and duration of hypertension. This differs from findings in studies from a specific clinical subgroup (chronic kidney disease population), where the PSQI Score was independently associated with LVH even after adjusting for systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure.30

The inconsistency across studies highlights the importance of population context, measurement tools and potential confounding, particularly the lack of nocturnal blood pressure monitoring in our sample.

In our study, the inverse association between higher BMI categories and LVH was unexpected and aligns with the term obesity paradox, where individuals with diagnosed cardiovascular disease who have overweight or moderate obesity show a lower structural disease burden compared with their normal-weight counterparts.3133 Several mechanisms may explain this observation. First, confounding by lean mass is possible because LVH was defined using LVMI, which is indexed to body surface area; this may overadjust for larger body size and not adequately distinguish lean mass from adiposity, particularly in individuals with overweight who may have higher skeletal muscle mass.27 28 Second, treatment-related confounding may contribute: patients with hypertension with overweight and obesity often receive earlier antihypertensive therapy which may be associated with attenuation or regression of LVH.29 Third, reverse causality must be considered given the cross-sectional design; individuals with long-standing, severe or undiagnosed LVH may develop unintentional weight loss or cardiac cachexia, creating an artificial impression that higher BMI is ‘protective’.31 32 Together, these factors highlight that BMI alone is an imperfect substitute for adiposity in South Asian populations and this counterintuitive association. Although it has been documented in several previous cardiovascular cohorts, it should be interpreted cautiously, underscoring the need for longitudinal studies incorporating direct measures of body composition and disease progression.3133

Our revised multivariable modelling (using modified Poisson regression with robust SEs and accounting for hospital clustering) strengthens the internal validity of these findings. The convergence of direction and magnitude across χ2 tests, correlation analyses and multivariable models supports the robustness of the associations observed. Where discrepancies emerged (eg, sleep quality losing significance), these were epidemiologically interpretable and likely reflected confounding by haemodynamic variables.

Given the cross-sectional design, however, causal inferences cannot be made, and all associations should be interpreted with caution.

Despite these limitations, the analytical choices including clustering correction, model diagnostics and sensitivity analyses enhance the study’s reproducibility and transparency, addressing key reviewer concerns.

Comparisons with global and LMIC studies

The prevalence of poor sleep between 40% and 60% aligns with various other meta-analyses and regional studies.3436 Meta-analyses and multiethnic cohort studies have shown that short sleep durations are significantly associated with the risk of hypertension.37 38 Our findings reinforce the results of other studies. A South Asian study reported that a sleep duration of less than 6 hours is significantly associated with the risk of hypertension in adults aged above 60 years, which coincides with our findings.39

Similarly, another East Asian study revealed that sleep timing regularity is linked to an approximately 32% increase in hypertension risk.40 Our findings of prolonged sleep latency and delayed bedtimes may suggest similar mechanisms contributing to elevated blood pressure.

Sleep, LVH and structural remodelling

A study conducted in an OSA-rich population revealed a high risk of LVH (45%), which coincides with our present study (43%).41 Similarly, a cross-sectional study from East Asia reported an OR of 1.32 for LVH among people with poor sleep quality,42 with sleep disturbances leading to concentric LVH through a mechanism of sympathetic overactivity.4345 Although we did not measure nocturnal blood pressure or OSA (obstructive sleep apnea) in our study, our findings that age is a risk factor for LVH align with other global studies.46 47

Mechanistic insights

Multiple neurophysiological pathways link poor sleep quality to hypertension and cardiac remodelling:

Sleep loss and fragmentation can lead to increased sympathetic nervous system activity and inflammatory responses, which may contribute to the disruption of normal nocturnal blood pressure.4850

A randomised trial with 30 sleep-restricted adults reported a 3–5 mmHg increase in nocturnal blood pressure after 1 week of restricted sleep.51

HPA Hypothalamic–Pituitary–Adrenal) axis activation elevates cortisol, aggravating vascular stiffness, as demonstrated in crossover sleep deprivation trials.44

Inflammation and oxidative stress, including elevated c-reactive protein and interkin 6 in sleep-restricted individuals, further impair endothelial function and promote left ventricular remodelling.49 50

RAAS overactivation during sleep deprivation contributes to volume retention and hypertrophic stimuli.52

OSA‐like intermittent hypoxia and arousals, known to amplify nocturnal blood pressure and sympathetic tone, cannot be measured but may underpin structural cardiac changes in poor sleepers.53

These interconnected mechanisms describe the pathways from poor sleep quality to hypertension and LVH, even though our cross-sectional design limits causal inference.

While these mechanisms are biologically plausible, our findings suggest that sleep quality alone was not independently associated with LVH in this population, likely because blood pressure, BMI and hypertension duration exert stronger direct effects.

Clinical implications and public health relevance

Our study underscores the association of poor sleep quality with hypertension and the indirect link with LVH mediated through age and blood pressure. While sleep quality was not independently associated with LVH after adjustment, it remains clinically relevant due to its influence on blood pressure regulation and overall cardiovascular risk.

Conclusions

This study provides evidence that increased age (>60 years), obesity, increased hypertension duration, and increased systolic and diastolic blood pressures are strongly associated with LVH. These findings reaffirm the haemodynamic and metabolic underpinnings of LVH and underscore the critical importance of early risk stratification and aggressive management of modifiable factors. Sleep quality was not a significant association in the multivariable analysis, suggesting that its association with LVH is indirectly mediated.

The improved analytical approach (modified Poisson regression, clustering adjustment and diagnostics) enhances confidence in these conclusions. These insights contribute meaningfully to cardiovascular risk profiling and support targeted intervention strategies in populations with high blood pressure.
