An illustration of formal moduli problems with differential graded Lie algebras
ethan eugene wynner

Abstract




Abstract: This article provides an exposition to the topic of formal moduli problems, emphasizing its connections with differential graded Lie algebras, and mainly following from Jacob Lurie’s
DAG X: Formal Moduli Problems
. As such, this paper should not be viewed as a presentation of original work, but rather a concise introduction to the subject in the form of a set of organized notes. I hoped to make this paper feel welcoming and insightful for the non-expert enjoyer of derived algebraic geometry, like myself. Enjoy!

1
Deformation theory and formal moduli problems

First we note a down to earth characterization of the study of deformations, and leap into the more ethereal realm of deformation theory connected to the study of formal moduli problems.

1.1
First order deformations and the ring of dual numbers

Let
k
be a field. The most straightfoward-to-the-point object of study in deformation theory is the ring of dual numbers
k[t]/(t^{2}).
The idea is to suppose that we are given some algebro-geometric structure (i.e., a scheme). Our deformation-theoretic impulse then is to try to classify extensions of this structure over
k[t]/(t^{2}).
These extensions are called
first order deformations.
A technical consideration is that we must assume that the extensions are
flat
over
k[t]/(t^{2}).
We will define this now.

1.1.1
Definition [5]

Let
A
be a ring. We call an
A
-module
M

flat
if the functor

N\longmapsto N\otimes_{A}M

is exact on the category of
A
-modules. We say that a morphism
f:X\longrightarrow Y
of schemes is flat if for every point
x\in X,
the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{X,x}
is flat over the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{Y,f(x)}.
Finally, a sheaf of
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-modules
\mathscr{F}
is flat over
Y
if for every
x\in X,
the stalk
\mathscr{F}_{x}
is flat over
\mathcal{O}_{Y,f(x)}.

1.1.2
Example-definition of a deformation (problem)

Let
X
be a scheme over
k,
and let
Y
be a closed subscheme of
X.
A
deformation of
Y
over
k[t]/(t^{2})
in
X
is a closed subscheme
Y\subset X\times k[t]/(t^{2}).
The ”problem” here is to classify all deformations of
Y
over
k[t]/(t^{2}).

1.2
The Rocketship

We are now interested in approaching deformation theory from the standpoint of higher category theory. In so doing, we still wish to study classes of algebro-geometric objects, but, since we are so infatuated with Grothendieck, we identify these objects with functors

X:\Gamma\longrightarrow\mathscr{S},

where
\Gamma
is some presentable
\infty
-category of these aforementioned objects, and
\mathscr{S}
is the
\infty
-category of spaces (see appendix B). We ask little of the category
\Gamma;
only that it has suitable objects and a terminal object
*.
This is so as to enable us to view a
point
of a functor
X
to be a point in the space
X(*).
We also denote by
\Gamma_{*}
the
\infty
-category of pointed objects in
\Gamma,
that is, pairs
(Y,\eta)
where
Y
is an object of
\Gamma
and
\eta:*\longrightarrow Y
is the unique map. We also take into high consideration the forgetful functors
\Omega^{\infty-n}_{*}:\text{Stab}(\Gamma)\longrightarrow\Gamma_{*}
defined on all
n\in\mathbb{Z}.
Moreover, we have
\Omega^{\infty-n}:\text{Stab}(\Gamma)\longrightarrow\Gamma
given by composition with the forgetful functor
\Gamma_{*}\longrightarrow\Gamma.
This brings us to a trampoline which we will use to attain the idea of a formal moduli problem:

1.2.1
Definition

A
deformation context
is a pair
(\Gamma,\{E_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in T})
where
\Gamma
follows our previous rules, and
\{E_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in T}
is a set of objects in
\text{Stab}(\Gamma).

Let
(\Gamma,\{E_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in T})
be a deformation context. A morphism
A^{\prime}\longrightarrow A
in
\Gamma
is called
elementary
if there exists
\alpha\in T,n>0,
and a pullback diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{*}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Omega^{\infty-n}E_{\alpha}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi_{0}}

1.2.2
Observation

Let
A\in\Gamma.
Every elementary map
A^{\prime}\longrightarrow A
in
\Gamma
is given by the fiber of a map
A\longrightarrow\Omega^{\infty-n}E_{\alpha}
for some
n>0,\alpha\in T.

1.2.3
Definition

We say that a morphism
\phi:A^{\prime}\longrightarrow A
is
small
if it can be written as a finite composition of elementary morphisms
A^{\prime}\simeq A_{0}\longrightarrow A_{1}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow A_{n}\simeq A.
We say that
A\in\Gamma
is small (as an object of
\Gamma
) if the map
A\longrightarrow*
is small. We denote by
\Gamma^{sm}
the full subcategory of
\Gamma
spanned by small objects.

We now give a definition of the titular object of this paper:

1.2.4
A general interpretation of a formal moduli problem

Let
(\Gamma,\{E_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in T})
be a deformation context. A
formal moduli problem
is a functor

X:\Gamma^{sm}\longrightarrow\mathscr{S}

satisfying

1.

The space
X(*)
is contractible

2.

If

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{B^{\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}

is a pullback diagram and
\phi:B^{\prime}\longrightarrow B
is small, then the image

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{X(A^{\prime})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{X(B^{\prime})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{X(A)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{X(B)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X(\phi)}

is a pullback diagram in
\mathscr{S}.

Let
\text{Moduli}^{\Gamma}
denote the full subcategory of
\text{Fun}(\Gamma^{sm},\mathscr{S})
spanned by formal moduli problems. We call
\text{Moduli}^{\Gamma}
the
\infty
-category of formal moduli problems.

1.2.5
Example

Let
(\Gamma,\{E_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in T})
be a deformation context,
A\in\Gamma
an object. Let
\operatorname{Spec}A:\Gamma\longrightarrow\mathscr{S}
be the functor corepresented by
A,
given on
\Gamma^{sm}
by
\operatorname{Spec}A(B)=\text{Map}_{\Gamma}(A,B).
Then
\operatorname{Spec}A
is a formal moduli problem, and, moreover, the assignment
A\longmapsto\operatorname{Spec}A
determines a functor
\operatorname{Spec}:\Gamma^{op}\longrightarrow\text{Moduli}^{\Gamma}.

1.2.6
Admissory note:

For much of what we wish to do in this paper, it will be necessary to appeal to notions of spectral algebraic geometry, such as
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
-rings/algebras; an example of this being the
\infty
-category
\text{CAlg}^{aug}_{k}
of augmented
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
-algebras over an
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
-ring
k,
as well as the
\infty
-category
\text{Mod}_{k}
of

A full and proper (in the non-mathematical sense) exposition of this material is literally unfeasible given the focus and length of this paper, although I have tried to make some reasonable accommodations when necessary. Fortunately, there is an abundance of resources, as arrayed in the references section.

1.3
The tangent complex

We will now delve into a very important framework upon which we can rest the laurels of formal moduli problems. In order to do so, we will generalize the construction of the
Zariski tangent space,
which we will first review.

1.3.1
The Zariski tangent space

Let
X
be an algebraic variety over
\mathbb{C},
and let
x:\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow X
be a point of
X.
A tangent vector to
X
at
x
is a dashed arrow making the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{C}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{C}[t]/(t^{2})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{C}}}

commute. The collection of tangent vectors at
x
, denoted
T_{x}X,
is called the
Zariski tangent space.
Moreover, if
\mathcal{O}_{X,x}
is the local ring of
X
at
x,
with
\mathfrak{m}
its maximal ideal, there is a canonical isomorphism
T_{x}X\simeq(\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^{2})^{\vee},
where
(\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^{2})^{\vee}
is called the
cotangent space
to
X.
Our first step in generalizing this construction into the setting of formal moduli problems is to associate the variety
X
with its ’functor of points’
X(A)=\text{Hom}_{\text{Sch}(\mathbb{C})}(\operatorname{Spec}A,X),
where
\text{Sch}(\mathbb{C})
is the category of schemes over
\mathbb{C}.
Then
T_{x}X
can be characterized as the fiber of the map

X(\mathbb{C}[t]/(t^{2}))\longrightarrow X(\mathbb{C})

over the point
x\in X(\mathbb{C}).
Note also that in a mercurial way the (commutative) ring of dual numbers
\mathbb{C}[t]/(t^{2})
is given by
\Omega^{\infty}E,
where
E
is the spectrum object in
\text{CAlg}^{aug}_{\mathbb{C}}
corresponding to
\mathbb{C}
in the sense that the suspension functor defined by the pushout
\Sigma\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{C}\coprod_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}\simeq\mathbb{C}[t]/(t^{2})
, where we take
E=\Sigma^{\infty}(\mathbb{C})\in\text{Stab}(\text{CAlg}^{aug}_{\mathbb{C}})
so that
\Omega^{\infty-0}(E)=\Sigma\mathbb{C}\simeq\mathbb{C}[t]/(t^{2}).

This determines a deformation context
(\text{CAlg}^{aug}_{\mathbb{C}},\{E\}),
and leads us to suspect a generalization.

1.3.2
Generalization

Let
(\Gamma,\{E_{\alpha}\})
be a deformation context. Let
Y:\Gamma^{sm}\longrightarrow\mathscr{S}
be a formal moduli problem. For every
\alpha,
the (generalized)
tangent space
of
Y
at
\alpha
is the space
Y(\Omega^{\infty}E_{\alpha}).

1.3.3
Definition

Let
\mathscr{C}
be an
\infty
-category with finite colimits, and
\mathscr{D}
an
\infty
-category with finite limits. We say that a functor
F:\mathscr{C}\longrightarrow\mathscr{D}
is
excisive
if for every pushout diagram
P
in
\mathscr{C},
the image
F(P)
is a pushout diagram in
\mathscr{D}.
We say that
F
is
strongly excisive
if it is excisive and maps initial objects to terminal objects.

Let
\mathscr{S}^{fin}_{*}
be the
\infty
-category of finite pointed sets. If
\mathscr{D}
is an
\infty
-category with finite limits, then
\text{Stab}(\mathscr{D})
is the full subcategory of
\text{Fun}(\mathscr{S}^{fin}_{*},\mathscr{D})
spanned by pointed excisive functors. In particular, we can identify the
\infty
-category
Spc
=
\text{Stab}(\mathscr{S})
of spectra in this way: as the full subcategory of
\text{Fun}(\mathscr{S}^{fin}_{*},\mathscr{S})
spanned by strongly excisive functors.

1.3.4
Construction

Let
(\Gamma,\{E_{\alpha}\})
be a deformation context. For all
\alpha,
we can identify
E_{\alpha}\in\text{Stab}(\Gamma)
with the functor
E_{\alpha}:\mathscr{S}^{fin}_{*},\Gamma.
Then

1.

For any map
f:K^{\prime}\longrightarrow K^{\prime}
of finite pointed spaces inducing a surjection on homotopy groups
\pi_{0}K\longrightarrow\pi_{0}K^{\prime},
the induced map
E_{\alpha}(K)\longrightarrow E_{\alpha}(K^{\prime})
is small in
\Gamma.

2.

For all
K\in\mathscr{S}^{fin}_{*},E_{\alpha}(K)\in\Gamma
is small.

1.3.5
Definition

Let
\Gamma^{sm}\longrightarrow\mathscr{S}
be a formal moduli problem. For every
\alpha,
we view the composition

Y(E_{\alpha})=\mathscr{S}^{fin}_{*}\xrightarrow{E_{\alpha}}\Gamma^{sm}\xrightarrow{Y}\mathscr{S}

as an object of
\textbf{Spc},
and we call
Y(E_{\alpha})
the
tangent complex
to
Y
at
\alpha.

1.3.6
Remark

We can further identify the tangent space
Y(\Omega^{\infty}E_{\alpha})
with the 0th rung of the tangent complex
Y(E_{\alpha}).
Moreover, there are pleasing canonical homotopy equivalences

Y(\Omega^{\infty-n}E_{\alpha})\simeq\Omega^{\infty-n}Y(E_{\alpha}).

1.4
Deformation Theories

We will introduce the notion here of (weak) deformation theories, as well as state a promising theorem. Our setup is as follows. Let
(\Gamma,\{E_{\alpha}\})
be a deformation context. Suppose that
\Xi
is an
\infty
-category. We want to figure out when there is an equivalence
\text{Moduli}^{\Gamma}\simeq\Xi.
To each
A\in\Gamma,
we associate the formal moduli problem
\operatorname{Spec}A\in\text{Moduli}^{\Gamma},
given by
\operatorname{Spec}A(R)=\text{Map}_{\Gamma}(A,R).
Moreover, if
\text{Moduli}^{\Gamma}\simeq\Xi,
we obtain a functor

\mathfrak{D}:\Gamma^{op}\longrightarrow\Xi.

1.4.1
Definition

Let
(\Gamma,\{E_{\alpha}\})
be a deformation context. A
weak deformation theory
is a functor
\mathfrak{D}:\Gamma^{op}\longrightarrow\Xi
satisfying the following conditions:

1.

\Xi
is presentable

2.

\mathfrak{D}
admits a left adjoint
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}:\Xi\longrightarrow\Gamma^{op}

3.

\Xi
has a full subcategory
\Xi_{0}
such that

(a)

For each
K\in\Xi_{0},
the unit map
K\longmapsto\mathfrak{D}(\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(K))
is an equivalence.

(b)

The initial object
\varnothing\in\Xi_{0}.
Hence
\varnothing\simeq\mathfrak{D}(\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\varnothing))\simeq\mathfrak{D}(*).

(c)

For all
\alpha,n\geq 1,
there exists an object
K_{\alpha,n}\in\Xi
and an equivalence
\Omega^{\infty-n}E_{\alpha}\simeq\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}K_{\alpha,n},
determining a map

v_{\alpha,n}:K_{\alpha,n}\simeq\mathfrak{D}(\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(K_{\alpha,n}))\simeq D(\Omega^{\infty-n}E_{\alpha})\longrightarrow D(*)\simeq\varnothing.

(d)

For every pushout

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{K_{\alpha,n}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varnothing}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{K^{\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces v_{\alpha,n}}

if
K\in\Xi_{0},
then
K^{\prime}\in\Xi_{0}.

1.4.2
Example

Let
k
be a field of characteristic zero and
(\text{CAlg}^{aug}_{k},\{E\})
be the deformation context from earlier. We will later construct a weak deformation theory

\mathfrak{D}:(\text{CAlg}^{aug}_{k})^{op}\longrightarrow\text{Lie}_{k}

(where
\text{Lie}_{k}
is the
\infty
-category of differential graded Lie algebras), where the adjoint is the cohomological Chevalley Eilenberg functor. In fact, this assignment
\mathfrak{g}_{*}\longrightarrow C^{*}(\mathfrak{g}_{*})
is actually a deformation theory, as it satisfies an extra condition which we will get to in a moment.

1.4.3
Moreover-proposition

Let
\mathfrak{D}:\Gamma^{op}\longrightarrow\Xi
be a weak deformation theory. Condition 3 above implies the following

1.

\mathfrak{D}
carries terminal objects in
\Gamma
to initial objects in
\Xi.

2.

Let
A=\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(K)\in\Gamma,K\in\Xi.
The unit map
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{D}(A))
is an eqiuivalence in
\Gamma.

3.

If
A\in\Gamma
is small, then
\mathfrak{D}(A)\in\Xi_{0},
and
A\longrightarrow\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{D}(A))
is an equivalence in
\Gamma.

4.

If
\sigma=

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A^{\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{B^{\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}

is a pullback diagram where
A,B,\phi
are small, then
\mathfrak{D}(\sigma)
is a pushout in
\Xi.

1.4.4
A pair of corollaries

1.

Let
y:\Xi\longrightarrow\text{Fun}(\Xi,\mathscr{S})
be the Yoneda embedding. For every
K\in\Xi,
the composition

\Gamma^{sm}\subset\Gamma\xrightarrow{\mathfrak{D}}\Xi^{op}\xrightarrow{y(K)}\mathscr{S}

is a formal moduli problem, which determines a functor
\Psi:\Xi\longrightarrow\text{Moduli}^{\Gamma}\subset\text{Fun}(\Gamma^{op},\mathscr{S}).

2.

Let
\mathfrak{D}:\Gamma^{op}\longrightarrow\Xi
be a weak deformation theory. For every
\alpha,K\in\Xi,
the composition

\mathscr{S}^{fin}_{*}\xrightarrow{E_{\alpha}}\Gamma\xrightarrow{\mathfrak{D}}\Xi^{op}\xrightarrow{y(K)}\mathscr{S}

is strongly excisive, and can be indeitifed with a spectrum object
e_{\alpha}(K)\in\textbf{Spc}.
This determines a functor

e_{\alpha}:\Xi\longrightarrow\textbf{Spc}.

1.4.5
Definition

Finally we get here: A
deformation theory
is a weak deformation theory satisfying one extra condition:

For every
\alpha,
if
e_{\alpha}:\Xi\longrightarrow\textbf{Spc}
is the functor above, then
e_{\alpha}
preserves small sifted colimits, and a morphism
f:A\longrightarrow B\in\Xi
is an equivalence iff
e_{\alpha}(f)
is an equivalence; i.e.
e_{\alpha}(f):e_{\alpha}(A)\longrightarrow e_{\alpha}(B)
is a weak equivalence in textbfSpc. This construction allows us to view
Spc
=
\text{Stab}(\mathscr{S})\subset\text{Fun}_{\text{exc}}(\mathscr{S}^{fin}_{*},\textbf{Spc})
of excisive functors.

1.4.6
Theorem (Lurie)

Let
\mathfrak{D}:\Gamma^{op}\longrightarrow\Xi
be a deformation theory. Then the functor

\Psi:\Xi\longrightarrow\text{Moduli}^{\Gamma}

is an equivalence of
\infty
-categories.

The proof of this theorem can be found in Lurie [7] §1.5

1.4.7
Remark

The composition

\Gamma^{op}\xrightarrow{\mathfrak{D}}\Xi\xrightarrow{\Psi}\text{Moduli}^{\Gamma}

carries an object
A\in\Gamma
to the formal moduli problem given by

B\longmapsto\text{Maps}_{\Xi}(\mathfrak{D}(B),\mathfrak{D}(A))\simeq\text{Map}_{\Gamma}(A,\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{D}(B)).

Hence the unit map
B\longrightarrow\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{D}(B))
determines a natural transformation
\beta:\operatorname{Spec}\longrightarrow\Psi\circ\mathfrak{D}.
It follows from proposition 1.4.3 that
\beta
is an equivalence. Combined with theorem 1.4.6, we observe that
\mathfrak{D}
is equivalent to the weak deformation theory
\operatorname{Spec}:\Gamma^{op}\longrightarrow\text{Moduli}^{\Gamma}.

2
Formal moduli problems for commutative algebras

2.1
Introduction

Our main objective here is to connect the theory of formal moduli problems with that of differential graded Lie algebras. We offer the following proposition of great importance (PGI), which we hope to properly unwind over the course of this paper:

2.1.1
Proposition of great importance

(PGI):
If
X
is a moduli space over a field
k
of characteristic zero, then a formal neighborhood of any point
x\in X
is controlled by a differential graded Lie algebra.

First of all, what do we mean by moduli space? For an example, let
k=\mathbb{C}.
We can take
X
to be a scheme, with a functor
R\longmapsto X(R)=\text{Hom}(\operatorname{Spec}R,X),
where
R
is a commutative ring. We define a
classical moduli problem
to be a functor

X:\text{Ring}_{\mathbb{C}}\longrightarrow\textbf{Set}

where
\text{Ring}_{\mathbb{C}}
is the category of commutative
\mathbb{C}
-algebras. For our purposes here, it is sometimes the case that a functor taking values in
Set
will not be adequate. As such, we define the following variant, which captures far more possibilities:

2.1.2
Definition

Let
\mathscr{C}
be an
\infty
-category. A
\mathscr{C}
-valued classical moduli problem
is a functor

N(\text{Ring}_{\mathbb{C}})\longrightarrow\mathscr{C},

where
N(\text{Ring}_{\mathbb{C}})
denotes the
nerve
of the category
\text{Ring}_{\mathbb{C}}.
We now see that our original definition is a special case of this new one, taking
\mathscr{C}=N(\textbf{Set}).
Now one might ask: what do we mean by a formal neighborhood? A pertinent and ubiquitous example follows: Let
k=\mathbb{C},
and let
X=\operatorname{Spec}A
be an affine variety over
\mathbb{C}.
A closed point
x\in X
is determined by a
\mathbb{C}
-algebra homomorphism
\phi:A\longrightarrow\mathbb{C},
which itself determined by the choice of maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}=\ker(\phi)\subset A.
The
formal completion
of
X
at the point
x
is the functor
X^{\wedge}:\text{Ring}\longrightarrow\textbf{Set}
given by taking
X^{\wedge}(R)
to be the collection of commuting ring homomorphisms
A\longrightarrow R
which carry elements of
\mathfrak{m}
to nilpotents in
R.
That is,

X^{\wedge}(R)=\{f\in X(R)\text{ }|\text{ }f(\operatorname{Spec}R)\subset\{x\}\subset\operatorname{Spec}A\}.

2.1.3
Definition

Let
R\in\text{Ring}_{\mathbb{C}}.
We say that
R
is
local artinian
if it is finite dimensional as a
\mathbb{C}
-vector space, and is a local ring, i.e. has a unique maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{R}.
We denote by
\text{Ring}^{art}_{\mathbb{C}}
the category of local Artinian
\mathbb{C}
-algebras. Importantly, we observe that if
X
is an affine variety over
\mathbb{C},
its formal completion
X^{\wedge}
at
x\in X
can be recovered by its values on local artinian rings. As such, we can further refine our definiton:

2.1.4
Refined definition

Let
\mathscr{C}
be an
\infty
-category. A
\mathscr{C}
-valued classical formal moduli problem is a functor

N(\text{Ring}^{art}_{\mathbb{C}})\longrightarrow\mathscr{C}.

If
X
is
Set
-valued, and we have a point
\eta\in X(\mathbb{C})
, we can define a
Set
-valued classical formal moduli problem
X^{\wedge}
by

X^{\wedge}(R)=X(R)\times_{X(R/\mathfrak{m}_{R})}\{\eta\},

which we call the
completion of
X
at
\eta.

Similarly, if
X
is Gpd-valued (where Gpd denotes the category of groupoids), we can use the same formula using a homotopy fiber product.

2.1.5
A palatable example

Let
R
be a commutative
\mathbb{C}
-algebra. Let
X(R)
be the groupoid whose objects are smooth proper
R
-schemes and whose isomorphisms are those of such
R
-schemes. Take
\eta\in X(\mathbb{C}),
corresponding to a smooth proper algebraic variety
Z.
The functor
X^{\wedge}
assigns to each
R\in\text{Ring}^{art}_{\mathbb{C}}
the groupoid
X^{\wedge}(R)
of
deformations
over
Z
(over
R
). That is, smooth proper morphisms

The functor

has some important properties:

1.

The image under
X^{\wedge}
of the ring of dual numbers,
X^{\wedge}(\mathbb{C}[t]/(t^{2}),
is the groupoid of first order deformations of the variety

Moreover, every first order deformation

has an automorphism group which is naturally isomorphic to
H^{0}(Z;T_{Z}),
where

is the tangent bundle of
Z.

2.

The collection of isomorphism classes of first order deformations of

3.

Every first order deformation
\eta_{1}

The first two of these properties are nice and friendly, and can be exposited without too much extra machinery. However, property 3 is rather unfriendly; in order to properly explain it, one must turn to the workings of spectral algebraic geometry, specifically the theory of commutative ring spectra, or
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
-rings/ring spaces. Unfortunately for Refined definition 2.1.4, our construction is not complete for arbitrary classical formal moduli problems; we cannot assume

2.1.8
A word of warning (not to be interpreted as foreboding)

In general, if

is a formal moduli problem over
\mathbb{C},
one can always restrict
X
to the subcategory of
\text{CAlg}^{sm}_{\mathbb{C}}
consisting of the ordinary local artinian algebras (i.e.,

to obtain a classical formal moduli problem

with values in

However the converse is not necessarily true. If we are given a classical formal moduli problem

with

An example where this is in fact true is the one outlined in Remark/Explication 2.1.7.

3
Differential graded Lie algebras and their (co)homology

3.1

In this section we will introduce some terminology and constructions surrounding the concept of a differential graded (or, sometimes, dg) Lie algebra, its homology and cohomology, and begin the see some of the connections with formal moduli problems.

3.1.1
Definition

Let
k
be a field. A
differential graded Lie algebra

\mathfrak{g}_{*}
over
k
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded vector space

\mathfrak{g}_{*}=\bigoplus\mathfrak{g}_{i}

equipped with a differential map

d:\mathfrak{g}_{i}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{g}_{i-1},\text{ }d^{2}=0

and a Lie bracket

[-,-]:\mathfrak{g}_{p}\otimes_{k}\mathfrak{g}_{q}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{g}_{p+q}

satisfying

[x_{p},x_{q}]+(-1)^{pq}[x_{q},x_{p}]=0

where
x_{p},x_{q}\in\mathfrak{g}_{p},g_{q}
respectively. Moreover, if we let
x_{\ell}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\ell},
the bracket satisfies the graded Jacobi identity, that is

(-1)^{p\ell}[x_{p},[x_{q},x_{\ell}]]+(-1)^{pq}[x_{q},[x_{\ell},x_{p}]]+(1)^{q}\ell[x_{\ell}[x_{p},x_{q}]]=0.

We also maintain that
d
is a derivation with respect to the bracket. We view
(\mathfrak{g}_{*},d)
as a chain complex, which gives us the impulse to make note of some categorical notions.

3.1.2
A forgetful subsubsection

We denote by
\text{Vect}^{dg}_{k}
the category of differential graded vector spaces over a field
k.
The objects in this category are chain complexes

\cdots\longrightarrow V_{1}\longrightarrow V_{0}\longrightarrow V_{-1}\longrightarrow\cdots

Note that
\text{Vect}^{dg}_{k}
is a symmetric monoidal category, with the tensor product structure given by

(V\otimes W)_{n}=\bigoplus_{p+q=n}V_{p}\otimes_{k}W_{q}

and the symmetric isomorphism

V\otimes W\simeq W\otimes V

being

\bigoplus_{p+q=n}V_{p}\otimes_{k}W_{q}\simeq\bigoplus_{p+q=n}W_{q}\otimes_{k}V_{p}

multiplied by the factor
(-1)^{pq}.
Let
V
be a graded vector space over
k,
and let
V^{\vee}
be its graded dual such that

(V^{\vee})_{p}=\text{Hom}_{k}(V_{-p},k).

For all
n\in\mathbb{Z},
let
V[n]
denote the graded shift of
V
by
n;
hence
V[n]_{p}=V_{p-n}.

Let
\text{Alg}^{dg}_{k}
denote the category of differential graded associative
k
-algebras (more precisely, associative algebra objects of
\text{Vect}^{dg}_{k},
and let
\text{CAlg}^{dg}_{k}
denote the category of commutative associative algebra objects in
\text{Vect}^{dg}_{k}.
An object
A
in
\text{Alg}^{dg}_{k}
is a chain complex
(A_{*},d)
with unit in
A_{0}
, and the differential
d
satisfying

d(x_{p},x_{q})=dx_{p}x_{1}+(-1)^{p}xdy

where
x_{p}\in A_{p},x_{q}\in A_{q}.
We say that
A
is commutative if

x_{p}x_{q}=(-1)^{pq}x_{q}x_{p}.

Finally, we define
\text{Lie}^{dg}_{k}
to be the category of differential graded Lie algebras over
k.
The morphisms in this category are morphisms of chain complexes which respect the Lie bracket. That is, a morphism
\varphi:(\mathfrak{g}_{*},d)\longrightarrow(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}_{*},d^{\prime})
satisfies

\varphi([x_{p},x_{q}])=[\varphi(x_{p}),\varphi(x_{q})].

3.1.3
Remark

Let
A=(A_{*},d)
be a differential graded algebra over
k.
Then
A_{*}
has the structure of a dg-Lie algebra, by

[-,-]:A_{p}\otimes_{k}A_{q}\longrightarrow A_{p+q}

given by

[x_{p},x_{q}]=x_{p}x_{q}-(-1)^{pq}x_{q}x_{p}.

This determines a forgetful functor
\text{Alg}^{dg}_{k}\longrightarrow\text{Lie}^{dg}_{k}
with left adjoint

U:\text{Lie}^{dg}_{k}\longrightarrow\text{Alg}^{dg}_{k}

which is given by assigning
g_{*}
to its universal enveloping algebra, defined

\mathfrak{g}_{*}\longmapsto U(\mathfrak{g}_{*}):=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\mathfrak{g}_{*}^{\otimes_{n}}/\Big((x\otimes y)-(-1)^{pq}(y\otimes x)-[x,y]\Big)

where
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{p},y\in\mathfrak{g}_{q}.

U(\mathfrak{g}_{*})
in fact admits a filtration

U(\mathfrak{g}_{*})^{\leq 0}\subset U(\mathfrak{g}_{*})^{\leq 1}\subset\ldots

where each
U(\mathfrak{g}_{*})^{\leq n}
is the image of
\bigoplus_{0\leq i\leq n}\mathfrak{g}_{*}^{\otimes_{i}}
in
U(\mathfrak{g}_{*}).

3.1.4
Definition

Let
\phi:\mathfrak{g}_{*}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}_{*}
be a morphism of dg-Lie algebras over
k.
We say that
\phi
is a
quasi-isomorphism
if the underlying map of chain complexes induces an isomorphism on homology.

We now come to an important construction in the general theory; that of a model category. See the appendix for further exposition!

3.1.5
Remark

The category
\text{Vect}^{dg}_{k}
has the structure of a model category, wherein we say that a map of chain complexes
f:V_{*}\longrightarrow W_{*}
is

1.

a fibration if each induced map
V_{n}\longrightarrow W_{n}
is surjective

2.

a cofibration if each induced map
V_{n}\longrightarrow W_{n}
is injective

3.

a weak equivalence if it is a quasi-isomorphism.

3.1.6
Proposition

Let
k
be a field of characteristic zero. The category

\text{Lie}^{dg}_{k}

has the structure of a left proper combinatorial model category
.

3.1.7
Lemma in aid of proposition 3.1.6

Let
f:\mathfrak{g}_{*}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}_{*}
be a morphism of differential graded Lie algebras over
k.
The following are equivalent:

1.

f
is a quasi-isomorphism

2.

The induced map
U(\mathfrak{g}_{*})\longrightarrow U(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}_{*})
is a quasi isomorphism of differential graded algebras.

Proof of lemma.
For every
n\in\mathbb{Z},
let
\psi:\mathfrak{g}_{*}^{\otimes_{n}}\longrightarrow U(\mathfrak{g}_{*})
denote the multiplication map. For any permutation
\sigma\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\},
let
\phi_{\sigma}
be the induced automorphism of
\mathfrak{g}_{*}^{\otimes_{n}}.
Then the map

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\sigma}\psi\circ\phi_{\sigma}

is invariant to precomposition with
\phi_{\sigma},
and thus factors as the composition

\mathfrak{g}_{*}^{\otimes_{n}}\longrightarrow\text{Sym}^{n}(\mathfrak{g}_{*})\longrightarrow U(\mathfrak{g}_{*})^{\leq n}\subset U(\mathfrak{g}_{*}).

We observe that the composition

\text{Sym}^{n}(\mathfrak{g}_{*})\longrightarrow U(\mathfrak{g}_{*})^{\leq n}\longrightarrow\text{gr}^{n}(U(\mathfrak{g}_{*}))

coincides with the isomorphism (by PBW, see [73])

\theta:\text{Sym}^{*}(\mathfrak{g}_{*})\longrightarrow\text{gr}(U(\mathfrak{g}_{*})).

It follows that the direct sum of maps

\text{Sym}^{n}(\mathfrak{g}_{*})\longrightarrow U(\mathfrak{g}_{*})^{\leq n}

is an isomorphism of chain complexes
\text{Sym}^{*}(\mathfrak{g}_{*})\simeq U(\mathfrak{g}_{*}).
Moreover, if
g:V_{*}\longrightarrow W_{*}
is a quasi-isomorphism of chain complexes of
k
-vector spaces, then
g
necessarily induces a quasi-isomorphism
\text{Sym}^{*}(V_{*})\simeq\text{Sym}^{*}(W_{*}).
Hence the completed proof follows by taking note of the isomorphism
\text{Sym}^{*}(\mathfrak{g}_{*})\simeq U(\mathfrak{g}_{*})).

Proof of proposition 3.1.6
Note that the forgetful functor
\text{Lie}^{dg}_{k}\longrightarrow\text{Vect}^{dg}_{k}
has as a left adjoint the free Lie algebra functor, denoted
\text{Free}:\text{Vect}^{dg}_{k}\longrightarrow\text{Lie}^{dg}_{k}.
For all
n\in\mathbb{Z},
we call
E(n)_{*}
the acyclic chain complex

\cdots\longrightarrow 0\longrightarrow 0\longrightarrow k\longrightarrow k\longrightarrow 0\longrightarrow 0\longrightarrow\cdots

which is nontrivial only in degrees
n
and
n-1.
Let
\partial E(n)_{*}
be the subcomplex of
E(n)_{*}
only nontrivial in degree
n-1.
Let
C_{o}
be the collection of morphisms of
\text{Lie}^{dg}_{k}
of the form

\text{Free}(\partial E(n)_{*})\longrightarrow\text{Free}(E(n)_{*}),

and let
W
be the collection of quasi-isomorphisms of
\text{Lie}^{dg}_{k}.
The claim here is that

1.

W
is
perfect
(see appendix B) (this follows from Lurie [9])

2.

If
f:\mathfrak{g}_{*}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}_{*}
is a quasi-isomorphism over
k,
and
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{n-1}
is a cycle which classifies the map

\text{Free}(\partial E(n)_{*})\longrightarrow\mathfrak{g}_{k},

then the induced map

\mathfrak{g}_{*}\coprod_{\text{Free}(\partial E(n)_{*})}\text{Free}(E(n)_{*})\longrightarrow\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}_{*}\coprod_{\text{Free}(\partial E(n)_{*})}\text{Free}(E(n)_{*})

is a quasi-isomorphism.

We will now sketch the proof of 2. Let
F:U(\mathfrak{g}_{*})\longrightarrow U(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}_{*})
be the map induced by
f.
By lemma 3.1.7,
F
itself is a quasi-isomorphism. We can construct a differential graded algebra
B_{*}
by adjoining, for the same cycle

3.2
Interlude into some emergent symbioses

We wish to begin leading ourselves into the connection between formal moduli problems and differential graded Lie algebras. For starters, we have a powerful theorem connecting the two, the proof of which can be found in Pridham’s [15].

3.2.1
Theorem (powerful)

Let
Moduli
denote the full subcategory of

\text{Fun}(\text{CAlg}^{sm}_{\mathbb{C}},\mathscr{S})

spanned by all of the formal moduli problems. Then there is a functor

\theta:N(\text{Lie}^{dg}_{\mathbb{C}})\longrightarrow\text{Moduli}

with the universal property that for every
\infty
-category
\mathscr{C},
composition with
\theta
induces a fully faithful embedding

\text{Fun}(\text{Moduli},\mathscr{C})\longrightarrow\text{Fun}(N(\text{Lie}^{dg}_{\mathbb{C}}),\mathscr{C})

whose essential image is the collection of all functors

F:N(\text{Lie}^{dg}_{\mathbb{C}})\longrightarrow\mathscr{C}

carrying quasi-isomorphisms of differential graded Lie algebras to equivalences in
\mathscr{C}.

3.2.2
Remark

To demonstrate the validity of this theorem’s namesake, one is invited to contemplate the following. Let
W
be the collection of quasi-isomorphisms of
\text{Lie}^{dg}_{\mathbb{C}}.
Let
\text{Lie}^{dg}_{\mathbb{C}}[W_{-1}]
be the
\infty
-category obtained from the nerve
N(\text{Lie}^{dg}_{\mathbb{C}})
by inverting all elements of
W.
Then the above theorem implies an equivalence of
\infty
-categories

\text{Lie}^{dg}_{\mathbb{C}}[W_{-1}]\simeq\text{Moduli}.
In particular, every differential graded Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{*}
determines a formal moduli problem. This significant result is the cornerstone of the connection between Lie algebras and commutative algebras, and finds itself one of the main focuses of this paper. This connection is controlled by the Chevalley-Eilenberg functor (or the Lie algebra cohomology of a dg-Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{*}),
which assigns
\mathfrak{g}_{*}
to the cochain complex of vector spaces
C^{*}(\mathfrak{g}_{*}).
Particularly, this construction determines a functor

(\text{Lie}^{dg}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\text{op}}\longrightarrow\text{CAlg}^{dg}_{\mathbb{C}},

carrying quasi-isomorphisms to quasi-isomorphisms, and in so doing induces a functor between
\infty
-categories

(\text{Lie}^{dg}_{\mathbb{C}}[W_{-1}])^{\text{op}}\longrightarrow\text{CAlg}^{dg}_{\mathbb{C}}[W^{\prime-1}],

where

is the
\text{CAlg}^{dg}_{\mathbb{C}}
version of

Now that we understand a lot of the machinery of differential graded Lie algebras, we can expand our categorical understanding of
\text{Lie}^{dg}_{k}.
Recall that, given a model category
\mathscr{C},
we can get its homotopy category

by effectively inverting its weak equivalences. The homotopy category gives us a nice way of condensing information about composable morphisms into just
\pi_{0}

for

So we have a bit of a goldilocks-style dilemma, and for this we turn to the notion of an underlying
\infty
-category. Let’s examine our category of interest,
\text{Lie}^{dg}_{k}.
Let
k
be a field of characteristic zero. We define the underlying
\infty
-category
\text{Lie}_{k}
of
\text{Lie}^{dg}_{k}
to be an
\infty
-category equipped with a functor

satisfying the (now familiar) universal property that for any
\infty
-category
\mathscr{C},
composition with
u
induces an equivalence from
\text{Fun}(\text{Lie}_{k},\mathscr{C})
to the full subcategory of
\text{Fun}(N(\text{Lie}^{dg}_{k}),\mathscr{C})
spanned by functors

which carry quasi-isomorphisms to equivalences in

(By equivalences, we mean isomorphisms up to higher homotopy). We call

the
\infty
-category of differential graded Lie algebras over

3.3

Let

Let

4.2
Koszul Duality

Let
k
be a field of characteristic zero. It follows from 3.3.5 that the functor
C^{*}:\mathfrak{g}_{*}\longmapsto C^{*}(\mathfrak{g}_{*})
carries quasi-isomorphisms to quasi-isomorphism. We then obtain a functor between
\infty
-categories
\text{Lie}_{k}\longrightarrow\text{CAlg}^{op}_{k},
which we still denote by
C^{*}.
Note that this functor carries the intial object
0\in\text{Lie}_{k}
to the terminal object
k\in\text{CAlg}^{op}_{k}.
We obtain another functor
\text{Lie}_{k}\longrightarrow(\text{CAlg}^{aug}_{k})^{op}
whose target is the
\infty
-category of augmented
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
-algebras over
k.
We continue to abuse notation by calling this functor
C^{*}
as well. This functor preserves small colimits, and we note that
\text{Lie}_{k}
is presentable. We define the functor

\mathfrak{D}:(\text{CAlg}^{aug}_{k})^{op}\longrightarrow\text{Lie}_{k}

to be the right adjoint of the functor
C^{*}:\text{Lie}_{k}\longrightarrow(\text{CAlg}^{aug}_{k})^{op},
and call it the
Koszul duality functor
. Our goal in introducing this is to prove that
\mathfrak{D}
is a deformation theory, which will help us prove the coPGI. First we need to verify that
\mathfrak{D}
is a weak deformation theory. Recall these axioms. The first two are automatic, since
\text{Lie}_{k}
is presentable and
\mathfrak{D}
admits a left adjoint by construction. For axiom 3, we will prove the following

4.2.1
Proposition

Let
k
be a field of characteristic zero, and
\mathfrak{g}_{*}
a differential graded Lie algebra over
k.
Let
\mathscr{C}
be the full subcategory of
\text{Lie}_{k}
spanned by cofibrant (with respect to the model on
\text{Lie}^{dg}_{k}
) objects satisfying the following

1.

There exists a graded vector space
V_{*}\subset\mathfrak{g}_{*}
such that for each integer
n,

\dim V_{n}<\infty.

2.

For all
n\geq 0,

V_{n}
is trivial

3.

V_{*}
freely generates
\mathfrak{g}_{*}
as a graded Lie algebra.

Then
\mathscr{C}
satisfies axiom 3. The proof of this relies on the following lemma, whose proof can be found in Lurie [7] §2.

4.2.2
Lemma in aid of Proposition

Let
\mathfrak{g}_{*}
be a differential graded Lie algebra over
k,
and assume that for each

\dim\mathfrak{g}_{n}<\infty,
and that
\mathfrak{g}_{n}
is trivial for each
n\geq 0.
Then the unit map
u:\mathfrak{g}_{*}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{D}(C^{*}(\mathfrak{g}))
is an equivalence in
\text{Lie}_{k}.

We are now approaching the proof of the coPGI. We just need to know how to construct the functor
\Psi:\text{Lie}_{k}\longrightarrow\text{Moduli}^{\Gamma}.
Let
\mathfrak{g}_{*}\in\text{Lie}^{dg}_{k},
and

We can identify
R
with an augemented commutative dg-algebra over
k.
Call its augmentation ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{R}.
Then the tensor product
\mathfrak{m}_{R}\otimes_{k}\mathfrak{g}_{*}
is a differential graded Lie algebra over
k.
To properly construct
\Psi,
we want
\Psi(\mathfrak{g}_{*})(R)
to be a space of
Maurer-Cartan elements
, i.e the space of solutions to the Maurer-Cartan equation
dx=[x,x].
We call such a space
MC(\mathfrak{g}_{*}).
Fortunately for us, there is a well defined bifunctor

MC:\text{CAlg}^{aug}_{k}\times\text{Lie}_{k}\longrightarrow\mathscr{S}

given by
(R,\mathfrak{g}_{*})\longmapsto MC(\mathfrak{m}_{R}\otimes_{k}\mathfrak{g}_{*}),
which we can also describe in terms of the Koszul duality functor, namely

MC(R,\mathfrak{g}_{*})=\text{Map}_{\text{Lie}_{k}}(\mathfrak{D}(R),\mathfrak{g}_{*}).

This is how we’ll define our functor
\Psi.

4.2.3
Theorem [7]

Let

be a field of characteristic zero. Let

(\text{CAlg}^{aug}_{k},\{E\})

be the deformation context we work with. Then the Koszul duality functor

.

Sketch of proof.

Let

such that

The previous proposition shows that
\mathfrak{D}(E)
is given by the infinite loop object
\{\text{Free}(k[-n-1])\}_{n\geq 0}
in
\text{Lie}^{op}_{k}
(see Lurie [7]). Here Free

denotes the left adjoint of the forgetful functor

It follows that the functor
e:\text{Lie}_{k}\longrightarrow\textbf{Spc}
from the prior chapter is given by the composition
(F\circ\theta)[1],
where
F:\text{Mod}_{k}=\text{Mod}_{k}(\textbf{Spc})\longrightarrow\textbf{Spc}
is the forgetful functor. The claim of this proof follows from the technical
\infty
-categorical considerations of observing that
F
and
\theta
preserve colimits and sifted colimits, respectively.

We are now ready to prove the main result:

4.2.4
Theorem (Lurie) a.k.a coPGI

Let
k
be a field of characteristic zero. Let

\text{Lie}_{k}

denote the
\infty
-category underlying

\text{Lie}^{dg}_{k}

obtained by inverting quasi-isomorphisms. Then there is an equivalence of
\infty
-categories

\Psi:\text{Lie}_{k}\longrightarrow\text{Moduli}_{k}.

Proof.
Let
k
be a field of characteristic zero. Let
\Psi:\text{Lie}_{k}\longrightarrow\text{Fun}(\text{CAlg}^{sm}_{k},\mathscr{S})
denote the functor given by objects of the form

\Psi(\mathfrak{g}_{*})(R)=\text{Map}_{\text{Lie}_{k}}(\mathfrak{D}(R),\mathfrak{g}_{*}).

Combining theorems 1.4.6 and 4.2.3, we observe that
\Psi
is a fully faithful embedding whose essential image (smallest subcategory respecting isomorphism which contains the image) is the fullsubcategory
\text{Moduli}_{k}\subset\text{Fun}(\text{CAlg}^{sm}_{k},\mathscr{S})
spanned by formal moduli problems.

5
Appendix A: Dan Quillen and model categories

Model categories were introduced by Dan Quillen in his book
Homotopical Algebra
from 1967, for the purpose of providing a framework for homotopy theory A
model category
is roughly speaking a category
\mathscr{C}
which has three classes of morphisms, called
weak equivalences, fibrations,
and
cofibrations.
Weak equivalences play (the more generalized) role of homotopy equivalences, while fibrations and cofibrations are more like inclusions and surjections, respectively, satisfying some lifting properties. This is of course very abstract and un-concrete, but we hope to resolve this with some examples, and, more importantly, the connection to the main subject matter of this paper.

5.1
Model categories

Suppose that
\mathscr{C}
is a category. A morphism
f\in\mathscr{C}
is called a
retract
of a map
g\in\mathscr{C}
if there exists a commutative diagram of the form

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}

such that compositions
A\longrightarrow C\longrightarrow A=\text{id}_{A}
and
B\longrightarrow D\longrightarrow B=\text{id}_{B}.
A (functorial) factorization is a pair of functors
(\alpha,\beta)
from
\text{Map}(\mathscr{C})\longrightarrow\text{Map}(\mathscr{C})
such that
f=\beta(f)\circ\alpha(f)
with agreements

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\text{domain of }f}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\text{domain of }\alpha(f)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\text{domain of }\beta(f)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\text{codomain of }f}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\text{codomain of }\beta(f)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\text{codomain of }\alpha(f)}}

Suppose that

and
\varphi:X\longrightarrow Y
are maps in
\mathscr{C}.
We say that
\psi
has the
left lifting property
with respect to
\varphi
and that
\varphi
has the
right lifting property
with respect to
\psi
if for every commutative square

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}

there exists a lift

such that

and

5.1.1
Definition

A
model structure
on a category
\mathscr{C}
is the three subcategories of
\mathscr{C}
of weak equivalences, fibrations, and cofibrations, satisfying

1.

Let
f,g
be morphisms in
\mathscr{C}
such that
g\circ f
is definable. If any pair of the three maps
f,g,
and
g\circ f
are weak equivalences, then so is the third.

2.

If
f
is a retract of
g
and
g
is a weak equivalence, fibration, or cofibration, then so is
f.

3.

A map
f
is called a trivial or acyclic (co)fibration if it is a (co)fibration and a weak equivalence. We mandate that acyclic cofibrations satisfy left lifting with respect to fibrations, and cofibrations satisfy left lifting with respect to acyclic fibrations.

A
model category
is then a category
\mathscr{C}
with a model structure and small (co)limits. By the first axiom, any model category has an initial object
\varnothing
and a terminal object *. We call an object
X\in\mathscr{C}

fibrant
if the unique map
X\longrightarrow*
is a fibration, and
cofibrant
if the unique map
\varnothing\longrightarrow X
is a cofibration.

5.1.2
Quillen functors

Suppose that
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
are model categories. A
left Quillen functor

F:\mathscr{C}\longrightarrow\mathscr{D}
is a functor which is left adjoint and preserves (acyclic) cofibrations. A
right Quillen functor
is similarly a functor
G:\mathscr{C}\longrightarrow\mathscr{D}
which is right adjoint and preserves (acyclic) fibrations.

5.1.3
A pertinent type of model category

Categories with their objects being chain complexes form important types of model categories. For instance, let
A
be an abelian Grothendieck category. We can define a category
C(A)
with objects being chain complexes

\cdots\longrightarrow X_{1}\longrightarrow X_{0}\longrightarrow X_{-1}\longrightarrow\cdots

and morphisms being chain maps. Then
C(A)
has a model structure by setting cofibrations as monomorphisms and weak equivalences as quasi-isomorphisms.

5.1.4
Definition: Combinatorial model category

A model category
\mathscr{C}
is called
combinatorial
if it contains

1.

a set
S
of small objects such that every object in
\mathscr{C}
is a colimit over objects in
S.
Equivalently,
\mathscr{C}
has a fully faithful right adjoint localization
\mathscr{C}\hookrightarrow\text{Psh}(S),
where
\text{PSh}(S)
is the category of presheaves on
S.

2.

a set of cofibrations and a set of acyclic cofibrations which ”generate” all (acyclic) cofibrations in
\mathscr{C}.

Note also that a model category is
left proper
if for every diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{X\cup B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h}

where
i
is a cofibration and
f
is a weak equivalence, the map
h
is also a weak equivalence.

5.2.1
Proposition

The following are equivalent:

1.

F
preserves (trivial) cofibrations

2.

G
preserves (trivial) fibrations

3.

F
preserves cofibrations and
G
preserves fibrations

4.

F
preserves trivial cofibrations and
G
preserves trivial fibrations.

If any of these are satisfied, we say that
(F,G)
deetermines a
Quillen adjunction
on the categories
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}.

Suppose that

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathscr{D}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}

is a Quillen adjunction. We also say that
F
is a left Quillen functor and
G
is a right Quillen functor. We can get the
homotopy category

h\mathscr{C}
of
\mathscr{C}
by first passing to the full subcategory of cofibrant objects in
\mathscr{C}
and inverting all weak equivalences (similarly for
\mathscr{D}).
Because
F
preserves trivial cofibrations (i.e., weak equivalences between cofibrant objects), it induces a functor
h\mathscr{C}\longrightarrow h\mathscr{D}
called the
left derived functor
of
F,
denoted
LF.
Similary, we can describe the right derived functor of
G,
called
RG.
Moreover, it is the case that

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{h\mathscr{C}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{h\mathscr{D}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces LF}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces RG}

determines an adjunction.

5.2.2
Proposition

Let

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathscr{D}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}

be a Quillen adjunction. Then the following are equivalent:

1.

LF:h\mathscr{C}\longrightarrow h\mathscr{D}
is an equivalence of categories.

2.

RG:h\mathscr{D}\longrightarrow h\mathscr{C}
is an equivalence of categories.

3.

For any cofibrant object
C\in\mathscr{C},
and any fibrant object
D\in\mathscr{D},
a map
C\longrightarrow G(D)
is a weak equivalence in
\mathscr{C}
if and only if the adjoint
F(C)\longrightarrow D
is a weak equivalence in
\mathscr{D}.

Proof.

1.\iff 2.
is immediate since
RG
and
LF
are adjoint. Both are equivalent to the statement that

u:\text{id}_{\mathscr{C}}\longrightarrow RG\circ LF,v:LF\circ RG\longrightarrow\text{id}_{\mathscr{D}}

are weak equivalences. Moreover, we have
(RG\circ LF)(C)=G(D)
where
F(C)\longrightarrow D
is a weak equivalence in
\mathscr{D}.
Thus
u
is a weak equivalence when evaluated on
C
if and only if for every weak equivalence
F(C)\longrightarrow D,
the adjoint
C\longrightarrow G(D)

is a weak equivalence. The same argument follows for
v,
and so
1\iff 2\iff 3.

If any of the three hold, we sat that
(F,G)
gives a
Quillen equivalence
on
\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D}.

5.2.3
Homotopy limits and colimits

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a category with weak equivalences, and let
\mathscr{D}
be a small diagram category. We can turn the functor category
\text{Fun}(\mathscr{D},\mathscr{C})
(the category with objects as functors
\mathscr{D}\longrightarrow\mathscr{C}
and morphisms natural transformations) into a category with weak equivalences by declaring them to be those natural transformations which are objectwise weak equivalences. The
homotopy limit
of a functor
G:\mathscr{D}\longrightarrow\mathscr{C}
is the image of
G
under the right derived functor of the limit
\lim_{\mathscr{D}}:\text{Fun}(\mathscr{D},\mathscr{C})\longrightarrow\mathscr{C}
with respect to weak equivalences on
\mathscr{C}
and
\text{Fun}(\mathscr{D},\mathscr{C}).
Similarly, the
homotopy colimit
of a functor
H:\mathscr{D}\longrightarrow\mathscr{C}
is the image of
H
under the left derived functor of
\text{colim}_{D}:\text{Fun}(\mathscr{D},\mathscr{C})\longrightarrow\mathscr{C}
with respect to weak equivalences on
\mathscr{C}
and
\text{Fun}(\mathscr{D},\mathscr{C}).

6.1.5
Definition: Perfection (of classes of morphisms)

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a presentable category. A class
W
of morphisms in
\mathscr{C}
is called
perfect
(in [40] A.2.6.12) if it satisfies the following conditions:

1.

Every isomorphism is an element of
W.

2.

For any pair of composable morphisms
f
and
g,
if any pair of the three maps
f,g,g\circ f
is in
W,
then the third is as well.

3.

Let
\{f_{\alpha}:X_{\alpha}\longrightarrow Y_{\alpha}\}
be a collection of morphisms indexed by a filtered poset. Let

X=\lim_{\longrightarrow}\{X_{\alpha}\},\text{ }Y=\lim_{\longrightarrow}\{Y_{\alpha}\}.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow Y
be the induced map. If each
f_{\alpha}\in W,
then
f\in W.
This is equivalent to the statement that
W
is stable under filtered colimits.

4.

There exists a subset
W_{0}\subset W
such that every
f\in W
is a filtered colimit of morphisms in
W_{0}.

6.1.6
The nerve of a small category

Let
\mathscr{J}
be a small category. The
nerve

N(\mathscr{J})
of
\mathscr{J}
is the simplicial set whose 0-simplices are objects of
\mathscr{J},
1-simplices are morphisms in
\mathscr{J},
2-simplices are pairs of composable morphisms, so on and so forth. We say that
N(\mathscr{J})
is
sifted
if for any family of diagrams
D_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{n}:\mathscr{J}\longrightarrow\text{Set},
the set-theoretic colimits of
D_{i}
commute with finite products. Concretely, if
N(\mathscr{J})
is sifted, then

\lim\limits_{\longrightarrow}(D_{1}\times\cdots\times D_{n})\simeq\lim\limits_{\longrightarrow}D_{1}\times\lim\limits_{\longrightarrow}D_{2}\times\cdots\times\lim\limits_{\longrightarrow}D_{n}.

6.1.7
The largest Kan complex

Let
\mathscr{C}^{\simeq}
denote the
\infty
-category one gets from throwing out all non invertible morphisms of
\mathscr{C}.
This is equivalently the largest Kan complex contained in
\mathscr{C}.

6.2
Stabilizations, loops, and suspensions

6.2.1
The loop and suspension functors

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a pointed
\infty
-category, i.e.
\mathscr{C}
has a 0-object and finite (co)limits. The
loop functor

\Omega:\mathscr{C}\longrightarrow\mathscr{C}
takes an object
X\in\mathscr{C}
to its space of loops based at the zero map. That is,
\Omega X=0\times_{X}0
is defined by the pullback square

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Omega X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}

Suggestively and similarly, the
suspension functor

\Sigma:\mathscr{C}\longrightarrow\mathscr{C}
is defined by
\Sigma X=0\coprod_{X}0,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}

6.2.2
Definition: Stabilization

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a pointed
\infty
-category. The
stabilization

\text{Stab}(\mathscr{C}),
which would benefit from less conspicuous notation, is the stable
(\Omega
and
\Sigma
are mutual inverses)
\infty
-category of spectrum objects in
\mathscr{C}.
Less mercurially, the objects of
\text{Stab}(\mathscr{C})
are sequences
X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
with equivalences
X_{n}\simeq\Omega X_{n+1}.

\text{Stab}(\mathscr{C})
satisfies the following universal property motivated by the existence of the a canonical functor

\Sigma^{\infty}:\mathscr{C}\longrightarrow\text{Stab}(\mathscr{C})

such that for any stable
\infty
-category
\mathscr{D},
precomposition with
\Sigma^{\infty}
yields an equivalence

\text{Fun}_{\text{ex}}(\text{Stab}(\mathscr{C}),\mathscr{D})\simeq\text{Fun}_{*}(\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D})

where the LHS consists of exact functors and the RHS of 0-preserving functors.
