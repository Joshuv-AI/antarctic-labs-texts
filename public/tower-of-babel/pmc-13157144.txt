Anatomical Predictors of Second Molar External Root Resorption: A Cone-Beam Computed Tomography Study of Impacted Third Molars
Authors: Mingyue Wang, Jingyi Wang, Ieoktong U, Yumin Wu, Sien Zhang, Zhiguo Liu, Zongxi Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to identify anatomical and radiographic risk factors associated with second molar (M2) external root resorption (ERR) induced by impacted third molars (M3) using cone-beam computed tomography, providing a strategic framework for clinical decision-making regarding prophylactic extraction.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on cone-beam computed tomography scans of 513 impacted M3s. Anatomical parameters, including M3 angulation, impaction depth (Pell and Gregory classification), and skeletal constraints (M3 to the posterior border of the maxilla, distance from the M3 root to the posterior border of the mandible, etc.) were quantified. The severity and anatomical location of M2 ERR were evaluated using stratified statistical methods to identify key predictors of resorption.ResultsIn the univariate analysis, M2 ERR severity was significantly associated with M3 impaction depth and skeletal space constraints rather than angulation alone (P < .05). Class C impaction with apical-third involvement represented the highest risk profile; notably, 100% of severe maxillary ERR cases were localized to the M2 apical third. In the multivariate model, an increased distance from the M3 to the posterior border of the maxilla (OR = 1.26) and a reduced cervical width of the M2 were key predictors of severity. Furthermore, a distinct sexual dimorphism was observed, with female patients exhibiting a significantly higher prevalence of severe maxillary ERR, likely due to more restricted skeletal space.ConclusionsThe severity of M2 ERR is primarily driven by impaction depth and anatomical constraints, particularly the limited space in the maxillary tuberosity and mandibular retromolar area. Class C impaction targeting the M2 apical third is the most critical risk factor for severe resorption.Clinical relevanceClinicians should consider a proactive management strategy, prioritizing preventive M3 extraction in high-risk cases – specifically Class C impactions in female patients – to avoid irreversible pulp involvement and potential M2 loss. These findings support shifting from passive observation to early intervention based on specific anatomical risk profiles.Key words: External root resorption, Cone-beam computed tomography, Impacted third molars, Anatomical risk factors, Sexual dimorphism

Introduction

Impacted third molars (M3) are the most common type of impacted teeth,1 the indications for M3 extraction broadly categorized as therapeutic or prophylactic. For M3s without clinical symptoms, clear criteria for preventive removal remain undefined. Research indicates that impacted M3 can easily lead to root resorption of the adjacent second molar (M2),2 a condition often diagnosed at a late stage. In severe cases, the affected M2 may even require extraction.3,4 Given the significant risks, such as nerve injury and oroantral fistula, posed by the extraction of deeply impacted M3, accurately predicting the degree of adjacent M2 root resorption becomes critically important for informing and guiding clinical decision-making.

Root resorption is defined as the odontoclast-mediated, nonbacterial destruction of dental hard tissues (dentin, cementum, and/or enamel).5 The process occurs in three stages: initiation, resorption, and repair. The resorptive process may be self-limiting and clinically undetectable. However, if persistent due to factors such as infection and/or sustained pressure, the initial surface resorption may progress, leading to continued destruction of dental hard tissues – a process which can culminate in irreversible tooth loss.6 Root resorption caused by M3 impaction is a type of external pressure resorption. Typically asymptomatic in its early stages and devoid of clinical signs, it is most often detected incidentally through radiographic examination. Consequently, external root resorption (ERR) of the M2 represents an important late sequela of M3 impaction. Early and accurate diagnosis of M2 root resorption is therefore paramount to arrest pathological progression. In the context of considering preventive M3 extraction, risk factors constitute a crucial consideration.

In previous studies, various risk factors for M2 ERR caused by impacted M3s have been identified. Wang et al7 primarily focused on factors such as M3 angulation and depth of impaction within the mandible, while Kou et al8 developed a predictive model incorporating variables including jaw location (maxilla/mandible), impact type, impact depth (PG: A-B-C), contact relationship, M3 root number, and patient age. Nevertheless, existing literature has predominantly concentrated on mandibular ERR, or with insufficient investigation into maxillary-specific risk factors such as the distance from the M3 cemento-enamel junction (CEJ) to the posterior border of the maxilla. Furthermore, analyses for mandibular ERR remain incomplete, lacking comprehensive evaluation of parameters such as the mandibular angle, the spatial relationship between the M3 root and the mandibular canal, and the minimum vertical distance from the M3 root to the posterior border of the mandible. Meanwhile, the management of asymptomatic impacted M3 remains a subject of intense clinical debate. The decision between watchful waiting and prophylactic extraction hinges on the accurate assessment of potential risks to the adjacent M2. However, current guidelines lack quantitative anatomical predictors, often leading to delayed intervention and irreversible M2 damage.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the influence of maxillary-specific factors, particularly the distance from the M3 CEJ to the posterior maxillary border. Furthermore, we conducted a more comprehensive analysis of mandibular ERR by incorporating additional anatomical factors, including the mandibular angle and spatial relationships with the mandibular canal and posterior border. By identifying specific anatomical risk factors via cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT),9,10 this study aims to provide clinicians with a reliable predictive framework for determining whether to opt for prophylactic extraction or periodic observation of impacted M3s.

Patients and methods

Clinical data were collected from 513 patients (210 male, 303 female, totalling 513 teeth) diagnosed with M2 ERR caused by impacted M3s at the Hospital of Stomatology, Sun Yat-sen University, from January 2020 to April 2023. Each patient underwent further CBCT examination. The ethical review number of this study was KQEC-2026-056-01, and was conducted in accordance with the institutional and national ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation and with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients with complete case data; CBCT scans that clearly depict the positional relationship between the impacted M3 and the adjacent M2.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with cystic lesions, extensively distributed carious lesions, or developmental anomalies affecting M2 or M3; development of the M3 less than 2/3; local tooth anatomy and structure obscured by the presence of high-density materials (eg, dental restorations, root canal filling materials) or other reasons.5

The sample size was determined based on the available cases meeting the inclusion criteria during the study period. For the multivariate logistic regression, which focused on predicting severe ERR (n = 134) vs nonsevere ERR (n = 379), the sample size provided an events-per-variable ratio of approximately 19.1 (with 7 independent variables included in the model). This exceeds the recommended threshold of 10 to 15 events-per-variable, ensuring sufficient statistical power and minimizing the risk of model overfitting.

Observation of CBCT images

The images were obtained by a CBCT scanner (NewTom VGI). The operating parameters were set at 110.0 kVp and 11.05 mA with an exposure time of 1.8 seconds. It had 3500 radiographs with a field of view of 16 × 16 cm and a voxel size of 0.30 mm, which were just enough to display the complete upper and lower dentition and were of sufficiently high resolution. The measurements were evaluated by using the Carestream Vue PACS software (Carestream Health Inc).

Severity of ERR

According to the Ericson classification,11 ERR was divided into three grades based on severity: Mild (resorption not exceeding half the thickness of the dentin) (Figure 1a, b); moderate (root resorption exceeding half the dentin thickness, but the pulp endothelium is not breached) (Figure 1c, d); severe (resorption extending into the pulp cavity) (Figure 1e, f).

Severity of ERR: Mild (a and b); moderate (c and d); severe (e and f).

ERR location of M2

Based on the root resorption location, the root was divided longitudinally into 3 equal parts: cervical 1/3 (Figure 1e), middle 1/3 (Figure 1a), and apical 1/3 (Figure 1c).

M3 impaction angulation

The angulation between the M2 and M3 was determined by measuring the angle formed between their occlusal plane (Figure 2a). M3 impaction was defined as distoangular (<−14°), vertical (−14° to 15°), mesioangular (16° to 75°), and horizontal (>75°) based on the impact angle.12

(a) M3 impaction angulation. (b) M3 impaction depth. (c) CEJ distance between M2 and M3.

M3 impaction depth

According to the Pell and Gregory classification,13 M3 impaction depth was divided into the following groups: A: The occlusal plane of the M3 crown is level with or above that of the M2. B: The occlusal plane of the M3 is below that of the M2 but above the cervical line (CEJ) of the M2. C: The occlusal plane of the M3 is below the cervical line (CEJ) of the M2 (Figure 2b). In our study, we combined Class A and B into a single reference group to avoid complete separation in the logistic regression model and excessively wide confidence intervals caused by an insufficient or even zero sample size of moderate and severe ERR in Group A.

CEJ distance between M2 and M3

According to Leone et al,14 the distance between the distal CEJ of the M2 and the mesial CEJ of the M3 was measured, abbreviated as the ‘CEJ distance’ (Figure 2c).

M3 to the posterior border of the maxilla

The distance from the distal CEJ of M3 to the posterior border of the maxilla was measured along the occlusal plane (or sagittal axis (Figure 3a).

(a) M3 to the posterior border of the maxilla. (b) Distance from the M3 root to the mandibular canal. (c) Cervical width of M3.

Distance from the M3 root to the mandibular canal

This parameter was defined as the shortest vertical distance from the root apex of the M3 (the root tip closest to the inferior alveolar nerve canal) to the canal (Figure 3b).

Cervical width of M3

From the mesial to the distal cervical of M3 (Figure 3c).

Minimum vertical distance from the M3 root to the posterior border of the mandible

A vertical line connecting the root of M3 to the posterior border of the mandibular (Co-Go) (Figure 4a).

(a) Minimum vertical distance from the M3 root to the posterior border of the mandible. (b) Mandibular angle.

Mandibular angle

Based on the facial skeletal landmarks described by Swennen, the mandibular angle was defined using the points of Co-Go-Me15 (Figure 4b).

Assessment of reliability and reproducibility

To ensure the reliability of the radiographic measurements, all CBCT images were evaluated by two independent observers (oral radiologists with over 5 years of experience). Both observers were blinded to the clinical information of the patients. A random subset of 10% of the total sample was re-examined by both observers to assess interobserver reliability. Furthermore, the first observer repeated the same measurements after a 2-week interval to determine intraobserver reliability. The intraclass correlation coefficient was utilized to evaluate the consistency of continuous variables, while the weighted Kappa coefficient was used for categorical variables.

Statistical analysis

The R software (version 4.3.1; http://www.r-project.org/) was utilized for all statistical analyses. Descriptive statistics were examined using the chi-square test, and intergroup comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni test. Factors that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis were included in a multivariate binary logistic regression analysis. Stratified analysis was performed to examine the effects of jaw and gender on ERR. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. In the mandibular multivariate analysis, 19 cases were excluded due to incomplete radiographic visibility of specific continuous anatomical landmarks.

Results

Reliability results

The intraobserver and interobserver reliability tests demonstrated excellent agreement. The intraclass correlation coefficient values for all linear and angular measurements ranged from 0.88 to 0.96. The weighted Kappa values for ERR severity and impaction depth classifications ranged from 0.85 to 0.92, indicating high reproducibility of the assessment criteria (Table S1).

Significant associations were observed between ERR severity and specific variables within each jaw (Table 1). In the maxilla, gender, M3 impaction depth, and the location of M2 root resorption were associated with the severity. In the mandible, CEJ distance, M3 impaction depth, and the location of M2 root resorption showed significant associations.

All severe maxillary M2 ERR cases (100%) were localized in the apical region. Mild M2 ERR is more common in maxillary M3 Class A and B impaction, while Class C impaction accounted for a large proportion (94.2%) of severe M2 ERR cases in the maxilla. Severe M2 ERR in the mandible was primarily located in the apical region (87.7%), with 12.3% located in the middle region. Class C impaction accounted for 66.7% of the severe group in the mandible.

In addition, horizontal impactions constituted a significantly smaller proportion in the maxilla compared to the mandible. Root resorption of the maxillary M2s was mainly caused by the mesial impaction of the M3s (59.7%).

While mandibular M2 resorption was predominantly characterized by mesial and horizontal impactions, with a higher prevalence of mesioangular type. This may be explained by the biomechanical difference: mesioangular impaction typically generates focused, single-point pressure, whereas horizontal impaction distributes force across multiple points; concentrated pressure is postulated to be more resorptive.

Distribution characteristics of M2 root resorption in maxilla

Among patients with maxillary M2 ERR, females predominated (72.9%). Univariate analysis showed a protective effect in males (OR = 0.33, P = .005), but this effect was attenuated and lost statistical significance in multivariate analysis (OR = 0.43, P = .081), suggesting that gender may not be an independent risk factor when other anatomical variables are controlled.

Root resorption located in the middle third (OR = 0.18, P = .023) and cervical third (OR = 0.13, P = .022) of the M2 root was identified as protective factors against severe resorption. In multivariate analysis, these two factors reduced the likelihood of severe M2 root resorption, whereas root resorption in the apical third was a high-risk factor. The cervical width of M2 was a protective factor, retaining significance in both univariate and multivariate analyses, indicating its role as a key factor. In the severe group, M3 to the posterior border of the maxilla (OR = 1.26, P = .022) was longer than the other two groups (Table 2).

Discussion

Whether in the maxilla or mandible, the severity of M2 ERR was associated with the depth of M3 impaction (P < .05) and the location of ERR (P < .001). This finding is inconsistent with Wang et al,7 who found no significant relationship between ERR severity and impaction depth or ERR location, but it fully aligns with the study by Kou et al.8 The discrepancy may be related to sample size. The risk of ERR increases as the impaction depth increases from Class A to Class C, consistent with the results of Cui et al.17

A statistically significant association exists between the location of ERR and its severity: the cervical third is most affected by mild ERR, while the apical third is most affected by severe ERR. This contrasts with the findings of Lacerda-Santos et al,18 who identified the middle third as the most severely affected site, and it is consistent with other CBCT-based studies.19 The cervical area often exhibits mild ERR, possibly because the dentin layer is thicker when it closer to the cervical region.

While traditional literature emphasizes that mesioangular and horizontal impactions pose the highest risk,17,20 our findings suggest that M3 angulation alone does not significantly dictate the severity of ERR. This discrepancy can be explained by the primacy of impaction depth and the precision of the contact point. Class C impaction aligns the M3 with the apical third of the M2 root – an area characterized by thinner cementum and higher anatomical vulnerability. At this depth, any intimate contact can result in significant stress concentration, triggering aggressive odontoclast-mediated resorption. In essence, the location of the impact and the constraint of the space (as evidenced by the CEJ distance) are more critical biological determinants of severe ERR than the mere geometric tilt of the tooth.8

In the mandibular stratified analysis, M3 impaction depth (Class C OR = 2.73, P < .001) is significant in the univariate analysis, suggesting that deep impaction may increase ERR risk, requiring larger sample sizes for verification. The cervical region is associated with mild ERR, making it a protective factor, a conclusion generally consistent with previous research. For the maxilla, the maxillary tuberosity serves as the primary bony space for the eruption of the maxillary M3. Due to anatomical constraints, when this area was underdeveloped, M3 tends to push M2 forward, which explains why severe maxillary ERR was often associated with M3 mesial impaction. All severe ERR cases in the maxilla were located apically, while severe ERR in the mandible was primarily apical (86.2%). The reason for the difference may be that in maxillary ERR, the deeper the impacted depth of the M3, the more likely it is to cause ERR. This may be related to the lower density of the maxilla, which results in lower eruption resistance of the maxillary M3 and thinner cementum in the apical region. It could also be due to the smaller maxillary sample size.

The higher prevalence of severe maxillary ERR in females may be primarily attributed to sexual dimorphism in maxillofacial skeletal dimensions. Previous anatomical studies have confirmed that females generally possess smaller maxillary bone dimensions and shorter dental arches compared to males. This reduced space in the maxillary tuberosity region limits the available eruption path for the M3, resulting in more frequent and intense physical contact between the M3 crown/root and the M2 distal root surface, thereby exacerbating the resorptive process.21,22 Class C impaction in females carried a high risk of severe ERR and was statistically significant, whereas impaction depth was not significant in males, possibly because maxillary M3 impaction is more common in females. In the female stratum, smaller M3 width was associated with higher risk of severe ERR, possibly because the smaller the M3 width, the more likely it is to be related to deeper impaction and development, while deeper impaction positions are more likely to affect the root.23 Increasing age was also a significant factor for females, consistent with observations by Li et al.24 This may be due to the effect of the mesial migration force of teeth with age. However, this pattern did not hold true for males, consistent with results from Ericson et al.11 Therefore, from a clinical perspective, clinicians should adopt a more proactive screening protocol for female patients with deep impactions. Once a Class C impaction is identified and the pressure point is directed towards the apical third of the M2 root, by taking into account factors such as the location of the inferior alveolar nerve,25 clinicians should consider preventive extraction of the M3 to avoid the progression of irreversible severe ERR and potential pulp involvement.

Our study has limitations. The patients included here were strictly filtered and selected according to our inclusion and exclusion criteria to minimize sample heterogeneity as much as possible. However, this study is retrospective, introducing potential selection bias. For example, there were very few cases of maxillary Class C impaction; the sample size should be expanded further. Meanwhile, the spatial resolution, which limited by a 0.30 mm voxel size, might influence the detection threshold for minor hard tissue changes. Although adequate for assessing the severity and depth of established ERR, the potential for missing very small, superficial resorptive pits cannot be entirely ruled out, particularly in the initial stages of the resorptive process.

It is important to note that the severity of M2 ERR may be influenced by factors beyond local anatomy, such as genetic predisposition or systemic conditions affecting cementum metabolism. Compared to previous similar studies, this study selected a larger sample size, providing stronger evidence for the conclusions. This study employed stratified statistical methods, potentially revealing more patterns.

The decision to retain or preventive extract impacted M3 has always been a hotly debated topic. Our research has found that the occurrence of M2 ERR was related to various factors. Combining CBCT may help analyse the probability of M2 ERR and provide effective clinical guidance for impacted M3 tooth extraction or regular observation.

Conclusion

The severity of M2 ERR is significantly associated with the depth of M3 impaction and the anatomical location of the resorption. Class C impaction and apical-third involvement are the most critical risk factors for severe ERR in both jaws, with 100% of severe maxillary ERR cases localized at the apical of M2. Anatomical measurements provide key predictive value: an increased distance from M3 to the posterior border of the maxilla and a shorter cervical width of M2 are associated with higher ERR severity. Furthermore, a distinct sexual dimorphism exists, with females exhibiting a significantly higher prevalence of severe maxillary ERR, likely due to restricted skeletal space. Clinically, when CBCT identifies a Class C impaction with the pressure point directed at the M2 apical third, proactive preventive extraction should be considered to prevent irreversible pulp exposure.
