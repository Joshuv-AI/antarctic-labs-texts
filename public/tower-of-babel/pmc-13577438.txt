Dumping Syndrome Following Endoscopic Sleeve Gastroplasty
Authors: Muhab Saadeh, Zain Albdour, Anas Zaher, Bashar Qumseya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dumping syndrome is a recognized complication of bariatric surgery but has not been previously reported following endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty (ESG). We present a woman in her mid-30s who underwent ESG in 2022, achieving a body mass index reduction from 35.4 to 26.7 kg/m2. Following subsequent laparoscopic cholecystectomy for cholelithiasis, she developed stereotyped postprandial episodes of diaphoresis, palpitations, lightheadedness, nausea, diarrhea, and bloating, with a Sigstad score of 12 confirming dumping syndrome. Dietary modification and acarbose provided meaningful relief. This case raises the possibility that ESG-related changes in gastric physiology may predispose to dumping syndrome, particularly after subsequent abdominal surgery.

INTRODUCTION

Endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty (ESG) is a minimally invasive bariatric procedure that creates a tubular gastric configuration through full-thickness sutures extend from the prepyloric antrum toward the proximal gastric body, preserving the fundus and gastroesophageal junction. Unlike surgical sleeve gastrectomy, ESG achieves its effect without resecting any stomach tissue—instead, full-thickness suturing along the greater curvature reduces gastric capacity by up to 70%, creating a tubular configuration that slows intake and blunts hunger while leaving the pylorus and fundus intact.1,2 The weight loss outcomes are meaningful: pooled data show a mean total body weight loss of roughly 15%–17% at 12 months, with excess weight loss approaching 55%–60% and a serious adverse event rate under 2%.3 The MERIT trial added prospective randomized evidence, confirming that ESG outperforms lifestyle intervention alone in producing sustained weight reduction.4

What drives these results is more than simple volume restriction. ESG alters the mechanical behavior of the stomach reducing compliance and delaying gastric emptying—and appears to shift the hormonal environment in ways that reinforce satiety.5 Increases in GLP-1, peptide YY, and fasting ghrelin have all been documented post-procedure, along with changes in insulin secretion that may, in susceptible individuals, set the stage for postprandial glycemic instability.5–7 Dumping syndrome is a recognized complication of gastric surgery, classified into early and late forms.8 Reported prevalence of dumping syndrome after bariatric surgery ranges from 10% to 75%, depending on the procedure and diagnostic criteria used. It is most commonly associated with Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB); however, more recent studies have reported rates of approximately 27% following laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy using Sigstad-based screening, and up to 60% in long-term follow-up, even with an intact pylorus.9–11 To our knowledge, dumping syndrome has not been previously described following ESG. We present a case of dumping syndrome after ESG, with symptoms that worsened after subsequent cholecystectomy.

CASE REPORT

A woman in her mid-30s with a history of obesity (body mass index 35.4 kg/m2), gastroesophageal reflux disease, and migraines underwent uncomplicated ESG in 2022 at our institution. She had no history of diabetes mellitus and was a nonsmoker. Medications included amitriptyline, omeprazole, hyoscyamine as needed for abdominal cramping, and ondansetron as needed for nausea. The ESG was performed using a full-thickness suturing. Anterior and posterior gastric walls were marked with argon plasma coagulation along lines extending from proximal to the incisura to just distal to the fundus. The endoscope was then removed, and the OverStitch device was attached. An overtube was placed, and the scope was reinserted. A total of 6 interrupted full-thickness sutures were placed using 2-0 polypropylene in alternating I and U configurations, achieving approximately 50%–60% reduction in gastric volume with excellent tissue approximation. Following the procedure, she achieved significant and sustained weight loss, with her body mass index declining to 26.7 kg/m2 at 18 months postprocedure representing a total body weight loss of approximately 23.9%. She was enrolled in structured nutritional follow-up per institutional protocol.

Before any additional interventions, she reported mild intermittent symptoms of nausea, diarrhea, and vomiting over the year following ESG, which prompted 1 emergency department visit and subsequent evaluation by the gastroenterology team. An abdominal ultrasound performed during the emergency department visit demonstrated cholelithiasis without sonographic evidence of acute cholecystitis. These symptoms were initially attributed to postprocedural dietary intolerance and managed conservatively; however, her symptoms persisted at a low level despite conservative measures until cholecystectomy was ultimately pursued. Formal evaluation for dumping syndrome was not pursued at that time, as the symptom constellation was not fully recognized as consistent with the condition.

Following cholecystectomy, her gastrointestinal symptoms worsened significantly and assumed a more stereotyped postprandial pattern. She began experiencing recurrent episodes occurring within 10–30 minutes of meals, consisting of diaphoresis, palpitations, lightheadedness, abdominal cramping, nausea, diarrhea, and bloating. A blood glucose measurement obtained during 1 symptomatic episode was 79 mg/dL.

Physical examination was unremarkable. Laboratory evaluation including complete blood count, comprehensive metabolic panel, and thyroid function tests was within normal limits. Magnetic resonance imaging of the abdomen and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography were unremarkable. Upper endoscopy performed approximately 2 years after the original ESG demonstrated intact ESG sutures with preserved gastroplasty geometry and no evidence of suture dehiscence, stenosis, or mucosal pathology (Figure 1). Bravo pH testing showed abnormal acid exposure with a total acid exposure time of 12.9%. Colonoscopy with biopsies was unremarkable. Alternative etiologies were considered, including medication-related adverse effects, endocrine disorders, and primary bile acid diarrhea. The marked worsening of diarrhea following cholecystectomy raised concern for concomitant bile acid diarrhea; however, initiation of cholestyramine did not result in symptomatic improvement. A Sigstad score of 12 (threshold for diagnosis: ≥10) supported the clinical diagnosis of dumping syndrome in this patient. These symptoms were consistent with features of both early and late dumping syndrome.

The patient was managed with structured dietary modification—small, frequent meals (5–6 per day); a low-carbohydrate, low-glycemic-index diet; avoidance of hyperosmolar beverages with meals; and a minimum 30 minute separation between solids and liquids. Nutritional counseling was provided by a registered dietitian. Pharmacologic therapy with acarbose was initiated, targeting the late dumping component by attenuating postprandial glucose excursion.

Following initiation of this multimodal regimen, the patient reported meaningful reduction in the frequency and severity of postprandial episodes. Her weight remained stable throughout the follow-up period.

DISCUSSION

This case illustrates dumping syndrome with features of both early and late dumping following ESG, with marked worsening after cholecystectomy. The occurrence of dumping-type symptoms after a procedure that does not involve gastric resection raises important mechanistic questions and suggests that functional gastric changes alone may be sufficient to produce this clinical syndrome.

A central mechanistic tension in this case is that ESG has been shown to delay, rather than accelerate, gastric emptying. Vargas et al5 demonstrated that gastric emptying half-time was significantly prolonged after ESG compared with lifestyle intervention alone (152 vs 89 minutes at 3 months), with this delay persisting at 12 months. Classical early dumping syndrome is attributed to rapid delivery of hyperosmolar contents into the small intestine, which would seem inconsistent with delayed emptying.8,12 Several hypotheses may reconcile this apparent paradox. Notably, endoscopic examination in this patient confirmed that the ESG sutures remained intact and the tubularized gastric configuration was preserved approximately 2 years after the index procedure, and its associated reduction in gastric compliance persisted well beyond the period of initial weight loss. One explanation comes from the international consensus on dumping syndrome, which recognized that a reduction in gastric volume capacity can drive dumping physiology on its own, independent of how fast the stomach empties.12 In other words, it is not just about speed of transit; a stomach that has lost its compliance and reservoir function may trigger the same cascade through entirely different means. Second, data from sleeve gastrectomy suggest that reduced gastric volume increases intragastric pressure, which may overcome pyloric resistance and produce rapid early-phase emptying of liquids despite overall delayed solid emptying.13–15 A similar dissociation between liquid and solid emptying could occur after ESG, particularly if suture configuration creates a high-pressure, low-compliance gastric tube. Finally, the hormonal changes documented after ESG including increased GLP-1 and peptide YY could contribute to a late dumping phenotype through incretin-mediated hyperinsulinemia, even in the absence of accelerated gastric emptying.

The comparison between ESG and sleeve gastrectomy warrants careful qualification. The mechanism of dumping after sleeve gastrectomy is thought to relate to reduced gastric compliance and reservoir capacity, loss of fundal accommodation, and accelerated gastric emptying with pooled analyses showing a reduction in gastric emptying half-time of approximately 29 minutes for solids after sleeve gastrectomy.15 ESG preserves the gastric wall and fundus, does not transect vagal nerve endings, and delays rather than accelerates emptying, making the pathophysiology fundamentally different. Similarly, Liu et al10 reported that dumping symptom prevalence after sleeve gastrectomy increased from 41% at 1 year to nearly 60% at approximately 7 years of follow-up. Although this raises the theoretical possibility that delayed presentations could also occur after ESG, this extrapolation remains unsubstantiated and should be interpreted with caution given the distinct anatomic and physiologic differences between the 2 procedures.

The marked worsening of this patient's symptoms following cholecystectomy is noteworthy. However, it should be acknowledged that the patient's initial post-ESG symptoms nausea, diarrhea, and vomiting overlapped temporally with the development of cholelithiasis. This limits the ability to attribute those early symptoms specifically to ESG-related dumping physiology, as symptomatic cholelithiasis alone could have accounted for part or all of the initial presentation. Van Furth et al16 reported that cholecystectomy is associated with an increased risk of dumping syndrome and postbariatric hypoglycemia after bariatric surgery, with relative risks of 1.6 in primary RYGB patients and up to 2.7 in subgroups with excess weight loss ≥70%. The likely mechanism runs through bile acid metabolism. Under normal physiology, bile is released in response to meals, a regulated, pulsatile signal. Cholecystectomy eliminates that regulation, replacing it with a continuous trickle of bile acids into the intestine regardless of food intake. That unregulated flow appears to drive sustained GLP-1 secretion, which in turn amplifies incretin-mediated insulin release and lowers the threshold for postprandial hypoglycemia. Postcholecystectomy diarrhea itself occurs in approximately 13% of patients and is attributed to bile acid-stimulated colonic secretion and motility.17 The lack of response to bile acid sequestrant therapy in this patient made bile acid diarrhea less likely as the primary etiology.

Several alternative diagnoses merit consideration. Functional dyspepsia, particularly the postprandial distress syndrome subtype, is highly prevalent after bariatric procedures—reported in up to 59% of patients after sleeve gastrectomy—and shares overlapping symptoms with dumping syndrome, including postprandial nausea, bloating, and early satiety.18 Irritable bowel syndrome was also on the differential; diarrhea-predominant symptoms are common after bariatric procedures and do not by themselves point anywhere specific. What tilted the diagnosis toward dumping syndrome was the fuller clinical picture: vasomotor features like palpitations, diaphoresis, and near-syncope are not part of irritable bowel syndrome, the symptoms locked tightly to the postprandial window with consistent onset within 30 minutes of eating, and a Sigstad score of 12 cleared the diagnostic threshold of ≥10 by a comfortable margin.8,12

We report a case of dumping syndrome following ESG, with symptoms that were markedly exacerbated by subsequent laparoscopic cholecystectomy. This case raises the possibility that ESG-related changes in gastric reservoir function may have contributed to the development of dumping syndrome in a susceptible patient, and that cholecystectomy-associated alterations in bile acid metabolism may further lower the threshold for symptomatic expression. These observations are hypothesis-generating and derived from a single case; larger prospective studies are needed to determine the true incidence of dumping syndrome following ESG, to clarify the mechanistic relationship between post-ESG anatomy and dumping physiology, and to identify procedural and patient-level risk factors. Clinicians should nonetheless consider dumping syndrome in post-ESG patients presenting with new postprandial gastrointestinal and vasomotor symptoms, particularly in those who have undergone subsequent abdominal surgery (Table 2).
