The influence of hypoxia‐mediated CEACAM6 upregulation on epithelial cell and macrophage response in the context of gastric cancer
Authors: Indrajit Poirah, Debashish Chakraborty, Pragyesh Dixit, Supriya Samal, Smaran Banerjee, Tathagata Mukherjee, Subhasis Chattopadhyay, Gautam Nath, Asima Bhattacharyya
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe hypoxic microenvironment is a key component of the gastric tumour niche. Carcinoembryonic antigen‐related cell adhesion molecule 6 (CEACAM6) is upregulated in gastric cancer and is considered a novel biomarker for the disease. However, no prior studies have elaborated on the status of CEACAM6 and its role in the hypoxic gastric cancer niche.MethodsIn this short study, we evaluated the effect of hypoxia in modulating CEACAM6 level in gastric cancer cells (GCCs) through western blotting and determined the effect of CEACAM6 upregulation on gastric cancer progression through clonogenicity, cell proliferation and migration assays. The wound‐healing ability of GCCs was downregulated by siRNA‐mediated CEACAM6 silencing. Human gastric cancer biopsy samples were examined by immunofluorescence microscopy to assess the level of a hypoxia marker HIF1α and CEACAM6. The effect of empty vector or CEACAM6‐expression on peripheral blood‐derived mononuclear cell (PBMC)‐derived macrophage polarization under normoxia or hypoxia was studied by incubating macrophages in conditioned medium collected from GCC cultures. Macrophage polarization status was observed using flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation by macrophages was evaluated using fluorescence microscopy.ResultsWe identified that hypoxia promoted CEACAM6 in GCCs, and these cells acquired increased proliferative potential and migration ability. Moreover, the cell culture supernatant from hypoxia‐exposed CEACAM6‐overexpressing cells promoted an M2‐like macrophage population and discouraged the M1 phenotype.ConclusionThis study established that hypoxia increased CEACAM6 which promoted gastric cancer progression by influencing GCC proliferation and motility as well as macrophage polarization.Keywords: CEACAM, gastric cancer, hypoxia, macrophage, ROS, tumour microenvironment

INTRODUCTION

CEACAM6 (CD66c) belongs to an immunoglobulin‐like large family of cell surface glycoproteins. 1 CEACAM6 remains tethered to the cell membrane via a glycophosphatidyl inositol (GPI) anchor, and its extracellular domains include an immunoglobulin (Ig)‐like variable N‐terminal domain and two (A1B1) C2‐type Ig domains. 1 CEACAM6 facilitates both homotypic interactions with other CEACAMs and heterotypic interactions with integrins. 2 It plays a crucial role in organizing tissue architecture and regulating signal transduction while its abnormal expression is associated with the onset of various human cancers. This protein is upregulated in several cancers and is associated with various aspects of cancer such as tumour development, progression, invasiveness, metastatic spread and drug resistance. 3 , 4 Fibronectin–integrin interactions are also modulated by CEACAM6 5 via its C‐terminal GPI anchor by homotypic and/or heterotypic binding, 6 thereby orchestrating extracellular matrix (ECM) components to influence cell–cell attachment, growth rate and signalling in cancer and immune response. 2 Overexpression of CEACAM6 in human epithelial carcinomas disrupts anoikis, a process of programmed cell death triggered by improper cell‐matrix interactions which also promotes the life span of cancer cells. 5 CEACAM6 overexpression enhances the mesenchymal nature of epithelial cells and increases the appearance of F‐actin stress fibres and a fusiform shape of cells. 7

CEACAM6 has a strong positive correlation with tumour stage, TNM classification as well as lymph node metastasis in gastric cancer. 8 Interestingly, CEACAM6 gets upregulated in colon cancer cells treated with an iron‐chelating chemical inducer of hypoxia, deferoxamine mesylate (DFO). 9 Hypoxia is a common characteristic of solid tumour microenvironment (TME), resulting from an imbalance between the supply and consumption of oxygen at the cellular level. Its involvement in gastric cancer is well documented. Hypoxia promotes gastric cancer progression as well as metastasis. It induces the expression of HIF1, the master transcription factor which regulates the expression of several hypoxia‐responsive genes as well. Hypoxia enhances the release of migration‐stimulating agents from tumour cells that cause macrophage recruitment in tumours. 10 Initially, tumour‐recruited macrophages have an M1‐like proinflammatory phenotype but tumour hypoxia exposure polarizes them to an M2‐like pro‐tumorigenic phenotype. M2 macrophages contribute towards immunosuppression, aggressive tumour growth, angiogenesis and drug resistance. Although hypoxia is a common feature inside a solid tumour, so far nothing has been reported regarding the influence of hypoxia on gastric epithelial cell CEACAM6 level or the role of CEACAM6 in modulating macrophages in the gastric cancer milieu.

Herein, we aimed to determine the effect of hypoxia in gastric cancer cell CEACAM6 regulation and the effect of hypoxia‐CEACAM6 axis in macrophage polarization. Our study involved hypoxia‐exposure experiments involving gastric cancer cells (GCCs) and analysis of CEACAM6 expression by various methods followed by human gastric cancer tissue analysis. In addition, treating human PBMC‐derived macrophages with supernatants collected from CEACAM6‐overexpressing cells revealed the macrophage‐reprogramming effects of epithelial origin CEACAM6. This study sheds some light on the still dark alleyways of epithelial cancer cell‐immune cell crosstalk in the hypoxic gastric cancer environment and raises hope for CEACAM6 and TME‐directed cancer treatments.

METHODS

Gastric epithelial cell lines, culture conditions, hypoxia exposure

AGS (ATCC, Manassas, Virginia, USA) and MKN45 (obtained from Dr. Sheila E. Crowe's lab, UVA, USA, under the material transfer agreement) were maintained in 10% heat‐inactivated FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA)‐supplemented RPMI 1640 (Himedia, Nashik, India) media under 16% O2 and 5% CO2 in a humidified CO2 incubator maintaining 37°C. Short tandem repeat (STR) profiling was performed to authenticate the cell lines. For hypoxia, GCCs were cultured at 37°C, 5% CO2 and 3% O2 in a Whitley H35 Hypoxystation (Don Whitley Scientific Ltd., West Yorkshire, UK).

Transfection, stable cell generation and supernatant collection

Transfection for stably expressing cell lines as well as transient expression was carried out using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to previous studies. 11 Stable clones were propagated after G418 (Sigma‐Aldrich, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA) selection. For suppression studies, control siRNA (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, Texas, USA) and CEACAM6 siRNA (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) were transfected into AGS cells using Lipofectamine 3000.

AGS, empty vector‐transfected and CEACAM6‐stable cells were incubated in hypoxia/normoxia for 12 h. Supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 2000 g for 5 min, and PBMC‐derived macrophages were treated with this conditioned medium for 12 h before sampling for flow cytometry.

Western blotting

Cell lysates prepared from GCCs after lysis were separated by SDS‐PAGE followed by western blotting onto PVDF membranes as described elsewhere. 12 Primary antibodies against CEACAM6 (Novus Biologicals, Colorado, USA), HIF1α (Abcam, Massachusetts, USA) and α‐tubulin (Biobharati, West Bengal, India) were used to probe the membranes followed by HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies. Chemiluminescence was detected with the SuperSignal West Femto kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and photographed using BioRad Chemidoc XRS+ (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Hercules, USA). Densitometry analysis was done with ImageLab (Bio‐Rad Laboratories).

Wound healing assay

1 × 106 empty vector‐ and CEACAM6‐expressing AGS stable cells were cultured in six‐well plates. Upon forming a confluent monolayer, straight scratches were made using a sterile pipette tip, followed by incubation at 3% O2. After 12 h, images were captured using an inverted microscope. The scratch area was measured at the start and end of the experiment, and the percentage of wound filled was calculated as a measure of cell migration. Imaging was performed using a Nikon Eclipse Ti‐U (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) imaging system, and area measurements were performed using NIS Elements Advanced Research software (Nikon).

Clonogenic assay

16.6 × 104 empty vector and CEACAM6‐expressing AGS cells were seeded in a 24‐well plate followed by treatment at 3% O2 for 12 h. Cells were trypsinized, 1000 cells were plated in each 60‐mm dish and colonies were allowed to grow for 3 weeks in an incubator. Then cells were stained with crystal violet (.5%) after fixation with 4% PFA. Plates were photographed and colonies were counted.

Cell population doubling assay

Empty vector and CEACAM6 stably‐transfected AGS cells were seeded in 24‐well plate and cultured till confluency. Cells were then subjected to 3% O2 for 12 h. After treatment, cells were trypsinized, counted and 10,000 cells were seeded in a 24‐well plate. The number of cells was counted on days 2, 3 and 4. The cumulative population doubling (cPD) level was enumerated as discussed elsewhere. 11

Human gastric biopsy collection

Biopsy specimens were collected from the gastric antral region of consenting metastatic gastric cancer patients. Healthy gastric tissue was also obtained from the adjoining area from each patient to be used as matched‐control. The Institutional Ethics Committee for Human Research, National Institute of Science Education and Research approved the tissue collection procedure that complied with the Helsinki Declaration (2013), World Medical Association.

Correlation analysis

The Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis 2 (GEPIA2) database (http://gepia2.cancer‐pku.cn/#index) provides RNA sequencing and expression data from normal and tumour samples. 13 The correlation between HIF1α and CEACAM6 was analysed using the ‘correlation analysis’ module of GEPIA2, leveraging the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) datasets for tumours and normal tissues. Pearson's correlation analyses calculated the correlation coefficient R R and p‐value (data accessed on October 30, 2024). Gene expression was normalized using TUBA1A, and scatter plots displayed the results as log2 transcripts per million (TPM).

Isolation of PBMCs from human blood, generation of macrophages

Blood drawn from consenting healthy volunteers was half diluted with PBS (Himedia). The diluted blood was layered over HiSep‐1077 (Himedia) and subjected to density gradient centrifugation for 35 min at 400 g . Next, the rings of PBMCs were collected, pelleted down, dissolved in RBC lysis buffer (Himedia) and incubated for 10 min, followed by a PBS wash. Cells were counted, and 10 × 106 cells were plated in RPMI 1640 per well in six‐well plates. Plates were washed after 1 h to remove loosely adherent cells. Fresh RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% heat‐inactivated human serum (Sigma‐Aldrich, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA) and 20 ng/mL M‐CSF (Prospec, Rheovot, Israel) was added. Media were replaced every 2 days and observed for 7–10 days until the differentiation of monocytes. Naïve macrophages were treated with 100 ng/mL LPS (Sigma‐Aldrich) and 20 ng/mL IFN‐γ (Prospec) for 48 h or 40 ng/mL IL‐4 (Prospec) for 48 h to generate M1 or M2 macrophages.

Flow cytometry and staining of macrophages

Macrophages were treated with supernatants derived from hypoxia or normoxia‐treated, empty vector or CEACAM6‐expressing AGS cells for 48 h. After treatment, macrophages were washed with PBS, collected using Zymefree (Himedia), centrifuged and the pellets were dissolved in IgG blocker (Thermo Fisher Scientific). These were incubated with CD64‐PE and CD206‐APC (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in FACS buffer (0.1% BSA, 0.05% sodium azide in PBS) for surface staining. After a wash with FACS buffer, cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min at room temperature. This was followed by permeabilization, blocking and incubation with CD68‐FITC (Thermo Fisher Scientific) antibody. Next, cells were washed and the pellets were dissolved in 1% PFA in FACS buffer and stored at 4°C, until acquisition in LSRFortessa™(BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA). All incubations took place in the dark at 4°C, unless otherwise mentioned.

ROS detection

Naïve macrophages growing on sterile glass coverslips were treated with supernatants derived from hypoxia or normoxia‐treated, empty vector or CEACAM6‐expressing AGS cells for 48 h. Control macrophages were generated as described earlier. After washing in PBS, macrophages were treated with 1 μM 2, 7‐dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFDA; Sigma‐Aldrich) for 1 h at 37°C in the dark followed by 4% PFA fixation. The nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindoledihydrochloride (DAPI; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Immunofluorescence microscopy (Nikon Eclipse TiU/Eclipse Ni‐E, Tokyo, Japan) was performed to measure ROS levels. Fluorescence intensity of ROS levels was calculated using Fiji. 14

Immunofluorescence staining of macrophage polarization markers

Naïve as well as control macrophages were generated and treated similarly as described in the previous sections. Following treatment, the cells were fixed, blocked and stained with primary antibody for M2 (Arginase1) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) phenotype. Then, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor‐conjugated secondary antibodies followed by counterstaining with DAPI. Stained macrophages were imaged using an Eclipse TiU (Nikon) fluorescence microscope. Fluorescence intensity was measured using Fiji.

Similarly, human biopsy tissue samples were immunostained for CD68 (Abcam) and iNOS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or Arginase1 to detect M1/M2 macrophages followed by DAPI counterstaining. The imaging was done in a DMi8 (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) confocal system.

Statistical analysis

Values were expressed as mean ± sem. Statistical analyses were executed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 software (GraphPad, La Jolla, California, USA). Student's t‐test or two‐way ANOVA was performed to determine statistical significance. Post hoc analysis was conducted using Tukey's test.

RESULTS

Hypoxia upregulates CEACAM6 in GCCs

To determine the effect of hypoxia on CEACAM6 protein levels, non‐metastatic (AGS), as well as metastatic (MKN45) cells were exposed to 3% O2 or left under normoxia for 12 h at 37°C. A representative western blotting result (n = 3) showed that when the HIF1α protein level (a marker of hypoxia induction) was significantly higher in AGS and MKN45 cells exposed to hypoxia than the corresponding control, CEACAM6 levels also increased significantly in both GCCs (Figure 1A). Indeed, a correlation analysis using the GEPIA2.0 web server also established a positive correlation between HIF1α and CEACAM6 (Figure S1A). Epifluorescence microscopy of human metastatic GC biopsy tissue (n = 3) paired with matched control tissues also revealed noticeably elevated status of HIF1α and CEACAM6 in GC tissues when compared with paired controls (Figure 1B). To achieve consistency with the CEACAM6 upregulation required for our future experiments, we generated CEACAM6‐overexpressing AGS cell lines. On assessment, CEACAM6‐overexpressed cells showed significantly enhanced CEACAM6 protein levels when compared with empty vector‐transfected cells (Figure 1C).

Bar graphs showed a significant increase in CEACAM6 levels in AGS and MKN45. (B) A representative (n = 3) immunofluorescence micrograph of human metastatic gastric cancer biopsy tissue sample showing the status of HIF1α (green) and CEACAM6 (red). Statistical significance was determined using the Student's t‐test (n = 3). *p < .05; ***p < .001.

CEACAM6 increases proliferation and migration abilities of GCCs

CEACAM6 has cancer‐inducing properties. This cell adhesion protein is upregulated in many cancers and is associated with tumorigenesis, invasiveness, metastasis or therapy resistance. 3 , 4 Since we are aware that gastric cancer is promoted in hypoxia, 15 , 16 , 17 next we wanted to assess the effect of elevated CEACAM6 on the proliferative potential of GCCs. Colony formation assay and cumulative population doubling assays using empty vector‐expressing and CEACAM6‐expressing stable AGS cells exposed to normoxia or hypoxia confirmed that the clonogenicity and proliferative potential of CEACAM6‐expressing stable AGS cells were significantly high when exposed to hypoxia (Figure 2A,B, respectively). Wound‐healing assays showed that there was significantly more wound healing in CEACAM6‐stable AGS cells left in hypoxia (Figure 2C). Contrarily, hypoxia‐induced cell migration was significantly less in cells transfected with CEACAM6 siRNA as compared with control siRNA‐transfected cells (Figure 2D).

CEACAM6 promotes cell proliferation and migration of GCCs. (A) Clonogenicity assay showed a significant increase in colony formation in the presence of hypoxia and CEACAM6. (B) Bar graphs from the cumulative population doubling assay indicated significantly increased cellular proliferation in CEACAM6 stably‐transfected and hypoxia‐treated cells. (C) CEACAM6 stably‐transfected cells showed significantly increased wound healing in the presence of hypoxia and CEACAM6. (D) AGS cells transfected with CEACAM6 siRNA displayed significantly reduced hypoxia‐driven cell migration when compared to control siRNA‐transfected cells. Accompanying western blot showing the effect of siRNA‐mediated CEACAM6 suppression. Statistical significance was determined using a two‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc analysis (n = 3). *p < .05; **p < .01; ***p < .001; ****p < .0001. H, hypoxia; N, normoxia.

CEACAM6 modulates macrophage polarization in gastric cancer

PBMCs, as well as tissue‐resident macrophages, are fundamentally important in the tumour microenvironment as they can play both protumorigenic and antitumorigenic roles and can very well determine the fate of cancer and disease prognosis. 18 We wanted to first find out the status of macrophages and CEACAM6 in GC. Bioinformatic analysis using TIMER2.0 showed that a combination of high CEACAM6 expression and high macrophage infiltration had the lowest cumulative survival compared to high CEACAM6 – low macrophage, low CEACAM6 – high macrophage and low CEACAM6 – low macrophage when followed up till 30 months (Figure 3A). Although, between 30 and 60 months, low gene expression and high macrophage infiltration had even lower survival, high CEACAM6 and macrophage infiltration still had lower overall survival compared to the rest. Next, we examined human metastatic gastric cancer biopsy tissues for macrophage infiltration and found a significantly higher number of arginase 1+CD68+ tumour‐associated macrophages (TAMs) in metastatic tissues indicating increased accumulation of M2‐type macrophages (Figure 3B, Figure S1B). iNOS+CD68+ M1‐type macrophage population was noticeably lower than in paired control tissues. Furthermore, to understand the paracrine effect of CEACAM6‐upregulated GCCs on macrophages under normoxia and hypoxia, we treated naïve macrophages with empty vector or CEACAM6‐expressing hypoxic or normoxic GCC supernatants and analysed for macrophage polarization markers. Flow cytometry data also showed that macrophages incubated with normoxic CEACAM6 stable cells had the maximal M1 (CD64+) population but surprisingly, M1 population was found to be the lowest in macrophages when exposed to supernatants from hypoxia‐exposed CEACAM6 stable cells indicating towards differential paracrine modulation of macrophages by CEACAM6 stable cells under normoxia and hypoxia (Figure 3C). Further, flow cytometry analysis revealed that although hypoxia‐mediated increase in the M2 (CD206+) population was noticed in macrophages exposed to empty vector supernatant, it was not the case with macrophages exposed to CEACAM6 stable GCC supernatant where normoxic cell supernatant resulted in slightly higher CD206 than hypoxic supernatant.

Gastric cancer cell‐macrophage crosstalk alters macrophage polarization status via CEACAM6. (A) Immune association analysis from TIMER2.0 showed that in STAD high CEACAM6 and macrophage infiltration caused lower cumulative survival, compared to lower CEACAM6 expression and low macrophage infiltration. (B) Representative (n = 3) immunofluorescence micrographs showed M2 macrophage infiltration in metastatic gastric biopsy tissue. (C) Representative (n = 3) flow cytometry analysis of PBMC‐derived macrophages treated with supernatants from normoxic or hypoxic CEACAM6 or empty vector‐overexpressing epithelial cells showed a downregulation of the M1(CD68+CD64+) phenotype in the presence of hypoxia and CEACAM6. H, hypoxia; N, normoxia.

Conditioned medium collected from CEACAM6 stable GCCs upregulates arginase1 and downregulates ROS generation in macrophages

While CD206 is associated with M2 functions, it may not regulate all aspects of M2 activation, 19 and therefore, we were interested in analysing a marker of M2 activation such as, arginase1. A reduction in ROS is directly correlated with arginase1 production. Arginase1 produces anti‐inflammatory cytokines which block nitric oxide synthesis. 20 Hence, we analysed treated macrophages with immunofluorescence imaging and found that treatment with hypoxia‐exposed CEACAM6‐stable GCC supernatant significantly increased arginase1 level in macrophages as compared to other treatment groups (Figure 4A).

(B) Macrophages treated with epithelial cell supernatants showed a significant lowering of hypoxia‐mediated ROS generation in CEACAM6‐overexpressed cells (n = 3). Nuclei were DAPI‐stained (blue). Objective used = 60X, scale bar = 25 μm. Statistical significance was determined using a paired t‐test for the AGS experiments and a two‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc analysis for Emp. vec. and CEACAM6 experiments (n = 3). *p < .05; **p < .01; ***p < .001. Since M1 polarization in macrophages is closely associated with ROS generation, 20 we wanted to validate the decrease in the M1 (CD64) marker with ROS production by macrophages treated with epithelial cell supernatants under hypoxia and normoxia. We found that although hypoxia‐treated and empty vector‐transfected epithelial supernatants led to an increase in ROS produced by macrophages, hypoxia‐exposed CEACAM6‐expressing cell supernatant‐treated macrophages showed a reduction in ROS levels (Figure 4B).

DISCUSSION

CEACAM6 is upregulated in gastric cancer and is considered an important marker for the disease. It is associated with gastric cancer progression, lymph node metastasis and tumour recurrence as well. This study finds a strong association of CEACAM6 upregulation with hypoxic GCCs and identifies that hypoxia‐induced upregulation of cell proliferation/invasiveness is mediated by CEACAM6. Soluble factors released by CEACAM6‐overexpressing hypoxic GCCs alter macrophage polarization resulting in M1 phenotype suppression and an increase in the M2 population. These findings provide novel insights towards the role of CEACAM6 in the hypoxic tumour niche and highlight its importance for therapeutic approaches.

Inside solid tumours, uncontrolled cell proliferation often outstrips oxygen supply from blood vessels. Hypoxia induces a multitude of responses, including altered gene expression suppressing apoptosis while promoting autophagy, stimulation of invasion/migration, enhanced angiogenesis, and reprograms cellular metabolism. Hypoxia also contributes to genomic instability through increased ROS and impaired DNA repair. 21 Moreover, it fosters tumour aggressiveness, metabolic heterogeneity and negatively impacts the immune system contributing to tumour resistance as well as recurrence.

The importance of hypoxia on gastric cancer progression and metastasis has been reported by our group. 16 , 17 , 22 Although no study has yet been reported in the context of physiological hypoxia, diet‐induced hypoxia‐response element (HRE) demethylation enhances CEACAM6 expression in intestinal epithelial cells. 23 Our study depicting hypoxia‐induced CEACAM6 upregulation in GCCs is unique since it places CEACAM6, known for its tumorigenic roles in other cancers, as a molecule of interest in the case of hypoxia‐driven gastric cancer progression. Hypoxia‐mediated increase in cell population doubling as well as migration abilities are significantly increased by CEACAM6 upregulation while CEACAM6 siRNA significantly stunts GCC migration. Although we know that CEACAM6 is significantly increased in acid‐resistant colorectal cancer cells with further increase noticed in hypoxia and contributes to the growth of colorectal cancer cells, 24 the impact of CEACAM6 in hypoxic gastric cancer TME is completely unknown. Some prominent research highlights on the effect of hypoxia on other CEACAMs in various cancers. CEACAM1 promotes hypoxia‐mediated non‐small cell lung cancer progression and shows angiogenic effects. 25 Although the cancer‐inducing effect of CEACAM1 in hypoxia is limiting, several evidences support the involvement of CEACAM5 in hypoxia‐associated cancer progression and is considered an important diagnostic or therapeutic target. HIF1α promotes CEACAM5 expression in breast and colorectal cancer cells, 26 and the latter is associated with poor prognosis. 6 , 27 CEACAM5 is also considered an important biomarker for gastric cancer. 28 Although hypoxia‐induced CEACAM1 and CEACAM5 upregulation is not known in case of gastric cancer cells, the possibility of homotypic and heterotypic interactions of CEACAM6 with other CEACAMs and integrins 6 hints towards probable complex ramifications of CEACAM6‐mediated signalling events in gastric cancer which warrant elaborate studies.

The gastric cancer niche includes non‐epithelial cells and along with hypoxia and soluble factors these cells form important components of the TME. Macrophages are the most abundant immune cells in the gastric cancer TME. PBMCs, upon being attracted by the cytokines and chemokines released by tumour cells or mesenchymal stromal cells, are recruited in the tumour site to create a diverse range of TAMs, contributing to the formation of the TME. 29 Piao et al. identified that a positive feedback loop between the gastric cancer cells and the M2‐type alternatively activated TAMs exacerbates gastric cancer malignancy. 30 We find that CEACAM6 imparts proliferative effects on gastric cancer cells while CEACAM6‐expressed GCCs alter macrophage phenotype. Incubation of PBMC‐derived macrophages with hypoxic CEACAM6‐overexporessed gastric epithelial cancer cell‐derived supernatants decreased M1 phenotype and promoted cancer‐promoting M2 phenotype. Our future research will focus to identify specific factors that trigger reprogramming of macrophage population with the hope of creating novel targets for therapies focused on the paracrine loop between tumour cells and macrophages. We also emphasize at this point that in addition to molecular targets, therapeutic approaches to cancer should also focus on the cancer microenvironment.

Collectively, this study shows that CEACAM6 not only promotes hypoxia‐mediated cell proliferation but also changes macrophage dynamics under hypoxia. Furthermore, CEACAM6 and TME emerge as promising targets in the treatment of gastric cancer.
