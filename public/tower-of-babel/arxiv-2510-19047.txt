Global time-frequency search for stellar-mass binary black holes in LISA
Diganta Bandopadhyay §, Christian E. A. Chapman-Bird §, Alberto Vecchio

Abstract
We present a complete pipeline for detecting and characterising gravitational waves (GWs) produced by the inspiral of stellar-mass binary black holes in data from the Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA). The analysis framework relies on an efficient time-frequency implementation of an adaptive semi-coherent detection statistic, which we show to be robust against non-stationary noise and the presence of gaps of varying duration and cadence. The search is able to detect signals down to a coherent signal-to-noise ratio ≈ 11 \approx 11 over the full parameter space of black holes with spins aligned to the orbital angular momentum and orbital eccentricity ≤ 0.01 \leq 0.01 when deployed on the 2-year-long LISA Data Challenge Yorsh . The search can be run within a day using ≈ 40 \approx 40 GPUs. The techniques presented here have wider applications in GW astronomy, in particular the search for extreme-mass-ratio inspirals in LISA data.

Methods —
Our goal is to detect smBBHs over multiple years of LISA observations.
The data set consists of the three pseudo-orthogonal time-delay interferometry (TDI) time series represented as
d_{\alpha}(t)=n_{\alpha}(t)+y_{\alpha}(t)
, where
\alpha
labels the second-generation TDI observables
A
,
E
and
T

Prince et al. 2002
.
Here
n_{\alpha}(t)
and
y_{\alpha}(t)
are the total noise and GW signal contributions to the data stream, respectively.
In this letter, we consider GWs from mildly eccentric (
i.e.
with eccentricity
e\lesssim 0.01
) smBBHs for which the two independent polarisations
h_{+,\times}(t)
are well-modelled by the dominant
\ell=|m|=2
mode. We will denote with
\theta
the set of parameters that describe a GW signal at the LISA output. The TDI observable
y_{\alpha}(t)
produced by
h_{+,\times}(t)

can be written as
y_{\alpha}(t)=\mathfrak{T}_{\alpha}[t,f,L(t)]h(t)
, where
\mathfrak{T}_{\alpha}[t,f,L(t)]
is an operator that projects
h=h_{+}-ih_{\times}
onto the laser paths connecting the movable optical sub-assemblies of the LISA constellation (the LISA “arms”) with the appropriate time delays.
The complexity of this response function, which depends on time,
t
, GW frequency,
f
, and the slowly-varying LISA constellation arm-lengths,
L\approx 8.3\,\mathrm{s}
, further complicates and increases the computational costs of constructing TDI variables, particularly in the frequency band relevant to smBBHs (and EMRIs) where the induced phase shifts, which scale as
\pi Lf\approx 0.33\,(L/8.3\,\mathrm{sec})\,(f/10\,\mathrm{mHz})
, cannot be ignored.

Our approach exploits a hierarchy of timescales,
T_{\mathrm{obs}}\geq T_{\mathrm{seg}}\geq\Delta T
, that we introduce here:
(i)
T_{\mathrm{obs}}
is the
total
duration of the data set that for LISA is several years (the nominal mission lifetime is
4.5\,\mathrm{yr}
with possible extension to
10\,\mathrm{yr}

Colpi et al. 2024
); we set the (arbitrary) reference time
t_{0}=0
at the start of observation, so that the time of coalescence of a smBBH,
t_{c}
, corresponds to the their remaining lifetime; (ii)
T_{\mathrm{seg}}
is the duration of the non-overlapping
time segments
into which we divide the data set, and defines the coherence length of our analysis; the total number of segments (which varies as the search progresses) is
N_{\mathrm{seg}}\equiv\tau/T_{\mathrm{seg}}=100\,(\tau/10^{8}\,\mathrm{s})\,(10^{6}\,\mathrm{s}/T_{\mathrm{seg}})
for
\tau=\mathrm{min}(T_{\mathrm{obs}},t_{c})
; (iii)
\Delta T
is the length of the data
tranche
over which an efficient time-frequency approximation of the inner product between the data and the signal’s templates is computed, and is set in advance ensuring that second-order contributions to the Taylor expansion of the signal phase
\approx 0.2\,\left(\mathcal{M}_{c}/{30\,M_{\odot}}\right)^{10/3}\,\left({f}/{10\,\mathrm{mHz}}\right)^{19/3}\,\left({\Delta T}/{10^{4}\,\mathrm{sec}}\right)^{3}
can be neglected; here
\mathcal{M}_{c}
is the binary’s chirp mass and the total number of tranches in a segment is
M_{\mathrm{tr}}\equiv T_{\mathrm{seg}}/\Delta T
.

In this work, we employ the semi-coherent statistic introduced in
Bandopadhyay and Moore 2023
;
Bandopadhyay and Moore 2024
;
Chua et al. 2017
.
Segmenting the data such that the
n-
th segment covers the time interval
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
, where
t_{n}=n\,T_{\mathrm{seg}}
, the detection statistic is:

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N_{\mathrm{seg}}-1}\frac{\left[\max_{\phi_{n}}\langle d_{n}\mid y_{n}(\mathbf{\xi})e^{i\phi_{n}}\rangle\right]^{2}}{\langle y_{n}(\mathbf{\xi})\mid y_{n}(\mathbf{\xi})\rangle}\,,

(1)

where we have defined the inner-product over the segment (for all TDI channels) as:

\left\langle d_{n}\mid y_{n}e^{i\phi_{n}}\right\rangle=4\,\mathrm{Re}\left\{e^{i\phi_{n}}\sum\limits_{\alpha=1}^{3}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N_{f}-1}\frac{\tilde{d}_{\alpha,n}^{*}[k]\,\tilde{y}_{\alpha,n}[k]}{S_{\alpha,n}[k]}\Delta f\right\}\,,

(2)

and analogously for
\langle y_{n}\mid y_{n}\rangle
. Here
\tilde{x}
denotes the Fourier transform of a time series
x
,
\Delta f=1/T_{\mathrm{seg}}
,
N_{f}=T_{\mathrm{seg}}/\Delta t
,
\Delta t
is the sampling cadence, and the index
k
corresponds to the discrete frequency
f_{k}=k\Delta f
.
S_{\alpha,n}
is the one-sided noise power spectral density (PSD) in the segment.

In the definition of the detection statistic we have explicitly split the vector of unknown parameters that characterise a signal into
\theta=\{\xi,\phi\}
, where
\xi
is the vector of parameters
excluding
a constant phase offset
\phi
. Note that we are also maximizing over an amplitude parameter implicitly within each segment in the definition of
Eq.

1
. For a given
N_{\mathrm{seg}}
, the unknown parameters are
(\xi,\{\phi_{n},A_{n};n=1,\dots,N_{\mathrm{seg}}\})
, and therefore the effective dimensionality of the parameter space is
\dim(\xi)+2N_{\mathrm{seg}}
, with maximisation over
2N_{\mathrm{seg}}
parameters.
As the search progresses,
N_{\mathrm{seg}}
is gradually reduced; the final stage,
N_{\mathrm{seg}}=1
, is fully coherent and the search statistic is
\Upsilon_{1}\equiv\mathrm{max}_{\phi}(\rho^{2})
, where
\rho
is the matched filter SNR
Bandopadhyay and Moore 2024
.

The key to our search is efficiently computing
Eq.

2
, which we achieve with a hardware-accelerated framework in the time-frequency domain under the assumption of a slowly-evolving GW frequency.
We divide each segment in tranches, so that the
m-
th tranche covers the time interval
[t_{nm},t_{n\,m+1}]
with
t_{nm}=nT_{\mathrm{seg}}+m\Delta T
.
We can now write the inner product Eq. (
2
) as:

\displaystyle\left\langle d_{n}\mid y_{n}e^{i\phi_{n}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\,\mathrm{Re}\left\{e^{i\phi_{n}}\sum\limits_{\alpha=1}^{3}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{M_{\mathrm{tr}}-1}\mathcal{T}_{\alpha,nm}\right.

(3)

\displaystyle\times\left.\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N_{\mathcal{F}}}\frac{\tilde{d}_{\alpha,nm}^{*}[k]\,\tilde{h}_{\alpha,nm}[k]}{S_{\alpha,nm}[k]}\delta f\right\}\,,

with
\delta f=1/\Delta T
. Equation
3
is significantly more efficient to compute than Eq. (
2
) because (i) the sum over frequency can be truncated to
N_{\mathcal{F}}\ll\Delta T/\Delta t
bins, centred on
f_{nm}\equiv f(t_{nm})
(see discussion in Supplemental Material); (ii) the frequency-domain waveform
\tilde{h}_{nm}(f)
is expressed analytically in terms of Fresnel integrals; (iii) the TDI transfer functions
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha,nm}\equiv\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(f_{nm})
are complex multiplicative factors, computed once per tranche for all frequency bins.
Following Ref.
Bandopadhyay and Moore 2025
we use TDI-1.5 variables which account for the rigid rotation of a constant-arm-length constellation
Cornish and Hellings 2003
;
Tinto and Dhurandhar 2021
(see equivalence between TDI-1.5 and TDI-2 in Appendix. E of Ref.
Valencia and Husa 2025
).
As shown in
Table

1
, under assumptions suitable for smBBHs (and GPU acceleration), this approach is orders of magnitude more efficient than computation of
Eq.

2
in the frequency domain, rendering a global smBBH search feasible.

We apply this search pipeline to the most recent LDC release,

.
This is a

The instrumental noise is Gaussian and stationary, with an additional cyclo-stationary foreground due to a population of

.
The data also include overlapping signals from
8
smBBHs with
\mathcal{M}_{c}\approx 7-40\,M_{\odot}
, mass ratio

, effective spin parameter
Schmidt et al. 2012

|\chi_{\mathrm{eff}}|\approx 0-0.5
,

, where

, (corresponding to

t_{c}\approx 1.5-300\,\mathrm{yr}
) generated using the

In the search, we employ the waveform approximant

;

The search parameter vector on which the detection statistic, Eq. (

In constructing the time-frequency representation of

, we set a tranche duration of

for

Shorter tranches are used for higher

Each tranche is Tukey-windowed with a shape parameter of

Application: data with gaps —
The time-frequency framework offers significant flexibility when considering signals of duration longer than the typical time-scales over which noise is stationary and data collection suffers interruptions.
For LISA, galactic confusion noise (which is expected to dominate instrumental noise below
\approx 3\,\mathrm{mHz}
) is cyclostationary and changes on a time-scale of
6
months, and the instrumental noise itself is expected to vary on timescales
\ll T_{\mathrm{obs}}
.
Moreover, gaps (either due to scheduled instrument adjustments or unforeseen data dropouts or contaminations
Baghi et al. 2022
) will be present; the LISA science data duty cycle requirement is
>82\%

Colpi et al. 2024
.
Viable strategies for LISA analysis must be robust to such features of the data stream.

To examine the performance of our approach under such conditions, we zero-out random portions of the
Yorsh
data stream to simulate the presence of gaps and produce an
85\%
duty-cycle
Yorsh
dataset.
Gaps are produced at random times with 6 hour durations, accumulating a total data dropout of
15\%
over the observation period.
The spectrogram of the resulting data is shown in the left panel of
Fig.

1
.
We then re-process the data with the same pipeline, with the only change being that tranches that overlap with data gaps are not included in the computation of the time-frequency inner-product [Eq. (
3
)], and therefore do not contribute to the detection statistic [
Eq.

1
].
For further flexibility, our framework permits a time-frequency PSD that varies between
tranches
, which can be used to model noise spectra which are locally stationary
Digman and Cornish 2023
;
Digman and Cornish 2022
; we do not demonstrate this capability in this work, but have verified this performs as expected for our implementation.

The end-to-end data processing remains efficient and is consistent with the results shown in
Table

.

data (in close agreement with the loss in
\rho^{2}
expected for a perfectly monochromatic signal) verifies that our approach is capable of analysing data containing frequent gaps without incurring additional sensitivity losses or sacrificing computational performance.

Outlook —
The time-frequency GPU-accelerated search approach for long-duration, broad-band GW signals that we have successfully demonstrated here provides a solution to the long-standing problem of searching globally for smBBHs in LISA.
In the most naïve version in which each tile is assigned to a GPU, it meets the requirement of completing a global search every few weeks as the mission progresses – the
\mathrm{SNR}
accumulates slowly, approximately
\propto T_{\mathrm{obs}}^{1/2}
– while achieving
\lesssim 1\,\mathrm{hr}
latency in specific tiles, should detection candidates, and the need for alerts, start to emerge.
Data processing in
\approx\mathrm{hr}-
long time segments seamlessly accounts for data-gaps and the complexity of noise non-stationarities over the (much longer) observation timescale
Burke et al. 2025a
;
Burke et al. 2025b
.

There are several extensions and improvements to the approach presented here that we intend to pursue in the future. While in this study analytic orbits are used to model the spacecraft
Cornish and Rubbo 2003
, this can be generalised, at no additional computational costs to numerical and fully generic orbits with varying arm-length and point-ahead corrections
Martens and Joffre 2021
;
Katz et al. 2022
. A more rigorous metric-based approach to the
(\mathcal{M}_{c},f_{\mathrm{in}})
tiling would ensure that the ratio between the search volume vs the posterior volume is constant across the parameter space, solving the issue that we have encountered with source 6 in
Yorsh

Owen 1996
;
Sakon et al. 2024
.
We plan to further expand the search parameter space to cover black holes with arbitrary spin orientation (which induces precession of the orbital plane and modulations of the emitted GWs), orbits with large eccentricity and radiation’s multiple modes, all of which will be particularly pertinent when tackling the even more challenging problem of EMRI identification.

More broadly, our approach is applicable to a wide variety of GW searches, both for LISA and the current ground-based detector network, providing a viable path to expanding the parameter space that can be covered.

Time-frequency search statistic —
Here we detail our procedure for constructing the detection statistic, Eq. (
1
), in the time-frequency domain by evaluating Eq. (
3
) instead of the more computationally expensive Eq. (
2
), and its massively parallel implementation on GPU hardware which further reduces the wall-time of the search.

Given two real time series
a(t)
and
b(t)
, with Fourier transform
\tilde{a}(f)
and
\tilde{b}(f)
, respectively, we define the usual noise-weighted inner-product

\langle a\mid b\rangle=4\,\mathrm{Re}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{a}^{*}(f)\,\tilde{b}(f)}{S(f)}df\,,

(4)

where
S(f)
is the one-sided noise power spectral density (PSD). We also introduce

(a\mid b)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{a}^{*}(f)\,\tilde{b}(f)}{S(f)}df\,,

(5)

which, with slight abuse of terminology, we call the complex inner product, such that
\langle a|b\rangle=4\mathrm{Re}\left\{(a|b)\right\}
.

We make the standard assumption that the three TDI channels, labelled by
\alpha
, are pseudo noise-orthogonal
Tinto and Dhurandhar 2021
(although this can be relaxed at minor additional cost).
From Eq. (
4
), the coherent inner product between the data,
d=\{d_{\alpha}\}
, and a GW signal template characterised by parameters
{\mathbf{\theta}}
,
y({\mathbf{\theta}})=\{y_{\alpha}({\mathbf{\theta}})\}
, is

\langle d\mid y(\mathbf{\theta})\rangle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}\langle d_{\alpha}\mid y_{\alpha}(\mathbf{\theta})\rangle\\
=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}4\,\real\left\{\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{d}_{\alpha}^{*}(f)\,\tilde{y}_{\alpha}(\theta;f)}{S_{\alpha}(f)}df\right\}\,,

(6)

where the noise weighting function for
\langle d_{\alpha}\mid y_{\alpha}(\mathbf{\theta})\rangle
is the one-sided noise PSD in the relevant TDI channel,
S_{\alpha}(f)
.

As we have described in the main text, one of the parameters that define the signal in the data is an overall constant phase,
\phi
, at some (arbitrary) reference time. Writing
\theta=\{\xi,\phi\}
, such that
\xi
is the vector of parameters
excluding
an overall constant phase, and substituting Eqs.(
4
) and (
5
) into Eq. (
6
), one can maximise the inner-product over this phase as

\displaystyle{\cal O}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\max_{\phi}\langle d\mid y(\mathbf{\xi})e^{i\phi}\rangle

\displaystyle=\max_{\phi}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}\langle d_{\alpha}\mid y_{\alpha}(\mathbf{\xi})e^{i\phi}\rangle

\displaystyle=4\left|\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}(d_{\alpha}\mid y_{\alpha}(\mathbf{\xi}))\right|.

(7)

The quantity
{\cal O}(\xi)
is also known as the
overlap
of the template with the data, and the last line in Eq. (
7
) shows that phase maximisation can be performed analytically and efficiently in the Fourier domain.

The detection statistic, Eq. (
1
), is the sum of (suitably normalised) overlaps over the time segments into which we partition the data set.
We divide the data set of duration
T_{\mathrm{obs}}
in non-overlapping time segments of length
T_{\mathrm{seg}}
. Each segment spans the time interval
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
, where
t_{n}=n\,T_{\mathrm{seg}}
and
n=0,\dots,N_{\mathrm{seg}}
, where the total number of segments is
N_{\mathrm{seg}}=T_{\mathrm{obs}}/T_{\mathrm{seg}}
. Using Eq. (
7
), the detection statistic (
1
) can be written as

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N_{\mathrm{seg}}-1}\frac{{\cal O}_{n}^{2}(\xi)}{\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}\langle y_{\alpha,n}(\mathbf{\xi})\mid y_{\alpha,n}(\mathbf{\xi})\rangle}\,,

(8)

where we have defined the overlap over the segment as

\displaystyle{\cal O}_{n}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\max_{\phi_{n}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}\langle d_{\alpha,n}\mid y_{\alpha,n}(\mathbf{\xi})e^{i\phi_{n}}\rangle\,,

\displaystyle=4\left|\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}(d_{\alpha,n}\mid y_{\alpha,n}(\mathbf{\xi}))\right|\,.

(9)

In the previous equation,
\langle d_{\alpha,n}\mid y_{\alpha,n}(\mathbf{\xi})e^{i\phi_{n}}\rangle
is given by Eq. (
2
), and in analogy with
Eq.

5
we have defined the segment-level complex inner product

\displaystyle\left(d_{\alpha,n}\mid y_{\alpha,n}(\xi)\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{d}_{\alpha,n}^{*}(f)\,\tilde{y}_{\alpha,n}(\xi;f)}{S_{\alpha,n}(f)}df\,,

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N_{f}-1}\frac{\tilde{d}_{\alpha,n}^{*}[k]\,\tilde{y}_{\alpha,n}(\xi)[k]}{S_{\alpha,n}[k]}\Delta f\,,

(10)

to emphasise the process by which one maximises over the
N_{\mathrm{seg}}
phase parameters.
In Eqs. (
8
), (
9
) and (
10
) we have defined a time-series
x_{\alpha,n}
as the output of the TDI channel
\alpha
over the time segment
n
. The data are discretely sampled at times
t_{j}=j\,\Delta t
, where
\Delta t
is the sampling cadence. We denote the discrete time series as
x_{\alpha,n}[j]=x(n\,T_{\mathrm{seg}}+j\Delta t)
. The discrete Fourier components of
x_{\alpha,n}
at frequency
f_{k}=k\Delta f
are denoted by
\tilde{x}_{\alpha,n}[k]
, where
\Delta f=1/T_{\mathrm{seg}}
and
N_{f}=T_{\mathrm{seg}}/\Delta t
;
S_{\alpha,n}[k]
is the one-sided noise spectral density of the data at frequency
f_{k}
.

It is essential to compute Eq. (
10
) efficiently, which we do by exploiting the slow phase evolution of GWs from smBBHs over
T_{\mathrm{obs}}
.
Assuming one waveform mode is dominant, the complex GW strain can be written as

h(t)=\mathcal{A}(t)e^{i\varphi(t)}.

(11)

We divide each segment into tranches of duration
\Delta T
.
For the
n-
th segment, the
m-
th tranche covers the time interval
[t_{nm},t_{n\,m+1}]
, where
t_{nm}=nT_{\mathrm{seg}}+m\Delta T
.
Within each tranche, we Taylor-expand the GW phase to second order:

\displaystyle\varphi(t)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{nm}+2\pi f_{nm}(t-t_{nm})+\pi\dot{f}_{nm}(t-t_{nm})^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+{\cal O}((t-t_{nm})^{3})\,,

(12)

where

\displaystyle\varphi_{nm}

\displaystyle\equiv\varphi(t_{nm})\,,

(13)

\displaystyle f_{nm}

\displaystyle\equiv f(t_{nm})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\frac{d\varphi}{dt}\right]_{t=t_{nm}}\,,

(14)

and

\dot{f}_{nm}\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\frac{d^{2}\varphi}{d^{2}t}\right]_{t=t_{nm}}\,.

(15)

The Fourier transform of
Eq.

11
over the tranche can now be approximated as

\tilde{h}_{nm}(f)=\frac{\mathcal{A}_{nm}}{\sqrt{2\dot{f}_{nm}}}e^{i(\varphi_{nm}-\pi\dot{f}_{nm}\zeta_{nm}^{2}(f))}\mathcal{F}_{nm}(f)\,,

(16)

where

\displaystyle\zeta_{nm}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{nm}-f}{\dot{f}_{nm}}\,,

(17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{nm}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(t_{nm})\,.

(18)

In Eq. (
16
) we have defined the kernel function

\mathcal{F}_{nm}(f)=\Delta C_{nm}(f)+i\Delta S_{nm}(f)\,,

(19)

with

\displaystyle\Delta C_{nm}(f)

\displaystyle=C[v_{nm}(t_{n\,m+1},f)]-C[v_{nm}(t_{nm},f)]\,,

(20)

\displaystyle\Delta S_{nm}(f)

\displaystyle=S[v_{nm}(t_{n\,m+1},f)]-S[v_{nm}(t_{nm},f)],

(21)

\displaystyle v_{nm}(t,f)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\dot{f}_{nm}}\,\left[(t-t_{nm})+\zeta_{nm}(f)\right]\,,

(22)

and
S(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\sin(\pi x^2/2)dx
and
C(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\cos(\pi x^2/2)dx
are the Fresnel integrals
Abramowitz and Stegun 1964
.

A crucial aspect for the efficient computation of
Eq.

10
is the behaviour of the kernel function (
19
) as a function of frequency: for the values of
\dot{f}_{nm}
typical of smBBHs in the relevant frequency band
\sim 1-100\,\mathrm{mHz}
,
Eq.

19
(and hence
Eq.

16
) peak sharply around
f_{nm}
and fall off rapidly in frequency.
Additionally, as
v_{nm}(t,f)\sim\delta f/\sqrt{\dot{f}}
is typically large (
\sqrt{\dot{f}}\ll\delta f
for much of the inspiral), we specifically employ asymptotic forms of the Fresnel integrals to further reduce computational costs
DLMF
.

Finally, we write the TDI observable as

y_{\alpha,nm}(f)=\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(f_{nm})\,\tilde{h}_{nm}(f).

(23)

where
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
are TDI transfer functions, and we have explicitly re-introduced the index
\alpha
to label the different TDI variables. The transfer functions for the single detector link observables can be found in Eqs. (62) and (63) in Ref.
Marsat and Baker 2018
, which are used in turn to construct the TDI variables. We use TDI-
1.5
which models a rigid, rotating constellation and point-ahead corrections are ignored. Note that the transfer function is only computed for the ‘central’ frequency
f_{nm}
within each tranche and applied to the whole waveform
\tilde{h}(f)
, making use of the relative compactness of
\tilde{h}(f)
compared to variations in
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(f)
.

Equation

10
can now be computed by combining the analogous quantities computed at the tranche level:

(d_{\alpha,n}|y_{\alpha,n}(\xi))=\sum_{m=0}^{M_{\mathrm{tr}}-1}(d_{\alpha,nm}|y_{\alpha,nm}(\xi)))\,,

(24)

where

(d_{\alpha,nm}|y_{\alpha,nm}(\xi))=\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(f_{nm};\xi)\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N_{\mathcal{F}}}\frac{\tilde{d}_{\alpha,nm}^{*}[k]\,\tilde{h}_{\alpha,nm}(\xi)[k]}{S_{\alpha,nm}[k]}\delta f\,,

(25)

and
M_{\mathrm{tr}}=T_{\mathrm{seg}}/\Delta T
is the number of tranches in each segment,
\delta f=1/\Delta T
, and
N_{\mathcal{F}}\neq N_{f}
is the number of frequency components over which the summation is performed. Analogously, Eq. (
3
) can be written in the form:

\displaystyle\left\langle d_{n}\mid y_{n}(\xi)e^{i\phi_{n}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\,\mathrm{Re}\left\{e^{i\phi_{n}}\sum\limits_{\alpha=1}^{3}(d_{\alpha,n}|y_{\alpha,n}(\xi))\right\}

(26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\,\mathrm{Re}\left\{e^{i\phi_{n}}\right.

\displaystyle\times\left.\sum\limits_{\alpha=1}^{3}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{M_{\mathrm{tr}}-1}(d_{\alpha,nm}|y_{\alpha,nm}(\xi))\right\}\,.

Equation

24
is significantly more efficient to compute than
Eq.

10
for several reasons.
The evaluation of
Eq.

23
does not require a (relatively) expensive numerical Fourier transform, but it is semi-analytical (via
Eq.

16
) and the LISA transfer function enters just as a (complex) multiplicative factor.
The data are Fourier transformed only once per tranche. Finally, the sum over the frequency components within the tranche inner-product (
25
) is not done over the full frequency grid but on a much smaller number of frequency bins
N_{\mathcal{F}}\ll\Delta T/\Delta t
.

In this work, we find that evaluating
Eq.

16
over
N_{\mathcal{F}}=21
bins centred on the frequency
\lceil f/f_{nm}\rceil\delta f
(where
\lceil\rceil
is the ceiling operator,
i.e.
the Fresnel kernel is centred on the nearest frequency bin above
f_{nm}
) , instead of all
2160\,(\Delta T/6\,\mathrm{hr})\,(5\,\mathrm{s}/\Delta t)
bins, is sufficient to recover
>99\%
of the waveform power.
This is a hundred-fold reduction in data volume with respect to the full frequency grid, greatly reducing the cost of
Eq.

8
.

We can further improve the computational efficiency of
Eq.

24
, and therefore reduce the overall cost of the evaluation of the detection statistic, by taking advantage of GPU hardware.
To do so, we must first identify what stage of the computation should be distributed among each block of GPU threads.
The architecture of modern GPUs typically requires specifying a size of
32^{n}
threads per block (where
n\in\mathbb{N}
), so imposing parallelism at the level of the evaluation of
Eq.

25
over Fourier bins within a tranche would significantly limit flexibility.
One could instead parallelise the computation with respect to the tranche index
m
, as multiple years of LISA data consists of thousands of these elements.
This choice is best when the desired application is to evaluate statistics for
\lesssim 10^{2}
sets of waveform parameters in parallel, as it makes full use of the GPU thread-count, but incurs additional memory management and synchronisation costs as many thread blocks must collaborate to evaluate the statistic for each set of source parameters.

),

In our pipeline, we typically employ swarms of more than
10^{5}
particles to rapidly explore each tile of the smBBH parameter space.
This motivates parallelising
Eq.

8
at the highest level possible, devoting one GPU thread to each set of source parameters.
Detection statistics can then in principle be computed en-masse for the entire swarm at each iteration of the optimisation algorithm, achieving extremely low wall-times per parameter set.
However, naïvely attempting to compute
10^{5}
waveforms in parallel would entail the construction of a matrix of waveforms of
(10^{5}\times N_{\mathrm{seg}}\times M_{\mathrm{tr}}\times N_{\mathcal{F}})\approx 10^{10}
elements, which is expensive and too large for the global memory of most GPUs.

Rather than storing waveforms and then computing statistics as two separate operations, we address this limitation by storing values of
\Upsilon_{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}
that are updated directly via the inner products
\langle d_{\alpha,n}|y_{\alpha,n}\rangle
and
\langle y_{\alpha,n}|y_{\alpha,n}\rangle
, tranche by tranche, as the waveform is constructed.
In doing so, no waveform storage is necessary, and the large matrix of waveforms is replaced with a vector of
10^{5}
statistic values (one per set of source parameters).
This level of memory management also necessitates the use of low-level
cuda
kernels, as opposed to the use of high-level frameworks such as
jax
.
Direct evaluation of
\Upsilon_{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}
is an essential component of our methodology — it minimises the number of memory allocations required, which ultimately bottlenecks GPU operations, and facilitates sufficiently large batch sizes for
Eq.

8
to effectively be computed in microseconds.
This is demonstrated in
Fig.

3
. We observed that speed-ups of a factor
\sim 10^{3}
with respect to a single-core CPU are achieved when the batch size is larger than
\sim 10^{4}
.
The resulting algorithm is also extremely lightweight with respect to global memory usage, requiring
\lesssim 1\,\mathrm{GB}
of memory even for large batch sizes; it is therefore well-suited to deployment on a large number of lower-grade GPUs, for which the memory available can be limited to a few
\mathrm{GB}
.

In
Fig.

4
, we show the GPU wall-time per statistic computed (for a batch size of
10^{4}
) in the
(\mathcal{M}_{c},f_{\mathrm{in}}
) plane explored by our search pipeline.
Performance varies from
\sim 1\,\mu\mathrm{s}
for higher
(\mathcal{M}_{c},f_{\mathrm{in}})
, to
\sim 25\,\mu\mathrm{s}
for lower
(\mathcal{M}_{c},f_{\mathrm{in}})
, over the parameter space.
The fuzzy boundary (due to averaging over other source parameters) in
Fig.

4
follows the parametric curve
t_{c}(\mathcal{M}_{c},f_{\mathrm{in}})=T_{\mathrm{obs}}
, with statistics for sources that chirp out-of-band during the observation being less expensive to compute (as the signal occupies fewer tranches).
Other features in
Fig.

While significantly varying performance over parameter space can limit the efficiency of large batch sizes (as during synchronising operations the GPU is forced to wait for the slowest threads to finish), our tile-based approach to the search ensures that the cost-per-statistic is roughly constant even for large batches.
Our approach is roughly three orders of magnitude faster than the GPU-accelerated frequency-domain implementation of
Eq.

8
employed in previous work (as shown in
Table

1
), a sufficient gain in efficiency to enable searches over the full parameter space of mildly eccentric smBBH signals.

Analysis of source 6 —

Source 6 is the only injected signal with
\rho>10
that was not found by the search.

Our search can fail to identify a signal for one of two reasons: (i)
\rho
is too low for the maximum segment count of
N_{\mathrm{seg}}=50
(
i.e.
, the signal’s statistic value is not significant with respect to noise fluctuations), or (ii) the peak in the search surface is too narrow at
N_{\mathrm{seg}}=50
to be reliably encountered by the particle swarms.
While sources 2 and 3 are not identified due to the former reason (as their SNRs are lower,
\rho^{\rm inj}\approx 8-9
), source 6 with
\rho^{\rm inj}\approx 14
is not found due to the latter reason. Indeed, we have tested that the signal is reliably detected by our pipeline once the search prior is narrowed by a factor of
\sim 4
.

To understand this behaviour, we performed targeted exploration of the primary mode of the posterior distributions
p(\xi)\propto\exp(\Upsilon_{50}/2)
for each source via nested sampling, as shown in
Fig.

6
.
This confirms that source 6 occupies a smaller fraction of the prior space than source 5 (the most comparable source in both SNR,
\rho^{\rm inj}\approx 13
, and
f_{\rm{in}}
), which explains why this source was identified despite having a lower SNR.
The volume occupied by the primary mode (for constant SNR) is an intrinsic property of the waveform model (and hence the search statistic) as a function of the
(\mathcal{M}_{c},f_{\mathrm{in}})
parameter space.
An adaptive tiling strategy that accounts for this behaviour (similarly to what is employed in the construction of template banks for ground-based searches
Owen 1996
;
Sakon et al. 2024
) and redistributes search efforts will improve the reliability of the search at little additional cost.
It is our expectation that such a tiling strategy will allow us to find source 6.
We will revisit strategies for parameter-space tiling in future work.
