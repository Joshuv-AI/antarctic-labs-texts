Several regions in Canada are evaluating whether to implement permanent standard time (pST) or permanent daylight saving time (pDST) after eliminating the twice-yearly clock changes. Although the seasonal time changes produce long summer evenings and earlier winter mornings, the spring “leap forward” causes health harms, including sleep loss and short-term increases in adverse health and injury outcomes. Most,1–5 but not all,6–8 recent reviews have suggested that ending seasonal clock adjustments would eliminate transition-related harms, and major sleep and chronobiology societies have all called for abolishing the time change and implementing pST (Appendix 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.261089/tab-related-content). However, North American jurisdictions eliminating time changes largely adopt pDST. Yukon adopted pDST in 2020, with British Columbia adopting it in March 2026.9 Alberta and the Northwest Territories followed suit a month later. In July 2026, the United States House of Representatives passed a bill to enact pDST nationwide, which is currently before the Senate. If it becomes law, Canada will likely be pressured to follow suit.10

In this analysis, we focus not on whether seasonal clock adjustments should end, but on which policy should be adopted if time changes are eliminated. We explore the public health implications of pDST and pST — with attention to mental health and broader determinants of health — and propose measures to monitor and mitigate the impacts of policy change on population health.

What are the public health implications of adopting a permanent time standard for different Canadian jurisdictions?

The public health implications of a change to pST or pDST would differ by season and geographic region across Canada. “Daylight saving time” is a misnomer — it does not save daylight but instead shifts it 1 hour later. Permanent DST preserves later dusk, which facilitates evening commuting, social participation, outdoor activity, and some retail and service activity (Table 1). It also delays dawn in winter months, which may leave many people waking and travelling to school or work in darkness, with potential adverse health, education, and productivity implications. In contrast, pST preserves earlier winter dawn, which better aligns with humans’ circadian rhythms and reduces pre-dawn wake-up and travel; pST also results in earlier dusk, which limits light for evening activities.

However, a single national or provincial label such as “standard time” or “daylight saving time” obscures large regional differences in actual light exposure, particularly during winter, across Canada’s vast geography, complicating the choice between pST and pDST.11 In principle, time zones should align closely with solar time, with the sun reaching its highest point near clock noon. In practice, Canadian time zones reflect political borders, transportation, commerce, and regional coordination, meaning that some regions already live substantially ahead of or behind local solar time. The Eastern Time Zone, for example, spans a wide east–west distance across Ontario and Quebec, leaving communities at its western and eastern edges with different experiences of morning and evening light. Although Saskatchewan has observed what is officially called Central Standard Time year-round since 1966, much of the province lies geographically closer to Mountain Time. In solar terms, Saskatchewan functions closer to pDST than to pST.11

Circadian and health consequences associated with clock adjustments are most pronounced in midlatitude regions. In polar regions, the period of daylight is already very short, which means a 1-hour clock adjustment is less consequential. Similarly, little variation occurs in daylight duration across seasons near the equator, likewise reducing the practical consequences of choosing one permanent time over another. Hence, although Yukon has observed pDST since 2020, the experiences of its residents may not reflect those of residents of lower latitudes.

How might human circadian biology respond to pST and pDST, respectively?

Internal circadian clocks generate and regulate people’s daily sleep–wake cycles and changes in alertness, performance, many cellular and physiologic functions, and behaviours, throughout the day.12,13 The master circadian clock in the hypothalamus runs slightly slow relative to the solar day, typically requiring about 24.2 hours to complete a cycle.14 To maintain alignment with a 24-hour day, it must adjust by synchronizing to environmental time cues. The most important cue is daylight, which can shift circadian clocks either forward or backward depending on the time of day, and intensity and wavelength of light exposure. Morning light exposure advances the clock, compensating for its tendency to lag; evening light exposure delays the clock, resulting in circadian phase delay. Alignment between body time and social time, the local time by which work, school, and other schedules are set, is therefore facilitated by morning light and impeded by evening light.

Modern environments already promote circadian delay. Many people spend much of the day indoors, reducing exposure to bright morning and daytime light, and electric lighting and screen use extend light exposure into the evening, thereby weakening morning phase-advancing cues and strengthening evening phase-delaying cues.13 This creates a misalignment, called “social jet lag,”15 whereby social time requires morning waking for school and work before the optimal waking time determined by the body’s delayed biological rhythm. Observational studies have associated social jet lag with sleep restriction, poorer mental health, metabolic risk, and cognitive or functional impairment. 13,15,16 Permanent DST worsens this pattern in winter by prolonging morning darkness and extending evening light, which works against biological adjustment at both ends of the day.15 In contrast, the earlier summer dawn with pST, although more aligned with the circadian rhythm, might advance the circadian phase when compared with pDST, which may lead people to wake too early.

How might pST or pDST affect physical and mental health?

Accumulated evidence suggests that later morning light with pDST is likely to have negative health consequences, particularly on seasonal mood vulnerability. An estimated 1% to 3% of people in Canada17 live with winter depression, known as seasonal affective disorder (SAD), and as many as 15% of people experience milder seasonal changes in mood, sleep, appetite, and energy, termed subsyndromal SAD. People in Canada experience more frequent major depressive episodes in winter than in summer,18 suggesting that seasonal light exposure has relevance at individual and population levels. The worsening of sleep, concentration, energy, and “feeling bad about yourself” in winter is most prominent among youth aged 12 to 17 years.19

The main biological hypothesis for SAD is circadian phase delay, with delayed morning light thought to contribute to later circadian timing and symptoms such as hypersomnia, increased appetite, low energy, and impaired concentration.20 Bright light therapy in the early morning, the evidence-based treatment for SAD, corrects phase-delayed circadian rhythms.20 In delaying morning light, pDST functions in the opposite direction to light therapy, further weakening the natural morning light cue and increasing vulnerability to seasonal mood disturbances.

Geographic correlations with latitude and longitude support the same conclusion as circadian biology: later morning light is likely to have negative consequences. Northern latitudes have later onset of winter sunrise and higher prevalence of SAD, with the latter increasing by 0.2% per 1° increase in latitude.21 Limited evidence suggests that people living farther west in a time zone experience later sunrise by the clock and have higher rates of SAD than those living farther east.22 In US county-level analyses, people in western parts of time zones averaged as many as 2 additional suicide deaths per 100 000 people each year (p < 0.001) compared with those living in eastern ones, with larger differences in northern regions.23 These “western edge effects” approximate the solar-time consequences of pDST.

Other geographic correlation studies link later solar time to shorter sleep, cardiovascular and metabolic outcomes, traffic risk, and cancer incidence.24,25 A 2025 modelling study simulated natural light exposure and circadian disruption, mapping these variables against the prevalence of several chronic diseases, to evaluate the health impacts of different time systems.26 Findings suggested that pDST and pST offered health advantages compared with the current twice-yearly clock changes, but the health benefits of pDST were lower than those of pST by a third. Notably, the comparative benefits of pST were greatest in northern regions of the continental US, which are similar to the latitudes in which most people in Canada reside.

How do economic and educational outcomes inform the choice of a permanent time policy?

The impact of pST and pDST on economic and education outcomes, and their subsequent effect on health, is unclear. The later solar time with pDST costs in terms of sleep, labour productivity, and human capital. A study that looked at variation in sunset timing within US time zones found that an hour of later sunset reduces weekly sleep by nearly an hour and long-run earnings by about 5%;27 similar results were found in Germany.28 Another study reported that workers on the late-sunset side of US time-zone borders earn about 3.5% less and work fewer hours than those on the early-sunset side, suggesting an economic cost of social jet lag.24 The energy savings from more afternoon light, the rationale behind a historical attempt in the US to switch to pDST, appear to be limited;4 a Canadian study found reduced energy use in some provinces and increased use in others.29

Educational outcomes may also be sensitive to morning light and school start times. A study of students at schools straddling the time-zone boundary in Florida showed that an extra hour of sunlight before school was associated with higher math and reading scores (0.08 standard deviations [SDs] and 0.06 SDs, respectively) among adolescents.30 These effects are comparable in magnitude to meaningful class-size reductions and largest at puberty when circadian rhythms are most sensitive.30

By contrast, pDST is more likely to have evening benefits. Later dusk may support after-work and -school outdoor leisure, social, and some commercial activity — unpublished data suggest that people adjust their schedules to coordinate leisure time and other activity when sunset is later (Baylis P, Borenstein S, Rubin EA: unpublished data, 2026)31 — whereas the fall transition to ST is associated with reduced retail spending.32 Under pST, earlier dusk may reduce opportunities for evening outdoor activity and related economic activity.

Available evidence is limited by being quasi-experimental and dependent on assumptions about comparability across boundaries or regions. Taken together, limited evidence suggests that pDST may redirect benefits toward evening leisure and retail activity; on the other hand, the costs of pDST on sleep, productivity, and learning are better established.

Which groups are most likely to be affected by the adoption of pST and pDST, respectively?

People with flexible schedules, discretionary time, and access to outdoor space may be more able to enjoy opportunities for recreation, economic activity, and social participation under pDST. Those with inflexible or shift work, or early school, work, or caregiving obligations; those who are unprotected road users; or those who rely on public transit could be more exposed to the harms of prolonged darkness of winter mornings, conditions which disproportionally affect lower-income and minoritized groups.33 People living in lower income or rural and remote areas with poorer street lighting and sidewalk infrastructure could also be disproportionately exposed to the risks of early-morning travel in darkness. Impacts could be further magnified for northern communities near the western edge of the time zone.

Some people are also more biologically vulnerable to phase delays. Chronotype describes an individual’s biological preference for sleep and activity, popularized as evening-type “owls” (preference for later sleep and wake times) and morning-type “larks” (earlier sleep and wake times). People with an evening chronotype are more likely to experience social jet lag when later winter dawn worsens phase delay, adversely affecting work functioning in winter.13 Children and adolescents are also vulnerable, as puberty is associated with delayed sleep timing, and school start times remain fixed and often early. For adolescents, pDST would exacerbate social jet lag, which is associated with sleepiness, depression, poorer mental health, cognitive difficulties, and impaired school functioning.34–36 Effects of pDST could also be amplified for people who already sleep too little or who have insomnia, obstructive sleep apnea, or other disorders associated with sleep problems.

What can Canada learn from international experiences of eliminating biannual time changes?

International experience suggests that switching to pDST becomes controversial when its winter trade-offs are felt, especially dark morning commutes and school travel. The US adopted pDST in 1974 as an energy-conservation measure during the oil crisis, with high initial public support; however, the policy was abandoned within the year after widespread opposition to dark winter mornings, concerns about children travelling to school before sunrise, workplace safety concerns, and limited evidence of meaningful energy savings.37 The recent congressional debate in the US appears to have given scant attention to this history.

Russia moved to pDST in 2011. Public dissatisfaction grew rapidly, particularly in northern regions, where winter morning darkness was even more prolonged, with critics citing disrupted biological rhythms, morning travel risks, and effects on daily functioning. The policy was reversed in 2014.15 The 2015 national move to pDST in Chile was also reversed after complaints about reduced school attendance, scheduling disruption, and agricultural effects.38 The far southern Magallanes region of Chile retained pDST after local advocacy,38 illustrating the importance of latitude in such policy decisions.

Several midlatitude jurisdictions, including most of Mexico, Japan, and South Korea, observe pST. Public surveys in Mexico39 showed majority opposition to seasonal DST before its 2022 abolition, whereas survey results from Japan and South Korea showed mixed public opinion but notable concern about reintroducing seasonal DST.40,41

Which mitigation and monitoring measures should be implemented?

Whichever time-policy option is adopted, mitigation and monitoring should be planned, with prioritization of disproportionately affected groups. The burden of mitigation would be substantially greater under pDST than under pST (Table 2). For pDST, the main winter priorities would be to take steps to reduce harms from prolonged darkness during winter mornings by improving road and pedestrian safety, supporting later or more flexible school and work start times, increasing morning light exposure in schools and workplaces, and preparing clinicians to monitor for increased sleep disruption and seasonal mood symptoms. Public education should focus on the risks and harms of chronic sleep loss, the effects of morning light exposure, use of bright light therapy where appropriate, and optimizing sleep hygiene. Mitigation measures for pST would relate to managing the effects of early summer dawn and earlier winter dusk. These could include education about maintaining activity despite earlier dusk and use of practical strategies, such as blackout curtains, for people prone to early-morning waking.

Evaluation and rigorous surveillance should also be prioritized. Provinces should monitor school absenteeism and lateness; mood and mental health presentations, including disordered sleeping and seasonal depression; workplace injuries; road injuries by time of day and vulnerable road-user status; physical activity; and cardiovascular events. Evening activities should also be monitored to detect healthy behaviours, such as outdoor play for children, and potentially harmful behaviours, such as increased alcohol consumption. To detect inequitable health impacts, data should be disaggregated by age, sex, geography, socioeconomic status, rurality, and shift-work exposure. Without evaluation, a permanent time policy becomes a natural experiment without a learning system.

Conclusion

Time-standard decisions require a cross-sectoral approach that considers health implications and coordinates actions across government sectors to avoid harmful health impacts and improve population health and health equity — also known as a Health in All Policies approach.42 Ending biannual clock adjustments may eliminate transition harms that last a few weeks, but the health effects of permanent time are likely to be chronic. Permanent ST may support health by preserving the winter morning light most important for circadian rhythms, sleep, and mood. Meanwhile, pDST would negatively affect sleep and mental health in winter, particularly in vulnerable groups including youth, workers among whom minorities are overrepresented, and those with sleep disorders. The preponderance of health evidence favours pST, which would align with the recommendations of sleep and chronobiology societies worldwide and help ensure that Canada does not repeat other countries’ failed past experiments with pDST. If regions in Canada choose pDST over pST, the decision should be implemented with careful attention to reducing population health impacts through anticipatory mitigation measures, protection for vulnerable groups, and transparent evaluation.
