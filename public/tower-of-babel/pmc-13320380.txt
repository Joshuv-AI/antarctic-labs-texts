Assessing techniques and outcomes in scrotal reconstruction: A sudanese cohort study
Authors: Albra Kamal, Momen Mohamed, Amin M Yassin, Hozifa Mohamed, Mohamed Dirar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAcquired scrotal defects, most commonly resulting from infection and trauma, present complex reconstructive challenges. This study evaluated the causes and reconstructive outcomes of scrotal defects in a Sudanese multi-center cohort, aiming to (1) identify predominant aetiologies and comorbidities, (2) describe reconstructive techniques and complication profiles, and (3) compare outcomes among different flap types.Materials and MethodsA prospective cohort study was conducted at two Sudanese hospitals from January 2024 to December 2025, including patients with acquired scrotal defects undergoing reconstruction. Data collected included demographics, comorbidities, aetiology, defect size and location, reconstruction timing, surgical technique, and complications. Defects were stratified by surface area involvement. Statistical analysis used descriptive methods and significance testing (p < 0.05).ResultsForty-two patients (mean age 37.4 years) were included; 43% had diabetes. Infection was the leading cause (76%), with diabetes significantly associated with infection-related defects. Most defects were bilateral (67%), and 78% were moderate or large. Reconstruction methods included scrotal advancement (36%), Singapore (31%), TFL (24%), and modified pudendal thigh flaps (10%), with skin grafts for penile resurfacing (7%). Complications occurred in 45%, most often partial flap necrosis and wound dehiscence; complication type was significantly associated with flap type.ConclusionInfection—often in diabetic patients—was the predominant cause of scrotal defects. Multiple flap techniques provided satisfactory coverage, especially thigh-based flaps for larger defects, with no donor site morbidity. High complication rates emphasize the need for individualized reconstructive strategies, as no single technique proved clearly superior.Keywords: Scrotal defects, Scrotal injuries, Fournier’s gangrene, Scrotal reconstruction, Pudendal flaps, Genital reconstruction

Introduction

The scrotal sac is a component of the external male genitalia whose anatomical structure provides an optimal environment for testicular function while also serving an aesthetic role. Various aetiologies can result in scrotal defects, necessitating surgical reconstruction to physically protect the testes, promote their function, and improve aesthetic appearance and psychological well-being. Scrotal defects are classified into congenital and acquired categories. Congenital causes are infrequent but have been documented in the literature, including conditions such as bifid scrotum, hypoplasia, and agenesis of the scrotal sac.1,2 By contrast, acquired causes are relatively common and are frequently encountered in urological, surgical, and plastic surgery departments. Among these acquired causes, infection stands out as the most prevalent, with Fournier’s gangrene—a polymicrobial, life-threatening necrotizing fasciitis—representing the most common acquired pathology affecting the genital organs.3 In addition to infection, trauma contributes significantly to scrotal defects, particularly through penetrating injuries that result in degloving injuries of varying sizes and components. Other acquired causes include malignancies such as squamous cell carcinoma, basal cell carcinoma, and apocrine carcinoma, as well as inflammatory conditions like hidradenitis suppurativa and eczema.1, 2, 3

Elimination of the underlying pathology is essential prior to reconstruction. The role of plastic surgery is typically defined following urological or general surgical procedures, which may include debridement or diversion of stool and urine. Reconstruction options range from simple suturing to advanced techniques such as free tissue transfer. The choice of reconstruction method depends on several factors, including defect size, patient comorbidities, the quantity and condition of remaining tissue, and the preferences and experience of both patient and surgeon. No single reconstruction technique is universally superior, provided it offers a thin, pliable cover that improves appearance and maintains function.4, 5, 6

Although some literature discusses the aetiology and management of scrotal defects, limited research originates from African countries, where hot and humid climates combined with inadequate hygiene contribute to increased infection rates. Moreover, cultural stigma associated with genital conditions can delay presentation, and poverty may serve as a barrier to obtaining medical advice, thereby impacting follow-up adherence.

This study aimed to evaluate the aetiological profile and reconstructive outcomes of acquired scrotal defects in a Sudanese multi-center cohort. The specific objectives were: (1) to identify the predominant causes and comorbidities associated with acquired scrotal defects in the study population; (2) to describe the reconstructive techniques employed and their associated complication profiles; and (3) to compare outcomes among the different flap types used for scrotal reconstruction.

Materials and methods

This prospective, multi-center cohort study was conducted at two plastic surgery units in Sudan—Atbara Teaching Hospital and Omdurman Teaching Hospital—between January 2024 and December 2025. Consecutive patients with acquired scrotal defects undergoing reconstructive surgery were enrolled; those who died before reconstruction, were managed conservatively, or had incomplete records were excluded.

Data collected included age, comorbidities, aetiology (infection, trauma, or tumour), defect laterality, defect size (small ≤30%, moderate 31–60%, large/extensive >60% of scrotal surface area), timing of reconstruction, surgical technique, and complications. Defect size was clinically estimated by the operating surgeon, and penile involvement (skin loss, corporal, or urethral) was documented when present. Reconstruction timing varied by aetiology: within 7 days for trauma, after negative wound cultures for infection, and within 2 weeks of confirmed clear margins for tumours.

All procedures were performed or supervised by consultant plastic surgeons using a standardized flap selection algorithm. Scrotal advancement flaps were used for unilateral defects ≤50% with adequate residual tissue. Modified pudendal thigh flaps addressed moderate penoscrotal defects extending to the perineum with limited thigh tissue. Singapore flaps covered larger defects (>50% or bilateral), typically using a unilateral flap for neoscrotum creation. TFL flaps were reserved for extensive scrotal, inguinal, and perineal defects. Skin grafts were used solely for penile resurfacing alongside flap-based scrotal reconstruction. For infectious aetiologies, debridement and negative wound cultures were mandatory before reconstruction.

Complications were categorized as recipient site (bleeding, infection, flap necrosis, wound dehiscence, graft loss) or donor site (bleeding, haematoma, seroma, infection, dehiscence) and graded using the Clavien-Dindo system. Partial flap necrosis was subclassified as minor (≤20%, conservative management) or major (>20%, surgical intervention), and wound dehiscence as minor (≤2 cm, secondary intention) or major (>2 cm, re-suturing). Time to healing and hospital stay were recorded. Follow-up lasted 6 months with twice-weekly reviews in the first month, monthly reviews for three months, and a final 6-month review.

Statistical analysis used SPSS. Fisher's Exact Test was the primary test for categorical comparisons with small subgroups (notably the modified pudendal thigh flap group, n = 4); chi-square tests were applied only when all expected cell frequencies exceeded 5. A secondary analysis collapsed flap groups into local flaps (scrotal advancement, n = 15) versus regional/thigh flaps (Singapore, modified pudendal, TFL; n = 27) to enhance statistical validity, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Reporting and ethics

This study was reported following the STROCSS 2021 guidelines to enhance reporting quality.7 Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethical Committee of Atbara Teaching Hospital. Patient confidentiality was upheld throughout the study. Data collection commenced only after the research objectives were clearly defined and written informed consent was obtained. For participants under the age of majority, parents or guardians provided voluntary and fully informed consent.

Results

Patient demographics and aetiology

Forty-two patients with acquired scrotal defects were treated through various reconstruction approaches during the study period. The mean age of the patients was 37.4 ± 17.4 years. Comorbidities included diabetes mellitus in 18 patients (42.9%), hypertension in 3 patients (7.1%), and HIV in 1 patient (2.4%).

The most common cause of scrotal defects was infection, affecting 32 patients (76.2%), followed by trauma in 10 patients (23.8%). No tumour-related defects were encountered during the study period. A statistically significant association was identified between diabetes mellitus and infection-related scrotal defects (p = 0.008) (Table 1).

Defect characteristics

Defects were bilateral in 28 cases (66.7%) and unilateral in 14 cases (33.3%). Defect size stratification revealed: small defects (≤30% of scrotal surface area) in 9 patients (21.4%), moderate defects (31–60%) in 17 patients (40.5%), and large/extensive defects (>60%) in 16 patients (38.1%).

Penile involvement was present in 3 cases (7.1%): one patient had isolated penile skin loss, one presented with a urethral defect and penile skin loss, and a third had corporal involvement.

Timing of reconstruction

For the 32 patients with infection-related defects, a median of 2 debridement sessions (range: 1–4) was required before reconstruction. The median interval from initial debridement to definitive reconstruction was 18 days (range: 10–35 days). Negative wound swab culture was confirmed in all cases prior to reconstruction.

For the 10 patients with trauma-related defects, reconstruction was performed at a median of 5 days (range: 2–7 days) from the time of injury.

Reconstructive techniques

The distribution of reconstructive techniques is presented in Table 1. Local flaps (scrotal advancement) were used in 15 patients (35.7%), while regional flaps were employed in 27 patients (64.3%). The specific flap types were: scrotal advancement flap (n = 15, 35.7%), Singapore flap (n = 13, 31.0%), TFL flap (n = 10, 23.8%), and modified pudendal thigh flap (n = 4, 9.5%). Skin grafts were applied in 3 patients (7.1%) exclusively for penile skin resurfacing.

The relationship between defect size and flap selection is noteworthy. Scrotal advancement flaps were predominantly used for small-to-moderate unilateral defects (11 of 15 patients, 73.3%). Singapore and TFL flaps were primarily employed for moderate-to-large bilateral defects (20 of 23 patients, 87.0%). Complications

Overall, 19 patients (45.2%) experienced one or more complications, while 23 patients (54.8%) had uncomplicated outcomes. The specific complications were: partial flap necrosis in 9 patients (21.4%), wound dehiscence in 7 patients (16.7%), and surgical site infection in 2 patients (4.8%) (Table 1).

Severity grading of partial flap necrosis: Minor (≤20% of flap area, managed conservatively) occurred in 7 of 9 cases (77.8%), while major (>20% of flap area, requiring surgical intervention) occurred in 2 of 9 cases (22.2%).

Severity grading of wound dehiscence: Minor (≤2 cm, healed by secondary intention) occurred in 5 of 7 cases (71.4%), while major (>2 cm, requiring re-suturing) occurred in 2 of 7 cases (28.6%).

Clavien-Dindo classification: Grade I (deviation from normal course without pharmacological or surgical intervention) was assigned to 15 patients; Grade II (requiring pharmacological treatment) to 3 patients; and Grade IIIb (requiring reoperation under general anaesthesia) to 2 patients (4.8%). No Grade IV (life-threatening) or Grade V (death) complications occurred.

Reoperation rate: Two patients (4.8%) required reoperation. One patient with a Singapore flap underwent debridement and re-advancement for major partial necrosis, and one patient with a scrotal advancement flap required re-suturing for major wound dehiscence.

Time to healing: The median time to complete wound healing was 3 weeks (range: 2–8 weeks) for patients without complications and 6 weeks (range: 4–12 weeks) for those who experienced complications.

Length of hospital stay: The median hospital stay was 7 days (range: 4–21 days) for patients with infection-related defects and 4 days (range: 2–9 days) for those with trauma-related defects. Patients who experienced complications had a median hospital stay of 12 days (range: 7–21 days) compared to 5 days (range: 3–10 days) for those without complications.

Complications by flap type

The distribution of complications by flap type is presented in Table 2. Fisher’s Exact Test revealed a statistically significant association between flap type and the pattern of complications (p = 0.026). Partial flap necrosis was most frequently observed with the Singapore flap (4 of 13 patients, 30.8%) and the TFL flap (3 of 10 patients, 30.0%), while wound dehiscence was most commonly associated with the scrotal advancement flap (4 of 15 patients, 26.7%). The modified pudendal thigh flap group (n = 4) had 1 case of dehiscence, 1 case of partial necrosis, and 2 cases of infection; however, the very small sample size precludes meaningful statistical inference for this subgroup.

When flap types were collapsed into two categories—local flaps (scrotal advancement, n = 15) versus regional/thigh flaps (Singapore, modified pudendal thigh, TFL; n = 27)—the overall complication rates were 33.3% (5 of 15) and 51.9% (14 of 27), respectively (Chi-square, p = 0.262), indicating no statistically significant difference in overall complication rates between these categories. However, the pattern of complications differed: local flaps were more commonly associated with wound dehiscence, whereas regional/thigh flaps were more frequently associated with partial flap necrosis.

Comorbidities and complications

No statistically significant association was identified between comorbidities and the occurrence of complications (p = 0.631). Nevertheless, partial flap necrosis and wound dehiscence were more prevalent among diabetic patients, accounting for 17 of the 19 complications observed. Similarly, no significant association was found between aetiology (infection vs. trauma) and the occurrence of complications (p = 0.185), although complications were more frequently observed in patients with infection-related defects.

Donor site morbidity

None of the 42 patients experienced donor site morbidity.

Discussion

Scrotal defects impact not only an individual’s physical well-being but also negatively affect self-image and psychological health. Surgical reconstruction aims to restore form and function while enhancing mental health. The unique structure of the scrotum makes reconstruction a challenging endeavour. Although numerous reconstructive options are described in the literature, each carries its own advantages and limitations. Small defects may heal through secondary intention, delayed primary closure, or skin grafting. Larger defects involving up to 50% of the scrotum can be managed through scrotal advancement, while those exceeding half of the scrotal surface area typically require formal flap coverage, which may include local flaps, thigh flaps, groin flaps, abdominal flaps, and advanced techniques such as free tissue transfer.5,8

This study identified infection as the primary cause of scrotal defects, followed by trauma, with no tumours observed during the study period. The high prevalence of genital infections in our cohort is likely attributable to hot weather, humidity in the genital area, and excessive sweating, which create conditions conducive to polymicrobial growth. Hygiene is a significant factor in infection development, particularly among patients with compromised immunity, such as transplant recipients and individuals with diabetes. Furthermore, shaving genital hair with razor blades can cause microtrauma, increasing susceptibility to microbial invasion. Inflammatory dermatoses induce itching and chronic irritation, resulting in skin excoriation and facilitating the onset of infection.9 Fournier’s gangrene is a serious syndrome that results in rapid skin necrosis and haemodynamic instability. It is a significant cause of male genital defects and leads to considerable tissue destruction. This condition constitutes a surgical emergency requiring urgent treatment and close medical monitoring. Typically, Fournier’s gangrene necessitates multiple debridement sessions and wound cultures until the infection is cleared. Its development can sometimes follow urological procedures and may also occur idiopathically.4,6

Trauma can result in varying degrees of scrotal defects, with the size of the defect correlating with the mechanism of injury. Blunt trauma may lead to localized contusions complicated by ischaemia, while haematomas can result in secondary infection and ischaemia. In contrast, sharp and weapon-related injuries can cause direct degloving injuries and varying degrees of tissue necrosis. These types of injuries are frequently observed in military personnel, industrial workers, and victims of road traffic accidents. In the present study, 23.8% of cases were attributed to trauma. In India, Maurya et al. reported trauma as the most common cause of scrotal defects (83%), whereas in Egypt, Megahed et al. found infection to be the primary cause (44.4%), which is lower than our finding of 76.2%.6,10, 11, 12 Burns and scrotal tumours are rare causes of scrotal defects that have been documented in the literature; however, none were recorded in our series.

Diabetes was the most significant comorbidity identified in our study and is recognized as a risk factor for developing defects due to infection (p = 0.008); however, it did not significantly influence postoperative outcomes (p = 0.631). Nisbet and Thompson reached a similar conclusion in their study regarding the impact of diabetes on the presentation and outcome of patients with Fournier’s gangrene.13 Additionally, Pandiaraja et al. identified diabetes and untreated perineal abscess as risk factors for extensive infection.14

The selection of reconstructive technique is influenced by multiple factors, with the remaining scrotal tissue and defect size being the primary considerations. The scrotal advancement flap is a preferred option for many surgeons, as it provides tissue similar to the native scrotum, ensuring optimal testicular coverage to maintain viability, better aesthetic results, and a reduced learning curve, along with fewer overall complications. In our study, this flap was employed in 35.7% of cases, predominantly for unilateral defects involving ≤50% of the scrotal surface area. Ahmedov et al. demonstrated in their research on scrotal reconstruction following Fournier’s gangrene that the use of the remaining scrotal flap with minimal scoring can effectively close small to medium-sized defects; however, extensive internal incisions can contribute to flap necrosis.15 Maurya et al. similarly noted that local flap advancement is the most commonly utilized method for scrotal reconstruction.11

Thigh-based flaps are widely utilized for scrotal defects, with the pudendal artery flap (Singapore) and its modifications recognized as the most prevalent options for penoscrotal defects of various origins. The pudendal flap is considered a robust reconstructive technique, as it provides a sensate, thin, and pliable covering for the testicles, thereby maintaining viability and improving appearance. These procedures are typically performed in a single stage and result in minimal donor site morbidity. A unilateral flap is frequently employed to create a neoscrotum, while bilateral flaps are used for larger defects involving both testes. In this series, nearly all patients received a unilateral flap, except for one patient who underwent bilateral flap reconstruction.16, 17, 18, 19

The tensor fascia lata (TFL) flap is a versatile reconstructive option for the lower abdomen and groin region. Although its use has declined in recent years—particularly in scrotal reconstruction—because many alternative techniques offer a single-stage procedure instead of the multiple surgeries sometimes required with the TFL flap, it remains a viable option for extensive defects. Distal necrosis and bulkiness are notable drawbacks, especially in obese patients. In our series, the TFL flap was utilized for extensive defects and provided reliable coverage, with a partial necrosis rate comparable to that of the Singapore flap (30.0%vs. 30.8%).20, 21, 22

Skin grafting is a viable reconstructive option that has been widely used for various defect sizes, either as a standalone method or in conjunction with other techniques. While some authors have reported successful total testicular coverage using skin grafts, concerns persist regarding the thin graft layer’s susceptibility to shear trauma, contraction deformities, and pruritus that may predispose to infection. In this study, skin grafts were specifically used for the resurfacing of penile skin loss and performed effectively.6,23, 24, 25, 26

It is important to acknowledge that the overall complication rate of 45.2% in this series is not insignificant. However, the majority of complications were minor and managed conservatively (Clavien-Dindo Grade I–II in 17 of 19 cases, 89.5%), with only two patients (4.8%) requiring reoperation. The higher rate of partial flap necrosis observed with Singapore and TFL flaps compared to scrotal advancement flaps may reflect the greater complexity and size of the defects for which these regional flaps were selected, rather than an inherent disadvantage of the techniques themselves. This confounding by indication is an important consideration when interpreting the comparison of outcomes across flap types.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the sample size of 42 patients divided across four reconstructive groups limits statistical power, particularly for the modified pudendal thigh flap group (n = 4). While Fisher’s Exact Test was employed to address small cell counts, the findings for individual flap types should be considered descriptive rather than definitive. Second, defect size was estimated clinically rather than measured using standardized tools (e.g., transparent templates or photographic planimetry), which introduces subjectivity into the size stratification. Third, although testicular viability was preserved in all cases, no formal functional assessment was performed—specifically, hormonal evaluation (serum testosterone, FSH, LH), semen analysis, fertility assessment, and patient-reported outcomes were not included. The claim of testicular preservation in this study refers solely to anatomical viability and should not be interpreted as evidence of functional preservation. Fourth, the study did not include an aesthetic satisfaction assessment or quality-of-life measures. Fifth, the absence of a control group and the non-randomized design preclude causal inferences regarding the superiority of any particular reconstructive technique. Sixth, hospital stay duration and time-to-healing data, though now reported, were not primary endpoints and may be subject to confounding by socioeconomic factors. Finally, this study reflects the experience of two centres in Sudan, and the findings may not be generalizable to populations with different aetiological profiles or healthcare systems.

Conclusion

Scrotal reconstruction aims to achieve thin, pliable coverage in a single surgical procedure while preserving testicular viability. In this Sudanese multi-center cohort, infection was the leading cause of acquired scrotal defects, with diabetes being a significant risk factor. Various local and regional flap techniques provided acceptable coverage, though no single method demonstrated clear superiority. Thigh-based flaps (Singapore and TFL) were most commonly employed for larger defects and were associated with no donor site morbidity; however, the observed complication rates—including partial flap necrosis and wound dehiscence—underscore that no single technique is universally optimal. The choice of reconstructive method should be individualized based on defect size and location, tissue availability, and patient comorbidities. Larger prospective studies with standardized defect classification, functional outcome assessment (including hormonal evaluation and patient-reported measures), and longer follow-up are needed to define the optimal reconstructive strategy for acquired scrotal defects.
