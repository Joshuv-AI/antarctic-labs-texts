Effects of dietary mulberry anthocyanins on meat quality, antioxidant capacity, gut microbiota, and plasma anthocyanin exposure in North China Chai chickens
Authors: Meijiao Liu, Ruijie Yu, Songhan Li, Jianyue Xu, Dingkuo Liu, Yuan Li, Chaoqi Ren, Xiaoxue Yu, Liuan Li, Xin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChicken is the second most consumed meat in China after pork and represents an important source of animal protein. Late-laying hens are a major by-product of the egg industry, but their meat quality often declines after prolonged egg production, limiting their economic value. Mulberry (Morus alba L.) is rich in anthocyanins with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and lipid-lowering effects, offering potential application in improving the meat quality of spent hens. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of dietary mulberry anthocyanins(MA) on slaughter performance, serum biochemical indices, antioxidant status, meat quality, cecal microbiota and plasma exposure to major anthocyanins in late-laying North China Chai chickens. A total of 240 healthy 370-day-old chickens were randomized into five groups: a basal diet control (Con) and four diets supplemented with MA at 50, 100, 200, or 400 mg/kg (MA_50, MA_100, MA_200, MA_400) for 30 days. The results showed that dietary MA increased dressing percentage and thigh muscle yield, while decreasing serum TG, ALT and AST levels compared with Con (P < 0.05). Antioxidant indices were improved, with increased T-AOC, T-SOD, glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) and catalase (CAT), accompanied by reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) (P < 0.05). Regarding meat quality, MA reduced cooking loss, crude fat content, and thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS), while increasing redness (a*), crude protein, moisture, myoglobin, and collagen contents (P < 0.05). 16S rRNA sequencing indicated that MA altered cecal microbial community structure and increased the relative abundance of potentially beneficial taxa. In a separate oral gavage experiment (1,500 mg/kg), cyanidin-3-O-glucoside (C3G) and cyanidin-3-O-rutinoside (C3R) were detectable in plasma at 15 min post-gavage, peaked at approximately 30 min, and declined to undetectable levels by 24 hours. Collectively, dietary MA improved antioxidant status and selected meat quality traits, and modulated gut microbiota in late-laying North China Chai chickens. These findings underscore the potential of MA as a natural feed additive for improving the utilization value of late-laying hens.Keywords: Mulberry anthocyanin, Antioxidant capacity, Meat quality, Plasma exposure, Gut microbiota

Introduction

Chicken meat represents a growing proportion of animal-derived foods in human diets because of its high nutritional value, high protein content, and low fat content (Cui et al., 2024). Chicken meat quality attributes, such as water-holding capacity, color, and flavor, crucially influence consumer preferences and industrial economic benefits (Song et al., 2025). However, approximately 5 billion spent laying hens are discarded worldwide each year, with 2 billion in China. Although the large number of spent hens represents substantial potential for meat utilization, the long rearing period and sustained egg production result in excessive nutrient mobilization and accumulated oxidative stress. Consequently, the meat from spent hens exhibits decreased water-holding capacity and inferior flavor, limiting its processing performance and market value (Li et al., 2025). The North China Chai chicken is a dual-purpose breed for meat and egg production, with a desirable basic meat quality profile; however, there is potential for further optimizing meat quality during aging. Therefore, developing natural and healthy dietary additives to improve the quality of spent laying hen meat is of paramount importance.

Mulberry (Morus alba L.), a medicinal and edible plant, contains many bioactive ingredients such as anthocyanins, polysaccharides, and flavonoids, and has demonstrated a wide spectrum of bioactivities, including antioxidant, hypolipidemic, hypoglycemic, and hepatoprotective effects (Du et al., 2025; Truzzi et al., 2024). Anthocyanins are water-soluble flavonoid pigments with biological properties including free radical scavenging, inhibition of lipid peroxidation, and anti-inflammatory activities. Anthocyanin supplementation has great potential to prevent diseases and improve meat quality in livestock and poultry (Bendokas et al., 2020a, 2020b).

The major anthocyanins in mulberry are cyanidin-3-O-glucoside (C3G), cyanidin-3-O-rutinoside (C3R), pelargonidin-3-O-glucoside (P3G) and pelargonidin-3-O-galactoside (P3R). Among these, C3G and C3R account for more than 98 % of the total anthocyanins (Chen et al., 2022). Studies have shown that cyanidin-3-O-glucoside (C3G) supplementation can alleviate oxidative stress by reducing intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels while restoring glutathione (GSH)-related metabolic balance (Yang et al., 2025). Anthocyanins derived from red corn have been reported to improve antioxidant status, immune function, and meat quality in lambs (Antunović et al., 2022). In addition, cactus anthocyanins may participate in host metabolic regulation by modulating gut microbiota composition and promoting the production of short-chain fatty acids (Zhang et al., 2022b). Moreover, numerous studies have confirmed the existence of the muscle-gut microbiota axis, which provides a potential strategy to improve meat quality in chicken by modulating intestinal microbiota (Lei et al., 2022). However, studies on the effects of mulberry anthocyanins (MA) on meat quality remain limited.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of dietary MA on slaughter performance, antioxidant status, meat quality, and cecal microbiota in late-laying North China Chai chickens, while characterizing plasma exposure profiles of C3G and C3R following intragastric gavage.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The Animal Care and Use Committee of Tianjin Agricultural University approved all animal procedures (License No. 2024LLSC51).

Animals, diets, and experimental design

Two hundred and forty healthy 370-day-old North China Chai chickens with an average initial body weight (BW) of 1.61 ± 0.29 kg (mean ± SD) were randomly allocated to five treatment groups with 4 replicates in each group and 12 chickens in each replicate. The chickens were housed in three-tier wire cages (47.5 cm × 55 cm × 37 cm) with feed and water available ad libitum for 30 days. The control group was fed a basal diet, and the dietary MA supplementation groups were fed the basal diet supplemented with MA at 50, 100, 200, and 400 mg/kg (MA_50, MA_100, MA_200, and MA_400), respectively.

Reagents and materials

MA (No. JHK-20240628; Xi’an Jinhekang Pharmaceutical, China). C3G (≥98 %, CAS 7084-24-4) and C3R (≥98 %, CAS 18719-76-1) were purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China). Commercial assay kits for TG, total cholesterol (TC), MDA, GSH-Px, CAT, T-SOD, ALT, AST, and T-AOC were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). TBARS and BCA kits were obtained from Elabscience (Wuhan, China). ELISA kits for chicken myoglobin and type Ⅰ collagen were purchased from Enzyme-linked Biotechnology (Yancheng, China). The basal diet was provided by Dacheng Wanda Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). The composition of the diet is shown in Table 1.

Blood sampling

For serum analysis, 12 chickens from each group were subjected to blood sampling via the wing vein on days 10, 20, and 30. Blood samples were centrifuged at 2,192 g for 10 min, and the serum was stored at −80 °C for subsequent assays.

For plasma exposure profiling, an additional 15 chickens were orally gavaged with mulberry anthocyanins at 1,500 mg/kg BW. Blood samples were collected into heparinized tubes at 15 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, 8 h, 12 h, and 24 h post-gavage with five randomly selected chickens per time point. Samples were centrifuged at 2,192 g for 10 min, then the plasma was harvested and stored at −80 °C.

Plasma anthocyanin determination by HPLC

Plasma C3G and C3R were quantified using HPLC equipped with a C18 column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm) maintained at 35°C with DAD detection at 520 nm. The mobile phases consisted of 3 % (v/v) formic acid in water (A) and 3 % (v/v) formic acid in acetonitrile (B). The flow rate was set to 0.8 mL/min, and the injection volume was 20 μL. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0 min, 93 % A; 25 min, 83 % A, followed by re-equilibration for 2 min.

For sample preparation, plasma was mixed with five volumes of acidified methanol (0.15 M HCl in methanol, HCl solution : methanol = 15 : 85, v/v), vortex-mixed, ultrasonicated at 27°C for 30 min, and centrifuged at 4,472 g for 10 min. The supernatant was evaporated to near dryness under nitrogen, reconstituted in 1 mL of the same acidified methanol, centrifuged again at 4,472 g for 10 min, and filtered through a 0.45 μm organic membrane prior to injection.

Slaughter performance and sample collection

After a 12-h feed withdrawal period, two chickens per replicate were randomly selected for carcass evaluation and sample collection. The selected chickens were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation performed by trained personnel, followed immediately by exsanguination (AVMA, 2024). Death was confirmed before carcass processing. After complete bleeding, the carcasses were defeathered and eviscerated according to standardized poultry carcass evaluation procedures, and the breast muscle, thigh muscle, abdominal fat and major visceral organs were separated and weighed individually. Cecal contents were aseptically collected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C for 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Carcass traits were recorded (n = 8), including pre-slaughter body weight, carcass weight, semi-eviscerated weight, eviscerated weight, as well as the weights of leg muscle, breast muscle, abdominal fat, and major organs. Semi-eviscerated weight was defined as the carcass weight after removal of the trachea, crop, esophagus, intestines, gallbladder, reproductive organs, gizzard contents and keratinized lining, spleen, and pancreas. Eviscerated weight was defined as the semi-eviscerated carcass weight after further removal of the gizzard, proventriculus, head, feet, heart, liver, lungs, and abdominal fat. After weighing, breast and leg muscle samples were collected.

16S rRNA gene sequencing and microbiota analysis

Total genomic DNA was extracted from cecal contents. For gut microbiota sampling, samples were collected from two chickens in each replicate, resulting in eight individual samples per treatment group. For 16S rRNA gene sequencing, six individual samples were randomly selected from the eight collected samples within each treatment group and pooled pairwise in equal proportions, generating three pooled samples per treatment group. The V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified, libraries were constructed, and sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform. Sequence data were processed in QIIME2 (Version QIIME2-202202), including quality filtering, denoising, and amplicon sequence variant (ASV) inference using DADA2. Taxonomic assignment was performed against the SILVA database (release [138.1]). The raw sequencing reads were submitted to the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) and are accessible under BioProject accession number PRJNA1440158.

Serum biochemical indices and antioxidant parameters

Serum triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and antioxidant/oxidative indices: total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), catalase (CAT), and malondialdehyde (MDA) were measured using commercial assay kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Meat quality measurements

Meat quality was measured using eight samples per group (n = 8). Muscle pH was measured in homogenates prepared by blending 1 g of muscle with 15 mL of distilled water using a homogenizer, followed by pH determination with a calibrated pH meter. Meat color was measured on the internal surface of the breast muscle after blooming at room temperature for 30 min. CIE L*, a*, and b* values were recorded using a spectrophotometer (CM-5, Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan). Three measurements were taken at different locations on each sample, and the mean value was used for statistical analysis.

Drip loss and cooking loss were determined using breast muscle samples according to previously described methods, with minor modifications. (Barbut, 2024; Song et al., 2023). After removal of the fascia and visibly bruised tissue, breast muscle samples of approximately 10 g were excised for each measurement. For drip loss, samples measuring approximately 2 × 1 × 6 cm were weighed as the initial weight (W1), suspended vertically in a sealed plastic container without contacting the wall or bottom, and stored at 4 °C for 24 h. Samples were then gently blotted dry and reweighed (W2). For cooking loss, samples measuring approximately 2 × 1 × 5 cm were weighed before cooking (W3), sealed in heat-resistant plastic bags and heated in a water bath (ZSBB-712, Shanghai Zhicheng Analytical Instrument Manufacturing Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 80°C until the internal temperature reached 72°C. After removal of skin, fascia, and visible connective tissue, breast and tight muscle samples were separately homogenized. Moisture, crude protein, and crude fat contents were determined using a near-infrared meat analyzer (Series 3000, MultiScan, Australia) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Near-infrared spectroscopy has previously been applied for the evaluation of poultry muscle composition (Khattak et al., 2025; Niu et al., 2024). Moisture, crude protein, and crude fat contents were expressed as percentages.

Samples of breast and tight muscles were separately homogenized for the determination of type I collagen, myoglobin, and thiobarbituric acid-reactive substances (TBARS). Type I collagen, myoglobin, and TBARS concentrations were expressed as μg/L, mg/mL, and μmol/gpro, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0. The normality of the residuals and homogeneity of variances were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. Treatment effects were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. When a significant treatment effect was detected, means were compared using Duncan’s multiple range test. Results are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SE). Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Antioxidant capacity

The effects of dietary MA supplementation on serum antioxidant capacities were shown in Table 4. At day 10, the activities of T-AOC and T-SOD in the MA_50 were much lower than Con (P < 0.05). Compared with the Con, GSH-Px activity was increased and MDA levels were decreased by dietary supplementation with MA (P < 0.05), while CAT activity was increased in the MA_100 and MA_200 (P < 0.05). At day 20, T-SOD activity was increased in the MA_100 and MA_200 (P < 0.05), CAT activity was increased in the MA_100, MA_200, and MA_400 (P < 0.05), and GSH-Px activity was increased in all MA-supplemented groups (P < 0.05), compared with Con. At day 30, T-AOC activity was increased in the MA_100 and MA_400 (P < 0.05), T-SOD activity was increased in all MA-supplemented groups (P < 0.05), GSH-Px activity was increased in the MA_400 (P < 0.05), and MDA levels were decreased in the MA_100 and MA_400 (P < 0.05), compared to the Con. These results suggested that dietary MA supplementation was associated with significantly enhanced serum antioxidant enzyme capacity and reduced lipid peroxidation in North China Chai Chickens.

Meat quality

Compared with Con, cooking loss was reduced in MA_50 (P < 0.05) and redness (a*) was increased in MA_100 (P < 0.05), whereas pH24h, drip loss, b* and L* were not significantly affected (Fig. 1). In breast muscle (Fig. 2), the MA_100 group had higher crude protein, moisture and myoglobin contents while lower crude fat content than Con (P < 0.05). TBARS values were reduced in MA_100, MA_200, and MA_400 (P < 0.05), and collagen content was increased in MA_100 and MA_200 (P < 0.05), compared to Con. As shown in Fig. 3, the crude fat content of the leg muscle was significantly lower in the MA_100 group, and TBARS values were significantly lower in the MA_100, MA_200, and MA_400 groups than in the control group (P < 0.05).

Gut microbiota

Venn diagrams were used to visualize shared and unique amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) among the five groups. A total of 787 ASVs were shared across all five groups, whereas unique ASVs were 227 in Con, and 233, 267, 267 and 292 unique in MA_50, MA_100, MA_200, and MA_400, respectively (Fig. 5A). The Shannon index was higher in MA_50 (P < 0.01) and MA_100 (P < 0.05) than in Con (Fig. 5B). The Simpson index was significantly elevated, with increased levels observed between Con and MA_50/MA_100 (P < 0.01), and between Con and MA_400 (P < 0.05). Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distances was used to assess the overall differences in community structure among groups (Fig. 5C). PC1 and PC2 explained 19.73 % and 9.80 % of the total variation, respectively. In the PCoA ordination, MA_50 formed a relatively compact cluster and showed separation from Con. MA_100 exhibited the most clear separation from the Con group with minimal overlap, whereas MA_200 partially overlapped with Con. MA_400 also showed separation from Con, although some overlap was observed. PERMANOVA confirmed a significant effect on the overall community (adjusted R² = 0.488, P = 0.001). The non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) ordination showed a stress value of 0.055 (< 0.2), indicating a good fit of the ordination (Fig. 5C). In the NMDS plot, MA_50 and MA_200 showed partial overlap with Con, whereas MA_100 showed the most distinct separation from the Con with the same overlap. Taken together with PERMANOVA, these ordination results suggest that MA supplementation, particularly at 100 mg/kg was associated with shifts in gut microbial community structure. At the phylum level, Bacteroidota and Firmicutes were dominant and together accounted for >90 % of total sequences (Fig. 5D). Compared with Con, Firmicutes showed a slight increase in all MA-supplemented groups, while Bacteroidota showed a slight decrease with an overall stable pattern across treatments (Fig. 5D). At the genus level, the relative abundance of uncultured_Muribaculaceae was higher in all MA-supplemented groups than in Con (Fig. 5D). LEfSe analysis (LDA score> 3.0) identified differentially enriched taxa among groups (Fig. 5F). Compared with Con, MA_100 was enriched in s_Lactobacillus aviarius and s_bacterium ic1395, as well as an unclassified g_unclassified Oscillospiraceae. MA_200 was enriched in CHKCI001-related taxa (s_unclassified CHKCI001 and g_CHKCI001). MA_400 showed enrichment of Akkermansia and its affiliated higher-level lineages (Fig. 5F).

Discussion

Dietary natural antioxidants have been investigated for their potential to support poultry health, productive performance and meat quality (Pasri et al., 2024; Serra et al., 2024). Carcass traits are commonly used to evaluate slaughter performance, and dressing percentage reflects carcass weight relative to live body weight, thereby serving as an indicator of carcass yield (Yuan et al., 2022). Previous studies have reported that anthocyanins may influence muscle protein synthesis and energy metabolism through signaling pathways such as PI3K/Akt/mTOR and AMPK, which are involved in anabolic regulation and metabolic homeostasis (Vergara Nieto et al., 2025). In the present study, dietary supplementation with MA increased the dressing percentage and leg muscle yield in North China Chai chickens, suggesting a shift toward improved carcass yield under the present conditions. However, the underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated, as no molecular endpoints of muscle protein synthesis or energy metabolism were assessed in this study. Serum biochemical indices reflected complementary information on metabolic status and tissue function in response to dietary interventions.

TG is a commonly used circulating lipid marker and is associated with dyslipidemia and cardiometabolic risk in mammals (Park et al., 2023). In an adipocyte model, an anthocyanin extract reduced intracellular levels of TG and cholesterol, consistent with inhibited lipid accumulation (Montaldo et al., 2023). Consistently, dietary MA supplementation significantly reduced serum TG levels in this study, supporting a potential effect under the current experimental conditions. Mechanistically, C3G has been reported to modulate peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPAR) signaling, which is involved in lipid homeostasis and hepatic fatty acid oxidation (Jia et al., 2020). ALT and AST are widely used indicators of hepatocellular injury and liver function (Aubrecht et al., 2025). In yellow-feathered broilers, anthocyanin supplementation attenuated lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced increases in serum TG, AST, and ALT (Wang et al., 2023). Here, serum AST significantly decreased in MA_50, whereas serum ALT decreased in MA_100, MA_200, and MA_400. These changes, together with the reduction in TG, suggested that MA may be associated with improved lipid-related indices and lower aminotransferase activities. However, direct evidence of hepatoprotection, such as liver histopathology or inflammatory/oxidative markers in hepatic tissue, was not assessed.

Oxidative stress and antioxidant defenses exist in a dynamic balance in vivo and disruption of this balance can contribute to tissue dysfunction and disease progression. Therefore, enhancing antioxidant defenses is important for maintaining cellular redox homeostasis (Yang et al., 2023). Anthocyanins are flavonoid polyphenols widely present in fruits and vegetables, which have been reported to scavenge reactive species and modulate antioxidant enzyme activities, thereby limiting oxidative damage in metabolic tissues (Maqsood et al., 2025). In Chishui black-bone chickens, dietary purple corn anthocyanins improved plasma antioxidant status (Luo et al., 2022). Similarly, dietary supplementation with an anthocyanin-rich Hibiscus extract significantly increased serum T-AOC and SOD activities, while decreasing MDA levels in chickens (Amer et al., 2022). T-AOC, SOD, GSH-Px, and CAT reflect the antioxidant defense status, whereas MDA is an indicator of lipid peroxidation and oxidative damage (Anwar et al., 2024; do Nascimento et al., 2025; Guan et al., 2017; Handy and Loscalzo, 2022; Jomova et al., 2024; Pedone et al., 2020). Antioxidants have been proposed to activate nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) signaling, thereby upregulating antioxidant genes and mitigating oxidative stress-related tissue damage (Pasri et al., 2024). In the present study, dietary MA increased serum T-AOC and the activities of SOD, GSH-Px, and CAT, while decreasing MDA. Collectively, these changes suggested enhanced systemic antioxidant defenses and reduced lipid peroxidation under the present conditions. This systemic antioxidant improvement may contribute to the lower TBARS observed in muscle.

Dietary natural antioxidants are of particular interest for improving animal-derived meat products because they can enhance oxidative stability and sensory quality while supporting animal health (Deng et al., 2024). Cooking loss reflects the loss of water and soluble components from muscle during heating and is commonly used as an indicator of cooking yield. During cooking, heat-induced protein denaturation and shrinkage promote water expulsion; together with fat loss, these changes contribute to cooking loss. Accordingly, cooking loss is often used to assess cooking-related quality attributes, including juiciness and tenderness (Pematilleke et al., 2022). In the present study, MA supplementation reduced cooking loss in breast muscle, suggesting improved water-holding capacity during heating. One plausible explanation is that enhanced antioxidant defenses may limit protein oxidation, thereby helping preserve membrane integrity, myofibrillar structures and ultimately improving moisture retention during heating (Deng et al., 2024). Given the improved systemic antioxidant status observed in this study, the reduction in cooking loss may be partly attributable to enhanced antioxidant capacity. Redness (a*) is a key meat color parameter; together with lightness (L*) and yellowness (b*), it contributes to the visual attributes that influence consumers' perception of freshness and acceptability (Sun et al., 2024). Redness is closely linked to oxidative processes in muscle: decreases in a* often coincide with increased lipid oxidation as reflected by elevated TBARS values and protein oxidation (Wang et al., 2021). TBARS are widely used as an index of lipid oxidation and are informative for evaluating meat oxidative stability and potential shelf life (Ghani et al., 2017; Possamai et al., 2018). TBARS values were negatively correlated with thiol group content, supporting crosstalk between lipid and protein oxidation. In poultry, anthocyanin-rich grape pomace supplementation has been shown to improve meat oxidative stability by reducing TBARS values (Liu et al., 2018; Turcu et al., 2020). Consistent with these reports, MA supplementation markedly reduced TBARS values in chicken muscle in the present study, supporting decreased lipid oxidation; this may also be related to the higher a* value observed in breast muscle. This interpretation is further supported by the increased myoglobin content observed in the MA100 group, as myoglobin is a major determinant of meat redness. Moisture, crude protein and crude fat are key components that determine the nutritional value of meat (Wei et al., 2025). Meat with high-protein and low-fat contents is generally preferred by health-conscious consumers (Ivanković, et al., 2025). In this study, MA_100 exhibited significantly higher moisture and crude protein contents as well as lower crude fat in breast muscle than Con. This change may be related to the reported ability of anthocyanins to modulate activating signaling pathways involved in muscle protein synthesis (MPS), including the mTOR pathway and its downstream effectors (Vergara Nieto Á, et al., 2025). Anthocyanins have also been reported to alleviate exercise-induced muscle damage, fatigue and inflammation, which may indirectly contribute to improved muscle water retention (Kimble et al., 2023). Evidence from clinical and animal studies suggests that anthocyanin intake may influence energy expenditure and lipid storage through multiple pathways, including modulation of adipose tissue activity and gut microbiota. Accordingly, anthocyanin supplementation has been associated with reduced body fat percentage and improved obesity-related indices in non-avian models (Khan et al., 2024; Oumeddour et al., 2024; Vannuchi et al., 2025). A similar lipid-modulating effect may occur in poultry and could partly explain the reduced crude fat content observed in the MA_100 group. In the thigh muscle, crude fat content was reduced, whereas moisture and crude protein contents remained unchanged. This tissue-specific response may be related to differences in muscle fiber composition: breast muscle is predominantly composed of fast-twitch glycolytic fibers, whereas thigh muscle contains a higher proportion of oxidative fibers, which may differ in metabolic responsiveness to dietary antioxidants (Huo et al., 2022). Myoglobin is a key determinant of meat color, and myoglobin content is generally associated with improved redness and color stability (Liu et al., 2021). In the present study, myoglobin content in breast muscle was increased in MA_100, which may help explain the higher a* value observed in this group. Similarly, Muzolf-Panek et al. reported that supplementation of pig diets with 0.1 % phenolic additives increased meat redness and myoglobin content, highlighting the potential role of antioxidant protection in color preservation and myoglobin stability (Muzolf-Panek et al., 2024). Collagen is a major extracellular matrix protein that contributes to structural support and mechanical strength in animal tissues (Park et al., 2021). In this study, collagen content in breast muscle was also increased in the MA_100 group. This increase may be related to the antioxidant capacity of anthocyanins, which could help protect collagen from oxidative degradation and maintain collagen integrity. In addition, anthocyanin-related stabilization mechanisms, such as interactions with collagen-derived peptides, may contribute to collagen retention in meat (Ren et al., 2025). Collectively, these findings suggest that MA supplementation may be a promising dietary strategy for improving selected meat quality traits in North China Chai chickens.

The gut microbiota plays an important role in poultry growth performance, nutrient metabolism, immune function and resistance to microbial infections. Microbial composition can be influenced by multiple environmental and management factors, including biosecurity level, housing conditions, litter, feed access and climate (Kers et al., 2018). Plant-derived feed additives have been shown to modulate the gut microbial communities, alleviate intestinal disorders and promote gut health (Wang et al., 2024a, 2024b). Liu et al. reported that oral anthocyanins altered gut microbial composition by maintaining an anaerobic intestinal environment, promoting secretion of secretory IgA and antimicrobial peptides, and downregulating bacterial motility and mobile genetic elements (Liu et al., 2022). In mice, anthocyanins have been reported to alleviate LPS-induced inflammation through activation of FXR/TGR5 signaling and modulation of the gut microbiota (Zhou et al., 2023). Alpha diversity reflects the richness and evenness of microbial communities within a sample and is commonly assessed using indices such as Shannon and Simpson (Thukral et al., 2019). In the present study, alpha diversity differed between Con and MA_50/MA_100 groups, suggesting that MA supplementation increased cecal microbial diversity in North China Chai chickens. Beta diversity reflects differences in microbial community composition among groups and is commonly assessed using PCoA and NMDS (Jing et al., 2024). The beta diversity results, particularly the distinct separation of MA_100, further suggest that MA supplementation was associated with a shift in gut microbial community structure. Taxonomic profiling showed a slight increase in the relative abundance of Firmicutes and a slight decrease in Bacteroidota, while the overall microbial community remained relatively stable. This shift may be related to changes in the intestinal microenvironment induced by anthocyanin-derived metabolites (Yue and Han, 2022). LEfSe analysis indicated enrichment of Lactobacillus aviarius in MA_100; members of the genus Lactobacillus are generally regarded as beneficial bacteria in poultry gut ecosystems. Similarly, black rice anthocyanins have been reported to promote the proliferation of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus (Zhu et al., 2018). In MA_400, Akkermansiaceae-related taxa were enriched. Akkermansia muciniphila colonizes the gut mucus layer and has been associated with intestinal barrier function and mucosal health (Grenda et al., 2022; Ioannou et al., 2025). In a DSS-induced colitis mouse model, anthocyanin intervention increased Verrucomicrobiota-related taxa, including Akkermansiaceae (Ren et al., 2023). Taken together, the diversity and differential taxa analyses suggest that MA supplementation may reshape cecal microbial ecology and promote the enrichment of potentially beneficial taxa, including Lactobacillus- and Akkermansia-related lineages. These microbial changes may also be linked to improvements in selected meat quality traits, although causal relationships remain to be established. Several studies have shown that different Lactobacillus species may improve meat quality by regulating the intestinal microbiota, modulating lipid metabolism or influencing the muscle transcriptome (Lin et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2022a, 2024). However, because short-chain fatty acids, intestinal pH, and barrier-related markers were not measured, the functional consequences of these microbial shifts require further validation. In addition, because gut microbiota sequencing was performed using three pooled samples per treatment group, the statistical resolution for detecting subtle microbial differences may be limited. Future studies including more independent microbiota sequencing samples would help further clarify the effects of MA on gut microbial composition.

Most studies on plasma anthocyanin exposure and metabolism have focused on humans and rodents, whereas data in chickens remain limited. In a pharmacokinetic study of bilberry anthocyanins in rats, plasma concentrations reached peak levels approximately 30 min after administration (Baron et al., 2017). In the present study, C3G and C3R were detectable in chicken plasma at 15 min after oral gavage with MA, reached maximum concentrations at 30 min, and then gradually declined to undetectable levels by 24 h. These results indicate that MA-derived anthocyanins are rapidly absorbed, have short systemic exposure, and undergo rapid metabolic clearance in chickens. In addition, target anthocyanin monomers were analyzed in plasma and muscle after dietary supplementation, but they were not detected in either matrix after 30 days of MA supplementation. This may be partly attributed to tissue and plasma concentrations below the analytical limit of detection. It may also be explained by in vivo conjugation, degradation, and microbial metabolism, through which anthocyanins can be rapidly converted into metabolites such as phenolic acids. Specifically, gut microbiota and host metabolic processes can catabolize anthocyanins into phenolic acids and other metabolites with biological activities, including regulation of lipid metabolism, inhibition of inflammation, and enhancement of antioxidant defenses (Victoria-Campos et al., 2024; Winter and Bickford, 2019). Given evidence from mammalian models that anthocyanins may regulate glucose and lipid metabolism, and activate antioxidant defense through the AMPK-related pathway, future studies should integrate transcriptomic and proteomic approaches to examine the effects of MA on key metabolic and antioxidant pathways, including AMPK, PPARγ and Nrf2 in chicken muscle (Garcia and Blesso, 2021; Herrera-Balandrano et al., 2021). Such work would help determine whether MA regulates lipolysis, fatty acid oxidation and antioxidant enzyme expression in avian muscle. Therefore, future studies using LC-MS/MS-based metabolomics are needed to identify anthocyanin-derived metabolites in plasma, intestinal contents, and muscle.

Overall, under the present experimental conditions, dietary supplementation with MA was associated with improved slaughter performance, enhanced serum antioxidant status, improved meat quality traits, and altered cecal microbial profiles in late-laying North China Chai chickens. The oral gavage experiment showed that C3G and C3R appeared rapidly in plasma, peaked at 30 min, and declined to undetectable levels by 24 h, suggesting a short systemic exposure window. Among the tested doses, 100 mg/kg MA showed the most consistent overall responses. Therefore, 100 mg/kg MA may be considered a potential dietary supplementation level for improving selected meat quality traits in late-laying North China Chai chickens. Further studies under commercial production conditions are warranted to validate its long-term effects and economic feasibility.
