A Review of Theory: Comparing Invasion Ecology and Climate Change‐Induced Range Shifting
Authors: Haley D Flickinger, Jeffrey S Dukes
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman actions have led to large‐scale shifts in the distributions of species, which have accelerated over recent decades. Two contributing factors include the introduction of non‐native species to new regions, and more recently, the shift of species into new ranges to track a human‐altered climate. While the context of these species redistributions is different, we argue here that scientists studying the effects of either invasion or range shifting are interested in the same underlying mechanistic questions: (1) What traits make a species likely to survive in a new location? and (2) Which recipient ecosystems are likely to support a newly arrived species? A survey of the theoretical literature surrounding these topics demonstrates the usefulness of this comparison and highlights key differences between range shifting and invasion in factors including genetic diversity, climatic tolerance, local adaptation, and coevolutionary history. This review does not support the blanket application of an invasion framework to climate change‐induced range shifts. However, we suggest the use of modified invasion theories, experimental designs, and risk assessments could aid in predicting outcomes and prioritizing management resources for climate‐threatened species.Keywords: climate change, conservation, invasion, native, non‐native, range shift, theory

Introduction

Humanity's increasing global connectedness and carbon emissions have irreversibly changed the distribution of species around the world. As we pollute the atmosphere with heat‐trapping gases, changes in temperature and precipitation are causing species to move beyond their historic geographic range boundaries (Table 1). Due to the consistent warming trend caused by anthropogenic climate change, around 80% of examined latitudinal range shifts are toward the poles while 75% of elevational shifts are toward higher elevations; places where temperatures are cooler (Chen et al. 2011; Parmesan and Yohe 2003; Thomas 2010). Meta‐analyses across hundreds of species have found average rates of poleward shifts ranging from 6.1 to 24.9 km per decade and average rates of elevational shifts around 12 m per decade (Chen et al. 2011; Hickling et al. 2006). The temperature change of a 1‐km increase in latitude is considered approximately equal to a 1‐m increase in elevation (Parmesan and Yohe 2003). The findings of these meta‐analyses suggest that climate change is the primary cause of range shifting for the majority of species considered.

Studies of range shifting focus largely on species that are shifting or will need to shift to survive the changing climate, rather than focusing on recipient ecosystems. Specifically, they estimate the abiotic conditions suitable for survival, known as the “climate envelope” of a species. Modelers then identify the locations of those abiotic conditions in future climate projections to map the potential future range of the species. The applicability of these maps is limited as they often do not account for biotic interactions, life‐history traits, or habitat fragmentation, all of which can impact a species' ability to track its climatic envelope (Araújo and Peterson 2012). Invasion ecology, however, often addresses questions of changing biotic interactions (Morriën et al. 2010). Lessons about biotic factors from invasion ecology could thus improve predictions of the rate, distance, and success of a species' spread.

The literature has numerous, often conflicting, terms to describe where a species originated and what the impact of a newly arrived species has been (Blackburn et al. 2011; Colautti and MacIsaac 2004; Courchamp et al. 2017; Richardson, Pyšek, and Carlton 2011). The terms as defined for this review enable comparison between invasion and range shifting (see Glossary, Table 1; Figure 1).

Several reviews have argued invasion ecology may provide useful insights into the future of climate change‐induced range shifting (Caplat et al. 2013; Essl et al. 2019; Hoffmann and Courchamp 2016; Nackley et al. 2017; Wallingford et al. 2020). However, these reviews have often been met with strong criticism (Sagoff 2020; Tong, Wang, and Chen 2018; Urban 2020; Wilson 2020; Wilson et al. 2016). There are three main arguments against viewing range shifting through an invasion lens. First, when compared to non‐native species, the incidence of native species becoming invasive (specifically through harming the recipient ecosystem) in the literature is relatively low (Simberloff et al. 2012). Second, describing native species as invaders can evoke a one‐sided view of native range shifters that frames them as the “enemy” (Tong, Wang, and Chen 2018). Third, if native range‐shifting species are unable to track the changing climate because managers treat them as invasive and prevent their movement, the native range‐shifting species could go extinct, leading to a loss of native biodiversity (Urban 2020). In this review, we do not argue that an invasion framework can be applied directly or uncritically to climate change‐induced range shifting. Instead, we compare range shifting and invasion at a mechanistic level to evaluate the possibilities and limitations of using invasion theory to understand range shifts.

The existing invasion literature is broadly focused on two main questions: (1a) What traits enable a species to invade a certain ecosystem? And, (2a) What makes the recipient ecosystem invasible by a species? When asked in more general terms, the questions can be used to predict outcomes of range shifting: (1b) What traits influence whether a species survives in a new location? And, (2b) What properties of a recipient ecosystem make it likely to support a newly arrived species?

Species redistributions, whether natural or human‐mediated, carry risks to both the moving species and to its recipient ecosystem (Figure 2). In the range‐shifting context, the conservation priority is often the moving species, while in the invasion context, the recipient ecosystem is the conservation priority. Some risks to the range‐shifting species can include inability to move at a rate that keeps pace with climatic changes, poor adaptation to non‐climatic conditions in the new range, and disruptions of biotic interactions. For climate‐threatened populations that are unlikely to be able to track the changing climate on their own, assisted migration has been discussed as a conservation strategy. Assisted migration involves humans collecting individuals from their current range and moving them to the predicted future location of their climatic envelope (Twardek et al. 2023). Thus, assisted migration may be important to avoid the extinction of climate‐threatened species. However, it may also lead to unknown consequences for the recipient ecosystem, such as those caused by invasive species (McLachlan, Hellmann, and Schwartz 2007). The introduction of new competitors, modification of abiotic conditions, and changes in other community dynamics caused by assisted migration could lead to the local decline of resident species (Carey et al. 2012). More than 60 years of invasion ecology focused on predicting harms to recipient communities can help us to develop informed conservation plans for climate‐threatened species (Wallingford et al. 2020).

Here, we consider only the local spread stage of invasion to allow for a more direct comparison to range shifting. We identify six different theories that apply to both invasion and range shifting. These theories help to highlight the primary factors that differ between non‐native invasions and climate change‐induced range shifting, enabling the informed application of invasion knowledge to range‐shifting predictions.

Stages of Invasion and Range Shifting

To gain a better understanding of how invasion relates to range shifting, it is useful to first go over what are commonly considered the four stages of invasion: transport, introduction, establishment, and spread (Figure 2; Table 1) (Lambrinos 2004; Theoharides and Dukes 2007). In the transport stage, human activity moves individuals of a species across a geographical gap that is beyond their natural dispersal ability to a new location where they are now non‐native. Next, in the introduction stage, the individuals escape or are released into the wild in the new location, experience the local abiotic conditions, and at least some individuals survive. The establishment stage occurs when introduced individuals experience the local biotic factors (e.g., competition, herbivory, pathogens), survive, and successfully reproduce. The spread stage is when offspring from the established population travel into a connected range nearby. This produces additional populations in the expanded range, making the species a non‐native invader. The spread stage mirrors that of invasion overall, except that in the spread stage, transport occurs locally through either natural dispersal or human actions, while in the initial transport stage, a non‐native species is transported by human actions alone. Therefore, for the purposes of this review, the spread stage of invasion has been broken down into substages described as local transport, local introduction, and local establishment (Figure 2). This review will focus on spread, as it is when the recipient ecosystem is most at risk from the non‐native species and is the most comparable to the more localized process of range shifting.

While the cause of redistribution is different for non‐native invasion versus range shifting, the two phenomena have many similar processes and impacts (Caplat et al. 2013; Essl et al. 2019; Hoffmann and Courchamp 2016; Morehouse and Tobler 2013; Nackley et al. 2017). Invasion is directly facilitated by humans either intentionally (e.g., direct stocking of fishes, horticulture trade) or unintentionally (e.g., pathogens, stowaways), while range shifting, as we define it here, is primarily driven by climate change (Bradley et al. 2012; Hulme et al. 2008; van Kleunen et al. 2015). Furthermore, range shifting takes place over shorter distances into recipient ecosystems connected to their historic ranges. For these reasons, native range‐shifting species do not undergo transport, introduction, and establishment in the same way as non‐native invasive species. Yet, both native range‐shifting and non‐native invasive species undergo the same substages of spread (Figure 2). In the local transport stage, non‐native invasive species and native range‐shifting species move into a connected range via natural dispersal or human actions. Then, during local introduction, non‐native invaders and native range shifters begin to experience conditions in a new, nearby location. Finally, in local establishment, both range shifters and non‐native invaders produce offspring in the recipient ecosystem, creating a self‐sustaining population in a new location.

As the stages of invasion progress, the group most at risk of local extinction transitions from the non‐native species to the native species (Figure 2). It is not until the spread stage that the non‐native species truly threatens the recipient ecosystem. A hypothetical example from the horticulture trade gives context to this concept (van Kleunen et al. 2018): When transporting a plant to be sold in a nursery on the other side of the world, only the plant is at risk. It may be smashed, frozen, or otherwise killed, but since it has not yet been introduced, the plant poses no harm to any native species. Following arrival at a nursery, the plant is purchased and may be introduced outdoors. During the introduction stage, the non‐native plant remains the most at risk as conditions in the garden may be too different from the environment to which it is adapted for it to survive and reproduce. But, if the non‐native plant produces a self‐sustaining population, thus entering the establishment phase, and begins to compete with native plants for space and resources, the risk moves from non‐native to native species. Ultimately, in the spread stage, the risk falls increasingly on the native species as the non‐native plant has produced new, competitive populations that spread out over a larger area. When the non‐native plant is able to compete with native species at this scale, thereby negatively impacting biodiversity and ecosystem function, the plant has reached the status of non‐native invader. Here, the spread stage of invasion is the focus for comparison to range shifting, because its process mirrors that of range shifting and spread is the stage when the risk of species redistribution to biodiversity and ecosystem function is fully realized.

Theories of Species Redistribution

The current literature comparing native and non‐native range shifters to non‐native invaders is almost entirely theoretical. Through an extensive survey of the literature, six theories were selected (Table 2) based on their perceived relevance to both range shifting and invasion, as well as their prevalence in the literature. Here, we consider how theories may be applied to both processes (Figure 3), highlighting the need to consider the potential risks of redistribution to both the species and their recipient ecosystems when developing management plans.

Range Boundary Determinants

The location of range boundaries for each species is dependent on both abiotic and biotic factors in the environment. The theory of spatial sorting provides one explanation for how the location of the front (or advancing) range edge is determined, while demographic compensation explains why the trailing (or retreating) edge of a range may lag. Together, these theories inform our understanding of local transport and why populations at the front range edge track the rate of climate change more closely than populations at the trailing edge.

Spatial Sorting

The theory of spatial sorting describes the rapid evolution of increased dispersal ability in individuals at the advancing edge of a range and the resulting gradient of dispersal traits throughout the range (Shine, Brown, and Phillips 2011; Travis and Dytham 2002). This is in accordance with the central‐marginal hypothesis, which says individuals at the range edge are genetically differentiated from individuals in the range core and are less genetically diverse. These genetic differences are derived as natural selection acts on traits needed for survival at the edge of the population's climatic tolerance (Pironon et al. 2017). In spatial sorting, the strong selection for dispersal ability is the result of fast‐dispersing individuals accumulating at the front range edge then mating with other fast dispersers. An empirical example of spatial sorting comes from non‐native invasive cane toads ( Bufo marinus ) at the front range edge in Australia. Compared to cane toads from the range core, front‐edge toads have evolved longer limbs (Hudson, Brown, and Shine 2016) as well as a genetically based behavior to disperse in straighter lines (Brown, Phillips, and Shine 2014; Lindström et al. 2013). Both traits contribute to an increased rate of dispersal. Another empirical example comes from non‐native invasive Japanese honeysuckle ( Lonicera japonica ), which was found to be larger near the front range edge than in the range core, enabling increased vegetative spread (Kilkenny and Galloway 2013). In both examples, a spatial gradient of dispersal traits is created by strong selection for dispersal ability at the front range edge followed by assortative mating.

Much of the existing empirical evidence for spatial sorting comes from non‐native invaders that are range shifting rather than from native species (Table 3). Studies of spatial sorting in native populations of lodgepole pines ( Pinus contorta ) and salamanders ( Plethodon vehiculum ) have examined fossils and genetic data spanning the climatic changes since the last glacial maximum (around 20,000 years ago) (Cwynar and MacDonald 1987; Pelletier and Carstens 2016). Differences in time scale and methodology of these studies make it difficult to compare rates of spatial sorting in non‐native and native populations. However, theory suggests the number of repeated introductions in the new range is likely to be higher for a nearby native species than for a non‐native species. As a result, non‐native invaders can experience a founder's effect upon introduction, causing a large loss of genetic diversity and resulting in a reduced ability to evolve new traits (Dlugosch and Parker 2008). In contrast, native range shifters are likely to be more genetically diverse and have more adaptive potential, leading to greater spatial sorting. Realistic predictions of dispersal in both native and non‐native populations require that we consider the impacts of genetic diversity and rapid trait evolution in addition to the changing climatic conditions.

Demographic Compensation

The theory of demographic compensation details how changes in a population's vital rates, such as survival, growth, reproduction, and recruitment, are countered by inverse changes in opposing vital rates (Doak and Morris 2010; Villellas et al. 2015). In some populations, more frequent climatic extremes may reduce survival rates, while opposing vital rates such as recruitment may increase to maintain population size. In range‐shifting species, demographic compensation has been shown to buffer the trailing edge from local extinction (García‐Camacho, Albert, and Escudero 2012; Sheth and Angert 2018). Doak and Morris (2010) found that decreases in survival and recruitment were balanced by increases in growth for southern edge populations of two native North American tundra plants ( Silene acaulis and Polygonum viviparum ), enabling the plants to remain in place despite environmental changes. This demographic plasticity is an effective strategy for responding to year‐to‐year weather variation, but there is concern that it will only delay the inevitable in a future where the conditions never return to their historic averages. Studies have suggested there is likely a climatic tipping point where demographic compensation will no longer sufficiently buffer populations, so they may experience either sudden range shifts or local extinctions (Doak and Morris 2010; Sheth and Angert 2018). Understanding these vital rate dynamics can help us understand how species persist in place and the risks of that persistence.

Demographic compensation is rarely discussed in the context of non‐native invasions because these populations are often actively spreading with no stable population size to maintain (Table 3) (Guo 2014). Additionally, non‐native invaders tend to have broader climatic tolerances than native species, making them less vulnerable to environmental changes (Bradley et al. 2024). Yet, these broad generalizations do not apply to all non‐native species and may become less important as populations stabilize and adapt to local conditions with increased time since introduction. Garlic mustard ( A. petiolata ), for example, has been well established in the United States and model predictions suggest there is demographic compensation; the vital rates of survival and growth increase when in low light conditions and seed production increases in higher light conditions (Merow et al. 2017). These responses lead to similar population growth across light conditions that enables the population to remain in place despite variable conditions. This is important as garlic mustard establishment in new locations is hindered by climate warming. Although the failure of a non‐native species to shift with the climate is of less conservation concern, understanding the roles of demographic compensation and climatic tolerance may improve the predictions of future ranges and spread rates for both native and non‐native populations.

Ecosystem Invasibility

When a species has shifted its boundaries, it enters the local introduction stage of spread. If the conditions of the recipient ecosystem permit establishment of a newly arrived species, it is considered invasible. In the invasion literature, there has been extensive discussion of the invasion paradox (Fridley et al. 2007) and Darwin's naturalization conundrum (Darwin 1859) as predictors of invasibility. These frameworks are not included here as they indirectly and inconsistently describe invasibility (Belote et al. 2008; Fan et al. 2023; Shea and Chesson 2002; Tilman 2004). Instead, this section describes how resource availability, niche space, and competitive ability can act as filters to the establishment of newly arrived species.

Fluctuating Resource Hypothesis

The fluctuating resource hypothesis states a community is more invasible when more resources are available for the newly arrived species to use (Davis, Grime, and Thompson 2000). Resources available in a community vary over space and time due to weather, human inputs, and disturbances that release resources into the environment. The local spread of populations may lag until an influx of resources enables the new range to support additional individuals. For example, the majority of seedlings of the spreading invasive plant Bunias orientalis sprouted from resource‐rich mole hill disturbances rather than from control plots without mole hills. The influx of resources promoted growth, acting as a gateway to further invasion in the region (Kiełtyk and Mirek 2015). Similarly, flying squirrels ( Glaucomys volans and Glaucomys sabrinus ) native to Maine are range shifting at irregular intervals, with models suggesting food availability in the new range is a significant contributor to the timing (Wood, Witham, and Hunter 2016). As fast‐growing species, both the invasive plant and native squirrel are able to take advantage of the influxes in resources. However, species with slower growth traits are less likely to be influenced by short‐term fluctuations as they acquire resources at a relatively slow, steady rate. While both non‐native and native species can have fast or slow growth traits, fast growth traits are common in successful invaders as they enable non‐native populations to acquire resources and spread quickly in their introduced range (Capellini et al. 2015; Montesinos 2022). Resource availability is critical to the growth, survival, and reproduction of all organisms, native or non‐native. But, the fast‐growing traits common to non‐native invaders may enable them to benefit more from short‐term increases in resources than climate‐threatened native species adapted to local baseline resource levels.

Modern Coexistence Theory

Modern coexistence theory describes how a community maintains biodiversity through the coexistence of multiple species. Based on a mathematical model, the theory suggests that the ability of species to remain in the same community depends on their niche requirements as well as their competitive ability. If species fill different niches, individuals will primarily compete for resources within species, allowing species to coexist in the community. But, if different species fill the same niche, there will be competition between the species, and the one with a competitive advantage will exclude the other (Chesson 2000). Although modern coexistence theory was not developed specifically for the contexts of non‐native invasion or range shifting, it is described in terms of invasion analysis. The term “invader” in this theory refers to a species that exists at low density in a community. A low‐density species is said to “invade” when it becomes less rare in the community. In the context of this review, then, “invader” correlates to both the non‐native invader and the native range shifter as they are at relatively low densities when initially introduced to recipient communities. This enables the use of modern coexistence theory to predict the biodiversity outcomes of native and non‐native species introductions.

The theoretical literature applying modern coexistence theory to non‐native invasions has existed almost as long as the theory has (Shea and Chesson 2002). Yet, direct empirical application remained limited until a 2014 study demonstrated the role of both phenological niche differentiation and competitive ability in the per capita population growth rate of competing plants (Godoy and Levine 2014). For range‐shifting species, the only modern coexistence theory literature identified included one theoretical paper and one empirical paper from the same authors (Table 4) (Usinowicz and Levine 2018). The empirical study considered how competitive relationships among three native alpine species are changing as range shifting affects their range overlap and climate change affects their competitive ability. Using the model of coexistence theory, they predicted one of the three species will not persist under climate change (Usinowicz and Levine 2021). Both empirical examples described above feature small‐scale field experiments designed specifically to parameterize coexistence models. This method provides detailed predictions for individual species that can guide conservation efforts in the region. Threatened native species can be protected, while invasive species expected to persist can be prioritized for management, especially if they further threaten native species. Modern coexistence theory's emphasis on species interactions allows for it to be useful in both non‐native invasion and native range‐shifting contexts.

Modified Biotic Interactions

The local establishment of a species is heavily influenced by interactions between the species and its recipient community. While it is possible for a species to be introduced alongside others from its historic range, the entire community does not move together. The change in community composition results in changes in biotic interactions (Morriën et al. 2010). In this section, we present two theories that explain how these modified biotic interactions may be beneficial to a non‐native species or a native range‐shifting species, even enabling a species to go from non‐problematic to invasive.

Enemy Release Hypothesis

The enemy release hypothesis states that when a species arrives in a new community, it is freed from the specialist enemies (consumers and pathogens) of its historic range. Other species in the recipient community are not free of their enemies, so the newly introduced species gains a competitive advantage (Keane and Crawley 2002). Invasion is distinguished from range shifting by the distance between historic and recipient communities, which influences the degree of enemy release experienced. There is extensive empirical literature supporting the contribution of enemy release to the successful establishment of non‐native invaders in new communities; indeed, the entire field of biocontrol is based on this premise (Table 5). Non‐native species are introduced beyond the range where they could naturally disperse, meaning their specialist enemies are unlikely to disperse over that distance to follow them. For example, grey mullet fish in their introduced range have only one‐fifteenth as many helminth parasites as in their native range (Sarabeev, Balbuena, and Morand 2017). Range‐shifting native species, on the other hand, primarily disperse locally where their enemies may already exist or be able to disperse naturally. Even so, enemy release has been demonstrated in the short‐distance moves of some range‐shifting species (Engelkes et al. 2008; Katz and Ibáñez 2016; Menéndez et al. 2008). For instance, the range‐expanding Brown Argus butterfly (Aricia agestis) has escaped parasitoids without leaving the parasitoid's range, as the butterfly has expanded into an area where the parasitoid relies on a different host (Menéndez et al. 2008). Enemy release across short distances could enable greater rates of establishment success for native range shifters than would be possible without release from coevolved enemies.

Invasional Meltdown Theory

Finally, the invasional meltdown theory posits that after a species successfully invades a new ecosystem, it can facilitate invasions by other species (Simberloff and Von Holle 1999). One example is the mutualistic interactions between non‐native invasive ants and non‐native invasive snails on Christmas Island. The ants facilitated the invasion of snails by feeding on the native crabs that were in direct competition with the invading snails (Green et al. 2011). There is evidence for the meltdown phenomenon in several different invaded systems (Table 5).

Research on invasional meltdown has unsurprisingly focused on non‐native invaders. Yet, for climate‐threatened native species, the relatively short distance from historic range to recipient ecosystem and the coevolved relationships with local species lead to increased potential for mutual facilitation (Cosmo et al. 2023). For example, range‐shifting Rhododendrons may form novel mutualisms with the soil biota of other nearby Rhododendron species (Mueller et al. 2022). As climate change leads to shifting ranges, there is increased potential for multiple native range‐shifting species to move into the same recipient community, creating the conditions for invasional meltdown. The result could be framed either as conservation of biotic interactions for range‐shifting species or as a compounding threat to the recipient ecosystem. However, whether the conservation priority is the climate‐threatened population or the recipient community, the invasional meltdown theory emphasizes the power of species interactions to drive an outcome.

Conclusions

Species redistributions can have significant implications for the maintenance of biodiversity and ecosystem processes. The introduction of a species, whether native or non‐native, adds a species to the recipient ecosystem but may also lead to a decrease in diversity of existing species (Nackley et al. 2017). Furthermore, the new species combinations that result from redistribution can lead to changing species interactions that impact ecosystem processes. On the other hand, due to climate change, a lack of redistribution can also harm biodiversity and ecosystems. Climate‐threatened species that fail to track the changing climate may go locally extinct, thus decreasing biodiversity and changing species interactions in the historic range.

The comparison of range shifting to invasion has been considered by some to be an oversimplification with the potential to harm conservation efforts for climate‐threatened species (Urban 2020). However, there is also potential harm in making assisted‐migration decisions without fully considering biotic interactions (Van der Putten 2012). Invasion ecology has long examined the impacts of novel species interactions that are given relatively little consideration in the range‐shifting literature (Morriën et al. 2010). Here, we examined six theories of species movements that apply to range shifting, nearly all developed for the field of invasion ecology with a focus on mechanisms of biotic interactions (Figure 3). The narrow focus on mechanisms involved in the local species movements of native and non‐native species reveals the key differences between invasion and range shifting that influence outcomes: genetic diversity, climatic tolerance, local adaptation, and coevolutionary history. The higher genetic diversity of native populations facilitates spatial sorting. The narrower climatic tolerance of native species leads to demographic compensation for declining vital rates. The local adaptation of native species to limited resources can prevent them from benefitting from fluctuations in resource availability. The biotic interactions between native species and their coevolved enemies can hinder establishment while interactions with coevolved mutualists can support establishment. With these in mind, the invasion framework that was developed over decades to predict invasive traits and ecosystem invasibility can be modified to improve predictions of range‐shifting outcomes.

Given limited time and resources, empirical methods and risk assessments for invasion can be used as a starting point for developing informed and effective conservation plans for climate‐threatened species. Experimentally, by including native pest species in competition studies, invasion ecologists can use well‐established methods to compare the outcomes of introducing native species to those of introducing non‐native invasive species. When analyzing data, known native range‐shifting species could be considered separately from other native species. By adding this category, more can be learned about the relative roles of natives, native range shifters, and non‐native invaders in an ecosystem. Furthermore, using a modified version of the Environmental Impact Classification for Invasive Alien Taxa (EICAT), conservation biologists could improve predictions of the effects of novel interactions between native species (IUCN 2020; Wallingford et al. 2020). A study by Salva and Bradley is an example of how EICAT can be used regionally to identify the non‐native species likely to shift their ranges and impact recipient communities (Salva and Bradley 2023). This methodology, if adjusted to focus on native species likely to shift ranges, could highlight potentially problematic species. Species considered for assisted migration could also undergo an EICAT assessment to predict environmental impacts and risks of their introduction. This would enable the use of assisted migration as a conservation technique in the face of climate change while minimizing unforeseen harm to recipient ecosystems. Invasion theories, experimental designs, and assessment techniques can inform the management of native range‐shifting species and help decrease the biodiversity losses caused by our own activities and emissions.
