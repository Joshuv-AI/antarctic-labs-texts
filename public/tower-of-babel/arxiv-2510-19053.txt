Invariant Theory Beyond Reductivity: Hilbert and Schwarz Theorems for Discrete Lorentz and Cocompact Groups Email address: leandro.oliveira@ufscar.br
Leandro Nery

Abstract
Classical invariant theory provides a complete framework for reductive and compact Lie groups, but the non-reductive and non-compact cases are significantly more complex and less completely understood. This work investigates two distinct regimes: discrete subgroups of the Lorentz group O(n,1) acting on ℝ n , 1 \mathbb{R}^{n,1} , and cocompact actions of discrete groups on ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} . We prove that for discrete Lorentz groups, the ring of polynomial invariants remains finitely generated, yet the classical Schwarz Theorem, stating that smooth invariants are generated by polynomial ones, fails. Conversely, for cocompact actions, the polynomial invariant ring collapses to constants, but a persistent structure of smooth invariants persists and is finitely generated over the smooth functions on the quotient. These results reveal a fourfold classification of invariant-theoretic regimes, extending the classical framework and delineating the boundaries of the Hilbert–Weyl and Schwarz theorems.



Keywords.
Invariant theory, Non-reductive groups, Lorentz group, Polynomial invariants, Smooth invariants, Hilbert basis, Schwarz theorem, Cocompact actions.

Introduction

Classical invariant theory centers on the actions of compact and reductive Lie groups. Its foundation is the Hilbert-Weyl theorem, which states that the ring of polynomial invariants is finitely generated
hilbert1890
;
hilbert1893
;
weyl1946
). This algebraic framework was subsequently shown to govern the associated analytic theory. Results by Schwarz and Luna established that for these groups, any smooth or analytic invariant function can be expressed as a function of the polynomial generators
luna1976
;
schwarz1975
. In this classical paradigm, the algebraic structure of polynomial invariants determines the structure of smooth invariants. The classical framework, as comprehensively presented in Olver’s book
olver1999
, centers on the study of invariant polynomials and their geometric interpretations. This was extended by the geometric invariant theory of Mumford, Fogarty, and Kirwan
mumford1994
, which provides a modern foundation for constructing quotient spaces and understanding the geometry of group actions.

Beyond this classical setting, the landscape is less understood. For non-reductive groups, Nagata’s counterexample demonstrates that finite generation of the polynomial ring is not guaranteed
nagata1959
. This raises two central questions: first, under what non-classical conditions does the polynomial ring remain finitely generated? Second, when the polynomial ring is finitely generated, does it still control the smooth invariant functions?

This paper investigates these questions in two distinct non-compact, non-reductive regimes. First, we examine the case of
discrete Lorentz groups
. We establish that their polynomial invariant rings are finitely generated, analogous to the Hilbert-Weyl result (Theorem A). We then show that the connection to the smooth theory fails: the polynomial invariants do not generate the algebra of smooth invariants (Proposition
4.1
).

Second, we analyze
cocompact actions
of discrete groups. In this context, an opposite behavior is observed. The polynomial invariant ring collapses to the constants (Theorem B). Despite this algebraic triviality, a well-structured smooth invariant theory exists. We prove a smooth analogue of the Schwarz theorem, showing that the algebra of smooth invariants is finitely generated by a set of basic smooth functions (Theorem C).

These results identify distinct regimes in invariant theory, characterized by the relationship between their algebraic and analytic structures. This leads to a fourfold classification of invariant-theoretic regimes, summarized in Table 1 (Section 6), which extends the classical framework and delineates the boundaries of the Hilbert–Weyl and Schwarz theorems.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the necessary background on the Lorentz group. Section 3 establishes the finite generation of polynomial invariants for the discrete Lorentz case (Theorem A). Section 4 is then dedicated to demonstrating the failure of the classical Schwarz theorem in this setting. Section 5 analyzes the cocompact regime, proving the collapse of polynomial invariants and the corresponding generation of smooth invariants (Theorems B and C). Finally, Section 6 provides the full discussion of this conceptual framework.

1.1.2
The Cocompact Regime: Polynomial Collapse, Smooth Generation

For cocompact actions of discrete groups, we find the opposite relationship. First, the polynomial invariant theory is trivial.

In a second, complementary direction, we analyze
cocompact actions
of discrete groups. Here, a dichotomy emerges: the ring of polynomial invariants collapses to the constants, invalidating the Hilbert-Weyl theorem, yet a well-structured theory of smooth invariants persists. For this setting, we prove two results in this direction:

Theorem B
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group acting freely, properly, and cocompactly on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then the algebra of polynomial invariants is trivial, i.e.,
P(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\Gamma}=\mathbb{R}
.

However, the smooth invariant theory is finitely generated, as described by the following smooth analogue of the Schwarz theorem.

Theorem C

(Smooth Invariants for Cocompact Actions)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group acting freely, properly, and cocompactly on a smooth manifold
M
. Let
\pi:M\to M/\Gamma
be the projection map.
Then:

1.

\pi^{*}:\mathcal{E}(M/\Gamma)\xrightarrow{\cong}\mathcal{E}(M)^{\Gamma}

is a topological isomorphism of Fréchet spaces.

2.

There exist smooth invariants
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}\in\mathcal{E}(M)^{\Gamma}
such that

\sigma^{*}\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{m})=\mathcal{E}(M)^{\Gamma},

where
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}):M\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

3.

For any manifold
X
with trivial
\Gamma
-action,

(\mathrm{id}\times\sigma)^{*}\mathcal{E}(X\times\mathbb{R}^{m})=\mathcal{E}(X\times M)^{\Gamma}.

Our results collectively demonstrate a persistence and reinterpretation of invariant theoretic structure far beyond the classical reductive framework, revealing a fourfold classification based on the interplay of algebra, analysis, and geometry, and precisely delineating the boundaries of the classical Schwarz Theorem.

Background: The Lorentz group and its discrete subgroups

Minkowski space and the Lorentz group

The Minkowski space of signature
(n,1)
, denoted by
\mathbb{R}^{n,1}
, is identified with
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
endowed with the quadratic form

Q(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y)=x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2}-y^{2}.

The Lorentz group
O(n,1)
is defined as the group of invertible linear transformations of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
that preserve
Q
, that is,

O(n,1):=\left\{A\in\operatorname{GL}(n+1,\mathbb{R})\mid Q(Ax)=Q(x)\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\right\}.

The geometric structure preserved by
O(n,1)
and the properties of its discrete subgroups are foundational to our analysis. For a comprehensive treatment of the Lorentz group and the geometry of its actions in the context of hyperbolic space, we refer to
ratcliffe2019
.

It is well known that any matrix in
O(1,1)
can be written in the form

\begin{bmatrix}\epsilon&0\\
0&\varepsilon\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\cosh\beta&\sinh\beta\\
\sinh\beta&\cosh\beta\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\epsilon&0\\
0&\varepsilon\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{H}_{\beta},

where
\epsilon,\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\mathcal{H}_{\beta}
denotes the standard hyperbolic rotation (see (
1
)). Similarly, every matrix
A\in O(n,1)
admits a factorization of the form

A=\begin{bmatrix}S&0\\
0&\epsilon\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{n-1}&0\\
0&\mathcal{H}_{\beta}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}R&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},

where
S\in O(n)
,
R\in SO(n)
,
\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
, and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.

The Lorentz group
O(n,1)
has four connected components. Let us define the matrices

\Lambda_{p}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{n-1,1}&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\quad\Lambda_{t}=J,\quad\Lambda_{pt}=\Lambda_{p}\Lambda_{t},

where
I_{n-1,1}
is the diagonal matrix with
n-1
entries equal to
1
and one equal to
-1
. Denoting by
SO_{0}(n,1)
the identity component of
O(n,1)
, we have the disjoint decomposition

O(n,1)=SO_{0}(n,1)\,\dot{\cup}\,\Lambda_{p}SO_{0}(n,1)\,\dot{\cup}\,\Lambda_{t}SO_{0}(n,1)\,\dot{\cup}\,\Lambda_{pt}SO_{0}(n,1).

Hyperbolic complex numbers and the Minkowski plane

The Minkowski plane
\mathbb{R}^{1,1}
, that is,
\mathbb{R}^{2}
equipped with the quadratic form
Q(x,y)=x^{2}-y^{2}
, admits a natural algebraic model via the set

\mathbb{D}:=\{x+hy\mid x,y\in\mathbb{R},\ h^{2}=1,\ h\notin\mathbb{R}\},

known as the algebra of hyperbolic complex numbers (also called Lorentz or split-complex numbers). While
\mathbb{D}
closely resembles the complex plane, it is not an integral domain; indeed,

(1+h)(1-h)=0.

Nevertheless, many of the standard operations from complex analysis – such as conjugation, modulus, and argument – carry over to this setting.

From a geometric standpoint, the algebra
\mathbb{D}
provides a natural language to describe Lorentz transformations, light cone geometry, and causal structure in dimension two. Further geometric interpretations of
\mathbb{D}
can be found in
gurses2015one
;
harkin2004geometry
;
yaglom2014complex
.

Discrete subgroups of the Lorentz group

We focus on discrete subgroups
\Gamma\subset O(n,1)
generated by matrices of the form

\mathcal{L}_{\alpha,\beta}=\begin{bmatrix}R_{\alpha}&0\\
0&\mathcal{H}_{\beta}\end{bmatrix},

where
R_{\alpha}\in O(n-1)
is a Euclidean rotation (or reflection) in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, and
\mathcal{H}_{\beta}\in O(1,1)
is a hyperbolic boost matrix defined as in (
1
), with fixed
\beta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

Remark 2.1
.

The subgroup
\Gamma=\langle\mathcal{H}_{\beta}\rangle\subset O(1,1)
, being infinite, discrete, and non-connected (isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}
), is not reductive. While
\Gamma
contains no nontrivial unipotent elements, its hyperbolic nature (
\beta\neq 0
) implies the absence of
\Gamma
-invariant positive-definite inner products on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
; a necessary condition for reductivity (see
(
Humphreys1975
, p. 125)
).

The discrete subgroups
\Gamma\subset O(n,1)
studied here are constructed via compositions of Euclidean isometries and hyperbolic transformations. These groups provide examples of non-compact, non-reductive symmetries. In the sections that follow, we analyze the invariant rings for these actions and find a divergence from the classical theory: while the polynomial invariant ring still admits a Hilbert basis, its differentiable analogue, the Schwarz theorem, does not hold.

Discrete Lorentz group acting on Minkowski space

In this section, we present and prove the main results of the paper concerning the structure of invariant rings under the action of certain discrete subgroups of the Lorentz group
O(n,1)
. We begin by analyzing the case of
O(1,1)
, where the geometry of the Minkowski plane allows for a concrete description of polynomial and equivariant invariants. We then extend the ideas to higher-dimensional Minkowski spaces and explore the interplay between algebraic invariants and smooth geometry.

3.1
\Gamma
as a Discrete Subgroup of
O(1,1)

Consider the group
\Gamma=\langle\mathcal{H}_{\beta}\rangle
acting on the Minkowski plane
\mathbb{R}^{1,1}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{\beta}
is the hyperbolic rotation in (
1
), with fixed

\beta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete subgroup of
O(1,1)
generated by a hyperbolic rotation
\mathcal{H}_{\beta}
with fixed
\beta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. Then the invariant ring
\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)
is finitely generated and

\mathbb{R}[x,y]^{\Gamma}=\mathbb{R}[x^{2}-y^{2}].

Proof.

Let
z=re^{h\varphi}
be a hyperbolic complex number. A direct computation shows that

e^{h\beta}z=r\left(\cosh(\beta+\varphi)+h\sinh(\beta+\varphi)\right).

On the other hand, under the standard linear action of
\Gamma
on
\mathbb{D}
, we have

\mathcal{H}_{\beta}z=\begin{bmatrix}\cosh\beta&\sinh\beta\\
\sinh\beta&\cosh\beta\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}r\cosh\varphi\\
r\sinh\varphi\end{bmatrix}=r\begin{bmatrix}\cosh(\beta+\varphi)\\
\sinh(\beta+\varphi)\end{bmatrix}.

Thus,
\mathcal{H}_{\beta}z=e^{h\beta}z
.
Now let
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{R}
be a polynomial of the form

f(z)=\sum a_{rs}z^{r}\overline{z}^{s},\quad\text{with }a_{rs}\in\mathbb{R}.

If
f
is invariant under
\Gamma
, then
f(e^{h\beta}z)=f(z)
for all
z
. Substituting
z=e^{h\varphi}
, we obtain

\displaystyle f(e^{h\beta}z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(e^{h(\beta+\varphi)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum a_{rs}\left(e^{h(\beta+\varphi)}\right)^{r}\left(e^{-h(\beta+\varphi)}\right)^{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum a_{rs}e^{h\beta(r-s)}z^{r}\overline{z}^{s}.

Therefore, invariance implies
a_{rs}e^{h\beta(r-s)}=a_{rs}
for all
r,s
. This is possible only if
r=s
or
a_{rs}=0
. Hence,
f
must be a real polynomial in
z\overline{z}
, that is,

f(x,y)=\sum_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}(x^{2}-y^{2})^{\alpha},

since
z\overline{z}=x^{2}-y^{2}
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Although the subgroups
\langle\mathcal{H}_{\beta}\rangle
and
\langle-\mathcal{H}_{\beta}\rangle
are distinct in
O(1,1)
(the latter consisting of matrices with determinant
-1
) they are both infinite cyclic and act identically on
\mathbb{R}^{1,1}
. In particular, they preserve the same quadratic form
x^{2}-y^{2}
and share the same invariant ring. Therefore, Theorem
3.1
also applies to subgroups generated by matrices in the component
\Lambda_{pt}SO_{0}(1,1)
.

The components
\Lambda_{p}SO_{0}(1,1)
and
\Lambda_{t}SO_{0}(1,1)
consist entirely of involutive matrices, i.e., elements
A\in O(1,1)
satisfying
A^{2}=I
. Any subgroup generated by such a matrix is isomorphic to the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

The only involutive elements in
O(1,1)
are matrices of the form

\begin{bmatrix}\cosh\beta&\sinh\beta\\
-\sinh\beta&-\cosh\beta\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{or}\quad\begin{bmatrix}-\cosh\beta&-\sinh\beta\\
\sinh\beta&\cosh\beta\end{bmatrix},

for some
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

A direct verification using the structure theory of
O(1,1)
(or its singular value decomposition) confirms this characterization.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

The matrices

\begin{bmatrix}\cosh\beta&\sinh\beta\\
-\sinh\beta&-\cosh\beta\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\kappa_{x}:=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\

are conjugate in

\begin{bmatrix}-\cosh\beta&-\sinh\beta\\

Proof.

The conjugating matrix for both cases is given by

\begin{bmatrix}\cosh\tfrac{\beta}{2}&-\sinh\tfrac{\beta}{2}\\

∎

Since conjugate matrices generate isomorphic group actions, the Hilbert bases of their invariant rings coincide. Moreover, the Hilbert–Weyl theorem guarantees finite generation of the invariant ring for finite groups.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\kappa_{x},\kappa_{y}\in O(1,1)

1.

The set

2.

The set

\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}(\kappa_{y}))

Proof.

This follows from the classification of polynomial invariants under finite reflection groups in

\kappa_{x}

Example 3.9
.

Consider the cyclic subgroup of

4
The Schwarz Theorem and the Non-Compactness Barrier

The Schwarz Theorem stands as a cornerstone of invariant theory for compact Lie groups. It establishes that for an orthogonal action of a compact Lie group
\Gamma
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the algebra of smooth invariants is generated by polynomial invariants. Precisely, if
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{k}
generate the ring
\mathbb{R}[\mathbb{R}^{n}]^{\Gamma}
, then the pullback map induces an isomorphism:

\rho^{*}:\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{k})\xrightarrow{\cong}\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\Gamma}.

This result has been fundamental in differential geometry, singularity theory, and the classification of group actions.

A natural question arises: to what extent is the compactness of
\Gamma
essential? In particular, does an analogous theorem hold for non-compact groups with a well-defined geometric structure, such as discrete Lorentz groups?

Let
\Gamma=\langle\mathcal{H}_{\beta}\rangle
be the discrete subgroup of
O(1,1)
generated by the hyperbolic rotation

\mathcal{H}_{\beta}

acting on
\mathbb{R}^{1,1}
. Although
\Gamma
is discrete and non-compact, it possesses a specific geometric structure. We demonstrate that at least three fundamental obstacles prevent any direct adaptation of Schwarz’s Theorem to this setting.

To elucidate the geometric structure, we employ light-cone coordinates:

u=x+y,\quad v=x-y.

In these coordinates, the quadratic form simplifies to
x^{2}-y^{2}=uv
, and the hyperbolic rotation acts diagonally as:

(u,v)\mapsto(ue^{\beta},ve^{-\beta}).

This reveals that for
t>0
, the hyperbola
x^{2}-y^{2}=t
consists of two disconnected branches: the right branch (
u>0,v>0
) and left branch (
u<0,v<0
), which are preserved by the
\Gamma
-action.

First obstacle: Insufficiency of polynomial invariants.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\Gamma=\langle\mathcal{H}_{\beta}\rangle
be the group from Theorem
3.1
, and let
\rho(x,y)=x^{2}-y^{2}
be the unique non-trivial polynomial invariant. Then the inclusion
\rho^{*}\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\Gamma}
is proper, i.e.,

\rho^{*}\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})\subsetneq\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\Gamma}.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma=\langle\mathcal{H}_{\beta}\rangle
be the hyperbolic rotation group acting on
\mathbb{R}^{1,1}
, and let
\rho(x,y)=x^{2}-y^{2}
be the unique non-trivial polynomial invariant.
We now construct a smooth
\Gamma
-invariant function that is not a smooth function of
\rho
. Define
G:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
by

G(x,y)=\begin{cases}e^{-1/(x^{2}-y^{2})}&\text{if }x^{2}-y^{2}>0\text{ and }x>0,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Step 1: Smoothness.
The function
G
is clearly smooth on the open sets where
x^{2}-y^{2}>0
and
x>0
, and where
x^{2}-y^{2}<0
or
x\leq 0
, since it is either a composition of smooth functions or identically zero.
On the boundary
x^{2}-y^{2}=0
, as
(x,y)
approaches from the region where
x^{2}-y^{2}>0
and
x>0
, we have
t=x^{2}-y^{2}\to 0^{+}
, and
e^{-1/t}\to 0
, matching the value of
G
on the other side. Moreover, all derivatives of
e^{-1/t}
also tend to
0
as
t\to 0^{+}
, ensuring a smooth transition.
The line
x=0
intersects the region
x^{2}-y^{2}>0
only if
-y^{2}>0
, which is impossible. Thus, the only potential discontinuity lies along
x^{2}-y^{2}=0
, where smoothness has been verified. Hence,
G\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Step 2:
\Gamma
-invariance.
In light-cone coordinates
u=x+y
,
v=x-y
, the action of
\mathcal{H}_{\beta}
is given by

(u,v)\mapsto(ue^{\beta},ve^{-\beta}),

and we have
\rho(x,y)=uv
.
For
t>0
, the level set
\rho^{-1}(t)
consists of two disconnected branches:

1.

The right branch, where
u>0
,
v>0
(equivalently,
x>0
and
x^{2}>y^{2}
),

2.

The left branch, where
u<0
,
v<0
(equivalently,
x<0
and
x^{2}>y^{2}
).

The group action preserves the signs of
u
and
v
, and hence preserves each branch. On the right branch,
G(x,y)=e^{-1/(uv)}
, which is invariant under scaling
u\mapsto ue^{\beta}
,
v\mapsto ve^{-\beta}
. On the left branch and in the region
uv\leq 0
,
G
is identically zero, which is also invariant. Therefore,
G\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\Gamma}
.

Step 3:
G\notin\rho^{*}\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})
.
Suppose, for contradiction, that
G=f\circ\rho
for some
f\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})
. Then for any
t>0
,
G
must be constant on the entire hyperbola
x^{2}-y^{2}=t
. However, on the right branch,
G=e^{-1/t}>0
, while on the left branch,
G=0
. This is a contradiction. Hence,
G\notin\rho^{*}\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})
.

We conclude that
\rho^{*}\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})\subsetneq\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\Gamma}
.
∎

Second obstacle: the map
\rho
fails to separate
\Gamma
-orbits.

Consider the points
p=(2,1)
and
q=(-2,1)
. Both satisfy
\rho(p)=\rho(q)=3
, yet they belong to distinct
\Gamma
-orbits. To verify this, observe that in light-cone coordinates
u=x+y
,
v=x-y
, the action of
\mathcal{H}_{\beta}^{n}
(for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
) scales
u\mapsto ue^{n\beta}
,
v\mapsto ve^{-n\beta}
. For
p
, we have
u=3
,
v=1
(both positive), placing it in the right branch of the hyperbola
x^{2}-y^{2}=3
. For
q
,
u=-1
,
v=-3
(both negative), placing it in the left branch. Since the signs of
u
and
v
are preserved by the
\Gamma
-action,
p
and
q
cannot be related by any group element. Consequently, the induced map
\hat{\rho}:\mathbb{R}^{2}/\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
is not injective, and the orbit space fails to be homeomorphic to the image of
\rho
. This fundamental geometric obstruction prevents the direct adaptation of Schwarz’s theorem, which relies on the Hilbert map separating orbits.

Third obstacle: Collapse of essential technical tools.

The classical proof of Schwarz’s Theorem (
schwarz1975
) relies on several technical constructs that fail fundamentally for non-compact groups:

1.

Nonexistence of an averaging operator
. For compact Lie groups
G
, the Haar average

\operatorname{Av}(f)(p)=\int_{G}f(g\cdot p)\,dg

provides a continuous projection
\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{G}
. For infinite discrete groups like
\Gamma
, no finite invariant measure exists, and thus no such averaging operator is available. This eliminates the primary mechanism for producing invariant functions.

2.

Non-properness of the action
. The map
\rho
fails to be proper – preimages of compact sets under
\rho
may be non-compact. For instance,
\rho^{-1}([0,1])
contains entire hyperbola branches extending to infinity. Consequently,
\rho
does not induce a homeomorphism between
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\Gamma
and
\rho(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, a key step in the original proof.

3.

Pathological orbit space topology
. The quotient space
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\Gamma
is non-Hausdorff. This is exemplified by distinct orbits on different branches of a hyperbola
x^{2}-y^{2}=t>0
having intersecting closures in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which prevents their separation by open sets in the quotient. Consequently, the orbit space fails to be a smooth manifold, or even a Hausdorff topological space, thereby violating a foundational assumption of Schwarz’s framework, which requires a well-behaved orbit space to which smooth invariants can be lifted.”

These structural failures collectively prevent any straightforward adaptation of the compact-group framework to the Lorentzian setting.

These obstructions collectively demonstrate that the Schwarz Theorem, in its full generality, is not adaptable to discrete Lorentz groups. The non-compactness of
\Gamma
introduces fundamental geometric and analytic challenges that cannot be overcome within the classical framework.

5
The Cocompact Case and the Hilbert-Weyl Barrier

The classical Hilbert-Weyl Theorem establishes that for a compact Lie group
\Gamma
acting linearly on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the ring of invariant polynomials
P(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\Gamma}
is finitely generated. Having successfully extended aspects of invariant theory to non-compact Lorentz groups in Theorem A, a natural question arises: does the Hilbert–Weyl theorem itself admit a generalization to other significant classes of non-compact groups, particularly those acting cocompactly?

In this section, we demonstrate a fundamental no-go theorem: the Hilbert-Weyl theorem fails for cocompact actions. The obstruction is not merely technical but structural, arising from an intrinsic incompatibility between polynomial growth and the geometric dynamics of cocompact actions. While the polynomial invariant theory collapses, the smooth invariant theory persists and admits a complete characterization. This reveals a sharp dichotomy between algebraic and smooth invariants in the non-compact setting, highlighting a divergence from the compact paradigm.

5.1
The Algebraic Obstruction: Periodicity vs. Polynomial Growth

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group acting freely, properly, and cocompactly on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The cocompactness implies the quotient space
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\Gamma
is compact. Geometrically, the
\Gamma
-action endows
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a periodic structure, and the quotient is a compact manifold (e.g., a torus if
\Gamma\cong\mathbb{Z}^{n}
).

Theorem B
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group acting freely, properly, and cocompactly on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then the algebra of polynomial invariants is trivial, i.e.,
P(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\Gamma}=\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists a non-constant invariant polynomial
p\in P(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\Gamma}
. Since
\Gamma
acts cocompactly, the quotient
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}/\Gamma
is a compact manifold. By
\Gamma
-invariance,
p
is constant on orbits and therefore descends to a continuous function
\tilde{p}:M\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying
p=\tilde{p}\circ\pi
, where
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to M
is the quotient projection.
As
M
is compact,
\tilde{p}
attains both a maximum and minimum, and is therefore bounded. Since
\pi
is surjective, it follows that
p
is bounded on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. However, any non-constant polynomial on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is unbounded. This contradiction establishes that no non-constant
\Gamma
-invariant polynomial exists.
∎

This elementary observation reveals a universal obstruction: cocompactness forces polynomial invariants to collapse to constants. This phenomenon, to the best of our knowledge, has not been explicitly
recorded in the literature. While the ingredients (compact quotient, polynomial growth) are classical, their combination in this invariant-theoretic context appears to be new. This obstruction shows that the Hilbert-Weyl theorem fails for cocompact actions. The ring of polynomial invariants is trivial, precluding any finite generating set beyond the constants themselves.

Remark 5.1
.

The hypothesis that the action is proper cannot be omitted. While cocompactness alone ensures the quotient is compact, properness is essential for the quotient space
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\Gamma
to be Hausdorff. Without properness, the quotient may be non-Hausdorff, and the continuous extension
\tilde{p}
of an invariant polynomial
p
need not exist. The three conditions together ensure that
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\Gamma
is a compact manifold and that the projection
\pi
is a covering map.

Despite this algebraic collapse, the situation for smooth invariants is remarkably different. As we now show, the failure of Hilbert–Weyl does not extend to the smooth category.

A clear example is the action of
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by translations:

•

Action
:
(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})\cdot(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=(x_{1}+k_{1},\dots,x_{n}+k_{n})
.

•

Properness and Freeness
: The action is free (no fixed points) and proper (the set of group elements moving a compact set is finite), satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem B.

•

Quotient
:
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cong\mathbb{T}^{n}
(compact).

•

Polynomial invariants
: Only constants (by periodicity).

•

Smooth invariants
: In contrast,
\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\cong\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
, which is an infinite-dimensional space.

For
n=1
, periodicity
p(x+1)=p(x)
forces any invariant polynomial to be constant, since non-constant polynomials are unbounded. The smooth case contrasts sharply: every smooth function on the torus lifts to a
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
-invariant function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

This example highlights the fundamental dichotomy: while polynomial invariants collapse to constants, smooth invariants reproduce the entire function theory of the compact quotient.

5.2
A Schwarz-Type Theorem for Cocompact Actions

Although the polynomial invariant theory collapses for cocompact actions, the smooth invariant theory admits a complete description. The following theorem provides a smooth analogue of Schwarz’s theorem, demonstrating that the generation phenomenon persists, but in the smooth category.

Theorem C

(Smooth Invariants for Cocompact Actions)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group acting freely, properly, and cocompactly on a smooth manifold
M
. Let
\pi:M\to M/\Gamma
be the projection map.
Then:

1.

\pi^{*}:\mathcal{E}(M/\Gamma)\xrightarrow{\cong}\mathcal{E}(M)^{\Gamma}

is a topological isomorphism of Fréchet spaces.

2.

There exist smooth invariants
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}\in\mathcal{E}(M)^{\Gamma}
such that

\sigma^{*}\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{m})=\mathcal{E}(M)^{\Gamma},

where
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}):M\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

3.

For any manifold
X
with trivial
\Gamma
-action,

(\mathrm{id}\times\sigma)^{*}\mathcal{E}(X\times\mathbb{R}^{m})=\mathcal{E}(X\times M)^{\Gamma}.

We begin by establishing two preliminary results.

Lemma 5.2
.

Under the hypotheses,
M/\Gamma
is a compact smooth manifold, and the projection
\pi:M\to M/\Gamma
is a smooth covering map.

Proof.

Since the action is free and proper, a standard result in differential geometry (see, e.g.,
(
Lee2012
, Theorem 7.10)
) ensures that
M/\Gamma
admits a unique smooth structure such that
\pi
is a smooth covering map. Cocompactness implies
M/\Gamma
is compact, as it is the continuous image of a fundamental domain under
\pi
.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

The pullback
\pi^{*}:\mathcal{E}(M/\Gamma)\to\mathcal{E}(M)^{\Gamma}
is continuous and injective.

Proof.

Continuity follows immediately from the definition of the
C^{\infty}
topology. Injectivity is clear since
\pi
is surjective.
∎

We now prove each part of the theorem.

Proof.

1.

By Lemma
5.2
,
M/\Gamma
is a compact manifold.
For surjectivity of
\pi^{*}
, let
f\in\mathcal{E}(M)^{\Gamma}
.
Define
\tilde{f}:M/\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
by
\tilde{f}([p])=f(p)
, which is
well-defined by
\Gamma
-invariance.
Since the action is free and proper,
\pi:M\to M/\Gamma
is a smooth covering map.
For any
[p]\in M/\Gamma
, there exists a neighborhood
U
of
[p]
and a smooth local section
s:U\to M
such that
\pi\circ s=\mathrm{id}_{U}
.
On
U
, we have
\tilde{f}|_{U}=f\circ s
, which is smooth as a composition of smooth maps.
Since such neighborhoods cover
M/\Gamma
,
\tilde{f}
is smooth and satisfies
\pi^{*}\tilde{f}=f
.
Thus
\pi^{*}
is a continuous linear bijection between Fréchet spaces
(continuity and injectivity follow from Lemma
5.3
).
By the Open Mapping Theorem for Fréchet spaces,
the inverse of
\pi^{*}
is continuous. Therefore,

\pi^{*}:\mathcal{E}(M/\Gamma)\xrightarrow{\cong}\mathcal{E}(M)^{\Gamma}

is a topological isomorphism.

2.

Since
M/\Gamma
is compact, Whitney’s Embedding Theorem (
(
Lee2012
, Theorem 6.14)
) guarantees the existence of a smooth embedding
j:M/\Gamma\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
j=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{m})
with
j_{i}\in\mathcal{E}(M/\Gamma)
, and define
\sigma_{i}=\pi^{*}j_{i}\in\mathcal{E}(M)^{\Gamma}
. Set
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}):M\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Let
f\in\mathcal{E}(M)^{\Gamma}
. By part (1), there exists a unique
\tilde{f}\in\mathcal{E}(M/\Gamma)
such that
f=\pi^{*}\tilde{f}
.
Since
j
is a smooth embedding of a compact manifold, the
Tubular Neighborhood Theorem
(
(
Lee2012
, Theorems 6.17 and 6.18)
) provides an open neighborhood
W
of
j(M/\Gamma)
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a smooth retraction
r:W\to j(M/\Gamma)
satisfying
r|_{j(M/\Gamma)}=\mathrm{id}
.
Define
F_{0}:W\to\mathbb{R}
by
F_{0}=\tilde{f}\circ j^{-1}\circ r
, which is smooth as a composition of smooth maps. Since
j(M/\Gamma)
is closed in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
(being compact) and
W
is an open neighborhood, there exists a smooth bump function
\rho\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
such that:

•

\rho\equiv 1
on an open neighborhood of
j(M/\Gamma)
,

•

\operatorname{supp}(\rho)\subset W
.

Define
F=\rho\cdot F_{0}
on
W
and
F=0
outside
W
. Then
F\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
and
F|_{j(M/\Gamma)}=\tilde{f}\circ j^{-1}
.

We now verify that
f=\sigma^{*}F
:

\displaystyle(\sigma^{*}F)(p)

\displaystyle=F(\sigma(p))=F(j(\pi(p)))

\displaystyle=(\tilde{f}\circ j^{-1})(j(\pi(p)))\quad\text{(since $j(\pi(p))\in j(M/\Gamma)$)}

\displaystyle=\tilde{f}(\pi(p))=f(p).

Thus
f=\sigma^{*}F\in\sigma^{*}\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
, completing the proof that
\sigma^{*}\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{m})=\mathcal{E}(M)^{\Gamma}
.

3.

Let
f\in\mathcal{E}(X\times M)^{\Gamma}
, where
\Gamma
acts trivially on
X
.
Define
\tilde{f}:X\times M/\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
by
\tilde{f}(x,[p])=f(x,p)
,
which is well-defined since
f
is
\Gamma
-invariant in the
M
-variable.
The same argument as in part (1) (applied fiberwise over
X
) shows that
\tilde{f}
is smooth.
Indeed, for each
(x_{0},[p_{0}])\in X\times M/\Gamma
, there exist neighborhoods

U
of
x_{0}
in
X
and
V
of
[p_{0}]
in
M/\Gamma
, and a smooth local section

s:V\to M
such that
\tilde{f}|_{U\times V}=f\circ(\mathrm{id}\times s)
is smooth.
Let
j:M/\Gamma\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
be the embedding from part (2),
and define
k=\mathrm{id}\times j:X\times M/\Gamma\hookrightarrow X\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Since
X\times M/\Gamma
is a closed submanifold of
X\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
(as
M/\Gamma
is compact),
the same tubular neighborhood argument as in part (2) (now parameterized by
X
)
shows that there exists
F\in\mathcal{E}(X\times\mathbb{R}^{m})
such that
\tilde{f}=F\circ k
.
We now verify that
f=(\mathrm{id}\times\sigma)^{*}F
:

\displaystyle f(x,p)

\displaystyle=\tilde{f}(x,\pi(p))

\displaystyle=F(k(x,\pi(p)))

\displaystyle=F(x,j(\pi(p)))

\displaystyle=F(x,\sigma(p))

\displaystyle=((\mathrm{id}\times\sigma)^{*}F)(x,p).

Thus
f\in(\mathrm{id}\times\sigma)^{*}\mathcal{E}(X\times\mathbb{R}^{m})
,
completing the proof.

∎

Remark 5.4
.

This theorem reveals a clear dichotomy: while the algebraic invariant theory collapses for cocompact actions (Theorem B), the smooth invariant theory persists and admits a complete geometric characterization via the quotient manifold.

To illustrate the content of Theorem C, let us examine the classical case of the
n
-torus, where the cocompact action is given by integer translations on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Example 5.5

(The
n
-torus)
.

Let
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
act on
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
by translations. This action is free, proper, and cocompact, with quotient
M/\Gamma\cong\mathbb{T}^{n}
, the
n
-torus.

•

Part (1)
:
\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\cong\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{T}^{n})

•

Part (2)
: The embedding
j:\mathbb{T}^{n}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2n}
from Whitney’s theorem yields invariants
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{2n}
such that

\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}}=\sigma^{*}\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}).

•

Part (3)
: For any manifold
X
, every
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
-invariant smooth function on
X\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a smooth function of
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{2n}
and the coordinates of
X
.

This example illustrates that Theorem C realizes the algebra of smooth invariant functions as the full algebra of smooth functions on a compact manifold, in sharp contrast with the polynomial case, where only constants remain.

The theorem applies to a wide range of geometrically interesting actions beyond translational ones. Notable examples where the hypotheses of Theorem C hold include:

•

Torsion-free crystallographic groups
: Properly discontinuous, cocompact actions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by isometries, yielding compact flat manifolds as quotients.

•

Torsion-free cocompact Fuchsian groups
: Discrete subgroups of
PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
acting freely on the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, with quotients being compact Riemann surfaces of genus
g\geq 2
.

•

Nilmanifolds
: A discrete cocompact subgroup
\Gamma
of a nilpotent Lie group
N
(such as the Heisenberg group) acting on
N
, yielding a compact nilmanifold
N/\Gamma
as the quotient.

In each case, the theorem provides a complete description of smooth invariants in terms of the geometry of the compact quotient.
