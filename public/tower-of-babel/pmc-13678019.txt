Interleukin13 Ameliorates Hepatocellular Steatosis via IL13Rα1/JNK/FOSL2 Signaling Pathway
Authors: Verena von Bülow, Dejan A Felske, Sarah B Riebeling, Fabian P Schmidt, Frederik Stettler, Andreas Schmid, Thomas Karrasch, Klaus Eder, Erika Most, Gertrud Morlock, Annika Haase, Jens Bier, Heike M Hermanns, Andreas Geier, Christopher Tuffs, Jörn Pons-Kühnemann, Martin Roderfeld, Elke Roeb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsSevere obesity is a major global health challenge and a key driver of hepatic steatosis through systemic insulin resistance and enhanced de novo lipogenesis. Excessive hepatic triglyceride accumulation represents a central metabolic consequence of this condition. This study aimed to identify immune-derived regulators of liver lipid metabolism and to define the mechanistic role of interleukin13 (IL13) in obesity-associated liver disease progression.MethodsIL13 levels and related signaling pathways were analyzed in plasma samples from patients with ≥class II obesity and liver biopsies from patients with hepatic steatosis and steatohepatitis. The effects of IL13 depletion on lipid metabolism were examined in mice, whereas its mechanistic role in fat metabolism was studied in primary hepatocytes.ResultsIn a cohort of severely obese patients, plasma IL13 levels rose after weight loss and were positively associated with fibroblast growth factor 19 (FGF19), fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF1). IL13-deficient mice showed worsened liver triglyceride accumulation, lower polyunsaturated fatty acids, and altered expression of peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor–dependent lipid genes. Primary murine hepatocytes revealed that IL13 decreases triglyceride content through interleukin13Rα1 (IL‑13Rα1) and a c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)–dependent c-JUN–FOS-like antigen 2 (FOSL2) axis, which enhances β-oxidation and ketogenesis. FOSL2 was required for maximal peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor (PPAR) activity and was upregulated in livers of patients with hepatic steatosis and steatohepatitis.ConclusionsThese data demonstrate that IL13 is a critical immunometabolic regulator of hepatic lipid homeostasis. Targeting the IL‑13-FOSL2-PPAR signaling pathway may offer a novel therapeutic approach to reduce hepatic steatosis.Keywords: FOSL2, IL13, Lipid Metabolism, MASH, MASLD, Obesity

Summary

Cytokines are recognized as mediators linking metabolic and inflammatory pathways. IL13 reduces intrahepatic lipid accumulation via an IL13Rα1/JNK/FOSL2-dependent mechanism.

What You Need to Know

Background

Cytokines are recognized as mediators linking metabolic and inflammatory pathways. The role of IL13 and its mode of action in hepatic steatosis remain unclear.

Impact

IL13 reduces intrahepatic lipid accumulation via an IL13Rα1/JNK/FOSL2–dependent mechanism. IL13 enhances fatty acid β-oxidation, promotes ketogenesis, and modulates key metabolic regulators.

Future Directions

These findings highlight IL13 as a promising therapeutic target for the treatment of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease.

Steatotic liver disease has emerged as a major global health challenge, mirroring the rise in obesity and type 2 diabetes.1 The condition is defined by hepatic steatosis, characterized by triglyceride (TG) accumulation within hepatocytes.2 This process is primarily driven by enhanced hepatic de novo lipogenesis secondary to insulin resistance and by an increased influx of free fatty acids from adipose tissue.3 Steatosis ensues when lipid acquisition exceeds the liver’s capacity for β-oxidation and export.3 Persistent lipid overload may provoke lipotoxicity, inflammation, and fibrotic remodeling, culminating in steatohepatitis.4 Chronic low-grade inflammation, mediated in part by cytokines, is now recognized as central to disease progression.5

Among these mediators, interleukin13 (IL13) has attracted growing interest. Traditionally associated with allergic inflammation and tissue repair, IL13 exerts pleiotropic effects on epithelial, immune, and mesenchymal cells, promoting extracellular matrix deposition and, in pathologic contexts, fibrosis.6 Signaling via interleukin13 receptor α1 (IL13Rα1) in concert with the IL4 receptor α chain activates signal transducer and activator of transcription 6,7 whereas IL13Rα2 enhances profibrotic transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) pathways. Intriguingly, IL13 also modulates glucose metabolism through IL13Rα1 and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3).8

Accumulating evidence implicates the important role of transcription factor activator protein-1 (AP-1), particularly members of the FOS family, in the pathogenesis of steatotic liver disease. Enhanced expression of FOS-like antigen 1 (FOSL1) and FOS-like antigen 2 (FOSL2; FRA) has been shown to confer protection against diet-induced steatotic liver injury.9 This effect is mediated through suppression of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) transcription via inhibitory heterodimers formed between c-JUN and either FOSL1 or FOSL2.9 Moreover, central pathogenic processes, including insulin resistance, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and autophagy, are coordinated by the upstream kinases c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)1 and JNK2, which regulate c-JUN activity.10

This pathway attenuates lipid uptake and limits triglyceride synthesis in hepatocytes, promotes β-oxidation in vitro, and is associated with increased ketogenesis in patients with severe obesity, whereas FOSL2 induction is observed in liver tissue from patients with hepatic fat accumulation.

Stratification by body mass index (BMI) showed increased IL13 levels from baseline to follow-up in the BMI 40–49.99 kg/m2 (P < .001) and 50–59.99 kg/m2 groups (P = .003) (Figure 1B). The Hepatic Lipid Metabolism Was Dysregulated in IL13−/− Mice

Disruptions in lipid metabolism are key drivers of hepatic steatosis.15 To delineate the physiological role of IL13 and to determine whether the absence of IL13 is sufficient to alter hepatic lipid metabolism under basal conditions, 8-week-old female IL13−/− mice on normal chow were compared with wild-type (WT) controls. Body weight and serum ALT were similar between groups (Figure 3A and B). However, IL13−/− mice showed elevated serum TGs (Figure 3C), whereas hepatic TGs and cholesterol remained unchanged (Figure 3D). Phosphatidylserine levels were increased in IL13−/− mice liver tissue (Figure 3D).

Analysis of hepatic long-chain fatty acids revealed reduced polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in IL13 −/− mice (Figure 3E). Specifically, linoleic acid (C18:2) and γ-linolenic acid (C18:3 c6,9,12), substrates for arachidonic acid synthesis, were reduced, whereas dihomo-γ-linolenic acid (C20:3 c8,11,14) was increased (Figure 3F).16 α-Linolenic acid (C18:3 c9,12,15) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) (C20:5 c5,8,11,14,17) were also decreased (Figure 3F). To assess transcriptional changes, lipid metabolism genes were analyzed using a real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) array17 in pooled liver samples from IL13−/− and WT mice (n = 12/group) (Figure 3G). Genes involved in lipid metabolism and transport, cholesterol metabolism, insulin signaling, and adipokine signaling showed >4-fold differential expression. Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) confirmed increased expression of de novo lipogenesis genes (Fasn, Srebp1, Acly), cholesterol metabolism genes (Hmgcr, Cyp7a1), lipid transport (Ldlr), and Pparγ (Figure 3H). In contrast, genes involved in β-oxidation (Pparα, Pparδ, Pdk4) and glucose metabolism (Igfbp1) were downregulated (Figure 3H). Consistently, immunohistochemistry revealed reduced nuclear PPARδ in IL13-deficient liver tissue (Figure 3I). Together, these findings indicate that IL13 deficiency disrupts hepatic lipid homeostasis, promoting lipogenesis and reducing β-oxidation.

Interleukin13 Reduced Hepatocellular Triglyceride Levels In Vitro

To examine whether IL13 directly affects hepatic lipid metabolism, primary mouse hepatocytes (PMHs) were treated with IL13 (12.5 ng/mL) for 24 hours in the presence or absence of oleic acid (OA; 1 mM) (Figure 4). This IL13 concentration reflects the upper range observed in patient plasma. Intracellular TG measurements showed that IL13 did not alter TG levels in nonsteatotic PMHs but significantly reduced TG accumulation under steatotic conditions (Figure 4A). Oil Red O staining confirmed reduced lipid accumulation in OA-treated hepatocytes exposed to IL13 (Figure 4B and C).

RT-PCR array analysis revealed modulation of genes involved in lipid metabolism, with downregulation of genes related to cholesterol metabolism and lipid transport (Figure 4D). IL13 increased expression of Pparδ (∼2-fold) and Pparγ (∼6-fold) in both nonsteatotic and steatotic PMHs, whereas Pparα (∼2-fold) and lipoprotein lipase (Lpl; ∼1.7-fold) were induced only under steatotic conditions (Figure 4E–G). Conversely, IL13 reduced the expression of Dgat2 (∼0.7-fold), Ldlr (∼0.8-fold), and Cyp4A10 (∼0.5-fold under OA stimulation) (Figure 4H–J). Although not included in the array, IL13 also decreased expression of the lipid droplet marker Plin2 in both steatotic and nonsteatotic PMHs (Figure 4K). Finally, lipid uptake assays demonstrated reduced fatty acid uptake over time in IL13-treated hepatocytes (Figure 4L), indicating that IL13 limits lipid accumulation by attenuating hepatocellular lipid uptake.

Interleukin13 Drives Hepatocellular JNK/c-JUN/FOSL2 Activation

Distinct transcription factor activator protein-1 (FOS/JUN) dimers exert protective effects in steatohepatitis,9 and IL13-dependent activation of FOSL2 has been described in epithelial cells.18 To assess whether IL13 regulates FOS/JUN family members in the liver, we analyzed hepatic tissue and primary hepatocytes. IL13 deficiency resulted in reduced hepatic Fosl2 and Jun expression (Figure 5A and B), whereas IL13 stimulation induced Fosl2 and Jun expression in both steatotic and nonsteatotic PMHs (Figure 5C and D).

Data represent the mean ± standard deviation from 3 independent experiments (technical replicates). (C and D) Gene expression analysis by qRT-PCR revealed that Fosl2 (C) and Jun (D) were upregulated by IL13 in steatotic and nonsteatotic PMHs in vitro. (E–H) Western blot analysis demonstrated that IL13 treatment of both non-steatotic and steatotic PMHs in vitro increased FOSL2 protein expression (E and F; N = 5 livers). IL13 treatment led to activation of c-JUN as shown by c-JUN phosphorylation (E and G). c-JUN protein expression was also increased by IL13 in steatotic PMHs (E and H; N = 5 livers). Western blot analysis confirmed increased FOSL2 protein levels and phosphorylation of c-JUN following IL13 treatment in PMHs (Figure 5E–H). Immunoprecipitation further demonstrated formation of a c-JUN–FOSL2 heterodimer, which was disrupted by JNK inhibition with SP600125 (Figure 5I). Consistent with previous reports,19 JNK inhibition reduced c-JUN protein levels but did not affect FOSL2 protein expression (Figure 5J and K).

Interleukin13 Reduced Intracellular Lipids via JNK/c-JUN/FOSL2 Axis

We next examined whether IL13-induced Fosl2 expression mediates its effects on hepatic steatosis. Silencing Il13ra1 by small interfering RNA (siRNA) in PMHs reversed the IL13-dependent reduction of lipid accumulation under steatotic conditions, as shown by Oil Red O staining (Figure 6A and B). Il13ra1 expression itself was not altered by OA or IL13 (Figure 6C). Consistently, FOSL2 overexpression reduced OA-induced lipid accumulation in PMHs (Figure 6G–I). To determine whether JNK-mediated activation of c-JUN–FOSL2 contributes to this effect, steatotic PMHs were treated with the JNK inhibitor SP600125. IL13 reduced TG SP600125 levels in OA-treated hepatocytes, but this effect was abolished by JNK inhibition, as shown by intracellular TG measurements (Figure 6J) and Oil Red O staining (Figure 6K and L). SP600125 alone did not affect lipid accumulation in OA-treated PMHs (Figure 6J–L).

Discussion

We demonstrate that IL13 mitigates hepatocellular steatosis via the IL13Rα1–JNK-c-JUN–FOSL2 axis. IL13 suppressed lipogenic genes (Dgat2, Ldlr, Cyp4A10), induced Pparα/δ/γ, reduced fatty-acid uptake, and normalized steatosis-associated increases in transport and β-oxidation genes. Conversely, IL13 deficiency in mice shifted hepatic lipid metabolism towards enhanced lipogenesis and diminished β-oxidation, consistent with metabolic dysregulation.8 These experimental findings were paralleled clinically by elevated plasma IL13, BHB, and altered eicosanoids in patients with class II and III obesity.

Weight loss—dietary or surgical—was associated with increased circulating IL13 and was positively associated with BMI reduction. Given the high prevalence of MASLD in bariatric cohorts, this increase may reflect metabolic improvement.24 Although physiological IL13 levels are approximately 10 pg/mL,25 concentrations in our cohort showed wide variability and reached the nanogram range, comparable to levels reported in inflammatory and malignant conditions.26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32 Visceral adipose tissue, particularly group 2 innate lymphoid cells, represents a major source of IL13 in obesity.33 We believe that elevated IL13 in individuals with higher BMI likely reflects a compensatory response to increasing metabolic stress and tissue inflammation. Similar compensatory increases have been described for several cytokines and hormones during obesity, where elevated circulating concentrations do not necessarily translate into sufficient biological activity to prevent disease progression. Importantly, our human analysis is cross-sectional and therefore does not establish causality. In fact, previous studies have demonstrated that IL13 expression is increased in the adipose tissue of obese humans and high-fat diet–fed mice. Kwon et al identified adipocytes as a major source of IL13 and showed that proinflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α and IL1β induce IL13 expression, suggesting that IL13 represents a compensatory anti-inflammatory feedback response to obesity-associated inflammation rather than a causal driver of obesity. Therefore, elevated circulating IL13 in individuals with higher BMI seems to be compatible with a protective role of IL13 in limiting metabolic inflammation.34

IL13 was associated positively with FGF21 and FGF19, endocrine regulators of metabolic homeostasis with therapeutic promise in MASLD.35 As PPARα governs fatty-acid oxidation and fasting-induced FGF expression, the IL13–PPAR–FGF axis may attenuate lipid overload.36 The association between IL13 and IGF1 further implicates improved insulin sensitivity, aligning with established metabolic effects of IL4 and IL13 signaling.37

To delineate the physiological role of IL13 and to determine whether the absence of IL13 is sufficient to alter hepatic lipid metabolism under basal conditions, we used IL13−/− mice. This model was not intended to recapitulate the clinical situation in patients but rather to investigate the physiological role of IL13 and to determine whether the absence of IL13 affects hepatic lipid metabolism under basal conditions. In IL13-deficient mice, normocaloric feeding induced de novo lipogenesis and cholesterol synthesis, accompanied by altered PPAR isoform expression (↑PPARγ; ↓PPARα/δ). IL13−/− mice maintained on a normal diet did not develop overt hepatic steatosis, as reflected by unchanged body weight, histologic scores, serum ALT, cholesterol levels, and hepatic TG content. This observation is fully consistent with our experimental design and supports the notion that IL13 deficiency alone is not sufficient to induce steatotic liver disease. Importantly, the absence of a macroscopic steatotic phenotype does not preclude biologically relevant alterations at the transcriptional and signaling level, which may become functionally relevant under metabolic challenge. Although PPARγ exerts complex, context-dependent effects in the liver,38 IL13 induced all PPAR isoforms in steatotic hepatocytes together with FOSL2 and c-JUN, suggesting a coordinated transcriptional control. IL13 also increased Pparδ and Lpl expression, indicating improved lipid handling.39 Given that our clinical observations were derived from a cohort of individuals with obesity rather than patients with metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH), we intentionally restricted our mechanistic investigations to the direct effects of IL13 on hepatocytes. Addressing the role of IL13 in MASH would necessitate the development of a cell type–specific experimental model capable of disentangling hepatocyte-intrinsic effects from those mediated through other relevant cell types, including hepatic stellate cells and intestinal epithelial cells. The establishment and validation of such a sophisticated conditional mouse model would represent a substantial undertaking that lies well beyond the scope of the present work. Although fibrosis is the clinically most relevant endpoint in MASLD and MASH,40,41 the present work was designed to address early metabolic and inflammatory events. Hepatic steatosis therefore serves as an appropriate and sensitive readout of early disease pathophysiology within the scope of this study.

Lipidomic analyses revealed marked hypertriglyceridemia and altered phosphatidylserine in IL13-null mice, alongside reduced PUFAs (EPA, α- and γ-linolenic acids). Given that PUFAs activate PPARα and suppress de novo lipogenesis,42 their reduction supports a shift towards lipid accumulation. In vitro, IL13 limited lipid uptake and downregulated fatty acid binding proteins in a FOSL2-dependent manner, likely reducing intracellular fatty acid trafficking in steatotic hepatocytes.

Furthermore, IL13 modulated mitochondrial and peroxisomal β-oxidation gene expression and enhanced ketogenesis in individuals with high circulating IL13. Nuclear FOSL2 in steatotic livers suggests a compensatory response to lipid excess. However, given the context-dependent roles of JNK1 in steatohepatitis,43 the precise pathophysiological significance of the IL13Rα1–JNK–FOSL2 axis in MASLD remains to be defined.

Limitations

As for the data from patients, we report only associations, which do not allow them to distinguish between cause and consequence. Not all clinical and biochemical parameters were available for every patient, which may have reduced the statistical power of certain analyses. We posit that IL13 may exert a dual pathogenic influence: first, by compromising intestinal epithelial barrier function, thereby facilitating the translocation of microbial products and amplifying hepatic innate immune activation6; and second, by directly enhancing fibrogenic responses within the liver through activation of mesenchymal effector cells.44 The convergence of gut-derived inflammatory cues6 and IL13-driven profibrotic signaling could thus represent a mechanistic axis that accelerates disease progression from metabolic injury to advanced fibroinflammatory remodeling. Our central hypothesis is that precise, organ- and cell type–specific modulation of the IL13 signaling pathway constitutes a rational and potentially transformative therapeutic strategy in hepatic steatosis rather than systemic cytokine application.

This study is the first to show that IL13 can directly reduce hepatic steatosis in primary hepatocytes and that IL13 deficiency disrupts hepatic lipid homeostasis, promoting lipogenesis and reducing β-oxidation in murine liver tissue. IL13 ameliorates fat metabolism by an IL13Rα1–JNK–FOSL2–dependent mechanism. Plasma analysis of obese patients as well as human liver biopsies from patients with hepatic steatosis extended our preclinical observations to the clinical setting.

Materials and Methods

Sex as a Biological Variable

Our study analyzed archived samples from both female and male participants enrolled in bariatric surgery studies. No relevant sex-specific differences affecting the study outcomes were observed within the analyzed data from the human cohorts. The animal experiments were primarily conducted in female mice because we cannot conclusively exclude sex-specific influences based on the mouse model. In addition, we confirmed the main finding of our work (decrease in Fosl2 expression in the absence of IL13) in a cohort of male mice.

Class II and Class III Obesity Study Subjects

Plasma samples were obtained from participants of the ROBS (Research in Obesity and Bariatric Surgery) cohort,45 a prospective, monocentric, observational study in patients with severe obesity treated at the University Hospital Giessen, Germany (approval number 101/14). All research was conducted in accordance with both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul, and written consent was given in writing by all subjects. Plasma collected at baseline (t0) and 3-month follow-up (t3) was analyzed. Because each assay required a previously unthawed aliquot, sample numbers varied by availability, and not all patients had matched t0 and t3 samples.

Overall, 75% of samples were from female participants, and 25% were from male participants; due to small male participant numbers (especially in the low IL13 group), analyses focused primarily on female participants. IL13 was measured in women at t0 (N = 188) and t3 (N = 150), and in men at t0 (N = 64) and t3 (N = 40). Female participants had BMI ≥35 kg/m2 and were categorized per guidelines46: class II obesity (BMI, 35–39.99 kg/m2; 10.1%) and class III obesity (BMI ≥40 kg/m2; 89.1%), including morbid (BMI, 40–49.99 kg/m2; 43.6%), super-obesity (BMI, 50–59.99 kg/m2; 36.2%), and ultra-obesity (BMI ≥60 kg/m2; 10.1%). Interventions comprised low-calorie formula diet (37%) or bariatric surgery (63%) between January 2015 and April 2021. Type 2 diabetes was present in 16.3% (n = 36). Fibrosis risk was assessed using the NFS per guidelines47; among women with available data (n = 75), 50.7% had no fibrosis (NFS <−1.455), 43.7% had intermediate fibrosis (−1.455 to 0.675), and 5.6% had advanced fibrosis (>0.675). NFS ranged from −3.81 to 2.71 at baseline (mean, −1.31 ± 1.25) and from −5.55 to 0.33 at 3 months (mean, −2.21 ± 1.13). Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, endocrine disorders, untreated bulimia nervosa or binge eating disorder, illicit drug use, malignancy, severe psychiatric disease, and psychopathologic instability. All participants provided written informed consent. Baseline characteristics and liver parameters stratified by sex and IL13 are shown in Tables 1 and 2. Values below the detection limit (<11.7 pg/mL) were excluded from mean calculations in the female low IL13 group.

Metabolic Dysfunction–Associated Steatotic Liver Disease Study Subjects

Model-derived findings were validated retrospectively in archived human liver tissue obtained during bariatric surgery. Liver biopsies were analyzed histologically to determine the presence of MASLD, especially MASH. Patients were excluded if they lacked informed consent, were younger than 18 years, had malignant disease, chronic inflammatory disorders, or were immunocompromised.

For immunohistochemical analysis, female patients with MASLD (steatosis grade 1–3) and non-MASLD controls (steatosis grade 0) were randomly selected (n = 4 per group). Control individuals had BMI >40 kg/m2 (NAS scores: S0A4F2, S0A3F2, S0A2F2, S0A2F2), whereas the MASLD group had BMI >50 kg/m2 (NAS scores: S1A2F2, S2A2F2, S2A3F2, S3A2F2). The study was approved by the ethics committee of Heidelberg University (approval number S-876/2021). All research was conducted in accordance with both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul, and written consent was given in writing by all subjects.

Animal Model

Female IL13−/− knockout mice (BALB/c-ll13tm2Anjm) and WT controls were used as previously described.6,51 At 8 weeks of age, mice were anesthetized by isoflurane inhalation and sacrificed by cervical dislocation. Liver and serum samples were collected from 12 animals per group and stored for further analyses. At study onset, serum samples were available from n = 9 WT and n = 12 IL13−/− mice, whereas body weight data were available for n = 11 WT and n = 12 IL13−/− animals. Reduced sample numbers in certain analyses were due to limited serum availability and exclusion of statistical outliers using Grubbs’ test. No significant differences in histologic scores between WT and IL13−/− mice were observed.6 All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the European Convention for the Protection of Vertebrate Animals used for Experimental and Other Scientific Purposes (ETS No. 123) and approved by the Regional Council Giessen (V54-19c 20 15 h 01 GI20/10 No. A29/2013 and No. 128/2014).

Cell Culture Experiments

PMHs were isolated from female C57BL/6J mice aged 4–8 months using gentleMACS Perfusion Technology (Miltenyi) as previously described.52

Hepatocytes were seeded on collagen I-coated culture dishes and maintained in Hepatozyme (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS; Anprotec), 1% L-glutamin (Anprotec), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Anprotec) for 24 hours before stimulation.

PMHs were stimulated with 1 mM OA (Sigma), 12.5 ng/mL recombinant IL13 (Peprotech), or vehicle control (2% FCS) in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Anprotec) containing 1% penicillin–streptomycin for 24 hours. For knockdown experiments, hepatocytes were transfected with siRNA targeting Fosl2 or IL13Rα1, or with negative control siRNA (Ambion), using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) 48 hours prior to stimulation. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by qRT-PCR. For JNK inhibition experiments, PMHs were pretreated with 10 μM SP600125 (Merck) or vehicle control dimethyl sulfoxide (Roth) for 30 minutes before stimulation with OA and/or IL13. Each hepatocyte experiment was performed at least 3 times using cells from independent animals.

Targeted Metabolomics: Eicosanoid Extraction and Mass Spectrometric Analysis via Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry

Eicosanoids were extracted from liver lysates and human plasma and analyzed by high-pressure LC-MS/MS as previously described.53 Briefly, 500 μL of human plasma samples were spiked with internal standard solution and treated with 1 μL of 10 mg/mL SW033291 (15-PGDH-Inhibitor, Cayman), 1 μL 0.1% butylated hydroxytoluene (Sigma) solution, 1 μL 12% EDTA (Fluka) solution. Subsequently, 1 mL 50% acetonitrile (Sigma-Aldrich) and 50% methanol (Sigma-Aldrich) were added for protein precipitation at 0 °C for 15 minutes. Precipitates were pelleted by centrifugation at 4 °C for 15 minutes (4450RCF). The supernatant was decanted and filled with pure water to 10-mL volume. The samples were prepared and extracted by solid-phase extraction as described previously.54 Specifically, the 1100 Series capillary LC system (Agilent Technologies Deutschland) was employed for analyzing the plasma of female patients with class III obesity (BMI >40 kg/m2) who underwent bariatric surgery. Eicosanoid metabolites were characterized by the following extracted ion–chromatogram method as described previously.53 For MS analysis, plasma samples were analyzed using the Daltonik amaZon SL (Bruker Corporation). Characteristic extracted ion chromatograms were utilized for both the identification and quantification of eicosanoids. The MS raw data were analyzed using Compass DataAnalysis Version 5.3 as previously described.54

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical detections were performed as described previously.55 Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded human liver specimens were stained with a FOSL2 antibody (1:250, GeneTex, #GTX64443). The same procedure was used to stain formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded mouse liver from IL13−/− and WT specimens with a PPARδ antibody (1:1000, Novus).

Histologic and Cell Staining

Human liver sections were stained with hematoxylin (Roth) and eosin (Shandon) for morphologic evaluation. Fibrosis was assessed using Sirius Red (Polysciences, Inc) and Fast Green (Roth).

For lipid staining, PMHs grown on coverslips were treated with OA for 24 hours, fixed in 5% paraformaldehyde (ICN Biochemical) and stained with Oil Red O (Serva). Coverslips were washed with 60% isopropanol (Sigma-Aldrich), rinsed with distilled water, and mounted using Fluoromount (Sigma-Aldrich). Images were acquired from randomly selected fields, and the staining intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Gene Expression Analysis

RNA isolation, reverse transcription, and qPCR were performed as described previously.55 Primers were obtained from Microsynth. Relative gene expression was calculated using the ΔΔCt method.17 Cycle threshold (Ct) values greater than 35 were excluded to ensure reliable detection.

RT fatty liver and fatty acid metabolism profiler arrays were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Qiagen). RNA from PMHs (N = 7 for fatty liver array; N = 5 for fatty acid metabolism array) and mouse liver tissue (N = 12) was pooled within groups, converted to complementary DNA, and analyzed using the array platform. Ct values were normalized to 5 housekeeping genes. Genes with Ct values >35 or Ct >30 in both control and treated samples were excluded due to unreliable expression levels.

Western Blot Analysis

Western blotting was performed as described previously.56 Overexpression of FOSL2 plasmid was determined in HepG2 by a polyclonal FOSL2 antibody (1:1000, PA5-99459, Invitrogen) and for PMHs by the anti-rabbit FOSL2 antibody (1:1000, HPA004817, Merck).

Immunoprecipitation

Protein interactions were analyzed by immunoprecipitation using the Capturem IP & Co-IP Kit (Takara). PMHs (1 × 106 cells) were lysed and incubated with anti-mouse c-JUN antibody (1:100, clone L70B11, CST#2315, Cell Signaling Technology). Immunoprecipitated proteins were analyzed by Western blot using an anti-rabbit FOSL2 antibody (HPA004817, Sigma-Aldrich).

Measurement of Triglycerides, Cholesterol, β-Hydroxybutyrate, and Alanine Aminotransferase

TGs and cholesterol were measured in serum from IL13−/− and WT mice and in hepatocyte lysates using an automated analyzer (respons910, DiaSys). BHB was measured in plasma samples from female study participants, and ALT was analyzed in plasma samples from both sexes using the same system.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assays

Venous blood samples were collected in citrate monovettes (Sarstedt), and plasma was obtained by centrifugation. Circulating IL13 FGF19, FGF21, and IGF1 were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (Biotechne).

All measurements were performed in technical duplicates. Samples with intraduplicate variation greater than 20% were repeated. To improve detection of low IL13 concentrations, the standard curve was extended down to 11.7 pg/mL. For undiluted plasma samples analyzed below 98.3 pg/mL, blank and capture antibodies were prepared in phosphate-buffered saline to minimize albumin interference.

Lipid Uptake Assay

PMHs were seeded in black 96-well plates (Greiner Bio-One) and stimulated with 50 mM TopFluor OA (Sigma/Merck KGaA) in the presence or absence of IL13. Fluorescence was measured at 0, 5, 15, 30, 60, 120, and 180 minutes using a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite Pro 200) at excitation 470 nm and emission 507 nm.

Analysis of Fatty Acid Composition by Gas Chromatography

The gas chromatography measurement was carried out once with 12 animals per group. The analysis of the hepatic fatty acid composition of 12 animals per group was performed using a Perkin Elmer Clarus 580 gas chromatograph with flame ionization detection. Initially, an aliquot of 35 mg liver was shaken in the dark for 18 hours at ambient room temperature with a mixture of n-hexane and isopropanol (3:2 (v/v)) containing C19:0 (10.59 mg/mL) as an internal standard. Samples were then centrifuged (1200 × g, 10 minutes, 15 °C). One hundred fifty microliters of the supernatant was evaporated with a N2 stream at 37 °C. Fatty acids were subsequently transmethylated using 0.2 M trimethylsulfonium hydroxide solution in methanol (Macherey-Nagel). The resulting fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) were separated by a gas chromatograph with flame ionization detection system equipped with a polar capillary column (Zebron, 30 m, 0.25 m internal diameter, 0.25 mm film thickness; Phenomenex Ltd) and a flame ionization detector. The injection volume was 1 μL with a 1:20 split, and the carrier gas was helium with a flow rate of 3.9 mL/min at the beginning. The following temperature program was used: 2 minutes at 100 °C, then the temperature was increased at a rate of 3 °C per minute until 240 °C was reached, and then the temperature was held at 240 °C for 5 minutes. A commercial standard mixture (Supelco 37-component FAME-Mix No 18919) was used to identify the individual FAME by comparing the retention times of the standards with those of the sample. Evaluation of peak areas was performed with the EZChrom Elite Software (Agilent). For qualitative evaluation in terms of percentage area, FAMEs were calculated as a percentage of the total peak area. Amounts of individual FAMEs were quantified by using C19:0 as an internal standard.

High-Performance Thin-Layer Chromatography of Lipids

High-performance thin-layer chromatography was performed in duplicate with slight modifications,57 using 12 animals per group. Briefly, liver tissue (50 mg) was extracted with n-hexane and 2-propanol 3:2 (V/V) for 1 hour and centrifuged, and the supernatant was dried under nitrogen gas. The residue was reconstituted in chloroform and methanol 2:1 (V/V) and analyzed as 4-mm bands (5 μL/band for polar lipids and 7.5 μL/band for nonpolar lipids) using respective calibration standards. Lipid separation was achieved on high-performance thin-layer chromatography silica gel 60 F254 MS grade plates with specific solvent systems: chloroform, methanol, ammonia, and water 30:17:2:1 (V/V/V) up to 75 mm for polar lipids and n-hexane, diethyl ether, and glacial acetic acid 43:7:1 (V/V/V) up to 65 mm for nonpolar lipids (previously chamber saturation with filter paper for 20 minutes along with plate activation for 10 minutes at 33% relative humidity). Quantification of the polar and nonpolar lipids was performed by fluorescence measurement at 366 and >400 nm (TLC Scanner 4, mercury lamp, measurement slit 6 mm × 0.3 mm, scanning speed 10 mm/s, data resolution 50 μm) after derivatization via the primuline reagent and drying for 5 minutes. Quantification of phosphatidylserine was performed by absorption measurement at 577 mm after specific derivatization via the ninhydrin reagent (1.5 mL, 8 g ninhydrin in 10 mL ethanol and glacial acetic acid 22:3 [V/V], blue nozzle, spraying level 3; TLC Plate Heater, 105 °C, 5 minutes) to distinguish from coeluted phosphatidylinositol.

Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay

HepG2 cells (CLS #330198; ordered 2019; cryostocks ≤10 passages) were cultured in DMEM with 10% FCS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO2 (Anprotec). Cells (4 × 104/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and transfected with 0.1 μg FOSL2 expression plasmid (https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.95.8.4333, Addgene plasmid #187907) or empty vector pcDNA3.1-Flag (Addgene plasmid #208051; RRID: Addgene_208051) together with the PPRE × 3-TK-luc reporter plasmid (https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.95.8.4333, Addgene plasmid #1015) and pGL4 [hRluc/TK] (0.1 μg; Promega). After 48 hours, cells were treated with 1 mM OA or control medium (DMEM with 2% FCS) for 24 hours. Luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, E1910), normalized to Renilla luciferase, and recorded with a Tecan microplate reader (Tecan Infinite Pro 200).

Statistical Analysis

Normal distribution was assessed using Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests or Q-Q plots.58 Correlations in the obesity cohort were analyzed using Pearson correlation. For certain analyses, data were logarithmically transformed (log10) to approximate normal distributions.

For BHB and eicosanoid analyses, plasma samples were stratified into low IL13 (<100 pg/mL or undetectable) and high IL13 (>100 pg/mL) groups. Comparisons between WT and knockout mice were performed using the Student t test. PMH experiments were analyzed using 2-way analysis of variance with repeated measures implemented as a mixed-effects model. Pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means were performed using the least significant difference test. Densitometric data are presented as means with 95% confidence intervals. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 and GraphPad Prism version 9.5.1.
