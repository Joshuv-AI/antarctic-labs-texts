The Cycle Counts of Graphs
Ryan McCulloch, Brendan D. McKay, Alireza Salahshoori, Thomas Zaslavsky

Abstract
. We prove that an inseparable graph can have any positive number of cycles with the six exceptions 2, 4, 5, 8, 9, 16, and that an inseparable cubic graph has the sole additional exception 13.

A
cycle count number
is a positive integer that is the number of cycles in some inseparable graph.
The principal result of this paper is as follows.

Theorem 1
.

The only positive integers that are not cycle count numbers are
2
,
4
,
5
,
8
,
9
, and
16
.

Figure 1.
Graphs that prove 10, …, 15 and 17, 18, 19 are cycle count numbers.

Since the number of cycles is unchanged upon subdividing edges, or the reverse operation, Theorem
1
is equally true for multigraphs and simple graphs.
However, by “graph” we mean to allow multiple edges and loops; results do not necessarily hold for simple graphs. The following is an example.

Theorem 2
.

The only positive integers that are not cycle counts of inseparable cubic graphs are
1
,
2
,
4
,
5
,
8
,
9
,
13
, and
16
.
The same positive integers are the only ones that are not cycle counts of inseparable planar cubic graphs.

These results are Sequences A385523 and A385524 in the Online Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences
[
4
]
.

The proof is in three parts. The first part is a detailed analysis of graphs with small cycle counts, proving nonexistence of the exceptional cases. The second part establishes a connection to subtree counts of trees, enabling us to prove all cases except a finite list ending at 89. The third part presents the results of a computer search that found graphs for all numbers in that finite list.

Let
c(G)
be the number of cycles in
G
. This number depends only on the homeomorphism type of
G
, so we may freely introduce new vertices by subdividing edges, or execute the reverse operation of suppressing divalent vertices.
We denote by
\widehat{G}
any graph that is homeomorphic to
G
(i.e., they have isomorphic subdivisions). All our graphs are assumed to be inseparable, which means connected and having no cutpoint (such as a vertex that supports a loop and is incident with another edge) but not necessarily simple. Inseparability is the same as 2-connectivity, except that the graph with one vertex and one loop is inseparable without being 2-connected.

An
ear
in a graph is a path (with distinct endpoints) of positive length whose internal vertices, if any, have degree 2, and whose endpoints have degree greater than 2.
When adding an ear, we allow ourselves to subdivide edges to become the endpoints of the ear.
It is known that every inseparable graph is a cycle or obtained from a cycle by successively adding ears, and that when we add an ear to an inseparable graph, the result is always inseparable (
[
6
, Theorem 19]
,
[
5
, Theorem III.11]
). The opposite action is deleting an ear, which involves removing the edges and internal vertices of the ear but not its endpoints.
We assume this is only done to leave an inseparable remaining graph.

1.
Proof for numbers from 1 to 19

First, we prove Theorem
1
is true for numbers from 1 to 3. For
c(G)=1
,
G
is a cycle. For higher
c(G)
we must add an ear, which gives a theta graph. The theta graph has 3 cycles. Thus, 2 is impossible and 3 is possible.

Next we state a valuable though simple lemma whose correctness is obvious.

Lemma 3

(Ear-Path Lemma)
.

Let
G
be a graph with vertices
v
and
w
. If an ear is added to
G
with endpoints
v
and
w
, the number of new cycles created equals the number of
vw
-paths in
G
.

Thus, if
G
is inseparable, then by Menger’s Theorem adding an ear must create at least two new cycles.

The notation
G/S
means the contraction of
G
by
S\subseteq E
. A
minor
of
G
is any contraction of a subgraph.

Proposition 4

(Isotonicity)
.

Let
G
be inseparable with
P
an ear of
G
. Then

(a)

c(H)\leq c(G)
for every minor
H
of
G
,

(b)

c(H)<c(G)
for every proper subgraph
H
of
G
, and

(c)

c(G/P)=c(G)
if and only if
G\smallsetminus P
is separable.

Proof.

Deleting an edge
e
from
G
reduces
c(G)
by the number of cycles on
e
, which is positive because
G
is inseparable.

Contracting an ear
P
with endpoints
v
and
w
reduces
c(G)
by the number of cycles
C
that contain both
v
and
w
but not
P
. This number is positive if and only if there is no cutpoint separating
v
and
w
in
G\smallsetminus P
. The cutpoint or cutpoints make
G\smallsetminus P
separable. There is no such cutpoint if and only if
G\smallsetminus P
is inseparable.
∎

We prove a lemma that tells us how to get the most or fewest cycles by adding an ear. A vertex is
divalent
if its degree is 2, and
multivalent
if it has degree
>2
. Note that an inseparable
G
will have at least two multivalent vertices unless it is a cycle.

Lemma 5

(Extremality Lemma)
.

Considering all subdivisions
\widehat{G}
of an inseparable graph
G
that is not a cycle:

(a)
The maximum number of cycles obtained by adding an ear to

\widehat{G}
is achieved (perhaps not uniquely) if the ear is added to
a pair of divalent vertices of
\widehat{G}
in different ears of
\widehat{G}
.

(b)
The minimum number of cycles obtained by adding an ear to

\widehat{G}
is achieved (perhaps not uniquely) if the ear is added to
some pair of multivalent vertices of
\widehat{G}
.

(c)
Adding a new ear joining any two vertices in one ear of
\widehat{G}
gives the same number of cycles as adding the new ear joining the endpoints of that ear.

Proof.

Suppose the ear is attached to vertices
v
and
w
of
\widehat{G}

and that
v
is a divalent vertex of
\widehat{G}
.
If
P
is the ear of
\widehat{G}
containing
v
and
x
is an end of
P
such that
the part
P_{xv}
of
P
from
x
to
v
does not include
w
, then
contracting that part of
P
effectively moves
v
to
x
and,
by Proposition
4
(a) does not increase the number of cycles.
This implies part (b).

To prove (c), suppose that
v
and
w
lie on the same ear
P
of
\widehat{G}
,
but
v
is not at the end. As before, contract
P_{xv}
.
Then
\widehat{G}\smallsetminus P^{\prime}
is separable,
w
being a cutpoint.
Therefore, by Proposition
4
(c) the number of cycles is unchanged.

Now we return to part (a). By the proof of part (b), we can assume
v
and
w

are divalent vertices. If they lie on different ears of
\widehat{G}
, we are done.
If not, by part (c) we can move
v
to
x
without changing the
cycle count. Then by the proof of part (b) we can move it to a divalent
vertex on an adjacent ear
without decreasing the cycle count. This completes the proof.
∎

2.
Proof for numbers beyond 89

We prove this by a general connection between trees and cycle counts. We construct a bijection between the subtrees of a tree and a graph that has one cycle for every subtree, and we apply a theorem of Czabarka, Székely, and Wagner
[
1
]
.

A graph is
outerplanar
if it has a plane embedding with all vertices on the outer face. The
inner dual
of an inseparable outerplanar graph is the planar
dual without the outside face. It is well known that the
inner dual is a tree. The following converse is also true:

Lemma 6
.

Let
T
be a tree with at least two vertices.
Then there is an inseparable outerplanar graph whose
inner dual is isomorphic to
T
.

Proof.

Draw
T
in the plane surrounded by a circle. For each edge

e
of
T
, add a chord to the circle that crosses
e
once but
does not otherwise intersect
T
or another chord (neither internally nor at an endpoint).
This is always possible, by induction: let
T
be a tree; remove a leaf from
T
, apply the
induction hypothesis to add chords, then return the leaf and
add one more chord that crosses the leaf edge.
∎

Figure
6
shows an example.
By choosing chords without common endpoints we have obtained
an inseparable outerplanar (and therefore Hamiltonian) cubic graph.

Figure 6.
An outerplanar graph (thin red lines) and the tree (heavy black lines) which is its inner dual.

Theorem 7
.

Let
G
be an inseparable outerplanar graph and let
T
be its inner dual.
Then the number of cycles in
G
equals the number of subtrees of
T
.

Proof.

A cycle of
G
together with its interior is an outerplanar graph,
so its inner dual is a subtree of
T
. We show that this gives a
bijection. Every cycle in a planar graph is the set sum (the symmetric difference) of the
boundary edges of the faces inside it. Let
S
be a subtree of
T
and consider the set sum
of the corresponding face boundaries of
G
. The vertices of
S
can be ordered
so that each vertex apart from the first is adjacent to exactly one
previous vertex. Performing the set sum of the corresponding face boundaries
of
G
in that order, each face apart from the first shares an edge
with only one of the previous faces, and that edge is unique. This
means we have a cycle at every stage, and moreover the final cycle
with its interior has
S
as its inner dual.
∎

Now we return to the proof of the main theorem.
It was shown in

1
]
that every integer other than 2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 12, 13, 14, 16, 18, 19, 22, 23, 26, 27, 29, 31, 33,
35, 38, 39, 42, 43, 46, 50, 52, 54, 60, 65, 68, 72, 77 and 89 counts the number of subtrees of a tree. (Cf.
[

, every positive integer other than those (and 1) is the number of cycles in a 2-connected graph.

5.
Consequences

Curiously, all the graphs we use to generate the cycle counts are planar (except
K_{3,3}
, but it has the planar alternate
\varTheta_{5}
). They are also Hamiltonian (those in the top row of Figure
5
are not needed for their cycle counts). That means Theorem
1
is valid for the restricted class of Hamiltonian planar graphs.

Corollary 8
.

Every positive integer is the cycle count of some inseparable Hamiltonian planar graph, except for
2
,
4
,
5
,
8
,
9
, and
16
.

A different kind of consequence is the following.

Corollary 9
.

The only inseparable graphs with fewer than
24
cycles that are not planar are
K_{3,3}
, with
15
cycles, and its subdivisions.

Proof.

Apply Kuratowski’s Theorem along with the following facts:
c(K_{5})=37
,
c(K_{3,3})=15
, and with one ear
e
added to a subdivision we have
c(\widehat{K}_{3,3}+e)\geq 24
. The latter follows from the fact in Lemma
5
(b) that adding an edge to
\widehat{K}_{3,3}
gives the fewest new cycles (9, to be exact) if the edge joins two multivalent vertices.
∎
