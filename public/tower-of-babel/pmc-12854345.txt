Application of health literacy promotion systems in sexually transmitted diseases: An altmetric analysis
Authors: Nadia Saniee, Hamid Bouraghi, Taleb Khodaveisi, Ali Ouchi, Soheila Saeedi, Hasti Mohammad Zaheri, Seyed Abedin Hoseini Ahangari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Health literacy encompasses a range of skills that individuals need to navigate the healthcare system effectively. One area where health applications can enhance health literacy is Sexually Transmitted Diseases (STDs). Therefore, this study aims to investigate research related to health literacy promotion systems in STDs through an altmetric method.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This altmetric study, conducted in 2024, examines all scientific outputs related to health literacy systems for sexually transmitted diseases indexed in Scopus from 1992 to 2023. The Altmetric Explorer was utilized to gather altmetric scores and indicators. The data were analyzed in Excel to create tables and charts.RESULTS:A search in Scopus yielded 177 articles on health literacy systems for STDs from 1992 to 2023. Only 133 (75.14%) of them were mentioned in online social media and had altmetric scores. The majority of these studies originated from the United States and the United Kingdom, published in reputable medical journals, including NEJM. The high number of citations to these articles does not mean a higher Altmetric score.CONCLUSION:The results showed that few studies have been published on the application of health literacy systems in STDs. It is suggested that future research should focus on increasing the presence of health literacy studies on STDs in social media and altmetric platforms. The presence of these studies on social media can help increase their visibility and improve society’s health literacy in STDs.

Introduction

Sexually Transmitted Diseases (STDs) are primarily spread through sexual contact and have become increasingly prevalent around the world.[1] There are at least 30 known pathogens responsible for STDs, with the most significant ones including Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Chlamydia trachomatis, trichomoniasis, syphilis, genital Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV), Human Papilloma Virus (HPV), and Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV).[2] Three bacterial sexually transmitted infections—chlamydia, gonorrhea, and syphilis—along with trichomoniasis, a parasitic STI, are currently treatable. In contrast, five viral diseases—AIDS, HPV, HSV, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C—can be chronic and last a lifetime.[3]

According to the latest statistics from the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2020, there were about 374 million new infections reported worldwide, including 129 million cases of chlamydia, 82 million cases of gonorrhea, 7 million cases of syphilis, and 156 million cases of trichomoniasis.[4] The global prevalence rates were reported as 3.8% for chlamydia, 0.9% for gonorrhea, 3.5% for trichomoniasis, and 0.5% for syphilis.[5] In Iran, the overall prevalence of STIs stands at 11.4%.[1] Given the high prevalence of these infections, it’s crucial to increase awareness and health literacy across all segments of society, particularly among young and adult populations, in light of the rise in risky sexual behaviors.

The concept of health literacy has been discussed in health literature since the 1970s.[6] The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services and the National Academy of Medicine (NAM) define health literacy as “the extent to which individuals can obtain, process, and understand basic health information and services needed to make informed health decisions”.[7] Only 12% of adults in the United States have a “high” level of health literacy, while most (53%) have only an “adequate” level.[8] This situation has been described as a “health literacy epidemic”.[9] Research in Iran shows that adults also lack sufficient health literacy regarding various health issues and diseases.[10] This highlights the urgent need to improve health literacy, especially about preventing and managing different diseases, including sexually transmitted diseases.

Today, there’s a growing interest in using health literacy systems, like mobile health applications (apps), to enhance the effectiveness and efficiency of healthcare delivery.[11] In the United States, programs are set to invest over $37 million in maintaining health information technologies over the next decade, ensuring that these resources are readily accessible to internet users. Additionally, around 76% of internet users search online for health information.[12] To effectively understand and use mobile health applications, users need to have a certain level of e-health literacy skills.[13] Therefore, staying informed about trends in research related to health literacy systems is crucial for gauging the global emphasis on the importance of health literacy.

Currently, assessing and evaluating scientific outputs is one of the most popular research topics in bibliometrics.[14] One common method for measuring research impact in bibliometric and citation studies is citation analysis.[15] However, indicators like the number of citations, journal impact factors, and average citations of articles are often insufficient for determining the factors that influence an article’s impact.[16] While citation rates may increase due to groundbreaking work, a rise in citations can also stem from controversial or inaccurate findings, self-citations, or even as a form of criticism. Moreover, citation rates do not take into account an article’s impact on online social networks.[17]

The rise of new technologies and social media has made traditional bibliometric indicators less effective in representing research impact, which has led to the adoption of complementary methods like altmetrics.[18] Altmetrics—using indicators based on social media to measure the social impact of scientific information—were first introduced by Priem and colleagues. Altmetrics focuses on the number of “mentions” of a paper across various online platforms, including blogs, digital news outlets, and social media sites like Facebook, Twitter, and YouTube.[19] Altmetric analysis of scientific outputs provides valuable insights for policymakers, research organizations, investors, and academic employers, highlighting the initial evidence of the impact that scientific contributions have on clinical policies, education, and health.[20] However, a review of the literature reveals that no altmetric studies have been conducted in this specific area. Consequently, there is a need to evaluate the productivity and social impact of scientific outputs in the domain of health literacy systems related to sexually transmitted diseases. Therefore, this study aimed to identify research related to health literacy systems in the context of sexually transmitted diseases through an altmetric method.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This quantitative study, conducted in 2024, aimed to explore the applications of health literacy promotion systems within the context of sexually transmitted diseases through an altmetric approach.

Search strategy

The research population and sample comprised all scientific outputs related to health literacy systems for sexually transmitted diseases that were indexed in Scopus from 1992 to 2023.

The search strategy in the Scopus database is as follows (A more detailed search strategy can be found in Appendix 1):

1# (health literacy OR HLQ)

2# (“mobile application” AND “mobile app” OR system OR application OR software)

3# (“sexually transmitted diseases” OR “venereal disease” OR STDs OR “sexually transmitted infections”)

1# AND 2# AND 3#

No additional limitations were applied, and all types of study documents were included. To extract altmetric scores and indicators, we used Altmetric Explorer, a service provided by the Altmetric Institute. The DOI of all documents were retrieved from Scopus into an Excel file. Next, a search was conducted in Altmetric Explorer using these DOIs. The results included data on altmetric scores, various indicators, demographic information, and other details related to health literacy systems, all of which were downloaded and saved in CSV format. As a result of the keyword search in Scopus conducted from 1992 to the end of 2023, 177 articles related to health literacy systems for sexually transmitted diseases were retrieved. Among these, 170 articles had DOIs. Since altmetric scores are calculated for articles with a DOI, these DOIs were entered into Altmetric Explorer to extract the corresponding scores and indicators. Seven articles were excluded from the study due to the lack of a DOI. For the top-ranked articles based on their altmetric scores, citation counts were extracted from Scopus, Google Scholar, and Dimensions to provide a more comprehensive overview.

Ethical considerations

This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee in Biomedical Research with ethical code “IR.ASAUMS.REC.1403.018” at the Deputy of Research and Technology in the Asadabad School of Medical Sciences.

Statistical analysis

After collecting the data, descriptive statistics were analyzed (frequency, percentage, mean) in Excel software to create tables and charts.

Results

The results from Altmetric Explorer revealed that out of 177 articles, 133 (75.14%) received mentions on online social media and had altmetric scores. Thus, 44 articles (24.86%) did not have an altmetric score. The presence of articles on health literacy systems for STDs across various online social media platforms is detailed in Figure 1.

Discussion

This study aimed to identify research related to health literacy systems in the context of sexually transmitted diseases through an altmetric method. The findings, based on altmetric data and citation analysis, underscore the prominence of certain platforms and journals in shaping scholarly communication in this domain. Mendeley emerges as the most utilized platform for sharing and saving articles, with 80.22% of the articles being mentioned there, amassing a total of 21,769 mentions. The primary reasons for the increasing use of Mendeley include its ability to help users efficiently organize their research papers and references, making categorization and access straightforward. The software automatically extracts citation information from various databases, saving valuable time during the reference-gathering process. Additionally, Mendeley facilitates easy collaboration among users, enabling the formation of research groups and the sharing of resources among team members. With both web and mobile versions available, users can access their research documents anytime and anywhere, enhancing the flexibility of their work. Mendeley also offers tools for data analysis and pattern identification across different sources, which can significantly improve the quality of research. Furthermore, the platform keeps users informed by providing access to new articles and scientific publications, ensuring they stay up-to-date with the latest developments in their fields. In line with the findings of the current study, Thelwall and Wilson found strong correlations between Mendeley readership and citation counts in medical research.[21]

Twitter, the second most utilized platform, accounted for 71.75% of article shares and totaled 2,255 mentions. However, its average mentions per article (17.75) are substantially lower than Mendeley. This finding aligns with studies suggesting that Twitter serves as a platform for immediate dissemination and public engagement rather than in-depth scholarly interaction.[22] The highest Twitter score, associated with the article “A Different Model — Medical Care in Cuba,” indicates the potential of specific topics to resonate widely within academic and non-academic communities. Interestingly, Facebook ranked third in usage, had similar average mentions per article (17.33) but significantly fewer total mentions, which could reflect its limited academic adoption compared to Twitter and Mendeley. Haustein et al.[23] pointed out that among social media, Mendeley is widely used, with 82% of articles appearing in Mendeley libraries, compared to 28% in CiteULike similar to the results of the current study. In another research, Gonzalez emphasized that social media, particularly Twitter, plays a crucial role in research dissemination, with mainstream news coverage being the most influential factor in driving engagement.[24] that align with the current study.

The results showed that most studies in the field of health literacy systems for sexually transmitted diseases were published in reputable medical journals such as NEJM. The analysis of this journal reveals that the NEJM dominates in total mentions and H-index, underscoring its reputation for high-impact publications. Eight of the top ten journals originate from the USA and the UK highlighting the centrality of these countries in health literacy research. The country-level analysis further corroborates the strong influence of the USA, which leads across all altmetric sources, including Twitter, Facebook, news outlets, and policy documents. The article with the highest altmetric score (1,370), demonstrates the combined impact of its extensive Mendeley and news mentions. These findings show how articles on innovative healthcare models can achieve widespread attention, especially on platforms like Twitter and news media.

Interestingly, a disconnect is observed between altmetric scores and traditional citations. For example, while the highest altmetric score article leads in Altmetric Explorer, its Scopus citations (1,142) are relatively moderate compared to its Altmetric score. Conversely, other highly cited works, such as those in The Lancet, exhibit lower altmetric scores, underscoring the divergent pathways of academic and social impact. These findings indicate that altmetrics offer complementary insights to traditional citation metrics, reflecting broader impacts of scholarly work across different audiences including STDs research through different platforms. In contrast with these results, Orimi et al.[25] pointed out that sharing research findings of articles in social scientific networks will increase the visibility of scientific works in the History of Medicine research, which is one of the most important factors influencing the citation of articles. From the results of their study and the current research, it can be inferred that as the presence of scientific studies on social networks increases, it may positively influence their citation counts. However, this is not absolute, and altmetrics can serve as a complementary indicator for assessing the scientific impact of research studies. Altmetrics often capture engagement from non-academic audiences on social media and blogs, which may not lead to formal citations in scholarly work. Not all discussions or shares on social media reflect the quality or significance of research. Some studies may attract attention for reasons unrelated to their scientific merit, resulting in high altmetric scores without an increase in citations. Additionally, there can be a lag between when a study gains attention online and when it is cited in academic literature. Researchers may need time to read, evaluate, and incorporate new findings into their work, which can delay citation counts. The relationship between altmetrics and citations can vary significantly across different disciplines. In some fields, traditional citation metrics may serve as stronger indicators of impact than altmetric scores. Furthermore, not all mentions on social media or in the news are meaningful; if a study is discussed superficially or misinterpreted, it may generate altmetric activity without leading to actual scholarly citations. Different academic cultures also have varying practices regarding citations. Some fields may prioritize formal publication records over social media presence, which can affect the correlation between altmetrics and citations. In highly saturated research areas, papers might receive social media attention without being cited simply because there are too many related publications competing for attention, diluting the impact of individual studies. These factors illustrate that while altmetrics can provide valuable insights into the broader impact of research, they do not guarantee increases in formal citation counts.

In line with the findings of the current study, Dehghankar et al.[26] highlighted that sexual health literacy significantly impacts women’s sexual function. Specifically, women with excellent sexual health literacy were 4.222 times more likely to have a desirable sexual function compared to those with inadequate health literacy. This study differed in methodology from the current research. Their research utilized a cross-sectional design with a descriptive-analytic approach, whereas the current study employed an altmetric research method that focused on the research outputs related to health literacy in STDs. A study conducted in Afghanistan revealed that maternal health literacy among Afghan women is insufficient. This literacy is closely linked to pregnancy outcomes, with both factors influenced by education level, age, number of pregnancies, amount of care received, and the timing of when prenatal care begins.[27] Unlike the current study, which focuses on health literacy in various groups affected by STDs—of which women are one group—the previous research specifically evaluated maternal health literacy. It is recommended that altmetric or bibliometric studies will be conducted to assess health literacy among pregnant women and other groups of the society like adolescents.

Limitations and recommendation

One notable limitation of this study is the reliance on altmetric data, which may not fully capture offline academic influence or dissemination in regions with limited digital access. Future studies could complement altmetric analysis with traditional bibliometric methods to provide a more holistic view in this field. Another potential limitation is the reliance on Altmetric Explorer as the only altmetric tool. Other platforms, such as PlumX, could provide additional insights. Moreover, exploring qualitative aspects, such as the nature of discussions around these articles on social media, could offer deeper insights into the societal impact of health literacy research regarding STDs. Another limitation was extracting data from only Scopus, while documents about STDs may be published in other databases like PubMed and Web of Science.

Conclusion

The results of this altmetric study showed that there have been published few studies on the application of health literacy systems in sexually transmitted diseases. However, most of these studies have been published in reputable medical journals and are primarily from developed countries. Also, most of them have appeared in social media. The high number of citations to articles does not mean a higher altmetric score. However, presence on social media can help increase the visibility of studies in this field. Therefore, given the limited number of studies in this area, it is suggested that more research be conducted by investigators in this field, considering the significant burden of this disease and the importance of health literacy systems for the general public. This could have a notable impact on the prevention and control of these diseases. Furthermore, it is recommended that research managers and policymakers emphasize the importance of social media presence to researchers in this field. On the other hand, conducting complementary studies on the quality of research in this area and its relationship with altmetric scores and citation counts could provide better insights into the significance of these studies.
