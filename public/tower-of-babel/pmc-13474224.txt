Development and external validation of an online interpretable machine-learning model for predicting delirium risk in acute heart failure
Authors: Weijie Huang, Shijun Tong, Lang Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDelirium is a frequent complication in acute heart failure and is associated with poor outcomes in the intensive care unit. Machine learning methods can leverage high-dimensional clinical data to support early risk stratification; however, models specifically designed to predict delirium in acute heart failure are limited, underscoring the need for reliable and clinically applicable tools.MethodsPatients with acute heart failure were identified using International Classification of Diseases codes, and delirium was assessed using the Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit. Baseline clinical data were subjected to feature selection using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator, Boruta and recursive feature elimination. Eight machine learning models were developed, and the optimal model was evaluated for discrimination, calibration and clinical utility, with SHapley Additive exPlanations used to interpret predictor contributions.ResultsTen key predictors were identified using three feature selection methods. The gradient boosting machine model demonstrated favourable discrimination and calibration in both internal and external validation cohorts, although the areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve of the neural network and Light Gradient Boosting Machine models were not significantly different from those of the gradient boosting model in the DeLong comparisons. SHapley Additive exPlanations identified the Glasgow Coma Scale score, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score and sedative use as major contributors to risk prediction. The final model was implemented as an online risk calculator to provide individualised risk estimates.ConclusionsThe model showed acceptable performance for estimating delirium risk in patients with acute heart failure and may support early intensive care unit risk stratification. The web-based calculator enables individualised assessment; however, prospective multi-centre validation is needed before broad clinical implementation.

Introduction

Acute heart failure (AHF) is a severe clinical syndrome characterised by persistently high rates of morbidity, mortality and hospital readmission. Patients frequently present with multi-organ dysfunction and generally poor outcomes, which are substantially influenced by demographic factors, regional variations and underlying comorbidities. 1 During an AHF episode, reduced cardiac output leads to cerebral hypoperfusion, triggering venous congestion, cerebral microthrombi formation and increased intracranial pressure. These pathophysiological processes damage neurons and disrupt the blood–brain barrier. Consequently, patients often experience marked cognitive decline, particularly in memory, executive function and processing speed. Clinically, this translates into increased in-hospital mortality, prolonged length of stay by approximately 5–10 days, higher readmission and complication rates, reduced functional independence, significantly impaired quality of life and loss of work capacity. With global population ageing and the growing burden of cardiovascular risk factors, the incidence of AHF continues to rise. The associated healthcare utilisation and economic expenditures are escalating, making AHF an urgent public health challenge requiring immediate attention and proactive intervention.2,3

Delirium is a common and serious acute neuropsychiatric disorder among hospitalised patients, characterised primarily by inattention and acute fluctuations in cognitive function. It is typically triggered by a combination of medical illnesses, medication effects and metabolic disturbances. The clinical presentation is heterogeneous; in addition to attention deficits and cognitive impairment, patients may exhibit psychomotor agitation, altered levels of consciousness and other behavioural disturbances. Delirium is particularly prevalent in intensive care unit (ICU) settings and exerts a substantial impact on patient outcomes and recovery.4–6 In recent years, accumulating evidence has revealed a strong pathophysiological link between AHF and delirium. Reduced cardiac output, systemic inflammatory activation and heightened physiological stress during AHF episodes can disrupt neural network (NN) integrity, precipitating acute cognitive impairment that may progress to delirium. This cascade not only prolongs the ICU length of stay but also markedly worsens clinical outcomes.7,8 Therefore, in the clinical management of patients with AHF, early identification of individuals at high risk of delirium and timely implementation of targeted interventions are crucial for improving long-term survival and functional recovery.

In recent years, machine-learning (ML) techniques have demonstrated tremendous potential in clinical risk prediction. Their advanced algorithmic design enables the identification of complex non-linear patterns within high-dimensional, multi-modal medical data, offering new strategies for disease risk assessment and early warning. Compared with traditional statistical approaches, ML models are better equipped to integrate rapidly expanding clinical and laboratory information, resulting in substantially improved predictive efficiency and discriminative performance. Although conventional models remain reliable for linear relationships and analyses involving small to moderate sample sizes, they often struggle to capture intricate non-linear interactions among variables and have limited adaptability to high-dimensional datasets.9,10 In contrast, ML methods can efficiently analyse large-scale datasets, uncover latent non-linear dependencies and interaction effects and identify key predictors most strongly associated with clinical outcomes. These capabilities markedly enhance modelling accuracy and generalisability.11,12 To date, studies using ML to predict delirium risk in patients with AHF remain relatively limited. Given the high incidence, rapid progression and strong association of delirium with adverse outcomes in ICU settings, developing an accurate, interpretable and clinically actionable risk prediction model is both valuable and urgently needed. In this study, we developed and validated an online prediction tool based on an interpretable ML framework to estimate the risk of AHF-related delirium. This model was designed to estimate individualised delirium risk and provide a basis for future studies evaluating risk-stratified delirium prevention strategies.

Materials and methods

Data and ethics statement

This study was conducted using data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC) databases, including MIMIC-IV version 3.1 and MIMIC-III version 1.4. MIMIC-III contains de-identified clinical information from approximately 60,000 ICU patients admitted to Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2001 and 2012, whereas MIMIC-IV includes health records for approximately 95,000 ICU patients admitted from 2008 to 2022. These databases provide extensive clinical data, including length of stay, nursing documentation, laboratory results, medication records and vital signs. Data extraction was performed using Structured Query Language (SQL) within a PostgreSQL environment. All investigators completed the National Institutes of Health Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative Programme on human participants research and obtained certification before accessing the data. This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and international ethical standards for medical research. Because all MIMIC data were fully de-identified, the requirement for additional institutional review board approval and written informed consent was waived for the present study. This retrospective observational cohort study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement.

Study population

This study utilised two publicly accessible intensive care databases, MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV, for data analysis. The overall study workflow is depicted in Figure 1. Patients with AHF were identified based on relevant International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions (ICD-9 and ICD-10) diagnostic codes. Delirium during the ICU stay was assessed using the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU). A diagnosis of delirium was established when patients met all the following criteria: (a) acute onset with a fluctuating course; (b) marked inattention; (c) disorganised thinking; and (d) altered level of consciousness. Delirium status was determined using all available CAM-ICU assessment records documented in the MIMIC databases. Because this was a retrospective database study, CAM-ICU assessments were not prospectively scheduled by the investigators but were extracted from routine ICU clinical and nursing documentation. Therefore, individual patients could have multiple CAM-ICU records during the ICU stay, although the exact assessment frequency varied according to clinical practice, patient condition, sedation or coma status and documentation availability. In accordance with the recommended use of the CAM-ICU for delirium screening in critically ill patients, patients were classified as having delirium if they had at least one valid positive CAM-ICU assessment. Patients with only valid negative CAM-ICU assessments were classified as delirium-negative. CAM-ICU records documented as ‘unable to assess’ because of coma, deep sedation or other reasons were considered non-informative and were not classified as either delirium-positive or delirium-negative. Patients without any valid CAM-ICU assessment after excluding non-informative records were excluded because their delirium status could not be reliably determined. Exclusion criteria included the following: (a) ICU length of stay <12 h; (b) age <18 years; (3) non-first ICU admission; (d) documented history of dementia, schizophrenia, traumatic brain injury or meningitis; and (d) missing >26% of key clinical variables. Individuals who met all inclusion and exclusion criteria were ultimately selected as study participants.

Variable selection and definition

This retrospective study collected detailed clinical information regarding 49 variables. Demographic characteristics and comorbidities were obtained from database records. Baseline clinical variables, including vital signs, laboratory parameters, disease severity scores and early ICU interventions, were extracted from records obtained within the first 24 h after ICU admission. Specifically, the dataset included patients’ demographic and baseline clinical characteristics, including age, weight and sex; vital signs recorded within the first 24 h after ICU admission, including heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), respiratory rate (RR), oxygen saturation (SpO2) and temperature; and disease severity scores, such as the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores. Laboratory parameters obtained within the first 24 h after ICU admission included red blood cell (RBC) count, white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet count, red cell distribution width (RDW), mean corpuscular haemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), international normalized ratio (INR), prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), anion gap (AG), partial pressure of oxygen (PO2), partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2) and creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), haemoglobin, haematocrit, bicarbonate, calcium, magnesium, phosphate, chloride, potassium, sodium, glucose, lactate and potential of hydrogen (pH) levels. Comorbidities recorded included sepsis, hypertension, acute kidney injury (AKI) stage, cerebrovascular accident (CVA), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and chronic bronchitis (CB). Therapeutic interventions initiated or recorded within the first 24 h after ICU admission were also captured, including continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), mechanical ventilation (MV), use of vasoactive agents (VP) and sedative administration. Detailed definitions and data sources for all variables are provided in Table S1. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation to reduce potential bias and maximise statistical power. Missingness patterns and proportions for all candidate variables are presented in Fig S1. Patients with >26% missingness in key clinical variables were excluded. For the remaining missing data, multiple imputation by chained equations was performed using the ‘mice’ package in R before statistical analysis and model development. The imputed dataset was then used for baseline comparisons, feature selection, model training and validation analyses.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were summarised using median and interquartile range (IQR) values. Differences in continuous variables between groups were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and between-group differences were evaluated using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. Based on mortality information from the MIMIC-IV database, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to compare time-to-event outcomes between groups, and statistical significance was assessed using the log-rank test. The MIMIC-IV dataset was randomly divided into training (70%) and internal validation (30%) sets, whereas MIMIC-III served as the external validation cohort. To minimise the risk of data leakage, feature selection was performed only on the original 70% training set after the MIMIC-IV cohort had been randomly divided into training and internal validation sets in a ratio of 7:3. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), Boruta, recursive feature elimination (RFE) and subsequent variance inflation factor (VIF) assessments were conducted without applying the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) or other resampling methods. After the final predictors had been selected, class imbalance was addressed using the SMOTE only during model training. During hyperparameter tuning, the SMOTE was embedded within the 10-fold cross-validation loop and applied only to the training portion of each fold, whereas the corresponding validation fold, internal validation cohort and external validation cohort were kept unchanged. For feature selection, we integrated the three complementary algorithms, LASSO, Boruta method and RFE. 13 LASSO introduces an L1-penalty term into the objective function, shrinking some coefficients towards zero to simplify the model structure and highlight essential predictors. To balance model complexity and predictive performance, 20-fold cross-validation was used, and the optimal regularisation parameter was selected according to the ‘lambda.1se’ criterion, ensuring a compact model with stable generalisability.14,15 Boruta is a wrapper-based feature selection method built upon random forests (RFs). By generating ‘shadow’ features from the original dataset, it constructs a baseline distribution of feature importance. Through iterative comparisons (up to 250 iterations), the algorithm identifies variables that significantly exceed the importance of their shadow counterparts, yielding a robust and stable subset of predictors. 16 RFE is a performance-driven backward elimination strategy that iteratively trains and evaluates models and removes features with limited predictive contribution, ultimately providing an optimised subset. 17 In this study, RFE was implemented using an embedded RF learner to leverage RF's strengths in capturing non-linear relationships and complex interactions. RFE is particularly robust and reproducible in high-dimensional, noise-prone datasets, effectively reducing redundancy and enhancing model generalisability. 18 The key predictors selected through these three feature selection methods are listed in Table S2.

We developed eight different ML models to predict delirium risk, including Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), NN, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN). The dataset was divided into training and internal validation sets for model development and performance assessment, allowing the identification of the best performing model. Hyperparameter tuning was conducted using grid search combined with 10-fold cross-validation, with detailed parameter settings provided in Table S3. Both internal and external validation phases included comprehensive performance evaluations. Metrics assessed were the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), DeLong test results, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), F1 score, calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA). DeLong tests were performed within the same validation cohort, using GBM as the reference model. In addition, the top-performing model was compared with commonly used ICU severity scoring systems in the internal validation cohort by assessing AUCs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), classification metrics and DeLong tests.

To address the potential redundancy and multi-collinearity between GCS and SOFA scores, we further assessed multi-collinearity among the final predictors using the VIF or adjusted generalised VIF. A VIF threshold of 5 was used to indicate potentially problematic multi-collinearity. In addition, to evaluate whether the model performance was overly dependent on the overlapping information between GCS and SOFA scores, we performed sensitivity analyses by separately excluding GCS and SOFA scores from the final predictor set and re-evaluating model performance in both internal and external validation cohorts.

The events per variable value was calculated using the original training cohort before synthetic oversampling. In this cohort, 1335 patients developed delirium. Because the final model included 10 predictors, the events per variable value was 133.5, which was substantially higher than the conventional minimum threshold of 10 events per variable, indicating that the number of outcome events was adequate for model development.

For the best performing model, we used SHapley Additive explanations (SHAP) to improve model interpretability and elucidate its prediction patterns. The SHAP summary plot was used to assess the global distribution of feature importance, whereas individual SHAP force plots quantified the direction and magnitude of each predictor's contribution to patient-specific risk estimates. Based on this model, we developed a streamlined web-based prediction tool using the ‘Shiny’ package in R. This tool provides clinicians with an interactive interface for real-time entry of patient-level variables and immediate generation of individualised delirium risk estimates, thereby supporting potential clinical translation. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2, and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value <0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study included 8340 patients with AHF from the MIMIC-IV database, with a 22.9% (n = 1906) incidence of delirium. An additional 848 patients from the MIMIC-III database were included as the external validation cohort, with a delirium incidence of 40.4% (n = 343). Detailed baseline characteristics of both cohorts are presented in Table 1. Compared with patients without delirium, those with delirium were older, suggesting a higher baseline vulnerability. Regarding comorbidities, the delirium group had significantly higher rates of sepsis and CVA and slightly lower proportion of patients with hypertension. In terms of clinical interventions, patients with delirium were more likely to be given MV, vasopressors, sedatives and CRRT, reflecting more severe critical illness and greater treatment complexity. With respect to severity of illness scores, the delirium group had significantly higher SOFA scores and markedly lower GCS scores, indicating more severe organ dysfunction and more pronounced neurological impairment. Vital signs also differed between groups, with the delirium group showing slightly higher HR and SpO2. Laboratory findings demonstrated lower RBC count, haemoglobin level and haematocrit level, along with elevated WBC count and RDW, suggesting infection, anaemia-related vulnerability and physiological stress. Coagulation parameters, including INR and PT, were prolonged, indicating a tendency towards coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas results showed significantly higher partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood (PaO2) and partial pressure of carbon dioxide in arterial blood (PaCO2) and lower pH, reflecting abnormalities in oxygenation, ventilation and acid–base balance. The delirium group also exhibited higher lactate levels, lower bicarbonate levels and varying degrees of electrolyte disturbances involving calcium, magnesium, chloride, sodium and potassium. Overall, compared with patients without delirium, patients with AHF who developed delirium tended to be older, had more complex comorbidities, required more intensive interventions, demonstrated more pronounced laboratory abnormalities and presented with higher illness severity. These findings suggest that delirium in this population reflects multi-system dysfunction and metabolic imbalance, highlighting the need for early risk identification and timely intervention to improve clinical outcomes.

Feature selection

To identify variables most strongly associated with delirium in patients with AHF, we employed three complementary feature selection approaches. First, LASSO regression was performed in the training set to enable sparse selection of candidate predictors. The distribution of regression coefficients is presented in Figure 3(a). Using 20-fold cross-validation, the optimal penalty parameter was identified as lambda.1se = 0.01491168, yielding 10 variables with predictive potential (Figure 3(b)). Second, Boruta, an RF-based feature selection algorithm, was used to further evaluate variable importance. By comparing each observed feature with its corresponding shadow feature, Boruta distinguishes strongly relevant predictors from weakly relevant or irrelevant variables, thereby improving the robustness and interpretability of feature selection. This analysis identified 37 significant variables (Figure 3(c)). Third, RFE was employed for further refinement. This approach iteratively removes less informative variables and is particularly useful for reducing redundancy in high-dimensional or noisy datasets. RFE retained 49 variables with predictive value (Figure 3(d)). The intersection of predictors selected by all three methods was then used to define the final feature set for model development. This process identified 10 core predictors, including sepsis, AKI stage, MV, sedative use, age, SOFA score, GCS score, chloride level, sodium level and PCO2 (Figure 3(e)). To assess potential multi-collinearity among these predictors, VIF analysis was performed. All 10 variables had VIF values <5, indicating no evidence of severe multi-collinearity within the final predictor set (Figure 3(f)).

Model interpretability analysis

After feature selection, class imbalance was addressed using the SMOTE only during the model training stage. During hyperparameter tuning, the SMOTE was embedded within the cross-validation procedure and applied only to the training portion of each fold, whereas the corresponding validation fold, internal validation cohort and external validation cohort were kept unchanged. Based on 10 clinically relevant predictors identified through feature selection procedures, we developed delirium risk prediction models for patients with AHF using eight ML algorithms, including LR, SVM, GBM, NN, KNN, XGBoost, AdaBoost and LightGBM. Model discrimination was evaluated in the internal and external validation cohorts using the AUC (Figure 4(a) and (b)). Among the candidate algorithms, GBM demonstrated the best overall performance, with an AUC of 0.811 (95% CI: 0.792–0.829) in the internal validation cohort and 0.766 (95% CI: 0.734–0.797) in the external validation cohort. In the internal validation cohort, the GBM model achieved an accuracy of 0.743 (95% CI: 0.725–0.760), sensitivity of 0.758 (95% CI: 0.722–0.792), specificity of 0.738 (95% CI: 0.718–0.757), precision of 0.462 (95% CI: 0.430–0.494) and F1 score of 0.574 (95% CI: 0.549–0.599). The corresponding values in the external validation cohort were 0.711 (95% CI: 0.680–0.741), 0.825 (95% CI: 0.781–0.862), 0.634 (95% CI: 0.591–0.675), 0.605 (95% CI: 0.560–0.648) and 0.698 (95% CI: 0.665–0.729), respectively.

Analysis of model interpretability

To enhance the clinical interpretability of the optimised GBM model, we applied SHAP, an approach grounded in cooperative game theory. SHAP uses the principle of fair Shapley value allocation to quantify the independent contribution of each input variable to the model output, which helps mitigate the inherent black box nature of complex ML models and improves their interpretability and consistency. 19 The SHAP summary plot (Figure 5(a)) illustrates the distribution of feature contributions across all patients. Under the default colour gradient, higher feature values (yellow) are generally associated with larger positive SHAP values, indicating increased delirium risk, whereas lower values (purple) tend to correspond to smaller or negative contributions, suggesting reduced risk. The bar plot of mean absolute SHAP values (Figure 5(b)) shows that GCS and SOFA scores, use of sedatives and MV are the most influential predictors in the model. In the context of ML, feature importance scores reflect the relative impact of each variable on model decisions, with higher scores indicating greater predictive relevance (Figure 5(c)). To further strengthen case-level interpretability, we generated individual SHAP force plots for two representative patients (Figure 5(d) and (e)), providing a clear visualisation of the positive and negative directional effects of each feature on the predicted risk for that specific individual.

In addition, we generated SHAP dependence plots to explore potential interactions among key predictors (Figure S2). Taking the use of MV as an example, clear co-varying patterns of SHAP values were observed between MV and variables such as the GCS and SOFA scores as well as sedative use. Among patients receiving MV, the model consistently produced higher predicted delirium risk, reflecting synergistic effects among these factors on the final output. This type of visualisation markedly improves the transparency of model interpretation, allowing clinicians to intuitively understand how individual predictors interact during the prediction process and providing a more evidence-based foundation for personalised risk assessment and clinical decision making.

Development of an online prediction tool

To facilitate real-world clinical implementation, we transformed the top-performing GBM model into an interactive online prediction tool. This web-based application uses key clinical features from AHF patients to estimate individualised delirium risk during ICU stay. Through an intuitive visual interface, clinicians can enter patient information and immediately obtain personalised risk predictions (Figure S3), thereby receiving guidance for risk stratification and clinical decision making. Details of the model variables and parameter settings are presented in Table S6. The online tool is accessible at: https://clinical-machine-learning.shinyapps.io/build_sphere/.

Discussion

This study developed and validated an ML-based predictive model to assess the risk of delirium in patients with AHF during ICU stay. Data were extracted from both MIMIC-IV and MIMIC-III databases, including 51 clinical variables recorded within the first 24 h after ICU admission. After selecting key features using LASSO regression, Boruta algorithm and RFE, eight ML models were constructed for performance comparison. The results showed that the GBM model achieved favourable overall performance, with AUC values of 0.811 and 0.766 in the internal and external validation cohorts, respectively. However, given the moderate performance in the external validation cohort and absence of prospective multi-centre validation, the model's generalisability should be interpreted cautiously. The markedly different incidence of delirium between the MIMIC-IV cohort and MIMIC-III external validation cohort deserves careful consideration. In this study, the incidence of delirium was 22.9% in the MIMIC-IV database and 40.4% in the MIMIC-III database. This difference may be partly explained by differences in database versions, study periods, ICU clinical practice, delirium screening procedures, CAM-ICU documentation patterns and patient case mix. In particular, variations in sedation practice, MV use, severity of illness and frequency of CAM-ICU assessment may have contributed to the higher delirium rate observed in the external validation cohort. Such variations in outcome prevalence may influence model calibration, PPV, NPV and interpretation of predicted probabilities, even when discrimination remains acceptable. Therefore, although the GBM model maintained reasonable performance in the external validation cohort, its external validity should be interpreted cautiously, and local recalibration may be necessary before implementation in clinical settings with substantially different delirium prevalence. Compared with several commonly used critical care scoring systems, including the APS III, SAPS II, SIRS, OASIS, CCI and APACHE II, the GBM model showed a higher AUC for predicting AHF-related delirium in the internal validation cohort. This difference was further supported by DeLong comparisons using GBM as the reference model, suggesting that the improved discrimination was not based solely on single-threshold classification metrics. To further interpret model decision mechanisms, we employed SHAP for feature-level visualisation and attribution. Based on this model, we developed an interactive online risk calculator capable of real-time, individualised risk estimation. This tool may provide individualised risk estimates for patients with AHF in the ICU; however, whether its use improves clinical processes or patient outcomes requires prospective evaluation.

Delirium is one of the most common neuropsychiatric complications among critically ill patients in the ICU. In individuals with AHF, hemodynamic instability may result in cerebral hypoperfusion, which is considered a primary mechanism contributing to delirium. Meanwhile, AHF is frequently accompanied with systemic inflammatory responses, and inflammatory mediators may impair central nervous system function, thereby diminishing cognitive reserve and markedly increasing delirium risk. Notably, delirium is strongly associated with prolonged hospitalisation, increased mortality and long-term deterioration in quality of life among older adults.20,21 However, clinical recognition of AHF-related delirium remains suboptimal in ICU settings, which may be partially attributed to insufficient awareness among healthcare professionals. Therefore, enhancing clinicians’ and nurses’ knowledge regarding delirium and training them for its early identification in high-risk older patients is of critical importance. Currently, commonly used delirium assessment tools in the ICU include the CAM-ICU (sensitivity: approximately 80% and specificity: approximately 93%) and the Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist (ICDSC) (sensitivity: approximately 80% and specificity: approximately 61%).6,22 Nevertheless, clinical experience suggests that some nursing staff members lack adequate understanding of delirium pathophysiology and proper assessment techniques. Along with insufficient standardised training, assessment procedures may be inconsistently performed, potentially compromising diagnostic accuracy. 23 In addition, delirium screening often relies on patient cooperation, whereas ICU patients frequently exhibit impaired consciousness, agitation or intubation, making it difficult for them to follow commands accurately. These factors can make the assessment process time consuming and cumbersome, which does not align well with the fast-paced workflow of critical care settings and ultimately limits widespread implementation.24,25 In comparison, the online prediction tool developed in this study based on the best performing ML model enables early and rapid identification of delirium risk in patients with AHF and may provide useful decision support for clinical management. However, despite showing acceptable preliminary performance in retrospective validation, further prospective multi-centre studies are required to evaluate its transportability, calibration and clinical impact across heterogeneous populations and healthcare settings. Such investigations will help objectively determine its clinical applicability and scalability, providing stronger evidence for future clinical practice.

Several studies have been conducted to develop predictive models for estimating the likelihood of delirium among critically ill patients in the ICU. Building upon previous work, the present study specifically focuses on the AHF population and offers important methodological and clinical advance. Based on their systematic review and meta-analysis, Niu et al. have reported that delirium is a common neuropsychiatric complication in patients with AHF and may significantly increase the risk of its occurrence. 20 Pak et al. applied Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and found that patients with AHF complicated by delirium had significantly higher 90-day mortality rates than those without delirium, further supporting the close association between delirium and adverse outcomes. 21 In addition, Iwata et al. used multi-variable LR analysis and identified advanced age and dementia as independent risk factors for delirium. 7 However, the diagnostic criterion of delirium focuses on acute onset cognitive changes, whereas cognitive impairment in dementia typically presents with chronic persistence without acute fluctuations, which often results in underdiagnosis or misclassification. 26 In contrast, our study rigorously excluded patients with dementia and schizophrenia during model development to minimise classification bias, due to which, chronic cognitive impairment may be mistaken for acute delirium, thereby ensuring that predictive results better reflect the true clinical characteristics of patients with AHF. Regarding model performance, the predictive model in this study demonstrated high AUC values and overall accuracy in both internal and external validation cohorts, with sensitivity and specificity superior to those reported in previous studies. To enhance accessibility, we designed and deployed an interactive online prediction tool that allows rapid estimation of AHF-associated delirium risk. Its effect on clinical decision making and patient outcomes should be evaluated in future prospective studies.

Feature selection is a crucial component in the development of predictive models. To preliminarily identify potential predictors, we systematically reviewed existing literature related to delirium risk factors.27–29 To further enhance model robustness and clinical interpretability, we applied three complementary variable selection strategies: LASSO regression, Boruta algorithm and RFE. LASSO introduces a penalty coefficient to achieve feature sparsity, which helps eliminate redundant variables. Boruta evaluates variable importance based on an RF framework to identify predictors with substantial contribution to the outcome. RFE incrementally removes less relevant features to identify the optimal subset. Following cross-validation and integrated analysis using these methods, a final set of 10 core predictors was determined, each independently associated with delirium risk among patients with AHF, thereby ensuring representativeness, stability and clinical applicability of model inputs. Kawazoe et al. and Correale et al. have reported that advanced age, sedative administration and the use of MV are important risk factors for delirium, which is consistent with our findings.30,31 However, these studies did not further develop an online ML-based prediction tool, which limits clinical accessibility and practical value. In comparison, we deployed the best performing ML model as an interactive web-based calculator capable of predicting delirium risk in patients with AHF within a few seconds. Application of this model in high-risk populations may enable earlier risk identification and timely intervention, thereby reducing delirium incidence and improving patient outcomes.

The application of ML techniques in medical research and clinical decision support has continued to expand. Compared with traditional LR models, ML algorithms offer stronger non-linear fitting capabilities, allowing them to capture complex higher order interactions among variables. In addition, ML methods rely on efficient computational frameworks that enable multivariate modelling of high-dimensional data, thereby substantially improving predictive accuracy and generalisability.32,33 In the present study, the predictive performance of eight mainstream ML algorithms was systematically evaluated, and model development incorporated grid search-based hyperparameter tuning, 10-fold cross-validation and the SMOTE algorithm. The DeLong test was applied to compare the AUCs of different models within the same validation cohort, with GBM used as the reference model. We found that the AUCs of NN and LightGBM were not significantly different from that of GBM in both internal and external validation cohorts, whereas the remaining models showed statistically significant differences compared with GBM. However, model selection was not based solely on AUC or DeLong test results. Considering the overall performance across discrimination, calibration, Brier score, C-index, Kappa value and clinical utility, GBM was selected as the final optimal predictive model.

In contemporary medical research and clinical practice, the interpretability of ML models has become a critical determinant for their successful clinical translation. Due to the inherently opaque and often black box characteristics of many algorithms, the lack of interpretability has long limited their acceptance and application in real-world healthcare settings. To address this challenge, the present study adopted SHAP analysis to systematically interpret model features and enhance model transparency and interpretability. The primary advantage of SHAP lies in its ability to quantitatively estimate the marginal contribution of each variable to the prediction and also revealing interactions and directionality of effects, thereby providing both global and patient-level interpretability. In this study, SHAP values were calculated and visualised based on the GBM model. A global overview of feature importance was presented and graphical outputs demonstrated the positive and negative influence of each predictor on the model outcome. This approach facilitates clinicians’ understanding of the pathophysiological rationale underlying ML outputs and strengthens their confidence in the model as a clinical decision support tool.34,35 Additionally, we compared traditional GBM-derived feature importance with SHAP-based interpretability results. A bar plot reflecting the contribution of each variable was generated using the built-in importance weights of the GBM model. SHAP analysis showed that GCS and SOFA scores, sedative use and MV were among the most important predictors in the GBM model. Although GCS and SOFA scores were the important predictors, their importance should be interpreted as markers of early neurological status and global organ dysfunction rather than causal determinants of delirium. This interpretation is consistent with the clinical context of patients with AHF in the ICU, in whom neurological impairment, organ dysfunction, sedative exposure and respiratory support often coexist and jointly reflect increased vulnerability to delirium. 36 This discrepancy is rooted in fundamental methodological differences. GBM feature importance reflects the average contribution of each variable across the entire model and is therefore a global interpretive measure. In contrast, SHAP is based on cooperative game theory and calculates marginal contributions at the individual sample level, allowing the identification of localised non-linear effects and variable interactions. Consequently, SHAP not only clarifies the magnitude and direction of feature effects but also uncovers inter-patient heterogeneity, offering more transparent and clinically meaningful interpretive insight compared with conventional GBM analysis.37,38 Taken together, SHAP analysis helped elucidate the internal prediction patterns of the selected model and may support more transparent interpretation in future clinical applications.

Certain limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, because this was a retrospective analysis, the study was inevitably susceptible to selection bias, and the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causality. Second, although external validation was conducted using a subset of the MIMIC-III database, the predictive performance and applicability of the model across different geographic regions, ethnic groups and healthcare systems need to be further assessed in large-scale, prospective, multi-centre studies. Third, the relatively limited sample size may have affected the stability and generalisability of some ML algorithms. To address class imbalance, the SMOTE was applied in the training cohort to increase the proportion of minority-class samples and reduce potential bias. However, this synthetic sampling process may have introduced differences between the generated samples and real-world clinical distributions, thereby affecting the model's translational performance in practice. Future studies should use prospective multi-centre designs to evaluate the robustness and clinical impact of the model in real-world settings as well as incorporate longitudinal dynamic information on laboratory parameters and vital signs to further improve its predictive accuracy and clinical utility. Finally, the incidence of delirium differed substantially between the development and external validation cohorts, which may reflect differences in database periods, CAM-ICU screening and documentation practices, patient severity and ICU management patterns. This shift in outcome prevalence may affect calibration and predictive values; therefore, further prospective multi-centre validation and, where appropriate, local recalibration are needed before broad clinical implementation.

Conclusion

This study developed and evaluated multiple ML-based models to predict delirium risk in critically ill patients with AHF. GBM was selected as the final model after comprehensive evaluation of discrimination, calibration, Brier score, C-index, Kappa value and clinical utility. However, NN and LightGBM models showed comparable AUCs, indicating that several ML algorithms achieved similar predictive performance in this task. The selected model may assist clinicians in early delirium risk stratification and provide individualised risk estimates through a web-based calculator. Nevertheless, its generalisability, clinical impact and implementation value require further evaluation in prospective multi-centre studies before broad clinical application.
