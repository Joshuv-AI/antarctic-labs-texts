The updated role of exosomes in cancer diagnosis and therapy
Authors: Xinlong Huang, Yafei Huang, Xiangcong Wei, Ya Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExosomes are nanoscale extracellular vesicles secreted by cells via endosomal pathways. They mediate intercellular communication by transporting bioactive molecules, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. During tumorigenesis, exosomes critically participate in multiple oncogenic processes, including metastatic dissemination, immune evasion, tumor microenvironment remodeling, angiogenesis, and the development of chemoresistance. Comprehensive characterization and molecular profiling of vesicular surface epitopes are essential for tracing their origins. These membrane-bound biomarkers not only facilitate precise vesicle classification but also enable the selective enrichment of tissue-specific molecular signatures that are crucial for targeted therapeutic applications. Accumulating evidence indicates that tumor-derived exosomes have emerged as promising diagnostic biomarkers with exceptional specificity, while their intrinsic biocompatibility and cargo-loading capacity confer transformative potential for next-generation drug delivery platforms. This review critically examines exosome roles in tumor biology. It highlights clinical applications in oncology. We focus on exosome biomarkers in liquid biopsy, engineered exosomes for drug delivery, and pharmacological or genetic strategies targeting exosome biogenesis.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Exosomes, Tumor, Diagnosis, Therapy, Drug delivery

Introduction

Exosomes are nanosized extracellular vesicles (EVs) (30–150 nm) released by nearly all mammalian cells through multivesicular body (MVB) pathways. Enclosed by a lipid bilayer and devoid of intracellular organelles, they shuttle diverse bioactive cargos between cells and thereby serve as important mediators of intercellular communication. Exosomes are widely present in multiple body fluids, including the blood, urine, saliva, and cerebrospinal fluid [1]. In 1981, Trams and colleagues first identified membrane-bound vesicles in the supernatant of cultured sheep erythrocytes and introduced the term “exosomes” to describe these nanosized extracellular particles [2]. Exosome biogenesis initiates with the formation of MVB within the endosomal compartments, where inward budding of endosomal membranes generates intraluminal vesicles (ILVs). During MVB trafficking to the plasma membrane, these vesicular structures undergo Rab GTPase-regulated docking and membrane fusion, culminating in the extracellular release of ILVs as functional exosomes (Fig. 1). A subset of MVBs can also be selectively routed to lysosomes through the endolysosomal degradation pathway, where membrane fusion facilitates cargo degradation [3]. ILV formation is regulated by both endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT)-dependent and -independent pathways. The four ESCRT subunits (ESCRT-0 to ESCRT-III) sequentially coordinate MVB biogenesis through membrane remodeling, ubiquitinated cargo sorting, and trafficking fate determination, directing MVBs either to lysosomes or the cell surface [4]. The ESCRT-independent pathway relies on lipid-rich components, including cholesterol, ceramide, sphingomyelin, and tetraspanins, with ceramide playing a key role in raft-like structure formation during ILV generation [5].

Exosomes were initially regarded as vesicles involved primarily in cellular waste disposal, with limited biological significance. However, paradigm-shifting studies have redefined them as active molecular shuttles that selectively package and transfer parental cell-derived biomolecules, including functional proteins, regulatory RNAs, and bioactive lipids, thereby facilitating targeted intercellular communication [6]. Beyond their fundamental roles in cell‑to‑cell signaling, exosomes have emerged as versatile liquid biopsy platforms, carrying disease‑specific biomarkers with substantial diagnostic value in diverse malignancies and inflammatory disorders [7]. Importantly, although some native and especially engineered exosome/EVs systems have shown the ability to interact with or traverse the blood-brain barrier under defined conditions, this is not a universal feature of all exosomes, and current evidence remains largely preclinical [8]. In cancer biology, exosomes serve as central mediators of tumor-stroma crosstalk, orchestrating multistep carcinogenesis and metastatic progression. By horizontally transferring oncogenic proteins, nucleic acids, and lipid mediators, exosomes remodel the tumor microenvironment (TME), promote angiogenesis, and facilitate extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling [9, 10]. These processes collectively establish pre‑metastatic niches (PMNs) by providing metabolic support, delivering growth factors, and inducing immunosuppressive conditioning at distant sites, thereby creating permissive microenvironments for metastatic colonization [11, 12]. This review provides a comprehensive update on the multifaceted role of exosomes in oncology, highlighting their applications as liquid biopsy biomarkers, their potential as engineered drug delivery platforms, and the pharmacological and genetic modulation of exosome biogenesis as a translational therapeutic strategy.

Literature search strategy: A structured literature search was performed in PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Google Scholar for studies published from inception to December 2025 using combinations of the terms “exosome,” “extracellular vesicle,” “cancer,” “diagnosis,” “biomarker,” “liquid biopsy,” “therapy,” and “drug delivery.” Reference lists of relevant studies were also screened manually. We included English-language original articles, clinical studies, and key reviews related to exosome-based cancer diagnosis and therapy, while excluding duplicate reports, conference abstracts without full data, studies not focused on cancer, and articles lacking sufficient methodological detail. This review was conducted as a narrative synthesis, and studies were selected according to relevance and translational importance.

The role of exosomes in cancer

Studies have shown that tumor-derived exosomes (TDEs) play a pivotal role in cancer progression by promoting angiogenesis, ECM remodeling, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) induction, invasion, migration, immune modulation, PMN formation, and drug resistance (Fig. 2). Consequently, exosome depletion has been proposed as a potential strategy to inhibit tumor progression [13].

The effect of TDEs on the growth and metastasis of cancer

Tumor angiogenesis, a fundamental process that ensures oxygen and nutrient delivery to neoplastic tissues, is indispensable for tumor progression and metastasis [14]. TDEs orchestrate this process not only by transferring canonical pro-angiogenic proteins, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), fibroblast growth factor (FGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-8 (IL-8) [15], but also by delivering bioactive noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) that stimulate endothelial cells and remodel the vasculature [16]. Glioblastoma-derived exosomes enriched with miR-221, glypican-1 and syndecan-4 synergistically activate the PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK signaling pathways in endothelial cells (ECs), thereby promoting ECs proliferation, tubulogenesis [16], and vascular network maturation [17]. Under hypoxic stress, HIF-1α enhances the exosomal loading of ncRNAs, including miR‑494, miR‑210, and miR‑92a, which act on ECs by suppressing PTEN, SMAD4, or integrin α5, thereby activating PI3K/AKT, eNOS, and MAPK/ERK signaling cascades [18–20]. These events promote ECs proliferation, migration, and tube formation. In parallel, exosomal long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) derived from glioma and Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), such as HOTAIR, CCAT2, POU3F3, and circRNA_100338 promote angiogenesis by upregulating VEGF, TGF‑β, and FGF, inhibiting pro-apoptotic pathways (Bax/caspase-3), and stabilizing vascular networks [21–24]. Collectively, these exosome-mediated mechanisms create a permissive microenvironment that accelerates malignant progression.

EMT is a central driver of cancer invasiveness, while ECM remodeling provides the structural foundation for tumor expansion and dissemination. TDEs coordinate both processes by delivering bioactive cargo, including miRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs, and proteins, to stromal and cancer cells. These molecules activate key signaling pathways, including TGF-β/SMAD [25], Wnt/β-catenin [26], PI3K/AKT [27], and NF-κB [28], collectively inducing cytoskeletal reorganization, downregulation of epithelial markers (e.g., E-cadherin), and upregulation of mesenchymal traits (e.g., N-cadherin, vimentin) [29]. For instance, exosomal miR‑92a‑3p suppresses PTEN in liver and colorectal cancers, activating the PI3K/AKT pathway to drive EMT and metastasis [30], while exosomal miR‑181 enhances breast cancer cell invasion through Wnt/β-catenin activation [31]. In gastric cancer, exosomal lncRNA HOTTIP upregulates HMGA1, inducing EMT and contributing cisplatin resistance [32]. Moreover, cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF)-derived exosomes transfer Snail1 protein and IL‑6, which inhibit E-cadherin and activate STAT3/NF‑κB signaling, respectively, in recipient lung (A549) and bladder cancer cells, reinforcing aggressive phenotypes [33, 34]. Beyond transcriptional reprogramming, TDE-driven EMT is tightly linked to ECM remodeling. Exosomal cargo such as MMP‑2, MMP‑9, MMP‑13, fibronectin, integrin α3, and annexins degrade ECM components, enhance vascular permeability, and generate migratory tracks for tumor cells [35–37]. These structural changes facilitate intravasation and promote the survival of circulating tumor cells (CTCs) by conferring anoikis resistance and supporting pre-metastatic niche formation [38]. Collectively, these findings highlight the multifaceted role of exosomal cargo in orchestrating EMT through integrated transcriptional, signaling, and ECM-modulatory mechanisms, thereby accelerating tumor progression, metastasis, and therapy resistance.

The immune system constitutes an intrinsic defense against tumorigenesis, and thus effective immunosuppression or immune evasion is essential for cancer progression. TDEs actively shape antitumor immunity by reprogramming diverse immune cell populations, including macrophages, natural killer (NK) cells, dendritic cells, and T lymphocytes. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) display marked functional plasticity and can polarize toward either anti-tumorigenic M1 or pro-tumorigenic M2 states [39]. Notably, TDEs deliver miR-222-3p to macrophages, activating the SOCS3/STAT3 signaling axis to drive M2 polarization and promote immune evasion [40]. Moreover, TDEs can facilitate tumor progression by upregulating PD-L1 expression, thereby attenuating CD8+ T cell-mediated cytotoxic responses [41]. In leukemia models, TDEs further suppress antitumor immunity by impairing NK cell function through NKG2D ligand shedding, collectively fostering the formation of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [42].

During metastasis, TDEs transfer a broad spectrum of bioactive cargos, including miRNAs, lncRNAs, proteins, and integrins, from primary tumors to distant organs, orchestrating the establishment of PMNs that favor metastatic colonization [12]. The formation of PMNs depends on coordinated vascular remodeling, ECM modification, and immune suppression. TDEs disrupt endothelial integrity by downregulating tight junction proteins, such as ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-5, through suppression of KLF2/KLF4. Notably, exosomal miR-25-3p derived from colorectal cancer and miR-182-5p derived from glioblastoma both enhance vascular permeability and angiogenesis. In addition, breast cancer-derived exosomes enriched in miR-939 further suppress vascular endothelial cadherin expression, thereby facilitating vascular leakage and tumor cell infiltration [43]. Organ-specific metastasis is largely governed by exosomal integrin profiles. Exosomes expressing integrins α6β4/α6β1 preferentially home to lung fibroblasts and epithelial cells, whereas αvβ5-positive exosomes target Kupffer cells in the liver [44]. Other integrin patterns, including β3 and α2β1, are associated with brain tropism, while α4 integrin has been linked to lymphatic dissemination [45, 46]. Through these integrin-dependent interactions, TDEs remodel the stromal microenvironment, induce inflammatory cytokine production, and recruit immunosuppressive immune cells, thereby conditioning distant organs for metastatic seeding [47]. Moreover, TDEs potentiate pro-tumor inflammation by recruiting bone marrow-derived macrophages and neutrophils [48]. For instance, pancreatic cancer-derived exosomal macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) induces Kupffer cell-derived TGF-β, which in turn activates hepatic stellate cells to produce fibronectin, thereby promoting the retention of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and neutrophils in the liver [49]. Likewise, breast cancer-derived exosomal miR-200b-3p suppresses PTEN in alveolar epithelial cells, leading to CCL2 production and MDSC recruitment that establishes a lung PMN [50]. Together, these events generate an immunosuppressive and pro-angiogenic niche that supports metastatic seeding and subsequent outgrowth.

TDEs and TME

The TME is a dynamic and heterogeneous ecosystem that plays a central role in tumor progression, metastatic dissemination, and therapeutic resistance. It consists of tumor cells, stromal elements such as tumor-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and vasculature, infiltrating immune cells, and a complex network of soluble mediators including cytokines and chemokines [10]. Within this ecosystem, TDEs function as critical mediators of intercellular communication by delivering oncoproteins, growth factors, and non-coding RNAs, including miRNAs and lncRNAs, to recipient cells, thereby driving angiogenesis, immune suppression, metabolic adaptation, and metastatic priming [9]. Through both cell-intrinsic and microenvironmental mechanisms, TDEs orchestrate multifaceted oncogenic programs and provide a mechanistic rationale for therapeutic intervention in metastatic and immunosuppressive tumors.

TDEs profoundly reshape the immune landscape of the TME by reprogramming both innate and adaptive immune cells. They impair cytotoxic T cell function through PD-L1 transfer and promote the expansion of regulatory T cells, collectively attenuating antitumor immunity [41, 51]. In parallel, exosomal lncRNAs such as LNMAT1 and LINC01094 enhance the recruitment and M2-like polarization of TAMs via CCL2 and CCL7-related signaling, thereby reinforcing an immunosuppressive niche [52, 53]. Tumor exosomal cargos can also inhibit dendritic cell maturation and suppress NK cell activity, further facilitating immune escape [54]. These immunomodulatory effects position TDEs as major drivers of immune evasion and resistance to immune checkpoint blockade.

Beyond immune remodeling, TDEs contribute substantially to metabolic adaptation within the TME. Hypoxia and nutrient deprivation stimulate tumor cells to release exosomes enriched with miRNAs and lncRNAs that promote glycolytic reprogramming, lactate accumulation, and extracellular acidification, thereby reinforcing a tumor-supportive metabolic milieu [55]. This metabolic shift not only enhances tumor survival and proliferation but also suppresses effector immune responses, generating a self-sustaining pro-tumor circuit. TDEs further promote angiogenesis and structural remodeling by delivering pro-angiogenic proteins and ncRNAs to endothelial cells, where they activate VEGF and PI3K/AKT signaling to stimulate vascular sprouting and facilitate metastatic dissemination [56]. At the same time, TDEs reprogram CAFs and ECM components, thereby promoting EMT, stromal stiffening, and pre-metastatic niche formation [53]. Together, these coordinated vascular, stromal, immune, and metabolic changes drive the aggressive and organotropic behavior of advanced tumors.

Given these multifaceted functions, TDEs have emerged as attractive therapeutic targets and delivery platforms. Engineering TDEs as intelligent carriers for antitumor agents, or selectively intercepting immunosuppressive exosomes, may represent a next-generation strategy to overcome TME-driven therapy resistance [57]. Their central role in integrating immune, angiogenic, and metabolic reprogramming identifies TDEs as pivotal hubs in TME remodeling and promising targets for combinatorial cancer therapy.

TDEs mediate tumor drug resistance

Exosomes function as intercellular carriers for bioactive molecules and mediate signaling pathways that are frequently hijacked in cancer to drive therapeutic resistance. Chemoresistant tumor cells exploit exosomal machinery through three major mechanisms. First, exosomes promote drug sequestration and efflux by serving as extracellular reservoirs or export vehicles, thereby lowering intracellular drug concentrations [58]. ABC (ATP-binding cassette) transporters, including ABCB1 and ABCC1, together with exosomal P-glycoprotein, facilitate the extrusion of chemotherapeutic agents, whereas HER2-enriched vesicles act as molecular decoys that sequester trastuzumab and diminish its binding to surface HER2 in breast cancer cells [59, 60]. Second, TDEs mediate the horizontal transfer of resistance-associated cargos, including drug-metabolizing enzymes, miRNAs, and resistance-related transcripts, which functionally reprogram recipient cells. Representative examples include miR-100-5p-driven cisplatin resistance in A549 cells [61], miR-221/222‑mediated taxane resistance in breast cancer cells [62], and LncRNA‑SNHG14‑ induced trastuzumab resistance through regulation of the Bcl‑2/BAX axis [63]. In parallel, exosomes enriched in specific integrins can disseminate oxaliplatin and 5-FU resistance traits in colorectal cancer, while tumor-derived vesicles carrying ABC transporters and Akt-activating miRNAs confer multidrug resistance to endothelial cells, thereby extending chemoresistance to stromal and vascular compartments [64]. Third, exosomes indirectly reinforce microenvironmental protection by promoting ECM remodeling, EMT, adaptation to hypoxia, and immune suppression, which together further compromise therapeutic efficacy [65].

Taken together, these multilayered mechanisms, encompassing drug extrusion, dissemination of resistance determinants, stromal conditioning, and therapeutic target interception, identify TDEs as central mediators of treatment refractoriness. Their pleiotropic role provides a strong rationale for targeting exosome biogenesis, secretion, or uptake as a potential chemosensitizing strategy in precision oncology.

Diagnostic value of exosomes

Exosome biogenesis is markedly upregulated in cancer, and TDEs carry molecular cargo, including HER2, integrins, and PD-L1, that recapitulates key features of their parental tumors [66, 67]. Because, they are readily detectable in multiple biofluids, such as blood, urine, and saliva, TDEs have emerged as highly promising liquid-biopsy analytes for the noninvasive assessment of tumor-specific biomarkers [68]. In addition, the lipid bilayer of exosomes protects their molecular contents from degradation, thereby enhancing analyte stability and improving their translational utility in clinical settings [69]. Importantly, exosomal biomarkers can also provide dynamic information on therapeutic response. Elevated exosomal PD-1 and CD28 levels have been associated with better treatment outcomes in melanoma [70], whereas detection of EGFRvIII in serum exosomes enables real-time monitoring of glioblastoma [71]. Together, these findings support the concept that exosomes function as multimodal liquid-biopsy platforms for both tumor profiling and treatment monitoring (Table 1; Fig. 3).

Exosomes compared to other biomarkers

Liquid biopsy has emerged as a powerful noninvasive strategy for cancer monitoring by enabling the detection of circulating tumor-derived components, including CTCs, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), and exosomes [110]. CTCs provide direct cellular evidence of hematogenous dissemination and preserve genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic information, making them particularly valuable for characterizing metastatic competence [111] and tumor evolution [112]. However, their rarity in circulation and the technical difficulties associated with their isolation and preservation limit their broader clinical application, especially in early-stage disease [113]. ctDNA, in contrast, is well suited for monitoring tumor-associated mutations, clonal evolution, and minimal residual disease, but its low abundance within cfDNA in early-stage tumors and its susceptibility to degradation can compromise detection sensitivity [114, 115]. Compared with CTCs and ctDNA, exosomes offer advantages in abundance, stability, and cargo diversity. Their lipid bilayer protects a broad range of biomolecules, including RNAs, DNA fragments, and proteins, thereby enabling a more integrated assessment of tumor biology and heterogeneity [116]. Their tumor-associated cargo diversity also enables a broader assessment of tumor heterogeneity, partially overcoming the sampling bias inherent to single-site tissue biopsies [117]. These features position exosomes as promising complementary biomarkers within the liquid biopsy framework.

Nevertheless, their clinical implementation remains hindered by major methodological challenges, particularly the lack of standardized isolation and characterization protocols [118]. Variability among ultracentrifugation, size-exclusion chromatography, precipitation-based methods, immunoaffinity capture, and microfluidic platforms leads to inconsistencies in yield, purity, and reproducibility [119]. Accordingly, overcoming barriers in standardization, scalability, and clinical-grade production will be crucial for translating exosome-based diagnostics and therapeutics into routine practice.

Breast cancer

Breast cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide [120], underscoring the need for more sensitive and informative biomarkers for diagnosis and therapeutic monitoring. Breast cancer-derived TDEs carry oncogenic cargos that reflect the evolving molecular landscape of tumor progression, and their increased secretion supports their value in early detection, metastatic surveillance, and treatment-response assessment [121]. HER2- and EGFR-enriched exosomes provide noninvasive diagnostic signatures, particularly for the HER2 + subtype, whereas exosomal glutathione S-transferase P1 (GSTP1) overexpression mediates doxorubicin resistance through metabolic reprogramming and may therefore serve as a predictive marker of chemotherapeutic efficacy [122]. In parallel, exosomal non-coding RNAs contribute to molecular stratification of breast cancer: miR-373 is elevated in triple-negative breast cancer relative to receptor-positive subtypes [123], while metastasis-associated miR-1246 promotes proliferation and migration by suppressing CCNG2 [124]. Moreover, plasma-derived exosomes from breast cancer patients exhibit enrichment of sphingolipid/glycerophospholipid species, revealing lipidomic signatures that complement proteomic and genomic biomarkers [125].

Ovarian cancer

Ovarian cancer, the fifth leading cause of cancer-related death among women, is associated with poor survival because it is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage, underscoring the need for noninvasive strategies for early detection [126]. TDEs serve as dynamic reservoirs of diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers that reflect tumor–stromal crosstalk and mechanisms of therapeutic resistance. In epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC), exosomal CD44 promotes peritoneal metastasis by reprogramming human peritoneal mesothelial cells (HPMCs). Specifically, TDE-mediated CD44 transfer induces MMP9 secretion and disrupts the mesothelial barrier, thereby facilitating invasive dissemination; notably, pharmacologic inhibition of exosome release, such as with GW4869, can block this stromal reprogramming and attenuate metastasis [127]. In parallel, exosomal heat shock proteins, including HSP70, HSP27, and HSP90, are upregulated in patients with EOC, correlate with tumor progression, and may serve as diagnostic candidates [128]. Macrophage-derived exosomes also contribute to chemoresistance through miR-223 transfer, which activates PI3K/AKT signaling in EOC cells [129]. In addition, circulating members of the miR-200 family are elevated in EOC and show promise as early diagnostic biomarkers [130]. EOC-derived exosomes further deliver pro-angiogenic miRNAs, such as miR-205 and miR-141-3p, to endothelial cells, thereby promoting angiogenic niche formation through paracrine signaling [131]. Collectively, the integration of exosomal protein markers, resistance-associated miRNAs, and angiogenic regulators provides a multi-analyte platform for early detection and personalized therapy. Thus, TDE-based liquid biopsy may help overcome current limitations in EOC management by capturing both spatial heterogeneity and dynamic treatment responses.

Lung cancer

Lung cancer (LC), the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, is primarily divided into small-cell lung cancer (SCLC, 15–20%) and non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC, 80–85%) [132]. Emerging evidence suggests that exosomal biomarkers have considerable diagnostic and prognostic value in LC. Plasma exosomes from patients with NSCLC show increased expression of the miR-500a-3p/501-3p/502-3p clusters, indicating potential utility for early detection [133]. In addition, exosomal miR-32 has been proposed as a marker of chemotherapeutic response, as elevated levels after chemotherapy correlate with prolonged progression-free survival and overall survival in platinum-treated NSCLC patients [134]. Proteomic studies have also identified NSCLC-associated enrichment of exosomal EGFR pathway components, including EGFR, GRB2, and SRC, which may promote proliferative signaling in recipient cells [135]. Moreover, elevated exosomal lncRNA HOTAIR is associated with advanced TNM stage and lymph node metastasis [136], while plasma exosomal circRNA_0000284 overexpression predicts poor survival, supporting its value as a metastatic risk biomarker [137].

Nevertheless, the clinical translation of exosomal biomarkers remains hindered by technical challenges in biofluid processing and exosome isolation. Matrix effects in complex biofluids can compromise analytical fidelity, making rigorous and standardized isolation procedures essential [138]. Commonly used methods, such as ultracentrifugation, size-exclusion chromatography, and density gradient centrifugation, differ substantially in yield, purity, and reproducibility [139]. Therefore, harmonization of biofluid selection, isolation workflows, and analytical criteria will be critical for establishing clinically reliable exosome-based diagnostic platforms.

Bladder cancer

Bladder cancer (BC), the tenth most common cancer worldwide and the second most lethal urological malignancy, remains a major clinical challenge because of its high recurrence and progression rates, particularly in non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), which represents approximately 75% of newly diagnosed cases [78]. In this context, urine-derived exosomes have emerged as highly attractive noninvasive biomarkers because they directly reflect urothelial biology and can be readily sampled for disease detection and monitoring. A meta-analysis of 21 articles encompassing 77 studies and 3,348 participants reported a pooled sensitivity of 0.75, specificity of 0.77, and an AUC of 0.83 for urinary exosomal biomarkers in the diagnosis of BC. Notably, multi-marker exosomal panels demonstrated the best overall performance, with a pooled AUC of 0.87. RNA sequencing-based exosomal RNA panels have yielded even higher diagnostic accuracy in exploratory settings, although these findings still require cautious interpretation in light of limited cohort sizes, differences in stage distribution, and incomplete external validation [140].

Emerging high-throughput and clinical studies further support the diagnostic relevance of multiple exosomal cargos in BC. A three-lncRNA urinary exosomal signature composed of CCDC148-AS1, XLOC_006419, and RP5-1148A21.3 showed promising diagnostic value across training, validation, and double-blind validation phases, with AUCs of 0.873, 0.825, and 0.834, respectively, and outperformed urinary cytology in distinguishing NMIBC from healthy controls [77]. Similarly, a five-RNA exosomal panel including KLHDC7B, CASP14, PRSS1, MIR205HG, and GAS5 differentiated BC from healthy controls with an AUC of 0.924 and early-stage disease with an AUC of 0.910, while also correlating with tumor stage, grade, and hematuria severity [141]. In addition, urinary exosomal miRNAs such as miR-21-5p, miR-96-5p, and miR-183-5p have shown strong diagnostic performance, particularly in cytology-negative patients, with AUCs of up to 0.90 and specificities approaching 95% [141]. Proteomic analyses have further identified enrichment of EGFR pathway-associated effectors and surface glycoproteins, including NRas, EPS8L1, EPS8L2, and Mucin 4, which are linked to progression and recurrence risk [142].

Nevertheless, the broader clinical implementation of exosome-based BC assays remains limited by methodological variability, particularly in isolation and characterization workflows. Establishing standardized, reproducible, and scalable protocols will be critical for translating urinary exosomal biomarkers into reliable clinical tools that complement existing liquid-biopsy modalities such as ctDNA and CTCs.

Prostate cancer

Prostate cancer (PCa), one of the leading causes of cancer-related death among men, remains a major clinical challenge because of delayed detection, marked biological heterogeneity, and therapy resistance, particularly in metastatic castration-resistant disease (mCRPC) [143]. In this context, plasma- and urine-derived exosomes have emerged as promising biomarkers that may complement PSA screening by improving diagnostic specificity and distinguishing aggressive from indolent disease. Exosomal miRNAs, including miR-21, miR-375, miR-141, and miR-409, are frequently elevated in the serum or urine of patients with PCa and are associated with disease progression, bone metastasis, and chemoresistance [79, 80]. Mechanistically, miR-21 and miR-141 promote osteoclastogenesis and osteoblastogenesis, thereby supporting metastatic colonization in bone, whereas miR-409 facilitates EMT through repression of Ras suppressor 1, enhancing tumor invasiveness. In parallel, exosomal integrins (α3, β1, and αvβ6) and signaling proteins (c-Src, FAK, and IGF1R) contribute to stromal activation, angiogenesis, and pre-metastatic niche formation, highlighting their diagnostic and therapeutic relevance [81].

Beyond RNA cargo, proteomic analyses have identified enrichment of exosomal tetraspanins (CD9 and CD81), sphingomyelin, and CD147, which are implicated in endothelial remodeling and immune escape. Moreover, exosome-based liquid biopsy across multiple specimen types, including plasma, urine, semen, and prostatic fluid, has shown improved sensitivity and specificity over PSA alone for early detection, risk stratification, and therapeutic monitoring [143]. Nevertheless, the clinical implementation of exosome-based assays will require standardized, scalable, and reproducible isolation workflows to establish them as robust liquid-biopsy tools alongside ctDNA and CTCs.

Colorectal cancer

Colorectal cancer (CRC), one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, remains a major clinical challenge because of its frequent late-stage presentation and the limited sensitivity of conventional biomarkers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and CA19-9, especially for early detection [87]. In this context, exosomes have emerged as highly promising biomarkers because they are abundant, stable, and readily detectable in plasma, serum, and other biofluids. Current biomarker research has focused predominantly on exosomal ncRNAs. Serum exosomal miR-21, miR-29a, miR-92a, and miR-143 are implicated in CRC proliferation, invasion, and chemoresistance through Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling [144]. In addition, a study including 172 patients with CRC and 164 healthy controls identified significant downregulation of serum exosomal tsRNA-Gly-5-0007, particularly in early-stage CRC, with AUC values of 0.7812 for overall CRC and 0.7726 for early-stage disease; preliminary functional analyses suggested that this tsRNA may participate in regulating cell adhesion and motility through Wnt-related signaling [89].

Beyond miRNAs and tsRNAs, other classes of exosomal ncRNAs also contribute importantly to CRC progression. Exosomal lncRNAs, including MIR4713HG and ZEB1-AS1, are associated with prognosis and immune checkpoint expression, whereas H19 and CRNDE promote metastasis via activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling [144]. Likewise, exosomal circRNAs such as circHIPK3 and circPVT1 contribute to invasion by sponging tumor-suppressive miRNAs. Proteomic analyses further expand the biomarker landscape: plasma exosomal MMP14, β1/β3 integrin subunits, and type I collagen (COL1A1) are enriched in CRC and adenomas, with MMP14 overexpression being associated with angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, and poor survival [87].

Taken together, these findings support the potential of exosome-based liquid biopsy as a more specific and stable alternative to conventional biomarkers, although standardized isolation and quantification workflows remain essential for clinical implementation.

Pancreatic cancer

Pancreatic cancer remains one of the deadliest malignancies, with a 5-year survival rate below 10%, and is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage because of nonspecific clinical manifestations and the limited sensitivity of conventional biomarkers such as CA19-9. In this context, exosomes, particularly those released by pancreatic cancer cells (PCCs), have emerged as attractive minimally invasive biomarkers, as they are enriched in the serum, pancreatic juice, and saliva of affected patients [145]. Current research has focused largely on exosomal non-coding RNAs. Under hypoxic conditions, PCC-derived exosomes enriched in miR-30b-5p and miR-27a promote angiogenesis through GJA1 inhibition and BTG2 downregulation, respectively, whereas exosomal miR-21, miR-1246, and miR-1290 activate pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs) and remodel the tumor stroma, thereby enhancing invasion and chemoresistance [95, 96]. Moreover, exosomal miR-125b-5p, which is abundant in highly metastatic pancreatic cancer cell lines, promotes EMT and metastatic behavior by suppressing STARD13 and activating the EGFR/MEK2/ERK2/MMP7 axis [145].

In addition to tumor-derived miRNAs, exosomes from stromal and immune cells also participate in pancreatic cancer progression. M2 macrophage-derived exosomes carrying miR-365, miR-221/222, and miR-501-3p promote tumor survival through BTG2 suppression and activation of FAK/AKT and VEGF/ANG2 signaling, while PCC-derived exosomal MIF and integrin αvβ5 target Kupffer cells and hepatic stellate cells to establish a liver-permissive metastatic niche [93]. Exosomal lncRNAs and circRNAs, including HOTAIR and circRNA-100,338, have likewise been linked to angiogenic reprogramming through upregulation of VEGF, TGF-β, and FGF, together with suppression of Bax/caspase-3-associated apoptotic signaling [49, 90, 145].

Taken together, these findings support the diagnostic and prognostic promise of exosome-based biomarkers in pancreatic cancer. However, their clinical implementation remains limited by the lack of standardized methods for exosome isolation and quantification. The development of reproducible, scalable, and clinically validated workflows will be critical for translating these biomarkers into routine practice and for strengthening existing diagnostic strategies centered on CA19-9.

Glioblastoma

Glioblastoma (GBM), the most aggressive primary brain tumor, accounts for nearly half of malignant gliomas and remains associated with dismal outcomes, with median survival rarely exceeding 15 months despite maximal resection, radiotherapy, and temozolomide-based chemotherapy [104, 146]. TDEs have emerged as key mediators of GBM progression and can cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB), allowing their detection in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and serum, where they carry bioactive cargos that support angiogenesis, invasion, and treatment resistance. Profiling studies have identified abundant exosomal miRNAs, including oncogenic miR-21, miR-221, miR-451, and miR-23a, which activate PI3K/AKT, NF-κB, and STAT3 pathways to enhance tumor proliferation, survival, and immune evasion [101, 146]. Deep sequencing of circulating exosomal small RNAs has further identified a seven‑miRNA signature (miR‑182‑5p, miR‑328‑3p, miR‑339‑5p, miR‑340‑5p, miR‑485‑3p, miR‑486‑5p, miR‑543) capable of distinguishing patients with GBM from healthy controls and lower‑grade glioma cohorts with high accuracy, underscoring its diagnostic potential [147–149].

Beyond RNA signatures, GBM-derived exosomes are enriched in pro-angiogenic proteins, such as VEGF and TGF-β, integrins including αvβ3 and α6β4, and metabolic regulators such as MCT1 and CD147, which collectively remodel the tumor microenvironment, enhance vascularization, and drive metabolic reprogramming, including lactate export and increased glycolytic flux [102, 150, 151]. These vesicles also contribute to therapeutic resistance by transferring multidrug resistance proteins and anti-apoptotic signals to recipient cells. Clinically, liquid biopsy approaches targeting GBM-derived exosomes have demonstrated greater sensitivity than free circulating nucleic acids for preoperative diagnosis and disease monitoring [104].

Although GBM-derived exosomes represent promising diagnostic and therapeutic tools, their clinical translation remains constrained by technical barriers, including the lack of standardized isolation methods, limited scalability, and poor reproducibility, as well as by biological challenges such as cargo heterogeneity and potential off-target effects. Ongoing advances in engineered exosomes, including the incorporation of brain-homing ligands such as transferrin receptor-targeting peptides for the delivery of siRNAs, CRISPR-Cas systems, and chemotherapeutic agents, may help overcome the BBB and stromal barriers, thereby opening new possibilities for exosome-based interventions in GBM [57, 101].

Thyroid cancer

Thyroid cancer, the most common endocrine malignancy, is largely represented by papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC, 85–90%) and continues to show a rising global incidence [105]. In recent years, plasma exosomal miRNAs have emerged as promising noninvasive biomarkers for PTC. A small RNA sequencing discovery study involving 17 patients with PTC and 10 benign thyroid nodule controls, followed by RT-qPCR validation in independent plasma cohorts, identified miR-485-3p and miR-4433a-5p as leading diagnostic candidates. In another study focused on lymph node metastasis, plasma exosomal miR-6774-3p and miR-6879-5p distinguished PTC-N1 from PTC-N0 cases with a combined AUC of 0.914, supporting the potential utility of exosomal miRNAs for both disease detection and metastatic risk assessment [107, 152, 153]. Notably, these biomarkers are also associated with aggressive clinicopathological characteristics, including tumor size ≥ 1 cm, BRAF mutation, extrathyroidal extension, and lymph node metastasis.

In addition to miRNAs, exosomal circRNAs such as hsa_circ_0082002 and hsa_circ_0003863 are enriched in PTC and correlate strongly with vascular invasion and lymph node metastasis, highlighting their additional diagnostic value [107, 154]. Urinary exosomal thyroglobulin may also serve as a useful noninvasive biomarker for post-ablation surveillance by detecting residual or recurrent disease even in patients with undetectable serum thyroglobulin [155].

Nevertheless, the clinical application of exosome-based diagnostics in thyroid cancer remains limited by methodological variability. Although exosomes offer greater molecular stability than ctDNA and CTCs and may better reflect tumor heterogeneity, inconsistent isolation workflows, method-dependent differences in recovery, and the absence of standardized quantification criteria continue to hinder their routine clinical implementation.

Hepatocellular carcinoma

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the most common primary liver malignancy and the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, is often diagnosed at an advanced stage because currently available screening approaches, including alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) and imaging, have limited sensitivity for early-stage disease [108]. In this context, exosomal cargos, particularly ncRNAs and proteins, have emerged as promising noninvasive biomarkers for HCC diagnosis and prognosis, with the potential to complement or improve upon AFP-based detection. Multiple cohort studies have demonstrated the diagnostic value of serum exosomal miRNAs. For instance, miR-122-5p, let-7d-5p, and miR-425-5p were identified in an sRNA-sequencing study of 124 patients with HCC and 46 non-tumor donors, whereas miR-148a distinguished HCC from cirrhosis with an AUC of 0.891. Notably, a combined miR-122/miR-148a/AFP panel achieved an AUC of 0.931, and miR-10b-5p showed strong performance for early-stage HCC detection in an independent validation cohort, with an AUC of 0.934 [156–158].

In addition to miRNAs, exosomal lncRNAs and circRNAs also contribute to HCC progression and biomarker development. Molecules such as LINC00511, TUC339, HMMR-AS1, and hsa_circ_0004658 regulate oncogenic programs involving PI3K/AKT signaling, ITGA5-mediated adhesion, and M2 macrophage polarization, thereby promoting tumor progression, immune escape, and treatment resistance [109, 159]. Furthermore, an exosomal RNA-based multilayer perceptron (MLP) model integrating CDK1, FEN1, and PCNA achieved AUCs of 0.85 and 0.84 in training and test datasets, respectively, for distinguishing HCC from HBV-infected and healthy individuals, although larger independent validation studies remain necessary [160]. Overall, these findings support the translational potential of exosome-based diagnostics in HCC; however, methodological heterogeneity in exosome isolation and the absence of standardized workflows continue to impede their routine clinical implementation.

The therapeutic value of exosomes

Exosomes regulate multiple aspects of tumor progression while simultaneously offering dual therapeutic potential, both as actionable intervention targets and as engineered drug delivery platforms. Their intrinsic biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and favorable biodistribution support penetration across biological barriers and prolonged systemic circulation [8]. Moreover, exosomes derived from immune cells can function as endogenous immunomodulatory vesicles by delivering checkpoint-targeting agents, including molecules directed against the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, together with co-stimulatory signals, thereby enhancing antitumor immune reprogramming [51](Fig. 4).

Exosomes as drug delivery vehicles

Exosomes are evolutionarily conserved nanovesicles secreted by mammalian cells and are increasingly recognized as attractive therapeutic delivery platforms because of their intrinsic biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and endogenous transport capacity. Their lipid bilayer not only shields encapsulated cargos from extracellular enzymatic degradation and clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system [161], but also facilitates transport across biological barriers, including the BBB [8]. Compared with synthetic liposomes, exosomes display enhanced tumor tropism mediated by integrin-mediated organotropic targeting [44], together with more efficient cellular uptake, thereby supporting more precise delivery to malignant tissues.

Preclinical studies of anthracycline- or taxane-loaded exosomes have shown reduced systemic toxicity while maintaining antitumor efficacy [162]. Combined with advances in surface engineering and cargo-loading strategies, these properties position exosomes as versatile platforms for the selective delivery of small molecules, nucleic acid therapeutics, and functional proteins, with considerable potential for next-generation precision oncotherapy.

Exosomes as carriers for small molecule drug delivery

Engineered exosomes have emerged as promising nanoscale therapeutic vectors, owing to their low immunogenicity and efficient transport across biological barriers, which together enable more precise drug delivery. Paclitaxel-loaded exosomes, for example, have shown enhanced antitumor efficacy in prostate, lung, and pancreatic cancer models by improving pharmacokinetics and increasing tumor-targeted accumulation [163]. Preclinical studies likewise demonstrate superior therapeutic performance of exosome-based formulations, with doxorubicin-loaded exosomes reducing tumor volume by 62% in murine colon cancer compared with 38% for free doxorubicin [164]. Importantly, the capacity of exosomes to cross the BBB extends their applicability to central nervous system tumors. In glioblastoma models, curcumin-loaded exosomes have been shown to suppress LPS-induced microglial activation, alleviate neuroinflammation, and improve drug bioavailability [165, 166]. Taken together, these findings underscore the dual utility of exosomes as drug delivery vehicles and bioavailability-enhancing platforms, highlighting their promise for overcoming persistent obstacles in cancer therapy.

Exosomes as protein delivery carriers

Exosomes possess distinct advantages as targeted delivery platforms because their intrinsic tropism facilitates the selective intracellular transfer of therapeutic proteins and membrane-associated cargos. Tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL)-loaded exosomes, for example, induce tumor-selective apoptosis through death receptor activation and have shown significant antitumor efficacy in preclinical models [167]. Likewise, engineered exosomes carrying granzyme B or perforin mimic cytotoxic lymphocyte activity and have achieved more than 60% inhibition of tumor growth in murine melanoma [168]. PH20-hyaluronidase-engineered exosomes further enhance drug penetration by degrading extracellular matrix hyaluronan, increasing intratumoral doxorubicin accumulation by 2.3-fold and producing significantly greater tumor regression than free doxorubicin [169]. In parallel, SIRPα-displaying exosomes act as CD47-blocking nanovesicles that promote macrophage-mediated phagocytosis; in triple-negative breast cancer models, they increased tumor phagocytosis 3.1-fold and suppressed tumor growth by approximately 60% within 21 days, outperforming anti-CD47 antibody therapy [170]. Exosomes functionalized with integrin- or EGFR-targeting moieties have also been incorporated into ferroptosis-based treatment strategies, enabling delivery of sorafenib or ferroptosis-sensitizing proteins to hepatocellular carcinoma and glioblastoma, with reported tumor volume reductions of 58–70% in vivo [171, 172]. Taken together, these studies identify exosomes as versatile delivery systems for therapeutic proteins and membrane-associated effectors, capable of improving bioavailability, penetrating stromal barriers, and achieving tumor-specific targeting.

Exosomes as nucleic acid delivery carriers

Exosomes have emerged as versatile vectors for nucleic acid delivery, enabling the selective transport of tumor‑suppressive miRNAs, small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), and therapeutic mRNAs to reprogram oncogenic signaling in cancer cells.

Growing evidence supports the efficacy of exosome-based nucleic acid delivery in multiple tumor models. For example, miR‑379‑loaded exosomes suppressed breast cancer growth and reduced tumor volume by approximately 55% in xenograft models, compared with approximately 22% for free miRNA mimics [173]. Similarly, exosomes carrying a miR-21 inhibitor, when combined with 5‑fluorouracil (5‑FU), reversed chemoresistance in colon cancer and achieved an approximately 71% reduction in tumor volume, compared with approximately 38% for 5-FU alone [174]. Red blood cell-derived exosomes loaded with CRISPR cargo, including Cas9 mRNA and guide RNA, also achieved highly efficient knockdown of oncogenic miR-125b in leukemia models, with reported inhibition rates of 90–98% [175]. Moreover, exosomal delivery of HChrR6 mRNA selectively targeted HER2-positive breast cancer cells, where enzyme-mediated prodrug activation inhibited proliferation by approximately 65%, exceeding the efficacy of liposomal mRNA delivery [176].

Taken together, these findings identify exosomes as modular and efficient nucleic acid delivery platforms with the capacity to silence oncogenic drivers, restore tumor-suppressive pathways, and overcome therapy resistance.

Exosome-mediated immunotherapy

Furthermore, exosomes are increasingly recognized not only as carriers for anticancer drug delivery but also as active modulators of antitumor immunity [51]. Their intrinsic biocompatibility and biomimetic trafficking characteristics enable the delivery of immunoregulatory cargos with reduced systemic toxicity, highlighting their value as precision therapeutic platforms [177, 178].

A central goal of cancer immunotherapy is to stimulate robust CTL-mediated antitumor immunity through activation of tumor-specific CD8 + T cells, which are the principal effectors of cancer immune surveillance [179]. CD4 + T cells further reinforce these responses by providing metabolic and signaling support, thereby amplifying CTL function [180]. In this setting, dendritic cell-derived exosomes (DEXs), and under certain conditions tumor-derived exosomes, can serve as endogenous immunomodulatory vesicles by presenting tumor antigens via MHC class I together with co-stimulatory molecules such as HSPs and CD86, thereby promoting activation of both CD8 + and CD4 + T cells [181, 182]. Engineered DEXs loaded with α-galactosylceramide (αGC) have shown potent immunostimulatory effects in preclinical models by activating invariant NKT cells, antigen-specific CTLs, and B cells, resulting in synergistic suppression of melanoma progression [183]. In parallel, pancreatic cancer-derived exosomes have been reported to cooperate with dendritic cells in inducing tumor antigen-specific CTL responses while modulating T-cell receptor signaling through inhibition of the CD44v6/Lck/ZAP70/ERK1/2 axis, thereby influencing the balance between leukocyte proliferation and cytotoxicity [184].

Exosome-based tumor vaccines have now advanced from preclinical proof-of-concept studies to early-phase clinical evaluation, where they have shown preliminary therapeutic activity in solid tumors [185]. DEXs, in particular, represent promising nanoplatforms for vaccine development. However, their clinical translation remains in its infancy and will require further improvements in cargo-loading efficiency, manufacturing consistency, and mechanistic understanding of their immunomodulatory pathways to maximize therapeutic benefit [186].

Tumor-derived exosomes as targets for cancer therapy

Exosomes are key mediators of cancer progression and metastatic dissemination, primarily by enabling the horizontal transfer of oncogenic signals between malignant cells and the TME. This mechanistic insight has stimulated the development of multiple therapeutic strategies designed to counteract exosome-driven malignancy, including inhibition of exosome biogenesis and secretion through modulation of Rab GTPases, blockade of tumor-specific exosome uptake, selective targeting of metastasis-associated exosomal components such as integrins and tetraspanins, and extracorporeal removal of circulating oncogenic exosomes [187].

A deeper understanding of the molecular basis of exosome-mediated chemoresistance and TME remodeling will facilitate the development of next-generation targeted therapies. Such approaches may offer new opportunities for cancer treatment by simultaneously disrupting tumor-stromal communication and preventing metastatic niche formation.

Inhibition of exosome biogenesis and secretion

Emerging strategies to inhibit TDE-driven malignancy can be broadly classified into three major approaches: suppression of exosome biogenesis, blockade of exosome secretion, and inhibition of exosome-receptor interactions or uptake [188]. Pharmacological targeting of exosome production pathways has demonstrated dual antitumor effects. For instance, manumycin A inhibits prostate cancer metastasis by antagonizing the Ras-Raf-ERK1/2-HNRNPH1 axis while concurrently suppressing ESCRT-dependent exosome biogenesis [189]. In addition, Rab GTPases, including Rab27A, Rab11, and Rab35, regulate exosome release through distinct vesicular trafficking mechanisms, and Rab27A silencing markedly reduces TDE-mediated pre-metastatic niche formation in multiple carcinoma models [190–193]. Similarly, loss of TSG101, a core ESCRT-I component, impairs secretion of Wnt5b-enriched exosomes in colorectal cancer and thereby attenuates β-catenin signaling [194].

Despite these advances, current inhibitors of exosome biogenesis and secretion remain limited by poor tumor selectivity. Systemic suppression of exosome-related pathways may disrupt physiological extracellular vesicle release in healthy tissues, thereby increasing the risk of on-target toxicity. Therefore, improving tumor specificity and defining an adequate therapeutic window will be essential for the successful development of exosome-targeted therapies [195].

Inhibition of exosome uptake

Pharmacological inhibition of exosome internalization has emerged as a promising strategy for limiting tumor progression by interrupting intercellular oncogenic communication. Exosomes are internalized by recipient cells mainly through clathrin-mediated endocytosis, lipid raft-dependent pathways, and HSPG-mediated uptake [196]. Accordingly, inhibitors of these pathways can impair exosome entry and attenuate downstream tumor-promoting effects. For example, chlorpromazine, a clathrin-mediated endocytosis inhibitor, blocks both clathrin-dependent and macropinocytosis-associated exosome uptake, thereby suppressing tumor-promoting signaling in vitro [197]. Likewise, disruption of HSPG-dependent uptake, as well as treatment with methyl-β-cyclodextrin or dynasore, has been shown to reduce exosome internalization in cancer cells [198]. Collectively, these observations support the therapeutic potential of targeting exosome uptake pathways to impair tumor-stromal communication.

Removal of circulating exosomes

As central mediators of intercellular communication, TDEs are broadly distributed in biological fluids such as blood and ascites, enabling systemic dissemination of tumor-associated signals under pathological conditions [68]. Through this circulatory trafficking, exosomal cargos can access distant tissues and recipient cells, thereby contributing to tumor progression and metastatic spread. On the basis of this mechanism, an affinity-based plasmapheresis platform has been developed [199] to selectively remove circulating exosomes using in vivo hemofiltration technology. By depleting pathogenic exosomal cargos, this approach may interrupt oncogenic intercellular signaling and limit pre-metastatic niche formation, highlighting a potentially innovative therapeutic strategy in precision oncology.

TDEs carry a wide range of biologically active molecules, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, that remodel the TME and promote metastasis as well as therapeutic resistance. Among these cargos, proliferative oncoproteins and multidrug resistance mediators such as P-glycoprotein and MRP1 [200] are of particular therapeutic interest. Accordingly, strategies targeting TDE-mediated oncogenesis mainly include inhibition of exosome biogenesis to reduce oncogenic cargo release [201], blockade of cargo-receptor interactions to prevent signal propagation within the TME, and suppression of downstream signaling pathways using molecular antagonists. Collectively, these approaches provide a mechanistic framework for disrupting exosome-driven tumor progression and resistance, and may support the development of next-generation precision cancer therapies.

Clinical use of exosomes in oncology

Emerging have emerged as dual-functional tools in cancer management, both contributing to tumor progression through TME reprogramming and serving as promising platforms for precision diagnostics and therapeutics. Clinically, the ExoDx™ Prostate IntelliScore™ is a urine exosomal RNA assay that has been used in more than 50,000 patients and has been incorporated into NCCN Prostate Cancer Early Detection guidance for biopsy decision support, underscoring the translational relevance of exosome-based diagnostics [202]. In parallel, a growing commercial ecosystem has developed around exosome workflows, including the PS Capture™ ELISA Kit, MagCapture™ Extraction System, EV-Save™ Stabilization Reagent, and ExoQuick™ [203]. The GlyExo-Capture® platform, a lectin-glycan affinity approach for enriching fucosylated extracellular vesicles, has also shown promise in hepatocellular carcinoma biomarker development and is being used in clinical-study settings [204].

Exosomes are also advancing as therapeutic modalities, including in vaccine development and engineered drug delivery. Clinical studies have expanded steadily worldwide, with oncology representing the dominant application area and regenerative medicine emerging as a secondary field of interest. This review systematically examines clinical translation of exosome technologies in oncology, emphasizing their expanding role in precision cancer management (Table 2).

Despite this momentum, the scalable clinical translation of engineered exosomes remains constrained by several major bottlenecks. Native exosome production is inherently limited, and current isolation methods, including ultracentrifugation, density gradient separation, and size-exclusion chromatography, remain labor-intensive, low-throughput, and variable across batches [208, 209]. Cargo loading is often inefficient or heterogeneous; endogenous loading strategies may achieve higher encapsulation efficiency but can impose cellular stress, whereas exogenous methods such as electroporation, sonication, and freeze-thaw cycles may disrupt membrane integrity and compromise payload stability [210, 211]. In addition, rapid reticuloendothelial system clearance, inconsistent pharmacokinetics, limited organotropism, and the manufacturing complexity introduced by surface engineering all undermine therapeutic reproducibility [212]. Good manufacturing practice-compliant production and quality control remain further obstacles, as no universally accepted standards currently exist for purity, potency, bioactivity, or long-term stability across exosome batches. Overcoming these barriers will require integrated bioprocess engineering, more efficient loading and purification strategies, and robust single-particle analytical technologies to improve reproducibility, standardization, and regulatory readiness [213].

Safety considerations for exosome-based therapeutics

Although exosomes are often described as biocompatible and weakly immunogenic, these properties are not universal and should not be generalized across all exosome products. Their safety profile depends on multiple factors, including donor cell source, autologous versus allogeneic origin, purification strategy, residual contaminants, and the biological environment after administration [214]. In particular, repeated dosing may increase the likelihood of immune recognition and reduce therapeutic consistency. In addition, exosomes can acquire a protein corona in biological fluids, which may alter immune interactions, targeting specificity, and biodistribution [215].

Potential adverse effects should therefore be evaluated more rigorously. These include complement activation, cytokine-mediated inflammatory responses, and thrombogenicity, especially for intravenously administered preparations or vesicles derived from tumor-associated sources [216]. Exosomes may also exert context-dependent immunostimulatory or immunosuppressive effects through their membrane composition and cargo, indicating that immune consequences are product-specific rather than intrinsically favorable [217]. Biodistribution likewise remains a major translational challenge. Following systemic administration, exosomes often accumulate predominantly in the liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, which may limit delivery efficiency and increase off-target exposure [218]. Taken together, these considerations indicate that exosome-based therapeutics require careful, standardized evaluation of immunogenicity, coagulation risk, cytokine effects, organ tropism, and repeat-dose tolerability before broad clinical application.

Conclusion and prospect

Exosomes, tumor-derived nanovesicles enriched with proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids, have emerged as multifunctional platforms with both diagnostic and therapeutic relevance in oncology. As liquid-biopsy analytes, their stability in biofluids and tumor-specific cargo profiles enable noninvasive cancer detection and dynamic disease monitoring. As therapeutic tools, their intrinsic biocompatibility, capacity to traverse biological barriers, and ability to carry diverse bioactive cargos make them attractive natural vectors for precision drug delivery. In addition, exosome-mediated intercellular communication shapes antitumor immunity and may complement current immunotherapeutic strategies. Collectively, these properties position exosomes as next-generation theranostic platforms that bridge cancer diagnosis, monitoring, and targeted intervention.

Despite their considerable promise in diagnostics, prognostics, and therapeutic delivery, several major challenges must be overcome before exosome-based technologies can be more broadly translated into clinical practice. First, the selection of donor cells for exosome production remains constrained by low yield, high cost, and limited scalability. Achieving rapid, cost-effective, and large-scale exosome production platforms therefore remains a persistent translational bottleneck. Approaches including bioreactors, three-dimensional scaffolds, and microfluidic systems have been explored to improve yield. Notably, Haraszti et al. reported that combining three-dimensional culture with tangential flow filtration increased exosome production by 140-fold relative to two-dimensional culture, three-dimensional culture alone, or tangential flow filtration alone [219]. Second, exosome isolation continues to limit the development of liquid-biopsy applications. An ideal clinical isolation workflow should ensure stability, reproducibility, specificity, and cost-effectiveness; however, commonly used methods such as ultracentrifugation often yield impure or mixed vesicle preparations. Although emerging techniques, including asymmetric flow field-flow fractionation, size-exclusion chromatography, and affinity-based or microfluidic platforms, offer important advantages, they still face limitations in purity, throughput, or standardization [220]. Third, the intrinsic heterogeneity of exosomes and other extracellular vesicle populations must be considered. Vesicles may differ substantially in size, cargo composition, membrane phenotype, and biogenesis pathway, even when derived from the same parental cell type. Such heterogeneity can compromise diagnostic reliability because distinct vesicle subpopulations may carry different biomarker signatures, thereby affecting sensitivity, specificity, and reproducibility across studies. It also complicates therapeutic development, as variations in vesicle composition may influence loading efficiency, biodistribution, cellular uptake, biological potency, and safety [221, 222]. Fourth, beyond yield and purity, manufacturing reproducibility remains a major barrier to clinical implementation. Variability in donor cell source, passage number, culture conditions, and isolation workflow may alter exosome composition and functional activity, resulting in substantial batch-to-batch variation and making product standardization difficult [223]. Fifth, efficient cargo loading remains technically challenging. Current strategies, including electroporation, incubation, and transfection, often suffer from limited efficiency and poor reproducibility. Although electroporation can facilitate nucleic acid loading, it may also induce aggregation, cargo degradation, and alterations in exosome integrity [224]. Likewise, passive incubation is constrained by low loading efficiency and a narrow range of compatible cargos [225]. Finally, regulatory and quality-control issues must also be addressed before exosome-based therapeutics can be widely adopted. In addition to compliance with good manufacturing practice, clinically relevant products will require standardized characterization criteria, potency assays, release specifications, and comparability frameworks. Given that regulatory expectations for exosome-based products are still evolving, the establishment of harmonized production and evaluation standards will be essential to ensure safety, reproducibility, and clinical readiness [226].

Recent advances in single-extracellular vesicle technologies are beginning to transform the study of exosome heterogeneity and accelerate clinical translation. Single-vesicle multi-omics approaches now integrate transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic profiling to resolve rare functional subpopulations and detect low-abundance cargos that are often obscured in bulk analyses. Emerging tools such as single-EV RNA sequencing and CRISPR-barcoded single-EV sequencing provide high-resolution detection of microRNAs, lncRNAs, and DNA fragments, revealing cell-cycle-dependent vesicle release and transcriptional diversity among tumor-derived EVs [227]. In parallel, single-vesicle membrane protein profiling platforms, including SPARTA, seiSEQ, and multiplexed single-vesicle flow cytometry, enable multidimensional mapping of tumor-, immune-, and therapy-associated EVs subpopulations [228–230]. Label-free and physicochemical approaches further enhance single-particle characterization. Nanofluidic scattering microscopy enables measurement of EVs size and mass, AFM-IR nanospectroscopy generates lipid-protein-nucleic acid fingerprints, and TIRF microscopy combined with deep learning supports ultrasensitive microRNA detection [231]. Meanwhile, digital and absolute quantification methods, such as hydrogel droplet digital amplification and droplet digital ExoELISA, allow single-EV nucleic acid amplification and absolute counting at very low concentrations, thereby facilitating rare biomarker detection and improving liquid-biopsy sensitivity [232]. Microfluidic and nanoengineering platforms are also integrating EVs capture, enrichment, and on-chip molecular analysis into increasingly standardized high-throughput workflows, while artificial intelligence-assisted imaging and multimodal data integration are improving analytical sensitivity, automating heterogeneity mapping, and supporting batch-level quality control for therapeutic exosomes [233]. Collectively, these emerging technologies strengthen the bridge between fundamental EVs biology and clinical application by resolving heterogeneity, improving rare-subpopulation detection, enhancing liquid-biopsy precision, and supporting future GMP-compliant exosome manufacturing. Continued progress will depend on further gains in sensitivity, standardization, and computational analysis.

As a rapidly evolving class of molecular biomarkers and therapeutic carriers, exosomes are poised to play an increasingly important role in personalized and precision medicine. Looking forward, integration of exosome technologies with artificial intelligence, bioengineering, and advanced analytical platforms is expected to enable more accurate diagnostic models, more refined disease stratification, and increasingly individualized therapeutic design. Beyond oncology, exosomes also show broad promise in regenerative medicine and medical aesthetics. Stem cell-derived exosomes are being explored for wound healing, tissue regeneration, and skin repair [234], while exosome-based approaches have also shown potential in anti-aging therapy, scar reduction, and hair-growth promotion [235]. Taken together, these expanding applications suggest that exosomes may become transformative tools across both clinical medicine and biomedical innovation, ultimately offering more precise, personalized, and versatile therapeutic options.
