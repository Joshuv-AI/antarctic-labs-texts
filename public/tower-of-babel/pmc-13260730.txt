CLEAR-IT, a framework for contrastive learning to capture the immune composition of tumor microenvironments
Authors: Daniel Spengler, Serafim Korovin, Kirti Prakash, Peter Bankhead, Reno Debets, Hayri E. Balcioglu, Carlas Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate phenotyping of cells in the tumor microenvironment is essential for understanding cancer biology but typically requires precise cell segmentation, limiting scalability. Here, we introduce Contrastive Learning Enabled Accurate Registration of Immune and Tumor cells (CLEAR-IT), a self-supervised framework that learns cell-level features from multiplexed images using only cell locations. CLEAR-IT encoders achieve strong linear evaluation performance, improve substantially with hyperparameter optimization, and maintain high accuracy across imaging modalities and with up to 90% fewer labels. When substituted for handcrafted features in a state-of-the-art classifier, CLEAR-IT features yield higher performance, and their combination enables comparable accuracy with less than half of the labeled data otherwise required. The learned representations also support prognostic modeling: using annotations from a single patient, CLEAR-IT-based phenotyping identifies survival-associated tissue features that generalize across two cohorts and modalities. CLEAR-IT provides a segmentation-light, label-efficient approach for scalable cell phenotyping and enhances existing workflows in digital pathology and tumor microenvironment analysis.

Introduction

Recent advances in multiplexed proteomics have underscored the critical role of tumor microenvironment (TME) spatial composition in cancer prognosis and therapy development1–4. As manual characterization of increasing amounts of patient imaging data becomes infeasible, machine learning algorithms for histopathology, and TME characterization in particular, are being developed5–8. Classifying individual cells in multiplexed images is essential across many areas of biomedical research and clinical diagnostics. However, current workflows remain labor-intensive and difficult to scale. A bottleneck is the need for accurate cell segmentation. This often involves multiple rounds of manual annotation and refinement to reach the quality required for clinical-grade results9.

After segmentation, two main strategies are used for cell classification. One is an image-based approach, where deep learning models are trained on annotated and segmented cells to predict phenotypes. An example is CellSighter, which is a fully supervised, end-to-end approach that combines raw image data and segmentation masks to discriminate between different cell types9. The second approach, currently outperforming the former, is based on handcrafted features to measure cell expressions. MAPS (Machine learning for Analysis of Proteomics in Spatial biology) exemplifies this strategy by using extracted features such as protein expression and cell size from segmented cells to classify them10.

While effective, these workflows raise three key challenges: first, whether handcrafted features capture all relevant biological information, second, how such time-intensive pipelines can be made more efficient, and third, the susceptibility to human bias from the manual efforts during cell feature extraction as well as during the fully supervised learning11. The overarching goal is to reduce manual effort while maintaining high predictive integrity.

Self-supervised learning (SSL), on the other hand, leverages unlabeled data to learn robust feature representations that maintain high performance with fewer labels. A widely adopted method is the Simple Framework for Contrastive Learning of Visual Representations (SimCLR) algorithm by Chen et al.12, valued for its straightforward architecture and training procedure. By pre-training on unlabeled images, SimCLR can substantially reduce the need for large annotated datasets. Ciga et al.13 were the first to apply SimCLR to brightfield histopathology images (primarily hematoxylin and eosin stained) and demonstrated that modality-specific tuning of both the types and strengths of data augmentations was critical to obtain strong representations. In a similar vein, Jiménez-Sánchez et al.’s NaroNet framework was based on an exhaustive hyperparameter sweep while learning features from multiplex fluorescence images, again uncovering optimal parameters that diverged from the original SimCLR defaults14. Altogether, these examples demonstrate that SSL, using SimCLR as a prototypical framework, can unlock powerful feature learning from medical images with reduced annotation requirements, provided that critical hyperparameters (especially augmentation settings) are carefully tuned.

In this work, we explore the application of SSL to learn cell features from multiplexed imaging, which involves multiple biomarker channels per tissue section, and second, we avoid iterative segmentation by working directly with image patches centered on approximate cell locations, thus bypassing the need for precise segmentation. We evaluate our method on two distinct modalities: multiplexed immunofluorescence (MxIF) microscopy and multiplexed ion beam imaging (MIBI). Our aim is to develop a flexible, channel-agnostic workflow that minimizes the time required for development and annotation. By processing each channel independently and combining features at the classification stage, we aim to enable accurate, scalable downstream cell classification for imaging modalities with an arbitrary channel count and with minimal manual intervention. The resulting features can also be used together with features from other methods to further enhance classification performance.

To develop a flexible, channel-agnostic workflow that minimizes the time required for development and annotation, the following steps are taken (see Methods); (i) using only individual channels of a multiplex image and cell centroids as center coordinates, single-channel image patches centered around individual cells are extracted (Supplementary Fig. 1a left); (ii) an encoder is then pre-trained on those patches following the self-supervised (i.e. annotation-free) SimCLR algorithm (Supplementary Fig. 1b); (iii) after pre-training, the encoder outputs of each channel for a given patch are concatenated to obtain a multi-channel representation of an image patch (Supplementary Fig. 1a right, c); (iv) a classifier is then trained to predict cell phenotypes from the multi-channel representations given cell annotations as supervised learning signal (Supplementary Fig. 1c).

Here, steps i and ii produce the CLEAR-IT self-supervised encoder, which learns informative feature representations of cells from unlabeled image patches. Steps iii and iv correspond to a process commonly called linear evaluation: we keep the encoder’s parameters fixed (a procedure known as “freezing”) and train only a simple SLP (single-layer perceptron, also referred to as a linear classifier) on those fixed features to assign cell phenotype probabilities. This approach lets us directly measure how well the encoder’s representations capture biologically relevant information without the results being influenced by the capacity of a more complex classifier12. As a metric for the linear evaluation, we compute the precision-recall area under the curve (PR-AUC) of the SLP’s predictions (Methods).

In this work, we made use of data from two triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cohorts imaged with two different modalities: MxIF microscopy and MIBI. The multiplex immunofluorescence images (TNBC1-MxIF8) are 1010 8-channel images from 62 patients1 and multiplexed ion beam time-of-flight images (MIBI-TOF) are 41 44-channel images from 41 patients (TNBC2-MIBI44)4. For direct comparison with the multiplexed immunofluorescence (TNBC1-MxIF8), we also reduced the MIBI images to 8-channel images (TNBC2-MIBI8) comparable to those of TNBC1-MxIF8 (Methods). For all classification experiments, we split the data by patients into approximately 75% training and 25% test sets (47/15 patients for TNBC1-MxIF8, 30/11 patients for TNBC2-MIBI8 and TNBC2-MIBI44) and further split the training sets into 80% training and 20% validation sets during training (Methods).

For the supervised training and testing of classifiers, we used multi-label (ML) annotations with 6 different class labels that were generated with TME-Analyzer15 (TME-A_ML6) for both the TNBC1-MxIF8 and TNBC2-MIBI8 datasets (Methods). Second, we made use of previously published multi-class (MC) annotations: multiplexed immunofluorescence with inForm annotations (inForm_MC7 and inForm_ML6)1 and multiplexed ion beam imaging with DeepCell annotations (DeepCell_MC17 and DeepCell_ML6)4 (Methods, note the distinction between ML and MC annotations).

To evaluate CLEAR-IT across both modalities, we first carried out separate hyperparameter optimizations, including loss-function parameters and augmentation strategies, for the TNBC1-MxIF8 and TNBC2-MIBI8 datasets, in line with previous findings that SimCLR hyperparameters should be tailored to specific imaging modalities13,14. This optimization involved systematically applying combinations of pre-training loss-function parameters, augmentation schemes, and training data composition (Supplementary Fig. 1d), and assessing their impact via linear evaluation by performing cell phenotyping using a linear classifier (Supplementary Fig. 1c) trained and tested on the TME-Analyzer annotations (TME-A_ML6) available for both datasets. Final PR-AUC after optimization was 0.86 for TNBC1-MxIF8 and 0.95 for TNBC2-MIBI8. This corresponds to a 55.6% and 13.7% improvement, respectively, over the base SimCLR hyperparameter configuration. For TNBC1-MxIF8, the largest performance gain (43%) was achieved by optimizing the pre-training loss function, followed by a ~ 7% gain from improved augmentations and a small additional improvement (~ 2%) from increasing the amount of pre-training data. For TNBC2-MIBI8, optimizing the pre-training loss function alone accounted for nearly the entire 13% improvement, while augmentation and increased data only had a small effect (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Figs. 2–11).

To investigate the consistency of the features learned by the CLEAR-IT encoders, we conducted a cross-validation study across different sources of annotations beyond the ones generated by TME-Analyzer. For each of the two datasets, we performed linear evaluation using two independent sets of cell annotations: 1) multiplexed immunofluorescence with TME-Analyzer annotations (TNBC1-MxIF8 + TME-A_ML6), 2) multiplexed immunofluorescence with inForm annotations (TNBC-MxIF8 + inForm_ML6), 3) multiplexed ion beam imaging with TME annotations (TNBC2-MIBI8 + TME-A_ML6) and multiplexed ion beam imaging with DeepCell annotations (TNBC2-MIBI8 + DeepCell_ML6). Performance across datasets was highest when the unseen test annotations originated from the same annotation source (inForm, DeepCell or TME-Analyzer) as the training annotations, highlighting the impact of annotation consistency on downstream phenotyping accuracy (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b, main diagonals, see also Supplementary Fig. 13).

To investigate how many and which images should be annotated, we conducted an experiment where we progressively reduced the number of training images and evaluated the resulting classifier performance. Remarkably, we found that using only 10% of the training set, equivalent to just 0.1% of all available data, was sufficient to maintain high performance across most datasets. An exception was observed for TNBC2-MIBI8 + DeepCell_ML6, where performance dropped significantly when using less than 20% of the training data (1% of all available data) (Supplementary Fig. 14).

In practical settings, annotations are typically made at the patient level rather than on individual images. To simulate this scenario, we evaluated the impact of reducing the number of annotated patients included in the supervised training set. We found that including only 10% of the total number of patients (TNBC1-MxIF8: 6 patients; TNBC2-MIBI8: 4 patients) resulted in <10% drop in performance across all cases (Supplementary Fig. 15).

We observed that data from some annotated patients yielded better classification performance than from others, prompting us to investigate whether this variation was due to differences in data quality rather than label quality. To guide annotation efforts, we analyzed which patients’ data contributed most effectively to model performance and found considerable variation between individuals (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Data 2). To assess whether these differences stemmed from data quality, we ranked the data based on signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) (Fig. 3a, Methods), which allowed us to evaluate the correlation between each patient’s signal quality and the resulting classification performance (Fig. 3b, c). With this ranking, we observed Spearman correlation of r = 0.51 for TNBC1-MxIF8 and r = 0.92 for TNBC2-MIBI8, p < 10−3 for both. A ranking based on more traditional metrics (mean of image with 99.5th percentile clipped, mean to median, Shannon entropy, see Supplementary Results, Supplementary Fig. 16) resulted in low correlation for TNBC1-MxIF8 (r ≤ 0.13) and moderate to high correlation for TNBC2-MIBI8 (r = − 0.75 for Shannon entropy). For the TNBC1-MxIF8 dataset, we then investigated the performance of training on an equivalent amount of images but irrespective of which patients those images belong to (since there is only one image per patient for TNBC2-MIBI8, this case is identical to the one already investigated). This corresponded to 761 images (equivalent to 47 patients) for the full training set and 21 images (equivalent to 1 patient) for the most reduced training set. Training on the 21 highest-ranked images resulted in a mere 10.1% reduction in performance over training on the full dataset (Fig. 1e dark blue circles).

We found upon visual inspection that cells with a strong signal in the center were classified correctly with higher confidence (Fig. 4, Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18; Supplementary Methods). Motivated by the relationship between this patch-level confidence and image-level SNR, we further investigated how individual pixels across all channels influenced classifier decisions using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP)16. Here, our analysis was performed by considering the encoder’s input. SHAP computes attribution scores for individual pixels, allowing us to visualize, which regions of each patch most strongly influence the encoder’s feature outputs and subsequently the classifier’s decision (Fig. 5, see also Supplementary Figs. 19–34, Supplementary Results, Methods). For a compact summary across classes, we aggregated absolute SHAP values over space and samples and row-normalized per class to obtain channel/class importance matrices for the 100 most confident true positive (TP) predictions in each dataset (see Methods; Fig. 5a, b for TNBC1-MxIF8 and TNBC2-MIBI8, respectively; corresponding importance heatmaps in Fig. 1g). The matrices show a clear diagonal pattern (class-matching marker channel) together with a consistently elevated nuclear channel importance (DAPI in MxIF, dsDNA in MIBI). For TNBC1-MxIF8 (Fig. 5a, see also Supplementary Figs. 19–26), the mean diagonal (class-matching channel) importance share was 0.19 (≈ 1.52 × a uniform 1/8 baseline), the nuclear share 0.32 (≈ 2.56×), and the combined diagonal + nucleus ("Top-2”) captured 0.51 of the attribution mass. For TNBC2-MIBI8 (Fig. 5a, see also Supplementary Figs. 27–34), the mean diagonal importance share was 0.12 (≈ 0.96×), the nuclear share 0.45 (≈ 3.6×), and Top-2 reached 0.57. Finally, we benchmarked the features learned by CLEAR-IT by comparing it as an input to a cell classifier against cell expressions and cell sizes, i.e., the set of “handcrafted features” derived from cell segmentations that serve as input to the state-of-the-art network MAPS (Machine learning for Analysis of Proteomics in Spatial biology)10.

MAPS consists of a four-layer multi-layer perceptron (MLP), which predicts cell phenotypes. For our benchmark, we copied this architecture and trained MLPs with three different feature sets as input in the cell classification task while varying the amount of training data: (i) learned features from the pre-trained CLEAR-IT encoder, (ii) cell expressions and sizes (standard MAPS input), and (iii) a concatenation of both learned and handcrafted features. We performed the benchmark on four datasets: TNBC1-MxIF8 with inForm_MC7 annotations8, TNBC2-MIBI44 with DeepCell_MC17 annotations4 (Fig. 6), CRC-CODEX26 (colorectal cancer imaged via CODEX) with CellSighter_MC14 annotations9,17, and TONSIL-IMC41 (tonsil tissue imaged via imaging mass cytometry, IMC) with OPTIMAL_MC21 annotations18 (Supplementary Results, Supplementary Fig. 35, see also Supplementary Figs. 36–43 for detailed confusion matrices). To evaluate the clinical relevance of our approach, we investigated whether the cell phenotyping performed based on features from CLEAR-IT encoders in a minimal label scenario was sufficient to identify clinically relevant parameters. Using predictions made by linear classifiers trained on top of CLEAR-IT encoders with minimal labels for TNBC1-MxIF8 (Fig. 1f, 21 images) and TNBC2-MIBI8 (Fig. 1g, 1 patient) as input for our recently published methodology that extracts prognostic parameters15, we were able to obtain survival classifiers that split the cohorts into two differential overall survival groups (Fig. 7a, b, top). We then cross-validated these classifiers (Fig. 7a, b, bottom) by predicting survival rates for patients from the respective other dataset (TNBC2-MIBI8 or TNBC1-MxIF8), which were considered to be the validation cohort. The predictions made by the TNBC1-MxIF8 minimal label linear classifiers mentioned above (Fig. 1f) were used to compute tumor and stroma area, as well as cellular density and distance TME parameters per image, which were then averaged across all images from each patient to get parameters per patient. This constituted the discovery cohort. Subsequently, using the TNBC1-MxIF8 survival data, we trained a survival classifier following our recently published methodology15 to distinguish patients based on overall survival (Fig. 7a, top). For validation, we first obtained patient TME parameters in the TNBC2 dataset by using the cell classifier that was trained on the TNBC1-MxIF8 dataset (linear cell classifier trained using CLEAR-IT encoder pre-trained on TNBC1-MxIF8 dataset with annotations from 21 images) to predict cell classes for the TNBC2-MIBI8 features generated by a CLEAR-IT encoder pre-trained on TNBC2-MIBI8. Application of the survival classifier obtained for the TNBC1 cohort to these TNBC2 TME parameters successfully assigned patients to differential survival groups in the TNBC2 dataset (Fig. 7a, bottom), validating the prognostication pipeline. We then tested the alternative scenario, where self-supervised pre-training was performed on unlabeled data, and all supervised training was performed with TNBC2-MIBI8 labels, blind to the TNBC1-MxIF8 labels, and the supervised training of the cell classifier used only labels for cells from a single TNBC2-MIBI8 image (Fig. 1g, 1 patient). This approach again achieved successful assignment of patients into differential survival groups for both cohorts (Fig. 7b), demonstrating that cell phenotype predictions made based on CLEAR-IT features by even very simple classifiers (i.e., a linear classifier), and even at highly reduced label presence, can be used to prognosticate TNBC patients. Furthermore, when investigating the parameters that are involved in survival classifiers, we observed that the parameter ranking generated from predictions made by CLEAR-IT-based classifiers were in good agreement with the published ranking based on predictions made by TME-Analyzer15 (Supplementary Fig. 44a, b), demonstrating that CLEAR-IT encoders are able to extract features that correspond to clinically relevant TME biology, with minimal user input. Discussion

In summary, CLEAR-IT demonstrates that contrastive learning-based encoders can be utilized for learning features for the task of cell classification in multiplex images with very sparse labels and cell locations, irrespective of imaging and analysis platforms. The label efficiency and self-supervised pre-training on the data of interest are of particular importance given the recent demonstration of demographic bias in existing computational pathology models and how SSL can address it11,19.

CLEAR-IT was based on a relatively simple architecture that enabled the exploration of the hyperparameter space to improve its performance, and cross-dataset performance demonstrating transferability across similar datasets. Despite this, classification performance based on features learned by CLEAR-IT was comparable to classification performance based on cell expressions and areas, the state-of-the-art, and the combination of these two sets of features provided further performance gain. The F1-scores for the CLEAR-IT feature concatenated with cell expressions follow the F1-scores of the CLEAR-IT features only, rather than the cell expressions, when there is a drop in performance. We suspect that this is because, per image channel, CLEAR-IT produces 32 features compared to the 1 feature for cell expressions. This can be possibly addressed by introducing weights to the CLEAR-IT features and cell expression for the classifier to treat these inputs equally. Furthermore, when comparing classification performance on CLEAR-IT features and cell expressions, we observed a dependency on cell types, whether one or the other or their concatenation, performs best. While this could be partially attributed to CLEAR-IT features being higher in number, as mentioned earlier, it is also worthwhile to further investigate which type of feature input is beneficial for the cell types of interest and tailor the inputs to a classifier accordingly in order to maximize performance.

To systematically quantify the contribution of each learned feature for specific cell types, one could leverage the PLM-eXplain framework20 to decompose the high-dimensional CLEAR-IT feature embeddings into a low-dimensional interpretable subspace aligned with known measurements (i.e., cell expression levels and cell sizes) and a residual subspace capturing complementary information. This would both perform implicit dimensionality reduction and enable targeted analysis of the most relevant features for each cell phenotype. We note, however, that some of the observed performance degradation may also stem from inaccuracies in the reference annotations used during supervised training. Future studies incorporating expert-curated labels will be important to disentangle model limitations from annotation errors when trying to investigate the features learned via SSL.

So far, we have limited our investigation to the ResNet-1821 architecture for the choice of CLEAR-IT encoder in order to follow the SimCLR algorithm more closely, with the choice of the 18-layer variant due to a limit in computational resources. Further exploration of larger and possibly different network architectures, combined with deeper exploration of pre-training hyperparameters (e.g., a more comprehensive grid-search and additional augmentations), could result in higher-quality features being learned. During every supervised classifier training, we kept the pre-trained encoder’s parameters fixed (a practice known as freezing), so that the encoder remains unchanged. Allowing the encoder to update its parameters during classifier training (a process known as fine-tuning) can further improve performance, albeit at the cost of computation time13. Lastly, investigation of different pre-training algorithms beyond SimCLR could also lead to improved feature learning. In future work, it could also be worthwhile to investigate the application of unsupervised techniques to the learned CLEAR-IT features, for example, clustering (see Supplementary Results, Supplementary Figs. 45–47 for an example of how k-means clustering and Leiden clustering could be used to analyze the extracted features). Furthermore, to eliminate the need for cell segmentations or cell locations, it could be worthwhile to investigate the application of the encoder to a full image by separating it into a grid of tiles similar to NaroNet14.

Irrespectively, CLEAR-IT encoders demonstrate high performance in multiplexed image analysis using sparse labels and without the need for accurate segmentation masks, thereby eliminating a labor-intensive step and reducing hands-on processing time. Moreover, the features learned by CLEAR-IT are comparable to traditional handcrafted features, such as cell expressions and sizes, in predictive performance and yield even higher performance in combination, underscoring their complementary nature. This flexibility enables CLEAR-IT to be integrated into existing analysis pipelines, such as those implemented in QuPath22, which supports user-in-the-loop training (Fig. 8), where they can be used either standalone or in combination with other feature sets. Importantly, our downstream survival analysis, conducted using a linear classifier trained with minimal supervision (from a single patient) on top of CLEAR-IT features, demonstrated that these features are clinically relevant, as the performance of the survival classifier aligns well with published results. There is also potential for further improvement by employing larger or more complex encoder architectures, fine-tuning the encoder weights instead of using them in a frozen state, and applying dimensionality reduction techniques to focus on the most informative features for specific cell types, as discussed above.Fig. 8CLEAR-IT usage guideline.a Flowchart showing the process from beginning with an unlabeled dataset to obtaining a CLEAR-IT classifier (encoder + classifier pair) that can perform cell phenotyping. In conclusion, by applying SSL to multiplex images for the purpose of single-cell phenotyping, CLEAR-IT offers a flexible solution for feature generation without the need for accurate cell segmentation, thereby enabling rapid analysis and supporting research-oriented digital pathology workflows. Our method provides the field of digital pathology and cancer research with a robust algorithm for this purpose.

Methods

Datasets

All datasets used are published and consist of multiplex images of tissue with individual cells to be identified. These are, briefly, TNBC1-MxIF81,23, TNBC2-MIBI444, CRC-CODEX269,17, and TONSIL-IMC4118.

For the pre-training of a CLEAR-IT encoder, only the images are necessary, as well as a table containing the following information: cell coordinate, image file name, image channel number (this is not required if the images are already separated by channel beforehand). In practice, if cell coordinates were unavailable from the annotations, we computed them from available nucleus segmentation using the scikit-image library24. In principle, coordinates could also be randomly generated, skipping the need to localize cells for pre-training. Although we have performed experiments where we pre-trained encoders on random locations, we do not discuss their results in the manuscript. Since all available datasets have nucleus segmentation masks provided, we also do not discuss the multitude of methods for obtaining them25,26 and merely point out the algorithms used by the authors of the respective datasets in the following subsections.

For the supervised training of a classifier on top of a CLEAR-IT encoder, the images are necessary, as well as a table containing the following information: cell coordinate, image file name, and cell class label. Here, the cell coordinates should correspond to actual cell locations, since the encoder uses this information to extract an image patch and compute the cell features. The computed features serve as input to the classifier, and the cell class label serves as a supervised learning signal. Cell class labels can be obtained using any tool or method, as long as they are provided in a form suitable for multi-label or multi-class classification.

TNBC1-MxIF8 dataset

The TNBC1-MxIF8 dataset1,23 consists of 1010 MxIF microscopy images of cancerous tissue from 62 TNBC patients. The images have a size of 1340 × 1008 pixels with pixel size of 0.5 μm, and have 8 channels: “DAPI”, “CK”, “CD3”, “CD68”, “CD8”, “CD56”, “CD20”, “background”. Overall, the images include approximately 2.3 million cells whose coordinates and cell phenotypes are specified in two separate sets of annotations, one being multi-label and the other being multi-class. The multi-label annotation set was obtained using TME-Analyzer15 with segmentations based on StarDist27 (referred to as “TME-A_ML6 labels”) and contains the following 6 classes: “CK+”, “CD3+”, “CD8+”, “CD20+”, “CD56+”, “CD68+”. Supplementary Table 1 (Supplementary Data 2) contains information about cell counts in the training set of TME-A_ML6. The multi-class annotation set was obtained by Hammerl et al.1 using inForm with segmentations also obtained using inForm (referred to as “inForm_MC7 labels”) and contains the following 7 classes: “other”, “CK”, “CD3”, “CD3 CD8”, “CD20”, “CD56”, “CD68”. Furthermore, we converted inForm_MC7 to the multi-label label set inForm_ML6: Here, the “CD3 CD8” class in inForm_MC7 was converted to both “CD3+” and “CD8+”, being positive in the multi-label equivalent.

Image intensity scaling

We leave all images in the same format as they are published (either 8-bit or 16-bit integer). Before feeding images to a neural network, we divide them by a constant to scale the intensity range to [0, 1]: 255 for TNBC1-MxIF8, 265 for TNBC2-MIBI44 and TNBC2-MIBI8, and 65535 for CRC-CODEX26 and TONSIL-IMC41.

Train/test splits

For every experiment, an identical split of data into a train and test set was used. For the considered datasets, the train/test splits are: TNBC1-MxIF8 47/15 patients (761/249 images), TNBC2-MIBI44 and TNBC2-MIBI8 30/11 patients (30/11 images), CRC-CODEX26 28/7 patients (28/7 images), TONSIL-IMC41 5/2 patients (17/7 images). For the supervised training of classifiers, the training set was further split by 80/20 into a training and validation set. To ensure that classifiers are trained and tested on a comparable amount of data for the cross-testing of TNBC1-MxIF8 and TNBC2-MIBI8, the train/test split was set to 30/11 patients for both datasets. Furthermore, for TNBC1-MxIF8, only 3 images per patient (captured at the tumor center) were included. This balanced the amount of training and testing data between the TNBC1-MxIF8 images (1340 × 1008 pixels) and the TNBC2-MIBI8 images (2048 × 2048 pixels) as much as possible to approximately 4 megapixels of image data per patient.

CLEAR-IT training pipeline

The CLEAR-IT training pipeline is summarized in Supplementary Fig. 1. The inference pipeline (Supplementary Fig. 1a) takes a multiplex image patch centered around a cell as input and outputs a vector containing probabilities of the cell belonging to the class labels considered. To achieve this, the multiplex image patch is first split into its individual channel components, which results in one grayscale single-channel image patch for each image channel. The grayscale image patches are then fed to the pre-trained encoder network (specifically, a ResNet encoder21), which outputs a feature vector. The individual channel feature vectors are concatenated and fed into a classification network, which predicts the cell’s class label.

Unsupervised encoder pre-training

Pre-training of the encoder network follows the SimCLR algorithm12 and is summarized in Supplementary Fig. 1b. The pre-training dataset consists of grayscale single-channel image patches centered around cells from individual color channels of the multiplex images. A minibatch is constructed by sampling a number of different image patches of random cell locations and random image channels. Every image patch in the minibatch is augmented in two random and distinct ways to create an augmented image pair, which is then encoded by a ResNet-1821 encoder. The encodings are non-linearly projected by the projection head, which is an MLP with three hidden layers. The loss function acts on the non-linear projections of the encodings to maximize the similarity between the outputs originating from an augmented image pair (positive pair), while minimizing the similarity across projections from augmented images that are not from the same image pair (negative pairs). After pre-training, the MLP is removed, and the ResNet encoder can be used to provide feature embeddings as input for training downstream networks.

The input size to the ResNet-18 encoder is 64 × 64 × 1 pixels (32 × 32 × 1 for the TONSIL-IMC41 dataset), and the output size is either 512 or 32, for a linear classifier or an MLP classifier, respectively, which is used to perform classification. The MLP projection head has an output layer of size 128 and 3 hidden layers, each of size 512, with batch normalization and ReLU (rectified linear unit) activations. Supervised classifier training

The supervised classifier training is the second step in the training pipeline and is summarized in Supplementary Fig. 1c. The pre-trained encoder is used as many times in parallel as there are color channels in the input image and the computed feature encodings are concatenated and input to a classification network. For every experiment, we keep the weights of the pre-trained ResNet encoder frozen and only train the classifier to make predictions based on the generated feature embeddings.

In order to evaluate the quality of the features learned by the pre-trained encoder, we use a single-layer perceptron (SLP), in other words, a linear classifier, to make predictions about cell classes. This is referred to as linear evaluation12. The linear classifier consists of a single layer with input size 512 × C and output size K, where C is the number of color channels in the input image, and K is the number of classes to predict. We train every linear classifier with a batch size of 64, a 30% dropout layer, using the Adam optimizer with both learning rate and weight decay of 10−4 and minimizing the binary cross-entropy loss. For every training run, we hold out a random 20% of the training set for validation, train for 50 epochs and choose the weights from the epoch where the classifier achieved the lowest loss on the validation set.

Since the class distribution in the used datasets is highly imbalanced, we undersampled the majority classes to ensure balanced training sets (see Supplementary Methods for an explanation of the algorithm).

3-round encoder optimization

The encoder hyperparameter search for optimizing downstream classification performance is performed in 3 separate rounds. Every change in hyperparameters results in a separate ResNet encoder-linear classifier pair being trained as described previously. The optimization goal is the maximization of the classifier’s performance, which we measure with the area under the PR-AUC and report the mean of medians for PR-AUC of each class. See Supplementary Results for further details.

Label-reduction by patient count

For the experiments that investigate the effects of reducing the number of patients in the supervised training set, we use the same encoders as for the cross-testing experiments, again depending on the supervised test set. For every case with a reduced number of patients, 10 separate classifiers are trained on image data from different combinations of randomly and independently sampled patients.

Supervised classifier training on individual patient data

For training the supervised classifiers on image data from individual patients, we use the same encoder pre-trained on the TNBC1-MxIF8 dataset (Fig. 2a) and TNBC2-MIBI8 dataset (Fig. 2b), respectively. Since, for some patients, there are none or only very few cells of a certain phenotype, balancing the training sets based on the least frequent cells is infeasible. Instead, we set a fixed target count T = 500 cells per label, for the amount that every class should be represented in the training sets. If the available amount is less than T, all available cells are included in the training set.

We consider the brightest pixels in a signal channel as representing the true signal of interest, while the overall pixel distribution in the background channel is indicative of noise. In practice, a high standard deviation among the top 0.1% brightest pixels in signal channels suggests a strong, well-defined signal that stands out against the noise, whereas a low standard deviation in the background channel indicates lower noise levels30. Relating CLEAR-IT output to clinical data

The ranking of parameters and survival classifiers was obtained as described previously15. In brief, 50 center parameters per patient were used, namely: densities of immune cell populations in tumor and stroma compartments (n = 12); areas of these compartments (n = 2); and distance z-scores between phenotypes in whole tissue (n = 36), and were ranked with a reiterative nested Monte Carlo approach. Here, the patient dataset was randomly split into train and test sets, and interim classifiers were constructed in the train set. First, the representative values of parameters for long and short survival were obtained through repeating the following steps 1000 times:

After 1000 repetitions, the means of statistically significant parameters were calculated for shorter and longer survival groups, and the parameters were ranked based on the degree of separation (p value) between the survival groups obtained in the train set. Second, the patients in the train set were split into two groups, based on most parameters for each patient being closer to the recorded means of shorter or longer survivors, where the lowest ranking parameter was reiteratively excluded until statistically significant performance was reached. Third, the surviving interim classifier (i.e., the resulting set of parameters and their recorded means) was tested in the test set. In the case when statistical significance was also observed for the test set, then the set of parameters that defined the classifier was considered to have a first hit. These three steps were repeated 5000 times, and the parameters were ranked based on the total number of hits in the interim classifiers. Subsequently, the top 10 ranking parameters were used to obtain the final survival classifier using the steps 1–4 above on the whole discovery cohort, but with a repetition of 5000 instead of 1000, and then applied to the discovery and validation cohort.

CLEAR-IT feature benchmark against MAPS algorithm

In order to benchmark the quality of feature encodings produced by a pre-trained CLEAR-IT encoder, we utilize the MAPS10 architecture for classification. Briefly, MAPS consists of an MLP with four hidden layers that performs cell classification based on cell expressions and cell size, resulting in an input size of C + 1, where C is the number of channels in the image, and an output size of K, where K is the number of class labels. In order to perform classification of CLEAR-IT features, we pre-train encoders to output feature vectors of size 32 and adapt the MAPS input size to be 32 × C. Lastly, we investigate the performance of concatenating both inputs, resulting in an input size of C × 33 + 1. When training MAPS on cell expressions, we use the following training parameters: batch size 128, 25% dropout, Adam optimizer with learning rate of 10−4 and minimizing the binary cross-entropy loss. When training MAPS on CLEAR-IT features and their concatenation with cell expressions, we use the following training parameters: batch size 1024, 25% dropout, Adam optimizer with learning rate of 10−4 and minimizing the binary cross-entropy loss.

Calculation of cell size and cell expressions

In order to provide input to the MAPS architecture, we calculate the cell sizes and cell expressions from the available images. Cell sizes are determined by counting the pixels belonging to a particular cell in the segmentation masks. Cell expressions per channel are calculated by summing the pixel intensities inside segmented regions per channel and dividing this by the cell size.

SHAP analysis of selected high- and low-confidence predictions

We performed pixel-level attribution via SHAP16 on the cells whose predictions are illustrated in Fig. 4 for both TNBC1-MxIF8 and TNBC2-MIBI8. For each of the six marker classes (CK, CD3, CD8, CD20, CD56, CD68) and each prediction outcome (TP, TN, FP, FN), we pooled the 100 most and 100 least confident examples (see Supplementary Notes for calculation of prediction confidence), removed duplicate cell IDs across classes, and extracted 64 × 64 pixel patches centered on those cells from the eight-channel images.

With the encoder and classifier in evaluation mode (frozen encoder), we wrapped the model in a PyTorch DeepExplainer using an all-zero baseline of the same shape. SHAP values were computed in batches over all selected patches, producing an attribution tensor of shape (N, C, 3, H, W, K), where N is the number of patches, C = 8 channels, H = W = 64, and K = 6 classes. We then averaged these values over the batch, yielding per-channel, per-class heatmaps of size (C, H, W, K) (Supplementary Figs. 19–34). To improve visual coherence, each spatial map was Gaussian-blurred (σ = 2 pixels).

Hardware and software implementation

Code has been developed and tested on a machine with an Intel Core i9-10900X CPU, 128 GB RAM and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU (24GB VRAM) within the PyTorch Release 24.07 Docker container provided by NVIDIA Optimized Frameworks. Image augmentations are implemented using Kornia31 and certain image processing steps using scikit-image24.

Training and inference times depend on the dataset. Encoder pre-training during the hyperparameter optimizations took ~2 hours per network, and supervised training of a linear classifier took around 10 minutes. Excluding loading times, inference can be performed at a speed of ~4500 cell image patches (of size 64 × 64 × 8 pixels) per second.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed in Python using standard scientific computing packages. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes; sample sizes were dictated by the availability of publicly released multiplex imaging datasets. All experiments using human tissue images were conducted once per dataset, and reproducibility was assessed by repeated training-validation splits, bootstrapping, or cross-evaluation procedures as described below.

For linear evaluation experiments, each reported performance value represents the median PR-AUC across classes, computed on held-out test sets defined at the patient level. When assessing label-reduction or image-quality effects, classifiers were retrained independently for each condition; each “replicate” corresponds to an independently trained encoder-classifier pair using a distinct subset of patients or images. For benchmarking with 4-layer MLP classifiers, results reflect 10 bootstrapped training runs per condition.

Correlation analyses between image-quality metrics and classification performance were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Survival analyses were conducted using Kaplan–Meier estimators with log-rank tests. All clustering quality metrics (adjusted mutual information, adjusted Rand index, homogeneity) were computed on test-set embeddings only.

All code, deterministic preprocessing steps, and pre-trained CLEAR-IT encoders are publicly available to ensure reproducibility of feature extraction, model training, and evaluation32,33.
