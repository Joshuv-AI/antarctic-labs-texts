Patient Experiences With Antibody-Drug Conjugates: Bridging Efficacy and Quality of Life in Metastatic Breast Cancer Care
Authors: Martha Carlson, Amy Beumer, Julia Maués, AJ Veach, Janice Cowden, Stephanie Walker, Kelly Shanahan, Jo Lynn Collins, Julia Maués
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeople diagnosed with metastatic breast cancer (MBC) comprise a growing population, with incidence increasing from 5.8 to 7.9 per 100,000 people between 2001 and 2021. Those living with MBC are often the first to receive newly approved breast cancer therapies. Recently, antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) have become increasingly common across the MBC treatment landscape, yet insufficient practical understanding of dosing and toxicity management remains. In this Patient Perspective, a patient-led effort highlights themes of quality of life for people treated with ADCs, as well as recommendations related to dose finding in clinical trials, toxicity management, and education efforts for healthcare providers and patients.Keywords: antibody-drug conjugate, cancer, communication, medical decision making, patient experience, quality of life, shared decision making, side effect management

Introduction

Balancing quality and quantity of life is a challenge for those living with metastatic breast cancer (MBC), in part because treatments always come with side effects (SEs) that tend to decrease quality of life. MBC treatment with antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) has brought this challenge into sharp focus. ADCs are targeted therapies that link a cytotoxic payload to a monoclonal antibody, guiding the ADC to cancer cells and releasing the payload inside these cells. With their targeted mechanisms and circulating cytotoxic payloads, ADCs often cause intense, cumulative toxicities, yet most were developed with limited dose-finding work beyond identifying a maximum tolerated dose. The first of these treatments for MBC, ado-trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1), was introduced in 2013, and ADCs have since become widespread with the introduction of trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd), sacituzumab govitecan (SG), datopotamab deruxtecan (Dato-DXd), as well as the many drugs in this class currently in clinical trials (Table 1). Because MBC remains a terminal disease, with a median life expectancy of three to five years, the expansion of ADCs was welcomed due to their improvements to progression-free and overall survival.

However, in patient circles, conversations about intolerable—and sometimes deadly—SEs with ADC therapy quickly grew into a groundswell. As ADC use increased, the Patient Centered Dosing Initiative (PCDI), a patient-led organization of people living with MBC and a medical advisory board, raised concerns related to patient quality of life, decision-making, SE management, the role of dosing adjustments, and the need for research centered on the patient experience.

Patient Perspectives

The authors are part of a growing number of people living with this diagnosis, even as incidence increases without substantial improvement in how long any one of us might survive. 1 In this section we use the story told by our own experiences, coupled with voices from the larger MBC community, to discuss the impact of ADCs on patients’ lives.

Making a Choice or Taking a Chance?

An MBC treatment change typically happens when a drug stops controlling the cancer. There may be more than one option available, but decision-making with ADCs has become more complex due to the many unknowns. Although ADCs can be quite effective, engaged patients are aware that treatment can result in poor quality of life and even death.2-5

Weighing the risks and benefits of a given treatment is difficult when real-world evidence is limited. The authors observed this decision-making firsthand as one of us (AB) navigated decisions about her own MBC treatment:

After hormone therapy and a couple lines of chemotherapy failed to control my MBC, my team and I decided SG would be the best next line of treatment. So, I did what many do, I went to social media and joined a closed group for others living with MBC on SG. And as a scientist, I also went to the literature. I knew that this was likely to be similar to traditional chemotherapy with a greater chance of diarrhea and low neutrophils, but specifics would have to wait until I started the drug. My oncologist prescribed a suite of premedications, partially due to past infusion reactions and a propensity for nausea, which I’m certain helped, and the first cycle brought mostly fatigue, nausea, and somewhat low white blood cells (WBCs). One of the other downsides to SG is the dosing schedule – days one and eight of a 21-day cycle. With travel, premeds, treatment, and observation times, this was almost a full day (5-6 hours), and exhaustion reigned the rest of the day. But I completed the three-week cycle with one good week, mostly side effect-free. In the second cycle, my absolute neutrophil count (ANC; a type of WBC that indicates the body’s ability to fight infection) dropped to a point where I had to skip the day eight dose, and fatigue and nausea were worse. I was losing too much time to treatment and side effects. I knew a dose reduction was an option, and my sense from the closed group was that reductions were common. However, I couldn’t find any efficacy data at the lower dose, which frustrated me and made decision-making much harder. By mid-November, I was advocating for reducing the dose so I could feel better during the holidays – I’d decided the risk was worth living the life I had. My oncologist was receptive, for which I am grateful, and at 75% of the starting dose I was able to have more time with more energy, and my nausea was better controlled. My WBCs also stayed high enough for treatment. It was a huge relief. This continued until the summer of 2025, and while my tumors remained stable in size, side effects were starting to accumulate. We then tried 50%, which was enormously beneficial to my quality of life, and I stayed on that treatment and dose until August when my cancer progressed. These dose reductions helped maintain my quality of life and enjoy what I knew was a shrinking timeline.

Dose reductions, such as those described above, are common with ADC treatment of MBC. However, as AB notes, little is known about the relationship between dose and ADC efficacy. This uncertainty is reflected in the difficult choices made by others in the MBC community: “I convinced my oncologist not to reduce the dose—but we don’t know if I could’ve stayed stable on less” or the opposite patient-oncologist decision, “My doctor lowered dosage to 80%, then 60% immediately. When [cancer markers] started going up, we went back to 80%.”

These reflections from our MBC community are especially resonant given our direct experience that dose reductions can meaningfully reduce SEs, allowing us to remain on ADCs longer with better quality of life. Both outcomes are priorities shared by patients and clinicians. Although up to two dose reductions for treatment toxicity are allowed on-label for the ADCs approved to-date, evidence on efficacy following dose reduction remains limited in both clinical trials and real-world use.

Finding and staying on an effective therapy is the goal shared by many with MBC, making it critical to understand management of ADC toxicities as their use expands. Unfortunately, terms such as “well-tolerated” or “acceptable toxicity” are often used when ADCs are discussed, even when lived experience suggests unsustainable toxicity. The effect of unmanaged toxicity on quality of life is difficult to convey.

Gaps in Supportive Care

In the Practical Recommendations section, we outline steps to improve the MBC patient experience with ADCs, but suffering due to ADC toxicity is happening now. While dose reductions may help, limited information on efficacy can mean difficult decision-making. Supportive care measures, such as preemptive medications, outlined by researchers also help address ADC toxicities, 6 but real-world barriers persist. 7 For instance, gaps in communication and understanding burden patients. 7 “They should have suggested OT cognitive therapy and palliative care as an option for brain fog, chemobrain and fatigue. I found out about it myself” and “Lack of insurance coverage for energy-boosting medication to combat the profound fatigue” make daily activities more difficult, say others on ADCs in the MBC community.

This experience can be amplified with ADCs, where serious toxicities are common. Notably, 19% of surveyed patients with low WBC counts while on an ADC reported in the PCDI survey 8 that they faced financial barriers to receiving granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, a protein your body produces to make neutrophils, the WBCs, while on SG. This financial barrier is experienced despite neutropenia being life-threatening and reported in clinical trials. 9 Additional gaps arise from the limited availability of clinicians specializing in supportive care. These challenges are compounded by the exclusion of people with MBC from many supportive-care studies, despite lifelong treatment and cumulative toxicities.

Patients with MBC make decisions when both life expectancy and quality of life are at stake, often making them quickly and within a system that does not always center patient needs. The limited information with ADCs affects multiple decision-making domains, including best treatment selection, best treatment sequence, treatment toxicities, and access to appropriate supportive care. However, the growing role of ADCs in MBC care has also highlighted achievable ways to improve care if all stakeholders work together (Figure 1).

Practical Recommendations

Develop education for all stakeholders, guided by patient experience with ADCs. When patients want to take an active role in shared decision-making, care teams should engage in open conversations about treatment options and SE management. Clinician training in risk-benefit discussions, including potential tradeoffs between quality of life and dose intensity or treatment effectiveness, can support individualized care. Being clear about possible ADC toxicities, and how they will be managed and monitored, is an important aspect in these conversations. There is information to help with this available on the PCDI website (therightdosetherightdose.org)], including SE mitigation strategies, dosing research, and patient factors to take into account, such as patient history of SEs, body mass index, indolent versus aggressive disease, and ability to afford medications/care to ease SEs.

Address manageable toxicities and advance understanding. We hear too often about ADC treatment toxicities that are not well managed.8,10 We urge clinicians to use available knowledge to respond promptly to patient-reported SEs, reassuring patients that these do not typically require treatment discontinuation or immediate dose reduction. Mitigation strategies exist, but they must be offered consistently. Routine strategies to help with SEs can be missed, as one MBC patient points out, “I feel that additional IV fluids after infusions and [on] day 3 and day 6 would be very helpful in managing [ADC] side effects. I…had to advocate for myself.” Overlooking low-grade adverse events that lead to chronic toxicity in clinical care underestimates their real burden on patients.

For severe, life-threatening toxicities that are relatively more common with ADCs—such as interstitial lung disease—we need continued work on risk-reduction strategies, clearer monitoring standards, and better tools to identify vulnerable patients. We also encourage research into biomarkers that may predict susceptibility and studies evaluating supportive-care interventions, including dose adjustments, symptom-management medications, and nutrition strategies.

Real-world care requires real-world evidence. As people on lifelong treatment, we live with what labels describe as “acceptable toxicities” and scientists and clinicians consider “tolerable treatments”. These experiences shape our days, our relationships, and our ability to remain on therapy. Dose reductions are an approved tool for mitigating toxicity, but choosing to reduce often feels risky because there is limited information about how dose changes affect disease control, including time to progression or death. Real-world experiences should inform research: “Before the dose reduction, my side effects lasted about 8 days. The dose reduction cut that in half and the side effects on those 4 days are more manageable. It took my oncologist awhile to convince me to accept the dose reduction because I was afraid [the ADC] would stop working.”

Patients want to understand: 1) how reduced dosing affects efficacy; 2) the safest and most effective dose-modification approaches (amount, frequency, or fractionation); and 3) which toxicities reliably improve with dose changes. The PCDI urges clinicians, researchers, and industry to examine dosing practices across the ADC category and clarify the dose-efficacy relationships.

Support dose-optimization trials and patient-reported outcomes. In 2021, the FDA’s Oncology Center of Excellence launched Project Optimus to reform the dose-optimization and dose-selection paradigm in oncology drug development. The initiative acknowledged that the maximum-tolerated-dose paradigm does not reflect the realities of treatment. Since its founding in the late 2010’s, the PCDI has advocated for more thoughtful, patient-centered dosing strategies. Our first patient survey and conference presentation, 10 as well as subsequent presentations, helped elevate the need for dose-finding trials that consider both benefit and burden. We support earlier dose-optimization efforts that prioritize quality of life and the rigorous use of patient reported outcomes in clinical trials and other health research.

Conclusion

The advent of ADCs across MBC subtypes has improved survival but also introduced toxicities that profoundly affect quality of life. Identifying the right dose has been complicated by reliance on the maximum-tolerated-dose paradigm of clinical trial dose-finding and limited follow-up studies addressing dosing, efficacy, and toxicity mitigation. Beyond dosing, ADCs present opportunities to better center patient needs and experiences outside of the controlled environment of clinical trials.
