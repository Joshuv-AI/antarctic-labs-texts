Coarse graining of stochastic differential equations: averaging and projection method
Hong Duong (1), Carsten Hartmann (2), Michela Ottobre (3)

Abstract
. We study coarse-graining methods for stochastic differential equations. In particular we consider averaging and a type of projection operator method, sometimes referred to as effective dynamic via conditional expectations. The projection method (PM) we consider is related to the “mimicking marginals” coarse graining approach proposed by Gyöngy. The first contribution of this paper is to provide further theoretical background for the PM and a rigorous link to the Gyöngy method. Moreover, we compare PM and averaging. While averaging applies to systems with time scale separation, the PM can in principle be applied irrespective of this. However it is often assumed that the two methods coincide in presence of scale separation. The second contribution of this paper is to make this statement precise, provide sufficient conditions under which these two methods coincide and then show – via examples and counterexamples – that this needs not be the case. Keywords. Coarse Graining for Stochastic Differential Equations, two-parameter Markov Semigroups, Averaging, Projection Methods. AMS Classification (MSC 2020). 60H10, 34F05,60J60, 34K33, 35B40, 82C31.



1.
Introduction

Coarse-graining of nonlinear dynamical systems, also called model (order) reduction, often involves a combination of systematic mathematical approaches and heuristics based on either physical intuition or observations drawn from data. Prime examples of this fact are projection operator methods, among which are optimal prediction (e.g.
[
CHK00
]
) or stochastic mode reduction approaches (e.g. in climate modelling
[
MTVE01
]
) that eliminate small-scale variables from a potentially high-dimensional system. Other coarse-graining approaches include multiscale methods, prominently averaging and homogenization,
[
PS08
]
, thermodynamic (mean field) limits
[
Szn91
]
, Mori-Zwanzig
[
Kup04
,
Mor65
]
, with this list being by far not exhaustive.

The general purpose of all such methods is to reduce a high dimensional system to a lower dimensional one, in such a way that the latter preserves certain properties of the original dynamics. The eliminated degrees of freedom are often referred to as unresolved variables. For many of the available methods the starting point is the modelling of the entire high dimensional system; this involves first a modelling step in which the unresolved variables are parametrised by e.g. a stochastic process or a probability measure, depending on the context, and then, secondly, the elimination step per se. The first step is an issue in its own right; for example, in the context of slow-fast systems, identifying suitable dimensionless small parameters and modelling the fast variables can be a big challenge
[
IS09
]
. In this paper we do not consider this first step but rather assume that a model for the entire system is given and focus solely on the elimination, i.e. the coarse graining step.
1
1

1

In this paper we use the terms dimension reduction and coarse graining interchangeably.
In particular we will be concerned with coarse graining of systems modelled by Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs).

Coarse graining is needed and routinely used in molecular dynamics, continuum mechanics, control engineering, atmosphere and ocean dynamics, and in many other fields of applied science. Yet some dimension reduction methods have by now undergone complete mathematization, others less so. Moreover, when more than one method is applicable to the system at hand, rigorous comparisions are often still lacking but certainly needed, as advocated e.g. in
[
GKS04
]
. Since appropriate mathematization can contribute towards comparison, the two issues are related.

In this paper we consider two types of coarse graining methods and compare them. Specifically, we consider the classical averaging principle, which assumes time scale separation between variables (e.g.
[
Kha68
,
Ver91
]
), and projection operator methods for SDEs in the spirit of the works
[
LL10
,
LLO17
]
. The latter are an approximation of the dimension reduction approach introduced by Gyöngy in his seminal paper
[
Gyö86
]
. In particular, we address the question under which conditions the projection method reduces to the averaging principle. We motivate addressing this question in some depth in Subsection
2.3
and more briefly below.

There are various projection operator type approaches in the literature that are closely related to the one we will consider, such as optimal prediction
[
CHK00
]
, stochastic modelling
[
JKRH01
]
, Born-Markov
[
MHS99
]
, or Born-Oppenheimer approximation
[
PST07
]
. There are subtle differences between the aforementioned methods though and, for the sake of clarity, we will recall the method presented in
[
LL10
]
, in Section
2
. This is the one we will study here and that from now on we refer to as the Projection Method (PM).

For now we just briefly mention that any projection operator-based method can
be understood as a form of best approximation in a weighted
L^{2}
space where the weight is a probability distribution of choice. In the PM one takes a conditional probability distribution as weight, namely the distribution of the entire invariant measure of the system, conditional on knowledge of the resolved variables. The resulting projection operator is a conditional expectation.

While averaging methods for SDEs are by now well grounded on rigorous mathematical foundations, the theory underpinning the PM is less developed (though there are results in the literature, see e.g.
[
LL11
,
HNS20
]
). The first goal of this paper is to contribute to the development of the mathematical understanding of the PM and to make the relation between the PM and the Gyöngy approach rigorous, which helps to shed light on potential and limitations of the PM. We show that, for a large class of SDEs, the dynamics produced via the GM and the one produced via the PM have the same long time behaviour. We moreover provide sufficient conditions under which the PM works (i.e. conditions under which the produced coarse grained dynamics has the desired properties) and examples where it fails.

The PM can in principle be used irrespective of whether there is a time-scale separation in the system (by which we mean that the high dimensional system is a slow-fast system, with the unresolved variables being the fast part of the dynamics). However, it is a common belief (e.g.
[
CHK06
,
KJB+04
]
) that, in presence of scale separation, the coarse-grained dynamics obtained by PM and the one obtained via averaging coincide (we will be more precise on this in Section
2
). This belief seems to stem from the fact that when the original system is reversible (in gradient form) then it is (to an extent, which we clarify in the next section) true that the PM approximation boils down to the approximation produced via averaging.
The second main goal of this paper is to point out that, in general, it is
not
the case that these two coarse graining procedures coincide in presence of scale separation. We provide both counterexamples (see Section
2
and Section
4
) and sufficient
conditions under which this statement holds.

We note in passing that it is moreover often argued that the so-called
Mori–Zwanzig projection operator formalism
is an extension of the optimal prediction projection operator formalism that can account for lack of scale separation
[
CHK02
]
. Nevertheless, it is not even clear, beyond special cases (e.g.
[
Kup04
]
), whether high-fidelity non-Markovian equations can be obtained from the Mori–Zwanzig coarse-graining procedure when no scale separation is present; cf.
[
ZHS16
,
LLVE18
]
. We will not touch upon the non-Markovianity issue, and focus on the comparison between PM and averaging.

Some Background, Main Results and relation to literature

We first give a minimal description of averaging, the PM and the Gyöngy method and introduce essential setup and notation - enough to be able to present the main results of the paper. More background on these methods and precise assumptions are deferred to subsequent sections.

\displaystyle dY^{\varepsilon}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}g(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})\,dt+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\beta(X_{t}^{\varepsilon},Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,dW_{t}\,,\qquad Y_{0}=y\,,

In the second system of equations,

\rho_{t}=\rho_{t}(x,y)

\rho=\rho(x,y)

We will often need to condition with respect to the

(6)

\rho(y|x):=\frac{\rho(x,y)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-d}}\rho(x,y)\,dy}=\frac{\rho(x,y)}{\bar{\rho}(x)}.

That is, if

2.1.
The Gyöngy method and the Projection method

For the purposes of this paper, the main message of
[
Gyö86
]
can be summarised as follows.

The content of

Suppose we have a system of the form (
1

\{X_{G}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
to preserve the law of the
x

to be such that

(7)

Law(X_{G}(t))=Law(X_{t})\quad\mbox{for every }t\geq 0.

According to
[

(8)

dX_{G}(t)=f_{G}(t,X_{G}(t))dt+\alpha_{G}^{1/2}(t,X_{G}(t))dU_{t}

where

(9)

\displaystyle f_{G}(t,x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-d}}f(x,y)\rho_{t}(y|x)dy\,,

(10)

\displaystyle\alpha_{G}(t,x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-d}}\alpha\alpha^{T}(x,y)\rho_{t}(y|x)dy\,,

where
\alpha_{G}^{1/2}
denotes the positive definite square root of the positive definite matrix
\alpha_{G}
and the superscript

We postpone a proof of the fact that (
8

3.3
.

While the Gyöngy approach preserves the laws of the marginals for every
t\geq 0
, when applying the PM the (minimal) requirement is that the resulting coarse grained dynamics
X_{P}(t)
should have the same asymptotic behaviour as
X_{t}
. Assuming that
\rho_{t}
converges to
\rho
as
t\rightarrow\infty
(in a suitable sense) then, under appropriate assumptions,
\bar{\rho}_{t}
(which is by definition the law of
X_{t}
) converges to
\bar{\rho}
as
t\rightarrow\infty
. Hence we require that the law of
X_{P}(t)
should converge (weakly), as
t\rightarrow\infty
, to
\bar{\rho}
.

Coming back to the GM for a moment, let us observe that, since the law of
X_{G}(t)
and the law of
X_{t}
coincide for every
t\geq 0
, then the asymptotic behaviour of these two processes coincides as well. So, if we want to construct a process with the same asymptotic behaviour as
X_{t}
, we would in principle be done. However explicitly simulating the process
X_{G}
can be difficult, because it requires sampling the conditional law
\rho_{t}(\cdot|x)
at every time
t
. So in some sense the construction of the process
X_{G}
has not achieved any real dimensionality reduction, as simulating/solving the equation for the process
X_{G}
still potentially requires simulating the whole process (
1
) in order to access the conditional distribution
\rho_{t}(\cdot|x)
. This is not surprising, as in going from
X_{t}
to
X_{G}(t)
no approximation has been made (the GM is an exact rewriting - this is explained in Section
3

The PM can then be viewed as an ‘approximation’ of the Gyöngy strategy in the sense that, since
\rho_{t}(\cdot|x)
may be difficult or expensive to access, via the PM one replaces projections on
\rho_{t}(\cdot|x)
with projections on the ECD

X_{P}
produced via the PM is the solution of the SDE

(11)

dX_{P}(t)=f_{P}(X_{P}(t))dt+\alpha_{P}^{1/2}(X_{P}(t))dU_{t}\,,\qquad X_{P}(0)=x\,,

where

(12)

\displaystyle f_{P}(x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-d}}f(x,y)\rho(y|x)dy\,,

(13)

\displaystyle\alpha_{P}(x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-d}}\alpha\alpha^{T}(x,y)\rho(y|x)dy\,,

and
\rho(\cdot|x)
is the ECD of (
1

6

\displaystyle dy_{t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{y}V(x_{t},y_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2}\,dW_{t}\,,\qquad y_{0}=y\,,

2.2.
Averaging for SDEs.

We have presented the PM and GM for systems of the form (
1
), but both can also be applied to models of the form (
2
). We will come back to this later. Averaging, on the other hand, is more commonly applied to systems of the form (
2
), so-called
slow-fast
systems. Indeed, due to the scaling properties of Brownian motion, as
\varepsilon
tends to zero
Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}
moves faster and faster, so that the parameter
\varepsilon
defines (in an explicit way) the scale separation between the slow process (SP)
X_{t}^{\varepsilon}
and the fast process
Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}
. The behaviour as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
of slow-fast processes is studied via
(stochastic) averaging
, the basics of which can be summarised as follows. Since
Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}
evolves much faster than
X_{t}^{\varepsilon}
, intuitively, the former process will have ‘reached equilibrium’ while the latter has remained substantially unchanged. Hence, to obtain a coarse grained description of (
2
), one fixes the value of the slow process, say to
X_{t}^{\varepsilon}=x
, and considers an intermediate process, the so-called ‘Frozen Process’ or ‘auxiliary process’, namely the process
Y^{(x)}_{t}
which solves the following SDE

(19)

dY^{(x)}_{t}=g(x,Y^{(x)}_{t})\,dt+\beta(x,Y^{(x)}_{t})\,dW_{t}\,\,\quad Y_{0}^{(x)}=y\,.

We emphasize that in the above
x
is fixed but arbitrary, so
\{Y^{(x)}_{t}\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}

can be seen as a one-parameter family of processes, parametrized by
x
.
Assuming that
Y_{t}^{(x)}
is ergodic, i.e. that it admits, for each
x
fixed, a unique invariant measure,
\rho^{(x)}
, and moreover that
Y_{t}^{(x)}
converges, in a suitable sense, to
\rho^{(x)}
(irrespective of the initial state
y
) one can show that, as
\varepsilon
tends to zero, the slow process
X_{t}^{\varepsilon}
converges (at least weakly) to the so called averaged dynamics,
X_{A}(t)
, namely to the solution of the SDE whose coefficients are obtained from those of the process (
2a
) by ‘averaging them’ with respect to (or ‘projecting them’ on) the measure
\rho^{(x)}
(which, for each
x
fixed, is a measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n-d}
):

(20)

dX_{A}(t)=f_{A}(X_{A}(t))dt+\sigma_{A}^{1/2}(X_{A}(t))dU_{t}\,,

where

(21)

f_{A}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-d}}f(x,y)\rho^{(x)}(y)dy\,,\quad\sigma_{A}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-d}}(\alpha\alpha^{T})(x,y)\rho^{(x)}(y)dy\,.

Notice that the resulting dynamics
X_{A}(t)
is a dynamics of the same dimension as
X^{\varepsilon}(t)
, so averaging has achieved dimension reduction by exploiting time-scale separation. The averaged dynamics
X_{A}(t)
(
20
) typically approximates the process (
2a
) on finite time intervals and, under conditions on the coefficients, on infinite-time horizons as well
[
PS08
,
CDG+22
,
SS24
]
. That is, one can typically provide quantitative estimates of the form

(22)

\left|\mathbb{E}u(X^{\varepsilon}(t))-\mathbb{E}u(X_{A}(t))\right|\leq C\varepsilon^{\gamma}

for some
\gamma>0
, functions
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
in an appropriate class, and
C
a constant that may or may not depend on time. When
C
does not depend on time, we say that
X^{\varepsilon}
converges to
X_{A}
uniformly in time.

2.3.
Comparing averaging and the projection method

Next, we address the following question

(Q)

In presence of scale separation, do projection method and averaging coincide?

That is, is it the case that the dynamics
X_{P}
in (
11
) and the dynamics
X_{A}
in (
20
) coincide, when there is some scale separation in the system?. In this paper we phrase this question precisely and provide sufficient conditions under which the answer is affirmative. The SDE (

This result can be applied in a straightforward way to the dynamics (

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}F_{G}(t,x)=F_{P}(x),\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{G}(t,x)=\alpha_{P}(x)\,,\quad\mbox{for every }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

3.3.
On the Gyöngy method.

Here we want to show that the equality (
7

\rho_{t}
is the law of the solution
(X_{t},Y_{t})

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\rho_{t}=\mathcal{L}_{x}^{\prime}\rho_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{y}^{\prime}\rho_{t}\,,

where

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}dx\,\bar{\rho}_{t}(x)\varphi(x)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{joint and marginal time t}}}{{=}}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-d}}\varphi(x)\rho_{t}(x,y)\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-d}}\varphi(x)\mathcal{L}_{x}^{\prime}\rho_{t}(x,y)\,dx\,dy+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-d}}\varphi(x)\mathcal{L}_{y}^{\prime}\rho_{t}(x,y)\,dx\,dy,

for every test function

Let us now look at the second addend in the above. Since

is a differential operator in the

is a function of

and each

3.3
), the inner integral of the second addend in the above vanishes. Indeed, under such an assumption, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-d}}\mathcal{L}_{y}^{\prime}\rho_{t}(x,y)\,dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-d}}\sum_{i}\partial_{y_{i}}\!\left[(g_{i}(x,y)\rho_{t}(x,y))+\sum_{j}\partial_{y_{j}}((\beta\beta^{T}(x,y))_{ij}\rho_{t}(x,y))\right]dy=0

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bar{\rho}_{t}(x)\varphi(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{x}\rho

\displaystyle=-\Big(\rho\mathrm{div}_{x}(J_{1}\nabla_{x}V)+J_{1}\nabla_{x}V\cdot\nabla_{x}\rho\Big)

\displaystyle=-\rho\Big(\sum_{ij}(J_{1})_{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}-J_{1}\nabla_{x}V\cdot\nabla_{x}V\Big)=0,

where the last equality holds because tyhe matrix
J_{1}
is anti-symmetric.
Similarly we also get
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{y}(e^{-V(x,y)})=0
. Furthermore, it is clear that
\bar{\rho}(x)=\int e^{-V(x,y)}\,dy>0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Hence by Proposition
4.1
,
\rho^{(x)}(y)=\rho(y|x)
.

Example 4.4

(non-reversible system such that
\rho^{(x)}(y)\neq\rho(y|x)
)
.

Now we consider again the non-reversible system (
52
), but with
J
being the classical symplectic block matrix

J=\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix}.

In this case, the dynamics for the components
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
are given by

\displaystyle dX_{t}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{y}V(x,y)\,dt-\nabla_{x}V(x,y)\,dt+\sqrt{2}dU_{t},

\displaystyle dY_{t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{x}V(x,y)\,dt-\nabla_{y}V(x,y)\,dt+\sqrt{2}dW_{t}.

Under suitable conditions on the potential
V
, the above system also has a unique invariant measure
\rho
with the same density given in (
15
)
[
HHMS05
,
LNP13
]
. Note that one can write the dynamics for
Y
as

(54)

dY_{t}=-\nabla_{y}(V(x,y)+A(x,y))\,dt+\sqrt{2}dW(t),

if
A
is a vector field satisfying

\nabla_{x}V(x,y)=\nabla_{y}A(x,y).

For simplicity we consider the one dimensional case, in which under suitable conditions on the potential
V
, such a vector field
A
always exists; for instance, one can take

A(x,y)=\int_{0}^{y}\partial_{y}V(x,w)\,dw.

We can view (
54
) as a gradient system with the external potential
V+A
. It implies that

\rho^{(x)}(y)=\frac{e^{-(V(x,y)+A(x,y))}}{\int e^{-(V(x,y)+A(x,y))}\,dy}.

We recall that
\rho(y|x)=\frac{e^{-V(x,y)}}{\int e^{-V(x,y)}\,dy}
. Thus
\rho^{(x)}(y)=\rho(y|x)\Longleftrightarrow e^{-A}=\frac{\int e^{-V-A}dy}{\int e^{-V}dy}
. In general, this condition is not satisfied, thus
\rho^{(x)}(y)\neq\rho(y|x)
. Alternatively, one can directly check that

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{x}\rho=-\mathrm{div}_{x}(\rho\nabla_{y}V),\quad\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{y}\rho=-\mathrm{div}_{y}(\rho\nabla_{x}V).

In general, it does not hold that
\mathcal{L}_{x}^{\prime}\rho=\mathcal{L}_{y}^{\prime}\rho=0
; thus according to Proposition
4.1

\rho^{(x)}\neq\rho(y|x)

4.2.
Formal asymptotic expansion of the ECD

We now discuss a similar question as in the previous paragraph, but we consider the case of slow-fast systems (
2
).
We recall that in this case the invariant measure of the system,
\rho^{\varepsilon}(x,y)
, can depend on
\varepsilon
, and this also holds for the conditional distribution
\rho^{\varepsilon}(y|x)
,

(55)

\rho^{\varepsilon}(y|x)=\frac{\rho^{\varepsilon}(x,y)}{\bar{\rho}^{\varepsilon}(x)}\,,\quad\bar{\rho}^{\varepsilon}(x)=\int\rho^{\varepsilon}(x,y)\,dy\,,

As explained in Section
2
, here we discuss the question of when the limit (
29
) holds, i.e. the question of when averaging and projection method are consistent in the limit of scale separation.

Before moving on with the formal asymptotic expansion, we illustrate with an example what can go wrong.

Example 4.5
.

We consider the situation of Example
2.1
. The solution
(X^{\varepsilon},Y^{\varepsilon})
to (
23
)
has a unique Gaussian invariant measure
\rho^{\varepsilon}
given by (
25
).
Clearly,
\rho^{\varepsilon}>0
for every
\varepsilon>0
, but the measure becomes singular in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
; in particular
\bar{\rho}^{\varepsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\delta_{0}
ceases to be strictly positive for
\varepsilon=0
. As a consequence, the conditional distribution
\rho^{\varepsilon}(\cdot|x)
as given by (
26
) does
not converge to
\rho^{(x)}=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

This example is consistent with what we find below via asymptotic expansion: a crucial assumption for the limit (
29
) to hold is that the marginal
\bar{\rho}^{\varepsilon}
of the invariant measure
\rho^{\varepsilon}
of the system remains a strictly positive function in the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. We will also identify the limit of
\bar{\rho}^{\varepsilon}
as the stationary solution of the averaged problem, see (
62
) below.

The Fokker-Planck operator associated to (
2
) is given by

(\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon})^{\prime}=\mathcal{L}_{x}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{L}_{y}^{\prime}\,,

where we recall that
\mathcal{L}_{x}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{L}_{y}^{\prime}
have been defined in (
33
). The invariant measure of (
2
) is then the solution (which we assume to be unique) of

(56)

(\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon})^{\prime}\rho^{\varepsilon}=0\,\,.

We write
\rho^{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\bar{\rho}^{\varepsilon}(x)\rho^{\varepsilon}(y|x)
and, as customary
[
PS08
]
, make the ansatz

(57a)

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}^{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=\bar{\rho}_{0}(x)+\varepsilon\bar{\rho}_{1}(x)+\ldots

(57b)

\displaystyle\rho^{\varepsilon}(y|x)

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}(x,y)+\varepsilon\rho_{1}(x,y)+\ldots\,\,.

We also make the key assumption that the function
\bar{\rho}_{0}
is smooth and strictly positive for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\bar{\rho}_{0}>0
.

Plugging (
57
) into (
56
) and equating different powers of
\varepsilon
, we obtain a hierarchy of equations; in particular, by equating the terms of order
\varepsilon^{-1}
and the order one terms, we obtain

(58a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{L}_{y}^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{0}(x)\rho_{0}(x,y))

(58b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{x}^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{0}(x)\rho_{0}(x,y))

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{L}_{y}^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{0}(x)\rho_{1}(x,y)+\bar{\rho}_{1}(x)\rho_{0}(x,y))\,.

Since
\bar{\rho}_{0}>0
is independent of
y
and the kernel of
\mathcal{L}_{y}^{\prime}
is the one-dimensional subspace of
L^{2}(dy)
spanned by
\rho^{(x)}
, equation (
58a
) implies

\rho_{0}(x,y)=h(x)\rho^{(x)}(y)+\tilde{h}(x)

for some functions
h,\tilde{h}
which depend on the
x
variable only. Imposing further the requirement that the integral of
\rho_{0}
in the
y
variable should be equal to one, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(which is a natural requirement in view of the fact that
\rho^{\varepsilon}(y|x)
enjoys the same property, for every
\varepsilon>0
and every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
), we conclude

(59)

\rho_{0}(x,y)=\rho^{(x)}(y)\,.

The above now implies two facts. First, equation (
57b
) becomes

(60)

\rho^{\varepsilon}(y|x)=\rho^{(x)}(y)+\varepsilon\rho_{1}(x,y)+\ldots\,\,.

Secondly, equation (
58b
) simplifies to

(61)

-\mathcal{L}_{y}^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{0}(x)\rho_{1}(x,y))=\mathcal{L}_{x}^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{0}(x)\rho^{(x)}(y))\,.

We view the above as a Poisson equation for the operator
\mathcal{L}_{y}^{\prime}
in the unknown
\rho_{1}
. Integrating both sides of (
61
) in the
y
variable one can see that a necessary condition for (
61
) to admit a solution is that the right hand side averages to zero (in
y
). This yields, as a by-product, a characterisation of
\bar{\rho}_{0}
as the equilibrium distribution with respect to the averaged dynamics,
since

(62)

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{A}\,\bar{\rho}_{0}(x)=\int\mathcal{L}_{x}^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{0}(x)\rho^{(x)}(y))\,dy=0\,.

Formally, if
\rho_{1}(x,y)
is a well-behaved function, then (
60
) states that
\rho^{\varepsilon}(x,y)\to\rho^{(x)}(y)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. The properties of the function
\rho_{1}(x,y)
can be deduced from the fact that it is a solution of the Poisson equation (
61
), which are well studied in the literature, see e.g.
[
PV03
,
CDG+22
,
ELVE05
,
PS08
]
(without claim to completeness of references) or also
[
SVE18
]
, which deals with the rigorous proof of related asymptotic problems.

To turn the above formal expansion into an actual proof one needs to combine these results on Poisson equations together with a more careful study of the difference
\rho^{\varepsilon}-\rho^{(x)}(y)-\varepsilon\rho_{1}(x,y)
, which needs to be shown to converge to zero as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. While not too difficult, this is not within the scope of this paper.

Example 4.6
.

We consider the following slight modification of of Example
4.5
:

(63a)

\displaystyle dX^{\varepsilon}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\left(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}-Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}\right)dt+\sqrt{2}dU_{t}\,,\quad X^{\varepsilon}_{0}=x

(63b)

\displaystyle dY^{\varepsilon}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}\,dt+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\varepsilon}}\,dW_{t}\,,\quad Y^{\varepsilon}_{0}=y\,.

This dynamics has a Gaussian invariant measure

with strictly positive limit density

5.1.
Non-autonomous SDEs

We have so far only informally stated the results of

Assumption A1
.

The coefficients
B,\sigma
of (
40
) are smooth in both arguments and the diffusion coefficient
\sigma
is globally Lipshitz. Moreover:

[C1]

The diffusion coefficient
\sigma
is uniformly elliptic, i.e. there exists a constant

\eta>0
, independent of
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that

\eta|\xi|^{2}\leq\langle\sigma(t,x)\xi,\xi\rangle,\quad\mbox{for every }\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

[C2]

There exist constants
c_{1}\geq 0,c_{2}>0
and a function
\varphi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with compact level sets such that

\mathcal{L}_{t}\varphi(x)\leq c_{1}-c_{2}\varphi(x)\quad\mbox{for every }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\mathcal{L}_{t}
is the generator of the dynamics (
40
), namely

\mathcal{L}_{t}\psi(x)=B(t,x)\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma^{[i]}(t,x)\cdot\nabla_{x}(\sigma^{[i]}(t,x)\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi(x))\,.

[C3]

There exists a constant
\lambda_{0}>0
such that the following holds for every function
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
sufficiently smooth, for every
i=1,\dots,d
, and for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\left([\sigma^{[i]}\cdot\nabla_{x},B\cdot\nabla_{x}]h(x)-(\partial_{t}\sigma^{[i]})\cdot\nabla_{x}h(x)\right)\left(\sigma^{[i]}\cdot\nabla_{x}h(x)\right)\leq-\lambda_{0}\left|\sigma^{[i]}\cdot\nabla_{x}h(x)\right|^{2}\,,

where the brackets
[\cdot,\cdot]
in the above denote the commutator between two differential operators. More explicitly:

[\sigma^{[i]}\cdot\nabla_{x},B\cdot\nabla_{x}]h(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sigma_{ji}(t,x)\partial_{x_{j}}(B^{j}(t,x)\partial_{x_{j}}h)-B^{j}(t,x)\partial_{x_{j}}(\sigma_{ji}(t,x)\partial_{x_{j}}h)\,.

[C4]

There exist smooth functions
\bar{B},\bar{\sigma}^{[i]}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}B(t,x)=\bar{B}(x),\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\sigma^{[i]}(t,x)=\bar{\sigma}^{[i]}(x),\quad\mbox{for every }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},i=1,\dots,d.

In what follows we denote by
\mathcal{X}_{s,x}(t)
the solution of (
40
) at time
t
, when the dynamics was started at time
s\geq 0
from the initial condition
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.
\mathcal{X}_{s,x}(s)=x
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let Assumption
A1
hold. Then:

a) There exists a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for every function

which is continuous and bounded the following limit holds:

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}[h(\mathcal{X}_{s,x}(t))]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(x)\mu(dx)\,,\qquad\mbox{for every }s\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

b) The dynamics (
42
) is well posed and it
admits a unique invariant measure, which is precisely the measure

. Furthermore the process (
42

\mu
, that is,

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}[h(\bar{\mathcal{X}}_{t})]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(x)\mu(x)dx,

for every

which is continuous and bounded (and irrespective of the initial datum
\bar{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
).

The proof of Theorem
5.1

can be found in Appendix

. In the note below we just comment on the assumptions of Theorem

Note 5.2
.

Theorem
5.1
is a slight extension of the results of
[
ALL13
, Section 6]
to the case when the diffusion coefficient is globally Lipshitz rather than bounded and it is a function of both time and space rather than a function of time only. This small extension requires no new ideas and can be achieved leveraging on
[
CCDO21
, Section 6]
.

The non-autonomous evolution (
40
) gives rise to a two-parameter semigroup
\mathcal{Q}_{s,t}
, namely

(\mathcal{Q}_{s,t}h)(x):=\mathbb{E}h(\mathcal{X}_{s,x}(t)),\quad h\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,.

When working with non-autonomous SDEs the concept of invariant measure is replaced by the concept of
evolution system of measures
(ESM), see e.g.
[
DPL07
,
ALL13
]
. A family
\{\mu_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is an ESM for the two-parameter semigroup
\mathcal{Q}_{s,t}
if

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}_{st}h(x)\mu_{s}(dx)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(x)\mu_{t}(dx),

for every
0\leq s\leq t
and
h\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Assumption
[C2]
ensures the existence of an evolution system of measures
\{\mu_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
and it moreover implies that such a system is tight. Assumption
[C3]
then implies uniqueness of the ESM. Since such an ESM is tight, one can take (up to subsequences) the limit of
\mu_{t}
, which is then the natural candidate for the limiting behaviour of the semigroup
Q_{st}
. Finally, letting
t
to infinity in the inequality of Assumption
[C2]
one can see that such an assumption implies that the function
\varphi
is a Lyapunov function also for the generator of (
42
), hence the existence of an invariant measure for (
42
). Such an invariant measure is unique because our uniform ellipticity assumption
[C1]
implies the uniform ellipticity of
\bar{\sigma}
. The proof of the theorem is then concluded by showing that
\mu
coincides with the invariant measure of (
42
).

The fact that Assumption
[C3]
implies uniqueness of the ESM is perhaps the least trivial statement in this discussion, so let us comment a little more on this. First of all note that if
\sigma=\sigma(t)
and it is bounded (which is the setting of
[
ALL13
]
) then Assumption
[C3]
boils down to
[
ALL13
, Condition (2.3)]
(this is perhaps easier to see in the simpler case when
d=1
and
\sigma
is constant). Assumption
[C3]
is a specific form of a condition which was termed in
[
CO16
]
the
obtuse angle condition
(OAC). The OAC implies that the space-derivatives of the semigroup associated to the dynamics decay exponentially fast in time. If the semigroup is a one-paramter semigroup (i.e. associated to an autonomous evolution) then this decay of the derivatives has been shown to imply uniqueness of the invariant measure
[
CCDO21
,
CDO21
]
. This can be seen as a simple consequence of the fundamental theorem of calculus, see e.g.
[
CCDO21
]
. It is then reasonable that, when dealing with two-parameter semigroups, this decay of derivatives implies uniqueness of the evolution system of measures, as shown in the works
[
KLL10
,
ALL13
]
and references therein.

To demonstrate in practice what assumption
[C3]
means, consider the simple one-dimensional SDE

dX_{t}=-f(t)X_{t}dt+\sigma dW_{t}\,

where
f
is a real valued function, assumed to be good enough that the above SDE is well posed and
\sigma
is a strictly positive constant. Then, in the notation of the above theorem, we have
d=1
,
B(t,x)=-f(t)x
and
\sigma^{[1]}=\sigma
. Hence Assumption
[C3]
is satisfied if the inequality

\left([\sigma\partial_{x},-f(t)x\partial_{x}]h(x)\right)(\sigma\partial_{x}h)\leq-\lambda_{0}|\sigma\partial_{x}h(x)|^{2},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\,,

holds for some
\lambda_{0}>0
and for every sufficiently smooth function
h
. After calculating the bracket in the above, this inequality boils down to

f(t)\left|\sigma\partial_{x}h\right|^{2}\geq\lambda_{0}\left|\sigma\partial_{x}h\right|^{2}\,,

which, as expected, is satisfied if
f(t)\geq\lambda_{0}
.

Let us consider again the system in Example

The definition of the conditional distribution (

for every

and
X_{G}(t)
is a Dirac for any

and therefore the law of
X_{G}(t)
cannot coincide with the law of
X_{t}
and the invariant measure of

cannot be
\bar{\rho}
. In our example this compounds with the fact that the equation obtained via GM is not well posed for small

, in case of deterministic initial data.

We make more precise observations below, but this discussion suffices to illustrate the fact that ellipticity of the SDE (

)-(
1b
) is an important requirement in order for the Gyöngy and projection methods to produce the desired results.

Let us now give more detail. The unique invariant measure of (

) is a mean zero Gaussian,
\rho\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
, where
\Sigma
is the unique symmetric positive definite solution of the Lyapunov equation

A\Sigma+\Sigma A^{T}=-CC^{T}
, namely

(70)

\Sigma=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&1\\
1&2\end{array}\right)\,.

We suppose that
Z_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{0},\Sigma_{0})
where the special case of Dirac initial data
Z_{0}=z
is included by the choice
m_{0}=z
and
\Sigma_{0}=0
. To simplify calculations a little bit, we take
X_{0}
independent of
Y_{0}
and
Z_{0}
independent of the Brownian motion
W_{t}
.
Then the law
\rho_{t}
of
Z_{t}
is also Gaussian,
\rho_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{t},\Sigma_{t})
where
m_{t}=\mathbb{E}(Z_{t})
,
\Sigma_{t}:=\mathrm{Cov}(Z_{t})=\mathbb{E}(Z_{t}Z_{t}^{T})-\mathbb{E}Z_{t}\mathbb{E}Z_{t}^{T}
, and the expressions for the mean and the covariance are

(71)

m_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-t}m_{0}^{x}+te^{-t}m^{y}_{0}\\
e^{-t}m^{y}_{0}\end{pmatrix}

and

(72)

\Sigma_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\displaystyle 1-e^{-2t}\left(2t^{2}+2t+1-2\Sigma^{xx}_{0}-2t^{2}\Sigma^{yy}_{0}\right)&1-e^{-2t}\left(2t+1-2t\Sigma^{yy}_{0}\right)\\
1-e^{-2t}\left(2t+1-2t\Sigma^{yy}_{0}\right)&2-2e^{-2t}\left(1-\Sigma_{0}^{yy}\right)\,.\end{pmatrix}

In the above we have used the standard notation

m_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}m^{x}_{t}\\
m^{y}_{t}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\Sigma_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma^{xx}_{t}&\Sigma^{xy}_{t}\\
\Sigma^{yx}_{t}&\Sigma^{yy}_{t}\end{pmatrix},\quad t\geq 0\,

and the expression (
72
) has been obtained by direct computation, using the fact that (
69
) implies the following expression for the covariance matrix

(73)

\Sigma_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}ds\,e^{A(t-s)}CC^{T}e^{A^{T}(t-s)}+e^{At}\mathrm{Cov}(Z_{0})e^{A^{T}t}\,,

with

\exp(At)=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-t}&te^{-t}\\
0&e^{-t}\end{pmatrix}.

The
x
-marginal
\bar{\rho}_{t}
of
\rho_{t}
is given by

\bar{\rho}_{t}=\mathcal{N}(m_{t}^{x},\Sigma_{t}^{xx})=\mathcal{N}(e^{-t}m_{0}^{x}+te^{-t}m_{0}^{y},\Sigma_{t}^{xx})\,.

Similarly, the marginal
\bar{\rho}
of
\rho
is
\bar{\rho}=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{xx})=\mathcal{N}(0,1/2)
.
Hence,
\bar{\rho}_{t}\rightarrow\bar{\rho}
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

For the dynamics (
66
)-(
67
) we now want to calculate both the approximation
X_{G}
produced via the Gyöngy method and the CG version
X_{P}
produced via the projection method. To this end we need to compute the conditional distributions
\rho_{t}(\cdot|x)
and
\rho(\cdot|x)
.
The conditional distribution
\rho_{t}(\cdot|x)
is

\rho_{t}(\cdot|x)=\mathcal{N}(m_{t}^{y|x},\Sigma_{t}^{y|x}),

with (e.g. see
[
PP12
, Sec. 8]
)

(74)

\displaystyle m_{t}^{y|x}

\displaystyle=m_{t}^{y}+\Sigma_{t}^{xy}(\Sigma_{t}^{xx})^{-1}(x-m_{t}^{x})

(75)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}^{y|x}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{t}^{yy}-\Sigma_{t}^{xy}(\Sigma_{t}^{xx})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}^{xy}

giving

(76)

\displaystyle m_{t}^{y|x}

\displaystyle=e^{-t}m_{0}^{y}+\frac{1-e^{-2t}\left(2t+1\right)+2te^{-2t}\Sigma^{yy}_{0}}{1-e^{-2t}\left(2t^{2}+2t+1-2\Sigma^{xx}_{0}-2t^{2}\Sigma^{yy}_{0}\right)}(x-m_{t}^{x})

(77)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}^{y|x}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{t}^{yy}-\Sigma_{t}^{xy}(\Sigma_{t}^{xx})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}^{xy}\,.

Using analogous formulas for
\rho=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
, we observe that

\rho(\cdot|x)\sim\mathcal{N}(x,1/2)
, which implies that
\rho_{t}(\cdot|x)\rightarrow\rho(\cdot|x)
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

Finally, since in our case
f(x,y)=-x+y
and
\alpha(x,y)=0
, using the expressions (

)-(

) and (

)-(

\alpha_{P}=\alpha_{G}=0
and

As a consequence, the projected equation reads

(78)

dX_{P}=0\,,\quad X_{P}(0)=X_{0}\,,

with the trivial solution
X_{P}(t)\equiv X_{0}
for all

.
Clearly, the result of Proposition

still holds for this example (even if the generator

is not elliptic). In particular, the calculation in the block of equations after (

3.1
, which only states that if
\rho
is invariant for
\mathcal{L}
then its marginal
\bar{\rho}
is invariant for
\mathcal{L}_{P}

\mathcal{L}_{P}=0

\mathcal{L}_{P}
has infinitely many invariant measures, while
\mathcal{L}
has only one. In other words, coarse graining by means of the projection method has in this case changed the number of stationary states.

This should be contrasted with the Gyöngy approximation that reads

(79)

\frac{dX_{G}}{dt}=(\phi(t)-1)X_{G}+e^{-t}m_{0}^{y}-\phi(t)\left(e^{-t}m_{0}^{x}+te^{-t}m_{0}^{y}\right)\,,

where

(80)

\phi(t)=\frac{1-e^{-2t}\left(2t+1\right)+2te^{-2t}\Sigma^{yy}_{0}}{1-e^{-2t}\left(2t^{2}+2t+1-2\Sigma^{xx}_{0}-2t^{2}\Sigma^{yy}_{0}\right)}\,.

If
\Sigma_{0}^{xx}>0
the function

as

.

Lemma A.1
.

If
A
is Hurwitz (i.e. all eigenvalues have strictly negative real part), and the matrix pair
(A,C)
satisfies the Kalman rank condition,

(83)

{\rm rank}\Big(C|AC|A^{2}C|\ldots|A^{n-1}C\Big)=n\,,

then the OU process
dZ_{t}=AZ_{t}dt+Cd\bf{W}_{t}
has a unique stationary Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
that is reached asymptotically as
t\to\infty
. Moreover
\Sigma
is symmetric positive definite (s.p.d.) and satisfies the linear Lyapunov equation

(84)

A\Sigma+\Sigma A^{T}=-CC^{T}\,.

Proof.

The convergence
\mathbf{E}[Z_{t}]\to 0
is a direct consequence of the Hurwitz property. Setting
\Sigma_{t}:={\rm Cov}(Z_{t},Z_{t})
and using that
\exp(At)\Sigma_{0}\exp(A^{T}t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, the matrix

\Sigma:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\exp(A(t-v))CC^{T}\exp(-A^{T}(t-v))\,dv=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(Av)CC^{T}\exp(A^{T}v)\,dv

exists. As a consequence of the Caley-Hamilton Theorem (e.g.
[
Axl97
]
), a vector
h\neq 0
lies in the kernel of
(e^{-At}C)^{T}
for any
t>0
iff
h^{T}A^{k}C=0
for all
k=0,\ldots n-1
. This implies that
\Sigma_{\infty}>0
if the Kalman condition holds. Integrating the rightmost integral by parts and using that
A
is invertible, yields the Lyapunov equation.
∎
