Beyond catheter-associated urinary tract infection: A prospective study of infectious, noninfectious, and psychosocial risks of indwelling catheters
Authors: Roshan Reddy, Rajan Ravichandran, Velmurugan Palaniyandi, Hariharasudhan Sekar, Sriram Krishnamoorthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Indwelling urethral catheters (IUCs) are indispensable in modern urology; however, prolonged dwell times increase the risk of infection and patient-reported morbidity. Evidence-based stewardship, therefore, requires risk estimates by catheter material and dwell duration.Methods:We conducted a prospective, single-center cohort study from July to December 2023. Consecutive adults who received an IUC were followed until its removal. Dwell was stratified as ≤5, 6–14, 15–30, and >30 days. A baseline urine culture was collected within 24 h of insertion. Follow-up cultures were planned for day 7 or at removal, and symptom-triggered thereafter, with additional sampling for catheters kept for more than 14 days. Culture-confirmed catheter-associated urinary tract infection (CAUTI) and noninfectious events were prospectively recorded.Results:Among 670 eligible participants, 20 refused; 592 were analyzed (median age 51.7 years, 41% of women). Latex, silicone, and Bactiguard Infection Protection (BIP)-coated silicone catheters were used in 451, 120, and 21 insertions, respectively. Culture-confirmed CAUTI occurred in 50% of Latex, 22% of silicone, and 5% of BIP-coated silicone catheterizations (P < 0.0001). The overall incidence of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention-defined symptomatic CAUTI in the study cohort was 16.7%. Versus ≤5 days, dwell >30 days markedly raised pain/discomfort (RR: 7.1, 95% confidence interval, 2.1–24), hematuria (RR: 10.6, 2.2–51) and sexual issues (RR: 21.2, 2.0–228). Regression identified dwell length (OR: 1.07 per day) and latex material (OR: 3.8) as independent CAUTI drivers. Prolonged catheterization was also associated with marked psychosocial morbidity, including social restriction and sexual dysfunction, which increased exponentially beyond 14 days.Conclusion:Complications escalate sharply after day 5 and are amplified by Latex. Replacing Latex with silicone and limiting dwell time could potentially halve the incidence of culture-confirmed CAUTI. BIP catheters appear highly protective, but demand confirmation in future, robust, multicenter trials.

INTRODUCTION

Indwelling urethral catheters (IUCs) have been indispensable ever since Foley’s rubber design was patented in 1937,[1] and contemporary audits indicate that nearly one-quarter of medical or surgical in-patients will have one inserted during their hospital stay.[2] Catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTIs) remain the most frequently reported healthcare-associated infections worldwide, accounting for approximately 40% of nosocomial UTIs.[34]

The prospective ward studies show that bacteriuria accrues at a rate of 3%–7% per catheter day, and nationwide surveillance demonstrates a clear step-up in both morbidity and cost once dwell time exceeds 5 days.[56] Multicenter patient-experience work has revealed that pain, bladder spasm, accidental traction, and limitations to mobility or intimacy occur in up to 80% of users and are often considered more troublesome than the infection itself.[78910]

Long-term trials comparing all-silicone Foley catheters with latex Foley catheters report lower encrustation, fewer blockages, and a significant reduction in CAUTI, particularly beyond 2 weeks of use.[11] Systematic reviews further suggest that antimicrobial coatings – such as silver alloy, nitrofurazone, or Bactiguard Infection Protection (BIP) technology – can halve the infection rate, albeit at an increased cost.[12] International guidelines now converge on three core preventive pillars: strict aseptic insertion, daily review with the aim of early removal, and judicious selection of the least-reactive or antimicrobial catheter when prolonged drainage is unavoidable.[21314]

Despite these recommendations, implementation remains patchy, and little prospective work has combined microbiological surveillance with granular patient-reported outcomes to quantify the real-world impact of catheter material and dwell time. The present study, therefore, aimed to (i) measure both infectious and noninfectious complications across dwell-time strata, (ii) compare Latex, silicone, and BIP catheters, and (iii) identify independent predictors of harm in a contemporary Indian tertiary-care cohort.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This 6-month prospective, single-center cohort study, conducted from July 2023 to December 2023, took place at a referral teaching institute to quantify infectious and noninfectious complications of IUCs and capture accompanying patient-reported outcomes. All consecutive adult in-patients undergoing indwelling urethral catheterization at our institution during the study period were screened for eligibility; patients fulfilling the inclusion criteria were prospectively enrolled and followed until catheter removal or completion of the 30-day follow-up. Of the 670 patients screened, 20 declined consent at baseline and were excluded prior to enrolment. A further 78 participants were lost to follow-up due to early hospital discharge without reachable contact details (n = 23), transfer to another facility (n = 14), or withdrawal of consent during follow-up (n = 41). Only patients with at least one completed follow-up assessment (n = 592) were included in the final analysis. Missing outcome data were handled using complete-case analysis, and no imputation was performed, as loss to follow-up was <15% and evenly distributed across catheter types and dwell-time strata. Each participant was observed for 30 days after catheter insertion, regardless of whether the catheter had been removed earlier. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Inclusion criteria

Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older who required transurethral catheterization for anticipated durations of 24 h or longer and who were hospitalized in an acute care setting, including medical/surgical wards, intensive care units, and progressive care units.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they were under the age of 18, had cognitive impairments such as severe dementia or delirium that precluded informed consent or participation in assessment procedures, refused to provide written informed consent, or failed to attend follow-up assessments.

Catheter materials

Three commercially available sterile catheters were used at the attending’s discretion: a natural rubber latex Foley catheter, a 100% silicone Foley catheter, and a BIP-impregnated silicone Foley catheter. Catheters were inserted under aseptic conditions using sterile lubricant.

Urine-culture schedule (microbiological surveillance)

A baseline urine culture was obtained within 24 h postinsertion to document any pre-existing bacteriuria. A routine urine culture was then performed either on day 7 or at the time of catheter removal, whichever occurred earlier. In addition, symptom-triggered urine cultures were collected at any time if the patient exhibited clinical signs suggestive of infection.

CAUTI was defined by the 2019 Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) criteria: ≥105 CFU/ml of ≤2 uropathogens plus compatible symptoms (fever ≥38°C, suprapubic tenderness, or urgency).[2]

Follow-up and data collection

Patients were monitored during their hospital stay and for 30 days postcatheter insertion, regardless of whether the catheter was removed. The follow-up included:

Initial in-person interview

Conducted within 3 days after IUC placement to gather baseline data on discomfort, pain, and immediate perceptions of the catheter.

14-day assessment

Conducted in-person or via telephone to document emerging complications, including infectious (e.g., catheter-associated urinary tract infections) and noninfectious (e.g., bladder discomfort, and catheter blockage) adverse effects.

30-day assessment

A final evaluation, also in-person or by telephone, to capture late-onset symptoms and complications throughout the catheterization period and during patient recovery.

Study measures

Primary outcomes were infectious and noninfectious catheter-related events. Infective complications were any episode of fever, dysuria, frequency, urgency, or similar symptoms that required clinical attention. Noninfectious events were classified by catheter status: after removal – hematuria, urgency, bladder spasm, incontinence, or voiding difficulty; while in situ – pain or discomfort, urgency, hematuria, bladder spasm, and meatitis. Secondary outcomes assessed the catheter’s impact on daily living, social interaction, and overall well-being through participant-reported comfort and ability to perform activities of daily living. Analyses were performed with SPSS v29.0 (IBM, New York, USA). Categorical variables were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, and continuous variables were compared using the Student t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Independent predictors of complications were identified with multivariable logistic regression, adjusting for age, sex, comorbidity burden, dwell time > 5 days, and catheter material. Significance was set at P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Of the 670 catheterized in-patients screened, 592 (88.5%) met the inclusion criteria and formed the analytic cohort [Figure 1]; 78 (11.6%) were lost to follow-up. The cohort comprised 592 adults (mean age: 51.7 ± 6.5 years, range 20–70 years), as shown in Table 1. Men predominated, accounting for 68.6% of participants (95% confidence interval [CI], 64.7–72.2). A prior history of urethral catheterization was uncommon (9.8%, 95% CI, 7.7–12.5). Most catheters were placed for perioperative management (72.8%), while urinary retention, output monitoring, functional incontinence, prolonged immobilization, and other indications accounted for the remainder. Diabetes was the most frequent comorbidity (20.6%, 95% CI, 17.5–24.1), followed by hypertension (12.0%), cardiovascular disease (6.9%), renal insufficiency (5.7%), and thyroid disorders (3.7%). Latex Foley catheters were used in three-quarters of cases, silicone devices constituted one-fifth, and silver-impregnated catheters accounted for only 3.5%. Two-thirds of patients had the catheter removed within 5 days, while 16.0% remained catheterized for 5–14 days, 9.6% for 14–30 days, and 6.4% for more than a month. Overall, 19.9% (95% CI, 16.9–23.3) had a positive urine culture during catheterization. Of these, 16.7% of patients fulfilled the CDC criteria for symptomatic CAUTI, while the remainder represented asymptomatic bacteriuria.

Table 2 illustrates the patient-reported complications within 1 month of urethral catheterization. Among the 592 participants, 67 retained their indwelling catheter for the entire month, whereas 525 had the device removed earlier. Pain or discomfort was the most common complaint, reported by 81% of individuals with the catheter still in place versus 56% of those whose catheter had been removed (risk ratio 1.43, 95% CI, 1.25–1.65; P < 0.001). Hematuria and bladder spasms were also significantly more frequent in the indwelling group, with risk ratios of 1.91 (95% CI, 1.42–2.58) and 2.02 (95% CI, 1.40–2.93), respectively. Local complications predominated, with nearly half of the patients with a persistent catheter developing meatal inflammation or balanoposthitis, a five-fold excess compared to the removal group. The presence of the catheter was additionally linked to a seven-fold increase in urinary calculi and an eight-fold rise in limitations to social or physical activities. By contrast, the incidence of dysuria after catheter removal did not differ significantly between groups (RR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.37–1.84). These findings highlight the substantial symptom burden associated with prolonged urethral catheterization and emphasize specific domains, particularly infectious and quality-of-life sequelae, in which early removal may confer a measurable benefit.

Patient-reported adverse effects

In addition to infectious outcomes, the study prospectively captured patient-reported physical, social, and sexual consequences of catheterization, allowing quantification of psychosocial morbidity alongside microbiological events. Both infectious and noninfectious complications were observed among patients with IUCs [Table 3]. Adverse-event frequency escalated sharply with prolonged catheterization. Infectious complications were uncommon within the 1st week (3.5%) but quadrupled after 2 weeks and reached 15.8% beyond 30 days (χ² =19.4, P = 0.0004). A similar temporal pattern was observed for irritative voiding symptoms, pain, hematuria. and sexual dysfunction, each displaying a significant linear trend (P < 0.01 for all). Notably, pain or discomfort increased almost ten-fold from 1.5% in the early window to 14% after 1 month. Even low-incidence events such as meatal bleeding became more prevalent over time (P = 0.046). These findings underline dwell-time as an independent driver of morbidity and reinforce the need for early catheter removal whenever feasible.

Table 4 demonstrates a clear duration-response relationship: Complication probability rises sharply beyond 2 weeks and peaks when catheters remain in situ >30 days, where sexual dysfunction, hematuria and pain/discomfort increase at least seven-fold. Clinically, the data support aggressive strategies to minimise dwell time, favouring early removal or intermittent catheterization when use beyond 14 days is anticipated, and heightened surveillance for infection and hematuria thereafter. Caution is warranted because each patient was assumed to contribute only one event per category, overlap between complications was not captured, and small numbers in the >30-day cohort yielded wide CIs. Nevertheless, the consistency and magnitude of risk highlight the need to limit prolonged catheterization.

Microbial isolates

The microbiological analysis revealed a marked predominance of Gram-negative organisms, which accounted for 85.3% of all positive cultures. Escherichia coli was the leading pathogen, representing nearly two-thirds of the isolates (61.4%), followed by Klebsiella spp. (15.9%). Less frequent but clinically important Gram-negative bacilli included Proteus spp. (5.0%) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (3.0%). Gram-positive cocci were led by Enterococcus spp., comprising 9.3% of isolates, while fungal growth – exclusively Candida spp. – was observed in 5.4% of cultures.

This distribution highlights the well-recognized dominance of Enterobacteria in catheter-associated urinary tract infections. Yet, it also highlights a nonnegligible contribution from Gram-positive and fungal species, particularly in patients with prolonged dwell times or prior exposure to antimicrobials. The isolation of Enterococcus and Candida suggests that biofilm maturation and altered urinary tract physiology may broaden the pathogen profile over time, reinforcing the need for diligent catheter stewardship and empiric therapy that covers Gram-negative bacilli and opportunistic organisms when clinically indicated.

Type of catheter used and associated outcomes

Figure 4 highlights the significant variation in UTI incidence rates among different catheter types. Latex catheters were associated with the highest UTI incidence, observed in 59.0% of patients (266 out of 451), and used as the comparative analysis baseline. Silicone catheters demonstrated a markedly lower incidence of UTIs, at 21.8% (26 out of 120 patients), corresponding to a RR of 0.37 and an absolute risk reduction of 37.2% compared to Latex. BIP-coated catheters showed the lowest incidence, with UTIs occurring in only 4.8% of cases (1 out of 21 patients), translating to a RR of 0.08 and an absolute risk reduction of 54.2% relative to Latex. The routine use of antibiotic-coated catheters, particularly for high-risk patients who require prolonged catheterization, appears to be an effective strategy for reducing infection rates.

DISCUSSION

Indwelling urinary catheters (IUCs) remain indispensable in everyday urological practice, yet our data confirm that they continue to carry a substantial physical and psychosocial burden. Infective events were detected in almost one-quarter of patients who still had a catheter in situ at 30 days and in one-sixth of those whose catheter had been removed, yielding an overall CAUTI rate of 16.7%. Noninfectious morbidity was even more striking: pain or discomfort affected four out of five patients during catheterization. At the same time, hematuria and bladder spasm were reported by nearly half and one-third, respectively. These figures align with contemporary studies, which show that the noninfectious burden is at least as prevalent as infection and equally detrimental to quality of life.[78]

Complication profiles changed with dwell time. Although pain scores decreased modestly between the 2-week and 1-month assessments (37.4% to 31.8%), the proportion of infectious complications increased from 11.2% to 16.7%. This divergence suggests that while nociceptive adaptation may occur, microbial colonization continues to accumulate, highlighting the importance of limiting catheter duration whenever possible.[7]

Proper catheter maintenance is pivotal. Education of both staff and patients on hand hygiene, aseptic handling of connections and routine meatal cleansing has consistently reduced CAUTI in implementation studies.[14] For chronic Foley catheters, most guidelines recommend replacement every 4–6 weeks, with an earlier exchange if encrustation, obstruction, or symptomatic infection occurs.[15] Our findings support these timelines: adverse-event density rose sharply beyond the 4-week mark.

Escherichia coli remained the leading pathogen, followed by Klebsiella, Enterococcus and, less frequently, Pseudomonas and Candida. The emergence of multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs) in 12% of positive cultures highlights the need for culture-directed therapy and stringent stewardship to prevent unnecessary broad-spectrum antibiotic use.[4] Minimising empirical coverage and narrowing treatment, once sensitivities are available, should be a mandatory practice.

Catheterization profoundly disrupted daily living: 42% of patients with an indwelling catheter curtailed social activities, and 46% reported sexual dysfunction – effects that largely resolved after removal. These findings, echoing the scant literature on patient-reported outcomes, highlight the need to integrate psychological support and counselling into catheter management pathways.

Our multivariable analysis confirmed dwell time as the strongest independent predictor of complications, consistent with meta-analytic data.[5] Electronic stop-orders, nurse-led reminder protocols, and automatic alerts have reduced CAUTI by up to 70% and could be considered for wider implementation in Indian hospitals, where removal bundles are inconsistently applied.[16] Material choice also mattered: latex catheters quadrupled the risk of infection relative to silicone, consistent with randomised evidence favouring silicone for long-term use.[11] Although antibiotic-coated devices achieved the lowest infection rate in our cohort, their higher cost argues for selective deployment in high-risk patients until robust local cost-effectiveness data become available.[17]

Taken together, the findings confirm that IUCs remain a double-edged sword: lifesaving for acute urinary management yet fraught with infectious, mechanical, and psychosocial complications. Limiting dwell time, adopting silicone or antimicrobial-coated catheters when indicated, enforcing evidence-based care bundles, and incorporating patient-centered monitoring can markedly reduce this burden.[18] In comparison with previous work, Saint et al.[5] reported a 5.3% daily increase in bacteriuria, whereas our cohort showed a comparable 7% daily odds rise after multivariable adjustment. Few studies have examined sexual dysfunction; our data highlight a 21-fold relative increase beyond 30 days,[19] pointing out the psychosocial burden of prolonged catheterization.

Limitations of our study

The limited generalizability of the findings and single-center design limit their wider relevance, necessitating a multicenter study to gain a more thorough understanding of catheter care. Long-term issues, such as recurring infections and urethral strictures, may not be detected during the 30-day follow-up. The reliability of results may be impacted by recall and reporting biases introduced by patient-reported outcomes. Furthermore, the study did not thoroughly examine antibiotic prophylaxis, catheter materials, or insertion methods, all of which could impact the rate of complications. In addition, the results are limited to hospitalised patients, excluding insights from outpatient and home care settings. Finally, differences in healthcare professional practices – such as training and catheter management strategies – were not evaluated, which may impact patient outcomes.

Take-home points

Indwelling urethral catheters remain a necessary tool in modern urology, yet their risks escalate rapidly with time and material choice. In this cohort, keeping a catheter beyond 5 days raised the odds of any complication by more than half, and dwell times > 30 days nearly quadrupled the risk of infection while amplifying hematuria, pain, and sexual dysfunction seven- to twenty-fold. Latex devices carried the heaviest burden, whereas switching to silicone halved the rates of CAUTIs, and antibiotic-impregnated catheters (BIP) cut infections by more than 90%. Notably, four in ten patients reported social withdrawal and nearly half experienced sexual difficulties – harms often ignored in routine care. Together, these findings mandate a catheter-stewardship strategy centered on early removal, evidence-based material selection, rigorous aseptic maintenance, and systematic capture of patient-reported outcomes to safeguard physical and psychosocial well-being.

CONCLUSIONS

Prolonged indwelling catheterization and latex material are strong, independent drivers of CAUTI and patient-centered morbidity. Early removal and silicone substitution represent low-cost, high-impact interventions to improve patient outcomes. Randomized trials are warranted to validate the observed benefits of BIP-impregnated catheters. With the rising incidence of MDROs, strict adherence to protocols on antibiotic stewardship will minimise resistance and improve patient outcomes.
