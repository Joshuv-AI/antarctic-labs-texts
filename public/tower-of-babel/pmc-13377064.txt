LNP-based delivery of a Toll-like receptor 9 agonist elicits potent adjuvant effects and antitumor immunity
Authors: Yarong Zeng, Jialin Xu, Feng Han, Sibo Zhang, Rong Su, Yufang Li, Renfei Gao, Xiuli Zhang, Lizhi Zhou, Tingting Li, Hai Yu, Qingbing Zheng, Ningshao Xia, Shaowei Li, Ying Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The success of therapeutic cancer vaccines hinges on overcoming the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) and activating robust, durable cellular immunity. Here, we engineered a highly translatable vaccine adjuvant delivery system (LNPC) by integrating the Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) agonist CpG with the FDA-approved ionizable lipid ALC-0315. This lipid nanoparticle (LNP) architecture establishes a safe, localized immunological depot that completely shields CpG from systemic nuclease degradation. Crucially, it enables the spatiotemporally coordinated co-delivery of physically admixed antigens and features a “smart” pH-responsive mechanism for targeted endosomal payload release. Consequently, LNPC efficiently activates the TLR9/MyD88 signaling pathway, promotes robust dendritic cell maturation, and drives a pronounced Th1-biased polarization. It uniquely induces the massive expansion of polyfunctional CD8+ T cells across diverse immunogens. In aggressive B16F10-OVA melanoma and HPV-associated TC-1 tumor models, LNPC-adjuvanted vaccines successfully remodeled the highly suppressive TME, achieving near-complete tumor clearance, significantly prolonged survival, and durable immune memory without inducing off-target systemic toxicity. Ultimately, the LNPC system represents a versatile, highly effective adjuvant platform with substantial clinical translational potential, offering a compelling new strategy for personalized tumor immunotherapy.

Introduction

The rapid development of lipid nanoparticle (LNP) technology has revolutionized mRNA vaccine development by addressing key challenges in nucleic acid delivery1. As multifunctional delivery vehicles, LNPs not only protect genetic payloads from enzymatic degradation but also promote efficient cellular uptake, in part through membrane fusion mechanisms2–4. Their structural features enhance immunogenicity and significantly improve the intracellular release and translation of the payload5,6. Characterized by excellent biocompatibility, multifunctionality, and scalable manufacturing, LNPs have emerged as a robust platform for diverse vaccine applications7,8. These advances have opened new avenues for the prevention and treatment of many diseases, particularly in tumor immunotherapy where conventional modalities often fall short9,10. In oncology, activating a robust and durable immune response remains central to restraining tumor growth and metastasis. Unfortunately, insufficient stimulation of antigen-presenting cells (APCs) severely limits the clinical efficacy of therapeutic tumor vaccines11, underscoring the urgent need for innovative vaccine adjuvants.

The term “adjuvant” has evolved from denoting a single component to encompassing rationally designed vaccine adjuvant delivery systems (VADS) that combine delivery vehicles with targeted immunomodulatory molecules12–15. While traditional liposomes have succeeded clinically (e.g., the AS01B adjuvant in Shingrix®, composed of DOPC, cholesterol, MPL, and QS-21), their efficacy is often constrained when delivering nucleic acid-based pattern-recognition receptor (PRR) agonists that require precise intracellular targeting16–18. A prime example is non-methylated CpG oligodeoxynucleotides (CpG ODNs), which are potent, selective agonists of Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9)19. Biologically, CpG motifs must engage TLR9 located strictly within the endosomes of dendritic cells (DCs) to trigger a MyD88-dependent pathway20. This engagement promotes DC maturation, upregulates co-stimulatory molecules (CD80/CD86), and drives the clonal expansion of T cells, thereby fostering a Th1-type cytokine milieu essential for cytotoxic T-lymphocyte (CTL) recognition and tumor killing21,22. However, the clinical translation of free CpG ODNs remains severely constrained by their high susceptibility to nuclease-mediated degradation in vivo, suboptimal APC cellular uptake, and inefficient endosomal accumulation23–25.

To overcome these strict biological barriers, utilizing LNPs emerges not merely as a convenient upgrade from traditional liposomes, but as a mechanistically essential delivery architecture26,27. While LNPs are widely recognized as sophisticated vehicles for large mRNA molecules, utilizing such a powerful tool for the delivery of immunostimulatory oligonucleotides like CpG is driven by three compelling rationales. First, the precise intracellular localization of TLR9 demands an active endosomal interaction mechanism19. Unlike traditional liposomes that often suffer from prolonged endosomal entrapment, LNPs formulated with ionizable lipids exhibit unique pH-responsive behavior. Upon entering the acidic endosomal environment, these lipids become rapidly protonated, facilitating membrane destabilization and ensuring the precise, high-efficiency engagement of CpG with TLR928,29. Second, LNPs of approximately 70–100 nm naturally drain into lymph nodes and are preferentially engulfed by APCs, serving as the critical immunological battlefield required for optimal adjuvant action30,31. Third, LNPs possess endogenous adjuvant properties that activate innate inflammatory pathways32–34. This intrinsic immunogenicity acts highly synergistically with CpG, creating a potent, two-pronged Th1-skewing immune response that is critical for overcoming profound tumor-associated immunosuppression.

Therefore, rather than unnecessarily complicating adjuvant design, we strategically engineered a highly translatable VADS platform utilizing clinically validated, FDA-approved LNP components (e.g., ALC-0315) manufactured via scalable microfluidics35,36. This LNP-CpG (LNPC) adjuvant system precisely encapsulates CpG ODNs to enhance their in vivo stability, enables the spatiotemporally coordinated co-delivery of physically admixed tumor antigens, and robustly activates both innate and adaptive immunity. In aggressive established B16-OVA tumors, as well as in the clinically relevant TC-1 cervical cancer model, the LNPC system successfully remodeled the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), reversed tumor immune tolerance, and demonstrated superior therapeutic antitumor activity. These findings establish LNPC as a versatile and highly translatable VADS platform adaptable to multiple recombinant cancer vaccines. Ultimately, this work highlights how the rational integration of proven LNP technologies with specific immunostimulants can overcome the distinct efficacy bottlenecks of current tumor immunotherapies.

Results

LNP-encapsulated CpG exhibits vaccine adjuvant activity

To establish a highly translatable vaccine adjuvant delivery platform, we constructed a CpG delivery system utilizing the core lipid formulation of the FDA-approved Pfizer–BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine (ALC-0315 LNP)36. As a foundational step to ensure optimal physicochemical properties for our microfluidic setup and the short oligonucleotide payload, we systematically evaluated the nitrogen-to-phosphorus (N/P) ratio, a critical parameter governing nanoparticle assembly37–39. A series of LNP-encapsulated CpG complexes (LNPCs; Fig. 1a) were synthesized by precisely mixing an aqueous phase containing graded concentrations of CpG with the lipid phase via microfluidic technology at a flow-rate ratio of 3:1. Consistent with previous reports on ALC-0315 LNPs for short ODN delivery, comprehensive physicochemical characterization confirmed the expected N/P-dependent trends (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) revealed that the hydrated particle sizes of the blank LNPs and all LNPC formulations were tightly clustered in the 70–100 nm range, a dimension highly favorable for efficient interstitial drainage to the lymph nodes40 (Fig. 1b, c). The polydispersity indices (PDI) remained <0.2, indicating uniform size distributions with excellent monodispersity (Fig. 1c). As the N/P ratio increased, both particle size and PDI exhibited an upward trend. Meanwhile, zeta potentials remained near-neutral to weakly negative (−5 to −20 mV), a charge regime that strongly favors cellular interactions and endo-lysosomal trafficking while minimizing systemic toxicity41–43 (Fig. 1d). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) provided further structural insight: the LNPC formulated at the lowest tested ratio (N/P = 2.5) displayed a characteristic type-III multicompartmental vacuolar morphology (Fig. 1e). This specific architecture is strongly associated with its remarkably high CpG encapsulation efficiency (>90%) and sustained payload-release kinetics44 (Fig. 1f). Given that nanoparticles of ~80 nm with near-neutral surface charge are widely documented to drain efficiently to lymphoid organs45, we selected the N/P = 2.5 LNPC as our standardized formulation for all subsequent in vitro and in vivo studies.

Formulation stability and biocompatibility are absolute prerequisites for evaluating novel adjuvants in preclinical settings. After storage in PBS at 4 °C for 7 days, the LNPCs exhibited minimal aggregation, with a particle-size increase of <10% and a PDI change of <0.1, robustly meeting the requirements for in vivo application (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). Furthermore, a viable nucleic acid delivery system must survive the nuclease-rich environment of the bloodstream before efficiently releasing its cargo within the target intracellular compartments. Therefore, we evaluated the serum stability of our formulation. Naked CpG and LNPCs were incubated with 10% mouse serum and analyzed via agarose gel electrophoresis (Supplementary Fig. 1d). As expected, the naked CpG was highly susceptible to nucleases, with its band intensity decreasing dramatically within 0.5 h and nearly disappearing thereafter. In sharp contrast, the LNPC formulation provided robust shielding for the payload. Even after 4 h of incubation in serum, the intact CpG band recovered from the LNPC remained distinctly bright, explicitly demonstrating the system’s ability to protect CpG from premature enzymatic cleavage. Having confirmed the structural integrity of LNPCs in physiological environments, we investigated their drug release kinetics. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1e, at physiological pH (pH 7.4), the LNPC exhibited excellent stability with minimal leakage (<10% CpG released) over the entire observation period. Strikingly, under an acidic condition mimicking the late endosomal environment (pH 5.0), a rapid and significant release of CpG was triggered, reaching approximately 58%. This indicates a “smart” pH-responsive release behavior ideal for intracellular delivery. It is worth noting that the slightly lower recovery rate of the free CpG-Cy5 control at pH 5.0 (~80%) compared to pH 7.4 (~100%) was likely an artifact caused by minor non-specific adsorption of the oligonucleotides onto the ultrafiltration membrane and slight fluorescence fluctuation of the Cy5 dye under acidic conditions. Nevertheless, the drastic contrast in the release profiles of LNPCs between the two pH conditions remains highly significant.

Cytotoxicity analysis demonstrated that the LNPC had no deleterious effect on the viability or proliferation of DC2.4 cells (Supplementary Fig. 2a), confirming its excellent biocompatibility. As professional APCs, dendritic cells (DCs) orchestrate T-cell activation, making them ideal models for in vitro immunological assessment46. To evaluate cellular uptake, we prepared LNPCs using Cy5-labeled CpG. Compared with free CpG-Cy5, LNP encapsulation markedly enhanced the internalization of the TLR9 agonist by DC2.4 cells (Fig. 1g, h). Furthermore, after 48 h of co-incubation, the LNPC formulation significantly upregulated the expression of surface activation markers (CD80 and CD86) on DC2.4 cells relative to the controls (Fig. 1i, Supplementary Fig. 2b), consistent with effective and sustained DC maturation. To directly address how this adjuvant system handles physically admixed antigens, we utilized FITC-conjugated ovalbumin (FITC-OVA) as a model protein. Flow cytometry quantitatively demonstrated that LNPC treatment drove a substantial increase in FITC-OVA cellular uptake compared to cells treated with the antigen alone or admixed with free CpG (Fig. 1j). This profound enhancement is likely mediated by the non-covalent adsorption of the soluble antigen onto the LNP surface, forming transient complexes that are efficiently endocytosed by the activated DCs47. Subsequent confocal microscopy analysis clearly corroborated these findings (Fig. 1k). While cells treated with OVA alone or other single-component controls exhibited negligible intracellular fluorescence (Supplementary Fig. 3), LNPC-treated cells displayed concentrated perinuclear accumulation of FITC-OVA. Notably, a quantitative colocalization analysis between the internalized FITC-OVA and LysoTracker-stained compartments yielded a modest correlation (Pearson’s Correlation Coefficient [PCC] = 0.30)48. Biologically, this distinct separation of the green antigen signal from the intact red endo-lysosomes is a highly desirable mechanistic outcome: it strongly suggests that the LNPC system not only drives the initial endocytic uptake of the admixed antigen but also efficiently facilitates its subsequent endosomal escape into the cytosol via lipid membrane disruption and the proton-sponge effect49. This targeted cytosolic delivery is a critical prerequisite for robust proteasomal processing and MHC Class I cross-presentation, which are required to activate cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocytes50.

Taken together, the N/P = 2.5 LNPC displayed optimal physicochemical and biological performance, characterized by favorable morphometric properties, high encapsulation efficiency, excellent stability, low cytotoxicity, and robust DC activation. These foundational features, coupled with its notable ability to drive antigen uptake and facilitate vital endosomal escape, firmly establish the LNPC as a highly potent vaccine-adjuvant delivery platform with substantial translational potential.

LNPC promotes TLR9 activation and induces a stronger early immune response

TLR9, a key member of the Toll-like receptor family, is predominantly localized in the endosomal membranes of B lymphocytes and DCs51,52. It recognizes conserved unmethylated CpG motifs in pathogen DNA and activates nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) via a myeloid differentiation primary-response protein 88 (MyD88)–dependent pathway. This cascade regulates B-cell activating factor (BAFF) and induces the production of granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), type I interferon (IFN-α), type II interferon (IFN-γ), and other pro-inflammatory cytokines53–55. Based on this mechanism, synthetic CpG ODNs with immunostimulatory sequences have been extensively developed as TLR9-specific agonists to enhance vaccine-induced Th1-type responses. However, their in vivo adjuvant efficacy is strictly constrained by their rapid degradation by endogenous nucleases and suboptimal cellular internalization into APCs56,57.

To address these delivery barriers, we employed LNP encapsulation to enhance the intracellular trafficking of free CpG and potentiate downstream pathway activation (Fig. 2a). Due to their polyanionic nature, unformulated CpG oligonucleotides exhibit inefficient cellular uptake, severely limiting their accumulation at intracellular target sites58. Indeed, confocal microscopy revealed that free CpG underwent minimal cellular internalization and negligible endo-lysosomal localization (PCC = 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 4). In contrast, the LNPC formulation facilitated robust cellular uptake and significantly augmented the trafficking of CpG into the endo-lysosomal compartments (PCC = 0.21) (Fig. 2b). Although the absolute colocalization with lysosomal markers appears relatively modest at this specific imaging time point, this phenomenon reflects the highly dynamic endosomal maturation, transit, and escape processes characteristic of ionizable LNPs59,60. Crucially, this LNP-driven enrichment within the endo-lysosomal pathway provides the essential spatiotemporal window for CpG to successfully engage its cognate receptor, TLR9. Having established the efficient intracellular delivery of LNPCs, we next verified whether this encapsulation preserved the innate immunostimulatory activity of CpG in vitro. Mouse splenocytes were co-cultured with blank LNPs, free CpG, or LNPCs for 48 h. We then analyzed the early activation marker CD69 across various immune subsets, including CD19+ B cells, CD4+/CD8+ T cells, and CD11c+ DCs (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 5). Both free CpG and LNPCs significantly upregulated CD69 expression, whereas empty LNPs showed no appreciable direct activation. Concurrently, both CpG formulations increased total TLR9 (tTLR9) expression in B cells (Fig. 2d). This confirms that LNP encapsulation does not compromise the intrinsic activity of the CpG payload. We further evaluated APC maturation by co-incubating bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) with each adjuvant and the model antigen OVA. Compared to the blank control, both free CpG and LNPCs significantly upregulated the maturation markers CD80 and CD86 (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 6). These results fully align with effective TLR9-driven APC activation.

Building on these in vitro findings, we investigated the early in vivo adjuvant effects. Mice were immunized intramuscularly, and draining lymph nodes (dLNs) were harvested 24 h later for RT-qPCR analysis (Fig. 2f–k). We first evaluated early upstream signaling components, including TLR9, NF-κB, and BAFF (Fig. 2f, g, i). The LNPC group exhibited an approximate twofold numerical increase in average mRNA expression for these genes. However, these differences did not reach strict statistical significance when compared to the single-adjuvant controls (blank LNP and free CpG). In striking contrast, the transcription levels of critical downstream effector cytokines—specifically GM-CSF, IFN-α, and IFN-γ—were significantly elevated in the LNPC group compared to both control cohorts (Fig. 2h, j, k). Biologically, the robust activation of the TLR9 pathway relies heavily on rapid protein-level amplification and post-translational modifications, rather than solely on the mRNA upregulation of the upstream receptor itself18. Therefore, the pronounced surge in downstream pro-inflammatory cytokines clearly indicates that LNP delivery successfully orchestrates and amplifies the ultimate functional output of CpG immunostimulation in vivo.

On day 3 post-immunization, the frequencies of follicular helper T cells (Tfh, CXCR5+PD-1+) and germinal center B cells (GC B, GL7+CD95+) in the dLNs were significantly increased in the LNPC group compared with the controls, indicative of an accelerated establishment of early adaptive immunity (Fig. 2l, m and Supplementary Fig. 7). Immunofluorescence further revealed an increased number of Ki67+ proliferating cells and enlarged lymph-node volumes in the LNPC group, consistent with rapid germinal-center formation (Supplementary Fig. 8). Furthermore, flow cytometric analysis of the injected muscle tissue revealed marked infiltration of macrophages, monocytes, and CD11b+ DCs in the LNPC group, demonstrating substantially greater local recruitment than in other cohorts (Fig. 2n and Supplementary Fig. 9, 10). Notably, the LNPC formulation produced a pronounced localized antigen-depot effect, reminiscent of aluminum adjuvants62. Consequently, infiltrating DCs at the injection site internalized significantly more antigen than those in the control groups, providing a strong mechanistic basis for the enhanced innate priming63 (Fig. 2o, p). In conclusion, LNPCs significantly augment the immunostimulatory effects of CpG in vivo by successfully overcoming cellular entry barriers, promoting extensive immune-cell recruitment via a localized depot effect, and accelerating germinal-center formation, thereby potently strengthening early vaccine-elicited immune responses.

LNPC drives antigen-specific cellular and humoral immune responses in vivo

The development of vaccine adjuvants—key components for enhancing antigen immunogenicity—has long faced the critical challenge of balancing efficacy and safety64,65. Traditional aluminum adjuvants, while widely used clinically, primarily induce Th2-type responses and suboptimally activate cellular immunity66. Conversely, many newer TLR-agonist adjuvants promote robust Th1-type responses but often suffer from poor in vivo stability and higher systemic reactogenicity67,68. Even unformulated LNPs, despite receiving considerable attention, display limitations in comprehensively shaping adaptive immunity when utilized as standalone adjuvants69,70. Motivated by these constraints, we developed the integrated LNPC adjuvant system. We systematically evaluated its immune-enhancing effects across distinct immunogens to elucidate its mechanism of action and translational potential, thereby informing the design of next-generation, safe, and highly effective vaccine adjuvants.

We first selected ovalbumin (OVA) as a classical model antigen owing to its well-characterized immunogenicity, structural stability, and standardized readouts, providing a reliable reference framework71. Mice received a three-dose intramuscular immunization regimen (days 0, 14, and 28) with saline, OVA alone, or OVA formulated with different adjuvants (blank LNP, free CpG, or LNPC) (Fig. 3a). Serial serological analysis revealed that the LNPC-adjuvanted vaccine consistently elicited significantly higher OVA-specific IgG titers than all other control groups throughout the entire immunization schedule (Fig. 3b). By week 6, the LNPC formulation induced markedly elevated levels of both IgG1 (Th2-associated) and IgG2c (Th1-associated) antibodies, exhibiting a pronounced bias toward a Th1-type humoral response (Fig. 3c–e). To evaluate cellular immunity, spleens were harvested 2 weeks after the final dose. ELISpot assays demonstrated a substantial 7- to 15-fold increase in IFN-γ- and IL-2-secreting spot-forming cells in the LNPC group compared with the other adjuvant cohorts (Fig. 3f, g). Notably, blank LNPs alone exhibited greater cellular immunogenicity than free CpG. This phenomenon reflects the inherent capacity of the particulate LNP to form a localized inflammatory depot and effectively recruit APCs in vivo, which contrasts sharply with the rapid systemic clearance of soluble CpG. However, the integrated LNPC system synergistically amplified these baseline responses to significantly higher levels. Flow cytometry further confirmed that LNPCs profoundly augmented antigen-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses (Supplementary Fig. 11). Within the CD8+ T-cell compartment, the proportions of IFN-γ+ (27.3 ± 3.5%), IL-2+ (0.7 ± 0.2%), and TNF-α+ (19.1 ± 3.5%) cells were markedly elevated. This was accompanied by a robust expansion of highly potent, multifunctional CD8+ T-cell subsets (TNF-α+IFN-γ+, IL-2+IFN-γ+, and IL-2+TNF-α+) (Fig. 3h–s; Supplementary Fig. 13) and an increased frequency of effector-memory CD8+ T cells (TEM, CD44highCD62Llow) (Fig. 3t, u; Supplementary Fig. 14). Although CD4+ responses were comparatively modest relative to the CD8+ compartment, the LNPC still successfully enhanced multifunctional CD4+ T cells to levels far exceeding those induced by LNP or CpG alone (Supplementary Fig. 12). Together, these data indicate that the LNPC potently activates comprehensive cellular immunity, with a striking ability to mobilize multifunctional CD8+ T cells. To rigorously assess the breadth of our platform and explicitly define its mechanistic superiority, we next evaluated a recombinant herpes zoster virus gEgI antigen model. Here, we specifically tested whether the LNPC’s activity reflects more than a simple additive effect of its individual components by comparing it directly to a physical mixture (LNP+free CpG)72–74. Mice received two intramuscular doses at weeks 0 and 3; sera were collected 2 weeks post-booster for antibody analyses, and spleens were isolated for T-cell assays (Supplementary Fig. 15a). Regarding humoral responses, both the integrated LNPC system and the simple physical mixture induced substantially higher gEgI-specific IgG and IgG2c titers compared to single-adjuvant controls, exhibiting a pronounced Th1 bias (Supplementary Fig. 15b–e). Because both mixed formulations efficiently drove total antibody production to a near-saturation plateau, no statistically significant difference was observed between the LNPC and the physical mixture in overall humoral titers. Crucially, however, the fundamental superiority of the LNPC system unequivocally emerged in the qualitative cellular immune response. ELISpot analysis revealed approximately twofold higher numbers of IFN-γ- and IL-2-secreting cells in the LNPC group compared to the physical mixture (Supplementary Fig. 15f, g). Flow cytometry profiling further demonstrated that the integrated LNPC formulation significantly increased the frequencies of highly potent, multifunctional CD4+ T-cell subsets (IFN-γ+IL-2+, IFN-γ+TNF-α+, and IL-2+TNF-α+) over the simple admixture (Supplementary Fig. 15h–k). While overall CD8+ T-cell responses were modest in this specific prophylactic model, the LNPC group still maintained an observable advantage over all other adjuvant configurations (Supplementary Fig. 15l–o). Given the critical clinical importance of multifunctional CD4+ Th1 cell responses for herpes zoster vaccine efficacy, this qualitative cellular shift is highly significant. Furthermore, this immune profile differs from the CD8+-dominant response observed with OVA, highlighting the highly adaptive, antigen-dependent immunomodulation uniquely conferred by the LNPC system.

Mechanistically, in the simple LNP+CpG physical mixture, the CpG remains exposed in the extracellular space. While this co-administration may partially improve local inflammatory signaling, asynchronous cellular uptake inherently limits true intracellular synergy. By contrast, the LNPC system physically encapsulates the CpG within the LNP core, ensuring rigorous nuclease protection. More importantly, driven by the localized depot effect and non-covalent interactions at the injection site, the LNPC guarantees the spatiotemporally coordinated cellular co-delivery of both the admixed antigen and the TLR9 agonist into the same endosome of the same APC. This synchronized intracellular delivery is a fundamental prerequisite to drive the potent, multifunctional T-cell activation observed here75. This definitively proves that the integrated LNPC structurally and functionally surpasses the simple additive effects of mixed adjuvants. In summary, using two mechanistically distinct antigen models, these results firmly establish LNPC as a superior, versatile adjuvant platform with highly promising applicability across diverse vaccine modalities.

LNPC exerts potent antitumor effects in the B16F10-OVA tumor model

Given the indispensable role of CD8+ T cells in antitumor immunity and our finding that the LNPC system significantly enhances polyfunctional T-cell responses, we next evaluated its therapeutic efficacy. Tumors in the saline and OVA-alone control groups grew rapidly. While the LNP + OVA and CpG+OVA formulations slowed tumor growth to some extent and extended median survival to 27 days, they ultimately failed to prevent continued tumor enlargement (p = 0.0013 vs. OVA). This revealed a dense infiltration of CD8+ T cells (red) specifically within LNPC-treated tumors (Fig. 4h). Concurrently, CD4+ T cells (green) displayed a distinct distribution pattern in the LNPC group, indicating their supportive role in sustaining a tumor-suppressive microenvironment. Beyond the robust T-cell infiltration, we further investigated the in situ activation status of professional APCs within the highly suppressive TME, which is a critical factor for locally sustaining adaptive immunity76. Comprehensive multi-color immunofluorescence on excised tumor sections revealed that the TME of LNPC-immunized mice was substantially reprogrammed. Specifically, co-staining for CD11c and the maturation marker CD86 revealed a significant accumulation of activated DCs infiltrating the tumor stroma. This inflamed state contrasted sharply with the immunologically “cold” microenvironment observed in the control groups (Supplementary Fig. 16c). Furthermore, staining for F4/80 and CD86 demonstrated an elevated density of activated macrophages within the LNPC-treated tumors (Supplementary Fig. 16c). The pronounced presence of these pro-inflammatory (M1-like) macrophages and mature DCs provides compelling visual evidence that the LNPC system successfully overcomes local immune tolerance. It effectively remodels the TME into an immunostimulatory state capable of supporting robust CD8+ T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity77–79.

To trace the mechanistic origins of this inflamed TME, we analyzed the tumor-draining lymph nodes (TDLNs) and systemic immunity76. Flow cytometry showed significantly increased recruitment and activation of DCs and macrophages in TDLNs from LNPC-treated mice (Fig. 4i, j). This observation is consistent with enhanced early antigen presentation and downstream T-cell priming. By optimizing early antigen presentation in the lymph nodes, driving the infiltration of activated APCs and cytotoxic CD8+ T cells into the tumor bed, and promoting systemic memory T-cell formation, LNPC effectively eradicates established tumors and provides durable immune protection. These findings highlight its substantial potential for therapeutic cancer vaccine development.

To evaluate the breadth and versatility of LNPC as an adjuvant for therapeutic cancer vaccines, we examined its immunoregulatory effects utilizing the HPV16 E7 (16E7) antigen in the TC-1 tumor model80. TC-1 cells stably express the HPV16 E6 and E7 oncoproteins, providing a clinically relevant and rigorously validated platform for immunotherapy studies targeting HPV-associated malignancies81,82.

Together, these data strongly substantiate the therapeutic superiority of LNPC as a highly versatile adjuvant platform for HPV-associated malignancies and underscore its significant translational relevance for broad clinical cancer immunotherapy.

Systemic safety and favorable biodistribution profile of the LNPC adjuvant system

The clinical deployment of vaccine adjuvants must satisfy two fundamental requirements: an excellent safety profile and robust immune-potentiating activity83. Having demonstrated that LNPC elicits potent immune responses across diverse antigen types, we rigorously assessed its preclinical safety through a systematic evaluation comprising comprehensive serum biochemistry and detailed histopathology following a three-dose immunization regimen.

To accurately interpret the biochemical data, we compared the results against both the saline control group and the established normal physiological reference ranges for healthy C57BL/6 mice84. The evaluated markers included hepatic function indicators (T-Bil: 1.7–15.4 μmol/L; ALT: 13–43 U/L; AST: 12–89 U/L; ALP: 49–230 U/L), a metabolic indicator (TC: 1.1–2.6 mmol/L), a renal function marker (CREA: 11–28 μmol/L), and markers of cardiac/skeletal muscle injury (CK: 30–267 U/L; LDH: 102–701 U/L) (Fig. 6a–h). Detailed analysis revealed that the administration of free CpG resulted in a statistically significant elevation in ALP (P = 0.0002 vs. Saline) and high individual variation in CK levels, hinting at potential mild hepatic or muscular stress associated with naked CpG exposure. Conversely, the biochemical profiles of the LNP and LNPC groups were highly comparable to the saline control. This observation reflects the instrument’s lower detection limit rather than pathology, representing a clinically healthy state. The excellent biocompatibility and absence of systemic toxicity observed with the LNPC system are mechanistically rooted in its unique in vivo spatiotemporal dynamics. To elucidate this, we systematically investigated the biodistribution profiles of the adjuvant formulations. Following intramuscular (i.m.) injection, longitudinal whole-body in vivo fluorescence imaging revealed divergent pharmacokinetic behaviors. The fluorescence signal of free CpG-Cy5 rapidly diminished from the injection site within 24 h due to rapid systemic clearance. This phenomenon likely accounts for the mild systemic stress (elevated ALP and CK) observed in its biochemical profile. In contrast, the LNPC-Cy5 formulation established a pronounced and sustained local depot. The intense LNPC-Cy5 signal persisted at the injection site through Day 1 and Day 3, and was still clearly detectable on Day 7 (Supplementary Fig. 22a). To confirm this precise tissue-level distribution, major systemic organs alongside the dLNs and the injected muscle were harvested for ex vivo imaging on Day 7. Notably, the LNPC-Cy5 fluorescence was localized primarily within the injected muscle, confirming the long-term depot effect. It was also clearly detected within the dLNs, with negligible off-target accumulation in vital systemic organs (Supplementary Fig. 22a). We further corroborated this intrinsic lymphatic tropism by administering empty DiR-labeled LNPs intramuscularly. At 6 h post-injection, the carrier primarily resided at the injection site and actively drained into the dLNs, exhibiting minimal systemic liver targeting (Supplementary Fig. 22b). Furthermore, to highlight how LNP encapsulation fundamentally alters the systemic pharmacokinetics of the CpG payload, we performed an intravenous (i.v.) injection control. At 6 h post-i.v. administration, free CpG-Cy5 exhibited widespread systemic distribution and rapid clearance through the kidneys, lungs, and spleen. Conversely, the LNPC system significantly restricted this rapid systemic metabolic clearance, effectively sequestering and targeting the payload primarily to the liver (a classic LNP metabolic sink) and lymphoid organs (Supplementary Fig. 22c).

Taken together, the comprehensive toxicological and biodistribution profiles confirm that LNPC achieves an optimal balance between potent immunostimulation and systemic safety. By establishing a sustained injection-site depot and facilitating precise lymphatic drainage, the nanoparticle architecture prevents the rapid systemic dissemination typically associated with soluble TLR9 agonists. This spatiotemporal control confines robust immune activation strictly to the targeted lymphoid tissues, thereby averting systemic adverse effects and positioning the LNPC platform as a rigorously validated, translatable adjuvant technology.

Discussion

Cancer vaccines represent a major research frontier in tumor prevention and therapy, with broad application prospects, particularly for virus-related malignancies such as HPV-induced cervical cancer and HBV-associated hepatocellular carcinoma85–87. While gene-based vaccines (e.g., mRNA) and peptide-based vaccines have shown substantial clinical potential due to their safety and rapid manufacturing88–90, most vaccine platforms inherently suffer from insufficient intrinsic immunogenicity. The highly suppressive TME further dampens vaccine efficacy, underscoring the urgent need for potent immunostimulatory adjuvants that can sustain durable antitumor responses91,92.

The TLR9 agonist CpG ODN exhibits robust Th1-skewing activity; however, its in vivo translational efficacy is severely constrained by its high susceptibility to nuclease degradation and inefficient cellular internalization, necessitating an advanced delivery vehicle93,94. Among nonviral vectors, LNPs have emerged as the clinical gold standard. In this study, to maximize clinical translatability rather than synthesizing de novo lipids, we strategically constructed an integrated LNPC delivery system utilizing ALC-0315, an ionizable lipid already authorized in human vaccines. While seminal studies by Zhong et al.37 have provided a robust materials-science foundation through high-throughput lipid screening, the novelty of our current study lies in shifting the paradigm toward a comprehensive elucidation of the downstream biological mechanisms and their rigorous application in highly suppressive tumor models.

Firstly, the dense lipid bilayer composed of ALC-0315, DSPC, and cholesterol acts as a robust physical barrier during systemic circulation. As evidenced by the serum stability assay (Supplementary Fig. 2a), LNPCs efficiently shielded the CpG from nuclease degradation for over 4 h, whereas free CpG was degraded within 30 min. This exceptional protective capability prevents premature drug leakage and systemic toxicity, ensuring that a sufficient intact dose survives the bloodstream to reach target immune cells. Following cellular uptake, the LNPC system exhibits its second major advantage: a “smart” pH-responsive release mechanism driven by the ionizable lipid ALC-0315 (pKa ~ 6.0–6.2). The ultrafiltration assay (Supplementary Fig. 2b) revealed that while LNPCs remain highly stable at the neutral pH of the bloodstream (pH 7.4), they undergo rapid disassembly and cargo release (~58%) in an acidic environment (pH 4.5–5.0). Upon localization into late endosomes or lysosomes, the amine groups of ALC-0315 become rapidly protonated, inducing strong electrostatic repulsion and lipid phase separation. Crucially, because TLR9 is specifically localized within these acidic endosomes, this spatially triggered release precisely unloads the CpG exactly where its receptor is located. This optimal spatial and environmental synchronization maximizes receptor engagement, thereby potently enhancing the subsequent immune cascade.

At the cellular level, the LNPC system effectively addresses the delivery limitations associated with unformulated CpG. A comparative analysis of our flow cytometry and confocal microscopy data provides interesting insights into the intracellular behavior of the adjuvant. While flow cytometry (utilizing pH-insensitive CpG-Cy5) detected moderate cell-associated fluorescence for free CpG, confocal imaging (with pH-sensitive CpG-FAM) revealed relatively low intracellular accumulation and endo-lysosomal colocalization (PCC = 0.05) (Fig. 1g, h; Supplementary Fig. 3). This variance likely arises from two contributing factors. First, a portion of the flow cytometry signal for free CpG may originate from the non-specific electrostatic adhesion of the polyanionic oligonucleotides to the exterior cell membrane, rather than complete functional internalization. Second, for the limited fraction of free CpG-FAM that does enter the highly acidic natural endosomes, its fluorescence is susceptible to partial quenching in the low-pH environment95,96. In contrast, the markedly enhanced intracellular FAM fluorescence and improved colocalization observed with the LNPC formulation (PCC = 0.21) suggest that LNPC not only promotes cellular uptake but also effectively protects the cargo (Fig. 2b). The ionizable lipids within the LNP may help buffer the endosomal pH during the dynamic transit process (the proton-sponge effect), thereby preserving the fluorescence signal. Collectively, these observations indicate that the LNP formulation significantly improves the true endocytic internalization of CpG over mere surface adherence, facilitating its efficient delivery to the endo-lysosomal compartments to engage the TLR9 receptor.

Moving from the cellular to the tissue level, the robust and sustained immune activation observed in the draining lymph nodes (Fig. 2) is mechanistically underpinned by the unique in vivo pharmacokinetic profile of the LNPC system. Historically, “depot-forming platforms” in vaccinology are conceptually restricted to macroscopic implants or viscous emulsions97. However, our comprehensive longitudinal imaging data provide compelling evidence that nanometer-sized LNPCs establish a highly potent, functional immunological depot in vivo. Unlike macroscopic hydrogels, the LNPC system establishes this localized depot through two primary synergistic nano-bio interactions. First, via physicochemical anchoring, the lipid components rapidly interact via electrostatic and hydrophobic forces with the negatively charged extracellular matrix (ECM) and resident cell membranes at the injection site. This restricts the rapid systemic washout that plagues free CpG98. Second, the LNPC establishes a dynamic immunological “cellular sink”. The LNP inherently triggers a transient, localized inflammatory microenvironment, robustly recruiting substantial numbers of professional APCs99. Because LNPs possess the optimal size (~100 nm) for phagocytosis, these infiltrating APCs efficiently internalize the retained LNPCs, transforming the injection site into a localized cellular reservoir that continuously dispatches adjuvant-loaded APCs to the draining lymph nodes over an extended period100.

Crucially, this localized depot effect effectively addresses the challenge of achieving coordinated co-delivery when LNPC is physically admixed with soluble protein antigens. The injection site acts as an inflammatory nidus where non-covalent electrostatic interactions between the LNP surface and the antigen, combined with robust macropinocytosis by infiltrating APCs, ensure that both the TLR9 agonist and the antigen are simultaneously engulfed into the same endosomal compartments. This spatiotemporally synchronized intracellular delivery is a fundamental prerequisite for synergistic TLR9 activation and optimal antigen cross-presentation. Furthermore, the pronounced in vivo adjuvant activity of the blank LNP, despite its inability to stimulate TLR9 in vitro, strongly parallels recent emerging paradigms in nanomedicine34. Standard in vitro monoculture assays cannot fully capture this complex, multi-cellular tissue-level response. Several studies have established that the LNP component itself is intrinsically inflammatory in vivo and acts as a potent adjuvant32. This intrinsic adjuvant property elucidates the superiority of the blank LNP over the rapidly cleared free CpG in vivo. It also underscores why integrating an immunostimulatory adjuvant into an LNP offers a synergistic, two-pronged strategy that surpasses the simple additive effects of mixed adjuvants. Functionally, this synergistic activation endows the LNPC system with a highly adaptive, antigen-dependent immunomodulation capability. In the herpes zoster model, LNPC with VZV-gEgI antigens elicited potent multifunctional CD4+ T-cell responses (Supplementary Fig. 14). Conversely, in the TC-1 (HPV 16E7) and B16-OVA tumor models (Figs. 4, 5), it significantly enhanced CD8+ T-cell-mediated cytotoxic immunity. Consequently, LNPC-adjuvanted vaccines achieved near-complete tumor regression and prolonged survival, outcomes tightly associated with increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration and elevated Th1-type cytokines within the suppressive TME.

In summary, we engineered a highly translatable LNPC adjuvant system that successfully overcomes the in vivo delivery barriers of nucleic acid adjuvants. By integrating the FDA-approved ionizable lipid ALC-0315 with the TLR9 agonist CpG, this unique architecture provides robust nuclease protection and functions as a localized immunological depot upon injection. This enables the spatiotemporally coordinated co-delivery of both the admixed antigen and the TLR9 agonist directly into the same APCs. Our data demonstrate that this synchronized delivery drives a pronounced Th1-biased polarization and elicits potent, multifunctional CD8+ T-cell responses across distinct tumor antigens. Ultimately, the LNPC-adjuvanted vaccines successfully remodeled the immunosuppressive microenvironment in aggressive tumor models, achieving near-complete tumor clearance and prolonged survival while maintaining excellent systemic safety. Nonetheless, several challenges remain for broader clinical translation. These include the need for GMP-compliant standardized manufacturing, comprehensive long-term (≥6 months) immunotoxicology evaluations, and further delineation of its therapeutic value when combined with existing immune checkpoint blockades (e.g., PD-1/CTLA-4 inhibitors)101–103. Looking forward, expanding to multiple administration routes, especially mucosal immunization for treating pulmonary metastases, and developing next-generation, organ-targeted vectors will remain critical objectives104–107. Collectively, this work establishes a robust, highly versatile adjuvant platform that provides a compelling new direction for tumor vaccine development and lays a solid foundation for the advancement of personalized cancer immunotherapy.

DC2.4, TC-1, B16-OVA cell lines used in this study were maintained in our laboratory stock collection, with regular authentication performed by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling. TC-1 tumor cells were derived from the primary lung epithelial cells of C57BL/6 mice and transformed with c-Ha-ras and HPV-16 E6- and E7-encoding genes were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin G sodium, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Pen/Strep). DC2.4 cells (mouse DCs) and B16-OVA (mouse melanoma cells expressing the chicken ovalbumin) were cultured in RPMI-1640 (containing 10% FBS and 1% (v/v) Pen/Strep). BMDCs were isolated from the bone marrow of C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks). Briefly, the bone marrow cells were extracted from the femur and tibia of C57BL/6 mice, and the above cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (containing 10% FBS, 1% (v/v) Pen/Strep, 10 ng/mL IL-4, and 20 ng/mL GM-CSF) for 6–7 days to harvest immature BMDC cells.

LNPC synthesis and characterization

The LNPs containing CpG ODN (LNPC) were prepared by using Microflow S (Nuohai Life Science Co., Ltd, China), which can mediate bottom-up self-assembly for nanoparticle synthesis with microfluidic mixing technology. Briefly, ALC-0315, ALC-0159, DSPC, Cholesterol were, respectively, dissolved in ethanol at a molar ratio of 46.3: 1.6: 9.4: 42.7. CpG ODN was prepared in 50 mM acetate buffer at pH 4.0. The lipid solution in ethanol and CpG ODN solution were, respectively, injected into the microfluidic mixer at a volumetric ratio of 1:3 and combined at final flow rate of 12 mL/min. The mixtures were immediately dialyzed (10-kD MWCO) against PBS to remove the ethanol and unloaded CpG ODN. The size distributions of LNPCs were measured by using dynamic light scattering (Zetasizer Nano-ZS, Malvern Panalytical Ltd., Malvern, UK). The CpG ODN concentration in the LNPCs was measured with PicoGreen reagent (Quant-iT PicoGreen ssDNA Assay Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA). Briefly, LNPC was incubated at 25 °C for 10 min in the presence of 1% Triton X-100 (Wako Pure Chemical Industries) and PicoGreen reagent was added. The fluorescence intensity (excitation/emission wavelength, 485/528 nm) was then measured. All LNP formulations were dialyzed extensively to remove ethanol. Endotoxin levels of all formulations used for in vivo studies were determined using the LAL assay and confirmed to be below 0.1 EU/mL, ensuring strict biocompatibility.

Antigen uptake assay

DC2.4 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at 5 × 105 cells per well. The cells were treated with mixture of free OVA, LNP, CpG, LNPC containing 10 μg fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled OVA in 0.5 ml serum-free Gibico 1640 medium for 6 h. The cells were then collected by centrifugation at 300 × g for 3 min, and the cellular uptake was detected by measuring the fluorescence intensity of FITC-OVA using a BD LSRFORTESSA X-20 (BD Biosciences).

DC activation

BMDC or DC2.4 cells were inoculated with 5 × 105 cells per well in a 24-well plate and stimulated with various formulations for 48 h (or containing 10 μg OVA). The investigated formulations included LNP, CpG and LNPC (or containing 10 μg OVA). Untreated cells were used as negative controls (NC) or free OVA was used as controls. After centrifugation and washing with PBS, the cells were stained with 50 μl PE-anti-CD40, FITC-anti-CD80 and APC-anti-CD86 for 40 min. Finally, the excess staining antibodies were removed by centrifugation and the expression of costimulatory molecules on the cell surface was measured by using the flow cytometry.

Analysis of splenocyte stimulation in vitro

Blank mouse spleens were taken to prepare a single cell suspension, and the cells were added to 24-well plates at a concentration of 2 × 106 cells/well. Subsequently, LNP, CpG and LNPC (diluted 100-fold according to the working concentration) were added respectively and cultured for 48 h (n = 4). 1 × 106 cultured splenocytes were collected, centrifuged and the supernatant was discarded, stained with AF700-anti-CD19 for 30 min, treated with Fixed Permeabilization Kit, and stained with AF647-anti-TLR9 for 30 min. 1 × 106 cultured splenocytes were collected, centrifuged and supernatants discarded, stained for AF700-anti-CD19, FITC-anti-CD4, PE-Cy7-anti-CD8, BV421-anti-CD11c, BV785-anti-CD69 for 30 min. Detection was performed using flow cytometry.

qRT-PCR

Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were used in this study. The mice were randomly divided into the following treatment groups: OVA + LNP, OVA + CpG, OVA + LNPC, OVA alone (control), and untreated negative control (NC). On day 0, mice were intramuscularly immunized with the respective formulations (containing 10 μg OVA). Twenty-four hours post-immunization, the mice were euthanized, and the inguinal lymph nodes were aseptically collected. Total RNA was extracted from the lymph node tissues using the TRIzol method for subsequent analysis. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. One microgram of total RNA was reverse-transcribed using the TransScript® First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix kit (TransGen Biotech). The resulting cDNA was used for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) with EvaGreen® dye (20× in water; Biotium). The primer sequences used in this study are provided in the Supporting Information. All reactions were performed in triplicate using the CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). β-Actin served as the housekeeping gene. Relative expression was calculated via the 2(−ΔΔCt) method, where ΔCt = (Ct of target gene) − (Ct of β-Actin).

In vitro antigen lysosomal escape

To evaluate the lysosomal escape capability of different formulations, DC2.4 cells (3 × 10⁵) were seeded on coverslips in 6-well plates and cultured overnight. The cells were then incubated with various formulations (LNP, CpG, and LNPC, each containing 10 μg FITC-OVA) for 6 h at 37 °C. Following treatment, cellular lysosomes and nuclei were stained with LysoTracker Red (50 min) and Hoechst 33342 (10 min), respectively. The intracellular trafficking and lysosomal escape of the formulations were subsequently visualized using a TCS SP8 X confocal laser scanning microscope.

ELISA for antibody measurement

C57BL/6 mice (6–8 week old) were immunized with different vaccine formulations on days 0 and 14 (and 28) or days 0 and 21. The levels of the OVA (or gEgI or 16E7)-specific IgG, IgG1 and IgG2c (or IgG2b) antibodies in the serum were determined by ELISA. ELISA-related reagents were obtained by Xiamen Innovax Biotech Co., Ltd. High-binding polystyrene 96-well plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with 100 ng/well of antigens and blocked with ELISA blocking solution-3. Serum samples were diluted in ELISA sample dilution buffer and added to wells for 0.5 h at 37 °C. At the same time, four pre-immune serums were included on each plate as a negative control for non-specific binding effects. Plates were washed, and specific antibodies against IgG, IgG1, IgG2b or IgG2c linked to horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Abcam) were diluted in ELISA antibody dilution buffer and added to wells. Plates were incubated for 0.5 h at 37 °C. Plates were washed, and HRP was developed with a substrate. Plates were incubated in the dark for 10 min at 37 °C, and then the reaction was stopped with 2 M H2SO4. Optical densities at a wavelength of 450 nm (OD450) were read by a PHOmo microplate reader (Autobio Labtec Instruments).

ELISpot assay

Spleen or lymph node single-cell suspensions were washed with PBS containing 2% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS). The cells were then seeded at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well and stimulated with pooled gEgI peptides (1.25 μg/mL), OVA peptide (257–264, 10 μg/mL), or 16E7 peptides (1.25 μg/mL) for 20 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator. Following stimulation, IFN-γ and IL-2 ELISPOT assays were performed using commercial kits (Mabtech, Sweden) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, plates were washed five times with PBS and incubated with biotin-conjugated anti-IFN-γ or anti-IL-2 antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Subsequently, HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:1000 dilution) was added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After five additional washes, TMB ELISpot substrate solution was applied to develop spots. The reaction was stopped by rinsing with water, and spots were quantified using an ImmunoSpot® S5 UV analyzer (Cellular Technology Co., Ltd.).

Flow cytometry

Preparation of single-cell suspensions for flow cytometry, Muscle samples were minced and enzymatically digested in collagenase medium (0.1% w/v collagenase type IV + 20 μg/mL DNase I in 2% FBS-PBS) for 90 min at 37 °C. The digested tissue was filtered through a 40-μm mesh, centrifuged (300 × g, 5 min), and resuspended in 2% FBS-PBS. Inguinal lymph nodes were mechanically dissociated in 2% FBS-PBS by gentle grinding between frosted glass slides, followed by filtration through a 40-μm mesh to obtain single-cell suspensions. Spleen tissues were gently pressed through a wire mesh screen into RPMI-1640 supplemented with 2% FBS (Gibco). Erythrocytes were lysed using RBC lysis buffer (Solarbio), and the remaining cells were washed and resuspended in 2% FBS-PBS. Heparinized whole blood (Peripheral blood) was diluted 1:5 in ice-cold PBS, centrifuged (300 × g, 5 min), and treated with 10 volumes of RBC lysis buffer (4 °C, 10 min). The reaction was quenched with 2% FBS-PBS, followed by two washes and resuspension in 2% FBS-PBS for counting.

The expression of phenotypic markers, activation markers, and cytokines was evaluated. Briefly, cells were washed and blocked with anti CD16/CD32 (clone 2.4G2) in 2% FBS-PBS for 30 min on ice to block the FcR, then stained with fluorochrome-labeled mAbs for 30 min on ice. For investigating innate cell activation and proliferation, cells were staining with combinations of anti-mouse antibodies: CD11c, CD11b, F4/80, Ly6-C, Ly6-G, MHC class II. To investigate germinal central responses, cell suspensions were stained with CD3, CD19, CD95, GL7. For Tfh analyses, the following antibodies were used: CD4, CD8, B220, CXCR5, PD-1. For memory T cell analysis, the cell pellet was stained with antibodies of CD4, CD8, CD44, and CD62L. Memory B experiments were contained following antibodies: TCR β, B220, IgD, CD38. For the intracellular cytokine analysis involved stimulating each sample with pooled gEgI peptides (1.25 μg/mL) or OVA peptide (257–264) (10 μg/mL) or 16E7 peptides (1.25 μg/mL) in a U-bottom plate and incubating at 37 °C for 4 h. GolgiPlug (BD Biosciences) was added to the culture at a final concentration of 1:1000 and cells were further incubated for 6 additional hours. After incubation, cells were washed and stained with fluorochrome-labeled mAbs for 30 min on ice. The stained cells were fixed and permeabilized with BD Cytofix/Cytoperm (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, United States) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were washed and intracellularly stained with fluorochrome-labeled mAbs for 45 min on ice.

A complete list of the antibody reagents used in this study can be found in the Supporting Information. Data were analyzed using FlowJo X 10.0.7r2 and GraphPad Prism 8.

Tumor therapy experiments

For the B16-OVA and TC-1 tumor therapy experiments, 6–8 week-old C57BL/6 mice were inoculated subcutaneously (s.c.) with 5 × 105 B16-OVA or TC-1 cells into the right back/flank. When the tumor volume reached approximately 40–50 mm3 (designated as Day 0), the mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 6 or 7 per group). On days 0 and 6, the mice were immunized via intramuscular (i.m.) injection with different adjuvant formulations (containing 10 μg/dose of OVA or 16E7 antigen). Importantly, while the tumors were inoculated subcutaneously, the therapeutic immunizations were consistently administered via the intramuscular route across all different tumor models. Subsequently, tumor growth and mouse body weights were monitored every other day. Tumor volume (V) was calculated using the formula: V = 0.5 × length × width2. The mice were euthanized in strict accordance with animal ethics guidelines and welfare regulations when the tumor volume reached 1500 mm3.

Safety evaluation

For systemic safety evaluation, female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, n = 5) were intramuscularly immunized on days 0, 14, and 28, followed by euthanasia on day 42. Heparin-anticoagulated peripheral blood was collected for serum biochemical analysis (CREA, T-Bil, AST, ALT, ALP, LDH, CK, TC) using a BS-240vet analyzer, while major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) were fixed in 4% neutral-buffered formalin for paraffin embedding, sectioning (3 μm), and H&E staining to assess potential tissue damage. This comprehensive approach enabled simultaneous evaluation of both systemic toxicity through serum biochemistry and organ-specific pathological changes through histomorphological analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad software Prism 8.0. FCM data were analyzed with FlowJo V.10 software. P values were calculated with One-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test and Two-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons tests or Tukey’s multiple comparisons tests. Data are expressed as the (geometric) mean ± SEM (mean and standard error of the mean). P values < 0.05 were considered significant, and ns refers to not significant (unmarked).
