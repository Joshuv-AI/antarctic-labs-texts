An immune signature of postoperative cognitive decline: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Franck Verdonk, Amélie Cambriel, Julien Hedou, Ed Ganio, Grégoire Bellan, Dyani Gaudilliere, Jakob Einhaus, Maximilian Sabayev, Ina A. Stelzer, Dorien Feyaerts, Adam T. Bonham, Kazuo Ando, Benjamin Choisy, David Drover, Boris Heifets, Fabrice Chretien, Nima Aghaeepour, Martin S. Angst, Serge Molliex, Tarek Sharshar, Raphael Gaillard, Brice Gaudilliere
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Postoperative cognitive decline (POCD) is the predominant complication affecting patients over 60 years old following major surgery, yet its prediction and prevention remain challenging. Understanding the biological processes underlying the pathogenesis of POCD is essential for identifying mechanistic biomarkers to advance diagnostics and therapeutics. This study aimed to provide a comprehensive analysis of immune cell trajectories differentiating patients with and without POCD and to derive a predictive score enabling the identification of high-risk patients during the preoperative period.Material and methods:Twenty-six patients aged 60 years old and older undergoing elective major orthopedic surgery were enrolled in a prospective longitudinal study, and the occurrence of POCD was assessed 7 days after surgery. Serial samples collected before surgery, and 1, 7, and 90 days after surgery were analyzed using a combined single-cell mass cytometry and plasma proteomic approach. Unsupervised clustering of the high-dimensional mass cytometry data was employed to characterize time-dependent trajectories of all major innate and adaptive immune cell frequencies and signaling responses. Sparse machine learning coupled with data-driven feature selection was applied to the presurgery immunological dataset to classify patients at risk for POCD.Results:The analysis identified cell-type and signaling-specific immune trajectories differentiating patients with and without POCD. The most prominent trajectory features revealed early exacerbation of JAK/STAT and dampening of inhibitory κB and nuclear factor-κB immune signaling responses in patients with POCD. Further analyses integrating immunological and clinical data collected before surgery identified a preoperative predictive model comprising one plasma protein and 10 immune cell features that classified patients at risk for POCD with excellent accuracy (AUC=0.80, P=2.21e-02 U-test).Conclusion:Immune system-wide monitoring of patients over 60 years old undergoing surgery unveiled a peripheral immune signature of POCD. A predictive model built on immunological data collected before surgery demonstrated greater accuracy in predicting POCD compared to known clinical preoperative risk factors, offering a concise list of biomarker candidates to personalize perioperative management.

Postoperative cognitive decline (POCD) represents a significant challenge for patients aged 60 and older, a population that undergoes over 90 million major surgical procedures worldwide annually1,2. POCD leads to impairments in verbal and visual memory, language comprehension, visuospatial abstraction, attention, and concentration following surgery. It ranks among the most prevalent complications after major surgery, particularly after major orthopedic or cardiac surgery3, affecting 25–55% of patients over 60 years old for up to a year with significant risk for long-term sequelae4–6. POCD correlates with increased mortality, earlier retirement, and heightened reliance on social financial assistance7,8. However, estimating an individual patient’s POCD risk remains challenging as previous attempts relying on clinical factors have yielded limited predictive power9. Accurate biological or clinical biomarkers of POCD are urgently needed to develop a prognostic test to guide personalized management of surgical patients over 60 years old10.

Surgery induces a significant and organized systemic immune response that mobilizes both peripheral innate and adaptive branches of the immune system11–16. Surgery also results in central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, a key pathobiological process that may drive POCD via sustained microglial activation, synaptic dysfunction, inhibition of neurogenesis, and neuronal apoptosis17–20. Several mechanisms underlying bidirectional interplays between systemic immune responses and the CNS have been proposed. These include i) the body–brain axis involving stimulation of vagal neurons by circulating inflammatory cytokines21,22, ii) the humoral pathway including cytokines produced by resident macrophages in the circumventricular organs and the choroid plexus23,24, and iii) the inflammatory infiltration pathway referring to infiltration of peripheral immune cells into the brain parenchyma facilitated by blood–brain barrier dysfunction25,26. However, human studies examining the contribution of the peripheral immune response to POCD outcomes in patients undergoing surgery are lacking. Prior clinical studies in patients undergoing cardiac or orthopedic surgery focusing on biomarkers of neuronal damage27 or systemic inflammation, including C-reactive protein and interleukin (IL)-628, have only revealed weak biological correlates of POCD. Given the dynamic and multicellular nature of the surgical immune response, prior analyses may have overlooked strong signals, as specific immune cell phenotypes or functional responses were not fully examined.

Highly multiplexed single-cell proteomic technologies, such as mass cytometry, provide powerful platforms to characterize complex and multicellular inflammatory processes like the human immune response to surgery14,29–36. Here, we employed an integrated approach combining the functional analysis of circulating immune cell subsets via mass cytometry and multiplex assessment of plasma proteins to quantify dynamic immunological changes in patients over 60 years old before and after major orthopedic surgery. The primary aim was to provide a comprehensive analysis of innate and adaptive immune cell trajectories differentiating patients with and without POCD. A secondary goal was to determine whether integrating single-cell and plasma proteomics with clinical data collected before surgery would yield a robust predictive model identifying patients at risk for POCD.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

This study, reported in accordance with the Strengthening The Reporting Of Cohort Studies in Surgery (STROCSS, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D509) guidelines37, was nested within the larger ‘Preoperative ketamine administration for prevention of postoperative cognitive decline after major orthopedic surgery in elderly patients’ study38, a multicenter, randomized, placebo-controlled, quadruple-blind, and superiority trial. This trial was approved in June 2016 by an institutional review board (Comité de Protection des Personnes Ile de France III, David Simhon, 06/17/2016, France - #2016-000691-16) and the French National Agency of Medicine and Health Products Safety. After approval by the institutional review and obtaining informed consent, patients aged 60 years and older, scheduled to undergo major orthopedic surgery under general anesthesia (e.g. spinal surgery including spinal decompression, hip or knee total joint arthroplasty, or shoulder arthroplasty) in one of the five university hospital centers in the Paris region (Hôpital Européen Georges Pompidou, Hôpital Lariboisière, Hôpital Pitié Salpêtrière, Hôpital Saint Antoine from Assistance Publique – Hôpitaux de Paris and Hôpital Foch) between March 20 2017 and May 28 2019, were enrolled. Exclusion criteria were an American Society of Anesthesiology score >4, emergency surgery (i.e. emergency hip fracture), an expected length of stay in hospital <48 h, known allergy or contraindication to ketamine, severe auditory or vision disorders, drug misuse history or chronic antipsychotic medications, severe alcoholic liver disease, lack of French language fluency, or absence of blood draw. Since there was no impact of ketamine on POCD or any other measured clinical outcome in the clinical trial, patients were included in this study regardless of their clinical trial arm.

Assessment of POCD

The primary clinical outcome of the study was the development of POCD on postoperative day (POD)7. While no consensus has been established regarding the timing of cognitive assessments after surgery, several studies have suggested an optimal timing at 1 week and 3 months postprocedure4,39,40. Here, POD7 was chosen for its alignment with previous studies and its relevance to patient discharge41, aiming to capture early cognitive decline while minimizing loss to follow-up. Cognition was assessed by trained members of the research team, using the MoCA test42 and Trail Making Tests (TMT) A and B, validated tools to measure working memory (TMT A) and central executive functioning (TMT B)43. A positive test for POCD was defined as an absolute difference in Z-score ≥1 for at least one cognitive test (MoCA, TMT A, TMT B, or TMT B-A) between the tests carried out before surgery on the day (D)0 and on POD744, aligning the objective criteria of POCD as described in the DSM-545.

Incidence of anxiety (defined as an anxiety score of 8 or above) and depression (defined as a depression score of 8 or above) were assessed using the Hospital and Anxiety Depression Scale46 at POD7 and POD90; incidence of POCD, assessed by the same cognitive tests (MoCA, TMT A, TMT B, or TMT B-A), 3 months after surgery at the surgical follow-up visit was also collected42,43. Preoperative and postoperative pain was assessed by a patient-reported Visual Analog Scale at rest, from 2 h after the end of surgery to POD7 daily and 3 months after surgery; cumulative opioid requirements were measured from D0 to POD7.

Single-cell and plasma proteomic analysis

Blood collection

Whole blood, plasma samples, and clinical data were collected on D0 before induction of anesthesia and on three additional timepoints after surgery (POD1, POD7, and POD90). Timepoints were chosen to capture acute and long-term immune responses to surgery15. The POD7 and POD90 timepoints coincided with the cognitive evaluations. Whole blood samples were collected in sodium-heparinized tubes. They were then either left unstimulated to measure endogenous intracellular activities or exposed to a combination of the toll-like receptor (TLR)4 agonist lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and the TLR9 agonist CpG to activate canonical surgical trauma-related signaling responses in vitro. Only samples collected before surgery were stimulated. All samples were then fixed with Proteomic Stabilizer in Smart Tubes (Smart Tube Inc.) and immediately stored at −80°C. All samples were sent on dry ice to Stanford University (Stanford).

Barcoding, antibody staining, and mass cytometry processing

After red blood cell lysis, samples were barcoded and stained with both surface and intracellular antibodies following standardized protocols29,47. The 39-marker antibody panel consisted of 25 antibodies targeting cell-surface markers and 14 intracellular antibodies specific to phosphorylated (p) signaling epitopes (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D510). Targeted immune cell types and intracellular signaling pathways were chosen according to previous work on the peripheral immune response to surgery14,29,30 and preclinical evidence of the immune processes at stake in POCD pathobiology48. To minimize variability between measurement, samples from each individual patient were processed simultaneously. The output FCS files were normalized and de-barcoded using MatLab-based software. The FCS files were then uploaded to the Cell Engine (https://cellengine.com, Primity Bio) platform for gating.

Cell frequency, endogenous intracellular signaling, and intracellular signaling responses

From each sample, 488 single-cell proteomic features, including the frequency of 31 major innate and adaptive immune cells defined using manual gates according to pre-established gating strategies29 (Fig. S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D510) and their intracellular signaling activities (e.g. the phosphorylation state of 11 proteins including pSTAT1, pSTAT3, pSTAT5, pSTAT6, pNF-κB, pMAPKAPK2, pP38, prpS6, pERK1/2, pCREB, and total IκBα) were analyzed. Immune cell frequency features were calculated for each immune cell subset from the unstimulated samples. For each cell-type, intracellular activities were expressed as the median signal intensity (arcsinh transformed value). Signaling changes in response to receptor-specific ligands were reported as the arcsinh transformed ratio over the endogenous signaling, that is, the difference in arcsinh transformed signal intensity between the stimulated and unstimulated condition. The proportion of mononuclear cells was expressed as a percentage of the gated live mononuclear cells. The proportion of neutrophils was expressed as a percentage of the gated live leukocytes.

Mass cytometry data preprocessing

Prior to statistical analysis, the initial dataset was filtered with two preprocessing steps. First, a knowledge-based penalization matrix was applied to intracellular signaling response features in the mass cytometry data based on mechanistic immunological knowledge, as previously described. This penalization did not include knowledge related to POCD33,49. Second, features with no variance were removed from the dataset. Finally, features were z-scored prior to any model fitting.

Plasma protein profiling

In parallel, the preoperative plasma concentrations of 997 proteins were quantified using a highly multiplex aptamer-based platform, the SOMAscan manual assay (version 1.4k) for human plasma by SomaLogic, Inc.50. Relative levels of plasma proteins are reported in arbitrary units calculated from data normalized to internal controls and reported after log2 transformation.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using python3, and the libraries NumPy (v1.26.2), pandas (v2.1.4), matplotlib (v3.8.2), seaborn (v0.13.0), SciPy (v1.11.4), and scikit-learn (v1.3.2). The code and the data are available.

Power analysis

A power analysis was performed to determine the required sample size based on the following parameters: an estimated AUROC (θ) of 0.90, indicating a high level of predictivity; a proportion (P) of 0.40, representing the expected prevalence of POCD within our cohort; a CI width (W) of 0.25; and a confidence level (CL) of 90%. The resultant sample size required for our study was determined to be 23 patients51.

Longitudinal analysis of the single-cell proteomic dataset

The single-cell mass cytometry dataset was visualized using a uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) layout. A correlation network using a t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) model was performed for visualization of the single-cell proteomic dataset. The correlation matrix was computed using all collected samples and all available features within the unstimulated whole-blood single-cell panel.

Features were then clustered using k-means52. The number of clusters was chosen to maximize the silhouette score53. Meta features, representing the median of all features within a given cluster, were defined to capture differences in the trajectory of individual clusters between the patients’ groups. Original features and meta-features were analyzed with a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to estimate how the mean of each variable changes according to the levels of the two categorical variables: time-point and POCD status. Despite the small sample size, ANOVA is robust under reasonable assumptions that are met after preprocessing of the data. All features at each timepoint were compared using a Mann–Whitney U-test.

Multivariable predictive modeling

The Stabl algorithm was employed to identify features predictive of POCD and fit a classification model. Stabl is a supervised machine learning framework that allows for sparse, reliable, and predictive feature selection54. Stabl is particularly well-suited for this study, given the relatively small number of observations (n) compared to the number of features (p). It yields a sparse solution that builds the final model from a limited number of selected features. Features were selected from three datasets (single-cell proteomics, plasma proteomics, and clinical data) separately and merged into a unified model. The package for Stabl is available online. To assess our model’s predictive performance, we utilized a Monte Carlo cross-validation approach tailored for the integration of multiomic data modalities, restricting our analysis to a subset of 22 fully characterized subjects with complete single-cell proteomic, plasma proteomic, and clinical data available. Post-hoc confounder analysis was performed using the level of education, age, BMI, surgery duration, and use of ketamine as potential confounders. For each confounder, a logistic regression model was fit, incorporating the confounder and the cross-validated values from the predictive model. F-statistics were computed to test whether each confounding variable accounts for the model’s predictions. We also evaluated the performance of a model based solely on clinical characteristics (Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D510), using data from all patients for whom such information was available. Ultimately, data from 261 patients were included in the analysis to predict outcomes seven days postsurgery. To ensure robust evaluation, the model underwent Monte Carlo cross-validation, a technique that applies multiple five-fold cross-validation runs to minimize random variability. We used logistic regression with L1 regularization (Lasso) to reduce dimensionality and prevent overfitting. During each iteration of the five-fold cross-validation, the Lasso model was fine-tuned to identify the optimal regularization parameter. Statistical significance was assessed using the Mann–Whitney U-test, with a P-value <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Thirty-three patients (aged 72.8±7.1 years) scheduled for major orthopedic surgery were enrolled in the study. Of these, 26, including 20 women (76.9%), underwent cognitive assessment 7 days after surgery and were included in the analysis (see CONSORT flowchart Fig. S1, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D510). Eleven patients (42.3%) had developed POCD at POD7 (Fig. 1). Amongst the patients with POCD, four (36.3%) presented with persistent POCD at POD90, six (54.5%) had recovered, and one did not undergo cognitive evaluation.

Longitudinal assessment of perioperative immune cell distribution and signaling states

The single-cell mass cytometry dataset, including samples collected longitudinally before (D0) and after surgery (POD1, POD7, and POD90), was visualized using a UMAP layout highlighting the dynamic changes in signaling states of distinct immune cell compartments at each of the perioperative timepoints (Fig. 2A). The UMAP analysis highlighted canonical trauma-induced immune cell responses, including the rapid phosphorylation of STAT3 at POD1 in both innate (monocyte and neutrophil) and adaptive (CD4+ and CD8+ T cell) subsets (Fig. 2B) and the biphasic response (de-phosphorylation followed by phosphorylation) of key elements of the myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88) signaling pathway in innate immune cells, including pP38, pERK1/2, pMAPKAPK2, and pNF-κB (Fig. 2B, Fig. S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D510).

To complement the single-cell UMAP visualization, immune cell subsets were identified using an established gating strategy (Fig. S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D510), and a total of 488 immune features were quantified, corresponding to either the frequency or the intracellular signaling activity of 11 proteins (measured as protein phosphorylation) within 31 innate and adaptive immune cell subsets. An unsupervised analysis using k-means clustering (Fig. 3A, Fig. S4, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D510, see methods) identified 10 meta-clusters of highly correlated immune features evolving synchronously over time (Fig. 3B). Meta-clusters 3, 5, 7, and 8 predominantly contained signaling response features that pointed at the JAK/STAT signaling pathways (98, 100, 100, and 68%, respectively). Other meta-clusters (e.g. 0, 2, 6, and 9) were mainly composed of signaling response features within the MyD88 signaling pathways (100, 54, 87, and 80%, respectively, Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D510).

Overall, results from the trajectory analysis highlighted the remarkably orchestrated organization of immune cell responses to surgical injury. Interestingly, while the temporal behavior of immune cell responses was generally conserved across patients, we observed significant interpatient variability in the amplitude of these immune cell trajectories, prompting us to investigate whether this variability reflected patient-specific differences associated with POCD.

Single-cell immune response trajectories differentiate patients with and without POCD

To determine whether postoperative immune cell trajectories differed between patients with and without POCD, we compared the median of all features within a given meta-cluster for each group using a two-way ANOVA with fixed effect analysis of cognition and time relative to surgery. The analysis identified time-dependent differences between the POCD and control groups for meta-clusters 0, 5, 6, and 7 (P-values=7×10−26, 6×10−19, 1×10−8, and 8×10−11, respectively). These meta-clusters also exhibited the strongest sensitivity to temporal progression (Fig. 3B), providing a meta-cluster-level synopsis of immune cell trajectories diverging between patients with and without POCD after surgery. Immune features within meta-clusters differing the most between the two patient groups were examined in further detail.

JAK/STAT signaling trajectories diverge early after surgery in patients who develop POCD

One of the most prominent differences between the two patient groups was the endogenous pSTAT3 signal in adaptive immune cell compartments, including in CD4+ T cell subsets (naïve and memory) and regulatory T cells (meta-cluster 5, Fig. 4A). Specifically, the pSTAT3 signal within these adaptive cell subsets diverged as early as POD1 in patients with POCD and remained higher than in the non-POCD group throughout all postoperative timepoints (P=0.005, 0.008, and 0.012 respectively, ANOVA with fixed effect for group). Fixed effect analysis of the time variable indicated that observed pSTAT3 signal differences in these adaptive immune cell subsets were also time-dependent (P=5×10−16, 2×10−11, and 2×10−10, respectively, ANOVA, fixed effect for time). Additional differences in pSTAT3 were observed in innate immune cells, including neutrophils, classical monocytes (cMCs), nonclassical (nc)MCs, and intermediate MCs (P=0.014, 0.011, 0.038, and 0.047, respectively, ANOVA, fixed effect for group, Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D510). In both innate and adaptive immune compartments, the pSTAT3 signal did not return to baseline at POD90, suggesting prolonged innate and adaptive immune dysfunction associated with POCD after surgery. Together, these results suggest JAK/STAT signaling responses in both innate and adaptive cells are exacerbated as early as POD1 in patients who develop POCD, and that this activated state can extend at least 90 days after surgery.

MyD88 signaling trajectories differentiate patients with and without POCD

In contrast to the time-dependent differences observed for JAK/STAT signaling responses, other immune features, such as those contained in cluster 6, differed at all-time points between the two patient groups, including prior to surgery (D0). Cluster 6 features were predominantly elements of the MyD88 signaling pathways in myeloid immune cell subsets, including cMCs, myeloid dendritic cells (mDCs), and plasmacytoid (p)DCs, as well as in natural killer (NK) cells subsets (CD56brightCD16neg NK cells). Notably, lower IκB signals in cMC, pDC, and mDC cells were observed at all-time points in patients who developed POCD compared to controls (P=0.032, 0.0041, and 0.0042, respectively, ANOVA, fixed effect for group, Fig. 4B and Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D510). Other time-independent differences in patients with POCD compared to controls included lower IκB signal in memory and naive B cells (P=0.039 and 0.043, respectively, ANOVA, fixed effect for group) and lower B cell frequencies (P=0.036 and <0.001, respectively, ANOVA, fixed effect for group).

A predictive model integrating preoperative single-cell and plasma proteomic immune responses accurately identifies patients at risk for POCD before surgery

The analysis of immune cell trajectories distinguishing patients with and without POCD highlighted postoperative immune cell features that may contribute to the pathogenesis of POCD. From a clinical perspective, the ability to differentiate patients’ immune states before surgery, in addition to the early postoperative period, is of paramount importance, as it allows for the implementation of effective, personalized preventive strategies. Remarkably, differences in cell-type-specific signaling pathways, including elements of the MyD88 signaling pathway in myeloid cell subsets, were observed prior to surgery (Fig. 4). Based on these findings, we broadened the immune profiling of patients’ samples collected before surgery with the goal of predicting later development of POCD. The mass cytometry immunoassay was expanded to include preoperative assessment of immune cell signaling activities in response to LPS and CpG, agonists of TLR4 and TLR9, respectively, and activators of the MyD88 signaling pathway. The analysis of preoperative samples also included the concentration of 997 plasma proteins, quantified using the Somalogic Proteomics Assay55, allowing the integration of single-cell immune responses within the larger inflammatory proteomic network.

To identify preoperative features predictive of POCD, the single-cell, and plasma proteomic datasets were integrated along with clinical and demographic data into a unique predictive model using a sparse machine learning approach, Stabl56 (Fig. 5A, Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D510). This analysis was performed using samples from a sub-cohort of 22 patients, including eight with POCD, for which the complete single-cell proteomic, plasma proteomic, and clinical datasets were available. The analysis yielded a model accurately classifying patients with and without POCD (AUC=0.80 [0.54–0.9], P=2×10−2 U-test, Fig. 5B). The multivariable predictive model comprised 11 features: 10 mass cytometry features including eight intracellular signaling responses and two cell frequencies, as well as one proteomic feature (Fig. 5C–F and Table S7, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D510), highlighting preoperative features potentially predisposing patients to POCD. To further ensure our biological findings were not confounded by clinical variables, a confounder analysis showed that a predictive model built on only single-cell and plasma proteomic data remained statistically significant when accounting for major clinical and demographic variables, including age, sex, level of education, and the surgery duration (Table S8, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D510).

Among the most informative features of the predictive model were several elements of the MyD88 signaling pathway measured in myeloid immune cell subsets, including the IκB, pCREB, and prpS6 signals in mDCs, ncMCs, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). Notably, the IκB signal was dampened in DCs from patients who developed POCD (8.65 -log2 fold difference in median expression), particularly in mDCs (2.03 -log2 fold difference in median expression), and in MDSCs (1.37 -log2 fold difference in median expression). Consistent with dampened MyD88 signaling responses in innate immune cells, the pCREB signal in mDCs was also decreased in patients with POCD (0.73 -log2fold difference respectively). Additional model features included prpS6 in ncMCs, pCREB signaling in naïve regulatory T cells in response to TLR4/9 stimulation, basal naive B cell and basophil frequencies, basal pMAPKAPK2 in ncMCs, and pNF-κB in mDCs (Table S9, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D510). In addition, the model included a single plasma proteomic feature, chemokine CXCL1 (0.43 -log2 fold difference in median expression).

Notably, the predictive model did not select any clinical features. For comparison, we also evaluated the performance of a model built solely on preoperative clinical data (Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D510), including known risk factors, such as age4,39, education level4,39, and baseline cognition57. The results showed AUROC values ranging from 0.385 [0.317–0.446], P=7.22×10− 4 to 0.561 [0.490–0.629], P=9.51×10−² for each risk factor individually, and an AUROC of 0.560 [0.488–0.628], P=1.03×10− 1 when combining all preoperative clinical data. These findings indicate limited predictive power of preoperative clinical characteristics compared to a model that includes immunological data in identifying patients at risk for POCD.

Discussion

We conducted an extensive analysis of the immune response to major orthopedic surgery with the aim of identifying immunological factors associated with POCD in patients over 60 years old. Analysis of immune cell composition and intracellular signaling response dynamics after surgery revealed distinct immunological trajectories differentiating patients who developed POCD from controls. Furthermore, a sparse multivariable model integrating clinical, proteomic, and single-cell mass cytometry data collected before surgery accurately identified patients at risk for developing POCD (AUC of 0.80). These results are reminiscent of recent transcriptomic, proteomic, and flow cytometry analyses30–32,58,59 linking immune trajectories with clinical recovery after surgery and emphasize the importance of studying immune responses dynamically, as clinically relevant biological differences could otherwise go undetected60. The results also underscore the importance of integrating biological variables that capture the pathophysiology of cognitive impairments into accurate prognostic assays10 that can be leveraged for the personalized management of patients at risk for POCD.

Two key findings specified immunological dysfunctions in patients who developed POCD: (i) early exacerbation of proinflammatory JAK/STAT signaling responses in both innate and adaptive cells and (ii) dampened MyD88 signaling responses in myeloid cell subtypes that were detectable before surgery and persisted for 90 days postsurgery. These results point at distinct immunological trajectories integral to the pathophysiology of POCD and build upon prior knowledge of immune mechanisms predisposing patients to acute alterations in cognition, such as postoperative delirium36,61,62, or to delayed recovery63–65. For example, increased STAT3 signaling activity (i.e., intracellular pSTAT3 signal) in CD4+ T cells and cMCs at POD1 emerged as particularly informative single-cell features associated with POCD. Dysregulation of the JAK/STAT pathway has previously been implicated in neuroinflammatory disease pathogenesis, triggering and polarizing myeloid and T cells to pathogenic phenotypes, resulting in an excess of inflammatory cytokines and neurodegeneration66. Notably, prolonged elevation of STAT3 has been recognized as a key contributor to neurodegenerative processes like Alzheimer’s disease67. A persistent maladaptive chronic inflammatory state is also seen in frail adults, characterized by high levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-668,69, the canonical upstream activator of STAT3 signaling, evoking ‘inflammaging’70, one of the most conserved biological characteristics of frailty71.

In our cohort, we also observed the dampening of innate immune cell MyD88 signaling responses to ex vivo TLR4/9 stimulation in patients who later developed POCD. This phenomenon affected key elements of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), including MAPKAPK2, rpS6, and CREB, and the NF-κB (IκB) branches of the MyD88 pathways. Interestingly, the dampened MyD88 responses, particularly NF-κB signaling, is a hallmark of immunosenescence70,72, a dynamic and multifactorial process affecting both innate and adaptive immune cells, an important marker of inflammaging. Our findings align with previous studies investigating disparities in immune cell MyD88 signaling between frail patients and age-matched controls73–75 and suggest a potential overlap in MyD88-dependent immunosenescence traits between patients with POCD and frail individuals73. While preclinical studies showed that esketamine76 and fluoxetine77 alleviate POCD in aged mice by dampening the MyD88 pathway, in this clinical trial38 racemic ketamine did not impact the onset of POCD. This resonates with the anergic MyD88 response to surgery in patients with POCD, suggesting an exhaustion of their immune response that is unresponsive to pharmacological modulation. In addition, patients at risk for POCD exhibited elevated plasma CXCL1 concentrations. CXCL1 is a chemokine involved in microglia activation78 that plays an important role in cell death and immunosenescense79, with increased concentrations both causing and resulting from senescence78. Together, the increased CXCL1 expression before surgery and dampened MyD88 innate immune cell responsiveness suggest that patients with POCD are in a state of chronic inflammation and immunosenescence, which may result in a maladaptive response to surgical stress, contributing to the development of POCD.

In addition, a model built on immunological and clinical data collected before surgery predicted POCD with good performance (AUROC=0.80). This model included 11 single-cell and plasma proteomic features but did not include clinical parameters. The results align with the limited predictive performance of POCD predictive models that are based solely on preoperative clinical factors. For example, a recent retrospective study of patients undergoing cardiac procedures reported a preoperative predictive model of long-term (1 year) memory decline with an AUROC of 0.7380. In our study, while age, education level, and baseline cognition are known preoperative risk factors, these clinical variables showed poor predictive performance for POCD (AUC<0.6) whether individually or in combination6,81. Other studies have proposed predictive models for POCD and reported predictive performance ranging from AUROC 0.71-0.829,82,83. However, these studies included preoperative, perioperative, and postoperative data, whereas our model focused exclusively on preoperative assessment.

This study has certain limitations. First, the cohort was assembled from an interventional trial investigating the effect of preoperative ketamine administration on POCD after major orthopedic surgery38. However, in our study, the absence of differences in ketamine administration between the POCD and control groups, as well as the lack of any effect of ketamine on the occurrence of POCD, allowed us to include all patients in this study regardless of their initial study arm. Additionally, the results of the confounder analysis, which accounted for major clinical and demographic variables, minimize the likelihood that other factors confounded our immunological analysis. Second, our cohort included only 26 patients, with a majority being women, therefore limiting its generalizability to a broader population. Future studies in a larger population undergoing a broader variety of surgical interventions will be required to establish the generalizability of our results. Third, our assessment of immune signaling responses before surgery was limited to TLR signaling. In the future, it will be important to expand the panel of ex vivo stimulations. For example, including stimulation of JAK/STAT3 signaling responses, for example, using IL-6, IL-10, and IL-21 family members84, will be informative and may further improve the performance of our predictive model of POCD. Fourth, while mass cytometry allows for simultaneous detection of up to 50 parameters at a single-cell level, the technology necessitates preselection of cell-surface and intracellular features. Similarly, the proteomic platform included a selected panel enriched for inflammatory mediators. Future studies, including untargeted transcriptomic, proteomic, or metabolomic approaches, will help to ascertain whether a more comprehensive profiling of patients’ inflammatory, metabolic, and hormonal responses to traumatic injury can enhance the predictive power of these models.

Conclusion

In summary, the longitudinal mass cytometry assessment of systemic immune responses to surgery identified patient-specific immune trajectories that tracked the development of POCD. The findings pinpoint the roles of proinflammatory JAK/STAT signaling responses in both innate and adaptive cells and dampened MyD88 signaling responses in myeloid subtypes in the human pathophysiology of POCD. These dysfunctions preceded surgery and persisted for 90 days after surgery. In addition, analysis of preoperative immune responses yielded a robust predictive model of POCD comprising a sparse number of immunological features detectable before surgery. The results hold promise for the development of clinically applicable predictive algorithms that can serve to facilitate an individual patient’s risk stratification and allocation of tailored care pathways to prevent POCD.
