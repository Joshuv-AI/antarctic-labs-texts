Abstract

In this work, we study the coupling matrix of
\mathcal{N}=2
Superconformal QCD in vicinity of the special vacuum. We find that although there are
\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\rfloor
independent coupling constants of the theory, only one of them plays a special role.

1
Introduction

In their seminal papers, Seiberg and Witten derived the exact low-energy effective actions for
\mathcal{N}=2

SU(2)
gauge theories, both with and without matter hypermultiplets, in terms of elliptic curves
[
1
,
2
]
. Theories whose matter content renders them conformal are of particular interest due to their ties with other conformal theories, for example, through the AGT duality
[
3
]
. In the conformal case, the theory is independent of any intrinsic scale and is instead characterised by a dimensionless parameter
\tau_{\rm IR}
, which may be interpreted as the infrared gauge coupling.

In this paper, we focus on the theory with
2N
hypermultiplets in the fundamental representation. For massless hypermultiplets, this theory is conformal. In the specific case of an
SU(2)
gauge group, the bare coupling constant is known to transform as a modular form of
\tau_{\rm IR}
under the modular subgroup
\Gamma(2)
.

For the higher rank
SU(N)
gauge symmetry, it was shown that for
N\geq 4
the effective coupling matrix is never proportional to the classical one
[
4
]
. In
[
5
]
the structure of the coupling matrix was studied in details for the so-called ’special vacuum’, where the vacuum expectations values of the Higgs field
a_{u}
are arranged near the vertices of a regular
N
-polygon. Based on the analysis of the perturbative contribution and the action of the
S
-duality group, it was predicted that the coupling matrix can be parametrised by
\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\rfloor
coupling constants. The constants were chosen in such a way that they transform independently under the action of the
S
-duality group, and the bare coupling constant is a modular form of any of them.

However, in our recent work
[
6
]
, we witnessed a natural emergence of a unique constant in the asymptotic regime of the special vacuum, when the radius of the above mentioned polygon tends to infinity. Moreover, this unique constant plays an important role not only in the special vacuum, but, due to its appearance in the recurrence relation, in other vacua of the theory as well. Comparing with
[
7
]
, we showed that in
N=4
case our unique constant coincides with one of the
2
independent couplings in an appropriate basis. Moreover, the analysis of the monodromy group associated with this constant indicates that it is always one of the
\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\rfloor
constants of
[
5
]
.

So how come that all of
\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\rfloor
constants are equally important, but one is more equally important than others? In the present paper we address this question and also find explicitly the coupling matrix and all the coupling constants.

The paper is organised as follows:

•

In Section
2
, we recall what the special vacuum is and its specific symmetry and introduce symmetrical variables convenient for its studying.

•

In Section
3
, we derive the most general form of the coupling matrix basing on the Weyl symmetry and dimensional reasons.

•

In Section
4
, we find all the coupling constants of the theory

•

and we study their modular properties in Section
5
.

•

In Section
6
we show how the high order corrections to the SW curve can be found.

•

Finally, in Section
7
we study how non-zero masses of the multiplets affect the coupling constants.

2
Special vacuum

2.1
Special vacuum and its symmetry

We will be working with the superconformal theory on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
with
\mathcal{N}=2
supersymmetries, the gauge group
SU(N)
and
2N
matter hypermultiplets in the fundamental representation.

We consider the “special vacuum” of the theory and small deviations from it. We start with the massless case, when the special vacuum is simply defined by positioning of the vacuum expectation values of the Higgs field
a_{u}
at the vertices of a regular
N
-polygon.

It will be convenient to use symmetric variables

w_{k}=\sum_{{i_{1}}<{i_{2}}<\ldots<{i_{k+1}}}a_{i_{1}}\ldots a_{i_{k+1}},\qquad k=0,\ldots,N-1.

(2.1)

In
SU(N)
theory we always have
w_{0}=0
.

Let us introduce a polynomial

P_{0}(x)=\prod_{u=1}^{N}(x-a_{u})=x^{N}+x^{N-2}w_{1}-x^{N-3}w_{2}+\ldots-A^{N},

(2.2)

where
A
is a complex constant such that

w_{N-1}=-(-A)^{N}.

(2.3)

Note that
w_{i}=0
for
i=1,\ldots N-2
in the unperturbed special vacuum, and the polynomial has the form

P_{0}(x)=x^{N}-A^{N},

(2.4)

so unperturbed
a_{u}^{(0)}
are indeed placed in the vertices of a polygon

a_{u}^{(0)}=A{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}u}{N}},\qquad u=1,\ldots,N.

(2.5)

One can note that special vacuum respects a residual Weyl symmetry
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
: although there is a preferred way of numeration of
a_{u}
, the first element can be chosen freely. The shift in the numeration of
a_{u}
is equivalent to choosing another root of (
2.3
) to define
A

\ a_{u}\to a_{u+d}\quad\leftrightarrow\quad A\to A\,{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}d}{N}}.

(2.6)

2.2
Perturbations of the special vacuum

Let us now allow small fluctuations
\delta a_{u}
around the special vacuum, which do not violate the condition
\sum_{u}a_{u}=0
. We will assume them to be of the same order, which we symbolically denote as
\delta a_{u}=\mathcal{O}(\delta)
for any
u
.

The symmetry (
2.6
) indicates that the discrete Fourier transform with respect to the index
u
may be useful. We define

v_{l}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{u=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}lu}{N}}\delta a_{u},\ l=1,\ldots,N-1.

(2.7)

Note that we can allow
l=0
, but then
v_{0}
is always zero.
Then

\delta a_{u}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}{\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}lu}{N}}v_{l}.

(2.8)

To see how the Fourier coefficients
v_{l}
are related to the symmetrical variables
w_{k}
, we introduce

w_{l}^{[u]}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{1}<u_{2}<\ldots<u_{l+1}\\
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{l+1}\neq u\end{subarray}}a_{u_{1}}\cdots a_{u_{l+1}}.

(2.9)

Then we can write

\delta w_{l}=\sum_{u=1}^{N}w_{l-1}^{[u]}\delta a_{u}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})

(2.10)

It is easy to see that

w_{l}^{[u]}=w_{l}-a_{u}w^{[u]}_{l-1}.

(2.11)

This rule is, in fact, correct also for
l=0
if we define
w_{-1}=1
.
Continuing this recurrently, and taking into account that for all
l=0,\ldots,N-2
in the spacial vacuum
w_{l}
vanish, we get

\delta w_{l}=(-1)^{l}\sum_{u=1}^{N}A^{l}{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}lu}{N}}\delta a_{u}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}),

(2.12)

or

\delta w_{l}=\sqrt{N}v_{l}(-A)^{l}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

(2.13)

We note that
w_{i}=\delta w_{i}=\mathcal{O}(\delta)
for
i=1,\ldots,N-2
.

We shall need also the second order correction for
\delta w_{1}
. Clearly, it is given by

\delta w_{1}^{(2)}=\sum_{u<v}\delta a_{u}\delta a_{v}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u,v}\delta a_{u}\delta a_{v}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u}\delta a_{u}^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}v_{l}v_{N-l},

(2.14)

so

w_{1}=-A\sqrt{N}v_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}v_{l}v_{N-l}.

(2.15)

This is exact, because
w_{1}
is a quadratic polynomial in
a_{u}
, so no higher order corrections may appear.

We also introduce the the dual variables

v^{D}_{l}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial v_{l}},

(2.16)

where the partial derivative is taken with
v_{j}
,
j\neq l
being fixed. We have

v^{D}_{l}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{u=1}^{N}(\hat{\partial}_{a_{u}}\mathcal{F}){\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}lu}{N}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{u=1}^{N}\hat{a}_{u}^{D}{\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}lu}{N}},

(2.17)

where
\hat{\partial}_{a_{u}}
denotes a formal operation of differentiation with respect to
a_{u}
with
a_{v}
being fixed for
v=1,\ldots,N
,
v\neq u
.

Strictly speaking, the operator
\hat{\partial}_{a_{u}}
can not act on the function
\mathcal{F}
, defined on the hypersurface
a_{1}+\ldots+a_{N}=0
only. As a consequence, the dual variables
\hat{a}_{u}^{D}
can not be rigorously defined by
\hat{a}_{u}^{D}=\hat{\partial}_{a_{u}}\mathcal{F}
. One can formally define
\hat{a}_{u}^{D}
in the Seiberg-Witten theory as an integral of the Seiberg-Witten differential over a certain contour, but this integral diverges
[
8
]
. Fortunately, the divergent part does not depend on
u
, and thus does not contribute to
v_{l}^{D}
for
l=1,\ldots,N-1
.
For convenience we can always renormalise
\hat{a}_{u}^{D}
as in
[
8
]
, by setting

v_{0}^{D}\triangleq\sum_{u=1}^{N}\hat{a}_{u}^{D}=0.

(2.18)

With such a convention, we can inverse the Fourier transform in (
2.17
) and get

\hat{a}_{u}^{D}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}v_{l}^{D}{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}lu}{N}}.

(2.19)

In this notation, the widely used in the literature variables can be written as

a_{u}^{D}=\frac{\partial{\mathcal{F}}}{\partial a_{u}}=\hat{a}_{u}^{D}-\hat{a}_{N}^{D}.

(2.20)

Here the derivative is taken with respect to
a_{u}
with
a_{v}
being fixed for
v=1,\ldots,N-1
and
v\neq u
, and keeping
\sum_{u=1}^{N}a_{u}=0
. Then

v^{D}_{l}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{u=1}^{N-1}a_{u}^{D}{\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}lu}{N}}.

(2.21)

So, the dual variables
v_{l}^{D}
can be understood as the Fourier coefficients of either
a_{u}^{D}
or
\hat{a}_{u}^{D}
.

It is also convenient to write
v_{l}^{D}
in terms of the differences
\hat{a}^{D}_{u-1}-\hat{a}^{D}_{u}
, which are clearly well-defined, with
\hat{a}^{D}_{0}=\hat{a}^{D}_{N}
. From (
2.17
) it is easy to see that

v^{D}_{l}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\frac{1}{(1-{\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}l}{N}})}\sum_{u=1}^{N}(\hat{a}_{u}^{D}-\hat{a}_{u-1}^{D}){\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}lu}{N}}

(2.22)

Note that
v_{l}
and their duals transforms under the residual Weyl symmetry (
2.6
) as

v_{l}\to{\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}ld}{N}}v_{l},\ v^{D}_{l}\to{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}ld}{N}}v_{l}^{D}.

(2.23)

From (
2.13
), we see that
\delta w_{l}
are invariants of this symmetry, as they should be.

3
Coupling matrix

In this Section we analyse the most general form of the coupling matrix basing on how the prepotential can depend on the variables
v_{l}
taking into account the dimensional and symmetric constrains.

We assume that the special vacuum itself with
A\neq 0
is a regular point of the prepotential. As a consequence, we expect that the prepotential in the vicinity of the special vacuum can be approximated by a sum of non-negative powers of
v_{l}
. We are interested in terms up to the order
\delta^{2}
.

First of all, the prepotential should have dimension two (assuming that
a_{u}
are of dimension one). The variables
v_{l}
are of dimension one, so up to
\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})
we can have terms of the form
A^{2}
,
v_{l}A
and
v_{l}v_{l^{\prime}}
.

Secondly, the prepotential should be invariant with respect to the residual Weyl symmetry given by (
2.6
), (
2.23
), so in the linear case we may have only
l=1
, while in the quadratic term
l+l^{\prime}=N
. Besides, we see immediately that for
N>2
the term
A^{2}
is not possible. We now will consider
N>2
, and see later that the term
A^{2}
possible for
N=2
does not bring anything new. Then the most general expansion of the prepotential is

\mathcal{F}={2\pi{\rm i}}\left(-{\tau}_{\mathrm{IR}}\sqrt{N}v_{1}A-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}v_{l}v_{N-l}\tau_{\mathrm{IR}}^{l}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{3})

(3.1)

with
\tau^{l}_{\mathrm{IR}}=\tau^{N-l}_{\mathrm{IR}}
.

In (
3.1
) we put

{\tau}_{\mathrm{IR}}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}A}\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial v_{1}}=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial w_{1}}

(3.2)

which is consistent with what we got for the unique coupling constant appearing in the asymptotic of the partition function in
[
6
]
.

Alternatively, we can interpret this coefficient as a background value of the dual variable
v_{1}^{D}
,

v_{l}^{D(0)}=\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial v_{l}}\right)_{v=0}=-2\pi{\rm i}A{\tau}_{\mathrm{IR}}\delta_{l,1}.

(3.3)

Then by (
2.19
),

\hat{a}_{u}^{D(0)}=-2\pi\sqrt{N}{\rm i}A{\tau}_{\mathrm{IR}}{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}u}{N}}=-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{\sqrt{N}}{\tau}_{\mathrm{IR}}a_{u}.

(3.4)

Let us see how the other coefficients are related to the coupling matrix

\tau_{uv}^{\mathrm{IR}}=\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial a_{u}\partial a_{v}}.

(3.5)

By (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) we get

\tau_{\mathrm{IR}}^{l}=-\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial v_{l}\partial v_{N-l}}=-\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}N}\sum_{u,v}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial a_{u}\partial a_{v}}{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}(u-v)l}{N}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{u,v}\tau_{uv}^{\mathrm{IR}}{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}(u-v)l}{N}}.

(3.6)

Furthermore, since

v_{N-1}=-(-1)^{N}\frac{\delta w_{N-1}}{\sqrt{N}A^{N-1}}+O(\delta^{2})=\sqrt{N}\delta A+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}),

(3.7)

and the prepotential
\mathcal{F}
can not depend individually on
A
or
\delta A
, but only on their sum, we conclude that
\tau_{\mathrm{IR}}=\tau_{\mathrm{IR}}^{1}
. For the same reason, the term with
A^{2}
possible for
N=2
theory cannot bring any new coupling constant in the picture.

For further convenience, let us write the prepotential in terms of the variables
w_{k}
. We use (
2.13
) for
k>1
and (
2.15
) for
w_{1}
, because the latter enters linearly.
We have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=2\pi{\rm i}\left(\tau_{\rm IR}w_{1}-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{l=2}^{N-2}\frac{w_{l}w_{N-l}}{w_{N-1}}(\tau_{\rm IR}-\tau_{\rm IR}^{l})\right)=\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{2N}\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}\frac{w_{l}w_{N-l}}{w_{N-1}}\hat{\tau}_{\rm IR}^{l},

(3.8)

where

\hat{\tau}_{\rm IR}^{l}=\tau_{\rm IR}^{l}-\tau_{\rm IR}(1-2N\delta_{l,0}).

(3.9)

We now see that
\tau_{\mathrm{IR}}=\tau_{\mathrm{IR}}^{1}
is special for two reasons. Firstly, only this term brings non-trivial background value for the dual variables (
3.4
). Secondly, from (
3.8
) is clear that this is always the only contribution that survives in the limit of large
w_{N-1}
studied in
[
6
]
.

From (
3.8
) we see that the classical part of the prepotential
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{class}}=w_{1}\ln(q_{0})

q_{0}
to be consistent with

\tau^{l}_{\mathrm{class}}=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\ln(q_{0}),\ l=1,\ldots,N-1

(3.10)

Let us now compute the full coupling matrix. It is convenient to start from (
3.1
) where the linear and quadratic parts are separated. We have

\displaystyle\tau_{uv}^{\mathrm{IR}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial a_{u}\partial a_{v}}=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}(\hat{\partial}_{a_{u}}-\hat{\partial}_{a_{N}})(\hat{\partial}_{a_{v}}-\hat{\partial}_{a_{N}})\mathcal{F}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}\tau_{l}^{\mathrm{IR}}\left({\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}ul}{N}}-1\right)\left({\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}vl}{N}}-1\right)

4
Coupling constants

Let us now find explicitly all the
\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\rfloor
coupling constants appearing in the coupling matrix (
3.11
).

Classical contribution.

As we have already discussed, the classical contribution is given by

2\pi{\rm i}\tau^{l}_{\mathrm{class}}=\ln(q_{0}),\qquad l=1,\ldots,N-1.

(4.1)

One-loop contribution.

The one-loop contribution to the coupling matrix are well-known and can be found for example in
[
4
]
or
[
5
]
. However, to get the right imaginary part we have to chose the branch of the logarithm properly. Namely, we have to chose it in such a way that the residual Weyl symmetry is preserved. For this we find it easier to start from the explicitly Weyl-symmetric one-loop prepotential presented in
[
9
]
(with corrected misprints)

\mathcal{F}_{\rm 1loop}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v=1\\
u\neq v\end{subarray}}^{N}(a_{u}-a_{v})^{2}\ln\frac{a_{u}-a_{v}}{\Lambda}-N\sum_{u=1}^{N}a_{u}^{2}\ln\frac{a_{u}}{\Lambda}.

(4.2)

Then for the second derivatives of the prepotential we get

\hat{\partial}_{u}\hat{\partial}_{v}\mathcal{F}_{\rm 1loop}=\delta_{u,v}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq u\end{subarray}}^{N}\ln\left(-\frac{(a_{u}-a_{k})^{2}}{a_{u}^{2}}\right)-\ln a_{u}^{2}\right)+(1-\delta_{u,v})(-\ln(-(a_{u}-a_{v})^{2}))+\rm{const},

(4.3)

where the last term denoted as
\rm{const}
does not depend on
a_{u}
and will not play any role.

With (
2.8
) for the deviation of the prepotential we get

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{F}_{\rm 1loop}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u,v=1}^{N}\delta a_{u}\delta a_{v}\hat{\partial}_{u}\hat{\partial}_{v}\mathcal{F}_{\rm 1loop}=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{m,l=1}^{N-1}v_{l}v_{m}\sum_{u,v=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}ul}{N}}{\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}vm}{N}}\cdot

(4.4)

\displaystyle\left(\delta_{u,v}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq u\end{subarray}}^{N}\ln\left(-(1-{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}(k-u)}{N}})^{2}\right)-\ln{\rm e}^{\frac{4\pi{\rm i}u}{N}}\right)+(1-\delta_{u,v})(-\ln(-({\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}u}{N}}-{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}v}{N}})^{2}))+{\rm const}\right).

Due to the fact that the Fourier transform of a constant is zero the last term disappears. Besides,

\sum_{u,v=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}ul}{N}}{\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}vm}{N}}\left(\delta_{u,v}\frac{4\pi{\rm i}u}{N}+(1-\delta_{u,v})\frac{2\pi{\rm i}(u+v)}{N}\right)=\sum_{u,v=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}ul}{N}}{\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}vm}{N}}\frac{2\pi{\rm i}(u+v)}{N}=0.

(4.5)

So the variation of the propetential turns into

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{F}_{\rm 1loop}=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{m,l=1}^{N-1}v_{l}v_{m}\cdot

(4.6)

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{u}^{N}{\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}u(l+m)}{N}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\ln\left(4\sin^{2}\frac{\pi k}{N}\right)+\pi{\rm i}(N-1)\right)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v=1\\
u\neq v\end{subarray}}^{N}{\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}(ul+mv)}{N}}\ln\left(4\sin^{2}\frac{\pi(u-v)}{N}\right)\Big)

Now it is easy to see that the sums over
u
,
v
vanish unless
l+m=N
.
Moreover,

\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\ln\left(4\sin^{2}\frac{\pi k}{N}\right)=2\ln N,

(4.7)

and we arrive to

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{F}_{\rm 1loop}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=1}^{N-1}v_{m}v_{N-m}\Big(2\ln 2N+\pi{\rm i}(N-1)-\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}{\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}km}{N}}\ln(\sin^{2}\frac{\pi k}{N})\Big)

(4.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=1}^{N-1}v_{m}v_{N-m}\Bigg(2\ln 2N+\pi{\rm i}(N-1)-\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi km}{N}\right)\ln\left(\sin^{2}\frac{\pi k}{N}\right)\Bigg).

(4.9)

Comparing this with (
3.1
)
and taking into account already found classical contribution (
4.1
) we get the perturbative contribution to the coupling constants as

2\pi{\rm i}\tau^{l}_{\mathrm{pert}}=\ln(q_{0})-2\ln 2N-\pi{\rm i}(N-1)+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi kl}{N}\right)\ln\left(\sin^{2}\frac{\pi k}{N}\right).

(4.10)

Rewriting it in terms of
q=(-1)^{N}q_{0}
and choosing the branch of the logarithm as

\ln(q)=\ln(q_{0})-\pi{\rm i}N

(4.11)

we arrive to

2\pi{\rm i}\tau^{l}_{\mathrm{pert}}=\ln(q)-2\ln 2N+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi kl}{N}\right)\ln\left(\sin^{2}\frac{\pi k}{N}\right)+\pi{\rm i}.

(4.12)

We note that by the Gauss’s digamma theorem for general
N

2\pi{\rm i}\tau^{l}_{\mathrm{pert}}=\ln(q)+2\psi\left(\frac{l}{N}\right)+2\gamma_{E}+\pi\cot\left(\frac{\pi l}{N}\right)

(4.13)

depends on the ratio
m/N
only.

Instanton contribution.

To describe the instanton contribution we will look for the effective number of instantons defined as

\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{\rm inst}(q)}{\partial\ln q}=\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}k_{\rm eff}(q).

(4.14)

With
k_{\rm eff}
and the boundary condition
\mathcal{F}^{\rm inst}|_{q=0}=0
we will be able to reconstruct the prepotential.

Since we are working with a non-equivariant problem, it is convenient to use the Seiberg-Witten curves
[
10
]
. The equation for the curve can be derived from the Nekrasov partition function
[
8
]
. For an auxiliary function
\omega(y)
, following notation of
[
6
]
, in the massless case we can write an equation

\omega\left(y\right)+\frac{1}{\omega(y)}=\kappa\frac{P_{N}(y)}{y^{N}},

(4.15)

where

\kappa=\sqrt{q}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}},

(4.16)

P_{N}(y)=y^{N}-p_{0}y^{N-1}+p_{1}y^{N-2}+\ldots+p_{N-1}.

(4.17)

The periods of the function
\omega(y)
are known to be

\oint_{\mathcal{A}_{u}}y\frac{{\rm d}\omega(y)}{\omega(y)}=-2\pi{\rm i}\frac{a_{u}}{A},

(4.18)

where the contour
\mathcal{A}_{u}
encircles the cut
\mathcal{C}_{u}
of the function
\omega(y)
such that the singular point
a_{u}
belongs to the cut.

The effective number of instantons appears in the asymptotic behaviour of
\omega(y)
at

(see
[
6
]
for details).

\displaystyle\omega(y)=\sqrt{q}\frac{1}{1+\frac{w_{1}}{A^{2}y^{2}}}\left(1-\frac{\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{1}}{A^{2}y^{2}}k_{\rm eff}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{y^{3}}\right)=\sqrt{q}\left(1-\frac{w_{1}+k_{\rm eff}\,\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}}{A^{2}y^{2}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{y^{3}}\right).

(4.19)

Comparing (
4.15
) and (
4.19
) we conclude, that the first two coefficients of the polynomial

are given by

p_{0}=0,

(4.20)

p_{1}=\frac{(w_{1}+k_{\rm eff}\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2})(1-q)}{A^{2}(1+q)}.

(4.21)

Equations (
4.15
), (
4.18
) and (

) are enough to find

, and hence the prepotential and the coupling matrix.

In order to perform the integration in (
4.18
) we follow the same way as in
[
6
]
. First, we change the variables: we now consider

as a new variable. Then
y
becomes a function of

defined by the equation

{\omega}(y(z))=z.

(4.22)

The multivalued function

in the region of interest has
N
branches, which we denoted by

(

), and all the contours
\mathcal{A}_{u}
can be defined as images of the same contour
\mathcal{Z}
under the functions

, so instead of (
4.18
) we get

\oint_{\mathcal{Z}}y_{u}(z)\frac{{\rm d}z}{z}=-2\pi{\rm i}\frac{a_{u}}{A},

(4.23)

where the contour
\mathcal{Z}
is to be identified. We note that, due to (
2.5
) and (
2.8
), in the variables
v_{l}
the equation above takes the form

\oint_{\mathcal{Z}}y_{u}(z)\frac{{\rm d}z}{z}=-2\pi{\rm i}\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}e^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}lu}{N}}\left(\delta_{l,N-1}+\frac{v_{l}}{A\sqrt{N}}\right).

(4.24)

Note that function
\omega(y)
does not respect the residual Weyl symmetry (
2.6
). Instead, it is invariant under simultaneous transformations of

and

\begin{cases}A\to A{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}d}{N}},\\
y\to y{\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}d}{N}}.\end{cases}

(4.25)

Since the left hand side of (
4.15
) is invariant under (
4.25
), the right hand side should be too. While the terms

and constant

of the polynomial

are automatically invariant under (

where

is an integer number. But parameter

is dimensional, while

is dimensionless, hence the dimensions of

should be compensated by only dimensional parameters left, which are

. We conclude, that

for

are at least of the order

, and therefore, in the leading order, equation (

) leads to the equation for the curve

where

There are

. We see now that the contour

Let us define a function

It can be expressed via the hypergeometric function

From the leading order of (

) we found

Let us now consider the first subleading order of (

We substitute (

).
Comparing the Fourier harmonics, and using (

) we find that

\displaystyle p_{k}^{(0)}=U^{k}\frac{w_{k}}{A^{k+1}}\frac{(-1)}{\mathcal{I}_{\frac{k}{N}-1}(\kappa)}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})

(4.35)

In particular,

p_{1}^{(0)}=\frac{w_{1}}{A^{2}}\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{1}{N}}(\kappa)\mathcal{I}_{\frac{1}{N}-1}(\kappa)}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

From (

As we see, it coincides with the effective number of instantons found in
[

]
in

limit. From this followed that the only full (with the perturbative contribution) coupling constant visible in

limit satisfied the equation

Together with the boundary condition

2\pi{\rm i}\tau_{\mathrm{IR}}^{1}=2\pi{\rm i}\tau^{1}_{\mathrm{pert}}+\mathcal{O}(q),\quad q\to 0

it gave us the solution

2\pi{\rm i}\tau_{\mathrm{IR}}^{1}=-\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)^{2}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{2}{N}\right)}\frac{{{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{N},\frac{1}{N},\frac{2}{N},1-q\right)}}{{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{N},\frac{1}{N},1,q\right)}+\pi\left(\cot\left(\frac{\pi}{N}\right)+{\rm i}\right).

The second subleading order of (

\displaystyle-\rho(z)(N(y^{(0)})^{N-1}y^{(2)}+\frac{N(N-1)}{2}(y^{(0)})^{N-2}(y^{(1)})^{2})

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}(-1)^{k}p_{k}^{(0)}(y^{(0)})^{N-2-k}(N-1-k)y^{(1)}+\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}(-1)^{k}p_{k}^{(1)}(y^{(0)})^{N-1-k}=0,

so

\displaystyle y^{(2)}=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}(-1)^{k}p_{k}^{(0)}(y^{(0)})^{-1-k}(N-1-k)y^{(1)}+\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}(-1)^{k}p_{k}^{(1)}(y^{(0)})^{-k}}{\rho(z)N}

\displaystyle-\frac{(N-1)}{2}\frac{(y^{(1)})^{2}}{y^{(0)}}.

(4.42)

Again, we substitute (
4.42
) in the left hand side of (
4.23
), while the right hand side was already canceled by the first order. Comparing the Fourier harmonics in the left and right hand sides of (

\displaystyle p_{k}^{(1)}=\frac{(-1)^{N}}{NA^{N+2}}\sum_{k=2}^{N-2}w_{k}w_{N-k}\left(\frac{N-1}{2}-k\right)\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}_{\frac{k}{N}-1}\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{k}{N}}}-\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}_{\frac{1}{N}-1}\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{1}{N}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{NA^{2}}\sum_{k=2}^{N-2}\frac{w_{k}w_{N-k}}{w_{N-1}}\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}_{\frac{k}{N}-1}\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{k}{N}}}-\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}_{\frac{1}{N}-1}\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{1}{N}}}\right)

(4.43)

We conclude that

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}k_{\rm eff}=\left(-1+\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{1}{N}}(\kappa)\mathcal{I}_{\frac{1}{N}-1}(\kappa)}\frac{1+q}{1-q}\right)w_{1}-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{k=2}^{N-2}\frac{w_{k}w_{N-k}}{w_{N-1}}\frac{1+q}{1-q}\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}_{\frac{1}{N}-1}\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{1}{N}}}-\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}_{\frac{k}{N}-1}\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{k}{N}}}\right)

(4.44)

Comparing it with (

) we see that the coupling constantans satisfy the differential equations

2\pi{\rm i}q\frac{\partial\tau^{k}_{\mathrm{IR}}}{\partial q}=\frac{1+q}{1-q}\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{k}{N}}(\kappa)\mathcal{I}_{\frac{k}{N}-1}(\kappa)}

(4.45)

and the boundary conditions

(4.46)

Naturally, the solutions are given by

2\pi{\rm i}\tau^{k}_{\mathrm{IR}}=-\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{k}{N}\right)^{2}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{2k}{N}\right)}\frac{{{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{k}{N},\frac{k}{N},\frac{2k}{N},1-q\right)}}{{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{k}{N},\frac{k}{N},1,q\right)}+\pi\left(\cot\left(\frac{\pi k}{N}\right)+{\rm i}\right).

This result up to notation agrees with the

]
and the

]

for the general case, but simpler.

Remark.

5
T
- and
S
-transformations

Let us now discuss the modular properties of the coupling constants.

Recall that in
[
5
]
the decomposition of the coupling matrix was designed so that each coupling constant transforms independently under the
S
-duality group. Our first goal is to show that (
3.11
) satisfies this property for all
N
.

The first question we should answer is how the
S
group action can be presented in terms of the symmetric variables.

The
S
and
T
duality transformations can be understood as linear maps acting on
2N
-dimensional vectors

\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}\\
\vdots\\
a_{N}\\
\hat{a}_{1}^{D}\\
\vdots\\
\hat{a}_{N}^{D}\end{pmatrix}

(5.1)

and preserving the symplectic form

\Omega=\sum_{u=1}^{N}a_{u}\wedge\hat{a}_{u}^{D}.

(5.2)

To rewrite the latter condition in terms of the symmetric variables we define

\Omega^{\prime}:=\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}v_{l}\wedge v_{N-l}^{D}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{u,v=1}^{N}a_{v}\wedge a_{u}^{D}\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}l(u-v)}{N}}.

(5.3)

Now note that

\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}l(u-v)}{N}}=\sum_{l=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}l(u-v)}{N}}-1=N\delta_{u,v}-1.

(5.4)

Taking into account the condition
\sum_{v=1}^{N}a_{v}=0
, we conclude that
\Omega^{\prime}=\Omega
.
Therefore, the
S
and
T
dualities should be given by linear maps, acting on
2N-2
dimensional vectors

\begin{pmatrix}\delta{v}_{N-1}^{D}\\
v_{1}\\
\delta{v}_{N-2}^{D}\\
v_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\delta{v}_{1}^{D}\\
v_{N-1}\\
\end{pmatrix}

(5.5)

and preserving the symplectic form
\Omega^{\prime}
, which, up to irrelevant constant factors, in this basis is presented by the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{y}&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&\sigma_{y}&0&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0&0\\
0&0&\ldots&\ldots&\sigma_{y}&0\\
0&0&\ldots&\ldots&0&\sigma_{y},\end{pmatrix}

(5.6)

where
\sigma_{y}
is the Pauli matrix acting on the two-dimensional spaces of the vectors

\begin{pmatrix}\delta v_{N-l}^{D}\\
v_{l}\end{pmatrix},

(5.7)

l=1,\ldots,N-1
. Here we took into account that, in general, the dual variables can have non-zero values, which have to be treated separately from the variations
\delta v_{l}^{D}
. We have seen that in the special vacuum this is the case for
l=1
.

It is natural to expect that
S
- and
T
-dualities commute with the residual Weyl symmetry. Since by (
2.23
) both components of a vector

\begin{pmatrix}\delta v_{N-l}^{D}\\
v_{l}\end{pmatrix},

(5.8)

transform under the residual Weyl symmetry with the same eigenvalue, we conclude that
S
- and
T
-dualities can act only by block diagonal matrices of the form

\begin{pmatrix}\gamma^{(1)}&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&\gamma^{(2)}&0&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0&0\\
0&0&\ldots&\ldots&\gamma^{(N-2)}&0\\
0&0&\ldots&\ldots&0&\gamma^{(N-1)}\end{pmatrix}.

(5.9)

Here for
l=1,\ldots,N-1
, the two-by-two matrix
\gamma^{(l)}
should preserve the standard symplectic form
\sigma_{y}
, which is equivalent to
\gamma^{(l)}\in SL(2)
.

We now see that the main advantage of introducing the coefficients
\tau^{l}_{\mathrm{IR}}
is, actually, the drastic simplification of the
S
group action it brings.

From the block diagonal form (
5.9
), inherent to any duality respecting residual Weyl symmetry, follows that for each
l
the variables
v_{l}
and
\delta v_{N-l}^{D}
transform via themselves by a matrix
\gamma^{(l)}\in SL(2)
. But this means that
\gamma^{(l)}
acts on the coupling constant

\tau^{l}_{\mathrm{IR}}=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}}{\partial v_{l}\partial v_{N-l}}=\frac{\partial v_{N-l}^{D}}{\partial v_{l}}

(5.10)

by the corresponding fractional linear transformation.
In other words, the constants
\tau^{\mathrm{IR}}_{l}
are exactly the
\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\rfloor
constants on which the
S
group acts diagonally, existence of which was predicted in
[
5
]
.

Let us now find the form of the matrices
\gamma^{(l)}
using the
S
and
T

[

,
11
]
.
The

6
Higher order corrections

In the previous sections, we found the function
y(z)
up to the second order of

(

The main purpose of this computation is to automatise the computations of the mass corrections in the next Section. However, it can used for other purposes, such as, for example, finding the derivatives of the coupling constants with respect to

We consider an equation of the form

(6.2)

For

h_{k}=p_{k-1}(-1)^{k},\ k=1,\ldots,N-1,

(6.3)

and

for all other

, we get the equation
\omega(y(z))=z
with
\omega
defined by (

) and with

given by (

). However, it is more convenient to define

by setting

We now allow all the coefficients

This equation can be solved iteratively. It is easy to see that its solution has the form

and can be written as

B^{n}_{r,N}(k)=B_{r,N}^{n,k}=\frac{r}{N^{n}}\prod_{t=1}^{n-1}(r+tN-k)=\frac{r}{N}\left(\frac{r-k}{N}+1\right)_{n-1}(-1)^{n}.

(6.8)

For

\displaystyle(x)_{-1}=\frac{1}{x-1},

(6.9)

so

, so we do not need to consider other valued of

6.4
).
This can be proven by induction in

6.7
-
6.8
) into (

In (

is not defined iteratively, so we do not need to keep arbitrary

We can now write the periods (

k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}\equiv l\ \mod N\end{subarray}}B_{N}^{n,k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}}\frac{h_{k_{1}}\cdots h_{k_{n}}}{n!}\mathcal{I}_{\frac{k_{1}+\ldots+k_{n}-1}{N}-n}(\kappa)U^{1-k_{1}-\ldots-k_{n}}.

(6.11)

Putting

Taking into account the connection between

and

7.1
Perturbative part of the prepotential

The classical contribution to the prepotential does not change with added masses, however, the one-loop part does.

\displaystyle\delta_{u,v}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\

\displaystyle(1-\delta_{u,v})\left(-\ln\left(-\frac{(a_{u}-a_{v})^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}\right)\right)+\rm{const}.
