Abstract
. This paper analyses the space of steady rotating solutions to the two-dimensional incompressible Euler equations nearby vortex patch solutions satisfying a natural nondegeneracy condition. We address the question of desingularization and prove that such vortex patch states are the limit of rotating Euler solutions that are smooth to infinite order, have compact vorticity support, and respect dihedral symmetry. Our nondegeneracy condition is proved to be satisfied by Kirchhoff ellipses and along the local bifurcation curves emanating from the Rankine vortex. The construction, that is based on a local stream function formulation in a tubular neighborhood of the patch boundary, is a synthesis of delicate analysis on thin domains, nonlinear a priori estimates, and a custom version of Newton’s method. Our techniques are robust enough to additionally allow us to construct exotic families of singular rotating vortex patch-like solutions nearby a given nondegenerate state. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this work constitutes the first desingularization procedure applicable to general families of steady rotating vortex patches.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Euler equations in the plane and in the disk, steady rotating solutions, and elliptical vortices

In this paper we study inviscid incompressible fluids whose motion are described by solutions to the two-dimensional Euler system of equations. Two fluid geometries are within the scope of our analysis: the planar case of a fluid defined on all of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the disk case of a fluid confined to
\overline{B(0,R)}
for some
R>0
. To consolidate notation, we shall make the convention that
\overline{B(0,R)}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\partial B(0,R)=\varnothing
when
R=\infty
.

In Eulerian variables, the system of Euler equations relates the velocity vector
u:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\overline{B(0,R)}\to\mathbb{R}
and the scalar pressure
p:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\overline{B(0,R)}\to\mathbb{R}
via the bulk equations

(1.1.1)

\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla p=0\text{ and }\nabla\cdot u=0

and, when
R<\infty
, the no-penetration boundary condition

(1.1.2)

u(t,x)\cdot x/|x|=0\text{ for all }x\in\partial B(0,R),\;t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}.

As is well-known (see, e.g.,
[
49
,
6
,
56
,
50
,
4
]
), the above equations (
1.1.1
) and (
1.1.2
) can be equivalently formulated in terms of the vorticity
\widetilde{\omega}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\overline{B(0,R)}\to\mathbb{R}
and the stream function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\overline{B(0,R)}\to\mathbb{R}
via the bulk equations

(1.1.3)

\partial_{t}\widetilde{\omega}+\nabla^{\perp}\psi\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\omega}=0\text{ and }\Delta\psi=\widetilde{\omega},

where
\nabla^{\perp}=(-\partial_{2},\partial_{1})
denotes the counterclockwise rotated gradient, and the boundary condition

(1.1.4)

\nabla^{\perp}\psi(t,x)\cdot x/|x|=0\text{ for all }x\in\partial B(0,R),\;t\in\mathbb{R}.

One recovers the velocity and pressure of (
1.1.1
) from the vorticity and stream function of (
1.1.3
) and (
1.1.4
) via the relationships

(1.1.5)

u=\nabla^{\perp}\psi\text{ and }-\Delta p=\nabla\cdot\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u).

We are interested in the class of
steady rotating
solutions to equations (
1.1.3
) and (
1.1.4
); these are time-independent when viewed in a coordinate system rotating at a constant angular velocity. Let us fix an arbitrary angular velocity
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}
and make the ansatz that there exists
\omega,\Psi:\overline{B(0,R)}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
x\in\overline{B(0,R)}
we have

(1.1.6)

\widetilde{\omega}(t,x)=\omega(\mathbf{R}(\Omega t)x)\text{ and }\psi(t,x)=\Psi(\mathbf{R}(\Omega t)x)+\Omega|\mathbf{R}(\Omega t)x|^{2}/2

where for
s\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
we have defined the rotation matrix

(1.1.7)

\mathbf{R}(s)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(s)&-\sin(s)\\
\sin(s)&\cos(s)\end{pmatrix}.

The corresponding bulk equations for the

\omega
and
\Psi

(1.1.8)

\nabla^{\perp}\Psi\cdot\nabla\omega=0\text{ and }\Delta\Psi=\omega-2\Omega.

When
R<\infty
, we additionally have the boundary condition

(1.1.9)

\nabla^{\perp}\Psi(x)\cdot x/|x|=0\text{ for all }x\in\partial B(0,R).

On the other hand, when
R=\infty
, we supplement the second equation of (
1.1.8
) with the condition
\nabla(\Psi+\Omega|\cdot|^{2}/2)(x)\to 0
as

While the equations (

1.1.9

1.1.1

Definition 1.1

An important and well-studied class of solutions to the two-dimensional steady rotating system Euler equations are the
steady rotating vortex patches
, which are also known as
V-states
, see, e.g.,
[

,

and references therein. These are weak solutions to the steady rotating Euler equations in the sense of Definition

in which the vorticity is singular, being the discontinuous characteristic function of an open, bounded, and simply connected set with boundary belonging to some regularity class. Among this class of steady rotating vortex patches, there is an

subfamily known as the Kirchhoff elliptical vortices (see Kirchhoff

or Article 159 of Chapter VII in Lamb
[

). Modulo the symmetries of the Euler equations, these form a one parameter family, that we enumerate in a particular way. For
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
we define

(1.1.16)

E^{\xi}=\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\;:\;x_{1}^{2}+(x_{2}/\tanh(\xi))^{2}<1}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\text{ and }\Omega^{\xi}=\tanh(\xi)/(1+\tanh(\xi))^{2}.

The

) weak solution (in the sense of Definition

) to (
1.1.8
) with vorticity

and, for some constant
c^{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}
, relative stream function
\Psi=\Psi^{\xi}
satisfying

(1.1.17)

\Psi^{\xi}(x)=(\mathbf{N}\ast\mathds{1}_{E^{\xi}})(x)-\Omega^{\xi}\char 12\relax{x}^{2}/2-c^{\xi}\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\text{ and }\Psi^{\xi}(\partial E^{\xi})=\{{0}\}

with

(1.1.18)

\mathbf{N}(x)=(1/2\pi)\log\char 12\relax{x}\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{{0}\}

the Newtonian potential. In the extreme case
\xi=\infty
we have that
E^{\infty}=B(0,1)
is simply the open unit disk and
\Omega^{\infty}=1/2
. Due to radial symmetry,
\omega=\mathds{1}_{B(0,1)}
is the vorticity of a weak solution to (
1.1.8
) not just for
\Omega=1/2
, but for all
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}
. This family of circular vortex solutions are known as the Rankine vortices
[
55
]
.

A motivation for this work’s forthcoming analysis is the following natural question: Are the class of singular solutions given by the steady rotating vortex patches able to be realized as limits of classical
C^{\infty}
-smooth solutions having the same qualitative properties, such as steady rotation and compact vorticity support, while respecting discrete symmetries? A positive answer to the previous desingularization question would show that these singular solutions are further from being pathological and, in a sense, are more physically meaningful.

In this paper we provide a partial positive answer to the preceding query. We formulate a notion of nondegeneracy for steady rotating vortex patches that is satisfied when a particular related linear operator (having the form of a compact perturbation of the identity) has a trivial kernel. When this condition is satisfied by a patch, we prove, among several other things, that the patch can be desingularized in the sense that it is the limit of smooth solutions to equations (
1.1.8
) and (
1.1.9
) that respect certain desirable qualitative properties.

It is clearly important to verify that there exists steady rotating patches that satisfy our nondegeneracy condition. We provide two uncountable families of examples. The first are the Kirchhoff elliptical vortices; more precisely, we show that there exists a countable set
\mathcal{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\setminus\mathcal{N}
the
\xi
-Kirchhoff vortex of equations (
1.1.16
) and (
1.1.17
) is nondegenerate and hence can be desingularized. The second family of examples are given by the
m
-fold symmetric V-states of Burbea
[
8
]
that locally bifurcate from the Rankine vortex (these are discussed in detail in Section
2.2
). The authors conjecture – but make no attempt to prove in this work – that our nondegeneracy condition, that is precisely stated in the fourth item of Definition
1.2
below, is a generic property of steady rotating vortex patches.

1.2.
Survey of prior work

The incompressible Euler equations (
1.1.1
) are among the first PDEs to be written down and have been at the focal point of intense research for nearly a quarter of a millennium. As such, we make no attempt to give a comprehensive literature review; rather we focus just on the results for the two-dimensional equations that are most closely related to our own.

The local and global well posedness of the initial value problem for system (
1.1.1
) in the class of classical solutions is due to Lichtenstein
[
47
]
, Hölder
[
41
]
, and Wolibner
[

[

[

]

[
3

[

]
, Serfati
[

, and Elgindi, Murray, and Said
[

]
.

Among the most well-known explicit solutions to (
1.1.1
) are the Kirchhoff elliptical vortices. Regimes of spectral stability and instability for the Kirchhoff elliptical vortices were first established by Love
[
48
]
. Promotion to nonlinear stability came with the work of Tang
[
60
]
and Wan
[

The distinguishing feature of two-dimensional Euler flow governed by equation (

The steady rotating vortex patch solutions discussed in Section

are evidently a special type of vortex patch solution whose dynamics are described simply by constant rotation. The only known explicit and nonradial rotating vortex patches are the Kirchhoff ellipses. There has long been an industry, starting with the numerical work of Deem and Zabusky

[

[

The literature related to the specific task of steady vortex patch desingularization for the Euler equations is much sparser, consisting of, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, two specific results. Castro, Córdoba, and Gómez-Serrano

Finally let us remark that our results belong to the general structural study of the space of stationary or rigidly evolving solutions to the two-dimensional system of Euler equations. We list a few representative results of this subfield: Choffrut and Šverák

1.3.
Main results and discussion

As we alluded to in Section
1.1
, our main theorems apply for a class of
admissible
steady rotating vortex patches obeying a certain nondegeneracy condition. We now make these precise in the next definition. Recall that
\mathbf{N}
from (
1.1.18
) denotes the Newtonian potential.

Definition 1.2

(Admissible steady rotating vortex patch solutions)
.

We say that
\Psi\in C^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
\Omega,c\in\mathbb{R}
,
\varnothing\neq\widetilde{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
form an admissible tuple if the following are satisfied.

(1)

The set
\widetilde{U}
is open, bounded, simply connected, and has
m
-fold dihedral symmetry in the sense that

(1.3.1)

x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\widetilde{U}\text{ if and only if }(x_{1},-x_{2})\in\widetilde{U}\text{ if and only if }\mathbf{R}(2\pi/m)x\in\widetilde{U},

where
\mathbf{R}
is defined in (
1.1.7
). The boundary
\Sigma=\partial\widetilde{U}
is class
C^{1,1}
.

(2)

For all
x\in\Sigma
we have
\Psi(x)=0
and if
\nu:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
denotes the outward pointing unit normal, then
\min_{\Sigma}(\nu\cdot\nabla\Psi)>0
.

(3)

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
it holds that

(1.3.2)

\Psi(x)=(\mathbf{N}\ast\mathds{1}_{\widetilde{U}})(x)-\Omega|x|^{2}/2-c.

(4)

If
\phi\in C^{0}(\Sigma)
has
m
-fold dihedral symmetry in the sense that

(1.3.3)

\phi(x)=\phi(x_{1},-x_{2})=\phi(\mathbf{R}(2\pi/m)x)

for all

, and satisfies for all
x\in\Sigma
the identity

(1.3.4)

\phi(x)+\int_{\Sigma}\mathbf{N}(x-y)\frac{\phi(y)}{\nu(y)\cdot\nabla\Psi(y)}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(y)=0,

then
\phi=0
. Here
\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}
denotes integration with respect to the one-dimensional Hausdorff measure.

The first three items of Definition
1.2
guarantee in particular that the triple
\Omega
,
\Psi
, and
\omega=\mathds{1}_{\widetilde{U}}
are a steady rotating weak solution in the sense of Definition
1.1

The fourth item is the nondegeneracy condition. Roughly speaking, this condition is saying that the linearization at the particular patch solution of (
1.3.2
) has no kernel. As we shall prove in Theorem
IV

is far from being empty.

Our next result, also addressing admissible states, shows that any neighborhood of the rotating vortex patch contains a whole zoo of singular solutions that look like linear combinations of nested patches. Thus one can think of the given nondegenerate patch solution as able to be split into sums of multiple patches in many different ways.

The remainder of this subsection is devoted to a brief and high-level discussion of the proof of our main results. Let
\Psi
,
\Omega
,
c
,
\widetilde{U}
, and
m
be an admissible steady rotating vortex patch solution in the sense of Definition
1.2
. The starting point is the equation (
1.3.2
) that relates the stream function
\Psi
to the interior vorticity support
\widetilde{U}
. For the purposes of this discussion, let us suppose that
\Psi<0
in
\widetilde{U}
. This condition is not built into Definition
1.2
, but is satisfied thanks to a maximum principal argument, if we know additionally that
\Omega<1/2
. Let us also pretend that
\Psi>0
in all of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\widetilde{U}}
– this is not really true, but it is convenient for the ensuing imprecise discussion. These assumptions allow us to imagine that

(1.3.18)

\mathds{1}_{\widetilde{U}}=\mathds{1}_{(0,\infty)}(-\Psi).

Then, we obtain a closed equation in terms of the stream function with a Heaviside
vorticity function
; we write this as an abstract operator equation
\mathsf{F}[\Psi]=0
with

(1.3.19)

(\mathsf{F}[{\Psi}])(x)=\Psi(x)-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\log|x-y|\mathds{1}_{(0,\infty)}(-\Psi(y))\;\mathrm{d}y+\Omega|x|^{2}/2+c=0\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

The goal then is to construct nearby Euler solutions
\widetilde{\Psi}
that solve a perturbed operator equation of the form
\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{v}}[\widetilde{\Psi}]=0
with

(1.3.20)

(\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{v}}[\widetilde{\Psi}])(x)=\widetilde{\Psi}(x)-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\log\char 12\relax{x-y}\mathsf{v}(-\widetilde{\Psi}(y))\;\mathrm{d}y+\Omega|x|^{2}/2+c\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

where
\mathsf{v}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a vorticity function that is in an appropriate sense close to the Heaviside function
\mathds{1}_{(0,\infty)}
. For example, in Theorem
I
we take (with
\varepsilon>0
small)

(1.3.21)

\mathsf{v}(t)=\gamma(t/\varepsilon)\text{ with }\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),\;\gamma(t)=0\text{ for }t\leqslant-1\text{ and }\gamma(t)=1\text{ for }t\geqslant 1;

on the other hand, for Theorem
II
, we take (with
\rho>0
small)

(1.3.22)

\mathsf{v}(t)=\sum_{k=-M}^{M}\sigma_{k}\mathds{1}_{(0,\infty)}(t-k\rho)\text{ with }\sum_{k=-M}^{M}\sigma_{k}=1.

Note that taking
\sigma_{0}=1
and
\sigma_{k}=0
for
k\neq 0
in (
1.3.22
) recovers the Heaviside vorticity function.

One might hope to address these perturbed equations (
1.3.20
) through some sort of implicit function theorem (IFT) or Banach fixed point argument. Indeed, it is this hope that is the inspiration for the instantiation of our nondegeneracy condition given in the fourth item of Definition
1.2
. By formally taking the Fréchet derivative of the expression for
\mathsf{F}
given in equation (
1.3.19
) at the admissible stream function
\Psi
in an arbitrary direction
\phi
and restricting the result to
\Sigma
, we get exactly the left hand side of (
1.3.4
). Thus we see precisely that the nondegeneracy condition is equivalent to the invertibility of this formal functional derivative.

However, substantial technical obstructions arise that preclude a direct invocation of the IFT or contraction mapping principle. More specifically, the operator
\mathsf{F}
(
1.3.19
) appears not to be
C^{1}
in the Fréchet sense or even locally Lipschitz for any choice of suitable function spaces. The reason for this failure is precisely the discontinuous vorticity function
\mathsf{v}=\mathds{1}_{(0,\infty)}
.

As a first step in solving the perturbed equations
\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{v}}[\widetilde{\Psi}]=0
, we can replace the Heaviside vorticity function of (
1.3.19
) with a nearby one that is qualitatively smooth. Executing this swap restores Fréchet smoothness and a local Lipschitz condition, at the expense of introducing a source term. We define the following regularized versions of (
1.3.22
):

(1.3.23)

\mathsf{v}_{\varepsilon,\rho}(t)=\sum_{k=-M}^{M}\sigma_{k}\gamma\Big(\frac{t-k\rho}{\varepsilon}\Big).

The singular case of the vorticity function is ultimately recovered by taking the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
in the above – of course, this requires carefully tracking various quantities.

We let
X=C^{1}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be an ambient Banach space and set up the operator

(1.3.24)

\mathsf{G}_{\varepsilon,\rho}:B_{X}(0,r)\to X\text{ with action }\mathsf{G}_{\varepsilon,\rho}[\phi]=\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{v}_{\varepsilon,\rho}}[\Psi+\phi]-\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{v}_{\varepsilon,\rho}}[\Psi]\text{ for }\phi\in B_{X}(0,r).

We endeavor to solve, at least for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
and
\rho
, the equation, that is equivalent to
\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{v}}[\widetilde{\Psi}]=0
,

(1.3.25)

\mathsf{G}_{\varepsilon,\rho}[\psi]=\mathsf{g}_{\varepsilon,\rho},\text{ for the source term }\mathsf{g}_{\varepsilon,\rho}=\mathsf{F}[\Psi]-\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{v}_{\varepsilon,\rho}}[\Psi]\in X

with
\psi=\widetilde{\Psi}-\Psi
denoting the relative stream function perturbation. Note crucially that in the formulation above, the operator
\mathsf{G}_{\varepsilon,\rho}
is smooth; however, the norms of its derivatives are blowing up in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
.

The following two facts are sufficient to guarantee the existence of solutions to the perturbed operator equation (
1.3.25
).

(1)

We have the limit:
\lim_{\varepsilon,\rho\to 0}\lVert\mathsf{g}_{\varepsilon,\rho}\rVert_{X}=0
. This essentially follows from the coarea formula.

(2)

Uniform local surjectivity. More precisely: there exists
r_{1},\varepsilon_{1},\rho_{1}>0
such that for all
0<\varepsilon\leqslant\varepsilon_{1}
and
0\leqslant\rho<\rho_{1}
we have
B_{X}(0,r_{1})\subset\mathsf{G}_{\varepsilon,\rho}[{B_{X}(0,r)}]
.

The proof of the latter fact is a delicate matter due to the development of singularities in the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
and its competition with the requirement that the radius
r_{1}>0
be taken
independent
of small
\varepsilon,\rho>0
. To wit: the nondegeneracy condition of Definition
1.2
does ensure that
D\mathsf{G}_{\varepsilon,\rho}[0]:X\to X
is invertible and so the IFT indeed gives local invertibility of
\mathsf{G}_{\varepsilon,\rho}
near zero; the problem, however, is that the operator norms satisfy
\min\{{\lVert DG_{\varepsilon,\rho}[0]\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(X)},\lVert DG_{\varepsilon,\rho}[0]^{-1}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(X)}}\}\to\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Thus, the domains granted by the IFT are shrinking to nothing and we do not yet satisfy the second fact above.

Our construction of the local inverse that does satisfy the uniform local surjectivity condition instead proceeds via a version of Newton’s method capable of handling this singular limit. We formulate this tool abstractly in Theorem
3.13
below, but the main hypotheses that guarantee a local inverse are:

(1)

A nonlinear a priori estimate. There exists
c_{1},c_{2}
with
c_{2}<r
(
r
being the domain radius from (
1.3.24
)) such that (uniformly for small
\varepsilon
and
\rho
)
\lVert\mathsf{G}_{\varepsilon,\rho}[\phi]\rVert_{X}\leqslant c_{1}
implies that
\lVert\phi\rVert_{X}\leqslant c_{2}
.

(2)

Qualitative invertibility of the derivative in a uniform neighborhood. There exists
c_{2}<c_{3}\leqslant r
(again uniform for small
\varepsilon
and
\rho
) such that for all
\lVert\phi\rVert_{X}\leqslant c_{3}
we have that
D\mathsf{G}_{\varepsilon,\rho}[\phi]:X\to X
is an invertible map.

The key feature is that the second item allows for the norm of the derivative to become unboundedly large in the singular limit of small parameters. What is important is that the size of the neighborhood in which the derivative’s inverse exists is not collapsing. The role of the nonlinear a priori estimate is to ensure that the iterates in Newton’s method do not leave a bounded region, thus facilitating the convergence. It is a pleasant fact that the two hypotheses of our uniform local surjectivity theorem when applied to the maps
\mathsf{G}_{\varepsilon,\rho}
(
1.3.24
) are satisfied as consequences of the nondegeneracy condition (
1.3.4
) from Definition
1.2
.

After we have constructed the uniform local inverses of the maps
\mathsf{G}_{\varepsilon,\rho}
, we, roughly speaking, obtain Theorem
I
by setting
\rho=0
. On the other hand, Theorem
II
is obtained by taking the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
for each fixed
\rho>0
. Theorem
III
is ultimately proved by a strategy similar to the one described above, but we introduce another type of perturbation to the base operator equation (
1.3.19
) by varying the Green’s function.

2.1.

Lemma 2.1

(Stream functions for Kirchhoff vortices)

For each
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
there exists

such that the function
\Psi^{\xi}\in C^{1,1}_{(2)}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

(1)

(2)

If

denotes
\Psi^{\xi}

\widetilde{\Psi}^{\xi}(\zeta,\eta)=\frac{\tanh(\xi)}{4}\Big(2(\zeta-\xi)-\frac{\cosh(2\zeta)-\cosh(2\xi)}{e^{2\xi}}\Big)+\frac{\tanh(\xi)}{4}\Big(\frac{1}{e^{2\zeta}}-\frac{1}{e^{2\xi}}\Big)\cos(2\eta).

(3)

For all

(2.1.4)

\partial_{1}\widetilde{\Psi}^{\xi}(\xi,\eta)=\frac{\tanh(\xi)}{2e^{2\xi}}(\cosh(2\xi)-\cos(2\eta)).

\min_{\partial E^{\xi}}\char 12\relax{\nabla\Psi^{\xi}}>0

Proof.

First, let

(2.1.5)

for all
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
.

Next, by repeating the computations in Article 159 of Chapter VII of Lamb
[

we obtain a particular

that satisfies for

\widetilde{\Psi}_{\mathrm{ell}}^{\xi}(\zeta,\eta)=\frac{\tanh(\xi)}{4}\Big(2\zeta+\frac{\cos(2\eta)}{e^{2\zeta}}\Big).

Now we define
\widetilde{\Psi}^{\xi}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
via

(2.1.7)

\widetilde{\Psi}^{\xi}(\zeta,\eta)=(\widetilde{\Psi}^{\xi}_{\mathrm{rot}}+\widetilde{\Psi}^{\xi}_{\mathrm{ell}})(\zeta,\eta)-(\widetilde{\Psi}^{\xi}_{\mathrm{rot}}+\widetilde{\Psi}^{\xi}_{\mathrm{ell}})(\xi,\eta).

After rote computation, it is clear that (

) on the set

,

Proof.

\int_{\Sigma^{\xi}}\mathbf{N}(x-y)\frac{\phi(y)}{\char 12\relax{\nabla\Psi^{\xi}(y)}}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(y)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{U\cap\{{|\Psi^{\xi}|<\varepsilon}\}}\mathbf{N}(x-y)\upphi(y)\;\mathrm{d}y

Now for

\int_{U\cap\{{|\Psi^{\xi}|<\varepsilon}\}}\mathbf{N}(x-y)\upphi(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\\

Notice that crucially the dependence on
\eta

Before substituting (

\char 12\relax{\mathbf{e}(\xi,\widetilde{\eta})-\mathbf{e}(\xi,\eta)}^{2}=4\sech^{2}(\xi)\sin^{2}((\eta-\widetilde{\eta})/2)(\sinh^{2}(\xi)+\sin^{2}((\eta+\widetilde{\eta})/2))

\int_{\Sigma^{\xi}}\mathbf{N}(x-y)\frac{\phi(y)}{\char 12\relax{\nabla\Psi^{\xi}(y)}}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(y)=\frac{2e^{2\xi}}{\sinh(2\xi)}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Big(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(2\sech(\xi))+K_{1}(\eta-\widetilde{\eta})+K_{2}(\eta+\widetilde{\eta})\Big)\rho(\eta)\;\mathrm{d}\eta.

The second stage of the proof is to analyze the action in Fourier space of the following integral operators:

(2.1.24)

defined for

and
\widetilde{\eta}\in 2\pi\mathbb{T}
. The following identities for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
are consequences of Lemmas 3.3 and 3.4 in Castro, Córdoba, and Gómez-Serrano
[

(2.1.25)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}K_{1}(\eta)e^{-\mathsf{i}k\eta}\;\mathrm{d}\eta=-\frac{1}{2}\begin{cases}\log 4&\text{if }k=0,\\
|k|^{-1}&\text{if }k\neq 0,\end{cases}\text{ and }\int_{0}^{2\pi}K_{2}(\eta)e^{-\mathsf{i}k\eta}\;\mathrm{d}\eta=-\frac{1}{2}\begin{cases}\log 4-2\xi&\text{if }k=0,\\
e^{-2|k|\xi}|k|^{-1}&\text{if }k\neq 0.\end{cases}

Therefore, for any

and

we can compute

(2.1.26)

\mathscr{F}[O_{1}\uprho](k)=-\frac{\mathscr{F}[\uprho](k)}{2}\begin{cases}\log 4&\text{if }k=0,\\
|k|^{-1}&\text{if }k\neq 0,\end{cases}\text{ and }\mathscr{F}[O_{2}\uprho](k)=-\frac{\overline{\mathscr{F}[\uprho](k)}}{2}\begin{cases}\log 4-2\xi&\text{if }k=0,\\

We now have all of the tools we need to prove the stated equivalence. Identity (

if and only if for

Then equality (

\phi
is

-symmetric, we deduce

for all

and

for all

∎

2.2.
Perturbations of the Rankine vortex

We now turn our focus to the verification of the admissibility of the steady rotating patches with discrete rotation symmetry, locally bifurcating from the Rankine vortex. We start by recalling the set-up of their construction, following Burbea
[
8
]
, Hmidi and Mateu
[
40
]
, and Hassainia, Masmoudi, and Wheeler
[
36
]
.

Let
D
be a bounded, simply connected domain corresponding to a rotating vortex patch solution, and denote by
\mathbb{D}=B(0,1)
the open unit-disk with boundary
\mathbb{S}^{1}=\partial\mathbb{D}
. Assuming that the boundary
\partial D
is of
C^{k,\alpha}
regularity (
k\in\mathbb{N}^{+},\alpha\in(0,1)
), we infer that a conformal mapping
\Phi:\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{D}
extends to a
C^{k,\alpha}
mapping
\Phi:\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}\setminus D
, such that
\Phi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\partial D
is a
C^{k,\alpha}
parametrization of the boundary of the patch (see, e.g., Theorems 3.5 and 3.6 in Pommerenke
[
53
]
). Interpreting the gradient of the relative stream function as a complex scalar under the usual identification
\nabla\Psi=\partial_{1}\Psi+\mathsf{i}\partial_{2}\Psi
, equation (
1.1.10
) on the boundary
\partial D
of the patch reads

(2.2.1)

-\overline{\nabla\Psi}(\Phi(w))=\Omega\overline{\Phi(w)}+\frac{1}{4\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{\overline{\Phi(\tau)}-\overline{\Phi(w)}}{\Phi(\tau)-\Phi(w)}\Phi^{\prime}(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau,

for all
w\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. On the other hand, since
\Psi(\Phi(w))=0
for all
w\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
and
\mathsf{i}w\Phi^{\prime}(w)
is the tangent vector at
\Phi(w)
, we have that

(2.2.2)

\mathrm{Re}\{{\overline{\nabla\Psi}(\Phi(w))\mathsf{i}w\Phi^{\prime}(w)}\}=0,

which when combined with (
2.2.1
) implies

(2.2.3)

\mathrm{Im}\Big\{{\Big(\Omega\overline{\Phi(w)}+\frac{1}{4\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{\overline{\Phi(\tau)}-\overline{\Phi(w)}}{\Phi(\tau)-\Phi(w)}\Phi^{\prime}(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau\Big)w\Phi^{\prime}(w)}\Big\}=0.

This is a version of the Burbea map, first introduced in
[
8
]
. Following Hassainia, Masmoudi, and Wheeler
[
36
]
, we condense the notation in (
2.2.3
) in terms of the Cauchy integral operator

(2.2.4)

\mathcal{C}(\Phi):f\mapsto\frac{1}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{f(\tau)-f(w)}{\Phi(\tau)-\Phi(w)}\Phi^{\prime}(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau.

Equation (
2.2.3
), then, becomes

(2.2.5)

\mathrm{Im}\{{(\Omega\overline{\Phi}+\mathcal{C}(\Phi)\overline{\Phi}/2)w\Phi^{\prime}}\}=0.

It is not difficult to see that, for all
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}
,
\Phi(z)=z
solves the equation above on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

In the following definition, we instantiate the Burbea map on perturbations around the Rankine vortex that satisfy the reflection symmetry
\overline{\phi(w)}=\phi(\overline{w})
.

Definition 2.5

(Burbea map around the Rankine vortex)
.

Let
k\geqslant 1
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Consider the Banach spaces

(2.2.6)

X^{k,\alpha}=\Big\{{\phi\in C^{k,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{C})\;:\;\phi(w)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\overline{w}^{n};a_{n}\in\mathbb{R}}\Big\}

and

(2.2.7)

Y^{k-1,\alpha}=\{{f\in C^{k-1,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R})\;:\;f(\overline{w})=-f(w)}\},

and the open subset
\varnothing\neq U^{k,\alpha}\subset X^{k,\alpha}
defined by

(2.2.8)

U^{k,\alpha}=\Big\{{\phi\in X^{k,\alpha}\;:\;\inf_{\tau\neq w}\Big|1+\frac{\phi(\tau)-\phi(w)}{\tau-w}\Big|>0}\Big\}.

We define the Burbea map around the Rankine vortex
\mathcal{F}:U^{k,\alpha}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow Y^{k-1,\alpha}
by

(2.2.9)

\mathcal{F}(\phi,\Omega)=\mathrm{Im}\{{(\Omega(\overline{w}+\overline{\phi})+\mathcal{C}(w+\phi)(\overline{w}+\overline{\phi})/2)w(1+\phi^{\prime})}\}.

In the previous definition, the space
X^{k,\alpha}
does not encode any discrete rotation symmetry for the boundary of the patch. We remark, however, that the spaces can be modified in a way that keeps track of this information.

Remark 2.6
.

The Burbea map considered in Definition
2.5
is well-defined and analytic thanks to the analyticity of the Cauchy integral operator on
w+U^{k,\alpha}
(see
[
46
]
; also Lemma 2.6 of
[
36
]
for a very similar setting to the one we consider here). Moreover, given
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+},
the spaces
X^{k,\alpha}
and
Y^{k-1,\alpha}
, and the open set
U^{k,\alpha}
can be replaced by

(2.2.10)

X_{m}^{k,\alpha}=\Big\{{\phi\in X^{k,\alpha}\;:\;\phi(w)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\overline{w}^{nm-1}}\Big\},\;Y^{k-1,\alpha}_{m}=\{{f\in Y^{k-1,\alpha}\;:\;f(e^{2\pi\mathsf{i}/m}w)=f(w)}\}

and
U^{k,\alpha}_{m}=U^{k,\alpha}\cap X^{k,\alpha}_{m}
, respectively (see Section 2.2 in
[
36
]
or Section 3.7 of
[
40
]
).

We now state the main existence result for nontrivial steady rotating vortex patches with dihedral symmetry. Most of the content of the following theorem is already proven in the literature in various forms, the closest to the formulation below being that of
[
36
]

D_{1}\mathcal{F}
on the bifurcating curve, locally near the bifurcation point.

, while using the assumption
\lambda^{\prime}(0)=0
.

We now claim that

(2.2.22)

D^{2}_{1}\mathcal{F}(0,\Omega_{m})[\overline{w}^{m-1},\overline{w}^{m-1}]=0

and that

(2.2.23)

D^{3}_{1}\mathcal{F}(0,\Omega_{m})[\overline{w}^{m-1},\overline{w}^{m-1},\overline{w}^{m-1}]=3(m-1)m(w^{m}-\overline{w}^{m})/4\mathsf{i}\notin\mathrm{ran}D_{1}\mathcal{F}(0,\Omega_{m})

Assuming equations (
2.2.22
) and (
2.2.23
) hold for the moment, note that (
2.2.18
) becomes

(2.2.24)

2D_{1}D_{2}\mathcal{F}(0,\Omega_{m})[\overline{w}^{m-1}]\lambda^{\prime}(0)+D_{1}\mathcal{F}(0,\Omega_{m})[\phi^{\prime\prime}(0)]=0.

By virtue of the transversality condition (
2.2.17
), we obtain
\lambda^{\prime}(0)=0
and, consequently, that
\phi^{\prime\prime}(0)\in\mathrm{ker}D_{1}\mathcal{F}(0,\Omega_{m})
, which implies
\phi^{\prime\prime}(0)=a\overline{w}^{m-1}
for some
a\in\mathbb{R}
. Knowing now that
\lambda^{\prime}(0)=0
, equation (
2.2.19
) holds unconditionally, and, under the assumptions (
2.2.22
) and (
2.2.23
), this equation becomes

(2.2.25)

D_{1}^{3}\mathcal{F}(0,\Omega_{m})[\overline{w}^{m-1},\overline{w}^{m-1},\overline{w}^{m-1}]+3D_{1}D_{2}\mathcal{F}(0,\Omega_{m})\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)+D_{1}\mathcal{F}(0,\Omega_{m})[\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)]=0.

Since the first two terms above are not in
\mathrm{ran}D_{1}\mathcal{F}(0,\Omega_{m})
, we obtain
\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)=-(m-1)/2

With the aim of proving relations (
2.2.22
) and (
2.2.23
), we take the second Fréchet derivative in (
2.2.12
):

(2.2.26)

D^{2}_{1}\mathcal{F}(\phi,\Omega)[\xi,\xi]=\mathrm{Im}\{{(D^{2}\mathcal{C}(\Phi)[\xi,\xi]\overline{\Phi}/2+D\mathcal{C}(\Phi)[\xi]\overline{\xi})w\Phi^{\prime}+(2\Omega\overline{\xi}+D\mathcal{C}(\Phi)[\xi]\overline{\Phi}+\mathcal{C}(\Phi)\overline{\xi})w\xi^{\prime}}\}

where

(2.2.27)

D^{2}\mathcal{C}(\Phi)[\xi,\xi]f(w)=\frac{2}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{(f(\tau)-f(w))(\xi(\tau)-\xi(w))^{2}}{(\Phi(\tau)-\Phi(w))^{3}}\Phi^{\prime}(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau\\
-\frac{2}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{(f(\tau)-f(w))(\xi(\tau)-\xi(w))}{(\Phi(\tau)-\Phi(w))^{2}}\xi^{\prime}(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau.

Restricting now to the case
\Phi(w)=w
and
\xi(w)=\overline{w}^{m-1}
, we find that

(2.2.28)

D^{2}\mathcal{C}(w)[\overline{w}^{m-1},\overline{w}^{m-1}]\overline{w}=\frac{2}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{(\overline{\tau}-\overline{w})(\overline{\tau}^{m-1}-\overline{w}^{m-1})^{2}}{(\tau-w)^{3}}\;\mathrm{d}\tau\\
+\frac{2(m-1)}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{(\overline{\tau}-\overline{w})(\overline{\tau}^{m-1}-\overline{w}^{m-1})}{(\tau-w)^{2}}\frac{1}{\tau^{m}}\;\mathrm{d}\tau\\
=-\frac{2}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{(\tau^{m-2}+...+w^{m-2})^{2}}{w^{2m-1}\tau^{2m-1}}\;\mathrm{d}\tau+\frac{2(m-1)}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{\tau^{m-2}+...+w^{m-2}}{w^{m}\tau^{2m}}\;\mathrm{d}\tau=0.

On the other hand,

(2.2.29)

D\mathcal{C}(w)[\overline{w}^{m-1}]w^{m-1}=-\frac{m-1}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{\tau^{m-1}-w^{m-1}}{\tau-w}\frac{1}{\tau^{m}}\;\mathrm{d}\tau-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{(\tau^{m-1}-w^{m-1})(\overline{\tau}^{m-1}-\overline{w}^{m-1})}{(\tau-w)^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}\tau\\
=-\frac{m-1}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{\tau^{m-2}+...+w^{m-2}}{\tau^{m}}\;\mathrm{d}\tau+\frac{1}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{(\tau^{m-2}+...+w^{m-2})^{2}}{\tau^{m-1}w^{m-1}}\;\mathrm{d}\tau=\frac{m-1}{w}.

We have calculated, then, that

(2.2.30)

D_{1}^{2}\mathcal{F}(0,\Omega_{m})[\overline{w}^{m-1},\overline{w}^{m-1}]=\mathrm{Im}\{{(m-1)(1-2\Omega_{m})}\}=0,

and (
2.2.22
) is proven. To show the validity of (
2.2.23
), we take the third Fréchet derivative in (
2.2.26
):

(2.2.31)

D_{1}^{3}\mathcal{F}(\phi,\Omega)[\xi,\xi,\xi]=\mathrm{Im}\{(D^{3}\mathcal{C}(\Phi)[\xi,\xi,\xi]\overline{\Phi}/2+3D^{2}\mathcal{C}(\Phi)[\xi,\xi]\overline{\xi}/2)w\Phi^{\prime}\\
+(3D^{2}\mathcal{C}(\Phi)[\xi,\xi]\overline{\Phi}/2+3D\mathcal{C}(\Phi)[\xi]\overline{\xi})w\xi^{\prime}\},

where

(2.2.32)

D^{3}\mathcal{C}(\Phi)[\xi,\xi,\xi]f(w)=\\
\frac{6}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{(f(\tau)-f(w))(\xi(\tau)-\xi(w))^{2}}{(\Phi(\tau)-\Phi(w))^{3}}\xi^{\prime}(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau-\frac{6}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{(f(\tau)-f(w))(\xi(\tau)-\xi(w))^{3}}{(\Phi(\tau)-\Phi(w))^{4}}\Phi^{\prime}(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau.

For
\Phi(w)=w
and
\xi(w)=\overline{w}^{m-1}
, we have

(2.2.33)

D^{3}\mathcal{C}(w)[\overline{w}^{m-1},\overline{w}^{m-1},\overline{w}^{m-1}]\overline{w}=\\
-\frac{6(m-1)}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{(\overline{\tau}-\overline{w})(\overline{\tau}^{m-1}-\overline{w}^{m-1})^{2}}{(\tau-w)^{3}}\frac{1}{\tau^{m}}\;\mathrm{d}\tau-\frac{6}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{(\overline{\tau}-\overline{w})(\overline{\tau}^{m-1}-\overline{w}^{m-1})^{3}}{(\tau-w)^{4}}\;\mathrm{d}\tau\\
=\frac{6(m-1)}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{(\tau^{m-2}+...+w^{m-2})^{2}}{\tau^{3m-1}w^{2m-1}}\;\mathrm{d}\tau-\frac{6}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{(\tau^{m-2}+...+w^{m-2})^{3}}{\tau^{3m-2}w^{3m-2}}\;\mathrm{d}\tau=0,

and

(2.2.34)

D^{2}\mathcal{C}(w)[\overline{w}^{m-1},\overline{w}^{m-1}]w^{m-1}=\\
\frac{2}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{(\tau^{m-1}-w^{m-1})(\overline{\tau}^{m-1}-\overline{w}^{m-1})^{2}}{(\tau-w)^{3}}\;\mathrm{d}\tau+\frac{2(m-1)}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{(\tau^{m-1}-w^{m-1})(\overline{\tau}^{m-1}-\overline{w}^{m-1})}{(\tau-w)^{2}}\frac{1}{\tau^{m}}\;\mathrm{d}\tau\\
=\frac{2}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{(\tau^{m-2}+...+w^{m-2})^{3}}{\tau^{2m-2}w^{2m-2}}\;\mathrm{d}\tau-\frac{2(m-1)}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{(\tau^{m-2}+...+w^{m-2})^{2}}{\tau^{2m-1}w^{m-1}}\;\mathrm{d}\tau\\
=2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}=0\\
k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}=2m-3\end{subarray}}^{m-2}\frac{w^{3(m-2)-(k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3})}}{w^{2m-2}}=\frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{w^{m+1}}.

By substituting what we have found in (
2.2.31
), we recover (
2.2.23
).

It remains to argue that the kernel of
D_{1}\mathcal{F}(\phi(s),\lambda(s))
is trivial for
|s|>0
sufficiently small. Since
\lambda^{\prime}(0)=0
and
\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)\neq 0
, it is the case that that
\lambda^{\prime}(s)\neq 0
for all
s\neq 0
sufficiently close to zero. The conclusion follows by Theorem 1.17 in Crandall and Rabinowitz
[
18
]
.
∎

Let now
\Phi_{m}:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be defined by
\Phi_{m}(w)=w+\phi(s)(w)
, with
\phi(s)\in X_{m}^{k,\alpha}
, for some
0<|s|<\widetilde{\epsilon}
such that
\mathrm{ker}D_{1}\mathcal{F}(\phi(s),\lambda(s))=\{{0}\}
and
\lambda(s)<\Omega_{m}<1/2
. Since
\phi(s)\in U^{k,\alpha}
, we have that

(2.2.35)

\inf_{\tau\neq w}\Big|\frac{\Phi_{m}(\tau)-\Phi_{m}(w)}{\tau-w}\Big|>0,

which implies the injectivity of
\Phi_{m}
. Consequently,
\Phi_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is a
C^{k,\alpha}
simple closed curve bounding a domain
D_{m}
. Moreover, since
\phi(s)\in X_{m}^{k,\alpha}
, we have that

\Phi_{m}(w)=w\Big(1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\overline{w}^{nm}\Big),

for some sequence
\{{a_{n}}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{R}
. Therefore,
\Phi_{m}
satisfies the reflection symmetry
\overline{\Phi_{m}(w)}=\Phi_{m}(\overline{w})
and the discrete rotation symmetry

(2.2.36)

\Phi_{m}(e^{2\pi\mathsf{i}/m}w)=e^{2\pi\mathsf{i}/m}\Phi_{m}(w).

These symmetries translate to
e_{2}
-reflection and
m
-fold symmetry, respectively, for the domain
D_{m}
. Let

(2.2.37)

\widetilde{\Psi}_{m}(x)=\mathbf{N}\ast\mathds{1}_{D_{m}}-\lambda_{m}|x|^{2}/2\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

where we denote
\lambda(s)=\lambda_{m}
. Then, (
2.2.1
) holds with
\Psi=\widetilde{\Psi}_{m}
,
\Phi=\Phi_{m}
, and
\Omega=\lambda_{m}
, while
(\Phi_{m},\lambda_{m})
solves (
2.2.3
). Consequently
\widetilde{\Psi}_{m}(\Phi_{m}(w))=c_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
. We then let
\Psi_{m}=\widetilde{\Psi}_{m}-c_{m}
. The tuple
(\Psi_{m},\lambda_{m},c_{m},D_{m},m)
satisfies the first, second, and third items of Definition
1.2
. Indeed, the regularity of
\Psi_{m}
follows from Proposition 1 in Bertozzi-Constantin
[
5
]
, the regularity of
\partial D_{m}
is satisfied as long as we choose
k\geqslant 2
, the fact that
\Psi_{m}
vanishes on
\partial D_{m}
holds by construction as the third item, and the condition
\min_{\partial D_{m}}(\nu\cdot\nabla\Psi_{m})>0
follows from the Hopf boundary point lemma (see, e.g., Lemma 3.4 in Gilbarg and Trudinger
[
33
]
), since
\Delta\Psi_{m}=1-2\lambda_{m}>0
in
D_{m}
.

The remainder of this subsection is devoted to showing that the fourth item in Definition
1.2
also holds and, thus, that these
m
-fold symmetric patches are admissible. We first prove a lemma which will aid us in mapping a non-trivial solution to the nondegeneracy condition to a non-trivial kernel of the linearization of the Burbea map.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
(\Psi_{m},\lambda_{m},c_{m},D_{m},m)
be one of the tuples described in the preceding paragraphs corresponding to a solution
(\phi(s),\lambda(s))\in U^{k,\alpha}_{m}\times\mathbb{R}
given by Theorem
2.7
, and
\varphi\in C_{(m)}^{0}(\partial D_{m})
satisfying

(2.2.38)

\varphi(x)+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{m}}\log|x-y|\frac{\varphi(y)}{|\nabla\Psi_{m}(y)|}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(y)=0,

for all
x\in\partial D_{m}
. Then, there exists a solution
\delta\in C^{k-1,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{C})
to the Riemann-Hilbert problem

(2.2.39)

\mathrm{Re}\{{\delta(w)\overline{\nabla\Psi_{m}}(\Phi_{m}(w))}\}=\varphi(\Phi_{m}(w))\text{ for all }w\in\mathbb{S}^{1},

such that

(2.2.40)

\delta(w)=\sum_{\mathbb{Z}\ni n\leqslant 1}a_{n}w^{n},

with
\{a_{n}\}_{\mathbb{Z}\ni n\leqslant 1}\subset\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

We claim first that
\varphi(\Phi_{m})\in C^{k,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R})
. Since
(\Psi_{m},\Phi_{m})
satisfy (
2.2.1
), the boundedness of the Cauchy integral operator
\mathcal{C}(\Phi_{m}):C^{k,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{C})\rightarrow C^{k,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{C})
implies that
\nabla\Psi_{m}(\Phi_{m})\in C^{k,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{C})
. Moreover, we can express equation (
2.2.38
) as

(2.2.41)

\varphi(\Phi_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta}))+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{2\pi\mathbb{T}}\log\char 12\relax{\Phi_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})-\Phi_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\vartheta})}\frac{\varphi(\Phi_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\vartheta}))}{|\nabla\Psi_{m}(\Phi_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\vartheta}))|}|\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(e^{\mathsf{i}\vartheta})|\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta=0,\quad\theta\in 2\pi\mathbb{T}

Taking a derivative along the unit circle, we obtain

(2.2.42)

\partial_{\theta}(\varphi\circ\Phi_{m})(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})+\mathrm{Re}\Big\{{\mathsf{i}e^{\mathsf{i}\theta}\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{2\pi\mathbb{T}}\frac{1}{\Phi_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})-\Phi_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\vartheta})}\frac{\varphi(\Phi_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\vartheta}))}{|\nabla\Psi_{m}(\Phi_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\vartheta}))|}|\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(e^{\mathsf{i}\vartheta})|\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta}\Big\}=0,

and, thus, with
w=e^{\mathsf{i}\theta}
, we have

(2.2.43)

\partial_{\theta}(\varphi\circ\Phi_{m})(w)+\mathrm{Re}\Big\{{w\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(w)\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{g(\tau)}{\Phi_{m}(w)-\Phi_{m}(\tau)}\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau}\Big\}=0,

where

(2.2.44)

g(\tau)=\frac{\varphi(\Phi_{m}(\tau))|\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(\tau)|}{|\nabla\Psi_{m}(\Phi_{m})|\tau\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(\tau)}.

Consequently, we can express the derivative of
\varphi\circ\Phi_{m}
in terms of the Cauchy integral operator:

(2.2.45)

\partial_{\theta}(\varphi\circ\Phi_{m})(w)+\mathrm{Im}\{{w\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(w)\mathcal{C}(\Phi_{m})g}\}=0.

By the proof of Proposition
3.7
,
\varphi\in C^{0}(\partial D_{m})
satisfying (
2.2.38
) implies
\varphi\in\mathrm{LL}(\partial D_{m})\subset C^{\alpha}(\partial D_{m})
. On the other hand, if
\varphi\circ\Phi_{m}\in C^{j,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R})
, then
g\in C^{\min\{j,k-1\},\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{C})
, so (
2.2.45
) implies that
\varphi\circ\Phi_{m}\in C^{\min\{j,k-1\}+1,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R})
. A finite induction argument proves our claim that
\varphi\circ\Phi_{m}\in C^{k,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R})
.

Since
\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{m}(w)-w,\lambda_{m})=0
, we have that
\mathrm{Im}\{{\overline{\nabla\Psi_{m}}(\Phi_{m}(w))w\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(w)}\}=0
. Consequently, there exists a nowhere-vanishing function
\widetilde{F}\in C^{k-1,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R})
such that
\overline{\nabla\Psi_{m}}(\Phi_{m}(w))w\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(w)=\widetilde{F}(w)
. Note that since
\Psi_{m}
is reflection invariant, we have that
\overline{\nabla\Psi_{m}}(w)=\nabla\Psi_{m}(\overline{w})
, and, therefore,
\widetilde{F}(\overline{w})=\widetilde{F}(w)
. We write

(2.2.46)

\overline{\nabla\Psi_{m}}(\Phi_{m}(w))=F(w)\overline{\Phi^{\prime}_{m}}(w)\overline{w},

with

(2.2.47)

F(w)=\widetilde{F}(w)/|\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(w)|^{2},

and note that
F
is, likewise, non-vanishing and reflection invariant. By the classical theory for the Riemann-Hilbert problem (see, e.g., Muskhelishvili
[
51
]
or Theorem A.1. in
[
36
]
for a compact statement), since
\Phi^{\prime}_{m}
has vanishing winding number, there exists a holomorphic
f:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, which extends as a
C^{k-1,\alpha}
mapping to the closure of the disk, is reflection invariant
\overline{f(w)}=f(\overline{w})
, and solves

(2.2.48)

\mathrm{Re}\{\Phi^{\prime}_{m}(w)f(w)\}=\varphi(\Phi_{m}(w))/F(w)\text{ for all }w\in\mathbb{S}^{1}.

We define, then,
\delta\in C^{k-1,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{C})
by
\delta(w)=w\overline{f(w)}
. We have that

(2.2.49)

\mathrm{Re}\{{\delta(w)\overline{\nabla\Psi_{m}(}w)}\}=F(w)\mathrm{Re}\{{\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(w)f(w)}\}=\varphi(\Phi_{m}(w)),

as desired. Moreover, since
f
is holomorphic in the disk and reflection invariant, there exists a sequence
\{b_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
f(w)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}w^{n}
. Identity (
2.2.40
) is then satisfied provided that
a_{n}=b_{1-n}
.
∎

At last, we are now ready to prove the main result of this subsection.

Theorem 2.9

(Admissibility of Rankine vortex perturbations)
.

Let
\mathbb{N}\ni k\geqslant 2
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
. There exists
0<\epsilon^{\prime}\leqslant\widetilde{\epsilon}
, with
\widetilde{\epsilon}>0
given by Theorem
2.7
, such that the tuple
(\Psi_{m},\lambda_{m},c_{m},D_{m},m)
corresponding, via Theorem
2.7
and the subsequent discussion, to a solution
(\phi(s),\lambda(s))\in U_{m}^{k,\alpha}\times\mathbb{R}
,
0<|s|\leqslant\epsilon^{\prime}
is admissible in the sense of Definition
1.2
.

Proof.

As already remarked in the discussion following the proof of Theorem
2.7
, the first, second, and third items in Definition
1.2
are easily seen to hold for the tuple
(\Psi_{m},\lambda_{m},c_{m},D_{m},m)
. For the purpose of obtaining a contradiction, assume that item four fails to hold; namely, that there exists a nontrivial solution
\varphi\in C_{(m)}^{0}(\partial D_{m})\setminus\{{0}\}
satisfying equation (
2.2.38
). Then, Lemma
2.8
applies and yields a function
\delta\in C^{k-1,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{C})
which satisfies (
2.2.39
) and, by the assumed non-triviality of
\varphi
, does not vanish identically.

Note that, while we have defined
\mathcal{F}
on the domains
U^{k,\alpha}
for the purpose of applying the Crandall-Rabinowitz Theorem, the expression (
2.2.9
) is also well-defined on the Banach space

(2.2.50)

X_{\mathrm{ext}}^{k-1,\alpha}=\Big\{{\phi\in C^{k-1,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C})\;:\;\phi(w)=\sum_{n=-1}^{\infty}a_{n}\overline{w}^{n};a_{n}\in\mathbb{R}}\Big\},

of which
\delta
is an element. The Fréchet derivative
D_{1}\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{m}-w,\lambda_{m})
exists, then, on
X_{\mathrm{ext}}^{k,\alpha}
and its expression coincides with the one in (
2.2.12
). We claim that
D_{1}\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{m}-w,\lambda_{m})[\delta]=0
. To this end, we compute

(2.2.51)

\partial_{\theta}\mathrm{Re}\{{\delta(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})\overline{\nabla\Psi_{m}}(\Phi_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta}))}\}=\mathrm{Im}\{e^{\mathsf{i}\theta}\delta^{\prime}(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})(\lambda_{m}\overline{\Phi_{m}}(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})+\mathcal{C}(\Phi_{m})\overline{\Phi_{m}}(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})/2)\\
-\mathsf{i}\delta(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})(\lambda_{m}\overline{\partial_{\theta}\Phi_{m}}(e^{i\theta})+\partial_{\theta}(\mathcal{C}(\Phi_{m})\overline{\Phi}_{m})(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})/2)\}.

For the tangential derivative of the Cauchy integral operator, we have

(2.2.52)

\partial_{\theta}\mathcal{C}(\Phi_{m})\overline{\Phi}_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{2\pi\mathbb{T}}\frac{\overline{\partial_{\theta}\Phi_{m}}(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})}{\Phi_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})-\Phi_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\vartheta})}\mathsf{i}e^{\mathsf{i}\vartheta}\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(e^{\mathsf{i}\vartheta})\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta\\
+\frac{\partial_{\theta}\Phi_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{2\pi\mathbb{T}}(\overline{\Phi}_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})-\overline{\Phi}_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\vartheta}))\partial_{\vartheta}\frac{1}{\Phi_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\vartheta})-\Phi_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})}\;\mathrm{d}\theta\\
=-\frac{1}{2}\overline{\partial_{\theta}\Phi_{m}}(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})+\frac{\partial_{\theta}\Phi_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})}{2\pi\mathsf{i}}\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{2\pi\mathbb{T}}\frac{\overline{\partial_{\vartheta}\Phi_{m}}(e^{\mathsf{i}\vartheta})}{\Phi_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\vartheta})-\Phi_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})}\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta=\partial_{\theta}\Phi_{m}(e^{\mathsf{i}\theta})\mathcal{C}(\Phi_{m})\Big[{\frac{\overline{\partial_{\theta}\Phi_{m}}}{\partial_{\theta}\Phi_{m}}}\Big](e^{\mathsf{i}\theta}).

Using the notation
w=e^{\mathsf{i}\theta}
, we can express (
2.2.51
) as

(2.2.53)

\partial_{\theta}\mathrm{Re}\{\delta\overline{\nabla\Psi_{m}}(\Phi_{m})\}(w)=\mathrm{Im}\{(\lambda_{m}\overline{\Phi_{m}}(w)+\mathcal{C}(\Phi_{m})\overline{\Phi_{m}}(w)/2)w\delta^{\prime}(w)\\
+(\lambda_{m}\overline{\delta}(w)+\delta(w)\mathcal{C}(\Phi_{m})[\overline{\partial_{\theta}\Phi_{m}}/\partial_{\theta}\Phi_{m}]/2)w\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(w)\}.

On the other hand, with
\varphi\circ\Phi_{m}=\mathrm{Re}\{\delta\overline{\nabla\Psi_{m}}(\Phi_{m})\}
, equation (
2.2.44
) becomes

(2.2.54)

g(w)=\frac{1}{w\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(w)}\mathrm{Re}\Big\{{\delta(w)\frac{\overline{\nabla\Psi_{m}}(\Phi_{m}(w))}{|\nabla\Psi_{m}(\Phi_{m}(w))|}|\Phi^{\prime}_{m}(w)|}\Big\}\\
=\frac{1}{w\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(w)}\mathrm{sgn}(F)\mathrm{Re}\{{\delta(w)\overline{\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(w)}\overline{w}}\}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\overline{\delta}(w)-\delta(w)\frac{\overline{\partial_{\theta}\Phi_{m}}}{\partial_{\theta}\Phi_{m}}(w)\Big),

where, for the second equality, we have recalled that
F
is defined in (
2.2.47
) and used (
2.2.46
). For the third equality, the fact that
\mathrm{sgn}(F)=1
was invoked. The latter can be deduced as follows: from (
2.2.46
), it follows that, as long as
\|\Phi_{m}-w\|_{C^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}
is sufficiently small,

(2.2.55)

\mathrm{sgn}(F)=\mathrm{sgn}(\mathrm{Re}\{{\overline{\nabla\Psi_{m}}w\Phi_{m}^{\prime}}\})=\mathrm{sgn}(\mathrm{Re}\{{\overline{\nabla\Psi_{m}}w}\})=\mathrm{sgn}(\partial_{\mathrm{r}}\Psi_{m})=1,

with
\partial_{\mathrm{r}}
denoting the derivative in the radial direction. Consequently, by plugging (

Corollary 3.6

Let

\sup_{x\in\overline{U}}\int_{(U\setminus B(x,r))\cap\{{\Psi+\psi=t}\}}\frac{1}{\char 12\relax{x-y}}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(y)\leqslant C(1+\log(1/r)).

(3.1.29)

\char 12\relax{\mathcal{H}^{1}(U\cap\{{\Psi+\psi=t}\})-\mathcal{H}^{1}(U\cap\{{\Psi=0}\})}\leqslant C(t+\lVert\psi\rVert_{C^{1}(\overline{U})}).

Proof.

We begin by proving the first item. Let
x\in\overline{U}
. From the first item of Lemma
3.5
we have

(3.1.30)

\mathcal{H}^{1}(U\cap\{{\Psi+\psi=t}\}\cap B(x,r))\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}^{1}(B(x_{i},\delta_{-})\cap\{{\Psi+\psi=t}\}\cap B(x,r))

so it suffices to estimate the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
-term in the sum above. For this we use the local parametrization of the
t
-level set determined by
G^{\psi}_{i}(\cdot,t)
where the latter is from the collection of coordinate functions produced by Lemma
3.5
. We get

(3.1.31)

\mathcal{H}^{1}(B(x_{i},\delta_{-})\cap\{{\Psi+\psi=t}\}\cap B(x,r))\leqslant\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\mathds{1}_{G^{\psi}_{i}(\cdot,t)\in B(x,r)}(y_{1})\char 12\relax{\partial_{1}G^{\psi}_{i}(y_{1},t)}\;\mathrm{d}y_{1}.

From estimate (
3.1.23

\char 12\relax{\partial_{1}G^{\psi}_{i}(y_{1},t)}\leqslant R
. On the other hand, by inspection of identity (

) from the proof of Lemma
3.5
, we deduce

(3.1.32)

\forall\;y_{1},\widetilde{y}_{1}\in[-\ell,\ell],\;\char 12\relax{G^{\psi}_{i}(y_{1},t)-G^{\psi}_{i}(\widetilde{y}_{1},t)}\geqslant\char 12\relax{y_{1}-\widetilde{y}_{1}}

(3.1.33)

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{{y_{1}\in[-\ell,\ell]\;:\;G^{\psi}_{i}(y_{1},t)\in B(x,r)}\})\leqslant 2r

Synthesizing the estimates (
3.1.30

, and summing over

.

We turn our attention to the second item, so again let

We prove the third item via a similar strategy as above. For

we dyadically decompose in a similar fashion, thus obtaining:

(3.1.37)

\int_{(U\setminus B(x,r))\cap\{{\Psi+\psi=t}\}}\frac{1}{\char 12\relax{x-y}}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(y)\leqslant\frac{1}{\delta_{-}}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{{\Psi+\psi=t}\})+\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{2^{n+1}}{\delta_{-}}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{{\Psi+\psi=t}\}\cap B(x,\delta_{-}/2^{n})).

We conclude by noting that

.

Finally, we consider the fourth item. We begin by using the triangle inequality to write

(3.1.39)

\char 12\relax{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{{\Psi+\psi=t}\})-\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{{\Psi=0}\})}\leqslant\mathbf{I}_{1}+\mathbf{I}_{2}

with

(3.1.40)

\mathbf{I}_{1}=\char 12\relax{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{{\Psi+\psi=t}\})-\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{{\Psi=t}\})}\text{ and }\mathbf{I}_{2}=\char 12\relax{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{{\Psi=t}\})-\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{{\Psi=0}\})}.

Synthesizing (

Estimating

Synthesizing the estimates on

Proposition 3.7

Proposition 3.8

(Limit identification)
.

Assume that

,
\{{\varepsilon_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{0}]

(3.1.60)

-\int_{U}g(x)[{(\Gamma_{\varepsilon_{n}}^{\Psi})^{\prime}(\psi_{n})}](x)\phi_{n}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x\to\int_{U\cap\{{\Psi+\psi=0}\}}\frac{g(y)\phi(y)}{\char 12\relax{\nabla(\Psi+\psi)(y)}}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(y)\text{ as }n\to\infty

and

(3.1.61)

\int_{U}g(x)[{\Gamma_{\varepsilon_{n}}^{\Psi}(\psi_{n})}](x)\phi_{n}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x\to\int_{U\cap\{{\Psi+\psi<0}\}}g(x)\phi(x)\;\mathrm{d}x\text{ and }n\to\infty.

We only prove (
3.1.60
) in the special case that
\psi=0
. The remaining claims in the general case follow by similar arguments. We shall use the uniform local level set coordinates constructed by Lemma
3.5
and the partition of unity (

).

Lemmas
3.2
and
3.5
imply that
(\Gamma^{\Psi}_{\varepsilon_{n}})^{\prime}(\psi_{n})
is supported in
\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}B(x_{i},\delta_{-})
for every

. In turn we can decompose into a sum of

-term make a change of variables with

\text{where }I^{i}_{n}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}}\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\int_{-\varepsilon_{n}}^{\varepsilon_{n}}\chi_{i}(G^{\psi_{n}}_{i}(s))g(G^{\psi_{n}}_{i}(s))\gamma^{\prime}(-s_{2}/\varepsilon_{n})\phi_{n}(G^{\psi_{n}}_{i}(s))\det\nabla G^{\psi_{n}}_{i}(s)\;\mathrm{d}s_{2}\;\mathrm{d}s_{1}.

Now, it is suggested to us by the structure above that we ought to make a further change of variables by replacing

by

. Doing so gives:

(3.1.63)

Next, we use the dominated convergence theorem in conjunction with the hypotheses (
3.1.59
) and the third item of Lemma
3.5
to deduce that the sequence
\{{I^{i}_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}
converges to the limit

given by

(3.1.64)

\lim_{n\to\infty}I^{i}_{n}=I^{i}=\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\chi_{i}(G^{0}_{i}(s_{1},0))g(G^{0}_{i}(s_{1},0))\phi(G^{0}_{i}(s_{1},0))\det\nabla G^{0}_{i}(s_{1},0)\;\mathrm{d}s_{1}.

We next use the right hand equality of (
3.1.22
) to introduce a line element to the above integral and rewrite it in the coordinate-free fashion:

In this subsection the nondegeneracy condition (

Proposition 3.10

(Quantitative closed range estimates)
.

There exists
C\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
0<\varepsilon_{1}\leqslant\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{0}
,
0<\rho_{1},r_{1}<\widetilde{r}_{0}

(3.2.1)

\lVert\phi\rVert_{C^{0}(\overline{U})}\leqslant C\Big\lVert\phi+\Big[{\int_{0}^{1}\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{\varepsilon,\rho,\text{\char 21\relax}}((1-\tau)\psi^{0}+\tau\psi^{1})\;\mathrm{d}\tau}\Big]\phi\Big\rVert_{C^{0}(\overline{U})}

Proof.

We shall argue by way of contradiction, so let us suppose that the claim of the proposition is false. Then we would be able to find sequences

(3.2.2)

\{{\varepsilon_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{0}],\;\{{\rho_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset[0,\widetilde{r}_{0}),\;\{{\text{\char 21\relax}_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset[0,\text{\char 21\relax}_{0}],\\

but

\lim_{n\to\infty}[{|\varepsilon_{n}|+|\rho_{n}|+|\text{\char 21\relax}_{n}|+\lVert\psi^{0}_{n}\rVert_{C^{1}(\overline{U})}+\lVert\psi^{1}_{n}\rVert_{C^{1}(\overline{U})}+\lVert\phi_{n}-\varphi_{n}\rVert_{C^{0}(\overline{U})}}]=0

3.3.
Quantitative local invertibility

Somewhat remarkably, the conclusions of Corollaries
3.11
and
3.12
are all we need to deduce local invertibility near zero for the smooth maps
\boldsymbol{F}_{\varepsilon,\rho,\text{\char 21\relax}}
. The properties established by these results ensure local surjectivity by guaranteeing the convergence of Newton’s method, despite the proximity to singularities. We have the following abstract result that is inspired by Hamilton’s version of the Nash-Moser inverse function theorem
[
35
]
.

Theorem 3.13

(Abstract quantitative local surjectivity)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces,
r^{+},r^{-},\chi\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
with
r^{-}<r^{+}
, and
F:B_{X}(0,r^{+})\to Y
be continuously differentiable and satisfy:

(1)

F(0)=0
and if
x\in B_{X}(0,r^{+})
is such that
\lVert F(x)\rVert_{Y}\leqslant\chi
then
\lVert x\rVert_{X}\leqslant r^{-}
.

(2)

There exists
L_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that for all
x\in B_{X}(0,r^{+})
we have the estimates

(3.3.1)

\lVert F(x)\rVert_{X}+\lVert DF(x)\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(X;Y)}\leqslant L_{0}.

(3)

There exists
L_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and a map
R:B(0,r^{+})\to\mathcal{L}(Y;X)
such that for all
x,\widetilde{x}\in B_{X}(0,r^{+})
with
x\neq\widetilde{x}
and all
y\in Y
it holds that

(3.3.2)

\lVert R(x)\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(Y;X)}+\lVert R(x)-R(\widetilde{x})\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(Y;X)}/\lVert x-\widetilde{x}\rVert_{X}\leqslant L_{1}\text{ and }DF(x)R(x)y=y.

Then, for each
y\in Y
with
\lVert y\rVert_{Y}\leqslant\chi
there exists
x\in X
with
\lVert x\rVert_{X}\leqslant r^{-}
with
F(x)=y
.

Proof.

We begin by defining an auxiliary map. We set
r^{0}=(r^{-}+r^{+})/2
and

(3.3.3)

T=\min\{{(r^{+}-r^{-})/2L_{1}(\chi+L_{0}),1/2L_{1}(\chi+2L_{0})}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}.

Then, for any
T_{0}\in[0,\infty)
let us define

(3.3.4)

\Phi_{T_{0}}:\overline{B_{X}(0,r^{-})}\times\overline{B_{Y}(0,\chi)}\times C^{0}([T_{0},T_{0}+T];\overline{B_{X}(0,r^{0})})\to C^{0}([T_{0},T_{0}+T];\overline{B_{X}(0,r^{0})})

to be the operator

(3.3.5)

\Phi_{T_{0}}(x,y,\gamma)(t)=x+\int_{T_{0}}^{t}R(\gamma(s))(y-F(\gamma(s)))\;\mathrm{d}s

for
x\in\overline{B_{X}(0,r^{-})}
,
y\in\overline{B_{Y}(0,\chi)}
,
\gamma\in C^{0}([T_{0},T_{0}+T];\overline{B_{X}(0,r^{0})})
, and
t\in[T_{0},T_{0}+T]
. Let us verify that
\Phi_{T_{0}}
is well defined in the sense that it maps into the codomain stated in (
3.3.4
). By using the hypotheses of the proposition and the definition of
T
in (
3.3.3
) we estimate for
x
,
y
,
\gamma
, and
t
belonging to the aforementioned sets

(3.3.6)

\lVert\Phi_{T_{0}}(x,y,\gamma)(t)\rVert_{X}\leqslant r^{-}+TL_{1}(\chi+L_{0})\leqslant r^{0}.

So indeed
\Phi_{T_{0}}
is well-defined.

We now claim that
\Phi_{T_{0}}
is a contraction in its final argument. We estimate for any
x\in\overline{B_{X}(0,r^{-})}
,
y\in\overline{B_{Y}(0,\chi)}
,
\gamma,\widetilde{\gamma}\in C^{0}([T_{0},T_{0}+T],\overline{B_{X}(0,r^{0})})
, and
t\in[T_{0},T]
that

(3.3.7)

\lVert\Phi_{T_{0}}(x,y,\gamma)(t)-\Phi_{T_{0}}(x,y,\widetilde{\gamma})(t)\rVert_{X}\leqslant TL_{1}(\chi+2L_{0})\lVert\gamma-\widetilde{\gamma}\rVert_{C^{0}([T_{0},T_{0}+T];X)}.

Thanks to (
3.3.3
), we are assured that
TL_{1}(\chi+2L_{0})\leqslant 1/2
and thus the above estimate shows the desired contraction estimates.

We are in a position to invoke the contraction mapping principle and deduce the existence of a function

(3.3.8)

\varphi_{T_{0}}:\overline{B_{X}(0,r^{-})}\times\overline{B_{Y}(0,\chi)}\to C^{0}([T_{0},T_{0}+T];\overline{B_{X}(0,r^{0})})

with the property that
\Phi_{T_{0}}(x,y,\varphi_{T_{0}}(x,y))=\varphi_{T_{0}}(x,y)
for all
x
and
y
belonging to the domain of (
3.3.8
). Immediately from (
3.3.5
) we deduce that
\varphi_{T_{0}}(x,y)\in C^{1}([T_{0},T_{0}+T];\overline{B_{X}(0,r^{0})})
and is a solution to the ODE

(3.3.9)

\varphi_{T_{0}}(x,y)(T_{0})=x\text{ and for all }t\in[T_{0},T_{0}+T]\;[\varphi_{T_{0}}(x,y)]^{\prime}(t)=R(\varphi_{T_{0}}(x,y)(t))(y-F(\varphi_{T_{0}}(x,y)(t))).

Therefore, for any

(the dual space of

) we can use (
3.3.9
) and the right hand identity of (
3.3.2
) to compute that

(3.3.10)

[\langle y-F(\varphi_{T_{0}}(x,y)(\cdot)),z\rangle_{Y,Y^{\ast}}]^{\prime}(t)=-\langle y-F(\varphi_{T_{0}}(x,y)(t)),z\rangle_{Y,Y^{\ast}}

for
t\in[T_{0},T_{0}+T]

we have the identity

(3.3.11)

\langle y-F(\varphi_{T_{0}}(x,y)(t)),z\rangle_{Y,Y^{\ast}}=e^{-(t-T_{0})}\langle y-F(x),z\rangle_{Y,Y^{\ast}}.

Since

was arbitrary, we deduce that for all

Let us move on to the next stage of our construction. We fix

and define a sequence

satisfying

we have

. Now if

and

where

is from (
3.3.3
) and
\varphi
is the fixed point from (

). Initially we only know that

. However, thanks to (

) and the induction hypothesis, we get

(3.3.14)

F(x_{n+1})=e^{-T}F(x_{n})+(1-e^{-T})y=(1-e^{-(n+1)T})y.

Then from the first hypothesis we find that
\lVert x_{n+1}\rVert_{X}\leqslant r^{-}
since (
3.3.14
) implies that
\lVert F(x_{n+1})\rVert_{Y}\leqslant\chi
. This completes the inductive construction.

Let us now show that the sequence

is Cauchy. To see this, we first use identity (
3.3.13
) and the fact that
\varphi
is a fixed point of

from (

(3.3.15)

Now from (

Therefore (

We conclude that the sequence in question is indeed Cauchy and so there exists

such that

as
n\to\infty
. From the identity
F(x_{n})=(1-e^{-nT})y
, that holds for every

, we deduce that

. This completes the proof.
∎

A.2.
A logarithmic interpolation inequality

Lemma A.1
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a smooth bounded domain. There exists constants
C,c\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that the following hold for all
f\in\mathrm{LL}^{1}(\overline{U})
:

(1)

We have
\lVert f\rVert_{\mathrm{LL}(\overline{U})}\leqslant c\lVert f\rVert_{\mathrm{LL}^{1}(\overline{U})}
.

(2)

We have

(A.2.1)

\lVert f\rVert_{C^{1}(\overline{U})}\leqslant C\lVert f\rVert_{\mathrm{LL}(\overline{U})}(1+\log(c\lVert f\rVert_{\mathrm{LL}^{1}(\overline{U})}/\lVert f\rVert_{\mathrm{LL}(\overline{U})})).

Proof.

Since
U
is a domain with
C^{1,1}
boundary, the extension operator
\mathfrak{E}
constructed for Theorem 5 of Chapter 6 in Stein
[
59
]
can be shown to map boundedly

(A.2.2)

\mathfrak{E}:X(\overline{U})\to X(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap\{{f\;:\;\mathrm{supp}(f)\subset B(0,R)}\}\text{ for }X=\{{\mathrm{LL},C^{1},\mathrm{LL}^{1}}\},

for some fixed
R<\infty
chosen sufficiently large depending on
U
. By using of
\mathfrak{E}
appropriately, we can reduce to proving the first and second items for the case
U=\mathbb{R}^{2}
; in this case the first item is trivial.

To prove the second item (when

) and

, we let

In turn, we arrive at the pointwise estimate

(A.2.4)

\char 12\relax{\nabla f(x)}\leqslant 2\log(1/r)\lVert f\rVert_{\mathrm{LL}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+r\log(1/r)\lVert f\rVert_{\mathrm{LL}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

The estimate (

∎
