Assessing the psychometric properties of Hindi-translated dispositional resilience (hardiness) scale and cognitive flexibility scale among police personnel
Authors: Hardik Singhal, Anamitra Basu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHardiness is a personality trait that enables individuals to cope with stress and adversity effectively based on three components: commitment, control, and challenge. A construct associated with hardiness is cognitive flexibility, which enables individuals to be conscious and adaptable in the face of stressful circumstances, make efficient decisions, and solve problems effectively. The Dispositional Resilience Scale (DRS-15) and Cognitive Flexibility Scale (CFS) are widely used, validated measures of assessing psychological hardiness and cognitive flexibility, respectively. The study aimed to assess the psychometric properties (reliability and validity) of the Hindi-translated version of the DRS-15 and CFS among Rajasthan police personnel.MethodsThe DRS-15 and CFS measures were translated using a recognized back-translation and transcultural process, which were then administered to Rajasthan police personnel between the ages of 21 and 60 years. To evaluate internal consistency (reliability), Cronbach’s α and McDonald’s coefficient were calculated. Factor loadings were computed for both measures using exploratory factor analysis (EFA), which were further used to calculate discriminant and convergent validity. The construct validity of the measures was established using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).ResultsThe findings revealed good and acceptable internal consistencies for the Hindi-translated DRS-15 (α = 0.75;ω = 0.69) and 10-item CFS (α = 0.78;ω = 0.78) measures. Acceptable reliabilities of the three components of the DRS-15 scale were also established. EFA described the presence of three factors in the DRS-15 and the unidimensionality of the CFS. Discriminant validity was established for DRS-15 as per Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) ratio. Convergent validity for the measures was computed through composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE). CFA further established the construct validity of both the measures with acceptable fit indices.ConclusionThe Hindi-translated DRS-15 and 10-item CFS versions are a good, easy-to-use, reliable, and valid measure in the sample of police personnel. The DRS-15 can be a helpful assessment tool for researchers in evaluating the association between hardiness and stress-related factors, psychological well-being, and quality of life. The CFS is useful for evaluating people’s cognitive flexibility in terms of handling and adapting to stressful situations, making decisions, and solving problems.Keywords: Hardiness, Cognitive flexibility, Stress, Police personnel, Reliability, Validity

Background

The job of policing is recognized as a stressful area of work, constituting ongoing stress, burnout, and excessive workload, with the constant exposure to these problems often linked to physiological, psychological, and behavioural issues in the long run [1, 2]. A connotation of resilience is the concept of ‘Hardiness’ that is regarded as a trait that allows individuals to perform at an optimal level under stressful conditions [3]. Similar to hardiness, cognitive flexibility is another construct that defines an individual’s awareness and adaptability in facing adversities, impacting their capacity to solve problems, make decisions, and modify their thinking in response to different circumstances [4]. Both constructs are emphasized in dealing with and coping with stressful situations. They are considered crucial in high-demand professions, such as the military [5, 6], policing [7, 8], firefighting [9], and other occupations that directly affect people’s mental health, job satisfaction, well-being, and quality of life.

Psychological hardiness

Psychological hardiness is considered a personality trait that serves as a resilience attribute, enabling individuals to cope with adverse life events [10]. High levels of hardiness are linked with three key characteristics (3 C’s): commitment, control, and challenges [3, 11]. The construct of hardiness has been studied by various authors with empirical evidence from multiple populations, including nursing staff and students [12], military and police officers [13, 14], sports athletes [15], organization employees [16], educational faculties [17], and college students [18]. The construct of hardiness has been found to have significant associations with multiple parameters, including stressful events, illness, quality of life, health outcomes, performance, the Big Five personality factors, grit, and psychological well-being [3, 19–22].

The Personal Views Survey (PVS) [23] and the Dispositional Resilience Scale (DRS) [24] have gained broader recognition in measuring levels of hardiness based on the 3 C’s [11, 25], with the latter found to show better reliability than the former across multiple populations. The DRS, after its initial development [24], has been reduced to 30-items from 45-items and then further reduced to 15-items, and has been a frequently used hardiness measure and provided satisfactory results of psychometric properties [26–28].

The Dispositional Resilience Scale (DRS-15) is a frequently used personality tool of psychological hardiness across a number of countries. The DRS-15 suggests that hardy individuals tend to have a greater sense of control, a stronger commitment, and are more receptive to challenges and change [29]. Throughout the years, the DRS-15 measure has been utilized and validated in various languages by several researchers, consistently providing satisfactory results [25, 28, 30–32]. Hardiness, alongside resilience, was found to act as a protective factor among police personnel, aiding them in coping with stress, reducing burnout, and promoting psychological well-being [33, 34]. The dimensions of the DRS-15 were found to be relevant for police officers who require different skill sets to deal with occupational stressors and remain effective in demanding environments [33]. Previous research has shown promising reliability and validity of the DRS-15 in police personnel [33], however, there are no validation studies of the DRS-15 in the Indian context.

Cognitive flexibility

Cognitive flexibility is characterized by an individual’s self-efficacy, willingness to be flexible and adaptable, and knowledge of several possibilities and alternatives in every given circumstance [35]. It is considered an ability to adapt to various stressors through a variety of cognitively focused behaviours, including multitasking, creativity, and decision-making [4]. The skill of cognitive flexibility, similar to psychological hardiness, is regarded as helpful in reducing stress while coping with adversities [36]. Individuals high in cognitive flexibility are regarded as successful in interpersonal relationships, possess effective problem-solving abilities, are resilient and adaptable in new situations, and feel confident in dealing with diverse challenges [36]. On the other hand, lower levels of cognitive flexibility are linked to ineffective behaviour, lower levels of well-being, and traumatic stress exposure, as illustrated by a predictor of post-traumatic stress severity [37]. The construct of cognitive flexibility in police personnel serves as a crucial executive function that allows them to adapt and adjust to new thinking and circumstances, making effective decisions under stressful circumstances, and managing complex demands. Studies have indicated that the presence of higher cognitive functioning in police officers, including flexibility and accuracy, significantly impacts performance by enhancing decision-making ability under stress, improving awareness, and increasing adaptability during critical situations [38, 39].

The Cognitive Flexibility Scale (CFS) is a 12-item, widely used inventory that assesses the attributes of cognitive flexibility in individuals, including self-awareness, flexibility, adaptability, and self-efficacy [35]. The CFS has been utilized in multiple languages and in various cultures, including the USA [40], Turkey [41], Japan [42], Saudi Arabia [43], and Italy [44]. The original and cross-culturally validated measures demonstrated prominent psychometric properties for the CFS, characterized by high and acceptable reliability and construct validity. The scale has been used in some Indian studies to understand the construct [45, 46], however, there are no studies that have established the reliability and validity of the measure in the Indian context.

The study, therefore, aims to utilize the Dispositional Resilience Scale and Cognitive Flexibility Scale, which have been translated and transculturally adapted into Hindi, and assess their psychometric properties (reliability and validity) among police personnel.

Methods

Translation and transcultural adaptation

The study utilizes Brislin’s back-translation method [47, 48] for cross-cultural adaptation of the dispositional resilience scale and cognitive flexibility scale.

Translation reconciliation

Firstly, a forward translation procedure was carried out, in which two bilingual translators, both proficient in English and Hindi, translated the DRS and CFS questionnaires into the target language (Hindi). Following this, the back translation procedure was carried out by having two additional proficient bilingual translators (who were not informed about the forward translation) to translate the target language back to the original language (English). The back-translated version and the original version were then compared for vocabulary and conceptual equivalence. The process was repeated until there was correspondence [42] between all the items in both the Hindi and English versions.

Pretest feedback and concrete changes

The Hindi versions of DRS (HDRS-15, Table 10 in Appendix 1) and CFS (CFS-H, Table 11 in Appendix 2) measures were then reviewed by a few professionals, including one psychology professor, two station house officers, and three higher rank police officials, to determine the face validity of the measures. Relevant feedback and opinions on syntax from professionals and ten police officers were taken into consideration for further modification of the measures. The desired number of experts for content validity is between 3 and 10 [32, 49]. The professionals then rated each item of both measures on a 4-point scale (1 = not relevant to 4 = highly relevant), and based upon number of experts and relevancy scores, content validity index (CVI) was calculated. The CVI values for both the measures exceeded the minimum threshold level of 0.78 [49, 50].

Summary of pilot reliability results

After establishing face and content validity, a pilot test was conducted on 20 police personnel to calculate the reliability of both, HDRS-15 (overall and its components) and CFS-H, which exceeded the conventional threshold level (α > 0.70), and acquired further feedback from the participants who found the translated measures understandable to follow.

Participants

The total sample consisted of 226 police personnel, comprising 87.61% males and 12.39% females, from the districts of Rajasthan who had completed the HDRS-15 and CFS-H. Inclusion criteria for the study involved participants aged 21 to 60 years, irrespective of gender, with a minimum 10th grade level of education, using purposive sampling. Exclusion criteria included being under 21 years of age and the self-reported presence of any psychological disorder. The majority of participants have completed a graduation-level education (48.67%). The sample comprises participants from newly joined to highly experienced police personnel, with positions including constable (73.89%), head constable (21.68%), and sub-inspector (4.43%).

Measure

Psychological hardiness was measured using the Dispositional Resilience Scale [29], which comprises 15 items measuring three aspects: commitment, control, and challenge. Based on a 4-point Likert scale, responses were recorded. Sample items are ‘It is up to me to decide how the rest of my life will be’ (control), ‘I really look forward to my work’ (commitment), and ‘Changes in routine are interesting to me’ (challenge). The measure comprises six negatively worded items, which, after reverse-scoring and adding the item scores for each of the three components, can be used to determine the overall score.

Cognitive flexibility was measured through the Cognitive Flexibility Scale [35], a unidimensional measure consisting of 12 items. The responses of this measure are based on a 6-point Likert scale format. ‘I can communicate an idea in many different ways,’ ‘I feel like I never get to make decisions,’ and ‘In any given situation, I am able to act appropriately’ are some sample items of the measure. The measure consists of four negatively worded items, which, following reverse scoring and summing up responses of all items, will provide the total cognitive flexibility score.

Procedure

Before beginning data collection, permission was obtained from the district’s Superintendent of Police. After obtaining approval, data were collected primarily by administering the H-DRS-15 and CFS-H to police personnel across the Kota and Dholpur districts of Rajasthan. Controls were implied as per the inclusion and exclusion criteria as was described earlier. The measures were translated into Hindi to minimize language barriers and increase accessibility to the target sample, as approximately 41% of the North Indian population is Hindi-speaking [51]. Participants received comprehensive information about the study, before proceeding with filling the measures. They were asked to provide their demographic details and review the informed consent form, which stated their voluntary participation and their right to withdraw at any time, and maintaining the confidentiality of the results in accordance with the American Psychological Association (APA) Code of Ethics [52]. Data from 234 participants were initially collected, and after screening through all the questionnaires, responses from 8 participants were excluded due to missing information and data, leading to a final sample of 226 participants.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 26 and AMOS version 21 were utilized for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were calculated from participants’ demographic details. Reliability was computed through Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega coefficients for the Hindi-translated hardiness and cognitive flexibility measures. Following reliability, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was employed to find factor loadings and correlations using principal component extraction (Varimax method). Through factor loadings, discriminant validity and convergent validity of the measures were established. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was then applied to establish the construct validity and examine the model fit indices and factor structure of the measures [53].

Psychometric properties of HDRS-15

For this study, the reliability of the measure was computed using Cronbach’s α coefficient (Table 2). The α coefficient for HDRS-15 was 0.75. The α coefficients for its components were 0.80 (commitment), 0.76 (control), and 0.77 (challenge), respectively. The value of the α coefficient indicates moderate to high reliability, as it exceeds the standard value of 0.70 [54, 55]. Furthermore, McDonald’s omega () coefficient was calculated (Table 2) to compute more accurate and robust reliability of the overall measure and its components [56]. The internal consistency of the HDRS-15 yielded a coefficient of 0.69. The value is slightly below the conventional 0.70 threshold, it is considered acceptable due to the limited number of items in the components [57]. Additionally, studies suggested that the range of 0.60 to 0.70 is acceptable for exploratory research [58], identifying it as a ‘moderate’ range [59]. The components of the HDRS-15, on the other hand, yielded coefficients of 0.77, 0.74, and 0.77 for commitment, control, and challenge, respectively. The mean inter-item correlation for the components of HDRS-15 was found to be within the optimal range of 0.20 to 0.50 for all three components [60, 61].

Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity illustrate the sampling suitability and identify the strength of the relationship between the items. It was postulated that the recommended value of a KMO should be a minimum of 0.50 (barely accepted), values between 0.70 and 0.80 are considered acceptable, and values above 0.90 are considered excellent [62, 63]. The KMO for HDRS-15 was found to be 0.744 (acceptable). Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity, on the other hand, was found to be significant. Principal component analysis confirmed the presence of three factors in the HDRS-15: a commitment factor, a control factor, and a challenge factor comprising five items each. Following principal component analysis, maximum likelihood extraction, and the varimax rotation method, factor loadings and factor correlations were extracted (Table 3). The factor loadings of all the items were above the level of 0.40 [64, 65]. The principal component analysis revealed the presence of four factors with eigenvalues exceeding one. However, there was a significant drop in the variance explained after the third factor. Therefore, a three-factor model was considered as the eigenvalues were larger than the first three 95th percentile random data eigenvalues [28]. The eigenvalues of the three-factor 15-item HDRS explained 54.16% of the total variance in psychological hardiness among the police personnel sample.

Discussion

The study aimed to assess the psychometric properties (reliability and validity) of the Hindi-translated versions of the Dispositional Resilience Scale (HDRS-15) [29] and the Cognitive Flexibility Scale (CFS-H) [35] among police personnel. The psychometric properties of the measures were evaluated in other countries, however, there were no specific studies evaluating these scales in the Indian context. As the majority of the population in the Northern region of India is well-versed in the Hindi language [51], the measures were translated in Hindi using the back-translation procedure [47, 48] and administered to police personnel.

While the HDRS-15 is a widely utilized scale to measure psychological hardiness, multiple studies have raised concerns related to its psychometric robustness and validity, specifically poor factor structure, construct validity, and low reliability of sub-components among the adult population [31, 71, 72]. The current study, however, contradicts these findings, demonstrating the satisfactory reliability of the scale and and factor structure and providing acceptable model fit indices.

The results indicated that the HDRS-15 scale was reliable and valid among the sample of Rajasthan police personnel, with acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α and McDonald’s ), EFA results, and model fit indices (via CFA). The results also showed good internal consistency for the three components of the measure among police personnel. The HDRS-15 scale tends to show acceptable reliability values (α = 0.75; = 0.69) for the overall scale and a similar result to that of the Norwegian version (α = 0.79) [28], Italian version (α = 0.73) [30], and Chinese version (α = 0.78) [25]. However, the scale structure of the Chinese version differed from the current study, as the Chinese Dispositional Resilience Scale had different names of the factors (commitment, control-adaptation, and positivity) and a different bifurcation of items as compared to the original DRS-15. Among the three components of the HDRS-15 scale, the commitment component tends to illustrate the highest value (α = 0.80; = 0.77) as compared to the other two factors. Studies have shown that police officers who exhibit hardiness, particularly hardiness commitment, have lower levels of psychological distress and depression [73] and have been found to be a powerful predictor of resistance to levels of burnout and other physical symptoms [74]. The Chinese and Brazilian versions also yielded the highest reliability coefficient for the commitment factor [25, 31], while the Korean version recorded a similar reliability value for the commitment factor [32]. The EFA identified three factor structures, consistent with the original version [24] and the Norwegian version [28], with individual loadings and factor correlations for the HDRS-15 scale. The total variance of 54.16% was extracted for the three-factor HDRS-15, similar to the Norwegian version [28], which accounted for 52.6% variance. Individual factors accounted for 23.95% (factor 1), 16.80% (factor 2), and 13.40% (factor 3) variances, respectively. Discriminant validity of the scale was also established through inter-item correlation and found the HTMT ratio below the normed value of 0.90 [66]. The AVE and CR of the components were computed to find convergent validity of the measure. The AVE for HDRS-15 was found to show moderate convergent validity as the values were slightly below the threshold level of 0.50 [58, 64] but deemed acceptable as the corresponding CR values were well above the 0.70 benchmark [58, 67–69]. This was found to be contradicting with the findings of the Korean version [32], which reported higher AVE values for the measure.

Following EFA, CFA was utilized to calculate normed χ2, df, RMR, CFI, GFI, TLI, RMSEA, and AGFI to measure construct validity of the three models (three-component model, three-component model with error covariance (e2 & e4), and three-component model after item 4 (CM2) exclusion). The CFA results indicated that Models B and C exhibited acceptable and satisfactory model fit indices, in line with the norms [53, 70]. This tends to show similar patterns of CFA results to those found in the Chinese and Korean versions of DRS-15, with both showing unsatisfactory results in Model A and satisfactory output in Model B and C [25, 32].

In line with fitting the models, the modification indices given by SPSS AMOS recommended that one should allow error covariance between two commitment items, that are, item 4 ‘I feel that my life is somewhat empty of meaning’ and item 10 ‘Most days, life is really interesting and exciting for me.’ Due to frequent perceived repetition in item content or indication of non-random error due to method effects, error correlation pairs can be justified [28, 75]. Therefore, the inclusion of error correlation can be theoretically justified for the said items based on semantic overlap and item redundancy, as both items reflect the same mental set. Furthermore, item 4 was suggested by AMOS to provide an overall improved model fit of the scale based on corrected inter-item correlation, similar to the Brazilian [31] and Korean versions of the scale [32]. Therefore, after performing CFA again following exclusion of item 4, which revealed no change in the model fit indices, Model B was recommended as final model of the study.

The Cognitive Flexibility Scale is a widely used inventory to measure cognitive flexibility. The results have established the psychometric properties of the CFS-H measure among the sample of police personnel. It has reported a high and satisfactory internal consistency (reliability) similar to other versions including original English (α = 0.83) [35], Japanese (α = 0.84; = 0.87) [42], Arabic (α = 0.80) [43], and Italian ( = 0.83) [44], while better reliability than the Turkish version (α = 0.74) [41]. EFA has identified the presence of one global factor in the scale, similar to the other version [35, 41–44], which confirms the unidimensionality of the measure and reports the total variance in line with the Japanese [42], Arabic [43], and Turkish [41] versions of the scale. Moreover, the EFA of the measure revealed low factor loadings for three items (item 2, item 5 and item 10). However, item 2 was retained as its inter-item correlation was within the significant range, as compared to item 5 and item 10, which were removed due to insignificant inter-item correlation.

Both, item 5 ‘I seldom have choices when deciding how to behave’ and item 10 ‘I have difficulty using my knowledge on a given topic in real life situations’ are reverse-coded items which, frequently introduce respondent bias or method effects as part of linguistic artifacts [75] along with semantic ambiguities. Theoretically, item 5 inadvertently focused on the external locus of control rather than the awareness aspect, while item 10 may incline more towards social competence rather than self-efficacy. This was found in accordance with the Japanese version [42], wherein three items were found to show relatively low factor loadings, while in the Turkish version [41], one item showed relatively low loading and excluding them resulted in improved total variance.

CFA of the measure revealed three models: Model A (the original 12-item model), Model B (exclusion of items 5 and 10), and Model C (exclusion of items 5 and 10, allowing error covariances). AMOS had suggested three error pairs of error covariances for modification in model indices, including e2 and e3 (item 2 and 3), e7 and e8 (item 7 and 8), and e11 and e12 (item 11 and 12). The shared residual variance between item 2 and item 3 can be attributable to method effects due to the reverse-coded nature and semantic overlap [75] as both items tap behaviours related to restriction or withdrawal. Item 7 and item 8 both tap self-regulated behaviour in everyday situations and fall under the realm of the self-efficacy component of cognitive flexibility, which may explain their shared residual variance. Lastly, the shared residual variance between item 11 and item 10 can be attributed to the ‘action’ phase of cognitive flexibility and taps into behaviours dealing with solving everyday problems in a positive way.

Based on CFA results of CFS-H, Model B showed permissible fit indices, while Model C showed acceptable and satisfactory fit indices, establishing the construct validity of the scale [53, 70]. The results of the study were in line with the findings of the Japanese version [42] and Arabic version [43] of the measure. In contrast, they were contrary to the original English version [35] and Italian version [44], which established the construct validity of the measure in a single model. The current study, thus, recommends the Hindi version of CFS with 10-items as it provided better factorial validity in the sample of police personnel.

The findings provide preliminary evidence for the psychometric properties (reliability and validity) of the Hindi translated dispositional resilience scale and cognitive flexibility scale for assessing psychological hardiness and cognitive flexibility, respectively, in a sample of police personnel from Rajasthan. Both measures are closely linked with stress, burnout, depression, and other issues across various high-demand jobs like teaching, military, nursing, policing and many other professions. Utilizing HDRS-15 can help establish hardiness as a protective factor against stressors, a moderator in buffering their effects, and a predictor of positive psychological attributes, such as mental well-being, flourishing levels, and quality of life among Indian police personnel. HDRS-15 can also be helpful in screening and selecting suitable candidates for specific jobs that require satisfactory performance under stressful circumstances. CFS-H can help assess the adaptability and problem-solving attributes of individuals under stress, aiding in improving positive psychological functioning.

However, the study comprises a few limitations. Firstly, the current study consisted of data from police personnel from Rajasthan state, therefore, there may be limitations in generalizing these results to police personnel from other states and other occupations. Although the current sample in the study yielded satisfactory results, it would be beneficial to consider a larger sample for improved reliability, factor loadings, and overall outcome [76] in future studies. Low values of the AVE were found for both measures, therefore, future research should focus on refining the measures and items to enhance their psychometric robustness. Secondly, the study used a cross-sectional design to collect the data. A longitudinal design can be used in future studies to collect and compare data across time. Thirdly, the sample was disproportionate, as the majority of the participants were male respondents. Thus, further work should include an adequate sample of female participants in the study, which can help in comparing gender differences.

Conclusion

The HDRS-15 showed good and adequate reliability and validity among the sample of Indian police personnel. The Dispositional Resilience Scale, being concise and easy to complete and score, can be a valuable assessment tool for researchers interested in evaluating the construct of psychological hardiness in relation to stress-related factors, well-being, flourishing, satisfaction with job, and overall quality of life across various fields of psychology. The CFS-H demonstrated good reliability and, with some item modifications, established construct validity among police personnel. The Cognitive Flexibility Scale, being both brief and straightforward to complete and score, demonstrates its utility in evaluating the cognitive flexibility of individuals in relation to coping with stressful circumstances, decision-making, problem-solving, and enhancing self-efficacy. Further validation of both measures in other regions, occupations, and gender balanced samples will enhance the generalizability of the findings across diverse populations.
