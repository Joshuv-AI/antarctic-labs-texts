Search-based Generation of Waypoints for Triggering Self-Adaptations in Maritime Autonomous Vessels
Karoline Nylænder, Aitor Arrieta, Shaukat Ali, Paolo Arcaini

Abstract
Self-adaptation in maritime autonomous vessels (AVs) enables them to adapt their behaviors to address unexpected situations while maintaining dependability requirements. During the design of such AVs, it is crucial to understand and identify the settings that should trigger adaptations, enabling validation of their implementation. To this end, we focus on the navigation software of AVs, which must adapt their behavior during operation through adaptations. AVs often rely on predefined waypoints to guide them along designated routes, ensuring safe navigation. We propose a multi-objective search-based approach, called WPgen, to generate minor modifications to the predefined set of waypoints, keeping them as close as possible to the original waypoints, while causing the AV to navigate inappropriately when navigating with the generated waypoints. WPgen uses NSGA-II as the multi-objective search algorithm with three seeding strategies for its initial population, resulting in three variations of WPgen. We evaluated these variations on three AVs (one overwater tanker and two underwater). We compared the three variations of WPgen with Random Search as the baseline and with each other. Experimental results showed that the effectiveness of these variations varied depending on the AV. Based on the results, we present the research and practical implications of WPgen.

1
Introduction

Self-adaptation enables systems to handle unforeseen situations while maintaining dependable operation. Implementing such self-adaptation in robotic software is a key objective of a large European project (
RoboSAPIENS
), within the framework of which the work reported in this paper is being conducted. The project focuses on extending the well-established MAPE-K self-adaptation loop
[
1
]
and applying it to various real-world industrial robotic software systems, enabling them to adapt effectively to unexpected situations. This paper focuses on one real-world application provided by the project, i.e., maritime autonomous vessels (AVs).

This paper considers the
navigation software
of the AV, as this is one of the most critical of its software components. This software typically relies on a navigation method using waypoints to help the AV accurately and safely generate its path to the intended destination
[
2
]
. When an AV follows its generated path, various conditions, such as environmental factors, sensor errors, or software malfunctions, can require it to adapt its behavior. Another type of event that may require adaptation is the presence of waypoints different from those the AV was supposed to follow. Indeed, the navigation software may not be properly tuned to handle these waypoints, and it may require an adaptation. This paper explicitly focuses on identifying waypoints that should trigger adaptations to prevent navigation software from malfunctioning. In particular, we propose a multi-objective search approach, called
WPgen
, to generate a set of waypoints by introducing minor modifications to an existing set of waypoints that should trigger adaptations. To guide the search, we defined two objectives, i.e., minimizing the variations of the original waypoints and maximizing the path length to generate waypoints. We employed NSGA-II as the multi-objective search algorithm in
WPgen
and implemented three strategies to seed its initial population, resulting in three variations of
WPgen
.

Our work focuses on generating minor variations to predefined waypoints and is thus related to test scenario generation for AVs. To this end, scenario-based simulation testing is commonly used
[

, with a specific focus on collision detection. Such methods often generate a limited number of scenarios (often manually created) under fixed settings

. Recent approaches have begun exploring techniques, such as machine learning

, random testing

[

[

To evaluate

Real-world context

RoboSAPIENS

[
12
]
is a large European initiative focused on developing self-adaptive software for robotic applications across several domains, including maritime, manufacturing, and service. The project aims to develop novel methods for testing and implementing robotic software adaptations in unforeseen situations, enabling robots to handle such situations during real-world operations. The project collaborates with research organizations and industrial partners, providing real-world robotic software use cases for evaluating the developed methods. This paper focuses on our use case from the maritime domain, i.e., AVs. These AVs operate in the marine environment and are subject to rapid changes, e.g., in wind and ocean currents, resulting in uncertain AV behavior that can lead to unsafe situations.

The long-term goal of the project is to implement self-adaptation using the MAPLE-K
[
13
]
(see
Figure

1

[
1
]

A typical MAPE-K loop works as follows: the
M

A

P

The project is currently in its early phase, focusing on implementing the MAPLE-K loop in its robotic software, while our work, in parallel, aims to develop an approach to test the MAPLE-K loop. Specifically, it generates modified waypoints that should trigger relevant adaptations, thereby assessing whether the Plan and Legitimate components can successfully select and verify appropriate adaptations, respectively.

3
Approach

3.1
Approach Overview

We represent an AV and its associated
navigation software
as
\mathcal{V}
. In particular, we consider
waypoint-based
navigation software that guides the AV through a set of predefined
waypoints
. A
waypoint

\mathit{wp}
is a coordinated destination point. For an overwater AV, a waypoint is defined in a North-East coordinate system with two dimensions (i.e.,
\mathit{wp}_{i}=(x_{i},

y_{i})
). Similarly, for an underwater AV, the waypoint is defined in a North-East-Down coordinate system with 3 dimensions (i.e.,
\mathit{wp}_{i}=(x_{i},

y_{i},

z_{i})
). A path is defined as a set of
\mathit{N}
waypoints
\mathcal{WP}=\{\mathit{wp}_{1},

\ldots,

\mathit{wp}_{\mathit{N}}\}
that the AV
\mathcal{V}
must navigate through.

Waypoints placement must ensure that the AV has sufficient space to navigate smoothly between them. Specifically, each pair of consecutive waypoints
\mathit{wp}_{i}
and
\mathit{wp}_{i+1}
cannot be closer than
\mathit{minWPdist}
. The value of
\mathit{minWPdist}
depends on the specific AV.

Our approach
WPgen
generates waypoints representing possible alterations of the original set of waypoints
\mathcal{WP}_{o}=\{\mathit{wp}^{o}_{1},

\ldots,

\mathit{wp}^{o}_{\mathit{N}}\}
that the AV
\mathcal{V}
is originally supposed to follow. These variations could occur during navigation due to environmental disturbances such as wind and sea current, or obstacles that block the planned path.

The goal of
WPgen
is to find a set of modified waypoints that are challenging for the current navigation software
\mathcal{V}
, i.e., that lead to obtaining
unstable
paths of the AV. These sets of waypoints will be used to implement a self-adaptive navigation software that can, at runtime, safely self-adapt to these runtime variations. Note that implementing such self-adaptive navigation software is not part of this work. This implementation is being carried out by our industrial partners in
RoboSAPIENS
, in parallel with this work.

WPgen
is cast as a multi-objective optimization problem that is described in detail in the following sections.

3.2
Solution Encoding

In
WPgen
, the
search variables

\overline{k}
represent a set of
\mathit{N}-1
consecutive waypoints that define the modified set of waypoints the AV
\mathcal{V}
must follow during navigation. We do not search for the first waypoint that is the same as that of
\mathcal{WP}_{o}
(i.e.,
\mathit{wp}^{o}_{1}
), as it is the vessel’s current starting point and it must be common to all the
\mathcal{V}
’s paths.

For each searched waypoint, there are two or three variables, depending on whether the search is conducted for an overwater or an underwater AV. For simplicity, in the following, we consider an overwater AV (the formalization for an underwater AV is similar); so, the search variables are
\overline{k}=\{k_{2,x},

k_{2,y},

\ldots,

k_{\mathit{N},x},

k_{\mathit{N},y}\}
, where
(k_{i,x},

k_{i,y})
defines the
i
th waypoint.

A
solution
(or
individual
) is a concrete assignment
\overline{v}=\{v_{x,2},

v_{y,2},

\ldots,

v_{x,\mathit{N}},

v_{y,\mathit{N}}\}
, that defines the modified waypoints
\mathit{wp}_{2}=(v_{x,2},v_{y,2})
,
\ldots
,
\mathit{wp}_{\mathit{N}}=(v_{x,\mathit{N}},v_{y,\mathit{N}})
. So, together with the starting waypoint
\mathit{wp}^{o}_{1}
, these define the set of waypoints
\mathcal{WP}=\{\mathit{wp}^{o}_{1},

\mathit{wp}_{2},

\ldots,

\mathit{wp}_{\mathit{N}}\}
that will be executed in the AV’s simulator MSS as test for the navigation software.

Since the generated set of waypoints
\mathcal{WP}
must be similar to the original set of waypoints
\mathcal{WP}_{o}=\{\mathit{wp}^{o}_{1},

\ldots,

\mathit{wp}^{o}_{\mathit{N}}\}
, the search space of variables
\overline{k}
is defined in the neighborhood of the values of
\mathcal{WP}_{o}=

\{(v^{o}_{x,1},v^{o}_{y,1}),

\ldots,

(v^{o}_{x,\mathit{N}},v^{o}_{y,\mathit{N}})\}
. Specifically, for each pair of variables
k_{i,x}
,
k_{i,y}
(with
i\in\{2,\ldots,\mathit{N}\}
), their search spaces are
[v^{o}_{x,i}-\Delta,v^{o}_{x,i}+\Delta]
and
[v^{o}_{y,i}-\Delta,v^{o}_{y,i}+\Delta]
, where
\Delta
is a hyperparameter of the approach.

As explained in
Section

3.1
, consecutive waypoints must be at least at a distance of
\mathit{minWPdist}
. Solutions generated by
WPgen
could violate such constraint. Therefore, for each individual,
WPgen
checks if the constraint is satisfied and, if it is not, it discards the individual (i.e., following a death penalty approach
[
14
]
).

3.4
Objective Functions

Given a set of waypoints
\mathcal{WP}
obtained from an individual
\overline{v}
(see
Section

3.2
), the approach simulates the navigation of AV
\mathcal{V}
over
\mathcal{WP}
, and obtains the path
\mathit{p}
followed by
\mathcal{V}
, i.e.,

\mathit{p}=\{(x^{p}_{1},y^{p}_{1}),\ldots,(x^{p}_{\mathit{K}},y^{p}_{\mathit{K}})\}=\mathit{simulate}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{WP})

(1)

If the AV managed to follow all the waypoints in
\mathcal{WP}
,
\mathit{p}
can be split in
\mathit{N}-1

sub-paths

\mathit{sp}_{1}
, …,
\mathit{sp}_{\mathit{N}-1}
, i.e., the sections of
\mathit{p}
between each consecutive pair of waypoints. Note that the AV may fail to follow all the waypoints: in that case, the number of sub-paths is lower than
\mathit{N}-1
.

WPgen
has two competing objectives that capture the overall goal of the approach, i.e., finding the minimal variations of the original waypoints
\mathcal{WP}_{o}
that are challenging for the AV.

The first objective aims at minimizing the distance between
\mathcal{WP}_{o}=

\{(v^{o}_{x,1},v^{o}_{y,1}),

\ldots,

(v^{o}_{x,\mathit{N}},v^{o}_{y,\mathit{N}})\}
and the set of waypoints
\mathcal{WP}=\{(v_{x,1},v_{y,1}),

\ldots,

(v_{x,\mathit{N}},

v_{y,\mathit{N}})\}
obtained from an individual
1
1

1

With an abuse of notation, we compute the fitness on the set of waypoints
\mathcal{WP}
obtained from an individual
\overline{v}
.
:

\mathit{fit_{distWPs}}(\mathcal{WP})=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{\mathit{N}}((v^{o}_{x,i}-v_{x,i})^{2}+(v^{o}_{y,i}-v_{y,i})^{2})}

(2)

The second objective aims at finding
unstable paths
. Intuitively, a path is more unstable if it largely deviates from the optimal path composed of stable segments between consecutive waypoints. So, the fitness function that must be maximized is defined as follows:

\mathit{fit_{unstable}}(\mathcal{WP})=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathit{N}-1}\frac{|\mathit{sp}_{i}|}{\sqrt{(v^{o}_{x,i+1}-v^{o}_{x,i})^{2}+(v^{o}_{y,i+1}-v^{o}_{y,i})^{2}}}

(3)

where
\mathit{p}=\{\mathit{sp}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{sp}_{\mathit{N}-1}\}=\mathit{simulate}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{WP})
. The intuition is that the length of each sub-path
\mathit{sp}_{i}
(approximated by the number of its points) is normalized by the Euclidean distance between
\mathit{wp}^{o}_{i}=(v^{o}_{x,i},v^{o}_{y,i})
and
\mathit{wp}^{o}_{i+1}=(v^{o}_{x,i+1},v^{o}_{y,i+1})
: the higher this value, the more unstable the sub-path is. The fitness is given by the sum of the unstability value of each sub-path.

3.5
Search Operators

WPgen
uses classical simulated binary crossover and polynomial mutation operators with the default settings from PlatEMO
[
18
]
– the framework we used for the implementation of NSGA-II while adapting them for our specific problem’s constraints.

In the crossover operation that recombines two individuals, we guarantee that the randomly selected crossover point occurs only between waypoints. This avoids splitting a waypoint, which could otherwise introduce too big changes to the waypoints.

The mutation operator performs changes at a coordinate level. It randomly changes the coordinate with a specified default probability from PlatEMO. While performing mutation, we check whether the constraint given by the solution encoding (see
Section

3.2
) is satisfied, i.e., consecutive waypoints must be at least at a distance of
\mathit{minWPdist}
; otherwise, the mutation is not applied.

4
Empirical Evaluation Design

4.1
Research Questions

We aim to study the effectiveness of the
WPgen
(with its three different seeding strategies) compared to a simple baseline, i.e., Random Search (
RS
). Moreover, we aim to study the characteristics of unexpected paths followed by AVs in response to waypoints generated by
WPgen
.
Consequently, we would like to answer the following research questions (RQs):

RQ1: How effective is
WPgen
compared to random search (
RS
)?

This RQ studies whether the problem we study is complex enough to require the sophisticated multi-objective algorithm NSGA-II or if it can be solved with a simple baseline technique, i.e.,
RS
.
To this end, we compared
RS
with the three versions of
WPgen
(i.e., for the three seeding strategies) with three different case studies.
To perform the analysis, we performed a pairwise comparison between
RS
and each of the
WPgen
variations utilizing statistical tests.

RQ2: How do different seeding strategies affect the effectiveness of the
WPgen
?

With this RQ, we aim to answer how the different seeding strategies affect
WPgen
’s effectiveness. To answer this RQ, we compared the effectiveness of the three seeding strategies,
WPgen
{}_{\text{{seed}}}
,
WPgen
{}_{\text{{comb}}}
and
WPgen
{}_{\text{{rnd}}}
, for each case study by utilizing statistical tests.

RQ3: How good is
WPgen
in generating paths leading to different types of unexpected paths?

This RQ studies the different path types generated by the three versions of
WPgen
. Studying this is important for understanding the characteristics of different unexpected paths, as such characterization helps determine which adaptations are required for each path type. To answer this RQ, we classified each case study and search approach’s sub-paths to get insight into the types of paths generated and differences in the different case studies.

To evaluate

MSS has been used in previous works to simulate ships, generating data for analysis

Our case studies are three AVs covering underwater and overwater AVs with different environments and a set of initial waypoints

Specifically, the selected AVs are the waypoint-guided AVs in MSS and comprise of one overwater tanker system,

The number of waypoints in

4.2.2
Search algorithms

We implemented

using the evolutionary multi-objective optimization platform PlatEMO

We utilized their implementation of NSGA-II

and adapted their crossover operator to our operator described in

To guide the search to

, all the initial populations of the different approaches contain one individual that is equal to

For our baseline algorithm, Random Search (

such that the total number of fitness evaluations is equal for

.

4.2.3
Parameter Settings

By following the guide of Arcuri and Briand
[
23
]
, each variation of
WPgen
and
RS
was run 30 times to account for the randomness of the search algorithms. Given that there are four approaches (the three seeding strategies of
WPgen
and

), in total we conducted 120 runs for each case study.

Due to the long execution time of the MSS simulator, an initial preliminary experiment was conducted to choose the most appropriate parameter settings, which involved analyzing the convergence of the Pareto Front.
The population size was set to 10, and the maximum number of generations to 1000, resulting in a total number of 10,000 fitness evaluations.
The default mutation operator and our slightly adapted crossover operator (see
Section

3.5
) were used with default settings from PlatEMO. Specifically, the probability of crossover and the expectation of the number of mutated variables were both set to 1, whereas the distribution index of crossover and mutation were both set to 20.

The hyperparameter

that defines the search space (see
Section

3.2
) was set to 400 and 150 for the overwater AV and underwater AVs, respectively. These values were set based on the AVs’ characteristics, such as AV length and original distance between waypoints.

4.3
Evaluation Metrics

4.3.1
Quality indicator

To assess the effectiveness of each approach, we employed the
hypervolume
(HV) metric
[
24
]
as our quality indicator.
HV measures the volume of the objective space covered by the Pareto front with respect to a reference point, which is chosen based on the worst value observed across all approaches and experiments.
A higher HV value indicates better coverage and, thus, better performance and diversity of an approach.

4.3.2

The stability/unstabilty of an AV can be observed by measuring how the angles of the AV change, such as rolling from side to side.
To detect this unstable behavior, we calculate the autocorrelation of the axis measurements to quantify the instability and differentiate between a stable measurement and an unstable one by calculating the number of peaks and the magnitude of each peak. We counted one angle as unstable if it had more than one threshold peak above 0.1
[
26
]

1.

stable
: all of the angles are stable,

2.

unstable
: at least one of the angles is unstable,
and

3.

missing
: the vessel fails to reach a waypoint.

Using the previous classification sub-paths, we have a metric for describing the full path. Specifically, given the
\mathit{N}-1
sub-paths of a path, a full path can have
2^{\mathit{N}-1}+2(\mathit{N}-2)
different categorizations.

6
Discussion

6.1
Research and Practical Implications

Based on the results from RQ1, we found that all three variations of
WPgen
either perform significantly better than
RS
or show no significant differences, depending on the AV. Similarly, results for RQ2 showed differences among the approaches for different AVs. These results suggest that the selection of a particular
WPgen
depends on the AV being tested.
For
Mariner
, while there were no significant differences among the three variations of
WPgen
, all were significantly better than
RS
, indicating that any variation of
WPgen
is sufficient for testing
Mariner
. For
Remus100
,
WPgen
proved to be the best, making it the natural choice for testing.
For
Nspauv
, there were no significant differences between
RS
and the three variations of
WPgen
, meaning that even
RS
is sufficient for testing
Nspauv
. Although we now have preliminary indications for selecting approaches, it raises further questions about why a specific approach is better for a particular AV. This requires additional empirical evaluations that consider more AV characteristics. Such a study is part of our future work.

From RQ3, we found that approaches incorporating randomness in the initial population outperformed those that fully seed the initial population for both underwater and overwater AVs. Therefore, such approaches—
WPgen
{}_{\text{{comb}}}
and
WPgen
{}_{\text{{rnd}}}
—are recommended for identifying waypoints that should trigger adaptations in self-adaptive AVs. From RQ3, we also found that
WPgen
{}_{\text{{seed}}}
generally identified fewer unique full paths than
WPgen
{}_{\text{{comb}}}
and
WPgen
{}_{\text{{rnd}}}
. These results were expected, as
WPgen
{}_{\text{{seed}}}
is designed to enable the search algorithm to focus around the seeded waypoints. Depending on the context, a tester may want to focus only on a given set of waypoints; in that case,
WPgen
{}_{\text{{seed}}}
is recommended. However, it naturally lacks the diverse coverage the other two approaches provide. On the other hand, if a tester seeks more diverse paths,
WPgen
{}_{\text{{comb}}}
or
WPgen
{}_{\text{{rnd}}}
are recommended.

In general, we conclude that
WPgen
can find unstable paths close to the original trajectory
\mathcal{WP}_{o}
. Thus, our results bring new knowledge about the importance of
\mathcal{WP}
generation and analysis of AVs. Furthermore, the waypoints that result in unstable paths can be utilized for different purposes. First, these waypoints can be used to trigger different adaptations in self-adaptive AVs to verify whether correct adaptations are triggered and implemented. Second, further analysis can be conducted to identify any patterns that explain why these
\mathcal{WP}
specifically result in unstable paths and how such
\mathcal{WP}
can be avoided in the future. Finally, our methods for categorizing the sub-paths can be further utilized to create more advanced fitness functions to further enhance the performance of
WPgen
.

Our approach
WPgen
can be used to pre-evaluate a set of waypoints
\mathcal{WP}
(constructed as minor variations of the original ones
\mathcal{WP}_{o}
), and determine the safety of the AV. Detection of a probable unstable path can be utilized as feedback to the system, letting the AV adjust its controllers to get a smoother and safer path. A set of waypoints
\mathcal{WP}
that lead to a path having unstable or missing sub-paths, can be utilized for different AV navigation software to compare and determine if it is better at handling
\mathcal{WP}
. Navigation software can also utilize the characteristics of the
\mathcal{WP}
to recognize critical situations that require adaptation.

6.2
Threats to Validity

Internal validity.

An internal validity threat is related to the parameters of the selected algorithm, i.e., NSGA-II, which is the core part of the three variations of
WPgen
. To mitigate this threat, we used the default crossover and mutation operators with default parameter settings from PlatEMO. These parameters were then kept the same for the three variations of
WPgen
for a fair comparison. One could argue that we selected only one algorithm, i.e., NSGA-II; however, it is widely used in search-based software engineering, and we naturally plan to investigate additional algorithms in the future.

External validity

We experimented with three AVs of different characteristics in a fixed environment. Thus, the results may not generalize to other AVs or environments. This highlights the need for additional empirical evaluations, which, along with exploring other search algorithms, will form the basis of our future studies.

Conclusion validity

All search algorithms have inherent randomness. To address this, we executed each approach and
RS
30 times, following a well-established guide
[
23
]
. Additionally, to ensure greater confidence in the results, we analyzed them using well-established statistical tests as recommended in the same guide.

Construct validity

A concern is related to measures used to draw conclusions. For example, to automatically classify paths, we used autocorrelation; however, other metrics, such as zero-crossing or frequency analysis, also exist. We did not select these metrics because they were less precise and misclassified some stable paths as unstable during our exploratory study. Another concern relates to the stopping criteria, which in our context was defined as the number of fitness evaluations. Based on an exploratory study, this was set to 10,000 and kept consistent across all approaches.

7
Related Work

In this work, we proposed an approach to generate tests exposing cases in which an AV follows unstable paths. Such cases will be considered during the development of a self-adaptive AV in the context of our project (see
Section

2
). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first approach proposed for this goal. On the other hand, in the self-adaptive community
[
27
,
28
]
, some works have been proposed to test the self-adaptive system itself. Hänsel et al.
[
29
]
proposed a model-based testing approach for MAPE-K that relies on architectural runtime models (RTM). Specifically, the approach checks at runtime the conformance between the running self-adaptive system and the oracle RTM. Eberhardinger et al.
[
30
]
proposed an approach based on model checking to generate test cases for self-adaptive systems. In order to make the approach scalable, they simplify the system using the Corridor Enforcing Infrastructure (CEI) architectural pattern. Arcaini et al.
[
31
]
also proposed an approach based on model checking to derive test cases from self-adaptive ASMs, a formal notation for the analysis of self-adaptive systems. Fredericks et al.
[
32
,
33
]
, instead, proposed online testing techniques able to better adjust to the uncertainty of the environment.

Our work is also related to Search-Based Software Engineering (SBSE), which formulates software engineering problems as optimization problems and solves them using search algorithms, such as single-objective and multi-objective search algorithms
[
9
]
. Since its inception, SBSE has been applied to various software engineering sub-areas, e.g., requirements engineering, modeling, testing, and debugging
[
34
]
. Within SBSE, Search-Based Testing (SBT) focuses specifically on testing software systems using search algorithms, which is the focus of this paper. SBT has been applied to testing a variety of systems, including autonomous driving systems
[
35
]
, autonomous robots
[
36
]
, unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs)
[
37
]
, automated warehouses
[
38
]
, and sports-related cyber-physical systems
[
38
]
. Despite the applications of SBT in various domains, its application for testing the navigation software of AVs remains understudied. To this end, this paper presents an application of SBT to generate tests (i.e., minor variations in the predefined waypoints) that should trigger adaptations in AVs.

Scenario-based testing with simulators is one of the key methods for testing AVs at design time
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
, which is also related to
WPgen
. Most scenario-based testing works have primarily focused on collision avoidance. Furthermore, existing works often generate a limited number of scenarios with fixed settings, and such generation is often manual, as concluded in a literature review
by
Porres et al. 2020a

[
3
]
.
Nonetheless, the need for scenario-based testing is being been recognized, and recent works have explored more systematic and automated approaches to simulation-based testing for collision avoidance, such as using machine learning techniques
[
4
,
5
]
, random testing
[
7
]
, or a search-based approach
[
8
]
. Our work distinguishes itself from existing works by generating tests that should trigger adaptation in AVs. Specifically, we generate variations of predefined waypoints with SBT, so reducing the manual effort required to identify variations that could potentially impact the AV’s ability to follow its intended path.

8
Conclusions and Future Work

We presented a multi-objective search approach,
WPgen
, to generate tests that should trigger adaptations in maritime autonomous vessels (AVs), where each test is a slight variation of the pre-defined waypoints that the AV must follow during navigation. The approach was further varied into three versions, each employing a different seeding strategy to study how initial population seeding affects performance. We compared the three variations with
RS
and with each other. Based on the results, we found that, in general,
WPgen
either performs significantly better than
RS
or similarly. Moreover, the best-performing variation of the approach depends on the AV. In the future, we will extend
WPgen
from several perspectives, including defining domain-specific mutation and crossover operators. Moreover, the empirical evaluation will be extended with more algorithms, different environmental setups, and similar systems, such as unmanned aerial vehicles. Finally, we also want to explore machine learning algorithms to detect unstable paths, gain insights into the different characteristics of an unstable path, and determine the root cause of the unstable paths.
