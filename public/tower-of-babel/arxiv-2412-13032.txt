Characterization of the directed landscape from the KPZ fixed point
Duncan Dauvergne, Lingfu Zhang

Abstract
We show that the directed landscape is the unique coupling of the KPZ fixed point from all initial conditions satisfying three natural properties: independent increments, monotonicity, and shift commutativity. Equivalently, we show that the directed landscape is the unique directed metric on ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} with independent increments and KPZ fixed point marginals. This unifies the two central objects in the KPZ universality class. Our main theorem also provides a general framework for proving convergence to the directed landscape given convergence to the KPZ fixed point. We apply this framework to prove landscape convergence in a range of models: exotic couplings of ASEP and TASEP, the random walk and Brownian web distances, and a class of non-integrable asymmetric exclusion processes with the basic coupling that perturb off of TASEP (this final class requires random initial data). All of our convergence theorems are new except for colored TASEP, where we provide a short alternative proof.

Introduction

The KPZ (Kardar-Parisi-Zhang) universality class is a broad family of one-dimensional random growth models and two-dimensional random metric or polymer models that exhibit the same behaviour under rescaling. The past twenty-five years have seen a period of
intense and fruitful research on this class, propelled by the discovery of a handful of exactly solvable models, including certain exclusion processes, directed polymers, last passage percolation, and the KPZ equation itself. For background, we refer the reader to

[
Qua12
,
Cor12
,
Rom15
,
BG16
,
Zyg22
,
Gan22
]
and references therein.

The KPZ fixed point is the universal scaling limit for growth models in the KPZ class, and the directed landscape is the universal scaling limit for metrics and polymer models. The KPZ fixed point can be constructed as a marginal of the directed landscape, and one may optimistically hope the reverse is also true: that the directed landscape can be constructed from the KPZ fixed point.
This is the goal of the present paper.

Random growth and the KPZ fixed point.

To describe the KPZ fixed point, we first consider one particular growth model: the asymmetric simple exclusion process (ASEP). ASEP is an interacting particle system on
\mathbb{Z}
, but for our purposes here it will be more convenient to define it directly as a height function evolution (see Section
7
for the connection to the particle system). Define

\operatorname{SRW}=\{h:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}:h(i)-h(i-1)=\pm 1\text{ for all }i\}.

This is the space of two-sided simple random walk paths on
\mathbb{Z}
. ASEP is a Markov process
(h_{t},t\geq 0)
on
\operatorname{SRW}
where

h_{t^{-}}(x)\mapsto h_{t}(x)+2

at rate
p
if
x\in\mathbb{Z}
is a local maximum of
h_{t^{-}}
and
h_{t^{-}}(x)\mapsto h_{t}(x)-2
at rate
p+1
if
x\in\mathbb{Z}
is a local minimum of
h_{t^{-}}
(here
h_{t^{-}}=\lim_{s\to t^{-}}h_{s}
). This is a natural interface growth model, where there is an asymmetry (depending on
p
) causing the interface to drift downwards at constant rate over time. We have normalized our rates the downward drift is speed
1/2
for any
p
.

The special case when
p=0
is referred to as TASEP, the totally asymmetric simple exclusion process. TASEP is one of the most tractable models in the KPZ universality class, and Matetski, Quastel, and Remenik
[
MQR21
]
showed that

h^{\epsilon}_{t}(x):=\epsilon^{1/2}h_{2\epsilon^{-3/2}t}(2\epsilon^{-1}x)+\epsilon^{-1}t

(1)

converges as
\epsilon\to 0
to a limiting Markov process
(\mathfrak{h}_{t},t\geq 0)
known as the
KPZ fixed point
. The state space for the KPZ fixed point we use in this paper is

\operatorname{UC}_{0}:=\Big\{f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}:f\text{ upper semicontinuous},f\not\equiv-\infty,\quad\limsup_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{f(x)}{x^{2}}\leq 0\Big\},

(2)

which is the largest space on which
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
is almost surely finite at all positive times, see
[
SV21
, Proposition 6.1]
.

Since the original work of
[
MQR21
]
, convergence to the KPZ fixed point has been verified for a handful of models, including intregrable last passage percolation (LPP) models
[
NQR20a
,
DV21b
]
, the KPZ equation and polymer models
[
Vir20
]
, certain non-integrable perturbations of TASEP
[
QS23
]
(see Theorem
7.6
for a precise statement), and more recently the stochastic six-vertex model and ASEP as described above
[
ACH24b
,
ACH24a
]
.

Random metrics and coupled random growth.

The KPZ universality class also certain
2

p

To make this discussion more concrete, we can consider Liggett’s basic coupling for ASEP
[
Lig76
]
. In the basic coupling, each point
x\in\mathbb{Z}
is associated to two Poisson processes on
\mathbb{R}
:
\Pi_{x,+}
, with intensity
p
, and
\Pi_{x,-}
, with intensity
p+1
. All Poisson processes are independent. Each copy
h_{t}
of ASEP will attempt the jump
h_{t^{-}}(x)\mapsto h_{t}(x)\pm 2
at each time in the Poisson process
\Pi_{x,\pm}
, succeeding if
h_{t^{-}}
has a local minimum/maximum or
x
. In brief, height functions jump together. This coupling is equivalent to colored or multi-type ASEP, see
[
AAV11
,
ACH24b
]
for background.

In the special case of TASEP, this coupling can be fully realized with a directed metric construction on
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}
.
For each
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, define
\Delta_{x}\in\SRW
by
\Delta_{x}(y)=-|x-y|
. This is the narrow wedge centred at
x
. Define

d_{\Pi}(x,s;y,t)=h_{t}(y;\Delta_{x},s),

(3)

for any
s\leq t
and
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Here the right hand side is the height function at time
t
and location
y
for TASEP started from
\Delta_{x}
at time
s
in the basic coupling through the Poisson clocks
\Pi=\{\Pi_{x,-}:x\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. Set
d_{\Pi}(x,s;y,t)=-\infty
for
t>s
. We have
d_{\Pi}(p;p)=0
for all
p\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}
and the function
d_{\Pi}
satisfies a reverse triangle inequality

d_{\Pi}(p;q)+d_{\Pi}(q;r)\leq d_{\Pi}(p,r).

(4)

This makes
d_{\Pi}
a directed metric, see Section
2
for background. Moreover, for any initial condition
h_{0}\in\SRW
, the TASEP evolution of
h_{0}
started at time
s
in the basic coupling governed by the clocks
\Pi
is given by the variational formula

h_{t}(\cdot;h_{0},s):=\max_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}[h_{0}(x)+d_{\Pi}(x,s;\cdot,t)].

(5)

We can actually define the metric
d_{\Pi}
using a notion of paths and path length. We say that a function
\gamma:[s,t]\to\mathbb{Z}
of bounded variation is a
path
from
(x,s)
to
(y,t)
if
\gamma(s)=x,\gamma(y)=t
, and we define its length

|\gamma|_{\Pi}=V(\gamma)+2\#\{r\in(s,t]:r\in\Pi_{\gamma(r),-}\},

where
V(\gamma)
is the total variation of
\gamma
. With this definition,
d_{\Pi}(p;q)
is the maximum length of a path from
p
to
q
. Almost surely, this maximum always exists, making
d_{\Pi}
a geodesic space. The metric
d_{\Pi}
is a prototypical random (directed) metric in the KPZ class: a planar metric built from paths defined through a field of i.i.d. random variables or a noise on the plane.

If we replace TASEP by a general ASEP in (
3
) then the triangle inequality (
4
) is still valid. However, the metric
d_{\Pi}
is no longer a geodesic space, and the variational formula (
5
) (which is closely related to the geodesic property) no longer holds.

Just as TASEP and other growth models converge to the KPZ fixed point, random metrics also have a KPZ scaling limit. Indeed, if we define

d_{\Pi}^{\epsilon}(x,s;y,t)=\epsilon^{1/2}d_{\Pi}(2\epsilon^{-1}x,2\epsilon^{-3/2}s;2\epsilon^{-1}y,2\epsilon^{-3/2}t)+\epsilon^{-1}(t-s)

then
d_{\Pi}^{\epsilon}
converges as
\epsilon\to 0
to the random function
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}\to\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}:=\{(x,s;y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:s<t\}
known as the
directed landscape
, constructed in
[
DOV22
]
two years after the construction of the KPZ fixed point and with a completely different method. For the basic coupling of TASEP and ASEP, directed landscape convergence was recently proven by Aggarwal, Corwin, and Hegde
[
ACH24b
]
. Since
[
DOV22
]
, convergence to the directed landscape has also been shown for classical last passage percolation models
[
DOV22
,
DV21b
]
, the KPZ equation
[
Wu23
]
, and the stochastic six-vertex model
[
ACH24b
]
. The directed landscape is connected to the KPZ fixed point through a limiting version of (
5
). For
f\in\operatorname{UC}_{0}
, the process

\mathfrak{h}_{t}f(x)=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}f(y)+\mathcal{L}(y,0;x,t)

(6)

is a KPZ fixed point started from
f
at time
0
, and so the directed landscape gives a coupling of the KPZ fixed point, simultaneously from all times and all initial conditions.

1.1
Main results: characterization theorems

The directed landscape contains much more information than the KPZ fixed point. Indeed, through formula (
6
) it gives a coupling of the KPZ fixed point simultaneously from all initial conditions with the same driving noise. Nonetheless, one may hope that the law of the directed landscape can still be characterized through the KPZ point. In this paper we provide such a characterization. Our main theorem shows that the directed landscape is the unique directed metric on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with independent increments and KPZ fixed point marginals.

Setting some notation, for an upper semicontinuous function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
, we write
(\mathfrak{h}_{t}f,t\geq 0)
for the KPZ fixed point evolution started from the initial condition
f
. When we use this notation for random
f
, we always assume that the KPZ fixed point evolution is independent of
f
. Here and throughout the paper, we write
\mathbb{Q}^{4}_{\uparrow}=\mathbb{Q}^{4}\cap\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}:\mathbb{Q}^{4}_{\uparrow}\to\mathbb{R}
is a random function satisfying:

1.

(Triangle inequality) For any
o=(x,s)
,
p=(y,r)
,
q=(z,t)\in\mathbb{Q}^{2}
with
s<r<t
, we have

\mathcal{M}(o;p)+\mathcal{M}(p;q)\leq\mathcal{M}(o;q)
.

2.

(Independent increments) For any
s_{1}<s_{2}<\cdots<s_{k}\in\mathbb{Q}
, the increments
\mathcal{M}(\cdot,s_{i};\cdot,s_{i+1}):\mathbb{Q}^{2}\to\mathbb{R},i=1,\dots,k-1
are independent.

3.

(KPZ fixed point marginals)
For any
p=(x,t)\in\mathbb{Q}^{2}
, and
y_{1},\dots,y_{k},s_{1},\dots,s_{k}\in\mathbb{Q}
with each
s_{i}>0
, we have

\{\mathcal{M}(p;p+(y_{i},s_{i}))\}_{i=1}^{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\{\mathfrak{h}_{s_{i}}\delta_{0}(y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{k}.

Here
\delta_{0}
is the narrow wedge initial condition given by
\delta_{0}(0)=0
and
\delta_{0}(x)=-\infty,x\neq 0
.

Then
\mathcal{M}
has a continuous extension to
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
which is a directed landscape.

Remark 1.2
.

1.

The choice of
\mathbb{Q}
is arbitrary, both in the above theorem and throughout the paper. We could replace it with any countable dense subset of
\mathbb{R}
. Note also that we could have replaced
\mathbb{Q}^{4}_{\uparrow}
with
\mathbb{Q}^{4}_{\uparrow}\cap(\mathbb{R}\times I)^{2}
for any open interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
; the proof does not change and we would recover a directed landscape defined only on the strip
\mathbb{R}\times I
.

2.

The KPZ fixed point marginal assumption can be alternately phrased as saying that for fixed
p\in\mathbb{Q}^{2}
, we have
\mathcal{M}(p;\cdot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}(p;\cdot)
. Johansson and Rahman
[
JR21
]
and Liu
[
Liu22
]
derived different exact formulas for this law, neither of which is a special case of the KPZ fixed point formula. Theorem
1.1
can alternately be viewed as a uniqueness theorem for the directed landscape given either the Johansson-Rahman or Liu multi-time formulas.

We also give an alternate version of
Theorem

1.1
that characterizes the directed landscape as the unique coupling of the KPZ fixed point satisfying certain natural conditions. This version, while not difficult to show given
Theorem

1.1
, may better demonstrate how the characterization links back to couplings of discrete models.

Let
\NW\subset\operatorname{UC}_{0}
be the set of functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
such that
\{x:f(x)>-\infty\}
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{Q}
, and
f(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathbb{Q}\cup\{-\infty\}
. In words,
\NW
is the set of all finite rational combinations of narrow wedges.

Theorem 1.3
.

Consider a family of random operators
\mathcal{K}_{s,t}:\NW\to\operatorname{UC}_{0},s\leq t\in\mathbb{Q}
such that:

1.

(KPZ fixed point marginals) For all
f\in\NW
and
s\leq t\in\mathbb{Q}
, we have
\mathcal{K}_{s,t}f\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathfrak{h}_{t-s}f
.

2.

(Independent increments) For any collection of disjoint intervals
[s_{i},t_{i}],i=1,\dots,k
, the operators
\mathcal{K}_{s_{i},t_{i}},i=1,\dots,k
are independent.

3.

(Monotonicity) For any
s\leq r<t\in\mathbb{Q}
and any
f,g\in\NW
, if
g\leq\mathcal{K}_{s,r}f
then
\mathcal{K}_{r,t}g\leq\mathcal{K}_{s,t}f
.

4.

(Shift commutativity) For all
s<t\in\mathbb{Q},f\in\NW,c\in\mathbb{Q}
,
\mathcal{K}_{s,t}(f+c)=\mathcal{K}_{s,t}f+c
.

Then there exists a directed landscape
\mathcal{L}
such that almost surely, for any
s<t\in\mathbb{Q},f\in\NW,x\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\mathcal{K}_{s,t}f(x)=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}f(y)+\mathcal{L}(y,s;x,t).

(7)

Remark 1.4
.

1.

As in the previous theorem, the time index set
\mathbb{Q}
could be replaced with
P\cap I
for any countable dense set
P
and any open interval
I
.
We could also replace
\mathbb{Q}
with any countable dense set when defining
\NW
.

2.

The marginal condition
1
in
Theorem

1.3
is different from condition
3
in
Theorem

1.1
: condition

3
in
Theorem

1.1
asks for matching multi-time marginals starting only from the narrow wedge initial condition, whereas
1
in
Theorem

1.3
asks only for fixed-time marginals, but from a dense set of initial conditions. In practice, these conditions are interchangeable; we have included the two versions for flexibility in applications.

3.

Finally, we point out that the existence of a variational formula of the form (
7
) for
some
field
\mathcal{L}
(not necessarily a directed landscape) is equivalent to saying that
\mathcal{K}_{s,t}
satisfies max-preservation:

\mathcal{K}_{s,t}(f\vee g)=\mathcal{K}_{s,t}f\vee\mathcal{K}_{s,t}g.

Max-preservation fails for many prelimiting models (e.g. ASEP in the basic coupling), and we do not have a simple argument for how it arises in the limit. Rather, we prove this through utilizing the full power of Theorem
1.1
and the variational characterization (
6
) of the law of the KPZ fixed point.

This point is related to the fact that there are many natural prelimiting couplings of random growth models that do not arise from an underlying geodesic space. One upshot of Theorem
5.2
is that geodesics
emerge
in the limit as long as we started from an underlying coupling with (approximate versions of) the independent increment, monotonicity, and shift commutativity properties.

In the statement of
Theorem

1.3
, we have restricted our initial conditions to the (essentially arbitrary) dense set
\NW

Corollary 1.5
.

In the setting of Theorem

, consider

satisfying

\limsup_{z\to x,z\in\mathbb{Q}}f(z)=f(x),\qquad\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}.

(8)

Suppose that for some
s\leq t\in\mathbb{Q}
, there is a random variable
\mathcal{K}_{s,t}f\in\operatorname{UC}_{0}
such that
\mathcal{K}_{s,t}f\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathfrak{h}_{t-s}f

. Then formula (

) holds almost surely for this

.

Note that condition (

1.2
Main results: new framework for directed landscape convergence

A dream of universality.
The central challenge in the field of KPZ—sometimes referred to as the
strong KPZ universality
problem—is to show that we will see the characteristic
1
:
2
:
3
scaling and all KPZ limit objects whenever we start with a microscopic model satisfying a few basic qualitative properties indicating membership in the KPZ class.

At present, we have an excellent understanding of the limit objects in the KPZ class, and there are techniques for showing that models with sufficient exact solvability converge to these limit objects. There are also a few comparison techniques available that allow for small perturbations of exactly solvable models, e.g. see
[
QS23
,
Ran23
]
. However, the problem of analyzing truly non-integrable models (e.g. first and last passage percolation with general weights) seems far out of reach.

Theorem
1.1
can be viewed as providing one small step towards universality. It implies that if we can prove convergence to the KPZ fixed point for a moodel, then we can often upgrade this to the directed landscape convergence by checking a few straightforward conditions. Informally, if you can find a way to get 99% of the way to universality, then Theorem
1.1
can take you the last 1%. Note that all previous approaches to studying landscape convergence rely on extra combinatorial structure coming from either some generalization of the Robinson-Schensted correspondence
[
DOV22
,
DZ24b
,
DV21b
,
Wu23
,
DV24
]
, or the Yang-Baxter equation
[
ACH24b
]
.

To illustrate this idea, we give a handful of simple applications to models where KPZ fixed point convergence has been previously established, but directed landscape convergence has remained elusive.

Theorem 1.6
.

The following models converge to the directed landscape after appropriate rescaling: the random walk web distance and Brownian web distance as conjectured in
[
VV23
]
(
Theorem

6.5
), ASEPs and TASEPs under various exotic couplings (
Theorem

7.4
), and general asymmetric exclusion processes in a ‘TASEP-perturbative’ limiting regime and with random initial data (
Theorem

7.7
).

Remark 1.7
.

1.

If a directed metric model converges to the directed landscape in a sufficiently strong sense, then its geodesics also converge to landscape geodesics, see
[
DOV22
, Section 12]
and
[
DV21b

. The required convergence is

. This convergence is weaker than uniform-on-compact convergence and can be established without hard estimates, see
[
DV21b
, Section 10]

We do not explore geodesic or hypograph convergence here, but note that one should be able to conclude geodesic convergence in the above models by applying our approach here with the aformentioned ideas in
[
DV21b
]
.

2.

The only two models in Theorem
1.6
where landscape convergence has been previously established are ASEP and TASEP in the basic coupling. For the other models covered in
Theorem

1.6
, directed landscape convergence seems beyond the scope of all previous approaches, since the necessary combinatorial structures (e.g.
line ensembles
or
the Yang-Baxter equation
) are unknown or absent. In particular, the general exclusion processes in
Theorem

7.7
gives the first example of a model where there are no exact formulas known for any marginals, but convergence to the directed landscape can be established. The caveat here is that we need to consider exclusion processes with random initial data and in a restrictive (but still highly non-trivial) TASEP-perturbative regime.

3.

For brevity, we do not attempt to be comprehensive in applying our convergence framework and have focused on models where no tractable line ensembles are expected to exist. Beyond the models in

1.6
, there are other models where our convergence framework applies straightforwardly. For example, in the original arXiv version of this paper
[

We finish this section by elaborating on the (T)ASEP exotic couplings in Theorem
1.6
. For convenience, we will only couple height function evolutions where
h(0)\in 2\mathbb{Z}
; adding in this condition makes the state space
\SRW
irreducible under the ASEP evolution.
First, we can alternately define the evolution of ASEP with parameter
p
using two arrays of Poisson processes
Q^{+}(x,h),Q^{-}(x,h)
on
\mathbb{R}
indexed by the even lattice

\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{e}=\{(x,h)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:x+h\text{ is even}\}.

The processes
Q^{+}(x,h)
have intensity
p
and the processes
Q^{-}(x,h)
have intensity
p+1
. An ASEP height function
h_{t}
will attempt the jump
h_{t^{-}}(x)\mapsto h_{t}(x)\pm 2
if
t\in Q^{\pm}(x,h_{t^{-}}(x))
and
h_{t^{-}}
has a local minimum/maximum at
x
. A pair of arrays
Q^{\pm}
defines an ASEP evolution when we have the following two conditions:

•

For any
h\in\SRW
with local minima at
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
and local maxima at
y_{1},\dots,y_{\ell}
, the processes

Q^{+}(x_{i},h(x_{i})),\;\;i=1,\dots,k,\quad Q^{-}(y_{j},h(y_{j})),\;\;j=1,\dots,\ell

are all independent.

•

For any fixed time
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the forward clocks
\{Q^{\pm}(u)\cap[t,\infty):u\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{e}\}
are independent of the backward clocks
\{Q^{\pm}(u)\cap(-\infty,t]:u\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{e}\}
.

Now, consider a coupling of ASEP simultaneously from all times and all initial conditions using the same clocks
Q^{-},Q^{+}
. Such a coupling is always monotone, and is jointly Markov by the second bullet point above. In other words, the coupling satisfies prelimiting versions of
2,3
in Theorem
1.3
. There are many ways to impose an additional constraint on the clocks
Q^{\pm}
so we always satisfy shift commutativity in the limit. To define the exotic couplings in
Theorem

1.6
, we fix some
u=(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{e}
with
b\geq|a|
, and ask that all clocks satisfy the additional condition
Q^{\pm}(u+x)=Q^{\pm}(u)
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{e}
. Beyond this, we set the clocks independently. This easily implies shift commutativity in the limit, allowing us to apply Theorem
1.3
to prove directed landscape convergence.

The above construction also shows how our main theorems fail away from the continuum limit. Indeed, there are many distinct exotic couplings of ASEP, which satisfy very precise pre-limiting versions of Theorem
1.3
.1-4. All these couplings become equivalent in the KPZ limit. We note also that the exotic coupling construction covers three classic couplings: the case when
u=(0,2)
is Liggett’s basic coupling, the case when
u=(1,1)
is the particle coupling, where Poisson clocks are attached to labelled particles rather than sites, and
u=(-1,1)
covers the similarly defined hole coupling.

Finally, there are many choices of the clocks
Q^{\pm}
where shift commutativity
does not
arise in the limit; here we expect a limiting grand monotone coupling of the KPZ fixed point
not
driven by the directed landscape. A particularly intriguing example is when all clocks are independent. Here the limiting object should be driven by a three-dimensional, rather than a two-dimensional, noise.

Structure of the paper and proof overview

After a short preliminary section, we prove
Theorem

1.1
in

3
, up to a key technical result that we leave for
Section

4
. These are the most important parts of the paper. We then prove
Theorem

1.3
from
Theorem

1.1
in

5
. This section also contains a technical extension of

1.3
that we will use to study general exclusion processes in Theorem

, where random initial data is required to reach the KPZ fixed point. Finally,

and

prove the applications in
Theorem

. The proofs in the final sections are mostly straightforward, though there are some technical lemmas required to handle monotonicity in general exclusion processes.

Broadly speaking,
Theorem

1.1
can be viewed as a consequence of the fact that information flows along a countable set of
coalescing geodesics
in the directed landscape. Very roughly, because this countable set is lower-dimensional and the directed landscape is independent in far away regions, we can hope to characterize the directed landscape using only some lower-dimensional marginal information.

Setting some notations, we will write
\mathcal{M}_{s,t}(x,y)=\mathcal{M}(x,s;y,t)
for the increment in
\mathcal{M}
from time
s
to time
t
, and similarly define
\mathcal{L}_{s,t}
for the directed landscape. For functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R},g:\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R},k,\ell\geq 1
, we define the
metric composition

f\diamond g:\mathbb{R}^{k-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell-1}\to\mathbb{R}
by

f\diamond g(x,y)=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}f(x,z)+g(z,r).

(9)

The directed landscape
\mathcal{L}
is characterized by the law of the increment
\mathcal{S}:=\mathcal{L}_{0,1}
, known as the Airy sheet, independent increment and scale invariance properties, and the metric composition law

\mathcal{L}_{s,t}=\mathcal{L}_{s,r}\diamond\mathcal{L}_{r,t},\qquad s<r<t.

Indeed, we can view
\mathcal{S}
as an analogue of the normal distribution, and
\mathcal{L}
as an analogue of Brownian motion in the metric composition semigroup. We will prove
Theorem

1.1
using a variant of the Lindeberg exchange method adapted to studying metric composition.

Call a function
\mathcal{M}
satisfying the assumptions of
Theorem

1.1
a
pre-landscape
.
With the above framework in mind, we first show that any pre-landscape
\mathcal{M}
can be extended to a continuous function on
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
satisfying the metric composition law, and with the property that for any
s<t
, and
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
we have

f\diamond\mathcal{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}f\diamond\mathcal{L}.

(10)

This is fairly quick, and we refer the reader to
Proposition

3.1
for details. Given this structure, to show that
\mathcal{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}
, it suffices to prove that
\mathcal{M}_{s,t}
and
\mathcal{L}_{s,t}
have the same finite dimensional distributions for all fixed
s<t
. We will outline how we check that for a set
F=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\}
, the two-point distribution
\mathcal{M}_{0,1}|_{F}
matches that of
\mathcal{L}
. The general case uses the same idea. For
s\in[0,1]
define

\mathcal{M}^{s}=\mathcal{L}_{0,s}\diamond\mathcal{M}_{s,1},

so that
\mathcal{M}^{0}=\mathcal{M}_{0,1}
and
\mathcal{M}^{1}=\mathcal{L}_{0,1}
. We will aim to show that with respect to the
L^{1}
-Wasserstein metric
d_{W}
, for
s<t=s+r
,

d_{W}(\mathcal{M}^{s}|_{F},\mathcal{M}^{t}|_{F})=o(r).

(11)

The equality in law between
\mathcal{M}^{0}|_{F}
and
\mathcal{M}^{1}|_{F}
will then follow by interpolating between
\mathcal{M}^{0}
and
\mathcal{M}^{1}
with
\mathcal{M}^{t}
for
t
in a fine mesh and applying the triangle inequality for
d_{W}
. To prove (
11
), we use the decomposition

\mathcal{M}^{s}=\mathcal{L}_{0,s}\diamond\mathcal{M}_{s,t}\diamond\mathcal{M}_{t,1},\qquad\mathcal{M}^{t}=\mathcal{L}_{0,s}\diamond\mathcal{L}_{s,t}\diamond\mathcal{M}_{t,1}.

(12)

We will construct a coupling of the increments
\mathcal{M}_{s,t},\mathcal{L}_{s,t}
so that
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{M}^{s}|_{F}=\mathcal{M}^{t}|_{F}]=1-O(r^{2/3+\delta})
for some
\delta>0
. To see why this implies (
11
), observe that on a compact set
K
, KPZ scaling gives that
\|\mathcal{M}_{s,t}\|_{K},\|\mathcal{L}_{s,t}\|_{K}=O(r^{1/3})
, and so a crude estimate implies

\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{M}^{s}|_{F}-\mathcal{M}^{t}|_{F}\|_{2}=O(r^{1/3})\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{M}^{s}|_{F}\neq\mathcal{M}^{t}|_{F}]=O(r^{1+\delta}).

Now to construct the coupling between
\mathcal{M}_{s,t},\mathcal{L}_{s,t}
, for
i=1,2
let
A_{i}
be the set of all
z\in\mathbb{R}
where

\mathcal{L}_{0,s}\diamond\mathcal{M}_{t,1}(x_{i},y_{i})\leq\mathcal{L}_{0,s}(x_{i},z)+\mathcal{M}_{t,1}(z,y_{i})+\log^{10}(r)r^{1/3}.

The set
A_{i}
is chosen so that in the candidate landscapes
\mathcal{M}^{s},\mathcal{M}^{t}
, the geodesic from
(x_{i},0)
to
(y_{i},1)
is in the set
A_{i}
at times
s
and
t
with very high probability; this is guaranteed by the fact that the threshold
\log^{10}(r)r^{1/3}\gg r^{1/3}
. Therefore if we can couple
\mathcal{L}_{s,t},\mathcal{M}_{s,t}
so that

\max_{z\in A_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{0,s}(x_{i},z)+\mathcal{L}_{s,t}(z,y)=\max_{z\in A_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{0,s}(x_{i},z)+\mathcal{M}_{s,t}(z,y),\qquad\forall y\in A_{i},

(13)

for
i=1,2
, then with high probability
\mathcal{M}^{s}|_{F}=\mathcal{M}^{t}|_{F}
.
The existence of a coupling where (
13
) holds only for exactly one value of
i
is immediate by the KPZ fixed point marginal property (
10
).
These two couplings (for
i=1
and
i=2
) are compatible if the sets
A_{1},A_{2}
are well-separated from each other, since in this case the pieces
\mathcal{M}_{s,t}|_{A_{1}\times A_{1}},\mathcal{M}_{s,t}|_{A_{2}\times A_{2}}
are approximately independent. Moreover, the couplings for
A_{1},A_{2}
are still compatible even if
A_{1},A_{2}
partially overlap, as long as whenever they overlap on an interval
I
, the functions
\mathcal{L}_{0,s}(x_{1},\cdot),\mathcal{L}_{0,s}(x_{2},\cdot)
differ by a constant
C_{I}
on
I
.

This type of compatibility can be viewed as roughly corresponding to the event where in a candidate landscape
\mathcal{M}^{t}
, if
\pi_{i},i=1,2
are the geodesics from
(x_{i},0)
and
(y_{i},1)
, then either
\pi_{1}(t),\pi_{2}(t)
are far away from each other (
A_{1},A_{2}
are separated), or else
\pi_{1}(t)=\pi_{2}(t)
and the geodesics coalesce together well before time
s
(
A_{1},A_{2}
overlap and the functions
\mathcal{L}_{0,s}(x_{1},\cdot),\mathcal{L}_{0,s}(x_{2},\cdot)
differ by a constant). In other words, the coalescence time for the geodesics
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
occurs far away from the interval
[s,t]
. If
\mathcal{M}^{s},\mathcal{M}^{t}
were true directed landscapes, we could prove that this compatibility event holds with probability
1-r^{1-o(1)}
. For pre-landscapes, we will show the weaker bound
1-r^{2/3+\delta}
for a fixed
\delta>0
, which still gives (
11
). In practice, we will also work with a stationary version of
\mathcal{M}^{s}|_{F},\mathcal{M}^{t}|_{F}
in order to facilitate technical computations.

On the Lindeberg exchange strategy and other related ideas.

Originating with Lindeberg’s proof of the central limit theorem
[
Lin22
]
, the method of replacement or exchange has been generalized (see
[
Cha05
,
Cha06
,
Rot79
,
MOO10
,
CSZ17
]
), and has been applied to derive universality for many probabilistic models, including some in the KPZ class, e.g. the
Sherrington-Kirkpatrick spin glass model
[
Cha05
]
,
random matrices
[
Cha05
,
TV11
,
TV10
,
KY17
]
,
last passage percolation in a thin rectangle
[
Sui06
]
,
and directed polymers
[
KQ18
,
Ran23
,
CSZ23
,
Tsa24
]
.

There are some similarities between our proofs and some existing arguments; for example, the Tsai’s characterization of the critical stochastic heat flow
[
Tsa24
]
, which appeared while we were finalizing this paper, also uses an exchange idea based on replacing time slices as in (
12
) to characterize a scaling limit.
However, beyond the starting idea of ‘exchange’, our approach significantly differs from the usual Lindeberg method. In particular, the study of test functions and moments has no relevance in our proof.
An explanation for this is that compared to the previous applications of the Lindeberg method to directed polymers, our setting of the directed landscape is in the
strongest
disorder regime, which is more sensitive to noise perturbations (see e.g.
[
GH24
]
and references therein), thereby requiring very different techniques.

Another related idea used at the limit shape level is that of
constructive Euler hydrodynamics
, e.g. see Theorem 3.1 in
[
BGRS02
]
and
[
BGRS19
]
for a survey. This strategy characterizes the entropy solution to Burgers’ equation using a set of axioms similar to some of those in our
Theorem

1.3
. For example, they require a version of monotonicity and a local dynamics assumption in their deterministic setting. The axioms are tailored to study hydrodynamic limit points of interacting particle systems.

Future work.

One challenge in KPZ is constructing limit objects with boundaries and boundary conditions. In this direction, a major breakthrough was recently made by X. Zhang
[
Zha25
]
, where the KPZ fixed point was constructed for functions on the half-line
[0,\infty)
, with a source at the point
0
. In forthcoming work, we build on the ideas of the present paper construct the directed landscape on the half-space
[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
using Zhang’s half-space fixed point as the starting point. The idea is similar to the suggested construction of the full-space directed landscape in Remark
1.9
. One key step is first constructing the half-space stationary horizon, which implies an analogue of Property
\ast
in the half-space setting.

2.1
The directed landscape and the KPZ fixed point

Let
\operatorname{UC}
be the set of all upper semicontinuous functions from
\mathbb{R}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
. A function
f
is in
\operatorname{UC}
if and only if its hypograph

\operatorname{hypo}(f)=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times\bar{\mathbb{R}}:t\leq f(x)\}

is closed. We equip
\operatorname{UC}
with a
local Hausdorff metric

d_{H,\operatorname{loc}}(f,g):=d_{H}(E(\operatorname{hypo}(f_{n}),E(\operatorname{hypo}(f)).

in the Hausdorff metric on closed subsets of
\mathbb{R}\times[-1,1]
, where
E:\mathbb{R}\times\bar{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{R}\times[-1,1]
is the map

E(x,s)=(x,e^{-|x|}s/(1+|s|)),

and the
d_{H}
metric on the right-hand side above is the usual Hausdorff metric.
It is straightforward to check that
\operatorname{UC}
is a compact Polish space. Setting some notations, we write
\delta_{x}\in\operatorname{UC}
for the
narrow wedge at
x
, i.e., the function with
\delta_{x}(x)=0
and
\delta_{x}(y)=-\infty
for
y\neq x
. For
f\in\operatorname{UC}
and
K\subset\mathbb{R}
we also write
f|_{K}\in\operatorname{UC}
for the function equal to
f
on
K
, and
-\infty
on
K^{c}
.

In
[
MQR21
]
, the KPZ fixed point is constructed as a time-homogeneous Markov process on an appropriate subset of
\operatorname{UC}
, taking a function
f
and mapping it to
\mathfrak{h}_{t}f\in\operatorname{UC}
, for any
t\geq 0
. More precisely, in
[
MQR21
]
the KPZ fixed point is defined by specifying the translation probabilities

\mathbb{P}[\mathfrak{h}_{t}f\leq g]

(14)

for all functions
f,-g\in\operatorname{UC}
with at most linear growth at
\pm\infty
through a Fredholm determinant formula. This characterizes the law of
\mathfrak{h}_{t}f
for fixed
t,f
, which then characterizes the law of
(\mathfrak{h}_{t}f)_{t\geq 0}
through the Markov property. The formula for the transition probabilities (
14
) is tractable enough to prove that for any
f
with at most linear growth at
\pm\infty
, the map
(x,t)\mapsto\mathfrak{h}_{t}f(x)
is Hölder-
1/2-
in
x
and Hölder-
1/3-
in
t
.

An alternate characterization of the KPZ fixed point comes through the directed landscape, constructed in
[
DOV22
]
. The directed landscape is built from the
Airy sheet
, a random continuous function
\mathcal{S}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
. We call the rescaling
\mathcal{S}_{s}(x,y)=s\mathcal{S}(x/s^{2},y/s^{2})
an Airy sheet of scale
s
. For the purposes of this paper, we will take the Airy sheet as a black box and only invoke its properties as necessary. Here recall that for
f:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R},g:\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}
we define
f\diamond g:\mathbb{R}^{\ell-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{k-1}\to\mathbb{R}
by
f\diamond g(x,y)=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}f(x,z)+g(z,y)
.

Definition 2.1
.

The directed landscape
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}\to\mathbb{R},(x,s;y,t)\mapsto\mathcal{L}(x,s;y,t)=\mathcal{L}_{s,t}(x,y)
is the unique random continuous function satisfying

I.

(Independent increments) For any disjoint time intervals
\{[t_{i},s_{i}]:i\in\{1,\dots k\}\}
, the random functions

\{\mathcal{L}_{s_{i},t_{i}}:i\in\{1,\dots,k\}\}

are independent.

II.

(Metric composition law) Almost surely, for any
r<s<t
we have

\mathcal{L}_{r,t}=\mathcal{L}_{r,s}\diamond\mathcal{L}_{s,t}.

III.

(Airy sheet marginals) For any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
s>0
,
\mathcal{L}_{t,t+s^{3}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{S}_{s}
, an Airy sheet of scale
s
.

Remark 2.2
.

The uninitiated reader may wish to compare Definition
2.1
with the conditions in Theorem
1.1
. The key difference is in the assumption on the marginals. In Theorem
1.1
, once we prove metric composition for
\mathcal{M}
it is not difficult to show that condition

is equivalent to knowing that for any fixed

we have the identity
f\diamond\mathcal{L}_{t+s^{3}}\diamond g\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}f\diamond\mathcal{S}_{s}\diamond g

is devoted to proving this. The difference between the assumption of metric composition and the weaker triangle inequality is easier to overcome.

As already alluded to, we can alternately describe the KPZ fixed point directly in terms of the directed landscape. Indeed, for
f\in\operatorname{UC}
, define
\mathfrak{h}_{0}f=f
,
and
\mathfrak{h}_{t}f

With definition (

.

(Domain of the KPZ fixed point,

)

2.2
Basic properties of the directed landscape

The directed landscape is best viewed as a random directed metric. Following
[
DV21b
, Section 5]
, a
directed metric of positive sign
on a set
X
is a function
d:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
satisfying
d(x,x)=0
for all
x
, together with the triangle inequality

d(v,w)+d(w,u)\geq d(u,v).

We say that
d
is a
directed metric of negative sign
if
-d
is a directed metric of positive sign. Many natural KPZ models are directed metrics but not metrics, and moreover, unlike the class of metrics, the class of directed metrics is preserved under the kinds of rescaling typical seen in the KPZ class. If we extend the directed landscape to equal
-\infty
on
\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
then it becomes a directed metric (of negative sign) on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

As with usual metrics, we can define path length and geodesics in the directed landscape. Paths will be continuous functions
\pi:[s,t]\to\mathbb{R}
with length

|\pi|_{\mathcal{L}}=\inf_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\inf_{r_{0}=s<r_{1}<\dots<r_{k-1}<r_{k}=t}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{L}(\pi(r_{i-1}),r_{i-1};\pi(r_{i}),r_{i}).

A path is a geodesic if
|\pi|_{\mathcal{L}}=\mathcal{L}(\pi(s),s;\pi(t),t)
, and geodesics exist between all points in the directed landscape
[
DOV22
, Lemma 13.2]
. The concept of geodesics is central to the proof of the main theorem. However, we will typically not use geodesics explicitly and instead think about them through the lens of metric composition. Indeed, a point
(y_{0},r)
lies on a geodesic from
(x,s)
to
(z,t)
if it is an argmax for the following metric composition:

y_{0}\in\argmax_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{L}_{s,r}(x,y)+\mathcal{L}_{r,t}(y,z).

(15)

Geodesics between nearby points in the directed landscape coalesce
[
BGH22
]
, which manifests itself through the fact that the argmax
y_{0}
above is typically stable if we perturb
x
or
z
. Coalescence also implies that for
x\neq x^{\prime}
, the difference profile
\mathcal{L}_{s,t}(x,\cdot)-\mathcal{L}_{s,t}(x^{\prime},\cdot)
is constant off of a lower dimensional set
[
BGH21
,
BGH22
,
GZ22
]
.

It will also be useful to note the following basic symmetries for
\mathcal{L}
, which we use frequently and without reference.

Lemma 2.6

(Lemma 10.2,
[
DOV22
]
and Proposition 1.23,
[
DV21b
]
)
.

We have the following equalities in distribution as continuous functions from
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}\to\mathbb{R}
. Here
r,c\in\mathbb{R}
, and
q>0
.

1.

(Time stationarity)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(x,s;y,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}(x,s+r;y,t+r).

2.

(Spatial stationarity)

\mathcal{L}(x,s;y,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}(x+c,s;y+c,t).

3.

(Flip symmetries)

\mathcal{L}(x,s;y,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}(-x,s;-y,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}(y,-t;x,-s).

4.

(Skew stationarity)

\mathcal{L}(x,s;y,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}(x+cs,s;y+ct,t)+(t-s)^{-1}[(x-y)^{2}-(x-y-(t-s)c)^{2}].

5.

(
1:2:3
rescaling)

\mathcal{L}(x,s;y,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}q\mathcal{L}(q^{-2}x,q^{-3}s;q^{-2}y,q^{-3}t).

In particular, we have the following identity at the level of the one-point law. For fixed
x,s;y,t
,

\mathcal{L}(x,s;y,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(t-s)^{1/3}\mathcal{L}(0,0;0,1)-\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{t-s}.

The random variable
T=\mathcal{L}(0,0;0,1)
has the Tracy-Widom GUE distribution; this goes back to
[
BDJ99
]
. The identity above shows that all one-point marginals of
\mathcal{L}
are affine shifts of this law. The symmetries
1
and
3
above imply that for any
f\in\operatorname{UC}_{0}
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
,

(f\diamond\mathcal{L}_{s,s+t})_{t\geq 0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(\mathcal{L}_{s-t,s}\diamond f)_{t\geq 0}.

This equality is known as skew-time reversibility of the KPZ fixed point, and plays an important role in Section
3
. It was first identified in
[
MQR21
]
.

Again using
Lemma

2.6
, if we fix three of the coordinates of
\mathcal{L}
and vary only one of the spatial coordinates
x
or
y
we end up with a rescaling of the parabolic Airy
2
process
\mathcal{A}(x):=\mathcal{L}(0,0;x,1)
. We will need a few basic facts about the Airy
2
process. In this next lemma, we record a standard comparison with a Brownian bridge with diffusion coefficient
\sqrt{2}
(which, at least for the direction of Airy
2
to Brownian bridge, has been known since
[
CH14
, Proposition 4.1]
). Throughout the paper, our convention for all Brownian motions and bridges is that they have diffusion coefficient
2
unless otherwise stated.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the parabolic Airy
2
process, fix a closed interval
[a,b]
, and let
L:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
be the random affine function with
L(a)=\mathcal{A}(a),L(b)=\mathcal{A}(b)
. Then conditional on
\mathcal{A}|_{[a,b]^{c}}
, the laws of
\mathcal{A}|_{[a,b]}-L
and a Brownian bridge
B:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
with
B(a)=B(b)=0
are mutually absolutely continuous.

Proof.

The process
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}
is the top curve in a sequence of continuous functions
\mathcal{A}_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\mathcal{A}_{1}>\mathcal{A}_{2}>\dots
known as the parabolic Airy line ensemble
[
PS02
,
CH14
]
. Corwin and Hammond
[
CH14
]
showed that the parabolic Airy line ensemble satisfies the following resampling property: if we condition on
\mathcal{A}_{i}(x)
for all
(i,x)
outside of some compact set
\{1,\dots,k\}\times[a,b]
then
(\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{k})|_{[a,b]}
is simply given by
k
independent Brownian bridges from
\mathcal{A}_{i}(a)
to
\mathcal{A}_{i}(b)
conditioned to not intersect. Applying this with
k=1
implies that conditional on
\mathcal{A}|_{[a,b]^{c}}
,
\operatorname{Law}(\mathcal{A}|_{[a,b]}-L)\ll\operatorname{Law}(B)
.

On the other hand, it is not difficult to show that
\sup\mathcal{A}_{k}|_{[a,b]}\to-\infty
almost surely as
k\to\infty
. This follows, for example, from the modulus of continuity in
[
DV21a
, Theorem 1.4]
and the one-point bound in
[
Sos00
, (1.17), Theorem 1]
. Therefore for any set
U
with
\mathbb{P}[B\in U]>0
, there exists a random
K\in\mathbb{N}
such that if we condition on
A_{i}(x)
for
(i,x)
outside of some compact set
\{1,\dots,k\}\times[a,b]
, then
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}|_{[a,b]}-L\in U]>0
as well. Hence conditional on
\mathcal{A}|_{[a,b]^{c}}
,
\operatorname{Law}(B)\ll\operatorname{Law}(\mathcal{A}|_{[a,b]}-L)
.
∎

We also require a quantitative Brownian comparison. This is much more difficult to achieve than the soft comparison in Lemma
2.7
, but still uses the Gibbs resampling property in the previous proof as the key input.

Lemma 2.8

(
[
Dau24
, Corollary 1.3]
)
.

Fix
a<b\in\mathbb{R}
. The law
\nu
of
\mathcal{A}(t)-\mathcal{A}(a),t\in[a,b]
is absolutely continuous respect to the law
\mu
of a Brownian motion in
[a,b]
with
B(a)=0
, with Radon-Nikodym derivative
\tfrac{d\nu}{d\mu}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
.

A quantitative comparison with Brownian motion for
\mathcal{A}
was first achieved in
[
CHH23
, Theorem 1.1]
(see also
[
Ham22
]
for an earlier comparison to Brownian bridge). In that paper the authors showed that
\tfrac{d\nu}{d\mu}\in L^{p}(\mu)
for all
p<\infty
, a result that would also suffice for our purposes.

2.3
The stationary horizon

We end the preliminary section by describing the stationary measure for the directed landscape. This stationary measure is the
stationary horizon
, constructed in
[
Bus24
,
SS23
]
and connected to the directed landscape in
[
BSS24
]
. The last-passage description we give below for the stationary horizon is from
[
DV24
]
. It is a version of the queuing-theoretic description given in the previously mentioned papers.

First, for a collection of functions
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{k}):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and a vector
\pi=(\pi_{1}\geq\dots\geq\pi_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, define

|\pi|_{f}=f_{k}(\pi_{k})+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}f_{j}(\pi_{j})-f_{j}(\pi_{j+1}),\qquad f[k\to x]=\sup_{\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{\geq},\pi_{1}=x}|\pi|_{f}.

Here
f[i\to x]
is a last passage value from
(i,-\infty)
to
(1,x)
in the sense of
[
DV24
]
, and
|\pi|_{f}
can be thought of as the length of a path associated to
\pi
. Now let
a_{1}<\dots<a_{k}
and let
\mathcal{B}=\{\mathcal{B}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be a family of independent two-sided Brownian motions where each
\mathcal{B}_{i}
has slope
a_{i}
. Define
\mathcal{R}=\{\mathcal{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
by

\mathcal{R}_{i}(x)=\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{i}(x)-\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{i}(0),\qquad\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{i}(x)=\mathcal{B}[i\to x].

(16)

The following theorem generalizes Property
*
from the introduction.

Theorem 2.9
.

(contained in
[
BSS24
, Theorem 2.1]
)

For any
a_{1}<\dots<a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
, if we let
\mathcal{R}
be defined as above and independent of the directed landscape, then for any
s<t
,

(\mathcal{R}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{R}_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(\mathcal{R}_{1}\diamond\mathcal{L}_{s,t},\dots\mathcal{R}_{k}\diamond\mathcal{L}_{s,t})-(\mathcal{R}_{1}\diamond\mathcal{L}_{s,t}(0),\dots\mathcal{R}_{k}\diamond\mathcal{L}_{s,t}(0)).

Moreover,
(\mathcal{R}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{R}_{k})
is the unique (in law)
k
-tuple of functions satisfying the stationarity property above and the asymptotic slope condition

\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}\frac{\mathcal{R}_{i}(t)}{t}=a_{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,k.

3
Main characterization: proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section we prove
Theorem

1.1
, up to the key technical input which we leave to the next section. Throughout the section, we call a function
\mathcal{M}
satisfying the assumptions of
Theorem

1.1
a
pre-landscape
.

3.1
Continuity and metric composition

The first step in the proof of Theorem
1.1
is extending any pre-landscape to a continuous function on all of
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
. This continuity claim follows from skew-time reversibility and continuity properties of the KPZ fixed point. As a byproduct of the proof, we will show that the continuous extension of
\mathcal{M}
has KPZ fixed point marginals in both the forward and backwards directions and satisfies metric composition, rather than just a triangle inequality.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a pre-landscape. Then almost surely,
\mathcal{M}
has a continuous extension
\mathcal{M}:\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}\to\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, for any fixed function
f\in\operatorname{UC}_{0}
and any
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
s\geq 0
, we have that

\mathfrak{h}_{s}f\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{M}_{t-s,t}\diamond f\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}f\diamond\mathcal{M}_{t-s,t}.

(17)

where here the equality in law is as functions in
\operatorname{UC}_{0}
. Finally, almost surely, for any
s<r<t
we have the metric composition law
\mathcal{M}_{s,r}\diamond\mathcal{M}_{r,t}=\mathcal{M}_{s,t}
.

We break the proof of
Proposition

3.1
into a series of lemmas building up properties of
\mathcal{M}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
f\in\operatorname{UC}_{0}
with
\text{supp}(f)\subset\mathbb{Q}
and
t-s,t\in\mathbb{Q}
. Then
\mathfrak{h}_{s}f|_{\mathbb{Q}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(\mathcal{M}_{t-s,t}\diamond f)|_{\mathbb{Q}}.

Proof.

Let
g\in\operatorname{UC}_{0}
be any function supported on a finite set
F\subset\mathbb{Q}
. Take
r\in\mathbb{Q},r<t-s
, and
A\subset\{h\in\operatorname{UC}_{0}:h\geq g\}
such that
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{L}_{r,t-s}(0,\cdot)\in A]>0
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{M}_{t-s,t}\diamond f\leq-g\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[g\diamond\mathcal{M}_{t-s,t}\leq-f\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{M}_{r,t-s}(0,\cdot)\diamond_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathcal{M}_{t-s,t}\leq-f\;\big\mid\;\mathcal{M}_{r,t-s}(0,\cdot)\in A\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{M}_{r,t}(0,\cdot)\leq-f\;\big\mid\;\mathcal{M}_{r,t-s}(0,\cdot)\in A\right].

In the second line, in the metric composition law
\diamond_{\mathbb{Q}}
, we take a supremum over all
z\in\mathbb{Q}
rather than over all
z\in\mathbb{R}
as in the usual definition. This is required since
\mathcal{M}
is not defined off of
\mathbb{Q}^{4}_{\uparrow}
. When we write
\mathcal{M}_{r,t-s}(0,\cdot)\in A
we mean that
\mathcal{M}_{r,t-s}(0,\cdot)
extends continuously to a function in
A
. This is a positive probability event since the same holds for
\mathcal{L}
and we have the equality in law in condition
3
of
Theorem

1.1
.

In the above computation, the first inequality uses the independent increment property of
\mathcal{M}
, and the second inequality uses the triangle inequality, which implies

\mathcal{M}_{r,t-s}\diamond_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathcal{M}_{t-s,t}\leq\mathcal{M}_{r,t}.

Now, the final probability above is the same in both
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{M}
by condition
3
of
Theorem

1.1
,
and so by the metric composition law and independent increment properties for
\mathcal{L}
, it is bounded below by

\displaystyle\inf_{h\in A}\mathbb{P}[h\diamond\mathcal{L}_{t-s,t}\leq-f].

(18)

Next, for any
\epsilon>0
, define the set
A_{\epsilon}=\{h\in\operatorname{UC}_{0}:g\leq h\leq\sup g+1,d_{H,\operatorname{loc}}(h,g)\leq\epsilon\}
.
Lemma

2.7
and the almost sure boundedness of the Airy
2
process
\mathcal{A}
(see
Proposition

2.5
) shows that
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{M}_{r,t-s}(0,\cdot)\in A_{\epsilon}]>0
. Moreover, since any
g^{\prime}\in A_{\epsilon}
in bounded above by
\sup g+1
,
Proposition

2.4
implies that

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\inf_{g_{\epsilon}\in A_{\epsilon}}\mathbb{P}[g_{\epsilon}\diamond\mathcal{L}_{t-s,t}\leq-f]=\mathbb{P}[g\diamond\mathcal{L}_{t-s,t}\leq-f]=\mathbb{P}[f\diamond\mathcal{L}_{t-s,t}\leq-g].

Therefore we conclude that
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{M}_{t-s,t}\diamond f\leq-g]\geq\mathbb{P}[f\diamond\mathcal{L}_{t-s,t}\leq-g]
.
In other words,

\mathfrak{h}_{s}f|_{\mathbb{Q}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(f\diamond\mathcal{L}_{t-s,t})|_{\mathbb{Q}}
stochastically dominates
(\mathcal{M}_{t-s,t}\diamond f)|_{\mathbb{Q}}
. To prove the lemma from here, it suffices to observe that for a single point
q\in\mathbb{Q}
:

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{M}_{t-s,t}\diamond f(q)\leq a\right]=\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{M}_{t-s,t}(q,\cdot)\leq-f+a]=\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{L}_{t-s,t}(q,\cdot)\leq-f+a]=\mathbb{P}\left[f\diamond\mathcal{L}_{t-s,t}(q)\leq a\right],

where the middle equality uses condition
3
of
Theorem

1.1
.
∎

Note that the method for evaluating the infimum in (
18
) above by approximating deterministic initial conditions
using random functions bears similarity to ideas from
[
QS23
, Section 7]
and
[
ACH24a
, Proposition 5.1]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Any pre-landscape
\mathcal{M}
satisfies the metric composition law on
\mathbb{Q}
: for all
s<r<t\in\mathbb{Q}
we have
\mathcal{M}_{s,r}\diamond_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathcal{M}_{r,t}=\mathcal{M}_{s,t}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{Q}
is countable, it suffices to prove that for fixed
s<r<t\in\mathbb{Q}
and
x,y\in\mathbb{Q}
we have
\mathcal{M}_{s,r}\diamond_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathcal{M}_{r,t}(x,y)=\mathcal{M}_{s,t}(x,y)
. By the triangle inequality we have
\mathcal{M}_{s,r}\diamond_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathcal{M}_{r,t}(x,y)\leq\mathcal{M}_{s,t}(x,y)
almost surely so it suffices to prove that equality holds in distribution. For this, observe that in the directed landscape
\mathcal{L}
we have
\mathcal{L}_{s,r}\diamond_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathcal{L}_{r,t}(x,y)=\mathcal{L}_{s,t}(x,y)
by the usual metric composition law for
\mathcal{L}
, continuity, and density of
\mathbb{Q}
. On the other hand,
\mathcal{L}_{s,t}(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{M}_{s,t}(x,y)
(by condition
3
of
Theorem

1.1
), and

(\mathcal{L}_{s,r}(x,z),\mathcal{L}_{r,t}(z,y))_{z\in\mathbb{Q}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(\mathcal{M}_{s,r}(x,z),\mathcal{M}_{r,t}(z,y))_{z\in\mathbb{Q}}.

(19)

This uses the independent increment property of
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{M}
, the equality
(\mathcal{L}_{s,r}(x,z))_{z\in\mathbb{Q}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(\mathcal{M}_{s,r}(x,z))_{z\in\mathbb{Q}}
from condition
3
of
Theorem

1.1
, and the equality
(\mathcal{L}_{r,t}(z,y))_{z\in\mathbb{Q}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(\mathcal{M}_{s,r}(z,y))_{z\in\mathbb{Q}}
from Lemma
3.2
. Therefore
\mathcal{M}_{s,r}\diamond_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathcal{M}_{r,t}(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{M}_{s,t}(x,y)
, as desired.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
u_{1}=(x_{1},s_{1};y_{1},t_{1}),u_{2}=(x_{2},s_{2};y_{2},t_{2})\in\mathbb{Q}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, and
\hat{x}_{2},\hat{y}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}

(x_{2},s_{2})

(\hat{x}_{2},s_{1})

(y_{2},t_{2})

Denote

, and
\tau=|t_{1}-t_{2}|+|s_{1}-s_{2}|
.
Then letting

, for any

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[|[\mathcal{M}+d](u_{1})-[\mathcal{M}+d](u_{2})|>M\tau^{1/3}+L\chi^{1/2}]<ce^{-dM^{3/2}}+ce^{-dL^{2}},

where

Proof.

It suffices to prove this estimate assuming that precisely one of

,
s_{1}-s_{2}
,

,

is non-zero. We claim that in all these cases we have

(20)

in which case the conclusion follows from

,

∎

Below we allow all the constants

Proposition 3.9
.

For each

, there exists

such that the following holds. For

, we can find a coupling of

The proof of this proposition will be given shortly; we first finish the proof of

3.8
given Proposition
3.9
.
We will also need a tail estimate for the difference

to convert the total variation bound in Proposition

to a bound on a Wasserstein distance.

Lemma 3.10
.

For any
0\leq s<t\leq 1
, under any coupling between
\mathcal{M},\mathcal{L},
and
\mathcal{R}
, we have the following:
for any

and

\mathbb{P}\left[|\mathcal{M}^{s}_{i}-\mathcal{M}^{t}_{i}|>M(1-\log(t-s))^{2}(t-s)^{1/3}\right]<C\exp(-cM).

Proof.

We can assume that
M
is large enough, since otherwise the conclusion is trivial.
We first prove that

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{M}^{s}_{i}<\mathcal{M}^{t}_{i}-M(1-\log(t-s))^{2}(t-s)^{1/3}\right]<C\exp(-cM).

(24)

By
Proposition

2.5
and

, with probability at least
1-C\exp(-cM)
, the bound in
Proposition

2.5
holds for both
\mathcal{M},\mathcal{L}
with some
R<M^{2/3}
.
Also, with probability
>1-C\exp(-cM)
, we have
|\mathcal{R}_{i}(x)-\mathcal{R}_{i}(0)-a_{i}x|<|x|+M
for all

Let
\mathcal{E}
denote the intersection of the above two events.
Then on

\mathcal{R}_{i}(x)+\mathcal{L}_{0,s}(x,w)+\mathcal{L}_{s,t}(w,z)+\mathcal{M}_{t,1}(z,y_{i})<\mathcal{R}_{i}(0)+\mathcal{L}_{0,s}(0,0)+\mathcal{L}_{s,t}(0,y_{i})+\mathcal{M}_{t,1}(y_{i},1)\leq\mathcal{M}^{t}_{i},

On the other hand, we have

\mathcal{M}^{s}_{i}\geq\mathcal{R}_{i}(x_{*})+\mathcal{L}_{0,s}(x_{*},w_{*})+\mathcal{M}_{s,t}(w_{*},z_{*})+\mathcal{M}_{t,1}(z_{*},y_{i}).

and

∎

Proof of
Proposition

3.8
.

It suffices to show that the equality in law holds with
\mathcal{R}
replaced by
\mathcal{R}|_{F}
where
F\subset\mathbb{R}
is a finite subset of the domain of
\mathcal{R}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We think of
\mathcal{R}|_{F}
as a random variable in
\mathbb{R}^{n|F|}
. Let
d_{W}(X,Y)=\inf_{\mu}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\|X-Y\|_{1}
be the
L^{1}
-Wasserstein distance between the laws of two
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued random variables
X
and
Y
. Here the infimum is over all couplings of the random variables
X,Y
. Fix
\theta\in(0,1/4)
.
By
Proposition

3.9
and
Lemma

3.10
, for
s<t\in[\theta,1-\theta]
in the coupling of
\mathcal{M}^{s},\mathcal{M}^{t}
from

we have

\mathbb{E}\|(\mathcal{R}|_{F},\mathcal{M}_{s})-(\mathcal{R}|_{F},\mathcal{M}_{t})\|_{1}=\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{M}_{s}-\mathcal{M}_{t}\|_{1}<C(t-s)^{1+\delta/2}.

This then bounds
d_{W}((\mathcal{R}|_{F},\mathcal{M}_{s}),(\mathcal{R}|_{F},\mathcal{M}_{t}))
.
Also,

3.10
alone implies that in any coupling.

\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{M}_{0}-\mathcal{M}_{\theta}|+\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{M}_{1}-\mathcal{M}_{1-\theta}|<C(\log(\theta))^{2}\theta^{1/3}.

Therefore by the triangle inequality Wasserstein distance, we have

\displaystyle d_{W}((\mathcal{R}|_{F},\mathcal{M}_{0}),(\mathcal{R}|_{F},\mathcal{M}_{1}))

\displaystyle<C(\log(\theta))^{2}\theta^{1/3}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{W}((\mathcal{R}|_{F},\mathcal{M}_{\theta+(1-2\theta)(i-1)/n}),(\mathcal{R}|_{F},\mathcal{M}_{\theta+(1-2\theta)i/n}))

\displaystyle\leq C(\log(\theta))^{2}\theta^{1/3}+Cn^{-\delta/2}(1-2\theta)^{1+\delta/2}

Taking

.
∎

The coupling

. Below we fix

,
\mathcal{L}_{0,s}
, and
\mathcal{M}_{t,1}

.
For each

and

\tilde{A}_{i}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:H_{i}(x)+\mathcal{M}_{t,1}(x,y_{i})>H_{i}\diamond\mathcal{M}_{t,1}(y_{i})-\log^{10}(r)r^{1/3}\}.

be the

Proposition 3.11
.

There is a constant
\delta>0
, such that for all sufficiently small

and
i,j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
,

, we have
\mathbb{P}[A_{i}\cap A_{j}\cap D_{i,j}=\emptyset]>1-r^{2/3+\delta}

The proof of this estimate relies on careful analysis of the joint law of

and
\mathcal{R}_{j}

\ast
from
Remark

1.2
.
We postpone it to
Section

4

Let
\mathcal{E}_{*}
be the event where

, for each
i,j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
,
i\neq j
.
Note that for each

, (using the shape bounds in

2.5
,
Corollary

3.6
on
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{M}
)

consists of finitely many open intervals

, each of length at least

.

Lemma 3.12
.

There exists a function
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
that is
\sigma(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{L}_{0,s},\mathcal{M}_{t,1})
-measurable, and satisfies the following conditions:

•

On
\mathcal{E}_{*}
, for each
i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
,
F-H_{i}
is constant on each interval
A_{i,j}
,
j=1,\dots,\ell(i)
.

•

F=-\infty
outside of
\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
,

•

For any
M>0
, with probability at least
1-C\exp(-cM)
the following is true:
\mathcal{E}_{*}
holds, and
|F(x)-F(y)|\leq M\log(|x-y|^{-1}+2)\sqrt{|x-y|}
for all

.

Proof.

On
\mathcal{E}_{*}^{c}
, set
F=0
on
\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
and
-\infty
off this set. Next, for any interval
A_{i,j}
and a function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
, we say that
f
is suitable on
A_{i,j}
, if
f-H_{i}
is constant on
I
. Sort the intervals
A_{i,j},i=1,\dots,k,j=1,\dots,\ell(i)
into a list
I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}
so that the
I_{j}
are ordered by their left endpoint (ordering arbitrarily if left endpoints agree). We define
F
on
I_{1},\dots,I_{n}
inductively.

For
I_{1}
, choosing
i
so that
I_{1}=A_{i,j}
for some
j
, and we let
F=H_{i}
on
I_{1}
. Now assume that
F
is already defined on
\bigcup_{\iota=1}^{\ell}I_{\iota}
for some
\ell\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
, and for each
\iota\in\llbracket 1,\ell\rrbracket
,
F
is suitable on
I_{\iota}
.
We then define
F
on
I_{\ell+1}
, as follows.
If
I_{\ell+1}
intersects
\bigcup_{\iota=1}^{\ell}I_{\iota}
, there is a unique way of defining
F
to make it suitable on
I_{\ell+1}
. Here we crucially use that under
\mathcal{E}_{*}
, for each
i<i^{\prime}
and

, we have that
H_{i}-H_{i^{\prime}}
is constant on
A_{i,j}\cap A_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
, since
A_{i}\cap A_{j}
is disjoint from
D_{i,j}
. Otherwise, we define
F
to be suitable on
I_{\ell+1}
, so the right limit of
F
at the left endpoint of
I_{\ell+1}
equals the left limit of
F
at the right endpoint of
\bigcup_{\iota=1}^{\ell}I_{\iota}
. Take

outside
\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
.

It remains to check the last condition. By
Theorem

2.9
the law of each

is a Brownian motion with drift

.
Then for any
M>1
, with probability
>1-C\exp(-cM)
we have that
|H_{i}(x)-H_{i}(y)|<M\log(|x-y|^{-1}+2)\sqrt{|x-y|}
for each

and
x,y\in[-M,M]
.
Also, by
Proposition

2.5
/
Corollary

3.6
, with probability
>1-C\exp(-cM)
, we have that
\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}\subset[-M,M]
.
Thus the last condition holds from the construction of

. The measurability is immediate from construction.
∎

Now, observe that if
\mathcal{M},\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
are all independent, then conditional on
X:=(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{L}_{0,s},\mathcal{M}_{t,1})
we have the equality in law

F\diamond\mathcal{M}_{s,t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}F\diamond\mathcal{L}_{s,t}.

(25)

Indeed, by the independent increment property of

, both
\mathcal{M}_{s,t},\mathcal{L}_{s,t}
are independent of

, and so by

3.1
, both sides of this equality are equal in law to
\mathfrak{h}_{r}F
. Therefore conditional on

, we can couple

so that this equality is realized almost surely. Observe that in this coupling, the pairs
(X,\mathcal{M}_{s,t})
and
(X,\mathcal{L}_{s,t})
are still respectively independent; it is only in the triple
(X,\mathcal{M}_{s,t},\mathcal{L}_{s,t})
where we lose the independence. In particular, the individual laws of
(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{M}^{s})
and
(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{M}^{t})
(defined by (
23

We use this new coupling to prove
Proposition

3.9
.

Proof of
Proposition

3.9
.

By Proposition
3.11
, it is enough to show that

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{M}^{s}\neq\mathcal{M}^{t},\mathcal{E}_{*}\right]<C\exp(-c\log^{2}(r)).

(26)

We let
\mathcal{E}
be the event where
\mathcal{E}_{*}
holds, and additionally:

•

|F(x)-F(y)|<\log^{2}(r)\log(|x-y|^{-1}+2)\sqrt{|x-y|}
for any
x,y\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
,
|x-y|\leq\log^{10}(r)r^{2/3}
.

•

|H_{i}(x)-H_{i}(y)|<\log^{2}(r)\log(|x-y|^{-1}+2)\sqrt{|x-y|}
for any
i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
and
x,y\in[-\log^{2}(r),\log^{2}(r)]
.

•

R<\log^{2}(r)
, for the
R
in
Proposition

2.5
/
Corollary

3.6
defined for both
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{M}
.

•

|H_{i}(x)-H_{i}(0)|<C|x|+\log^{2}(r)
, for any
i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

By
Lemma

3.12
, standard Brownian motion estimates, and
Proposition

2.5
/
Corollary

3.6
,

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}_{*}\setminus\mathcal{E}]<C\exp(-c\log^{2}(r)).

It now remains to show that
\mathcal{M}^{s}=\mathcal{M}^{t}
on
\mathcal{E}
.

Let
\hat{A}_{i}
be the
\log^{10}(r)r^{2/3}/2
neighborhood of
\tilde{A}_{i}
.
We next show that on
\mathcal{E}
,

H_{i}\diamond\mathcal{M}_{s,t}|_{\hat{A}_{i}}=H_{i}\diamond\mathcal{L}_{s,t}|_{\hat{A}_{i}}.

(27)

First observe that on
\mathcal{E}
, the third bullet point (for
\mathcal{M}
) and the fourth bullet point above guarantee

\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}\subset[-\log^{2}(r)/2,\log^{2}(r)/2].

(28)

Now take any
z\in[-\log^{2}(r)/2,\log^{2}(r)/2]
. On
\mathcal{E}
, for both of the functions

F+\mathcal{M}_{s,t}(\cdot,z),\quad H_{i}+\mathcal{M}_{s,t}(\cdot,z),

any argmax must be in the interval
I_{z}:=(z-\log^{10}(r)r^{2/3}/2,z+\log^{10}(r)r^{2/3}/2)
. This follows by invoking the first and third bullet point to bound the sum with
F
, and the second, third, and fourth bullet point to bound the sum with
H_{i}
, and using (
28
) to ensure that we can apply the bound in the second bullet point near
z
. Then for each
i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
, since
F-H_{i}
is constant on

when
z\in\hat{A}_{i}
(by the construction of
F
), we must have that

F\diamond\mathcal{M}_{s,t}(z)-H_{i}\diamond\mathcal{M}_{s,t}(z)=F(z)-H_{i}(z).

This equality with
\mathcal{L}_{s,t}
in place of
\mathcal{M}_{s,t}
holds by the same reasoning.
Hence (
27
) follows from (
25
).

Next, let
z_{*}=\argmax_{z}H_{i}(z)+\mathcal{M}_{t,1}(z,y_{i})
and let
Y_{i}=H_{i}\diamond\mathcal{M}_{t,1}(y_{i})
. On
\mathcal{E}
, (
28
) gives that
|z_{*}|\leq\log^{2}(r)
, and so by the third bullet point of
\mathcal{E}
we have the bound

H_{i}(z_{*})+\mathcal{M}_{s,t}(z_{*},z_{*})+\mathcal{M}_{t,1}(z_{*},y_{i})\geq Y_{i}-\log^{4}(r)r^{1/3}.

On the other hand, for

and

with
|x|,|z|\leq\log^{2}(r)
we must have

\displaystyle H_{i}(x)+\mathcal{M}_{s,t}(x,z)+\mathcal{M}_{t,1}(z,y_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\;\;

\displaystyle|H_{i}(x)-H_{i}(z)|+\mathcal{M}_{s,t}(x,z)+H_{i}(z)+\mathcal{M}_{t,1}(z,y_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\;\;

\displaystyle\log^{4}(r)r^{1/3}+(Y_{i}-\log^{10}(r)r^{1/3}).

Here the final inequality uses the second and third bullet points to bound

or

by the shape bound from the third and fourth bullet points.

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{s}_{i}

\displaystyle=\max_{x,z}H_{i}(x)+\mathcal{M}_{s,t}(x,z)+\mathcal{M}_{t,1}(z,y_{i}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{t}_{i}

\displaystyle=\max_{x,z}H_{i}(x)+\mathcal{L}_{s,t}(x,z)+\mathcal{M}_{t,1}(z,y_{i}),

. Therefore by (

\mathcal{M}^{s}_{i}=\mathcal{M}^{t}_{i}
on

In this subsection we deduce

Proof of

.

By the continuity of both
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{L}
, it suffices to consider the case when

are all distinct.
Without loss of generality, we further assume that
x_{1}<\cdots<x_{k}
.

We consider
\mathcal{R}=(\mathcal{R}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{R}_{k})
constructed as in

from independent Brownian motions
\mathcal{B}=(\mathcal{B}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{B}_{k})
, with slopes

Now, since Proposition
3.8
makes a claim about the

laws of
(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}\diamond\mathcal{M}_{0,1})
and
(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}\diamond\mathcal{L}_{0,1})
, for any random function
\mathcal{Q}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose law
\mu_{\mathcal{Q}}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the law
\mu_{\mathcal{R}}
of
\mathcal{R}
, we have that (
22
) holds with
\mathcal{Q}
in place of
\mathcal{R}
(where
\mathcal{Q}
is independent of
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{M}
). With this in mind, we will construct a sequence of functions
\mathcal{R}^{\epsilon},\epsilon>0
with
\mu_{\mathcal{R}^{\epsilon}}\ll\mu_{\mathcal{R}}
such that as

,

\sup_{x:|x-x_{i}|>\epsilon}\mathcal{R}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x)-2k|x|-\mathcal{R}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}-\infty,

(29)

and

\sup_{x:|x-x_{i}|\leq\epsilon}\mathcal{R}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x)-\mathcal{R}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}0.

(30)

Then by (
22
) for
\mathcal{R}^{\epsilon}
, continuity of
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{L}
, and the tail bounds in
Proposition

2.5
/
Corollary

3.6

We now give the construction of
\mathcal{R}^{\epsilon}
.
Recall that
\mathcal{R}=f(\mathcal{B})
where
f
is given by (
16

\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is any deterministic function with
\phi(0)=0,\int\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}<\infty
, then by the Cameron-Martin theorem,
f(\mathcal{B}+\phi)
has an absolutely continuous law with respect to
\mathcal{R}
. With this in mind, define
\phi_{\epsilon}
to be the unique continuous function with
\phi_{\epsilon}(0)=(0,\dots,0)
such that for all
i
:

•

\phi_{\epsilon,i}^{\prime}(x)=0
for
x<x_{i}-\epsilon,x>x_{k}+1
.

•

\phi_{\epsilon,i}^{\prime}(x)=i\epsilon^{-2}
for
x\in(x_{i}-\epsilon,x_{i})
.

•

\phi_{\epsilon,i}^{\prime}(x)=-2i\epsilon^{-2}
for
x\in(x_{i},x_{k}+1)
.

We set
\mathcal{R}^{\epsilon}=f(\mathcal{B}+\phi_{\epsilon})
and check that (
29
), (

) hold. First, for all

, we have the inequalities

\phi_{\epsilon}[i\to x]+|\pi^{i,x}|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq(\mathcal{B}+\phi_{\epsilon})[i\to x]\leq\mathcal{B}[i\to x]+\phi_{\epsilon}[i\to x]

where
\pi^{i,x}
is a vector achieving the supremum of
\phi_{\epsilon}[i\to x]
. Recognizing that
\pi^{i,x_{i}}=(x_{i},\dots,x_{i})
, these bounds imply that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x)-2k|x|-\mathcal{R}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\phi_{\epsilon}[i\to x]-2k|x|-\phi_{\epsilon}[i\to x_{i}]+\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{i}(x)-\mathcal{B}_{i}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=(\phi_{\epsilon}[i\to x]-\phi_{\epsilon}[i\to x_{i}])+(\mathcal{R}_{i}(x)-2k|x|+\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{i}(0)-\mathcal{B}_{i}(x_{i})).

As

, the second bracketed term above remains bounded even after we take a supremum over

\mathcal{R}_{i}
is a Brownian motion with drift

; while by straightforward algebra the first term goes to

uniformly over

with

. This yields (
29
). For the bound (

), observe that for any

and

\displaystyle f[i\to x]-f[i\to x_{i}]

\displaystyle\leq f_{1}(x)-f_{1}(x_{i})

5
Variants of the characterization theorem

In this section we prove
Theorem

1.3
,
Corollary

1.5
, and a technical variant of
Theorem

1.3
that will be used later to study convergence of general exclusion processes. Throughout this section, we will appeal to the following simple lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

1.

If
X,Y
are
\operatorname{UC}
-valued random variables with
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Y
and
X\leq Y
almost surely, then
X=Y
almost surely.

2.

Let
X_{n}\leq Y_{n}
be sequences of
\operatorname{UC}
-valued random variables such that
X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}Y
and
Y_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}Y
. Then
X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}Y
. The same is true if instead
X_{n}\geq Y_{n}
.

Proof.

Proof of 1.
First, any upper semicontinuous function
f\in\operatorname{UC}
is determined by the countable set of values
f_{q,n}:=\sup_{x\in[q-1/n,q+1/n]}f(q)
where
q\in\mathbb{Q},n\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore it suffices to show that
X_{q,n}=Y_{q,n}
almost surely for all fixed
q,n
. Now, if
X_{q,n}<Y_{q,n}
with positive probability, then we could find some
p
in
\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathbb{P}(X_{q,n}<p)-\mathbb{P}(Y_{q,n}<p)=\mathbb{P}(X_{q,n}<p\leq Y_{q,n})>0)>0,

contradicting that
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Y
. Here the equality in the display above uses the almost sure ordering
X\leq Y
which implies that
X_{q,n}\leq Y_{q,n}
almost surely as well.

Proof of 2.
First observe that
(X_{n},Y_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}(Y,Y)
. Indeed, since
X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}Y
and
Y_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}Y
, the sequence
(X_{n},Y_{n})
is tight. Let
(X,Y)
be any subsequential limit of
(X_{n},Y_{n})
in distribution. Since
X_{n}\leq Y_{n}
(resp.
X_{n}\geq Y_{n}
) almost surely for all
n
we have
X\leq Y
(resp.
X\geq Y
) almost surely. Since
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Y
, part
1
implies
X=Y
almost surely. Next, by the continuous mapping theorem
d_{H,\operatorname{loc}}(X_{n},Y_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}d_{H,\operatorname{loc}}(Y,Y)=0
. Since the limit is constant, this convergence also holds in probability. By the triangle inequality,

d_{H,\operatorname{loc}}(X_{n},Y)\leq d_{H,\operatorname{loc}}(X_{n},Y_{n})+d_{H,\operatorname{loc}}(Y_{n},Y),

and so since the right-hand side converges to
0
in probability, so does the left-hand side, yielding the conclusion.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

We first show that for any
f\in\NW
and
s\in\mathbb{Q}
,

(\mathcal{K}_{s,s+t}f:t\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,\infty))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(\mathfrak{h}_{t}f:t\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,\infty)),

(31)

where here
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
is the usual KPZ fixed point. To prove the equality, it is enough to show that if
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(\mathcal{K}_{s,s+r}f:0\leq r\leq t)
and
t^{\prime}>t
, then conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{t}
we have

\mathcal{K}_{s,s+t^{\prime}}f\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathfrak{h}_{t^{\prime}-t}(\mathcal{K}_{s,s+t}f),

(32)

where on the right-hand side of (
32
), the KPZ fixed point evolution
\mathfrak{h}_{t^{\prime}-t}
is independent of
\mathcal{K}_{s,s+t}f
. To prove (
32
), let
g_{n}\in\NW
be a (random) sequence such that
g_{n}\uparrow\mathcal{K}_{s,s+t}f
almost surely in
\operatorname{UC}
. The existence of such a sequence
g_{n}
uses that
\mathcal{K}_{s,s+t}f
is continuous almost surely. Then by the independence of the increments of
\mathcal{K}
, conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{t}
we have

\mathfrak{h}_{t^{\prime}-t}g_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{K}_{s+t,s+t^{\prime}}g_{n}

for all
n
. On the other hand, monotonicity of
\mathcal{K}
implies that
\mathcal{K}_{s+t,s+t^{\prime}}g_{n}
is a monotone sequence in
\operatorname{UC}
, and so it converges to a limit
G
. By the previous display and the dominated convergence theorem for the KPZ fixed point (
Proposition

2.4
) we have that conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{t}
,
G\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathfrak{h}_{t^{\prime}-t}(\mathcal{K}_{s,s+t}f)
. Finally, since
g_{n}\leq\mathcal{K}_{s,s+t}f
, another application of monotonicity for
\mathcal{K}
implies that
\mathcal{K}_{s+t,s+t^{\prime}}g_{n}\leq\mathcal{K}_{s,s+t^{\prime}}f
and so
G\leq\mathcal{K}_{s,s+t^{\prime}}f
almost surely. On the other hand, unconditionally we have that
G\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{K}_{s,s+t^{\prime}}f
. Therefore
G=\mathcal{K}_{s,s+t^{\prime}}f
almost surely by
Lemma

5.1
, yielding (
32
) (and therefore (
31
)).

Now, for
(x,s;y,t)\in\mathbb{Q}^{4}_{\uparrow}
define
\mathcal{M}(x,s;y,t)=\mathcal{K}_{s,t}\delta_{x}(y)
. Then
\mathcal{M}
satisfies conditions
1-3
of
Theorem

1.1
. Condition
2
follows by the independent increment property of
\mathcal{K}
, condition
3
follows from (
31
), and for the triangle inequality (condition
1
), observe that for any rationals
q,q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}
with
q^{\prime}\leq\mathcal{K}_{s,r}\delta_{x}(y)\leq q
we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(x,s;y,r)+\mathcal{M}(y,r;z,t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}_{s,r}\delta_{x}(y)+\mathcal{K}_{r,t}\delta_{y}(z)

\displaystyle\leq q+\mathcal{K}_{r,t}\delta_{y}(z)=\mathcal{K}_{r,t}(\delta_{y}+q^{\prime})(z)+(q-q^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{K}_{s,t}\delta_{x}+(q-q^{\prime})=\mathcal{M}(x,s;z,t)+(q-q^{\prime}).

Here in the equality on the second line we have used shift commutativity of
\mathcal{K}
. In the inequality on the third line we have used monotonicity of
\mathcal{K}
together with the fact that
\delta_{y}+q^{\prime}\leq\mathcal{K}_{s,t}\delta_{x}
by the choice of
q^{\prime}
. Since
q,q^{\prime}
were arbitrary, this yields the triangle inequality.

Applying
Theorem

1.1
, we see that
\mathcal{M}
has a continuous extension to
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
which is a directed landscape. This implies (
7
) when
f=\delta_{x}
for some
x\in\mathbb{R}
. For general
f\in\NW
, we can write
f=\max_{x\in F}(\delta_{x}+q_{x})
for some finite rational set
F
and rationals
q_{x},x\in F
. Therefore

\mathcal{K}_{s,t}f\geq\max_{x\in F}\mathcal{K}_{s,t}(\delta_{x}+q_{x})=\max_{x\in F}[q_{x}+\mathcal{K}_{s,t}\delta_{x}]=\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}}f(x)+\mathcal{M}(x,s;\cdot,t).

(33)

Here the inequality uses monotonicity of
\mathcal{K}
, the first equality uses shift commutativity and the second equality is by definition. Now, the left and right-hand sides above are equal in law since
\mathcal{M}
is a directed landscape, so by
Lemma

5.1
they are equal almost surely.
∎

Proof of
Corollary

1.5
.

We have that almost surely,

\mathcal{K}_{s,t}f\geq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{Q}}\mathcal{K}_{s,t}(f|_{\{x\}})=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{Q}}f(x)+\mathcal{L}(x,s;\cdot,t)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}f(x)+\mathcal{L}(x,s;\cdot,t).

Here the inequality uses monotonicity of
\mathcal{K}
, the first equality uses
Theorem

1.3
, and the final equality uses condition (

) and continuity of
\mathcal{L}
. Finally, the two sides above are equal in law and so by

5.1
they are equal almost surely.
∎

We now state and prove a technical variant of
Theorem

1.3
. First, let
\mathscr{H}
be the set of continuous functions
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

•

There exists a finite set
\{q_{1}<\dots<q_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{Q}
such that
h
is linear on each of the pieces
[q_{i},q_{i+1}]
, and
h(q_{i})\in\mathbb{Q}
for all

.

•

h^{\prime}=0
off of the compact interval
[q_{1},q_{k}]
.

Note that the set
\mathscr{H}
is countable.

Theorem 5.2
.

Suppose that for every
s\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}
and every

we have a random continuous function
B_{s,n}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that the tail
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{T}_{s}
for each of the sequence

is trivial.

Let
\mathscr{H}_{s,n}=\mathscr{H}+B_{s,n}
and
\mathscr{H}_{s}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathscr{H}_{s,n}
.
Suppose that for every
s\leq t\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}
there is a family of random operators
\mathcal{K}_{s,t}:\mathscr{H}_{s}\to\operatorname{UC},s\leq t\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}
such that:

1.

(KPZ fixed point marginals) For
s\leq t\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}
and

, we have the equality in law
(\mathcal{K}_{s,t}f,f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(\mathfrak{h}_{t}f,f)
.

2.

(Conditional independence) Fix

, and define the
\sigma

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\{B_{s,n},\mathcal{K}_{s,r}:s\leq r\leq t\in\mathbb{Q}_{+},n\in\mathbb{N}\}

,
\mathcal{K}_{t,r}|_{\mathscr{H}_{t,n}}

3.

(Comparability) For all
s\leq r\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}
and

4.

(Monotonicity) For any
s\leq r<t\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}
and

.

5.

(Shift commutativity) For all
s<t\in\mathbb{Q}_{+},f\in\mathscr{H}_{s},c\in\mathbb{Q}
,
\mathcal{K}_{s,t}(f+c)=\mathcal{K}_{s,t}f+c
.

Then there exists a directed landscape
\mathcal{L}
such that almost surely, for any
s<t\in\mathbb{Q}_{+},f\in\mathscr{H}_{s}
,

\mathcal{K}_{s,t}f=f\diamond\mathcal{L}_{s,t}.

(34)

Proof.

For every
f\in\NW,s\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define a (random) sequence
f_{m}=f_{m}(s,n),m\in\mathbb{N}
in
\mathscr{H}
as follows. Let
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell}\}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:f(x)>-\infty\}
. Define
f_{m}=\max_{i=1,\dots,\ell}(g_{m,i}+f(x_{i}))
where
g_{m,i}
is any function chosen that
g_{m,i}(x_{i})\in(-B_{s,n}(x_{i})+1/(m+1),-B_{s,n}(x_{i})+1/m]

g_{m,i}^{\prime}(x)=\begin{cases}m,\qquad&x\in(x_{i}-1,x_{i}),\\
-m,\qquad&x\in(x_{i},x_{i}+1),\\
0,\qquad&x\notin[x_{i}-1,x_{i}+1].\end{cases}

Then
B_{s,n}+f_{m}(s,n)\downarrow f
in
\operatorname{UC}_{0}
. By monotonicity of
\mathcal{K}
, for any fixed
t\geq s\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}
the functions
\mathcal{K}_{s,t}(B_{s,n}+f_{m}(s,n))
form a monotone sequence almost surely. Let
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{s,t}^{n}f
denote the almost sure limit of this monotone sequence. By
Proposition

2.4
,
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{s,t}^{n}f\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathfrak{h}_{t-s}f
.

We check that
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{s,t}^{n}f=\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{s,t}^{n^{\prime}}f
almost surely for all
n,n^{\prime}
. Indeed, by Condition
3
(comparability) and our choice of approximation, there exists some constant

such that
B_{s,n}\leq B_{s,n^{\prime}}+c
. Then for any fixed
m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
m_{0}(m^{\prime})
such that for all

we have that

B_{s,n}+f_{m}(s,n)\leq B_{s,n^{\prime}}+f_{m^{\prime}}(s,n^{\prime}).

Using this inequality and monotonicity of
\mathcal{K}
implies that
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{s,t}^{n}f\leq\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{s,t}^{n^{\prime}}f
. By a symmetric argument, we also have that
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{s,t}^{n}f\geq\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{s,t}^{n^{\prime}}f
. From now on we drop the superscript

and write
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{s,t}f=\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{s,t}^{n}f
. We check that the family
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{s,t}f
satisfies the conditions of
Theorem

1.3
.

The KPZ fixed point marginal condition was established above, and shift commutativity is immediate since we chose the prelimiting sequences so that
f_{m}+q=(f+q)_{m}
for

, and the original operators
\mathcal{K}_{s,t}:\mathscr{H}_{s}\to\operatorname{UC}
satisfy shift commutativity.

We move to the independent increment property.
Fix
t\leq r\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}
.
First observe that by condition
2
above (conditional independence), for any

\mathcal{A}
with
\sigma(B_{t,n})\subset\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{F}_{t}

\mathcal{G}_{t}=\sigma(\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{s,s^{\prime}}:s\leq s^{\prime}\leq t\in\mathbb{Q}_{+})

. Let

and note that

for any fixed

\mathbb{P}[A\cap B\mid\mathcal{T}_{t,n}]=\mathbb{P}[A\mid\mathcal{T}_{t,n}]\mathbb{P}[B\mid\mathcal{T}_{t,n}].

(35)

Taking

. Since we constructed

In the remaining sections, we apply our general framework to prove convergence of specific models to the directed landscape.
All convergence results are new, except for convergence of colored TASEP and ASEP. In the case of colored TASEP, our method offers a shorter route to proving convergence that bypasses a technical analysis of the underlying line ensembles.

6
Web distances

For coalescing random walks, a classical probability model and the dual of the voter model, the trajectories form an infinite tree.
In the discrete-time setting with state space
\mathbb{Z}
, this structure gives rise to a
(1+1)
-dimensional random walk web.
Under diffusive scaling limits, it converges to the Brownian web, which can be understood as coalescing Brownian motions. The Brownian web was first constructed by Tóth and Werner
[
TW98
]
, building on the work of Arratia
[
Arr79
]
.
The convergence of the random walk web to the Brownian web was established in
[
FINR04
]
, where the term Brownian web was introduced. More background and results about this model can be found in the related survey
[
SSS17
]
.

In the random walk web and the Brownian web, Vető and Virág
[
VV23
]
introduced two models of random directed metrics. Through a correspondence with classical models of last passage percolation, they showed that certain two-parameter marginals of these directed metrics converge to the KPZ fixed point run from the narrow wedge initial condition. The correspondence with classical last passage percolation models does not extend to the full four-parameter field in these models and so convergence to the directed landscape was not clear. They left this as a conjecture in their paper. We can resolve this conjecture with an application of Theorem
1.1
.

We next define these two models precisely. Let
\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{e}=\{(i,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:i+n\text{ is even}\}
be the even lattice, and from every point
v=(i,n)
assign two outgoing directed edges to
(i-1,n+1)
and
(i+1,n+1)
. Let
\{\zeta(v),v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{e}\}
be a field of i.i.d. Rademacher random variables. The
random walk web

Y
is a family of coalescing random walks
Y_{(i,n)}:\llbracket n,\infty\rrbracket\to\mathbb{Z}
started at each point
(i,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{e}
, given by setting
Y_{(i,n)}(n)=i
and

Y_{(i,n)}(j+1)=Y_{(i,n)}(j)+\zeta(Y_{(i,n)}(j),j),\qquad\text{ for }j\in\llbracket n,\infty\rrbracket.

We can now define the
random walk web distance

D^{\operatorname{RW}}
.

Definition 6.1
.

For any
(i,n;j,m)\in(\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{e})^{2}
let
D^{\operatorname{RW}}(i,n;j,m)
be the smallest non-negative
integer
k
such that there are points
(i_{0},n_{0})=(i,n),\dots,(i_{k},n_{k}),(i_{k+1},n_{k+1})=(j,m)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{e}
with the following property. There
are random walk paths in
Y
from
(i_{0},n_{0})
to
(i_{1},n_{1})
and from
(i_{j}-\zeta(i_{j},n_{j}),n_{j}+1)
to
(i_{j+1},n_{j+1})
for all
1\leq j\leq k
. We set
D^{\operatorname{RW}}(i,n;j,m)=\infty
if no such
k
exists.

In the scaling limit where the system of random walks
D^{\operatorname{RW}}
converges to a system of coalescing Brownian motions (the Brownian web), the random walk web distance converges to a limit known as the
Brownian web distance

D^{\operatorname{Br}}
which has a description similar to
Definition

6.1
. To define this distance, we first need a precise definition of the Brownian web.

We can give an explicit recursive construction of the
rational Brownian web

(B_{q})_{q\in\mathbb{Q}^{2}}
:

1.

Let
q_{i}=(x_{i},s_{i}),i\in\mathbb{N}
be any enumeration of
\mathbb{Q}^{2}
, and sample independent standard Brownian paths
W_{q_{i}},i\in\mathbb{N}
, where
W_{q_{i}}:[s_{i},\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
has initial condition
W_{q_{i}}(s_{i})=x_{i}
. Set
B_{q_{1}}=W_{q_{1}}.

2.

For all
i\geq 2
, given
B_{q_{1}},\dots,B_{q_{i-1}}
, let
T
be the first time in
[s_{i},\infty)
when
W_{q_{i}}(t)=B_{q_{j}}(t)
for some
j<i
, and let
J
be the minimal index such that
W_{q_{i}}(T)=B_{q_{J}}(T)
. Then set
B_{q_{i}}(t)=W_{q_{i}}(t)
for
t\leq T
and
B_{q_{i}}(t)=B_{q_{J}}(t)
for
t\geq T
.

Intuitively, this construction gives a countable collection of Brownian motions that run independently until they hit, after which point they run together. The law of this construction is independent of the choice of the enumeration of
\mathbb{Q}
. To construct the full Brownian web, we take the closure of the set of paths
(B_{q})_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}
is an appropriate topology.

Following
[
FINR04
]
, we put a topology on the space
\Pi
of all continuous functions
f:[t,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
, where
t\in\mathbb{R}
. First, for such a function
f
, let
\hat{f}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the function equal to
f
on
[t,\infty)
and equal to
f(t)
on
(-\infty,t)
.
We say that a sequence of continuous functions
f_{n}:[t_{n},\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
converges to a limit
f:[t,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
if
t_{n}\to t
and
\hat{f}_{n}\to\hat{f}
uniformly on compact sets. This defines a Polish space. The
Brownian web
is the closure
\mathcal{B}
of the rational Brownian web
\{B_{q}:q\in\mathbb{Q}^{2}\}
in this space. Given the Brownian web, we can now define the Brownian web distance.

Definition 6.2
.

For
(x,s;y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
, define the
Brownian web distance

D^{\operatorname{Br}}(x,s;y,t)
to be the infimum over all non-negative integers
k
for which there exist points

(x_{0},t_{0})=(x,s),(x_{1},t_{1}),\dots,(x_{k},t_{k}),(x_{k+1},t_{k+1})=(y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

such that
s<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{k}<t
, and there is a continuous path
\pi:[s,t]\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\pi(t_{i})=x_{i}
for each
i\in\llbracket 0,k+1,\rrbracket
, and for each
i\in\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket
there exists
B_{i}\in\mathcal{B}
with
\pi|_{[t_{i},t_{i+1}]}=B_{i}|_{[t_{i},t_{i+1}]}
. The infimum is equal to
+\infty
if there is no such
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Note that in
Definition

6.2
, the points
(t_{i},x_{i}),i=1,\dots,k
are special: these are points that both lie on the interior of some Brownian path
B\in\mathcal{B}
,
and
at the beginning of a different Brownian path
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
which is not contained in
B
. Such points are not visible if we restrict our attention to the rational Brownian web, since when restricted to the rationals, there is a unique Brownian path emanating from each point.

Neither of the distances
D^{\operatorname{RW}}
or
D^{\operatorname{Br}}
are true metrics, because they are not symmetric or finite everywhere, but both are directed metrics on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{e}
and
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, respectively.

One of the main results of
[
VV23
]
is a two-parameter convergence result relating
D^{\operatorname{RW}},D^{\operatorname{Br}}
to the directed landscape. To set up this theorem, for
\eta\in(0,1)
define positive constants

a_{\eta}=\frac{(1-\eta^{2})^{1/6}}{(\eta/2)^{2/3}},\quad b_{\eta}=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-\eta^{2}}}{2},\quad c_{\eta}=\frac{\eta^{1/3}(1-\eta^{2})^{1/6}}{2^{1/3}},\quad d_{\eta}=2^{2/3}\eta^{1/3}(1-\eta^{2})^{2/3},

and for
n\in\mathbb{N}
define rescalings

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\eta}_{n}(x,s;y,t)

\displaystyle=-a_{\eta}n^{-1/3}\left(D^{\operatorname{RW}}(\eta nt+d_{\eta}n^{2/3}y,-nt;\eta ns+d_{\eta}n^{2/3}x,-ns)-b_{\eta}n(t-s)-c_{\eta}n^{2/3}(y-x)\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{n}(x,s;y,t)

\displaystyle=-n^{1/3}\Big(D^{\operatorname{Br}}(2yn^{2/3}+2tn,-tn;2xn^{2/3}+2sn,-sn)-n(t-s)-2n^{2/3}(y-x)\Big).

Here in the rescaling of
D^{\operatorname{RW}}
we actually need to take integer parts in the argument in order to put the points on the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{e}
; throughout this section we ignore this minor point, for the convenience of notations. Note also that while we consider rescalings of
D^{\operatorname{RW}}
in all directions
\eta
, we only consider the rescaling of
D^{\operatorname{Br}}
in one direction; other directions are equivalent by Brownian scaling. Finally, it is worth pointing out the argument exchange on
(x,s)
and
(y,t)

Let

Exclusion processes

Finally we consider general finite range asymmetric exclusion processes (AEPs). Consider a jump distribution
p
on
\mathbb{Z}
such that
\{v:p(v)+p(-v)>0\}
is finite and additively generates
\mathbb{Z}
, and whose expectation satisfies
\sum_{v}vp(v)=1
.

We encode particle configurations on
\mathbb{Z}
by functions
\eta:\mathbb{Z}\to\{0,1\}
. For each
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\eta(x)=1
means there is a particle at
x
, and
\eta(x)=0
means there is no particle at
x
. For any particle configuration
\eta
, we can write down its height function
h:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
through the rule that
h(x+1)-h(x)=2\eta(x)-1
for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}
. For an AEP evolution
h_{t}
, as discussed in the introduction we have a choice as to the anchor point
h_{0}(0)
. We always assume that
h_{0}(0)\in 2\mathbb{Z}
.

As in
[
QS23
]
, we let
\SRW_{\epsilon}\subset\operatorname{UC}
be the set of functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for each
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,
f|_{[x\epsilon/2,(x+1)\epsilon/2]}
is linear with slope
\pm 2\epsilon^{-1/2}
and
f(0)\in 2\epsilon^{1/2}\mathbb{Z}
. We call
\SRW_{\epsilon}
the set of simple random walk paths at scale
\epsilon
. Then
\SRW:=\SRW_{1}
is the state space for an AEP if we extend our AEP height functions to be linear on each of the pieces
[x,x+1],x\in\mathbb{Z}
. Let
A_{\epsilon}:\SRW\to\SRW_{\epsilon}
be the natural rescaling map given by
A_{\epsilon}f(x)=\epsilon^{1/2}f(2\epsilon^{-1}x)
.

To put things into the framework of
Theorem

1.3
and
Theorem

5.2
, we will encode the dynamics of a collection of coupled AEPs using a collection of random operators
\mathcal{K}^{\epsilon}=\{\mathcal{K}^{\epsilon}_{s,t}:\SRW_{\epsilon}\to\SRW_{\epsilon},s\leq t\in\mathbb{R}\}
.
Specifically, suppose that we have a collection of coupled AEPs

,

, defined for all

7.1
ASEPs under various couplings

As discussed in the introduction, the nearest neighbor case where
p(v)\neq 0
only when
v\in\{-1,1\}
is also referred to as the asymmetric simple exclusion process (ASEP). The case where
p(1)=1,p(-1)=0
is TASEP. For ASEP, Aggarwal, Corwin, and Hegde
[
ACH24b
,
ACH24a
]
proved convergence to the KPZ fixed point.

Theorem 7.1
.

Consider a collection of functions
f^{\epsilon}\in\SRW_{\epsilon},\epsilon>0
, and
f\in\operatorname{UC},f\not\equiv-\infty
such that
f^{\epsilon}\to f
in
\operatorname{UC}
. Suppose that there exists a constant
A>0
with
f^{\epsilon}(x)\leq A(|x|+1)
for each
\epsilon>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Consider ASEP with jump distribution
p
. Then for any fixed
t>0
,
\mathcal{K}^{\epsilon}_{0,t}f^{\epsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathfrak{h}_{t}f
in the topology of uniform convergence on compact sets.

In the special case of TASEP, this is the main theorem of
[
MQR21
]
. For general ASEP, this is proven as part of Theorem 2.10 in
[
ACH24a
]
. That theorem actually shows the stronger claim that when
k
ASEP evolutions are coupled together in the basic coupling, then evolutions converge jointly to
k
KPZ fixed points coupled through the directed landscape. One consequence of the upcoming Theorem
7.4
is a proof of this in the special case of basic coupled TASEP that bypasses the Yang-Baxter framework of
[
ACH24b
,
ACH24a
]
.

To describe different couplings of ASEP, we will think of the dynamics as being generated by a collection of Poisson point processes on
\mathbb{R}
, which we call
Poisson clocks
. A Poisson point at time
t\in\mathbb{R}
will encode the attempt of a certain particle to make a certain jump at time
t
. The following natural couplings can be described in this way:

•

Basic coupling (colored ASEP).
This coupling was previously described in the introduction. To each
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, we associate two Poisson clocks, with rate
p(1)
and
p(-1)
respectively. All Poisson clocks are jointly independent.
The rate
p(1)
and
p(-1)
clocks associated to
x
encode the jumps of particles from site
x
to
x+1
and
x-1
, respectively, simultaneously for all instances of ASEP.

•

Particle coupling.
In each of the ASEPs, we label all the particles by integers, such that if a particle is labeled
\ell
, then the next particle to its right is labeled
\ell-1
.

For each
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
, we associate two Poisson clocks, with rate
p(1)
and
p(-1)
respectively. All such Poisson clocks are jointly independent.
The rate
p(1)
(resp.
p(-1)
) clock associated to
\ell
encodes the right (resp. left) jumps of the particle with label
\ell
, simultaneously for all instances of ASEP.

•

Hole coupling.
Instead of thinking of ASEP as particles attempting to move to the right with rate
p(1)
and to the left with rate
p(-1)
, the model can equivalently be described as each hole attempting to move to the left with rate
p(1)
and to the right with rate
p(-1)
, independently for all the holes.
In each of the instances of ASEP, we label all the holes by integers, such that if a hole is labeled
\ell
, then the next hole to its right is labeled
\ell+1
.

For each
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have two Poisson clocks, with rate
p(1)
and
p(-1)
respectively. All such Poisson clocks are jointly independent.
The rate
p(1)
(resp.
p(-1)
) clock associated to hole
\ell
encodes the left (resp. right) jumps of this hole.

These three couplings are examples of more general
exotic couplings
, as defined in the introduction. We give an alternate, equivalent, description of the exotic couplings here using labels on particles and holes rather than on sites and heights.

For any ASEP configuration, we label all particles and holes by integers via the height function.
More precisely, for a particle configuration with height function
h:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
, if a particle is at location
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, it is given the label
-(h(x)+x)/2
; if a hole is at location
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, it is given the label
(-h(x)+x)/2
.
It is straightforward to check that this labeling rule is compatible with ASEP dynamics.

Labels.
For technical reasons to handle non-nearest neighbor moves, we make an important augmentation to the above dynamics, via

Whenever a move happens, the labels evolve under the following rules:

•

•

The type-2 (resp. type-3) labels are always increasing from left to right.

•

The set of all type-2 (resp. type-3) labels either remains the same, or has one label removed.

For any type-2 label
