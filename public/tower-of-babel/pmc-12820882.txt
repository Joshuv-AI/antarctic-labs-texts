School-based and animation-based comprehensive sexuality education in Kenya: protocol for a quasiexperimental study
Authors: Yifan Gao, Shuangyu Zhao, Douglas Okenyoru, Yiping Lu, Rudong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAdolescents in Kenya face a high burden of sexual and reproductive health (SRH) challenges, including early pregnancy, sexually transmitted infections and limited access to accurate sexuality education. While school-based comprehensive sexuality education (SBCSE) has shown promise globally, its implementation in Kenya has faced significant barriers, including cultural resistance and lack of trained personnel. To address these gaps, the Afya Kesho (‘Health for Tomorrow’) programme introduces an innovative, animation-based, school-integrated and community-integrated comprehensive sexuality education curriculum aimed at enhancing adolescent SRH knowledge, attitudes and behaviours in rural and periurban Kenya. This manuscript presents the protocol of the Afya Kesho programme.Methods and analysisThis quasiexperimental study will employ a preintervention and postintervention design to evaluate the effectiveness of the Afya Kesho programme. The intervention, delivered in three schools across Nairobi and Kiambu counties, includes 17 animated episodes structured into eight interactive SBCSE sessions facilitated by trained school health teachers and community health promoters. A total of 422 adolescents aged 10–19 will be recruited using multistage sampling. Quantitative data will be collected at baseline and endline using a structured questionnaire adapted from the Global School-based Student Health Survey and the Global Diet Quality Score. Primary outcomes include SRH knowledge, gender norms and intimacy, while secondary outcomes include physical and mental health, dietary behaviours and healthcare utilisation. Statistical analysis using STATA V.17 will include descriptive, inferential and multivariate regression analyses. The final protocol is available on Open Science Forum (https://osf.io/fx38c).Ethics and disseminationEthical approval has been granted by the Kenyatta National Hospital-University of Nairobi Ethics and Research Committee (P854/12/2024). Informed consent will be obtained from all participants and their guardians. The study prioritises minimal disruption and adheres to ethical standards of adolescent research. Findings will be disseminated through open-access publications, infographics, school-based brochures and digital platforms. Anonymised datasets will be made publicly available on the Innovation for Health Equity in Africa website on completion.Keywords: Health, Adolescent, Health Education, SEXUAL MEDICINE

Introduction

Adolescence (defined as 10–19 years) is a crucial period for personal development as it sets the foundation for lifelong capacities and aspirations,1 including those related to sexual and reproductive health (SRH).2 While adolescent sexual and reproductive health (ASRH) has improved significantly worldwide, for instance, through increased contraceptive use,3 ASRH challenges remain prevalent in low- and middle-income countries.4 In Kenya, for instance, 15% of adolescent girls aged 15–19 have ever been pregnant in the country.5 Poor ASRH outcomes, such as early and unintended pregnancies, unsafe abortions, increased risks of sexually transmitted infections (STI), including HIV, and sexual violence,6 7 are closely linked to a range of adverse health consequences. For example, adolescents face higher maternal health risks due to early pregnancy, including pre-eclampsia, eclampsia, puerperal endometritis and systemic infections, which are among the leading causes of adolescent mortality worldwide.3 ASRH problems might also lead to psychological impacts such as trauma and depression.8 Lack of education,9 limited access to healthcare services,10 cultural stigma of adolescent sexuality8 and gender inequality served as major reasons for ASRH problems in the country, particularly in low-income areas such as rural and periurban regions.

Multiple interventions at the individual, community and structural levels have been implemented in Kenya and other sub-Saharan African (SSA) countries to address the persistent challenges in ASRH.11 12 Global actions from international organisations, such as the Global Early Adolescent Study of the WHO,13 have also been implemented in the region to investigate the causes of poor ASRH outcomes and to address their impacts through evidence-based interventions. Among all types of interventions, school-based comprehensive sexuality education (SBCSE) has been argued to be the most inclusive of all intervention approaches.12 Schools serve as an important platform for socialising adolescents into healthy and/or unhealthy behaviours.14 School-based ASRH education and intervention programmes therefore showed high potential in improving ASRH outcomes, including improved sexual knowledge,15 delayed sexual debut14 16 and more contraceptive use,17 thus resulting in less teenage pregnancy and reduced STIs.16

Different from traditional sex education programmes that primarily focused on abstinence, comprehensive sexuality education (CSE) emphasises a holistic approach that includes the ‘full range of topics’ related to sexuality,18 aimed at empowering adolescents through thorough education on SRH-related concerns and practices, positive sexualities and respectful relationships, young people’s rights, participation and agency, as well as gender equality and power relations.19 Compared with abstinence-only education, CSE programmes have been proved to be more effective in improving ASRH outcomes such as contraceptive use.20 However, research has showcased mixed results in SBCSE’s influence on ASRH across regions.21 22 The inconsistency of findings might be due to structural barriers that hindered the effective implementation of SBCSE interventions in SSA countries, for example, cultural and religious resistance to adolescent sexuality,23 24 and lack of trained personnel or standardised manuals.25 Similarly, while the Kenyan government agreed on scaling up rights-based CSE programmes starting from primary school in 2013,24 adolescents still faced inadequate and partial sexuality education, leading to unexpected SRH outcomes and quest for comprehensive SRH information.26

Taken together, there is an urgent need for more rigorous, context-sensitive and evidence-based approaches to SBCSE interventions for Kenyan adolescents. Under such circumstances, this manuscript presents an animation-based SBCSE programme Afya Kesho (Kiswahili for ‘Health for Tomorrow’), which was developed to deliver innovative, comprehensive and gender-transformative sexuality education to adolescents in periurban and rural Kenya. In response to the shortage of qualified personnel and the lack of CSE in Kenyan schools, the Afya Kesho programme developed a scientific curriculum including 17 animated videos that are aligned with key topics under UNESCO’s international technical guidance on sexuality education.18 Capacity-building sessions of health teachers and community health promoters (CHPs) will be conducted prior to the intervention. Moreover, the programme was designed to be culturally sensitive and contextually appropriate, guided by ongoing consultations with a local community advisory board. A quasiexperimental study was further developed to evaluate the programme’s intervention outcomes.

Method and analysis

Study design and locations

This study employs a quasiexperimental preintervention and postintervention design to evaluate the effectiveness of the Afya Kesho (‘Health for Tomorrow’ in Swahili), an animation-based SBCSE intervention, in improving adolescent health outcomes in rural and periurban Kenya. The intervention further integrates digital health education, school and community engagement, as well as gender-transformative strategies to create broad and sustainable influences on ASRH. To assess its impact, data will be collected at baseline and postintervention, allowing for a comparative analysis of changes in ASRH knowledge, attitudes and behaviours.

The study is set in Nairobi and Kiambu counties, representing periurban and rural populations, respectively. Participants will be recruited from three schools situated in low- and middle-income communities. The selection of schools is intended to capture a broad spectrum of adolescent experiences, and all participating schools will receive the intervention. The effectiveness of the programme will be measured through within-group comparisons over time.

Study population and sampling

The study population consists of adolescents aged 10–19 years enrolled in selected schools within Nairobi and Kiambu counties. Eligibility requires a minimum 6-month school attendance before enrolment, informed consent from participants and parental consent for those under 18. Adolescents with severe cognitive or physical impairments that would prevent participation will be excluded. The sample will include both male and female students to allow for a comprehensive analysis of health behaviours across sex.

We assumed a 50% prevalence of key adolescent health behaviours, a 5% margin of error and a 95% confidence level. Based on these parameters, a sample size of 384 participants was calculated. To account for potential non-response or incomplete data, the final adjusted sample size is set at 422 participants. The sample will be evenly distributed across the three selected schools, ensuring representation across different age groups. Specifically, 140 participants will be recruited from the 10–13 age group, 140 from the 14–16 age group and 142 from the 17–19 age group.

A multistage sampling strategy will be employed to enhance representativeness. Schools will be purposively selected from low- and middle-income communities based on established networks. The sampling approach ensures the inclusion of institutions that serve diverse adolescent populations in rural and periurban settings. Within each selected school, all eligible students will be included in the study, ensuring comprehensive participation, which allows for an inclusive analysis of health behaviours while minimising selection bias and improving the generalisability of findings.

Data collection and measures

Data collection will occur at two time points: baseline and postintervention. A structured questionnaire, adapted from the Global School-based Student Health Survey (GSHS),28 will measure key adolescent health indicators. The survey will be administered anonymously in a classroom setting. Each participant will complete the questionnaire individually, without providing names or identifying information. To ensure privacy, students will be seated with adequate spacing, and trained research assistants will provide standardised instructions and respond to questions without reviewing individual answers.

The primary focus is on gender norms and intimacy, as well as SRH knowledge and behaviours. Secondary outcomes include physical health, mental health, diet behaviours and healthcare utilisation. The instrument covers multiple domains, with diet quality assessed using the Global Diet Quality Score (GDQS),29 a validated tool for measuring dietary diversity and nutrient adequacy. Before full implementation, the questionnaire will be pilot tested with a small group of students to evaluate clarity, cultural relevance and reliability. More details are provided in table 1.

Interventions

Animation-based CSE curriculum

The animation series used in this study was originally designed for rural Chinese adolescents based on UNESCO’s technical guidance for sexuality education,18 and has been shown to be effective in improving sexual health knowledge and attitudes among Chinese students.30 With permission from the animation producers, the research team held a workshop in July 2024 with local stakeholders in Nairobi and Kiambu counties. This gathering brought together CHPs, researchers, youth representatives and local officials to discuss the cultural relevance and appropriateness of adapting the series for the Kenyan context.

Based on the feedback from local stakeholders and insights from pilot qualitative studies in local schools, the research team modified the series by incorporating Kiswahili names, translating content into English and re-editing episodes to feature English narration. The final curriculum consists of 17 stand-alone episodes, each 3–5 min long, covering key Sexual and Reproductive Health and Rights (SRHR) topics. These episodes were further structured into eight interactive sessions, covering topics including puberty, menstruation, interpersonal relationships, intimate relationships, sexual harassment, campus violence and gender equality.

CHPs and school health teachers will be designated as CSE session facilitators. For each session, around three CHPs and one health teacher will be present to facilitate the lesson. Prior to implementation, facilitators received training and a comprehensive guide to support effective session delivery. Each lesson was designed to last between 45 min and 1 hour, incorporating interactive games and quizzes aligned with the animation content to promote adolescent engagement and reinforce key messages. The roll-out of the intervention will align with each school’s academic calendar and policies, lasting approximately two academic semesters (~6 months). Adolescents were also encouraged to invite peers and parents from their communities to participate, fostering broader engagement and support.

Gender-transformative approach

Recognising the critical need for addressing traditional gender norms in SRHR education, the Afya Kesho programme adopts a gender-transformative approach, which refers to strategies that challenge harmful gender norms, stereotypes and power imbalances.31 The programme integrates these components to promote gender equality by actively engaging both boys and girls in all intervention sessions. Evidence showed that involving boys and men in sexuality education contributes to advancing gender equality and improving health outcomes for all.32 Findings from pilot focus group discussions among Kenyan adolescents in the study also revealed notable gender-based SRHR knowledge gaps, for example, boys showed little knowledge of menstruation. By ensuring boys’ inclusion, the intervention fosters open dialogue and mutual understanding.

Meanwhile, different from previous boy-engaged interventions that mostly failed to be gender transformative,33 or focused solely on empowering girls while neglecting harmful masculinities,31 Afya Kesho developed educational materials that challenge gender norms for both girls and boys. One animation episode, for example, addresses career stereotypes, illustrating that boys can become kindergarten teachers. The curriculum also encourages adolescents to question stereotypes and embrace diverse expressions of identity. Through the gender-transformative approach, the project seeks to contribute to long-term shifts in gender norms and improve SRHR outcomes for adolescents of all genders.

Monitoring and evaluation

The implementation of the intervention will be monitored using data collected through the GSHS and GDQS tools. The local implementation team, including teachers and CHPs, will track progress of sessions and ensure alignment with the intervention design. Biweekly facilitator reports will provide real-time insights into implementation, while postintervention surveys will measure changes in adolescent health knowledge, attitudes and self-reported behaviours. Statistical analyses will assess intervention outcomes, with findings shared with community advisory boards to ensure transparency and inform future decision-making.

Data management and analysis plan

In addition to survey data, digital health platform analytics will be used to assess engagement with online learning modules and participation in virtual health consultations. The platform will record usage metrics such as time spent on health modules, frequency of logins and completion rates of digital health education materials. These data will provide insights into how adolescents interact with the digital component of the intervention and the extent of its reach. Following the completion of the intervention, we will conduct focus groups with participants to obtain their feedback.

On completion of data collection, survey responses will be entered into a secure database and subjected to thorough cleaning and validation procedures. Quantitative data will be analysed using STATA V.17, employing descriptive statistics to summarise baseline characteristics and outcome measures. Inferential analyses will use multivariate regression models to assess associations between exposure to the intervention and self-reported SRH knowledge, attitudes and behaviours, adjusting for potential confounders. Analyses will be stratified by age group (10–14 years and 15–19 years) to account for developmental and contextual differences.

Patient and public involvement

The active involvement of the public is central to public health research agenda, providing local insight into research design and implementation procedures.34 Afya Kesho is grounded in a participatory, community-driven approach that prioritises the voices and experiences of adolescents and relevant local stakeholders, including parents, teachers, CHPs and local health and education officials. Between July and August 2024, four group meetings were held with teachers, CHPs and local and national officials to inform the research design. Adolescents from local communities were invited to participate in the design and pilot phases, providing feedback on the questionnaire and intervention content. A community advisory board, composed of CHPs and teachers, was established to maintain cultural relevance and responsiveness throughout the project. This collaborative approach ensures that Afya Kesho remains aligned with local needs, reinforcing its potential to drive sustainable improvements in adolescent SRHR outcomes.

Trial status

This trial began recruitment in March 2025. Baseline data collection started in Ngewe Primary School in Kiambu county in March 2025 and will be finalised in the other two schools in Nairobi and Kiambu counties by May 2025. After the 1-year intervention period and the follow-up assessments, the study will be completed in December 2026. Innovation for Health Equity in Africa (IHEA), a local non-profit organisation leading the programme, will manage the trial and provide technical supervision over the duration of the study.

Registration and reporting

The final protocol has been registered in advance on the Open Science Forum, available at https://osf.io/fx38c, on 8 April 2025, based on the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Nonrandomized Designs (TREND) statement checklist. We will systematically record the date of each change, describe the modifications made and provide the rationale behind changes on the Open Science Forum.

Ethics and dissemination

The study protocol has been approved by the Kenyatta National Hospital-University of Nairobi Ethics and Research Committee (approval number: P854/12/2024), Kenya. Prior to the data collection and intervention implementation, official permissions will be sought from all participating schools. In line with the standards of GSHS and GDQS, all parents/guardians will be notified about the study. Informed consent will be obtained from both parents/guardians and the adolescent participants, in accordance with local regulations and school policies. The consent forms for adolescents and their guardians are listed in the online supplemental appendices.

The study is designed to minimise disruption to adolescents, schools and local communities. All data collection tools have been previously validated and applied in populations similar to the study sample. Through participatory planning and thorough review, the interventions will be carefully adapted to ensure low physical, psychological and social risks. Adolescents who participated in the codesigning procedure will receive compensation considered appropriate by the community advisory board. In all, the adolescent participants are expected to benefit from the intervention through enhanced knowledge, attitudes and behaviours, as well as improved health-related policies in local schools.

Following the completion of both baseline and endline data collection, anonymised datasets will be made publicly accessible on the IHEA website with the informed consent of adolescents and their guardians. Personal information about potential and enrolled participants will be collected using anonymised codes, stored in password-protected databases accessible only to the research team; all identifiers will be removed from datasets before analysis and publication to ensure confidentiality is protected before, during and after the trial. Results of the GSHS and GDQS surveys will be summarised in easily understandable formats, for example, infographics and brochures, to share with adolescents, parents, teachers, communities and broader audiences through social media.

Strengths and limitations

This study employs a quasiexperimental pre-post design to assess the effectiveness of an animation-based CSE programme in improving self-reported ASRH knowledge, attitudes and behaviours in periurban and rural Kenya. The intervention draws on a participatory adaptation of UNESCO-aligned animated videos originally developed in China, codesigned with local stakeholders and piloted for cultural relevance and contextual appropriateness. The intervention incorporates a gender-transformative approach, meaning that it challenges harmful gender norms and stereotypes while engaging both boys and girls to promote equitable ASRH knowledge, attitudes and relationships. Additionally, the integration of digital learning with in-person facilitation by trained school health teachers and CHPs is intended to strengthen both reach and sustainability within school and community sexuality education systems.

However, the study has several limitations. All behavioural outcomes are based on self-reported survey data, which may be subject to recall errors and social desirability bias, and do not allow assessment of whether changes translate into sustained or objectively measurable SRH practices. The requirement for parental consent may exclude some adolescents, introducing potential selection bias and limiting representativeness. In addition, limited electricity and audiovisual infrastructure in some rural schools may restrict full implementation of the video-based intervention, potentially affecting fidelity and reach.
