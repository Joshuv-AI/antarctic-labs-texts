CRISPR editing of HPFH3 genotype induces γ-globin expression and reverses sickle cell disease and β-thalassemia phenotypes
Authors: Sangam Giri Goswami, Pragya Gupta, V. R. Arvinden, Nupur Bhargava, Aditya Ramdas Iyer, Vinodh Saravanakumar, Poonam Yadav, Somesh K. Jha, Shivani Singh, Ajay Kumar, Praveen Singh, Padma Gunda, Suman Jain, Pallavi Mehta, Yukio Nakamura, Ryo Kurita, Avinash Bajaj, Sivaprakash Ramalingam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHereditary persistence of Fetal Hemoglobin (HPFH) is a benign condition known to mitigate symptoms in individuals with co-inherited β-hemoglobinopathies, such as β-thalassemia (BT) and sickle cell disease (SCD), through the reactivation of fetal hemoglobin (HbF). HPFH typically arises from deletions of varying sizes affecting the β-globin gene cluster or point mutations in the promoters of the γ-globin genes. While the therapeutic benefits of point mutations have been extensively studied, the potential of deletional forms of HPFH remains underexplored in preclinical settings.MethodIn this study, we generated benign deletional HPFH3 genotype in SCD and BT patient-derived HSPCs using CRISPR/Cas9 and showed that therapeutically relevant levels of HbF reactivation result in the alleviation of the pathological phenotypes.ResultsIn edited cells derived from SCD patients, we observed reduced sickling and oxidative stress, while in edited from BT cells, restoration of the α-globin/β-globin ratio improved erythroid lineage maturation and reduced ROS levels. Importantly, HPFH3-edited HSPCs retained their genome integrity and showed no detrimental effect on their regeneration or differentiation into erythroid, myeloid, T, and B cell lineages in immunodeficient NBSGW mice post-xenotransplantation. Additionally, we showed a reduced interaction between the LCR and HBB, suggesting that the HPFH3 deletion specifically promoted LCR interactions with HBG1/2, likely due to the absence of the HBB locus.ConclusionsCollectively, our preclinical findings suggest that the generation of the HPFH3 genotype has the potential to significantly enhance HbF levels, offering a promising universal therapeutic strategy for treating both SCD and β-thalassemia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04582-1.

Background

Β-hemoglobinopathies namely sickle cell disease (SCD) and β-thalassemia (BT), are the most prevalent recessively inherited monogenic disorders, posing a significant public health challenge due to mutations occurring in the β-globin gene[1]. An estimated 7% of the world's population carries a defective hemoglobin gene, and around 330,000 infants are born each year with severe hemoglobin abnormalities [2, 3]. SCD is caused by the substitution of glutamic acid with valine at the 6th position of the β-globin gene, resulting in the synthesis of sickled hemoglobin (HbS) which tends to polymerize, causing the erythrocytes to develop a characteristic sickle shape. This deformation significantly shortens the lifespan of circulating RBCs, leading to vaso-occlusive crises and subsequent multiorgan damage [4]. In contrast, BT, which affects approximately 70,000 newborns globally each year, is caused by over 350 distinct mutations in the β-globin gene, resulting in reduced or absent production of the β-globin chain [5–8].

Despite extensive research efforts over many decades,current management options for these disorders remain limited. The only globally recognized curative therapy is allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) transplantation. However, its application is challenged by donor scarcity and the risk of graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) as a potential complication [9, 10]. In 2022, the FDA approved Zyntelgo, a gene addition therapy product for β-thalassemia [11]. More recently, the FDA approved Casgevy, a genome-editing-based product that disrupts the erythroid enhancer BCL11A to reactivate HbF [12, 13]. However, with up to 50% of hemoglobin in SCD patients persisting as HbS, it is crucial to explore strategies aimed at reducing or eliminating the production of defective β-globin. Additionally, Lyfgenia, a lentiviral-based gene therapy, has been approved for the treatment of SCD [14, 15]. While these approaches are effective in managing SCD and BT, they have limitations in efficacy and safety. Low vector copy numbers reduce therapeutic globin expression, and the semi-random integration of Lentiviral Vectors (LVs) poses a genotoxic risk, especially in SCD where high vector copies are needed for clinical benefits [16, 17]. Recent advances in genome editing offer promising options for treating genetic disorders like SCD and BT [18–20]. CRISPR-mediated double-strand break (DSB) repair via non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) has advantages over homology-directed repair (HDR) for β-globin gene editing in β-hemoglobinopathies [21, 22]. As DSBs occur before HDR, error-prone NHEJ can compete with HDR, reducing the efficiency of genetic correction. This can lead to unintended gene disruptions, such as converting sickle cell alleles into β-thalassemic alleles, complicating precise repair in patient cells. Moreover, developing effective correction methods for genetically diverse β-thalassemia remains challenging as it requires tailored repair strategies for each mutation, compounded by the difficulties associated with therapeutic HDR in quiescent CD34 + HSPCs.

Several studies have demonstrated that enhanced HbF production is linked to decreased morbidity and mortality in both SCD and BT [23–26]. Among the various targets for HbF reactivation, BCL11A has been extensively studied. Two very recent studies have demonstrated that BCL11A plays a critical role in erythroid development and that editing the BCL11A erythroid enhancer in CD34 + HSPCs adversely affects erythroid production in a xenotransplantation setting [27, 28]. In this context, the long-term safety of targeting BCL11A remains uncertain. Therefore, it is essential to explore alternative targets for fetal hemoglobin reactivation. HPFH (hereditary Persistence of Fetal Hemoglobin) presents a promising avenue, as Individuals with HPFH show minimal symptoms of β-hemoglobinopathies due to lifelong γ-globin production, resulting in elevated HbF (α₂γ₂) levels that compensate for deficient β-globin [25]. Unlike other HbF-reactivating mutations, HPFH deletions eliminate complete HbA/HbS synthesis. These deletions vary in size from 12.5 to over 106 kb within the β-globin locus [29–32]. Importantly, numerous clinical reports have shown that individuals with large deletional HPFH mutations do not show clinical abnormalities over generations [33–35]. The above clinical observations have sparked interest in exploring strategies to introduce HPFH deletions into HSPCs to reactivate HbF synthesis in patients with β-hemoglobinopathies as a unified therapeutic approach. However, the translational potential of these naturally occurring large deletional HPFH mutations has not been fully investigated in vitro or preclinically, particularly concerning their effects on genome integrity, engraftment, and the repopulation potential of HSPCs, as well as their effectiveness in reversing disease phenotypes.

We recently demonstrated that introducing the beneficial HPFH3 genotype significantly enhances HbF levels in pathophysiologically relevant erythroid progenitor models of SCD and BT [36]. In this study, we further investigated the potential of HPFH3 to reactivate HbF expression in preclinical models. To examine the translational potential of HPFH deletions, we leveraged CRISPR/Cas9 to introduce the HPFH3 genotype in erythroid progenitor line HUDEP-2 as well as SCD and BT patient-derived HSPCs. The results indicate that the generation of benign HPFH3 genotype in patient cells effectively enhances the therapeutically relevant HbF levels without causing substantial genomic rearrangements. The editing effectively reversed the disease phenotype in patient-derived edited cells and showed long-term in vivo engraftment in an immunocompromised mouse model. Moreover, we demonstrated a reduced interaction between the LCR and HBB, implying that the HPFH3 deletion selectively enhanced LCR interactions with HBG1/2, likely due to the absence of the HBB locus. These findings highlight the potential of this universal strategy for future clinical applications in treating β-hemoglobinopathies. While BCL11A has been a key target in HbF reactivation research, its long-term therapeutic viability remains uncertain. In contrast, our study is the first to show that replicating a naturally occurring HPFH-linked genotype presents a promising and potentially safer therapeutic strategy for patients with sickle cell disease and β-thalassemia major.

Materials and methods

HUDEP-2 cell expansion

HUDEP-2 cells, an immortalized erythroid progenitor cell line generously provided by Profs. Yukio Nakamura and Ryo Kurita from the RIKEN BioResource Research Center, Japan, were cultured following previously established protocols [37]. Briefly, cells were cultured and proliferated in serum-free expansion medium (StemSpan SFEM II; STEMCELL Technologies) supplemented with 1X Antibiotic Antimycotic solution (Sigma-Aldrich), 50 ng/mL recombinant human Stem Cell Factor (ImmunoTools), 3 IU/mL Erythropoietin (PeproTech), 1 μg/mL Doxycycline (Sigma-Aldrich), and 1 µM Dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich). The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Nucleofection of HUDEP-2 cells

The 3 × 106 cells HUDEP-2 cells were used for electroporation using a Nucleofector 2b device (Lonza) under Program U-008[38]. The RNP complex was used 1:3 (Cas9: sgRNA) ratio for the generation of HPFH3 genotype.

single-cell sorting and screening

Following nucleofection of HUDEP-2 cells, the resulting cell population was heterogeneous, comprising both edited (GFP-positive) and unedited (GFP-negative) cells. To isolate clonal populations of successfully edited cells, GFP-expressing cells were identified and single-cell sorted using the BD FACS Melody™ cell sorter. GFP-positive cells were sorted into 96-well plates, one cell per well, to establish genetically uniform clonal populations.

A total of 30 clones were subsequently expanded and subjected to PCR-based genotyping. Of these, 3 clones were confirmed to carry the HPFH3 genotype.

CD34 + hematopoietic stem progenitor cells (HSPCs) purification and expansion

Mobilized and cryopreserved peripheral blood samples were obtained from SCD and BT patient donors with prior ethics committee approval (Reference Nos. CSIR-IGIB/IHEC/2017-18/12; TSCS-1112018; 2020-001-EMP-28; RCCIRC/IRB-BHR/99/201). PBMCs from blood samples of three different donors for each disease were isolated and subsequently, CD34 + cells were purified from each donor using the EasySep™ Human CD34 Positive Selection Kit II (STEMCELL Technologies) following the manufacturer's instructions. The cells were cultured and maintained as previously described [36].

Nucleofection of HSPCs

On day 3, 0.2 × 106 of CD34 + cells were electroporated with 6 ug of Cas9 (Sigma-Adrich) and 3 µg of modified sgRNA (Sigma-Aldrich) using a P3 Primary cell 4D-Nucleofector Kit (Lonza) as per manufacturer’s instructions.

ddPCR-based quantification of genome editing

To determine the deletional efficiency, two sets of primers were used for PCR amplification: 1) deletion-specific primers, 5BP-F and 3BP-R, and 2) primers specific to unedited/wildtype sequences, 5BP-F with 5BP-R and 3BP-F with 3BP-R. Primer details are mentioned in Supplementary Table 1. Following the initial amplification, the products were then amplified using EvaGreen® Supermix (BioRad) with specific primers on the QX200 BioRad System.

Erythroid differentiation of HUDEP-2 cells

The HUDEP-2 edited and unedited cells were induced to differentiate into erythroid cells by culturing them in a differentiation medium of IMDM (Sigma) supplemented with 3% human AB serum (Sigma-Aldrich), 2% Fetal Bovine Serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1X Antibiotic Antimycotic solution (Sigma-Aldrich), 200 µg/mL Holo-transferrin (Sigma-Aldrich), 10 µg/mL recombinant human insulin (Sigma-Aldrich), 3 IU/mL heparin (STEMCELL Technologies), 3 IU/mL erythropoietin (PeproTech), 1 µg/mL doxycycline (Sigma-Aldrich), 10 ng/mL stem cell factor (ImmunoTools), and 1 ng/mL Interleukin-3 (IL-3) (ImmunoTools) [39]. The cells were initially seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells/mL on day 0 and increased to 3.5 × 105 cells/mL on day 2. On day 4, the cell density was maintained at 5 × 105 cells/mL, and doxycycline was removed from the medium. From day 6 onwards, the cell density was maintained at 1 × 106 cells/mL, and the concentration of holo-transferrin was increased to 500 µg/mL. On day 6, both SCF and IL-3 were removed from the medium, and the cell density was maintained until Day 12 [39].

Erythroid differentiation of HSPCs

CD34 + HSPCs were differentiated into mature erythroid cells following a three-phase erythroid differentiation protocol [40]. In the first phase (day 0 to day 8), 5 × 104 cells/mL were grown in an erythroid differentiation medium (EDM) containing IMDM (SIGMA), supplemented with 100 ng/mL recombinant human SCF, 330 μg/mL human holotransferrin, 5 ng/mL human IL-3, 3 IU/mL recombinant human erythropoietin (EPO), 2 IU/mL heparin, 5% human AB serum, 20 μg/mL insulin, 1% GlutaMAX, and 1X Antibiotic–Antimycotic.In the second phase (day 9 to 14), cells were cultured at a density of 2 × 105 in EDM without IL-3. In Phase (day 14 to 21), cells were cultured in EDM without IL-3 and SCF and cultured at a density of 5 × 105 cells/mL.

Giemsa staining

On day 12 of differentiation, 1 × 105 cells were harvested and washed with PBS. The cells were resuspended in 30 µL of PBS and smeared onto a clean glass slide Once air-dried, the slide was fixed in methanol for 2 min. This was followed by staining the slide, with 20% Giemsa in milli-Q water for 5 min, followed by rinsing with milli-Q water. The slide was then air-dried, and images were captured using a Brightfield microscope (EVOS M5000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 40X magnification.

Reverse phase-high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) analysis of Globin chains from erythroid cells

The analysis of globin chains was done using a reverse-phase HPLC system, as described by [41]. Briefly, on day 12 for HUDEP-2 cells and day 21 for CD34 + HSPCs during the differentiation process, 1 × 106 erythroid cells were collected and centrifuged at 200 g for 5 min. The cell pellet was lysed by adding 900 μL of ice-cold Milli-Q water, followed by vortexing and incubation on ice for 20 min. The lysate was then centrifuged at 9500 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was diluted 1:100 with ice-cold Milli-Q water and transferred into an HPLC vial. For separation, 20 μL of the sample was injected into a column coupled with Agilent 1200 infinity HPLC System. The loading buffer (Buffer A) consisted of 5% acetonitrile and 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid in Milli-Q deionized water, while the elution buffer (Buffer B) was composed of 95% acetonitrile and 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid in Milli-Q deionized water.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

2 × 105 cells were harvested for RNA extraction. RNA isolation was performed using the TRIzol™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific) method. The concentration of total RNA was determined using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For cDNA synthesis, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed using the high-capacity cDNA synthesis kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The resulting cDNA was then used as a template for qPCR with KAPA SYBR Fast (Kapa Biosystems) to analyze the expression of genes HBA, HBB, and HBG. The specific primers used for this study are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

3C (Chromatin conformation capture) analysis

On day 5 of differentiation, chromatin interactions in the target cells were cross-linked with formaldehyde. The DNA was extracted and digested with EcoRI, generating chromatin fragments with specific ends. These fragments were then ligated under dilute conditions to favor intramolecular ligation. Following ligation, the DNA was reverse cross-linked, and proteins were removed via proteinase K digestion. The purified DNA was analyzed by qPCR to measure interaction frequencies between specific genomic loci, offering insights into the three-dimensional chromatin organization in the target cells[42]. The primer details are listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Flow cytometry

On day 12 for HUDEP-2 cells and day 21 for HSPCs undergoing erythroid differentiation, a total of 1 × 105 cells were collected for further analysis. These cells were stained with differentiation markers, anti-CD71-PE, and Glycophorin-A-FITC (GYPA). Cells were stained for 20 min, followed by a thorough PBS wash using centrifugation at 150 g for 5 min at room temperature, and the subsequent assessment through flow cytometry.

To evaluate the efficiency of enucleation, Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), a cell-permeable double-stranded DNA dye was used, as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were incubated with Hoechst 33342 at a concentration of 2 μg/mL for 20 min at room temperature. After staining, the cells were washed with PBS, and the analysis was carried out using flow cytometry.

For the determination of the percentage of hemoglobin variants expressed in the cell population, 1 × 105 erythroid-differentiated cells were fixed using 4% formaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 min. Subsequently, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min, followed by a wash with 0.5% BSA containing PBS. The cells were then stained using anti-HBF-1 (ThermoFisher Scientific), anti-hemoglobin β antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), and anti-hemoglobin α antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). After staining, cells were washed with PBS followed. To determine the levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), 5 × 105 cells were stained with CM-H2DCFDA (D399, ThermoFisher Scientific) for 30 min following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The stained cells were subsequently washed with PBS and analyzed using flow cytometry. All data acquisition was done using the BD Accuri C6 analyzer, and data analysis was performed using FloJo 10.10.0 software. The antibody details are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Colony formation assay

Colony Forming Unit (CFU) Assay was performed using MethoCult™ GF medium (STEMCELL Technologies). On day 6 of CD34 + HSPCs expansion, 2.5 × 104 cells were resuspended in 500 µL of medium and seeded in a 12-well plate. Cells were incubated at 37ºC in a 5% CO2 for 16 days, after which colony composition was identified using microscopy. Hematopoietic colonies were quantified and categorized based on cellular morphology.

In vitro sickling assay

At the end of phase 3 differentiation,1 × 104 cells were resuspended in the HBSS buffer and incubated at 0.2% oxygen, 37 ºC, and 5% CO2 ( Eppendorf New Brunswick Galaxy 48R). The cells were examined manually for abnormal or sickle-shaped morphology versus normal cells using a FLoid Cell Imaging System microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Karyostat assay

Genomic DNA was extracted from gene-edited HSPCs using the PureLink Genomic DNA Mini Kit and quantified using the Qubit dsDNA assay. 250 ng of genomic DNA was digested with the NspI restriction enzyme, followed by adapter ligation and DNA amplification. The amplified DNA was then fragmented, labeled with biotin, and hybridized onto GeneChip arrays (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). The GeneChip Fluidics Station 450 was employed for washing and staining the chips, which were then scanned using the GeneChip Scanner 3000 7G. Data analysis was performed with ChAS 3.2, with raw data processed through Genotyping Console v4.0 and Chromosome Analysis Suite 3.2, using NetAffx na33.1 (UCSC GRCh37/hg19) as a reference. The final output was interpreted via the UCSC Genome Browser.

Amplicon based next generation sequencing

To identify potential off-target sites for CRISPR/Cas9 sgRNAs in the human genome, COSMID (https://crispr.bme.gatech.edu/); an off-target prediction tool was used. Off-target sites for 5’ and 3’ guide RNAs were analyzed using amplicon-based next-generation sequencing methods. Library preparation was carried out as previously described [43]. PCR amplicons were fragmented and tagged with adapter sequences through tagmentation. The adapter-tagged fragments underwent another round of PCR, after which the adapter-indexed libraries were pooled and purified using magnetic beads. The final library was quantified using the High Sensitivity dsDNA quantification kit (Invitrogen). After normalization and quality assessment, the synthesized library was subjected to paired-end sequencing on an Illumina sequencing platform (Illumina Inc.). The data has been deposited in the BioProject database and can be accessed at http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/1167629 with BioProject ID PRJNA1167629 or accession no. 1167629.

For analysis, the Fastq files were quality-trimmed using Trimmomatic and aligned with BWA-MEM. Duplicate reads were subsequently removed with Picard's MarkDuplicates. The resulting BAM file was then analyzed using the R package CrispRVariants to identify editing events and assess efficiency for each sample.

Statistical analysis

Data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (Version 9.5.0, GraphPad Software Inc, USA) with One-way ANOVA and two-tailed Student's t-test. Experiments were conducted in triplicate, and standard errors of the mean are shown as error bars in all Figures. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The differentiated erythroblasts from edited and unedited populations were characterized to assess cellular morphology and maturation (S3 Figure). Enucleation, a key marker of erythroid maturation, was analyzed using flow cytometry and showed no significant differences between the edited (36.5% ± 2.75) and unedited cell populations (36.0% ± 1.4) (Fig. 1C). Additionally, erythroid-specific cell surface markers such as CD235a (GPA) and CD71 were evaluated by flow cytometry, further confirming no changes in the differentiation potential upon editing (S3 Fig.).

In the edited erythroblasts, we observed increased levels of HBG1/2 transcripts and a higher F-cell population (44.26% ± 1.7) while the unedited population showed 2.8% ± 0.33 (Figs. 1D and E). RP-HPLC analysis further revealed elevated levels of γ-globin chains at the protein level (Fig. 1F).

Further, to assess the correlation between HbF reactivation and the generation of the HPFH3 genotype, we isolated clonal cells with HPFH3 deletions from the heterogeneous HPFH3 population. Our flow cytometric data of differentiated erythroblasts from 3 clones of HPFH3 deletions, GAP1, GAP2, and GAP3 revealed substantial increases in F-cell population (Supplementary Fig. 4). Hemoglobin levels were further quantified using HPLC, revealing reactivated HbF levels (Supplementary Fig. 5). These findings collectively demonstrate that the HPFH3 genotype effectively enhances HbF levels while maintaining essential erythropoiesis characteristics in the HUDEP-2 cell line.

Structural interaction of LCR-HBG1/2 leads to HBG reactivation in HUDEP-2

To assess chromatin structure changes in the β-globin gene cluster upon HPFH3 genotype, we performed Chromatin Conformation Capture (3C) on a GAP-1 clone of HPFH3 deletion. This allowed us to examine chromatin looping and spatial proximity between the locus control region (LCR; HS2) and the HBG1/2 and HBB loci. Our analysis showed significant interactions between these regions, supporting their role in regulating HbF expression, as confirmed by qPCR quantification of chromatin interaction frequencies in HPFH3-edited clones (Fig. 1G, H, I). We observed reduced LCR-HBB interactions, suggesting that the HPFH3 deletion enhanced LCR-HBG1/2 interactions, likely due to the absence of the HBB locus. Sanger sequencing confirmed the interactions between the LCR and the HBG2 promoter in HPFH3 deletion clonal cells (Fig. 1G). These findings provide key insights into the mechanistic role of the LCR in HbF induction, emphasizing its recruitment to the HBG1/2 loci and further corroborating the previously published structural basis of HbF re-activation.

HPFH3 genotype enhances HbF production while reducing sickling in SCD HSPC-derived erythroid cells

We next sought to investigate the reduction of SCD-specific characteristics in patient-derived HSPCs with the HPFH3 genotype (Fig. 2A). Mobilized CD34 + HSPCs were purified from SCD patient-derived PBMCs and characterized CD34 and CD45 marker profiles using flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. 6). The HPFH3 genotype was introduced through nucleofection of Cas9/sgRNA RNP complex, achieving the HPFH3 deletional efficiency of 49.33% ± 2.46 in the edited cells (Fig. 2B). The cell expansion profile during differentiation indicated comparable cell counts between edited and unedited populations (Fig. 2C). Flow cytometric analysis of differentiated erythroblast revealed no significant differences in the enucleated populations between edited (44.8% ± 0.90) and unedited cells (42.3% ± 0.66) (Supplementary Fig. 7; Fig. 2D). Discussion

Advances in lentiviral-based therapies and genome editing of HSPCs have accelerated the development of treatments for β-hemoglobinopathies. Increasing HbF levels, particularly through reactivation of developmentally silenced HbF, shows promise as a therapeutic strategy. Different genome editing targets have been explored to reactivate HbF, including cis and trans regulators of HBG1/2 genes. Notably, naturally occurring HPFH, whether deletional or non-deletional, has shown an increase in HbF levels at the heterozygous state, offering substantial clinical benefits, and studies have shown that individuals homozygous for the large HPFH deletions (HPFH1, HPFH2, and HPFH3) do not develop any pathological conditions aside from elevated levels of HbF [33, 34, 44]. Recently, we demonstrated that genome-editing mediated generation of HPFH3-genotype in cellular systems of SCD and BT led to a significant increase in γ-globin expression, reaching potentially therapeutic levels [36]. This finding encouraged us further to investigate the potential of HPFH3 in pre-clinical settings. In this study, we successfully demonstrated the CRISPR-mediated generation of a beneficial HPFH3 genotype within the β-globin gene cluster in both SCD and BT patient-derived HSPCs. This genetic modification resulted in therapeutically relevant levels of HbF, sufficient to ameliorate the disease phenotypes associated with both SCD and BT.

Our findings demonstrated consistent gene-editing frequencies in HSPCs derived from multiple patients with SCD and BT, contrasting with previous studies that reported inconsistent editing efficiency among experiments and donors [45]. Importantly, we found no significant differences in key progenitor populations, including BFU-E, CFU-E, CFU-GEMM, and CFU-GM, prior and post-HPFH3 editing, highlighting the safety of our approach. In vitro, HPFH3-edited samples from SCD-HSPCs exhibited increased HbF levels and a corresponding decrease in HbS, effectively reducing the pathological phenotype of SCD cells. Studies indicate that HbF levels exceeding 20% provide a protective threshold against sickle cell-related morbidity [46]. Moreover, induction of HbF in erythroid cells derived from HPFH3-edited BT-HSPCs restores the α-globin/β-like globin ratio, leading to efficient erythropoiesis, enhanced maturation of the erythroid lineage, and significantly reduced levels of ROS compared with the unedited cells. The reduced ROS levels is likely due to a reduction in free alpha-globin, a key contributor to oxidative stress in erythroid cells. Reduced ROS has been linked to improved clinical outcomes in mouse models [47], reinforcing the potential therapeutic benefits of our approach.

The HbF-mediated disease rescue effects observed in HPFH3-edited cells can be attributed to several key mechanisms: (i) The presence of enhancer-like sequences at the 3’ breakpoint of HPFH3, which have been shown to enhance γ-globin expression in various systems, including transgenic mice [48], along with the repositioning of DNA sequences at the 3’ deletion breakpoints near the γ-globin genes, potentially driving increased γ-globin expression (29,45); [49–52]; and (iii) The altered structural interaction between the locus control region (LCR) and the HBG1/2 genes in HPFH3-edited cells, which supports the hypothesis of redirecting the LCR-HBB interaction to LCR-HBG1/2 [40]. This redirection occurs in the absence of the defective HBB gene, fostering the reactivation of HbF and reducing the synthesis of defective globin chains such as HbS and truncated HbA in patient-derived HSPCs-erythroid cells. The reactivated γ-globin not only contributes to the formation of functional Hb but also restores the balance of globin chain synthesis by pairing with available α-globin chains. This balanced pairing effectively mitigates the clinical manifestations of both SCD and BT, addressing the pathological defects by reducing the proportion of abnormal hemoglobin and improving erythroid function.

In recent years, deletional HPFH has emerged as a potential target for targeted genome engineering for HbF induction in patient HSPCs [45]; [53]. There has been a lack of studies on the effects of large deletions on genome integrity and long-term engraftment of SCD and BT patient-derived HSPCs post-editing. Our study fills this gap by showing that HSPCs with large deletions can successfully engraft and repopulate in immunocompromised NBSGW mice. Our analysis of editing frequency indicates that generating the HPFH3 genotype does not adversely affect in vivo survival. Flow cytometric analysis revealed no disruptions in lineage reconstitution or abnormal hematopoiesis, supporting the safety of the HPFH3 editing process. Additionally, our work demonstrates that the targeted generation of large HPFH3 deletions does not cause chromosomal abnormalities or negatively impact globin gene expression and erythroid development. Although in vitro and NBSGW mouse models have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of HPFH3-mediated HbF reactivation, clinical trials are essential to evaluate the safety, efficacy, and durability of CRISPR-edited HPFH3 HSPCs in patients. These will include comprehensive monitoring and multi-omics analyses to validate and optimize the therapeutic impact of HPFH3 editing.

Very recently, researchers employed a dual guide RNA approach targeting + 55 and + 58 sites of the BCLA11A erythroid enhancer. This approach generated indels and a 3.1 kb deletion, leading to robust HbF reactivation and long-term engraftment potential in primates [54], thereby further supporting the safety of our approach.

In contrast to other NHEJ-based genome-editing approaches, like recreating naturally occurring HPFH-associated small deletions or suppressing the BCL11A HbF repressor, our approach necessitates the introduction of two targeted DSBs, which may influence the overall efficiency of genome editing. However, deletional HPFH3 holds an edge over non-deletional HPFH due to its ability to simultaneously eliminate competing genes (HBD and HBB) and specific HBG1/2 repressor binding sites [55]. Conversely, trans regulator targets such as BCL11A, ZBTB7A, and MYB have been under consideration. However, only BCL11A has advanced to clinical approval. ​​In HPFH3 recapitulation, genome editing removes the defective sickle β-globin gene, halting the production of HbS. By mimicking natural HPFH3 mutations, this approach enhances HbF production, which can compensate for the missing adult hemoglobin (HbA) and improve clinical outcomes. On the other hand, editing the BCL11A enhancer raises HbF levels by reducing BCL11A, a repressor of HbF. Therefore, genome editing that both reduces HbS and enhances HbF, like HPFH3 recapitulation, may offer a potentially more effective and lasting treatment for β-hemoglobinopathies.

Conclusions

In summary, our comprehensive evaluation of pre-clinical models provides a proof-of-concept demonstrating that recapitulation of the HPFH3 genotype through CRISPR-mediated genome editing induces therapeutically appreciable HbF in the adult stage. This compensates for the defective β-globin and presents a potentially universal strategy for any β-thalassemia mutation and sickle cell disease.
