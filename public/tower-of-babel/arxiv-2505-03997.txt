Quiet Feature Learning in Algorithmic Tasks
Prudhviraj Naidu, Zixian Wang, Leon Bergen, Ramamohan PaturiDepartment of Computer ScienceUC San DiegoSan Diego, CA 92093, USA {prnaidu,ziw081,lbergen,rpaturi}@ucsd.edu

Abstract
We train Transformer-based language models on ten foundational algorithmic tasks and observe pronounced phase transitions in their loss curves that deviate from established power-law scaling trends. Over large ranges of compute, the validation loss barely improves, then abruptly decreases. Probing the models’ internal representations reveals the learning of quiet features during the stagnant phase, followed by sudden acquisition of loud features that coincide with the sharp drop in loss. Our ablation experiments show that disrupting a single learned feature can dramatically degrade performance, providing evidence of their causal role in task performance. These findings challenge the prevailing assumption that next-token predictive loss reliably tracks incremental progress; instead, key internal features may be developing below the surface until they coalesce, triggering a rapid performance gain.

Introduction

Understanding how and when large language models acquire new capabilities has become an important question in the study of deep learning. While language models have demonstrated remarkable performance across a broad range of tasks, the precise mechanisms driving their improvements remain unknown. Recent discussions of “emergent abilities” – where larger-scale models outperform baselines abruptly, even though smaller-scale counterparts exhibit little improvement – have led to debate over whether such phenomena are genuine or artifacts of measurement
(
Wei et al. 2022
;
Ganguli et al. 2022
;
Schaeffer et al. 2023
)
.

Questions about emergent abilities are closely tied to the observation of scaling laws in model training
(
Kaplan et al. 2020
;
Ruan et al. 2024
;
Henighan et al. 2020
;
Dubey et al. 2024
;
OpenAI 2023
)
. These scaling laws typically show a smooth, power-law relationship between compute and model performance. However, most empirical demonstrations of these laws derive from heterogeneous data and tasks, leaving open the possibility that “averaging out” many distinct learning behaviors masks more abrupt transitions that occur for individual skills or subtasks.

We focus on ten foundational algorithmic problems spanning various input types. These algorithmic tasks have precisely defined solutions, making it straightforward to identify clear success criteria, isolate the specific features or subtasks the model must learn, and ensure that improvements cannot be attributed to memorization or partial heuristics. These tasks allow us to investigate fine-grained learning phenomena which might otherwise be obscured by heterogeneous data.

Our key findings include:

1.

Phase transitions in scaling laws
: We observe two distinct phases in scaling laws across tasks and task lengths. In the “
slow phase
,” loss improves minimally or remains flat. Then, loss drops rapidly (“
fast phase
”). We refer to the change between these two phases as phase transition. We observe this phase transition when we vary model size & training dataset size, fix model size & vary training dataset size and within individual training runs.

2.

Quiet features
precede phase transitions
: Models learn meaningful internal representations during the slow phase, but these features do not yet yield noticeable gains in the output loss (we call these “quiet features”). Ablating them severely degrades performance, demonstrating they are causally related to the eventual sharp drop in loss.

3.

Lack of strict ordering by computational dependence
: The emergence of “quiet” vs. “loud” features (learnt during the fast phase) does not always mirror the dependency graph of the underlying algorithm. Some pairs of features with ordering dependencies appear to emerge simultaneously.

These findings challenge the assumption that improvements in loss necessarily coincide with improvements in feature representations.
Instead, substantive internal reorganization may occur below the surface, revealing itself only at discrete points during training.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: Section
2
reviews related work in scaling laws, emergent abilities, and algorithmic learning. Section
3
describes our experimental methodology and presents our observations of phase changes across tasks and task lengths. Section
4
introduces our feature analysis framework and demonstrates how quiet and loud features evolve during training. Finally, Section
5
discusses the broader implications of our findings and suggests directions for future research.

Related Work

2.1
Scaling Laws

Hestness et al. 2017
observed scaling dataset size and deep neural network model size led to a predictable decrease in generalization error for neural machine translation, language modeling, image classification and speech recognition.
Kaplan et al. 2020
,
Hoffmann et al. 2022
observed predictable relationships between training compute and language modeling loss.

Henighan et al. 2020
extended this work for generative models across modalities: image, video, multimedia image-text and math. They demonstrated classification loss and error rates predictably decreased on downstream image classification tasks.
Chen et al. 2021
studied language model performance on coding. They observed a predictable relationship between language modeling loss on a held out code corpus and model size.

2.2
Predicting LLM abilities

Ganguli et al. 2022
,
Wei et al. 2022
demonstrated one could not predict large language models capabilities from capabilities of small language models. However,
Schaeffer et al. 2023
,
OpenAI 2023
,
Ruan et al. 2024
,
Dubey et al. 2024
showed this was due to choice of metrics and large language models capabilities could be predicted from capabilities of small language models.

2.3
Proposed explanations for scaling laws

Michaud et al. 2023
proposed neural networks learn discrete skills called ”quanta.” They argue that there is a strict ordering, which they called Q sequence, in which quanta must be learned. They proposed the frequencies of these quanta follow a power law, leading to the power law relationship observed by
Kaplan et al. 2020
and others.
Hutter 2021
propose that the relationship between the error rate and dataset size is guided by the distribution of features in the data. They show a Zipfian distribution results in power law scaling.

2.4
Grokking

In grokking (
Power et al. 2022
,
Nanda et al. 2023
,
Varma et al. 2023
), a model trained for many epochs quickly memorizes the training set (thus achieving high training accuracy early) but only later learns a more generalizing representation, causing a sudden jump in test accuracy. In contrast, our phase transitions occur during single epoch training, with both training and test performance starting off poor. The model gradually learns “quiet” internal features before suddenly mastering the entire task. Hence, our model does not show a transition from memorization to generalization.

2.5
Progress Measures

Several previous works have identified measures which track progress toward the final, fully-generalizing solution, even when the test loss shows no improvement.

Barak et al. 2022
propose a metric for measuring similarity of network weights in the context of sparse parity, and demonstrate that this metric continuously improves throughout training, including prior to measurable improvement in generalization performance.
Nanda et al. 2023
propose a different metric on network weights in the context of modular arithmetic grokking, and demonstrate that this metric improves before the phase transition.
Mallinar et al. 2024
propose tracking features using Average Gradient Outer Product (AGOP) for Recursive Feature Machines. While these prior works demonstrate increasing similarity between network weights and a generalizing solution prior to the emergence of improved generalization behavior, they do not demonstrate that the networks are computing interpretable activations prior to generalization. We close this gap by directly probing for algorithm-specific intermediate variables and showing they appear well before the loss drop.

2.6
Probing for Features

Hanna et al. 2023
,
Ye et al. 2024
investigate how models learn mathematical skills through circuit discovery or training probes to detect features.
Haghighatkhah et al. 2022
,
Belrose et al. 2023
,
Arditi et al. 2024
,
Beaglehole et al. 2025
showed one can use feature detectors to steer or remove concepts of large language models. We use a simple orthogonal projection to erase features.

2.7
Phase Transitions

Phase transitions have been previously observed for a limited number of algorithmic tasks.
Olsson et al. 2022
,
Garg et al. 2022
,
Edelman et al. 2024
find phase transitions for in context learning during individual training runs.
Barak et al. 2022
observe phase transitions in parity.
Lee et al. 2024
measures relationships between test accuracy and number of examples (over a fixed model size), with observed phase transitions potentially being explained by the metric artifacts of
Schaeffer et al. 2023
. We measure language modeling loss and scale both model size & dataset size as prescribed by
Schaeffer et al. 2023
,
Kaplan et al. 2020
,
Hoffmann et al. 2022
.

Scaling Laws for Algorithmic Tasks

3.1
Defining Algorithmic tasks

We study the following algorithmic tasks: binary addition, binary multiplication, majority of majorities, maximum subarray, activity selection, breadth first search, depth first search, shortest path, topological sorting and minimum spanning tree. We chose these tasks because we can control the task complexity and understand the algorithms which solve these tasks. For illustration, we describe how we formulate binary addition as a language modeling task below. For other tasks, please see Appendix
A.1

Binary Addition

We formulate
n
-bit binary addition as a sequence prediction task:

\texttt{x}_{1}\texttt{x}_{2}\ldots\texttt{x}_{n}\texttt{+}\texttt{y}_{1}\texttt{y}_{2}\ldots\texttt{y}_{n}\texttt{=}\texttt{z}_{1}\texttt{z}_{2}\ldots\texttt{z}_{n+1}\texttt{<EOS>}

Where
x
,
y
, and
z
represent binary numbers, presented from the least significant bit to the most significant bit. Each bit is represented as a separate token, and
+
,
=
, and
<EOS>
are also presented as individual tokens.

3.2
Experimental Methodology

3.2.1
Generating Samples

Binary Tasks.

For addition and multiplication, we uniformly sample pairs of n-bit binary numbers
(a,b)
without replacement. To prevent memorization, if a pair
(a,b)
appears in the training set, we ensure
(b,a)
does not appear in the validation or test sets. For majority of majorities, we uniformly sample bit strings without replacement.

Graph Tasks.

For graph-based tasks (breadth first search, depth first search, shortest path, minimum spanning tree and topological sorting), we uniformly sample non-isomorphic undirected connected graphs from
McKay 2025
and randomly permute the vertex labels. For topological sorting, we first sample an undirected graph, then determine edge directions based on a random vertex ordering.

Integer Sequence Tasks

For maximum subarray and activity selection, we uniformly sample multisets without replacement.

3.2.2
Training the Language Model

We employ Transformer++ as our language model architecture. Transformer++ is a standard decoder-only transformer model with enhancements detailed in Table
4
, based on modifications in LLAMA and PALM (
Gu & Dao 2024
). We train our language model on these tasks with the standard next token prediction objective. Loss is only computed over the output tokens positions (including & after the ‘=’ position). We use AdamW optimizer
Loshchilov & Hutter 2017
with linear warmup followed with cosine annealing learning rate scheduler as prescribed by
Hoffmann et al. 2022
to train the model.

3.2.3
Estimating Scaling Laws

For each task and task size, we estimate scaling laws by training over a range of training compute budgets, ensuring at least one trained model reaches 100% test accuracy on a 1000-example held-out set. For each compute budget, we conduct a grid search across model sizes, batch sizes, and learning rates (see Appendix Table
3
for hyperparameter space), filtering out hyperparameter combinations that exceed a pre-defined maximum number of steps.

Scaling Law Results

We observe phase transitions for compute-optimal validation loss in three scenarios: (1) when we vary both model size & dataset size, (2) when we fix the model size & vary the dataset size, and (3) during individual (compute-optimal) training runs. Focusing on the first scenario, for six of the tasks, the compute-optimal validation loss undergoes a clear phase transition as the training compute budget increases (See Figure

For addition, majority of majorities, activity selection and maximum subarray the validation loss is roughly constant in the slow phase then suddenly goes to near zero during the fast phase. For multiplication and breadth first search, the slow phase has a gradual decrease followed by a steeper decrease in the fast phase.

4
Feature Learning before and after Phase Change

4.1
Investigating Feature Learning

In order to better understand the observed phase transitions, we investigate the emergence of computationally useful features during learning. We define computationally useful features to be the intermediate outputs from standard algorithms used to solve the task. We use linear probing to identify whether the model learned these computationally useful features.

4.1.1
Defining Algorithm-Specific Features

Addition & Multiplication.

For n-bit binary addition, we probe for the following at each token
\texttt{z}_{i}
:
first_operand
, which is input bit
\texttt{x}_{i+1}
(required to compute
\texttt{z}_{i+1}
); and carry, which is the
(i)^{th}
bit, and is used to compute
\texttt{z}_{i}
. Carry for
z_{1}
is not considered since the first carry is always zero. For multiplication, we check whether the model learns carries generated when adding the last partial product to the sum of the previous
n-1
partial products.

Breadth/Depth First Search.

For breadth first search, we probe at each token
\texttt{v}_{t_{i}}
for the following:
queue
, which is the set of vertices on the queue (in the standard search algorithms) after we have explored vertex
\texttt{v}_{t_{i}}
. We also probe for
adjacency_list
, which is the set of vertices adjacent to
\texttt{v}_{t_{i}}
.

Maximum Subarray.

For the maximum subarray problem, we probe at each token
\texttt{k}_{i}
(before the
=
token) for:
is_prev_negative
, which represents whether
\texttt{k}_{i-1}
is negative; and
max_ending_here
, which is the maximum sum of the contiguous subarray ending at
\texttt{k}_{i}
. (Refer to
Kadane 2023
for the standard algorithm.)

Activity Selection.

For the activity selection problem, we probe at each token
\texttt{f}_{i}
for:
start_time
, which is the corresponding start time
\texttt{s}_{i}
. Since the model has to output
\texttt{s}_{i}\texttt{f}_{i}
in order, it must know which start times correspond to which finish times. (Refer to
Kleinberg & Tardos 2005
for the standard algorithm.)

4.1.2
Probing Methodology

For each task and task length, we identify a fixed model size that achieves close to the compute-optimal loss across compute budgets.
2
2

2

A fixed model size is chosen in order to make feature metrics easier to compare for models across training runs.

We then select the best training run for this model size and for each compute budget. Linear probes are trained using 10,000 held-out examples. For each algorithm-specific feature, we train separate linear probes across each token position and each layer where the feature is defined. Separate probes are computed for the residual streams after each layer. For each token position, we independently select the probe that achieves the lowest train loss for that position. We establish random baselines by applying the same probing methodology to an identical model initialized with random weights. Test loss is reported on a separate test set of 1000 unseen examples. This allows us to track when and how distinctly these algorithmic features emerge in the model’s representations during training.

Depending on the task and feature characteristics, the probes are either logistic or linear regression models. We train a logistic regression model for

,

4.3
Are
quiet features
causal?

For a given task, we ablate a
quiet feature
from the residual stream at each position, using the feature probes (one probe per position) identified in
4.1.2
. We restrict our analyses to binary features. A linear probe
w^{\top}x^{*}+b
is
0
(assigns
0.5
probability to each label) when it detects no information from the ablated residual stream at that layer. Letting
x
be the residual stream at a desired layer, we perform the following optimization:

\displaystyle\operatorname*{argmin}_{x^{*}}

\displaystyle\|x-x^{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle w^{\top}x^{*}+b=0.

Solving this yields
x^{*}=x-\frac{w^{\top}x+b}{||w||^{2}}w
. Our results are shown in Table
1
. Results are compared to ablating a random direction. When we ablate quiet features, we observe test loss generally increases compared to ablating a random feature, indicating a causal role for quiet features. However, for
first_operand
at task length 16, we do not see any significant change after ablating the feature in addition or multiplication. At larger task lengths, ablating addition
first_operand
leads to test loss degradation.

5
Discussion

Our findings show that, across different algorithmic tasks, there is often a long phase of training with little apparent improvement in next-token prediction loss. Despite this plateau, we observe that essential internal features (e.g., carry bits in binary addition, adjacency in breadth first search) emerge during these periods. We call them “quiet features” because they precede any substantial improvement in performance. Ablation experiments confirm that these features are causally important to solving the tasks, suggesting that models can accumulate partial competence that does not immediately translate into lower loss.
One reason for this “quiet” period may be the all-or-nothing nature of these tasks: obtaining just some of the required subroutines (e.g., some correct carry bits) does not prevent errors on next token prediction. Consequently, any reduction in loss is small until all sub-features are aligned. In over-parameterized models, there is sufficient capacity to learn these subroutines in the background, allowing partial solutions to remain in the representations until they can be combined into a correct overall procedure.
While reminiscent of phenomena like grokking, the mechanism here appears different—our training runs typically do not hinge on memorization but rather on incrementally assembling dependent features. These observations raise broader questions about how well cross-entropy loss reflects hidden progress during training. They also connect to discussions about emergent behaviors in larger models, where sudden gains can appear despite prolonged stagnation on particular tasks.

6
Conclusion

We observe that Transformer-based models for algorithmic tasks can encode important intermediate computations well before they show significant gains in next-token prediction. This
quiet period
exposes a gap between internal representation learning and external task performance, indicating that sub-features may lie dormant until the final pieces align. We hope these insights motivate new methods for probing and monitoring internal learning dynamics – particularly in larger, more complex models – where hidden phases of progress may likewise precede sudden improvements in capability.

A.1
Task formulation

Binary Addition

Binary addition involves combining two
n
-bit numbers to produce an
(n+1)
-bit result. We formulate this as a sequence prediction task:

\texttt{x}_{1}\texttt{x}_{2}\ldots\texttt{x}_{n}\texttt{+}\texttt{y}_{1}\texttt{y}_{2}\ldots\texttt{y}_{n}\texttt{=}\texttt{z}_{1}\texttt{z}_{2}\ldots\texttt{z}_{n+1}\texttt{<EOS>}

Where
x
,
y
, and
z
represent binary numbers, with
\texttt{x}_{1}
denoting the least significant bit (LSB). Each bit is represented as a separate token, and
+
,
=
, and
<EOS>
are special tokens.

Binary Multiplication

Binary multiplication combines two
n
-bit numbers to produce a
2n
-bit result. We formulate this as:

\texttt{x}_{1}\texttt{x}_{2}\ldots\texttt{x}_{n}\texttt{*}\texttt{y}_{1}\texttt{y}_{2}\ldots\texttt{y}_{n}\texttt{=}\texttt{z}_{1}\texttt{z}_{2}\ldots\texttt{z}_{2n}\texttt{<EOS>}

Following the same convention as in binary addition, with bits ordered from least significant bit to most significant bit.

Majority of Majorities

Given an
n
-bit number (where
n
is divisible by 4), we partition the bits into 4 equal consecutive groups. For each group, we compute its majority bit value
g_{i}
. The final output is the majority bit value among
g_{1},g_{2},g_{3},g_{4}
.

We formulate this as:

\texttt{x}_{1}\texttt{x}_{2}\ldots\texttt{x}_{n}\texttt{=}\texttt{z}_{1}\texttt{<EOS>}

Where
\texttt{z}_{1}
is the final majority bit.

Breadth First Search

Given a connected undirected graph
G
with
n
vertices
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\}
, a set of edges
E
, and a start vertex
v_{s}
, we predict the BFS traversal order.

We formulate this as:

\texttt{v}_{s}\texttt{v}_{i_{1}}\texttt{v}_{j_{1}}\ldots\texttt{v}_{i_{m}}\texttt{v}_{j_{m}}\texttt{=}\texttt{v}_{t_{1}}\texttt{v}_{t_{2}}\ldots\texttt{v}_{t_{n}}\texttt{<EOS>}

Where
(\texttt{v}_{i_{k}},\texttt{v}_{j_{k}})
represents an edge in
E
,
m=|E|
is the number of edges, and
\texttt{v}_{t_{1}}\texttt{v}_{t_{2}}\ldots\texttt{v}_{t_{n}}
is the complete BFS traversal sequence starting from
v_{s}
(where
\texttt{v}_{t_{1}}=\texttt{v}_{s}
).

Depth First Search

This follows the same formulation as BFS, but the expected output
\texttt{v}_{t_{1}}\texttt{v}_{t_{2}}\ldots\texttt{v}_{t_{n}}
represents the DFS traversal order:

\texttt{v}_{s}\texttt{v}_{i_{1}}\texttt{v}_{j_{1}}\ldots\texttt{v}_{i_{m}}\texttt{v}_{j_{m}}\texttt{=}\texttt{v}_{t_{1}}\texttt{v}_{t_{2}}\ldots\texttt{v}_{t_{n}}\texttt{<EOS>}

Shortest Path

Given a connected undirected graph
G
with
n
vertices, a set of edges
E
, and two vertices
v_{s}
(source) and
v_{f}
(destination), we predict the shortest path between them.

We formulate this as:

\texttt{v}_{s}\texttt{v}_{f}\texttt{v}_{i_{1}}\texttt{v}_{j_{1}}\ldots\texttt{v}_{i_{m}}\texttt{v}_{j_{m}}\texttt{=}\texttt{v}_{p_{1}}\texttt{v}_{p_{2}}\ldots\texttt{v}_{p_{k}}\texttt{<EOS>}

Where
\texttt{v}_{p_{1}}\texttt{v}_{p_{2}}\ldots\texttt{v}_{p_{k}}
is the shortest path from
v_{s}
to
v_{f}
(with
\texttt{v}_{p_{1}}=\texttt{v}_{s}
and
\texttt{v}_{p_{k}}=\texttt{v}_{f}
).

Topological Sorting

Given a directed acyclic graph (DAG)
G
with
n
vertices and a set of edges
E
, we predict a valid topological ordering of vertices.

We formulate this as:

\texttt{v}_{i_{1}}\texttt{v}_{j_{1}}\ldots\texttt{v}_{i_{m}}\texttt{v}_{j_{m}}\texttt{=}\texttt{v}_{t_{1}}\texttt{v}_{t_{2}}\ldots\texttt{v}_{t_{n}}\texttt{<EOS>}

Where
(\texttt{v}_{i_{k}},\texttt{v}_{j_{k}})
represents a directed edge from
\texttt{v}_{i_{k}}
to
\texttt{v}_{j_{k}}
, and
\texttt{v}_{t_{1}}\texttt{v}_{t_{2}}\ldots\texttt{v}_{t_{n}}
is a valid topological ordering.

Minimum Spanning Tree

Given a connected undirected graph
G
with
n
vertices and a set of weighted edges
E
, we predict the edges that form the minimum spanning tree (MST).

We formulate this as:

\texttt{v}_{i_{1}}\texttt{v}_{j_{1}}\texttt{w}_{1}\ldots\texttt{v}_{i_{m}}\texttt{v}_{j_{m}}\texttt{w}_{m}\texttt{=}\texttt{v}_{p_{1}}\texttt{v}_{q_{1}}\ldots\texttt{v}_{p_{n-1}}\texttt{v}_{q_{n-1}}\texttt{<EOS>}

Where
(\texttt{v}_{i_{k}},\texttt{v}_{j_{k}},\texttt{w}_{k})
represents an edge with weight
\texttt{w}_{k}
, and
\{(\texttt{v}_{p_{1}},\texttt{v}_{q_{1}}),\ldots,(\texttt{v}_{p_{n-1}},\texttt{v}_{q_{n-1}})\}
are the edges in the MST.

Maximum Subarray

Given a sequence of
n
integers
k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{n}
where
k_{i}\in[-9,9]
, we predict the contiguous subarray with the maximum sum.

We formulate this as:

\texttt{k}_{1}\texttt{k}_{2}\ldots\texttt{k}_{n}\texttt{=}\texttt{k}_{i}\texttt{k}_{i+1}\ldots\texttt{k}_{j}\texttt{<EOS>}

Where
\texttt{k}_{i}\texttt{k}_{i+1}\ldots\texttt{k}_{j}
is the maximum sum subarray (
i\leq j
). For a single-element result, only
\texttt{k}_{i}
is output.

Activity Selection

Given a sequence of
n
activities represented by their start times
(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{n})
and finish times
(f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{n})
, we predict the largest subset of non-overlapping activities.

We formulate this as:

\texttt{s}_{1}\texttt{s}_{2}\ldots\texttt{s}_{n}\texttt{f}_{1}\texttt{f}_{2}\ldots\texttt{f}_{n}\texttt{=}\texttt{s}_{i_{1}}\texttt{f}_{i_{1}}\ldots\texttt{s}_{i_{k}}\texttt{f}_{i_{k}}\texttt{<EOS>}

Where
\texttt{s}_{i_{1}}\texttt{f}_{i_{1}}\ldots\texttt{s}_{i_{k}}\texttt{f}_{i_{k}}
represents the selected non-overlapping activities in ascending order of finish times.
