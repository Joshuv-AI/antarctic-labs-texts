A multidisciplinary quality improvement initiative to improve neonatal end-of-life care in a level IV NICU
Authors: Hanna Lofgren, Samantha Lentin, Anna DiMatteo, Erin Pasquale, Jennifer Salant, Priyanka Tiwari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPalliative care interventions are underutilized in infants due to a knowledge deficit among providers surrounding neonatal end-of-life care and a pervasive lack of guidelines in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs). The aim of this study was to increase end-of-life symptom management by 50% and improve staff attitude scores and awareness of end-of-life cases by 20% by August 2024.MethodsThe Model for Improvement was used to examine the baseline of mortalities and test interventions to increase analgesia administration and improve staff attitude scores and awareness during end-of-life care.ResultsFentanyl equivalent administration in the 24 h preceding death increased by 309% between epochs. A comparison of survey responses across epochs revealed a significant increase in staff comfort, confidence, preparedness, and awareness when providing end-of-life care.ConclusionThe creation of a multidisciplinary quality improvement (QI) team, the implementation of end-of-life guidelines, electronic health record (EHR) modifications, and staff education improved symptom management, staff attitude scores, and awareness when providing end-of-life care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-025-01899-9.Keywords: Quality improvement, Multidisciplinary, Neonatal, NICU, Guidelines, Palliative care, End-of-life care

Introduction

Among all pediatric patients, those in the first year of life have the highest mortality rate, with those in the NICU being an especially vulnerable population [1]. Most deaths in the NICU occur after withdrawal of life sustaining therapies (WLST) due to a life-limiting diagnosis or imminent death despite maximum treatment efforts [2]. Palliative care aims to relieve suffering through the assessment and treatment of pain, as well as other physical, psychosocial, and spiritual issues, thus improving the quality of life of those facing life-limiting conditions [3]. Despite the American Academy of Pediatrics’ publication of guidelines in 2013, palliative care interventions remain underutilized and highly variable in the infant and neonatal populations [4].

Historically, pain in neonates has been underrecognized and undertreated [5]. Some studies have shown that less than 25% of infant deaths involve palliative, comfort-focused interventions aimed at relieving end-of-life symptoms [6]. Symptoms include agitation, dyspnea, neuro-irritability, pain, and increased secretions [7]. When perinatal palliative care programs are available, there is still significant variability in the services provided, secondary to a lack of staff education and a lack of standardization [4]. In 2018, nearly half of all NICUs across the United States did not have any end-of-life guidelines, and less than one-third had formal education for their staff [1, 8]. Numerous studies have illustrated a knowledge deficit surrounding neonatal palliative care for nurses and other healthcare providers [2]. In one such study, 90% of surveyed staff stated that their institution would benefit from further education or training in neonatal end-of-life care [1]. In another study, 93% of nurses reported a knowledge deficit surrounding neonatal end-of-life care [9].

The literature suggests that targeted education improves staff attitudes when providing end-of-life care [10]. One relevant study revealed an increase in nurses’ comfort in providing care to dying infants following the attendance of a bereavement seminar [11]. This finding corroborates a previous study that illustrated a correlation between knowledge and comfort when providing end-of-life care to pediatric patients [12]. Standardized guidelines and staff education also increase consistency in patient care, helping to optimize the end-of-life experience for patients and families [3, 8]. This may be achieved through the adoption of a visual indicator or symbol to increase awareness of sensitive situations, enabling staff to interact compassionately with patients and families and helping to create a calm and dignified environment [13, 14].

In our NICU there was no standardization of end-of-life practices, resulting in significant staff discomfort when providing end-of-life care. With the implementation of our guidelines and EHR modifications, we aimed to improve symptom management during end-of-life care, as measured by increasing pain medication usage by 50%, improve staff attitude scores toward providing end-of-life care and staff awareness of end-of-life cases on the unit by 20% by August 2024. Although there is no nationally recognized standard, these goals were chosen by the multidisciplinary team to be realistic and attainable.

Methods

Context

This study was conducted at NewYork-Presbyterian (NYP) Weill Cornell, a 60 bed academic level IV regional NICU in New York City with a single patient room design. Approximately 8,000 infants are born at this facility each year, with approximately 800 NICU admissions annually, including inborn deliveries and out-born transfers. The NICU receives approximately 100 transfers per year, both from hospitals within the NYP enterprise, consisting of 10 facilities, and outside hospitals. There are approximately 10 NICU patient deaths yearly, occurring in both the delivery room and the NICU. The NICU staff includes, but is not limited to, neonatologists, neonatology fellows, pediatric residents, neonatal and pediatric nurse practitioners, clinical pharmacists, respiratory therapists, physical and occupational therapists, speech language therapists, registered nurses, child life specialists, social workers, and care coordinators.

Improvement team

Our multidisciplinary QI team consisted of neonatologists, neonatology fellows, nurse practitioners, NICU quality leadership physicians, nursing champions, social workers, Certified Child Life Specialists, clinical pharmacists, members of the Department of Spiritual Care and Chaplaincy, and members of our palliative team, the Pediatric Advanced Care Team (PACT).

Interventions

The Model for Improvement was used to define the study aims, design measures and interventions, and to test interventions via Plan, Do, Study, Act (PDSA) cycles [15, 16]. The QI team created a key driver diagram (Fig. 1) as a visual tool representing the team’s shared understanding of what must change (primary and secondary drivers) and which changes may result in improved outcomes [15, 16]. This manuscript adheres to the SQuIRE 2.0 guidelines.

Improving symptom management and staff attitude and awareness during end-of-life care in the NICU were the targets for improvement, highlighted in SMART (specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, timely) Aims (left). PDSA 1: Baseline (Epoch 1) survey (August 2023)

We created a Likert scale survey to assess baseline staff comfort, confidence, and preparedness in providing end-of-life care. A total of 164 responses were received; respondents included both clinical and non-clinical staff members. Questions included: “I am comfortable talking about impending death with the family of a dying infant” and “I have sufficient education and clear guidelines on how to provide care for a dying infant.” An additional question “I always know if there is a dying patient in the room I am about to enter” was asked to examine staff awareness of end-of-life cases in the NICU.

PDSA 2: Generation of clinical guidelines and checklist (September 2023)

We developed evidence-based, standardized guidelines for end-of-life care in our level IV NICU to address the secondary drivers of interdisciplinary involvement, staff knowledge, and EHR incorporation. The guidelines included: delivery room management of infants prenatally diagnosed with life-limiting conditions, recommended medication dose(s), a process for withholding and withdrawing life sustaining therapies, use of the Purple Butterfly symbol, a memory making checklist, and recommendations for standardized EHR charting based on clinical role during end-of-life and postmortem care. A purple butterfly symbol was utilized as a visual communication tool to raise awareness of a sensitive situation, including but not limited to: imminent death, loss of a twin, or WLST. Use of this symbol helped create a dignified, respectful, and compassionate environment for end-of-life care. In addition, a checklist was created for ease of reference at bedside. The checklist consisted of a list of available memory making options, information regarding the Purple Butterfly symbol, nonpharmacological and pharmacological pain management recommendations, a process for WLST, suggested charting for nurses and providers leading up to and following the death of an infant, and frequently utilized contact information.

PDSA 3: Generation of EHR order set (September 2023)

We developed an EHR order set titled “NICU Advance Care Plan” to address the driver of EHR incorporation to improve workflow. The order set included: less frequent vital sign measurements (once a shift axillary temperatures, no manual blood pressures), no daily weights, no weekly head circumference or length measurements, and PACT consultation. The created order set was based on recommendations from the National Association of Neonatal Nurses’ position statement on Palliative and End-of-Life Care for Newborns and Infants and the American Academy of Pediatrics’ Guidance for Pediatric End-of-Life-Care, which focused on minimizing unnecessary interventions, promoting comfort and bonding, and involving palliative care specialists [17, 18]. The order set is available throughout the NYP enterprise on EHR.

PDSA 4: Staff education (October 2023)

To increase staff knowledge, awareness, and perception we conducted nursing education through small group sessions on the unit via read and sign, at the unit-based nursing skills fair, at the NICU monthly education series, and via email to all nursing staff. On the basis of the survey results, newer nurses were identified to be the least comfortable with end-of-life care. We conducted an educational session for those nurses that included: how to have difficult conversations, how to approach ethical dilemmas, symptom assessment and management, EHR charting, and postmortem care. Provider education was conducted through attendance at the neonatology fellow staff meeting, nurse practitioner staff meeting, and neonatologist faculty meeting. Other staff members were informed of the Purple Butterfly symbol via email.

PDSA 5: Follow-up (Epoch 2) survey (August 2024)

We conducted a follow-up survey in August 2024, a year following the previous survey. The questions and roles of respondents remained the same; however, the individual respondents differed. In Epoch 2, there were 136 responses. An additional question included: “Do you feel the end-of-life practices on this unit have changed in the past year?”.

Measures

Retrospective data were collected via individual manual review of EHR charts for all deaths that occurred in our NICU from January 2021 to June 2024. NYP utilizes Epic. We recorded demographic data (gestational age at birth, postmenstrual age at death, sex assigned at birth, race, primary language) and clinical characteristics (advanced care plan (ACP) status and duration of fentanyl equivalent administration) for each infant prior to and post-initiative. ACP status included: DNR (do not resuscitate), DNI (do not intubate), WLST, death after cardiopulmonary resuscitation, or delivery room death. We collected survey data via nonrandomized sampling of NICU staff, with the anonymous questionnaire being distributed via email and quick-response (QR) codes posted throughout the unit.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was symptom management, as measured by pain medication usage 24 h prior to death. Pain medication use was assessed by converting doses of all medications to fentanyl equivalents (mcg/kg) given that fentanyl is the primary opioid used in our patient cohort. Secondary outcome measures were staff attitude scores toward providing end-of-life care and staff awareness of end-of-life cases on the unit, both of which were assessed via a survey distributed to the staff.

Process measures

Process measures included the number of end-of-life cases that received a PACT consult, the number of end-of-life cases in which the Purple Butterfly was displayed outside the infant’s room, and the number of end-of-life cases that utilized our standardized vital sign measurements. Authors visited the room of each dying patient to assure placement of the Purple Butterfly symbol. Standardized vital sign measurement is defined as obtaining only one axillary temperature per shift, as the method of blood pressure monitoring is variable and dependent on the presence of an umbilical arterial catheter.

Balancing measures

The balancing measures were side effects of opioid administration and interdisciplinary conflict regarding plans of care between the NICU team and consulted services.

Statistical analysis

Chi-square tests were used to compare the race and ACP status of the pre- and post- intervention populations. Unpaired t-tests were used to compare gestational age at birth, postmenstrual age at death, frequency of vital sign measurement, and duration of fentanyl equivalent administration in both populations. Fisher’s Exact tests were used to compare sex assigned at birth, primary language, and PACT consults in the pre- and post- intervention populations.

To analyze the staff survey data initially between Epoch 1 and 2, Mann–Whitney U tests were performed. Additionally, survey data was dichotomized and Chi-square tests were run to determine if there was a change in the percentage of respondents who selected “strongly agree”. To assess staff awareness of end-of-life cases, Chi-square tests were performed. Statistical analysis was performed using the Graph Pad Prism 10.0 software. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

A Statistical Process Control (SPC) chart was used to analyze the primary outcome measure (XmR chart) [16]. A center line (CL) change was made based on the special cause signals on the chart and is defined as the mean. The upper control limits (UCL) and lower control limits (LCL) were defined as 3 sigma above and below the mean. The CL, upper control limits (UCL), and lower control limits (LCL) were calculated using QI Charts (licensed by Richard Scolville, Scolville Associated, 2009). We applied Associates in Process Improvement rules to detect special cause variations [16].

Results

We received a total of 300 survey responses, 164 in Epoch 1 and 136 in Epoch 2. Respondents included both clinical and non-clinical staff members. Clinical staff included attendings, fellows, residents, nurse practitioners, registered nurses, physical therapists, occupational therapists, respiratory therapists, patient care technicians, radiology technicians, and lactation consultants. Non-clinical staff included unit clerks, social workers, care coordinators, pharmacists, dieticians, chaplains, and child life specialists. In both epochs, most respondents were registered nurses, with 49% of respondents in Epoch 1 and 72% of respondents in Epoch 2. Outcome measures

Symptom management

The average fentanyl equivalents administered in the 24 h prior to death was 17.1 mcg/kg (CL) in Epoch 1 and increased to 70.1 mcg/kg (CL) in Epoch 2 following the generation of clinical guidelines, a bedside checklist, EHR modifications, and extensive staff education (Fig. 2). This was a 309% increase in the average fentanyl equivalents (in mcg/kg) administered in the 24 h prior to death. There was no significant difference in the number of days infants received fentanyl between Epochs 1 and 2. One infant in Epoch 1 was excluded from data collection regarding pain medication administration. They had undergone WLST, but were discharged home on hospice and died at home.

Staff awareness

To evaluate staff awareness of end-of-life cases on the unit, we included “I always know when there is a dying patient in the room I am about to enter” in both surveys. There was a significant increase in awareness as demonstrated by a 19-percentage point increase in those who answered "yes" (χ 2 (1, 298) = 13.59, p < 0.001).

In both epochs, 95% of respondents felt they would be better prepared to perform their job duties in a compassionate, patient-centered manner if they knew that there was a dying patient in a room they were about to enter.

Process measures

PACT consults

There was no statistically significant increase in the percentage of end-of-life patients who received a PACT consult from Epoch 1 to Epoch 2. 71% of end-of-life patients received PACT consults in Epoch 1 and 88% received them in Epoch 2.

Purple butterfly symbol

In 100% of the deaths in Epoch 2, the Purple Butterfly was displayed outside the infant’s room.

Vital sign measurement

There was a significant increase in the percentage of end-of-life cases that utilized our standardized vital sign measurements, from 33% in Epoch 1 (M = 33.3, SD = 48.8, n = 15) to 100% in Epoch 2 (M = 100, SD = 0, n = 8) with an unpaired t-test p-value of 0.0001.

Balancing measures

No adverse side effects were documented as a result of increased opioid administration. Although no formal data were collected, there was an increase in interdisciplinary conflict anecdotally between the primary team and the PACT team between Epoch 1 and Epoch 2.

Discussion

The creation of a multidisciplinary QI team focusing on end-of-life care, and in particular, implementation of end-of-life guidelines and EHR modifications, substantially improved symptom management for dying infants. Additionally, staff attitude scores improved and awareness surrounding end-of-life care increased.

One of the core tenants of palliative care is symptom management and the promotion of comfort throughout the dying process. Pain in neonates is often underappreciated and undertreated [5, 7]. This could be a result of off-label pharmaceutical use, inconsistent pain assessment tools, and difficulty interpreting symptoms [19]. It is important, not only for the dignity of the infant, but for the end-of-life experience of the caregivers that pain and other symptoms are adequately managed. Our goal was to increase palliative medication administration in the 24 h prior to death to ensure adequate symptom management, which was achieved with a 309% increase in the mean fentanyl equivalent administration. This increase in fentanyl administration cannot be explained by children who had developed opioid tolerance, as there was no significant difference in the number of days infants received fentanyl between Epochs 1 and 2.

In Epoch 2, our interventions ensured that all infants received palliative medication prior to their death. In contrast, in Epoch 1, three infants had ACPs, had undergone a redirection of care and received no palliative medication in the 24 h prior to their death. The increase in opioid administration was dramatic, perhaps because the recommended dosing alleviated provider discomfort with the prescription of medications. Extensive nursing education regarding end-of-life symptoms and increased advocacy for pain medication could also be contributors.

There has been conflicting evidence regarding the effects of end-of-life guidelines on medication administration to dying infants. One study showed that the implementation of a palliative care program in the NICU did not affect morphine use during the dying process [6]. However, other studies have demonstrated an increase in palliative medication use and improved parental assessment of infant comfort following the implementation of a perinatal palliative care program [20, 21]. Our study adds to the body of evidence that suggests that symptoms are better managed at end-of-life when there are standardized guidelines.

To our knowledge, this study is one of the first to reveal a statistically significant increase in self-reported staff comfort, confidence, and preparedness when providing end-of-life care in the NICU following the development of end-of-life guidelines. There is sufficient research that details the knowledge deficit and discomfort experienced by staff providing care to dying infants [1, 2, 9]. Research has also indicated that bereavement education increases staff comfort in the NICU; however, no studies have utilized standardized clinical practice guidelines to increase comfort [10, 11]. Our study highlights the importance of both education and guidelines to increase staff confidence, comfort, and preparedness. Accessible, comprehensive guidelines allow for continued reference to standards of care and lead to more consistent application in complex and nuanced scenarios.

Our study also revealed a significant increase in staff awareness of end-of-life cases between Epochs 1 and 2. We hypothesize that the increase in awareness was due to the adoption of a universal symbol for end-of-life, the Purple Butterfly. All infants who died in Epoch 2 had the Purple Butterfly displayed outside their room and awareness of end-of-life cases on the unit increased among both clinical and nonclinical staff. This data substantiates claims made by Catlin & Wool and Duffin regarding the importance of visual indicators in creating an optimal environment for end-of-life [13, 14]. We have since expanded the use of the Purple Butterfly symbol to all of maternal child health, including labor and delivery, postpartum, and pediatrics.

Our guidelines also included PACT consults, which remained frequently utilized, although the difference between the epochs was not statistically significant. This is perhaps due to the frequent utilization of PACT consults at baseline (71%) in Epoch 1. The incorporation of PACT services into our management led to an anecdotal increase in interdisciplinary conflict amongst the two services. This was thought to be due to differing plans of care perceived by the families. To combat this, we began a practice of pre-huddling with the entire interdisciplinary team prior to interactions with the family to discuss goals of care or deliver difficult news.

Limitations

There are various limitations to this study. The results were generated at a single, large level IV academic institution, potentially limiting their generalizability. Units with varying resources may produce varying results. The sample size for this study was limited, with only 32 infant deaths, and did not include deaths that occurred in the delivery room under the care of the obstetric team. The NICU team was not present at all deliveries of infants prenatally diagnosed with life-limiting conditions when palliative care was elected and provided by the obstetric team. Additionally, survey questions were not part of a validated tool. The team explored the use of the Neonatal Palliative Care Attitude Scale (NiPCAS) scale, but given its lengthiness and questions out of the realm of interest of this initiative, we generated a shorter, more specific survey. Furthermore, there is a potential for sampling bias given the nonrandomized sampling of survey participants. Lastly, interdisciplinary conflict was not formally measured and future surveys will assess this balancing measure in further detail.

Conclusion

The establishment of a multidisciplinary QI team is crucial in guiding end-of-life care for the most vulnerable pediatric population, those in the NICU. With the formation of the QI team, and in particular, with the generation of guidelines and EHR modifications we were able to surpass our aims and improve symptom management in dying infants by 309%. Additionally, we enhanced staff attitude scores and awareness regarding end-of-life care. Future research should involve surveying caregivers to evaluate the impact of our interventions on their experience with end-of-life care.
