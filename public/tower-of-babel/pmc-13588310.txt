Cranial MRI in Girls With Central Precocious Puberty: Clinical Characteristics and Predictors of Abnormal Findings
Authors: Fahad N. Al Juraibah, Mashael M. Alharbi, Reem D. Alharbi, Joud S. Albaraki, Sarah S. AlDharman, Hala A. Alghamdi, Nagarajkumar Yenugadhati, Abdullah I. Alhammad, Angham N. Almutair, Ibrahim A. Al Alwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectives:To describe the clinical profile of girls with central precocious puberty (CPP) and determine the factors associated with abnormal cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings.Methods:This retrospective study included girls aged 8 years or younger with a diagnosis of CPP who received care at King Abdullah Specialized Children's Hospital in Riyadh, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, between 2015 and 2023. Data on clinical presentation, biochemical results, imaging findings, and treatment were obtained from medical records. Logistic regression analysis was performed to determine variables associated with MRI utilization and the occurrence of abnormal MRI findings.Results:The cohort comprised 70 girls with CPP. The median age at breast development was 7.12 years, and pubertal onset occurred at ≥6 years in 84.3% of patients. Cranial MRI was obtained in 43 patients (61.4%), and abnormal findings were identified in 17 patients (24.3%). Pituitary gland enlargement (n = 6) and hypothalamic hamartoma (n = 4) were the most frequent abnormalities. Girls with a bone age-to-chronological age ratio <1 year had lower odds of undergoing MRI (OR = 0.18, p-value = 0.009). Higher BMI z-scores were independently associated with abnormal MRI findings (OR = 1.95, p-value = 0.043). Other clinical and biochemical variables were not significantly associated with abnormal MRI results. GnRH analogue therapy was initiated in 81.4% of patients.Conclusion:Abnormal cranial MRI findings were identified in nearly one-quarter of girls with CPP. Higher BMI z-scores were associated with abnormal MRI findings, whereas age at puberty onset and other clinical variables did not reliably predict intracranial abnormalities.

Introduction

Precocious puberty (PP) is defined as pubertal development that begins before 8 years of age in girls and before 9 years of age in boys. This condition may cause rapid linear growth and skeletal advancement, which can ultimately reduce adult height potential and contribute to psychosocial difficulties [1]. Central precocious puberty (CPP) is the gonadotropin-dependent subtype of PP, resulting from early activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis. Although most cases are idiopathic, CPP may also occur secondary to organic intracranial lesions [1].

In patients with CPP, cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is performed to investigate possible intracranial causes. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 15 studies involving 1,853 girls younger than 8 years evaluated the prevalence of intracranial abnormalities in this population. The overall prevalence of central nervous system (CNS) lesions was 9%, with pooled rates of 25% among girls younger than 6 years and 3% among those aged 6-8 years. Hypothalamic hamartoma was the most frequently reported abnormality [2]. In Saudi Arabia, another study evaluated 43 patients with CPP. Among girls, most cases of CPP were classified as idiopathic (67%), whereas half of the boys were found to have an underlying CNS abnormality as the cause of CPP. The most frequently reported finding was a hypothalamic hamartoma [3].

While clinical and biochemical markers such as age at onset, elevated basal luteinizing hormone (LH) levels, and rapid pubertal progression may indicate underlying pathology, predicting abnormal MRI results remains challenging [45]. The routine use of brain MRI in girls with CPP remains controversial, particularly among those whose pubertal development begins between 6 and 8 years of age. Understanding predictive factors for abnormal MRI findings is essential to refine diagnostic approaches and ensure timely intervention of neurological conditions contributing to CPP. The present study was undertaken to evaluate potential clinical and biochemical markers linked to abnormal brain MRI findings among girls with CPP, with the goal of improving identification of intracranial pathology.

Methods

Medical records of girls diagnosed with CPP and managed in the pediatric endocrinology clinic at a tertiary care pediatric hospital in Saudi Arabia, during 2015–2023 were retrospectively examined. The study excluded girls with peripheral causes of PP as well as boys with any form of PP. Additionally, girls with a history of brain tumors or CNS diseases such as epilepsy, developmental delay, or CNS malformation were excluded. Data were abstracted from electronic medical records, including caregiver- and/or patient-reported age at breast development, neurological symptoms such as headache, visual disturbance, seizures, and behavioral changes, height and BMI z-scores at the first pediatric endocrinology visit, pubertal stage, basal and stimulated LH, follicle stimulating hormone (FSH), random estradiol, left-hand X-ray findings for bone age assessment, and cranial MRI results.

A pediatric radiologist assessed bone age using the Greulich and Pyle atlas. Serum LH, FSH, and estradiol concentrations were measured on the Alinity I platform through chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassay. The CPP was confirmed using one or more of the following criteria: random LH ≥ 0.3 IU/L, peak LH ≥ 5 IU/L after GnRH agonist stimulation, or evidence of progressive pubertal development [6]. Cranial MRI was requested at the discretion of the treating pediatric endocrinologist because imaging decisions were not governed by a standardized institutional protocol. Cranial MRI examinations in our study were performed using either a 3.0 Tesla or 1.5 Tesla magnet (Philips Achieva system). The MRI protocol incorporated whole-brain axial T2 FLAIR and diffusion-weighted sequences, obtained with a slice thickness of 4 mm and an interslice interval of 1 mm. Sellar imaging comprised sagittal and coronal T1- and T2-weighted sequences, each obtained using 2-mm sections with 0.2-mm spacing. Post-contrast sagittal and coronal T1-weighted non-fat-suppressed images were added when clinically required. All scans were reviewed by a board-certified pediatric neuroradiologist.

The study protocol received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC), Ministry of National Guard Health Affairs, Riyadh, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (IRB No. IRB/2793/22).

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed with SAS 9.4. Continuous data were reported as medians with IQRs, and categorical data were presented as counts with corresponding percentages. Comparisons of continuous variables across groups were performed using the t-test or Mann–Whitney test, depending on the distribution of the data. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test. Logistic regression modeling was used to examine the association of patient-level characteristics with the study outcomes. ORs with 95% CIs were derived using Firth's penalized likelihood method to minimize bias related to the small sample size and quasi-complete separation. Condition indices were computed to assess multicollinearity; a value of 10 or more was considered high. A significance level of .05 was used in all the analyses.

Results

The cohort comprised 70 girls with CPP. Breast development occurred at a median age of 7.12 years, and 14 patients (20%) had documented CNS symptoms. At presentation to the pediatric endocrinology clinic, the median CDC-based height and BMI z-scores were 0.79 SD and 0.81 SD, respectively. Bone age was advanced by more than 1 year in 49 girls (70%). GnRH stimulation testing was performed in 25 patients, and baseline median LH and FSH concentrations were 0.6 IU/L and 2.6 IU/L, respectively (Table 1).

A cranial MRI was conducted in 61.4% of the cohort (n = 43/70). The decision to obtain a cranial MRI was significantly influenced by the bone age-to-chronological age ratio (p = 0.009). In contrast, the age of breast development, the presence of CNS symptoms, BMI z-score, and levels of LH and FSH did not demonstrate a significant association. Notably, children with a bone to chronological age ratio of less than 1 year had an 82% reduced likelihood of getting a cranial MRI compared to those with a ratio of 1 year or more (OR : 0.18; 95% CI : 0.05–0.64) (Table 2).

The cranial MRI findings were abnormal in 24.3% of the total cohort (n = 17/70). Among the patients who underwent MRI evaluation, abnormalities were identified in 39.5% (17/43). Within the subgroup with abnormal MRI findings, early pubertal onset (<6 years) was documented in 5 girls, whereas most cases occurred among girls whose pubertal development started at or beyond 6 years of age.

A bulky pituitary gland, regarded as a CPP-associated lesion, was the most prevalent abnormal MRI finding, identified in 6 girls. Among these, 3 exhibited a thickened pituitary stalk, necessitating evaluation by both an oncologist and a neurosurgeon to investigate the possibility of Langerhans cell histiocytosis or germinoma. They are currently under continuous monitoring, and according to the most recent assessment, neither Langerhans cell histiocytosis nor germinoma has been detected. The second-most common MRI finding was hypothalamic hamartoma, which is considered a CPP-causing lesion, observed in 4 girls. Additionally, 7 girls had incidental findings, including a mild form of hydrocephalus identified in 3 girls, 2 girls with an abnormal corpus callosum, 1 girl with periventricular leukomalacia, and 1 girl with subgaleal hematoma. All these cases had no prior neurological diagnosis or follow-up in pediatric neurology or neurosurgery, and these MRI findings were noted for the first time during the CPP workup (Fig. 1). Among the 11 girls whose pubertal development began before 6 years of age, 6 (55%) had normal MRI findings, and 5 (45%) demonstrated pathological lesions, which included 3 hypothalamic hamartomas, one case of periventricular leukomalacia, and one subgaleal hematoma.

Girls with hypothalamic hamartoma had a median age of 5.07 years, whereas the youngest affected patient was diagnosed at 1.74 years of age. The BMI z-score was a significant predictor of abnormal MRI results (p-value = 0.043), with the likelihood of detecting an abnormal MRI increasing by 95% for each unit rise in the BMI z-score among children (OR : 1.95; 95% CI: 1.02–3.71). Conversely, no significant relationship was observed between abnormal MRI findings and age at breast development, CNS symptoms, bone age advancement relative to chronological age, or LH and FSH concentrations (Table 3).

GnRH analogue therapy was initiated in 57 girls (81.4%). Treatment was more frequently prescribed among those with higher basal LH levels, elevated basal LH/FSH ratios, and higher stimulated LH/FSH ratios (p-value = 0.001, p-value = 0.002, and p-value = 0.03, respectively). Treatment decisions were significantly associated with cranial MRI results, with 68.4% of those treated undergoing MRI, and 31.6% of those cases exhibiting abnormal findings. Additionally, GnRH analogue was more likely to be prescribed in girls who had cranial an MRI performed or had abnormal results of cranial MRI (p-values = 0.024 and 0.016) (Table 4).

Discussion

Among girls diagnosed with CPP, we characterized clinical features and examined variables associated with intracranial lesions identified through MRI evaluation. A notable predictor for undergoing cranial MRI scan was the bone to chronological age ratio, with girls having a ratio below 1 year being 82% less likely to undergo cranial MRI evaluation. Additionally, higher BMI z-scores were associated with an increased likelihood of abnormal MRI findings, as girls with elevated BMI z-scores were twice as likely to exhibit such abnormalities. The decision to treat using GnRH analogues was associated with cranial MRI results; among those who received treatment, 68.4% underwent MRI, and 31.6% exhibited abnormal findings.

Puberty began at ≥6 years of age in most participants (84%), with a median onset age of 7.12 years. Cranial MRI was obtained in 61% of the cohort. The indication for cranial MRI in girls with CPP remains debated, particularly when puberty begins after 6 years. However, our findings suggest that pubertal onset age was not associated with physicians’ decisions to request MRI or with the detection of intracranial abnormalities. This aligns with a previous study indicating that age alone is an unreliable predictor of brain abnormalities, supporting the recommendation to perform MRI for all girls diagnosed with CPP [7]. Notably, in large cohorts, abnormal brain MRI findings have been identified in girls whose puberty started after 6 years of age [89]. Our study showed skeletal age acceleration to be a significant factor influencing the decision to perform imaging. While prior studies have not specifically assessed the bone to chronological age ratio as an independent indicator for performing cranial MRI scan in CPP patients, this ratio is a key diagnostic marker of CPP progression. A BA/CA ratio greater than 1.2 is commonly interpreted as evidence of progressive CPP and may support the decision to obtain MRI in girls aged 6–8 years [1011]. Nevertheless, the literature remains inconsistent, as some studies have found no significant difference in bone age between patients with normal and abnormal MRI results. Advanced bone age may signal rapid pubertal progression, prompting physicians to recommend cranial MRI to rule out underlying brain abnormalities.

Abnormal MRI results were found in 24.3% of the cohort, with most cases occurring in girls whose puberty began at ≥6 years. Age at pubertal onset was not significantly associated with MRI findings. Age at onset of puberty has been considered an influential factor for abnormal MRI finding, with conflicting data existing. One study involving 208 girls with CPP reported all abnormal MRIs occurring in girls older than 6 years old, consistent with our study's findings and suggesting a potential increase in abnormalities within this age group [12]. Another investigation of 229 girls with PP who underwent cranial MRI identified pathological brain lesions in 13 patients whose puberty began after 6 years of age, while 20 patients had incidental imaging findings [9]. More recently, a cohort of 30 patients showed MRI abnormalities in 46% of cases, with a mean pubertal onset age of 7.2 ± 0.96 years [5]. In a large multicenter cohort, abnormal brain MRI findings were reported in 13.5% of participants. Although breast development before 6 years of age predicted abnormal MRI findings, both patients with neoplastic lesions were older than 6 years [13]. Additionally, a retrospective multicenter cohort study involving girls with CPP from seven centers across six European countries was conducted. The study concluded that age alone cannot be safely utilized to determine which girls with CPP require brain imaging [14]. In contrast, a systematic review of 15 studies from 1990 through 2015 reported a pooled prevalence of MRI abnormalities at 25% in girls under 6 years old, which decreased to 3% in those aged 6 to 8 years [2]. Similarly, a study involving 182 girls with CPP found that 86% had normal brain MRIs, whereas hypothalamic hamartomas were identified in 3%, all occurring in girls younger than 6 years old [15]. Another investigation of sellar MRIs in 317 girls revealed normal findings in 91.8%, with incidental findings in 8.2%, none of which were pathological [16]. A large French study involving 443 girls with CPP identified CNS lesions in 19% of those under 6 years old, compared to only 2% in those aged 6 to 8 years [14]. Similarly, another study analyzing sellar MRIs in 295 girls reported abnormal findings in 13.2%, with prevalence decreasing from 24.2% among girls younger than 6 years to progressively lower rates as age increased beyond 6 years [8]. The existing conflicting data underscore that a proportion of girls aged 6 to 8 with CPP may have undetectable MRI abnormalities based on clinical or biochemical assessments alone. Therefore, brain MRI may warrant consideration as part of the diagnostic assessment of all girls diagnosed with CPP before 8 years of age.

The present study found that that abnormal MRI findings were more prevalent in children with higher BMI z-scores. High BMI and the early onset of puberty in girls has been well studied [17]. Although in our study the median BMI z-scores show a significant difference between girls with normal and abnormal MRI results, there is insufficient evidence to suggest it directly affect cranial MRI findings. The observed association between elevated BMI z-scores and abnormal MRI findings may be partly explained by physiological pituitary enlargement associated with obesity [18]. This suggests that pituitary hypertrophy in obese girls may mimic pathological changes, warranting careful radiological interpretation. Further research is needed to explore potential metabolic or hormonal pathways linking high BMI to CNS anomalies in CPP.

Our study found that 80% of 70 girls had no CNS symptoms at time of presentation, whereas only 14 had CNS symptoms. Studies have investigated the association of CNS symptoms and MRI findings, concluding that there is no strong relationship between the two. A retrospective study involving 67 girls with CPP who underwent cranial MRI demonstrated intracranial abnormalities in 15% of patients. Notably, none of the individuals with abnormal imaging findings had neurological manifestations at the time of presentation [7]. Another study showed that 13 out of 208 girls (6.3%) with CPP who had pathological findings on brain MRI had no CNS symptoms [9].

Treatment decisions for CPP are multifaceted and depend on factors such as the child's age at onset, bone age advancement, predicted adult height (PAH), and brain MRI findings [17]. In our cohort, most treated patients (50 out of 57) experienced puberty onset at or after 6 years old, and nearly three-quarters (42 out of 57) exhibited advanced bone maturation (BA/CA ratio ≥ 1 year). Advanced skeletal maturation is associated with earlier epiphyseal closure and a potential reduction in adult height attainment. Accordingly, careful assessment of bone age and PAH is important for guiding treatment decisions and estimating treatment benefit [17]. A study examined how GnRH agonist therapy impacts PAH based on treatment initiation timing among girls diagnosed with CPP [19]. At the time therapy was initiated, patients had a mean chronological age of 6.8 years and a baseline PAH averaging approximately 156 cm. During therapy, the mean BA/CA ratio decreased from 1.5 to 1.2, indicating a reduction in the rate of skeletal maturation relative to chronological age. In our study's cohort, undergoing cranial MRI was significantly associated with receiving treatment. Girls with abnormal MRI results were more frequently treated with GnRH analogues, reflecting a cautious approach to potential CNS involvement and rapid progression. Additionally, stimulated LH/FSH ratios emerged as significant predictors of treatment decisions, aligning with prior research linking CNS abnormalities to peak stimulated LH/FSH ratios of >0.6 [20].

Although the study contributes valuable evidence regarding MRI abnormalities and their predictors in CPP, its retrospective methodology and the low proportion of participants under 6 years of age remain important constraints that should be acknowledged. This age imbalance may have contributed to the lower detection rate of pathological lesions in younger patients. To strengthen the evidence base, additional prospective multicenter studies that include a larger proportion of younger girls should be undertaken to verify these findings and further delineate the diagnostic usefulness of cranial MRI across age categories. Cranial MRI was not obtained for every patient, and imaging decisions were based on individual physician judgment. Because imaging decisions were influenced by clinical judgment, girls with features suggestive of greater disease severity, such as younger age, CNS-related symptoms, or accelerated pubertal progression, may have undergone MRI more often. This may have introduced selection bias and increased the observed prevalence of MRI abnormalities. Nonetheless, the findings highlight key predictors—such as skeletal maturation ratios and hormonal markers—that inform diagnostic imaging decisions while emphasizing the importance of integrating clinical data into therapeutic strategies for managing CPP effectively.

In conclusion, the results showed that MRI utilization and abnormal imaging outcomes were associated with specific clinical characteristics in girls with CPP. Age at pubertal onset was not a determinant of abnormal MRI findings. Girls with a bone age-to-chronological age ratio of <1 year had lower odds of undergoing MRI, whereas higher BMI z-scores were related to greater odds of detecting MRI abnormalities. Abnormal MRI results were also associated with subsequent treatment, suggesting that imaging findings may contribute to management decisions. These observations strengthen the available evidence on CPP assessment and care. Confirmation through larger multicenter studies is needed to support more precise recommendations on cranial MRI use in this patient population.
