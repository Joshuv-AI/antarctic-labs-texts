Adaptive EEG preprocessing to mitigate electrode shift variability for robust motor imagery classification
Authors: Wenjing Xiong, Lin Ma, Haifeng Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Electrode placement variability poses a critical challenge in EEG-based motor imagery tasks, often resulting in reduced classification robustness. We present the Adaptive Channel Mixing Layer (ACML), a plug-and-play preprocessing module that dynamically adjusts input signal weights through a learnable transformation matrix based on inter-channel correlations. By leveraging the inherent spatial structure of EEG caps, ACML effectively compensates for electrode misalignments and noise, enhancing resilience to signal distortion. Experimental validation on two motor imagery datasets with varying channel counts demonstrated consistent improvements in accuracy (up to 1.4%), kappa scores (up to 0.018), and robust performance across subjects, using five neural network architectures including a state-of-the-art model (ATCNet). Notably, ACML requires minimal computational overhead and no task-specific hyperparameter tuning, ensuring compatibility with diverse applications. This method offers a robust and efficient solution for advancing EEG-based motor imagery classification, with potential applications in real-time brain-computer interface systems and neurorehabilitation.

Introduction

Electroencephalography (EEG) is a non-invasive method for recording brain electrical activity and is widely applied in brain-computer interface (BCI) technologies. BCIs aim to enable direct communication and control between the human brain and computers or other devices by decoding brain signals1. Among various BCI applications, the motor imagery paradigm (MI-BCI) has garnered significant attention due to its ability to facilitate control through the user’s imagination of specific movements.

However, MI-BCI faces several challenges in practical applications, with cross-trial and cross-subject variability being particularly prominent2. These issues manifest in real-world scenarios where users must undergo a calibration phase each day before they can effectively use the BCI system. This necessity arises because even the same subject can exhibit significant differences in EEG signals across different trials. These variations are influenced by physiological and psychological differences within the individual3, as well as inconsistencies in the placement of the EEG cap, which may shift slightly between sessions. Moreover, differences in head shape between subjects result in varying brain regions being covered by identically numbered electrodes4.

The variability in EEG patterns across trials and subjects leads to several application difficulties: The stability and consistency of signals are hard to maintain, causing a significant drop in model performance on new trials or subjects.The scarcity of data hinders effective model training for new subjects, limiting the generalization ability of BCI systems. This is because acquiring sufficient EEG data is time-consuming, labor-intensive, and may be constrained by ethical considerations and participant willingness5.

While existing research has extensively explored approaches to address activation pattern differences and noise variability through transfer learning, few solutions have been proposed for the problem of inaccurate electrode positioning. Electrode position shifts can significantly affect classification performance in BCI systems. Pawlitzki et al.18 conducted a t-test on simulated EEG data to assess the impact of single-electrode shifts on classification accuracy and reported a statistically significant performance degradation. Similarly, Park et al. systematically investigated the effects of dual-electrode shifts in three directions (1 cm displacement) and found a consistent decline in classification accuracy across four commonly used EEG features: power spectral density (PSD), phase locking value (PLV), a combination of PSD and PLV, and cross-correlation (CC)22. Furthermore, previous studies in high-density electromyography (EMG) have explored methods to mitigate the adverse effects of electrode shifts on classification accuracy23. While Riemannian geometry-based approaches (e.g., tangent space mapping9 and kernel-based alignment25 have been proposed to mitigate session and subject variability through alignment of covariance structures in a statistical manifold, they do not explicitly model spatial electrode displacement at the signal level. However, few existing works have explicitly targeted the mitigation of classification degradation caused by spatial electrode displacement at the signal level, despite its recognized impact on BCI reliability. Our proposed ACML addresses this gap by offering a learnable transformation directly applied to raw EEG signals, allowing the model to re-weight channels based on spatial interdependence. Unlike Riemannian methods that require matrix decomposition and geometric operations, ACML can be seamlessly embedded into any deep learning architecture with minimal computational overhead. Given the multi-channel nature of EEG signals, it is reasonable to expect that similar spatial variability issues would also impact EEG-based BCI performance. In this paper, we specifically address the challenge of inaccurate electrode positioning by introducing a plug-and-play pre-calibration module designed for deep learning models. This module first applies a linear transformation using a mixing weight matrix to generate a set of mixed signals, capturing relationships between different input channels. The mixed signals are then scaled by control weights and added back to the original input signals. This operation aims to correct or enhance the input data, enabling the model to emphasize or de-emphasize specific channels based on their learned importance. The corrected signals are subsequently passed to downstream layers for further processing. This adaptive channel mixing approach allows the model to better capture complex dependencies within the input data, improving its accuracy in tasks involving multichannel signals. Our solution could lead to more reliable MI-BCI systems, potentially reducing the need for daily recalibration and making BCI technology more accessible26.

Method

Adaptive channel mixing layer

The ACML is designed to mitigate spatial variability in EEG signals caused by electrode displacement due to head movement across trials or by repositioning when the same subject re-engages with the BCI system. The key principle of ACML lies in performing a linear transformation on the original EEG signals through a trainable mixing weight matrix, thereby generating a set of channel-mixed signals. These signals capture the spatial inter-channel dependencies that traditional methods often overlook. A set of trainable control weights is then used as a control mechanism, allowing the model to adaptively balance the proportion between the original signals and the channel-mixed signals. This further enables the dynamic adjustment of the importance of each channel, correcting for positional shifts and enhancing the relevant signal features. Figure 2 shows the pipeline of ACML integrated into the model. Notably, although the datasets provide electrode location information, ACML does not require coordinate input. Instead, it operates directly on the EEG signals, learning to adaptively mix channels based on learned spatial dependencies, making it suitable even in scenarios with incomplete or noisy electrode metadata.

The core function of ACML is to specifically account for spatial variability in electrode placement, which can distort signal readings. Electrode misalignment affects the measured potential distribution; ACML aims to mitigate this by adjusting the weights of signals based on their relative positions. This re-weighting process helps to normalize the spatial contribution of each electrode, thereby improving measurement accuracy25,27.

Dataset and preprocessing

This study selected two classic publicly available motor imagery EEG datasets with varying numbers of channels: BCI Competition IV 2a28 and the High Gamma dataset29. The selection serves two primary purposes: first, to assess the generalization capability of the proposed method across datasets with different electrode configurations; and second, to evaluate the robustness of the method by comparing motor imagery tasks that involve “imagined” movements (as in BCI Competition IV 2a) with tasks involving “real” movements (as in the High Gamma dataset). The latter is expected to introduce more significant noise interference, thus providing a rigorous test of the method’s resilience.

BCI competition IV 2a

The BCI Competition IV 2a dataset is a well-established benchmark for evaluating motor imagery classification algorithms. It comprises EEG data from nine subjects, recorded over 22 electrode channels with a sampling rate of 250 Hz. Each subject participated in two sessions conducted on different days, with each session containing 288 trials, resulting in a total of 576 trials per subject. The dataset includes four distinct motor imagery tasks: left hand, right hand, both feet, and tongue, each with an effective duration of 4 s. Typically, one session is designated for training, while the other is reserved for testing, given the separation of sessions by alternate days.

High-gamma dataset

The High-Gamma Dataset is a four-class motor task EEG dataset acquired from 14 healthy subjects. It consists of approximately 1000 trials (963.1 ± 150.9) per subject, each with a duration of 4 s. The tasks include left-hand movements, right-hand movements, movements of both feet, and rest. The training set comprises around 880 trials, excluding the last two runs, while the test set includes approximately 160 trials from the final two runs.

Although both datasets used in this study are four-class classification tasks, the proposed method is independent of the number of task labels and is mainly related to the physical characteristics of the multichannel data.

Preprocessing

The data preprocessing pipeline consists of three key steps: channel-wise normalization, Euclidean space alignment of the covariance matrices30, and trial-level global normalization. Channel Normalization ensures that the data distribution is standardized across channels by normalizing each channel’s mean and standard deviation. This helps mitigate the amplitude differences between channels. Euclidean Space Alignment reduces inter-trial covariance variations by aligning the data in a consistent geometric space, improving robustness against trial-to-trial variability. Global Trial Normalization scales each trial to a uniform range, preventing extreme values from disproportionately affecting model performance. These steps are designed to improve data consistency and enhance model stability in the presence of electrode displacement.

Baseline models and experiment setup

To thoroughly evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed electrode channel calibration layer, we selected four highly cited neural network models for motor imagery classification—DeepConvNet29, EEGNet31, TCNet_Fusion32, and EEG-TCNet33—as representative classical baselines. These models have been extensively cited and serve as foundational architectures that emphasize temporal, spatial, or hybrid feature extraction using convolutional and/or recurrent structures.

In addition, we included ATCNet34 as the state-of-the-art reference model. Compared to the classical models, ATCNet incorporates advanced modules such as attention mechanisms and dilated temporal convolutions, enabling it to capture both local and long-range dependencies more effectively. It has demonstrated superior performance on multiple MI-BCI datasets and serves as a strong benchmark for evaluating the incremental benefit of integrating the proposed ACML module.

The experimental setup in this study involved two rounds of training and testing on five neural network models using two selected motor imagery datasets. Identical random seeds and hyperparameter settings were applied across all experiments. In the first round, the original models were used, while in the second round, the models were augmented with the Adaptive Channel Mixing Layer (ACML) proposed in this paper. The effectiveness of the ACML module was assessed by comparing the classification metrics from both rounds. The results for BCI Competition IV 2a are presented in Tables 1 and 2, while the results for the High-Gamma dataset are shown in Tables 3 and 4.

All experiments were conducted in a subject-specific manner, following standard data splits adopted in prior studies. For each model, we used random seeds 1, 2, and 3 to independently re-initialize and train the model. For each run, 20% of the training set was randomly selected as a validation set. The model parameters that achieved the best performance on the validation set were retained. These selected models were then evaluated on the test set, and the corresponding results for each metric were recorded. We report the best test set performance across the three runs. The batch size was set to 64 for BCI Competition IV 2a and 32 for the High-Gamma dataset. The learning rate was fixed at 0.001, and the Adam optimizer was used. All models were trained for 500 epochs, and their implementation followed the original code published in the corresponding papers.

Discussion

While inter-session variability was considered as an implicit approximation of electrode displacement, it should be noted that such variability also involves non-spatial factors such as physiological state, hardware differences, and environmental noise. Therefore, this approximation only partially reflects the structured spatial distortions introduced by electrode shifts. Nevertheless, in practical EEG recordings, electrode repositioning is a common source of inter-session variability, and thus remains relevant for assessing the spatial robustness of decoding models. Future work will further integrate the physical characteristics of cross-session variability into the design of the ACML to enhance its practical robustness. Building on this understanding, our results reveal the following three key observations:

First, from the classification results of both datasets, it is evident that incorporating the ACML module leads to an improvement in the average classification accuracy of all neural network models while also reducing the standard deviation of accuracy across subjects. In clinical EEG applications, acquisition systems vary widely in terms of electrode number, layout, and manufacturer specifications. The consistent improvements achieved by ACML across datasets with different electrode configurations and across multiple backbone models highlight its strong generalization potential under such heterogeneous conditions. Although the average improvement over ATCNet (1.4%) may appear modest, it is consistent and achieved against a highly competitive baseline, indicating enhanced robustness. Since ACML is designed to improve spatial stability, such small yet stable gains can contribute meaningfully to the reliability of BCI systems in both practical and clinical deployment.

Second, the performance gains brought by ACML vary across models, likely due to differences in architectural capacity. Models such as ATCNet and EEGTCNet_Fusion, which have deeper structures and stronger temporal-spatial representation capabilities, benefit more from ACML. In contrast, simpler models like EEGNet may not fully exploit the adaptive alignment due to limited depth.

Finally, it is noteworthy that the magnitude of performance improvement varies between the two datasets. Specifically, the increase in average classification accuracy and kappa values is significantly higher for Dataset 1 than for Dataset 2. Several potential reasons may explain this discrepancy: Baseline performance differences determine the potential for improvement. The baseline accuracy of Dataset 1 is relatively low, leaving more room for improvement. For instance, within the same model, the minimum classification accuracy difference between the two datasets is 13.9% points, while the maximum difference reaches 29.6% points. Furthermore, some subjects in Dataset 2 already achieve baseline classification accuracies exceeding 90%, limiting the potential for further improvement.Electrode placement errors impact Dataset 1 more significantly. While Dataset 1 includes only 22 electrodes and Dataset 2 employs 128, it is possible that a smaller number of channels makes the signal more sensitive to electrode shifts across sessions. Based on this observation, we hypothesize that the ACML module is particularly beneficial for datasets with fewer electrodes, as it helps compensate for spatial inconsistencies. However, we acknowledge that this interpretation is preliminary and would benefit from validation on additional datasets with varying channel densities.Differences in task-related brain regions affect spatial transformation benefits. Dataset 1 consists of four motor imagery tasks, whereas Dataset 2 comprises four specific movement tasks. The former engages broader brain regions, requiring coordinated activity across multiple cortical areas, including the primary motor cortex, supplementary motor area, and somatosensory regions. In contrast, the latter is more localized, primarily involving activity within the motor cortex. As a result, global spatial transformations introduced by the ACML module are more advantageous in Dataset 1.

It is worth noting that established alignment techniques such as Euclidean and Riemannian alignment focus on covariance-level normalization and are typically validated through standard cross-subject evaluations. These methods do not explicitly address channel shift at the signal level. In contrast, ACML provides an adaptive, learnable mechanism that can enhance the spatial robustness of deep learning models without requiring geometric transformations. Future work may explore the combination of ACML with statistical alignment techniques for improved cross-subject generalization.

To further validate the effectiveness of ACML, we conducted paired t-tests comparing model performance with and without ACML across subjects on kappa values. The results indicate statistically significant improvements (p < 0.05) for DeepConvNet and EEGTCNet_Fusion on both datasets, and for ATCNet on the High Gamma dataset. For EEGNet and EEGTCNet, while improvements were observed, they were not statistically significant. Prior to conducting t-tests, we performed Shapiro-Wilk tests to assess the normality of subject-wise accuracy differences. Most model comparisons across both datasets met the normality assumption (p > 0.05). For models where normality was not satisfied (e.g., DConv and TCNet on the High Gamma dataset), Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were additionally applied. The results remained statistically significant for DConv (p < 0.05), while EEGTCNet did not reach significance under either test, consistent with the overall trend. These findings suggest that the benefits of ACML are particularly pronounced in models with higher representational capacity or greater temporal depth.

Conclusion

In both research and practical applications, there is a substantial variability in EEG patterns across subjects, making it impractical to learn a unified model architecture that generalizes well across all individuals. Instead, fine-tuning the model on small sample data from new subjects becomes more critical, a process that can be facilitated through methods such as meta-learning or domain adaptation. This is feasible in real-world scenarios, where it is typically possible to pre-train the model using a small amount of task-specific data from new subjects and subsequently fine-tune the existing model. Additionally, while inter-trial noise variations can be mitigated to some extent by the feature extraction capabilities of neural networks, not all noise can be completely eliminated. Therefore, in the context of current experiments, the focus should be more on addressing cross-trial variability rather than cross-subject variability.

In this study, we introduced an Adaptive Channel Mixing Layer (ACML) designed to address the challenges posed by electrode misplacement in EEG caps, a common issue that can significantly impact the accuracy of motor imagery on cross-trial classification. The ACML is a flexible preprocessing layer that can be seamlessly integrated into existing neural networks, enabling joint training and testing without necessitating major modifications to the original models. Our experiments, conducted on two motor imagery EEG datasets with varying channel numbers and five different neural network architectures, demonstrate that the ACML not only enhances the performance of baseline models but also improves their robustness by reducing sensitivity to random seed initialization. Notably, even when applied to state-of-the-art models that have already undergone significant optimization, our method achieved further improvements—boosting the average accuracy by 1.4% and 1.2% on two different datasets and increasing the average kappa score by 0.018 and 0.01, respectively. This highlights the effectiveness and potential of the ACML in refining already high-performing models.

The key strength of the ACML lies in its versatility and ease of integration, making it an invaluable tool for a broad spectrum of multichannel signal processing tasks, from motor imagery classification to other applications requiring real-time processing. Despite its effectiveness, the current implementation of ACML is most suitable for subject-dependent classification tasks, where it excels at adapting to the significant variability in skull shape and hairstyle across individuals.

Looking ahead, future work should explore encoding-based approaches to cluster the influence patterns of skull shape and hairstyle on electrode positioning. Such advancements would enable the system to generalize more effectively to new subjects, reducing the need for extensive model fine-tuning and offering a more comfortable and resource-efficient experience in practical applications. While ACML is designed to mitigate electrode displacement, we did not simulate artificial perturbations in this study. This aligns with prior alignment-based methods9,25, which also rely on subject session variability for evaluation. We acknowledge that explicit spatial distortions could provide further insights and plan to explore this in future work.
