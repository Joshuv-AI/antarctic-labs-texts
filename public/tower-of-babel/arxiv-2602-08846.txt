Impredicativity in Linear Dependent Type Theory
Sam L. Speight, Niels van der Weide

Abstract
We construct a realizability model of linear dependent type theory from a linear combinatory algebra. Our model motivates a number of additions to the type theory. In particular, we add a universe with two decoding operations: one takes codes to cartesian types and the other takes codes to linear types. The universe is impredicative in the sense that it is closed under both large cartesian dependent products and large linear dependent products. We also add a rule for injectivity of the modality turning linear terms into cartesian terms. With all of the additions, we are able to encode (linear) inductive types. As a case study, we consider the type of lists over a linear type, and demonstrate that our encoding has the relevant uniqueness principle. The construction of the realizability model is fully formalized in the proof assistant Rocq.

Introduction

Various proof assistants,
such as Rocq
[
62
]

and Lean
[
21
]
, are based on
higher-order type theory
.
Higher-order type theories, such as the calculus of constructions
[
18
]
,
are powerful systems that provide a suitable foundation for both mathematics and computer science, that is, for both proving and programming.
One of their key features is impredicativity.

Impredicativity comes in many forms,
and our focus on an
impredicative universe of types
in dependent type theory.
This means that the universe is closed under functions from any type to a small one.
Concisely, small types are closed under “large” products.

Impredicativity adds a lot strength to a logical system.
For instance,
such a system admits a translation from System F
[
25
,
52
]
,
which allows one to use
impredicative encodings
(or polymorphic encodings) to reduce inductive types to (dependent) function types.
Such encodings guarantee that the resulting types satisfy the necessary recursion and
\beta
-principles.
However, they do not satisfy the relevant induction principle in general
[
23
,
55
]
.
To fix these defects,
various methods have been developed
[
7
,
9
,
43
,
71
,
72
]
.

One challenge with higher-order type theories lies in finding semantics for them.
Since there is no (classical) set-theoretic model of higher-order type theories
[
53
]
,
one needs alternative techniques to construct models for them.

Realizability
,
which dates back to Kleene
[
34
]
,
has established itself as one of the main methods to do so
[
61
]
.
Its applications range from program extraction
[
49
]
to the construction of models that demonstrate the consistency of axioms
such as the formal Church’s thesis
[
31
,
69
]

and parametricity
[
36
]
.

Our goal in this paper is to extend the methods of realizability to higher-order type theories that incorporate
linearity
in their typing system.
Intuitively,
linearity says that functions are not allowed to delete or duplicate their arguments:
they must use each of their arguments exactly once.
This discipline lends itself to various application domains,
for instance,
quantum programming languages
[
22
]

and session types
[
63
]
.

Specifically,
we set out to to construct a realizability model of linear dependent type theory (LDTT)
[
12
,
13
,
37
,
68
,
67
,
40
,
41
]
that features impredicativity.
The model demostrates consistency of impredicativity with LDTT.
As an application of impredicativity in LDTT,
we use impredicative encodings
to construct an initial algebra of lists with elements taken from a linear type.

Formalization

The results in
\Cref
sec:linearrealizability,sec:linearrealizabilitymodel
are formalized in the proof assistant Rocq
[
62
]
using the UniMath library
[
70
]
.
We make heavy use of numerous results on category theory in UniMath
[
4
,
6
,
73
]
.
Our formalization is publicly available online
[
60
]
,
and the file
Paper.v
indicates the relation between the paper and the formalized results.

Throughout this paper,
we use set-theoretic notation,
whereas our formalization is based on type theory.
While the largest part of the development remains unaffected by this difference in foundations,
there is one aspect worth mentioning:
in set theory, one defines Grothendieck fibration using functors;
whereas one uses displayed categories in type theory
[
5
]
.

Linear dependent type theory

In this section, we recall the syntax of
linear dependent type theory
(LDTT),
and we introduce a number of additions that we use throughout this paper.
The version of LDTT that we use is closest to that of Lundfall
[
40

[

The different forms of judgement for linear dependent type theory are laid out in

Note that we have both cartesian contexts and mixed contexts; the latter contains a cartesian part and a linear part (though either may be empty). Types (whether cartesian or linear) are only well-formed in a cartesian context, hence cannot depend on linear variables. Cartesian terms are typed in a cartesian context; whereas linear terms are typed in a mixed context.

The remaining rules that we postulate are justified by our realizability model in

Equalizers

The first set of rules corresponds to having equalizers on the linear side. The formation rule is:

\frac{\Gamma\mid\cdot\vdash f:A\multimap B\quad\Gamma\mid\cdot\vdash g:A\multimap B}{\Gamma\vdash{\mathsf{Eq}(f,g)}\,\mathsf{lin}}\quad\textsc{right=eq-f}

Next we have the introduction rule, which corresponds to the existence part of the universal property of equalizers.

\frac{\Gamma\mid\Xi\vdash h:C\multimap A\quad\Gamma\mid\Xi\vdash f\circ h\equiv g\circ h:C\multimap B}{\Gamma\mid\Xi\vdash\overline{h}:C\multimap\mathsf{Eq}(f,g)}\quad\textsc{right=eq-i}

The elimination rule supplies the equalizer map.

\frac{}{\Gamma\mid\Xi\vdash\iota_{f,g}:\mathsf{Eq}(f,g)\multimap A}\quad\textsc{right=eq-e}

We have two
\beta
-rules.
The first
\beta
-rule expresses that the maps
f
and
g
are equalized by the map
\iota_{f,g}
. Note that they are equalized up to judgemental equality.
The second
\beta
-rule corresponds to the commutativity condition on the universal morphism.

\displaystyle\Gamma\mid\Xi\vdash f\circ\iota_{f,g}\mathrel{\equiv}g\circ\iota_{f,g}:\mathsf{Eq}(f,g)\multimap B

(
eq-
\beta
1
)

\displaystyle\Gamma\mid\Xi\vdash\iota_{f,g}\circ\overline{h}\mathrel{\equiv}h:C\multimap A

(
eq-
\beta
2
)

Finally, we have an
\eta
-rule that corresponds to uniqueness of the universal morphism.

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma\mid\Xi\vdash h,h^{\prime}:C\multimap\mathsf{Eq}(f,g)\quad\Gamma\mid\Xi\vdash\iota_{f,g}\circ h\mathrel{\equiv}\iota_{f,g}\circ h^{\prime}:C\multimap A}{\Gamma\mid\Xi\vdash h\mathrel{\equiv}h^{\prime}:C\multimap\mathsf{Eq}(f,g)}\quad\textsc{right=eq-\text{$\eta$}}

Injectivity

We add a rule for injectivity of the
\mathsf{M}
modality.

\frac{\Gamma\mid\cdot\vdash\mathsf{M}(a)\mathrel{\equiv}\mathsf{M}(a^{\prime}):\mathsf{M}(A)}{\Gamma\mid\cdot\vdash a\mathrel{\equiv}a^{\prime}:A}\quad\textsc{right=\text{$\mathsf{M}$}-inj}

Semantically, this corresponds to faithfulness of the functor
\mathsf{M}
(see
16
). Note that the linear context is empty, as we are only allowed to apply
\mathsf{M}
in an empty linear context.

Identity types of cartesian types together with the modality
\mathsf{M}
facilitates derived identity types of
linear
types (c.f.
[
40
, Section 6.2]
). We write
a=_{\mathsf{L}}a^{\prime}
to mean
\mathsf{M}(a)=\mathsf{M}(a^{\prime})
, where we use
=
for identity types of cartesian types. Thus we may only consider identity types of two linear terms in the empty linear context.

Combining
\mathsf{M}
-
inj
with equality reflection yields equality reflection for derived linear identity types: Suppose that we have a proof
a=_{\mathsf{L}}a^{\prime}
. This is, by definition, a proof that
\mathsf{M}(a)=\mathsf{M}(a^{\prime})
. By equality reflection we can deduce
\mathsf{M}(a)\equiv\mathsf{M}(a^{\prime})
. Finally, using
\mathsf{M}
-
inj
, we conclude that
\mathsf{M}(a)\equiv\mathsf{M}(a^{\prime})
. We will make use of linear equality reflection in
section 5
.

We derive the following principle of function extensionality for
\mathop{\mathchoice{\scalebox{2.0}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.6}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.0}{$\sqcap$}}}\displaylimits
. To obtain this, we first derive a judgemental version using
\mathop{\mathchoice{\scalebox{2.0}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.6}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.0}{$\sqcap$}}}\displaylimits
-
\eta

\frac{\Gamma\mid\cdot\vdash f,g:\mathop{\mathchoice{\scalebox{2.0}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.6}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.0}{$\sqcap$}}}\displaylimits_{x:A}B\quad\Gamma,x:A\vdash p:f\>x=_{\mathsf{L}}g\>x}{\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{FunExt}(p):f=_{\mathsf{L}}g}\quad\textsc{right=\text{$\mathop{\mathchoice{\scalebox{2.0}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.6}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.0}{$\sqcap$}}}\displaylimits$}-FunExt}

Similarly,
we have function extensionality for non-dependent linear function types.

Impredicative universe

Finally, we have rules are for an impredicative Tarski-style universe
\mathcal{U}
.
Note that
\mathcal{U}
itself is a cartesian type, as we want to be able to use variables of type
\mathcal{U}
(i.e. codes for types) in an unrestricted manner.

\frac{\vdash\Gamma\,\text{context}}{\Gamma\vdash{\mathcal{U}}\,\mathsf{type}}\quad\textsc{right=\text{$\mathcal{U}$}-f}

The universe comes equipped with two decoding operations: the first takes codes to cartesian types, and the second takes codes to linear types.
This is in contrast to having separate cartesian and linear universes.

\frac{\Gamma\vdash A:\mathcal{U}}{\Gamma\vdash{\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{C}}\left(A\right)}\,\mathsf{type}}\quad\textsc{right=\text{$\mathsf{El_{C}}$}}\qquad\frac{\Gamma\vdash A:\mathcal{U}}{\Gamma\vdash{\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(A\right)}\,\mathsf{lin}}\quad\textsc{right=\text{$\mathsf{El_{L}}$}}

The universe is impredicative in the sense that it is closed under “large” cartesian dependent products
and
“large” linear dependent products.

\frac{\Gamma\vdash{A}\,\mathsf{type}\quad\Gamma,x:A\vdash B:\mathcal{U}}{\Gamma\vdash\mathop{\widehat{\prod}}_{x:A}B:\mathcal{U}}\quad\textsc{right=\text{$\widehat{\prod}$}-f}\qquad\frac{\Gamma\vdash{A}\,\mathsf{type}\quad\Gamma,x:A\vdash B:\mathcal{U}}{\Gamma\vdash\mathop{\widehat{\mathop{\mathchoice{\scalebox{2.0}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.6}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.0}{$\sqcap$}}}\displaylimits}}_{x:A}B:\mathcal{U}}\quad\textsc{right=\text{$\widehat{\mathop{\mathchoice{\scalebox{2.0}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.6}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.0}{$\sqcap$}}}\displaylimits}$}-f}

To distinguish these “
\mathcal{U}
-internal” type formers from the “external” type formers, we have used the

decoration. We stipulate that associated internal and external type formers are related by an isomorphism:

\displaystyle\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{C}}\left(\mathop{\widehat{\prod}}_{x:A}B\right)\cong\prod_{x:A}\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{C}}\left(B\right)\qquad\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(\mathop{\widehat{\mathop{\mathchoice{\scalebox{2.0}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.6}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.0}{$\sqcap$}}}\displaylimits}}_{x:A}B\right)\cong\mathop{\mathchoice{\scalebox{2.0}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.6}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.0}{$\sqcap$}}}\displaylimits_{x:A}\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(B\right)

The above isomorphisms can be used to derive abstraction and application operators. When there is no ambiguity, such as when we are working entirely within

Definition 1

An

The objects defined in the following are usually called

Definition 2

A

, from any LCA
A=(A,\bullet,!)
, we may construct a CCA
A_{!}\coloneq(A,\bullet_{!})
on the same underlying set where
a\bullet_{!}b\coloneqq a\mathrel{\bullet}{!b}
.

Combinatory completeness

The key property of CCAs is their ability to mimic
\lambda
-abstraction. This property is known as
combinatory completeness
. LCAs enjoy their own form of combinatory completeness, which relates to the linear
\lambda
-calculus. These properties are extremely useful when exhibiting realizers for our model in
section 4
.

Let
A
be a set and
V
be a countably infinite set of variables. Polynomials over
A
are given by the first three clauses of the following grammar;
!
-polynomials are given by the whole grammar.

\displaystyle t,t^{\prime}::=x\in V\,\mid\,a\in A\,\mid\,t\bullet t^{\prime}\,\mid\,!t

We adopt the same conventions for (
!
-)polynomials as for applicative systems with
!
.

Closed polynomials can be identified with elements of
A
. If
A
comes with a
!:A\rightarrow A
, then closed
!
-polynomials can be identified with elements of
A
. Two (
!
-)polynomials
t,t^{\prime}
with variables in
\{x_{1},...,x_{n}\}
are considered equal when for all
a_{1},...,a_{n}\in A
,
t[a_{1},...,a_{n}/x_{1},...,x_{n}]=t^{\prime}[a_{1},...,a_{n}/x_{1},...,x_{n}]
.
A variable occurring in a
!
-polynomial
t
is
linear
iff it appears exactly once and not in scope of a
!
.
We write
\mathsf{vars}(t)
for the set of variables occurring in a (
!
-)polynomial
t
, and
\mathsf{linvars}(t^{\prime})
for the set of linear variabels occurring in a
!
-polynomial
t^{\prime}
.

Proposition 4

(Curry
[
20
]
)
.

The following are equivalent for an applicative system
(A,\bullet)
:

•

(A,\bullet)
is a cartesian combinatory algebra.

•

(A,\bullet)
is cartesian combinatory complete. That is, for every polynomial
t
over
A
, there is a polynomial
\lambda^{*}x.\,t
over
A
, with
\mathsf{vars}(\lambda^{*}x.\,t)=\mathsf{vars}(t)-\{x\}
, such that
(\lambda^{*}x.\,t)x=t
.

Proposition 5

(Simpson
[
59
]
)
.

Let
(A,\bullet)
be an applicative system and
!:A\rightarrow A
. The following are equivalent:

•

(A,\bullet,!)
is a linear combinatory algebra.

•

(A,\bullet,!)
is linear combinatory complete. That is, for every
!
-polynomial
t
over
A
:

–

if
x\in\mathsf{linvars}(t)
, then there is a polynomial
\lambda^{*}x.\,t
over
A
,
with
\mathsf{vars}(\lambda^{*}x.\,t)=\mathsf{vars}(t)-\{x\}

and
\mathsf{linvars}(\lambda^{*}x.\,t)=\mathsf{linvars}(t)-\{x\}
,
such that
(\lambda^{*}x.\,t)x=t
;

–

there is a polynomial
\lambda!^{*}x.\,t
over
A
,
with
\mathsf{vars}(\lambda!^{*}x.\,t)=\mathsf{vars}(t)-\{x\}

and
\mathsf{linvars}(\lambda!^{*}x.\,t)=\mathsf{linvars}(t)-\{x\}
,
such that
(\lambda!^{*}x.\,t)!x=t
.

4
Linear realizability model

In this section,
we construct the realizability model of linear dependent type theory. Traditional approaches to realizability start with a (cartesian) combinatory algebra,
and then a model of Martin-Löf type theory is constructed from assemblies over that CCA
[
42
,
51
]
.
Such a model is represented using some categorical structure,
and often, comprehension categories are used
[
33
]
.
We set out to do the same, but for LDTT using linear combinatory algebras.
In order to model LDTT,
we use a more refined categorical structure,
namely
linear comprehension categories
,
which are based on work by Lundfall
[
40
,
41
]
.

We start this section by recalling comprehension categories (
\Cref
def:comp-cat) and the realizability model of Martin-Löf type theory (
\Cref
exa:assembly-comp-cat).
After that,
we introduce linear comprehension categories (
\Cref
def:linear-comp-cat).
Throughout this section we show that every LCA gives rise to a linear comprehension categories, called the
linear realizability model
.
We discuss the relevant features step by step.
Specifically,
we first discuss the interpretation of cartesian types (
\Cref
exa:assembly-comp-cat)
and linear types (
\Cref
exa:monoidal-fibration-lca),
and the modalities (
\Cref
exa:lca-linear-comp-cat).
We finish by interpreting various type formers (
\Cref
exa:linear-realizability-types),
including equalizer types (
\Cref
exa:linear-realizability-equalizers) and an impredicative universe (
\Cref
exa:impred-universe).

4.1
Realizability models of type theory

We begin with a brief overview of comprehension categories
and the usual realizability model of dependent type theory.
The key ingredient for comprehension categories is the notion of a
Grothendieck fibration

[
26
]
,
which is defined as follows.

Definition 6
.

Let
\mathsf{p}:\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
be a functor,
and let
f:x\rightarrow y
be a morphism in
\mathcal{E}
.
We say that
f
is
cartesian

if for all objects
z\in\mathcal{E}

and morphisms
h:z\rightarrow y
and
g:\mathsf{p}(z)\rightarrow\mathsf{p}(x)

such that
\mathsf{p}(h)=\mathsf{p}(f)\circ g
,
there is a unique
k:z\rightarrow y
with
\mathsf{p}(k)=g

making the resulting triangle commute.
We say that
\mathsf{p}
is a
(Grothendieck) fibration

if for all
f:x\rightarrow\mathsf{p}(y)
in
\mathcal{B}

there is an object
\overline{x}\in\mathcal{E}
with
\mathsf{p}(\overline{x})=x

and a cartesian morphism
\overline{f}:\overline{x}\rightarrow y

with
\mathsf{p}(\overline{f})=f
.

If we have a fibration
\mathsf{p}:\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
,
then we can think of
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{E}
as the categories of contexts and of types respectively.
Given an object
x\in\mathcal{B}
,
we denote the fibre category of
\mathsf{p}
along
x
by
\mathsf{p}[x]
.
Every morphism
s:x\rightarrow y
gives rise to a functor
s^{*}:\mathsf{p}[y]\rightarrow\mathsf{p}[x]
,
which gives us a substitution operation on types.

Comprehension categories
[
33
]
combine fibrations with a comprehension functor,
which is used to interpret context extension.
We define them as follows.

Definition 7
.

A
comprehension category
is a strictly commuting diagram of functors as indicated below.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{B}^{\rightarrow}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\chi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{p}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{cod}}

We assume that

and that

.
We say that

is

if

is fully faithful.

If we have

,
then we denote

by

.
The fact that

preserves cartesian morphisms
means that it maps each cartesian lift to a pullback square.

Remark 8
.

Throughout this work we only consider full comprehension categories.
If a comprehension category is not full,
then it comes with additional structure,
namely morphisms between types.
Such structure gives an interpretation of subtyping
[
19
,
47
]
,
which usually is not present in Martin-Löf type theory.
Since we are only interested in models without subtyping,
we only consider full comprehension categories.

In addition,
we only look at comprehension categories for which
\mathsf{p}
has a fibrewise terminal object
and for which
\chi
preserves terminal objects.
In such comprehension categories,
terms of type
\overline{x}
in context
x
are the same as
morphisms in
\mathsf{p}[x]
from the terminal object to
x
.

The interpretation of dependent products and sums are based on the idea by Lawvere
that quantifiers are adjoints
[
33
,
39
]
.

Definition 9
.

We say that a full comprehension category

supports
\sum
-types

if for all for all
s:x\rightarrow y
the functor

has a left adjoint
satisfying the usual Beck-Chevalley condition.
We denote this left adjoint by
\sum
.
If
\chi:\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}^{\rightarrow}
supports
\sum
-types,
then we say that these
\sum
-types are

if for all objects
x\in\mathcal{B}
,
\overline{x}\in\mathsf{p}[x]
and
\overline{y}\in\mathsf{p}[x.\overline{x}]

the canonical morphism from
x.\overline{x}.\overline{y}
to

is an isomorphism.

A comprehension category
\chi:\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}^{\rightarrow}
supports
\prod
-types

if for all for all
s:x\rightarrow y
the functor
s^{*}
has a right satisfying the usual Beck-Chevalley condition.

Remark 10
.

There are some important observations to be made about
\Cref
def:quantifiers.
While one generally only considers adjoints to the weakening functors
\pi_{\overline{x}}^{*}
,
we look at adjoints for all morphisms.
As a consequence,
\Cref
def:quantifiers captures not only
\sum
-types,
but also extensional identity types,
which are given by left adjoints to the contraction functors.
In addition,
one only needs to check the Beck-Chevalley condition for
\sum
-types,
because the one for
\prod
-types follows from it
[
57
]
.
Finally,
we require our
\sum
-types to be strong in order to interpret the
\eta
-rule for dependent sums.

Now we construct the realizability model of dependent type theory
[
42
,
51
]
.

4.2
Linear comprehension categories

Next we extend our notion of comprehension category to incorporate the features of linear type theory,
and we define linear comprehension categories.
There are two additional features to such comprehension categories.
The first feature is that
they come with two notion of types,
namely cartesian types and linear types.
We represent these two kinds via two fibrations over the same category.
To incorporate linearity,
we require one of these fibrations to be
symmetric monoidal closed
.

Definition 12
.

Let
\mathsf{q}:\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
be a fibration.
We say that
\mathsf{p}
is a
symmetric monoidal closed fibration

if for each
x\in\mathcal{B}
the fibre
\mathsf{q}[x]
is a symmetric monoidal closed category
and if for each
s:x\rightarrow y
the functor
s^{*}:\mathsf{q}[y]\rightarrow\mathsf{q}[x]
is a symmetric monoidal closed functor.

Note that there are various notions of monoidal fibrations in the literature
[
46
,
58
]

depending on whether the total category or the fibre categories have a monoidal structure.
For our purposes,
it is necessary to require a monoidal structure for the fibre categories.

From a type theoretic point of view,
we can understand symmetric monoidal closed fibrations as follows.
Each fibre category
\mathsf{q}[x]
is seen as the category of types
in some context
x
.
The monoidal structures equip these categories with three type formers:
a unit, a monoidal product, and linear function types,
and thus each
\mathsf{q}[x]
forms a model of linear logic.
Since the functors
s^{*}:\mathsf{q}[y]\rightarrow\mathsf{q}[x]
are symmetric monoidal closed,
the aforementioned type formers are stable under substitution up to isomorphism.

Using linear combinatory algebras,
we construct an example of a monoidal fibration.

Example 13
.

Let

be an LCA.
We define the category

and morphisms between them.

and

.

The unit for the monoidal structure on

4.3

Finally,
we look at type formers in linear comprehension categories,
and in the linear realizability model in particular.
We start with the linear quantifiers,

.

.

Next we look at equalizer types.
We interpret these using fibrewise limits.

Definition 19
.

Let
\mathsf{p}:\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
be a fibration.
We say that
\mathsf{p}
has a
fibrewise equalizers

if each fibre
\mathsf{p}[x]
has equalizers
and if the functors
s^{*}:\mathsf{p}[y]\rightarrow\mathsf{p}[x]
preserve equalizers.
We say that a linear comprehension category supports
equalizer types

if the fibration
\mathsf{q}:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
has fibrewise equalizers.

If
s^{*}
has a left and right adjoint, it preserves limits and colimits, hence it suffices to check that each
\mathsf{p}[x]
has equalizers.

Example 20
.

The linear comprehension category defined in
\Cref
exa:lca-linear-comp-cat supports equalizer types.
Equalizers in the fibre
q[x]
are constructed in the usual way.

The additive connectives of linear logic are treated in the same way.
For instance, the additive conjunction is interpreted via fibrewise binary products.
Since we do not use these connectives in the remainder,
we do not discuss them in detail.

We finish our development by showing that our model supports an impredicative universe of types.
In realizability,
such universes build forth upon the notion of
modest set
,
and the key theorem is that modest sets are equivalent to
partial equivalence relations
.
Note that this theorem holds for BI-algebras,
which are applicative structures that come with a
\mathsf{B}
combinator and an
\mathsf{I}
combinator.
This generality is important in our development,
because it allows us to apply it to both linear and cartesian types in our realizability model.

Definition 21
.

Let
X
be an assembly for a BI-algebra
A
.
We say that
X
is
modest

if we have
x=y
whenever
a\Vdash x
and
a\Vdash y
for some
a
.
A
partial equivalence relation
(PER) is a relation on
A
that is symmetric and transitive.

Theorem 22

(Hyland
[
32
]
)
.

Let
A
be a BI-algebra.
The categories of modest sets over
A
and of partial equivalence relations over
A
are equivalent.

Note that
\Cref
thm:modest-set-equiv has been formalized before for partial combinatory algebras
[
14
]
.
The importance of
\Cref
thm:modest-set-equiv comes from that fact that
we can represent the large collection of modest sets via the small set
of PERs.
Specifically,
every modest set is isomorphic to one induced by a PER.
This equivalence forms the basis of the impredicative universe in our realizability model.

Example 23
.

Given an assembly
X\in\mathsf{Asm}(A_{!})
,
we define the family
\mathcal{U}
over
X
to be the set of partial equivalence relations over
A
.
If
R
is a PER,
then
a\Vdash R
always holds.
Let
t
be a term of type
\mathcal{U}
in context
X
.
Since every PER gives rise to a modest set,

t
induces a cartesian type
\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{C}}\left(t\right)
in context
X
,
and we define the linear
\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(t\right)
in the same way.

The fact that
\mathcal{U}
is an impredicative universe
follows from two observations.
First,
we note that by
\Cref
thm:modest-set-equiv every modest set is isomorphic
to a modest set induced by a PER.
Second,
modest sets are closed under both dependent products and linear dependent products.
Specifically,
if we have assemblies
Y_{x}
and
Z_{y}
for each
x:X

and
y:\int{Y}
,
then their dependent product is modest if each
Z_{y}
is modest.
One can prove a similar statement for linear dependent products.

5
Impredicative encodings

Finally,
we show how one can use impredicative encodings in linear dependent type theory
to construct initial algebras.
Recall that in second-order dependent type theory with an impredicative universe
\mathcal{U}
,
one can encode inductive data types via their recursion principle
[
24
]
.
For example,
if we have some type
A
,
then we define the type of lists over
A
to be

\prod_{X:\mathcal{U}}X\to(A\rightarrow X\to X)\to X

Since we assume
\mathcal{U}
to be an impredicative universe,
this type lives in
\mathcal{U}
.
One can show that this type satisfies the recursion principle and computation rules for lists definitionally.

However,
such an impredicative encoding does not necessarily give rise to an initial algebra
[
23
,
55
]
,
which means that one cannot prove a suitable induction principle for this type.
There are various ways to solve this problem,
and one solution was given by Awodey, Frey, and Speight
[
9
]
.
Their idea is that a suitable refinement of this impredicative encoding satisfies the induction principle.
Specifically, the desired initial algebra can be constructed as a subtype of the given impredicative encoding.
This idea can be applied in a wide variety of settings.
For example, it works for higher inductive types, even without truncation restrictions on the eliminator
[
54
]
. Also, one can construct coinductive types
[
11
]
.

In the remainder of this section,
we adapt the ideas of Awodey, Frey, and Speight to construct initial algebras in linear dependent type theory.
Our focus lies on initial algebras in the category of linear types,
because the methods of Awodey, Frey, and Speight can be used directly
to construct initial algebras in cartesian types.
Throughout this section,
we focus on the type of lists whose type of elements is some closed linear type
A
.
Since we only work within the universe
\mathcal{U}
in the remainder,
we use normal notation instead of
\mathcal{U}
-internal notation,
e.g. we write
\mathop{\mathchoice{\scalebox{2.0}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.6}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.0}{$\sqcap$}}}\displaylimits
instead of
\widehat{\mathop{\mathchoice{\scalebox{2.0}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.6}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.0}{$\sqcap$}}}\displaylimits}
.

Following Girard
[
24
]
,
we start with the following encoding.

\mathsf{List}^{*}(A)\mathrel{:\equiv}\mathop{\mathop{\mathchoice{\scalebox{2.0}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.6}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.0}{$\sqcap$}}}\displaylimits}_{X:\mathcal{U}}\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)\multimap\mathbin{!}(A\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right))\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)

We define the constructor
\mathsf{nil}^{*}:\mathsf{List}^{*}(A)
as follows.

\mathsf{nil}^{*}\mathrel{:\equiv}\lambda{(X:\mathcal{U})(n:\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right))(c:\mathbin{!}(A\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)))}.\,{n}

If we have
a:A
and
l:\mathsf{List}^{*}(A)
,
then
a::^{*}l:\mathsf{List}^{*}(A)
is defined as follows.

a::^{*}l\mathrel{:\equiv}\lambda{(X:\mathcal{U})(n:\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right))(c:\mathbin{!}(A\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)))}.\,{c\>a\>(l\>X\>n\>c)}

To define the recursor,
we assume that we have a type
X:\mathcal{U}
, an element
n:\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)
, and an operation
c:\mathbin{!}(A\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right))
,
and we define
\mathsf{rec}_{X}(n,c,l):\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)
as

\mathsf{rec}_{X}(n,c,l)\mathrel{:\equiv}l\>X\>n\>c

In general, the type
\mathsf{List}^{*}(A)
only is a weakly initial algebra
and not necessarily initial
[
23
,
55
]
.
We obtain the desired initial algebra by taking a suitable subtype,
and for that reason,
we first define algebras and their morphisms.

\mathsf{Alg}\mathrel{:\equiv}\sum_{X:\mathcal{U}}\mathsf{M}(\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right))\times\mathsf{M}(\mathbin{!}(A\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)))

An algebra is given by a type
X:\mathcal{U}

together with cartesian terms of type
\mathsf{M}(\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right))
and
\mathsf{M}(\mathbin{!}(A\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)))
.
Such cartesian terms correspond to linear terms of types
\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)
and
\mathbin{!}(A\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right))
in the empty linear context.
We introduce the following notation:
if we have an algebra
X:\mathsf{Alg}
,
we write
n_{X}:\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)

and
c_{X}:\mathbin{!}(A\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right))

for the structure maps.

Next we define morphisms between algebras.
Given
f:\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(Y\right)
,
we define

\displaystyle\mathsf{IsMor}(X,Y,f)\mathrel{:\equiv}(f\>n_{X}=_{\mathsf{L}}n_{Y})\times\prod(a:A)(x:X).f(c_{X}\>a\>x)=_{\mathsf{L}}c_{Y}\>x\>(f\>y)

\displaystyle\mathsf{Mor}(X,Y)\mathrel{:\equiv}\sum(f:\mathsf{M}(\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(Y\right))).\mathsf{IsMor}((X,n_{X},c_{X}),(Y,n_{Y},c_{Y}),f)

Every cartesian term
f:\mathsf{M}(\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(Y\right))
gives rise to a linear term of type
\mu(f):\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(Y\right)
in the empty context (c.f. the
\mathsf{M}
-
e
rule),
and, to lighten the notation,
we omit the
\mu
.
Thus,
every morphism
f
of algebras gives rise to a linear term
f:\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(Y\right)
in the empty context.
By injectivity and reflection,
the expected diagrams commute.

Now we are ready to define the refined encoding.
Awodey, Frey, and Speight construct the initial algebra using an equalizer,
which gives a subtype of
\mathsf{List}^{*}(A)
.
Although they use
\sum
-types to construct the equalizer,
we refrain from the linear analogue,
namely
\mathop{\mathop{\mathchoice{\scalebox{2.0}{$\sqsubset$}}{\scalebox{1.6}{$\sqsubset$}}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\sqsubset$}}{\scalebox{1.0}{$\sqsubset$}}}\displaylimits}
-types.
The type
A
in
\mathop{\mathop{\mathchoice{\scalebox{2.0}{$\sqsubset$}}{\scalebox{1.6}{$\sqsubset$}}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\sqsubset$}}{\scalebox{1.0}{$\sqsubset$}}}\displaylimits}_{x:A}B\>x
is required to be cartesian,
so we cannot use
\mathop{\mathop{\mathchoice{\scalebox{2.0}{$\sqsubset$}}{\scalebox{1.6}{$\sqsubset$}}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\sqsubset$}}{\scalebox{1.0}{$\sqsubset$}}}\displaylimits}
-types to construct subtypes of linear types.
This is why we included equalizers in
\Cref
sec:dltt.

The necessary morphisms are defined as follows.

\displaystyle\varphi,\psi:\mathsf{List}^{*}(A)\multimap\mathop{\mathop{\mathchoice{\scalebox{2.0}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.6}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.0}{$\sqcap$}}}\displaylimits}_{(X,n_{X},c_{X}):\mathsf{Alg}}\mathop{\mathop{\mathchoice{\scalebox{2.0}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.6}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.0}{$\sqcap$}}}\displaylimits}_{(Y,n_{Y},c_{Y}):\mathsf{Alg}}\mathop{\mathop{\mathchoice{\scalebox{2.0}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.6}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\sqcap$}}{\scalebox{1.0}{$\sqcap$}}}\displaylimits}_{(f,p,q):\mathsf{Mor}(X,Y)}\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(Y\right)

\displaystyle\varphi\mathrel{:\equiv}\lambda l\,X\,n_{X}\,c_{X}\,Y\,n_{Y}\,c_{Y}\,f\,p\,q.f(\mathsf{rec}_{X}(n_{X},c_{X},l))

\displaystyle\psi\mathrel{:\equiv}\lambda l\,X\,n_{X}\,c_{X}\,Y\,n_{Y}\,c_{Y}\,f\,p\,q.\mathsf{rec}_{Y}(n_{Y},c_{Y},l)

Note that in the definition of
\varphi
,
the morphism
f
refers to the corresponding linear term of type
\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(X\right)\multimap\mathsf{El}_{\mathsf{L}}\left(Y\right)
in the empty linear context.

We define the type
\mathsf{List}(A)
to be
\mathsf{Eq}(\varphi,\psi)
.
Now we prove that
\mathsf{List}(A)
is an initial algebra,
and we start by showing that that
\mathsf{List}(A)
is an algebra.
To do so,
we prove that
\mathsf{nil}^{*}
and
::^{*}
give rise to operations on
\mathsf{List}(A)
,
which is stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 24
.

We have
\varphi(\mathsf{nil}^{*})=\psi(\mathsf{nil}^{*})
.
In addition,
we have
\varphi(a::^{*}l)=\psi(a::^{*}l)

for all
a:X
and
l:\mathsf{List}(A)
.

Proof.

To show that
\varphi(\mathsf{nil}^{*})=\psi(\mathsf{nil}^{*})
,
we first note that

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathsf{nil}^{*})=f(\mathsf{rec}_{X}(n_{X},c_{X},\mathsf{nil}^{*}))=f\>n_{X}

\displaystyle\psi(\mathsf{nil}^{*})=\mathsf{rec}_{Y}(n_{Y},c_{Y},\mathsf{nil}^{*})=n_{Y}

Since
f
is a morphism,
it is indeed the case that
f\>n_{X}=n_{Y}
by reflection and injectivity.

For the other statement, we assume that

\varphi\>l=f(\mathsf{rec}_{X}(n_{X},c_{X},l))=\mathsf{rec}_{Y}(n_{Y},c_{Y},l)=\psi\>l.

Now we have

\displaystyle\varphi(a::^{*}l)=f(\mathsf{rec}_{X}(n_{X},c_{X},a::^{*}l))=f(c_{X}\>a\>(\mathsf{rec}_{X}(n_{X},c_{X},l)))

\displaystyle\psi(a::^{*}l)=\mathsf{rec}_{Y}(n_{Y},c_{Y},a::^{*}l)=c_{Y}\>a\>(\mathsf{rec}_{Y}(n_{Y},c_{Y},l))=c_{Y}\>a\>(f(\mathsf{rec}_{X}(n_{X},c_{X},l)))

From injectivity,
it follows that
\varphi(a::^{*}l)=_{\mathsf{L}}\psi(a::^{*}l)

since
f
is a morphism.
∎

By
\Cref
lem:list-alg,
we have an element
\mathsf{nil}:\mathsf{List}(A)

and a function sending
a:A
and
l:\mathsf{List}(A)

to
a::l:\mathsf{List}(A)
.
From this
we get an algebra
\mathsf{List}(A)

whose carrier is
\mathsf{List}(A)

and whose algebra map is given by
\mathsf{nil}
and
::
.
We conclude this section by proving that
\mathsf{List}(A)
is initial.

Theorem 25
.

The algebra
\mathsf{List}(A)
is initial.

Proof.

Given an algebra
X
,
we define
f
to be
\lambda(l:\mathsf{List}(A)).\mathsf{rec}_{X}(n_{X},c_{X},l)
.
Note that

\displaystyle\mathsf{rec}_{X}(n_{X},c_{X},\pi_{1}\>\mathsf{nil})=\mathsf{rec}_{X}(n_{X},c_{X},\mathsf{nil}^{*})=n_{X}

\displaystyle\mathsf{rec}_{X}(n_{X},c_{X},\pi_{1}\>(a::l))=\mathsf{rec}_{X}(n_{X},c_{X},a::^{*}l)=c_{X}\>a\>(\mathsf{rec}_{X}(n_{X},c_{X},l))

To prove that the map
f
is unique,
it suffices to show that
\mathsf{rec}_{\mathsf{List}(A)}(\mathsf{nil},::,l)=l

for each
l:\mathsf{List}(A)
.
By function extensionality,
we need to show for all
X
,
n_{X}
, and
c_{X}
that

\mathsf{rec}_{\mathsf{List}(A)}(\mathsf{nil},::,l)\>X\>n_{X}\>c_{X}=l\>X\>n_{X}\>c_{X}.

By definition,
we have
l\>X\>n_{X}\>c_{X}=\mathsf{rec}_{X}(n_{X},c_{X},l)

and that

\mathsf{rec}_{\mathsf{List}(A)}(\mathsf{nil},::,l)\>X\>n_{X}\>c_{X}=\mathsf{rec}_{X}(n_{X},c_{X},\mathsf{rec}_{\mathsf{List}(A)}(\mathsf{nil},::,l)).

It suffices to show that

\mathsf{rec}_{X}(n_{X},c_{X},\mathsf{rec}_{\mathsf{List}(A)}(\mathsf{nil},::,l))=\mathsf{rec}_{X}(n_{X},c_{X},l).

Since
\lambda l.\mathsf{rec}_{X}(n_{X},c_{X},l)
is a morphism,
the theorem follows from the fact that
l:\mathsf{List}(A)
.
∎

6
Related work

Linear combinatory algebras were first introduced in a series of lectures given by Abramsky
[
3
]
; the first published account is
[
1
]
, wherein there are many examples. The first linear realizability model is due to Abramsky and Lenisa
[
2
]
, who consider categories of partial equivalence relations over LCAs in order to obtain full completeness results for various typed
\lambda
-calculi. Hoshino
[
30
]
offered the first in-depth study of assemblies and modest sets over (relational) linear combinatory algebras. Since then, there has been a growing interest in “substructural” combinatory algebras and their associated categorical structures, for example: ribbon combinatory algebras
[
29
,
28
]
and planar combinatory algebras
[
64
,
65
,
66
]
. Kuzmin, Nester, Reimaa and Speight
[
38
]
formulate a quite general theory of combinatory completeness using structured multicategories.
Recently, monadic combinatory algebras have been studied for effectful realizability
[
17
,
15
,
16
]
.

Various treatments of linear dependent type theory are given throughout the literature.
The first development of LDTT was by Cervesato and Pfenning
[
12
,
13
]
,
but they only considered a subset of the desired type formers
[
13
]
.
Krishnaswami, Pradic, and Benton gave a version of LDTT
that contains all connectives from linear logic
[
37
]
.
Similar treatments were given by Vákár
[
68
]

and by Lundfall
[
40
]
,
with some differences.
Compared to Krishnaswami-Pradic-Benton,
Lundfall gives different rules for identity types and the modality,
and Vákár has a slightly different treatment of
\sum
-types.
We use a similar syntax as Krishnaswami, Pradic, and Benton.
The key differences are indicated in
\Cref
sec:dltt:
our syntax comes with equalizer types, an injectivity axiom for the modality
\mathsf{M}
, and an impredicative universe of types.

Both Lundfall
[
40
]
and Vákár
[
68
]
give a notion of categorical model for LDTT.
Lundfall’s treatment is based on comprehension categories,
whereas Vákár uses indexed categories instead.
In addition,
Lundfall represents the modality of linear logic via an adjunction
whereas Vákár uses a comonad.
We base our semantics on Lundfall’s notion of linear comprehension category.
Neither Lundfall nor Vákár consider a realizability model.
Krishnaswami, Pradic, and Benton
[
37
]
give semantics to their calculus using partial equivalance relations,
but they do not provide a general development of realizability models.

Quantitative type theory
[
8
,
44
,
48
]

is a generalization of linear dependent type theory
that refines the linearity condition by allowing variables to
appear in quantities indicated by some semiring.
Atkey
[
8
]
constructed a model of quantitative type theory using a quantitative combinatory algebra.
The resulting model shows resemblance to ours,
a crucial difference being that our model is impredicative.
Since Atkey interprets types as assemblies indexed by a set rather than an assembly,
there cannot be an impredicative universe in that model
[
50
]
.
Hence, the impredicative encoding in
\Cref
sec:encodings can only be interpreted in our model.

The method of refining impredicative encodings that we use in
\Cref
sec:encodings is based on the work by Awodey, Frey, and Speight
[
9
]
, which treats (higher) inductive types.
The truncation restriction on eliminators is lifted in
[
54
]
.
The method has also been extended to coinductive types
[
11
]
.
Another way to fix impredicative encodings is via parametricity.
By assuming internal parametricity,
initiality for impredicative encodings can be proven
[
7
,
71
,
72
]
.
Such axioms are validated by PER models of type theory
[
36
]
.
One can use impredicative encodings in higher-order separation logic to define inductive predicates
[
35
]
.
Since they use proof-irrelevant predicates,
they do not need to refine their encodings.

7
Conclusion

In this paper,
we defined a realizability model of linear dependent type theory with an impredicative universe for cartesian and linear types, all parametrized by a linear combinatory algebra.
We also showed how the method of Awodey, Frey and Speight
[
9
]
transfers to impredicative encodings of linear types, constructing the initial algebra of linear lists.

There are several ways to further develop this work.
One can further generalize the development in
\Cref
sec:encodings
by considering arbitrary inductive types rather than only lists.
We conjecture that the same ideas can be used.
In addition,
we use extensionality throughout this paper,
and our injectivity axiom is tailored for this setting.
One question is how to generalise our work to intensional versions of linear dependent type theory.
