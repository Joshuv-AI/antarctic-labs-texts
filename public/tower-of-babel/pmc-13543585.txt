Clinical characteristics of the reduced bone mineral density subgroup among adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus: a retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Song Lu, Bo Wan, Chun-sheng Qian, Jia-jia Shao, Qing-shan Fu, Chang-ma Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSkeletal fragility in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is incompletely represented by areal bone mineral density (BMD). We defined an operational reduced-BMD subgroup using routine hospital information-system data and examined whether routinely available clinical variables showed incremental associations and whether complex classifiers improved internal discrimination beyond age and sex.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cross-sectional analysis included 329 de-identified analytical records from adults with T2DM who underwent routine dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. The operational reduced-BMD subgroup was defined by the lowest T-score among the lumbar spine L1-L4, femoral neck, and total hip: records with at least one site-specific T-score ≤ − 1.0 were classified into the subgroup, and a stricter < -1.0 definition was examined as a boundary sensitivity analysis. Because menopausal status and Z-scores were unavailable, this rule was treated as an operational subgroup definition rather than a universal clinical diagnosis. Descriptive analyses, deliberately parsimonious Jeffreys-prior bias-reduced logistic models, fixed-unit age sensitivity analyses, and repeated nested internal classification were performed.ResultsTwenty-six records (7.9%) met the primary operational definition and 22 met the stricter definition; no record met a T-score ≤ − 2.5 threshold at the retained primary sites. Twenty-five of the 26 records in the reduced-BMD subgroup were from women. In the prespecified full-cohort model, age had an odds ratio (OR) of 5.39 per within-sample standard deviation (95% profile-likelihood confidence interval [CI], 2.71–12.15), and the female-sex estimate was large but imprecise (OR, 34.47; 95% CI, 8.29-321.11). On a common five-year scale, the age OR was 2.33 (95% CI, 1.65–3.50) in the full cohort and 2.29 (95% CI, 1.60–3.47) among records aged ≥ 50 years. The fixed age-sex classifier had a median internally validated ROC AUC of 0.921 (range, 0.915–0.923), decreasing to 0.901 (range, 0.897–0.904) in the age ≥ 50 subset. Among the tested algorithms and prespecified features, no complex classifier materially exceeded the fixed age-sex benchmark.ConclusionsIn this selected single-center record-level dataset, the operational reduced-BMD subgroup was characterized principally by older age and marked female predominance. Restriction to age ≥ 50 years reduced apparent discrimination because of narrower case mix, while the age association remained similar on a fixed five-year scale. The additional tested variables did not provide precisely estimated incremental associations in the prespecified age-sex-plus-one-variable models, and the tested complex classifiers did not materially improve internal discrimination over the age-sex benchmark. All classification estimates were internally validated only, and the findings do not support causal, diagnostic, prognostic, deployment, or treatment claims.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10346-6.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Bone mineral density, Reduced BMD, Bias-reduced logistic regression, Nested cross-validation, Internal validation

Introduction

Diabetes is a major and expanding global health burden. Contemporary estimates indicate that hundreds of millions of adults are living with diabetes, and the number is expected to rise substantially as populations age and metabolic risk factors accumulate [1, 2]. Musculoskeletal consequences deserve particular attention because osteoporosis, falls, and fragility fractures contribute to pain, disability, loss of independence, health-care use, and excess mortality. In people with diabetes, post-fracture recovery may be complicated by multimorbidity, reduced physical reserve, microvascular disease, impaired wound healing, and a high competing burden of cardiovascular and renal disease. Recent clinical reviews therefore regard skeletal health as an integral, but still under-recognized, component of long-term diabetes care [3, 4].

The relationship between T2DM and skeletal fragility is not adequately described by the assumption that lower areal BMD necessarily explains fracture vulnerability. Individuals with T2DM may experience fractures despite BMD values that are normal or higher than those of people without diabetes. Imaging studies and contemporary reviews have implicated altered cortical and trabecular microarchitecture, impaired bone material properties, unfavorable geometry, low bone turnover, microvascular complications, falls, sarcopenia, and treatment-related effects [3–6]. DXA-derived BMD consequently remains clinically important, but it represents only one component of a broader diabetic skeletal phenotype. This distinction is essential when a study uses BMD as its endpoint: the result can describe mineral-density status without measuring the full biological construct of bone quality or the future probability of fracture.

Metabolic and tissue-level mechanisms provide biologically plausible context, but they should not be conflated with measurements available in a retrospective hospital dataset. Advanced glycation and glycoxidation end products can accumulate in collagen-rich bone matrix, alter collagen cross-linking, and reduce tissue toughness [7–9]. Human bone-expression studies have also described dysregulation of Wnt-pathway genes, osteocalcin, and extracellular-matrix transcripts in T2DM and diabetic Charcot arthropathy [10]. Such observations support the plausibility of impaired osteoblast-lineage regulation and altered matrix quality. However, they do not permit mechanistic attribution in a cohort without bone tissue, oxidative-stress assays, histomorphometry, or direct material-strength testing.

Bone-turnover markers may offer complementary information about the dynamic balance between formation and resorption. A recent meta-analysis reported an overall pattern of reduced turnover in T2DM, while individual studies have shown substantial variation in P1NP, osteocalcin, beta-CTX, and related markers [11]. Low turnover has been linked to insulin sensitivity, bone microarchitecture, adiposity, kidney function, and glycemic characteristics in different clinical settings [12–19]. The most recent international consensus continues to recognize P1NP and beta-CTX as reference markers for osteoporosis assessment and treatment monitoring, while emphasizing pre-analytical variation, renal function, treatment exposure, and the need for clinically appropriate interpretation [20]. Thus, a group difference in a marker cannot automatically be interpreted as an independent cause of reduced BMD, especially in a small cross-sectional sample with pronounced demographic imbalance.

The muscle-bone unit is another clinically relevant dimension. Reduced muscle mass and strength, impaired mobility, falls, and osteosarcopenia may compound skeletal vulnerability through shared mechanical loading, endocrine signaling, physical inactivity, and age-related functional decline. Wnt/beta-catenin signaling and other pathways provide plausible biological links, while clinical reviews increasingly advocate integrated assessment of osteoporosis, sarcopenia, falls, and fracture risk [21–25]. Nevertheless, age, sex, muscle quantity, muscle strength, gait performance, comorbidity, and treatment status are tightly correlated. A small number of outcome events cannot reliably disentangle these domains in a single high-dimensional model.

Technically valid interpretation of DXA depends on skeletal site, reference data, patient age and sex, scan quality, and clinical context. Contemporary official positions and practice guidance emphasize standardized reporting, appropriate vertebral exclusion, awareness of artifacts, and age- and sex-specific interpretation [26–29]. T-scores are generally applied to postmenopausal women and men aged 50 years or older, whereas Z-scores are generally preferred in younger men and premenopausal women. Fracture-risk assessment and treatment decisions additionally require clinical risk factors and should not be reduced to one BMD threshold [30, 31].

Reduced BMD was selected as the primary endpoint in the present study because finalized T-scores at the lumbar spine, femoral neck, and total hip were directly available in the hospital information-system export and could be reconstructed by an explicit rule. The study did not prospectively adjudicate incident fracture, measure trabecular bone score, quantify cortical porosity, assess material strength, or evaluate longitudinal BMD change. Retrospective fracture-history variables were available but were not treated as incident outcomes. The endpoint therefore captures one reproducible and clinically accessible component of diabetic skeletal health while leaving fracture susceptibility and bone quality outside the scope of inference.

The corrected analysis addressed four clinical objectives: first, to define the operational reduced-BMD subgroup transparently from site-specific DXA fields; second, to describe its demographic, muscle, biochemical, renal, vascular, fracture-history, and treatment characteristics; third, to estimate deliberately parsimonious bias-reduced associations under sparse events; and fourth, to compare complex internally validated classifiers with an interpretable age-sex benchmark. These objectives were prespecified to match the information content of the corrected clinical dataset and to avoid causal or mechanistic claims unsupported by direct measurements.

Methods

Study design, setting, and source records

This was a single-center retrospective cross-sectional analysis of routinely collected clinical data from the Department of Orthopedics (Sports Medicine Department), The Third People’s Hospital of Hefei, China. Source records covered the years 2022–2024. The final de-identified export contained 329 analytical records labeled as adults with a clinician-recorded diagnosis of T2DM who had undergone a routine clinical DXA examination and had nonmissing T-scores at the lumbar spine L1-L4, femoral neck, and total hip. The T2DM diagnosis was accepted as recorded in the clinical system and was not independently re-adjudicated against research diagnostic criteria.

Participants were not prospectively recruited, no study-specific intervention was administered, and no prospective follow-up was undertaken. The export contained only the final analytical records; the total number screened before export and detailed exclusion reasons were not retained. A stable patient-level hospital identifier was not present in the research file. No exact duplicate rows or duplicate composite signatures based on age, sex, primary-site T-scores, BMI, diabetes duration, HbA1c, fasting glucose, ASMI, and grip strength were detected, but patient-level duplicate encounters could not be definitively excluded beyond the exported record structure. The analysis therefore describes the available analytical records and assumes record-level independence as represented by the source export; this limitation is stated explicitly.

The source file did not retain a single harmonized index date or exact day-level intervals between DXA, laboratory tests, muscle-function assessments, carotid ultrasonography, fracture-history recording, and medication recording. These variables were collected within the same routine clinical period but could not be synchronized retrospectively to a prespecified time window. The study consequently evaluates contemporaneous record-level associations rather than incidence, prognosis, treatment response, or future fracture risk.

Ethics approval and consent arrangements

The study was reviewed and approved by the Medical Research Ethics Review Committee of The Third People’s Hospital of Hefei (Ethics Approval No. 2026LLWL032). The approval document addressed the scientific, informed-consent, and ethical aspects of the work and granted approval for submission and publication. The retrospective analysis used de-identified routinely collected clinical data and involved no additional examination, intervention, biological sampling, or participant contact. The consent arrangements for this retrospective study were reviewed and approved by the committee. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable institutional and national ethical requirements. No consent waiver beyond that documented by the approving committee is claimed.

Data reconstruction, mapping audit, and record-level quality control

The analytical master dataset was rebuilt from the raw spreadsheet by reconciling two header rows and mapping exact source-column names to analysis variables (Supplementary Table S2). The earlier cleaned file was not used because a formal mapping audit showed that several analytical labels had been assigned to unrelated raw columns, including HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, and triglycerides. All regression, classification, figures, and tables derived from that file were discarded and recalculated from the reconstructed dataset.

Quality control comprised row-count reconciliation, duplicate-row checks, numeric coercion, missingness assessment, inspection of observed categorical values, review of physiological plausibility, and cross-field consistency checks. One impossible BMI value of 209.78 kg/m2 was set to missing rather than corrected by inference. The three primary T-score fields were complete. A source field labeled as an osteoporosis diagnosis contained only binary values and exactly matched the reconstructed operational reduced-BMD endpoint in all 329 records. Because this exact identity was compatible with an exported rule-based flag or a mislabeled derived field, it was not used as independent clinical validation. The analysis relied on the explicit site-specific T-score rule, and the source-field anomaly was retained as a data-dictionary limitation.

The final analysis dictionary used the following recorded units: age in years; BMI in kg/m2; diabetes duration in years; HbA1c in percent; fasting plasma glucose in mmol/L; ASMI in kg/m2; grip strength in kg; gait speed in m/s; Timed Up and Go in seconds; P1NP in micrograms/L; osteocalcin in ng/mL; beta-CTX in ng/mL; TRAP-5b in U/L; eGFR in mL/min/1.73 m2; cystatin C in mg/L; and carotid intima-media thickness in mm. The research export did not retain device manufacturers, instrument models, assay platforms, reagent lots, operator identifiers, reference ranges, detailed testing protocols, or image-level adjudication records. These values were therefore analyzed as finalized clinical-report fields rather than independently standardized research measurements.

DXA records and operational outcome definition

DXA examinations were performed as part of routine care by the hospital bone-densitometry service. The de-identified export retained finalized site-specific T-scores but not the manufacturer, exact scanner model, software version, reference database, image-level quality annotations, excluded-vertebra decisions, phantom records, or numerical precision error. The research analysis used the reported values without assigning an unverified platform or retrospectively modifying image-level measurements.

The primary skeletal sites were the posteroanterior lumbar spine L1-L4, femoral neck, and total hip. The one-third radius was available for some records but was not part of the recorded primary three-site rule. Decisions concerning artifacts, arthroplasty, internal fixation, vertebral exclusion, or selection of the contralateral hip were made during routine clinical interpretation and were not separately available for research re-adjudication. Least significant change was not evaluated because longitudinal BMD change was outside the study design.

The primary operational reduced-BMD subgroup was defined using the lowest T-score among the lumbar spine L1-L4, femoral neck, and total hip. Records with at least one site-specific T-score ≤ − 1.0 were classified into the reduced-BMD subgroup, whereas records with T-scores > − 1.0 at all three sites were classified as having normal BMD. Because the exact boundary of − 1.0 may be categorized as normal under conventional clinical descriptions, a stricter < − 1.0 definition was prespecified as a boundary sensitivity analysis. No record met a T-score ≤ − 2.5 threshold at the retained primary sites. Menopausal status and Z-scores were unavailable; accordingly, neither threshold was interpreted as a universal clinical diagnosis. An additional sensitivity analysis restricted the dataset to records aged ≥ 50 years, an age range in which T-score interpretation is more directly aligned with contemporary densitometry guidance, while acknowledging that menopausal status remained unavailable.

Clinical variables and ascertainment boundaries

The demographic and metabolic domains included age, sex, BMI, diabetes duration, HbA1c, and fasting plasma glucose. The muscle and function domain included ASMI, grip strength, gait speed, Timed Up and Go performance, and a binary sarcopenia field as recorded in the clinical export. Because the export did not preserve the bioimpedance or DXA body-composition device, grip dynamometer, gait-course length, Timed Up and Go protocol, or diagnostic criteria used to create the sarcopenia field, these measurements were treated as routine recorded values rather than harmonized research phenotypes.

The bone-marker domain included P1NP, osteocalcin, beta-CTX, and TRAP-5b. The renal domain included cystatin C and eGFR, and the vascular domain included carotid intima-media thickness. Exact assay platforms, blood-sampling times, fasting status for bone markers, the eGFR equation, ultrasound device, arterial segment, and reader protocol were not retained. Consequently, these variables were used to describe the clinical record and to test limited incremental association, not to establish standardized biomarker thresholds.

Fracture and treatment fields included recorded prior fragility fracture, recorded vertebral compression fracture, bisphosphonate use, calcium supplementation, and vitamin D supplementation. The export did not preserve imaging modality, fracture date, trauma mechanism, morphometric versus clinical vertebral-fracture definition, bisphosphonate compound, treatment duration, adherence, or timing relative to DXA. FRAX probabilities and treatment variables were not used as classifier features because they could encode consequences of BMD assessment, referral, or treatment indication. The discordance between 33 recorded vertebral compression fractures, 12 recorded fragility fractures, 45 bisphosphonate users, and the absence of a primary-site T-score ≤ − 2.5 was therefore interpreted as a reason for caution and future source-document audit rather than as evidence that the fields were interchangeable.

Missing data and descriptive analysis

Variable-specific availability was reported in Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1. Descriptive comparisons used available observations for each variable. Continuous variables were summarized as mean and standard deviation when approximately symmetric and as median and interquartile range when skewed. Categorical variables were summarized as number and percentage. Between-group comparisons used Welch t tests or Mann-Whitney tests for continuous variables and Fisher exact tests for categorical variables when cell counts were small. Standardized mean differences were reported to describe the magnitude of group separation.

These comparisons were exploratory and descriptive. Nominal P values were not adjusted for multiple comparisons, were not used as a stepwise screening rule, and were not interpreted as independent effects. Missing continuous values were not imputed for descriptive statistics or bias-reduced regression. Each regression used the complete cases available for that prespecified model. In the classification analysis, median imputation was performed exclusively within the training portion of each resample to prevent information leakage.

Parsimonious Jeffreys-prior bias-reduced logistic regression

Only 26 records met the primary operational endpoint, and 25 of these 26 reduced-BMD records were from women. Standard maximum-likelihood logistic regression was therefore vulnerable to sparse-data bias, unstable coefficients, and separation. Bias-reduced estimates were obtained by maximizing the Jeffreys-prior penalized likelihood through adjusted-score Fisher scoring with step halving. Profile penalized-likelihood confidence intervals and penalized likelihood-ratio tests were calculated. Convergence required both a maximum absolute adjusted score < 1 × 10 − 7 and a maximum parameter step < 1 × 10 − 7. Linear-programming feasibility was used to assess complete separation; sparse-cell near-separation was additionally judged from sex-by-outcome cell counts, coefficient magnitude, and profile-likelihood interval width.

Candidate variables were selected a priori to represent distinct, routinely available clinical domains rather than on the basis of univariate P values. The benchmark association model included age and sex. Nine separate domain extensions added one variable at a time: ASMI, grip strength, cystatin C, eGFR, carotid intima-media thickness, P1NP, beta-CTX, HbA1c, or fasting plasma glucose. No stepwise selection, automated screening, interaction search, or high-dimensional multivariable model was undertaken. The model structure was deliberately restricted because 26 events could not support simultaneous estimation of the correlated muscle, metabolic, renal, vascular, and bone-marker domains.

No formal confirmatory sample-size calculation was possible because the research export represented the available eligible records rather than a prospectively recruited cohort. The information content was instead judged from the 26 outcome events, the one-event male cell, the number of parameters in each prespecified model, and the width of profile-likelihood intervals. Accordingly, the regression analyses were designed for estimation and hypothesis generation, not for definitive exclusion of clinically meaningful associations. Bias reduction was used to reduce small-sample and separation bias; it was not interpreted as a substitute for additional events or independent validation.

Continuous variables were standardized within each complete-case model for the prespecified Table 2 estimates. However, an odds ratio per analysis-specific standard deviation cannot be compared directly across samples when the standard deviation changes. For the full-cohort versus age ≥ 50 sensitivity comparison, the age coefficient and profile-likelihood limits were therefore additionally expressed per a fixed five-year increment. The same model structures were repeated for the stricter T-score < -1.0 endpoint. No formal multiplicity-adjusted confirmatory inference was claimed; estimates were interpreted from direction, precision, and consistency across prespecified sensitivity analyses.

Exploratory internal classification analysis

The classification analysis assessed contemporaneous discrimination between the two operational BMD groups. It did not predict future fracture, BMD decline, mortality, or treatment response. A prespecified non-leakage feature set included age, sex, BMI, diabetes duration, HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose, ASMI, grip strength, cystatin C, and carotid intima-media thickness. FRAX probabilities were excluded because they contain strong demographic and potentially BMD-related information. Bisphosphonate use was excluded because treatment may follow recognition of low BMD. Gait speed, Timed Up and Go, sarcopenia, P1NP, beta-CTX, and eGFR were not included in the primary classifier set to limit dimensionality and redundancy under only 26 events. The conclusion regarding model complexity therefore applies only to this prespecified corrected-analysis feature set.

Internal validation used five stratified outer folds repeated five times, yielding 25 held-out assessments. Three-fold stratified inner cross-validation selected hyperparameters. Median imputation, standardization where required, and tuning were performed exclusively within the training portion of each resample. A fixed random seed of 20,260,718 was used. The benchmark was a fixed L2-regularized logistic model using age and sex only, with C = 1.0 after standardization. Complex classifiers comprised class-balanced penalized logistic regression, a class-balanced random forest, k-nearest neighbors, and Gaussian naive Bayes. The complete feature definitions, tuning grids, and selected hyperparameter frequencies are provided in Supplementary Tables S4A-S4C.

For each repeat, the five held-out folds were pooled to obtain one complete set of out-of-fold predictions. ROC AUC, precision-recall AUC, Brier score, Brier skill relative to a prevalence-only prediction, calibration intercept, and calibration slope were calculated separately within each repeat. Table 3 reports the median and observed minimum-maximum across five repeats; these ranges describe resampling variation within one dataset and are not confidence intervals. Class weighting differed across model families. Probability calibration and Brier comparisons were therefore treated as descriptive rather than formal evidence of model superiority. No clinical threshold analysis, decision-curve analysis, best-model declaration, or external validation was performed. The fixed age-sex classifier was additionally rerun in the age ≥ 50 subset using the same repeated resampling logic.

Software, reproducibility, and reporting

Final numerical analyses were implemented in Python. Data handling used pandas and NumPy; numerical procedures for bias-reduced estimation and calibration calculations used SciPy and statsmodels-compatible routines; and repeated nested classification used scikit-learn pipelines. A fixed random seed of 20,260,718 was used for the classification analysis. The complete model structures, feature definitions, tuning grids, convergence diagnostics, and repeat-level performance summaries are reported in the manuscript and Supplementary Materials. No participant-level clinical records are included in the submission files.

The study is reported as an exploratory retrospective cross-sectional analysis of routinely collected record-level data. The clinical association component is not a causal model, and the classification component is not a deployable clinical prediction model. Reporting distinguishes prespecified analyses, boundary and age-restricted sensitivity analyses, train-fold-only preprocessing, internal validation, unavailable measurement metadata, and conclusions that cannot be supported by the source records.

Results

Analytical records and endpoint reconstruction

The reconstructed dataset contained 329 analytical records from adults with T2DM (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S1). Twenty-six records (7.9%) had a T-score ≤ − 1.0 at one or more primary sites, and 303 (92.1%) had T-scores > − 1.0 at all three sites. Across all 329 records, no retained primary-site T-score was ≤ − 2.5. Eleven records met the operational threshold at the lumbar spine, 16 at the femoral neck, and 13 at the total hip; these site counts were non-mutually exclusive (Supplementary Figure S1), and two records met the threshold at all three sites. Five additional records had radius-only T-scores ≤ − 1.0, but the radius was not part of the primary rule. Under the stricter T-score < − 1.0 definition, 22 records were classified as reduced BMD (Supplementary Figure S2). All 26 primary-definition reduced-BMD records occurred at age ≥ 50 years; 25 were from women and one was from a man.

Descriptive clinical characteristics of the reduced-BMD subgroup

The reduced-BMD group was older and included a markedly higher proportion of women (Table 1). It also had lower BMI and ASMI, weaker grip strength, slower gait speed, longer Timed Up and Go performance, and a higher recorded prevalence of sarcopenia. P1NP, osteocalcin, beta-CTX, and TRAP-5b were higher in the reduced-BMD group. eGFR was lower, cystatin C was higher, and carotid intima-media thickness was greater. These differences were large for several variables, as reflected by standardized mean differences, but they were not interpreted as independent effects because the two BMD groups differed profoundly in age and sex.

Recorded vertebral compression fracture occurred in 6/26 (23.1%) reduced-BMD records and 27/303 (8.9%) normal-BMD records. Bisphosphonate use was recorded in 15/26 (57.7%) and 30/303 (9.9%), respectively. In contrast, the recorded prior-fragility-fracture field was positive in only 12 records overall. The definitions, chronology, imaging adjudication, and overlap of these fields were not available. The fracture and treatment differences were therefore considered susceptible to referral selection, prior clinical recognition, treatment-by-indication, and inconsistent source coding.

Fixed-unit age sensitivity analysis

The age ≥ 50 subset contained 273 records and retained all 26 primary events. The age standard deviation was 9.97 years in the full cohort and 7.20 years in the restricted subset; therefore, the two analysis-specific one-standard-deviation odds ratios were not directly comparable. On the common five-year scale, the age OR was 2.33 (95% CI, 1.65–3.50) in the full cohort and 2.29 (95% CI, 1.60–3.47) in the age ≥ 50 subset. The female-sex estimate remained large and imprecise in the restricted subset (OR, 34.12; 95% CI, 8.22-317.67). Full fixed-unit results are provided in Supplementary Table S3D.

These results distinguish association from discrimination. Removal of 56 younger event-free records narrowed the age distribution and reduced the contrast between outcome groups, but it did not materially reduce the age slope when age was measured on the same scale.

Repeated nested internal classification

Across five outer repeats, the fixed L2-regularized age-sex logistic benchmark produced a median ROC AUC of 0.921 (range, 0.915–0.923), median precision-recall AUC of 0.465 (range, 0.427–0.512), and median Brier score of 0.052 (range, 0.051–0.053) (Table 3; Fig. 3; Supplementary Figure S3). These values were calculated from pooled out-of-fold predictions within the same dataset and do not constitute external validation or confidence intervals.

When the fixed age-sex classifier was repeated in records aged ≥ 50 years, the median ROC AUC was 0.901 (range, 0.897–0.904). Discussion

The principal finding was that the operational reduced-BMD subgroup in this selected T2DM/DXA dataset was dominated by older age and marked female predominance. Descriptive differences also involved muscle quantity and function, bone-turnover markers, renal and vascular indices, vertebral-compression-fracture history, and anti-osteoporosis treatment. These accompanying features are clinically recognizable, but the cross-sectional design, small number of events, pronounced demographic imbalance, incomplete measurement metadata, and selected orthopedic setting prevent causal interpretation.

The fixed-unit age analysis materially changes how the sensitivity result should be understood. In the prespecified models, age was standardized separately within the full and restricted samples. The full-cohort standard deviation was 9.97 years, whereas the age ≥ 50 standard deviation was 7.20 years. Directly comparing OR 5.39 with OR 3.29 would therefore compare different age increments. Once both coefficients were expressed per five years, the ORs were 2.33 and 2.29, showing little change in the association slope. In contrast, the ROC AUC decreased from 0.921 to 0.901 after the younger event-free records were removed. The restriction therefore reduced discrimination through narrower case mix rather than materially weakening the age coefficient. This distinction between association and discrimination is important in any study that compares standardized effects across populations with different predictor distributions.

The sex estimate requires equal restraint. Twenty-five of the 26 records in the reduced-BMD subgroup were from women, leaving only one male record. Jeffreys-prior bias reduction produced a finite estimate and formal feasibility testing did not identify complete separation, but penalization cannot replace information that would have been provided by additional male outcome records. The wide profile-likelihood interval is direct evidence that the magnitude is unstable. The defensible conclusion is marked female predominance in this sample, not a precise or transportable risk multiple. Menopausal status was unavailable, so the study also could not separate biological sex, menopause, age, and referral patterns.

The marked descriptive differences in ASMI, grip strength, gait speed, Timed Up and Go performance, and recorded sarcopenia are compatible with contemporary concepts of muscle-bone crosstalk and osteosarcopenia. Exercise and physical activity remain important components of comprehensive osteoporosis management, and systematic evidence suggests that exercise can complement pharmacological care in people at increased fracture risk [32]. However, the present data do not establish that low muscle mass or impaired function independently caused reduced BMD. These measures were strongly patterned by age and sex, represented overlapping dimensions of physical reserve, and were not collected under a research-standardized protocol. Their attenuation after age-sex adjustment should therefore be interpreted as limited incremental information in this dataset, not as evidence against muscle-bone biology.

The renal and vascular findings require similar caution. Cystatin C was higher, eGFR lower, and carotid intima-media thickness greater in the reduced-BMD group, but their separate age-sex-adjusted estimates were imprecise and compatible with no clear incremental association. Age is related to renal filtration, arterial structure, muscle mass, and BMD. Sparse-event coefficients are also sensitive to model specification and measurement error. Direction changes after adjustment are therefore more plausibly explained by confounding, collinearity, overlapping constructs, sparse-data instability, or overadjustment than by credible protective effects.

Bone-turnover-marker interpretation is complicated by treatment and pre-analytical context. The reduced-BMD group had substantially higher P1NP, osteocalcin, beta-CTX, and TRAP-5b, whereas much of the broader T2DM literature describes a low-turnover tendency. This apparent contrast may reflect the older and predominantly female composition of the reduced-BMD group, renal function, fracture history, referral for metabolic-bone assessment, or prior treatment. International guidance emphasizes that P1NP and beta-CTX are influenced by collection conditions, kidney function, recent fracture, and antiresorptive or anabolic therapy [20]. The hospital export did not retain sampling time, fasting status, assay platform, treatment chronology, or fracture date. The observed values should therefore be viewed as a descriptive clinical profile, not as evidence that T2DM with reduced BMD universally produces high turnover.

Glycemic and medication-related interpretation is also limited. HbA1c did not differ materially between groups, and the lower contemporaneous fasting glucose in the reduced-BMD group was not stable after adjustment. A single glucose measurement cannot represent cumulative glycemic exposure, glycemic variability, hypoglycemia, or historical treatment. Diabetes medications may have heterogeneous skeletal effects. Recent syntheses have evaluated incretin therapies, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and SGLT2 inhibitors, with findings that vary by outcome, drug, duration, weight loss, and marker [33–35]. Anti-osteoporosis therapy can alter BMD and bone-turnover markers, but the timing, duration, and indication for treatment were unavailable in the present export [20]. Because bisphosphonate use was much more common in the reduced-BMD group, the treatment field is more plausibly a marker of prior clinical recognition and treatment-by-indication than an etiologic exposure in this cross-sectional analysis.

The internal classification analysis provides a useful negative result, but its scope is narrow. ROC AUC values near 0.90 may appear impressive, yet the operational endpoint was rare and strongly separated by age and sex. Contemporary prediction-model guidance emphasizes comparison with simple baselines, leakage control, calibration, transparent resampling, and external validation before clinical claims are made [36, 37]. The complex classifiers did not exceed the fixed age-sex benchmark in the present repeated internal validation. Nevertheless, this does not prove that complex modeling is generally unhelpful in diabetic bone disease. The conclusion is conditional on the selected ten-variable feature set, the source population, the 26-event sample, the outcome definition, and the specific algorithms and tuning grids used.

Probability metrics deserve particular restraint because class weighting differed across model families. Class-balanced fitting can improve sensitivity to a rare class but changes the relationship between fitted probabilities and the observed prevalence. The poor calibration intercepts and negative Brier skill of some models may therefore reflect both model behavior and the weighting strategy. We retained the metrics for transparency but did not use them to declare a superior or inferior algorithm family. Future work should use a harmonized imbalance strategy, nested probability recalibration, a prespecified primary performance target, and external validation in a clinically relevant population.

The distinction between BMD and skeletal fragility is reinforced by recent studies of complementary imaging and fracture outcomes. An updated meta-analysis reported lower trabecular bone score in T2DM despite generally preserved or higher areal BMD, and an earlier clinical study related lower trabecular bone score to adiposity and insulin resistance beyond contemporaneous hyperglycemia [38, 39]. These data support the present study’s decision to treat reduced BMD as one limited, clinically accessible dimension rather than as a complete measure of diabetic bone quality.

Recent cohort evidence also indicates that the relation between T-score and fracture risk differs in T2DM and that vertebral-fracture burden cannot be inferred from BMD alone [40, 41]. Fracture risk attributable to T2DM varies by skeletal site, further emphasizing that a cross-sectional three-site T-score classification should not be translated into a universal fracture-risk statement [42]. The present findings therefore concern contemporaneous low-BMD classification within the selected DXA-referred records, not incident fracture prediction.

Muscle health remains a relevant clinical context. Meta-analytic and mechanistic reviews indicate that sarcopenia is common in T2DM and reflects interacting effects of age, insulin resistance, chronic hyperglycemia, inflammation, and reduced physical activity [43, 44]. Clinical work examining osteosarcopenia and trabecular bone score in T2DM similarly supports integrated assessment of muscle and skeletal status [45]. In the present dataset, however, muscle variables were not collected under a research-standardized protocol and were strongly patterned by age and sex; their imprecise adjusted estimates should not be interpreted as evidence against the muscle-bone relationship.

Treatment context is equally important. Pooled trial evidence suggests that diabetes does not abolish the antifracture, BMD, or bone-turnover-marker benefits of licensed antiresorptive therapies [46]. Contemporary clinical reviews consequently recommend integrating BMD with fracture history, falls, diabetes complications, medication exposure, and other risk factors rather than relying on a single threshold [47]. The present treatment fields lacked chronology and indication, so they were retained as descriptive markers of prior care rather than modeled as causal exposures.

The endpoint itself should remain narrowly defined. No record met a primary-site T-score ≤ − 2.5 threshold, but this does not establish absence of clinical osteoporosis. Vertebral compression fractures and prior bisphosphonate use were recorded, and osteoporosis may be diagnosed or treated on the basis of fragility fracture, other skeletal sites, historical measurements, or prior clinical assessment. In addition, the primary ≤ − 1.0 rule classified four boundary records that were not classified by the stricter < -1.0 rule. The boundary sensitivity was therefore necessary. Future studies should retain menopause status, Z-scores, full DXA reports, fracture adjudication, and treatment chronology and should define the endpoint prospectively before extraction.

The exact match between the source field labeled as an osteoporosis diagnosis and the reconstructed operational endpoint also warrants transparency. A field that matches an algorithmic endpoint in every record cannot provide independent validation. It may represent an exported rule-based flag, a recoded variable, or a misleading label. Prospective data dictionaries should distinguish low BMD, T-score-defined osteoporosis, Z-score categories, fracture-defined osteoporosis, clinician diagnosis, and treatment status. The mapping failure identified in the earlier cleaned file further illustrates why exact source-to-analysis dictionaries, automated assertions, and reproducible QC outputs are essential when routine clinical data are repurposed for research.

This study has several strengths. The endpoint was reconstructed directly from site-specific T-scores; erroneous prior variable mappings were identified and discarded; a circular diagnosis field was not used as validation; model complexity was deliberately restricted under sparse events; convergence and separation were evaluated explicitly; complex classifiers were compared with a simple benchmark using complete out-of-fold predictions; and the age sensitivity analysis was re-expressed on a common scale. The revision also distinguishes record-level evidence from unverified patient-level uniqueness and provides detailed model specifications, diagnostics, and repeat-level summaries in the Supplementary Materials. These practices improve transparency and reduce avoidable overstatement.

The limitations remain substantial. The design was retrospective and cross-sectional. The data came from one orthopedic hospital and likely reflect referral and DXA-testing patterns. The screened denominator and detailed exclusions were unavailable. A stable patient-level identifier was not retained, so definitive patient-level deduplication could not be demonstrated. Only 26 events occurred, with one male event; wide intervals therefore reflect genuine information limits rather than a technical defect resolved by penalization. Menopausal status, Z-scores, DXA platform metadata, scan-quality annotations, harmonized measurement time windows, assay platforms, medication duration, diabetes complications, nutrition, physical activity, and several secondary-cause variables were unavailable or incomplete. Fracture and treatment fields were not independently adjudicated. Probability comparisons were affected by model-specific class weighting. No external validation, prospective outcome assessment, threshold-specific clinical utility analysis, or independent replication was available.

Future work should use prospectively specified, multicenter cohorts with patient-level identifiers, complete DXA metadata, standardized measurement protocols, menopause and Z-score information, adjudicated fractures, and explicit treatment chronology. Sample-size and precision requirements should be calculated before model development, and simple clinical models should be externally validated before complex algorithms are considered. Longitudinal multicenter studies have begun to demonstrate how imaging and metabolic features may be linked to vertebral-fracture risk in older people with T2DM, while treatment analyses emphasize the importance of baseline BMD and clinical risk [48, 49]. Clinical reviews further underscore that fracture assessment and osteoporosis management in T2DM must integrate BMD with age, prior fracture, falls, complications, and treatment context [50].

Conclusions

In this single-center retrospective cross-sectional analysis of 329 de-identified analytical records from adults with T2DM, 26 met the primary operational reduced-BMD definition and 22 met the stricter boundary definition. Older age and marked female predominance were the clearest features. On a common five-year scale, the age association was similar in the full cohort and the age ≥ 50 subset, whereas internal ROC discrimination decreased after restriction because the age distribution and case mix narrowed. Descriptive differences across muscle, bone-turnover, renal, vascular, fracture-history, and treatment domains did not translate into precisely estimated incremental associations in the prespecified age-sex-plus-one-variable models. Within this dataset, prespecified feature set, and internal-validation framework, the tested complex algorithms showed no material advantage over a fixed L2-regularized age-sex benchmark. The findings are exploratory and selected-sample specific and require patient-level identifiers, external clinical validation, standardized measurement, and prospective follow-up before diagnostic, prognostic, causal, or therapeutic interpretation.
