Representation theory of mirabolic quantum 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 n \mathfrak{sl}_{n}
Pallav Goyal, Daniele Rosso

Abstract
. We show that the mirabolic quantum group M ​ U ​ ( n ) MU(n) is a comodule algebra over the quantized enveloping algebra U v ​ ( 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 n ) U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n}) , and use this structure to give a complete classification of its finite dimensional representations. We also explicitly describe the correspondence between the irreducible finite dimensional representations of M ​ U ​ ( n ) MU(n) and the ones for the mirabolic Hecke algebra, given by the mirabolic quantum Schur-Weyl duality.



1.
Introduction

One way of studying representations of the general linear group
\operatorname{GL}_{n}=\operatorname{GL}(V)
(or the special linear group
\operatorname{SL}(V)
) is by taking advantage of its Schur-Weyl duality with the symmetric group
S_{d}
. We have a left diagonal action of
\operatorname{GL}(V)
on the tensor product
V^{\otimes d}
, and a right action of
S_{d}
by permuting the tensor factors. Since the two actions satisfy the double centralizer property, we can use knowledge of the representations of
S_{d}
to gain information about the representations of
\operatorname{GL}(V)
. This was originally done in the work of Schur
[
Sch01
]
, which was popularized by Weyl
[
Wey39
]
. This idea can be extended to their respective quantum versions: the quantized enveloping algebra of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
on one side, and the Iwahori-Hecke algebra
H_{d}
on the other side. This quantum Schur-Weyl duality was first described by Michio Jimbo in
[
Jim85
]
.

In a classic paper from 1990 (
[
BLM90
]
) Beilison, Lusztig and MacPherson defined a convolution product on the
\operatorname{GL}_{d}
-invariant functions over the variety of pairs of
n
-step partial flags in a
d
-dimensional vector space over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. This gave a geometric construction of the quantum Schur algebras, which are finite dimensional quotients of the quantized enveloping algebra of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
(or
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}
). They also obtained the idempotented version of the quantized enveloping algebra via a stabilization procedure for
d\to\infty
.

Given that the Iwahori-Hecke algebra
H_{d}
can be realized as the invariant functions on the variety of pairs of
complete
flags in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
(see
[
Iwa64
]
), the quantum Schur-Weyl duality can then be interpreted as the convolution action on the space of invariant function on pairs of one partial and one complete flag, as was done by Grojnowski and Lusztig in
[
GL92
]
.

All the above mentioned definitions can be extended to the ‘mirabolic’ setting, as follows. Instead of considering pairs of flags (partial or complete), we can consider triples of two flags and a vector in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, where a convolution product was defined by the second author. By working with complete flags, this construction gives the mirabolic Hecke algebra, whose representations were studied in
[
Ros14
]
. Analogously to
[
BLM90
]
and
[
GL92
]
, by working with triple of two
n
-step partial flags and a vector (resp. one partial flag, one complete flag, and a vector) we obtain mirabolic versions of the quantized enveloping algebra and a mirabolic quantum Schur-Weyl duality. These were first defined in
[
Ros18
]
, where, in particular, a complete classification of the finite dimensional representations for
MU(2)
(the mirabolic quantum group version of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
) was obtained. In
[
Ros18
, Thm 6.9]
there was also a first step towards an explicit combinatorial description of the mirabolic quantum Schur-Weyl duality, by establishing an isomorphism of left
MU(2)
-modules.

Recently,
[
FZM
]
expanded on the work of
[
Ros18
]
: they obtained explicit multiplication formulae and a description in terms of generators and relations for the mirabolic quantum group corresponding to
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
, as well as explicit formulae for any
n
of the left and right action involved in the mirabolic quantum Schur-Weyl duality. In
[
FZM
]
, there was also a stabilization procedure for
d\to\infty
, analogous to
[
BLM90
]
.

In this paper, we take advantage of the formulae of
[
FZM
]
to give a complete classification of the finite dimensional representations of mirabolic quantum
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}
, as well as an explicit description of the mirabolic quantum Schur-Weyl duality, in particular proving a generalization for any
n
of Conjecture 6.8 of
[
Ros18
]
.

A summary of our main results and the organization of the paper is as follows:

•

In Section
2
we give the necessary definitions and background on the mirabolic quantum group
MU(n)
and show that it has a comodule algebra structure over
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
. We then use that structure to state one of our main results, Theorem
2.17
, which gives a complete classification of the finite dimensional simple representations of
MU(n)
. In particular, we show that simple
MU(n)
-representations are parametrized by pairs
(\lambda,r)
of a dominant integral weight
\lambda
for
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}
and an integer
0\leq r\leq n
.

•

In Section
3
we give the proof of Proposition
2.25
, which is one of the main technical steps needed to prove the classification Theorem.

•

In Section
4
the main result is Theorem
4.4
, which says that the category of finite dimensional representations for
MU(n)
is semisimple. In particular this completes the classification of all finite dimensional representations, since we already described the simple ones.

•

Finally, Section
5
discusses the mirabolic quantum Schur-Weyl duality. The main result here is Theorem
5.8
which gives an explicit description of which simple modules of
MU(n)
correspond to which simple modules of the mirabolic Hecke algebra under the duality.

1.1.
Future Directions

Our work raises several interesting questions, which can be the basis for future research.

•

The classification of representations and semisemplicity here are proven in the generic case, but these results will not hold if we allow
v
to become a root of unity. It should be possible to use what is known about
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
at roots of unity to investigate the representation theory of the mirabolic quantum group in that setting.

•

Here, we have worked in the category of finite dimensional representations, but it should also be interesting to extend the study of representations to some analogue of category
\mathcal{O}
. We do use some Verma-inspired constructions in the proof of the classification theorem.

•

The algebra
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
has an RTT-type presentation, involving the universal
R
-matrix, which is interesting from the point of view of mathematical physics. Since the mirabolic quantum group
MU(n)
is a comodule algebra over
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
, it would be interesting to investigate an RLL-type presentation for
MU(n)
and its consequences in the study of (for example) integral forms, quantum symmetric pairs and categorification.

•

There is a potentially interesting structure coming from induction and restriction functors between representations over
MU(n)
and
MU(n+1)
that can be studied, in analogy to the Gelfand-Zeitlin results for Lie algebras/quantum groups of type A.

2.
The mirabolic quantum group and its representations

2.1.
Background and Notation

We work over the field
K=\mathbb{C}(v)
, where
v
is an indeterminate. Let
A=(a_{ij})
be the Cartan matrix of type
A_{n-1}
, that is, for all
1\leq i,j,\leq n-1
we have

a_{ij}=\begin{cases}2&\text{ if }i=j\\
-1&\text{ if }|i-j|=1\\
0&\text{ if }|i-j|>1.\end{cases}

Definition 2.1
.

The quantum enveloping algebra of the simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}
, denoted by
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
, is the unital associative algebra over
K
with generators
e_{i},f_{i},k_{i},k_{i}^{-1}
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
, subject to the relations:

(2.1)

\displaystyle k_{i}k_{i}^{-1}=1,

\displaystyle\quad k_{i}k_{j}=k_{j}k_{i}

(2.2)

\displaystyle k_{i}e_{j}k_{i}^{-1}=v^{a_{ij}}e_{j},

\displaystyle\quad k_{i}f_{j}k_{i}^{-1}=v^{-a_{ij}}f_{j}

(2.3)

\displaystyle e_{i}f_{j}-f_{j}e_{i}

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}\frac{k_{i}-k_{i}^{-1}}{v-v^{-1}}

(2.4)

\displaystyle e_{i}e_{j}=e_{j}e_{i},

\displaystyle\quad f_{i}f_{j}=f_{j}f_{i}\quad\text{ if }|i-j|\neq 1

(2.5)

\displaystyle(v+v^{-1})e_{i}e_{j}e_{i}

\displaystyle=e_{i}^{2}e_{j}+e_{j}e_{i}^{2}\quad\text{ if }|i-j|=1

(2.6)

\displaystyle(v+v^{-1})f_{i}f_{j}f_{i}

\displaystyle=f_{i}^{2}f_{j}+f_{j}f_{i}^{2}\quad\text{ if }|i-j|=1.

Remark 2.2
.

It is well known that
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
is a Hopf algebra, with comultiplication
\Delta:U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})\to U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})\otimes U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
given on the generators by

\Delta(e_{i})=1\otimes e_{i}+e_{i}\otimes k_{i},\quad\Delta(f_{i})=k_{i}^{-1}\otimes f_{i}+f_{i}\otimes 1,\quad\Delta(k_{i}^{\pm 1})=k_{i}^{\pm 1}\otimes k_{i}^{\pm 1},

counit
\varepsilon:U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})\to K
given by

\varepsilon(k_{i}^{\pm 1})=1,\qquad\varepsilon(e_{i})=\varepsilon(f_{i})=0,

and antipode
S:U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})\to U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
given by:

S(k_{i}^{\pm 1})=k_{i}^{\mp 1},\qquad S(e_{i})=-k_{i}^{-1}e_{i},\qquad S(f_{i})=-f_{i}k_{i},

for all
1\leq i\leq n-1

The main object of study of this paper is the following algebra.

Definition 2.3
.

The
mirabolic quantum group
, denoted by
MU(n)
, is the unital associative algebra over
K
with generators
\ell
, and
e_{i},f_{i},k_{i},k_{i}^{-1}
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
, subject to the relations of Definition
2.1
, plus the additional ones involving
\ell
:

(2.7)

\displaystyle\ell^{2}=\ell,

\displaystyle\quad k_{i}\ell=\ell k_{i}

(2.8)

\displaystyle\ell e_{i}=e_{i}\ell,

\displaystyle\quad\ell f_{i}=f_{i}\ell\quad\text{ if }i\geq 2

(2.9)

\displaystyle\ell e_{1}\ell=\ell e_{1},

\displaystyle\quad\ell f_{1}\ell=f_{1}\ell

(2.10)

\displaystyle(v+v^{-1})e_{1}\ell e_{1}

\displaystyle=v^{-1}e_{1}^{2}\ell+v\ell e_{1}^{2}

(2.11)

\displaystyle(v+v^{-1})f_{1}\ell f_{1}

\displaystyle=v^{-1}\ell f_{1}^{2}+vf_{1}^{2}\ell.

Remark 2.4
.

The algebra
MU(n)
is
\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}
-graded by setting, for all
1\leq i\leq n-1
,

\deg e_{i}=(0,\ldots,0,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{1}},0,\ldots,0),\quad\deg f_{i}=(0,\ldots,0,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{-1}},0,\ldots,0),

\deg k_{i}=\deg k_{i}^{-1}=\deg\ell=(0,\ldots,0).

Remark 2.5
.

The definition of this algebra comes from a convolution construction, we recall some of the basic concepts here and we refer the reader to
[
Ros18
, §2]
for more details. We let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be the finite field with
q
elements, and
\mathcal{F}(n,d)
be the variety of
n
-step partial flags in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
, then we consider the diagonal action of
\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d})
on
\mathcal{F}(n,d)\times\mathcal{F}(n,d)\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
which has finitely many orbits parametrized by decorated matrices. The space of invariant functions
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{U}_{q}(n,d):=\mathbb{C}\left(\mathcal{F}(n,d)\times\mathcal{F}(n,d)\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\right)^{\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d})}
is a unital associative algebra with the convolution product defined by

(2.12)

(\alpha*\beta)(F,F^{\prime},u):=\sum_{F^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{F}(n,d),~u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}}\alpha(F,F^{\prime\prime},u^{\prime})\beta(F^{\prime\prime},F^{\prime},u-u^{\prime}),

which we call the
mirabolic quantum Schur algebra
. Since the structure constants for
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{U}_{q}(n,d)
are polynomials in
q
, we can consider it to be the specialization of the
generic mirabolic quantum Schur algebra

MU(n,d)
, which is defined over
\mathbb{C}(v)
, where
q=v^{2}
. By
[
FZM
]
, the generators of
MU(n,d)
satisfy the relations in Definitions
2.1
and
2.3
(in addition to other relations that depend on
d
) hence we have a surjective map
MU(n)\twoheadrightarrow MU(n,d)
for all
d
. We notice here that in
[
FZM
]
the authors prefer to work with the ‘general linear’ analogue of the mirabolic quantum group, while we are using the ‘special linear’ version, which is why the ‘Cartan part’ has
n
generators in their case (
H_{a}
,
1\leq a\leq n
, see
[
FZM
, Def. 2.1.2]
), and
n-1
in our case (
k_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n-1
). This difference is not significant for our purposes.

Remark 2.6
.

There are two anti-involutions
\star
and
\dagger
on
MU(n)
defined on the generators by

\ell^{\star}=\ell,\quad(k_{i}^{\pm 1})^{\star}=k_{i}^{\pm 1},\quad e_{i}^{\star}=f_{i}\quad f_{i}^{\star}=e_{i}.

\ell^{\dagger}=\ell,\quad(k_{i}^{\pm 1})^{\dagger}=k_{i}^{\pm 1},\quad e_{i}^{\dagger}=v^{-1}k_{i}^{-1}f_{i}=vf_{i}k_{i}^{-1}\quad f_{i}^{\dagger}=ve_{i}k_{i}=v^{-1}k_{i}e_{i}.

Proposition 2.7
.

(1)

There are two surjective algebra maps
\pi_{0},\pi_{1}:MU(n)\to U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
that map the generators
e_{i},f_{i},k_{i},k_{i}^{-1}
to the ones of the same name, and
\pi_{0}(\ell)=0
,
\pi_{1}(\ell)=1
.

(2)

There is an injective algebra map
\iota:U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})\to MU(n)
that maps the generators
e_{i},f_{i},k_{i},k_{i}^{-1}
to the same ones for
MU(n)
.

(3)

The algebra
MU(n)
is a co-module algebra for
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
via the map:

\rho:MU(n)\rightarrow U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})\otimes MU(n),

defined on generators via:

\rho(e_{i})=\Delta(e_{i})=1\otimes e_{i}+e_{i}\otimes k_{i},\qquad\rho(f_{i})=\Delta(f_{i})=k_{i}^{-1}\otimes f_{i}+f_{i}\otimes 1,

\rho(k_{i})=\Delta(k_{i}^{\pm 1})=k_{i}^{\pm 1}\otimes k_{i}^{\pm 1},\qquad\rho(\ell)=1\otimes\ell.

Proof.

(1)

Since the generators
e_{i},f_{i},k_{i},k_{i}^{-1}
satisfy the same relations in
MU(n)
as they do in
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
, and since replacing
\ell
with either
1
or
0
satisfies the relations of Definition
2.3
, it is clear that both maps are well defined. They are clearly surjective since the generators of
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
are in the image of both maps.

(2)

It is clear that the map is well defined, since the generators of
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
satisfy the same relations in
MU(n)
. Notice that the composition
\pi_{0}\circ\iota=\operatorname{id}_{U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})}
, hence
\iota
is injective.

(3)

It is clear that all the axioms of the coaction are satisfied on the generators
e_{i}
,
f_{i}
,
k_{i}^{\pm 1}
, since on those the coaction is equal to the comultiplication in
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
. Then, we have

(\Delta\otimes\operatorname{id})\rho(\ell)=1\otimes 1\otimes\ell=(\operatorname{id}\otimes\rho)\rho(\ell)
, and
(\varepsilon\otimes\operatorname{id})\rho(\ell)=\ell
.
To conclude, we check that
\rho
satisfies the relations in Definition
2.3
.

\rho(\ell^{2})=\rho(\ell)\rho(\ell)=(1\otimes\ell)(1\otimes\ell)=1\otimes\ell^{2}=1\otimes\ell=\rho(\ell).

For all
i=1,\ldots,n-1
, we have:

\rho(k_{i}\ell)=(k_{i}\otimes k_{i})(1\otimes\ell)=(k_{i}\otimes k_{i}\ell)=(k_{i}\otimes\ell k_{i})=(1\otimes\ell)(k_{i}\otimes k_{i})=\rho(\ell k_{i}).

For all
i=2,\ldots,n-1
, we have:

\rho(\ell e_{i})=(1\otimes\ell)(1\otimes e_{i}+e_{i}\otimes k_{i})=1\otimes\ell e_{i}+e_{i}\otimes\ell k_{i}=1\otimes e_{i}\ell+e_{i}\otimes k_{i}\ell=(1\otimes e_{i}+e_{i}\otimes k_{i})(1\otimes\ell)=\rho(e_{i}\ell);

\rho(\ell f_{i})=(1\otimes\ell)(k_{i}^{-1}\otimes f_{i}+f_{i}\otimes 1)=k_{i}^{-1}\otimes\ell f_{i}+f_{i}\otimes\ell=k_{i}^{-1}\otimes f_{i}\ell+f_{i}\otimes\ell=(k_{i}^{-1}\otimes f_{i}+f_{i}\otimes 1)(1\otimes\ell)=\rho(f_{i}\ell).

Finally,

\rho(\ell e_{1}\ell)=(1\otimes\ell)(1\otimes e_{1}+e_{1}\otimes k_{1})(1\otimes\ell)=1\otimes\ell e_{1}\ell+e_{1}\otimes\ell k_{1}\ell\\
=1\otimes\ell e_{1}+e_{1}\otimes\ell^{2}k_{1}=1\otimes\ell e_{1}+e_{1}\otimes\ell k_{1}=(1\otimes\ell)(1\otimes e_{1}+e_{1}\otimes k_{1})=\rho(\ell e_{1})

\rho(\ell f_{1}\ell)=(1\otimes\ell)(k_{1}^{-1}\otimes f_{1}+f_{1}\otimes 1)(1\otimes\ell)=k_{1}^{-1}\otimes\ell f_{1}\ell+f_{1}\otimes\ell^{2}\\
=k_{1}^{-1}\otimes f_{1}\ell+f_{1}\otimes\ell=(k_{1}^{-1}\otimes f_{1}+f_{1}\otimes 1)(1\otimes\ell)=\rho(f_{1}\ell)

\displaystyle\rho((v+v^{-1})e_{1}\ell e_{1})

\displaystyle=(v+v^{-1})(1\otimes e_{1}+e_{1}\otimes k_{1})(1\otimes\ell)(1\otimes e_{1}+e_{1}\otimes k_{1})

\displaystyle=(v+v^{-1})(1\otimes e_{1}\ell e_{1}+e_{1}\otimes k_{1}\ell e_{1}+e_{1}\otimes e_{1}\ell k_{1}+e_{1}^{2}\otimes k_{1}\ell k_{1})

\displaystyle=1\otimes(v+v^{-1})e_{1}\ell e_{1}+v\otimes e_{1}\otimes k_{1}\ell e_{1}+v^{-1}e_{1}\otimes k_{1}\ell e_{1}+

\displaystyle~+ve_{1}\otimes e_{1}\ell k_{1}+v^{-1}e_{1}\otimes e_{1}\ell k_{1}+ve_{1}^{2}\otimes k_{1}\ell k_{1}+v^{-1}e_{1}^{2}\otimes k_{1}\ell k_{1}

\displaystyle=1\otimes(v^{-1}e_{1}^{2}\ell+v\ell e_{1}^{2})+v\otimes e_{1}\otimes\ell k_{1}e_{1}+v^{-1}e_{1}\otimes v^{2}\ell e_{1}k_{1}+

\displaystyle~+ve_{1}\otimes v^{-2}k_{1}e_{1}\ell+v^{-1}e_{1}\otimes e_{1}k_{1}\ell+ve_{1}^{2}\otimes\ell k_{1}^{2}+v^{-1}e_{1}^{2}\otimes k_{1}^{2}\ell

\displaystyle=v^{-1}(1\otimes e_{1}^{2}\ell)+v^{-1}e_{1}\otimes k_{1}e_{1}\ell+v^{-1}e_{1}\otimes e_{1}k_{1}\ell+v^{-1}e_{1}^{2}\otimes k_{1}^{2}\ell

\displaystyle~+v(1\otimes\ell e_{1}^{2})+ve_{1}\otimes\ell k_{1}e_{1}+ve_{1}\otimes\ell e_{1}k_{1}+ve_{1}^{2}\otimes\ell k_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=v^{-1}(1\otimes e_{1}^{2}+e_{1}\otimes k_{1}e_{1}+e_{1}\otimes e_{1}k_{1}+e_{1}^{2}\otimes k_{1}^{2})(1\otimes\ell)

\displaystyle~+v(1\otimes\ell)(1\otimes e_{1}^{2}+e_{1}\otimes k_{1}e_{1}+e_{1}\otimes e_{1}k_{1}+e_{1}^{2}\otimes k_{1}^{2})

\displaystyle=v^{-1}(1\otimes e_{1}+e_{1}\otimes k_{1})^{2}(1\otimes\ell)+v(1\otimes\ell)(1\otimes e_{1}+e_{1}\otimes k_{1})^{2}

\displaystyle=\rho(v^{-1}e_{1}^{2}\ell+v\ell e_{1}^{2}).

The computation for
f_{1}\ell f_{1}
is similar to the one for

.

2.2.
Representations of
MU(n)

2.2.1.
Weight Spaces

Recall the theory of weight modules for
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
(see for example
[
Jan96
, §5]
). To be explicit, we define an equivalence relation on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, given by the orbits for the
\mathbb{Z}
-action

r\cdot(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})=(\lambda_{1}+r,\ldots,\lambda_{n}+r),\qquad r,\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{Z},

then a
weight
will be an equivalence class
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}/\mathbb{Z}
. With a slight abuse, we will also refer to a representative
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
as a weight, when it does not create confusion, and we will often choose the representative with
\lambda_{n}=0
.

Every finite dimensional module
M
for
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
decomposes as a direct sum of weight spaces
M=\bigoplus_{\lambda,\sigma}M_{\lambda,\sigma}
, where
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
is a weight,
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n-1})\in\{\pm 1\}^{n-1}
is a choice of signs, and

M_{\lambda,\sigma}:=\{x\in M~|~k_{i}\cdot x=\sigma_{i}v^{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{i+1}}x,\quad\forall i=1,\ldots,n-1\}.

If
M=M^{\sigma}
, where for each
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}^{n-1}
,
M^{\sigma}:=\oplus_{\lambda}M_{\lambda,\sigma}
, then we say that
M
is of type
\sigma
. The category of finite dimensional
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
-modules decomposes as a direct sum (over all
\sigma
) of the categories of modules of type
\sigma
, and the direct summands are all equivalent to the category of modules of type
\mathbb{1}=(1,\ldots,1)\in\{\pm 1\}^{n-1}
(
[
Jan96
, §5.2]
), it is therefore enough to restrict to such modules.
In the category of modules of type
\mathbb{1}
, for each dominant weight
\lambda
, which in this case means that
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}
(notice that this condition is well defined on equivalence classes),
there is a unique simple finite dimensional module
L_{\lambda}
of highest weight
\lambda
and all simple finite dimensional modules are of this form. We define a partial order on the set of weights by saying that
\lambda\geq\mu
if and only if
\lambda-\mu
is dominant.

We note at this point that the action of the Weyl group on the weights can just be identified with the action of the symmetric group
S_{n}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by permutation of the factors, descended to the equivalence classes.

Now let
M
be a finite dimensional representation for
MU(n)
, which in particular can be seen as a
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
-representation given the inclusion of Prop.
2.7
(2), then it decomposes as a direct sum of weight spaces for the action of the
k_{i}
’s. Since
\ell
is an idempotent, it is diagonalizable with eigenvalues
0
and
1
. Furthermore, it commutes with all the
k_{i}
’s, hence we can further refine the weight spaces to

M_{\lambda,\sigma}=M_{\lambda,\sigma,0}\oplus M_{\lambda,\sigma,1}

where, for
\epsilon=0,1
,

M_{\lambda,\sigma,\epsilon}:=\{x\in M~|~\ell\cdot x=\epsilon x,~k_{i}\cdot x=\sigma_{i}v^{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{i+1}}x,\quad\forall i=1,\ldots,n-1\}.

Definition 2.8
.

We refer to
M_{\lambda,\sigma}
as
weight spaces
, and to an element
x\in M_{\lambda,\sigma}
as a
weight vector
. We will use the terms
mirabolic weight spaces
and
mirabolic weight vectors
, respectively, to refer to
M_{\lambda,\sigma,\epsilon}
and its elements. So, in particular, a mirabolic weight vector is also a weight vector but the vice-versa is not necessarily true. A (mirabolic)
highest weight vector
is a (mirabolic) weight vector
x
such that
e_{i}\cdot x=0
for all
i=1,\ldots,n-1
.

Exactly like in the case of
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
, we can define modules of type
\sigma
for
MU(n)
and, for the exact same reasons as in
[
Jan96
, §5.2]
, we have that the category of finite dimensional modules for
MU(n)
decomposes as a direct sum of the categories of modules of type
\sigma
, and the automorphism
\widetilde{\sigma}:MU(n)\to MU(n)
given by

\widetilde{\sigma}(e_{i})=\sigma_{i}e_{i},\quad\widetilde{\sigma}(f_{i})=f_{i},\quad\widetilde{\sigma}(k_{i}^{\pm 1})=\sigma_{i}k_{i}^{\pm 1},\quad\widetilde{\sigma}(\ell)=\ell,

gives an equivalence of categories between modules of type
\mathbb{1}
and modules of type
\sigma
. Therefore, since no information is lost and all the other modules can be obtained by twisting with the automorphism
\widetilde{\sigma}
, from now on we will assume that all modules are of type
\mathbb{1}
and we will write (mirabolic) weight spaces more simply as

M_{\lambda}:=M_{\lambda,\mathbb{1}},\quad M_{\lambda,\epsilon}:=M_{\lambda,\mathbb{1},\epsilon}\qquad\forall\lambda,~\epsilon.

2.2.2.
Mirabolic Representations

Let
V=K^{n}
be the defining representation of
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
, with basis
\{u_{1},u_{2},\cdots,u_{n}\}
,
where the action is given by

k_{i}u_{j}=v^{\delta_{i,j}-\delta_{i+1,j}}u_{j};\qquad e_{i}u_{j}=\delta_{i+1,j}u_{i};\qquad f_{i}u_{j}=\delta_{i,j}u_{i+1}.

Definition 2.9
.

For any
0\leq r\leq n
, we consider the quantum wedge product
W_{r}:=\wedge_{v}^{r}(V)\subset V^{\otimes r}
, defined to be the span of all elements of the form

u_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{i_{r}}:=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{r}}(-v^{-1})^{\mathbf{len}(\sigma)}u_{i_{\sigma(1)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{i_{\sigma(r)}}

where
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n
, and
\mathbf{len}
is the length function on the symmetric group
S_{r}
.

Definition 2.10
.

For any subset
I\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we can order its elements in increasing order, say
I=\{i_{1}<\ldots<i_{r}\}
, then we will denote
u_{I}:=u_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{i_{r}}
. We also define the lexicographic total order on the set
[n]_{r}:=\{I\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}~|~\#I=r\}
by saying that
\{i_{1}<\ldots<i_{r}\}<\{i^{\prime}_{1}<\ldots<i^{\prime}_{r}\}
if there is an index
1\leq m\leq r
such that
i_{1}=i^{\prime}_{1}
, …,
i_{m-1}=i^{\prime}_{m-1}
and
i_{m}<i^{\prime}_{m}
.

Then
W_{r}:=\wedge_{v}^{r}(V)
is in fact isomorphic to the simple highest weight representation
L_{\omega_{r}}
of
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
with highest weight
\omega_{r}=(1,1,\cdots,1,0,0,\cdots,0)=(1^{r}0^{n-r})
(
1
occurs
r
times).

Remark 2.11
.

To write the action of
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
on
W_{r}
, we consider the action on
V^{\otimes r}
, which is given by the iteration of the coproduct, so we get

\Delta^{r-1}(k_{i})=k_{i}^{\otimes r};\quad\Delta^{r-1}(e_{i})=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}1^{\otimes j}\otimes e_{i}\otimes k_{i}^{\otimes r-j};\quad\Delta^{r-1}(f_{i})=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}(k_{i}^{-1})^{\otimes j}\otimes f_{i}\otimes 1^{\otimes r-j}.

and applying that to

u_{I}:=u_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{i_{r}}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{r}}(-v^{-1})^{\mathbf{len}(\sigma)}u_{i_{\sigma(1)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{i_{\sigma(r)}}

we get that,

k_{i}\cdot u_{I}=\begin{cases}vu_{I}&\text{ if }i\in I,~i+1\not\in I,\\
v^{-1}u_{I}&\text{ if }i\not\in I,~i+1\in I,\\
u_{I}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases},\quad e_{i}\cdot u_{I}=\begin{cases}u_{I\setminus\{i+1\}\cup\{i\}}&\text{ if }i+1\in I,~i\not\in I,\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

f_{i}\cdot u_{I}=\begin{cases}u_{I\setminus\{i\}\cup\{i+1\}}&\text{ if }i\in I,~i+1\not\in I,\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Definition 2.13
.

Let

Lemma 2.14
.

The module
L_{\lambda,r}
is generated by the vector
x_{\lambda,r}
as an
MU(n)
-module.

Proof.

When
r=0
or
r=n
, the result is clear since
L_{\lambda,0}=\pi_{1}^{*}(L_{\lambda})
and
L_{\lambda,n}=\pi_{0}^{*}(L_{\lambda})
are the pullbacks along the maps defined in Prop.
2.7
(1), and
L_{\lambda}
is a simple
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
-module. Therefore, we assume that
1\leq r\leq n-1
.

Note that the space
L_{\lambda}
is spanned by elements of the form
Q(f)\cdot x_{\lambda}
, where
Q(f)
is a non-commutative monomial in the
f_{i}
’s.
We start by proving the claim that for all non-commutative monomials
Q(f)
and
r
-element subsets

, we have

(Q(f)\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u_{I}\in MU(n)\cdot x_{\lambda,r}.

We proceed by induction on the degree of
Q(f)
.
For the base case, suppose
\deg(Q(f))=0
. We need to show that
x_{\lambda}\otimes u_{I}\in MU(n)\cdot x_{\lambda,r}
. To this end, we compute:

\displaystyle\ell f_{1}f_{2}\cdots f_{r}\cdot x_{\lambda,r}

\displaystyle=\ell f_{1}f_{2}\cdots f_{r}\cdot(x_{\lambda}\otimes u)

\displaystyle=(1\otimes\ell)\left(k_{1}^{-1}\cdots k_{r}^{-1}x_{\lambda}\otimes u_{\{2,\ldots,r+1\}}+\sum_{J\in[n]_{r},~1\in J}x_{J}\otimes u_{J}\right)

\displaystyle=v^{\sum_{j=1}^{r}(\lambda_{j+1}-\lambda_{j})}x_{\lambda}\otimes u_{\{2,\ldots,r+1\}}.

Hence
x_{\lambda}\otimes u_{\{2,\ldots,r+1\}}\in MU(n)\cdot x_{\lambda,r}
. Next, for any
1\leq i\leq r-1
, we see that:

e_{i}e_{i-1}\cdots e_{1}\cdot(x_{\lambda}\otimes u_{\{2,\ldots,r+1\}})=x_{\lambda}\otimes u_{I_{i}},

where
I_{i}=\{1,2,\dots,r+1\}\setminus\{i\}
. This proves the base case. Now suppose that the claim is true for a given
Q(f)
, so in particular we have

(Q(f)\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u,~(Q(f)\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u_{\{2,\ldots,r+1\}}\in MU(n)\cdot x_{\lambda,r}.

Then for all
j\neq r+1
(this condition is vacuous when
r=n-1
), we have that:

f_{j}\cdot((Q(f)\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u_{\{2,\ldots,r+1\}})=(f_{j}Q(f)\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u_{\{2,\ldots,r+1\}}\in MU(n)\cdot x_{\lambda,r}

since
f_{j}\cdot u_{\{2,\ldots,r+1\}}=0
.
And we also have that:

f_{r+1}\cdot((Q(f)\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u)=(f_{r+1}Q(f)\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u\in MU(n)\cdot x_{\lambda,r},

since
f_{r+1}\cdot u=0
.
Then, just as in the base case, we observe that:

\ell f_{1}f_{2}\cdots f_{r}\cdot((f_{r+1}Q(f)\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u)=d(f_{r+1}Q(f)\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u_{\{2,\ldots,r+1\}},

where
d
is the constant from the equation
d(f_{r+1}Q(f)\cdot x_{\lambda})=k_{1}^{-1}\cdots k_{r}^{-1}(f_{r+1}Q(f)\cdot x_{\lambda})
.

This shows that
(\widetilde{Q}(f)\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u_{\{2,\ldots,r+1\}}\in MU(n)\cdot x_{\lambda,r}
whenever
\deg\widetilde{Q}(f)\leq\deg Q(f)+1
.
Finally, we compute for
1\leq i\leq r-1
:

(2.13)

\displaystyle e_{i}e_{i-1}\cdots e_{1}\cdot((\widetilde{Q}(f)\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u_{\{2,\ldots,r+1\}})

(2.14)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{i}(e_{i}e_{i-1}\cdots e_{j+1}\widetilde{Q}(f)\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes(k_{i}\cdots k_{j+1}e_{j}\cdots e_{1}\cdot u_{\{2,\ldots,r+1\}}).

Since
e_{k}\cdot x_{\lambda}=0
for all
k
, we have:

\displaystyle e_{i}e_{i-1}\cdots e_{j+1}\widetilde{Q}(f)\cdot x_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=e_{i}e_{i-1}\cdots e_{j+2}[e_{j+1},\widetilde{Q}(f)]\cdot x_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\vdots

\displaystyle=[e_{i},[e_{i-1},[\cdots,[e_{j+1},Q^{\prime}(f)]\cdots]]]\cdot x_{\lambda}.

The iterated commutator in the last equality above can be simplified using the defining relations in
MU(n)
to get that it is equal to a linear combination of monomials in the
f_{t}
’s each having degree lesser than or equal to
\deg Q(f)
(up to a factor of some
k_{t}
’s). Then, by the induction assumption, we have that the summands in (

) for

all lie in
MU(n)\cdot x_{\lambda,r}
.
This implies that the same must be true for the summand when
j=i
, which is equal to:

(\widetilde{Q}(f)\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes(e_{i}e_{i-1}\cdots e_{1}\cdot u_{\{2,\ldots,r+1\}})=(\widetilde{Q}(f)\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u_{I_{i}},

where
I_{i}=\{1,2,\dots,r+1\}\setminus\{i\}
. This completes the proof of the claim.

To conclude the proof of the Lemma, we use induction on the totally ordered set
[n]_{r}
with the lexicographic order, as defined in Def.
2.10
, to show that
L_{\lambda}\otimes\{u_{I}\}\subset MU(n)\cdot x_{\lambda,r}
for all
I\in[n]_{r}
. The claim that we have proved shows that
L_{\lambda}\otimes\{u\}\subset MU(n)\cdot x_{\lambda,r}
which is our base case. Now suppose that
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\}\neq\{1,\ldots,r\}
, we want to prove that for any weight vector
x\in L_{\lambda}
,
x\otimes u_{I}\in MU(n)\cdot x_{\lambda,r}
. Since
I\neq\{1,\ldots,r\}
, there exists an index
1\leq j\leq r
such that
i_{j}-1\in[n]\setminus I
and therefore
I^{\prime}_{j}:=I\setminus\{i_{j}\}\cup\{i_{j}-1\}\in[n]_{r}
. By inductive hypothesis, since
I^{\prime}_{j}<I
in the lexicographic order,
x\otimes u_{I^{\prime}_{j}}
,
f_{i_{j}-1}x\otimes u_{I^{\prime}_{j}}\in MU(n)\cdot x_{\lambda,r}
. We compute

\displaystyle f_{i_{j}-1}\cdot\left(x\otimes u_{I^{\prime}_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=k_{i_{j}-1}^{-1}x\otimes f_{i_{j}-1}u_{I^{\prime}_{j}}+f_{i_{j}-1}x\otimes u_{I^{\prime}_{j}}

\displaystyle=cx\otimes u_{I}+f_{i_{j}-1}x\otimes u_{I^{\prime}_{j}}

where

equals a power of
v
because
x
is a weight vector. Since all the other terms are in
MU(n)\cdot x_{\lambda,r}
, then so is
cx\otimes u_{I}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 2.15
.

\lambda

-representation

and
L_{\mu,s}
do not have the same weight space decomposition. For this, we note that the
0
-eigenspace of
\ell
in
L_{\lambda,r}
has dimension
\dim(L_{\lambda}){n-1\choose r-1}
and the
1
-eigenspace has dimension
\dim(L_{\lambda}){n-1\choose r}
. The ratio of these two dimensions is
{n-1\choose r-1}/{n-1\choose r}=r/(n-r)
. This unique determines the value of
r
, and so, we conclude that if
L_{\lambda,r}
and
L_{\mu,s}
have the same weight space decomposition, then

. Next, since
\lambda+\omega_{r}
is a weight of
L_{\lambda,r}
, it must also be a weight of
L_{\mu,r}
, showing that
\lambda\leq\mu
. Similarly, we must also have
\mu\leq\lambda
, which implies that
\lambda=\mu
, completing the proof.
∎

We now state the main result of this section.

Theorem 2.17
.

The representations
\{L_{\lambda,r}\}
where
\lambda
varies among all dominant weights for

, and

, are a complete list of isomorphisms classes of irreducible finite dimensional

-modules.

We start with some preliminaries towards the proof of Theorem

. All modules are assumed to be finite dimensional over

and hence weight modules.

Remark 2.19
.

The

n
.

Example 2.20
.

Let
\lambda
be a dominant weight and

Lemma 2.21
.

Let
M\neq 0
be a module for
MU(n)
, then the depth of
M
is also equal to the maximum of the depth of
x
, where
x
varies among all nonzero mirabolic weight vectors in

.

Proof.

All mirabolic weight vectors are also weight vectors, so all that is needed here is to show that for all
0\leq r\leq n
, if there is a weight vector of depth
r
, then there is also a mirabolic weight vector of depth
r
. First suppose that
x
is a weight vector of depth

, which means that
\ell x\neq 0
, then

is a mirabolic weight vector such that
\ell\cdot y=\ell\cdot(\ell\cdot x)=\ell\cdot x=y

has depth

∎

Lemma 2.22
.

Let
M\neq 0
be a module for
MU(n)
, then the depth of
M
is also equal to the maximum of the depth of
x
, where
x
varies among all nonzero highest weight vectors in
M
.

Proof.

To prove the lemma, we show that if
y
is a weight vector of depth
r
,
r\geq 0
, then for all
i=1,\ldots,n-1
,
e_{i}\cdot y
is a weight vector of depth
\geq r
. This gives the result because if there is a vector
y
of maximum depth that is not a highest weight vector, then there is an index
i
such that
e_{i}\cdot y\neq 0
, and
e_{i}\cdot y
also has maximum depth. By iterating this procedure we eventually get a highest weight vector of maximum depth.
Suppose that
\ell f_{1}\cdots f_{k}\cdot y=0
, for all
0\leq k<r
, then we show that we also have
\ell f_{1}\cdots f_{k}e_{i}\cdot y=0
for all
0\leq k<r
.
If
r=0
the statement is vacuously true, so suppose that
r\geq 1
. For
k=0
, we have that
\ell\cdot y=0
and
\ell e_{i}y=\ell e_{i}\ell\cdot y=\ell e_{i}\cdot(0)=0
.
Suppose that
k\geq 1
, and consider
\ell f_{1}\cdots f_{k}e_{i}\cdot y
. If
k+1\leq i\leq n-1
, then
\ell f_{1}\cdots f_{k}e_{i}\cdot y=e_{i}\ell f_{1}\cdots f_{k}\cdot y=0
. If
1\leq i\leq k
, then

\displaystyle\ell f_{1}\cdots f_{k}e_{i}\cdot y

\displaystyle=\ell f_{1}\cdots f_{i-1}f_{i}e_{i}f_{i+1}\cdots f_{k}\cdot y

\displaystyle=\ell f_{1}\cdots f_{i-1}\left(e_{i}f_{i}-\frac{k_{i}-k_{i}^{-1}}{v-v^{-1}}\right)f_{i+1}\cdots f_{k}\cdot y

\displaystyle=\ell f_{1}\cdots f_{i-1}e_{i}f_{i}f_{i+1}\cdots f_{k}\cdot y-\ell f_{1}\cdots f_{i-1}\frac{k_{i}-k_{i}^{-1}}{v-v^{-1}}f_{i+1}\cdots f_{k}\cdot y

\displaystyle=\ell e_{i}f_{1}\cdots f_{i-1}f_{i}f_{i+1}\cdots f_{k}\cdot y-\frac{v^{-1}k_{i}-vk_{i}^{-1}}{v-v^{-1}}\ell f_{1}\cdots f_{i-1}f_{i+1}\cdots f_{k}\cdot y

\displaystyle=\ell e_{i}\ell f_{1}\cdots f_{i-1}f_{i}f_{i+1}\cdots f_{k}\cdot y-\frac{v^{-1}k_{i}-vk_{i}^{-1}}{v-v^{-1}}f_{i+1}\cdots f_{k}\ell f_{1}\cdots f_{i-1}\cdot y

\displaystyle=\ell e_{i}\cdot(0)-\frac{v^{-1}k_{i}-vk_{i}^{-1}}{v-v^{-1}}f_{i+1}\cdots f_{k}\cdot(0)

\displaystyle=0.

∎

Lemma 2.23
.

Let
V
be any
MU(n)
-module and fix
0\leq s\leq n
. Then, there exists a unique
MU(n)
-module
V^{\leq s}
and a surjection
\pi:V\to V^{\leq s}
satisfying:

(1)

V^{\leq s}=0
or
V^{\leq s}
has depth lesser than or equal to
s
.

(2)

Given any
MU(n)
-module
W
of depth lesser than or equal to
s
and an
MU(n)
-module map
\phi:V\to W
, there exists a unique map
\overline{\phi}:V^{\leq s}\to W
such that
\phi=\overline{\phi}\circ\pi
.

Proof.

For any module
V
, let
V_{\leq s}
be the quotient of
V
by the
MU(n)
-submodule generated by all vectors having depth greater than
s
. There exists a canonical surjection
V\to V_{\leq s}
and any map from
V
to a module
W
of depth lesser than or equal to
s
uniquely descends to a map from
V_{\leq s}
to
W
.

So, we can iterate this construction and define the direct limit:

V^{\leq s}:=\lim_{\longrightarrow}\Big(V\to V_{\leq s}\to(V_{\leq s})_{\leq s}\to((V_{\leq s})_{\leq s})_{\leq s}\to\cdots\Big).

Then, it is clear that
V^{\leq s}
is the unique
MU(n)
-module satisfying the required properties by abstract nonsense.
∎

Remark 2.24
.

Since the modules
L_{\lambda,r}
are simple, it is clear that:

(L_{\lambda,r})^{\leq s}=\begin{cases}0&s<r\\
L_{\lambda,r}&r\leq s\leq n\end{cases}.

A crucial step in the proof of the classification theorem is the following proposition, which we will prove in Section
3
.

Proposition 2.25
.

Let
M
be an
MU(n)
-module of depth
r
, and let
x\in M
be a mirabolic highest weight vector of weight
\lambda
and depth
r
. Let
MU(n)_{\mathbb{0}}\subseteq MU(n)
be the degree
\mathbb{0}=(0,\ldots,0)
subalgebra according to the
\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}
-grading of Remark
2.4
. Then, there exists a character:

\chi_{\lambda,r}:MU(n)_{\mathbb{0}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C},

such that for all

, we have
m\cdot x=\chi_{\lambda,r}(m)x
. Moreover, this character doesn’t depend upon the module
M
, but only on
\lambda
and
r
.

Now the next Proposition shows how Proposition
2.25
implies the main result Theorem
2.17
.

Proposition 2.26
.

Let
M
be a finite dimensional simple
MU(n)
-module of depth
r
,
0\leq r\leq n
, then
M\simeq L_{\lambda,r}
for some dominant weight
\lambda
.

Proof.

Let
x\in M
be a mirabolic highest weight vector of depth
r
and fix a weight
\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\cdots,\mu_{n})
such that
k_{i}\cdot x=v^{\mu_{i}-\mu_{i+1}}x.
Since
M
is finite dimensional, we must have that
\mu
is a dominant integral weight for
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
.

Let

MU(n)^{+}

be the graded subalgebra of
MU(n)
generated by
MU(n)_{\mathbb{0}}
, and
e_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n-1
. Let
V^{+}
be a one-dimensional
K
-vector space spanned by a vector
w
. Then, we can define an
MU(n)^{+}
-module structure on
V^{+}
by defining:

e_{i}\cdot w=0,z\cdot w=\chi_{\mu,r}(z)w,

for all
z\in MU(n)_{0}
.

Define the following induced
MU(n)
-module
V
:

V:=MU(n)\otimes_{MU(n)^{+}}V^{+}.

Then, we have a natural surjection of
MU(n)
-modules
\Phi:V\rightarrow M
that sends the vector
1\otimes w
to the mirabolic highest weight vector
x
.
Consider the quotient
\overline{V}
of
V
by the
MU(n)
-submodule generated by elements of the form:

\ell f_{1}\cdots f_{j}\otimes w

for all
0\leq j<r
. Thus, (the image of) the element

in
\overline{V}
has depth at least
r
. Since

has depth
r
, the map
\Phi
descends to a map
\overline{V}\to M
. By Lemma
2.23
, we get that
\Phi
actually descends to a map
\widetilde{V}:=(\overline{V})^{\leq r}\to M
. (We abuse notation and also call this map

.) It is clear that this map is surjective and that
\widetilde{V}
is a non-zero module of depth

such that the element

is a highest weight vector of depth

that generates
\widetilde{V}
.

By Proposition
2.25
, the module
\widetilde{V}
has a one dimensional weight space with weight
\mu
which is spanned by the vector

, because if we have any

which has the same weight as

, then

. So,

z\otimes w=1\otimes(z\cdot w)=\chi_{\mu,r}(z)(1\otimes w).

Since

generates
\widetilde{V}
as an

-module and any submodule of
\widetilde{V}
is a direct sum of its weight spaces, a proper submodule of

cannot contain vectors of weight

. Therefore, there exists a unique maximal proper submodule of
\widetilde{V}
, implying that
\widetilde{V}
has a unique simple quotient. The above implies that

is indeed that quotient as it was assumed to be simple.

However, another simple quotient of
\widetilde{V}
is given by

, where we define:

\mu^{\prime}:=\mu-\omega_{r}.

(Since

, it is clear that

, and thus,

is a well-defined dominant weight.) To see that this is a quotient of
\widetilde{V}
, we note that Proposition
2.14
implies that

is generated by a mirabolic highest weight vector

having weight

and depth

. By Proposition

, the

-action on the vectors

and

M\cong L_{\mu^{\prime},r}.

∎

As a consequence of the above proposition, we state another characterization of depth of a module

Remark 2.28

The

Lemma 2.30

Let

-module of depth

(1)

(2)

3.
Proof of Proposition
2.25

We retain the notation from the previous section. Any element in
MU(n)
can be expressed as a linear combination of elements of the form:

RQ_{k}(f)P_{k}(e)\ell\cdot\cdots\ell Q_{1}(f)P_{1}(e)\ell Q_{0}(f)P_{0}(e),

where each
P_{i}(e)
(resp.
Q_{i}(f)
) is a non-commutative monomial in the

’s (resp
f_{j}
’s) and

is a monomial in the
k_{j}
’s and
k_{j}^{-1}
’s. In what follows, we’ll denote by a ‘block’ the expression occurring between two successive occurrences of the element

in the above expression. So, each block is of the form

for some

.

We claim that each such monomial, that has weight zero, acts on

by a scalar that is completely determined by the weight
\lambda
and

. Without loss of generality, we will assume that

because it acts by a scalar on weight spaces. Similarly, we will assume that
P_{0}(e)=1
as

is a highest weight vector. The proof of our claim will be based on induction on the degree of the monomial, that is, the length of the monomial in terms of the generators
e_{i}
’s,
f_{j}
’s and
\ell
.

We start with a couple of lemmas.

Lemma 3.1
.

Consider an element of the form
m=P(e)\ell
in
MU(n)
, where
P(e)
is some non-commutative monomial in the
e_{i}
’s. Suppose
P(e)
is not of the form
e_{s}e_{s-1}\cdots e_{2}e_{1}
for some
s
. Then, it is possible to express

as a linear combination of monomials of the form
P^{\prime}(e)\ell Q^{\prime}(e)
(having the same degree as

) for some non-constant monomial
Q^{\prime}
.

What the above lemma is essentially saying is that if the monomial
P
is not of the specified form, then it is possible to commute one or more of the
e_{j}
’s past the
\ell
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the claim when
P(e)
is of the form
e_{s}e_{k}e_{k-1}\cdots e_{2}e_{1}
, where
s\neq k+1
.

We first treat the cases
k=0
and
k=1
. If
k=0
, we just get that
m=e_{s}\ell=\ell e_{s}
as

, and we’re done. If
k=1
, we get
m=e_{s}e_{1}\ell
. If
s>2
, we can commute the
e_{s}
past both

and
\ell
. If
s=1
, we get
m=e_{1}^{2}\ell=(v^{2}+1)e_{1}\ell e_{1}-v^{2}\ell e_{1}^{2}
, which is of the required form.

Now, suppose
k>1
. We use induction on

. If

, we can commute
e_{s}
past all the other elements in

to get the required claim. If

, we can commute
e_{s}
past

and be done by induction. The cases that remain are

and

If

m=e_{k}^{2}e_{k-1}\cdots e_{2}e_{1}\ell.

By using
e_{k}^{2}e_{k-1}=(v+v^{-1})e_{k}e_{k-1}e_{k}-e_{k-1}e_{k}^{2}

in both the above terms, following which we can commute the

If

m=e_{k-1}e_{k}e_{k-1}\cdots e_{2}e_{1}\ell.

First, we use
e_{k-1}e_{k}e_{k-1}=\dfrac{1}{v+v^{-1}}(e_{k-1}^{2}e_{k}+e_{k}e_{k-1}^{2})

∎

Suppose

First, we suppose that

Lemma 3.5

Suppose

\cdots f_{i_{k}}\ell f_{1}f_{2}\cdots f_{r-1}f_{r}\cdot y.

\cdots\ell f_{1}f_{2}\cdots f_{i_{k}}f_{i_{k}-1}f_{i_{k}}\cdots f_{r}\cdot y.

\cdots f_{i_{k}}^{2}\ell f_{1}f_{2}\cdots f_{i_{k}-2}f_{i_{k}-1}f_{i_{k}+1}\cdots f_{r}\cdot y.

\ell f_{1}f_{2}\cdots f_{i_{k}-1}

\ \cdots\ell f_{1}f_{2}\cdots f_{i_{k}-1}f_{i_{k}}^{2}f_{i_{k}+1}\cdots f_{r}\cdot y.

Next, we repeat the same procedure by bringing

\cdots\ell f_{1}f_{2}\cdots f_{i_{k-1}-1}f_{i_{k-1}}^{2}f_{i_{k-1}+1}\cdots f_{i_{k}-1}f_{i_{k}}^{2}f_{i_{k}+1}\cdots f_{r}\cdot y.

\cdots f_{1}\ell f_{1}\cdots.

Finally, we deal with the case

\ell\cdot y^{\prime}=\ell e_{r}e_{r-1}\cdots e_{1}\ell f_{1}\cdots f_{r-1}f_{r}\cdot y=\ell e_{r}e_{r-1}\cdots e_{1}f_{1}\cdots f_{r-1}f_{r}\cdot y

4.
Semisimplicity of
MU(n)

In this section, we prove that the category of finite dimensional
MU(n)
-modules is semisimple. This is equivalent to showing that there are no non-trivial extensions of
MU(n)
-modules between any of the simple modules. As noted earlier, we can restrict our attention to simple modules of Type
\mathbb{1}
.

We recall the construction of the standard modules for the usual quantum group
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
. For any weight
\lambda
, there exists a universal highest weight module or Verma module
M_{\lambda}
. Furthermore, this module is simple if and only if
\lambda
is not a dominant weight. If
\lambda
is a dominant weight, then this module has a unique finite dimensional quotient, which exactly happens to be the simple module
L_{\lambda}
having highest weight
\lambda
.

We define the space
M_{\lambda,r}:=M_{\lambda}\otimes W_{r}
, which is naturally an
MU(n)
-module via the comodule map. When
\lambda
is a dominant weight, the simple module
L_{\lambda,r}
is a finite dimensional simple quotient of
M_{\lambda,r}
. The key to proving semisimplicity will be to show that this is the unique finite dimensional quotient of
M_{\lambda,r}
, which we will establish now.

The module
M_{\lambda,r}
has a unique (up to scaling) vector of weight
\lambda+\omega_{r}
which is given by
x_{\lambda,r}:=x_{\lambda}\otimes u
, where
x_{\lambda}
is the unique (up to scaling) highest weight vector of the
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
-module
M_{\lambda}
of weight
\lambda
and
u=u_{1}\wedge u_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{r}\in W_{r}
.

Let
\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n-1}\}
denote the standard choice of positive simple roots for the type
A_{n}
root system. That is, we let
\alpha_{i}=(0,\dots,0,1,-1,0,\dots,0
, where
1
and
-1
are in the
i^{th}
and
(i+1)^{th}
spots respectively.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose
\lambda
is a dominant weight and
M=M_{\lambda,r}/I
is a finite dimensional quotient of the module
M_{\lambda,r}
, where
I
is some left submodule of
M_{\lambda,r}
. Then, for all
i
, we have that
(f_{i}^{m_{i}+1}\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u\in I
, where
m_{i}=2(\lambda,\alpha_{i})/(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i})
.

Proof.

For any
f_{i}
such that
i\neq r
, we have that
f_{i}\cdot u=0
and so,
f_{i}^{t}\cdot(x_{\lambda}\otimes u)=(f_{i}^{t}\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u
for all
t
. Then, by
[
Jan96
, Lemma 5.4(b)]
, we get that
(f_{i}^{m_{i}+1}\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u=f_{i}^{m_{i}+1}\cdot(x_{\lambda}\otimes u)\in I

Next, consider the action of
f_{r}
on
x_{\lambda}\otimes u
. Again by
[
Jan96
, Lemma 5.4(b)]
, we get that
f_{r}^{m+1}\cdot(x_{\lambda}\otimes u)\in I
, where
m=2(\lambda+\omega_{r},\alpha_{r})/(\alpha_{r},\alpha_{r})=m_{r}+1
. We’ll show that by acting some element of
MU(n)
on
f_{r}^{m+1}\cdot(x_{\lambda}\otimes u)
, we can construct
(f_{r}^{m_{r}+1}\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u
.

Recall that
f_{r}
acts on the tensor product
M_{\lambda}\otimes W_{r}
via
k_{r}^{-1}\otimes f_{r}+f_{r}\otimes 1
. Also, it is clear that
f_{r}\cdot u=u_{1}\wedge u_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{r-1}\wedge u_{r+1}=:u^{\prime}
and that
f_{r}^{2}\cdot u=0
. Thus,
f_{r}^{m+1}\cdot u
is equal to:

(f_{r}^{m+1}\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u+\sum_{i=0}^{m}(f_{r}^{m-i}k_{r}^{-1}f_{r}^{i}\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u^{\prime}

=(f_{r}^{m+1}\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u+c_{m}(f_{r}^{m}k_{r}^{-1}\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u^{\prime},

where
c_{m}=1+v^{2}+\cdots+v^{2m}.

Next, we act on this element by the product
\ell f_{1}f_{2}\cdots f_{r-1}
. For
i<r
, it is clear that
f_{i}\cdot u=0
. Therefore,

\ell f_{1}f_{2}\cdots f_{r-1}\cdot((f_{r}^{m+1}\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u)=\ell\cdot((f_{1}f_{2}\cdots f_{r-1}f_{r}^{m+1}\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u),

which is zero because
\ell\cdot u=0
. Hence, the first term of the above sum doesn’t survive when we act by
\ell f_{1}f_{2}\cdots f_{r-1}
. Next, when we act on the second term, each
f_{i}
acts via
k_{i}^{-1}\otimes f_{i}+f_{i}\otimes 1
. Here, we can ignore the
f_{i}\otimes 1
part, because that will not survive in the end when we act by
\ell
, and so, we get up to a scalar multiple:

(f_{r}^{m}\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u^{\prime\prime},

where
u^{\prime\prime}=u_{2}\wedge u_{3}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{r+1}
. Next, we act on this element by
e_{r}e_{r-1}\cdots e_{1}
. Each
e_{i}
acts on the tensor product via
1\otimes e_{i}+e_{i}\otimes k_{i}
. Next, we note that for all
i
, we have that
e_{i}\cdot(f_{r}^{m}\cdot x_{\lambda})=0
. This is clear when
i\neq r
since
e_{i}
commutes with
f_{r}
. When
i=r
, this follows from the fact that
m=m_{r}+1
using the computation in
[
Jan96
, Lemma 5.6]
. So, we get that

e_{r}e_{r-1}\cdots e_{1}\cdot((f_{r}^{m}\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u^{\prime\prime})=(f_{r}^{m}\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes(e_{r}e_{r-1}\cdots e_{1}\cdot u^{\prime\prime}),

which is exactly equal to
(f_{r}^{m}\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u=(f_{r}^{m_{r}+1}\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u,
completing the proof.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose
\lambda
is a dominant weight and
M=M_{\lambda,r}/I
is a non-zero finite dimensional quotient of the module
M_{\lambda,r}
, where
I
is some left submodule of
M_{\lambda,r}
. Then, we have the equality
M=L_{\lambda,r}
.

Proof.

Let
I^{\prime}
by the
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
-submodule of
M_{\lambda}
generated by
f_{i}^{m_{i}+1}\cdot x_{\lambda}
for all

. Then, by Proposition 5.9 and Theorem 5.15 of
[
Jan96
]
, we have that
M_{\lambda}/I^{\prime}=L_{\lambda}
.

By the previous lemma, we know that the submodule

contains the elements
x_{i}=(f_{i}^{m_{i}+1}\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes u
for all
i
. Each
e_{j}
acts by zero on each
x_{i}
: This is clear when
i\neq j
since
e_{i}
commutes with
f_{j}
. When

[
Jan96
, Lemma 5.6]
.

Therefore, the vector

is a highest weight vector. Then, using an argument similar to the one in the proof of Lemma

, we get that
(f_{i}^{m_{i}+1}\cdot x_{\lambda})\otimes W_{r}\subseteq I
for all

. This, in turn, implies that

. Therefore, we have the following surjection of

-modules:

L_{\lambda,r}=L_{\lambda}\otimes W_{r}=\frac{M_{\lambda}}{I^{\prime}}\otimes W_{r}=\frac{M_{\lambda}\otimes W_{r}}{I^{\prime}\otimes W_{r}}\twoheadrightarrow\frac{M_{\lambda}\otimes W_{r}}{I}=\frac{M_{\lambda,r}}{I}=M.

As
L_{\lambda,r}
is simple and

M=L_{\lambda,r}
.

Apart from the above proposition, we need one more tool for the proof of simplicity. We recall the anti-involution ‘
\star
’ on
MU(n)
:

\ell^{\star}=\ell,\quad(k_{i}^{\pm 1})^{\star}=k_{i}^{\pm 1},\quad e_{i}^{\star}=f_{i}\quad f_{i}^{\star}=e_{i}.

Using this anti-involution, we get that for any finite dimensional
MU(n)
-module

, there is a natural
MU(n)
-action on the dual space
V^{*}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
V
is a finite dimensional simple

-module, then
V\cong V^{*}
.

Proof.

Note that if
V
is simple, so is
V^{*}
. Also, as ‘
\star
’ acts via the identity on the

’s and
\ell

and

have the same
(k_{i},\ell)
-weight space decomposition. Hence, to prove the lemma, it suffices to show that if
(\lambda,r)\neq(\mu,s)
, then the simple modules
L_{\lambda,r}
and
L_{\mu,s}
do not have the same weight space decomposition. This follows from the proof of Proposition
2.16
.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

The category of finite dimensional
MU(n)
-modules is semisimple.

Proof.

(This proof adapts the arguments of Theorem 5.17 of
[
Jan96
]
.) Pick any two simple modules
L_{\lambda,r}
and
L_{\mu,s}
. Suppose we have a short exact sequence of
MU(n)
-modules:

0\rightarrow L_{\mu,s}\xrightarrow{i}M\xrightarrow{\pi}L_{\lambda,r}\rightarrow 0.

Proving the theorem is equivalent to showing that the above sequence splits. Let
x_{\lambda,r}\in L_{\lambda,r}
be the mirabolic highest vector
x_{\lambda}\otimes u
. Pick
x^{\prime}\in M
such that
\pi(x^{\prime})=x_{\lambda,r}
. Then,
x^{\prime}
has the same weight as
x_{\lambda,r}
. Suppose
e_{i}\cdot x^{\prime}=0
for all
i
. Then, the module
M^{\prime}=MU(n)\cdot x^{\prime}\subseteq M
must be a quotient of
M_{\lambda^{\prime},r^{\prime}}
for some
(\lambda^{\prime},r^{\prime})
. Furthermore, as
\pi(M^{\prime})=L_{\lambda,r}
, we get by Proposition
4.2
that
(\lambda^{\prime},r^{\prime})=(\lambda,r)
and
M^{\prime}\cong L_{\lambda,r}
. Thus,
\ker(\pi)\cap M^{\prime}=0
, and so,
M=M^{\prime}\oplus\ker(\pi)
, giving us the required splitting.

Next, suppose
e_{i}\cdot x^{\prime}\neq 0
for some
i
. Then, as the weight of
x_{\lambda,r}
is
\lambda+\omega_{r}
, the vector
e_{i}\cdot x^{\prime}
is a non-zero vector of weight
\lambda+\omega_{r}+\alpha_{i}
. As the above exact sequence preserves weight spaces, we conclude that
L_{\mu,s}
has a non-zero weight space with weight
\lambda+\omega_{r}+\alpha_{i}
. This allows us to conclude that
\lambda+\omega_{r}<\mu+\omega_{s}
. Hence, the above proof works as long as this inequality doesn’t hold. If it does, we dualize the above sequence to get an exact sequence:

0\rightarrow L_{\lambda,r}^{*}\xrightarrow{\pi^{*}}M^{*}\xrightarrow{i^{*}}L_{\mu,s}^{*}\rightarrow 0.

We can now use the previous lemma and repeat the argument above to get that this sequence splits, which, in turn, implies that the original sequence splits, completing the proof.
∎

Proposition 4.5

Let

be a finite dimensional

-module, then its decomposition as a direct sum of simples is uniquely determined by the dimension of its mirabolic weight spaces
V_{\mu,\epsilon}:=\{x\in V~|~k_{i}\cdot x=v^{\mu_{i}-\mu_{i+1}}x,~\ell\cdot x=\epsilon x\}

Let
\lambda
be a maximal weight of

as a

-module (i.e. as weight spaces for the

\displaystyle L_{\lambda,0}

\displaystyle=L_{\lambda}\otimes W_{0},

\displaystyle L_{\lambda-\omega_{1},1}

\displaystyle=L_{\lambda-\omega_{1}}\otimes W_{1},

\displaystyle L_{\lambda-\omega_{n-1},n-1}

\displaystyle=L_{\lambda-\omega_{n-1}}\otimes W_{n-1},

\displaystyle L_{\lambda,n}

\displaystyle=L_{\lambda}\otimes W_{n};

where the ones where
\lambda-\omega_{r}
is not a dominant weight are not defined, and the ones where
\lambda-\omega_{r}
is a dominant weight are precisely the simple modules for

with the highest

We consider the extremal weight spaces for the

Consider an

(5.2)

\displaystyle e^{2}

(5.5)

\displaystyle e\tau_{i}

\displaystyle=\tau_{i}e

\displaystyle i\geq 2

(5.6)

\displaystyle e\tau_{1}e\tau_{1}

defined above at

Main theorem

Definition 5.7

Finally, we construct the pullback map from the specialization of the usual quantum tensor space
V_{n,d}
to that of the mirabolic tensor space
MV_{n,d}
:

\phi:\mathbb{C}(\mathcal{F}(n,d)\times\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d})/B)^{\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d})}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}(\mathcal{F}(n,d)\times\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d})/B\times V)^{\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d})}

such that for any

\phi(\gamma)(F,F^{\prime},v):=\gamma(F,F^{\prime}).

The space

has a basis given by the set
\{m_{\mathbf{r}}:\mathbf{r}\in S_{row}\}
, and each of these basis vectors can be viewed as an

\phi
is given by:

\phi(m_{\mathbf{r}})=\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in J_{\mathbf{r}}}m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}}.

That is, the map
\phi
sends an

The following lemma follows immediately from the definition of convolution.

Lemma 5.12
.

The embedding
\phi
is equivariant under the
U(n,d)
-left action and the
H_{d}
-right action, with respect to the maps
i
and
j
respectively. More precisely, for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}(\mathcal{F}(n,d)\times\mathbb{C}(\mathcal{F}(n,d))^{\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d})},

\beta\in\mathbb{C}(\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d})/B\times\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d})/B)^{\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d})}
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}(\mathcal{F}(n,d)\times\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d})/B)^{\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d})}
, we have the equalities:

\phi(\alpha*\gamma)=i(\alpha)*\phi(\gamma),

\phi(\gamma*\beta)=\phi(\gamma)*j(\beta).

Lemma 5.13
.

On the image of
\phi
in
MV_{n,d}
, we have that both
\ell\in MU(n)
and
e\in MH_{d}
act by
1
.

Proof.

Note that:

\displaystyle\phi(m_{\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in J_{\mathbf{r}}}m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{j}\in J_{\mathbf{r}}\\
1\in\mathbf{j}\end{subarray}}m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{j}\in J_{\mathbf{r}}\\
1\not\in\mathbf{j},~\forall~j\in\mathbf{j}~r_{1}>r_{j}\end{subarray}}m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{j}\in J_{\mathbf{r}}\\
1\not\in\mathbf{j},~\exists~j\in\mathbf{j}\text{ s.t. }r_{1}\leq r_{j}\end{subarray}}m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{j}\in J_{\mathbf{r}}\\
1\in\mathbf{j}\end{subarray}}(m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}}+m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}\setminus\{1\}})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{j}\in J_{\mathbf{r}}\\
1\not\in\mathbf{j},~\exists~j\in\mathbf{j}\text{ s.t. }r_{1}\leq r_{j}\end{subarray}}m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}},

which lies in the
1
-eigenspace of
e
by Corollary
5.9
.

Next, let
J_{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
be the set of all decorations
\mathbf{j}
of
m_{\mathbf{r}}
such that
r_{j}\neq 1
for any
j\in\mathbf{j}
. Then, we can write:

\displaystyle\phi(m_{\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in J_{\mathbf{r}}}m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in J_{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}}\Big(m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}}+\sum\limits_{k>\max\mathbf{j},~r_{k}=1}m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}\cup\{k\}}\Big).

To show that
\ell
acts on
\phi(m_{\mathbf{r}})
by
1
, it suffices to show that it acts by
1
on each summand above. To see this, we compute for any
\mathbf{j}\in J_{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\ell\cdot\Big(m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}}+\sum\limits_{k>\max\mathbf{j},~r_{k}=1}m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}\cup\{k\}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\ell\cdot m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}}+\sum\limits_{k>\max\mathbf{j},~r_{k}=1}\ell\cdot m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}\cup\{k\}}

\displaystyle=\ell\cdot m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}}+\sum\limits_{k>\max\mathbf{j},~r_{k}=1}v^{2\#\{i<k~|~r_{i}=1\}}(v^{2}-1)\ell\cdot m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}}\qquad(\text{By Corollary~\ref{cor:lconse}})

\displaystyle=\Big(1+(v^{2}-1)\sum_{r=0}^{\#\{k>\max\mathbf{j}~|~r_{k}=1\}-1}v^{2r}\Big)\ell\cdot m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}}

\displaystyle=v^{2\#\{k>\max\mathbf{j}~|~r_{k}=1\}}\ell\cdot m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}}

\displaystyle=v^{2\#\{k>\max\mathbf{j}~|~r_{k}=1\}}\Big(v^{-2\#\{i>\max\mathbf{j}~|~r_{i}=1\}}\left(m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}}+\sum\limits_{k>\max\mathbf{j},~r_{k}=1}m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}\cup\{k\}}\right)\Big)

\displaystyle=m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}}+\sum\limits_{k>\max\mathbf{j},~r_{k}=1}m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}\cup\{k\}},

completing the proof.
∎

Lemma
5.13
says that under the map
\phi:V_{n,d}\to MV_{n,d}
of left
U(n)
-modules, the isotypic component of
L_{\lambda}
maps into the isotypic component of
\pi^{*}_{1}L_{\lambda}=L_{(\lambda,\emptyset)}
as
\ell
acts by 1 on the image. Similarly, considered as right

-modules, the isotypic component of

exactly goes to the isotypic component of
M^{(\lambda,\emptyset)}
as none of the Jucys-Murphy elements act by zero on the image (since
e
does not).

But then, Theorem
5.11
tells us that the isotypic components of
L_{\lambda}
and
M^{\lambda}
in

are actually the same. Hence, in the mirabolic tensor space
MV_{n,d}
, the
MU(n)
-representation

exactly pairs up with the

-representation

, which proves the following:

Proposition 5.14
.

Let
\lambda
be any partition of

with at most

\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda,\emptyset)
. Then, the isotypic component of

-module

.

Before we end this section, we state a partial generalization of Lemma

that will be used in the proof of Theorem
5.8
. Fix

, and define the map

\phi_{k}:\mathbb{C}(\mathcal{F}(n,d)\times\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d})/B)^{\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d})}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}(\mathcal{F}(n,d)\times\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d})/B\times V)^{\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d})}

\phi(\gamma)(F,F^{\prime},v):=\begin{cases}\gamma(F,F^{\prime})&\text{if }v\in F_{k}\\

(Recall that

is an

, which can be viewed as a collection of vector spaces

.) The map

\displaystyle\phi_{k}:V_{n,d}

\displaystyle\to MV_{n,d}

\displaystyle m_{\mathbf{r}}

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in J_{\mathbf{r}}(k)}m_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{j}}.

The proof of the following lemma follows by the definition of convolution.

Lemma 5.15
.

The embedding
\phi_{k}
is equivariant under the right
H_{d}
-action.

Next, we look at the action of
MU(n)
on the image of
\phi_{k}.
Henceforth, suppose that

.

Lemma 5.16
.

The elements
1-\ell,(1-\ell)e_{1},(1-\ell)e_{1}e_{2},\dots,(1-\ell)e_{1}e_{2}\dots e_{k-1}
of the mirabolic quantum group

act by zero on
\textrm{Im}(\phi_{k})\subseteq MV_{n,d}
.

Consequently,
\textrm{Im}(\phi_{k})
lies in the span of the isotypic components of those

’s that have co-depth at least
k
(or depth at most

Proof.

The proof of the lemma is implied by the following two assertions:

(a)

The idempotent
\ell
acts by 1 on
\textrm{Im}(\phi_{k})
.

(b)

The map
\phi_{k}
is equivariant with respect to the left action of the elements
e_{1},e_{2},\cdots,e_{k-1}
on
V_{n,d}
and
MV_{n,d}
.

The first assertion follows by repeating the proof of the same assertion in Lemma
5.13
. To prove the second assertion, note that we have an embedding:

V_{k,d}\to MV_{k,d},

which is equivariant under the left action of the quantum group
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{k})
by Lemma
5.12

\phi_{k}
applied to any

gives the same result as this embedding after appending
n-k

∎

5.5.
Proof of Mirabolic quantum Schur-Weyl duality as left modules

In this section, we prove the following proposition, which is the first step towards the proof of Theorem
5.8
and is a generalization of
[
Ros18
, Theorem 6.9]
:

Proposition 5.17
.

As a left
MU(n)
-module, we have a decomposition of the mirabolic tensor space:

(5.10)

MV_{n,d}=\bigoplus_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in M\Lambda_{n,d}}L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\otimes M^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}.

Here,
M\Lambda_{n,d}=\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda,1^{s}):|\lambda|+s=d,s\leq n\text{ and }\lambda\text{ has }\leq n\text{ parts}\}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.5
, it suffices to verify that both spaces above have the same
(k_{i},l)
-weight spaces. Recall that for any
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
-module
M
, we define its character
\chi(M)
:

\chi(M):=\sum_{\mu}\dim(M_{\mu})v^{\mu},

where
M_{\mu}
denotes the weight space of
M
with weight
\mu.
(Note that for any
i
, we have
\mathrm{Tr}(k_{i}|_{M})=\sum_{\mu}\dim(M_{\mu})v^{\mu_{i}-\mu_{i+1}}=\sum_{\mu}\dim(M_{\mu})v^{\mu(\alpha_{i})}
.)

In order to show that the two sides of Equation (
5.10
) have the same
k_{i}
-weight spaces, it suffices to show that their characters are equal. To this end, we recall the decomposition from the usual quantum Schur-Weyl duality (see Theorem
5.11
):

V_{n,d}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{n,d}}L_{\lambda}\otimes M^{\lambda},

where
\Lambda_{n,d}
is the set of all partitions
\lambda
of
d
with at most
n
parts. Equating the characters
\chi
of both sides, we have the equality:

(5.11)

\chi(V_{n,d})=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{n,d}}\chi(L_{\lambda})f^{\lambda},

where
f^{\lambda}=\dim(M^{\lambda})
. Since the space
V_{n,d}
is spanned by
n\times d
matrices, we can compute the character on the left explicitly. An
n\times d
matrix has weight
\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots,\mu_{n})
with respect to the
k_{i}
’s exactly when there there are
\mu_{i}
1’s in the
i^{th}
row, and so, the number of such matrices is
{d\choose\mu_{1}!\mu_{2}!\cdots\mu_{n}!}=:{d\choose\mu}
. Therefore, we have:

(5.12)

\chi(V_{n,d})=\sum_{\mu}{d\choose\mu}v^{\mu},

where the sum ranges over all
\mu
such that
\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\cdots+\mu_{n}=d
.
By a similar argument, we can compute the character of the mirabolic tensor space using the decorated matrix basis. A decorated matrix with
k
decorations and weight
\mu
can be chosen by deciding which
k
columns and rows the decorated
1
’s will be in, and then distributing the other

ones arbitrarily. Therefore,

\chi(MV_{n,d})=\sum_{\mu}\sum_{k=0}^{\min(n,d)}\sum_{\epsilon}{d-k\choose\mu-\epsilon}{d\choose k}v^{\mu}.

Here, the first sum ranges over all
\mu
such that
\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\cdots+\mu_{n}=d
.
The last sum ranges over all
\epsilon\in\{0,1\}^{n}
with exactly
k
entries equal to 1, such that all the entries of
\mu-\epsilon
are non-negative, and corresponds to the rows in which the decorations exist. The above sum can be rewritten as:

\chi(MV_{n,d})=\sum_{\mu}\sum_{k=0}^{\min(n,d)}\sum_{\epsilon}{d-k\choose\mu}{d\choose k}v^{\mu+\epsilon},

where
\mu
ranges over all
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots,\mu_{n})
such that
\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\cdots+\mu_{n}=d-k
and
\epsilon
ranges over the entire set of sequences in
\{0,1\}^{n}
with
k
entries equal to
1
. Then, we can write:

\displaystyle\chi(MV_{n,d})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu}\sum_{k=0}^{\min(n,d)}\sum_{\epsilon}{d-k\choose\mu}{d\choose k}v^{\mu+\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\min(n,d)}\sum_{\mu}{d-k\choose\mu}v^{\mu}\sum_{\epsilon}{d\choose k}v^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\min(n,d)}{d\choose k}\chi(V_{n,d-k})\sum_{\epsilon}v^{\epsilon},

where the last equality follows from Equation (
5.12
). Next, we compute the character of the right side of Equation (
5.10
).

\displaystyle\chi(\bigoplus_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in M\Lambda_{n,d}}L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\otimes M^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in M\Lambda_{n,d}}\chi(L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\dim(M^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\min(n,d)}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{n,d-k}}\chi(L_{\lambda}\otimes W_{k})\dim(M^{(\lambda,1^{k})})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\min(n,d)}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{n,d-k}}\chi(L_{\lambda})\chi(W_{k})\dim(M^{\lambda}){d\choose k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\min(n,d)}{d\choose k}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{n,d-k}}\chi(L_{\lambda})f^{\lambda}\sum_{\epsilon}v^{\epsilon}.

Here, we’re using the fact that
\chi(W_{k})=\sum_{\epsilon}v^{\epsilon}
where
\epsilon
ranges over the entire set of sequences in
\{0,1\}^{n}
with
k
entries equal to
1
. The equality of the two character expressions obtained above follows from Equation (
5.11
).

In order to complete the proof of the proposition, we do a slightly refined version of the above computation in order to compute the character of both sides of Equation (
5.10
) when restricting to the
\ell=1
eigenspace. (The
\ell=1
eigenspace isn’t necessarily a
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
-representation, but it is still preserved under the action of the
k_{i}
’s since they commute with
\ell
.) We get almost the same character expressions for both sides, with the only difference being that
\epsilon
ranges over the set of sequences in
\{0,1\}^{n}
with
k
entries equal to
1
and first entry necessarily being
0
. This shows that the
(k_{i},\ell=1)
-eigenspaces of both sides of Equation (
5.10
) have the same dimension, and this completes the proof.
∎

Definition 5.19
.

Let

and fix

. We define the

Supp_{k}(m_{\mathbf{r}}):=\{j\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}:r_{i}=j\text{ for some }1\leq i\leq k\}.

For any

for some scalars

It follows that

Lemma 5.20
.

Let
W_{k}
denote the simultaneous zero eigenspace for
L_{1},L_{2},\dots,L_{k}
in
MV_{n,d}
. Then, a basis for
W_{k}
is given by the elements
\textbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\textbf{r}},\widetilde{\textbf{j}}}
. Furthermore, if

, the elements
L_{1},L_{2},\dots,L_{k^{\prime}}
do not have a simultaneous zero eigenvector in
MV_{n,d}
.

The action of the Jucys-Murphy elements being zero on
\textbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\textbf{r}},\widetilde{\textbf{j}}}
follows from Lemma
5.18
. Linear independence is obvious. The hard part is to show that they indeed span
W_{k}.
Before we prove the lemma, we prove two special cases (Lemmas

and
5.22
), and then show that the general case can be reduced to these two cases.

Lemma 5.21
.

Let
W_{k}
denote the simultaneous zero eigenspace for
L_{1},L_{2},\dots,L_{k}
in
MV_{k,k}
. Then, the space
W_{k}
is one-dimensional and is spanned by the vector
\textbf{s}_{S,\emptyset,\emptyset}
, where
S=\{1,2,\dots,k\}
.

Proof.

In the space
MV_{k,k}
, the space
W_{k}
is exactly the isotypic component of the one-dimensional (zero)
MH_{k}
-representation corresponding to the bipartition
(0,1^{k})
. By Proposition
5.17
, the only
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
’s that pair with a one-dimensional representation of
MH_{k}
correspond to
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(k,0)
,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(1^{k},0)
or
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(0,1^{k})
. Each of the
L_{i}
’s act with non-zero eigenvalues in the first two cases by Proposition
5.14
. Thus, the zero representation of
MH_{k}
must pair with the
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
when
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(0,1^{k})
, which is itself a one-dimensional representation. Hence, the space
W_{k}
is one dimensional and is spanned by the vector
\textbf{s}_{S,\emptyset,\emptyset}
, where
S=\{1,2,\dots,k\}
.
∎

Lemma 5.22
.

Let
W_{k}
denote the simultaneous zero eigenspace for
L_{1},L_{2},\dots,L_{k}
in
MV_{k,k+1}
. Then, the space
W_{k}
is
k
-dimensional and is spanned by the vectors
\textbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\textbf{r}},\widetilde{\textbf{j}}}
where
S=\{1,2,\dots,k\}
, arbitrary
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}
chosen from
\{1,2,\dots,k\}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}=\emptyset
. Furthermore, the element
L_{k+1}
does not have a zero eigenvector in
W_{k}
.

In particular, each of these basis vectors has
k
-support of size
k
and no summands with decorations in the last column.

Proof.

We first show that all the
k+1
Jucys-Murphy elements can not simultaneously act by zero on any non-zero vector of
M_{k,k+1}
. Any such non-zero vector would span the one-dimensional zero representation of
MH_{k+1}
. By Proposition
5.17
, the only
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
’s in
MV_{k,k+1}
that pair with one-dimensional
MH_{k+1}
-representations correspond to
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(k+1,0)
. However, by Proposition
5.14
, none of the Jucys-Murphy elements act by zero on the isotypic component of this
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
. This proves the required claim.

For the rest of the lemma, we proceed by induction on
k
with the base case being the same as the base case in the proof of Lemma
5.20
. The action of the first
k
Jucys-Murphy elements doesn’t affect anything in the last (
(k+1)^{th}
) column. Hence, we can write a decomposition of the space
W_{k}
depending on where the
1
exists in the last column and whether it is decorated or not. More concretely, we can write:

W_{k}=\bigoplus_{\theta}{W_{\theta}},

where
\theta
ranges over pairs
(i,j)
where
i\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
denotes the position of the 1 in the last column and
j
is
1
or
0
depending on where the last column is decorated or not respectively.

For pairs of the form
\theta=(i,0)
, the space
W_{\theta}
can be embedded into the tensor space
MV_{k,k}
by simply chopping off the last column. This embedding respects the
MH_{k}
-action. In particular, the space
W_{\theta}
lies in the simultaneous zero eigenspace of the first
k
Jucys-Murphy elements in
MV_{k,k}
. By the previous lemma, this space is one-dimensional showing that
\dim(W_{\theta})=1
. Thus, we have:

\sum_{\theta=(i,0)}\dim(W_{\theta})=k,

and so, to prove the lemma, it suffices to show that
W_{\theta}=0
whenever
\theta=(i,1)
for any
i
.

So, suppose
\theta=(i,1)
. On the space
W_{\theta}
, the first
k-1
(in fact, the first
k
) Jucys-Murphy elements act by zero. Since these Jucys-Murphy elements can not distinguish between the last two columns, by induction, any decorations in the last two columns must lie in a row above the
(k-1)
-support of a simultaneous zero eigenvector for the first
k-1
Jucys-Murphy elements. Thus, the only possible location for a decoration in the last column is in the first row, and so, we have that
W_{\theta}=0
unless
i=1
.

Finally, we suppose that
\theta=(1,1)
. The
(k-1)
-support of any non-zero element of
W_{\theta}
must be exactly
\{k,k-1,\dots,2\}
. By induction, since
L_{1},L_{2},\dots,L_{k}
do not have a simultaneous zero eigenvector in
MV_{k-1,k}
, the
1
in the
k^{th}
column of (any summand of) a non-zero element of
W_{\theta}
must lie in the first row. Thus, the space
W_{\theta}
is at most
1
-dimensional and (if it is non-zero) is spanned by the vector
\mathbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\mathbf{r}},\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}}
with
S=\{k,k-1,\dots,2\}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}=\{1,1\}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}=\{k+1\}
. We claim that
\mathbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\mathbf{r}},\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}}\cdot L_{k}\neq 0
, which will show that
W_{\theta}=0
.

To this end, we recall that
L_{k}=v^{-2}\tau_{k-1}L_{k-1}\tau_{k-1}
. Thus, if
\mathbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\mathbf{r}},\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}}\cdot L_{k}=0
, we must have
\mathbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\mathbf{r}},\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}}\cdot\tau_{k-1}L_{k-1}=0
. Furthemore, since
L_{j}
commutes with
\tau_{k-1}
for all

, we have that
\mathbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\mathbf{r}},\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}}\cdot\tau_{k-1}L_{j}=0
. Therefore,
\mathbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\mathbf{r}},\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}}\cdot\tau_{k-1}
is a simultaneous zero eigenvector of
L_{1},L_{2},\dots,L_{k-1}
, and thus,
\mathbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\mathbf{r}},\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}}\cdot\tau_{k-1}
must lie in
W_{\theta}
. However, since
W_{\theta}
is at most one-dimensional, this implies that
\mathbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\mathbf{r}},\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}}
must be an eigenvector for
\tau_{k-1}
. That this is not true follows immediately from the explicit formula for
\mathbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\mathbf{r}},\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}}
. (For example, the action of
\tau_{k-1}
pushes the

We prove the claim using induction on

Corollary 5.23

We have the following equalities:

(5.14)

\dim(W_{k})=\sum_{t=0}^{\min(n,d)-k}{n\choose k+t}{d-k\choose t}n^{d-k-t},

(5.15)

\dim(\ell\cdot W_{k})=\sum_{t=0}^{\min(n,d)-k}{n-1\choose k+t}{d-k\choose t}n^{d-k-t}.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.20
, the space
W_{k}
has a basis given by elements of the form
\textbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\textbf{r}},\widetilde{\textbf{j}}}
where
S\in[n]_{k}
,
\widetilde{\textbf{r}}\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}^{d-k}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}
is a decoration of
m_{\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}}
, such that all decorations are in rows above the ones indexed by
S
. To count the number of these triples
(S,\widetilde{\mathbf{r}},\widetilde{\mathbf{j}})
, suppose we assume that
|\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}|=t
for some
t
. Then, we can select the subset
S
as well as the
t
rows that are being decorated in
{n\choose k+t}
ways and the
t
columns being decorated in
{d-k\choose t}
ways. The remaining
d-t-k
entries of
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}
can be chosen arbitrarily. Hence, summing over all
t
, we get:

\dim(W_{k})=\sum_{t=0}^{\min(n,d)-k}{n\choose k+t}{d-k\choose t}n^{d-k-t},

proving equality (
5.14
).

Next, we compute the
\ell
-action. If
1\in S
, we must have
\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}=\emptyset
, and so,
\ell\cdot\textbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\textbf{r}},\widetilde{\textbf{j}}}=0
by Lemma
5.18
. Now, suppose
1\not\in S
. Fix
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}
such that for all
j\in\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}
, we have
r_{j}\neq 1
. Then, we have by Equation (
5.9
):

\ell\cdot\textbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\textbf{r}},\widetilde{\textbf{j}}}=v^{-2\#\{i>\max\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}~|~r_{i}=1\}}\left(\textbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\textbf{r}},\widetilde{\textbf{j}}}+\sum\limits_{t>\max\widetilde{\mathbf{j}},~r_{t}=1}\textbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\textbf{r}},\widetilde{\textbf{j}}\cup\{t\}}\right).

Furthermore, for any
\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}
such that there exists
j\in\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}
with
r_{j}=1
, we have by Corollary
5.10
:

\ell\cdot\textbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\textbf{r}},\widetilde{\textbf{j}}}=(v^{2}-1)\ell\cdot\textbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\textbf{r}},\widetilde{\textbf{j}}\setminus\{j\}}.

Thus, a basis for the space
\ell\cdot W_{k}
is given by
\ell\cdot\textbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\textbf{r}},\widetilde{\textbf{j}}}
for those triples
(S,\widetilde{\mathbf{r}},\widetilde{j})
such that
1\not\in S
and for all
j\in\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}
, we have
r_{j}\neq 1
. (That these elements are linearly independent follows from the fact that they lie in the span of different basis elements
m_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\mathbf{j}^{\prime}}
in
MV_{n,d}
.) Hence, the dimension of the space
\ell\cdot W_{k}
is exactly the number of such triples. This number can be computed by repeating the computation we did for
\dim(W_{k})
above after replacing
n
by
n-1
to account for the fact that the first row shouldn’t be included in
S
and shouldn’t be decorated in
\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}
, thus completing the proof.
∎

5.6.1.
Proof of base case
s=\min(d,n)
.

If
s=d\leq n
, by Lemma
5.20
, there exist vectors on which all the Jucys-Murphy elements act by zero. These vectors each span one-dimensional (zero)
MH_{d}
-representations corresponding to the bipartition
(0,1^{d})
. By Proposition
5.17
, the only
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
’s that pair with a one-dimensional representation of
MH_{d}
correspond to
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(d,0)
,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(1^{d},0)
or
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(0,1^{d})
. In the first two cases, each of the
L_{i}
’s act with non-zero eigenvalues by Proposition
5.14
. Thus, the zero representation of
MH_{d}
must pair with the
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
when
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(0,1^{d})
, proving the claim.

Next, suppose
s=n<d
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda,1^{n})
so that
|\lambda|+n=d
. Then by Lemma
5.20
, the space
W_{n}
is spanned by elements of the form
\textbf{s}_{S,\widetilde{\textbf{r}},\widetilde{\textbf{j}}}
with
S=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, arbitrary
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}
chosen in
\{1,2,\dots,n\}^{d-n}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{j}}=\emptyset
. Lemma
5.18
shows that the idempotent
\ell
acts by zero on each of these elements. This shows that as a left
MU(n)
-module, the space
W_{n}
lies in the span of the isotypic components of those
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
’s on which
\ell
acts by zero, that is, those
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
’s that have
s=n
.

For any
\lambda
with
|\lambda|=d-n
and a tableau
U
of shape
\lambda
, by Theorem
5.11
, there exists an element in the quantum tensor space
V_{n,d-n}
:

s_{\lambda}=\sum_{\textbf{r}\in\Pi}c_{\textbf{r}}m_{\textbf{r}},

on which the Jucys-Murphy elements of the Hecke algebra
H_{d-n}
act like they do on the tableau
U
and the quantum group
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
acts with weight
\lambda
. (Here,
\Pi\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,n\}^{d-n}
is some index set and
c_{\textbf{r}}
are some scalars.) Then, we can construct:

s_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}=}\sum_{\textbf{r}\in\Pi}c_{\textbf{r}}\textbf{s}_{S,\textbf{r},\emptyset},

where
S=\{n,n-1,\dots,1\}\in[n]_{n}
.

Note that the linear map:

\displaystyle V_{n,d-n}

\displaystyle\to MV_{n,d}

\displaystyle m_{\mathbf{r}}

\displaystyle\mapsto\textbf{s}_{S,\textbf{r},\emptyset}

intertwines the right action of
\tau_{i}\in H_{d-n}
on the domain with the action of
\tau_{i+n}\in MH_{d}
on the co-domain for
1\leq i\leq d-n-1
, as there are no decorations in the last
d-n
columns of any summand of
\textbf{s}_{S,\textbf{r},\emptyset}
. Furthermore, by Lemma
5.20
, the elements
L_{1},L_{2},\dots,L_{n}
act on
s_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
by zero, whereas
L_{n+1}
acts by
1
. Therefore, the algebra
MH_{d}
acts on
s_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
like it does on the bitableau
(U_{1},U_{2})
of shape
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, where
U_{1}
is obtained from
U
by increasing each entry by
n
and
U_{2}
is the one-column tableau with entries
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
increasing from top to bottom. Hence,
s_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
lies in the right isotypic compenent of the simple
MH_{d}
-representation
M^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
.

Next, since every summand in
\textbf{s}_{S,\textbf{r},\emptyset}
has exactly one more
1
in each row as
m_{\mathbf{r}}
, the algebra
MU(n)
acts on
s_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
with the same weight (
=\lambda
) as it does on
m_{\mathbf{r}}
. Thus, since
\ell
acts on it by zero, the element
s_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
lies in the left isotypic compenent of the
MU(n)
-representation
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}
for some
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}=(\lambda^{\prime},1^{n})
such that
\lambda^{\prime}\geq\lambda
.

Thus, we conclude that for all
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda,1^{n})\in M\Lambda_{n,d}
, the isotypic component of the
MH_{d}
-representation
M^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
in
MV_{n,d}
is non-zer. Furthermore, if the representation
M^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
pairs with
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}
in the mirabolic tensor space, then
\lambda^{\prime}\geq\lambda
. Using induction on
\lambda
with respect to the partial order on the set of partitions of
d-n
, we conclude that
M^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
pairs exactly with
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
for all
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
when
s=d
, completing the base case of the induction.

5.6.2.
Induction step

Fix a bipartition
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda,1^{s})
. We assume that the theorem has been proven whenever the second partition has more than
s
parts. That is, we assume that
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}
pairs with
M^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}
in the mirabolic tensor space whenever
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}=(\lambda^{\prime},1^{s^{\prime}})
for
s^{\prime}>s
.

Recall the subspace
W_{s}
of all elements in the mirabolic tensor space on which the first
s
Jucys-Murphy elements act by zero. We can write
W_{s}=W\oplus W^{\prime}
, where
W
is the intersection of
W_{s}
with the isotypic components of those
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}
’s having depth at most
s
and
W^{\prime}
is the intersection of
W_{s}
with the isotypic components of those
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}
’s (and by induction, those
M^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}
’s) whose depth is bigger than
s
.

Lemma 5.24
.

(1)

The space
W
is the intersection of
W_{s}
with the isotypic components of those
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}
’s whose depth is exactly
s
.

(2)

For any
k\geq s+1
, the Jucys-Murphy element
L_{k}
does not act by zero on any non-zero vector of
W
. In particular, the Jucys-Murphy element
L_{s+1}
acts on
W
by 1.

Proof.

(1)

Fix
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}=(\lambda^{\prime},1^{s^{\prime}})
for some
s^{\prime}>s
. The space
M^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}
has a basis given by bitableaux, such that exactly
s^{\prime}
of the
d
Jucys-Murphy elements act by zero on any bitableau. Therefore, the dimension of the simultaneous zero eigenspace of
L_{1},L_{2},\dots,L_{s}
in
M^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}
is equal to

\frac{{d-s\choose s^{\prime}-s}}{{d\choose s^{\prime}}}\dim(M^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}).

The dimension of the space
W^{\prime}
is equal to the sum of dimensions of the simultaneous zero eigenspaces of
L_{1},L_{2},\dots,L_{s}
in the isotypic components of those
M^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}
’s such that
s^{\prime}>s
. By induction, the isotypic component of such a
M^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}
is equal to
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}\otimes M^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}
.

Therefore, we have that (all the sums below are over partitions with at most
n
parts):

\displaystyle\dim(W^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}=s+1}^{\min(n,d)}\frac{{d-s\choose s^{\prime}-s}}{{d\choose s^{\prime}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}=(\lambda^{\prime},1^{s^{\prime}})\\
|\lambda^{\prime}|=d-s^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\dim(L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}})\dim(M^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\min(n,d)-s}\frac{{d-s\choose k}}{{d\choose s+k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}=(\lambda^{\prime},1^{s+k})\\
|\lambda^{\prime}|=d-s-k\end{subarray}}\dim(L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}})\dim(M^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\min(n,d)-s}\frac{{d-s\choose k}}{{d\choose s+k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\lambda^{\prime}|=d-s-k\end{subarray}}\dim(L_{\lambda^{\prime}})\dim(M^{\lambda^{\prime}}){n\choose s+k}{d\choose s+k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\min(n,d)-s}{n\choose s+k}{d-s\choose k}n^{d-s-k},

where the last equality follows from Theorem
5.11
and the fact that
\dim(V_{n,d-s-k})=n^{d-s-k}
.

Therefore, by Corollary
5.23
, the dimension of
W
is
\dim(W_{s})-\dim(W^{\prime})={n\choose s}n^{d-s}
.
Next, by a computation similar to the one above, the dimension of the
1
-eigenspace of
\ell
in
W^{\prime}
is:

\dim(\ell\cdot W^{\prime})=\sum_{k=1}^{\min(n,d)-s}{n-1\choose s+k}{d-s\choose k}n^{d-s-k}.

Therefore, by Corollary
5.23
, the dimension of the
1
-eigenspace of
\ell
in
W
is
{n-1\choose s}n^{d-s}
. We observe that:

\frac{\dim(\ell\cdot W)}{\dim(W)}=\frac{{n-1\choose s}n^{d-s}}{{n\choose s}n^{d-s}}=\frac{n-s}{n}.

Note that for any
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}
for which
s^{\prime}\leq s
, we have

\frac{\dim(\ell\cdot L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}})}{\dim(L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}})}=\frac{n-s^{\prime}}{n}\leq\frac{n-s}{n}.

Since
W
lies in the span of such
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}
’s, the above equality implies that
W
lies in the span of those
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}
’s whose depth is exactly
s
.

(2)

In order to show this, it suffice to show that the dimension of the space:

W_{s}\cap\Big(\sum_{t=s+1}^{d}\ker(L_{t})\Big)=\bigcap_{t=1}^{s}\ker(L_{t})\cap\Big(\sum_{t=s+1}^{d}\ker(L_{t})\Big)

equals the dimension of
W^{\prime}
. To compute this dimension, we use inclusion-exclusion:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\min(n,d)-s}\sum_{s+1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{k}\leq d}(-1)^{k-1}\dim\Big(\bigcap_{t=1}^{s}\ker(L_{t})\cap\bigcap_{t=1}^{k}\ker(L_{i_{t}})\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\min(n,d)-s}\sum_{s+1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{k}\leq d}(-1)^{k-1}\dim\Big(\bigcap_{t=1}^{s+k}\ker(L_{t})\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\min(n,d)-s}(-1)^{k-1}{d-s\choose k}\dim\Big(\bigcap_{t=1}^{s+k}\ker(L_{t})\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\min(n,d)-s}(-1)^{k-1}{d-s\choose k}\sum_{t=0}^{\min(n,d)-s-k}{n\choose s+k+t}{d-s-k\choose t}n^{d-s-t-k}

\displaystyle(\textrm{By Corollary~\ref{cor:zerodim}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{\min(n,d)-s}{n\choose s+l}n^{d-s-l}\sum_{k=1}^{l}(-1)^{k-1}{d-s\choose k}{d-s-k\choose l-k}

\displaystyle(\textrm{where }l=k+t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{\min(n,d)-s}{n\choose s+l}{d-s\choose l}n^{d-s-l}

\displaystyle=\dim(W^{\prime}).

(The first equality holds since the simultaneous zero eigenspaces of any
s+k
Jucys-Murphy elements have the same dimension.)

∎

Next, we construct a subspace
U
of
W_{s}
as follows.
Let
S=\{n,n-1,\dots,n-s+1\}\in[n]_{s}
. Fix arbitrary
{\mathbf{r}}\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}^{d-s}
. Define:

s_{S,{\mathbf{r}}}=\sum_{{\mathbf{j}}\in J_{\mathbf{r}}(n-s)}s_{S,{\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{j}}}.

Recall that
J_{\mathbf{r}}(n-s)
is the set of decorations
{\mathbf{j}}
of
m_{{\mathbf{r}}}
such that
a\in{\mathbf{j}}
only if
r_{a}\leq n-s
. Let
U
be the space spanned by the elements
s_{S,{\mathbf{r}}}
for varying
{\mathbf{r}}
. As these elements are all linearly independent, we have that
\dim(U)=n^{d-s}
. Furthermore, it is clear that the first
s
Jucys-Murphy elements act by zero on
U
.

By Lemma
5.16
, the space
U
lies in the span of the isotypic components of those
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
’s that have depth at most
s
, and thus,
U
must be a subspace of
W
. Then, by the first part of Lemma
5.24
, the space
U
must lie in the span of the isotypic components of those
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
’s that have depth exactly
s
, and that the elements
s_{S,{\mathbf{r}}}
have co-depth exactly
d-s
. The second part of the same lemma shows that the Jucys-Murphy element
L_{s+1}
acts on the space
U
by 1.

The rest of the argument is fairly similar to the one used in the base case (Section
5.6.1
). For any
\lambda
with
|\lambda|=d-s
and a tableau
U
of shape
\lambda
, by Theorem
5.11
, there exists an element in quantum tensor space
V_{n,d-s}
:

s_{\lambda}=\sum_{\textbf{r}\in\Pi}c_{\textbf{r}}m_{\textbf{r},\emptyset},

on which the Hecke algebra
H_{d-s}
act like it does on the tableau
U
and the quantum group
U_{v}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
acts on it with weight
\lambda
. (Here,
\Pi\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,n\}^{d-n}
is some index set and
c_{\textbf{r}}
are some scalars.) Then, we can construct:

s_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}=}\sum_{\textbf{r}\in\Pi}c_{\textbf{r}}\textbf{s}_{S,\textbf{r}}.

By Lemma
5.15
and an argument similar to the one in Section
5.6.1
, the mirabolic Hecke algebra
MH_{d}
acts on
s_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
like it does on the bitableau
(U_{1},U_{2})
of shape
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, where
U_{1}
is obtained from
U
by increasing each entry by
n
and
U_{2}
is the one-column tableau with entries
\{1,2,\dots,s\}
increasing from top to bottom.

Furthermore, the mirabolic quantum group
MU(n)
acts on
s_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
with weight
\lambda+\omega_{r}^{\prime}
, where
\omega_{r}^{\prime}=(0,\dots,0,1,\dots,1)=(0^{n-r}1^{r})
. By Lemma
2.30
(2), there exists a vector
s_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\prime}
with the same
MH_{d}
-action as
s_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
such that
MU(n)
acts on
s_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\prime}
with weight
\lambda+\omega_{r}
, where
\omega_{r}=(1,\dots,1,0,\dots,0)=(1^{r}0^{n-r})
.

Using induction on
\lambda
with respect to the partial order on the set of partitions of
d-s
, we conclude that
M^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
pairs with
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
for all
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, completing the proof.
