Fano resonances induced by symmetry protected bound states in the continuum in dielectric metasurfaces: line-shape prediction by machine learning method
V. S. Gerasimov 1,2, A. S. Kostyukov 1, A. E. Ershov 1,2, D. N. Maksimov 1,3, V. Kimberg 4, M. S. Molokeev 3,5, S. P. Polyutov 1

Abstract
We consider resonances induced by symmetry protected bound states in the continuum in dielectric gratings with in-plane mirror symmetry. It is shown that the shape of the resonance in transmittance is controlled by two parameters in a generic formula which can be derived in the framework of the coupled mode theory. It is numerically demonstrated that the formula encompasses various line-shapes including asymmetric Fano, Lorentzian, and anti-Lorentzian resonances. It is confirmed that the transmittance zeros are always present even in the absence up-down symmetry. At the same time reflectance zeros are not generally present in the single mode approximation. It is found that the line-shapes of Fano resonances can be predicted to a good accuracy by the random forest machine learning method which outperforms the standard least square methods approximation in error by an order of magnitude in error with the training dataset size N ≈ 10 4 N\approx 10^{4} .

In this work we investigate the line shape-shapes of Fano resonances induced by symmetry protected optical BICs in dielectric gratings. The symmetry breaking leading to transformation of BICs to quasi-BICs is controlled by small deviation of the angle of incidence from the normal. Our goal is to analyse possible resonant line-shapes and find out weather the line-shapes can be predicted by machine learning methods using the geometric and optical properties as the input parameters. II
TCMT equations

As one can see in Fig.
1
(a) the system under scrutiny is a ruled grating made of dielectric bars with refractive index
n_{b}
. The grating is placed on top of a dielectric substrate with refractive index
n_{s}
. The supestrate of the system is air with
n_{0}=1
. All geometric parameters, including the period
p
, the width
w
, and the height
h
are specified in Fig.
1
(a). In what follows we take
p=0.697~\mu{\rm m}
. I this
work we only consider TM-waves which propagate along the
x
-axis but not along the bars, so the scattering problem can be solved in the framework of 2D electrodynamics. The mode profile of a symmetry protected BIC is shown in the Fig.
1
(a) in the form of the
z
-component of the electric field.

One can see in Fig.
1
(b) that the scattering problem is described by a
4\times 4
scattering matrix
\widehat{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle{4}}
, which links the vectors of incident and outgoing amplitudes as follows

{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}=\widehat{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle{4}}{\bf s}_{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)},

(1)

where the outgoing
{\bf s}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}
and incident
{\bf s}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}
vectors are given by

\bf{s}_{\scriptscriptstyle{(\pm)}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}s_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\pm)}}\\
s_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\pm)}}\\
s_{3}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\pm)}}\\
s_{4}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\pm)}}\end{array}\right).

(2)

We assume that all dielectric are lossless so the
S
-matrix is unitary
\widehat{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle{4}}^{\dagger}\widehat{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle{4}}=\widehat{\mathbb{I}}
. The system also possesses time-reversal symmetry therefore the
S
-matrix is symmetric
\widehat{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle{4}}^{\intercal}=\widehat{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle{4}}
. Importantly the matrix
\widehat{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle{(4)}}
must be of the block form

\widehat{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle{4}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\widehat{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\\
\widehat{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}^{\intercal}&0\end{array}\right)

(3)

to ensure the momentum conservation in scattering from the metasurface
Zhou et al. 2016
, where
\widehat{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
is a
2\times 2
unitary matrix. Since the system has the
\sigma_{v}
mirror symmetry the problem can be reduced to finding
\widehat{S}_{2}
that is a symmetric matrix as shown in Appendix
A
.
Without a loss of generality we can focus on the left-going waves, so that

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}s_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}\\
s_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}\\
\end{array}\right)=\widehat{S}_{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}s_{3}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}\\
s_{4}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}\\
\end{array}\right).

(4)

According to
Fan et al. 2003
the TCMT equations describing single-mode scattering take the following form

\displaystyle\frac{da(t)}{dt}=-(i\omega_{0}+\gamma)a(t)+{\bf\kappa}^{\intercal}{\bf s^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}}(t),

\displaystyle{\bf s}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}(t)=\widehat{C}{\bf s}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}(t)+a(t){\bf d},

(5)

where
\widehat{C}
is the matrix of direct (non-resonant) process,
\omega_{0}
is the resonance center frequency,

\gamma
is the radiation decay rate,
a(t)
– the amplitude of the resonant eigenmode,
{\bf\kappa}
is the coupling vector and

{\bf d}
is the decoupling vector. In what follows we assume that the system is illuminated by a monochromatic wave of frequency
\omega
, so all time-dependent quantities in Eq. (
II
) oscillate in time with the harmonic factor
e^{-i\omega t}
. Importantly, the parameters in the TCMT equations are not independent, but linked to each other due to constraints imposed by energy conservation, Lorentz reciprocity and time reversal symmetry
Fan et al. 2003
;
Zhao et al. 2019
. As we have already mentioned, the energy conservation manifests itself in the unitarity of the S-matrix whereas the time-reversal symmetry forces the S-matrix to be symmetric.
In this situation the parameters of the TCMT equations are known to satisfy the following three equations
Fan et al. 2003

\displaystyle 2\gamma={\bf d}^{\dagger}{\bf d},

\displaystyle{\bf\kappa}={\bf d},

\displaystyle\widehat{C}{\bf d}^{*}+{\bf d}=0.

(6)

In our case both energy conservation and time-reversal are present, however, care is needed in application of the time reversal operation since it maps the left-going waves onto the right-going ones. In Appendix
B
we show that the
2\times 2
unitary and symmetry of
\widehat{S}_{2}
lead to the same constraints for the coupling parameters as
in Eq. (
II
).

Now we have to solve Eq. (
II
) for the decoupling vector. We set out from the most generic form of
\widehat{C}
, which is unitary and symmetric,

\widehat{C}=e^{i\phi}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\rho e^{-i\eta}&i\tau\\
i\tau&\rho e^{i\eta}\end{array}\right),\ \rho=\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}}\\

(7)

with
\tau\in[-1,~1]
, where all parameters are real valued. The number of parameters in Eq. (
7
) can be reduced by redefining the incident channels via a unitary transformation

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{s}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}\\
{s}_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{i\phi_{1}}&0\\
0&e^{i\phi_{2}}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{s}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}\\
\tilde{s}_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}\end{array}\right).

(8)

To be consistent with the time-reversal symmetry the outgoing channels have to be transformed as follows

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{s}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}\\
{s}_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{-i\phi_{1}}&0\\
0&e^{-i\phi_{2}}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{s}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}\\
\tilde{s}_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}\end{array}\right).

(9)

Then, by using Eq. (
8
) and Eq. (
9
) together with Eq. (
7
) one arrives at a single parameter family of unitary symmetric matrices

\widehat{C}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\rho&i\tau\\
i\tau&\rho\end{array}\right),\ \ \rho=\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}},\ \ \tau\in[-1,1],

(10)

if one chooses

\displaystyle\phi_{1}=\frac{\eta-\phi}{2},\ \phi_{2}=-\frac{\eta+\phi}{2}

(11)

in Eq. (
8
). It can be easely checked that the above unitary transformation complies with Eq. (
II
). The unitary transformation in Eq. (
8
) can be thought of as a shifting the reference plane between the scattering domain and the outer space along the
y
-axis. This is always possible in the far-field where the solution is exhaustively described by the scattering channels. By using Eq. (
10
) in Eq. (
II
) one finds a single-parametric family of solutions for
\bf{d}
as follows

\begin{split}{\bf d}=&\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{(1+\rho)}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tau\cos\alpha-i(1+\rho)\sin\alpha\\
\tau\sin\alpha-i(1+\rho)\cos\alpha\end{array}\right),\\
\alpha\in&[-{\pi}/{2},{\pi}/{2}],\end{split}

(12)

The derivation details are presented in Appendix
C
.

After using
{\bf\kappa}={\bf d}
in Eq. (
B
) one finds the final solution for the
S
-matrix

\widehat{S}=\widehat{C}+\frac{{\bf d}{\bf d}^{\intercal}}{i(\omega_{0}-\omega)+\gamma}.

(13)

The transmission coefficient independent of the direction of incidence is written as

T=\frac{[\tau(\omega_{0}-\omega)+\rho\gamma\sin(2\alpha)]^{2}}{(\omega_{0}-\omega)^{2}+\gamma^{2}}.

(14)

If
\alpha=\pm\pi/4
Eq. (
14
) limits to the well-known solution presented in
Fan et al. 2003
for system with up-down symmetry. The transmettance spectrum Eq. (
14
) complies with the earlier result from
Wang et al. 2013
on the fundamental bounds on decay rates in asymmetric single-mode optical resonators, where it was shown that the transmittance is only bound to peak to unity in symmetric resonators.

The system supports a symmetry protected BIC in the
\Gamma
-point. With variation of the angle of incidence

\theta
in the vicinity of the
\Gamma
-point the
BIC is transformed to a high-
Q
resonant mode with the resonant frequency
\omega_{0}
and the decay rate
\gamma

given by the following Taylor expansion

\displaystyle\omega_{0}=\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{BIC}}+\kappa_{\omega}\theta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\theta^{4}),

\displaystyle\gamma=\kappa_{\gamma}\theta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\theta^{4}).

(15)

Upon using the Taylor expansion Eq. (
II
) in Eq. (
14
) one arrives at

T=\frac{[\tau(\omega_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{BIC}}}+\omega_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(2)}}\theta^{2}-\omega)+\rho\gamma^{\scriptscriptstyle{(2)}}\sin(2\alpha)\theta^{2}]^{2}}{(\omega_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{BIC}}}+\omega_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(2)}}\theta^{2}-\omega)^{2}+(\gamma^{\scriptscriptstyle{(2)}}\theta^{2})^{2}}+{\cal O}(\theta^{6}),

(16)

which gives the line-shape of the Fano resonance induced by a symmetry protected BIC.

III
Dataset acquisition

Our goal is to predict the shapes of the BIC-induced Fano resonance in the system shown in Fig.
1
. According to Eq. (
14
), besides the resonance center-frequency
\omega_{0}
and the
radiation decay rate
\gamma

only two parameters characterizing the shape of the Fano resonance, namely
\alpha
and
\tau

Both parameters can be found by fitting the numerically computed transmittance
spectrum at the incidence angle slightly different from normal. In this work we take
\theta=2\ \mathrm{deg}

The radiation decay rate and the center-frequency dictate the position and the width of the Fano resonance, correspondingly. Therefore, for predicting the shape of the resonance we have to analyse how the four parameters
n_{1},~n_{2},~h
and

affect the quantities of
\alpha
and
\tau

\displaystyle n_{1}\in[2,5],\ n_{2}\in[1.5,4],\ h\in[0.2p,0.8p],\ w\in[0.2p,0.8p].

(17)

Note that the line-shape of the resonance is the same after a simultaneous change of the signs of both
\alpha
and
\tau

Molokeev et al. 2023
we focus on the wavelengh range
1~100-2~000~{\rm nm}

\displaystyle\tau\in[-1,1],\ g\in[0,1],\ \ 2~000~{\rm nm}>\lambda>1~100~{\rm nm},\ \gamma>0,

(18)

where
\lambda
is the wavelength of the resonance
\lambda=2\pi c/\omega_{0}
and

g=\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}}\sin(2\alpha).

(19)

Random Forest method

In order to improve the precision of predicting the property parameters (

,

For the prediction task, we utilized the random forest (RF) algorithm, a powerful ensemble method based on regression trees
Breiman 2001
;
Ho 1995
. This approach involved constructing multiple decision trees by recursively partitioning the multidimensional predictor space. During the prediction phase, the RF model outputs the mode of the classes (for classification) or the mean average prediction (for regression) derived from the individual trees
Liu et al. 2012
;
Segal
.
For the implementation of the RF model, we developed a Python script named RandomForest.py using the Python 3.6 programming language
Van Rossum and Python Dev
Team 2016
. The script utilises the standard libraries, including
numpy, pandas, sklearn, matplotlib,
and
mpl_toolkits
. To account for the stochastic nature of the RF algorithm, we performed the 5-fold cross-validation test, aggregating the results to obtain an averaged performance and calculate the mean average error (MAE). Each iteration involved randomly splitting the data into two sets. One set comprising 70% of the total data was used for training the model. The remaining 30% of the data was used for testing. As a result, we constructed four distinct RF models, one for each property parameter (
\tau
,
g
,
\lambda
,
\gamma
).

Besides it predictive power, the RF algorithm can also quantify the importance of each feature parameter after training. This can be achieved by permuting the values of a selected feature within the training data and calculating the error on the perturbed dataset. The importance score for the feature is obtained by averaging the difference in error before and after permutation across all trees and subsequent normalization by the differences
Altmann et al. 2010
;
Wehenkel et al. 2018
. The features that yield higher values of this score are ranked as more important compared to features with lower values.

Finally, in applying the RF method, it is found out that the algorithm fails to correctly predict the property parameter

\omega_{0}

This does not, however, impose a difficulty since in any prefabricated set-up the resonant width is easily controlled by the angle of incidence. Finally, the line-shape of the resonance is controlled by

VI
Conclusion

In this work we investigated line-shapes of the Fano resonances induced by symmetry protected bound states in the continuum in dielectric gratings. It is numerically demonstrated that the line-shapes are controlled by two parameters in Eq. (
14
) which encompasses various line-shapes including asymmetric Fano, Lorentzian, and anti-Lorentzian resonances. In full accordance with the previous studies
Gippius et al. 2005
;
Wang et al. 2013
;
Yuan et al. 2022
it is confirmed that the transmittance zeros are always present even in the absence up-down symmetry. At the same time the reflectance zeros can only be approached accidentally when parameter
\alpha
in Eq. (
14
) is close to
\pi/4
. It is found that the line-shapes of Fano resonances can be predicted to a good accuracy by the random forest machine learning methods which outperforms the standard least square methods approximation in error by an order of magnitude in error with the training dataset size
N\approx 10^{4}
. We speculate that the results presented can be of use in application for resonant response synthesis from all-delectric metasurfces.

Appendix B
Derivation of Eq. (
II
)

The first line in Eq. (
II
) can be proven by the same method as suggested in
Fan et al. 2003
. First of all, we assume that the resonant eigenmode is normalized to
carry a unit energy whereas the scattering carry a unit a energy per unit of time across the interface between the far-field and the scattering domain. Thus, the absence of incidence wave the energy conservation leads to

\frac{dE}{dt}=\frac{d|a|^{2}}{dt}={\bf d}^{\dagger}{\bf d}|a|^{2}

(24)

with
E
being the energy stored in the resonant mode. Given that the solution of the first line in Eq. (
II
) is

a(t)=a_{0}e^{-(i\omega_{0}+\gamma)t}

(25)

we immediately have

{\bf d}^{\dagger}{\bf d}=2\gamma.

(26)

The derivation of the other relationships in Eq. (
II
) is more complicated. We start from the time-harmonic substitution in Eq. (
II
) which leads to the time-stationary TCMT equations in the following form

\displaystyle[i(\omega_{0}-\omega)+\gamma]{a}={\bf\kappa}^{\intercal}{\bf s}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)},

\displaystyle{\bf s}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}=\widehat{C}{\bf s}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}+{\bf d}a.

(27)

The solution of Eq.(
B
) can be written in the form of
S
-matrix

\widehat{S}_{2}(\omega)=\widehat{C}+\frac{{\bf d}{\bf\kappa}^{\intercal}}{i(\omega_{0}-\omega)+\gamma}.

(28)

We notice that since
\widehat{S}_{2}(\infty)=\widehat{C}
the matrix
\widehat{C}
has the same symmetry and unitarity properties as
\widehat{S}_{2}(\omega)
. After applying
\widehat{S}_{2}^{-1}=\widehat{S}_{2}^{\dagger}
we find

\displaystyle\widehat{C}^{*}{\bf d}{\bf\kappa}^{\intercal}[-i(\omega_{0}-\omega)+\gamma]+{\bf\kappa}^{*}{\bf d}^{\dagger}\widehat{C}[i(\omega_{0}-\omega)+\gamma]

\displaystyle+2\gamma{\bf\kappa}^{*}{\bf\kappa}^{\intercal}=0.

(29)

By considering
\omega
-dependent terms one finds that

{\bf\kappa}^{*}{\bf d}^{\dagger}\widehat{C}=\widehat{C}^{*}{\bf d}{\bf\kappa}^{\intercal}.

(30)

Applying the above equation in Eq. (
B
) and examining the terms independent of
\omega
we write

\widehat{C}^{*}{\bf d}{\bf\kappa}^{\intercal}=-{\bf\kappa}^{*}{\bf\kappa}^{\intercal}

(31)

Next, assuming that the coupling vector
{\bf\kappa}
has at least one non-zero element we have

\widehat{C}{\bf d}^{*}=-{\bf\kappa}.

(32)

Let us now utilize the symmetry of the scattering matrix. After applying
\widehat{S}_{2}=\widehat{S}_{2}^{\intercal}
in Eq. (
28
) we immediately have

\displaystyle{\bf d}{\bf\kappa}^{\intercal}={\bf\kappa}{\bf d}^{\intercal}.

(33)

Multiplying the above equation from the left by
{\bf d}^{\dagger}
one obtains

{\bf\kappa}=\frac{{\bf d}^{\dagger}{\bf\kappa}}{2\gamma}{\bf d}.

(34)

Alternatively, by multiplying Eq. (
33
) from the left by
{\bf\kappa}^{\dagger}
one has

{\bf d}=\frac{{\bf\kappa}^{\dagger}{\bf d}}{{\bf\kappa}^{\dagger}{\bf\kappa}}{\bf\kappa}.

(35)

The latter two equations combined result in

2\gamma{\kappa}^{\dagger}{\bf\kappa}=|{\bf d}^{\dagger}{\bf\kappa}|^{2}

(36)

Next, by multiplying Eq. (
32
) from the left by its Hermitian adjoint one obtains

2\gamma={\kappa}^{\dagger}{\bf\kappa}

(37)

After analysing Eq. (
34
), Eq. (
36
), and Eq. (
37
) one can write

{\bf d}=e^{i\eta}{\bf\kappa},\ \eta\in[0,2\pi).

(38)

Let us now give interpretation to the phase
\eta
. By recollecting that the resonant mode is normalized to carry a unit energy we immediately see that its normalization constant is defined up to an arbitrary phase factor. Thus, all the TCMT equations have to be invariant under the
\rm{U}(1)
transformation

a=e^{i\alpha}a^{\prime}

(39)

By plugging the above into Eq. (
B
) we find that it remains invariant, i.e. the same for the primed quantities, if the coupling and decoupling vectors are transformed as follows

\displaystyle{\bf d}=e^{-i\alpha}{\bf d}^{\prime},

\displaystyle{\bf\kappa}=e^{+i\alpha}{\bf\kappa}^{\prime}.

(40)

By choosing
\alpha=\eta/2
one derives from Eq. (
32
) and Eq. (
38
)

\displaystyle\widehat{C}({\bf d}^{\prime})^{*}+{\bf d}^{\prime}=0,

\displaystyle{\bf\kappa}^{\prime}={\bf d}^{\prime}.

(41)

This equations are identical to the last two lines in Eq. (
II
).

Appendix C
Decoupling vector

We start with the third line in Eq. (
II
) that reads

\widehat{C}{\bf d}^{*}=-{\bf d}.

(42)

Vector
{\bf d}
is in general parameterized by four independent real numbers

{\bf d}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm(r)}}+ia_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm(i)}}\\
b_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm(r)}}+ib_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm(i)}}\end{array}\right).

(43)

Substituting the above into Eq. (
42
) one obtains a set of linear homogeneous equations of rank two

\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}1+\rho&0&0&\tau\\
0&1-\rho&\tau&0\\
0&\tau&1+\rho&0\\
\tau&0&0&1-\rho\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle{(r)}}\\
a_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle{(i)}}\\
b_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle{(r)}}\\
b_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle{(i)}}\end{array}\right)=0,

(44)

which has the general two-parametric solution

{\bf d}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tau b_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle{(i)}}-i(1+\rho)a_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle{(i)}}\\
\tau a_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle{(i)}}-i(1+\rho)b_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle{(i)}}\end{array}\right).

(45)

By recollecting that according to Eq. (
II
)
2\gamma=\bf{d}^{\dagger}\bf{d}

one can find

\gamma=(1+\rho)(a_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle{(i)}}^{2}+b_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle{(i)}}^{2}).

(46)

After combining Eq. (
45
) with Eq. (
46
) one arrives at the general solution of the form

{\bf d}\!=\!\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{(1\!+\!\rho)}}\!\left(\!\begin{array}[]{c}\!\tau\cos\alpha-i(1\!+\!\rho)\sin\alpha\\
\!\tau\sin\alpha-i(1\!+\!\rho)\cos\alpha\end{array}\!\right)\!,\ \alpha\!\in\!\left[-{\pi}/{2},{\pi}/{2}\right],

(47)

where the range
[-{\pi}/{2},{\pi}/{2}]
is chosen since
{\bf\kappa}={\bf d}
and, therefore, the sign of
\bf{d}
is not important for the
S
-matrix Eq. (
28
).
