A note on the unique properties of the Kullback–Leibler divergence for sampling via gradient flows
Francesca Romana Crucinio

Abstract
We consider the problem of sampling from a probability distribution π \pi . It is well known that this can be written as an optimisation problem over the space of probability distribution in which we aim to minimise a divergence from π \pi . and The optimisation problem is normally solved through gradient flows in the space of probability distribution with an appropriate metric. We show that the Kullback–Leibler divergence is the only divergence in the family of Bregman divergences whose gradient flow w.r.t. many popular metrics does not require knowledge of the normalising constant of π \pi .



1
Introduction

Sampling from a target probability distribution whose density is known up to a normalisation constant is a fundamental task in computational statistics and machine learning. A natural way to formulate this task is optimisation of a functional measuring the dissimilarity to the target probability distribution.
Following this point of view, one can derive many popular sampling frameworks including variational inference
(
Blei et al. 2017
)
, algorithms based on diffusions
(
Roberts and Tweedie 1996
;
Durmus et al. 2019
)
and deterministic flows
(
Liu 2017
)
, and algorithms based on importance sampling
(
Chopin et al. 2024
;
Crucinio and Pathiraja 2025
)
.
The connection between minimisation of a divergence and Monte Carlo algorithms is established through gradient flows over the space of probability measures (see, e.g.,
Chewi et al. 2025
;
Carrillo et al. 2024
for a recent review); with different metrics over this space leading to different differential equations whose discretisations correspond to many popular Monte Carlo algorithms.

The most widely used divergence is the reverse Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence whose gradient flow w.r.t. the Wasserstein-2 metric can be implemented by a Langevin diffusion
(
Jordan et al. 1998
)
and easily discretised in time, resulting in the Unadjusted Langevin algorithm
(
Roberts and Tweedie 1996
)
. Recently, gradient flows of the reverse Kullback–Leibler divergence w.r.t. the Fisher–Rao metric have also been considered
(
Lu et al. 2019
;
Crucinio and Pathiraja 2025
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Domingo-Enrich and Pooladian 2023
)
. The Fisher–Rao gradient flow is intimately tied to mirror descent dynamics
(
Chopin et al. 2024
)
and can be efficiently implemented via importance sampling
(
Crucinio and Pathiraja 2025
;
Korba and Portier 2022
;
Dai et al. 2016
)
.
Combination of the Wasserstein and Fisher–Rao metric leads to the Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao gradient flow, which, in the case of the Kullback–Leibler divergence, corresponds to a combination of exploration moves using a Langevin diffusion and reweigthing
(
Chewi et al. 2025
;
Lambert et al. 2022
;
Yan et al. 2024
)
, and can be implemented via sequential Monte Carlo
(
Crucinio and Pathiraja 2025
)
.

Minimisation of divergences (including the Kullback–Leibler) can also be obtained by deriving ordinary differential equations which follow velocity fields which reduce the KL
(
Liu 2017
;
Korba et al. 2021
;
Nüsken 2024
)
.
These algorithms can also be interpreted as gradient flows of the selected divergence w.r.t. kernelised metrics
(
Duncan et al. 2023
;
Chewi et al. 2020
)
.

Despite the widespread use of the KL divergence to derive gradient flows, it is well known that other families of divergences, e.g.
f
-divergences and Bregman divergences, are very flexible and allow to enforce desirable properties such as ability to capture tail behaviour or target the mode
(
Regli and Silva 2018
)
.
The rationale behind the choice of KL divergence is that this divergence leads to Monte Carlo sampling algorithms which do not require knowledge of the normalising constant of the target, and can therefore be implemented in general settings.

In this note, we show that the Kullback–Leibler divergence is the only divergence within the class of Bregman divergences which leads to a gradient flow which does not depend on the normalising constant of
\pi
. This result complements that of
Chen et al. 2023
which establishes an equivalent result for the class of
f
-divergences.

Notation

Fix a vector space of (signed) measures
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Let
\mathcal{M}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the dual of
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
For
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
f\in\mathcal{M}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we denote
\langle f,\mu\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\mu(dx)
.
We denote by
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the set of probability measures over

\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and endow this space with the topology of weak convergence. The subset of
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of probability measures admitting a density w.r.t. Lebesgue is denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

2
Background

2.1
Sampling as optimisation over measures

Let us denote the target distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\pi
and
let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a functional on
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
measuring the distance between
\mu
and
\pi
. Consider the optimisation problem

\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\mathcal{F}(\mu),

(1)

that is the minimum of
\mathcal{F}
restricted to the space of probability measures, which recovers
\pi
if solved exactly.
The optimisation problem (
1
) can be solved by following the direction of steepest descent of
\mathcal{F}
, given by the gradient flow partial differential equation (PDE)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=-\textrm{grad}_{\mathtt{m}}\mathcal{F}(\mu_{t}),

(2)

where
\textrm{grad}_{\mathtt{m}}\mathcal{F}(\mu)
denotes the gradient of
\mathcal{F}
w.r.t. metric
\mathtt{m}

(
Ambrosio et al. 2008
;
Carrillo et al. 2024
)
.
Depending on the choice of metric
\mathtt{m}
, the gradient
\textrm{grad}_{\mathtt{m}}\mathcal{F}(\mu)
takes different shapes; for most commonly used metrics the gradient is related to the natural gradient (also known as first variation) of
\mathcal{F}

(
Amari 1998
)
:

Definition 1
.

If it exists, the first variation of
\mathcal{F}
is the functional
\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\mu}(\nu,x):\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
s. t. for any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, with
\xi=\mu-\nu
:

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\nicefrac{{1}}{{\epsilon}}(\mathcal{F}(\nu+\epsilon\xi)-\mathcal{F}(\nu))=\langle\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\mu}(\nu,x),\xi\rangle

(3)

and is defined uniquely up to an additive constant.

Restricting the optimisation problem in (
1
) to the set of probability measures with finite second moment,
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and considering the

2
-Wasserstein distance as metric, one obtains the Wasserstein gradient flow
(
Ambrosio et al. 2008
)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=-\textrm{grad}_{\mathtt{W}}\mathcal{F}(\mu_{t})=\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}\nabla_{x}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\mu}(\mu_{t},\cdot)\right)\quad t\in[0,\infty),

where
\nabla_{x}
denotes the gradient in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Selecting
\mathcal{F}(\mu)=\mathop{\mathrm{KL}}\nolimits(\mu|\pi)
leads to the Fokker–Planck equation of the Langevin diffusion
(
Jordan et al. 1998
)

\displaystyle dX_{t}=\nabla\log\pi(X_{t})dt+\sqrt{2}dB_{t},

(4)

where
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion.
The Euler–Maruyama discretisation of (
4
) is known as the unadjusted Langevin algorithm (ULA;
Durmus et al. 2019
), which leads to the Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm (MALA;
Roberts and Tweedie 1996
) when combined with a Metropolis–Hastings accept/reject step.

If instead one considers the Fisher–Rao metric, the gradient flow PDE (
2
) is
(
Carrillo et al. 2024
)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}

\displaystyle=-\textrm{grad}_{\mathtt{FR}}\mathcal{F}(\mu_{t})

\displaystyle=\mu_{t}\left(-\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\mu}(\mu_{t},\cdot))+\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}\left[\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\mu}(\mu_{t},\cdot))\right]\right)\quad t\in[0,\infty).

Selecting
\mathcal{F}(\mu)=\mathop{\mathrm{KL}}\nolimits(\mu|\pi)
and observing that in this case
\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\mu}(\mu)=\log(\mu/\pi)
, one obtains the PDE

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=\mu_{t}\left(\log\left(\frac{\pi}{\mu_{t}}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\pi}{\mu_{t}}\right)\right]\right)\quad t\in[0,\infty).

This PDE is exactly solved by
\mu_{t}\propto\pi^{1-e^{-t}}\mu_{0}^{e^{-t}}

(
Chen et al. 2023
, Theorem 4.1)
which corresponds to geometric tempering or annealing dynamics
(
Domingo-Enrich and Pooladian 2023
;
Chopin et al. 2024
)
.
A natural way to numerically approximate the Fisher–Rao flow of
\mathop{\mathrm{KL}}\nolimits
is to first discretise time and then use importance sampling
(
Crucinio and Pathiraja 2025
)
: given
\mu_{t}
, one can obtain an approximation of
\mu_{t+1}
using
\mu_{t}
as proposal and weights

\displaystyle w_{t+1}=\left(\frac{\pi}{\mu_{t}}\right)^{1-e^{-\gamma}},

where
\gamma
denotes the time discretisation step.

Combining the Wasserstein and Fisher–Rao metrics one obtains the Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao (WFR) gradient
\textrm{grad}_{\mathtt{m}}\mathcal{F}(\mu)=\textrm{grad}_{\mathtt{W}}\mathcal{F}(\mu)+\textrm{grad}_{\mathtt{FR}}\mathcal{F}(\mu)

(
Gallouët and Monsaingeon 2017
)
. If
\mathcal{F}(\mu)=\mathop{\mathrm{KL}}\nolimits(\mu|\pi)
the WFR gradient flow is given by the sum of the Wasserstein and of the Fisher–Rao PDE. Numerical approximations can be obtained combining the unadjusted Langevin algorithm with importance sampling which naturally leads to sequential Monte Carlo methods
(
Del Moral et al. 2006
)
as described in
Crucinio and Pathiraja 2025
.

Closely related to the 2-Wasserstein metric is Stein’s metric
(
Duncan et al. 2023
)
, in this case the gradient flow PDE is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}

\displaystyle=-\textrm{grad}_{\mathtt{STEIN}}\mathcal{F}(\mu_{t})

(5)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}\int k(x,\cdot)\nabla_{x}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\mu}(\mu_{t},x)\mu_{t}(dx)\right)\quad t\in[0,\infty),

where
k
is a positive semi-definite kernel. In the case
\mathcal{F}(\mu)=\mathop{\mathrm{KL}}\nolimits(\mu|\pi)
, the gradient flow w.r.t. Stein’s metric corresponds to the continuity equation of Stein variational gradient descent (SVGD;
Liu 2017
)

\displaystyle dX_{t}^{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}[\nabla\log\pi(X_{t}^{j})k(X_{t}^{i},X_{t}^{j})+\nabla_{1}k(X_{t}^{j},X_{t}^{i})]dt,

for
i=1,\dots,N
, where
\nabla_{1}
denotes the gradient w.r.t. the first component.

2.2
Bregman divergences

We now introduce Bregman divergences
(
Bregman 1967
)
a class of divergences between measures which encompasses many commonly used measures of distance.

Definition 2
.

Let
\phi:\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
a convex functional on
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The
\phi
-Bregman divergence is defined for any
\mu,\pi\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by:

B_{\phi}(\mu|\pi)=\phi(\mu)-\phi(\pi)-\langle\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta\mu}(\pi,\cdot),\mu-\pi\rangle

(6)

where
\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta\mu}(\pi,\cdot)
is the first variation of
\phi
at
\pi
.

Setting
\mathcal{F}(\mu)=B_{\phi}(\mu|\pi)
results in
\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\mu}(\mu,\cdot)=\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta\mu}(\mu,\cdot)-\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta\mu}(\pi,\cdot)
.
When restricted to the set of measures which admit a density w.r.t. Lebesgue, the Bregman divergence can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle B_{\phi}(\mu|\pi)

\displaystyle=\int[\Phi(\mu(x))-\Phi(\pi(x))dx

(7)

\displaystyle-\int(\mu(x)-\pi(x))\Phi^{\prime}(\pi(x))dx

where we use
\mu,\pi
to denote both the densities and the measures and
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
is a strictly convex function such that
\Phi^{\prime}(\pi(x))=\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta\mu}(\pi,x)
.

Among the class of Bregman divergences we recall the Kullback–Leibler divergence

\mathop{\mathrm{KL}}\nolimits(\mu|\pi)=\int\log(\nicefrac{{d\mu}}{{d\pi}})\mu
obtained considering the negative entropy functional

\phi:\mu\mapsto\int\log(\mu(x))\mu(dx)-\int\mu(dx)+1.

The Kullback–Leibler divergence is the only Bregman divergence which is also an
f
-divergence
(
Amari 2009
)
.

More generally, if
\mu
admits a density w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
we can consider
\beta
-divergences
(
Cichocki and Amari 2010
)
, which are obtained setting

\displaystyle\Phi(t)=\begin{cases}t\log t-t+1\qquad\qquad\ \textrm{if }\beta=1\\
t-\log t-1\qquad\qquad\quad\textrm{if }\beta=0\\
\frac{\beta-1+t^{\beta}-\beta t}{\beta(\beta-1)}\qquad\qquad\qquad\textrm{otherwise}\end{cases}.

This family encompasses the
L^{2}
norm
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\|\mu-\pi\|_{2}^{2}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\int|\mu(x)-\pi(x)|^{2}dx
, obtained for
\beta=2
and the Kullback–Leibler divergence obtained for
\beta=1
.

3
Main Result

We are now ready to derive our main result which complements
Chen et al. 2023
and shows that the reverse Kullback–Leibler divergence is the unique divergence within both the
f
-divergence class and the Bregman divergences class whose gradient flows do not depend on the normalising constant.

Proposition 1
.

The Kullback–Leibler divergence is the only Bregman divergence such that
B_{\phi}(\mu|c\pi)-B_{\phi}(\mu|\pi)
is independent of
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for any
c\in(0,+\infty)
and for any
\pi\in\mathcal{M}_{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

We have that

\displaystyle B_{\phi}(\mu|c\pi)-B_{\phi}(\mu|\pi)

\displaystyle=\phi(\pi)-\phi(c\pi)

(8)

\displaystyle-\langle\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta\mu}(c\pi,\cdot),\mu-c\pi\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta\mu}(\pi,\cdot),\mu-\pi\rangle,

for any
c\in(0,+\infty)
.
In the case of
\phi(\mu)=\int\mu\log\mu-\int\mu+1
, corresponding to the Kullback–Leibler divergence, we have

\displaystyle B_{\phi}(\mu|c\pi)-B_{\phi}(\mu|\pi)=c-1-\log c,

for any
c\in(0,+\infty)
, showing that the KL satisfies the result.

To establish uniqueness, we follow the proof of
Chen et al. 2023
and consider, for
\xi\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
which integrates to 0, the difference

\displaystyle[B_{\phi}(\mu+\xi|c\pi)-B_{\phi}(\mu+\xi|\pi)]-[B_{\phi}(\mu|c\pi)-B_{\phi}(\mu|\pi)]

\displaystyle\qquad=\langle\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta\mu}(\pi,\cdot)-\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta\mu}(c\pi,\cdot),\xi\rangle,

where we used (
8
).
If
B_{\phi}
is such that
B_{\phi}(\mu|c\pi)-B_{\phi}(\mu|\pi)
is independent of
\mu
, then

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta\mu}(\pi,\cdot)-\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta\mu}(c\pi,\cdot),\xi\rangle

\displaystyle=\int[\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta\mu}(\pi,x)-\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta\mu}(c\pi,x)]\xi(dx)

which implies

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta\mu}(\pi,x)-\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta\mu}(c\pi,x)=K,

(9)

for some constant
K\in\mathbb{R}

and all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
since
\xi
is arbitrary.
Restricting (
9
) to
\mathcal{M}_{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
gives

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(\pi(x))-\Phi^{\prime}(c\pi(x))=K.

(10)

Let us denote
f(z):=\Phi^{\prime}(e^{z})
, we will show that
f
is necessarily an affine function. Using (
10
) we have

\displaystyle h(z_{1}+z_{2})

\displaystyle=\Phi^{\prime}(e^{z_{1}+z_{2}})

\displaystyle=K+\Phi^{\prime}(ce^{z_{1}+z_{2}})

\displaystyle=h(z_{1})-\Phi^{\prime}(ce^{z_{1}})+\Phi^{\prime}(ce^{z_{1}+z_{2}})

\displaystyle=h(z_{1})-K,

where we used the fact that (
10
) must hold for all
(\pi,x)\in\mathcal{M}_{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and all
c\in(0,+\infty)
and therefore
\Phi^{\prime}(ce^{z_{1}})-\Phi^{\prime}(ce^{z_{1}}e^{z_{2}})=\Phi^{\prime}(y)-\Phi^{\prime}(ye^{z_{2}})=K
.
Using
Friedman 1962
and observing that
\Phi^{\prime}
is necessarily continuous at one point since it is monotonically decreasing, we can conclude that
h(z)=az+b
for some
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and therefore
\Phi^{\prime}(\pi(x))=a\log\pi(x)+b
.

It follows that (
8
) is constant iff

\Phi(\pi(x))=a[\pi(x)\log\pi(x)-\pi(x)]+b\pi(x)

(where we set the constant to 0 since the first variation
\Phi^{\prime}(\pi(x))=\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta\mu}(\pi,x)
is defined up to additive constants). Plugging this
\Phi
into
B_{\phi}
we obtain

\displaystyle B_{\phi}(\mu|\pi)=a\mathop{\mathrm{KL}}\nolimits(\mu|\pi),

which completes the proof.
∎

As a consequence of Proposition
1
, for every Bregman divergence which is not the Kullback–Leibler divergence,
\mathcal{F}(\mu)=B_{\phi}(\mu|\pi)
, the first variation
\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\mu}(\mu,\cdot)=\Phi^{\prime}(\mu(x))-\Phi^{\prime}(\pi(x))
does not remain unchanged if we scale
\pi

by any positive constant. It follows that the gradient flows discussed in Section
2.1
necessitate knowledge of the normalising constant of
\pi
to be implemented as replacing
\pi(x)=\gamma(x)/Z
with its unnormalised version
\gamma
will lead to a different gradient flow.

4
Discussion

Proposition
1
shows that the Kullback–Leibler divergence is the only divergence within the Bregman class whose gradient flows do not require knowledge of the normalising constant of
\pi
. Combining this result with
Chen et al. 2023
reduces the class of divergences for which one can develop sampling algorithms based on gradient flows which do not require knowledge of the normalising constant. In particular, our result shows that
\alpha
–divergences and
\beta
–divergences lead to gradient flows requiring the normalising constant.
Our result and
Chen et al. 2023
do not cover the class of kernel discrepancies, whose gradient flows often do not require knowledge of the normalising constant of the target
(
Korba et al. 2021
;
Arbel et al. 2019
;
Belhadji et al. 2025
)
.

Despite requiring the normalising constant, many gradient flows of the
\chi^{2}
-divergence (which belongs to the class of
f
-divergences) have been proposed. These alternatives rely on approximations of the
\chi^{2}
-divergence obtained through mollifiers
(
Li et al. 2023
;
Lu et al. 2023
)
, kernelised metrics
(
Chewi et al. 2020
)
and jump processes
(
Lindsey et al. 2022
)
.
These approximations provide a promising venue for obtaining gradient flows of divergences beyond the Kullback–Leibler as discussed in
Carrillo et al. 2019
for some special cases.
