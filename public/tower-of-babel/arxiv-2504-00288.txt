Full classification of anti-van der Waerden numbers of graph products of forests
Zhanar Berikkyzy, Joe Miller, Nathan Warnberg

Abstract
The anti-van der Waerden number of a graph G G is the fewest number of colors needed to guarantee a rainbow 3 3 -term arithmetic progression in G G , denoted aw ​ ( G , 3 ) \textup{aw}(G,3) . It is known that the anti-van der Waerden number of graph products is 3 ≤ aw ​ ( G ​ □ ​ H , 3 ) ≤ 4 3\leq\textup{aw}(G\square H,3)\leq 4 . Previous work has been done on classifying families of graph products into aw ​ ( G ​ □ ​ H , 3 ) = 3 \textup{aw}(G\square H,3)=3 and aw ​ ( G ​ □ ​ H , 3 ) = 4 \textup{aw}(G\square H,3)=4 . Some of these families include the product of two paths, the product of paths and cycles, the product of two cycles, and the product of odd cycles with any graph. Recently, a partial characterization of the product of two trees was established. This paper completes the characterization for aw ​ ( T ​ □ ​ T ′ , 3 ) \textup{aw}(T\square T^{\prime},3) where T T and T ′ T^{\prime} are trees. Moreover, this result extends to a full classification of products of forests.

anti-Ramsey, arithmetic progressions, graphs, trees

Introduction

Ramsey Theory is a branch of mathematics that assigns colors to elements of a set and then determines if monochromatic substructures exist within the set, whereas Anti-Ramsey Theory determines if rainbow (polychromatic) substructures exist within the set. Ramsey Theory has a long history dating back to around
1920
with Schur, Ramsey and van der Waerden making the earliest contributions (see
[
12
,
14
,
16
]
) where the sets considered were integers or graph edges. It was not until
1973
when when Erdős, Simonovits, and Sós, in
[
8
]
, introduced the idea of Anti-Ramsey Theory. Thirty years later, Jungić et al started investigating anti-van der Waerden problems where the sets being colored were
\{1,2,\dots,n\}=[n]
and
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and the rainbow substructures were
3
-term arithmetic progressions (see
[
9
,
10
]
). These papers focused on the conditions on the sizes of the color classes that guarantee rainbow arithmetic progressions. The anti-van der Waerden number was first defined in
[
15
]
by Uherka in 2013.
The question asked was: given a fixed value of
n
, what is the fewest number of colors needed to guarantee a rainbow
k
-term arithmetic progression in
[n]
or
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, denoted
\textup{aw}([n],k)
and
\textup{aw}(\mathbb{Z}_{n},k)
, respectively?
In
[
7
]
, Butler et al. bounded
\textup{aw}(\mathbb{Z}_{n},3)
based on the prime factorization of
n
and found a logarithmic bound for
\textup{aw}([n],3)
. The exact values of
\textup{aw}([n],3)
were then determined by Berikkyzy, Schulte and Young in
[
5
]
.
Young, in
[
18
]
, determined the anti-van der Waerden numbers for finite abelian groups based on the order of the group. Concurrently, authors noted that a
3
-term arithmetic progression
(a_{1},a_{1}+d,a_{1}+2d)
satisfies the equation
x_{1}+x_{3}=2x_{2}
and anti-van der Waerden numbers for Sidon sets and other linear equations were investigated (see
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
6
]
).

Investigations then turned to arithmetic progressions in graphs as it was observed that the path
P_{n}
behaves like
[n]
and the cycle
C_{n}
behave like
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.

Given a graph
G
, an
exact
r
-coloring of
G
is a surjective function
c:V(G)\to[1,\dots,r]
. An
arithmetic progression
in
G
of length
k
(
k
-AP) with common difference

is a list of vertices
(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
such that
\textup{d}(v_{i},v_{i+1})=d
for
1\leq i<k
.
An arithmetic progression is

if all of the vertices are colored distinctly.
The fewest number of colors that guarantees a rainbow
k
-AP is called the
anti-van der Waerden number of
G
and is denoted
\textup{aw}(G,k)
. To show

we construct an exact
(r-1)
-coloring that avoids rainbow
k
-APs. To show
\textup{aw}(G,k)\leq r
, we show that every exact
r
-coloring gives a rainbow
k
-AP.

The definition of
\textup{aw}(G,k)
was introduced in
[
13
]
and provided the first results about graph products. In particular, Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
have proven to be essential in most results about
\textup{aw}(G\square H,k)
.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
13
]
)
.

For
m,n\geq 2
,

\textup{aw}(P_{m}\square P_{n},3)=\begin{cases}3&\text{if $m=2$ and $n$ is even, or $m=3$ and $n$ is odd,}\\
4&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

A subgraph
H
of
G
is

we have

.
Theorem

within

to either show that our coloring must have a rainbow within the isometric subgraph or that we know we can color the isometric subgraph in some way and avoid rainbows. This result was used extensively when determining
\textup{aw}(P_{m}\square C_{n},3)
in
[

Theorem 1.2

(
[

]
)

If

and

Authors investigated

on trees and graphs with small diameter in
[

]
, graph products of paths and cycles in
[

, and graph products of trees

Theorem 1.3
.

Let

\textup{aw}(T\square T^{\prime},3)\hskip-1.42271pt=\hskip-1.42271pt\begin{cases}3&\text{if $T$ or $T^{\prime}$ is weakly non-$3$-peripheral or isomorphic to $P_{2}$},\\
4&\text{if $T$ and $T^{\prime}$ are both strongly non-$3$-peripheral and not $P_{2}$}.\end{cases}

Corollary 1.4

Let

\textup{aw}(T\square T^{\prime},3)\hskip-1.42271pt=\hskip-2.84544pt\begin{cases}&\text{if $T$ or $T^{\prime}$ is $3$-peripheral, or}\\
3&\text{$\textup{diam}(T\square T^{\prime})$ is even and $T$ or $T^{\prime}$ is $P_{2}$, or}\\
&\text{$\textup{diam}(T\square T^{\prime})$ is even and $T$ or $T^{\prime}$ is weakly non-$3$-peripheral,}\\

Theorem 2.11

(Jordan 1869)
.

The center of a tree consists of one vertex or two vertices.

In general, the center of a graph need not be connected, and in fact, components of the center can be arbitrarily far apart. Consider, for example, a

with a leaf on every other vertex. For trees, this is not the case. We include the following useful observation and its proof for completeness.

Observation 2.12

All diameter paths in a tree
T
intersect every central vertex of
T
. In particular, all diameter paths in a tree
T
intersect.

Proof.

Suppose we have a diameter path
P
with peripheral vertices
u

to be the center of

to be the center of

. Let

and assume

. Since
\textup{rad}(T)\in\left\{\frac{\textup{diam}(T)}{2},\frac{\textup{diam}(T)+1}{2}\right\}
, there exists some

such that

. But now, either
d(u,x)=d(u,w)+d(w,x)
or

. Since
d(v,w),d(u,w)\geq\frac{\textup{diam}(T)-1}{2}
, either case gives a distance larger than the diameter, a contradiction.

Thus,

. Theorem

implies the center of our tree is one or two vertices depending on the parity of the diameter. This means

in either case. So,

, our desired result.
∎

3
Strongly and weakly peripheral trees

In this section, we prove our main theorem below which classifies the anti-van der Waerden number of all products of trees when the diameter is even.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let

and
T^{\prime}
be nontrivial trees, where
\textup{diam}(T\square T^{\prime})

\textup{aw}(T\square T^{\prime},3)=\begin{cases}3&\text{if $T$ or $T^{\prime}$ is weakly non-$3$-peripheral or isomorphic to $P_{2}$},\\
4&\text{if $T$ and $T^{\prime}$ are both strongly non-$3$-peripheral and not $P_{2}$}.\end{cases}

4

-peripheral, then the anti-van der Waerden number is

The first step in this process is handling when the anti-van der Waerden number is four, which as we stated is when both trees are strongly non-

-peripheral. To achieve this, Theorem

implies that it suffices to provide a

-AP free. Lemma

The intuition of why these definitions are important can be seen by exploring past papers. In

Corollary 1.4
.

Let

T^{\prime}
be trees. Then,

\textup{aw}(T\square T^{\prime},3)=\begin{cases}&\text{if $T$ or $T^{\prime}$ is $3$-peripheral, or}\\

&\text{$\textup{diam}(T\square T^{\prime})$ is even and $T$ or $T^{\prime}$ is weakly non-$3$-peripheral,}\\
4&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
