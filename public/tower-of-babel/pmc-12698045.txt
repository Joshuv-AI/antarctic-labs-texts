Trends and determinants of 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine revaccination among older adults in Shanghai, China, 2013–2023: A database-based study
Authors: Kuan Wan, Lewen Wang, Caihong Gao, Ruijie Gong, Wanjing Lin, Jingyi Ye, Qiangsong Wu, Mi Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPneumococcal disease remains a significant public health concern among older adults in China. The 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPV23) has been shown to reduce the incidence of pneumococcal diseases, and timely revaccination helps maintain high levels of protective antibodies. However, data on PPV23 revaccination practices in this population remain limited. This study analyzed vaccination records from Shanghai’s Immunization Program Information System (2013–2023) to evaluate PPV23 revaccination rates, temporal trends, and associated determinants (gender, household registration, residence, type of first dose, other vaccination history) among older adults. Joinpoint regression assessed temporal trends, while a 1:1 matched case-control analysis with conditional logistic regression and restricted cubic splines identified determinants. Among 136,454 eligible individuals, the overall revaccination rate was low (4.3%). Rates declined with increasing age in successive birth cohorts. From 2018 to 2023, annual revaccination rates showed a non-significant downward trend (APC = −15.9, 95% CI: −38.1–14.2). Multivariable analysis identified three determinants independently associated with PPV23 revaccination. Both urban residency (adjusted OR = 6.43, 95% CI: 5.54–7.46) and prior influenza vaccination (adjusted OR = 9.09, 95% CI: 7.91–10.45) were strongly associated with higher revaccination odds. Conversely, COVID-19 vaccination (adjusted OR = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.14–0.26) were associated with substantially lower revaccination odds. These findings highlight the generally low PPV23 revaccination coverage among older adults in Shanghai, particularly among suburban residents, and individuals without a history of influenza vaccination. Public health strategies should prioritize education – particularly in suburban areas – and integrate PPV23 with influenza vaccination programs to enhance uptake and mitigate pneumococcal disease burden.

Introduction

Bacterial infection is the second leading cause of death globally. Among 33 common pathogenic bacteria, Streptococcus pneumoniae ranks third, causing a total of 829,000 deaths in 2019.1 It affects all ages, yet older adults are the most vulnerable group of pneumococcal infections, carrying the heaviest load, and their pneumococcal disease burden rises with age increasing, especially those with chronic diseases and low resistance.2 China is aging much faster than other low- and middle-income countries. The seventh national census found 18.7% of citizens were ≥60 y in 2020.3 According to WHO estimates, this proportion will rise to 28% by 2040.4 From 2009 to 2017, the hospitalization rate of pneumonia among adults in China showed an upward trend, and the hospitalization rate was even higher among older adults aged 60 and above or those with underlying diseases.5 The proportions of pneumonia caused by pneumococcal infection in Chinese older adults varies by regions, varying from 28.0% to 71.5%,6 and it ranks as the fourth leading cause of death in this group, imposing a heavy burden.

Vaccination with pneumococcal vaccine is an effective strategy for preventing pneumococcal disease.6 Currently, the global supply of pneumococcal vaccines primarily consists of two main types: pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPV) and pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV). According to the “Vaccination schedule for Pneumococcal disease” published on the World Health Organization website (accessed 24 August 2025), only 37 countries and regions have included pneumococcal vaccines into their national immunization programs (NIP) for older adults.7 Among these, 12 include both PCV and PPV, 17 include only PPV, and 8 include only PCV.7 In China, the pneumococcal vaccine is categorized as a non-NIP vaccine, meaning it is voluntary and self-funded. Currently, only 23-valent PPV (PPV23) is licensed for adult vaccination in China, with products from multiple manufacturers (both domestic and imported) available in the market. Since 2013, several Chinese provinces and municipalities – including Shanghai, Shenzhen, and Beijing – have successively launched public health programs offering one free dose of pneumococcal vaccine to older adults.8 The PPV vaccine effectiveness against vaccine-type pneumococcal disease in older adults after receiving the first dose was 26% to 68%,9 54% against invasive pneumococcal disease,10 and 63% against community-acquired pneumonia.11 After PPV vaccination, the antibody levels initially increase and maintain a stable and effective level for about 5 y.6 However, antibody levels induce by vaccination wane over time, reducing effectiveness by half and increasing vaccine-preventable diseases incidence.6,12 Timely revaccination is a both important and effective way to avoid these risks and keep healthy. Studies indicate that PPV revaccination lowers pneumococcal diseases incidence and increases additional quality-adjusted life-y, especially for those older adults with chronic diseases and low resistance.12–14 In Japan, only 10% adults aged ≥60 y who had previously received PPV chose to be revaccinated, compared with 18% in the United States.15,16 Studies from developed countries indicate that multiple factors influence pneumococcal vaccine revaccination among older adults. These include age, smoking history, previous hospitalization for pneumonia, awareness of pneumococcal diseases and vaccines, and recommendations from peers or healthcare providers.17

Few studies have described PPV revaccination coverage, trends, or determinants among older adults in China. Shanghai is one of the cities experiencing the most rapid population aging in China. According to the seventh national census, 23.4% of Shanghai citizens were ≥60 y.3 In order to promote the health of older adults and fully implement “the Healthy China 2020 strategy,” since September 2013, Shanghai has implemented a major public health program providing one free PPV23 dose to all local residents aged ≥60 y. By June 2020, 1.56 million older adults had received this free dose, corresponding to an estimated coverage of 30%.18 This program provides a unique setting to examine revaccination patterns. The Shanghai Immunization Program Information System is the official system that records all vaccine information of every individual living in Shanghai. Therefore, we analyzed PPV23 revaccination among older adults in Shanghai from the system to describe current revaccination coverage, assess temporal trends, and identify determinants influencing revaccination behavior. The findings provide evidence to refine PPV23 vaccination strategies in Shanghai and offer valuable insights for other Chinese municipalities and regions using PPV23 vaccination programs.

Methods

Study individuals

We conducted a cross-sectional study using stratified convenience sampling in Shanghai, China. Urban Xuhui District and suburban Fengxian District served as study sites. From the Shanghai Immunization Program Information System, we identified residents aged ≥60 y who had received ≥1 PPV23 dose between 1 September 2013 and 31 December 2023. Exclusion criteria included: age <60 y at first PPV23 dose, interval <5 y between doses, first dose before September 2013, duplicate records, or missing key variables including gender, vaccination date, and vaccination clinic information. After exclusions, 136,454 individuals remained eligible. Of these, 5909 received a second PPV23 dose ≥5 y after the first (revaccination group), and 130,545 received only one dose (Figure 1). Data collection

We extracted PPV23 records for 136,454 eligible individuals from the Shanghai Immunization Program Information System. For each participant we also collected date of birth, gender, household registration, vaccination clinic, residence, date and type of the first PPV23 dose, date and type of any revaccination dose, influenza vaccination history (dates and number of doses), and COVID-19 vaccination history (dates and number of doses).

Study design

Revaccination was defined as receipt of ≥2 PPV23 doses separated by ≥5 y. Revaccination rate = (number of revaccinated individuals ÷ number of eligible individuals) × 100%. We first described revaccination patterns overall and by birth cohort from 2018 to 2023. Second, to identify determinants of PPV23 revaccination (including gender, age, household registration, residence, type of first dose, other vaccination history), we constructed a 1:1 matched case – control study. Cases were older adults who received a second PPV23 dose from 1 September 2018 to 31 December 2023 and the interval between the first and second doses is ≥5 y. Controls were older adults who had received only one PPV23 dose as of 31 December 2023. A 1:1 matching was implemented between cases and controls, with matching criteria including age at first PPV23 dose, and the year and month of initial vaccination. The final matched cohort included 11,732 participants (5,866 pairs). Research indicates that a history of influenza vaccination is associated with PPV23 revaccination uptake,15,17,19 and COVID-19 vaccination history may influence the acceptance of other vaccines.20,21 Given the annual recommendation for influenza vaccination and the multi-dose regimen of COVID-19 vaccines, we further examined the relationship between the number of doses received for both vaccines and PPV23 revaccination. Influenza vaccine doses were counted between the first PPV23 dose and 31 December 2023. Older adults in Shanghai began receiving COVID-19 vaccinations on 10 March 2021. COVID-19 vaccine doses were counted between 10 March 2021 and 31 December 2023. Additionally, we conducted a case-control analysis involving 2009 matched pairs to assess the correlation between COVID-19 vaccination history and PPV23 revaccination specifically for the period following the implementation of Shanghai’s COVID-19 vaccination program on March 10, 2021.

Statistical analysis

Data were managed in Excel and analyzed using SPSS 23.0 and R 4.5.0. Continuous variables are shown as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range, IQR); categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Group comparisons applied t-tests, Mann – Whitney U tests, or χ2 tests as appropriate. Conditional logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for revaccination determinants, including gender (male vs. female), household registration (local vs. non-local), residence (urban vs. suburban), type of first dose PPV23 (domestic vs. imported), other vaccination history (influenza and COVID-19). Restricted cubic splines were employed to assess relationships between influenza or COVID-19 vaccine doses and PPV23 revaccination. Joinpoint regression was used to evaluate temporal trends in revaccination rates from 2018 to 2023 and by birth cohorts, calculating annual percent change (APC). The APC denotes the slope of a given trend; APC < 0 indicates a yearly decline, whereas APC > 0 reflects a yearly increase. Significance of the trend change is tested with a t-test for the segment slope. All tests were two-sided, and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of study participants

From 2013 to 2018, a total of 136,454 older adults in Shanghai’s Xuhui and Fengxian Districts received at least one dose of PPV23, and 5,909 of these individuals received a second dose ≥5 y later, yielding a revaccination rate of 4.3%. The median age at revaccination was 74 y (IQR 70–79), and the median interval between first and second dose was 6.0 y (IQR 5.1–7.0). The cohort comprised 47.7% males and 52.3% females. Age distribution showed that 71.81% of participants were aged under 75 y, while 28.19% were aged 75 y or older. The vast majority (96.4%) were local residents. Residential distribution indicated that 55.8% resided in urban areas and 44.3% in suburban regions. Regarding vaccine type, domestic vaccines accounted for 98.5% of first doses, while imported vaccines constituted 1.5%. Revaccination patterns

From 2018 to 2023, annual revaccinations mirrored first-dose trends: a peak in 2020 followed by a trough in 2021–2022, and a rebound in 2023 (Supplementary Material Figure 1). During 2018–2020, 64.9% of revaccinations and 62.5% of first doses were administered. In 2021–2022, these proportions declined to 16.0% and 17.6%, respectively, then increased to 19.2% and 19.8% in 2023. Seasonally, 79.8% of revaccinations occurred in autumn-winter, 12.0% in spring, and 8.2% in summer from 2018 to 2023. First doses also peaked in autumn-winter (62.8%), followed by summer (21.3%), with spring lowest (15.9%) (Supplementary Material Figure 2).

Trends in revaccination

Joinpoint analysis showed that revaccination rates declined steadily with older age across the 1920s–1950s birth cohorts. From 2018 to 2023, the decline was steepest for the 1920s cohort (APC = −52.4, 95% CI: −64.2− −36.6), slowest for the 1940s cohort (APC = −28.6, 95% CI: −40.7– −14.2), and similar for the 1950s (APC = −41.9, 95% CI: −58.7– −18.1) and 1930s cohorts (APC = −43.7, 95% CI: −54.1– −31.0) (Figure 2). From 2018 to 2023, PPV23 revaccination rates in Shanghai older adults were steady during 2018–2020 (135.6–143.3 per 10,000 doses), declined sharply in 2021–2022 (34.4–43.0 per 10,000 doses), and rebounded to 86.0 per 10,000 doses in 2023. Joinpoint analysis showed a non-significant decline from 2018 to 2023 (APC = −15.9, 95% CI: −38.1–14.2) (Figure 3). By district, urban Xuhui consistently recorded higher revaccination rates than suburban Fengxian (151.2 vs. 20.1 per 10,000 doses; χ2 = 3090.98, P < .001). Neither district showed a significant annual decline: Xuhui: APC = −17.6 (95% CI: −39.5 ~ 12.3) and Fengxian: APC = −10.8 (95% CI: −32.5–17.9). Determinants of PPV23 revaccination among older adults in Shanghai, China.

Univariate analysis identified several factors significantly associated with PPV23 revaccination. Prior influenza vaccination demonstrated a strong positive association (OR = 11.56, 95% CI: 10.11–13.22). Urban residence was also significantly correlated with revaccination (OR = 9.14, 95% CI: 7.96–10.48). Additionally, local residents (OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.02–1.41) and the use of imported vaccine for the first dose (OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.06–1.72) showed a modest association. No significant association was observed for gender (OR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.92–1.07). After adjusting for gender, multivariate logistic regression confirmed that prior influenza vaccination (adjusted OR = 9.09, 95% CI: 7.91–10.45) and urban residence (adjusted OR = 6.43, 95% CI: 5.54–7.46) remained independently associated with PPV23 revaccination. The association observed for imported vaccine type in the univariate analysis was no longer statistically significant after adjustment (adjusted OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.65–1.26). Similarly, household registration status showed no significant association in the final model (adjusted OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 0.88–1.34).

Relationship between influenza vaccination doses and PPV23 revaccination

Influenza vaccine doses were grouped as 0 (reference), 1–3, 4–6, 7–9 and ≥10 doses. In the unadjusted model, the odds ratios for PPV23 revaccination increased steeply with each additional group: 1–3 doses, 10.60 (95% CI: 9.18–12.25); 4–6 doses, 16.00 (95% CI: 12.59–20.34); and 7–9 doses, 13.73 (95% CI: 8.88–21.25). After adjusting for gender, age at first PPV23 dose, vaccine type, residence, household registration and COVID-19 vaccination history, the associations remained significant and positive: 1–3 doses, 8.40 (95% CI: 7.23–9.77); 4–6 doses, 12.33 (95% CI: 9.61–15.81); and 7–9 doses, 10.79 (95% CI: 6.93–16.80). Receipt of ≥10 doses was not statistically significant (adjusted OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.26–3.67) (Table 3). Restricted cubic spline analysis, adjusted for the same covariates, demonstrated a non-linear relationship between influenza vaccination doses and PPV23 revaccination (P for non-linear < 0.001). Odds increased sharply to the third dose (OR = 15.9) and then declined (Figure 4(A)). Relationship between COVID-19 vaccination doses and PPV23 revaccination

Of the 2009 participants revaccinated after March 10, 2021, 1997 matched case-control pairs were selected (IQR: 62–71). Multivariable conditional logistic regression, adjusted for gender, age, first-dose vaccine type, residence, household registration, and influenza vaccination history, showed that any COVID-19 vaccination reduced the odds of revaccination (adjusted OR = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.14–0.26) (Supplementary Material Table 1). COVID-19 vaccination was categorized as 0 (reference), 1–2 and 3–5. In the adjusted model (Model 2), the odds of PPV23 revaccination decreased progressively with each additional group. Compared with no COVID-19 vaccination, recipients of 1–2 doses had an odds ratio of 0.37 (95% CI: 0.27–0.51), while those who received 3–5 doses showed a further reduction to 0.15 (95% CI: 0.11–0.21) (Table 4). Discussion

Our findings indicate that only approximately 4% of older adults in Shanghai received PPV23 revaccination, far below the 10% in Japan and 18% in the United States.15,16 Several factors may contribute to this low uptake. First, Shanghai provides only one free dose to residents aged ≥60 y; any subsequent doses must be paid out-of-pocket, despite evidence that free vaccines boost uptake.18,22 In contrast, some high-income countries use supplementary health-insurance schemes and government subsidies to cover any doses for high-risk groups, markedly removal economic barriers and increasing revaccination coverage.23 Second, several developed countries, including the United States, the United Kingdom, and the European Union, have adopted a sequential vaccination strategy – administering the first dose followed by booster dose at intervals every few y.24–26 These regions are transitioning from PPV to PCV or have already completed this substitution. However, the absence of national revaccination guidelines in China undermines the basis for healthcare providers to recommend revaccination and conduct related public education, while also potentially raising public concerns about adverse events associated with over-vaccination. Public education campaigns can enhance vaccine confidence and improve vaccination uptake.27 A study showed that clinicians and healthcare workers are concerned about the potential patient-doctor disputes caused by adverse reactions from inappropriate revaccination,28 reducing their willingness to recommend revaccination, which is a key determinant of revaccination.6,15,29–31 Third, vaccine hesitancy represents a significant barrier to vaccine uptake, with vaccine efficacy being a key factor.32 Evidence demonstrates that PCV provides superior protection against pneumococcal diseases compared to PPV and offers better cost-effectiveness.33,34 These advantages explain why many developed countries recommend PCV for older adults.24 The relatively lower efficacy of PPV may lead to doubts among older adults and medical personnel, resulting in a ‘wait-and-see’ attitude that reduces the likelihood of revaccination. Additionally, awareness of pneumococcal disease and vaccine effectiveness strongly influences uptake,35,36 yet Chinese older adults remain poorly informed and have limited knowledge.37,38 Because vaccine-induced immunity and vaccine efficacy wanes with time, revaccination is critical.39 It seems common that older adults’ coverage rate of PPV revaccination is much lower than the one of first dose: 4% vs 30% in Shanghai, 10% vs 33% in Japan and 18% vs 69% in the United States.15,16,18,40,41 Given the substantial burden of pneumococcal diseases in China, evolving global adult vaccination strategies,42 and evidence on revaccination effects,43 public health authorities should accelerate the inclusion of PCV into adult immunization schedules and evaluate the feasibility of a “PCV followed by one or more PPV23 doses” strategy. To improve revaccination rates among older adults, government agencies should consider reducing vaccine costs through medical insurance coverage or commercial health insurance schemes. Further integration into national or local immunization programs could enable free vaccine provision for this population. Technical guidance departments should develop and regularly update guidelines for pneumococcal vaccine revaccination in older adults. Additionally, multiple government departments, healthcare institutions, and social organizations should collaborate to implement diverse health education campaigns. These initiatives should aim to enhance older adults’ awareness and understanding of pneumococcal diseases and vaccination benefits to increase PPV23 uptake and reduce pneumococcal disease burden.

Our trend analysis revealed a clear inverse correlation: the period of the COVID-19 pandemic coincided with a marked downturn in PPV23 revaccination rates. We observed that revaccination counts increased from 2018 to 2020, declined in 2021–2022, and rebounded in 2023. This pattern largely reflects the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic: before COVID-19 vaccines were available, the public, especially older adults, felt uneasy and anxious about COVID-19,44 leading to increased vaccine willingness,45 and driving up both first-dose and revaccination uptake for PPV23, which preliminary evidence shows that public-health emergencies may increase coverage of associated vaccination. This increase also existed in other countries and regions in the world.19 Revaccination rates reached a nadir in 2021. This drop coincided with the 2021 national COVID-19 vaccine campaign, when the Chinese CDC advised separating COVID-19 vaccines from others until evidence for co-administration was available, which showed that the government’s policy and advice had a significant impact on vaccination.46 These findings align with our observation that any COVID-19 vaccination – and each additional dose – further reduces PPV23 revaccination probability. Once pandemic controls eased and COVID-19 vaccination slowed in 2023, PPV23 revaccinations rebounded, partially because after the COVID-19 epidemic, the older adults’ health consciousness and awareness of the importance of vaccines has greatly improved, and the suppressed demand for routine vaccination has been released.45 During the public health emergency, the diversification of vaccination information release, the level of emergency collaboration between health management departments and vaccinologists, and the public’s awareness of emergency protection can effectively promote and strengthen the implementation of vaccination.47 Therefore, government should be deeply involved in all aspects of vaccination, including boost cooperation among internal departments, fully exchange opinions with health experts, vigorously promote the benefits of vaccines and carry out practical and effective policies.

Simultaneously, our birth cohort analysis identified a clear and concerning pattern: revaccination rates exhibited a steady decline with each successively older cohort. Birth-cohort analysis showed that PPV23 revaccination declined steadily with increasing age. A qualitative study showed that the older the elderly, the more they tended to maintain the status quo and focus on their current health status, while neglecting future and long-term preventive effects.48 This may reflect waning immunity, vaccine contraindications and heightened safety concerns among the oldest adults.49,50 Older age is linked to greater lack of vaccine confidence and vaccine hesitancy, further explaining the decline.51 There is a worrying phenomenon that the older population who require vaccine protection, the lower actual revaccination coverage, which forms a vicious cycle. To resolve the dilemma of older adults’ vaccination, targeted education on safety and efficacy is therefore essential to reduce hesitancy and improve both initial and repeat PPV23 uptake. Moreover, community plays an important role in vaccine promotion.52 Community health workers should promptly notify and mobilize eligible elderly people to vaccinate and revaccinate, ensuring that every older adult has appropriate vaccine protection as far as possible.

Our conditional logistic regression identified a history of influenza vaccination as the strongest positive predictor of PPV23 revaccination. This phenomenon aligns with findings from studies conducted in Japan and the United Kingdom, where individuals with a history of influenza vaccination demonstrated significantly higher rates of PPV23 revaccination.15,17 Influenza vaccination history strongly predicts PPV23 revaccination, because previous influenza vaccination is associated with greater vaccine knowledge, recognition of revaccination needs, and reduced hesitancy.53 Co-administration of pneumococcal and influenza vaccines has been shown to reduce the respiratory disease burden in older adults with chronic conditions, further encouraging both first and repeat PPV23 doses.54,55

Furthermore, the same multivariate model highlighted urban residency as another major determinant, revealing a substantial disparity in revaccination access and uptake between geographic regions. A clear urban – suburban gap exists: urban districts have far higher revaccination rates, and urban residence independently predicts greater uptake. Socioeconomic status and access are key determinants of vaccination.8 Shanghai currently funds only the first PPV23 dose; lower suburban incomes likely hinder revaccination among suburban older adults. Moreover, the suburban older adults’ low level of knowledge, attitude, and risk perception regarding vaccines obstruct the orderly progress of vaccination.56 In addition, compared with urban areas, the public transportation is more backward and vaccination clinics are more scattered and less accessible in suburban areas. The longer distance also reduces the willingness of older adults in the suburbs to vaccinate and revaccinate.57 Currently, China is promoting adult vaccine prescriptions, combining general outpatient services with immunization to improve vaccination and revaccination.58 For suburban populations – the population with the lowest uptake – targeted measures such as financial subsidies and door-to-door vaccination services could improve availability and access.59,60

Using data from the Shanghai Immunization Program Information System, we examined PPV23 revaccination status and its determinants among older adults from 2013 to 2023. The large sample and trusted data sources strengthen the reliability of our findings. Several limitations should be noted. First, as PPV23 vaccination policies differ across regions and countries,7,42,61,62 the generalizability of our results may be limited. With the introduction of higher-valent PCVs, a growing number of high-income countries and regions now recommend that adults or high-risk groups receive either higher-valent PCV or a sequential schedule of “PCV followed by one or more PPV23 doses” for prevention of pneumococcal disease.42 Nevertheless, certain aspects of our findings – such as the determinants of revaccination – may still offer valuable insights for regions implementing the “PCV first followed by one or more PPV23 doses” strategy. Second, the use of a convenience sampling may introduce selection bias and limit the extrapolation of the findings to the broader population. However, the large sample of 136,545 individuals obtained through stratified cluster sampling helps mitigate potential bias to some extent. Additionally, our results may not be applicable to ineligible individuals for pneumococcal vaccination, such as patients who have undergone stem cell transplantation or those with graft-versus-host disease. Third, the data for this study were derived solely from the Shanghai Immunization Program Information System. Other potential determinants of revaccination – such as socioeconomic status, education level, and health literacy – warrant further investigation through questionnaires or in-depth interviews. Additionally, the system data did not capture real-time updates on mortality within the elderly cohort, which may have led to an underestimation of the revaccination rate and an overestimation of the age-related decline in revaccination.

Conclusion

PPV23 revaccination rate among older adults in Shanghai was approximately 4%, indicating low coverage. Revaccination rates peak in autumn – winter and decreases with increasing age. Revaccination behavior is influenced by prior vaccination history, and geographic region. Despite its clinical importance, there remains limited research on revaccination practices and its determinants in China. Given the substantial burden of pneumococcal disease among older adults in China, we urge health authorities to promptly adjust vaccination strategies for this population – including approving PCV for broader use. We primarily recommend the use of updated, higher-valent PCVs due to their optimal cost-effectiveness.63 In settings where higher-valent PCVs are unavailable, a sequential strategy of “lower-valent PCV followed by one or more PPV23 doses” may provide durable protection and broader serotype coverage.12 In addition, health authorities should implement integrated measures – such as issuing evidence-based guidelines, securing dedicated funding, strengthening health education, promoting vaccine prescriptions, and recommending co-administration strategies – improve pneumococcal vaccination coverage among older adults and reduce the burden of pneumococcal disease.64,65
