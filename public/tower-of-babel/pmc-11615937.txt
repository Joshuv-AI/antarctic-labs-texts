Elucidation of the Transformation of Coordination Polymer Intermediates into 2D Metal–Organic Framework Films during Chemical Vapor Deposition
Authors: Myeonggeun Choe, Sarah S. Park, Hee Cheul Choi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Chemical vapor deposition (CVD) is a rising tool to synthesize metal–organic framework (MOF) films. Despite growing interest and usage, its mechanism is less known. Especially, the identification of intermediates is crucial for understanding the growth mechanism and further controlling their structures. In this paper, we investigated the growth mechanism of 2D MOF Cu3(C6O6)2 film by CVD. We identified a novel intermediate phase: an octahedral coordination polymer that transforms into an edge-on-oriented 2D MOF. In situ grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering, X-ray absorption near-edge structure, high-resolution transmission electron microscopy, and Raman spectroscopy studies confirmed that this transformation involves the removal of pillars, leading to the formation of a square-planar 2D MOF. With the identification of intermediates, our study deepens the understanding of MOF film formation by CVD, which lays a foundation to control the structure.

Comprehending the mechanism of the formation of metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) is crucial for advancing fundamental knowledge and tailoring their properties for specific applications.1−4 The identification of intermediates is of special interest, providing valuable information about the reaction. There are many reports identifying intermediates of bulk MOFs formed by solution-phase or mechanochemical reactions.5−10 For example, Beamish-Cook et al. revealed that the formation of MOF-74 involves a four-step process with short-lived intermediates, including nonporous coordination polymers (CPs), prior to the crystallization of MOF-74.11 Zheng et al. demonstrated that MOF-5 formation occurs through a nonclassical mechanism, involving phase changes of a layered inorganic–organic composite and subsequent recrystallization, as shown by a microstructural study.12 In the case of chemical vapor deposition (CVD), the MOF formation mechanism is barely known. Recently, we reported the synthesis of 2D MOF Cu3(C6O6)2 thin films by CVD.13 Despite the advantages of CVD in isolating pure intermediate phases under various reaction conditions, the mechanism of vapor-phase synthesis remains largely unexplored. We now perform a mechanistic investigation of the CVD synthesis of Cu3(C6O6)2.

For this, we conducted in situ grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS), X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES), high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM), and Raman spectroscopy studies to identify the crystalline CP intermediate and its transformation into a 2D MOF. Our study unveils a previously unseen vapor-phase MOF synthesis mechanism, where pillar molecule removal from the pillar-inserted CP leads to a 2D MOF.

The CVD reaction for Cu3(C6O6)2 synthesis used two inner tubes containing bis(2,4-pentanedionato)copper(II) [Cu(acac)2] and tetrahydroxy-1,4-benzoquinone (THQ) as precursors, with a SiO2/Si substrate placed between the tubes (Figures 1a and S1). To isolate intermediates, time-dependent quenching experiments were conducted by stopping the reaction at designated temperatures based on the temperature–time graph of the furnace (Figure 1b). The reaction was stopped at various time points (2.5, 5, 7.5, 10, 25, 45, and 70 min), and samples were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD; Figure 1c). XRD revealed distinct peaks different from the final product, suggesting a potential intermediate phase. Assuming that the asterisked peaks represent the intermediate phase, the reaction conditions were adjusted accordingly to isolate the pure intermediate (Table S1). Under the adjusted conditions, a pure intermediate (INT) was obtained. The XRD pattern of the isolated phase matches that from the quenching experiment, confirming the successful isolation of INT (Figure 1d). INT formed a thin film with a high reflectivity over a large area (Figures 2a and S2).

HRTEM images and the corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns of INT and the product reveal notable structural differences. The product exhibits a 2D-layered honeycomb structure with a lattice distance of 1.2 nm (Figure S3a–d), while INT shows hexagonal symmetry with a lattice distance of 0.65 nm (Figure 2b). This hexagonal structure of INT resembles the ab plane of the reported Cu3(C6O6)1 2D CP (a = b = 7.5 Å, c = 2.9 Å, hexagonal space group), featuring a Kagome lattice (Figure 2c).14,15 However, synchrotron GIWAXS analysis indicates that the c parameter of INT is approximately 8.4n Å (n = integer), significantly longer than that of the reported Cu3(C6O6)1 2D CP (Figures 2d and S4). This discrepancy implies a novel structural variation in the axial direction of Cu within the Cu3(C6O6)1 2D CP.16

For a better understanding of the coordination geometry of INT, XANES analysis was employed. Figure 2e shows the Cu K-edge XANES spectra of Cu (light gray), Cu2O (linear geometry, gray), CuO [square-planar (SP) geometry, light blue], CuSO4 [octahedral (Oh) geometry, light red], product (SP, blue), and INT (red). The XANES spectra of INT and the product exhibit clear differences in shape, chemical shift, and white-line (W) intensity. The chemical shift of INT is larger than that of the product and similar to that of CuSO4. While W can be influenced by factors such as the oxidation state and local electronic structure, the W of INT matches that of CuSO4, suggesting similar electronic environments or coordination numbers.17,18 In contrast, the product and CuO, both with SP geometries, show a prominent shoulder (S), while INT exhibits a very weak S peak.

Derivative XANES spectra of INT and the product prominently display two peaks, labeled A and B (Figures 2f and S5 and Table S2). For an easier comparison, 0 eV represents the K-edge energy of each material.19 The K-edge energy is determined from the first peak in the derivative spectra of each material. Peak A originates from the tetragonal distortion in the metal cluster, and B represents the main absorption transition (1s → continuum), reflecting the difference in the axial geometry of the metal clusters.19 The A–B energy gap values can vary with the coordination geometry and provide an estimate for destabilization of the 4pz metal orbital.20 The SP geometry typically exhibits a larger A–B gap due to the greater d-orbital splitting compared to the Oh geometry. The Oh geometry, with its higher symmetry, tends to exhibit a smaller A–B gap. The A and B peaks of the product and INT show similarity to CuO and CuSO4, respectively, strongly supporting that INT assumes an Oh geometry, whereas the product is SP. Additionally, a weak pre-edge (P) corresponding to the 1s → 3d transition is also observed in INT, suggesting distorted Oh geometry (Figure S5).19 Therefore, very interestingly, INT is believed to have an Oh geometry, distinct from both the reported Cu3(C6O6)1 CP and the product [Cu3(C6O6)2]. This analysis confirms the distinct structural characteristics of INT, emphasizing its unique role in MOF formation. The transformation from INT to the product involves a significant structural rearrangement, particularly in the coordination of Cu, highlighting the complex nature of the MOF formation process.

On the basis of the distinct structural characteristics and coordination, we propose a detailed crystal structure of INT. The proposed INT structure features an elongated c of 8.515 Å compared to the reported Cu3(C6O6)1 2D CP. It includes THQ as pillars along the axial direction of Cu between the Cu3(C6O6)1 CP layer to make the Oh geometry (Figure 3a).16,21,22 The INT structure model, relaxed through density functional theory calculations and subsequently refined using Pawley refinement (a = 7.568 Å, b = 7.492 Å, c = 8.515 Å, α = 90°, β = 90°, and γ = 121.078°), supports these characteristics (Figure S6a). This structural model matches correctly with the HRTEM image (Figure S3e–g). Unlike the product, the Raman spectrum of INT exhibits more pronounced peaks at 1064 and 1680 cm–1, indicative of the uncoordinated C–O and C=O stretching of the pillar THQ, further supporting the structure (Figures 3b and S7).23−25 To verify the oxidation state and evaluate the proposed model, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used. High-resolution Cu 2p XPS spectra of INT and the product display Cu 2p2/3 peaks at 934.6 and 932.9 eV, with a satellite peak of Cu2+, indicating that the major oxidation state of Cu is +2, but both +2 and +1 states exist in a mixed-valence state (Figure S8).26−28 However, INT has a higher proportion of Cu+ compared to the product. This indicates that μ2-O coordination in the Cu3(C6O6)1 layer in INT shows Cu+,29 but additional axial coordination by the oxygen pillar increases Cu+ to Cu2+. The higher proportion of Cu+ in INT implies that not all Cu sites are occupied by pillars to form an Oh geometry. This is supported by the small peak at 8983.3 eV in the first derivative XANES spectrum of INT, which is attributed to the remaining SP geometry (Figure S5 and Table S2).

To further validate the structural model, we compared the simulated scattering data generated from the model to the experimental data. Figures 3c and S9 show the experimental GIWAXS pattern, where the Bragg positions are well-matched with the GIWAXS pattern.30 Additionally, the simulated XRD pattern also matches the integrated GIWAXS profile (Figure S6b). It is observed that the (001) peak intensity is lower than that of the (002) peak, which can be attributed to the structural instability and defects in the (001) layer (Figure S3d).31 Furthermore, we confirmed that the intensity ratio of the (001) and (002) peaks varies across different INT samples (Figure S10). This indicates that the structure incorporating THQ pillars is unstable and difficult to control.

To elucidate the MOF formation mechanism from INT, in situ GIWAXS experiments under vacuum were conducted by heating INT from room temperature (RT) to 250 °C in 10 °C intervals. Figure 4a shows that, during annealing of INT, the (001) and (002) planes of INT in qz disappear, while the (02k0) series of the product appears in qz. The (100) crystal plane of INT remains in qxy. A control experiment confirmed the transformation of INT to the product upon annealing in an Ar environment (Figure S11). This leads to the formation of 2D MOF Cu3(C6O6)2 and 2D CP Cu3(C6O6)1, both with SP geometry (Figure 4b). The fact that the XANES spectrum of the product lacks the Oh character but shows a clear SP feature suggests that all pillars are removed during the INT-to-product transformation. Our findings reveal that the Oh structure of INT converts into 2D MOF and 2D CP through pillar removal (Figure 4c,d). HRTEM images and FFT patterns (Figure 4c,d) provide evidence of this transformation. This discovery highlights a unique instance where the INT phase acts as a crucial transitional stage, facilitating the partial transformation of the MOF.

In conclusion, we have elucidated the mechanistic pathway of Cu3(C6O6)2 synthesis via a CVD method, emphasizing the formation and transformation of INT. Through detailed analyses, including XRD, HRTEM, GIWAXS, XANES, and XPS, we identified INT with an elongated c parameter and Oh geometry, by THQ acting as pillars. This INT undergoes a sequential transformation into a stable 2D MOF Cu3(C6O6)2 and a 2D CP Cu3(C6O6)1 upon annealing. This transformation illustrates the unique role of INT as a transition step in the MOF synthesis. Although it is not clear yet whether the transformation of pillar-inserted Oh CP into SP 2D MOF is a universal pathway, our findings provide a comprehensive understanding of the vapor-phase synthesis mechanism for the Cu3(C6O6)2 MOF film, revealing unprecedented insight into the structural evolution of MOFs. This work opens new avenues for exploring intermediate phases in MOF synthesis and their potential applications in advanced materials science.
