Different Questions, Different Models: Fine-Grained Evaluation of Uncertainty and Calibration in Clinical QA with LLMs
Alberto Testoni, Iacer Calixto

Abstract
Accurate and well-calibrated uncertainty estimates are essential for deploying large language models (LLMs) in high-stakes domains such as clinical decision support. We present a fine-grained evaluation of uncertainty estimation methods for clinical multiple-choice question answering, covering ten open-source LLMs (general-purpose, biomedical, and reasoning models) across two datasets, eleven medical specialties, and six question types. We compare standard single-generation and sampling-based methods, and present a case study exploring simple, single-pass estimators based on behavioral signals in reasoning traces. These lightweight methods approach the performance of Semantic Entropy while requiring only one generation. Our results reveal substantial variation across specialties and question types, underscoring the importance of selecting models based on both the nature of the question and model-specific strengths.

Introduction

Evaluation of uncertainty estimation methods across LLMs. Each subplot shows AUROC (discrimination) vs. ECE (calibration). Clinical documentation plays a central role in modern healthcare, encompassing large volumes of structured and unstructured text. Its increasing complexity and scale place growing burdens on clinicians and make information retrieval and interpretation difficult
(
Ehrenfeld and Wanderer 2018
;
Gesner et al. 2019
;
Tierney et al. 2024
)
. These challenges have intensified efforts toward automatic clinical NLP tasks such as summarization, information extraction, and medical question answering. Large Language Models (LLMs), with their ability to generate fluent text, transfer across domains, and perform contextual reasoning, have emerged as promising tools for processing complex medical documents
(
Thirunavukarasu et al. 2023
;
Singhal et al. 2023
;
Van Veen et al. 2024
)
.

Unlike more traditional NLP tasks, which benefit from mature benchmarks and well-characterized task formulations, clinical NLP remains a relatively emergent field, with limited benchmarking resources and a lack of consensus on best practices
(
Blagec et al. 2023
;
Au Yeung et al. 2023
;
Thirunavukarasu 2023
)
. Despite this, LLMs are increasingly being deployed in clinical contexts, often without rigorous evaluation of their reliability, calibration, or robustness. This gap in validation raises serious concerns about their trustworthiness in high-stakes applications such as medical question answering, where both factual accuracy and calibrated uncertainty are essential for safe and responsible deployment.

Estimating uncertainty in natural language generation tasks, particularly with LLMs, poses unique challenges. Unlike traditional classification settings, where the output space is discrete and well-defined, text generation involves an open-ended, high-dimensional output space that is both stochastic and autoregressive in nature
(
Baan et al. 2023
)
. Prior work has explored uncertainty quantification (UQ) techniques such as token-level confidence
(
Huang et al. 2023
)
and semantic consistency via sampling
(
Kuhn et al. 2023
;
Farquhar et al. 2024
)
, using calibration metrics like Expected Calibration Error and Brier Score
(
Van Calster et al. 2019
)
. However, relatively few studies have examined UQ in clinical NLP tasks, such as medical question answering. Although recently published, existing work in this direction
(
Wu et al. 2024
;
Savage et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
is limited in scope, evaluating only a small number of outdated models and excluding recent advances such as reasoning models and domain-specific LLMs. Moreover, these evaluations lack granularity, offering little insight into performance variation across medical specialties or question types – two clinically relevant dimensions that could enable more nuanced understanding of model capabilities and limitations.

In our work, we evaluate UQ methods for a wide range of different LLMs in clinical multiple-choice question answering across two datasets (S-MedQA and MedExQA), 11 medical specialties—six common and five less-represented ones—and we also introduce a new annotation that categorizes questions by type (e.g., diagnosis, definition, etc.). We assess uncertainty estimates from 10 general, reasoning, and biomedical LLMs. Our results demonstrate an advantage of reasoning models over other model types in terms of both accuracy and discrimination/calibration of their estimated uncertainty. However, this advantage does not generalize across all medical specialties and question types. Crucially, we frequently observe a mismatch between the models with the highest accuracy and those with the best calibration.

Starting from the observation that sampling-based methods consistently yield stronger uncertainty estimates (Figure
1
), we investigate the potential of a more efficient, single-generation approach as an effective surrogate for sampling-based UQ methods. To this aim, we introduce a method that leverages behavioral signals from the reasoning traces of LLMs—such as response length, self-questioning, and self-verification—as lightweight proxies for uncertainty, offering competitive performance without requiring multiple generations. Overall, our work underscores the importance of fine-grained evaluation in Clinical QA and offers practical guidance for selecting models based on specialty- and question-aware criteria.

2
Related Work

Uncertainty quantification has traditionally been studied in NLP tasks such as sentiment analysis, machine translation, and named entity recognition
(
Xiao and Wang 2019
;
Zerva et al. 2022
;
Hashimoto et al. 2024
)
. Ensuring well-calibrated predictions is essential in clinical NLP, where overconfidence can translate into risk for patients or diagnostic errors
(
Ghoshal et al. 2021
;
Kompa et al. 2021
;
Dawood et al. 2023
)
. With the advent of LLMs, recent work has proposed token-level confidence scores
(
Huang et al. 2023
)
, semantic entropy over multiple samples
(
Farquhar et al. 2024
)
, and prompt sensitivity analysis
(
Si et al. 2023
;
Zhou et al. 2023
)
to assess uncertainty
(
Shorinwa et al. 2024
)
. Yet, few of these approaches have been systematically evaluated in the clinical domain. Recent datasets such as MedQA
(
Jin et al. 2021
)
and MedExQA
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
have enabled more rigorous evaluation, but studies that examine uncertainty estimates in this context are sparse and often restricted to small model sets or outdated architectures
(
Wu et al. 2024
;
Savage et al. 2025
)
. Furthermore, evaluations typically focus on accuracy, neglecting how uncertainty estimates vary by clinical specialty or question type – two dimensions that are crucial for deployment in real-world healthcare environments.

Recent studies have compared general-purpose LLMs with domain-specific biomedical models on clinical tasks, showing mixed evidence on the advantage of biomedical LLMs against general-purpose models
(
Dorfner et al. 2024
)
. However, these comparisons pay little to no attention to uncertainty calibration and robustness. A recent and closely related study by
Savage et al. 2025
investigated uncertainty estimation methods for clinical QA. The authors found that sample consistency—assessing agreement across multiple answers generated via stochastic decoding—outperformed other approaches in terms of both discrimination and calibration, though the latter remained suboptimal. While their findings provide valuable initial insights, their evaluation is limited in several key ways. First, it focuses on a small set of outdated models, excluding recent advances such as instruction-tuned reasoning and domain-specific LLMs. Second, their analysis is restricted to diagnosis and treatment questions, overlooking other clinically relevant question types. Third, they do not examine how performance varies across medical specialties.

Datasets and Question Annotation

We focus on two dimensions that have been largely overlooked in prior work: medical specialty and question type.

By Medical Specialty

We leverage two English multiple-choice question answering datasets: S-MedQA
(
Yan et al. 2025
)
and MedExQA
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
.

S-MedQA
combines questions from MedQA
(
Jin et al. 2021
)
and MedMCQA
(
Pal et al. 2022
)
. The dataset introduced in S-MedQA includes a validated annotation pipeline that maps each question to one of 55 medical specialties recognized in the European Union. We consider the six most frequent specialties in the S-MedQA test set—
Gastroenterology, Cardiology, Obstetrics and Gynecology, Neurology, Infectious Diseases, and Pediatrics
, which we refer to in the following as ‘common specialities’.To ensure balanced evaluation across specialties while keeping the total number of questions manageable for computational and analytical purposes, we fix the sample size to 145 questions per specialty, resulting in a total of 870 questions. Each question includes four answer options and one correct answer.

MedExQA
focuses specifically on underrepresented medical specialities:
Biomedical Engineering
(143 questions in the MedExQA test set),
Clinical Laboratory Science
(372 questions),
Clinical Psychology
(106 questions),
Occupational Therapy
(189 questions), and
Speech Language Pathology
(130 questions), summing up to 940 questions. Questions in MedExQA were manually gathered from a variety of freely accessible online sources, such as mock tests and practice exams designed for specific medical specialties. Each question includes four answer options and one correct answer.

By Question Type

After manually inspecting a random sample of 50 questions from each dataset, we identify six clinically meaningful question types:
Diagnosis, Treatment, Diagnostic Test, Definition, Procedure/Operation
, and
Other
. We annotate all 1,810 questions using
Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct

(
Meta AI 2024b
)
, and validate its outputs against manual annotations from one of the authors on a sample of 100 questions. The agreement is high, with 94% accuracy; the few disagreements involve borderline cases of the broad
Other
category. Full details on the prompt, question type definitions, and distribution are provided in the Appendix.

4
Methodology

4.1
Extracting Uncertainty Estimates

Uncertainty quantification methods for LLMs can be broadly categorized into two groups:
single-pass
methods that require only one response, and
sampling-based
methods that rely on generating multiple responses for the same input, inspired by the self-consistency theory
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
. Most single-pass methods leverage token-level probabilities. In our work, we evaluate two representative approaches from this class. First, we compute the average token-level probability of the model’s response, following
Huang et al. 2023
. Consistent with prior findings
(
Savage et al. 2025
)
, we observed negligible differences when using the minimum or maximum token probability instead. We use responses generated via greedy decoding, as (a) sampling yields no consistent performance gain for this metric, and (b) greedy decoding ensures compatibility with the next method. Second, we include Greedy Negative Log-Likelihood (G-NLL), recently introduced by
Aichberger et al. 2024
, which computes the negative log-likelihood of the output sequence produced via greedy decoding. The authors provide a theoretical justification for G-NLL as an efficient proxy for model uncertainty, showing that it correlates well with confidence and calibration metrics.

For methods that rely on sampling multiple responses and evaluating their consistency, we adopt Semantic Entropy, inspired by
Kuhn et al. 2023
;
Farquhar et al. 2024
. Though closely related, the Sample Consistency approach in
Savage et al. 2025
quantifies uncertainty by measuring agreement across multiple generated outputs, while Semantic Entropy directly captures the entropy of the responses distribution. We adopt the latter given the multiple-choice nature of our task, grouping together responses that select the same answer option and computing entropy over the resulting answer distribution. The underlying assumption is that high semantic entropy reflects greater model uncertainty, as it indicates multiple competing interpretations or answers; conversely, low entropy reflects consistent semantic intent and thus higher confidence.
This method has been shown to outperform token-level confidence metrics in prior work, though it relies on repeated sampling to estimate uncertainty. We generate 10 responses per input, in line with previous work and the analyses presented in
Manakul et al. 2023
. While prior work has explored clustering strategies based on bidirectional entailment or GPT-4 annotations—each with its own trade-offs and potential sources of noise—we leverage the structure of our task: models are prompted to place their selected answer option in square brackets, allowing us to group responses reliably (details in Section
4.3
).

We evaluate 10 open-source instruction-tuned LLMs. The diverse model set enables a comprehensive evaluation of uncertainty estimation across architecture, scale, and domain specialization. The prompt used for all models, as well as additional details, are reported in the Appendix. We include six

models of varying sizes and architectures:
LLaMA-3.1-8B
,

,
LLaMA-3.2-3B
,
LLaMA-3.3-70B

(
Meta AI 2024a
)
, as well as
Mistral-7B-v0.2
and
Mixtral-8x7B-v0.1

(
Mistral AI 2023
)
; two
reasoning-optimized
models:
DeepSeek-R1-70B

(
Guo et al. 2025
)
and
s1.1-32B

(
Muennighoff et al. 2025
)
; and, finally, two
domain-specific biomedical
models:
Bio-Medical-LLaMA-3-8B

(
ContactDoctor 2024
)
and
LLaMA3-OpenBioLLM-70B

(
Ankit Pal 2024
)
, both fine-tuned on biomedical literature and clinical corpora.

4.3
Evaluation

Answer Extraction and Accuracy

Models are prompted to generate their selected answer option in square brackets (e.g.,
[C]
). We extract this using regular expressions. In cases where models deviate from the expected format (occurring in approximately 5–6% of responses – e.g., repeating the full answer text within brackets), we employ
LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct
to map the model response to one of the four answer options. For this task, we validate the accuracy of
LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct
against human annotations on a random sample of 100 model responses and find that the model achieves 92% accuracy. After extracting the predicted answer option, we compute accuracy by comparing it to the ground-truth label provided in the datasets. To control for potential position bias, we randomly shuffle the order of the answer options four times per question when using greedy decoding; during sampling (for Semantic Entropy), we shuffle the order at each generation (ten times per question).

Discrimination and Calibration

To evaluate the discrimination ability of uncertainty estimates, we compute the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC). This measures how well the uncertainty scores separate correct from incorrect predictions. An AUROC of 1.0 indicates perfect discrimination, while 0.5 corresponds to random guessing. We assess calibration using Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Brier Score. ECE measures the discrepancy between predicted confidence and actual accuracy, quantifying over- or under-confidence. Following
Rivera et al. 2024
;
Savage et al. 2025
, we use 10 bins for the implementation of ECE. The Brier Score captures the squared error between predicted confidence and correctness, providing a proper scoring rule that combines aspects of both calibration and sharpness. For both ECE and Brier Score, a value of 0 indicates perfect calibration.

DC Score

To enable aggregate comparisons of uncertainty estimation quality across models, we introduce a simple composite metric—
DC Score
—as the average of AUROC, ECE, and Brier Score, after scaling the latter two so that higher values consistently indicate better performance. Formally:

\text{DC Score}=\frac{\text{AUROC}+(1-\text{ECE})+(1-\text{Brier})}{3}

By design, a DC Score of 1 corresponds to perfect uncertainty estimation: ideal discrimination (
\text{AUROC}=1
) and perfect calibration (
\text{ECE}=0
,
\text{Brier Score}=0
).

Uncertainty Estimation Methods

We compare the three uncertainty estimation methods discussed in Section
4.1
to assess (a) which yields the most calibrated and discriminative estimates, and (b) whether model type or size systematically affect them. Figure

The top-left quadrant marks the region of desirable performance (high discrimination, low miscalibration). As expected,

Based on these findings, we adopt Semantic Entropy as our primary uncertainty estimation method for the remainder of this section. We revisit single-generation alternatives in Section

Impact of Question Type

Turning to uncertainty estimation, Figure

Treatment
category—where

Definition

In summary, our findings highlight the importance of moving beyond overall model performance, emphasizing the need for fine-grained, specialty-aware and question-aware evaluation to ensure that uncertainty estimates remain reliable and informative across diverse clinical domains and question types. Without such granularity, models may appear robust on average while failing in specific, high-risk areas where accurate and calibrated predictions are most critical. This analysis also offers practical guidance for practitioners, enabling more informed model selection based on the specialty under consideration, and suggesting opportunities for ensemble methods that combine complementary strengths across models.

6
Behavioural Signals of Uncertainty in Reasoning Models: A Case Study

Figure 4:
Comparison of uncertainty estimation methods for
DeepSeek-R1-70B
and
s1.1-32B
. Our proposed methods, based on behavioral features (shown in bold), offer strong performance across AUROC, ECE, and Brier Score, approaching that of Semantic Entropy, which instead relies on multiple generations via sampling.

In Section
5
, we found that Semantic Entropy generally yields well-calibrated uncertainty estimates across a wide range of model types and sizes. While performance varies across medical specialties and question types, Semantic Entropy substantially outperforms methods based on sentence-level logprobs in both calibration and discrimination. While effective, the method entails repeated generation per input (10 samples): this feature may hinder its deployment in real-world applications, particularly in latency-sensitive or resource-constrained settings, such as clinical decision support. In contrast, single-pass methods like G-NLL and average token probability require only one forward pass per input, albeit with reduced discrimination and calibration performance.

To address these limitations, we investigate whether behavioral signals observed in the reasoning traces of LLMs can act as lightweight proxies for uncertainty. In our experiments, we notice that models such as
DeepSeek-R1-70B
and
s1.1-32B
frequently produce intermediate reasoning steps containing linguistic cues that may correlate with uncertainty. For instance, we observe frequent use of self-directed questions and tokens like “Wait…”, which appear to initiate self-verification or reconsideration. Additionally, we also hypothesize that longer responses may signal greater difficulty in arriving at a final answer. See Appendix
D
for an illustrative example of these features. Interestingly, the authors of
s1.1-32B
demonstrate the effectiveness of using “Wait” tokens for test-time scaling (see
Muennighoff et al. 2025
for more details).

To operationalize these hypotheses, we extract three surface-level behavioral features from each model response: (1) the number of tokens in the reasoning trace, (2) the number of self-posed questions, and (3) the number of occurrences of the token “Wait”. We then explore two strategies for turning these features into uncertainty estimates.

The first is a simple normalized sum of the three features, referred to as the
Linear Sum
method. Let
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
, and
x_{3}
denote the normalized values of the three features, and compute the uncertainty estimate as
u=x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}
. The second approach treats the three features as input to a supervised model: either a
Logistic Regression
or a
Calibration Regression
(the former models a sigmoid likelihood, the latter fits a linear function with L2 regularization as
Ridge Regression
). To train these models, we use ground-truth correctness labels (1 for correct, 0 for incorrect) associated with each response. Formally, the model learns a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow[0,1]
. Although the regression models are trained to predict correctness, we hypothesize that their outputs can serve as proxies for confidence. We define uncertainty as their complement,
u=1-f(\mathbf{x})
, enabling direct comparison with other uncertainty estimation methods.
To ensure a fair comparison and avoid overfitting, we train the regression models on 40% of the dataset while evaluating on the remaining 60%, preserving the original distribution of medical specialties. All responses are generated via greedy decoding to enable direct comparison with single-pass baselines such as G-NLL and average token probability. We assess the quality of the behavioral uncertainty signals using AUROC, ECE, and Brier Score. To further disentangle model behavior, we report results separately for common and uncommon medical specialties (see Appendix
D.3
for the latter). We include results with Semantic Entropy for comparison, noting that it relies on repeated sampling to estimate uncertainty.

The results, reported in Figure
4
, reveal that our lightweight behavioral feature-based methods—
Linear Sum
,
Logistic Regression
, and
Calibration Regression
— outperform other sentence-level methods and closely match the performance of Semantic Entropy, while requiring only a single forward pass per input. This pattern holds for both reasoning LLMs,
DeepSeek-R1-70B
and
s1.1-32B
. Even the simple
Linear Sum
method outperforms token-level baselines like G-NLL and average token log-probability in most cases, particularly in discrimination (AUROC).
Calibration Regression
performs consistently well, especially in terms of calibration. In Appendix
D.2
, we provide further details on the regression model coefficients.

Overall, these results support our hypothesis that behavioral cues in reasoning traces encode relevant information about model uncertainty, and highlight a promising direction for efficient, lightweight, and interpretable uncertainty estimation without requiring sampling or token-level probabilities. This has important implications for real-world applications, where uncertainty needs to be estimated reliably and efficiently – for instance, in settings such as clinical decision support, where both latency and interpretability are critical. Results on less-represented specialties are reported in Appendix
D.3
. They confirm the effectiveness of our approach for
DeepSeek-R1-70B
, while showing limited improvement for
s1.1-32B
, highlighting the need for model-specific tuning or more expressive behavioral features in lower-resource settings. Our case study offers a starting point for future work that should validate this approach across a broader range of tasks, datasets, and models. We view our contribution as an initial step toward establishing a more general framework for behavioral uncertainty estimation that leverages the output of recently released reasoning LLMs.

7
Discussion and Conclusion

Our work presents a fine-grained evaluation of uncertainty estimation methods for LLMs in clinical multiple-choice question answering. Our study spans ten LLMs across general-purpose, biomedical, and reasoning models, evaluated on two datasets, eleven medical specialties, and six question types. Our results reveal substantial variation in the calibration of uncertainty estimates by specialty and question type, with high-accuracy models not always yielding well-calibrated estimates. Despite the lower accuracy, we highlight the potential of small LLMs, such as
LLaMA-3.2-3B
, to produce well-calibrated uncertainty estimates, an essential feature for clinical support applications. Moreover, we find that the recently-released
s1.1-32B
, a sample-efficient reasoning model trained on just 1K curated examples, stands out among other models, demonstrating the value of strategic data curation and reasoning-focused objectives. Our findings underscore the limitations of aggregate reporting and highlight the value of adaptive, context-aware strategies for uncertainty estimation in clinical NLP and beyond, as well as providing actionable guidelines for practitioners to leverage the strengths of different models.

While Semantic Entropy achieves the best overall performance in calibration and discrimination, its reliance on multiple generations may limit its practicality in certain settings. To address this, we present a case study where we introduce a single-pass, interpretable method based on behavioral cues in reasoning traces (such as length, self-questions, and self-verification tokens) that closely match Semantic Entropy’s performance and outperform standard token-probability baselines, paving the way for lightweight ensembling and model selection strategies.

Limitations

In the following, we discuss some limitations of our experimental setup.

•

First, we focus on multiple-choice question answering datasets. While this setup ensures clear correctness labels and scalable evaluation, it does not capture the full spectrum of clinical NLP tasks, particularly those involving free-form generation. We believe this focus is justified as a first step: to the best of our knowledge, ours is the first study to systematically evaluate uncertainty estimation across model types, medical specialties, and question types at this scale. Nonetheless, future work should extend this analysis to more open-ended clinical scenarios.

•

Second, we rely on automated extraction of model predictions and question-type annotations. Although our heuristics and model-assisted annotations are validated against manual labels with high agreement, some residual noise may persist, especially for ambiguous questions or borderline categories (e.g., “Other”).

•

Third, although our model set is diverse (spanning general-purpose, reasoning, and biomedical LLMs), it remains limited to representative open-source models available at the time of writing and does not include closed-source proprietary models.

•

Fourth, while we show that behavioral features offer a promising, interpretable alternative to multi-sample methods like Semantic Entropy, our feature space is deliberately simple and task-specific. Generalizing these findings to other tasks, languages, or reasoning paradigms will require broader validation and potentially more sophisticated feature design.

•

Lastly, we do not apply post-hoc calibration techniques such as temperature scaling. Our focus is on comparing raw uncertainty estimates without additional tuning. Applying temperature scaling consistently is challenging in our setup, as models differ in tokenization schemes and output structure – e.g., in how they format answers (direct label vs. more complex sentences), affecting how likelihoods are computed and making calibration non-trivial. We leave the integration of model-specific calibration as an important direction for future work. As for additional considerations on the temperature setup and decoding details, see Appendix
C
.

Appendix C
Decoding Details

For experiments requiring multiple samples, we reviewed commonly suggested decoding configurations for nucleus sampling to approximate how language models are typically used in practice. Based on this review, we set the top-
p
value to 0.9 and the temperature to 0.6. As an exploratory analysis, we also repeated our experiments using a higher temperature of 1.0 (Table
3
). We found that this change had little effect on the overall ranking of models in terms of accuracy or on the calibration and discrimination performance of uncertainty estimates derived from Semantic Entropy. However, for
DeepSeek-R1-70B
, we observed a decline in both accuracy and calibration. This effect warrants further investigation, which would require a held-out validation set and a systematic analysis across a broader range of temperature values. Such an analysis might ultimately support model-specific or even task-specific decoding configurations, varying across models, medical specialties, or question types. In this work, however, we adhere to decoding settings—temperature and top-
p
—that reflect typical real-world usage, especially for users with limited technical knowledge. As for annotating question types and mapping the responses to the corresponding option, we employ greedy decoding.

Appendix E
Additional Details on Uncertainty Quantification Methods

First, we chose not to include confidence elicitation methods that directly prompt LLMs to report a confidence score. While such approaches have been explored in prior work, their reliability remains limited. In particular,
Savage et al. 2025
report poor performance and calibration when relying on model-generated confidence scores.

Second, for methods based on log-probabilities (logprobs), we carefully considered how to handle outputs from models that include explicit reasoning traces. A notable example is
DeepSeek-R1-70B
, whose responses are structured with clearly delimited reasoning segments, marked by special tokens such as
<think>
. In these cases, a key decision is whether to compute uncertainty scores over the entire response (including the reasoning trace) or only over the final answer portion. After empirical inspection and preliminary analyses, we found that restricting the computation of logprobs to the final answer—excluding the reasoning trace—produced more reliable and comparable uncertainty estimates.
