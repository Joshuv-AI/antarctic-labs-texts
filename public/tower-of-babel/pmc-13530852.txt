Clinical Benefit of Pembrolizumab in Lynch Syndrome‐Associated Advanced Ovarian Cancer With Germline MSH6 Variant: A Case Report
Authors: Tomomi Yokozawa, Sayako Nakagawa, Yukio Suzuki, Aya Tokinaga‐Uchiyama, Katsuyuki Konnai, Hiroto Narimatsu, Yukihiko Hiroshima, Haruya Saji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis case highlights the importance of genetic evaluation during treatment in young patients with advanced ovarian cancer. Lynch syndrome caused by a germline MSH6 pathogenic variant was diagnosed during first‐line maintenance therapy, and pembrolizumab achieved a durable response in recurrent disease.Keywords: comprehensive genomic profiling, lynch syndrome, MSH6, ovarian cancer, pembrolizumab

Introduction

Lynch syndrome (LS) is an autosomal dominant hereditary cancer syndrome characterized by the familial development of malignancies caused by germline pathogenic variants in mismatch repair (MMR) genes, including MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, and EPCAM. Colorectal cancer, endometrial cancer, gastric cancer, ovarian cancer (OC), pancreatic cancer, biliary tract cancer, small intestinal cancer, and cancers of the renal pelvis and ureter are recognized as LS‐associated malignancies [1]. In LS‐associated colorectal cancer, surgical resection is recommended for resectable disease, whereas immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are recommended for recurrent or unresectable disease, similar to the treatment strategy for deficient MMR (dMMR)/microsatellite instability‐high (MSI‐H) colorectal cancer [2, 3]. However, treatment guidelines for LS‐associated OC have not yet been established, and ICIs are not routinely used in the standard treatment for OC [4, 5, 6]. Here, we present the case of a young woman with stage IVB LS‐associated OC, in whom early genetic diagnosis through comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) led to successful treatment with pembrolizumab, highlighting the importance of early genetic diagnosis and the potential role of ICIs in LS‐associated OC.

Case History/Examination

A 33‐year‐old woman, gravida 0, para 0, presented to our hospital with right flank pain for 2 months and gross hematuria for 1 month. The patient's medical history was notable for a deep vein thrombosis of the lower extremity diagnosed 8 months prior, for which she had been receiving anticoagulant therapy. Her family history was notable for endometrial cancer in her mother and in two additional maternal relatives as well as gastric cancer in four maternal relatives (Figure 1).

Investigations, Diagnosis and Treatment

Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed a pelvic mass and right‐sided hydronephrosis caused by ureteral obstruction at the tumor site. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed an irregular 57 × 35 mm mass arising from the right ovary, with invasion of the distal right ureter and diffusion restriction (Figure 2). Positron emission tomography with fluorodeoxyglucose showed increased uptake in the left obturator, right common iliac, and left supraclavicular lymph nodes, leading to the diagnosis of multiple lymph node metastases (Figure 3). Laboratory testing revealed an elevated serum cancer antigen 125 (CA125) level of 274 U/mL (reference range, < 35 U/mL).

The right ureter is obstructed by the ovarian tumor, and decreased fluorodeoxyglucose uptake in the right kidney suggests impaired renal function (white arrowhead).

A diagnostic CT‐guided biopsy of the right ovarian tumor revealed malignant cells consistent with adenocarcinoma. Based on these findings, the patient was clinically diagnosed with International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics 2014 stage IVB OC, corresponding to cT2bN1aM1b according to the 8th edition of the Union for International Cancer Control TNM classification.

Given the presence of multiple lymph node metastases and anticipated difficulty in achieving optimal cytoreduction, neoadjuvant chemotherapy was selected. The patient received 3 cycles of paclitaxel (175 mg/m2) plus carboplatin (area under the curve, 5) every 3 weeks. Following chemotherapy, the serum CA125 level decreased to 135.8 U/mL after 3 cycles; however, repeat CT revealed no remarkable radiological change, and the disease was assessed as stable. Chemotherapy was continued; however, after 5 cycles, the serum CA125 level increased to 168 U/mL. Because the distant metastatic left supraclavicular lymph node remained reduced in size, interval debulking surgery was performed (Figure 4).

The tumor invaded the right ureter, resulting in a nonfunctional right kidney. Therefore, in collaboration with urologists, en bloc resection of the tumor with concomitant right nephroureterectomy was performed. The tumor originating from the right ovary encased the right ureter, extended caudally to invade the bladder muscularis, and laterally formed a conglomerate mass with the obturator lymph nodes, showing firm adhesion to the external iliac vessels. Total abdominal hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo‐oophorectomy, omentectomy, and right nephroureterectomy were performed; however, approximately 3 cm of residual tumor remained owing to firm adhesion to the pelvic vessels and pelvic wall, resulting in suboptimal debulking surgery. Histopathological examination revealed residual adenocarcinoma components in the right ovary. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated p53 positivity in 20%–30% of tumor cells, while napsin A was negative. Although immunohistochemistry showed estrogen receptor negativity, which was atypical, squamous differentiation was observed, and the tumor was diagnosed as Grade 2 endometrioid carcinoma (Figure 5).

Areas of squamous differentiation are also observed. (b) Hematoxylin and eosin staining (original magnification ×400) shows tumor cells with round to oval nuclei and marked nuclear pleomorphism.

Postoperatively, combination chemotherapy with gemcitabine (1000 mg/m2 on Days 1 and 8) and carboplatin (area under the curve, 4 on Day 1) was administered every 3 weeks, along with bevacizumab (15 mg/kg on Day 1). After nine cycles, contrast‐enhanced CT revealed a partial response; therefore, maintenance therapy with bevacizumab monotherapy was initiated 6 months after surgery.

Genetic testing of the tumor biopsy specimen obtained during neoadjuvant chemotherapy revealed no pathogenic variants in BRCA1 or BRCA2, and homologous recombination deficiency testing was negative, with a genomic instability score (GIS) of 2. Given the patient's family history and anticipated poor sensitivity to chemotherapy, which suggested an early recurrence, and the marked concern of both the patient and her family regarding hereditary cancer, CGP testing was performed during maintenance therapy with bevacizumab. CGP was performed using a specimen obtained via CT‐guided biopsy before the initiation of chemotherapy; the analysis was performed using the FoundationOneCDx assay (Chugai Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd.). The tumor cell content was estimated to be 25%. Although the MSI status was inconclusive, the tumor mutation burden was high (22.93 mutations/Mb), leading to the recommendation of pembrolizumab as an insurance‐covered therapeutic option. In addition, a somatic pathogenic variant of the MSH6 gene was identified (variant allele frequency = 53.15%). Following genetic counseling, a germline pathogenic variant in MSH6 was confirmed using a blood sample, leading to the diagnosis of LS. Following this diagnosis, we planned to continue treatment for OC while conducting surveillance for other malignancies through regular upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy and urinalysis.

CT performed after 12 cycles of bevacizumab maintenance therapy revealed regrowth of the residual tumor, and the disease was assessed as progressive. Although recurrence occurred at a time when platinum rechallenge was generally considered appropriate (with a platinum‐free interval of 9 months), pembrolizumab (200 mg/body every 3 weeks) was initiated because of the limited response to prior chemotherapy. After one cycle, the serum CA125 level decreased to baseline, and a partial response was observed at 3 months. Pembrolizumab was administered for 15 months, achieving durable tumor control without clinically significant adverse events.

Discussion

This case highlights two important clinical insights. First, in young patients with advanced OC and a suggestive family history, early suspicion of LS and timely genetic diagnosis during the initial phase of treatment are crucial for optimizing the management strategies. Second, this case suggests that ICIs may play a key therapeutic role in LS‐associated OC.

The lifetime risk of OC in patients with LS and in those carrying pathogenic variants in the MSH6 gene has been reported to be approximately 6%–24% and 1%–13%, respectively [7, 8, 9, 10]. Approximately 20%–25% of OCs are hereditary tumor syndromes; about 90% are attributable to hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (HBOC), and the remaining 10% are attributable to other gene variants, including LS [6, 11]. In Japan, although germline BRCA1/2 testing for patients with OC is covered by national health insurance, this testing is not necessarily performed routinely at diagnosis. In addition, while genetic evaluation for LS is recommended by academic societies, universal germline testing for LS has not yet been widely implemented in routine clinical practice because of reimbursement and practical limitations. In this case, the patient developed OC in the context of a strong family history, with her mother and maternal relatives having been diagnosed with endometrial cancer in their 40s or 50s, and both the patient and her family were aware of a potential hereditary predisposition. Based on the detailed family history, LS was diagnosed at a relatively early stage after the initiation of treatment for advanced OC using CGP. In this case, CGP was performed using the tumor tissue–based FoundationOneCDx assay. Because the evaluation was conducted before the introduction of paired tumor‐normal genomic profiling, germline testing was required separately to determine whether the detected MSH6 pathogenic variant was of hereditary origin. In addition to family history, awareness of the characteristic features of LS‐associated OC is important. The median age at diagnosis is 63 years for sporadic OC [6] and 52 years for the HBOC [12]. LS‐associated OC is diagnosed at a younger age, with a median age of 42–48 years [9, 10], and approximately 6% of cases have been reported to occur in patients aged < 35 years [10]. With respect to histological subtypes, sporadic OC is predominantly composed of high‐grade serous carcinoma (70%–80%), followed by endometrioid carcinoma (10%–20%) and clear cell carcinoma (5%–10%) [6, 13]. In contrast, > 90% of OCs associated with the HBOC are of the high‐grade serous subtype [13]. In contrast, LS‐associated OC shows a low proportion of high‐grade serous carcinoma (20%–30%) and a high frequency of endometrioid carcinoma (30%–50%) [10, 14]. This case involved a young patient with endometrioid carcinoma, consistent with the clinicopathological features of LS‐associated OC. In LS, colorectal cancer is the most common first primary malignancy; however, endometrial cancers and OCs account for approximately 23% and 6% of the initial cancers, respectively [15]. These findings highlight the importance of gynecologists in diagnosing LS through the recognition of gynecological malignancies.

The standard treatment for early‐stage OC consists of surgical resection with comprehensive staging, followed by adjuvant platinum‐based chemotherapy. In contrast, advanced epithelial OC is managed with cytoreductive surgery, systemic chemotherapy, and individualized maintenance therapy [4, 5, 6]. Although poly (ADP‐ribose) polymerase inhibitor maintenance therapy has been shown to significantly prolong progression‐free survival and is used in approximately 80% of advanced OC cases [16, 17, 18], its benefits appear to be limited in patients with low‐grade serous carcinomas, BRCA wild‐type tumors, and suboptimal responses to initial chemotherapy [19, 20, 21]. Among BRCA wild‐type tumors, patients with low GIS are unlikely to derive a progression‐free survival benefit from poly (ADP‐ribose) polymerase inhibitor maintenance therapy [22], whereas patients with unfavorable KELIM (the modeled CA125 elimination rate constant K), a kinetic parameter reflecting tumor chemosensitivity, are reported to benefit from bevacizumab [23]. Therefore, given the low GIS score of 2 and unfavorable KELIM of 0.59, bevacizumab was selected as maintenance therapy in this case.

LS‐associated tumors arise from biallelic inactivation of MMR genes, resulting in dMMR with impaired correction of replication errors and consequently a high mutational burden, manifested as MSI [24]. Pembrolizumab, an anti‐programmed cell death protein 1 therapy, has shown superiority over investigator's choice chemotherapy and is now the standard treatment for MSI‐H/dMMR metastatic colorectal cancer [2, 25]; its efficacy in MSI‐H/dMMR noncolorectal cancers has also been demonstrated [26]. In the KEYNOTE‐158 trial, pembrolizumab demonstrated an objective response rate of 33.1% in 15 patients with previously treated MSI‐H/dMMR OC [26]. Although the comparative efficacy of ICIs used for LS‐associated and sporadic MSI‐H/dMMR cancers remains unclear in real‐world settings, the characteristics of infiltrating immune cells suggest that LS‐associated MSI‐H/dMMR cancers are more sensitive to immune checkpoint blockade than sporadic MSI‐H/dMMR cancers [27, 28]. To our knowledge, only a few cases of ICI therapy for LS‐associated OC have been reported in the literature, and pembrolizumab induced a complete response in a patient with platinum‐resistant recurrent OC with a pathogenic MSH2 variant [29].

In colorectal and endometrial cancers, routine MMR and MSI testing is recommended by clinical guidelines to guide treatment decisions. In contrast, routine MMR/MSI testing is not currently recommended for ovarian cancer. Nevertheless, MSI‐H is observed in approximately 6% of ovarian endometrioid carcinomas, a frequency that is higher than in many other ovarian cancer histological subtypes. Therefore, failure to consider LS or MMR/MSI status in patients with ovarian cancer may result in missed opportunities for treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors. In this case, as the tumor mutation burden‐high status had not been identified preoperatively, surgery was performed following neoadjuvant chemotherapy. However, had this been known, treatment with pembrolizumab without surgery might have been considered. Furthermore, the efficacy of ICIs has been reported in cases of fertility‐sparing treatment for LS‐associated endometrial cancer, including one case complicated by OC [30]. Although young BRCA1 mutation carriers have been suggested to have decreased ovarian reserve compared with wild‐type women [31], current evidence in women with LS is limited and does not support an association with decreased ovarian reserve [32, 33]. This represents a promising area for future therapeutic advancement in LS‐associated OC, which often affects younger patients.

In conclusion, we report a case of recurrent OC in a young patient in whom ICI therapy was effective after a diagnosis of LS with a pathogenic MSH6 variant identified through CGP during first‐line treatment. This case highlights the clinical importance of diagnosing LS at an early stage of treatment, not only to inform therapeutic decision‐making but also to optimize long‐term surveillance strategies. Further studies are warranted to clarify the efficacy of ICIs and determine the optimal timing of ICI administration in the management of LS‐associated OC.
