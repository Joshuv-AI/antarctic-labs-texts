Face covers and rooted minors in bounded genus graphs
Samuel Fiorini, Stefan Kober, Michał T. Seweryn, Abhinav Shantanam, Yelena Yuditsky

Abstract
A rooted graph is a graph together with a designated vertex subset, called the roots . In this paper, we consider rooted graphs embedded in a fixed surface. A collection of faces of the embedding is a face cover if every root is incident to some face in the collection. We prove that every 3 3 -connected, rooted graph that has no rooted K 2 , t K_{2,t} minor and is embedded in a surface of Euler genus g g , has a face cover whose size is upper-bounded by some function of g g and t t , provided that the face-width of the embedding is large enough in terms of g g . In the planar case, we prove an unconditional O ⁡ ( t 4 ) O(t^{4}) upper bound, improving a result of Böhme and Mohar [ BM02 ] . The higher genus case was claimed without a proof by Böhme, Kawarabayashi, Maharry and Mohar [ BKMM08 ] .

2.3
Graphs on surfaces

This section establishes some background notions needed for
Theorem

2
. See the book by Armstrong
[
Arm79
]
for an introduction to topology, or that by Mohar and Thomassen
[
MT01
]
for a thorough treatment of graphs on surfaces.

A
surface

\Sigma
is a non-empty compact connected Hausdorff topological space in which every point has a neighborhood that is homeomorphic to the plane. We will encounter slightly more general topological spaces, which we define right away. A
surface (possibly) with boundary
is a topological space
\Pi
that can be obtained from a surface
\Sigma
by deleting the interiors of pairwise disjoint closed disks
\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{c}\subseteq\Sigma
(we allow
c=0
). The boundaries of the disks
\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{c}
are the
cuffs
of
\Pi
.

Some examples of surfaces with boundary are: a closed disk (obtained from a sphere by removing the interior of a single closed disk), a Möbius band (obtained from a projective plane by removing the interior of a single closed disk), and a cylinder (obtained from a sphere by removing the interiors of two disjoint closed disks).

The operation of gluing a closed disk along its boundary to a cuff of a surface with boundary is called
capping
the cuff. Capping all cuffs of a surface with boundary yields a surface. The surface obtained from
\Pi
by capping all the cuffs is denoted by
\widehat{\Pi}
.

For
h,\gamma,c\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\Pi(h,\gamma,c)
denote the surface with boundary obtained from a sphere by removing the interiors of
2h+\gamma+c
pairwise disjoint closed disks and gluing
h
cylinders and
\gamma
Möbius strips along any
2h+\gamma
cuffs (each cylinder is glued along two cuffs and each Möbius band along one), leaving the remaining
c
cuffs untouched. It is known that every surface with boundary is homeomorphic to
\Pi(h,\gamma,c)
for some
h,\gamma,c\in\mathbb{N}
.
4
4

4

We remark that

\gamma
are however not uniquely determined, since
\Pi(h,\gamma,c)
is homeomorphic to

.

Consider a surface with boundary
\Pi
.
A

\Pi

The simple curve

A loop contained in the interior of

4.1

Let

Lemma 13
.

Let
G
be a
3
-connected graph embedded in a fixed surface
\Sigma
with
\fw(G)\geqslant 3
and let
(\Pi,\Pi^{+})
be a pair of nested surfaces with boundary contained in
\Sigma
and contoured by
G
.
Then there exists a
3
-connected surface minor
H
of
G\cap\Pi^{+}
that agrees with
G
in the neighborhood of
\Pi
.

Proof.

We define
H
as the surface minor of
G\cap\Pi^{+}
obtained by contracting each component of
(G\cap\Pi^{+})-V(G\cap\Pi)
into a single vertex.
We obtain an embedding of
H
in
\widehat{\Pi^{+}}
by modifying the canonical embedding of
G\cap\Pi^{+}
in this surface. Specifically, we continuously contract the components of
(G\cap\Pi^{+})-V(G\cap\Pi)
and their enclosed faces into single points.
This contraction step can be modeled by a continuous map
\sigma:\widehat{\Pi^{+}}\to\widehat{\Pi^{+}}
, in such a way that the embedding of

is the image under
\sigma
of the embedding of
G\cap\Pi^{+}
.
We choose
\sigma
to be injective outside of the components of
(G\cap\Pi^{+})-V(G\cap\Pi)
, and the identity within
\Pi
. This can be done without loss of generality.

.

agrees with

in the neighborhood of
\Pi
. Since
\sigma
is the identity within
\Pi
, we see immediately that

. Now consider some face

. We let

denote the corresponding face of

as

If

is contained in

Therefore, we may assume that

is contained in
\widehat{\Pi^{+}}-\Pi

We observe that
\sigma^{-1}(f)
is a face of

since we obtained

from

Because

is the identity within

It remains to show that

is

We begin by inspecting the

of

, that is, the vertices that corresponds to a contracted component of

has degree at least

.
Assume otherwise.

Notice that, since

and
\Pi^{+}
are nested, there is a collection

of pairwise disjoint closed disks contained in

such that

.

Let

be such that

is embedded in the interior of
\Delta_{i}

We can find a simple closed

-normal curve

,
Hence,

-normal curve in

\intt(\Pi^{+})
, intersecting

in at most two vertices, which are both on the boundary cycle of

.
If

.
Otherwise,

.

Next, we show that

is
3
-connected. We heavily rely on

5
, which we use repeatedly below (without explicit mention). Recall that an embedding is called polyhedral if each face is an open disk bounded by a cycle and every two facial cycles intersect in a vertex, an edge, or not at all. By hypothesis,

-connectivity of

.

Every face of
G
is an open disk bounded by a cycle. Since

Finally, consider two faces of

and

. Since

agrees with

in the neighborhood of

, we may assume that

and

Hence,

∎

Lemma 14
.

Let
(G,R)
be a rooted graph embedded in a surface
\Sigma
.
Let
\left\{(\Pi_{i},\Pi_{i}^{+})\right\}_{i\in[k]}
be surface cover of
\Sigma
with respect to
G
, and let
(H_{i})_{i\in[k]}
be a collection of corresponding minors, such that
H_{i}
agrees with
G
in the neighborhood of
\Pi_{i}
for each
i\in[k]
.
Let

denote the roots contained in
\Pi_{i}
for each
i\in[k]
.
If each
(H_{i},R_{i})
admits a face cover of size
\phi_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
, then
(G,R)
admits a face cover of size at most
\sum_{i\in[k]}\phi_{i}
.

Proof.

For each root
r\in R
, there is some
i=i(r)\in[k]
, such that
r\in\Pi_{i}
.
Therefore,
r\in R_{i}
and it is covered by some face
f
in the face cover of
(H_{i},R_{i})
.
Since
r\in\Pi_{i}
and
H_{i}
agrees with
G
in the neighborhood of
\Pi_{i}
, we can find a face
\varphi_{i}(f)
of

covering the same set of roots within
\Pi_{i}
. In particular,
\varphi_{i}(f)
covers

.
We add
\varphi_{i}(f)
to the face cover and repeat this process for every root in

.
We obtain a face cover of
(G,R)
, which is the union for
i\in[k]
of the image under
\varphi_{i}
of the face cover of
(H_{i},R_{i})
. The result follows.
∎

4.2
The general case

Let
G
denote a graph embedded in a surface
\Sigma
. A
planarizing set of cycles
is a set of disjoint cycles
\mathcal{C}
in
G
such that cutting along
\mathcal{C}
yields a graph
G^{\prime}
embedded in a disjoint union of
spheres with boundary

\Sigma^{\prime}
.
Notice that
\Sigma^{\prime}
is contoured by
G^{\prime}
.
Further, if
\mathcal{C}
is an inclusion-wise minimal planarizing set of cycles, then
\Sigma^{\prime}
has only one component, and thus is a
single
sphere with boundary.
(Indeed, otherwise there exists a two-sided cycle
C\in\mathcal{C}
such that undoing the operation of cutting along
C
joins two distinct connected components of
\Sigma^{\prime}
, hence
\mathcal{C}-\{C\}
is also planarizing, contradicting the minimality of
\mathcal{C}
.)
If
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
denotes the set of cycles in
G^{\prime}
corresponding to the cycles from
\mathcal{C}
in
G
,
then we call the tuple
(\mathcal{C},G^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})
a
planarization
of
G
and
\Sigma
. Notice that, whenever
\mathcal{C}
is inclusion-wise minimal
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
contains precisely
g
cycles, where
g
is the Euler genus of
\Sigma
.
Figure

3
above provides an example with
\mathcal{C}=\{C\}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\{C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime}\}
.

For a graph
G^{\prime}
embedded in a sphere with boundary
\Sigma^{\prime}
contoured by
G^{\prime}
, a cuff
C
and an integer
d\geqslant 1
, a
depth-
d
nest
around
C
is a sequence of disjoint cycles
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}(C)=(D_{0},\ldots,D_{d})
in
G^{\prime}
and closed disks
\Delta_{0}\subsetneq\Delta_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\Delta_{d}
in
\widehat{\Sigma^{\prime}}
, the sphere obtained from
\Sigma^{\prime}
by capping all cuffs, such that
D_{0}=C
, and for each
i\in\{0,\ldots,d\}
, cycle
D_{i}
is the boundary of
\Delta_{i}
.

A cycle
C^{\prime\prime}
in
G^{\prime}
is said to be

the nest

if
C^{\prime\prime}
is disjoint from the disk
\Delta_{d}
. Two depth-

nests
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=(D_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,D_{d}^{\prime})
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime}=(D_{0}^{\prime\prime},\ldots,D_{d}^{\prime\prime})
around distinct cuffs are

if
V(D_{i}^{\prime})\cap V(D_{j}^{\prime\prime})=\emptyset
for all
i,j\in\{0,\ldots,d\}
.

Theorem 16

(Yu
[

)
.

Let

Let

Then there exists a planarization

of

in

(which is embedded in

16
. Denote by

Using

Lemma 18
.

Fix
S\in\mathcal{S}
.
Then
(\Pi(S),\Pi(S^{+}))
is a pair of nested surfaces with boundary.
Furthermore,
\Pi(S)
and
\Pi(S^{+})
are projective planes with boundary if
S\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
and spheres with boundary otherwise.

Proof.

Let
S
be any member of
\mathcal{S}
. We invoke
Lemma

17
and conclude that
\Pi^{\prime}(S)
is a sphere with boundary whenever
S\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\mathcal{B}
, and the disjoint union of two spheres with boundary whenever
S=A_{i}\cup A_{j}\in\mathcal{A}_{2}
. (Recall that
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
are at distance at least
d/3\geqslant 4
whenever
i,j\in[g]
are distinct.)

Recall that
\pi
is injective outside of the
g
cuffs of
\Sigma^{\prime}
, namely, the cycles
D_{i,0}
for
i\in[g]
. In order to understand
\Pi(S)=\pi(\Pi^{\prime}(S))
topologically, we have to see which cuffs of
\Sigma^{\prime}
belong to
\Pi^{\prime}(S)
.

Observe that
\Pi^{\prime}(S)
contains at most two cuffs of
\Sigma^{\prime}
. More precisely,
S
contains exactly one cuff if
S\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
, two cuffs if
S\in\mathcal{A}_{2}
and no cuff if
S\in\mathcal{B}
. Furthermore, we made sure that if
S
contains one cuff, this cuff is mapped to a one-sided cycle in
\Sigma
by
\pi
, and if
S
contains two cuffs, these cuffs originate from the same two-sided cycle in
\Sigma
, and are both mapped to this cycle by
\pi
.

We derive the following consequences. First, if
S\in\mathcal{B}
, then
\Pi(S)
is a sphere with boundary. Second, if
S=A_{i}\cup A_{j}\in\mathcal{A}_{2}
, then
\Pi(S)
is obtained by gluing the spheres with boundary
\Pi^{\prime}(A_{i})
and
\Pi^{\prime}(A_{j})
along one common cuff, and is hence again a sphere with boundary. Third, if
S=A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
, then
\Pi(S)
is obtained from the sphere with boundary
\Pi^{\prime}(A_{i})
by identifying pairs of points within one cuff, and is thus a projective plane with boundary.

Notice that
\Pi(S^{+})
is obtained from
\Pi(S)
by gluing a sphere with boundary along each cuff of
\Pi(S)
. The spheres with boundary that are glued are pairwise disjoint, since the distance of

and
A_{j}
is at least

. Hence
\Pi(S)
and
\Pi(S^{+})
are nested. In particular,
\Pi(S^{+})
is a sphere with boundary exactly when
\Pi(S)
is and a projective plane with boundary exactly when
\Pi(S)
is. Their respective number of cuffs can of course differ.
∎

Lemma 19

For

Proof.

which intersects

Since the noose intersects

∎

By the above construction and

We define the
strong product
of two graphs
G
and
H
as
G\boxtimes H
, such that the vertex set corresponds to the cartesian product, that is,
V(G\boxtimes H):=V(G)\times V(H)
.
Further, we have an edge
(u,v)(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in E(G\boxtimes H)
if either
u=u^{\prime}
and
vv^{\prime}\in E(H)
, or
uu^{\prime}\in E(G)
and
v=v^{\prime}
, or
uu^{\prime}\in E(G)
and
vv^{\prime}\in E(H)
.

For
p\geqslant 3
and
n=2p
, we define the
bagel
as
B_{n}:=K_{2}\boxtimes C_{p}
, that is, the strong product of an edge and the cycle
C_{p}
. We denote the vertices of the first copy of
C_{p}
by
v_{1},\dots,v_{p}
, and the vertices of the second copy by
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}
. Note that there is an edge between
v_{i}
and
w_{j}
if and only if
i-j\in\{0,\pm 1\}
(recall that indices are computed cyclically). To turn
B_{n}
in a rooted graph, we mark each vertex as a root.

Proposition 20
.

For all even
n\geqslant 6
,
B_{n}
has no (rooted)
K_{2,5}
minor.

Proof.

Toward a contradiction, assume that
B_{n}
has a
K_{2,5}
-model
M
for some even
n=2p\geqslant 6
, and assume that
n
is minimum among such counterexamples.

As before, we denote by
M(x_{1})
and
M(x_{2})
the two branch sets that are adjacent to each
central
branch set
M(y_{1}),\dots,M(y_{5})
.
Since every vertex of
B_{n}
is a root, we can assume without loss of generality that each central branch set
M(y_{i})
contains a single vertex. Summing up, we have seven pairwise disjoint connected sets, namely
M(x_{1})
,
M(x_{2})
plus five singleton sets
M(y_{1})
, …,
M(y_{5})
, such that
M(y_{i})\subseteq N(M(x_{1}))\cap N(M(x_{2}))
holds for all
i\in[5]
.

In case there is some
i\in[2]
and
j\in[p]
such that
M(x_{i})
contains both
v_{j}
and
w_{j}
, we can safely delete one from
M(x_{i})
without destroying our
K_{2,5}
-model.
Observe further that for any
j\in[p]
, exchanging
v_{j}
and
w_{j}

yields a graph isomorphism of
B_{n}
.
Hence, we may assume without loss of generality that
M(x_{1})\subseteq\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{p}\}
and
M(x_{2})\subseteq\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{p}\}
.
Further,
M(x_{1})
and
M(x_{2})
induce connected subgraphs in their respective cycles, that is, each induces a path or a cycle.

Since there is a total of
5

, we can assume without loss of generality that at least
3
of them are contained in
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{p}\}
.
Thus in particular

cannot contain all vertices in

and it induces a path

.
Observe that for any such path

.
∎
