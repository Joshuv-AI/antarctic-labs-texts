Protocol for extrusion 3D bioprinting of human-scale cardiac patches using cardiac spheroid-laden bioinks
Authors: Niina Matthews, Carmine Gentile
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryExtrusion-based bioprinting is a promising approach for developing novel clinical therapies for heart failure patients. Here, we present a protocol for bioprinting personalized human-scale cardiac patches incorporating human cardiac spheroids in a 1% silk fibroin/4% alginate/8% gelatin hydrogel. We describe steps for extrusion 3D bioprinting using a temperature-controlled hydrogel bioink. We also detail adjusting print parameters for optimal results. Although prepared for a specific experimental setup, the techniques described can be applied to other temperature-controlled biomaterials or extrusion 3D bioprinters.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Roche et al.,1,2 Sharma et al.,3 and Vettori et al.4Subject areas: Cell culture, Organoids, Tissue Engineering

Before you begin

This protocol describes the steps for 3D bioprinting a personalised cardiac patch for the treatment of heart failure. The cardiac patch 3D design is based on patient-specific medical data, and the resulting G-code is tailored for printing two distinct regions (Figure 1). Bioprinting is performed using a CELLINK BIO X6 bioprinter (CELLINK, Gothenburg, Sweden) fitted with pneumatic printheads. Bioink incorporating human cardiac spheroids (CSs) is used to deposit 405 CSs into a scarring region specified in the cardiac patch design (Figure 1A). The patch is completed by bioprinting a larger outer area (Figure 1B) over the scarring region using 1% Silk Fibroin/4% Alginate/8% Gelatin (1%SF) hydrogel.

The protocol combines results from our previously published research, including co-culturing of human CSs,3 mixing a CS bioink using a 1%SF hydrogel base,4 and extrusion bioprinting of cardiac patches for the treatment of myocardial infarction.1,2 As a result, the protocol translates our previous studies from small animal models toward clinical application by scaling up the cardiac patch design and the biofabrication process.

Note: Although our protocol is written for a particular extrusion 3D bioprinter and hydrogel base, the underlying principles for adjusting print parameters can be applied to extrusion bioprinting of different temperature-controlled hydrogels with other extrusion 3D bioprinters. To optimise the protocol for other extrusion bioprinting systems, it is important to consider the ability to control print parameters, including temperature, pressure, and speed. Additionally, further parameter considerations may be relevant if a different combination of hydrogel base and cells is used for the same purpose.

It is important to note that varying the CS density and polymer concentrations may impact the rheological properties of the bioink. This could be evaluated using classic rheological methods, by using a rheometer, or in combination with non-destructive methods, such as a Brillouin system.4

CRITICAL: Before bioprinting the cardiac patch with CS bioink, performing test prints is essential for evaluating the design and hydrogel performance, and establishing optimal print parameters.

The final volumes of the 1%SF hydrogel and CS bioink are directly dependent on the experimental setup and the cardiac patch design. Establishing the 1%SF hydrogel volume for the scarring region is essential for preparing an accurate amount of CS bioink. Test prints are used to optimise bioprinter settings, modify the 3D design, and determine the hydrogel volume for printing the cardiac patch. Physical dimensions, infill parameters, and corresponding 1%SF hydrogel volumes used for bioprinting the cardiac patch in this protocol are presented in Table 1. Changing the dimensions, the number of layers, or the infill parameters will affect the required 1%SF hydrogel volume. Similarly, due to the temperature-controlled nature of the 1%SF hydrogel, ambient conditions and batch-to-batch variability may affect the hydrogel’s requirements, as evident from the parameter range for the 1%SF hydrogel volume in Table 1.

CRITICAL: Maintaining sterility is imperative for preventing biological contamination. Ensure that all materials, equipment, and work areas are sterilized before starting and use aseptic laboratory techniques throughout the protocol.

Innovation

To our knowledge, the protocol described here is one of the first strategies for 3D bioprinting a personalised human-size cardiac patch containing CSs. The complex cardiac patch 3D design is based on patient-specific medical data and incorporates human CSs into a targeted area for treatment of heart failure.

The protocol could provide a significant translational step from small animal models toward clinical application. Steps in the protocol merge existing methods from our previously published research into a single workflow.

Institutional permissions

Human CSs used in this protocol are prepared from commercially available cells and do not require ethics approval. If the use of the protocol requires ethics approval, acquire permissions according to relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations.

Expected outcomes

Optimal bioprinting results are typically a compromise between cell viability, print resolution, and performance and durability of the construct after printing. The critical factor is maintaining cell viability, this is particularly relevant for large bioprints that require prolonged printing time. Depending on the hydrogel composition the extruded filament may swell significantly, and ionic crosslinking and incubation in media are likely to cause further filament expansion. As a result, bioprinted cardiac patches change in appearance and may not be visually perfect.

When bioprinted successfully, CSs demonstrate strong contractile activity, e.g., fractional shortening % and contraction frequency,2,4 and the cardiac patch maintains structural integrity in culture medium post-printing.1,4 Specifically, we would expect cell viability above 95% at 28 days after printing, contraction frequency at 0.25–0.5 Hz, and fractional shortening % between 0.5 – 1%. In mechanical testing 1%SF hydrogel based bioinks typically expand 1.6–2.2 times to the bioprinter needle lumen size and maintain ≥90% level durability 28 days after bioprinting. Optimal bioprint

Bioink extrudes in a smooth, continuous strand, layer height, and bioink coverage is consistent on all layers (Figure 9A). The resulting bioprint has high resolution with minimal bioink expansion (Figure 9B). Crosslinked patch can be handled without breaking the printed structure (Figure 9C).

Poor definition of the print pattern

The print pattern may not form clearly if the bioink is extruded at a high flow rate. Over-extrusion leads to loss of design geometry as the excess bioink typically spreads downwards, widening the filament (Figures 10A–10C, see troubleshooting, problem 4). This is often caused by high print pressure, which can be reduced during printing with immediate effect. If the printhead temperature is too high, the bioink becomes less viscous making the hydrogel runny. Lowering the printhead temperature is effective in controlling the viscosity but may require several min to impact the flow rate. In addition, large bioprints with several layers and long print times require constant monitoring of the print height, as bioink expansion can increase the height of the printed layers.

Variability in print results

Temperature-controlled biomaterials react to environmental conditions. Bioinks containing gelatin are sensitive to changes in the ambient temperature and humidity. Variability in print results can therefore be expected between hydrogel batches using natural polymers, across patches bioprinted on different days, and poor control over the ambient environment (Figures 11A–11C, see troubleshooting, problem 5). Key factors for a successful bioprint include thorough testing of the print design and biomaterials, continuous adjustment of print parameters, and setting clear expectations for what constitutes an acceptable print result. If the bioprinted design maintains structural integrity and, most importantly, supports the CSs incorporated in the print, the print result can be considered successful regardless of variability in the physical appearance of the bioprinted patch.

Print height, printhead speed, and extrusion pressure are adjusted continuously during printing as the parameters have an immediate effect on bioink flow. As with controlling the filament spread, adjusting the printhead temperature impacts bioink viscosity but is not instantaneous. Printed layers may seem uneven if the extruded bioink does not form a continuous filament (Figures 12A and 12B, see troubleshooting, problem 5). If the Z-axis distance between the needle and the print surface is too high, the extruded filament may not attach properly to the surface below. Similarly, if the printhead moves too fast, the needle will pull the bioink away leaving visible gaps in the filament. Bringing the needle closer to the print surface often resolves this issue. If the print height is correct, increasing the pressure or reducing print speed can improve bioink flow from drops to a solid strand. Poor lamination of layers compromises biomechanical performance of the cardiac patch. Delamination of layers is typically observed after a 10-12 h incubation period in media. If the layers are separated, bioprinted patches lose their structural integrity and must be discarded (Figure 12C).

Limitations

Working with temperature-controlled biomaterials adds an element of uncertainty to biofabrication processes. Even in a regulated laboratory setting, environmental factors including ambient temperature and humidity have a direct effect on the print parameters. In addition, hydrogel properties such as viscosity may be subject to batch-to-batch variability.

Large bioprints, such as the cardiac patch described here, can have a print time of up to 45 min. The prolonged print time may impact the hydrogel performance and lead to drying of the printed layers.
