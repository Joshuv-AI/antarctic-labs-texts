Stronger Language Models Produce More Human-Like Errors
Andrew Keenan RichardsonRyan Othniel Kearns, Sean MossVincent Wang-Maścianica, Philipp Koralus The Laboratory for Human-Centered AI (HAI Lab)Institute for Ethics in AIFaculty of PhilosophyUniversity of Oxford

Abstract
Do language models converge toward human-like reasoning patterns as they improve? We provide surprising evidence that while overall reasoning capabilities increase with model sophistication, the nature of errors increasingly mirrors predictable human reasoning fallacies: a previously unobserved inverse scaling phenomenon. To investigate this question, we apply the Erotetic Theory of Reasoning (ETR), a formal cognitive framework with empirical support for predicting human reasoning outcomes. Using the open-source package PyETR, we generate logical reasoning problems where humans predictably err, evaluating responses from 38 language models across 383 reasoning tasks. Our analysis indicates that as models advance in general capability (as measured by Chatbot Arena scores), the proportion of their incorrect answers that align with ETR-predicted human fallacies tends to increase ( ρ = 0.360 , p = 0.0265 \rho=0.360,p=0.0265 ). Notably, as we observe no correlation between model sophistication and logical correctness on these tasks, this shift in error patterns toward human-likeness occurs independently of error rate. These findings challenge the prevailing view that scaling language models naturally obtains normative rationality, suggesting instead a convergence toward human-like cognition inclusive of our characteristic biases and limitations, as we further confirm by demonstrating order-effects in language model reasoning.

Introduction

As language models rapidly advance in capabilities, they increasingly demonstrate sophisticated reasoning abilities across diverse domains
[
1
,
2
]
. A naive prediction would have future language models achieving perfect rationality simply through scale. However, as these models become more capable of mimicking human language and problem-solving, do they also inherit our systematic reasoning errors?

Human reasoning exhibits robust patterns of fallacious inference that have been extensively documented in cognitive science
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
. These patterns are not random mistakes, but systematic deviations from normative standards that persist even among educated participants and that follow predictable patterns across diverse reasoning tasks. The study of these fallacies has been central to our understanding of human cognition and has profound implications for fields ranging from economics to medical decision-making.

Our work investigates whether increasingly sophisticated language models show a similar pattern of fallacious reasoning to humans. This question has both theoretical and practical significance. Theoretically, it provides insight into whether human-like reasoning errors emerge naturally from systems trained on human-generated text. Practically, it raises important considerations for AI systems deployed in contexts requiring reliable reasoning, such as medical diagnosis, legal analysis, or high-stakes decision support
[
8
,
9
]
.

To rigorously investigate this question, we leverage the Erotetic Theory of Reasoning (ETR)
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
, a formal cognitive theory that precisely predicts human reasoning patterns across multiple domains. ETR posits that human reasoning operates by maintaining disjunctive alternatives and filtering these alternatives when taking on new information, a process that can systematically lead to error. ETR provides formal specifications of both how and when humans will make specific reasoning errors, allowing us to generate arbitrary reasoning problems with predictable failure patterns.

ETR has been implemented as PyETR
[
13
]
, an open-source Python library. Using PyETR, we mechanically generate reasoning problems predicted to lead human-like reasoners into making errors. For our experiments we fix a diverse corpus of 383 such problems for which ETR predicts a specific fallacious response.

Our experimental investigation evaluates 38 language models ranging from smaller models (e.g.,
Mistral 7B Instruct v0.1
) to state-of-the-art systems (e.g.,
GPT-4.5
,
Claude 3.7
), using Chatbot Arena Elo as a proxy for model sophistication
[
14
]
. Our central finding is striking: as model capability increases, models become significantly more likely to commit human-like reasoning errors (
\rho=0.360,p=0.0265
).

This finding represents a notable example of an inverse scaling law in AI development—where model improvement on general metrics corresponds to decreased performance on specific desirable properties. While language models have demonstrated improved performance with scale on many reasoning tasks
[
1
,
15
]
, our work suggests that they may simultaneously become more susceptible to human-like reasoning fallacies. Beyond practical implications, our findings provide novel evidence relevant to the debate on whether LLMs develop internal reasoning processes analogous to human cognition, or simply mimic surface-level patterns from their training data.

Contributions

(1) We empirically demonstrate a significant inverse scaling relationship between language model sophistication and susceptibility to human-like reasoning fallacies.
(2) We provide a theoretical analysis connecting our findings to broader questions about the emergence of human-like cognitive properties in language models.
(3) With our methodology, we give the first substantial application of PyETR, an open-source computational implementation of the Erotetic Theory of Reasoning, to generate reasoning problems with predictable human-like failure patterns.

The Erotetic Theory of Reason

Modern understanding of human reasoning reveals a fascinating duality: remarkable achievements coexist with persistent, predictable errors. The Erotetic Theory of Reasoning (ETR) provides a unified framework explaining both capacities through a single mechanism: humans construe problems as questions requiring answers, mentally representing disjunctive alternatives that they filter based on how well they match new information. This filtering process, where we prematurely dispose of alternatives before incorporating new data, enables cognitive efficiency but introduces systematic biases. Importantly, ETR demonstrates that reasoning success or failure depends not on separate cognitive systems but on whether one achieves “erotetic equilibrium,” a state where conclusions remain stable against potential follow-up questions.

At its core, ETR (full formalism in Appendix
A
) proposes a computational model where reasoning operates through three key mechanisms: (1) maintaining disjunctive alternatives representing possible answers to implicit questions, (2) filtering these alternatives by best-match with new information, potentially eliminating relevant possibilities prematurely, and (3) correcting potential errors by raising explicit questions that reintroduce necessary alternatives. This conceptually parsimonious framework has demonstrated remarkable empirical success in predicting systematic reasoning fallacies across propositional and first-order logic, probability theory, and decision-making. Mathematical proofs further establish that as reasoners approach complete erotetic equilibrium by raising appropriate questions, their conclusions converge to normative standards including logical soundness and completeness, Bayesian updating, and expected utility maximization, making ETR a compelling explanation for human reasoning as a unified cognitive capacity whose errors arise from computational constraints rather than irrationality.

Methodology

In our experiment, a
reasoning problem
is given by a list of views (the
premises
), and the problem is to answer the question “what if anything follows?”. We are most interested in reasoning problems where the ETR-predicted answer to this question is a logical fallacy.

3.2
Generative pipeline

We define a collection of mutation functions that give slight modifications to ETR view objects. These functions serve to expand our bank of reasoning problems by modifying existing problems by single premises. Table
2
presents the complete list of possible mutation rules.

Table 2
:

Mutation Type

Description

Introduces a new constant symbol to the formal system.

Adds a new variable to the set of arbitrary objects.

Generating a new problem proceeds by iterative building a list of views (the premises in the problem).
A new view is selected at random from the original problem bank and subjected to a random number of random mutation rules, it is added to the list if ETR predicts the problem admits a non-trivial answer. New views are added until all of the following stopping conditions are met.
(1) The problem is the right size, meaning that summing the number of atoms in each view gives between 4 and 11. If 11 is exceeded, backtracking is used. (2) The ETR-predicted conclusion contains a single alternative (no disjunctions). (3) The ETR-predicted conclusion is a logical fallacy.
To mitigate concerns about dataset-specific effects, we intentionally generated problems across diverse domains and structures. The PyETR-based generation ensures our problems test reasoning capacities rather than memorization of training examples.

3.3
Mapping to natural language

To evaluate language model reasoning in a more naturalistic context, we developed a systematic approach to convert formal logical statements into natural language prompts. We created 12 themes for natural language framings of logical problems, such as a researcher figuring out the properties of a novel element or a newly discovered creature. For each theme, we established consistent mappings between logical elements and thematic equivalents. The thematic conversion process serves multiple purposes: it ensures our results are robust against content effects
[
24
]
; it enables us to test whether the reasoning patterns are consistent in diverse contexts; and it mitigates potential data contamination, a significant problem for LLM evaluation
[
30
,

,

]
by reformulating problems in novel scenarios unlikely to appear in training data.

3.4
Data Collection

Model Selection and Testing Conditions

We evaluated 38 language models of varying sophistication, including several specifically designed as reasoning models. Selection informally prioritised quantity, popularity of usage, and representation of a diverse range of scores. All models were accessed via OpenRouter and tested using LLMharness. Chatbot Arena scores were obtained from
https://huggingface.co/spaces/lmarena-ai/chatbot-arena-leaderboard/resolve/main/elo_results_20250423.pkl
, released under the Apache 2.0 license. To ensure a fair comparison across architectural differences, all reasoning models were allocated 2,400 thinking tokens during evaluation. This standardization was justified given our use of the Chatbot Arena Elo score as our primary capability metric, which already accounts for performance differences regardless of underlying mechanisms
[
14
]
.

Response Processing and Filtering

Models provided conclusions in natural language, which we converted to PyETR format for evaluation. To ensure fair assessment of reasoning (not formatting), GPT-4.1-mini served as our translation layer without access to the original premises. Spot-checking confirmed translation fidelity.

Each model was evaluated on 383 reasoning tasks generated by PyETR. During analysis, we encountered occasional parse errors when processing model responses. To maintain data quality while preserving statistical power, models with parse error rates exceeding 20% across the test set were excluded from analysis, which resulted in the exclusion of one model (
google__gemini-2.5-pro-preview-03-25
). For the remaining models, individual responses that produced parse errors were excluded from analysis on a case-by-case basis rather than discarding all responses from that model.

Testing Framework

The full set of logical problems were framed as evals, using Eleuther’s Language Model Evaluation Harness
[
34
]
, which handles evaling and metric collection against a wide range of models. OpenRouter
[
35
]
was used for all models, in order to have a unified API. This consumed less than $1,000 of compute resources. Each model was given a token limit of 3,000 output tokens.

Key Measures

We collected the following measures to evaluate both the logical correctness of model responses and their alignment with human-like reasoning patterns, enabling our analysis of how these patterns correlate with model sophistication. A model’s answer was considered
‘logically correct’
if it was a logical consequence of the premises. This was tested using PySMT
[
36
]
to check whether the negation of the conclusion was inconsistent with the premises.
LLM responses were classified as
‘ETR-predicted’
if ETR predicts an endorsement of that conclusion according to PyETR’s implementation of
default_procedure_does_it_follow
.

Statistical Tests

We selected Pearson correlation to capture linear relationships between variables while also reporting Spearman’s rank correlation to account for potentially non-linear monotonic relationships without assuming normality in the distribution of fallacy rates. For our premise-order intervention analysis, we employed two-proportion z-tests to rigorously evaluate whether reversing premise order produced statistically significant changes in fallacy rates. The z-test was specifically chosen because it allows us to determine if the observed differences in proportions (original vs. reversed premise order) represent genuine effects rather than random variation, providing a reliable measure of the significance of order effects across different model capabilities. This statistical approach enables us to quantify the extent to which models exhibit the same premise-order sensitivity documented in human reasoning studies.

4
Experimental Results

Our analysis reveals several notable patterns in how language models exhibit human-like reasoning fallacies. We report the results of Pearson correlation tests, as well as Spearman tests for the detection of non-parametric monotonic relationships. We consider
p\leq 0.05
a suitable boundary for statistical significance.

Proportion of Errors that are Fallacies

We operationalize a “human-like fallacy” (or simply “fallacy”) as a response that is both ETR-predicted and logically incorrect. This definition captures instances where models make the same systematic reasoning errors that ETR predicts humans would make.
Formally, for a given model
m
and reasoning problem
p
, we define:

\text{HumanLikeFallacy}(m,p)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if ETR-predicted}(m,p)\land\neg\text{LogicallyCorrect}(m,p)\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Our metric of interest is the
fallacy rate
, defined as the proportion of human-like fallacies to total logically incorrect answers for each model:

\text{FallacyRate}(m)=\frac{\sum_{p\in P}\text{HumanLikeFallacy}(m,p)}{\sum_{p\in P}\neg\text{LogicallyCorrect}(m,p)}

where
P
is the set of all reasoning problems in our evaluation set. This measure represents the degree to which a model’s errors align with predictable human reasoning patterns rather than other forms of logical error.
We find as model strength increases, so does the proportion of logically incorrect answers that are fallacies
(Figure

Conclusion and Discussion

Interpretation of Key Findings
Our results reveal a statistically significant trend: as language models advance in capability (measured by Chatbot Arena Elo), their logical errors increasingly align with human errors predicted by ETR. Critically, while stronger language models demonstrate improved capabilities across most benchmarks, we observe a systematic increase in human-like fallibility: an inverse scaling trend that persists across model families, architectures, and training paradigms.

Moreover, we demonstrate order-effects for LLM reasoning analogous to those observed in humans. This finding suggests that beyond merely emulating modal human reasoning patterns, LLMs exhibit signs of mirroring human cognition at deeper levels, including characteristic fallacies.

Methodological Considerations and Limitations
While traditional ablation studies might seem appropriate for investigating this phenomenon, they face significant limitations in the current landscape of language models: the diversity of modern architectures, training approaches such as distillation
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
, and reasoning capabilities with test-time scaling
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
means that simple parameters like model size may no longer serve as reliable proxies for model capability. Our use of Chatbot Arena scores provides a performance-based metric that naturally incorporates a diverse range of models, reflecting real-world ability rather than architectural specifics, similar to
Ruan et al. 2024
.

Though the correlation strength (
\rho=0.360
) may appear moderate, it is robust across diverse model architectures and training paradigms, suggesting a fundamental relationship rather than artifact. The statistical significance (
p=0.0265
) exceeds conventional thresholds despite our conservative analytic approach.

While our results demonstrate correlation between model capability and human-like errors, we acknowledge limitations in attributing causality. Alternative explanations include the possibility that stronger models are increasingly trained on human-produced reasoning traces that themselves contain these fallacies. The absence of improved logical accuracy with model capability may reflect ceiling effects in our test set or fundamental limitations in current training paradigms.

Theoretical Implications
These findings challenge the assumption that scaling alone leads to more rational systems. Instead, we observe convergence toward human-like reasoning patterns including characteristic fallacies, suggesting that current language models may be developing internal processes that functionally mirror human cognitive shortcuts rather than idealized rational agents. If models increasingly adopt human-like reasoning patterns, including our characteristic errors, this suggests the emergence of cognitive structures functionally similar to human reasoning, rather than alternative solution methods that happen to produce similar outputs.

Connection to Inverse Scaling Laws and Practical Applications
Our findings represent an interesting case of an inverse scaling law
[
45
]
, where improvement on general metrics correlates with increasing susceptibility to specific reasoning fallacies. This phenomenon has both theoretical significance and practical applications for AI development.

Our results provide a foundation for several promising research directions. For benchmark development, our approach offers a systematic method for generating reasoning problems where human-like fallacies are predicted, creating evaluation sets that could complement existing reasoning benchmarks. For capabilities, the identified fallacy patterns could serve as targeted training examples to fortify models against human-like reasoning errors while maintaining their general capabilities. As a tool for assessing engineering choices, our methodology provides a quantitative framework for evaluating the effectiveness of various interventions aimed at improving model reasoning, from prompt engineering to architectural modifications.

Broader Implications
This inverse scaling phenomenon has profound implications for AI alignment. As models become more capable, anticipating and mitigating human-like reasoning errors becomes increasingly important, especially in high-stakes contexts requiring reliable reasoning, such as medical diagnosis, legal analysis, or decision support
[
8
,
9
]
.

We see these results as opening new avenues for understanding both artificial and human reasoning. As language models continue to advance, this work provides a foundation for developing systems that combine human-like understanding with more robust reasoning capabilities: systems that can successfully reason as we do while avoiding the pitfalls that characterize human cognition.
