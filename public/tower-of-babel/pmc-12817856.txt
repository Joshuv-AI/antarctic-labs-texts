The toll of the cyclone: mental health impacts of tropical cyclones in coastal communities of India
Authors: Pallavi Rachel George, Sandip Chakrabarti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith extreme weather events growing in severity and frequency, interventions aimed at preventing associated health disorders in coastal communities have become salient. Coastal communities are expected to develop coping mechanisms to climate change related stressors. However, these frontline communities may need special assistance to effectively adapt to unprecedented climatic disasters. Existing infrastructure and policy support systems may be inadequate, resulting in residual vulnerabilities that require attention. We examine whether tropical cyclones inflict adverse mental health impacts on coastal communities, and if the negative effect is disproportionately greater in coastal areas relative to non-coastal and coast-adjacent areas. We leverage the landfall and inland penetration of cyclones in India between April 2018-March 2023, and a novel dataset of select mental disorder treatment medicine sales across districts over the five-year period to analyze the cyclone-effect using district-year panel data. We use autoregressive distributed lag (ARDL) models with fixed effects to control for unobserved location-specific and time-specific factors affecting aggregate mental health status at the district level. Our findings suggest that cyclone occurrence negatively affects the mental health of communities in coastal districts, with 144% higher sales in that year; the one-year lagged effect is stronger at 173% higher sales than the contemporaneous effect, and the effect weakens over time. We do not find any statistically significant cyclone-effect in non-coastal districts. The cyclone-effect on coast-adjacent districts appears after a longer lag. Coastal communities already face relatively greater climate risks; our study highlights the additional burden of mental health challenges and calls for greater planning and policy attention to mental health to build resilient communities.Keywords: Cyclones, Mental health, Panel data, Climate resilience, Coastal communities

Background

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), in its Sixth Assessment Report (2022), discussed the impact of climate change on mental health for the first time in its history [1]. Climate change, as we know, has led to an increase in the severity and frequency of extreme weather events, among other episodic and long-term environmental changes, that negatively affect human health and well-being [2]. Preventing or reducing the adverse impacts of climate change on mental health is critical for enhancing community and individual resilience through effective public health policymaking, particularly within relatively more vulnerable communities.

According to various studies, settlements by the sea are “on the frontline of climate change,” facing the highest climate-compounded risks [3]. Tropical cyclones periodically cause widespread damage in tropical coastal areas due to high wind speeds, excessive rainfall, and storm surges [4]. Particularly, many deltaic and estuarine coasts are vulnerable because of high levels of poverty and inequality, along with additional risks of pluvial flooding [3]. While coastal areas have been historically challenged by various hydrometeorological hazards, rising sea levels make them more precarious and unpredictable [5].

A coastal focus in the analysis of cyclone impact on mental health is critical in the climate change and public health policy context, particularly from the perspective of resilience planning for vulnerable communities. People living in coastal regions are relatively more vulnerable due to their geographic location, population density, and dependence on coastal livelihoods such as fishing and tourism [3]. While past experiences of living with water are likely to make coastal communities more adept at coping with natural hazards such as flooding, cyclones, and storm surges, we do not know enough about the impact of such hazards on the health, particularly mental health, of coastal communities. Further, whether we should expect mental health effects of cyclones to be relatively greater in coastal areas is unclear. After making landfall, cyclones can cause widespread damage to private property, public infrastructure, and human lives along their path, thereby affecting many non-coastal communities that may have a relatively lower level of preparedness to cyclone impacts. If coastal areas are found to bear disproportionately greater cyclone-induced negative mental health burdens on average, then increased public health policy attention should be directed toward the coast.

On the one hand, we expect coastal districts to have more intense cyclone impacts (than non-coastal districts) on mental health because coastal districts are subject to stronger winds, rain, and storms as cyclones make landfall. We also anticipate a greater impact due to more precarious occupations (agriculture and fishing) and frequent damage to household assets due to the vagaries of the weather. On the other hand, it is unclear whether coastal communities, by virtue of their experience of coping with cyclones and other hydrometeorological hazards, show psychological resilience to cyclones. At the same time, non-coastal districts and coast-adjacent districts are also subject to impact because cyclones tend to travel large distances inland. While coastal districts are quickly evacuated as soon as cyclone warnings are issued, coast-adjacent and non-coastal districts may be less prepared to deal with cyclones, thereby causing unanticipated damage. While the relatively lesser intensity of cyclonic storms in these districts could lead to lesser damages and minimal mental health impacts, it is also possible that limited experience with such hazards and weaker coping mechanisms could intensify adverse mental health effects in these districts.

In this study, we use data from India to explore whether cyclones inflict adverse mental health impacts within coastal communities, and whether the adverse effect is disproportionately greater in coastal areas relative to non-coastal as well as coast-adjacent areas. We leverage the landfall of cyclones in India between April 2018 and March 2023 (i.e., five April-March financial years) that affected coastal as well as non-coastal districts and a novel dataset of mental health medicine sales (as a proxy for mental health impacts) to analyse the cyclone-effect using district-year panel data. We use autoregressive distributed lag (ARDL) models to study contemporaneous and lagged effects of cyclones on mental health medicine sales. Our results show that cyclone occurrence negatively affects the mental health of communities in coastal districts, and that the one-year lagged effect is stronger compared to the contemporaneous (same year) effect. We also find that the effect weakens beyond one year. We do not find any statistically significant cyclone effect in non-coastal districts. The negative cyclone-effect on coast-adjacent districts appears after a longer lag (specifically, after two years in the context of our analysis). Note that a coast-adjacent district is a district that does not have a coastline but shares part of the boundary with a coastal district. The contemporaneous and one-year lagged cyclone effect on coast-adjacent districts is counterintuitive and requires further investigation. We offer plausible explanations in the paper. In general, our study highlights the presence of a strong “coast effect” of cyclones on mental health.

Coastal communities already face relatively greater climate change-related risks to life and livelihood than other population groups; our study highlights the additional risk of mental health illnesses associated with the growing frequency and intensity of cyclonic storms. We argue that effective policy and planning actions to prevent climate-induced mental health illnesses within vulnerable coastal communities are imperative to building healthy communities in India and comparable geographic contexts globally.

Literature review

Mental health, climate change, and cyclones

Extreme weather events influenced by climate change have immediate direct impacts (such as physical injury, loss of life, air pollution, and damage to infrastructure, nature and agricultural land) and long-term direct impacts (such as conflict and violence, forced migration, community breakdown, worsened physical health, loss of livelihoods, disruption to health systems, and disrupted food and water supplies) that affect mental health and emotional wellbeing through acute stress, substance abuse, suicide, PTSD and trauma, insomnia, grief and loss, anxiety and depression, and worsened emotional wellbeing [6]. There are also additional impacts on mental health from witnessing changes to landscapes and loss of ecosystems, or through heightened awareness about the effects of extreme weather events, such as solastalgia, climate grief, and eco-anxiety [6]. Solastalgia is distress caused by one’s environment being negatively impacted [7], climate grief is grief about ecological losses [8], and eco-anxiety is the “chronic fear of environmental doom” [9, 10].

Tropical cyclones cause widespread damage in coastal areas due to the high wind speeds, precipitation, and storm surges induced by the cyclone. Impacts from cyclones are likely to intensify with climate change in coastal areas. IPCC reports state that the average peak wind speeds, average rain rates, and the frequency of severe cyclones (category 4 and 5) are likely to increase with global warming [4]. Tropical cyclones are projected to be 17 per cent more intense by the end of the 21st century [11]. The intensity of cyclones is influenced by climate change because warmer sea surface temperatures increase wind speeds, rising sea levels enhance the risk of coastal flooding, and atmospheric warming makes cyclones travel more slowly (subjecting coastal regions to more rainfall for more extended periods) [12, 13]. Even though cyclones most readily impact coasts, they also impact coast-adjacent and inland regions [3]. According to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) of the United States, 25 per cent of tropical cyclone-related deaths in the United States occur inland, and not at the coast [14]. Cyclone rainfall and associated flooding can impact regions hundreds of kilometres away from the coast, causing significant loss of life [15]. Tropical cyclones can impact the hydrological cycle of places thousands of kilometres away from landfall at the coast [16]. Among cyclones that affected India between 2009 and 2018, the mean inland track length is 392.4 kms in the Bay of Bengal and 266.9 kms in the Arabian Sea [17]. Therefore, the impacts of cyclones are not trivial in non-coastal areas, with inland communities being far less prepared than coastal communities against the hazard.

Research suggests that risks of mental health morbidity, PTSD, and all-cause mortality or hospitalisations are associated with cyclones [18]. A study found that older people, women, members of historically disadvantaged communities, such as African Americans, the less educated, and the poor, were more prone to long-term health effects induced by Hurricane Katrina [19]. A longitudinal survey of low-income mothers from New Orleans found that post-traumatic stress symptoms and symptoms of psychological distress decline over time, but do not return to pre-hurricane levels, even twelve years after Hurricane Katrina [20, 21]. Interviews with Hurricane Harvey survivors identified post-traumatic stress symptoms [22], as did surveys implemented after Hurricane Sandy [23]. Studies have found the mental health impact of cyclones to be most pronounced in the year post a cyclone occurrence, but symptoms can also show up within a month of the cyclone or even after three years [18].

The low-elevation coastal zones (LECZ) in many developing countries have a high incidence of poverty, with coastal hazards impacting livelihoods (agriculture and fishing) and natural protective ecosystems such as mangroves and marshes [24]. Studies have shown that poor and marginalised communities sort themselves into risk-prone (and therefore inexpensive) regions, such as the banks of rivers, near marshes and mangroves [25]. Hence, we are interested in investigating the cyclone-mental health connection in coastal areas, and also in exploring whether the connection (if it exists) is stronger in coastal relative to non-coastal or even coast-adjacent areas.

Mental health and cyclones in India

Despite growing evidence of intense cyclones becoming more frequent in the Indian Ocean [26], studies in India regarding the impact of cyclones on mental health are limited. According to the Global Climate Risk Index 2021, India was among the top 10 affected countries in terms of weather-related loss events (storms, floods, heat waves, etc.) in 2018 (rank 5) and 2019 (rank 7) [27]. According to the National Disaster Management Authority of India (NDMA), the Indian subcontinent faces five to six tropical cyclones every year, of which two or three could be severe [28]. The western coast, which typically has a lower number of cyclone landfalls than the eastern coast, has seen an increase in the frequency of cyclonic disturbances in the last few years due to the rise in sea surface temperature of the Arabian Sea. Studies show a 52 per cent increase in the frequency of cyclonic storms since the beginning of the 21st century in the Arabian Sea and an 8 per cent decrease in the Bay of Bengal, with the duration of very severe cyclones increasing almost threefold [29].

Though India has managed to reduce fatalities from cyclones with the help of improved early warning systems, the lives of coastal communities in cyclone-prone regions continue to be precarious, with frequent evacuations and repeated damage to household assets and infrastructure. Post Orissa’s Super Cyclone in 1999, survivors who were closer to the landfall experienced more anxiety, depression, and stress, a result of the larger magnitude of loss experienced by them (loss of family members, relatives, property), leading to heightened external locus of control [30]. A study on children affected by a super-cyclone in Odisha found that the majority of children had post-traumatic symptoms a year after the cyclone, with more children in highly exposed coastal regions showing symptoms [31]. Another study examined anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress symptoms a month after Cyclone Fani (2019) and found that 42.9 per cent of the sample had psychiatric morbidities significantly associated with housing damage and displacement [32]. The impacts of Cyclone Aila on households in the worst-affected blocks of West Bengal suggest a severe breakdown of household adaptive capacity, particularly due to health shocks [33].

Methodology

Approach

Studies in India on cyclones and mental health illnesses are predominantly based on interviews and questionnaires administered immediately after disasters, restricted to a specific cyclonic event and location. Unlike the extensive research on the long-term mental health effects of hurricanes in the United States, there are no studies in India that have examined the mental health effects of cyclones using multiple locations and time periods, leading to a lack of generalizable findings for policy action. Such studies are crucial for understanding the short-term, medium-term, and long-term mental health effects of cyclones and for developing community- and government-initiated plans to address or manage such effects. In general, studies from emerging and developing countries are limited due to the scarcity of data on mental health illnesses.

The ideal research design involves a longitudinal study of disaster survivors and affected populations in multiple locations, with data collected using clinical questionnaires. However, in the absence of such data, we have relied on the sale of medicines used to treat mental health illnesses as a proxy for capturing the prevalence of mental health illnesses in a given geographic region at a given time. While we acknowledge that mental health treatments, including the purchase of medicines, may not be availed by those most affected by climate extremes, we consider it to be a helpful indicator of the relative impact of cyclones on mental health, rather than an indicator of the actual magnitude of mental health outcomes. Our research approach – specifically the fixed-effects regression specification (i.e., within estimation) using panel data that tracks changes in medicine sales along with environmental parameters in each district over time – addresses this issue effectively. The sale of medicines has been used for public health surveillance by health departments since they provide information about larger geographies and populations, often capturing changes to population health status faster than diagnosis-based surveillance [34]. Studies find a strong correlation between drug sales data and clinical case reporting, indicating its suitability as a proxy for health status [34–36]. The sale of medicines has been used as a proxy for studying depression and anxiety during the COVID-19 pandemic in various European countries as well [37, 38].

Based on findings of previous studies from India, we focus on the three most commonly reported mental disorders attributed to cyclones - anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and depression. Anxiety is understood as “a state of apprehension or unease arising out of anticipation of danger” [39]. PTSD can be seen as “a delayed and/or protracted response to an exceptionally stressful or catastrophic life event or situation, which is likely to cause pervasive distress in almost any person (e.g. disasters, war, rape or torture, serious accident)” [39]. People with PTSD tend to re-experience the traumatic event in their thoughts or dreams persistently, and extreme weather events are traumatic events that have been found to cause PTSD in survivors [40]. Depression is “a mood disorder in which individuals experience extreme unhappiness, lack of energy, and several related symptoms” [40].

We analyse the variation in cyclone events and mental health status (i.e., the prevalence of mental health illnesses, proxied by the number of medicine tablets sold per million population) across 125 selected coastal and non-coastal districts of India over a period of five years (April 2018-March 2023; five April-March financial years). We use the ARDL modelling approach and an appropriate model specification to investigate whether a cyclone-mental health association exists in coastal areas. We are also interested in assessing whether the cyclone effect is more pronounced in coastal areas relative to non-coastal regions, as well as non-coastal but coast-adjacent areas that can also receive significant cyclone impacts due to their proximity to the coast or the cyclone’s path.

Data

Mental health medicines

Drug therapy is one way to address mental disorders. Other therapies include cognitive and behavioural therapies. The literature has indicated that natural hazards are associated with heightened anxiety, PTSD and depression, so we focus our attention on drugs used in their treatment. Commonly prescribed drug types for such disorders include tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs), Noradrenergic and Specific Serotonergic Antidepressants (NaSSAs), and Monoamine Oxidase Inhibitors (MAOIs), among others [39]. MAOIs are not prescribed frequently anymore because of the more severe side effects associated with them [40]. SSRIs are the most widely prescribed medication for such disorders, with NaSSAs prescribed for those who might not be able to take SSRIs. Tricyclic antidepressants are prescribed for more severe forms of depression. Therefore, we have restricted our study to a sample of three drug types – TCAs, SSRIs, and NaSSAs.

Studies show that escitalopram, sertraline, fluoxetine (drugs that belong to the SSRI group), and mirtazapine (a drug that belongs to the NaSSA group) are used in the treatment of anxiety and depression in adults [41]. Moreover, amitriptyline, dosulepin (dothiepin), and imipramine (drugs that belong to the TCA group) are also commonly prescribed for the treatment of anxiety, PTSD and depression in India [39]. Suitable drugs are prescribed based on various factors, such as the patient’s demographics, potential side effects, illness profile and comorbidities [42]. In India, studies have shown that almost 80 per cent of drugs prescribed for treating anxiety, PTSD, and depression comprised SSRIs, with escitalopram, sertraline, and fluoxetine being the most common drugs [43]. The Government of India has included amitriptyline, escitalopram, and fluoxetine as the drugs of choice for depressive disorders in the latest National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM, 2022), a list of medicines considered essential for the health of the population. According to the American Psychological Association, sertraline and fluoxetine are among the recommended set of drugs for the treatment of PTSD (refer to https://www.apa.org/ptsd-guideline/treatments/medications; accessed on 1st March 2024). According to Ahuja, SSRIs and mirtazapine (a NaSSA drug) have emerged as the treatment of choice for several anxiety disorders [39].

It is important to note that for anxiety, benzodiazepines are short-term drugs that bring quick relief during any panic attack or anxiety episodes (providing relief for a few hours), but they are not recommended for long-term treatment. Instead, SSRIs are prescribed for more enduring anxiety problems. Because our focus is on medium to long-term impacts, we do not include benzodiazepines in our study. For PTSD, the American Psychiatric Association has recommended SSRIs as first-line interventions, with TCAs such as amitriptyline and imipramine as second-line interventions [44].

Along with an extensive study of the medical literature on medications for anxiety, PTSD, and depression, we carried out consultations with medical practitioners in India. We discussed commonly prescribed medicines for these disorders with them and identified a representative sample. We then approached HLL Lifecare Limited, a public sector enterprise under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, Government of India, to request data for our research. We approached them with a list of medicines which are globally recognised as frontline medicines for these disorders, are commonly prescribed in India, and are also sold by HLL through their pharmacies. In our sample of medicines, we have included three TCAs (amitriptyline, imipramine, dosulepin (dothiepin), three SSRIs (escitalopram, fluoxetine, sertraline), and one NaSSA (mirtazapine). Following the findings from Grover et al. regarding prescription patterns in India, our selected drugs represent more than 90 per cent of the drugs commonly prescribed in India for anxiety, PTSD, and depression [43].

We were provided district-level quantities of sales (number of tablets) of the respective medicines from the AMRIT (Affordable Medicines and Reliable Implants for Treatment) Pharmacies that HLL operates across India. AMRIT pharmacies dispense medicines, surgical instruments, implants, and other medical items against valid prescriptions from medical practitioners. The advantage of getting data from HLL is the ability to control for administrative and logistical factors across the pharmacies, since all are expected to follow similar protocols. We converted the quantities into per million population for each district and created a variable (Quantity (tablets per million population)). Population data was collected from the DistrictMetrics database (projections based on the 2001–2011 Census growth rates) (https://districtmetrics.com/; data retrieved on 22nd October, 2023) and demographic data published by the National Informatics Centre for each district. For our study, 135 coastal and non-coastal districts of India had HLL pharmacies with sales data for the medicines of interest in at least one year over the five-year study period (April 2018 to March 2023; five April-March financial years).

Cyclone impact and frequency

Information about cyclone impact at the district-level was collected from the Regional Specialized Meteorological Centre for Tropical Cyclones over the North Indian Ocean (India Meteorological Department, Ministry of Earth Sciences, Government of India) from the reports prepared by the Cyclone Warning Division for each cyclone (see rsmcnewdelhi.imd.gov.in). Reports of cyclones (higher than a ‘deep depression’ category, i.e., more than 50–61 kmph of maximum sustained wind) were collected. Specifically, we have included disturbances categorised by the India Meteorological Department as Cyclonic Storm (62–88 kmph), Severe Cyclonic Storm (89–117 kmph), Very Severe Cyclonic Storm (118–221 kmph), and Super Cyclonic Storm (> 222 kmph). We also restricted the sample to those cyclones which made landfall in India. In total, we collected data on 18 cyclones that made landfall in India between April 2018 and March 2023.

For each cyclone that fulfilled our selection criteria (i.e., disturbances greater than a deep depression and ones that made landfall in India), we scanned the reports for information regarding the impact of the cyclone on the weather conditions of districts or locations within districts. The effect of a cyclone on weather conditions is provided in these reports as “realized adverse weather” comprising three categories: heavy rainfall, storm surges, and winds. For each category, the report lists the locations impacted, along with their districts and states. We identified the districts associated with adverse weather impacts for each cyclone. We created a binary variable (“Cyclone”) that takes a value of “1” if a particular district’s weather was impacted by any cyclone in a given year, and zero otherwise.

We capture data (0/1) for all districts (list of districts as of October 2023, see https://igod.gov.in/districts) in India, including districts that a cyclone has not impacted in any year over the study period and districts that cyclones have influenced in one or more years over the study period. This approach allows us to code all districts, not just those that experienced cyclone landfall (which would inevitably be coastal districts).

Study period, sample, and coastal, non-coastal and coast-adjacent district classification

Our study period is the five-year period between April 2018 and March 2023. A year is the April-March financial year. Financial rather than calendar year is used as the temporal unit (i.e., year or wave) of analysis, given the level of aggregation of the medicine sales data. Our study sample comprises districts for which HLL had data for our query (in any year over the study period), excluding districts in the Union Territories of India.

The locations of HLL pharmacies are neither influenced by the occurrence of cyclones nor the prevalence of mental health illnesses, as confirmed by HLL. HLL pharmacies sell various medications and medical equipment, with the sale of medicines for mental health representing only a small percentage. Therefore, the sample of districts used in our study is a random selection from India’s total districts in the context of our research.

We code districts as coastal (i.e., the district has a coastline) or non-coastal (i.e., the district does not have a coastline) based on information collected from the Centre for Coastal Zone Management and Coastal Shelter Belt (hosted by Institute for Ocean Management, Anna University Chennai, sponsored by the Ministry of Environment, Forest & Climate Change, Government of India) and district maps. Coast-adjacent districts are those districts that are non-coastal but share a boundary with a coastal district.

Excluding districts belonging to Union Territories (because they are administratively different from States), we have 125 districts from 22 states in our study – 26 coastal districts (across six states) and 99 non-coastal districts (across 20 states). Out of the 99 non-coastal districts, 19 are coast-adjacent districts (across five states).

Figure 1 shows the districts included in the study, classified by coastal, coast-adjacent, and other non-coastal districts. The actual number of districts included in regression models may be less than the study sample described in this section, driven by the availability of district-level data across all variables (i.e., dependent and independent variables) included in the models and years. Although the study districts may appear to be geographically clustered (since HLL may have a greater presence in some states or regions of India because of logistical or other reasons), our choice of the fixed-effects approach in regression analysis helps control for time-invariant location-specific factors that can potentially confound the cyclone-mental health relationship.

Models and variables

We use the autoregressive distributed lag (ARDL) model with fixed effects to analyse the cyclone-mental health connection using our district-year panel data (i.e., 125 districts and 5 years or waves). ARDL models are commonly used to analyse time series or panel data. For example, ARDL models have been used to study the relationship between consumption, emissions and economic growth [45, 46], to explore factors influencing health spending [47], to forecast tourism during COVID-19 [48], and to study the effects of meteorological parameters on COVID-19 cases [49], among others.

We turn to ARDL models because our dependent variable (the quantity of medicines sold per million people in a given district in a given year) is expected to be influenced by past values of itself as well as the contemporaneous and past values of the key determinant of interest, i.e., cyclone [50]. Since we use a fixed effects specification, our models analyse within-district variations in the variables of interest over time.

We specify an ARDL (p, q) model with district (d) as the geographic unit and year (t) as the temporal unit that includes p lags of the dependent variable (y; i.e., medicines sold per million population) and q lags of the key independent variable (x; i.e., cyclone occurrence) [50]. We use fixed effects to control for unobserved district-level factors affecting mental health status that do not change over time (i.e., entity-fixed effects) and unobserved year-specific factors affecting mental health status that are identical across all districts in a given year (i.e., time-fixed effects). We test two model specifications given the objective of our study, as follows:

First, we consider all coastal and non-coastal districts in our dataset. We interact the cyclone dummy variable (including lags) with the coast dummy variable to analyse whether a cyclone effect on mental health exists in coastal districts, and, simultaneously, analyse the coast vs. non-coast difference in cyclone effect.

Second, we consider only the coastal and coast-adjacent districts in our dataset. We interact the cyclone dummy variable (including lags) with the coast dummy variable to analyse whether a cyclone effect on mental health exists in coastal districts (using a reduced set of districts), and, simultaneously, analyse whether coast-adjacent districts experience a similar or different cyclone effect. This helps analyse whether the effects, if any, are localised to the coast.

To determine the appropriate lag length, we use two methods – statistical significance and information criteria. We find ARDL [2] to have significant coefficients, the lowest Akaike information criterion (AIC), theoretical salience, and sufficient data points for analysis [50]. Therefore, we select ARDL [2] as our final model. Since we have panel data, we use the Hausman Test to determine whether to use a fixed effects or random effects model, and the results support the use of fixed effects (p < 0.001).

In addition to the key dependent (medicine sales per million population; variable named “Quantity (tablets per million population)”) and independent (cyclone occurrence dummy variable; variable named “Cyclone”) variables, we also include select control variables, measured at the district-year level, in our models. The control variables are likely confounders in the cyclone-mental health relationship, and therefore their inclusion improves causal analysis. Variables – Description and data sources

First, we control for economic conditions. Previous literature has shown that lower household incomes are associated with higher risk for mental disorders [51, 52] and hence we control for income levels using the annual per-household purchasing power in each district. We collect year-wise household purchasing power per district data from the DistrictMetrics database (accessed on 17th June, 2024). The measure is calculated by dividing the total purchasing power of the district by the projected number of households in the district for the year. According to the documentation, purchasing power is estimated by DistrictMetrics based on the Reserve Bank of India’s district-level observed data. It is constructed by relying on the strong correlation between state per-capita GDP and state-level aggregates of the banking sector data adjusted for each district’s population. The state-level purchasing power is considered to be a function of the banking sector data, and hence, the state purchasing power is disaggregated to the district level based on the district-level observed values of the banking sector data. The total number of households in a district/town is projected based on the decadal growth rates in the Census between 2001 and 2011 for each year. The variable name used in our models is Annual Household Purchasing Power.

Studies have shown the association between agrarian distress and mental health [53, 54] and occupational impacts on mental health [55]. Therefore, to account for district and year-level variations in different economic sectors (such as any sector-wide shocks in districts), we control for the contribution of three main economic sectors - agriculture, manufacturing and processing, and professional services - to district incomes under three variables: Agriculture, Manufacturing and Processing, and Professional and Other Services. We also control for the overall business activity in a district, as represented in the banking sector and indicative of the district’s business potential, using the variable Composite. We selected the three main sectors to represent primary, secondary and tertiary economic activities, with the composite variable indicating the overall business activity in a district.

We use DistrictMetrics’ Index of Economic Activity (IEA) for the data on economic sectors and business activity in each district. IEA provides the intensity of a particular economic activity in a district, reflecting the contribution of that sector to the income of the district. According to DistrictMetrics, a district’s IEA indicates its share in the sector compared to the all-India total for that business, as well as the relative importance of that activity in the district’s purchasing power (DistrictMetrics documentation; accessed on 17th June 2024). These variables reflect the origin and source of income for households at the district level.

We also control for the frequency of cyclones in a district in a given year, since studies have shown that twice-flood affected individuals had worse mental health than once-flood affected individuals [56]. In our data, we have districts experiencing between one to three cyclones in a given year. We have named the variable Cyclone Frequency in our model.

Finally, we control for the number of AMRIT pharmacy outlets per district per year through the variable Outlets. This is an essential control in our fixed-effects framework because the number of outlets varied in some districts during the study time period (specifically, new outlets were opened), and the variation in medicine sales in a district over time is in part a function of the available outlets.

Discussion

Our findings suggest that the mental health of coastal communities is negatively affected by cyclone events, and that no significant effect exists within communities in non-coastal areas, on average. This indicates that coastal residents bear a relatively greater mental health burden associated with cyclone events, and that they are at relatively higher risk of climate change-induced environmental hazards than other communities because of a combination of location and sociodemographic factors that exacerbate their vulnerabilities. Consistent with previous literature [18, 31] we find that the loss, disruption and trauma associated with a cyclonic event affect mental health conditions of coastal communities immediately (i.e., in the same year that a cyclone event occurs), and gets aggravated or more pervasive in the subsequent year, before diminishing. The cyclone-induced mental health burden in coastal communities seems to decline over time. However, estimation of the time taken for the mental health status of coastal communities to get back to pre-cyclone levels is beyond the scope of the current study. Future research should analyze the recovery time and how targeted treatment and other interventions can help reduce the time.

The psychological impacts of extreme weather and climate events have not received sufficient policy and political attention despite their large implications for individuals and communities [6]. Our study shows that urgent preventive planning and policy actions are necessary to enhance the adaptive capacity of coastal communities in India and comparable geographic contexts globally.

The observed decline in post-cyclone mental disorder treatment medicine sales in coast-adjacent districts, although outside the core research objective of this study, is counterintuitive and therefore requires further investigation. We know that coastal and coast-adjacent districts are usually affected simultaneously by a given cyclone, as most cyclones (of the scale/severity considered in our study) penetrate deep into the landmass after landfall. We offer two possible reasons for the decline in sales in coast-adjacent districts. First, healthcare resources and supplies, including mental disorder medicines such as those that are used to treat PTSD, anxiety, and depression in the aftermath of a disaster, may be temporarily reallocated to coastal communities that are relatively more adversely affected by cyclonic impacts, under supply-side constraints, at least in the short term. If such reallocation happens, then the observed decline in sales in coast-adjacent districts is plausible. We also estimate that the sales of mental disorder treatment medicine two years after a cyclone event in a coast-adjacent district are greater compared to a no-cyclone scenario. Although future research should systematically explore reasons behind this observation, which is outside our core research objective, it is possible that eventual supply-side restoration, coupled with pent-up demand for mental health disorder treatment in coast-adjacent areas that are also affected by widespread damage caused by cyclones, explains the finding, at least in part.

Second, there could be psychological reasons why people in coast-adjacent districts may reduce seeking mental health help in the aftermath of cyclones. According to the downward social comparison theory, “persons experiencing negative affect can enhance their subjective well-being through comparison with a less fortunate other” [61]. Studies have examined the role of downward social comparison in improving wellbeing during the COVID-19 pandemic [62], among survivors of Hurricane Katrina [63], survivors of the Indian Ocean Tsunami [64], and earthquake survivors in Turkey [65], among others. In our study, it is possible that individuals living in coast-adjacent districts felt they were better off than those facing the cyclone’s full onslaught in the coastal districts, thereby achieving an improvement in mental health. However, this coping mechanism is temporary, with mental health worsening in coast-adjacent districts two years after a cyclone. A similar confounding effect was observed in another study where the use of anxiety medication first dropped after a cyclone, increasing after six months [59]. They offer two possibilities: people may have moved due to the disaster, or they may have required a different drug post-disaster. Therefore, while we offer these possible explanations, further research is required to understand such results that are being seen in ours and other similar studies.

Limitations

There are some limitations of our study that future research can address. First, as discussed in detail in our paper, while medicine sales data serves as a novel and acceptable proxy for mental health status in the absence of clinical data, it does not help accurately determine the number of persons with mental health illnesses in a given district at a given time. This is because many individuals with mental diseases seek alternative therapies or no treatment at all due to various reasons, including personal preference, sociocultural factors, lack of awareness or knowledge, stigma, etc. Therefore, mental health illness is underdiagnosed and often untreated in India. This limitation, however, likely affects the magnitude of the cyclone effect estimated by our models but not the direction or significance of the cyclone effect, if we can assume that an individual’s choice to seek medical treatment is uncorrelated with cyclone occurrence and coastal/non-coastal residence. We consider there to be no reason to believe that the likelihood of seeking medical care and subsequently purchasing medicines among those with a mental health illness would be ex-ante any different between coastal, non-coastal, and coast-adjacent districts. Our focus in this paper has been on identifying the relative levels of medicine sales across each of these three types of districts, over time, in response to changing environmental factors. It is possible that, in terms of actual magnitude, we are on the conservative side.

Second, we exclude prescriptions for short-term relief (benzodiazepines) from anxiety to capture medium to long-term mental health disorders, given our research objective. Future research can explore short-term episodes of anxiety associated with cyclone events.

Finally, we have utilised the district-aggregated sales of select medicines from AMRIT Pharmacies, which is only one chain of pharmacies with a presence in different parts of India. Previous studies have relied on medicine sales data from a single chain of pharmacies [34, 66], and hence, this approach is not new. However, our specific model design, namely a fixed effects within-estimator at the district-level, especially mitigates any potential bias emerging from using only one chain of pharmacies to the extent that the market share of AMRIT pharmacies does not change significantly at the district-level in the five year time-period (which is a plausible assumption, given the short time-frame of the study). Additionally, we control for the number of AMRIT outlets at the district-year level in our regression analysis. Medicine sales in India occur through various pharmacies, and we do not claim that we are able to estimate the total volume of mental health medicine sales at the district level. Our objective is not to estimate the actual magnitude of the medicine sales, but to estimate the change in sales from the specific pharmacy chain over time, in each study district, with changing environmental factors. Therefore, this limitation does not affect our central findings on the mental health burden of cyclones across coastal, viz., non-coastal and coast-adjacent regions.

Conclusion

The findings of our study using data from India underscore the need for concerted efforts to address cyclone-induced mental health illnesses in coastal regions, compared to non-coastal or coast-adjacent regions, even when they all face comparable cyclonic events and impacts. It implies a strong “coast effect” of cyclones on mental health, which might be driven by experiences of more intense destruction and loss of lives, precarity regarding livelihoods, and continuous worry about an impending cyclone. Enhancing the adaptive capacity of coastal communities involves addressing psychological resilience, going beyond the infrastructural solutions currently being planned and implemented to achieve climate-resilient development [3]. Poor mental health affects the overall wellbeing of individuals, households, and communities, and hence policy efforts must include plans for addressing the mental traumas of cyclones. Particularly, the delayed onset and persistence of mental health challenges after a cyclonic event indicate the need for continuous psychological support for longer durations than just the immediate post-disaster recovery phase.

Recently, India’s National Disaster Management Authority published updated guidelines on providing mental health support during disasters [67] which indicates growing policy-level attention to the mental health impacts of disasters. The guidelines include pre-disaster and post-disaster assessments at the community level, including extended assessments that are planned to be carried out once recovery, reconstruction, and rehabilitation efforts have started [67]. The guidelines also call for more research on the mental health impacts of disasters [67]. This is a step in the right direction, and the policy focus should open doorways for understanding the natural hazard-mental health connection better.

Creating ‘resilient communities’ involves enhancing the adaptive capacity of agents, such as vulnerable individuals and households [68–70]. Incorporating mental resilience as a critical component of climate resilience in coastal regions requires transforming existing institutions that take an emergency management approach to embrace a long-term, evolutionary resilience perspective [68]. It involves shifting attention from critical infrastructure systems and physical structures to individuals and households in our coastal communities, providing psychological support through counsellors, doctors, and enhanced mental health infrastructure. Even if rebuilding after cyclones is undertaken successfully, there are mental traumas that remain because of the experience of the cyclone, which are currently not sufficiently addressed.

We expect our research to draw planning and policy attention to addressing the mental health impacts of cyclones in coastal areas globally. Prevention of mental health illnesses among coastal communities should be a health policy priority in the face of climate change and the resultant inevitable rise in environmental hazards affecting coastal areas.
