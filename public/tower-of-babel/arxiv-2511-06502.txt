Poset-enriched categories and free exact completions
Vasileios Aravantinos-Sotiropoulos

Abstract
. We give an elementary construction of the exact completion of a weakly lex category for categories enriched in the cartesian closed category 𝖯𝗈𝗌 \mathsf{Pos} of partially ordered sets. Paralleling the ordinary case, we characterize categories which arise as such completions in terms of projective objects. We then apply the results to categories of Eilenberg-Moore algebras for monads on 𝖯𝗈𝗌 \mathsf{Pos} . In particular, we show that every variety of ordered algebras is the exact completion of a subcategory on certain free algebras, thereby answering a question of A. Kurz and J. Velebil.



1.
Introduction

The notion of regularity and the stronger Barr-exactness are by now ubiquitous in the literature on Category Theory and have found many applications in different areas such as Algebra and Logic. First considered in
[
6
]
, exactness can be thought of as the non-additive part of the axioms defining an abelian category. This manifests in Tierney’s theorem, which states that a category is abelian if and only if it is both exact and additive. However, there are at least a few perspectives from which these notions can be approached.

From one perspective, a finitely complete category is regular when it has quotients for kernel pairs and these behave well, in the sense that they are stable under pullback. From another, regularity is the existence of stable factorizations of morphisms into a regular epimorphism followed by a monomorphism, while a third states that regular categories are those which afford a useful calculus of internal relations. Exact categories are then those which are regular and in which, in addition, every equivalence relation is
effective
, i.e. a kernel pair of some morphism.

There are many interesting contexts which can be described as a regular or exact category satisfying some additional axioms. We mentioned abelian categories above, but exactness holds in elementary toposes as well, which save for this are of a quite different nature than the former. It is also present in the varieties of Universal Algebra (in fact, in all categories
monadic
over
\mathsf{Set}
), while regularity holds more generally in quasi-varieties. Actually, the distinction between the two algebraic notions corresponds precisely to the difference between the two categorical ones.

Given the significance of regular and exact categories, it is natural to ask whether a category lacking these properties can be “completed” to one which does not. The first such consideration is due to A. Carboni & R. Celia-Magno
[
8
]
, who gave a construction of the
free
exact category
\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}
on a left exact category
\mathcal{C}
. Here “free” has its usual meaning, i.e. that it provides a left adjoint to a certain forgetful functor. In particular, this means that there is an exact category
\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}
together with a lex embedding
\mathcal{C}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}
such that there is an equivalence

\mathsf{Lex}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{E})\simeq\mathsf{Reg}(\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}},\mathcal{E})

between lex functors
\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{E}
and regular functors
\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}\to\mathcal{E}
, for any exact category
\mathcal{E}
.

It was later realized by Carboni that the construction of the exact completion
\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}
can still be carried out even if
\mathcal{C}
is merely
weakly
lex, which is to say that it only possesses
weak
finite limits. Then Carboni & Vitale
[
9
]
produced constructions for both the regular and exact completion of a weakly lex category together with the associated universal properties. What is noteworthy here is that these regular and exact completions of a weakly lex
\mathcal{C}
are no longer part of left adjoints to the obvious forgetful functors. In particular, it is not weakly lex functors (=those preserving weak finite limits)
\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{E}
into exact categories
\mathcal{E}
that are involved in the universal property of
\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}
. Rather, it is those functors which E. Vitale discovered and termed
left covering
[
20
]
, a property which has since been identified as a form of flatness (see
[
12
,
19
]
).

On the other hand, in a more recent paper
[
14
]
, A. Kurz and J. Velebil considered for the first time notions of regularity and exactness specifically suited to the context of categories enriched over the cartesian monoidal category
\mathsf{Pos}
of partially ordered sets and monotone functions. The main result therein is a pair of characterization theorems for quasivarieties and varieties respectively of ordered algebras in the sense of S. Bloom & J. Wright
[
7
]
. One aspect of these results which is remarkable is how they precisely mirror known results concerning ordinary varieties of algebras. More precisely, varieties of ordered algebras can be characterized as exact
\mathsf{Pos}
-categories which have a suitably nice generator, while for quasivarieties the corresponding result simply replaces exactness with regularity. In other words, the characterization of ordered (quasi-)varieties reads identical to the one of ordinary (quasi-)varieties, as long as regularity and exactness are interpreted in the appropriate enriched sense.

The work of Kurz & Velebil has been the starting point for extensive research into the realm of
\mathsf{Pos}
-enriched categories in recent years, as for example in
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
10
]
. In particular, in
[
5
]
we used a suitable calculus of relations to construct the exact completion of a regular
\mathsf{Pos}
-enriched category. The latter gives an enriched analogue of the ordinary ex/reg completion, which was initially suggested by Lawvere
[
16
]
and first described in detail by R. Succi Cruciani
[
18
]
. However, (regular and) exact completions of weakly lex categories have not yet been considered in this context. In fact, one of the three open questions in
[
14
]
concerns the existence of such completions and their connection to (quasi-)varieties of ordered algebras.

The main purpose of this paper is to attempt to provide an answer to this question. By adapting the construction of Carboni-Vitale from the ordinary setting, we give a construction of the exact completion
\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}
of any weakly lex
\mathsf{Pos}
-category
\mathcal{C}
. Here the notion of “weakly lex” category can be seen as an instance of a general framework for weak notions described by Lack & Rosícky
[
15
]
. The universal property of
\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}
again involves left covering functors, where the latter is interpreted in the obvious enriched sense. In addition, we characterize free exact categories. What is once more noteworthy is how the results precisely mimic those for ordinary categories. In particular, every exact
\mathsf{Pos}
-category with enough projectives is an exact completion of any of its projective covers. Finally, we observe that in every variety of ordered algebras there is a projective cover given by those algebras which are free on a discrete poset. Thus, every variety is the exact completion of the full subcategory on such free algebras. In fact, the same holds more generally for categories
\mathsf{Pos}^{\mathbb{T}}
for a sufficiently well behaved monad
\mathbb{T}
.

Outline of the paper

In Section 2 we quickly record some preliminaries on
\mathsf{Pos}
-categories which will be required in the rest of the paper. For the most part, we do not go into much detail, so the reader should consult the indicated references if need be.

In Section 3, starting with a weakly lex
\mathsf{Pos}
-category
\mathcal{C}
, we describe a construction of the exact completion
\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}
and prove that it indeed produces an exact category. We also describe the canonical functor
\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}
and make a couple of observations on the manner in which
\mathcal{C}
sits inside
\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}
.

The universal property of the completion is established in Section 4, which also contains the characterization of free exact categories via projectives. The latter is applied in Section 5 to show that varieties of ordered algebras are exact completions and more generally to deduce a type of monadicity theorem over
\mathsf{Pos}
.

2.
Preliminaries on
\mathsf{Pos}
-enriched Categories

A
poset-enriched category

\mathcal{C}
is a category enriched in the cartesian monoidal category
\mathcal{V}=\mathsf{Pos}
of partially ordered sets and monotone functions. In more explicit terms this means that for any two objects
X,Y\in\mathcal{C}
the set
\mathcal{C}(X,Y)
of morphisms
X\to Y
is equipped with a partial order
\leq
and composition of morphisms is monotone in both variables. Similarly,
\mathsf{Pos}
-enriched functor
F\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
is a functor between
\mathsf{Pos}
-enriched categories which is
locally monotone
, i.e. such that for all objects
X,Y\in\mathcal{C}
the function
\mathcal{C}(X,Y)\to\mathcal{D}(FX,FY)
is order-preserving.

In this paper, the terms
category
and
functor
will always be used to indicate the
\mathsf{Pos}
-enriched notions just recalled. If we want to consider the non-enriched (or
\mathsf{Set}
-enriched) notions, we will explicitly use the prefix
ordinary
.

2.1.
Finite Weighted Limits

Throughout the paper we shall use the term “limit” to mean
weighted
limit. Hence, over our base
\mathcal{V}=\mathsf{Pos}
, the
limit
of the diagram
D\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C}
weighted by
W\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathsf{Pos}
is an object
\{W,D\}
with an isomorphism

\mathcal{C}(X,\{W,D\})\cong[\mathcal{D},\mathsf{Pos}]^{op}(\mathcal{C}(X,D-),W)

which is natural in
X
.

If the weight
W\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathsf{Pos}
is taken to be the constant functor
\Delta 1\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathsf{Pos}
on the one element poset, then we speak of a
conical
limit. An example of such would be the equalizer
e\colon E\to X
of a parallel pair
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
. Unraveling the definition, this means that
e
satisfies the familiar universal property from ordinary category theory, but that in addition it is order-monic (as opposed to simply monic) in the following sense.

Definition 2.1
.

A morphism
m\colon X\to Y
in a category
\mathcal{C}
is called an
\mathsf{ff}
-morphism
or an
order-monomorphism
if for every
Z\in\mathcal{C}
the monotone map
\mathcal{C}(Z,m)\colon\mathcal{C}(Z,X)\to\mathcal{C}(Z,Y)
in
\mathsf{Pos}
also reflects the order.

A similar remark applies to any type of conical limit. Namely, the universal property will be the same as in the ordinary case, but augmented by the requirement that the legs of the limit cone are jointly order-monomorphic.

In this paper, we shall particularly like to consider
finite
limits. Indeed, there is a standard notion of finite limit in enriched category theory which is due to Kelly
[
13
]
. Over the base category
\mathsf{Pos}
, a limit
\{W,D\}
is called finite if the weight
W\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C}
satisfies the following:

(1)

\mathcal{D}
has finitely many (isomorphism classes of) objects.

(2)

\mathcal{D}(d,d^{\prime})
is a finite poset for all
d,d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
.

(3)

Wd
is a finite poset for all
d\in\mathcal{D}
.

We now quickly recall some examples of (co)limits that will appear in the remainder of the paper. All except the last one are finite.

•

The
comma object
(also called
lax pullback
) of an ordered pair of morphisms
(f\colon X\to Z,g\colon Y\to Z)
is a square

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f/g}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\leq}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}

such that
fc_{0}\leq gc_{1}
and which is universal with this property, in the following sense:

(1)

Given
u_{0}:W\to X
and
u_{1}\colon W\to Y
in
\mathcal{C}
such that
fu_{0}\leq gu_{1}
, there exists a
u\colon W\to f/g\in\mathcal{C}
such that
c_{0}u=u_{0}
and
c_{1}u=u_{1}
.

(2)

If
h,h^{\prime}\colon A\to f/g
are such that
c_{0}h\leq c_{0}h^{\prime}
and
c_{1}h\leq c_{1}h^{\prime}
, then
h\leq h^{\prime}
.

•

In particular, the
kernel congruence
(or
lax kernel
) of a morphism
f\colon X\to Y
is the comma object
f/f
of

with itself.

•

The
inserter
of an ordered pair
(f,g)
of parallel morphisms

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
is a morphism
e\colon E\to X\in\mathcal{C}
which is universal such that
fe\leq ge
.

•

The
coinserter
of an ordered pair
(f,g)
of parallel morphisms
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
is a morphism
q\colon Y\to Q\in\mathcal{C}
which is universal such that
qf\leq qg
.

•

The
copower
(also called
tensor
) of an object
C\in\mathcal{C}
to a poset
P\in\mathsf{Pos}
is an object
P\bullet C
defined by the natural isomorphism

\mathcal{C}(P\bullet C,X)\cong\mathsf{Pos}(P,\mathcal{C}(C,X))

2.2.
Regular and Exact Categories

The notions of regularity and exactness pertinent to the
\mathsf{Pos}
-enriched context can be considered as simpler versions of corresponding 2-categorical notions that go back to the work of Street
[
17
]
. However, they were first considered on their own merits by Kurz & Velebil in their pioneering paper
[
14
]
. In this section we quickly recall these notions. The reader can also consult
[
5
,
4
]
for details.

The appropriate notion of monomorphism has already been recalled above. For the epimorphic part we take the class of morphisms which is left orthogonal to the order-monomorphisms, in the following sense.

Definition 2.2
.

We say that
e\colon A\to B
is an
\mathsf{so}
-morphism
if the square
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}(B,X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}(A,X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}(B,Y)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}(A,Y)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces-\circ e}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces m\circ-}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces m\circ-}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces-\circ e}

is a pullback in
\mathsf{Pos}
for every order-mono
m\colon X\to Y
.

We can then define regularity as follows.

Definition 2.3
.

A category
\mathcal{C}
is called
regular
if it has all finite limits, every morphism factors as an
\mathsf{so}
-morphism followed by an order-mono and
\mathsf{so}
-morphisms are stable under pullback.

One can then prove that in any regular category an
\mathsf{so}
-morphism is always an
effective
epimorphism, i.e. a coinserter of some pair. It then follows that it is necessarily the coinserter of its kernel congruence (see
[
5
]
). As a result, one can also phrase regularity in terms of stable (effective epi, order-mono) factorizations or via the existence and stability of coinserters of kernel congruences.

Examples of regular categories are all quasivarieties of ordered algebras in the sense of Bloom & Wright
[
7
,
14
]
, as well as categories of enriched presheaves
[\mathcal{C}^{\rm{op}},\mathsf{Pos}]
on any small category
\mathcal{C}
. Further examples include some categories of ordered topological spaces, like the category
\mathsf{Pries}
of
Priestley spaces
or the larger category
\mathsf{Nach}
of Nachbin’s
compact ordered spaces
are also regular in this sense.

To talk about exactness, we first need to recall the corresponding notion of congruence in the
\mathsf{Pos}
-enriched setting. These will be relations which are preorders and are in some sense compatible with the existing order furnished by the enrichment. By a
relation
on an object
X
we as usual mean a subobject
R\subseteq X\times X
, i.e. an equivalence class of an order-mono
R\rightarrowtail X\times X
.

Definition 2.4
.

A
congruence

E
on an object
X
in a regular category
\mathcal{C}
is a relation on
X
which is:

(1)

reflexive

(2)

transitive

(3)

an
order-ideal
, i.e. whenever
x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime}\colon A\to X
are such that
(x,y)\in_{A}E
,
x^{\prime}\leq x
and
y\leq y^{\prime}
, then also
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in_{A}E
.

We note here for future use that properties (1) and (3) can be combined into one. Namely, a relation
E
on
X
is a congruence if and only if it is transitive and
order-reflexive
(see
[
5
]
). The latter means that
E
contains not merely the diagonal
\Delta_{X}
but the full comma object
I_{X}\coloneqq 1_{X}/1_{X}
. In other words, it is to say that any pair of morphisms
x,x^{\prime}\colon A\to X
with
x\leq x^{\prime}
must factor through
E
.

Remark 2.5
.

In
[
5
]
, the term
weakening-closed
was used for relations satisfying property (3) above. We prefer here to switch to
order-ideal
, since the latter is more established in the literature.

A congruence is
effective
if it is the kernel congruence of some morphism. The definition of exactness can then be stated as follows.

Definition 2.6
.

[
14
]

A category
\mathcal{E}
is called
exact
if it is regular and every congruence in
\mathcal{E}
is effective.

Of the examples mentioned earlier, exact are all varieties of ordered algebras, all presheaf categories
[\mathcal{C}^{\rm{op}},\mathsf{Pos}]
and the category of Nachbin spaces
\mathsf{Nach}
.

2.3.
Projective Covers and Weak Weighted Limits

For ordinary categories, projective objects are intimately connected to both the regular and exact completion of a category with (weak) finite limits. The exact same connection is present in our enriched setting. Of course here projectivity should be defined in terms of the appropriate notion of epimorphism.

Definition 2.7
.

An object
P
in a regular category
\mathcal{C}
will be called
(\mathsf{so}-)
projective
if
\mathcal{C}(P,-)\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathsf{Pos}
preserves surjections.

Definition 2.8
.

A full subcategory
\mathcal{P}
of a regular category
\mathcal{C}
is called a
projective cover
of
\mathcal{C}
if:

(1)

Every
P\in\mathcal{P}
is projective in
\mathcal{C}
.

(2)

For every
C\in\mathcal{C}
there exist an object
P\in\mathcal{P}
and an
\mathsf{so}
-morphism
P\twoheadrightarrow C
.

A category which has a projective cover is said to have
enough projectives
. We will show later on that every variety of ordered algebras and more generally every category of algebras
\mathsf{Pos}^{\mathbb{T}}
for a sufficiently nice monad
\mathbb{T}
enjoy this property.

Remark 2.9
.

Projectives in
\mathsf{Pos}
-enriched categories are often closely related to discrete objects. In particular, in any category which has enough discrete objects, every projective object must itself be discrete, being a subobject of a discrete one. This is the case, for example, in the variety
\mathsf{OrdMon}
of ordered monoids and in
\mathsf{Nach}
.

Next, we need to make precise what our notion of weak weighted limit will be. In layman’s terms, this can be done almost immediately: a weak limit of some given type will satisfy only the “existence part” of the universal property which defines the corresponding strong limit.

So for example, a
weak inserter
of an ordered pair
(f,g)
of parallel morphisms
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
is a morphism
e\colon E\to X\in\mathcal{C}
such that:

•

fe\leq ge
.

•

Given any
h\colon Z\to X
such that
fh\leq gh
, there exists a
u\colon Z\to E
with
eu=h
.

So we’ve essentially dropped the requirement that
e
be an order-monomorphism. The same observation applies to any other type of limit. However, to alleviate the feeling that this is somewhat of an ad hoc choice and with an eye towards other bases of enrichment over which one might want to consider similar constructions of exact completions, let us say a few more words.

There is a very general framework for weak notions in enriched category theory, so in particular weak limits, which is due to Lack & Rosícky
[
15
]
. The data one needs consist of a monoidal category
\mathcal{V}
and a class of morphisms
\mathcal{E}
therein, which are required to satisfy some conditions. Then given a
\mathcal{V}
-functor
D\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C}
and a weight
W\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{V}
, a
weak
W
-weighted limit
consists of:

•

An object
\{W,D\}_{w}

•

A natural transformation
W\to\mathcal{C}(\{W,D\}_{w},D-)

such that for all
X\in\mathcal{C}
the induced morphism
\mathcal{C}(X,\{W,D\}_{w})\to[\mathcal{D},\mathcal{V}](W,\mathcal{C}(X,D-))
lies in the class
\mathcal{E}
.

Applying this general definition to the case where
\mathcal{V}=\mathsf{Pos}
and
\mathcal{E}
is the class of surjections, we have the following definition.

Definition 2.10
.

Given a functor
D\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C}
and a weight
W\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathsf{Pos}
, a
weak
W
-weighted limit
consists of:

•

An object
\{W,D\}_{w}

•

A natural transformation
W\to\mathcal{C}(\{W,D\}_{w},D-)

such that for all
X\in\mathcal{C}
the induced morphism
\mathcal{C}(X,\{W,D\}_{w})\to[\mathcal{D},\mathsf{Pos}](W,\mathcal{C}(X,D-))
is a surjection.

It is then easy to see that, for example, in the case of the weight defining an inserter,
\{W,D\}_{w}
will have precisely the property we mentioned above. In this paper we will be interested in categories which have weak finite limits, so we accordingly introduce the following.

Definition 2.11
.

A category
\mathcal{C}
is said to be
weakly lex
if the weak limit
\{W,D\}_{w}
exists for every finite weight
W\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathsf{Pos}
.

In order to have a better handle on the existence of weak finite limits, it will be useful to know how to construct all of them from specific types of such. In particular, we will need the following result.

Proposition 2.12
.

If a category
\mathcal{C}
has weak finite products and weak inserters, then it is weakly lex.

It is also not hard to check that, much like in the ordinary case, limits in a category imply the existence of the corresponding weak limits in any projective cover.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category and
\mathcal{P}
a projective cover of
\mathcal{C}
. If
W\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathsf{Pos}
is any weight for which
\mathcal{C}
has
W
-weighted limits, then
\mathcal{P}
has weak
W
-weighted limits. In particular, if
\mathcal{C}
is lex, then
\mathcal{P}
is weakly lex.

3.
The Exact Completion

In this section we give the construction of the category
\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}
and the verification that it is indeed exact. To make the proof easier to digest, we have split it up into a sequence of separate steps verifying each successive layer of properties.

Our intent is to closely follow the construction of the ordinary exact completion of a weakly lex category, as presented in
[
9
]
and especially its detailed exposition in
[
20
]
. To begin with, the objects of
\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}
are weak versions of equivalence relations, called
pseudo-
equivalence relations, which are the appropriate notion of congruence in the ordinary setting. By “weak” here we mean the absence of the jointly monic property of the span representing a relation. Thus, it seems natural that the corresponding objects to consider here should be the following.

Definition 3.1
.

A
pseudocongruence
in
\mathcal{C}
is a parallel pair of morphisms

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r_{1}}

which is:

(1)

order-reflexive
: every pair of morphisms
a_{0},a_{1}\colon A\to X
with
a_{0}\leq a_{1}
factors through
(r_{0},r_{1})
.

(2)

transitive
: For a weak pullback square
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R*R}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r_{0}}

there exists a
\tau\colon R*R\to R\in\mathcal{C}
such that
r_{0}\tau=r_{0}d_{0}
and
r_{1}\tau=r_{1}d_{1}
.

Remark 3.2
.

(1)

Observe that transitivity does not depend on a choice of weak pullback, since any two such choices will factor through each other.

(2)

Order-reflexivity of

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r_{0}}

factors through

We now define a category
\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}
as follows:

•

Objects
: The pseudocongruences of
\mathcal{C}
. The object
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r_{1}}
will be denoted by a pair

. This is to be thought of as the would-be quotient of

by

.

•

Morphisms
: Given objects
(X,R)
and
(Y,S)
, to define morphisms

we first consider morphisms

for which there exists an

such that

and

.
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bar{f}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s_{1}}

Given our intuition on the objects, this is to say that we consider those morphisms which would be (well-defined and) order-preserving on the corresponding quotients.
We then define a relation

on the collection of such morphisms as follows:

f\preccurlyeq g\iff(\exists\Sigma\colon X\to S\in\mathcal{C})\quad s_{0}\Sigma=f,s_{1}\Sigma=g

This is easily seen to be a preorder relation, reflexivity and transitivity following from the two corresponding internal properties of
S
. We denote by
\sim
the induced equivalence relation and then define a morphism
[f]\colon(X,R)\to(Y,S)\in\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}
to be an equivalence class with respect to
\sim
.

It is easy to check that
\preccurlyeq
is compatible with composition of morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
so that
\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}
canonically becomes a
\mathsf{Pos}
-category.

We now begin the painstaking process of verifying that
\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}
is exact. In all proofs that follow, whenever we take a (weak) limit of some finite diagram in
\mathcal{C}
, we will denote the corresponding cone by broken arrows, while for the diagram itself we will use solid ones. Hopefully this alleviates some of the notational complexity of the situation.

4.
The Universal Property and Characterization of the Completion

In the case of the exact completion
\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}
of an ordinary category
\mathcal{C}
, a fact which at first appeared curious is that its universal property does not involve the type of functors that one might expect.

To state a prospective universal property, one has in any case to consider functors
\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{E}
from the merely weakly lex category
\mathcal{C}
into exact categories
\mathcal{E}
. It is natural to initially expect that the appropriate functors should be the weakly lex ones, i.e. the ones preserving weak finite limits, and that any such functor will uniquely extend to an exact functor
\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}\to\mathcal{E}
. In other words, this is to say that the construction of the exact completion should be the left biadjoint to the obvious forgetful 2-functor
\mathsf{EX}\to\mathsf{WLEX}
. However, as observed in
[
9
]
, this is simply not the case. Furthermore, the authors show that this issue cannot be avoided by choosing a different class of functors. It is simply impossible to turn the construction
\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}
into a left biadjoint.

Instead, the universal property of
\mathcal{C}_{\rm{ex}}
is of a different flavor. The identification of the functors involved is a fundamental contribution of Enrico Vitale. He has termed them
left covering
and they form a central part (and provide the title) of his 1994 PhD thesis
[
20
]
. It is this notion which we now simply adapt to the
\mathsf{Pos}
-enriched context.

Definition 4.1
.

Consider a functor
F\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{E}
where
\mathcal{C}
is weakly lex and
\mathcal{E}
is regular. We say that
F
is
left covering
if, given any finite weight
W\colon\mathcal{I}\to\mathsf{Pos}
and diagram
D\colon\mathcal{I}\to\mathcal{C}
, for every weak limit
\{W,D\}_{w}
in
\mathcal{C}
the canonical factorization
F\{W,D\}_{w}\to\{W,FD\}
is an effective epimorphism in
\mathcal{E}
.

We will not go into an extensive analysis of the properties that such functors enjoy, but rather restrict ourselves to those that are strictly necessary for proving the universal property of the exact completion. Perhaps the most fundamental is the one contained in the following, which should be compared to Theorem 26 of

5.
Exact Completion and Categories of Ordered Algebras

In this section we connect the exact completion with varieties of ordered algebras and thus answer an open question of Kurz & Velebil from
[
14
]
. More generally, we will apply the exact completion to obtain a characterization of categories which are monadic (in the enriched sense) over
\mathsf{Pos}
, following Vitale’s approach in
[
21
]
for the ordinary setting. However, it is clear that in our present context we must restrict to sufficiently “nice” monads in order to reconcile monadicity with exactness. Indeed, as observed for example in
[
14
]
, a category of the form
\mathsf{Pos}^{\mathbb{T}}
may be badly behaved. In particular, such a category need not even be regular. The reason that such issues do not occur in the ordinary setting is essentially because of the Axiom of Choice in
\mathsf{Set}
.

The monads we will need to restrict to are those which preserve quotients of congruences. From one perspective, this is on a practical level the minimum requirement that one needs to impose so that the proof of exactness of
\mathsf{Pos}^{\mathbb{T}}
works. From another point of view, it is known
[
14
,
1
]
that ordered varieties correspond precisely to those monads which are
strongly finitary
. And in turn, the latter property is equivalent to being finitary and preserving quotients of congruences. So it seems reasonable that dropping the finitary part should correspond to general (exact) categories
\mathsf{Pos}^{\mathbb{T}}
.

We begin with an easy observation on projectives in a category of algebras
\mathsf{Pos}^{\mathbb{T}}
, at least for sufficiently nice monads
\mathbb{T}
.

.

. Applying the free algebra functor
F\colon\mathsf{Pos}\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\mathbb{T}}
, which is a left adjoint, we obtain an
\mathsf{so}
-morphism
Fp\colon F|A|\to FA
. Then the composition
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F|A|}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces FA}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(A,\alpha)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Fp}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
is an
\mathsf{so}
-morphism as well, while
F|A|=(T|A|,\mu_{|A|})
is projective by part (1).
∎

If
\mathsf{Kl}(\mathbb{T})_{d}
denotes the full subcategory of the Kleisli category on the algebras which are free on a discrete poset, then the above lemma shows that
\mathsf{Kl}(\mathbb{T})_{d}
is a projective cover of
\mathsf{Pos}^{\mathbb{T}}
. When moreover
\mathbb{T}
preserves coinserters of congruences,
\mathsf{Pos}^{\mathbb{T}}
is exact and so we immediately have the following.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
\mathbb{T}
be a monad on
\mathsf{Pos}
which preserves coinserters of congruences. Then
\mathsf{Pos}^{\mathbb{T}}\simeq(\mathsf{Kl}(\mathbb{T})_{d})_{\rm{ex}}

In particular, since the monads corresponding to varieties of ordered algebras satisfy the condition above, we also obtain the following.

Corollary 5.3
.

If
\mathcal{V}
is a variety of ordered algebras, then
\mathcal{V}\simeq\mathcal{P}_{\rm{ex}}
, where
\mathcal{P}
is the full subcategory of
\mathcal{V}
on the algebras which are free on a discrete poset.

Before the main result of this section, we will need the following easy characterization of Kleisli categories for monads on
\mathsf{Pos}
. This again precisely mimics the ordinary case of monads over
\mathsf{Set}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

For a category
\mathcal{C}
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{C}\simeq\mathsf{Kl}(\mathbb{T})
for a monad
\mathbb{T}
on
\mathsf{Pos}
.

(2)

There exists an object
G\in\mathcal{C}
such that:

•

P\bullet G
exists for all
P\in\mathsf{Pos}
.

•

(\forall C\in\mathcal{C})(\exists P\in\mathsf{Pos})\hskip 14.22636ptC\cong P\bullet G

Proof.

If
\mathcal{C}\simeq\mathsf{Kl}(\mathbb{T})
, then we can simply take
G=F1
.

For the converse, the existence of all
P\bullet G
implies that there is a functor
-\bullet G\colon\mathsf{Pos}\to\mathcal{C}
, which is then by definition left adjoint to
\mathcal{C}(G,-)
and hence there is an associated monad
\mathbb{T}
. The comparison functor
\mathsf{Kl}(\mathbb{T})\to\mathcal{C}
is always fully order-faithful and then the second condition says that it is also essentially surjective on objects, hence an equivalence of
\mathsf{Pos}
-categories.
∎

We are now ready for the main application.

Theorem 5.5
.

For a category
\mathcal{E}
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{E}\simeq\mathsf{Pos}^{\mathbb{T}}
for a monad
\mathbb{T}
on
\mathsf{Pos}
which preserves coinserters of congruences.

(2)

\mathcal{E}
is exact and there exists an object
G\in\mathcal{E}
such that:

•

P\bullet G
exists for all
P\in\mathsf{Pos}
.

•

G
is an
\mathsf{so}
-projective.

•

For every
X\in\mathcal{E}
, there exists an
\mathsf{so}
-morphism
P\bullet G\twoheadrightarrow X
for some
P\in\mathsf{Pos}
.

Proof.

The implication (1)
\implies
(2) is more or less contained in
[
14
]
, so we only prove the converse.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the full subcategory of
\mathcal{E}
spanned by all objects of the form
P\bullet G
, for any
P\in\mathsf{Pos}
. Then we have
\mathcal{C}\simeq\mathsf{Kl}(\mathbb{T})
for a monad
\mathbb{T}
on
\mathsf{Pos}
, namely the one induced by the adjunction
-\bullet G\dashv\mathcal{E}(G,-)
. We will show that this monad preserves coinserters of congruences, for which it suffices of course to show that
\mathcal{E}(G,-)
preserves them.

So consider a coinserter
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}
in
\mathcal{E}
, where
(r_{0},r_{1})
is a congruence. Since
\mathcal{E}
is exact,
(r_{0},r_{1})
must in fact be the kernel congruence of its coinserter
q
. Since
\mathcal{E}(G,-)
preserves limits, in the diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}(G,R)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}(G,X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}(G,Y)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r_{0}\circ-}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r_{1}\circ-}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q\circ-}
in
\mathsf{Pos}
,
(r_{0}\circ-,r_{1}\circ-)
is the kernel congruence of
q\circ-
. But
G
is projective, so
\mathcal{E}(G,-)
also preserves
\mathsf{so}
-morphisms, whence
q\circ-
is also an
\mathsf{so}
-morphism in
\mathsf{Pos}
. Thus,
q\circ-
must be the coinserter of its kernel congruence.

Now let
\mathcal{C}_{d}
be the full subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
on the copowers
I\bullet G
with
I
a discrete poset, i.e. on the coproducts
\bigsqcup_{I}G
. The equivalence
\mathcal{C}\simeq\mathsf{Kl}(\mathbb{T})
clearly restricts to an equivalence
\mathcal{C}_{d}\simeq\mathsf{Kl}(\mathbb{T})_{d}
. Then
G
being projective implies that every object of
\mathcal{C}_{d}
is as well. Now
\mathcal{C}_{d}
is a projective cover of
\mathcal{E}
: indeed, for any
P\in\mathsf{Pos}
there is a canonical
\mathsf{so}
-morphism
|P|\twoheadrightarrow P
with
|P|
discrete. Thus, for any object
X\in\mathcal{E}
we can find a composite
\mathsf{so}
-morphism
|P|\bullet G\twoheadrightarrow P\bullet G\twoheadrightarrow X
(since
-\bullet G
is left adjoint). In addition,
\mathsf{Kl}(\mathbb{T})_{d}
is a projective cover of
\mathsf{Pos}^{\mathbb{T}}
by
5.1
. Thus, by
4.7
we conclude that
\mathcal{C}_{d}\simeq\mathsf{Kl}(\mathbb{T})_{d}\implies(\mathcal{C}_{d})_{ex}\simeq(\mathsf{Kl}(\mathbb{T})_{d})_{ex}\implies\mathcal{E}\simeq\mathsf{Pos}^{\mathbb{T}}
.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

From
5.5
we can, for example, recover the fact that the category
\mathsf{Nach}
of Nachbin’s compact ordered spaces is monadic over
\mathsf{Pos}
, which is originally due to Flagg
[
11
]
. Indeed, the one element space
G=\mathfrak{1}
is easily seen to satisfy the conditions of the theorem, while we know that
\mathsf{Nach}
is exact from
[
5
, Proposition 5.8]
.

Remark 5.7
.

As is clear from the proof of
5.5
, in the third condition of (2) we can in fact assume that every
X\in\mathcal{E}
is covered by a copower
P\bullet G
with
P
discrete, i.e. by a coproduct of copies of
G
. Such an object is then furthermore projective, while an arbitrary
P\bullet G
of course need not be.
