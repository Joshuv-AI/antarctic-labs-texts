Clinicians don’t know what explanations they need: A case study on eliciting AI software explainability requirements
Tor Sporsem, Stine Rasdal Finserås, Inga Strümke

Abstract
This paper analyses how software developers elicit explainability requirements when creating a software application with an AI component, through a case study using AI in the medical context of predicting cerebral palsy (CP) risk in infants. Following a small software development team at a Norwegian hospital, we observe their process of simultaneously developing the AI application and discovering what explanations clinicians require from the AI predictions. Since clinicians struggled to articulate their explainability needs before interacting with the system, an iterative approach proved effective: the team started with minimal explanations and refined these based on clinicians’ responses during real patient examinations. Our preliminary findings from the first two iterations show that clinicians valued “interrogative explanations” - i.e., tools that let them explore and compare the AI predictions with their own assessments - over detailed technical explanations of the AI model’s inner workings. Based on our analysis, we suggest that successful explainability requirements emerge through iterative collaboration between developers and users rather than being fully specified upfront. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first empirical case study on eliciting explainability requirements in software engineering.

Introduction

Hanne, a physiotherapist who specializes in examining infants for cerebral palsy (CP), has just finished examining a three month old infant. I observe from beside her, wearing medical scrubs. She notes in the patient journal that while the overall assessment is optimistic, she and two colleagues have identified some slightly concerning signals.

“I will call the child back in three months for another examination as a precaution.”

I turn my laptop toward her to display a CP risk prediction generated by a machine learning (ML) model, developed in a collaboration between her hospital, a startup, and a university. After analyzing a three-minute video of the infant, the model shows “Total CP risk score 1%.”

“Well, it is good to see that we agree with the model, I guess.”

She leans back in her chair with a troubled expression on her face.

“However, the machine seems very certain, while we are slightly worried. The machine isn’t worried at all. This would be an easier pill to swallow if the machine agreed with us.”

She looks out the window, deep in thoughts, before she turns to me and says:

“It would be nice to have some kind of explanation about why the model is so certain. Something to compare with my own assessment.”

In this short-paper, we outline the process through which a small team of developers identify the explanations needed for clinicians to trust in ML application predicting the risk of CP. Specifically, we focus on how they elicited the necessary explanations to ensure clinicians at a Norwegian hospital could trust the model.

The infants included in this study are part of a screening program due to complications during pregnancy or childbirth. This screening process can continue until the age of six, during which the infants are assessed for various diagnoses, including CP. Notably, nine out of ten infants in the screening program do not have CP, making it crucial to spare parents from the nerve-wracking process of screening their child for CP as early as possible. Furthermore, early identification of CP is beneficial for the child’s further development due to the plasticity of their young brains, which allows for more effective interventions and support
[
14
]
.

At present, the screening process for CP is performed by specifically trained clinicians. However, even highly skilled clinicians are typically unable to confidently determine a CP diagnose before the infant reaches seven months of age. The absence of a biomarker for CP makes early diagnosis particularly challenging. Clinicians undergo specialized training to recognize CP and accumulate many years of experience to develop this expertise. The ability to predict CP is largely based on intuition and tacit knowledge, as described by one clinician: “It is a holistic assessment; you get this gut feeling, this suspicion, from observing the child.” This rare skill is primarily found among clinicians working in Norway’s largest hospitals, which often necessitates parents traveling considerable distances to have their child examined. The scarcity of this expertise highlight the need for more accessible methods for early CP detection.

Over the course of 17 years, a clinician has collected 557 videos of infants participating in the screening program as part of CP research. The recent advancements in ML technology, particularly deep learning, have enabled the collaboration between the hospital and university to develop an image recognition model using this unique dataset
[
7
]
. The model, completed in 2022, predicts CP risk from a three-minute video of the infant, typically recorded by parents using their smartphones. The model’s output is a CP risk prediction ranging from 0 to 1 (0-100%). To develop and validate the model, the dataset was divided into a training set consisting of videos from 418 infants (75% of the total) and an external validation sample (test set) comprising videos from 139 infants (25%). For a comprehensive description of the model development process and performance scores, refer to
[
7
]
.

Given the CP prediction model, a small team of developers set out to create an application making the model accessible to clinicians. Early in the process, the developers expected that clinicians would likely require explanations of the model’s predictions. This expectation is based on several facts, primarily that clinicians are accountable for any erroneous diagnoses. The large variety of explanation methods available for ML models, and the lacking consensus regarding what constitutes a valid and complete explanation, led to considerable uncertainty in how to proceed. Furthermore, a constrained budget necessitated careful decision-making regarding which explanation methods to develop and implement.

Considering the challenge described above, we pose the following research question:
How do developers elicit explainability requirements?
To address this question, we present preliminary findings from a case study that chronicles the journey of a small development team as they navigated the process of discovering and prioritizing the need for explanations from users while adhering to a limited resource budget. By examining their experiences, we aim to provide insights into the strategies and methods employed by developers when faced with the task of eliciting explainability requirements in resource-constrained settings. Through this narrative, we seek to contribute to the Software Engineering literature on developing AI systems and offer practical guidance for developers and researchers working in similar contexts.

2
Background

Recent advancements in ML have accelerated the integration of AI capabilities into software, significantly transforming software engineering processes
[
12
]
. Developing AI software differs from traditional software development in three ways
[
2
]
. First, it is more challenging and complex to discover and manage the data required for ML applications. Second, it demands skills that are often not present in typical software teams. Third, AI components are harder to handle as modular units.

Another significant change is that users now require explanations of how AI applications work, which compels developers to elicit requirements for these explanations to effectively meet user needs. Studies on explainability requirements first surfaced in the Software Engineering literature in 2019
[
1
]
with Köhl et al.’s
[
9
]
attempt to define explainability requirements as: “A system S must be explainable for target group G in context C with respect to aspect Y of explanandum X.” This definition emerged from their observation that there was no systematic and overarching approach to ensure explainability by design, and that it was often not even clear what precisely was meant when demanding explainability from a system. Chazette et al.
[
5
]
criticized the definition of Köhl et al., arguing that it leaves open what exactly is to be explained and that we need a better understanding of what explainability means from a software engineering perspective. Based on a systematic literature review, they propose the following definition: “A system S is explainable with respect to an aspect X of S relative to an addressee A in context C if and only if there is an entity E (the explainer) who, by giving a corpus of information I (the explanation of X), enables A to understand X of S in C.”

In order to make these definitions easier to use, Balasubramaniam et al. created a model where they are broken down into four components that can be summarized as the following questions
[
3
]
:

•

To whom to explain? I.e. customers, users, employees, developers, partners, etc.

•

What to explain? I.e., purpose of the AI system, inputs to the AI system, outputs, data used for training the system, behavior of the system, etc.

•

In what kind of situation? I.e. when using the AI system, when building it, when testing it, etc.

•

Who explains? I.e. the AI system explains itself, humans explain, an explainability tool explains, etc.

Explainability requirements should be treated as non-functional requirements (NFRs) since they constrain how a system should be implemented rather than specifying a function
[
6
,
9
,
3
,
8
]
. Furthermore, there is a consensus that explainability can be a double-edged sword, as it can have both positive and negative effects depending heavily on design choices made during requirements analysis. This double-edged sword effect was particularly evident in Chazette and Schneider’s survey
[
6
]
, where participants expressed both strong advantages and disadvantages to receiving explanations. For example, positive impacts of explanations included better understanding, increased trust, and improved transparency; however, the explanations could also easily contribute to negative outcomes such as reduced usability if poorly designed, information overload, and more complex user interfaces. This highlights the importance of carefully considering how explanations are implemented to avoid unintended negative consequences while achieving the desired benefits. Chazette and Schneider also identified challenges that developers experience in eliciting explainability requirements, such as different users needing different levels and types of explanations, users wanting control over when they receive explanations, and the need to strike a balance between providing enough information and overwhelming users. Balasubramaniam et al.
[
3
]
further expand the list of challenges, noting that multidisciplinary teams are needed to capture different viewpoints, understanding the user and organizational processes is essential, and the purpose of the AI system must be clearly defined before eliciting explainability requirements. However, none of these challenges have been empirically validated through case studies, indicating a need for further research in real-world settings.

Despite the increasing interest and number of studies on this topic within the RE and SE literature, there are no empirical case studies on eliciting explainability requirements
[
1
]
. This is echoed in Balasubramaniam et al.’s
[
4
]
recent literature review in RE where they found 30 practices that support the development of explainability requirements: “most proposed solutions have not been evaluated in real projects, highlighting the need for empirical studies.” We have not been able to identify any empirical case-studies as of January 2025. Therefore, this study answer the call from our fellow researchers in providing case studies that describe these phenomenons in an industrial case.

3
Method

Case study design & case description

The objective of this study was to enhance our understanding of the process of eliciting explainability requirements when developing software with ML components in an industrial setting. The research literature on software engineering and requirements engineering benefits from case studies that provide narratives on how these phenomena unfold in real-world industrial cases. Such case studies form a foundation for creating evidence-based advice for practitioners and contribute to the construction of new theories in academia
[
20
]
. To contribute to this research mission, we designed a case study
[
18
]
where we follow a software team from the point at which they were delivered an ML model to the point where a running application was considered complete.

The development team responsible for creating the application for the clinicians consisted of one traditional software developer, one data scientist enrolled as a software developer, two AI researchers with special competence in XAI and one product owner (the clinician who had collected the 557 training videos and ran the startup aiming to commercialize the application). The team was primarily collocated, with the software developer occasionally working from a satellite office. Additionally, two ML developers, who played key roles in developing the ML model, served as advisors and supported the team in discussions. A group of three clinicians at the local hospital were designated as users and participated in testing the application in the clinical setting.

Since all technology used in hospitals in Europe must be approved by the Medical Device Regulatory (MDR), the development team was not automatically permitted to have the clinicians test the application in clinical settings. However, we as researchers had thorough ethical discussions with the head of the clinic and in June 2024, the team was allowed to test the application on ten infants. This decision allowed the team to collect a three-minute video of each baby, run the videos through the model, and present the CP risk prediction to the clinicians. To minimize the impact of this experimental technology on the treatment of the infants, the team was only permitted to present the prediction after a decision for further treatment had been made. Then, for each infant, the clinicians provided feedback in a meeting with the team. During these meetings, the team prioritized what to develop until the next infant arrived for their three-month screening. Each baby represented a potential iteration, resulting in the development team planning ten iterations to conduct their application development. The team estimated that all ten iterations will be completed by June 2025.

In this short paper, we report findings from the first two iterations, during which the team heavily focused on eliciting explainability requirements. The overarching goal of this case study is to gain longitudinal insights into the process. Therefore, we will continue collecting data on the remaining eight iterations.

Data collection & analysis

To gain a deep understanding of the case, we decided to interview and observe both groups: the clinicians in the clinic and the team of developers. Interviews can be a valuable source of information as it allows in-depth conversation with experts, but it is only one of many potential methods to collect data in field studies
[
11
]
. Interviews fall in a category of methods that Lethbridge et al. have labeled inquisitive techniques
[
11
]
, in that a researcher must actively engage with interviewees to get information from them. A second category is observational techniques, which includes observation of professionals. Both types of techniques have benefits and drawbacks; interview data may be less accurate than observational data, but observational techniques may introduce the Hawthorne effect, where professionals alter their behavior because they know they are being observed
[
11
]
.

To thoroughly understand the process of eliciting explainability requirements, we needed to comprehend the reasons behind the developers’ actions. We also needed to understand the thoughts and reflections behind their decisions. Therefore, we conducted four interviews and observed ten meetings where they held discussions and made decisions.

To understand the needs of clinicians and their context within a hospital clinic, we conducted ethnographically inspired observations. Since a children’s clinic is a delicate context, only the first author conducted these observations. He spent entire days together with the clinicians, wore scrubs to blend into the environment, resulting in 40 hours of observations, 50 pages of field notes and 25 pictures. This meant we had observations in the examination of the infants for whom the model provided predictions. During observations, spontaneous interviews were conducted to capture clinicians’ thoughts and reflections. To support the team of developers, we provided them with summaries of our observations. This was potentially problematic because we risked stepping out of our role as researchers and into a developer role. We have gone to great lengths to maintain a role where we merely provide what we deemed to be objective observations and avoided giving direct advice or participating in decision-making within the team.

As this is a short paper presenting preliminary findings, the coding and analysis process is ongoing. We use a word processor for both open-ended coding and memoing
[
19
]
. Additionally, a study diary has been maintained throughout the study, containing descriptions of our research process to ensure reflectivity on past events and decisions. When analyzing the data, we hold an interpretative view, understanding the world and its truths as subjective realities
[
15
]
.

4
Findings

After the CP prediction model was completed, verified, and tested in 2022
[
7
]
, researchers began working on explaining the model, including to uncover the patterns it had identified. As there are no known biomarkers for CP, the researchers were highly interested in investigating the patterns discovered by the model, essentially “cracking open the black box.” However, this endeavor of using XAI methods proved to be exceedingly challenging. Identifying and developing functional XAI methods for the model – as was done continuously in the project, see i.a.
[
16
,
17
,
22
,
21
]
– was not sufficient to provide clinicians with satisfactory explanations, as the clinicians did not have sufficient prior understanding of the ML model. After spending years attempting to generate meaningful explanations, the team ultimately decided to develop a software application making the model accessible to clinicians, the primary end-users of the model.

The development team re-encountered this challenge when testing the prototype of the application: how to provide a convincing explanation of the model’s predictions to users? Consequently, the task of creating a software application presenting the model’s conclusions transformed from a purely software engineering endeavor into an effort to determine what explanations users would require to accept the application. That is, the task became eliciting the
explainability requirements
of the application. This new focus required the team to deeply understand their users’ needs and expectations regarding the model’s transparency, ensuring that the explanations provided would be meaningful for the clinicians.

The team, having access to clinicians at the hospital, initiated interviews to ascertain the explanations required for the clinicians to trust a model predicting CP. However, they soon discovered that it was challenging for the clinicians to conceptualize an ML model and its capabilities in a simulated setting. The absence of a clear understanding of ML models hindered the clinicians from providing informed responses. Nevertheless, the developers acquired domain knowledge from these sessions and began formulating assumptions regarding the necessary explanations. Although the developers were unable to elicit the explanation needs of their users directly, the effort was not wasted. The team now had several hypotheses regarding potentially valuable explanations, and initiated experiments to test explanations with clinicians. Conducting experiments in a hospital setting with real patients is not straightforward, as all technology used in hospitals must comply with the MDR requirements. Conducting the experiments in a laboratory setting was feasible, but it entailed developing each candidate explanation, integrating these into the application, and testing them with healthcare personnel, which was prohibitively resource-intensive.

Fortunately, the project was funded through the hospital’s research budget and the Norwegian Research Council. This gave the project a ’research’ label, and activities such as experimenting with technology were classified as ’research activities’. The head of clinic accepted that a number of 10 infants could be included in testing the application. “We have the opportunity to test different explanations on [videos of] these babies,” a developer explained and described their plan to present the predictions to the clinicians, and observe their reactions. The same developer hypothesized, “The questions they start asking can reveal what explanations they need [in order] to trust the model.” By analyzing the clinicians’ responses, the team hoped to gain insights into the necessary explanations required to establish trust in the ML model’s predictions.

“Approximately 30 babies are included to the screening program for CP each year,” a clinician explained. Each infant underwent a routine examination at 3 months of age. These examinations provided the development team with an opportunity to test the ML model and explanations in a clinical setting. In essence, each 3-month examination represented a potential iteration for the team to test and improve their explanations based on the reactions of the clinicians.

Iteration 1.
To serve as a basis for the software application and benchmark for later iterations, the team created a minimum viable product (MVP), or version 0.1, which provided the model’s conclusions without any accompanying explanations, for the first infant. The rationale behind this approach was that presenting the model’s predictions without explanations would prompt the clinicians to ask questions that could reveal the types of explanations they were seeking.

Continuing the scenario presented in the introduction, the clinician Hanne, who felt uneasy about the model’s high level of certainty in its conclusion, remarked, “Is it possible for me to see what the model has seen [in the video]? It would be interesting to see if we are thinking alike.” This single comment was pivotal for the development team, who interpreted it as an indication that Hanne did not necessarily require an explanation of
how
the model reasoned or
what
patterns it had identified, but rather needed to understand which point(s) in time the model had focused on. Following discussions, the team decided to design a graph indicating which points in time within the three-minute video were significant for the prediction score. This would essentially involve marking the video timeline to highlight the snippets that were decisive for the prediction. With the next infant scheduled for examination two weeks later, the team sat out to develop this basic explainability requirement.

Iteration 2.
Two weeks later, when the second infant arrived for the 3-month examination, version 0.2 of the application was ready for use. The developers had implemented a timeline feature that visualized the model’s prediction divided into five-seconds intervals of the video the (see figure
1
). Despite this advancement, the team remained concerned that the clinicians might inquire
why
the model predicted high or low risk in these snippets, as such questions are the ones that current XAI methods aim to answer, due to the non-interpretable nature of most ML models.

Back in the clinic, Hanne and her colleague had just concluded their examination of the second infant and determined the subsequent treatment plan. The application displayed the model’s prediction at 8% total risk of CP. Hanne appeared satisfied and remarked that this finding was consistent with their examination results. Figure
1
illustrates the timeline that reveals the model’s predictions for each time period. Hanne and a colleague immediately started investigating the timeline and observed that the model had identified some signals of CP in the final 30 seconds of the video. They meticulously analyzed this 30-second snippet. “Look there, yes, the fist is clenched, the foot is positioned like that.” They pointed at the screen, comparing their observations with what the model had detected. Hanne commented, “Now I can compare what I see with what the model sees.” This direct comparison between the physiotherapists’ assessment and the model’s predictions increased the clinicians’ trust in the model.

Our preliminary findings from the first two iterations demonstrate that the development team successfully elicited explainability requirements by collaborating iteratively with the clinicians. A software developer described the process: “Each new increment [part of the application developed] of the application triggered new reflections on what explanations they [the clinicians] needed. Then we tried to meet these needs in the next iteration and tested the effects.” This strongly indicates that the familiar software engineering concepts of increments and iterations can efficiently support the discovery and development of explanations for AI systems. We plan to continue studying the development process through upcoming iterations to gain a more comprehensive, longitudinal understanding of these phenomena and to validate our initial findings.

5
Discussion

This case study offers a descriptive account on how the process of eliciting explainability requirements can unfold within a small team in a healthcare context, offering some interesting insights. Concurrently, our findings answer another timely question: Who is responsible for eliciting explanations?

As our findings show, users only began to reflect on their need for explanations
after
interacting with the application. This left the software developers to provide the necessary explanations to secure user acceptance of the system. Although resources had been allocated to develop XAI methods for the CP prediction model earlier in the process, it was first after the users had interacted with the application that they could provide constructive input regarding AI explanations to the developer team. Interestingly, we can regard handling explainability requirements as a fourth way in which AI system development differs from traditional software engineering, adding to the three identified by Amershi et al
[
2
]
. In our case, the responsibility for eliciting explainability requirements fell to the software development team because they were in charge of the system during its introduction to the users’ context, which would have been impossible without the two AI developers staying on. However, as this is a single case study, we cannot generalize this finding, it raises an important question: will the developer team in charge post deployment of the model bear the main responsibility for explainability requirements, effectively making it a part of software engineering?

In our case, this challenge was addressed by including two AI researchers with expertise in XAI within the development team. Their involvement enabled the team to manage both software development and XAI tasks, highlighting the need for software teams to incorporate specialized AI expertise and become increasingly interdisciplinary.

Our findings also highlight another important point: iterations facilitated the elicitation of “interrogative explanations.” To the developers’ surprise, the clinicians accepted the absence of a detailed explanation for how the ML model predicted CP. Instead, they were provided with a graph (Figure 1) that allowed them to explore the model’s output and compare it to their own assessments. In this case, the model’s predictions were not
explained
– no biomarkers or specific factors used in the predictions were identified – but rather
enriched
with additional information. This enrichment enabled the clinicians to navigate and decide where to focus on the output, and manually align it with their own evaluations of the infants.

This suggests that developers can build trust in AI models by providing
investigative explanations
, namely tools that allow users to explore and make sense of the output themselves. This finding, which will be further explored in future iterations, align with Miller’s
[
13
]
argument that explainable AI should prioritize explanations that complement human cognitive processes rather than providing convincing arguments. A similar phenomenon was observed by Lebovitz et al.
[
10
]
in their influential study on diagnostic radiologists. They found that when radiologists could explore an AI system’s output – such as identifying which parts of radiology images the model focused on – they incorporated its output into their decision-making. This process, termed
AI interrogation practices
, involves human experts connecting their knowledge to the AI system’s knowledge
[
10
]
. Our preliminary findings indicate that “interrogative explanations” are a type of explainability requirement co-created by developers and users (as domain experts) through iterative collaboration.

6
Conclusion

Researchers are actively engaged in discussions regarding the nature of explainability requirements and how to define them
[
9
,
4
,
6
]
. To gain a comprehensive understanding of explainability in industrial settings, detailed accounts from real-world cases are essential. The findings presented in this paper analyze and demonstrate the usefulness and importance of such accounts, hopefully marking a beginning of applying iterative software development techniques for the integration of XAI methods.
