Sound-color cross-modal correspondences in the Chinese pentatonic modes played on traditional instruments
Authors: Chen KangHao, I-Ta Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSound–color cross-modal correspondences have been widely studied across different cultures and age groups. However, few studies have explored these associations using the Chinese pentatonic modes or adolescent participants. Due to the cultural and developmental nature of cross-modal correspondences, this gap limits the use of cross-modal strategies in Chinese music education. The present study tested 117 Chinese secondary school students (N = 117, Ma = 14.2) from three schools. Using two matching tasks, this study examined the associations between five Chinese modes (Gong, Shang, Jue, Zhi, Yu), three instrumental timbres (dizi, guzheng, erhu), and the Berkeley Color Project (BCP) colors. The results showed that, in terms of hue, Gong, Jue, Zhi, and Yu were linked to red, green, yellow, and blue, respectively. In lightness and saturation, Gong mode was matched with bright and saturated colors, while Yu mode was linked to dark and less saturated colors. These patterns formed the red–blue contrast between Gong and Yu modes, which differs from the red–green or yellow–blue contrasts found in the major and minor systems. In addition, instrumental timbre had a strong effect on perceived brightness: dizi was matched with the brightest colors, guzheng with medium brightness, and erhu with the darkest colors. This ranking was consistent with the spectral centroid values of each instrument. This study provides systematic evidence of sound–color correspondences for the Chinese pentatonic modes in adolescents. The findings offer a foundation for designing multisensory teaching tools in Chinese music education.Keywords: Cross-modal correspondences, Sound-color correspondences, Chinese pentatonic modes, Chinese traditional instrumental timbre, AdolescentsSubject terms: Psychology, Neurology

Introduction

The five senses (vision, hearing, smell, taste, and touch) interact with each other to help humans understand the world1. In the past, cross-sensory correspondences have sometimes been confused with synesthesia2, a rare and stable form of sensory pairing found in a small number of people3. A well-known case comes from Cytowic4, who documented a sound–touch synesthete named WM. He reported that flavors triggered sensations of weight, temperature, and texture along his limbs. Spence5 used the term cross-modal correspondences to describe common sensory pairings shared by most people, in order to distinguish them from synesthesia in neuroscience. Over the past decade, more than 1000 peer-reviewed articles on cross-modal correspondence have been published, encompassing both simple and complex pairings across nearly all human senses6.

Even today, although cross-modal correspondences and synesthesia are now classified as separate fields of study, associations between vision and hearing remain a major focus in both areas. The first known report of synesthesia came from an ophthalmologist with albinism, who reported that he and his sister saw colors when they heard sounds7. Spence and Di Stefano8 also emphasized that vision and hearing are the most dominant modalities in cross-modal correspondences. Numerous earlier studies have demonstrated that sound attributes—such as pitch, volume, and timbre—relate to visual features including size9, angle10, luminance9, brightness11, height9, and motion direction10. Palmer et al.12 initiated a cross-modal correspondence study of music and color, using 18 classical orchestral works by Bach, Brahms, and Mozart and asking participants to pair them with the Berkeley Color Project (BCP)13 (see Fig. 1). They found that music with a major tonality was matched with lighter, more saturated colors, while music with a minor tonality was matched with darker, less saturated colors. Since BCP colors are linked to emotions, Palmer et al.12 tested whether the selected musical pieces also conveyed emotional meanings and proposed the emotional mediation hypothesis. This hypothesis posits that color and music are associated because they evoke similar emotional meanings (e.g., red and C major both convey happiness).

Subsequent researchers expanded the sample tested by Palmer et al.12. Whiteford et al.14 included 34 music genres and invited participants from the United States and Mexico to take part in their experiment. Albertazzi et al.15 used Spanish music and thematic paintings to demonstrate correspondences between visual and complex auditory stimuli. Their findings reaffirmed the role of the emotional mediation hypothesis in music–color correspondences. Jonauskaite et al.16 investigated color–emotion associations in over 4500 participants from 30 countries. The results showed that these associations, such as red with happiness and blue with sadness, were widely shared across cultures. Since emotional mechanisms have been consistently supported in sensory pairings, Spence17 included them as one of the explanations for cross-modal correspondences.

Although both synesthesia and cross-modal correspondences refer to multisensory phenomena, one key difference lies in their stability6. Synesthesia is fixed and consistent. For example, a synesthete who associates the color red with the C tone will consistently perceive red whenever hearing that tone. In contrast, cross-modal correspondences are relative; people often link higher-pitched sounds with brighter rather than specific colors. Research on synesthesia often relies on case reports, due to its rarity (< 4%) and the uniqueness of individual associations3. By contrast, studies on cross-modal correspondences commonly use standard psychological matching tasks18. Considering the relative nature of cross-modal correspondences, exploring sensory pairings across different cultural contexts may help deepen our understanding of multi-sensory processing. For example, Whiteford et al.14 expanded emotional mediation tests beyond classical music, including 34 distinct musical genres. Following this approach, some researchers have begun to explore cross-modal correspondences based on Chinese music.

Tsai et al.19 found that high-pitched, fast melodies played in Gong mode on the guqin, a traditional plucked string instrument with five strings, are associated with bright colors. Ma et al.20 used the same instrument and mode to conduct an event-related potential (ERP) study grounded in the theory of the Five Elements in Chinese medicine. Their results showed that when the guqin was played with harmonics, participants preferred black or cyan, while open strings tended to elicit a preference for black. The two studies focused solely on a single instrument and mode.

Qi et al.21 investigated the cross-modal correspondences among color, texture, and taste for four Chinese musical instruments: erhu, guzheng, yunluo, and dizi. The results showed that the yunluo is associated with pink, umami taste, and the softest fabric. The erhu is associated with black and bitterness. The dizi is associated with brown, saltiness, and faux fur. The guzheng is associated with sweetness and the softest fabric. Liu et al.22 established a timbre database for 48 different Chinese musical instruments and analyzed their associations with color. Although the four instruments used by Qi et al.21 were also included in Liu et al.’s22 database, the timbre–color associations reported in the two studies showed differences. This discrepancy may be due to the use of different color stimuli in the two experiments. Nevertheless, the findings support the idea that cross-modal correspondences are relative rather than fixed.

Wang et al.23 conducted a cross-cultural timbre–emotion study using three Chinese instruments (erhu, dizi, pipa) and three Western instruments (violin, flute, and guitar). The sample included 160 participants, both musicians and non-musicians, from China and Western countries. The results showed that cultural background had a greater effect on emotional judgment than instrumental timbre. In addition, musicians also showed a stronger ability to identify emotional categories than non-musicians. Using a similar protocol, Heng and McAdams24 tested 90 participants from Singapore. Their results showed that Singaporean Chinese musicians were more sensitive to timbre–emotion associations than their Western musician and non-musician counterparts.

In contrast to studies focusing on specific musical features, Gao et al.25 examined the aesthetic relationships among 19 pieces of traditional Chinese music, Chinese paintings, and emotion. The results showed that music labeled as high-arousal was often associated with images of animals, color-rich patterns, and fine brushwork styles. In contrast, music labeled as low-arousal was linked to landscapes, ink-wash elements, and freehand brushwork styles. In this study, “color” referred broadly to the presence of color in the paintings, rather than to specific hues. “Ink-wash” indicated the use of black-and-white tonal patterns only. As a result, the study did not establish specific correspondences between Chinese music and color.

In summary, most existing studies have used the timbre of Chinese instruments as the primary auditory stimulus. Tsai et al.19 and Ma et al.20 employed Gong mode, one of the five traditional Chinese pentatonic modes, but the full pentatonic system has not yet been systematically examined in sound–color cross-modal research, despite the Chinese pentatonic system, comprising Gong, Shang, Jue, Zhi, and Yu, forms the foundational tonal framework of traditional Chinese music. In addition, to date, all cross-modal studies involving Chinese music have been conducted with participants over the age of 18. One possible explanation is the complexity of data collection and ethical approval processes, which may have limited research involving children or adolescents.

Cross-modal associations change across age groups. Gregory et al.26 found that children aged 7–8 were better at labeling major and minor modes with emotions than children aged 3–4. Similarly, Dalla Bella27 reported that five-year-olds could match musical tempo with emotional content, whereas children aged 6–8 could also link musical mode with emotion when listening to Renaissance music. Gerardi and Gerken28 observed that children more easily associated major modes with happiness but had more difficulty linking minor modes with sadness. Bonetti and Costa29 showed that associations between musical mode and emotion, movement, and brightness tended to strengthen with age, from four-year-olds to adults. In contrast, associations between mode and warm–cold colors were observed only in adults. Nieminen30 pointed out that children’s music–emotion associations also depend on cultural experience and learning. However, all of the cited studies focused on Western music and participants from Western cultural contexts.

Moreover, Scheller et al.31 found that, compared to individuals with early-onset blindness (before age 8), those with late-onset blindness during adolescence (13–15 years old) showed weaker audio–haptic integration. They further noted that the audio–haptic integration established during adolescence remains stable until late adulthood (over 65 years old). These findings suggest that adolescence is a critical period for multisensory integration.

Given the variability of cross-modal correspondences across age groups29 and cultural backgrounds12,14, understanding associations between Chinese pentatonic mode and color could inform music education practices. Webb32 points out that visual aids have long been used to support students’ understanding of music from different cultures. However, for educators seeking to incorporate cross-modal approaches into Chinese music teaching, a challenge remains: the lack of validated color correspondences for the pentatonic modes among adolescents. This study adapts and extends the protocol developed by Palmer et al.12 to investigate mode–color associations among Chinese secondary students. This study addresses the following research question: What are the associations between Chinese pentatonic modes, Chinese instrumental timbre, and BCP colors among Chinese secondary students?

The first task replicates Palmer et al.’s12 protocol: students are asked to select matching or non-matching colors for each of the five Chinese pentatonic modes. Their responses are weighted to calculate a music–color association (MCA) score. The second task serves as a validation procedure. Students are presented with selected colors and asked to choose the matching or non-matching mode. This study aims to provide empirical support for developing future multisensory music education programs involving Chinese traditional music.

Results

Associations between Chinese pentatonic modes and BCP color

Task I: Selected color for mode

Following the protocol by Palmer et al.12, MCA scores were computed for each Chinese pentatonic mode (Gong, Shang, Jue, Zhi, Yu) across four color dimensions: red–green, yellow–blue, light–dark, and saturated–unsaturated. Separate one-way ANOVAs were conducted for each dimension to assess whether MCA scores differed across modes. Figure 2(a–d) shows the average MCA scores for each color dimension across timbres and modes.

(a–d) The MCA scores of Gong, Shang, Jue, Zhi, and Yu mode in four color dimension.

In the red–green dimension, the effect was significant, F(4, 580) = 321.73, p < 0.001. Post hoc comparisons using Tukey’s HSD test showed that Gong mode (M = 24.95) had significantly higher scores than all other modes (ps < 0.001), while Jue mode (M = − 24.62) had significantly lower scores. The yellow–blue dimension also showed a significant effect, F(4, 580) = 794.29, p < 0.001. Post hoc tests indicated that Zhi mode (M = 67.47) was associated with significantly higher scores than all other modes (ps < 0.001), whereas Yu mode (M = − 73.02) was associated with significantly lower scores (ps < 0.001).

In the light–dark dimension, the mode effect remained significant, F(4, 580) = 582.56, p < 0.001. Post hoc tests showed that Gong mode (M = 28.14) had significantly higher brightness scores, while Yu mode (M = − 8.50) had significantly lower scores (ps < 0.001). A similar pattern was observed in the saturation dimension, F(4, 580) = 269.91, p < 0.001. Post hoc tests revealed that Gong mode (M = 20.08) had significantly higher saturation scores than all other modes (ps < 0.001), whereas Yu mode (M = − 15.33) again had significantly lower scores (ps < 0.001). All comparisons across the four dimensions remained significant after Bonferroni correction (adjusted α = 0.005).

Task I demonstrated associations between five Chinese pentatonic modes and BCP color. Gong, Jue, Zhi, and Yu modes were consistently linked with red, green, yellow, and blue, respectively. Additionally, Gong mode associated with brighter and more saturated colors, whereas Yu mode was associated with darker and less saturated colors.

Meanwhile, colors linked to Yu mode (M = 58.48, SD = 20.90) had significantly lower saturation than those associated with other modes (M = 69.04, SD = 23.53), t(232) = − 4.76, p < 0.001, d = − 0.46, 95% CI [− 0.66, − 0.25].

The findings from Task II further confirmed associations between Chinese pentatonic modes and BCP color from Task I. When selecting a mode based on color, participants were more likely to choose Gong mode for red, bright, and highly saturated colors, and Yu mode for blue, dark, and less saturated ones. Similarly, Zhi mode was frequently chosen for yellow colors, and Jue mode was associated with green colors.

Associations between chinese instrumental timbre and BCP color

A positive linear relationship was observed between acoustic brightness and perceived color brightness, as shown in Fig. 4. We used spectral centroid and spectral spread to represent acoustic brightness, and calculated perceived brightness from lightness (L*) values in the L*ab color space, based on participants’ color selections.

Brightness correspondence between acoustic features and colors across chinese instruments.

To assess the effect of timbre on color brightness, we conducted a one-way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with musical mode entered as a covariate. The results showed a significant main effect of timbre on L* values, F(2, 428) = 1158.64, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.84. The effect of mode also remained significant, F(4, 428) = 60.74, p < 0.001. Descriptive statistics revealed a clear brightness ranking across timbres. Participants selected the brightest colors for dizi (M = 83.99), moderately bright colors for guzheng (M = 71.83), and the darkest colors for erhu (M = 40.78). These results suggest that a color brightness mapping is dizi → bright, guzheng → medium, and erhu → dark.

To examine the relationship between acoustic brightness and perceived color brightness, we analyzed the correlations between acoustic features and participants’ selected L* values. Spectral centroid was strongly positively correlated with L* values (r = 0.70, p < 0.001), while log-transformed spectral spread also showed a strong positive correlation (r = 0.63, p < 0.001). Tukey’s HSD post hoc tests further confirmed these differences. Dizi elicited significantly higher L* values than both guzheng (M difference = 12.16, 95% CI [9.42, 14.89], p < 0.001) and erhu (M difference = 43.21, 95% CI [40.51, 45.92], p < 0.001), while guzheng also showed significantly higher values than erhu (M difference = 31.06, 95% CI [28.29, 33.82], p < 0.001). All pairwise comparisons remained significant after Bonferroni correction (adjusted α = 0.005). The results indicate that instrumental brightness influenced participants’ perception of color brightness.

This task demonstrated associations between Chinese instrumental timbre and BCP color. Specifically, both acoustic features and participants’ selections revealed a consistent brightness pattern: the brightest colors were chosen for dizi, moderately bright colors for guzheng, and the darkest for erhu.

Discussion

This study examined the relationships between the Chinese pentatonic modes (Gong, Jue, Zhi, and Yu modes), Chinese instrumental timbres, and BCP colors among Chinese adolescents using a two-way matching task. The results showed that in hue dimensions, Gong, Jue, Zhi, and Yu modes were associated with red, green, yellow, and blue, respectively. For color intensity, Gong mode was linked to light and saturated colors, while Yu mode was linked to dark and unsaturated ones. Among the three instruments, dizi was matched with bright colors, erhu with dark ones, and guzheng fell in between.

The relationship between modes and colors

Due to the limited number of studies on mode–color correspondences, especially within the Chinese pentatonic system, we referred to the findings of Palmer et al.12 and Whiteford et al.14 as a comparative framework. These studies were based on Western major and minor modes. Tab. 1 presents a comparison between our findings and those from the two studies. Although our study focused on the Chinese pentatonic system, which differs in structure and cultural context, some of the results showed similar patterns. Gong and Zhi modes were associated with red and yellow, colors that had been linked to major modes in Western research, while Jue and Yu modes were associated with green and blue, which had been linked to minor modes.

These mode–color associations in this study may have been influenced by the the relative characteristics of stimulus set and the choice strategy in the two-way matching task. Gong mode (C–D–E–G–A) includes a major third (C–E) and a perfect fifth (C–G), similar to the Western major. Yu mode (A–C–D–E–G) consists a minor third (A–C) and resembles the pitch structure of the minor mode. In contrast, the structures of Zhi mode (G–A–C–D–E) and Jue mode (E–G–A–C–D) lack a clear tonal center and show weaker tonality.

According to Marks11 and Brunetti et al.33 cross-modal mappings are based on contrasts within the stimulus set, rather than on absolute physical values. In the BCP color system, red-family colors have the highest brightness and saturation, while blue-family colors have the lowest in both dimensions. A systematic review of Chinese music education over the past 20 years also noted that school music education has placed greater emphasis on major/minor systems than on traditional Chinese music theory34. Di Stefano et al.35 suggest that complex musical concepts can lead to stable cross-cultural mappings. Since Gong and Yu modes are structurally similar to major and minor, students may have related more easily to their interval qualities (major or minor third) during the task. This familiarity may have encouraged students to treat these two modes as salient reference points in the matching task. Despite the randomized order of presentation, Gong and Yu often became the first modes participants identified, after which they assigned color poles to them and then distributed the remaining colors to Zhi, Jue, and Shang.

The mode–color correspondences observed in this study may also be interpreted through the harmony of cognitive processing. According to Spence and Di Stefano36, harmonious stimuli, whether within a unisensory modality or across modalities, can promote processing fluency. The Chinese pentatonic modes do not include tritones, which are considered the most unstable intervals in harmonic systems. At the same time, the BCP color was designed based on affective preferences and tends to be visually pleasant. The unisensory harmony between the musical and color stimuli may have contributed to cross-modal harmony in the matching task, thereby enhancing the sound–color associations observed in our findings.

The color polarity of red/blue in mode-color correspondences

Gong and Yu modes showed the contrast in brightness and saturation. Specifically, Gong mode had the highest scores in the red–green dimension (a*), while Yu mode showed the lowest scores in the yellow–blue dimension (b*). These findings suggest that the color polarity in the sound–color association of the Chinese pentatonic system aligns with a red–blue contrast, rather than with the red–green contrast14 or the yellow–blue contrast12 observed in major and minor modes. According to the Ecological Valence Theory (EVT)13, environmental and cultural factors may influence color preferences and cross-modal associations. Although color preferences among Chinese adolescents remain understudied, existing findings on Chinese adults may help explain this red–blue contrast of mode-color correspondences.

A color preference study on cross-age and cross-cultural differences by Hurlbert and Ling37 reported that Chinese participants preferred red compared to those from the UK. Moreover, the red–blue color contrast has also been observed in other cognitive studies, such as color–emotion associations38, cognitive task performance39, and color preference ratings40.

Wang et al.38 found that blue was associated with positive emotions and red with negative emotions, attributed to physiological mechanisms. However, red could also evoke positive feelings in a linguistic or cultural context. Xia et al.39 showed that red enhanced performance in simple, detail-focused tasks, while blue supported complex detail-focused tasks and creative tasks. Zhang et al.40, using a color system similar to that in this study, conducted a large-scale survey (N = 1290) and found that red, cyan, and blue were the most preferred colors across all age and gender groups. Blue–green colors had the largest share of overall preference.

It is worth noting that some studies have found preferences for other colors in specific contexts, such as yellow in hotel design41 or purple and pink in fashion42. Zhang et al.40 also noted that color preference can change with age. Despite these variations, the findings of this study suggest that the red–blue contrast was the dominant color polarity in Chinese pentatonic mode–color matching. This pattern aligns with previous cognitive studies involving Chinese participants on color–emotion associations38 and color preferences40.

The association between chinese instrumental timbre and brightness

The findings on instrumental timbre and brightness in this study are consistent with those of Qi et al.21 and Liu et al.22. Although different color stimuli were used, erhu was consistently associated with the darkest color across all three studies. In cross-cultural research on timbre–emotion associations, Wang et al.23 and Heng and McAdams24 also found that erhu was linked to low-arousal emotions. Taken together, the association between erhu and low-brightness colors was stable.

However, Qi et al.21 did not include the guzheng, and Liu et al.22 did not include the dizi. Moreover, Heng and McAdams24 reported no statistically significant timbre–emotion mappings for aerophones (e.g., dizi) and plucked chordophones (e.g., pipa). Given that the guzheng also belongs to the plucked chordophone family, the color associations of both dizi and guzheng warrant further investigation.

Limitations

Because this study was conducted in three secondary schools and aimed to minimize disruption to students’ school life, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the gold standard for synesthesia diagnosis (Synesthesia Battery), such as the Test of Genuineness (TOG) and its revised version (TOG-R), requires repeated assessments and could not be implemented within the time of this setting. Second, although we used a weighted scoring method to analyze the match and non-match choices, more detailed response formats, such as sliders or rating scales, were not used because of space and equipment restrictions. Third, all three schools were located in the same area, and nearly all of the 115 students were Chinese, except for two Italian students. This means the findings may not apply to students from other regions or cultural backgrounds. Fourth, although the modes were presented in a randomized order, their structural similarity to major/minor may have increased familiarity for some modes, which could have influenced participants’ matching results.

Previous studies in Chinese music have mapped pitch, volume, timbre, and rhythm to color. This study extends that work by confirming mode–color correspondences in the Chinese pentatonic system. By identifying these associations among adolescents, it adds a missing piece to the framework of sound–color research in Chinese music and offers evidence-based guidance for selecting color aids in music education.

Method

Participants

A total of 117 participants (66 males, 51 females; mean age = 14.2 years) were recruited from three middle schools through purposive sampling. Prior to the study, all participants self-reported having no synesthetic experiences (e.g., hearing sounds accompanied by visual sensations). The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the authors’ institution (Approval No. UM.TNC2/UMREC_2787). This study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were informed about the procedure, potential risks, and conduct requirements. Signed informed consent was obtained from both the participants and their legal guardians.

The 117 students were then randomly divided into four groups corresponding to one of the following timbres: dizi, guzheng, erhu, or pure tone. Each group would hear the same order of the Chinese pentatonic modes but played using only their assigned timbre.

Measurement metrics

Visual stimuli

The study used the BCP (see Fig. 1), developed by Palmer & Schloss13. It contained eight colors, each with four levels of saturation and luminance: saturated (S), light (L), medium (M), and dark (D). In addition, five achromatic colors were included: black (B), white (W), and three shades of gray. All visual stimuli were displayed on a Seewo teaching screen (86-inch, 3840 × 2160 resolution, 0.49 × 0.49 mm pixel pitch, 10-bit color depth, 72% NTSC color gamut, 4000:1 contrast ratio).

Auditory stimuli

The study used five Chinese pentatonic modes: Gong, Shang, Jue, Zhi, and Yu, each centered around the standard tone A. The timbres included pure tone, guzheng, erhu, and dizi. The stimuli were grouped into four timbre-based sets, each containing five tracks corresponding to the five modes. All audio stimuli were encoded in MP3 format (44.1 kHz sample rate, 192 kbps bitrate). The sounds were played through a Marshall Woburn III speaker, calibrated to ensure consistent volume and clarity across sessions.

Experimental tasks

The study employed a two-way cross-modal matching design. The 117 participants were randomly divided into four groups, each tested with a different timbre: pure tone, dizi, erhu, and guzheng. Each group completed two tasks.

Task I: Selected color for sound

The 37 BCP colors were displayed on the screen. Five Chinese pentatonic modes were played randomly, with each sound stimulus played three times. During the first three playbacks, participants selected three colors that matched the track. During the subsequent three playbacks, they selected three colors that did not match the track.

Task II: Selected sound for color

One of the 37 BCP colors was displayed on the screen. Simultaneously, five Chinese pentatonic modes were played randomly, with each sound stimulus played three times. At the end of the playback, participants were required to choose the track that matched the currently displayed color.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis of task 1

Following the analytical protocol established by Palmer et al.12, this study examined the relationship between the dimensional variations in Chinese pentatonic modes (instrumental timbre and mode) and the dimensional structure of color. This was achieved by computing a music-color association (MCA) score for each mode (Gong, Shang, Jue, Zhi, Yu) across the four-color appearance dimensions: Red/Green, Yellow/Blue, Light/Dark, and Saturated/Unsaturated, as rated by the same participants.

For each color dimension, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to assess whether MCA scores significantly differed across the five modes. If a significant main effect was observed, post hoc comparisons were then conducted using Tukey’s HSD test to identify which specific modes differed from one another. To control for family-wise error due to multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied, setting the adjusted significance threshold at α = 0.005.

Data analysis of Task 2

The research team first counted the frequency of the 37 BCP colors selected for each mode. The colors were also numerically coded as follows: 1 = red, 2 = orange, 3 = yellow, 4 = chartreuse, 5 = green, 6 = cyan, 7 = blue, 8 = purple, 9 = grays, 10 = white, 11 = black. To analyze color–mode associations, independent-samples t-tests were conducted to compare the color values selected for each mode to those selected for all other modes combined. These comparisons were carried out separately for four dimensions: red–green (a), yellow–blue (b), light–dark (L*), and saturation. For each test, we reported Cohen’s d and 95% confidence intervals. To control for multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied with an adjusted significance threshold of α = 0.005.

Data analysis of timbre-color associations

One-way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted to examine the effect of instrumental timbre (dizi, guzheng, erhu) on perceived color brightness. Chinese pentatonic modes were included as a covariate to control for their potential influence. When a significant main effect of timbre was identified, post hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey’s HSD test. The significance threshold was adjusted to α = 0.005 using Bonferroni correction to account for multiple comparisons. Acoustic features, including spectral centroid and spectral spread, were extracted using the open-source library Essentia (v2.1). Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between these acoustic measures and the average L values for each timbre.
