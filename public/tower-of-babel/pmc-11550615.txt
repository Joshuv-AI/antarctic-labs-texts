Introduction

Ascariasis, a parasitic infestation caused by Ascaris lumbricoides, remains a significant health issue in regions where sanitation is poor, particularly in tropical and subtropical countries [1]. Biliary ascariasis, though uncommon, can lead to significant biliary tract obstruction, cholangitis, or cholecystitis [2]. Severe complications, such as gangrene or gallbladder perforation, are exceedingly rare, even in cases of heavy infestation [3].

This manuscript is prepared in line with the SCARE criteria [4].

Case presentation

A 28-year-old female farmer from a rural area with low socioeconomic status presented with a one-year history of intermittent dull aching pain in the right upper quadrant. The pain was exacerbated upon the intake of fatty meals. A week prior to her presentation, she had developed a low-grade fever, jaundice, dark urine, and pruritus. Otherwise, she had no history of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, or retroviral infection.

On examination, the patient appeared acutely ill but remained conscious and alert. Vital signs showed a blood pressure of 110/70 mmHg, tachycardia with a heart rate of 120 beats per minute, a respiratory rate of 20 per minute, a fever of 38.6 °C, and oxygen saturation of 99 % on room air. The sclera was markedly icteric. Abdominal examination revealed tenderness in the right upper quadrant with a positive Murphy sign, but no organomegaly or palpable masses were noted.

Laboratory investigations revealed leukocytosis, with a white blood cell count of 42 × 103/μL, consisting of 89 % granulocytes. Hemoglobin and platelet levels were within normal ranges. Serum bilirubin was elevated at 2.77 mg/dL, with direct bilirubin at 1.2 mg/dL, and alkaline phosphatase at 480 IU/L, though liver enzymes remained within normal limits. An abdominal ultrasound revealed the presence of worms in the common bile duct (Fig. 1), and a CT scan confirmed choledocholithiasis with ascending cholangitis.

Abdominal ultrasound showing ascaris worm in the common bile duct.

The patient was resuscitated with intravenous fluids and started on ceftriaxone, metronidazole, vitamin K, and analgesics. She was prepared for surgery with a diagnosis of severe acute cholangitis due to Ascaris in the common bile duct (CBD) complicated by choledocholithiasis.

Intraoperatively, 300 mL of free bile was found in a cavity formed by the abdominal wall, omentum, liver, and transverse colon. Significant adhesions were observed between the gallbladder, CBD, duodenum, and transverse colon. A sealed perforation was noted at the fundus of the gallbladder (Fig. 2), which was distended and filled with multiple small stones. One dead Ascaris and three large stones, along with sludge, were found in the dilated (2 cm) and edematous CBD (Fig. 3). The bile was aspirated, adhesions were released, Kocherization and cholecystectomy was performed. The CBD was explored, and the dead Ascaris, stones, and sludge were removed. The CBD was then irrigated with normal saline using an 8 Fr NG tube. The peritoneum was washed with warm saline, a 16 Fr T-tube was inserted, and a drain was placed in the subhepatic space. The abdomen was closed in layers.

Intraoperative picture revealing perforated gallbladder.

Photograph showing gallbladder stone and Ascaris lumbricoides.

Postoperatively, the patient showed significant improvement, with resolution of jaundice and pruritus, and a reduction in T-tube drain output. She was discharged with antihelminthic medication and scheduled for follow-up.

Discussion

Ascaris lumbricoides, commonly referred to as Ascaris or ascariasis, is the most prevalent soil-transmitted intestinal parasite affecting humans. Ascariasis continues to be widespread, with an estimated 772 to 892 million individuals infected globally [5].

Intestinal obstruction by a bolus of worms is the most common ascariasis related emergency [6]. Biliary ascariasis is a rare but important cause of biliary obstruction and cholangitis, particularly in endemic regions. The migration of Ascaris lumbricoides into the biliary tree can lead to various complications, including biliary colic, cholangitis, cholecystitis, and gallbladder perforation. The clinical presentation can vary from asymptomatic to severe biliary sepsis [7].

Ascariasis has a natural inclination to migrate and seek small orifices [8]. The pathophysiology of biliary ascariasis involves the migration of adult worms from the intestine into the biliary ducts. This migration can cause mechanical obstruction, inflammation, and secondary bacterial infection, leading to cholangitis. In severe cases, ischemia of the gall bladder wall from infection and pressure can cause perforation, as seen in our case [9].

Diagnosis is often based on clinical suspicion, especially in patients from endemic areas presenting with biliary symptoms. Imaging modalities such as ultrasound and CT scans are crucial for identifying the presence of worms and stones in the biliary tree. Ultrasonography is highly sensitive and specific in visualizing a worm in the biliary system, as well as monitoring its mobility to and from the ducts over time [10]. Ultrasonographic imaging of ascariasis in a longitudinal plane reveals a linear echogenic structure. The central sonolucent line corresponds to the parasite's digestive tract, while two parallel echogenic lines on either side delineate the body's outer boundaries. In the transverse view, the parasite appears as a “bull's-eye target,” characterized by an echogenic outer margin surrounding a central sonolucent line, representing the digestive tract [11]. CT scans provide additional information on the extent of biliary obstruction and any associated complications. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) can be both diagnostic and therapeutic, allowing for the removal of worms and stones [12,13].

Management typically involves a combination of medical and surgical interventions. Initial resuscitation with intravenous fluids and antibiotics is essential to stabilize the patient. Broad-spectrum antibiotics such as ceftriaxone and metronidazole are commonly used to cover both gram-negative and anaerobic bacteria. Surgical intervention is often required to remove the obstructing worms and stones and to address any complications such as gallbladder perforation [14,15]. In this case, Laparotomy was performed to remove Ascaris, stones, and the gallbladder.

According to Neimeier, there are three different types of gallbladder perforation: type I, which is chronic perforation with fistulous communication; type II, which is subacute perforation with a surrounding abscess that is contained by adhesions; and type III, which is acute perforation and spillage to the cavity with generalized biliary peritonitis [16]. According to Niemeier classification, our patient had a type II perforation.

Postoperative care includes antihelminthic therapy to prevent recurrence. Albendazole and mebendazole are commonly used antihelminthic agents that are effective against Ascaris lumbricoides. Follow-up is essential to monitor for any signs of recurrence or complications [17].

This case underscores the importance of considering parasitic infections in the differential diagnosis of biliary diseases, particularly in endemic regions. Improved sanitation and hygiene practices are crucial for preventing ascariasis and its complications. Public health measures such as mass deworming programs and health education can significantly reduce the burden of ascariasis in endemic areas [18].

Conclusion

This case highlights the rare occurrence of gallbladder perforation due to biliary ascariasis, emphasizing the need for early recognition of parasitic infections as a cause of severe biliary complications, particularly in endemic areas.
