The development of a patient-reported outcome measuring opioid use disorder financial toxicity: Establishing content validity and reliability
Authors: Precious Anyanwu, Tyler J. Varisco, Olajumoke A. Olateju, Lauren R. Gilbert, Motolani E. Ogunsanya, E. James Essien, Michael L. Johnson, Vaishnavi Tata, J. Douglas Thornton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFinancial barriers are a major obstacle to engagement in medication treatment for opioid use disorder (OUD), contributing to poor adherence, reduced quality of life, and psychological distress. Globally, the economic impact of substance use disorders, including OUD, strains health systems and individual finances alike, yet measurement of the patient-level financial impact remains underdeveloped. Financial toxicity captures the combined financial and psychosocial impact of treatment, yet no validated patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) exists for individuals receiving medications for OUD (MOUD). This study describes the development and preliminary validation of the Measuring Opioid Use Disorder Financial Toxicity (MOUD-FiT) PROM.MethodsThe MOUD-FiT PROM was developed in three steps: (1) item generation via literature review, focus groups with adults receiving MOUD (n = 28 patients across 7 focus groups), and expert interviews (n = 5 treatment professionals); (2) content validation through cognitive debriefing with patients receiving MOUD (n = 7) and the Lawshe method with a panel of 30 lay experts (patients receiving MOUD); and (3) psychometric evaluation of internal consistency using the same 30 participants. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, and analyses included inter-item and item-total correlations and Cronbach’s α. The MOUD-FiT PROM requires approximately 5–10 min to complete.ResultsThe initial 96-item pool was reduced to 40 items following expert review and thematic classification (affect, coping, direct cost, indirect cost, support). Cognitive debriefing refined wording, and 18 items were excluded based on Lawshe content validity ratios. Further reduction for redundancy and low item-total correlations yielded a 15-item MOUD-FiT PROM. The final instrument showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α=0.90), with mean inter-item and item-total correlations of 0.38 and 0.59, respectively. Items included five affect, three coping, three direct-cost, and four indirect-cost items.ConclusionsThe MOUD-FiT PROM demonstrates preliminary content validity and excellent reliability for assessing financial toxicity in patients receiving MOUD. As a development study, the current findings establish content validity and internal consistency; additional evaluation of construct and criterion validity, test-retest reliability, and factor structure is needed before broader implementation.

Introduction

Substance use disorders, including opioid use disorder (OUD), represent a significant public health challenge worldwide. The Global Burden of Disease Study estimates that opioid use disorders account for substantial disability-adjusted life-years across regions, with associated economic costs affecting individuals, families, and health systems globally (GBD, 2019 Diseases and Injuries Collaborators, 2020). While the scale and nature of the opioid crisis vary by country, driven by differences in prescribing practices, illicit supply, and health system structures, the financial burden of accessing and sustaining treatment is a shared concern across settings (Degenhardt et al., 2014). In the United States, where the opioid epidemic has been particularly acute, financial barriers to treatment have been extensively documented.

In 2017, the United States Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) recognized the growing threat of the opioid epidemic and made a commitment to improving access to treatment, prevention, and recovery services as part of their five-point plan to combat the public health crisis (United States Department of Health and Human Services, 2017). While medications for OUD have been proven an effective strategy in treating OUD, many barriers to receiving this treatment exist (Rapp et al., 2006). These barriers include stigma, lack of readiness to quit, inability to access treatment, and financial barriers, including the inability to afford or maintain treatment costs (Sharma et al., 2017, University of Michigan, 2019, Bapat et al., 2022). In addition, structural barriers to MOUD access, including gaps in insurance coverage, geographic disparities in provider availability, and socioeconomic constraints, have been well documented. (Lagisetty et al., 2019, Saloner et al., 2020, Andrilla et al., 2019, Pilarinos et al., 2022) For example, studies have shown that a substantial proportion of U.S. counties, particularly in rural areas, lack a waivered buprenorphine provider, and access to MOUD varies significantly by insurance status and income. (Saloner et al., 2020, Andrilla et al., 2019) These barriers often resulting in delayed or forgone treatment and increased reliance on out-of-pocket expenditures and indirect costs such as transportation and lost productivity, which contribute to financial strain experienced during treatment. The U.S. Department of Defense estimated the annual cost of treating OUD with a medication for OUD (MOUD) from a certified opioid treatment program (OTP) was between $7476 and $17,642 USD in 2022 (TRICARE, 2016). Although these estimates derive from the U.S. context, they illustrate the magnitude of treatment costs that patients may face, a challenge not unique to any single country. The individual cost for a person to treat OUD includes direct (e.g., out-of-pocket expenditures) and indirect (e.g., productivity loss) costs, collectively known as the illness-related financial burden (Hodgson and Meiners, 1982). When the illness-related financial burden is deemed unaffordable, it can lead to declines in quality of life and psychological symptoms (Hodgson and Meiners, 1982, de Souza et al., 2014, Pollack, 2009).

Popularized in oncology due to the extreme out-of-pocket cost to the patient, the term “financial toxicity” can be described as the illness-related financial burden and the psychosocial effects associated with the cost of treatment, which can lead to diminished patient outcomes and poorer quality of life (de Souza et al., 2014, Pollack, 2009). It has also been associated with increased symptom burden, medication non-adherence, avoiding mental health treatment, medical appointments, and recommended medical tests due to the cost of treatment (Chino and Zafar, 2019, Knight et al., 2018, Lathan et al., 2016, Sharp et al., 2013, Zullig et al., 2013). Financial toxicity has been studied extensively in oncology, and to a lesser extent in other chronic conditions such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease, but remains largely unexplored in the context of substance use disorders (Witte et al., 2019). Unlike cancer, currently there is no validated instrument to measure financial toxicity among patients with OUD, a gap that this study aims to address by creating a patient-reported outcome measure (PROM).

The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) defines patient-reported outcomes as “any report of the status of a patient’s health condition that comes directly from the patient, without interpretation of the patient’s response by a clinician or anyone else,” – giving importance to the patient perspective as the most important in assessing this construct (FDA, 2009). Despite the clear impact that financial strain can place on treatment access, engagement, and recovery trajectories, financial toxicity in OUD care remains poorly understood, inconsistently measured, and largely overlooked in both research and clinical settings. Existing OUD research tends to focus on structural barriers to OUD treatment, such as insurance coverage, geographic access, and provider availability (Rapp et al., 2006, Sharma et al., 2017), but does not directly capture patients’ lived experiences of financial stress, trade-offs, or the psychological toll of treatment-related expenses. Importantly, although validated financial toxicity instruments exist in oncology, most notably the COmprehensive Score for financial Toxicity (COST) PROM (de Souza et al., 2017), these tools were developed for cancer populations and do not address the unique treatment contexts of OUD, including daily or frequent clinic visits for methadone, transportation barriers, employment disruption related to treatment schedules, and the stigma-related financial consequences specific to substance use disorders. As a result, clinicians, policymakers, and treatment programs lack the data needed to fully understand how financial pressures shape treatment decisions, retention, or recurrence risk. This study describes the development of the Measuring Opioid Use Disorder Financial Toxicity (MOUD-FiT) PROM and the process of establishing content validity and reliability.

Methods

We employed a sequential, multi-phase instrument development process to construct and preliminarily assess the MOUD-FiT PROM, a measure of financial toxicity among individuals receiving MOUDs. Procedures aligned with established guidelines for PROM development and included item generation, content validation, and psychometric evaluation. All study activities were approved by the University of Houston Institutional Review Board. Participants were recruited in Houston, Texas, United States, from December 2021 to August 2022 and were eligible if they were ≥ 18 years of age, proficient in English, diagnosed with OUD, and currently receiving MOUD treatment. Participants were recruited from a local opioid treatment program (OTP). All participants across the study phases were patients with OUD currently receiving MOUD; no providers served as participants in any phase. The expert panel consulted during item generation consisted of treatment professionals and was distinct from the patient participant sample. The MOUD-FiT PROM requires approximately 5–10 min to complete.

Item generation

Establishing content validity is an essential step in PROM development to ensure that the instrument accurately reflects the construct of interest (Patrick et al., 2011a, Patrick et al., 2011b). Incorporating input from both clinicians and patients enhances the relevance of the PROM to the lived experiences and treatment contexts of individuals with OUD receiving MOUD (Patrick et al., 2011a, Patrick et al., 2011b). Since content validity provides preliminary evidence for construct validity and reliability and construct validity cannot be demonstrated in its absence, item generation for the MOUD-FiT followed a systematic, multi-source approach (Brod et al., 2009a). Items for the MOUD-FiT PROM were generated in three parts: a literature review, semi-structured qualitative focus group interviews in adult individuals with OUD treated with a MOUD, and structured interviews with treatment experts.

The literature review served to ensure alignment between the conceptual definition of financial toxicity and existing research, and to identify any previously developed items or scales that could inform item development (Gehlbach et al., 2010). We reviewed studies describing the cost of illness, financial burden, or cost-related barriers among individuals treated with MOUD, as well as existing financial toxicity instruments from oncology and other chronic disease populations, to identify items and domains potentially relevant to OUD. Findings were used to draft a preliminary conceptual framework outlining components of financial toxicity relevant to OUD treatment with a MOUD. Of the initial item pool, nine items were drawn or adapted from existing instruments and the published literature, while the remaining items were generated de novo from qualitative data.

Following the literature review, qualitative focus groups were conducted with adults diagnosed with OUD and currently receiving MOUD (n = 28 participants across 7 focus groups). These focus groups allowed us to assess whether the preliminary conceptualization of financial toxicity reflected respondents’ lived experiences and to refine the conceptual framework accordingly (Gehlbach and Brinkworth, 2011). Participant narratives were analyzed to identify domains and nuances of financial impact not captured in the literature. Item wording was drafted directly from salient themes emerging from the focus groups and the literature review.

To further enhance content validity, structured interviews were conducted with five treatment experts (one practitioner, one opioid treatment program director, two support coaches, and one counselor) (i.e., clinicians and professionals with extensive experience working with individuals receiving MOUD). Experts reviewed the preliminary item pool, identified gaps, and suggested additional items deemed important to assess financial toxicity in this population. These experts were not participants in any subsequent study phases; the expert panel was distinct from the lay expert (patient) panel used for Lawshe content validation. The research team then reviewed all items, removing those that did not adequately reflect the construct, demonstrated unclear or problematic wording, or were redundant or tangential (Fowler, 1995, Healy et al., 2003, Krosnick, 2018). The remaining items were classified into components from the conceptual framework.

Content validation

Items were retained for the MOUD-FiT PROM when consensus was reached through two complementary processes: cognitive debriefing interviews and quantitative content validation using the Lawshe method.

Cognitive debriefing interviews were conducted with patients receiving MOUD to evaluate the clarity, relevance, and acceptability of the draft items and to ensure the structure and format of the questionnaire were understandable to individuals receiving MOUD (Frost et al., 2007). These interviews followed an iterative process in which participants reviewed the instrument and identified issues with item wording, instructions, or response options (Turner et al., 2007). Items were then revised based on participant feedback, and the updated version was tested with a new set of participants. This cycle was repeated until consensus was achieved and the instrument was deemed acceptable for further validation (Turner et al., 2007).

The verbal probing approach is the most appropriate cognitive debriefing interview method for PROM development and was used in this study (Brod et al., 2009b). During in-person interviews, participants were asked standardized probes for each item, including: “How did you interpret the question?”, “How easy or difficult was it to understand?”, and “How easy or difficult was it to answer?” (Turner et al., 2007). Items were revised when two or more participants demonstrated similar challenges with a given component. Iterations continued in rounds of at least three participants until no new comprehension issues were identified (Turner et al., 2007).

Quantitative content validity was assessed using the Lawshe method (Lawshe, 1975). The Lawshe method is a structured, quantitative approach to content validation that has been widely used in educational and health sciences research to determine whether items on an instrument are considered essential by subject matter experts (Lawshe, 1975, Ayre and Scally, 2014). Classically, the method involves a panel of content experts; however, in PROM development, lay experts (i.e., individuals from the target population) may serve as raters to ensure that items reflect the patient perspective, consistent with FDA guidance on patient-reported outcomes (FDA, 2009, Wilson et al., 2012). This approach is particularly relevant when the construct of interest is inherently experiential, such as financial toxicity. In this study, lay experts were patients currently receiving MOUD who rated each item from the patient perspective. This approach requires a panel of lay experts (potential end-users) who serve as the raters. Lay experts rate each item as “essential,” “useful but not essential,” or “not necessary.” Items rated as “essential” by a sufficient proportion of experts are considered to have content validity and are retained for further testing (Lawshe, 1975).

Content validity ratios (CVRs) were calculated for each item using the formula, CVR = (ne – N/2) / (N/2), in which ne represents the number of experts rating the item as “essential” and N is the total number of experts (Lawshe, 1975). CVR values range from −1 (perfect disagreement) to + 1 (perfect agreement), with values above zero indicating that more than half of experts consider an item essential (Lawshe, 1975). Lawshe proposed that a 50% agreement (CVR greater than 0) provides some assurance of content validity. However, to account for levels of agreement that may have occurred by chance, critical CVR (CVRcritical) values were computed by Lawshe and his colleague Lowell Schipper (Lawshe, 1975). CVRcritical is the lowest level of CVR where the agreement of experts exceeds that of chance for a given item with a type I alpha error suggested to be 0.05 with a one-tailed test (Lawshe, 1975). As described by Ayre and Scally (2014), for a panel of 30 experts, the minimum CVR required for statistical significance at α = 0.05 (one-tailed) is 0.33, corresponding to at least 20 experts rating an item as essential.

This approach aligns with updated recommendations for improving the reporting and interpretation of Lawshe-based content validity estimates in health and social science research (Romero Jeldres et al., 2023, Ayre and Scally, 2014)

For a comprehensive review of the evolution and methodological refinements of the Lawshe approach, including updated critical value calculations and applications in the social sciences, we refer readers to Wilson et al. (Wilson et al., 2012).

For this study, lay experts were recruited by phone in August 2022. Recruitment ceased when 30 lay experts consented to participate. Participants completed the survey anonymously through Qualtrics®, rating items developed during Step 1. Applying the CVRcritical threshold for a panel of 30 experts, at least 20 experts were required to rate an item as “essential” for it to be retained for subsequent analysis (Ayre and Scally, 2014). The Lawshe method was implemented in addition to cognitive debriefing to provide a complementary quantitative assessment of content validity, thereby strengthening the overall rigor of the instrument development process.

Psychometric evaluation (internal consistency)

A quantitative item analysis was conducted to assess the internal consistency of the MOUD-FiT PROM and to reduce the item pool while preserving the conceptual breadth of the instrument. Internal consistency is a key indicator of reliability, and excessive questionnaire length can increase respondent fatigue and limit feasibility in clinical and research settings (Beaton et al., 2005). To address these concerns, we applied a concept-retention approach that prioritized removing redundant or low-performing items while maintaining adequate internal consistency, as assessed by Cronbach’s alpha, along with adequate inter-item and item-total correlations (Beaton et al., 2005).

Participants completed the survey electronically via an anonymous Qualtrics® survey link. Demographic information was collected at the beginning of the survey. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “not at all” to 5 = “a lot”), consistent with the response format used in the COmprehensive Score for financial Toxicity (COST) PROM. Inter-item correlations (IICs) and item-total correlations (ITCs) were used to evaluate item performance (Clark and Watson, 1995). Associations among item pairs were examined using IICs; when two items demonstrated an IIC > 0.70, indicating substantial redundancy, the item with the lower ITC was removed. ITCs were then used to determine each item’s contribution to the overall scale, with items below the commonly accepted threshold of 0.40 eliminated (Nunnally, 1967). All correlation analyses used Spearman’s rho due to the ordinal nature of the item responses (Ruscio, 2008). This reduction strategy was intended to preserve the internal consistency of the measure while ensuring that retained items adequately represented the domains identified in the conceptual framework (Clark and Watson, 1995, Nunnally, 1967).

Results

A total of 30 individuals participated in the Lawshe content validation process and the subsequent psychometric evaluation (Table 1). All 30 participants were patients currently receiving MOUD; the same cohort of 30 participants completed both the Lawshe ratings and the psychometric evaluation survey. The sample was evenly split by sex and most participants identified as White (80%) and non-Hispanic (80%). The age distribution reflected a predominantly younger to mid-adult population, with 30% aged 18–29 years, 43.3% aged 30–39 years, and 26.7% aged 40 years or older. Educational attainment varied, though the majority had at least some college education (56.7%), and 36.7% had completed high school or a GED. Over half of participants were employed full-time (56.7%). Payment sources were evenly distributed across Medicare/Medicaid, private insurance, and no financial support (each 26.7%), with 20% receiving grant-funded assistance. Participants were receiving a range of MOUDs, most commonly buprenorphine with naloxone (63.3%), followed by naltrexone (16.7%), methadone (13.3%), and buprenorphine alone (6.7%). Using deductive methods, the literature review yielded nine candidate items, while seven focus group interviews with 28 participants generated 34 candidate items through inductive thematic analysis. In total, 96 candidate items were generated across all sources. Members of the research team reviewed the full item pool and removed items that did not adequately reflect the construct of interest or demonstrated problematic wording, redundancy, overlap, or tangential content. Content validation

During step 2, seven participants completed cognitive debriefing interviews across two rounds. In round one (n = 3), two participants reported misunderstanding the term treatment, interpreting it exclusively as in-patient rehabilitation. To address this, the instrument instructions were revised for round two to clarify that treatment for OUD can occur across multiple settings and modalities. No additional comprehension issues were identified in round two (n = 4), defined a priori as concerns raised by two or more participants; therefore, consensus was reached and no further modifications were required.

Content validity was subsequently evaluated using the Lawshe method with 30 participants (lay experts; all patients receiving MOUD). Eighteen items did not meet the CVRcritical threshold—defined as at least 20 participants rating an item as essential—and were excluded (items 4, 5, 9, 10, 11, 16, 17, 18, 19, 22, 24, 25, 26, 27, 33, 41, 43, and 64). Psychometric evaluation (internal consistency)

In step 3, 30 participants completed the 40-item instrument; however, only 22 items were eligible for analysis following the removal of 18 items during the Lawshe content validity procedure. Of the 22 remaining items, six items (3, 28, 38, 51, 70, and 92) were excluded due to high inter-item correlations (IIC), indicating redundancy, and one item (15) was excluded due to low item-total correlation (ITC). The mean IIC for the final 15 items was 0.38 (range: 0.02–0.68) and the mean ITC was 0.59 (range: 0.41–0.76), suggesting that items were homogeneous yet demonstrated sufficient unique variance to avoid isomorphism or redundancy, supporting good construct validity (Figure A.1 in the Appendix) (Piedmont, 2014, Qin, 2006). The remaining four domains (affect, coping, direct cost, indirect cost) are consistent with the core components of the conceptual framework and align with the multidomain conceptualization of financial toxicity as described in prior literature (de Souza et al., 2017). Discussion

Financial toxicity is an underrecognized but potentially critical barrier to engagement in treatment with a MOUD that has long-reaching implications for adherence, retention, and quality of life. Despite evidence that financial strain can influence treatment decisions and outcomes, no validated PROM existed to assess this impact in individuals receiving treatment with a MOUD. The present study addresses this gap by developing the Measuring Opioid Use Disorder Financial Toxicity (MOUD-FiT) PROM through a multi-step, patient-centered approach. Using literature review, focus groups, and expert input, we generated a comprehensive item pool that was refined through cognitive debriefing and quantitative content validation. The resulting 15-item instrument demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α=0.90) and captured multiple domains of financial toxicity, including affect, coping, and both direct and indirect costs. These findings suggest that the MOUD-FiT PROM provides a reliable and relevant tool to quantify the financial and psychosocial impact experienced by patients receiving MOUD, filling a critical gap in both research and clinical practice.

Although financial toxicity has been most extensively studied in oncology, growing evidence suggests that the concept is relevant across chronic disease populations, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease (Witte et al., 2019). In these settings, patients similarly face direct treatment costs, lost income, and emotional distress related to financial strain. However, OUD treatment presents additional and distinct financial challenges, including frequent (often daily) clinic visits, transportation costs, employment disruption related to rigid treatment schedules, and stigma-related economic consequences. Existing instruments such as the COST PROM developed and validated in oncology populations capture general financial distress but do not fully reflect the indirect costs, coping strategies, and affective responses specific to the MOUD treatment context (de Souza et al., 2017). For these reasons, an OUD-specific instrument was warranted to ensure content validity and relevance to this population. At the same time, we acknowledge that financial toxicity is a cross-cutting construct, and future research should explore whether the MOUD-FiT, or an adapted version, may be applicable to broader substance use disorder populations or incorporated into hybrid frameworks that combine generic and condition-specific measures.

The multi-step development process ensured that the MOUD-FiT PROM reflects both the objective and subjective aspects of financial toxicity in OUD treatment. The combination of deductive and inductive methods allowed the initial 96-item pool to capture a wide range of experiences, from direct costs, such as medication or clinic fees, to indirect costs, including lost productivity and time. Of the 96 initial items, 9 were drawn or adapted from existing instruments identified in the literature review (including items informed by the COST PROM and prior cost-of-illness studies), while 87 were generated de novo through focus groups and expert interviews. Cognitive debriefing interviews confirmed that items were understandable and relevant to patients’ lived experiences, while the Lawshe method provided quantitative evidence of consensus on essential items. Subsequent psychometric evaluation demonstrated that the final 15-item instrument retained strong internal consistency and adequate item-total correlations, indicating that the items are cohesive yet sufficiently distinct to measure separate aspects of financial toxicity. Although not suggested in the FDA guidelines or ISPOR recommendations, the research team implemented the Lawshe method to quantitatively establish content validity and introduce more rigor to the MOUD-FiT development process. The utilization of the CVRcritical as a cut point ensured that the consensus of the items included was not due to chance and that the items included were important to the patient population, quantitatively ensuring content validity. These item-reduction techniques have previously been described, and the participation of patients and experts helped ensure the existence of the proposed underlying theoretical framework with enhanced face validity (Beaton et al., 2005, Bowling, 2014, Oppenheim, 2000).

It is important to note that participants may have difficultly isolating the financial impact attributable solely to OUD treatment, given that OUD frequently co-occurs with other conditions such as alcohol use disorder, major depressive disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, and other medical comorbidities (Hser et al., 2017). The MOUD-FiT items were designed to reference treatment costs and experiences specific to MOUD; however, the extent to which respondents can distinguish OUD-specific financial strain from the cumulative impact of multiple conditions warrants further investigation. Future validation studies should evaluate the discriminant validity of the MOUD-FiT in populations with varying comorbidity profiles.

Importantly, the inclusion of multiple domains (affect, coping, direct costs, and indirect costs) ensures that the MOUD-FiT captures the multidimensional nature of financial impact, encompassing both tangible financial strain and the emotional and behavioral responses that can influence treatment engagement (de Souza et al., 2017). This approach aligns with patient-centered care principles and highlights the complexity of financial stress in OUD treatment, extending beyond what can be measured by structural or administrative indicators alone. The current psychometric evaluation provides evidence for internal consistency but does not establish the factor structure of the instrument. Whether the four domains (affect, coping, direct cost, indirect cost) represent distinct subscales or contribute to a single overall financial toxicity score remains an empirical question to be addressed through confirmatory factor analysis in future studies.

The MOUD-FiT PROM builds on prior work in financial toxicity measurement, most notably instruments developed in oncology, such as the COmprehensive Score for financial Toxicity (COST) PROM, but addresses unique aspects of OUD treatment that are not captured in existing measures (de Souza et al., 2017). Unlike general financial toxicity tools, MOUD-FiT incorporates domains specific to the lived experience of patients receiving MOUD, including indirect costs related to transportation, lost income, and time spent in treatment, as well as affective and coping responses to financial strain (Zhu et al., 2022). Existing OUD research has largely focused on structural barriers, such as insurance coverage or geographic access, without directly capturing patients’ psychosocial experiences of financial impact (Rapp et al., 2006). By systematically incorporating patient perspectives through focus groups and cognitive debriefing, the MOUD-FiT PROM provides a patient-centered tool capable of quantifying both the financial and emotional consequences of treatment costs. This approach enhances the ability of clinicians, researchers, and policymakers to identify individuals at risk of treatment disruption due to financial stress, tailor interventions to reduce barriers, and evaluate the impact of financial support programs on treatment adherence and recovery outcomes. Recent research has also highlighted how ownership structures in substance use disorder treatment may influence the financial landscape for patients; for example, Havlik et al. (2026) found that private equity–acquired residential treatment facilities differed from other for-profit facilities in pricing and characteristics, underscoring the importance of understanding the economic environment in which OUD treatment is delivered and the potential for facility-level financial factors to affect patient-level financial toxicity.

The MOUD-FiT PROM offers a practical, patient-centered tool to assess financial toxicity in individuals receiving MOUD, enabling clinicians to identify patients who may be at risk for treatment disruption due to financial stress. Routine use of the instrument in clinical settings could guide referrals to financial counseling, social services, or medication assistance programs, ultimately supporting treatment retention and recovery. In research, the PROM provides a standardized measure to evaluate interventions aimed at reducing the financial impact of OUD treatment, assess policy impacts, and explore associations between financial toxicity and treatment outcomes. Its multidimensional structure allows for a nuanced understanding of both direct and indirect costs, as well as emotional and coping responses, which may influence adherence and engagement.

Strengths of this study include a rigorous, multi-step, patient-centered development process that incorporated input from literature, patients, and treatment experts, as well as quantitative content validation and psychometric evaluation. The instrument captures multiple domains of financial toxicity relevant to MOUD, providing a comprehensive assessment tool. We characterize the current stage of the MOUD-FiT as instrument development with preliminary psychometric testing. However, tpresent study establishes content validity and internal consistency reliability but does not constitute full validation. Other limitations include the relatively small sample size and limited diversity of participants, which may affect generalizability. Additional forms of validity, including construct, criterion, and predictive validity, remain to be evaluated. Additionally, test-retest reliability was not assessed in this study and should be examined in future work to establish the temporal stability of the instrument. In addition, future studies should evaluate alternative reliability estimates and further assess the structural validity of the MOUD-FiT. Furthermore, Cronbach’s α was used to assess internal consistency; however, it does not provide a direct estimate of scale reliability and may be influenced by the number of items, the presence of multidimensionality, and potential item redundancy (Raykov et al., 2010, Sijtsma, 2009). Accordingly, α should be interpreted with caution, as it may not fully capture the reliability or structural validity of the instrument. Future research should employ confirmatory factor analysis to evaluate the dimensionality of the MOUD-FiT and determine whether it functions as a unidimensional scale or a multidimensional instrument with distinct subscales.

Future research should examine the MOUD-FiT PROM in larger and more diverse populations, assess its responsiveness over time, and explore its predictive utility for treatment engagement, adherence, and recurrence. A major limitation of this study is that all participants were recruited in Houston, Texas, where publicly funded OUD treatment support programs are more accessible. This access to more affordable treatment support could minimize the perspective of the financial impact of OUD treatment. Therefore, results may not be generalized to individuals in treatment for OUD in rural areas or areas with less publicly funded treatment support. The MOUD-FiT is a provisional instrument that requires further validation. Additional research is needed to determine the correlation between financial toxicity and quality of life, treatment retention, and survival in OUD patients. Before its widespread adoption, other forms of validity and psychometric characteristics should be determined, including confirmatory factor analysis to evaluate whether the MOUD-FiT functions as a unidimensional scale or comprises distinct subdomain subscales. In addition, the causality, and hypothesized predictors of financial toxicity, including employment status, out-of-pocket costs, social support, lack of insurance, and financial literacy, should be evaluated in greater detail. Integration into clinical workflows or digital platforms may facilitate routine screening and early identification of patients experiencing financial strain.

Conclusion

The MOUD-FiT PROM fills a critical gap in patient-centered measurement of financial toxicity in OUD care. Preliminary evidence demonstrates that it is a reliable and relevant tool for capturing both the financial and psychosocial impact of treatment, offering potential to inform interventions, support services, and policy decisions aimed at improving treatment access and outcomes.
