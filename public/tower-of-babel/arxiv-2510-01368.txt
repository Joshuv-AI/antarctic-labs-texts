The role of self-adjoint extensions in the bulk-edge correspondence
Johannes Kellendonk, Tom Stoiber

Abstract
We investigate the role of self-adjoint extensions in the bulk-edge correspondence for topological insulators. While the correspondence is well understood in discrete models with spectral gaps, complications arise in the presence of unbounded Hamiltonians and varying boundary conditions, leading to anomalous behavior that has recently been dubbed violations of bulk-edge correspondence. In this work we use a K-theoretic framework to identify precise conditions needed for unbounded Hamiltonians to be affiliated to the respective observable algebras and define K-theory classes. In special cases we can then exclude anomalous behaviour and obtain the standard bulk-edge correspondence, or, under weaker conditions, obtain a relative bulk-edge correspondence theorem, which compares pairs of Hamiltonians. Applying that relative approach in the bulk we recover among other things the so-called bulk-difference-interface correspondence for Hamiltonians that fail to define a bulk K-theory class in the conventional way. The second main result is that one can define K-theory classes in terms of von Neumann unitaries, which under changes in boundary conditions directly contribute to the number of protected edge states. This approach clarifies apparent violations of the classical bulk-edge paradigm and provides a systematic account of boundary-induced topological corrections.

Introduction

The bulk-edge correspondence is an important paradigm in the theory of topological insulators. In this context, infinitely extended boundary-less insulators (bulk systems) are described by Hamiltonians
which have a gap in their spectrum at the Fermi energy. Topological invariants can be associated to these gaps. These so-called bulk invariants are stable under homotopy as long as the gap stays open.
A system with boundary can be seen as a system with an interface with empty space and since empty space is a system with trivial topology the gap of a topologically non-trivial bulk system should “close at the boundary” coinciding with the formation of gap-filling edge modes.

Let us make those notions explicit in the best-understood setting of two-dimensional free Fermi models. There, a bulk topological insulator corresponds to a one-particle Hamiltonian
H
with a gap
\Delta
in its spectrum and such that the Fermi energy lies in that gap.
Its topological class is encoded in its Fermi projection onto the spectral subspace below the gap
\Delta
. Adding a constant to the Hamiltonian if necessary, we may assume that the gap
\Delta
is around zero energy. Then
H
is boundedly invertible. From the Fermi projection
P_{\leq 0}(H)
one computes a quantized bulk invariant, the integer quantum Hall conductance
\sigma_{b}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Truncating
H
to a space with boundary will result in a Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
which may have spectrum in
\Delta
; to detect that one can compute the conductivity
\sigma_{e}
of the edge states in
\Delta
. It turns out that the latter is also quantized
\sigma_{e}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
in proper units.

Let us explain the general principles for models defined by translation invariant differential operators. The involved algebras can be described explicitly as follows.

1.

The bulk algebra

2.

The partial Fourier transform in the first

The half-space algebra

(later also denoted by

We will also look at matrix-valued operators, in which case the above algebras are to be enlarged by taking their tensor product with a matrix algebra.
In Section

A bulk Hamiltonian

As already mentioned, the K-theoretic bulk-edge correspondence need not hold. However,
there is a simple sufficient condition which resolves the bulk-edge correspondence for realistic quantum Hall systems, which are naturally bounded from below. This is Theorem

Proposition 1.4
.

Given an exact sequence (
1.1
). Let
\hat{H}
be a self-adjoint operator affiliated to

More generally, if

is not necessarily bounded from below then the K-theoretic bulk-edge correspondence holds under the strong affiliation condition

F(\hat{H})=\hat{H}(1+\hat{H}^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\in M_{N}(\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}^{\sim})

If

is not bounded from below or otherwise not sufficiently regular (especially if it is non-elliptic), then a bulk-edge correspondence in form of an equality between a bulk invariant and an edge invariant which can be expressed as a spectral flow of edge modes might not exist. This has been most prominently observed in Dirac-type models or shallow-water models

;

;

;

;

;

;

. In essence, the spectral flow of the half-space model can be seen to depend on the choice of self-adjoint boundary condition for

Importantly, this does not depend on how the spectral flow of the edge modes is employed to define the edge invariant. We use a definition of edge invariant which is motivated both by physics (the Hatsugai relation) and K-theory, looking at spectral flow through the gap. Other versions of the edge invariant are defined by counting how many edge modes flow out or into the bulk spectrum. In that case there are models for which the difference between the edge invariant and the bulk invariant is given by the winding number of a certain scattering phase at infinite energy

;
GJT21
;

;

One objective of this work is to derive an analogous correction term for the edge conductivity which seamlessly fits into K-theory, therefore showing robustness under a large class of perturbations, as long as certain affiliation conditions hold.

Since the Hatsugai relation can be violated, the K-theoretic bulk-edge correspondence (

The combination of these two theorems allows us to understand a large part of phenomena which have previously been dubbed somewhat misleadingly violations of bulk-edge correspondence. Another powerful application is bulk-interface correspondence, which is algebraically set up very similarly by an exact sequence which relates two asymptotic bulk operators on either side of a boundary hypersurface to an interface Hamiltonian. 2.1
Affiliation of unbounded operators

Let

{\mathcal{H}}
. We simply write this as
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
. One can identify
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
with the multiplier algebra of the compact operators
\mathbb{K}({\mathcal{H}})
in such a way that Proposition
2.2
applied to the inclusion map guarantees that the image of any affiliated operator corresponds canonically to an operator affiliated to
\mathbb{K}({\mathcal{H}})
. The latter are precisely the closed densely defined operators on
{\mathcal{H}}
. Under this correspondence the bounded affiliated operators, i.e. the elements of the multiplier algebra
{\mathcal{M}}({\mathcal{A}})
, are mapped bijectively to the set of all operators
T\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
for which
Ta
and
aT
belong to
{\mathcal{A}}
for all
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
.

Characterizing the unbounded affiliated operators is more subtle. It is well-known that a multiplier

is the bounded transform

of a (unique) regular affiliated operator
T
if and only
\left\lVert z\right\rVert\leq 1
and

is norm-dense
(

, Theorem 10.4)
. One can rewrite those conditions in terms of

to give a criterion for affiliation:

Lemma 2.4

(
W90
:Example 4)
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
. The operators affiliated to
{\mathcal{A}}
are in one-to-one correspondence with the closed operators
T
on
{\mathcal{H}}
, acting on
{\mathcal{A}}
by left multiplication, for which
F(T)\in\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{A}})
and
{(1+T^{*}T)^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathcal{A}}}
is norm-dense in
{\mathcal{A}}
.

A closed operator on
{\mathcal{H}}
which satisfies this condition will be called an
{\mathcal{A}}
-multiplier. If

is an
{\mathcal{A}}
-multiplier then its adjoint

is also an
{\mathcal{A}}
-multiplier and

is compatible with the usual Hilbert space adjoint.

Let

be a closed operator on
{\mathcal{H}}
and
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
.
We may always define its maximal domain as an operator on
{\mathcal{A}}
, namely the set

consisting of all

for which

and

.

If

is an

-multiplier then

We can now reformulate the density condition of Lemma
2.4
:

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
T
be a closed densely defined operator on
{\mathcal{H}}
. Let
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{H}}
and suppose that
F(T)\in\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{A}})
. The following properties are equivalent:

(i)

(1+T^{*}T)^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathcal{A}}
is dense in
{\mathcal{A}}
.

(ii)

(1+T^{*}T)^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}
is dense in
{\mathcal{A}}
.

(iii)

{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{A}}(1+T^{*}T)
is dense in
{\mathcal{A}}
.

Proof.

First we note that if
F(T)\in\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{A}})
then
(1+T^{*}T)^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{A}}
as
(1+T^{*}T)^{-\frac{1}{2}}=(1-F(T^{*})F(T))^{\frac{1}{2}}\in\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{A}})
. Then
(1+T^{*}T)^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathcal{A}}
is dense in
{\mathcal{A}}
if and only if
(1+T^{*}T)^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}
is dense in
{\mathcal{A}}
. The norm-density of
(1+T^{*}T)^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}
and
(1+T^{*}T)^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathcal{A}}
in
{\mathcal{A}}
is equivalent, since on the one hand
(1+T^{*}T)^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathcal{A}}\subset(1+T^{*}T)^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}
and on the other
(1+T^{*}T)^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}=(1+T^{*}T)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1+T^{*}T)^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathcal{A}}
is the image of a dense subset under a bounded operator with dense range. This shows
(i)\iff(ii)
.

The implication
(i)\implies(iii)
holds since
F(T)\in\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{A}})
implies that
(1+T^{*}T)^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{A}}(1+T^{*}T)
.

For the implication
(iii)\implies(ii)
, since
1+T^{*}T
is a self-adjoint operator on
{\mathcal{H}}
with a bounded inverse, one can write

a=(1+T^{*}T)^{-1}(1+T^{*}T)a

for each
a\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{A}}(1+T^{*}T)
, thus
(1+T^{*}T)^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}
contains the norm-dense set
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{A}}(1+T^{*}T)
.
∎

The reformulation (iii) of the density condition is convenient if one wants to show that a concrete operator is an

-multiplier since one needs to consider neither inverses nor fractional powers. For example, if

is a differential operator then

is again a differential operator which may map a class of smoothing elements of

to itself; in contrast the inverse

can be rather inaccessible.

Let

Proof.

(i)
\implies

.

For (ii)
\implies

We will also want to study stability of
{\mathcal{B}}
-affiliation under perturbations:

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
H
be a self-adjoint
{\mathcal{A}}
-multiplier,
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
, and
V
a closed symmetric operator on
{\mathcal{H}}
whose domain contains
\mathrm{Dom}(H)
.

(i)

If

satisfies the Kato-Rellich bound
\left\lVert V\psi\right\rVert\leq\alpha\left\lVert H\psi\right\rVert+\beta\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert
with some
\alpha<1
for all
\psi\in\mathrm{Dom}(H)
and

V(H+\imath)^{-1}\in\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{A}})

then

is also a self-adjoint
{\mathcal{A}}
-multiplier on the same domain as
H
.

(ii)

Let
{\mathcal{I}}
be a norm-closed ideal of
{\mathcal{A}}
. If

V(H+\imath)^{-1}\in{\mathcal{I}}

then (i) holds and further

F(H+V)-F(H)\in{\mathcal{I}}.

Proof.
(i) By the Kato-Rellich theorem (which is more generally also true in the Hilbert module context

) the sum is a self-adjoint operator with the same domain as
H
and for every
\lambda\neq 0
one has

\displaystyle(H\pm\lambda\imath)^{-1}-(H+V\pm\lambda\imath)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(H+V\pm\lambda\imath)^{-1}V(H\pm\lambda\imath)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(H\pm\lambda\imath)^{-1}V(H+V\pm\lambda\imath)^{-1}.

Iterating this resolvent identity for large enough

gives a norm-convergent series expansion from which one can conclude that the right-hand side is in
\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{A}})
and also
(H+V\pm\lambda\imath)^{-1}({\mathcal{A}})\subset(H\pm\lambda\imath)^{-1}({\mathcal{A}})
. Lemma

(ii) therefore shows that
H+V
is an
{\mathcal{A}}
-multiplier if
H
is an
{\mathcal{A}}
-multiplier.

(ii) For bounded

it is easy to show that the difference of bounded transforms lies in
{\mathcal{I}}
, e.g. by
(
CP98
, Lemma 2.7(i))
which gives a norm-convergent integral formula for the difference of bounded transforms with an integrand that lies in
{\mathcal{I}}
under the given condition.

For the case of relatively bounded
V
note first that the Kato-Rellich bound holds with
\alpha<1
since

\left\lVert V\psi\right\rVert=\left\lVert V(H+\lambda\imath)^{-1}(H+\lambda\imath)\psi\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert V(H+\lambda\imath)^{-1}\right\rVert(\left\lVert H\psi\right\rVert+\lambda\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert)

for any
\lambda\neq 0
and one has operator-norm-convergence

V(H+\lambda\imath)^{-1}=V(H+\imath)^{-1}(H+\imath)(H+\lambda\imath)^{-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda\to\infty}}{{\to}}0

due to strict convergence of the functional calculus

to

in

(see Proposition

).

One can then reduce to the bounded case using continuity of the bounded transforms w.r.t. to graph norms

by the bounded operators

for

one can show that

\Box

For

we will call perturbations

2.3
Self-adjoint extensions

To implement and compare different boundary conditions of a differential operator on a manifold with a boundary one usually starts with a symmetric operator and considers its self-adjoint extensions. An important question in our algebraic formalism is to characterize those extensions of the symmetric operator which are affiliated to a relevant algebra. If the symmetric operator is already affiliated then this can be studied by means of von Neumann’s theory of self-adjoint extensions adapted to the Hilbert module case (where the Hilbert module is just the
C^{*}
-algebra itself)
WK92
;
Lance
. Recall that, if
\mathring{H}
is a closed symmetric operator on
{\mathcal{H}}
whose deficiency spaces
\ker(\mathring{H}^{*}-i)
and
\ker(\mathring{H}^{*}+i)
have equal dimension, then the self-adjoint extensions of
\mathring{H}
are classified by the partial isometries
u:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}
whose domain projections
u^{*}u
coincide with the orthogonal projection
e_{-}
onto
\ker(\mathring{H}^{*}-i)
while their range projections
uu^{*}
coincide with the orthogonal projection
e_{+}
onto
\ker(\mathring{H}^{*}+i)
.

The corresponding self-adjoint extension
H_{u}
is uniquely defined through its Cayley transform which has the form

{\mathcal{C}}(H_{u})=(H_{u}-\imath)(H_{u}+\imath)^{-1}:={\mathcal{C}}(\mathring{H})+u

Here
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathring{H})=(\mathring{H}-\imath)(\mathring{H}+\imath)^{-1}
is a partial isometry from
\ker(\mathring{H}^{*}-\imath)^{\perp}
to
\ker(\mathring{H}^{*}+\imath)^{\perp}
. The correspondence between such partial isometries
u
and extensions
{H}_{u}
is one-to-one with the domain of the extensions

\mathrm{Dom}({H}_{u})=\{\psi+\psi_{+}+u\psi_{+}:\,\psi\in\mathrm{Dom}(\mathring{H}),\psi_{+}\in{\rm Ker}(\mathring{H}^{*}-\imath)\}.

It is customary to call the partial isometry
u
associated to a self-adjoint extension the
von Neumann unitary
, since

In the most common formulation of operator

-theory, the group

for a unital
C^{*}
-algebra

is defined as the Grothendieck group
of the semigroup of stable homotopy classes of projections in

with addition induced by the direct sum. Here
M_{n}({\mathcal{A}})
is included into
M_{n+m}({\mathcal{A}})
as the upper left block in a block diagonal matrix which has the zero matrix
0_{m}
in the lower right block. Similarily
K_{1}({\mathcal{A}})
is the group of homotopy classes of unitaries in
M_{n}({\mathcal{A}})
. Now

U_{n}({\mathcal{A}})
is included into
U_{n+m}({\mathcal{A}})
as an upper left block in a block diagonal matrix which has the identity matrix
{\bf 1}_{m}
in the lower right block.
Homotopy classes of unitaries form a group under the operation of direct sum and the direct sum of two unitaries is stably homotopic to their product.
Any unital homomorphism
\phi:{\mathcal{A}}\to{\mathcal{B}}
between two unital
C^{*}
-algebras induces a group homomorphism
\phi_{*}:K_{i}({\mathcal{A}})\to K_{i}({\mathcal{B}})
by
\phi_{*}([x]_{i})=[\phi(x)]_{i}
.

If
{\mathcal{A}}
is non-unital its

-groups are defined via the homomorphism

induced by the scalar map

,

, namely

. Every class in

can be represented by a formal difference
[p]_{0}-[q]_{0}
of a pair of projections
p,q\in M_{N}({\mathcal{A}}^{\sim})

with
s(p)=s(q)
. Every class
[u]_{1}
in
K_{1}({\mathcal{A}})
by a unitary
u\in M_{N}({\mathcal{A}}^{\sim})
with

. In that case we say that we have representatives in the standard picture.

Below we will use another picture for the elements of the
K
-group of a non-unital algebra. Let
{\mathcal{B}}
be a unital
C^{*}
-algebra which contains
{\mathcal{A}}
as an ideal and consider the

-algebra of pairs

Then we have a split exact sequence of algebras

Let

{\mathcal{E}}

\partial_{{\mathbb{P}}}([(p_{+},p_{-})]_{0}+t_{*}(K_{0}({\mathcal{B}})))=([e^{-\imath 2\pi\hat{p}_{+}},e^{-\imath 2\pi\hat{p}_{-}}]_{1}+\partial_{{\mathbb{P}}}(t_{*}(K_{0}({\mathcal{B}}))).

Since
t_{*}(K_{0}({\mathcal{B}}))
is represented by diagonal elements and the boundary map preserves the diagonal form it is clear that
\partial_{{\mathbb{P}}}(t_{*}(K_{0}({\mathcal{B}})))\subset t_{*}(K_{1}(\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathcal{E}}))
, hence (
3.5
) is compatible with the quotients.
∎

With this formulation of
K
-theory in mind we now define
K
-theory classes for pairs of Hamiltonians. We start with the invertible (gapped) the case:

Definition 3.11
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a (non-unital)
C^{*}
-algebra and
{\mathcal{B}}
a unital sub-algebra of
\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{A}})
containing
{\mathcal{A}}
as an ideal. Let
H,H^{\prime}
be self-adjoint invertible
and
M_{N}({\mathcal{A}})
-comparable
M_{N}({\mathcal{A}})
-multipliers whose bounded transforms lie in
M_{N}({\mathcal{B}})
. We define

[H,H^{\prime}]_{0}:=[P_{\leq 0}(H),\,P_{\leq 0}(H^{\prime})]_{0}\in K_{0}({\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{B}},{\mathcal{A}}))/t_{*}K_{0}({\mathcal{B}}).

In this notation
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are left implicit, but since we specify
H
as an
M_{N}({\mathcal{A}})
-multiplier and the group on the right hand side does not depend on
{\mathcal{B}}
up to a canonical isomorphism this will not pose a problem. In any case, if
{\mathcal{B}}
is not specified then we take
{\mathcal{B}}=\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{A}})
. Note that, if
H,H^{\prime}
both are strongly affiliated to
{\mathcal{A}}
then we may take
{\mathcal{B}}={\mathcal{A}}^{\sim}
and get

[H,H^{\prime}]_{0}=\tilde{\imath}_{*}([H]_{0})-\tilde{\imath}_{*}([H^{\prime}]_{0}).

For models with boundary we are interested in
K
-theoretic obstructions to gap opening for pairs of Hamiltonians.

Definition 3.12
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a
C^{*}
-algebra and
{\mathcal{I}}
an ideal in
{\mathcal{A}}
.
Let
H,H^{\prime}
be two self-adjoint
M_{N}({\mathcal{A}})
-multipliers which are locally
{\mathcal{I}}
-comparable and such that their bounded transform belongs to
M_{N}({\mathcal{B}})
. Then
H,H^{\prime}
define a class in
K_{1}({\mathcal{I}})
, namely

[H,H^{\prime}]^{\mathcal{I}}_{1}=[e^{\pi\imath\Theta(H)},e^{\pi\imath\Theta(H^{\prime})}]_{1}.

Here
\Theta
is a normalizing function
\Theta
whose derivative
\Theta^{\prime}
is supported in an interval
\Delta
as required by Definition
2.13
. If the ideal
{\mathcal{I}}
is clear from context we will more simply denote
[H,H^{\prime}]_{1}=[H,H^{\prime}]^{\mathcal{I}}_{1}
.

We note that
[H,H^{\prime}]_{1}
can also be written
[e^{\pi\imath\Theta(H)}e^{-\pi\imath\Theta(H^{\prime})},1]_{1}
and then directly viewed as an element of
K_{1}({\mathcal{I}})
as we explained above.
In nice cases we have a more concrete description of this relative invariant. Recall that
{\mathcal{C}}(H)=(H-\imath)(H+\imath)^{-1}
(Cayley transform).

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
H,H^{\prime}
be as in Definition
3.12
. We have

(i)

The class
[H,H^{\prime}]_{1}
is well-defined and independent of
\Theta
.

(ii)

If
H
,
H^{\prime}
are invertible modulo
{\mathcal{I}}
then

[H,H^{\prime}]_{1}=[H]_{1}-[H^{\prime}]_{1}.

(iii)

If
H
,
H^{\prime}
are
{\mathcal{I}}
-resolvent comparable then

[H,H^{\prime}]_{1}=[{\mathcal{C}}(H),{\mathcal{C}}(H^{\prime})]_{1}=[f(H),\overline{f}(H^{\prime})]_{1}

for any function
f\in C({\mathbb{R}})
of modulus
1
which tends to
1
at
\pm\infty
and has winding number one.

Proof.

(i) We have
x\mapsto e^{\imath\pi\Theta(x)}-e^{\imath\pi\Theta(x)}\in C_{0}(\Delta)
as
the derivative of
\Theta
is supported in
\Delta
. Hence
e^{\imath\pi\Theta(H)}-e^{\imath\pi\Theta(H^{\prime})}\in I
. Therefore
[e^{\imath\pi\Theta(H)},e^{\imath\pi\Theta(H^{\prime})}]_{1}
is well-defined and its independence of the choice of
\Theta
follows by the same homotopy argument as in Proposition
2.18
.

(ii) If
H
and
H^{\prime}
are invertible modulo
{\mathcal{I}}
then
x\mapsto e^{\imath\pi\Theta(x)}-1
and
x\mapsto e^{\imath\pi\Theta(x)}-1
belong to
C_{0}(\Delta)
and the classes
[(e^{2\pi\imath\Theta(H)},1)]_{1}
and
[(1,e^{2\pi\imath\Theta(H^{\prime})})]_{1}
are well defined. The result follows from

[e^{2\pi\imath\Theta(H)},e^{2\pi\imath\Theta(H^{\prime})}]_{1}=[e^{2\pi\imath\Theta(H)},1]_{1}+[1,e^{2\pi\imath\Theta(H^{\prime})}]_{1}

and anti symmetry
[1,e^{2\pi\imath\Theta(H^{\prime})}]_{1}-[e^{2\pi\imath\Theta(H^{\prime})},1]_{1}
.

(iii) If
H
and
H^{\prime}
are
{\mathcal{I}}
-resolvent comparable then

f(H)-f(H^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{I}}
for all

f\in C({\mathbb{R}})
with
\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)=1
. Therefore

[e^{\pi\imath\Theta(H)},e^{\pi\imath\Theta(H^{\prime})}]_{1}
is well-defined even without the condition that the support of the derivative of
\Theta
lies in
\Delta
. By the same homotopy argument as in (ii), it is independent of the choice of normalizing function. The function entering the Cayley transform is of that form,
{\mathcal{C}}(x)=\frac{x-i}{x+i}=-e^{\pi\imath\Theta(x)}
for some normalizing function
\Theta
. Moreover, again the same type of homotopy argument shows that we can replace
\Theta
by any continuous function tending to
\pm 1
at
\pm\infty
still obtaining the same homotopy class.
∎

3.3
Relative bulk-boundary correspondence

In this section we formulate the abstract algebraic version of bulk-edge correspondence for comparable pairs of Hamiltonians. It is also based on the short exact sequence (
3.3
) describing edge, half-space and bulk of a material.

Proposition 3.14
.

Consider the short exact sequence (
3.3
).
Let
\hat{H},\hat{H}^{\prime}
be two self-adjoint
\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}
-multipliers which are
\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}
-comparable and for which the self-adjoint
{\mathcal{A}}
-multipliers
q(\hat{H})
and
q(\hat{H}^{\prime})
are invertible.

Then
\hat{H}
,
\hat{H}^{\prime}
are locally
{\mathcal{E}}
-comparable and hence the class
[\hat{H},\hat{H}^{\prime}]_{1}\in K_{1}({\mathcal{E}})
is well-defined.

Proof.

As
q(\hat{H})
and
q(\hat{H}^{\prime})
are invertible their resolvent sets contain an open interval
\Delta
around
0
. As
\hat{H},\hat{H}^{\prime}
are
\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}
-comparable there is a normalizing function
\Theta
such that

\Theta(\hat{H})-\Theta(\hat{H}^{\prime})\in\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}
. Hence also

f(\hat{H})-f(\hat{H}^{\prime})\in\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}
for any function
f
of the form
f=G\circ\Theta
with
G\in C_{0}({\mathbb{R}})
. Since
\Theta^{\prime}(0)>0
any function compactly supported in a small enough interval
\Delta^{\prime}
around
0
can be written in that form. We may suppose
\Delta^{\prime}\subset\Delta
. Then any
f\in C_{c}(\Delta^{\prime})
satisfies

q\left(f(\hat{H})-f(\hat{H}^{\prime})\right)=q\left(f(\hat{H})\right)-q\left(f(\hat{H}^{\prime})\right)=0

which implies that
f(\hat{H})-f(\hat{H}^{\prime})
lies in the ideal
{\mathcal{E}}
.
∎

Theorem 3.15

(Relative bulk-edge correspondence)
.

Consider the short exact sequence (
3.3
).
Let
\hat{H},\hat{H}^{\prime}
be two self-adjoint
\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}
-multipliers which are
\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}
-comparable and for which the self-adjoint
{\mathcal{A}}
-multipliers
H:=q(\hat{H})
and
H^{\prime}=q(\hat{H}^{\prime})
are invertible.

Then

[\hat{H},\hat{H}^{\prime}]_{1}=\partial[{H},{H}^{\prime}]_{0}.

Here,
[{H},{H}^{\prime}]_{0}
is an element of
K_{0}({\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{A}}),{\mathcal{A}}))/t_{*}(K_{0}(\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{A}})))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tilde{\imath}_{*}^{-1}}}{{\simeq}}K_{0}({\mathcal{A}}))
and
[\hat{H},\hat{H}^{\prime}]_{1}
an element of
K_{1}({\mathcal{E}})
.

Proof.

By Propositions
3.14

[\hat{H},\hat{H}^{\prime}]_{1}
is a well-defined element of
K_{1}({\mathcal{E}})
. The statement follows therefore from Proposition
3.10
and the definition of the relative classes.
∎

The above immediately also applies to matrix valued operators, by just replacing
{\mathcal{A}}
with
M_{N}({\mathcal{A}})
etc. which has isomorphic K-groups. In contrast, for the notion of strong affiliation we needed to take the matrix size into account explicitly, as unitization does not commute with tensor products. If the bounded transforms of two matrix valued
M_{N}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})
-comparable operators
H
and
H^{\prime}
take values in
M_{N}({\mathcal{B}})

for some smaller unital algebra
{\mathcal{B}}
containing
{\mathcal{A}}

then
[{H},{H}^{\prime}]_{0}
can be represented by an element in
K_{0}({\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{B}},{\mathcal{A}}))
.
In particular, for strong affiliation
{\mathcal{B}}={\mathcal{A}}^{\sim}
one always has
[H,H^{\prime}]_{0}=[H]_{0}-[H^{\prime}]_{0}
as a difference of two classes in the standard picture of
K_{0}({\mathcal{A}})
. Note in particular that if we choose
\hat{H}^{\prime}
to be a scalar matrix as in Lemma
3.9
then we recover Theorem
3.7
as a special case of Theorem
3.15
.

Remark 3.16
.

The condition of
\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}
-comparability is much more general than strong affiliation, but can still be rather subtle since functional calculus with functions from
C([-\infty,+\infty])
can be difficult to control. The most important sufficient condition is relative compactness, namely the sufficient condition of Proposition
2.10
, which for relatively bounded perturbations
\hat{V}:=\hat{H}-\hat{H}^{\prime}
reads

\hat{V}(\hat{H}+\imath)^{-1}\in\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}.

(3.6)

Note that this condition can only hold if
\hat{H}
and
\hat{H}^{\prime}
have the same domain, whereas generally self-adjoint operators can be
\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}
-comparable without having equal domains.

3.4
K-theoretic comparison of self-adjoint extensions

We now consider the relative invariants
[\hat{H},\hat{H}^{\prime}]_{1}
defined by self-adjoint extensions
\hat{H}
,
\hat{H}^{\prime}
of a common symmetric operator. As will be seen in the examples,
\hat{H}
and
\hat{H}^{\prime}
can have different edge invariants, i.e. a non-trivial relative class
[\hat{H},\hat{H}^{\prime}]_{1}
while being asymptotically equal, i.e.
q(\hat{H})=q(\hat{H^{\prime}})
. They must then fail the conditions of Theorem
3.15
. It will usually be the
\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}
-comparability which fails when we compare different self-adjoint extensions.

Remark 3.17
.

In general
(\hat{H}+\imath)^{-1}-(\hat{H}^{\prime}+\imath)^{-1}\in M_{N}({\mathcal{A}}^{\sim})
does not imply
F(\hat{H})-F(\hat{H}^{\prime})\in M_{N}({\mathcal{A}}^{\sim})
since the difference of the bounded transforms
F(\hat{H})-F(\hat{H}^{\prime})
cannot generally be expanded as a norm-convergent integral formula in terms of resolvent differences (as can also be seen by the fact that the Riesz-topology is finer than the gap-topology for unbounded operators, see e.g.
Lesch05
). Indeed, we will see many examples of
\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}
-resolvent comparable self-adjoint extensions which are not
\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}
-comparable.

If the relative class
[\hat{H},\hat{H}^{\prime}]_{1}
comes from different self-adjoint extensions of a single symmetric operator it can be connected more transparently to the data which define the self-adjoint extension:

Proposition 3.18
.

Let
\mathring{H}
be a symmetric
{\mathcal{E}}
-multiplier and let
\hat{H}_{u}
,
\hat{H}_{v}
be self-adjoint extensions determined by partial isometries
u,v\in\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{E}})
. Then the following conditions are equivalent

(i)

{\mathcal{C}}(\hat{H}_{u})-{\mathcal{C}}(\hat{H}_{v})\in{\mathcal{E}}

(ii)

(\hat{H}_{u}+\imath)^{-1}-(\hat{H}_{v}+\imath)^{-1}\in{\mathcal{E}}

(iii)

u-v\in{\mathcal{E}}
.

If either holds one has

[\hat{H}_{u},\hat{H}_{v}]_{1}=[1+uv^{*}-e_{+}]_{1}\in K_{1}({\mathcal{E}})

with the projection
e_{+}=uu^{*}=vv^{*}
onto the deficiency subspace
{\rm Ker}(\mathring{H}^{*}+\imath)
.

Proof.
All conditions are equivalent to
\hat{H}_{u}
and
\hat{H}_{v}
being
{\mathcal{E}}
-resolvent comparable and then the equivalence follows from Lemma
3.13
and Proposition
2.20
.

\Box

If one can compute deficiency subspaces and the corresponding von Neumann unitaries of self-adjoint extensions then this gives a rather explicit formula for the relative invariant.

In practice one often would like to simplify those computations by adding or dropping some symmetric relatively bounded terms
\mathring{H}^{\prime}=\mathring{H}+V
, which are known not to affect the domains of their self-adjoint extensions by the Kato-Rellich theorem.

Proposition 3.19
.

Let
\mathring{H}
be a symmetric
{\mathcal{E}}
-multiplier with two self-adjoint extensions
\mathring{H}_{u},\mathring{H}_{v}
corresponding to von Neumann unitaries
u,v\in\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{E}})
with
u-v\in{\mathcal{E}}
. If
V
is a symmetric
{\mathcal{E}}
-multiplier on
\mathrm{Dom}(\mathring{H}_{u})\cup\mathrm{Dom}(\mathring{H}_{v})
whose restrictions to
\mathrm{Dom}(\mathring{H}_{u})
and
\mathrm{Dom}(\mathring{H}_{v})
both satisfy the conditions of the Kato-Rellich theorem for
\mathring{H}_{u}
and
\mathring{H}_{v}
respectively, then the self-adjoint multipliers
\mathring{H}_{u}+V
and
\mathring{H}_{v}+V
are
{\mathcal{E}}
-resolvent comparable and

[{\mathcal{C}}(\mathring{H}_{u}){\mathcal{C}}(\mathring{H}_{v})^{*}]_{1}=[{\mathcal{C}}(\mathring{H}_{u}+V){\mathcal{C}}(\mathring{H}_{v}+V)^{*}]_{1}\quad\in K_{1}({\mathcal{E}}).

Proof.

The Kato-Rellich bound means that for
\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}
large enough one has

\left\lVert V(\mathring{H}_{u}+\imath\mu)^{-1}\right\rVert<1,\quad\left\lVert V(\mathring{H}_{v}+\imath\mu)^{-1}\right\rVert<1.

Thus one can expand into a norm-convergent sum

(\mathring{H}_{u}+tV+\imath\mu)^{-1}=(\mathring{H}_{u}+\imath\mu)^{-1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}(-V(\mathring{H}_{u}+\imath\mu)^{-1})^{n}

and similarly for
\mathring{H}_{v}+tV
. The term-wise differences

(\mathring{H}_{u}+\imath\mu)^{-1}(-V(\mathring{H}_{u}+\imath\mu)^{-1})^{n}-(\mathring{H}_{v}+\imath\mu)^{-1}(-V(\mathring{H}_{v}+\imath\mu)^{-1})^{n}

lie in
{\mathcal{E}}
since
(\mathring{H}_{u}+\imath\mu)^{-1}-(\mathring{H}_{v}+\imath\mu)^{-1}\in{\mathcal{E}}
by assumption and all other factors are bounded
{\mathcal{E}}
-multipliers. The usual resolvent identities then allow us to analytically continue to obtain

(\mathring{H}_{u}+tV+\imath)^{-1}-(\mathring{H}_{v}+tV+\imath)^{-1}\in{\mathcal{E}}

which implies the same for the difference of the Cayley transforms.

To prove that the
K
-theory classes coincide it is then sufficient to note that
t\in[0,1]\mapsto\mathring{H}_{u}+tV,\mathring{H}_{v}+tV
leads to norm-resolvent continuous paths of
{\mathcal{E}}
-resolvent comparable operators, hence

[\hat{H}_{u},\hat{H}_{v}]_{1}=[{\mathcal{C}}(\hat{H}_{u}),{\mathcal{C}}(\hat{H}_{v})]_{1}=[{\mathcal{C}}(\hat{H}_{u}+V),{\mathcal{C}}(\hat{H}_{v}+V)]_{1}

by norm-continuous homotopy in
{\mathbb{P}}(M({\mathcal{E}}),{\mathcal{E}})
.

\Box

In particular, for bounded
V=V^{*}\in M({\mathcal{E}})
the extensions of
\mathring{H}^{\prime}
and
\mathring{H}
are in one-to-one correspondence:

Proposition 3.20
.

Let
\mathring{H}
be a symmetric
{\mathcal{E}}
-multiplier and
\mathring{H}^{\prime}=\mathring{H}+V
with
V
a bounded self-adjoint
{\mathcal{E}}
-multiplier. If
\mathring{H}_{u}
is a self-adjoint extension of
\mathring{H}
(as an
{\mathcal{E}}
-multiplier) then there exists a unique extension
(\mathring{H}^{\prime})_{\tilde{u}}
with the same domain. If two extensions of
\mathring{H}_{u},\mathring{H}_{v}
satisfy
u-v\in{\mathcal{E}}
then the corresponding extensions satisfy
\tilde{u}-\tilde{v}\in{\mathcal{E}}
and

\begin{split}[{\mathcal{C}}(\mathring{H}_{u}){\mathcal{C}}(\mathring{H}_{v})^{*}]_{1}&=[1+uv^{*}-uu^{*}]_{1}\\
&=[1+\tilde{u}\tilde{v}^{*}-\tilde{u}\tilde{u}^{*}]_{1}=[{\mathcal{C}}(\mathring{H}^{\prime}_{\tilde{u}}){\mathcal{C}}(\mathring{H}^{\prime}_{\tilde{v}})^{*}]_{1}\in K_{1}({\mathcal{E}}).\end{split}

(3.7)

Proof.

Since
\mathring{H}_{u}+V
is a self-adjoint extension of
\mathring{H}^{\prime}
there must be a corresponding von Neumann unitary
\tilde{u}
and by exchanging the roles of
\mathring{H}
and
\mathring{H}^{\prime}
this is a one-to-one correspondence. By the previous Lemma, given two such pairs of extensions
(u,v)
respectively
(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})
one has

[{\mathcal{C}}(\mathring{H}_{u}){\mathcal{C}}(\mathring{H}_{v})^{*}]_{1}=[{\mathcal{C}}(\mathring{H}_{u}+V){\mathcal{C}}(\mathring{H}_{v}+V)^{*}]_{1}=[{\mathcal{C}}(\mathring{H}^{\prime}_{\tilde{u}}){\mathcal{C}}(\mathring{H}^{\prime}_{\tilde{v}})^{*}]_{1},

which completes the proof upon rewriting the K-theory classes in terms of the von Neumann unitaries.

\Box

Note that the version for unbounded perturbations
V
cannot hold in the same generality, since there can be extensions
\mathring{H}_{u}
on whose domain
V
is not even symmetric, hence one clearly cannot always define a self-adjoint extension of
\mathring{H}^{\prime}
via
\mathring{H}_{u}+V
.

4
Applications to differential operators

In the introduction we gave a working model for the algebras and the exact sequence they form which works for translational invariant differential operators. In this simple situation it was possible to directly define the algebras as norm-closed subalgebras of the bounded operators on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
or
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+})
. For aperiodic models which,
by general principles, are studied through covariant families of operators, the situation is more complicated and the standard approach is to study them with the help of crossed product
C^{*}
-algebras.

We recall the definition of a twisted crossed product of a twisted
C^{*}
-dynamical system
({\mathcal{C}},{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\alpha,\gamma)
.
Here
{\mathcal{C}}
is a
C^{*}
-algebra which carries a strongly continuous
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-action
\alpha
and
\gamma:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to S^{1}
a continuous
2
-cocycle.

Let
\rho
be a representation of
{\mathcal{C}}
on some Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
. It gives rise to a covariant representation
(\pi_{\rho},U)
of the pair
({\mathcal{C}},{\mathbb{R}}^{d})

on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathcal{H}})
:

\begin{split}(\pi_{\rho}(c)\psi)(x)&=\rho(\alpha_{-x}(c))\psi(x),\quad c\in{\mathcal{C}}\\
(U(t)\psi)(x)&=\gamma(-t,x)\psi(x-t),\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.\end{split}

(4.1)

If
\rho
is faithful and non-degenerate then the twisted crossed product

{\mathcal{C}}\rtimes_{\alpha,\gamma}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is isomorphic to the norm closed sub-algebra of
{\mathcal{B}}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathcal{H}}))
generated be elements of the form

\tilde{\pi}_{\rho}(f):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\pi_{\rho}(f(t))U(t)dt

(4.2)

where
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathcal{C}})
. Equivalently,
\tilde{\pi}_{\rho}
can be understood as a faithful representation of the Banach algebra
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathcal{C}})
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathcal{H}})
equipped with the
\gamma
-twisted convolution product and the twisted crossed product
C^{*}
-algebra is its completion.

Identifying the twisted crossed product with such a faithful representation
\tilde{\pi}
(we suppress the dependence on
\rho
) we have another description of its elements.
Denote by
p_{1},...,p_{d}
the unbounded self-adjoint generators of the one-parameter groups of unitaries
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\mapsto U(\lambda e_{i})
,
i=1,...,d
. Then the elements of the form

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tilde{\pi}(c_{j})f_{j,1}(p_{1})\cdots f_{j,d}(p_{d})

(4.3)

with
c_{j}\in{\mathcal{C}}
and
f_{j,i}\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}})
yield a norm-dense subset of the crossed product algebra. This follows from the density of
\tilde{\pi}(L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathcal{C}}))
as the pre-image of operators of this form under (
4.2
) contains a total subset of
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathcal{C}})
. Note, however, that the form of (
4.3
) is not preserved under products or adjoints, hence they form has a dense linear subspace but not a subalgebra.

4.1
Bulk algebras for differential operators on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

In this section we investigate how covariant families of magnetic Schrödinger operators on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

are affiliated to twisted crossed product algebras. The reader should have in mind operators of the form

H=D_{A}+V

where
D_{A}
is a magnetic elliptic differential operator, i.e. a non-commutative polynomial in the magnetic derivatives
\nabla_{A}
, whose components we write as
\nabla_{i,A}:=\partial_{i}+\imath A_{i}
, and
V
is a covariant potential. Here

A
is the vector potential (in the symmetric gauge)

A_{i}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}{\bf B}_{ik}x_{k}

of the magnetic field which is defined by a constant antisymmetric matrix
{\bf B}\in M_{d}({\mathbb{R}})
.
In applications one is also interested in the case where
D
and
V
are matrix valued, but this is a simple modification, essentially obtained by tensoring an internal Hilbert space
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
onto
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
onto the algebra.

The magnetic derivatives generate a unitary projective representation

t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\mapsto U_{A}(t):=e^{-\nabla_{A}\cdot t}=e^{-\frac{\imath}{2}\langle t,{\bf B}X\rangle}U_{0}(t)

(4.4)

where
X
is the vector-valued position operator on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\mapsto U_{0}(t)
are the usual translations
\psi\mapsto\psi(\cdot-t)
. They satisfy the twisted multiplication rule

U_{A}(t+s)=e^{\frac{\imath}{2}\langle t,{\bf B}s\rangle}U_{A}(s)U_{A}(t),\qquad\forall t,s\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Algebraically this is encoded by the twisting
2
-cocycle

\gamma:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to S^{1},\quad\gamma(t,s)=e^{\frac{\imath}{2}\langle t,{\bf B}s\rangle}.

(4.5)

Other gauge choices for
A
are also possible, e.g. the Landau gauge, and correspond to cohomologous cocycles, thus isomorphic crossed product algebras.

To describe covariant potentials one needs to fix a locally compact Hausdorff space
\Omega
, which physically encodes the space of configurations, and which carries a continuous action of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, denoted
\omega\mapsto t\triangleright\omega
, and an invariant locally finite Borel measure
{\mathbb{P}}
with full support. There is an induced strongly continuous action

\alpha:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times C_{0}(\Omega)\to C_{0}(\Omega),\quad\alpha_{t}(f)(\omega)=f((-t)\triangleright\omega)

(4.6)

which extends to a strictly continuous action on the multiplier algebra
C_{b}(\Omega)
by the same formula.
A family of potentials
(V_{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega}\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{\times\Omega}
is called a covariant family if

V_{\omega}(x)=f(x\triangleright\omega)

for some function
f\in C_{b}(\Omega)
. Then
H=(H_{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega}
,
H_{\omega}=D_{A}+V_{\omega}
is an example of a covariant family of magnetic Schrödinger operators.

Consider the evaluation representation
\mathrm{ev}_{\omega}:C_{0}(\Omega)\to{\mathbb{C}}
at
\omega\in\Omega
,

\mathrm{ev}_{\omega}(f)=f(\omega)
. This representation induces the covariant representation on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

given by

\begin{split}(\pi_{\omega}(f)\psi)(x)&=f(x\triangleright\omega)\psi(x)\\
(U_{A}(t)\psi)(x)&=\gamma(-t,x)\psi(x-t)\end{split}

(4.7)

where we abbreviated
\pi_{\mathrm{ev}_{\omega}}
as
\pi_{\omega}
.

In general, the associated integrated representation
\tilde{\pi}_{\omega}
(as in
4.2
) is not faithful and so we have to consider the direct sum representation

\rho=\int_{\Omega}^{\oplus}\mathrm{ev}_{\omega}\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{P}}(\omega)
.

The corresponding integral representation
\tilde{\pi}=\int_{\Omega}^{\oplus}\tilde{\pi}_{\omega}\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{P}}(\omega)
(we suppress the dependence on
\rho
) is faithful and so that we can identify
C_{0}(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha,\gamma}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with the closure of the algebra generated by
\tilde{\pi}(f)
with
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},C_{0}(\Omega))
.
In practice, its elements can often be represented by covariant integral kernels. We call a measurable function
k:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}}
covariant if there is a measurable function
f:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}}
such that almost surely (in all variables)

k(\omega,x,y)=k_{f}(\omega,x,y):=e^{-\imath\langle x,{\bf B}y\rangle}f(x\triangleright\omega,x-y).

(4.8)

If
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},C_{0}(\Omega))
then, by construction,
the associated integral operator

(\mathrm{op}_{\omega}(k_{f})\psi)(y)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}k_{f}(\omega,x,y)\psi(y)\mathrm{d}x

{\mathbb{P}}
-almost surely satisfies
\mathrm{op}_{\omega}(k_{f})=\tilde{\pi}_{\omega}(f)
.

In particular, integral kernels affiliated to the crossed product can be spotted by the covariance property

k(\omega,x+z,y+z)=k(z\triangleright\omega,x,y)e^{-\frac{\imath}{2}\langle z|{\bf B}|x-y\rangle}

combined with fast enough off-diagonal decay.

We study under which condition a covariant family of magnetic Schrödinger operators is affiliated to the twisted crossed product
C_{0}(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha,\gamma}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
First we look for affiliation and then we investigate which operators are even resolvent or strongly affiliated.

With the usual multi-index notation for differentiable operators, if
I=(i_{1},\cdots,i_{d})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
we define

\nabla_{A}^{I}:={\nabla_{1,A}}^{i_{1}}\cdots{\nabla_{d,A}}^{i_{d}}

We denote by
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)\subset C(\Omega)
the functions which are compactly supported and norm-smooth w.r.t. the action
\alpha
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Consider a family
H=(H_{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega}
of essentially self-adjoint differential operators

H_{\omega}=\sum_{I\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}}\pi_{\omega}(c_{I})\nabla_{A}^{I}

(4.9)

with
c_{I}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)

on
D_{0}:=C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega))\subset L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathbb{P}})\otimes L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
(i.e.
H
a direct integral over
\Omega
of essentially self-adjoint operators on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
). The closure of
H
is a self-adjoint
{\mathcal{A}}
-multiplier if and only if
(H+\imath)^{-1}\in\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{A}})
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.6
(iii) we need to show that
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{A}}(H)
is dense in
{\mathcal{A}}
.
For
f\in D_{0}
the ”quantization map”

Q:D_{0}\mapsto{\mathcal{A}},\quad Q_{f}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}e^{t\cdot\nabla_{A}}\pi(f(t_{1},...,t_{d}))\mathrm{d}t

is well-defined and has norm-dense range in
{\mathcal{A}}
.
We claim that
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{A}}(H)
contains
Q(D_{0})
and hence is dense in
{\mathcal{A}}
.

Each
Q_{f}
preserves
D_{0}\subset{\mathcal{H}}:=L^{2}(\Omega)\otimes L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
HD_{0}\subset D_{0}
. Using the commutation relations of the
e^{t\cdot\nabla_{A}}
one computes that

HQ_{f}\psi=Q_{Hf}\psi

for all
\psi\in D_{0}
, i.e. left multiplication of
H
acts on the symbol exactly as it does under the inclusion
D_{0}\subset{\mathcal{H}}
. Hence
Q(D_{0})\subset{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{A}}(H)
.
∎

The last result easily extends to matrix valued operators, showing that these are affiliated to
M_{N}({\mathcal{A}})
under the analogous condition.

The next question is for which differential operators the resolvent lies in
M_{N}({\mathcal{A}}^{\sim})
. In the translation-invariant case, that is when
\Omega=\{*\}
is one point and the magnetic field
0
, this question is simple to study using the Fourier transform: Any differential operator transforms into a matrix-valued function
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
and one can always compute the resolvent using linear algebra, though the resulting expressions may be difficult to analyze by hand already for moderately sized matrices.

In the presence of a magnetic field and for non-trivial coefficients one has the following result which applies to the most common spaces
\Omega
:

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume that
\Omega
is compact and contains an element
\omega\in\Omega
with dense orbit under the
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-action
\alpha
. The associated representation
\pi_{\omega}
is faithful and allows us to identify
C(\Omega)
with a subalgebra
{\mathcal{C}}\subset C_{\mathrm{buc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
of the bounded uniformly continuous functions by setting
\tilde{f}(x)=(\alpha_{x}f)(\omega)
for
f\in C(\Omega)
.

Let
H=\sum_{I\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}}\pi_{\omega}(c_{I})\nabla_{A}^{I}
,
c_{I}\in C(\Omega)
, be a self-adjoint operator which is the magnetic Weyl quantization of a smooth elliptic polynomial symbol with coefficients in
{\mathcal{C}}
in the sense of
LeinEtAl10
, i.e. the magnetic quantization of a real function of the form
{\mathcal{P}}(x,\xi)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}}\tilde{f}_{n}(x)\xi_{1}^{n_{1}}....\xi_{d}^{n_{d}}
with smooth coefficients
\tilde{f}_{n}\in{\mathcal{C}}
. Then the resolvents of
H
lie in
\pi_{\omega}(C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha,\gamma}{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, hence
H
defines a resolvent-affiliated multiplier of
C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha,\gamma}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Proof.

If
\omega\in\Omega
has a dense orbit then
f\in C(\Omega)\mapsto(\phi_{\omega}f)(x)=(\alpha_{x}f)(\omega)
is an
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-equivariant isomorphism onto a closed translation-invariant subalgebra
\mathcal{C}\subset C_{\mathrm{buc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. This is exactly the setting for the pseudodifferential calculus developed in
LeinEtAl10
. The algebra generated by the quantizations of negative-order symbols with coefficients in
{\mathcal{C}}
can be identified with the twisted crossed product algebra
{\mathcal{C}}\rtimes_{\alpha,\gamma}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and by
(
LeinEtAl10
, Theorem 3.19)
that algebra contains the resolvents of elliptic differential operators with coefficients in
{\mathcal{C}}
. We conclude
(H+\imath)^{-1}\in{\mathcal{C}}\rtimes_{\alpha,\gamma}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\simeq\pi_{\omega}(C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha,\gamma}{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and since
\pi_{\omega}
is faithful we can conclude resolvent-affiliation (noting that the argument of Proposition
4.1
already shows dense norm-dense domain as an unbounded multiplier).
∎

Different types of additive or multiplicative perturbations can also be more simply incorporated perturbatively. In particular, as already mentioned above, if
H_{0}
is an elliptic differential operator and
V=V^{*}
a matrix over
C_{b}(\Omega)
then

H=H_{0}+V

is again a multiplier by Proposition
2.10
. If more strongly
(H_{0}+\imath)^{-1}\in M_{N}({\mathcal{A}})
and
\Omega
is compact then
(H+V+\imath)^{-1}\in M_{N}({\mathcal{A}})
. If only
(H+\imath)^{-1}\in M_{N}({\mathcal{A}}^{\sim})
(which can happen if the symbol of the differential operator is not elliptic, see Example
5.5
)
then whether
(H+V+\imath)^{-1}\in M_{N}({\mathcal{A}}^{\sim})
holds depends on the shape of the matrix

and the scalar part of the resolvent.

4.2
Algebras for halfspace and interface problems

In this section we fix an algebra
{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}:=C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha,\gamma}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
based on a compact space
\Omega
modeling bulk observables.
A simple approach to model (families of) half-space Hamiltonians or interface Hamiltonians that asymptotically look like ones affiliated to the bulk algebra is to augment the space
\Omega
by the two-point compactification of the real line
[-\infty,\infty]
whose elements describe the position of the boundary or interface.
We thus set
\Omega^{*}:=\Omega\times[-\infty,\infty]
. Since
\Omega
is compact, we have
C(\Omega^{*})\simeq C(\Omega)\otimes C([-\infty,\infty])
. The space
\Omega^{*}
is a compactification of
\Omega^{0}:=\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}
for which
C_{0}(\Omega^{0})=C(\Omega)\otimes C_{0}({\mathbb{R}})
. The augmented spaces
\Omega^{*}
,
\Omega^{0}
carry an
{\mathbb{R}}
action

x\triangleright(r,\omega)=(x\triangleright\omega,r-x_{d})

where
\pm\infty-x_{d}=\pm\infty
. It gives rise to a strongly continuous action on
C(\Omega^{*})
which we denote by
\alpha^{*}
.

Since the evalutations
q_{\pm}:C(\Omega^{*})\to C(\Omega)
at
\pm\infty
are
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-equivariant they extend to the crossed product and one gets an exact sequence

C_{0}(\Omega^{0})\rtimes_{\alpha^{*},\gamma}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\hookrightarrow C(\Omega^{*})\rtimes_{\alpha^{*},\gamma}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle q_{-}\oplus q_{+}}}{{\twoheadrightarrow}}C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha,\gamma}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,\oplus\,C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha,\gamma}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

(4.10)

We denote the ideal by
{{\mathcal{E}}}
, the extension by
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
and the quotient by
{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}\oplus{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}
.
Since
\Omega^{*}
is compact Proposition
4.1
and Theorem
4.2
imply that
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
(or rather its representations on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
) contains the resolvents of magnetic differential operators which are perturbed by covariant potentials over
\Omega^{*}
.
Now, covariance implies that these potentials depend on the distance to the hypersurface at
x_{d}=0
, which is supposed to mimic the interface, and to converge at
\pm\infty
to potentials in the multiplier algebra of the bulk algebra. We therefore call
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
the interface algebra.
Note that the limiting potentials are covariant w.r.t.
\Omega
only. The kernel
{{\mathcal{E}}}
of the evaluation map must finally be generated by operators that decay with the distance from the hypersurface.

By restricting the algebras to the kernel of either
q_{-}
or
q_{+}
one obtains the two exact sequences

C_{0}(\Omega^{0})\rtimes_{\alpha^{*},\gamma}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\hookrightarrow C_{0}(\Omega^{\pm})\rtimes_{\alpha^{*},\gamma}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle q_{\pm}}}{{\twoheadrightarrow}}C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha,\gamma}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

(4.11)

with
\Omega^{+}=\Omega\times(-\infty,\infty]
and
\Omega^{-}=\Omega\times[-\infty,\infty)
respectively.
This approach of defining algebras associated to the half-spaces
{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\pm}
is the one originally used in
KS04
;
KS04b
, its main advantage is that the above exact sequences are Wiener-Hopf extensions. However, we will use below a compressed version as half-space algebras.

As for the bulk algebra, the interface algebra admits a family of representations
(\tilde{\pi}_{\omega,r})_{(\omega,r)\in\Omega^{*}}
giving rise to a covariant family of operators on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.
These are obtained by extending the evaluation representations which induce the covariant representations (
4.7
) to the points of
\Omega^{*}
. Then
\tilde{\pi}_{\omega,r}
is the associated integrated representation. There are two interesting features about these representations: The first is that the representation
\tilde{\pi}_{\omega,\pm\infty}
for
\omega\in\Omega
can be interpreted directly as the natural surjection onto
\tilde{\pi}_{\omega}({{\mathcal{A}}_{b}})
which factors through the evaluation map
q_{\pm}
. The second is that the representations
\tilde{\pi}_{\omega,r}
and
\tilde{\pi}_{re_{d}\triangleright\omega,0}
are unitarily equivalent for any finite
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
through conjugation by a so-called dual magnetic translation, hence one can actually ignore the variable
r
if it is finite and focus on the family of representations
(\tilde{\pi}_{\omega,0})_{\omega\in\Omega}
. In particular, if
\Omega=\{\omega\}
is one point only, which is the case if the operators are translation invariant, then the representation alone
\tilde{\pi}_{\omega,0}
is faithful and we can describe the algebras as in the introduction. In the absence of an external magnetic field we then get
{\mathcal{A}}_{b}={\mathbb{C}}\rtimes{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\cong C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
the isomorphism being given by the Fourier transform on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}={\mathbb{C}}\rtimes{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}\otimes C([-\infty,+\infty])\rtimes_{tr}{\mathbb{R}}\cong C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d-1})\otimes C([-\infty,+\infty])\rtimes_{tr}{\mathbb{R}}
.
The latter isomorphism is given by a partial Fourier transform and
tr
denotes the translation action. Finally,

{\mathcal{E}}\cong C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d-1})\otimes{\mathcal{K}}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}))
.

In
KS04
;
KS04b
the extension
C_{0}(\Omega^{+})\rtimes_{\alpha^{*},\gamma}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of (
4.11
) was used to describe Hamiltonians which live on the positive half-space with the argument that the integral kernels of their resolvents tend to
0
when
x_{d}\to-\infty
. However,
below we want to consider half-space operators which are obtained from restrictions to the half-space of differential operators on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. It is not clear that the integral kernels of their resolvents are given by elements of the above extension. The apparent difficulty is that the set of elements used to define
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
, notably
\tilde{\pi}(f)
with
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},C(\Omega^{*}))
, is built from functions that depend continuously on the position of the boundary, so it is not clear how sharply truncating a differential operator can result in a multiplier. The fact of the matter is, the algebras are larger than one might think:

Lemma 4.3
.

Consider the Hilbert-space representations
(\tilde{\pi}_{\omega,0})_{\omega\in\Omega}
of
{{\mathcal{E}}}=C_{0}(\Omega^{0})\rtimes_{\alpha^{*},\gamma}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, which define a faithful non-degenerate representation
\tilde{\pi}=\int_{\Omega}^{\oplus}\tilde{\pi}_{\omega,0}\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{P}}(\omega)
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\otimes L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Let
X
be the unbounded multiplication operator

(Xf)(x)=x_{d}f(x)

which is a self-adjoint operator when defined on a suitable dense subspace of
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and thus also on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\otimes L^{2}(\Omega)
.

(i)

X
is an
{{\mathcal{E}}}
-multiplier when we consider
{{\mathcal{E}}}
via
\tilde{\pi}
to be a
C^{*}
-subalgebra of
{\mathcal{B}}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\otimes L^{2}(\Omega))
.

(ii)

For every bounded Borel function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
,
f(X)
is a multiplier of
{{\mathcal{E}}}
. Moreover, for every pointwise convergent sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of Borel functions with
\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left\lVert f_{n}\right\rVert_{\infty}<\infty
one has that
f_{n}(X)
is a strictly convergent sequence of multipliers.

(iii)

The spectral projections
P_{\pm}=\chi(\pm X>0)
are
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
-multipliers. When we consider
{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}
to be a subalgebra of
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
then every element
\hat{a}\in{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
has a unique decomposition

\tilde{\pi}(\hat{a})=P_{+}\tilde{\pi}(a_{+})P_{+}+P_{-}\tilde{\pi}(a_{-})P_{-}+\tilde{\pi}(e)

(4.12)

with
a_{\pm}\in{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}
and
e\in{{\mathcal{E}}}
.

Proof.

(i):
X
is the well known self-adjoint
C_{0}({\mathbb{R}})
-multiplier
extended with the identity to
C_{0}(\Omega^{0})=C_{0}({\mathbb{R}})\otimes C(\Omega)
.
All multipliers of a
C^{*}
-algebra
{\mathcal{B}}
canonically define multipliers of all its twisted crossed products
{\mathcal{B}}\rtimes G
.

We prove that
P_{\pm}
are
{{\mathcal{E}}}
-multipliers, first for the case
d=1
. We have

{{\mathcal{E}}}=C_{0}(\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}})\rtimes_{\alpha^{*}}{\mathbb{R}}\simeq C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}\rtimes_{\hat{\alpha}}{\mathbb{R}}\simeq C(\Omega)\otimes{\mathbb{K}}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}))

where
\hat{\alpha}
is the dual action. Via the explicit form of this Takai duality isomorphism
Raeburn88
it is easy to check that any element
e\in{{\mathcal{E}}}
via its family of representations
(\tilde{\pi}_{\omega,0}(e))_{\omega\in\Omega}
corresponds one-to-one to a norm-continuous family
(k_{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega}
where
k\in C(\Omega,{\mathbb{K}}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})))
. Any
\omega
-independent bounded operator on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
is therefore a multiplier of
{{\mathcal{E}}}
, which includes any bounded Borel function
f(X)
of
X
. Moreover, for a sequence of uniformly bounded pointwise convergent Borel functions, the measurable functional calculus gives rise to a
*
-strong convergent sequence of bounded operators
(f_{n}(X))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. It is well-known that a product of a strongly-convergent sequence of operators and a compact operator converges in operator-norm, hence the strict convergence of
(f_{n}(X))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
as an
{{\mathcal{E}}}
-multiplier follows easily from a density argument, proving (ii) in the case
d=1
.

To conclude the same for
d>1
we note that one can choose a gauge for the vector potential of the magnetic field so that one can factor the twisted crossed product

C_{0}(\Omega^{0})\rtimes_{\alpha^{*},\gamma}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\simeq\left(C_{0}(\Omega^{0})\rtimes_{\alpha^{*}_{d}}{\mathbb{R}}\right)\rtimes_{\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\gamma}}{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}

with
\alpha^{*}_{d}
the translation in the direction
e_{d}
,
\tilde{\gamma}
the restriction of
\gamma
and
\tilde{\alpha}
a modification of the action parallel to the edge which implements the magnetic field. The case
d=1
shows that
f(X)
is a multiplier of the innermost crossed product, hence
f(X)
is an
{{\mathcal{E}}}
-multiplier also for
d>1
and any bounded Borel function.

For (iii) the natural
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-equivariant inclusion of
C(\Omega)
into
C(\Omega^{*})
as a unital subalgebra extends to crossed products and then allows us to identify
{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}
with a subalgebra of
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
. Choose a continuous switch function
\Theta\in C_{0}({\mathbb{R}})
with
\Theta(-\infty)=0
and
\Theta(\infty)=1
.
Setting
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}=\Theta(X)
one easily can read off from (

) that left multiplication with
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
defines a bounded multiplier of
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
. Thus we have a well-defined map

(a_{+},a_{-})\in{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}\oplus{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}\mapsto{\mathcal{P}}_{+}\tilde{\pi}(a_{+}){\mathcal{P}}_{+}+(1-{\mathcal{P}}_{+})\tilde{\pi}(a_{-})(1-{\mathcal{P}}_{+}).

This provides a linear inverse to the surjection
q:{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}\to{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}\oplus{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}
since
q({\mathcal{P}}_{+})=(1,0)
for the extension of

to the multiplier algebra. By exactness we can then conclude that any element
\hat{a}\in{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
can be written uniquely in the form

\tilde{\pi}(\hat{a})={\mathcal{P}}_{+}\tilde{\pi}(a_{+}){\mathcal{P}}_{+}+(1-{\mathcal{P}}_{+})\tilde{\pi}(a_{-})(1-{\mathcal{P}}_{+})+\tilde{\pi}(e)

(4.13)

for some
e\in{{\mathcal{E}}}
and
a_{\pm}\in{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}
.

We may assume w.l.o.g. that the support of
\Theta
is contained in
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
. There then exists some

such that

{\mathcal{P}}_{+}-P_{+}=P_{+}g(X).

As
g(X)\tilde{\pi}(a_{\pm})\in\tilde{\pi}({{\mathcal{E}}})
and
P_{+}
is an
{{\mathcal{E}}}
-multiplier we then conclude that
({\mathcal{P}}_{+}-P_{+})\tilde{\pi}(a_{\pm})\in\tilde{\pi}({{\mathcal{E}}})
.
Successively one can therefore replace all
{\mathcal{P}}_{\pm}
in (
4.13
) by the projections
P_{\pm}
and only produce corrections in
{{\mathcal{E}}}
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

This shows
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
does in fact contain many elements which appear to be “discontinuous” in the direction normal to the boundary. In particular, it contains sharp truncations of resolvents of bulk differential operators to a half-space.

In general, the set of covariant potentials which define a multiplier of some algebra
C_{0}(\Omega)\rtimes{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is usually larger than
\mathcal{M}(C_{0}(\Omega))\simeq C_{b}(\Omega)
, e.g. containing also potentials with jump-discontinuities. One can intuitively make sense of that by noting all generators (
4.3
) involve a smoothing pseudodifferential operator which regularizes the discontinuities.

We can now define the versions of half-space and edge algebras
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\pm}}
,
{{\mathcal{E}}}_{\pm}
which we use here, as compressions of the interface and edge algebra to half-space, namely

\displaystyle{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\pm}}

\displaystyle=P_{\pm}{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}P_{\pm}

\displaystyle{{\mathcal{E}}}_{\pm}

\displaystyle=P_{\pm}{{\mathcal{E}}}P_{\pm}.

Here we consider
P_{\pm}
as bounded multipliers of the abstract algebra, thus those are subalgebras of
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
and
{{\mathcal{E}}}
respectively.
They fit into the exact sequences

{{\mathcal{E}}}_{\pm}\hookrightarrow{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\pm}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle q_{\pm}}}{{\twoheadrightarrow}}{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}.

Note that, in the introduction where we considered translation invariant operators, we denoted
{\mathcal{E}}_{+}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{+}
simply by
{\mathcal{E}}
and
{\mathcal{A}}
.

4.3
Affiliation and boundary conditions for translation-invariant differential operators

In this section we consider the problem of affiliation to
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
for matrix-valued self-adjoint differential operators. Since this is difficult in general we restrict ourselves to the translation-invariant case so that Fourier transform reduces it to a family of one-dimensional problems.
Consider a general translation-invariant (matrix-valued) self-adjoint differential operator
H
which is affiliated to
M_{N}(C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
, i.e. formally

H=\sum_{I\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}}c_{I}\partial^{I}

with scalar matrices
c_{I}\in M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
.
Restricting
H
as an operator on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
to the domain
C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+})\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
and taking the graph closure we obtain a symmetric operator
\mathring{H}
which after a partial Fourier transform (in the components parallel to the interface line) has the form

\mathring{H}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}}^{\oplus}\mathrm{d}k\,\mathring{H}(k).

For almost all
k
,
\mathring{H}(k)
is a matrix-valued symmetric operator which depends polynomially on
k
and has the core
C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
.

The first question is whether
\mathring{H}
is affiliated to
M_{N}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}})
, so that we can apply the
C^{*}
-algebraic version of self-adjoint extension theory to see which self-adjoint extensions of
\mathring{H}
are affiliated to
M_{N}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}})
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Consider a symmetric operator
\mathring{H}
obtained from a translation invariant differential operator
H
as above. Suppose that there exists one self-adjoint extension
\mathring{H}_{u_{0}}
which is an
M_{N}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}})
-multiplier and such that
u_{0}\in\mathcal{M}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}})\otimes M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
. Then
\mathring{H}
is a multiplier of
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}\otimes M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
. Moreover, a self-adjoint extension
\mathring{H}_{u}
of
\mathring{H}
is a multiplier of
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}\otimes M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
if and only if

u\in\mathcal{M}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}})\otimes M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
.

Proof.

To simplify the notation we assume
N=1
.

The conditions Proposition
2.21
(i) and (ii) hold by assumption for
H_{u}
, hence it suffices to exhibit a norm-dense subset of
{\mathcal{D}}_{{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}}(\mathring{H})
.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}
be the set of integral operators on

whose integral kernel

has the form

k(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x_{d})g_{i}(x-y)

for some finite

and functions

and bounded uniformly

(i.e. smooth functions on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
which admit limits at
0
and
\infty
). Define similarly

with the additional condition that the functions

vanish in a neighborhood of

. It is a consequence of Lemma
4.3
that
{\mathcal{S}}
is norm-dense in
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
since there is a dense subset of
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
whose projections to the positive halfspace take this form. By Lemma

(ii) the subset
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
is norm-dense in
{\mathcal{S}}
since approximating

{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
map the domain

of

to itself and

being a linear combination of partial derivatives acts on

by differentiating the integral kernels, hence

by construction. In particular,

The last statement now follows from Theorem

.
∎

Additional bounded or even relatively bounded potentials can be incorporated using Proposition

, hence it is in most cases enough to work out the extensions for the pure differential operators and then add the potentials afterwards.

Remark 4.6
.

The proposition as stated also holds for magnetically covariant differential operators; one must merely modify the construction of
{\mathcal{S}}
with the appropriate magnetic covariance relation. However, since one generally cannot use the Fourier transform to reduce to a family of one-dimensional problems, computation of deficiency subspaces and von Neumann unitaries will be unfeasible in most cases. An exception is the two-dimensional case where one can use the Landau gauge to reduce to a differential operator that is translation-invariant in the direction parallel to the line
x_{d}=0
. We will leave those problems to future work.

The second question is which of the affiliated self-adjoint extensions of
\mathring{H}
are also resolvent-affiliated. It has a similar answer: if one can exhibit one extension which is resolvent-affiliated then this boils down to the study of the von Neumann unitaries determining the extension.
In the translation invariant case any multiplier
u
of
{{\mathcal{E}}}_{+}\cong C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d-1})\otimes{\mathbb{K}}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}))
can be viewed as a strongly continuous function
{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}\ni k\mapsto u(k)
. If each

is a partial isometry with finite and constant rank then this function is even continuous in the norm topology.

Proposition 4.7
.

Consider a symmetric operator

\mathring{H}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}}^{\oplus}\mathrm{d}k\,\mathring{H}(k)
obtained from a translation-invariant differential operator
H
as above. Suppose that there exists one self-adjoint extension
\mathring{H}_{u_{0}}
which is a resolvent-affiliated
M_{N}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}})
-multiplier with
u_{0}\in M_{N}(\mathcal{M}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}))
. Assume furthermore that the deficiency subspaces of
\mathring{H}(k)
have finite and equal dimensions. Then
a self-adjoint extension
\mathring{H}_{u}
of
\mathring{H}
is a resolvent-affiliated multiplier of
M_{N}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}})
if and only if
u-u_{0}\in{{\mathcal{E}}}
. This is equivalent to
k\mapsto h(k)-u_{0}(k)
being continuous and

\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\lVert u(k)-u_{0}(k)\right\rVert=0,

(4.14)

or,
k\mapsto(\mathring{H}(k)_{u(k)}-\imath)^{-1}
being continuous and

\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\lVert(\mathring{H}(k)_{u(k)}-\imath)^{-1}\right\rVert=0.

(4.15)

Proof.

Assume again
N=1
for simplicity.
As part of the assumption,
\mathring{H}_{u_{0}}
is affiliated to
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
hence by the last result all self-adjoint extensions of
\mathring{H}
are affiliated to
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
.
As
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}\cong C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d-1})\otimes C([-\infty,+\infty])\rtimes_{tr}{\mathbb{R}}
we see that resolvent-affiliation of a self-adjoint extension
(\mathring{H}(k)_{u(k)}
is equivalent to the statement that

k\mapsto(\mathring{H}(k)_{u(k)}-\imath)^{-1}
is a
C_{0}
-function in
k
. This is the last statement.

Now suppose that
\mathring{H}_{u_{0}}
and
\mathring{H}_{u}
are resolvent-affiliated self-adjoint extensions. Then
k\mapsto(\mathring{H}(k)_{u(k)}-\imath)^{-1}-(\mathring{H}(k)_{u_{0}(k)}-\imath)^{-1}
is

in

. The latter is equivalent to

being
C_{0}
, which is (
4.14
).
As the deficiency spaces are finite-dimensional by assumption,

has finite rank and hence
k\mapsto u(k)-u_{0}(k)\in C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d-1})\otimes C_{0}({\mathbb{R}})\rtimes_{tr}{\mathbb{R}}\cong{{\mathcal{E}}}
.

For the converse,
u-u_{0}\in{{\mathcal{E}}}
implies that the difference of the resolvents belong to
{{\mathcal{E}}}
hence
(\mathring{H}_{u}-\imath)^{-1}\in{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
.

\Box

Remark 4.8
.

If (
4.15
) holds then it is easy to see that for any finite interval
\Delta
there can at most be a compact set of
k\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}
such that
(\mathring{H}_{k})_{u(k)}
has any spectrum in
\Delta
. Moreover, whenever
\Delta
does not overlap the spectrum of the bulk operator then
\mathring{H}_{u}
is invertible modulo
{{\mathcal{E}}}_{+}
in that interval. Since
{{\mathcal{E}}}_{+}\simeq C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d-1})\otimes{\mathbb{K}}
this means
k\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}\mapsto(\mathring{H}_{k})_{u(k)}-\lambda
is a family of self-adjoint Fredholm operators for any
\lambda
not in the bulk spectrum. For fixed
k
there are therefore at most isolated eigenvalues inside any bulk gap, which form continuous bands of edge modes as one varies
k
. Resolvent-affiliation on the half-space requires in particular that all bands of edge modes eventually leave any finite energy interval as
\left\lvert k\right\rvert\to\infty
.

For a complete characterization of (resolvent-affiliated) boundary conditions we therefore need to find at least one self-adjoint boundary condition for which we can assert that we have a (resolvent-affiliated)
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
-multiplier. Sometimes one can directly find such an operator, for example, for the Laplacian with Dirichlet boundary conditions where it is easy to compute the resolvent explicitly using the reflection principle (see Section
5.2
below), thereby providing a reference extension that is known to be a resolvent-affiliated multiplier.

For the general case it will be useful to have a more constructive way to proceed. Instead of the half-space we now work on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
but add a fiducial boundary at
x_{d}=0
, i.e. we restrict a translation-invariant differential operator
H
to a symmetric operator
\mathring{H}
by restricting its domain to
C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}\times({\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}))
. In that case we can still use a partial Fourier transform to decompose into one-dimensional differential operators on

H=\sum_{I\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}}c_{I}\partial^{I},\qquad\mathring{H}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}}^{\oplus}\mathrm{d}k\,\mathring{H}(k).

Since

is affiliated to
{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}
the inclusion
{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}\hookrightarrow{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
implies that it is also a
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
-multiplier. Therefore we have a canonical self-adjoint extension and can prove exactly as Proposition
4.5
:

Proposition 4.9
.

Assume that
H
,
\mathring{H}
are translation-invariant differential operators as above. We can write

for a unique unitary

corresponding to the so-called transparent boundary condition.

If

then

is a symmetric unbounded multiplier of
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
.

This criterion is, for example, satisfied in all of our examples in Section

.

Half-space operators then arise naturally from considering interface conditions that decouple the two half-spaces:

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
\mathring{H}
be the restriction of a translation-invariant bulk operator as above and assume it is a symmetric
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
-multiplier.

If the positive halfspace is an invariant subspace for
\mathring{H}_{{u}}
in the sense that

for the halfspace projection

-multiplier. If

is

{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
-resolvent-affiliated.

Proof.
It is a standard result that
\mathring{H}_{{u}}
is a direct sum of two self-adjoint operators defined on dense subsets of

and

respectively. Therefore
F(\mathring{H}_{{u}})=F(P_{+}\mathring{H}_{{u}}P_{+})\oplus F(P_{-}\mathring{H}_{{u}}P_{-})
which is in

since by definition

from the proof of Proposition

{\mathcal{D}}_{{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}}(P_{+}\mathring{H}P_{+})
.

\Box

Note that
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
-resolvent-affiliation can be characterized exactly by the analogous condition as Proposition

. If the bulk Hamiltonian

is
{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}
-resolvent-affiliated then the
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
-resolvent-affiliated and ultimately the

.

Remark 4.11
.

Decoupling matching conditions obviously need not exist. For example, the restriction of the one-dimensional momentum operator
-i\partial
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
to
C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\})
cannot have self-adjoint extensions which decouple the two half lines, as the momentum operator restricted to the positive halfspace has no self-adjoint extensions.
In general, the deficiency subspaces each have the finite-dimensional subspace
{\mathcal{N}}_{k,\pm}=P_{+}{\rm Ker}(\mathring{H}^{*}_{k}\pm\imath)
. For fixed
k
, decoupling matching conditions exist if and only if
\mathrm{dim}({\mathcal{N}}_{k,+})=\mathrm{dim}({\mathcal{N}}_{k,-})
, which is clearly the case if and only if one and thus both half-space restrictions individually have self-adjoint extensions. To see this, note that the Cayley transform
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathring{H})
is block-diagonal w.r.t. the decomposition
P_{+}\oplus P_{-}
and hence the resolvent of
\mathring{H}_{u}
commutes with
P_{\pm}
if and only if the fiberwise finite-rank operator
u
is also block-diagonal.

4.4
Bulk-boundary correspondence

We have the bulk-edge exact sequence
{{\mathcal{E}}}_{+}\hookrightarrow{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle q_{+}}}{{\twoheadrightarrow}}{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}
for the positive halfspace. Throughout the explicit examples we will set
\Omega=\{*\}
, i.e. we there will be no disorder and also vanishing magnetic field, but the abstract statements cover all of those cases as well. In particular, all K-theoretic statements we derive are robust to introduction of homogeneous disorder which breaks the translation-invariance.

A half-space Hamiltonian is a self-adjoint
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
-multiplier
\hat{H}
. We call
\hat{H}

asymptotically invertible
if it is invertible modulo
{{\mathcal{E}}}_{+}
. In particular, the bulk operator
H:=q_{+}(\hat{H})
then has a spectral gap around
0
. Such
\hat{H}
defines the edge class
[\hat{H}]_{1}\in K_{1}({{\mathcal{E}}})
.

The goal of bulk-edge correspondence is to derive as much information as possible about the edge invariants from knowledge about the bulk operators. For this we can rely on the following strategies developed in Section
3
:

(i)

Simple bulk-edge correspondence: When the conditions for Theorem
3.7
are satisfied then the edge invariant
[\hat{H}]_{1}
is determined by applying the boundary map to the class
[H]_{0}=[P_{\leq 0}(H)]_{0}-[s(P_{\leq 0}(H))]_{0}
of the bulk Fermi projection.

(ii)

Relative bulk-edge correspondence: If
\hat{H},\hat{H}^{\prime}
are invertible modulo
{{\mathcal{E}}}_{+}
and
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
-comparable then Theorem
3.15
applies and

[\hat{H}]_{1}-[\hat{H}^{\prime}]_{1}=\partial[H,H^{\prime}]_{0}=\partial([P_{\leq 0}(H),P_{\leq 0}(H^{\prime})])\,.

This applies when
\hat{H},\hat{H}^{\prime}
have the same boundary conditions and are a relatively
\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}
-compact perturbation of each other.

(iii)

Extension theory: If
\hat{H},\hat{H}^{\prime}
are locally
{{\mathcal{E}}}_{+}
-comparable self-adjoint extensions of a common symmetric
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
-multiplier corresponding to von Neumann unitaries
u,v
, respectively, then

[\hat{H}]_{1}-[\hat{H}^{\prime}]_{1}=[1+uv^{*}-vv^{*}]_{1}\,.

This allows us to compare different boundary conditions for the same symmetric operator.

The methods (ii) and (iii) only make statements over the differences of edge invariants, to obtain complete information one will need to use them to reduce to a system where the simple bulk-edge correspondence applies or is simple enough to compute the edge invariant directly.

Remark 4.12
.

As mentioned before, the simple bulk-edge correspondence (i) is just a special case of (ii) which applies precisely when
\hat{H}^{\prime}
can be taken to be a scalar matrix, whence
[\hat{H}^{\prime}]_{1}=0
.

Applying the theory requires several analytical/algebraic tasks from verifying affiliation to computing von Neumann unitaries and finding asymptotically invertible extensions etc. Importantly the required algebraic conditions can and need to fail in some cases, thus one needs to check them very carefully. To avoid the technical overhead we will mostly restrict ourselves where
\hat{H}
is
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
-resolvent-affiliated
(\hat{H}+\imath)^{-1}\in M_{N}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}})
, which is simultaneously also the case where one can formulate the most robust bulk-edge correspondence.

We want to consider a class of bulk Hamiltonians of the form

H_{V}=D+V

where
D
is a
M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
-valued invertible elliptic differential operator and
V
is a matrix with entries in
\mathcal{M}({{\mathcal{A}}_{b}})
. The kinetic part (contained in
D
) is fixed, but one can still add a potential or tune some parameters (mass terms, coupling strengths etc.). For simplicity we assume
V
is bounded; in principle our formalism can handle relatively
{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}
-compact perturbations, such as differential operators of lower order than
D
, but that would lead to substantial complications when considering the eventual half-space problems. We impose the resolvent-affiliation
(D+\imath)^{-1}\in M_{N}({{\mathcal{A}}_{b}})
as is appropriate for elliptic
D
(see Theorem
4.2
) which also implies the resolvent-affiliation
(H_{V}+\imath)^{-1}\in M_{N}({{\mathcal{A}}_{b}})
.

The corresponding half-space models then are to take the form

\hat{H}_{\hat{V},u}=\mathring{D}_{u}+\hat{V}

(4.16)

where
\mathring{D}
is the symmetric restriction of
D
to the positive halfspace of which one chooses a self-adjoint extension corresponding to a von Neumann unitary
u
. Finally one can add a potential
\hat{V}\in C([0,\infty])\otimes M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})\otimes\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{A}}_{b})
which converges to a scalar matrix at infinity.

Our main result about this class of Hamiltonians is as follows:

Theorem 4.13
.

Let
\hat{H}_{\hat{V},u}
be of the form

\hat{H}_{\hat{V},u}=\mathring{D}_{u}+\hat{V}

where
\mathring{D}
is a symmetric
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
-multiplier,
V=V^{*}\in{\mathcal{M}}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}})
. Assume there exists a von Neumann unitary
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{M}}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}})
which determines a
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
-resolvent-affiliated self-adjoint extension
(\hat{H}_{\hat{V},u_{0}}+\imath)^{-1}\in{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}\otimes{\mathbb{K}}
and that
H_{V}:=q(\hat{H}_{\hat{V},u})
is invertible. For any other
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
-resolvent-affiliated
\hat{H}_{\hat{V},u}
one has

[\hat{H}_{\hat{V},u}]_{1}=\partial_{+}([H_{V},D]_{0})+[\mathring{D}_{u_{0}}]_{1}+[1+uu_{0}^{*}-u_{0}u_{0}^{*}]_{1}.

Proof.
Due to the resolvent-affiliation any perturbation
\hat{V}
is relatively
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
-compact, hence for equal boundary condition

[\hat{H}_{\hat{V},u}]_{1}-[\mathring{D}_{u}]_{1}=\partial_{+}([H_{V},D]_{0})

by Theorem
3.15
(here we use that
D
is invertible by assumption). If we instead want to compare with
\mathring{D}_{u_{0}}
then apply Proposition
3.18
to obtain

[\mathring{D}_{u}]_{1}-[\mathring{D}_{u_{0}}]_{1}=[1+uu_{0}^{*}-u_{0}u_{0}^{*}]_{1}\in K_{1}({{\mathcal{E}}}_{+}).

(4.17)

\Box

In our examples in Section
5
we will consider different translation-invariant operators
D
and parametrized families of self-adjoint boundary conditions (e.g., arising from local boundary equations). Identifying which of the corresponding extensions are resolvent-affiliated and the resulting corrections to the bulk–boundary correspondence is then intricate but tractable in principle, since then all computations reduce to computations with families of one-dimensional differential operators. Both the relative bulk invariant
\partial_{+}([H_{V},D]_{0})
as well as the correction
[1+uu_{0}^{*}-u_{0}u_{0}^{*}]_{1}
coming from the boundary condition can be computed without knowing the full spectral decomposition of the halfspace operators. To determine from this information the boundary invariant
[\hat{H}_{\hat{V},u}]_{1}
one then merely needs to fix the normalization by computing the edge invariant for the model Hamiltonian
\mathring{D}_{u_{0}}
for any choice of resolvent-affiliated boundary condition.

Remark 4.14
.

Denote by
{\mathcal{U}}
the set of von Neumann unitaries for which
\mathring{D}_{u}
is self-adjoint and
{\mathcal{U}}_{R}\subset{\mathcal{U}}
those for which
\mathring{D}_{u}
is resolvent-affiliated. The norm-topology on this set is equivalent to the norm-resolvent topology of the corresponding self-adjoint extensions, since

\left\lVert u-v\right\rVert=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert(\mathring{D}_{u}+\imath)^{-1}-(\mathring{D}_{u}+\imath)^{-1}\right\rVert,\quad\forall u,v\in{\mathcal{U}}.

The set
{\mathcal{U}}_{R}
can have infinitely many connected components. Fixing any
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{R}
to serve as a reference the correction term
[1+uu_{0}^{*}-u_{0}u_{0}^{*}]_{1}
in (
4.17
)
for each
u\in{\mathcal{U}}_{R}
depends only the connected component of
u
in
{\mathcal{U}}_{R}
. Conversely, jumps of
[1+uu_{0}^{*}-u_{0}u_{0}^{*}]_{1}
can only happen when resolvent-affiliation fails. Given a parametrized family of self-adjoint boundary conditions one can in principle work out the connected components and the corresponding corrections to bulk-boundary correspondence to obtain a complete classification of the resolvent-affiliated boundary conditions. Moreover, this classification remains unchanged under bounded perturbations by Proposition
3.20
.

We can similarly also consider interface models, which plays out almost exactly the same. Here we have interface Hamiltonians affiliated to
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
of the form

\hat{H}_{\hat{V},u}=\mathring{D}_{u}+\hat{V}

where
\hat{V}\in{\mathcal{M}}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}})
and
\mathring{D}
is the symmetric restriction of the formal differential operator
D
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus({\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}\times\{0\})
as considered as in Section
4.3
. Such a Hamiltonian models an interface between asymptotic bulk Hamiltonians
H_{V_{\pm}}
where
V_{\pm}
is the limit of
\hat{V}
at
\pm\infty
. At the fiducial line
x_{d}=0
one can enforce non-trivial matching conditions by the choice of self-adjoint extension, as is sometimes necessary to model sharp boundaries where two materials meet. There is always a preferred extension
u_{T}
, the transparent boundary condition, determined by
D=\mathring{D}_{u_{T}}
.

Theorem 4.15
.

Let
\hat{H}_{\hat{V},u}
be of the form

\hat{H}_{\hat{V},u}=\mathring{D}_{u}+\hat{V}

where
\mathring{D}
is a symmetric
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
-multiplier and
V=V^{*}\in\mathcal{M}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}})\otimes M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
. Assume that there exists a von Neumann unitary
u_{T}\in\mathcal{M}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}})\otimes M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
(the transparent matching condition) such that
D:=D_{u_{T}}
is
{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}
-resolvent-affiliated.

If
H_{V_{\pm}}:=q_{\pm}(\hat{H}_{\hat{V},u})=D+V_{\pm}
are invertible
{\mathcal{A}}_{b}
-multipliers then comparing any resolvent-affiliated matching condition
u
with
u_{T}
one has

[\hat{H}_{\hat{V},u}]_{1}=\partial_{+}([H_{V_{-}},H_{V_{+}}]_{0})+[uu_{T}^{*}-u_{T}u_{T}^{*}]_{1}.

Proof.

We have

[\hat{H}_{\hat{V},u}]_{1}=[\hat{H}_{\hat{V},u_{T}}]_{1}+[uu_{T}^{*}-u_{T}u_{T}^{*}]_{1}

by Proposition
3.20
which reduces the computation to the transparent case.

Note that
\hat{H}_{\hat{V},u_{T}}=D+\hat{V}
is
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}
-resolvent-affiliated for any bounded multiplier
\hat{V}
due to
{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}
-resolvent-affiliation of
D
. We can therefore apply the relative bulk-edge correspondence Theorem
3.15
under the exact sequence

0\to{{\mathcal{E}}}\to{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}}\to{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}\oplus{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}\to 0

to yield

[\hat{H}_{\hat{V},u_{T}}]_{1}-[D]_{1}=\partial[P_{\leq 0}(H_{V_{+}})\oplus P_{\leq 0}(H_{V_{-}}),P_{\leq 0}(D)\oplus P_{\leq 0}(D)]_{0}

for the boundary map
\partial:K_{0}({{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}\oplus{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}})\to K_{1}({\mathcal{E}})
.
Note that
[D]_{1}=0
since
D
is invertible. The boundary map satisfies

\partial=(\partial_{+}\oplus 0)-(0\oplus\partial_{+})

with the homomorphism
\partial_{+}:K_{0}({{\mathcal{A}}_{b}})\to K_{1}({{\mathcal{E}}}_{+})\simeq K_{1}({{\mathcal{E}}})
since one can consider the two copies of
{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}
separately and the minus sign appears, since changing the orientation of the translation action in a Wiener-Hopf extension induces a minus sign for the boundary maps. This gives

\displaystyle\partial[P_{\leq 0}(H_{V_{+}})\oplus P_{\leq 0}(H_{V_{-}}),P_{\leq 0}(D)\oplus P_{\leq 0}(D)]_{0}

\displaystyle=\partial_{+}[P_{\leq 0}(H_{V_{+}}),P_{\leq 0}(D)]_{0}-\partial_{+}[P_{\leq 0}(H_{V_{-}}),P_{\leq 0}(D)]_{0}

\displaystyle=\partial_{+}([P_{\leq 0}(H_{V_{+}}),P_{\leq 0}(D)]_{0}-[P_{\leq 0}(H_{V_{-}}),P_{\leq 0}(D)]_{0}

\displaystyle=\partial_{+}([P_{\leq 0}(H_{V_{+}}),P_{\leq 0}(D)]_{0}+[P_{\leq 0}(D),P_{\leq 0}(H_{V_{-}})]_{0}

\displaystyle=\partial_{+}[P_{\leq 0}(H_{V_{+}}),P_{\leq 0}(H_{V_{-}})]_{0}\,.

\Box

The correction vanishes by definition for transparent boundary conditions, in the case where a domain wall is produced by a continuous variation of a potential the interface invariant is therefore entirely determined by the bulk Hamiltonians.

Remark 4.16
.

Halfspace models are a special case of interfaces where the boundary condition decouples both sides. Since the boundary condition can change the edge invariant in the halfspace setup it is therefore easy to see that there are also non-trivial matching conditions which lead to a non-vanishing corrections
[uu_{T}^{*}-u_{T}u_{T}^{*}]_{1}
.

5
Examples

In this section we consider translation-invariant models in two dimensions with constant coefficients with the aim to obtain explicit expressions for the bulk and edge invariants.
Let us emphasize, however, that our K-theoretic results apply equally well to aperiodic models and tell us in particular that the observed bulk- and edge invariants are stable under arbitrary disorder that does not close the bulk spectral gap.

We start by recalling a fundamental result about certain pairings of
K
-theory classes with cyclic cohomology classes.
The exact sequence underlying the bulk-edge correspondence is equivalent to the Wiener-Hopf extension (

5.1
Self-adjoint extensions using boundary triples

According to von Neumann’s theory, the self-adjoint extensions of a closed symmetric operator
H
with equal deficiency indices are parametrized by unitaries
u:{\mathcal{N}}_{-\imath}\to{\mathcal{N}}_{\imath}
between the deficiency subspaces
{\mathcal{N}}_{\mp\imath}
where
{\mathcal{N}}_{z}:=\ker({H}^{*}-z)
is the eigenspace to eigenvalue
z
of the adjoint operator
{H}^{*}

ReedSimon
. The self-adjoint extension
H_{u}
defined by
u
is the restriction of
{H}^{*}
to the space (its domain)

\mathrm{dom}H_{u}=\{\psi+u(f)+f:\psi\in\mathrm{dom}{H},f\in{\mathcal{N}}_{-\imath}\}.

If
H
is a one-dimensional differential operator which is the (graph) closure of an operator defined on
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
for some open
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}
then the self-adjoint extensions are equivalently defined by local boundary conditions, that is, by specifying linear relations between the values of the functions and their derivatives (which belong to the domain of
{H}^{*}
) at the boundary points of
\Omega
. We are interested in the two cases,

\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{>0}
and
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}\backslash\{0\}
, corresponding to the half-space and the interface extension, respectively.
In simple cases, applying these linear relations to
\psi+u(f)+f
can directly be solved for the von Neumann unitary
u
, but already the regularised Dirac operator is sufficiently complicated so that we make use of the powerful theory of boundary triples. We give a very brief overview of this referring the reader to
BruningGeylerPankrashkin
for the details.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
H
be a symmetric operator on
{\mathcal{H}}
with isomorphic deficiency subspaces. A boundary triple for
H
is a triple
(V,\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2})
where
V
is an auxiliary Hilbert space and

\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}:{\mathcal{H}}\to V
two linear maps such that, for all
\psi,\phi\in\mathrm{dom}H^{*}

\langle\psi,H^{*}\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}-\langle H^{*}\psi,\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle\Gamma_{1}\psi,\Gamma_{2}\phi\rangle_{V}-\langle\Gamma_{2}\psi,\Gamma_{1}\phi\rangle_{V}

and moreover,

\mathrm{dom}H^{*}\ni\psi\mapsto(\Gamma_{1}\psi,\Gamma_{2}\psi)\in V\oplus V

is surjective.

Given two linear operators
A,B:V\to V
such that
iA+B
is invertible and
AB^{*}
selfadjoint, the restriction
H_{A,B}
of
H^{*}
to all
\psi\in\mathrm{dom}H^{*}
which satisfy

A\Gamma_{1}\psi=B\Gamma_{2}\psi

(5.1)

is a self-adjoint extension. Boundary triples always exist, though are not unique; if we have one then all self-adjoint extensions of
H
arise in the above way for different choices of
A
and
B
.

If
z
lies in the resolvent set of
H
then the restrictions
\Gamma_{1}(z)
,
\Gamma_{2}(z)
of
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
to
{\mathcal{N}}_{z}

are bijections and we can define the so-called Krein function
Q(z):V\to V

Q(z):=\Gamma_{2}(z)\Gamma_{1}(z)^{-1}.

Furthermore, if
\Im(z)\neq 0
then

W(z):=A-BQ(z)

is invertible (still under the requirement that
iA+B
is invertible and
AB^{*}
selfadjoint).
A direct calculation now yields that the von Neumann unitary
u_{A,B}:{\mathcal{N}}_{-\imath}\to{\mathcal{N}}_{\imath}
describing the extension

is given by

u_{A,B}=-\Gamma_{1}(\imath)^{-1}W(\imath)^{-1}W(-\imath)\Gamma_{1}(-\imath).

Indeed, if

then

satisfies
A\Gamma_{1}F=B\Gamma_{2}F
which can be solved for
u_{A,B}
. For
A=1
and

u_{1,0}=-\Gamma_{1}(\imath)^{-1}\Gamma_{1}(-\imath)

which will serve as our reference system.
Thus
u_{A,B}{u_{1,0}}^{*}
is conjugate to the unitary

U_{A,B}:=W(\imath)^{-1}W(-\imath)

on the auxiliary Hilbert space.
From Proposition
4.7
one can conclude that if
H_{1,0}
is resolvent affiliated to the half-space algebra (or the interface algebra) and
u_{1,0}
in its multiplier algebra then
H_{A,B}
is also resolvent affiliated to this algebra if and only if
u_{A,B}-u_{1,0}
belongs to the edge algebra. In the context of translationally invariant differential operators on a two-dimensional half-space when we reduce the problem to a family of half line operators parametrized by the wave vector
k
along the edge as described in the last section, we study their self-adjoint extensions through a family of boundary triples
(V(k),\Gamma_{1}(k),\Gamma_{2}(k))
and matrices
A(k)
,

. Now the dependence on

needs extra care.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
V=V(k)
be independent of

and
k\in{\mathbb{R}}\mapsto\Gamma_{i}(z,k)
be norm-continuous for

and

\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{R}}}\left\lVert\Gamma_{1}(z,k)\right\rVert\left\lVert\Gamma_{1}(\overline{z},k)^{-1}\right\rVert<\infty

(5.2)

with

k\mapsto u_{A,B}(k)
and
k\mapsto U_{A.B}(k)
are norm continuous and

u_{A,B}(k)-u_{1,0}(k)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k\to\pm\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
if and only if
U_{A,B}(k)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k\to\pm\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}1_{V}
.

Proof.

The norm continuity of
k\mapsto u_{A,B}(k)
follows directy from the assumption.
We have

u_{A,B}(k)-u_{1,0}(k)=-\|\Gamma_{1}(-\imath,k)\|\Gamma_{1}(\imath,k)^{-1}(U_{A,B}(k)-1_{V})\frac{\Gamma_{1}(-\imath,k)}{\|\Gamma_{1}(-\imath,k)\|}

showing that
u_{A,B}(k)-u_{1,0}(k)
is multiplication of
U_{A,B}(k)-1
by two invertible operators which are uniformly bounded in

.
Therefore

implies
u_{A,B}(k)-u_{1,0}(k)\to 0
. The converse follows in a similar way, as

U_{A,B}(k)-1_{V}=-\Gamma_{1}(\imath,k)(u_{A,B}(k)-u_{1,0}(k))\Gamma_{1}(-\imath,k)^{-1}

∎

If

5.2
Laplacian

The two-dimensional Laplacian is the unbounded operator

H=-\partial^{2}_{x}-\partial^{2}_{y}

on

[0,\infty)

It is strongly affiliated to

(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})(1+(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}

(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})\to+\infty

Since

has no bounded gaps there is no non-trivial bulk invariant.
Nevertheless this operator constitutes an interesting example, because its edge invariants are not necessarily trivial.

Remark 5.4
.

One can add a covariant potential

described by a compact dynamical system
(\Omega,\alpha,{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
,
so as to obtain a bulk Hamiltonian

which is strongly affiliated to the enlarged algebra

and may have bounded gaps in its spectrum. The spectral projections of

5.2.1
Affiliation to the half-space algebra and boundary conditions

Let
\mathring{H}
be the restriction of
H
to the core

C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{>0})

K\Psi(x,0)+L\partial_{x}\Psi(x,0)+M\partial_{y}\Psi(x,0)=0

where

are complex numbers which do not depend on

Proposition 5.5
.

The half-space Laplacians
\Delta_{D}
and
\Delta_{N}
with Dirichlet respectively Neumann boundary conditions are resolvent-affiliated to
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
and bounded from below, hence also strongly affiliated.

Proof.

Since they are norm-densely defined (cf. the proof of Proposition

) it is enough to check that some resolvents of
\Delta_{D}

{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}

(\Delta_{b}+\imath)^{-1}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{b}

(\Delta_{D}+\imath)^{-1}=P_{+}(\Delta_{b}+\imath)^{-1}(1-{\mathcal{R}})P_{+}

(\Delta_{N}+\imath)^{-1}=P_{+}(\Delta_{b}+\imath)^{-1}(1+{\mathcal{R}})P_{+}

∎

5.3
The Dirac operator

The Dirac operator with mass
m\in{\mathbb{R}}
is the matrix valued differential operator

H_{m}=-\imath(\sigma_{x}\partial_{x}+\sigma_{y}\partial_{y})+m\sigma_{z}

(5.4)

acting on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. Here we used the standard
2\times 2
Pauli matrices. The spectrum is the complement of

(-\left\lvert m\right\rvert,\left\lvert m\right\rvert)
, hence neither bounded above nor below. We assume that
m\neq 0
. As
H_{m}
is an elliptic differential operator it is resolvent affiliated.
The Fourier transform of
F(H_{m})
is

\frac{\sigma_{x}k_{x}+\sigma_{y}k_{y}+m\sigma_{z}}{\sqrt{1+k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}+m^{2}}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\arg(k_{x}+\imath k_{y})\\
\arg(k_{x}-\imath k_{y})&0\end{pmatrix}+O((k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}})

which tends for large wave vectors to a direction-dependent matrix. This shows that
H_{m}
is
{\mathcal{B}}
-affiliated to
M_{2}({{\mathcal{A}}_{b}})
where
{\mathcal{B}}
is the sub-algebra
M_{2}(C(\overline{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}))
of the multiplier algebra of
M_{2}({\mathbb{C}}\rtimes{\mathbb{R}}^{2})\cong M_{2}(C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2}))
obtained when radially compactifying
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
to the closed disk
\overline{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}
by adding the circle
{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
of directions at infinity of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. But
H_{m}
is not strongly affiliated to
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
because the above matrix depends on
k
. Therefore the spectral projection
P_{\leq 0}(H_{m})
is not an element of
M_{2}({{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}^{\sim})\cong M_{2}(C({\mathbb{S}}^{2}))
. In particular, it does not define an element of
K_{0}({{\mathcal{A}}_{b}})
.
Technically,
P_{\leq 0}(H_{m})
defines a non-trivial element of
K_{0}({\mathcal{B}})
at least, however, since the disk is contractible one has
K_{0}({\mathcal{B}})\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}
yet the integer is not the Chern number but just the rank of the projection as a vector bundle over the disk. As a consequence, one cannot formulate a meaningful bulk-edge correspondence for a single Dirac Hamiltonian but must go over to the relative theory.

Lemma 5.7
.

Any two Dirac Hamiltonians with masses
m_{1},m_{2}\neq 0
are invertible and
M_{2}({{\mathcal{A}}_{b}})
-comparable, therefore have a well-defined relative bulk invariant
[H_{m_{1}},H_{m_{2}}]_{0}=[p_{m_{1}},p_{m_{2}}]_{0}\in K_{0}({{\mathcal{A}}_{b}})
with
p_{m}=P_{\leq 0}(H_{m})
.

Under the isomorphism
K_{0}({{\mathcal{A}}_{b}})\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}
induced by the Chern number this class is given by

\langle{\rm Ch}_{2},[p_{m_{1}},p_{m_{2}}]_{0}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}({\rm sgn}(m_{1})-{\rm sgn}(m_{2})).

Proof.

Since
(H_{m}+\imath)^{-1}\in M_{2}({\mathcal{A}}_{b})
and
H_{m_{1}}-H_{m_{2}}=(m_{1}-m_{2})\otimes\sigma_{3}
is bounded one has
M_{2}({{\mathcal{A}}_{b}})
-comparability.

To compute the pairing of the Chern cocycle with this class, we follow the recipe of Appendix
A
and extend the Chern cocyle to a pair algebra.

Specifically, as in Example
A.6
we identify the upper half-sphere
H{\mathbb{S}}^{2}={\mathbb{S}}^{2}\cap({\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+})
with the radial compactification
\overline{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. The boundary
\partial H{\mathbb{S}}^{2}\simeq\mathbb{S}^{1}
is exactly the circle at infinity of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. In this sense one has
p_{m}\in C(H\mathbb{S}^{2})\otimes M_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
and
\left(p_{m_{1}}-p_{m_{2}}\right)\rvert_{\partial H\mathbb{S}^{2}}=0
.

As seen in Example
A
we can then compute the pairing with the Chern cocycle as an integral over the sphere

\begin{split}\langle&{\rm Ch}_{2},[p_{m_{1}},p_{m_{2}}]_{0}\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi\imath}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}\hat{\omega}_{m_{1},m_{2}}\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi\imath}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\mbox{\rm Tr}(p_{m_{1}}dp_{m_{1}}dp_{m_{1}}-p_{m_{2}}dp_{m_{2}}dp_{m_{2}})\\
&=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\left(\frac{m_{1}}{\sqrt{m_{1}^{2}+k^{2}}}-\frac{m_{2}}{\sqrt{m_{2}^{2}+k^{2}}}\right)dk\\
&=\frac{1}{2}({\rm sgn}(m_{1})-{\rm sgn}(m_{2}))\end{split}

(5.5)

where
\hat{\omega}_{m_{1},m_{2}}
is the unique differential form on
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
which pulls back to the form
\mbox{\rm Tr}(p_{m_{i}}dp_{m_{i}}dp_{m_{i}})
on the upper (
i=1
) and the lower (
i=2
) half-sphere respectively. The integral over
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
can be solved analytically. The fact that the contribution of either projection individually is not an integer reiterates that
p_{m_{i}}
fails to define a class in
K_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

The above K-theoretic arguments justify exactly the prescription of

which directly defines the relative bulk invariant by gluing the Fermi projections on a sphere along the equator.

5.3.1
Extension to the interface

We now study the self-adjoint extensions arising from local matching conditions at an interface defined by
y=0
. For that we restrict the Dirac operator to the core of matrix valued functions which together with their derivatives vanish on the line defined by
y=0
.
After a Bloch transform along the boundary it decomposes into the family of symmetric operators

\mathring{H}(k)=\begin{pmatrix}m&k+\partial_{y}\\
k-\partial_{y}&-m\end{pmatrix}=\sigma_{x}k+Y\partial_{y}+m\sigma_{z},

Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}
.
For
z
in the resolvent set, the kernel
\ker(H^{*}(k)-z)
is spanned by the two vectors

\Psi_{\alpha,z}(k,y)=\phi_{\alpha,z}(k)\exp(-\alpha\mu(k)y)\chi_{\alpha y>0}(y),\quad\phi_{\alpha,z}(k)=\begin{pmatrix}m+z\\
k+\alpha\mu(k,z)\end{pmatrix}

where
\alpha\in\{\pm\}
and

\mu(k)=\sqrt{k^{2}+m^{2}-z^{2}}

(square root with positive real part). If
\alpha=+
then
\Psi_{\alpha,z}
vanishes on the left and decays exponentially to the right
whereas for
\alpha=-
it is the other way around.

To study the self-adjoint extensions of
\mathring{H}(k)
we consider the boundary triple
(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
given on
\Psi\in\mathrm{dom}H(k)^{*}
by

\Gamma_{1}\Psi=-\Psi(0^{+})+\Psi(0^{-}),\quad\Gamma_{2}\Psi=\frac{1}{2}Y(\Psi(0^{+})+\Psi(0^{-})).

Local matching conditions which are invariant under translation along the boundary are then determined by two
2\times 2
-matrices
A
,
B
and given in the form

A\Gamma_{1}\Psi=B\Gamma_{2}\Psi
. These matrices must satisfy that
A+iB
is invertible and
AB^{*}
self-adjoint.
For example, the conditions
\Psi(0^{+})=\Psi(0^{-})
correspond to

A=1
and
B=0
. The corresponding self-adjoint extension
\hat{H}_{1,0}

coincides with the original Dirac operator on the plane and so these boundary conditions are called the transparent boundary conditions.

When using the orthonomal basis
(\hat{\Psi}_{+,z},\hat{\Psi}_{-,z})
of normalized eigenfunctions
for
{\mathcal{N}}(z)
and the canonical basis for
V
we obtain the following matrix representation
\Gamma_{1}(z)
and
\Gamma_{2}(z)
:

\Gamma_{1}(z,k)=\begin{pmatrix}-(m+z)&(m+z)\\
-(k+\mu)&(k-\mu)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\|\Psi_{+,z}(k,\cdot)\|^{-1}&0\\
0&\|\Psi_{-,z}(k,\cdot)\|^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

\Gamma_{2}(z,k)=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}(k+\mu)&(k-\mu)\\
-(m+z)&-(m+z)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\|\Psi_{+,z}(k,\cdot)\|^{-1}&0\\
0&\|\Psi_{-,z}(k,\cdot)\|^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

Hence

Q(z)=\Gamma_{2}(z)\Gamma_{1}(z)^{-1}

is given by

Q(z)=-\frac{1}{2\mu}\begin{pmatrix}m-z&k\\
k&-m-z\end{pmatrix}.

Lemma 5.9
.

The self-adjoint extension
\hat{H}_{A,B}
defined by
A
and
B
is resolvent affiliated to
M_{2}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}})
if and only if

\det(A\pm\frac{1}{2}B\sigma_{x})\neq 0
.

Proof.

We have

\Gamma_{1}(z,k)\sim_{+\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2k}}\begin{pmatrix}0&m+z\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\Gamma_{1}(z,k)\sim_{-\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2k}}\begin{pmatrix}-(m+z)&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}

from which we conclude that the conditions of Lemma
5.3
are satisfied and so in particular
u_{1,0}\in M_{2}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}})
. As
\hat{H}_{1,0}
is the Dirac operator on the bulk, of which we know that it is resolvent affiliated to
M_{2}({{\mathcal{A}}_{b}})
and hence also to
M_{2}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}})
, we can apply Proposition
4.7
and again Lemma
5.3
to see
that
\hat{H}_{A,B}
is resolvent affiliated to
M_{2}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}})
if and only if

W(k,\imath)^{-1}W(k,-\imath)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|k|\to+\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}1

where
W(k,z)=A-BQ(z)
is given by

W(k,z)=A+\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(k)}{2}B\sigma_{x}-\frac{1}{2\mu}B(\mathrm{sgn}(k)(\mu-|k|)\sigma_{x}+z1_{2}-m\sigma_{z}).

If
A\pm\frac{1}{2}B\sigma_{x}
is invertible then
W(k,z)\sim_{|k|\to\infty}A+\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(k)}{2}B\sigma_{x}
which is independent of
z
and hence
W(k,\imath)^{-1}W(k,-\imath)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|k|\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}1
.
If
A+\frac{1}{2}B\sigma_{x}
has a zero eigenvalue with eigenvector
v
then, for
k>0
,
W(k,z)v=\frac{1}{2\mu}B\tilde{v}
where

\tilde{v}=((\mu-|k|)\sigma_{x}+m\sigma_{z}+z1_{2})v=:\tilde{v}_{0}+O(k^{-1}),\quad\tilde{v}_{0}=(m\sigma_{z}+z1_{2})v.

As
\frac{1}{2\mu}W(k,z)^{-1}B\tilde{v}=v
we see that the norm of
\frac{1}{2\mu}W(k,\imath)^{-1}B
remains bounded from below by
\frac{\|v\|}{\|\tilde{v}_{0}\|}+O(k^{-1})
(
\tilde{v}_{0}
does not vanish).
Therefore,

W(k,\imath)^{-1}W(k,-\imath)=1-W(k,\imath)^{-1}(W(k,\imath)-W(k,-\imath))=1-\frac{\imath}{\mu}W(k,\imath)^{-1}B

cannot tend to
1
as
k\to+\infty
.
If
A-\frac{1}{2}B\sigma_{x}
has a zero eigenvalue a similar argument shows that
W(k,\imath)^{-1}W(k,-\imath)
cannot tend to
1
as
k\to-\infty
.
∎

For example, the boundary conditions

\Psi_{1}(0^{+})=\Psi_{1}(0^{-})=0

lead to a self-adjoint extension which is not resolvent affiliated, as these correspond to
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}
and

B=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
.

5.3.2
Decoupling matching conditions

Decoupling matching conditions are given by

\Psi_{1}(0^{\pm})=a^{\pm}\Psi_{2}(0^{\pm}),\quad a^{\pm}\in{\mathbb{R}}\cup{\infty}

(one point compactification).
They correspond to boundary conditions for the half-spaces defined by
y>0
and
y<0
separately.
We can rewrite this as
A\Gamma_{1}(\Psi)=B\Gamma_{2}(\Psi)
with

A=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-1&a^{+}\\
1&-a^{-}\end{pmatrix},\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}a^{+}&1\\
a^{-}&1\end{pmatrix}.

One checks that
AB^{*}
is self-adjoint and
A+iB
invertible. Furthermore,

A+\frac{1}{2}B\sigma_{x}=\begin{pmatrix}0&a^{+}\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad A-\frac{1}{2}B\sigma_{x}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
0&-a^{-}\end{pmatrix}

from which we conclude by the Lemma
5.9
that decoupling conditions lead to
resolvent affiliated self-adjoint extensions if and only if
a^{\pm}\neq\{0,\infty\}
. We now obtain

\displaystyle A-BQ(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{-2\mu}\begin{pmatrix}\mu-k-a^{+}(m-z)&-a^{+}(\mu+k)+m+z\\
-\mu-k-a^{-}(m-z)&a^{-}(\mu-k)+m+z\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{-2\mu}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\mu-k}{m-z}-a^{+}&0\\
0&\frac{\mu+k}{m-z}+a^{-}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}(m-z)&(\mu+k)\\
-(m-z)&(\mu-k)\end{pmatrix}

(we used that
\frac{\mu-k}{m-z}=\frac{m+z}{\mu+k}
and
\frac{\mu+k}{m-z}=\frac{m+z}{\mu-k}
). Its determinant is

\det(A-BQ(z))=\frac{1}{2\mu(m-z)}((\mu-k)-a^{+}(m-z))((\mu+k)+a^{-}(m-z))

which is a product of three factors of which the second depends only on
a^{+}
whereas the third depends on
a^{-}
. As a consequence, the winding number of the von Neumann unitary is a sum of two winding numbers corresponding to the extensions of the two different half-spaces. This is to be expected, as the matching conditions decouple the two half-spaces. Moreover, the fact that the two factors depend on
k
with opposite sign implies that these winding numbers contribute in an opposite way.

Let us compute the winding of the von Neumann unitary for the upper half-space
y>0
. Since the transparent matching conditions are not decoupled we choose a new reference system, namely
a^{+}=1
(this is
\Psi_{1}(0^{+})=\Psi_{2}(0^{+})
and corresponds to infinite mass boundary conditions). Then the relative von Neumann unitary becomes

\displaystyle U^{a^{+}}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(\mu-k)-a^{+}(m-\imath)}{(\mu-k)-a^{+}(m+\imath)}\,\frac{(\mu-k)-(m+\imath)}{(\mu-k)-(m-\imath)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu-k-(a^{+}+1)m+{a^{+}}\frac{m^{2}+1}{\mu-k}+(a^{+}-1)\imath}{\mu-k-(a^{+}+1)m+{a^{+}}\frac{m^{2}+1}{\mu-k}-(a^{+}-1)\imath}

from which we infer that
the numerator goes from
\infty
to
\mathrm{sgn}(a^{+})\infty
when
k
varies from
-\infty
to
+\infty
and since its imaginary part is constant, we see that
the winding number is
0
if
a^{+}>0
and
-1
if
a^{+}<0
.

5.3.3

The edge modes have dispersion relation determined by
\det(A-BQ(\lambda))=0
where
\lambda
is now real but outside the spectrum of the reference extension. The equation is equivalent to

\mu(k,\lambda)-k=a^{+}(m-\lambda)\quad\mbox{or}\quad\mu(k,\lambda)+k=a^{-}(m-\lambda).

The first relation determines the dispersion relation for the edge modes of the upper half plane and the second for the edge modes of the lower one.
We focus on the first relation which we can write (
a=a^{+}
)

m^{2}+k^{2}-\lambda^{2}=a^{2}(m-\lambda)^{2}+k^{2}+2a(m-\lambda)k,\quad a(m-\lambda)+k>0.

5.4
Regularised Dirac operator

For the massive Dirac operator the Chern number the spectral projection onto the negative energy states is not a well-defined topological invariant. To avoid this problem one can regularize the Dirac operatorby adding a second-order term
\epsilon(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2})\sigma_{z}
with some small
\epsilon\neq 0
which we suppose to be smaller in size than
\frac{1}{2}|m|
. This operator is referred to as the regularized (massive) Dirac operator and it is a continuum model for a Chern insulator. On a half-space the spectral flow can be seen to depend on the choice of boundary conditions
TDV20
;
JudTauber2025
, which we will study using our formalism in this section.

The regularised Dirac operator is the matrix valued operator

H_{m,\epsilon}=-\imath(\sigma_{x}\partial_{x}+\sigma_{y}\partial_{y})+m\sigma_{z}-\epsilon(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2})\sigma_{z}.

(5.8)

If
\epsilon
is small enough then
the spectrum does not change under the regularisation, it is the complement of
(-\left\lvert m\right\rvert,\left\lvert m\right\rvert)
. The effect of adding this term is that
H_{m,\epsilon}
is now strongly affiliated to
{{\mathcal{A}}_{b}}
. This can be checked by explicitly computing the asymptotic behavior of the Fourier transform of
F(H_{m,\epsilon})
, or simply by noting that the Hamiltonian is a relatively compact perturbation of the strongly affiliated Hamiltonian
\epsilon(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2})\sigma_{z}
in the sense of Proposition
2.10
.
As a consequence, the spectral projection
P_{\leq 0}(H_{m,\epsilon})
defines an element of
K_{0}({{\mathcal{A}}_{b}})
and its Chern number is well-defined; it is

\sigma_{b}=\langle{\rm Ch}_{2},[H_{m,\epsilon}]_{0}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}({\rm sgn}(m)-{\rm sgn}(\epsilon))

(5.9)

as one can compute by direct evaluation of the integral formula.

5.4.1
Affiliation to the half-space algebra and boundary conditions

Let
\mathring{H}
be the closure of the symmetric operator obtained from restricting
H
to
y>0
, that is, to the core
C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+})
. We are interested to know which boundary conditions furnish us a self-adjoint extension which is resolvent affiliated to the half-space algebra.
We consider here only local translation invariant boundary conditions:

K\Psi(x,0)+L\partial_{x}\Psi(x,0)+M\partial_{y}\Psi(x,y)=0

(5.10)

where
K,L,M
are constant complex
2\times 2
matrices (with some restrictions owing to self-adjointness, see below).
When performing a Bloch decomposition along the boundary we obtain a family of symmetric Hamiltonians

\mathring{H}(k)=\begin{pmatrix}m+\epsilon(k^{2}-\partial_{y}^{2})&k+\partial_{y}\\
k-\partial_{y}&-m-\epsilon(k^{2}-\partial_{y}^{2})\end{pmatrix}

and the boundary conditions can be written

K\Psi(k,0)-ikL\Psi(k,0)+M\Psi^{\prime}(k,0)=0.

(5.11)

Proposition 5.14
.

The self-adjoint extensions
\hat{H}_{D,D}:=\hat{H}_{{\bf 1}_{2},0,0}
(Dirichlet-Dirichlet) and
\hat{H}_{D,N}:=\hat{H}_{{\rm diag}(1,0),0_{2},{\rm diag}(0,1)}
(Dirichlet-Neumann) are resolvent affiliated to
M_{2}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}})
and strongly affiliated to
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
.

Proof.

Denote
\Delta_{D}
and
\Delta_{N}
the Dirichlet respectively Neumann Laplacian on
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Since those are resolvent-affiliated to
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
it is easy to show directly that
\hat{H}^{(0)}_{D,D}:=-\epsilon\sigma_{3}\otimes\Delta_{D}
and
\hat{H}_{D,N}^{(0)}:=-{\rm diag}(\epsilon\Delta_{D},-\epsilon\Delta_{N})
are
{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
-resolvent-affiliated and moreover strongly affiliated since

F(\hat{H}_{D,*}^{(0)})=-{\rm diag}(F(\epsilon\Delta_{D}),-F(\epsilon\Delta_{*})).

One can check that
\hat{V}_{D,*}:=\hat{H}^{(0)}_{D,*}-\hat{H}_{D,*}
is a first-order differential operator which is symmetric on the given domain for both choices
*\in\{D,N\}
. With

(\hat{H}^{(0)}_{D,*}+\imath)^{-1}=-{\rm diag}\left((\epsilon\Delta_{D}-\imath)^{-1},-(\epsilon\Delta_{*}+\imath)^{-1}\right)

and the explicit expressions of Proposition
5.5
for the resolvents it is simple to verify that
\hat{V}_{D,*}(\hat{H}^{(0)}_{D,*}+\imath)^{-1}\in M_{2}({\mathbb{C}})\otimes{\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}}
. Therefore, the conditions of Proposition
2.10
are satisfied.
∎

Remark 5.15
.

For general boundary conditions this perturbation argument fails since the first-order terms are generally neither symmetric on their own nor relatively bounded.

It is convenient to set up the boundary triple in such a way that the Dirichlet Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{1,0,0}
corresponds the reference system
(A,B)=(1,0)
.

To derive an expression for the von Neumann unitary
U_{K,L,M}(k)

we use the boundary triple
({\mathbb{C}}^{2},\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2})
,

\Gamma_{1}\Psi=\Psi(0),\quad\Gamma_{2}=-\frac{1}{2}Y\Gamma_{1}-\epsilon\sigma_{z}\Gamma_{1}^{\prime},\quad\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}\Psi=\Psi^{\prime}(0)

where
Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}
.
Now
A\Gamma_{1}\Psi=B\Gamma_{2}\Psi
corrresponds to (
5.11
) if
A+B\frac{1}{2}Y=K-\imath kL
and

\epsilon B\sigma_{z}=M
. Recall that
U_{K,L,M}(k)=W(k,\imath)^{-1}W(k,\imath)
where

5.4.2
Edge modes

The dispersion relation of the edge modes for energies outside the spectrum of
\hat{H}_{1,0,0}
are determined by the relation
W(k,\lambda)=0
. This equation is analytically difficult to solve and so we content ourself here to some special solutions which are mostly numerically determined.

We start with flat band modes. There are two of them. Indeed

\Psi_{-m}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
|k|+k\end{pmatrix}e^{-|k|y},\quad\Psi_{m}=\begin{pmatrix}|k|-k\\
0\end{pmatrix}e^{-|k|y}

\mathring{H}(k)\Psi=\lambda\Psi
with energy
\lambda

k

\lambda=-m
and
k
must be positive, while in the second
\lambda=m
and
k
must be negative. Note that the first mode satisfies Dirichlet boundary conditions in the first component and
(k-\partial_{y})\Psi_{2}(0)=0
. So it is an edge mode of
\hat{H}_{a=1}

\hat{H}_{a=1}
cannot be resolvent affiliated. The failure of
\hat{H}_{a=-1}
to be resolvent affiliated is more subtle, as it has an edge mode with a dispersion relation which is only asymptotically flat. Note that
the second solution above does not correspond to a mode of
\hat{H}_{a}
for some
a
as it
satisfies Dirichlet boundary conditions in the second component and
(k+\partial_{y})\Psi_{1}(0)=0
.

In general, the dispersion relation for edge modes is given implicitly by the equation
W(k,\lambda(k))=0
with real
\lambda(k)
outside of the spectrum of
\hat{H}_{1,0,0}(k)

This means that the exterior product

(\nu_{+}+\lambda)(\nu_{-}-\lambda)=(\mu_{-}-k)(\mu_{+}+k)

Jud and Tauber

Shallow water model

We consider an effective model for shallow water waves introduced in

In the Hamiltonian formulation the model is described by

.

\nu=0
.

where the Coriolis parameter is now a function which interpolates between

and

. This can be derived by arguing exactly as in Theorem

M_{3}({\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}})
-comparable to

. As seen in

a unitary defining a
K_{1}
class

\langle{\rm Ch}_{d},[u]_{1}\rangle=c_{d}\,\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma){\mathcal{T}}\big((u^{*}-1)\nabla_{\sigma(1)}(u)\nabla_{\sigma(2)}(u^{*})\cdots\nabla_{\sigma(d)}(u)\big)

(A.3)

\diamond

Above we have used the standard picture to represent
K
-theory elements. As seen in Section
3
have seen that otherwise there is a way to represent
K
-theory elements by means of pairs of Hamiltonians, defining elements of
the pair algebra

{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{B}},{\mathcal{A}})
where
{\mathcal{B}}
is a unital
C^{*}
-algebra which contains
{\mathcal{A}}
as an ideal. To adapt the pairing with an existing cocycle over
{\mathcal{A}}
to this case we suppose that

{\mathcal{B}}
contains a smooth unital subalgebra
B
, and that
B
contains a smooth subalgebra
A
of
{\mathcal{A}}
as an ideal. Let then
(\Omega_{B},d)
be a differential graded algebra over
B
and consider the ideal

\Omega_{A}:=\Omega_{B}(\rho(A)+d\rho(A))\Omega_{B}

which is a differential graded algebra over
A
.
Define the graded algebra
\Omega=\bigoplus_{j\geq 0}\Omega^{j}
with

\Omega^{j}:=\{(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\in\Omega^{j}_{B}\oplus\Omega^{j}_{B}:\,\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}\in\Omega^{j}_{A}\}

which we equip with the differential
d(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=(d\omega_{1},d\omega_{2})
. This then is a differential graded algebra over

{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)=\{(b_{1},b_{2})\in B\oplus B:\,b_{1}-b_{2}\in A\}.

Finally let
\varphi_{A}:\Omega_{A}^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
be a linear map which is graded cyclic in the stronger sense

\varphi_{A}(\omega_{B}\omega_{A})=(-1)^{\mathrm{deg}(\omega_{A})\mathrm{deg}(\omega_{B})}\varphi_{A}(\omega_{A}\omega_{B})

(A.4)

where
\omega_{A}\in\Omega_{A}^{k}
and
\omega_{B}\in\Omega_{B}^{n-k}
.

Proposition A.3
.

In the above setting

\varphi_{{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)}((\omega_{1},\omega_{2})):=\varphi_{A}(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})

defines a graded trace on the differential graded algebra
(\Omega,d)
over

{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)
.

Proof.

Let
\omega=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\in\Omega^{k}
,
\tilde{\omega}=(\tilde{\omega}_{1},\tilde{\omega}_{2})\in\Omega^{n-k}
then

\displaystyle\varphi_{{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)}(\omega,\tilde{\omega})

\displaystyle=\varphi_{A}(\omega_{1}\tilde{\omega}_{1}-\omega_{2}\tilde{\omega}_{2})

\displaystyle=\varphi_{A}(\omega_{1}(\tilde{\omega}_{1}-\tilde{\omega}_{2})+(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})\tilde{\omega}_{2})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\mathrm{deg}(\omega)\mathrm{deg}(\tilde{\omega})}\varphi_{A}((\tilde{\omega}_{1}-\tilde{\omega}_{2})\omega_{1}+\tilde{\omega}_{2}(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}))

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\mathrm{deg}(\omega)\mathrm{deg}(\tilde{\omega})}\varphi_{{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)}(\tilde{\omega},\omega).

the third equation following from
(
A.4
).
∎

As
A
embeds into
{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)
,

A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\hookrightarrow}}{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)
,
i(a)=(a,0)
,
the above
n
-cycle can be considered as an extension of the
n
-cycle
(\Omega_{A},d,{\varphi_{A}})
over
A
. This extension is exactly what one needs to compute the pairings with K-theory under the isomorphism (
3.2
):

Corollary A.4
.

Assume that
A
is smooth in the
C^{*}
-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)
is smooth in
{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{B}},{\mathcal{A}})
. The pairings with the characters
\tilde{\varphi}_{A},\tilde{\varphi}_{{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)}
defines homorphisms
\langle\tilde{\varphi}_{A},\cdot\rangle:K_{i}({\mathcal{A}})\to{\mathbb{C}}
and
\langle\tilde{\varphi}_{{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)},\cdot\rangle:K_{i}({\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{B}},{\mathcal{A}}))\to{\mathbb{C}}
.

For the injection
i_{*}:K_{i}({\mathcal{A}})\to K_{i}({\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{B}},{\mathcal{A}}))
as in (
3.1
) one has

\langle\tilde{\varphi}_{A},\cdot\rangle=\langle\tilde{\varphi}_{{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)},i_{*}(\cdot)\rangle

as maps on
K_{i}({\mathcal{A}})
. Moreover,

\langle\tilde{\varphi}_{{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)},{t_{*}(K_{i}({\mathcal{B}}))}\rangle=0

and hence
\langle\tilde{\varphi}_{{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)},\cdot\rangle
descends to a homomorphism

\langle\tilde{\varphi}_{{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)},\cdot\rangle:K_{i}({\mathbb{P}}(B,A))/t_{*}(K_{i}({\mathcal{B}}))\to{\mathbb{C}}

which is equivalent to
\langle\tilde{\varphi}_{A},\cdot\rangle
under the isomorphism (
3.2
).

Proof.

The first statement is clear since
\tilde{\varphi}_{{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)}
is an extension of the original cocycle
\tilde{\varphi}
if one considers
\Omega_{A}
as a subalgebra of
\Omega_{{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)}
.

For the remaining statements, note that every class
x\in t_{*}(K_{i}({\mathcal{B}}))\subset K_{i}({\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{B}},{\mathcal{A}}))
is represented by a pair
(b,b)
with a representative in
b\in M_{n}({\mathcal{B}})
, hence it is easy to see that
\langle\varphi_{{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)},x\rangle=0
.
∎

Example A.5
.

We can apply this prescription to extend the Chern cocycles in the setting of Example
A.2
to a pair algebra
{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)
.
Here we take for
B

which are infinitely often norm-differentiable w.r.t. to
\nabla_{1},...,\nabla_{d}
.
As the derivations
\nabla_{i}

\varphi_{A}
from (

.

{\mathcal{B}}=\overline{B}
is the
C^{*}
-closure in
\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{A}})

The pairing with the
K

{\mathcal{A}}

If

is even and
p,q\in M_{N}(B)
projections such that

\langle{\rm Ch}_{d},[p,q]_{0}\rangle=c_{d}\,\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}(-1)^{\sigma}{\mathcal{T}}\big(p\nabla_{\sigma(1)}(p)\cdots\nabla_{\sigma(d)}(p)-q\nabla_{\sigma(1)}(q)\cdots\nabla_{\sigma(d)}(q)\big)

\diamond

For the special case
\alpha=\mathrm{id}
of a trivial action and trivial twist
\gamma
there is also a more geometric way extend the cocycles:

Example A.6
.

We consider the algebra
{\mathcal{A}}=C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with the dense subalgebra
A={\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, the Schwartz functions, so that

A\otimes\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a dense subalgebra of the exterior algebra over
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. The top Chern cocycle is an integral over a differential form

{\rm Ch}_{d}(a_{0},\cdots,a_{d})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}a_{0}da_{1}\cdots da_{d}

where
d
is the exterior derivative.

Consider
B=C^{\infty}_{vg}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
the smooth functions whose derivatives lie in
C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
(vg stands for vanishing gradient at infinity). Choosing a homeomorphism
\sigma:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to H{\mathbb{S}}^{d}
with the upper half-sphere
H{\mathbb{S}}^{d}={\mathbb{S}}^{d}\cap({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+})
provides an isomorphism
C_{0}(H{\mathbb{S}}^{d})\simeq C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. The
C^{*}
-closure
{\mathcal{B}}=\overline{B}
corresponds to
C(H{\mathbb{S}}^{d})
since
{\mathcal{B}}
is exactly the algebra of continuous functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
which admit continuous radial limits. There is then an isomorphism
{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{B}},{\mathcal{A}})\simeq C({\mathbb{S}}^{d})
given by mapping the second component of the pair onto the lower half-sphere, which glues continuously since the image of
{\mathcal{A}}
vanishes at the equator. If
\sigma
is a diffeomorphism the DGA’s
\Omega_{A}
and
\Omega_{B}
can be pushed forward to differential forms on
H{\mathbb{S}}^{d}
and the extended cocycle can be seen as

\displaystyle({\rm Ch}_{v})^{{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)}((\omega_{1},\omega_{2}))

\displaystyle=\int_{H{\mathbb{S}}^{d}}(\sigma_{*}(\omega_{1})-\sigma_{*}(\omega_{2}))=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{d}}\hat{\omega}

where
\hat{\omega}
is the form on
{\mathbb{S}}^{d}
obtained by gluing
\sigma_{*}(\omega_{1})
together with the reflection of
\sigma_{*}(\omega_{2})
onto the lower half-sphere. In this way the Chern cocycle associated to
{\mathbb{P}}(B,A)
can be thought of as the usual Chern cocycle on
{\mathbb{S}}^{d}
.
\diamond
