Healthcare providers’ perspectives on factors influencing treatment choices for HIV-associated cryptococcal meningitis in Tanzania: a qualitative exploratory study in two regional referral hospitals
Authors: Ritah Francis Mutagonda, Mlela Msongela, Madeline Victor Kinyonga, Mercy B Omary, George N Musiba, Manase Kilonzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Cryptococcal meningitis (CM) is a life-threatening fungal infection of the central nervous system that predominantly affects people with HIV (PHIV).1 Globally, CM causes an estimated 200 000 deaths annually, with approximately 70% occurring in sub-Saharan Africa.2 3 In Africa, the pooled prevalence of CM among PHIV is estimated at 5.11%.4 Additionally, the prevalence of cryptococcal antigenemia among PHIV ranges from 5.1% to 12.9%, while CM-associated mortality ranges from 26% to 65% in countries including Botswana, Cameroon, Malawi and Tanzania.4 5

Most international and national treatment guidelines recommend amphotericin B-based combination therapy rather than fluconazole monotherapy for HIV-associated CM. The standard regimen includes an induction therapy that consists of either a single high dose of liposomal amphotericin B combined with 14 days of flucytosine and fluconazole (the AMBITION trial regimen) or 7 days of amphotericin B deoxycholate plus flucytosine, followed by 7 days of fluconazole.6 7 Evidence from clinical trials and observational studies demonstrates that combination regimens are superior to fluconazole monotherapy. For instance, a clinical trial comparing flucytosine plus high-dose fluconazole with fluconazole monotherapy reported lower mortality among patients receiving combination therapy than among those receiving fluconazole monotherapy, both at 2 weeks (10% vs 37%) and at 10 weeks (43% vs 58%).8 Similarly, a Tanzanian study reported substantially lower in-hospital mortality among patients treated with amphotericin B-based therapy compared with those receiving fluconazole monotherapy (16% vs 80%).9 Another study from Tanzania reported no deaths among patients treated with amphotericin B-based therapy, whereas mortality was 10% among those who received fluconazole monotherapy.10

Despite these strong recommendations and clear evidence, fluconazole monotherapy continues to be widely used in the initial treatment of HIV-related CM in many endemic regions.10 11 Reported reasons include shortages of amphotericin B and flucytosine, supply chain challenges, and the high cost of these agents relative to patients’ purchasing power. Limited provider knowledge and hands-on skills in administering amphotericin B, together with concerns about its toxicity, further hinder uptake of the guideline-preferred regimen.11–13

Like other WHO Member States, Tanzania has updated its national guidelines for the management of HIV-associated cryptococcal meningitis, recommending amphotericin B-based regimens as first-line therapy. HIV care and the management of opportunistic infections are delivered through a vertical programme based in HIV Care and Treatment Clinics (CTCs). Both amphotericin B and flucytosine are registered in Tanzania and are provided free of charge through CTCs for the treatment of opportunistic infections.14 15 However, the availability of these medicines is often inconsistent due to periodic stockouts and limited supply.16 In addition, coordination between CTCs and inpatient medical or infectious disease wards is often suboptimal. Consequently, PHIV may be admitted with CM without the knowledge or involvement of their CTC.17 This is particularly common among patients who receive HIV care at CTCs located far from their place of residence and present to nearby hospitals when acutely ill or encounter difficulties travelling to their usual HIV clinic. In such situations, if amphotericin B or flucytosine is unavailable through the CTCs, patients or their caregivers may be required to purchase these medicines from hospital or community pharmacies.

Although amphotericin B-based regimens are registered and recommended in Tanzania, recent studies by Makyao et al and Msongela et al9 reported that most patients with HIV-related CM admitted to medical wards in Tanzania were treated with fluconazole monotherapy.10 18 These studies also showed that treatment outcomes favoured amphotericin B-based regimens, while fluconazole monotherapy was associated with higher in-hospital mortality. Despite these insights, no previous work in Tanzania has examined why healthcare providers (HCPs) continue to select certain CM treatment regimens over others. Understanding the underlying decision-making processes is essential for improving CM management. Therefore, this exploratory qualitative study aimed to explore factors influencing treatment choices for HIV-associated CM among HCPs in Tanzania.

Methods

Study design and setting

A hospital-based qualitative exploratory study was conducted from March to April 2025 in two Regional Referral Hospitals (RRHs) in Tanzania, Dodoma and Singida RRHs. The healthcare system in Tanzania operates in a pyramidal structure, with primary healthcare (PHC) facilities (dispensaries, health centres and district hospitals) forming the base, followed by secondary-level facilities (RRHs) in the middle, and zonal referral, specialised and national hospitals at the top. RRHs serve as referral centres for PHC facilities within their respective regions.18 The selected hospitals, Dodoma and Singida RRHs, act as referral hubs for most PHC facilities in the Central Zone of mainland Tanzania. HIV services in Tanzania are delivered through a vertical programme implemented via CTCs, which are available in most healthcare facilities from tertiary to primary levels, including some private and faith-based hospitals.19

Study population, sample size and eligibility

HIV-associated CM is a condition that requires hospitalisation, and in Tanzania, patients are typically managed in medical wards. A multidisciplinary team approach is recommended for the provision of optimal patient care. In line with this approach, medical doctors are responsible for diagnosis, prescribing and monitoring; nurses for medication preparation and administration; and pharmacists for clinical pharmacy services as well as medicines supply chain management. Therefore, their participation in ward rounds influences treatment choice decisions.20

We conducted face-to-face in-depth interviews (IDIs) with medical doctors, nurses and pharmacists working in the medical wards of the participating hospitals. Participants were purposively selected based on their clinical experience in managing adult patients with HIV-associated CM. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved, defined as the point at which no new themes emerged. All invited healthcare providers agreed to participate. A total of 17 IDIs were conducted, eight at Singida Regional Referral Hospital and nine at Dodoma Regional Referral Hospital.

Interns and healthcare providers with less than 12 months of clinical experience were excluded. In addition, those who were unavailable on the scheduled dates of data collection were not included in the study.

Data collection procedure

A semi-structured interview guide was developed and pre-tested among Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences postgraduate medical students. Adjustments to the guide were made based on the pre-test feedback (online supplementary material 1). The guide was prepared in English and later translated into Swahili, the language fluently spoken by both the interviewer and participants. It included questions exploring HCPs’ experiences in managing HIV-associated CM, focusing on individual, facility-level and policy-related factors influencing CM treatment choices among HCPs.

Data were collected by a Master of Pharmacy in Clinical Pharmacy student (MM), assisted by a pharmacist (MVK) from Singida RRH and a pharmacist from Dodoma RRH. The lead interviewer (MM) received training from experienced qualitative research experts who were part of the study team and also conducted the tool pre-testing. The research assistants at Singida and Dodoma RRHs were responsible for explaining the purpose of the interview, scheduling interviews, ensuring a quiet and private environment, obtaining written informed consent, collecting basic demographic information and ensuring the proper functioning of audio recorders throughout the sessions. On average, each IDI lasted between 30 and 60 min.

Data analysis

After completing all IDIs, audio recordings were transcribed verbatim. Data were coded both manually and using qualitative data analysis software. The transcripts and field notes were read repeatedly to ensure familiarisation with the data and to capture HCPs’ insights before formal analysis.

An inductive, data-driven thematic analysis approach was employed, allowing themes to emerge directly from participants’ narratives without imposing predefined categories. The analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework: (1) familiarisation with the data, (2) generation of initial codes, (3) searching for themes, (4) reviewing themes, (5) defining and naming themes and (6) producing the report.21 Two investigators, MM and MVK, independently coded the transcripts and generated initial codes using QDA Miner software. The two coders compared their coding and discrepancies were discussed with RFM, GM and MK until consensus was reached. After two transcripts had been coded, the research team reviewed and refined the preliminary codebook before coding of the remaining transcripts continued. This process facilitated the development of a preliminary codebook. The research team reviewed and discussed the codebook after two transcripts were analysed to reach consensus before the two coders proceeded with the coding. Codes with similar meanings were merged and refined into subthemes, which were then grouped into broader overarching themes. Finally, representative quotes were selected to illustrate key findings and ensure that participants’ voices were authentically reflected in the analysis.

Summary of findings

Following data analysis, factors influencing CM treatment choices among HCPs were categorised into four main themes: influence of treatment guidelines, provider experience and competence, medicine availability and patients’ financial capacity. Summary of the findings. CM, cryptococcal meningitis.

Factors influencing CM treatment choices among HCPs

Theme 1: Influence of treatment guidelines

Participants reported managing HIV-associated CM according to guidelines, which recommend amphotericin B-based regimens as first-line therapy.

Most of the time, we follow the standard treatment guideline, which outlines the treatment plan for cryptococcal meningitis. (Prescriber-06)

In addition to available treatment guidelines, participants reported that they occasionally referred to findings from recent clinical trials, particularly when managing severe cases.

A key development came from the 2022 AMBITION trial, which significantly changed the treatment of cryptococcal meningitis. (Prescriber-05).

Participants noted that, despite the availability of updated guidelines, some HCPs continue to manage HIV-associated CM using the outdated standard of care, namely fluconazole monotherapy. However, they emphasised that the use of fluconazole alone is gradually declining.

Previously, fluconazole monotherapy was commonly used, and some providers continue to rely on this outdated practice. (Pharmacist-05).

Theme 2: Influence of CM treatment experiences

Participants noted that providers’ past experiences shaped CM management practices, with some discouraging fluconazole monotherapy after seeing better outcomes with amphotericin B-based regimens.

From our hospital’s experience, the use of amphotericin B has resulted in better outcomes for most patients compared to fluconazole monotherapy. (Prescriber 01)

Participants noted that some providers avoid amphotericin B-based regimens due to previous experiences with its side effects and serious toxicity.

Acute side effects in some patients, sometimes requiring resuscitation, discourage the use of amphotericin B in unstable cases. (Nurse-03)

Participants also explained that a patient’s clinical status sometimes determines which treatment option to choose. Participants said that fluconazole monotherapy is preferred in patients with renal impairment to prevent further deterioration of renal function due to amphotericin B.

If a patient has compromised renal function, we switch to fluconazole monotherapy, usually administered as oral tablets or capsules. (Prescriber-05)

Participants noted that providers’ knowledge and skills in using amphotericin B and flucytosine shape their treatment choices for HIV-associated CM.

We still lack full competency in reconstitution, administration, and monitoring, including electrolytes, when using amphotericin B. (Pharmacist-06)

Theme 3: Influence of medicine availability

Participants reported that the treatment of HIV-associated CM is sometimes influenced by medication availability. They frequently experience stock-outs of amphotericin B and 5-flucytosine. In contrast, fluconazole stock-outs are less of a problem, as the drug is more readily accessible through community pharmacies. Amphotericin B and flucytosine, however, depend on the HIV vertical programme or the national public hospital supply chain via the Medical Store Department.

When amphotericin B is out of stock, patients are treated without triple therapy and receive fluconazole alone. (Nurse-04)

Another participant added that access to these medications outside the vertical programme was unaffordable.

Through the vertical program, patients receive them for free, but outside the program, the drugs are expensive and often unaffordable. (Prescriber-04)

Participants further noted that stock-outs of essential electrolyte supplements, such as potassium chloride, needed during amphotericin B administration, influence treatment decisions.

The availability of supporting medication and medical equipment is the problem; we need to have good access to electrolyte supplementation medications, which always change the direction of treatment. (Pharmacist 01)

Theme 4: Influence of patients’ financial status

Participants reported that most HIV-associated CM patients are required to either pay in cash or through their respective insurance for mandatory laboratory tests, fluids and electrolyte supplements required for amphotericin B administration. The vertical programme does not cover this important supportive care. As a result, patients frequently face challenges in covering the costs of essential supportive care for CM treatment.

Non-insured patients cannot afford fluids for hydration, related medical devices, or lab tests, whereas insured patients have these costs covered by their insurance. (Prescriber-04)

Discussion

This study aimed to explore factors influencing treatment choices for HIV-associated CM among HCPs in Tanzania. Following thematic analysis, treatment decisions for HIV-associated CM were found to be influenced by several factors, including adherence to guidelines, transition from outdated to updated treatment practices, providers’ experience and competence, stock-outs of medicines and essential electrolyte supplements, reliance on out-of-pocket health financing and patients’ financial constraints.

Clinical guidelines support HCPs to make informed decisions and promote consistency in disease management across different settings.22 23 These guidelines provide evidence-based recommendations for clinical practice, typically developed with consideration of contextual factors.24 25 Consistent with the purpose of guidelines, participants in this study reported that they manage HIV-associated CM according to the national guidelines’ recommendations. They also noted that, in some cases, treatment decisions are informed by emerging evidence from recent clinical trials, as is common in clinical practice. However, aligning with literature indicating that only about 60% of healthcare is consistent with guideline recommendations, with 30% being ineffective or of low quality and 10% potentially harmful, some participants acknowledged that certain HCPs still use fluconazole monotherapy for CM, as recommended in the outgoing guidelines.26 Barriers to effective guideline implementation identified in this study reflect those reported in broader literature and include limited knowledge and suboptimal attitudes among HCPs, the level and clarity of supporting evidence, complexity and accessibility of guidelines, applicability to local practice, resource constraints and prevailing social or cultural norms.27 To improve adherence, multifaceted, targeted and context-specific strategies addressing these barriers should be implemented.

Health belief models illustrate that practice is shaped by attitudes and perceptions, which in turn are influenced by knowledge.28 Consistent with this framework, our findings show that HCPs’ experiences and competency with recommended regimens significantly influenced their treatment decisions. This aligns with previous literature indicating that limited education and awareness among HCPs regarding cryptococcal disease and its management, particularly limited knowledge and skills in administering amphotericin B and lack of familiarity with flucytosine, often leads to reliance on fluconazole monotherapy.25 29 Therefore, efforts are needed to strengthen HCPs’ competencies in the management of HIV-associated cryptococcal meningitis while ensuring strict adherence to clinical guidelines.

Moreover, even among providers who are knowledgeable about amphotericin B and flucytosine and understand their toxicity profiles, hesitation persists due to the need for frequent laboratory monitoring, an area where many facilities face significant capacity constraints. In settings where laboratory systems function adequately, affordability becomes a major barrier.25 Patients often struggle to cover the costs of laboratory investigations, electrolyte supplementation and intravenous fluids, even though antifungal medications are provided free through vertical programmes.11 30 These observations are consistent with our findings, where participants reported that most HIV-related CM care relies on out-of-pocket payments. Consequently, many patients are unable to afford the costs associated with amphotericin B-based regimens. A limited number of individuals with health insurance further limits access to appropriate care, particularly in LMICs, which bear the highest burden of HIV.30

Furthermore, this study found that the unavailability of amphotericin B, flucytosine and essential electrolyte supplementation such as potassium chloride significantly influences treatment choices. These findings are supported by studies conducted in other settings, which report that despite amphotericin B and flucytosine being long-established, off-patent medications, their availability in LMICs, particularly in Africa and Asia, remains very low.13 29 31 This limited availability is attributed to the small number of manufacturers producing generic formulations, the lack of drug registration in some countries and a limited number of importers.32 Deliberate efforts are needed to ensure the consistent availability of essential medicines and supportive care components, including intravenous fluids and laboratory services, through integration into vertical health programmes while promoting community enrolment in health insurance schemes. In addition, liposomal amphotericin B and flucytosine are better tolerated than conventional amphotericin B formulations, as they require minimal laboratory monitoring, do not require electrolyte supplementation and are safe even in patients with impaired renal function at baseline.33 Therefore, where feasible, supply chain strategies in LMICs should prioritise liposomal amphotericin B formulations to maximise the use of amphotericin-based treatment regimens. These efforts are critical to improving access to quality care and advancing progress toward universal health coverage by 2030.

In addition, poor forecasting practices have been shown to result in overstocking and subsequent expiry, which affects donor-supported supply chains in vertical programmes or in frequent stock-outs.32 These forecasting challenges are largely driven by the absence of reliable facility-level data on patients requiring amphotericin B and flucytosine.34 Furthermore, the effectiveness of current antiretroviral therapy, combined with improved adherence, has reduced the prevalence of HIV-associated opportunistic infections, including CM.35 36 While this represents progress, it also contributes to limited demand data, which in turn weakens forecasting accuracy and reduces the incentive for manufacturers and importers to supply these critical antifungal medicines. Taken together, national and global efforts are needed to accurately quantify the demand for amphotericin B and flucytosine and to ensure that these lifesaving medicines are consistently available at affordable prices in HIV-endemic regions.

This study has several limitations. First, as a qualitative study, the findings provide context-specific insights into factors influencing treatment choices for HIV-related CM and are not intended to be statistically generalisable. Second, the study was conducted in only two regional referral hospitals in the central zone of mainland Tanzania, which may not reflect the perspectives of HCPs working in other geographical regions or at different levels of the healthcare system. Third, although data saturation was achieved with 17 participants, the number of participants within each professional cadre was relatively small; therefore, perspectives may differ among HCPs with different qualifications or levels of experience. Finally, because procurement of medicines in the public sector follows a centralised supply system and availability varies over time, we did not verify which formulation of amphotericin B (liposomal or deoxycholate) was available at the participating hospitals during the study period. This may have influenced HCPs’ reported treatment preferences and prescribing decisions.

In conclusion, the management of HIV-associated CM is influenced by HCP-, health system- and patient-related factors. Addressing these barriers requires a comprehensive health system approach. Strengthening continuous training for HCPs, ensuring a reliable supply of antifungal medicines, improving laboratory diagnostic capacity and expanding health insurance coverage are essential to improving the quality and accessibility of care. In addition, supportive commodities such as intravenous fluids and potassium chloride where required should be integrated into HIV vertical programmes to facilitate implementation of the recommended amphotericin B-based treatment regimens. Collectively, these interventions are critical for reducing the morbidity and mortality associated with HIV-related CM in Tanzania and other resource-limited settings facing similar health system challenges.
