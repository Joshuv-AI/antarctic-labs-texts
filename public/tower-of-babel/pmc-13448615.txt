Use of cost analysis in examinations of health coaching interventions: a scoping review
Authors: Maura Dougherty, Sophia M Bartels, Jason Smith, Steven Zeliadt, Justeen Hyde, Bo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundHealth coaching is the process of using conversation, clinical strategies and interventions to engage clients in setting goals and identifying strategies to facilitate behaviour changes that allow clients to better self-manage their health. Health coaching is a new and growing industry in the USA. With increasing budgetary strain and limited resources, understanding the financial outcomes of adding health coaching is critical.ObjectivesThe objective of this review was to examine what is known in the literature about health coaching and cost analyses since 2017, building and expanding on the findings of a prior review. This review seeks to identify existing and persisting gaps in the literature and suggest future directions for cost analyses in health coaching interventions.Eligibility criteriaFull-text publications, excluding editorials and opinion pieces, included in this scoping review were published in 2017 or later. Included publications had to be written in English and align with the definition of health coaching described by the National Board for Health and Wellness Coaching. They also had to contain some measurement of cost or cost analysis, indicated by the mention of cost or a related synonym in the methods section.Sources of evidencePubMed, Embase and the Health Medicine Collection were searched from 1 January 2017 to 7 January 2025 for peer-reviewed research, with an additional search on 5 May 2026 to reflect any additions to the literature since the initial submission.Charting methodsThe scoping review was structured according to the enhancement by Levac et al to Arksey and O’Malley’s framework for conducting scoping reviews.Information related to the cost analysis methodology and results was extracted from each of the included articles. Trends across articles were summarised based on key characteristics of the study and the main questions being addressed in the review.ResultsOnly two studies found statistically significant effects on healthcare costs from health coaching: one found an increase in costs and the other found a decrease. Only one study found health coaching to be cost-effective, with another two studies finding it to be not cost-effective or cost-effective only if the coaching costs less than a certain amount or has an assumed efficacy threshold. Overall, the cost analyses for health coaching lacked transparency in methodology reporting and had a wide variety of methodology and costs included. Synthesis of results was not possible due to inter-study heterogeneity.ConclusionAs there is a move towards standardisation and credentialing of health coaches, there needs to be a similar move towards the standardisation of their cost analyses. This scoping review found mixed, limited evidence regarding the cost outcomes and cost-effectiveness of health coaching. Future research should conduct cost analyses of health coaching interventions according to the structured framework listed in the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards, with greater transparency of methods, longer-term results and greater generalisability of results to other geographic locations and health conditions. Cost analysis of health coaching remains an urgent area for standardised and rigorous analysis to better understand the financial implications of adding health coaching to an institution.Keywords: HEALTH ECONOMICS, Health Care Costs, Patient-Centered Care, Review

Introduction

Health coaching (sometimes referred to as health and wellness coaching) is a rapidly growing practice in the USA and globally.1 2 Although there are a range of activities that define health coaching, most coaching interventions include: (1) exploration of behaviour or lifestyle changes that may help improve health, (2) identification of specific and measurable goals aligned with interests, (3) action steps to achieve goals, (4) regular check-ins to provide support and accountability and (5) connection to additional resources that may offer supplementary support and skill-building to achieve health goals. Health coaching is currently a US$ 13 billion annual industry in the USA and is expected to double in global market size by 2030.3 It is also a growing priority with many national healthcare providers in the USA, such as the US Department of Defense and the US Department of Veterans Affairs (VA).3–5

Health coaching generally leads to better overall health outcomes and long-term maintenance of chronic health conditions.6 7 A synthesis of health coaching research found statistically significant effects on decreases in haemoglobin A1c (blood sugar) levels and body mass index, increases in physical activity trends and quality of life, and decreases in primary care visits required for maintenance of chronic conditions.8 9 Improved long-term maintenance of health conditions improves overall health and has the potential to lower current and future healthcare costs. However, previous literature looking into the effects of health coaching on healthcare costs found mixed results. An integrative review in 2017 found that health coaching lowered healthcare costs in some studies and increased healthcare costs in others. The review recommended further long-term research in cost-effectiveness for health coaching.10 A systematic review published in 2025 looking at health and wellness coaching also mentioned that future research should explore long-term cost-effectiveness, particularly in diverse populations and settings.11 Unfortunately, calculating long-term outcomes of health coaching is difficult since it requires years of patient follow-up and finding a methodology to isolate the effects of health coaching alone. However, these longer-term, over 1 year, studies are particularly important. The financial implications and potential outcomes of health coaching can help health insurers decide if and how they will reimburse health coaching visits.11 12

Objective

This scoping review aims to build on the prior integrative review by Hale et al in 2017.10 It seeks to update the results of cost analyses in health coaching research, adding to the literature available for policy makers to better decide whether to reimburse health coaching services and/or recommend health coaching’s inclusion in a healthcare package/benefit. This review will also identify existing gaps in the literature to suggest areas for future research and best practices for future evaluations.

Methods

Overview

The review was structured according to enhancement by Levac et al to Arksey and O’Malley’s framework for conducting scoping reviews.13 To ensure methodological rigour and transparency, this review was aligned to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist for systematic reviews and meta-analyses, seen in online supplemental table S1 in the appendix. A detailed description of the methods used in this review is provided in our team’s previously published protocol paper14 via open access on BMJ Open. This protocol is also included as an online supplemental file to this work. A brief overview is provided below, outlined according to the steps of the chosen framework.14

Identifying relevant literature

The search strategies used in this review focused on the use of health coaching and the inclusion of some type of cost analysis or measurement in the article, though cost itself or cost analysis did not have to be the primary outcome for article inclusion. Prior to starting this review, a definition for health coaching was created. This was done to focus on more comparable health coaching interventions, since health coaching can be used to describe a great variety of interventions. Using the definition provided by the National Board for Health and Wellness Coaching, we defined health coaching as ‘evidence-based, client-centred processes that facilitate and empower clients to develop and achieve self-determined, health and wellness goals’.11 15 This definition also reflected the emerging consensus for what is considered health coaching in the literature, specifically, a “patient-centred process… based on behaviour change theory, entail(ing) goal-setting determined by the patient… and incorporates [a] mechanism for developing accountability in health behaviours”.16

The search terms used in this study were developed in conjunction with a research librarian with extensive experience in the field. Further search strategy refinement included conducting expert-guided preliminary searches using possible combinations of the terms. We then finalised a strategy of narrowed-down terms and combinations that maximised the comprehensiveness of the search while minimising duplicative or irrelevant results.17 The full search strategy including search terms, strategies, filters and limits for all databases used is shown in table 1. Specific search terms included, ‘health coaching’, ‘whole health coaching’, ‘health and wellness coaching’, and ‘cost’ and other similar Medical Subject Heading terms (including ‘health coaching AND insurance companies’, ‘health coaching AND cost’, ‘health coaching AND insurance cost’). This search strategy, specifically using similar search terms, was constructed to closely model the strategy used by Hale et al in 2017. This was done per this work’s intent to directly build on the integrative review done on this topic.10 This search strategy was used in three databases: PubMed, Embase and Health & Medical Collection, importing the 334 unique articles found into Covidence. The last search was done on 7 January 2025, with an updated search done on 5 May 2026.

Study selection

Study selection occurred in two stages: (1) title and abstract screening and (2) full-text screening. In the first stage, title and abstract screening, articles were excluded if the health coaching intervention described was not consistent with the health coaching definition provided by the National Board for Health and Wellness Coaching. Articles were determined to not be consistent with the definition of health coaching if, on reading the title and abstract, the screener was able to answer no to one of the following questions:

Was there an opportunity for bidirectional goal setting (patient-centred goal setting for health goals or behaviour change either partially or wholly determined by the patient) between the patient and the coach?

Was there an opportunity for bidirectional goal setting (patient-centred goal setting for health goals or behaviour change either partially or wholly determined by the patient) between the patient and the coach?

Was there ongoing collaboration and/or meetings between the coach and the patient?

Was there ongoing collaboration and/or meetings between the coach and the patient?

Was there at least one individual coaching session between the coach and the patient?

Was there at least one individual coaching session between the coach and the patient?

Did the health coaching include a personalised aspect, tailored to the patient, and not just a general education plan?

Did the health coaching include a personalised aspect, tailored to the patient, and not just a general education plan?

Examples of articles excluded during this stage included articles reporting on automated non-personalised text message interventions (violates question 1) and general health education programmes aimed at groups instead of individuals (violates questions 3 and 4). The definition of health coaching used in this analysis was also kept on hand as a reference when deciding on article inclusion/exclusion.

Articles were also excluded if they were published prior to 2017, did not have a full-text version available, were not a research article or were published in a language other than English. Articles published prior to 1 January 2017, were excluded to focus on the available literature published since the 2017 integrative review by Hale et al.10 For articles without full-text options available, attempts were made to find the author’s contact information to get the full-text article. Articles that were not research articles, such as editorial and opinion articles (n=1), oral or poster abstracts (n=6), errata (n=1), or brief summary paragraphs (n=4), were excluded. Articles not published in English were excluded to avoid reliance on an external translation service.

Finally, any duplicate articles not previously excluded were removed.

A summary of the inclusion/exclusion criteria, in addition to the list of questions used for inclusion in the title and abstract screening phase, is listed in the appendix in online supplemental table S2.

After the first title and abstract screening stage, there was a second full text screening stage for additional article inclusion and exclusion, using the criteria shown in online supplemental table S3 in the appendix. This additional screening phase was added since cost analyses and their results are often not included in the title or abstract. Relying only on the title and abstract to mention the cost would have eliminated relevant articles, so this second screening was required. Articles that did not mention the cost or related synonyms in the methods section were excluded. Exceptions were permitted in the rare instances where the results of the cost analyses were included only in the results section, without being described in the methods section. The inclusion criterion of mentioning cost in the methods section was added to ensure that the cost analyses performed or proposed were gathered in a methodologically valid manner. Protocol papers were included, even though they did not have any results, to study the type of cost analyses proposed and the methodology behind them. Additionally, if upon full-text screening, the health coaching described in the article was not aligned with the definition of health coaching used in this analysis (ie, answered ‘no’ to any of the questions listed above for title and abstract screening), the article was excluded from the review. With further information available in the full-text version of the articles, additional exclusions were made to allow this review to focus on health coaching that is in line with the definition provided by the National Board for Health and Wellness Coaching.

For both of the screening stages, two reviewers, MD and SMB, each screened half of the articles after meeting to ensure consistent application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria.18 The screening criteria were first applied by both team members to a random subset of 5% of the articles that both team members screened. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, adjusting a few criteria definitions to prevent future discrepancies. Single screeners were used due to limited personnel and resources available. Similar smaller-scale reviews used single screeners using specific criteria. Additionally, single screeners are recommended for rapid reviews that maintain transparency and rigour.18 19 Both reviewers met periodically during the screening stages to discuss articles that raised questions or articles that they were uncertain about. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved by adding in a third reviewer, BK, to the discussion. After the full-text screening stage, all the included articles were checked to make sure they were in line with the definition of health coaching used in this review. The screeners checked the half of the articles they had not screened in the full text screening stage for compliance with this definition, using the inclusion criteria for the title and abstract screening.

Data extraction

The characteristics and results related to the study characteristics, health coaching information and cost analyses were recorded in a data extraction template in Covidence. The data extraction template was piloted on 10 articles, around one-third of the articles that were included in the full-text review. Based on this pilot, the template was refined for data extraction from the remaining articles, focusing on creating clear and focused template questions. Descriptions about where the relevant information for each question could be found in the article were added to the extraction template to eliminate uncertainty between reviewers. For each article, one reviewer was the primary extractor (MD or SMB), with additional feedback from the secondary reviewer as necessary. Disagreements were settled by a third reviewer (BK), as necessary. The list of variables and extraction fields are shown in online supplemental table S4 in the appendix.

For some of the included articles, there was not much information included about the health coaching intervention in the article, leading us to input ‘N/A’ or ‘Not mentioned’ into the extraction template. Of the original 30 articles, 9 cited previously published protocol papers or results papers to describe the intervention (rather than including the details directly in the costing paper). To extract the requisite detailed information about the coaching intervention, SMB and MD scanned through the cited papers, either the protocol or main results paper. Both screeners double-checked whether the intervention described was in line with the definition of health coaching used in this analysis. This additional extraction process was only conducted for articles that had ‘N/A’ or ‘Not mentioned’ listed in the extraction field for a coaching intervention description and only the extraction fields related to the health coaching details were pulled from these supplementary articles.

Collating, summarising and reporting the results

Once all the data from the included articles were extracted in Covidence, the results were summarised. These summaries focused on the type of cost analyses performed, methodology used, type of costs included and timespans considered for the analyses (short term (<1 year), long term (≥1 year), etc). Whether or not the health coaching was cost-effective (ie, provides good value for its cost compared with another programme or national threshold) and/or resulted in cost savings (ie, people who had health coaching had lower healthcare costs than a comparison group that did not) was also extracted for each non-protocol article that reported results. The extracted data was analysed for trends. Differences in methodologies and outcomes of cost analyses were reviewed and characterised. Limitations in the cost analyses were also noted and compared between studies. Risks of bias were not included in the analysis because of the high variability in the types of studies included.

All the numerical results regarding the costs of health coaching, implementation costs and the cost outcomes of health coaching were adjusted to 2025 US$ to allow for better comparability between studies and account for inflation between years. Values listed in the articles were adjusted using the non-seasonally adjusted consumer price index annual rates for all urban consumers.20 For costs reported in currencies other than US$, the costs were converted to US$ using end of year annual exchange rates for the year the study costs were reported in. The costs were then adjusted to 2025 US$ using the non-seasonally adjusted consumer price index annual rates for all urban consumers.21 22 For articles that did not specifically indicate what year or currency the values were in, the years and countries in which the health coaching intervention took place were used to determine the currency and year of the costs.

For the included articles that were reviews, the articles included in each of the reviews were also screened for inclusion in this review, following the same two-stage process above. Articles that met the inclusion criteria were added to the list of included articles, and the information from each was extracted and synthesised. The review articles are still listed in the included articles of this analysis to show the current literature available on this topic, as a survey of the field.

Consultation process and engagement of knowledge users

Initial and ongoing feedback were sought throughout the protocol design and data collection from a team of researchers familiar with Health and Wellness Coaching at the VA and with diverse educational backgrounds. The results of this review are being disseminated to those practising and teaching Health and Wellness Coaching at the VA.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of this research. Overall and specifically relevant findings for health and wellness coaching offered at the VA were presented to appropriate VA leadership.

Results

The initial database searches identified 334 relevant articles. Forty articles were excluded in the title and abstract screening phase since they did not align with the definition of health coaching used in this analysis. After the full-text review, another 264 articles were excluded for either not containing any cost analysis or measurement of cost, or not aligning with the definition of health coaching used in this analysis. See figure 1 for a depiction of the exclusion process and the number of articles excluded at each stage. Of the 30 articles that remained after full text screening, 28 were unique health coaching programmes; 2 were follow-up articles describing the results of health coaching interventions described in other included articles; and 4 articles included were literature reviews. On screening the references in the literature review articles, 2 additional articles were found that met the inclusion criteria for this scoping review analysis. This resulted in a total of 32 articles included in this analysis, with 30 unique health coaching interventions. On repeating this search strategy on 5 May 2026, an additional three articles eligible for inclusion were found, with one being a scoping review that included two additional articles relevant to this review. The total updated number of unique articles included in this review was therefore 37. The table of included articles is provided in the appendix in online supplemental tables S6a–c.

What are the types of cost analyses done in health coaching research?

Fifteen of the included studies did not specifically list the type of cost analysis performed.24 29 40 42 44 45 47–50 53 54 57 58 In these articles, some form of cost measurement or cost analysis was performed, but the specific type was not identified. For the studies that did not have a specific type of cost analysis named, 9 compared healthcare costs between people who received health coaching and those who did not,24 27 40 42 44 45 47 50 57 7 measured the costs of running the health coaching programme,33 44 49 50 53 54 58 and eight reported potential changes in healthcare costs through projected estimations or based on national averages and/or published literature values.25 28 32 41 43 46 52 54 The most commonly named type of analysis was cost-effectiveness analysis, which was conducted in 14 of the included articles.23 25–28 30–32 39 41 43 46 52 55 Three articles specifically listed performing cost-utility analyses.30 31 41

What types of costs are included in the current cost analyses done for health coaching?

Eighteen studies measured the direct costs associated with delivering the health coaching.23 25–30 33 41 43 44 46 49–51 53–55 Common components included in the total direct cost of delivering the health coaching were personnel time, resources (materials, equipment and other physical objects needed for the health coaching), information technology support, technology, training costs and other programme-specific features. The direct costs of delivering the health coaching were mostly measured using microcosting and activity-based costing (ie, directly measuring the time spent per activity in the intervention, enumerating that time and then adding up the cost of every other input used in the study). Five studies broadly stated measuring the costs of delivering the health coaching without any further specification of the types of costs included in the measurement or information about the overall methodology or information about how these costs were gathered.27 30 43 54 58

Twenty-two studies reported looking at the cost outcomes of health coaching. Often these were the healthcare costs of the health coaching participants, but some studies included costs for specific resource usage or for productivity gains/losses.23–32 40–43 45 47 50–52 54 56 57 Twenty-one studies measured the healthcare costs directly as part of the study.23–32 39–42 44 45 47 50 57 Five studies included healthcare costs from projected estimations or national standard costs of care for the relevant diagnostic groups.43 46 51 52 54

Eighteen studies used the payer perspective in their included costs, where the payer perspective reflects the costs borne by the company/healthcare system paying for the health coaching and the patient’s subsequent healthcare use.24–27 29 31 32 40–44 46 47 52 54–56 This may include the perspective of costs borne by the insurance company or larger healthcare system. Ten studies included did not report the cost perspective included in the analysis,23 30 33 39 48 49 51 53 57 58 making it difficult to contextualise the analysis results and determine who would be impacted by the potential financial implications of the health coaching study.

None of the included studies separated the costs of running the health coaching programme from the intervention’s implementation activities, if implementation activities were included in the study. This may result in inaccurate cost estimations for running the health coaching intervention, as the costs of implementation activities would increase the programme cost. The implementation costs typically occur prior to or at the beginning of the programme and may not apply in later years. Additionally, implementation costs vary dynamically with the level and phase of implementation, causing a potentially inaccurate cost estimate for any specific point in time.55

What are the results of the cost analyses done for health coaching?

Only 1 of the included studies found that health coaching statistically significantly lowered healthcare costs.42 Five studies found that health coaching lowered healthcare costs, but these results were not statistically significant.30 33 41 46 50 One simulation study found projected savings for a health coaching programme focused on preventing teen pregnancy, but this was based on average healthcare costs and an assumed programme efficacy.40 Average healthcare cost savings from health coaching ranged from US$406 to US$6929, with an average of US$2243. These cost savings occurred over a range of 21 days to 1 year.30 33 41 42 46 50 One article focused only on the costs of diabetic medications for participants as the outcome, but no statistically significant differences were found, even at this level of specificity.57 The magnitude of cost savings did not seem to rely on the timespan over which the healthcare costs were collected, the type of healthcare costs measured or the timespan during which the health coaching sessions were offered.

Conversely, one study found health coaching to statistically significantly increase healthcare costs.45 Another two studies found non-statistically significant increases in healthcare costs.55 56 The measured increases in healthcare costs from health coaching were smaller in magnitude than the decreases in this study, averaging US$151 and ranging from US$71 to US$294 over a period of 9 months to 1 year.23 25 26 A comparison of the changes in healthcare costs from health coaching are shown in figure 2.

Including the costs of running the health coaching intervention, four studies found health coaching to be cost-effective, but three studies found it either to be not cost-effective or cost-effective only with certain treatment cost caps and assumed efficacies.39 41 46 With only two studies being statistically significant and finding opposing results, the financial implications of offering health coaching still remain unknown.45 47 Additional caution also needs to be applied to the interpretation of the cost-effectiveness results here. As indicated in online supplemental tables S7a–c in the appendix, some of the studies reporting on cost-effectiveness did not report the costs of delivering the health coaching.39 52 These studies are included here to broadly highlight how the field is reporting costs related to health coaching, often emphasising healthcare use sequelae following coaching, not necessarily including the costs of health coaching delivery.

What are the timespans considered for the cost analyses of health coaching?

The timespans of health coaching cost analyses ranged from 3 months to a patient’s lifetime, with 12 months being the most common timespan considered. Twenty-seven studies were short-term studies, less than 1 year.23–25 27–29 31 32 39–44 46–49 51–55 58 Despite the call for longer-term cost analyses of health coaching given by Hale et al, many of the studies still failed to consider the longer-term cost implications in this study.10

Discussion

This scoping review was both an update and an extension to the integrative review done by Hale et al in 2017.10 This analysis added a specific focus on the methodology used in the cost analyses, and the types of cost and cost analyses performed in each study. The study by Hale et al looked at the appropriateness of the methods and the quality of medical data to determine efficacy but did not address the quality or rigour of the cost analyses performed.10 This review sought to look at the results of the cost analyses of health coaching, since a more standardised definition emerged, and permanently approved standards were established for training and education programmes in 2017.56 This review also added the context of the methodology of each analysis.

Like the review by Hale et al, only two studies found statistically significant changes in healthcare costs.45 47 The results from these studies were contradictory, with one finding health coaching lowered healthcare costs and the other finding that it increased healthcare costs.45 47 Even including the non-statistically significant studies, the results were still mixed, with some studies finding healthcare cost increases and others finding healthcare cost decreases.40 42 44 50 55–57 These results were similar to the mixed evidence found in the review by Hale et al, which also found mixed results on the cost-effectiveness of health coaching.10 Overall, there is a need for more studies with statistically significant results to determine the effects of health coaching on healthcare cost outcomes.

The included studies that analysed changes in healthcare costs also varied greatly in the types of costs included, timespan of the healthcare costs, and health conditions targeted by the coaching. Two articles that measured healthcare costs had no details about the costs used in the analysis or did not elaborate on how these costs were obtained.43 46 For the studies that did report where the healthcare costs came from, the source varied: from using average costs assigned to a diagnostic group, only emergency room visit costs and using total healthcare costs. The differences in healthcare costs could therefore not be compared in a direct way to each other across studies. Included studies also did not mention all the details necessary to fully compare the results between studies. Given the known and unknown variability between studies, it is not possible to combine these results to synthesise an average cost outcome for health coaching. Adding to this variability is the difference in the types of health coaching interventions included in this analysis. In 2017, the National Board for Health and Wellness Coaching established a curriculum and training standards for health and wellness coaching, creating national standards for the field.59 Despite this move towards standardisation, there was still a variety of health coaching types included in this analysis, that is, virtual versus in person, length of health coaching, and types of topics included. This additional variability makes it not possible to synthesise the results of the cost analyses of health coaching; they can only be compared separately, indicating the type of coaching undertaken and cost analysis for each.

Limitations of this study

The results of this study were geographically concentrated mainly in the USA and Europe. Only three studies looking at health coaching in Asia were included,23 31 45 with another two studies looking at health coaching in Australia.27 45 No studies looked at other countries in North America or any country in South America or Africa. Results in the USA often do not apply to other settings, due to differences in healthcare systems and higher prices in the USA; therefore, results of this analysis may not generalise beyond the USA and Europe.60

The health coaching interventions included in this study also had more of a focus on long-term chronic conditions, particularly diabetes, and lifestyle behaviours. Health coaching for more severe and shorter-term conditions may have differing results and therefore different effects on healthcare costs. The results are not generalisable to other types of conditions, and the results of the cost analyses must be taken in the context of longer-term chronic condition management or prevention only.

This review was necessarily limited to full-text research published in English, contributing to the limited generalisability of these findings. The search strategy may also be a limitation, as there is wide heterogeneity in the term ‘health coaching’. However, this review was conducted in consultation with an experienced research librarian, economist and researchers familiar with health coaching. This strategy likely was robust and captured all the relevant literature, due to the experience of the team members involved.

Additionally, many of these analyses focused on entirely virtual or telehealth health coaching sessions, so the costs of delivering health coaching may be lower in these studies than in in-person studies, altering the cost-effectiveness of these interventions.

Another limitation of this work was the conversion of all reported costs to 2025 US$. For the articles that did not report what year the costs were measured in, the cost data was assumed to come from the last year that data was collected in the study. This assumption may not be true, and thus the costs may have been over-inflated or under-inflated to adjust to 2025 US$. However, this was the best attempt to standardise reported costs, given the available data, using the standard methodology suggested by the US Department of Health and Human Services Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality.59

Due to the choice of a scoping review for this analysis, this review does not assess the methodological quality of the individual studies beyond indicating what was reported in the study. This is in line with the purpose of conducting scoping reviews, which do not synthesise knowledge and appraise rigour. Rather, the scoping review here identified current gaps in knowledge and methodological transparency. This approach was further strengthened by including a broad range of types of cost analyses to allow for a comprehensive examination of the cost analyses/cost studies performed in the literature. By focusing on these gaps, this scoping review is essential for establishing a new research agenda, specifically for suggesting how future cost analyses should be performed for health coaching research.

Directions for future work in this field

The results from this scoping review indicate the need for rigour in future cost analyses regarding the cost outcomes of health coaching. As there is a growing move towards standardisation and credentialing of health coaches in the USA, there should be a similar standardisation of cost analyses methodology. This will allow for interstudy comparability and better understanding of the cost impact of health coaching on a healthcare system. Standardisation of cost analyses can come from a general consensus of what types of costs to include in the analysis (eg, including the cost of running the health coaching, implementation costs, outpatient costs, inpatient costs, emergency room visits and prescription medication costs). Including the same types of costs allows the changes in healthcare costs and cost-effectiveness results to better be comparable between studies. To increase the methodological rigour, the cost analyses should adhere, as much as possible, to the checklist and recommendations provided by the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS),61 as seen in the supplemental materials in the appendix. Using this checklist ensures a minimal level of quality for reporting economic analyses to facilitate transparent reporting, facilitate peer and editorial review and increase the interpretability and generalisability of findings.62 Only three studies included in this analysis used the CHEERS checklist or mentioned it.27 28 30 For greater rigour and reproducibility, all studies should strive to follow the recommendations in this list.

Future studies should also continue to address the gaps in the literature first addressed by Hale et al and also found in this study.10 Hale et al emphasised the need for longer-term cost analysis studies to study the longer-term effects of health coaching on healthcare use and costs.10 All but two of the studies included in this analysis still had cost analyses limited to 1 year or less.43 44 Longer-term cost analyses may show changes in costs of care if health coaching is effective, particularly if health coaching leads to better long-term management of chronic health conditions, such as diabetes, which, if left uncontrolled, can lead to high long-term costs of care.63 Changes to healthcare costs may not appear in the first year, particularly for longer-term chronic conditions. Longer-term analyses are rare because of funding constraints and the data collection burden for researchers and participants. However, use of longer-term study designs embedded in trials for potentially smaller groups of participants and routinely collected data may alleviate some of the burden associated with long-term outcome collection. Simulation studies may also be used to estimate longer-term healthcare costs, particularly if the costs used are based on initial studies and programme information.

For increased generalisability of results, health coaching studies should focus on other geographic regions outside the USA and Europe. How health coaching affects other populations in other geographic locations remains unknown. Additionally, future health coaching studies should look at different types of health conditions to see the shorter-term and longer-term effects of health coaching on healthcare costs.

Conclusion

As the world is moving towards the standardisation of health coaching, there should be a standardisation of cost analyses as well to better understand the financial implications. This scoping review found only two statistically significant studies looking at the cost outcomes of health coaching, with mixed results between the two. Longer-term outcomes remain unknown and there persists a lack of generalisability in results. Greater methodological rigour and transparency is required in cost analyses for health coaching to understand the cost implications of adopting health coaching.
