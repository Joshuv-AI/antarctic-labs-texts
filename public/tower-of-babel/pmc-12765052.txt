Dmxl1 is required for survival in the mouse to organogenesis stages of development
Authors: Andrew M. Vontell, Logan Willeke, Paul P.R. Iyyanar, Rolf W. Stottmann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cleft lip and cleft palate are among the most common congenital anomalies and are the result of incomplete fusion of embryonic craniofacial processes or palatal shelves. Genetic factors are known to play a large role in these anomalies, but the list of known causal genes is far from complete. As part of a larger effort to sequence patients with micrognathia and cleft palate, we identified candidate pathogenic variants in dmx-like 1 (DMXL1). We used genome editing to create an allelic series of Dmxl1 in the mouse: a small deletion and the two orthologous missense variants. We do not find evidence that either missense allele is pathogenic, but we do see that loss of Dmxl1 leads to very early embryonic lethality. This confirms and extends two recent findings about Dmxl1, suggesting this gene has crucial basal functions in the cell and should be further considered in human disease genetics.

Introduction

Cleft lip with or without cleft palate ranks in the top four of the most prevalent congenital malformations with an incidence of 1/1000 live births in the United States (Mai et al., 2019). Globally, clefting disorders occur in 1/700 live births with variation seen between different populations (Mossey et al., 2009). Cleft lip and/or cleft palate often require multiple surgeries inflicting a significant psychosocial and economic burden on affected individuals and families (Wehby and Cassell, 2010). Therefore, understanding the pathology of clefting disorders remains important for potential disease prevention. Clefting disorders arise from failure of the lip and/or palate to properly fuse during embryonic development. Causes of cleft lip and cleft palate are quite diverse with known contributions from genetics, environmental influences (teratogens) and the interplay between them (Beames and Lipinski, 2020; Dixon et al., 2011). Efforts towards understanding the genetics of clefting disorders have been somewhat successful and identified some key genes (e.g., Moreno et al., 2009; Schutte et al., 1993; Sutton and van Bokhoven, 1993), but the field is far from a complete understanding of genetic causes. We have been sequencing families with syndromic cleft lip/palate without a definitive genetic diagnosis to try to close this gap in knowledge. Through genome sequencing, we identified potentially pathogenetic missense variants in the protein coding gene dmx-like 1 (DMXL1). DMXL1 and its paralogue DMXL2 encode the Rabconnectin-3A (RBCN3a) protein. RBCN3a forms the Rabconnectin-3 complex as a heterodimer with Rabconnectin-3B (RBCN3b). This complex is required for V-ATPase activity (Jaskolka et al., 2021). Supporting this model, a recently published study indicates that Dmxl1 is required for V-ATPase assembly (Eaton et al., 2024). V-ATPase plays an essential cellular housekeeping role in organelle acidification, which is relevant for signaling pathways such as mTOR, NOTCH and Wnt (Sun-Wada and Wada, 2015). Notably, Rbcn3a knockdown has been shown to disrupt migration of some neural crest cells through downregulation of Wnt signaling in zebrafish (Tuttle et al., 2014). Improper migration of neural crest cells is a known mechanism for cleft palate (Ji et al., 2020). The role of Dmxl1/Rbcn3a in V-ATPase activity, along with the variants we identified from sequencing, suggest DMXL1 may be a factor in the genetics of cleft lip/cleft palate.

Results & discussion

Potential pathogenic variants in DMXL1 identified by genome sequencing

We have been recruiting families with congenital craniofacial anomalies as part of a larger project on the human genetics of syndromic cleft lip/palate phenotypes. One of the families enrolled in our study included a child born with cleft palate and micrognathia as part of a Pierre Robin Sequence diagnosis. Another child from the same unaffected parents had no similar anomalies (Fig. 1A). We performed genome sequencing on the affected child and parents and identified compound heterozygous variants in the gene dmx-like 1 (DMXL1). The father passed on a missense variant: Chr5(GRCh38):g.119,167,658:A > G; NM_001290321.3:c.519A > G; NP_001277250.1: p.Tyr1731Cys (Fig. 1B). This variant is noted in dbSNP (rs147221153), is not seen in the gnomAD v4 control population databases as a homozygote (Chen et al., 2024) and is quite rare with an allele frequency of 1.7e−5 (28/1, 608,998). The variant is similarly rare with an allele frequency of 2.7e−5 (45/1,630,216) in the Regeneron million exome collection (Sun et al., 2024) and not present in the Gabriella Miller Kids First Data Portal which has sequence data from hundreds of children with orofacial clefting from multiple ethnicities. The amino acid affected is in a RAVE protein 1 C-terminal domain and is in a highly conserved region of the protein (Fig. 2A). Interestingly, the position codes for a phenylalanine residue in mice as compared to the human tyrosine. Both are aromatic amino acids with hydrophobic side chains and only differ by the addition of one hydroxyl group on the six-carbon ring. The variant is predicted to be damaging by 12 of the 13 prediction tools we commonly use including scores in CADD of 25.0, “damaging” (Rentzsch et al., 2019), Polyphen of 0.99, “damaging” (Adzhubei et al., 2013), and SIFT at 0.00, “damaging” (Ng and Henikoff, 2003).

The maternal allele we identified is Chr5(GRCh38):g.119,178,115:G > A; NM_001290321.3:c.7006G > A; NP_001277250.1: p.Ala2336Thr (Fig. 1C). This variant is not recorded in the dbSNP database, gnomad v4 with over 800,000 individuals represented (Chen et al., 2024), the Regeneron exome collection databases (Sun et al., 2024) or the Gabriella Miller Kids First Data Portal. The variant is predicted to be damaging by 7 of the 13 tools we commonly use with a CADD score of 23.0 (“damaging”), Polyphen2 0.932 (“damaging) and SIFT 0.144 (“tolerated”). The amino acid is not in an annotated domain but in a highly conserved region of the protein (Fig. 2A).The unaffected sibling did not inherit either allele of DMXL1 described here.

Novel mouse alleles of Dmxl1

At the time of ascertaining the DMXL1 variants, no mouse models for Dmxl1 were available or reported. We therefore used CRISPR-CAS9 genome editing in one cell mouse embryos through injection of a mixture containing Cas9-gRNA and both variant knock-in and silent wild-type donors. Multiple founders were identified with the desired knock-ins with Sanger sequencing and were further bred with wild-type females. Animals from this outcross and their progeny were used in the analysis reported here. We recovered and maintained three alleles from founder mice. CRISPR-CAS9 transgenesis often results in creation of multiple alleles as some zygotes will experience cutting of the DNA by the CAS9 and undergo non-homologous end joining which can be imprecise and leave small insertions and deletions. We hypothesized that the Dmxl1 alleles might be hypo-morphic, and that such an insertion/indel would be a good experimental complement. Therefore, we kept a deletion allele resulting from a four base pair deletion in the Dmxl1 gene near the targeted phenylalanine residue. We designate this allele to be Dmxl1em1Rstot but hereafter refer to it as Dmxl14bpdel (Fig. 2C). This allele is predicted to result in a transcript with twenty-five missense amino acids after position 1,734, followed by a stop codon. The second allele is a phenylalanine to cysteine missense allele recapitulating the human Y1731C variant (amino acid position 1729 in mouse, Fig. 2C). We designate this allele to be Dmxl1em2Rstot but hereafter refer to it as Dmxl1F1729C. The third allele is orthologous to the human variant at amino acid position 2,336, where a threonine is substituted for an alanine. We designate this allele to be Dmxl1em3Rstot but hereafter refer to it as Dmxl1A2330T.

Mice heterozygous for any of the three Dmxl1 alleles generated were viable and fertile. Given our hypothesis that the patient phenotypes we observed are a recessive loss of function condition, we attempted to create biallelic, recessive models of Dmxl1. We first determined if Dmxl14bpdel/4bpdel homozygotes were viable and found that they could not be recovered at any stage examined from weaning to early embryonic stages of organogenesis at embryonic day (E) 8.5 (Fig. 3A, Table 1). We recovered multiple Dmxl1F1729C/F1729C and Dmxl1A2330T/A2330T homozygotes at E18.5 in near-Mendelian ratios but observed no gross morphological phenotypes (Fig. 3B and C). We also recreated the human allelic combination with Dmxl1F1729C/A2330T animals and saw no gross phenotypes in these animals either at early postnatal stages (P14) or E18.5 (Fig. 3D). We finally tested the hypothesis one or another allele may need to be in trans to the deletion to reveal a phenotype. Both Dmxl1F1729C/4bpdel and Dmxl1A2330T/4bpdel animals were recovered at weaning stages with no obvious gross morphological anomalies and survive normally as adults (Fig. 3E–G). We conclude that Dmxl1 is required to reach organogenesis stages of development in the mouse. Furthermore, we suggest that the Dmxl1F1729C and Dmxl1A2330Tmissense alleles do not affect survival or gross development.

Expression of Dmxl1

We examined a published dataset of single-cell RNA sequencing of mouse embryos at gastrulation and early organogenesis (Pijuan-Sala et al., 2019) to explore Dmxl1 early expression. Consistent with a requirement for Dmxl1 in early development as revealed by the lack of Dmxl14bpdel/4bp del homozygotes, Dmxl1 is widely expressed across the embryo at these stages (Fig. 4A and B). Given a proposed role in craniofacial development, we also explored a database of mouse cranial neural crest cells (Keuls et al., 2023) and again see Dmxl1 in a cluster of cells with expression consistent with the neural crest (4C,D). To investigate the expression pattern of Dmxl1 during embryonic development, we performed whole-mount RNA in situ hybridization on wild-type embryos at E10.5 using antisense and sense probes for Dmxl1. Dmxl1 is expressed in the forebrain as well as in the facial prominences such as the frontonasal, maxillary, and mandibular prominences (Fig. 4E–G). Furthermore, Dmxl1 is also expressed in the head mesoderm, somites, as well as the developing heart and limb region (Fig. 4E–G, n = 8 embryos). These findings collectively suggest that Dmxl1 is widely expressed throughout the developing embryo, with a notable presence in the craniofacial region.

Mandible development is unaffected by Dmxl1 missense alleles

Although there was no postnatal lethality or gross morphological difference in the surviving Dmxl1 mice, we examined skeletal preps to highlight bone and cartilage to test if there were differences in mandible length given the micrognathia evident in the proband inspiring this study (Fig. 5A–H). First, we noted no difference in mandibular length (normalized to body weight) among the embryos at E18.5 (Fig. 5I). We continued this analysis for the Dmxl1F1729C/A2330T animals and see no reductions in mandible length at either P21 (Fig. 5J) or P90 (Fig. 5K). We conclude that Dmxl1 function is required for development, but the variants we identified in the family sequenced do not affect craniofacial development.

Dmxl1 levels in missense mutants

We performed western immunoblotting to address the hypothesis the F1729C and/or A2330T mutations act as hypomorphic alleles by reducing DMXL1 protein levels. The expected molecular weight of DMXL1 is 336 kDa. Our western blot analyses using two different DMXL1 antibodies (Proteintech and Bethyl Laboratories) showed a band at approximately 250 kDa, which is significantly smaller than the expected size (Supp Figs. 1A, B, E, F). To discern whether this band was a proteolytic isoform of DMXL1 or a non-specific band, we transfected 293T cells with Dmxl1 siRNA and performed western blot from lysates collected after 48 and 72 h. The 250 kDa band remained unchanged after siRNA transfection at both 48 and 72-h time points (Supp Fig. 2A–D). However, a parallel qPCR analysis revealed that the siRNA successfully reduced Dmxl1 mRNA levels by about 70 % after 48 h (Supp. Fig. 2E). This indicates that while the siRNA was effective at knocking down the target mRNA, the DMXL1 antibodies we used did not detect a specific DMXL1 protein band. Consequently, due to the lack of a reliable reagent, we could not directly assess DMXL1 protein levels in the Dmxl1F1729C/F1729C or Dmxl1A2330T/A2330T mutant mice. Next, to identify if the Dmxl1 transcript levels are affected in these Dmxl1 mutants, we performed quantitative real-time PCR in craniofacial tissues at E13.5. Dmlx1 was reduced by approximately 20 % in the Dmxl14bpdel/wt embryos compared to Dmxl1wt/wt (Fig. 6A, t-test p = 0.039, n = 3). There was a similar decrease of approximately 25 % in Dmxl1 expression in Dmxl1F1729C/wt heterozygous samples and a mild increase (approximately 22 %) in the Dmxl1 expression in Dmxl1F1729C/F1729C homozygous embryos (Fig. 6B, ANOVA p = 0.055, n = 3). Dmxl1 transcript levels were slightly reduced by about 25 % and 40 % in the Dmxl1A2330T/wt heterozygous and Dmxl1A2330T/A2330T homozygous embryos, respectively (Fig. 6C, ANOVA p = 0.067, n = 3). These data indicate that the Dmxl1A2330T allele could be a hypomorph with sufficient transcript levels and/or protein activity for DMXL1 function and animal survival.

Dmxl1 is required for basal processes in mammalian cells

Dmxl1 is a poorly understood gene first identified in Drosophila (Kraemer et al., 1998). The function of this gene was initially determined in yeast where the orthologous protein was found to be active in the RAVE complex (Regulator of H + -ATPase of Vacuolar and Endosomal membranes, Seol et al., 2001; Smardon et al., 2002). DMXL1 is a crucial assembly factor for the vacuolar H + -ATPase (V-ATPase), and its interaction with the ATP6V1B1 subunit is essential for the proper formation and function of this proton pump. To further address any possible perturbation in DMXL1 activity in mutant mice, we assessed the level of ATP6V1B1 in mandibular lysates of F1729C and A2330T mutants at E13.5 (Fig. 7 and Supp. Fig. 3). Western blot analysis did not reveal a change in the ATP6V1B1 protein expression in the Dmxl1F1729C/wt or Dmxl1F1729C/F1729 homozygous mutants (Fig. 7A, C and Supp. Fig. 3A and C). However, there was a slight increase in the ATP6V1B1 protein in the Dmxl1A2330T/A2330T homozygous mutants (Fig. 7B, D and Supp. Fig. 3B and D). A previous report showed an ablation of Dmxl1 in the kidneys using B1-Cre resulted in significant decrease in the ATP6V1B1 protein levels, which was functionally attributed to the inhibition of proton pumping function (Eaton et al., 2024). Contrary to the loss of function of Dmxl1 in the kidneys, we noticed a slight increase in the ATP6V1B1 levels in the Dmxl1A2330-T/A2330T mutant mandibles. It is plausible that in order to compensate for the lack of proper V-ATPase assembly, the cell may be upregulating the production of individual subunits, such as ATP6V1B1, to increase the pool of available components. In a normal cell, V-ATPase subunits that are not properly assembled or are no longer needed can be tagged for degradation. Secondly, it is also possible that the DMXL1 mutation somehow could interfere with this degradation pathway leading to a buildup of ATP6V1B1 protein. In this scenario, the increased protein level may not represent an increase in functional, assembled V-ATPases, but rather an accumulation of non-functional or mis-localized subunits (Eaton et al., 2024). Finally, it is also a possibility that the Dmxl1A2330T allele creates a new interaction or alters an existing one in a way that leads to the upregulation of ATP6V1B1 expression. Given that the Dmxl1A2330T/A2330T homozygous mice survive normally, this is highly unlikely but cannot be ruled out. Regardless, these data indicate an intricate relationship between DMXL1 and the ATP6V1B1 subunit, where DMXL1 variants can lead to varied effects on V-ATPase assembly and subunit expression. V-ATPase is the primary enzyme for acidifying cellular organelles, which is essential for signaling pathways such as mTOR, Notch and Wnt (Sun-Wada and Wada, 2015). Consistent with this role, a knockdown of Rabconnectin-3a in the zebrafish model resulted in a neural crest cell migration defect due to downregulation of Wnt targets (Tuttle et al., 2014).

The data presented here are consistent with data since published by the International Mouse Phenotyping Consortium for a null allele of Dmxl1 where no homozygous Dmxl1 em1(IMPC)Bay null embryos were recovered among 107 progeny of heterozygous carrier intercrosses (Groza et al., 2023). A separate study also reports a conditional allele of Dmxl1, and global deletion throughout the embryo did not yield any homozygous offspring (Eaton et al., 2024). Neither study reports any work at any embryonic stages. Our work has extended these findings by demonstrating a requirement for Dmxl1 to even survive to organogenesis stages in the mouse. Despite the computational predictions that the variants may be pathogenic, we do not have any evidence that either allele of Dmxl1 reduces function. However, it is possible that phenotypes from the Dmxl1 missense alleles may be observed on genetic backgrounds different from those we used here and/or in different physiological contexts than those explored here. Furthermore, while the mouse is currently the closest tractable genetic model for human biology, we must always consider the possibilities that alleles may be tolerated in the mouse but pathogenic in humans. Finally, using the conditional model to ablate Dmxl1 in the neural crest may prove to be a valuable complementary approach to understanding its role in craniofacial development.

Methods

DNA sequencing & analysis

DNA was collected as part of an IRB-approved recruitment protocol and purified using the Qiagen DNeasy Kit and manufacturer’s protocols (Qiagen, USA). Library generation, enrichment, sequencing, alignment and variant detection were performed by BGI Americas (in the CCHMC Genetic Variation and Gene Discovery Core Facility (Cambridge, MA, USA). VCF format small SNP and InDels were annotated and filtered using standardized protocols using the VarSeq program (GoldenHelix, Bozeman, MT).

Mouse model creation

Mice (NCBI Taxon ID 10090) were created at the Cincinnati Children’s transgenic animal and genome editing core (RRID:SCR_022642). In order to create the F1729C allele, mouse zygotes (C57BL/6N strain) were injected with 200 ng/μl CAS9 protein (IDT and ThermoFisher), 100 ng/μl Dmxl1-specific sgRNA (TTTGAATCTGAATTTGATAAGTCAG), 75 ng/μl single-stranded donor oligo-nucleotide (KI) (CATTTATGCTTGACGAACTGAGCTCACTGGCTGGAGAACCAATTCCCAAAACTTTTTTGCGTAAAATAGACTTGTATGTTGCTGACTTgTCgAATTCtGAcTCAcATAGTCTTGCTATTACAAGAGCCAACTGAATGTCATTC; IDT, Iowa) followed by surgical implantation into pseudo-pregnant female (CD-1 strain) mice. Both donors are used together to prevent possible lethality of homozygous target mutations. Silent mutations (indicated by the lowercase letters in Fig. 2C) are also introduced in the knock-in donor to prevent recutting of the target allele by CRISPR and to create a restriction enzyme site to facilitate genotyping. PCR genotyping was performed by amplification of genomic DNA (F:GTTTTAAAGGTATGTCTTGAGAAACTG and R: TCCAGAGCAGCACTATAATCTTC primers). The PCR products were subject to Sanger Sequencing and pups exhibiting editing of interest were then crossed to wild-type (C57BL/6J) mice and the resulting progeny were Sanger sequenced (CCHMC DNA Sequencing and Genotyping Core) to confirm the alleles generated. Future genotyping was also done with a digest with MboI recognizing a unique sequence in the Dmxl14bpdel allele or EcoRI for the Dmxl1F1729C allele. The A2330T allele was similarly generated with a guide RNA (TAGTCTGTTCATTCATGGGCTGG) and donor oligo-nucleotide (ACCAAATACTGCAGACCACATTTTTTCATTTAGGGGGTGGGCTACAA TCCGAAATAGCTCATTACTAGAGTGTGTTGtCAGgCCATGgATGAACAGACTAAGATATACTGCTGTGAGAATTTCAC). PCR genotyping was performed by amplification of genomic DNA (F: TGAATTCCTCTGGGGAAGAAGC and R: CAGATAAAGCTTTTGAGTTTGCC primers) followed by Sanger sequencing and NcoI digestion as the KI variants creates a recognition site for this enzyme.

Propagation of the Dmxl1 alleles was done by crossing to wild-type C57BL/6J mice and/or intercross. Further genotyping was with a combination of PCR followed by restriction digest and/or Sanger sequencing. Mouse models have been deposited in the Mutant Mouse Resource & Research Centers (MMRRC). These are MMRRC_71734-MU (Dmxl1em1Rstot; Dmx114bpdel), MMRRC_71735-MU (Dmxl1em2Rstot; Dmx11F1729C), and MMRRC_71736-MU (Dmxl1em2Rstot; Dmx11A2330T).

Animal housing

All experiments using mice in this study were performed using ethically acceptable procedures as approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Nationwide Children’s Hospital (AR21–00067). Mice were fed mouse breeder diet and housed in ventilated cages with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. Timed matings were established to generate embryos and noon on the day of noting a vaginal plug was set as embryonic day (E) 0.5.

Skeletal preparations

Skeletons from were stained for Alcian Blue and Alizarin Red to visualize cartilage and bone, respectively. Briefly, animals were skinned, eviscerated and fixed for 2 days in 95 % ethanol. They were stained overnight at room temperature in 0.03 % (w/v) Alcian Blue solution (Sigma- Aldrich, A3157) containing 80 % ethanol and 20 % glacial acetic acid. Samples were destained in 95 % ethanol for 24 h followed by pre-clearing in 1 % KOH overnight at room temperature. Skeletons were then stained overnight in 0.005 % Alizarin Red solution (Sigma-Aldrich, A5533) containing 1 % KOH. A second round of clearing was performed by incubating tissues in 20 % glycerol/1 % KOH solution for 24 h. Finally, they were transferred to 50 % glycerol/50 % ethanol for photography. Skeletal preparations were imaged using a Zeiss Discovery.V12 Stereoscope.

Western blotting

Western blot analysis was carried out using the Turbo transfer method (BioRad) using standard procedures (Michaels et al., 2025). Briefly, mandibles from E13.5 indicated genotypes were micro-dissected and homogenized in RIPA buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Pierce). Proteins were quantified using BCA assay (Pierce) and 20 μg of protein was separated on a 4–20 % mini-Protean TGX stain-free gels (Bio-Rad) or 7.5 % TGX gels. Proteins were transferred to 0.45 μm PVDF membrane by Turbo blot transfer and imaged using stain free imaging (Bio-Rad). Blots were blocked with Everyblot blocking buffer (Bio-Rad) for 10 min. Primary antibodies were DMXL1 (Proteintech, 24413–1-AP, 1:1000 and Bethyl Laboratories, A304–685A) and ATP6V1B1 (Origene TA502519S, 1:1000). Blots were washed in 1X TBST and probed with the secondary anti-rabbit IgG-HRP conjugated antibody (Abcam; Ab6759, 1:3000) and developed using Clarity ECL substrate (Bio-Rad). Western blot images were quantified using the Image Lab Software (Bio-Rad). Band intensities for each lane were normalized to their respective total protein levels from stain free total protein gel images. The relative intensities were normalized to their respective total protein levels and analyzed ordinary one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison post-hoc test.

Dmxl1 in situ probes and wholemount in situ hybridization

Wholemount RNA in situ hybridization was performed using standard procedures. Dmxl1 transcript was amplified using specific primers 5′ TTCGTCAAGTTCAGGTGTCC3′, and 5′CCGAGTGGCAAGCTAAGTCA3’. The insert was cloned into PCR4-TOPO vector and the orientation of the insert was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. For the antisense probe, the vector was linearized with PmeI and probe was synthesized using T7 RNA polymerase. For the sense probe, the vector was linearized with NotI and probe was synthesized using T3 RNA polymerase. The experiment was repeated two times with at least n = 3 and consistent patterns were observed both times (total n = 8).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Quantitative real-time PCR was carried out using standard procedures as previously described (Iyyanar and Nazarali, 2017). RNA was isolated from the craniofacial region of E13.5 embryos and cDNA was synthesized using iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad). Dmxl1 transcripts were quantified using SYBR green assay (Bio-Rad) using specific primers: 5′TTCGGTCTGGTGGCAATTCA3′, and 5′ TGGCAGGTAAAAGCATGGACT3′ and normalized to gapdh levels with primers: 5′ CTTTGGCATTGTGGAAGGG3′ and 5′CCTCTCTTGCTGCAGTGTC3’. Relative quantification was carried using the delta cT method.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using the Microsoft Excel CHITEST function or GraphPad Prism10 (ANOVA with multiple comparisons testing or t-test).
