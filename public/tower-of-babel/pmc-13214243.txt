Resurgence of measles in Bangladesh amid a global upsurge: an urgent call for emergency public health response
Authors: Sadeed Hossain, Ahsan Ahmed, Mohammad Sorowar Hossain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Measles, a highly contagious yet vaccine-preventable disease, is resurging in Bangladesh following a global rise in cases. This study highlights recent epidemiological trends, including widespread transmission across most districts, rising pediatric vulnerability and increased mortality. Many affected children are either too young to be vaccinated or have missed their doses. Vaccination coverage has dropped in recent years due to pandemic-related disruptions, health system challenges, and policy delays, weakening community protection. Urgent steps are needed to improve routine vaccination, carry out catch-up campaigns, and strengthen surveillance to control the outbreak and protect children’s health.

To the Editor,

Measles, a highly contagious yet vaccine-preventable disease, is re-emerging as a major global public health concern. Recent outbreaks in the United States, several European countries, the Middle East, and South Asia indicate a substantial global resurgence of measles [1]. According to WHO and UNICEF, the WHO European Region reported 127,350 measles cases in 2024, the highest number recorded since 1997 [2]. The United States alone reported more than 1700 measles cases in 2025, compared with approximately 100 annual cases during the early 2000s [1]. Bangladesh also joins the spike in measles incidence along with many countries after years of progress toward elimination. This resurgence is largely attributed to disruptions in routine immunization during the COVID-19 pandemic, along with vaccine hesitancy, misinformation, and persistent health system gaps [1–3].

The World Health Organization (WHO) has confirmed that as of April 4, 2026, measles has spread to 58 districts in Bangladesh (~ 91% districts out of 64), with only six districts remaining unaffected [3]. Until 8 May 2026, 46,710 suspected measles cases and 6490 laboratory-confirmed cases were reported by the Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS), including 285 suspected measles-related deaths and 58 laboratory-confirmed deaths [4]. WHO reported an approximate case fatality rate of 0.9%, primarily among unvaccinated children under 2 years of age [3]. Alarmingly, WHO reported that the majority (79%) of reported cases occurred among children under 5 years of age, including children younger than 2 years (66%) and infants younger than 9 months (33%) [3]. Since infants under 9 months are generally ineligible for routine measles vaccination, these findings indicate substantial herd immunity failure and increased vulnerability among unvaccinated populations. WHO further reported that most deaths occurred among unvaccinated children younger than 2 years [3]. According to WHO, the outbreak is the result of missed vaccine doses, uneven coverage across regions, and disruptions in service delivery over recent years, which collectively reflect gaps in population-level immunity [3, 5].

In Bangladesh, despite strong performance under the Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI) historically, recent data show a sharp decline in measles–rubella vaccination coverage. Coverage of the first dose (MR1) dropped from 100% in 2022 to 56.5% in 2025, while the second dose (MR2) declined from 97.9% to 57.1% over the same period [6]. These levels fall far below the ≅ 95% threshold required for herd immunity, indicating a critical breach in population-level protection [7].

The EPI has been run by the government, aided by the global funders of vaccines under the Health, Population and Nutrition Sector Program (HPNSP) since 1998. From purchasing to administration—all are implemented by a 5-year cycle plan, and the 4th plan was between 2017 and 2022 extended till 2024 [8]. A recent report published in Science further highlighted that changes in vaccine procurement procedures following the 2024 political transition in Bangladesh contributed to delays in vaccine supply, cancellation of planned immunization campaigns, and interruptions in routine vaccination activities nationwide [1].

Multiple factors appear to be driving this resurgence, including missed immunization schedules, reduced drives and awareness, and notably, policy and operational delays.

The implications of rising measles incidence are substantial. Measles can lead to severe complications—pneumonia, encephalitis, diarrhea, secondary ear infection, blindness, and long-term immune suppression, contributing to increased morbidity and mortality among children [3]. WHO has warned that the current outbreak poses a high national and regional risk due to ongoing transmission, immunity gaps, cross-border population movement, and the concentration of susceptible children [3]. In addition, Bangladesh’s densely populated urban settings and strained healthcare infrastructure may further amplify transmission and worsen clinical outcomes [1].

Urgent action is required. Strengthening routine immunization services, implementing targeted catch-up campaigns even under the usual 9-month age bar, and restoring high vaccination coverage must be prioritized. WHO has already recommended maintaining at least 95% coverage with two doses of measles-containing vaccine and strengthening surveillance in border and high-transmission areas [3]. Surveillance systems should be enhanced to detect early and respond rapidly, supported by digital tools and community-level reporting. In parallel, policymakers must ensure a clear and coordinated strategy to sustain immunization programs and prevent further coverage gaps.

We urge the Bangladesh Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, WHO, UNICEF, and Gavi to support emergency catch-up vaccination campaigns, restore uninterrupted vaccine supply chains, strengthen surveillance systems, and ensure sustainable immunization financing to prevent further morbidity and mortality among children.

Bangladesh has previously demonstrated success in controlling vaccine-preventable diseases. However, the current decline in immunization coverage represents a critical warning. Without immediate and sustained intervention, the country risks prolonged outbreaks with significant consequences for child health and health system resilience.
