State-space algorithm for detecting the nanohertz gravitational wave background 2024 State-space algorithm for detecting the nanohertz gravitational wave background – G.2
Tom Kimpson, Andrew Melatos, Joseph O’Leary, Julian B. Carlin, Robin J. Evans, William Moran, Tong Cheunchitra, Wenhao Dong, Liam Dunn, Julian Greentree, Nicholas J. O’Neill, Sofia Suvorova, Kok Hong Thong, Andrés F. Vargas

Abstract
The stochastic gravitational wave background (SGWB) can be observed in the nanohertz band using a pulsar timing array (PTA). Here a computationally efficient state-space framework is developed for analysing SGWB data, in which the stochastic gravitational wave strain at Earth is tracked with a non-linear Kalman filter and separated simultaneously from intrinsic, achromatic pulsar spin wandering. The filter is combined with a nested sampler to estimate the parameters of the model, and to calculate a Bayes factor for selecting between models with and without a SGWB. The procedure extends previous state-space formulations of PTA data analysis applied to individually resolvable binary black hole sources. The performance of the new algorithm is tested on synthetic data from the first International PTA Mock Data Challenge. It is shown that the algorithm distinguishes a SGWB from pure noise for A gw ≥ 3 × 10 − 14 A_{\rm gw}\geq 3\times 10^{-14} , where A gw A_{\rm gw} denotes the standard normalization factor for a power spectral density with power-law exponent − 13 / 3 -13/3 . Additional, systematic validation tests are also performed with synthetic data generated independently by adjusting the injected parameters to cover astrophysically plausible ranges. Full posterior distributions are recovered and tested for accuracy. The state-space procedure is memory-light and evaluates the likelihood for a standard-sized PTA dataset in ≲ 10 − 1 \lesssim 10^{-1} s without optimization on a standard central processing unit.

State-space representation

Figure 1
:
Diagram of the general state-space structure used in this paper. A discrete, temporal sequence of latent (hidden), state variables,

In this section we outline the general form of the state-space framework used in this paper and its underlying assumptions. In Section
3
we explain how the general framework maps onto the specific case of PTA data analysis and the search for a stochastic GW background.

The state-space representation describes a sequence of hidden or latent states,
\bm{x}(t)
, which are not directly observed, and are related to a set of noisy measurements,
\bm{y}(t)
. The hidden states form a Markov sequence and evolve according to a first-order stochastic differential equation

\dot{\bm{x}}(t)=\bm{f}\left[t,\bm{x}(t)\right]+\bm{w}(t)\,,

(1)

where
\bm{f}
is an arbitrary function which governs the deterministic dynamics, and
\bm{w}(t)
is a stochastic process. Importantly, the “noise” in
\bm{w}(t)
is physical; it is intrinsic to the dynamical system and does not include measurement errors, for example. The hidden states are related to the measurements recorded at a detector via a measurement equation, viz.

\bm{y}(t)=\bm{h}\left[t,\bm{x}(t)\right]+\bm{v}(t)\,.

(2)

In Equation (
2
),
\bm{h}
is an arbitrary deterministic function, and
\bm{v}(t)
is a stochastic process associated with the measurement procedure which includes measurement errors and is distinct from the stochastic process which acts on the states,
\bm{w}(t)
. Figure
1
presents a diagram of the state-space structure for a discrete subsequence of states and measurements, where the subscript
k
indexes the timestep, viz.
\bm{x}_{k}=\bm{x}(t_{k})
. One moves horizontally from
\bm{x}_{k-1}
through
\bm{x}_{k}
to
\bm{x}_{k+1}
via Equation (
1
). One moves vertically from
\bm{x}_{k}
to
\bm{y}_{k}
via Equation (
2
).

Typically, one wants to solve the inverse problem of estimating the discrete sequence of
T
hidden states
\bm{x}_{1:T}=\{\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{T}\}
given the discrete sequence of measurements
\bm{y}_{1:T}=\{\bm{y}_{1},\dots,\bm{y}_{T}\}
. In the Bayesian sense, one wishes to compute the joint posterior
p(\bm{x}_{1:T}|\bm{y}_{1:T})
. However, computing the joint posterior becomes computationally intractable, as
T
increases, and the dimensionality of the posterior increases in tandem. Instead, state-space algorithms such as the Kalman filter
(
Kalman 1960
)
, seek to compute at each timestep
k
the marginalised posterior
p(\bm{x}_{k}|\bm{y}_{1:k})
. One can show that the marginalised posterior at
k
satisfies
(
Särkkä 2013a
;
Gurajala
et al. 2021
)

p(\bm{x}_{k}|\bm{y}_{1:k})\propto p(\bm{y}_{k}|\bm{x}_{1:k})\int d\bm{x}_{k-1}p(\bm{x}_{k}|\bm{x}_{k-1})p(\bm{x}_{k-1}|\bm{y}_{1:k-1})\,.

(3)

Equation (
1
) determines
p(\bm{x}_{k}|\bm{x}_{k-1})
. Equation (
2
) determines
p(\bm{y}_{k}|\bm{x}_{1:k})
. The Kalman filter is the optimal estimator of
p({\bm{x}}_{k}|{\bm{y}}_{1:k})
, in the sense that it minimizes the mean square error between the estimated and true state sequences, if
{\bm{f}}
and
{\bm{h}}
are linear in
{\bm{x}}(t)
, irrespective of the statistics of
{\bm{w}}(t)
and
{\bm{v}}(t)
. It is also optimal, when
{\bm{f}}
and
{\bm{h}}
are nonlinear, as long as the statistics of
{\bm{w}}(t)
and
{\bm{v}}(t)
are Gaussian. Importantly, and counterintuitively, the Kalman filter distinguishes the physical noise
{\bm{w}}(t)
from the measurement noise
{\bm{v}}(t)
, even if both noises share the same statistics. This is a key advantage in PTA data analysis, where the challenge is to distinguish stochastic spin wandering intrinsic to the pulsar, which enters through Equation (
1
), from stochastic modulations of the pulse arrival times caused by the stochastic GW background, which enter through Equation (
2
). This key point is discussed further below.

3
State-space formulation of PTA analysis

In this section we formulate PTA detection of the stochastic GW background as a state-space problem. There are
N
pulsars in the array, labelled
1\leq n\leq N
, whose independent rotational states define an
N
-dimensional state space. The
n
-th pulsar’s spin frequency
f_{\rm p}^{(n)}(t)
, as measured in the local, freely-falling rest frame of the pulsar’s centre of mass, evolves according to a stochastic differential equation of motion. In this paper, we copy an approach adopted successfully elsewhere
(
Meyers et al. 2021b
;
Vargas &
Melatos 2023
;
Kimpson
et al. 2024a
;
Kimpson
et al. 2024b
)
and assume on phenomenological grounds that
f_{\rm p}(t)
obeys a mean-reverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. A short review of the model is presented in Section
3.1
; details regarding its justification may be found in the foregoing references. The spin frequency
f_{\rm p}^{(n)}(t)
is related to the pulse frequency measured by an observer at Earth,
f_{\rm m}^{(n)}(t)
, via a measurement equation, which involves the time-varying random amplitude
a^{(n)}(t)
of the stochastic GW background at the location of the
n
-th pulsar as well as the instrumental noise in the PTA. The measurement equation relating
f^{(n)}_{\rm p}(t)
to
f^{(n)}_{\rm m}(t)
via
a^{(n)}(t)
is presented in Section
3.2
, supplemented by stochastic differential equations describing the approximate evolution of
a^{(n)}(t)
for
1\leq n\leq N
, which are derived rigorously via a generalized Hellings-Downs argument in Appendix
A
. The dynamical equations of motion in Section
3.1
, and the measurement equations in Section
3.2
, match exactly the mathematical state-space structure presented in Section
2
. That is, given a temporal sequence of measurements
f_{\rm m}^{(n)}(t_{k})
labelled by the integer
k
, one can use a non-linear Kalman filter
(
Kalman 1960
;
Zarchan &
Musoff 2000
)
to estimate the most likely sequence of hidden states
f_{\rm p}^{(n)}(t_{k})
and
a^{(n)}(t_{k})
, as well as the most likely values for the model parameters in the problem, e.g. the root-mean-square amplitude of the stochastic GW background. We summarise how the PTA problem maps onto the state-space structure in Section
3.3
. We summarise the parameters of the model in Section
3.4
and set out the connection to the Kalman filter in Section
4
and Appendix
B
.

To maintain consistency with previous work
(
Meyers et al. 2021b
;
Kimpson
et al. 2024a
;
Kimpson
et al. 2024b
)
, and in order to validate the approach in its simplest form, the analysis in this paper operates on

instead of a sequence of pulse times of arrival (TOAs). When applying the algorithm to real astronomical data it will be necessary to modify it to accept pulse TOAs, a subtle problem which is solved in a forthcoming paper.

3.1
Spin evolution

In this paper, we assume that the rest frame spin frequency of the
n
-th pulsar evolves according to a mean-reverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, described by a Langevin equation with a time-dependent drift term

\frac{df_{\rm p}^{(n)}}{dt}=-\gamma^{(n)}[f_{\rm p}^{(n)}-f_{\rm em}^{(n)}(t)]+\dot{f}_{\rm em}^{(n)}(t)+\xi^{(n)}(t)\ ,

(4)

where

t

is a damping constant whose reciprocal specifies the mean-reversion timescale, and

is a white noise stochastic process which satisfies

\displaystyle\langle\xi^{(n)}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=0\ ,

(5)

\displaystyle\langle\xi^{(n)}(t)\xi^{(n^{\prime})}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=[\sigma^{(n)}]^{2}\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\ .

(6)

In Equation (
6
),

parameterises the noise amplitude and produces characteristic root mean square fluctuations
\approx\sigma^{(n)}/[\gamma^{(n)}]^{1/2}
in

(

. The factor

(7)

but not others, e.g. PSR J1909

. We do not tune the exact form of the implied phase residual PSD while performing the validation tests in this paper for two reasons. First, when analysing real astronomical data, it is straightforward to modify Equations (

) approximate formally the modulations imprinted on PTA TOAs by the stochastic GW background as an Ornstein-Uhlnebeck process, following the derivation in Appendix
A.3
.

We emphasise that Equations (
16
)–(
20
) represent an inference model, specified so as to reproduce accurately the statistics of a stochastic GW background from SMBHBs, and to circumvent the non-identifiability of the
7M
SMBHB source parameters. The procedure for generating synthetic data with which to validate the inference model does not use Equations (
16
)–(
20
), as explained in Section
5
. Moreover, there exists a well-defined connection between the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process in Equations (
16
)–(
20
) and the PSD (e.g. broken power law) used in traditional PTA analyses. This connection is derived in Appendix
C
. In short, one finds that Equations (
16
)–(
20
) correspond to a PSD in the timing residuals which goes as
f^{-4}
at high frequencies and
f^{-2}
at low frequencies.

3.3
State-space structure

In this section we relate explicitly the dynamical equations for PTAs presented in Section
3.1
and Section
3.2
to the general state space structure presented in Section
2
. We specify the latent and measurement state vectors,
\bm{x}(t)
and
\bm{y}(t)
, the latent state dynamical equations involving
\bm{f}[t,\bm{x}(t)]
and
\bm{w}(t)
in Equation (
1
), and the measurement process involving
\bm{h}\left[t,\bm{x}(t)\right]
and
\bm{v}(t)
, in Equation (

).

On the dynamics side of the problem, the latent state is a vector of length
2N

\bm{x}(t)=\left[f_{\rm p}^{(1)}(t),\dots,f_{\rm p}^{(N)}(t),a^{(1)}(t),\dots,a^{(N)}(t)\right]\,.

(22)

The deterministic function
\bm{f}[t,\bm{x}(t)]
is set by Equation (
4
) and takes the form

\bm{f}[t,\bm{x}(t)]=\bm{A}\bm{x}(t)+\bm{B}(t)\,.

(23)

In Equation (
23
),
\bm{A}
is a diagonal
2N\times 2N
block matrix,

\bm{A}=\begin{pmatrix}-\bm{\gamma}_{\rm p}&0\\
0&-\bm{\gamma}_{\rm a}\end{pmatrix}\ ,

(24)

with

\bm{\gamma}_{\rm p}=\text{diag}\left[\gamma^{(1)},...,\gamma^{(N)}\right]\,,

(25)

and

\bm{\gamma}_{\rm a}=\gamma_{\rm a}I_{N}\,,

(26)

where
I_{N}
denotes the identity matrix of dimension
N
. In Equation (
23
),
\bm{B}(t)
is a control vector of length
2N

\bm{B}(t)=\left[B^{(1)}(t),\dots,B^{(N)}(t),0,\dots,0\right]\,,

(27)

with

B^{(n)}(t)=\gamma^{(n)}f_{\rm em}^{(n)}(t)+\dot{f}_{\rm em}^{(n)}(t)

(28)

The stochastic process associated with the latent state dynamics is

\bm{w}(t)=\left[\xi^{(1)}(t),\dots,\xi^{(N)}(t),\Xi_{\rm a}^{(1)}(t),\dots,\Xi_{\rm a}^{(N)}(t)\right]\,,

(29)

where
\xi^{(n)}(t)
is defined by Equation (
6
),
and
\Xi_{\rm a}^{(n)}(t)
is defined by Equation (
18
).

On the measurement side of the problem, the data are packaged in a vector of length
N
,

\bm{y}(t)=\left[f_{\rm m}^{(1)}(t),\dots,f_{\rm m}^{(N)}(t)\right]\,.

(30)

The deterministic function
\bm{h}\left[t,\bm{x}(t)\right]=\bm{h}\left[\bm{x}(t)\right]
, is set by Equation (
8
) and takes the form

\displaystyle\bm{h}\left[\bm{x}(t)\right]=\begin{pmatrix}\left[1-a^{(1)}(t)\right]f_{\rm p}^{(1)}(t)\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\left[1-a^{(2)}(t)\right]f_{\rm p}^{(2)}(t)\\
\vdots\\
\left[1-a^{(N)}(t)\right]f_{\rm p}^{(N)}(t)\\
\end{pmatrix}\,,

(31)

The random variate associated with the measurement process, which includes measurement errors, is given by

\bm{v}(t)=\left[\varepsilon^{(1)}(t),\dots,\varepsilon^{(N)}(t)\right]\,,

(32)

where
\varepsilon^{(n)}(t)
is defined by Equation (
10
).

D
. The glossary highlights the similarities and differences with the symbols used in traditional PTA analyses for the reader’s convenience.

3.4
Parameters of the model

The state-space model described in Sections
3.1
and
3.2
comprises
4N
parameters, that are specific to the pulsars in the array, viz.

\bm{\theta}_{\rm psr}=\left\{\gamma^{(n)},\sigma^{(n)},f_{\rm em}^{(n)}(t_{1}),\dot{f}_{\rm em}^{(n)}(t_{1})\right\}_{1\leq n\leq N}\ .

(33)

It also comprises two parameters, that are specific to the stochastic GW background, viz.

\bm{\theta}_{\rm gw}=\left\{h_{\rm a},\gamma_{\rm a}\right\}\ ,

(34)

where we define
h_{\rm a}=\langle h^{2}\rangle^{1/2}
. The parameters
h_{\rm a}
and
\gamma_{\rm a}
can be respectively related to the amplitude of the PSD and the minimum GW frequency in a traditional PTA analysis, as explained in Section
3.2
and Appendix
C
. The complete set of
4N+2
parameters is denoted by
\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{\rm psr}\cup\bm{\theta}_{\rm gw}
. They are inferred from the TOA-derived data
f_{\rm m}^{(n)}(t)
by combining a Kalman filter with a nested sampler, as described in Section
4
. The identifiable two-member set
\bm{\theta}_{\rm a}
parameterising the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck approximation to the stochastic GW background is smaller than the non-identifiable set of
7M
SMBHB source parameters used to generate synthetic data by superposition, as discussed in Section
3.2
. None of the
4N+2
parameters
\bm{\theta}
vary with time in this paper.

4
Bayesian inference with a Kalman filter

In this section we briefly review how state-space algorithms for PTA data analysis estimate the parameters of the inference model,
\bm{\theta}
, and calculate the model evidence
\mathcal{Z}
. State-space algorithms use a two-step inference procedure whereby a Kalman filter
(
Kalman 1960
)
tracks the intrinsic achromatic pulsar spin wandering simultaneously with the modulation induced by a gravitational wave. The Kalman filter then provides a likelihood for Bayesian inference algorithms such as nested sampling.

A Kalman filter recovers a temporal sequence of stochastically evolving system state variables,
\bm{x}(t)
, which are not directly observed, given a temporal sequence of noisy measurements,
\bm{y}(t)
. Previous state-space analyses of single-source PTA experiments
(
Kimpson
et al. 2024a
;
Kimpson
et al. 2024b
, e.g.)
and neutron star spin wandering
(
Meyers
et al. 2021a
;
Meyers et al. 2021b
;
Melatos et al. 2021
, e.g.)
employ a linear Kalman filter. In this paper the non-linear relation between
{\bm{X}}(t)
and
{\bm{Y}}(t)
in Equation (
8
) necessitates the use of the extended (non-linear) Kalman filter
(
Zarchan &
Musoff 2000
, EKF, see, e.g.)
. Other non-linear filters are also suitable, such as the unscented Kalman filter
(
Wan & Van
Der Merwe 2000
)
or the particle filter
(
Simon 2006
)
.

Implementation of the EKF for the PTA state-space model in Section
3
, including the full set of recursion relations, is presented in Appendix
B
. At each discrete timestep indexed by
1\leq k\leq t_{N_{t}}
, the Kalman filter returns an estimate of the state variables,
\hat{\bm{x}}_{j}=\hat{\bm{x}}(t_{j})
, and the covariance of those estimates,
{\bm{P}}_{j}=\langle{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{j}{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{j}^{\rm T}\rangle
, where the superscript T denotes the matrix transpose. The filter tracks the error in its predictions of
\bm{x}_{j}
by converting
{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{j}
into predicted measurements
{\bm{\hat{y}}}_{j}
via Equation (
8
) and comparing with the actual, noisy measurements
{\bm{y}}_{j}
. It defines a residual
\bm{\epsilon}_{j}=\bm{y}_{j}-\hat{\bm{y}}_{j}
, which is sometimes termed the innovation. The EKF also calculates the uncertainty in
\bm{\epsilon}_{j}
via the innovation covariance
\bm{S}_{i}=\langle\bm{\epsilon}_{j}\bm{\epsilon}_{j}^{\rm T}\rangle
. At each timestep, the EKF returns a Gaussian log-likelihood

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{L}_{i}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(N\log 2\pi+\log\left|\bm{S}_{i}\right|+\bm{\epsilon}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{S}_{i}^{-1}\bm{\epsilon}_{i}\right)\ .

(35)

The total log-likelihood for the entire sequence
1\leq i\leq J
is

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{J}\log\mathcal{L}_{i}\ .

(36)

Given
{\bm{y}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{y}}_{J}
,
\mathcal{L}
is a function of the estimates
{\bm{\hat{\theta}}}
of the parameters passed to the Kalman filter, i.e.
\mathcal{L}
=
\mathcal{L}(\bm{y}|\bm{\hat{\theta}})
.

The likelihood returned by the EKF for a particular
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
is used to estimate the posterior distribution of
\bm{\theta}
by Bayes’s Rule,

p(\bm{\theta}|\bm{y})=\frac{\mathcal{L}(\bm{y}|\bm{\theta})\pi(\bm{\theta})}{\mathcal{Z}}\ ,

(37)

where
\pi(\bm{\theta})
is the prior distribution on
\bm{\theta}
, and
\mathcal{Z}
is the marginalised likelihood or evidence,

\mathcal{Z}=\int d\bm{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\bm{y}|\bm{\theta})\pi(\bm{\theta})\ .

(38)

In this paper, the calculation of
p(\bm{\theta}|\bm{y})
and
\mathcal{Z}
proceeds via a nested sampling algorithm
(
Skilling 2006
;
Ashton
et al. 2022
)
. Multiple nested sampling algorithms and computational libraries exist
(
Feroz &
Hobson 2008
;
Feroz
et al. 2009
;
Handley
et al. 2015
;
Speagle 2020
;
Buchner 2021
)
. In gravitational wave research it is common to use the
dynesty
sampler
(
Speagle 2020
)
via the
Bilby

(
Ashton &
Talbot 2021
)
front-end library. We follow this precedent and use
Bilby
in this paper.

Appendix
B
outlines how to discretise the continuous dynamical and measurement equations in Section
3
, specifically Equations (
4
)–(
7
), (
16
)–(
20
), and (
8
)–(
11
). Appendix
B
goes on to demonstrate how to solve the discretised equations using an EKF. Appendix
E
summarizes the state-space analysis workflow of the combined Kalman filter and nested sampler. We refer the reader to
Kimpson
et al. 2024a
for additional details on the state-space analysis workflow.

6
Computational cost

The state-space algorithm in this paper, coupled with a generic nested sampler, runs roughly as fast as a traditional PTA analysis. Hence it is suitable for running in tandem as a cross-check. Moreover, the state-space algorithm scales favourably with increasing data volume, i.e. the number of pulsars in the array, or the number of time samples. In this section we benchmark the prototype software implementation of the state-space PTA analysis scheme in this paper, as a rough practical guide. We also discuss briefly the theoretical, asymptotic scaling of the computational cost as a function of
N
, the number of pulsars in the PTA, and
N_{t}
, the number of TOAs. We compare the asymptotic scaling with that of traditional PTA analysis methods, especially standard methods based on Gaussian processes
(
van
Haasteren & Vallisneri 2014
, see also Appendix
G
)
.

In this paper, as a first pass, we implement a prototype of the Kalman filter, without optimisation. As a benchmark, a single likelihood evaluation implemented in Python for the synthetic data presented in Section
F.1
takes
T_{\mathcal{L}}\approx 0.05\,{\rm s}
of central processing unit (CPU) time on a 2.6 GHz Intel Core i7 processor. Preliminary empirical tests yield
T_{\cal L}\propto N^{1.5}N_{t}
roughly for
1\leq N\leq 50
. This unoptimized scaling will be refined in future work. Translation into more performant languages such as C++
(
Andrist
et al. 2020
)
or Julia
(
Bezanson et al. 2012
)
, the use of Python pre-compilation libraries such as Numba
(
Lam
et al. 2015
)
or JAX
(
Bradbury
et al. 2018
)
, or additional optimisation
(
Gorelick &
Ozsvald 2014
)
, would reduce
T_{\mathcal{L}}
.

It is important to understand how
T_{\mathcal{L}}
scales with both
N_{t}
and
N
. Regarding the former, the theoretical time complexity of the Kalman filter (i.e. its asymptotic behaviour), is
\mathcal{O}(N_{t})
, because the Kalman filter is an iterative algorithm. Regarding the latter, the rate-limiting step is set by matrix multiplication, e.g. in Equations (
70
) and (
73
) in the Kalman filter’s predict and update steps respectively; see Appendix
B.1
. The dimension of the Kalman filter matrices, e.g. Equations (
68
)–(
77
), is set by
N
. Matrix multiplication without optimization scales as
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})

(
Daum 2021
)
. Modern routines for matrix multiplication reduce the complexity to
\sim\mathcal{O}(N^{2.4})

(
Trefethen &
Bau 1997
)
. Advanced techniques to further reduce the complexity include CKMS recursion
(
Kailath
et al. 2000
)
or low-rank perturbation methods
(
Eftychios A. Pnevmatikakis &
Paninski 2014
)
. We refer the reader to
Raitoharju &
Piché 2019
for further information about complexity reduction for the Kalman filter. The memory complexity of the Kalman filter is
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
, independent of
N_{t}
, because the algorithm is iterative. A naive implementation of Gaussian process regression
(
Rasmussen &
Williams 2005
)
has time complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(N_{t}^{3}\right)
and memory complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(N_{t}^{2}\right)

In traditional PTA analyses, approximation methods have been developed to improve the above scalings. They take advantage of the specific structure of the PTA covariance matrices and approximate the covariance function as a low rank matrix via a sum over
q
Fourier modes
(
van
Haasteren & Vallisneri 2014
, e.g. Sections 3 & 5, )
. These low-rank methods have time complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(q^{2}N_{t}\right)
and memory complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(qN_{t}\right)

7
Conclusion

In this paper we demonstrate how to apply state-space methods for PTA data analysis to the stochastic GW background. Previous work on this topic focuses on individually resolvable SMBHBs
(
Kimpson
et al. 2024a
;
Kimpson
et al. 2024b
)
. This paper applies state-space methods to the linear superposition of a large number of SMBHBs, which form a stochastic GW background. The SMBHBs are not identifiable individually in the formal sense borrowed from engineering applications
(
Bellman &
Åström 1970
)
, but it is shown that their summed influence on the measured spin frequency of the
n
-th PTA pulsar can be modelled approximately through a random variable
a^{(n)}(t)
, which obeys a mean-reverting, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process; see Equations (
16
)–(
20
) and Appendix
A
. Simultaneously, the rotational state of the
n
-th pulsar evolves according to another mean-reverting, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process in the absence of a stochastic GW background but in the presence of achromatic timing noise (i.e. spin wandering); see Equations (
4
)–(
7
) and Section
3.1

(
Melatos et al. 2020
;
Meyers et al. 2021b
;
Meyers
et al. 2021a
;
Vargas &
Melatos 2023
;
O’Neill
et al. 2024
)
. The measurement equations (
8
)–(
10
) and dynamical equations, Equations (
4
)–(
7
) and (
16
)–(
20
), are in the correct mathematical form to be analysed by a Kalman filter, which inverts TOA data to infer the most probable state sequence
a^{(n)}(t)
. The Kalman filter is combined with a nested sampler to estimate the posterior distributions of the parameters of the state-space model,
\bm{\theta}_{\rm psr}
and
\bm{\theta}_{\rm gw}
, with a particular focus on the elements of
\bm{\theta}_{\rm gw}
, namely
h_{\rm a}
and
\gamma_{\rm a}
. The associated Bayesian evidence
\mathcal{Z}
of the model is also calculated.

The state-space model is tested on synthetic data obtained from the first IPTA MDC. We start by considering a stochastic background with amplitude
A_{\rm gw}=5\times 10^{-14}
, observed by
N=37
pulsars with
T_{\rm obs}=5\,{\rm years}
and
T_{\rm cad}=2
weeks. The detection of the stochastic GW background with a state-space model is formulated in terms of Bayesian model selection. At
A_{\rm gw}=5\times 10^{-14}
, the background is detected with strong support, with log Bayes factor
\ln\beta=89
. It is found that the inference scheme can successfully detect injected signals with
\beta\geq 10
for
A_{\rm gw}\geq 3\times 10^{-14}
. The state-space model successfully converges to a well-behaved, unimodal posterior which does not rail against the prior bounds and matches the injected parameters used to generate the synthetic data.

State-space methods for PTA data analysis complement traditional techniques. Traditional techniques average over the ensemble of possible timing noise realisations, to form and estimate the phase residual PSD. In contrast, state-space methods track the actual, astrophysical, time-ordered realisation of the timing noise in every PTA pulsar by harnessing the adaptive gain of a Kalman filter. The noise model utilised in state-space methods is related, but different, to the noise model in traditional techniques. Traditional techniques assume a stationary Gaussian process described by an ensemble-averaged, power-law PSD, whose amplitude and exponent are adjustable. In this paper, the state-space model assumes a mean-reverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process also maps onto a stationary Gaussian process, whose PSD is a power law
\propto f^{-4}
at high frequencies and
\propto f^{-2}
at low frequencies, and whose amplitude and exponent can be adjusted by modifying the form of the damping term and Langevin driver in Equation (
4
); we refer the reader to Appendix
C
for a discussion on the PSD of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process and a comparison with the GW PSD used in traditional analyses. We also refer the interested reader to Appendix B in
Kimpson
et al. 2024a
for a pedagogical comparison of state-space and traditional PTA analysis methods. An in-depth study clarifying the similarities and difference between various approaches promises to be a fruitful avenue of future work.

It will be valuable in future to generalize the method in this paper in at least two ways.

1.

Real PTA analyses operate on data which contain both a stochastic GW background and multiple, individually resolvable SMBHB sources. State-space analysis schemes for PTA data now exist for both the stochastic GW background and individual SMBHBs, as set out in this paper and by
Kimpson
et al. 2024a
;
Kimpson
et al. 2024b
. It is feasible to combine the two state-space approaches and separate the two signal contributions, as long as the individual SMBHBs are few enough to avoid formal identifiability obstacles
(
Bellman &
Åström 1970
)
.

2.

Published state-space analyses operate on a sequence of pulse frequencies
f_{\rm m}^{(n)}(t)
, instead of a sequence of pulse TOAs, as happens in traditional PTA analyses
(
Zhu et al. 2016
;
Chen &
Wang 2022
;
Agazie
et al. 2023a
;
Antoniadis
et al. 2023a
;
Reardon
et al. 2023
;
Xu
et al. 2023
;
Arzoumanian
et al. 2023
;
Antoniadis
et al. 2023b
, e.g.)
. The generalisation of the state-space framework to accept pulse TOAs, necessary for application to real astrophysical data, is a subtle task and will be presented in a forthcoming paper.

,

\displaystyle\langle a^{(n)}_{\rm OU}(t)a^{(n^{\prime})}_{\rm OU}(t^{\prime})\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{[\sigma^{(n,n^{\prime})}_{\rm a}]^{2}}{2\gamma^{(n)}_{\rm a}}

\displaystyle\times\left[e^{-\gamma^{(n)}_{\rm a}|t-t^{\prime}|}-e^{-\gamma^{(n)}_{\rm a}(t+t^{\prime})}\right]\ .

(63)

In general the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process is not stationary, as evidenced by the
t+t^{\prime}
term in Equation (
63
); the statistics are not exclusively a function of the lag,
|t-t^{\prime}|
. However, if the process
a^{(n)}(t)
starts in the remote past, it is approximately stationary for
t,t^{\prime}\gg[\gamma_{\rm a}^{(n)}]^{-1}
, consistent with standard PTA analyses
(
Maggiore 2018
;
Arzoumanian
et al. 2018
;
Arzoumanian
et al. 2023
, e.g.)
. The stationary component of Equation (
63
) for
t,t^{\prime}\gg[\gamma_{\rm a}^{(n)}]^{-1}
is given by

\displaystyle\langle a^{(n)}_{\rm OU}(t)a^{(n^{\prime})}_{\rm OU}(t^{\prime})\rangle\approx\frac{[\sigma^{(n,n^{\prime})}_{\rm a}]^{2}}{2\gamma^{(n)}_{\rm a}}e^{-\gamma_{\rm a}|t-t^{\prime}|}\ .

(64)

By comparing Equation (
61
) with Equation (
64
) it is evident that the ensemble-averaged statistics of
a^{(n)}(t)
from
M\gg 1
SMBHBs can be reproduced approximately by an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with

\displaystyle\gamma^{(n)}_{\rm a}

\displaystyle=2.7\times 10^{-2}\,\Omega_{\rm min}\,,

(65)

\displaystyle\left[\sigma^{(n,n^{\prime})}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\langle h^{2}\right\rangle}{12}\gamma_{\rm a}^{(n)}\rho\left[\theta^{(n,n^{\prime})}\right]\,.

(66)

In Equation (
65
)
\gamma^{(n)}_{\rm a}
is the same for all
n
. It is written as
\gamma_{\rm a}
without the superscript in the main text, e.g. in Section
3.2
.

Appendix B
Extended Kalman filter

In this appendix, we describe the extended Kalman filter (EKF) algorithm used in this paper. The Kalman filter ingests a temporal sequence of noisy measurements,
\bm{Y}(t)
, and recovers a temporal sequence of stochastically evolving state variables,
\bm{X}(t)
, which are not directly observed. Previous state-space analyses of individual continuous GW sources have used a linear Kalman filter
(
Kimpson
et al. 2024a
;
Kimpson
et al. 2024b
, e.g.)
. In this paper the non-linear relation between
{\bm{X}}(t)
and
{\bm{Y}}(t)
necessitates the use of the EKF
(
Zarchan &
Musoff 2000
)
. General recursion relations for the discrete-time EKF are written down for an arbitrary dynamical system in Appendix
B.1
. The mapping onto the specific continuous-time state-space model in Section
3
is written down in Appendix
B.2
. The reader who wishes to reproduce the results in this paper can do so entirely by using the equations in Appendices
B.1
and
B.2
.

B.1
General recursion equations

The EKF operates on temporally discrete, noisy measurements
\bm{Y}_{k}
, which are related to a set of unobservable discrete system states
\bm{X}_{k}
, via a general differentiable function, viz.

\bm{Y}_{k}=h\left(\bm{X}_{k}\right)+\bm{v}_{k}\ ,

(67)

where
h(\bm{X}_{k})
is a differentiable measurement function acting on the state,
\bm{v}_{k}\sim{\cal N}(0,\bm{R}_{k})

is a zero-mean Gaussian measurement noise variable with covariance
\bm{R}_{k}
, and the subscript
k
labels the time-step. Similarly, the underlying states evolve as

\bm{X}_{k}=f\left(\bm{X}_{k-1}\right)+\bm{w}_{k-1}\ ,

(68)

where
f(\bm{X}_{k})
is a differentiable state transition function, and
\bm{w}_{k}\sim{\cal N}(0,\bm{Q}_{k})
is a zero-mean Gaussian process noise variable, with covariance
\bm{Q}_{k}
.

As with the linear Kalman filter, the EKF is a recursive estimator involving a “predict” stage and an “update” stage. The predict stage predicts
\hat{\bm{X}}_{k|k-1}
, the estimate of the state at step
k
, given the state estimate from step
k-1
. Specifically, the predict step proceeds as

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{X}}_{k|k-1}

\displaystyle=f\left(\hat{\bm{X}}_{k-1|k-1}\right)\,,

(69)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{P}}_{k|k-1}

\displaystyle=\bm{F}_{k}\hat{\bm{P}}_{k-1|k-1}\bm{F}_{k}^{\intercal}+\bm{Q}_{k}\ ,

(70)

where
\hat{\bm{P}}_{k|k-1}
is the covariance of the prediction and
\bm{F}_{k}
is the Jacobian

\bm{F}_{k}=\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{X}}\right\rvert_{\hat{\bm{X}}_{k|k-1}}\ .

(71)

The predict stage is independent of the measurements. The information in
\bm{Y}_{k}
is included by updating the prediction during the update stage as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{\epsilon}_{k}

\displaystyle=\bm{Y}_{k}-h\left(\hat{\bm{X}}_{k|k-1}\right)\ ,

(72)

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{k}

\displaystyle=\bm{H}_{k}\hat{\bm{P}}_{k|k-1}\bm{H}_{k}^{\intercal}+\bm{R}_{k}\ ,

(73)

\displaystyle\bm{K}_{k}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{P}}_{k|k-1}\bm{H}_{k}^{\intercal}\bm{S}_{k}^{-1}\ ,

(74)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{X}}_{k|k}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{X}}_{k|k-1}+\bm{K}_{k}\bm{\epsilon}_{k}\ ,

(75)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{P}}_{k|k}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{I}-\bm{K}_{k}\bm{H}_{k}\right)\hat{\bm{P}}_{k|k-1}\,,

(76)

where
\bm{H}_{k}
is the Jacobian

\bm{H}_{k}=\left.\frac{\partial h}{\partial\bm{X}}\right\rvert_{\hat{\bm{X}}_{k|k-1}}\ .

(77)

For a full review of the EKF, including its derivation, we refer the reader to
Zarchan &
Musoff 2000
.

To apply the EKF in practice means specifying the vectors
\bm{X}_{k}

and
\bm{Y}_{k}
, the two functions
h(\bm{X}_{k})
and
f(\bm{X}_{k})
, their associated Jacobians
\bm{H}_{k}
and
\bm{F}_{k}
, and the covariance matrices
\bm{Q}_{k}
and
\bm{R}_{k}
. In Section
B.2
we describe how these components are determined for the state-space model in Section
3
.

B.2
Application to a PTA analysis

We apply the Kalman recursion relations in Appendix
B.1
to the state-space model of a PTA composed of
N
pulsars described in Section
3
as follows.

Let us define for convenience the ephemeris-subtracted variables

\displaystyle f_{\rm p}^{*(n)}=f_{\rm p}^{(n)}-f_{\rm em}^{(n)}(t)\,,

(78)

\displaystyle f_{\rm m}^{*(n)}=f_{\rm m}^{(n)}-f_{\rm em}^{\diamond(n)}(t)\,,

(79)

where
f_{\rm em}^{(n)}(t)
is given by Equation (
7
) and
f_{\rm em}^{\diamond(n)}(t)
is based on the ephemeris returned by
tempo2
, which is measured to high accuracy in practice. For synthetic data we can set
f_{\rm em}^{\diamond(n)}=f_{\rm em}^{(n)}
without loss of generality, but this is impossible generally for astronomical observations, because the ephemeris is only known approximately. We emphasise that the change of variables in Equations (
78
) and (
78
) is a convenient device to simplify the Kalman filter equations and to bring the numerical values into a reasonable dynamic range, avoiding numerical errors due to finite precision arithmetic and sidestepping the requirement for excessively long floating point formats (e.g. long double, quadruple). It does not remove any degrees of freedom nor does it involve an approximation. A similar reparameterisation is used in other states-space analyses of PTA experiments
(
Kimpson
et al. 2024a
;
Kimpson
et al. 2024b
, e.g.)
.

We now identify
\bm{X}(t)
with a vector of length
2N
composed of the intrinsic ephemeris-subtracted pulsar frequency state
f^{*(n)}_{\rm p}(t)
, and the stochastic GW background
a^{(n)}(t)
, viz.

\bm{X}(t)=\left[f_{\rm p}^{*(1)}(t),\dots,f_{\rm p}^{*(N)}(t),a^{(1)}(t),\dots,a^{(N)}(t)\right]\ .

(80)

We package the ephemeris-subtracted, measured pulsar frequencies as

\bm{Y}(t)=\left[f_{\rm m}^{*(1)}(t),\dots,f_{\rm m}^{*(N)}(t)\right]\ .

(81)

In Section
3
we assume that the states evolve linearly according to the continuous stochastic (Langevin) differential equation

\frac{d\bm{X}}{dt}=\bm{A}\bm{X}+\bm{\Sigma}(t)\ ,

(82)

according to Equations (
4
) and (
16
). In Equation (
82
),
\bm{A}
is a diagonal
2N\times 2N
block matrix,

\bm{A}=\begin{pmatrix}-\bm{\gamma}_{\rm p}&0\\
0&-\bm{\gamma}_{\rm a}\end{pmatrix}\ ,

(83)

with

\bm{\gamma}_{\rm p}=\text{diag}\left[\gamma^{(1)},...,\gamma^{(N)}\right]\,,

(84)

and

\bm{\gamma}_{\rm a}=\gamma_{\rm a}I_{N}\,,

(85)

where
I_{N}
denotes the identity matrix of dimension
N
. Furthermore,
\bm{\Sigma}(t)
is a vector of length
2N
,

\bm{\Sigma}(t)=\left[\xi^{(1)}(t),\dots,\xi^{(N)}(t),\Xi_{\rm a}^{(1)}(t),\dots,\Xi_{\rm a}^{(N)}(t)\right]

(86)

where
\xi^{(n)}(t)
is defined by Equation (
6
)
and
\Xi_{\rm a}^{(n)}(t)
is defined by Equation (
18
).

The general solution to Equation (
82
) is given by
(
Gardiner 2009
)
,

\bm{X}(t)=e^{\bm{A}t}\bm{X}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}e^{\bm{A}(t-t^{\prime})}\bm{\Sigma}(t^{\prime})\ .

(87)

From Equation (
87
) we construct the discrete, recursive solution for
\bm{X}(t_{k})=\bm{X}_{k}
following the template in Appendix
B.1
. The result is

\bm{X}_{k}=\bm{F}_{k}\bm{X}_{k-1}+\bm{w}_{k}\ ,

(88)

with

\displaystyle\bm{F}_{k}

\displaystyle=e^{\bm{A}\Delta t}\,,

(89)

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}d\bm{B}(t^{\prime})e^{\bm{A}\left(t_{k+1}-t^{\prime}\right)}\bm{\Sigma}(t^{\prime})\ .

(90)

where
d\bm{B}(t^{\prime})
denotes a standard Brownian increment and we write
\Delta t=t_{k+1}-t_{k}
. From Equation (
90
) the process noise covariance matrix is

\displaystyle\bm{Q}_{k}\bm{\delta}_{kj}

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{w}_{k}\bm{w}_{j}^{\intercal}\rangle

(91)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{Q}_{\rm p}&0\\
0&\bm{Q}_{\rm a}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(92)

with

\bm{Q}_{\rm p}=\text{diag}\left[Q^{(1)},\dots,Q^{(N)}\right]\,,

(93)

and

\bm{Q}_{\rm a}=\frac{\langle h^{2}\rangle}{12}\bm{\Gamma}\left(1-e^{-2\gamma_{\rm a}\Delta t}\right)\,.

(94)

In Equation (
92
), the Einstein summation convention is suppressed temporarily, i.e. there is no summation implied over the repeated index
k
. In Equation (
93
),
Q^{(n)}
is given by

Q^{(n)}=\frac{[\sigma^{(n)}]^{2}}{2\gamma^{(n)}}\left[1-e^{-2\gamma^{(n)}\Delta t}\right]\ .

(95)

In Equation (
94
),
\bm{\Gamma}
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}=\begin{Bmatrix}1&\Gamma\left[\theta^{(1,2)}\right]&\dots&\Gamma\left[\theta^{(1,N)}\right]\\
\Gamma\left[\theta^{(2,1)}\right]&1&\dots&\Gamma\left[\theta^{(2,N)}\right]\\
\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\\
\Gamma\left[\theta^{(N,1)}\right]&\Gamma\left[\theta^{(N,2)}\right]&\dots&1\end{Bmatrix}

\displaystyle\,,

(96)

where the scalar Hellings-Downs correlation function,
\Gamma(\theta)
, is defined by Equation (
20
).

Due to the linear evolution of
\bm{X}(t)
, the non-linear predict stage of the EKF, viz. Equations (
69
) and (
70
), can be replaced by the predict step from the linear Kalman filter, viz.

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{X}}_{k|k-1}

\displaystyle=\bm{F}_{k}\hat{\bm{X}}_{k-1|k-1}\ ,

(97)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{P}}_{k|k-1}

\displaystyle=\bm{F}_{k}\hat{\bm{P}}_{k-1|k-1}\bm{F}_{k}^{\intercal}+\bm{Q}_{k}\,.

(98)

The relation between
\bm{X}(t)
and
\bm{Y}(t)
remains non-linear, viz.

f_{\rm m}^{*(n)}(t)=\left[1-a^{(n)}(t)\right]f_{\rm p}^{*(n)}-a^{(n)}(t)f_{\rm em}^{(n)}(t)+\varepsilon^{(n)}(t)\ ,

(99)

from Equation (
8
). As such, the non-linear EKF algorithm for the update stage, Equations
(
72
)–(
77
), is retained. The remaining unspecified component matrices of the EKF are the measurement function
h(\bm{X}_{k})
, its Jacobian
\bm{H}_{k}
, and the measurement covariance matrix
\bm{R}_{k}
. These are derived straightforwardly from Equation (
99
), with

\displaystyle h(\bm{X}_{k})=\begin{pmatrix}\left[1-a^{(1)}(t)\right]f_{\rm p}^{*(1)}(t)-a^{(1)}(t)f_{\rm em}^{(1)}(t)\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\left[1-a^{(2)}(t)\right]f_{\rm p}^{*(2)}(t)-a^{(2)}(t)f_{\rm em}^{(2)}(t)\\
\vdots\\
\left[1-a^{(N)}(t)\right]f_{\rm p}^{*(N)}(t)-a^{(N)}(t)f_{\rm em}^{(N)}(t)\\
\end{pmatrix}\,,

(100)

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{V}_{k}&\bm{W}_{k}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(101)

\displaystyle\bm{R}_{k}=E\left[\varepsilon^{(n)}(t)\varepsilon^{(n)}(t)^{\rm T}\right]=\sigma^{2}_{\rm m}\bm{I}_{k}\,,

(102)

\displaystyle\bm{V}_{k}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}\left[1-a^{(1)},\dots,1-a^{(N)}\right]\,,

(103)

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{k}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}\left[-f_{\rm p}^{(1)},\dots,-f_{\rm p}^{(N)}\right]\,,

(104)

Equations (
90
), (
92
)–(
96
), and (
100
)–(
104
) contain all the required components in order to apply the EKF to the state space model in Section
3
.

Appendix C
PSD for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

In standard PTA analyses, the stochastic GW background is described by a stationary Gaussian process, with an ensemble-averaged, power-law PSD, parameterised by an amplitude,
A_{\rm gw}
, and exponent,
\gamma_{\rm gw}
. Specifically, the TOA residuals PSD used in standard analyses is
(
van
Haasteren & Vallisneri 2014
;
Taylor 2021
, e.g.)

P(f)=\frac{A_{\rm gw}^{2}}{12\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{f}{\text{yr}^{1}}\right)^{-\gamma_{\rm gw}}\text{yr}^{3}\,.

(105)

In this paper the influence of the stochastic GW background on the
n
-th pulsar,
a^{(n)}(t)
, is modelled as an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, governed by Equation (
16
) and defined formally in Appendix
A
. The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process also maps onto a stationary Gaussian process. The PSD of the Gaussian process follows from Equations (
16
) and (
18
) and is given by

P^{(n)}_{\rm a}(f)=\frac{\left[\sigma_{\rm a}^{(n,n)}\right]^{2}}{\gamma_{\rm a}^{2}+\left(2\pi f\right)^{2}}\,,

(106)

where
\sigma_{\rm a}^{(n,n)}
is given by Equation (
20
). Here
P^{(n)}_{\rm a}(f)
is the Fourier transform of the temporal autocorrelation function of
a^{(n)}(t)
.

We can relate
P^{(n)}_{\rm a}(f)
to the PSD of the TOA residuals,
P_{\rm\delta t}(f)
, as follows. We use the notation
P_{\rm\delta t}(f)
to refer to the PSD of the TOA residuals generated by the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process and
P(f)
to refer to the TOA residuals PSD used in standard analyses, cf. Equation (
105
). For conciseness we drop the
(n)
-superscript temporarily. Let the measured phase be
\phi_{\rm m}
. With
d\phi_{\rm m}/dt=f_{\rm m}
, Equation (
8
) implies

\frac{d\phi_{\rm m}}{dt}=f_{\rm p}-f_{\rm p}a\,.

(107)

In this appendix, we focus on the effect of the stochastic GW background and assume for simplicity that
f_{\rm p}
is approximately constant over the autocorrelation time-scale of
a(t)
. Letting the random part of
\phi_{\rm m}
be
\delta\phi_{\rm m}
, Equation (
107
) implies

\frac{d\left(\delta\phi_{\rm m}\right)}{dt}=-f_{\rm p}a\,.

(108)

Taking the Fourier transform of Equation (
108
) (denoted with a circumflex) and evaluating the squared modulus gives

|\delta\hat{\phi}_{\rm m}|^{2}=\omega^{-2}f_{\rm p}^{2}|\hat{a}|^{2}\,,

(109)

with
\omega=2\pi f
. If a TOA has a residual
\delta t>0
then the pulse arrives late, and the measured phase is slightly less than expected, with
\delta\phi_{\rm m}\approx f_{\rm p}\delta t<0
and hence

|\delta\hat{t}|^{2}=\omega^{-2}|\hat{a}|^{2}\,.

(110)

Now, the PSD
P_{x}(\omega)
of a general function
x(t)
is given by

P_{x}(\omega)=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}|\hat{x}_{T}(\omega)|^{2}\,,

(111)

with
x_{T}(t)=x(t)\text{ for }t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{0}+T\text{ and }x_{T}(t)=0\text{ otherwise}
. Alternatively, the Wiener-Khintchin theorem gives

\langle\hat{x}(\omega)\hat{x}^{*}(\omega^{\prime})\rangle=2\pi\delta\left(\omega-\omega^{\prime}\right)P_{x}\left(\omega\right)\,.

(112)

Equations (
110
)–(
112
) imply that the TOA residuals PSD generated from the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process for
a(t)
is

P_{\delta t}(\omega)=\omega^{-2}P_{a}(\omega)\,,

(113)

or equivalently

P_{\delta t}(f)=\frac{\langle h^{2}\rangle\gamma_{\rm a}}{48\pi^{2}f^{2}\left[\gamma_{\rm a}^{2}+\left(2\pi f\right)^{2}\right]}\,.

(114)

In the limit
f\gg\gamma_{\rm a}
, Equation (
114
) reduces to

P_{\rm\delta t}(f)=\frac{\langle h^{2}\rangle\gamma_{\rm a}f^{-4}}{192\pi^{4}}\,.

(115)

In the limit
f\ll\gamma_{\rm a}
, Equation (
114
) reduces to

P_{\rm\delta t}(f)=\frac{\langle h^{2}\rangle\gamma_{\rm a}f^{-2}}{48\pi^{2}}\,.

(116)

Appendix D
Glossary of symbols

In this appendix, for the reader’s convenience, we present a glossary of certain key symbols used in this paper and, where appropriate, relate them to symbols or quantities used in standard PTA analyses.

•

a^{(n)}(t)
. Fractional change in
f_{\rm m}^{(n)}(t)
caused by the stochastic GW background. It is described by a stochastic Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, parameterised by
\bm{\theta}_{\rm gw}=\left\{h_{\rm a},\gamma_{\rm a}\right\}
.

•

f_{\rm em}^{(n)}(t)
. The deterministic electromagnetic spin-down of the
n
-th pulsar’s intrinsic spin frequency.

•

f_{\rm m}^{(n)}(t)
. The pulse frequency of the
n
-th pulsar, as measured on Earth. One has
f_{\rm m}^{(n)}(t)\neq f_{\rm p}^{(n)}(t)
in general because of measurement errors
\varepsilon^{(n)}(t)
and modulation by the stochastic GW background; see Equations (
8
)–(
10
). The Kalman filter solves the inverse problem of estimating
f_{\rm p}^{(n)}(t)
given
f_{\rm m}^{(n)}(t)
.

•

f_{\rm p}^{(n)}(t)
. The intrinsic spin frequency of the
n
-th pulsar in the array, as evaluated in the pulsar’s rest frame. It obeys a stochastic Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, with mean-reverting, random fluctuations; see Equations (
4
)–(
6
). The evolution of
f_{\rm p}^{(n)}(t)
forms a hidden Markov sequence, cf. Equation (
1
).

•

h_{\rm a}
. Amplitude parameter of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck description of the stochastic GW background. It is analogous, but not equal, to the amplitude factor
A_{\rm gw}
in the PSD of traditional PTA analyses, cf. Equation (
105
).

•

\gamma_{\rm a}
. Mean reversion timescale of the stochastic GW background in the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck model. It is set approximately by the minimum SMBHB orbital frequency, i.e.
\gamma_{\rm a}\sim 2\pi f_{\rm min}
.

•

\chi^{(n)}(t)
. A white noise stochastic process, parameterised by
\sigma^{(n)}
.

Appendix E
Workflow summary

For the reader’s convenience we summarise the workflow for a representative PTA analysis using the Kalman filter in conjunction with a Bayesian inference algorithm for parameter estimation and model selection. In this paper nested sampling is used for the Bayesian inference algorithm, but any likelihood-based sampling scheme can be used.

1.

From a PTA composed of
N
pulsars, obtain
N
measurement time series,
f_{\rm m}^{(n)}(t)
, collectively labelled
\bm{Y}
.

2.

Specify a state-space model
\mathcal{M}
(see Section
F.2.3
) with parameters
\bm{\theta}
.

3.

Specify a prior distribution
\pi(\bm{\theta})
.

4.

For each iteration in the Bayesian inference algorithm:

(a)

Sample from
\pi(\bm{\theta})
to obtain
\bm{\theta}_{\rm sample}

(b)

Pass
\bm{\theta}_{\rm sample}
to the Kalman filter.

(c)

Iterate over
\bm{Y}
using the Kalman filter and obtain a single likelihood value
\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{Y}|\bm{\theta}_{\rm sample}\right)
value.

(d)

Update
p\left(\bm{\theta}|\bm{Y}\right)
and
\mathcal{Z}

5.

Repeat steps (a)–(d) until a user-selected convergence criterion are satisfied.

In order to compute the odds ratio
\beta
in Equation (
125
) the above workflow is repeated for a two models
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, and the two
\mathcal{Z}
values are divided. We remind the reader that the above workflow differs from a realistic PTA analysis in one important respect, namely that the data are input as frequency time series
f_{\rm m}^{(n)}(t)
instead of pulse TOAs. The generalization to TOAs is subtle and will be tackled in a forthcoming paper.

Generating

No direct measurements exist for

\displaystyle\sigma^{(n)}\approx\sigma_{\rm TOA}^{(n)}f_{\rm p}^{(n)}(t_{1}){T_{\rm cad}}^{-3/2}\ .

For consistency with previous work, we calculate

are generated for the same choice of

. The injected elements of

are summarised in the top section of Table

F.1.2
Generating
a^{(n)}(t)

The multiplicative modulation of
f_{\rm m}^{(n)}(t)
by the linear superposition of
M
SMBHB sources is given by Equations (
8
)–(
18
). For the
n
-th pulsar,
a^{(n)}(t)
in Equations (
16
)–(
18
) is parameterised by
7M
parameters,

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{\rm a}=\left\{h_{0}^{(m)},\iota^{(m)},\psi^{(m)},\delta^{(m)},\alpha^{(m)},\Omega^{(m)},\Phi_{0}^{(m)}\right\}_{1\leq m\leq M}\ ,

(118)

where
h_{0}^{(m)}
is the characteristic wave strain,
\iota^{(m)}
is the orbital inclination,
\psi^{(m)}
is the polarisation angle,
\delta^{(m)}
is the declination and
\alpha^{(m)}
is the right ascension. These parameters enter
Equations (
12
) – (
15
) via
H_{ij}^{(m)}=H_{ij}^{(m)}\left[h_{0}^{(m)},\iota^{(m)},\psi^{(m)},\delta^{(m)},\alpha^{(m)}\right]
and
\bm{n}^{(m)}=\bm{n}^{(m)}\left[\delta^{(m)},\alpha^{(m)}\right]
. To generate a stochastic background of GW sources we randomly sample the
7M
parameters in
\bm{\theta}_{\rm a}
from the probability density functions (PDFs) specified in the middle section of Table
2
. For the five angles
\{\iota,\psi,\delta,\alpha,\Phi_{0}\}
, uniform distributions are used
(
Bhagwat et al. 2021
, e.g.)
. The five angles are independent. In contrast,
h_{0}
and
\Omega
are not independent astrophysically; an SMBHB nearer the end of its inspiral (higher
\Omega
) is likely to be louder at Earth (higher
h_{0}
). However, the joint PDF
p(h_{0},\Omega)
is complicated and uncertain to calculate in a realistic astrophysical manner without large-scale cosmological simulations
(
Middleton et al. 2018
;
Mannerkoski et al. 2019
;
Sykes et al. 2022b
;
Muhamed Kozhikkal
et al. 2023
, e.g.)
, which lie outside the scope of this paper. For the purposes of validating the algorithm in Sections
3
and
4
, and for the sake of simplicity, it is sufficient to treat
h_{0}
and
\Omega
as independent random variables and factorize
p(h_{0},\Omega)=p(h_{0})p(\Omega)
. We fix
h_{0}
as a constant for all sources, so that
p(h_{0})
is a delta function in Table
3
, and treat it as an adjustable parameter to tune the strength of the synthetic stochastic GW background. For
\Omega
we adopt a power-law PDF, on the bounded domain
\Omega_{\rm min}\leq\Omega\leq\Omega_{\rm max}
, viz.

\displaystyle p\left(\Omega\right)=K_{1}\Omega^{-\kappa}\,,

(119)

with

\displaystyle K_{1}=\frac{1-\kappa}{\Omega_{\rm max}^{1-\kappa}-\Omega_{\rm min}^{1-\kappa}}\,.

(120)

In Equations (
119
) and (
120
) we set
\kappa=3
,
\Omega_{\rm min}=T_{\rm obs}^{-1}
and
\Omega_{\rm max}=T_{\rm cad}^{-1}
for the sake of definiteness. The value of
\kappa=3
and the power-law form of Equation (
119
) are chosen arbitrarily but are astrophysically reasonable given current assembly models of SMBHBs
(
Sesana
et al. 2008
;
Gültekin
et al. 2009
;
Kormendy &
Ho 2013
;
Chen
et al. 2017
;
Middleton et al. 2018
, e.g.)
and the scale invariance of the inspiral physics
(
Peters 1964
;
Phinney 2001
)
. The values of
\Omega_{\rm min}
and
\Omega_{\rm max}
are upper and lower bounds respectively, set by the effective bandwidth of the PTA. The true, physical values satisfy
\Omega_{\rm min}\ll T_{\rm obs}^{-1}
(start of inspiral) and
\Omega_{\rm max}\gg T_{\rm cad}^{-1}
(end of inspiral), but SMBHBs with
\Omega\ll T_{\rm obs}^{-1}
and
\Omega\gg T_{\rm cad}^{-1}
do not contribute measurably to the stochastic GW background in the PTA band. Appendix
F.1.4
demonstrates that the main results of this paper are insensitive to the exact value of
\kappa
.

The complete procedure for generating
a^{(n)}(t)
is as follows:

1.

Specify a synthetic PTA, determining
q^{(n)}
and
d^{(n)}
.

2.

For
M
(here
M=10^{4}
) discrete quasi-monochromatic GW sources, randomly sample the
7M
parameters in
\bm{\theta}_{\rm a}
from the probability distributions summarised in Table
2
.

3.

Evaluate Equations (
12
)–(
15
) to obtain
a^{(n)}(t)
.

We emphasise that
a^{(n)}(t)
depends on the pulsar-specific quantities
\bm{q}^{(n)}
and
d^{(n)}
, specified in Section
F.1.1
as well as
{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm a}
. We also emphasize that we do not approximate
a^{(n)}(t)
as a stochastic, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process represented by Equations (
16
)–(
18
) (see Appendix
A
) when generating the synthetic data. The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck approximation is reserved for the inference model in Section
3
, to circumvent the otherwise insurmountable identifiability challenge posed by the
7M
parameters in
{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm a}
.

F.1.3
Generating
\varepsilon^{(n)}(t)

The measurement noise
\varepsilon^{(n)}(t)
is assumed for simplicity to be additive, Gaussian, and white. It excludes chromatic disturbances in PTA TOAs, such as those induced by propagation through the turbulent interstellar medium
(
Goncharov
et al. 2021
, e.g.)
. Its variance
\sigma_{\rm m}^{(n)}
can be related to
\sigma_{\rm TOA}^{(n)}
(in units of
s
) by

\sigma_{\rm m}^{(n)}\approx f_{\rm p}^{(n)}(t_{1})\sigma_{\rm TOA}^{(n)}\ {T_{\rm cad}}^{-1}\ .

(121)

For an MSP with
f_{\rm p}^{(n)}\sim 0.1
kHz,
T_{\rm cad}=1\,{\rm week}
, and
\sigma_{\rm TOA}^{(n)}\sim 1\mu
s, Equation (
121
) implies
\sigma_{\rm m}^{(n)}\sim 10^{-10}
Hz. The most accurately timed pulsars have
\sigma_{\rm TOA}^{(n)}\sim 10
ns and
\sigma_{\rm m}^{(n)}\sim 10^{-12}
Hz. We fix
\sigma_{\rm m}^{(n)}=10^{-11}
Hz for all
n
, copying
Kimpson
et al. 2024a
, and take it as known
a priori
rather than a parameter to be inferred. Despite
\sigma_{\rm m}^{(n)}
taking the same value for every pulsar,
f_{\rm m}^{(n)}(t)
is constructed from a different random realisation of
\varepsilon^{(n)}(t)
generated by a different random seed for each pulsar. We refer the reader to Section 4 in
Kimpson
et al. 2024a
for additional discussion on the specification of astrophysically representative values of
\sigma_{\rm m}^{(n)}
.

F.1.4
Justification of the injection PDF

.

When generating synthetic data in Section
F.1.2
, it is necessary to sample the
7M
parameters of
\bm{\theta}_{\rm a}
from the probability distributions specified in Table
2
. For the five angles
\{\iota,\psi,\delta,\alpha,\Phi_{0}\}
, we draw from uncorrelated and uniform PDFs, consistent with the standard assumption that the stochastic GW background is isotropic
(
Hellings &
Downs 1983
;
Maggiore 2018
)
. In contrast, the parameters
h_{0}
and
\Omega
are correlated; both
h_{0}
and
\Omega
increase, as a SMBHB inspiral proceeds. Moreover,
\Omega
and hence
h_{0}
are not distributed uniformly; a SMBHB spends more time in the earlier than in the later stages of inspiral, so lower values of
\Omega
are more common
(
Phinney 2001
;
Sesana
et al. 2008
;
Middleton et al. 2018
)
. In general, therefore, one should sample
h_{0}
and
\Omega
from the joint PDF
p(h_{0},\Omega)
, which is not uniform and correlates
h_{0}
positively with
\Omega
. A realistic and accurate calculation of
p(h_{0},\Omega)
requires large-scale cosmological
N
-body simulations
(see e.g.
Middleton et al. 2018
, and references therein)
and falls well outside the scope of this paper. Instead, we factorize the joint PDF in the form
p(h_{0},\Omega)=p(h_{0})p(\Omega)
and set
p(h_{0})=\delta(h_{0}-h_{0,{\rm inj}})
for simplicity, as a practical way of tuning the mean square amplitude of the background
\langle h^{2}\rangle
in Equation (
19
) and hence
h_{\rm a}
in
\bm{\theta}_{\rm gw}
in Equation (
34
). For the remaining parameter
\Omega
, the PDF is a power law, Equation (
119
), with exponent
-\kappa
. In this paper we take
\kappa=3
, consistent with standard assembly models of SMBHBs
(
Sesana
et al. 2008
;
Gültekin
et al. 2009
;
Kormendy &
Ho 2013
;
Chen
et al. 2017
;
Middleton et al. 2018
, e.g.)
.

We now show that modifying
\kappa
over an astrophysically plausible range does not hamper the ability of the state-space inference scheme in Sections
3
and
4
to detect a stochastic GW background, although of course it does modify the temporal cross-correlation properties of the background through
\langle a^{(n)}(t)a^{(n^{\prime})}(t^{\prime})\rangle
. The value of
\kappa
controls the shape of Equation (
54
). In Figure
6
we evaluate Equation (
54
) for four astrophysically plausible values of
2\leq\kappa\leq 5
. Current PTA experiments operate in the region to the left of the grey dashed vertical line, where
I_{1}(t-t^{\prime})
decreases monotonically with
|t-t^{\prime}|
, and Equation (
54
) is approximated accurately by Equation (
60
). Therefore the state-space inference scheme can be applied safely without modification for
2\leq\kappa\leq 5
.

F.2
Detecting a synthetic background

In this section we test the state-space analysis algorithm on synthetic data
\bm{Y}
produced by the method described in Section
F.1
. The test has three goals: (i) to verify that the algorithm in Sections
3
and
4
can detect successfully a synthetic background of the form constructed in Section
F.1
; (ii) to determine the minimum background amplitude
h_{a}
which the algorithm detects, assuming a NANOGrav-like PTA configured as described in Section
F.1
; and (iii) to determine the accuracy with which the parameters describing the background are estimated. In Section
F.2.1
we define the priors on
\bm{\theta}
. In Section
F.2.2
we calculate the joint posterior probability distribution of the parameters,
p(\bm{\theta}|\bm{Y})
. In Section
F.2.3
we calculate the detectability of the GW background as a function of
h_{0}
. To quantify the impact of cosmic variance, the algorithm is validated on multiple random realisations of the stochastic processes that generate
\bm{Y}
: the pulsar process noise
\xi^{(n)}(t)
, the telescope measurement noise
\varepsilon^{(n)}(t)
and the realisation of the
M
synthetic sources parameterised by
\bm{\theta}_{\rm a}
. A real PTA analysis witnesses a unique realisation of
\bm{Y}
— the actual, astronomical one — but one never knows where this realisation lies within the admissible statistical ensemble.

Appendix G
Comparison of Kalman filtering and Gaussian process regression

The Kalman filter used in this paper is a special case of the Bayesian filter
(
Särkkä 2013b
)
for Gaussian probability distributions. Generally, the Bayesian filter recursively computes the posterior distribution of the Markov state
\bm{X}_{k}
at each time step
k
, given the history of the measurements up to that timestep, via Bayes’s rule, viz.

p\left(\bm{X}_{k}|\bm{Y}_{1:k}\right)\propto p\left(\bm{Y}_{1:k}|\bm{X}_{k}\right)p\left(\bm{X}_{k}|\bm{Y}_{1:k-1}\right)\,.

(129)

For the case where the prior, likelihood and posterior are Gaussian, the Kalman filter equations presented in Appendix
B
apply
(
Särkkä 2013b
, see e.g. )
. One may ask if there is a connection between the Kalman filter and Gaussian process (GP) regression
(
Rasmussen &
Williams 2005
)
, a technique which is already commonly used in PTA data analysis
(
van
Haasteren & Vallisneri 2014
)
. The answer is yes; there is a clear, established connection between the Kalman filter and GPs. In this appendix we discuss this connection in more detail. In Appendix
G.1
we briefly review GPs. In Appendix
G.2
we discuss the similarities and differences between GPs and the Kalman filter. For more detail on the connection between GPs and the Kalman filter, we refer the reader to
Hartikainen &
Särkkä 2010
;
Reece &
Roberts 2010
and
Sarkka &
Hartikainen 2012

G.1
Gaussian processes

A GP
(
Rasmussen &
Williams 2005
)
is a Bayesian framework for solving the problem of estimating the value of a function
f
at a series of arbitrary times
\bm{t}_{*}
, given a set of observations
\bm{y}
at times
\bm{t}
. The
i
-th observation is assumed to be the function value obscured by Gaussian noise, viz.

y_{i}=f(t_{i})+\epsilon_{i}\,,

(130)

with
\epsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{m})
. GPs assume that the joint prior distribution of the function values is Gaussian, viz.

f\left(\bm{t}\right)\sim\mathcal{N}\left[\bm{0},\bm{K}(\bm{t},\bm{t}^{\prime},\bm{\theta}_{\rm hyper})\right]

(131)

for covariance matrix
\bm{K}(\bm{t},\bm{t}^{\prime},\bm{\theta}_{\rm hyper})
, with hyperparameters
\bm{\theta}_{\rm hyper}
. Going forwards, for notational convenience, we drop the explicit
\bm{\theta}_{\rm hyper}
dependence and take it to be implicit, i.e.
\bm{K}(\bm{t},\bm{t}^{\prime},\bm{\theta}_{\rm hyper})=\bm{K}(\bm{t},\bm{t}^{\prime})
. From Equation (
130
) and (
131
) it follows that the posterior and the prior are conjugate, satisfying

p\left[f(\bm{t}_{*}|\bm{y})\right]\sim\mathcal{N}\left[\bm{\mu}(\bm{t}_{*}),\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{t}_{*})\right]\,,

(132)

with mean

\bm{\mu}(\bm{t}_{*})=\bm{K}(\bm{t}_{*},\bm{t})\left[\bm{K}(\bm{t},\bm{t})+\sigma_{\rm m}^{2}\bm{I}\right]^{-1}\bm{y}\,,

(133)

and covariance

\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{t}_{*})=\bm{K}(\bm{t}_{*},\bm{t}_{*})-\bm{K}(\bm{t}_{*},\bm{t})\left[\bm{K}(\bm{t},\bm{t})+\sigma_{\rm m}^{2}\bm{I}\right]^{-1}\bm{K}(\bm{t},\bm{t}_{*})\,.

(134)

The choice of the kernel function
\bm{K}(\bm{t},\bm{t}^{\prime})
encodes the relevant physics; see e.g. Section 3,
van
Haasteren & Vallisneri 2014
for the choice of
\bm{K}(\bm{t},\bm{t}^{\prime})
in the context of PTA data analysis. For a particular choice of
\bm{K}(\bm{t},\bm{t}^{\prime})
, the hyperparameters
\bm{\theta}_{\rm hyper}
are unknown a priori and must be estimated through a Bayesian inference procedure. The computational complexity of the GP procedure is dominated by the matrix inverse in Equations (
133
) and (
134
). If there are
T
observations, the asymptotic scaling goes as
\mathcal{O}\left(T^{3}\right)
in general.

G.2
Kalman filtering

A Kalman filter is related to, yet differs from, a GP as follows. First, rather than constructing a model of a physical process by postulating a mean
\bm{\mu}
and covariance function,
\bm{K}(\bm{t},\bm{t}^{\prime})
, cf. Equation (
131
), one specifies a (parameterised) stochastic differential equation (SDE) for a sequence of hidden states which are related to the measurements through a (parameterised) measurement equation. That is to say, one uses a state-space representation. Specifying an SDE and measurement equation implicitly defines a mean and a kernel function in Equation (
131
). Indeed, an essential result from
Hartikainen &
Särkkä 2010
and
Sarkka &
Hartikainen 2012
demonstrates that for a given GP and covariance function, it is always possible to find an equivalent SDE. Second, for GPs one wants to determine
\bm{\theta}_{\rm hyper}
. For the Kalman filter, one wants to determine the parameters of the SDE and the measurement equation, i.e.
\bm{\theta}
, cf. Section
3.4
. Third, GPs often operate in batch mode on all discrete data points simultaneously. Accordingly, the computational cost is
\mathcal{O}\left(T^{3}\right)
. The Kalman filter operates recursively, iterating though the dataset, and has cost
\mathcal{O}\left(T\right)
(see also Section
6
). Fourth, its nature as an iterative estimator means that the Kalman filter can be readily applied to non-stationary processes
(
Zarchan &
Musoff 2000
)
. In contrast, standard GP implementations are restricted to stationary processes as they rely on the conjugacy of Gaussians to obtain tractable, closed-form posteriors. Extensions to GPs to handle non-stationary processes have been developed
(
Heinonen et al. 2016
;
Sauer
et al. 2023
, e.g.)
. Finally, the Kalman filter can be applied readily to nongaussian systems. Indeed, the linear Kalman filter is an optimal estimator for a linear system, in the sense that it minimises the mean square error, without assuming Gaussianity
(
Uhlmann &
Julier 2024
)
. For nongaussian likelihoods, standard GPs are often intractable, and approximation methods based on variational inference
(
Galy-Fajou et al. 2020
, e.g.)
or MCMC sampling
(
Kındap &
Godsill 2022
, e.g.)
must be used.
