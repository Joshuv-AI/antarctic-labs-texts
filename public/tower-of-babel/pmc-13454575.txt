YOLOv10-based multi-scale variant object detection for multi-category PPE non-compliance monitoring on construction sites
Authors: Seunghyeon Wang, Hyuna Kim, Jiyoung Yeo, Juhyung Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multi-category Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) non-compliance monitoring on construction sites remains challenging because visual cues for missing PPE often appear at multiple scales, particularly as small objects. To address these challenges, an enhanced YOLOv10-based detector is developed that incorporates Omni-Dimensional Dynamic Convolution (ODConv) to strengthen fine-grained feature representation, an Efficient Multi-scale Attention–guided Bidirectional Feature Pyramid Network (EMA-BiFPN) to improve cross-scale fusion, a four-scale prediction strategy to increase sensitivity to extremely small instances, and the Minimum Points Distance IoU (MPDIoU) loss to stabilize bounding-box regression. Experiments are conducted on a dataset of 4060 images annotated for five violation categories (No-helmet, No-mask, No-vest, No-glove, and No-shoes), using stratified splits and a controlled hyperparameter search (243 configurations). On the test set, the proposed method achieves 92.49 mAP@50 and 70.04 mAP@50:95 while maintaining 57.28 FPS, outperforming representative two-stage, one-stage, and transformer-based baselines. Introduction

Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)—such as safety helmets, high-visibility vests, protective gloves, face masks, and safety shoes—plays a fundamental role in preventing injuries on construction sites. However, workers do not always comply with PPE requirements, often due to discomfort, time pressure, or limited safety awareness1. Maintaining consistent compliance among all personnel is therefore essential for improving safety performance. In practice, PPE enforcement often relies on manual inspections, routine patrols, or lengthy reviews of surveillance recordings. These approaches are labor-intensive, difficult to scale, and susceptible to human error, particularly when prolonged monitoring leads to inspector fatigue2.

Deep-learning-based computer vision offers a practical route for automating this process. Modern object detectors can identify and localize predefined targets using bounding boxes, enabling automated screening of compliance indicators in images collected from fixed cameras or wearable devices. When trained on datasets covering diverse viewpoints, lighting conditions, worker postures, and object sizes, such models can achieve more robust performance across real-world scenes3. Within this context, the You Only Look Once (YOLO) family has been widely adopted for construction safety applications because of its real-time inference capability and continued architectural improvements across successive versions4–6. Accordingly, a growing body of research has introduced YOLO-based variants for safety monitoring, showing that detector-level refinements can yield measurable performance gains.

Nevertheless, multi-category PPE non-compliance detection remains challenging in operational environments. Evidence of PPE absence is not concentrated in a single body region but is distributed across multiple locations and often appears at very small scales. For example, violations related to helmets and masks depend on cues around the head and face, whereas missing gloves and safety shoes must be inferred from the hands and feet—regions that are frequently obscured by tools, materials, body pose, or other workers. In wide-field or distant imagery, these regions may occupy only a few pixels, making them highly vulnerable to feature loss during repeated downsampling. These factors reduce feature quality at small scales and can lead to unstable localization under occlusion, crowding, and dense worker interactions7–9. Despite these practical difficulties, detection architectures explicitly tailored to small, occluded, and multi-scale PPE violations remain relatively limited.

Although modules such as Omni-Dimensional Dynamic Convolution (ODConv), BiFPN-style feature fusion, and IoU-based regression refinements have been explored in prior object-detection studies, this study does not claim novelty from any single standalone component. Rather, the contribution lies in a task-specific adaptation of YOLOv10 for multi-category PPE non-compliance detection on construction sites. Compared with prior ODConv- or BiFPN-based detectors developed for other domains, the proposed framework is specifically configured to address four task-driven challenges in PPE monitoring: (1) weak and fine-grained visual cues from localized body regions, (2) loss of small-object information during feature extraction and multi-scale fusion, (3) unstable localization of tiny or overlapping violation instances, and (4) insufficient prediction resolution for extremely small distant workers.

To address these limitations, this study develops an enhanced YOLOv10-based framework for automated PPE non-compliance monitoring across five violation categories: no-helmet, no-mask, no-vest, no-glove, and no-shoes. First, ODConv is incorporated into the backbone to enable content-adaptive feature extraction across spatial positions, channels, and kernel settings, thereby improving responsiveness to small and partially visible cues under clutter and scale variation. Second, an Efficient Multi-scale Attention-guided Bidirectional Feature Pyramid Network (EMA-BiFPN) is introduced to strengthen cross-scale information exchange, allowing shallow spatial details and deeper semantic features to be fused more effectively. Third, the Minimum Points Distance Intersection over Union (MPDIoU) loss is adopted to provide stronger geometric constraints for bounding-box regression, thereby improving localization stability for small and highly overlapping instances. Finally, a four-scale prediction design is employed to increase sensitivity across extremely small, small, medium, and large targets, reflecting the wide scale variation of PPE-related cues in real construction imagery.

Accordingly, the novelty of this study lies not in proposing an entirely new detection algorithm, but in the problem-driven integration and evaluation of complementary architectural modifications within YOLOv10 for PPE non-compliance monitoring. Related work

Vision-based studies on non-PPE monitoring in construction

A substantial body of work has explored vision-based PPE/non-PPE monitoring on construction sites using deep-learning detectors, most commonly CNN-based object detection frameworks. Earlier studies frequently adopted two-stage architectures. For example, Fang et al.10 applied Faster R-CNN and achieved high performance (90.1–98.4%) under multiple scenarios, but the reported inference time was about 0.2 s per image, which can hinder real-time operation. Gu et al.11 further improved Faster R-CNN by incorporating multi-scale training and expanding anchor settings, reporting an additional accuracy gain of roughly 7%, although runtime efficiency was not detailed.

Because practical monitoring systems must balance detection accuracy with computational cost, many subsequent studies shifted toward one-stage detectors. Yang et al.12 enhanced YOLOv3 (with a DarkNet53 backbone) by introducing an image-pyramid strategy to better retain multi-scale information, achieving 95.13% accuracy with 0.017 s latency per image and outperforming SSD and Faster R-CNN. Yan and Wang13 modified the YOLOv3 backbone using dense connections, improving processing speed by 52% and increasing accuracy by 5.7% while preserving efficient inference. Shen et al.14 proposed a hybrid pipeline in which faces were first detected via VGG16, after which helmet status was determined using regression-based region mapping and DenseNet classification. Nath15 initially detected workers using YOLOv3 and then classified PPE using VGG16, ResNet, and Xception, reporting 71.23% accuracy.

More recent work has investigated stronger YOLO variants and broader PPE categories. Wang et al.16 compared YOLOv5 models, showing that YOLOv5x achieved higher accuracy (86.55%), whereas YOLOv5s enabled faster inference (0.019 s per frame). Lee et al.17 developed a real-time approach based on YOLACT with a MobileNetV3 backbone, reporting 66.4% accuracy for helmet and vest detection. Nguyen et al.18 optimized YOLOv5 using the Seahorse Optimization algorithm (SHO), obtaining an average accuracy of approximately 66% across multiple PPE-related categories (helmets, masks, gloves, vests, and shoes). Park et al.19 analyzed individual augmentation strategies and combined YOLOv8 with transformer backbones to improve non-PPE detection. More recently, Wang et al.20 introduced YOLOv10-based helmet/no-helmet detectors enhanced with transformer-based variants. Related work by Wang21 examined non-PPE detection in surveillance and body-worn imagery using benchmark datasets, while Wang22 proposed a domain-adaptive Faster R-CNN framework to mitigate class imbalance, with particular attention to underrepresented non-helmet classes.

Overall, prior research indicates that deep-learning detectors can perform well within their respective experimental settings. However, in operational construction footage, PPE-related cues often appear at very small scales, and performance can degrade due to strong scale variation, repeated downsampling, dense crowds, and frequent occlusion. Consequently, approaches that explicitly target small-object behavior—together with evaluations that report size-aware metrics—remain comparatively limited in the broader literature on construction PPE non-compliance monitoring.

YOLO-oriented research for small-object detection

There is no single universally accepted definition of a “small object,” because apparent object size depends on image resolution, camera distance, viewing angle, and the evaluation protocol. Many benchmarks therefore use a practical pixel-based convention; for instance, objects with bounding-box areas below 32 × 32 pixels are commonly treated as small instances23.

Within the YOLO family, numerous variants have been proposed, and attempts to improve small-object performance in YOLO-style detectors can be summarized into three major directions. First, many studies emphasize preserving spatial detail by reinforcing high-resolution feature pathways and strengthening multi-scale fusion through pyramid-style neck designs, such as Feature Pyramid Network (FPN), Path Aggregation Network (PANet), and Bidirectional Feature Pyramid Network (BiFPN). As one example, Chao et al.24 proposed a YOLOv8 variant incorporating a Deep Spatial Pyramid Fusion (DSPF) module that reduces redundancy and computation associated with residual structures while maintaining effective multi-scale aggregation in lightweight settings.

Second, other methods aim to amplify weak representations of tiny targets by adding lightweight attention modules—such as Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) and the Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM)—or by adopting dynamic convolution strategies such as ODConv. In this line of work, He and Wei25 improved wear-particle recognition by embedding CBAM into a YOLOv5-based detector to emphasize informative features.

Third, localization under stricter IoU conditions has been improved using IoU-aware regression objectives. Su et al.26 compared several IoU-based losses and found that Generalized IoU (GIoU) can outperform the standard IoU loss in localization quality, although the degree of improvement can vary across datasets (e.g., PASCAL VOC). More recently, Zou and Liu27 combined YOLOv10 with ODConv and an MPDIoU-based regression loss, reporting improved precision and recall for traffic-sign detection involving irregular shapes.

Despite these developments, small-object detection remains difficult. Tiny instances often yield weak activations after repeated downsampling, may lose discriminative detail during multi-scale fusion, and can be removed by post-processing in crowded scenes. These issues are particularly pronounced for worker-centered PPE non-compliance monitoring, where evidence of violations is distributed across multiple body regions (e.g., head/face for helmets and masks, torso for vests, and hands/feet for gloves and safety shoes) and is frequently partially visible due to occlusion and overlap. In wide-angle surveillance imagery, such cues can occupy only a few pixels, amplifying the impact of scale variation and clutter.

Proposed approach

Overview of the YOLOv10 baseline for multi-category non-PPE detection

YOLOv10 is a recent one-stage detector designed for end-to-end object detection without relying on Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS). This NMS-free inference is enabled by a consistent dual-assignment strategy that aligns training and inference objectives21. As illustrated in Fig. 1, YOLOv10 follows the standard backbone–neck–head design.Fig. 1Baseline YOLOv10 network architecture.

Baseline YOLOv10 network architecture.

The backbone constructs a hierarchical feature representation through lightweight convolutional building blocks (e.g., C2f., SCDown, and C2fCIB) together with PSA-based attention, producing multi-level feature maps with increasing semantic abstraction (Fig. 1). Shallow stages retain fine spatial detail that is important for small-instance localization, whereas deeper stages emphasize higher-level semantics. The neck then aggregates these multi-resolution features through an FPN–PAN-style pathway, combining high-resolution spatial cues with deeper semantic information.

The detection head adopts a lightweight decoupled structure for classification and box regression and leverages the dual-assignment strategy to support end-to-end prediction. In the baseline setting, YOLOv10 is configured for multi-class non-PPE detection by predicting the following violation categories: no-helmet, no-mask, no-vest, no-glove, and no-shoes. This formulation directly supports compliance assessment because each predicted box corresponds to a specific type of PPE absence observable in the scene.

Architectural enhancements of YOLOv10 for non-PPE compliance monitoring

In real construction imagery, non-PPE cues are often extremely small—particularly in wide-angle surveillance views or long-range shots—because relevant evidence may be confined to localized body regions (e.g., head/face for helmets and masks, torso for vests, and hands/feet for gloves and safety shoes). Under these conditions, the target region may occupy only a few pixels and is easily degraded by (i) repeated downsampling during feature extraction, (ii) smoothing and information dilution during multi-scale fusion, and (iii) suppression effects during inference, especially in crowded scenes with heavy overlap. These difficulties are further amplified by frequent occlusions caused by tools, scaffolding, materials, or inter-person interactions, which can lead to missed detections, inaccurate localization, and confusion between visually similar patterns.

ODConv-enhanced backbone for fine-grained non-PPE cues

Visual evidence of PPE non-compliance is often weak, fragmented, and easily obscured. For example, the absence of a helmet or mask may only be reflected through faint contour information, exposed hair or skin regions, or incomplete boundary patterns. Likewise, missing gloves or safety shoes may be only partly visible because of body pose, self-occlusion, or surrounding objects. These weak signals can be confused with background structures such as concrete textures, reflective surfaces, clothing folds, or scaffold edges. Since these cues are also frequently very small, even minor changes in viewpoint or illumination can noticeably alter their appearance.

To improve feature extraction under these challenging conditions, ODConv is incorporated into the C2f. block. Unlike standard convolution, ODConv dynamically adjusts the convolution process across four dimensions: spatial position, input channel, output channel, and kernel index. This allows the effective convolutional response to change adaptively according to the input content. Compared with earlier dynamic convolution approaches such as CondConv and DyConv, which usually modulate only part of the convolution operation, ODConv provides a richer form of adaptive kernel selection while still remaining efficient enough for real-time detection28,29. This is especially suitable for non-PPE monitoring, where the target cues are often weak, localized, and highly dependent on scene context.

EMA-BiFPN for attention-guided, bidirectional multi-scale fusion

Non-PPE indicators appear across a broad range of scales because worker distance, camera location, and body posture vary widely in construction imagery. Very small targets, such as distant facial, hand, or foot regions, require high-resolution feature maps to preserve spatial detail, yet such maps often lack strong semantic information. In contrast, deeper layers provide richer semantic context but are spatially coarse and may fail to retain fine details after repeated downsampling. As a result, effective non-PPE detection depends on a fusion mechanism that can inject semantic information into detailed high-resolution features without overwhelming weak small-scale signals.

To address this issue, an EMA-BiFPN structure is introduced by combining bidirectional feature-pyramid fusion with EMA-based attention refinement29. The EMA module strengthens feature representation by jointly capturing channel relationships and spatial dependencies, enabling the network to emphasize regions that are more relevant to PPE-related cues during fusion. At the same time, the BiFPN-style design promotes repeated top-down and bottom-up information exchange, helping spatially detailed shallow features and semantically rich deep features interact more effectively. Shortcut connections are also included between intermediate fusion nodes to reduce information loss and better preserve small-scale features throughout the pyramid30.

Four-scale prediction heads for extremely small non-PPE instances

In compliance monitoring, the hardest cases are often the most safety–critical: distant workers may appear small in surveillance footage, and cues for masks, gloves, and safety shoes can be especially tiny and frequently occluded. If predictions are generated only from coarser feature maps, these cues may collapse into a few activations, increasing the likelihood of missed detections or merged boxes in dense scenes.

To increase sensitivity at the finest scale, the detector is extended to a four-head multi-scale prediction scheme. An additional high-resolution pyramid level is derived from shallower neck features that preserve richer spatial detail, refined via bidirectional fusion, and connected to an extra detection head. As a result, predictions are produced at four resolutions intended to cover extremely small, small, medium, and large instances, respectively. Regression is defined using MPDIoU:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{*{20}c} {L_{{{\mathrm{Re}} g}}^{\left( s \right)} = 1 - MPDIoU\left( {B_{gt}^{\left( s \right)} , B_{prd}^{\left( s \right)} } \right)} \\ \end{array}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${B}_{gt}^{\left(s\right)}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${B}_{prd}^{(s)}$$\end{document} represent the matched ground-truth and predicted boxes assigned to head s. The added high-resolution head, together with MPDIoU-based supervision, is intended to reduce missed detections for extremely small non-PPE cues while maintaining robustness across scales.

Evaluation metrics

AP

Average Precision (AP) is a standard metric for object detection that summarizes the precision–recall relationship by integrating precision over the recall interval [0,1] under a given IoU threshold. In this study, AP is computed as:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{*{20}c} {AP = \mathop \sum \limits_{i = 1}^{n} p\left( i \right){\Delta }r\left( i \right)} \\ \end{array}$$\end{document}where detections are ranked by confidence, p(i) is the precision after the top i predictions are considered, and Δ r(i) is the recall increment from rank i-1 to i. AP50 denotes AP at IoU = 0.50, which allows relatively tolerant matching. AP50:95 is more stringent and is obtained by averaging AP over IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05. Because the latter rewards tighter localization—particularly important in crowded scenes with overlapping instances—both AP50 and AP50:95 are reported.

Size-specific average precision

To analyze performance across different object scales, size-dependent AP is additionally reported. All images are resized to 640 × 640 using aspect-ratio-preserving letterbox resizing, and bounding-box sizes are measured in this resized coordinate system. For a ground-truth box with width w and height h (in pixels), the size indicator is defined as:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{*{20}c} {s = \min \left( {w, h} \right)} \\ \end{array}$$\end{document}

Using s, instances are divided into four groups: Extremely Small (ES) if s < 8, Small (S) if 8≤ s < 16, Medium (M) if 16≤ s < 32, and Large (L) if s ≥32. For each subset, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{A}\mathrm{P}}_{ES}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{A}\mathrm{P}}_{S}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{A}\mathrm{P}}_{M}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{A}\mathrm{P}}_{L}$$\end{document} are computed by restricting evaluation to instances within the corresponding size range, following the same AP procedure used for the primary results (AP50) in this work.

Detection speed

Inference efficiency is measured as the processing time per image and is reported as Frames Per Second (FPS), i.e., the number of images processed per second. This metric reflects computational practicality and indicates whether a model is suitable for real-time construction-site PPE non-compliance monitoring.

Experiments

Data preparation

Dataset sources and original data-collection protocols

The data used in this study were obtained from a previously released body-worn camera dataset and three publicly available datasets. The first source was the body-worn camera dataset, which was originally collected using a GoPro HERO9 Black camera mounted on the safety helmet of construction workers across five construction sites, as described in our previous publications20,21. In the original data-collection protocol, video was recorded continuously, and frames were sampled at fixed intervals to capture typical construction-site working conditions. Fixed surveillance-camera footage was not used for the multi-category non-PPE experiments because, at common viewing distances, it typically provides reliable cues only for helmet status, whereas other PPE items, such as masks, vests, gloves, and safety shoes, are often indistinguishable. The original on-site data collection was conducted with permission from the participating construction companies, and informed consent was obtained from the participating workers, as reported in the previous publications20,21.

In the present study, no new image or video data were collected, and there was no direct interaction with human participants. All methods in the present study, including dataset selection, curation, annotation processing, model training, and evaluation, were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. All previously released and publicly available datasets were used in accordance with the original sources’ stated conditions and applicable licenses/terms of use.

Informed consent was obtained from all identifiable participants, or their legal guardians where applicable, for the publication of the relevant images and related information in an online open-access publication. No participant names, patient names, or direct personal identifiers are included in the manuscript, figures, tables, or supplementary materials.

The sampling procedure initially yielded 53,875 frames, from which 3197 representative images were curated to support a balanced distribution of PPE-compliant and non-compliant cases. To broaden scene diversity and contextual variability, three public datasets were additionally incorporated: the Safety Helmet Wearing Dataset (SHWD)33, the Safety Helmet Computer Vision Project (SHCVP)34, and the Hard Hat Workers Dataset (HHWD)35.

Representative samples from the four dataset sources are shown in Fig. 4. Because these public datasets primarily provide helmet-related annotations, images in which other PPE items were not visible were excluded from the multi-category non-PPE analysis. Annotation

For annotation, each occurrence of missing PPE was labeled using rectangular bounding boxes for five categories: no-helmet, no-mask, no-glove, no-vest, and no-shoes. All image regions not associated with these categories were treated as background. Because the detector was designed to localize only PPE violation categories, compliant PPE use was not annotated as a separate class and was instead treated as background during training and evaluation. The annotation process was conducted using LabelImg, and annotations were saved in VOC XML format. Experimental settings

All experiments were conducted on a workstation running Windows 10. The system featured a high-performance Intel Core i7-13700H CPU equipped with 14 cores and 20 threads, along with 128 GB of RAM. Computational tasks were accelerated using a powerful NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU.

Transfer learning with a pre-trained model

Training deep detectors from random initialization typically requires large annotated datasets, substantial computational resources, and long optimization times. To mitigate these requirements, transfer learning is applied: each model is initialized with weights pretrained on a large-scale benchmark and then fine-tuned on the target dataset36. COCO-pretrained weights are adopted for initialization because COCO is a widely used object-detection benchmark and provides strong supervision for both classification and bounding-box regression37–39.

Pretraining on diverse object categories and scene contexts yields generalizable visual features that can accelerate convergence and improve generalization when adapting to non-PPE detection, where targets may be small, densely distributed, and embedded in cluttered backgrounds. During fine-tuning, the pretrained parameters are updated using the experimental dataset to specialize the learned representations for the target domain.

Hyperparameter optimization

Detection accuracy is highly sensitive to training hyperparameters, and performance can vary substantially under different optimization settings. Since no single configuration is optimal across tasks and datasets, hyperparameter tuning is typically conducted by specifying plausible ranges informed by prior studies and empirical evidence from related detection problems. Accordingly, the search ranges adopted here follow commonly reported YOLO-style training practices, including those used in construction-site vision applications.

For consistency across models, all methods were trained and evaluated using an identical search space and the same training protocol. Each method was tested under 243 hyperparameter combinations, and the best-performing configuration was selected for reporting. The search considered batch size {1, 2, 4}, learning rate {0.00025, 0.0005, 0.001}, weight decay {0.00001, 0.00003, 0.00005}, momentum {0.3, 0.5, 0.7}, and maximum training iterations {10,000, 20,000, 30,000}. Additional details on the chosen ranges and combinations are provided in40–42.

An additional observation is that the relative ordering of architectures is stable across seeds: backbones that perform strongly on average (e.g., Proposed, PVT/ConvNeXt) remain consistently competitive, whereas weaker backbones (e.g., ViT/MobileViT) do not close the gap under different initializations. Sensitivity to data split When randomness is introduced through dataset partitioning, variability increases markedly (Table 4; Fig. 7), resulting in broader violin distributions than under weight initialization. Standard deviations in the split-seed setting are consistently larger across metrics, indicating that changes in the composition of training/validation/test subsets dominate performance uncertainty. Despite this, the proposed method remains both the best-performing and among the most stable models (AP50 94.21 ± 0.41, AP50:95 70.02 ± 0.57), evidencing robust generalization across alternative partitions. ViT again exhibits the strongest sensitivity to split selection (AP50:95 61.54 ± 0.88), aligning with the widest distributions.

A further noteworthy pattern is that split variation tends to penalize AP50:95 more than AP50, suggesting that partition-induced distribution shift primarily degrades bounding-box tightness (high-IoU localization) rather than coarse detection. Finally, the proposed method shows only a modest reduction in mean performance from the initialization setting to the split setting (e.g., AP50:95 70.68 → 70.02), indicating that its gains are not specific to a single partition and transfer across multiple splits.

Comparison with other detectors

Comparative detectors and implementation settings To evaluate the proposed approach under consistent experimental conditions, comparative experiments were conducted using representative recent one-stage, two-stage, and transformer-based object detectors. First, YOLOv10 was used as a common detection framework while varying only the backbone network, including CNN-based backbones (CSPNet, ConvNeXt, and EfficientNet) and transformer-based backbones (Swin Transformer, Vision Transformer, Pyramid Vision Transformer, MobileViT, and Axial Transformer). With the detection head and training pipeline held constant, the resulting performance differences primarily reflect the influence of backbone design43–45.

To broaden the benchmarking scope, additional comparisons were conducted with recent external detectors. The one-stage group included YOLOv8 variants (CSPDarknet–DETR, CSPDarknet–T-FPN, and CSPDarknet–CBAM) and YOLOv9 models at multiple scales (YOLOv9t–YOLOv9e). The transformer-based group included DINO with ResNet-50 and Swin Transformer backbones, as well as Deformable DETR with ResNet-50 and ResNet-101 backbones. The two-stage group comprised Faster R-CNN with FPN using ResNet-101, ResNet-152, and MobileNetV3 backbones. To ensure fair comparison, the baseline detectors were implemented and trained using the MMDetection framework46, whereas the YOLOv8-based baselines were built and trained using the Ultralytics toolkit47. Conclusions

This study presented a task-specific enhanced YOLOv10 framework for multi-category PPE non-compliance detection on construction sites by integrating ODConv, EMA-BiFPN, MPDIoU, and a four-scale prediction strategy to better handle small, weak, and partially occluded violation cues.

Experimental results on the benchmark dataset (4060 images with stratified splits and a controlled hyperparameter search) demonstrate that the proposed method achieves the best overall test performance, reaching 92.49 mAP@50 and 70.04 mAP@50:95 at 57.28 FPS. The model also yields consistently strong class-wise results, with test AP50 ranging from 90.83–93.92 across the five categories, and AP50:95 ranging from 68.39–71.60, indicating balanced performance rather than gains concentrated in a single class.

The most pronounced gains are observed under challenging scale regimes. The proposed method achieves \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{A}\mathrm{P}}_{\mathrm{E}\mathrm{S}}$$\end{document} = 82.74 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{A}\mathrm{P}}_{\mathrm{E}}$$\end{document} = 88.96, outperforming strong baselines such as YOLOv9e by + 3.12 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{A}\mathrm{P}}_{\mathrm{E}\mathrm{S}}$$\end{document}) and + 1.00 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{A}\mathrm{P}}_{\mathrm{S}}$$\end{document}), highlighting improved robustness to distant workers and tiny PPE cues. The generalization analysis indicates a moderate validation-to-test gap (e.g., − 1.52 for mAP@50 and − 1.72 for mAP@50:95), with the largest degradations observed for No-shoes and No-glove, and for extremely small objects (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{A}\mathrm{P}}_{\mathrm{E}\mathrm{S}}$$\end{document} gap − 3.18), which aligns with the most difficult visual conditions. Condition-stratified evaluation further confirms that the largest performance drops occur in far-field views and overlapping-worker scenarios, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{A}\mathrm{P}}_{\mathrm{E}\mathrm{S}}$$\end{document} decreases most sharply, indicating that occlusion and tiny-object ambiguity remain key failure modes.

Additional external validation on two previously unseen construction sites provided a stricter assessment of robustness beyond the standard internal split. Although both the baseline YOLOv10 and the proposed method showed some performance degradation under unseen-site conditions, the proposed method remained consistently superior and exhibited slightly smaller reductions across key metrics, particularly for extremely small and small targets.

Overall, the results demonstrate that the proposed method offers a strong accuracy–speed trade-off for deployment-oriented PPE monitoring. Future work should prioritize improving generalization to unseen sites and camera setups, for example through site-wise splitting protocols, domain adaptation, and training strategies that explicitly target far-field and crowded scenes. In addition, deployment-focused optimization (e.g., model compression and edge acceleration) and the use of richer temporal cues from video could further enhance robustness under occlusion and motion blur, particularly for small PPE instances.
