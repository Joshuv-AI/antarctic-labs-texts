Genomic insights into population structure, genetic diversity, and key traits of Jiangxi indigenous pigs
Authors: Huayuan Ji, Yanan Wang, Quanyong Zhou, Mingchun Wan, Nengshui Ding, Huihua Zhou, Meifang Tan, Zhiheng Zou, Hao Chen, Chenlong Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractJiangxi indigenous pigs are a vital genetic resource in China, known for their unique coat colors, high-quality meat, and resilience to environmental stressors. However, their genetic diversity is increasingly threatened by habitat loss, African swine fever, and the introduction of exotic breeds. This study systematically analyzed the population structure, genetic diversity, and selective sweeps in 342 individuals from eight Jiangxi pig breeds using whole-genome sequencing. Population structure analysis revealed distinct genetic clusters for each breed, with some populations, such as Gandong Black, Ganzhongnan Spotted and Hang pigs, showing internal instability and low genetic diversity in breeds like Leping Spotted and Yushan Black (YS) pigs. Selective sweep analysis identified key genes associated with economically important traits: EDNRB and DKK1 were strongly linked to coat color variation, while FGF2 was implicated in white abdominal patterns. For meat quality, PPFIBP1 and SERPINE2 were highlighted in YS pigs, influencing muscle development, fat metabolism, and tenderness. These findings provide crucial insights into the genetic basis of economically significant traits, emphasizing the need for tailored conservation strategies. The study establishes a genomic foundation for sustainable breeding and conservation efforts to safeguard Jiangxi indigenous pigs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12465-1.Keywords: Jiangxi indigenous pigs, Population genetics, Genetic diversity, Coat color genetics, Meat quality traits

Introduction

China is rich in indigenous pig breeds, which are characterized by superior meat quality, strong adaptability, and resilience, making them valuable genetic resources. However, the widespread adoption of intensive farming practices and the introduction of exotic pig breeds have led to a dramatic decrease in the population size of many indigenous pig breeds. Some valuable breeds are now endangered or even on the brink of extinction [1]. Furthermore, the outbreak of African swine fever in recent years has increased the survival pressure on indigenous pigs, making their conservation and sustainable utilization even more urgent [2]. The degradation and loss of indigenous pig genetic resources not only weaken the genetic diversity of China’s pig populations but also impact their ecological adaptability and the stability of local specialty pork production. Therefore, conserving and sustainably utilizing indigenous pig genetic resources in China is of paramount importance.

Jiangxi Province is one of the key regions for indigenous pig genetic resources in China, with eight major indigenous pig breeds: Binhu Black Pig (BH), Yushan Black Pig (YS), Gandong Black Pig (GD), Leping Spotted Pig (LP), Ganzhongnan Spotted Pig (GZ), Hang Pig (HP), Ganxi Two-End Black Pig (GX), and Dongxiang Spotted Pig (DX). These breeds have developed unique traits, such as superior meat quality, diverse coat colors, and docile temperaments, through long-term adaptation to local natural and production environments. However, many of these breeds have faced significant challenges, including severe inbreeding, as indicated by high runs of homozygosity and reduced observed heterozygosity in DX and YS breeds [3]. In addition, introgression from European pig breeds, driven by historical crossbreeding practices aimed at improving growth rates and feed efficiency, has diluted the unique genetic characteristics of these indigenous breeds [4]. Although these breeds are known for their notable meat quality traits, the genetic basis underlying these qualities remains underexplored. Comprehensive genome-wide analyses are lacking, which limits our understanding of the genetic mechanisms and selection signals contributing to these desirable traits [5]. Thus, further genomic studies are urgently needed to identify specific genetic factors influencing the valuable traits of Jiangxi indigenous pigs, and to establish effective conservation and breeding strategies that will ensure the sustainability of these genetic resources.

Coat color is an important external characteristic of indigenous pigs and serves as a crucial indicator for selection in breeding programs. In recent years, several key genes associated with coat color have been identified through genomic studies, including MC1R, KIT, ASIP, EDNRB, and TYRP1, which are considered primary genes controlling coat color diversity in pigs [6, 7]. For instance, mutations in the MC1R gene can lead to variations in coat color, while the KIT gene is associated with the formation of white patches [8]. TYRP1 was identified as a gene associated with brown coat color [9]. Additionally, polymorphisms in the SLC45A2 locus have been linked to reduced or absent melanin levels in mammals, which is strongly associated with the red and golden coat colors observed in European Mangalitza pigs due to missense mutations [10]. The black and white coat color phenotype of Bama Xiang pigs, also known as “Two-End-Black,” is controlled by the EDNRB gene, whereas the phenotype of Jinhua Two-End-Black pigs differs [5]. The Two-End-Black coat color phenotype of GX remains undetermined and warrants further study to elucidate the underlying genetic mechanisms. These findings suggest that the coat color of GX may involve complex interactions among multiple genes, underscoring the need for additional research to provide a scientific basis for the conservation and improvement of this breed.

Meat quality is another key indicator of the economic value of pigs and is influenced by a combination of genetic and environmental factors. In recent years, significant progress has been made in studying meat quality traits in pigs. Researchers have identified several key genes associated with meat quality traits, including IGF2, RYR1, MC4R, and CAST, which are known to play important roles in muscle development, fat deposition, and meat quality [11–13]. These studies have provided important insights into the genetic basis of meat quality in pigs. Jiangxi indigenous pigs, particularly the YS breed, are known for their tender meat and high alpha-linolenic acid content, giving them a unique competitive advantage in the market. However, the genetic basis of meat quality in Jiangxi indigenous pigs remains largely unexplored. Further research on the genetic mechanisms underlying meat quality in these breeds is essential for improving meat quality traits and developing scientifically sound breeding strategies.

This study aims to comprehensively analyze the genetic structure and selective sweeps of Jiangxi indigenous pigs through whole-genome sequencing and genetic diversity analysis, identifying genomic regions associated with economically important traits such as meat quality and coat color. Under the threat of African swine fever, the conservation of many indigenous pig breeds is becoming increasingly critical, making this study essential for understanding genetic diversity and developing effective conservation measures. By analyzing the whole-genome data of eight Jiangxi indigenous pig breeds, we hope to provide a scientific basis for the conservation and utilization of these valuable genetic resources. Additionally, by revealing breed-specific genes and selective sweeps, we aim to support genetic improvement and sustainable breeding of indigenous pigs, ensuring their competitiveness in intensive production and market contexts.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and sequencing

This study included 342 individuals from eight indigenous pig breeds from Jiangxi Province, China. Ear tissue samples were collected, and genomic DNA was extracted using the phenol/chloroform method. The quality of genomic DNA, including purity, concentration, and integrity, was assessed using a Nanodrop 2000 UV spectrophotometer and agarose gel electrophoresis. DNA samples that met the quality criteria were used for library construction and whole-genome sequencing. The pig breeds included in the study were: BH (n = 30), GD (n = 45), LP (n = 23), YS (n = 59), GX (n = 46), DX (n = 45), GZ (n = 54), and HP (n = 40). Whole-genome libraries (insert size ≈ 350 bp) were sequenced in paired-end mode (2 × 150 bp) on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The mean raw coverage per individual was 12×.

The reference genome Sus scrofa 11.1 was indexed using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) v0.7.17 [14]. Sequence alignment was then performed with the BWA-MEM algorithm. SAMtools v1.15.1 [15] was used to convert alignment results from SAM to BAM format and sort the binary files. Sentieon’s DNAseq pipeline [16] was employed for variant calling, using the LocusCollector and Realigner functions to remove duplicate reads, realign indices, and recalibrate base quality scores. Only properly paired alignments with mapping quality (MAPQ) ≥ 30 were retained. Variants were generated with the Sentieon DNAseq pipeline and filtered using GATK v4.1.9 VariantFiltration [17] with the following site-level thresholds: QD < 2.0, FS > 60.0, MQ < 40.0, MQRankSum < − 12.5, ReadPosRankSum < − 8.0, and SOR > 3.0; we additionally required site QUAL ≥ 30 and retained only biallelic autosomal SNPs. At the genotype level, we required DP ≥ 5 and GQ ≥ 20. SNP datasets were extracted using VCFtools [18], and PLINK v1.9 [19] was used for SNP genotyping and quality control. SNPs with call rate ≤ 0.90 and individuals with call rate ≤ 0.90 were removed, and variants with minor allele frequency (MAF) ≤ 0.05 were excluded. After filtering, 31,005,858 high-quality SNPs were retained for downstream analyses. Annotation of SNPs was performed using SnpEff v5.0 [20].

Population structure and genetic diversity

To investigate the population structure of Jiangxi indigenous pigs, multiple analytical approaches were applied, including neighbor-joining (NJ) tree construction, principal component analysis (PCA), and individual ancestry components. Sex chromosomes were excluded to ensure only high-quality autosomal SNP data were used. Pairwise identity-by-state (IBS) distances were computed in PLINK v1.9 and converted to a distance matrix (1 − IBS) for neighbor-joining tree construction in phylip v3.69 [21]. The tree was visualized using iTOL[22]. PCA was performed with GCTA v1.26 [23] to investigate population stratification. The genetic relationship matrix was created using the “-make-grm” option, and the first four principal components were calculated using the “-pca4” option to assess genetic variation among samples. To further explore admixture, the dataset was pruned using the command “--indep-pairwise 50 10 0.2” on the initial 31 million SNP dataset. The pruned dataset was analyzed using ADMIXTURE [24] to estimate individual ancestry components, with Q-values visualized in R, assuming ancestral populations ranging from 2 to 8.

The genetic diversity of Jiangxi indigenous pig populations was comprehensively assessed through 11 genetic parameters. SNPs within each breed were categorized based on minor allele frequency (MAF): common variants (CSNP) were those with an MAF greater than 0.2, while rare variants (RSNP) were defined by an MAF between 0 and 0.05. The ratio of heterozygous loci to the total loci was denoted as Pn. PLINK was employed to evaluate several genetic diversity metrics, including the heterozygosity ratio (Het), expected heterozygosity (He), and observed heterozygosity (Ho), using the “--freq,” “--het,” and “--hardy” commands, respectively. The inbreeding coefficient (F), derived from genomic heterozygosity, as well as the genetic distances within breeds (DST) and accumulated runs of homozygosity (FROH), were also computed using PLINK. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) was assessed by calculating the squared correlation coefficient (r²) between pairs of SNPs, applying PLINK with the parameters “--r2 --ld-window-kb 1000 --ld-window-r2 0” to determine the extent of LD over a 1,000 kb genomic window. Nucleotide diversity (π), which provides an estimate of population polymorphism levels, was calculated genome-wide using VCFtools. This was conducted with a sliding window of 20 kb and a step size of 10 kb to capture variations in genetic diversity across the genome. The visualization of these genetic diversity parameters was conducted in R.

Selective sweep analysis

To identify genomic regions associated with coat color in Jiangxi indigenous pigs, three comparison groups were designed, and selective sweep analyses were conducted using locus-specific branch length (LSBL) analysis [25] and cross-population extended haplotype homozygosity (XPEHH) analysis [26]. The analyses were combined to improve detection efficiency. The pigs were divided into three groups: group A (GX and DX), group B (BH, YS, and GD with black coat color), and group C (GZ, LP, and HP with a mixed black-and-white coat pattern). LSBL scores for all qualified SNPs in the dataset were calculated using custom R scripts. The locus-specific branch length (x) for group A compared to groups B and C was calculated using pairwise Fst as follows: x = (FstAB + FstAC - FstBC) / 2, where A, B, and C represent the three populations. Subsequently, XPEHH analysis was performed using a 10-kb non-overlapping sliding window to calculate XPEHH scores between group A and groups B and C. Positive XPEHH values indicated that the experimental group underwent positive selection, while negative values suggested positive selection in the control group. Windows with fewer than 10 SNPs were excluded, and the top 1% of windows were set as the significance threshold. To reduce false-positive signals, the intersection of regions identified by both LSBL and XPEHH was used to identify candidate regions under significant selection. These regions were considered reliable and subjected to gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis with Metascape [27], querying GO Biological Process, KEGG, and Reactome using the whole Sus scrofa genome as background. P values were adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure; terms with FDR < 0.05 were considered significant. For further exploration of Two-End black coat color selection signals in GX, XPEHH analysis was performed with GX as the experimental group compared to DX, group B and C. To investigate selection signals related to meat quality in YS, we conducted Fst and XPEHH between YS and other breeds, followed by gene enrichment analysis to elucidate the potential genetic basis for meat quality traits.

Functional annotation and haplotype analysis of candidate genes

Haplotype construction of target regions was performed using the fastPhase function in Beagle [28], and visualized using the pheatmap package in R. π and Hp within a 10-kb window of the target regions were calculated using VCFtools and PLINK, respectively. A haplotype network was constructed using the pegas v1.1 package in R [29] to visualize relationships among haplotypes. Additionally, absolute and relative allele frequencies within the studied populations were calculated to verify the relevance of target regions, and the potential effects of SNPs were predicted using SnpEff v5.0.

Results

Population structure and genetic diversity of Jiangxi Indigenous pigs

To elucidate the genetic relationships among Jiangxi indigenous pig breeds, a neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree was constructed based on an individual pairwise IBS matrix (Fig. 1A). The clustering of individuals strictly according to their respective breeds highlighted pronounced genetic differentiation among the eight pig populations. However, some pig breeds exhibited internal separation, which was particularly evident in GD, GZ, and HP. PCA provided additional insights into population stratification (Fig. 1B). The first principal component (PC1), which explained 8.87% of the total genetic variance, effectively separated seven Jiangxi indigenous pig breeds. The second principal component (PC2) further delineated the genetic differentiation between YS and the other breeds. To investigate the origin, differentiation, and potential historical admixture events of these breeds, ADMIXTURE analysis was performed, assuming the number of ancestral populations (K) to range from 2 to 8 (Fig. 1C). The lowest cross-validation (CV) error was observed at K = 8, supporting the genetic subdivision of the individuals into eight distinct subpopulations. This finding reflects both the historical isolation and selective pressures acting on these breeds, which have maintained their genetic distinctiveness over generations.

The genetic diversity of the eight Jiangxi indigenous pig breeds demonstrates generally consistent patterns across key metrics, with notable differences among specific breeds (Table 1, Supplementary Figure S1 and Supplementary Figure S2). Overall, GD, GX, GZ, and HP exhibited relatively high levels of genetic diversity. These breeds are characterized by a large number of common variants—15.2 million (≈ 49%), 13.6 million (≈ 44%), 14.1 million (≈ 45%), and 12.9 million (≈ 42%), respectively—high heterozygosity rates (0.66 ± 0.07; 0.62 ± 0.05; 0.60 ± 0.08; and 0.61 ± 0.05), low inbreeding coefficients (0.07 ± 0.09; 0.16 ± 0.07; 0.18 ± 0.10; and 0.17 ± 0.07), and a rapid decay of LD. These findings suggest a relatively healthy genetic structure in these populations. In contrast, LP and YS exhibited the lowest levels of genetic diversity, as evidenced by reduced heterozygosity, higher inbreeding coefficients, and slower LD decay, indicating a more vulnerable genetic status that requires urgent conservation efforts. BH and DX showed intermediate levels of genetic diversity, reflecting relatively balanced but less robust genetic structures compared to the higher-diversity breeds.

Selection signals associated with coat color

Coat color is one of the most visually distinctive phenotypic traits for identifying pig breeds. Among the eight indigenous pig breeds in Jiangxi, coat color exhibits significant variation, serving as a key indicator of breed differentiation. To investigate selection signals related to coat color in GX and DX pigs, genome-wide analyses were conducted using locus-specific branch length (LSBL) and cross-population extended haplotype homozygosity (XPEHH) methods. These complementary approaches enabled the identification of genomic regions under selection that distinguish GX and DX pigs from other Jiangxi indigenous pig breeds, offering valuable insights into the genetic basis of coat color variation.

LSBL analysis was performed by comparing GX and DX pigs with two reference groups: black-coated pigs (YS, GD, and BH) and mixed-coat pigs (GZ, LP, and HP). At the top 1% threshold, 2,140 selective windows were identified, involving 656 unique genes after filtering (Fig. 2A). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of these genes in pathways related to brain development (Supplementary Figure S3). XPEHH analysis was applied to evaluate haplotype-based selection signals between GX and DX pigs and the black-coated pig group (YS, GD, and BH). This analysis identified 2,140 selective windows, with 417 unique genes annotated at the top 1% threshold (Fig. 2B). GO enrichment analysis highlighted five genes associated with pigment cell differentiation (GO:0050931), emphasizing their functional relevance in coat color determination (Supplementary Figure S4). Additionally, comparisons of GX and DX with mixed-coat breeds (GZ, LP, and HP) revealed 2,257 selective windows and 447 unique genes (Fig. 2C). GO enrichment of these genes indicated significant involvement in receptor tyrosine kinase and Wnt signaling pathways, both of which are known regulators of melanogenesis and pigmentation (Supplementary Figure S5).

To enhance the reliability of the candidate regions associated with coat color traits in GX and DX pigs, overlapping selective windows from two types of XPEHH analyses were consolidated. This integrative approach identified 781 selective windows, encompassing 162 unique genes (Supplementary Table S1). GO enrichment analysis revealed key pathways related to pigmentation, including melanogenesis and the Wnt signaling pathway (Supplementary Figure S6). Among these, EDNRB and DKK1 emerged as highly significant candidate genes across multiple analytical frameworks, underscoring their importance in coat color regulation. Further analysis identified 37 overlapping windows between LSBL and the two XPEHH analyses, which contained 14 unique genes (Supplementary Table S2). Notably, DKK1 exhibited the most significant signals across all three analyses, with LSBL = 0.31, XPEHHblack-coated=0.54, and XPEHHmixed-coat=0.70. These findings reinforce the pivotal role of DKK1 in pigmentation processes.

Selection characteristics of coat color-associated genes

To further investigate the selection characteristics of genes associated with coat color in GX and DX pigs, a haplotype heatmap was generated for the genomic region containing the EDNRB gene. The results revealed significant haplotype differences in GX and DX compared to other Jiangxi indigenous pig breeds (Fig. 3A). Nucleotide diversity (π) and heterozygosity (Hp) were subsequently calculated for the EDNRB region across all groups (Fig. 3B), providing evidence of strong selection in GX and DX. A haplotype network for the EDNRB region identified 19 major haplotypes shared by GX and DX, with the most frequently observed haplotype appearing 78 times across four breeds: 27 occurrences in GX, 39 in DX, 1 in GD, and 11 in GZ (Fig. 3C). Within the EDNRB gene region, GX and DX exhibited extreme allele frequency differences (> 0.95) at 68 loci compared to black-coated pigs, all of which were located within intronic regions (Supplementary Table S3). Among these loci, the site at CHR11:50088403 displayed the largest allele frequency difference (0.96).

Selection signals associated with coat color in Ganxi Two-End black pigs

To further elucidate the genetic basis underlying the Two-End Black coat color trait, comparative analyses were performed between GX pigs and DX pigs, black-coated pigs, and mixed-coat pigs. First, an XPEHH analysis comparing GX and DX pigs identified 2,257 selective windows containing 466 unique genes (Figure S7A). GO enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of 45 genes in the receptor tyrosine kinase signaling pathway (Supplementary Figure S8), highlighting its potential involvement in pigmentation processes. Among these, FGF2 emerged as a key gene, with previous studies demonstrating its role in regulating melanocyte activity via PAX3 activation through the STAT3 pathway [30], further supporting its relevance in pigmentation biology. Subsequently, XPEHH analyses were performed to compare GX pigs against black-coated pigs and mixed-coat pigs (Figures S7B and S7C). By intersecting the top 1% of selective windows from both comparisons, 1,134 selective windows were identified, annotated with 229 unique genes (Supplementary Table S5). Notably, seven of these genes, including EDNRB, MITF, CDK14, MAGI2, IGF1R, PRKG1, and SEMA3B, were associated with the Wnt signaling pathway, a key regulator of melanogenesis and pigmentation (Supplementary Figure S9).

Analysis of the EDNRB gene region revealed that GX and DX pigs exhibited nearly identical haplotype structures and selection patterns (Fig. 3), underscoring their shared genetic foundation for the Two-End Black coat color. To deepen our understanding, we conducted a detailed investigation of the FGF2 gene region. The haplotype heatmap showed a nearly fixed haplotype in GX pigs, distinguishing them from other breeds (Figure S10A). π and Hp analyses corroborated these findings, indicating strong selection in the FGF2 region (Figure S10B). A haplotype network analysis identified three major haplotypes in the region, one of which was unique to GX pigs (Figure S10C). These results suggest that FGF2 and EDNRB play crucial roles in determining the distinctive coat color of Ganxi Two-End Black pigs.

Selection signals associated with meat quality in Yushan black pigs

The YS breed is renowned for its exceptional meat quality, characterized by thin skin, fine bones, tender texture, and rich flavor. To identify selection signals linked to these traits, we conducted Fst and XPEHH analyses comparing YS pigs with seven other Jiangxi indigenous pig breeds (Fig. 5). These analyses identified 43 selective windows, with 40 unique genes shared between the top 1% of both approaches (Supplementary Table S6). Among these, 17 genes were significantly enriched in the glutamate receptor signaling pathway (Supplementary Figure S11), including GRIA1, GRID2, GRIK2, GRM5, DLG2, ERBB4, PPFIBP1, NBEA, SERPINE2, PCDH17, DACH1, GLI3, PKHD1, PARD3, SLC30A8, and CPNE4. This pathway is known to influence muscle development and meat quality traits such as texture and tenderness.

Haplotype analysis of the PPFIBP1 gene region in YS pigs revealed unique haplotypes compared to other breeds (Supplementary Figure S12A). π and Hp analyses further confirmed strong selection in this region (Supplementary Figure S12B). A haplotype network identified 10 major haplotypes, all exclusive to YS (Supplementary Figure S12C). Similarly, haplotypes of the SERPINE2 gene region displayed significant differentiation between YS and other Jiangxi pig breeds (Fig. 6A). π and Hp analyses again indicated strong selection in YS (Fig. 6B). Haplotype network analysis identified nine major haplotypes unique to YS in the SERPINE2 region (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, 56 loci within the SERPINE2 region exhibited extreme allele frequency differences (> 0.9) between YS and the other breeds (Supplementary Table S7). One notable variant, CHR15:125767538, located within exon 5 (exon5:c.G405A), displayed an allele frequency difference of 0.92, while the most divergent site, located in an intergenic region (dist = 1,292), showed a frequency difference of 0.98.

Discussion

Genetic status of Jiangxi Indigenous pigs

Jiangxi indigenous pigs are a valuable reservoir of genetic resources, characterized by their high-quality meat, diverse coat colors, docile temperament, and resilience to environmental stressors. These unique traits position them as an essential gene pool for both local and global breeding programs, offering opportunities to improve commercial pig traits and conserve genetic diversity.

Genetic diversity and population structure analyses revealed that each Jiangxi indigenous pig breed forms a distinct genetic cluster. This highlights significant genetic isolation among the breeds, shaped by historical geographic and reproductive isolation, as well as the effects of natural and artificial selection. These factors have preserved the genetic integrity and uniqueness of each breed. Despite this differentiation, some populations, such as GD, GZ, and HP, displayed signs of internal separation, characterized by relatively distant genetic relationships among individuals within the same breed. This instability suggests insufficient conservation measures, exacerbated by challenges such as African swine fever outbreaks and habitat loss.

The conservation status of GD, GZ, and HP is suboptimal and warrants immediate intervention. Efforts should aim to clarify breed characteristics, establish more cohesive genetic relationships within populations, and enhance their overall stability. Similarly, breeds such as LP and YS, which exhibit low levels of genetic diversity, are particularly vulnerable to genetic erosion and require tailored conservation strategies. These strategies should include the establishment of core breeding populations, implementation of genetic monitoring programs, cryopreservation of genetic material, promotion of sustainable breeding practices, and integration of local and national conservation efforts.

Conversely, breeds such as GD, GX, GZ, and HP exhibit relatively high levels of genetic diversity, making them valuable candidates for sustainable management and utilization. These genetically diverse populations should be leveraged in breeding programs to enhance resilience to environmental stressors and improve economically important traits in commercial breeds. However, maintaining their genetic diversity requires careful population management to prevent genetic drift and minimize inbreeding. BH and DX breeds, which displayed intermediate levels of genetic diversity, present opportunities for targeted improvement. These breeds can serve as models for balancing genetic diversity with trait-specific selection, providing insights into sustainable breeding strategies for other populations.

Conservation implications

Based on our diversity and inbreeding metrics (Ho/He, F, FROH, and LD decay), we outline practical conservation targets to stabilize at-risk populations and maintain diversity in genetically richer breeds: prioritize matings that keep genomic inbreeding at F < 0.10, avoid pairwise kinship ≥ 0.125, increase the number of unrelated sires and dams to raise the effective breeding base, and implement diversity-constrained mating, alongside cryo-banking (semen/embryos) and annual genomic monitoring. Because the current sampling integrates core herds of uneven sizes and incomplete pedigrees, LD-based Ne estimates would be sensitive to sampling bias; accordingly, we rely here on F and FROH as robust proxies and identify standardized Ne estimation under a unified sampling design as a priority for follow-up work.

The genes of coat color traits in Jiangxi indigenous pigs

Coat color is one of the most visually distinctive traits of Jiangxi indigenous pigs, with significant variation observed among breeds. Understanding the genetic mechanisms underlying these phenotypic differences provides critical insights into breed differentiation and selection history. In this study, we identified two key candidate genes, EDNRB and DKK1, associated with coat color through comparative analyses involving GX, DX, black-coated pigs, and mixed-coat pigs.

GX and DX pigs share the characteristic Two-End Black phenotype, with DX pigs further distinguished by an additional black spot on the midsection. When GX and DX were analyzed as a group against other pig breeds, EDNRB emerged as a primary candidate gene. This gene is well-documented for its pivotal role in melanocyte differentiation and function, contributing to pigmentation through its involvement in melanogenesis pathways, including tyrosine metabolism, Wnt signaling, and cAMP signaling. These pathways are integral to the production and distribution of melanin. Moreover, EDNRB signaling promotes the proliferation and differentiation of melanocyte stem cells, significantly enhancing the regeneration of hair and epidermal melanocytes [31]. Previous studies have also linked EDNRB to the Two-End Black coat color in pigs [5, 32, 33], supporting its critical role in pigmentation. In our analysis, a unique haplotype in the EDNRB region was detected in GX and DX populations, with 68 intronic loci showing extreme allele frequency differences (> 0.95) compared to other breeds. Although introns do not encode proteins, they play crucial roles in regulating gene expression, splicing, and alternative transcription, which can significantly impact phenotypic traits. Notably, while this haplotype was also present in GZ pigs, its frequency was much lower, corresponding with GZ’s lighter coat color, which may be influenced by additional genetic factors.

DKK1 also emerged as a key candidate gene in our analysis. This gene functions as an antagonist of the Wnt signaling pathway, modulating melanocyte activity and pigment production by inhibiting β-catenin-mediated transcription. This inhibition reduces melanocyte proliferation and melanogenesis [34, 35]. Here, DKK1 was consistently identified across all pairwise analyses, with 28 loci showing significant frequency differences. These loci were located in intergenic regions containing enhancer sequences, which regulate the expression of genes at distant loci. Alterations in enhancer-binding proteins in these regions can lead to reprogrammed gene expression and phenotypic changes, thereby influencing coat color.

Interestingly, direct comparisons between GX and DX revealed no significant signals for EDNRB, suggesting that this gene is specifically associated with the shared Two-End Black phenotype rather than the midsection black spot observed in DX pigs. However, EDNRB was consistently identified in comparisons of GX with black-coated and mixed-coat pigs, reinforcing its role as a determinant of the Two-End Black phenotype. In addition to EDNRB, the gene FGF2 was highlighted in comparisons involving GX, black-coated pigs, and mixed-coat pigs. FGF2 activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, increasing β-catenin nuclear localization and promoting the expression of MITF, a critical regulator of melanogenesis [36]. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that FGF2 may influence epithelial cell differentiation and pigment deposition, contributing to the white abdominal pattern observed in GX pigs. These findings provide valuable insights into the genetic basis of coat color variation in Jiangxi indigenous pigs and highlight potential targets for breeding and conservation efforts aimed at preserving these unique phenotypic traits.

The genes of meat quality traits in Yushan black pigs

YS pigs are renowned for their exceptional meat quality, characterized by tenderness, fine texture, and excellent flavor. Using genome-wide selection analyses, we identified 43 selective windows, within which 17 genes were significantly enriched in the glutamate receptor signaling pathway. This pathway plays a crucial role in muscle development and meat quality traits, such as tenderness and texture, emphasizing its functional importance in shaping these phenotypic characteristics [37].

Among the identified genes, PPFIBP1 and SERPINE2 emerged as primary candidates associated with the superior meat quality of YS pigs. PPFIBP1, a critical regulator within the NF-κB pathway, governs inflammation, immune responses, and cell proliferation. Dysregulation of the ERK/NF-κB/AP-1 signaling axis, where PPFIBP1 is involved, has been linked to alterations in muscle growth and meat quality traits [38]. In our study, unique haplotypes were identified in the PPFIBP1 gene region in YS pigs, with significantly lower π and Hp compared to other breeds, indicating strong selection pressure in this genomic region. This suggests PPFIBP1 plays an important role in regulating muscle development and improving meat texture and tenderness.

Similarly, SERPINE2, a gene previously associated with fat metabolism in broilers [39], exhibited pronounced haplotype differentiation between YS and other Jiangxi pig breeds. Within this region, 56 loci displayed extreme allele frequency differences (> 0.9) between YS and other breeds. Notably, one variant located at CHR15:125767538 within exon 5 (exon5:c.G405A) may influence protein function, potentially affecting fat deposition and meat tenderness. These findings suggest that SERPINE2 contributes not only to fat metabolism but also to the overall quality of YS pork.

Generalizability and breeding application

Beyond Jiangxi, the analytical framework and candidate loci identified here can inform diversity-constrained genomic improvement in other indigenous breeds under national conservation initiatives: the pigmentation loci (EDNRB, DKK1, FGF2) and the meat-quality loci (PPFIBP1, SERPINE2) can be incorporated into genomic selection indices that explicitly balance trait gains with diversity maintenance. While cross-provincial, like-for-like whole-genome datasets were not available within the scope of this study, extending validation to additional conservation herds is a defined next step to assess transferability and refine index weights under different production goals.

Conclusion

This study highlights the genetic diversity and population structure of Jiangxi indigenous pigs, identifying key candidate genes underlying coat color (EDNRB, DKK1, and FGF2) and meat quality (PPFIBP1 and SERPINE2) traits. These findings underscore the functional importance of pathways such as Wnt signaling in pigmentation and NF-κB in muscle development, offering valuable insights into the genetic basis of economically important traits. The results provide a foundation for integrating genomic tools into conservation and breeding programs to preserve the unique genetic resources of Jiangxi indigenous pigs and enhance their utility in sustainable livestock improvement.
