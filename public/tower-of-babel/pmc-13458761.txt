Cumulative mild stress during early life alters prefrontal astrocytic proteins and elevates peripheral S100B in young adult mice
Authors: Jinho Kim, Geon Ko, Jong-Hoon Kim, Keun-A Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe prevalence of major depressive disorder (MDD) in young adulthood has increased in recent decades and represents a leading cause of psychological burden, highlighting the importance of prevention and early intervention for depression in young adults. Early-life stress has been closely associated with the onset of depression in young adulthood, leading to adverse outcomes, including reduced quality of life, impaired social functioning, and socioeconomic burden. Among the various neuropathological features of depression, astrocytic abnormalities are increasingly recognized as key contributors. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the association between dysregulated astrocyte expression and depression in young adults. We utilized CPMS (cumulative mild prenatal stress, maternal separation and social defeat) mice as a young adult depression model, in which animals were exposed to a cumulative mild stress (CMS) paradigm during development, including prenatal stress, maternal separation, and social defeat. We further categorized CPMS mice into susceptible and resilient groups based on social interaction behavior. To explore the translational relevance of our findings, we subsequently analyzed blood samples from drug-naïve patients with MDD in young adulthood (mean age of 24 years). CPMS-susceptible mice of both sexes exhibited anxiety- and depression-like behaviors, accompanied by reduced expression of astrocytic markers in the prefrontal cortex. Furthermore, elevated S100B protein levels were observed in the blood of both CPMS mice and patients, showing a positive correlation with the severity of clinical symptoms. Together, these findings indicate that exposure to CMS during early life is associated with altered astrocytic protein expression and elevated circulating S100B, highlighting its potential relevance as a biomarker for depression in young adulthood.Subject terms: Molecular neuroscience, Depression

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a prevalent psychiatric condition characterized by persistent low mood, anxiety, anhedonia, and diminished self-worth, which collectively impair social and occupational functioning [1–4]. In recent years, the incidence of clinically diagnosed depression has increased among younger populations, with young adults exhibiting the highest rates across age groups [5–7]. Although MDD can manifest at any stage of life, onset most commonly occurs between the ages of 15 and 29 years, a critical developmental window referred to as young adulthood [5, 8].

Early life stress is strongly associated with increased vulnerability to psychiatric disorders, including MDD and post-traumatic stress disorder [9, 10]. The brain undergoes rapid structural and functional maturation from childhood through adolescence, accompanied by extensive epigenetic remodeling. These changes render the brain particularly sensitive to the long-term effects of early life stress, potentially resulting in disrupted neural connectivity and impaired cognitive and emotional regulation [11]. Given these vulnerabilities, early detection and intervention in young adults are essential to mitigate adverse outcomes, such as recurrent episodes, chronic symptomatology, and long-term functional impairments [12]. Despite the increasing clinical burden, the neurobiological mechanisms underlying MDD in young adulthood remain poorly understood, and age-specific preclinical and clinical studies are limited.

Astrocytes, the most abundant glial cells in the central nervous system, play key roles in maintaining brain homeostasis, regulating neurotransmitter metabolism, and supporting synaptic plasticity [13, 14]. Increasing evidence implicates astrocyte dysfunction in the pathophysiology of MDD, with both structural and functional abnormalities observed in affected individuals [15, 16]. Notably, reductions in glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)-positive astrocytes have been reported in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) of young adult patients with MDD, whereas such reductions appear less pronounced or absent in older individuals [17]. Consistent findings have been reported in animal models, where chronic stress reduces GFAP mRNA expression in the PFC [18]. In addition to astrocyte loss or marker downregulation, functional impairments, such as disrupted neurovascular coupling and altered glutamate homeostasis, have been identified in both clinical and preclinical contexts, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based studies [19]. These findings suggest that astrocytic dysfunction in the PFC contributes to the neurobiology of depression, particularly in young adults.

Blood–brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction is another critical factor implicated in MDD pathogenesis. The BBB serves as a specialized interface between the central nervous system and peripheral circulation, essential for maintaining neuronal homeostasis and protecting the brain from systemic insults [20–22]. Increased BBB permeability has been observed in patients with MDD, particularly those aged 28–30 years [23]. Neurovascular abnormalities have also been described in rodent models of stress-induced depression [24]. Astrocytes, endothelial cells, and pericytes form the cellular components of the BBB [25]. Astrocytic endfeet enwrap cerebral blood vessels and are vital for maintaining barrier integrity by regulating ion, water, and neurotransmitter homeostasis [26]. Importantly, reduced astrocytic endfeet coverage has been observed in the brains of patients with MDD, correlating with increased BBB permeability [27]. In addition, S100B, a calcium-binding protein primarily synthesized and secreted by astrocytes, serves as a clinical peripheral marker of BBB disruption following brain injury [28, 29]. Elevated serum S100B levels have been reported in both stress-induced mouse models and patients with MDD, likely reflecting astrocytic dysfunction, inflammation, and elevated corticosterone levels [30, 31].

The present study investigated the impact of cumulative mild stress (CMS) during critical periods of brain development on astrocytic and depression-related phenotypes in young adulthood using a mouse model of young adult depression, termed CPMS. The CMS paradigm has been used to model early-life adversity commonly experienced by individuals who develop depression in young adulthood, reflecting exposure during critical stages of brain development [32]. It incorporates mild, developmentally timed stressors, including prenatal stress, maternal separation, and social defeat stress. The preclinical findings were validated using serum samples from young adult patients with MDD (aged 23–25 years). Both sexes were included to account for potential sex-dependent differences in vulnerability and stress responsiveness [33].

In this context, we hypothesized that CMS during early life disrupts astrocytic integrity and BBB stability, potentially contributing to increased peripheral S100B levels, and that an astrocyte–BBB–S100B axis may represent a shared pathophysiological mechanism linking preclinical models to young adult patients with MDD. To examine this hypothesis, astrocytic expression in the PFC and associated behavioral phenotypes were assessed, and serum S100B levels were measured in CPMS mice. To validate the translational relevance of these findings, peripheral S100B levels were analyzed in young adult patients with MDD and examined for correlations with clinical symptom severity.

Materials and methods

Animals and CMS protocol

Male and female C57BL/6N (Dae Han Bio-Link Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea) were used in this study. Following an adaptation period, the mice were housed under a 12:12 h light-dark cycle in a temperature- and humidity-controlled environment (22 ± 2 °C, 40–60%), with ad libitum access to food and water, except during stress procedures. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Lee Gil Ya Cancer and Diabetes Institute, Gachon University (IACUC No. LCDI-2023-0081).

To establish a mouse model of young adult depression (CPMS), the mice were exposed to CMS across key developmental periods, from the prenatal to adolescent stages, as described in our previous study [32]. Pregnant dams were exposed to unpredictable mild stress for 16 consecutive days during gestation (Supplementary Figure 1A). After birth, the pups were subjected to daily maternal separation for 3 h per day from postnatal day 2 (P2) to P14 (Supplementary Figure 1B). The pups were housed individually under standard conditions following each separation. At 4 weeks of age, the mice were exposed to mild social defeat stress for 10 consecutive days (Supplementary Figure 1C). These stressors were applied using modified protocols with reduced duration and intensity relative to standard paradigms (e.g., shortened physical and sensory contact in the social defeat paradigm), resulting in a milder cumulative stress exposure.

A modified social defeat paradigm based on a previous study [34] was used for the CPMS protocol for female mice. Prior to each session, urine from an unfamiliar male CD-1 mouse was applied to the vaginal region of the experimental female mouse to stimulate aggression toward the resident CD-1 aggressor. After a brief defeat encounter, the female mice were returned to their home cages.

Behavioral tests

At 7 weeks of age (the time corresponding to young adulthood in mice), a series of behavioral tests were conducted to evaluate anxiety- and depression-like behaviors. Anxiety-like behavior was assessed using the elevated plus maze (EPM) test, while depression-like behavior was evaluated using the social interaction test (SI), sucrose preference test (SPT), and forced swim test (FST). All behaviors were recorded automatically and analyzed using an Ethovision XT 17 tracking system (Noldus Information Technology, Netherlands).

Prior to testing, the mice were habituated to the behavioral testing room for 30 min. After each test, the apparatus was cleaned with 70% ethanol and allowed to dry completely to eliminate olfactory cues. Detailed protocols for each behavioral test are provided in the Supplementary Material.

For behavioral analyses, sample sizes were determined based on prior studies using similar behavioral paradigms and empirical experience. Animals were classified into experimental groups based on their performance in the SI test.

Brain tissue sampling and immunohistochemistry

The mice were anesthetized via an intramuscular injection of Zoletil (16.7 mg/kg) and Rompun (15.5 mg/kg), as previously described [35]. For immunofluorescence analysis, the mice were transcardially perfused with saline, and the brains were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight at 4 °C. Fixed brains were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose solution for 72 h at 4 °C and subsequently embedded in OCT compound (Sakura Finetek, Japan) prior to freezing. For western blot analysis, the PFC was dissected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for later use.

Frozen brains were coronally sectioned at a thickness of 30 μm using a cryostat (Cryotome, Thermo Electron Corporation, USA). The sections were stored in cryoprotectant solution at 4 °C until use. For immunostaining, the sections were washed in PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100 (PBS-T), blocked in PBS-T supplemented with 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 3% normal goat serum, and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody against GFAP (DAKO, z0334), aquaporin-4 (Proteintech, 16473-1-AP), and connexin-43 (Sigma-Aldrich, c6219). The following day, the sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Invitrogen, USA). After washing, the sections were mounted on glass slides using Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI (Vector Laboratories, USA). Fluorescent images were acquired using a Nikon TS2-S-SM microscope (Nikon Microscopy, Tokyo, Japan), equipped with a Nikon DS-Qi2 camera (Nikon Microscopy). Four representative coronal sections per mouse were imaged at 200x magnification for quantitative analysis.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed as described previously [36]. PFC tissues were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 50 mM Tris, pH 8.0), supplemented with protease inhibitors (Roche Applied Science, Germany) and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min and centrifuged at 17,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were determined using the Bradford protein assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA).

Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) on 8% or 12% gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck, USA). Membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature in 3% BSA in TBS-T (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20), followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-GFAP (DAKO, z0334), anti-IL-17 (Santa Cruz, sc-374218), anti-aquaporin-4 (Proteintech, 16473-1-AP), and anti-connexin-43 (Sigma-Aldrich, c6219). After three washes with TBS-T, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents, PicoEPD (ELPISBIO, Korea) and Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Millipore, USA). Chemiluminescent signals were visualized using BLUE X-ray film (AGFA, Belgium), and band intensities were quantified with ImageJ software (v1.4.3.67).

Participants and clinical data

This clinical study was conducted according to a previously published protocol [37]. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Gachon University Gil Medical Center (approval no. GFIRB2018-156, approved on May 15, 2018) and in compliance with international ethical guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Given the focus of the study on MDD in young adulthood, participants between the ages of 19 and 35 years were recruited. MDD was diagnosed based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV), using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV (SCID-IV). Exclusion criteria included current or past Axis I psychiatric comorbidities, substance abuse or dependence, neurological or significant medical disorders, a history of psychiatric treatment, and use of psychotropic medications. A total of 91 patients who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled. Of these, 49 individuals were diagnosed with MDD, and 42 age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HCs) were recruited. The HCs had no personal or family history of psychiatric, neurological, or medical disorders and were not taking any medications that could affect the central nervous system.

Clinical assessments included the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), State-Trait Anxiety Inventory-X-1 (STAI-X-1), Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression-17 (HAMD-17), Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSE), and Resilience Appraisal Scale (RAS), as described previously [37]. The detailed demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Blood collection

Murine blood samples were collected from the abdominal inferior vena cava of mice under anesthesia. Whole blood was allowed to clot at RT for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to isolate the serum. The serum was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Human blood samples were obtained from the forearm vein of each participant using a Cell Preparation Tube (CPT; BD, USA) under sterile conditions. The samples were centrifuged at 1,600 × g for 20 min at RT, and the upper plasma layer was carefully aliquoted into microcentrifuge tubes. A protease inhibitor cocktail (EMD Biosciences, Inc., Germany) was added immediately, and the samples were stored at −80 °C until further use.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Peripheral S100B protein was quantified using ELISA kits for mouse (Cusabio, USA) and human (R&D Systems, USA) samples, following the manufacturer’s instructions. All samples were assayed in duplicate, and the mean value of each duplicate was used for analysis. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a VICTOR X4 Multimode Plate Reader (PerkinElmer, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0.3 software (GraphPad Software Inc., USA). Outliers were identified and removed using the Outlier Calculator in GraphPad Prism, with a significance level set at α = 0.05. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Between-group differences were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test or the Student’s t-test, depending on the experimental design. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Correlations were analyzed using the nonparametric Spearman’s rank correlation test.

Results

CMS induces depressive-like behaviors in young adult male and female mice

In a previous study, we demonstrated that the CPMS model is a valid tool for investigating depression in young adults [32]. We aimed to explore the relationship between astrocytic alterations and depression-like phenotypes in young adulthood. To this end, the CPMS model was established in both male and female mice by applying CMS across distinct developmental stages: mild prenatal stress, mild maternal separation stress, and mild social defeat stress (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Figure 1). To assess individual differences in stress vulnerability and the persistence of stress responses, mice were evaluated using a social interaction (SI) test at adolescence (5.5 weeks old) and young adulthood (7 weeks old). CPMS mice were robustly categorized into the susceptible (Sus) and resilient (Res) subgroups based on their social interaction ratios during adolescence (Supplementary Figure 2A). Social interaction was significantly reduced in Sus group (Male: 0.49 ± 0.07, p = 0.004; Female: 0.53 ± 0.06, p < 0.0001) compared to the Con (Male: 1.16 ± 0.17; Female: 1.23 ± 0.07) and the Res group (Male: 1.36 ± 0.07, p = 0.002; Female: 1.38 ± 0.10, p < 0.0001) in both sexes.

At 7 weeks of age, mice underwent the (B) social interaction test (SI), (C) elevated plus maze (EPM), (D) sucrose preference test (SPT), and (E) forced swim test (FST). To determine whether these behavioral alterations persisted into young adulthood, sociability was re-evaluated using the SI test at 7 weeks of age. Both male (0.43 ± 0.09, p = 0.002, n = 12) and female (0.59 ± 0.06, p = 0.001, n = 13) susceptible mice exhibited significantly lower social interaction ratios compared to the Con group (Male: 1.29 ± 0.17, n = 11; Female: 1.37 ± 0.18, n = 14) and the Res groups (Male: 1.45 ± 0.15, p < 0.0001, n = 11; Female: 1.23 ± 0.07, p = 0.017, n = 14; Fig. 1B). The proportions of Sus and Res group among those exposed to CPMS during young adulthood were 51% and 49% in males, and 68% and 32% in females, respectively (Supplementary Figure 2B). Further monitoring of behavioral phenotypes from adolescence to young adulthood revealed that 72% of male and 61% of female mice maintained their initial subgroups (Supplementary Figure 2C).

Given that anxiety is a common comorbid symptom of MDD, anxiety-like behaviors in CPMS mice were next assessed using the EPM test [38, 39]. Susceptible male (68.25 ± 2.95%, p = 0.0243) and female (77.62 ± 1.71%, p = 0.0009) mice spent significantly more time in the closed arms compared to the Con (male: 57.18 ± 3.41%; female: 60.79 ± 4.59%) and the Res group (male: 58.10 ± 1.81%, p = 0.0474; female: 67.40 ± 1.48%, p = 0.0492; Fig. 1C), indicating heightened anxiety.

To further evaluate depression-like symptoms, anhedonia and behavioral despair were assessed using the sucrose preference test (SPT) and forced swim test (FST), respectively [40–42]. Sucrose preference was significantly decreased in Sus group compared to both Con and Res groups (Male: 79.50 ± 3.66%, p = 0.0010 vs. Con: 92.73 ± 0.94% and Res: 90.73 ± 0.89%, p = 0.0053; Female: 81.15 ± 3.15%, p = 0.0121 vs. Con: 90.21 ± 1.60% and Res: 89.73 ± 1.30%; Fig. 1D). Similarly, immobility times in the FST were significantly increased in Sus group (Male: 244.60 ± 17.64 s, p = 0.0005; Female: 268.70 ± 11.67 s, p = 0.0038) compared to the Con group (Male: 135.90 ± 22.65 s; Female: 194.9 ± 13.36 s) and the Res group (Male: 181.9 ± 11.96 s, p = 0.0495; Female: 197.40 ± 17.84 s, p = 0.0052; Fig. 1E). Taken together, CMS-exposed susceptible mice exhibited reduced social interaction, decreased sucrose preference, and increased immobility time compared with control and resilient groups.

CMS reduces GFAP-positive astrocyte expression in the PFC of young adult male and female mice

Because astrocytic alterations in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) have been consistently observed in MDD, we focused our analysis on this region [43–47]. Given prior mechanistic and clinical evidence linking IL-17 to depression in young adulthood [32, 37], IL-17 was selected as a biologically relevant inflammatory mediator for the CPMS model, and its levels in the PFC were measured to assess the inflammatory component of the model.

IL-17 levels in the PFC were significantly elevated in susceptible mice compared with both control and resilient groups in male mice (Sus: 1.48 ± 0.13, p = 0.0201; Con: 1.00 ± 0.11; Res: 1.06 ± 0.07, p = 0.028; Supplementary Figure 3A) and females (Sus: 1.67 ± 0.10, p = 0.0251; Con: 1.00 ± 0.07; Res: 1.08 ± 0.21, p = 0.0357; Supplementary Figure 3B). These results are consistent with our previous findings and support the reproducibility of the CPMS model.

We next examined astrocytic alterations in the PFC by assessing GFAP-positive astrocytes in male and female mice. To examine the astrocytic alterations, we performed immunofluorescence staining to visualize GFAP-positive astrocytes in the PFC of male and female mice (Fig. 2A). GFAP-reactive astrocytes were significantly reduced in the PFC of Sus male (0.14 ± 0.02, p < 0.001) and female (0.25 ± 0.01, p < 0.0001) compared with Con (male: 1.00 ± 0.06; female: 1.00 ± 0.08) and Res (male: 0.59 ± 0.04, p = 0.0001; female: 1.04 ± 0.06, p < 0.0001) group. We further quantified GFAP expression levels in the PFC by western blot analysis. In both male and female mice, the Sus groups exhibited significantly reduced GFAP levels compared with Con and Res group (Male-Sus: 0.54 ± 0.04, p = 0.0016; Con: 1.00 ± 0.08; Res: 0.79 ± 0.08, p = 0.0442; Fig. 2B; Female-Sus: 0.59 ± 0.06, p = 0.0019; Con: 1.00 ± 0.09; Res: 0.82 ± 0.03, p = 0.0461; Fig. 2C).

CMS reduces astrocyte-related protein expression in the PFC of young adult male and female mice

Reduced astrocyte density and thickness have been reported in postmortem brains of individuals with depression [15, 48, 49]. Notably, several clinical studies have shown that decreased GFAP-immunoreactive astrocyte levels represent a prominent pathological feature in the brains of individuals with MDD, particularly during young adulthood [17, 50]. Based on these findings, we investigated whether CMS in early life alters the expression of astrocyte-related proteins in the PFC of CPMS mice.

In addition to GFAP, we assessed two key astrocytic proteins: aquaporin-4 (AQP4), a water channel protein localized at astrocyte end-feet that contributes to BBB regulation, and connexin-43 (Cx43), a major gap junction protein involved in astrocyte–astrocyte communication. AQP4 immunofluorescence intensity in the PFC of male (0.63 ± 0.08) and female (0.61 ± 0.04) Sus mice was significantly decreased compared with Con (male: 1.00 ± 0.04, p = 0.0082; female: 1.00 ± 0.06, p = 0.0011) and Res mice (male: 1.14 ± 0.06, p = 0.0010; female: 0.97 ± 0.05; p = 0.0020; Fig. 3A). Similarly, Cx43 intensity in male (0.14 ± 0.03) and female (0.10 ± 0.03) Sus mice was significantly reduced relative to Con (male: 1.00 ± 0.16, p = 0.0006; female: 1.00 ± 0.07, p < 0.0001) and Res mice (male: 0.53 ± 0.06, p = 0.0636; female: 0.45 ± 0.04, p = 0.0012; Fig. 3B). Consistent with the immunofluorescence results, we quantified protein expression by western blot. AQP4 expression was significantly reduced in the PFC of Sus group relative to both Con and Res groups across sexes (Male–Sus: 0.79 ± 0.02, p = 0.0147; Con: 1.00 ± 0.04; Res: 0.95 ± 0.05, p = 0.0482; Female–Sus: 0.65 ± 0.02, p = 0.0311; Con: 1.00 ± 0.06; Res: 0.97 ± 0.12, p = 0.0369; Fig. 3A). Similarly, Cx43 expression was significantly lower in the Sus group of both sexes compared with Con and Res groups (Male–Sus: 0.54 ± 0.08, p = 0.0175; Con: 1.00 ± 0.16; Res: 1.16 ± 0.04, p = 0.0016; Female–Sus: 0.46 ± 0.11, p = 0.0059; Con: 1.00 ± 0.10; Res: 1.03 ± 0.07, p = 0.0028; Fig. 3B).

Differential effects of stress exposure at distinct developmental stages on depressive-like behaviors and GFAP expression in the PFC

We next examined the effects of stress exposure during different developmental periods on astrocytic alterations. Specifically, we compared stress exposure during three neurodevelopmental stages—prenatal (P; mild prenatal stress), postnatal (M; mild maternal separation), and pubertal (S; mild social defeat)—and assessed behavioral phenotypes and astrocyte expression in young adulthood. This analysis focused on male mice, as no significant sex differences were observed in astrocyte-related protein expression patterns.

Anxiety- and depression-like behaviors were assessed following exposure to each stress condition (Fig. 4A). In the EPM, all three stress groups showed significantly increased time spent in closed arms compared to the Con group (Con: 63.09 ± 3.78%; P: 91.71 ± 2.34%, p < 0.0001; M: 82.33 ± 5.81%, p = 0.0098; S: 87.00 ± 3.33%, p = 0.0004; Fig. 4B), indicating heightened anxiety.

In the SPT and FST results, only the P group exhibited significant depression-like behaviors. In the SPT, sucrose consumption was significantly reduced in the P group (64.13 ± 8.96%, p = 0.0496) compared with the Con group (88.85 ± 2.94%), while no significant differences were observed in the M (92.96 ± 1.19%, p = 0.9756) or S (73.66 ± 9.95%, p = 0.3543) groups (Fig. 4C). In the FST, immobility time was significantly increased in the P group (252.40 ± 34.57 s, p = 0.0004) relative to the Con group (103.50 ± 15.85 s), whereas M (187.60 ± 28.77 s, p = 0.0797) and S (93.18 ± 16.49 s, p = 0.9883) groups did not differ significantly from Con group (Fig. 4D).

To determine whether these behavioral alterations were associated with astrocytic changes, GFAP expression in the PFC was measured. Immunoblotting revealed a significant reduction in GFAP expression in the P group (0.65 ± 0.03, p = 0.0149) relative to the Con group (1.00 ± 0.12; Fig. 4E). In contrast, GFAP levels in the M (1.06 ± 0.03, p = 0.1334) and S (1.04 ± 0.06, p = 0.688) groups did not differ significantly from their respective Con group (Con-M group: 1.00 ± 0.02; Con-S group: 1.00 ± 0.06; Fig. 4F and G).

Stress exposure at distinct developmental stages differentially affects GFAP-immunoreactive astrocyte expression

To rule out the possibility that stress-induced reductions in astrocyte expression recover over time, GFAP-immunoreactive astrocyte levels were assessed immediately after each stress exposure. Specifically, mice were subjected to prenatal stress (P), maternal separation (M), or social defeat stress (S), corresponding to the prenatal, early postnatal, and pubertal developmental periods, respectively (Fig. 5). In this analysis, a significant reduction in GFAP expression was observed in the P group 24 h after prenatal stress exposure (0.23 ± 0.03, p < 0.0001) compared with Con group (1.00 ± 0.13; Fig. 5A). Although no significant difference in GFAP expression was evident at 8 weeks of age in the M group (see Fig. 4F), a significant reduction was detected immediately following maternal separation (M group: 0.81 ± 0.03, p = 0.05; Con group: 1.00 ± 0.08; Fig. 5B). In contrast, the S group showed no significant change in GFAP levels either immediately after social defeat stress (1.11 ± 0.14, p = 0.5402; Con: 1.00 ± 0.11; Fig. 5C) or at 8 weeks of age (Fig. 4G). These results indicate that prenatal stress induced a sustained reduction in GFAP expression that was evident both immediately after stress exposure and in young adulthood, whereas postnatal stress induced a transient reduction that recovered by adulthood. In contrast, social defeat stress during puberty did not significantly alter GFAP expression at either time point.

Elevated peripheral S100B levels correlate with depressive-related behavioral and clinical measures in CPMS mice and young adult patients with MDD

Recent studies have reported that disruption of the BBB, driven by aberrant inflammatory responses, is a key pathological feature of depression, leading to the leakage of astrocyte-derived proteins such as S100B into peripheral circulation [21, 22, 30]. To determine whether a similar phenomenon occurred in the current model, peripheral S100B levels were measured in CPMS mice using ELISA.

S100B levels were significantly elevated in susceptible CPMS group (5.41 ± 0.69 pg/mL, p = 0.0005) compared with Con group (2.13 ± 0.37 pg/mL; Fig. 6A). When analyzed by sex, both male (5.36 ± 0.92 pg/mL, p = 0.013 vs. 2.46 ± 0.32 pg/mL) and female (5.47 ± 1.11 pg/mL, p = 0.025 vs. 1.73 ± 0.72 pg/mL) Sus group exhibited significantly higher serum S100B levels than respective Con group (Fig. 6B and C). In addition, peripheral S100B concentration was significantly correlated with despair (r = 0.3924, p = 0.0179), and anhedonia (r = −0.3959, p = 0.0168) of mice but not significantly correlated with anxiety-like behavior (r = 0.2785, p = 0.1000; Fig. 6D).

To validate these findings in a clinical context, peripheral S100B levels were measured in drug-naïve young adult patients with MDD (mean age of 24 years) and age-matched HCs (Supplementary Table 1). S100B levels were significantly higher in patients with MDD (29.66 ± 24.12 pg/mL, p < 0.0001) than in HCs group (10.72 ± 10.01 pg/mL; Fig. 6E). When stratified by sex, elevated S100B levels were observed in both male (28.78 ± 25.78 pg/mL, p = 0.0306 vs. 20.35 ± 9.50 pg/mL) and female (30.40 ± 23.20 pg/mL, p = 0.0004 vs. 12.68 ± 10.73 pg/mL) patients compared with their respective HCs group (Fig. 6F and G). Correlation analyses of peripheral S100B levels revealed positive associations with depressive scores, including BDI (r = 0.3447, p = 0.0016), HAMD-17 (r = 0.3245, p = 0.0031), and STAI-X-1 (r = 0.2627, p = 0.0408), and negative associations with self-esteem (r = −0.2788, p = 0.0194) and RAS scores (r = −0.2719, p = 0.0340; Fig. 6G).

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated the relationship between altered astrocytic protein expression and depression-related phenotypes following early life CMS. During critical periods of brain development, CMS led to significant reductions in GFAP-immunoreactive astrocyte expression in the PFC of both susceptible male and female CPMS mice, accompanied by anxiety- and depression-like behavior in young adulthood. These changes were not observed in resilient mice. Additionally, expression levels of AQP4 and Cx43, two astrocytic proteins essential for maintaining BBB integrity, were significantly reduced in the PFC of susceptible mice. The developmental stage most vulnerable to stress-induced astrocyte impairment was subsequently evaluated, with findings indicating that prenatal stress caused the most substantial and sustained reduction in GFAP-positive astrocytes, both immediately after stress and in adulthood, compared with postnatal or pubertal stress exposure. These alterations in astrocyte development and maturation were associated with increased peripheral S100B levels, consistent with observations in young adult patients with MDD.

Although the precise mechanisms remain unclear, categorizing mice as stress-susceptible or resilient is important in stress-related experiments, as both mice and humans exhibit individual differences in stress responses [51]. Accordingly, we assessed depressive and anxiety-like behaviors of mice after categorization based on the SI test. Results demonstrated that Sus mice exhibited anxiety- and depression-like behaviors in young adulthood, whereas Res mice did not display these phenotypes (Fig. 1). These findings underscore the importance of individual differences in stress susceptibility. In previous work, it was reported that mice subjected to CMS displayed more severe depressive symptoms than those exposed to single or double stress paradigms, reflecting the complex and layered nature of early-life adversity experienced by young adult patients with MDD [32].

One of the hallmark pathological features of MDD is reduced astrocyte expression and dysfunction in individuals with depression [19, 46]. Postmortem studies have reported decreased astrocyte density in various cortical regions, including the anterior cingulate cortex [52] and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex [50]. Specifically, GFAP-immunoreactive astrocytes are significantly reduced in the PFC of young adults with MDD, whereas increased GFAP expression has been observed in older individuals ( > 50 years), possibly reflecting age-related reactive gliosis [17, 49, 53]. Furthermore, both chronic and early life stress have been associated with volumetric reductions in the PFC of individuals with MDD [54, 55], further supporting a link between stress exposure and astrocytic vulnerability. Based on these findings, the current study investigated GFAP-immunoreactive astrocytes in the PFC of CPMS mice. A significant decrease in GFAP expression was observed in the PFC of susceptible male and female mice at 7–8 weeks of age, corresponding to young adulthood (Fig. 2). Together, these findings suggest that CMS exposure during early life may disrupt astrocyte homeostasis in the PFC during young adulthood, contributing to depression-related phenotypes.

Neurovascular dysfunction, particularly BBB hyperpermeability, is increasingly recognized as a key pathophysiological feature of MDD [20, 22]. Although the precise mechanisms and temporal sequence linking BBB disruption and depression remain unclear, clinical studies using noninvasive imaging techniques have identified BBB leakage in the brains of relatively young individuals (aged 28–30 years), with such disruptions correlating with symptom severity [23]. In preclinical models, social stress has been shown to alter the expression of several endothelial proteins essential for maintaining BBB structure and function [56]. Astrocytes, which contribute to the BBB via their perivascular endfeet, play a central role in regulating BBB integrity. Dysregulation of astrocyte-associated proteins involved in water balance, metabolism, and cell–cell communication has been implicated in MDD. For example, AQP4, a water channel protein localized to astrocytic endfeet, regulates cerebral water homeostasis and blood flow [57, 58]. AQP4 expression and vascular coverage are reduced in the grey matter of individuals with MDD [27]. Similarly, Cx43, a gap junction protein that interacts with connexin 30 to enable intercellular communication, is critical for BBB stability and ion regulation [59–61]. In the present study, significantly reduced expression of both AQP4 and Cx43 was observed in the PFC of susceptible CPMS mice compared with Con and Res groups (Fig. 3). Together with the previously reported upregulated inflammatory response in this model [32], these findings suggest that CMS during neurodevelopment disrupts expression of astrocytic end-foot proteins, potentially contributing to BBB instability. Importantly, these findings support a mechanistic continuum in which early life stress–induced astrocytic dysfunction compromises BBB integrity.

Astrogenesis and astrocyte maturation begin in late gestation and continue through the first three postnatal weeks in rodents [62, 63]. Based on this developmental timeline, we examined which developmental window is intrinsically most vulnerable to stress-induced astrocytic alterations using two complementary experimental approaches: (i) assessment in young adulthood following each single stress exposure (Fig. 4) and (ii) assessment immediately after each stress exposure (Fig. 5). Using this window-specific framework, stress during prenatal and postnatal periods significantly reduced GFAP-immunoreactive astrocyte levels acutely, whereas persistent reductions extending into young adulthood were observed in animals exposed to prenatal stress. Notably, the prenatal stress group exhibited the most pronounced and sustained decrease in GFAP expression, which was accompanied by the most severe depression-like behavioral phenotype in young adulthood. These findings suggest that stress exposure during sensitive phases of brain development, particularly during periods of astrocyte proliferation and maturation, is associated with long-lasting structural and behavioral alteration. In contrast, social defeat stress during puberty did not induce significant changes in GFAP expression in PFC, either immediately after stress exposure or in young adulthood. This finding suggests that astrocytes in the PFC may be relatively less sensitive to structural alterations induced by a single stressor during adolescence. Importantly, however, the absence of detectable GFAP changes in the PFC does not exclude the possibility of region-specific astrocytic dysfunction. Indeed, previous studies have reported astrocytic alterations, including reduced GFAP expression in the hippocampus and other brain regions following social defeat stress [64–66]. In addition, mice exposed to CMS during the prenatal, postnatal, and pubertal periods exhibited the most severe depressive-like symptoms compared to mice subjected to fewer or isolated stressors in our previous study [32]. These findings indicate that although a single exposure to pubertal stress did not result in measurable astrocytic changes in the PFC, it may contribute to astrocyte-related pathology in depression when combined with earlier stress exposure. Together, these findings highlight the importance of early life stress and developmental timing in shaping astrocytic vulnerability, with lasting consequences for depression-related outcomes [67].

Elevated serum S100B levels have been associated with treatment response to antidepressants [30, 68, 69], further underscoring its clinical relevance. Notably, increased S100B levels have been reported in adolescent and young adult patients with MDD (aged 13–25 years) compared with HCs [70]. Consistent with these findings, the present study demonstrated that CPMS led to a significant increase in serum S100B levels in susceptible mice, which was associated with depressive-like behavior. A similar trend was observed in young adult patients with MDD, in whom peripheral S100B levels were elevated and significantly correlated with clinical symptom scores (Fig. 6). Together, these findings directly link the preclinical CPMS model to human pathophysiology and strengthen the translational integration of the study. Although elevated peripheral S100B levels are frequently interpreted as an indicator of BBB disruption [71], the precise mechanisms by which brain-associated S100B appears in the circulation remain incompletely understood. In addition to structural breakdown of the BBB, several alternative pathways have been proposed to mediate the transfer or clearance of S100B from the central nervous system. These include paracellular diffusion through compromised tight junctions, vesicular or receptor-mediated transcytosis across endothelial cells potentially involving the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) and glymphatic clearance pathways that facilitate the drainage of interstitial solutes into the peripheral circulation [72]. In this context, the elevated serum S100B observed in susceptible CPMS mice may reflect functional BBB instability or altered neurovascular clearance rather than direct structural leakage alone. Together, these findings suggest that early life stress–induced astrocytic dysfunction may contribute to peripheral S100B elevation through multiple neurovascular mechanisms, thereby linking preclinical observations to human pathophysiology.

Despite the strengths of the present study, several limitations and directions for future research should be acknowledged. First, astrocytic alterations were evaluated using a limited set of markers in the PFC of male and female mice. Future studies should extend these analyses to additional subcortical limbic regions involved in mood regulation and depressive disorders [73], and further examine sex-dependent effects, given known sex differences in astrocyte development [63] and depression risk [74]. Second, direct investigation of BBB-related pathology and the origin of circulating S100B is required to clarify the underlying neurovascular mechanisms. Although reductions in astrocytic end-foot–associated proteins and elevated peripheral S100B levels provide indirect evidence of BBB dysfunction, imaging-based approaches will be necessary to determine whether circulating S100B reflects BBB permeability changes and to delineate the underlying mechanisms. Third, although IL-17 was examined based on prior evidence linking inflammation to CMS-induced depressive phenotypes [32] and young-adult MDD [37], the relationship between astrocytic dysfunction, S100B elevation, and broader inflammatory pathways was not systematically explored and warrants further investigation. Finally, because the longitudinal design required repeated behavioral testing (SI test) in the same animals, habituation effects may have influenced behavioral responses and should be considered when interpreting the results.

In summary, the present findings demonstrate that CMS during early brain development is associated with reduced astrocytic protein expression in the PFC. These alterations include downregulation of astrocytic markers and end-foot–associated proteins implicated in the maintenance of BBB stability. The observed astrocytic changes are accompanied by depression-like behaviors and elevated peripheral S100B levels in stress-susceptible CPMS mice. Importantly, these patterns are mirrored in young adults with MDD, in whom serum S100B levels correlate with clinical symptom severity. Taken together, these results highlight astrocytes as a developmentally relevant substrate linking early-life stress to depression-related phenotypes in young adulthood and provide a translational framework for astrocyte-based biomarkers and therapeutic strategies in MDD.
