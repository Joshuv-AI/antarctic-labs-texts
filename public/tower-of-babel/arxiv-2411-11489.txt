Harris Dispersion Relation and Bernstein Modes in Dense Magnetized Quantum Plasmas
Tian-Xing Hu, Dong Wu, Jie Zhang

Abstract
The Bernstein wave is a well-known electrostatic eigen-mode in magnetized plasmas, and it is of broad connection to multiple disciplines, such as controlled nuclear fusions and astrophysics. In this work, we extend the Bernstein mode from classical to quantum plasmas by means of the quantum kinetic theory in a self-consistent manner, and especially the quantum version of the Harris dispersion relation is derived. The studied quantum effects appear in the form of pseudo-differential operators (ΨDO) in the formula, which are exactly solved using numerical methods. Furthermore, by utilizing the magnetized equilibrium Wigner function, Landau quantization and finite temperature effects are rigorously contained. It is found that behaviours of the quantum Bernstein wave departure significantly from its classical counterpart, especially when ℏ ​ ω c \hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}} is of the same order of the Fermi energy.

In solid-state physics, magnetoplasmons in a 2D electron gas are a

The main objective of this paper is to extend the linear analysis
of Bernstein modes from classical plasmas to quantum plasmas. In this
context, quantum plasma refers to a plasma where the electron density
is sufficiently high that the thermal de Broglie wavelength of electrons
becomes comparable to the average distance between them, or where
the energy of a plasmon is comparable to the thermal/Fermi energy.
Quantum plasmas are ubiquitous in the universe, for example, white
dwarfs and other old or dead stars contain a significant amount of
quantum plasmas. Some models used to explain certain high-energy astrophysical
phenomena (such as X- or
\gamma
-ray bursts) also involve quantum

,

,

,
22
,
23
]
,
and electrons in metals and electronic devices can be treated as one-component

The linear properties of classical Bernstein modes and instabilities
have been thoroughly studied long time ago by many authors. For example,
Tataronis and Crawford
[
24
]
calculated
the Bernstein instabilities triggered by ring distribution and spherical
shell distribution, and they also studied oblique modes
[
25
]
.
However, there have been few studies on the Bernstein mode in quantum
plasmas. To the best of our knowledge, Eliasson and Shukla
[
26
]

were the first to study the quantum Bernstein modes in a zero-temperature
degenerate plasma, but the finite temperature and quantum diffraction
effects are neglected in their work. Iqbal etc.
[
27
]

introduced the quantum diffraction effect by adding a Bohm potential

although employed by some other authors as well
[
28
]
,
has not yet been verified
[
29
,
30
]
.
And, they obtained a Friedel-like oscillatory dispersion relation,

The realistic distribution function of a magnetized quantum plasma

]

which is also not addressed in previous works on Bernstein modes.

The most self-consistent statistical model used to describe quantum
plasmas is the quantum kinetic theory, which reduces to the Wigner
equation in the collisionless limit. The linearization of the Wigner

same method are reviewed in, e.g., Refs.

33

In this paper, we study the Bernstein modes by means of the linearized
Wigner-Poisson system of equations. In Section
II
,
a general quantum Harris dispersion relation involving a pseudo-differential
operator (
\Psi
DO) in velocity space is derived. This
\Psi

we introduce an exact Wigner distribution function that incorporates

magnetic fields. In Section

quantized Wigner equilibrium function, the exact quantum Bernstein
modes are solved.

II
Quantum Harris dispersion relation

Generally, motion of electrons in a quantum plasma obey the electromagnetic
quantum kinetic equation (the Wigner equation)
[
34
]
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+\left\{\frac{\boldsymbol{p}}{m}-\frac{e}{2mc}\vartheta^{+}\left[\boldsymbol{A}\right]\right\}\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol{x}}f+\frac{\mathrm{i}e}{\hbar}\vartheta^{-}\left[\phi\right]f

(1)

\displaystyle+\frac{\mathrm{i}e}{\hbar mc}\left\{\boldsymbol{p}\cdot\vartheta^{-}\left[\boldsymbol{A}\right]-\frac{e}{2c}\vartheta^{-}\left[\left|\boldsymbol{A}\right|^{2}\right]\right\}f=0,

where
\boldsymbol{p}=m\boldsymbol{v}+e\boldsymbol{A}/m
is the canonical momentum, and the
\Psi
DO

\vartheta^{\pm}
is defined as

\displaystyle\vartheta^{\pm}\left[\phi\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right]\equiv

\displaystyle\phi\left(\boldsymbol{x}+\frac{\mathrm{i}\hbar}{2}\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}\right)\pm\phi\left(\boldsymbol{x}-\frac{\mathrm{i}\hbar}{2}\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}\right)

(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\begin{Bmatrix}\cos\\
-\mathrm{i}\sin\end{Bmatrix}\left(\frac{\hbar}{2}\partial_{\boldsymbol{x}}\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}\right)\phi\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right).

Apply an external magnetic field
\boldsymbol{B}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{A}_{0}
along

z
-axis, the last term in Eq. (
1
) reduce to the classical
Lorentz force term, and the brace in front of
\partial_{\boldsymbol{x}}
reduces
to velocity
\boldsymbol{v}
. In an electrostatic system, the magnetic perturbation

\delta\boldsymbol{B}
is ignored. Then we have

\partial_{t}f+\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol{x}}f+\frac{\mathrm{i}e}{\hbar}\vartheta^{-}\left[\phi\right]f+\frac{e}{mc}\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}_{0}\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol{v}}f=0,

(3)

and it is evident that a uniform static magnetic field behaves identically
in a quantum plasma as it does in a classical plasma. After linearization
to
f
and
\phi
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left[\mathrm{i}\left(\omega-\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\right)+\omega_{\mathrm{c}}\partial_{\varphi}\right]

\displaystyle\delta f=\delta\phi\frac{e}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}2\sinh\left(\frac{\hbar\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol{v}}}{2m}\right)f_{0},

(4)

in the space-time Fourier space, where
\omega_{\mathrm{c}}\equiv eB_{0}/m

is the gyro-frequency of electrons, and
\varphi
the gyro-angle
in the cylindrical coordinate system

defined by
\boldsymbol{v}=\left(v_{x},v_{y},v_{z}\right)\equiv\left(v_{\perp}\cos\varphi,v_{\perp}\sin\varphi,v_{\parallel}\right)
.

Integrate over both sides of Eq. (
4
), and cancel the
perturbed electrostatic potential by means of the Poisson’s equation

\delta\phi=\frac{4\pi e^{2}}{k^{2}}\int\delta f\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}^{3}v,

(5)

we thus obtain

\displaystyle 1+\frac{4\pi e^{2}}{\hbar k^{2}\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}\iiint\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}v_{\parallel}v_{\perp}\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}v_{\perp}\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}\varphi\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\left(\alpha\varphi+\beta\sin\varphi\right)}

(6)

\displaystyle\times\int^{\varphi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\left(\varphi^{\prime}\alpha+\beta\sin\varphi^{\prime}\right)}2\sinh\left(\frac{\hbar\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol{v}}}{2m}\right)f_{\text{0}}\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}\varphi^{\prime}=0,

where

\alpha=\frac{k_{\parallel}v_{\parallel}-\omega}{\omega_{\mathrm{c}}},\quad\beta=\frac{k_{\perp}v_{\perp}}{\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}.

(7)

Noticing that

\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol{v}}=k_{\parallel}\partial_{v_{\parallel}}+k_{\perp}\cos\varphi\partial_{v_{\perp}}-\frac{\sin\varphi}{v_{\perp}}\partial_{\varphi}.

(8)

Assume the plasma is gyrotropic, then
\partial_{\varphi}=0
. After
some straightforward calculations, we finally obtain

\varepsilon\left(\omega,\boldsymbol{k}\right)\equiv 1-\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}}{k^{2}}\sum_{n,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\int\frac{\mathcal{B}_{n\ell}\left[F_{0}\right]}{\alpha+n}\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}v_{\parallel}=0,

(9)

where we use the uppercase letter
F=f/n_{0}
to denote the normalized
distribution,
\omega_{\mathrm{p}}=\sqrt{4\pi e^{2}n_{0}/m}
is the
plasma frequency, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{n\ell}\left[F_{0}\right]\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{2m}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}\int\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}\left(\partial_{v_{\parallel}},\partial_{v_{\perp}}\right)F_{0}

(10)

\displaystyle\times J_{n+\ell}\left(\frac{k_{\perp}v_{\perp}}{\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}\right)J_{n}\left(\frac{k_{\perp}v_{\perp}}{\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}\right)2\pi v_{\perp}\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}v_{\perp},

with the ΨDO defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}\left(\partial_{v_{\parallel}},\partial_{v_{\perp}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\kappa_{\parallel}\partial_{v_{\parallel}}}-\left(-1\right)^{\ell}\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa_{\parallel}\partial_{v_{\parallel}}}\right]I_{\ell}\left(\kappa_{\perp}\partial_{v_{\perp}}\right)

(11)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sinh\left(\kappa_{\parallel}\partial_{v_{\parallel}}\right)I_{\ell}\left(\kappa_{\perp}\partial_{v_{\perp}}\right),&\ell\text{ is even,}\\
\cosh\left(\kappa_{\parallel}\partial_{v_{\parallel}}\right)I_{\ell}\left(\kappa_{\perp}\partial_{v_{\perp}}\right),&\ell\text{ is odd},\end{cases}

where
\kappa_{\perp/\parallel}\equiv\hbar k_{\perp/\parallel}/2m
,
and
I_{\ell}\left(x\right)=\mathrm{i}^{-\ell}J_{\ell}\left(\mathrm{i}x\right)

is the modified Bessel function of order
\ell
. In addition, the
two exponential parallel ΨDO terms are displacement operators
on opposite directions, hence

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}\left[F\right]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}I_{\ell}\left(\kappa_{\perp}\partial_{v_{\perp}}\right)

(12)

\displaystyle\times\left[F\left(v_{\parallel}+\kappa_{\parallel},v_{\perp}\right)-\left(-1\right)^{\ell}F\left(v_{\parallel}-\kappa_{\parallel},v_{\perp}\right)\right].

In the classical limit, i.e.,
\frac{\hbar\boldsymbol{k}}{m}\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol{v}}\ll 1
,
using

I_{\ell}\left(x\right)\simeq\frac{1}{\ell!}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{\ell},

(13)

as
x\rightarrow 0
, the lowest order
\ell=0
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{n0}=

\displaystyle\frac{2m}{\hbar}\int 2\pi v_{\perp}\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}v_{\perp}J_{n}^{2}\left(\beta\right)\sinh\left(\kappa_{\parallel}\partial_{v_{\parallel}}\right)I_{0}\left(\kappa_{\perp}\partial_{v_{\perp}}\right)

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int 2\pi v_{\perp}\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}v_{\perp}J_{n}^{2}\left(\frac{k_{\perp}v_{\perp}}{\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}\right)\partial_{v_{\parallel}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\hbar\right)

and the second least order
\ell=\pm 1
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{n1}+\mathcal{B}_{n,-1}=

\displaystyle\frac{2m}{\hbar}\int 2\pi v_{\perp}\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}v_{\perp}\cosh\left(\kappa_{\parallel}\partial_{v_{\parallel}}\right)I_{1}\left(\kappa_{\perp}\partial_{v_{\perp}}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle\times J_{n}\left(\beta\right)\left[J_{n+1}\left(\beta\right)+J_{n-1}\left(\beta\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int 2\pi v_{\perp}\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}v_{\perp}J_{n}^{2}\left(\frac{k_{\perp}v_{\perp}}{\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}\right)\frac{n\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}{v_{\perp}}\partial_{v_{\perp}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\hbar\right)

where we have used the identity
J_{n+1}\left(x\right)+J_{n-1}(x)=2nJ_{n}(x)/x
.
Combining Eq. (
14
) and Eq. (
15
), we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{c}\left(\omega,\boldsymbol{k}\right)\equiv

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}}{k^{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\iint 2\pi v_{\perp}\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}v_{\perp}\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}v_{\parallel}

(16)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\frac{J_{n}^{2}\left(k_{\perp}v_{\perp}/\omega_{\mathrm{c}}\right)}{\omega-k_{\parallel}v_{\parallel}-n\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}\left(k_{\parallel}\partial_{v_{\parallel}}+\frac{n\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}{v_{\perp}}\partial_{v_{\perp}}\right)F_{0}=0,

which is exactly the well-known Harris dispersion relation of classical
plasmas
[
35
,
36
]
. Hence,
we may refer to Eq. (
9
) as the quantum Harris dispersion
relation, which is one of the core result of this paper.

The indices of
\mathcal{B}_{n\ell}
range from
-\infty
to
\infty
,
alternatively, we can define a matrix
\bar{\mathcal{B}}_{n\ell}

with non-negative indices:

\bar{\mathcal{B}}_{n\ell}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{B}_{00},&n=\ell=0,\\
\mathcal{B}_{n0}+\mathcal{B}_{-n0},&n\neq 0,\ell=0,\\
2\left(\mathcal{B}_{n,\ell}+\mathcal{B}_{n,-\ell}\right),&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(17)

We refer to
\bar{\mathcal{B}}_{n\ell}
as the quantum Bernstein
matrix (QBM), which is a matrix functional of the distribution function

f_{0}
. Then, Eq. (
9
) becomes

\displaystyle\varepsilon\left(\omega,\boldsymbol{k}\right)=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}}{k^{2}}\sum_{n,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}\int\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}v_{\parallel}\frac{\bar{\mathcal{B}}_{n\ell}\left[F_{0}\right]}{\left(\omega-k_{\parallel}v_{\parallel}\right)^{2}-n^{2}\omega_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}

(18)

\displaystyle\times\begin{cases}\left(\omega-k_{\parallel}v_{\parallel}\right)\omega_{\mathrm{c}},&\ell\text{ is even,}\\
n\omega_{\mathrm{c}}^{2},&\ell\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

Noting that the Bessel perpendicular ΨDO in Eq. (
11
)
can be formulated as (see Appendix

for details)

\begin{aligned} I_{\ell}\left(\kappa\partial_{v}\right)F=&\frac{\left(-\right)^{\ell}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}F\left(v-\kappa\cos\varphi\right)\cos\left(\ell\varphi\right)\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}\varphi\end{aligned}.

(19)

With the aid of this formula, we can easily solve the quantum Harris
dispersion relation Eq. (

) numerically, order by order.

Equilibrium Wigner distribution function in a Magnetic field

If the magnetic field is strong enough such that
\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}

is comparable, or even much larger than the thermal or Fermi energy.
The energy of an electron is quantized into a series of Landau levels.
In such case, the equilibrium Wigner function should be (see Appendix

B
for details)

\displaystyle f_{B}\left(\boldsymbol{v}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(-\right)^{n}{\displaystyle 2\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{mv_{\perp}^{2}}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}}L_{n}\left(\frac{2mv_{\perp}^{2}}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}\right)}}{1+{\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\beta\left[\frac{m}{2}v_{\parallel}^{2}+\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)-\mu\right]}}},

(20)

where
L_{n}\left(x\right)
is the Laguerre function of
n
th order.
The constant

\mathcal{A}=2\left(\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar}\right)^{3}=\frac{3n_{0}}{4\pi v_{\mathrm{F}}^{3}}

(21)

appears here to convert the dimensionality of the Wigner function
into the density in the
x
-
v
phase space, and the factor 2 stands
for fermions. Some authors prefer to alter the velocity differential
from
\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}v
to
\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}p/\left(2\pi\hbar\right)
to achieve this.

In high-temperature limit, i.e.,
\beta\rightarrow 0
,

\displaystyle f_{B}\left(\boldsymbol{v}\right)\simeq

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\left(\frac{m}{2}v_{\parallel}^{2}-\mu+\frac{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}{2}\right)}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{mv_{\perp}^{2}}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\frac{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}{2}}

(22)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}\right)^{n}{\displaystyle L_{n}\left(\frac{2mv_{\perp}^{2}}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{A}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\mu}}{\cosh\mathcal{M}_{T}}\exp\left[-\frac{\beta m}{2}\left(v_{\parallel}^{2}+v_{\perp}^{2}\frac{\tanh\mathcal{M}_{T}}{\mathcal{M}_{T}}\right)\right],

where we have used the identity

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}L_{n}\left(x\right)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{xt/\left(1-t\right)}}{1-t},

(23)

and

\mathcal{M}_{T}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\beta\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}

(24)

is the ratio between magnetic field and temperature. Eq. (
22
)
is identical to the zero-order Wigner-Kirkwood expansion in a strong
magnetic field, firstly derived by Alastuey and Jancovici in Ref.
[
37
]
.
And noting that it reduces to Maxwellian distribution when
\mathcal{M}_{T}\rightarrow 0
.
By normalizing the frequency of our system to
\omega_{\mathrm{c}}

and velocity to the Fermi velocity

, it is found
that the quantum effect is characterized by another dimensionless
parameter,

\mathcal{M}_{F}\equiv\frac{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}{2\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{F}}^{0}}=\Theta\mathcal{M}_{T},

(25)

where
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{F}}^{0}=\hbar^{2}\left(3\pi^{2}n_{0}\right)^{2/3}/2m

is the Fermi energy at zero magnetic field. Hence,
\mathcal{M}_{F}

denotes the ratio between the lowest Landau level energy and the unmagnetized
Fermi energy. As
\mathcal{M}_{F}\rightarrow 0
, the system also reduces
to classical.

In low-temperature limit, i.e.,
\beta\rightarrow\infty
,

f_{B}\left(\boldsymbol{v}\right)\simeq\mathcal{A}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(-\right)^{n}{\displaystyle 2\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{mv_{\perp}^{2}}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}}L_{n}\left(\frac{2mv_{\perp}^{2}}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}\right)},

(26)

where

N=\left\lfloor\frac{1}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{F}}^{0}-\frac{1}{2}mv_{\parallel}^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor

(27)

is the largest Landau quantum number that lies under the Fermi surface.
From Eq. (
23
) we have

2\mathrm{e}^{-x/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(-1\right)^{n}L_{n}\left(x\right)=1,

(28)

and since now
\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}\ll\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{F}}
,
namely,
N\gg 1
, one can deduce that

2\mathrm{e}^{-x/2}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(-1\right)^{n}L_{n}\left(x\right)\simeq\theta\left(x-\sqrt{2N}\right),

(29)

then Eq. (
20
) reduces to Fermi-Dirac distribution:

f_{0}\left(v_{\parallel},v_{\perp}\right)=\frac{\mathcal{A}}{1+\mathrm{e}^{\beta\frac{m}{2}\left(v_{\parallel}^{2}+v_{\perp}^{2}\right)-\beta\mu}}.

(30)

In the study of Bernstein waves, the dimension parallel to the magnetic
field is usually irrelevant, which means we can integrate over

in advance. We define the normalized parallel and perpendicular Fermi-Dirac
function here:

\displaystyle F_{\parallel}\left(v_{\parallel}\right)=

\displaystyle n_{0}^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}2\pi v_{\perp}\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}v_{\perp}f_{0}\left(v_{\parallel},v_{\perp}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{2m\beta v_{\mathrm{F}}^{3}}\mathrm{Li}_{1}\left[-\mathrm{e}^{\beta\left(\mu-\frac{m}{2}v_{\perp}^{2}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2m\beta v_{\mathrm{F}}^{3}}\ln\left[1+\mathrm{e}^{\beta\left(\mu-\frac{m}{2}v_{\parallel}^{2}\right)}\right],

\displaystyle F_{\perp}\left(v_{\perp}\right)=

\displaystyle n_{0}^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}v_{\parallel}f_{0}\left(v_{\parallel},v_{\perp}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{m\beta}}\mathrm{Li}_{\frac{1}{2}}\left[-\mathrm{e}^{\beta\left(\mu-\frac{m}{2}v_{\perp}^{2}\right)}\right],

where

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{mv_{\perp}^{2}}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(-\right)^{n}L_{n}\left(\frac{2mv_{\perp}^{2}}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\sqrt{\frac{\Theta}{\pi}}\mathrm{Li}_{\frac{1}{2}}\left\{-\mathrm{e}^{\beta\left[\mu-\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right]}\right\},

\displaystyle F_{B\perp}^{0}\left(v_{\perp}\right)=

(37)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\sqrt{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{mv_{\perp}^{2}}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(-\right)^{n}L_{n}\left(\frac{2mv_{\perp}^{2}}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}\right)

From Eq. (

(44)

V
Discussion and Summary

The quantum mechanical version of the Harris dispersion relation is
derived in this paper, and the non-local pseudo-differential operators
arising from quantum recoil effects are addressed self-consistently.
The effects of Landau quantization are also considered by introducing
the magnetized Wigner equilibrium function. As a example, quantum
Bernstein modes under this framework are numerically calculated.

Figure 5:
The applicability of this paper generally covers cases where both

\mathcal{M}_{F}
and
\omega_{\mathrm{p}}/\omega_{\mathrm{c}}

are of the order of 1, and RPA remains valid.

All the results obtained in the main text are in normalized units,
meaning they can be applied to any density, temperature and magnetic
field strength. However, a Bernstein mode is only important when
\omega_{\mathrm{p}}

and
\omega_{\mathrm{c}}
are of the same order. Additionally, in
our paper,
\mathcal{M}_{F}
also should be in order 1, as quantum
effects can be neglected if it is too small. We marked the range of
magnetic field and electron number density of this paper as a purple
ellipse in Fig.
5
, which is roughly where
\mathcal{M}_{F}=0.1\sim 10

and
\omega_{\mathrm{p}}/\omega_{\mathrm{c}}=0.1\sim 10
overlaps.
Note that the ellipse does not extend into the region where
n\lesssim 10^{24}\mathrm{cm}^{-3}
,
which corresponds to where the Wigner-Seitz radius
r_{s}>1
, i.e.,
where the RPA breaks down. Such strong magnetic field strength (
10^{9}\sim 10^{13}\mathrm{G}
)
are quite common in extreme astrophysics. For example, the magnetosphere
of a pulsar
[
43
]
, or a high field magnetic
white dwarf
[
44
,
45
]
. And the electron
number density range,
10^{24}\sim 10^{30}\mathrm{cm}^{-3}
, also
encompasses non-relativistic white dwarfs or accretion layers of compact
objects such as a black holes or a neutron stars.

\displaystyle I_{\ell}\left(\kappa\partial_{v}\right)f\left(v\right)=

(48)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{i}^{-\ell}}{2\pi}\iint\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}\lambda\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}v^{\prime}J_{\ell}\left(\kappa\lambda\right)f\left(v^{\prime}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\left(v-v^{\prime}\right)\lambda}

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}^{-\ell}\mathcal{F}\left[J_{\ell}\left(\kappa\lambda\right)\right]\left(v\right)*f\left(v\right),

(52)

Appendix B
Derivation of the magnetized equilibrium Wigner function

We start from the definition of the Wigner function
[
46
,
47
]

f\left(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v},t\right)=\int\frac{\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}\omega}{2\pi}g^{<}\left(\omega,\frac{m\boldsymbol{v}}{\hbar};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)

(57)

where

g^{<}\left(\omega,\boldsymbol{q};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)=\int\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}\tau\int\mathrm{\mathrm{d}}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}-\omega\tau\right)}g^{<}\left(\tau,\boldsymbol{\xi};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right),

and
g^{<}\left(\tau,\boldsymbol{\xi};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)
is the less Green function

g^{<}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\boldsymbol{x}_{1};t_{2},t_{1}\right)
in the Wigner coordinate:

\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\tau=t_{2}-t_{1},\boldsymbol{x}=\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{2}+\boldsymbol{x}_{1}}{2},t=\frac{t_{2}+t_{1}}{2}.

(58)

In an uniform equilibrium system,
g^{<}
is independent on
\boldsymbol{x}

and
t
.

g^{<}\left(\omega,\boldsymbol{q}\right)=\bar{f}^{<}\left(\omega\right)a\left(\omega,\boldsymbol{q}\right),

(59)

where

\bar{f}^{<}\left(\omega\right)=\frac{1}{1+\mathrm{e}^{\beta\left(\hbar\omega-\mu\right)}},

(60)

and
a\left(\omega,\boldsymbol{q}\right)
is the spectrum function. In the RPA
limit, it is simply the Fourier component of the non-interactive single
particle Green function
[
48
,
49
]
,
which is

a\left(\omega,\boldsymbol{q}\right)=2\mathrm{i}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(-\right)^{n}\mathrm{e}^{-q_{\perp}^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}L_{n}\left(2q_{\perp}^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}\right)}{\omega-\hbar q_{\parallel}^{2}/2m-\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\omega_{\mathrm{c}}}.

(61)

Hence, by means of the residue theorem, we have

\displaystyle f\left(\frac{\hbar q_{\parallel}}{m},\frac{\hbar q_{\perp}}{m}\right)=2

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-q_{\perp}^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(-\right)^{n}L_{n}\left(2q_{\perp}^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}\right)

(62)

\displaystyle\times\bar{f}^{<}\left[\frac{\hbar q_{\parallel}^{2}}{2m}-\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\omega_{\mathrm{c}}\right].

With an additional normalizing factor
\mathcal{A}
, we obtain Eq. (
20
)
in the main text.
