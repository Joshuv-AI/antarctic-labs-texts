Low intensity cognitive behavioral therapy to improve psychological well-being among family carers of older adults in Hong Kong: a cluster randomized controlled trial study protocol
Authors: Tom Chun Wai Tsoi, Kenneth Yu Hon Kwok, Wai Sze Chan, Vera Mun Yu Tang, Tarani Chandola, Jianchao Quan, Vivian Weiqun Lou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe rapid aging population in Hong Kong has led to an increased reliance on family carers to support older adults age in place. This demographic shift poses significant mental health challenges for carers, who often experience elevated levels of psychological distress, including anxiety and depression. Development of effective interventions to improve the mental health of carers of older adults in Hong Kong is needed.ObjectiveThis study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of a low intensity cognitive behavioral therapy (Li-CBT) intervention to improve the psychological well-being among family carers of older adults in Hong Kong, specifically focusing on reducing symptoms of depression and anxiety.MethodsA two-armed, multi-site, single-blind clustered randomized controlled trial will be conducted over 3 months. A target of 270 carers will be recruited from nine district-based carer support units and randomly assigned to either the Li-CBT intervention group or a control group. The primary outcomes will include changes in depression and anxiety levels, measured by the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7), assessed at baseline, immediate post-intervention (6-week) and 3-month post-intervention. Secondary outcomes will encompass a range of assessments, including carer multidimensional need assessment, social support, quality of life, caregiver burden, and resilience. It is hypothesized that participants in the Li-CBT intervention group will show a greater reduction in depression and anxiety scores compared to those in the control group. This study seeks to provide evidence for an integrated, evidence-based intervention model that can be widely disseminated to support family carers of older adults, ultimately improving their mental health and quality of life.DiscussionThe findings of this study will inform future research and practice on supporting carers of older adults. If effective, the Li-CBT intervention will contribute to reduce depression and anxiety levels and improve the overall quality of life while addressing a critical literature and service gap of culturally sensitive intervention for family carers of older adults in Hong Kong.Trial registrationThe protocol for this study has been registered with ClinicalTrials.gov under the identifier NCT06473012. The registration was completed prospectively before the enrollment of the first participant on July 2, 2024.Keywords: Carers, Caregivers, Psychological distress, Cognitive behavioral therapy, Caregiver burden

Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Population aging is a ubiquitous global phenomenon with an estimated one-sixth of the world population expected to be aged 60 years or more by 2030 [1].

With 22% of the population aged over 65 in 2023 [2], Hong Kong has become a super-aged society, and it is continuously aging at its fastest rate. It is also estimated that, by 2046, the percentage of the population aged over 65 will increase to 36% [3]. In comparison, the estimated percentage of global aging population is only 22% by 2050 [1]. Hong Kong is trending to be one of the oldest populations in the world. The resultant societal problems, which come with an ultra-aged society, will be challenging if not addressed promptly.

In response to Hong Kong’s transformation into a super-aged society, the role of family carers has become increasingly critical. There are approximately 280,000 family carers for older adults in Hong Kong [4], representing about 4% of the local population. This percentage is significantly lower than those in European countries, e.g. 13% in Spain and Portugal and 25% in Denmark and Belgium [5]. Thus, the burden on family carers in Hong Kong is relatively high. Without formal training, family carers often begin their caregiving journeys unprepared, leading to high levels of stress and mood symptoms [6–8]. Support for family carers is essential for maintaining an effective caregiving ecosystem for older adults in Hong Kong [9], for instance, an intervention for carers of dementia patients has been localized and tested in Hong Kong, yielding a favorable result that supports its efficacy [10].

Significance of distresses in carers

Various aspects and risk factors for carers’ mental health have been investigated. In a cross-sectional study by Lee et al. [11], 28% of carers were diagnosed with at least one psychiatric disorder—depressive disorder (11%), insomnia (6%)—which is higher than the prevalence in the general population (depression, 2%–5%; insomnia 4%) ([12], p. 204, 325). After their loved ones passed away, bereaved carers with symptoms of prolonged grief disorder may develop depressive symptoms as these psychiatric conditions are correlated [13]. Therefore, the adverse impact on carers’ mental health can persist long after they exit the caregiving role.

The poor mental health of older adults’ carers was shown to be related to caregiving burden and difficulties in the caregiving experience. A study involving Chinese family carers of older adults in Shanghai, China, revealed that caregiving burden significantly mediates the relationship between carer competence and health-related quality of life [14]. The researchers advocated for early identification and targeted measures, such as alleviating caregiving burden, to reduce potential health-related problems of family carers. Furthermore, a group of researchers led by Ho et al. [15] conducted in-depth interviews with Chinese-Canadian women who were family carers to their loved ones with Alzheimer’s disease. They reported that they felt obliged to ensure the wellbeing of their spouse and that institutionalization of their loved ones was highly undesirable. They also reported feeling fatigued and not being able to relax psychologically. Despite the cultural expectation and their perceived obligation, the burden of caregiving tasks and associated emotional demands became overwhelming, leading them to consider giving up their caregiving role after approximately 4 years.

The mental health of family carers, particularly older spousal carers, is a critical area of concern that warrants attention. In Hong Kong, Chow and Ho [16] studied depression among older spousal carers. They found that depression in this particular group was explained largely by individual psychological resources (e.g. purpose in life, caregiver burden, and personal wellbeing). Thus, they called for the development of mental health programs aimed at enhancing individual psychological resources for spousal carers. Given the elevated prevalence of psychological disturbances in carers and the need to increase carers’ individual psychological resources, evidence-based interventions and effective models of service delivery specifically for carers are needed.

Interventions for carers

Low intensity cognitive behavior therapy

Given the high prevalence of psychological distress among carers, effective interventions are needed to alleviate symptoms such as anxiety and depression. Low intensity cognitive behavior therapy (LiCBT) has been a primary focus of studies concerning psychological interventions for carers [17–19]. LiCBT is a scalable approach to delivering CBT, characterized by shorter total duration of group program (i.e. 60 to 90 min per session over 6–8 weeks) and diverse formats, including self-help materials and group delivery, often delivered by mental health workers rather than specialized clinicians [20, 21].

A meta-analysis by Kaddour et al. [18] found that LiCBT significantly reduced psychological distress and caregiving burden in dementia carers, with a medium-sized effect on burden measured by the Zarit Burden Interview. Additionally, self-help interventions using CBT techniques in community settings have shown greater effectiveness in managing depressive symptoms [22]. These findings suggest that LiCBT is a potentially effective approach to alleviating depressive symptoms in carers in the community [23].

Family relationships

Family relationships have been reported as one of the stressors for carers of older adults [24–26]. Several empirical studies have investigated interventions aimed at positively influencing family relationships through communication skills training in various forms, including peer facilitation [27], home-visit [28], and internet programs [29]. Drossel et al. [30] demonstrated that dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) skills training, including mindfulness and interpersonal effectiveness, improved carers’ problem-focused coping, enhanced emotional well-being, and reduced sense of fatigue among carers of people with dementia in group settings ([31], p.15–16).DBT has been studied for its efficacy in treating mood disorders such as depression and anxiety [32–34]. Focusing on interpersonal effectiveness could enhance family relationships and psychological well-being, though resource constraints limit the inclusion of all DBT components in this study.

Lifestyle intervention

Numerous studies have provided supporting evidence for the positive influence of daily habits on both short- and long-term health [35]. It is intuitive to know that a healthier lifestyle, which may include being a non-smoker, maintaining a healthy body-mass-index, regular physical activities, and adhering to a nutritious diet, can positively affect one’s physical health [36–38].

In recent decades, researchers have been increasingly intrigued by the connection between lifestyle and mental health. Lifestyle improvement, if implemented effectively, can be considered a low-cost and self-sustaining intervention for promoting mental health. In New Zealand, Bonnie et al. [39] showed that consuming fruits and vegetables improved affect in young adults, with benefits extending to the following day. Other groups of researchers from the USA and Europe found a similar connection between a healthy diet, physical activities, and mental well-being [40, 41]. A systematic review by Mammen and Faulkner [42] found that physical activity (e.g. 150 min of walking per week) is negatively associated with depression risk. The carers of older adults also tend to report high levels of stress due to the heavy and non-stop caregiving demands on them. Lifestyle interventions focusing on nutrition, daily exercise, and mind–body practices offer a low-cost, sustainable approach to managing psychological distress [43].

Limitations of previous studies

The aforementioned studies illustrate the need to promote mental wellbeing and alleviate psychological disturbances in carers, highlighting the potential of LiCBT and lifestyle interventions to address these concerns in the community. However, the effectiveness of an integrated evidence-based intervention designed specifically for carers has yet to be established. Moreover, various ways of disseminating such integrated intervention, e.g. face-to-face group settings, can be further studied to enhance literature knowledge and improve existing service models for carers of older adults.

Objectives {7}

This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of a low intensity cognitive behavioral therapy (LiCBT) intervention in improving the psychological well-being of family carers of older adults in Hong Kong by reducing anxiety and depression symptoms and enhancing secondary psychosocial outcomes. The hypotheses are as follows: (1) participants in the Li-CBT intervention group will exhibit greater reductions in anxiety scores, as measured by the GAD-7, at 6-week and 3-month follow-ups compared to the control group; (2) participants in the Li-CBT intervention group will show greater reductions in depression scores, as measured by the PHQ-9, at 6-week and 3-month follow-ups compared to the control group; and (3) participants in the intervention group will demonstrate improvements in secondary outcomes, including the multidimensional needs assessment, quality of life, and other psychosocial factors compared to the control group.

Trial design {8}

This is a two-armed, multi-site, single-blind clustered RCT over 3 months with three outcome measures at baseline, immediate post-intervention and 3-month post-intervention. In this trial, clusters are defined as the nine district-based carer support units in Hong Kong, each serving as a distinct group of family carers of older adults. These clusters will be randomized in a 1:1 allocation ratio to either Li-CBT intervention group, focusing on symptom management, stress management and coping skills intervention (n = 135), or a control group (n = 135). The trial is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov with the identifier NCT06473012.

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting {9}

Carers will be recruited from the designated centres of Jockey Club Carer Space Project through purposive sampling (Appendix 2). Recruitment began in August 2024 to align with the operational timeline of the Jockey Club Carer Space Project and to allow sufficient time to engage the hard-to-reach carer population. Due to challenges in identifying and engaging eligible participants, rolling recruitment is ongoing as of October 2025 and is expected to continue until March 2026 to achieve the target sample size (n = 270 across nine carer-support units). Project staff of designated centres will obtain informed consent for eligibility screening by the research team from the potential participants. The screening test will be conducted by research assistants trained in test administration. They will contact participants who fulfil the inclusion criteria to explain the research procedure, obtain research consent, and schedule the time for the baseline assessment.

Eligibility criteria {10}

The following inclusion and exclusion criteria will be adopted to recruit the participants.

Inclusion criteria: (1) being an adult aged 18 or above with a family member aged 60 or above requiring caregiving, providing at least 6 h of care per week (Silver et al., 2004); (2) self-identifying as a family carer; (3) living in Hong Kong; (4) able to communicate in Cantonese to effectively engage with the interventionist during the intervention; (5) voluntary participation, and (6) reporting moderate need in Carer Multidimensional Need Assessment [44]. Exclusion criteria: (1) having diagnosed Alzheimer’s or other dementia, and (2) experiencing acute health conditions that hinder caregiving support or intervention participation.

Who will take informed consent? {26a}

This study will use a digital consent form, which is reviewed by the research team. Eligible carers will receive a WhatsApp message with a Qualtrics link to sign the consent form before filling in the online questionnaire for baseline assessment by the research assistants.

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {26b}

Not applicable. This study does not involve the collection of biological specimens from participants.

Interventions

Intervention description {11a}

Low intensity cognitive behavioral therapy group

The experimental group receives a comprehensive intervention based on cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), lifestyle intervention, and effective communication skills. This intervention is designed to be implemented in a group setting with 12 to 15 carers. It consists of 6 weekly sessions, each lasting for 90 to 120 min. The sessions utilize cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) techniques, lifestyle modifications, and effective communication strategies to reduce depression and anxiety levels (Table 1). In between each session, participants are assigned home practices to further reinforce the skills learned.

Two booster sessions will be delivered in a 4-week interval after the initial 6 weekly sessions to help carers maintain and build upon the skills and strategies they have learned, ensuring a sustained impact over time. The intervention is designed by a clinical psychologist, under the supervision of an Assistant Professor of Psychology and a clinical psychologist, who is an expert in applying the CBT approach to develop and test interventions using RCT design. The intervention will be conducted by interventionists composed of clinical psychologist, social worker, and staff with clinical/therapy professional training with membership/registration of relevant professional bodies in Hong Kong or overseas.

Control group

On the other hand, the control group attends 6 weekly classroom sessions that primarily provide educational information about caregiving, lifestyle, and communication. These sessions aim to increase the participants’ knowledge and understanding of the factors that impact caregiving (Table 2). Each session in the control group lasts for 90 to 120 min. The participants will have the opportunity to engage in open-ended reflective discussions about the content without guidance from the group facilitator. These activities will focus on developing practical caregiving skills and promoting overall well-being, rather than directly addressing specific psychological or mental health factors. By implementing this type of control condition, we can more effectively isolate the effects of the psychological intervention being studied, since the control group will take part in a different activity that does not directly address the specific mental health domains being studied.

Explanation for choice of comparators {6b}

In the study design, a structurally equivalent control condition was selected to ensure that all participants received some form of intervention, thereby controlling for the effects of attention and engagement that can influence outcomes. By providing relevant information and skills related to caregiving, educational sessions were expected to help improve their experiences and skills. This active comparator allows for the assessment of the specific efficacy of Li-CBT while minimizing potential biases related to passive controls. This approach allows for a clearer understanding of the unique benefits of Li-CBT compared to general education, ultimately contributing to more informative research outcomes.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

Participants in the trial may discontinue or modify their involvement in the group therapy intervention based on several criteria. First, the carers may choose to withdraw from the intervention at any time if they feel it is not meeting their needs or if they are experiencing increased stress or discomfort related to participation. Additionally, significant changes in a participant’s caregiving circumstances, such as a change in the health status of the care recipient, may necessitate a modification or discontinuation of their involvement. If participants experience any adverse effects from their involvement in the group therapy, such as heightened anxiety or emotional distress, their health concerns will be taken into consideration. In such cases, they will have the option to either adjust their level of participation or withdraw from the program.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

Interventionist adherence

Different strategies will be employed to ensure consistent adherence assessment by the interventionists and group facilitators. They will receive training before delivering the intervention or control group sessions, which will include an overview of each program’s background, delivery methods, standards for each arm, and opportunities to practice delivering the sessions. Training includes self-study materials, didactic training with the research team leader, and roleplay of intervention delivery. Standardized materials like intervention slides, educational videos, and a facilitator guide will be provided. The interventionists and group facilitators will meet with the research team leader at least biweekly to address any issues that arise during delivery.

Participant adherence

To enhance adherence, participants will be provided with clear instructions and support throughout the intervention process. Regular check-ins and reminders will be implemented to encourage ongoing engagement in intervention and control groups.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

There are no relevant concomitant care or interventions permitted or prohibited during the trial. Participants will not receive any additional treatments or interventions that could affect the outcomes of the study.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

Since the risk of harm from participating in the trial is minimal, there is no provision for compensating those who may suffer harm due to their involvement. The research will include two follow-up assessments: one immediately after the intervention and another 3 months later, to evaluate participants’ outcomes.

Primary outcomes

Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7)

The GAD-7 is a widely used screening tool that measures the severity of generalized anxiety disorder by assessing the frequency of anxiety symptoms experienced over the past 2 weeks. Respondents rate their experiences on a 4-point scale from “not at all” to “nearly every day,” providing a reliable assessment of anxiety severity [45].

Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9)

The PHQ-9 is a self-report instrument used to assess depression symptoms, consisting of nine items that correspond to the diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder. Participants will rate the frequency of symptoms experienced over the past 2 weeks, helping to determine the severity of depressive symptoms [46]. The last item serves as a screening question for suicide risk, requiring further assessment if answered affirmatively.

Secondary outcomes

22-item Carer Multidimensional Need Assessment

The 22-item Carer Multidimensional Need Assessment was developed based on a large-scale population survey on carers’ well-being in Hong Kong. This tool covers six need domains: (1) carers’ health challenges, (2) carers’ mental ill health, (3) lack of support, (4) high care challenges, (5) caregiving capacity, and (6) risk/need for immediate support (Appendix 1).

MOS Social Support Survey (MOS-SSS-6)

The MOS-SSS-6 is a 6-item scale that measures an individual’s perceived social support. It covers emotional, informational, tangible, and affectionate support. Respondents rate the availability of each type of support on a 5-point scale. Higher scores indicate greater perceived social support [47].

EuroQol-5 Dimensions-5 Levels (EQ-5D-5L)

The EQ-5D-5L is a standardized instrument for measuring general health status. It consists of 5 dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. For each dimension, respondents choose from 5 levels ranging from “no problems” to “extreme problems.” The EQ-5D-5L is a widely used, preference-based measure of health-related quality of life. It is designed for self-completion by respondents and is applicable to a wide range of health conditions and treatments [48].

The World Health Organization Quality of Life–BREF (WHOQOL-BREF)

The WHOQOL-BREF is a 28-item instrument developed by the World Health Organization to assess an individual’s quality of life across four domains: physical health, psychological health, social relationships, and environment. Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating a better quality of life. The WHOQOL-BREF provides a concise yet comprehensive evaluation of an individual’s overall well-being, making it a widely used tool in research, clinical practice, and public health assessments. Policy 21 Hong Kong Limited has been commissioned by the Hong Kong Jockey Club Charities Trust to conduct a survey on the quality of life of the target service users in the Jockey Club’s elderly and elderly services. The survey results will help the Jockey Club to plan and formulate projects [49, 50].

Cantonese short version of Zarit Burden Interview (CZBI-Short-12)

The CZBI-Short-12 is a brief questionnaire used to measure carer burden in individuals caring for a family member with a chronic illness or disability. It is validated in Hong Kong and a spoken Cantonese version of the 12-item ZBI to assess the burden on carers in clinical and community settings. Each item subjectively measured caregiver burden on a 5-point Likert scale, with possible answers ranging from 0 = never to 4 = very frequently [51, 52].

Self-Care Self-Efficacy Scale (SCSES-10)

The SCSES-10 is a measurement tool used to assess an individual’s self-efficacy. Self-care self-efficacy for chronic illness has been tested in the Hong Kong population. This 10-item instrument measures self-efficacy related to self-care maintenance, monitoring, and management in patients with chronic illness. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = not at all confident to 5 = totally confident. A higher score represents a higher level of self-efficacy [53].

Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-10)

The CD-RISC-10 is a 10-item self-administered scale that assesses an individual’s level of resilience. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 = not true at all to 4 = true all of the time. The total possible scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores being an indicator of high resilience [54, 55].

Participant timeline {13}

This study will consist of a 6-week intervention, with a screening process to be conducted prior to the intervention. Participants will complete a baseline survey up to 2 weeks before intervention. They will participate in either the intervention or control group on weekly basis for 6 weeks. A follow-up survey will be completed within 2 weeks of the last session. A second follow-up survey will be administered 3 months after the last session.

The participation of carers in tests, surveys, and interviews will not affect the quality of care they receive. No drug usage or medical treatment is involved in this study, and there are no anticipated physical or medical risks to participants. Withdrawal from the study is voluntary at any time and will not impact participants’ ongoing medical care or legal rights.

Sample size {14}

According to a meta-analysis of effectiveness of CBT compared with care as usual and other psychotherapies, CBT had moderate to large effect size compared to control conditions such as care as usual and waitlist and were still significant at 6–12 month follow-up [56]. According to a systematic review and meta-analysis of effectiveness of cognitive behavioral therapy for carers of people with dementia, CBT had moderate to large effect size compared to control conditions [57]. However, the effect size is expected to be smaller than what was found in the meta-analysis. This study aims to detect a clinically relevant difference in change over time, which is targeted to be a medium effect size.

G*power software was used to estimate the sample size for this cluster randomized controlled trial. Accounting for clustering in sample size estimates, power calculations were conducted assuming a cluster-level intraclass correlation (ICC) = 0.05, a medium treatment effect equivalent to Cohen’s d = 0.5 at α = 0.05 (two-tailed) and power = 0.8. With 9 clusters (5 in the intervention arm and 4 in the control arm), approximately 24 participants per cluster are required, yielding a base sample size of 216 participants. To account for a 15% attrition rate, informed by pilot study results, and the potential effect of moderators, the research team increased the sample size to 270 participants with 135 participants in each arm [57]. This ensures sufficient power to detect the anticipated effect while accommodating potential dropout and variability in treatment response across clusters.

Recruitment {15}

Participants will be recruited from the 9 district-based carer support units of the Jockey Club Carer Space Project. Recruitment began in August 2024 to align with the project’s operational timeline and to allow sufficient time to engage the hard-to-reach carer population. Due to challenges in identifying and engaging eligible participants, rolling recruitment is ongoing as of October 2025 and is expected to continue until March 2026 to achieve the target sample size (n = 270). Participants will be recruited if they meet the inclusion criteria and are willing to join. Researchers will also connect with direct service providers at these organizations to encourage them to share information with family carers.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

Carer-support units will be stratified prior to randomization based on three levels of socioeconomic status (SES)—low, medium, and high—to ensure balanced distribution of socioeconomic factors across the intervention and control groups, as SES can influence carers’ access to resources and psychological outcomes [58]. These SES strata are defined based on a composite measure considering the carer’s median monthly household income of households by District and economically inactive employment status. To construct the composite measure, principal component analysis (PCA) will be employed, a statistical technique that reduces the dimensionality of the data while retaining the most significant variance. This approach allows us to combine the median income and employment status into a single composite score that reflects the relative socioeconomic position of each carer-support unit. Randomization will be conducted prior to baseline assessment by an independent clinical trial staff, separately within each SES stratum, to ensure a comparable distribution of socioeconomic factors between the Li-CBT intervention group and the control group. Nine district-based carer support units will be randomized in a 1:1 allocation ratio to Li-CBT symptom management, stress management and coping skills intervention (n = 135) and control group (n = 135).

Concealment mechanism {16b}

The randomization list will be generated before the beginning of the trial. Only the research team leader can access the sequence file where randomization occurs.

Implementation {16c}

The research team leader will generate the allocation sequence prior to enrolling any participants. The allocations will not be revealed to the research staff involved in the enrolment process until participants have been randomized.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

The research assistants who are involved in post-randomization data collection will be blinded to the intervention assignment. Other research team members and project staff of the designated centres will not be blinded. Assessments will be self-administered by participants and supported by research assistants.

Procedure for unbinding if needed {17b}

This study utilizes an open-label design. In this setup, interventionists and group facilitators are aware of the treatment assignments. However, the outcome assessors and data analysts will be blinded to the treatment assignments. This approach helps maintain the integrity of the analysis by preventing any potential bias that could arise from unblinding.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

This study will use a digital consent form. Eligible carers will receive a WhatsApp message with a link to the consent form and assessment, which is sent by trained research assistants. Prior to sending this online form, key information from the documents is reviewed by the research team leader. All assessors will complete training that includes practice in administering surveys, particularly in situations where participants require support to complete the survey.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}

Several strategies will be implemented to enhance participant retention and ensure comprehensive follow-up. Participants who complete three measurements and attend at least 70% of the scheduled sessions will receive coupon incentives as a token of appreciation for their commitment. This incentive aims to encourage active participation and adherence to the intervention protocols. Additionally, regular communication will be maintained with participants to foster engagement and address any concerns they may have. For participants who discontinue or deviate from the intervention, relevant outcome data will be collected to understand the reasons for withdrawal and assess the impact on overall study results.

Data management {19}

To check the accuracy of data entry, the research team leader will select 5% of surveys for data quality check. Data quality checks will consist of a second research team member marking the accuracy of how response options will be captured in the Qualtrics platform.

Confidentiality {27}

To maintain participant confidentiality, each individual in the study will be assigned a unique ID, which is a sequentially generated identifier used to monitor participant status throughout the trial. A study key linking identifiable information to study data will be kept in an encrypted folder, accessible only to the principal investigator (PI) and the research team leader. When data collectors are assigned to gather follow-up survey data, they will receive the participant’s name, phone number, and a link to the corresponding study survey. All identifying information related to participants will be deleted upon completion of the trial.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

Primary outcome

Linear mixed model will be conducted to evaluate the hypothesis regarding whether there is a reduction in anxiety and depression symptoms in the Li-CBT group compared to the control group. The model will include fixed effects for treatment condition (Li-CBT and control); assessment time points (baseline, immediate post-intervention and 3-month post-intervention); and their interaction. Time will be modeled as a categorical variable, with the three assessment time points treated as discrete levels to account for potential non-linear changes in outcomes over time. Anxiety and depression scores, measured using GAD-7 for anxiety and PHQ-9 for depression, will serve as the dependent variables. To account for potential baseline imbalances, baseline variables (e.g. age, sex, and education level) that differ significantly between groups (p < 0.05 in baseline comparisons) will be included as covariates in the model to adjust for their potential confounding effects. A random intercept will be included for clusters (e.g. district-based carer support units) to account for the correlation within clusters due to the cluster randomized design. Statistical significance is defined as alpha levels less than 0.05.

Secondary outcomes

Separate linear mixed models will be conducted to examine the effects of the Li-CBT intervention on the secondary outcomes, which include the MOS Social Support Survey (MOS-SSS-6), EuroQol-5 Dimensions-5 Levels (EQ-5D-5L), World Health Organization Quality of Life – BREF (WHOQOL-BREF), Cantonese Short Version of the Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI-12), Self-Care Self-Efficacy Scale (SCSES-10), and c-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-10). The models will include treatment conditions (Li-CBT and control); assessment time points (baseline, immediate post-intervention and 3-month post-intervention); and their interaction effects as fixed effects. Statistical significance will be defined as alpha levels less than 0.05.

For the 22-item Carer multidimensional assessment, a logistic mixed model will be conducted, as the outcome will be binary, with 0 indicating not moderate need and 1 indicating moderate need. The model will include treatment conditions (Li-CBT and control); assessment time points (baseline, immediate post-intervention and 3-month post-intervention); and their interaction effects as fixed effects. Statistical significance for this model will also be defined as alpha levels less than 0.05.

Interim analyses {21b}

An interim analysis will be conducted after 50% of participants (n = 135) have been randomized and completed the primary outcome assessment to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of the intervention. The analysis will use descriptive statistics and a preliminary comparison of mean well-being scores between intervention and control groups (two-sided t-test, alpha = 0.05). An independent statistician will perform the analysis. The results will be reviewed by the independent Data Monitoring Committee, which meets weekly to monitor data quality and trial safety. The Data Monitoring Committee will evaluate the interim results against predefined termination rules and provide recommendations to the Trial Steering Committee on whether to continue, modify, or terminate the trial. The Trial Steering Committee may recommend trial termination under the following instances: (1) if less than 60% of the target sample (n = 162) is recruited within 12 months of study start; (2) if the intervention group shows a statistically significant decline in mean well-being (10-point increase on PHQ9/GAD7 that worsens post-intervention and exceeds any decline in the control group by 5 points; or (3) if more than 15% of carers report unexpected serious adverse events linked to participation in the intervention.

Any decision to terminate the trial early will be made by the Trial Steering Committee, informed by the Data Monitoring Committee recommendations, and communicated to the ethics committee (Institutional Review Board of the University of Hong Kong), and funder within 7 days. A full report, including rationale and interim data summaries, will be documented and submitted.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g. subgroup analyses) {20b}

No additional analyses, such as subgroup analyses, will be conducted. This decision is based on the study’s primary focus on the overall efficacy of Li-CBT across the entire sample and the limited sample size at the cluster and participant levels, which may reduce statistical power for reliable subgroup comparisons. However, exploratory subgroup analyses (e.g. by socioeconomic status or carer age) may be conducted as secondary analysis to explore potential heterogeneity in treatment effects, provided sufficient data are available and statistical power permits. Any such analyses will include appropriate adjustments for multiple comparisons (Bonferroni correction) to maintain statistical rigor.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

Linear mixed modelling with full information maximum likelihood (FIML) will be used to conduct intention-to-treat analysis to account for missing data due to attrition. While FIML assumes that data are missing at random (MAR), we acknowledge the potential concern regarding unequal dropout rates between treatment and control groups. To mitigate this risk, we will perform sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings under different missing data mechanisms, including missing completely at random (MCAR), MAR, and missing not at random (MNAR). For MCAR and MAR, we will use multiple imputation to fill in missing values based on observed data. For MNAR, we will use pattern-mixture models to test how different dropout patterns affect results. Additionally, sporadic missing data on questionnaire measures are prevented by Qualtrics’ function of validation that requires complete responses.

Plans to give access to the full protocol, participant level-data, and statistical code {31c}

Documentation, such as code names and original survey questions, along with protocol documents, will also be uploaded to the repository Sharing of analytic code will be provided at the discretion of the investigator team, based on the assessed qualifications of the access requester, with access to code files granted to individuals holding an advanced academic degree or possessing relevant experience. Discussion

The present study aims to overcome the limitations of previous research by integrating essential therapeutic components from various interventions into a comprehensive, Li-CBT framework. By consolidating these elements, we expect to create a holistic approach that not only enhances psychological well-being but also actively prevents psychological symptoms among carers of older adults. This integrated intervention is designed to address the unique challenges faced by carers, offering them tailored support that is both practical and effective.

Our study design incorporates a randomized controlled trial (RCT) with an active control group. This design enables a robust comparison between the effectiveness of the Li-CBT intervention and the control, thereby providing more reliable evidence of its efficacy. By utilizing an active comparator, we can account for potential placebo effects and better isolate the impact of our intervention. This methodological strength enhances the validity of our findings, allowing us to draw clearer conclusions regarding the benefits of the integrated approach for carers.

Furthermore, the RCT design facilitates random allocation of participants, which minimizes selection bias and enhances the generalizability of our results. By ensuring that participants are evenly distributed across both intervention and control groups, we can more accurately assess the specific effects of the Li-CBT intervention. This study, therefore, represents a significant advancement in the field, aiming to contribute valuable insights into effective support strategies for carers.

In this study, the interventionists in the LiCBT group comprise clinical psychologists, social workers who are responsible for delivering the intervention materials, while the control group consists of group facilitators with diverse backgrounds who will oversee the control group. This separation aims to minimize the risk of unintentionally delivering treatment components from one intervention to participants assigned to the other.

As each intervention is guided by a separate treatment protocol, we ensure that the fidelity of the treatment is carefully monitored. The components of the experimental intervention and the control intervention are designed to be distinct, thus reducing the likelihood of contamination between the two groups. Additionally, we anticipate that intervention appointments may need to be rescheduled due to participant illness, scheduling conflicts, or other unforeseen circumstances. We will make every effort to accommodate missed sessions within a reasonable timeframe.

The actual number of weeks for the intervention to be completed will be recorded for both groups. Participants are instructed not to engage in any additional interventions related to the study’s focus during the trial; however, some may choose to do so. We will evaluate whether any additional support they receive might lead to confounding influences on the trial outcomes.

Limitations

A potential limitation of this study is that randomization of carer-support units was conducted prior to baseline assessment. This approach may introduce bias if baseline measurements reveal imbalances in characteristics (age, sex, anxiety, or depression scores) between Li-CBT and control groups, as units and participants were assigned to conditions before baseline data were collected. To mitigate this, baseline variables showing significant differences (p < 0.05) will be included as covariates in the statistical analyses to adjust for potential confounding effect. Additionally, stratified randomization by SES was employed to minimize imbalances in socioeconomic factors, which are known to influence psychological outcomes.

The study’s findings can significantly impact both clinical practice and future research. If the Li-CBT intervention is found to be effective, it could be widely disseminated to support carers of older adults to improve their mental health and overall quality of life. In conclusion, this study seeks to fill a critical gap in the literature by developing and testing a comprehensive Li-CBT intervention for carers of older adults. The results of this study have the potential to inform both clinical practice and future research, contributing to the enhancement of mental health resources and support for carers.
