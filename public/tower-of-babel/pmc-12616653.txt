A study on the association between serum antiphospholipase A2 receptor antibodies and the hypercoagulable state in Chinese patients with primary membranous nephropathy
Authors: Baolian Liu, Qiuyue Qin, Shuilin He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary membranous nephropathy (PMN) increases venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk. This study investigated the relationship between anti-phospholipase A2 receptor (PLA2R) antibody status and coagulation profiles via thromboelastography (TEG) in PMN patients. In this retrospective study, 53 PMN patients were stratified by anti-PLA2R antibody status. Thromboelastography parameters, including reaction time (R), clot kinetics (K), maximum amplitude (MA), coagulation index (CI), alpha angle (angle), and lysis at 30 minutes (LY30), and clinical outcomes were compared.Among the participants, 58.5% were anti-PLA2R-positive. All five thrombotic events (9.4% of cohort) occurred in seropositive patients, though this was not statistically significant (16.1% vs. 0%, p = 0.13). TEG profiles indicated hypercoagulability in anti-PLA2R-positive patients, showing significantly shorter R and K times, higher MA, greater CI, larger angle, and elevated LY30 (all p < 0.05). Anti-PLA2R-positive PMN patients demonstrate a hypercoagulable state on TEG compared to seronegative patients. Thrombotic events were only observed in the seropositive group, but the small event number precludes definitive conclusions. These results suggest a link between anti-PLA2R antibodies and coagulation abnormalities, warranting further large-scale prospective studies.

Introduction

Primary membranous nephropathy (PMN) is the predominant etiology of adult-onset nephrotic syndrome (NS), with an increasing prevalence in contemporary populations [1]. Venous thromboembolism (VTE), which encompasses deep vein thrombosis (DVT), renal vein thrombosis (RVT), and pulmonary embolism (PE), is a frequent and severe complication of NS that substantially contributes to morbidity and mortality. Notably, patients with PMN exhibit increased thrombotic susceptibility compared with patients with other types of nephropathy, with a particular predisposition toward RVT development [2]. Contemporary Chinese epidemiological data indicate VTE, RVT, and PE incidence rates of 36%, 33%, and 17%, respectively, within this cohort [3]. In addition to venous complications, arterial thrombotic events (ATEs) are significantly associated with NS [4].

Anti-phospholipase A2 receptor (PLA2R) autoantibodies, which are detectable in 70%–80% of PMN cases, demonstrate exceptional disease specificity [2]. These immunoglobulins serve as established pathogenic mediators and prognostic indicators of the PMN [5]. Substantial evidence has confirmed their correlation with clinical disease activity and progression dynamics [6,7]. Emerging research has further implicated anti-PLA2R antibodies in the pathogenesis of hypercoagulable states [2]. Motivated by these observations, this investigation specifically examined the relationship between anti-PLA2R seropositivity and characteristically elevated VTE risk in PMNs.

Methods

Design

This retrospective investigation analyzed all inpatients diagnosed with primary membranous nephropathy at Ruijin Hospital’s Wuxi Branch, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, between May 2020 and October 2023. The prevalence of thromboembolic events was evaluated at initial diagnosis and throughout the follow-up period. Follow-up information was obtained through a comprehensive review of the institution’s electronic health records.

Participants

Study inclusion required meeting both of the following conditions: individuals aged 18–80 years with PMN confirmed through either renal biopsy or positive anti-PLA2R antibody testing. The exclusion criteria were secondary membranous nephropathy etiologies (systemic lupus erythematosus, hepatitis B virus infection, malignancies, or medication-induced), along with three specific circumstances: absence of baseline anti-PLA2R antibody testing; insufficient thrombosis screening via lower extremity venous ultrasound during diagnostic and follow-up phases; and coexistence of additional venous thromboembolism risk factors, including antiphospholipid syndrome, prolonged immobilization, active malignancies, or dialysis catheter-associated thrombosis. Sample size consideration

This being a retrospective cohort study, the sample size (n = 53) was determined by the total number of eligible patients meeting the stringent inclusion criteria during the study period, rather than by a prior power calculation. A post-hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1 based on the observed incidence of thromboembolic events (16.13% in seropositive vs. 0% in seronegative patients), resulting in a statistical power of 0.53 (α = 0.05, two-tailed).

Screening for thromboembolic events

Standardized screening protocols were implemented for all participants. Venous thrombosis assessment involved lower-extremity vascular ultrasound examination. Arterial thrombosis evaluation involves troponin testing, echocardiography, electrocardiography, and neuroimaging (CT or MRI) when clinically warranted. Supplemental investigations included renal vein ultrasound for acute unilateral flank pain or macroscopic hematuria and computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) in patients with decreased oxygen saturation, hemoptysis, or respiratory compromise.

Following the initial blood draw for conventional coagulation tests, thromboelastography (TEG) was performed to comprehensively assess the hemostatic function of each patient. TEG analysis was conducted using a TEG 5,000 Hemostasis Analyzer System (Haemonetics Corporation) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Citrated whole blood samples were used for the analysis. The following TEG parameters and their physiological significance were recorded: Reaction time (R time), representing the latency from coagulation initiation to initial fibrin clot formation, primarily reflects the activity of coagulation factors; Clot kinetics (K time), the time taken for the clot strength to reach 20 mm amplitude, mainly indicates fibrinogen function and level; Alpha angle (α angle), which measures the kinetics of fibrin build-up and cross-linking, also reflects fibrinogen function; Maximum amplitude (MA), representing the ultimate strength of the fibrin clot, is primarily determined by platelet function; Coagulation Index (CI), a computed overall measure of coagulability that incorporates the R, K, α, and MA parameters; and Lysis at 30 min (LY30), the percentage of clot lysis 30 min after the MA is achieved, indicating fibrinolytic activity. The reference ranges provided by the manufacturer and used in our laboratory for these parameters are as follows: R time, 5–10 min; K time, 1–3 min; α angle, 53–72 deg; MA, 50–70 mm; CI,−3–3; and LY30, 0%–7.5%.

Clinical evaluation

Baseline and follow-up clinical parameters included demographic characteristics (age and sex, smoking history), medication usage (immunosuppressive regimens, anticoagulant therapy, and conservative management with ARBs or SGLT2 inhibitors), renal function indicators (24-h urine protein, serum albumin, serum creatinine, and estimated glomerular filtration rate), lipid profiles (low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, total cholesterol, and triglycerides), and hemostatic markers (platelet count, plasma fibrinogen, prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, D-dimer, and thrombelastogram). Anti-PLA2R antibody quantification was performed via a commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; EUROIMMUN, Lübeck, Germany). According to the manufacturer’s instructions and established clinical practice, seropositivity was defined as >20 Ru/mL, seronegativity as <14 Ru/mL, and values between 14–20 Ru/mL were considered borderline. For the purpose of this study’s binary classification, samples within the borderline range (14–20 Ru/mL) were excluded to ensure clear demarcation between the anti-PLA2R-positive and negative cohorts, as per the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 22.0; IBM Corp.). The normality of distribution for all continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation, and intergroup comparisons were performed using Student’s independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as the median with interquartile range (IQR), and intergroup comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %), and intergroup comparisons were performed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. To explore the potential influence of clinical parameters on thromboelastography findings, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed between key TEG parameters (CI and LY30) and serum albumin levels, as well as 24-h urinary protein. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p-value of <0.05.

Results

Clinical characteristics of patients

This study included 47 patients with biopsy-proven PMN and six patients who tested positive for anti-PLA2R antibodies. Patients with a serum ALB level <25 g/L received subcutaneous injections of low-molecular-weight heparin for anticoagulation during their hospital stay and were then switched to oral anticoagulants such as levasaban or edoxaban for outpatient treatment, as long as there were no contraindications. The 53 patients with PMN were divided into anti-PLA2R-positive group and anti-PLA2R-negative groups on the basis of their anti-PLA2R antibody status. Among the 53 patients, 31 were positive for anti-PLA2R antibodies, and 22 were negative. Eleven patients received rituximab monotherapy, five received a combination of rituximab and other immunosuppressants, 19 received a combination of glucocorticoids and cyclophosphamide, and seven received a combination of glucocorticoids and calcineurin inhibitors. The remaining 11 patients were treated conservatively with medications, such as ARBs and SGLT2Is, as much as possible.

These included two cases of PE, two cases of DVT in the lower limbs, and one case of extensive venous thrombosis involving the left femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial, and calf intermuscular veins, with extension to the left external iliac vein and right calf intermuscular veins.

Notably, two patients were diagnosed with PE prior to the formal diagnosis of MN: one presented with chest pain and hemoptysis, and the other developed hemoptysis one week after initial presentation with bilateral lower limb edema, leading to CTPA confirmation. The remaining three venous thromboses were identified at the time of MN diagnosis.

All five patients exhibited elevated anti-PLA2R antibody levels compared to those without thrombosis (p = 0.017). Although the incidence of thromboembolic events was numerically higher in the anti-PLA2R-positive group (16.1% vs. 0%, p = 0.133), the difference did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the limited number of events (n = 5).

Regarding management, four patients received full-dose anticoagulation with low-molecular-weight heparin during hospitalization, followed by transition to rivaroxaban for outpatient care. One patient with bilateral calf muscular vein thrombosis did not receive anticoagulation based on the interventional physician’s assessment, as the patient did not have nephrotic syndrome.

TEG profiling revealed a consistent hypercoagulable pattern in all five patients with thrombosis, characterized by elevated CI values (range: 1.9–5.0) and MA values (Table 3). Given the small number of thrombotic events, a reliable multivariable regression analysis to adjust for potential confounders such as serum albumin levels or immunosuppressant use was not feasible.

Discussion

The hypercoagulable state is more pronounced in patients with membranous nephropathy, increasing their risk for venous thrombosis than in those with other forms of glomerulonephritis [8]. The discovery of the anti-PLA2R antibody in 2009 by Back [9] revealed that it is highly specific for MN and is rarely detected in other nephropathies, autoimmune diseases, or healthy individuals. Furthermore, the PLA2R autoantibody is not only a marker of activity and progression in MN [10,11] but also a useful tool for monitoring the response to immunosuppressants [12,13]. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between anti-PLA2R antibodies and the hypercoagulable state in patients with PMN.

Clinically, the incidence of thromboembolic events was numerically greater in seropositive patients (16.13%) than in seronegative patients (0%, p = 0.133), with venous thrombosis occurring in 9.43% of the overall cohort, a frequency aligned with prior reports [1,2]. The mechanistic basis for this thrombotic predisposition appears multifactorial: high anti-PLA2R antibody titers correlate with severe proteinuria and hypoalbuminemia, which are established thrombotic risk factors that also predict a reduced likelihood of clinical remission [14]. Additionally, secretory phospholipase A2, which serves as a high-affinity PLA2R ligand, promotes arachidonic acid release, which is intricately involved in both lipid metabolism and coagulation cascades [15]. Our TEG findings provide potential mechanistic insights into how these pathways might converge to promote clotting. The significantly elevated MA value, which primarily reflects platelet function, suggests enhanced platelet-fibrin interaction and aggregation. This could be driven by anti-PLA2R-mediated activation of inflammatory pathways or via sPLA2-induced thromboxane A2 production from arachidonic acid, a potent platelet activator [16]. Similarly, the elevated CI, a global measure of hypercoagulability, indicates a prothrombotic state that likely results from a combination of increased platelet reactivity (high MA), accelerated clot formation (low K, high angle), and potential endothelial dysfunction. Emerging evidence further implicates syndecan-1, a endothelial glycocalyx component, in mediating hypercoagulability in this patient population [17]. Syndecan-1 shedding is a marker of endothelial injury and could be a consequence of anti-PLA2R related immune activity [18]. This endothelial damage exposes subendothelial collagen and releases ultra-large von Willebrand factor multimers, which could further contribute to the accelerated clot initiation (shortened R time) and enhanced platelet aggregation (high MA) observed in our seropositive patients [19].

Our findings align with those of prior studies highlighting the association between anti-PLA2R antibodies and thrombotic risk in PMNs but also reveal critical nuances. Zhu et al. [2] demonstrated that anti-PLA2R antibody levels independently predict venous thrombosis in a large cohort of 365 PMN patients (OR = 1.269, p < 0.05), which is consistent with our observation of heightened hypercoagulability in antibody-positive individuals. However, unlike their multivariable model identifying LDL as a co-risk factor, our study did not replicate this association, potentially because of differences in cohort size (n = 53 vs. n = 365) or variability in lipid management protocols. Notably, Li et al. [20] extended this paradigm by identifying anti-PLA2R positivity as a VTE predictor, even in patients with serum ALB levels >25 g/L, a subgroup traditionally deemed low risk. Our data corroborate this stratification, as thrombotic events occurred exclusively in anti-PLA2R-positive patients, regardless of albumin level, underscoring the role of antibodies beyond hypoalbuminemia-driven hypercoagulability. Xu et al. [14] proposed that PLA2R-sPLA2 interactions promote arachidonic acid release, linking antibody activity to platelet aggregation and coagulation cascades. This hypothesis is partially supported by our TEG findings of accelerated clot initiation (reduced R/K values) and enhanced platelet–fibrin interaction (elevated MA/CI). While these studies collectively implicate anti-PLA2R antibodies in thrombogenesis, our work uniquely integrates TEG to dynamically quantify hypercoagulability, revealing subclinical abnormalities undetected by conventional assays such as D-dimer. Nevertheless, the limited sample size precluded multivariable confirmation of anti-PLA2R as an independent VTE predictor, emphasizing the need for a larger prospective validation of TEG-guided risk stratification.

In this retrospective study, we compared the hypercoagulable state of anti-PLA2R-positive patients and anti-PLA2R-negative patients via conventional coagulation indicators such as D-dimer, PT, and APTT with TEG. Conventional coagulation tests can detect only certain aspects of the coagulation process stages or components with low sensitivity and limited application value [21]. Research has shown that when coagulation factors reduce to 30%−50% of normal values, PT and APTT significantly change [22,23]. TEG can dynamically monitor the entire process of whole blood coagulation, which is an effective and reliable indicator for the timely detection of a hypercoagulable state [24]. The R value reflects the role of coagulation factors in the initial stage of coagulation. The MA value mainly reflects the aggregation function of platelets, and its value is influenced by the fibers and platelets. An increase in the MA value indicates that platelets tend to show increased aggregation and dysfunction. The K value and angle can provide feedback regarding the rate of blood clot formation. LY30 reflects fibrinolytic activity, and CI can determine the comprehensive status of coagulation and bleeding. In this study, the values of R and K in the positive PLA2R group were significantly lower than those in the anti-PLA2R-negative group. The Ma, CI, Angle, and LY30 were significantly greater in the anti-PLA2R-positive group than in the anti-PLA2R-negative group. No significant difference was found in conventional coagulation indicators between the two groups. This result indicates that the hypercoagulable state is more pronounced in the anti-PLA2R-positive group than in the anti-PLA2R-negative group. These findings align with recent studies demonstrating the utility of TEG for predicting VTE in nephrotic syndrome, where traditional parameters such as D-dimer lack sensitivity [25]. For example, Huang et al. reported that TEG examination helps detect hypercoagulability in patients with MN and that hypoalbuminemia is the most important risk factor for hypercoagulability in these patients [26]. Our data further extend the relevance of TEG to anti-PLA2R-driven hypercoagulability, suggesting its role in stratifying thrombotic risk and guiding personalized anticoagulation in the PMN.

Thromboelastographic analysis revealed consistent hypercoagulable patterns in anti-PLA2R-positive patients across clinical presentations. Compared with their seronegative counterparts, nephrotic syndrome patients exhibit significantly accelerated clot formation (reduced K values) and enhanced fibrinolysis (elevated LY30). Similarly, non-NS patients demonstrated substantially shortened reaction times, decreased clot formation times, and increased CIs in the antibody-positive group. These findings collectively indicate that anti-PLA2R seropositivity is associated with increased coagulation activation, irrespective of nephrotic syndrome status. Furthermore, our correlation analysis showed that the hypercoagulable TEG patterns (elevated CI and LY30) in anti-PLA2R-positive patients were not significantly correlated with serum albumin levels or the degree of proteinuria. This suggests that the observed hypercoagulable state may be directly linked to the presence of anti-PLA2R antibodies themselves, rather than being merely a secondary consequence of the classic nephrotic-state thrombotic risk factors. This finding provides preliminary support for the role of anti-PLA2R antibodies as an independent driver of coagulation abnormalities, although this hypothesis requires confirmation in larger studies capable of multivariable adjustment.

This study had several limitations. First, and most importantly, the sample size, particularly the low number of observed thrombotic events (n = 5), precluded the performance of a reliable multivariable regression analysis. Consequently, we could not statistically adjust for potential confounding variables such as serum albumin levels, degree of proteinuria, or specific immunosuppressive and anticoagulant therapies to definitively establish anti-PLA2R antibody positivity as an independent risk factor for hypercoagulability or thrombosis. Second, the serological assessment was limited to anti-PLA2R antibodies; we did not test for other known pathogenic antigens (e.g., THSD7A, NELL-1, EXT1/EXT2, or Sema3B) in the anti-PLA2R-negative group. This heterogeneity within the seronegative cohort may have confounded the observed associations, as we could not determine whether hypercoagulability is specific to anti-PLA2R positivity or a broader characteristic of antigen-positive PMN. Third, the assessment of coagulation was limited to thromboelastography and conventional tests; we did not perform more comprehensive assays such as thrombin generation testing (e.g., calibrated automated thrombogram), which have been shown to precisely quantify thrombin potential and predict thrombosis risk in other populations. Fourth, the timing of TEG measurement relative to treatment initiation was inconsistent; testing was performed in some patients at diagnosis prior to any anticoagulant therapy and in others during follow-up while on therapy. This heterogeneity introduces a potential confounding effect from medication exposure. Fifth, its retrospective design inherently restricted the protocol for thrombosis screening to symptomatic patients, likely missing subclinical events. Finally, the subgroup analyses, particularly regarding nephrotic syndrome status, should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size in these strata, which significantly underpowered formal interaction testing. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts, systematic screening for all known PMN antigens, uniform pretreatment assessment incorporating advanced coagulation assays, and standardized screening protocols are needed to validate these findings and clarify the specific role of anti-PLA2R antibodies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study identified differences in thromboelastography parameters between anti-PLA2R-positive and negative PMN patients, suggesting altered coagulation profiles in seropositive individuals. We observed that all thrombotic events occurred in anti-PLA2R-positive patients, though the small number of events precludes definitive conclusions about clinical thrombosis risk. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than establishing causal relationships, due to the retrospective design, limited sample size, and absence of multivariable adjustment for potential confounders. Our results highlight the need for further investigation into the potential role of anti-PLA2R antibodies in coagulation abnormalities, but do not support changes to current thromboprophylaxis strategies. Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes and comprehensive thrombosis screening are needed to validate these observations and clarify whether TEG parameters might contribute to risk stratification in PMN patients.
