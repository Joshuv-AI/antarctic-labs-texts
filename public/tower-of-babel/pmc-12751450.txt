“The unheard adversity”: the lived experiences of socially isolated survivors of wartime sexual assault
Authors: Sentayehu Oljira Bekele, Ashenafi Kibret Sendekie, Mengesha Shumet Dessalegn, Mohammed Aragaw Abera, Alechew Assefa Sete, Deriba Bekele Bedada, Sindew Asmare Wedi, Biruk Beletew Abate
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionWar has a profound impact on women, leading to gang rape, sexual violence, and the deliberate destruction of healthcare facilities, underscoring the urgent need for comprehensive solutions. This study aimed to explore the lived experiences of socially isolated survivors of sexual assault during the war in North Wollo Zone, Amhara, Ethiopia.MethodsA qualitative research design with a phenomenological approach was used to achieve the study’s objectives. Study participants were recruited using purposive sampling. A total of 25 participants were involved. Data was collected through focus group discussions (FGDs) and in-depth interviews. The data were transcribed, coded, organized, and then analyzed and interpreted using thematic analysis.ResultsThe results of this study reveal that socially isolated women who survived sexual assault during wartime often experienced abuse, segregation, and demoralization. Furthermore, these survivors faced significant psychological and social challenges, including feelings of helplessness, isolation, loss of dignity, and diminished self-confidence. Coping strategies identified by the participants included placing trust in God, seeking guidance from religious leaders, sharing their experiences with friends, elders, and experts, and participating in coffee ceremonies as a means of communal support.ConclusionThe trauma of war-related sexual violence has led to significant psychological, physical, and health impacts. Survivors report high levels of isolation, degradation, and mistreatment. Given service disruptions, international support is crucial, and adopting successful approaches from other countries is recommended. Comprehensive rehabilitation services from governmental and non-governmental agencies are essential for supporting these vulnerable individuals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03803-9.

Introduction

Sexual violence in conflict, particularly the use of rape as a tool of war, has long been recognized as a deliberate strategy employed to terrorize, humiliate, and destabilize communities. It is not only a devastating physical violation of individuals but also a calculated tactic to exert control, create fear, and destroy social fabrics [1, 2]. The prevalence of sexual violence during armed conflict is global, with horrific examples seen in the Yugoslav Wars, the Rwandan Genocide, and most recently, in conflicts in regions like the Democratic Republic of Congo and Northern Ethiopia [1, 2]. International bodies, such as the United Nations, have acknowledged the gender-specific impacts of war, as highlighted in Resolutions 1325 and 1820, which aim to protect women and children from the atrocities of sexual violence in conflict zones. These resolutions reflect the recognition that sexual violence in war is not merely a byproduct of conflict, but a deliberate and systemic tool of warfare, used for political, ethnic, and military objectives [3, 4].

Globally, sexual violence in war has profound consequences, particularly for women and girls, who face the highest risk. The United Nations reports that approximately 18.3% of women have been sexually assaulted, illustrating that sexual violence is a widespread issue even outside conflict zones [5, 6]. However, its impact is magnified in conflict settings where women not only suffer the trauma of the assault but are also left vulnerable to HIV/AIDS, unwanted pregnancies, fistulas, and severe mental health issues such as Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) [7, 8]. The stigma attached to being a victim of wartime sexual violence further isolates survivors, preventing them from accessing support and hindering their reintegration into society [9].

In the context of Africa, the ongoing conflict in Tigray, Ethiopia, serves as a recent and poignant example of the horrors of wartime sexual violence. The Tigray conflict, which erupted in 2020, has resulted in widespread displacement, loss of life, and severe human rights violations, including reports of mass sexual violence committed by combatants on all sides [9, 10]. Despite a ceasefire brokered by the African Union in late 2022, the trauma inflicted on the survivors of sexual violence persists, deeply affecting the people of Tigray [11]. These acts of sexual violence were not random; rather, they were employed as tools of war, aimed at humiliating and destroying communities, spreading fear, and displacing populations. Women and girls, in particular, have borne the brunt of this violence, often subjected to gang rape, sexual slavery, and forced pregnancies [12].

While there is an increasing body of literature addressing sexual violence in global conflicts, there remains a significant gap in understanding the specific impacts of such violence in the Ethiopian context. The existing research on wartime sexual violence tends to focus on more widely studied regions such as the Balkans or Central Africa, while Ethiopia, despite its recent history of conflict, has received comparatively little attention [13]. The conflict in Northern Ethiopia, especially in the Amhara and Tigray regions, has unique socio-political and cultural dynamics that must be understood to fully grasp the scope and nature of wartime sexual violence in these contexts [14].

Understanding how women navigate the trauma of wartime sexual violence, including coping with isolation, stigma, and limited support, is vital for shaping effective policies and interventions in post-conflict settings [2, 14–17]. While significant literature focuses on the immediate impacts of conflict, significant gaps remain in understanding the long-term coping mechanisms of survivors, especially those who experienced sexual violence. Most studies highlight short-term consequences, and data is scarce on how survivors rebuild their lives and foster resilience over time.

This study seeks to fill this gap by investigating the experiences of sexual violence survivors among Amhara women in Northern Ethiopia. Specifically, the study aims to explore how wartime sexual violence has impacted these women, particularly in terms of the stigma they face and the long-term psychological and social consequences [18, 19]. This study examines not only the sexual acts of violence but also the covert and overt stigmas survivors experience, such as social ostracism, forced displacement, and fear of rejection by their communities [18, 19]. By focusing on the Amhara women’s experiences, this study provides a nuanced understanding of the cultural, social, and psychological aspects of wartime sexual violence that are often overlooked in broader, more generalized studies [20].

By integrating Ethiopia-specific cultural and contextual frameworks, the research aims to develop culturally sensitive and tailored responses that enhance interventions and long-term recovery efforts [21, 22]. The study addresses the gaps above by examining coping strategies among survivors of sexual violence in conflict settings, emphasizing resilience and recovery, focusing on women in the North Wollo Zone in the Amhara region. Specific objectives include understanding the unique experiences of these women and identifying their coping mechanisms. Ultimately, the study sheds light on the hidden trauma of survivors and offers recommendations for improving support systems and policies for those affected by sexual violence in conflict.

Methods

Study area

This study examines North Wollo Zone in Ethiopia’s Amhara Region, a conflict-affected area with a pre-war population of over 1.5 million, predominantly rural with urban centres like Woldiya [23]. Located about 521 km from Addis Ababa, the zone is one of ten in the region and comprises 14 woredas (districts) and 313 kebeles (wards). The conflict, initially confined to the Tigray Region, spread into North Wollo bordering Tigray where Tigray People’s Liberation Front (TPLF) forces controlled parts of the zone from late June 2021 to early January 2022 [24]. Widespread internal displacement occurred, with many households losing homes, crops, and income sources [25]. The conflict caused extensive damage to infrastructure; numerous schools were looted or used as shelters, disrupting access. Health services were similarly affected—facilities were destroyed or looted, leading to acute shortages of medicine, personnel, and supplies [24]. Critical services, such as maternal and child health, were severely disrupted [26].

Study design

A qualitative approach with a phenomenological study design was used to explore the lived experiences of rape victims in the Amhara region during the ongoing war, focusing on their personal perceptions and emotional responses to the trauma. A qualitative approach was chosen to allow in-depth exploration of sensitive, complex human experiences that cannot be quantified. Specifically, a phenomenological design was used to capture the lived experiences of rape victims during the conflict in the Amhara region. This approach centres on understanding participants’ personal perceptions, meanings, and emotional responses to trauma, providing rich, contextual insight into how survivors make sense of their experiences insight that structured methods would fail to capture [27]. Semi-structured in-depth interviews and focus group discussions (FGDs) data collection designs were used to collect rich data regarding the topic.

Study participants and inclusion criteria

Survivors of war-related sexual violence were recruited for this study. Socially isolated female survivors of wartime sexual violence aged 18 years and above were purposively selected for inclusion in this study. Women who had already successfully reintegrated and rehabilitated into society were excluded, as the study sought to focus on those who were currently facing social isolation and struggling to return to normalcy, to better understand the barriers and coping strategies in real time.

Sample size and sampling techniques

Eligible participants were recruited using purposive sampling, supplemented by snowball sampling, to reach a highly sensitive and hard-to-access group: socially isolated survivors of wartime sexual violence. Initial participants were identified through trusted gatekeepers, including local administrative bodies, women’s affairs offices, health facilities, and community organizations with knowledge of affected individuals. These intermediaries facilitated ethically responsible access and ensured the relevance of participant selection. Subsequent participants were referred confidentially by those already enrolled, helping to overcome barriers of stigma and mistrust. In total, 25 survivors who met the inclusion criteria and continued to experience social isolation were selected for their rich, firsthand insights into war-related sexual violence. The researcher’s prioritized trust-building through partnerships with community leaders and support organizations, while upholding strict ethical standards, including informed consent, confidentiality, and cultural sensitivity. A range of recruitment strategies was employed to ensure both depth and credibility, ultimately fostering a comprehensive and respectful understanding of the complex realities faced by this vulnerable population.

Data collection instruments

The data was collected using semi-structured in-depth interviews and FGDs. In-depth interviews and FGDs were chosen to capture both individual experiences and collective perspectives related to wartime sexual violence. In-depth interviews allowed participants to share personal and emotionally sensitive narratives in a private, safe setting, facilitating deep exploration of their lived experiences. Meanwhile, FGDs enabled discussion of shared social dynamics, such as stigma, community response, and coping strategies, which are better understood through group interaction. Together, these methods provided a richer, more nuanced understanding of the phenomenon than either approach alone. The study utilized interviews and FGD guides prepared using previously published literature [9]. The in-depth interview guide was used to explore survivors’ experiences during and after wartime sexual violence, while FGDs were used to generate evidence regarding their coping mechanisms. The in-depth interview guide consisted of questions about participants’ lived experiences, emotions, beliefs, and interpretations of the event and its context. The in-depth interview guide included:

“What happened when TPLF soldiers took control of North Wollo Zone?”“What emotions and thoughts did you experience after the sexual assault?”“Have you shared your experience with anyone, such as friends or family?”“What physical and social changes have you noticed as a result of the experience?”

The FGD guide focused on exploring the ongoing coping mechanisms survivors were employing such as reliance on religious or cultural practices, informal family support, and attempts to access professional help to manage trauma and navigate daily life.

Data collection procedures

To ensure accurate data collection, the researchers obtained permission from relevant authorities and secured informed consent from participants. Participants were strategically selected using a specified methodology, and their preparation for the interview was ensured through a brief, guided orientation designed to focus their responses Ethical considerations, including informed consent, confidentiality, and participant support, were thoroughly addressed and detailed in the manuscript.

Twenty-five in-depth individual interviews, averaging 35 min, were conducted in participants’ homes to ensure comfort and privacy, with audio recordings used for accuracy. To address challenges related to interviewer gender and confidentiality, interviewers were trained on the interview guide, privacy maintenance, and safeguarding participant confidentiality.

Four focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted, three consisted of six participants, and one consisted of seven participants. An experienced FGD facilitator guided the discussions, which were audio-recorded and lasted 55 min on average per session.

Data validity and verification management

To ensure the study’s trustworthiness, multiple strategies were implemented. Credibility was achieved through member checking, peer debriefing, and prolonged engagement to verify findings, critically assess interpretations, and build participant rapport. Transferability was supported by providing detailed contextual descriptions and purposive sampling to capture relevant experiences. Dependability was ensured through an audit trail documenting research decisions and a code-recode strategy for consistent data interpretation. Confirmability was strengthened via triangulation (multiple data sources and methods), reflexivity (researcher bias tracking through reflective journaling), and member validation (participant feedback on findings).

Data were securely managed with anonymized transcripts, encrypted storage, and integrity checks, ensuring accuracy and confidentiality. These measures enhanced the study’s rigor, reliability, and ethical integrity.

Data analysis

The study employed verbatim, audio-recorded interviews conducted in Amharic. These were first transcribed in Amharic and then translated into English by a bilingual expert fluent in both languages and familiar with qualitative research. To ensure accuracy and validity, a second bilingual reviewer cross-checked a sample of the transcripts against the original audio recordings and translations. Discrepancies were discussed and resolved collaboratively. Reflexive thematic analysis was guided by Braun and Clarke’s (2006) framework, including familiarizing the data, generating initial codes, identifying themes, and reviewing them. An inductive, collaborative approach supported reliability through constant comparison and iterative refinement of themes. Participant quotes were used to ground findings on their lived experiences.

The majority of participants were women aged 20–30 years, indicating that many of the women who experienced rape were in their productive years. As shown in Table 1, a significant portion (76%) of participants did not attend primary school, while 24% attended elementary school. Regarding their place of residence, 52% of participants were from rural areas, 28% were from urban areas, and 20% were from semi-urban areas. Many survivors reported being assaulted by multiple perpetrators. The study found varying numbers of perpetrators involved in the assaults: twelve women were raped by two perpetrators simultaneously, four by three perpetrators in an orderly and simultaneous manner, three by four perpetrators, and three by five perpetrators, with a similar number assaulted by six perpetrators at the same time.

Theme one: social isolation

During the interviews, participants expressed feelings of isolation, citing the stigma associated with their experiences of violence as a significant factor. When the community learned of these women’s rape experiences, the women were stigmatized and felt ashamed. Being known in the community as a rape survivor is a big deal. One participant reflects her feelings as follows:I was disrespected and isolated, and people compared me to prostitutes because I accepted dating any man who wanted to have sex with me during the war. (P01, 25 years old)

I was disrespected and isolated, and people compared me to prostitutes because I accepted dating any man who wanted to have sex with me during the war. (P01, 25 years old)

The participant reflection underscores the profound impact of societal stigma on survivors, revealing how disrespect and isolation lead to harmful comparisons. This highlights the urgent need for empathy and support to dismantle stigma and promote healing within the community.

Another participant also expressed her feelings as follows: During the war, women experienced various psychological and social challenges; sometimes, they found it difficult to accept themselves as human beings.After I was raped by soldiers, people ignored me as if I were a sinful person, and even if they didn’t my word as usual because of this, my eyes are tearful all the time. My relative, thank you for considering me as a human. (P02, 30 years old).

Theme two: loss of identity

In Ethiopian society, prevailing definitions of girlhood and womanhood significantly shaped participants’ sense of identity, particularly concerning virginity. Many girls regarded virginity as a core component of their identity and a defining marker of being a “girl.” As such, it served as a social and personal boundary between girlhood and womanhood. For those who lost their virginity through rape, this boundary was abruptly and traumatically disrupted. These girls often felt excluded from both the category of “girls,” due to the loss of virginity, and from the category of “women,” as they had not transitioned voluntarily or in a socially recognized manner. This in-between status contributed to a profound sense of identity loss and social dislocation.

During the interviews, participants conveyed a profound sense of losing their identity. One participant articulated this sentiment powerfully: One girl who was raped by the TPLF narrated her wartime experiences as follows:When I was raped by members of the TPLF group, I lost my virginity. At that time, two men assaulted me simultaneously. I felt as though I had lost myself and was on the verge of death. Imagine being forced to have sex with two different men my genitals were bleeding continuously, and no one was willing to take me to the hospital. During that period, I kept asking myself, who will ever marry me? Should I now be considered a woman or still a girl? It was an incredibly difficult and painful time in my life. Even so, I believe I am alive today only by the grace of God! (P03, 27 years old)

According to the findings of the study, almost all rape survivors have lost their self-esteem. In their community, they felt inferior and worthless. Survivors do not place value on themselves and do not believe they are worthy. One of the study participants narrated her idea as follows:I try to pretend that nothing happened to me, but sometimes I get overwhelmed. I thought I wasn’t a human because I was raped by Junta? In my life, I have been humiliated. When I think about it, I feel inferior and worthless. (P11, 28 years old)

The coping mechanisms used by the study participants were distilled into four key themes, each analyzed individually. These themes encompass faith in God, navigating emotional disclosure in intimate relationships, professional help, community support, and social activities.

Discussion

The study explored the lived experiences of socially isolated women who were victims of wartime sexual assault in the North Wollo Zone, Amhara, Ethiopia. It focused on understanding the nature of the sexual violence and the coping mechanisms employed by these women. Using a phenomenological approach, the research delved into the experiences of women affected by war rape and sexual violence in the Amhara Regional State. The discussion is framed around two main research questions, which align with the study objectives mentioned earlier:

(1) what are the lived experiences of socially isolated women who have suffered wartime sexual assault in the North Wollo Zone? (2) What coping mechanisms do these women use in response to their trauma?

Violence has a devastating impact on women’s physical and mental health, as well as on families. Many victims, such as those subjected to rape, endure both physical and emotional trauma. Previous studies also support the current study; sexual violence in war has profound consequences, particularly for women and girls, who face the highest risk. sexual violence is a common problem globally, not just in war-torn areas, because the United Nations found that roughly 18.3% of all women have experienced sexual assault [5, 6]. For mothers, the pain is compounded, as they not only suffer personally but also witness the abuse of their children [28].

The ongoing conflict in Ethiopia has led to the disintegration of countless families, resulting in severe emotional and economic crises. Children, who were dependent on their mothers for care, were deprived of it as military forces targeted women, subjecting them to abduction, sexual violence, and gang rape. Victims of this violence included both housewives and students. The war’s broader goal, especially from the perspective of the Tigrayan forces, appeared to be the disbanding of family structures, which are central to societal cohesion.

The impact on children is particularly profound, as mothers, who often struggle to regain their emotional stability, face the compounded trauma of loss, a lack of justice, and the absence of social healing or compensation.

Lived experiences of socially isolated women survivors of wartime sexual assault in the North Wollo Zone

The study revealed that women in the North Wollo Zone, particularly those targeted by the TPLF rebels, experienced extreme brutality, including physical violence, intimidation, and sexual assault. The victims were subjected to both physical and psychological harm, with many survivors reporting feelings of helplessness and isolation.

These findings align with the study conducted by Tirualem and Asmama (2022), which also highlighted that the primary motives behind these acts of sexual violence were to humiliate, seek revenge, and instill fear in the Amhara population. The use of rape as a weapon in this conflict was not only intended to harm individuals but also to break the spirit of the community, further deepening the social and emotional scars left by the violence. Similarly, another study revealed that the stigma attached to being a victim of wartime sexual violence further isolates survivors, preventing them from accessing support and hindering their reintegration into society [9].

Coping mechanisms used by socially isolated wartime sexual assault women in the North Wollo zone

The women in the study all tried to avoid being raped by telling the attackers about their serious health problems or their recent childbirth status. However, the attackers did not listen to their requests, and none of the women were able to escape the sexual violence. Gang rapes were intentional, to “cleanse” Amharan ethnicity and damage reproductive organs, ultimately preventing women from reproducing. Only one woman reported reduced rape due to looting.

The main coping mechanisms identified in the study included turning to faith, seeking professional help, and discussing their experiences with partners (both male and female) to address ongoing psychological distress and minimize the recurrence of painful issues. Additionally, the study highlighted the importance of community support, particularly through the traditional practice of the coffee ceremony. This gathering allowed participants to engage in meaningful conversation and emotional support, especially among women. According to the survivors, common coping strategies included praying, attending church, playing with children, and walking with elderly women, all of which helped reduce stress, anxiety, and depression. These findings align with other studies on sexual assault victims. For example, research from Malaysia found that victims of sexual violence often relied on spiritual coping mechanisms such as praying, reading the Quran, and meditation to manage their emotional trauma [29]. Rape victims spent far too much time doing things like watching movies, doing housework, reading books, and working just to forget [30]. The gang troops in Amhara targeted women with pretexts to sexually abuse them, often in the presence of family members or barracks. This cruelty suggests the execution of orders from higher officials as part of the war strategy. The war aimed to destroy the family, which is the nucleus of society. Gang rape was also intended to sterilize the womb.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. The study focused specifically on the lived experiences of socially isolated women who were survivors of sexual violence and reintegrated into low-income farming communities in the North Wollo Zone. As such, the experiences of survivors in other regions or countries, with different socio-economic conditions and cultural contexts, may differ significantly. Moreover, data was collected through interviews, focus group discussions, and document analysis, which, while insightful, may not be sufficient to generalize the findings to more diverse or heterogeneous populations.

Implication

Despite the study’s limitations, it offers important implications, highlighting the profound and multifaceted impact of war, which not only harms the productivity of the country but also causes lasting humiliation and suffering for survivors, particularly raped women. The findings emphasize the need for international, national, and local non-governmental organizations (NGOs) and other relevant stakeholders to recognize and address the long-term effects of war, even after survivors’ reintegration. Additionally, the study underscores the value of an integrated resilience approach that incorporates social-ecological frameworks, along with perspectives from mental health and developmental psychology. This approach advocates for fostering adaptive relationships within social-ecological systems and identifying community strengths, which are crucial for enhancing the psychological well-being and internal resilience of women who have survived war-related sexual violence.

Conclusions

This study highlights the severe social, psychological, and health challenges faced by women and girls in North Wollo Zone due to wartime sexual violence, including isolation, degradation, and mistreatment. Survivors cope through counseling, faith, friendships, and support from spouses. Their experiences are deeply traumatic, with significant physical and mental health impacts. To support survivors, strengthening trauma-informed local mental health and healthcare services is essential. Community-based approaches, like support groups and culturally relevant practices (e.g., coffee ceremonies), can foster healing. Increased international and governmental support, humanitarian aid, legal justice mechanisms, stigma-reduction campaigns, and economic empowerment programs are critical. Religious and spiritual support, alongside ongoing evaluation, will promote resilience and recovery.

Future research is crucial to formally measure the effectiveness of culturally relevant interventions (e.g., coffee ceremonies) against standard care and requires longitudinal studies to determine the optimal, integrated services (mental health, economic, spiritual support) that promote lasting survivor recovery.
