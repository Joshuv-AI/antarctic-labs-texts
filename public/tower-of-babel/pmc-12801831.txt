How are cognitive functions affected by different sedation methods in geriatric endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography patients?
Authors: Filiz Kaya, Esra Özayar, Ayça Tuba Dumanlı Özcan, Mehmet Uçar, Levent Öztürk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVarious sedation methods are used during endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) procedures, which are increasingly performed in elderly patients. Our study aimed to compare the effects of propofol anesthesia with a propofol-dexmedetomidine combination on cognitive function in elderly patients undergoing ERCP.MethodsThis prospective observational study included 184 inpatients aged 65 years and older who received either propofol or a propofol-dexmedetomidine combination for sedation during ERCP. Cognitive function was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) at three time points: before the procedure and at 2 and 24 h after the procedure. The degree of frailty in patients was assessed via a frailty questionnaire. In the propofol (P) group, the propofol loading dose was 0.2–0.5 mg/kg, with a maintenance infusion dose of 0.5-4 mg/kg/h. In the propofol + dexmedetomidine (P + D) group, in addition to propofol infusion at the same doses, a loading dose of 0.5 µg/kg dexmedetomidine was administered over 10 min, followed by an infusion at a dose of 0.2–0.7 µg/kg/h.ResultsThere was no statistically significant difference between the P and P + D groups in terms of the MMSE score before the procedure (p > 0.05). However, in the P + D group, a significant improvement in the MMSE score was observed at both 2 h (z = 2.943, p = 0.003) and 24 h (z = 2.816, p = 0.005) after the procedure. The increase in MMSE scores was significantly greater in the P + D group, particularly among pre-frail patients.ConclusionOur study demonstrated that dexmedetomidine yielded more favorable results in terms of cognitive function than propofol following ERCP in geriatric patients.Clinical trials registrationNCT06719960. Retrospectively registered.

Introduction

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is a complex and often painful procedure used for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. Compared to routine gastrointestinal endoscopy, it requires deeper sedation and reduced body movement. However, deep sedation is frequently associated with increased cardiopulmonary complications, including respiratory depression, hypoxemia, and hypotension, which can exacerbate perioperative issues, especially in elderly patients [1–3]. In this regard, dexmedetomidine offers an advantage by providing sedation without causing respiratory depression [4].

While the primary goal of sedation during ERCP is to facilitate the procedure, it can also lead to cognitive impairment, which may delay patient discharge [5]. Cognitive dysfunction affects 15–25% of surgical patients, with elderly individuals being particularly vulnerable. Common impairments include difficulties with recent memory, concentration, language comprehension, and social integration [6]. Therefore, rapid recovery and preservation of cognitive function after endoscopic procedures are crucial for elderly patients to return to their normal activities safely.

Frailty, a condition often observed in the geriatric population, refers to weakness, debility, and a decline in functional reserves. Numerous studies have linked frailty to increased postoperative mortality, a higher incidence of complications, cognitive dysfunction, and delirium [3, 7, 8]. Given the increasing prevalence of ERCP procedures in elderly patients, the effects of different sedation methods on cognitive function remain unclear. Aging increases the risks and complications associated with anesthesia, and the drugs used for sedation, along with the degree of frailty, can influence cognitive outcomes following the procedure. Thus, sedation management in elderly patients undergoing ERCP requires careful consideration and tailored approaches [9–13].

Our study aimed to evaluate the impact of different sedation methods commonly used in geriatric ERCP patients on cognitive function.

Methods

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Ankara Bilkent City Hospital on July 19, 2023, with registration number E2-23-4565. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (ID number: NCT06719960). Initially, a total of 199 inpatients aged 65 years and older who met the study criteria were assessed for eligibility. Patients were scheduled to receive either propofol or propofol + dexmedetomidine for sedation during ERCP and were included in the study. Patients with second- or third-degree atrioventricular (AV) block, a recent history of stroke, severe hypotension, cardiorespiratory instability, substance abuse, psychotic illness, or severe dementia were excluded. Fifteen patients were subsequently excluded: eight patients due to severe dementia, two who did not want to continue, and five others who were excluded for reasons such as early discharge. Thus, a final total of 184 patients were included in the study (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram

Patients were brought to the preparation room in the endoscopy unit 45 min prior to the start of the ERCP procedure. Demographic and clinical data, including age, education level (categorized as high school and above in one group and below high school in another group), Body Mass Index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) risk status, and comorbidities, were recorded. To assess baseline cognitive function, the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) was administered by an investigator who was blinded to the sedation agent used. The degree of frailty in each patient was assessed using the Frail Frailty Questionnaire (O Normal, 1–2 Pre-frail, 3–5 Frail). The MMSE was repeated at 2 h and 24 h after the ERCP procedure. Following anesthesia, patients were monitored using the Modified Aldrete Scoring System in the recovery room. Once the Aldrete Score reached 10 points, the 2-hour evaluation was performed.

At our clinic, ERCP procedures are performed in two separate rooms by two specialist gastrointestinal endoscopists and two specialist anesthesiologists. Sedation was administered at the discretion of the attending anesthesiologist, who was independent of the study. Upon entry, all patients were started on 4 L/min of oxygen via nasal cannula. Continuous monitoring of heart rate (HR), systolic arterial blood pressure (SAP), diastolic arterial blood pressure (DAP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), and arterial oxygen saturation (SpO2) was performed. Baseline hemodynamic measurements were recorded, followed by subsequent measurements at 5, 10, and 15 min. Sedation was initiated in both groups with patients in the lateral decubitus position. Both groups received 0.5 µg/kg fentanyl intravenously (IV). In the propofol group (P), the loading dose of propofol was 0.2–0.5 mg/kg, followed by a maintenance infusion of 0.5–4 mg/kg/h. In the propofol + dexmedetomidine group (P + D), 0.5 µg/kg of dexmedetomidine was administered as a loading dose over 10 min, followed by continuous infusion at a dose of 0.2–0.7 µg/kg/h. The propofol loading and maintenance doses were identical to those used in the propofol group. The Ramsey sedation score was maintained at 3–4 for effective sedation. The duration of the ERCP procedure, total doses of propofol and dexmedetomidine used, and the administration of additional atropine and ephedrine were recorded. Sedation agents were discontinued upon completion of the ERCP procedure.

Patients were excluded from the study if they developed complications such as bleeding, perforation, or respiratory or cardiac arrest related to ERCP or anesthesia.

Statistical analysis

The conformity of the continuous variables to a normal distribution was evaluated graphically and using the Shapiro‒Wilk test. The mean ± standard deviation (SD) and median (minimum‒maximum) values were used to calculate descriptive statistics.

For the comparison of MMSE scores between the propofol (P) and propofol + dexmedetomidine (P + D) groups, an independent samples t-test was used for normally distributed data, and the Mann‒Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed data.

To evaluate changes in parameters over time (before the procedure, 2 h after the procedure, and 24 h after the procedure), repeated-measures ANOVA was used for normally distributed data, and the Friedman test was used for non-normally distributed data. Bonferroni correction was applied for pairwise comparisons, and the results are reported accordingly.

For the comparison of categorical variables between the P and P + D groups, cross-tabulations were performed, and the number (n), percentage (%), and chi-square (χ²) test statistics were calculated.

Spearman’s nonparametric correlation coefficient was used in the correlation analysis between the FRAIL score and MMSE scores. The Kruskal Wallis Test was used to compare the parameters according to the FRAIL classification. Analysis results were presented using Bonferroni correction in intergroup pairwise comparisons. IBM SPSS Statistics 21.0 (IBM Corp. Released 2012. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 21.0. The Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.) and MS-Excel 2007 software were used. The statistical significance level was set at p < 0.05.

The sample size of the study was calculated using the G*Power 3.1.9 (G*Power, Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) software [14]. The effect size was calculated as 0.445, referencing the study by Geng et al. [6]. In this study, type I error was set at α = 0.05, the targeted power of the test was 1–β = 0.80, and the minimum sample size required for statistical analyses was determined to be 81 for one group. To account for potential missing or erroneous data, the study was designed to enroll 180 participants (90 per group).

Findings

Similarly, there was no significant difference in the MMSE score before the procedure (p > 0.05). However, a significant difference was observed between the P and P + D groups at 2 h and 24 h post procedure, with the P + D group showing higher MMSE scores at both time points (z = 2.446, p = 0.014 and z = 2.280, p = 0.023). Twenty-four hours after the procedure, the mean MMSE score in the P + D group was greater than that in the P group. A statistically significant difference was also found in the MMSE scores at the three time points (before the procedure, 2 h, and 24 h) in both groups (χ2 = 17.069; p < 0.001 and χ2 = 61.218; p < 0.001). The MMSE scores of individuals in the P + D group increased over time. (Table 2).

With respect to the hemodynamic parameters, when the systolic arterial pressure (SAP), diastolic arterial pressure (DAP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), and SpO2 were analyzed at baseline and at 5, 10, and 15 min between the propofol and P + D groups, a statistically significant difference was found only for the 15-minute DAP measurement (z = 2.062, p = 0.039).

Additionally, significant differences in heart rate (HR) were detected between the two groups at 5, 10, and 15 min (p < 0.001). The mean HR values in the propofol group were greater than those in the P + D group. A statistically significant difference was found between the four time-dependent measurements of MAP (baseline, 5 min, 10 min, 15 min) in the P + D group (χ2 = 56.256; p < 0.001) (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Distribution of HR and MAP values in the Propofol and Propofol + Dexmedetomidine Groups

There were no significant differences in the administration of atropine, ephedrine, or additional fentanyl between the two groups (p > 0.05). Discussion

In recent years, interest in studies on postoperative cognitive disorders has increased. Neurocognitive impairments decrease the quality of life of patients, prolong hospital stays, and increase morbidity and mortality, especially in elderly patients [15].

Adequate sedation for interventional ERCP directly affects the duration and success of the procedure. Propofol, which is commonly used for ERCP sedation, is combined with other drugs because of its respiratory depression and hypotensive effects. In the study by Koruk et al., a dexmedetomidine-propofol combination resulted in shorter recovery times, with sedative and side effects comparable to those of a midazolam-propofol combination. The authors concluded that the dexmedetomidine‒propofol combination is a safe and effective alternative for sedation during ERCP [4]. Additionally, dexmedetomidine has been shown to reduce the required dose of propofol during anesthesia because of its analgesic effects [16]. In our study, we found that the total dose of propofol was lower in patients who received dexmedetomidine.

Dexmedetomidine acts through the locus coeruleus in the pons. Unlike other sedating drugs, it activates the α2 receptor in noradrenaline neurons instead of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptors and does not cause respiratory depression [17]. This unique mechanism makes dexmedetomidine particularly favorable for elderly patients, who are often at increased risk of respiratory complications. Additionally, the lateral decubitus position commonly used during ERCP procedures, along with shared airway management with gastroenterologists, can complicate the maintenance of respiratory function.

In our clinic, we wanted to evaluate the cognitive functions of patients sedated with two different combinations of propofol, fentanyl, and dexmedetomidine, which are commonly used in ERCP procedures, using the MMSE. The age, sex, education level, ASA status, comorbidities, and frailty classification of the patients in both groups were similar. This homogeneity between the groups ensures a more reliable comparison and strengthens the validity of our MMSE results. A previous study revealed that increasing age and duration of anesthesia, inadequate education, a second procedure, postoperative infections, and respiratory complications were risk factors for early postoperative cognitive dysfunction. Notably, age alone was found to be a significant risk factor for late postoperative cognitive dysfunction [18].

The MMSE is an 11-item scale that tests five domains of cognitive function: orientation (10 points), working memory (3 points), attention and calculation (5 points), recall (3 points), and language (9 points), with a maximum score of 30 points. Hülya et al. administered oral premedication with 0.15 mg/kg midazolam or 1 mg/kg tramadol to 40 patients (18–70 years; ASA I-III) undergoing elective ERCP. Cognitive function was evaluated with the MMSE. They reported a decrease in the total MMSE score and all subcategories in the midazolam group at 60 min after ERCP compared with baseline [11]. In another study involving 100 colonoscopy patients, cognitive function was evaluated with the MMSE, and the combination of propofol/remifentanil was found to be superior to the combination of meperidine/midazolam in preserving cognitive function during sedation [19].

In our study, there was no significant difference between the MMSE scores of the P and P + D groups before the procedure, whereas the MMSE scores of both groups increased after sedation. At 2 and 24 h post procedure, the increase in MMSE score was significantly greater in the P + D group, with the most notable improvement observed at 2 h post procedure. These findings suggest that dexmedetomidine may play a role in better preserving cognitive function in patients undergoing ERCP.

Hemodynamic balance is also very important in the preservation of cognitive functions. In the study by Wu et al., the MAP in the propofol group decreased during the procedure compared with the baseline level. In the dexmedetomidine group, the HR decreased after the loading dose. Although there were similar results in our study, the decrease in MAP was more pronounced in the P + D group, but this was not sufficient to disrupt hemodynamics [20].

One study also explored the effects of dexmedetomidine versus midazolam on cognitive and memory function during coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery. The study included 42 participants aged 40–65 years who were evaluated with both the MMSE and the Wechsler Memory Scale (WMS) the day before surgery. The results indicated that cognitive impairment in the dexmedetomidine group was significantly lower than that in the midazolam group on both the 5th and 30th postoperative days [21].

A study investigating how dexmedetomidine protects the brain after major surgery in middle-aged patients reported that postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) may contribute to the hippocampal inflammatory response and neuronal apoptosis and that selective alpha 2 adrenal receptor stimulation may play a protective role [22]. Similarly, Lei et al. demonstrated that dexmedetomidine has significant neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects and potentially prevents postoperative cognitive dysfunction in elderly surgical patients [23].

Mei et al. conducted a prospective, randomized, controlled study in 366 patients aged 65 years and older. They administered propofol or dexmedetomidine for sedation to patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty under spinal anesthesia. In this study, the incidence of POCD was evaluated using the MMSE. POCD was more common in the propofol group, and MMSE scores decreased more on the third and seventh days than in the dexmedetomidine group. In the dexmedetomidine group, the difference between the preoperative and postoperative MMSE scores was found to be small [24]. Unlike our study, although Mei et al. included a patient population with higher baseline MMSE scores (those with MMSE scores lower than 24 were excluded from the study), their postoperative MMSE scores were lower. In our study, cognitive evaluations were improved after sedation in both groups. The differences between our study and that of Mei et al. may be attributed to the larger scale of surgery and the fact that cognitive evaluations were conducted at different postoperative intervals, with their assessments occurring on the third and seventh days. Despite these differences, both studies concluded that dexmedetomidine offers better postoperative neurocognitive protection in elderly patients.

Additionally, in their recently completed study, Zangnan et al. examined the effect of low-dose dexmedetomidine on perioperative neurocognitive dysfunction in elderly ERCP patients. They reported a slight increase in the MMSE score on the 1st, third and fifth postoperative days, which is consistent with our findings [25].

Dexmedetomidine infusion alone is not as effective as propofol in terms of sedation quality and is associated with significantly lower blood pressure and heart rate and prolonged recovery time in ERCP procedures [12, 13, 16–18]. Therefore, it is generally used in combination in our clinic. Similar findings have been reported in other studies. For example, Mo et al. studied elderly ERCP patients and reported that a single dose of dexmedetomidine combined with propofol for deep sedation could reduce the loading dose, propofol consumption, and artificial airway intervention and provide better hemodynamic stability than propofol [2].

Different tests can also be used to assess the postoperative consciousness of patients. In a study by Sanath et al., 169 outpatients who received sedation with propofol for gastrointestinal endoscopy were evaluated for cognitive recovery using EncephalApp. The study revealed that cognitive flexibility returned to baseline 30–45 min after propofol sedation for outpatient endoscopy, despite the delayed return of psychomotor speed and reaction time. If enough time is given to recover after endoscopy, discharging patients without the need for an accompanying family member requires further study [13]. However, as in our study group, more detailed cognitive function testing is needed in procedures requiring longer and deeper sedation, such as ERCP, particularly in elderly patients.

As frailty increases in patients, their cognitive function is expected to deteriorate [7]. In our study, the MMSE scores of frail patients before the procedure were lower in both groups. Post-procedure MMSE scores increased in both normal and pre-frail/frail individuals, with the increase being statistically greater in the dexmedetomidine group, especially among pre-frail patients. However, the small number of frail patients in both groups may have limited our ability to detect significant differences in the postoperative cognitive function of frail individuals.

Furthermore, the repeated administration of MMSE may have resulted in a learning effect, contributing to the observed increase in MMSE scores [26]. The reduction of stress following the procedure may have had a positive effect on cognitive performance. However, this effect was consistent across both groups.

There are also limitations in our study. Owing to the operational schedule of the ERCP unit, we were only able to assess patients in the preoperative waiting room approximately one hour before the procedure. The high number of patients per day in the ERCP unit limited our ability to conduct more comprehensive cognitive assessments. Therefore, we believe that incorporating additional cognitive screening tests could have provided clearer and more robust results.

Another limitation was the lack of advanced monitoring. Our study was observational, and the cases involved relatively minor and short-duration interventional procedures. Since the study was observational, drug doses were given within predetermined ranges. Given that patients were discharged within a short timeframe, we were able to perform only short-term postoperative consciousness assessment.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that dexmedetomidine yielded more favorable results in preserving cognitive function than propofol in geriatric patients after an ERCP procedure. These findings contribute to the growing body of literature supporting the use of dexmedetomidine as a viable sedation option for elderly patients. We believe that using a propofol–dexmedetomidine combination may be a good choice in terms of cognitive outcomes in elderly ERCP patients.
