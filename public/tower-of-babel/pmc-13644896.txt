Respiratory Care Trajectory in Patients With Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy in the Advanced Stage: A Retrospective Single‐Center Study
Authors: Keisuke Yorimoto, Yosuke Ariake, Yuta Miyazaki, Hotake Takizawa, Yuko Shimizu‐Motohashi, Hirofumi Komaki, Yuji Takahashi, Takatoshi Hara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsData on respiratory status and care in older adults with Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) remain limited. This study aimed to characterize respiratory status, respiratory physiotherapy, and associated clinical features in patients with DMD aged ≥ 30 years.MethodsThis single‐center retrospective study analyzed medical records of 44 individuals with DMD aged ≥ 30 years who received physiotherapy at our center between March 2017 and March 2023. Data included respiratory function (vital capacity [VC], cough peak flow [CPF], and lung volume recruitment [LVR]‐assisted measurements), respiratory complications, and clinical characteristics.ResultsThe median age was 37 years. Respiratory function was severely impaired, with a median VC of 275 mL and CPF of 0 L/min. LVR enabled assessment of assisted lung inflation, achieving a median insufflation volume of 1040 mL and increasing CPF to 150 L/min. Non‐invasive ventilation (NIV) and mechanical insufflation–exsufflation (MI‐E) were each used by 95.5% of patients, and 79.5% required continuous ventilatory support. A history of pneumonia was documented in 70.5% (31 patients), and a history of pneumothorax in 29.5% (13 patients). The median ejection fraction was 42%, and 18.2% of patients had died.DiscussionLVR enabled evaluation of lung insufflation capacity (LIC) even in patients with advanced‐stage DMD. Although causal inference cannot be made, these findings suggest that LVR may serve as a component of respiratory physiotherapy for assessment in adults with DMD.

Introduction

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is associated with progressive respiratory impairment, and advances in multidisciplinary care—including corticosteroid therapy, cardioprotective management, and respiratory support—have improved survival [1, 2, 3]. In particular, non‐invasive ventilation (NIV), mechanical insufflation–exsufflation (MI‐E), and lung volume recruitment (LVR) play central roles in respiratory management and are widely used in the care of neuromuscular disorders [4]. Epidemiological studies have shown that survival has markedly improved in the modern therapeutic era, with increasing numbers of patients surviving into adulthood [5].

With increased longevity, however, older adults with DMD present new and complex clinical challenges. Patients aged ≥ 30 years frequently exhibit advanced musculoskeletal deformities and respiratory complications that may limit the effectiveness of airway clearance techniques and ventilatory support. Despite these changes, the respiratory status and clinical management of this population remain insufficiently characterized.

Therefore, this study aimed to retrospectively characterize respiratory status, respiratory physiotherapy, and associated clinical features in patients with DMD aged ≥ 30 years treated at our center.

Methods

Ethical approval: This study was approved by the ethics committee of our hospital (Approval No: 2022‐020).

Patient selection: This single‐center retrospective study included all eligible patients with DMD aged ≥ 30 years who received physiotherapy at our center between March 2017 and March 2023. Patients were identified from physiotherapy prescriptions issued by the Department of Physical Rehabilitation. Duplicate records corresponding to the same individual were removed.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) a clinical diagnosis of DMD documented in the electronic medical record, (2) genetic findings consistent with DMD documented in the medical record, and (3) age ≥ 30 years at the time of final evaluation. Because this was a retrospective chart review, the level of detail available for mutation classification was heterogeneous, and some cases could not be fully categorized. Exclusion criteria included female DMD carriers and patients with other neuromuscular disorders, including Becker muscular dystrophy.

Data collection: Medical records from patients meeting inclusion and exclusion criteria were retrospectively analyzed. When multiple evaluations were available for the same patient, data from the final physiotherapy session were used.

Data collected included respiratory function, respiratory complications, and clinical characteristics. Respiratory function variables included vital capacity (VC), cough peak flow (CPF), LVR‐assisted insufflation volume, and LVR‐assisted CPF. Measurements of VC, CPF, and LVR‐assisted parameters were obtained on the same day at the final evaluation. LVR‐related values represent the immediate physiological response rather than cumulative effects. Lung volume recruitment (LVR), commonly known as “breath stacking,” is a stacked‐breath assisted inflation technique. The maximum volume achieved by assisted inflation is termed maximum insufflation capacity (MIC) when glottic control is required to retain successive insufflations, or lung insufflation capacity (LIC) when glottic control is not required. MIC was measured as the standard LVR‐assisted volume. In patients unable to perform breath stacking, LIC was measured using the LIC TRAINER (Carter Technologies Co. Ltd., Kawaguchi, Saitama, Japan), which can be directly connected to a spirometer or peak flow meter [6]. Three to five consecutive manual insufflations were delivered using a bag‐valve mask to achieve maximal assisted lung inflation. Following maximal assisted lung inflation, patients were instructed to cough, and CPF was measured as LVR‐assisted CPF.

Respiratory complications included a documented history of pneumonia and pneumothorax, as well as age at first occurrence.

Clinical data included age at diagnosis, age at loss of ambulation, and the timing and implementation of medical interventions, including corticosteroid therapy, cardioprotective medications, respiratory physiotherapy, NIV, MI‐E, gastrostomy or tube feeding, and tracheostomy.

Physical status was assessed using body mass index (BMI), ejection fraction (EF), and the presence and Cobb angle of scoliosis at the final evaluation. Mortality and age at death were also recorded.

Statistical analysis: Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Paired comparisons between spontaneous and LVR‐assisted respiratory measurements were performed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Because longitudinal data under uniform conditions were not consistently available, analyses were limited to a single time point per patient.

Discussion

Respiratory Impairment and the Significance of Sustained Respiratory Care in Older Adults With DMD

In this study of individuals with DMD aged 30 years and older, conventional respiratory function measures such as VC and CPF were frequently severely reduced or no longer measurable, reflecting advanced respiratory impairment.

Progressive respiratory muscle weakness, scoliosis, and prolonged immobility can increase the mechanical load on the respiratory system and reduce chest wall compliance in neuromuscular disease [4, 7]. LVR is used to inflate the lungs to volumes greater than those achieved by spontaneous inspiration, and its role in maintaining lung and chest wall expansion has been discussed in DMD and other neuromuscular disorders [4, 8, 9]. In the present cohort, LVR‐assisted insufflation volume remained measurable and was significantly greater than VC despite severe impairment of spontaneous respiratory function. This finding does not demonstrate preserved chest wall or lung compliance; however, it indicates that externally assisted lung inflation remained feasible even at an advanced disease stage. For patients unable to perform breath stacking, LC measurement using the LIC TRAINER also enables assisted insufflation volume to be assessed without requiring glottic control [6]. These findings support the continued importance of respiratory physiotherapy aimed at assisted lung expansion and cough augmentation in advanced‐stage DMD.

Comparison With Current DMD Standard Treatment

This cohort consisted exclusively of individuals with DMD who survived beyond 30 years of age and should therefore be interpreted as a selected population subject to survivorship bias. Accordingly, prolonged survival in this group cannot be attributed to respiratory care alone and may also reflect underlying disease characteristics, including relatively slower disease progression, better‐preserved cardiac function, or other unmeasured factors.

Several major interventions, including corticosteroid therapy, cardioprotective medication, ventilatory support, and respiratory physiotherapy, were initiated later in this cohort than currently recommended in DMD care guidelines (Table 3) [2, 10, 11]. This discrepancy likely reflects the historical treatment era in which these patients were born and managed, before current standards of multidisciplinary care were fully established.

Given the limited sample size and the absence of systematic genotype–phenotype analysis, the relationship between genetic background and long‐term survival could not be evaluated in the present study.

Physical Characteristics of DMD Patients Over Age 30 and Implications for Respiratory Physiotherapy

Advances in ventilatory support and cardiopulmonary management have extended survival in DMD and have been accompanied by a shift in the principal causes of death from respiratory failure toward cardiac disease [5, 12]. However, this change in mortality pattern does not imply that pulmonary complications have become uncommon. In the present cohort, histories of pneumonia and pneumothorax were frequent, indicating a substantial burden of pulmonary complications among adults with advanced‐stage DMD. Because these data reflect documented clinical histories rather than events collected during a standardized observation period, the present study cannot estimate incidence or establish causal relationships. Nevertheless, the high prevalence of pulmonary complications emphasizes the need for continued surveillance and respiratory care even in an era of prolonged survival.

Long‐term NIV, including continuous non‐invasive ventilatory support, can be maintained in some patients with DMD and may avoid or delay tracheostomy [13]. In the present cohort, NIV was widely used, whereas tracheostomy with invasive ventilation was used in a smaller proportion of patients. Nevertheless, when NIV becomes ineffective, transition to invasive ventilation may be required [14]. The present retrospective study cannot determine whether the respiratory management strategy used at our center contributed to survival or delayed invasive ventilation. These findings instead describe a predominantly non‐invasive respiratory management pattern in adults with advanced‐stage DMD.

As respiratory support becomes increasingly complex in advanced DMD, clinical management must also address nutrition, swallowing, communication, environmental control, and participation in daily life. Adult DMD care guidelines emphasize coordinated multidisciplinary management across respiratory, cardiac, nutritional, rehabilitation, and psychosocial domains [8].

Limitations and Future Perspectives

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single‐center design and reliance on a single assessment point per patient limit generalizability and may have introduced reporting bias. Second, because the cohort consisted exclusively of individuals with DMD who survived beyond 30 years of age and continued to receive specialized care, survivorship bias must be considered, and patients with more aggressive disease courses or earlier mortality may be underrepresented. Third, no control group was included, and the study design does not allow causal inference regarding the effects of respiratory physiotherapy, LVR, or other interventions on survival or respiratory complications. In addition, systematic genotype–phenotype analysis was not performed. Finally, repeated respiratory assessments and LVR were performed as part of routine care, but longitudinal data under standardized conditions were not consistently available, and detailed information on actual adherence, frequency, and long‐term implementation of LVR was not systematically recorded. Therefore, the analysis was limited to a single time point per patient.

Future prospective studies with standardized repeated measurements, appropriate comparator groups, and broader inclusion across the DMD disease spectrum are needed to clarify age‐related trajectories of respiratory function and the role of respiratory physiotherapy in older adults with DMD. Further studies incorporating more detailed genetic characterization may also help clarify the contribution of genetic background to long‐term outcomes.

Conclusions

Severe respiratory impairment was evident in adults with DMD aged 30 years and older; however, LVR‐assisted insufflation volume and LVR‐assisted CPF remained measurable despite marked limitations in conventional respiratory measurements. Pulmonary complications remained common despite prolonged survival and widespread use of NIV, MI‐E, and respiratory physiotherapy. These findings support continued respiratory assessment and respiratory physiotherapy as components of longitudinal multidisciplinary rehabilitation in advanced‐stage DMD.
