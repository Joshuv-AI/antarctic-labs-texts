Feasibility and histological basis of predicting post-operative pancreatic fistula by pre-operative shear wave elastography: a prospective study
Authors: Jiayao Huang, Lili Chen, Qingyu Wu, Xiaoer Zhang, Lu Yao, Jiaming Lai, Xiaoyan Xie, Manxia Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to explore the feasibility and histological basis of pancreatic two-dimensional shear wave elastography (2D-SWE) measurement in predicting post-operative pancreatic fistula (POPF) following partial pancreatectomy and to build a prediction model by pre-operative features.Methods305 pancreatic or periampullary disease patients were enrolled, divided into a training (n = 203) and a validation group (n = 102). POPF was graded as A (or the so-called “biochemical leak”), B, and C. Spearman correlation analysis assessed the histological basis of 2D-SWE measurement value’s (Emean’s) relationship with POPF grades. Multivariate logistic regression analyzed risk factors of POPF occurrence in the training group, and a predictive model was built. The discrimination ability of the model to differentiate POPF grades was analyzed by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).ResultsEmean could discriminate between the occurrence and grades of POPF (all AUC > 0.75, p < 0.050). Pancreatic fibrosis and acinar atrophy correlated with both Emean and POPF (all p < 0.050). The higher BMI (p = 0.002), non-dilated pancreatic duct (p = 0.001), and lower Emean (p < 0.001) were the independent risk factors of POPF occurrence and could help discriminate the occurrence of different grades of POPF in both groups (all AUC > 0.80, p < 0.050).ConclusionsLower pancreatic 2D-SWE measurements indicated a higher risk of POPF, and the histological basis may lie in 2D-SWE’s correlation with pancreatic fibrosis and acinar atrophy. A model integrating pancreatic 2D-SWE measurements, BMI, and the presence of pancreatic duct dilation could predict POPF risk across varying severity levels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04518-2.Keywords: Post-operative pancreatic fistula, Two-dimensional shear wave elastography, Pancreas stiffness

Background

Pancreatic cancer poses a great threat to human health, ranking 12th in global incidence and 6th in mortality among malignancies and causing over 460,000 deaths annually [1]. Partial pancreatectomy is the primary curative treatment for focal pancreatic and periampullary lesions, particularly malignancies [2, 3]. However, this procedure is challenging, with complication rates reaching 58%–80% and peri-operative mortality rates up to 17% [4–6]. Post-operative pancreatic fistula (POPF) is one of the most common and critical complications following partial pancreatectomy, occurring in up to 45% [4, 7] and significantly increases the risk of other complications, including infections and hemorrhage, as well as mortality, which can double due to POPF [8–10]. Pre-operative prediction of POPF is of utmost interest, which can influence the choice of treatment and the evaluation of prognosis in patients with pancreatic and periampullary lesions.

Previous studies indicated that the risk factors of POPF included old age, male sex, obesity, small diameter of the pancreatic duct, and soft pancreas tissue [9, 11–15]. Particularly, pancreas stiffness was thought to be one of the key factors associated with POPF [16–18]. Currently, intra-operative texture, magnetic resonance elastography, endoscopic ultrasound elastography, and pancreatic parenchymal fibrosis evaluation were used to assess pancreas stiffness and were shown to be associated with POPF occurrence [14, 19–22]. However, magnetic resonance elastography is expensive; endoscopic ultrasound elastography is technically complex; pancreatic fibrosis and texture assessment are difficult to evaluate pre-operatively and lack objective quantification. Thus, a non-invasive, objective, quantitative, and widely accessible method for pre-operative pancreas stiffness assessment remains to be explored.

Two-dimensional shear wave elastography (2D-SWE) is a non-invasive method to understand tissue stiffness. While 2D-SWE is widely used in organs, including the liver and thyroid, its application in the pancreas remains limited [23–25]. Previous studies indicated pancreatic 2D-SWE measurement value‘s association with POPF [18, 26–29]. Other studies have also demonstrated the correlation between 2D-SWE measurement value and histological features such as pancreatic fibrosis [30–32], indicating its potential for predicting POPF by non-invasively assessing pancreatic histology. However, large-scale studies are still scarce, and the histological basis for using pancreatic 2D-SWE to predict POPF remains to be explored. Additionally, since the incidence of POPF varies across different types of surgeries, the application of 2D-SWE in predicting POPF in different surgery approaches requires further investigation [33, 34].

The present study aimed to explore the feasibility and histological basis of pancreatic 2D-SWE measurement value in predicting POPF following partial pancreatectomy, and to build a prediction model of POPF by pre-operative features.

Materials and methods

Ethics

The protocol of this single-center study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, and the written informed consent of the patients was collected before the data analysis.

Patients enrollment

From August 2023 to August 2024, 305 patients with pancreatic or periampullary diseases and undergoing partial pancreatectomy were prospectively enrolled (Fig. 1). The patients were randomly divided into a training group (n = 203) and a validation group (n = 102). (Post hoc evaluation indicated an events-per-variable of 43, supporting the adequacy of the sample size.)

Flowchart of patient enrollment. *2D-SWE, two-dimensional shear wave elastography; POPF, post-operative pancreatic fistula

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) treatment-naïve pancreatic or periampullary diseases; (3) prepared to undergo partial pancreatectomy; (4) collected pre-operative data within one week before the surgery, including pancreatic 2D-SWE measurement.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of any pancreas-related treatment, including surgery, radiotherapy, or neoadjuvant therapy; (2) history of upper abdominal surgery or systemic treatment; (3) current acute phase of pancreatitis; (4) history of other malignancy; (5) history of severe systemic diseases, including acquired immunodeficiency syndrome; (6) inability to cooperate with pre-operative examinations or the surgery due to severe mental illness, cardiopulmonary dysfunction, or other reasons; (7) failure to obtain a valid 2D-SWE measurement.

Pancreatic 2D-SWE measurement

The pancreatic 2D-SWE examination was performed by the Aplio i900 ultrasound system (Canon Medical Systems, Japan) with the i8CX1 convex array probe (frequency 1.8–6.2 MHz). The examination was randomly conducted by five radiologists, each with over 3 years and more than 100 cases of pancreatic 2D-SWE examination experience. All patients fasted for at least 8 h before the examination. A comprehensive upper abdominal ultrasound examination was first performed. Then, the key acquisition parameters of 2D-SWE measurement were set as follows:

Partial pancreatectomy

The partial pancreatectomy was performed by a dedicated surgical team. The surgeons were divided according to their experience in pancreatic surgery as senior (with > 20 years of experience) and mid-level surgeons (with 10–20 years of experience). Partial pancreatectomy was categorized by the type of pancreatectomy into pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) and distal pancreatectomy (DP), and other surgeries (local excision of the lesion in any part of the pancreas), and by surgical technique into open surgery and minimally invasive surgery (including laparoscopic and Da Vinci robotic surgery). All patients underwent evaluation by a multidisciplinary medical team before the surgery. The surgeons and other peri-operative care personnel were blinded to the 2D-SWE measurement.

According to the 2016 consensus of the International Study Group on Pancreatic Surgery (ISGPS) [35], POPF was defined as amylase exceeding more than three times the upper limit of normal level (641 U/L at our center), measured in the drain fluid on or after the third post-operative day. POPF was graded as A (or biochemical leak), B, and C, and clinically-relevant POPF (CR-POPF) was referred to as POPF grades B and C. In this study, we used a broad definition of POPF (including grades A, B, and C) as the primary outcome for risk analysis, while also analysing CR-POPF and individual grades separately. POPF grading is detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Pancreatic histological features

The interpretation of histological features was based on surgical specimens stained with hematoxylin and eosin after fixation in 10% formalin and paraffin embedding, with a section thickness of approximately 5 × 10− 3 mm. The interpretation was performed by a pathologist with over 10 years of experience with a BX53 microscope (Olympus, Japan), and the eyepiece was adjusted to 10x magnification, while the objective lens was set to magnifications of 10-20x. The pathologist was blinded to the patients’ peri-operative conditions.

For the lesion area, the lesion was categorized into benign lesions, malignant lesions, or neuroendocrine tumors. For the pancreatic parenchyma, a semi-quantitative histological assessment was performed (Supplementary Table 2), which included the degree of pancreatic fibrosis (F0-F3), lipomatosis (L0-L3), acinar atrophy (A0-A2), and stromal edema (E0-E3) [21, 36, 37]. In 24 patients, there was insufficient volume of the non-lesion pancreatic parenchyma. For the correlation analysis between Emean and histological features, patients without available histological data for the non-lesion pancreatic parenchyma were excluded. While histological variables of all the patients were not included in the analysis of the multivariate logistic regression model, which was developed exclusively using pre-operative parameters.

Data collection

Patient characteristics, including demographics, serum laboratory analyses, imaging characteristics, histopathologic data, and surgical procedures, were collected.

Pancreatic duct dilation was defined as the maximal internal diameter of the duct exceeding 3 mm on imaging. The location of the lesion was classified as pancreatic head, body, tail, or outside the pancreas. Lymph node metastasis and vascular invasion were determined based on the pathological diagnosis of the surgical resection specimens. Patients’ survival status was monitored for up to one month post-operatively.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean (standard deviation), median [25th, 75th percentile], or frequency (percentage) as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using the Student t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, while categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as needed. The relationship between Emean and pancreatic conditions was analyzed using Spearman correlation analysis, and the discrimination ability was assessed by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and the area under the curve (AUC). The optimal ROC cutoffs were selected based on maximizing the Youden index, and sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictive values were calculated.

Patients were randomly assigned to the training or the validation group. Univariate and multivariate forward logistic regression analyses were used to explore the risk factors of POPF occurrence in the training group. Multivariate analyses were subsequently performed on the significant factors (p < 0.050) in the univariate analyses. According to the multivariate analysis, a predictive model was built. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with values < 2 considered acceptable. The goodness-of-fit of the model was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. Model performance was evaluated using AUC, calibration plots, Brier score, and calibration intercept and slope. Moreover, to assess potential overfitting and evaluate model robustness, a five-fold cross-validation was performed. The dataset was randomly partitioned into five subsets of approximately equal size. Each subset was sequentially used as a validation set, while the remaining four subsets served as the training set. The AUC was calculated for each fold, and the mean AUC with standard deviation was reported. Moreover, to evaluate the incremental predictive value of Emean, an Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) analysis was performed by comparing the model with and without Emean.

A two-sided p < 0.050 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software version 26.0. (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY), MedCalc Statistical Software version 20.100 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium) and R software version 4.4.1.

Results

Patient characteristics

Three hundred and five patients were enrolled in this study, with 157 (51.5%) males. The median age was 56.00 [44.00,66.00] years, and the median body mass index (BMI) was 22.43 [20.26, 24.48] kg/m². The median lesion size was 2.90 [2.00, 4.10] cm, with 120 (39.3%) cases located at the pancreatic head, while 52 (17.0%), 58 (19.0%), and 75 (24.6%) cases were located at the pancreatic body, tail, and other areas, respectively. The pancreatic duct was dilated in 88 (28.9%) patients. The median Emean was 7.00 [6.10, 8.50] kPa {or 1.53 [1.43, 1.68] m/s}, and 35 (11.5%) measurements were conducted at the pancreatic head and 270 (88.5%) at the body. Regarding the pancreatectomy type, 174 (57.0%) patients underwent PD, 106 (34.8%) underwent DP, and 25 (8.2%) underwent local excision of the lesion. As regards the surgical technique, 133 (43.6%) patients underwent open surgery and 172 (56.4%) underwent minimally invasive surgery [39 (12.8%) were laparoscopic and 133 (43.6%) were Da Vinci robotic surgery]. Among the surgeons, the senior surgeons performed 203 (66.6%) surgeries, while the mid-level surgeons performed 102 (33.4%).

POPF occurred in 129 (42.3%) patients, with 75 (24.6%) at grade A (or biochemical leak). The occurrence rate of CR-POPF was 17.7%, including 40 (13.1%) in grade B and 14 (4.6%) in grade C. Three (1.0%) patients died within one month post-operatively, with two dying due to severe infections and POPF, and one dying from abdominal bleeding and non-infectious multiple organ failure.

Aside from age (p = 0.009), there were no significant differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups (all p > 0.050; Table 1; Supplementary Table 3).

Association between Emean and POPF

Compared to the patient without POPF after partial pancreatectomy, Emean was lower in those with POPF {7.80 [6.65, 9.30] kPa vs. 6.30 [5.60, 7.00] kPa, p < 0.001}. According to ROC analysis of the entire cohort, Emean was significantly able to discriminate between the POPF occurrence {AUC [95% confidence interval (CI)] = 0.77 (0.72–0.83), cutoff (95% CI) = 7.34 (7.10–7.50) kPa, p < 0.001}. Emean’s discrimination to grade B [AUC (95% CI) = 0.80 (0.72–0.87), cutoff (95% CI) = 6.15 (5.60–7.10) kPa, p < 0.001] and grade C [AUC (95% CI) = 0.76 (0.62–0.89), cutoff (95% CI) = 6.15 (5.60–7.00.60.00) kPa, p < 0.001) was also significant, and as the POPF grade increased, the cutoff of Emean decreased (Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 4).

Histological basis of emean’s association with POPF

In 24 patients, the non-lesion pancreatic parenchyma sample was insufficient for histological assessment, and these patients were excluded from the following histological correlation analysis. Compared with patients who had complete histological data, they showed differences in pre-operative carbohydrate antigen 125, red blood cell count, and type of pancreatectomy (all p < 0.050), while no significant differences were observed in other baseline characteristics (all p > 0.050; Supplementary Table 5).

Emean was significantly correlated with pancreatic fibrosis (r = 0.20, p < 0.001), acinar atrophy (r = 0.15, p = 0.015), and stromal edema (r =−0.16, p = 0.008), but no significant correlation was found between Emean and pancreatic lipomatosis (r =−0.01, p = 0.881). The discriminant ability of Emean to pancreatic fibrosis stage F1-F2, F2-F3, acinar atrophy stage A0-A1, A1-A2, and stromal edema stage E0-E1 was also significant (all p < 0.050; Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 6).

Risk factors of the occurrence of POPF

In the model, PPOPF represented the predicted POPF incidence. BMI was expressed in kg/m². DMPD referred to the pancreatic duct status, with dilation recorded as 1 and non-dilation as 0. Emean was the 2D-SWE measured pancreatic stiffness expressed in kPa. The intercept was 1.28, and the β for BMI, DMPD, and Emean was 0.17, −1.42, and − 0.72, respectively.

All VIF values are very close to 1 (Emean: 1.05; BMI: 1.04; pancreatic duct dilation: 1.02), indicating an absence of concerning multicollinearity among the predictor variables. Furthermore, tests for two-way interactions between Emean, pancreatic duct dilation, and BMI revealed no statistically significant interactions (all p > 0.050), supporting the adequacy of the main effects model.

Moreover, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the impact of excluding patients with failed 2D-SWE measurements. In the worst-case scenario, OR was 0.60 (95% CI: 0.51–0.71, p < 0.001), while in the best-case scenario, the OR was 0.56 (95% CI: 0.46–0.67, p < 0.001), indicating the robustness of the findings regarding Emean. Additionally, the IDI analysis confirmed that the inclusion of Emean significantly enhanced the model’s performance (IDI: 0.17, 95% CI: 0.12–0.22, p < 0.001).

Performance of the model in predicting POPF occurrence

The Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated good calibration of the predictive model, with p = 0.879 in the training group, p = 0.805 in the validation group, and p = 0.904 in the total cohort. In both groups, the PPOPF model effectively discriminated POPF occurrence [AUC (95% CI) = 0.84 (0.79–0.89), p < 0.001 in the training group and AUC (95% CI) = 0.81 (0.82–0.90), p < 0.001 in the validation group]. Calibration plots of each group was shown in Supplementary Fig. 2 and the decision-curve analysis was shown in Supplementary Fig. 3. The model also distinguished CR-POPF and grade C POPF occurrence (all p < 0.050; Fig. 3; Table 3). According to the model, the Emean ranges corresponding to different probabilities of developing POPF were calculated under different BMI and the status of pancreatic duct dilation (Table 4). Moreover, in the five-fold cross-validation, the model achieved an average AUC of 0.82 (standard deviation 0.06; range 0.73–0.89), which was close to the original AUC of 0.83 calculated from the full dataset. The small reduction in AUC suggests minimal overfitting and good internal stability of the model. Moreover, the model showed significant associations with post-operative infection (r = 0.16, p = 0.005), longer hospital stay (r = 0.12, p = 0.034), and higher 1-month readmission rates (r = 0.14, p = 0.014).

As regards POPF occurrence, there were significant differences among different types of pancreatectomy and surgical techniques (all p < 0.050; Supplementary Table 8, Supplementary Table 9); while among the minimally invasive surgeries, there was no significant difference between laparoscopic and robotic surgeries (p > 0.050). ROC analysis was thus performed to investigate the performance of the model to predict POPF across surgical procedures. Accordingly, the model could distinguish between POPF grades across different surgical techniques (all AUC > 0.70, p < 0.050; Supplementary Table 10). As regards pancreatectomy type, the model showed significant discrimination between POPF grades in PD surgery (all AUC > 0.80, p < 0.050). However, in DP surgery, the model only distinguished POPF and CR-POPF occurrence (both AUC > 0.80, p < 0.050), with no significant ability to differentiate grade C POPF (p = 0.984).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that lower Emean is significantly associated with an increased risk of POPF. The histological analysis provides an explanation for this association, confirming that Emean reflects key histological features, including pancreatic fibrosis and acinar atrophy. One of the key innovations of the study is the development of a pre-operative predictive model that incorporates 2D-SWE, BMI, and pancreatic duct dilation, which was internally validated for its robustness. Furthermore, this study includes a large, diverse cohort that spans various pancreatic diseases and surgical procedures, enhancing the generalizability of our findings. These highlight the broader applicability of 2D-SWE in POPF risk prediction and underscore its value as a non-invasive, pre-operative assessment tool. Collectively, this study contributes to a deeper mechanistic understanding of pancreatic 2D-SWE and extends its clinical utility in the peri-operative setting.

Consistent with previous studies, this study indicated that those with softer pancreatic tissue were more likely to develop higher-grade POPF [18, 21, 26, 27]. Compared to intra-operative texture, magnetic resonance elastography, endoscopic ultrasound elastography, and pancreatic parenchymal fibrosis, 2D-SWE offers a convenient, non-invasive, objective, and quantitative assessment of pancreas stiffness. A possible reason for the soft pancreas’s association with the higher POPF occurrence was that the tissue might be more susceptible to injury during surgical manipulation and have a low suture-holding capacity [38–40]. Also, in the surgery, the soft tissue might have technical difficulty in sealing off a delicate, friable transection margin [15].

Another reason for the elevated POPF occurrence, as regards the histological basis, might be the lower fibrosis degree and the higher content of pancreatic lobules in the soft pancreas. The lobules could produce more pancreatic juice, which is rich in proteolytic enzymes and could increase the risk of pancreaticoenteric anastomosis disruption [16, 18, 41–45]. Our analysis of pancreatic histological features and POPF supports this hypothesis: pancreatic fibrosis and acinar atrophy may underlie the predictive ability of 2D-SWE to POPF. However, Emean was not only correlated with pancreatic fibrosis and acinar atrophy but also negatively correlated with stromal oedema. Stromal oedema also showed a significant association with pancreatic stiffness and POPF in this study. Although the underlying mechanism remains uncertain, it may reflect inflammatory or reactive changes influencing tissue stiffness. Given the lack of prior evidence, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and warrants further investigation in future histopathological and imaging studies. The semi-quantitative histological scoring system used in this study is not internationally standardised, and potential intra- and inter-observer variability cannot be excluded. Future studies are warranted to validate the reproducibility of pancreatic histological assessment across observers.

Similar to previous studies, this research found that pancreatic duct dilation and BMI also influenced POPF [16, 40, 42]. The narrow pancreatic duct might complicate the anastomosis creation [42]. Also, the non-dilated pancreatic duct might be susceptible to occlusion or dehiscence in the duct-to-mucosa anastomosis procedure in partial pancreatectomy, and the obstructed pancreatic juice in the duct may erode the pancreatic intestinal anastomosis [40]. However, the assessment of pancreatic duct status was necessarily limited to a binary classification rather than a continuous diameter measurement. This was due to the inherent difficulty in obtaining precise millimeter measurements from imaging for non-dilated or minimally dilated ducts, alongside the lack of a universally accepted multi-category classification. While our binary classification approach ensures clinical practicality, it may result in some loss of information. Moreover, the association between BMI and POPF might result from the visceral fat hindering operative exposure and obscuring the anatomical structures, particularly the vessels [46]. However, previous studies suggested that BMI might affect the success rate of pancreatic 2D-SWE examination [25]. Since this study only included patients with successful 2D-SWE examinations (most of them had normal BMI), factors influencing POPF in those with abnormal BMI remain to be explored. Moreover, potential confounders such as smoking, alcohol consumption, diabetes, and nutritional status may influence the pancreas and thus affect the POPF risk. In our analysis, these factors showed no significant associations with POPF, but their binary classification in our data without quantitative assessment may have introduced residual confounding.

Consistent with previous studies [34, 47], this study showed that the POPF grade varied across different surgical types. However, the model built in this study showed limited predictive performance for grade C POPF. Advanced approaches such as penalised regression or resampling could potentially address class imbalance and enhance prediction in future studies. However, given the small number of grade C cases, applying such methods might lead to overfitting. The model built in this study should be further refined and externally validated in larger, multicenter cohorts. In addition, this study found no significant differences in POPF occurrence rates across different surgical types or surgeons. However, the selection of surgeons and surgical types was greatly influenced by socioeconomic and subjective factors; thus, further studies were warranted. Moreover, in our analysis, surgeon experience was not significantly associated with POPF occurrence, though surgical expertise is often considered an important determinant of postoperative outcomes. However, all operations were performed by surgeons from the same experienced team. This limited variability may have obscured potential effects of surgical expertise, and future multicenter studies with broader operator diversity are needed to confirm these findings.

Previous studies in healthy volunteers have shown that pancreatic 2D-SWE measurements demonstrate the best intra- and inter-operator reproducibility in the pancreatic body and that Emean does not differ significantly between the head and body, but is markedly higher in the tail [25]. Based on these findings, the pancreatic body was prioritised as the preferred measurement site in this study, followed by the head when the body was not suitable. Consequently, all 2D-SWE measurements in this cohort were obtained from the pancreatic head and body, with no tail measurements performed. In our patient population, one of the main factors influencing the measurement site was the location of the lesion. Patients with lesions involving the pancreatic body were relatively less likely to be included, as reliable 2D-SWE acquisition was more challenging in such cases. However, the relationship between the pre-operative measurement site and the subsequent surgical resection line remains to be clarified. In addition, operator judgment and imaging feasibility may also have influenced site selection to some extent, potentially introducing variability. Moreover, although earlier studies reported acceptable inter- and intra-operator reproducibility [24, 25], we acknowledge that our findings warrant further large-scale, multicenter verification regarding the inter- and intra-operator reproducibility.

The present study still had other limitations. First, the study cohort was heterogeneous, including patients with both pancreatic and periampullary diseases who underwent different types of partial pancreatectomy. This design reflected real-world surgical practice, where POPF risk assessment is clinically relevant across these types of disease. However, inherent variations in histological characteristics and surgical approaches among disease categories were unavoidable, introducing potential confounding. Moreover, the limited number of patients within subgroups for each specific disease and surgical procedure reduced the statistical power for detailed stratified analyses. Second, though different surgical types were analysed, data on several technically important surgical factors (such as pancreatic gland thickness, gland texture, specific stump closure or anastomotic techniques, and the use of drains or stents) were not consistently available for analysis. The absence of these variables introduces the potential for residual confounding, and future prospective studies should aim to incorporate them. Third, in this study, both the training and validation groups were derived from the same centre and surgical team; the generalizability of our conclusions to other institutions, equipment, or operators remains uncertain. External validation in prospective, multicenter studies is warranted to confirm its broader applicability. Fourth, the absence of histological data in 24 patients due to the insufficient pancreatic parenchyma sample may have introduced potential selection bias, and this limitation should be considered when interpreting the histological findings. Fifth, patients who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy were excluded due to substantial heterogeneity and incomplete documentation of treatment regimens, which prevented reliable assessment of its potential impact on pancreatic 2D-SWE and post-operative outcomes. Sixth, only patients with successful 2D-SWE examinations were included, while 39 patients failed. This inclusion criterion may have introduced selection bias, and the predictive factors of POPF in such technically challenging cases warrant further investigation. Furthermore, a direct comparison with established POPF risk scores, such as the Fistula Risk Score [48, 49], was not feasible due to the pre-operative design of our study, which did not collect intra-operative data like pancreatic gland texture. Such a comparison is necessary to fully contextualize the clinical utility of our model, and it might be one key objective for future studies. Additionally, the relationship between Emean and post-operative pancreatitis was not systematically evaluated in this study due to the difficulty in diagnosis of pancreatitis. The necessary biochemical data (post-operative amylase and lipase levels) [50, 51] were not routinely collected in our centre, as testing was triggered by clinical symptoms, and thus an exploratory analysis regarding post-operative pancreatitis is not considered reliable. Investigating the potential of 2D-SWE to predict post-operative pancreatitis remains an important area for future prospective studies that incorporate systematic post-operative monitoring.

Conclusions

The lower pancreatic 2D-SWE measurements were associated with a higher risk of developing POPF, and the histological basis for this may lie in 2D-SWE’s correlation with pancreatic fibrosis and acinar atrophy. The present study developed a pre-operative predictive model for POPF using pancreatic 2D-SWE measurements, BMI, and the presence of pancreatic duct dilation, which could predict POPF risk across varying severity levels and was applicable to different surgical types.
