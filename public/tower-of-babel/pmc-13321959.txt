Self-regulatory fatigue and its associated factors among nursing interns: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Qiaoying Chang, Xiangying Liu, Yizhou Tan, Jiajia Duan, Jinping Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelf-regulatory fatigue is an emerging concern within nursing education, with implications for professional competence, emotional resilience, and long-term career development. To assess the level of self-regulatory fatigue among nursing interns and explore its related influencing factors.MethodsA total of 340 nursing interns participated in this survey. Data were collected from March to December 2024 using an online survey comprising a general information questionnaire, the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), the Adolescent Mental Health Literacy Assessment Questionnaire (AMHLAQ), and the Psychological Detachment Scale. Descriptive statistics, t-test, ANOVA, Pearson’s correlation coefficients, and multiple linear regression analyses were conducted.ResultsPerceived stress (β = 0.47, p < 0.001), mental health literacy (β = -0.13, p = 0.008), psychological detachment (β = -0.10, p = 0.025), academic performance (β = 0.15, p < 0.001), length of clinical placement (β = -0.11, p = 0.016) and left-behind experience (β = -0.10, p = 0.032) were significant predictors of self-regulatory fatigue. The model explained 39.1% of the variance in self-regulatory fatigue.ConclusionsNursing interns showed a moderate level of self-regulatory fatigue. Three psychological factors, namely, perceived stress, mental health literacy, and psychological detachment, along with academic performance, clinical placement duration, and left-behind experience, were significantly associated factors of self-regulatory fatigue. These findings suggest that targeted strategies should be developed to mitigate interns’ fatigue and promote their holistic health.Keywords: Self-regulatory fatigue, Perceived stress, Mental health literacy, Psychological detachment, Nursing interns

Introduction

The development of professional competence in nursing requires not only the accumulation of knowledge and technical skills but also places high demands on nurses’ capacity for self-regulation. Self-regulation involves internal processes of regulating emotions, behavior, and thoughts to adapt to a changing context or achieve goals [1, 2]. However, according to the strength model of self-regulation, a person’s capacity for self-control is finite [3, 4]. Sustained efforts to regulate emotions, thoughts, or behaviors gradually deplete cognitive and emotional reserves, leading to self-regulatory fatigue [4]. Self-regulatory fatigue is defined as a temporary decrease in an individual’s capacity or willingness to engage in self-directed activities where self-control is required [5]. It manifests as a diminished ability to regulate emotions, thoughts, and behaviors [6]. Empirical studies have demonstrated that self-regulatory fatigue is associated with reduced psychological resilience and impaired decision-making [7, 8]. For nursing students experiencing prolonged self-regulatory fatigue, the depletion of regulatory resources may limit their ability to reframe negative thoughts and cope effectively with psychological stress [9]. Furthermore, individuals experiencing self-regulatory fatigue may become more vulnerable to negative emotional states. This condition may undermine motivation and reduce work engagement [10].

Clinical placement is an essential stage in the transition of nursing interns from school to the workplace. During this period, interns must adapt to the clinical environment, learn and master clinical knowledge and skills, and manage interpersonal relationships through self-regulation and self-management [11, 12]. However, because of the limited time and energy of nursing interns, long-term and high-load self-regulation will gradually deplete psychological resources, leading to self-regulatory fatigue, affecting the quality of nursing services and their physical and mental health [13]. Although self-regulatory fatigue plays a vital role in the healthy development of nursing interns [3], few studies have investigated it. Most existing studies focus on patient groups [14] or registered nurses [13], leaving a gap in research targeting nursing interns. Investigating the determinants of self-regulatory fatigue among nursing interns is therefore essential for promoting their well-being and professional development.

This study is primarily grounded in the strength model of self-regulation and further informed by the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory. According to the strength model, different forms of self-regulation rely on a shared and limited pool of psychological resources known as self-regulatory strength [15]. When individuals continuously exert self-control, these resources may become depleted, resulting in self-regulatory fatigue. Complementarily, COR theory proposes that individuals strive to acquire, maintain, and protect valuable resources in order to reduce psychological distress and prevent burnout [16]. Together, these theoretical perspectives provide a framework for examining how perceived stress, mental health literacy, and psychological detachment may influence self-regulatory fatigue among nursing interns.

We hypothesize that perceived stress, mental health literacy, and psychological detachment are important factors influencing self-regulatory fatigue in nursing interns. Perceived stress refers to an individual’s subjective assessment of their psychological stress and feelings when faced with a stimulating event [17]. According to the strength model of self-regulation, when individuals perceive stress, they must draw upon their limited self-regulatory resources to cope with stressors. Repeated or prolonged exposure to stress may therefore accelerate the depletion of these resources and increase the likelihood of experiencing self-regulatory fatigue. The impact of perceived stress on self-regulation is demonstrated across diverse high-stress populations, including chemotherapy patients [7] and shift-working healthcare professionals [18]. Previous research has suggested that perceived stress may contribute to self-regulatory fatigue and subsequently impair individuals’ self-management behaviors in clinical populations [19]. With respect to nursing interns, they are widely recognized a high-stress group due to their practice-based educational program and the combination of academic and clinical training [20, 21]. In the clinical setting, stressors include exams, pressure of grades, fear of failing and meeting deadlines, lack of professional knowledge and skills, handling clinical emergencies, and relations with clinical staff [22]. A cross-sectional study involving 1,703 nursing students reported a significant association between academic stress and self-regulatory fatigue [3]. The chronic stress experienced by nursing interns may deplete their self-regulatory resources, leading to self-regulatory fatigue.

Mental health literacy is defined as knowledge and beliefs about mental disorders which aid their recognition, management, and prevention [23]. Scientific evidence indicates that improved mental health literacy can facilitate help-seeking attitudes and relieve stress and negative emotions [24, 25], while lower levels of mental health literacy may lead to isolation or negative coping behaviors [26]. Previous studies have shown that higher levels of mental health literacy are associated with better psychological well-being and more proactive help-seeking behaviors [27]. By facilitating adaptive coping strategies, mental health literacy may help individuals avoid excessive rumination and ineffective emotional suppression, thereby reducing unnecessary consumption of psychological resources. From the perspective of COR theory, mental health literacy can be regarded as an important personal resource that enables individuals to recognize psychological distress and respond to it more effectively. Adequate mental health literacy may thus function as a buffer against the depletion of regulatory resources, thereby reducing the risk of self-regulatory fatigue.

Psychological detachment refers to disengagement from work or not thinking about work-related things and avoiding work-related activities during non-work hours [28]. From the perspective of COR theory, psychological detachment may help interrupt the process of resource depletion by allowing individuals to temporarily disengage from work-related demands, thereby facilitating the recovery of psychological resources. Research demonstrates that psychological detachment not only restores resources depleted at work but also generates new ones [29]. Empirical evidence suggests that psychological detachment can mediate the relationship between perceived social support and job burnout among nurses [30]. This finding indicates that detachment may help transform available support into psychological resources, thereby alleviating burnout and fatigue. Without sufficient detachment, the continuous cognitive and emotional demands associated with clinical training may accumulate and lead to persistent exhaustion. Therefore, by serving as an effective form of self-regulation, it has been shown to reduce stress and fatigue among nurses [31, 32].

This study aims to assess the level of self-regulatory fatigue among nursing interns and to examine its associations with perceived stress, mental health literacy, and psychological detachment, while accounting for sociodemographic variables. The findings are expected to provide empirical evidence for developing targeted support strategies aimed at reducing interns’ self-regulatory fatigue and promoting their well-being and professional development.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional descriptive design to assess the level of self-regulatory fatigue among nursing interns and to explore its associated influencing factors. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling from nursing interns undergoing clinical training.

Settings and participants

This study selected 340 nursing interns in four tertiary hospitals in Wuhan City, Hubei Province, from March 2024 to December 2024. The inclusion criteria were: (1) currently in the nursing internship, and (2) informed consent and voluntary participation in this study. Exclusion criteria: those on leave during the investigation period were excluded.

The sample size was at least 20 times larger than the number of variables based on the multiple regression sample size standard [33]. The independent variables designed in this study were 11. A sample size of at least 275 was expected, considering a 20% non-response rate. Finally, 340 nursing interns were included based on the actual research.

Measurements

The data collection instruments used in this study included a general information questionnaire, the Self-regulatory Fatigue Scale, the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), the Adolescent Mental Health Literacy Assessment Questionnaire (AMHLAQ), and the Psychological Detachment Scale.

General information questionnaire

It was self-compiled based on the purpose of the study, including gender, age, academic stage, place of residence, personality, left-behind experience, student leaders, academic performance, the only child, etc. Among them, left-behind experience refers to the experience of being left alone in one or both parents’ hometowns or the care of a single parent or entrusted to another person for more than half a year when the person was under 16 years of age [34]. Academic performance is categorized into the top 50% and the bottom 50% of the class.

Self-regulatory fatigue scale

This scale was developed by Nes et al. [5] and translated by Wang et al. [35]. There are 16 items, divided into three dimensions: cognition (6 items), emotion (5 items), and behavior (5 items). A 5-point scale was used, with scores ranging from 1 to 5 from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”, and total scores ranging from 16 to 80. The higher the total score, the higher the levels of self-regulatory fatigue. The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.861 in this study.

Perceived stress scale (PSS)

Based on Cohen et al. [17], the PSS was revised by Yang and Huang [36] and used to assess the subjective perceived stress level of the participant. It consists of two dimensions and 14 items: tension (7 items) and loss of control (7 items). The scale used the five-point Likert point scoring method, ranging from “never” to “always” assigned 1 and 5, respectively. Higher scores represent higher levels of perceived stress in individuals. The scale had an overall Cronbach’s alpha of 0.772 in this study.

Adolescent mental health literacy assessment questionnaire (AMHLAQ)

This study used the Adolescent Mental Health Literacy Assessment Questionnaire (AMHLAQ) developed by Li et al. [37]. With 22 items, it contains four dimensions: knowledge, cognition, attitude, and practice. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5 where 1 means strongly disagree and 5 means strongly agree. The scale scores range from 22 to 110, with higher total scores indicating higher levels of mental health literacy in individuals. This scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity among nursing students in China [38] and has been found to be suitable for assessing mental health literacy in individuals in late adolescence and emerging adulthood [39], which corresponds to the age range of most participants in this study. In this study, our Cronbach’s alpha was 0.923.

Psychological detachment scale

The scale was first developed and produced by Sonnentag and Fritz [29] and later translated by Lu et al. [40]. It was a widespread tool that assessed the status of psychological detachment of caregivers on a single dimension. The scale consists of 4 items, and each question is a simple statement such as “I will forget about the unit after work”. The scale was scored on a five-point Likert scale, and the scale’s total score ranged from 4 to 20. The higher the score, the higher the levels of psychological detachment. The Cronbach’s alpha for this scale was 0.852.

Theoretical framework

This study is theoretically grounded in the strength model of self-regulation and COR theory. According to this model, perceived stress requires individuals to continuously mobilize their limited self-regulatory resources to cope with environmental demands, which may accelerate resource depletion and result in self-regulatory fatigue. Complementing this perspective, COR theory proposes that individuals are motivated to acquire, maintain, and protect valuable resources in order to cope with stress and prevent resource loss. Within this framework, mental health literacy can be viewed as an important personal psychological resource that helps individuals better recognize and manage psychological distress, while psychological detachment serves as a recovery mechanism that facilitates the restoration of depleted resources. Together, these theoretical perspectives provide a conceptual basis for examining how perceived stress, mental health literacy, and psychological detachment may influence self-regulatory fatigue among nursing interns.

Data collection

The online questionnaire was collected through the Questionnaire Star with the consent of the hospital’s nursing department and the head nurses of each department. Before completing the questionnaire, the purpose and procedure were explained in detail. All options are “Required Answers”. Nursing interns voluntarily scanned the QR code or link to the questionnaire posted on Questionnaire Star after class. In addition, to minimize potential response bias or coercion, it was clearly stated that there were no right or wrong answers. After collecting the questionnaires, the researchers eliminated questionnaires containing highly consistent answers or obvious logical errors. 362 questionnaires were collected, 22 invalid questionnaires were excluded, and 340 valid questionnaires were obtained, with a reasonable return rate of 93.92%.

Data analysis

This study used IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0, for data analysis. For a normal distribution, skewness and kurtosis values within the range of ± 2 are considered acceptable [41]. Continuous variables were displayed in mean and standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were shown in frequency and percentage. Comparison of participant characteristics between groups was used for t-test and one-way ANOVA. Bivariate correlation analysis was used to examine the correlations and correlation coefficients between self-regulatory fatigue and perceived stress, mental health literacy, and psychological detachment. Variables with statistical significance in univariate analysis were included in a multivariate linear regression model. Multivariate stepwise linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the influencing factors of self-regulatory fatigue with p-in = 0.05 and p-out = 0.10. Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The variance inflation factor (VIF) in the model was calculated to avoid invalid statistical results. The determination coefficient of the regression model (R2 and adjusted R2, the amount of variation in self-regulatory fatigue explained by influencing factors) and the standardized regression coefficients (β) of the influencing factors were used to calculate the effect size.

Ethical considerations

The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Medicine, Wuhan University of Science and Technology. The study followed the principles of voluntary participation and informed consent. The registered number is 2025226.

Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted using self-regulatory fatigue scores as a dependent variable. Discussion

This study evaluated self-regulatory fatigue levels and related factors among nursing interns. In this study, we found that self-regulatory fatigue among nursing interns was at a medium level. The factors influencing self-regulatory fatigue were perceived stress, mental health literacy, psychological detachment, academic performance, length of clinical placement, and left-behind experience.

Self-regulatory fatigue among nursing interns

This study found a moderate level of self-regulatory fatigue among nursing interns, consistent with previous findings reported among registered nurses [42]. One possible explanation is that, although registered nurses face substantial professional responsibilities and clinical workloads, nursing interns encounter a different set of challenges during their transition into clinical practice. Compared with registered nurses, interns have limited clinical experience and have not yet fully developed the professional competencies that may buffer against self-regulatory fatigue. During clinical placement, they must simultaneously manage clinical tasks, meet training requirements, respond to patient needs, and navigate interpersonal relationships [43, 44]. These demands can lead to a continuous drain on psychological resources, thereby increasing the risk of self-regulatory fatigue.

Of the three dimensions, cognitive fatigue was the most pronounced, suggesting that nursing interns’ cognitive resources are under the greatest strain and may contribute most to overall self-regulatory fatigue. Unlike experienced nurses, who can often rely on intuition and automated processing, nursing interns must continuously adapt to unfamiliar clinical environments while actively integrating theoretical knowledge with clinical practice. During clinical placements, interns rotate across different departments and must become familiar with varying workflows, equipment, and care routines. They are also required to manage the cognitive demands associated with clinical tasks, such as memorizing medication information, understanding operating procedures, reviewing patient records, and following care plans under supervision. These demands substantially increase cognitive load, making interns particularly susceptible to cognitive fatigue. To alleviate this burden, clinical preceptors may provide structured checklists or procedural guides to help clarify clinical procedures and workflows [45], thereby reducing unnecessary cognitive load.

Behavioral fatigue was the second most pronounced dimension. One possible explanation is that interns’ actions are often constrained by strict clinical protocols and consist largely of routine or repetitive tasks. This environment limits interns’ autonomy and provides little positive feedback, which may reduce motivation and contribute to behavioral fatigue. To mitigate behavioral fatigue, clinical preceptors could consider increasing task variety by gradually involving interns in more complex clinical activities, such as participation in multidisciplinary ward rounds or assisting in care planning. In addition, establishing regular feedback mechanisms and providing constructive recognition of interns’ contributions may help sustain motivation. Allowing limited opportunities for supervised choice in clinical tasks may also promote a sense of professional autonomy and reduce fatigue associated with highly constrained behavioral routines.

Emotional fatigue, by contrast, was the least severe among the three dimensions. This finding differs from a recent study on nursing students [3], in which emotional fatigue was more prominent. One possible explanation is that the clinical internship environment may provide protective factors that are less available in academic settings. Unlike nursing students who primarily operate in classroom contexts, nursing interns work alongside clinical instructors and healthcare teams. These individuals may serve as important sources of emotional support, offering guidance, reassurance, and encouragement during challenging clinical situations. Such support can help buffer emotional stress and prevent the accumulation of emotional fatigue. To further support interns’ emotional well-being, nursing managers may consider strengthening peer-support systems and fostering a supportive team climate [46].

More broadly, nursing administrators and educators should focus on reducing overall self-regulatory fatigue among nursing interns, particularly cognitive and behavioral fatigue. Evidence suggests that emotion regulation training can significantly improve nurses’ ability to manage professional challenges and mitigate the depletion of psychological resources [47]. In addition, incorporating mindfulness training and cognitive reappraisal strategies may help alleviate psychological fatigue and facilitate a smoother professional transition among nursing interns [48, 49].

Associated factors of self-regulatory fatigue among nursing interns

Nursing interns with higher levels of perceived Stress had higher levels of self-regulatory fatigue

According to Field’s [50], this study found that perceived stress is strongly correlated with self-regulatory fatigue (r > 0.5) and positively correlated with all three of its dimensions. Specifically, it showed strong correlations with the cognitive and emotional dimensions, while the association with the behavioral dimension was moderate (0.3 < r < 0.5). These results indicate that high perceived stress significantly depletes the cognitive and emotional resources of nursing interns. Among the dimensions of perceived stress, tension demonstrated a strong correlation with self-regulatory fatigue, whereas loss of control showed a moderate correlation. This suggests that stress-related tension may contribute more substantially to the depletion of regulatory resources. Our findings further indicate that perceived stress is a key predictor of fatigue among nurses, which is consistent with the findings reported by Lee [51]. The stressors faced by nursing interns stem primarily from academic burdens, internship pressures, and job competition [52]. Consistent with the strength model of self-regulation [15], perceived stress may act as an important source of resource depletion, requiring individuals to continuously mobilize their limited self-regulatory resources to cope with perceived demands. This sustained regulatory effort may further intensify the consumption of cognitive and emotional resources, thereby contributing to higher levels of self-regulatory fatigue among nursing interns. Additionally, in high-pressure work environments, nursing interns with higher levels of perceived stress may feel more sensitive, hence leading to self-regulatory fatigue when stress accumulates and cannot be regulated [53]. Given the central role of perceived stress in shaping self-regulatory fatigue, interventions should focus on developing effective stress-coping strategies, such as mindfulness-based stress reduction, emotion regulation training, and cognitive restructuring, to help nursing interns better manage stress in academic and clinical settings [54].

Nursing interns with higher levels of mental health literacy had lower levels of self-regulatory fatigue

Mental health literacy demonstrated moderate negative correlations with overall self-regulatory fatigue and each of its three dimensions. The attitude and practice dimensions of mental health literacy showed the strongest associations with lower levels of fatigue, suggesting that positive attitudes and proactive help-seeking and coping behaviors may help mitigate self-regulatory fatigue. This finding is consistent with research linking mental health literacy to reduced depressive and anxiety symptoms among college students [55]. One possible explanation is that mental health literacy, acting as a proactive resource-preservation mechanism, facilitates the development of psychological resilience [56]. Consistent with the strength model of self-regulation [15], individuals with greater psychological resources are better able to regulate their emotions and behaviors when facing stress. Interns with higher mental health literacy may therefore be more capable of adopting adaptive coping strategies during clinical challenges, reducing the likelihood that limited self-regulatory resources are consumed by maladaptive rumination or ineffective coping. Furthermore, prior studies have reported associations between health literacy and fatigue in clinical populations [57]. Our findings extend this evidence by suggesting that mental health literacy may also play a protective role in reducing self-regulatory fatigue among nursing interns. Conversely, nursing interns with insufficient psychological resources may rely on continuous self-regulation when facing stress. This persistent drain on resources without timely replenishment may eventually lead to self-regulatory fatigue. Therefore, targeted educational interventions, such as mental health literacy training, may help strengthen interns’ psychological resources and improve their ability to manage stress, thereby reducing the risk of self-regulatory fatigue.

Nursing interns with higher levels of psychological detachment had lower levels of self-regulatory fatigue

Psychological detachment showed a negative correlation with self-regulatory fatigue. This aligns with previous research demonstrating an association between effective psychological detachment and reduced fatigue [58], indicating that interns who can effectively detach from work during off-hours may be better able to conserve and restore their psychological resources. Interestingly, the correlations were strongest for the cognitive and emotional dimensions of fatigue, but non-significant for the behavioral dimension, suggesting that psychological detachment facilitates recovery from cognitive and emotional resource depletion but has a limited impact on behavioral fatigue. One possible explanation is that behavioral fatigue among nursing interns may be more strongly influenced by structured clinical routines and repetitive task demands [59], which continue during clinical practice and may not be substantially alleviated by psychological detachment during off-duty periods. Psychological detachment can help nurses avoid further resource drain from work stressors [60] and facilitate the restoration of diminished cognitive and emotional resources [32], thereby conserving energy for subsequent work engagement and decreasing job burnout. To protect interns’ psychological resources and mitigate self-regulatory fatigue, nursing administrators should institute reasonable work schedules and guarantee interns’ uninterrupted off-duty time.

The higher the academic performance, the lower the levels of self-regulatory fatigue

This study found that nursing interns with higher academic performance exhibited lower levels of self-regulatory fatigue. This finding is consistent with prior research showing that students with stronger academic performance generally possess better self-regulatory abilities [61], suggesting that these students may be more capable of managing academic and clinical demands without excessively depleting their cognitive and emotional resources. Research has shown that self-efficacy is a key factor supporting the academic performance of nursing students [62], and it is significantly correlated with academic success and self-regulated learning abilities [63]. Students with higher self-efficacy are more likely to adopt effective self-regulated learning strategies and less likely to engage in energy-draining cycles of repeated effort when facing unfamiliar clinical tasks [64]. Within this framework, stronger academic performance may reflect greater confidence and more efficient use of self-regulatory strategies, which can help conserve psychological resources and reduce the likelihood of self-regulatory fatigue. Empirical research indicates that repeated simulation experiences can significantly bolster nursing students’ self-efficacy [65]. Therefore, incorporating simulation-based training into nursing education may help strengthen students’ confidence and coping abilities, potentially reducing the risk of self-regulatory fatigue during clinical practice.

The longer the clinical placement of nursing interns, the lower the levels of self-regulatory fatigue

This study found that nursing interns with longer clinical placement exhibited lower levels of self-regulatory fatigue. One possible explanation is that longer internship durations allow interns to accumulate richer clinical knowledge and practical skills while becoming more familiar with institutional policies, procedures, and clinical routines [66]. Such familiarity can reduce the cognitive load associated with unfamiliar settings and tasks, allowing for more automated processing and thereby decreasing self-regulatory depletion. This interpretation is supported by previous research showing that longer clinical placement is associated with higher training satisfaction, greater self-efficacy, and improved clinical competence [67, 68]. These factors may enhance interns’ confidence in handling clinical tasks and enable them to use their psychological resources more efficiently, thereby reducing the likelihood of self-regulatory fatigue. Nursing administrators should consider the role of placement duration when designing internship policies, ensuring that nursing interns have adequate time to adapt to the clinical environment. Special attention should be given to interns with relatively shorter clinical placements, as they may be at higher risk of experiencing self-regulatory fatigue.

Interns with left-behind experiences were more likely to have higher levels of self-regulatory fatigue

This study shows that nursing interns with left-behind experience have significantly higher levels of self-regulatory fatigue. This finding highlights the long-term psychological impact of early-life family separation and is consistent with evidence that childhood left-behind experiences are associated with poorer mental health outcomes [69]. Interns with a left-behind childhood background may have experienced limited parental guidance and emotional support during critical developmental stages, which has been linked to stronger self-criticism and lower self-esteem in adulthood [14]. According to the strength model of self-regulation [15], individuals rely on limited psychological resources to regulate emotions and behaviors when facing stress. Those with fewer psychological resources or weaker emotional support systems may therefore need to expend greater regulatory effort to cope with stressful situations, which may increase their vulnerability to self-regulatory fatigue. Nursing educators and administrators should pay greater attention to interns with a left-behind background. Targeted mental health education programs can be considered, along with improving the accessibility of psychological counseling services. This approach will help interns restore psychological resources and enhance their coping abilities in stressful clinical environments.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at four tertiary hospitals in Wuhan, Hubei Province, a major metropolitan city, which may not represent the situation of nursing interns in other geographical or healthcare settings. Future research should adopt a multi-center approach across diverse geographical regions to enhance representativeness. Second, as a cross-sectional design, this study establishes association but not causation between the variables and self-regulatory fatigue. Future studies could use longitudinal surveys that could track the development of self-regulatory over time and establish causal relationships among the variables. Third, all data were collected through self-reported questionnaires, which may introduce social desirability bias and affect the accuracy of participants’ reported levels of self-regulatory fatigue. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Future research could integrate objective physiological markers or behavioral assessments to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of self-regulatory fatigue. Finally, although the AMHLAQ demonstrated high reliability in our sample of emerging adults, we acknowledge that its original design targets an adolescent population. Future research should consider validating these findings using adult-specific instruments or developing tailored scales for nursing interns to ensure higher measurement precision.

Conclusions

The findings of this study demonstrated a medium level of self-regulatory fatigue among nursing interns. Academic performance, length of clinical placement, left-behind experience, perceived stress, mental health literacy, and psychological detachment were significant factors influencing self-regulatory fatigue. Nursing educators should pay greater attention to self-regulatory fatigue among interns to facilitate their successful role transition. Evidence from nursing research suggests that interventions targeting emotion regulation and stress management can effectively improve nurses’ psychological resilience. For example, implementing emotion regulation training, promoting cognitive reappraisal strategies, and providing mindfulness-based programs or counseling services may help nursing interns manage stress more effectively and mitigate emotional exhaustion. In addition, interventions aimed at enhancing mental health literacy and promoting psychological detachment from work may further reduce self-regulatory fatigue.
