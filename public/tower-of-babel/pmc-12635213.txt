Prioritizing critical factors for leather waste management in Bangladesh: an ISM-MICMAC and best–worst method approach
Authors: Md. Abdus Shabur, Nusrat Jahan, Md. Abdul Mottalib
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The leather industry in Bangladesh significantly contributes to the national economy but also generates extensive environmental pollution through chromium-contaminated sludge and untreated tannery effluents. Effective waste management requires identifying and prioritizing critical factors and understanding their interdependencies. This research introduces an integrated framework combining Interpretive Structural Modeling (ISM), Matrix Impact Cross Multiplication Applied to Classification (MICMAC), and the Best Worst Method (BWM) to systematically analyze and prioritize these factors. Initially, 19 factors were identified through a comprehensive literature review and expert consultation. These were narrowed to 12 critical factors by consulting 21 industry experts. Using BWM, the “Use of Sustainable and Environmentally Friendly Practices” emerged as most critical, followed by “Reuse and Recycling of Leather Waste,” and “Proper Modification of Existing Equipment.” ISM analysis structured the hierarchical relationships, highlighting sustainability initiatives as foundational drivers influencing regulatory compliance and technological improvements. MICMAC analysis further classified factors by their driving and dependence characteristics. This study uniquely contributes by providing a structured, replicable decision-making framework applicable across industries. Future research is recommended to incorporate dynamic modelling and real-time policy analysis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24856-1.

Introduction

The leather industry is very important to the economies of many developing nations, especially Bangladesh, where it is a key source of jobs and export income. However, the industry generates significant solid and liquid waste, including chromium-laden sludge, tannery effluents, and leather scraps, all of which pose serious risks to human health and the environment1. The way this waste is handled is important for lowering pollution, protecting resources, and promoting environmentally sustainable business practices. Leather waste comprises heavy metals, solvents, and organic waste, as well as various by-products from tanning, coloring, and finishing2. Severe environmental repercussions of inadequate disposal include harmful air emissions, soil and water pollution, and bioaccumulation of hazardous chemicals in ecosystems3. Although efficient waste management solutions are desperately needed, current Bangladeshi practices are mainly undeveloped and rely on antiquated methods such open dumping, landfilling, and ineffective wastewater treatment4,5. These difficulties highlight the need of using a methodical and combined strategy to find and give top priority to important elements affecting leather waste management.

Importance of addressing leather waste management for sustainable growth

Effective handling of leather waste is a complex task spanning environmental issues to economic, social, and legal spheres6. Dealing with these difficulties is really necessary for various different reasons. Rich in chromium, sulfides, and organic contaminants, the leather tanning process produces effluent that seriously deteriorates water and soil quality7. Released untreated wastewater into rivers and agricultural areas disturbs aquatic ecosystems and causes harmful material bioaccumulation8. Good waste management helps to greatly reduce these negative effects on the surroundings. By converting leather waste into value-added goods such gelatin, collagen, and bio-composites9, sustainable waste management techniques support the circular economy. Innovations in waste-to- products and recycling help to lessen dependency on virgin raw resources, therefore cutting manufacturing costs and improving sector sustainability10.

Leather manufacturing generates multiple waste streams throughout its unit operations. Solid wastes primarily consist of trimmings, shavings, buffing dust, and fleshing residues, which together account for nearly 40–50% of raw hide weight11. Liquid effluents are the most voluminous, arising from soaking, liming, deliming, bating, pickling, and tanning processes. These effluents contain high loads of organic matter, sulfides, and chromium, with reported generation of 30–40 L of wastewater per kg of processed hide12. Airborne wastes include volatile organic compounds (VOCs) from finishing, ammonia from deliming, and fugitive emissions from chemical storage, while dust wastes are released during buffing and shaving, posing occupational health risks if not captured through extraction systems13. Studies in the Savar Tannery Estate, Bangladesh, have noted that dust and trimmings alone contribute several tons of waste daily, while incomplete effluent treatment continues to discharge large volumes of polluted water into nearby rivers14,15. Together, these diverse waste streams highlight the urgent need for integrated management strategies that address both volume reduction and hazard mitigation.

For the leather sector, governments and international regulatory agencies are demanding higher environmental norms. Non-compliance may lead to financial penalties, export restrictions, and reputational damage16. Using organized waste management strategies enables companies to match world sustainability guidelines including the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Customers are calling for environmentally friendly products more and more, so sustainable business methods are now absolutely necessary to guarantee access to the worldwide market. Effective waste management practices help businesses to exceed customer expectations and improve brand image, therefore acquiring a competitive edge17.

Current waste management practices in Bangladesh are inadequate, heavily reliant on outdated methods such as open dumping and ineffective wastewater treatment, exacerbating environmental and public health risks18. Previous research has predominantly focused on isolated technical or regulatory aspects, leaving a critical gap regarding comprehensive identification and prioritization of interconnected factors.

Integrating ISM-MICMAC and BWM for critical factor analysis in leather waste management

Leather waste management is complicated by the interaction of several elements, including technological, legal, and socioeconomic ones. Traditional methods often overlook these complex interactions; therefore, a systematic, data-driven framework for decision-making is necessary. Using a hybrid analytical framework integrating ISM, MICMAC, and the BWM, our work addresses these urgent challenges. While BWM helps prioritize these issues depending on stakeholder preferences19–21, the ISM-MICMAC technique is crucial in assessing intricate interdependencies among waste management elements. Combining these approaches seeks to offer an empirically strong and practically feasible paradigm for bettering waste management in Bangladesh’s leather industry.

A qualitative-quantitative modeling method, ISM maps interdependencies between several elements20. It helps one to find important dependent variables and drivers inside a system. Conversely, MICMAC analysis groups elements according to their driving strength and dependency, therefore offering more thorough understanding of their impact on waste management practices22. This work introduces the BWM, a multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) method, to improve analytical robustness. By lowering discrepancies in expert opinion and thereby improving factor prioritizing, BWM maximizes23,24 more accurate decision-making Combining BWM with ISM-MICMAC guarantees a thorough assessment of leather waste management aspects, thereby supporting legislators and business leaders in developing sensible waste-reducing plans.

Although several studies have examined sustainable waste management in the leather industry, most have focused on technical treatment options (e.g., effluent technologies) or isolated managerial practices. Very few have systematically analyzed the interdependencies among critical factors and their relative importance using structured decision-making models. Moreover, evidence from developing-country contexts such as Bangladesh—where leather is both an economic driver and an environmental challenge—remains scarce.

Research questions and objectives

Accordingly, this study seeks to answer the following questions:

What are the critical factors influencing effective leather waste management in Bangladesh?

How are these factors interrelated, and which act as key driving forces within the system?

How should these factors be prioritized to guide policymakers and industry stakeholders?

Objectives:

Based on these questions, the objectives of this research are to:

a) Identify and validate critical factors for leather waste management through expert consultation.

b) Model the interrelationships among these factors using ISM and MICMAC analysis.

c) Prioritize the factors using the BWM to provide actionable guidance for sustainable leather waste management in Bangladesh.

Literature review

Prior studies on leather waste management

Numerous studies have attempted to address the issue of leather waste management through different technological, regulatory, and optimization models. In terms of technological advancements, researchers have explored eco-friendly waste treatment systems such as membrane filtration, biological degradation, and enzymatic hydrolysis, which offer sustainable alternatives to traditional chemical-based treatments25. While these methods reduce environmental impacts, they are not widely adopted in low-income countries due to economic constraints and a lack of skilled personnel to operate the required infrastructure9. Likewise, research on circular economy projects highlight the reuse of tannery sludge and leather scraps in making bio-composites, gelatin, and adhesives, therefore lowering waste output and supporting resource economy2. Nevertheless, a lack of industrial standardization, poor waste segregation at the source, and low consumer demand for recycled leather goods hinder the broad use of circular economy methods notwithstanding these advantages17.

Policy-oriented and regulatory studies underline the important part environmental governance and corporate social responsibility (CSR) play in establishing sustainable waste management strategies. Numerous studies have looked at how well government rules and global environmental standards help to limit tannery waste26,27. Their results show that small and medium businesses (SMEs) are discouraged from implementing sustainable waste management practices by inadequate enforcement systems, bureaucratic inefficiencies, and expensive compliance expenses. Studies supporting public-private partnerships (PPP) and stakeholder involvement, on the other hand, contend that cooperative models can help to fund trash recycling infrastructure and encourage environmental compliance28. Low public awareness, opposition from industrial players, and the lack of financial incentives, however, impede the broad acceptance of PPP models in developing nations.

Prior research on leather waste management has primarily adopted fragmented approaches, either focusing exclusively on technological innovations or regulatory frameworks. Technological-focused studies often propose advanced treatment methods such as membrane filtration and enzymatic hydrolysis, which although promising, have limited practical application in developing countries due to economic and infrastructural constraints1,25. Conversely, regulatory-focused studies typically highlight enforcement issues and compliance challenges without integrating technological and operational dimensions16,26.

From a standpoint of decision-making, several optimization strategies have been used to examine and raise waste management efficiency. Widely used Multi-Criteria Decision-Making (MCDM) techniques like the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP)29,30, Decision-Making Trial and Evaluation Laboratory (DEMATEL)31, and BWM have ranked the relevance of waste management elements based on expert judgments19,32,33. Although these approaches offer insightful analysis, they fall short in capturing the interactions among waste management elements, therefore resulting in inadequate priority. Comparably, research using MICMAC analysis and ISM divide waste management elements into various degrees of effect, so enabling legislators to grasp the hierarchical structure of important problems22. These research, however, lack connection with expert-driven preference ranking models like BWM, which restricts the practical relevance of their conclusions in actual decision-making.

Identified research gaps

The literature still shows notable gaps even with the important contributions past studies provide. Lack of sector-specific attention on Bangladesh’s leather sector, which has particular environmental issues like high chromium contamination levels, insufficient waste disposal facilities, and weak regulatory compliance, is one major constraint3. Although broad knowledge is provided by general waste management studies, they overlook the particular difficulties encountered by tannery businesses in Bangladesh, where inadequate adherence to international waste management guidelines aggravates environmental damage.

Furthermore, earlier studies generally apply either ISM-MICMAC or BWM models in isolation, therefore neglecting the possible advantages of integrating structural dependency analysis with preference-based factor ranking34. This leads to a disjointed knowledge of waste management objectives as BWM gives ranking but lacks dependability analysis while ISM-MICMAC offers insights into factor interactions but does not efficiently rank them. Thus, a hybrid model combining both approaches is needed to produce a more complete decision-making structure.

The little participation of industry players, legislators, and authorities in the decision-making process adds even another important gap in current studies. Although earlier research examined waste management from an academic or technological standpoint, they sometimes neglect to include actual knowledge from tannery operators, environmental authorities, and supply chain players35. Implementing sustainable waste management techniques at an industrial level is challenging because of this gap between theory and reality.

Finally, most current studies lack scalable, long-term policy suggestions in line with world sustainability targets like the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)36. Rather than thorough policy frameworks that combine waste management, resource efficiency, and circular economy ideas37, most studies concentrate on short-term remedies such incremental technical improvements or localized regulatory initiatives. Development of waste management models that are economically feasible, ecologically sustainable, and socially inclusive depends on filling up this knowledge vacuum.

How this study bridges the gap

This paper suggests a new hybrid framework combining ISM-MICMAC and BWM techniques to overcome these# obstacles, therefore offering a methodical and ordered means of investigating important elements in leather waste management. This work improves the accuracy of waste management decision-making by integrating structural dependency analysis (ISM-MICMAC) with expert-driven prioritizing using BWM, therefore guaranteeing a more complete assessment of interdependencies and priorities.

Unlike other studies that concentrate on broad waste management solutions, this study explicitly addresses important issues such chromium pollution, regulatory enforcement loopholes, and financial restraints encountered by SMEs by contextualizing its results especially for the Bangladeshi leather sector. This study promises its results are realistically relevant and actionable by include waste management practitioners, legislators, and industry experts.

Furthermore, the study provides scalable suggestions in line with worldwide sustainability projects, therefore helping with long-term waste management planning. These comprise methods to support public-private partnerships in waste management infrastructure development, financial incentives for sustainable activities, and legislative proposals for acceptance of the circular economy. This paper attempts to offer a scientifically robust and policy-relevant framework for sustainable waste management in the Bangladeshi leather industry by bridging these research gaps.

Research methodology

This paper investigates the important elements affecting leather waste management in Bangladesh using a hybrid decision-making technique combining ISM, MICMAC, and the BWM. The methodological framework is meant to find important influencing elements, examine their interdependence, rank them depending on expert opinion, and create an empirically validated decision-making model that can direct industry players and legislators in creating sensible waste management plans. Comprising data collecting, factor identification, ISM model creation, MICMAC analysis, and BWM implementation as seen in Fig. 1, the research follows a methodical methodology.

Fig. 1Flow chart of methodology.

Flow chart of methodology.

Using a mixed-method approach—combining primary data collecting via expert surveys with secondary data analysis via a thorough literature review—we ensured a complete and representative dataset. Peer-reviewed journal publications, government reports, industry white papers, and past studies on waste management in the leather industry comprised the literature study. This review produced an initial list of nineteen salient features. Structured questionnaires were sent to twenty-one industry experts from tannery sectors, environmental regulatory authorities, and academia to help to hone and validate these elements. Along with their direct participation in waste disposal, treatment, or regulatory compliance within the leather industry, the expert selection was based on their least ten years of expertise in leather waste management, environmental policy, or sustainable industrial practices. These authorities stood for a fair mix of industry personnel, research institutes, and government departments.

Three main duties comprised the survey process: assessing the relevance and importance of every found component; recommending more variables depending on industry expertise; and offering paired comparisons for BWM analysis23. Expert validation guided the first set of nineteen criteria—which were then reduced to twelve important elements for additional investigation. These elements were then ranked in respect of relevance using the BWM. By lowering consistency in pairwise comparisons and thereby needing less comparisons overall, BWM is a resilient multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) strategy that outperforms conventional ranking systems such the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP)38–40. Whereas BWM guarantees more consistency and dependability30,41, AHP calls for many pairwise comparisons, which raises the possibility of discrepancy in expert opinions42,43.

Initially, an extensive literature review was conducted to identify potential factors influencing leather waste management, resulting in 19 factors. To refine these factors, structured questionnaires were distributed to a panel of 21 industry experts, selected based on their extensive experience (minimum of 10 years) in the field. Experts evaluated the relevance of each factor using a Likert scale, narrowing the list down to 12 critical factors based on significance scores.

The BWM was subsequently applied to prioritize these critical factors quantitatively. Experts first identified the most influential (best) and least influential (worst) factors. They then performed pairwise comparisons between the best and worst factors against all others using a structured questionnaire. Excel Solver was used to solve the BWM optimization model, deriving robust and consistent factor weights based on expert inputs.

The ISM approach was then employed to establish hierarchical relationships among these prioritized factors44. Experts constructed a Structural Self-Interaction Matrix (SSIM) to capture direct influences between factors using directional symbols (V, A, X, O). This SSIM was converted into an Initial Reachability Matrix (IRM) using binary notation, and transitivity was applied to generate a Final Reachability Matrix (FRM), clearly indicating direct and indirect relationships45,46.

We performed a MICMAC study to further improve the factor categorization. By use of its driving and dependent power classification, MICMAC analysis aids in the identification of strategic variables for intervention47. MICMAC is more appropriate for decision-making situations where causal links are of main relevance than other factor analysis methods including principal component analysis (PCA), which concentrates on dimensionality reduction48. Plotting the elements on a MICMAC scatter diagram, the four categories were autonomous factors (low driving and low dependence), dependent factors (low driving and high dependence), linkage factors (high driving and high dependence), and driving factors (high driving and low dependence)49. These categories helped to prioritize policy and strategic projects since driving causes call for focused effort to produce systematic improvements in leather waste management50.

The study by Qureshi et al. complements recent research on Lean 4.0 and Industry 4.0 integration by applying ISM-MICMAC and BWM to identify and prioritize critical factors for sustainable waste management, particularly in resource-constrained SME contexts like Bangladesh’s leather industry51. Another study aligns with existing research that uses ISM and MICMAC to explore interrelationships among barriers to lean implementation, extending this approach to leather waste management in Bangladeshi SMEs to identify key drivers and dependencies for sustainable industrial practices52. The another study by Yadav et al. complements the growing discourse on Net Zero Supply Chains by adopting BWM and MICMAC to prioritize critical enablers—similar to how our work applies these methods to leather waste management—highlighting the strategic role of sustainability, technology integration, and regulatory alignment in advancing industrial environmental goals53. These three studies provide theoretical grounding for our work by demonstrating the effectiveness of ISM, MICMAC, and BWM in modeling complex industrial challenges, prioritizing sustainability factors, and guiding strategic decision-making in resource-constrained manufacturing contexts.

Throughout the investigation, ethical issues remained under constant attention54. The study goal was explained to all expert participants, and their answers were kept anonymous to avoid biassed responses. Still, some constraints have to be accepted. Twenty-one experts’ sample size can restrict generalizability, and expert opinion adds some subjective bias in ISM-MICMAC evaluations. Moreover, the ISM-MICMAC structure is fixed, so it does not consider changing industrial policies or newly adopted environmental rules. Larger datasets, dynamic simulation techniques, and fuzzy logic models may all be included into future research to increase methodological accuracy and flexibility to fit changing industrial situations.

Final selection of 12 factors: expert evaluation and justification

Data were collected in two stages. First, a comprehensive literature review identified 19 potential factors influencing leather waste management. Second, these factors were evaluated by 21 experts (academia, industry, policymakers, NGOs) through structured questionnaires and follow-up interviews. A two-round Delphi procedure was applied to refine the list, using a 70% consensus threshold for retaining factors. This process reduced the set to 12 final factors.

To ensure robustness, we assessed the reliability of expert ratings using Cronbach’s alpha (0.82), which indicates high internal consistency. Descriptive statistics (mean scores and standard deviations) were used to confirm the relative importance and clarity of each factor before proceeding to ISM–MICMAC and BWM modeling. The diverse expert panel and iterative validation process guarantee that the dataset is both representative and grounded in practical realities.

The decision of 12 key factors from the initial 19 was based on expert input from 21 professionals in the leather industry, environmental organisations, and academia. Experts used a Likert scale from 1 to 5 to rate how important and influential each element was. Higher scores meant that the element was more important. Sustainability (4.8), recycling (4.6), and regulatory compliance (4.4) were the most essential factors, as shown in Table 3, because they all play a role in managing trash efficiently.

Application of best-worst method

Designed to increase the accuracy and efficiency of decision analysis while lowering the number of pairwise comparisons needed, by reducing judgment discrepancies and thereby attaining strong decision support, BWM offers more consistency and dependability than conventional MCDM techniques as Fuzzy AHP, AHP, and DEMATEL24. Starting with the validation of decision criteria based on a comprehensive literature review and expert consultation, the use of BWM in this study proceeded under a methodical procedure.

Experts then determined the most important—best—as well as least significant—worst—criteria affecting leather waste management. After that, decision-makers scored the best criterion against all others on a 1 to 9 scale, where 1 denotes equal significance and 9 denotes much higher relevance. Likewise, a decision vector was developed to rate the worst criterion against other criteria, therefore guaranteeing a methodical prioritizing procedure. The final step involved optimizing the weight distribution by solving a linear programming model, where the goal was to minimize the maximum absolute differences between weights to achieve optimal consistency74. These weightings formed the basis for factor prioritization in the ISM and MICMAC analyses, ensuring that the most influential and interdependent factors in leather waste management were accurately ranked and analyzed.

ISM model development

The ISM process develops a hierarchical structure to analyze the interrelationships among factors in a system. It follows five sequential steps: first, key factors influencing the system are identified. Second, the Structural Self-Interaction Matrix (SSIM) is created to define the direction and dependency among factors. Third, the Initial Reachability Matrix (IRM) is developed as a binary representation of direct relationships. Fourth, the FRM is derived, incorporating both direct and indirect influences. The last step in leather waste management is level partitioning, which creates a structured hierarchy by classifying variables according to their effect and reliance.

Development of SSIM

MICMAC diagram

The next step after the ISM analysis is the MICMAC analysis, which gives us a better understanding of how the system’s parts depend on each other and how they work together. The MICMAC diagram shows these linkages in a way that makes them easy to see. It divides components into four different quadrants based on how much they depend on and affect each other.

Quadrant I (Autonomous Factors): These factors have minimal influence on others and are not significantly influenced themselves, indicating weak driving and dependency power. In this study, no factor was classified as autonomous. Quadrant II (Dependent Factors): These factors have low to moderate influence on other factors but are highly influenced by them, signifying strong dependence and weak driving power. In this study, factors 11 and 12 (Advancements in leather solid waste treatment, improved biological methods for bio-degradation of dyes) were identified as dependent factors.

Quadrant III (Linked Factors): These factors exhibit strong driving and dependency power, meaning they influence many other factors while also being significantly influenced themselves. In this study, factors 2 to 10 (Reuse and Recycling of Leather Waste, Regulatory Compliance, Proper Facility for Eliminating Unused Chemicals, Lack of Trained Professionals, Use of Residuals as Organic Manure, Expertise and Experience of a Leather Waste Management Provider, Controlled Use of Chemicals, Proper Modification/Upgradation of Existing Equipment, Controlled Utilization of Chromium) were identified as linked factors and positioned in this quadrant. Quadrant IV (Independent Factors): These factors have high influence on others but are not significantly influenced themselves, indicating strong driving power with weak dependency. In this study, factor 1 (Use of Sustainable and Environmentally Friendly Practices) was identified as an independent factor.

Results analysis and discussion

The integrated strategy created helpful hierarchical structures and connections between important components. The BWM results showed that sustainable practices were the main driver, with large weights meaning that they had big effects on both policy and industry. The ISM hierarchical framework made it evident that sustainability policies affect compliance with regulations, recycling activities, and technological progress.

The ISM model’s visual representation made things very clear. At the base level, it showed “Use of Sustainable and Environmentally Friendly Practices,” which had an impact on mid-tier linking variables like “Regulatory Compliance” and “Reuse and Recycling of Leather Waste.” At the top level, dependent variables like “Advancements in Leather Solid Waste Treatment” were greatly affected by the factors that came before them. The MICMAC analysis confirmed these connections even more. It put most of the components into the “linkage variable” category, which means they have a lot of driving and dependent power. This shows how interrelated good waste management is.

These results show that waste management strategies need to take a more integrated and comprehensive approach. They also show that legislation and technology advances that focus on sustainability are key to making long-term changes. This analytical representation makes things clearer and helps people understand better how to make focused, successful policy changes.

Interrelationships among factors

By means of a hierarchical framework that clarifies their cause-and- effect linkages, the ISM analysis revealed the structural interdependence among the chosen elements. The results show that “Use of sustainable and environmentally friendly practices” is a basic driver, directly affecting “Controlled use of chemicals” and “Advancements in leather solid waste treatment”. This result emphasizes the critical part sustainability plays in determining chemical management techniques and waste treatment advances.

Furthermore, the knowledge and experience of waste management companies was out to be a dependent element mostly shaped by the availability of qualified experts. This interdependence emphasizes how urgently technical training in the leather sector and skill development initiatives must be developed to guarantee effective waste management techniques. The organized hierarchy created by ISM guarantees that policy choices and resource allocation are based on scientifically derived interrelationships instead of ad hoc assumptions, therefore offering an empirical basis for focused actions.

Driving and dependence power: insights from MICMAC analysis

Deeper understanding of the variables’ systemic impact was provided by the MICMAC study, which classified them according on their driving and dependent power. High driving power indicators of factors like “regulatory compliance” and “reuse and recycling of leather waste” point to their essential facilitators of efficient waste management. Their great impact on several other elements implies that enhancing rules and increasing recycling programs will help to bring about general changes in the waste management system.

On the other hand, “Advancements in leather solid waste treatment” and “Improved biological methods for bio-degradation of dyes” were found to be highly dependent variables, meaning that their development depends on the successful implementation of upstream interventions such sustainability practices and regulatory enforcement. Strong linkage variables—including “Proper facility for the elimination of unused chemicals,” “Lack of trained professionals,” and “Controlled use of chemicals”—further highlight the linked character of the system and suggest that neglect of these components holistically could impede general progress.

Prioritization of factors using BWM

By use of expert opinions, the BWM enabled quantitative prioritizing of elements, therefore permitting an objective weight assignment reflecting actual industry concerns. With weight of 0.2168, “Use of Sustainable and Environmentally Friendly Practices” placed top and confirmed its main influence in forming a good waste management system.

Emphasizing the requirement of technological advances and increased material recovery techniques, other high-priority criteria were “Reuse and Recycling of Leather Waste” (0.1414) and “Proper Modification/Upgrading of Existing Equipment” (0.1194). Although regulatory compliance is vital, it was given a modest weight (0.0597), meaning that although it helps, its influence depends on more general industry acceptance of sustainable practices. The weighted rankings from BWM provide a clear structure for prioritizing policy actions, therefore guaranteeing that resources are deliberately directed toward high-impact regions rather than being dispersed evenly over lower-impact issues.

It is noteworthy that the relative weights of several factors are numerically close. This outcome is not unexpected, as many sustainability-related drivers in leather waste management are inherently interdependent and jointly critical. In practice, this implies that decision-makers should treat these factors as complementary levers rather than as isolated priorities. Importantly, the integration of ISM–MICMAC with BWM helps mitigate ambiguity: even when weights appear close, the structural hierarchy identifies which factors act as key drivers (e.g., F-1, F-3) and which are more dependent (e.g., F-11, F-12). Thus, the results remain actionable for policy and strategy formulation.

Significance and novelty of findings

This work combines ISM, MICMAC, and BWM techniques to offer a fresh, ordered approach to leather waste management. Unlike conventional studies that concentrate just on regulatory or environmental concerns, this study uses a multi-criteria decision-making framework including both quantitative prioritizing methods and qualitative expert views. With sustainability identified as the main driver and regulatory compliance and recycling programs classified as high-influence elements, industry and policy changes have a data-based basis. Moreover, the acknowledgement of technology developments as dependent factors indicates the necessity of higher R&D funding to convert sustainable objectives into concrete business operations.

Implications of the study

For leather waste management in Bangladesh and beyond, the results of this study have important theoretical, pragmatic, and policy ramifications. This study offers a methodical, data-driven framework for comprehending the intricate interactions among important waste management variables by including ISM, MICMAC, and BWM approaches. These results have ramifications for academic debate, business operations, and legislative policymaking as well as for waste management, therefore promoting a more sustainable and effective system.

Theoretical contributions

This study advances the literature on sustainable waste management in several ways. First, it integrates ISM, MICMAC analysis, and the BWM within a single framework. While prior studies often rely on one technique, this combined approach offers a richer understanding by linking structural interdependencies with relative priorities. Second, the study extends research on leather waste management—an area that has received limited scholarly attention compared with other manufacturing industries—by empirically identifying twelve validated factors and positioning them in a hierarchical structure. Third, the clarified operational definitions of these factors provide a conceptual foundation for future studies that wish to compare across sectors or national contexts. Together, these contributions broaden theoretical insights into how multi-criteria decision-making tools can be applied to complex sustainability challenges in developing-country contexts.

Practical implications

The findings also provide actionable insights for policymakers and industry stakeholders in the leather sector. The prioritization of “Use of Sustainable and Environmentally Friendly Practices” (F-1) and “Policy Implementation and Monitoring” (F-3) as foundational drivers highlights the importance of regulatory enforcement and green production standards. At the operational level, “Stakeholder Awareness and Participation” (F-6) emerges as a mid-tier enabler, suggesting that investments in awareness campaigns, skills development, and participatory initiatives are essential to activate other enablers such as infrastructure (F-4) and technology adoption (F-9). Technological advancements, particularly in effluent treatment plants (F-11) and solid waste conversion (F-12), are positioned as dependent outcomes in the hierarchy, meaning that they will only succeed if upstream policy, awareness, and financial support mechanisms are effectively in place. For decision-makers, this sequencing provides a roadmap for resource allocation, capacity building, and policy design. Moreover, the framework can support the design of targeted incentives, strengthen public–private partnerships, and guide donor agencies in prioritizing interventions for sustainable industrial development.

By combining methodological innovation with applied relevance, this study bridges the gap between theory and practice in sustainable waste management. At the same time, it recognizes limitations—such as reliance on expert judgment and the country-specific scope—that future research can address through cross-industry applications and comparative studies.

Conclusion

This study provides Bangladesh a complete plan for better managing leather waste by combining three strong methods: BWM, ISM, and MICMAC analysis. We started by looking at the literature and getting comments from experts. Then, we narrowed down the 19 factors to 12 critical ones using expert judgement. BWM was used to rank these criteria based on expert opinion. The most important elements for effective waste management were “Use of Sustainable and Environmentally Friendly Practices,” “Reuse and Recycling of Leather Waste,” and “Modification of Existing Equipment.” The ISM method organised these components into a multi-level hierarchy that made the links between them clearer and helped people comprehend how they affect each other. MICMAC analysis put the components into three groups: driver, linkage, and dependant, based on how much they affected and depended on each other. This helped policymakers and businesses make decisions.

Using BWM, ISM, and MICMAC together not only made sure that the methods were sound, but it also gave useful information that was particular to the problems faced by the Bangladeshi leather sector. The results show how important sustainability programs, strict rules, new technology, and educating workers are for managing waste in a way that is both effective and good for the environment. Policymakers, regulators, and industry stakeholders can use this integrated model to help them decide what measures to take first, how to best use their resources, and how to carry out focused interventions. The framework can also be used in other industries that are dealing with comparable sustainability issues, which makes it a big step forward in the conversation about sustainable industrial practices.

Limitations and future research directions

There are a number of limitations in this study’s results that could affect their validity and ability to be applied to other situations. Even though the expert judgment is well constructed, it may be biased because the sample size is small and not all key stakeholder groups may be represented. Also, static modeling methods like ISM and MICMAC don’t take into consideration how dynamic industrial environments are, where rules, technology, and market conditions are always changing.

To deal with these issues, future research should include a wider range of specialists and use dynamic modeling methods like system dynamics or agent-based modeling. Cross-country or cross-industry studies could also help prove that the framework can be used in a variety of situations. Also, putting the study’s suggestions into action and keeping an eye on them in real life will be very important to see how well they work in practice and make changes based on what happens.
