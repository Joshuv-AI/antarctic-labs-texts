Refractory Bacterial Meningitis Associated with an Occult Posterior Fossa Dermal Sinus Tract in an Infant: A Case Report
Authors: Rong Zhuang, Chao Liang, Jia-ping Jin, Bo Jin, Ming-ming Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPosterior fossa dermal sinus tracts are extremely rare congenital lesions that may extend intracranially and communicate with the dura or brain tissue. This abnormal connection can provide a persistent route for bacterial entry into the central nervous system, predisposing patients to severe infections such as meningitis. We report the case of a 1-year-old boy with refractory bacterial meningitis that remained unresponsive to prolonged broad-spectrum antibiotic treatment. MRI revealed an occult posterior fossa lesion, and cerebrospinal fluid NGS detected multiple microorganisms of uncertain clinical significance, none of which was considered the definitive causative pathogen. Intraoperatively, a sinus tract was found communicating with an intracranial cystic lesion, subsequently confirmed as an infected dermoid cyst on histopathology. After surgical excision and postoperative intraventricular antimicrobial therapy, the patient showed sustained improvement with no recurrence at 6-month follow-up. This case suggests that an occult posterior fossa dermal sinus tract may represent an underrecognized anatomical source of treatment-resistant meningitis and highlights the importance of early structural evaluation when standard antimicrobial therapy fails. Timely surgical intervention may be important for source control and prevention of recurrence.

Introduction

Bacterial meningitis is a severe central nervous system infection associated with high mortality, particularly in low-income countries, where the fatality rate can reach 54%. Most cases arise from hematogenous dissemination of pathogens or direct extension from adjacent infectious foci.1 However, a subset of recurrent or refractory infections is associated with underlying structural abnormalities that allow persistent microbial entry into the intracranial compartment.2 Such conditions are clinically important because they may not respond adequately to antimicrobial therapy alone.

Pilonidal sinus is conventionally reserved for a sacrococcygeal defect without communication to the central nervous system (CNS). In contrast, dermal sinus tracts are congenital epithelial-lined tracts arising from incomplete disjunction of cutaneous ectoderm and neuroectoderm, which may extend to the dura, subarachnoid space, or brain parenchyma, and are frequently associated with dermoid or epidermoid cysts.3,4 Dermoid cysts contain skin appendages such as hair follicles and sebaceous glands, whereas epidermoid cysts are lined solely by squamous epithelium without such adnexal structures.5 Unlike sacrococcygeal pilonidal sinus, an intracranial dermal sinus tract provides a direct anatomical route for recurrent bacterial seeding of the CNS. Posterior fossa dermal sinus tract with an intracranial dermoid cyst is exceedingly unusual. In one such case, an unrecognized occipital dermal sinus in an infant led to intracranial infection only after a posterior fossa dermoid became symptomatic, underscoring how easily this entity can be overlooked.6

Here, we report a rare case of refractory bacterial meningitis associated with an occult posterior fossa dermal sinus tract in an infant, highlighting the importance of considering occult congenital anatomical abnormalities in children with persistent or treatment-resistant meningitis. A subtle occipital skin lesion, easily mistaken for a benign birthmark or dimple, should prompt early cranial imaging, while definitive source control typically requires timely surgical intervention rather than antimicrobial therapy alone.

Case Presentation

A 1-year-old boy was transferred to our hospital with a 13-day history of intermittent fever. Before referral, he had undergone cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination at a local hospital, which showed findings suggestive of bacterial meningitis, including a CSF glucose concentration of 2.27 mmol/L, a protein level of 0.432 g/L, and a white blood cell count of 1820/µL. Empirical antimicrobial therapy with meropenem (40 mg/kg/dose, intravenous, every 8 hours) and linezolid (10 mg/kg/dose, intravenous, every 8 hours) had already been initiated before transfer. On admission, physical examination revealed subcutaneous nodules in the occipital region with a history of recurrent redness and swelling. No visible cutaneous sinus opening or discharge was observed, and no abnormalities were noted in the sacrococcygeal region. Kernig’s and Brudzinski’s signs were negative.

Despite continuation of antimicrobial treatment after admission, the patient remained febrile, with a peak temperature of 40°C on hospital day 1. Repeat CSF examination showed worsening pleocytosis, with a nucleated cell count of 2447/µL. On hospital day 2, both blood and CSF cultures were negative, but the next-generation sequencing (NGS) of the CSF detected multiple microorganisms, including Prevotella bivia (sequence count: 180), Corynebacterium diphtheriae (sequence count: 5), and human herpesvirus 5 (sequence count: 2702). Given the initial diagnosis of bacterial meningitis, the existing regimen of meropenem plus linezolid was continued. However, no substantial clinical improvement was observed after 8 days of combined therapy spanning the pre-admission and post-admission periods. Linezolid was therefore replaced with vancomycin (15 mg/kg/dose, intravenous, every 6 hours) in an attempt to optimize antimicrobial coverage. On hospital day 8, the CSF nucleated cell count decreased to 316/µL, suggesting partial laboratory improvement. Nevertheless, the patient continued to experience recurrent fever. On hospital day 13, vancomycin was discontinued because of a rash, and drug fever was considered in the differential diagnosis. By hospital day 23, fever persisted and the CSF nucleated cell count had risen again to 760/µL. Because of the inadequate response to treatment, the antimicrobial regimen was adjusted to linezolid plus rifampicin (10 mg/kg/dose, intravenous, every 12 hours). On hospital day 29, the CSF nucleated cell count remained elevated at 524/µL, indicating that the infection was not fully controlled. Given the prolonged disease course and inadequate response to multiple antimicrobial regimens, an underlying anatomical source of infection was strongly suspected. Multidisciplinary consultation, together with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), revealed a posterior fossa dermal sinus tract as the likely focus of persistent infection (Figure 1). Specifically, MRI demonstrated a cystic lesion in the right cerebellopontine angle region with ring enhancement of the cystic wall, together with a dermal sinus tract extending posteriorly from the lesion toward the occipital skin surface. Preoperative re-examination confirmed that the occipital nodule remained unchanged from admission, without recurrent redness, swelling, or discharge. On hospital day 31, the patient underwent posterior cranial fossa lesion resection. Intraoperatively, a subcutaneous cord-like sinus tract was identified traversing the skull into the cranial cavity. The distal end of the tract formed a well-encapsulated cystic dilation intracranially, with clear borders and no adhesion to the adjacent brain tissue. Complete excision revealed cheese-like sebaceous material admixed with abundant hair strands. Histopathological examination showed pale yellow lipid-like material within the cyst and extensive acute and chronic inflammatory cell infiltration, consistent with an infected dermoid cyst (Figure 2). After surgery, the patient developed hydrocephalus and required an external ventricular drain. Intraventricular vancomycin (20mg per dose, once daily) was administered, together with dexamethasone (1 mg per dose, once daily), to achieve high local antimicrobial concentrations and anti-inflammatory effects. By hospital day 44, the patient’s temperature had stabilized, and the CSF nucleated cell count had markedly decreased to 31/µL (Figure 3). Sustained clinical and laboratory improvement following surgical excision further supported the lesion as a likely primary source of infection. Subsequent monitoring showed normalization of clinical and laboratory parameters. The drainage device was removed on hospital day 52. The patient was discharged in stable condition on hospital day 59. At the 6-month follow-up, in addition to the absence of meningitis recurrence, audiological assessment revealed no sensorineural hearing loss. There were no clinical signs of recurrent hydrocephalus after external ventricular drain removal, and follow-up cranial imaging showed no significant ventricular enlargement. Discussion

Posterior fossa dermal sinus tracts causing meningitis in infancy are rarely reported. Dermal sinus tracts most commonly occur in the lumbosacral region, accounting for approximately 32–54% of cases, whereas cervical or occipital locations are considerably less frequent.3 Kanev and Salazar described the case of an infected subtorcular sinus tract presenting as posterior fossa subdural abscess with hydrocephalus, in which polymicrobial cultures implicated the sinus tract as the infectious source rather than hematogenous seeding.6 Notably, a recently reported pediatric case was associated with recurrent meningitis, suggesting a potential link between posterior fossa dermal sinus tract and persistent intracranial infection. However, the lack of detailed follow-up data has limited understanding of its pathogenic role.7 Regardless of anatomical location, dermal sinus tracts are frequently mistaken for benign cutaneous dimples, as illustrated by a lumbosacral case initially dismissed as a benign dimple that progressed to E. coli meningitis at 4 months of age.3

Given the negative microbiological cultures, the diagnosis of bacterial meningitis relied on clinical and CSF criteria, including markedly elevated pleocytosis, decreased glucose, and elevated protein. Despite more than three weeks of sequential antimicrobial therapy, CSF inflammation persisted with only transient improvement, and the patient was considered clinically refractory, prompting further structural investigation. In the present case, the infection persisted despite prolonged and appropriate antimicrobial therapy, with only transient improvement. Sustained clinical resolution was achieved only after surgical excision of the lesion, suggesting the role of the sinus tract as a continuous source of infection. Nonetheless, in the absence of direct microbiological evidence from the lesion, this causal relationship remains inferential rather than definitively established. The excised cyst and sinus tract tissue were not submitted for bacterial, anaerobic, or fungal culture, which represents a notable limitation of this report and precludes definitive microbiological confirmation of the causative organism. From a clinical perspective, this case highlights important diagnostic implications. In patients with meningitis unresponsive to appropriate antimicrobial therapy, underlying structural abnormalities should be actively considered, particularly when atypical features are present, such as recurrent fever, fluctuating cerebrospinal fluid parameters, or occipital cutaneous abnormalities. Early MRI is critical for identifying occult dermal sinus tracts. In addition, the interpretation of NGS results requires caution. Detection of multiple microorganisms does not necessarily indicate causality and should be assessed in the clinical context.8,9 Here, the low sequence counts for P. bivia and C. diphtheriae likely reflected contamination or colonization rather than infection. Although the sequence count for cytomegalovirus was comparatively higher, the CSF profile was inconsistent with typical viral meningoencephalitis. In this case, definitive improvement after surgical resection suggests that source control, rather than antimicrobial escalation alone, was the key determinant of treatment success. Nonetheless, this study has certain limitations. As this report describes a single case, the generalizability of these findings is limited. Although the 6-month follow-up period was sufficient to demonstrate the absence of short-term recurrence, longer-term observation would be valuable to exclude delayed recurrence or late-onset complications associated with the sinus tract.

In conclusion, posterior fossa dermal sinus tract should be considered a rare but possible contributing anatomical source of refractory meningitis in infants. Early recognition and timely surgical intervention may facilitate effective management.
