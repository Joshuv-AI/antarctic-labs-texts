Superpotentials, flat bands and the role of Quantum Geometry for the superfluid stiffness
T. Bauch, F. Lombardi, G. Seibold

Abstract
We investigate the stability and enhancement of superconductivity in a system subject to a 2D periodic superpotential. This externally imposed modulation leads to a reconstructed multi-band structure with flat bands emerging in certain energy regions. When the chemical potential is tuned into one of these flat bands, the associated increase in the density of states can strongly enhance the superconducting critical temperature T c compared to the homogeneous case while maintaining the superfluid stiffness D s D_{s} at a sizeable value. We also clarify the role of the quantum geometric effect for flat bands in such topologically trivial systems. While the overall paramagnetic interband contribution to the stiffness is negative we identify a class of certain interband scattering processes which enhance D s D_{s} and scale with the interaction, playing therefore the same role as quantum geometric effects in conventional flat band models. The enhancement of superconductivity is found to be robust against weak to moderate disorder, suggesting that the system is not overly sensitive to imperfections which is an important prerequisite for experimental realizations. Our results demonstrate that engineered superpotentials offer a compelling alternative to twist-based moiré systems, providing a pathway toward tailoring superconductivity via lithographic design.



I
Introduction

In two-dimensional materials, the use of twisted layers, most notably in graphene, at specific “magic angles” gives rise to flat electronic bands. These flat bands emerge from a superpotential generated by the moiré interference pattern between the layers, and they host a rich variety of correlated phenomena including superconductivity.

One striking prediction, originally discussed by Volovik and others

, is that in flat bands the critical temperature
T_{c}
of superconductivity can scale linearly with the superconducting interaction strength, rather than being exponentially suppressed as in conventional BCS systems in a weak coupling regime. This suggests a route to strongly enhanced superconductivity. However, a major puzzle arises: flat bands have vanishing kinetic energy, corresponding to an infinite effective mass for Cooper pairs and, consequently, negligible superfluid stiffness—implying that superconductivity should not be robust, if it occurs at all. Recent theoretical advances
toermae15
;
toermae16
;
toermae17
resolve this paradox by invoking quantum geometry contribution, specifically the quantum metric, which is the real part of the quantum geometric tensor, which originates an additional term to the superfluid weight. This geometric term can render the Cooper pair mass finite and restore a non vanishing stiffness, enabling superconductivity in otherwise dispersionless systems.

This raises a compelling question: can one replicate the effect of twisting—and the associated superpotential—through other means, such as applying an external or engineered periodic superpotential directly to a two dimensional system? By imposing such a potential, the system’s Brillouin zone is folded, leading to the formation of minibands. If these minibands become flat at high superpotential strenghts, one could in principle recover the same ingredients critical to moiré-based superconductivity: enhanced density of states, increased
T_{c}
, and finite stiffness via quantum geometry. Crucially, this approach would not require physical twisting, opening the door to designer flat-band systems realized via electrostatic gating or nanostructured substrates in combination with epitaxial growth. A related proposal for enhancing T
c
is related to ’stripe-engineering’, where quasi-1D electronic structures are artificially created and induce so-called ’shape-resonances’ in the electronic spectrum

;
saini97
;
bianconi13
.

In this work, we explore this idea using a tight-binding model with an attractive interaction with a checkerboard superpotential and demonstrate that the engineered minibands can indeed mimic the flat-band behavior of twisted systems, offering a new paradigm for flat-band superconductivity driven by superpotentials.
Such structures can also be artificially engineered by e.g. gating a thin SC film grown on a nanopatterned substrate, cf. Fig.

III.2
Quantum geometric contribution

In Ref.
toermae15
it was shown that the superfluid weight in topologically non-trivial systems is related to the quantum metric and bounded from below by the Chern number in the isolated flat-band limit. According to this finding flat bands can sustain a finite superfluid stiffness when the Chern number is non-zero. In following investigations the lower bound to the stiffness was refined and shown to be related to a non-zero magnitude of the Berry curvature
|\Omega_{\alpha\beta}|

toermae17
. In fact, this is much stronger lower bound than the Chern number bound which is related to the integral of the Berry curvature. Thus, even flat bands in topologically trivial systems, like the Lieb lattice, can promote a finite superfluid stiffness
toermae16
;
nagaosa21
.

Therefore the question arises if quantum geometric effects also play a role for systems with ’quasi flat’ bands arising from a superpotential.
This can be checked by separating the stiffness
in a conventional (intraband)
contribution which becomes small in the flat band limit, and a geometric
contribution. The latter depends on the (real part of the) quantum metric tensor

g^{mn}_{\alpha\beta}=\langle n|\partial k_{\alpha}|m\rangle\langle m|\partial k_{\beta}|n\rangle

nagaosa21
which is associated with the
interband matrix elements of the current operator.
The geometric contribution therefore manifests as a superconductivity induced
spectral weight transfer in the optical conductivity of a multiband system, cf. appendix
C

IV
The ratio
\pi D_{s}/kT_{c}
and the influence of impurities

Within our ’mean-field’ BCS approach the critical temperature
kT_{c}
can be
associated with the pairing energy scale whereas
\pi D_{s}
, i.e. the weight in the
\delta
-function of

\sigma_{SC}(\omega)
[cf. Eq. (
25
] sets the scale for phase coherence.
In the weak-coupling BCS limit one therefore
always finds
\pi D_{s}/kT_{c}\gg 1
since phase coherence is already established when Cooper pairs
start to form. In the low-disorder regime where
the mean free path is much longer than the inverse Fermi wave vector,
k_{F}l\gg 1
, the stiffness slightly decreases due to the paramagnetic contribution while
Anderson’s theorem
anderson59
for the pairing of time-reversed exact eigenstates leaves
T_{c}
unchanged.
The situation changes when
k_{F}l\approx 1

and Cooper pairs can start to form above the temperature where phase coherence sets in,
cf. e.g. Fig. 3 in
mondal13
.
In this regime the stiffness becomes comparable
with
kT_{c}
eventually leading to a superconductor-insulator transition (
D_{s}=kT_{c}=0
) when disorder exceeds a critical value as e.g. observed in
TiN
,
InO_{x}
and
NbN
films
sac10
;
sac11
;
mondal11
.

Charge carriers in flat band systems are very susceptible toward localization
so that the stiffness, which depends inversely on the
mass, also sensitively depends on the amount of disorder when the strength of the superpotential
becomes sizeable.
In particular, one can expect that the limit

\pi D_{s}/kT_{c}\approx 1
is reached when the mean-free path becomes of the same order than the periodicity
of the superpotential.

In order to test this hypothesis we add to the hamiltonian Eq. (
1
) an impurity potential

V=\sum_{n\sigma}I_{n}n_{n\sigma}
where the
I_{n}
are drawn from a flat distribution with
-I_{0}\leq I_{n}\leq I_{0}
.

The resulting hamiltonian is then solved on finite lattices in mean-field using the Bogoljubov-de Gennes (BdG)
transformation

\displaystyle\omega_{k}v_{n}(k)

\displaystyle+\Delta^{*}_{n}u_{n}(k)\,,

V
Summary and Conclusions

The investigations in this paper have been inspired by the question if imposing artificial superpotentials, through gating or nanopatterned substrates, on otherwise homogeneous superconductors can enhance
T_{c}
via the formation of flat bands, and how robust this mechanism is
with regard to phase fluctuations.
We have shown that a substantial enhancement of the critical temperature can be induced by periodic gating while the stiffness keeps a sizeable value which scales with the interaction. While this property and the associated spectral weight transfer in the optical conductivity from normal to superconducting state resembles similar results due to quantum geometry, we have shown that the quantum geometric effect only partially accounts for the
stiffness in our system.
In fact, our periodically modulated system differs from ’conventional’ flat band models with regard to the
following aspects: (a) In the presence of a superpotential each of the minibands below the chemical potential contributes to the kinetic energy, whereas in flat band systems, as e.g. in twisted layers of graphene, the ’filled’ bands usually do not
contribute. Therefore, in the latter systems the diamagnetic response becomes zero when the band at

E_{F}
becomes completely flat, whereas in the folded band structure one can have a completely flat band at the chemical potential but a finite diamagnetic contribution due to the lower minibands.
As a consequence, in the ’conventional’ flat band systems a finite stiffness only arises due to interband scattering (the quantum geometric contribution) whereas in the presence of a superpotential the overall interband term is negative but contains a positive ’quantum geometric’ part.
Despite this difference the transfer
of spectral weight, cf. Fig.
9
, closely resembles what is found for the interband contribution in systems with a purely quantum geometric effect as e.g. in Ref.
nagaosa21
which, however, can
also be understood from a more conventional perspective, cf.
appendix
D
.

It is interesting that recent investigations on the periodic patterning of a 2D electron gas
naumis25
reveal a non-zero local Berry phase for antisymmetric triangular superpotentials. This geometry therefore could further sustain the superfluid stiffness, however, our present approach does not allow for the investigation of this possibility since a graphene-like superpotential cannot be imposed on a square lattice.

It should be noted that the model here differs from the system studied by Balseiro and Falicov
balseiro77

where CDW and SC originate from the same
interaction and therefore compete since the CDW
scattering depletes the DOS at the Fermi energy.
Here instead, the superpotential is imposed on the system via an external periodic potential so that the Fermi level can in principle be tuned into one of the flat bands. This leads to
an enhancement of
T_{c}
which at weak coupling can reach
one or two orders of magnitude, cf. inset to Fig.
2
b.
The influence of phase fluctuations is expected to impact
on this enhancement when the mean-free path in the disordered
system becomes of the same order than the periodicity
of the superlattice which corresponds to the limit
\pi D_{s}/kT_{c}\approx 1
. For strongly disordered NbN films
mondal13
it has been shown that
T_{c}
is reduced by a factor
\sim 3
when
this ratio decreases from
\pi D_{s}/kT_{c}\approx 5
to

\pi D_{s}/kT_{c}\approx 1
so that our results still are compatible with a significant enhancement of
T_{c}
.

However, there are still a few hurdles to overcome in order to realize such a system with increased transition temperature and robust stiffness. First, as we have seen the system should be sufficiently clean so that the stiffness still dominates the energy scale of the transition temperature. Second and more severe is the problem of secondary instabilities when the Fermi level is located in one of the flat bands. Besides higher order instabilities also transitions to some magnetic order can be easily realized when E
F
falls in a region with large DOS. The concomitant opening of additional gaps will then spoil the T
c
enhancement although the system may remain in a state with sizeable stiffness.
