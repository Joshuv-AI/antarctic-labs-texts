Distributional Treatment Effect Estimation across Heterogeneous Sites via Optimal Transport
Borna Bateni Department of Statistics & Data Science, University of California, Los Angeles, Los Angeles, CA, USA Yubai Yuan Department of Statistics, The Pennsylvania State University, University Park, PA, USA Qi Xu Department of Statistics & Data Science, Carnegie Mellon University, Pittsburgh, PA, USA and Annie Qu Department of Statistics & Applied Probability, University of California, Santa Barbara, Santa Barbara, CA, USA

Abstract
We propose a novel framework for synthesizing counterfactual treatment group data in a target site by integrating full treatment and control group data from a source site with control group data from the target. Departing from conventional average treatment effect estimation, our approach adopts a distributional causal inference perspective by modeling treatment and control as distinct probability measures on the source and target sites. We formalize the cross-site heterogeneity (effect modification) as a push-forward transformation that maps the joint feature–outcome distribution from the source to the target site. This transformation is learned by aligning the control group distributions between sites using an Optimal Transport–based procedure, and subsequently applied to the source treatment group to generate the synthetic target treatment distribution. Under general regularity conditions, we establish theoretical guarantees for the consistency and asymptotic convergence of the synthetic treatment group data to the true target distribution. Simulation studies across multiple data-generating scenarios and a real-world application to patient-derived xenograft data demonstrate that our framework robustly recovers the full distributional properties of treatment effects.



Keywords:
Causal Inference, Effect Modification, Metric Learning, Synthetic Data

1
Introduction

Adapting successful interventions from one experimental site to other sites is a critical challenge in experimental research and policy making. Researchers routinely examine past successful interventions and attempt to replicate their outcomes in other sites. Yet, the differences in site-specific characteristics, implementation protocols, and socio-economic environments can influence how a particular intervention could be implemented effectively in other sites. Overlooking these critical nuances could undermine the relevance and applicability of past experimental findings.

While conducting a fresh experiments remains the most reliable method for assessing the efficacy of an intervention in a new site, it is sometimes infeasible due to financial, logistical, and temporal limitations. Consequently, there is a pressing need for new methods that can extrapolate the outcomes of previous interventions to new contexts, without requiring full-scale replications.

Most existing work focuses on the transfer estimation of the
average treatment effects
(ATE).
Hotz et al. 2005
employ a matching method to estimate the ATE of a job training program in one site using data from previous implementations of similar programs in other sites.
Meager 2022
employs a Bayesian hierarchical model to generalize the effect of microcredits across seven different randomized control trials.
Adjaho and Christensen 2022
derive bounds on the ATE for a target experimental site using a Wasserstein neighborhoods of other comparable sites.
Menzel 2023
applies functional principal component analysis to transfer ATEs across sites. However, relying solely on the ATE could lead to misinterpretations regarding an intervention’s true benefits. Other distributional features of the treatment effects such as quantiles, variance and higher-order moments, and the heterogeneity of effects across different sub-populations are also crucial for a comprehensive evaluation. Therefore, studies suggest deeper exploration of the distribution of treatment effects for more accurate causal analysis
(
Heckman et al. 1997
;
Firpo and Ridder 2008
)
.

A less explored approach to address this problem is to synthesize the
full counterfactual data distribution
for a target site, using information from other sites. Synthetic data generation extends beyond the estimation of ATE only, encompassing inferences about the full distribution of data. The Synthetic Control method
(
Abadie and Gardeazabal 2003
;
Abadie et al. 2010
;
Abadie et al. 2015
)
exemplifies this idea by using pretreatment time series in a panel setup to generate synthetic counterfactuals. The present problem, however, lacks panel time series data, and calls for a method that instead employs cross-sectional data sourced from other sites to construct the counterfactual estimates.

The present research aims to generate synthetic data for treated units in a target site, using data from control units in the target site, and data from both treated and control units from a similar experiment at a source site. We develop a distributional framework that treats treatment and control as
probability measures
on the joint space of features and outcome in the source and target sites, and formalizes the cross-site heterogeneity (effect modification) as a
push-forward
transformation between sites. Our study uses concepts from optimal transport theory to learn the effect modification transformation by comparing the control distributions in the source and target sites. We then apply the learned transformation to push the treatment distribution from the source to the target site, thereby generating a synthetic sample in the target site that approximates the
entire
target treatment distribution.

Our contributions are as follows:

(i) Conceptual:
We formalize cross-site effect modification as a distributional push-forward map on the joint space, enabling inference on the full target treatment distribution rather than only the ATE.

(ii) Methodological:
We introduce an optimal transport–based procedure that aligns source and target controls, and then transports the source treatment distribution to the target. In doing so, we relax common assumptions made by previous research: we do
not
impose “unconfounded location” (no effect modification) and we do
not
require RCT-style equality of feature distributions across treatment and control. Instead, we assume a single cross-site map shared between treatment and control together with mild feature-alignment structure.

(iii) Empirical:
Across various simulation scenarios and in a real application, the proposed method yields accurate joint distributional reconstructions and maintains superior performance on marginal and distributional metrics relative to other baselines.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section
2
reviews distributional causal inference, Optimal Transport theory and metric learning, and introduces a new formalization for cross-site effect modification. Section
3
, presents the methodology, detailing its two phases of optimal transport-based learning and data synthesis, and theoretical properties. Section
4
reports simulation results. Section
5
, presents the application to the patient-derived xenograft (PDX) data, a real-world controlled dataset used for studying cancer treatment responses. Section
6
provides concluding remarks.

2
Background and Notations

2.1
Distributional Causal Inference

Classical causal inference
(
Neyman 1923
;
Rubin 1974
)
treats an intervention as a binary variable that partitions units into control and treatment groups, and seeks to compare the average potential outcomes across these groups. By contrast,
distributional
causal inference
(
Lin et al. 2023
)
takes the entire distribution of potential outcomes, rather than just the average, as the object of interest. Accordingly, interventions are considered probability measures reflecting different potential outcome distributions under treatment and control.

In this context, let
\mathcal{Z}:=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
denote the joint measurable space of features
X\in\mathcal{X}
, and outcome
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
. We write
\mathcal{W}:=\{\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\}
for the pair of Borel probability measures on
\mathcal{Z}
corresponding to control and treatment, respectively. Further, let
d_{\mathcal{Z}}
be a similarity metric on
\mathcal{Z}
, which extends these measure spaces into metric measure spaces
(\mathcal{Z},d_{\mathcal{Z}},\mu_{0})
and
(\mathcal{Z},d_{\mathcal{Z}},\mu_{1})
for control and treatment. For ease of notation, we use
(\mathcal{Z},d_{\mathcal{Z}},\{\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\})
to collectively represent these two metric measure spaces.

This framework generalizes the main challenges of the classical causal inference. For instance, the issue of
confounding variables

(
Pearl 1995
)
occurs when only a subset of features,
{\mathcal{X}}_{\text{obs.}}\subsetneq\mathcal{X}
is observed. Consequently, we observe the projected measures

\begin{matrix}{\mu}^{\text{obs.}}_{0}:\;A\longmapsto\mu_{0}((\mathcal{X}\setminus{\mathcal{X}}_{\text{obs.}})\times A)\\
{\mu}^{\text{obs.}}_{1}:\;A\longmapsto\mu_{1}((\mathcal{X}\setminus{\mathcal{X}}_{\text{obs.}})\times A)\end{matrix}\qquad\text{for any set }A\in\sigma({\mathcal{X}}_{\text{obs.}}\times\mathcal{Y}),

with
\mu_{0}
,
\mu_{1}
exhibiting significant distributional discrepancy compared to
{\mu}^{\text{obs.}}_{0}
,
{\mu}^{\text{obs.}}_{1}
.

2.1.1
The Effect Modification Problem

Another classic problem in causal inference is the
effect modification problem

(
Miettinen 1974
;
Rothman et al. 2008
;
Knol and VanderWeele 2012
)
. Effect modifiers
are characteristics that change the magnitude or direction of the effect of the intervention on the outcome across different circumstances. While the confounder problem is caused by insufficient information about the data and can be controlled by instruments or proxy variables, effect modifiers represent an inherent heterogeneity in effect that persists regardless of being observed or controlled for. As noted by Olli Miettinen, “In the face of the Lord, there is no confounding, but there is effect modification”
(
Knottnerus and Tugwell 2019
)
.

Given the distinction, in our framework we formalize effect modification as follows. Under two data spaces
(\mathcal{Z},d_{\mathcal{Z}},\{\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\})
and
(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime},d_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}},\{\mu_{0}^{\prime},\mu_{1}^{\prime}\})
, an effect modifier is a measurable map,
\varphi:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
that pushes forward the treatment and control measures from the source space,
\mathcal{Z}
, to the target space,
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
. Hence,
\mu_{0}^{\prime}=\varphi_{\#}{\mu_{0}}
, and
\mu_{1}^{\prime}=\varphi_{\#}{\mu_{1}}
, where
\varphi_{\#}{\mu}:A\mapsto\mu(\varphi^{-1}(A))
for
A\in\sigma(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime})
denotes the
push-forward
operator. Such representation highlights the nature of effect modification as a distributional transformation, as illustrated in Figure
1
.

Figure 1
:
Illustration of the effect modification between the measure spaces
(\mathcal{Z},\mu)
and
(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
. An arrow represents the mapping
\varphi:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
such that
\mu^{\prime}
is a push-forward measure of
\mu
along
\varphi
; that is, for all
A\in\sigma(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime})
, we have
\mu^{\prime}(A)=\mu(\varphi^{-1}(A))
.

2.2
Optimal Transport for Causal Inference

Optimal transport theory
(
Villani et al. 2009
)
provides a mathematical framework for comparing and analyzing metric measure spaces, making it particularly well-suited for distributional causal inference. Optimal Transport seeks to find an optimal
coupling
between two probability measures
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
defined on the Polish metric spaces
(\mathcal{Z},d_{Z})
and
(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime},d_{Z^{\prime}})
, respectively. A coupling
\pi
is a measure on the product space
\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
such that its projections onto each coordinate space yield the original measures:

\pi(A\times\mathcal{Z}^{\prime})=\mu(A)\;\text{, and }\;\pi(\mathcal{Z}\times A^{\prime})=\mu^{\prime}(A^{\prime})\quad\quad\text{for all}\quad A\in\sigma(\mathcal{Z})\text{, and }A^{\prime}\in\sigma(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}).

The family of all such couplings between two measures forms a
coupling space

\Pi(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
.

The classical optimal transport problem
(
Monge 1781
;
Kantorovich 1942
)
intends to identify the coupling between two measures,
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
on the same metric space
(\mathcal{Z},d_{Z})
which minimizes the p-Wasserstein distance between the two measures:

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{p}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}):

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\!\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})}\!\!\!\!\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\big[d_{Z}(z_{1},z_{2})^{{}^{p}}\big]^{\frac{1}{p}}=\!\!\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})}\left(\int_{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}}\!\!d_{Z}(z_{1},z_{2})^{{}^{p}}d\pi(z_{1},z_{2})\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

While being a powerful tool for comparing measures, the application of the Wasserstein distance is limited to cases where both measures are defined on the same metric space. To adress this, the p-Gromov-Wasserstein (GW) distance
(
Mémoli 2011
;
Mémoli 2014
)
, generalizes the Wasserstein distance to compare probability measures
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
on different metric spaces,
(\mathcal{Z},d_{Z})
and
(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime},d_{Z^{\prime}})
, respectively:

\displaystyle\mathbb{GW}_{p}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):

\displaystyle=\!\!\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\!\!\!\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\scalebox{.78}{$\displaystyle\bigg|$}d_{Z}(z_{1},z_{2})-d_{Z^{\prime}}(z^{\prime}_{1},z_{2}^{\prime})\scalebox{.78}{$\displaystyle\bigg|$}^{{}^{p}}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=\!\!\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\!\!\left(\int_{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\int_{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\scalebox{.78}{$\displaystyle\bigg|$}d_{Z}(z_{1},z_{2})-d_{Z^{\prime}}(z^{\prime}_{1},z_{2}^{\prime})\scalebox{.78}{$\displaystyle\bigg|$}^{{}^{p}}\ d\pi(z_{1},z^{\prime}_{1})\cdot d\pi(z_{2},z^{\prime}_{2})\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Although the GW distance captures the geometric shape of the data distribution, it is invariant to isometric transformations like rotations and shifts, which do not alter the shape but can misalign data features. The lack of attention to feature alignment can distort causal relationships during coupling.
As a refinement, the Fused Gromov-Wasserstein (FGW) distance
(
Vayer et al. 2020
)
augments the GW cost on the internal geometry of each space with a Wasserstein cost on a shared features subspace, and combines the two via a convex weight
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Vayer et al. 2020
consider the case where the two spaces
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
share a common feature space
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathcal{Z}\cap\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
endowed with the metric
d_{\tilde{X}}
, while
\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
carry their own structural metrics,
d_{Z}
and
d_{Z^{\prime}}
. For probability measures
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
on
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
, respectively, the FGW distance is:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{FGW}}_{p,q,\alpha}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):\!=\!\!\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\!\!\!\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\left((\!1\!-\!\alpha\!)\cdot d_{\tilde{X}}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})^{{}^{q}}+\alpha\cdot\scalebox{.78}{$\displaystyle\bigg|$}d_{Z}(z_{1},z_{2})-d_{Z^{\prime}}(z^{\prime}_{1},z_{2}^{\prime})\scalebox{.78}{$\displaystyle\bigg|$}^{{}^{q}}\right)^{{}^{p}}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=\!\!\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\!\!\left(\scalebox{.85}{$\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\int\limits_{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}$}\scalebox{.85}{$\displaystyle\!\!\!\left((\!1\!-\!\alpha\!)\cdot d_{\tilde{X}}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})^{q}+\alpha\cdot\scalebox{.78}{$\displaystyle\bigg|$}d_{Z}(z_{1},z_{2})-d_{Z^{\prime}}(z^{\prime}_{1},z_{2}^{\prime})\scalebox{.78}{$\displaystyle\bigg|$}^{q}\right)^{p}\!\!d\pi(z_{1},z^{\prime}_{1})\cdot d\pi(z_{2},z^{\prime}_{2})$}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

where
x_{1},x^{\prime}_{1}
are the projections of
z_{1}\in\mathcal{Z}
and
z^{\prime}_{1}\in\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
onto the common feature space,
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
. Accordingly, the first term,
(1-\alpha)\,d_{\tilde{X}}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})^{q}
, enforces feature alignment in
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
, while
\alpha\cdot|d_{Z}(z_{1},z_{2})-d_{Z^{\prime}}(z_{1}^{\prime},z_{2}^{\prime})|^{q}
captures the structural discrepancy via the GW cost.

2.3
Metric Learning

Optimal transport methods rely critically on the choice of metric, which determines how each features influences the similarity of data points. Different metric choices can yield substantially different couplings, and thus different statistical conclusions. To address this issue, we adopt metric learning techniques to learn the metric for our experimental sites.

Metric learning aims to minimize distances between similar data points and maximize distances between dissimilar ones, based on a priori knowledge of pairwise relationships
(
Xing et al. 2002
)
.
In
linear
metric learning, the aim is to learn a generalized “Mahalanobis” distance of the form
{d}_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})=\sqrt{\langle z_{2}-z_{1},\,{A}\,(z_{2}-z_{1})\rangle}
, where
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}
, and
A
is a bounded, self‐adjoint, positive semi‐definite operator on the separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{Z}
. Through the factorization
A=L^{*}\,L
, this becomes the norm of a linear embedding
L
:

d_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})\;=\sqrt{\langle L(z_{2}-z_{1}),\,{L}\,(z_{2}-z_{1})\rangle}\;=\;\bigl\|\,L\,z_{2}-L\,z_{1}\bigr\|_{\mathcal{Z}}.

Nonlinear
metric learning
(
Kedem et al. 2012
;
Hu et al. 2015
)
extends this framework by replacing
L
with a composition
\phi=L\circ\psi
:

d_{\phi}(z_{1},z_{2})=\|\phi(z_{2})-\phi(z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{Z}}\,,

where
\psi:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}
is a nonlinear transformation implemented via machine learning tools such as gradient-boosted trees
(
Kedem et al. 2012
)
or neural networks
(
Hu et al. 2015
)
.

3
Methodology

3.1
Problem Definition

Let the metric measure spaces
(\mathcal{Z},d_{Z},\{\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\})
and
(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime},d_{Z^{\prime}},\{\mu^{\prime}_{0},\mu^{\prime}_{1}\})
represent the space of data for ‘source’ and ‘target’ experiment sites, respectively. We assume that both
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
are
Polish
, meaning they are complete and separable under their respective metrics. Following the notation introduced in Subsection
2.1
, each space is the product of features and responses,
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}=\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
, and is equipped with two Borel probability measures:
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}
, representing the distribution of data under control and treatment in the source site, and
\mu^{\prime}_{0},\mu^{\prime}_{1}
in the target site. The metrics
d_{Z},d_{Z^{\prime}}
capture the similarity structure in the source and target sites and are invariant between control and treatment. The source-space metric,
d_{Z}
, is known, while the target-space metric,
d_{Z^{\prime}}
, is unknown.

Consider observing a randomized control trial at the source site, from which two datasets of independent and identically distributed observations were drawn under the control and treatment distributions:
Z_{0}=\left[z_{0i}\right]_{i=1}^{n_{0}}\overset{\footnotesize{\text{i.i.d.}}}{\sim}\mu_{0}
and
Z_{1}=\left[z_{1j}\right]_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\overset{\footnotesize{\text{i.i.d.}}}{\sim}\mu_{1}
. In contrast, only a one-armed trial is observed at the target site, resulting in a dataset drawn from the control distribution:
Z^{\prime}_{0}=\left[z^{\prime}_{0i}\right]_{i=1}^{n^{\prime}_{0}}\overset{\footnotesize{\text{i.i.d.}}}{\sim}\mu^{\prime}_{0}
.
The objective of the study is to generate a synthetic dataset
{Z^{\prime}_{1}}^{\text{(synth.)}}=\left[{{z^{\prime}_{1j}}^{{}^{\!\!\!\!\text{(synth.)}}}\!}\right]_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}_{1}}
that resembles an i.i.d. sample from the target treatment distribution
\mu^{\prime}_{1}
.

3.2
Assumptions

Hotz et al. 2005
pioneered cross-site ATE identification under three assumptions:
random treatment assignment
in the source site,
unconfounded location
to prevent any effect modification (referred to as
macro effects
in their study) across the source and target sites, and
support overlap
in the features’ distribution across sites. Their framework assumes having full treatment and control data from the source site, but only feature data, without treatment status or outcomes, from the target site.

In contrast, our study incorporates full control data from the target site. Thereby, we relax the unconfounded location assumption and enable identification of the treatment despite latent cross-site effect modification. Moreover, we generate target treatment by transporting the source treatment through the effect modification map. As a result, we do not require random treatment assignment either. Identification in our framework relies on a shared transformation between sites, and a mild feature alignment structure.

Assumption 3.1

(Parallel Effect Modification across Treatment and Control)
.

There exists a map from the source to the target space,
T:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
such that
T
is a
bi-measurable isomorphism
; that is,
T
is bijective, and both
T
and
T^{-1}
are measurable with respect to sigma algebras induced by
(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\prime})
and
(\mu_{1},\mu_{1}^{\prime})
, and
T
acts as a push-forward between the source and target sites, shared by both the treatment and control measures:

{T}_{\#}{\mu_{0}}=\mu^{\prime}_{0},\quad\text{and }\quad{T}_{\#}{\mu_{1}}=\mu^{\prime}_{1}.

Assumption
3.1
is notably weaker than the
unconfounded location
assumptions of
Hotz et al. 2005
, which assumed no effect modification across sites, hence restricting
T
to the identity map. We allow a more flexible effect modification structure across sites under the constraint that
T
is shared between treatment and control distributions, enabling both to be pushed forward between source and target sites through a common mapping structure.

Assumption 3.2

(Feature–alignment Structure)
.

\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
share a common feature subspace
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
, and there exists a
continuous

feature–alignment kernel

\mathcal{K}:\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
that depends only on the
projections
of
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
onto
\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{P}^{{}^{\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{H}}}
and
\mathcal{P}^{{}^{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{H}}}
, such that the effect modification map
T
minimizes the alignment penalty:

\mathcal{K}\Big(\mathcal{P}^{{}^{\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{H}}}(z),\mathcal{P}^{{}^{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{H}}}(T(z))\Big)=0,\quad\quad\text{for all}\quad\,z\in\mathcal{Z}.

For notational simplicity, we henceforth write
\mathcal{K}(z,z^{\prime})
for
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P}^{{}^{\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{H}}}(z),\mathcal{P}^{{}^{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{H}}}(z^{\prime}))
.

Assumption
3.2
ensures the existence of at least one “stable” feature direction across sites along which
T
behaves nearly as an identity or a monotone transformation. Typical examples include demographic attributes (e.g., age or gender) or slowly varying clinical indices, which are expected to remain comparable across sites, in contrast to context–sensitive measurements (e.g., device–specific scales or lab values) that may shift substantially across sites. In practice, we build
\mathcal{K}
on a chosen subset of stable features
\mathcal{H}
by comparing distances between raw values of features across sites (appropriate when levels are directly comparable), or their rank-based distances (appropriate when
T
is monotone on
\mathcal{H}
).

Assumption 3.3

(Support Condition)
.

The treatment distribution is absolutely continuous with respect to the control distribution

\mu_{1}\ll\mu_{0}\;.

Equivalently,
\operatorname{supp}(\mu_{1})\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(\mu_{0})
up to
\mu_{0}
–null sets,

Assumption
3.3
is the standard positivity requirement: treated units occur only on regions of the data space where control units are observed, enabling valid counterfactual comparisons. Moreover, because
T
is bijective and pushes forward both measures, absolute continuity is preserved in the target space, resulting in
\mu_{1}^{\prime}\ll\mu_{0}^{\prime}
.

3.3
Proposed Method

We now present our approach for generating synthetic treatment data in the target site.

We begin by defining empirical measures over the observed datasets,
Z_{0}
,
Z_{1}
, and
Z^{\prime}_{0}
as:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{0}\!=\!\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}}{p}_{0i}\cdot\delta\scalebox{.8}{$\displaystyle(z_{0i})$}{\quad\text{for all}\quad z_{0i}\!\in\!Z_{0}\ \text{ such that }\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}}{p}_{0i}\!=\!1},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{1}\!=\!\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}{p}_{1i}\cdot\delta\scalebox{.8}{$\displaystyle(z_{1i})$}{\quad\text{for all}\quad z_{1i}\!\in\!Z_{1}\ \text{ such that }\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}{p}_{1i}\!=\!1},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{\prime}\!=\!\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}^{\prime}}{p}_{0i}^{\prime}\cdot\delta\scalebox{.8}{$\displaystyle(z_{0i}^{\prime})$}{\quad\text{for all}\quad z_{0i}^{\prime}\!\in\!Z_{0}^{\prime}\ \text{ such that }\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}^{\prime}}{p}_{0i}^{\prime}\!=\!1};

where
\delta(z)
is the Dirac delta function, and the weights
\{{p}_{0i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{0}},\{{p}_{1i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{1}},\{{p}^{\prime}_{0i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{0}^{\prime}}
are set to be uniform:
{{p}}_{0i}=\frac{1}{n_{0}}
,
{p}_{1i}=\frac{1}{n_{1}}
, and
{p}^{\prime}_{0i}=\frac{1}{n_{0}^{\prime}}
, unless otherwise specified.

3.3.1
Regularized Fused Gromov-Wasserstein Loss between the control groups

Next, we define a discrete fused Gromov-Wasserstein loss between the empirical control measures in the source and target sites
\widehat{\mu}_{0}
and
\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{\prime}
, as a function of the discrete coupling
\widehat{{\pi}}\in\Pi(\widehat{\mu}_{0},\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{\prime})
between the measures and the (unknown) target-space metric
d_{Z^{\prime}}
:

{\mathscr{L}}_{\mathbb{FGW}_{\alpha}}({\widehat{{\pi}},d_{Z^{\prime}}})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n_{0}}\;\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{n_{0}^{\prime}}\Big((\!1\!-\!\alpha\!)\cdot\mathcal{K}(z_{0i},z^{\prime}_{0k})+\alpha\cdot\big|d_{Z}(z_{0i},z_{0j})-d_{Z^{\prime}}(z_{0k}^{\prime},z_{0\ell}^{\prime})\big|\Big)\cdot\widehat{{\pi}}_{ik}\cdot\widehat{{\pi}}_{j\ell}\ .

(1)

Here,
z_{0i},z_{0j}\in Z_{0}
and
z_{0k}^{\prime},z_{0\ell}^{\prime}\in Z_{0}^{\prime}
are observations from the source and target control datasets,
d_{Z}
is the (known) metric on the source space, and
\mathcal{K}
is the feature alignment kernel. The coupling
\widehat{{\pi}}
is a
{n_{0}\times n_{0}^{\prime}}
non-negative matrix that satisfies marginal constraints ensuring consistency with the empirical weights of
\widehat{\mu}_{0}
and
\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{\prime}
:

\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}^{\prime}}\widehat{{\pi}}_{ik}=p_{0i}\quad\text{, and }\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}}\widehat{{\pi}}_{ik}=p_{0k}^{\prime}\ .

To enable learning a target-space metric, we restrict our attention to the family of
pull-back metrics
on
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
. Let
\Phi:=\{\phi:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}\}
denote the space of all bijective transformations from the source to the target space. By Lemma A.1 in the Supplementary Material, for any
\phi\in\Phi
, the corresponding pull-back metric:

d_{\phi}^{\prime}(z_{1}^{\prime},z_{2}^{\prime})=d_{Z}(\phi^{-1}(z_{1}^{\prime}),\phi^{-1}(z_{2}^{\prime}))\quad\quad\text{for all}\quad z_{1}^{\prime},z_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}^{\prime},

defines a valid metric on
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
. Hence, we may restrict the second argument of
\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{FGW}_{\alpha}}
to the space of pull-back metrics,
\mathcal{D}_{\phi}:=\{d_{\phi}^{\prime}:\phi\in\Phi\}
.

Although the FGW loss in (
1
) alone can identify a unique pull-back metric, it may still admit multiple couplings that attain the minimum. To eliminate this ambiguity, we augment the FGW loss with a
graph-consistency penalty
that discourages mass assigned away from the graph
G_{\phi}:=(\text{Id},\phi)(\mathcal{Z})=\{(z,\phi(z)):z\in\mathcal{Z}\}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
of the candidate map:

\mathscr{L}_{G}(\widehat{{\pi}},d^{\prime}_{\phi})=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}^{\prime}}d^{\prime}_{\phi}(\phi(z_{0i}),z^{\prime}_{0k})\cdot\widehat{{\pi}}_{ik}\;,

(2)

and for
\lambda>0
, consider the regularized objective:

(\widehat{{\pi}}^{*},\phi^{*})=\arg\!\!\!\inf_{\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!{\widehat{{\pi}}\in\Pi(\widehat{\mu}_{0},\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{\prime})},\;{\phi\in\Phi}}\;\left\{{\mathscr{L}}_{\mathbb{FGW}_{\alpha}}\big(\widehat{{\pi}},d^{\prime}_{\phi}\big)+\lambda\cdot\mathscr{L}_{G}(\widehat{{\pi}},d^{\prime}_{\phi})\right\}\;.

(3)

This optimization constitutes the first step of our method,
Optimal Transport-based Transfer Metric Learning
. In Lemma (
3.1
), we establish that the optimization problem in (
3
) admits a unique minimizer, characterized by the pull-back metric induced by the true effect modification map,
d^{\prime}_{T}
, and the
deterministic coupling
,
\pi_{T}
defined in (
5
).

3.3.2
Synthetic treatment generation

We now proceed to the second step of our method, which is to use the learned pair of coupling and transformation,
(\widehat{{\pi}}^{*},{{\phi^{*}}})
, to generate synthetic treatment data at the target site. For each source treatment observation,
z_{1j}\in Z_{1}
, we define the following optimization process to construct its counterfactual analog,
{{z^{\prime}_{1j}}^{{}^{\!\!\!\!\text{(synth.)}}}\!}\in\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
, as if it had been observed in the target site:

\displaystyle{{z^{\prime}_{1j}}^{{}^{\!\!\!\!\text{(synth.)}}}}

\displaystyle=\arg\inf_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\;\mathscr{L}_{S}\big(z^{\prime}\big|z_{1j},(\widehat{{\pi}}^{*},{\phi^{*}}),\lambda_{S}\big)\;\quad\quad

(4)

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle=\arg\inf_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}^{\prime}}\scalebox{.78}{$\displaystyle\bigg|$}d_{Z}(z_{0i},z_{1j})-d^{\prime}_{\phi^{*}}(z^{\prime}_{0k},z^{\prime})\scalebox{.78}{$\displaystyle\bigg|$}\cdot\widehat{{\pi}}^{*}_{ik}+\lambda_{S}\cdot d^{\prime}_{\phi^{*}}\big(z^{\prime},{\phi^{*}}(z_{1j})\big)\right\}\;.

The loss function in (
4
) has two components. The first term

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}^{\prime}}\Big|d_{Z}(z_{0i},z_{1j})-d^{\prime}_{\phi^{*}}(z^{\prime}_{0k},z^{\prime})\Big|\,\widehat{{\pi}}^{*}_{ik}\;,

measures the discrepancy between the source-side distances from the treatment point
z_{1j}
to the source controls
z_{0i}\in Z_{0}
, and the target-side distances from the candidate
z^{\prime}
to the target controls
z^{\prime}_{0k}\in Z^{\prime}_{0}
, weighted by the optimal coupling
\widehat{{\pi}}^{*}
. Accordingly,
{{z^{\prime}_{1j}}^{{}^{\!\!\!\!\text{(synth.)}}}\!}
is constructed such that its distance to any target control data point mirrors the distance between
z_{1j}
and a source control data point. The second term

\displaystyle\lambda_{S}\;d^{\prime}_{\phi^{*}}\big(z^{\prime},{\phi^{*}}(z_{1j})\big)\;,

encourages
z^{\prime}
to be the forward image of
z_{1j}
under
\phi^{*}
, with the term vanishing exactly at
z^{\prime}=\phi^{*}(z_{1j})
, thereby stabilizing the optimization.

Applying (
4
) to each treatment data point in the source space, we generate a synthetic dataset in the target space,
{Z^{\prime}_{1}}^{\text{(synth.)}}=\left[{{z^{\prime}_{1j}}^{{}^{\!\!\!\!\text{(synth.)}}}\!}\right]_{j=1}^{n_{1}}
. In Theorem
3.1
we demonstrate that
{Z^{\prime}_{1}}^{\text{(synth.)}}
converges to the
image
of the source treatment dataset
Z_{1}
under the effect modification map,
T(Z_{1})
, thereby approximating the dataset that would have been observed under the treatment measure
\mu_{1}^{\prime}
in the target space.

3.4
Theoretical Results

We now establish the theoretical properties of the proposed method, showing that the synthetic dataset generated by our method converges to the true treatment distribution in the target site.

Since
\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
are Polish and the losses in (
3
) and
(
4
) are Borel-measurable, it is guaranteed that measurable
selections can be fixed from the (possibly set-valued) minimizer sets
without further comment
(
Kuratowski and Ryll-Nardzewski 1965
)
. This convention is adopted in all that follows. Also, throughout,
\Omega^{\star}
denotes a measurable event on which all the almost-sure convergences of
the selected minimizers are realized simultaneously.

Under Assumption
3.1
, the effect modification map
T:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
acts as a push-forward between the control measures,
T_{\#}{\mu_{0}}=\mu^{\prime}_{0}
. Hence,
T
induces a deterministic coupling between the control measures
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{0}^{\prime}
, defined as:

{\pi}_{T}=(\text{Id},T)_{\#}{\mu_{0}}\;:B\mapsto\mu_{0}\big(\{z\in\mathcal{Z}:(z,T(z))\in B\}\big),\,\text{for any $B\in\sigma(\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}^{\prime})$.}

(5)

This motivates the following Lemma, which establishes the consistency of the solution to the FGW optimization problem in (
3
).

Lemma 3.1
.

For each
(n_{0},n_{0}^{\prime})
, let

(\widehat{\pi}^{*}_{n_{0},n_{0}^{\prime}},\phi^{*}_{n_{0},n_{0}^{\prime}})
denote a measurable
selection of global minimizers of (
3
) built from control
samples
(Z_{0},Z_{0}^{\prime})
of sizes
(n_{0},n_{0}^{\prime})
. Then, under Assumptions (
3.1
–
3.2
), there exists an event

\Omega^{\star}
with
\mathbb{P}(\Omega^{\star})=1
such that, as
n_{0},n_{0}^{\prime}\to\infty
,

\displaystyle d^{\prime}_{\phi^{*}_{n_{0},n_{0}^{\prime}}}\ \longrightarrow\ d^{\prime}_{T}\;\;\text{ $\mu_{0}^{\prime}\!\otimes\!\mu_{0}^{\prime}$-a.e.,}\qquad\widehat{\pi}^{*}_{n_{0},n_{0}^{\prime}}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\longrightarrow}}\ \pi_{T}\quad\text{on }\Omega^{\star}\;.

Building on Lemma (
3.1
), we now state the main convergence result:

Theorem 3.1
.

Under Assumptions (
3.1
–
3.2
–
3.3
) and
Lemma
3.1
, fix
\lambda_{S}>0
. For each
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, let

\displaystyle\widehat{z}^{\prime}_{n_{0},n_{0}^{\prime}}(z)\;\in\;\arg\min_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\;\mathscr{L}_{S}\!\big(z^{\prime}\mid z,(\widehat{\pi}^{*}_{n_{0},n_{0}^{\prime}},\phi^{*}_{n_{0},n_{0}^{\prime}}),\lambda_{S}\big),

denote a measurable selection of the global minimizers of
\mathscr{L}_{S}(\cdot|z,(\widehat{\pi}^{*}_{n_{0},n_{0}^{\prime}},\phi^{*}_{n_{0},n_{0}^{\prime}}),\lambda_{S})
. Then
there exists an event
\Omega^{\star}
with
\mathbb{P}(\Omega^{\star})=1

such that, for every
\omega\in\Omega^{\star}
,

\displaystyle d^{\prime}_{T}\!\big(\widehat{z}^{\prime}_{n_{0},n_{0}^{\prime}}(z)(\omega),\,T(z)\big)\;\longrightarrow\;0,\qquad\mu_{1}\text{-a.e.\ }z\in\mathcal{Z},

as
n_{0},n_{0}^{\prime}\to\infty
. Consequently, for i.i.d. treated samples

\{z_{1j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\sim\mu_{1}
and

z_{1j}^{\prime\,\mathrm{(synth)}}:=\widehat{z}^{\prime}_{n_{0},n_{0}^{\prime}}(z_{1j})
, the
empirical measure

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{\prime}\;:=\;\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\delta_{z_{1j}^{\prime\,\mathrm{(synth)}}}

satisfies, as
n_{0},n_{0}^{\prime}\to\infty
and
n_{1}\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{\prime}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\longrightarrow}}\;\mu_{1}^{\prime}=T_{\#}\mu_{1}\qquad\text{on }\Omega^{\star}.

The theorem ensures that our synthetic procedure recovers the correct treatment distribution in the target site. Once the true pull-back metric and coupling
(\widehat{{\pi}}^{*},\phi^{*})
are identified, the synthetic loss
\mathscr{L}_{S}(\cdot\mid z)
has its unique minimum at the true transported point
T(z)
. This guarantees that each synthetic treated unit converges in probability to its image under
T
, and that, when applied across the treated sample, the empirical distribution of the synthetic points converges to the true target treatment law
\mu_{1}^{\prime}=T_{\#}\mu_{1}
. Hence, the method is consistent both at the level of individual points and at the level of the full distribution.

4
Simulations

We evaluate the empirical performance of our method in numerical experiments designed to mimic cross–site heterogeneity and departures from idealized assumptions. Each method receives source control, source treatment, and target control,
(Z_{0},Z_{1},Z_{0}^{\prime})
, and generates a target treatment sample
{Z^{\prime}_{1}}^{\text{(synth.)}}
. The oracle
Z_{1}^{\prime}
is withheld during training and used only for evaluation. We assess the performance in recovering the oracle distribution, both marginally on the outcome
Y^{\prime}_{1}\subset Z^{\prime}_{1}
, and jointly over
Z^{\prime}_{1}
.

4.1
Design overview

For each subject
i
, we generate covariates and responses in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, according to

\displaystyle X_{i}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}_{d}(\mu_{x}\;,\;\sigma_{x}^{2}\cdot\mathcal{I}_{d}),

(6)

\displaystyle Y_{i}

\displaystyle=\alpha+X_{i}^{\top}\beta+D_{i}\cdot(\tau+X_{i}^{\top}\gamma)+\varepsilon_{i},\quad\varepsilon_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0\;,\;\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}),

with randomized treatment
D_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.5)
, unless otherwise noted.
The source–site control and treatment samples are
Z_{0}=\{Z_{i}:D_{i}=0\}
and

Z_{1}=\{Z_{i}:D_{i}=1\}
.
Cross–site heterogeneity is induced through a site–specific transformation

T:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, which transforms a random subset of individuals from each source dataset to the target site.
The quadruple
(Z_{0},Z_{1},Z_{0}^{\prime},Z_{1}^{\prime})
defines the full experimental environment;
(Z_{0},Z_{1},Z_{0}^{\prime})
are observed and supplied to each model, while
Z_{1}^{\prime}
serves as the oracle for evaluation.

We fix sample sizes at
n_{0}=n_{0}^{\prime}=1000
and
n_{1}=n_{1}^{\prime}=500
and repeat each scenario over

R=50
independent replicates, resampling
(X,\varepsilon)
at each run while keeping

(\beta,\gamma,T)
fixed.

The primary metrics reported in Tables
1
,
2
are: mean, standard deviation, quantiles, Wasserstein and Hellinger distances, and KL divergence between the synthetic response
{Y^{\prime}_{1}}^{\text{(synth.)}}
in
{Z^{\prime}_{1}}^{\text{(synth.)}}
and
Z_{1}^{\prime}
; and Energy distance and Sliced Wasserstein for the joint law of
{Z^{\prime}_{1}}^{\text{(synth.)}}
compared to
Z^{\prime}_{1}
. The description of each metric and additional diagnostics (total variation on
Y^{\prime}_{1}
, projection–averaged divergences on
Z^{\prime}_{1}
and MMD
2
) are reported in Sections B.3–B.4 in the Supplementary Material.

4.2
Data-generating scenarios

We evaluate the proposed method under six representative data-generating processes. Each scenario defines the same underlying outcome model in (
6
), while varying in the structure of the site transformation
T
, the treatment-assignment regime, and the dimensionality
d
. Except for the
sixth
scenario, dimensionality is kept at
d=2
.

Scenario 1
(baseline) is the fully parametric regime in which both the outcome model and the site-mapping are linear,

T(z)=\Omega z+b,\;\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}.

This corresponds to an ideal randomized trial with perfect covariate overlap and affine cross-site shifts.

Scenario 2
(smooth nonlinear) introduces a nonlinear transformation,
T(Z)=\;\Omega\cdot\psi(Z)+b
, which applies a smooth distortion, introducing mild curvature while preserving global geometry.

Scenario 3
(strong nonlinear) increases the degree of nonlinearity.
Here
T(Z)=\;\Omega\cdot\psi(Z)+b
acts through a map that produces half-circular curvature in the response surface.

Scenario 4
(discontinuous) introduces discontinuities in
T(Z)=\!\Omega\cdot\psi(Z)+\!b
with
\psi(Z)=\bigl[X+\mathbbm{1}_{X_{1}\geq\mu_{x,1}},Y\bigr],
creating regime changes across subregions of the covariate space.

Scenario 5
(nonRCT) is identical to the linear case but with covariate means depending on treatment assignment,

X_{i}\mid D_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}_{d}(\mu_{x}+\kappa D_{i},\sigma_{x}^{2}\mathcal{I}_{d}),

thereby violating randomization and introducing systematic imbalance between treatment and control.

Scenario 6
(
d=30
) follows the linear transformation of scenario 1, but extends the linear regime to a higher-dimensional covariate space with
30
covariates, allowing assessment of scalability and robustness to dimensionality.

Together these scenarios span linear, nonlinear, and discontinuous structures, as well as violations of randomization and increases in dimensionality. For a visualization of how each scenario transforms a reference planar surface in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, see the bottom row of Figure
2
. Also, for full specifications of each scenario, including explicit transformation
T
formulas, parameter values, and additional robustness scenarios, see Section B.1 in the Supplementary Material.

4.3
Methods compared

We evaluate the performance of our proposed method, denoted
OTSynth
, as described in Algorithm
1
.
Two variants are considered: a linear variant, in which the transformation
\phi
is restricted to an affine map, and a nonlinear variant, where
\phi
is parameterized by a neural network to capture complex, nonlinear structures in
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
.

We compare
OTSynth
with several alternative approaches, each representing a distinct class of strategies for counterfactual or cross-site data synthesis.

The first baseline is the traditional
TWFE
estimator, a two-way fixed-effects regression that accounts for both treatment and site-specific shifts.
We then include three methods adapted from related literature:

MatchSynth
, based on nearest-neighbor matching following
Hotz et al. 2005
;

GenSynth
, which extends matching by incorporating the generalized synthetic control structure of
Xu 2017
;
and
GANSynth
, which applies cyclic generative adversarial networks
(
Zhu et al. 2017
)
to learn mappings between source and target samples.

These latter methods are adaptations of techniques originally developed for related but distinct tasks:
Hotz et al. 2005
assume
having source and target sites, but only target-site features are observed;
Xu 2017
is
designed for panel data with observed treatment and control units pre- and post-intervention; and
Zhu et al. 2017
propose CycleGANs for unpaired image-to-image translation.
Because no established benchmark exists for the present problem, we utilize these approaches to construct reasonable benchmarks specifically designed to generate synthetic target-treatment datasets.

A complete specification of these baseline models, including their estimation procedures and implementation details, is provided in Section B.2 in the Supplementary Material.

4.4
Results

Tables

,
2
, and Table 4 in the Supplementary Material, present quantitative results across the six simulation scenarios, and Figure

provides a visual comparison of the synthesized versus oracle joint distributions.
Across all designs, the proposed
OTSynth
achieves the closest agreement with the oracle distributions, with the linear variant excelling under affine or piecewise-affine distortions, and the neural variant performing best under nonlinear or high-dimensional transformations.

When the transport between sites is strictly affine (
scenario 1

It attains the lowest marginal errors for all summary statistics of

Under mild curvature (

attains the smallest
\mathrm{W1}(Y)
and Sliced Wasserstein distance on

while
OTSynth (linear)
achieves the lowest Hellinger, KL divergence, and energy distance.
Although
GenSynth

The stronger nonlinear transformation of
scenario 3

With the discontinuous regime shift of

It yields the smallest divergences on all
Y
and

,

5
Real Data Application

To evaluate the practical applicability of our proposed method, we apply it to patient-derived xenograft (PDX) data
(
Gao et al. 2015
)
, a rich and highly controlled resource for understanding cancer treatment responses. PDX provides a framework where patient tumor samples are implanted into mice, enabling comprehensive screening of multiple treatment options while controlling for genetic and environmental variability.

The dataset includes five different cancer types—breast cancer (BRCA), melanoma (CM), colorectal cancer (CRC), non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and pancreatic cancer (PDAC)— and contains three categories of genomic features for each cancer type: gene expression (RNA-seq measurements), copy number variants, and mutation indicators. Given the high dimensionality of the genomic data, we performed sparse principal component analysis
(
Zou et al. 2006
)
for each genomic features category, and reduced the original features to 40 predictors.

We focused on the log-transformed time to tumor doubling (TTD) as the primary response variable, as it reflects the treatment efficacy in delaying tumor progression and is recognized as one of the most important predictors of survival in oncology
(
Tanaka et al. 2004
;
Miura et al. 2019
)
. The scaled tumor shrinkage metric (BAR) was also available in the dataset, but it was not directly used. Among the treatments administered, we identified
BKM120
(Buparlisib) as the treatment group and untreated observations as the control group. BKM120, a PI3K inhibitor, has been studied across multiple cancer types due to its role in targeting dysregulated signaling pathways, making it a relevant choice for evaluating treatment effects
Xing et al. 2021
. By organizing the data into subsets based on cancer types, we used
BRCA
, the cancer type with the largest sample size, as the source dataset, and samples corresponding to each remaining cancer types (
NSCLC, CM, CRC, PDAC
) as target. Each source-target pair was analyzed as a separate scenario. We applied our method and the baseline methods to generate synthetic treatment data for the target site and compared the generated treatment data to the actual observed treatment data from the target site.

Table (
3
) compares synthetic treatment data generation methods across different cancer type pairs, on the response distribution of time to tumor doubling (TTD). Our
OTSynth
framework demonstrates strong alignment with Oracle distributions and achieves the lowest Wasserstein distance, KL divergence, and Hellinger distance across all pairs.
OTSynth
also better preserves the mean and median effects of the response. These findings position
OTSynth
as particularly valuable for counterfactual policy applications requiring geometric faithfulness of treatment effect distributions.

6
Conclusion and Discussion

We proposed a novel Optimal Transport-based framework for synthesizing counterfactual treatment outcomes by leveraging treatment and control data from a source site and only control data from a target site. Unlike traditional approaches, our model adopts a distributional causal inference perspective, enabling detailed reconstruction of counterfactual distributions and capturing critical distributional characteristics, including variance, quantiles, and higher-order moments. Our approach explicitly accommodates latent effect modifications through flexible push-forward mappings, relaxing assumptions such as the “unconfounded location” condition typically imposed in the existing literature.

Our theoretical results provide rigorous guarantees regarding the uniqueness of the learned metric and asymptotic convergence of synthesized treatment data toward the true underlying target distribution. Empirical evaluations demonstrated that our methodology consistently outperforms baseline models. Specifically,
OTSynth
showed robust performance across diverse synthetic data-generating scenarios and a real-world patient-derived xenograft dataset, highlighting its capability to accurately capture complex structural relationships and effect modifications across sites.

Future research directions could include extensions of the framework to settings with partially observed or missing covariate data, the exploration of alternative kernel choices or embedding strategies to enhance metric learning effectiveness further, and broadening the method’s applicability to longitudinal data structures. Our proposed framework represents a significant advancement in causal transportability methodologies, offering researchers and policymakers powerful tools for assessing and extrapolating intervention efficacy across heterogeneous contexts.
