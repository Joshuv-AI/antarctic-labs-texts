The Riesz-Kantorovich formulas for 𝕃 \mathbb{L} -vector lattices
Tomas Chamberlain and Marten Wortel

Abstract
Let 𝕃 \mathbb{L} be a Dedekind complete unital f f -algebra. We prove the Riesz-Kantorovich formulas for order bounded 𝕃 \mathbb{L} -module homomorphisms from a directed partially ordered 𝕃 \mathbb{L} -module with the Riesz Decomposition Property into a Dedekind complete 𝕃 \mathbb{L} -vector lattice satisfying an additional mild condition.



1
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{L}
be a real or complex Dedekind complete unital
f
-algebra. In
[
6
]
, a general theory of
\mathbb{L}
-functional analysis was developed, investigating
\mathbb{L}
-normed spaces, i.e.,
\mathbb{L}
-modules equipped with an
\mathbb{L}
-valued norm. This theory was mainly inspired by two recent developments. Firstly, the theory of Kaplansky-Hilbert modules (developed by Kaplansky in 1953) was successfully applied in
[
3
]
to obtain a functional-analytic proof of the famous Furstenberg-Zimmer structure theorem of ergodic theory. Kaplansky-Hilbert modules are
\mathbb{L}
-Hilbert spaces where
\mathbb{L}
is an abelian AW*-algebra, which is a complex Dedekind complete unital vector lattice. Secondly, a comprehensive theory of probability theory in vector lattices was recently developed (see for example
[
7
,
5
]
and references therein) based on a conditional expectation operator
T
on a vector lattice
E
. In this theory certain spaces
L^{p}(T)
are of crucial importance, and these are
\mathbb{L}
-normed spaces with
\mathbb{L}=R(T)
, a real universally complete (and hence Dedekind complete) vector lattice.

In both of these motivating examples, the corresponding
\mathbb{L}
-normed spaces have additional structure. Indeed, in
[
3
]
the Kaplansky-Hilbert module investigated is
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathrm{X}|\mathrm{Y})
as a complex
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathrm{Y})
-module (here
\mathrm{X}
and
\mathrm{Y}
are certain probability spaces), and
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathrm{X}|\mathrm{Y})
has a natural modulus turning it into a complex vector lattice. The
R(T)
-module
L^{p}(T)
is also naturally a real vector lattice.

It therefore seems desirable to also develop a theory of
\mathbb{L}
-vector lattices, i.e.,
\mathbb{L}
-modules that are also lattices compatible with the module structure. This paper is a first step towards developing such a theory. In the classical case (i.e., if
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{C}
) the theory of complex vector lattices is developed based on real vector lattice theory, so in this paper we only consider the case where
\mathbb{L}
is a real Dedekind complete unital vector lattice.

One of the most important results in classical vector lattice theory is the Riesz-Kantorovich formulas, and the main goal of this paper is to develop the
\mathbb{L}
-valued version in full generality. Note that in
[
5
, Lemma 3.2]
these formulas were proven in the very specific case of
L^{2}(T)^{*}
(the order bounded
R(T)
-module homomorphisms from
L^{2}(T)
to
R(T)
) using techniques tailored towards that setting.

The structure of this paper is as follows. In
Section

2
we explain the basics of
\mathbb{L}
as well as partially ordered
\mathbb{L}
-modules and
\mathbb{L}
-vector lattices.
Section

3
contains a thorough investigation of Archimedean properties, which turn out to be more involved than in the classical case. The classical Riesz-Kantorovich formulas rely on an extension lemma for additive maps between cones, and in
Section

4
we develop its
\mathbb{L}
-valued analogue. Even in the classical case, our version of this lemma is a slight improvement of the classical result, since the codomain is only required to be almost Archimedean instead of Archimedean. Finally, in
Section

5
we prove the Riesz-Kantorovich formulas (
Theorem

5.1
).

2
Preliminaries

In this paper,
\mathbb{L}
is an arbitrary Dedekind-complete unital
f
-algebra. We use the phrase “in the classical case” to refer to the particular case where
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{R}
. Since
\mathbb{L}
is a unital algebra over
\mathbb{R}
, we identify each real number
r
with its image
r\cdot 1
in
\mathbb{L}
, and hence consider
\mathbb{R}
as a sub-lattice and sub-algebra of
\mathbb{L}
. In particular, we denote the multiplicative unit of
\mathbb{L}
by
1
. The symbol
\mathbb{P}
denotes the set
\{\pi\in\mathbb{L}:\pi^{2}=\pi\}
of idempotents in
\mathbb{L}
. Under the order inherited from
\mathbb{L}
,
\mathbb{P}
is a sublattice of
\mathbb{L}
and a complete Boolean algebra with least element
0
, greatest element
1
, meet operation
(\pi,\rho)\mapsto\pi\rho
, and complementation operation
\pi\mapsto\pi^{c}:=1-\pi
.
\mathbb{P}
is isomorphic to the Boolean algebra of bands of
\mathbb{L}
; for every band of
\mathbb{L}
, the corresponding element of
\mathbb{P}
is the image of
1
under the band projection, and for every idempotent
\pi
, the corresponding band is
\pi\mathbb{L}
.

As explained in
[
6
, §2.1]
, there exists a unique Stonean space
K
such that
\mathbb{L}
is (isomorphic to) an order-dense vector lattice ideal and sub-algebra of
C_{\infty}(K)
, and furthermore
C(K)\subseteq\mathbb{L}
. Using this representation, the elements of
\mathbb{P}
are the
\{0,1\}
-valued functions in
C(K)
, and they are precisely the indicator functions of the clopen subsets of
K
. The real numbers, as elements of
\mathbb{L}
, correspond to the constant functions in
C(K)
. Note that
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{R}
if and only if
\mathbb{P}
is the two-element Boolean algebra. We use the notation
\mathbb{L}_{1}
to denote the vector lattice ideal of
\mathbb{L}
generated by
1
. Under the representation of
\mathbb{L}
using
C_{\infty}(K)
,
\mathbb{L}_{1}
corresponds to
C(K)
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be an
\mathbb{L}
-module and
x\in X
. The
support
of
x
is denoted by
\pi_{x}
and is defined as
\pi_{x}:=\inf\{\pi\in\mathbb{P}:\pi x=x\}
.

Note that
\pi_{x}\in\mathbb{P}
. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{L}
with
\mathbb{L}
considered as a module over itself,
\pi_{\lambda}
is the indicator function of the closure of the set
\{k\in K:\lambda(k)\neq 0\}
. Furthermore,
\pi_{\lambda}\lambda=\lambda
.

The representation of
\mathbb{L}
explained above makes it clear that, for arbitrary
\lambda
and
\mu
in
\mathbb{L}
, the following are equivalent:
\lambda\mu=0
;
\lvert\lambda\rvert\wedge\lvert\mu\rvert=0
;
\pi_{\lambda}\wedge\pi_{\mu}=0
; and
\pi_{\lambda}\pi_{\mu}=0
.

An element
x
of an
\mathbb{L}
-module is said to be
support-attaining
if
\pi_{x}x=x
, and an
\mathbb{L}
-module itself is said to be support-attaining if each of its elements is support-attaining. The following example shows that not every
\mathbb{L}
-module is support-attaining.

\mathbb{L}
-module is support-attaining if and only if every cyclic submodule is projective, if and only if the module is non-singular.

Example 2.2
.

Let
\mathbb{L}=\ell^{\infty}
,
X=\ell^{\infty}/c_{0}
, and

. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\pi_{n}
be the indicator function of the set

. Then
\pi_{n}x=x
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, but
\pi_{n}\ \mathord{\downarrow}\ 0
, so
\pi_{x}=0
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
X
be an
\mathbb{L}
-module. We say that
X
is a
partially ordered
\mathbb{L}
-module
(or
PO
\mathbb{L}
-module
) if
X
has a partial order such that, for all
x,y,z\in X
and all
\lambda\in\mathbb{L}
,

1.

x\leq y
implies
x+z\leq y+z
, and

2.

0\leq\lambda
and
x\leq y
imply
\lambda x\leq\lambda y
.

The
positive cone
of
X
is the set
X^{+}:=\{x\in X:0\leq x\}
. If
X
is a PO
\mathbb{L}
-module, then we say that
X
is
directed
if every pair of elements of
X
has an upper bound. We say that
X
is an
\mathbb{L}
-vector lattice
if the partial order on
X
is a lattice order.

As in the classical case, the partial orders on a given
\mathbb{L}
-module which make it into a PO
\mathbb{L}
-module are in bijection with the cones of
X
, i.e., subsets of
X
which are closed under addition and positive scaling and contain no non-trivial submodules.

A PO
\mathbb{L}
-module
X
is directed if and only if the submodule generated by
X^{+}
is equal to
X
. We will refer to directed PO
\mathbb{L}
-modules as
directed
\mathbb{L}
-modules
.

Let
X
and
Y
be PO
\mathbb{L}
-modules. An
\mathbb{L}
-module homomorphism from
X
to
Y
is called an
operator
from
X
to
Y
, and the set of operators is denoted by
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
. An operator is said to be
positive
if it maps
X^{+}
to
Y^{+}
. We make
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
a PO
\mathbb{L}
-module by letting the positive cone be the set of positive operators. An operator is said to be
regular
if it is the difference of two positive operators, and the set of regular operators is denoted
\mathcal{L}_{\text{r}}(X,Y)
. An operator
T
is said to be
order-bounded
if, for every order interval
[x,y]\subseteq X
,
T([x,y])
is contained in an order interval in
Y
. The set of order-bounded operators is denoted
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
. As in the classical case, every regular operator is order-bounded. The expression
X^{\sim}
denotes the set of order-bounded operators from
X
to
\mathbb{L}
.

For
x,y
in an
\mathbb{L}
-vector lattice, let
\pi_{x<y}
denote the support of
(y-x)^{+}
. In particular, for
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{L}
,
\pi_{\lambda<\mu}
is the indicator function of the closure of the set
\{k\in K:\lambda(k)<\mu(k)\}
. It follows that
\pi_{\lambda<\mu}\lambda\leq\pi_{\lambda<\mu}\mu
, and this gives the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{L}
. Then there exists a sequence
\pi_{n}\ \mathord{\uparrow}\ 1
in
\mathbb{P}
such that
-n\leq\pi_{n}\lambda\leq n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Furthermore, if
\lambda\in\mathbb{L}^{+}
, then
\pi_{n}\lambda\ \mathord{\uparrow}\ \lambda
.

Proof.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\pi_{n}=\pi_{\lvert\lambda\rvert<n}
. It is clear from the representation of
\mathbb{L}
that
\pi_{n}\ \mathord{\uparrow}\ 1
, and
-n\leq\pi_{n}\lambda\leq n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

For a PO
\mathbb{L}
-module
X
with
x\in X^{+}
and
\pi\in\mathbb{P}
, we have
[0,\pi x]=\pi[0,x]
.

Proof.

For any
y\in[0,x]
,
\pi y\in[0,\pi x]
, so
\pi[0,x]\subseteq[0,\pi x]
. Conversely, suppose
z\in[0,\pi x]
. Then
z\in[0,x]
, and
\pi^{c}z\in[0,\pi^{c}\pi x]=\{0\}
, so
(1-\pi)z=\pi^{c}z=0
. Thus
z=\pi z\in\pi[0,x]
, which shows that
[0,\pi x]\subseteq\pi[0,x]
.
∎

If
X
is a PO
\mathbb{L}
-module,
x\in X
, and
D\subseteq X
, then the expression
x\leq D
means that
x
is a lower bound of
D
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
X
be a PO
\mathbb{L}
-module. If
\lambda\in\mathbb{L}^{+}
and
D\ \mathord{\downarrow}\ x
in
X
, then
\lambda D\ \mathord{\downarrow}\ \lambda x
.

Proof.

First suppose
x=0
. Clearly
0\leq\lambda D
. Let
y\leq\lambda D
. Then, for all
d\in D
,
y\leq y+d\leq\lambda d+d=(\lambda+1)d
. Since
\lambda+1\geq 1
, it is clear from the representation of
\mathbb{L}
that
\lambda+1
is invertible, and its inverse is positive. This gives
(\lambda+1)^{-1}y\leq d
for all
d\in D
. Hence
(\lambda+1)^{-1}y\leq 0
, and multiplying by
\lambda+1
gives
y\leq 0
.

For arbitrary
x\in X
, observe that
D\ \mathord{\downarrow}\ x
if and only if
D-x\ \mathord{\downarrow}\ 0
. Applying the above result gives
\lambda D-\lambda x\ \mathord{\downarrow}\ 0
, and hence
\lambda D\ \mathord{\downarrow}\ \lambda x
.
∎

3
Archimedean Properties

We begin with the main definitions.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
X
be a PO
\mathbb{L}
-module and
S\in\{\mathbb{L},\mathbb{R},\mathbb{P}\}
. Consider the following conditions.

(i)

For all
D\subseteq S
and all
x\in X^{+}
, if
D\ \mathord{\downarrow}\ 0
in
\mathbb{L}
, then
Dx\ \mathord{\downarrow}\ 0
in
X
.

(ii)

For all
D\subseteq S
and all
x\in X^{+}
, if
D\ \mathord{\downarrow}\ 0
in
\mathbb{L}
,
y\in X
, and
-Dx\leq y\leq Dx
, then
y=0
.

We say that
X
is
S
-Archimedean
if it satisfies condition
(i)
. We say that
X
is
almost
S
-Archimedean
if it satisfies condition
(ii)
.

We say that
X
is
Archimedean
if it is
\mathbb{L}
-Archimedean, and we say that
X
is
sequentially

S
-Archimedean
(resp.
sequentially almost
S
-Archimedean
) if it satisfies condition
(i)
(resp. condition
(ii)
) with
D
replaced by an arbitrary sequence in
S
which decreases to zero.

In the classical case, the only idempotents are
0
and
1
, and so the
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean condition is trivially satisfied.

Note that the “
\mathbb{R}
-Archimedean” and “almost
\mathbb{R}
-Archimedean” properties are equivalent to their sequential versions. As in the classical case, each Archimedean property implies the corresponding “almost” version, and for an
\mathbb{L}
-vector lattice, each Archimedean property is equivalent to the corresponding “almost” version.

In
[
4
, §2]
, it is shown that multiplication by a positive element in an
f
-algebra is an orthomorphism, and thus is order-continuous. Hence,
\mathbb{L}
, considered as a PO module over itself, is Archimedean.

A directed
\mathbb{L}
-module
X
is
\mathbb{R}
-Archimedean if and only if, for all
x,y\in X
, if
x\leq\frac{1}{n}y
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then
x\leq 0
. This is the classical definition of an Archimedean space. In the classical case, a directed
\mathbb{L}
-module is Archimedean if and only if it is
\mathbb{R}
-Archimedean. In the general case, an Archimedean PO
\mathbb{L}
-module is always
\mathbb{R}
-Archimedean, but the two properties are not equivalent. Indeed, Example
2.2
gives an
\mathbb{R}
-Archimedean
\mathbb{L}
-module that is not Archimedean. But notice that, in that example, the failure of
X
to be Archimedean is exhibited by a net of idempotents. This turns out to be the only source of counterexamples, as we will now show.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
X
be a PO
\mathbb{L}
-module. Then
X
is Archimedean if and only if it is both
\mathbb{R}
-Archimedean and
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean.

Proof.

Clearly, if
X
is Archimedean, then it is
\mathbb{R}
-Archimedean and
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean. Now assume
X
is
\mathbb{R}
-Archimedean and
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean. Let
x\in X^{+}
and let
D\ \mathord{\downarrow}\ 0
in
\mathbb{L}
. We wish to show that
\inf(Dx)=0
, so let
y\leq Dx
.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{R}
with
0<r
. For each
\lambda\in D
, we have
\lambda+r=\lambda\wedge r+\lambda\vee r
, giving
\lambda=\lambda\wedge r+(\lambda\vee r-r)=\lambda\wedge r+(\lambda-r)^{+}
. Note also that the function
\lambda\mapsto\pi_{\lambda>r}
(with domain
D
) is order-preserving.

We claim that
\inf_{\lambda\in D}\pi_{\lambda>r}=0
. To prove this, let
\sigma:=\inf_{\lambda\in D}\pi_{\lambda>r}
and let
\mu\in D
. Then
0\leq\sigma\leq\pi_{\mu>r}=\pi_{(\mu-r)^{+}}
, and since
(\mu-r)^{-}
and
\pi_{(\mu-r)^{+}}
are disjoint,
\sigma
is disjoint from
(\mu-r)^{-}
. Hence
(\mu-r)\sigma=(\mu-r)^{+}\sigma\geq 0
, and so
r\sigma\leq\mu\sigma
. Taking the infimum over all
\mu\in D
gives
r\sigma\leq\inf(D\sigma)=0
, and dividing by
r
gives
\sigma\leq 0
. Therefore
\sigma=0
, as claimed.

We now show that
y\leq rx
. For each
\lambda,\mu\in D
, let
\nu\in D
be a lower bound of
\{\lambda,\mu\}
. (Such a
\nu
exists because
D
is downward-directed.) Then
y\leq(\nu\wedge r)x+(\nu-r)^{+}x\leq rx+(\nu-r)^{+}x
, so

\displaystyle y-rx

\displaystyle\leq(\nu-r)^{+}x

\displaystyle=\pi_{\nu>r}(\nu-r)^{+}x

\displaystyle\leq\pi_{\lambda>r}(\mu-r)^{+}x.

Since
X
is
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean and
\inf_{\lambda\in D}\pi_{\lambda>r}=0
, we have
\inf_{\lambda\in D}(\pi_{\lambda>r}(\mu-r)^{+}x)=0
, which implies that
y-rx\leq 0
.

Hence,
y\leq rx
for all
r\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Since
X
is
\mathbb{R}
-Archimedean,
y\leq 0
.
∎

The proof of the next theorem is a simple modification of the proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
X
be a PO
\mathbb{L}
-module. Then
X
is sequentially Archimedean if and only if it is sequentially
\mathbb{R}
-Archimedean and sequentially
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean. ∎

Recall that a poset is said to be
Dedekind-complete
if every non-empty subset with an upper bound has a supremum and every non-empty subset with a lower bound has an infimum. (Equivalently, every non-empty subset with an upper bound has a supremum.) The following theorem can be proven exactly as in the classical case.

Theorem 3.4
.

Every Dedekind-complete
\mathbb{L}
-vector lattice is
\mathbb{R}
-Archimedean. ∎

However, a Dedekind-complete
\mathbb{L}
-vector lattice need not be sequentially almost
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean.

Example 3.5
.

Suppose
\mathbb{L}=\ell^{\infty}
. Then
K=\beta\mathbb{N}
, the Stone-Čech compactification of the natural numbers. Let
k\in\beta\mathbb{N}\setminus\mathbb{N}
, and let
I\subseteq\mathbb{L}
be the (ring- and lattice-) ideal of elements which vanish at
k
. It can be shown that the quotient ring
\mathbb{L}/I
is ring-isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}
and that the quotient map is order-preserving, so
\mathbb{L}/I
is in fact a Dedekind-complete totally-ordered
\mathbb{L}
-module. If we define the sequence
\pi_{n}
as in Example
2.2
, and if
[1]
denotes the equivalence class in
\mathbb{L}/I
of the constant
1
sequence, then we see that
\pi_{n}[1]=[1]
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence
-\pi_{n}[1]\leq[1]\leq\pi_{n}[1]
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\pi_{n}\ \mathord{\downarrow}\ 0
, but of course
[1]\neq[0]
, showing that
X
is not sequentially almost
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean.

The
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean property is closely connected to support-attainment. In fact, if
X
is a directed
\mathbb{L}
-module, then it can be shown that
X
is support-attaining if and only if it is almost
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
X
be a directed
\mathbb{L}
-module. Then
X
is support-attaining if and only if it is almost
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean.

Proof.

Suppose
X
is almost
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean. Let
x\in X
and, since
X
is directed, let
y\in X
be an upper bound of
\{x,-x\}
. For each
\pi\in\mathbb{P}
satisfying
\pi x=x
, we have
\pi-\pi_{x}\geq 0
and thus
-(\pi-\pi_{x})y\leq(\pi-\pi_{x})x=x-\pi_{x}x\leq(\pi-\pi_{x})y
. Since the set
\{\pi-\pi_{x}:\pi\in\mathbb{P},\pi x=x\}\subseteq\mathbb{P}
decreases to zero in
\mathbb{L}
and
X
is almost
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean, we have
x-\pi_{x}x=0
, and thus
x=\pi_{x}x
.

Now suppose
X
is support-attaining. Let
x\in X^{+}
and
y\in X
with
D\ \mathord{\downarrow}\ 0
in
\mathbb{P}
and
-\pi x\leq y\leq\pi x
for all
\pi\in D
. It follows that
\pi_{y}\leq\pi
for all
\pi\in D
. Indeed, since
-\pi x\leq y\leq\pi x
, multiplying by
\pi^{c}
gives
0=-\pi^{c}\pi x\leq\pi^{c}y\leq\pi^{c}\pi x=0
, which implies
\pi^{c}y=0
and thus
\pi y=y
. Since
D\ \mathord{\downarrow}\ 0
, it follows that
\pi_{y}=0
, and hence, by support-attainment,
y=\pi_{y}y=0
, showing that
X
is almost
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean.
∎

4
Extension Lemma

To prove the Riesz Kantorovich Theorem, we need an extension lemma. Classically, we have the following (given, for instance, as Lemma 1.26 in
[
2
]
).

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be directed
\mathbb{R}
-vector spaces, with
Y
Archimedean. Let
T:X^{+}\longrightarrow Y^{+}
be an additive function. Then
T
has a unique extension to a positive operator from
X
to
Y
.

Our version of the extension lemma for directed
\mathbb{L}
-modules is similar to the classical version, but there are some differences. Firstly, we note that the proof of Lemma 1.26 in
[
2
]
does not actually require
Y
to be Archimedean; it goes through if
Y
is merely almost Archimedean. In our case, we assume that
Y
is sequentially almost Archimedean (that is, sequentially almost
\mathbb{L}
-Archimedean). Secondly, we require the map
T
to be
\mathbb{P}
-homogeneous. (This condition is trivially satisfied in the classical case.) If
T
is not
\mathbb{P}
-homogeneous, then it need not admit a linear extension. For example, if
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
X=Y=\mathbb{L}
, then the function
T:\mathbb{L}^{+}\longrightarrow\mathbb{L}^{+}
given by
T(x,y)=(y,x)
is additive, and it admits a unique additive extension
\hat{T}:\mathbb{L}\longrightarrow\mathbb{L}
given by the same expression. But
\hat{T}
is not an operator because it is not
\mathbb{P}
-homogeneous.

Recall that
\mathbb{L}_{1}
is the vector lattice ideal of
\mathbb{L}
generated by
1
. Under the representation of
\mathbb{L}
using
C_{\infty}(K)
,
\mathbb{L}_{1}
corresponds to the set of elements of
\mathbb{L}
that are real-valued everywhere.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
X
and
Y
be directed
\mathbb{L}
-modules with
Y
sequentially almost Archimedean, and let
T:X^{+}\longrightarrow Y^{+}
be an additive
\mathbb{P}
-homogeneous function. Then
T
extends uniquely to a positive operator
\widehat{T}:X\longrightarrow Y
.

Proof.

We first note that since
Y
is sequentially almost Archimedean, it is almost
\mathbb{R}
-Archimedean and sequentially almost
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean.

As in the classical case,
T
is order-preserving and
\mathbb{Q}^{+}
-homogeneous. In particular, by additivity,
\mathbb{Q}^{+}
-homogeneity, and
\mathbb{P}
-homogeneity,
T
is homogeneous with respect to any
\mathbb{Q}^{+}
-linear combination of idempotents. We refer to a
\mathbb{Q}^{+}
-linear combination of idempotents as a “
\mathbb{Q}^{+}
-step function”.

Suppose we have shown that
T
is
\mathbb{L}^{+}
-homogeneous. Then we extend
T
to
X
as follows. For each
x\in X
, by the directedness of
X
there exist
y,z\in X^{+}
such that
x=y-z
. Set
\widehat{T}(x):=T(y)-T(z)
. This gives the desired extension. The proof of the fact that
\widehat{T}
is well defined and unique is the same as in the classical case. Thus, it remains to show that
T
is
\mathbb{L}^{+}
-homogeneous.

To show that
T
is
\mathbb{L}_{1}^{+}
-homogeneous, let
\beta\in\mathbb{L}_{1}^{+}
and
x\in X^{+}
. By the Freudenthal Spectral Theorem
[
1
, Theorem 2.8]
, there exists a sequence
(\alpha_{n})
of
\mathbb{Q}^{+}
-step functions such that
\alpha_{n}\ \mathord{\uparrow}\ \beta
uniformly relative to
1
in
\mathbb{L}
. Since
\beta\in\mathbb{L}_{1}
, there also exists a sequence
(\gamma_{n})
of
\mathbb{Q}^{+}
-step functions such that
\gamma_{n}\ \mathord{\downarrow}\ \beta
uniformly relative to
1
.

Let
0<r\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\beta-r<\alpha_{n}
and
\gamma_{n}<\beta+r
. Then,

\begin{array}[]{rcccccccl}(\beta-r)T(x)&\leq&\alpha_{n}T(x)&=&T(\alpha_{n}x)&&&&\\
&&&\leq&T(\beta x)&&\\
&&&\leq&T(\gamma_{n}x)&=&\gamma_{n}T(x)&\leq&(\beta+r)T(x).\end{array}

Therefore,
T(\beta x)-\beta T(x)\in\big[\!-\!rT(x),rT(x)\big]
. Since
Y
is almost
\mathbb{R}
-Archimedean, we obtain
T(\beta x)=\beta T(x)
, concluding the proof that
T
is
\mathbb{L}_{1}^{+}
-homogeneous.

We now show that
T
is
\mathbb{L}^{+}
-homegeneous. Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{L}^{+}
. By Lemma
2.4
, there exists a sequence
\pi_{n}\ \mathord{\uparrow}\ 1
in
\mathbb{P}
such that
-n\leq\pi_{n}\lambda\leq n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Using the
\mathbb{P}
-homogeneity of
T
and then the fact that
\pi_{n}\lambda\in\mathbb{L}_{1}^{+}
, we have

\pi_{n}T(\lambda x)\ =\ T(\pi_{n}\lambda x)\ =\ \pi_{n}\lambda T(x)

for all
n
. Therefore, we have the following two inequalities:

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\pi_{n}\lambda T(x)&=&\pi_{n}T(\lambda x)&\leq&T(\lambda x)\\
-\lambda T(x)&\leq&-\pi_{n}\lambda T(x)&=&-\pi_{n}T(\lambda x).\end{array}

Adding the two inequalities gives

(\pi_{n}-1)\lambda T(x)\ \leq\ T(\lambda x)-\lambda T(x)\ \leq\ (1-\pi_{n})T(\lambda x).

Since
Y
is directed, there exists a
y\in Y
such that
\{\lambda T(x),T(\lambda x)\}\leq y
, giving

-(1-\pi_{n})y\ \leq\ T(\lambda x)-\lambda T(x)\ \leq\ (1-\pi_{n})y

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
Y
is sequentially almost
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean and
(1-\pi_{n})\ \mathord{\downarrow}\ 0
, we have
T(\lambda x)=\lambda T(x)
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

In the proof of Theorem
4.2
, to show that
T
is
\mathbb{L}_{1}^{+}
-homogeneous, only the almost
\mathbb{R}
-Archimedean property of
Y
was used. The sequential almost
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean property was only used to show that
T
is homogeneous with respect to elements of
\mathbb{L}^{+}
not in
\mathbb{L}_{1}^{+}
. Hence, if
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{L}_{1}
, then the conclusion of Theorem
4.2
still holds if we weaken the assumption that
Y
is sequentially almost Archimedean to merely the assumption that
Y
is almost
\mathbb{R}
-Archimedean.

5
The Riesz-Kantorovich Theorem

Recall that a PO real vector space
X
is said to have the
Riesz Decomposition Property (RDP)
if, for all
x
and
y
in
X^{+}
,
[0,x+y]=[0,x]+[0,y]
. It is well-known (for instance, it is shown in
[
2
, §1.8]
) that this is equivalent to the condition that, for all
x,y,p,q\in X
, if
\{x,y\}\leq\{p,q\}
, then there exists a
z\in X
such that
\{x,y\}\leq z\leq\{p,q\}
. This is clearly equivalent to the statement that for all
x,y\in X
, the (possibly empty) set of upper bounds of
\{x,y\}
in
X
is downward-directed. The definition of the RDP extends to PO
\mathbb{L}
-modules in the obvious way, and the same equivalences hold. An
\mathbb{L}
-vector lattice is a PO
\mathbb{L}
-module
X
such that, for all
x,y\in X
, the set of upper bounds of
\{x,y\}
in
X
has a least element, so it is clear that every
\mathbb{L}
-vector lattice has the RDP (and is directed). Hence, in particular, the following theorem applies when
X
is an
\mathbb{L}
-vector lattice.

For an
\mathbb{L}
-vector lattice
Y
, as explained in
[
6
, §3.3]
, if
Y
admits an
\mathbb{L}
-valued norm (which is also defined there), then it is support-attaining and thus
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean.

Theorem 5.1
.

[The Riesz-Kantorovich Theorem]
Let
X
be a directed
\mathbb{L}
-module with the RDP, and let
Y
be a Dedekind-complete
\mathbb{L}
-vector lattice. If
Y
is sequentially
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean or
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{L}_{1}
, then the following hold.

1.

\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
is an
\mathbb{L}
-vector lattice;

2.

\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)=\mathcal{L}_{\text{r}}(X,Y)
;

3.

\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
is Dedekind-complete;

4.

the lattice operations in
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
are given by the following formulas: for
S
and
T
in
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
, and for all
x\in X^{+}
, we have

\displaystyle(S\vee T)(x)

\displaystyle=\sup\{S(y)+T(x-y):y\in[0,x]\}

\displaystyle(S\wedge T)(x)

\displaystyle=\inf\{S(y)+T(x-y):y\in[0,x]\}

\displaystyle S^{+}(x)

\displaystyle=\sup\{S(y):y\in[0,x]\}

\displaystyle S^{-}(x)

\displaystyle=\inf\{S(y):y\in[0,x]\}

\displaystyle\lvert S\rvert(x)

\displaystyle=\sup\{S(y):y\in[-x,x]\};

5.

for every increasing net
(T_{\alpha})
in
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
, we have
T_{\alpha}\ \mathord{\uparrow}\ T
in
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
if and only if
T_{\alpha}(x)\ \mathord{\uparrow}\ T(x)
in
Y
for all
x\in X^{+}
.

Proof.

We first show that
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
is an
\mathbb{L}
-vector lattice, and then show that every directed set with an upper bound has a supremum.

As in the classical case, it suffices to show that for every
S\in\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
,
S^{+}=S\vee 0
exists in
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
. To that end, define
R:X^{+}\longrightarrow Y^{+}
by setting, for each
x\in X^{+}
,
R(x):=\sup\{S(y):0\leq y\leq x\}
. We will use Theorem
4.2
to extend
R
to all of
X
. The additivity of
R
can be proven exactly as in the classical case,
[
2
, Theorem 1.59]
. (In particular, this is where the RDP is used.) For
\mathbb{P}
-homogeneity, let
x\in X^{+}
and
\pi\in\mathbb{P}
. By Lemma
2.5
,
S([0,\pi x])=S(\pi[0,x])
, and by linearity,
S(\pi[0,x])=\pi S([0,x])
. Taking the supremum gives
\sup\pi S([0,x])=\pi\sup S([0,x])
by Lemma
2.6
, and hence
R(\pi x)=\pi R(x)
.

Since
Y
is Dedekind-complete, it is
\mathbb{R}
-Archimedean, and thus sequentially almost
\mathbb{R}
-Archimedean. Now, recall that the statement of the present theorem requires that either
Y
is sequentially
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean or
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{L}_{1}
. If
Y
is sequentially
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean, then by Theorem
3.3
,
Y
is sequentially Archimedean and thus sequentially almost Archimedean. In this case, by Theorem
4.2
, we see that
R
extends uniquely to a positive operator from
X
to
Y
. If
Y
is not sequentially
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean but
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{L}_{1}
, then by Remark
4.3
, we also see that
R
extends uniquely to a positive operator from
X
to
Y
. Hence, in both cases,
R
extends uniquely to a positive operator
R\in\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
.

To see that
R
is an upper bound of
\{0,S\}
in
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
, note that for all
x\in X^{+}
,
\{0,S(x)\}\subseteq S([0,x])
and so
\{0,S(x)\}\leq R(x)
. Now let
T\in\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
be an upper bound of
\{0,S\}
. Then if
0\leq y\leq x
in
X
, we have
S(y)\leq T(y)\leq T(x)
, which implies that
R(x)=\sup S([0,x])\leq T(x)
. Thus
R
is the supremum of
\{0,S\}
in
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
.

This shows that
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
is a lattice, and thus
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)=\mathcal{L}_{\text{r}}(X,Y)
. The validity of the formulas for the absolute value and supremum of two elements of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
can be proven exactly as in the classical case.

We now show that
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
is Dedekind-complete. Since
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
is a lattice, it suffices to show that every non-empty directed subset of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
with an upper bound has a supremum. In doing so, we also prove the last statement of the present theorem.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)^{+}
be directed and bounded above, say by
R\in\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
. Then, for all
x\in X^{+}
, the set
\{T(x):T\in\mathcal{A}\}
is bounded above by
R(x)
. Since
Y
is Dedekind-complete, the set
\{T(x):T\in\mathcal{A}\}
has a supremum, which we denote by
S(x)
. We will use Theorem
4.2
to extend
S
.

To show that
S
is additive, let
x,y\in X^{+}
. Then
S(x+y)=\sup_{T\in\mathcal{A}}T(x+y)=\sup_{T\in\mathcal{A}}(T(x)+T(y))\leq\sup_{T\in\mathcal{A}}T(x)+\sup_{T\in\mathcal{A}}T(y)=S(x)+S(y)
. For the reverse inequality, let
T,U\in\mathcal{A}
. Since
\mathcal{A}
is directed, there is a
V\in\mathcal{A}
such that
T,U\leq V
. Hence
T(x)+U(y)\leq V(x)+V(y)=V(x+y)\leq S(x+y)
. Taking the supremum over all
T\in\mathcal{A}
and then over all
U\in\mathcal{A}
gives
S(x)+S(y)\leq S(x+y)
, concluding the proof that
S
is additive. For
\mathbb{P}
-homogeneity, let
x\in X^{+}
and
\pi\in\mathbb{P}
. By Lemma
2.6
,
S(\pi x)=\sup_{T\in\mathcal{A}}T(\pi x)=\sup_{T\in\mathcal{A}}\pi T(x)=\pi S(x)
. By Theorem
4.2
(again, using the assumption that either
Y
is sequentially
\mathbb{P}
-Archimedean or
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{L}_{1}
),
S
extends uniquely to a positive operator from
X
to
Y
, which we also denote by
S
. It is immediate that
S
is the supremum of
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
, and so
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(X,Y)
is Dedekind-complete.
∎

Since
\mathbb{L}
is a Dedekind-complete Archimedean
\mathbb{L}
-vector lattice, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
X
be a directed
\mathbb{L}
-module with the RDP. Then
X^{\sim}
is a Dedekind-complete
\mathbb{L}
-vector lattice, and its lattice operations are given by the Riesz-Kantorovich formulas. ∎
