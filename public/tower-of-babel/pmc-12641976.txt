Secondary ocular syphilis in an immunocompromised patient: a case report
Authors: Richard Farnan, Hamid Nafees, Gordon Farnan, Maedbh Rhatigan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOcular syphilis is an uncommon but important complication of syphilis, most often presenting as posterior or panuveitis in latent syphilis of unknown duration. If left untreated, ocular syphilis can result in permanent vision loss, which underscores the importance of appropriate evaluation and treatment of ocular syphilis.Case presentationA unique case, involving a 32-year-old Irish man who presented with sudden-onset deterioration in his right-sided visual acuity, is presented. This man reported significant deterioration in his right-sided visual acuity from a previous baseline of 6/6 vision to “counting fingers.” His ophthalmic history was unremarkable up until that event, but his medical history encompassed psoriatic arthritis, managed by sustained monoclonal antibody therapy in the form of adalimumab.Clinical findingsThe clinical findings at initial presentation included painless monocular right-sided visual loss with gradual onset and discomfort associated with active eye movement alongside frank scalp tenderness. Further examination findings demonstrated a grossly swollen right optic nerve, right-sided visual acuity diminished to “counting fingers” amid normal intraocular pressure measurements. The patient was admitted to hospital and initially managed with intravenous steroid therapy. Subsequent serologic testing revealed an underlying diagnosis of syphilis. Targeted antibiotic therapy was commenced thereafter with further ophthalmic reviews to follow.ConclusionSyphilis may go undetected without a high index of clinical suspicion owing to its often nonspecific presentation. All patients with ocular inflammation should have syphilis testing as a part of their infectious workup with both treponemal and nontreponemal testing.Keywords: Ocular syphilis, Steroids, Monoclonal antibody

Introduction

Ocular syphilis refers to an infection affecting the central nervous system with subsequent invasion of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), secondary to Treponema pallidum infection [1]. This was a historically common clinical entity in the pre-antibiotic era, with estimates of 25–35% in patients with the infection experiencing ocular complication(s) [2]. In the contemporary context, ocular syphilis is more commonly associated with coexistent human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection. A recent state-of-the-art-review published in Clinical Infectious Diseases showed that, among 41,187 syphilis cases, neurological or ocular manifestations were reported in 2% of cases, with higher prevalence among patients with HIV [15].

Ocular syphilis may develop at any stage of the infection. It can affect any eye structure, however posterior uveitis and panuveitis are most appreciated by clinicians. These conditions tend to surface with diminished visual acuity that can culminate over time to permanent—and irreversible—visual loss. Additional ophthalmic associations include interstitial keratitis, intermediate uveitis, and retinal vasculitis. Of note, ocular syphilis may intensify if not diagnosed expediently and in cases where inappropriate steroid therapy is given to affected patients [3].

Ocular syphilis may be the seminal presentation for underlying infection. This affords clinical grounds for a comprehensive infection screen to achieve diagnostic goals in those impacted by unconventional visual loss. We present the case of an immunocompromised patient who presented with unilateral vision loss.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old Irish man was referred to our eye emergency department with a myriad of symptoms which included jaw pain, right-sided headache, scalp discomfort, and sudden-onset painless right visual loss over a duration of 3 days. He denied any significant antecedent trauma event as a lucid historian but cited pain following eye movement. He was initially and incorrectly managed by a medical team as suspected giant cell arteritis (GCA) with oral steroid therapy initiated. The jaw pain and scalp discomfort created a clinical picture suggestive of GCA, but this differential was weakened by the young age profile of the man. His right-sided visual acuity was reduced to “counting fingers” from a previous baseline of 6/6, while his left-sided visual acuity remained intact with 6/6 documented. Medical history included psoriatic arthritis that had been managed medically with monoclonal antibody therapy in the form of adalimumab administered via the subcutaneous route at a dose of 40 mg every 2 weeks. His family history was noncontributory. He worked as a hotel manager and is a nonsmoker. He was initially treated with oral prednisolone 60 mg once daily, for which he received 7 days of treatment.

Clinical findings

Family and personal history were unremarkable for any ophthalmic pathology. His intraocular pressures lay within an acceptable range bilaterally. Significant pain on eye movement that was most pronounced on lateral gaze was recognized during the physical examination. Clinical examination revealed blot hemorrhages observed superiorly in the right retina accompanied by gross optic nerve swelling with a flat retina. The left eye examination did not relevant significant pathology. The anterior segment findings were noncontributory in relation to both eyes examined. The anterior chamber was deep and quiet bilaterally. There was no evidence of flare or fibrin in each eye. The optical coherence tomography (OCT) macula scan was unremarkable bilaterally.

Diagnostic assessment

A battery of tests were requested on admission to hospital. Admission blood work illustrated a significant elevation in white cell count and neutrophils in addition to elevated inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR). Initial white cell count was shown to be 17.0 × 10⁹/L alongside an elevated neutrophil count of 14.21 × 10⁹/L. A CRP of 59 mg/L and ESR of 58 mm/hour were reported by the hospital laboratory. Additional immunology testing revealed the patient to be negative for the following tests: quantiferon, anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP), antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA), and antimitochondrial antibody.

Given the significant rise in acute inflammatory markers, a blood-borne viral screen was sent in addition to a complete infectious disease panel. Treponema pallidum was later detected, and the remaining screen was noncontributory. Serum protein electrophoresis was normal alongside normal immunoglobulin reports. The man was HIV negative on serological investigation. Liver autoantibody profile testing was also reassuring. The anti-parietal cell, anti-smooth muscle antibody, and anti-liver–kidney microsomal (anti-LKM) antibody results were negative. A lumbar puncture was performed and was proven to be noncontributory.

The urea and electrolytes and liver function tests all lay within an acceptable range. Rheumatological panel testing was reported as normal. The patient was negative for myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) antibodies and neuromyelitis optica (NMO) antibodies. Relevant imaging included an erect chest x-ray and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) orbits including contrast. Radiological investigations were reported as entirely unremarkable. There were no financial concerns in relation to ordering tests owing to the level 4 nature of the clinical site. Additionally, the native English-speaking nature of the patient removed any language barriers.

Differential diagnosis

The ophthalmology team initially treated this case as an atypical optic neuritis presentation. The significant pain following eye movement accompanied with monocular visual loss rendered optic neuritis a reasonable differential and important component of the clinical workup. Reassuring optical coherence tomography (OCT) scans of the disc accompanied with normal retinal nerve fiber layers rendered optic neuritis an unlikely cause for pathology. MRI orbit with contrast was noncontributory and accompanied by negative blood test results for MOG antibodies and NMO antibodies, making this an unlikely cause for presentation.

A wide variety of other differentials were considered, including intermediate uveitis, white dot syndromes, macular choroiditis, acute retinal necrosis (ARN), progressive outer retinal necrosis (PORN), and Behçet’s disease. The history and clinical examination findings excluded these listed differentials systematically.

Laboratory tests revealed positive syphilis serology with detectable Treponema pallidum antibodies (TPHA) and rapid plasma reagin (RPR) at a titer of 1:128. In the aforementioned clinical context, this result was suggestive of ocular syphilis. At this point, the infectious disease team were invited for their expert oversight.

Therapeutic intervention

This man faced initial medical management with oral prednisolone 60 mg taken once daily for a duration of 7 days, prior to the timely referral to ophthalmology colleagues. A clinical decision was made to treat the patient on an inpatient basis. He was subsequently commenced on intravenous methylprednisolone therapy 1 g once daily for a period of 3 days. The intravenous methylprednisolone therapy provided analgesic effect but no change from presenting visual acuity. In total, the patient received 7 days of oral steroid therapy and 3 days of intravenous prior to antibiotic commencement.

The visual acuity prior to antibiotic was “counting fingers.” Knowledge of the specific blood results prompted a shift toward intravenous antibiotic therapy in the form of intravenous benzylpenicillin 2.4 g four times daily, as advised by the infectious diseases team. A significant improvement in visual acuity was demonstrated following benzylpenicillin therapy with a visual acuity of 6/15 noted in the clinic review 3 weeks subsequently. He completed a 14-day course of intravenous benzylpenicillin and was later switched to a tapering course of oral steroids alongside this. He was discharged from hospital following completion of pharmacotherapy and reviewed in the outpatient clinic setting thereafter.

Follow-up and outcomes

He recorded right-sided visual acuity of 6/15 without any aid amid uncompromised color vision at the 2-month follow-up following his planned discharge. The OCT macula scan did not show signs of evolution, however the right-sided OCT disc highlighted issues related to ganglion cell loss and retinal nerve fiber layer decay, consistent with the infectious disease process.

Follow-up clinical examination excluded right-sided papilledema. This represented a departure from the initial assessment. The formative pain following eye movement had subsided during the hospital admission. A gradual decline in symptom severity had been cited by the patient throughout the treatment course.

At the 2-month follow-up, the patient was entirely asymptomatic. Right-sided visual acuity had stabilized to a revised baseline of 6/15 from an initial visual acuity of “counting fingers.” The RPR test revealed a titer of 16 at the follow-up review with an initial RPR titer of 128 noted. This reduction is an important marker for disease resolution.

The most recent follow-up revealed a right visual acuity of 6/12 unaided with no improvement with pinhole test. His intraocular pressure stood at 15 mmHg in the right eye and 16 mmHg in the left eye. Optos wide-field imaging revealed a decrease in disc edema in the right eye consistent with clinical improvement in his condition. The patient had completed relevant antibiotic therapy and is being monitored by the infectious disease and ophthalmology team. This man will continue to be followed up by the ophthalmology clinical service on an annual basis.

Discussion and conclusion

Syphilis refers to a multisystem bacterial infectious disease caused by the Gram-negative spirochete Treponema pallidum species and continues to pose a formidable public health challenge [4]. It can be subdivided into four stages: primary, secondary, tertiary, and latent, which are further described in Table 2. The primary and secondary stages represent the peak infectious periods.

There is a growing global rise in all stages of syphilis in the last decade, with rising trends most pronounced in territories such as Canada, Europe, and China [5]. A report produced by the Centers for Disease and Prevention (CDC) documented that 86% of new cases of syphilis occur in men [6]. The overall incidence of syphilis pathology is higher in men, particularly in the men who have sex with men (MSM) cohort, with reports showing 250 times greater prevalence compared with females [8]. Surprisingly, emerging evidence has shown an increase in female involvement alongside a growing increase in the prevalence of congenital syphilis [7]. This impacts greatly on pregnancy outcomes, including stillbirth and neonatal blindness [8].

Ocular syphilis is a less recognized manifestation of its parent infection, with prevalence rates ranging from 0.6% to 2.6% [6]. The ocular presentation is broad and can involve anterior uveitis, intermediate uveitis, posterior uveitis, panuveitis, optic neuropathy, retinal vasculitis, and interstitial keratitis [9]. Its clinical presentation is often nonspecific and involves blurred vision, floaters, red eye, and photophobia [10]. Ocular involvement can occur at any stage of the disease process, with some studies notable for almost half (47%) being diagnosed at the later advanced stages in stark contrast to a minority of 2% happening during primary syphilis [9].

Our specific case presentation involved painless monocular vision loss accompanied by a grossly swollen optic nerve and ganglion cell loss affecting the right eye. This initially directed management toward atypical optic neuritis. Objective investigations remolded the diagnosis and consequential therapy. In this case, ultrawide-field imaging was utilized and involved the use of nonmydriatic wide-angle digital cameras. These produced ultrawide-field (UWF) imaging, enabling evaluation of the central and peripheral retina and included Optos Optomap and Zeiss Clarus 500 [17]. In our case, Optos widefield imaging was utilized, with the right posterior segment revealing extensive optic disc edema and areas of vasculitis in the periphery with nasal vessel involvement, as seen in Fig. 1. This disc edema is not typical for optic neuritis and prompted a shift in focus toward other differentials. An infrared disc image accompanying the OCT is shown in Fig. 2, revealing a swollen optic disc with retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) ganglion cell loss. The Optos image of the right posterior segment at the 3-week follow-up illustrated a significant reduction in optic nerve swelling, as seen in Fig. 3. There are areas of peripapillary disc hemorrhages shown here, and persistent regions of vasculitis. Infrared disc OCT imaging also confirmed the reduction in optic nerve swelling, as illustrated in Fig. 4. In the literature, in a retrospective study involving 20 patients diagnosed with acute and chronic syphilitic posterior uveitis, 75% revealed chorioretinitis and 15% panuveitis [21]. Our case differed from this published literature as the anterior chamber was quiescent in our patient, resulting in a slightly atypical presentation. Other posterior segment manifestations include vasculopathy, necrotizing retinitis, and retinal detachment [22].

Prompt detection and decisive management of ocular syphilis are fundamental to preserve lifelong vision in affected patients. A single retrospective study showed that a delay in treatment of greater than 12 weeks culminated in diminished visual acuity at the 6-month follow-up [11]. An additional case series illustrated two affected patients being subject to permanent vision loss, as a consequence of delayed treatment [12]. In our case, appropriate antibiotic therapy was initiated in a timely manner, resulting in a desirable visual outcome. Treponema pallidum cannot be cultured in the laboratory, which means that diagnosis relies on clinical findings allied with serologic analysis involving specific nontreponemal and treponemal tests. The treponemal test chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassay (CMIA) detects immunoglobulin G and immunoglobulin M antibodies to Treponema pallidum [13]. This test is highly sensitive but lacks specificity, meaning that supplementary testing is required. In our case, the patient was anti-Treponema pallidum IgM and T. pallidum total positive at initial presentation with a titer value of 128.

Ocular syphilis—as a clinical entity—is managed in a similar manner to neurosyphilis. The British Association for Sexual Health and HIV guidelines recommend that benzylpenicillin 1.8–2.4 g be administered intravenously every 4 hours for a period of 14 days [14]. The role of steroids is contentious in the treatment of ocular syphilis and is the subject of much debate. The Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction can occur in late syphilis and can be life-threatening if it involves specific sites. Its pathogenesis is not fully understood, and the role of steroids is yet to be fully clarified, with many data reports proving inconclusive [15]. The timing and role of steroids in this regard have not been fully elucidated in randomized controlled trials. Steroids are believed to help prevent wider complications. It is believed that early steroid introduction may prevent this reaction and associated clinical deterioration including optic neuritis and neuroretinitis [16]. In our case, the patient had been treated with a total of 10 days of systemic steroids prior to appropriate antibiotic intervention.

Our case of ocular syphilis was challenging owing to an atypical and slightly misleading clinical presentation that specified jaw pain and scalp tenderness. While the patient showed elevated inflammatory markers, there was a dearth of infective features noted, and the patient was systemically well. These findings along with his medical background of psoriatic arthritis swayed attention toward an autoimmune cause for symptom pathology. Our patient was managed on adalimumab therapy owing to a background of psoriatic arthritis. Adalimumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that targets and inhibits tumor necrosis factor alpha (anti-TNF-α) agent and is used to treat a variety of inflammatory conditions. TNF-α is an important proinflammatory cytokine and is associated with inflammatory and autoimmune disease. Its role lies in the formation of granuloma and macrophage activation. Inhibition of TNF-α increases the risk of bacterial, viral, and fungal pathology [18]. The association between anti-TNF therapy and neutropenia is a relevant clinical point with opportunistic infection prevalent here [19]. Adalimumab has been associated with optic neuritis, demyelination, and multiple sclerosis [23]. For this reason, ocular adverse effects must be assessed accordingly. In the literature, there are approximately 21 cases of TNF-α inhibitor-associated optic neuritis defined [23]. The role of adalimumab in this case was considered as a factor in this presentation.

In this regard, consideration was shown toward this bloodwork, with our resulting findings subsequently discounting such effect. From a clinical learning standpoint, adequate attention toward the infectious agents associated with ocular syphilis is fundamental here. Other clinicians could avoid similar mistakes by including a full infective blood screen at first presentation. This case is challenging owing to its atypical clinical presentation, complex medical background, and noncontributory clinical history. Ocular syphilis can occur at any stage of the infection but typically occurs in the late stages. Also, panuveitis is the most common ocular manifestation in syphilis and involves eye pain, photophobia, and flashes of light [20]. Our patient showed no clinical signs suggestive of uveitis, and his infection was in its early stages. These nuances within the presentation make this case noteworthy and differ from other cases of ocular syphilis published in the literature.
