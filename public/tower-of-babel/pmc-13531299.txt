Eosinophil biology in COPD: from scientific insight to clinical treatment action
Authors: Steven P. Cass, Mona Bafadhel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
COPD is a common lung disease that causes significant global morbidity. Strikingly, elevated blood and/or airway eosinophil levels are observed in up to 40% of COPD patients. These innate immune cells are clinically associated with increased exacerbation frequency and risk. Eosinophils are key in COPD symptom management and a drive to uncover their related immunopathology is critical to our understanding of disease progression. Recent biologic treatments targeting eosinophils have had success in reducing symptoms in eosinophilic COPD patients. However, the underlying pathways that lead to these improvements is incompletely understood. Given eosinophils are important in pathogen clearance, mucus viscosity, barrier integrity and tissue remodelling, any disease modifying treatments will have several proposed mechanisms. In this narrative review, we summarise the developmental, recruitment, activation and related pathology of eosinophils in the context of eosinophil-depleting treatments to elucidate treatment efficacy. Furthermore, we highlight potential future biological pathways for therapeutics aimed at specific eosinophil mechanisms.

Introduction

COPD is an irreversible progressive lung disease that affects 480 million people worldwide [1]. COPD is punctuated with acute episodes of respiratory symptom deterioration (exacerbations) that account for both increased healthcare costs [2] and contribute directly to accelerated lung function decline [3]; thus, treatments to alleviate these events are paramount. Eosinophils are an innate immune cell associated with up to 40% of COPD patients [4]. Importantly, eosinophils can be used as an indicator of overall exacerbation risk [5, 6] as well as a predictor for positive response rate to inhaled and systemic corticosteroids (I/SCS) [5, 7–9]. Monoclonal antibodies (mABs) targeting eosinophilic pathways, such as anti-interleukin (IL)-5 (mepolizumab) [10–12] and anti-IL-4Rα (dupilumab) [13, 14], are also now used to prevent exacerbations. Direct apoptosis of eosinophils with anti-IL5 receptor (R) (benralizumab) has also been shown to be effective at treating acute exacerbations [15]. These improvements can be life altering for patients and highlight a vital role for the eosinophil in COPD immunopathogenesis. However, to date the role of eosinophils in COPD and how disease modifying treatments improve symptoms, lung function decline and exacerbation risk is incompletely understood.

To understand the role of eosinophils in COPD it is important to first establish the current immunopathogenesis paradigm in which eosinophils enter. This environment is of importance as it directly influences the mediators that induce eosinophil development, recruitment and function. COPD development is considered to result from chronic exposure to inhaled noxious particles which lead to a persistent activation of epithelial cells and macrophages in the small airways [16, 17]. A subsequent proinflammatory environment orchestrates an aberrant chronic cellular environment in the lungs which includes the recruitment of neutrophils and monocytes [17], skewed CD4+/CD8+ ratio (favouring increased CD8+ T-cell numbers [18]), and increased circulating and small airways containing B-cells [17, 19]. The resulting inflammatory milieu further drives cellular senescence in COPD airways further elevating the inflammatory state [20, 21]. Due to this perpetual inflammation, a skewed autoantibody profile weakly linked to emphysema is established [22–24]. To date, no studies assessing type-IV hypersensitivities in COPD have been studied. The persistent release of proteases and immune mediators from activated cell types contribute to the hallmarks of disease by inducing mucus hypersecretion [16], advancing lung aging [25–27] and causing tissue remodelling [28]. In some patients, this skewed inflammatory state is related to eosinophilic and/or type 2 inflammation. Whilst much is known about treatments in this group of patients in asthma, it is clear the immunopathogenesis of eosinophils in COPD is less studied. Here, we review the impact of COPD on eosinophil development, recruitment, and functionality to offer insight into disease pathogenesis and treatment benefits.

Development, egress and chemotaxis of eosinophils

Up to 40% of COPD patients have increased blood and/or sputum eosinophil counts during stable disease [29–31]. This threshold is not based on the clinical blood eosinophil cutoff of ≥500 cells·µL–1 but rather a cutoff determined post adjustment for factors such as atopy in the general population [32]. Thus, while not an eosinophilic disease, it has become accepted that eosinophilic COPD, defined as a sputum eosinophil count >3% and/or blood eosinophil count ≥300 cells·µL–1 are important thresholds for clinical outcomes associated with positive treatment responses [30, 33–35] to available treatments including ICS, SCS and mABs, as referred to above. However, whilst randomised clinical trials and guidelines propose using these cutoffs for therapeutic management of COPD, it is worth noting that cell activity not just cell number, as is seen in cancer biology [36], may be playing a greater role in clinical disease manifestation. Notably, there is inherent variability in eosinophil levels, as is seen with neutrophils and lymphocytes [37], and are seen to transiently decrease over the course of each day in nonshift workers [38]. Blood eosinophil levels remain stable in the majority of COPD patients over both a 1-month period [39] and between 1 and 4 years [40, 41]. The underlying processes that lead to some COPD patients to have elevated eosinophils is not yet known but for therapeutic decision-making it is necessary to know that these levels remain relatively stable.

Eosinophilopoiesis mediated via soluble and transcriptional factors

Eosinophils mature in the bone marrow in a process that takes 5–7 days during homeostasis, which can be expedited post insult to less than 24 h [42]. The immediate trigger response of IL-13 and IL-5 could explain the increase in eosinophils noted at acute exacerbations [4]. The combination of IL-3, IL-5 and granulocyte–macrophage-colony stimulating factor (GM-CSF) is needed for eosinophil commitment from other myeloid cell paths [43, 44]. Using elegant murine studies these soluble mediators have been demonstrated to be integral to an orchestrated cascade of transcriptional factors including CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein (C/EBP), PU.1, interferon consensus sequence binding protein (Icsbp) and GATA-1 [45–47]. C/EBP activation commits myeloid cells to eosinophil progenitor cells (CD125+CD38+CD34+) [48]. The subsequent interaction of GATA-1 and PU.1, an E26 transformation-specific transcription factor, together synergistically amplify GATA-1-mediated gene transcription regulating eosinophil lineage differentiation [49–51]. To note, Icsbp provides an additional key level of regulation but the mechanism by which this occurs is not understood [52]. Ultimately, through GATA-1 binding sites, eosinophil-related genes [53] including major basic protein (MBP) [49], C–C chemokine receptor 3 (CCR3) and IL-5R [54] are activated and eosinophil lineage is committed (figure 1). While eosinophilopoiesis has been determined in murine models at stable state the impact of noxious particles such as cigarette smoke, key to the initiation of COPD, have not yet been modelled in these systems. Future studies aimed at uncovering how COPD influences eosinophilopoiesis is needed.

Between IL-3, IL-5 and GM-CSF, several murine studies demonstrate the importance of IL-5 to eosinophil development. A redundancy for IL-3 and GM-CSF in eosinophil production [55, 56] has been observed with 10-fold higher levels of IL-3 and GM-CSF than IL-5 needed for eosinophil lineage commitment and maturation [57]. Furthermore, murine models demonstrate IL-5 overexpression is sufficient to cause eosinophilia [58–60], while deficiency results in mature blood eosinophil level reductions [61, 62]. IL-5 performs several roles in maturation with incremental upregulation of IL-5R in eosinophil progenitors important for promote downstream affects [63, 64]. Notably, IL-5 enables transit amplification of eosinophil progenitors [65], the proliferation capacity of early eosinophil progenitor cells, but cellular or transcriptional maturation is still possible in IL-5 deficiency [65]. These data highlight that IL-5 is critical for swift eosinophil proliferation as might occur in response to stimuli. Thus, while treatments that inhibit IL-5 can decrease eosinophil development they do not fully ablate eosinophil numbers. This inability to totally ablate eosinophils, especially cells resident in damaged tissue [66] may explain the differential clinical efficacy seen between asthma and COPD trials with these mABs. Indeed, mAB such mepolizumab decrease blood eosinophil numbers differentially in asthma and COPD suggesting that difference in eosinophil subsets or environmental-induced factors in different disease states leading to differences in clinical efficacy [11, 67]. Importantly, the ability to rapidly and directly deplete eosinophils (via IL-5R), in the case of benralizumab has proven to be effective at acute exacerbation treatment in Ramakrishnan et al. [15]. However, this has not translated to chronic treatment for COPD [68] and is likely due to population selection rather than nonimportance of the underlying biology.

Egress and recruitment

Eosinophilic COPD is largely defined by blood eosinophil counts due to the ease and reproducibility in different clinical settings. Eosinophil egress into the circulation following development in the bone marrow in a process involving IL-5 as a predominant factor. Combined with eotaxin-1 (CCL11) [69], IL-5 stimulates β2 integrin-mediated adhesion [70] while diminishing α4 integrin-mediated adhesion to allow eosinophil egress out of the bone marrow [71]. Palframan et al. [71] postulate that plasma CCL11 and bone marrow/plasma IL-5 acts synergistically to enable eosinophil mobilisation into the circulation over the course of 7 days. Therefore, it is proposed that anti-IL-5 strategies in addition to decreasing eosinophilopoiesis have a double-hit strategy which also inhibit mature eosinophil egress to reduce blood levels. This is vital as evidence show increased blood eosinophils are associated with lung function decline [72–74] and poorer lung function trajectories [75] in healthy volunteers, active smoker without lung disease and in patients with COPD.

Noxious particles perform a critical role in the initiation of COPD and the recruitment of immune cells to the lungs but surprisingly there is little evidence of the direct effects of smoking particulate on eosinophil migration. Clinical evidence suggest that current smoking is associated with increased circulating eosinophils [76, 77]. Moreover, within the human lung increased eosinophil products are found in the airway of ever-smokers [77]. In contrast, in vivo murine studies showed that cigarette smoke exposure does not induce airway eosinophils in allergen models [78]. Whilst mechanisms have not been studied in COPD, post hoc analyses of clinical trials have shown that in current smokers, exacerbation risk is increased with eosinophilic COPD, but that the magnitude of ICS response is reduced in current smokers [79]. This marks the eosinophil as a pathogenic inflammatory pathway associated with increased risk of exacerbations, even in the context of smoking. One potential mechanism for this finding is that while ever-smoking status in COPD may induce greater circulating numbers the ongoing toxic effects of smoke in the airways may led to increase eosinophil toxicity.

In addition to IL-5, other chemokines recruit eosinophil to the lungs but are yet to be utilised clinically. Arachidonic acid metabolites (including leukotrienes (LT)B4, LTC4, LTCD4, LTE4 and prostaglandin (PG)D2) are found to be increased in COPD [80, 81]. These are involved in eosinophil recruitment through LTB4 [82] and PGD2 type 2 receptors [83]. To date, anti-leukotriene treatments have been trailed in COPD in small inconclusive clinical trials, but no study has yet stratified on eosinophil counts [84]. Other potent lipid chemoattractant are yet to be studied include platelet-activating factor (PAF), which activates trafficking via the PAF receptor across the endothelium [85], as well as self-produced arachidonic acid metabolites termed eoxins which can lead to increased endothelial cell permeability [86]. Overall, these data suggest that lipid pathways require further investigation in eosinophilic COPD.

Several other protein chemoattractants are involved in eosinophil chemotaxis. IL-5 and eotaxins (CCL11, CCL24 and CCL26) perform a pivotal role in lung recruitment alongside macrophage inflammatory protein-1 α and β (CCL3 and CCL4), monocyte chemoattractant proteins (CCL7 and CCL13) and RANTES (CCL5) [87–91]. Furthermore, the prototypical type 2 cytokines IL-4 and IL-13 are indirectly associated with eosinophil recruitment [91]. Notably all these factors are increased in COPD, promoting conditions for eosinophil lung recruitment [4, 80, 92, 93]. In contrast to IL-5, which has direct effect on eosinophil recruitment, IL-4 and IL-13 activate a signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6)-dependent pathway, releasing CCL11, and IL-5 to initiate chemotaxis [94–96]. To this end, eotaxins are the dominant lung recruiter functioning via the CCR3 and CCR1 [97] with CCL11 induced within 6 h of insult with further subsequent CCL24 and CCL26 release correlating with eosinophil accumulation up to 24 h post-insult [90] through weaker CCR3 interaction [98, 99]. Notably, a recent study in nasal polyps showcases a role for IgE in the upregulation of CCR3 to enhance eosinophil migration into diseased tissue [100]. CCR3 can be inhibited by C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 6 (CXCL9) and thus leads to inhibition of eosinophil chemotaxis [101]. To date, no clinical studies have investigated antagonism of CCR1/3 in COPD. Dupilumab, an mAB targeting IL4Ra and thus the IL4/13 pathways, showed clinical efficacy in reducing exacerbations in patients with eosinophilic COPD [13, 14]. Interestingly, biomarker analysis demonstrated dupilumab reduced serum CCL26 by 35.5% without any impact on eosinophil levels in the blood. This data would suggest the relationship of mediators responsible for recruitment of eosinophils in COPD may have greater impact on clinical outcomes (figure 2).

Eosinophil counts and subsets in the lung

Following recruitment to the lung, eosinophils largely remain in the sub-epithelium whereupon they are capable of tissue homeostatic or pathogen defence processes [17, 102–104]. The transmigratory process from the blood to the lungs in COPD patients have not been fully explored. Weak correlations of eosinophils in the blood and lung [31, 105, 106] may reflect methodological variance in collection rather than the true absence of correlation. Alternatively, differences of extravasation into the lung which is mediated by several extracellular molecules. L-selection (CD62L) is expressed on all nonactivated eosinophils [107] and is used to mediate initial adhesion to the endothelium [108] to enable arrest plus transmigration into the lung. Eosinophil integrins, lymphocyte function antigen (LFA-1; CD11a/CD18) and Mac-1 (CD11b/CD18) then interact with intercellular adhesion molecule (ICAM)-1 domains to enable transmigration [109, 110]. VLA-4 (α4β1) is also an important tether for eosinophils [111] through its interaction with vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) on the endothelial surface in a process assisted by CD4+ T-cell upregulation of VCAM-1 [112]. Currently, no studies in COPD have investigated these mechanisms to understand the relevance to the site of the eosinophil and its interplay with clinical outcomes to offer a new paradigm to target.

Once in the lung, emerging evidence suggests that eosinophil subsets are present. Resident (CD62L+) and inflammatory (CD123+) eosinophil subsets are proposed based on markers observed in asthma [113] and observed in the airways post-natally [113–115]. Specifically, Mesnil et al. [113] showed that mouse resident eosinophils are IL-5-independent and capable of homeostatic piecemeal degranulation with a role in type-2 regulation, whereas mouse inflammatory eosinophils were observed in areas of eosinophilic airway inflammation. Recent evidence by Hu et al. [116] did not fully support these observations with no inflammatory subsets observed in the lung in unstimulated conditions based on the Mesnil et al. [113] markers assessed transcriptionally, instead highlighting that lung subsets were short-lived and mostly homeostatic resident cells. Continued investigation into whether these subtypes are present and represent distinct lineages in mice is needed, to offer insight into whether resident eosinophils transform into inflammatory eosinophils [113, 116]. By understanding migration or maturation mechanisms in mice this will allow future studies to elucidate how alterations to human eosinophil subsets may modify disease processes. To date, a single study has assessed blood eosinophil subsets in COPD patients where ∼99.4% cells were resident eosinophils compared to ∼0.6% inflammatory eosinophils as defined by the antibodies used in the analysis [117]. However, the stratification of inflammatory versus resident based on gain or loss of CD62L must be treated with caution. Recent evidence in asthmatic children post mepolizumab demonstrated that CD62L intermediate and high subsets were elevated in the sputum at exacerbation [115]. Moreover, these CD62L intermediate and high eosinophil subsets had increased inflammatory markers compared to CD62L low eosinophils [115]. These data highlight the current difficulties in translating murine to human findings, and that solitary antibody investigation (here with CD62L) to assess subsets is likely to yield unpredictable results. Overall, the effect of depleting specific eosinophil subsets, and therefore the potential removal of homeostatic processes of eosinophils, is not understood. It is therefore vital that the function of eosinophil subsets in COPD is needed to identify key subsets for exacerbation risk and response to treatment and that studies evaluating mABs demonstrate whether there is switching of subsets which leads to mechanistic efficacy.

When considering eosinophil numbers in the lung and airways, the survival of eosinophils in the lung must be considered. Eosinophil lung half-life is 8 days in allergic models [118] and cell viability for up to 2 weeks in tissue [119]. The availability of IL-3, IL-5, GM-CSF plus alarmins IL-25 and IL-33 promote eosinophil survival [120–123] and sputum IL-5 [102, 124], IL-33 [125] and bronchial GM-CSF [126] are observed to be increased in COPD. Whether these survival factors are preferentially released in eosinophilic COPD to support functional outputs is not fully understood. Transcriptional analysis in bronchial studies have observed transcriptional correlations of type-2 related genes [127, 128]. Specifically in bronchial brushings and sputum cells, increased CLCA1, CCL26, CST1 and IL-13 gene expression [106, 128–130] in addition to mast cell markers and activation genes [127, 130] are observed in eosinophilic COPD patients. On a protein level, serum IgE [128] plus sputum IL-5 [102, 124], IL-33 [125] and bronchoalveolar lavage CCL20 and CCL24 [102] were increased in eosinophilic COPD. Together, these data suggest that a type 2 proteomic environment in eosinophilic COPD airways but evaluation at the tissue level post mABs in eosinophilic COPD is warranted to understand these mechanisms further.

Eosinophil degranulation and activation

Degranulation pathways are key for functional eosinophil responses and involve the release of granular proteins into the local environment. Forming this granule complex is a crystalloid matrix of MBP-1 and MBP-2 surrounded by a matrix of eosinophil cationic protein (ECP), eosinophil derived neurotoxin (EDN) and eosinophil peroxidase (EPX) [131–133]. Eosinophil granules can remain intact extracellularly functioning autonomously as an organelle contributing to the immune milieu [134]. To this end, Charcot–Leyden crystal protein (CLC) also known as galectin-10, function as a carrier for granule components extracellularly [135, 136]. These S-type lectins also activate proinflammatory pathways including IL-1β release [137]. Composing up to 10% of cellular protein in the cytoplasm [138] CLC is released as part of eosinophil degranulation [136], extracellular vesicles [139] as well as part of eosinophil extracellular traps (EETs) [140]. Notably, CLC transcripts are observed to be increased 5-fold in eosinophilic COPD sputum [141] suggesting that in eosinophilic COPD it is a combination of increased eosinophil numbers and granule release that may potentiate damage.

Three degranulation processes are used by eosinophils to release granule proteins to enact eosinophil activity. These include piecemeal degranulation, exocytosis and cytolysis. The predominant method for eosinophil secretion is piecemeal degranulation, which involves a progressive release of granules [142]. This selective release is through self-induced shuttling vesicles without fusion to other granules or the plasma membrane [143–145]. While not fully understood, exposure to CCL11, CCL5, IL-4 and IL-5 among other analytes induce lipid body pathways that lead to the piecemeal degranulation of granule-induced vesicles [146, 147]. Thus, inhibition of IL-4, IL-13 and IL-5 pathways with available mABs (dupilumab and mepolizumab) may reduce eosinophil piecemeal degranulation and therefore decrease potential chronic cytokine release of tissue-residing eosinophils.

The alternative degranulation pathways are exocytosis and cytolysis. Exocytosis processes are split between classical and compound mechanisms. Both exocytosis mechanisms involve granule content release because of fusion with the plasma membrane controlled by SNAP receptors (SNAREs) [148, 149]. If this process occurs when a singular granule fuses directly with the plasma membrane and is extruded this is termed classical exocytosis [150]. However, if cytosolic granule–granule fusion occurs, in a frequent process that can occur multiple times before plasma membrane fusion this is termed compound exocytosis [151]. This focused release by eosinophils is target specific typically following cell adherence [152] in the presence of tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and/or immunoglobulin (Ig)G/A [146]. In contrast, cytolysis is an orchestrated form of cell death, whereupon the eosinophil plasma membrane loses integrity allowing the release of intracellular contents to the surrounding environment [142, 153]. Post cell adherence, eosinophil undergo cytolysis via activation of the receptor-interacting protein kinase 3 (RIPK3)–mixed lineage kinase-like (MLKL) signalling pathway and resultant p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase and phosphatidylinositol 3′-kinases-activated ROS production [154]. In addition to direct cell contact [155], several mediators can cause cytolysis including IgG/A [156] but the main factor is the presence of PAF [146, 153]. Overall, each eosinophil degranulation process releases granular content into the lungs exerting functional consequences.

It is predicted in COPD that the combination of the increased proinflammatory environment and ever-smoking status will impact eosinophil degranulation. Studies in asthma demonstrate increased airway eosinophil degranulation pathways in ever-smokers but are yet to be directly studied in COPD [157]. Cigarette smoke and COPD status are associated with proinflammatory environment that includes increases in CCL11, CCL5, and IL-5 linked to piecemeal degranulation as well as TNF-α which triggers exocytosis pathways [93]. The impact on cytolysis pathways in COPD is less clear with no direct evidence of increased PAF in COPD. However, PAF is inducible by cigarette smoke [158] and PAF-receptor is increased in COPD [159] predicting cytolysis pathways may be increased in COPD. Overall, the increased proinflammatory environment would predict increased degranulation in COPD, but this must be also considered in respect to cellular senescence which is increased in COPD [25–27]. Eosinophil degranulation decreases with age [160], highlighting eosinophil degranulation pathways may become deficient in an older population like patients with COPD. To date, no study has directly assessed the dual impact of cell senescence and the proinflammatory environment on eosinophil degranulation. By understanding eosinophil degranulation in COPD, specific treatments designed to target eosinophil degranulation pathways to halt function could be developed but anti-IL4 and -IL-5 may be sufficient to reduce these processes.

Eosinophil-related disease processes

The positive response in reducing exacerbation rates in eosinophilic COPD patients suggests that eosinophils and their function is key to COPD symptomology [11, 13, 15]. Eosinophils respond to multiple mediators in the local environment to combat infectious agents by releasing reactive oxygen species (ROS), EETs and granule proteins. As first proposed by Lee et al. [161] in the local immune and remodelling/repair (LIAR) hypothesis, these products also have homeostatic roles. Broadly these roles include epithelial barrier maintenance via barrier integrity [162], tissue remodelling [163, 164], mucus production/viscosity [165–167], recruitment of effector T-cell responses [168, 169] and regulation of B-cell survival [170, 171], thus promoting sustained IgA release [172]. A role for eosinophils in glucose homeostasis in adipose tissue is emerging but this role in the lung is currently not known [173, 174]. Despite these numerous roles, the functional output of eosinophils in COPD has not been evaluated. Given the broad range of functions eosinophils perform in the inflammatory environment, it is expected that eosinophils have important implications in exacerbation risk and overall lung health (figure 3). Combating infection

Pathogen clearance is implemented by eosinophils in multiple different ways. The first mechanism that eosinophils use to kill pathogen utilises the most abundant cationic protein, EPX, a heme peroxidase composed of a heavy chain subunit with similar properties to neutrophil myeloperoxidase [175, 176]. EPX is involved in the catalysis metabolism of oxygen species generation that attack nucleophiles, inducing cell death [177–179] particularly towards Gram-negative bacteria [180]. In addition to bacterial effects in EPX and MPO transgenic knockout mice, these deficiencies also elevated parasite susceptibility. EPX−/− mice generate greater IL-5 production while MBP−/− mice also demonstrated increased IL-10 in the thoracic cavity fluid [181]. ECP (RNase-3) and EDN (RNase-2), both belonging to the RNase A superfamily, have marked RNase activity [182, 183] leading to toxicity versus multiple pathogens in particular single-stranded RNA viruses [184]. While these RNases share similar functions, ECP has been observed to also have direct antibacterial properties [185] whereas EDN has a role in dendritic cell activation to upregulate antigen presentation [186]. Notably, these RNases have off-target cytotoxicity and neurotoxicity requiring careful modulation [187, 188]. Decrease in Proteobacteria colonisation and infections are observed in the presence of eosinophils in patients with COPD [189, 190]. Together, these release mediators combine to defend against viral and bacterial pathogens, and maybe of relevance during exacerbations of COPD. Granular proteins may serve as biomarkers of eosinophils, but no study has yet designed treatments to alleviate granular burden or activity.

Supporting granular release, eosinophils release ROS in response to stimuli. ROS encompasses a range of highly reactive chemicals including oxygen superoxide, hydroxyl radicals, peroxides and hydroperoxides generated predominantly through a byproduct of aerobic respiration [191]. Eosinophils have sixfold the number of mitochondria per cell compared to neutrophils, highlighting a potential greater capacity for eosinophil-related ROS release [192]. In addition to byproducts of the electron transport chain, ROS can be directly generated through oxidative bursts by eosinophils [193, 194]. Complementing the direct effects of ROS via DNA and protein oxidation, in a ROS-dependent manner, eosinophils eject mitochondrial DNA with membrane-bound cell-free granules to produce EETs in a process called EETosis [195, 196]. The selective release of only mitochondrial DNA prevents eosinophils from undergoing cell death (unlike neutrophils) thus maintaining eosinophil ability to bind and kill bacteria via other mechanisms [197]. It has been postulated that EETs prevent invasion of bacteria at points of epithelial damage where eosinophils migrate [162, 197, 198]. However, the uncontrolled release of ROS and EETs has the potential to lead to senescence phenotypes [20, 21] via excess ROS release as well as a proposed source of autoantigens. The eosinophil performs a critical role in combatting infection, but its removal is associated with lowered exacerbation risk despite exacerbations being associated with a pathogen trigger [4]. This dichotomy is intriguing and proposes in COPD that it is an uncontrolled eosinophil response, regardless of the trigger, which induces increased symptomology. To this end, Radhouani et al. [199] demonstrated lung eosinophil innate immune memory in response to bacterial infection. Therefore, this suggests that, in COPD, eosinophil progenitors can be imprinted and primed to over-respond to pathogens and thus result in exacerbation in addition to responding to the local immune environment. Investigations aimed at elucidating eosinophil function at baseline and at exacerbation are now warranted to understand increase exacerbation symptoms and risk. Efficacy of mABs in reducing exacerbations in eosinophilic COPD (mepozilumab and dupilumab) is between 15 and 34% [10, 13]. Critically, which types of exacerbation are being reduced by these mABs has not been studied in clinical trials. It could thus be postulated that efficacy in reducing eosinophilic exacerbations with these targets may be much higher and that “breakthrough” exacerbations are underlying noneosinophilic exacerbations, especially in patients with severe and very severe disease. Eosinophilic COPD exacerbations are known to be responsive to SCSs [7, 8] and, more recently, depletion of eosinophils at the time of an acute eosinophilic exacerbation with IL-5Ra (benralizumab) [15] as found to be clinically and significantly superior to SCSs. However, the relationship between the airway “pathogen-ome” and treatment with mABs in eosinophilic COPD both at chronic and acute state warrants further evidence.

Airway remodelling

Eosinophil granular proteins are active molecules capable of damaging self and nonself. For instance, the cationic charge of MBP facilitates binding to anionic surfaces such as membrane lipids and thus increases membrane permeability [200] resulting in toxicity indiscriminately for both bound infectious agents and host membranes [201, 202]. For instance, in the lung, MBP release leads to lung barrier damage [203]. As mechanism to mitigate off target effects, MBP precursors are lost during differentiation resulting in mature eosinophils becoming unable to produce more MBP upon activation [204]. Eosinophil-derived IL-13 has also been shown to be associated with airspace enlargement through induced alveolar macrophage matrix metalloproteinase-12 (MMP-12) release [205]. Furthermore, human chronic airways disease patients with increased eosinophils have elevated IL-13 and MMP-12, further associated with lung function decline [205]. Overall, data suggests eosinophils contribute to epithelial damage in the airways in part via an IL-13 mechanism. These data suggest that anti-IL-4R therapies may protect the epithelial barrier.

Expanding on damage to the epithelium, evidence also suggests the contribution of eosinophil to collagen deposition and barrier integrity. In mouse models of asthma, eosinophil-deficient mice were protected from peribronchiolar collagen deposition and increases in airway smooth muscle, without an effect on airway hyperresponsiveness [206]. Reports further suggest that eosinophil granules are an important stimulant for transforming growth factor (TGF)-β1 release and fibroblast contraction in tissue remodelling processes [163, 164, 207]. These processes may contribute to the increased basement membrane thickening observed in eosinophilic COPD [102, 104]. The role eosinophils perform in collagen deposition in COPD is not known. In addition to direct effects on structural cells, emerging evidence demonstrates a role for eosinophils in the regulation of B-cell survival [170, 171, 208] and IgA release [172]. For instance, in the gastrointestinal tract, the loss of eosinophils is associated with increased leakage in the gut and a loss of epithelial barrier maintenance [209]. To this end, increased airway IgA and genes associated with B-cells are increased in eosinophilic COPD [210] and elevated eosinophils are associated with lymphoid aggregates in severe disease [103]. These processes resulting in IgA maintenance [172] are vital in modulating the microbiota to prevent damage via opportunistic infections. To date, how eosinophils contribute to airway remodelling in COPD is not known but together these eosinophilic roles highlight that removal of eosinophils in COPD may reduce aberrant airway remodelling at a cost of microbiota control to help alleviate symptoms.

Mucus hypersecretion

Eosinophils and the eosinophilic environment contribute to mucus plugging in the airways. IL-3, GM-CSF, IL-5 and eosinophil-derived TGF-α are sufficient to stimulate MUCSAC expression in airway epithelial cell lines leading to increased mucin production [165]. Furthermore, the oxidation capacity of ROS also contributes to increased mucin cross-linking increasing mucus viscosity [166, 167]. To this end, it is expected that eosinophilic COPD would be associated with mucus plugging. However, increased mucus plug scores have not been shown to associated with sputum eosinophil numbers in COPD current or former smokers [211]. Nonetheless, dupilumab treatment can reduce mucus plugging in asthma [212]. This data suggests that IL-4/13 pathways are important for mucus production, but the effects may not be predominately caused by eosinophil function. The role of eosinophils in COPD mucus hypersecretion and their contribution to exacerbation risk and treatment benefits warrants further investigation.

Bridging eosinophil immunobiology and eosinophilic COPD

Eosinophils define a biologically distinct inflammatory axis within COPD that links airway immunopathology to clinical phenotype. Unlike neutrophilic inflammation, which predominates across the COPD spectrum, eosinophilic inflammation reflects a distinct, inflammatory pathway characterised by eosinophil survival, trafficking, activation and regulated degranulation. This biology is sufficiently stable to translate into clinically meaningful patterns of risk and treatment response.

Elevated blood eosinophils identify patients at increased exacerbation risk in the absence of ICSs and predict corticosteroid responsiveness rather than disease severity [5, 7, 8]. This eosinophilic endotype overlaps with the frequent exacerbator phenotype and likely reflects regulated eosinophil activation and mediator release consistent with chronic airway inflammation. At the tissue level, evidence suggests eosinophils in COPD reside within an activating immune [93] rather than cytolytic environment, suggesting a regulated mediator release rather than explosive degranulation, consistent with a chronic inflammatory state that contributes to airway dysfunction and exacerbation susceptibility. Smoking modifies this relationship by suppressing type 2-associated pathways and inducing corticosteroid resistance [79, 127, 128], but paradoxically ever-smoking is associated with increased eosinophils [77] retaining eosinophils prognostic value in current smokers. During acute exacerbations, eosinophilic inflammation identifies events that are more responsive to both SCSs and mABs that target the IL-5 receptor alpha found on eosinophils offering precision medicine at acute triggers [7, 10, 15].

Summary

The eosinophil is an important innate immune cell increased in up to 40% of stable COPD patients. These eosinophilic patients are at greater risk of exacerbation but in receipt of eosinophil-depleting treatments capable of reversing this association. The underlying mechanisms why eosinophils are associated with exacerbation risk and preferential treatment responses is not known. It is well established that the eosinophil performs several roles in response to infectious agents, barrier integrity and remodelling plus mucus viscosity. Based on eosinophil biology it can be speculated that IL-5-targeting treatments reduce eosinophil maturation and egress reducing eosinophil burden and damage. Whereas anti-IL-4/IL-13 strategies likely block eosinophil activation pathways and mucus hypersecretion. Future studies using pre- and post-biologics now offer an opportunity to discover mechanisms linked to disease pathology in eosinophilic COPD. Moreover, a thorough understanding of eosinophil biology may lead to novel and targeted eosinophil treatment regimens that impact less on other immune cells. Ultimately, eosinophil-depleting treatments have provided a large step forward in the management of COPD and now provide opportunity to understand eosinophil-related pathologies in COPD progression.
