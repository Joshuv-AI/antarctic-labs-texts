Joint numerical ranges of three 4 × 4 4\times 4 matrices
Piotr Pikul, Ilya Spitkovsky, Konrad Szymański, Stephan Weis, Karol Życzkowski

Abstract
. We analyze the joint numerical range W W of three complex hermitian matrices of order four. In the generic case this 3 ​ D 3D convex set has a smooth boundary. We analyze non-generic structures and investigate non-elliptic faces in the boundary ∂ W \partial W . Fifteen possible classes regarding the numbers of non-elliptic faces are identified and an explicit example is presented for each class. Secondly, it is shown that a nonempty intersection of three mutually distinct one-dimensional faces is a corner point. Thirdly, introducing a tensor product structure into ℂ 4 = ℂ 2 ⊗ ℂ 2 \mathbb{C}^{4}=\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2} , one defines the separable joint numerical range — a subset of W W useful in studies of quantum entanglement. The boundary of the separable joint numerical range is compared with that of W W .

(c)

Deciding unitary reducibility.

A square matrix is

diagonal matrix with at least two proper blocks. Otherwise the matrix is

unitarily irreducible

We claim that the
3\times 3

Sylvester criterion allows us to decide the positive definiteness of

We prove our claim. If

(the smallest *-algebra on

\mathrm{M}_{3}

Clearly, the unital *-algebra generated by

is the set of the values of all
noncommutative polynomials in the variables

and
B_{2}
(we identify scalars with

[

5.
One-dimensional faces and corner points

Let
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}
be hermitian
n\times n
matrices and let

have
dimension
\dim(W)=3
. We prove that the intersection of any three mutually distinct
one-dimensional faces of
W
that have a point in common is a corner point of
W
if

n=4
. A counterexample is given for
n=5
.

Remark 5.1

(Recurrent arguments)
.

(a)

Every one-dimensional nonexposed face
G_{1}
of
W
is an exposed face of a
two-dimensional exposed face
H_{1}
of
W
, see the discussion below
equation (
2.5
). The face
H_{1}
is unique (because a
one-dimensional face that is included in two distinct two-dimensional
exposed faces is exposed). If
n=4
, then
H_{1}
is an example of what we call a
non-elliptic face in Definition
1.1
.

(b)

If two or more mutually distinct one-dimensional faces of
W
have
a point in common, then their intersection is an extreme point of
W
(because the infimum
in the lattice of faces is the intersection).

Lemma 5.2
.

Let three mutually distinct one-dimensional faces of
W
, at least two of which are
exposed, have a point in common. Then their intersection is a corner point of
W
.

Proof.

Let
G_{1},G_{2},G_{3}
denote the one-dimensional faces in question and assume
that
G_{2},G_{3}
are exposed. Then
G_{2}\cap G_{3}=\{p\}
holds for
an exposed point
p
of
W
, because the infimum in the lattice of exposed
faces is the intersection. Also,
G_{1}\cap G_{2}\cap G_{3}=\{p\}
.

If
G_{1}
is exposed then, by Proposition
2.4
, the normal cones

N_{W}(G_{1}),N_{W}(G_{2}),N_{W}(G_{3})
are rays or two-dimensional convex cones that
are mutually distinct and that are proper faces of
N_{W}(p)
. This implies

\dim N_{W}(p)=3
. Let
G_{1}
be a nonexposed face and
H_{1}
the
unique two-dimensional face containing
G_{1}
. The same argument as before
when
N_{W}(G_{1})
is replaced with
N_{W}(H_{1})
proves
\dim N_{W}(p)=3
.
∎

Lemma 5.4

Suppose that

P_{1}=\diag(1,1,1,0),\quad P_{2}=\diag(1,1,0,0),\quad P_{3}=\diag(1,0,0,0),

(5.1)

\bra{\varphi}&0\end{bmatrix},

\ket{\varphi}=(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}){}^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}

Since

Lemma 5.5

Assume

7.
Separable joint numerical range

Some applications in quantum physics rely of the notion of
restricted numerical range
,
in which the set of states
\mathcal{D}_{A}
is replaced with a specific subset
[
21
]
.
In the case of systems composed of two parts – e.g., two qubits – this subset might
correspond to states which are in some sense classical, and the numerical range restricted
to such states highlights the difference between classical and quantum behaviors of
quantum systems.

Let us start with the mathematical description of the physical scenario. If the physical
operations and observable quantities of the first system are described by the set
\mathrm{M}_{d}

of matrices of size d (forming a *-algebra on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
), and the operations and
observables on the second subsystem by
\mathrm{M}_{d^{\prime}}
(analogously, a *-algebra on

\mathbb{C}^{d^{\prime}}

tensor product of algebras.
For brevity, let us denote the set of density matrices of size
d

\mathcal{D}_{d}

terms of (
2.2

\mathcal{D}_{d}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{M}_{d}}

matrices of the entire system can be denoted by
\mathcal{D}_{d\times d^{\prime}}

semidefinite, unit trace operators of order
(d\cdot d^{\prime})

Here, we mostly concentrate on two-qubit systems, as this matches the topic of this paper:
two qubits correspond to the state space
\mathcal{D}_{4}
needed in the definition of joint numerical
ranges for matrices of size four.

One of the most interesting features of such a description is that some states give
rise to correlations that can not be explained by classical probability theory. In some sense, the "classical" nature of some states is captured by the fact that they can be decomposed into a convex sum of tensor products of one-subsystem states – such states are called
separable

(7.1)

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sep}}_{d\times d^{\prime}}=\operatorname{conv}\{\ \rho^{A}\otimes\rho^{B}:\rho^{A}\in\mathcal{D}_{d},\rho^{B}\in\mathcal{D}_{d^{\prime}}\}.

separable states of two qubits

here we take the ruled surfaces to be the regions with large segments
between the vertices of the convex hull, which persist despite subsampling.

The separable numerical range must lie within the joint numerical range, as separable states form a subset of all states. The two ranges can be tangent to each other, and this is known to happen if the joint numerical range contains an exposed face which is not a point.
