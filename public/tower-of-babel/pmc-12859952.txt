A novel one-tube nested fluorescence melting curve analysis for rapid detection of drug-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis
Authors: Yingchao Chang, Gaowen Liu, Xiaofei Li, Mi Zhang, Tingting Diao, Cuixian Yang, Xinlin Wu, Yungui Tu, Hongji Zhang, Chao Zhou, Pei Lei, Li Liu, Xueshan Xia, Yue Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRapidly detecting drug-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) is essential for effective tuberculosis (TB) treatment. We have developed a one-tube nested fluorescence melting curve assay (OTN-FMCA) that allows rapid and simultaneous detection of resistance to rifampicin, isoniazid, fluoroquinolones, and bedaquiline.ResultsThe OTN-FMCA uses nested PCR and locked nucleic acid (LNA)-modified probes to identify 44 mutations in six key genes (rpoB, katG, inhA, gyrA, gyrB, and atpE), achieving a detection limit of 5 copies per microliter and identifying up to 10% heterogeneous resistance. When tested on 198 culture-positive isolates, OTN-FMCA demonstrated high concordance with microarray susceptibility testing, with 100% sensitivity and 96.89% specificity. Further validation with 518 sputum samples confirmed its ability to classify patients into different drug resistance categories, including DS-TB, RR-TB, MDR-TB, pre-XDR-TB, and XDR-TB. OTN-FMCA offers a faster, more affordable, and more accessible alternative to current comprehensive drug resistance profiling methods, especially in resource-limited settings.ConclusionsOTN-FMCA is a promising tool for the rapid and accurate detection of drug resistance in MTB, which could improve the management of drug-resistant TB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04601-4.Keywords: Tuberculosis, Drug resistance, Fluorescence melting curve analysis, Mycobacterium tuberculosis

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB), remains a major global health challenge. After being overshadowed by COVID-19, TB is expected to become the leading cause of death from a single infectious disease [1]. In 2023, approximately 10.8 million people will have TB, with 8.2 million new cases diagnosed [1]. TB is expected to cause 1.25 million deaths this year, particularly in resource-limited settings. The rise of drug-resistant strains, particularly multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) and extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis (XDR-TB), further complicates control efforts. In 2023, approximately 400,000 patients are expected to develop MDR/RR-TB, but only 175,923 have been diagnosed and treated, representing only 44% of the estimated total [1]. Tackling drug-resistant TB is an urgent challenge for global health efforts.

The choice of treatment for TB depends on the drug susceptibility of the infecting strain. TB is classified based on phenotypic drug susceptibility testing (pDST) into categories such as drug-susceptible TB (DS-TB), rifampicin-resistant TB (RR-TB), multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB), extensively drug-resistant TB (pre-XDR-TB), and extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB) [2]. Effective regimens for these strains are critical for treatment success. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends several regimens for MDR/RR-TB, including a 6-month BPaL/M regimen [3], a 6-month BDLLfxCfz regimen [4], three 9-month all-oral regimens [5–7], and an 18-month individualized regimen for patients with extensive resistance [8]. Bedaquiline (BDQ) is a key drug in the treatment of MDR/RR-TB and XDR-TB, but the development of resistance during long-term treatment is a concern [9]. Accurate detection of drug resistance is essential for effective TB management.

The traditional phenotypic drug susceptibility test (pDST) is considered the gold standard for detecting drug-resistant TB, but it has drawbacks such as being time-consuming, prone to contamination, requiring specialized personnel, and high biosafety levels [10, 11]. The WHO recommends rapid molecular diagnostic tests for patients with TB symptoms [12]. Among the recommended methods, GeneXpert MTB/RIF detects common rifampicin (RIF) resistance mutations in the rpoB gene, while the Linear Probe Assay (LPA) Genotype MTBDR-plus identifies mutations in the rpoB, katG, and inhA genes for RIF and isoniazid (INH) resistance detection [13]. More recent platforms include Truenat MTB, Abbott Real-Time MTB RIF/INH, and Cobas MTB-RIF/INH [13, 14]. The Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR Assay Kit uses multiplex real-time PCR technology to detect MTB and its resistance genes [15]. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) is gaining attention for its ability to comprehensively predict drug resistance. Despite its effectiveness, high instrument costs have limited the widespread use of these assays in countries with high TB prevalence [16–18].

Probe-based fluorescence melting curve analysis (FMCA) is an effective method for detecting mutations by measuring the melting temperatures of probe-target hybrids. It has been used to identify resistance in MTB to drugs such as RIF, INH, and ethambutol (EMB) [19, 20]. However, existing assays often focus on a limited number of resistance loci, and emerging resistance patterns, including newer drugs such as BDQ, highlight the need for improved assays. To overcome these limitations, we have developed a new method called one-tube nested FMCA (OTN-FMCA). This approach increases sensitivity through a nested amplification technique and effectively detects key gene mutations associated with resistance to RIF, INH, fluoroquinolones (FQs), and BDQ in clinical samples. In addition, OTN-FMCA classifies TB patients into: DS-TB, RR-TB, MDR-TB, pre-XDR-TB, and XDR-TB, supporting targeted treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Design of primers and probes for OTN-FMCA assay

We first downloaded the nucleotide sequences for MTB (H37Rv, accession NC_000962), specifically the rpoB (759807–763325), katG (2156111 − 2153889), inhA (1674202 − 167501), gyrA (7349–7940), gyrB (5240–7267), and atpE (1461045–1461290) genes. We designed eleven sets of primers and probes to target 18 high-frequency resistance mutation sites within these genes for the OTN-FMCA method. In addition, we developed a primer and probe set for the 16S rRNA gene of a non-infectious Bacillus species as an exogenous quality control. This control was used to confirm the effective treatment of the target bacteria and to monitor for the presence of PCR inhibitors throughout the OTN-FMCA reaction. The 5’ and 3’ ends of the probes were labeled with fluorescent and quenching moieties, dual-labeled hydrolysis probes (TaqMan probes) were used for all targets, with FAM/BHQ1, HEX/BHQ1, ROX/BHQ2, and Cy5/BHQ3 serving as the fluorophore-quencher pairs. Detailed information on the primers and probes is provided in Table S1. All primers and probes were purchased from Taihe Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Preparation of gene mutant’s plasmid library

To optimize and standardize the OTN-FMAC assay, we established a quality control (QC) system that included both wild-type and mutant plasmids. The wild-type and mutant plasmids were developed from engineered plasmids based on target gene fragments. The synthesis of the mutant plasmid was provided by Taihe Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

In this study, we investigated 18 significant resistance loci associated with the resistance determinant genes of MTB, specifically rpoB, katG, inhA, gyrA, gyrB, and atpE. We designed and constructed plasmid libraries containing 44 mutations in these key resistance loci. These plasmids were used to evaluate the ability and accuracy of the OTN-FMAC assay to identify mutations associated with drug-resistance genes.

Establishment and optimization of OTN-FMCA assay

The OTN-FMCA Assay is designed to simultaneously detect 18 high-frequency resistance mutation loci associated with four antituberculosis drugs. The assay uses an asymmetric, single-tube, nested real-time PCR method for target gene amplification followed by probe melting curve analysis. The OTN-FMCA assay procedure consists of three main steps: DNA extraction, PCR reaction system preparation, and sample detection and analysis (Fig. 1a). The OTN-FMCA assay uses 12 TaqMan probes for detection. Of these, 11 target gene probes are designed to detect 18 significant resistance mutation loci within the rpoB, katG, inhA, gyrA, gyrB, and atpE genes (columns 1–11). The 12th probe is an internal positive control (IPC) to ensure the quality of the assay (Fig. 1B).

Workflow of the OTN-FMCA assay for the detection of drug resistance in Mycobacterium tuberculosis. a DNA is extracted from sputum samples using the EX-DNA MTB Kit. The OTN-FMCA reaction mixture is then prepared and aliquoted into a 96-well plate. The plate is then analyzed using a real-time PCR instrument. b Schematic layout of a 96-well plate. Each sample (rows A-H) occupies 11 wells (columns 1–11), testing for 18 high-frequency mutations associated with resistance to four anti-TB drugs. Column 12 serves as an internal positive control to ensure the quality of the reaction. Thus, the assay can assess four-drug resistance in eight samples per plate

The reaction mixture contained 27 µL PCR mix and 3 µL DNA template consisting of 3.6 µL inner forward primer (1 µM), 3.6 µL inner reverse primer (10 µM), 0.5 µL outer forward primer (5 µM), 0.5 µL outer reverse primer (5 µM), 2.4 µL probe (5 µM), 15 µL 2× Pro Taq HS Probe Premix (Accurate Bio Ltd., Hunan, China), and 1.4 µL ddH2O. This mixture was dispensed into a 96-well workstation and analyzed using a SLAN-96P real-time PCR instrument (Hongshi Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

The OTN-FMCA protocol consists of four steps: pre-denaturation, outer PCR amplification, inner PCR amplification, and melting curve analysis. It starts with pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 10 cycles of outer PCR at 95 °C for 10 s and a touchdown phase from 70 °C to 60 °C for 20 s. Inner PCR amplification is performed over 40 cycles at 95 °C for 10 s, 56 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, during which fluorescence data is collected. Finally, melting curve analysis includes denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, hybridization at 45 °C for 2 min, and a temperature ramp from 45 °C to 90 °C at 0.04 °C/s, continuously acquiring fluorescence data from the FAM, HEX, ROX, and Cy5 channels.

Evaluation of the analytical performance of the OTN-FMCA assay

The constructed wild-type and mutant plasmid DNAs were used to evaluate the lower limit of detection (LOD) and the ability of the OTN-FMCA assay to identify heterogeneous drug resistance. The wild-type plasmid DNA was diluted in a 10-fold series from 5 × 10^6 copies/µL to 5 copies/µL, with five replicates for each concentration to evaluate the LOD. A representative mutant plasmid was selected for each resistance locus, and the wild-type plasmid DNA was mixed with the mutant plasmid DNA in equal proportions to produce samples containing 0%, 1%, 5%, 10%, 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100% mutant DNA. These mixtures were tested to evaluate the detection capabilities of the assay. In addition, a set of 12 NTM bacterial strains and six non-tuberculosis strains were selected as negative controls to evaluate the specificity of the OTN-FMCA assay, with strains provided by the Yunnan Infectious Disease Hospital.

The assessment of clinical diagnostic performance

The analysis included 198 MTB strains that underwent drug susceptibility testing (DST) using TB MicroDST and OTN-FMCA methods. The MicroDST assay was performed using the Drug Susceptibility Test Kit for Mycobacterium (Culture Method), manufactured by Zhuhai YinKe Medical Engineering Co., Ltd. MicroDST results were compared with OTN-FMCA and Sanger sequencing results to evaluate the accuracy of the OTN-FMCA assay. In addition, the 518 sputum samples from MTB-positive patients were tested. DNA was extracted using the EX-DNA Mtb Kit and samples were analyzed by OTN-FMCA, with positive cases confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

DNA sequencing validation

Positive samples for mutations in resistance genes were validated by Sanger sequencing. PCR amplification of six genes-rpoB, katG, inhA, gyrA, gyrB, and atpE-was initially performed using the primer sequences listed in Table S1. The amplification program consisted of an initial step at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 30 s at 95 °C, 30 s at 50 °C, 2 min at 72 °C, and a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. Sequencing results were confirmed using the BLAST algorithm and compared with reference sequences of rpoB, katG, inhA, gyrA, gyrB, and atpE to identify the presence of mutations. The results were then analyzed using MEGA software version 7.0.

Data processing and statistical analysis

The diagnostic performance of Sanger sequencing and OTN-FMCA assays was compared by calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and concordance, along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), using MicroDST as the gold standard. The McNemar test (a paired chi-squared test) was used to assess the difference in performance between the two assays, with a significance level of p < 0.05. Statistical calculations and graphs were generated using SPSS version 22.0, GraphPad Prism version 8, and related web resources available at https://www.Chiplot.online.

Results

Development of OTN-FMCA assay

In this study, we optimized the core elements affecting the assay performance of the OTN-FMAC assay using a positive template with a concentration of 5 × 106 copies/µL of wild-type plasmid DNA. We initially evaluated six annealing temperatures: 52 °C, 54 °C, 56 °C, 58 °C, 60 °C, and 62 °C. The results indicated that the optimal annealing temperature for the inner primer set was 56 °C (Fig.S1). We also applied the probe melting curve method using an asymmetric PCR reaction and tested different forward/reverse primer ratios (1:1, 1:5, 1:10, 1:20, and 1:30). The results showed that the optimal forward/reverse primer ratio was 1:10 (Fig.S2). For OTN-FMCA, we investigated outer primer concentrations of 0.01 µM, 0.05 µM, 0.1 µM, 0.15 µM, and 0.2 µM. The results showed that the optimal outer primer concentration was 0.1 µM (Fig.S3). At this stage, we have successfully optimized the core reaction system and conditions for OTN-FMCA and established a reliable assay.

Accuracy and reproducibility of OTN-FMAC assay

In this study, we constructed a mutant plasmid with 44 resistance mutation sites in addition to the corresponding wild-type plasmid. These plasmids served as positive templates for the OTN-FMAC assay, which allowed the detection of drug-resistance genes, including rpoB, katG, inhA, gyrA, gyrB, and atpE (Fig. 1b). To validate the reproducibility of each melting temperature (Tm) value and the assay’s ability to detect mutations, we performed ten replicate OTN-FMCA assays for each of the 44 mutant and wild-type targets. The results showed clear Tm peaks for both wild-type and mutant sequences (Fig. 2). Importantly, the standard deviation (SD) of each Tm did not exceed ± 0.38 °C (Table 1).

Specificity analysis of OTN-FMCA assay

Using the 12 non-tuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) bacterial strains along with six non-mycobacterial strains as negative controls to validate cross-reactivity, the assay results are shown in Table 2. Only the MTB standard strain H37Rv showed melting peaks in all channels. Most NTM showed no melting peaks in any fluorescence channel, and only a few NTM showed very low melting peak signals; in addition, the melting temperature (Tm) values of these peaks were significantly lower than those of wild-type MTB. The test results confirmed that the OTN-FMCA method has good specificity for detecting drug resistance mutations, and although a few NTM bacteria showed lysis peaks, this did not affect the interpretation of the detection results for both wild-type and mutant strains.

Specificity analysis of OTN-FMCA assay

Sensitivity study of OTN-FMCA assay

We evaluated the lower limit of detection (LOD) for 11 detection probes targeting drug resistance genes and IPC detection probes using wild-type plasmids as templates. The test results showed that the LOD for all channels reached 5 copies/µL (Fig. 3), demonstrating strong detection capability for low-load TB samples. Notably, because our method uses a single-tube nested amplification strategy, we compared it with conventional FMCA. The results showed that the LOD for all channels in standard FMCA was 50 copies/µL(Fig.S4). This comparison clearly shows that the detection capability of our method increased tenfold with the introduction of nested amplification, significantly improving its sensitivity.

Evaluation of the analytical sensitivity of the OTN-FMCA assay. Genomic DNA from Mycobacterium tuberculosis standard strain H37Rv was diluted from 5 × 106 copies/µL to 5 copies/µL, and the OTN-FMCA assay was performed to determine the detection limit of the method

Examination of the ability of OTN-FMCA assay

To evaluate the system’s ability to detect heterogeneous resistance, we selected 11 mutant plasmid DNAs containing relevant resistance genes. We mixed these mutant plasmids with wild-type plasmid DNA at different mutation ratios: 0%, 1%, 5%, 10%, 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100% for OTN-FAMC detection. The results showed that when the mixed templates had mutation ratios of 10% or higher, all detection probes successfully identified the mutant melting peaks (Fig. 4). Furthermore, some detection probes were able to detect templates with mutation ratios as low as 1%, specifically for inhA − 15 C > T, gyrA Gly88Cys (GGC > TGC), and gyrA Asp94Tyr (GAC > TAC) (Fig. 4). This shows that the OTN-FAMC system is capable of detecting mutation rates as low as 10%, demonstrating its excellent performance in identifying heterogeneous resistance.

Comparison of OTN-FMCA, MircoDST, and Sanger sequencing

We designed a method comparison procedure, as shown in Fig. 5, in which MircoDST and OTN-FMAC were performed simultaneously on 198 clinical isolates. MircoDST identified 143 samples as susceptible and 55 as resistant to antituberculosis drugs, including 37 resistant to RIF, 44 to INH, and 12 to FQs (Table S2, Fig.S5). The OTN-FMCA assay found 5 of the 143 susceptible specimens to be resistant to RIF and noted that 1 resistant specimen was not detected as resistant to INH, with overall consistent results. Sanger sequencing confirmed mutations at resistance loci in all identified resistant specimens (Table S2). Resistance to the newly marketed drug BDQ was not analyzed because it was not tested by MircoDST.

Summary of results from three types of testing on 198 clinical isolates (OTN-FMCA, MicroDST, DNA sequencing)

We also performed a systematic analysis comparing DNA sequencing and OTN-FMCA in terms of sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, concordance, and McNemar’s test, using MircoDST as the gold standard. The results, summarized in Table 3, showed that OTN-FMCA had high sensitivity for detecting RIF, INH, and FQs resistance (100%, 97.73%, and 100%, respectively), with no significant differences between the assays (p > 0.05). Overall, OTN-FMCA is a reliable alternative for the accurate detection of drug resistance to these agents.

Clinical evaluation of the OTN-FMCA assay

We evaluated the clinical performance of the OTN-FMCA assay by screening 518 sputum samples from tuberculosis patients for resistance to RIF, INH, FQs, and BDQ. DNA sequencing confirmed the results. Of the 518 patients, 454 had drug-susceptible TB, while 64 were identified as drug-resistant TB (Fig. 6a). Among the resistant samples, 41% (26/64) had MDR-TB, 26% (17/64) were mono-drug resistant, 22% (14/64) were pre-XDR, and 11% (7/64) had XDR-TB (Fig. 6b). The most common mutation associated with INH resistance was katG 315 (39/49), while rpoB 531 was most common in RIF-resistant samples (32/53) (Table S3). The only mutation found in BDQ-resistant cases was at atpE 61 (Fig. 6c). All mutations were confirmed by DNA sequencing, and the electrograms of 10 representative samples are shown in Fig.S6. The OTN-FMCA method successfully detected heterogeneous resistance in clinical samples, and DNA sequencing confirmed this result in Fig S7. These results provide empirical support for the incorporation of OTN-FMCA into routine clinical testing for the detection of heterogeneous antimicrobial resistance, highlighting its utility in identifying mixed microbiota with different resistance profiles.

Discussion

Rapid and accurate identification of drug resistance is critical for effective TB treatment. Conventional pDST often takes weeks due to the slow growth of MTB, delaying the delivery of resistance profiles [21]. To address this, we have developed OTN-FMCA, a novel assay that uses nested amplification to increase the sensitivity of FMCA for the detection of key resistance mutations in rpoB, katG, inhA, gyrA, gyrB, and atpE. By providing rapid detection of drug resistance, OTN-FMCA enables the timely formulation of TB treatment regimens, ultimately improving patient outcomes, and classifies TB patients into drug resistance categories - DS-TB, RR-TB, MDR-TB, pre-XDR-TB, and XDR-TB - based on the profiles detected. This classification is critical for effective infection control, especially in resource-limited settings with high MDR- and XDR-TB burden [22]. By facilitating accurate patient stratification, the OTN-FMCA helps guide isolation practices, thereby reducing the risk of hospital transmission of drug-resistant strains.

The OTN-FMCA assay uses the difference in melting Tm of the detection probe after hybridization to wild-type and mutant templates to detect drug-resistant mutations [20]. Mutation types such as G/A, G/T, C/A, and C/T are relatively easy to distinguish (∆Tm > 0.5 °C). However, mutations such as G/C (∆Tm < 0.4 °C) and A/T (∆Tm < 0.2 °C) are more difficult to distinguish [23]. To address this limitation, we incorporated locked nucleic acid (LNA) modifications at the core resistance mutation sites into the probe design. This modification significantly increased the ∆Tm value, allowing for clearer differentiation between specific mutants and the wild type (Table 1; Fig. 2). In addition, the use of asymmetric PCR instead of standard PCR generates a larger amount of single-stranded DNA, which enhances probe hybridization and results in stronger melting curve signals (Table 1, Fig.S2).

In this study, we analyzed 44 different drug resistance mutations, focusing on commonly reported high-frequency mutation sites in the rpoB, katG, inhA, gyrA, gyrB, and atpE genes [24–26]. The results of the OTN-FMCA assay for the various mutant DNAs and wild-type DNAs showed a minimum melting temperature shift of 1.86 °C (∆Tm ≥ 1.86 °C) (Table 1; Fig. 2). This confirmed that the OTN-FMCA assay effectively discriminated between drug-susceptible (wild-type) and drug-resistant (mutant) strains. In addition, the addition of a 10-cycle touchdown PCR pre-amplification step to the FMCA assay improved template binding and amplification. This enhancement resulted in a significantly improved LOD of 5 copies/µL compared to the previously reported LOD of 50 copies/µL for FMCA [27]. Regarding the detection of heterogeneous resistance, our method was able to identify a proportion of resistance mutations as low as 10%, which is consistent with previous GeneXpert results reporting detection rates of 10% to 20% [28, 29]. These results suggest that the OTN-FMCA assay is a promising tool for the detection of drug-resistance genes in TB patients. Currently, the clinical significance of low-level heterogeneous resistance (1%–10%) remains under investigation. Our detection technology (OTN-FMCA) exhibits high sensitivity and is capable of detecting minor resistant subpopulations. Some studies suggest that the presence of low-frequency resistance may increase the risk of treatment failure or resistance progression, particularly in the context of drug pressure or suboptimal therapy [11]. However, international guidelines lack clear thresholds for low-level heteroresistance. Such findings should prompt further monitoring or testing, but not immediate treatment changes. More research is needed to define their clinical significance and management.

To evaluate the clinical performance of OTN-FMCA, we analyzed 198 culture-positive clinical isolates. Our results showed that OTN-FMCA had a high concordance with the gold standard microDST, achieving a sensitivity of 100% (95% CI: 90.51%−100%) and a specificity of 96.89% (95% CI: 92.90%−98.98%). In this study, we used DNA sequencing, a widely accepted method in similar research, as the reference standard [30]. This approach allowed us to validate the accuracy of the OTN-FMCA test in detecting drug resistance by providing genotypic data. Notably, five samples identified as RIF resistant by OTN-FMCA and confirmed by DNA sequencing were classified as RIF susceptible by microDST. This discrepancy resulted in lower specificity for the OTN-FMCA method compared to microDST. This may be due to the inability of microDST to detect MTB isolates with controversial rpoB mutations, which have low levels of resistance to RIF [31, 32].

Our data revealed that among the five phenotypically RIF-susceptible isolates, several disputed rpoB mutations were detected. Specifically, the L511P mutation was identified in two isolates, S531L in another two, and one isolate co-harbored both D516A and S531L mutations. According to the World Health Organization’s Mycobacterium tuberculosis mutation catalogue (2021), only D516A is classified as a group 2 mutation (sensitivity of only 3.5%), whereas L511P and S531L are categorized as group 1 mutations (sensitivity of 92.3%) [13]. Notably, previous studies have documented other controversial mutations, such as H526N, D516Y and S531C. Although distinct from those observed here, similarly highlight the ongoing ambiguity regarding the phenotypic correlation of certain group 1 rpoB mutations [33–35]. Relevant studies have also confirmed the presence of disputed rpoB mutations among patients with multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) [31]. Patients infected with such strains often experience treatment failure or relapse, underscoring the need to strengthen RIF resistance screening in suspected MDR-TB cases. Unfortunately, we were unable to obtain the final treatment outcomes for patients from whom Mycobacterium tuberculosis isolates with disputed rpoB mutations were identified, limiting our ability to further demonstrate their clinical significance. Overall, despite these discrepancies, the OTN-FMCA assay did not show a statistically significant difference from microDST at the 95% confidence interval (p > 0.05). Thus, this small difference does not significantly affect the performance of OTN-FMCA in clinical testing.

This study aimed to develop a novel OTN-FMCA assay for the detection of drug resistance directly from clinical samples such as sputum and alveolar lavage. We validated the assay using sputum samples from 518 TB patients and found different patterns of drug resistance. Specifically, approximately 63% of patients had multidrug-resistant TB or a history of multidrug-resistant TB, and 11% were multidrug-resistant, defined as resistance to two or more anti-TB drugs (excluding those resistant to both RIF and INH). Unlike assays that only detect resistance to RIF and INH, the OTN-FMCA provides more comprehensive drug resistance information and allows for detailed patient classification. This accurate drug resistance information is essential for the development of effective treatment regimens and is particularly important for resource-limited countries with a high TB burden [36].

In resource-limited countries with a high burden of TB, implementation of MTB culture, the gold standard for pDST, and genotypic DST has been hampered by inadequate facilities and the high cost of the WHO-recommended GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay [37]. In China, for example, the responsibility for TB prevention and control rests primarily with numerous county-level secondary hospitals, which often lack the resources to perform MTB culture and the gold standard DST [38]. In contrast, OTN-FMCA offers a cost-effective and accessible alternative, requiring only a multichannel fluorescent PCR instrument for detection. Based on our internal evaluation, the OTN-FMCA assay requires only approximately 2 h per run, which is much faster than phenotypic drug susceptibility testing (which takes several weeks) and whole genome sequencing (48–72 h), and comparable to line probe assays (LPA). From a cost‑effectiveness perspective, OTN‑FMCA offers the lowest per‑sample cost (≈ ¥145), a rapid turnaround time (≈ 2 h), and the simultaneous detection of resistance mutations for four key drug classes—significantly outperforming existing methods such as GeneXpert (¥510/sample), MTBDRplus (¥325/sample), and conventional culture (¥350–430/sample). Moreover, for resource‑limited primary laboratories, OTN‑FMCA requires only a standard real‑time PCR instrument, with an equipment investment of ≈ ¥50,000–80,000, which is substantially lower than GeneXpert (≈ ¥120,000–150,000) or Illumina sequencing platforms (≥ ¥300,000) (Table S4). Notably, the widespread availability of fluorescent PCR instruments in primary healthcare settings during the COVID-19 pandemic has created a favorable opportunity for rapid and accurate detection of drug resistance by OTN-FMCA in these settings [39].

The OTN-FMCA method shows promise, yet it has significant limitations. Firstly, it only considers six drug-resistance genes, whereas WGS and targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) detect a broader range of mutations [16–18]. Notably, the OTN-FMCA method overlooks several critical resistance loci, including pncA, embB, Rv0678, and pepQ. Therefore, expanding its detection panel is crucial for future studies. Moreover, as a targeted detection method, OTN-FMCA is inherently restricted to identifying predefined mutations. Although it can detect heteroresistance levels as low as 10%, it may miss less common or novel mutations outside its targeted regions, potentially leading to an underestimation of drug resistance. As a result, in diverse clinical settings where rare or previously uncharacterized resistance variants are present, this approach may yield false-negative results limitation shared by all targeted molecular DST methods. Therefore, in cases where there is a high clinical suspicion of drug resistance despite a negative OTN-FMCA result, subsequent confirmation using sequencing-based methods (such as WGS or tNGS) is recommended to rule out undetected resistance. In summary, while OTN-FMCA offers a rapid and cost-effective approach for identifying common mutations, WGS and tNGS provide a more comprehensive resistance profile. Future research should focus on expanding the gene coverage of OTN-FMCA and validating its performance across diverse clinical settings.

Secondly, the clinical trial of this study was based on strains and sputum samples from Yunnan, China. It is worth noting that there are differences in the drug resistance spectra in different regions. Although the mutations targeted by this method - such as S531L and S526L in rpoB, S315T in katG, and D94G in gyrA, etc., which are common high-frequency sites have been widely confirmed as the main molecular basis of drug resistance in multiple geographical regions such as Eastern Europe, Africa, and South Asia [28], the applicability of its detection in regions with different mutation prevalence still needs further evaluation.

Finally, a key limitation of this study is the absence of phenotypic drug susceptibility testing for bedaquiline. Although the bedaquiline resistance-associated atpE mutations reported in this study were confirmed genotypically by sequencing, their correlation with clinical resistance remains to be further validated. Therefore, the ability of our assay to predict phenotypic bedaquiline resistance should be interpreted with caution. Future prospective studies integrating phenotypic drug susceptibility testing are needed to determine the clinical predictive value of these genotypic findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the OTN-FMCA assay is a novel, rapid, and accurate method for detecting drug resistance in MTB. Its high sensitivity and specificity, along with its ability to distinguish between different categories of drug resistance, make it a valuable tool for guiding TB treatment and infection control. In addition, the affordability and accessibility of OTN-FMCA, especially in resource-limited settings, underscore its potential to significantly improve the management of drug-resistant TB.
