A capacitive-piezoelectric hybrid MEMS microphone with signal fusion for enhancing signal-to-noise ratio
Authors: Yangyang Guan, Michael Schneider, Dongsheng Li, Hemin Zhang, Jing Mi, Alexander Bertrand, Sina Sadeghpour, Chen Wang, Huicong Liu, Christ Glorieux, Michael Kraft
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work describes a hybrid micro-electro-mechanical-systems (MEMS) microphone integrating capacitive and piezoelectric transduction mechanisms for signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) improvement. A theoretical system-level model was established to characterize the hybrid device, which was fabricated using a silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafer-based process. The piezoelectric transduction component employs a Si/SiO2/Au/AlN/Pt material stack, while the capacitive transduction part consists of a variable capacitor formed by a silicon handle layer and a silicon device layer. Experimental results at 1 kHz show that the hybrid MEMS microphone achieves sensitivities of −64.3 dB (re: 1 V/Pa) in piezoelectric mode, −54.9 dB (re: 1 V/Pa) in capacitive mode, and −52.4 dB (re: 1 V/Pa) in hybrid mode, with corresponding SNR values of 65.7 dB, 59.8 dB, and 62.2 dB, respectively. To enhance the overall SNR of the hybrid MEMS microphone, a signal fusion technique is applied to the dual synchronized signals, resulting in an enhanced SNR of 66.7 dB, exhibiting a 14.1 dB improvement compared to the 52.6 dB reported in previous work.

Introduction

Recently, microelectromechanical system (MEMS) sensors have been widely used in many different fields1–3, Among them, MEMS microphones are acoustic sensing devices that have been employed in various applications, including earphones, mobile phones, laptops, and other consumer electronics4,5, MEMS microphones exhibit several key advantages compared to electret condenser microphones (ECM), such as miniaturized sizes6, excellent electro-acoustical performance7, outstanding environmental compatibility8, and low-cost batch fabrication feasibility9. Piezoelectric and capacitive transduction techniques are the two main methods used in MEMS microphones10.

Piezoelectric MEMS microphone features a single membrane structure, which consists of a structural material/metal/piezoelectric/metal layers stack11,12, The performance of the piezoelectric MEMS microphone highly depends on the piezoelectric thin film. Lead zirconate titanate (PZT)13,14, zinc oxide (ZnO)15, and aluminum nitride (AlN)16,17, are the most commonly used piezoelectric materials. By doping AlN film with Scandium, the performance of MEMS microphone can be further improved18, at the expense of a challenging fabrication process. Novel structural designs and optimization can also be employed to improve the microphone performance19,20.

Capacitive MEMS microphone typically consists of a deformable diaphragm and a backplate forming a variable capacitor, which serve as the movable and fixed electrodes, respectively21,22, The air gap and the acoustic perforations play an important role in the performance of MEMS capacitive microphones22. Using new materials for diaphragm fabrication is an efficient method to improve the device performance21,23,24. Optimization of the device structure can also be a useful approach25,26.

MEMS microphones using individual transduction technology, either piezoelectric or capacitive transduction mechanisms, depend on novel structural designs and the use of new materials to improve device performance. Integrating multiple transduction principles in a single device is a promising approach for performance enhancement. In our previous work, a PZT-based hybrid MEMS microphone combining piezoelectric and capacitive transduction mechanisms has been reported to improve the sensitivity27. However, the overall signal-to-noise ratio in hybrid mode is limited by the high noise floor of piezoelectric transduction.

This work presents a hybrid MEMS microphone using aluminum nitride (AlN) film as piezoelectric material, which is beneficial to further improve the microphone performance28. Signal fusion techniques for multi-modal sensors are employed to enhance the overall SNR of the hybrid device29. In Section II, the lumped element model of the hybrid device is established and compared with the finite element simulation results. Section III presents the fabrication technique for the AlN-based hybrid microphone. Experimental characterizations of the hybrid microphone are presented in Section IV. Conclusions are given in Section V.

Design and Theory

Device Structure and Lumped Element

A hybrid microphone 3D structure view is demonstrated in Fig. 1a. The piezoelectric diaphragm consists of a silicon layer, a thermal oxide layer, a bottom Au electrode, an AlN film, and a top Pt electrode. The capacitive transduction structure consists of a silicon handle layer and device layer, which serve as the stationary and movable electrodes, respectively. Ring top electrodes are designed to counterbalance the moments induced by residual stress during the thin film deposition. Without proper compensation, these residual stresses could cause the membrane to deflect upward or downward due to the unbalanced moments, and in the worst case, the initial static deflection may cause membrane collapse. The diaphragm deformation under an incident sound pressure wave induces stress in the piezoelectric film and capacitance change in the capacitor. To predict the MEMS microphone behavior efficiently, the lumped element modeling technique is employed. In this analysis, the device is assumed to operate in the linear regime where the membrane displacement is significantly smaller than the nominal electrode gap. Under this small-signal approximation, nonlinear capacitive effects, gap-reduction-induced nonlinearities, and variations in bias-dependent stiffness are considered higher-order terms and are neglected. COMSOL vs LEM

To verify the proposed LEM model, the frequency response of the hybrid microphone is simulated using the finite element method (FEM) and compared to the predictions from the LEM technique.

A 3D COMSOL model is constructed to capture the acoustic and electronic behavior associated with the microphone. Figure 2b, c show the front view and perspective view of the 3D COMSOL model. The Solid Mechanics (solid) module is employed to capture the mechanical behavior of the diaphragm, the Electrostatics (es) module accounts for the piezoelectric and capacitive transduction, and the Thermoviscous Acoustics (ta) module describes the acoustic wave propagation and acoustic damping effects. These physics interfaces collectively capture the coupled multiphysics behavior of the proposed microphone. The diaphragm is mechanically clamped on the edge, and the bottom surface of the air gap is set as a rigid boundary with perforations. This rigid boundary is interfaced with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${V}_{{bias}}$$\end{document}Vbias, while the top surface of the air gap is set as a Terminal port for capacitive transduction. The bottom interface of the AlN layer is set as a GND condition, while the bottom interface of the top electrode is set as a Terminal port for piezoelectric transduction. Figure 2d compares the frequency response of the hybrid device obtained from the LEM and COMSOL models in piezoelectric, capacitive, and hybrid transduction modes, respectively, with the reference value set at 1 V/Pa. The theoretical and simulated results match very well over a frequency of interest ranging from 100 Hz to 10 kHz. The diaphragm’s fundamental resonance lies above 30 kHz, which is beyond the plotted range, while the low-frequency cut-off caused by the venting hole and the charge-amplifier feedback network occurs below 20 Hz. Both the resonance and the low-frequency roll-off fall outside the measurement window and therefore do not appear in the figure.

Frequency Response

Figure 7a shows the output response signal of the hybrid device at 1 kHz for various acoustic sound pressure levels in piezoelectric, capacitive, and hybrid transduction modes. The response slope in the hybrid transduction mode is 2.39 mV/Pa, higher than 0.61 mV/Pa in the purely piezoelectric transduction mode and 1.79 mV/Pa in the capacitive transduction mode. The frequency response of the device was measured from 100 Hz to 10 kHz. By combining both output signals, the sensitivity is enhanced across the whole frequency band of interest compared to the individual piezoelectric and capacitive transduction modes. Noise Floor

Figure 7 (c) shows the measured noise density of the device in the three working modes. The power spectral density (PSD) of the device in the piezoelectric mode is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.32\,{\rm{\mu }}V/\sqrt{{Hz}}$$\end{document}0.32μV/Hz, with a minimum detectable pressure (MDP) of 28.3 dB (re: 20 µPa) at 1 kHz. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is calculated as 65.7 dB. The PSD in the capacitive mode is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.84\,\mu V/\sqrt{{Hz}}$$\end{document}1.84μV/Hz, with a MDP of 34.2 dB (re: 20 µPa) at 1 kHz. The SNR is determined as 59.8 dB. The PSD in hybrid mode is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.86\,\mu V/\sqrt{{Hz}}$$\end{document}1.86μV/Hz, with a MDP of 31.8 dB. The SNR is calculated as 62.2 dB, which is 9.6 dB higher than the previously reported value of 52.6 dB27. However, the overall SNR in hybrid mode is not improved compared to the SNR in piezoelectric mode, because the overall noise floor in the hybrid mode is dominated by the contribution from the capacitive mode, which is more than five times that in the piezoelectric mode. The significantly higher noise floor observed in the capacitive mode—and by extension, the hybrid mode—is fundamentally governed by the interaction between the parasitic capacitances of the capacitive transduction branch and the interface amplifier. Specifically, the large parasitic elements, while inherently noiseless, substantially increase the total input capacitance seen by the amplifier. This interaction reshapes the noise contribution of the amplifier by altering the input impedance, thereby causing the amplifier’s input-referred noise to become the dominant source that determines the overall noise floor. Consequently, minimizing parasitic capacitance is identified as the promising strategy to mitigate this adverse circuit-noise coupling and significantly reduce the device noise level in future designs.

Linearity

Linearity and total harmonic distortion (THD) of the microphone are measured to evaluate how the microphone output varies with sound pressure levels. The THD ratio is measured at 1 kHz sound pressure and computed based on the first six harmonic components using the included software. Figure 7d, e shows the output voltage of the DUT operating in different transduction modes under the varying reference microphone sound pressure level at 1 kHz, shown in both linear and decibel scales. The upper limit of the sound pressure level of the testing instrument is approximately 381 Pa, which corresponds to 145.6 dB SPL. Figure 7f shows the measured THD. At maximum achievable sound pressure level, microphones exhibit a THD of 2.3%, 11.23%, and 8.81% in piezoelectric, capacitive, and hybrid transduction modes, respectively. The sudden rise in THD for the capacitive mode might be because the diaphragm displacement at high SPLs reaches several hundred nanometers, driving the capacitive response into a nonlinear regime.

Conclusion

In summary, this work presented an AlN-based MEMS microphone integrating piezoelectric and capacitive transduction mechanisms to improve the microphone performance. A lumped element model is established and compared to the finite element simulation results. This model characterizes the device in the linear regime, capturing the dominant behavior while neglecting higher-order nonlinear effects for analytical clarity. The silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafer-based fabrication technology is introduced. The measured sensitivities of the hybrid MEMS microphone in piezoelectric, capacitive, and hybrid modes at 1 kHz are -64.3 dB, -54.9 dB, and -52.4 dB (re: 1 V/Pa), respectively. The SNR of the device in three modes at 1 kHz is 65.7 dB, 59.8 dB, and 62.2 dB, respectively. By employing the signal fusion technique, the overall SNR of the hybrid device is improved to 66.7 dB. The proposed hybrid transduction architecture suggests a promising direction for future multi-mode MEMS sensor designs. By integrating multiple transduction mechanisms within a single device and leveraging signal fusion algorithms, such designs can achieve superior performance compared to single-mode devices.
