Fuzzy logic-based speed guidance strategy for mixed platoons at intersections with communication delay compensation and driver reaction time modeling
Authors: Xiaofeng Liu, Menghua Yan, Runjia Dai, Mingxing Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper proposes a comprehensive speed guidance strategy for mixed vehicle platoons at signalized intersections. The strategy integrates communication delay compensation and models driver reaction time. This research addresses critical challenges in Cooperative Vehicle-Infrastructure Systems (CVIS), where heterogeneous vehicle behaviors and communication uncertainties significantly impact traffic efficiency. The key innovations include: (1) A four-dimensional speed guidance framework (acceleration, deceleration, constant speed, stopping) for mixed CAV-HV platoons; (2) A fuzzy logic-enhanced Smith predictor for dynamic V2X delay compensation; (3) A multi-factor driver reaction time model incorporating age, fatigue, and cognitive load effects. Quantitative results demonstrate that the system achieves maximum acceleration error reduction of 80.9% and speed error reduction of 62% under communication delays of 0.5–2.0 s. The driver reaction time model improves trajectory prediction accuracy, increases intersection throughput by approximately 36% compared to fixed-parameter approaches. Prescan - MATLAB/Simulink co - simulation with 5 – vehicle platoons and field experiments with 3 - vehicle platoons confirm the practical effectiveness and safety of the proposed strategy. The integrated approach significantly enhances platoon coordination, reduces travel time, and improves overall intersection efficiency in mixed autonomous-human traffic environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32008-8.

Introduction

Rapid urbanization and increasing vehicle ownership have intensified traffic congestion, particularly at signalized intersections where traditional fixed-timing signals fail to adapt to dynamic traffic demand. Signalized intersections serve as critical transition points between continuous and intermittent traffic flow in urban road networks. Frequent acceleration, deceleration, and stopping maneuvers1, along with speed variations, contribute to traffic bottlenecks2, traffic accidents3, higher energy consumption, increased emissions, and related issues4.

Traffic flow efficiency and safety at signalized intersections represent fundamental challenges in modern transportation systems. This is particularly true for emerging Cooperative Vehicle-Infrastructure Systems (CVIS). CVIS enables comprehensive real-time information exchange between Vehicle-to-Vehicle (V2V) and Vehicle-to-Infrastructure (V2I), providing new technical approaches for solving intersection traffic efficiency problems. For instance, the U.S. Department of Transportation has outlined a draft plan to deploy Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) at 25% of signalized intersections by 2026, highlighting its strategic importance5. The development of mixed platoons introduces unprecedented challenges for maintaining optimal traffic flow and ensuring safety at critical intersection points. On one hand, different vehicle types exhibit significant differences in perception capabilities, decision-making mechanisms, and response characteristics. On the other hand, inevitable communication delays and driver heterogeneity both affect cooperative control effectiveness. Additionally, factors such as the penetration rate of CAVs, platoon composition, and communication topology significantly impact the performance of CVIS, introducing additional complexity and challenges to the development and implementation of speed guidance strategies. Extensive research has been carried out on speed advisory systems developed for signalized intersections to address the aforementioned issues.

Currently, speed guidance strategies for signalized intersections primarily fall into two categories: those based on optimization techniques and those relying on predefined rules6. The optimization-based approach focuses on determining the optimal vehicle trajectory by incorporating multiple constraints, primarily targeting reduced fuel usage and enabling smooth passage through intersections. For instance, Yao et al. developed an integrated optimization framework that coordinated traffic signal timing and vehicle trajectories for mixed traffic consisting of CAVs and human-driven vehicles7. Jiang et al. formulated a two-tier model that coordinated signal timing and trajectory planning for multiple CAVs, demonstrating a significant decrease in both vehicle delays and fuel use across varying traffic conditions8. Liu et al. proposed a unified control method that simultaneously optimized signal phases and the trajectories of automated vehicle platoons at urban intersections9. Rule-based methods typically provide speed guidance for vehicles approaching intersections based on real-time traffic data and predefined operational rules. For example, Ye et al. proposed a trigonometric function-based model for determining economical driving speeds, offering the benefit of producing smoother speed curves that are easier for drivers to follow10. In addition, several other approaches to CAV speed guidance have been explored11.

Existing research on mixed traffic primarily addresses either individual vehicle speed guidance or platoon-based coordination to improve intersection efficiency and safety. Individual vehicle studies integrate dynamic speed advisories with signal control to reduce collision risk and enhance flow12. At the platoon level, cooperative driving combined with green-wave strategies optimizes speed and signal coordination, improving throughput and traffic stability13. Furthermore, investigations on varying intelligent connected vehicle penetration rates reveal that CACC enhances stability and fuel efficiency through eco-driving and trajectory optimization14,15. Communication delay compensation is pivotal for enhancing platoon stability and speed guidance effectiveness. Prior research includes reassessment of platoon stability in lossy V2V environments16 and applications of deep learning for communication reliability17. Smith predictor-based controllers are widely used for delay compensation, with approaches combining predictive control and classical feedback demonstrating experimental efficacy18–20.

In most traditional speed advisory studies, driver reaction time is either ignored or assumed to be a fixed constant. In reality, however, reaction time varies considerably across drivers due to differences in age, fatigue, attention, and experience. Such variability reduces the accuracy of speed tracking and poses challenges for cooperative control in mixed traffic with both automated and human-driven vehicles. Recent works have begun to incorporate driver heterogeneity into modeling. For instance, Qi et al. introduced driver errors into eco-driving strategies via Markov chain predictions21; Di et al. applied a learning-based stochastic MPC approach to adapt to behavioral changes22; and Li et al. classified drivers by acceleration characteristics to improve intersection throughput23. While these studies highlight the importance of human drivers, they primarily focus on driving style or behavioral errors, and pay limited attention to reaction time—a key factor for safe and accurate speed following. Existing approaches such as Markov chains, SVMs, and learning-based methods21,22,24 are powerful in capturing dynamic behavior but require extensive data and do not explicitly handle the uncertainty and vagueness of reaction times. By contrast, fuzzy logic provides a more intuitive and interpretable framework: it naturally represents uncertainty, does not rely heavily on large datasets, and aligns well with the cognitive nature of human decision-making. Prior studies have applied fuzzy reasoning for driver style classification25, performance inference, and safety distance modeling with variable reaction times26, demonstrating its potential for capturing individual differences. Nevertheless, few studies have systematically modeled driver reaction time variability in speed advisory strategies using fuzzy logic. To address this gap, this paper analyzes driver reaction time heterogeneity in responding to speed guidance and proposes a fuzzy logic-based modeling approach, thereby enhancing the adaptability and robustness of advisory systems in mixed traffic environments.

To address these limitations and improve the efficiency and safety of urban traffic flow, this paper proposes a novel speed guidance strategy specifically designed for mixed platoons at four-way intersections. This strategy incorporates communication delay compensation and a driver reaction time model to provide robust and adaptive speed recommendations. The objective is to enable mixed platoons to traverse intersections efficiently, minimize stops, and enhance overall traffic flow, thereby contributing to the development of more intelligent and sustainable urban transportation systems. Methodology

Problem description

Traffic control and speed guidance systems at signalized intersections aim to improve traffic efficiency, safety, and driving comfort by optimizing vehicle movements through intersections. The effectiveness of such control is influenced by multiple factors, including vehicle composition, car-following behavior, communication delays and modes, as well as driver characteristics such as reaction time variability. In CVIS environments, vehicles communicate using Vehicle-to-Vehicle (V2V) and Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) technologies. Within a vehicle platoon, a predecessor-following (PF) communication topology is commonly adopted due to its simple structure and efficient use of communication resources, enabling vehicles to obtain real-time information from preceding vehicles with minimal overhead27. If vehicles maintain their current speeds and travel uniformly toward the intersection stop line without coordinated control, there is a risk that the traffic signal may turn red unexpectedly, requiring all vehicles in the platoon to perform emergency braking and stop abruptly. Such unanticipated stops significantly reduce platoon throughput, increase delays and fuel consumption, decrease driving comfort, and may elevate the risk of rear-end collisions. By appropriately adjusting acceleration and deceleration within the platoon, cooperative speed guidance strategies can facilitate smooth passage through intersections without stopping or with gentle slowing or stopping when necessary, thereby enhancing safety, comfort, and traffic flow efficiency. As illustrated in Fig. 1, considering key factors such as initial platoon speed, maximum acceleration limits, the length of the intersection guidance zone, and traffic signal timings, adaptive speed guidance strategies including acceleration, deceleration, uniform speed, and stop can be implemented. This paper analyzes the performance of mixed vehicle platoons in intersection scenarios, assuming that vehicles can precisely execute speed control commands, while temporarily abstracting away detailed vehicle dynamics control.

Safety constraints

To ensure safe platoon operation under dense traffic, vehicle spacing must satisfy safety constraints based on vehicle length, initial speeds, and maximum deceleration, guaranteeing a minimum headway distance to prevent collisions. These safety constraints include the following:

(1) Minimum safe following distance.

The following distance between vehicles must account for the relative speeds, maximum braking deceleration, and reaction time, ensuring that vehicles maintain a safe distance, especially during emergency braking. (2) Time-to-Collision (TTC) Constraint.

The time-to-collision (TTC) between two vehicles must be kept above a threshold, typically 1.5 s, ensuring that the following vehicle has sufficient time to respond to changes in the leading vehicle’s speed32.

(3) Emergency Braking Capability.

This constraint ensures that the following vehicle has enough distance to stop even if the leading vehicle applies maximum braking deceleration, thus ensuring the safety of the platoon in emergency situations33,34.

Communication topology and information

In cooperative vehicle-infrastructure systems, the transmission of vehicle state and signal phase information is strongly influenced by the adopted communication topology. Six typical topologies are commonly used in platoon communication: predecessor following (PF), bidirectional predecessor following (BPF), leader predecessor following (LPF), two predecessors following (TPF), two bidirectional predecessors following (TBPF), and two predecessor-leader-following (TPLF). Each topology determines how vehicles receive and forward information within the platoon, thereby shaping the accuracy and timeliness of speed guidance.

Their characteristics are summarized in Table 2. The PF topology is the most widely adopted in intersection scenarios due to its simplicity and low communication overhead. In this structure, each vehicle only obtains information from its immediate predecessor and transmits its state to the immediate follower. While resource-efficient, PF is more sensitive to single-link delays and packet losses, which may degrade the performance of speed guidance strategies. In contrast, topologies such as TPF or TPLF provide redundant information from multiple vehicles or the leader, thereby improving robustness to poor transmission quality but at the expense of higher communication resource consumption.

Considering the trade-off between communication efficiency and robustness, this study adopts the PF topology as the baseline communication structure. To mitigate the potential negative impact of degraded transmission rate or packet loss, the proposed strategy integrates a fuzzy logic-enhanced Smith predictor for dynamic delay compensation. In this way, reliable and accurate speed guidance can be maintained under realistic V2X communication conditions.

While the PF topology is adopted in this study due to its simplicity and wide implementation in practice, alternative topologies offer distinct theoretical advantages. Bidirectional topologies (BPF, TBPF) can enhance robustness through two-way information flow35,36, multi-predecessor topologies (TPF, MPF) may reduce sensitivity to individual link delays at the cost of increased communication overhead37,38, and leader-follower architectures provide centralized coordination but introduce potential single-point-of-failure vulnerabilities39. The trade-offs between communication complexity, computational requirements, and control performance warrant systematic investigation. The PF topology serves as a baseline to evaluate the proposed delay compensation mechanism, which can be extended to other topologies in future work.

Design of speed guidance strategy

We emphasized that the effectiveness of speed guidance strategies is also subject to communication topology and the quality of information transmission. Imperfect communication (e.g., reduced transmission rate or packet loss) can lead to delayed or incomplete state information. To address these issues, we highlighted that the proposed fuzzy logic–enhanced Smith predictor provides dynamic delay compensation, while the driver reaction time model increases robustness under communication uncertainties.

Based on traffic signal timing, vehicle states, preceding vehicle, and intersection geometry, four adaptive speed guidance strategies are developed. Note that yellow lights typically serve as a warning, signaling drivers to prepare to stop. However, according to traffic regulations in most jurisdictions, it is legally permissible to proceed through a yellow light if: (1) the vehicle is too close to the intersection to stop safely without risking a rear-end collision, or (2) stopping would require harsh deceleration exceeding comfort thresholds (typically − 3.0 m/s²). In this study, the effective green phase duration includes both green and yellow light periods, as vehicles already committed to intersection approach can legally and safely proceed through yellow lights. This approach aligns with standard traffic engineering practice, where yellow intervals are designed to provide sufficient clearance time for vehicles that cannot safely stop.

Fuzzy logic control-based communication delay compensation

Communication delays in V2X systems introduce time lags that degrade the responsiveness and stability of car-following control at intersections34,40. The Smith predictor has been widely adopted in delayed control systems due to its capability to compensate for fixed time delays by predicting future plant outputs based on a delay-free model41. We selected the Smith predictor model because it effectively mitigates the destabilizing effect of communication delays in vehicle platoons, improving control accuracy and safety. However, traditional Smith Predictors rely on fixed controller parameters, which limit their efficacy under the non-linear and time-varying traffic conditions commonly observed at intersections.

To address these challenges, we incorporate a fuzzy logic control mechanism to dynamically adjust the Smith predictor parameters in response to real-time acceleration errors and their rates of change. This adaptive modification enhances the predictor’s robustness and responsiveness, enabling it to maintain efficient platoon control despite variable communication delays and complex vehicle interactions. The integration of fuzzy control thus allows for smooth compensation of delay-induced errors, preserving driving comfort and safety.

The delay compensation process is applied as follows: for each vehicle in the platoon, the communication delay is calculated based on the relative velocity and distance between the host and leading vehicles. The Smith predictor model predicts the future acceleration of the host vehicle by compensating for the time lag introduced by the communication delay. This prediction is then fed into the fuzzy logic controller, which dynamically adjusts the control parameters to correct any acceleration errors due to the delay. The fuzzy logic system takes the difference between the desired and actual acceleration values and classifies these errors into three linguistic categories: Negative, Zero, and Positive. Based on these categories, the system adjusts the control action to compensate for the delay.

The fuzzy logic controller takes as inputs the acceleration error and its rate of change—defined as the difference between the acceleration received by the host vehicle and the actual acceleration emitted by the preceding vehicle. These inputs are fuzzified into three linguistic categories using Gaussian membership functions: “Negative,” “Zero,” and “Positive.” Here, “Negative” and “Positive” represent negative and positive deviations, respectively, while “Zero” corresponds to values close to zero, indicating minimal error. The output control signal, representing the degree of corrective action, is categorized into three fuzzy sets using triangular membership functions: “Low,” “Medium,” and “High.” Specifically, “Low” corresponds to scenarios requiring slight parameter adjustments, “Medium” indicates balanced or moderate corrections, and “High” applies when rapid or strong adjustments are necessary. Using these fuzzy sets helps translate imprecise or uncertain input values into appropriate control outputs, thereby achieving a smoother and more efficient control process.

Fuzzy theory-based driver reaction time modeling

During the ongoing transition between Level 2 and Level 3 automation in intelligent vehicle systems, human drivers are required not only to perform dynamic driving tasks but also to manage, configure, and monitor automated functions. Advancements in in-vehicle human-machine interaction systems provide rich informational assistance, but may also lead to driver cognitive overload and reduced reaction efficiency, thus making driver reaction time a critical factor influencing the effectiveness of vehicle-road cooperative speed guidance systems. Driver reaction time is subject to the nonlinear influence of multiple factors, and traditional precise modeling methods struggle to capture its complexity. Fuzzy theory42, on the other hand, can effectively process these fuzzy variables through fuzzy sets and rules, establishing fuzzy models of driver behavior and thereby more accurately predicting reaction capabilities, providing a theoretical foundation for intelligent speed guidance system design. To improve the accuracy of driver reaction time in mixed platoon environments, we propose a fuzzy theory-based model that dynamically adjusts reaction time based on key factors, including age, fatigue, and cognitive load. Traditional models assume fixed values for reaction time, which fails to account for the inherent variability in human drivers’ responses. The fuzzy theory is capable of incorporating this variability through linguistic variables, allowing for more accurate predictions of driver behavior under different conditions.

Design of fuzzy input variables

In the Cooperative Vehicle-Infrastructure Speed Guidance System, human driver reaction time critically impacts the effectiveness of speed guidance systems under mixed traffic conditions. The selection of fuzzy evaluation indicators for driver reaction time requires a comprehensive consideration of directness and measurability. Our model incorporates three key factors affecting driver reaction time: age, fatigue level, and the number of voice prompts. Age is a well-established physiological factor influencing neural conduction velocity and cognitive processing speed, with older drivers demonstrated to have significantly longer reaction times43. Age data can be reliably obtained from driver records, ensuring model applicability. Fatigue level directly affects cognitive alertness and information processing capabilities, leading to increased reaction delays in drivers experiencing tiredness due to prolonged driving. Fatigue assessment can be facilitated by wearable sensors or eye-tracking technologies, making it a practical input. Voice prompt quantity reflects the cognitive load imposed by in-vehicle human-machine interfaces. Excessive voice guidance can lead to information overload, distracting drivers, and thereby prolonging reaction times44. Collectively, these factors cover physiological, psychological, and external informational influences on driver responsiveness. The chosen inputs thus capture primary sources of variability in human reaction time, enabling a nuanced fuzzy logic-based prediction model that enhances the realism and precision of driver behavior representation within the speed guidance framework.

In this study, a Predecessor-Following (PF) communication topology is adopted for vehicle-to-vehicle interactions within the mixed platoon due to its structural simplicity and efficient utilization of communication resources, which contribute to platoon stability. The integrated V2V and V2I communication framework is essential for coordinated speed guidance at signalized intersections, as it facilitates timely data exchange despite network delays. The choice of PF topology and V2I-based cooperative communication is supported by existing literature (e.g., [27]) demonstrating their advantages in maintaining platoon stability and communication efficiency. Conclusions and discussions

This paper addresses critical challenges in intersection speed guidance for mixed vehicle platoons through integrated advanced modeling techniques and cooperative control strategies. This paper developed a mixed platoon car-following model incorporating CACC, ACC, and IDM to capture heterogeneous vehicle behaviors. Designed four adaptive speed guidance strategies—acceleration, uniform speed, deceleration, and stop—tailored to intersection signal timings and vehicle dynamics. Proposed a fuzzy logic control-based Smith Predictor to dynamically compensate for V2X communication delays, enhancing control robustness. Constructed a fuzzy theory-driven driver reaction time model accounting for nonlinear effects of age, fatigue, and voice prompt frequency, enabling individualized driver behavior prediction. It should be noted that the current study is limited to the PF communication topology, which serves as a baseline for evaluating the proposed delay compensation mechanism. While this choice is justified by its prevalence in practical implementations, the performance characteristics under alternative topologies (BPF, TPF, MPF, leader-follower) may differ and warrant systematic investigation, as discussed below.

Correspondingly, the quantitative results validating these improvements are summarized as follows:

(1) The communication delay compensation strategy significantly reduces maximum acceleration errors by up to 80.9% and speed errors by up to 62% for communication delays between 0.5 and 2 s.

(2) The driver reaction time model improves trajectory accuracy and effectively prevents platoon disruption, resulting in an approximate 36% increase in intersection throughput.

(3) Overall, the integrated speed guidance strategy enhances vehicle coordination, reduces platoon travel time, and improves traffic efficiency and safety at signalized intersections under complex mixed traffic conditions.

Despite the promising contributions, several avenues remain open for further investigation and improvement:

(1) Communication Network Topologies:

Future research should systematically compare communication topologies (PF, BPF, TPF, MPF, leader-follower) to evaluate their impacts on mixed platoon stability and control performance at signalized intersections. Bidirectional topologies may enhance robustness through two-way information flow35,36, while multi-predecessor topologies could reduce delay sensitivity at the cost of communication overhead37,38. Centralized leader-follower architectures warrant evaluation for their coordination benefits versus potential vulnerabilities39. The proposed delay compensation mechanism should be adapted to these alternative structures to assess its generalizability. Field trials with real-world traffic data and hardware-in-the-loop testing are also needed to validate simulation findings under diverse operational conditions. Comparative studies can guide optimal network design tailored to urban intersection scenarios.

(2) Driver Physiological and Psychological Factors:

Exploring the relationships between drivers’ physiological states (e.g., heart rate variability, stress levels) and psychological traits with reaction time and driving behavior can enhance the precision of driver modeling. Integration of multi-modal sensing technologies, such as eye tracking and wearable biosensors, will enable real-time assessment and adaptive control.

(3) Diverse Platoon Composition:

Investigating the mixed platoon dynamics with varied vehicle types—including passenger cars, buses, trucks—with differing acceleration capabilities, safety distances, and control characteristics will improve the generalizability of speed guidance strategies. This includes modeling vehicle-specific start-up delays and maneuvering behaviors.

(4) Speed Guidance and Energy Consumption:

Developing multi-objective speed guidance frameworks that jointly optimize traffic throughput and energy efficiency—especially differentiating among electric, hybrid, and fuel vehicles—will address growing environmental concerns. Consideration of regenerative braking effects and vehicle-specific energy consumption models will be vital.

(5) Integration with Traffic Signal Optimization and Queue Management:

Coupling speed guidance algorithms with dynamic traffic signal control and queue length prediction can further reduce idle time and emissions, while improving intersection throughput. Large-scale simulation and real-world pilot deployments are needed to validate these integrated approaches.

By addressing these challenges, future work can substantially advance the robustness, scalability, and environmental sustainability of cooperative vehicle-infrastructure systems, aligning with the vision of intelligent and connected urban mobility. We clarified that simulation and field experiment results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed delay compensation mechanism under communication delays of 0.5–2.0 s, thereby mitigating the vulnerability of the PF topology to single-link dependence. In addition, we pointed out in the Conclusion that other topologies (e.g., BPF, TPLF) could further enhance robustness by providing redundant information, and this will be investigated in future work. It should also be acknowledged that the simulation experiments were conducted under simplified assumptions (e.g., no overtaking, flat terrain), which may limit generalizability. These assumptions were necessary to isolate and validate the core effects of fuzzy logic–based delay compensation and driver reaction time modeling. To enhance applicability, future work will extend the framework to multi-lane and heterogeneous traffic scenarios, include road gradients and adverse weather conditions, and explore large-scale field validation.
