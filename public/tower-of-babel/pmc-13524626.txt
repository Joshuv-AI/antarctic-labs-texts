The microbial community composition and potential for carbon sequestration differences among mudflats with and without biofilm colonization
Authors: Yun-han Fu, Kuo-jian Ma, Yong-lian Ye, Yue-hong Wu, Dao-qiong Zheng, Cong Sun, Xue-wei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMudflats play an important role in coastal carbon cycling. The colonization of diatom-dominated microphytobenthic biofilms and associated heterotrophic microbial communities, affects the biogeochemical cycling in mudflats. Yet, the influence of biofilm colonization on the microbial communities and organic matter profiles in mudflats remains poorly understood. Here, 84 samples were collected in different seasons, locations and depths with/without biofilms in tidal flats of Zhejiang, China, and a multiparametric analysis was performed on environmental factors, microbial communities and dissolved organic matter (DOM) profiles to reveal the multidimensional influence of biofilm colonization on mudflats. The results showed that, compared with surface adjacent bare sediment (surface ABS), biofilms exhibited higher carbon stocks, lower bacterial diversity, and a shift in the dominant community assembly process from dispersal limitation to homogeneous dispersal. The microbial communities in biofilms and related sediments showed a higher abundance of functional genes involved in carbon, nitrogen and sulfur cycling, including carbon fixation and metabolism, leading to high proportion of recalcitrant DOM in the biofilm-related sediments, in which, Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, Chloroflexota and Bacillota acted as key functional groups. This study highlights the association between biofilm occurrence, microbial community composition, functional potential, and organic matter characteristics in mudflat sediments, highlighting the potential contribution of biofilms to carbon retention and transformation processes in mudflat ecosystems.Keywords: Mudflat, Biofilm colonization, Microbial community, Dissolved organic matter, Carbon sequestration

Introduction

Biofilms are matrix-enclosed microbial communities that colonize diverse substrates, forming ecologically distinct functional units across global ecosystems [1,2]. In most intertidal mudflats, such biofilms are mainly dominated by benthic microalgae and are commonly termed microphytobenthos or microphytobenthic biofilms [3]. Usually consisting of diatoms, cyanobacteria, and related microorganisms, microphytobenthic biofilms constitute an important biological component of coastal ecosystems, particularly tidal flats [4]. These biofilms are extensively distributed across intertidal zones globally, ranging from temperate coastlines in Europe and North America to subtropical regions of East Asia [4]. Their spatial patterns and development are largely regulated by hydrodynamic conditions, light availability, and substrate characteristics [5,6]. Substrate characteristics further regulate biofilm development: sediment roughness directly affects biomass accumulation, and patchy distributions of microalgae are typically more pronounced in muddy than in sandy sediments [7]. In addition, the dynamics of total carbon (TC) and total organic carbon (TOC) in sediments have been found to be tightly coupled with EPS production. This relationship highlights the integral role of microorganisms in mediating the turnover and accumulation of sedimentary organic matter [8]. In addition to these observed correlations, the development of biofilms and the associated production of EPS are increasingly considered important factors influencing the accumulation, transformation, and long-term preservation of organic matter in sediments [9].

Microphytobenthic biofilms in tidal flats produce large quantities of EPS that form cohesive matrices at the sediment surface and profoundly influence associated microbial communities through complex symbiotic interactions [10,11]. These biofilms contain diverse microbial communities, in which bacteria such as Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, and Chloroflexota frequently form core functional groups [12]. EPS and organic exudates derived from algae generate specific microenvironments that selectively enrich specialized microbial taxa, thereby altering microbial diversity and functional potential. For instance, higher algal biomass inputs may increase the abundance of organic matter-degrading bacteria while influencing the expression of functional genes associated with biogeochemical cycling [13,14]. Previous studies have further demonstrated that diatoms can directly regulate microbial community composition through the release of species-specific secondary metabolites [15,16]. Such algal-microbial interactions indicate that biofilms function as tightly coupled functional units rather than simple assemblages of co-occurring organisms [8]. Although biofilms are known to shape prokaryotic community composition and abundance, whether and how they systematically enhance the functional potential of these communities remains unresolved [17].

Tidal flats, including unvegetated mudflats and sandflats, cover about 500,000 km2 globally, accounting for about 10% of the total area of coastal wetland ecosystems. Tidal flats contribute substantially to coastal carbon storage, with average sediment carbon accumulation rates (CAR) of ∼130 g C m−2 yr−1 and a global burial of about 6.8 Tg C yr−1 in tidal flat sediments [18]. Although traditional blue carbon research has primarily focused on vegetated ecosystems such as mangroves, salt marshes, and seagrass meadows, increasing evidence indicates that unvegetated mudflats also contribute to coastal carbon storage through sedimentary carbon burial and microbial transformation processes [19]. Microbial communities are considered important contributors to carbon cycling in unvegetated mudflats through their roles in organic matter transformation, regulating nutrient fluxes, and facilitating the formation of refractory dissolved organic matter (RDOM), processes that are thought to contribute to long-term carbon storage [20,21]. RDOM represents a major portion of the organic carbon pool in tidal flat environments [22]. Owing to its high molecular complexity and chemical stability, RDOM may persist in aquatic systems for decades to centuries, making it an important component of long-term carbon storage [23]. Although biofilms are increasingly recognized as important components of intertidal sediments and have been associated with carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur biogeochemical cycling, significant knowledge gaps remain. Specifically, existing studies have not systematically investigated the role of biofilms in regulating the molecular composition of DOM, nor have the spatiotemporal dynamics of microbially driven element cycling in mudflats systems been clearly resolved. Consequently, the relationships among biofilms, microbial community composition, microbial functional potential, and DOM transformation remain insufficiently characterized, limiting our understanding of how biofilm-associated microbial processes contribute to carbon cycling in mudflats.

To address these critical knowledge gaps, a comprehensive spatiotemporal study spanning four seasons was conducted at three mudflats in Zhejiang Province, China. A total of 48 biofilms and related sediments samples and 36 adjacent bare sediment (ABS) samples were collected across three depth intervals (0, 5 and 10 cm). By integrating 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing and shotgun metagenomics with Fourier-transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometry (FT-ICR MS), this study aimed to address three pivotal questions: Under varying spatiotemporal conditions, (1) How do microbial community composition and environmental factors differ between biofilms-related sediments and ABS, especially the biofilms and surface ABS? (2) How are biofilms associated with differences in the functional potential of microbial communities involved in biogeochemical cycling? (3) How are differences in microbial communities and functional potential associated with variations in sedimentary organic matter composition? Collectively, these findings provide an integrated understanding of the associations among biofilm occurrence, microbial community composition, microbial functional potential, and organic matter composition in tidal flat sediments, improving our understanding of the potential contribution of biofilms to carbon storage in mudflats.

Results

Changes in the diversity and composition of bacterial communities in biofilms

Across three geographically distinct sites, biofilm samples obtained from four locations were examined (Fig. 1a and b). In total, 84 samples were collected, including biofilms and deep layers of biofilm-related sediments, surface layer of sediments near biofilms (surface adjacent bare sediments; surface ABS), as well as deep layers of sediments near biofilms (deep adjacent bare sediments; deep ABS) (Supplementary Table 1). High-throughput sequencing targeting the 18S rRNA gene V4 region produced 7,629,276 high-quality reads, with sequencing depths varying from 10,079 to 251,670 reads per sample. Comparative assessment of the relative proportion of 18S rRNA gene reads assigned to algal taxa showed that algal sequences were substantially enriched in biofilms. Specifically, algal sequences accounted for an average of 74.75 ± 34.75% of the total 18S rRNA gene reads in biofilms, significantly higher than the 25.83 ± 17.83% observed in surface ABS. Consistent with the morphological classification, biofilms exhibited substantially higher proportions of algal 18S rRNA gene reads (>40%) compared with surface ABS (Supplementary Table 2). Meanwhile, 16S rRNA gene V4 hypervariable region amplicon sequencing generated 32,641,413 high-quality reads, with per-sample sequencing depths ranging from 43,627 to 419,570 reads. To evaluate bacterial community diversity, three α-diversity indices were determined: Chao1 richness, Shannon diversity, and Simpson evenness. Across all sampling sites, biofilms consistently displayed lower α-diversity compared with surface ABS (Fig. 1c), with the strongest differences observed in June and September. Shannon and Simpson indices were significantly lower in biofilms during these months, whereas Chao1 richness differed significantly only in June. Spatially, biofilm diversity remained lower than that of surface ABS. Chao1 richness was consistently reduced in biofilms across all three sampling locations. At the SBQT site, both Simpson and Shannon indices were significantly decreased in biofilms, and the Shannon index was also significantly lower in biofilms compared with surface ABS at the AWW site. Microbial diversity in the deeper layers of biofilm-related sediments and deep ABS was further examined, and no significant differences were observed among these subsurface samples (Supplementary Fig. S1). Collectively, these findings suggest that the bacterial diversity in biofilms is markedly lower than that of surface ABS irrespective of geographical location or seasonal variation.

Based on 16S rRNA gene V4 amplicon sequencing of biofilms and surface ABS samples, a total of 3158 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were identified, encompassing 31 bacterial phyla, 104 classes, 205 orders, 333 families, 517 genera, and 559 species. Overall community composition was similar between biofilms and surface ABS at the phylum level, with observed differences primarily reflected in the relative abundance of several subdominant phyla, including Acidobacteriota, Campilobacterota, Actinobacteriota, Gemmatimonadota, Nitrospirota, Myxococcota, and Latescibacteriota (Fig. 1d; Supplementary Fig. S2). To further separate the influences of seasonality and geographic location, the relative abundance of ten prevalent phyla (Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, Chloroflexota, Bacillota, Acidobacteriota, Gemmatimonadota, Nitrospirota, Desulfobacterota, Actinobacteriota, and Verrucomicrobiota) was evaluated across four seasons and three locations (Supplementary Fig. S2). In terms of seasonal patterns, among the dominant taxa, Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, Chloroflexota, and Verrucomicrobiota were significantly influenced by seasonal changes, and significant differences were identified for Chloroflexota, Desulfobacterota, Gemmatimonadota, Nitrospirota, and Verrucomicrobiota across sites.

Relationships between biofilm microbial diversity and environmental factors

A comprehensive suite of environmental variables, including salinity (SAL), temperature (T), pH, water content (WC), oxidation-reduction potential (ORP), total carbon (TC), total organic carbon (TOC), dissolved organic carbon (DOC), and total nitrogen (TN), was measured. TC, TOC, and DOC were consistently higher in biofilms, whereas SAL, T, pH, TN, and WC showed only minor differences between biofilms and surface ABS (Supplementary Fig. S3, Sheet S1). Temperature demonstrated the most pronounced seasonal fluctuation, peaking in September and reaching the lowest value in December. Carbon fractions in biofilms also exhibited distinct seasonal dynamics: TC were highest in September and December, whereas TOC peaked in June followed by September. DOC reached its maximum in June, followed by that in September, while other environmental variables remained comparatively stable across seasons. Spatially, biofilms at the AWW site exhibited the highest TC, TOC, and DOC, followed by XMD and SBQT, whereas ORP was lowest at AWW and highest at SBQT. Other environmental variables displayed minimal spatial heterogeneity. Overall, TC, TOC, and DOC were consistently higher in biofilms relative to surface ABS across all investigated spatiotemporal scales, whereas other physicochemical parameters showed limited variation between the two habitats.

To investigate differences in community assembly between biofilm-related sediments and ABS, we applied the β net relatedness index (βNRI) framework to evaluate the relative importance of deterministic and stochastic assembly processes (Fig. 2a). The median βNRI value for biofilm-related sediments and ABS was 0.6 and −0.2, suggesting that stochastic processes predominated in both habitats. To further clarify the drivers of community assembly, the relative contributions of deterministic and stochastic processes were quantified. Deterministic processes included homogeneous selection and variable selection, whereas stochastic processes consisted of ecological drift, homogeneous dispersal, and dispersal limitation. Across all samples, community assembly was generally dominated by drift; however, the relative contribution of homogeneous dispersal was consistently greater in biofilm-related sediments than in ABS (Fig. 2b). This difference became more evident when biofilms were compared with surface ABS. Biofilm communities exhibited a higher contribution of homogeneous dispersal, consistent with greater community homogenization under shared local environmental conditions. In contrast, dispersal limitation was dominant in surface ABS, suggesting a stronger influence of stochastic processes (Fig. 2c). These results indicate that although stochastic processes dominated microbial assembly in both habitats, biofilm colonization was associated with a shift in the dominant stochastic assembly process from dispersal limitation toward homogeneous dispersal.

To investigate the potential effects of environmental variables on bacterial α-diversity and dominant taxa within biofilms, structural equation modeling (SEM) was performed by incorporating environmental factors, diversity indices, and major bacterial groups (Fig. 2d). The SEM demonstrated a good model fit (χ2 = 52.692, df = 41, P = 0.104; CFI = 0.976; TLI = 0.937; RMSEA = 0.059; SRMR = 0.064), indicating the proposed relationships among environmental drivers, microbial diversity, and dominant taxa. The model explained 14.1%, 9.0%, and 10.7% of the variance in the Shannon, Simpson, and Chao1 indices, respectively. The explained variance for dominant bacterial phyla ranged from 5.9% (Bacteroidota) to 39.3% (Chloroflexota). Across all samples, TC and DOC showed closely associations with microbial diversity. TC showed negative associations with both the Shannon index (standardized path coefficient β = −0.11) and the Simpson index (β = −0.12), while exhibiting a positive relationship with the Chao1 index (β = 0.12). DOC displayed highest positive correlation with all three diversity indices, including Simpson, Chao1, and Shannon (β = −0.12, −0.16, −0.12). At the taxonomic level, Pseudomonadota showed high negative association with Shannon index (β = −0.81) and a positive association with Chao1 richness (β = 0.43). The abundance of Chloroflexota was positively associated only with Chao1 richness (β = 0.69). Bacillota was negatively influenced by Shannon index (β = −0.54) while exhibiting a positive association with Chao1 richness (β = 0.31). In contrast, Bacteroidota were negatively associated only with Shannon index (β = 0.61).

Taken together, these findings indicate that dominant biofilm-associated bacterial groups adopt different ecological strategies in response to community diversity. Pseudomonadota and Bacillota were associated with communities characterized by low evenness and high richness, whereas Chloroflexota primarily benefits from increased species richness, and the dominance of Bacteroidota increases as community evenness decreases. The SEM supports a hierarchical association linking environmental factors, microbial diversity, and dominant taxa: TC and DOC are associated with microbial diversity in biofilms, whereas stronger associations were observed between diversity and dominant bacterial taxa.

Changes in the functional potential of bacterial communities in biofilms

The relative abundances of functional genes involved in carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur biogeochemical cycling were further compared in biofilm-related sediments compared to ABS (Fig. 3). Genes associated with major carbon fixation pathways, including the Calvin–Benson–Bassham (CBB) cycle, the reductive tricarboxylic acid (rTCA) cycle, and the Wood–Ljungdahl (WL) pathway, were consistently more abundant in biofilm-related sediments compared to ABS, indicating a greater potential for inorganic carbon (CO2) conversion into organic carbon within biofilm habitats. In addition, numbers of genes related to methane metabolism were significantly higher in biofilm-related sediments compared to ABS. The abundant genes included both methanogenesis-related genes and those driving subsequent methane transformation (e.g., the conversion of CH4 to CH3OH), collectively indicating an enhanced potential for methane turnover. Furthermore, genes associated with the Entner–Doudoroff (ED) pathway and glycolysis were more abundant in biofilms and biofilm-related sediments, reflecting an increased capacity to generate metabolic intermediates that support downstream energy production and biosynthetic processes. In terms of nitrogen cycling, key functional genes involved in nitrogen fixation, nitrification, denitrification, anaerobic ammonium oxidation (anammox), and dissimilatory nitrate reduction to ammonium (DNRA) exhibited pronounced differences in abundance between biofilm-related sediments and ABS, highlighting divergent nitrogen transformation potentials in biofilm-related environments. Genes associated with nitrogen fixation and denitrification were more abundant in biofilms, whereas genes related to nitrification were more abundant in ABS. DNRA-related genes were more prevalent in biofilm-related sediments. Similarly, nearly all genes involved in sulfur cycling processes, except for those associated with assimilatory sulfite reduction (ASR), were more abundant in biofilm-related sediments relative to ABS. In particular, genes associated with sulfite oxidation and dissimilatory sulfate reduction were more abundant in biofilm-related sediments. Genes related to sulfide oxidation and sulfur reduction also exhibited higher abundances in biofilm-related sediments, suggesting an enhanced potential for the rapid interconversion between hydrogen sulfide and elemental sulfur. Genes involved in sulfur disproportionation were likewise more abundant in biofilm-related sediments. Overall, functional genes related to carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling consistently exhibited higher abundances in biofilm-related sediments than in ABS, indicating enhanced biogeochemical cycling potential within biofilm habitats.

Comparison of microbial functional potential in biofilm-related sediments and ABS. a)–c) Carbon-, nitrogen-, and sulfur-cycling pathways in biofilm-related sediments and sediments near biofilms.

To more clearly elucidate the direct effects of biofilms on sediment microbial communities, comparisons were further conducted for the abundances of functional genes associated with biogeochemical cycling pathways between biofilms and surface ABS (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. S4 and Sheet S2). Overall, genes related to carbon fixation and complex organic carbon degradation consistently showed higher abundances in biofilms, and their spatiotemporal distributions further indicated an enhanced carbon-cycling potential within biofilms. Biofilms contained more genes associated with complex organic carbon degradation and heterotrophic energy metabolism. Specifically, key pathways including glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, the TCA cycle, and methane metabolism exhibited consistently higher abundances in biofilms compared to surface ABS (Fig. 4a). Carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) analysis showed that glycoside hydrolases (GHs) were predominant in biofilms, whereas polysaccharide lyase (PL) families in surface layers exhibited higher abundances compared with surface ABS. Furthermore, genes encoding glycosyltransferases (GTs) involved in polysaccharide synthesis were more abundant in biofilms, collectively indicating strong carbohydrate metabolic potential in biofilms (Sheet S3). Among carbon fixation pathways, key genes of the rTCA cycle, including aclAB, were primarily detected in biofilms, and korABCD displayed significantly higher abundances in biofilms than in surface ABS. Genes associated with the 3HP pathway, particularly accABCD, were more abundant in biofilms, while mcr were predominantly detected in biofilms. Key genes of the WL pathway (acsA, acsB, and cdhD) and the CBB cycle (rbcLS), together with auxiliary genes (prkB, PGK, glpX, and tktA), were consistently more abundant in biofilms (Fig. 4b).

Functional genes involved in nitrogen cycling were consistently abundant in biofilms relative to surface ABS (Fig. 4d). This included pathways for nitrogen fixation (nifD), nitrification (amoA and hao), DNRA (narG, nirB and nrfA), denitrification (nirK, norB and nosZ), and assimilatory nitrate reduction (narB, nasA and nirA), indicating enhanced nitrogen transformation capacity. Similarly, sulfur cycling genes were more abundant in biofilms and exhibited stronger seasonal responses to temperature (Fig. 4e). Key genes involved in sulfate reduction (sat, aprA, dsrA) and sulfur oxidation (soxCD, soxAX, soxB) were consistently high, highlighting intensified sulfur turnover in biofilms.

Further analyses of genes annotated on contigs and their predicted taxonomic affiliations revealed clear phylogenetic partitioning of key biogeochemical pathways. Genes involved in the 3HP pathway, including aceABCD, were mainly affiliated with Pseudomonadota, with additional contributions from Bacteroidota, Bacillota, and Chloroflexota. CBB cycle genes (rbcL and rbcS) were primarily associated with Pseudomonadota. Genes related to the rTCA cycle, such as porAB and korA, were mainly distributed across Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, Bacillota, and Actinobacteriota. Methane metabolism-related genes (e.g., mcrA) were largely linked to Pseudomonadota. Analyses of nitrogen cycling further indicated that functional genes associated with denitrification (e.g., narG, norB), dissimilatory nitrate reduction (e.g., nrfA, nirB), and nitrification (e.g., hao, amoA) were predominantly contributed by Pseudomonadota, with secondary contributions from Bacteroidota and Chloroflexota. Similarly, sulfur-related genes, including SOX system components (soxABC) and genes involved in dissimilatory sulfate reduction and oxidation (dsrAB, aprAB), were mainly affiliated with Pseudomonadota, with a subset associated with Chloroflexota (Supplementary Fig. S5).

To further clarify how taxonomic composition mediates biogeochemical potential, SEM was employed to examine the hierarchical relationships among spatial-temporal heterogeneity (location, season, and vertical depth), dominant bacterial phyla, and functional genes involved in carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling (Fig. 4f). Spatial and temporal factors exerted both positive and negative effects on the distribution of dominant phyla. For instance, seasonal variation was positively associated with Chloroflexota, whereas geographic location showed negative associations with Pseudomonadota and Bacillota. Among these taxa, Chloroflexota was identified as a central mediator, exhibiting a positive association with carbon-cycling genes (β = 0.47); in contrast, Pseudomonadota displayed a weak negative association (β = −0.14). With regard to nitrogen cycling, Chloroflexota was positively associated with nitrogen-related functional genes (β = 0.33), consistent with its involvement in processes such as anaerobic ammonium oxidation and denitrification, and Pseudomonadota also showed a positive association (β = 0.37). Similar trends were observed for sulfur cycling, where Chloroflexota exhibited a highest positive correlation (β = 0.42), reflecting its contribution to sulfate reduction and sulfur oxidation pathways, and Bacteroidota exerted a secondary positive effect (β = −0.28), potentially through sulfur release during amino acid degradation; Bacillota displayed a negative association (β = −0.20). Overall, the SEM framework indicated that spatial and temporal heterogeneity primarily structures dominant microbial lineages, among which Chloroflexota functions as an important integrator, together with other major phyla, mediating carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling through complementary metabolic potentials, thereby enhancing the overall biogeochemical functional capacity of biofilms.

Functional potential of metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs)

A total of 860 medium-quality, non-redundant MAGs were recovered after dereplication (95% ANI), spanning 18 major phyla dominated by Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, Bacillota, and Chloroflexota (Fig. 5a). MAGs within the top 10% of relative abundance in biofilm-related sediments were designated as dominant. A greater number of MAGs were recovered from biofilm-related sediments, in which dominant MAGs were primarily affiliated with Chloroflexota, Pseudomonadota, Desulfobacterota, and Actinomycetota (Sheet S4). In addition, 58 MAGs unique to biofilm-related sediments were identified, mainly belonging to Campylobacterota, Pseudomonadota, Bacillota, Bacteroidota, and Verrucomicrobiota. Biofilm-associated communities exhibited higher average genome size (AGS) and GC content. AGS was significantly higher in biofilms than surface ABS and GC content was significantly lower in ABS at both surface and 5 cm depths. These genomic characteristics indicate that biofilm-associated microorganisms possess expanded metabolic and adaptive potential to cope with complex and fluctuating environmental conditions (Fig. 5b–d; Sheet S5).

Comparative analysis of dominant microbial taxa and their functional potentials in carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling showed that dominant MAGs in biofilms exhibited both higher diversity and abundance of metabolic potential across biogeochemical cycles. In the carbon cycle, biofilm-associated MAGs contained a higher abundance of CAZymes, along with heterotrophic metabolic potentials including pyruvate, methanol, and formate metabolism, as well as the TCA cycle. At the same time, these MAGs possessed autotrophic carbon fixation potential through the CBB cycle, the rTCA cycle, and the 3HP pathway. Within the nitrogen cycle, biofilm-associated MAGs encoded genes involved in nitrate reduction and nitrite reduction to ammonia, while sulfur metabolism genes, including those related to sulfur oxidation and sulfite reduction, were also identified (Sheets S6 and S7).

Specifically, Chloroflexota MAGs contained more GH and PL, harbored TCA cycle genes, and contained carbon cycle genes including key 3HP pathway genes, suggesting potential involvement in carbon fixation as well as organic carbon degradation. Nitrogen metabolism genes and sulfite reduction genes were detected. Biofilm-associated Pseudomonadota MAGs carried genes for pyruvate oxidation, acetate fermentation, formate metabolism and rTCA cycles. Nitrogen cycle genes were present. Sulfur metabolism genes were consistently identified across most Pseudomonadota MAGs. Actinomycetota MAGs contained carbon, nitrogen metabolism and sulfite reduction. Desulfobacterota MAGs exhibited polysaccharide degradation potential (GH), genes for carbon metabolism, nitrogen cycling, and sulfur metabolism. Nitrospinota MAGs contained genes for TCA cycle and nitrite reduction. Collectively, these dominant biofilm-associated taxa were involved in carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur metabolic processes, highlighting their important role in mediating biogeochemical cycling within biofilm ecosystems.

Further analysis of biofilm-specific MAGs showed that these genomes predominantly belonged to Campylobacterota, Pseudomonadota, Bacillota, Bacteroidota, and Verrucomicrobiota (Fig. 6, Sheet S8). Within these biofilm-unique MAGs, Campylobacterota harbored genes associated with acetate metabolism, acetyl-CoA synthesis, and methane oxidation, alongside carbon fixation genes. Nitrogen cycling genes detected included nitrate reduction, denitrification, and DNRA. Sulfur metabolism potential was indicated by genes involved in sulfur oxidation and sulfate reduction. Bacillota MAGs contributed to acetate fermentation and pyruvate fermentation. Bacteroidota MAGs contained more polysaccharide-degrading enzymes (GH and PL) and carried genes associated with acetate fermentation, pyruvate oxidation, acetyl-CoA synthesis, and the TCA cycle, indicating substantial heterotrophic metabolic capacity. In addition, Bacteroidota MAGs contained nitrate reduction and DNRA genes as well as sulfur metabolism genes. Although present at lower abundance, Pseudomonadota MAGs exhibited notable metabolic potential, encoding TCA cycle genes, acetate fermentation genes, and methanol metabolism genes. These MAGs also possessed nitrogen cycling genes for nitrate reduction and nitrite reduction to ammonia, along with sulfur metabolism genes and thiosulfate oxidation genes. Overall, analyses of both dominant and biofilm-specific MAGs indicated that biofilm-associated microbial communities possessed enhanced potential for carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling, highlighting their central role in facilitating multielement biogeochemical transformations.

Changes in dissolved organic matter profile in biofilm-related sediments

Molecular diversity in biofilms and surface ABS was analyzed and compared across four seasons (Fig. 7a). The organic matter composition based on Van Krevelen (VK) plots showed that biofilms exhibited higher molecular diversity of proteins (Class II), lipids (Class I), carbohydrates (Class IV), and CRAM-like compounds (Class VI), indicating that biofilms substantially modify the composition of organic matter. In particular, the abundances of bioactive compounds, including amino sugars, proteins, carbohydrates, and lipids, were increased. From the perspective of molecular diversity, biofilms exhibited greater diversity of protein, carbohydrate, and CRAM-like molecules, accompanied by higher organic matter complexity indices, such as double bond equivalents (DBE) and the modified aromaticity index (AImod) (Fig. 7b). These patterns were consistent across seasons, indicating that biofilms reshape sediment organic matter composition and distribution by accumulating reactive organic substrates while increasing molecular structural complexity. In contrast, analysis of molecular signal intensities revealed a complementary pattern. Biofilms showed higher relative abundances of carbohydrate, tannin, and amino sugar compounds, whereas the relative signal intensity of CRAM-like compounds remained largely unchanged (Supplementary Fig. S6). This decoupling between molecular diversity and signal intensity indicates that biofilms enhance the compositional heterogeneity of organic matter without proportionally increasing the abundance of recalcitrant CRAM-like components. Seasonally, differences in organic matter composition between biofilms and surface ABS were most evident during June and September, whereas contrasts were weaker during March and December.

Vertical profiling of samples collected in September, representing the active biofilm growth period, and in March, representing the non-active biofilm growth period, was therefore conducted (Fig. 7c). The results showed that biofilm colonization influenced the vertical distribution of organic matter in underlying sediments in terms of both molecular diversity and signal intensity. From a molecular diversity perspective, deep layers of biofilm-related sediments exhibited higher CRAM-like molecular diversity than deep ABS in both March and September. Notably, compared with March samples, biofilm-specific molecular signatures in September samples showed substantially greater CRAM-like diversity in deep layers of biofilm-related sediments compared with deep ABS. Concurrently, biofilm-related sediments displayed increasing values of DBE and modified AImod with depth, along with significant depth-related differences in lipid compounds. Together, these patterns are consistent with increasing molecular complexity and a higher proportion of biorecalcitrant CRAM-like molecular compositions in biofilm-related sediments (Fig. 7d). Complementary evidence was also provided by molecular signal intensity analyses, the relative high ratio of recalcitrant CRAM-like compounds from the surface to 10 cm depth was consistently greater in biofilm-related sediments than in ABS in both March and September (Supplementary Fig. S6). Importantly, this vertical accumulation of CRAM-like material was more pronounced in September than in March, consistent with enhanced biofilm growth and metabolic activity during the active biofilm growth period. Collectively, these findings suggest that biofilm colonization is associated with differences in the vertical distribution of DOM molecular compositions, including a higher proportion of biorecalcitrant CRAM-like molecules in deeper biofilm-related sediments.

Correlation analysis indicated that Chloroflexota, Actinobacteriota, Gemmatimonadota, MBNT15, and Nitrospirota exhibited significant positive associations with recalcitrant organic matter, including CRAM-like compounds and condensed aromatics (p ≤ 0.05). Notably, Chloroflexota played a distinct role within this group, as it also showed significant negative correlations with proteins, amino sugars, and carbohydrates (Fig. 7e). In contrast, Verrucomicrobiota displayed a divergent pattern, characterized by significant negative correlations with condensed aromatics (p ≤ 0.05). These findings indicate that dominant taxa within biofilms substantially influence the abundance and composition of different classes of organic matter.

In summary, a schematic was developed to illustrate the relationships among biofilm colonization, bacterial communities, functional potential, and dissolved organic matter (DOM) (Fig. 8). Biofilms were characterized by reduced microbial diversity and r-strategist dominance, in contrast to dispersal-limited conditions in surface ABS that favored k-strategists and higher diversity. Biofilm formation enhanced the metabolic potential of dominant bacterial taxa such as Chloroflexota, Bacteroidota, Pseudomonadota, and Bacillota, as well as biofilm-specific groups including Campylobacterota, Verrucomicrobiota, Desulfobacterota, and Actinomycetota. These taxa contributed to carbon fixation, facilitated heterotrophic processes, and regulated nitrogen and sulfur metabolism, resulting in the high ratio and specific vertical distributions of CRAM-like compounds, carbohydrates, and proteins in biofilms and biofilm-related sediments. The results demonstrate that biofilms reshape bacterial community strategies, strengthen functional potential, and significantly influence sedimentary organic matter composition, associated with higher proportions of fresh organic matter in biofilms and biorecalcitrant CRAM-like molecular compositions in biofilm-related sediments.

Discussion

Distinct assembly patterns and adaptive strategies of microbial communities in biofilms

In this study, microbial diversity and functional potential were investigated in biofilms and their associated sediments, with the aim of comparing microbial community composition and functional potential between these samples and exploring their associations with environmental factors. Analysis of microbial communities in biofilms and surface ABS showed that algae were significantly more abundant in biofilms, accompanied by a marked decrease in microbial α-diversity (Fig. 1c). This pattern was consistently observed across seasons and geographic locations, indicating that biofilm occurrence was consistently associated with distinct surface microbial community structures across seasons and locations. The observed reduction in bacterial diversity within biofilms was likely closely associated with environmental factors [24]. SEM analysis identified TC and DOC as the strongest variables associated with reduced bacterial diversity, suggesting that carbon availability may contribute to differences in bacterial community composition. This is consistent with that we found both TC and DOC concentrations were higher in biofilm-related sediments than in ABS. However, this comparison should be interpreted with caution because biofilms and surface ABS represent two ecologically distinct surface compartments. Biofilms comprise a biologically structured matrix dominated by microphytobenthic biomass and EPS, whereas surface ABS are predominantly mineral sediments lacking this surface biological layer [25,26]. Therefore, the higher carbon stocks observed in biofilms likely reflect both the accumulation of photosynthetically derived biomass and the intrinsic characteristics of the biofilm compartment, rather than being interpreted directly as enhanced carbon accumulation within the underlying mineral sediment. This interpretation is consistent with the ecological role of biofilms, in which photosynthetic carbon fixation contributes to the elevated carbon content of the biofilm layer [27]. Elevated DOC concentrations likely favored the growth of heterotrophic bacteria specialized in organic carbon degradation and may have also driven carbon-mediated selective competition and niche simplification [28], as observed in studies of eutrophic intertidal sediments where high organic carbon loads were frequently associated with reduced microbial diversity [29]. In addition, EPS production by biofilms may modify interspecies interactions, thereby further influencing community structure [30]. Recent evidence indicates that reduced microbial diversity in resource-rich environments can result from intensified resource competition rather than environmental stress, increasing nutrient diversity enhances resource uptake, strengthening competitive exclusion while promoting ecosystem functioning [31]. In biofilms, the accumulation of EPS and diverse algal-derived organic compounds likely increased resource diversity, favoring fast-growing taxa with high resource acquisition capacity. This mechanism is consistent with the lower microbial diversity, higher rrn copy numbers, and enhanced metabolic potential observed in our biofilms.

On the other hand, microbial taxa exhibit distinct response strategies to biofilm-associated environmental changes, consistent with their ecological traits in other systems [32]. Dominant taxa in biofilms include Chloroflexota, Pseudomonadota, Bacillota, and Bacteroidota (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. S2), and biofilms may selectively regulate the colonization and succession of specific groups by altering local microenvironments or interspecies interactions [33]. Pseudomonadota and Bacillota exhibited competitive advantages in communities with low evenness and high richness, reflecting opportunistic traits, metabolic flexibility, and rapid response capacity in coastal ecosystems [34]. Chloroflexota abundance was strongly positively correlated with species richness, consistent with previous studies reporting high relative abundance, metabolic versatility, and ecological resilience of this phylum, enabling stable dominance across diverse aquatic, sedimentary, and symbiotic habitats [[35], [36], [37]]. In contrast, Bacteroidota dominated communities with low evenness; prior studies have identified this phylum as a key heterotrophic degrader that thrives in environments with high organic inputs and abundant labile substrates [38]. It is therefore inferred that the dominance of Bacteroidota reflects the sustained availability and rapid turnover of easily degradable organic carbon, which enhances substrate utilization, favors fast-growing degraders, and reduces the relative abundance of rarer or environmentally constrained taxa, ultimately lowering community evenness.

Consistent with the “Hunger Games” hypothesis [39], biofilm communities were characterized by a higher relative abundance of r-strategists and homogeneous dispersal, whereas ABS were characterized by dispersal limitation and K-strategists. The predominance of homogeneous dispersal within biofilms may be related to their complex structure. This structured matrix provides a stable, uniform microenvironment that buffers fluctuations in salinity, nutrient distribution, and pH. Consequently, this stability allows microbes to disperse evenly and minimizes the impact of broader environmental heterogeneity [40]. EPS-mediated attachment and close microbial interactions may facilitate a more even spatial distribution of microbial cells, which is consistent with the predominance of homogeneous dispersal observed in biofilms and extends the Hunger Games framework to biofilm-associated microbial communities.

Enhanced metabolic potential and corresponding microbial groups in biofilms

Biofilm-associated microbial communities possessed a broad repertoire of genes involved in carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling, suggesting substantial metabolic potential for biogeochemical transformations. Compared with surface ABS, biofilm communities were significantly abundant in key genes associated with complex organic matter degradation and heterotrophic energy metabolism, including CAZymes, glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, pyruvate oxidation, and the TCA cycle, indicating that biofilm-associated microbes possess enhanced metabolic potential for organic matter degradation [41]. In addition, the increased abundance of autotrophic carbon fixation genes in biofilms suggested that these communities not only actively participated in heterotrophic metabolism but also may contributed to the organic carbon pool through autotrophic processes. In nitrogen cycling, biofilms exhibit higher abundances of genes associated with denitrification and DNRA, indicating enhanced nitrogen transformation potential. In sulfur cycling, the high abundance of the SOX system and assimilatory sulfate reduction genes in biofilms indicates that sulfide can simultaneously serve as an electron donor and acceptor, thereby providing flexible energy sources for heterotrophs.

Within this tightly integrated network, microbial taxa exhibit clear functional partitioning. Bacteroidota possess abundant GH and PL genes, suggesting a potential role as primary degraders capable of initiating the breakdown of complex organic matter into smaller substrates that may support heterotrophic metabolism within biofilms [42]. Previous studies have demonstrated that Bacteroidetes are key drivers of high-molecular-weight organic carbon degradation, consistent with the present findings showing that these taxa dominate complex organic matter breakdown [43,44]. Functionally, Bacteroidota suggests a potential role in initiating the breakdown of complex organic matter and supply ammonium via DNRA, whereas Pseudomonadota and Bacillota further process these intermediates through fermentation and respiration, potentially contributing to carbon redistribution [45,46]. This complementary nitrogen metabolism sustains continuous nitrogen cycling under rapidly fluctuating redox conditions driven by tides, consistent with the functional traits of Pseudomonadota and Bacillota in estuarine and marine environments [47]. Bacillota and Pseudomonadota also harbored genes associated with sulfur oxidation through the SOX pathway, suggesting a potential role in sulfur–nitrogen coupling previously reported in marine ecosystems [48]. Notably, Chloroflexota possessed a broad repertoire of genes involved in carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur metabolism, suggesting metabolic versatility. Chloroflexota simultaneously carried heterotrophic genes for the TCA cycle and GH and PL enzymes, autotrophic carbon fixation genes for the 3HP and WL pathways, as well as DNRA and sulfur metabolism capabilities, indicating broad metabolic potential. The presence of DNRA-associated genes indicates the potential to retain ammonium in biofilms, whereas sulfide oxidation/reduction supplied energy for both carbon fixation and nitrogen metabolism [49]. Methane is a potent greenhouse gas, and the observed higher count of methanogenesis genes in biofilm-related sediments suggests an elevated potential for sedimentary methane production, which could partially offset the potential carbon storage benefits provided by biofilm carbon fixation and DOM retention. However, our metagenomic data also revealed enhanced potential for methane transformation, suggesting that biofilms may regulate methane cycling through both production and consumption pathways. For example, we observed high numbers of genes involved in methane oxidation, including the conversion of CH4 to CH3OH. Furthermore, biofilm-specific MAGs, particularly those affiliated with Campylobacterota, harbored genes associated with methane oxidation. These findings suggest that biofilms may provide ecological niches for methane oxidation and potentially attenuate methane accumulation before its release to the overlying water column or atmosphere. Ultimately, the net climate benefit of these mudflats relies on this delicate trade-off between carbon sequestration, methanogenesis, and methanotrophy, underscoring the need for future in situ gas flux measurements to quantify these competing processes. The high proportion of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling genes in dominant bacterial groups further suggests that enhanced resource uptake may strengthen the metabolic potential of competitive taxa rather than maintain high microbial diversity. This pattern is consistent with the framework proposed by Shalev et al. [31], in which greater resource uptake supports higher ecosystem functioning despite reduced microbial diversity.

Vertical variation in dissolved organic matter composition associated with biofilm colonization

The higher abundance of labile DOM observed in biofilms may be associated with microbial metabolic activity, EPS production, local microenvironmental conditions, and multiple sources of soluble organic matter. The substantial EPS secreted by microalgae and bacteria significantly enhances the input and retention of labile DOM, suggesting that biofilms may represent important sources of active DOM [50]. The internal pore structure and water channels of biofilms may further facilitate the accumulation and rapid turnover of labile DOM, consistent with observations in marine environments [51,52]. Studies conducted at the land-sea interface similarly indicate that interstitial waters beneath biofilm-covered sediments act as important temporary reservoirs of labile DOM, with surface DOM concentrations substantially higher than those in deeper layers, driven by efficient microbial synthesis and secretion. These processes may help explain the molecular composition characteristics of DOM observed in biofilms in the present study, such as elevated AImod and DBE values: the peak AImod observed in September in biofilms correlates with higher summer temperatures that enhance microbial synthesis of alkylated carbon compounds [53,54]. Furthermore, protein, carbohydrate, and lipid indices in biofilms were significantly greater than those in surface ABS, providing additional support for their potential role as a source of labile DOM. These differences were associated with the active involvement of dominant biofilm taxa, such as Bacteroidota and Chloroflexota, in organic matter synthesis, transformation, and retention. As indicated in this study, Bacteroidota showed a positive correlation with protein content, likely due to rapid degradation and turnover of proteins through extensive enzyme systems [55], whereas Chloroflexota demonstrated both the potential for organic matter production and the ability to transform recalcitrant compounds, such as aromatics and condensed aromatics, with their abundance closely linked to the increasement of these recalcitrant components [56].

The observed differences between biofilms and surface ABS may be related to DOM exchange and microbial metabolic interactions. Specifically, labile DOM produced in biofilms may migrate through biofilm water channels and pore structures, and subsequently diffuse downward via vertical exchange in sediment interstitial waters, potentially providing a continuous carbon supply to deeper microbial communities [57]. Simultaneously, redox gradients established by surface microbial metabolism propagate downward through electron transfer networks, potentially supporting microbial metabolism in subsurface microbial communities [58]. Driven by both carbon input and electron transfer, microbes in deeper layers may contribute to changes in DOM molecular composition, resulting in a higher proportion of biorecalcitrant CRAM-like molecular compositions [59]. The condensed, heterocyclic, and highly unsaturated structures of CRAM-like compounds contribute to increasing molecular signal intensity with depth, which are commonly regarded as molecular signatures associated with relatively refractory DOM in biofilm-related sediments ([60], 2023; [61]). Consequently, increases in DBE, NC, and SC values with depth were observed, reflecting a typical “microbially mediated DOM degradation and polymerization cycle” in biofilm-related sediments [62]. The accumulation of RDOM in deeper sediment layers may also be associated with the enhanced carbon retention and transformation capacity of anaerobic microbial communities. Under low-oxygen conditions, these microbes utilize specific carbon compounds as both substrates and electron acceptors. This dual utilization pathway reduces overall mineralization rates and facilitates the long-term accumulation of refractory compounds [63]. It should be noted that the present interpretation is based on spatial comparisons among sediment depths rather than temporal observations. Therefore, the observed increase in biorecalcitrant CRAM-like molecular compositions with depth should be regarded as a spatial pattern consistent with progressive microbial DOM transformation, rather than direct evidence that labile DOM was converted into biorecalcitrant DOM in situ. Moreover, although the biorecalcitrant CRAM-like molecules analyzed in this study were identified according to the experimentally constrained criteria proposed by Cai et al. [64], the persistence of DOM in natural environments remains context-dependent and is also influenced by microbial accessibility and environmental conditions.

Although biofilm and surface ABS samples were collected consecutively during the lowest tidal stage and under daytime illumination to minimize tidal and diel migration effects, these factors may still influence biofilm dynamics. Microphytobenthic biofilms commonly exhibit light-driven vertical migration [65], and their development can vary along elevation gradients associated with tidal exposure and inundation frequency [66]. In this study, tidal elevation, inundation frequency, and diel migration were not directly quantified. Future studies integrating tidal gradients and biofilm migration dynamics will help distinguish biofilm-mediated effects from environmental variability in intertidal ecosystems.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

The sampling sites were located at Sanbeiqiantan (SBQT) (30.28° N, 121.43° E) in Ningbo, Aiwanwan (AWW) (28.3° N, 121.5° E) in Taizhou, and Ximendao (XMD) (28.32° N, 121.19° E) in Wenzhou, China. In this study, biofilms were operationally defined as visually apparent microphytobenthic biofilm mats dominated by diatoms on the sediment surface. Sampling locations were initially identified based on the presence of visible biofilm coverage, characterized by a brownish-green surface layer typical of diatom-dominated microphytobenthic communities. Surface biofilm was carefully scraped from the sediment surface, whereas adjacent sediments lacking visible biofilm coverage were collected as surface ABS. To further validate this classification, the relative proportion of algal 18S rRNA gene reads was examined. Samples classified as biofilms exhibited >40% algal 18S rRNA gene reads, whereas surface ABS samples showed lower proportions (Supplementary Table S2). This threshold was used as an operational criterion to distinguish algal-dominated biofilm from surface ABS. At each sampling station, ABS were collected immediately next to biofilm to minimize differences in tidal elevation, inundation frequency, and other site-specific environmental conditions. All samples were collected consecutively during the lowest tidal stage under daytime and strong natural illumination to minimize potential effects of tidal variation and diel vertical migration of phototrophic biofilms. For deep sediment layers, 10-cm plunger cores were collected at four sampling stations, namely S1 and S2 (SBQT), S3 (AWW), and S4 (XMD). Sampling was conducted in June, September, and December 2023 and in March 2024 (Fig. 1). The sediment cores were divided into three depth intervals (i.e. surface, 0–5 and 5–10 cm), resulting in a total of 84 samples. Samples were transported at 4 °C and subsampled for DNA (−80 °C) and physicochemical analyses.

Physicochemical properties analysis

Pore water salinity (SAL), pH, oxidation-reduction potential (ORP), dissolved oxygen (DO), and temperature (T) were measured in situ using a portable multimeter (HQ40D, Hach, USA). Total carbon (TC) was determined using a combustion-based elemental analyzer (Elementar, Vario EL, Germany), representing the sum of organic and inorganic carbon. Total organic carbon (TOC) content was measured using the potassium dichromate volumetric method, whereas total nitrogen (TN) in sediments was determined via the Kjeldahl method. Water content (WC) was quantified using the standard drying method. Triplicate cores at each station were composited prior to analysis.

16S/18S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing and statistical analysis

Environmental DNA from sediment samples (0.5 g each) was extracted using the Magnetic Soil and Stool DNA Kit (TIANGEN, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted DNA was used as a template for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and amplified using the Earth Microbiome Project (EMP) standard primers 515F (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′), targeting the V4 hypervariable region of bacterial 16S rRNA genes [67], and primers 528F (5′-GCGGTAATTCCAGCTCCAA-3′) and 706R (5′-AATCCRAGAATTTCACCTCT-3′), targeting the V4 hypervariable region of eukaryotic 18S rRNA genes [68]. The constructed amplicon libraries were subjected to PE250 sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Guangdong Magigene Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China). For each biological sample, three independent PCR amplifications and library preparations were performed and sequenced as technical replicates to assess sequencing reproducibility. The sequencing results of the three technical replicates were averaged before downstream ecological and statistical analyses, and only the averaged values were used for statistical comparisons.

Raw sequences were processed using QIIME2 version 2021.4 software [69], where adapter removal, quality filtering, and dereplication were performed to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Taxonomic annotation was conducted against the SILVA 132 database. To minimize sequencing depth effects on treatment comparisons, sediment samples were randomly rarefied to the same sequencing depth as the sample with the fewest sequences. Alpha diversity indices, including Simpson index, Shannon index, and Chao1, were calculated for each sample using QIIME2. Furthermore, βNRI and RCbray (Bray–Curtis-based Raup–Crick) indices were computed to infer the relative contributions of ecological processes, including homogeneous dispersal and dispersal limitation, in shaping community structure [[70], [71], [72]]. Bray–Curtis similarity analysis was performed to evaluate differences in bacterial community structure among sampling sites.

Structural equation modelling (SEM)

Relationships between environmental variables and microbial communities were evaluated using Pearson correlations and Mantel tests via the ‘linkET’ package in R (https://github.com/Hy4m/linkET), and results were visualized with the ‘pheatmap’ package (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=pheatmap). The SEM analysis was performed using the lavaan R package. Additionally, Mantel tests were conducted using the ‘linkET’ package to examine potential linkages between environmental variables and microbial community structure. To further investigate the direct and indirect effects of environmental drivers on microbial diversity and key taxa, SEM was applied to assess the relationships among season, geographic location, vertical depth, microbial diversity, and species. Initially, a hypothesized conceptual model including all plausible pathways was constructed [73]. Subsequently, non-significant pathways were sequentially removed to optimize the model, unless the pathways were biologically meaningful, or additional pathways were introduced based on residual correlations. This procedure was repeated until the model achieved satisfactory fit, indicated by a χ2 test p-value greater than 0.05 and a Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) less than 0.08.

Metagenomic sequencing and analysis

Metagenomic libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were quality-filtered and assembled de novo using MEGAHIT within the MetaWRAP pipeline (v1.2.1) [74]. Taxonomic assignment was performed using the blast2lca module in MEGAN (v6.24.20) based on the lowest common ancestor (LCA) algorithm, with reference to the megan-map-Feb2022.db database [75]. Metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) were reconstructed using binning modules implemented in the MetaWRAP pipeline (version 1.2.1) [74]. The quality of MAGs was assessed using CheckM and taxonomy assigned using GTDB-Tk, and classified in accordance with the Minimum Information about a Metagenome-Assembled Genome (MIMAG) standards. MAGs with completeness > 50% and contamination < 5% were defined as medium-quality MAGs [76]. In addition, Community-level average genome size (AGS) and rRNA operon (rrn) copy numbers were calculated as the abundance-weighted sums of NCBI reference genome sizes and rrnDB copy numbers, respectively [77,78]. The relative abundance of each MAG in each sample was subsequently normalized using the TPM method. Relative abundances of MAGs was estimated using CoverM, and dereplication was conducted with dRep (ANI ≥95%). Prior to detailed metabolic analyses, MAGs were assigned to initial taxonomy using the GTDB-Tk classification pipeline, and a phylogenomic tree was generated with IQ-TREE v1.6.12 [79]. MAGs with relative abundances ranking in the top 10% in biofilm-related samples were defined as dominant MAGs of biofilms; MAGs detected only in biofilm-related samples but not in ABS were defined as biofilm-specific MAGs. (ORFs) were predicted using Prodigal and annotated via METABOLIC against KOfam and custom HMM profiles via METABOLIC v.4.0 [80], while CAZymes were annotated using the CAZy database [81]. The final trees were visualized using online platforms (https://www.chiplot.online/tvbot.html).

Fourier-transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometry (FT-ICR MS) analysis

The molecular composition was analyzed using a 9.4 T Apex Ultra FT-ICR MS spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source (Bruker Apollo II). Instrument settings were specified previously [82]. The generated elemental formulas were validated by applying appropriate chemical constraints. DOM relative abundance values were obtained by normalizing peak intensities to the total FT-ICR MS peak intensities. For each assigned formula, the double bond equivalent (DBE), modified aromaticity index (AImod), and nominal oxidation state of carbon (NOSC) were applied to characterize composition and reactivity [83]. The assigned molecular formulae were subsequently grouped into six molecular categories according to their specific oxygen-to-carbon (O/C) and hydrogen-to-carbon (H/C) ratios, as previously described [84]. Van Krevelen diagrams were constructed by plotting O/C against H/C using OriginPro 2024 to visualize compositional changes in molecular profiles. The subset of biorecalcitrant CRAM-like molecules was defined as described by Cai et al. [64], whose molecular signatures were experimentally validated as being resistant to microbial degradation. To explore associations between dissolved organic matter (DOM) components and microbial taxa, Spearman's rank correlation analysis was conducted using OriginPro 2024 [85].
