Adaptive Retrieval for Reasoning-Intensive Retrieval
Jongho Kim, Jaeyoung Kim, Seung-won Hwang, Jihyuk Kim, Yu Jin Kim, Moontae Lee, Interdisciplinary Program in Artificial Intelligence, Seoul National University, LG AI Research {jongh97, jae.young, seungwonh}@snu.ac.kr{jihyuk.kim, yujin.kim, moontae.lee}@lgresearch.ai

Abstract
We study leveraging adaptive retrieval to ensure sufficient ‘‘bridge’’ documents are retrieved for reasoning-intensive retrieval. Bridge documents are those that contribute to the reasoning process yet are not directly relevant to the initial query. While existing reasoning-based reranker pipelines attempt to surface these documents in ranking, they suffer from bounded recall. Naive solution with adaptive retrieval into these pipelines often leads to planning error propagation. To address this, we propose REPAIR, a framework that bridges this gap by repurposing reasoning plans as dense feedback signals for adaptive retrieval. Our key distinction is enabling mid-course correction during reranking through selective adaptive retrieval, retrieving documents that support the pivotal plan. Experimental results on reasoning-intensive retrieval and complex QA tasks demonstrate that our method outperforms existing baselines by 5.6%pt. 1 1 1 Source code: https://anonymous.4open.science/r/RIFT-BEB0/README.md



Figure 1:
A comparison of reranking and adaptive retrieval paradigms.
(a) Reasoning-based reranking first generates a reasoning trace and ranks documents based on the trace, but is limited by bounded recall since it operates on a fixed candidate set.

Introduction

The central challenge of reasoning-intensive retrieval
22
lies in the similarity gap between the user’s query and the necessary evidence for its resolution.
To illustrate, consider the query
q
:
“At home, after I water my plants, the water goes to plates below the pots. Can I reuse it for my plants next time?”

A similarity-based retriever would search for documents containing terms like “reuse plant water”, while the gold document should “bridge” between the premise (collecting runoff water) to the conclusion (it is harmful to reuse).
Such bridging documents are often not similar to the query and ranked low.

Existing approaches rely on
reasoning-based reranking
to surface these bridging documents higher in ranking.
As illustrated in Figure
1
(a), these rerankers generate a reasoning trace for a given query and prioritize documents that appear to support this trace.
While effective at improving precision within a fixed candidate set, reasoning-based rerankers fundamentally suffer from the
bounded recall
problem:
any relevant document missing from the initial retrieval pool
\mathcal{D}
cannot be recovered by reranking alone.

Neighborhood-aware adaptive retrieval (NAR)
17
;
18
offers a principled solution to bounded recall by expanding candidates through document neighborhoods, as shown in Figure
1
(b).
A seemingly natural approach for reasoning-intensive retrieval is therefore to combine reasoning-based rerankers with NAR.

However, such a naive integration is insufficient and can even be detrimental, because effectively combining the two requires identifying which reasoning step should guide expansion—a challenge that neither component can address on its own.
First, reasoning-based rerankers typically generate unstructured reasoning traces and remain a black box to NAR module.
These traces make it difficult for the NAR process to interpret which reasoning steps are under-supported and should trigger expansion.
Second, standard NAR relies solely on top-ranked documents. When these documents are biased toward an incorrect or weak reasoning direction, adaptive retrieval amplifies this bias by expanding their neighbors.

To address this gap, we propose
REPAIR
(
RE
asoning-aware
P
lan-
A
dapt
I
ve
R
etrieval), a framework that enables adaptive retrieval to leverage reasoning signals through an explicit interface.
As illustrated in Figure 1(c), REPAIR exposes planning steps as interpretable signals, transforms them into dense, step-level rewards, and selectively performs adaptive retrieval for the most pivotal reasoning steps.
Guided by these signals, REPAIR selectively performs adaptive retrieval for the most pivotal reasoning steps, enabling mid-course correction during retrieval.

Empirical results on reasoning-intensive retrieval and multi-hop QA benchmarks demonstrate that REPAIR consistently outperforms reasoning-based rerankers and naive extension methods, validating the importance of plan-adaptive retrieval.

Our contributions are threefold:

•

We clarify the role of reranker and NAR in reasoning-intensive retrieval, and show that their naive combination is detrimental.

•

We propose REPAIR, a plan-adaptive retrieval framework that exposes reasoning steps as interpretable signals to focus adaptive retrieval on pivotal reasoning steps, as dense step-level rewards.

•

We demonstrate the effectiveness of REPAIR on reasoning-intensive retrieval and multi-hop question answering benchmarks, achieving consistent improvements over strong reranking and adaptive retrieval baselines by up to 5.6%pt.

Preliminaries

2.1
Reasoning-Intensive Retrieval in RAG

We consider reasoning-intensive retrieval under the RAG framework, where
given a complex query
q
, a first-stage retriever (e.g., BM25) returns an initial candidate set
\mathcal{D}
.
As motivated in Section 1, answering
q
in reasoning-intensive retrieval requires bridge documents that support intermediate reasoning steps, which are often only indirectly related to
q
and thus ranked low by the retriever.
As a result, effective systems must enhance the precision and recall of the initial ranking, using reranking and NAR, respectively.

2.2
Improving Precision: Reranking

A listwise (or setwise) reranker
\phi
is typically used to take a query
q
and a document list to output a ranked order by estimating collective relevance for reasoning.
Figure 1(a) illustrates such reranking, commonly implemented by prompting an LLM with a ranking prompt
p
.
Since the candidate set is typically large (e.g., top-100), reranking is performed over overlapping sliding windows
\mathcal{W}
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{W}^{\prime}&=\phi\left(p,q,\mathcal{W}\right)\\
&=[d_{r_{1}}>\dots>d_{r_{|\mathcal{W}|}}],\end{split}

(1)

where
\{r_{1},\dots,r_{|\mathcal{W}|}\}
is a permutation indicating the estimated ranking.

While effective at promoting documents directly relevant to
q
, rerankers suffer from
bounded recall
: any relevant document missing from
\mathcal{D}
cannot be recovered. Hence, we turn to adaptive retrieval for recall.

2.3
Improving Recall: NAR

Figure 1(b) illustrates how NAR, following reranking, addresses its bounded recall by expanding candidates using a pre-computed corpus graph
G

17
;
18
.
After reranking a window
\mathcal{W}
, neighbors of the top-
k
documents are retrieved and merged into the next window:

\begin{split}\mathcal{W}^{\prime}&=\phi\left(p,q,\mathcal{W}\right)\\
\mathcal{N}&=\mathrm{Neighbors}(G,\mathcal{W}^{\prime}[:k])\\
\mathcal{W}_{\text{next}}&\leftarrow\mathcal{W}^{\prime}[:k]\oplus\mathcal{N}[:b-k],\end{split}

(2)

where
b
is the window size and
\oplus
denotes concatenation.
In conventional prior works
17
,
\mathrm{Neighbors}
retrieves and ranks neighbors using document–document similarity from
G
.
2
2

2

Neighbor expansion is typically applied every other window.

Under the Clustering Hypothesis
7
, documents similar to highly ranked ones are likely relevant to the same query.
By iteratively expanding the candidate set, NAR improves recall by recovering documents missed by the first-stage retriever.

2.4
Baseline: Reranking and NAR

A naive baseline to integrate them would be simply appending standard NAR to the reasoning rerankers as in Figure
1
(b).
While reasoning-based rerankers such as Rank-R1
27
and ReasonRank
10
internally perform planning-like reasoning, their unstructured reasoning traces are produced solely for ranking and cannot be used by NAR, as we later propose to reuse them for identifying under-supported steps.

This leads to a shared challenge in determining which reasoning step should guide expansion, as both the reranker and the adaptive retrieval component lack the necessary information to make this decision.
From the reranking perspective, its reasoning process remains a black box, making it unclear which parts of the reasoning steps are under-supported and should guide expansion. Existing rerankers are optimized for final ranking rather than step-wise interpretability, so their intermediate traces are often not reliably structured.
From the NAR perspective, NAR expands candidates based on top-ranked documents as shown in the second line in Eq.
2
, without knowing which reasoning steps they support.
Consequently, the two components operate largely independently, limiting the effectiveness of adaptive retrieval in reasoning-intensive settings.
These limitations motivate a tighter integration between reranking and adaptive retrieval, where reasoning signals are made explicit and interpretable for retrieval.

3
Proposed: Dense PSR and SAR

We propose REPAIR combining PSR and SAR that plan, rerank, and adaptively retrieve for each window.
Our key innovation is that the results from each window are leveraged as a dense reward signal for the next window.
Based on the densified reward, REPAIR corrects high-ranked documents for each window, by selectively retrieving for the step with high reward.
The overall algorithm is presented in Algorithm
1
.
We elaborate on these modules below.

3.1
Planning-based Step Reranker (PSR)

As shown in line 2-6 in Algorithm
1
, PSR is a listwise reranker that first generates a plan comprising reasoning steps to answer
q
. It then uses the plan as guidance to rerank
\mathcal{W}
, producing
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
.

Our goal is to build a deliberately simplified planning reranker interface that can better leverage the planning signal in prior work.
Reasoning-based rerankers already emit planning as part of their reasoning (as illustrated in Appendix
D.6
),
but these traces are discarded after ranking.
Instead, we repurpose trace as the signal an adaptive retriever needs to decide where to expand.

To this end, we train a lightweight LLM-based reranker that takes
(p_{\text{plan}},q,\mathcal{W})
as input and outputs a re-ordered document list
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
and plan
\mathcal{R}
, where
p_{\text{plan}}
is LLM prompt for planning (presented in Appendix
B
).
We emphasize that this reranker is not intended to outperform prior planning-based rerankers in isolation. Rather, it reproduces their core behavior through a minimal interface that enables tight integration with adaptive retrieval.

Specifically, at each window, we formalize the interface as:

\begin{split}(\mathcal{W}^{\prime},\mathcal{R})&=(\mathcal{W}^{\prime},\{t_{1},\dots,t_{\ell},\dots,t_{M}\}),\\
&=\phi(p_{\text{plan}},q,\mathcal{W})\\
\end{split}

(3)

where
\mathcal{W}
denotes the window.
We use
\mathcal{R}
as an explicit planning interface, making intermediate reasoning steps interpretable for subsequent modules.

For reranking,
\mathcal{R}
replaces a single query
q
with a set of intermediate information requirements.
This design closely follows existing reasoning-based rerankers, but is deliberately simplified to ensure that the planning signal is interpretable.

3.2
Step Selection Reward

While prior works naively expand documents for every individual step, our distinction is to compute which steps we should selectively target retrieval to maximize the answer coverage of
q
.
The computation is shown in line 8 in Algorithm
1
.
Unlike
sparse
signals
24
that only emerge after all the windows are processed, we evaluate each step in
\mathcal{R}
within the current window
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
to guide selective expansion during the process.
We use two dense reward functions, elaborated on below.

Baseline Reward

Similar to prior work
24
that improves similarity for each sub-query, we compute a baseline reward
r^{\textrm{base}}
based on how much individual steps are similar to the top-ranked documents in the current window.
Given
t_{\ell}
, the reward can be formulated as:

r^{\textrm{base}}_{\ell}=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{W}^{\prime}|}\sum_{d\in\mathcal{W}^{\prime}}\sigma\left(\mathrm{sim}(t_{\ell},d)\right),

(4)

where
\mathrm{sim}(t_{\ell},d)
measures embedding similarity between the step description and a document, and
\sigma
denotes the sigmoid function for normalization.
A higher
r^{\textrm{base}}_{\ell}
indicates that a step
t_{\ell}
has not yet been sufficiently covered by the top-ranked documents observed so far, suggesting remaining room for improvement.

However, not all reasoning steps contribute equally to answering the query
13
. A pivotal step may exist upon which other steps are dependent. Nevertheless,
r^{\textrm{base}}
measures step-level similarity gaps in isolation, over-prioritizing less important steps, while overlooking critical ones that substantially affect final ranking.

Consistency Reward

Therefore, we introduce a consistency reward
r^{\textrm{con}}
that evaluates whether a step in the current window
i
consistently aligns with the reranker’s preferences across previous window iterations (
1\dots i-1
).
This reward captures a longer-term notion of contribution by aggregating evidence from past ranking behaviors.

Specifically, we interpret the iterative reranking process as generating implicit pairwise preferences between documents over time.
Rather than relying on single-window outcomes, we aggregate these preferences using the Bradley–Terry model
1
to estimate a consensus preference distribution
P
:

P(d_{p}\succ d_{q})=\frac{e^{\gamma_{pq}}}{e^{\gamma_{pq}}+e^{\gamma_{qp}}},

(5)

where
\gamma_{pq}
denotes how frequently document
d_{p}
has been ranked above
d_{q}
across past iterations.

Let
t_{i,\ell}
a step at
i
-th window and
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}_{t_{i,\ell}}
the document ranking produced using only step
t_{i,\ell}
.
We define the set of all ordered document pairs
\mathcal{T}_{t_{i,\ell}}
induced by this ranking as:

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}_{t_{i,\ell}}=&\{(d_{p},d_{q})\mid d_{p}>d_{q}\text{ in }\mathcal{W}^{\prime}_{t_{i,\ell}}\}\end{split}

(6)

We then define the consistency reward for a step as the average alignment between this step-specific ranking
\mathcal{T}_{t_{i,\ell}}
and the consensus model
P
:

r^{\textrm{con}}_{i,\ell}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{t_{i,\ell}}|}\sum_{(d_{p},d_{q})\in\mathcal{T}_{t_{i,\ell}}}P(d_{p}\succ d_{q}),

(7)

A low
r^{\textrm{con}}_{i,\ell}
indicates that the ranking induced by
t_{i,\ell}
contradicts the established consensus from earlier windows, suggesting that
t_{i,\ell}
may negatively contribute to the reasoning chain.

Finally, our goal is to identify a step that has maximum reward.
We define the final reward
r_{i,\ell}
, for the step
t_{i,\ell}
, by combining two rewards
3
3

3

For simplicity, we implemented
f
as linear combination (
r^{\textrm{base}}_{i,\ell}+r^{\textrm{con}}_{i,\ell}
).

:

r_{i,\ell}=f(r^{\textrm{base}}_{i,\ell},r^{\textrm{con}}_{i,\ell})

(8)

We highlight that this reward formulation is computationally efficient.
A standard reranker makes one LLM call per window to produce a single ranked list, yielding only one piece of feedback. In contrast, our approach extracts multiple signals from that same output. For a given window with
M
reasoning steps, we generate
M
distinct reward signals.
By the
i
-th iteration, this accumulates
i\times M
signals, forming a much denser feedback loop without incurring additional LLM cost.

3.3
Step-Adaptive NAR (SAR)

In contrast to standard NAR (§
2.3
), which indiscriminately retrieves neighbors based on the top-
k
documents from the current window, our SAR selectively constructs the next window to focus on the highest-reward step
t_{i,\star}
using the generated reward.

With
r_{i,\ell}
, we compare each step’s score and identify the step
t_{i,\star}
that should be selected for the candidate expansion.

t_{i,\star}=\argmax_{t_{i,\ell}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}}~r_{i,\ell}

(9)

We then gather additional candidate documents from the neighbors of top-
k
documents in the graph
G
, following standard NAR.
To enlarge the candidate pool for
t_{i,\star}
, we combine these neighbor documents with the next-window candidates
\mathcal{W}_{i+1}
that the first-stage retriever would have retrieved.
From this combined pool, we select the top-
(b-k)
documents most relevant to
t_{i,\star}
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}=&\mathrm{Neighbors}(G,\mathcal{W}_{i}^{\prime}[:k])\\
\mathcal{W}_{i+1}\leftarrow&\mathcal{W}^{\prime}_{i}[:k]~\oplus\\
\{d\in\mathcal{N}&\cup\mathcal{W}_{i+1}\mid\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{sim}}(d)\leq b-k\}\end{split}

(10)

The resulting
\mathcal{W}_{i+1}
serves as the input window for the next iteration of PSR.
To ensure a fair comparison with standard NAR (§
2.3
) in retrieval frequency, we apply adaptive retrieval only in the latter half of iterations.
This scheduling also allows the preference model based on the accumulated reranking results (§
3.2
) to become sufficiently stable before guiding adaptive retrieval.

4.1
Experimental Setting

Dataset

We evaluate our method on both reasoning-intensive information retrieval (IR) multi-hop question answering (QA) tasks.

For information retrieval evaluation, we used BRIGHT
22
, which consists of 12 subsets. In BRIGHT, the gold document is often dissimilar to the given query, yet it is necessary to traverse the reasoning steps to answer the query (i.e., bridge documents).
For example, in StackExchange subsets, the target document is defined by whether the document is cited in the reasoning to reach the answer.

For multi-hop QA benchmarks, we include HotpotQA
25
, 2WIKI
3
, and MusiQue
23
, where answering a query requires composing multiple intermediate facts. We randomly sample 500 queries for each dataset due to the cost issue.
We use nDCG@10 for the IR task and Exact Match (EM) and F1 scores for QA, following the common practice.

Baselines

We compare REPAIR against the following baselines.
BM25
19
is a traditional lexical matching retriever widely used in IR.
RankZephyr
12
is a GPT4-distilled listwise reranker.
SUNAR
R

24
is a NAR method that decomposes the query and applies reranker with NAR for each sub-query.
Rank-R1
27
, REARANK
26
, and ReasonRank
10
are reasoning-based setwise and listwise rerankers, respectively, that perform intermediate reasoning before generating the ranked output.
For rerankers, including ours, we apply reranking to the top 100 documents retrieved in the first stage.

5
Conclusion

This paper addressed the limitations of naively combining reasoning-based reranking with neighborhood-aware adaptive retrieval in reasoning-intensive retrieval by introducing REPAIR.
PSR provides an interpretable planning interface to enable tighter integration between reranking and adaptive retrieval, while the SAR uses a densified reward for targeted, mid-course correction.
Our experiments show that REPAIR is an effective and efficient solution, successfully bringing bridge documents through selective adaptive retrieval in reasoning-intensive retrieval.

6
Limitation

We designed a simplified PSR to emit an interpretable plan instead of leveraging existing rerankers.
Although many rerankers generate planning-like traces, their output formats and contents differ substantially, which makes direct plug-and-play integration non-trivial and potentially confounded by additional engineering choices.
Developing general conversion or alignment techniques to make diverse reranker outputs compatible with SAR remains open.

Also, our framework relies on the quality of the planning steps exposed by the reranker, which are used as an interface to guide adaptive retrieval.
Although the proposed step-level rewards help mitigate the impact of weak or misleading steps, errors in the initial planning can still affect retrieval decisions.
An important direction for future work is to go beyond using a fixed plan per window and explore mechanisms for revising or refining planning steps during retrieval.

Appendix A
Related Works

A.1
Reasoning-Intensive Retrieval

BRIGHT
22
first introduced the reasoning-intensive retrieval, which necessitates in-depth reasoning to identify relevant documents that go beyond surface form matching. Prior works involve three directions: reranker, query-rewriter, and retriever.
Reasoning-based rerankers such as Rank-R1
27
, ReasonRank
10
, and REARANK
26
use explicit reasoning, often with RL or chain-of-thought style supervision, to promote latent supporting evidence within a fixed candidate pool.
Another line of work focuses on query rewriting such as TongSearch-QR
14
, where the system rewrites the query with intermediate reasoning to increase retrieval recall upstream.
Complementarily, reasoning-aware retrievers like ReasonIR
21
train the retriever itself to cover in-depth information needs.
REPAIR is orthogonal to those approaches, by repurposing the reranker’s reasoning plan as a dense feedback signal to drive adaptive retrieval.

A.2
Neighborhood-Adaptive Retrieval

The effectiveness of reranking systems is fundamentally constrained by the recall of the initial retrieval stage
11
;
17
. Relevant documents that are missing from the candidate set cannot be recovered later, which leads to a significant decline in overall performance.
To address this, a line of research adopts Neighborhood Aware Retrieval (NAR) based on the Clustering Hypothesis
7
.
Conventional adaptive algorithms alternate between scoring first-stage results and exploring the corpus graph with reranked documents, either by pointwise
11
or slidegar manner
17
. A recent extension, SUNAR
24
, incorporates query decomposition to support multi-hop retrieval.
Our framework is distinguished by selecting the effective step for the adaptive retrieval.
