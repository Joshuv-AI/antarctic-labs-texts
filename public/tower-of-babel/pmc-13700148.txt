Simulation diabetes prevalence under modifiable risk and healthcare access scenarios among low-income adults in high-growth U.S. states
Authors: Buddhika Patalee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes remains a major public health concern in the United States, with a disproportionate burden among low-income populations. Despite rapid economic growth in several U.S. states, improvements in population health have not been evenly distributed. Understanding how modifiable behavioral and healthcare access factors correspond to variation in diagnosed diabetes prevalence is important for informing effective public health strategies. This study evaluates how diagnosed diabetes prevalence among low-income adults in high-growth U.S. states may vary under alternative behavioral and healthcare access scenarios.MethodsWe used Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) data from 2020 to 2024 to analyze diagnosed diabetes prevalence among low-income adults in the ten U.S. states with the highest economic growth. Scenario-based simulations were constructed by modifying key risk factors, including body mass index (BMI), physical activity, smoking status, cost-related barriers to healthcare, and utilization of routine preventive checkups. A conditional generative modeling framework was applied to estimate model-based predicted prevalence under these alternative scenarios while accounting for nonlinear relationships, latent heterogeneity, and survey design features.ResultsAcross all states, lower BMI profiles corresponded to the largest and most consistent reductions in predicted diabetes prevalence, with substantially greater magnitudes than those observed under other scenarios. Higher levels of physical activity were also associated with consistent and statistically significant reductions, although of smaller magnitude. Scenarios characterized by the absence of cost-related barriers to care corresponded to moderate but consistent decreases in predicted prevalence. Similarly, scenarios in which current smoking was eliminated produced uniformly negative but comparatively modest changes. In contrast, increased utilization of preventive checkups corresponded to higher predicted diagnosed diabetes prevalence in several states, consistent with greater detection of previously undiagnosed cases rather than changes in underlying disease risk.ConclusionsWithin the predictive simulation framework, lower BMI scenarios corresponded to the largest reductions in model-predicted diagnosed diabetes prevalence among the individual covariate modifications examined, whereas healthcare access scenarios primarily influenced predicted diagnosed prevalence through detection-related pathways. These findings highlight the importance of distinguishing between underlying disease risk and diagnostic processes when interpreting population-level patterns in diagnosed diabetes prevalence. Because each scenario modified selected predictors individually while all other observed characteristics were held constant, the estimated differences should be interpreted as model-based responses to isolated covariate modifications rather than realistic changes in the joint distribution of risk factors or causal intervention effects. Such predictive simulations may nevertheless provide useful evidence to inform public health planning and the prioritization of future interventions among low-income populations.Keywords: Diabetes prevalence, Scenario simulation, Low-income population, BRFSS, Conditional variational autoencoder

Introduction

Economic growth at the state level is often assumed to translate into improved population health through increased employment opportunities, higher incomes, and expanded public resources [1, 29]. However, a growing body of evidence indicates that aggregate economic expansion does not benefit all segments of the population equally. Disadvantaged groups may remain exposed to structural barriers, unhealthy environments, and limited access to healthcare despite rising gross domestic product (GDP) or population inflows [5, 9, 19]. In several rapidly growing U.S. states, rising GDP has coincided with persistent or widening health disparities, raising concerns that economic expansion alone may be insufficient to improve health outcomes among low-income adults [7, 20, 35].

Low-income adults face a disproportionate burden of chronic diseases such as diabetes, reflecting the combined influence of metabolic and behavioral risk factors, socioeconomic constraints, and barriers to preventive healthcare. Obesity, physical inactivity, smoking, and limited access to medical care are among the key contributors to diabetes incidence and progression [11, 12, 24, 36]. These risk factors are not determined solely by individual choices but are also shaped by broader social and policy environments that influence healthcare access, affordability, and health-related behaviors [20, 32]. Consequently, regional economic growth may coexist with substantial heterogeneity in health infrastructure, social safety nets, and affordability of care, leading to uneven health outcomes even when economic indicators improve. These patterns are closely linked to broader social determinants of health that shape diabetes risk and management [11].

Traditional observational analyses have linked socioeconomic, behavioral, and clinical factors to health outcomes but typically rely on parametric specifications and strong identification assumptions. Methodological advances such as regression discontinuity designs have provided quasi-experimental frameworks for causal inference when treatment assignment is determined by threshold rules [16]. Multivariable logistic regression is widely employed to assess associations between health behaviors and outcomes in survey data [14] however, this framework provides limited insight into how population-level prevalence may shift under alternative risk factor or hypothetical scenarios, particularly in the presence of nonlinear and high-dimensional relationships.

Recent developments in machine learning offer complementary tools for modeling complex dependencies and generating scenario-based projections from large observational datasets. Deep generative models, such as variational autoencoders, learn joint distributions of outcomes and covariates while capturing latent heterogeneity in the data [15]. Conditional variational autoencoders (CVAEs) extend this framework by conditioning the generative process on observed covariates and enabling the generation of predicted outcomes under modified covariate profiles [31]. CVAE-based approaches have been used for policy simulation and scenario analysis in healthcare contexts [18, 27]. Such methods are particularly appropriate for examining how diabetes prevalence might vary across hypothetical distributions of metabolic risk factors and healthcare access, while emphasizing that the resulting projections are simulations rather than causal effects.

Building on these strands of literature, we apply a CVAE-based generative modeling framework to Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) data from 2020 to 2024 to simulate diagnosed diabetes prevalence among low-income adults in the ten U.S. states [33] with the highest GDP growth. We generate predicted prevalence under a set of hypothetical scenarios characterized by lower body mass index (BMI) profiles, higher levels of physical activity, elimination of current smoking, absence of cost-related barriers to care, and increased utilization of routine preventive checkups. By preserving complex nonlinear dependencies and latent heterogeneity while incorporating survey weights, our approach provides projections of how diabetes prevalence may vary under alternative distributions of modifiable risk factors and healthcare access characteristics.

This study contributes to the literature in three ways. First, it provides new evidence on how modifiable behavioral and healthcare access factors relate to variation in diabetes prevalence among low-income adults in rapidly growing U.S. states, a population for whom economic expansion has not necessarily translated into improved health outcomes. Second, it demonstrates the use of a conditional generative modeling framework to generate scenario-based prevalence projections using nationally representative survey data, offering an alternative to parametric or quasi-experimental approaches for examining projected prevalence patterns under modified risk factor profiles. Third, by comparing metabolic risk scenarios with healthcare access scenarios, the study compares the relative magnitude of projected prevalence differences across scenarios involving weight management, physical activity, smoking behavior, healthcare access, and preventive care utilization. The findings may inform public health planning regarding diabetes prevention and healthcare access among low-income populations in heterogeneous state contexts.

Data and methods

Data

We used data from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), administered by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), for survey years 2020 through 2024. The BRFSS provides state-representative information on health conditions, health-related risk behaviors, healthcare access, and use of preventive services among noninstitutionalized U.S. adults aged 18 years and older [6]. From these data, we extracted variables related to diabetes status, smoking behavior, body mass index (BMI), healthcare access, preventive care utilization, and demographic characteristics.

Self-reported measures in BRFSS introduce several limitations that should be considered when interpreting prevalence estimates. First, diabetes status in BRFSS is based on self-reported physician diagnosis and may therefore underestimate true prevalence because individuals without regular healthcare access may remain undiagnosed. Previous research suggests that approximately 20%−25% of diabetes cases in the United States are undiagnosed, with underdiagnosis disproportionately affecting low-income populations that face greater barriers to clinical screening. Second, reliance on self-reported diagnosis may generate differential misclassification across socioeconomic groups, since individuals with higher healthcare utilization are more likely to receive a formal diagnosis. Third, survey response patterns during 2020 were affected by the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have altered response rates and the composition of survey participants in some states. Despite these limitations, validation studies indicate that BRFSS remains a reliable source for population-level surveillance of chronic disease risk factors and health behaviors when appropriate survey weights are applied [25]. Because both observed prevalence and scenario-based simulations in this study rely on the same self-reported outcome measure, the results should be interpreted as reflecting diagnosed diabetes prevalence rather than the total burden of disease.

To ensure population-representative inference, all analyses incorporated the BRFSS final sampling weights. During model estimation, sampling weights were normalized to improve numerical stability and were incorporated into the loss function so that the conditional CVAE learned from observations in proportion to their representation in the target population. After model training, state-level diabetes prevalence was estimated as a design-weighted mean. For observed prevalence, the binary diabetes outcome was averaged using survey weights. For scenario-specific predicted prevalence, model-predicted probabilities were averaged using the same survey weights to maintain population representativeness. This ensured that both observed and predicted prevalence estimates reflected the underlying population distribution of low-income adults in the selected states.

Uncertainty was quantified using a stratified primary sampling unit (PSU) bootstrap that accounts for the complex survey design of BRFSS. Within each stratum defined by the survey stratification variable, PSUs were resampled with replacement while preserving the original number of PSUs per stratum. Strata containing a single PSU was treated as certainty strata and were included unchanged in each bootstrap replicate to avoid instability. For each replicate, weighted prevalence was recomputed, and percentile-based 95% confidence intervals were obtained from the empirical bootstrap distribution. This approach preserves clustering, stratification, and unequal probability weighting, thereby providing design-consistent variance estimation for both observed and scenario-specific predicted prevalence estimates. For estimated changes, the bootstrap was applied directly to the within-replicate difference between predicted and observed prevalence in order to preserve covariance between the two quantities. All prevalence estimates reported in this study are computed within the low-income subpopulation and therefore represent state-level diabetes prevalence among low-income adults rather than the full state population.

Methods

We used survey data from U.S. adults to identify low-income individuals across selected high-growth states and to measure their diabetes status. Our main outcome measure was diagnosed diabetes prevalence, which was modeled at the individual level. To examine how behavioral and healthcare access factors correspond to variation in this outcome, we constructed a series of simulation scenarios based on key health-related factors, including physical activity, smoking, routine checkups, body weight, and cost-related barriers to care.

We defined low-income adults as individuals with annual household income below $35,000. This threshold aligns closely with commonly used U.S. low-income benchmarks during 2020–2024, including federal poverty guidelines and eligibility thresholds for several means-tested programs when adjusted for household size and inflation. To assess robustness, we additionally evaluated more restrictive definitions (< $15,000 and < $25,000). While results were directionally consistent across specifications, the < $35,000 threshold provided the most stable model performance, including improved discrimination and calibration metrics. Given its alignment with policy-relevant income cutoffs, we use < $35,000 as our primary low-income definition. In addition to the variables used to define the simulation scenarios, we incorporated a broader set of demographic, socioeconomic, and behavioral characteristics such as age group (18–34, 35–49, 50–79, and 80 +), sex, education, and racial/ethnic background to improve model performance and ensure that predictions reflected realistic population-level patterns. State and survey year were also included as categorical conditioning variables in the model, allowing the CVAE to account for state-specific and year-specific variation in covariate distributions and diagnosed diabetes prevalence.

To generate scenario-based predictions, we employ a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE). CVAEs are deep generative models that extend the VAE framework to estimate conditional distributions of outcomes given observed covariates [15, 31]. CVAEs introduce a latent variable that captures unobserved heterogeneity and complex nonlinear relationships between predictors and outcomes, while explicitly conditioning the generative process on individual-level characteristics. The latent variable captures residual variation and complex dependence structures in the data. However, it does not imply valid adjustment for unobserved confounding. From a causal perspective, such adjustment would require strong and untestable assumptions about the data-generating process, which are not imposed or evaluated in this framework. Model parameters are estimated by maximizing a variational lower bound on the conditional log-likelihood, which balances accurate reconstruction of the outcome with regularization of the latent space through a Kullback–Leibler divergence penalty.

This conditional latent-variable structure enables CVAEs to flexibly model high-dimensional dependencies and interactions that are difficult to capture using traditional parametric or tree-based approaches. Prior methodological work has shown that deep latent-variable models can be used to simulate outcomes under modified covariate distributions in observational settings, particularly in contexts characterized by distributional shifts and unobserved heterogeneity [18, 22]. In this study, the CVAE is used to learn the joint distribution of diabetes outcomes and observed covariates and to generate predicted outcomes under alternative covariate configurations while preserving realistic population-level patterns.

The model estimates the conditional distribution of diabetes outcomes Y given observed covariates X and uses this learned distribution to generate predicted outcomes under modified covariate profiles. Accordingly, the framework approximates the conditional expectation E[Y∣ X] under alternative covariate configurations rather than interventional quantities such as E[Y∣ do(X)]. The latent variable captures residual variation and nonlinear dependence structures in the data; however, it does not imply adjustment for unobserved confounding. From a causal perspective, such adjustment would require strong and untestable assumptions about the data-generating process, which are not imposed or evaluated in this framework. Consistent with this distinction, the resulting simulations should be interpreted as model-based projections under distributional changes in observed variables rather than as causal effects of interventions [10]. All analyses were conducted in Python (version 3.12) using packages including pandas, NumPy, PyTorch, scikit-learn, and GeoPandas.

Simulation scenarios

To examine how variation in modifiable health behaviors and healthcare access factors corresponds to differences in diagnosed diabetes prevalence, we constructed a series of simulation scenarios using selected predictors representing modifiable behavioral and healthcare access characteristics. These factors are routinely measured in national health surveillance systems because they capture modifiable behavioral risk factors and healthcare access characteristics associated with diabetes prevalence. Each scenario was generated by modifying one selected predictor at a time while retaining all other observed individual characteristics at their recorded values. The trained Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) was then used to estimate the model-predicted probability of diagnosed diabetes for each individual under the modified covariate profile. These simulations provide structured projections of how diabetes prevalence may vary under alternative covariate configurations within the joint distribution learned from the data.

Each simulation scenario modified one or more selected predictor variables while retaining all remaining observed covariates at their recorded values. Consequently, the scenarios do not model the complex joint dependencies among behavioral, socioeconomic, and healthcare-related factors represented conceptually in Fig. 1. Instead, they provide model-based predictive projections under hypothetical covariate modifications and should not be interpreted as realistic population interventions or policy implementations. The resulting simulated covariate profiles are therefore intended to illustrate how predicted diabetes prevalence varies under specified predictor values rather than to represent feasible changes in population behavior or healthcare systems.

Lower BMI scenario:

BMI is one of the strongest behavioral and metabolic risk factors linked to diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and numerous other chronic conditions. Public health programs frequently target obesity reduction, making BMI a central variable in scenario-based modeling.

Scenario specification:

For individuals classified as overweight or obese, BMI was reduced to a healthier weight category (approximated using the 25th percentile of the BMI distribution), while all other covariates remained unchanged. This scenario represents a hypothetical shift toward lower BMI values and allows evaluation of how model-predicted diabetes prevalence varies under this modified covariate profile.

Increased physical activity scenario:

Physical activity is linked to a lower risk of diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease. Increasing activity levels is a major focus of national health guidelines [2–4, 23, 26].

Scenario specification:

All individuals reporting no physical activity were recoded as physically active. This scenario represents a hypothetical increase in physical activity and allows evaluation of how model-predicted diabetes prevalence varies under this modified covariate profile.

No-cost barrier scenario:

Inability to seek care due to cost is a major structural barrier contributing to delayed diagnoses and unmanaged chronic conditions.

Scenario specification:

Individuals reporting cost-related barriers to healthcare were recoded as having no cost barrier. This scenario represents a hypothetical reduction in financial barriers to healthcare and allows evaluation of how model-predicted diabetes prevalence varies under this modified covariate profile.

Routine checkup scenario:

Routine checkups are important for early detection and management of chronic diseases. States with lower preventive care utilization often face higher disease burdens.

Scenario specification:

Individuals who had not received a checkup within the past year were recoded as having received a routine checkup in the past 12 months. This scenario represents a hypothetical increase in preventive healthcare utilization and allows evaluation of how model-predicted diagnosed diabetes prevalence varies under this modified covariate profile.

Non-smoking scenario:

Smoking is a significant behavioral risk factor for Type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and macrovascular complications. Because smoking status is routinely collected in BRFSS and represents a modifiable behavioral risk factor, it was included as one of the hypothetical simulation scenarios.

Scenario specification:

Individuals classified as current smokers were recoded as non-smokers, while all other covariates remained unchanged. This scenario represents a hypothetical reduction in smoking exposure and allows evaluation of how model-predicted diabetes prevalence varies under this modified covariate profile.

Together, these scenarios represent structured hypothetical modifications to selected behavioral and healthcare access variables. The resulting predictions are model-based projections under modified covariate profiles and should be interpreted as predictive scenario analyses rather than estimates of realistic population interventions or causal intervention effects.

The dataset is partitioned into training, validation, and test subsets for model estimation and predictive performance evaluation. However, for scenario-based prevalence estimation, predicted probabilities are generated for the full analytic sample using the trained model. This approach incorporates the full survey design, including sampling weights, strata, and PSUs, thereby improving statistical efficiency and yielding valid design-based confidence intervals for population-level prevalence under each scenario. For each scenario, bootstrap replicates are used to compute mean predicted prevalence and corresponding 95% confidence intervals, as well as confidence intervals for differences relative to the observed baseline.

To clarify the conceptual relationships among the variables included in the simulation framework, we present a conceptual directed acyclic graph (DAG) (Fig. 1) illustrating the hypothesized links among demographic characteristics, socioeconomic position, state and policy context, behavioral and metabolic risk factors, healthcare access, healthcare utilization, and diagnosed diabetes prevalence. The DAG is intended as a conceptual illustration to support interpretation of the predictive simulation framework and is not a causal identification model, nor was it used to determine adjustment sets or estimate intervention effects. The DAG distinguishes pathways related to underlying diabetes risk from pathways related to healthcare access and disease detection. Specifically, behavioral and metabolic factors, including BMI, physical activity, and smoking, are conceptualized as contributing to underlying diabetes risk, whereas healthcare access factors may influence the likelihood that existing diabetes is detected and recorded in survey data. In particular, recent checkups and healthcare utilization may affect the diagnosis of previously undiagnosed diabetes rather than the underlying disease process itself.

The DAG provides a conceptual framework for interpreting the simulation scenarios and the relationships among variables represented in the model. To maintain interpretability, the figure focuses on the primary pathways relevant to the simulation framework and does not attempt to represent the full complexity of diabetes etiology or healthcare utilization. Accordingly, several important determinants, including diet, insurance coverage, comorbidities, and health system characteristics, are represented only conceptually rather than explicitly modeled. In addition, reciprocal feedback mechanisms (e.g., diagnosed diabetes influencing subsequent healthcare utilization) and other time-varying relationships are not depicted because DAGs are acyclic.

Under this hypothetical scenario, the model predicted lower diagnosed diabetes prevalence across all high-growth states relative to the observed baseline. In each state, the estimated differences were negative and statistically significant, although their magnitude varied, with smaller differences observed in Utah and South Carolina and larger differences in Nevada and Tennessee. Because diagnosed diabetes in BRFSS reflects physician-confirmed cases, changes in healthcare access may influence both the likelihood of diagnosis and subsequent disease management. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as model-based predictive projections under a hypothetical covariate modification rather than as estimates of the causal effects of removing cost-related barriers.

Discussion

This study examines how behavioral risk factors and healthcare access mechanisms correspond to variation in diabetes prevalence among low-income populations in high-growth U.S. states using a CVAE-based simulation framework. The reported estimates represent model-based projections under altered covariate profiles rather than causal treatment effects, highlighting the importance of distinguishing between factors associated with underlying metabolic risk and those that influence disease detection through healthcare engagement.

Across all scenarios, the BMI simulation scenario is associated with the largest and most consistent reductions in model-predicted diabetes prevalence. This pattern is consistent with extensive clinical and epidemiological evidence linking excess body weight and metabolic dysfunction to diabetes risk [8, 12, 17, 21, 28, 34]. Within the predictive simulation framework, the isolated modification of BMI produced larger model-predicted differences in diagnosed diabetes prevalence than the other individual covariate modifications examined. This comparison reflects the model’s response to the specified scenario values and should not be interpreted as evidence that BMI-focused interventions would necessarily produce larger real-world effects than interventions targeting other behavioral or healthcare-related factors.

The exercise and smoking simulation scenarios are also associated with consistent reductions in model-predicted diabetes prevalence, although the magnitude of these reductions is smaller than that observed under the BMI scenario. These results are broadly consistent with prior evidence linking physical activity and smoking behavior to metabolic health and chronic disease risk, while also indicating that their associations may operate through more complex or indirect pathways within this modeling framework.

In contrast, healthcare access scenarios exhibit distinct patterns. Under the no-cost barrier simulation scenario, model-predicted diabetes prevalence is lower, indicating that variation in healthcare access is associated with differences in modeled diabetes outcomes. Conversely, the preventive checkup simulation scenario is associated with higher model-predicted diabetes prevalence across several states. Given that diabetes status in BRFSS reflects physician diagnosis, these increases are most plausibly interpreted as reflecting improved detection of previously undiagnosed cases rather than changes in underlying disease burden. This distinction highlights the importance of separating mechanisms related to diagnosis from those related to disease occurrence.

In addition, variation across states may further reflect differences in policy environments, including Medicaid expansion, tobacco taxation, and public health investments [20]. Although these institutional factors are not explicitly modeled, they likely influence both behavioral risk factors and healthcare access. Future research could incorporate explicit policy variables to further examine how policy environments are associated with variation in model-predicted diabetes prevalence.

An important limitation of the scenario analysis is that each simulation modifies a selected predictor while holding the remaining observed covariates at their recorded values. The scenarios therefore evaluate the model’s response to isolated covariate modifications rather than realistic changes in the joint distribution of behavioral, metabolic, socioeconomic, and healthcare-related factors. Because changes in factors such as BMI, physical activity, smoking, and healthcare access would likely occur together and influence one another in real-world settings, the relative magnitudes of the simulated differences should not be interpreted as rankings of feasible interventions or expected population-level policy effects.

Conclusions

Using BRFSS data from 2020–2024 and a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE), this study generated structured, model-based projections of diagnosed diabetes prevalence under alternative behavioral and healthcare access scenarios.

Within the predictive simulation framework, the lower BMI scenario produced the largest reductions in model-predicted diagnosed diabetes prevalence among the individual covariate modifications examined, whereas healthcare access scenarios primarily influenced predicted diagnosed prevalence through detection-related pathways. These findings highlight the importance of distinguishing between underlying disease risk and diagnostic processes when interpreting population-level patterns in diagnosed diabetes prevalence.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The analysis relies on self-reported BRFSS data and may therefore underestimate true diabetes prevalence due to undiagnosed cases. In addition, each simulation modified selected predictors individually while retaining all other observed characteristics at their recorded values. Consequently, the scenarios represent structured hypothetical covariate modifications rather than realistic changes in the joint distribution of behavioral, metabolic, socioeconomic, and healthcare-related factors. The modeling framework does not support causal interpretation and cannot rule out unobserved confounding.

We evaluated predictive performance using standard metrics under the observed data distribution and quantified uncertainty through a design-consistent bootstrap procedure. We also conducted an additional appendix analysis (Appendix B) to assess predictive stability under scenario-induced covariate shift using density-ratio reweighting. The results indicate that model performance remained broadly stable across scenarios, although some sensitivity was observed under the preventive checkup and BMI scenarios. These diagnostics provide useful evidence regarding model performance under modified covariate distributions while appropriately distinguishing predictive simulations from interventional effects.

Overall, the proposed framework demonstrates how conditional generative models can be used to evaluate model-predicted responses to isolated modifications of selected behavioral and healthcare access variables. Although these simulations should not be interpreted as estimates of realistic interventions or causal effects, they provide a structured approach for exploring potential variation in diagnosed diabetes prevalence under alternative covariate profiles and may help inform future public health research and planning.

Model performance comparison across policy and behavioral scenarios

Performance differences become more pronounced under scenarios such as BMI reduction, where discrimination levels decline modestly across all models. In these settings, GBM tends to exhibit marginally higher AUC values and lower Brier scores, reflecting the strong calibration properties commonly associated with ensemble tree-based methods. Similarly, logistic regression performs competitively in several states under access-related scenarios such as recent checkups and removal of cost barriers. Importantly, however, no single modeling approach uniformly dominates across all states or scenario configurations, indicating that predictive accuracy remains broadly stable across modeling frameworks despite changes in the distribution of input variables.

While the CVAE does not consistently outperform benchmark models in terms of conventional classification metrics, its predictive accuracy remains comparable. Logistic regression relies on parametric assumptions regarding linear relationships in the log-odds space, and GBM models are optimized primarily for predictive accuracy under observed data distributions. In contrast, the CVAE provides a conditional generative structure that explicitly models latent heterogeneity and captures complex, nonlinear dependencies among covariates and outcomes. This structure enables internally consistent, scenario-based simulation under modified covariate distributions, allowing the model to generate predictions that reflect realistic joint patterns observed in the data.

This distinction is particularly relevant in applications where multiple behavioral and access-related variables are jointly modified. Accordingly, the use of the CVAE framework is motivated not solely by predictive performance but by its ability to support structured, model-based projections under alternative covariate configurations.

Appendix B

Predictive stability under scenario-induced covariate shift

To assess predictive stability under scenario-induced covariate shifts, we estimated density-ratio weights between the observed test-set covariate distribution and each scenario-specific covariate distribution. A logistic regression classifier was trained to distinguish observed covariate vectors from scenario-modified covariate vectors, and predicted probabilities were used to construct importance weights. These weights were then applied to the observed test set to approximate model performance under scenario-like covariate distributions.

Using these weights, we recalculated weighted AUC and Brier scores for each scenario for the CVAE model used in the main analysis. The reweighted AUC values ranged from 0.763 to 0.799, while reweighted Brier scores ranged from 0.093 to 0.142 (Table 6 in Appendix B). Overall, these results indicate that predictive performance remained reasonably stable under the covariate shifts induced by the simulation scenarios, although some deterioration was observed under the preventive checkup and BMI scenarios. These diagnostics provide evidence on predictive stability under shifted covariate distributions, but they should not be interpreted as validation of causal or interventional effects.
