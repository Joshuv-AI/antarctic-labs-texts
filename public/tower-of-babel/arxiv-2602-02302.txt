Abstract
. The Bodirsky-Pinsker conjecture asserts a P vs. NP-complete dichotomy for the computational complexity of Constraint Satisfaction Problems (CSPs) of first-order reducts of finitely bounded homogeneous structures. Prominently, two structures in the scope of the conjecture have log-space equivalent CSPs if they are pp-bi-interpretable, or equivalently, if their polymorphism clones are topologically isomorphic. The latter gives rise to the algebraic approach which regards structures with topologically isomorphic polymorphism clones as equivalent and seeks to identify structural reasons for hardness or tractability in topological clones. We establish that the equivalence relation of pp-bi-interpretability underlying this approach is reasonable: On the one hand, we show that it is decidable under mild conditions on the templates; this improves a theorem of Bodirsky, Pinsker and Tsankov (LICS’11) on decidability of equality of polymorphism clones. On the other hand, we show that within the much larger class of transitive ω \omega -categorical structures without algebraicity, the equivalence relation is of lowest possible complexity in terms of descriptive set theory: namely, it is smooth, i.e., Borel-reduces to equality on the real numbers. On our way to showing the first result, we establish that the model-complete core of a structure that has a finitely bounded Ramsey expansion (which might include all structures of the Bodirsky-Pinsker conjecture) is computable, thereby providing a constructive alternative to previous non-constructive proofs of its existence.



1.
Introduction

For a countable relational structure
\mathbb{A}
in a finite relational language, the constraint satisfaction problem with template
\mathbb{A}
, for short
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathbb{A})
, denotes the computational problem of deciding whether or not a given finite relational structure homomorphically maps into
\mathbb{A}
. This
provides
a very flexible framework capable of modelling, up to polynomial-time Turing reductions, every computational problem one dares to dream about
[
7
]
. A typical example is the
3
-colouring problem of graphs, modelled as the CSP of the complete loopless graph on three vertices. As a further example, the computational problem of checking if a finite input graph is triangle-free can be modelled as the CSP of Henson’s (infinite) generic triangle-free graph. This problem is provably not a finite-domain CSP, i.e., there does not exist any finite graph
\mathbb{G}
such that
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathbb{G})
describes said problem. This demonstrates the necessity
to also consider
templates with an infinite domain, at least if one wishes to capture some of the most natural CSPs such as vertex-colouring problems with forbidden patterns (CSPs expressible in MMSNP
[
28
]
), edge-colouring problems with forbidden patterns (CSPs expressible in GMSNP
[
4
]
), and many more.

As of today, however, the computational complexity of finite-domain CSPs is much better understood. A deep algebraic theory using the language of universal algebra has been developed for this situation after Feder and Vardi had conjectured a P vs. NP-complete complexity dichotomy for CSPs with a finite domain, culminating in the independent confirmation of this dichotomy in 2017, by Bulatov and Zhuk
[
27
,
51
,
52
]
. In the infinite world such a complexity dichotomy is simply not possible even if one restricts to templates that are
\omega
-categorical, a model-theoretic notion rendering countable structures “close to being finite”. Formally,
\omega
-categoricity of a structure means that its automorphism group acts with finitely many orbits in the componentwise action on its
n
-tuples, for all
n\geq 1
; intuitively, this means that the structure only distinguishes finitely many
n
-tuples up to automorphisms, and a typical example is the order of the rational numbers
(\mathbb{Q},<)
. While this mathematical property makes an algebraic approach to its CSP possible as we will explain further in Section
1.0.1
, it has in itself little bearing on the computational tameness of the CSP
[
31
,
7
]
.

There is however, a class of
\omega
-categorical structures,
first-order reducts of finitely bounded homogeneous structures
, giving rise to CSPs within NP while substantially generalising the class of computational problems that can be modelled as finite domain CSPs. For example, in this class we can model problems arising in temporal and spatial reasoning
[
9
]
, as well as the aforementioned colouring problems. The meaning of this class is the following. A homogeneous structure in a finite relational language satisfies a particularly strong form of
\omega
-categoricity, where not only the number of orbits of
n
-tuples is finite, but where the orbit of each tuple is completely determined by the relations that hold between its components (for which there are only finitely many possibilities). Again, the structure
(\mathbb{Q},<)
is a good example: the three possible orbits of a pair
(a,b)
are given by
a=b
,
a<b
, and
b<a
.
Finite boundedness means that moreover, the orbits that appear in the structure are described by a finite set of “forbidden patterns”. In the example of
(\mathbb{Q},<)
, no triple
(a,b,c)
satisfies
a<a
or
a<b<c<a
; it is easy to come up with a finite set of forbidden patterns that suffice to define the possible relations that can hold on a tuple. Finally,
\mathbb{C}
being a first-order reduct of a finitely bounded homogeneous structure
\mathbb{A}
means that each relation of
\mathbb{C}
is a union of orbits of
\mathbb{A}
of the same arity. This is the same as
\mathbb{C}
being first-order definable in
\mathbb{A}
, or equivalently, since finitely bounded homogeneous structures have quantifier elimination, being definable in
\mathbb{A}
by quantifier-free formulas.

It is easy to see that if
\mathbb{C}
is a first-order reduct of a finitely bounded homogeneous structure
\mathbb{A}
, then its CSP is in NP: an input structure can be enumerated to obtain a tuple, and relations for the language of
\mathbb{A}
be randomly “guessed” on the tuple; it can then be verified in polynomial time if this assignment yields an orbit of
\mathbb{A}
and whether or not it yields a homomorphism into
\mathbb{C}
.

Conjecture 1

(Bodirsky and Pinsker
\sim
2011, see
[
19
]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{C}
be a first-order reduct of a finitely bounded homogeneous structure
\mathbb{A}
. Then
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathbb{C})
is either in P or NP-complete.

While the conjecture has been confirmed in many instances such as first-order reducts of the linear order of
(\mathbb{Q},<)

[
12
]
, a countable set with equality
[
11
]
, the universal homogeneous poset
[
37
]
, homogeneous undirected graphs
[
15
]
, in particular the random graph
[
21
]
, the universal homogeneous tournament
[
41
]
as well as MMSNP
[
13
,
14
]
, and graph orientation problems with forbidden tournaments
[
8
,
6
,
29
]
, it is still wide-open in general.

The common toolbox for finite-domain CSPs and
more generally
CSPs within the Bodirsky-Pinsker conjecture is the analysis of polymorphisms, which are certain algebraic invariants of the solution space of a CSP; we shall discuss them next.

1.0.1.
Polymorphisms and the complexity of a CSP

A polymorphism of a relational structure
\mathbb{A}
is a homomorphism from a finite power of
\mathbb{A}
into itself. The collection of these “symmetries of higher arity”, denoted by
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathbb{A})
, forms an algebraic object called a clone.
The computational complexity of the CSP of an
\omega
-categorical structure is tightly connected with its polymorphism clone.
In
[
17
]
Bodirsky and Nešetřril show that two
\omega
-categorical structures with the same polymorphism clones have polynomial-time equivalent CSPs.
Thus, if one is interested in the complexity of the CSP of such structures up to polynomial-time equivalence, two structures may be regarded as equivalent if their polymorphism clones are equal.

The underlying mathematical content of this reduction is that the
equivalence of having equal polymorphism clones admits a description using a fragment of first-order logic: primitive positive (pp) formulas. A first-order formula is said to be primitive positive if it is of the form
\exists y_{1}\cdots\exists y_{k}\phi
, where
\phi
is a conjunction of atomic formulas. Namely, in
[
17
]
it is shown that two structures
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
have the same polymorphism clones precisely when they pp-define the same sets of relations, i.e., when they are
pp-interdefinable
. This insight shaped the early days, the first regime, of infinite-domain CSP research and lead to complexity dichotomies for first-order reducts of, e.g., a countable set with equality
[
11
]
and reducts of the rational linear order
(\mathbb{Q},<)

[
12
]
. Such classifications were obtained by first fixing a finitely bounded homogeneous “ground structure” (e.g.,
(\mathbb{Q},<)
) and then considering all possible polymorphism clones of its first-order reducts; these are precisely the polymorphism clones containing
the automorphism group of the ground structure.

Moving to the meta-perspective, the equivalence of pp-interdefinability underlying this first regime was investigated from a computational viewpoint in
[
20
]
.
More precisely it is shown that it is decidable to determine whether two first-order reducts
\mathbb{C},\mathbb{D}
of a finitely bounded homogeneous structure
\mathbb{A}
are pp-interdefinable, provided that
\mathbb{A}
is a Ramsey structure. While this latter condition of combinatorial nature might seem like a strong restriction at first, this need not be the case whatsoever:
it is an open question whether every first-order reduct of a finitely bounded homogeneous structure (i.e. a structure within the scope of Conjecture
1
) is also a first-order reduct of a finitely bounded homogeneous Ramsey structure, see
[
20
]
;
[
25
]
, Conjecture 1.1. From a practical point of view, all methods available today depend on the ground structure
\mathbb{A}
to be Ramsey; see, e.g., the survey
[
47
]
.
We remark that the decidability statement indeed makes sense since finitely bounded homogeneous structures are uniquely determined by their finite set of finite bounds, and hence can be fed to an algorithm; a first-order reduct is then given to the algorithm in the form of quantifier-free formulas defining its relations.

Theorem 1

(Bodirsky, Pinsker, and Tsankov
[
20
]
)
.

The following problem is decidable:

•

Given:
A finitely bounded homogeneous Ramsey structure
\mathbb{A}
and first-order reducts
\mathbb{C},\mathbb{D}
of it.

•

Question:
Are
\mathbb{C}
,
\mathbb{D}
pp-interdefinable?

We remark that as a subset of their proof, the authors obtain the same statement for existential positive (ep-)interdefinability; on the other hand, deciding first-order (fo-)interdefinability, stated as an open problem in
[
20
]
, remains open to this day.

Vastly generalizing the mentioned result on pp-interdefinability, Bodirsky and Pinsker
[
22
]
later showed that in fact the complexity of a CSP, up to log-space reductions, is already determined by the polymorphism clone up to topological clone isomorphism. Similarly to the previous situation with equality of polymorphism clones, the mathematical content underlying this reduction admits a description using pp-formulas, this time using the concept of
pp-bi-interpretability
instead of pp-interdefinability. A pp-interpretation of a structure
\mathbb{B}
in
\mathbb{A}
is a partial surjective function
\Gamma
from a finite power of
\mathbb{A}
onto
\mathbb{B}
so that preimages of pp-definable sets remain pp-definable. It is a pp-bi-interpretation if there additionally exists a pp-interpretation
\Delta
of
\mathbb{A}
in
\mathbb{B}
that is, in some sense, inverse to
\Delta
.

This result of Bodirsky and Pinsker allows, in principle, for a more structured approach to CSPs within Conjecture
1
: rather than considering each polymorphism clone one-by-one (say, for all fo-reducts
\mathbb{A}
of a fixed finitely bounded homogeneous structure
\mathbb{B}
, as was done previously in the above-mentioned first-generation classifications), develop a theory for the possible structure of polymorphism clones, viewed as topological clones. This direction was first taken in a CSP dichotomy for problems expressible in the logic MMSNP
[
13
,
14
]
, and culminated in the technique of “smooth approximations”
[
40
,
41
]
, which was successfully used to essentially reprove all known CSP complexity dichotomies for templates within Conjecture
1
to this day. Such classifications have been called second-generation proofs in
[
40
,
41
]
.

The new philosophy means precisely that the equivalence relation on structures of being pp-bi-interpretable (rather than the finer equivalence of being pp-interdefinable) lies at the heart of modern-day infinite-domain CSP research. Therefore, showing the reasonability of this equivalence would justify in the validity of this approach.
In particular it would be reassuring to know that, just like pp-interdefinability, pp-bi-interpretability is decidable, and this is the central theme of the present work.

1.0.2.
Descriptive set theory

While the above-mentioned theorem of Bodirsky and Pinsker on the equivalence of topological isomorphism of polymorphism clones with bi-interpretability is useful for a structured approach to Conjecture
1
, it actually holds in the larger class of all
\omega
-categorical structures. A decidability result like Theorem
1
, however, does not make sense for this class, as there are uncountably many non-isomorphic
\omega
-categorical structures and we therefore cannot encode them as inputs of an algorithm. Therefore, if we wish to measure how reasonable, or how complex the equivalence of bi-interpretability on the class of
\omega
-categorical structures is, we have to resort to other, non-computational measures; here, descriptive set theory turns out to provide the an adequate language.

Namely, it provides a framework to compare the complexity of classification problems encountered throughout mathematics. Such problems can often be described by equivalence relations on Polish spaces, and
Borel-reducibility
provides a robust notion to compare their complexity
[
35
]
. It is defined as follows, see Section
2
for definitions of the underlying notions. Let
(X,E_{X})
and
(Y,E_{Y})
be Polish spaces (think
\mathbb{R}
with the Euclidean topology) equipped with equivalence relations respectively.
The relation
E_{X}
Borel-reduces to
E_{Y}
, denoted by
(X,E_{X})\leq_{B}(Y,E_{Y})
if there is a Borel map
f\colon X\to Y
that reduces
E_{X}
to
E_{Y}
, i.e., for all
x,y\in X
it holds

x\mathrel{E_{X}}y\iff f(x)\mathrel{E_{Y}}f(y).

Note the analogy to many-one Turing reductions: the function
f
is “nice” (it is Borel) and allows us to reduce the question whether
x,y
are equivalent in
E_{X}
to the question whether
f(x),f(y)
are equivalent in
E_{Y}
.
In this sense, one can say that the classification problem of
E_{X}
is at most as difficult as the one of
E_{Y}
. We write
E_{X}<_{B}E_{Y}
if
E_{X}\leq_{B}E_{Y}
and
E_{Y}\not\leq_{B}E_{X}
.
This notion of reducibility was introduced in the seminal paper
[
32
]
by Harrington, Kechris, and Louveau, and it provides a framework for comparing the intrinsic complexity of classification problems. Prominent examples of this approach are the classifications of countable torsion-free abelian groups up to isomorphism
[
44
]
, the classification of separable
C^{*}
-algebras
[
49
]
, and the
classification of ergodic measure preserving transformations
[
30
]
.

Work of Silver
[
50
]
and Harrington-Kechris-Louveau
[
32
]
shows that Borel equivalence relations on Polish spaces have the following initial segment under Borel-reducibility

1<_{B}2<_{B}\cdots<_{B}\mathbb{N}<_{B}(\mathbb{R},=).

Here each natural number
n
stands for a Borel equivalence relation with
n
-many equivalence classes,
\mathbb{N}
stands for Borel equivalence relations with countably many classes and
(\mathbb{R},=)
simply stands for equality on the real numbers. Any Borel equivalence relation is either equivalent to one of the above, and in this case is called
smooth
, or strictly lies above
\mathbb{R}
under Borel-reducibility. In this sense smooth equivalence relations are the most basic, “tractable”, equivalence relations from a Borel-reducibility point of view.

CSP templates, i.e., countable structures in a finite language, can naturally be encoded as (countable) 0-1-sequences, and hence as elements of the real numbers
\mathbb{R}
. The subclass of
\omega
-categorical structures form a natural Polish space (a subspace of
\mathbb{R}
), and thus the complexity of any equivalence relation on this class can be evaluated in terms of Borel reducibility. In particular, this is true for the equivalence pp-bi-interpretability, and smoothness of this relation would be a strong indication for its reasonability as a foundation for a structured algebraic approach to the CSPs of this class.

1.0.3.
Model-complete cores

In our quest to understand the complexity of pp-bi-interpretability, we will naturally come across a central concept of the algebraic approach to CSPs, namely that of a (model-complete) core. We shall now explain this notion.
An easy, yet important insight for finite-domain CSPs is that for any finite relational structure
\mathbb{A}
there is a unique structure
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
of smallest domain size with the same CSP, called the
core
of
\mathbb{A}
.
This can be generalized to
\omega
-categorical structures,
where the notion of minimal size is replaced to mean that all endomorphism of
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
locally resemble, i.e., restricted to finite sets are equal to, (restrictions of) automorphisms. The existence of these
model-complete cores
for
\omega
-categorical structures is, as opposed to the finite case, non-trivial and was first established in
[
24
]
; they are again
\omega
-categorical and unique up to isomorphism.

Example 2
.

The model-complete core of

•

(\mathbb{Q},\leq)
is a single point with equality;

•

(\mathbb{Q},<)
is
(\mathbb{Q},<)
;

•

the random graph is the countable infinite clique;

•

K_{\omega,\omega}
, the complete bipartite graph with two countable parts, is a single edge,
K_{2}
.

The model-complete core
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
of a structure
\mathbb{A}
has desirable model-theoretic properties, e.g., the orbits of its automorphism group on
n
-tuples are pp-definable in it. As a result, it is systematically used in the study of constraint satisfaction in infinite domains
[
26
]
. Even in second-generation complexity classifications, a standard “preprocessing” step when determining the complexity of the CSP of a template is replacing the template by its model-complete core, see e.g.,
[
41
]
. To do, combinatorial effort is made to
compute
this core. Unfortunately, neither the original existence proof from
[
24
]
, nor the later alternative proof from
[
2
]
do help with that task as they are non-constructive, relying on a weak version of the axiom of choice, the compactness theorem of first-order logic.

While not providing a computing procedure either as it relies on the compactness theorem again, the following more recent result due to Mottet and Pinsker has helped finding the model-complete core in practice.

Theorem 3

(Mottet and Pinsker
[
39
]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{C}
be a first-order reduct of a finitely bounded homogeneous Ramsey structure
\mathbb{A}
. Then its model-complete core
\mathbb{C}^{\prime}
is a first-order reduct of a finitely bounded homogeneous Ramsey substructure
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{A}
. The relations of
\mathbb{C}^{\prime}
are defined by the same quantifier free formulas in
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
as were those of
\mathbb{C}
in
\mathbb{A}
.

In this paper, we will provide an algorithm which outputs
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
and
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
in the situation of this theorem; this will be of help for our bi-interpretability questions.

1.1.
Our contributions

1.1.1.
The complexity of bi-interpretability

Our first main contribution lies in showing that the equivalence relation of pp-bi-interpretability, underlying the algebraic approach to infinite-domain CSPs since
[
22
]
, is reasonable both from the perspective of computation as well as from the perspective of descriptive set theory. We start with the former, where we show the following variant of Theorem
1
from
[
20
]
.

Theorem 4
.

The following problem is decidable:

•

Given:
Finitely bounded homogeneous Ramsey structures
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
with first-order reducts
\mathbb{C},\mathbb{D}
, respectively, whose model-complete cores
\mathbb{C}^{\prime},\mathbb{D}^{\prime}
are without algebraicity.

•

Question:
Are
\mathbb{C}^{\prime},\mathbb{D}^{\prime}
(fo/ep)-bi-interpretable?

Moreover, if
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
are transitive, then the same statement holds for pp-bi-interpretability.

Comparing this theorem with Theorem
1
, we observe the appearance of model-complete cores, no algebraicity, and transitivity. With model-complete cores being the unique “smallest” template representing an
\omega
-categorical CSP, and our ability to compute the model-complete core (see Theorem
8
), we are not disturbed by them. The assumption of no algebraicity in the model-complete core can be made without loss of generality by recent work in
[
46
]
; it is ubiquitous in the infinite-domain CSP literature (e.g., in the theory of smooth approximations
[
41
]
), and we refer to Section
2
for more details.
Finally, the transitivity in the case of pp-bi-interpretability is the only true restriction; it means that the automorphism group of the structure acts with a single orbit on its domain.
A good example is, once again, the structure
(\mathbb{Q},<)
: any element of
\mathbb{Q}
can be sent to any other element by an automorphism. Among all the complexity dichotomies only very few, e.g., the ones for unary structures
[
16
]
and MMSNP
[
13
]
, deal with non-transitive structures. Thus, this assumption still encompasses the vast majority of all work on Conjecture
1
.

Similarly to the computational perspective, we will show that the equivalence relation of bi-interpretability of
\omega
-categorical structures is reasonable from the point of view of descriptive set theory, under mild assumptions.

Theorem 5
.

The equivalence relation of ep-bi-interpretability is a smooth equivalence relation on the class of
\omega
-categorical model-complete cores without algebraicity. Moreover, pp-bi-interpretability is smooth on the subclass of structures that are additionally transitive.

1.1.2.
Between interdefinability and bi-interpretability: bi-definability

There is an intermediate equivalence relation on
\omega
-categorical structures between the one given by equality of polymorphism clones (characterized by pp-interdefinability) and the coarser one given by topological
isomorphism of polymorphism clones (characterized by pp-bi-interpretability): conjugacy of polymorphism clones. Namely, we can consider structures
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
equivalent if there is a bijection
\theta\colon\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{B}
between their domains such that the map
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto\theta\circ f(\theta^{-1}(x_{1}),\ldots,\theta^{-1}(x_{n}))
is a bijection from the polymorphism clone of
\mathbb{A}
onto the polymorphism clone of
\mathbb{B}
. In that case, it is easy to see that this map is a topological isomorphism between the two polymorphism clones, and hence
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
are pp-bi-interpretable. Structures whose polymorphism clones are conjugate in this sense are called
pp-bi-definable
. If one replaces bi-interpretability by bi-definability in Theorems
4
, and
5
, then one can drop the assumptions on no algebraicity and transitivity.
In fact, we first show the following statements, the former also being a generalization of Theorem
1
.

Theorem 6
.

The following problem is decidable:

•

Given:
Finitely bounded homogeneous Ramsey structures
\mathbb{A}
,
\mathbb{B}
and first-order reducts
\mathbb{C}
,
\mathbb{D}
in a finite language.

•

Question:
Are the model-complete cores of the structures
\mathbb{C}
,
\mathbb{D}
(fo/ep/pp)-bi-definable?

Theorem 7
.

The equivalence relation of being (fo/ep/pp)-bi-definable on
\omega
-categorical structures is smooth.

We then use model-theoretic tricks going back to Rubin
[
48
]
and Behrisch and Vargas-García
[
3
]
to lift the result from bi-definability to bi-interpretability, or in other words from conjugacy to topological isomorphism.

1.1.3.
Computing the model-complete core

Regarding model-complete cores, we give the first constructive proof of their existence for first-order reducts of finitely bounded homogeneous Ramsey structures; in fact, we show that in that situation, we can compute the model-complete core algorithmically. This is one of the ingredients of our proof of Theorem
4
.

Theorem 8
.

Given a first-order reduct
\mathbb{C}
of a finitely bounded homogenous Ramsey structure
\mathbb{A}
we can compute a finitely bounded homogeneous Ramsey structure
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
and a first-order reduct
\mathbb{C}^{\prime}
that is the model-complete core of
\mathbb{C}
.

The role of this theorem in the proof of Theorem
6
is not only the computation of the model-complete core of a given structure. In fact, given
\mathbb{C}
we can compute an “optimal” finitely bounded homogeneous Ramsey structure
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
that yields the core
\mathbb{C}^{\prime}
of
\mathbb{C}
. We call the structure
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
the
optimal presentation
of
\mathbb{C}^{\prime}
, and show that it is unique up to fo-bi-definability (Lemma
19
). We believe that this insight is of independent interest in the quest to prove Conjecture
1
.

1.2.
Discussion

We show decidability of bi-definability for model-complete cores of structures under the assumptions of Conjecture
1

while additionally assuming
no algebraicity, which is no loss of generality, and the Ramsey property, which is a standard assumption and might be no loss of generality. Although not directly relevant for the study of the conjecture, it would also be interesting from a purely mathematical point of view whether
bi-definability
can be decided for the original structures rather than their model-complete cores: as will become apparent, our proof relies crucially on going to the model-complete core first.

The same can be asked for the case of
bi-interpretability:
can we decide whether two given structures satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
4
are bi-interpretable? Moreover, can we drop the transitivity assumption in the primitive positive case?

Regarding the descriptive viewpoint, we can ask similar questions: is bi-interpretability smooth among
\omega
-categorical structures without algebraicity? Currently we only know this in the first-order case, by the breakthrough result of Nies and Paolini
[
43
]
. Note the interesting contrast of this fact with the discussion after Theorem
1
, which leaves precisely the first-order case open.

We remark that it is folklore that bi-definability as well as bi-interpretability are decidable for finite structures. The least obvious case here is that of decidability of bi-interpretability in the primitive positive case: it is a consequence of the proof of Birkhoff’s theorem
[
5
]

providing a bound on the power required in an interpretation.

1.3.
Organization of the paper

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
we introduce relevant definitions and notions. In Section
3
we show that, in the correct setting, we can compute the model-complete core of an input structure. We use this computability of the model-complete in Section
4
to show that the equivalence relation of being bi-definable is tractable. In Section
5
we extend work of Rubin to derive the bi-interpretability results from the bi-definability results obtained in the previous section. In the course of this we rely on a positive answer to a question of Bodirsky and Junker which is established in Section

.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
The topology of pointwise convergence

Let
\Omega,\Delta
be countable sets. The set of functions
\Omega\to\Delta
will be denoted by
\Delta^{\Omega}
; the
topology of pointwise convergence
on
\Delta^{\Omega}
is the product topology on
\Delta^{\Omega}
if we equip
\Delta
with the discrete topology. It can be characterized as follows:
f\in\Delta^{\Omega}
lies in the closure of
F\subseteq\Delta^{\Omega}
if and only if, for every finite set
S\subseteq\Omega
there is some
f^{\prime}\in F
such that
f
and
f^{\prime}
agree when restricted to
S
. The induced subspace topology on subsets of
\Delta^{\Omega}
is referred to as the topology of pointwise convergence as well. Spaces of functions are tacitly assumed to be equipped with the topology of pointwise convergence.

2.2.
Actions of groups, monoids, and clones

Let
\Omega
be a countable set. A
permutation group on
\Omega
is a subgroup
G
of the group of all permutations of
\Omega
. We also say that
G
acts on
\Omega
and write
G\curvearrowright\Omega
in this situation. Given a finite tuple
y\in\Omega^{<\omega}
, the
stabiliser of
y
,
G_{y}
, is the subgroup of
G
consisting of all permutations that fix
y
pointwise.
More generally, for a set
X\subseteq\Omega
,
G_{X}
denotes the subgroup of
G
consisting of all permutations that fix
X
pointwise.
An element
x\in\Omega
is
algebraic over
y
if the orbit of
x
under
G_{y}\curvearrowright\Omega
is finite. The action
G\curvearrowright\Omega
is
without algebraicity
if this only happens if
x
is contained in
y
.

A
transformation monoid on
\Omega
is a submonoid
M
of
\Omega^{\Omega}
. We also say that
M
acts on
\Omega
and write
M\curvearrowright\Omega
in this situation. A
function clone on
\Omega
is a subset
C
consisting of finitary operations on
\Omega
, i.e., functions of the form
\Omega^{n}\to\Omega
for
n\geq 1
, that contains all projection maps, and is closed under composition in the sense that for all
f,g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\in C
with
f\colon\Omega^{k}\to\Omega
(i.e.,
f
is has
arity
k
) it holds

f\circ(g_{1},\dots,g_{k})\in C.

We also say that
C
acts on
\Omega
and write
C\curvearrowright\Omega
in this situation. If
C,D
are function clones on
\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}
a bijection
\phi\colon C\to D
is a
clone isomorphism
if it preserves arities and compositions, and for all
n\geq i\geq 1
sends the
i
th projection of arity
n
to the
i
th projection of arity
n
.

Let
(G/M/C)\curvearrowright\Omega
be a permutation group / transformation monoid / function clone. A set
U\subseteq\Omega^{n}
, for
n\geq 1
, is said to be

•

G
-invariant
, if for all
g\in G
and
x\in U
it holds
gx\in U
, evaluated coordinate-wise;

•

M
-invariant
, if for all
e\in M
and
x\in U
it holds
ex\in U
, evaluated coordinate-wise;

•

C
-invariant
, if for all
f\in C
, and all
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in U
, where
k
is the arity of
f
, it holds
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in U
, evaluated coordinate-wise.

Observe that if
U\subseteq\Omega^{n}
, for
n\geq 1
, is a
(G/M/C)
-invariant subset and
\sim
is a
(G/M/C)
-invariant equivalence relation on
U
, then
G/M/C
naturally induces a permutation group / transformation monoid / function clone on the set
U/_{\sim}
of equivalence classes; we write
(G/M/C)\curvearrowright U/_{\sim}
for the induced permutation group / transformation monoid / function clone on the quotient
U/_{\sim}
.

Permutation groups and transformation monoids are naturally equipped with the topology of pointwise convergence. The topology of pointwise convergence on a function clone
C\curvearrowright\Omega
refers to the sum topology of the spaces
C\cap\Omega^{\Omega^{n}}
,
n\geq 1
. These topologies turn them into topological groups / monoids / clones, in the sense that composition is continuous, and, for groups, also the inversion map is.
We say that a permutation group on
\Omega
is
topologically closed
if it is closed in the space of all permutations of
\Omega
, a transformation monoid on
\Omega
is
topologically closed
if it is closed in the space of unary operations of
\Omega
, and a function clone on
\Omega
is
topologically closed
if it is closed in the space of finitary operations on
\Omega
.

Definition 9
.

An
isomorphism of permutation group actions

G\curvearrowright\Omega
and
H\curvearrowright\Omega^{\prime}
is a bijection
\theta\colon\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
with the property that the assignment
\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{\theta}:=\theta\circ\alpha\circ\theta^{-1}
is an isomorphism from
G
onto
H
. We write
G^{\theta}
for the conjugate action (by
\theta
) of
G
; then this says that
G^{\theta}=H
. Similarly, isomorphisms between actions of closed transformation monoids and closed function clones are defined.

We point out that this notion of isomorphism between permutation groups
G\curvearrowright\Omega
and
H\curvearrowright\Omega^{\prime}
implies that
G,H
are
isomorphic as topological groups
, since the map
\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{\theta}
is a group isomorphism
G\to H
that is a homeomorphism of the underlying topological spaces; the analogous statements hold for transformation monoids and function clones.

2.3.
Structures and symmetries

We assume structures to be countable and in a relational signature, and denote them by blackboard bold letters
\mathbb{A}
. If
\mathbb{A}
is some structure and
R
is some relational symbol of arity
n
in the signature of
\mathbb{A}
, we denote the interpretation of
R
in
\mathbb{A}
, some subset of its
n
-th power, by
R^{\mathbb{A}}
. Abusing notation, we usually do not distinguish between the symbol
R
and the interpretation
R^{\mathbb{A}}
when the structure is clear from the context.
If not specified otherwise the domain of a structure is assumed to
\Omega
, a countable set.

Let
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
be structures in the same signature with domains
\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}
. A
homomorphism
form
\mathbb{A}
to
\mathbb{B}
is a function
f\colon\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
that
preserves
all relations, i.e., for every relational symbol
R
it holds
f(R^{\mathbb{A}})\subseteq R^{\mathbb{B}}
, or by abuse of notation
f(R)\subseteq R
. An
isomorphism

\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{B}
is an invertible homomorphism whose inverse is a homomorphism
\mathbb{B}\to\mathbb{A}
.
An
endomorphism
of a
\mathbb{A}
is a homomorphism
\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{A}
. An
automorphism
of
\mathbb{A}
is an isomorphism
\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{A}
. A
polymorphism (of arity
n\geq 1
)
is a map
f\colon\Omega^{n}\to\Omega
, that
preserves all relations of
\mathbb{A}
in the sense that for all
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in R^{\mathbb{A}}
it holds
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in R^{\mathbb{A}}
.

We will consider various sets of symmetries of a structure
\mathbb{A}
:

•

by
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})
we denote the group of automorphisms of
\mathbb{A}
, a permutation group on
\Omega
;

•

by
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
we denote the monoid of endomorphisms of
\mathbb{A}
, a transformation monoid on
\Omega
;

•

by
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathbb{A})
we denote the set of polymorphisms of
\mathbb{A}
, a function clone on
\Omega
.

It is not hard to see that
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})
/
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
/
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathbb{A})
are topologically closed, and that also the converse is true: every topologically closed permutation group / transformation monoid / function clone on
\Omega
is the automorphism group/ endomorphism monoid / polymorphisms clone of some structure on
\Omega
.

A structure
\mathbb{A}
is a
model-complete core
if
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})
is dense in
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
, i.e., for every finite subset
S
of the domain of
\mathbb{A}
and every endomorphism
e
, the restriction of
e
to
S
extends to an automorphism of
\mathbb{A}
.

2.4.
Model theory

A permutation group
G\curvearrowright\Omega
is
oligomorphic
if for all
n\geq 1
the induced permutation group
G\curvearrowright\Omega^{n}
has finitely many orbits. A structure
\mathbb{A}
is
\omega
-categorical
if
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})\curvearrowright\Omega
is oligomorphic.

A structure
\mathbb{A}
is
homogeneous
if every isomorphism between finite (induced) substructures of
\mathbb{A}
extends to an automorphism of
\mathbb{A}
. The
age
of
\mathbb{A}
is the class of its finite induced substructures up to isomorphism. The structure
\mathbb{A}
is called
finitely bounded
if there is a finite set
F
of finite structures such that for any finite structure
\mathbb{X}
we have:
\mathbb{X}
belongs to the age of
\mathbb{A}
if and only if it contains no member of
F
as an induced substructure. The structure
\mathbb{A}
has
no algebraicity
if the action of its automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})\curvearrowright\Omega
is without algebraicity.

A first-order (fo) formula is said to be
existential positive (ep)
if it is of the form
\exists x_{1}\cdots\exists x_{n}\phi
, where
\phi
is quantifier free and without negations; it is said to be
primitive positive (pp)
if it is of the form
\exists x_{1}\cdots\exists x_{n}\phi
, where
\phi
is a conjunction of atomic formulas. An
n
-ary relation
R
on
\Omega
(this simply means that
R\subseteq\Omega^{n}
) is
(fo/ep/pp)-definable
in
\mathbb{A}
if there is some (fo/ep/pp)-formula
\phi
in the signature of
\mathbb{A}
such that
a\in R
if and only if
\phi(a)
.

In the following theorem, the first-order statement is a well-known consequence of the Theorem of Engeler, Ryll-Nardzewski and Svenonius (see, e.g.,
[
33
]
), the existential positive statement is folklore, and the primitive positive statement is due to
[
17
]
.

Theorem 10
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be an
\omega
-categorical structure and let
R
be a relation on
\Omega
.

R
is (fo/ep/pp)-definable in
\mathbb{A}
if and only if it is preserved by all automorphisms / endomorphisms / polymorphisms of
\mathbb{A}
.

Two structures
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
are said to be
(fo/ep/pp)-interdefinable
if they (fo/ep/pp)-define the same sets of relations; for
\omega
-categorical structures, the above theorem immediately implies that this is equivalent to them having the same automorphism groups / endomorphism monoids / polymorphism clones. The first step of abstraction from this notion is the following.

Definition 11
.

Let
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
be structures with domains
\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}
. A bijection
\theta\colon\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
is a
(fo/ep/pp)-bi-definition
of
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
if
\theta
and
\theta^{-1}
map (fo/ep/pp)-definable sets onto (fo/ep/pp)-definable sets.

Lemma 12
.

Two
\omega
-categorical structures
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
are (fo/ep/pp)-bi-definable if and only if their automorphism groups / endomorphism monoids / function have isomorphic actions.

Proof.

We show this for ep-bi-definability and endomorphism monoids. Let
\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}
be the domains of
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
.

Since
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
are
\omega
-categorical, ep-definable relations of
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
are precisely the relations that are invariant under the actions of their respective endomorphism monoids. Thus, an isomorphism
\theta
of the actions
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})\curvearrowright\Omega
and
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{B})\curvearrowright\Omega^{\prime}
is an ep-bi-definition of
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
.

Conversely, let
\theta
be an ep-bi-definition of
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
. By the symmetry of the situation it suffices to show that for all
e\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{B})
, it holds
\theta e\theta^{-1}\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{B})
, i.e., that it preserves all relations of
\mathbb{B}
. This is the case because every relation of
\mathbb{B}
is of the form
\theta(X)
, where
X
is an ep-definable in
\mathbb{A}
, and
e
preserves ep-definable relations.
∎

Abstracting even further, one arrives at the notion of bi-interpretations.
Let
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
be structures with domains
\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}
. A
(fo/ep/pp)-interpretation of
\mathbb{B}
in
\mathbb{A}
is a partial surjective map
\Omega^{n}\to\Omega^{\prime}
such that preimages of relations definable by atomic formulas over
\mathbb{B}
are (fo/ep/pp)-definable in
\mathbb{A}
. The interpretation
\Gamma
is said to be a

(fo/ep/pp)-bi-interpretation
if there is a (fo/ep/pp)-interpretation
\Delta
of
\mathbb{A}
in
\mathbb{B}
such that the graphs of the naturally defined compositions
\Delta\circ\Gamma
and
\Gamma\circ\Delta
are (fo/ep/pp)-definable in
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
.

It is not hard to see that (fo/ep/pp)-bi-interpretations of
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
induce topological isomorphisms between the automorphism groups / endomorphism monoids / polymorphism clones of
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
(see, e.g.,
[
22
]
). But also the converse is true, unconditionally even in two of the three cases. This is due to Coquand, see
[
1
]
in the fo-setting, due to Bodirsky and Junker
[
10
]
in the ep-setting, and due to Bodirsky and Pinsker
[
22
]
in the pp-setting.

Theorem 13
.

Let
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
be
\omega
-categorical structures.

•

If
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
have topologically isomorphic automorphism groups / polymorphism clones, then they are (fo/pp)-bi-definable;

•

If
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
do not contain constant endomorphisms, the analogous implication holds for endomorphism monoids and ep-bi-definability.

2.5.
Ramsey theory

Let
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
be structures with domains
\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}
, and let
f\colon\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
be any function. Then
f
is called
(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
-canonical
if the image of any
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})
-orbit of a tuple in
\mathbb{A}
is contained in an
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{B})
-orbit. This implies that
f
induces a mapping from
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})
-orbits to
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{B})
-orbits; this mapping is called the
behaviour
of
f
. If
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
are homogeneous and in a finite signature, and
k
is a common upper bound for the arities of their relations, then this behaviour is completely determined its restriction to orbits of
k
-tuples; hence all potential behaviours can be enumerated in a finite list. If the structures are moreover finitely bounded, then one can even determine the behaviours that are actually possible.

Theorem 14

(Bodirsky, Pinsker, and Tsankov
[
20
]
)
.

There is an algorithm which, given two finitely bounded homogeneous structures
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
, outputs all possible behaviours of canonical functions from
\mathbb{A}
to
\mathbb{B}
(the number of such behaviours is finite).

The importance of canonical functions lies in this fact that they can be computed between finitely bounded homogeneous structures; for
Ramsey structures
, they are even common in a certain sense. We will not require the definition of a Ramsey structure, for which we refer to, e.g.,
[
34
]
, but only the following consequence of the Ramsey property from
[
20
]
, see also
[
23
]
.

Theorem 15

(Bodirsky, Pinsker, and Tsankov
[
20
]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a Ramsey structure, let
\mathbb{B}
be
\omega
-categorical, and let
\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}
be their domains. Let
f\colon\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
be any function. Then the closure of the set

\{\beta\circ f\circ\alpha\,\mid\,\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A}),\beta\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{B})\}

in the space of all functions from
\Omega
to
\Omega^{\prime}
(see Section
2.1
) contains an
(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
-canonical function.

2.6.
Descriptive set theory

The following material can be found in any textbook on descriptive set theory, e.g.,
[
36
]
.

A Polish space is a topological space that is
separable
(it contains a countable dense subset), and
completely metrisable
(its topology is induced by a complete metric).
Recall that a complete metric is one where every Cauchy-sequence converges. One example of a Polish space is the space
\mathbb{R}
of the real numbers with the topology induced by the Euclidean metric. Another example is the space of all countably infinite 0-1-sequences: equipped with the topology of pointwise convergence from Section
2.1
, it is a Polish space, which is not isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}
as it is compact. However, from the point of view of their Borel structure, the two spaces are isomorphic: we shall introduce this structure next.

Formally, a
Borel space
is a pair
(X,\mathcal{B})
where
X
is a topological space and
\mathcal{B}
is the smallest
\sigma
-algebra
, i.e., the smallest family of subsets of
X
that is closed under complements and countable intersections, that contains all open subsets of
X
. Sets contained in
\mathcal{B}
are referred to as
Borel sets
. For example, open sets are Borel by definition, closed sets are, and so are countable unions of closed sets. As the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}
is completely determined by the topological space
X
we simply refer to
X
as a Borel space, without explicitly mentioning
\mathcal{B}
; however, in this context we will be interested in
Borel maps
rather than, say, continuous ones.
A map between two Borel spaces
f\colon X\to Y
is
Borel
if all preimages of Borel sets are Borel sets. The map
f
is an
isomorphism
of Borel spaces if it is invertible and its inverse is Borel again. Two Borel spaces are
isomorphic
if there is an isomorphism from one onto the other.

A
standard Borel space
is a Borel space whose underlying topological space is a Polish space. Kuratowski’s theorem states that the only standard Borel spaces up to isomorphism are

•

finite discrete spaces;

•

the countable discrete space;

•

the real numbers
\mathbb{R}
.

This implies that, viewed as Borel spaces,
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are isomorphic Borel spaces for all
n\geq 1
. This contrasts the perspective of topology, where
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
are isomorphic as topological spaces, i.e., homeomorphic, if and only if
n=m
.

This illustrates nicely why Borel isomorphism, in contrast to topological isomorphism (also called homeomorphism), is the right generalisation of computable isomorphism when moving from countable to uncountable. In computability theory, the set
\mathbb{N}
of natural numbers is the central object and the relevant morphisms are computable maps. These make all finite powers of
\mathbb{N}
isomorphic from the computability perspective: there is a computable bijection from
{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
onto
\mathbb{N}
. For Borel-reducibility,
\mathbb{R}
is the central object, and Borel-isomorphism makes all finite powers of it isomorphic.

The class of all countable
\omega
-categorical structures can be encoded, for example, as 0-1-sequences, and forms an uncountable standard Borel space, see
[
36
, II.16.C]
, and also
[
42
]
. By the above, this space is Borel-isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}
. Therefore, equivalence relations on this class can be compared with respect to Borel reductions, already formally defined in Section
1.0.2
. Since, as the notion of tractability for classification problems on all
\omega
-categorical structures, it is central to this work, we repeat the notion of smoothness:

Definition 16

(Smoothness)
.

An equivalence relation
E
on a Borel space
X
is
smooth
if it Borel-reduces to equality on the space
\mathbb{R}
.

3.
On the model-complete core

3.1.
Characterization bi-definitions

In this section all structures have a fixed countable set
\Omega
as their domain unless explicitly defined otherwise.
We will show that if a structure is a first-order reduct of a finitely bounded homogeneous Ramsey structure, then we can compute its model-complete core along with a finitely bounded homogeneous Ramsey expansion of this model-complete core.

Lemma 17
.

Let
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
be
\omega
-categorical homogeneous structures and let
\mathbb{C},\mathbb{D}
be reducts in the same signature
\tau
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
are fo-bi-definable by an isomorphism
\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{D}
;

(2)

There exist injective functions
f,g\colon\Omega\to\Omega
such that

(i)

f
is
(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
-canonical;

(ii)

g
is
(\mathbb{B},\mathbb{A})
-canonical;

(iii)

g\circ f
preserves all orbits of
\mathbb{A}
;

(iv)

f\circ g
preserves all orbits of
\mathbb{B}
.

Moreover, for every relation
R\in\tau
we require that

(v)

f
takes
\mathbb{A}
-orbits in
R^{\mathbb{C}}
to
\mathbb{B}
-orbits in
R^{\mathbb{D}}
;

(vi)

g
takes
\mathbb{B}
-orbits in
R^{\mathbb{D}}
to
\mathbb{A}
-orbits in
R^{\mathbb{C}}
.

Proof.

(2) implies (1): Set
\Omega^{\prime}:=f(\Omega)
.
Let
\mathbb{B}^{\prime},\mathbb{D}^{\prime}
be the restrictions of
\mathbb{B},\mathbb{D}
to
\Omega^{\prime}
. Since
f\circ g

preserves the orbits of
\mathbb{B}
by (iv), it is an embedding of
\mathbb{B}
into
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
, and in particular the age of
\mathbb{B}
is contained in that of
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
. The converse inclusion being trivial, we conclude that
\mathbb{B}
and
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
have the same age.

We now prove that
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
is homogeneous. To see this, note that
G:=\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})
acts on
\Omega^{\prime}
by its conjugate action
G^{f}
. Observe that this action preserves
\mathbb{B}
-orbits: by (i) and (ii)
f
and
g
induce functions between
\mathbb{A}
-orbits and
\mathbb{B}
-orbits, respectively, and (iii) and (iv) imply that these functions are bijections. Since any
\alpha\in G
does not move
\mathbb{A}
-orbits, we see that
\alpha^{f}(a)
belongs to the
\mathbb{B}
-orbit of
a
for any tuple
a
in
\Omega^{\prime}
. Thus, the action
G^{f}\curvearrowright\Omega^{\prime}
is an action by automorphisms of
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
, i.e.,
G^{f}\leq\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{B}^{\prime})
.
We next observe that this action is transitive on every
\mathbb{B}
-orbit (restricted to
\Omega^{\prime}
). Indeed, for arbitrary tuples
a,b
of
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
in the same
\mathbb{B}
-orbit, we have that
f^{-1}(a)
and
f^{-1}(b)
belong to the same
\mathbb{A}
-orbit since
f
is a bijection between
\mathbb{A}
-orbits and
\mathbb{B}
-orbits. Thus, there is
\alpha\in G
sending
f^{-1}(a)
to
f^{-1}(b)
; then
\alpha^{f}(a)=b
. Putting this together, if two tuples
a,b
in
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
are isomorphic, then they belong to the same
\mathbb{B}
-orbit, and hence there exists
\alpha^{f}\in G^{f}\leq\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{B}^{\prime})
with
\alpha^{f}(a)=b
. This shows homogeneity of
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
, and actually also
G^{f}=\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{B}^{\prime})
since
G^{f}
is closed and dense in
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{B}^{\prime})
.

In particular, this shows, using Lemma
12
, that
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
are fo-bi-definable via
f
. Moreover, item (v) and item (vi) show that
f
is an isomorphism
\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{D}^{\prime}
.
By Fraïssé’s theorem (see, e.g.
[
33
]
),
\mathbb{B}^{\prime},\mathbb{B}
are isomorphic and any such isomorphism is an isomorphism
\mathbb{D}^{\prime}\to\mathbb{D}
. Therefore,
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
are fo-bi-definable by an isomorphism
\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{D}
.

Definition 20

and the model-complete core of

-canonical endomorphisms

\mathbb{A}
-orbits. Observe that this is possible.
Namely, by Theorem

(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{A})
-canonical functions, and for each behaviour we can then check if it preserves the relations of
\mathbb{C}

\mathbb{A}
-orbits, and if it is range-rigid and hits a minimal set of
\mathbb{A}

4.
On bi-definability

Again, we assume all structures to have a fixed countable set
\Omega
as their domain unless explicitly defined otherwise.

We will show that the relation of being bi-definable is simple in two interpretations of the word. First, we will show that it is simple in the computational sense, i.e., that it is decidable if one restricts to a subclass of structures where asking such question makes sense, namely when the input structures can be presented in a finite way. Second, we will show that it is simple in the descriptive set theory sense, namely that it is smooth, i.e., as simple as possible among equivalence relations on Borel spaces.

4.2.

This section contains an elementary proof of the fact that for
\mathcal{S}\in\{\operatorname{Aut},\operatorname{End},\operatorname{Pol}\}

Definition 23
.

Let

.

Let

.

First, let

To show that it is an isomorphism of structures

Conversely let

G:=\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{eq}})
.

(i) For the forward direction, note that the set of all elements of
\Omega
that entangle
b/_{\sim}
is finite, invariant under
G_{b/_{\sim}}
, and contains
a
. For the backward implication note that, if
a
is algebraic over
b/_{\sim}
, then
a
is algebraic over every
c\in b/_{\sim}
, so
a
has to be contained in
c
since
\mathbb{A}
has no algebraicity.

(ii) By the density of
B
there exist
b_{1}/_{\sim_{1}},\ldots,b_{n}/_{\sim_{n}}\in B
such that
G_{a}\supseteq G_{b_{1}/_{\sim_{1}},\ldots,b_{n}/_{\sim_{n}}}
.
Note that if we pick any tuples
c_{i}
in
b_{i}/_{\sim_{i}}
, then
G_{a}\supseteq G_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}}
, in particular
a
is algebraic over the entries of
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}
, which is a finite subset of
\Omega
. Thus, if we could pick all
c_{i}
so that they do not contain
a
, we would arrive at a contradiction to
\mathbb{A}
not having algebraicity. Hence, there is
i
such that
a
entangles
b_{i}/_{\sim_{i}}
.

(iii) Pick
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\in b/_{\sim}
. If none of the
a_{i}
strongly entangled
b/_{\sim}
, then for all
i
there would be
b_{i}/_{\sim_{i}}
distinct from
b/_{\sim}
that is entangled by
a_{i}
. But then
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
, and in particular
b/_{\sim}
would be algebraic over
b_{1}/_{\sim_{1}},\dots,b_{k}/_{\sim_{k}}
by (i), contradicting that
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})\curvearrowright B
is without algebraicity.

(iv) If
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})
fixes
a^{\prime}
, then
\alpha(b/_{\sim})=\alpha(b)/_{\sim}
is entangled by
a^{\prime}
. Since
a^{\prime}
strongly entangles
b/_{\sim}
, the automorphism
\alpha
fixes
b/_{\sim}
. By item (i),
a
is algebraic over
b/_{\sim}
, and so the previous argument shows that it is also algebraic over
a^{\prime}
. Since
\mathbb{A}
is without algebraicity we obtain
a=a^{\prime}
.

(v) The element
a
entangles some
b/_{\sim}
by (ii); by (iii), we know that
b/_{\sim}
is strongly entangled by some
a^{\prime}
. By (iv) it follows that
a=a^{\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 31
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
and
B\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{eq}}
as in Lemma
30
. Then
entanglement is a bijection
\theta\colon\Omega\to B
preserved by
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})
. Hence, the actions of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})
on
\Omega
and
B
are isomorphic via
\theta
.

Proof.

The notions of entanglement and strong entanglement agree by Lemma
30
: if
a\in\Omega
entangles
b/_{\sim}\in B
, then there is
a^{\prime}\in\Omega
that strongly entangles
b/_{\sim}
, so
a=a^{\prime}
. Thus entanglement defines a mapping
\Omega\to B
that is easily seen to be a bijection using Lemma
30
. It is easy to see that it is preserved by
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})
.
∎

Corollary 32
.

Let
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
be
\omega
-categorical and without algebraicity. Then any topological isomorphism between
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})
and
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{B})
is an isomorphism of permutation group actions.

Proof.

Let
\phi\colon\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{B})
be a topological group isomorphism. It is induced by a fo-bi-interpretation
\Gamma
of
\mathbb{B}
in
\mathbb{A}
(Coquand; see
[
1
]
). Let
U
be the domain and
\sim
be the kernel of
\Gamma
, and let
\tilde{\Gamma}\colon U/_{\sim}\to\Omega^{\prime}
be induced bijection, where
\Omega^{\prime}
denotes the domain of
\mathbb{B}
. Saying that
\phi
is induced by
\Gamma
simply means that it admits the following factorisation

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})\curvearrowright U/_{\sim}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{B})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi_{2}}

where
\psi_{1}
is the canonical homomorphism, sending
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})
to its action on
U/_{\sim}
, and
\psi_{2}
is the homomorphism of permutation groups induced by
\tilde{\Gamma}
.

The set
U/_{\sim}
is clearly
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})
-invariant, and it is moreover dense in
\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{eq}}
by Lemma
28
. Moreover, as
\psi_{2}
is an isomorphism of permutation group actions (its surjectivity follows from the surjectivity of
\phi
), the action
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})\curvearrowright U/_{\sim}
is without algebraicity. An application of Lemma
31
shows that
\psi_{1}
is an isomorphism of permutation group actions. Thus
\phi
is an isomorphism of permutation group actions as well.
∎

Corollary 33

(Nies and Paolini
[
43
]
)
.

Fo-bi-interpretability between
\omega
-categorical structures without algebraicity is smooth.

Proof.

Two
\omega
-categorical structures
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
without algebraicity are bi-interpretable if and only if their automorphism groups are topologically isomorphic (Coquand; see
[
1
]
); this is the case if and only if the automorphism groups have isomorphic actions, by Corollary

; this is the case if and only if
\mathbb{A}
and

Extending to the endomorphism monoid

In the following we show an analogue of Corollary
32
for endomorphism monoids. We let
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
be
\omega
-categorical structures and let
\Gamma
be a ep-bi-interpretation of
\mathbb{B}
in
\mathbb{A}
. Again,
U
denotes the domain, and
\sim
denotes the kernel of
\Gamma
. Recall that

\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})

Lemma 34
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be an
\omega
-categorical structure without algebraicity and let
U/_{\sim}\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{eq}}
be a dense set, such that
U
and
\sim
are
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
-invariant and moreover
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})\curvearrowright U/_{\sim}
is without algebraicity. Then the entanglement relation between
\Omega
and
U/_{\sim}
is preserved by all finite-to-one endomorphisms.

Proof.

By applying Lemma
31
we obtain that entanglement is a bijection
\Omega\to U/_{\sim}
that is moreover preserved by
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})
.

Let
e\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
, and assume that
a\in\Omega
entangles
u/_{\sim}\in U/_{\sim}
; we want to show that
e(a)
entangles
e(u)/_{\sim}
.
Suppose not, then some other element

entangles
e(u)/_{\sim}
.
Let
X
be the finite pre-image of

under

. As
\mathbb{A}
has no algebraicity there exists
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})
that fixes

such that no element of

appears in
\alpha(u)
. Since

also entangles
\alpha(u)_{\sim}
, and entanglement is a bijection we have
\alpha(u)\in u/_{\sim}
. But now
e(\alpha(u))\in e(u)/_{\sim}
does not contain
y
, a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 35
.

Let
\mathbb{A},U,{\sim}

The proof of the following result is analogous to the proof of Corollary

.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be an
\omega
-categorical structure without algebraicity such that its finite-to-one endomorphisms are dense in

\mathbb{B}

6.
A question of Bodirsky and Junker

Theorem 38

(
[
10
]
)
.

Two
\omega
-categorical structures without constant endomorphisms are ep-bi-interpretable if and only if their endomorphism monoids are topologically isomorphic.

While the authors of the above theorem show that any ep-bi-interpretation gives rise to a topological isomorphism of endomorphism monoids they point to some restriction regarding converse implication. Namely, some restriction regarding constant endomorphisms is necessary as any structure interpretable in a structure having a constant endomorphism will have a constant endomorphism itself. Further, one easily constructs two
\omega
-categorical structures with topologically isomorphic endomorphism monoids such that only one of them has a constant endomorphism. It it left open, however, if such a bi-interpretability result holds if both structures have a constant endomorphism.
We observe a positive answer.

Proposition 39
.

Let
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
be
\omega
-categorical structures such that either both or neither have a constant endomorphism. Then
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
are existential positive bi-interpretable if and only if
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
, and
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{B})
are topologically isomorphic.

Proof.

In
[
10
]
it is shown that ep-bi-interpretations of
\omega
-categorical structures induce isomorphisms on the level of endomorphism monoids.

Conversely, assume that
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
, and
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{B})
are topologically isomorphic and that
\mathbb{A}
has a constant endomorphism precisely if
\mathbb{B}
has one. By
[
38
, Lemma 2.9]

\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
are existential positive bi-interpretable if and only if the topologically closed clones generated by
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
and
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{B})
are topologically isomorphic. To see that this is the case let
\phi\colon\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})\to\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{B})
be a topological isomorphism. It extends to a topological isomorphism of the generate clones if
\phi
and
\phi^{-1}
preserve constant functions, see
[
18
, Proposition 5]
. In case neither
\mathbb{A}
nor
\mathbb{B}
have constant endomorphisms this condition is vacuously true. But
\phi
also provides a correspondence between the constant functions in case both,
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
have constant endomorphisms: in this case
e\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
is constant if and only if for all
f\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
it holds
e=e\circ f
; clearly this true for constant endomorphisms and conversely, if
e
has said property, then we may take a constant endomorphism
c\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
to see that
e=e\circ c
is constant. The same being true for
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{B})
we conclude that
\phi
extends to a topological isomorphism between the generated clones.
∎

We now further observe that in the case of no algebraicity, we can additionally drop the assumption on constant endomorphisms.

Proposition 40
.

Let
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
be
\omega
-categorical and without algebraicity. Then any topological isomorphism between
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A}),\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{B})
maps constants to constants. Consequently,
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
and
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{B})
are topologically isomorphic if and only if
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
are ep-bi-interpretable.

Proof.

The restriction of any topological isomorphism
\phi
between
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
and
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{B})
to the sets of invertibles
G:=\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{A})
and
H:=\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{B})
is an isomorphism of permutation group actions by Corollary
32
. For
e
in
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
, set
L(e):=\{\alpha\in G\,\mid\,\alpha\circ e=e\}
. We have
\phi(L(e))=L(\phi(e))
. Observe that
L(e)=G_{e(\Omega)}
. If
e
is constant, then the stabiliser
G_{e(\Omega)}
is not the intersection of two strictly larger stabilisers; if
e
is not constant, then it is, by no algebraicity. But
\phi
restricts to an isomorphism of permutation group actions
G\to H
, so it has to preserve this property. Hence it must send constants to constants.

The second statement now follows from Proposition
39
.
∎
