A Kermack–McKendrick type epidemic model with double threshold phenomenon (and a possible application to Covid-19)
Joan Ponce, Horst R. Thieme

Abstract
The suggestion by K.L. Cooke (1967) that infected individuals become infective if they are exposed often enough for a natural disease resistance to be overcome is built into a Kermack-McKendrick type epidemic model with infectivity age. Both the case that the resistance may be the same for all hosts and the case that it is distributed among the host population are considered. In addition to the familiar threshold behavior of the final size of the epidemic with respect to a basic reproductive number, an Allee effect is generated with respect to the final cumulative force of infection exerted by the initial infectives. This offers a deterministic explanation why geographic areas that appear to be epidemiologically similar have epidemic outbreaks of quite different severity.



Keywords:
force of infection, state-dependent
delay, host heterogeneity, density-dependent
incidence, minimal solutions

Infective influence

(2.2)

Here

The cumulative force of infection is given by

By (

Let

By (

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{t}u(r,a)dr=&\int_{a}^{t}u(r,a)dr+\int_{0}^{a}u(r,a)dr\\

(2.8)

We substitute (

J(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(C(t-a)P(a)+\int_{0}^{t}u_{0}(a-r)\frac{P(a)}{P(a-r)}dr\Big)\kappa(a)da.

Since

2.3
A dose-dependent delay for becoming infective

Following

Recall (

J(t)=\int_{0}^{t}C(t-a)P(a)\kappa(a)da+J_{0}(t)

Notice that

4
The model for distributed resistance

It is very unlikely that all susceptibles have the same
resistance to the disease though we assume
that they all have some resistance. So we assume that, at time
t\geq 0
,
the number of susceptibles is given by
\int_{0}^{\infty}S(t,m)dm
,
where, as a function of
m
,
S(t,m)
is the density
of susceptible hosts with resistance
m\geq 0
.
We still assume that all susceptible hosts have the same chance
of being exposed to the infection.
For models with distributed susceptibility
or distributed other traits,
see
[
7
,
8
]
and the references therein.
We also make
the simplifying assumption that
the level of resistance does not affect the disease mortality
of an infective host or its infectivity.
See the discussion.

At time
t\geq 0
, let
u_{1}(t,a)
be the density of primary infective
hosts with infectivity age
a\geq t
,

u_{1}(t,a)=u_{0}(a-t)\frac{P(a-t)}{P(a)},\qquad a\geq t\geq 0,

(4.1)

where
u_{0}(a)
is the density of initially infective hosts
with infectivity age
a

and
u_{0}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is a given finitely integrable function.

\int_{t}^{\infty}u_{1}(t,a)da

is the number of primary infective hosts at time
t
.
Notice that all primary infective hosts at time
t
have
an infectivity age
a\geq t
.

At time
t\geq 0
, let
u_{2}(t,a,m)
be the density of secondary infective hosts with
infectivity age
a\in[0,t]
and resistance
m
, such that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}u_{2}(t,a,m)\;da\,dm,\quad t\geq 0,

(4.2)

is the number of secondary infective individuals at time
t
. Infectivity age
is the time since becoming infective. Notice that all secondary
infective hosts at time
t
have an infectivity age
a\in[0,t)
.

The force of infection is now

I(t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}u_{1}(t,a)\kappa(a)da+\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{2}(t,a,m)dm\Big)\kappa(a)da.

(4.3)

Let
B(t,m)
be the rate of individuals with resistance
m
that become infective
at time
t
. Let
P(a)
be the probability that an infective
individual has not died from the disease at age
a
.
Then

u_{2}(t,a,m)=B(t-a,m)P(a),\quad t>a\geq 0.

(4.4)

The equation for the susceptible hosts becomes

\partial_{t}S(t,m)=-S(t,m)I(t),

(4.5)

with
\partial_{t}
denoting the partial derivative with respect to time
t
.
It is solved by

S(t,m)=S(0,m)e^{-J(t)},\qquad J(t)=\int_{0}^{t}I(s)ds.

(4.6)

Let, for
t\geq 0
,

C(t,m)=\int_{0}^{t}B(s,m)ds,\qquad m\geq 0,

(4.7)

be the density of hosts with resistance
m

that have become infectious in the time interval from
0

to
t
. The same reasoning as in Section
2.3
provides

C(t,m)=S(0,m)f([J(t)-m]_{+}),\qquad t,m\geq 0,

(4.8)

where
[r]_{+}=\max\{r,0\}
is the positive part of the number

r\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
The same reasoning as in Section

2.1
leads to

J(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}S(0,m)f([J(t-a)-m]_{+})dm\Big)P(a)\kappa(a)da+J_{0}(t),

(4.9)

with
J_{0}
from (
2.23

At this point, for consistency with Section
2
,
we introduce the number of the initial susceptibles

S_{0}=\int_{0}^{\infty}S(0,m)dm

(4.10)

\sigma(m)=S(0,m)/S_{0}.

(4.11)

Let

(4.12)

Then

\begin{split}J(t)=&\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}\sigma(m)f([J(t-a)-m]_{+})dm\Big)\xi(a)da\\
&+J_{0}(t),\qquad t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.\end{split}

can be found as the pointwise
limit of an increasing iteration with start function

as in Section

for all

Notice that

to obtain the final size of the cumulative

Analysis of an abstract final size equation

Let
x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
denote the final cumulative force
of infection due to the initial infectives
and
z(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
the final cumulative force of
infection due to all infectives. In terms of the previous sections,
x=J_{0}^{\infty}
and
z(x)=J^{\infty}
. We have found the following equation,

z(x)=x+F(z(x)),\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

(5.1)

Here,
F:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is continuous, bounded and increasing,
F(0)=0
, with the finite limit

F(\infty):=\lim_{y\to\infty}F(y).

We want to find information about the the shape
of the function
z:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

For fixed resistance
m
, we have

F(y)={\mathcal{R}}_{0}f([y-m]_{+}),\qquad y\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

(5.2)

See Section
3
.
For continuously distributed resistance, we employ

F(y)={\mathcal{R}}_{\diamond}\int_{0}^{y}\sigma(m)f(y-m)dm,\qquad y\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

(5.3)

See Section
4
. In both cases,

is the Ivlev-Skellam function

f(y)=1-e^{-y},\qquad y\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},

(5.4)

and
{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{R}}_{\diamond}
are the basic reproduction number
(without resistance)
given by the identical formulas in (
3.4
) and (
4.15
).
In the second case,
\sigma:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is continuous and
its integral is 1,
{\mathcal{R}}_{\diamond}\in(0,\infty)
.
Different symbols,
{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{R}}_{\diamond}
, are
used because
{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
will act as a threshold
parameter as expected from a basic reproduction number while
{\mathcal{R}}_{\diamond}
will not.

Remark 5.1
.

In both cases,

(5.5)

where

of the disease given by (

)
(it there were no resistance:
Definition

)

and continuous and

.

In the spirit of

.
In that case, continuity of

follows from Lebesgue’s
theorem of dominated convergence.

and

,

. Since

{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, for each

,

and the limits

exist and provide an increasing function

(5.9)

In general, the convergence is only pointwise and

not continuous. See Theorem

.
Since

(5.10)

.

For each

,

for any solution
\tilde{z}
of this

is continuous from the left.

.

,

the final size curve. It gives the final size
of the cumulative force of infection
as a function of the final size of the cumulative
initial force of infection. In general, there
may be several solution of (

). The minimal solution is the epidemiologically relevant one because it
also is the limit of the cumulative force of infection
as time tends to infinity (as we have shown
in the previous sections). Compare
[

, Sec.3]
.

Proof.

Let
x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

x\leq z_{n}(x)\leq\tilde{z}
for any solution

where

is from the recursion

).

By taking the limit as

),

x\leq z(x)\leq\tilde{z}
.

.

is the minimal solution,

.
Further, since

is increasing,
z(x)\geq F(x)+x
.
So, if

,

.

Suppose that
z
is not continuous from the left at

.
Then there exists some

and

. After choosing

such that

Theorem 5.4

Theorem 5.15

[
20
, Cor.6.3.3]

Notice that

Assume that

We provide the induction step.

Let

For

is increasing and convex on

\begin{split}&F\big(z_{n-1}(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\big)\leq F\big(\alpha z_{n-1}(x)+(1-\alpha)z_{n-1}(y)\big)\\

This shows that

is convex on

The concave case is shown similarly.

8
Discussion

In
[
30
,
64
]
, a more general
condition than the one involving
(
1.4
)
is considered, where
a host that becomes infectious at time
t

was infected at a time
r
with

\int_{r}^{t}(\delta+I(s))ds=m.

If
\delta>0
, every infected individual
will become infectious after a delay

m/\delta
at the latest, and our explanation
why there are different disease outcomes in
similar cities does
no longer work. A possible proportionality constant in front of
I

[
11
,
29
,
30
,
64
]

has been absorbed into the
resistance
m
.

These threshold delay conditions are early examples of
state-dependent delays
and differential equations containing them
have become topics of
a rich theory (
[
6
,
24
,
39
,
49
,
58
]

and the references therein). The specialty of
our threshold delay condition (
\delta=0
) allows us to
bypass the intricacies of this theory and
transform the problem into a single integral
equation of Volterra type from which much desirable
information can be obtained.
One obtains a deterministic explanation of why there are different
epidemic outcomes in cities of similar size, namely
that the final sizes of the primary infective force
or the disease resistance distributions of the initial
susceptibles were different.

In the version of the model with distributed
resistance, we have made the simplifying assumption
that the disease-mortality
P
in (
4.4
)
and the
infectivity-age dependent infectious influence
of an infectious host
\kappa

in (
4.3
)
do not depend on its
resistance
m
. Of course, one could consider
a dependence instead. Such a generalization
would bring greater
mathematical difficulty requiring functional
analysis instead of what mostly is advanced calculus. See
[
7
]
and
[
8
]

for examples of models that
incorporate other distributed traits
than distributed resistance.
Since we expect the essence of the epidemiological
message to be similar, we use Occam’s razor (principle of parsimony) for this presentation.
In this spirit, we also stick to the equation

S^{\prime}(t)=-S(t)I(t)

rather than the more general equation

S^{\prime}(t)=-\eta(S(t))I(t)

(8.1)

with
\eta:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
continuous and

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\eta(s)}ds=\infty

considered in
[
61
]
.

The epidemic model with host resistance to the disease
provides a handy theoretical laboratory for
studying importance and the effects of host heterogeneity.
We consider both models with fixed resistance
and with distributed resistance.
We find that the threshold for the existence of jumps of the
final size curve is (often substantially) higher for distributed resistance than for fixed resistance.

Further, for fixed resistance, the jump of the final
size curve always occurs when the
final size of the initial cumulative force of infection passes the (fixed) resistance level;
in our example for Gamma-distributed
resistance, the jumps occur at smaller final sizes
of the initial cumulative force of infection.

It is difficult to relate our threshold
parameters
{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{T}}

to estimates of reproduction numbers found in the literature like in
[
66
]

because these estimates have been performed under
the assumption that there is no resistance
to the disease. In particular for distributed
resistance, it is not clear whether they should
be related to
{\mathcal{R}}_{\diamond}
or
{\mathcal{T}}
.

As expected, incorporating resistance towards
the disease into epidemic models creates an
Allee effect for the final size of the force of
infection. Less expected, the Allee effect is quite pronounced
and even occurs in the form of jumps, whether the resistance is the
same for all hosts or is distributed over the
host population.
