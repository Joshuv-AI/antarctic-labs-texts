Genetic Characterisation and Risk Factors Associated With Capripoxvirus Outbreaks Among Small Ruminants in Uganda, 2021–2022
Authors: Gerald Nizeyimana, Robert Tweyongyere, Joseph Erume, Savino Biryomumaisho, Eugene Arinaitwe, Chrisostom Ayebazibwe, Patrick Vudriko, Dennis Muhanguzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSheeppox and goatpox are systemic, contagious viral diseases of small ruminants caused by sheep and goat pox viruses of the genus capripoxvirus. These diseases cause fever, generalised skin lesions and negatively affect health, economics and welfare. Between 2011 and 2020, an average of four clinically suspected Capripox outbreaks were reported annually in Uganda through passive surveillance. However, information on the genetic characteristics of circulating capripoxviruses affecting remains limited. We conducted this study to (i) genetically characterise capripoxvirus strains associated with outbreaks in small ruminants and (ii) identify farm‐level risk factors associated with the infections.Samples comprising skin scabs, nasal, ocular swabs and whole blood from were collected from clinically affected goats and sheep during suspected outbreaks reported between September 2021 to September 2022 in Nakaseke, Gomba and Kiryandongo districts. Samples were screened using conventional polymerase chain reaction [PCR] targeting the 30 kDa RNA polymerase subunit (RPO30) gene, followed by dideoxy Sanger sequencing and phylogenetic analysis based on RP030, GPCR and P32 gene Farm level risk and factors were documented and assessed during the study.ResultsCapripoxvirus DNA was detected by PCR in 30 out of 107 samples, reflecting a true positivity rate of 28.7%. All positive samples were from goats, while none of the sheep samples tested positive. Phylogenetic analysis based on all three genes (RPO30, GPCR and P32) classified the isolates as goatpox virus and separated it from other capripoxvirus reference strains. Goatpox virus strains detected across the four outbreaks were 100% identical to each other based on phylogenetic analysis, indicating circulation of a closely related strain within the affected districts. Phylogeny showed that Uganda goatpox virus isolates clustered closely [96%–100% with i reference strains from Kenya, Ghana, Sudan, Oman, Yemen, Burkina Faso and Chad. Low farm level biosecurity characterised by poor carcass disposal methods, human traffic and co‐mingling of infected and apparently healthy] as well as uncontrolled animal and animal product movement, were identified at field level as probable exposure factors. The genetic relationship between GTPV strains from outbreaks and regional reference strains underpins the transboundary nature of goat pox virus disease.ConclusionOur study confirmed goatpox virus as the causative agent of outbreaks investigated between 2021–2022. Multi‐gene phylogenetic analysis showed a close relationship between Uganda GTPV isolates and strains in parts of Africa and Middle East. The findings provide a basis for sustained surveillance to determine diversity of GTPV strains, increased adoption of good biosecurity practices, as well as improvement in animal movement control as key measures to reduce future outbreaks.Keywords: capripoxvirus, goatpox virus, GPCR, outbreak investigation, P32 genes, phylogenetic analysis, RPO30, Uganda

Background

Sheeppox and goatpox are systemic, contagious viral diseases of small ruminants that present as mild to severe illnesses characterised by extensive painful skin lesions (Wondimu et al. 2021). Sheeppox and goatpox, caused by sheep pox virus (SPPV) and goat poxvirus (GTPV), respectively, are closely related diseases caused by distinct viruses that belong to genus Capripoxvirus within the Poxviridae family (Zewdie et al. 2021). Infections with SPPV and GTPV present with fever, generalised macules that progress into papules, necrotic lesions of the skin and nodular lesions in internal organs at death(OIE 2017). Morbidity in indigenous small ruminant breeds ranges from 1%–90%, while mortality varies from 10%–50%. Case fatality rate of up to 100% in highly susceptible young imported naïve herds is often the norm (Rovid Spickler 2003; Tuppurainen 2005; Bowden et al. 2008; Nizeyimana et al. 2023; Jadhav et al.

Sheeppox and goatpox are spread through direct contact with infected animals, aerosols from nasal secretions, saliva and dried scabs and indirectly by fomites as well as transportation vehicles (Tuppurainen et al. 2017). Sheeppox and goatpox have no definitive curative treatment, with their management relying primarily on supportive care to reduce morbidity and severity of the disease alongside strict biosecurity measures and animal movement control (Manosur et al. 2021). Goatpox and sheeppox economic losses arise from reduced productivity, morbidity, mortality, treatment and prevention (including vaccination) costs and loss of income due to market‐access restrictions (Adedeji et al. 2021; Hamdi et al. 2021; Modise et al. 2021; Pham et al. 2021).

Suspected clinical cases of capripoxvirus infections [sheeppox and goatpox] were reported in 18 districts of Uganda between 2011 to 2020 (Nizeyimana et al. 2023). However, none of these outbreaks were confirmed by any laboratory diagnostic means, limiting data availability to stakeholders. In addition, capripoxvirus infections often go unreported due to the absence of an active surveillance plan for these diseases in Uganda (Nizeyimana et al. 2023).

Sheeppox and goatpox are Class A listed diseases of the World Organisation for Animal Health [WOAH] as highly notifiable following confirmation of an outbreak (Lubroth and De Balogh 2009). Sheeppox and goatpox, like any other transboundary animal diseases (TADs), require an enhanced surveillance system with serious zoo‐sanitary measures to control (Gongal et al. 2022). However, this is not always feasible in areas where these diseases are endemic because of limiting policies, inadequate resources and veterinary governance constraints, as is the case in Uganda (FAO 2019).

Sheeppox and goatpox diagnosis can be completed by combining clinical signs and laboratory testing using serology, molecular methods and histopathology. SPPV and GTPV antigens and nucleic acids can be detected from whole blood, tissue scabs, skin scrapings, oral, nasal and ocular secretions (Rovid Spickler 2003). Samples for viral antigen detection are taken within the first week of infection before neutralising antibodies develop. Virus isolation is likely to be unsuccessful after generalised disease (Rovid Spickler 2003). SPPV and GTPV may be differentiated by use of the RPO30 gene that reveals lineage‐specific signatures and 21 nucleotides deletion exclusive in SPPV (Santhamani et al. 2014). This is possible through amplification of short gene fragments of 172 and 151 bp for goat pox and SPPV, respectively (Lamien et al. 2011; Manosur et al. 2021). In instances where none biased detection of capripoxvirus DNA is required, targeting the conserved 554 bp region of the RPO30 gene that amplifies both SPPV and GTPV is preferred (Bayyappa et al. 2024). Although RP030 gene is useful for capripoxvirus detection, additional markers such as the G protein‐coupled chemokine receptor (GPCR) and P32 genes provide greater insight into genetic diversity. Together, these genes are widely used in molecular epidemiological studies to improve phylogenetic resolution and enable comparisons of circulating strains across geographic regions (El‐Sabagh et al. 2020; Zewdie et al. 2021). Using all three genetic markers enhances the identification of capripoxviruses and improves the understanding of their genetic relationships.

Previous studies have indicated exposure to sheeppox and goatpox virus in goats and sheep in Uganda as well as neighbouring countries such as Ethiopia and Kenya amidst interconnected small ruminant grazing patterns (Aklilu, Y., Little, P.D., Mahmoud, H. and McPeak 2013; Nkamwesiga et al. 2019; Zewdie et al. 2021; Nizeyimana et al. 2023). However, these studies have highlighted gaps in information on capripoxvirus outbreak occurrence, spread, transmission and risk factors thereof, including genetic characterisation, especially in Uganda (Nizeyimana et al. 2023). This study therefore aimed to genetically characterise capripoxvirus strains associated with outbreaks in small ruminants using RPO30, GPCR and P32 gene sequences and to identify farm‐level risk factors associated with infection in affected herds from Nakaseke, Gomba and Kiryandongo districts of Uganda.

Methodology

Study Area and Population Description

This study was conducted in Gomba, Nakaseke and Kiryandongo districts located in the cattle corridor of Uganda. These districts were selected based on suspected reports of capripoxvirus outbreaks [Figure 1]. Uganda is a moderately warm country with temperatures ranging from 20–25 degrees centigrade, an altitude of 1000 to 1400 meters above sea level and mean annual rainfall of 900 to 1500 millimetres (WorldStats 2024). The cattle corridor represents Uganda's savannah and woodlands favourable for goats and sheep production. With a steady rise in population of goats and sheep at 3.1% and 2.8% annually since 2008, Uganda's goat and sheep population stands at 16.9 million and 4.6 million, respectively (UBOS 2020). The predominant small ruminant production system is extensive, with very few sheep and goat intensive farms. In Uganda, 95% of the goat and sheep breeds are indigenous [small East African, Mubende and Kigezi] while about 5% are exotic [Boer, Toggenburg, Savannah and Saanen (Oluka et al. 2004). 99.2 % of sheep breeds are indigenous [long‐tailed sheep] and 0.8 % exotic Dorper sheep (Behnke and Nakirya 2012).

Study Design

This was a purposive descriptive study of suspected capripoxvirus [sheeppox and goatpox] outbreaks reported between 2021 to 2022. Study districts were selected based on outbreak reports from local/District veterinary authorities and later verified through the national epidemio‐surveillance system. However, this verification process could have affected the number of outbreaks included since only those confirmed through the national surveillance system were investigated. This was in spite of its low sensitivity of up to 18% as previously reported (FAO 2019).

Sheeppox and goatpox virus infection‐associated exposure factors were assessed and documented using a predefined data collection tool during the study. The assessment was conducted alongside observation of the veterinary service system‐wide disease control measures. Samples [tissues, swabs and whole blood] from clinically affected goats and sheep were collected for detection of capripoxvirus nucleic acids. Conventional PCR targeting the RPO30 RNA polymerase subunit gene was performed.

We employed primers targeting the ∼554 bp region of the RPO30 gene, which is conserved and amplified equally for both SPPV and GTPV for initial confirmation. This choice was equally informed by field observations, as one of the affected farms had sheep showing clinical signs and the broader amplicon (554 bp) allowed us to detect infection without species bias. The resultant amplicons were subsequently sequenced. In addition, molecular characterisation and genotyping of the detected capripoxviruses were performed using the full length GPCR gene and the P32 major envelope protein gene. The GPCR gene was selected because of its high discriminatory power for resolving phylogenetic relationships among capripoxvirus strains, while the P32 gene provided further confirmation of species identity and evolutionary relatedness.

Capripoxvirus Outbreak Investigation

Four suspected sheeppox and goatpox outbreaks reported in 2021 and 2022 were investigated using a standard outbreak investigation procedure. Outbreak epidemiological intelligence data was obtained from the area veterinary officers through the main social media platforms and corroborated with National surveillance reports prior to investigation. Affected farmers reported to area veterinary officers a strange disease in goats and sheep, characterised by generalised nodular skin swellings with high morbidity.

Procedure

Physical and clinical examinations of clinically affected goats and sheep were conducted and samples for sheeppox and goatpox virus molecular analysis were collected (Figure 1). Detailed data were collected on vaccination status, herd size and husbandry system, including biosecurity practices, farm‐level characteristics, outbreak timelines and risk factors associated with sheeppox and goatpox outbreaks in the different districts, compiled (Table 1).

Tissue scabs, nasal and ocular swabs were collected based on WOAH‐recommended standardard operating procedures. Tissues and swabs were collected into 5ml falcon tubes containing 2ml phosphate buffered saline (PBS), while whole blood was collected into EDTA vacutainer tubes. Each of the samples collected was given a unique identifier and placed in a cool box with ice and transferred to NADDEC for storage. On arrival at the laboratory, samples were aliquoted into a 2ml cryovial each and frozen at −80°C until PCR analysis was done.

DNA Extraction Procedure

Capripoxvirus DNA was extracted from sample homogenates using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germany) following manufacturer's instructions.

Briefly

20 µL of proteinase K was added to a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube, followed by 100 µL of the sample and 100 µLs of PBS to make a final volume of 220 µL. To this mixture, 200 µL buffer AL was added and thoroughly mixed by vortexing and incubated for 10 minutes at 56°C. After incubation, 200 µL of moleculr grade absolute ethanol was added to this mixture and vortexed to form a homogeneous mixture. The mixture was transferred into mini‐spin columns placed in a 2 mL collection tube and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for I minute and flow‐through discarded. The spin columns were placed into new 2 mL collection tubes and 500 µL of buffer AW1 added and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 1 minute and flow‐through and collection tubes discarded. The spin columns were again placed in the new 2 mL collection tubes and 500 µL of buffer AW2 was added and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 3 min and the flow‐through discarded. Finally, the spin columns were placed in 2 mL sterile microcentrifuge tubes and 100 µL of buffer AE was added directly onto the membrane, incubated at room temperature for 1 min and later centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 3 min to elute the DNA. Collected tubes with eluted DNA were labelled properly and stored at −20°C until the PCR was performed.

PCR Confirmation of Capripoxvirus

Samples collected from clinically suspected sheeppox and goatpox cases in the study districts were tested for capripoxvirus DNA using PCR. Initial molecular confirmation of sheeppox and goatpox virus DNA was performed based on conventional PCR targeting the RNA polymerase 30 Kda subunit (RPO30) gene, a marker commonly used in epidemiological studies for capripoxviruses. This was possible through amplification of a 554 bp fragment of RP030 gene using a pair of primers viz; forward (CpRPO30– OL1 F) 5’‐CAG CTG TTT GTT TAC ATT TGA TTT TT‐3’ and reverse (CpRPO30—OL1R) 5’‐TCG TAT AGA AAC AAG CCT TTA ATA GA‐3’). The PCR reaction was carried out in a final volume of 25 µL containing: Taq PCR master mix (12.5µl) (Bioline, London United Kingdom), nuclease‐free water (8.5 µL), forward and reverse primers (1 µL each) and the DNA extract (2 µL). The final volume was loaded on the ProFlex Base PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) set at 95°C for 4 min (one cycle) for initial denaturation, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 30 seconds, annealing at 55°C for 30 s, extension at 72°C for 30 s and final extension at 72°C for 2 min and final hold at 4°C indefinitely.

Further characterisation and genotyping of the detected capripoxviruses was done by additional PCR assays targeting the GPCR and P32 major envelope protein genes. A nested PCR targeting the full length 1158 bp GPCR gene was conducted using the primer pairs GPCR For (5′‐TTA AGT AAA GCA TAA CTC CAA CAA AAA TG‐3′) and GPCR Rev (5′‐TTT TTT TAT TTT TTA TCC AAT GCT AAT ACT‐3′) for the primary reaction and GPCR‐PR1 (5′‐TTA AGT AAA GCA TAA CTC CAA AAA TG‐3′) and GPCR‐PR2 (5′‐TTT TTT TAT TTT TTA TCC AAT GCT AAT ACT‐3′) for the secondary reaction, following the protocol previously described (Goff et al. 2015).

The complete open reading frame of the P32 major envelope protein gene, a commonly used marker for capripoxvirus diagnosis and evolutionary studies, was amplified by conventional PCR using the forward primer p32‐1 (5′‐ATG GCA GAT ATC CCA TT‐3′) and reverse primer p32‐2 (5′‐TTA CCA CAG GCT ATT AGA AG‐3′), producing an expected amplicon size of 1,181 bp based on a protocol previously described (Zhou et al. 2012).

PCR amplifications for both GPCR and P32 genes were performed using the Taq PCR Master Mix Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) in a final reaction volume of 25 µL consisting of 12.5 µL of 2× Taq PCR Master Mix, 0.4 µL of each primer (10µM), 4 µL of template DNA and 7.7 µL of nuclease‐free water. Annealing temperatures were 50°C for both primary and secondary GPCR reactions and 52°C for the P32 assay. PCR products were visualised on a 1.5% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide in an Enduro gel documentation system (Labnet International, Cary, North Carolina, USA). Expected sizes of the PCR products were purified and later sequenced using Sanger sequencing. Generated sequences from the RPO30, GPCR and P32 genes were subsequently used for phylogenetic analyses.

PCR Product Purification, Sequencing and Nucleotide Sequence Analysis

PCR products containing capripoxvirus were purified using QIAquick, PCR purification kit (QIAGEN, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions. DNA quantities in final PCR products were quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer before shipping to Inqaba Biotec in South Africa for Sanger sequencing. Obtained nucleotide sequences were edited using BioEdit software (Ibis Biosciences, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and FASTA sequence files for each sample were generated. Of the sequences edited, forward and reverse bidirectional reads were aligned using Molecular Evolutionary Genetic Analysis version 7.2 (MEGA 7.2) Software to obtain consensus sequences. Edited sequences for each sequenced target gene were compared with available capripoxvirus nucleotide sequences in GenBank using the National Centre for Biotechnological Information's web‐based Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (NCBI BLAST) <https:blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov> to check for any similarities.

For RPO30 for phylogenetic analysis, study sequences were aligned with 618 reference capripoxvirus sequences retrieved from GenBank, including 431 lumpy skin disease virus (LSDV), 107 SPPV and 75 GTPV isolates. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using MAFFT with default parameters (Katoh and Standley 2013). Reference sequences were prioritised based on BLAST results that showed the highest nucleotide similarity and origin to the sequences generated under this study. Sequences representing the key regional blocks such as East Africa, West Africa, North Africa, Southern Africa, Middle East and Europe were included. Priority was given to isolates from Uganda's trading partners in live animal trade, animal product trade and vaccine sources.

To undertake phylogenetic analysis for GPCR and p32 genes, we downloaded the respective sequences from GenBank and combined them with the respective study sequences. Multiple sequence Alignment was done using MAFFT v7.526 (2024/Apr/26) (Katoh and Standley 2013). Thereafter, maximum‐likelihood phylogenetic inference was performed using IQ‐TREE v3.0.1(Nguyen et al. 2014). and the TIM3 + F + G4 nucleotide substitution model was selected as the best‐fit model based on the Bayesian Information Criterion. Branch support was assessed using 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates. The generated phylogenetic trees were then visualised, styled and annotated using an online bioinformatics software tool used to phylogenetic trees (iTOL) interactive tree of life as previously described (Letunic 2024)

The RPO30 phylogenetic tree was displayed in a rectangular format with branch lengths proportional to evolutionary distance, allowing assessment of genetic diversity and relatedness among isolates. In contrast, the GPCR and P32 phylogenies were presented as circular cladograms, facilitating visualisation of clustering patterns and evolutionary relationships among capripoxvirus isolates from different geographic regions and host species. All nucleotide sequences generated in this study were deposited in the GenBank database under accession numbers OQ107315‐OQ107342, PZ812088‐PZ812089 and PZ812090‐PZ812091.

Results

Temporal and Spatial Distribution of Suspected Capripox Outbreak Cases Investigated

Four outbreaks were reported and investigated from September 2021 to September 2022. In these four outbreaks, six farms reported cases of sheep and goat pox and prompted clinical investigations of the farms. Of the six herds involved in the outbreaks, only one herd in Gomba district had a mixed herd of sheep and goats. All the others had only goats at the farm.

In terms of time, outbreaks were reported, verified and investigated over different dates from September 2021 through to September 2022. These were spread in Nakaseke [3 outbreaks], Kiryandongo [one outbreak] and Gomba [one outbreak] in order, occurring sporadically over time. The time lag between the first [September 2021] and second outbreak was approximately six months; the subsequent outbreaks clustered over 3 months in the 1st half of 2022. Nakaseke, on the other hand, reported more outbreaks [3 outbreaks] compared to the other districts [one outbreak each] [Figure 2]

Field Observations and Clinical Examination of Cases

Field‐level observations and review of the herd sizes across districts revealed varying herd sizes from 70 goats per farm to up to 450 small ruminants, above the average national household numbers in Central Uganda. Of all the suspected herds, up to 40% [n = 520] of the animals showed clinical signs [Table 2]. Clinically, all affected goats and sheep showed nodular skin lesions all over the body, enlarged superficial lymph nodes, especially precrural and prescapular, high temperature up to 41 degrees, dyspnea due to respiratory involvement and lethargy.

All the farms practised an extensive production system where the herds were housed overnight and grazed on free range in designated paddocked savanna grass and woodlands. All the farms reported no history of vaccination against sheep pox and goat pox. All the farms except two had a history of recent introduction of goats in the last one month prior to the time of investigation.

Based on the clinical signs, Nakaseke farms had the highest attack rate of up to 64% compared to Kiryandongo and Gomba districts of 32% and 0.5 % respectively. At all six farms, documented and observed exposure practices included poor biosecurity across including poor carcass disposal, communal grazing, absence of vaccination, uncontrolled traffic to and from the farms including new animal introductions (Table 1).

Of the six herds, 107 samples were collected, 75 of them being tissues and nasal swabs, while 35 were whole blood from animals where skin lesions and nasal/ocular secretions had subsided.

Phylogenetic Analysis

Newly sequenced Capripoxvirus (GTPV) isolates obtained from Uganda were genetically identical across all four documented outbreaks, indicating a single circulating strain or closely related virus lineage. Comparative sequence analysis revealed that the Ugandan isolates shared a high genetic relationship with previously published GTPV sequences originating from Sudan, Oman, Yemen and Kenya, suggesting a shared viral ancestry. BLAST analysis further supported these findings, with all sequences exhibiting greater than 96% nucleotide identity to GTPV reference strains archived in GenBank.

Phylogenetic analysis based on 554 bp fragment of the RPO30 gene placed all Ugandan isolates firmly within the GTPV clade, clearly separated from SPPV and LSDV lineages. This classification confirms that GTPV was the etiological agent responsible for the outbreaks investigated in this study (Figure 4 ). This classification was enabled through sequencing of a 554 bp fragment of the RPO30 gene, a genomic region known for its discriminatory power in distinguishing Capripoxvirus species and for its utility in global strain comparison.

Maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree of 633 capripoxvirus RNA polymerase subunit (RPO30) gene sequences constructed using the GTR + F model. Study sequences (highlighted in red, 15 sequences total) are labelled with _SGP suffix and represent capripoxvirus isolates analysed in this study. The analysis includes 431 lumpy skin disease virus (LSDV), 107 sheeppox virus (SPV) and 75 goatpox virus (GPV) sequences for comprehensive phylogenetic context. Bootstrap values ≥70% are shown at major nodes.

Further analysis of the full‐length GPCR gene provided additional phylogenetic resolution, with all study sequences clustering exclusively with GTPV isolates retrieved from GenBank. Notably, the Ugandan isolates grouped most closely with GTPV isolates from Kenya, Ghana, Burkina Faso and Yemen (Figure 5). In addition to this, phylogenetic reconstruction based on the complete P32 major envelope protein gene further confirmed the classification of the Ugandan isolates as GTPV. All representative sequences clustered with GTPV reference sequences and remained distinct from SPPV and LSDV strains. However, the P32 gene phylogeny provided lower resolution of genetic relationships and geographical clustering patterns than the GPCR gene phylogeny (Figure 6 ).

Collectively, the concordant clustering patterns observed across the RPO30, GPCR and P32 gene analyses provide molecular evidence that the outbreaks were caused by GTPV and demonstrate the close genetic relatedness of the Ugandan isolates to other African and Middle Eastern GTPV strains.

Discussion

Until recently, suspected capripoxvirus infections [sheep pox and goat pox] were reported as a sporadic, self‐limiting diseases of small ruminants [goats and sheep] in Uganda often going un confirmed by laboratory methods (Nizeyimana et al. 2023). As early as 2009, sheep pox and goat pox infections were reported to WOAH as present in Uganda, albeit with limited genetic details (Tuppurainen 2005). This study presents genetic confirmation of capripoxvirus circulating in suspected outbreaks investigated between September 2021 and September 2022 in Uganda.

Molecular characterisation based on the RPO30, GPCR and P32 GTPV as the causative agent of the outbreaks investigated in this study. Phylogenetic analyses consistently resolved three distinct capripoxvirus clades corresponding to GTPV, SPPV and LSDV. All study isolates clustered within the GTPV clade with highly supported bootstrap values clearly separating them from the SPPV and LSD subclades [Figures 4, 5, 6]. Furthermore, the analysis revealed no detectable genetic variation among the GTPV strains from the four outbreaks, a finding consistent with previous studies that indicated reported limited genetic variation among GTPVs (Roy et al. 2018; Zewdie et al. 2021). The close temporal and geographical occurrence of the outbreaks between September 2021 and September 2022 (Figure 2 ) could be probably be responsible for the similarities observed among the strains and possible epidemiological links between affected districts (Şener R and T. Turk 2023). The higher number of outbreaks reported in Nakaseke district may indicate sustained local transmission, potentially facilitated by livestock movements and outbreak‐associated panic sales (Nizeyimana et al. 2023).

Comparative sequence and phylogenetic analyses further demonstrated a close relationship between the Ugandan isolates and GTPV strains previously reported from Kenya, Sudan, Ghana, Burkina Faso, Yemen and Oman. Notably, the GPCR gene phylogeny provided the highest resolution, clustering the Ugandan isolates with African and Middle Eastern GTPV strains [Figure 5 ], while the P32 gene confirmed species identity but showed lower genetic resolution and geographical clustering patterns (Figure 6 ). The RPO30 gene effectively differentiated GTPV from SPPV and LSDV, confirming its utility for species‐level classification. Our findings are consistent with related studies that documented limited genetic diversity among capripoxviruses (Tuppurainen 2005; Muhaidi, Hamad and Al‐Hayani 2018; El‐Sabagh et al. 2020; Zewdie et al. 2021).

Clinically affected goats and sheep exhibited fever, generalised skin nodules, scab formation and nasal discharge, all consistent with capripoxvirus infection (Figure 3). The clinical disease observed in sheep on the farm that had both species was milder than that seen in goats, although the full course of disease in sheep could not be followed. This difference may reflect strain characteristics of certain sheeppox or goatpox viruses circulating in Eastern Africa, which can infect either species but tend to cause severe disease in their homologous host (Jadhav et al. 2025). This observation aligns with previous findings from Ethiopia and East Africa at large, where non–host‐specific capripoxvirus strains could infect both sheep and goats (Tuppurainen 2005; Bowden et al. 2008; Santhamani et al. 2014; Hamdi et al. 2021). This is contrary to sheep pox and GTPV strains in West Africa that are predominantly host‐specific and capable of infecting only their homologous species (Adedeji et al. 2021; Jadhav et al. 2025).

Overall, few animals were positive by PCR although they showed clinical signs, an observation that could be associated with the time samples were taken, as field investigation depended on the field‐level reports at the subnational level. Consistent with the findings by Nizeyimana et al. 2023, the sensitivity and low national epireporting level affected the time taken for samples collection with majority of the samples taken from Day 7–21 from the onset of the outbreak (Nizeyimana et al. 2023). This finding is important for resource‐limited settings because it helps optimise sample collection for molecular diagnosis. The low PCR positivity in whole blood compared with tissues and swabs is likely due to the epitheliotropic nature of capripoxviruses and rapid clearance of circulating virus by the host immune response. Consequently, tissues and lesion swabs may be more suitable samples for laboratory confirmation(Bowden et al. 2008). Vireamia due to capripoxvirus is short lived because of the fast control of virus shedding by both innate immunity (cytokines and interferons) and subsequently adaptive immune response. Early immunoglobulins appear within 7–10 days, followed by long lasting immunoglobulin G. This implies that whole blood is not the sample of choice unless collected in during the first week (within 7 days) of suspected infection (Tuppurainen 2005; Wondimu et al. 2021).

Although swabs contain low virus load, they provide a non‐invasive sampling approach considering animal welfare concerns and yet capable of yielding sufficient DNA for molecular detection (Bowden et al. 2008). These findings highlight the utility of swab samples for capripoxvirus diagnosis, providing results comparable to invasive sampling methods while minimising animal discomfort. More invasive samples may only be required for virus isolation.

Across all the farms assessed and investigated, a plethora of factors that increase the risk of capripoxvirus exposure were evident. Notable among these were weak biosecurity measures, poor case‐management practices, inadequate carcass‐disposal methods and uncontrolled human movement on affected farms. At the national level, limited implementation of coordinated control measures further compounded the risk. These observations could probably be linked to a low level of awareness of small ruminant capripoxvirus infections, as well as gaps in epidemiological information. These findings are consistent with the FAO Surveillance for Action Assessment Tool report, which highlights the centrality of timely surveillance and data for effective animal disease control (FAO 2019)

The identified GTPV showed a close relationship with reference strains retrieved from GenBank originating from Sudan, Oman, Yemen and Chad, with over 96% genetic identity. Additionally, the genetic similarity of up to 99% between GTPV strains from Ugandan outbreaks and those from Sudan, Yemen, Oman and Vietnam indicates limited genomic divergence. This high level of identity suggests a possible epidemiological link between the Ugandan strains and strains circulating in these countries (El‐Sabagh et al. 2020).

Goatpox is a TAD with the potential to cross international boundaries, particularly during periods of uncontrolled animal movement (Hasahya et al. 2023). This is particularly relevant in Uganda, which frequently receives large numbers of small ruminants from neighbouring countries. Small ruminants inflows are driven by drought‐induced migrations in search of pasture and water, as well as movements associated with preparing animals for export following prolonged drought periods as s those commonly experienced in Karamoja cluster(Aklilu and Catley 2009; Aklilu, Y., Little, P.D., Mahmoud, H. and McPeak 2013). The porous nature of Uganda's borders, coupled with the transboundary features of capripoxviruses underscore the need for a coordinated, national regional and cluster approach to sheeppox and goatpox control (Kardjadj 2016). Furthermore, analysis of livestock trade patterns within Uganda shows extensive animal movement with the potential to facilitate rapid disease spread across the country (Hasahya et al. 2023).

It is worth noting that goatpox, together with other capripoxviruses, is categorised as a priority TAD in the Horn of Africa by the Inter‐African Union Bureau of Animal Resources. This designation underscores the need for strengthened cross‐border coordination and harmonised management approaches, further supporting our earlier assertion. This is attributed to documented genetic similarity among capripoxvirus strains circulating within the Horn of Africa, as well as the prevalent intra‐regional, shared risk factors that facilitate their spread (Tuppurainen 2005; IGAD Centre for Pastoral Areas and Livestock Development 2025).

The characteristic movement of animals into Uganda from neighbouring countries during drought periods, followed by their eventual return, is one of the most likely mechanisms facilitating the spread of GTPV (Abbas et al. 2014). Together, these factors provide important insight into the probable epidemiological relationship between the confirmed GTPV strains detected in this study and those circulating in neighbouring countries (IGAD Centre for Pastoral Areas and Livestock Development 2025).

In practical terms, animal movement seems to be unidirectional from Uganda to neighbouring countries a head export. However, an analysis of the trade dynamics in the Karamoja cluster reveals an interesting pattern. Herds from neighbouring with herds complex pattern enter Uganda in search of water and pasture during drought and later return to their origin for trade purposes (Aklilu and Catley 2009).

This is in addition to fattening arrangements of livestock in Uganda by some farmers from neighbouring countries taking advantage of the pasture before trading with other countries (Aklilu and Catley 2009). Seasonal livestock trekking along the Blue Nile within the horn of Africa in dry season, facilitates comingling of before returning during the wet season further explaining the similarity of the identified GTPV strains with other regional strains (Abbas et al. 2014). Co‐incidentally, these movements occur during period when animals immunity is compromised due to feed scarcity and stress associated with long‐distance trekking further exacerbating the risk of GTPV among small ruminants (Tadesse, Aregahagn and Tegegne 2024).

In this manuscript, although three genomic targets (RPO30, GPCR and P32) were successfully sequenced and analysed, we could not conduct GTPV full genome sequencing and as a limitation, fine‐scale genomic variation, evolutionary dynamics and transmission pathways among outbreak strains could not be fully resolved. In addition, the fewer number of outbreaks studied and low PCR positivity among clinically affected animals could have been affected by the low sensitivity of outbreak reporting, timing of sampling and lack of proper farm records. These factors may have affected the identification of index cases during field investigations and other and other critical parameters.

Conclusion

Molecular analysis based on the RPO30, GPCR and P32 genes confirmed GTPV as the causative agent of small ruminant Capripox outbreaks investigated in Uganda between 2021 and 2022. Phylogenetic analysis of Uganda isolates showed genetic similarity [96%–100 % nucleotide identity] with reference strains from GenBank from Africa and middle (Kenya, Ghana, Burkina Faso, Yemen, Sudan and Oman). The absence of detectable genetic variation among the outbreak isolates points to the circulation of a closely related goatpox virus lineage during the study period. These findings provide information on the molecular epidemiology of GTPV in Uganda and underscore the transboundary nature of the GTPV disease.

We recommend, based on the findings, an extended molecular analysis using whole‐genome sequencing to identify any possible molecular diversity and epidemiological dynamics to guide the design of a small ruminant capripoxvirus national control strategy. In addition, a suite of biosecurity practices alongside enhanced regulation of animal movement are recommended to farmers and public sector veterinary services, respectively.
