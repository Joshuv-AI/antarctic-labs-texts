Intravenous administration of walnut leaf extract attenuates oxidative stress and improves insulin sensitivity and adipose tissue metabolism in feed-restricted fat-tailed ewes
Authors: Maryam Sahebi-Ala, Hamed Khalilvandi-Behroozyar, Rasoul Pirmohammadi, Ehsan Anassori, Morteza Hosseini-Ghaffari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Walnut leaf extract improved oxidative status and metabolism in feed-restricted ewes.•High-dose Walnut leaf extract (100 mg/kg BW) enhanced insulin sensitivity and upregulated INSR/GLUT4.•Walnut leaf extracts reduced plasma NEFA and MDA, indicating decreased lipolysis and oxidative stress.•Histologic analysis showed fewer, larger adipocytes, reflecting altered adipose metabolism in negative energy balance.Keywords: Insulin sensitivity, Metabolic health, Oxidative stress, Adipose tissue, Phytochemicals, Phyto-biotics, Negative energy balance, Lipolysis

Introduction

Plants synthesize a wide array of secondary metabolites that exert diverse physiological effects on animals (Wink, 2010; Fraenkel, 1959). Among these, polyphenolic compounds, particularly flavonoids, have attracted growing interest because of their well-documented antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties, which can improve both the health and productivity of livestock (Jomova et al., 2024; Messinese et al., 2024). In ruminant nutrition, polyphenol-rich plant extracts are increasingly regarded as promising natural feed additives, not only for their capacity to alleviate oxidative stress and metabolic disturbances, but also for their environmental sustainability and cost-effectiveness (Chandni Ahmad et al., 2024). However, despite extensive evidence of their beneficial effects in monogastric animals, the efficacy of polyphenols in ruminants remains less clear due to their unique digestive physiology and extensive pre-absorptive metabolism (Scalbert et al., 2005; Manach et al., 2004; Vasta & Luciano, 2011). A major limitation arises from the extensive microbial biotransformation of polyphenols within the rumen, which can modify or degrade these compounds before absorption, leading to reduced bioavailability and possible underestimation of their systemic effects (Vasta & Luciano, 2011). Intravenous (IV) administration offers a valuable strategy to bypass ruminal metabolism, allowing for a direct assessment of the physiological impacts of these phytochemicals (Bionaz et al., 2015; Van Soest, 1994). Despite this, few studies have investigated the metabolic, endocrine and molecular responses to IV-administered plant polyphenols in ruminants, and their safety and efficacy remain largely uncharacterized (Vasta & Luciano, 2011).

Flavonoids have been shown to modulate metabolic pathways in both monogastric and ruminant species including processes related to insulin sensitivity, lipid metabolism, and oxidative balance (Hanhineva et al., 2010; Williamson, 2017; Gessner et al., 2017). Experimental evidence indicates that flavonoid supplementation can enhance antioxidant enzyme activity, attenuate lipid peroxidation and support glucose–insulin homeostasis during metabolic stress (Hassanpour & Doroudi, 2023). The leaves of Juglans regia L. (walnut) represent a rich source of flavonoids such as quercetin, kaempferol and ellagic acid, along with phenolic acids, conferring a strong antioxidant capacity (Cordova et el., 2025). Walnut leaf extract (WLE) has demonstrated beneficial effects on oxidative balance, glucose regulation and lipid metabolism in cell culture and rodent models (Mirzababaei et el., 2022; Popescu et al., 2020). In ruminants, evidence remains limited: feeding walnut leaves has been reported to improve antioxidant status in lambs (Kazemi & Mokhtarpour, 2021), but the systemic impacts of IV WLE administration have not been investigated. Oxidative stress and chronic inflammation are key mechanisms contributing to insulin resistance during negative energy balance (NEB), a common metabolic challenge in ruminants that enhances lipolysis, elevates circulating non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA) and impairs glucose utilization (Qiao et el., 2024). Identifying effective interventions to mitigate NEB-induced metabolic disorders is therefore of considerable practical importance. To fill this gap, the present study employed a feed restriction model in Makui fat-tailed ewes to simulate NEB and examine the dose-dependent effects of standardized IV WLE on oxidative status, metabolic parameters, and the transcriptional activity of genes involved in insulin-related pathways, lipid regulatory processes, and inflammatory responses. We hypothesized that IV WLE administration would attenuate oxidative stress, improve insulin sensitivity, and modulate key molecular regulators of energy metabolism. This work provides the first direct evidence of the metabolic and molecular efficacy of IV WLE in sheep and highlights its potential as a phytogenic intervention for ruminant health management.

Materials and methods

Animals, housing, and environmental conditions

The study was conducted from October to December 2023 at the Urmia University Research Farm, Urmia, Iran, where all experimental procedures and animal management were carried out. Twelve healthy, multiparous, non-pregnant and non-lactating Makui ewes (Ovis aries) with an average body weight of 60.0 ± 1.2 kg and a body condition score (BCS) of 2.5 ± 0.25 were selected from a uniform genetic flock of Urmia University Research Farm, Urmia, Iran. All ewes were examined by a veterinarian to ensure that they were free of diseases such as brucellosis and internal parasites and were given a standard deworming protocol (ivermectin, 0.2 mg/kg subcutaneous injection). Ewes were housed individually in pens (2 m × 1.5 m) equipped with rubber floors, individual feeding troughs and automatic water dispensers. Environmental parameters, including temperature (18–22 °C), relative humidity (50–60%) and ventilation rate, were continuously monitored using HOBO® data loggers (Onset Computer Corp., Bourne, MA, USA). Lighting was provided by natural daylight supplemented by artificial lighting to ensure a constant 12:12 h light-dark cycle throughout the study period.

During the trial period, non-pregnant and non-lactating ewes were subjected to a feed restriction protocol designed to induce hunger and stimulate lipolysis by providing approximately 50% of their estimated metabolizable energy (ME) requirements, based on NRC (2007) guidelines. Each ewe received 0.25 kg dry matter (DM) of alfalfa hay twice daily at 08:00 and 16:00 h, with the offered feed fully consumed within each feeding period. Laboratory analysis of the hay indicated it contained approximately 15.0% crude protein, 1.78 Mcal/kg DM of metabolizable energy, and about 50% neutral detergent fiber (NDF). This restricted feeding protocol was designed to supply roughly half of the maintenance energy requirements, ensuring a pronounced negative energy balance (NEB) to promote lipolysis. Throughout the study, fresh drinking water, salt blocks, and a commercial vitamin–mineral premix (80 gr vitamin–mineral premix per each ewe including vitamins A, D3, E, and minerals such as Zn, Cu, Mn, and Se, according to Table 1) were made available ad libitum.

A replicated 4 × 4 Latin square design was used, consisting of four treatment periods, each lasting 10 days, with intervening 20-day washout periods to eliminate carryover effects and restore body condition. During the washout periods, the experimental animals were fed a basal diet (Table 1) offered ad libitum. Ewes were randomly assigned to treatments based on initial body weight (BW) and body condition score (BCS) to ensure homogeneity between groups. The four experimental treatments were: Control (intravenous infusion of sterile saline, 0.9% NaCl), WLE50 (walnut leaf extract at 50 mg/kg BW), WLE75 (walnut leaf extract at 75 mg/kg BW) and WLE100 (walnut leaf extract at 100 mg/kg BW). A schematic representation of the experimental design, including treatment periods, washout intervals, feeding restriction protocol, and sampling schedule, is presented in Fig. 1.

Schematic representation of the experimental design.

Walnut leaves (Juglans regia L.) were harvested from mature trees, certified by a botanist and air-dried at room temperature (25 °C) for 7 days before grinding to a fine powder (<1 mm). Extraction was performed using the Soxhlet method with 70% ethanol for 6 h at 60 °C, followed by rotary evaporation under reduced pressure (45 °C) and freeze-drying. The resulting WLE was standardized to contain ≥ 60 mg total flavonoids per gram (as quercetin equivalents). The flavonoid content was verified by aluminum chloride colorimetric assay and HPLC analysis (Agilent 1200 Series, USA), which confirmed the presence of quercetin, kaempferol and ellagic acid as major polyphenolic compounds. For intravenous infusion, the freeze-dried WLE was freshly resuspended in sterile normal saline (0.9% NaCl) immediately before administration to ensure solubility and sterility. The dosage levels (50, 75, and 100 mg/kg BW) were selected based on preliminary dose–response experiments conducted in our laboratory and on previously published reports of the biological activity and safety of walnut leaf extract (Sahebi Ala et al., 2025). The WLE solution was administered intravenously via a jugular catheter twice daily, concurrently with the morning and afternoon feedings, on days 4 to 10 of each experimental period.

Blood sampling and biochemical assays

Jugular vein catheters (14 gauge, BD Insyte™, USA) were inserted under sterile conditions and local anesthesia (2% lidocaine HCl, 5 mL subcutaneously) on day 2 of each period. The insertion site was cleaned with 70% ethanol and an iodine-containing antiseptic solution. The procedure was performed using aseptic surgical techniques and the catheters were fixed with 3–0 silk sutures. Postoperative treatment included the administration of flunixin meglumine (2.2 mg/kg, subcutaneous) for pain management, and the animals were monitored for signs of infection for 48 h after surgery. Catheters were flushed daily with 50 IU/mL heparinized saline to maintain patency and prevent blood clot formation, and patency was checked daily.

Blood samples (10 mL) were collected in lithium-heparinized tubes at two time points per day: before feeding (0730 h) and 4 h post- feeding (1130 h) on days 4 through 10 of each experimental period. Samples were immediately placed on ice to minimize degradation and then centrifuged at 2000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C in a Thermo Scientific refrigerated centrifuge. Plasma samples were dispensed into pre-identified microcentrifuge tubes and kept at –80 °C until further analysis. Samples were stored for a maximum of 6 months and thawed once prior to analysis to prevent degradation due to repeated freezing and thawing cycles. Plasma was processed within 2 h of collection to avoid changes in metabolite concentrations. Plasma oxidative stress biomarkers and antioxidant enzyme activities were determined to evaluate systemic redox status. Malondialdehyde (MDA), an index of lipid peroxidation, was quantified using the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) assay according to the method described by Ohkawa et al. (1979). Briefly, plasma samples were mixed with thiobarbituric acid (TBA), trichloroacetic acid, and sodium dodecyl sulfate, and the reaction mixture was heated in a boiling water bath to form the MDA–TBA adduct. After cooling and centrifugation, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured spectrophotometrically at 532 nm using an automated microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, USA). MDA concentrations were calculated from a standard curve prepared using 1,1,3,3‑tetraethoxypropane and expressed as nmol/mL of plasma. Total antioxidant capacity (TAC) was measured using an enhanced chemiluminescence assay according to Whitehead et al. (1992), which assesses the cumulative antioxidant activity of plasma against reactive oxygen species. Results were expressed as mmol Trolox equivalents per liter of plasma. Activities of antioxidant enzymes superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH‑Px) were determined using commercially available spectrophotometric assay kits (Cayman Chemical Company, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions and previously validated methods. The SOD assay is based on the inhibition of superoxide‑induced reduction of tetrazolium salt (Aebi, 1984), whereas GSH‑Px activity was measured through a coupled reaction with glutathione reductase, in which the oxidation rate of NADPH was monitored at 340 nm (Paglia & Valentine, 1967).

Intravenous glucose and insulin tolerance tests (ivGTT and ivITT)

Intravenous glucose tolerance tests (ivGTT) and insulin tolerance tests (ivITT) were conducted on the final days of the experimental periods (days 9 and 10) to maximize the duration of extract consumption and allow sufficient time for the animals to fully adapt to the diet and experimental conditions. Performing these tests at the end of each period (rather than at the beginning) minimizes transient and short-term effects caused by dietary changes, ensuring that the obtained results more accurately reflect the animals’ true physiological responses to the treatments. Ewes were fasted overnight for 12 h prior to each tolerance test and had unrestricted access to water to standardize baseline metabolic conditions. For ivGTT, animals received a single intravenous bolus injection of 0.25 g glucose/kg body weight in the form of a 50% sterile dextrose solution (Glucojet®, Zoopha, Parnian Pars, Iran) administered rapidly (within 1 min) via a jugular catheter. For ivITT, a bolus of insulin (0.1 IU/kg body weight; Lansulin R®, EXIR Pharmaceutical Co., Tehran, Iran) was also administered intravenously within 30 s. The injection volumes were accurately calculated based on the body weight measured 24 h before the test. After each test infusion, serial blood samples (approximately 5 ml per sample) were collected at fixed intervals: −15, −5 (baseline), 0 (immediately post-infusion) and 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 90, 120, 150 and 180 min post-infusion. Samples were collected via an indwelling catheter in the jugular vein into lithium-heparinized tubes and immediately placed on ice. The patency of the catheter was maintained by regular flushing with heparinized saline solution (50 IU/mL). The collected blood volume was topped up intravenously with an equal volume of sterile saline after each collection to prevent hemodynamic effects. Plasma was immediately separated by centrifugation (2000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C) and stored at −80 °C until subsequent biochemical analysis for glucose and insulin using previously described validated enzymatic and immunologic assays. Dynamic metabolic parameters derived from the ivGTT and ivITT datasets included area under the concentration-time curve (AUC), peak plasma concentrations (Cmax), glucose disappearance rate (K_glucose), insulin clearance rate (K_insulin), and half-life for glucose (T½_glucose) and insulin (T½_insulin). These parameters were calculated by nonlinear regression analysis with a single-phase exponential decay model and trapezoidal integration using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.4, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Tissue sampling and histological analysis

At the end of each experimental period, on day 10 after administration of treatments (including the control and intravenous injection of walnut extract at doses of 50, 75, and 100 mg/kg), adipose tissue samples were collected from caudal adipose tissue and subcutaneous fat over the lumbar region of the ewes. Prior to sampling, the sampling sites were shaved and disinfected with 70% ethanol to minimize contamination. Approximately 1 cm³ of adipose tissue was excised using sterile surgical scissors and forceps under local anesthesia to minimize animal discomfort. The samples were immediately rinsed in cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove blood and debris, then fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24–48 h at room temperature to preserve tissue architecture. Following fixation, samples were processed using standard histological procedures: deparaffinization in xylene, dehydration through a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 95%, and 100%), clarification with xylene, and embedding in paraffin wax. Serial sections of 5 µm thickness were cut from paraffin blocks using a rotary microtome. Sections were mounted on glass slides coated with poly-L-lysine to enhance tissue adherence and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for morphological evaluation. Microscopic examination was performed using a light microscope at magnifications of 40x and 100x. Multiple fields per section (at least 5 fields per sample) were evaluated to ensure representative analysis. Digital images were captured using a calibrated camera system connected to the microscope for morphometric and qualitative comparison. All procedures followed ethical guidelines for animal experimentation and were designed to ensure reproducibility and accuracy of adipose tissue morphological assessment.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

On day 10 of each experimental period, approximately 40 mg of subcutaneous adipose tissue was harvested under local anesthesia (2 lidocaine hydrochloride, 3 ml, subcutaneous injection) from the tail region and fat tail using sterile 6-mm biopsy punches (Miltex®, York, PA, USA). Immediately following sampling, the tissues were rapidly immersed in liquid nitrogen—within 1 min—to maintain RNA integrity, and subsequently stored at –80 °C until analysis. Total RNA was extracted from adipose tissue using TRIzol reagent (Dena Zist Asia, Mashhad, Iran), following the manufacturer's protocol exactly. The quality and purity of RNA was quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), ensuring an A260/A280 absorbance ratio between 1.8 and 2.0, indicating high purity RNA. The integrity of the RNA and the absence of genomic DNA contamination were confirmed by electrophoresis on 2% agarose gels stained with ethidium bromide (0.5 μg/mL). Distinct 28S and 18S rRNA bands were visible with minimal degradation.

Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of purified total RNA per sample using PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Biofact™, Yuseong-gu, Daejeon, Korea) according to the manufacturer's instructions (incubation at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 85 °C for 5 s). The synthesized cDNA was stored at –20 °C until further analysis. Quantitative real-time PCR assays were performed using SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara Bio Inc., Japan) and a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The reaction mixtures (20 µL total volume) contained 10 µL SYBR Premix, 0.8 µL each of forward and reverse primer (10 µM), 2 µL cDNA template and 6.4 µL nuclease-free water. PCR amplification was performed under the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 120 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s (denaturation) and 60 °C for 30 s (annealing/extension). Melting curve analysis (temperature range 60–95 °C) was performed immediately after amplification to check the specificity of the PCR products.

Specific primers (Table 2) for the genes encoding glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4), hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL), interleukin-6 (IL-6), insulin receptor (INSR), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), perilipin (PLIN) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) were designed using Primer3Plus software (www.bioinformatics.nl/cgi-bin/primer3plus/primer3plus.cgi) and synthesized by Invitrogen (USA). The specificity of the primers was confirmed by BLAST alignment with the NCBI genome database (GenBank). PCR efficiency, as determined by standard curve analysis using serial dilutions of pooled cDNA, was between 90 and 110%. All assays were performed in triplicate, with each run including non-template controls (NTC) to monitor for contamination. Expression levels of target genes were normalized to the three most stable reference genes (GAPDH, RPL19, and SDHA) selected using the geNormPLUS algorithm of qBASEplus software (version 3.1; Biogazelle, Ghent, Belgium). Normalized expression data were then used for statistical analyses.

Statistical analysis

The sample size of N = 12 ewes, allocated to three replicated 4 × 4 Latin squares, was selected to provide adequate statistical power. Based on the resource equation method for Latin square designs, this configuration yields a residual error degree of freedom of E = 30, which exceeds the recommended minimum threshold (10 ≤ E ≤ 20) required to reliably detect moderate to large physiological effect sizes while appropriately accounting for background variance. Because treatments were randomly assigned to animals sequentially across four periods, the individual ewe–period combination was considered the experimental unit for evaluating treatment effects, while ewe nested within square was included as a random subject effect to account for individual baseline variation.

All statistical analyses were performed using linear mixed-effects models in SAS (PROC MIXED, SAS version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Prior to fitting the models, the underlying assumptions of the analyses were evaluated sequentially. The normality of residuals was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (PROC UNIVARIATE, SAS) in combination with visual inspection of quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots. Homogeneity of variances among treatment groups was examined using Levene’s test. For variables that violated the normality assumption, a base-10 logarithmic transformation was applied before analysis. Following transformation, both normality and homogeneity of variance were reassessed to confirm that the transformed data satisfied the assumptions of the statistical models. Results

Animal status

Intravenous administration of WLE had significant effects on BW and BCS in feed-restricted ewes (Table 3). Ewes receiving the WLE100 treatment exhibited a significantly lower change in BW compared with those in the control, WLE50, and WLE75 groups (P < 0.05). Moreover, the average daily weight loss was markedly lower in the WLE100 group (0.235 kg/d) than in the control (0.422 kg/d; P < 0.01), WLE50 (0.385 kg/d; P < 0.01), and WLE75 (0.439 kg/d; P < 0.01) groups. Regarding BCS, ewes in the control, WLE50, and WLE75 groups exhibited a decline in BCS from 2.50 to 2.25 (P < 0.05), whereas the WLE100 group maintained their initial BCS (2.50; P < 0.05).

Metabolites in blood and antioxidant enzymatic factors

The effects of intravenous administration of different amounts of WLE on the biochemical parameters in the blood of ewes, both before and 4 h after morning feeding are shown in Table 4. Cholesterol levels were significantly lower in the WLE100 group compared with controls both before (54.3 vs 61.5 mg/dL; P < 0.01) and after feeding (56.2 vs 64.4 mg/dL; P < 0.01). A strong linear (P < 0.001) and quadratic trend (P < 0.01) was observed, with a significant treatment × day interaction (P = 0.01 before, P = 0.03 after). Serum TG concentrations were significantly reduced in WLE-treated ewes before feeding (WLE75: 23.1 mg/dL vs control: 25.3 mg/dL; P < 0.01) and after feeding (WLE100: 26.3 mg/dL vs control: 28.9 mg/dL; P < 0.01), with significant treatment × day interactions (P < 0.01). Day effects and treatment × day interactions were significant (P < 0.01). Blood urea concentrations showed a significant reduction in response to WLE treatment before feeding (P = 0.04) and a numerical, albeit non-significant, reduction after feeding (P = 0.94). The WLE100 group had the lowest urea concentrations at both time points (20.9 and 25.8 mg/dL, respectively) compared to the controls (24.1 and 26.2 mg/dL). Serum creatinine and total protein concentrations were unaffected by WLE administration both before and after feeding (P > 0.05) and no significant day or treatment × day interactions were observed (P > 0.70). For pre-feeding glucose concentration during the 8-day infusion period, there was no significant difference in the treatment × day interaction (P > 0.57) (Fig. 2A). Plasma glucose concentrations showed a decreasing trend during the experimental period. After feeding, plasma glucose levels were higher than before feeding, with the lowest concentration observed in the WLE100 group (Fig. 2A and a).

Histological effects of walnut leaf extract on adipose tissues

The histological effects of different doses of walnut leaf extract on tail and subcutaneous adipose tissue in feed-restricted ewes are shown in Fig. 6 and Table 6. Intravenous administration of WLE had significant effects on adipocyte morphology in both tail and subcutaneous fat depots of ewes. Although the number of adipocytes remained unaffected by the different WLE treatment levels (P = 0.098), adipocyte diameter increased significantly in response to higher WLE doses (P < 0.001). In both fat depots, ewes receiving WLE75 and WLE100 exhibited markedly larger adipocytes compared with the control and WLE50 groups. A significant tissue effect was observed (P = 0.03), although the treatment × tissue interaction remained non-significant (P = 0.83). Similarly, adipocyte cross-sectional area was significantly affected by WLE administration (P < 0.001). The greatest adipocyte areas were recorded in the WLE100 group, while the smallest areas occurred in the control and WLE50 groups. A significant tissue effect was also detected (P = 0.02), but no significant interaction between treatment and tissue was found (P = 0.33). Morphological analysis showed that intravenous administration of WLE at doses of 50, 75, and 100 mg/kg significantly modified the structure of adipose tissue (Fig. 6). In the control group, adipocytes were smaller, intercellular spaces were enlarged, and cell shape was irregular. With increasing WLE dose, adipocytes became larger, with regular borders and a uniform structure.

Gene expression

The expression of PPARγ, INSR, GLUT4, HSL, Perilipin, TNF-α and IL-6 genes in the WLE-treated groups was analyzed by qRT-PCR, as shown in Fig. 7. The comparative effect of the extract on gene expression between tail fat and subcutaneous adipose tissue is shown in Fig. 8. The gene expression analysis showed that WLE treatments significantly upregulated INSR and GLUT4 genes and downregulated TNF-α genes compared with the control group. Gene expression analysis showed that walnut leaf extract (WLE), especially at the dose of 100 mg (WLE100), significantly increased GLUT4 expression in both subcutaneous and tail adipose tissues compared with the control group, although the differences between tissue types were not statistically significant (Fig. 6, P = 0.057). The expression levels of HSL and IL-6 remained unchanged across all WLE doses and fat depots. In contrast, the INSR expression showed a dose-dependent upregulation. The highest expression was observed in tail fat at WLE100, while in subcutaneous fat WLE50 and WLE75 induced greater expression than in controls. Interestingly, INSR expression at WLE50 was significantly higher in subcutaneous fat than in tail fat; however, overall expression was higher in tail fat (Fig. 6, P < 0.001). Similarly, PPARγ expression at WLE100 was significantly increased in tail fat but showed no significant change in subcutaneous fat. Perilipin expression remained unaffected by WLE treatment in both fat depots. TNF-α, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, showed a significant dose-dependent decrease in expression, with the greatest reduction occurring at WLE100, particularly in the tail fat depots (P < 0.001). These results suggest that WLE, especially at higher doses, may improve insulin sensitivity by upregulating key metabolic genes (GLUT4, INSR and PPARγ) and exert anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing TNF-α expression.

Discussion

Sustainable production of small ruminants fundamentally depends on the efficient utilization of nutrients to maintain optimal animal health, productivity, and resilience under challenging environmental or nutritional conditions (Nehra et al., 2024). While walnut leaf extract has been extensively studied in rodents and human models for its anti-oxidative, anti-inflammatory, and metabolic modulatory effects, its influence on ruminant metabolism, particularly in sheep, has remained largely unexplored. The present study provides novel evidence that intravenous administration of WLE can mitigate several adverse metabolic consequences associated with feed restriction in ewes, offering important insights into its potential as a phytogenic intervention for enhancing ruminant health.

Flavonoids, which constitute the primary bioactive components of WLE, have been shown to regulate feed intake, modulate oxidative stress, and enhance metabolic efficiency in small ruminants (Lucio-Ruíz et al., 2024; Zhuang et al., 2024). In this study, WLE administration at 100 mg/kg BW effectively prevented declines in both BW and BCS during periods of negative energy balance. In contrast, control and lower-dose groups experienced significant reductions in these parameters, indicating that high-dose WLE confers a protective metabolic effect. These outcomes likely reflect the multifaceted action of polyphenolic compounds in improving energy efficiency, reducing excessive mobilization of body reserves, and maintaining redox homeostasis (Piao et al., 2023). The ability of WLE to stabilize BW and BCS aligns with prior evidence demonstrating that phytogenic compounds can support hepatic function, regulate lipid metabolism, and enhance energy utilization in ruminants. For instance, Kazemi and Mokhtarpour (2021) reported that various tree leaves, including walnuts, possess sufficient nutritional potential to meet the requirements of small ruminants during periods of feed scarcity. In the present study, administration of WLE also led to reductions in serum triglyceride and cholesterol concentrations, particularly in the WLE100 group, reinforcing the notion that polyphenolic and phytogenic compounds can beneficially modulate lipid metabolism and metabolic health in ruminants (Piao et al., 2023).

In addition to effects on lipid and energy metabolism, ewes receiving WLE100 exhibited significantly lower BUN concentrations. Wang et al. (2021a) reported that in Hu lactating sheep, BUN content is strongly influenced by protein intake, with reductions in dietary protein associated with decreased BUN levels at similar energy intakes. These observations suggest that WLE may influence protein turnover or rumen–liver nitrogen cycling under feed-restricted conditions (Reynolds & Kristensen., 2008). Concurrently, serum NEFA concentrations decreased in WLE-treated animals. Circulating NEFA predominantly reflects triglyceride mobilization from adipose tissue and, under fasting conditions, may also reflect dietary fat composition (Tor et al., 2021). Although reduced NEFA could indicate improved metabolic efficiency or decreased adipose lipolysis (Contreras & Sordillo, 2011), its interpretation should be made cautiously, as lower NEFA levels do not inherently imply a beneficial effect. Nevertheless, NEFA remains a valuable biomarker for monitoring lipid mobilization and metabolic status in ruminants (LeBlanc, 2010), warranting further studies to clarify the functional significance of these changes.

Another pivotal observation in this study was the increase in plasma insulin concentrations in WLE-treated ewes. Insulin plays a critical role in regulating lipolysis and fat mobilization, effectively suppressing NEFA release from subcutaneous adipose tissue (Lafontan et al., 2005). The observed insulin elevation may partially explain the reduced NEFA levels, reflecting enhanced insulin sensitivity and a modulatory effect on adipose tissue metabolism. Several studies support the influence of walnut leaves on glucose-insulin dynamics. Mukarram et al. (2024) reported that walnut leaves enhance glycemic control, potentially by improving carbohydrate absorption. Similarly, Ansari et al. (2023) demonstrated that bioactive compounds in walnut leave stimulate insulin secretion from activated pancreatic β-cells. Mohammadi et al. (2011) further highlighted that WLE enhances glucose uptake in vitro and inhibits protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B), which negatively regulates insulin signaling, providing a potential mechanism for improved insulin activity. Oxidative stress is a critical factor in the development of insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), primarily through the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and pro-inflammatory mediators, which impair insulin secretion and promote a self-perpetuating cycle of metabolic dysfunction (Weinberg Sibony et al., 2024). In line with these mechanisms, the present findings indicate that WLE, through its high content of flavonoids and phenolic acids, exerts potent anti-oxidative and anti-inflammatory effects (Pereira et al., 2007) that may contribute to improved insulin sensitivity. Dai et al. (2024), and Shabbir et al. (2024) have demonstrated that walnut leaves can modulate pro-inflammatory metabolic pathways and scavenge free radicals, further supporting their potential role in mitigating oxidative stress-induced metabolic disturbances. Mirzababaei et al. (2022) identified 3- and 5-caffeoylquinic acids and quercetin as key components in walnut leaves, which significantly decreased fasting blood glucose and increased fasting insulin levels. These effects are hypothesized to be mediated by antioxidative mechanisms, given the central role of inflammation and innate immune activation in T2DM pathogenesis (Shabbir et al., 2024).

Moravej et al. (2016) proposed that the glucose-lowering effects of walnut leaves may involve the mimicry of pancreatic β-cell activity, leading to enhanced insulin release and reduced intestinal carbohydrate absorption. Samei et al. (2024) further confirmed that oral WLE can improve glycemic control via multiple mechanisms, including inhibition of PTP1B, increased peripheral glucose uptake, and α-amylase suppression. Together, these findings provide a robust in vitro and in vivo rationale for the traditional use of walnut leaf preparations in managing elevated blood glucose levels. Despite extensive rodent and human studies, comparable research in ruminants has been lacking. Sun et al. (2020) reported that administration of walnut oil-derived polyunsaturated fatty acids improved glucose and lipid metabolism, mitigated oxidative stress, and reduced insulin resistance in rats with gestational diabetes. Ali and Zeb (2024) also highlighted the beneficial effects of walnut oil on lipid profiles. In ruminant models, Jamali Emam Gheise et al. (2018) demonstrated that dietary walnut meal supplementation during prepartum and postpartum periods lowered NEFA concentrations in high-producing dairy cows, suggesting a potential role for walnut-derived products in regulating lipid mobilization. Collectively, these findings underscore the strong antioxidant capacity of WLE and its potential to modulate lipid peroxidation and β-oxidation in adipose tissues, thereby contributing to metabolic stability and health in feed-restricted ruminants.

This study uniquely employed intravenous WLE administration in ewes, revealing for the first time its systemic antioxidant and metabolic effects in obese ruminants. Unlike dietary supplementation, intravenous infusion bypasses ruminal degradation, protecting polyphenolic compounds and enhancing their bioavailability. This approach allowed the observation of mechanisms such as the reduction of dyslipidemia and oxidative damage (Manach et al., 2004). WLE administration was associated with favorable changes in oxidative stress biomarkers, including decreased MDA concentrations (Cordiano et al., 2023). Daddam et al. (2023) reported that plant polyphenols may improve adipose tissue resilience under stress by activating the Nrf2-mediated antioxidant response, corroborating our findings. In addition, serum analysis indicated elevated insulin and total antioxidant capacity (TAC), supporting the conclusion that intravenous WLE can enhance redox status and overall metabolic health in ewes (Sordillo & Aitken, 2009).

The intravenous glucose tolerance test (ivGTT) provided additional evidence of WLE’s metabolic impact. Ewes treated with WLE100 exhibited lower peak glucose concentrations and reduced glucose area under the curve (AUC) during the first 60 and 180 min, accompanied by higher post-infusion insulin levels (Table 6, Fig. 3). Although WLE100 resulted in higher initial peak glucose concentrations, glucose declined more rapidly thereafter, leading to a reduced overall AUC. These results contrast with studies in transition dairy cows supplemented with plant-derived oilseeds, where glucose peaks were not substantially affected (Hashemzadeh-Cigari et al., 2015). The enhanced glucose clearance observed here likely reflects upregulated peripheral glucose uptake resulting from prolonged WLE infusion, despite higher insulin responses (Saltiel & Kahn, 2001). Furthermore, NEFA concentrations declined sharply following glucose infusion, consistent with the anti-lipolytic action of insulin. Notably, WLE significantly modulated NEFA responses during ivGTT (590 vs. 260 ± 70 μEq/L for CON and WLE100, respectively; p < 0.001), indicating maximal suppression of lipolysis due to the insulin response (Jahani-Azizabadi et al., 2022).

Histological analysis of adipose tissues revealed that WLE dose-dependently influenced adipocyte morphology in both fat tail and subcutaneous depots. The highest dose (WLE100) reduced adipocyte number while increasing diameter and area (Table 6, Fig. 5), suggesting lipid reaccumulation and attenuated lipolytic activity. Adipocyte hypertrophy may represent a compensatory response to suppressed lipolysis, potentially mediated by antioxidant-induced feedback on AMPK and hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL) signaling (Lafontan & Langin, 2009). High doses of polyphenols can inhibit lipolysis by neutralizing ROS, which serve as critical signaling molecules in lipid mobilization (Wang et al., 2021b). Tannins and flavonoids in WLE appear to exert dose-dependent dual effects, stimulating lipolysis at low concentrations while attenuating it at higher doses through catecholamine signaling interference (Park et al., 2023). Liu et al. (2025) further reported that lower WLE doses enhance adipose browning gene expression (UCP1, PGC-1α), whereas higher doses suppress these effects, highlighting regional depot-specific sensitivity. Tail fat exhibited a more pronounced reduction in adipocyte number, consistent with previous observations of depot-specific regulation of lipid metabolism (Lafontan et al., 2005).

At the molecular level, WLE significantly upregulated INSR and GLUT4 expression in adipose tissues without affecting HSL, Perilipin, or IL-6. These findings indicate that WLE enhances glucose uptake by modulating insulin receptor and GLUT4 expression. Phenolic constituents such as flavonoids and chlorogenic acid can inhibit PTP1B activity, thereby enhancing insulin signaling and glucose uptake (Ali & Zeb., 2024; Sheng et al., 2021; Tahtah et al., 2016; Sohail et al., 2022). Future studies are warranted to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms underlying WLE-mediated effects on lipolysis, energy metabolism, and insulin sensitivity. Collectively, these findings suggest that WLE can act as an effective metabolic modulator during periods of energy restriction, although further research with larger sample sizes, extended experimental durations, and detailed physiological measurements is required to confirm these effects and elucidate the underlying mechanisms. Investigation of alternative administration routes and combinations with dietary strategies may provide additional insights into the translational potential of WLE in ruminant nutrition.

Conclusion

Intravenous administration of walnut leaf extract (WLE) in feed‑restricted ewes improved metabolic health, oxidative status, and insulin sensitivity through enhanced antioxidant capacity, modulated glucose–insulin dynamics, and favorable alterations in adipocyte morphology and the expression of INSR and GLUT4, with the strongest responses observed at 100 mg/kg BW. These findings indicate that WLE may serve as a promising metabolic modulator to support ruminant resilience under energy restriction. To advance the translational application of WLE in ruminant physiology, future studies should investigate the circulating profiles of flavonoid conjugates following IV administration, characterize rumen fermentation dynamics during feed restriction and under parenteral delivery of plant‑derived bioactives, and evaluate long‑term implications of WLE use—particularly potential effects on reproductive performance and overall health. Larger sample sizes, extended experimental periods, and more detailed physiological and molecular assessments will further strengthen the understanding of WLE’s mechanisms and practical relevance.
