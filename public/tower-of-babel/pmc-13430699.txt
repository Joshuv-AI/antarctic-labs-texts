Emerging agents and regimens for myeloproliferative neoplasms: updates from ASH 2025 Annual Meeting
Authors: Tiantian Zhang, Heng Jiang, Delong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Current Myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) therapies provide meaningful symptom and event-risk control but are limited by infrequent molecular remissions, treatment-limiting cytopenias (particularly anemia), and continued reliance on non–mutation-directed cytoreduction or phlebotomy with attendant morbidity. At ASH 2025 Annual Meeting, multiple investigational strategies aimed to address these limitations, including clone-directed targeting (INCA033989), phlebotomy-sparing physiologic therapy (rusfertide), and epigenetic disease-modifying combinations (pelabresib). We summarized the latest updates on these emerging agents and regimens for MPNs from the 2025 ASH Annual Meeting.

To the editor

Current therapies for myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) provide meaningful control of symptoms and thrombotic risk; however, they remain limited by infrequent molecular remissions, treatment-limiting cytopenias, particularly anemia, and continued reliance on non–mutation-directed cytoreduction or phlebotomy, both of which carry cumulative morbidity. At the 2025 American Society of Hematology (ASH) Annual Meeting, several investigational approaches were presented to address these challenges, including mutation-directed targeting (INCA033989), iron modulation therapy (rusfertide), and epigenetic combination therapy (pelabresib), collectively signaling a shift toward more disease-modifying approaches.

INCA033989: toward mutation-targeted therapy in CALR-mutant MPN

INCA033989 is an Fc-silenced IgG1 antibody engineered to selectively bind mutant calreticulin (CALR exon 9) in complex with the thrombopoietin receptor, thereby interrupting oncogenic signaling. In a phase 1 study of patients with CALR-mutant essential thrombocythemia (ET), no dose-limiting toxicities were observed, and hematologic responses were rapid and frequent, allowing many patients to discontinue background cytoreductive therapy. Notably, approximately 90% of evaluable patients demonstrated reductions in mutant allele burden, with correlative analyses suggesting selective depletion of CALR-mutant progenitors, an effect not typically observed with conventional therapies [1].

In myelofibrosis (MF), INCA033989 demonstrated spleen volume reductions (SVR25 37%, SVR35 30% at week 24), accompanied by anemia improvements and concordant decrease in variant allele frequency (VAF). Preliminary combination data with ruxolitinib further supported the benefit of additional therapy, with encouraging spleen responses and VAF reductions [2]. Patients with MF exhibited higher baseline CALR VAF compared to those with ET, while molecular responses (MRs) were more frequent in ET [3]. These findings raise questions regarding optimal integration of CALR-targeted therapies within current treatment landscape. Given prior reports that ruxolitinib may attenuate T-cell function [4], further investigation is needed to determine optimal sequencing and combination strategies (Table 1).

Rusfertide: sustained hematocrit control and reduced phlebotomy burden in PV

Rusfertide, a hepcidin mimetic, targets iron metabolism that underlies polycythemia vera (PV). Earlier phase 2 REVIVE data showed that rusfertide substantially reduced phlebotomy requirements and maintained hematocrit control, with a randomized-withdrawal response in 60% of patients receiving rusfertide versus 17% receiving placebo [5]. The phase 3 VERIFY trial reported also durable phlebotomy avoidance and hematocrit control through week 52 in a larger cohort. In Part 1b (weeks 32–52), most patients receiving rusfertide maintained durable absence of phlebotomy eligibility through week 52, while crossover patients demonstrated marked improvement. Median time to first phlebotomy was not reached, and mean hematocrit remained consistently below 43% [6] (Table 2).

Longer-term data from the THRIVE extension study showed sustained efficacy, with near elimination of phlebotomy requirements—decreasing from approximately 9.2 annually pre-study to < 1 per year on treatment. Hematocrit control remained stable over extended follow-up, and thrombotic events were infrequent. The safety profile was favorable, characterized by primarily low-grade injection-site reactions and manageable anemia [7]. While these findings highlight the potential of rusfertide as a phlebotomy-sparing strategy, its long-term impact on thrombosis, clonal evolution, and overall survival remains to be defined [8].

Pelabresib plus ruxolitinib: epigenetic augmentation of JAK inhibition in MF

The phase 3 MANIFEST-2 trial evaluated pelabresib in combination with ruxolitinib in JAK inhibitor–naïve MF. Updated 96-week data demonstrated sustained clinical benefit. In the intention-to-treat population, SVR35 rates remained higher with combined therapy compared with ruxolitinib alone (45.3% vs. 30.1%), with durable symptom improvement and greater rates of combined spleen and symptom responses (31.8% vs. 15.7%) [9]. Hemoglobin responses were modestly improved, and transfusion requirements were numerically lower (33.1% vs. 39.9%) [9]. Safety profiles were broadly comparable, although discontinuation due to adverse events was more frequent in the combination arm. These findings suggest that epigenetic modulation may enhance the depth and durability of JAK inhibitor responses. Supporting evidence from other ruxolitinib-based combinations, including XPO1 inhibitors and additional BET inhibitors such as BMS-986,158, further supports multi-pathway targeted strategies [10].

Other highlighted agents and regimens at ASH 2025

Sapablursen (ISIS 702843), a TMPRSS6 antisense inhibitor, reduced phlebotomy requirements and improved hematocrit control with symptom improvement in PV [11]. In ET, the phase 3 extension Shorespan-017 reported longer-term tolerability of bomedemstat, an LSD1 inhibitor, with promising treatment continuation rate and a manageable safety profile [12]. The IMproveMF phase 1b trial is evaluating imetelstat with ruxolitinib in MF, focusing on symptom improvement and spleen responses [13].

Conclusion

Collectively, these emerging data suggest that MPN management may be entering a transitional phase—from mutation-agnostic cytoreduction toward more biologically informed, potentially disease-modifying strategies. A key limitation of this report is that much of the evidence summarized was preliminary and presented in abstract form, with limited long-term follow up, survival, and safety data. Challenges still remain, including defining optimal treatment sequencing and combination strategies, identifying clinical and molecular biomarkers of benefit, and determining whether early hematologic or molecular responses translate into reduced thrombosis, delayed disease progression, and improved survival.
