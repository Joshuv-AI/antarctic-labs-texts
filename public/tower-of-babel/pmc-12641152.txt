Comparative Evaluation of Glycaemic and Non‐Glycaemic Factors as Determinants of Retinopathy Status in an Adult Population: A Case‐Control Study
Authors: Moses Banyeh, Raymond Buadee, Amy Louisa Anaman, Felix Ofori, Baba Madde Mohammed, Marian Griffin, Emmanuel Kofi Mensah, Gloria Adutwumwaa Sarpong, Cornelius Elikem Fiawotso, Solomon Aweworo Wepia, Abdul Salam Abdul Fattah, Ernest Kofi Annan, Cornelius Nifaa, Emmanuel Ansah Owusu, Albert Obeng, Prince Kwaku Ayisi, Charles Nkansah, Gabriel Abbam, Joshua Anzagra Dery, Benjamin N Mayeem, Clement Binwatin Dagungong, Amos Tuoyintir Yelvielledem, Nafiu Amidu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsHyperglycemia is a known risk factor for retinopathy. With diabetes cases expected to increase from 366 million in 2011 to 552 million by 2030, the global burden of retinopathy may rise. Given this observation, more attention is focused on glycaemic determinants of retinopathy to the neglect of non‐glycaemic factors, particularly in Ghana. However, genetic and environmental variations may highlight the importance of non‐glycaemic factors as determinants of retinopathy.MethodsThis case‐control study was conducted between January and August 2024 at the Emmanuel Eye Medical Center in Accra, Ghana. The study involved 160 participants aged between 34 and 81 years. Of the 160 participants, 80 (50%) were diagnosed with retinopathy. Fasting venous blood samples were collected and analyzed for glycaemic factors, including fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and non‐glycaemic factors, including fasting lipids and complete blood count indices.ResultsResults showed that the FBG (8.4 ± 2.6 vs. 6.3 ± 1.6, p < 0.001) and HbA1c (6.3 ± 1.6 vs. 6.3 ± 1.4, p < 0.001) levels were higher in the retinopathy. After adjusting for age and BMI, persons with hyperglycemia had about four‐fold greater odds of developing retinopathy [AOR = 4.378 (95% CI: 2.212–8.666)]. In comparison, persons with poor glycaemic control had about two‐fold greater odds of developing retinopathy [AOR = 2.499 (1.298–4.813)]. Only FBG and HbA1c reached statistical significance in operator characteristics curve analysis, with the area under the curve, sensitivity, and specificity at 0.75, 87.5, 56.3 and 0.79, 76.3, and 78.8, respectively. A significant sex difference between sex and retinopathy was only observed in the red blood cell count.ConclusionGlycaemic factors are more strongly associated with retinopathy than non‐glycaemic factors. Early screening should prioritize glycaemic factors while considering non‐glycaemic factors due to genetic and environmental variations in retinopathy development.Keywords: determinants, Ghana, glycaemic, retinopathy, sex differences

Introduction

In 2020 alone, the number of blind people was estimated to be 43.3 million, with a prevalence of 5.49 per 1000 population [1, 2]. It is projected that the number of people who will be affected by blindness globally will increase to 61.0 million by 2050. Low‐ and middle‐income countries contribute the majority of the global burden of blindness since it has been estimated that the crude prevalence of blindness ranges from 1.49/1000 in North America to 8.75/1000 in Southeast Asia, while the age‐standardized prevalence of blindness ranges from 1.24/1000 in North America to 11.1/1000 in Western Sub‐Saharan Africa [1]. Reports from the Ghana Health Service indicate that about 227,920 people are blind in Ghana, with an additional 340,000 people experiencing severe visual impairment [3].

Diabetes mellitus, characterized by hyperglycemia, is one of the major contributors to the global burden of blindness. A study showed that the number of people living with diabetes in Sub‐Saharan Africa will increase from 19.4 million in 2019 to 47.1 million in 2045. Several determinants of blindness and visual impairments exist, including age‐related macular degeneration, uncorrected cataract, glaucoma, and retinopathy [4]. Retinopathy is a retinal microvascular abnormality and can be classified into early‐onset (retinopathy of prematurity, occurring in children) and late‐onset forms [5]. Although retinopathy may be associated with several factors, diabetic retinopathy (DR) is the most widely known and studied form of retinopathy [6]. Because diabetes is a major risk factor for retinopathy, it is inferred that the prevalence of DR will continue to rise globally and in sub‐Saharan Africa [7]. DR is the leading cause of vision loss among both the working‐age and elderly populations [8].

Anti‐vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), laser, and vitrectomy remain standard treatments for vision‐threatening posterior eye disease, but noninvasive, plant‐based alternatives are gaining attention as preventive strategies for early retinopathy. Among these, polyphenols and carotenoids, particularly lutein and zeaxanthin, which accumulate in the macula, show strong potential in mitigating oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, angiogenesis, and visual deficits, offering safe, low‐cost, and accessible options [9]. However, just like any other disease, researchers try to use various indices and biomarkers to determine its development for early diagnosis and intervention [10]. Aside from sociodemographic and anthropometric data, laboratory and blood‐based investigations form most of the biomarkers for the prediction of retinopathy. These blood‐based variables are broadly categorized into glycaemic and non‐glycaemic variables. The former includes fasting blood glucose (FBG) and glycated hemoglobin, while the latter includes fasting plasma lipids, indices of liver and renal function, and hematological indices [11, 12].

Given that the prevalence of diabetes has increased over the years due to urbanization, ageing population, lifestyle changes, and air pollution, much attention is paid to the glycaemic determinants of retinopathy, such as hyperglycemia and glycated hemoglobin. However, there is increasing evidence that retinopathy is not only related to glycaemic but also non‐glycaemic factors [12, 13]. A cohort study involving 3402 persons who were nondiabetic at baseline showed that about 7.3% of them developed retinopathy at the end of the study [14]. In yet another study in a Japanese population, the prevalence of retinopathy in people with a history of diabetes and those without was 23.3% and 4.2%, respectively. Moreover, the age‐standardized prevalence of all vision impairments, as well as retinopathy, is reported to be higher in females than in similarly aged men [1, 15].

Previous studies in Ghana regarding retinopathy have largely either focused on DR treatment services in Ghana, the utilization of health services by persons with diabetes for retinopathy, or incidence and risk factors of retinopathy of prematurity [16, 17, 18]. The identification of biomarkers is most important, first for the early detection of diseases, second to assess prognosis, and third to assess the response to response. Despite the potential association of non‐glycaemic factors with retinopathy, little to no attention, to the best of my knowledge, has been given to them in Ghana. In addition, the possible sex differences in the determinants of retinopathy have been least explored. Moreover, as far as is known, no known study in Tamale has explored the potential of non‐glycaemic factors as determinants of retinopathy. The identification of possible non‐glycaemic determinants of retinopathy and possible sex differences will not only offer new insights into potential biomarkers. Nevertheless, they also reveal sex‐specific strategies for the differential detection and management of retinopathy.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

This case‐control study was conducted between January and August 2024 at the Emmanuel Eye Medical Center in the Greater Accra region. The study included 160 participants, comprising 80 persons diagnosed with retinopathy as cases and 80 healthy persons without any vision‐related condition as controls. The study participants were between 34 and 81 years old and comprised 67 males (41.9%) and 93 (58.1%) females. Among the 80 control subjects, 30 (37.5%) were male, while 50 (62.5%) were female. Similarly, among the 80 cases, 37 (46.3%) were male, and the remaining 43 (53.8%) were female. Retinopathy was defined as a range of conditions impacting the retina, characterized by damage to retinal blood vessels, retinal swelling, or abnormal blood vessel growth, often leading to blurred vision, vision loss, or blindness. A board‐certified ophthalmologist diagnosed retinopathy. To select the participants, the register of the clinic was used. Persons who were confirmed to have retinopathy within the study period were contacted to participate in the study as cases. Similarly, persons who attended the clinic within the study period but were not found to have retinopathy or any major eye pathology were also contacted to participate in the study as the controls. Individuals with comorbid vision‐related diseases and a known history of chronic conditions such as diabetes and hypertension were excluded from the study. All participants underwent ophthalmologic examination following recommended national and international guidelines and standards [19]. A standard external examination, anterior segment evaluation, and fundus assessment were conducted. Pupil dilation was achieved using 1% compound tropicamide, administered 3–4 times at 10‐min intervals before the examination. Fundus imaging was performed using the RetCam wide‐field digital imaging system (Clarity Medical System, Pleasanton, California, USA). Digital images of the posterior pole, as well as the superior, nasal, and temporal retinal fields, were captured and assessed for ocular findings.

Laboratory Analysis

Sociodemographic data, including age, sex, educational level, religious affiliation, smoking, and alcohol consumption status, were collected from all participants using an interviewer‐administered questionnaire. The body weight and standing height were measured while the participants were barefoot and in light clothing to the nearest 0.1 kg and cm, respectively. The body mass index (BMI) was then calculated as the ratio of the weight in kilograms and the square of the height in meters: BMI [weight/(height)2]. Blood pressure was measured, after rest, on two separate occasions using a mercuric sphygmomanometer and averaged. Blood pressure was then categorized according to the new ACC/AHA hypertension guidelines [20] into normal (< 120 mmHg SBP and < 80 mmHg DBP), stage 1 hypertension (130–139 SBP or 80–89 mmHg DBP), and stage 2 hypertension (≥ 140 mmHg SBP or ≥ 90 mmHg DBP). As part of the laboratory investigations, a single venous blood sample was collected after an 8 to 10‐h fast. The blood sample was then dispensed into an ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid‐anticoagulant, sodium citrate antiglycolytic, and gel‐separator vacutainer tubes. Hematological indices were determined within an hour of blood collection by analyzing the anticoagulated blood on the Norma Icon‐5 automated hematology analyzer (Elitech Group, Luxembourg, Belgium). The anticoagulated blood was also used to determine the glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level. After clotting at 4°C–8°C, the blood in the gel‐separator tubes, together with the antiglycolytic tubes, was centrifuged for 10 min at 1500 rpm to obtain serum or plasma. The serum or plasma samples were then aliquoted into Eppendorf tubes and stored at −25°C until laboratory analysis without thawing and refreezing. Serum fasting lipids and FBG levels were determined using a BT 1500 automated biochemistry analyzer (Biotechnica Instruments, SPA, Italy). The participants were categorized based on the laboratory results. Hyperglycemia was defined as FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L. HbA1c was categorized as good glycaemic control ≤ 7% and poor (> 7%) [21, 22]. Dyslipidaemia was defined based on the NCEP‐ATP III criteria [23] as follows: triglycerides (TG) ≥ 2.26, low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) ≥ 4.14, high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) < 1.04, and total cholesterol (TC) ≥ 6.22 mmol/L. Anemia was defined according to the WHO guidelines as a hemoglobin level < 12 g/dL for females or 13 g/dL for males [24].

Statistical Analysis

The data were collected on an MS Excel Spreadsheet, sorted, cleaned, and then analyzed using SPSS (v27) and MedCalc statistical packages. The normality of the quantitative data was determined using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. The categorical data were summarized as frequency (%), while the quantitative data were summarized as mean ± standard deviation. Bivariate analysis using a Student's t‐test was performed to determine the differences in the data distribution between two groups for quantitative variables. The association between retinopathy and predictor categorical variables was determined using both the Chi‐Square/Fisher's exact and logistic regression analysis. To determine possible interactions between sex and retinopathy on glycaemic and non‐glycaemic variables, a two‐way interaction term (sex × retinopathy) was computed by multiplication. Since both sex and retinopathy were categorical variables, mean centering to reduce collinearity was not required. Multivariable linear regression models were created with the glycaemic and non‐glycaemic variables as the dependent variables. The independent variables comprising sex, retinopathy, and the interaction term were entered into the model. The age, BMI, and blood pressure variables were also entered as covariates to minimize possible confounding. To graphically display interactions between sex and retinopathy on a predictor variable, the dependent variable's unstandardized predicted value (PRE) was saved and then plotted on the y‐axis against retinopathy on the x‐axis with sex as the marking variable (Figure 1). The assumptions of multivariable linear regression were tested using the P–P plot for normality, the Durbin–Watson test for autocorrelation, the variance inflation factor for collinearity, Cook's and Mahalanobis distance for outliers, and the standardized residual versus standardized predicted value scatter plot for homoscedasticity. The receiver operator characteristics curve (ROC) analysis was performed by adopting the Hanley–McNeil technique to determine the predictive abilities of the glycaemic and non‐glycaemic variables for retinopathy. All the statistical analyses were two‐sided, and significance was considered at a p‐value of less than 0.050.

Ethical Approval

The study was approved by the Protocol and Ethical Review Committee of Baldwin University College (ECC‐22023326). The 1964 Declaration of Helsinki or its later amendments regarding human subject studies were followed in conducting this study. All participants provided written informed consent before they were enrolled in the study. The study was voluntary and was not restricted by one's sex, religion, political, or cultural affiliations or beliefs.

Results

Variations Between the Retinopathy and the Control Groups

The associations between retinopathy and sociodemographic variables are shown in Table 1. There were no significant associations between retinopathy and socio‐demographic variables, including sex, BMI, smoking, and alcohol consumption, as well as religious affiliations and educational status. After adjusting for age in the multivariable analysis, no significant association was observed. Table 2 summarizes and compares anthropometric, glycaemic, and non‐glycaemic variables between the retinopathy and control groups. It was observed that persons with retinopathy were characterized by higher levels of FBG (8.4 ± 2.6 vs. 6.3 ± 1.6, p < 0.001) and glycated hemoglobin levels (9.0 ± 2.7 vs. 6.3 ± 1.4, p < 0.001). The variables were categorized based on the recommended definitions (Table 3). Retinopathy showed a significant association with hyperglycemia ( χ 2 = 21.558, p < 0.001) as well as poor glycaemic control ( χ 2 = 7.226, p = 0.01). After controlling for age and BMI, persons with hyperglycemia had about four‐fold greater odds of being diagnosed with retinopathy compared to non‐hyperglycaemic persons [AOR = 4.045 (95% CI: 2.002–8.171)]. Similarly, persons with poor glycaemic control had about two‐fold greater odds of being diagnosed with retinopathy when compared to those with good glycaemic control [AOR = 2.657 (95% CI: 1.334–5.293)].

Sex Differences

Sex differences in the relationship between retinopathy and those of glycaemic and non‐glycaemic variables were assessed using moderated linear regression with interaction effects, as shown in Tables 4 and 5 as well as Figure 1. After adjusting for multiple covariates, there was still a significant relationship between retinopathy and glycaemic variables, that is, FBG and HbA1c (p < 0.001), but not between non‐glycaemic biochemical (Table 4) and hematological (Table 5) variables. No statistically significant interactions between sex and retinopathy were observed for glycaemic variables. However, a significant interaction was observed in the RBC count (p = 0.03), such that the RBC count was higher in retinopathy than in controls for males and vice‐versa for females (Figure 1). The assumptions of multivariable linear regression were tested for the model where RBC was the dependent variable for autocorrelation (Table S1), collinearity (Table S2), Cook's and Mahalanobis distance for outliers (Table S3), P–P plot for normality (Figure S1), and scatter plot for homoscedasticity (Figure S2).

ROC Analyses of Glycaemic and Non‐Glycaemic Factors

The predictive abilities of the glycaemic and non‐glycaemic variables for retinopathy are compared in Table 6 and graphically presented in Figures 2, 3, 4. The area under the curve (AUC) of the non‐glycaemic variables ranged from 0.50 to 0.55, corresponding to MCV and HDL, respectively. However, the AUC of the glycaemic variables for predicting retinopathy was 0.75 for FBG and 0.79 for glycated hemoglobin. The sensitivity and specificity were 87.5% and 56.2% for FBG, and those for glycated hemoglobin were 76.3% and 78.8%, respectively.

Discussions

This study sought to compare the biochemical status of glycaemic and non‐glycaemic factors as contributing factors with relevance in patients who are clinically confirmed to have retinopathy, and examine potential sex differences in the relationship between retinopathy and these factors. Results showed that, unlike the non‐glycaemic group, the glycaemic variables, that is, FBG and glycated hemoglobin, were significantly higher in the retinopathy group than in the control group. When the FBG and glycated hemoglobin levels were categorized, hyperglycemia and poor glycaemic control were positively associated with retinopathy, even after adjusting for covariates. Similarly, only FBG and glycated hemoglobin achieved statistical significance when predicting retinopathy, outperforming the non‐glycaemic variables. However, a statistically significant interaction between sex and retinopathy on RBC was observed, such that the RBC count was higher in retinopathy than in controls in males and vice versa in females.

It was observed in this study that retinopathy was significantly associated with glycaemic factors, which were also better predictors of the disease than the non‐glycaemic factors, independent of age, BMI, and blood pressure. Hyperglycemia is significantly associated with retinopathy, as reported by previous studies [25, 26, 27].

Retinopathy is usually characterized by microvascular damage, and hyperglycemia may be involved in this process, as it is implicated in many metabolic pathways, including the protein kinase C (PKC) pathway, polyol pathway, hexosamine pathway, and accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) [28]. One possible mechanism by which diabetes may affect retinal health is the metabolic autoregulation of the retina, where there is an increase in blood flow in the retinal vessels in response to hyperglycemia in an attempt to increase retinal metabolism. However, this metabolic autoregulation causes changes in retinal blood flow, thereby affecting retinal function [28]. Another possible explanation for retinopathy in diabetes is pericyte loss through apoptosis. Pericytes are crucial in maintaining the integrity of the capillary walls. The loss of pericytes may lead to an increase in the permeability of the retinal capillary walls and can lead to microaneurysm formation in the retina, thereby affecting retinal function [28].

The Müller glia on the capillary walls are also affected, affecting the active transport mechanisms of the Müller glia‐capillary interphase, thereby causing swelling of the Müller glia in DR [26]. In addition, Müller glia can activate microglia through P2X7 purinergic receptors that may destabilize neural‐vascular interactions in the retinal neurovascular unit, which can lead to vascular damage, neuroinflammation, and possible leakage [26]. In addition to pericyte loss, impairment of the blood‐retinal barrier due to apoptosis of endothelial cells and thickening of the basement membrane is another hallmark of DR [25, 26, 27]. Furthermore, the apoptosis of endothelial cells and the loss of pericytes eventually lead to capillary occlusion, leading to ischemia or hypoxia in the retina. The ischemic retinal environment may eventually cause upregulation of anti‐VEGF through activation of hypoxia‐inducible factor 1. In addition to ischemia/hypoxia, VEGF may also be upregulated in response to the elevation of phospholipase A2 under hyperglycaemic conditions [28]. In addition, VEGF, a crucial factor in the progression of proliferative DR and diabetic macular edema, is thought to increase vascular permeability by triggering the phosphorylation of tight junction proteins, such as occludin and zonula occludens‐1. Additionally, as an angiogenic factor, VEGF stimulates endothelial cell proliferation by activating mitogen‐activated proteins. Elevated levels of VEGF have been observed in the vitreous fluid of patients with proliferative DR and diabetic macular edema [28].

In addition to VEGF, other angiogenic factors implicated in DR for increasing retinal vascular permeability include angiopoietins 1 and 2, which are known to achieve this by interacting with the endothelial receptor tyrosine kinase Tie2 [28]. The kinin–kallikrein system has also been suggested as a possible alternative neurovascular pathway that can lead to retinal leakage, whereby the cleavage of kininogen by plasma kallikrein generates bradykinin, which acts on the blood vessels to induce permeability through bradykinin receptors [26].

The neuronal function of the retina may also be altered in DR. It has been noted that there is a gradual degeneration of the retina, as observed in the thinning of the nerve fibers and ganglion cell layers due to apoptosis of nonvascular cells, even without observable retinal vascular pathology [26]. Reduced electrical response and contrast sensitivity are additional neuronal functions that may occur in DR before any visible vascular pathology [26].

Poor glycaemic control was a significant determinant of retinopathy, which is consistent with the findings of an African meta‐analysis [21]. It has been observed in diabetic patients that AGEs are found in retinal vessels, and their levels correlate with the severity of retinopathy and serum. Through the Polyol pathway, excess glucose in diabetes is converted to sorbitol by aldose reductase and further converted to fructose by sorbitol dehydrogenase and onwards to fructose‐3‐phosphate and deoxyglucosone, which are potent glycating agents. The activities of these glycating agents lead to the nonenzymatic formation of AGEs through the Mallard reaction [27, 29]. AGEs not only correlate with disease severity but also participate actively in the progression of retinopathy as they promote inflammation and neurodegenerative processes as well as the activation of PKC, which may increase vasopermeability and capillary occlusion [27]. In addition, AGEs are involved in the formation of excess reactive oxygen species and subsequent endothelial damage and oxidative stress, as the upregulation of the polyol pathway causes the depletion of NADPH and reduced glutathione, as well as an imbalance in the NADH/NAD+ levels. Moreover, the accumulation of AGEs in diabetes has been demonstrated to correlate with the loss of pericytes, which are crucial for retinal function [29, 30].

Fasting blood sugar and glycated hemoglobin levels could predict retinopathy with a sensitivity and specificity of 87.5% and 56.2%, respectively, for fasting blood sugar and 76.2% and 78.7% respectively, for glycated hemoglobin. A 10‐year cohort study established that FBG levels could predict retinopathy with a sensitivity of 27% and a specificity of 88%. Regarding glycated hemoglobin, the sensitivity and specificity were 19% and 92%, respectively [31]. The sensitivities of fasting blood sugar and glycated hemoglobin are higher than those in Massin et al. [31], although the specificities are comparable. The observed differences may be due to differences in the study design. While a cross‐sectional study may be easier and simpler to undertake, it is less robust than a cohort study [32].

It was observed that there were sex differences in the RBC count between the retinopathy and control groups, where the RBC count was higher in the retinopathy than in the control group in males, but the reverse was seen in females. There is a physiological difference in the RBC count between the sexes, where adult males have a higher count than similarly aged adult females. This sex difference in RBC count is largely attributed to the activity of sex hormones, which have differing effects on haematopoiesis [33]. Testosterone tends to induce erythrocytosis by increasing erythropoietin production and suppressing hepcidin [34]. It is known that sex hormones play a neuroprotective role in the retina and can regulate ocular blood flow. Notably, there are differences between male and female retinas, and sex hormones may affect the likelihood of developing certain disorders [35]. Sex hormones could be involved in the pathophysiology of retinopathy and could account for the observed sex difference in RBC count between the retinopathy and control groups. However, this requires further investigation to validate the findings.

The strength of the current study lies in the fact that most studies on retinopathy in Ghana focus on prevalence, knowledge, and treatment, and rarely do studies on the determinants of retinopathy and their comparative abilities in predicting the disease. Moreover, such studies are not common among indigenous Black African populations. This study provides vital baseline information for further primary or secondary studies, such as systematic reviews and meta‐analyses. However, a cohort study is more suitable for determining early predictors of disease, although it is relatively costly and time‐consuming.

Conclusion

The study showed that glycaemic factors or variables are more strongly associated with retinopathy and are more suggestive of the disease than their non‐glycaemic counterparts. Early screening for retinopathy should therefore give more weight to glycaemic factors without ignoring the non‐glycaemic ones, since population variabilities in genetic and environmental factors can influence the pathophysiology of retinopathy.
