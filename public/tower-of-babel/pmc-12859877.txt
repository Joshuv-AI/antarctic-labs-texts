Targeted blockade of extracellular vesicles-mediated profibrotic vicious circle using EVs-based dual-drug delivery system to prevent liver fibrosis
Authors: Jie Sun, Yi Shen, Xiaoze Wang, Ruoting Men, Xian Xing, Linling Lv, Fan Yang, Mengyi Shen, Yanyi Zheng, Xiaoli Fan, Jingping Liu, Li Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Liver fibrosis, caused by various chronic liver injuries, is one of the major causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, but there is no efficient treatments in clinic until now. Cell-cell interactions in the injured liver microenvironment play critical roles in hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) activation and excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, but the exact mechanism involved and specific therapies remain elusive. Here, we report that an extracellular vesicles (EVs)-mediated profibrotic vicious circle might contribute to HSCs activation during the early stages of liver fibrosis development. In brief, increased EVs secretion was observed in patients with liver cirrhosis, mice with liver fibrosis, and injured hepatocytes, whereas pharmacological inhibition of EVs secretion partially alleviated liver fibrosis in mice in vivo. However, the injured hepatocytes-derived EVs (IH-EVs) alone only promoted HSCs proliferation but not ECM deposition. The robust activation of HSCs requires the participation of liver macrophages, which can engulf IH-EVs and secrete various pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic factors, thereby sustaining hepatocytes injury and providing costimulation signals to promote HSCs activation and excessive ECM production. Mechanistically, IH-EVs might synergize with macrophages to promote HSCs proliferation by activating the PI3K-AKT and JAK-STAT pathways. We further developed a liver-targeted dual-drug delivery system using normal liver tissue-derived EVs (LT-EVs), which display significant antifibrotic effects in vivo by synergistically suppressing liver EVs secretion and macrophages activation. This study reveals an endogenous EVs-mediated pro-fibrotic mechanism in early liver fibrosis, and provides a potential therapeutic strategy for treating liver fibrosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03940-8.

Introduction

Liver fibrosis is a chronic liver disease caused by various etiologies, such as viral hepatitis, alcoholic hepatitis, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, autoimmune hepatitis, and metabolic disorders [1]. If early profibrotic signals are not efficiently prevented via removal of the etiology and/or timely intervention, it can further progress to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [2]. The pathogenesis of liver fibrosis is complex and multiple processes, such as hepatocyte necrosis, inflammation, oxidative stress, and excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, are involved [3]. The activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs, transition from a quiescent state to myofibroblasts) is a central event in the progression of liver fibrosis, and is regulated by various mediators, such as transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), chemokines, and their ligands, as activated HSCs are the major effector cells of ECM deposition [4]. During HSCs activation, alpha smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), fibronectin (FN) and type I collagen (Col.I) are highly expressed. Despite some possible anti-fibrotic strategies that target a single factor (e.g., inhibition of HSCs activation, inhibition of hepatocyte necrosis, anti-inflammation) have been reported, their clinical efficacy in preventing liver fibrosis is still unsatisfactory [5, 6]. Increasing evidence indicates that the activation status of HSCs is jointly regulated by complicated interactions among multiple cell types (e.g., injured hepatocytes, immune cells and HSCs) in the injured liver microenvironment [6]. Therefore, exploration of the detailed intracellular interactions during liver fibrosis may aid the development of efficient antifibrotic therapeutics.

As cell-derived small vesicles, extracellular vesicles (EVs) play a critical role in regulating intercellular communication by transferring various bioactive molecules (e.g., proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids) between diverse cells and tissues and mediating various physiological and pathological processes in organ tissues [7]. It has been reported that multiple types of EVs in liver play critical roles in the pathologenesis of liver fibrosis through various pathways [8], hepatocytes as the predominant parenchymal cells in the liver, the EVs from which they are derived play a role in liver fibrotic processes [9]. For example, It has been found that hepatocyte-derived EVs could activate fibrogenic activity of HSCs by stimulates ROS production via transferring iron, thereby promoting liver fibrosis [10], and injured hepatocytes-derived EVs (IH-EVs) were also shown to exacerbate fibrosis by activating HSCs via transferring MASP1 protein [11]. Although the possible interactions between injured hepatocytes and HSCs through EVs secretion have been reported, the detailed mechanism by which injured hepatocytes induce HSCs activation during liver fibrosis and efficient treatments to prevent such processes have not been fully clarified.

Here, we report that elevated EVs release from injured hepatocytes, however, these EVs alone only promoted HSCs proliferation. The robust activation of HSCs required the participation of liver macrophages, which can engulf injured hepatocytes-EVs and secrete proinflammatory and profibrotic factors, thereby sustaining hepatocyte injury and providing costimulation signals to promote HSCs activation and excessive ECM production. Conversely, synergistic suppression of hepatocyte EVs release and macrophage activation using liver tissue-derived EVs (LT-EVs)-based dual-drug delivery system showed significant therapeutic potential in preventing liver fibrosis. This study reveals an endogenous EVs-mediated profibrotic loop in liver fibrosis and provides a potential therapeutic strategy for treating liver fibrosis.

Results

Elevated EVs secretion from injured hepatocytes during liver fibrosis

To investigate the alterations in EVs in patients with cirrhosis, we analysed EVs in plasma samples from healthy controls (HC-EVs) and patients with liver cirrhosis (LC-EVs) (Fig. 1A). The isolated HC-EVs and LC-EVs exhibited typical bilayer membrane structures (Fig. 1B), and similar mean particle sizes (Fig. S1A). A significant increase in plasma EVs concentrations (determined using particle numbers or protein amounts) was observed in patients with liver cirrhosis compared with healthy individuals (Fig. 1C; Fig. S1B). These results indicate that plasma EVs in patients with cirrhosis is significantly higher than in normal individuals. It has been reported that EVs can be secreted by various liver cell types and into the extracellular space and circulation [9], and increased levels of circulating or liver EVs have been found in diverse types of liver injury, such as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [12, 13], viral hepatitis [14] and cirrhosis [8]. Notably, the biogenesis and secretion of EVs is tightly controlled by multiple signaling pathways, such as soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor-activating protein receptor (nSMase), neutral sphingomyelinase (Smpd3), and Rab GTPases (e.g., Rab27a, Rab27b) [15, 16], and the changes in these key genes expression of tissue cells can be used to assess their EVs secretion ability. Thus, we detected the changes in the key regulators of EVs secretion, such as Rab27a, Rab27b, and Smpd3, in liver tissues of healthy control (HC) or liver cirrhotic patients (LC). The results showed a significant upregulation of Rab27a and Rab27b, and slightly increased Smpd3 in the liver tissues of LC group compared to HC group (Fig. 1D; Fig. S1C), which indicated the enhanced hepatic EVs secretion in patients with liver cirrhosis. In order to clarify the changes of EVs in the liver during fibrosis, changes in liver tissue derived-EVs (LT-EVs) secretion in CCl4-induced mouse liver fibrosis model were also assayed (Fig. 1E). Livers of injection of CCl4 with 3 weeks-induced mice displayed early liver fibrosis, as indicated by hepatocyte necrosis, extensive inflammatory cell infiltration and collagen deposition, which was consistent with previous report [17] (Fig. S2A, B), and the expression of the fibrotic markers FN, α-SMA was significantly increased (Fig. S2C). The isolated LT-EVs were bilayer vesicles (Fig. 1F; Fig. S3A) and they expressed positive markers (Hsp70, Tsg101) and did not express the negative marker Cyt-C (Fig. 1G). Compared with normal mice, the secretion of LT-EVs increased gradually in fibrosis mice and peaked at 3 to 4 weeks, which further reduced at the later stages (6 weeks) (Fig. 1H). There were no significant differences in the size of LT-EVs across different stages of liver fibrosis (Fig. S3B). The liver expression of Rab27a, a key regulator of EVs biogenesis that promotes the docking of multivesicular bodies (MVBs) with the cell membrane [18], was upregulated in fibrotic mice (Fig. 1I-K).

Although multiple cell types within the liver might be involved in LT-EVs secretion during early liver fibrosis, hepatocytes are the major resident subpopulation of the liver in terms of both numbers and proportion (higher than 65%) [19], suggesting that the majority of LT-EVs might be released from injured hepatocytes during liver fibrosis progression. To verify the findings in vitro, CCl4-induced hepatocyte injury model [20] was further used to clarify the changes in EVs by injured hepatocytes during early liver fibrosis (Fig. 1L). Consistently, the levels of CCl4-EVs were increased (as indciated by EVs particle numbers or EVs protein amounts) compared to control hepatocyte-derived EVs (Ctrl-EVs) (Fig. 1M; Fig. S4A). Ctrl-EVs and CCl4-EVs showed similar vesicle structure, sizes (Fig. 1N; Fig. S4B), as well as protein markers expression (Fig. 1O). Together, these results suggest that injured hepatocyte-derived EVs secretion is significantly elevated during liver fibrosis.

Pharmacological Inhibition of EVs secretion partially alleviated liver fibrosis in mice

To assess the role of EVs secretion in liver fibrosis, we next blocked liver EVs production using an pharmacological inhibitor (GW4869, GW). GW is a neutral sphingomyelinase (N-SMase) inhibitor that can decrease EVs secretion by blocking ceramide-mediated MVBs budding [21]. It has been reported that intraperitoneal injection of GW can inhibit N-SMase and its mediated EV biogenesis [22], thereby alleviating the progression of the disease. In our study, liver fibrosis mice were treated with GW by intraperitoneal injection for 3 weeks (Fig. 2A). GW treatments reduced the numbers of LT-EVs compared to the CCl4 group (Fig. 2B; Fig. S5A), but did not affect the particle sizes of LT-EVs (Fig. S5B). Compared with CCl4-mice group, GW treatments lowered the levels of serum ALT levels (Fig. 2C), pro-inflammatory cells and vacuoles (Fig. 2D), and collagen deposition (Fig. 2E; Fig. S5C). The levels of liver α-SMA+ activated HSCs, and ECM proteins (FN, Col1a1) in CCl4-mice were also significantly decreased after GW treatments, indicating that the degree of liver fibrosis was partially alleviated (Fig. 2F, G; Fig. S5D). It has been reported that GW treatments significantly reduced liver EVs secretion while had minor impact on other organs (e.g., heart, lungs, and kidneys) in mice with cirrhosis [23]. However, GW treatments might affect diverse types of cells and their EVs secretion in liver tissues, despite that hepatocytes are the primary parenchymal cells in the liver. Thus, we further assessd the changes in EVs secretion (CD63) of different cell types, including CK18-positive hepatocytes, F4/80-positive Kupffer cells, and α-SMA-positive HSCs, in liver of fibrotic mice with or without GW treatments. The results showed that the levels of EVs secretion (CD63 signals) were upregulated in hepatocytes, HSCs, or macrophages of fibrotic mice, while CD63 signals of these cell types were decreased after GW treatment, especially in hepatocytes (Fig. 2H). These results indicated that GW treatments might affect EVs secretion in diverse cell types of fibrotic liver, in which hepatocytes were prominently affected. Together, pharmacological inhibition of EVs secretion partially alleviated liver fibrosis, and hepatocytes-derived EVs might play a role in the pathology of liver fibrosis.

Injured hepatocyte-derived EVs alone is sufficient to promote HSCs proliferation

HSCs are the major effector cells in liver fibrosis, which can transition from a quiescent state (~ 10% of resident liver cells) to an activated state by stimulated with various external factors, and the activated HSCs are proliferative, inflammatory, chemotactic and featured with enhanced ECM production [24]. Hepatocytes are the predominant parenchymal cells (~ 2/3 of resident liver cells) in the liver [25], and some previous studies have reported that the IH-EVs might promote liver fibrosis by directly inducing HSCs activation [11, 26, 27]. To test this possibility, we evaluated the impact of injured hepatocyte-derived EVs (CCl4-EVs) on HSCs activation (Fig. 3A). HSCs (LX2 cells) could internalize Ctrl-EVs or CCl4-EVs, and there is no difference in uptake rates between them (Fig. 3B). Compared with Ctrl-EVs group, LX2 cells with CCl4-EVs treatments showed increased levels of proliferation rates, Edu-labeled cells (Fig. 3C, D), and cell populations in the G2 phase (Fig. S6A), suggesting enhanced HSCs proliferation induced by CCl4-EVs. However, even with prolonged time (up to 96 h), no significant difference in the expressions of α-SMA and ECM components (FN, Col1a1) was observed between the Ctrl-EVs group and the CCl4-EVs group (Fig. 3E-G; Fig. S6B, C), indicating that EVs alone cannot promote HSCs activation. Unlike previous reports, these results indicate that injured hepatocyte-derived EVs alone only promote HSCs proliferation, and some additional signals are required.

Injured hepatocyte-derived EVs synergize with macrophages to promote the HSCs activation

Interestingly, in vivo inhibition of EVs secretion partially alleviated liver fibrosis, whereas injured hepatocyte-derived EVs alone can promote HSCs proliferation but not ECM deposition, suggesting that additional pathways participant in hepatocytes-EVs mediated fibrosis. Hepatocytes injury triggers the recruitment and abnormal activation of diverse immune cells (e.g., macrophages, and neutrophils), which in turn secrete various proinflammatory cytokines and fibrogenic factors (TGF-β, PDGF, etc.) to sustain liver damage [5]. HSCs that are primed by initiating stimuli can then respond to a host of cytokines and growth factors, particularly from macrophages, contributing to the development of liver fibrosis [28]. Consistently, we also observed that many F4/80+ macrophages were localized near α-SMA+ HSCs in the liver of CCl4-mice (Fig. 4A, B). Moreover, macrophages are professional phagocytes that can engulf EVs with high efficiency in vivo [29]. Collectively, these findings suggest that liver macrophages might be involved in injured hepatocyte-derived EVs-mediated liver fibrosis.

Next, the effect of injured hepatocytes-derived EVs on macrophages’ phenotype was assessed (Fig. 4C). Mouse macrophages (RAW264.7 cells) could efficiently internalize Ctrl-EVs or CCl4-EVs with similar uptake rates (Fig. 4D). Macrophages with CCl4-EVs treatments showed increased levels of proinflammatory factors (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and profibrotic factors (TGF-β, PDGF) expression compared to Ctrl-EVs group (Fig. 4E-G), suggesting that the uptake of injured hepatocytes-derived EVs promote macrophages toward an proinflammatory/profibrotic phenotype. However, co-culture of conditioned medium (CM) from EVs-primed macrophages with HSCs slightly increased α-SMA and ECM (FN, Col1a1) in such cells (Fig. 4H, I; Fig. S7). Further results showed that HSCs stimulated with CCl4-EVs plus CM of CCl4-EVs-primed macrophages displayed elevated levels of α-SMA and ECM deposition (Fig. 4J-L; Fig. S8), suggesting that injured hepatocyte-derived EVs synergize with macrophages to promote HSCs activation.

In addition, the effects of macrophages-induced by injured hepatocyte-derived EVs on hepatocytes (AML12 cells) was assessed (Fig. 4M). Compared with Ctrl-EVs group, hepatocytes cultured in CM from CCl4-EVs-primed macrophages showed increased levels of proinflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-1β), ROS, and MDA (lipid peroxidation indicator) (Fig. 4N-P), suggesting that IH-EVs-induced macrophages can sustain hepatocyte injury, which in turn form a vicious circle of liver injury. These results suggest that, in addition to promoting HSCs proliferation, IH-EVs can induce liver macrophages to adopt a proinflammatory/profibrotic phenotype, creating an immune microenvironment to sustain hepatocytes injury and HSCs activation.

Injured hepatocyte-derived EVs synergize with macrophages to promote HSCs activation via PI3K/AKT and JAK/STAT pathways

Having found that injured hepatocyte-derived EVs can synergize with liver macrophages to promote HSCs activation, we sought to explore its underlying mechanism. The protein compositions of hepatocytes-derived EVs were firstly profiled using LC-MS/MS-based proteomics (Fig. 5A). CCl4-EVs showed 57 up-regulated and 107 down-regulated proteins compared to Ctrl-EVs (Fig. 5B; Fig. S9A). GO analysis revealed that these altered EVs proteins were enriched in many pathways, such as ECM organization, regulation of cell division, positive regulation of cell cycle process, ribosomes biogenesis, ribosome and structural constituent of ribosome (Fig. 5C). These results suggest that injured hepatocyte-derived EVs might affect multiple biological processes related to cell proliferation, protein synthesis, and inflammatory response in target cells.

Further RNA-seq results showed distinct genes expression in HSCs between Ctrl, CCl4-EVs and CCl4-EVs plus CM groups (Fig. 5D; Fig. S9B). Compared to Ctrl, CCl4-EVs treatments induced numbers of differentially expressed genes (DEGs, 65 up-regulated, 64 down-regulated, FC > 1.2, p < 0.05) in HSCs (Fig. 5E; Fig. S9C), and these DEGs were enriched in multiple pathways, such as metabolic, PI3K-AKT and AMPK (Fig. 5F). We further verified up-regulated levels of p-AKT in HSCs with CCl4-EVs treatments (Fig. 5G), whereas Akt inhibitor (MK2206, MK) treatments decreased the proliferative effect of CCl4-EVs in HSCs (Fig. 5H-K). These findings indicate that CCl4-EVs might promote the HSCs proliferation by activating PI3K-AKT pathway.

In addition, HSCs with CCl4-EVs plus CM treatments showed many DEGs (271 up-regulated, 181 down-regulated, FC > 1.5, p < 0.05) compared to CCl4-EVs alone group (Fig. 5L; Fig. S9D). These DEGs were mainly enriched in the pathways, such as Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, TNF, IL-17 and JAK-STAT (Fig. 5M). HSCs of CCl4-EVs plus CM group showed up-regulated levels of p-STAT3 protein compared to CCl4-EVs alone (Fig. 5N). JAK-STAT pathway plays a vital role in regulating macrophages polarization and cytokines production [30]. Thus, we further assessed if the activation of HSCs could be affect by JAK-STAT pathway using a JAK inhibitor (Ruxolitinib, Rux), and Rux treatments reduced the proteins expression of FN and α-SMA in activated HSCs induced by CCl4-EVs plus CM (Fig. 5O). Together, our results indicate that injured hepatocyte-derived EVs synergize with macrophages to promote HSCs activation via PI3K/AKT and JAK/STAT pathways.

Liver targeted co-inhibition of EVs secretion and macrophage activation using LT-EVs-based DDS yields robust antifibrotic potency in vivo

Having found that hepatocytes EVs secretion and activated liver macrophages coordinately promote liver fibrosis, we aimed to develop specific therapies to block their effects. In vitro, EVs secretion from injured hepatocyte were reduced by GW treatments (Fig. 6A, B; Fig. S10A). The expressions of proinflammatory factors (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α), and profibrotic factors (TGF-β, PDGF) in CCl4-EVs-primed macrophages could be suppressed by curcumin (Cur) treatments (Fig. 6C-F; Fig. S10B), a natural compound with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effect [31]. In vivo, co-administration of EVs secretion inhibitor (GW, 1.25 mg/kg) and macrophages activation inhibitor (Cur, 25 mg/kg) showed improved inhibitory effect on F4/80+CD11b+ macrophages infiltration (Fig. 6G; Fig. S11A). Compared with GW or Cur alone group, co-administration also decreased the levels of serum ALT levels (Fig. 6H; Fig. S11B), Suzuki’s liver injury score, α-SMA+ HSCs and ECM (FN, Collagen) expression in liver tissues of CCl4-mice (Fig. 6I-L; Fig. S11C, D), indicating that co-inhibition of EVs secretion and macrophage activation exert enhanced anti-fibrotic efficacy.

To further improve the liver-targeting efficacy and the therapeutic index of these hydrophobic compounds, normal liver tissue-derived EVs (LT-EVs)-based drug delivery system (DDS) was utilized to co-delivering the two inhibitors, because systemically administrated EVs primarily accumulate in liver and the homing ability of EVs to their parent cells [32]. In line with previous reports, we found that intraperitoneally injected Cy7-labeled LT-EVs accumulated mainly in the liver compared to the other organs, such as the kidneys, heart, lungs and spleen, and no signals were detected in the PBS or dye control groups (Fig. 7A, B). Liver tissues staining further showed the uptake of LT-EVs in CK18+ hepatocytes (Fig. 7C), suggesting that LT-EVs-based DDS can efficiently deliver drugs to hepatocytes in the liver.

Next, the two inhibitors (GW and Cur) were loaded into LT-EVs (GW/Cur@EVs) as previously reported [32] (Fig. 7D), and the drug-loading capacity of GW and Cur in LT-EVs detected by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was ~ 11.3% (w/w) and ~ 17.4% (w/w), respectively (Fig. S12A, B). GW/Cur@EVs showed similar morphology and increased levels of average size (142 ± 14.46 nm) compared to blank LT-EVs (128 ± 4.74 nm) (Fig. 7E, F; Fig. S12C). In vitro drug release results showed that ~ 29.2% of GW and ~ 11.8% of Cur were released from GW/Cur@EVs within 48 h (Fig. 7G), confirming the slow release of both inhibitors by LT-EVs-based DDS. The therapeutic effects of GW/Cur@EVs was further evaluated in vivo (Fig. 7H). Compared with GW + Cur group (free drug, GW:1.25 mg/kg, Cur: 25 mg/kg, injected every day), serum ALT and AST levels of GW/Cur@EVs group (containing GW: ~1.02 mg/kg, Cur: ~1.58 mg/kg, injected every other day) were significantly lower (Fig. 7I; Fig. S13A). GW/Cur@EVs treatment showed a significantly greater potential to reduce the levels of F4/80+CD11b+ macrophages infiltration (Fig. S13B), α-SMA+ activated HSCs and ECM (FN, Collagen) deposition in the liver of CCl4-mice (Fig. 7J-M; Fig. S13C, D). The in vivo biosafety of GW/Cur@EVs was also examined, and there was no obvious tissue injury or inflammatory response in major organs, such as the heart, spleen, and kidney of the mice after 3 weeks of injections (every other day) (Fig. S14). These results suggest that co-inhibiting EVs secretion and macrophage activation is a potent strategy for reducing liver fibrosis, and liver targeted delivery of the two inhibitors using LT-EVs-based DDS can yield robust anti-fibrotic potency in vivo.

Discussion

Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing have indicated that intercellular interactions play a crucial role in the development of liver fibrosis [33]. During liver fibrosis onset and progression, multiple types of resident and unresident liver cells can interact with HSCs and affect their activation. As the major resident liver cells, injured hepatocytes can release a spectrum of DAMPs, cytokines and substrates to promote HSCs activation in a direct and/or indirect manner [6, 34]. Additionally, HSCs were found to phagocytose apoptotic vesicles from hepatocytes, facilitating their differentiation into myofibroblasts [35]. Abnormally activated immune cells, such as macrophages, can secrete multiple cytokines, such as TGF-β, PDGF, TNF-α and various chemokines, directly promoting HSCs activation [36]. These findings suggest that multiple cell types, such as hepatocytes and immune cells, can interact with HSCs to establish a complex cellular network that plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of liver fibrosis. However, the detailed cell-cell interactions within the liver microenvironment and their contributions to liver fibrosis onset and progression remain elusive.

Notably, EVs play a critical role in intercellular communication by conveying information between cells through shedding various bioactive molecules [37]. Increasing evidence has indicates the complicated role of EVs-mediated cell-cell interactions in the pathology of liver fibrosis [8], and EVs released by different cell types in the liver might influence the proliferation and phenotype of HSCs. Hepatocytes are the primary parenchymal cells in the liver, accounting for approximately two-thirds of the total liver cell population [38]. The possible role of injured hepatocytes in triggering liver fibrosis is multifaceted and diverse pathological processes, such as enhanced oxidative stress, proinflammatory necrosis, release of various cytokines, may be implied [6]. In the early stages of liver fibrosis, hepatocyte injury triggers a series of complex cellular signaling and immune responses, and these effect is at least partly dependent on EVs secretion [4]. In this study, we also found increased EVs secretion in the liver of fibrotic mice as well as in injured hepatocytes, suggesting that injured hepatocytes-derived EVs (IH-EVs) might contribute to the pathology of liver fibrosis. In fact, previous studies have shown that IH-EVs might exacerbate liver fibrosis by directly activating HSCs via inducing Toll-like receptor 3 pathway [39], sheding mitochondrial DNA [40], or p38 MAPK/ATF2 pathway via transferring MASP1 protein [11]. However, unlike the previous reports, we found that IH-EVs alone cannot directly induce the fully activation of HSCs, which only promote HSCs proliferation but not ECM deposition. Besides, IH-EVs can be internalized by the macrophages within injury sites to induce the release of proinflammatory and profibrotic factors, thereby coordinately activating proliferation and ECM deposition of HSCs (profibrotic vicious circle). This effect might explain why solely inhibiting inflammation or profibrotic pathways is insufficient to prevent early liver fibrosis in clinical trials. Thus, our study might provide a novel therapeutic target for alleviating liver fibrosis.

The exact mechanism of HSCs proliferation and activation is complicated, and many signaling pathways, such as the TGF-β, PI3K/AKT, NF-κB, and MAPK pathways have been reported [2]. The PI3K/Akt pathway is a receptor tyrosine kinase signaling pathway that is indispensable for transmitting growth and survival signals into cells, such as mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β) [41]. Increasing evidence indicates that the PI3K/AKT pathway is strongly associated with the activation and proliferation of HSCs. Inhibition of AKT can reduce the proliferation of HSCs [42]. In this study, we found that IH-EVs contained abundant proteins related to cell proliferation, such as Cit, Kif14, Vps4b, etc., which promote the proliferation of HSCs and activate the PI3K/Akt pathway. Furthermore, the JAK/STAT pathway is a pleiotropic signaling cascade of multiple growth factors and cytokines that mediate a variety of cellular functions, and can act independently or in conjunction with other profibrotic pathways, such as the TGF-β, to promote the inflammation and activation of fibroblasts in liver fibrosis [43]. In line with these findings, we found that ribosomal proteins in IH-EVs induce the transformation of a pro-fibrotic phenotype in macrophages, and activate the JAK/STAT pathway. Collectively, these results suggest that IH-EVs might synergize with macrophages to promote HSCs activation via activating the PI3K-AKT and JAK-STAT pathways.

Having found that hepatocytes EVs secretion and the activated macrophages coordinately promote liver fibrosis, we intended to develop a liver-targeted therapy to block this pro-fibrotic vicious circle. Although co-administration of two hydrophobic inhibitors (GW and Cur) showed certain anti-fibrotic effect, their therapeutic effects in vivo and clinical translation are largely limited due to poor bioaviability, unspecific tissue distribution and severe side effects when used at high concentrations and with repeated dosing. In recent years, EVs-based DDS have been proposed as a promising tool for delivering diverse types of drugs, because its unique small size and bilayer lipid vesicle microstructure, excellent biocompatibility, and low immunogenicity in vivo [44]. Importantly, the liver is one of the primary accumulated organs of the systemically administrated EVs [32], suggesting that EVs may be a natural liver-targeted DDS for treating liver disease. Moreover, compared with in vitro cultured cell-derived EVs, tissue-derived EVs have displayed several advantages, such as high yield, easy preparation, and low cost [45]. Thus, normal liver tissue-derived EVs (LT-EVs) were used to deliver two drugs (GW/Cur@ EVs) in this study. The results showed that LT-EVs were widely distributed in liver, and LT-EVs enables slowly release of GW and Cur in vivo. GW/Cur@EVs treatments significantly reduced the deposition of collagen fibres and alleviated liver fibrosis, and the dosage (GW: ~81.6% of the original dose; Cur: ~6.32% of the original dose) and frequency (every other day) of drugs administration was also reduced compared to free GW + Cur group. In addition, long-term and repeated injection of LT-EVs showed good biosafety and tolerance in vivo. Together, these results indicate that LT-EVs-based DDS significantly alleviated liver fibrosis, and LT-EVs may be an effective and safe DDS for the treatment of liver fibrosis.

Although these results are promising, some questions are unclear and need to be addressed in future studies. For example, the exact mechanism of increased EVs secretion in response to liver damage remains elusive. We found that the expression of Rab27a is upregulated in fibrotic mice, and studying the mechanism of EVs synthesis in hepatocytes after injury in liver fibrosis has great therapeutic potential for clinical purposes. The combinated small molecule inhibitors (GW4869 and curcumin) showed antifibrotic effect in mouse model, but they have not been tested in clinical trials; thus, further drug development is needed. Although EVs have shown great potential for drug delivery, some limitations are still need to be overcome before clinical translation. Developing clinical grade EVs for mass production remains a challenge, requiring the development of scalable manufacturing processes to produce EVs in a fast, cost-effective and repeatable manner, requires the establishment of standardized processes in terms of production, purification, storage and delivery, etc. Nevertheless, targeted blockade of EVs-mediated profibrotic vicious circle using LT-EVs-based DDS is a potential therapeutic strategy for preventing liver fibrosis.

Conclusion

In summary, our results showed that EVs secretion was enhanced in the injured hepatocytes, and these EVs might synergize with surrounding macrophages to promote the activation of HSCs in the injured liver microenvironment. Moreover, normal liver tissue-derived EVs (LT-EVs)-based dual-drug delivery system (DDS) was employed to inhibit liver EVs secretion and macrophage activation, leading to alleviation of liver fibrosis. This study reveals an endogenous EVs-mediated pro-fibrotic mechanism in early liver fibrosis and LT-EVs-based DDS as a novel EVs engineering strategy for targeted drug delivery.

Materials and methods

Isolation of human plasma-derived EVs

This study included 13 healthy control individuals and 18 patients with liver cirrhosis. Blood samples were collected from both healthy individuals and cirrhosis patients. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University. All enrolled patients signed informed consent forms. EVs were isolated from the plasma samples of healthy individuals or patients with liver cirrhosis as previously reported [46]. Briefly, plasma samples were centrifuged at 1500 × g (Thermo Fisher) for 20 min, 3000 × g for 15 min, and centrifuged at 13,000 × g (Beckman XPN-100, Optima XPN-100) for 30 min to remove platelets and cellular debris. Next, the medium was ultracentrifuged at 120,000 ×g for 2.5 h at 4 °C with an SW32Ti rotor in an Optima XPN-100 (Beckman Coulter, USA). Subsequently, the isolated EVs were washed with PBS and subjected to a second ultracentrifugation (120,000 × g, 2.5 h). The purified EVs were resuspended in PBS and stored at − 80 °C for further experiments.

Mouse model of liver fibrosis

The animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (NO. 20221121008). Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks, 20 ± 2 g), were purchased from the GemPharmatech Co.,Ltd.(Chengdu, China). The animals were housed in individual cages with controlled temperature and humidity and a 12-h cycle of light and dark and were fed standard chow and tap water ad libitum. Mice are intraperitoneally (i.p) injected with carbon tetrachloride (CCl4, 1 mL/kg, dissolved in olive oil, Adamas, China) twice a week for 3 weeks to induce liver fibrosis models [47]. The control (Ctrl) group were injected with the same volume of olive oil.

Isolation of liver tissue-EVs (LT-EVs)

EVs were isolated from liver tissues as previously reported [48]. After the mice were anaesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium, the abdomen was wiped with 75% ethanol, and the abdominal cavity was exposed. The portal vein was perfused with Hanks’ solution without calcium (Ca, Gibco, USA), then ligated and fixed the puncture needle, cut the inferior vena cava. The liver turned from red to white and then was perfused with collagenase IV (Col.IV, Gibco, USA) until the liver became jelly like. The liver was transferred to a Petri dish, rinse with pre-chilled PBS, then transfer the liver to warm 10 ml Col.IV perfusion solution, and the liver was torn with forceps to show cloudy cellular effusion. The liquid was then filtered using a 70 μm aperture filter. LT-EVs were isolated from the filtrate using ultracentrifugation. Briefly, continuous centrifugation of the filtrate: 300 × rpm (Thermo Fisher) 10 min; 300 × g 10 min; 2000 × g 20 min. Next, 10,000 × g (Beckman XPN-100, Optima XPN-100) for 70 min. Next, the medium was ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 70 min with an SW32Ti rotor in an Optima XPN-100. The purified EVs were resuspended in PBS and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent experiments.

Cell cultures

The mouse hepatocyte cell line AML12 was purchased from the Cell Bank of Type Culture Collection of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China); the human hepatic stellate cell line Lieming Xu-2 (LX2, CL-0560), and the murine monocyte/macrophage line RAW264.7 (CL-0190) were kindly provided by Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co.,Ltd. All cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Biological Industries, Israel) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (HyClone, USA) at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Isolation of cell-derived EVs

AML12 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, When the cells reached ~ 40% confluence, the medium was replaced with DMEM containing 10% EV-depleted FBS (EV-depleted FBS by ultracentrifugation at 120,000 × g for 18 h). To induce hepatocyte injury, AML12 cells were treated with CCl4 (10 mM, dissolved in DMSO, Adamas, China) for 24 h as previously reported [49]. Then, the CCl4-contained medium of cells were removed and washed with PBS buffer, and the medium was replaced with DMEM containing 10% EV-depleted FBS. After 48 h, the culture medium were collected, and EVs were isolated as previously reported [50]. Briefly, the culture medium was sequential centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min, 2000 × g for 20 min and 10,000 × g for 30 min, followed by two ultracentrifugation steps at 100,000 × g for 90 min with an SW32Ti rotor in an Optima XPN-100. The purified EV pellets were resuspended in PBS for further experiments.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM)

To observe EVs morphology, negative staining was performed [51]. Briefly, diluted EVs was dropped onto carbon-coated copper grids, followed by negative staining with 2% phosphotungstic acid solution. Subsequently viewed by TEM (H-600, Hitachi, Japan) at 75 kV.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

We employed nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) using the Zeta View PMX 120 system from Particle Metrix, Germany, to measure the size and quantity of EVs [51]. The specified parameters for the analysis were as follows: sensitivity: 80, shutter: 100, minimum brightness: 30, minimum area: 10, and maximum area: 1000. All analyses were conducted at a temperature of 25 °C.

Western blotting (WB)

Total protein was extracted from EVs, liver tissues and cells using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA buffer, CWBIO, China) with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Selleckchem, USA), then their concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher). The proteins were electrophoresed on a 12.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate‒polyacrylamide gel (SDS‒PAGE) and then transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (PVDF, Merck Millipore). After blocking with 5% BSA or milk for 1.5 h, the membranes were incubated with the primary antibodies anti-Rab27a (HuaBio, China), anti-Hsp70 (HuaBio, China), anti-Tsg101 (HuaBio, China), anti-cytochrome C (HuaBio, China), anti-FN (Abcam, USA), anti-α-SMA (HuaBio, China), anti-AKT (HuaBio, China), anti-p-AKT (HuaBio, China), anti-STAT3 (HuaBio, China), anti-p-STAT3 (HuaBio, China), anti-Tubulin (HuaBio, China), anti-β-actin (HuaBio, China) at 4 °C overnight. Next washing with TBST, the PVDF membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Proteintech, SA00001-2) for 1 h at room temperature. The bands were detected by an ultrahigh sensitivity ECL kit (MedChemExpress, USA). The expression level was quantified using ImageJ software and relative to Tubulin/β-actin expression.

Inhibition of EVs secretion in vivo

Male C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into the following groups (n = 6): Ctrl, CCl4, CCl4 + GW4869. For GW4869 (GW) treatment, mice were i.p. injected with GW (1.25 mg/kg/d, dissolved in DMSO, CSNpharm, USA) once a day for 3 weeks. The mice were sacrificed 24 h after the last injection, and the blood and liver samples were collected for further analysis.

Serum ALT and AST measurements

The liver function of mice was assessed by measuring serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels using an automatic dry biochemical analyzer (Cobas®8000 702, Roche, Switzerland).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

RNA was extracted from cells and liver tissues by a RNA extraction kit (Axygen, USA), followed by cDNA synthesis using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Japan). Primer sequences are presented in Table S1. qRT–PCR was performed with SYBR Green on an qRT–PCR detection system (CFX96, Bio-Rad, USA). The expression levels of mRNAs were calculated relative to the β-actin/GAPDH mRNA level.

Histological and Immunofluorescence examination

Tissue samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, cut into 5 μm thick sections for hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining (Servicebio, China). Suzuki’s histological criteria for the assessment of liver damage were employed [52]. In addition, liver sections were stained with Masson and Sirius red staining (Servicebio, China). For immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence, liver tissues were incubated with Rab27a (HuaBio, China), α-SMA (HuaBio, China), F4/80 (Servicebio, China), CK18 (HuaBio, China) and CD63 (Servicebio, China) with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Six images were randomly selected from each slice, then quantification analysis of images was performed using ImageJ software.

Treatment of HSCs with cell-derived EVs

LX2 cells were treated with Ctrl-EVs or CCl4-EVs (4*108 particles/mL) and incubated for 48 h. The cells were detected using qRT-PCR and immunofluorescence.

Intracellular uptake of EVs

PKH26 (Umibio, China) was used to label Ctrl-EVs or CCl4-EVs according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, PKH26-labeled EVs (4 × 108 particles/mL) were incubated with LX2 cells or RAW264.7 cells. The cells were washed with PBS and subsequently stained with phalloidin (Servicebio, China) and Hoechst (Servicebio, China). Fluorescence in the cells was observed with a confocal microscope (STELLARIS 5, Leica, Germany).

Treatment of macrophages with Cell-derived EVs

RAW264.7 cells were treated with Ctrl-EVs or CCl4-EVs (4*108 particles/mL) for 16 h. The gene expressions of pro-inflammatory factors (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and pro-fibrotic factors (TGF-β, PDGF) of cells were detected by qRT-PCR, and the cell supernatants were collected for ELISA (MultiSciences, China) tests of cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, TGF-β).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The levels of IL-6, TNF-α, TGF-β were quantified with ELISA (MultiSciences, China) detection kits following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The optical density values were recorded at 450–570 nm using a microplate reader.

Co-culture macrophages with HSCs in vitro

RAW264.7 cells were treated with Ctrl-EVs or CCl4-EVs (4*108 particles/mL) for 16 h, and then conditioned medium (CM) was collected and concentrated using a centrifugal filter unit (15 ml, 3 kDa MWCO, Millipore) at 3000 × g for 20 min, named Ctrl-EVs-CM or CCl4-EVs-CM. LX2 cells were seeded in 12-well plates for 24 h, and then washed with PBS, after which Ctrl-EV-CM or CCl4-EV-CM was added to the cells. After 48 h, the cells were collected, and qRT-PCR was used to detect gene expression.

Cell proliferation assay

The proliferation rate of LX2 cells was assessed using a cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8, Absin, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, LX2 cells were treated with Ctrl-EVs, CCl4-EVs (4*108 particles/mL) or MK2206 (2.5 mΜ, dissolved in DMSO, CSNpharm, USA) for 48 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to cells and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm in each well of the plate was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA), then calculated the relative cell proliferation rates.

Cell EDU assay

LX2 cells were treated with Ctrl-EVs or CCl4-EVs (4*108 particles/mL) or MK2206 for 48 h, and then cell proliferation was measured using the Edu-Apollo DNA Proliferation Assay Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Servicebio, China). The cell nuclei were stained with Hoechst. Subsequently, the stained cells were examined and imaged under a microscope (BioTek, China).

Flow cytometry assay of cell cycle

LX2 cells were treated with Ctrl-EVs or CCl4-EVs (4*108 particles/mL) or MK2206 for 48 h. Then cells were collected and resuspend in cold PBS. The cells were fixed with 70% ethanol for 24 h at 4 °C. Cell proliferation was assessed using the PI cell cycle assay kit (4 A Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Flow Cytometry were performed by CytoFlex flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). The data obtained were analysed using FlowJo software.

Co-culture EVs plus CM of EVs-primed macrophages with LX2 cells in vitro

LX2 cells were seeded in 12-well plates for 24 h, then washed with PBS, after which Ctrl-EVs-CM or CCl4-EVs-CM, and Ctrl-EVs or CCl4-EVs (4*108 particles/mL) were added to the cells. After 48 h, the cells were detected using qRT-PCR and immunofluorescence.

Co-culture macrophages with hepatocytes in vitro

Co-culture of conditioned medium (CM) from EVs-primed macrophages with AML12 cells. Briefly, AML12 cells were seeded in 12-well plates for 24 h, then washed with PBS, and added Ctrl-EVs-CM or CCl4-EVs-CM to the cells. After 48 h, cells were collected and qRT-PCR, ROS, MDA was used to detect.

Intracellular ROS assay

The levels of ROS in AML12 cells were assessed using the ROS Assay Kit (Servicebio, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, co-culture of conditioned medium from EVs-primed macrophages with AML12 cells for 48 h, the cells were incubated with DCFH-DA for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark, then washed with PBS and DCFH-DA fluorescence were visualized by CytoFlex flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). The data obtained were analysed using FlowJo software.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay

Lipid peroxidation in AML12 cells were determined by the level of malondialdehyde (MDA). MDA level was measured following the manufacturer’s protocol (Beyotime, China). Briefly, co-culture of conditioned medium from EVs-primed macrophages with AML12 cells for 48 h, cells were washed with PBS and lysed with lysis buffer. After centrifuge, the cells were collected and used for MDA detection. A microplate reader was used to measure the absorbance of cellular MDA at 532 nm. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA protein assay kit.

Proteomic analysis of EVs

Ctrl-EVs and CCl4-EVs extracted from AML12 cells were proteomic analyzed in Aksomics (Shanghai, China). Briefly, EVs were mixed with 8 M urea lysis buffer, vortexed, ultrasonically dissolved, centrifuged, and then protein quantification was performed. For trypsin digestion, protein solutions were treated with DTT and IAM, diluted with Tris-HCl, and digested with trypsin at a 1:100 ratio for 16 h at 37 °C, followed by peptide desalting and freeze-drying. LC‒MS analysis involved redissolving peptide powder in 0.1% formic acid using an EASY-nLC 1200 ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography system for elution. Mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (2% acetonitrile), and mobile phase B was 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (80% acetonitrile), following a specific elution gradient. The ionized peptides were analysed with a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive™ mass spectrometer in DIA mode (Novogene Co., Ltd., China) with high-resolution detection. The secondary mass spectrometry data were searched using MaxQuant (2.6.5.0) with parameters. GO and KEGG were used to analyse the protein families and pathways.

RNA sequencing analysis of cells

RNA sequencing was performed in Aksomics (Shanghai, China). Total RNA was extracted from LX2 cells treated with CCl4-EVs or CCl4-EVs plus CM of CCl4-EVs-primed macrophages with TRIzol. Quantification was carried out with the NanoDrop ND-1000 (NanoDrop Inc., USA), and libraries were constructed using the KAPA Stranded RNA-Seq Library Prep Kit (Roche). Library quality was assessed via the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument (Illumina Inc., USA), including the generation of denatured single strands with NaOH, in situ amplification on the Illumina flow cell, and 150 paired-end sequencing cycles. The differential gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R-package (1.20.0), and the P value was adjusted via the Benjamini–Hochberg method. The KEGG pathway and Gene Ontology (GO) terms enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed using cluster Profiler R software (3.8.1) with a p-value < 0.05 as statistically significant cutoffs.

Treatment of macrophages with Curcumin

RAW264.7 cells were treated with CCl4-EVs (4*108 particles/mL) for 16 h. For curcumin treatment, curcumin (Cur, 5 µM, dissolved in DMSO, CSNpharm, USA) was added to the cells.

Treatment of hepatocytes with GW4869

AML12 cells were cultured with DMEM containing 10% EV-depleted FBS, then treated with GW4869 (GW, 10 µM, CSNpharm, USA, dissolved in DMSO) for 48 h. EVs were isolated from the culture medium.

Co-Treatment of fibrotic mice with GW4869/Curcumin

Male C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into the following groups (n = 6 per group): Ctrl, CCl4, CCl4 + GW; CCl4 + Cur; CCl4 + GW + Cur. Mice are administered CCl4 (1 mL/kg) by i.p. injection to induce liver fibrosis. For treatment, in CCl4 + GW group, mice were i.p. injected with GW (1.25 mg/kg/d) once a day; in CCl4 + Cur group, mice were i.p. injected with Cur (25 mg/kg/d, dissolved in DMSO, CSNpharm, USA) once a day; in CCl4 + GW + Cur group, mice were i.p. injected with GW (1.25 mg/kg/d) and Cur (25 mg/kg/d) once a day. After 3 weeks, the mice were sacrificed, the blood and liver samples were collected for further analysis.

Biodistribution of LT-EVs in mice

LT-EVs were labeled with Sulfo-Cy7-NHS ester (Xi’an Qiyue Biology, China) as previously described. Dye-labeled EVs (200 µg protein/mice) and an equal amount of free dye (negative control) or PBS were i.p. injected into mice. After 12 h, the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, and kidney) of the mice were harvested and observed using IVIS Spectrum (PerkinElmer, USA). In addition, liver Sect. (5 μm thick) were prepared and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, then stained with CK18 (HuaBio, China) and DAPI.

Preparing dual inhibitor-loaded LT-EVs (GW/Cur@EVs)

For drug loading, LT-EVs (200 µg) were incubated with GW (50 µg) and Cur (50 µg) at 37 °C for 30 min [32]. Excessive GW and Cur were removed from LT-EVs with a centrifugal filter unit (0.5 ml, 3 kDa MWCO, Millipore) at 3000 × g for 20 min. The purified GW and Cur loaded EVs (GW/Cur@EVs) were collected. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was used to measure the content of GW and Cur in EVs. In brief, 0.1% Triton X-100 was added to GW/Cur@EVs in equal volume, incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, centrifuged at 13,000 × rpm for 5 min, the intermediate layer was transferred to a sample vial, and analyzed by LC-MS (mobile phase: 0.1% formic acid-acetonitrile, flow rate: 1 mL/min), with GW concentration was analysed at 351 nm and Cur concentration was analysed at 423 nm.

In vitro drug release assay

For the drug release assay, GW/Cur@EVs (containing 50 µg GW, 50 µg Cur) were added to a dialysis unit (cutoff MW 3.5 kDa, Thermo Scientific) and incubated with release buffer (0.1% Triton X-100 in 1 mL PBS) at 37 °C [32]. At each indicated time point, release buffer was collected and replaced with an equal volume of fresh buffer. The concentrations of GW/Cur in the release buffer were analysed using UV spectrophotometry (Nanodrop).

Evaluating the therapeutic effects of GW/Cur@EVs in vivo

Male C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into the following groups (n = 6 per group): Ctrl, CCl4, CCl4 + GW + Cur, CCl4 + GW/Cur@EVs. Mice are administered CCl4 (1 mL/kg) by i.p. injection to induce liver fibrosis. For treatment, in CCl4 + GW + Cur group, mice were i.p. injected with GW (1.25 mg/kg/d) and Cur (25 mg/kg/d) once a day; in CCl4 + GW/Cur@EVs group, mice were i.p. injected with GW/Cur@EVs (GW/Cur@EVs, containing GW: ~1.02 mg/kg, Cur: ~1.58 mg/kg) every other day. After 3 weeks, the mice were sacrificed, the blood and liver samples were collected for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). The statistical significance of the differences between groups was determined by t tests (for two-group comparisons) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (for more than two group comparisons), and P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.
