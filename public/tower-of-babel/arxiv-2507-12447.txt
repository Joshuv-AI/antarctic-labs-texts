Minmax Exclusivity Classes for Power-Type Loss Functions
Stanisław M. S. Halkiewicz

Abstract
In statistical decision theory, the choice of loss function fundamentally shapes which estimators qualify as optimal. This paper introduces and develops the general concept of exclusivity classes of loss functions: subsets of loss functions such that no estimator can be optimal (according to a specified notion) for losses lying in different classes. We focus on the case of minmax optimality and define minmax exclusivity classes , demonstrating that the classical family of power-type loss functions L p ​ ( θ , a ) = | θ − a | p L_{p}(\theta,a)=|\theta-a|^{p} forms such a class. Under standard regularity and smoothness assumptions, we prove that no estimator can be simultaneously minmax for losses belonging to two distinct L p L_{p} classes. This result is obtained via a perturbation argument relying on differentiability of risk functionals and the conic structure of loss spaces. We formalize the framework of exclusivity partitions, distinguishing trivial and realizable structures, and analyze their algebraic properties. These results open a broader inquiry into the geometry of estimator optimality, and the potential classification of the loss function space via exclusivity principles.

Introduction

The choice of loss function is fundamental in statistical decision theory, as it determines how estimation errors are evaluated and penalized. Different loss functions yield different notions of estimator optimality, shaping the selection of statistical procedures. One of the most widely studied optimality criteria is the
minmax principle
, introduced by
Wald 1950
, which advocates selecting an estimator that minimizes the worst-case expected loss over the parameter space. This principle was further developed in the foundational works of
Cam 1986
and
Brown 1986
, and is systematically presented in standard references such as
Berger 1985
. Recent contributions continue to refine its scope in both classical and modern settings
[
11
,
4
,
5
]
.

Formally, let
\theta
denote a parameter in a statistical model, and let
X
be the observed data with distribution
P_{\theta}
. For a loss function
L(\theta,a)
and an estimator
a(X)
, the frequentist risk is defined by

R(\theta,a)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[L(\theta,a(X))].

(1)

An estimator
a^{*}
is said to be
minmax
if it satisfies

\sup_{\theta}R(\theta,a^{*})=\inf_{a}\sup_{\theta}R(\theta,a),

(2)

i.e., it minimizes the maximum risk over all parameter values.

It is well known that the form of the loss function critically affects which estimators are minmax. Under squared-error loss
L(\theta,a)=(\theta-a)^{2}
, the sample mean is minmax for the location parameter of i.i.d. normal observations (see
8
, Ch. 5
). In contrast, under absolute-error loss
L(\theta,a)=|\theta-a|
, the sample median achieves the minmax risk
[
6
]
. These classical examples illustrate that distinct loss functions yield distinct minmax estimators, raising a natural question: can a single estimator be minmax for more than one type of loss?

In this paper, we develop a general framework for addressing this question. We introduce the notion of
exclusivity classes
of loss functions—collections of losses such that no estimator is optimal (with respect to a given criterion) for losses drawn from different classes. An exclusivity class reflects the idea that the optimality of an estimator is inherently tied to the form of the loss used. When the optimality criterion is minmaxity, we speak of
minmax exclusivity classes
.

As a first case study, we examine the family of
power-type
loss functions, given by

L_{p}(\theta,a)=|\theta-a|^{p},\qquad p>0,

(3)

which includes the absolute-error (
p=1
) and squared-error (
p=2
) losses as special cases. Earlier asymptotic theory has studied minmax procedures under fixed
L_{p}
losses
[
7
,
9
]
, but these works did not address whether minmaxity could persist across different exponents
p
and
q
. In particular, it has remained an open question whether a single estimator could be minmax under both
L_{p}
and
L_{q}
for
p\neq q
.

We resolve this question under mild regularity and smoothness assumptions, proving that no estimator can be minmax for both
L_{p}
and
L_{q}
when
p\neq q
. That is, the power-type loss classes
\mathcal{L}_{p}
form disjoint minmax exclusivity classes. The proof relies on a perturbative argument exploiting differences in the local curvature of risk functionals under different
L_{p}
losses.

Beyond this main result, we initiate a broader investigation of exclusivity classes and their structure. We formalize a general theory of exclusivity regions and realizable exclusivity partitions, and show that the
L_{p}
power classes are closed under positive scaling, forming convex cones in the ambient space of loss functions. These geometric and algebraic properties suggest a deeper slicing of the loss function space along optimality boundaries, raising new questions about the classification of loss functions via exclusivity.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
provides formal definitions and notation, including loss spaces, risk functions, and exclusivity classes. In Section
3
, we develop the general theory of exclusivity partitions. Section
5
investigates the algebraic structure of loss function classes. Section
4
states our main theorem on the exclusivity of minmax estimators for power-type losses. The formal proof is presented in Section
6
. Finally, Section
7
discusses open problems and future directions for research.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the notation and foundational concepts used throughout the paper. We adopt a frequentist decision-theoretic framework for the estimation problem, defining estimators, loss functions, and risk, and then formalizing the minmax criterion. For completeness, we summarize all relevant notation here.

Definition 2.1

(Parameter space)
.

Let
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}
denote the
parameter space
, i.e., the set of all possible values of the unknown parameter
\theta
. We assume
\Theta
is a nonempty closed interval (possibly unbounded), and that the true parameter value lies in its interior. We consider a statistical model
\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
, where
X
is a random variable taking values in a measurable space
\mathcal{X}
, and
P_{\theta}
denotes the distribution of
X
under parameter
\theta
.

Definition 2.2

(Estimator)
.

An
estimator
is a measurable function
\hat{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
producing an estimate of
\theta
based on the observed data
X
. We denote the set of all admissible estimators by
\mathscr{D}
. For any
\hat{\theta}\in\mathscr{D}
, we write
\hat{\theta}(X)
for the realized estimate.

Remark 2.3

(Oracle estimators)
.

The function
\delta(X)=\theta
minimizes the pointwise loss
L(\theta,a)=|\theta-a|^{p}
for all
p>0
, but it is not a valid estimator since it depends on the unknown parameter
\theta
. Estimators must be measurable functions of the observed data
X
alone. Throughout, we restrict attention to estimators
\delta\in\mathscr{D}
that are data-based decision rules. The oracle rule
\delta(X)=\theta
is excluded from consideration.

Definition 2.4

(Loss function)
.

A
loss function
is a measurable map

L:\Theta\times\Theta\to[0,\infty)

that quantifies the cost incurred when the true parameter value is
\theta
but action
a\in\Theta
is taken. It is typically required that
L(\theta,\theta)=0
for all
\theta\in\Theta
, and that
L(\theta,a)
increases as
a
deviates from
\theta
. A classical example is the squared-error loss:

L(\theta,a)=(\theta-a)^{2}.

(4)

Definition 2.5

(Power-class loss functions)
.

For any exponent
p>0
, the
power-class
of loss functions is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{p}:=\left\{L\in\mathscr{L}\;\middle|\;L(\theta,a)=c|\theta-a|^{p}+o\left(|\theta-a|^{p}\right)\text{ as }a\to\theta,\text{ for some }c>0\right\},

(5)

where the small-
o
term is uniform in a neighborhood of
a=\theta
, and
L
is continuous (and differentiable in
a
when
p>1
). The canonical example in this class is
L_{p}(\theta,a)=|\theta-a|^{p}
.

Definition 2.6

(Risk function)
.

Given a loss function
L
and an estimator
\hat{\theta}\in\mathscr{D}
, the
risk function
of
\hat{\theta}
at parameter value
\theta
is defined as

R_{L}(\theta,\hat{\theta}):=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[L\big(\theta,\hat{\theta}(X)\big)\right],

(6)

where the expectation is taken under
P_{\theta}
. The function
R_{L}(\theta,\hat{\theta})
is typically assumed to be finite and continuous in
\theta
.

Definition 2.7

(minmax estimator)
.

An estimator
\hat{\theta}^{*}\in\mathscr{D}
is called
minmax
with respect to a given loss function
L
if it achieves the smallest worst-case risk:

R^{*}:=\inf_{\hat{\theta}\in\mathscr{D}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}R_{L}(\theta,\hat{\theta}),

(7)

and

\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}R_{L}(\theta,\hat{\theta}^{*})=R^{*}.

(8)

If no exact minimizer exists, a sequence of estimators
\hat{\theta}_{n}
is said to be
asymptotically minmax
if
\sup_{\theta}R_{L}(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{n})\to R^{*}
.

Remark 2.8

(Bayesian approach)
.

While our focus is on the frequentist notion of minmax risk, we briefly note the Bayesian alternative. Given a prior distribution
\pi
on
\Theta
, the Bayes risk of an estimator
\hat{\theta}
is defined as

r(\pi,\hat{\theta}):=\int_{\Theta}R_{L}(\theta,\hat{\theta})\,\pi(d\theta).

(9)

In certain cases, a Bayes estimator for a
least-favorable prior
achieves the minmax risk. We do not pursue this connection here.

Assumption 2.9

(Regularity assumptions)
.

We assume the following conditions throughout:

1.

\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a closed interval, and the true parameter value lies in its interior.

2.

The model
\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
is dominated by a
\sigma
-finite measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
, and each
P_{\theta}
admits a density
f(x;\theta)
that is continuous in
\theta
.

3.

For each
\hat{\theta}\in\mathscr{D}
, the risk
R_{L}(\theta,\hat{\theta})
is finite and continuous in
\theta
.

4.

The worst-case risk
\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}R_{L}(\theta,\hat{\theta})
is finite for all
\hat{\theta}\in\mathscr{D}
.

5.

The supremum in
\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}R_{L}(\theta,\hat{\theta})
is attained (or approximated uniformly on compacts).

Assumption 2.10

(Smoothness assumptions)
.

We assume:

1.

L(\theta,a)
is continuous in both arguments, and for
p>1
, it is continuously differentiable in
a
near
a=\theta
.

2.

For each
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the function
\theta\mapsto f(x;\theta)
is continuously differentiable.

3.

For estimators in a sufficiently regular subclass of
\mathscr{D}
, the Fréchet derivative
\nabla R_{L}(\hat{\theta})
exists in an
L_{2}
sense, and Taylor expansions are valid around local minimizers.

4.

For
p>1
and
q>1
, the worst-case risk functionals
R_{p}(\hat{\theta})
and
R_{q}(\hat{\theta})
are twice Fréchet differentiable along smooth paths in
\mathscr{D}
.

Remark 2.11
.

These smoothness conditions justify the use of perturbation and Taylor expansion arguments in proving exclusivity results. For
p=1
or
q=1
, the nonsmoothness of the loss function requires separate treatment, which we do not consider here.

3
Exclusivity Classes and Partitions

We now introduce the central theoretical concepts of this work: exclusivity regions, exclusivity classes, and realizable exclusivity partitions. These notions formalize the idea that different families of loss functions may uniquely characterize the optimality behavior of estimators.

Definition 3.1

(Exclusivity region)
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be a space of admissible loss functions, and let
\mathcal{O}
be a notion of estimator optimality (e.g., minmaxity, admissibility, Bayes optimality).
A subset
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathscr{L}
is called an
exclusivity region under
\mathcal{O}
if no estimator is
\mathcal{O}
-optimal for both some
L\in\mathcal{C}
and any
L^{\prime}\in\mathscr{L}\setminus\mathcal{C}
.

Definition 3.2

(Exclusivity class of an estimator)
.

Given an estimator
\delta^{*}
, a subset
\eta(\delta^{*},\mathcal{O})\subseteq\mathscr{L}
is called an
exclusivity class for
\delta^{*}
under
\mathcal{O}
if:

(i)

\eta(\delta^{*},\mathcal{O})
is an exclusivity region under
\mathcal{O}
;

(ii)

\delta^{*}
is
\mathcal{O}
-optimal for at least one loss
L\in\eta(\delta^{*},\mathcal{O})
.

Remark 3.3

(Trivial exclusivity classes)
.

Trivially, every estimator admits an exclusivity class. Proposition
3.5
shows that even taking
\eta(\delta^{*},\mathcal{O})=\mathscr{L}
satisfies the definition, although such classes are uninformative. Our interest lies in identifying
nontrivial
, structured exclusivity classes that reflect deeper properties of the loss functions and estimator behavior.

Remark 3.4

(Non-uniqueness and maximality)
.

The exclusivity class
\eta(\delta^{*},\mathcal{O})
is not necessarily unique. Different estimators may share the same exclusivity class, and a given estimator may admit multiple classes under different notions of optimality. If
\eta(\delta^{*},\mathcal{O})
is not properly contained in any larger exclusivity region for
\delta^{*}
, we call it
maximal
.

Proposition 3.5

(Existence of trivial exclusivity classes)
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be a space of admissible loss functions and
\mathcal{O}
a notion of optimality. For every estimator
\delta^{*}
, there exists at least one exclusivity class
\eta(\delta^{*},\mathcal{O})\subseteq\mathscr{L}
satisfying Definition
3.2
.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathscr{L}
denote the set of loss functions under which
\delta^{*}
is
\mathcal{O}
-optimal. If
\mathscr{A}\neq\emptyset
, then
\mathscr{A}
satisfies the conditions of an exclusivity class. If
\mathscr{A}=\emptyset
, then formally taking
\eta(\delta^{*},\mathcal{O})=\mathscr{L}
satisfies the exclusivity condition vacuously. Thus, an exclusivity class exists for every estimator.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

The set
\mathscr{L}
itself is always a trivial exclusivity class for any estimator
\delta^{*}
: if
\delta^{*}
is not optimal for any loss function in
\mathscr{L}
, then the exclusivity condition in Definition
3.2
(i) is vacuously satisfied.
This follows immediately from Proposition
3.5
.

We now introduce a refined notion of realizability for exclusivity partitions, which emphasizes the relevance and non-emptiness of each class without requiring the partition to be total.

Definition 3.7

(Realizable exclusivity partition)
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be a space of admissible loss functions, and let
\mathcal{O}
be a notion of optimality.

A family of pairwise disjoint sets
\{\mathcal{C}_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is called a
realizable exclusivity partition under
\mathcal{O}
if:

(i)

Each
\mathcal{C}_{i}
is an exclusivity region under
\mathcal{O}
;

(ii)

The sets
\mathcal{C}_{i}
are pairwise disjoint:

\mathcal{C}_{i}\cap\mathcal{C}_{j}=\emptyset\quad\text{for all }i\neq j;

(iii)

For each
i\in I
, there exists an estimator
\delta_{i}
such that
\delta_{i}
is
\mathcal{O}
-optimal for at least one loss function
L\in\mathcal{C}_{i}
.

We call such a partition
total
if, in addition, the union of the regions covers the entire space:

\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{C}_{i}=\mathscr{L}.

Remark 3.8

(Nontriviality of partitions)
.

We are primarily interested in
nontrivial
realizable partitions. This excludes, for instance:

•

Partitions in which one class equals the entire space
\mathscr{L}
;

•

Artificial constructions where each class
\mathcal{C}_{i}
is simply the set of loss functions for which a particular estimator
\delta_{i}
is optimal.

In particular, the partition should reflect intrinsic properties of the loss functions themselves (e.g., asymptotic behavior, convexity, or sensitivity) rather than arbitrary indexing based on estimators. Only then does the structure of the partition carry meaningful geometric or analytic information.

4
Exclusivity of Minmax Estimators for Power-Class Losses

We now present our main theoretical result, which formalizes the idea that a minmax-optimal estimator for one power-class loss cannot simultaneously be minmax-optimal for a different power-class loss with a distinct exponent.

Throughout this section,
\mathcal{L}_{p}
and
\mathcal{L}_{q}
denote the exclusivity classes of loss functions defined in Definition
2.5
, associated respectively with exponents
p
and
q
. We consider arbitrary functions
L_{p}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
and
L_{q}\in\mathcal{L}_{q}
throughout.

Theorem 4.1

(Exclusivity of minmax estimators across power-class losses)
.

Assume that the model and losses satisfy Assumptions
2.9
–
2.10
.
Let
p\neq q
be two positive exponents (
p,q\neq 1
), and let
L_{p}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
,
L_{q}\in\mathcal{L}_{q}
be two loss functions from their respective power-classes.

Suppose an estimator
\delta^{*}\in\mathscr{D}
is minmax for
L_{p}
. Then
\delta^{*}
cannot be minmax for
L_{q}
.
In particular, no single estimator can be minmax for both a loss in
\mathcal{L}_{p}
and a loss in
\mathcal{L}_{q}
when
p\neq q
.
Thus, the power-classes
\mathcal{L}_{p}
form minmax exclusivity classes.

Sketch of Proof.

Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists an estimator
\delta^{\dagger}
that is simultaneously minmax for both
L_{p}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
and
L_{q}\in\mathcal{L}_{q}
with
p\neq q
.

First, we define the corresponding worst-case risk functionals:

\displaystyle R_{L_{p}}(\delta)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[L_{p}\left(\theta,\delta(X)\right)\right],

(10)

\displaystyle R_{L_{q}}(\delta)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[L_{q}\left(\theta,\delta(X)\right)\right].

(11)

By assumption,
\delta^{\dagger}
minimizes both. Since the supremum in
R_{L_{q}}
is attained, let
\theta_{q}^{*}\in\Theta
denote a point where the worst-case
L_{q}
risk is achieved.

We perturb
\delta^{\dagger}
slightly in the direction of
\theta_{q}^{*}
by defining

\tilde{\delta}(X):=\delta^{\dagger}(X)+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{sgn}\bigl(\theta_{q}^{*}-\delta^{\dagger}(X)\bigr),

(12)

with a small
\varepsilon>0
.

By the local structure of
\mathcal{L}_{q}
, each
L_{q}\in\mathcal{L}_{q}
behaves like
|\theta-a|^{q}
near
\theta
. Hence this perturbation reduces the pointwise loss at
\theta_{q}^{*}
, and thus:

R_{L_{q}}(\tilde{\delta})<R_{L_{q}}(\delta^{\dagger})

(13)

for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
.

Meanwhile, since
\delta^{\dagger}
is minmax for
L_{p}
, and
L_{p}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
behaves like
|\theta-a|^{p}
, the perturbation does not affect the
L_{p}
risk to first order:

R_{L_{p}}(\tilde{\delta})=R_{L_{p}}(\delta^{\dagger})+o(\varepsilon).

(14)

Remark 4.2
.

Because each
\mathcal{L}_{p}
is a positive cone, scalar multiples of canonical losses belong to the same exclusivity class. Therefore, without loss of generality, it suffices to perform the argument using canonical representatives (e.g.,
L_{p}(\theta,a)=|\theta-a|^{p}
) up to scaling, since optimality is invariant under positive scaling of the loss.

Thus,
\tilde{\delta}
yields a strictly smaller worst-case
L_{q}
risk than
\delta^{\dagger}
, while the worst-case
L_{p}
risk remains essentially unchanged. This contradicts the assumed minmaxity of
\delta^{\dagger}
for
L_{q}
.

Therefore, no estimator can be minmax for both a loss in
\mathcal{L}_{p}
and a loss in
\mathcal{L}_{q}
when
p\neq q
.

A complete, rigorous proof including formal differentiability and Taylor expansions is provided in Section
6
.
∎

Theorem
4.1
shows that each power-class
\mathcal{L}_{p}
is isolated from the others with respect to minmax optimality: a minmax estimator tailored to one class fails to be minmax under another. This immediately implies a broader limitation on the existence of universal procedures:

Corollary 4.3

(Non-existence of universal minmax estimators)
.

There does not exist an estimator that is minmax under every loss function in the union
\bigcup_{p>0}\mathcal{L}_{p}
. In particular, no single estimator can be minmax for all power-class losses.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Theorem
4.1
. For any pair
p\neq q
, no estimator can be minmax for both
L_{p}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
and
L_{q}\in\mathcal{L}_{q}
. Hence, a universal estimator minmax over all
\mathcal{L}_{p}
cannot exist.
∎

Proposition 4.4

(Realizability of the power-class exclusivity partition)
.

Under the model and Assumptions
2.9
–
2.10
, for every exponent
p>0
, there exists an estimator
\hat{\theta}_{p}\in\mathscr{D}
that is minmax for a loss function in the class
\mathcal{L}_{p}
. In particular, taking the canonical loss
L_{p}(\theta,a)=|\theta-a|^{p}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
, there exists a minmax estimator for
L_{p}
. Consequently, the family
\{\mathcal{L}_{p}:p>0\}
forms a realizable (though not total) exclusivity partition under minmax optimality.

Proof.

Fix any
p>0
, and consider the canonical power-type loss

L_{p}(\theta,a):=|\theta-a|^{p},

which belongs to the class
\mathcal{L}_{p}
by Definition
2.5
.

Under Assumptions
2.9
–
2.10
, the statistical model
\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
is dominated by a reference measure, and each
P_{\theta}
admits a density
f(x;\theta)
that is continuous in
\theta
. The risk function

R_{L_{p}}(\theta,\delta):=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[L_{p}(\theta,\delta(X))\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[|\theta-\delta(X)|^{p}\right]

is finite and continuous in
\theta
for all
\delta\in\mathscr{D}
, and
\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}R_{L_{p}}(\theta,\delta)
is finite and attained (or can be approximated arbitrarily closely) for every
\delta\in\mathscr{D}
.

By standard decision-theoretic arguments (e.g.,
Berger 1985
, Ch. 5), these regularity conditions imply the existence of a minimax estimator
\hat{\theta}_{p}\in\mathscr{D}
satisfying

\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}R_{L_{p}}(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{p})=\inf_{\delta\in\mathscr{D}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}R_{L_{p}}(\theta,\delta).

Thus, for each
p>0
, we have exhibited a specific loss
L_{p}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
and an estimator
\hat{\theta}_{p}
that is minimax for that loss. Therefore, every class
\mathcal{L}_{p}
contains at least one estimator that is minmax-optimal for a loss in that class.

Combined with Theorem
4.1
, which establishes that no estimator can be minmax for losses in two distinct classes
\mathcal{L}_{p}
and
\mathcal{L}_{q}
with
p\neq q
, we conclude that the family
\{\mathcal{L}_{p}:p>0\}
satisfies the conditions of a realizable exclusivity partition (cf. Definition
3.7
).

Note that this partition is not total, since
\bigcup_{p>0}\mathcal{L}_{p}\subsetneq\mathscr{L}
, but it is realizable: every class is populated by at least one estimator, and the classes are pairwise exclusive with respect to minmax optimality.
∎

5
Topological and Algebraic Structure of Loss Functions and Exclusivity Classes

Before presenting the full proof of Theorem
4.1
, we briefly examine the algebraic and topological structure of the space of loss functions, with particular attention to how exclusivity classes are embedded within it.
This structure motivates the reduction to canonical representatives in our arguments and illuminates the nature of separation between optimality classes.

Let
\mathscr{L}
denote the ambient space of all continuous (and, when required, differentiable) non-negative loss functions

L:\Theta\times\Theta\longrightarrow[0,\infty).

We endow
\mathscr{L}
with the natural pointwise operations:

(\alpha L_{1}+L_{2})(\theta,a):=\alpha\,L_{1}(\theta,a)+L_{2}(\theta,a),\qquad(\theta,a)\in\Theta\times\Theta,

for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathscr{L}
.
Because loss functions must remain non-negative,
\mathscr{L}
is closed under addition and non-negative scalar multiplication, but not under multiplication by negative scalars.
Hence,
\mathscr{L}
is a convex cone (rather than a vector space) embedded in the Banach space
C(\Theta\times\Theta)
of continuous real-valued functions.
We use the sup-norm topology (or uniform convergence on compacta) on
\mathscr{L}
throughout, though we do not depend on finer topological structure.

Let us focus on one particular family of exclusivity classes: the power-type classes, introduced in Definition
2.5
.
Each such class
\mathcal{L}_{p}
is defined by the local behavior

L_{p}(\theta,a)=c\,|\theta-a|^{p}+o\left(|\theta-a|^{p}\right)\quad\text{as }a\to\theta,\quad\text{with }c>0.

That is,
\mathcal{L}_{p}
consists of all loss functions that exhibit local power-type behavior with exponent
p
and positive leading coefficient.
As such,
\mathcal{L}_{p}
is an exclusivity class for minmax estimators, and we are interested in its algebraic properties within
\mathscr{L}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any fixed
p>0
, the class
\mathcal{L}_{p}
is closed under multiplication by positive scalars.
Thus, each
\mathcal{L}_{p}
is a convex cone inside
\mathscr{L}
.

Proof.

Let
L\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
and let
\lambda>0
. Then

(\lambda L)(\theta,a)=\lambda\,c\,|\theta-a|^{p}+\lambda\,o(|\theta-a|^{p}),

which preserves the local behavior required for membership in
\mathcal{L}_{p}
.
Hence,
\lambda L\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
, establishing closure under positive scaling.

However,
\mathcal{L}_{p}
is not closed under general addition.
If
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
have distinct leading constants or higher-order terms, then
L_{1}+L_{2}
generally fails to be proportional to
|\theta-a|^{p}
, and may not even retain the required asymptotic form.
Moreover, additive inverses are not present in
\mathscr{L}
due to the non-negativity constraint.
Therefore,
\mathcal{L}_{p}
is a convex cone but not a vector subspace.
∎

5.1
Implications

Each exclusivity class
\mathcal{L}_{p}
is conically structured within
\mathscr{L}
: it is closed under scaling but not under addition.
Thus, it behaves like a ray or sector within the ambient loss function space.
Moving between two such exclusivity classes
\mathcal{L}_{p}
and
\mathcal{L}_{q}
for
p\neq q
requires a qualitative change in local behavior of the loss, not a smooth or linear interpolation.

This rigidity has two major implications.
First, it supports the intuition behind the main result: an estimator minmax-optimal for some loss in
\mathcal{L}_{p}
cannot generally remain optimal under a loss from
\mathcal{L}_{q}
.
Second, in the formal proof of Theorem
4.1
, the conic structure allows us to reduce the argument to the canonical representatives
L(\theta,a)=|\theta-a|^{p}
without loss of generality—any other loss in
\mathcal{L}_{p}
differs only by a multiplicative constant and negligible higher-order terms.

Proof of Theorem
4.1

Proof.

Remark (Reduction to canonical losses).

From Lemma
5.1
, each class
\mathcal{L}_{p}
is a cone under positive scalar multiplication.
This means that for any
L_{p}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
there exists a constant
c_{p}>0
such that

L_{p}(\theta,a)=c_{p}|\theta-a|^{p},

and similarly for
L_{q}\in\mathcal{L}_{q}
with constant
c_{q}>0
.
Since scaling the loss function by a positive constant uniformly scales all risks, the minmax property of an estimator is preserved under such scaling.
Therefore, we may assume without loss of generality that the losses under consideration have the canonical forms

L_{p}(\theta,a)=|\theta-a|^{p}\quad\text{and}\quad L_{q}(\theta,a)=|\theta-a|^{q}.

We proceed by contradiction.
Suppose that there exists an estimator
\delta^{*}_{p}
that is minmax simultaneously for both
L_{p}
and
L_{q}
, with
p\neq q
.
Without loss of generality, assume
p<q
.

Since
\delta^{*}_{p}
is minmax for
L_{p}
, it satisfies

\displaystyle R_{p}(\delta^{*}_{p})

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\bigl[L_{p}\bigl(\theta,\delta^{*}_{p}(X)\bigr)\bigr]

\displaystyle=\inf_{\delta\in\mathscr{D}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\bigl[L_{p}\bigl(\theta,\delta(X)\bigr)\bigr].

(15)

Remark 6.1

(Notation)
.

For any estimator
\delta\in\mathscr{D}
, we write

\displaystyle R_{p}(\delta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}R_{p}(\theta,\delta),

(16)

\displaystyle R_{q}(\delta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}R_{q}(\theta,\delta),

(17)

\displaystyle R_{r}(\theta,\delta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\bigl[L_{r}\bigl(\theta,\delta(X)\bigr)\bigr],\quad r\in\{p,q\}.

(18)

By Assumptions
2.9
–
2.10
, the functionals
R_{p}(\cdot)
and
R_{q}(\cdot)
are Fréchet differentiable in a neighborhood of
\delta^{*}_{p}
.

Let

g:=\nabla R_{q}(\delta^{*}_{p}).

If
g=0
, then
\delta^{*}_{p}
would be a stationary point of
R_{q}
, and if additionally the Hessian
\nabla^{2}R_{q}(\delta^{*}_{p})
were positive definite, this would imply that
\delta^{*}_{p}
is a local minimizer of
R_{q}
, contradicting the difference in structure between
R_{p}
and
R_{q}
.
Therefore, we must have
g\neq 0
.

Remark 6.2
.

It is not necessary for
g
to point in a specific direction.
If
g^{\top}v>0
for some direction
v
, then moving along
-v
gives a local descent.
Hence, the existence of
g\neq 0
guarantees the existence of a descent direction for
R_{q}
.

Let
v:=-\frac{g}{\|g\|}
be a normalized descent direction.
We define the perturbed estimator as

\delta_{\alpha}:=\delta^{*}_{p}+\alpha v,\qquad\text{for small }\alpha>0.

(19)

6.1
Change in the
L_{q}
worst-case risk

By Taylor expansion:

\displaystyle R_{q}(\delta_{\alpha})=R_{q}(\delta^{*}_{p})+\alpha\nabla R_{q}(\delta^{*}_{p})\cdot v+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}v^{\top}\nabla^{2}R_{q}(\xi_{q})v,

(20)

for some
\xi_{q}
on the segment between
\delta^{*}_{p}
and
\delta_{\alpha}
.
Since
v=-\frac{g}{\|g\|}
, the first-order term is
-\|g\|<0
, and the second-order term is bounded.
Thus, for sufficiently small
\alpha
, the whole expression decreases:

R_{q}(\delta_{\alpha})<R_{q}(\delta^{*}_{p}).

6.2
Change in the
L_{p}
worst-case risk

Similarly,

\displaystyle R_{p}(\delta_{\alpha})=R_{p}(\delta^{*}_{p})+\alpha\nabla R_{p}(\delta^{*}_{p})\cdot v+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}v^{\top}\nabla^{2}R_{p}(\xi_{p})v,

(21)

for some
\xi_{p}
between
\delta^{*}_{p}
and
\delta_{\alpha}
.
Since
\delta^{*}_{p}
is a minimizer of
R_{p}
, we have
\nabla R_{p}(\delta^{*}_{p})=0
, so:

R_{p}(\delta_{\alpha})=R_{p}(\delta^{*}_{p})+O(\alpha^{2}),

which can be made arbitrarily small for small
\alpha
.

6.3
Conclusion

We have constructed an estimator
\delta_{\alpha}
such that:

R_{q}(\delta_{\alpha})<R_{q}(\delta^{*}_{p}),\quad R_{p}(\delta_{\alpha})\leq R_{p}(\delta^{*}_{p})+\eta,

for arbitrarily small
\eta>0
. That is,
\delta_{\alpha}
achieves strictly smaller worst-case risk under the loss
L_{q}
, while remaining essentially as good as
\delta^{*}_{p}
under
L_{p}
.

This violates the assumed joint minmaxity of
\delta^{*}_{p}
: if
\delta^{*}_{p}
were truly minimax for both losses, then no perturbation should be able to improve the risk for one loss function without worsening it for the other.

Intuition.
The key idea is that losses with different exponents penalize deviations differently. A loss with larger exponent
q>p
disproportionately emphasizes larger errors. Therefore, by shifting the estimator in a direction that reduces error under
L_{q}
, we can lower its worst-case risk, while the increase in
L_{p}
-risk remains negligible — due to its flatter penalty profile. This asymmetry in sensitivity is what forces the contradiction.

Hence, no estimator can be minmax for both
L_{p}
and
L_{q}
when
p\neq q
, completing the proof.
∎

Example: Illustration for arbitrarily close exponents.

A natural concern may arise regarding the strength of Theorem
4.1
, especially when exponents
p
and
q
differ by an arbitrarily small amount (e.g.,
q=p+\varepsilon
for small
\varepsilon>0
). One might question whether such a slight perturbation in the loss still forces an estimator to leave its optimality class.

To address this, we present in Appendix
A
a concrete analytical example for
p=2
and
q=2+\varepsilon
, where we show that the sample mean (which is minmax for squared error loss) ceases to be minmax under a slightly perturbed loss
L_{q}(\theta,a)=|\theta-a|^{2+\varepsilon}
. This illustrates that the exclusivity result holds even in the limit of vanishing difference in exponents.

7
Discussion and Future Work

This work introduces a new framework for understanding the relationship between loss functions and estimator optimality via the concept of
exclusivity classes
—collections of loss functions such that no estimator can be optimal (in a given sense) for any two functions from distinct classes.
We have formalized this notion, proposed conditions under which exclusivity classes form conic substructures of the loss function space, and provided a rigorous proof that the power-type losses
\mathcal{L}_{p}
form such an exclusivity class with respect to minmax optimality.

This insight elevates a long-understood phenomenon—namely, that optimal estimators under different loss functions tend to differ—into a general structural theory.
Our results suggest that the dependency of estimator optimality on the loss function is not merely a feature of specific examples, but reflects a deeper incompatibility across qualitatively distinct families of loss criteria.

7.1
Outlook and Research Directions

Several promising directions emerge from the framework developed in this work.

First, although our analysis focuses on minmax estimators in a finite-sample setting, the concept of exclusivity is not inherently limited to this regime. One natural extension is to consider exclusivity classes under various
asymptotic
notions of optimality. These include asymptotic minmaxity, local asymptotic normality (LAN) settings, and risk convergence under increasing sample size. Exploring whether asymptotic exclusivity classes exist—and how they relate to their finite-sample counterparts—could shed new light on estimator behavior in large-sample regimes. Such results may further help reconcile frequentist and Bayesian asymptotics under loss-sensitive conditions.

Second, exclusivity may also be defined relative to other optimality criteria beyond minmaxity, such as admissibility, Bayes optimality, or asymptotic efficiency. These lead to what we call
optimality-dependent exclusivity classes
, whose structure, overlap, and comparative strength remain to be systematically understood. This could reveal deeper incompatibility results between estimators across decision-theoretic paradigms.

Third, from a geometric and algebraic perspective, we have shown that power-type exclusivity classes form convex cones in the ambient space
\mathscr{L}
of admissible loss functions. This raises natural questions: Are all exclusivity classes conic? Are some closed or linearly generated? Can one exploit geometric properties to classify incompatibilities among estimators? While we have proven that
\mathcal{L}_{p}
is not a linear subspace, further investigation may uncover conditions under which exclusivity classes are convex, closed under addition, or possess manifold-like structure.

Fourth, we introduced the notion of
realizable exclusivity partitions
: partitions of the loss function space such that each class contains at least one estimator that is not optimal for any loss outside it. We also identified several trivial examples—such as singleton classes, full space
\mathscr{L}
or partitions defined solely by the set of optimal estimators—that lack conceptual depth. This motivates the search for
nontrivial, informative
exclusivity partitions, grounded in structural or analytic properties of the loss functions themselves.

Finally, we propose the following conjecture, which would represent a major step forward in understanding the geometry of loss functions and estimator design:

Conjecture 7.1

(Existence of a total nontrivial realizable exclusivity partition)
.

There exists a nontrivial realizable partition of the space of admissible loss functions into pairwise disjoint exclusivity classes, such that every loss function belongs to exactly one class and each class admits an estimator that is not optimal for any loss function outside the class.

Establishing such a global structure would uncover a fundamental decomposition of the decision-theoretic landscape—an analogue of foliation or phase separation—based not on data or models, but on the geometry of loss function spaces and the limits of estimator generalizability.

In conclusion, exclusivity classes provide a new lens through which to view optimality in statistical inference. Extending this framework to asymptotic regimes, new criteria, and refined geometric settings opens the door to a richer understanding of how estimators behave under competing demands of performance and generality. We hope this line of inquiry bridges perspectives from decision theory, asymptotic analysis, and functional geometry.

Power-Shift Exclusivity for

Proposition A.1

(Exclusivity of the sample mean for
L_{2}
vs.
L_{2+\varepsilon}
)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,1)
be i.i.d. observations, and consider the loss
L_{q}(\theta,a)=|\theta-a|^{q}
, with
q=2+\varepsilon
, for small
\varepsilon>0
.
Then the sample mean
\delta_{0}(X)=\bar{X}
is the unique minmax estimator under squared loss
L_{2}
, but it is not minmax for
L_{q}
.

Proof.

It is classical that for the Gaussian location model with known variance, the sample mean
\delta_{0}(X)=\bar{X}
minimizes the worst-case risk under squared loss
L_{2}(\theta,a)=(\theta-a)^{2}
, and is thus minmax.

We show that
\delta_{0}
fails to be minmax under the slightly perturbed loss
L_{q}
with
q=2+\varepsilon
, by constructing a perturbation that strictly decreases the worst-case
L_{q}
risk.

Let
R_{q}(\theta,\delta):=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[|\delta(X)-\theta|^{q}]
and define a perturbed estimator:

\delta_{\alpha}(X):=\bar{X}-\alpha,\qquad\alpha>0.

Fix any
\theta\in\Theta
, and let
Z:=\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}-\theta)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Then:

\delta_{\alpha}(X)-\theta=\bar{X}-\theta-\alpha=\frac{Z}{\sqrt{n}}-\alpha,

so

R_{q}(\theta,\delta_{\alpha})=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\left|\delta_{\alpha}(X)-\theta\right|^{q}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{Z}{\sqrt{n}}-\alpha\right|^{q}\right]=:f(\alpha).

We compute the derivative
f^{\prime}(\alpha)
to assess how the risk changes under this perturbation:

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{Z}{\sqrt{n}}-\alpha\right|^{q}\right]=-q\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{Z}{\sqrt{n}}-\alpha\right|^{q-1}\cdot\operatorname{sgn}\left(\frac{Z}{\sqrt{n}}-\alpha\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-q\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{Z}{\sqrt{n}}-\alpha\right)\cdot\left|\frac{Z}{\sqrt{n}}-\alpha\right|^{q-2}\right].

Since the distribution of
Z
is symmetric around 0, and
\alpha>0
, the random variable
\frac{Z}{\sqrt{n}}-\alpha
is negatively biased, and thus the expectation is strictly positive. Therefore:

f^{\prime}(\alpha)<0.

In particular, for small
\alpha>0
, the worst-case
L_{q}
risk decreases strictly:

R_{q}(\theta,\delta_{\alpha})<R_{q}(\theta,\delta_{0}).

Since this holds for every
\theta\in\Theta
, it follows that:

\sup_{\theta}R_{q}(\theta,\delta_{\alpha})<\sup_{\theta}R_{q}(\theta,\delta_{0}),

so the worst-case risk of
\delta_{\alpha}
is strictly smaller than that of the sample mean. Hence,
\delta_{0}
is not minmax for
L_{q}
, even for arbitrarily small
\varepsilon>0
.

This example illustrates that power-type exclusivity holds even in the limit as
q\to p
, confirming the sharp separation of minmax optimality between different
\mathcal{L}_{p}
classes.
