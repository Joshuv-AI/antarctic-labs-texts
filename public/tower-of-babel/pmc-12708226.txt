Outcome Analysis of Cohesive Gel Implants With Dual-Plane Technique in Primary Buttock Augmentation
Authors: Alexander Aslani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Buttock augmentation is increasingly popular in body contouring but remains controversial within the surgical community, partly due to limited larger scale studies on cohesive gel buttock implants, in contrast to the extensive data available for breast implants. The author previously described a composite buttock augmentation technique involving subcutaneous expansion vibration lipofilling and later proposed a dual-plane pocket dissection for improved implant stability.Methods:A retrospective review was conducted on 482 patients who underwent buttock augmentation using the same surgical technique by a single surgeon from June 2020 to May 2022. After excluding cases involving secondary implants or additional mesh, 423 patients were included. All patients received composite augmentation, consisting of an implant combined with fat grafting.Results:In the 423 dual-plane procedures, the average surgery time was 1 hour and 50 minutes, with follow-up ranging from 21 to 55 months. The overall complication rate related to implants was 10.8%, with seroma being the most frequent issue. Complications were categorized based on whether they stemmed from the implant or from the liposuction/fat grafting component and were analyzed for frequency, severity, and resolution.Conclusions:Composite buttock augmentation using a dual-plane pocket technique offers the benefits of both submuscular and intramuscular dissection. This method provides better soft tissue coverage and implant stability, presenting it as a reliable and effective technique for gluteal enhancement.

Takeaways

Question: What is the performance of cohesive silicone gel buttock implants in body contouring surgery, given that all existing larger studies have focused on solid silicone block implants?

Findings: The use of cohesive silicone gel buttock implants is associated with an acceptable complication, which possibly differs from that of solid rubber block implants.

Meaning: A consistent technique and the use of silicone gel buttock implants make buttock augmentation a reliable option in body contouring surgery.

INTRODUCTION

The American Society of Plastic Surgeons annual statistics report a 6% annual increase in buttock implant procedures in the United States in 2023.1 Different surgical approaches have been described for buttock implant pocket design, including subfascial, submuscular, and intramuscular techniques.2–4 Composite buttock augmentation has been described using both the syringe technique5 and expansion vibration lipofilling (EVL).6 The advantage of additional fat injection is enhanced soft tissue padding, which strengthens the soft tissue envelope. With the gluteus muscle not having uniform thickness, but being thinner in the cranial third and thickening toward the caudal third,7 the cranial portion of the pocket is an anatomically less favorable zone for a purely intramuscular pocket design. Hence, we proposed combining sub- and intramuscular dissection to obtain a dual-plane pocket with double muscle coverage in the cranial portion (fibers of both gluteus medius and maximus),8 and our hypothesis is that this technique allows the interested surgeon to perform buttock implant surgery with optimized soft tissue cover, an acceptable complication rate, and a good long-term safety profile.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

In this retrospective study, we reviewed the records of 482 patients who underwent composite buttock augmentation using the dual-plane technique at our clinic between June 2020 and May 2022. We excluded patients who had secondary buttock augmentation (47 patients) or composite buttock augmentation with 4-hydroxybutyrate mesh inlay around the implant (12 patients). Beyond the stated criteria, no further exclusion criteria were implemented in the analysis of the patient group.

All of the remaining 423 patients underwent primary composite buttock augmentation with the dual-plane dissection technique using cohesive silicone gel implants combined with EVL into the subcutaneous plane, as described previously.8 The records were reviewed for the occurrence of any unforeseen complications and their treatment. Patient follow-up ranged from 21 to 55 months. All procedures performed in this study were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

COMPOSITE DUAL-PLANE BUTTOCK AUGMENTATION TECHNIQUE: LIPOSUCTION AND FAT GRAFTING

The operation was started with the patient in a supine position. Through incisions in the groin area (bikini line), at the umbilicus, and depending on the case, along the inframammary line, we started the procedure with liposuction of the abdomen and flanks. We prefer the simultaneous aspiration and fat equalization technique, as described by Wall and Lee.9 Tumescent infiltration was calculated in relation to the patient’s body mass index to avoid overinfiltration and dilutional anemia due to excessive intravascular absorption of the infiltrated fluid.6 Fat was collected in a closed sterile-circuit canister system (Wells Johnson Company, AZ).

Following decantation of the harvested fat, the tubing was reversed, and the fat was reinjected into the buttocks using EVL technique with a 4-mm basket cannula and vibrating handpiece at 300 mL/min infiltration speed as described by Del Vecchio and Wall.6 We favor infiltration of fat before preparing implant pockets, but this can be performed after pocket dissection and implant placement, depending on surgeon preference. COMPOSITE DUAL-PLANE BUTTOCK AUGMENTATION TECHNIQUE: DUAL-PLANE POCKET DISSECTION

Through 2 incisions, 1 cm from the gluteal cleft, dissection was carried down to the gluteus muscle fascia. Gluteus maximus muscle fibers were cut vertically approximately 2 cm lateral to the edge of the sacrum bilaterally. In the cranial part of the pocket, the surgeon seeks to find a submuscular plane by dissecting down to the concave posterior surface of the iliac bone (iliac shovel) above the level of the upper edge of the sciatic foramen, taking the dissection beneath the gluteus maximus and the fibers of the gluteus medius by separating them from the bone.

Submuscular dissection using blunt instruments is continued until reaching the upper edge of the sciatic foramen. Downward blunt dissection consistently provides access to a deep intramuscular plane. The level of change of the plane is at the height of the upper border of the sciatic foramen, and up to this point, submuscular dissection does not endanger the sciatic nerve. Dissection proceeds into the natural intramuscular cleavage plane, as described by Rodríguez-García et al.7

Toward the lateral side, it is essential to avoid injuring the lateral border of the gluteus maximus muscle. Cohesive gel buttock implants were inserted using a sterile introducer sleeve.

Size 14 drains were inserted, and the wounds were closed in 3 layers using a contralateral dynamic suspension technique. A Prevena vacuum assisted wound closure system was used for dressings and left in place for 7 days.

POSTOPERATIVE TREATMENT

A Prevena vacuum-assisted wound dressing was applied and kept in place for the first 7 postoperative days to optimize wound healing and avoid superficial wound breakdown. Patients were nursed on their backs for the first 24 hours after surgery to promote pressure hemostasis, after which they were turned to a prone position.

Mobilization is limited for the first 2 weeks to small walks and only essential movements. In our experience, restricting patient movement in the immediate postoperative period is beneficial to reduce the incidence of seromas but should not be to a degree that would expose the patient to a risk of thrombosis. Drains are removed on day 7 after surgery. Patients can use a foam bar placed under the posterior upper third of the thigh after day 5. The foam bar avoids full flexion of the hip and tension on the pocket sutures. Hip flexion beyond 45 degrees should be avoided to prevent tension on the dynamic suspension sutures. Patients are instructed to start light stretching exercises after day 3.

RESULTS

Over the years of developing our technique, we have gradually reduced and eliminated the use of anatomical implants. We use either plain round or biconvex round implants for all our cases. Plain round implants were used in 391 (92%) patients, whereas biconvex implants were used in 32 (8%) patients. The reason for this imbalance in the use of implant shape is that biconvex implants only start at a minimum size of 370 mL, and this is too large for most patients.

In the reviewed patient cohort, implant sizes ranged from 235 to 440 mL. The overall average volume was 300 mL. The volume of transferred fat ranged from 350 to 900 mL per side.

Procedures were completed in an average of 97 minutes with the conventional simultaneous aspiration and fat equalization liposuction technique and EVL fat grafting. Forty-eight (11%) patients underwent additional invasive radiofrequency energy-based liposuction, which meant an average additional surgery time of 25 minutes. This additional time was deducted from the calculation of the mean surgery time.

Results were assessed photographically by measuring the waist-to-hip ratio in the lateral view, which is best suited to describe buttock projection. We did not include patient satisfaction parameters (patient-reported outcome measures).

The average improvement in projection index was 26% for round implants and 32% for biconvex implants. COMPLICATIONS

The overall incidence of implant-related complications was 10.8%. Complications were evaluated in aggregate for all affected patients, without further differentiation based on implant type, implant volume, or the quantity of fat infiltrated. Complications were categorized as minor if they resolved without the need for surgical intervention, and as major if operative management was required to achieve resolution.

Seroma was the most frequent complication, occurring in 27 cases (6.3%). Nine (2.2%) of these seromas required surgical action, and the remaining 18 cases were resolved with conservative treatment only. Of the 9 surgically revised seromas, 5 were found to be infected, requiring implants to be removed (1.2%), whereas in the other 4 cases, open drainage of seroma, washout, and implant exchange solved the problem (0.94%).

Verified flipping of implants occurred in 8 cases (1.89%), which were treated with implant exchange or capsulorrhaphy. Verification of implant flipping was done clinically.

Further complications requiring surgical action were sciatic neuropraxia, which occurred 8 times in total in our series with sensorimotor involvement of the sciatic nerve (1.89%). We differentiated between sciatic nerve affection that occurs later than 24 hours postprocedure (secondary neuropraxia) and symptoms noted immediately postoperatively (primary neuropraxia). The clinical relevance of this finding is extremely high. Secondary neuropraxia is most likely caused by swelling and responds to anti-inflammatory medication (dexamethasone 10 mg intravenously) and physiotherapy. In our experience, with appropriate treatment, full resolution was achieved in all cases.

Primary neuropraxia refers to the presence of symptoms immediately after surgery. This dramatic complication warrants immediate implant removal. Therefore, it is very important to check sciatic nerve function directly after surgery, and our protocol is to perform this in the recovery room before the patient returns to the ward. Primary neuropraxia occurred in 1 case in our series, with M0 motoric paralysis of the sciatic nerve being noted before discharge from the recovery room.

The patient immediately returned to the theater, and the implants were removed. The integrity of the sciatic nerve was confirmed using magnetic resonance imaging and nerve conducton test. Complete recovery was achieved within 4 months, and the patient returned to have her implants 8 months later, without any problems.

In the residual cases, symptoms occurred more than 24 hours after surgery and were related to swelling. They were completely resolved with anti-inflammatory medication and physiotherapy. The average follow-up for patients with complications was 31 months. Outcomes are summarized in Table 1.

DISCUSSION

There is relatively little comparative literature on buttock implants. Mofid et al10 published a summary of 2226 cases operated on by 19 surgeons. This suggests that participants had a limited “per-head” experience. In addition, the article reports a broad variance of different techniques, which are difficult to compare (subfascial, intramuscular, and submuscular).

Although not explicitly specified, the US-based publication solely summarizes the experiences of various surgeons with Food & Drug Administration (FDA) class II solid silicone implants, rather than class III cohesive silicone gel implants like those used in Europe and South America, which correspond to FDA class III. There is a substantial difference in consistency of these different implant types, so it seems logical to suspect a different soft tissue response.

Sinno et al11 and Oregi et al12 report a large number of complications of gluteal augmentation with implants and fat grafting. Again, the implant cases analyzed were from many different surgeons, and different techniques were used, which are difficult to compare. Additionally, all cases involved the FDA class II solid block implants, which have a very different consistency compared with cohesive gel implants.

Morales et al13 published a technique they labeled “dual-plane buttock augmentation,” again using solid rubber block implants. The article was published in 2023 in the Aesthetic Surgery Journal and summarizes the authors’ experience of 175 cases of combined subfascial and intramuscular pocket design. The unfortunate thing in this context is that we8 had already published our concept of dual-plane design, combining a superior submuscular and inferior intramuscular pocket, in Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery in 2022. We published our article in the Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery just 6 months before this publication. It is regrettable that 2 very different surgical approaches were published with the same name, which may be puzzling for interested research physicians. Petit et al14 reported on 100 patients who underwent submuscular cohesive gel implant buttock augmentation without fat transfer. Elsaftawy et al15 published a series on submuscular buttock augmentation on 80 patients, using cohesive gel implants with fat transfer. Godoy et al16 reported on hybrid intramuscular buttock augmentation with cohesive gel implants, combining both primary and secondary cases, and using a syringe injection technique for fat transfer without hip expansion. Cárdenas-Camarena et al17 published a mixed series of 908 buttock augmentation cases, but only 62 patients received cohesive gel buttock implants. As far as we know, our series represents the largest number of patients treated with primary composite buttock augmentation using cohesive gel implants and a consistent dissection technique reported in the English-language literature so far. For a comprehensive overview of the newest developments in buttock augmentation surgery, Dai et al18 summarized a total of 492 publications related to buttock augmentation from 1999 to 2021.

CONCLUSIONS

Composite buttock augmentation with cohesive gel implants represents a reliable option in the armamentarium of the body contouring surgeon, achieving good changes in the waist-to-hip ratio with an acceptable complication rate in comparison to existing studies cited in this article. Further large-scale studies on the performance of cohesive gel buttock implants with consistent technique are desirable. When considering publications on outcomes in buttock implant surgery, we urge the surgical community to differentiate between those reporting on solid rubber block implants and cohesive silicone gel implants.
