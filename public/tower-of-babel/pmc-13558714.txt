Genetically diverse CC and DO mouse models enable comprehensive assessments of novel tuberculosis vaccines
Authors: Rocky Lai, Sherry L. Kurtz, Victoria Gould, Anthony Tran, Sam Afkhami, Roland Brosch, Cecilia S. Lindestam Arlehamn, Zhou Xing, Karen L. Elkins, Samuel M. Behar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Development of a new vaccine for tuberculosis (TB) to replace the BCG vaccine has been hampered by the lack of a preclinical vaccine model that accurately reflects the genetic heterogeneity of the human population. We previously tested both Collaborative Cross (CC) and Diversity Outbred (DO) mice, which collectively reflect the genetic diversity of an outbred population, as models for preclinical vaccine testing. Using both CC and DO mice, we compared the ability of two different strategies to enhance BCG-mediated protection against Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb). BCG::ESX-1Mmar is genetically modified to express the esx-1 region of Mycobacterium marinum. In contrast, the BCG/ChAd:TB strategy relies on boosting BCG-induced immunity using a chimp adenoviral vectored vaccine expressing the mycobacterial antigens Ag85A, TB10.4, and RpfB administered by the intranasal route. Both BCG::ESX-1Mmar and BCG/ChAd:TB vaccination strategies significantly protect mouse strains that were not well protected by BCG. Although both vaccination strategies were associated with reduced bacterial growth in CC and DO mice, bacterial control did not correlate with survival. Both BCG::ESX-1Mmar and BCG/ChAd:TB induced immune signatures after Mtb challenge in the tested CC strains that were previously associated with protection in traditional inbred mouse strains. Our findings established the use of both CC and DO mice as robust platforms that incorporate host genetic diversity for the preclinical evaluation of TB vaccines.

Introduction

Developing a new vaccine that provides better protection against tuberculosis (TB), the contagious pulmonary disease caused by Mycobacterial tuberculosis (Mtb) infection, is a global priority. Despite the wide use of BCG, the most widely administered vaccine against TB, the disease continues to be endemic in many parts of the world. Identifying a novel vaccine strategy that is more effective against pulmonary TB in adolescents and adults than the current BCG regimen is particularly urgent because of the continued emergence and spread of multidrug and extensively drug resistant (i.e., MDR and XDR) Mtb strains1.

Development of novel TB vaccines faces several challenges. Clinical testing is difficult due to high costs and logistics that are complicated by the lengthy time frame of disease progression. To enable scientific evaluation of novel vaccine strategies and to de-risk clinical assessment, animal models have been an integral part of the preclinical vaccine development pipeline for TB. Currently, most vaccines are tested in a limited number of inbred mouse strains, such as C57BL/6, BALB/c, and BALB/c x C57BL/6J F1 (CB6F1) hybrid2–4. While these inbred strains are established and well-characterized models, we have explored the use of genetically diverse mice to better model the heterogeneity present in the human populations5–7. The Collaborative Cross (CC) mice are a series of new recombinant inbred strains created to be genetic mosaics of eight diverse founder mouse strains that were obtained from around the world, and include three Mus musculus subspecies and three wild-derived strains8. Approximately 70 inbred CC strains are now available, which are homozygous at most loci and genetically stable. In contrast, Diversity Outbred (DO) mice are derived from the same eight founder strains, but instead of creating recombinant inbred strains, DO mice have been successively outbred. DO mice are predicted to be heterozygous at most genetic loci, and each mouse has a unique, individual genotype.

While the DO mouse model represents a genetically heterogenous population, the unique nature of each individual makes immunological and mechanistic studies challenging9. On the other hand, downstream immunological analyses are feasible with the CC strains, which are now inbred and genetically stable. Moreover, each CC strain has a different inherent susceptibility to Mtb infection and is protected to varying degrees by BCG5,6,10. We have established the feasibility of applying CC and DO mice to evaluate BCG-induced protection against low-dose aerosol Mtb infection. Our previous data6,7,11,12 and that of others13 demonstrated that vaccine responses and ensuing protection against TB in these models are heterogenous, at the level of the individual for DO mice and across strains for CC mice. Importantly, at least part of this heterogeneity is due to host genetics5–7,14. These findings suggest that the use of CC and DO models may improve the stringency of vaccine evaluation, especially when used together, and provide additional vaccine quality information compared to the use of traditional inbred strains.

Several approaches have been evaluated to improve the efficacy of BCG, such as by changing the route by which it is administered (i.e., oral, respiratory mucosa, or intravenously15); genetically modifying BCG to make it more immunogenic16; or boosting BCG with a second dose or a second vaccine (i.e., heterologous boosting17). In this study, we tested vaccines that represent different approaches to improving BCG, including a modified BCG and a vaccine intended to boost BCG. Modified BCG, denoted BCG::ESX-1Mmar, was developed by introducing the ESX-1 locus from M. marinum into BCG18. The deletion of the RD1 locus, which contains the ESX-1 secretion system, is the main attenuating mutation in BCG19,20. Because the bacterial factors expressed by the ESX-1 locus promote increased host immune responses, insertion of the M. marinum ESX-1 locus is intended to enhance vaccine antigenicity without affecting BCG virulence18. Previous results using C57BL/6 mice demonstrated that BCG::ESX-1Mmar enhanced Mtb control in lungs and spleens after challenge with the Mtb strains H37Rv or HN87818,21. The second vaccine is a trivalent chimpanzee adenoviral-vectored vaccine containing three antigens from Mtb (AdCh68_Ag85A:rpfB:TB10.4, here after referred to ChAd:TB)4. When given as a booster following BCG vaccination, ChAd:TB increased protection against Mtb in BALB/c, C3HeB/FeJ, and humanized mice. We compared these two novel TB vaccine candidates to BCG using a classical inbred mouse strain (C57BL/6), DO mice, and two CC strains that represent different responses to both BCG vaccination and Mtb susceptibility. We find that these two novel vaccines provided equivalent, if not superior, protection compared to BCG, as reflected in an expanded panel of challenge outcomes revealed by using genetically diverse mice.

Results

Evaluation of novel vaccine candidates using inbred C57BL/6 and Diversity Outbred mice

Using C57BL/6J and DO mice, we evaluated the novel vaccine candidate BCG::ESX-1Mmar, and we examined boosting BCG vaccination with intranasal ChAd:TB, compared to protection induced by standard BCG. Mice were vaccinated once subcutaneously with PBS as a control (naïve), BCG, or BCG::ESX-1Mmar. A subset of BCG-vaccinated mice was boosted four weeks after vaccination with intranasal ChAd:TB or PBS. Four weeks after the ChAd:TB booster (8 weeks after BCG administration), all mice were challenged with low dose aerosolized Mtb (Fig. 1A). Protection was evaluated after 7 or 14 weeks, based on previous data obtained using DO mice11,14. A subpopulation of “super-susceptible” DO mice succumb to infection within the first 5–6 weeks; therefore, evaluating early protection at 7 weeks allowed an assessment of protection in this super-susceptible subpopulation7,22. Seven weeks after Mtb challenge, BCG vaccination reduced lung and spleen Mtb burdens by ~ 1.0 log10 CFU in both C57BL/6 and DO mice compared to control naïve mice (Fig. 1B, C). As previously described, the outcomes were more heterogenous across the DO groups than among C57BL/6 mice7,11,12. BCG::ESX-1Mmar and BCG/ChAd:TB vaccination led to a similar level of CFU reduction in organs as standard BCG in both C57BL/6 and DO mice (Fig. 1B, C). The proportion of lung tissue involved with disease was similar in each vaccine group, and vaccination did not reduce the proportion of involved lung in any of the vaccine groups compared to naïve C57BL/6 or DO mice (Fig. 1D). Finally, BCG::ESX-1Mmar and BCG/ChAd:TB vaccination protected DO mice from weight loss based on the weights at euthanasia (seven weeks after Mtb challenge) compared to peak body weights (Fig. 1E); in contrast, BCG vaccination did not prevent weight loss compared to naïve mice.

When we evaluated protection 14 weeks after challenge, we found that the degree of protection had waned across BCG-, BCG::ESX-1Mmar-, and BCG/ChAd:TB-vaccinated DO and C57BL/6 mice. Although vaccination still resulted in low lung bacterial burdens in several animals, the reductions were not significant across the populations (Fig. 1F). In C57BL/6J mice, all vaccine strategies significantly reduced spleen Mtb burdens compared to naïve animals (Fig. 1G). In DO mice, only BCG/ChAd:TB-vaccinated mice had significantly reduced Mtb bacterial burdens compared to naïve mice. However, no significant differences in tissue Mtb burdens were observed between any vaccine groups in either C57BL/6 or DO mice. We examined lung histopathology at 14 weeks after Mtb challenge and found that while BCG vaccination protected lungs against tissue damage in C57BL/6 mice, no significant improvement was observed in BCG-vaccinated DO mice (Fig. 1H). In contrast, vaccination with both novel vaccine candidates BCG::ESX-1Mmar and BCG/ChAd.TB improved lung pathology in DO mice compared to standard BCG vaccination (Fig. 1H). At 14 weeks after Mtb challenge, BCG, BCG::ESX-1Mmar, and BCG/ChAd:TB vaccination helped prevent weight loss compared to control DO mice, but no significant differences were found between the different vaccine groups (Fig. 1I).

Finally, for 2 years we monitored survival of unvaccinated and vaccinated C57BL/6 and DO mice challenged with Mtb. Vaccination significantly extended survival in C57BL/6 mice, with a mean survival time (MST) of 427 days for BCG-vaccinated mice (p = 0.0001) or 619 days for BCG::ESX-1Mmar vaccinated mice (p = 0.0006), compared to 308 days for unvaccinated animals. BCG/ChAd:TB-vaccinated mice were not significantly protected, although MST was 455 days (p = 0.0891) (Fig.2A). BCG::ESX-1Mmar-vaccinated mice lived significantly longer than BCG-vaccinated mice (p = 0.0005). In DO mice, both BCG and BCG::ESX-1Mmar vaccination extended survival compared to unvaccinated mice. BCG::ESX-1Mmar vaccination did not significantly increase survival compared to BCG vaccination, and BCG/ChAd:TB vaccination did not extend survival compared to unvaccinated or BCG-vaccinated mice: unvaccinated DO mice had an MST of 257 days; 342 days for BCG-vaccinated DO mice (vs. unvaccinated mice, p = 0.0488); 335 for BCG::ESX-1Mmar-vaccinated mice (vs. unvaccinated mice, p = 0.0169; vs. BCG-vaccinated mice, p = 0.69), and 278 days for BCG/ChAd:TB-vaccinated mice (vs. unvaccinated mice, p = 0.2845; vs. BCG-vaccinated mice, p = 0.21) (Fig. 2B). However, all vaccines prevented early mortality, with 3/20 naïve DO mice succumbing to infection before 7 weeks compared to no early mortality in the vaccinated DO groups (Fig. 2B). Taken together, both BCG::ESX-1Mmar and BCG/ChAd:TB vaccination protected inbred C57BL/6 and DO mice as well as, if not better than, BCG. Evaluation of vaccines using a CC strain that is susceptible to Mtb infection but well-protected by BCG

To evaluate these novel vaccines in a more immunologically tractable model, we used two CC lines chosen based on prior work6. We previously identified CC037 as a line that was among the most protected by BCG vaccination at 4 weeks after challenge, despite having a greater susceptibility to low dose aerosol Mtb infection than C57BL/6 mice6. In the current study, we further evaluated the susceptibility of naïve CC037 mice to Mtb infection by performing a low dose aerosol challenge of C57BL/6 and CC037 mice and following all mice for survival. CC037 mice were clearly susceptible to aerosol Mtb infection, succumbing with an MST of 220 days, while C57BL/6 mice had an MST > 400 days (Mantel-Cox, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3). Given the susceptibility of CC037 mice by the criteria of both 4-week assessments and by long-term survival, we considered that the increased dynamic range of BCG-induced protection in CC037 mice might more effectively distinguish between vaccine strategies. To evaluate the ability of the vaccines to provide long-term protection against Mtb challenge, we monitored the survival of vaccinated CC037 mice using predetermined humane endpoints (Fig. 4E). As seen in initial studies (Fig. 3), Mtb-infected naïve CC037 mice had an MST of 220 days. Despite conferring superior Mtb control in terms of bacterial burdens when evaluated at four weeks, BCG vaccination did not increase the survival of CC037 mice (Fig. 4C). In contrast, while both BCG::ESX-1Mmar and BCG/ChAd:TB vaccination significantly enhanced the survival of CC037 mice compared to BCG, with an MST of 289 days and 370 days, respectively (p = 0.030; p = 0.003), BCG/ChAd:TB vaccination further significantly improved the survival of these mice compared to BCG::ESX-1Mmar.

We previously detected IFNγ+IL-17+ CD4 T cells in the lungs of several CC strains, including CC037, that had been vaccinated with BCG and later challenged with Mtb6. These IFNγ+IL-17+ CD4 T cells correlated with enhanced bacterial control, and few, if any, were detected in the lungs of C57BL/6 mice. We therefore examined whether vaccination with either BCG::ESX-1Mmar or BCG/ChAd:TB primed similar responses. Lung mononuclear cells (LMNC) obtained four weeks after Mtb infection of vaccinated CC037, or C57BL/6 mice were stimulated in vitro with anti-CD3 or the Mtb300 peptide megapool23. As expected, the frequency of CD4 T cells that produced IFNγ only, IL-17 only, or both IFNγ and IL-17, was increased in the lungs of BCG-vaccinated CC037 mice that were infected with Mtb (Fig. 5A–C). Few IFNγ-IL-17+ (Fig. 5A) or IFNγ+IL-17+ (Fig. 5B) CD4 T cells were detected in lungs of BCG-vaccinated Mtb-infected C57BL/6J mice. Surprisingly, IFNγ+IL-17+ (Fig. 5B) and IL-17 single-positive CD4 T cells in lungs of CC037 mice were reduced in BCG/ChAd:TB-vaccinated mice compared to mice vaccinated only with BCG. Previous studies using BCG::ESX-1Mmar in C57BL/6 mice found the induction of IFNγ+TNF+ producing CD8 T cells correlated with enhanced control of Mtb compared to BCG18. Similarly, the induction of CD8 TRM by BCG/ChAd:TB vaccination was associated with enhanced protection in BALB/c, C3HeB/FeJ, and humanized mouse models4. Based on these observations, we sought to determine whether vaccination with either BCG::ESX-1Mmar or BCG/ChAd:TB would induce these immune features in CC mice. In CC037 mice, fewer IFNγ- and TNF-producing CD4 T cells were detected after stimulation with anti-CD3 antibodies in Mtb-infected mice that had been vaccinated with BCG/ChAd:TB, compared to unvaccinated mice or mice vaccinated with BCG::ESX-1Mmar (Fig. 5D). Compared to unvaccinated C57BL/6 mice, we observed fewer IFNγ- and TNF-producing CD4 T cells after stimulation with anti-CD3 in all conditions. Similar frequencies of Mtb-specific IFNγ+TNF+ CD4 T cells from the lungs of infected CC037 mice were detected after stimulation with the P300 megapeptide pool, irrespective of vaccination (Fig. 5E). In C57BL/6 mice, all vaccination conditions correlated with a reduction in the frequency of IFNγ- and TNF-producing CD4 T cells (Fig. 5D, E). Stimulation with anti-CD3 (Fig. 5F) antibodies or P300 (Fig. 5G) elicited a similar pattern of IFNγ+TNF+ CD8 T cells, with unvaccinated mice having the highest frequencies after pulmonary Mtb infection (Fig. 5F, G). Of interest, BCG/ChAd:TB represented the only vaccination regimen that significantly increased lung Trm-like CD8 T cells (CD103+CD69+) in CC037 and C57BL/6 mice (Fig. 5H). Thus, some immune features primed by vaccines (e.g., IFNγ+IL-17+ T cells) are only detected in certain mouse genotypes, while others are shared by multiple genotypes (e.g., Trm-like CD8 T cells). These data indicate that the genetic background and the nature of vaccine affect the type of immune responses primed by vaccines in the lungs.

Evaluation of vaccines using a CC strain that is hypersusceptible to Mtb infection but not protected by BCG

We previously demonstrated that CC004 mice had similar bacterial lung burden as C57BL/6 mice four weeks after primary aerosol Mtb infection. However, in contrast to C57BL/6 or CC037 mice, CC004 mice were not protected by vaccination with BCG when evaluated at four weeks after Mtb challenge6. To further evaluate the phenotype of CC004 mice, we compared survival of these mice to C57BL/6 and CC037 mice following low-dose Mtb infection. Surprisingly, we found that CC004 were hypersusceptible to Mtb infection in terms of survival, succumbing rapidly with an MST of 55 days, compared to 220 days for CC037 and >400 days for C57BL/6 (Mantel-Cox p < 0.0001 for CC004 compared to C57BL/6J mice) (Fig. 3).

We next assessed the utility of CC004 mice in the evaluation of TB vaccines. CC004 and C57BL/6 mice were vaccinated with BCG or BCG::ESX-1Mmar and challenged 12 weeks later with aerosolized Mtb strain Rv.YFP. Mtb burdens were measured in lungs four weeks after challenge. As previously observed6, BCG vaccination correlated with significant reductions in lung and spleen bacterial burdens in C57BL/6 mice but not in CC004 mice (Fig. 6A). In contrast, vaccination of CC004 mice with BCG::ESX-1Mmar correlated with significant reductions in lung and spleen Mtb burdens, although the absolute reduction was modest (Fig. 6B). BCG and BCG::ESX-1Mmar vaccination correlated with similar reductions of bacterial burdens in lungs and spleen of C57BL/6J mice, in contrast to previous findings18. To corroborate the reductions in Mtb lung burdens in CC004 mice, we also measured the number of Mtb-infected MDM in lungs by flow cytometry. BCG::ESX-1Mmar but not BCG vaccination correlated with a significant reduction in the number of infected MDM in CC004 mice (Fig. 6C). Both BCG and BCG::ESX-1Mmar vaccination correlated with a reduction of infected MDM in C57BL/6 mice (Fig. 6D). We next vaccinated mice with BCG or BCG/ChAd:TB. Like BCG::ESX-1Mmar, BCG/ChAd:TB vaccination of CC004 mice correlated with a significant, albeit modest, reduction in lung bacterial burdens at four weeks after Mtb challenge (Fig. 6E), with no differences observed in the spleens (Fig. 6F). BCG/ChAd:TB vaccination reduced the bacterial burdens in C57BL/6 mice but not significantly better than BCG alone. As with BCG::ESX-1Mmar, vaccination of CC004 mice with BCG/ChAd:TB correlated with significant reductions in the frequency of Mtb-infected MDM compared to BCG alone in CC004 (Fig. 6G) or in C57BL/6 mice (Fig. 6H). Thus, in CC004 mice, two alternative vaccine strategies provided improved outcomes compared to BCG.

As CC004 mice, like CC037 and C57BL/6 mice, express the H-2b haplotype, vaccine antigenicity can be directly compared. We were interested in whether vaccination of CC004 mice with either novel vaccine would induce previously reported immune signatures4,18. As performed for CC037 mice, we determined whether the changes in lung CFU were accompanied by alterations in T cell immunity following challenge in CC004 mice by re-stimulating LMNC four weeks after Mtb challenge as described above23. The increased protection, as measured by a reduction in lung bacterial burdens in CC004 mice, conferred by BCG::ESX-1Mmar, correlated with an increase in the frequencies of IFNγ+TNF+ CD4 T cells in both CD4 and CD8 T cells (Fig. 6I–L). Conversely, the reductions in lung bacterial burdens in CC004 mice after BCG/ChAd:TB vaccination and Mtb infection were associated with a significant increase in the number of lung Trm-like CD8 T cells in CC004 mice following BCG::ESX-1Mmar (Fig. 6M) or BCG/ChAd:TB vaccination (Fig. 6N).

Finally, we compared the survival of Mtb-challenged CC004 mice after administration of these vaccines. Here, naïve CC004 mice succumbed to aerosol Mtb challenge with an MST of 84 days. Although vaccination with BCG did not enhance tissue bacterial control (Figs. 6A, E), vaccination had a small but significant impact on survival (MST, 93 days; p = 0.0087; Fig.6O). Compared to unvaccinated mice, BCG::ESX-1Mmar vaccination also had a small impact on survival (MST, 96 days). Similarly, the BCG/ChAd:TB vaccine strategy prolonged the survival of CC004 mice (MST, 126 days) compared to unvaccinated mice (p = 0.0046) or mice vaccinated with BCG::ESX-1Mmar (p = 0.0049).

Discussion

Mtb infection results in a spectrum of outcomes in people, ranging from subclinical bacterial infection to fulminant pulmonary tuberculosis and disseminated disease24. Some of these outcomes are recapitulated in Mtb-infected non-human primates (NHP); yet most NHP species appear to be more susceptible to Mtb than humans25. Moreover, primary screening of all novel TB vaccine candidates in NHP is not realistic given ethical, financial, and logistical constraints. Consequently, developing small animal models to test TB vaccines that are more informative than traditional inbred mouse strains remains of interest. To that end, in the current study, we used genetically diverse CC and DO mice to extend our previous findings with BCG to two novel TB vaccine candidates6,7,14. These candidates, BCG::ESX-1Mmar and BCG/ChAd:TB, were previously found to be superior to BCG when evaluated in standard inbred mouse strains4,18. The outcomes, summarized in Table 1, indicate that both vaccines protected CC and DO mice at both short-term and long-term time points. Compared to BCG, BCG::ESX-1Mmar vaccination resulted in improved survival of both C57BL/6 J and CC037 mice, as well as improved splenic bacterial burdens in CC004 mice. BCG/ChAd:TB vaccination resulted in improved lung bacterial burdens as well as survival in CC004 mice, and improved lung inflammation at a late time point in DO mice. Both vaccines, therefore, were effective in genetically diverse populations. Vaccination with BCG::ESX-1Mmar or BCG/ChAd:TB correlated with lower bacterial burdens at early time points in DO mice; however, neither vaccine was consistently superior to BCG in C57BL/6 or DO mice in terms of bacterial burdens, lung inflammation, or weight changes (Fig.1). This differs from published data, in which BCG::ESX-1Mmar and BCG/ChAd:TB were superior to BCG at eliciting improved Mtb bacterial growth control by 5 weeks post infection (wpi) in C57BL/6 mice, or at 4–15 wpi in inbred BALB/c and C3HeB/FeJ mice, respectively4,18. Interestingly, none of the vaccines reduced Mtb lung burdens in DO mice at 14 weeks after challenge, although vaccination with both BCG::ESX-1Mmar and BCG/ChAd:TB, but not BCG, reduced lung inflammation and weight loss (Fig. 1). While BCG/ChAd:TB vaccination of DO mice reduced splenic bacterial burdens and lung inflammation (Fig. 1B, E, F), survival was not extended (Fig. 2). In contrast, BCG and BCG::ESX-1Mmar vaccination did improve survival compared to unvaccinated DO mice (Fig. 2B). The evaluation of survival following Mtb challenge is an important difference between our study and previously published work.

In extending our studies to genetically diverse CC mouse strains, we chose CC037 mice because BCG vaccination correlated with the largest reduction in lung CFU at 4 wpi, compared to other CC strains or C57BL/6 mice6. Surprisingly, despite this early protection, BCG did not extend the survival of CC037 mice. In contrast, although BCG and BCG::ESX-1Mmar vaccination conferred a reduction in lung bacterial burdens measured at 4 wpi, BCG::ESX-1Mmar-vaccinated CC037 mice survived significantly longer than CC037 mice vaccinated with BCG (Fig. 4C). The BCG/ChAd:TB prime/boost strategy reduced Mtb CFU in the lungs of CC037 mice significantly better than BCG alone. Although the protection was modest (Δlog10 = –0.75, p = 0.01), BCG/ChAd:TB vaccination significantly extended the survival of CC037 mice compared to BCG alone (MST 370 vs. 217 days, p = 0.0018). The greater survival conferred by the BCG/ChAd:TB vaccination strategy is associated with inducing CD8 TRM responses (Fig. 5C), which are implicated in providing long-term protection against disseminated Mtb26,27.

In CC004 mice, which we previously showed were not protected by BCG at 4 wpi6, we found that both novel vaccines engendered modest but significant lung bacterial burden reductions at 4 weeks after Mtb challenge compared to unvaccinated mice (Fig. 6). The observed protection was associated with previously reported immune signatures4, and the current immunological evaluations did not identify any signatures that were unique to these novel vaccines in different host backgrounds. The Mtb organ burdens in unvaccinated CC004 mice at 4 wpi, which are similar to unvaccinated C57BL/6 mice, did not predict the early mortality of these hypersusceptible mice, whose MST was 55 days compared to >400 days for C57BL/6 mice (Fig. 3). While both novel vaccines extended the survival of CC004 mice, none were better than BCG. Nevertheless, our results demonstrate that both BCG::ESXMmar and BCG/ChAd:TB vaccination strategies can reduce Mtb burdens in the context of a genetic background where BCG does not.

Overall, our evaluation of these novel vaccine strategies using genetically diverse mice provided a complex picture of protection outcomes that may better represent heterogenous populations (Table 1). CC and DO mice provide a means to address the contribution of diverse host genetics in the evaluation of TB vaccine candidates, which up to now has rarely been considered in preclinical vaccine evaluation5,14. Altogether, the discordance between Mtb bacterial reduction and survival following vaccination highlights the importance of examining multiple parameters when evaluating vaccine protective efficacy. Further, the results underscore the complexity of how tissue Mtb burden and immunopathology interact to affect the overall outcome of infection. Nonetheless, these studies have limitations and caveats. Reproducibility in vaccination and challenge studies in mice across different laboratories can be complicated by vaccine (BCG) and Mtb challenge strain, vaccine and challenge doses, aerosol equipment, animal husbandry conditions, and more. Here, our studies were performed across two different laboratories, with different bacterial strains used for BCG vaccination and Mtb challenge, raising concerns about potentially confounding lab-to-lab variability. A direct comparison of protection by the two BCG strains revealed no difference in CFU reduction at 4 wpi (Kurtz, unpublished data), and the Mtb Erdman and H37Rv:YFP strains had equivalent growth in lungs at 4 wpi in a direct comparison (Fig. S1). Even with other potentially confounding variables, however, the data using C57BL/6 mice between the two contributing labs were highly concordant, suggesting these differences had minimal impact on conclusions to be drawn from these studies.

Another important element of our study is the evaluation of immunity after Mtb challenge, as previous studies focused only on the immunogenicity of the vaccine4,18. A further advantage of CC mice is the ability to perform in-depth mechanistic studies that may reveal novel correlates related to genetic control that are not detectable in standard inbred strains. Although the development of immunity to Mtb in vaccinated mice could obfuscate the contribution of immune responses to the vaccine itself, it is important to consider how infection alters recall responses when evaluating vaccine efficacy. For example, Th1/17 responses identified in CC037 mice, and other CC strains, were only observed in BCG-vaccinated mice after Mtb infection (Fig. 5A, and6). Similarly, although BCG vaccination induces antigen-specific IFN-γ responses in C57BL/6 mice, their frequency was lower in vaccinated mice than in unvaccinated mice, presumably because of reduced Mtb burdens (Fig. 5B, and6). We also previously identified immune signatures associated with enhanced protection by BCG::ESX-1Mmar and BCG/ChAd:TB, which correlated with enhanced Mtb control in common inbred mice4,18. These features were also detected and persisted after Mtb challenge of vaccinated C57BL/6 and CC mice, which is a condition not previously examined. However, both BCG::ESX-1Mmar and BCG/ChAd:TB vaccination prolonged survival compared to BCG-vaccinated CC037 mice, despite reduced Th1/17 responses. Further understanding of these results awaits future mechanistic studies.

For both logistic and ethical reasons, survival studies after vaccination and Mtb challenge are infrequently conducted; common inbred mouse strains such as BALB/c or C57BL/6 mice are relatively resistant to Mtb infection, and even unvaccinated mice often survive >300 days after challenge. Thus, in addition to the long nature of the experiment, interpreting results when a vaccine enhances resistance in mouse strains that are already inherently resistant to Mtb is challenging. As such, the use of susceptible CC mouse models can circumvent some of these challenges. Survival studies integrate the combined effects of infection, immunity, and pathology, and, therefore, such studies are a critical part of vaccine assessment.

The data presented here support the application of multiple novel mouse models, particularly in conjunction with each other, to provide complementary and additional information for preclinical TB vaccine testing. Further, the data highlight the importance of assessing multiple endpoints and parameters when evaluating vaccine efficacy. Ideally, chosen endpoints would align with human clinical endpoints (e.g., chest X-rays), but doing so with small animals is challenging. We continue to seek approaches to combine available animal data, such as early and late bacterial burden, tissue damage, inflammatory markers, and/or survival, to better capture the impact of vaccination on overall TB disease. We will therefore continue to explore the use of these novel mouse populations for vaccine testing and mechanistic studies to refine how to define protection in vaccination-challenge studies to better assess vaccine efficacy.

Methods

Mice

Female Diversity Outbred (DO) mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories, obtained from breeding generation 46. CC004 (CC004/TauUnc) and CC037 (CC037/TauUnc) mice were initially obtained from the UNC Systems Genetics Core Facility and bred locally. C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Jax Laboratories (000664). All animal studies were performed under protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of FDA or UMass Chan Medical School Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and conformed to all relevant U.S. regulatory standards, including the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health. All mice were housed in microisolator cages and were given autoclaved food and water ad libitum. Within each experiment, all animals were age matched. At designated time points, mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation followed by dissection or cervical dislocation in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and consistent with AVMA guidelines.

Bacteria and growth conditions

Mycobacterium tuberculosis Erdman (Mtb Erdman) was derived from the mycobacterial culture collection of the Trudeau Institute and passaged through mice28. Mycobacterium bovis BCG Pasteur (BCG) was obtained from the Trudeau Institute, and M. bovis BCG Denmark was obtained from Serum Statens Institut (Denmark). BCG::ESXMmar was taken from stocks held at the Institut Pasteur. To generate laboratory stocks, single vials of Mtb Erdman, BCG Pasteur, and BCG Denmark were grown in 7H9 media supplemented with OADC, glycerol, and 0.05% Tween 80 (Difco Laboratories, Detroit, Michigan) to mid-logarithmic phase as previously described, then frozen in 0.5 ml aliquots at −70 °C until use29. BCG::ESXMmar was expanded as previously described18. A sample from each bacterial stock was used for quality control experiments to determine the number of colony forming units (CFU), to confirm typical colony morphologies, and to confirm vaccination efficacy or challenge virulence in mice.

Vaccinations and bacterial infections

Groups of 20–25 female DO mice and five C57BL/6, CC037, or CC004 mice were vaccinated intradermally with 105 BCG or BCG-ESXMmar in 100 μl of PBS. In some mice, groups of BCG-vaccinated animals were boosted intranasally at 4 weeks after initial BCG vaccination with 1 × 107 PFU of a replication-deficient chimp serotype 68 adenoviral vector expressing Ag85A, TB10.4, and RpfB (ChAd:TB)4 in a 20 μl volume. Mice were anesthetized in an induction chamber with 3–5% isoflurane in oxygen for intranasal vaccinations. Vaccinated and naïve PBS-treated control mice were challenged by aerosol with H37Rv expressing yellow fluorescent protein (sfYFP) or Mtb Erdman as indicated to assess protection. For each aerosol experiment, a frozen aliquot of Mtb was thawed, diluted in PBS to the desired concentration, and added to the nebulizer. Mice were challenged over a 30-minute exposure in a Middlebrook chamber (GlasCol, Terre Haute, IN). To determine actual delivered dose, five C57BL/6J mice were euthanized 4–24 h after challenge, and the entire lung for each animal was homogenized and plated to determine uptake CFU. The target dose was approximately 50–100 CFU delivered to lungs.

Assessment of bacterial organ burdens and tissue pathology

Bacterial burdens in organs were determined at 4–7 and 14 weeks after infection for C57BL/6 and DO mice7 and 4 weeks after infection for CC004 and CC037 mice. Mice were euthanized, and organs were removed aseptically and transferred to sterile homogenizer bags containing 5 ml of sterile PBS per organ with 10 µg/ml ampicillin per organ. Organs were disrupted using a Stomacher® (Seward, England). To quantitate CFU, diluted homogenates were plated onto 7H11 plates containing 10% OADC enrichment (Becton Dickinson, Sparks, MD) medium, 10 µg/ml ampicillin, 50 µg/ml cycloheximide, and 2 µg/ml 2-thiophenecarboxylic acid hydrazide (TCH; Sigma). The addition of TCH to the agar plates was used to selectively inhibit BCG but not Mtb growth30. In some experiments, portions of each lung were removed and preserved in 10% formalin. Formalin fixed samples were then sent to American Histolabs, Inc. (Gaithersburg, MD), where the tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The Image-Pro Plus software (Media Cybernetics, Rockville, MD) was utilized to assess the level of inflammation present in densitometry scans of each H&E-stained image. Lung inflammation was quantitated by assigning areas with dark pink and purple color staining as inflamed31. The percentage of dark pink and purple colored areas (compared to light pink and open areas) from a lung section of each mouse was determined by the software and reported as percent lung inflammation per sample.

Lung cell preparation

Lung single cell suspensions were prepared first by homogenizing in a GentleMACS tissue dissociator (Miltenyi), followed by a 30-minute incubation in collagenase (300 units/mL (Sigma) in 6 mL of total RPMI (Gibco). Lungs were then homogenized for a second time in the GentleMACS before being sequentially filtered through 70 µM and 40 µM cell strainers (Fisher Scientific) and finally suspended in complete RPMI (10% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine, 50,000 units penicillin/streptomycin, 1 mM sodium-pyruvate, 1x non-essential amino acids, 1x essential amino acids, 25 mM of HEPES, 7.5 mM of sodium hydroxide and 0.55 µM 2-mercaptoethanol; all from Gibco). Lung and spleen cells were treated with ACK lysis buffer (Gibco) for 1–2 min to lyse red blood cells before filtering through 40 µM strainer.

Flow cytometry analysis

Cells were first stained with Live/Dead Aqua (Biolegend) for 10 min at room temperature (RT), after which cells were stained with 5 μg/ml of anti-mouse CD16/32 monoclonal antibody (BioXcell) in autoMACS running buffer (Miltenyi) for 10 min at 4 °C. The cells were then stained with a surface antibody cocktail for 20 min at 4 °C. For intracellular staining, cells were first stimulated for 5 h with either anti-CD3 antibodies (1 μg/mL, Biolegend) or MTB300 peptide megapool23, which contains 300 peptides representing 90 antigens and contains epitopes recognized by both murine CD4 and CD8 T cells. GolgiPlug (BD Biosciences) was also added at this time (0.5 μg/well). Cells were then stained with an intracellular cytokine antibody cocktail for 20 min at 4 °C. To inactivate bacteria, samples were fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde/PBS for 1 h at room temperature and then washed with MACS buffer. Samples were run on either a 4 or 5 laser Cytek Aurora. Erdman (non-fluorescent) infected mice used for unstained control, single stains, and YFP-FMO. After unmixing FMOs were used to define positive and negative populations for each parameter. Flow data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.10.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10) software. P values were calculated using one-way ANOVA, or two-way ANOVA with post-tests as indicated in the figure legends. P values > 0.05 are not shown for clarity. Box plots indicate median (middle line), 25th and 75th percentile (box), and minimum and maximum (whiskers). Two way comparisons of groups using the Mantel-Cox Log-rank test were used to compare the significance of the survival differences between conditions.
