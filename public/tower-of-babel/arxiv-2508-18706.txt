Dimension of Inhomogeneous Sub-Self-Similar Sets
Shivam Dubey, Saurabh Verma

Abstract
. In this paper, we introduce the concept of Inhomogeneous sub-self-similar (ISSS) sets, building upon the foundations laid by Falconer (Trans. Amer. Math. Soc. 347 (1995) 3121–3129) in the study of sub-self-similar sets and drawing inspiration from Barnsley’s work on inhomogeneous self-similar sets (Proc. Roy. Soc. London Ser. A 399 (1985), no. 1817, 24). We explore a range of examples of ISSS sets and elucidate a method to construct ISSS sets. We also investigate the upper and lower box dimensions of ISSS sets and discuss the continuity of the Hausdorff dimension.

Primary 28A80; Secondary 28A78, 26A18

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall the notion of sub-self-similar sets and introduce the concept of inhomogeneous sub-self-similar sets.

Let
\displaystyle(X,d)
be a complete metric space, and let
\displaystyle f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{N}
be similarity mappings on
\displaystyle X
. A unique non-empty compact set
\displaystyle A
is called the
self-similar set
associated with the iterated function system (IFS)
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}:=\{X;f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{N}\}
if
\displaystyle A
satisfies the following equation, known as the
self-similar equation
:

A=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(A).

(1)

Falconer
[
14
]
generalized the concept of self-similar sets by replacing equality in the self-similar equation with set inclusion. A non-empty compact set
\displaystyle F\subseteq X
is called a
sub-self-similar set (SSS set)
if it satisfies:

F\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(F).

(2)

Given a compact set
\displaystyle C\subseteq X
, referred to as the
condensation set
, a unique non-empty compact set
\displaystyle A_{C}
is called the
inhomogeneous self-similar set
associated with the IFS
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}:=\{X;f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{N},C\}
if it satisfies the following equation, known as the
inhomogeneous self-similar equation
:

A_{C}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(A_{C})\cup C.

(3)

Analogously, we define
inhomogeneous sub-self-similar sets (ISSS sets)
, which extend the concept of inhomogeneous self-similar sets by replacing equality in (
3
) with set inclusion. A non-empty compact set
\displaystyle F_{C}\subseteq X
is called an ISSS set if it satisfies:

F_{C}\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(F_{C})\cup C.

(4)

Recall that if
\displaystyle N_{\delta}(F)
represents the minimum number of sets of diameter
\displaystyle\delta
covering
\displaystyle F
, then the upper and lower box-dimension of a non-empty compact set
\displaystyle F\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by

\overline{\dim}_{B}(F)=\limsup_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\frac{\log N_{\delta}(F)}{-\log\delta},~\text{and}~\underline{\dim}_{B}(F)=\liminf_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\frac{\log N_{\delta}(F)}{-\log\delta},~\text{respectively}.

Furthermore, if the above two limits exist and are equal, then the box dimension of
\displaystyle F
is given by

{\dim}_{B}(F)=\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\frac{\log N_{\delta}(F)}{-\log\delta}.

If
\displaystyle|U_{i}|\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
represents the diameter of set
\displaystyle U_{i}.
Then, for
\displaystyle 0\leq s\leq n
the
\displaystyle s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of a set
\displaystyle F\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by

H^{s}(F)=\lim_{\delta\to\infty}H^{s}_{\delta}(F),

where
\displaystyle H^{s}_{\delta}(F)=\inf\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|U_{i}|^{s}:F\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}U_{i}~\text{and}~|U_{i}|\leq\delta\big\}.

The Hausdorff dimension of
\displaystyle F\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the threshold value at which the Hausdorff measure jumps from infinity to zero, i.e., the Hausdorff dimension of
\displaystyle F
is the number
\displaystyle 0\leq d\leq n
such that
\displaystyle H^{s}(F)=\infty,
if
\displaystyle 0\leq s<d
and
\displaystyle H^{d}(F)=0,
if
\displaystyle d<s\leq n.

2.1.
Code Space

Let
\displaystyle I^{\infty}
denote the set of all infinite sequences in
\displaystyle\{1,\ldots,N\}
, that is,

I^{\infty}:=\left\{\{\omega_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\,\middle|\,\omega_{k}\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\right\}.

An element of
\displaystyle I^{\infty}
is written as
\displaystyle\omega:=\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\dots\omega_{k}\omega_{k+1}\dots
, and the
\displaystyle k
-th term of the sequence is denoted by
\displaystyle\omega_{k}
.

For any subset
\displaystyle S\subseteq I^{\infty}
and a positive integer
\displaystyle n
, define

S^{n}:=\left\{\omega_{|_{n}}=\omega_{1}\dots\omega_{n}\,\middle|\,\omega=\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\dots\omega_{k}\dots\in S\right\},\quad\text{and}\quad S^{*}:=\bigcup_{n}S^{n}.

Suppose
\displaystyle(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{N})
is a collection of real numbers such that
\displaystyle 0<s_{i}<1
for all
\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq N
. A metric
\displaystyle d_{\infty}
on
\displaystyle I^{\infty}
is defined by

d_{\infty}(\omega,\tau):=s_{1}\cdots s_{k},

if the sequences
\displaystyle\omega=\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\dots\omega_{k}\omega_{k+1}\dots
and
\displaystyle\tau=\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\dots\tau_{k}\tau_{k+1}\dots
agree up to the
\displaystyle k
-th term and differ at the
\displaystyle(k+1)
-th term. If the sequences are identical, then
\displaystyle d_{\infty}(\omega,\tau)=0
; and if they differ at the first term, then
\displaystyle d_{\infty}(\omega,\tau)=1
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}
be an iterated function system (IFS) consisting of similarity mappings
\displaystyle\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{N}\}
, and let
\displaystyle A
be the unique self-similar set associated with
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}
. There is a well-known map
\displaystyle\Theta:I^{\infty}\to A
defined by

\Theta(\omega):=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{\omega_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{\omega_{n}}(x)=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}f_{\omega_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{\omega_{n}}(A),\quad\text{for all}~\omega=\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\dots\in I^{\infty},

which associates each infinite sequence
\displaystyle\omega
with a unique point in the attractor
\displaystyle A
.

A compact subset
\displaystyle K\subseteq I^{\infty}
is said to be
closed under left shift
if, for every
\displaystyle\omega=\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\dots\in K
, the sequence
\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}:=\omega_{2}\omega_{3}\dots
also belongs to
\displaystyle K
. Falconer characterizes sub-self-similar sets and establishes a natural correspondence between such sets and subsets of the code space. For more details, see
[
14
]
.

Proposition 2.1

(see
[
14
]
, Proposition 2.1)
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=\{(X,d),f_{1},\ldots,f_{N}\}
be an IFS. Then a non-empty compact set
\displaystyle F\subseteq X
is a sub-self-similar set for
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}
if and only if
\displaystyle F=\Theta(S)
, for some compact set
\displaystyle S\subseteq I^{\infty}
that is closed under left shift. Here,
\displaystyle\Theta(\omega)=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{\omega_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{\omega_{n}}(x)
for any
\displaystyle\omega=\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\dots\in S
and any
\displaystyle x\in X
.

Remark 2.2
.

The convergence
\displaystyle\Theta(\omega)=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{\omega_{1}\ldots\omega_{n}}(x)
is independent of the choice of the point
\displaystyle x\in X
.

For any
\displaystyle\omega:=\omega_{1}\dots\omega_{n}\in I^{n}
, we write

f_{\omega}:=f_{\omega_{1}}\circ f_{\omega_{2}}\dots\circ f_{\omega_{n}},\qquad s_{\omega}:=\prod_{i=1}^{n}s_{\omega_{i}}.

3.
Examples and Construction of Inhomogeneous Sub-Self-Similar Sets

In this section, we present various examples of inhomogeneous sub-self-similar (ISSS) sets and provide one method for their construction.

Example 5.1 (Trivial Cases)

Inhomogeneous self-similar sets and sub-self-similar (SSS) sets are trivial examples of ISSS sets that satisfy Equation (
4
).

Example 5.2 (Union of ISSS Sets)

Let
\displaystyle F_{C}
be an ISSS set associated with the IFS
\displaystyle\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{N},C\}
, and let
\displaystyle F_{C^{\prime}}
be another ISSS set associated with the IFS
\displaystyle\{f^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,f^{\prime}_{N^{\prime}},C^{\prime}\}
. Then the union
\displaystyle F_{C}\cup F_{C^{\prime}}
is also an ISSS set associated with the IFS
\displaystyle\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{N},f^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,f^{\prime}_{N^{\prime}},C\cup C^{\prime}\}
.

\displaystyle\displaystyle F_{C}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(F_{C})\cup C,

\displaystyle\displaystyle F_{C^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup_{i^{\prime}=1}^{N^{\prime}}f^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}(F_{C^{\prime}})\cup C^{\prime}.

Thus,

F_{C}\cup F_{C^{\prime}}\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(F_{C}\cup F_{C^{\prime}})\cup\bigcup_{i^{\prime}=1}^{N^{\prime}}f^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}(F_{C}\cup F_{C^{\prime}})\cup(C\cup C^{\prime}).

Example 5.3 (Augmenting ISSS Sets)

Let
\displaystyle F_{C}
be an ISSS set associated with the IFS
\displaystyle\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{N},C\}
. If
\displaystyle K\subseteq F_{C}
is a compact subset and
\displaystyle f
is a contracting similarity, then the set
\displaystyle F_{C}\cup f(K)
is an ISSS set for the IFS
\displaystyle\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{N},f,C\}
. Since
\displaystyle f(K)\subseteq f(F_{C})
, we have:

F_{C}\cup f(K)\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(F_{C}\cup f(K))\cup f(F_{C}\cup f(K))\cup C.

Example 5.4 (Boundary of an ISSS Set)

Let
\displaystyle F_{C}
be an ISSS set satisfying Equation (
4
). Then the topological boundary
\displaystyle\partial F_{C}
of
\displaystyle F_{C}
is also an ISSS set. Any neighborhood of
\displaystyle x\in\partial F_{C}
contains points outside
\displaystyle F_{C}
, implying that
\displaystyle x\in\partial f_{i}(F_{C})\subseteq f_{i}(\partial F_{C})
or
\displaystyle x\in\partial C\subseteq C
.

Remark 3.1
.

Unlike inhomogeneous self-similar sets, ISSS sets are not unique. For example, consider the IFS
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=\{f_{1}(x)=\frac{x}{2},f_{2}(x)=\frac{x}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\}
with condensation set
\displaystyle C
. Both the set
\displaystyle\{0,1\}
and the set of all dyadic numbers in
\displaystyle[0,1]
are ISSS sets corresponding to
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}
and
\displaystyle C
.

Remark 3.2
.

Let
\displaystyle E
be a bounded set that is not sub-self-similar with respect to an IFS
\displaystyle\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Then the set
\displaystyle C=\overline{E\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(E)}
ensures that
\displaystyle E=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(E)\cup C
, making
\displaystyle E
an ISSS set. Moreover, for any
\displaystyle C^{\prime}\supseteq C
,
\displaystyle E
remains an ISSS set.

Remark 3.3
.

In
[
31
]
, McClure and Vallin provide examples of non-sub-self-similar sets, such as
\displaystyle E=\{0,1,\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{3},\ldots\}
, which is not a sub-self-similar set. According to the previous remark, there exists a condensation set
\displaystyle C
such that
\displaystyle E
becomes an ISSS set. Furthermore, for any compact set
\displaystyle E
, the minimal condensation set
\displaystyle C=\overline{E\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(E)}
ensures that
\displaystyle E
is an ISSS set.

Remark 3.4
.

Inhomogeneous self-similar sets are ISSS sets. Also, if we consider the condensation sets
\displaystyle C
as the empty set, then ISSS sets reduce to SSS sets, which is a generalization of self-similar sets. In this way, ISSS sets are a generalization of self-similar sets.

4.
Construction of Inhomogeneous Sub-Self-Similar Sets

We now present a constructive method for generating ISSS sets from a given sub-self-similar set and a condensation set.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle E
be a sub-self-similar set associated with the IFS
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}
such that
\displaystyle\Theta(S)=E
, and let
\displaystyle C\subset X
be a non-empty compact set. Define

O_{S}=\bigcup_{{\omega}\in S^{*}}f_{{\omega}}(C).

Then
\displaystyle E\cup O_{S}
is an ISSS set corresponding to the IFS
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}
with condensation set
\displaystyle C
.

Proof.

To prove that
\displaystyle E\cup O_{S}
is an ISSS set, we first show that it is a compact set. Since
\displaystyle O_{S}
is bounded, it suffices to prove that
\displaystyle E\cup O_{S}
is closed. Let
\displaystyle\{x_{n}\}
be a sequence in
\displaystyle E\cup O_{S}
converging to some
\displaystyle x\in X
. If
\displaystyle x_{n}\in E
for infinitely many
\displaystyle n
, then there exists a subsequence
\displaystyle\{x_{n_{r}}\}
with
\displaystyle x_{n_{r}}\in E
. Since
\displaystyle E
is closed,
\displaystyle\lim x_{n_{r}}=x\in E\subseteq E\cup O_{S}
. If
\displaystyle x_{n}\in E
for only finitely many
\displaystyle n
, i.e.,
\displaystyle\{x_{n}\}
is eventually in
\displaystyle O_{S}
, then there exists
\displaystyle n_{0}
such that
\displaystyle x_{n}\in O_{S}
for all
\displaystyle n\geq n_{0}
. Hence, for each
\displaystyle n\geq n_{0}
, there exists a string
\displaystyle\omega_{|_{n}}\in S^{*}
such that
\displaystyle x_{n}\in f_{\omega_{|_{n}}}(C)
.
Let us denote the length of any string
\displaystyle\omega:=\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\dots\omega_{k}\in S^{*}
by
\displaystyle|\omega|=k.

Case 1:

\displaystyle\liminf|\omega_{|_{n}}|=k<\infty
. Then there exists a subsequence
\displaystyle\{\omega_{{|_{n}}_{j}}\}
of
\displaystyle\{\omega_{|_{n}}\}
such that
\displaystyle\omega_{{|_{n}}_{j}}\in S^{k}
for all
\displaystyle j
, where
\displaystyle S^{k}
is a finite set. Thus, there exists a string
\displaystyle t\in\{\omega_{{|_{n}}_{j}}\mid j\in\mathbb{N}\}
and a further subsequence
\displaystyle\{\omega_{{|_{n}}_{j_{p}}}\}
such that
\displaystyle\omega_{{|_{n}}_{j_{p}}}=t
for all
\displaystyle p
. Then
\displaystyle x_{n_{j_{p}}}\in f_{t}(C)
, and since
\displaystyle f_{t}(C)
is closed,
\displaystyle\lim x_{n_{j_{p}}}=x\in f_{t}(C)\subseteq O_{S}
.

Case 2:

\displaystyle\liminf|\omega_{|_{n}}|=\infty
. Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{diam}(f_{\omega_{|_{n}}}(C)\cup f_{\omega_{|_{n}}}(E))

\displaystyle=\rho_{\omega_{|_{n}}}\operatorname{diam}(C\cup E)

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{\max}^{|\omega_{|_{n}}|}\operatorname{diam}(C\cup E)\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,

since
\displaystyle\rho_{\max}=\max\{\rho_{i}\}<1
.

Now, for each
\displaystyle n
, choose
\displaystyle y_{n}\in f_{\omega_{|_{n}}}(E)
. For positive integers
\displaystyle m
and
\displaystyle n
,

\displaystyle|y_{n}-y_{m}|

\displaystyle\leq|y_{n}-x_{n}|+|x_{n}-x_{m}|+|x_{m}-y_{m}|

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{diam}(f_{\omega_{|_{n}}}(C)\cup f_{\omega_{|_{n}}}(E))+|x_{n}-x_{m}|+\operatorname{diam}(f_{\omega_{|_{m}}}(C)\cup f_{\omega_{|_{m}}}(E)).

Hence,
\displaystyle\{y_{n}\}
is a Cauchy sequence and converges to some
\displaystyle y\in X
. Then,

\displaystyle|x-y|

\displaystyle\leq|x-x_{n}|+|x_{n}-y_{n}|+|y_{n}-y|

\displaystyle\leq|x-x_{n}|+\operatorname{diam}(f_{\omega_{|_{n}}}(C)\cup f_{\omega_{|_{n}}}(E))+|y_{n}-y|\to 0.

Thus,
\displaystyle x=y
.

Since
\displaystyle y_{n}\in f_{\omega_{|_{n}}}(E)
for each
\displaystyle n
, there exists
\displaystyle y^{\prime}\in E
such that
\displaystyle y_{n}=f_{\omega_{1}\ldots\omega_{n}}(y^{\prime})
. Let
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
. Choose
\displaystyle n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
\displaystyle n\geq n_{1}
,

|y_{n}-y|=|f_{\omega_{1}\ldots\omega_{n}}(y^{\prime})-y|<\epsilon/2.

Since
\displaystyle\Theta(S)=E
, we have
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{\omega_{1}\ldots\omega_{n}}(y^{\prime})=z_{n_{\epsilon}}\in E
. Choose
\displaystyle n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
\displaystyle n\geq n_{2}
,

|f_{\omega_{1}\ldots\omega_{n}}(y^{\prime})-z_{n_{\epsilon}}|<\epsilon/2.

Let
\displaystyle n^{\prime}=\max\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
. Then,

\displaystyle|y-z_{n_{\epsilon}}|

\displaystyle\leq|f_{\omega_{1}\ldots\omega_{n}}(y^{\prime})-y|+|f_{\omega_{1}\ldots\omega_{n}}(y^{\prime})-z_{n_{\epsilon}}|

\displaystyle<\epsilon/2+\epsilon/2=\epsilon.

Since
\displaystyle E
is closed,
\displaystyle x=y=\lim z_{n_{\epsilon}}\in E\subseteq E\cup O_{S}
. Thus,
\displaystyle E\cup O_{S}
is closed, and hence compact.

It remains to show that
\displaystyle E\cup O_{S}
satisfies Equation (
4
).

Clearly,

\displaystyle E\cup O_{S}

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(E)\cup O_{S}

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(E\cup O_{S})\cup O_{S}\cup C.

Now we show that
\displaystyle O_{S}\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(O_{S})
. Let
\displaystyle x\in O_{S}
. Then there exists
\displaystyle\omega_{|_{n}}\in S^{*}
and
\displaystyle c\in C
such that

x=f_{\omega_{|_{n}}}(c)=f_{\omega_{1}\ldots\omega_{n}}(c).

Let
\displaystyle x^{\prime}=f_{\omega_{2}\ldots\omega_{n}}(c)\in O_{S}
. Then,

x=f_{\omega_{1}}(x^{\prime})\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(O_{S}).

Thus,

E\cup O_{S}\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(E\cup O_{S})\cup C.

This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\displaystyle E
be a sub-self-similar set with
\displaystyle\Theta(S)=E
, and let

O_{S}=\bigcup_{\omega\in S^{*}}f_{\omega}(C).

Then,

\overline{O}_{S}=E\cup O_{S}.

Proof.

By the construction,
\displaystyle\overline{O}_{S}
is the smallest closed set containing
\displaystyle O_{S}
. Since
\displaystyle E\cup O_{S}
is closed and
\displaystyle O_{S}\subseteq E\cup O_{S}
, it follows that
\displaystyle\overline{O}_{S}\subseteq E\cup O_{S}
.

To prove the reverse inclusion, let
\displaystyle x\in E\cup O_{S}
be any element. If
\displaystyle x\in O_{S}
, then nothing to prove, if
\displaystyle x\in E
, then by
2.1
, there exists a sequence
\displaystyle\omega=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots)\in S
such that
\displaystyle x=\Theta(\omega)
. We will construct a sequence
\displaystyle\{x_{n}\}
in
\displaystyle O_{S}
which converges to
\displaystyle x\in E.
Fix
\displaystyle c\in C
and define
\displaystyle x_{n}=f_{\omega_{1}\cdots\omega_{n}}(c)\in O_{S}
. Then
\displaystyle x_{n}\to x\in E
, so
\displaystyle x\in\overline{O}_{S}
. Thus,
\displaystyle E\cup O_{S}\subseteq\overline{O}_{S}
.
∎

5.
Dimension of inhomogeneous sub-self-similar sets

In this section, we aim to estimate the Hausdorff and box dimensions for ISSS sets (arrived from a given SSS set
\displaystyle E
and a condensation set
\displaystyle C
), which generalize to the inhomogeneous self-similar sets.

5.1.
Hausdorff dimension

Theorem 5.1
.

(see,
[
14
, Theorem 3.5]
)
Let
\displaystyle E
be a SSS set for a family of contracting similarities
\displaystyle f_{1},\ldots,f_{N}
. Let
\displaystyle s
be the unique non-negative number such that

\displaystyle\tau(s)\equiv\lim_{k\to\infty}\bigg(\sum_{i\in S_{k}}\rho_{i}^{s}\bigg)^{1/k}=1,
then

\dim_{H}E\leq\underline{\dim}_{B}E\leq\overline{\dim}_{B}E\leq s.

Furthermore, if it satisfies the open set condition, then

\dim_{H}E=\underline{\dim}_{B}E=\overline{\dim}_{B}E=s.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
\displaystyle E\cup O_{S}
be an ISSS set with SSS set
\displaystyle E
and condensation set
\displaystyle C.
Then
\displaystyle E\cup O_{S}=\max\{s,\dim_{H}C\}
.

Proof.

Since the Hausdorff dimension is countably stable, the Hausdorff dimension of
\displaystyle O_{S}
equals the Hausdorff dimension of
\displaystyle C
. Additionally, the Hausdorff dimension of the union of
\displaystyle E\cup O_{S}=\max\{s,\dim_{H}C\}
.
∎

5.2.
Upper box dimension

In order to estimate the box dimension of these ISSS sets, we will follow Fraser’s
\displaystyle\delta
-covering technique. For more details, one can see
[
15
]
.

Lemma 5.3
.

If
\displaystyle t>s,
then there exists a constant
\displaystyle m_{t}
(depends only on
\displaystyle t
) such that

\sum_{\omega\in S^{*}}\rho_{\omega}^{t}=m_{t}<\infty.

Proof.

The function
\displaystyle\tau(s)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\bigg(\sum_{\omega\in S^{k}}\rho_{\omega}^{s}\bigg)^{1/k}
defined in Theorem (
5.1
) is strictly decreasing continuous function with
\displaystyle 0\leq\tau(s)<\infty
and
\displaystyle\sum_{\omega\in S^{k}}\rho_{\omega}^{s}\geq\tau(s)^{k}
for all
\displaystyle k=1,2,\ldots

Also observe that
\displaystyle\sum_{\omega\in S^{*}}\rho_{\omega}^{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\omega\in S^{k}}\rho_{\omega}^{t}
converges if
\displaystyle\tau(s)<1

\displaystyle\tau(s)>1.

\displaystyle t>s

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega\in S^{*}}\rho_{\omega}^{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\omega\in S^{k}}\rho_{\omega}^{t}=m_{t}<\infty,
completing the assertion.
∎

For
\displaystyle\delta\in(0,1],
defining
\displaystyle\delta
-stopping, for
\displaystyle S
as

\displaystyle S(\delta)=\{\omega\in S^{*}:\rho_{\omega}<\delta\leq\rho_{\omega_{-}}\}.

\displaystyle\omega

.

For all

Proof.

By the above lemma, we have

\displaystyle m_{t}=\sum_{\omega\in S^{*}}\rho_{\omega}^{t}\geq\sum_{\omega\in S(\delta)}\rho_{\omega}^{t}.

Also for

Using this, we get

Which completes the proof.
∎

.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
\displaystyle E

\displaystyle\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{N}\}

\displaystyle E\cup O_{S}

Proof.

Since the upper box dimension is finitely stable and monotonic, we have

\max\{\overline{\dim}_{B}E,\overline{\dim}_{B}C\}\leq\overline{\dim}_{B}E_{C}\leq\max\{\overline{\dim}_{B}E,\overline{\dim}_{B}O_{S}\}.

\displaystyle\overline{\dim}_{B}E\leq s,

\displaystyle\overline{\dim}_{B}O_{S}\leq\max\{s,\overline{\dim}_{B}C\}.

Let
\displaystyle\max\{s,\overline{\dim}_{B}C\}<t,
then by the definition of the box-dimension, there exists some constant
\displaystyle c_{t}
such that

N_{\delta}(C)\leq c_{t}\delta^{-t},

for all
\displaystyle\delta\in(0,1].
Consider

\displaystyle N_{\delta}(O_{S})

\displaystyle=N_{\delta}\bigg(C\cup\bigcup_{\omega\in S^{*}}f_{\omega}(C)\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq N_{\delta}(C)+N_{\delta}\bigg(\bigcup_{\omega\in S^{*},\rho_{\omega}\geq\delta}f_{\omega}(C)\bigg)+N_{\delta}\bigg(\bigcup_{\omega\in S^{*},\rho_{\omega}<\delta}f_{\omega}(C)\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq N_{\delta}(C)+N_{\delta}\bigg(\bigcup_{\omega\in S^{*},\rho_{\omega}\geq\delta}f_{\omega}(C)\bigg)+N_{\delta}\bigg(\bigcup_{\omega\in S(\delta)}f_{\omega}(X)\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq N_{\delta}(C)+\sum_{\omega\in S^{*},\rho_{\omega}\geq\delta}N_{\delta}(f_{\omega}(C))+\sum_{\omega\in S(\delta)}N_{\delta}(f_{\omega}(X))

\displaystyle\leq N_{\delta}(C)+\sum_{\omega\in S^{*},\rho_{\omega}\geq\delta}N_{{\delta}/{\rho_{\omega}}}(C)+\sum_{\omega\in S(\delta)}N_{{\delta}/{\rho_{\omega}}}(X)

\displaystyle\leq(c_{t}+c_{t}m_{t}+m_{t}{\rho}^{-t}_{\min}N_{1}(X))\delta^{-t}.

The third line comes from Lemma (
5.6
). The sixth line comes as
\displaystyle X

5.3.
Lower box dimension

In this subsection, our goal is to estimate the upper bound for the lower box dimension of the ISSS sets. We observe that if
\displaystyle\overline{\dim}_{B}C=\underline{\dim}_{B}C
, then the box dimension of ISSS sets is equal to the maximum of the dimension of the corresponding homogeneous SSS set and the box dimension of the condensation set. Since the lower box dimension follows the monotonicity property, we have

\max\left\{\underline{\dim}_{B}E,\underline{\dim}_{B}C\right\}\leq\underline{\dim}_{B}(E\cup O_{S})\leq\max\left\{s,\overline{\dim}_{B}C\right\}.

Given the stability of the lower box dimension under closure, we have

\underline{\dim}_{B}(E\cup O_{S})=\underline{\dim}_{B}\overline{O}_{S}=\underline{\dim}_{B}O_{S}.

We will use the concept of the covering regularity index of the condensation set
\displaystyle C
from
[
15
]
to estimate an upper bound for the lower box dimension of the set
\displaystyle O_{S}
.

For
\displaystyle t\geq 0
and
\displaystyle\delta\in(0,1]
, the
\displaystyle(t,\delta)
covering regularity exponent of
\displaystyle C
is defined as

P_{t,\delta}(C)=\sup\left\{p\in[0,1]:N_{\delta^{p}}(C)\geq\delta^{-pt}\right\}.

The
\displaystyle t
-covering regularity exponent of
\displaystyle C
is defined as

P_{t}(C)=\liminf_{\delta\to 0}P_{t,\delta}(C).

Lemma 5.8
.

(see
[
15
, Lemma 2.6]
)
For any
\displaystyle C\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have:

(1)

For all
\displaystyle t,\delta>0
,
\displaystyle P_{t,\delta}(C)
,
\displaystyle P_{t}(C)\in[0,1]
.

(2)

\displaystyle P_{t}(C)
is decreasing in
\displaystyle t
, and if
\displaystyle t<\underline{\dim}_{B}C
, then
\displaystyle P_{t}(C)=1
, and if
\displaystyle t>\overline{\dim}_{B}C
, then
\displaystyle P_{t}(C)=0
.

(3)

For all
\displaystyle t>\underline{\dim}_{B}C
, we have
\displaystyle P_{t}(C)\leq\underline{\dim}_{B}C<t
.

Lemma 5.9
.

(see,
[
15
, Lemma 3.6]
)
For
\displaystyle t\geq 0
, if
\displaystyle P_{t}(C)<1
, then for all
\displaystyle\epsilon\in(0,1-P_{t}(C))
, there exists
\displaystyle\delta\in(0,\epsilon)
such that:

P_{t}(C)-\epsilon<P_{t,\delta}(C)<P_{t}(C)+\epsilon,

and for all
\displaystyle\delta_{0}\in[\delta,\delta^{P_{t}(C)}]
, we have:

N_{\delta_{0}}(C)\leq\delta_{0}^{-t}.

By Lemma (
5.4
), for
\displaystyle t>s
, the cardinality of the
\displaystyle\delta
-stopping set
\displaystyle S
is given by:

|S(\delta)|\leq m_{t}\rho_{\min}^{-t}\delta^{-t}.

Using the above two lemmas, we can estimate the lower box dimension.

Theorem 5.10
.

For all
\displaystyle t>\max\{s,\underline{\dim}_{B}C\}
, we have:

\underline{\dim}_{B}(E\cup O_{S})\leq\max\{s,\underline{\dim}_{B}C\}.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle t>\max\{s,\underline{\dim}_{B}C\}
. Then, by Lemma (
5.8
), we have
\displaystyle P_{t}(C)\leq\underline{\dim}_{B}C<t
. Thus, by Lemma (
5.9
), for all
\displaystyle\epsilon\in(0,1-P_{t}(C))
, there exists
\displaystyle\delta\in(0,\epsilon)
such that:

P_{t}(C)-\epsilon<P_{t,\delta}(C)<P_{t}(C)+\epsilon.

Moreover, for all
\displaystyle\delta_{0}\in[\delta,\delta^{P_{t}(C)}]
, we have:

N_{\delta_{0}}\leq\delta_{0}^{-t}.

For a fixed
\displaystyle\epsilon\in(0,1-P_{t}(C))
and choosing
\displaystyle\delta\in(0,\epsilon)
, we have:

\displaystyle N_{\delta}(O_{S})

\displaystyle=N_{\delta}\left(C\cup\bigcup_{\omega\in S^{*}}f_{\omega}(C)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\omega\in S^{*},\delta^{1-P_{t,\delta}(C)-\epsilon}\leq\rho_{\omega}<1}N_{\delta}f_{\omega}(C)+\sum_{\omega\in S^{*},\delta\leq\rho_{\omega}<\delta^{1-P_{t,\delta}(C)-\epsilon}}N_{\delta}f_{\omega}(C)

\displaystyle+N_{\delta}\left(\bigcup_{\omega\in S^{*},\rho_{\omega}<\delta}f_{\omega}(C)\right)+N_{\delta}(C)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\omega\in S^{*},\delta^{1-P_{t,\delta}(C)-\epsilon}\leq\rho_{\omega}<1}N_{\delta/\rho_{\omega}}(C)+\sum_{\omega\in S^{*},\delta\leq\rho_{\omega}<\delta^{1-P_{t,\delta}(C)-\epsilon}}N_{\delta/\rho_{\omega}}(C)

\displaystyle+N_{\delta}\left(\bigcup_{\omega\in S(\delta),\rho_{\omega}<\delta}f_{\omega}(X)\right)+N_{\delta}(C)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\omega\in S^{*},\delta^{1-P_{t,\delta}(C)-\epsilon}\leq\rho_{\omega}<1}(\delta/\rho_{\omega})^{-t}+\sum_{\omega\in S^{*},\delta\leq\rho_{\omega}<\delta^{1-P_{t,\delta}(C)-\epsilon}}N_{\delta^{P_{t,\delta}(C)+\epsilon}}(C)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\omega\in S(\delta)}N_{\delta/\rho_{\omega}}(X)+\delta^{-t}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\omega\in S^{*},\delta^{1-P_{t,\delta}(C)-\epsilon}\leq\rho_{\omega}<1}(\delta/\rho_{\omega})^{-t}+\sum_{\omega\in S^{*},\delta\leq\rho_{\omega}<\delta^{1-P_{t,\delta}(C)-\epsilon}}N_{\delta/\rho_{\omega}}(C)

\displaystyle+N_{1}(X)|S(\delta)|+\delta^{-t}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\omega\in S^{*}}(\delta/\rho_{\omega})^{-t}+|\{\omega\in S^{*}:\delta\leq\rho_{\omega}\}|\delta^{-(P_{t,\delta}(C)+\epsilon)t}

\displaystyle+N_{1}(X)m_{t}\rho_{\min}^{-t}\delta^{-t}+\delta^{-t}

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{-t}\sum_{\omega\in S^{*}}\rho_{\omega}^{t}+|\{\omega\in S^{*}:\delta\leq\rho_{\omega}\}|\delta^{-(P_{t,\delta}(C)+\epsilon)t}

\displaystyle+N_{1}(X)m_{t}\rho_{\min}^{-t}\delta^{-t}+\delta^{-t}

\displaystyle\leq\left(m_{t}+N_{1}(X)m_{t}\rho_{\min}^{-t}+1+m_{t}\frac{\log\delta}{\log\rho_{\max}}\delta^{-(P_{t,\delta}(C)+\epsilon)t}\right)\delta^{-t}

\displaystyle\leq\left(m_{t}+N_{1}(X)m_{t}\rho_{\min}^{-t}+1+m_{t}\frac{\log\delta}{\log\rho_{\max}}\delta^{-(P_{t}(C)+2\epsilon)t}\right)\delta^{-t}.

Letting
\displaystyle\epsilon\to 0
, we get
\displaystyle\underline{\dim}_{B}O_{S}\leq t
. This concludes
\displaystyle\underline{\dim}_{B}(E\cup O_{S})\leq\max\{s,\underline{\dim}_{B}C\}
.

∎

6.
Continuity of the Hausdorff dimension of ISSS

In this section, we observe that for each ISSS set of the form
\displaystyle F:=E\cup O_{S}
, where
\displaystyle E
is an SSS set and
\displaystyle C
is the condensation set, there exists a sequence of inhomogeneous IFSs
\displaystyle\{I_{k}\}
with corresponding ISSS sets
\displaystyle\{F_{k}\}
such that
\displaystyle F=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}F_{k}
. Furthermore, we discuss the continuity of the Hausdorff dimension of ISSS sets.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\displaystyle F=E\cup O_{S}
be the ISSS set corresponding to the IFS
\displaystyle\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
with condensation set
\displaystyle C
, which satisfies the open set condition. Then, there exists a sequence of IFSs
\displaystyle\{I_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
such that

F=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}F_{k},

where
\displaystyle F_{k}=E_{k}\cup O_{S}
are the ISSS sets corresponding to the IFSs
\displaystyle I_{k}
, each with condensation set
\displaystyle C
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle I_{k}=\{f_{\omega}:\omega\in S^{k}\}
. For any
\displaystyle x\in F=E\cup O_{S}
, we consider two cases:

Case 1:
If
\displaystyle x\in E
, then there exists a string
\displaystyle\omega=\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\ldots\in S
such that

x=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{\omega_{1}}\circ f_{\omega_{2}}\circ\ldots\circ f_{\omega_{n}}(z).

For each
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}
, define
\displaystyle\omega_{j}|_{k}^{k}:=\omega_{jk+1}\omega_{jk+2}\ldots\omega_{jk+k}
for
\displaystyle j=0,1,2,\ldots
. Then
\displaystyle\omega_{j}|_{k}^{k}\in S^{k}
, and we have

x=\lim_{j\to\infty}f_{\omega_{0}|_{k}^{k}}\circ f_{\omega_{1}|_{k}^{k}}\circ\ldots\circ f_{\omega_{j}|_{k}^{k}}(z),

which implies that
\displaystyle x\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}F_{k}
.

Case 2:
If
\displaystyle x\in O_{S}
, then there exists some
\displaystyle\omega=\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\ldots\omega_{n}\in S^{*}
and
\displaystyle c\in C
such that

f_{\omega_{1}}\circ\ldots\circ f_{\omega_{n}}(c)=x.

Clearly,
\displaystyle\omega_{1}\ldots\omega_{n}\in S^{n}
, so
\displaystyle x\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}F_{k}\cup O_{S}
.

Conversely, suppose
\displaystyle x\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}F_{k}\cup O_{S}
. If
\displaystyle x\in O_{S}
, then the claim holds trivially. Now suppose
\displaystyle x\in F_{k}
for some
\displaystyle k
. Then there exists a sequence
\displaystyle\omega_{0}|_{k}^{k},\omega_{1}|_{k}^{k},\ldots,\omega_{j}|_{k}^{k}\in S^{k}
such that

x=\lim_{j\to\infty}f_{\omega_{0}|_{k}^{k}}\circ f_{\omega_{1}|_{k}^{k}}\circ\ldots\circ f_{\omega_{j}|_{k}^{k}}(z).

Using the fact that
\displaystyle\Theta(S)=E
and that the concatenation
\displaystyle\omega=\omega_{0}|_{k}^{k}\omega_{1}|_{k}^{k}\ldots\omega_{j}|_{k}^{k}\in S
, we conclude that
\displaystyle x\in F
.

Thus, we obtain
\displaystyle F=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}F_{k}
.
∎

7.
Self-similar structure on the product of IFS

In this section, we define the product of inhomogeneous iterated function systems (IFSs) and examine the associated separation conditions. We conclude by demonstrating that the product of the inhomogeneous invariant measures corresponding to the individual IFSs constitutes an inhomogeneous type invariant measure for the product IFS.
Let
\displaystyle(X,d_{1})
and
\displaystyle(Y,d_{2})
be two complete metric spaces; then it is well known that the product space
\displaystyle(X\times Y,d)
is a complete metric space with respect to the metric
\displaystyle d
given by

d((x,y),(z,w))=\max\{d_{1}(x,z),d_{2}(y,w)\}.

Now, for two IFSs

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}=\{(X,d_{1});f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{N}\}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}=\{(Y,d_{2});g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{M}\}
we define product IFS
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}=\{X\times Y;\Phi_{(ij)},(ij)\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}\times\{1,2,\ldots,M\}\}
, where
\displaystyle\Phi_{(ij)}(x,y)=(f_{i}(x),g_{j}(y)).

It is easy to observe that
\displaystyle\Phi_{ij}
is contraction for all
\displaystyle ij\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}\times\{1,2,\ldots,M\}.

For any
\displaystyle(x,y),(z,w)\in X\times Y,
we have

\displaystyle d(\Phi_{ij}(x,y),\Phi_{ij}(z,w))

\displaystyle=d((f_{i}(x),g_{j}(y),(f_{i}(z),g_{j}(w)))

\displaystyle=\max(d_{1}(f_{i}(x),f_{i}(z)),d_{2}(g_{j}(y),g_{j}(w)))

\displaystyle\leq\{c^{1}_{i}d_{1}(x,z),c^{2}_{j}d_{2}(z,w)\}

\displaystyle\leq c\max\{d_{1}(x,z),d_{2}(z,w)\}

\displaystyle=c~d((x,y),(z,w)),

where
\displaystyle c=\max\{c^{1}_{i},c^{2}_{j}\}.
Let
\displaystyle A_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}
and
\displaystyle A_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}
be the attractors corresponding to the IFSs
\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}_{1}}
and
\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}_{2}},
respectively then
It is easy to verify that the attractor corresponding to the product IFS
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
is
\displaystyle A_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\times A_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}.

We claim that
\displaystyle\bigcup_{i,j}f_{i}(A_{\mathcal{I}_{1}})\times g_{j}(A_{\mathcal{I}_{2}})=\bigcup_{i}f_{i}(A)\times\bigcup_{j}g_{j}(A_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}).

\displaystyle\text{Let}

\displaystyle(x,y)\in\bigcup_{i,j}f_{i}(A_{\mathcal{I}_{1}})\times g_{j}(A_{\mathcal{I}_{2}})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow(x,y)\in f_{i}(A_{\mathcal{I}_{1}})\times g_{j}(A_{\mathcal{I}_{2}})~\text{for some}~(ij)\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}\times\{1,2,\ldots,M\}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow(x,y)\in f_{i}(A_{\mathcal{I}_{1}})\times g_{j}(A_{\mathcal{I}_{2}})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow x\in f_{i}(A_{\mathcal{I}_{1}})~\text{and}~y\in g_{j}(A_{\mathcal{I}_{2}})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow x\in\bigcup_{i}f_{i}(A_{\mathcal{I}_{1}})~\text{and}~y\in\bigcup_{j}g_{j}(A_{\mathcal{I}_{2}})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow(x,y)\in\bigcup_{i}f_{i}(A_{\mathcal{I}_{1}})\times\bigcup_{j}g_{j}(A_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}).

Now,

\displaystyle A_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\times A_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{i}f_{i}(A_{\mathcal{I}_{1}})\times\bigcup_{i}g_{j}(A_{\mathcal{I}_{1}})

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{i,j}f_{i}(A_{\mathcal{I}_{1}})\times g_{j}(A_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}).

In the similar fashion, we define the product of two inhomogeneous IFS. Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{C_{1}}=\{(X,d_{1});f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{N},C_{1}\}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{C_{2}}=\{(Y,d_{2});g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{M},C_{2}\}
be two inhomogeneous IFSs with condensation set
\displaystyle C_{1}\subset X
and
\displaystyle C_{2}\subset Y
respectively, then we define inhomogeneous product IFS
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{C}=\{X\times Y;\Phi_{(ij)},(C_{1},C_{2}),(ij)\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}\times\{1,2,\ldots,M\}\}.
If
\displaystyle A_{C_{1}}
and
\displaystyle A_{C_{2}}
are the inhomogeneous attractors corresponding to the IFSs
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{C_{1}}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{C_{2}},
respectively. Then
\displaystyle A_{C_{1}}\times A_{C_{2}}
is a super-self-similar set corresponding to the product IFS
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{C},
i.e.,
\displaystyle A_{C_{1}}\times A_{C_{2}}\supseteq\bigcup_{i,j}\Phi_{ij}(A_{C_{1}}\times A_{C_{2}})\cup C_{1}\times C_{2}.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
\displaystyle A_{C_{1}}
and
\displaystyle A_{C_{2}}
be the inhomogeneous attractors corresponding to the inhomogeneous IFSs
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{C_{1}}=\{(X,d_{1});f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{N},C_{1}\}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{C_{2}}=\{(Y,d_{2});g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{M},C_{2}\},
respectively. Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{C}=\{X\times Y;\Phi_{(ij)},(C_{1},C_{2}),(ij)\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}\times\{1,2,\ldots,M\}\}
be the product IFS, then
\displaystyle A_{C_{1}}\times A_{C_{2}}
be a super-self-similar set.

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle A_{C_{1}}
and
\displaystyle A_{C_{2}}
are the inhomogeneous attractors. We have

A_{C_{1}}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(A_{c_{1}})\cup C_{1},~\text{and}~A_{C_{2}}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{M}g_{j}(A_{C_{2}})\cup C_{2}.

Also,

\displaystyle A_{C_{1}}\times A_{C_{2}}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\bigcup_{i}f_{i}(A_{C_{1}})\cup C_{1}\bigg)\times\bigg(\bigcup_{j}g_{j}(A_{C_{2}})\cup C_{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\bigcup_{i}f_{i}(A_{C_{1}})\times\bigcup_{j}g_{j}(A_{C_{2}})\bigg)\cup\bigg(\bigcup_{i}f_{i}(A_{C_{2}})\times C_{2}\bigg)\cup

\displaystyle\bigg(C_{1}\times\bigcup_{j}g_{j}(A_{C_{2}})\bigg)\cup(C_{1}\times C_{2})

\displaystyle\supseteq\bigcup_{i,j}f_{i}(A_{C_{1}})\times g_{j}(A_{C_{2}})\cup(C_{1}\times C_{2}).

∎

In the next theorem, we see that the inhomogeneous separation conditions on
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{C_{1}}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{C_{2}}
can be transferred to the inhomogeneous product IFS
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{C}
.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}=\{(X,d_{1});f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{N},C_{1}\}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}=\{(Y,d_{2});g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{M},C_{2}\}
be two IFSs. Then the following hold:

(1)

The IFS
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{C}
satisfies the ISSC if the IFSs
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{C_{1}}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{C_{2}}
satisfy the ISSC.

(2)

The IFS
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{C}
satisfies the IOSC if both IFSs
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{C_{1}}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{C_{2}}
satisfy the IOSC.

Proof.

(1)

Since
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{C_{1}}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{C_{2}}
satisfy the ISSC, we have

f_{i}(A_{C_{1}})\cap f_{j}(A_{C_{1}})=\emptyset\quad\text{for all }i\neq j,\qquad f_{i}(A_{C_{1}})\cap C_{1}=\emptyset\quad\text{for all }1\leq i\leq N,

and

g_{k}(A_{C_{2}})\cap g_{l}(A_{C_{2}})=\emptyset\quad\text{for all }k\neq l,\qquad g_{k}(A_{C_{2}})\cap C_{2}=\emptyset\quad\text{for all }1\leq k\leq M.

This leads to

\Phi_{ij}(A_{C_{1}}\times A_{C_{2}})\cap\Phi_{kl}(A_{C_{1}}\times A_{C_{2}})=(f_{i}(A_{C_{1}})\times g_{j}(A_{C_{2}}))\cap(f_{k}(A_{C_{1}})\times g_{l}(A_{C_{2}}))=\emptyset,

for all
\displaystyle(ij)\neq(kl)
. Since
\displaystyle A_{\mathcal{P}_{C}}\subseteq A_{C_{1}}\times A_{C_{2}}
, it follows that

\Phi_{(ij)}(A_{\mathcal{P}_{C}})\cap\Phi_{(kl)}(A_{\mathcal{P}_{C}})=\emptyset\quad\text{for all }(ij)\neq(kl).

Furthermore,

\Phi_{ij}(A_{C_{1}}\times A_{C_{2}})\cap(C_{1}\times C_{2})=(f_{i}(A_{C_{1}})\times g_{j}(A_{C_{2}}))\cap(C_{1}\times C_{2})=\emptyset.

Therefore, the product IFS
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{C}
satisfies the ISSC.

(2)

Since
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{C_{1}}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{C_{2}}
satisfy the IOSC, there exist open sets
\displaystyle U\subset X
and
\displaystyle V\subset Y
such that

\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(U)\subset U,\quad f_{i}(U)\cap f_{j}(U)=\emptyset\quad\text{for all }i\neq j,\quad\text{and }U\subset\overline{C_{1}},

and

\bigcup_{k=1}^{M}g_{k}(V)\subset V,\quad g_{k}(V)\cap g_{l}(V)=\emptyset\quad\text{for all }k\neq l,\quad\text{and }V\subset\overline{C_{2}}.

This implies

\Phi_{(ij)}(U\times V)\cap\Phi_{(kl)}(U\times V)=(f_{i}(U)\times g_{j}(V))\cap(f_{k}(U)\times g_{l}(V))=\emptyset\quad\text{for all }(ij)\neq(kl).

Moreover,

\Phi_{(ij)}(U\times V)=f_{i}(U)\times g_{j}(V)\subset U\times V\quad\text{for all }(ij)\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\times\{1,\ldots,M\}.

Also, since
\displaystyle U\subset\overline{C_{1}}
and
\displaystyle V\subset\overline{C_{2}}
, we have

U\times V\subset\overline{C_{1}}\times\overline{C_{2}}.

Define the open set
\displaystyle O:=U\times V\subset X\times Y
. Then

\bigcup_{(ij)}\Phi_{(ij)}(O)\subset O,\quad\Phi_{(ij)}(O)\cap\Phi_{(kl)}(O)=\emptyset\quad\text{for all }(ij)\neq(kl),\quad\text{and }O\subset\overline{C}.

Hence, the product IFS
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{C}
satisfies the IOSC.

∎

Let us see evidence of a connection between fractal measures of the IFSs
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}
,
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}
, and
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{C}.

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}=\{(X,d_{1});f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{N},C_{1}\}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}=\{(Y,d_{2});g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{M},C_{2}\}
be two inhomogeneous iterated function systems (IFSs) with associated probability vectors
\displaystyle(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{N})
and
\displaystyle(q_{0},q_{1},\ldots,q_{M})
, respectively. Define the inhomogeneous product IFS
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{C}:=\{X\times Y;\Phi_{(ij)},(C_{1},C_{2}),(ij)\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}\times\{1,2,\ldots,M\}\}
with
\displaystyle\Phi_{(ij)}(x,y)=(f_{i}(x),g_{j}(y))
and probability vector

\left\{r_{(ij)},\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{0}p_{i},\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{0}q_{j},p_{0}q_{0}~;~r_{(ij)}=p_{i}q_{j},1\leq i\leq N,1\leq j\leq M\right\}.

Let
\displaystyle\nu_{1}
and
\displaystyle\nu_{2}
be Borel probability measures on
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle Y
with supports
\displaystyle C_{1}
and
\displaystyle C_{2}
, respectively. Denote by
\displaystyle\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}
and
\displaystyle\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}
the inhomogeneous invariant measures associated with the IFSs
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}
. Then the product measure
\displaystyle\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\times\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}
is an inhomogeneous-type measure, given by

\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\times\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}=\sum_{i=1,j=1}^{N,M}r_{ij}\mu_{\mathcal{P}_{C}}\circ\Phi_{ij}^{-1}+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{0}p_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{0}q_{j}+p_{0}q_{0}\right)\nu,

where
\displaystyle(\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\times\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{2}})(A\times B)=\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}(A)\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}(B)
for Borel sets
\displaystyle A\subset X
,
\displaystyle B\subset Y
, and
\displaystyle\nu
is a Borel probability measure on
\displaystyle X\times Y
.

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle r_{(ij)}=p_{i}q_{j}
, we have

\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\circ f_{i}^{-1}+p_{0}\nu_{1},\quad\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}q_{j}\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}\circ g_{j}^{-1}+q_{0}\nu_{2}.

Then,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\times\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\circ f_{i}^{-1}+p_{0}\nu_{1}\right)\times\left(\sum_{j=1}^{M}q_{j}\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}\circ g_{j}^{-1}+q_{0}\nu_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1,j=1}^{N,M}r_{(ij)}\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\circ f_{i}^{-1}\times\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}\circ g_{j}^{-1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}q_{0}\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\circ f_{i}^{-1}\times\nu_{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{0}q_{j}\nu_{1}\times\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}\circ g_{j}^{-1}+p_{0}q_{0}\nu_{1}\times\nu_{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1,j=1}^{N,M}r_{(ij)}\left(\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\times\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}\right)\circ\Phi_{(ij)}^{-1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}q_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{0}q_{j}+p_{0}q_{0}\right)\nu,

where

\nu:=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}q_{0}\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\circ f_{i}^{-1}\times\nu_{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{0}q_{j}\nu_{1}\times\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}\circ g_{j}^{-1}+p_{0}q_{0}\nu_{1}\times\nu_{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}q_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{0}q_{j}+p_{0}q_{0}}

is a Borel probability measure on
\displaystyle X\times Y
.
∎

8.
Conclusion and future remarks

We have established a set of significant results on dimension theory and constructed new fractal sets using Falconer’s sub-self-similar concept. This work generalizes several existing results in the literature and, in the future, will be extended to more general fractal sets or attractors of dynamical systems. The fractal sets we have constructed have potential applications in the animation industry, computer-aided geometric design (CAGD), and graphic design. In future work, we plan to estimate the Assouad dimension of these ISSS sets and investigate the dimension spectrum.
