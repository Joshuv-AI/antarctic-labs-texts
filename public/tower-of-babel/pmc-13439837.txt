Resting-state fMRI-based machine learning for predicting SSRI treatment response in major depressive disorder
Authors: Yuxuan Hu, Jinrui Gao, Yaoze Liu, Rui Zhong, Zixuan Wu, Juan Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMajor Depressive Disorder (MDD) is a prevalent mental health condition with significant societal impact. Although prior research has highlighted the brain changes modulated by antidepressant therapy, their efficacy and effectiveness are debated. The low rates of treatment response still existed in the pharmacological therapy of MDD. Exploring an optimal neurological predictor of symptom improvement caused by pharmacotherapy is urgently needed for improving response to treatment. Our purpose is to develop a predictive model for MDD therapy using machine learning techniques based on resting-state fMRI metrics.MethodsA total of 116 MDD patients underwent 3.0T resting-state magnetic resonance image scanning. Demographic data and the 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-24) were collected from all participants. An additional independent cohort of 25 MDD patients was included for external model validation. Based on the reduction rate of HAMD-24 scores at different time points, the patients were divided into an early improvement group, an early response group, and a clinical response group: (1) Early improvement group: HAMD-24 reduction rate ≥ 20% after 1 week of treatment; (2) Early response group: HAMD-24 reduction rate ≥ 25% after 2 weeks of treatment; (3) Clinical response group: HAMD-24 reduction rate ≥ 50% after 4 weeks of treatment. For model construction, LASSO regression was applied for feature selection, integrating identified brain regions, HAMD-24 symptom clusters, and clinical variables. Five machine learning models were established based on features screened by the LASSO regression model to predict treatment response in MDD. All models were trained and assessed using 10-fold cross-validation, the most stable logistic regression model was selected for external validation in a temporally independent cohort (n = 25).ResultsAt 1 week, alterations were mainly observed in the frontal and sensorimotor regions. At 2 weeks, differences were found in the opercular, postcentral, orbitofrontal, temporal, and angular areas. At 4 weeks, additional abnormalities appeared in the frontal, occipital, and postcentral cortices. Moreover, right Postcentral gyru was found to be present in the differential brain regions observed at 1 week, 2 weeks, and 4 weeks comparisons. In addition, ten features related to the treatment response were identified using the LASSO regression model, including age, years of education, core depressive symptoms, and several brain region features primarily involving the postcentral gyrus. Among the constructed machine learning models, the Logistic Regression model based on LASSO-selected features (including age, education, core depressive symptoms, and the postcentral gyrus) demonstrated the most stable performance. It achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.801 in the internal validation and maintained robust predictive performance in the independent external validation set, indicating promising generalizability. Following independent external validation, the results showed that the predictive performance of the Logistic regression model on the external validation set yielded an AUC value of 0.643 and an accuracy of 0.60. This indicates that the brain regions involved in the model—namely the right postcentral gyrus, right precentral gyrus, left superior frontal gyrus, left middle occipital gyrus, and the orbital part of the right inferior frontal gyrus—hold promise as exploratory neural correlates for predicting the efficacy of SSRIs in depression.ConclusionMDD exhibits early functional alterations in brain regions involved in emotion regulation, cognition, and sensorimotor processing. Integrating these multi-metric neural features with clinical variables via machine learning models suggests potential clinical utility for evaluating and predicting early SSRI treatment outcomes.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a highly prevalent psychiatric condition that affects more than 332 million individuals worldwide in 2021 and imposes a substantial burden on both patients and society [1, 2]. The first-line treatment for MDD is typically antidepressant medication, particularly selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) [3, 4]. However, the efficacy of pharmacotherapy for MDD varies between individuals [5], with 40–50% of patients not responding to treatment in clinical practice [6]. The study demonstrated that early symptomatic improvement during antidepressant treatment was predictive of later clinical remission. Conversely, MDD patients who showed no response after 2 weeks of treatment achieved a final remission rate of only 4% when the treatment regimen remained unchanged [7]. Retrospective analyses of multiple clinical trials indicated that a lack of response after 2 weeks of antidepressant treatment reliably predicted unfavorable outcomes at 6–8 weeks [8]. A study demonstrated that lack of response to SSRI therapy at 2 weeks is a robust predictor of poor clinical treatment outcome at the end of the standard treatment course [9]. Therefore, elucidating the neuropathological mechanisms underlying MDD and exploring potential neuroimaging correlates of early treatment response could provide valuable insights for optimizing individualized therapeutic strategies in clinical practice.

Several resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) studies have reported functional differences between patients with MDD and healthy controls. MDD patients have lower node strength in the right hippocampal CA3/4 subregion and a reduced clustering coefficient in the right dentate gyrus, while the clustering coefficient in the right amygdala’s central nucleus correlates positively with depression severity [10]. Compared to healthy controls, MDD also show reduced functional connectivity between the left amygdala and both the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) and the right anterior insula. In contrast, the right amygdala has stronger connectivity with the bilateral hippocampus and left middle temporal gyrus [11]. Individual differences in functional indices are often linked to treatment outcomes. High neuroticism is linked to increased connectivity within the salience, executive control, and somatomotor networks, which predicts poorer treatment outcomes. After 8 weeks of treatment, this connectivity was reduced [12]. Longitudinal studies on first-episode adolescent MDD patients have shown that the amygdala’s connectivity with the superior temporal gyrus and cingulate gyrus increases before treatment. After eight weeks of SSRI treatment, these connections strengthen further, alongside symptom improvement [13]. The functional connectivity between the hippocampus and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) is negatively correlated with cognitive symptoms, such as inattention and memory issues, but improves with treatment [14]. Several studies suggest that neuroimaging markers, such as cortical thickness in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) or early changes in functional connectivity in the subgenual ACC, may predict relapse or remission [15]. For instance, in an escitalopram-treated cohort study, higher subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC)–frontoparietal connectivity in baseline distinguished remitters from non-remitters with 72.64% accuracy [16]. However, the results across studies are still inconsistent, highlighting the need for further work to confirm which brain activity patterns predict antidepressant treatment outcomes.

Recently, machine learning has shown significant potential to assist in predicting individualized treatment responses in MDD by identifying latent multivariate patterns in neuroimaging data. Rather than relying on traditional univariate comparisons, researchers are increasingly applying advanced algorithms (Gaussian Naive Bayes, AdaBoost, Support Vector Machines, Random Forest, ensemble learning, and deep learning) to high-dimensional structural and resting-state functional MRI metrics [17, 18]. For example, some pioneering studies have utilized regional volumetric features or intrinsic functional connectivity within specific large-scale brain networks to construct predictive classifiers for symptom improvement and clinical remission [16, 19]. Furthermore, recent multi-center neuroimaging consortiums and systematic reviews have further confirmed that integrating multi-metric fMRI data with machine learning frameworks can capture subtle neurobiological heterogeneity, thereby achieving preliminary stratification of antidepressant efficacy prior to treatment initiation [20]. Although these studies robustly demonstrate that neuroimaging features contain valuable predictive signals, translating these models into clinical practice still faces major methodological challenges.

First, psychiatric neuroimaging machine learning studies are commonly plagued by high-dimensional data; combining high-dimensional MRI features with relatively small sample sizes drastically inflates the risk of model overfitting. Second, there is a pervasive lack of testing on temporally or geographically independent external validation cohorts, which severely limits the reproducibility and generalizability of the reported models. Finally, due to these validation deficits, predictive performance is often overstated, prematurely labeling exploratory findings as definitive clinical “biomarkers” rather than acknowledging them as potential neural correlates.

In this study, we predicted antidepressant treatment outcomes in MDD using rs-fMRI, clinical symptom clusters and machine learning models. The objectives were to: (1) use resting-state fMRI analytical metrics to identify indicators related to antidepressant treatment in MDD, and (2) construct machine learning models based on brain regions with significant discriminative features to help identify treatment-responsive characteristics in MDD patients. Furthermore, to address these methodological challenges, we validated our finalized model on a temporally independent external cohort, aiming to identify reliable, exploratory neuroimaging correlates.

Methods

Participants

A total of 129 patients with MDD were recruited from the Department of Psychology at Xuzhou Oriental Hospital affiliated with Xuzhou Medical University between June 2021 and December 2024. All patients were diagnosed with MDD by two experienced psychiatrists based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria and the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) [21]. Only inpatients aged 18–60 years with either a first episode or a recurrent episode of MDD were included. Baseline clinical assessments were conducted on the day of admission using the MINI and the HAMD-24 [22], and only patients with HAMD-24 scores ≥ 21 were enrolled. Exclusion criteria included: (1) meeting DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for any other Axis I psychiatric disorder; (2) a history or current presence of other psychiatric illnesses besides MDD; (3) secondary depressive episodes due to organic brain disorders or other medical conditions; (4) neurodegenerative diseases, traumatic brain injury, cerebrovascular disease, or other structural brain lesions; (5) major physical illnesses such as severe cardiac, hepatic, or renal dysfunction or uncontrolled diabetes; (6) pregnancy, breastfeeding, or plans for pregnancy; and (7) any contraindications to MRI, including visible head scars. All participants were right-handed and free of substance or drug abuse. Among the initially enrolled patients, 13 were excluded due to excessive head motion during scanning (translation > 3 mm or rotation > 3°), resulting in 116 patients with usable imaging data. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Oriental Hospital affiliated with Xuzhou Medical University, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the study. The participant screening flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Xuzhou Oriental Hospital affiliated with Xuzhou Medical University. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical evaluation

The assessment of MDD patients included rs-fMRI neural and scale symptom assessment in the current study. Before pharmacotherapy, all patients with MDD completed resting-state fMRI scan and HAMD scale to get the time series of each voxel in the whole brain and HAMD scores; MDD patients then began to receive antidepressant treatment with typical selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs). To account for the heterogeneity in SSRIs types and dosages, the weekly prescribed medication for each patient was converted into fluoxetine dose equivalents. This allowed for standardized statistical control and comparison of medication effects across the cohort. The medication doses were prescribed and adjusted by the treating clinicians according to routine clinical practice and followed the recommended dose ranges.

Based on the reduction rate of HAMD-24 scores at different time points, the patients were divided into an early improvement group, an early response group, and a clinical response group: (1) Early improvement group: HAMD-24 reduction rate ≥ 20% after 1 week of treatment; (2) Early response group: HAMD-24 reduction rate ≥ 25% after 2 weeks of treatment; (3) Clinical response group: HAMD-24 reduction rate ≥ 50% after 4 weeks of treatment. The HAMD-24 reduction rate was calculated using the following formula: HAMD reduction rate= (HAMD score before treatment – HAMD score after treatment)/ HAMD score before treatment *100%.

The 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-24) is divided into five symptom clusters according to symptomatic dimensions: 1. Core depressive emotional symptom: depressed mood, feelings of guilt, suicidal thoughts, insomnia, disturbed sleep, early awakening, work and interest impairment; 2. Psychomotor and agitation symptom: psychomotor retardation, agitation; 3. Anxiety and somatization symptom: psychic anxiety, somatic anxiety, gastrointestinal symptoms, general somatic symptoms, sexual symptoms, hypochondriasis; 4. Weight and insight symptom cluster: weight loss, lack of insight; 5. Additional symptom cluster: diurnal variation, depersonalization or derealization, paranoid symptoms, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, feeling of incapacity, hopelessness, inferiority feelings. This classification is based on clinical and research definitions of core depressive symptoms, conventional categorization protocols, symptomatic dimensions of the HAMD-24, and standard item grouping strategies adopted in comparable neuroimaging studies.

Clinical outcomes were evaluated using the HAMD-24 reduction rate, and patients were divided into the early improvement group and non-early improvement group at week 1, the early response group and non-early response group at week 2, as well as the clinical response group and non-clinical response group at week 4. Baseline differences in amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF), regional homogeneity (ReHo), were examined among different therapeutic efficacy subgroups. A random forest model was then constructed to predict antidepressant treatment efficacy using neuroimaging features.

Imaging data acquisition

We collected rs-fMRI data from all subjects used 3.0T Siemens Verio magnetic resonance scanner in the radiological department of Xuzhou Oriental Hospital affiliated to Xuzhou Medical University. Using Gradient Echo-Echo Plane Imaging (GRE-EPI) sequence to collect BOLD images. The parameters are as follows: pulse repetition time (TR) = 3000 ms, echo time (TE) = 40 ms, flip angle = 90 °, number of layers = 32, layer thickness = 4 mm, field of view (FOV) = 240 × 240 mm2, matrix = 64 × 64, voxel value = 3.75 × 3.75 mm, time point = 135, BOLD image scanning requires a total of 6 min 56 s. During scanning, participants were instructed to remain awake with eyes closed, keep their heads still, lie quietly on the scanner bed, and avoid active thinking.

Functional image pre-processing

The raw imaging data were acquired in DICOM format and converted to NIFTI format using the dcm2nii tool from the MRIcron toolkit for further processing (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/mricron/). Functional images were preprocessed using the DPARSF [23] toolbox. The preprocessing steps included removing the first 10 volumes to eliminate initial signal instability and scanner noise; slice timing correction; realignment; regression of nuisance signals (white matter, cerebrospinal fluid, and head motion parameters using the Friston 24-parameter model); spatial normalization via echo-planar imaging (EPI); and linear detrending. The ALFF was measured by taking the square root of the power spectrum of each voxel in the 0.01–0.08 Hz frequency range. To standardize the data, ALFF values for each voxel were divided by the global mean ALFF of each participant (mALFF). Fractional ALFF (fALFF) was also calculated as the ratio of power within the low-frequency range (0.01–0.08 Hz) to the total power across the full frequency range (0–0.25 Hz) [24]. Standardized ReHo maps were then computed for each participant.

Resting-state data from participants were excluded if head motion exceeded 3 mm translation or 3° rotation in any direction. Accordingly, 13 MDD patients were excluded based on head motion criteria, leaving 116 MDD patients for subsequent analyses.

Statistical analysis

To compare general demographic characteristics and mean head motion (Jenkinson) between remission groups and non-remission groups with MDD, we conducted the F test followed by independent sample t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables, and chi-squared tests for categorical variables. Repeated-measures ANOVA was further performed to assess baseline brain region metrics across different time points (1, 2, and 4 weeks) between the remission and non- remission groups. We conducted repeated-measures ANOVA to compare ALFF and ReHo, using DPARSF, with a voxelwise P-value of 0.005 and a clusterwise P-value of 0.05, corrected by the Gaussian random field (GRF) correction [25]. The statistical thresholds were set to P < 0.05 for all analyses.

Feature selection

To reduce feature dimensionality and identify variables most relevant to antidepressant treatment response, we applied a LASSO regression model (LassoCV), with the regularization parameter (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:$$\end{document}) automatically optimized via 10-fold cross-validation. All analyses were performed using R 4.2.3 and Python 3.11.4. LASSO implements L1 regularization to achieve simultaneous feature selection and coefficient shrinkage.

Machine learning modeling

Based on the 10 features selected by LASSO, we constructed multiple machine learning models to predict the clinical efficacy of antidepressant treatment, including XGBoost, logistic regression, LightGBM, AdaBoost, and Gaussian Naïve Bayes (GaussianNB). Model performance was assessed using 10-fold cross-validation, with primary evaluation metrics including area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, F1 score, and Cohen’s Kappa coefficient. Differences in AUC between models were statistically compared using DeLong’s test.

Brain visualisation

The surface-view brain results and ROIs were produced using BrainNet Viewer (RRID: SCR_009446: https://www.nitrc.orgprojects/bnv/ [26]) with the “nearest voxel” mapping algorithm on a standard MNI space brain surface. The multislice view brain results were produced using MRIcroGL, (RRID: SCR_002403; https://www.nitrc.org/projects/mricrogl).

External validation

To evaluate the clinical application potential of our predictive framework, the Logistic Regression model—which demonstrated the most stable performance among the five evaluated predictive models—was selected for external validation. This validation was conducted on a temporally independent external cohort comprising 25 newly recruited patients with MDD. Statistical comparisons confirmed that the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between the primary training cohort and the external validation cohort, ensuring that the model evaluation was conducted on a strictly representative independent sample. A “locked-model” strategy was employed during the validation process; specifically, all model parameters and feature selection thresholds were kept strictly identical to the optimized results from the primary cohort training phase. The baseline features of each patient in the external cohort (including age, years of education, the core depressive emotional symptom, and the corresponding regional brain functional metrics) were inputted into the finalized model. Finally, the predictive performance was quantitatively evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity.

Machine learning prediction models

Importantly, to ensure the interpretability of our models, all predictive neuroimaging and clinical features were extracted exclusively from the baseline (pre-treatment) assessments, which were then used to predict stage-specific clinical outcomes (weeks 1, 2, and 4). To provide a transparent assessment, we evaluated the algorithms across distinct validation stages. In the internal dataset, while the XGBoost model achieved an exceptionally high AUC of 0.962 on the training set, its performance discrepancy during internal validation suggested a tendency toward overfitting. Conversely, the Logistic Regression model based on LASSO-selected features demonstrated the most balanced and stable performance, achieving an internal validation AUC of 0.801 (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The present study reveals that pre-treatment enhancements in spontaneous brain activity within specific networks can distinguish patients who achieve early improvement at week 1, early response at week 2, and clinical response at week 4 from their non-responding counterparts. Critically, we found that the predictive neuroanatomical substrates differed across early (1-week), intermediate (2-week), and late (4-week) treatment phases. This suggests distinct neural prerequisites for initial versus sustained response to SSRIs. A machine learning model integrating these neural features with clinical demographics demonstrated robust predictive efficacy, underscoring the translational potential of this connectomic signature. We identified a functional connectomic signature characterized most strongly by increased functional indicators in the somatosensory and motor feedback regions, somatomotor control, and executive control brain areas. These findings provide insight into the potential multi-level mechanisms of antidepressant action.

Our investigation of baseline brain regions across different time points reveals a dynamic temporal pattern of regional functional changes associated with antidepressant treatment response. During the early treatment phase (week 1), we observed increased ALFF/ReHo in the SFG.L, PoCG.R, FuG.R, and PreCG.L. These findings are consistent with previous studies, suggesting that early functional alterations in prefrontal and sensorimotor regions may represent initial neural adaptations to antidepressant treatment [27]. The involvement of the SFG.L is particularly noteworthy, as this region constitutes a key node of the cognitive control network, and its enhanced function may underlie early improvements in executive function and emotion regulation [28]. At week 2, functional changes involved more brain regions in the treatment process. We noted enhanced functional metrics in the R.Oper.R, bilateral PoCG, IFG.Orb.R, MTG.R, and AG.R. This expansion of affected regions suggests a progressive normalization of functional networks, consistent with the hierarchical model of antidepressant action proposed by several groups, which describes the temporal dynamics of antidepressant onset and the sequential recovery of brain networks [27]. The emergence of temporoparietal junction regions aligns with evidence indicating their critical role in self-referential processing and theory of mind - functions often impaired in depression [29]. At week 4, the most extensive changes were observed, involving the SFG.R, PreCG.R, OMG.L, OSG.R, and SFGm.L. The progressive recruitment of visual processing regions (OMG.L, OSG.R) may reflect the restoration of attentional networks and improvement in sensory integration, which are frequently compromised in depression [30, 31]. The involvement of the SFGm.L, a key node of the default mode network, suggests a normalization of self-referential processes, potentially corresponding to reduced rumination and improved cognitive control [32, 33].

Early investigations into treatment outcomes in depression primarily focused on clinical symptomatology or psychosocial determinants, but recent rapid advances in neuroimaging have enabled human neuropathological studies that systematically probe the anatomical and functional mechanisms underlying depressive-spectrum disorders and their associations with treatment response [34]. Within these investigations, the sensorimotor cortex—particularly the PoCG.R—has consistently emerged as a key node associated with clinical phenotype and treatment response, with resting-state connectivity patterns and graph-theoretical metrics highlighting its contributions to individualized outcome prediction [27, 35]. Several studies have further reported altered connectivity between the PoCG and PreCG, and these connectivity changes are closely associated with early antidepressant effects, suggesting that functional reorganization within the sensorimotor circuit may represent an early predictor of treatment efficacy [27]. Evidence regarding the functional significance of the primary motor cortex (PreCG.R) also indicates that its interactions with the PoCG constitute high-importance features in multiple predictive models, supporting the view that the topology of the sensorimotor loop is critical for explaining symptom change and treatment heterogeneity [35]. The PreCG.R has likewise been identified as a meaningful node, and its interactions with the Postcentral often emerge as high-importance features in predictive models, underscoring the importance of the sensorimotor loop in explaining symptom change [27]. By contrast, frontal regions—particularly the OFG and IFG—play central roles in emotion regulation and reward processing, and structural or functional alterations within these areas are closely linked to depressive symptomatology and pharmacological response [36]. In addition, the OMG.L has been repeatedly identified in brain function indices’ studies as related to early treatment response, suggesting that visual and attentional processes may influence early clinical improvement [37]. Taken together, converging evidence suggests that the PoCG, PreCG, frontal, and occipital regions each provide complementary information for explaining clinical heterogeneity and predicting treatment outcomes in depression [27, 36]. These regions therefore warrant systematic inclusion in feature-selection and model-validation pipelines within multi-metric fMRI frameworks.

In this study, we integrated group-level voxel-wise statistics with machine learning predictive modeling. This dual approach is complementary: the group-level comparisons elucidate the shared neurobiological mechanisms underlying SSRI therapeutic effects, while the machine learning framework leverages these features for individualized clinical outcome prediction. The multi-metric Logistic Regression model achieved an internal AUC of 0.801 and maintained an AUC of 0.643 on the independent external validation set. Given the modest external performance, our model should currently be viewed as an exploratory proof-of-concept rather than a finalized clinical tool for precise prediction. Nevertheless, the results underscore the added value of incorporating neurobiological markers alongside traditional clinical predictors to understand treatment heterogeneity.

The specific brain regions identified by LASSO – the right PoCG, IFG.L (orbital part), PreCG.R, SFGm.L, and OMG.L – show significant consistency with the existing literature on neural correlates of antidepressant response [38, 39]. The consistent emergence of sensorimotor regions (PoCG.R and PreCG.R) in both our univariate and multivariate analyses reinforce their importance in the mechanism of treatment response. This convergence suggests that functional reorganization within the sensorimotor network may represent a fundamental process of antidepressant action, potentially related to the alleviation of psychomotor symptoms commonly observed in depression [27, 37]. The selection of IFG.Orb.R and SFG.L as key predictors is particularly insightful, as these regions are central to emotional processing and cognitive control networks, respectively [40]. Their inclusion in the optimal feature set supports emerging theories that antidepressant efficacy involves the coordinated normalization of limbic (emotional) and cognitive control systems [28, 41]. The presence of the OMG.L further suggests that visual processing circuits may play a previously underappreciated role in treatment response, potentially related to attentional biases and sensory integration deficits in depression [30, 31].

Regarding the machine learning results, while XGBoost demonstrated superior performance on the training set (AUC = 0.962), its tendency toward overfitting on the validation set highlights the critical importance of model generalizability for clinical applications. To transparently address this, we deployed the Logistic Regression model onto a strictly independent external validation set (n = 25). The external validation results demonstrate that the baseline functional alterations in the selected brain regions—namely the right postcentral gyrus, right precentral gyrus, left superior frontal gyrus, left middle occipital gyrus, and the orbital part of the right inferior frontal gyrus—contain modest but generalizable biological information associated with SSRI efficacy. However, given the modest external AUC (0.643), these regions should currently be interpreted as exploratory neural correlates reflecting underlying disease mechanisms, rather than definitive, standalone clinical biomarkers. Future research should explore ensemble methods that balance predictive power with clinical utility. The stable performance of logistic regression across datasets suggests it may be more suitable for real-world implementation, where model interpretability and reliability are paramount [41]. Future studies could incorporate additional healthy control groups to further identify imaging features associated with the clinical pathophysiology of MDD. Moreover, multicenter investigations with larger cohorts are needed to validate the predictive models and enhance their generalizability. Longitudinal follow-up examining dynamic changes in neural function may also provide important insights and improve the robustness and clinical utility of prediction models.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the lack of healthy controls restricts our ability to explore neural substrates dimensionally associated with clinical MDD. Second, the sample size (n = 116) was relatively small and derived from a single center. Third, the reliance on baseline cross-sectional data limits our ability to capture dynamic longitudinal changes in brain function throughout the course of treatment. Fourth, and critically, we must acknowledge the methodological limitations inherent in our machine learning feature selection pipeline. Because our models were constructed using an integrated clinical analysis software, feature selection (LASSO) was performed prior to the cross-validation loop. We fully recognize that the lack of a strictly nested cross-validation framework introduces a risk of feature selection bias and potential data leakage, which can artificially inflate internal model performance. Despite these limitations, we effectively mitigated the risks of data leakage and overfitting by validating the finalized model on a strictly independent external cohort (n = 25). Furthermore, it should be noted that the sample size of our external validation cohort is relatively small (n = 25), which may constrain the statistical power and potentially contribute to the modest validation performance observed. This external validation step successfully confirmed the robustness and generalizability of the identified neuroimaging features. Future studies employing strictly nested validation architectures and longitudinal tracking in larger, multi-center samples are imperative to definitively establish these neuroimaging markers for clinical translation.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated specific baseline functional alterations in brain regions associated with antidepressant treatment response. Increases in ALFF and ReHo were observed in the salience, sensorimotor, and executive control regions after 1, 2, and 4 weeks of treatment. Furthermore, integrating these baseline multi-metric neuroimaging features, clinical data, and demographic profiles into machine learning models provided robust and generalizable predictions of SSRI treatment efficacy. These baseline functional features were correlated with subsequent clinical improvement in depressive symptoms, suggesting that they may serve as exploratory neural correlates of treatment response. Thus, these pre-treatment brain activities provide valuable insights into the neurobiological mechanisms underlying antidepressant efficacy.
