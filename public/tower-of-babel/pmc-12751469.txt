Antimicrobial resistance and stewardship in a dental curriculum in South Africa: a single-site curriculum mapping study
Authors: Suwayda Ahmed, RZ Adam, R Coetzee, RD Maart
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) currently poses a significant public health crisis. Shortcomings in the knowledge and awareness of AMR and antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) among dental practitioners and undergraduate dental students have been widely reported, highlighting the need for comprehensive education and training programs to address AMR and AMS in curricula. Curriculum mapping is a valuable tool in healthcare education, allowing for the alignment of content with competencies, identifying gaps, and establishing best practices.ObjectivesMapping an undergraduate dental curriculum at a dental school in South Africa against the AMR and AMS core competencies as outlined by the Health Professions Council of South Africa (HPCSA) and World Health Organization (WHO).MethodsUndergraduate courses (n = 47) within the dental program were included. The outcomes were mapped against the HPCSA core competencies for dental graduates and the WHO curricula guide for health workers’ education and training on AMR.ResultsSpecified core competencies as described by the HPCSA and WHO as they relate to AMR and AMS were present in two third-year courses and two fourth-year courses of the undergraduate dentistry program.ConclusionsThe curriculum mapping exercise highlighted the limited integration of AMR and AMS competencies in the undergraduate dental curriculum. Although the core competencies as outlined by the HPCSA and WHO on AMR and AMS have been addressed, it is present in the third and fourth years of the undergraduate program. This highlights the need for enhanced education on these competencies, to equip graduates for antimicrobial stewardship.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08313-1.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance, Antimicrobial stewardship, Dental education, Curriculum, South africa

Introduction

AMR is recognized as a leading global public health threat. The increased use and misuse of antibiotics have contributed to increased AMR rates, resulting in complex and potentially ineffective patient treatment, extended hospitalization and increased mortality rates [1–3]. In addition, the development of new antimicrobial agents has stagnated, making AMR an ever-growing global concern [4].

Antimicrobial resistance is characterized by the ability of microbial organisms to survive the effects of antimicrobial agents and preserve viability, despite having previously been susceptible to these agents [5]. The prescribing of antibiotics has increased consistently in recent years, with dental practitioners considered to be the leading prescribers of systemic antibiotics, and account for approximately 10% of all antibiotics prescribed globally [6–8].

Various strategies are proposed to decrease AMR in order to mitigate this crisis. One such strategy is to increase awareness of AMR and promote antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) in healthcare education [9]. Undergraduate healthcare education is acknowledged as an opportunity to develop competencies surrounding antibiotic prescribing and AMR awareness, and thus effectively practice positive antibiotic stewardship measures [10].

Rising global AMR rates highlight the critical need to evaluate AMR awareness and AMS within undergraduate healthcare education to ensure the competence of future prescribers. Embedding AMR and AMS content in health professional curricula is essential for preparing future healthcare workers to address this challenge [11, 12]. AMS programs include methods to measure the prescribing of antibiotics, improve rational antibiotic prescribing, decrease antibiotic resistance, reduce health complications of needless and incorrect antibiotic use and thus improve patients’ health status [12].

Brink et al. (2017) have emphasized the importance of expanding AMS efforts and undergraduate education among healthcare professionals, including those in dentistry, veterinary science, and nursing within the South African healthcare system [13]. Similarly, an editorial in an issue of the SADJ, expanded on the increased rate of AMR globally and highlighted the need for AMS in the dentistry profession in South Africa [14]. A scoping review conducted in 2024, examined antimicrobial knowledge among various healthcare disciplines, and found that the dental profession was least represented in the data collected. This is of concern, as evidence suggests that the dental profession plays a considerable role in antibiotic prescribing, AMR awareness, and AMS. This underscores the need to integrate these themes into the curriculum; and additionally, consider multidisciplinary healthcare education to broaden the understanding of factors related to AMR and AMS [15].

Curriculum mapping in healthcare education has garnered significant interest in recent years, as a means to measure the relevance and effectiveness of educational programs. Higher education institutions are increasingly using curriculum mapping to ensure compliance with accreditation standards and maintaining quality assurance [2, 16]. Curriculum mapping in healthcare education can strengthen AMR and AMS competencies by linking learning to real-world practice. An effective approach is an integrated curriculum, where subjects are connected through clinical themes to foster interdisciplinary learning, critical thinking, and practical problem-solving [17]. Curriculum mapping provides a means of reviewing and evaluating curricula by highlighting connections between components, benefiting both established and developing programs [18].

The process may also assist in identifying any insufficiencies in a curriculum. It is a powerful tool that lecturers can utilise to guide course content with competence in the field of study [19]. It is also important to note that stakeholders should consider the various barriers that may influence and constrain curriculum redesign and change. These include concerns about curriculum overload and balancing in-depth coverage of the subject; where existing curricula is already full and the addition of content may be limited. There may also be perceived low priority of the content, and thus resistance to change within educational institutions [20, 21].

Curriculum changes are driven by community needs, accreditation requirements, policy shifts, and advances in knowledge. Constant revision of curricula allows for addressing issues and developing skills amongst graduates. Moreover, changes in the needs of communities being served by medical or dental students may necessitate a reassessment or overhaul of the curriculum [22, 23]. The primary focus of an undergraduate healthcare course should be to align with the needs of the population and the healthcare system. Consequently, these needs will recommend the graduate skills required; as well as provide guidance to the focus, content and structure of the academic program [24].

Dental schools are required to graduate well-rounded dental professionals who possess the necessary knowledge and skills to meet the evolving health needs of society. By providing comprehensive undergraduate training on the careful prescribing of antibiotics, awareness of AMR and AMS, graduating healthcare professionals would acquire the necessary skills to prescribe cautiously in clinical practice [11, 12].

Methods

Institutional context

The Faculty of Dentistry at the University of the Western Cape (UWC) served as the setting for this curriculum mapping exercise. We obtained ethical approval for this project from the Biomedical Research Committee at the UWC (BMREC Reference Number: BM24/4/6). The Bachelor of Dentistry program at UWC consists of a full-time five-year program. For this study, the researchers evaluated the undergraduate curriculum for AMR and AMS content through an initial curriculum mapping exercise, ensuring alignment with the competencies as outlined by the HPCSA and WHO. The undergraduate dentistry curriculum at UWC consists of 47 courses in a 5-year degree course. The researchers used the faculty yearbook to access the undergraduate course descriptions, which comprises of course descriptors which includes the following information: course topic, home department, year of study, learning outcomes and main content of each course. The Bachelor of Dentistry curriculum at UWC, must comply with HPCSA requirement for accreditation. It is important to consider that methods of integrating AMR and AMS content may vary based on institutional context, resources, and specific curriculum approach.

National competency requirements

Dental schools are required to graduate well-rounded dental professionals who possess the necessary knowledge and skills to meet the evolving health needs of society. The Medical and Dental Professions Board of the HPCSA together with the national Department of Higher Education oversee the undergraduate training of healthcare workers in South Africa. The HPCSA determines the standards of education and training, and as such, the dental undergraduate program is guided by core competencies that have to be achieved as determined by the HPCSA [25, 26].

International competency standards

The WHO provides guidelines and resources that may guide the content and emphasis of health education programs globally. Various global health priorities, initiatives and frameworks are offered by the WHO, which may provide guidance and stimulus to educational programs; with the end goal of promoting public health and supporting health educators. One such guideline as it relates to AMR is the WHO curricula guide for health workers’ education and training on antimicrobial resistance. The curricula guide outlines an AMR competency framework by recommending learning objectives and outcomes for healthcare workers concerning AMR and AMS. These competencies are relevant and can be modified to dental education, spanning different contexts and settings. This curricula guide was developed to provide healthcare workers with essential knowledge and skills required for the effective management of AMR, and outlines crucial competencies that need to be included in healthcare workers education; in an attempt to comprehensively manage AMR [27]. While the WHO does not determine standards for the education and training of undergraduate dentistry programs in South Africa, its core competencies for health education may influence the content of these programs, and may provide internationally recognized standards for curriculum content and evaluation.

Mapping of the curriculum

The first researcher (SA) conducted the curriculum mapping manually. A second researcher (RM) independently reviewed and verified the curriculum mapping exercise to enhance the trustworthiness of the exercise. We based the curriculum mapping on Harden’s framework [28].

Harden describes the following possible windows to be included in a curriculum map: learning outcomes, curriculum content, learning resources, assessment, learning opportunities, location, resources, management of curriculum, staff and students [27]. For this study, the expected learning outcomes and curriculum content or areas of expertise covered were included as windows.

Mapping methodology

The curriculum mapping exercise was conducted through a manual review using an Excel spreadsheet, where the primary researcher (SA) examined each of the seven core competencies (three HPCSA competencies and four WHO competencies) against the learning outcomes and content descriptions of all 47 undergraduate courses in the faculty yearbook, to demonstrate the link between the undergraduate dentistry courses and HPCSA and WHO competencies concerning AMR and AMS.

The primary researcher reviewed the learning outcomes and content descriptions from each course, searching for references of antimicrobial resistance, antibiotic stewardship, infection management, and related pharmacological principles. These learning outcomes and content descriptions were highlighted for evidence of competency analysis. We marked the competency as being present (√) when the learning outcomes and course descriptions addressed the competency area, or a reasonable inference could be made based on the content description. We marked the competency as being absent (X), when no related content was observed in the content description. The second researcher (RM) independently examined the same learning outcomes and content descriptions in the faculty yearbook. The researchers used investigator triangulation, with two researchers (SA and RM) independently reviewing the curriculum data. They then compared and discussed their findings to resolve any discrepancies. This approach minimized individual bias and enhanced the credibility and trustworthiness of the mapping results [29].

Stages in the curriculum mapping process: (Table 1)

The curriculum mapping was conducted through a three-stage process. All undergraduate courses were displayed vertically from year 1 to 5 to provide a comprehensive overview of the curriculum structure (Stage 1). Thereafter, the HPCSA core competencies for dental graduates were mapped horizontally; followed by the WHO curricula guide for health workers’ education and training on antimicrobial resistance competencies across each year of study. The specific focus was on antimicrobial resistance and antimicrobial stewardship content (Stage 2). Lastly, each course’s learning outcomes and content descriptions were reviewed to identify and document the evidence of the HPCSA and WHO competencies, by marking each competency as either present or absent (Stage 3).

Evidence of HPCSA and WHO competencies in year of study

The curriculum mapping exercise revealed a concentrated pattern of AMR and AMS competency coverage within specific years of the undergraduate dental program. Examining the first- and second-year content, none of the competencies related to AMR and AMS are covered. These are the foundational science years, and no emphasis is placed on these competencies. During the initial mapping phase, the researchers identified clear alignment between course content and competencies in four specific courses. The Medical Microbiology for Dentistry course addressed microbial resistance mechanisms and the threat of antimicrobial resistance, directly aligning with WHO competency frameworks. Similarly, the Dental Pharmacology course content described principles of drug action, therapeutic interventions, and clinical decision-making related to antimicrobial use, meeting several HPCSA competency requirements. Evidence of competencies in the fourth year of study was covered in the Periodontology and Maxillofacial and Oral Surgery modules, especially focussing on clinical protocols. The fourth year of the undergraduate program covers some of the competencies, however, some integrative concepts that enhance clinical practice are not covered. There is a measure of content specifically related to clinical applications and protocols, but foundational principles like core skills and essential clinical knowledge are absent. The final year of the undergraduate program does not overtly cover the competencies, but this may suggest that it could be part of the enacted curriculum or considered assumed knowledge. The lack of evidence of AMR and AMS competencies in the first, second and final year groups indicates the gap in the curriculum where undergraduate dental students may not receive suitable exposure or integration of this topic.

Discussion

This study presents a representation of the alignment of the current curriculum content related to AMR and AMS in the undergraduate dentistry program at UWC with national standards and international recommendations. The results of this study mapped an alignment between the undergraduate dental curriculum and the core competencies of the HPCSA and WHO, specifically in the third year, with the commencement of clinical training and treatment of patients, which continued into the fourth year of the program. The HPCSA core competencies relating to drug action principles, therapeutic interventions, and clinical procedures were focused in the third year of the program. Although the HPCSA competencies were highlighted in the third and fourth year of the undergraduate program, there was a lack of evidence of their integration in the early years as well as the final year of study. This suggests gaps in the early academic years which should cover necessary introductory concepts related to antimicrobial use, antimicrobial resistance mechanisms, and antimicrobial stewardship principles. Introducing this content and competencies in earlier years of the undergraduate program may provide constant foundational knowledge.

Literature has documented various approaches to integrating AMR and AMS content in health professions curricula. 15,31,32 The mapping results from this study suggest the need for opportunities to enhance AMR and AMS integration, by using evidence based educational strategies. For example, Nowburth et al. 2023, studied the use of gamification as a way to incorporate AMR and AMS content in undergraduate curricula, with an aim to improve knowledge, improve prescribing practices and change behaviour [30]. An interactive approach could have a positive effect on the identified gaps observed early in the curriculum. Similarly, AMS interprofessional education activities in undergraduate healthcare curricula has been highlighted to expand the understanding of the various influences that may contribute to AMR [15]. Another example of curricula enhancement to address AMR and AMS is the use of online interactive courses, as was offered by the University of Glasgow dental school. This online course was conducted in an effort to increase AMR awareness. It was found to be a valuable addition to the university teaching and had a positive impact on clinical practice [31]. These educational approaches from literature demonstrate the potential strategies that could be used to address the limited integration of AMR and AMS competencies identified in year 1, 2, and 5 of the curriculum.

It is important to examine how the curriculum is conceptualized when dealing with specific themes. Although the intended curriculum addresses concepts based on how educators choose to introduce them, the concept of the enacted curriculum provides additional insight. The enacted curriculum reflects the translation of the intended curriculum into practical instruction and learning experiences [32, 33]. It is possible that competencies related to AMR and AMS are integrated into clinical settings as part of the enacted curriculum. In such cases, topics like antibiotic prescribing, antimicrobial resistance, and stewardship could be taught through practical, real-world scenarios in the clinical environment. These competencies could be transferred currently, as part of the enacted curriculum in clinical courses, such as Oral Medicine, Endodontics, Periodontology, Maxillofacial and Oral Surgery, Paediatric Dentistry and Comprehensive Patient Management.

When observing current and/or proposed curriculum models, it is beneficial to visualize these models in relation to time and the various clinical courses. This approach facilitates the understanding of how integration occurs within the curriculum. Integration across disciplines within courses over a finite time period is described as horizontal integration, whereas vertical integration occurs across time from the start to the end of a course [34]. Vertical integration is especially important in healthcare curricula as it evolves with time, in an attempt to breach the barrier between basic science and clinical content [35].

This study has shown that while there is horizontal integration of AMR and AMS competencies in the third year of the program (across Medical Microbiology and Dental Pharmacology courses), there is limited vertical integration within the undergraduate program when examining the program from the first year to the final year. This limited vertical integration suggests that AMR and AMS concepts are not reinforced gradually throughout the curriculum, which may potentially limit students’ development of comprehensive understanding from foundational principles through to more advanced clinical applications.

Curriculum mapping has been proven to be an effective method to review or adjust curriculum content, and as such, a curriculum map has the potential to initiate dialogue around the content matter at all levels, i.e. subject, faculty and institutional level [36]. By mapping outlined competencies to educational content in an attempt to develop the anticipated skills in healthcare professionals, the value of an educational program is enhanced [37]. Given the global crisis surrounding AMR and AMS, it is worthwhile to reassess the curriculum and align the core competencies set by the HPCSA and WHO. This alignment could assist in addressing this public health crisis by fully integrating these competencies into dental education.

Curriculum mapping, review and amendments is not without challenges, especially in a loaded healthcare curriculum. Many of these barriers include academic faculty training, financial constraints, unfamiliarity with new content, increased study time in a full undergraduate programme and institutional challenges. It is therefore important to carefully plan and collaborate on any curriculum review or amendments, with the clear focus being a curriculum that enhances healthcare education [38, 39]. A key barrier to integrating AMR and AMS content is curriculum overload. Incorporating additional material into an already densely structured program poses considerable challenges. Additionally, introducing new content may be perceived as detracting from essential topics and existing course material. To address these challenges and address the current mapping results, a strategic approach which involves embedding AMR and AMS principles within existing courses rather than creating separate modules may be attempted. Furthermore, the vertical integration of key AMR concepts throughout the five-year program further supports learning by allowing reinforcement over time without requiring a dedicated time period.

The researchers have conducted an initial curriculum mapping exercise to demonstrate the alignment of AMR and AMS content with the competencies established by the HPCSA and WHO. By analysing the results of this mapping exercise, the researchers have initiated a dialogue within the Faculty of Dentistry at UWC, by engaging with the Dentistry Academic Planning Committee to facilitate a comprehensive review of AMR and AMS content in the undergraduate curriculum. This effort aims to ensure that clinical best practices are effectively translated into the training of undergraduate dental students, particularly regarding prudent antibiotic prescribing, thereby enhancing AMR awareness and AMS for the benefit of the patient community.

Limitations

The curriculum mapping exercise relied on researchers’ interpretation of course descriptions from the faculty yearbook, which contained varying levels of detail and specificity. Some course descriptions provided minimal information about specific learning methods, assessment strategies, or depth of coverage related to AMR and AMS competencies. This limitation in source material detail required researchers to make inferences about competency alignment based on limited documented information. Although investigator triangulation enhanced the credibility and trustworthiness of the mapping results, by comparing interpretations and resolving discrepancies, the subjective nature of suggesting the competency presence from course descriptors may have an influence on the exactness of the mapping.

As the curriculum mapping exercise was conducted at only one dental school, i.e. UWC, the sample size is limited. Dental schools in South Africa are regulated by the HPCSA, and competencies would be more likely to be aligned with the HPCSA requirements. WHO competencies are specifically designed to address AMR, and therefore, it may differ from the core competencies established by the HPCSA. The different dental schools in South Africa may have different methods to integrate AMR and AMS content, which may be based on their specific institutional context and resources. This study has demonstrated that while AMR and AMS content is not adequately covered in the undergraduate course content through the undergraduate curriculum, the researchers have not mapped theory vs. clinical practice. This exercise would be valuable to determine if the theoretical content is translated to clinical practice.

Recommendations

Conducting a comprehensive curriculum review of the core competencies related to AMR and AMS within the Faculty of Dentistry at UWC; with a focus on mapping theoretical content against practical learning experiences within the context of the core competencies. This will identify gaps in course alignment, strengths and weaknesses of the current curriculum, positive reporting of required competencies, identification of educational needs, evidence-based best practices, integration potential within the program, and stakeholder input within the Faculty of Dentistry at UWC. To manage the limitation of relying only on course descriptors, a comprehensive curriculum review would use multiple data collection methods, such as direct class/clinical observations, faculty interviews/surveys, review of teaching content and review of enacted curriculum. By introducing AMR and AMS content and competencies in earlier years of the undergraduate program (second and third year), it may provide constant foundational knowledge. While the third year is suitable for introducing AMR and AMS content (as is currently the case), enhancing the vertical integration of content during the clinical years (fourth and final years) may be considered in order to ensure a more comprehensive and balanced exposure to the subject matter.

It may also be valuable to consider aligning the HPCSA core competencies with those outlined in the WHO curriculum guide for healthcare workers on antimicrobial resistance. This alignment would specifically address areas such as antibiotic prescribing, AMR awareness, and AMS measures.

Conclusion

This study identifies the strengths and omissions in how undergraduate dental programs integrate AMR and AMS core competencies. The curriculum aligns partly with HPCSA and WHO core competencies but concentrates them mostly in the third and fourth years. Students encounter these competencies in later years, yet the curriculum does not consistently translate them into clinical practice. Limited early exposure demonstrates minimal horizontal and vertical integration and emphasizes the need for a more comprehensive approach across the program. By introducing AMR and AMS content earlier through vertical integration, students can progressively build knowledge from their initial years into clinical practice. Curriculum mapping highlights both strengths and gaps, enabling academic staff to strengthen the undergraduate dental curriculum to address AMR and AMS more effectively. As AMR continues to threaten global health, dental education must equip future practitioners with the knowledge and skills to act as stewards of antimicrobial use. Integrating AMR awareness and AMS into dental curricula offers more than academic insight; it provides a strategy to manage rising AMR rates and upholds our responsibility to patients, the profession, and the global community.
