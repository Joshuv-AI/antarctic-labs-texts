Genome-wide identification of BcMYB in Bupleurum Chinense DC. and its regulatory roles in saikosaponins synthesis under temperature stress
Authors: Xupeng Gu, Linlin Yang, Jie Wan, Ning Dong, Leixia Chu, Di Zhang, Feiyu Zhang, Gaoyang Zhang, Hanwei Li, Wenze Liu, Tengda Song, Weisheng Feng, Chengming Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMYB transcription factors play critical roles in secondary metabolite biosynthesis in medicinal plants and are crucial for abiotic stress responses. However, no studies have identified BcMYB genes in Bupleurum chinense DC. or analyzed their expression profiling under temperature stress. The role of BcMYB in ​B. chinense’s response to temperature stress and its regulation of saikosaponins synthesis remains unclear.ResultsThis study performed a genome-wide identification of the BcMYB gene family in B. chinense using its genomic data, and analyzed the changes in saikosaponins content, physiological indicators, and BcMYB expression patterns under temperature stress, along with their correlations with saikosaponin biosynthesis. A total of 85 BcMYBs genes were identified, classified into four subfamilies, with amino acid lengths ranging from 306 to 482 aa and relative molecular masses between 23,710.84 and 53,062.36. Except for BcMYB10 and BcMYB50, all other BcMYB genes were localized in the nucleus. Most BcMYB promoter regions contained low-temperature and drought-responsive elements. BcMYB genes underwent strong purifying selection after duplication. Short-term temperature stress (6 days) significantly stimulated saikosaponins synthesis: Ss-a content increased by 94% under low temperature (15 °C) and 34% under high temperature (35 °C), accompanied by elevated antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD: 58%, POD: 41%) and MDA accumulation. Temperature stress predominantly upregulated upstream genes in the saikosaponins biosynthesis pathway (1-Deoxy-D-xylulose-5-phosphate synthase (DXS), 1-Deoxy-D-xylulose-5-phosphate reductoisomerase (DXR), Isopentenyl pyrophosphate isomerase (IDI), 3-Hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGR)), while mid- and downstream genes (Farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase (FPS), β-Amyrin synthase (β-AS), Cytochrome P450 monooxygenase (P450-1 P450-8)) were more responsive to high temperature. BcMYB28, 41, 44, 46, 47, 68, and 79 were significantly upregulated under both low and high temperatures (p < 0.05), whereas BcMYB25 and 56 were specifically induced by low temperature. BcMYB25, 28, 41, 44, 46, 47, 56, 68, and 79 demonstrated strong connectivity and correlations with saikosaponins biosynthesis genes and saikosaponins content.ConclusionsThe physiological responses of B. chinense to temperature stress and its secondary metabolic regulatory network exhibit dynamic synergistic characteristics. BcMYB, a critical transcription factor in B. chinense, plays a pivotal role in adapting to temperature fluctuations under temperature stress. This transcription factor may participate in establishing a “temperature signal-regulatory gene-metabolite” cascade regulatory network, thereby modulating the synthesis of saikosaponins in B. chinense. In agricultural practices, harvesting B. chinense after gradual cooling and exposure to low-temperature conditions for 6–12 days significantly enhances medicinal material quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07634-z.

Introduction

Bupleurum chinense DC., a perennial medicinal plant of the genus Bupleurum in the family Apiaceae, produces bioactive saponins that serve as the key therapeutic basis for its pharmacological effects, including anti-inflammatory, antiviral, antipyretic, and analgesic properties [1–3]. Among the various saponin components found in B. chinense, Ss-a, Ss-d, and Ss-c are present in the highest concentrations and are the most significant. Other saponins are predominantly acetylated derivatives of Ss-a and Ss-d. Furthermore, the Chinese Pharmacopoeia uses the levels of Ss-a and Ss-d as the current standard for evaluating the quality of B. chinense. Currently, the production areas of B. chinense are primarily distributed in Henan, Shanxi, Shandong, Jilin, Inner Mongolia, and other provinces. However, studies have shown significant differences in quality among B. chinense from different regions [4]. The quality attributes and therapeutic efficacy of genuine regional medicinal materials, which distinguish them from their counterparts in other producing regions, are jointly determined by genetic factors and environmental modifications [5, 6]. From the perspective of ​B. chinense's ecological regions, moisture and temperature are likely the dominant factors influencing its quality formation [7]. The research group has conducted preliminary research on moisture. Yang et al. elucidated the transient stress-induced effects of drought stress on saikosaponins biosynthesis through transcriptional regulation in B. chinense, demonstrating that controlled drought exposure can acutely upregulate key genes in terpenoid backbone pathways, thereby enhancing saikosaponins production [2, 8, 9]. However, as another critical ecological factor, the regulatory mechanism underlying the synthesis of saikosaponins controlled by temperature remains unclear.

It is noteworthy that the underlying mechanisms by which environmental stress induces the accumulation of secondary metabolites are intricately linked to the sophisticated molecular regulatory networks within plants [10]. Through long-term natural evolution, terrestrial plants have developed a sophisticated protective mechanism to cope with abiotic stress [11]. When exposed to such stress, plants initially perceive and recognize the signals through multiple sensory pathways, subsequently triggering signal transduction that activates various physiological and metabolic processes to enhance their survival capabilities [12]. Recent studies have revealed that transcription factors, as central regulatory hubs responding to environmental signals, play a pivotal role in this process [13, 14]. When plants perceive stress signals such as drought or low temperature, signaling pathways involving MAPK and calcium ions are rapidly activated. These pathways regulate transcription factor activity through post-translational modifications like phosphorylation [10, 15]. The activated transcription factors (e.g., MYB, bHLH, and WRKY family members) specifically bind to promoter regions of downstream target genes, orchestrating the coordinated expression of key enzymatic genes in plant secondary metabolic pathways.

The MYB transcription factors, characterized by a conserved MYB domain, represent the largest gene family among transcription factors in higher plants [16]. These proteins typically contain 1 to 4 incomplete tandem repeat domains (designated as R1, R2, R3, and R4) that define their structural signature. Based on the number of MYB domain repeats and their spatial organization, this family is systematically classified into four subtypes: 4R-MYB containing four repeat units, R1R2R3-MYB (3R-MYB) with triple-repeat architecture, MYB-related proteins (1R-MYB) possessing a single repeat motif, and the ubiquitous R2R3-MYB (2R-MYB) featuring the characteristic double-repeat structure [17]. The MYB transcription factors typically contain a C-terminal transcriptional activation domain composed of multiple acidic amino acid residues, which mediates diverse protein-protein interactions and functional regulations. Duan et al. identified the cotton MYB transcription factor GhMYB4, demonstrating that it negatively regulates cotton fiber cell elongation by suppressing the expression of lipid transfer protein gene GhLTP4 and sucrose transporter gene GhSWEET12 [18]. Tang et al. revealed that three R2R3-MYB transcription factors (PlMYB43/83/103) in Paeonia lactiflora enhance secondary cell wall thickening and stem mechanical strength through transcriptional activation of lignin biosynthesis genes (PlCOMT2 and PlLAC4) [19]. Su et al. further discovered that the MsMYB206-MsMYB450-MsHY5 protein complex regulates salt stress tolerance in alfalfa (Medicago sativa) by modulating flavonoid biosynthesis during circadian cycles [20].

The MYB gene family plays an important role in plant growth and development, especially in abiotic stress environments, but no studies have been conducted to identify and analyze the BcMYB genes in B. chinense and their expression pattern analysis under different temperature stresses. Based on this, the present study was conducted to identify the whole gene family of BcMYB transcription factors in BcMYB, to investigate the expression pattern of BcMYB transcription factors under different temperatures by artificially regulating the key ecological factor of temperature in the growth environment of BcMYB, and to screen for potential BcMYB transcription factors affecting the synthesis of saikosaponins in BcMYB under temperature stress, in order to provide an opportunity for the environmental quality control study of BcMYB. This will provide a theoretical basis for the study of environmental quality control of B. chinense, and at the same time provide a theoretical basis for the stabilization and controllability of the quality of B. chinense.

Results

Characterization of BcMYB family members and their physicochemical properties in B. chinense

A total of 85 BcMYB genes, named BcMYB01-BcMYB 85, were identified from the whole genome of BcMYB and Arabidopsis thaliana by bi-directional BLAST. The physicochemical properties of BcMYB proteins were predicted by using TBtools, and the results are shown in Table S1. The BcMYB66 protein encoded the most amino acids with 482 and the BcMYB22 and BcMYB57 protein encoded the least amino acids with 206, and the relative molecular mass distribution region was 23710.84–53062.36.84.36. Among the 85 BcMYB proteins, 52 BcMYB proteins isoelectric points were less than 7.00, and 33 BcMYB proteins isoelectric points were more than 7.00, which indicated that most of the BcMYB proteins were acidic. Among all the BcMYB proteins, only the hydrophilic coefficient of the BcMYB52 protein was greater than 0. All the rest of the BcMYB proteins hydrophilic coefficients were less than 0, indicating that almost all of the proteins in this family are hydrophilic proteins. Meanwhile, the instability coefficients of all BcMYB proteins were greater than 40, indicating that most of the proteins in this family are unstable proteins.

A. thaliana is a model plant in plant biology research, possessing the most complete and annotated genome and functional genome information. Its entire transcription factor family, including the MYB family, has been extensively studied. Therefore, to clarify the potential functions of BcMYB proteins, we statistically analyzed the biological roles of its homologous A. thaliana AtMYB proteins, as detailed in Table S2. Analysis of the AtMYB proteins with defined functions among the 85 (A) thaliana homologs revealed that they possess diverse functions. These include regulating plant growth and development (such as stomatal formation, root endodermal differentiation, Casparian strip formation, floral organ development, seed development, and secondary cell wall biosynthesis), mediating responses to biotic and abiotic stresses (such as UV-B protection, drought, salinity, cold, pathogens, and ABA signaling), and controlling various secondary metabolic pathways (such as lignin, flavonol, volatile benzenoid compounds, and anthocyanin synthesis). Notably, homologs corresponding to several BcMYB protein members are implicated in stress responses (including UV-B tolerance, drought response, salt stress, cold stress, and ABA signal transduction), specifically AtMYB12, AtMYB15, AtMYB17, AtMYB85, AtMYB86, AtMYB111,and AtMYB113.Additionally, homologs of several other BcMYB proteins members are directly involved in secondary metabolism (such as flavonoid, lignin, and benzenoid compound synthesis), including AtMYB9, AtMYB14, AtMYB15, AtMYB52, AtMYB73, AtMYB77, and AtMYB93. This evidence further underscores the potential importance of MYB transcription factors in (B) chinense’s environmental adaptation and metabolic regulation.

Phylogenetic analysis of the BcMYB gene family in B. chinense

TBtools was used to build a tree between the screened BcMYB transcription factors and 168 (A) thaliana AtMYB downloaded from plantTFDB, and the size of the circles in the evolutionary tree represents the support, as shown in Fig. 1. The BcMYB were divided into four subfamilies, 1R-BcMYB, 2R-BcMYB, 3R-BcMYB, and 4R-BcMYB, of which the subfamily R2R3-BcMYB/2R-BcMYB had the most pairs, with a total of 56, accounting for 66% of all BcMYBs; there were 21 1R-BcMYBs, accounting for 25%, which is the second largest subfamily in the MYB family of (B) chinense; 3R-BcMYB and 4R-BcMYB had 5 and 3, respectively, and together occupied 9% of all BcMYBs. Studies have shown that the R2R3-BcMYB/2R-BcMYB subfamily is mainly responsible for regulating plant-specific processes, including plant growth and development, and biotic and abiotic stress responses [16, 21]. Analysis of motif sequence, conserved sequence and gene structure of BcMYB gene in B. chinense

To further analyze the structural and evolutionary relationships of BcMYB transcription factors in B. chinense, we conducted phylogenetic tree construction, conserved motif analysis, conserved domain identification, and gene structure analysis for all BcMYB genes. The results are shown in Fig. 2. In Figs. 2-a and -a and 10 characteristic motifs were identified across all BcMYB proteins. These proteins contain 3–11 motifs, with Motifs 3, 1, 2, and 7 exhibiting high-frequency distribution. Conserved motifs were observed at the N-terminal (Motifs 4 and 5) and C-terminal (Motifs 2, 6, 8, 5, and 10) regions of B. chinense BcMYB proteins. In Fig. 2-b, all BcMYB proteins contain 5 conserved domains. BcMYB10 and BcMYB50 exclusively possess the SANT domain, while BcMYB84 uniquely retains the REB1 domain. BcMYB52 and BcMYB16 are characterized by the presence of both PLN03091 superfamily and PRK12323 superfamily domains. 14 BcMYB proteins (BcMYB03, 07, 17, 24, 31, 33, 36, 40, 53, 58, 62, 67, 70, 74) specifically contain the PLN03212 superfamily domain. In Fig. 2-c, the BcMYB genes exhibit high conservation at their loci. All BcMYB genes contain 2–7 untranslated regions (UTRs), with intron numbers ranging from 0 to 11. Ten BcMYB genes (BcMYB10, 26, 28, 43, 46, 50, 60, 65, 78, 80) lack introns entirely. Exon counts vary between 1 and 11, with BcMYB84 possessing the highest number of exons.

Prediction of transient regulatory elements in B. chinense BcMYB genes

We extracted the 2000 bp promoter regions upstream of all BcMYB genes in B. chinense and predicted their cis-acting elements (Fig. 3-a). A total of 14 types of cis-acting elements were identified. All BcMYB promoters contain light-responsive elements, with ​BcMYB16 and ​BcMYB52 exclusively possessing this element. Most BcMYB promoters harbor multiple hormone-responsive elements, including abscisic acid (ABA), methyl jasmonate (MeJA), auxin (IAA), salicylic acid (SA), and gibberellin (GA). Additionally, several BcMYB genes contain stress-related elements such as low-temperature response (LTR), drought-inducibility (MBS), and defense/stress response motifs. Notably, a subset of BcMYB promoters (e.g., ​BcMYB07, 08, 13, 15, 20, 25, 28, 41, 44, 46, 47, 56, 59, 68, 69, 75, 78, 79) specifically carry low-temperature response elements (LTRs), identifying them as candidate genes for subsequent functional validation of cold stress adaptation. The presence of MYB-binding sites associated with flavonoid biosynthesis in some BcMYB promoters further underscores their regulatory roles in stress responses.

Chromosomal localization and synteny analysis of BcMYB genes in B. chinense

Chromosomal localization analysis of the B. chinense BcMYB family members was performed based on the genome data (Fig. 3-b). The 85 BcMYB genes are unevenly distributed across 10 chromosomes, predominantly clustered near chromosomal termini. ChrA02 harbors the highest number of BcMYB genes (16), while ChrB01 contains the fewest (2), with other chromosomes bearing 4–13 genes. Notably, no BcMYB gene clusters were observed.

Intraspecific synteny analysis revealed 177 collinear gene pairs involving BcMYB genes (Fig. 3-c), including 61 BcMYB-BcMYB paralogous pairs (Table S3). The Ka/Ks ratio (nonsynonymous to synonymous substitution rate), a key indicator of evolutionary selection pressure [22], showed values < 1 for all 61 paralogous pairs, indicating strong purifying selection following gene duplication.

Interspecific synteny analysis between B. chinense and A. thaliana identified 106 homologous gene pairs (Fig. 3-d). Each B. chinense chromosome contains at least one BcMYB gene syntenic with 2–3 (A) thaliana genes. This pattern suggests divergent evolutionary strategies: as a medicinal plant, B. chinense likely relies on conserved core MYB genes for stress resilience, whereas the model plant A. thaliana achieves regulatory diversity through extensive gene duplication.

Variations in saikosaponins content and physiological parameters in B. chinense under different temperature stresses

The HPLC chromatograms for all samples are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1. The accumulation of saikosaponins in B. chinense exhibited significant dynamic changes under different temperature regimes, as shown in Fig. 4. Among the five saikosaponins, Ss-a and Ss-d contents substantially exceed those of the other three, therefore this study focuses on analyzing changes in Ss-s, Ss-a, and Ss-d across treatment groups. Short-term stress (day 6) significantly promoted saponin synthesis. In the low-temperature group (15 °C) and high-temperature group (35 °C), Ss-s contents reached 2.58% and 2.02% respectively, representing increases of 69% and 32% compared to the control group (25 °C) at 1.53%. Ss-a content increased by 94% and 34%, while Ss-d content increased by 100% and 33%, respectively. However, with prolonged treatment duration, the high-temperature group showed a precipitous decline in saponin content by day 12, with all monomers dropping to their lowest levels during this period and significantly below control levels (p < 0.05). Although the low-temperature group maintained higher levels than the control at day 12, it reached a content trough by day 18 (Ss-s, Ss-a, and Ss-d decreased by 69%, 78%, and 74% from peak values, respectively). By the late stress period (day 24), the low-temperature group showed a recovery trend in saponins (Ss-s, Ss-a, and Ss-d rebounded by 21%, 46%, and 31% from trough levels), while the high-temperature group remained persistently low.

Temperature stress simultaneously triggered intense physiological responses, as shown in Fig. 5. The membrane lipid peroxidation marker malondialdehyde (MDA) surged dramatically in the 35 °C high-temperature group, showing significant increases of 0.78-fold, 4.53-fold, 5.07-fold, and 1.16-fold compared to the control group on days 6, 12, 18, and 24 respectively. This indicates that sustained high temperatures caused membrane lipid peroxidation damage, severely compromising cell membrane integrity. Although MDA accumulation was slower initially in the 15 °C low-temperature group (with increases 20% and 62% lower than the high-temperature group on Days 6 and 12), its content surged sharply during days 12–24 (with a 250% higher increase than the high-temperature group by day 24). The osmotic adjustment substance soluble protein (SP) peaked in the high-temperature group on day 18 (4.17 g/L), significantly exceeding the control group (1.60 g/L, P < 0.05), demonstrating activation of osmotic protection mechanisms through dynamic SP regulation to maintain cellular osmotic balance under high-temperature conditions.

The enzymatic defense system is one of the core systems in plants under stress conditions, responsible for scavenging excess free radicals to effectively enhance stress resistance [23]. The high-temperature group displayed phased response characteristics in its antioxidant enzyme system. During the initial stress period (day 6), superoxide dismutase (SOD), Peroxidase (POD), and Catalase (CAT) activities increased by 4.04-fold, 2.37-fold, and 1.21-fold respectively compared to the control group, effectively eliminating excess reactive oxygen species (ROS). During the mid and late stress periods (days 12–24), enzyme activities generally decreased and fell below control levels, potentially related to enzyme protein inactivation caused by ROS accumulation. The low-temperature group exhibited differentiated dynamic responses from the high-temperature group: SOD activity remained consistently higher than the control throughout the entire stress cycle (17%−67% increase), indicating stable maintenance of superoxide radical scavenging capacity; POD activity was significantly higher than the control during days 6–18 (P < 0.05), but by day 24, it became significantly lower than the control level; CAT activity showed a continuous declining trend, decreasing from 23% above the control on day 6 to significantly below the control by day 24, suggesting that hydrogen peroxide scavenging efficiency declined with prolonged stress duration. Glutathione reductase (GR) activity demonstrated temperature-dependent response characteristics. The high-temperature group (35 °C) exhibited triphasic changes: during the early stress period (days 6–12), activity was significantly lower than the control, with the minimum value being only 29% of the control group (P < 0.01); it sharply increased to its peak on day 18, 1.14-fold higher than the control; but by day 24, it decreased again to significantly below the control level. In the low-temperature group, GR activity displayed a “V-shaped” trend of initial decline followed by an increase: on day 6, it was significantly 34% higher than the control (P < 0.05); subsequently during days 12–18, it remained continuously below the control, with a maximum decrease of 42% (P < 0.05); by day 24, it rebounded strongly to 2.45 times the control level. This temperature-dependent regulatory response pattern also illustrates the plant’s survival strategy of coordinating “defense survival-secondary metabolic resource allocation” through GR.

In summary, there is a clear temporal correlation between the changes in saikosaponins accumulation and the physiological responses in B. chinense. The short-term stress-promoting phase (day 6) coincided with a significant enhancement of antioxidant enzyme activities. In the high-temperature group, the activities of SOD, POD, and CAT increased to 4.04-fold, 2.37-fold, and 1.21-fold those of the control group, respectively. This indicates that the timely improvement in ROS scavenging capacity helps maintain the metabolic environment required for saponin synthesis. As the stress duration extended, the decline in saponin synthesis intensity closely corresponded with membrane system damage and weakened defense functions. The sharp decrease in saponins in the high-temperature group on day 12 synchronized with a surge in MDA content (reaching 5.07-fold the control by day 18) and a significant reduction in SOD/POD/CAT activities. Similarly, the saponin trough in the low-temperature group on day 18 was accompanied by continuously rising MDA (content 2.16-fold higher than the control by day 24) and a systemic weakening of CAT activity. This confirms that sustained membrane lipid peroxidation damage and the decline in antioxidant enzyme function collectively limit the biosynthetic capacity for saikosaponins. Notably, a recovery trend was observed later. In the low-temperature group on day 24, GR activity significantly rebounded to 2.45-fold the control level, while Ss-a and Ss-d contents increased by 46% and 31% compared to their trough values. Concurrently, the high-temperature group on day 18 exhibited a transient peak in GR activity (1.14-fold higher than the control) alongside a recovery in saponin content. This suggests that GR-driven restoration of redox homeostasis has a potential coordinating role in the allocation of resources for secondary metabolism. Essentially, this role represents a coordinated balance within the plant between allocating defense resources (ROS scavenging) and investing in secondary metabolism (saikosaponins synthesis).

Analysis of expression patterns of BcMYB family genes and saikosaponins biosynthesis pathway-related genes in B. chinense under different temperature stresses

Since the saikosaponins content in B. chinense exhibited the most significant differences under different temperature treatments on day 6, we measured the gene expression levels in the day 6 B. chinense samples subjected to these temperature treatments.

The synthesis pathway of saikosaponin in B. chinense is shown in Fig. 6a. The relative expression levels of genes in the saikosaponin biosynthesis and metabolic pathways in B. chinense are shown in Fig. 6-b. Under both high and low temperature treatments, the upstream MEP pathway genes (DXS, DXR, IDI) and MVA pathway gene (HMGR) in the saikosaponins biosynthesis pathway showed significant increases compared to the control group (p < 0.05). For the midstream biosynthesis genes (FPS, β-AS), their expression levels were significantly upregulated under high-temperature conditions (p < 0.05). In the downstream biosynthesis pathway, P450−3 exhibited significant upregulation under both low- and high-temperature stresses (p < 0.05), while P450-1 and UGT-8 were significantly induced under high-temperature treatment (p < 0.05). In summary, low- and high-temperature stresses predominantly enhanced the expression of upstream genes in the saikosaponins biosynthesis pathway, whereas mid- and downstream genes were more responsive to high-temperature stress. These findings further indicate that temperature stress likely stimulates saikosaponins synthesis by upregulating the expression of key genes in its biosynthesis pathway.

Correlation network analysis between BcMYB genes and saikosaponins under different temperature stresses

We constructed a correlation network diagram of genes in the saikosaponins biosynthesis pathway, BcMYB genes, and saikosaponins content in B. chinense, as shown in Fig. 7. From this network, we observed that the core hub nodes IDI, DXR, P450-12, and MYB25 form the structural backbone of the network. The BcMYB25, 28, 41, 44, 46, 47, 56, 68, and 79 genes exhibited high connectivity with both saikosaponins biosynthesis pathway genes and saikosaponins content. Notably, BcMYB79 showed strong positive correlations with saikosaponins biosynthesis genes (DXS, DXR, FPS, β-AS, UGT-8), while BcMYB25, 28, 41, 44, 46, 47, 56, and 68 were highly positively correlated with saikosaponins content. These results suggest that the synthesis and metabolism of saikosaponins in B. chinense under temperature stress are likely regulated by BcMYB transcription factors. Furthermore, they indicate that BcMYB transcription factors may dynamically regulate gene expression in the saikosaponins biosynthesis pathway by forming a “temperature signal-regulatory gene-metabolite” cascade network, thereby enhancing the plant’s physiological adaptability to temperature stress.

Discussion

Relationship between MYB gene family structural features and evolutionary selection

This study identified 85 BcMYB genes in the B. chinense genome, with subfamily distribution showing a dominance of R2R3-MYB (66%), a proportion similar to that in the model plant (A) thaliana (approximately 74%). This suggests functional conservation of the R2R3-MYB subfamily during plant evolution. Notably, the proportion of 1R-MYB subfamily genes in (B) chinense (25%) was significantly higher than in (A) thaliana (approximately 10%), implying that gene duplication events in (B) chinense may have enhanced regulatory network complexity to adapt to dynamic environments. Selection pressure analysis revealed that all BcMYB genes, except BcMYB30, exhibited Ka/Ks ratios < 1, indicating strong purifying selection during evolution. This further reflects the evolutionary conservation of the BcMYB transcription factor family and underscores its core regulatory role in stress resistance in B. chinense.

Cao et al. found that TcMYB29a acts as an activator to enhance paclitaxel accumulation in Taxus cells via the ABA signaling pathway [24]. Li et al. reported that the (A) thaliana ABA receptor RCAR1/PYL9 interacts with the R2R3- MYB transcription factor AtMYB44 [25]. In our transient response element analysis of BcMYB promoters, we observed abundant hormone-responsive elements (e.g., ABA, JA), suggesting that (B) chinense may integrate temperature-sensing signals with hormone pathways to activate MYB-mediated defense responses.

Temperature-induced physiological responses and secondary metabolic regulatory network

The physiological responses and secondary metabolic regulatory network of B. chinense under temperature stress exhibit dynamic coordination. This study found that short-term temperature stress (6 days) significantly induced saikosaponins biosynthesis: low temperature (15 °C) and high temperature (35 °C) treatments increased Ss-a content by 94% and 34%, respectively, accompanied by surges in SOD and POD antioxidant enzyme activities and elevated MDA levels. During this phase, B. chinense activated “defense mobilization” to scavenge excess ROS and mitigate oxidative damage. However, prolonged stress led to divergent response strategies: in the high-temperature group, saikosaponins content sharply declined by day 12, coinciding with a peak MDA level, suggesting membrane system collapse disrupted secondary metabolism. In contrast, the low-temperature group maintained membrane integrity through sustained upregulation of GR activity, enabling gradual recovery of saikosaponins content after an initial decline. Gene co-expression network analysis further revealed that the R2R3-MYB member BcMYB79, acting as a core hub, showed strong positive correlations with MEP/MVA pathway genes (DXS, DXR, HMGR) and P450 enzymes. The low-temperature-responsive element (LTR) in its promoter region may mediate temperature signal transduction into metabolic pathways. Notably, in the high-temperature group, the expression levels of BcMYB28, BcMYB41, and BcMYB68 were highly synchronized with saikosaponins content, implying that these MYB transcription factors may dynamically regulate terpenoid biosynthesis while potentially influencing redox homeostasis of the membrane system.

Regulation of saikosaponins biosynthesis under temperature stress and selection of optimal harvest window in B. chinense

Although short-term artificial climate-simulated stress (6–24 days) significantly induces saikosaponins synthesis, the “pulse-like” temperature fluctuations in controlled environments fundamentally differ from the “gradual” seasonal changes in natural habitats. This study observed that saikosaponins content in the high-temperature group (35 °C) sharply declined by 56% at day 12, coinciding with a peak in MDA levels. Such oxidative damage caused by acute stress contrasts starkly with the sustained saikosaponins accumulation observed in natural habitats, where gradual cooling or heating induces progressive responses. Notably, the “window period” of low-temperature treatment (6–12 days) increased saikosaponins content by 68%–94% while maintaining membrane system stability (MDA increase < 15%), highlighting the application value of a “moderate stress window period” in artificial cultivation. For instance, harvesting B. chinense after 6–12 days of low-temperature exposure following gradual cooling could avoid secondary metabolic imbalances caused by prolonged stress while enhancing medicinal quality. This strategy offers a novel approach for improving the quality of artificially cultivated medicinal herbs.

Research limitations and future directions

This study elucidates the expression patterns of the BcMYB gene family under temperature stress and their correlation with saikosaponins biosynthesis, yet certain limitations remain. First, the co-expression network analysis relied solely on q-PCR results, lacking validation of protein interactions or direct regulatory relationships between key transcription factors (e.g., BcMYB79) and their target genes (e.g., DXS, HMGR). Furthermore, from a holistic perspective on herbal quality formation, the ecological environment exerts complex and comprehensive influences on the quality of medicinal materials. Changes in environmental factors trigger responsive adjustments across growth, physiological, and metabolic processes in medicinal plants. However, the growth environment of medicinal herbs constitutes a complex ecosystem, and the impact of a single environmental factor cannot fully reflect the holistic nature of ecological influences on herbal quality, which contradicts the integrated view of herbal quality formation. In future studies, we aim to investigate the effects of multiple environmental factors on saikosaponins synthesis in B. chinense and delve deeper into the functional interactions between BcMYB transcription factors and genes in the saikosaponins biosynthesis pathway.

Conclusions

The physiological responses of B. chinense to temperature stress and its secondary metabolic regulatory network exhibit dynamic synergistic characteristics. BcMYB, a critical transcription factor in B. chinense, plays a pivotal role in adapting to temperature fluctuations under temperature stress. This transcription factor may participate in establishing a “temperature signal-regulatory gene-metabolite” cascade regulatory network, thereby modulating the synthesis of saikosaponins in B. chinense. In agricultural practices, harvesting B. chinense after gradual cooling and exposure to low-temperature conditions for 6–12 days significantly enhances medicinal material quality. This study provides a theoretical foundation for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying quality formation in B. chinense under environmental control, while offering practical guidance for the rational cultivation and harvesting of medicinal plants.

Materials and methods

Experimental instruments

The following instruments and equipment were utilized in this study: a Waters e2695 Separation Module coupled with a 2489 UV/Visible Detector HPLC system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA); an Agilent ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 column (5 μm, 4.6 × 250 mm); an MS105DU analytical balance (1/10,000 g readability; Mettler-Toledo, Zurich, Switzerland); a KQ-500DV numerically-controlled ultrasonic cleaner (Kunshan Ultrasound Instrument Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China); an A590 dual-beam UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Aoyi Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China); a Varioskan LUX multimode microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA); a Centrifuge 5810 R refrigerated high-speed centrifuge (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany); and two artificial climate incubators (models FH-400, Haibote Co., Ltd., Taiwan, China; and HQH-H300, Shanghai Yuejin Medical Device Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Experimental materials

The B. chinense medicinal material used in this study was cultivated in the medicinal garden of Henan University of Chinese Medicine. It was botanically identified as Bupleurum chinense DC. by Professor Chengming Dong of the same university.

The reference compounds Ss-a (Batch No.: B20146), Ss-d (Batch No.: B20150), Ss-c (Batch No.: B20149), Ss-e (Batch No.: B24458), and Ss-f (Batch No.: B20151), each with a purity of ≥ 98%, were procured from Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Commercial assay kits for determining the content of malondialdehyde and soluble protein, as well as the activity of superoxide dismutase, peroxidase, catalase, and glutathione reductase, were obtained from the Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China).

Experimental design

Artificial climate chambers, arranged in a randomized block design, were utilized to simulate three distinct temperature conditions: a high-temperature group (35 °C), a low-temperature group (15 °C), and a control group (25 °C). The chambers were maintained under white fluorescent lighting (10,000 lx) with a 12-h/12-h light/dark cycle and 60% relative humidity. The first sampling was conducted on the 6th day of the experiment, and samples were taken every six days from 8:00 am to 9:00 am every day thereafter. During each sampling session, eight potted plants were randomly selected from each treatment group as biological replicates. Both aboveground leaf tissues and belowground root systems were uniformly collected from every plant for further analysis. The harvested leaves were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C in an ultra-low temperature freezer for subsequent physiological assays. The roots were carefully washed, segmented, and divided into two aliquots: one was oven-dried and ground into a fine powder for secondary metabolite quantification, while the other was preserved at − 80 °C for gene expression analysis via quantitative real-time PCR (q-PCR).

Whole Gene family identification of MYB transcription factors in B. chinense

The whole genome data of B. chinense comes from the self-test database of our research group. Tbtools software meme suite wrapper tool was used to predict the conserved motifs of BcMYB proteins in B. chinense. The BcMYB proteins domain was predicted using the CDD online website in NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd).

Download A. thaliana whole genome data from the NCBI website, use the One Step MCScanX Super Fast tool in TBtools to compare the whole genomes of B. chinense and A. thaliana to obtain interspecific collinearity relationships, and visualize them.

Method for detecting the content of saikosaponins in B. chinense

Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Symmetry C18 column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase was composed of acetonitrile (A) and water (B), eluted in a gradient mode as follows: 0–5 min, 30% A; 5–10 min, 30–35% A; 10–20 min, 35–50% A; 20–25 min, 50–52% A; 25–40 min, 52–30% A. The detection wavelength was set at 200 nm, the column temperature was maintained at 21 °C, and the injection volume was 20 µL.

Preparation of reference standard solutions

Accurately weighed individual standards of Ss-c, Ss-f, Ss-a, Ss-e, and Ss-d were separately dissolved in methanol and transferred to 10 mL volumetric flasks. The solutions were then diluted to volume with methanol to obtain mixed standard solutions at final concentrations of 3.98 mg/mL, 4.01 mg/mL, 4.00 mg/mL, 4.00 mg/mL, and 4.01 mg/mL, respectively.

Extraction of the test sample: A 0.4 g portion of powdered B. chinense was accurately weighed into a conical flask. Then, 10 mL of 10% (v/v) ammonia-methanol solution was added. After recording the total weight, the mixture was sonicated for 40 min and subsequently filtered. This extraction process was repeated three times. The combined filtrate was evaporated in a water bath at 50 °C. The residue was dissolved in methanol and transferred to a 10 mL volumetric flask, which was then brought to volume with methanol to yield the final test sample solution.

q-PCR method

Total RNA was extracted from B. chinense materials using the 411 C-1 Polyphenolic Polysaccharide Plant Total RNA Extraction Kit (Beijing Zhuangmeng International Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription using the HiScript Ⅲ RT SuperMix for q-PCR (gDNA wiper) kit (Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Gene-specific primers for BcMYB and the reference gene EF-1α were designed using SnapGene software. All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The primer sequences for genes involved in the saikosaponins biosynthesis pathway were referenced from the study by Gu et al. [7], with detailed information provided in Table S4. The q-PCR reaction was performed in a 20µL mixture containing the following components: 7µL of nuclease-free water (ddH₂O), 10µL of PowerTrack™ SYBR Green Master Mix (Apus Applied Biosystems Trading Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), 1µL of forward primer, 1µL of reverse primer, and 1µL of cDNA template. After gentle mixing, the mixture was aliquoted into PCR tubes or plate wells. The thermal cycling protocol was set as follows: initial incubation at 50 °C for 2 min; pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min; followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min. Melting curve analysis was immediately conducted after amplification under the following conditions: 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, and a gradual increase to 95 °C with continuous fluorescence acquisition to verify amplicon specificity. The EF-1α gene was used as the internal reference. Three biological replicates were analyzed for each sample, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

SPSS software (version 21.0; SPSS Inc., USA) was used to process and statistically analyse the experimental data. Principal component analysis (PCA) of HPLC data was performed using SPSS software. GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0; GraphPad Software Inc., USA) was used to graphically present the statistically analysed data.
