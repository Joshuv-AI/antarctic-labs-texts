TREML2 Modulates Microglial Phagocytosis of Myelin Debris via TLR9 in Chronic Cerebral Ischemia
Authors: Zhao-Han Xu, Jing-Wen Qi, Xin-Yu He, Shi-Yao Wang, Shuai-Yu Chen, Yu-Cheng Gu, Yi Xie, Ying-Dong Zhang, Teng Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Demyelination is a significant pathological feature of chronic cerebral ischemia. Recent evidence suggested that microglia played a protective role in mitigating brain ischemic injury via phagocytosis of myelin debris resulting from demyelination. Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells-like 2 (TREML2) is a newly discovered inflammation-associated transmembrane receptor expressed by microglia. To date, whether microglial TREML2 contributes to the phagocytosis of myelin debris in chronic cerebral ischemia has not been fully clarified. In this study, employing a bilateral carotid artery stenosis animal model and a CoCl2-treated cellular model, we demonstrated for the first time that microglial TREML2 expression was upregulated in response to chronic cerebral ischemia. Utilizing Treml2-knockout mice, we provided the first evidence that Treml2 deficiency alleviated demyelination and cognitive deficits induced by chronic cerebral ischemia. Furthermore, this protective effect might be attributed to the microglial M2-type polarization and enhanced phagocytosis of myelin debris, both of which were induced by the Treml2 deficiency. Additionally, we showed that TREML2 regulated microglial phagocytosis of myelin debris via toll-like receptor 9 under ischemic conditions. These findings elucidated the mechanisms by which microglia modulated the phagocytosis of myelin debris in response to brain ischemic injury and suggested that inhibition of TREML2 might represent a novel therapeutic strategy for treating demyelination and cognitive decline induced by chronic cerebral ischemia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10753-025-02397-z.

Introduction

Chronic cerebral ischemia, characterized by a sustained decrease in cerebral blood flow, significantly contributes to neurodegeneration and cognitive decline [1, 2]. One of the pathological hallmarks of this condition is demyelination, which disrupts the normal neuronal function and exacerbates neurological deficits [3]. Emerging evidence indicates that microglia, the innate immune cells of the central nervous system, are activated by ischemic insults [4, 5]. Activation of microglia can be polarized into two opposite types: M1-type polarization and M2-type polarization [6]. Mounting evidence suggests that M2-type polarized microglia actively participate in phagocytosing myelin debris resulting from demyelination [7, 8]. By phagocytosing myelin debris, microglia may help maintain myelin integrity and thus offer potential protection against ischemic injury [9, 10]. However, the mechanisms by which microglia modulate this phagocytic process in the context of chronic cerebral ischemia are not well understood.

Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells-like 2 (TREML2) is a recently identified inflammation-associated transmembrane receptor [11]. Within the brain, TREML2 is expressed by microglia, and our previous studies showed that microglial TREML2 expression was upregulated during normal aging and the progression of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [12, 13]. Interestingly, we recently demonstrated that TREML2 activated the microglial NOD-, LRR- and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and facilitated the release of inflammatory cytokines mediated by microglia [12]. Moreover, Treml2 knockdown promoted microglial M2-type polarization, which was associated with enhanced phagocytic activity and tissue repair [13]. These findings suggested that TREML2 regulates microglial functions, particularly the phagocytic process. In light of these findings, we speculated that microglial TREML2 regulated the phagocytosis of myelin debris in chronic cerebral ischemia.

In this study, using bilateral carotid artery stenosis (BCAS) animal model and CoCl2-treated cellular model, we demonstrated for the first time that microglial TREML2 expression was up-regulated in response to chronic cerebral ischemia. Utilizing Treml2-knockout (KO) mice, we provided the first evidence that Treml2 deficiency ameliorated demyelination and cognitive deficits induced by chronic cerebral ischemia. Moreover, this protective effect might be attributed to the microglial M2-type polarization and enhanced phagocytosis of myelin debris, both of which were induced by Treml2 deficiency. Additionally, we showed that TREML2 regulated microglial phagocytosis of myelin debris via toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) under ischemic conditions. These findings elucidated the mechanisms by which microglia regulated the phagocytosis of myelin debris in response to brain ischemic injury and suggested that inhibition of TREML2 might represent a novel strategy for treating demyelination and cognitive decline induced by chronic cerebral ischemia.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Male wild type (WT) C57BL/6 mice (25–29 g) and Treml2-KO mice (Strain NO. T011871, C57BL/6JGpt-Treml2em2Cd6619/Gpt) were generated by GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Animals were housed under standardized environmental conditions (12-hour light-dark cycle, 45–65% humidity, 23 ± 2 °C ambient temperature) with ad libitum access to standard rodent chow and water. The animal experiments in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing First Hospital (approval No. DWSY-25010642). All procedures involving animal experiments were performed according to the guidelines of ARRIVE. The sample size for our animal experiments was estimated using an online sample size calculator designed for animal studies (http://wnarifin.github.io). This calculator operates based on the Resource Equation Approach [14].

BCAS Model

The 4-week BCAS model was established as described [15]. Briefly, mice were deeply anesthetized with 5% isoflurane and maintained with 2% isoflurane in 33% oxygen (RWD Life Science CO. LTD, China). Both common carotid arteries were carefully dissected and each side was wrapped with a micro-coil with an internal diameter of 0.18 mm (Sawane Spring Co., Ltd, Japan). Sham animals underwent the same procedure without the placement of the micro-coils. All subsequent experiments were conducted starting from the 28th day after the surgery.

Morris Water Maze (MWM) Test

Spatial learning and memory were assessed using the MWM test as previously described [16]. Both WT and Treml2-KO mice were introduced to a circular pool filled with tap water and white paint (Zhejiang Jinhai New Material Co., Ltd, China) to locate a submerged platform positioned 2 cm beneath the water’s surface. During the training phase (days 1–5), mice were subjected to four trials daily to locate the hidden platform. If a mouse did not reach the platform within 90 s, it was gently guided to it and allowed to remain there for 15 s before being returned to its cage. On the sixth day (probe trial), the platform was removed, and each mouse was allowed to swim freely for 90 s. The swim path length and the number of crossings over the original platform location were recorded and analyzed using ANY-maze video tracking software (Stoelting, USA).

Y‑maze Test

The short-term spatial memory was evaluated in a blinded manner through the Y-maze test as previously described [17]. The experimental apparatus was a Y-shaped acrylic maze composed of three horizontal arms (30 cm in length, 8 cm in width, and 15 cm in height; manufactured by Jiangsu SANS Biotechnology Company, Nanjing, China). In brief, individual mice were placed at the distal end of one arm and allowed to explore freely for 8 min. The frequency and sequence of arm entries were quantitatively measured and recorded. A spontaneous alternation event was defined as consecutive entries into three distinct arms within overlapping triplet sequences (e.g., ABC, ACB, BCA, BAC, CBA, CAB). The spontaneous alternation rate (%) was calculated using the following formula: Alternation rate = [Number of spontaneous alternations/(Total arm entries − 2)] × 100.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed as previously described [18]. The 15 μm-thick brain sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (#BL539A, Biosharp, China) for 15 min, blocked with 10% goat serum (#CW0130S, CWBIO, China), 1% bovine serum albumin (#ST023, Beyotime, China), and 0.1% Triton X-100 (#ST1723, Beyotime, China). The sections were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: anti-myelin basic protein (MBP) (#sc-271524, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), anti-myelin-associated glycoprotein (MAG) (#ab277524, Abcam, USA), anti-Iba-1 (#ab178846, Abcam, USA), anti-TREML2 (#sc-390167, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), followed by incubation with appropriate secondary antibodies (#ab150077/#ab150116/#ab150113, Abcam, USA) and DAPI (#0100–20, SouthernBiotech, USA) on the next day. Imaging was performed using a fluorescence microscope (Chongqing Optec Instrument Company, China).

Primary Microglia Isolation and Treatment

Primary mixed glia cells were cultured as previously described [19]. Briefly, the cortex tissues were isolated from the brains of 1-day-old WT or Treml2-KO pups, digested with 0.125% trypsin (#25200-56, Gibco, USA), and then the mixed cells were gently triturated in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12) (#A4192001, Gibco, USA) containing 10% FBS (#FBSF-500, Moregate Biotech, New Zealand) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (#SV30010, HyClone, USA) at 37 °C for 14 days. The flask was sealed with foils and shaken at 250 rpm for 1 h to collect microglia. The isolated microglia were then grown in DMEM/F12 for 2–3 days. Chronic hypoxia was induced by 10 µM CoCl2 (#15862-1ML-F, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) incubation for 5 days.

Myelin Debris Purification and Incubation

Myelin debris was extracted as described [20]. In brief, adult WT C57BL/6 mice brains were homogenized in 0.32 M sucrose. Myelin debris was isolated by sucrose density gradient centrifugation at 75,000 g for 30 min and resuspended in PBS (pH 7.4). Microglia were co-cultured with myelin debris of 1 mg/ml for 24 h. Non-ingested debris was washed away. Proteins from microglia incubated with debris were collected. Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA Assay Kit (#P0011, Beyotime, China). Phagocytic function was assessed by quantifying MBP levels via western blot.

Western Blot Analysis

Western blot analysis was conducted as previously described [21]. WT and Treml2-KO mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation following an overdose of isoflurane (5% in oxygen; #26675-46-7, Millipore, USA). Total protein was extracted from the corpus callosum or cultured primary microglia. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (#03010040001, Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk (#232100, BD, USA) and incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies: anti-CD68 (#ab283654, Abcam, USA), anti-Iba-1 (#ab178846, Abcam, USA), anti-MBP (#sc-271524, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), anti-TREML2 (#sc-390167, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), anti-TLR9 (#ab134368, Abcam, USA), and anti-β-actin (#66009-1-Ig, Proteintech, China). The membranes were then incubated with anti-mouse/rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (#7076/#7074, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) for 2 h at room temperature. Blots were imaged using High-sig ECL Western Blotting Peroxide Buffer (#180–5001, Tanon, China). Protein density was analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). Protein expression was normalized to β-actin.

Lentiviral Transduction

Mouse Tlr9 shRNA lentiviral knockdown particles (#sc-40271) and control lentiviral particles (#sc-108080) were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. Purified primary microglia were seeded at a density of 4 × 105 cells/mL into 24-well plates. Lentiviral particles with 8 µg/mL polybrene were added and co-cultured with microglia for 6 h. Post-infection supernatant was replaced with basal medium containing 10% FBS (#FBSF-500, Moregate Biotech, New Zealand) and 50% pre-transduction glial culture supernatant. Transduction efficiency was assessed by western blot at 72 h.

Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR) Analysis

Total RNA from microglia or brain tissues was extracted using RNAiso Easy (#TCH020, Takara, Japan). Reverse transcription was performed with PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (with gDNA Eraser) (#RR047A, Takara, Japan), followed by qPCR reaction in 8-strip tubes containing template/control matrix, primer sets (Supplementary Table S1) and kit reagents (#RR820A, Takara, Japan). qPCR was run on the ABI 7500 type sequence detection system (ABI, USA). Data were analyzed by the 2−ΔΔCt approach. Phagocytosis-related genes were profiled using a PCR Array (#WC-MRNA0121-M, Wcgene Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with analysis via Wcgene Biotech software (http://www.wcgene.com). Gene lists were shown in Supplementary Table S2.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism software, version 12.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., USA). Data were presented as mean ± SD. Student’s t-test was used for the comparison between two groups. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test or Kruskal-Wallis test was employed to analyze differences among three or more groups. MWM data were analyzed by two-way repeated measures ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test. P < 0.05 was considered as statistical significance.

Activated Microglia Participated in Myelin Debris Clearance After Chronic Cerebral Ischemia

Microglia serve as local immune cells in the brain [24]. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that the levels of Iba-1, a marker for microglial activation, increased following BCAS (Fig. 2A-B). This outcome suggested that microglia were activated by chronic cerebral ischemia. Double immunofluorescence staining indicated that MBP co-localized with Iba-1 in the brain (Fig. 2A). Moreover, the areas double-stained with both MBP and Iba-1 in the brain were significantly enlarged after BCAS (Fig. 2A and C). These results implied that activated microglia might be involved in the clearance of myelin debris following chronic cerebral ischemia. To confirm these findings in vitro, primary microglia were exposed to CoCl2 to mimic hypoxic conditions (Fig. 2D). As expected, CoCl2 treatment significantly elevated Iba-1 levels in microglia (Fig. 2E). Meanwhile, the levels of CD68, another microglial activation marker [25], were increased after CoCl2 treatment (Supplementary Fig. S1), indicating that hypoxia induced the activation of microglia. Subsequently, primary microglia, with or without CoCl2 treatment, were co-incubated with myelin (Fig. 2D). As shown in Fig. 2F, CoCl2 treatment substantially enhanced microglial phagocytosis of myelin. Treml2 Deficiency Ameliorated Demyelination and Cognitive Deficits Induced by Chronic Cerebral Ischemia

To further investigate the role of microglial TREML2 in chronic cerebral ischemia, Treml2-KO mice were generated by GemPharmatech using CRISPR/Cas9 technology (Fig. 4A-B). It is noteworthy that brain Iba-1 levels were unaffected by Treml2 deficiency after BCAS (Fig. 4A and C). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that Treml2 KO partially reversed the BCAS-induced reduction of MBP levels (Fig. 4D-E), suggesting that demyelination caused by chronic cerebral ischemia was alleviated by Treml2 deficiency. During the hidden platform task of the MWM test, WT mice subjected to BCAS exhibited significantly longer path lengths than sham-operated WT mice from day 3 to day 5, and the two-way repeated measures ANOVA further indicated that WT BCAS mice exhibited significantly poorer performance compared to sham-operated WT mice (Fig. 4F). In the probe trials of the MWM test, sham-operated WT mice had a significantly higher chance of crossing the underwater platform than WT BCAS mice (Fig. 4G). In the Y-maze test, the spontaneous alternation rate of WT BCAS mice was significantly lower than that of sham-operated WT mice (Supplementary Fig. S2A). These results indicated that spatial learning and memory were significantly impaired after chronic cerebral ischemia. Treml2-KO mice subjected to BCAS exhibited improved performance in both the hidden platform task (Fig. 4F) and probe trials (Fig. 4G) of the MWM test compared to WT BCAS mice. Additionally, Treml2-KO mice subjected to BCAS also demonstrated a higher spontaneous alternation rate than WT BCAS mice in the Y-maze test (Supplementary Fig. S2A). These findings suggested that spatial learning and memory deficits caused by chronic cerebral ischemia were rescued by Treml2 deficiency. It is noteworthy that neither BCAS nor Treml2 deficiency had an impact on the swimming speed during the MWM test (Fig. 4H) or the total number of entries in the Y-maze test (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Treml2 Deficiency Promoted Microglial M2-type Polarization and Facilitated Phagocytosis of Myelin Debris After Chronic Cerebral Ischemia

In the brain, activated microglia can polarize into either M1-type or M2-type phenotypes [26]. As shown in Fig. 5A-B, the expression of M1-type polarization markers, such as iNOS and NLRP3, was significantly reduced in the brains of Treml2-KO mice subjected to BCAS compared to WT mice subjected to BCAS. Conversely, Treml2-KO mice subjected to BCAS displayed elevated expression of M2-type polarization markers, including ARG-1 and CD206 in the brain, compared to WT BCAS mice (Fig. 5C-D). These results suggested that Treml2 deficiency promoted microglial M2-type polarization following BCAS. Given that M2-polarized microglia exhibit enhanced phagocytic activity, we subsequently investigated the impact of Treml2 deficiency on microglial phagocytosis of myelin debris after chronic cerebral ischemia. CoCl2-treated Treml2-KO microglia (Fig. 5E) were co-incubated with myelin. As depicted in Fig. 5F, Treml2 deficiency enhanced microglial phagocytosis of myelin. TREML2 Modulated Microglial Phagocytosis of Myelin Debris via TLR9

To identify the key mediators involved in TREML2-regulated microglial phagocytosis of myelin debris, we examined the expression of 90 phagocytosis-related genes (Supplementary Table S2) using a PCR Array in WT and Treml2-KO mice subjected to BCAS. Compared to WT BCAS mice, approximately 21% of the phagocytosis-related genes were significantly upregulated in the corpus callosum of Treml2-KO BCAS mice (Supplementary Fig. S3). Among these upregulated genes, we identified the top 5 with the most substantial increase (fold change ≥ 2) in Treml2-KO BCAS mice relative to WT BCAS mice, including CD44, ITGB2, TLR9, VAV1, and WAS (Fig. 6A). Next, we tried to validate these results using microglia treated with CoCl2. As shown in Fig. 6B-F, TLR9 expression was notably elevated in WT microglia after CoCl2 treatment. Furthermore, Treml2-KO microglia displayed elevated expression of CD44, ITGB2, and TLR9 compared to WT microglia following CoCl2 treatment, with TLR9 exhibiting the most pronounced increment. These observations suggested that TLR9 was implicated in TREML2-regulated microglial phagocytosis of myelin debris. To further ascertain whether TREML2 modulated microglial phagocytosis of myelin debris through TLR9, we suppressed TLR9 expression in Treml2-KO microglia using a lentiviral-mediated approach (Fig. 6G). It is important to note that Tlr9 knockdown did not provoke TREML2 expression (Fig. 6G). As depicted in Fig. 6H, the enhancement in phagocytosis of myelin observed in CoCl2-treated Trem12-KO microglia was attenuated by Tlr9 knockdown. Discussion

Demyelination, which refers to the loss of the myelin sheath, can disrupt neural signaling and potentially contribute to cognitive decline and other neurological disorders [3]. In this study, we employed the BCAS model in mice to reveal that chronic cerebral ischemia induces demyelination, as evidenced by the reduction of MBP and MAG, two markers of myelin integrity [22, 23]. This finding aligns with our previous observations in BCAS rodent models [27–29]. We also observed that chronic cerebral ischemia triggered the activation of microglia, the resident immune cells in the brain [30]. Interestingly, the co-localization of microglia with myelin debris suggested that these activated microglia participated in phagocytosing myelin debris, a process significantly amplified following chronic cerebral ischemia [7, 8]. This hypothesis was further supported by our in vitro experiments, which showed that hypoxic conditions induced by CoCl2 treatment enhanced microglial phagocytosis of myelin. This indicates that the activation of microglia and their subsequent phagocytosis of myelin debris are, at least in part, a response to ischemic damage. By phagocytosing myelin debris, microglia may help maintain myelin integrity and thus offer potential protection against ischemic injury [9, 10]. However, the mechanisms by which microglia modulate this phagocytic process in the context of chronic cerebral ischemia remain poorly understood.

As a recently identified inflammation-associated transmembrane receptor, TREML2 is predominantly expressed by microglia in the brain [11]. Emerging evidence suggests that TREML2 is upregulated in several neurological diseases, such as AD [11]. Previously, we demonstrated elevated microglial TREML2 levels in the brains of a sporadic AD animal model [13]. Our findings are further supported by Sierksma et al., who reported similar microglial TREML2 elevations in the brains of APP/PS1 mice, a transgenic AD model [31]. In this study, using the BCAS model of mice and a CoCl2-induced in vitro hypoxia model, we showed that the immune receptor TREML2 is upregulated in microglia in response to chronic cerebral ischemia. To our knowledge, this is the first study reporting an alteration in microglial TREML2 expression following ischemic insult. These results implied that microglial TREML2 might play a role in the pathogenesis of chronic cerebral ischemia.

To further explore the role of TREML2 in chronic cerebral ischemia, Treml2-KO mice were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 technology by GemPharmatech. Utilizing Treml2-KO mice, we provided the first evidence that Treml2 deficiency alleviated demyelination caused by chronic cerebral ischemia, suggesting a protective role for microglial TREML2 inhibition in maintaining myelin integrity under ischemic conditions. Importantly, we demonstrated for the first time that Treml2 deficiency rescued cognitive impairments induced by chronic cerebral ischemia. Additionally, the improved cognitive performance was not a result of changes in motor functions. Given that demyelination disrupts normal neuronal function and contributes to neurological deficits [3], the enhanced cognitive functions observed in Treml2-KO mice might be attributed to the amelioration of demyelination.

As previously mentioned, microglia contributed to the maintenance of myelin integrity by phagocytosing myelin debris [9, 10]. In this study, we revealed that Trem12 deficiency shifted microglial polarization towards the M2 phenotype, as evidenced by a reduction in M1 markers and an increase in M2 markers. These results were consistent with our earlier observations that Trem12 knockdown promoted microglial M2-type polarization in LPS-stimulated microglia and in the brains of SAMP8 mice, an animal model of sporadic AD [12, 13]. Notably, our observations revealed that Trem12 deficiency promoted microglial phagocytosis of myelin debris in vitro. Given that M2-polarized microglia are known for their enhanced phagocytic capabilities, which are crucial for the clearance of myelin debris and tissue repair [32, 33], it appeared that Trem12 deficiency may offer protection against demyelination by promoting M2-polarization of microglia.

To elucidate the signaling pathways underlying TREML2-mediated regulation of microglial phagocytosis, we utilized a PCR array to assess the expression of genes associated with phagocytosis. Our findings revealed that Trem12 deficiency resulted in the upregulation of several genes, with TLR9 exhibiting the most significant increase. To ascertain the involvement of TLR9 in TREML2-regulated phagocytosis, we suppressed TLR9 expression in Trem12 deficiency microglia and observed a decrease in the augmented phagocytosis of myelin debris. This indicated that Trem12 deficiency enhanced microglial phagocytosis of myelin debris through TLR9. TLR9, a member of the toll-like receptor family, is pivotal in the innate immune response and has been associated with microglial activation and phagocytic activity [34]. Previous studies by Cacace et al. demonstrated that TLR9 promoted the phagocytosis of Aβ42 oligomers in microglia [35], while Redlich et al. showed that stimulating murine microglia with TLR9 agonists enhanced the phagocytosis of encapsulated Cryptococcus neoformans [36]. Our study provides the first evidence that TLR9 participated in microglial phagocytosis of myelin debris. Furthermore, to our knowledge, this is the first report of a potential interaction between the immune receptors TREML2 and TLR9 under ischemic conditions.

It should be noted that this study also presents some limitations. Firstly, the precise mode of interaction between TREML2 and TLR9 was not elucidated here. Future investigations utilizing co-immunoprecipitation and mRNA sequencing should be carried out to explore whether TREML2 interacts with TLR9 directly or via other potential signaling pathways. Secondly, in this study, we demonstrated for the first time that the Treml2 deficiency ameliorated demyelination and cognitive deficits induced by chronic cerebral ischemia. However, in addition to the brain, TREML2 is also expressed in the lung, spleen, and heart. Therefore, further studies should be conducted to investigate whether systemic Treml2 deficiency has unintended impacts on the normal physiological functions of these organs.

Conclusion

Summarily, in this study, we demonstrated for the first time that microglial TREML2 expression was up-regulated in response to chronic cerebral ischemia. Utilizing Treml2-KO mice, we provided the first evidence that Treml2 deficiency ameliorated demyelination and cognitive deficits induced by chronic cerebral ischemia. Moreover, this protective effect might be attributed to the microglial M2-type polarization and enhanced phagocytosis of myelin debris, both of which were induced by Treml2 deficiency. Additionally, we showed that TREML2 regulated microglial phagocytosis of myelin debris via TLR9 under ischemic conditions. These findings elucidated the mechanisms by which microglia regulated the phagocytosis of myelin debris in response to brain ischemic injury and suggested that inhibition of TREML2 might represent a novel strategy for treating demyelination and cognitive decline induced by chronic cerebral ischemia (Supplementary Fig. S4).
