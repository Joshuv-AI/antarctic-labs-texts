Designing intranasal formulations for Alzheimer’s disease: A material-based perspective
Authors: Yu-Shuan Chen, Hsin-Yi Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlzheimer’s disease (AD) remains a major global health challenge, as currently available therapies have a limited impact on disease progression and rely largely on systemic administration. Effective treatment is further hindered by the blood–brain barrier, which restricts brain exposure for most therapeutic agents. Intranasal delivery offers a noninvasive nose-to-brain approach by utilizing olfactory and trigeminal pathways to bypass systemic circulation and first-pass metabolism. Clinically, intranasal insulin and mesenchymal stromal cell-derived exosomes have demonstrated feasibility and safety in mild cognitive impairment and AD, and imaging studies have confirmed direct brain uptake following intranasal dosing. However, variable clinical outcomes suggest that therapeutic efficacy is determined primarily by formulation properties rather than by the delivery route alone. This review adopts a material-centric perspective to summarize rational intranasal formulation strategies for AD, including chitosan derivatives, thermoresponsive poloxamer and amphiphilic block copolymer platforms, lipid-based nanocarriers, surface-engineered systems, and biomimetic nanovesicles. Key translational considerations are discussed with emphasis on quantitative validation of brain exposure and reproducibility. Emerging methodological tools are briefly noted as potential facilitators of formulation development, without detracting from the central role of rational material design.

INTRODUCTION

Why intranasal delivery matters in Alzheimer’s disease

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) represents a growing global health challenge, affecting an estimated 58 million people worldwide as of 2020 and projected to reach 152 million by 2050 [1]. Despite decades of research, currently approved therapies, such as cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine) and N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonists (memantine), offer only symptomatic relief without halting neurodegeneration [2]. More recently, monoclonal antibodies targeting amyloid-β (aducanumab and lecanemab) have gained conditional approval [3], yet their intravenous administration, cost, and risk of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities restrict widespread use. These limitations underscore the critical need for a more efficient, safe, and patient-friendly route for delivering therapeutics directly to the brain.

The blood–brain barrier (BBB) remains a central obstacle in the treatment of AD. This highly selective interface blocks nearly 98% of small molecules and virtually all macromolecules from entering the brain parenchyma [4]. Consequently, even drugs with proven neuroprotective or anti-inflammatory potential fail to achieve effective concentrations in target regions such as the hippocampus and cortex. Strategies to bypass or transiently open the BBB, including focused ultrasound (FUS), osmotic disruption, or nanocarrier-mediated transport, have shown promise but are often associated with systemic risks such as off-target systemic exposure depending on the modality and coadministered therapeutics [5].

Intranasal delivery provides an elegant, noninvasive alternative by leveraging the anatomical continuity between the nasal cavity and the central nervous system (CNS) [6]. Drugs administered intranasally can access the brain via the olfactory and trigeminal nerve pathways, thereby circumventing systemic circulation and hepatic first-pass metabolism [6]. This route not only enables rapid brain exposure but also minimizes peripheral side effects, offering a particularly valuable strategy for chronic neurodegenerative disorders requiring long-term dosing.

Clinically, programs such as intranasal insulin and mesenchymal stromal cell-derived exosomes have demonstrated safety and signals of cognitive benefit in mild cognitive impairment (MCI)/AD, although outcomes have been heterogeneous across trials [7891011]; critically, first-in-human positron emission tomography (PET) work has now provided direct visualization of brain uptake after intranasal dosing [10], reinforcing the biological plausibility of this route. However, variability in outcomes highlights the influence of formulation design on mucosal residence, epithelial permeation, and neural transport. Thus, rational material design has become the decisive factor distinguishing success from failure in nose-to-brain therapeutics.

In this review, we discuss design strategies for intranasal formulations targeting AD, emphasizing material-driven optimization and translational readiness. We first outline the anatomical basis and clinical advances of intranasal therapy, followed by a material-centric analysis of polymers, lipids, and biomimetic vesicles that enable effective nose-to-brain transport.

Finally, we explore emerging hybrid systems and imaging-supported validation that may transform intranasal therapeutics into a practical route for AD management. In addition, we briefly highlight forward-looking strategies, including data-driven approaches and physical adjuncts, that may further streamline formulation design and enhance regional brain delivery. These perspectives are discussed to frame future directions without detracting from the central role of rational material engineering.

We performed a structured literature search in PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science Core Collection, and Scopus (last search: January 2026) using combinations of keywords including “intranasal” OR “nose-to-brain,” “Alzheimer,” “amyloid,” “tau,” “neuroinflammation,” and formulation/material terms (e.g., “nanoparticle,” “liposome,” “nanoemulsion,” “in situ gel,” “poloxamer,” “chitosan,” “exosome,” “extracellular vesicle,” “ligand,” and “cell-penetrating peptide”). We included (i) clinical trials or imaging studies in MCI/AD reporting safety and/or CNS readouts; (ii) in vivo studies in AD or AD-relevant models with quantitative brain exposure metrics (e.g. percentages of the injected dose per gram (%ID/g), brain/plasma ratio, drug targeting efficiency (DTE)/direct transport percentage (DTP) and/or neuropathological or behavioral endpoints; and (iii) key mechanistic reviews for nasal anatomy/physiology. We excluded nonintranasal routes and non-AD indications. Reference lists of eligible articles were manually screened to capture additional relevant studies.

ANATOMICAL AND PHYSIOLOGICAL FOUNDATIONS OF THE NOSE-TO-BRAIN PATHWAY

The human nasal cavity provides a unique and direct anatomical interface with the CNS, allowing therapeutic agents to bypass the BBB. Two primary neural pathways mediate this connection, the olfactory route and the trigeminal nerve route, which together enable direct access to the brain from the nasal mucosa [6].

Olfactory pathway

The olfactory region, located in the upper part of the nasal cavity, represents the principal site for nose-to-brain transport. It contains specialized olfactory receptor neurons whose dendrites are exposed to the nasal lumen while their axons traverse the cribriform plate to form the olfactory nerve projecting to the olfactory bulb [6]. From there, neural connections extend to higher cognitive centers, including the olfactory cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala, regions crucial for memory and emotion that are profoundly affected in AD.

Experimental studies in rodents have demonstrated that substances deposited in the olfactory epithelium can reach the olfactory bulb within approximately 0.7–2.3 h, reflecting the short physical distance of 4-5 mm between the nasal roof and the bulb [6]. Transport occurs through multiple mechanisms, including paracellular diffusion, transcellular transport, and axonal migration, depending on the molecular size and physicochemical characteristics of the formulation.

Trigeminal pathway

The second major route involves the ophthalmic (V1) and maxillary (V2) branches of the trigeminal nerve, which innervate the respiratory region of the nasal cavity and project centrally via the trigeminal ganglion to brainstem trigeminal nuclei, including the pons and medulla (e.g., the spinal trigeminal nucleus) [61213]. Because of its longer anatomical distance (20–30 mm in rodents), drug delivery via the trigeminal route is typically slower, with transport times ranging from 17 to 56 h [6]. Compared with the olfactory-bulb-dominant distribution, trigeminal-associated transport tends to contribute more to caudal CNS exposure (brainstem-related regions), thereby complementing olfactory transport and supporting broader CNS distribution [14].

Barriers to intranasal transport

Despite this unique connectivity, the nasal mucosa presents several physiological barriers that restrict efficient delivery [1516]. Mucociliary clearance: a self-cleaning defense mechanism that removes inhaled particles and pathogens within 20–30 min after deposition, thereby limiting the residence time of formulations [16]. Enzymatic degradation: the nasal epithelium expresses cytochrome P450 enzymes and peptidases that can metabolize drugs before they traverse the mucosa [17]. Tight junctions: the pseudo-stratified epithelium is connected by tight junctions that impede paracellular diffusion, particularly for hydrophilic and large molecules [18].

Hence, effective intranasal systems must be engineered to prolong mucosal residence, penetrate the mucus layer, preventing enzymatic degradation, and facilitate epithelial transport without compromising tissue integrity.

Clinically, intranasal delivery in MCI/AD has advanced most clearly for intranasal insulin and MSC-derived exosomes [Table 1], supporting feasibility and safety under repeated dosing. However, heterogeneous outcomes, particularly in insulin trials, highlight that deposition control and formulation/device reproducibility are decisive determinants of efficacy rather than the route alone. Accordingly, tissue integrity, preserving engineered systems, should be evaluated alongside clinically realistic devices and imaging-anchored confirmation of brain exposure [Section 3].

Implications for Alzheimer’s disease therapy

In AD, the olfactory bulb and hippocampal, entorhinal circuits are affected early by amyloid-β and tau pathology, providing disease-relevant targets reachable via nasal pathways. Clinically, intranasal programs have primarily enrolled MCI/early AD (e.g., intranasal insulin) and mild-to-moderate AD (e.g., MSC-derived exosomes), supporting the feasibility of repeated dosing while highlighting outcome variability that depends on deposition and formulation/device control [Section 3 and Table 1] [6810].

Aging and Alzheimer’s disease-associated changes relevant to intranasal delivery

Intranasal delivery is primarily intended for older adults; therefore, age-associated nasal changes can materially influence residence time, epithelial interaction, and the probability of reproducible upper-nasal (olfactory-region) deposition. Human studies report slower ciliary beat frequency and prolonged mucociliary clearance with aging, which can increase interindividual variability in exposure and therapeutic response [19].

In addition, olfactory dysfunction is common early in AD, and amyloid-β and p-tau pathology have been detected in the olfactory epithelium in a substantial fraction of pathologically confirmed AD cases, suggesting that disease-associated remodeling may also influence the olfactory interface relevant to nose-to-brain transport [20].

From a clinical staging perspective, intranasal strategies are most compelling in MCI to early AD, where repeated, patient-friendly dosing is feasible and disease-modifying goals (antiamyloid/tau, anti-inflammatory, and synaptic support) are most relevant. In moderate-to-severe AD, caregiver-assisted administration remains possible, but variability in nasal patency, deposition efficiency, and adherence becomes a dominant practical barrier. Accordingly, formulations and devices that maximize reproducible upper-nasal deposition and provide quantitative confirmation of brain exposure are essential for clinical translation.

CLINICAL PROGRESS IN INTRANASAL THERAPY FOR ALZHEIMER’S DISEASE

Among the various CNS disorders explored for nose-to-brain therapy, AD has advanced the furthest toward clinical translation. To date, the most extensively investigated programs involve intranasal insulin and mesenchymal stromal cell-derived exosomes (MSC-Exos), both of which exemplify how formulation optimization and imaging validation can define translational success [Table 1].

Intranasal insulin: The foundational clinical paradigm

Insulin plays a crucial role in cerebral glucose metabolism and synaptic plasticity, and insulin resistance has been implicated in the pathogenesis of AD. Pioneering clinical studies by Craft et al. demonstrated that intranasal administration of regular human insulin could modulate cognition and cerebral glucose utilization without altering systemic glycemia [7]. In a Phase 2 randomized controlled trial (NCT00438568), daily intranasal insulin stabilized or improved memory and functional performance in patients with amnestic MCI and early AD, with good tolerability [7]. In the pilot randomized trial (N = 104), participants received placebo, 20 IU, or 40 IU intranasal insulin for 4 months; 20 IU improved delayed memory (P < 0.05) and both doses preserved caregiver-rated functional ability (P < 0.01), with no treatment-related severe adverse events reported [7]. However, subsequent larger trials revealed heterogeneous results. A multicenter Phase 2 study (NCT01767909) reported no significant cognitive or functional improvement compared with placebo, though treatment remained safe and well tolerated [9]. In the multicenter randomized trial (N = 240), intranasal insulin using “Device 2” did not improve cognition or function compared with placebo, and a device-related reliability issue was identified as a major confounder, reinforcing that delivery control and formulation/device reproducibility are decisive for clinical outcomes [9]. This discrepancy highlighted the influence of device precision, dosing frequency, and formulation reproducibility on therapeutic efficacy.

A recent milestone was achieved with the first-in-human PET imaging study using (68Ga) NOTA-insulin (4 IU), which directly visualized drug distribution following intranasal administration. The study confirmed measurable uptake across 11 memory-related regions, including the hippocampus and posterior cingulate cortex, with differences observed by cognitive status and sex (P < 0.01) [10]. These findings provided the first quantitative in vivo evidence that peptides can reach human brain targets via nasal dosing, marking a critical translational breakthrough.

Kellar et al. (2021), from the same research group that published the 2012 [7] and 2020 [9] trials, reported another phase 2 randomized, double-blind study (N = 49). Participants received placebo or 20 IU intranasal insulin (Humulin R U-100) twice daily for 12 months. Intranasal insulin significantly reduced the progression of deep and frontal white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), and WMH changes correlated with cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers and cognitive outcomes [11]. Although the authors did not specify the delivery device used in this trial, they emphasized that device performance and reliability likely contribute to variability in therapeutic efficacy compared with earlier studies [911].

Collectively, these data demonstrate that while insulin’s cognitive effects remain variable, the route itself is clinically validated for safe, reproducible brain delivery.

Mesenchymal stromal cell-derived exosomes: Emerging biologic strategy

Beyond small peptides, biologic nanovesicles such as MSC-derived exosomes have recently entered early-phase clinical evaluation for AD. Exosomes are nanoscale extracellular vesicles capable of crossing biological barriers and delivering regulatory RNAs and proteins to target cells. In a three-arm phase I/II trial (NCT04388982; n = 9), patients with mild-to-moderate AD received intranasal allogenic human adipose mesenchymal stromal cells (ahaMSC)-derived exosomes (ahaMSCs-Exos) twice weekly for 12 weeks; the treatment was safe and well tolerated. In the medium-dose arm (≥4 × 108 particles), cognition improved, with AD Assessment Scale–Cognitive section (ADAS-cog) decreasing by 2.33 ± 1.19 and basic version of Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) increasing by 2.38 ± 0.58 at week 12, and ADAS-cog further decreasing to − 3.98 by week 36; no significant differences in amyloid or tau biomarkers were reported [8]. Notably, a delivery device was used for administration, supporting the view that device performance can influence effective nose-to-brain dosing.

These results establish MSC-Exos as a clinically feasible platform for noninvasive biologic therapy, opening new avenues for RNA or protein payload delivery.

Lessons learned and translational implications

The cumulative clinical experience provides several critical lessons: (1) Deposition control and delivery-system performance are paramount; differences in device reliability, spray pattern, administration technique, or viscosity can shift deposition from upper (olfactory-targeted) to lower (respiratory) regions and alter effective brain exposure. (2) Imaging-anchored pharmacokinetics (e.g., PET, single photon emission computed tomography, and magnetic resonance [MR] imaging perfusion) are indispensable for verifying true brain uptake. (3) Safety and tolerability have been consistently favorable across both peptide and vesicle studies, supporting chronic administration feasibility. (4) The translational frontier is achieving reproducible nose-to-brain exposure through device-aware formulation and delivery-system co-optimization, as outcome heterogeneity likely reflects insufficient control of regional deposition and exposure rather than route feasibility alone.

Together, these findings justify further exploration of intranasal therapeutics for AD, particularly in combination with rationally engineered carriers that ensure mucosal retention, epithelial penetration, and reproducible neural transport.

RATIONAL FORMULATION DESIGN STRATEGIES: A MATERIAL-CENTRIC PERSPECTIVE

Successful nose-to-brain delivery does not rely on a single physiological mechanism but rather on the intrinsic properties of the materials used to construct the formulation. Many materials combine mucoadhesive, mucus-penetrating, and epithelial-modulating properties, rendering strict function-based classification impractical. Therefore, a material-centric framework provides a more coherent and translationally relevant basis for understanding formulation design in AD therapy [Table 2].

Chitosan and its derivatives: From mucoadhesion to barrier modulation

Chitosan is one of the most extensively investigated polymers in intranasal drug delivery because of its cationic nature, biocompatibility, and chemical tunability. Its protonated amine groups enable strong electrostatic interactions with negatively charged mucins, conferring pronounced mucoadhesive properties that prolong nasal residence time [154243]. Beyond adhesion, chitosan can transiently loosen epithelial tight junctions, thereby enhancing paracellular transport and thereby enhance paracellular transport, particularly for hydrophilic drugs and macromolecules [4344].

In AD-focused applications, multiple chitosan derivatives have been developed to extend these functions. Quaternized and thiolated chitosan nanoparticles have been used to deliver rivastigmine, insulin, and galantamine, resulting in markedly enhanced nasal permeation and improved cognitive outcomes compared with free drugs or oral administration [2145]. Carboxymethyl chitosan micelles further expand the design space by enabling codelivery of therapeutic cargos such as black phosphorus and methylene blue, demonstrating efficacy against tau pathology and mitochondrial dysfunction in AD models [22].

Hybrid designs illustrate how chitosan can serve as both a functional material and a structural scaffold. For example, chitosan conjugated with phenylboronic acid enabled synergistic central and peripheral amyloid-β clearance when formulated as polymeric nanodiscs [23]. Similarly, thiolated chitosan incorporated into liposomal systems enhanced brain exposure of donepezil by combining adhesion with sustained release [24].

Despite its versatility, chitosan-based systems may raise concerns related to batch variability, pH sensitivity, and potential mucosal irritation, emphasizing the need for molecular-weight optimization and long-term tolerability studies in aged nasal mucosa.

Poloxamers and amphiphilic block copolymers: Thermoresponsive structural platforms

Poloxamers, particularly poloxamer 407 and poloxamer 188 (P188), are amphiphilic PEO–PPO–PEO triblock copolymers widely used as thermoresponsive in situ gelling agents. Their temperature-dependent sol–gel transition allows formulations to remain fluid during administration and rapidly gel upon contact with the nasal mucosa, forming a localized drug depot [252646].

In AD therapy, poloxamers rarely act alone; instead, they function as structural matrices that host nanoparticles, liposomes, or nanoemulsions. Donepezil-loaded poloxamer gels have demonstrated sustained release, favorable sprayability, and significantly enhanced brain targeting indices compared with oral dosing [25]. When combined with Poly Lactic-co-Glycolic Acid nanoparticles or nanostructured lipid carriers, poloxamers further improved nose-to-brain transport efficiency relative to solid lipid nanoparticle controls [26].

Auxiliary excipients may further optimize mucus interaction within poloxamer-based systems; for example, incorporation of Capryol 90 into a P188–donepezil formulation markedly enhanced brain delivery DTE 321.2% and DTP 74.6% despite a relatively large particle size (192.5 ± 7.3 nm), highlighting the robustness of the poloxamer platform [27].

Amphiphilic block copolymers other than classical poloxamers, such as poly (ethylene glycol)-block-poly(D, L-lactic acid) (PEG–PLA) conjugates functionalized with targeting peptides, have also shown promise. Intranasal delivery of rapamycin via PEG–PLA–CS1 (fibronectin CS1 peptide) micelles resulted in reduced amyloid burden and improved cognition, highlighting how block copolymer architecture can integrate mucus penetration with receptor-mediated uptake [28].

The principal limitation of poloxamer-based systems lies in dilution sensitivity and cold-chain requirements; nevertheless, their clinical familiarity and scalability make them attractive backbones for translational intranasal formulations.

Lipid-based nanocarriers: Vesicles, nanoemulsions, and deformable systems

Lipid-based systems constitute a dominant class of intranasal carriers due to their ability to encapsulate lipophilic drugs, protect labile cargos, and interact favorably with epithelial membranes. In AD research, this category includes liposomes, transfersomes, nanoemulsions, and nanostructured lipid carriers.

Nanoemulsions and nanostructured lipid carriers are frequently employed to enhance mucus diffusion through small particle size and surface neutrality. Intranasal donepezil and memantine nanoemulsions have demonstrated quantitative brain accumulation, supported by gamma scintigraphy and %ID/g measurements, providing rare pharmacokinetic evidence for effective nose-to-brain delivery [2930]. More recent nanostructured lipid carriers-in-gel hybrids achieved high (DTE >200%) and improved cognitive outcomes, illustrating the benefit of combining lipid mobility with gel-based retention [31].

Deformable vesicles such as transfersomes, liposomes, and cubosomes further exploit membrane elasticity to traverse epithelial barriers. Hyaluronic acid (HA)-coated transfersomes carrying donepezil achieved some of the highest reported brain targeting indices in AD models, underscoring the importance of vesicle flexibility and surface engineering [32]. Liposomal formulations of peptides, H102, a novel β-sheet breaker peptide, similarly demonstrated enhanced brain uptake compared with solution controls [47]. Hybrid systems further extend liposomal nose-to-brain delivery, as surface-modified liposomes of berberine hydrochloride embedded in a poloxamer-based in situ nasal gel achieved enhanced brain targeting after intranasal administration, with a reported (DTE = 138.43%) in AD models [48]. The cubosomes, formulated with S14G-HN, illustrate how membrane elasticity enhances nasal passage while the external gel matrix provides depot-like behavior in the superior nasal cavity [33].

Overall, lipid-based systems offer a balance of encapsulation versatility, manufacturability, and safety, though long-term stability and batch reproducibility remain key translational considerations.

Key translational limitations for synthetic-based nanocarriers include long-term colloidal stability (phase separation/drug leakage), batch-to-batch reproducibility of size/surface properties, and tolerability of surfactant systems under chronic dosing issues that can dominate regulatory feasibility even when preclinical DTE/DTP is high.

Surface-engineered polymers and ligand-functionalized systems: Targeting and biocompatibility enhancement

HA and other surface-functionalized polymers are increasingly employed as outer-shell modifiers in intranasal formulations to enhance biocompatibility, targeting specificity, and mucus interaction. Owing to its high hydrophilicity and affinity for CD44 receptors expressed on nasal epithelial and glial cells, HA can improve cellular uptake while mitigating local irritation, making it particularly suitable for chronic administration in neurodegenerative diseases. In AD-oriented intranasal delivery, HA-coated transfersomes and polymeric nanoparticles have demonstrated markedly superior brain targeting compared with unmodified counterparts, achieving high DTE and DTP while maintaining favorable safety profiles [32]. These findings highlight how surface modification can substantially alter biological performance without changing the core carrier architecture.

Beyond HA, ligand-mediated surface engineering has emerged as a versatile strategy to promote epithelial interaction and receptor-mediated transport. Aleuria aurantia lectin, an L-fucose-binding ligand recognizing L-fucose residues expressed on the olfactory epithelium, has been successfully applied to polymeric nanoparticles carrying siRNA, reinforcing the role of surface ligands in enhancing nose-to-brain transport efficiency [34].

Similarly, lactoferrin (Lf)-functionalized nanoparticles represent an effective receptor-targeted strategy for intranasal delivery. Lf-modified lipid nanoparticles, exemplified by curcumin-or α-mangostin-based systems codelivering BACE1 siRNA, exploit receptor-mediated transcytosis to achieve enhanced brain accumulation, resulting in neuroprotection, reduced amyloid-β pathology, and improved cognitive outcomes in AD models [3549].

Wheat germ agglutinin-modified carriers have also shown enhanced cerebral uptake in neurodegeneration-related models and are frequently cited in the context of intranasal AD development [3637].

In parallel, cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) represent a distinct yet complementary surface-functionalization approach. CPPs such as penetratin facilitate transcellular transport across the nasal epithelium and have been effectively used to deliver peptides, including insulin, via the intranasal route. In aged mouse models relevant to AD, CPP-assisted delivery yielded cognitive benefits and enhanced hippocampal drug exposure, underscoring their potential for macromolecular therapeutics [38].

Collectively, these studies demonstrate that surface engineering, through hydrophilic polymer coatings, receptor-specific ligands, or CPP conjugation, provides a powerful and modular means to modulate biological interactions at the nasal interface, enabling enhanced brain targeting while preserving the physicochemical integrity of the underlying carrier system.

Despite their strong proof-of-concept performance, surface-engineered systems face several translational constraints. The main translational limitation is achieving efficient and reproducible ligand grafting with controlled ligand density/orientation and minimal batch-to-batch variability, as these parameters strongly determine mucus interaction and receptor-mediated uptake.

Exosomes and biomimetic nanovesicles: Endogenous delivery platforms

Exosomes represent a distinct material class, functioning as endogenously derived nanocarriers with inherent stability, immune compatibility, and targeting potential. Intranasally administered MSC-derived exosomes have been detected in the olfactory nerve, hippocampus, neocortex, microglia, and endothelial cells, confirming their ability to traverse nasal-to-brain pathways [3940]. While Clinical feasibility has been established in early-phase trials, positioning exosomes at the frontier of intranasal biologic therapy [8]. In advance, engineered exosome systems illustrate how biomimetic nanovesicles can be endowed with new functional properties beyond their native cargo. For example, exosomes equipped with photoinducible protein delivery modules have enabled spatiotemporally controlled epigenome editing in AD models, demonstrating that exosomes can serve as programmable therapeutic platforms rather than solely endogenous bioactive vesicles [41].

Moreover, conceptual and mechanistic studies emphasize that extracellular vesicles function as biologically active communication units, capable of modulating neurogenesis, inflammation, and tissue repair through their endogenous cargo, rather than acting solely as formulation-defined materials. This view supports the classification of exosomes and related vesicles as biomimetic nanovesicles, exemplified by platelet concentrate-derived extracellular vesicles and Ganoderma lucidum-derived exosome-like nanovesicles, whose therapeutic relevance arises from their intrinsic biological activities [5051].

Key challenges include scalable production, such as related impurities and stability, cargo loading efficiency, such as requiring clearly defined identity/potency assays, and regulatory standardization, but their material-level advantages make them uniquely suited for complex neurodegenerative targets.

Pathophysiology-anchored framing for material choices

AD pathophysiology provides a rational basis for material selection beyond “better delivery.” For antiamyloid strategies (e.g., BACE1 knockdown [34], amyloid clearance enhancers [2533]), formulations must support sufficient regional exposure in olfactory bulb–hippocampal circuits while minimizing peripheral effects. For neuroinflammation-modulating cargos (e.g., Rapamycin [28], exosomes/EVs [39]), prolonged mucosal residence and biocompatible uptake pathways are prioritized for chronic dosing. For synaptic and metabolic support (e.g., insulin [38], neuropeptides [33], miR-132 [36]), preserving cargo bioactivity and achieving reproducible hippocampal exposure are critical. These disease-linked requirements motivate layered or hybrid architectures where retention, mucus penetration, and cell interaction are distributed across components rather than forced into a single excipient.

TRANSLATIONAL CONSIDERATIONS

Accumulating evidence indicates that intranasal delivery has progressed from a conceptual alternative to a clinically validated route for brain targeting in AD. Importantly, heterogeneous outcomes across studies suggest that therapeutic success depends less on the route itself than on the rational design of formulation materials and architectures, as repeatedly demonstrated in both preclinical and clinical investigations [61516].

Across formulation classes, intrinsic material properties govern key interactions at the nasal interface. Chitosan derivatives combine mucoadhesion with transient epithelial modulation, poloxamers function primarily as thermoresponsive structural platforms, lipid-based nanocarriers facilitate membrane interaction and cargo protection, and surface-engineered polymers or ligands refine targeting specificity [23283234353945]. Notably, the strongest preclinical outcomes, often reflected by high DTE and DTP, are consistently reported for hybrid systems, in which complementary functions are distributed across material layers rather than concentrated in a single component [24253233].

Material-driven design inevitably involves trade-offs among retention, penetration, and safety. Strongly mucoadhesive systems, such as poloxamers, prolong nasal residence but may hinder diffusion through mucus or raise concerns regarding local irritation, whereas mucus-penetrating systems, such as Capryol 90, enhance epithelial access but are more susceptible to rapid clearance without a stabilizing matrix [2627]. Ligand- or CPP-mediated strategies further improve cellular uptake but introduce potential risks related to receptor saturation or immunogenicity, particularly under chronic dosing conditions relevant to AD therapy [3738]. Accordingly, optimal formulations should prioritize balanced functionality aligned with clinical feasibility, rather than maximal performance in a single parameter.

A critical advance in the field is the shift from indirect efficacy inference to quantitative confirmation of brain delivery. Preclinical studies increasingly report brain concentration (%/g), brain-to-blood ratios, or targeting indices following intranasal administration of nanoformulations [253237]. More importantly, first-in-human PET imaging of intranasal insulin has provided direct visualization of regional brain uptake, establishing unequivocal evidence of nose-to-brain transport in humans [10]. Together, these data underscore the necessity of imaging-anchored validation to distinguish true central delivery from systemic or nonspecific effects.

A notable translational reality is that most ongoing clinical trials of intranasal trials in AD employ free insulin alone [791052], while vesicle-based approaches are largely limited to exosomes [8]. This discrepancy reflects not a lack of formulation potential, but the dominant influence of chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC) considerations. In practice, combining drugs with synthetic polymers, liposomes, or newly engineered vesicles often constitutes a new drug entity, substantially increasing CMC cost and regulatory burden. Consequently, industry efforts favor repurposed drugs or biologically derived vesicles with established manufacturing pathways. These constraints underscore the need for universal vesicle platforms that balance formulation versatility with regulatory and manufacturing feasibility.

From a disease perspective, intranasal delivery is anatomically and pathophysiologically relevant to AD, as the olfactory bulb and hippocampus, key nodes in nose-to-brain transport, are also among the earliest regions affected by Alzheimer’s pathology [53]. Nevertheless, most preclinical studies are conducted in young, healthy animals, whereas clinical populations often exhibit age-related alterations in nasal epithelium and mucus composition. This gap highlights the need for aged or disease-relevant nasal models and long-term safety assessment, particularly for biologics and gene-regulating cargos such as exosomes and siRNA-loaded systems [5455].

FUTURE PERSPECTIVES AND CHALLENGES

Future advancement of intranasal delivery for AD therapy hinges on resolving several unmet translational gaps rather than further optimization of individual formulations. First, the field lacks standardized and predictive evaluation frameworks for nose-to-brain delivery. Current studies rely on heterogeneous endpoints, limiting cross-study comparison and rational candidate selection [2530333637]. Establishing harmonized metrics that directly link formulation performance to regional brain exposure is essential. Second, early integration of imaging and device considerations is needed. Imaging-based confirmation of brain exposure, as demonstrated in intranasal insulin studies, should become a routine translational requirement, alongside optimization with clinically realistic nasal delivery devices [10]. Third, the expansion toward biologic and nucleic acid-based payloads, including exosomes and siRNA systems, necessitates early attention to scalability, quality control, and long-term safety under repeated intranasal administration [363941].

Despite rational formulation strategies, intranasal drug delivery remains limited by the high dimensionality of polymer and formulation parameter space, rendering empirical optimization labor-intensive. Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted modeling, including machine learning and graph neural networks [56], have demonstrated the ability to identify structure–function relationships in polymeric and lipid-based delivery systems and to efficiently prioritize high-performing candidates [57585960]. Such data-driven approaches have been shown to markedly reduce experimental burden by eliminating large fractions of ineffective formulations. For nose-to-brain applications, where performance depends on complex interactions with mucus, epithelium, and neural transport pathways, AI-guided design may serve as a decision-support tool to streamline polymer selection and formulation refinement. Nevertheless, these approaches remain constrained by data availability and should complement rather than replace experimentally and imaging-validated evaluation.

Beyond formulation optimization, FUS has emerged as a physical adjunct capable of adding spatiotemporal control to brain drug delivery. Preclinical platform studies have shown that applying FUS after intranasal administration can enhance regional brain accumulation, giving rise to the concept of FUS-enhanced intranasal delivery and the related FUS intranasal strategy [616263]. In parallel, MR-guided FUS has been clinically evaluated in AD, demonstrating safe, reversible, and repeatable BBB opening, primarily in combination with intravenous therapeutics [6465]. However, it should be noted that current preclinical evidence in AD predominantly supports intravenous with FUS paradigms, whereas direct clinical validation of intranasal delivery combined with FUS remains limited, underscoring a translational gap for future investigation.

CONCLUSION

Intranasal delivery represents a non-invasive and clinically relevant strategy for brain targeting in AD therapy. Accumulating evidence across preclinical and clinical studies indicates that therapeutic success is driven primarily by material selection and formulation architecture, rather than the delivery route alone. Imaging-supported human studies further confirm the feasibility of achieving brain exposure via intranasal administration. In addition, emerging adjunct strategies, such as computationally assisted formulation prioritization and FUS for regional delivery enhancement, may further support translational development without replacing rational formulation principles. Together, these insights position intranasal therapeutics as a promising and adaptable platform for AD treatment.
