Brief communication: COVID-19 in patients with HIV—a prospective, single-centre cohort study in Slovakia
Authors: Katarína Šimeková, Yaroslav Syrota, Martin Jozef Péč, Róbert Rosoľanka, Daniela Antolová
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvidence on COVID-19 among people living with HIV (PLWH) remains mixed and context-dependent.MethodsWe prospectively followed 64 PLWH receiving antiretroviral therapy at a tertiary university hospital in Martin, Slovakia (September 2020–September 2021). SARS-CoV-2 infection was confirmed by RT-PCR.ResultCOVID-19 was diagnosed in 54 of 64 participants (84.4%) and was generally mild; none required intensive care, and no deaths were reported. There were no statistically significant differences between PLWH who did and did not develop COVID-19 with respect to age, duration of HIV infection, CD4 cell count, or viral suppression. Participants who developed COVID-19 were more likely to have at least one documented comorbidity; however, this did not appear to translate into severe outcomes in this small cohort. Among those with COVID-19, exploratory comparisons showed no clear differences in short-term outcomes according to mRNA vaccination status.ConclusionsIn this small exploratory cohort of ART-treated individuals in Slovakia, we did not identify clear clinical or HIV-related predictors of COVID-19 outcomes; however, the study was underpowered and the findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Background

People living with HIV (PLWH) have been considered potentially vulnerable to severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) because residual immunodeficiency, chronic immune activation, and a higher prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidities may contribute to adverse coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) outcomes [1]. Published evidence, however, has been heterogeneous across settings and pandemic phases, reflecting differences in antiretroviral therapy (ART) coverage, baseline immune status, testing strategies, and access to acute care [2–4].

Central and Eastern Europe have been poorly represented in prospective studies that include outpatients and individuals with mild-to-moderate HIV infection, despite the region’s distinct models of healthcare organisation and HIV service delivery. Slovakia, an EU member state, offers universal compulsory health insurance and publicly funded HIV treatment, yet care is largely centralised in specialised centres. The HIV clinic in Martin is located within a tertiary university hospital and functions as a regional referral centre for northern Slovakia; consequently, the characteristics and outcomes of its cohort may differ from those in settings where care is fragmented or antiretroviral therapy access is constrained.

We carried out a prospective, single-centre cohort study among PLWH on ART to characterise the incidence and clinical course of RT-PCR-confirmed COVID-19 over a one-year period, to compare baseline characteristics between PLWH who did and did not develop COVID-19 during follow-up, and to investigate whether documented COVID-19 complications varied according to mRNA vaccination status among those infected. The study timeframe encompassed both the pre-vaccination and early vaccination periods, enabling an exploratory comparison of complications by mRNA vaccination status.

Methods

We conducted a prospective observational cohort study at the Department of Infectology and Travel Medicine, Outpatient Clinic for Monitoring and Treatment of HIV-Infected Persons, University Hospital in Martin, Slovakia, between September 2020 and September 2021. The University Hospital in Martin is a publicly funded tertiary care teaching hospital that offers specialized outpatient and inpatient services. HIV care is provided through a dedicated outpatient clinic. Antiretroviral therapy (ART) and standard laboratory monitoring are covered by the national health insurance system. The clinic serves a geographically diverse catchment area that includes both urban and rural populations in northern Slovakia, and the majority of patients receive ongoing longitudinal care at this single centre.

Eligible participants were adults (≥ 18 years) with confirmed HIV infection who had been receiving ART for at least 6 months and who attended the clinic during the enrolment period. Participants provided written informed consent. We did not exclude participants based on comorbidity status.

SARS-CoV-2 infection was defined as a positive real-time reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) test from a nasopharyngeal swab processed in a certified laboratory. Testing was performed after onset of symptoms or due to epidemiological indication (e.g., contact tracing). Reinfection was recorded if a new RT-PCR-positive episode occurred after clinical recovery from a previous episode.

Baseline variables were extracted from routine clinical records and included age, sex, duration of HIV infection, and ART status. CD4 T-cell counts were measured by flow cytometry as part of standard care. HIV-1 viral load was measured using Xpert HIV-1 Viral Load (limit of detection 40 copies/mL). Viral suppression was defined as viral load below the assay limit of detection. Comorbidities were abstracted from clinician documentation and included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, obesity, cardiovascular disease, and other chronic conditions recorded during routine visits.

Among participants with confirmed COVID-19, clinician-reported symptoms and complications were systematically documented both during the acute phase of illness and at a follow-up visit conducted approximately one month after the index infection. The outcomes assessed included pneumonia, dyspnoea, and persistent symptoms reported at the follow-up visit approximately 30 days after infection. Vaccination status was classified as of the date of SARS-CoV-2 infection. Given that vaccine uptake in Slovakia during the study period was predominantly attributable to early deployment of mRNA-based vaccines, subgroup analyses were restricted to individuals who had received the mRNA vaccine BNT162b2.

Clinical outcomes were determined through a review of outpatient clinic documentation and participant self-reports. Pneumonia was defined as present if the treating clinician either documented radiologically confirmed pneumonia or initiated pharmacologic management consistent with standard treatment for pneumonia. Hospital-based evaluation was recorded when the participant presented to an emergency department or was admitted to a hospital for indications attributable to COVID-19. Persistent symptoms were evaluated at the scheduled approximately 30-day follow-up visit and were documented if they were reported as ongoing at that time.

We conducted bivariate analyses of baseline characteristics to compare PLWH who experienced and did not experience RT-PCR-confirmed COVID-19 during follow-up (Table 1). Among participants with COVID-19, we assessed the frequency of documented complications in vaccinated versus unvaccinated individuals (Table 2). Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges and were analysed using Mann–Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were analysed using Fisher’s exact tests due to small cell sizes. All p-values are two-sided; post-hoc power calculations were not performed.

Results

Sixty-four PLWH on ART were recruited and observed over a one-year period. The study population consisted mainly of middle-aged men, mirroring the demographics of the local HIV treatment setting. The majority were engaged in stable HIV care, demonstrated preserved immune function on standard laboratory assessments, and showed a high proportion of virologic suppression. Cardiometabolic comorbidities affected a notable subset of participants, whereas severe end-organ complications were relatively rare.

Over the follow-up period, 54 participants developed RT-PCR-confirmed COVID-19; two of these individuals had two separate episodes. Infections were observed throughout the major community surges in 2020–2021. The clinical presentation was predominantly mild to moderate: most participants described self-limited upper respiratory or flu-like illnesses, and severe complications were infrequent. None of the participants required admission to intensive care, and there were no fatalities. At the post-infection follow-up assessment, persistent symptoms were rare at approximately 30 days after infection; one participant reported ongoing shortness of breath.

The majority of cases were managed in outpatient or community settings with supportive symptomatic therapy and implementation of contact isolation measures. When in-person medical assessment was sought, it was most commonly prompted by persistent pyrexia, ongoing respiratory manifestations, or clinical concern for pneumonia; no instances of oxygen supplementation or advanced respiratory support were reported. Among the two individuals who experienced reinfection, the clinical presentation was comparable to that observed during their initial episodes.

Table 1 presents baseline bivariate comparisons of PLWH who did and did not develop COVID-19 during follow-up. No statistically significant differences were observed with respect to age, sex, duration of HIV infection, CD4 cell count, or viral suppression status. However, participants who developed COVID-19 were more likely to have at least one documented comorbidity (67% vs. 30%; p = 0.04).

Discussion

In this prospective, single-centre cohort of antiretroviral therapy (ART)-treated people living with HIV (PLWH) in Slovakia, followed during 2020–2021, reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection was frequent; however, the clinical course was predominantly mild, and no admissions to intensive care units or deaths were recorded. In bivariate analyses, we did not identify clear associations between incident COVID-19 and baseline age, duration of HIV infection, CD4 cell count, or viral suppression. However, participants who developed COVID-19 were more likely to have at least one documented comorbidity, although this did not appear to translate into severe outcomes in this small cohort. Among participants who acquired SARS-CoV-2 infection, exploratory comparisons did not show clear differences in documented short-term complications according to mRNA vaccination status. Persistent symptoms were uncommon at the approximately 30-day follow-up visit; however, the study was not designed to assess Long COVID according to standard international definitions, which typically require a longer follow-up period.

These findings align with reports from settings characterized by high ART coverage and high rates of viral suppression and support the notion that well-controlled HIV infection with preserved immune function may not, in isolation, substantially increase the risk of severe COVID-19 [5, 6]. By contrast, larger datasets and meta-analyses have demonstrated elevated risks of hospitalisation and mortality among PLWH, particularly in individuals with advanced HIV disease, low CD4 + T-cell counts, uncontrolled viraemia, or a high burden of comorbid conditions [3, 4]. A plausible interpretation is that excess risk is predominantly concentrated in subgroups with more pronounced immune suppression and multimorbidity, whereas clinical outcomes may be broadly consistent with outcomes reported in some cohorts from the general population, although this study was not designed for a direct comparison [7].

Our cohort was recruited from a specialised HIV outpatient clinic within a publicly funded, tertiary-care university hospital that functions as a regional referral centre in northern Slovakia. The universal health insurance system ensures access to antiretroviral therapy (ART) and routine clinical and laboratory monitoring, thereby potentially mitigating inequities related to medication availability. Concurrently, the centralisation of HIV services may influence the characteristics of individuals retained in care (e.g. preferentially including persons with stable treatment engagement and adequate capacity to attend follow-up visits), a factor that should be considered when interpreting the relatively mild clinical outcomes observed.

Although the study was not statistically powered to detect modest effect sizes, the absence of severe COVID-19 outcomes in a cohort with high ART coverage highlights the critical role of sustained engagement in HIV care and proactive management of comorbidities. From a clinical perspective, reliance solely on routinely measured HIV-related biomarkers is likely insufficient for accurate COVID-19 risk stratification in clinically stable people living with HIV. Instead, risk assessment should broadly adhere to general population guidelines, with targeted emphasis on individuals with advanced HIV disease [8].

Several key limitations should temper causal inference. First, the sample size was small and the non–COVID-19 comparison group was restricted, leading to substantial statistical imprecision and an elevated risk of type II error. Second, comorbidities and COVID-19–related complications were ascertained from routine clinical documentation and are likely to be under-recorded, particularly for milder manifestations managed outside the hospital system. Third, CD4 cell counts and HIV viral load measurements were obtained during scheduled clinical visits and may not accurately represent immunovirological status at the time of SARS-CoV-2 infection. Fourth, this was a single-centre study, and the findings may not be generalisable to settings with different socioeconomic conditions, patterns of healthcare access, or levels of antiretroviral therapy (ART) coverage. The study should therefore be regarded as a small pilot/exploratory cohort study, not as a definitive assessment of risk factors for COVID-19 among PLWH.

Estimates of post-acute symptom burden in people living with HIV (PLWH) vary substantially across studies [9]. In summary, in this small, prospective Slovak cohort of PLWH receiving ART in a tertiary-care setting, COVID-19 clinical courses during 2020–2021 were predominantly mild, and we did not identify robust associations between outcomes and routinely collected HIV biomarkers. Participants who developed COVID-19 were more likely to have at least one documented comorbidity; however, this finding was not accompanied by severe clinical outcomes in this small cohort.

Larger, multicentre investigations in Central and Eastern Europe, employing standardised severity definitions and more granular socioeconomic and clinical characterisation, are required to elucidate risk modifiers and to support the development of targeted prevention strategies for PLWH.
