Multidimensional Dickman distribution and operator selfdecomposability
Anastasiia S. Kovtun, Nikolai N. Leonenko, Andrey Pepelyshev

Abstract
The one-dimensional Dickman distribution arises in various stochastic models across number theory, combinatorics, physics, and biology. Recently, a definition of the multidimensional Dickman distribution has appeared in the literature, together with its application to approximating the small jumps of multidimensional Lévy processes. In this paper, we extend this definition to a class of vector-valued random elements, which we characterise as fixed points of a specific affine transformation involving a random matrix obtained from the matrix exponential of a uniformly distributed random variable. We prove that these new distributions possess the key properties of infinite divisibility and operator selfdecomposability. Furthermore, we identify several cases where this new distribution arises as a limiting distribution.



Keywords:
multivariate Dickman distribution, operator selfdecomposability, random affine transformation, random difference equation, random matrix

,

,

]
.

Recently, the generalisation of the one-dimensional Dickman distribution to the multidimensional case has appeared in
[
3
]

[
19
]
. In this paper, we propose to extend the definition given in
[
3
]
to a class of vector-valued random elements, which we define, in analogy to the one-dimensional version, as fixed points of a certain affine transformation with random coefficients. In particular, the affine transformation we consider is induced by translating a given point by a random vector and further multiplying it by a factor defined as the operator (matrix) exponential of a uniformly distributed random variable. We refer to the elements of this class as the operator Dickman distributions. The motivation for this extension initially comes from the property of operator selfdecomposability, which we shall demonstrate holds for members of our class. It is also supported by some applications analogous to the classical applications of the one-dimensional Dickman distribution.

2
One-dimensional Dickman distribution

The one-dimensional Dickman distribution with parameter
\theta>0
can be defined as a distribution of a random variable
X_{D}
satisfying the equation

X_{D}\overset{d}{=}U^{1/\theta}(1+X^{\prime}_{D}),

(1)

where the symbol “
\overset{d}{=}
” denotes the equality in distribution,
X^{\prime}_{D}\overset{d}{=}X_{D}
,
U
has the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
and is independent of
X^{\prime}_{D}
, see e.g.
[
37
]
.
Equivalently, the random variable
X_{D}
is the fixed point (in a distributional sense) of the random affine transformation
\cdot\mapsto U^{1/\theta}(1+\cdot)
, where
U
is as above.
We will further denote
GD_{\theta}
the Dickman distribution with parameter
\theta>0
.

The distribution
GD_{\theta}
is absolutely continuous for all
\theta>0
, and its density can be represented as

f_{\theta}(x)=\frac{e^{-\gamma\theta}}{\Gamma(\theta)}\rho_{\theta}(x),\penalty\ x\in\mathbb{R},

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the gamma function,
\gamma=-\Gamma^{\prime}(1)\approx 0.5772
is Euler’s constant, and the function
\rho_{\theta}(x)
satisfies the difference-differential equation

\displaystyle x\rho^{\prime}_{\theta}(x)+(1-\theta)\rho_{\theta}(x)+\theta\rho_{\theta}(x-1)=0,\quad x>1,

(2)

with initial conditions

\displaystyle\rho_{\theta}(x)=0,\quad x\leq 0,

\displaystyle\rho_{\theta}(x)=x^{\theta-1},\quad 0<x\leq 1.

In the case of
\theta=1
, the function
\rho_{1}(\cdot)
is known as the Dickman function. It occurred in the work of Karl Dickman
[
12
]
in the number-theoretical context from which it gained its name. However, it can be found in even earlier Ramanujan’s unpublished notes
[
16
, Ch. 8]
.
The equation (
2
) and its generalisations have been studied in
[
50
]
.

The density
f_{\theta}(x)
of the
GD_{\theta}
distribution has been an object of independent studies, and we recall here a few results related to it.
The asymptotic behaviour of the density
f_{\theta}(x)
for
\theta=1
has been established in
[
10
]
and for general
\theta
in
[
47
, Lem. 4.7.9]
. It was also proven in
[
47
, Thm. 4.7.7]
that the density
f_{\theta}(x)
can be expressed explicitly as follows

\displaystyle f_{\theta}(x)=\frac{e^{-\gamma\theta}}{\Gamma(\theta)}\left(x^{\theta-1}+\sum_{k=1}^{[x]-1}\frac{(-\theta)^{k}}{k!}\idotsint\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}>1\\
t_{1}+\cdots+t_{k}\leq x\end{subarray}}(x-t_{1}-\cdots-t_{k})^{\theta-1}\frac{dt_{1}\ldots dt_{k}}{t_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot t_{k}}\right),

where
x>0
,
[\cdot]
is the integer part function;
see
[
24
, Thm. 1]
and
[
26
, Prop. 1]
for similar representations.
Moreover, it was shown in
[
5
]
that the following recurrent relation holds

f_{\theta}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{e^{-\gamma\theta}}{\Gamma(\theta)}x^{\theta-1},&0<x\leq 1,\\
\frac{e^{-\gamma\theta}}{\Gamma(\theta)}x^{\theta-1}-\theta x^{\theta-1}\int_{0}^{x-1}\frac{f_{\theta}(z)}{(1+z)^{\theta}}dz,&x>1.\end{cases}

Note also that it follows from (
1
) that
X_{D}
can be represented as

X_{D}\overset{d}{=}U_{1}^{1/\theta}+(U_{1}U_{2})^{1/\theta}+(U_{1}U_{2}U_{3})^{1/\theta}+(U_{1}U_{2}U_{3}U_{4})^{1/\theta}+\cdots,

(3)

where
\{U_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is the sequence of mutually independent identically distributed random variables with uniform distribution on
[0,1]
, see e.g.
[
48
]
. The series of the form (
3
) is commonly referred to as a perpetuity and is usually related to a random difference equation, see the equation (
7
) below.
The random difference equations, which allow modelling discrete processes with a random discounting coefficient and random input at each time step, have been studied in a series of works, see, for example,
[
31
,
48
,
13
]
.

The characteristic function of
X_{D}
distributed according to
GD(\theta)
is given by

\psi_{D}(z)=\mathbb{E}e^{izX_{D}}=\exp\left\{\theta\int\limits_{0}^{1}(e^{izu}-1)\frac{du}{u}\right\},\penalty\ z\in\mathbb{R},

(4)

and it follows from (
4
) that the distribution
GD_{\theta}
is infinitely divisible with Lévy measure

\upsilon(du)=\frac{\theta}{u}\mathds{1}_{(0,1)}(u)du.

The
GD_{\theta}
distribution is also self-decomposable with the canonical function
k(x)
given by
k(x)=\theta\mathds{1}_{(0,1)}(x)

[
30
, Cor. 15.11]
.

3
Dickman distributions over
\mathbb{R}^{d}

The multidimensional Dickman distribution (as in Definition
3.1
below) was first introduced in
[
3
]
and further studied in
[
20
]
, see also
[
19
]
. In this section, we generalise the definition given there to a new class of distributions, which we will refer to as the operator Dickman distributions. We further study its properties. In particular, we derive the expression for the characteristic function of the operator Dickman distributions and consequently show that they constitute a subset of infinitely divisible distributions over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We also demonstrate that the class of operator Dickman distributions forms a subset of the set of operator selfdecomposable distributions. Some specific examples of the distributions arising from this class are considered at the end of the section.

First, we introduce some notation which will be used throughout the paper.
For elements
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
of the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
, we denote their inner product by
\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}
and the Euclidean norm of
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
|\mathbf{x}|
. The set
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|\mathbf{x}|=1\}
is the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For any topological space
S
let
\mathscr{B}(S)
denote the Borel sigma-algebra over
S
. Let also
\mathbb{R}_{+}
denote the set of positive real numbers, and
\mathbb{C}
denote the set of complex numbers.
We will also use
\delta
and
\mathds{1}
as standard notation for the Dirac delta function and the indicator function, respectively;
a\wedge b
denotes
\min\{a,b\}
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

The linear transformations of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are usually identified with their matrix representations, and the set of all
d\times d
matrices is denoted by
\mathscr{M}
. Additionally,
\mathscr{M_{+}}\subset\mathscr{M}
(
\mathscr{M}_{-}\subset\mathscr{M}
) denotes the set of all elements of
\mathscr{M}
whose eigenvalues have positive (negative) real parts. Let
I\in\mathscr{M}
and
O\in\mathscr{M}
denote, respectively, the identity and zero transformations, let also
\mathbb{I}=\{cI,c>0\}
be a set of positive multiples of the identity transformation. For any transformation
Q\in\mathscr{M}
, let
Q^{*}
denote its adjoint. For any
Q\in\mathscr{M}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
u\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
define the matrix exponential as follows

e^{tQ}\coloneq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}Q^{k}\quad\text{and}\quad u^{Q}\coloneq e^{Q\log u}.

Let
\|Q\|\coloneq\sup_{|\mathbf{x}|\leq 1}|Q\mathbf{x}|
denote the operator norm of
Q\in\mathscr{M}
.
For any measure
\vartheta
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any invertible operator
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote
T\vartheta
the measure given by
T\vartheta(A)\coloneq\vartheta(T^{-1}(A))
,
A\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Let
\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the set of probability measures over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and for any
\pi\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
let
\hat{\pi}
denote its characteristic function, i.e.
\hat{\pi}(\mathbf{z})=\int\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\mathbf{z}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\pi(d\mathbf{x})
. Let
H\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the set of all probability measures
\nu
such that

\int\nolimits_{|\mathbf{x}|>1}\log|\mathbf{x}|\nu(d\mathbf{x})<\infty,

and let
\mathscr{S}\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the set of all probability distributions concentrated on
d
-dimensional sphere, i.e.
\sigma\in\mathscr{S}
whenever
\sigma(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})=1
. Note that
\mathscr{S}\subset H
.
For a random vector
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
let
\mathscr{L}(\mathbf{X})\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote its probability distribution.

We also denote
ID(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the set of all infinitely divisible distributions. Recall that for any
\varrho\in ID(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the characteristic function of
\varrho
is given by the Lévy-Khintchine formula

\hat{\varrho}(\mathbf{z})=\exp\left[i\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{z}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{z}\cdot A\mathbf{z}+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(e^{i\mathbf{z}\cdot\mathbf{x}}-1-i\mathbf{z}\cdot\mathbf{x}\mathds{1}_{|\mathbf{x}|\leq 1})\ell(d\mathbf{x})\right],\;\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
A\in\mathscr{M}
is a symmetric non-negative definite matrix and
\ell
is the Lévy measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying
\ell(\{0\})=0
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1\wedge|\mathbf{x}|^{2})\ell(d\mathbf{x})<\infty
; the triplet
(\mathbf{a},A,\ell)
is the characteristic triplet of the distribution
\varrho
, see
[
30
]
for more details.

Let us present the definition of the multidimensional Dickman distribution, which was introduced in
[
3
]
.

Definition 3.1

(Multidimensional Dickman distribution)
.

We call the distribution
\mathbb{D}_{\theta,\sigma}=\mathscr{L}(\mathbf{D})
of the random vector
\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{D}_{\theta}^{(\sigma)}
a multidimensional Dickman distribution if
\mathbf{D}
satisfies the distributional equation

\mathbf{D}\overset{d}{=}U^{1/\theta}(\mathbf{D}^{\prime}+\mathbf{V}),

(5)

where
\theta>0
,
\mathbf{D}\overset{d}{=}\mathbf{D}^{\prime}
,
U
has the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
,
\mathscr{L}(\mathbf{V})=\sigma\in\mathscr{S}
, and the random elements
U,\mathbf{V},\mathbf{D}^{\prime}
are mutually independent.

Next, we generalise this definition to a wider class of distributions by substituting the scalar function
u^{1/\theta},\theta>0
with a matrix exponential
u^{Q},Q\in\mathscr{M}_{+}
and a probability measure
\sigma\in\mathscr{S}
with a probability measure
\nu\in H
.

Definition 3.2
.

For any
Q\in\mathscr{M}_{+}
and
\nu\in H
, the
probability distribution
\mathscr{D}(Q,\nu)\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the distribution of a vector
\mathbf{X}
, which satisfies

\mathbf{X}\overset{d}{=}U^{Q}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}+\mathbf{W}),

(6)

where
\mathbf{X}\overset{d}{=}\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
,
U
is uniformly distributed over
[0,1]
,
\mathscr{L}(\mathbf{W})=\nu
, and the random elements
U,\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{W}
are mutually independent.

Moreover, for any
\mathscr{Q}\subseteq\mathscr{M}_{+}
and
\mathscr{H}\subseteq H
let

\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{Q},\mathscr{H})
be a set of distributions defined as

\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{Q},\mathscr{H})=\{\mathscr{D}(Q,\nu),Q\in\mathscr{Q},\nu\in\mathscr{H}\},

and the set
\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{\mathscr{M}}_{+},H)
will be referred to as the class of
operator Dickman distributions
.

Remark 1
.

Note that the random operator
U^{Q}
is well-defined with probability one since the operator
s^{Q}
,

, is well-defined for any
s>0
. The properties of the exponential operators, which we will use later, are investigated in
[

Notice that for any
Q\in\mathscr{M}_{+}
the map
\gamma(a,\mathbf{b}):\cdot\mapsto a^{Q}(\cdot+\mathbf{b})
with

and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
defines an affine transformation on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover the family
\gamma=\{\gamma(a,\mathbf{b}),a>0,\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
forms a non-commutative group under the composition, which is isomorphic to the group
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with group operation

defined as
(a_{1},\mathbf{b}_{1})*(a_{2},\mathbf{b}_{2}):=(a_{1}a_{2},(a_{1}a_{2})^{Q}\mathbf{b}_{1}+a_{2}^{Q}\mathbf{b}_{2})
. We can now equip the group
\gamma
with a probability measure defined as the product measure
Leb_{1}\times\nu
, where
Leb_{1}
is the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
and
\nu\in H
is some probability measure. Therefore, the vector
\mathbf{X}
, defined in (
6
), can be viewed as the distributional fixed point of the random affine transformation described above. For
d=1
this problem was studied in multiple works, see
[
48
]
for a short overview and references, and
[
2
]
for a generalisation to non-affine transformations.

As another point of view on Definition
3.2
we can consider the difference equation

\mathbf{Y}_{n}=M_{n}\mathbf{Y}_{n-1}+\bm{\xi}_{n},\;n\geq 1,

(7)

with some initial condition
\mathbf{Y}_{0}
and random coefficients

and
\bm{\xi}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

.
Indeed, by iteration, it is easy to see that the solution of (

\mathbf{X}\overset{d}{=}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(U_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot U_{k})^{Q}\mathbf{W}_{k},

where
U_{1},U_{2},\ldots
and
\mathbf{W}_{1},\mathbf{W}_{2},\dots
are independent sequences of mutually independent random elements, sampled from the uniform distribution on

and the measure
\nu
, respectively. Thus, the solution

of (

given by (

and

The existence of a weak limit of

In particular, Kesten

. However, as shown in

It is evident that the set of multidimensional Dickman distributions

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{1}\psi((ur)^{Q^{*}}\bm{\omega})\hat{\nu}((ur)^{Q^{*}}\bm{\omega})du

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}\int\limits_{0}^{r}\psi(u^{Q^{*}}\bm{\omega})\hat{\nu}(u^{Q^{*}}\bm{\omega})du.

Since the latter expression is differentiable with respect to
r
, we can obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\psi(r^{Q^{*}}\bm{\omega})=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int\limits_{0}^{r}\psi(u^{Q^{*}}\bm{\omega})\hat{\nu}(u^{Q^{*}}\bm{\omega})du+\frac{1}{r}\psi(r^{Q^{*}}\bm{\omega})\hat{\nu}(r^{Q^{*}}\bm{\omega})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}\psi(r^{Q^{*}}\bm{\omega})(\hat{\nu}(r^{Q^{*}}\bm{\omega})-1),

or, alternatively,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\log\psi(r^{Q^{*}}\bm{\omega})=\frac{1}{r}(\hat{\nu}(r^{Q^{*}}\bm{\omega})-1).

It follows from the above that there exists
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{C}
such that

\displaystyle\log\psi(r^{Q^{*}}\bm{\omega})=\int\limits_{0}^{r}(\hat{\nu}(s^{Q^{*}}\bm{\omega})-1)\frac{ds}{s}+g(\bm{\omega})=\int\limits_{0}^{1}(\hat{\nu}((sr)^{Q^{*}}\bm{\omega})-1)\frac{ds}{s}+g(\bm{\omega}).

Letting
r=0
we can deduce from
\psi(\mathbf{0})=1
that
g(\bm{\omega})=0
for all
\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Returning to the variable
\mathbf{z}
, we finally get

\displaystyle\log\psi(\mathbf{z})=\int\limits_{0}^{1}(\hat{\nu}(s^{Q^{*}}\mathbf{z})-1)\frac{ds}{s}=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int\limits_{0}^{1}(e^{is^{Q^{*}}\mathbf{z}\cdot\mathbf{x}}-1)\nu(d\mathbf{x})\frac{ds}{s},

and the result follows since
\left(s^{Q^{*}}\right)^{*}=s^{Q}
.
∎

\hat{\nu}(\mathbf{w})=Y_{d}(|\mathbf{w}|),\;\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

see, for example,
[
38
]
.
Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\theta^{-1}\varphi(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{1}\left(Y_{d}(s|\mathbf{z}|)-1\right)\frac{ds}{s}=\int\limits_{0}^{|\mathbf{z}|}\left(Y_{d}(s)-1\right)\frac{ds}{s}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{1}\left(Y_{d}(s)-1\right)\frac{ds}{s}+\int\limits_{1}^{\infty}Y_{d}(s)\frac{ds}{s}-\int\limits_{|\mathbf{z}|}^{\infty}Y_{d}(s)\frac{ds}{s}-\log|\mathbf{z}|.

Since the function
Y_{d}(\cdot)
satisfies

\displaystyle Y_{d}(s)=1+O(s^{2}),\;s\to 0;

\displaystyle Y_{d}(s)=\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)^{\frac{1-d}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)\pi^{-1/2}\left[\cos\left(s-\frac{\pi(d-1)}{4}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)\right],\;s\to\infty,

we get (
18
), see
[
38
, Prop. 2.1]
.

Therefore, we can rewrite

\displaystyle\hat{\mathscr{D}}(\mathbf{z})=

\displaystyle\exp\left(\theta\alpha_{d}+\log|\mathbf{z}|^{-\theta}-\theta\int\limits_{1}^{\infty}Y_{d}(s|\mathbf{z}|)\frac{ds}{s}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\theta\alpha_{d}}|\mathbf{z}|^{-\theta}\exp\left(-\theta\int\limits_{1}^{\infty}Y_{d}(s|\mathbf{z}|)\frac{ds}{s}\right).

Denote now
\tilde{F}_{k}(s)=s^{-d}\mathds{1}\{1<s<k\}
,
k\geq 2
and
\tilde{F}(s)=s^{-d}\mathds{1}\{s>1\}
. Let also
F_{k}(\mathbf{x})=\tilde{F}_{k}(|\mathbf{x}|)
and
F(\mathbf{x})=\tilde{F}(|\mathbf{x}|)
. Note that
\{F_{k},k\geq 2\}
is a sequence of functions such that

1.

F_{k}(\mathbf{x})\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

2.

F_{k}(\mathbf{x})\overset{L_{2}}{\to}F(\mathbf{x})
as
k\to\infty
,

3.

F_{k}
is radial for any
k\geq 2
and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\mathbf{z}\cdot\mathbf{x}}F_{k}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}=(2\pi)^{d/2}|\mathbf{z}|^{1-d/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{F}_{k}(s)J_{d/2-1}(s|\mathbf{z}|)s^{d/2}ds,

see
[
42
]
; note that for
d=1
this relation is obvious due to properties of even functions.

Thus, the function
-\theta\int\nolimits_{1}^{\infty}Y_{d}(s|\mathbf{z}|)\frac{ds}{s}
is the Fourier transform of the function
-\theta\zeta_{d}F(\mathbf{x})\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(see
[
42
]
for more details on the theory of the Fourier transform on
L_{2}
), and we can write

-\theta\int\limits_{1}^{\infty}Y_{d}(s|\mathbf{z}|)\frac{ds}{s}=-\theta\zeta_{d}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\mathbf{z}\cdot\mathbf{x}}|\mathbf{x}|^{-d}\mathds{1}\{|\mathbf{x}|>1\}d\mathbf{x},

understanding the integral on the right as a limit in
L_{2}
of integrals

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\mathbf{z}\cdot\mathbf{x}}|\mathbf{x}|^{-d}\mathds{1}\{1<|\mathbf{x}|<k\}d\mathbf{x},\;k\to\infty.

We arrive at

\displaystyle e^{-\theta\alpha_{d}}\hat{\mathscr{D}}(\mathbf{z})=

\displaystyle|\mathbf{z}|^{-\theta}\exp\left(-\theta\zeta_{d}\int\limits_{|\mathbf{x}|>1}e^{i\mathbf{z}\cdot\mathbf{x}}|\mathbf{x}|^{-d}d\mathbf{x}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\mathbf{z}|^{-\theta}\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-\theta\zeta_{d})^{n}}{n!}\idotsint\limits_{D_{n}}e^{i\mathbf{z}\cdot(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\cdots+\mathbf{x}_{n})}\frac{d\mathbf{x}_{1}\ldots d\mathbf{x}_{n}}{|\mathbf{x}_{1}|^{d}\cdot\ldots\cdot|\mathbf{x}_{n}|^{d}}\right),

where
D_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},

n\geq 1
is defined as above.
Notice first that the expression in the brackets above can be written as

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\mathbf{z}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\theta\zeta_{d})^{n}}{n!}F^{(*n)}(\mathbf{x})\right),

where
(∗n)
denotes the
n

F^{(*0)}(\cdot)=\delta_{\mathbf{0}}(\cdot)
. Moreover, the series above converges in
L_{2}
, indeed

\displaystyle\biggl\|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\theta\zeta_{d})^{n}}{n!}F^{(*n)}(\mathbf{x})\biggl\|_{L_{2}}\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\theta\zeta_{d})^{n}}{n!}\|F^{(*n)}\|_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\theta\zeta_{d})^{n}}{n!}\|\hat{F}\|_{L_{2}}^{n}=e^{-\theta\zeta_{d}\|\hat{F}\|_{L_{2}}}<\infty,

where we have used the triangle inequality, the Plancherel theorem and the convolution property of the Fourier transform, see again
[
42
]
.

Notice also that
|\mathbf{z}|^{-\theta}
is the Fourier transform of a generalised (on a space of Schwartz functions) function
g(\mathbf{y})
as defined above
[
15
, p. 194]
.

Thus, it follows now from the convolution property of the Fourier transform (on a space of generalised functions this time) that
\hat{\mathscr{D}}(\mathbf{z})
is the Fourier transform of a generalised function
f(\mathbf{x})
as defined in (
17
). Here, we also used the fact that the space
L_{2}
constitutes a subspace of the space of generalised functions.

Moreover, as noted in the discussion prior to the theorem, the distribution
\mathscr{D}(\frac{1}{\theta}I,\nu)
is absolutely continuous. It means that there exists

,
q\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}q(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}=1,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\mathbf{z}\cdot\mathbf{x}}q(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}=\hat{\mathscr{D}}(\mathbf{z}).

It follows then that the functions
f(\mathbf{x})
and
q(\mathbf{x})
should coincide in the weak sense, meaning

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathbf{x})\phi(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}q(\mathbf{x})\phi(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}

for every function
\phi(\mathbf{x})
belonging to a space of test functions. Thus, it is not hard to show, using the relations above and choosing
0\leq\phi_{K}(\mathbf{x})\leq 1,

\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\phi_{K}(\mathbf{x})=1,

|\mathbf{x}|\leq K
, where
K>0
, that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}=1
. The fact that
f(\mathbf{x})\geq 0
for almost every (with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
)
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
follows similarly. We can then conclude that the equality
f(\mathbf{x})=q(\mathbf{x})
holds in
L_{1}
and, consequently, almost everywhere with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Therefore, formula (
17
) gives an explicit expression for the density function of
\mathbf{X}
.
∎

Proposition 3.16
.

Let
\theta>0
and
\nu\in H
be given. Let

,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denote the probability density function (given as a generalised function) of the

\sum_{k=1}^{d}x_{k}\frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{k}}=(\theta-d)f(\mathbf{x})-\theta\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\nu(d\mathbf{y})

Proof.

Notice that, due to Proposition

\log\hat{f}(\mathbf{z})=\theta\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int\limits_{0}^{1}(e^{is\mathbf{z}\cdot\mathbf{x}}-1)\nu(d\mathbf{x})\frac{ds}{s},\;\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Consequently,

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}z_{k}\frac{\partial\hat{f}(\mathbf{z})}{\partial z_{k}}=\theta\hat{f}(\mathbf{z})\hat{\nu}(\mathbf{z})-\theta\hat{f}(\mathbf{z}).

The result follows now by properties of the Fourier transform on the space of generalised functions, see, for example,
[

4
Some applications of the operator Dickman distributions

The one-dimensional Dickman distribution has applications in various probabilistic problems and beyond, as discussed in Section
2
. Moreover, the multidimensional Dickman distribution from Definition
3.1
was proven to be useful in approximating small jumps of some multidimensional Lévy process. This section presents examples of how the above-mentioned applications can be extended to the case of operator Dickman distributions.

Hereafter “
\overset{v}{\to}
” and “
\overset{w}{\to}
” denote respectively vague and weak convergence of measures. In particular,
\mu_{n}\overset{v}{\to}\mu
,
n\to\infty
, for measures
\mu_{n}
and
\mu
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whenever

\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathbf{x})\mu_{n}(d\mathbf{x})\to\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathbf{x})\mu(d\mathbf{x}),\;n\to\infty,

for all bounded, continuous maps
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
vanishing on a neighbourhood of
\mathbf{0}

[
30
, p. 41]
.
Moreover, by convention, “
\overset{w}{\to}
”, when applied to random elements, means the weak convergence of their corresponding probability measures.

First, we present the following multidimensional counterpart for
[
47
, Example 4.7.5]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Consider a sequence
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\ldots
of independent random variables such that
\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon_{k}=1)=1-\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon_{k}=0)=p_{k}
,
k\geq 1
, and assume that

\displaystyle p_{k}\in[0,1),\;k\geq 1,

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}p_{k}=0,\;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}=\infty.

Let
L(n)
denote the index of
n
-th
1
in the sequence
\varepsilon_{k},k\geq 1
.

Fix now
Q\in\mathscr{M}_{+}
and assume that

is as in the Example

. Then

(L(n))^{Q}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(L(n+k))^{-Q}\mathbf{W}_{k}\overset{w}{\to}\mathbf{X},\text{ as }n\to\infty,

where
\mathbf{W}_{1},\mathbf{W}_{2},\dots
is the sequence of independent identically distributed random elements with a common law
\nu\in H
, independent also of a sequence
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\ldots
, and
\mathscr{L}(\mathbf{X})=\mathscr{D}(Q,\nu)

Proof.

By the assumption
Q=S\Lambda S^{-1}
for some invertible matrix

and a diagonal matrix

,

for any

,

, it follows from
[

(L(n))^{a_{j}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(L(n+k))^{-a_{j}}\overset{w}{\to}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(U_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot U_{k})^{a_{j}}\text{ as }n\to\infty,

for any

Rewrite now

(L(n))^{Q}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(L(n+k))^{-Q}\mathbf{W}_{k}=S(L(n))^{\Lambda}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(L(n+k))^{-\Lambda}S^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{k}.

The result follows then from the continuous mapping theorem
[
4
, Thm. 2.7]
,
[
4
, Thm. 2.8]
and (
8
).
∎

Example 4.2
.

Notice that the sequence
L(n),n\geq 1
, is an integer-valued Markov chain with transition probability function given by

P(m,k)=\mathbb{P}(L(n)=k|L(n-1)=m)=p_{k}\prod_{j=m+1}^{k-1}(1-p_{j}),\;k\geq m+1,

where
n\geq 1
and
L(0)\coloneq 0
.
Let us consider, for example,
p_{k}=\alpha/k,k\geq 1
for some

. It then holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(L(n)-L(n-1)

\displaystyle=r|L(n-1)=m)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{m+1}\right)\cdot\ldots\cdot\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{m+r-1}\right)\frac{\alpha}{m+r},

and we can recognise that, conditionally on

, the random variable

,

, has generalised Sibuya distribution

as defined in
[
32
]
.

The sequence

has been studied extensively in relation to record epochs in
[
47
]
.

Recall now that it has been proven in
[
7
]

that the Dickman distribution can be used to approximate small jumps of Lévy processes when the Brownian approximation is not applicable. It therefore makes simulation of the Lévy process (or the corresponding infinitely divisible random variable) easier. The analogous approximation for some class of multidimensional Lévy processes has been established in

]
using the multidimensional Dickman distribution defined as in (

with

and

. Note that our aim here is not to establish the most general case, but rather to demonstrate the possible scope of applications of the newly defined distributions.

We denote
\bar{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
a one-point compactification of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\bar{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}=\bar{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
,
\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{+}=(0,\infty]

the set

given by

S_{Q}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}:|r^{Q}\mathbf{x}|>1\text{ for every }r>1\},

see the proof of
[

To present the main result, we need the following generalisation of

.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mu,\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots
be Borel measures on

\mu(B)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int\limits_{\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\mathds{1}_{B}(r^{Q}\mathbf{x})\sigma(d\mathbf{x})\lambda(dr),\;B\in\mathscr{B}(\bar{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}),

\mu_{n}\overset{v}{\to}\mu\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\mu_{n}(A_{t,D})\to\mu(A_{t,D}),\;n\to\infty

and

The proof repeats the proof of

.

Recall now that for

and

.

Fix

and
\sigma\in\mathscr{S}
. Let

Proof.

(iii)

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{x}c(\mathbf{x})\mu_{n}(d\mathbf{x})=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{x}c(\mathbf{x})M(d\mathbf{x})

Remark 10
