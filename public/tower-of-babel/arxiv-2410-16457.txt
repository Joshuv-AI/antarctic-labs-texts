The circular law for random band matrices with arbitrary doubly stochastic variance profiles Thanks: A conditional Lean formalization is available; see Section 1.5 .
Yi HAN

Abstract
. We study the circular law for non-Hermitian random band matrices with independent entries and arbitrary doubly stochastic variance profiles. For matrices of size n n and bandwidth n γ n^{\gamma} , we prove the circular law when γ > 5 6 \gamma>\frac{5}{6} for Gaussian entries, or γ > 8 9 \gamma>\frac{8}{9} for subgaussian entries under bounded-density assumptions. These thresholds improve the earlier sufficient conditions γ > 32 33 \gamma>\frac{32}{33} for block band matrices and γ > 33 34 \gamma>\frac{33}{34} for periodic band matrices. Subsequent work proves the circular law under the optimal condition of diverging bandwidth for specific open-boundary models [ 24 ] and periodic models [ 26 ] . Those results rely on local band structure and do not cover the arbitrary doubly stochastic variance profiles considered here. We also derive weak delocalization estimates for right eigenvectors in the Gaussian case. Our main technical input consists of polynomial lower bounds for certain intermediate small singular values, requiring no structural assumptions on the variance profile beyond double stochasticity. We also prove a circular law for the root empirical spectral measure of products of an arbitrary number of i.i.d. matrices, under bounded-density assumptions and a finite fourth moment. For smaller numbers of factors, we apply the same strategy as in our main band-matrix theorem to the cyclic linearization. For larger numbers of factors, the simpler structure of the product model allows us to apply the singular-value universality theorem of Hanin and Jiang [ 27 ] directly, together with a shifted log-determinant comparison. The two methods apply in overlapping ranges and together cover all growth rates of the number of factors.



1.
Introduction

For an
n\times n
matrix
A
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}(A),\cdots,\lambda_{n}(A)\in\mathbb{C}
we denote its empirical measure of eigenvalues by

\mu_{A}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\lambda_{i}(A)},

where
\delta_{z}
is the Dirac measure at point
z
.

For a symmetric random matrix
A
, the convergence of
\mu_{A}
to the semicircle distribution under mild assumptions dates back to the celebrated work of Wigner
[
47
]
. The spectrum of symmetric random matrices can essentially be determined via the method of moments.
For non-Hermitian random matrix
A
, the convergence of
\mu_{A}
does not follow from method of moments arguments (see for example
[
9
]
), and fundamentally new ideas are needed.
When all the entries of
A=(a_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
are i.i.d. with mean zero and variance one, the circular law theorem, i.e. the convergence of
\mu_{A/\sqrt{n}}
to the uniform distribution on the unit disk in the complex plane, is one of the fundamental theorems in modern random matrix theory. This circular law was proven under increasing generality in a series of works including
[
18
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
43
]
,
[
42
]
, and we refer to the references in these works and
[
9
]
for a more complete list.
Beyond dense matrices with i.i.d. entries, the circular law was subsequently proven for sparse i.i.d. matrices
[
8
]
,
[
36
]

[
39
]
, for sparse regular digraphs
[
32
]
, and for some random matrices with inhomogeneous variance profile
[
12
]
. A more complete list of references can be found in the review
[
46
]
.

Proving the circular law for sparse non-Hermitian random matrices with arbitrary inhomogeneous variance profiles remains challenging. Many entries in these models are identically zero. General techniques for dense inhomogeneous matrices were developed in
[
13
]
,
[
12
]
, but their assumptions do not cover arbitrary sparse profiles. The guiding examples of sparse inhomogeneous matrices are random band matrices, which we will define shortly. Their Hermitian analogue, namely Hermitian Random band matrices, play a fundamental role in modern mathematical physics. They serve as prototypical models of Anderson transition: when the bandwidth scales like
n^{\gamma}
for
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
, the eigenvectors are expected to be delocalized and eigenvalues have GOE local statistics; whereas a localized phase and Poisson statistics should occur when
\gamma<\frac{1}{2}
. Some very recent progress on this topic can be found in
[
48
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
40
]
and the references therein.

In this work we study non-Hermitian random band matrices, focusing on global universality, i.e. convergence of the empirical spectral distribution to the circular law, for arbitrary doubly stochastic variance profiles without a prescribed local band structure. The works
[
30
]
and
[
45
]
justified the circular law for certain non-Hermitian band matrices with bandwidth
n^{\gamma}
, with sufficient power-law conditions
\gamma>\frac{32}{33}
and
\gamma>\frac{33}{34}
, respectively. The main contribution of this paper is to consider narrower bandwidth and expand the variety of models where global universality holds: we show
\gamma>\frac{5}{6}
is enough for the circular law limit for Gaussian entries whenever the variance profile is doubly stochastic.

1.1.
Circular law for general models

Definition 1.1
.

A real or complex random variable
\xi
of mean zero, variance one is said to be
K
-subGaussian if

\mathbb{E}\exp(|\xi|^{2}/K^{2})\leq 2.

For
p>0
, a random variable
\xi
has a finite
p
-th moment if
\mathbb{E}|\xi|^{p}<\infty
.

We consider the following very general definition of inhomogeneous matrices:

Definition 1.2

(General model)
.

We define an ensemble of inhomogeneous random matrices as follows:
X=(x_{ij})
is a
n\times n
random matrix with independent entries. For each
(i,j)\in[n]^{2}
,
x_{ij}
is distributed as
b_{ij}\xi
, where
b_{ij}\geq 0
and
\xi
has mean zero, variance one, and is
K
-subgaussian for some fixed
K>0
. The bandwidth of this general matrix model is defined by

b_{n}^{-1}=\sup_{(i,j)\in[n]^{2}}b_{ij}^{2}.

We assume that
X
has a doubly stochastic variance profile:

\mathbb{E}[XX^{*}]=\mathbb{E}[X^{*}X]=I_{n}.

In other words, we assume that

\sum_{j\in[n]}b_{ij}^{2}=1\quad\forall i\in[n],\quad\sum_{i\in[n]}b_{ij}^{2}=1\quad\forall j\in[n].

The generality of this definition is already illustrated by the following subclasses of models: consider
G_{n}=([n],\mathcal{E})
a
b_{n}
- regular directed graph on
n
vertices. Then we set
b_{ij}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{b_{n}}}
if
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}
and
b_{ij}=0
otherwise. Observe that the graph
G_{n}
can be arbitrary and we never require the structure of
G_{n}
to be sufficiently pseudo-random.

Example 1.3
.

(Well-mixing variance profiles) We first review some typically used variance profiles for a random band matrix in its non Hermitian form, that have rapid mixing properties. The first example is the following defined periodic band matrix.
Let

X
be an
n\times n
matrix. We say
X
is sampled from a block band matrix ensemble with bandwidth
b_{n}
if it has the form

(1.1)

X=\begin{pmatrix}D_{1}&U_{2}&&&T_{m}\\
T_{1}&D_{2}&U_{3}&&\\
&T_{2}&D_{3}&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&U_{m}\\
U_{1}&&&T_{m-1}&D_{m}\end{pmatrix}

where the unfilled sites are set zero. The blocks
D_{1},U_{1},T_{1},\cdots,D_{m},U_{m},T_{m}
are
b_{n}/3\times b_{n}/3
independent random matrices with i.i.d. entries having the same distribution
\frac{1}{\sqrt{b_{n}}}\xi
, where
\xi
is a standard Gaussian.

The second example is periodic band matrices defined as follows. Let

X
be an
n\times n
square matrix. We say
X
is sampled from a periodic band matrix model with bandwidth
b_{n}
if
x_{ij}=0
for any
\frac{b_{n}-1}{2}<|i-j|<n-\frac{b_{n}-1}{2}
, and
x_{ij}
are i.i.d. copies of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{b_{n}}}\xi
if
|i-j|\leq\frac{b_{n}-1}{2}
or
|i-j|\geq n-\frac{b_{n}-1}{2}
. Here
\xi
is a standard gaussian and we assume
\frac{b_{n}-1}{2}
is an integer.

In some other concrete settings of applications, and as a conceptual question itself, it is of major interest to study variance profiles that do not have good mixing properties. Two examples are a cyclic shift of a periodic band profile and the variance profile of a cyclic product linearization:

Example 1.4
.

(Variance profiles without good mixing) For a given bandwidth
b_{n}
such that
n
is divisible by
b_{n}
, we can consider the following two variance profiles: (1) a shifted periodic band matrix, where we additionally assume that
b_{n}
is odd and take

b_{ij}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{b_{n}}}\mathbf{1}\left\{\min_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|j-i-3b_{n}-kn|\leq\frac{b_{n}-1}{2}\right\}.

This is obtained by cyclically shifting each row of the periodic variance profile to the right by
3b_{n}
positions. Each row and each column has exactly
b_{n}
nonzero entries, so both variance sums equal one. (2) A variance profile from matrix product, where
b_{ij}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{b_{n}}}\mathbf{1}(\lfloor\frac{j-1}{b_{n}}\rfloor-\lfloor\frac{i-1}{b_{n}}\rfloor=1\text{ or }1-\frac{n}{b_{n}})
, which is the variance profile of the cyclic linearization (
2.2
) with
n/b_{n}
blocks of size
b_{n}
, normalized to be doubly stochastic.

The main results of this paper are as follows.

Theorem 1.5

(Circular law for general model)
.

Let
X
be the general inhomogeneous random matrix model defined in Definition
1.2
, and we assume that

(1)

Either
\xi
has Gaussian distribution, and
b_{n}\geq n^{\frac{5}{6}+\epsilon}
for some
\epsilon>0
,

(2)

or
\xi
is
K
-subgaussian for some
K>0
,
b_{n}\geq n^{\frac{8}{9}+\epsilon}
for some
\epsilon>0
, and
\xi
is either real-valued with a distributional density on
\mathbb{R}
bounded by some
L>0
, or
\xi
has independent real and imaginary parts, both having a distributional density bounded by some
L>0
.

Then the ESD
\mu_{X}
of
X
converges in probability to the circular law.

Remark 1.6
.

The later works
[
24
]
and
[
26
]
establish the circular law under the optimal bandwidth condition
b_{n}\to\infty
for open-boundary block models and specific periodic scalar and full-block models, respectively, assuming bounded densities and finite moments. The revised work
[
25
, Theorem 1.6]
proves the circular law for real or circular complex Gaussian entries when
b_{n}\geq n^{2/3+\epsilon}
and the variance profile is either symmetric or invariant under a transitive group of simultaneous index permutations. Its generic bounded-density subgaussian result gives the exponent
8/9+\epsilon
, with improvements under additional moment matching. Theorem
1.5
requires neither these symmetry assumptions nor a prescribed local band geometry. Its variance-profile assumptions therefore remain more general than those of these subsequent results.

1.2.
Weak delocalization estimates

A major component of our proof is to bound the diagonal entries of the resolvent
\mathcal{G}_{z}(\eta)
of the Hermitian dilation (
3.1
) of
X-zI_{n}
, at scales
\Im\eta\ll 1
. Here
z
is the matrix shift and
\eta\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
is the resolvent spectral parameter. Although our estimates do not reach the optimal scale
\Im\eta\sim n^{-1+\epsilon}
, they yield weak delocalization for right eigenvectors in the Gaussian case under arbitrary doubly stochastic variance profiles.

We introduce the most general model where our delocalization statement is valid.

Theorem 1.7

(Weak delocalization for Gaussian case)
.

Consider a general random matrix ensemble in Definition
1.2
. Assume that
b_{n}\geq n^{\epsilon}
for some sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
. Then for any (sufficiently small)
\mathbf{c}>0
the following holds. If
\xi
has (real or complex) Gaussian distribution, then with probability at least
1-n^{-10}
, all right eigenvectors
\psi
of
X
with unit
L^{2}
norm satisfy

\|\psi\|_{\infty}\leq(b_{n})^{-1/10}n^{\mathbf{c}}.

Remark 1.8
.

(Optimality and generality of delocalization upper bound)
Theorem
1.7
wins by generality but may be suboptimal for specific instances.
In the i.i.d. setting where
b_{n}=n
, this estimate is clearly suboptimal: for a random matrix with i.i.d. entries, complete delocalization (i.e.
\|\psi\|_{\infty}\leq n^{-1/2+\epsilon})
has been proven in
[
37
]
, see also
[
2
]
for the inhomogeneous case and
[
3
]
for the elliptic case. For an arbitrary doubly stochastic variance profile, this weak delocalization result is new.

1.3.
Product circular law

Combining the approach developed for our band-matrix theorem with singular-value universality for longer products, we obtain a product circular law with no restriction on the number of factors:

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
(m_{n})
be any sequence of positive integers, and let

X_{1},\ldots,X_{m_{n}}
be independent
n\times n
random matrices with
i.i.d. entries distributed as
\xi/\sqrt{n}
. Assume that

\mathbb{E}\xi=0
,
\mathbb{E}|\xi|^{2}=1
, and

\mathbb{E}|\xi|^{4}<\infty
.
Suppose either that
\xi
is real with a density bounded by
L>0
,
or that its real and imaginary parts are independent and both have
densities bounded by
L>0
. Set

P_{n}:=X_{1}\cdots X_{m_{n}},

and define the
m_{n}
-th-root empirical spectral measure of
P_{n}
by

\nu_{P_{n}}^{(m_{n})}:=\frac{1}{nm_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{\zeta^{m_{n}}=\lambda_{j}(P_{n})}\delta_{\zeta},

where the eigenvalues and their roots are counted with algebraic
multiplicity. Then
\nu_{P_{n}}^{(m_{n})}
converges in probability to the
circular law as
n\to\infty
.

The proof combines two mechanisms in overlapping parameter ranges.
The cyclic linearization has dimension
N=nm_{n}
and bandwidth
b_{N}=n
.
For a fixed
0<\epsilon<1/16
, the first mechanism applies when

m_{n}\leq n^{1/8-\epsilon}
, equivalently

b_{N}\geq N^{8/(9-8\epsilon)}
, with a bandwidth exponent strictly larger
than
8/9
. In this range, we apply the same strategy as in
Theorem
1.5
: singular-value comparison with a free
probability limit, together with control of small singular values of
the cyclic linearization. This gives Proposition
4.4

in Section
4
. The transfer-operator method in

[
26
]
motivates the expectation that the product circular law holds for every growth rate of
m_{n}
.
For larger products, we use a different argument, developed in Section
6
, which applies
whenever
m_{n}/\log n\to\infty
, equivalently
b_{N}\log b_{N}=o(N)
.
The simpler product structure allows us to apply the singular-value
universality theorem of Hanin and Jiang
[
27
, Theorem 1]

directly, together with a comparison of shifted log determinants and
singular-value statistics using one independent factor. The normalized
comparison error is
O(\log n/m_{n})
. The two mechanisms overlap whenever

m_{n}/\log n\to\infty
and
m_{n}\leq n^{1/8-\epsilon}
.
In the proof, we use the convenient cutoff
m_{n}>n^{1/16}
for the
second mechanism, equivalently
b_{N}<N^{16/17}
; this cutoff lies within
the overlap for
0<\epsilon<1/16
. Together the two arguments yield the
circular law for every growth rate of
m_{n}
, including
m_{n}\gg n
.

Remark 1.10

(Moment and density assumptions)
.

The least singular value estimate in Theorem
2.2

requires only a finite second moment, whereas the resolvent comparison
in Proposition

requires a finite third moment.
The fourth moment in Theorem

enters only through the

, used in Section

. We expect

For complex entries, the least singular value estimate in

, proved in Section

,
requires a bounded density for only one of the
independent real and imaginary components. Accordingly,
Proposition

also allows this weaker assumption for

. Requiring both component densities to be

1.9
allows us to invoke the complex

[

.
We expect this
singular-value result to extend to the one-component density
assumption, which would give the same relaxation here; we do not
pursue that adaptation in this paper.

For a fixed number of factors, the product circular law was proved in

[
35
]
under a finite
2+\eta
moment assumption. A local
circular law and universality of linear statistics for i.i.d. matrix
products were subsequently established in
[

]
and

[

]
, respectively. For related work on products with

and

, we refer to

,

1.4.
Main ideas and outline

We use Girko’s Hermitization and the replacement principle of Tao and Vu, recalled in Theorem
4.1
; see
[
9
]
for background. Write
X_{z}=X-zI_{n}
, and let
G
be an
n\times n
complex Ginibre matrix with entry variance
n^{-1}
. With singular values ordered as
\sigma_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{n}
, the main task is to prove

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\sigma_{j}(X_{z})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\sigma_{j}(G-zI_{n})\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{in probability}

for every fixed
z\neq 0
. This supplies the almost-everywhere convergence of normalized log determinants required by the replacement principle.

Earlier band-matrix proofs
[
30
,
45
]
combined singular-value comparison in Kolmogorov distance with exponentially small lower bounds on the least singular value, obtaining the sufficient bandwidth exponents
32/33
and
33/34
, respectively. We use the least singular value estimate of Theorem
2.1
and improve the control of intermediate singular values, so that only a much smaller set of singular values requires this crude lower bound.

The starting point is the Hermitian dilation
\mathcal{Y}_{z}
in (
3.1
). For the Gaussian model, we first replace the diagonal entries by independent circular complex Gaussians with matching variances. The normalized trace
m^{o}_{z,\mathrm{free}}(\eta)
of the resulting free resolvent is independent of the variance profile under the doubly stochastic assumption and equals the limit for the Ginibre reference. We compare the Gaussian resolvent with its free counterpart using
[
6
]
, and control the change of diagonal by the perturbation estimate (
3.5
). In the non-Gaussian case, the trace universality argument of
[
10
]
first compares with a Gaussian model having matching first and second real moments. These comparisons provide quantitative bounds at mesoscopic scales, without requiring a quantitative stability estimate for the matrix Dyson equation. This allows the argument to cover arbitrary doubly stochastic profiles, including those without good mixing.

Under the respective assumptions of Theorem
1.5
, Propositions
3.2
and
3.4
control the normalized resolvent trace down to the singular-value scales

a_{n}=b_{n}^{-\alpha}n^{\mathbf{c}},\qquad\alpha=\begin{cases}1/5,&\text{Gaussian entries},\\
1/8,&\text{non-Gaussian entries},\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{c}>0
can be arbitrarily small. A bound on the trace at
\eta=ia_{n}
yields the counting estimate

\#\{j:\sigma_{j}(X_{z})<a_{n}\}\leq Cna_{n}

with high probability, as made precise in Corollaries
3.3
and
3.5
. Thus all but
O(na_{n})
singular values have a polynomial lower bound. More generally, a trace bound at scale
n^{-\tau}
gives an
O(n^{1-\tau})
count of small singular values.

We split the logarithmic sum at the numerical cutoff
a_{n}
. For fixed
z\neq 0
, Theorem
2.1
bounds the logarithmic size of the remaining small singular values by
C_{z}n^{1+3\kappa}b_{n}^{-1}\log b_{n}
, with high probability, for any sufficiently small
\kappa>0
. Combining this with the count gives

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\sigma_{j}(X_{z})<a_{n}}|\log\sigma_{j}(X_{z})|\leq C_{z}n^{1+\mathbf{c}+3\kappa}b_{n}^{-(1+\alpha)}\log b_{n}.

Choosing
\mathbf{c}
and
\kappa
small makes this vanish when
b_{n}\geq n^{5/6+\epsilon}
in the Gaussian case and
b_{n}\geq n^{8/9+\epsilon}
in the non-Gaussian case. Above
a_{n}
, the polynomial Kolmogorov comparison in Theorem
3.6
, together with a deterministic operator-norm bound on an event of high probability, controls the truncated logarithmic sum. The logarithm costs only an
O(\log n)
factor. The same truncation for
G-zI_{n}
then proves the required log-determinant comparison.

The counting argument also gives a conditional perspective on the method: if the trace bound were available down to
b_{n}^{-1+\delta}
for arbitrarily small
\delta>0
, only
O(nb_{n}^{-1+\delta})
singular values would require the least singular value estimate of Theorem
2.1
. Their normalized logarithmic contribution would vanish for
b_{n}\geq n^{1/2+\epsilon}
. This is a conditional sufficient range for this method, rather than a necessary bandwidth threshold or a result proved here.

Subsequent work improves the bandwidth range under additional structure. For symmetric or transitively invariant profiles, the revised work
[
25
]
obtains a coarse expected resolvent bound at scale
b_{n}^{-1/2}
for real or circular complex Gaussian entries. Gaussian integration by parts, relative variance estimates and positivity yield the sufficient bandwidth exponent
2/3+\epsilon
; this is not a fine local law. The optimal-bandwidth results for open-boundary block models
[
24
]
and periodic models
[
26
]
use transfer arguments. In the periodic case, transfer operators are calibrated on short auxiliary rings with singular-value inputs at large bandwidths, including estimates developed here. A local comparison uniform over boundary conditions transfers the calibration to arbitrarily long periodic rings. These approaches use prescribed local band geometry, whereas our free probability comparison applies to arbitrary doubly stochastic variance profiles.

The Gaussian resolvent comparison also yields Theorem
1.7
. We bound diagonal resolvent entries uniformly for
|z|\leq 3
at scale
v=b_{n}^{-1/5}n^{\mathbf{c}}
. For a normalized right eigenvector
\psi
with eigenvalue
\lambda
, the vector
(0,\psi)
lies in
\ker\mathcal{Y}_{\lambda}
. The spectral projection onto this kernel then gives
|\psi_{j}|^{2}\leq Cv
. Uniformity in
z
, together with the operator-norm bound, makes this estimate simultaneous for all normalized right eigenvectors and yields the
b_{n}^{-1/10}
bound with an arbitrarily small polynomial loss.

For the product circular law, we combine two arguments in the overlapping ranges described in Section
1.3
. When
m_{n}\leq n^{1/8-\epsilon}
, we apply the same comparison and truncation strategy to the cyclic linearization, using the least singular value estimate of Theorem
2.2
. The second argument applies whenever
m_{n}/\log n\to\infty
: we condition on all but one independent factor and compare the shifted log determinant with a clipped singular-value statistic of the product of the conditioned factors. After division by
nm_{n}
, the comparison error is
O(\log n/m_{n})=o(1)
, and the singular-value universality theorem of Hanin and Jiang
[
27
]
identifies the limiting potential. We use
n^{1/16}
only as a convenient boundary for joining the arguments: Proposition
4.4
with
\epsilon=1/16
covers
m_{n}\leq n^{1/16}
, and Section
6
covers the remaining indices. This proves Theorem
1.9
for every sequence
(m_{n})
.

2.1.
The smallest singular value lower bound

We recall Tikhomirov’s least singular value estimate for shifted inhomogeneous non-Hermitian random matrices
[
45
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

[
[
45
]
, Theorem 3.4] Let
K,\rho_{0}>0
be fixed. Consider a random matrix
A=V\odot W
where
W
is a real or complex square random matrix of size
n
,
V
is a deterministic matrix with non-negative entries, and
\odot
is the Hadamard (i.e., entrywise) product. Denote by

\sigma^{*}:=\max_{i,j\leq n}|V_{i,j}|,\quad\sigma:=\max\left(\max_{j\leq n}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i,j}^{2}},\max_{i\leq n}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{n}V_{i,j}^{2}}\right).

We further assume that the following two conditions both hold:

(1)

The entries of
W
are independent,
K
-sub-Gaussian, having mean zero and a unit second moment.

(2)

The entries of
W

\rho_{0},
or entries have independent real and imaginary parts and both have distribution densities bounded from above by
\rho_{0}
.

Then for any given

\kappa\in(0,1]

we have that whenever

For dense square matrices with centered i.i.d. entries, polynomial lower bounds on the least singular value are available under suitable moment and nondegeneracy assumptions; see
[

2.2.
Squared singular-value measures and Kolmogorov distance

We introduce the notation for the polynomial comparison of squared singular-value measures in Theorem

from the Gaussian and non-Gaussian resolvent estimates. An earlier use of such a comparison for block band matrices appears in

Consider the following empirical squared singular-value measures of

and

, where

\nu_{X_{z}}(\cdot)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\sigma_{i}^{2}(X_{z})}(\cdot),

For two probability measures

When both measures are supported on

(2.1)

\|\mu-\nu\|_{[0,\infty)}:=\sup_{x\geq 0}\left|\mu([0,x])-\nu([0,x])\right|.

Unless otherwise stated, we assume that

We use free probability and concentration inequalities from

For any

We consider the dilation matrix of

Define for any

the dilation matrix

(3.1)

\mathcal{Y}_{z}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&X-zI_{n}\\
X^{*}-\bar{z}I_{n}&0\end{pmatrix}.

The Stieltjes transform of

\mathcal{G}_{z}(\eta):=(\mathcal{Y}_{z}-\eta I_{2n})^{-1},\quad m_{z}(\eta):=\operatorname{tr}_{2n}\mathcal{G}_{z}(\eta)=\frac{1}{2n}\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal{G}_{z}(\eta),

denotes the normalized trace: for a

\operatorname{tr}_{n}M=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}M

For the Gaussian matrix
X

be obtained by replacing each diagonal entry

of variance
\mathbb{E}|x_{ii}|^{2}

We also introduce the following

\mathcal{Y}^{o}_{z,\text{free}}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&{X^{o}}_{\text{free}}-zI_{n}\\

\mathcal{G}_{z,\text{free}}^{o}(\eta):=(\operatorname{id}_{2n}\otimes\tau)\left[(\mathcal{Y}_{z,\text{free}}^{o}-\eta I_{2n})^{-1}\right],\quad m_{z,\text{free}}^{o}(\eta):=\operatorname{tr}_{2n}\mathcal{G}_{z,\text{free}}^{o}(\eta)=\frac{1}{2n}\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal{G}_{z,\text{free}}^{o}(\eta).

]

By

We need the following perturbation estimate between

and

Lemma 3.1
.

The perturbation formula (
3.5
) holds for the resolvent between
X
and
X^{o}
.

Proof.

Set

D=X-X^{o}=\operatorname{diag}(x_{11}-r_{1},\cdots,x_{nn}-r_{n})

where
r_{i}
is a circular Gaussian of the same variance as
x_{ii}
. Then the Hermitization difference is

\mathcal{Y}_{z}-\mathcal{Y}_{z}^{o}=\begin{bmatrix}0&D\\
D^{*}&0\end{bmatrix}:=\Delta.

Then we have

\|\Delta\|=\|D\|=\max_{i}|x_{ii}-r_{i}|.

Let
\eta\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
with
v=\Im\eta>0
, the resolvent identity gives

\mathcal{G}_{z}-\mathcal{G}_{z}^{o}=-\mathcal{G}_{z}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{z}^{o}.

Since

|m_{z}(\eta)-m_{z}^{o}(\eta)|\leq\frac{C_{D}\sqrt{\log n}}{\sqrt{b_{n}}(\Im\eta)^{2}},\quad\|\mathcal{G}_{z}(\eta)-\mathcal{G}_{z}^{o}(\eta)\|\leq\frac{C_{D}\sqrt{\log n}}{\sqrt{b_{n}}(\Im\eta)^{2}}.

∎

a=c

Proof.

\displaystyle|m_{z}(\eta)-m_{z,\text{free}}^{o}(\eta)|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{b_{n}}v^{4}}+\frac{C}{b_{n}v^{5}}+\frac{C_{D}\sqrt{\log n}}{\sqrt{n}v}+\frac{C\sqrt{\log n}}{\sqrt{b_{n}}v^{2}},

We explain the four terms on the right hand side separately.

(1)Trace universality:

|\mathbb{E}m_{z}(\eta)-\mathbb{E}m_{z}^{G}(\eta)|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{b_{n}}v^{4}}.

We apply the trace universality argument in

by matching Gaussians
G_{ij}
one summand at a time. Then we Taylor expand
F(A)=\operatorname{tr}_{2n}(\eta-A)^{-1}
up to second order. Then the zeroth, first and second order terms cancel since they have matching first and second real moments. Finally we bound the third order remainder via

|D^{3}F(A)[H,H,H]|\leq 6v^{-4}\operatorname{tr}_{2n}|H|^{3},

and sum over
i,j
.

(2)The Gaussian to free object comparison:

|\mathbb{E}m_{z}^{G,o}(\eta)-m_{z,\text{free}}^{o}(\eta)|\leq\frac{C}{b_{n}v^{5}},

this already follows from
[
6
]
, Theorem 2.8 as in the Gaussian case Proposition
3.2
.

(3) Scalar concentration: with probability
1-n^{-D}
we have

|m_{z}(\eta)-\mathbb{E}m_{z}(\eta)|\leq\frac{C_{D}\sqrt{\log n}}{\sqrt{n}v}.

To prove this, we note that replacing one row of
X
changes
\mathcal{Y}_{z}
by rank at most 2, so that it changes its normalized Stieltjes transform by an amount at most
C/nv
. Then McDiarmid concentration inequality gives

\mathbb{P}(|m_{z}(\eta)-\mathbb{E}m_{z}(\eta)|\geq t)\leq 4e^{-cnv^{2}t^{2}}.

No moment assumption on
X
is needed at this step.

(4) Diagonal pseudovariance correction:

|\mathbb{E}m_{z}^{G}-\mathbb{E}m_{z}^{G,o}|\leq\frac{C\sqrt{\log n}}{\sqrt{b_{n}}v^{2}}.

This follows from (
3.5
).

We now make the decay exponent independent of the spectral parameter. The variance normalization implies
1\leq b_{n}\leq n
. If
b_{n}v^{8}\geq n^{\mathbf{c}^{\prime}}
, then the four terms in (
3.11
) satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{C}{\sqrt{b_{n}}v^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq Cn^{-\mathbf{c}^{\prime}/2},

\displaystyle\frac{C}{b_{n}v^{5}}

\displaystyle\leq Cb_{n}^{-3/8}n^{-5\mathbf{c}^{\prime}/8},

\displaystyle\frac{C_{D}\sqrt{\log n}}{\sqrt{n}v}

\displaystyle\leq C_{D}\sqrt{\log n}\,n^{-3/8-\mathbf{c}^{\prime}/8},

\displaystyle\frac{C\sqrt{\log n}}{\sqrt{b_{n}}v^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{\log n}\,b_{n}^{-1/4}n^{-\mathbf{c}^{\prime}/4}.

Their sum is at most
n^{-\mathbf{c}^{\prime\prime}}
for
\mathbf{c}^{\prime\prime}=\min\{\mathbf{c}^{\prime}/8,1/8\}
and all sufficiently large
n
. The required lower bound on
n
may depend on
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}
, the atom distribution and
D
, but is independent of
\eta
and
z
. Taking

proves (
3.9
), also for any deterministic sequence
\eta=\eta_{n}\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
satisfying the displayed scale condition.

For the uniform bound (
3.10
), fix

and set

. If

n^{-2}
-net of

at these points and use the preceding calculation with

∎

3.1.
Kolmogorov comparison of squared singular-value measures

We now use the already derived Green function convergence estimates in Proposition
3.2
and
3.4
to obtain the following polynomial convergence rate in Kolmogorov distance:

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
X
be defined as in Definition
1.2
with subgaussian entries and
b_{n}\geq n^{\epsilon}
for some small
\epsilon>0
. Then for any fixed
z\in\mathbb{C}
we can find
\zeta>0
such that with probability
1-o(1)
, we have

(3.13)

\|\nu_{X_{z}}(\cdot)-\nu_{G_{z}}(\cdot)\|_{[0,\infty)}=O(n^{-\zeta}).

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

Let
\widetilde{G}_{z}
denote the dilation matrix, where
G
is complex Ginibre:

\widetilde{G}_{z}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&G-zI\\
G^{*}-\bar{z}I&0\end{pmatrix}.

Observing that
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
X_{z}X_{z}^{*}
(resp.
G_{z}G_{z}^{*}
) if and only if
\pm\sqrt{\lambda}
are both eigenvalues of
\mathcal{Y}_{z}
(resp.
\widetilde{G}_{z}
), we see that to prove (
3.13
), it suffices to prove

(3.14)

\|\mu_{\mathcal{Y}_{z}}(\cdot)-\mu_{\widetilde{G}_{z}}(\cdot)\|_{(-\infty,\infty)}=O(n^{-\zeta}),

where we recall that
\mu_{X}
denotes the empirical eigenvalue distribution (it differs from
\nu_{X}
by considering the eigenvalues instead of considering squared singular values) because we have

\|\nu_{X_{z}}(\cdot)-\nu_{G_{z}}(\cdot)\|_{[0,\infty)}\leq 2\|\mu_{\mathcal{Y}_{z}}(\cdot)-\mu_{\widetilde{G}_{z}}(\cdot)\|_{(-\infty,\infty)}.

The benefit of this reduction is that both
\mathcal{Y}_{z}
and
\widetilde{G}_{z}
have independent entries modulo symmetry. To prove (
3.14
), we show the Stieltjes transform of
\mathcal{Y}_{z}
converges to the Stieltjes transform of
\widetilde{G}_{z}
at a polynomial rate. Recall that
m_{z}(\eta):=\operatorname{tr}_{2n}\mathcal{G}_{z}(\eta)
is the Stieltjes transform of
\mathcal{Y}_{z}
, and
m_{z,\text{free}}^{o}(\eta)
is the Stieltjes transform of
\mathcal{Y}^{o}_{z,\text{free}}
. Here the expression
m^{o}_{z,\text{free}}(\eta)
has been explicitly computed in (
3.3
), (
3.4
).

We first record the operator-norm input used for both cases. There is a fixed
K_{\xi}\geq 3
, depending only on the subgaussian parameter of the atom, such that, for every fixed
D>0
and all sufficiently large
n
,

(3.15)

\mathbb{P}(\|X\|>K_{\xi})\leq n^{-D}.

Indeed, write
X=U+iV
with real matrices
U,V
, and symmetrize each using an independent copy. By conditional Jensen’s inequality, the
2p
-th moments of their operator norms are bounded by those of the symmetrized matrices. For a fixed
L>0
depending only on the subgaussian parameter, each symmetrized real atom
\theta
satisfies
\mathbb{E}\theta^{2q}\leq L^{2q}\mathbb{E}g^{2q}
for all integers
q\geq 1
, where
g
is a standard real Gaussian; odd moments vanish. Expanding the even traces of the real symmetric dilations therefore gives Gaussian moment domination. The Gaussian comparison dilation, multiplied by
\sqrt{b_{n}}
, has dimension
2n
, maximum row variance
b_{n}
and maximum coefficient at most one. Thus
[
7
]
, Proposition 2.1 and Lemma 2.2, together with the
L^{2p}
triangle inequality, give

\bigl(\mathbb{E}\|X\|^{2p}\bigr)^{1/(2p)}\leq 2L(2n)^{1/(2p)}\left[2\sqrt{\frac{\lceil b_{n}\rceil+p}{b_{n}}}+2\sqrt{\frac{2p}{b_{n}}}\right],\qquad p\in\mathbb{N},\ p\geq 2.

Take
p=\lceil(D+2)\log(2n)\rceil
. Since
b_{n}\geq n^{\epsilon}
, the right hand side is eventually bounded by a fixed
C_{\xi}
, independently of
D
. Markov’s inequality with
K_{\xi}\geq eC_{\xi}
gives (
3.15
). This argument does not require independence between the real and imaginary parts of an atom. The same argument applies to the Ginibre reference
G
; enlarging
K_{\xi}
if necessary, we use (
3.15
) for both
X
and
G
.

(The Gaussian case) First assume that
X
has Gaussian entries.
Let
\mu_{z}
denote the common free spectral measure of
\mathcal{Y}_{z,\mathrm{free}}^{o}
and
\widetilde{G}_{z,\mathrm{free}}
. Its Stieltjes transform is
m_{z,\mathrm{free}}^{o}(\eta)
, since both free models satisfy (
3.3
)–(
3.4
). The comparison with the original
X
is already included in (
3.6
) through the diagonal perturbation estimate (
3.5
). By
[
21
]
, Lemma 3.1 and Remark 3.1,
\mu_{z}(\cdot)
has a bounded support and bounded density: for each
x\in\mathbb{R},y\geq 0:

(3.16)

|\mu_{z}((-\infty,x+y))-\mu_{z}((-\infty,x))|\leq y.

By (
3.6
), for each fixed
z\in\mathbb{C}
and each deterministic
\eta=\eta_{n}\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
, with probability at least
1-n^{-100}
we have

(3.17)

|m_{z}(\eta)-m^{o}_{z,\text{free}}(\eta)|\lesssim\frac{C}{b_{n}|\Im\eta|^{5}}+\frac{C(\log n)^{3}}{\sqrt{b_{n}}(\Im\eta)^{2}}.

Fix a sufficiently large
A>0
and define the region

\mathcal{D}_{A}:=\{\eta\in\mathbb{C}:-A\leq\Re\eta\leq A,\quad(b_{n})^{-1/6}\leq\Im\eta\leq 1\}.

Using the Lipschitz continuity of
m_{z}(\eta)
and
m_{z,\mathrm{free}}^{o}(\eta)
in
\eta
, we can upgrade the convergence in (
3.17
) to be uniform over
\eta\in\mathcal{D}_{A}
, with probability at least
1-n^{-10}
.

In the following take a fixed
K>K_{\xi}+|z|+2
. By (
3.15
), the empirical Hermitization laws of both
X_{z}
and
G_{z}
are supported in
[-K,K]
with probability
1-o(1)
; the common free law is also supported in this interval. Also take some
a>0
sufficiently large.
Now we use
[
5
]
, Corollary B.15 to derive, for some
A>0
large depending on
K
and
a
, (writing
\eta=\theta+i\tau
)

\displaystyle\|\mu_{\mathcal{Y}_{z}}(\cdot)-\mu_{z}(\cdot)\|_{(-\infty,\infty)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left[\int_{-A}^{A}|m_{z}(\eta)-m^{o}_{z,\text{free}}(\eta)|d\theta+\frac{1}{\tau}\sup_{x}\int_{|y|\leq 2\tau a}|\mu_{z}((-\infty,x+y])-\mu_{z}((-\infty,x])||dy\right]

for some
C
depending only on
A
,
a
and
K
. Now we set
\tau=(b_{n})^{-1/6}
. To bound the first term on the second line we use (
3.17
), and to bound the second term on the second line use (
3.16
). Since
G
has circular Gaussian entries, its free probability Stieltjes transform is equal to
m_{z,\text{free}}^{o}
as they are both the solution to (
3.4
). Doing the same computation for
\widetilde{G}_{z}
completes the proof of (
3.14
).

(The non-Gaussian case) Set
\tau=b_{n}^{-1/16}
. We use the explicit finite-third-moment estimate (
3.11
). Apply it with
D=12
on an
n^{-2}
-net of
[-A,A]
, and use the
\tau^{-2}
Lipschitz bound for both Stieltjes transforms to extend the comparison to the whole interval. Since

and

, this gives, with probability

,

\displaystyle\sup_{|\theta|\leq A}|m_{z}(\theta+i\tau)-m^{o}_{z,\mathrm{free}}(\theta+i\tau)|

\displaystyle\leq Cb_{n}^{-1/4}+Cb_{n}^{-11/16}+C\sqrt{\log n}\bigl(n^{-7/16}+b_{n}^{-3/8}\bigr)

\displaystyle=O(b_{n}^{-1/4}),

where the interpolation error
O(n^{-2}\tau^{-2})
is absorbed into the right hand side. The same estimate holds for the Ginibre reference, whose bandwidth is
n\geq b_{n}
. On the fixed operator-norm event used above, apply the same compact-support smoothing inequality and (
3.16
). Each Hermitization law is then within
O(b_{n}^{-1/4}+\tau)=O(b_{n}^{-1/16})
of the common free law in Kolmogorov distance. Passing to squared singular values proves (
3.13
); in particular, one may take

.
∎

3.2.
Weak delocalization

To obtain eigenvector delocalization, we will prove the following version of diagonal Green function entry estimates.

Proposition 3.7
.

Assume that
X=(x_{ij})
has independent centered real or complex Gaussian entries, and
\max_{ij}\mathbb{E}|x_{ij}|^{2}\leq(b_{n})^{-1}
and
\mathbb{E}[XX^{*}]=\mathbb{E}[X^{*}X]=I_{n}
. Assume that
b_{n}\geq n^{\epsilon}
for some
\epsilon>0
. For any fixed (small)
\mathbf{c}>0
, we set

v_{n}=(b_{n})^{-1/5}n^{2\mathbf{c}}.
Then with probability at least
1-n^{-10}
, we have

(3.18)

\sup_{|z|\leq 3}\max_{1\leq k\leq 2n}\|\mathcal{G}_{z}(iv_{n})_{kk}\|\leq C,

where the constant
C>0
is independent of
n
.

Proof.

Fix
D>0
. We prove the estimate with failure probability at most
n^{-D}
. The diagonal entries of
\mathcal{G}_{z,\mathrm{free}}^{o}(iv_{n})
equal
m_{z,\mathrm{free}}^{o}(iv_{n})
and have modulus at most one, by the global bound proved in Corollary
3.3
. By
[
6
]
, Theorem 2.8,

\|\mathbb{E}\mathcal{G}_{z}^{o}(iv_{n})-\mathcal{G}_{z,\mathrm{free}}^{o}(iv_{n})\|\leq\frac{C}{b_{n}v_{n}^{5}}=Cn^{-10\mathbf{c}}.

The perturbation estimate (
3.5
) holds uniformly in
z
on the event

\|X-X^{o}\|\leq C_{D+2}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{b_{n}}},

which has probability at least
1-n^{-D-2}
. On this event its contribution is at most

\frac{C_{D+2}\sqrt{\log n}}{\sqrt{b_{n}}v_{n}^{2}}=C_{D+2}\sqrt{\log n}\,b_{n}^{-1/10}n^{-4\mathbf{c}}=o(1).

For each fixed
z
and
k
, Gaussian concentration applied separately to the real and imaginary parts, as in
[
6
]
, Corollary 4.14, gives

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|(\mathcal{G}_{z}^{o}(iv_{n}))_{kk}-\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{G}_{z}^{o}(iv_{n}))_{kk}\right|\geq 1\right)\leq 4\exp(-c_{0}b_{n}v_{n}^{4}).

Here
b_{n}v_{n}^{4}=b_{n}^{1/5}n^{8\mathbf{c}}\geq n^{\epsilon/5}
. Take an
n^{-2}
-net of the disk
|z|\leq 3
, of size
O(n^{4})
, and apply a union bound over its points and the
2n
diagonal entries. The resulting concentration failure probability is at most

Cn^{5}\exp(-c_{0}n^{\epsilon/5})\leq n^{-D-2}

for all sufficiently large
n
. Finally, the resolvent identity gives

\left|(\mathcal{G}_{z}(iv_{n}))_{kk}-(\mathcal{G}_{z^{\prime}}(iv_{n}))_{kk}\right|\leq\frac{|z-z^{\prime}|}{v_{n}^{2}}.

Since
b_{n}\leq n
and
v_{n}\geq n^{-1/5}
, interpolation costs only
O(n^{-2}v_{n}^{-2})=O(n^{-8/5})
. The free bound, comparison and concentration estimates therefore prove (
3.18
), with failure probability at most
2n^{-D-2}\leq n^{-D}
. This also records the stronger confidence estimate needed below.
∎

Then the weak delocalization statement for the Gaussian model follows:

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

It suffices to prove the claim for
0<\mathbf{c}<\epsilon/10
, since a bound with a smaller positive exponent implies the bound for any larger exponent. Apply the preceding proposition with its parameter
\mathbf{c}/2
, so that now

v_{n}=b_{n}^{-1/5}n^{\mathbf{c}}.

Its proof gives (
3.18
) with probability at least
1-n^{-12}
.

We also justify the operator-norm event. The row and column variance matrices are
I_{n}
, entry independence gives
\|\operatorname{Cov}(\operatorname{vec}X)\|\leq b_{n}^{-1}
, and

\sup_{\|u\|_{2}=\|w\|_{2}=1}\mathbb{E}|u^{*}Xw|^{2}=\sup_{\|u\|_{2}=\|w\|_{2}=1}\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}|x_{ij}|^{2}|u_{i}|^{2}|w_{j}|^{2}\leq b_{n}^{-1}.

Thus
[
6
]
, Corollary 2.2 and Lemma 2.5, imply, for every fixed
D>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\|X\|>2+Cb_{n}^{-1/4}(\log n)^{3/4}+Cb_{n}^{-1/2}\sqrt{D\log n}\right)\leq n^{-D}.

Since
b_{n}\geq n^{\epsilon}
, taking
D=12
shows that
\|X\|\leq 2.5
with probability at least
1-n^{-12}
for all sufficiently large
n
. The intersection of this event with (
3.18
) consequently has probability at least
1-2n^{-12}\geq 1-n^{-10}
. On this event all eigenvalues of
X
have modulus at most
2.5
.

For all sufficiently large
n
, we have
v_{n}<1
, since
\mathbf{c}<\epsilon/10
. Let
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
be any normalized right eigenvector of
X
with eigenvalue
\lambda
. Set
u:=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\psi\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n}
. Then
\mathcal{Y}_{\lambda}u=0
, so
u
is a unit vector in
\ker\mathcal{Y}_{\lambda}
. Let
P_{0}
be the orthogonal projection onto this kernel. The spectral theorem applied to
\mathcal{Y}_{\lambda}
gives, for any
1\leq k\leq 2n
,

(3.19)

\Im(\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(iv_{n}))_{kk}=\langle e_{k},\frac{v_{n}}{\mathcal{Y}_{\lambda}^{2}+v_{n}^{2}}e_{k}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{v_{n}}\|P_{0}e_{k}\|_{2}^{2}.

Since for each
1\leq j\leq n
we have, using
u\in\ker\mathcal{Y}_{\lambda}
,

|\psi_{j}|=|\langle e_{n+j},u\rangle|\leq\|P_{0}e_{n+j}\|_{2},

we have

\frac{|\psi_{j}|^{2}}{v_{n}}\leq\Im(\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(iv_{n}))_{n+j,n+j}\leq|(\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(iv_{n}))_{n+j,n+j}|\leq C.

Therefore,

\|\psi\|_{\infty}\leq C^{1/2}v_{n}^{1/2}=C^{1/2}b_{n}^{-1/10}n^{\mathbf{c}/2}\leq b_{n}^{-1/10}n^{\mathbf{c}}

for all sufficiently large
n
, because
C^{1/2}\leq n^{\mathbf{c}/2}
. Since the event is uniform over
|z|\leq 3
, this holds for all normalized right eigenvectors simultaneously and completes the proof.
∎

4.
Circular law for general profiles and small products

We prove the circular law for the general variance-profile model and for small products by combining the singular-value counting estimates, least singular value bounds and Kolmogorov comparison of squared singular-value measures to compare normalized logarithmic potentials. This strategy follows
[
30
]
; earlier versions appear in
[
41
]
,
[
5
]
and
[
43
]
.

To complete the proof of circular law, we shall use the replacement principle by Tao and Vu
[
42
]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that for each
n
,
X
and
G
are two ensembles of size
n\times n
random matrices satisfying

(1)

The quantity

\frac{1}{n}\|G\|_{\text{HS}}^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\|X\|_{\text{HS}}^{2}

is bounded in probability (resp, almost surely), where
\|\cdot\|_{\text{HS}}
denotes the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of a square matrix.

(2)

For a.e.
z\in\mathbb{C}
with respect to Lebesgue measure, we have

\frac{1}{n}\log|\det(X-zI_{n})|-\frac{1}{n}\log|\det(G-zI_{n})|

converges to 0 in probability (resp, almost surely).

Then
\mu_{X}-\mu_{G}
converges weakly to zero in probability (resp. almost surely).

Also recall that for an
n\times n
matrix
M
, denote by
\sigma_{1}(M)\geq\sigma_{2}(M)\cdots\geq\sigma_{n}(M)
its singular values, then

|\det M|=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}(M).

We will also need a useful technical lemma from
[
30
]
:

Lemma 4.2
.

Consider two probability measures
\mu,\nu
on
\mathbb{R}
and
0<a<b
. Then

\left|\int_{a}^{b}\log(x)d\mu(x)-\int_{a}^{b}\log(x)d\nu(x)\right|\leq 2(|\log b|+|\log a|)\|\mu-\nu\|_{[a,b]},

in which we define

\|\mu-\nu\|_{[a,b]}:=\sup_{x\in[a,b]}|\mu([a,x])-\nu([a,x])|.

We now prove Theorem
1.5
and establish the small-product result used in Theorem
1.9
. The latter theorem is completed in Section
6
.

Proofof Theorem
1.5
.

First consider the Gaussian case and fix
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. Choose
\mathbf{c},\kappa>0
sufficiently small that
\mathbf{c}+3\kappa<6\epsilon/5
and
n^{2\kappa}/\sqrt{b_{n}}\to 0
. We truncate the sum

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\sigma_{i}(X_{z})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\sigma_{i}(X_{z})1_{\{\sigma_{i}(X_{z})\geq(b_{n})^{-1/5}n^{\mathbf{c}}\}}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\sigma_{i}(X_{z})1_{\{\sigma_{i}(X_{z})<(b_{n})^{-1/5}n^{\mathbf{c}}\}}.

Set

a_{n}:=(b_{n})^{-1/5}n^{\mathbf{c}}.

By Corollary
3.3
applied to
I=[-a_{n},a_{n}]
, with probability
1-n^{-10}
there are at most
C_{2}n(b_{n})^{-1/5}n^{\mathbf{c}}
terms in the second summation.

To apply Theorem
2.1
, choose
R>\max(1,|z|^{-1})
. Here
\sigma=1
and
\sigma^{*}=b_{n}^{-1/2}
, so its shift condition holds for all sufficiently large
n
. For a general complex Gaussian atom, a fixed phase rotation diagonalizes the covariance of its real and imaginary parts, making them independent, or making the atom real-valued in the degenerate case. Rotating
X
and
z
together preserves
|z|
and all singular values. Theorem
2.1
therefore applies. Together with the norm bound
\sigma_{1}(X_{z})\leq K_{\xi}+|z|
from (
3.15
), it gives, with probability
1-o(1)
,
|\log\sigma_{i}(X_{z})|\leq\frac{n^{1+3\kappa}}{b_{n}}\log b_{n}
for every
i\in[n]
and all sufficiently large
n
; the factor
\log b_{n}
absorbs the fixed
z
-dependent constants. The choice
\mathbf{c}+3\kappa<6\epsilon/5
and the assumption
b_{n}\geq n^{5/6+\epsilon}
now give:

(4.1)

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\sigma_{i}(X_{z})1_{\{\sigma_{i}(X_{z})<(b_{n})^{-1/5}n^{\mathbf{c}}\}}\right|\leq C_{2}\frac{1}{n}n^{\mathbf{c}}\frac{n}{(b_{n})^{1/5}}\frac{n^{1+3\kappa}}{b_{n}}\log b_{n}=o(1),\quad n\to\infty.

The same estimate in (
4.1
) is true if we replace
X_{z}
by
G_{z}
in the sum, with a much simpler proof.

Now we consider the first sum difference

(4.2)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\sigma_{i}(X_{z})1_{\{\sigma_{i}(X_{z})\geq(b_{n})^{-1/5}n^{\mathbf{c}}\}}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\sigma_{i}(G_{z})1_{\{\sigma_{i}(G_{z})\geq(b_{n})^{-1/5}n^{\mathbf{c}}\}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{z}(1+\log n)\|\nu_{X_{z}}-\nu_{G_{z}}\|_{[0,\infty)}=o(1),\quad n\to\infty,

Here we applied Lemma
4.2
to the squared
singular-value measures
\nu_{X_{z}}
and
\nu_{G_{z}}
on the interval

[a_{n}^{2},K_{z}^{2}]
, where
a_{n}=(b_{n})^{-1/5}n^{\mathbf{c}},

and
K_{z}
is a deterministic upper bound for the singular values on
the event under consideration. We also used

\log\sigma=\frac{1}{2}\log(\sigma^{2})
. Theorem

3.6
then shows that the right-hand side is
o(1)
. Here one may take
K_{z}=K_{\xi}+|z|
on the common norm event for
X
and
G
provided by (
3.15
).
Thus, for every fixed
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, the second criterion of Theorem
4.1
holds in probability. Since
\{0\}
has planar Lebesgue measure zero, this supplies the required almost-everywhere statement. The doubly stochastic variance normalization gives
\mathbb{E}[n^{-1}\|X\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[n^{-1}\|G\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}]=1
, so Markov’s inequality verifies the first criterion. The circular law for the complex Ginibre reference
G
then yields the circular law for
X
.

For the non-Gaussian case, fix again
z\neq 0
and choose
\mathbf{c},\kappa>0
sufficiently small that
\mathbf{c}+3\kappa<9\epsilon/8
and
n^{2\kappa}/\sqrt{b_{n}}\to 0
. The same argument applies with Corollary
3.5
in place of Corollary

, giving:

(4.3)

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\sigma_{i}(X_{z})1_{\{\sigma_{i}(X_{z})<(b_{n})^{-1/8}n^{\mathbf{c}}\}}\right|\leq C_{2}\frac{1}{n}n^{\mathbf{c}}\frac{n}{(b_{n})^{1/8}}\frac{n^{1+3\kappa}}{b_{n}}\log b_{n}=o(1),\quad n\to\infty,

where the right-hand side tends to zero by

and the joint choice of

and

.
∎

We use the following smoothing estimate to avoid a fixed operator-norm bound in the small-product comparison.

Lemma 4.3

(Stieltjes smoothing with a compact reference measure)
.

Let
\nu
be a probability measure supported in
[-B,B]
, where
B\geq 1
, with a density bounded by
M
, and let
\mu
be any probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
. For
\rho=\mu,\nu
, write

m_{\rho}(\eta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{x-\eta}\,d\rho(x),\qquad\eta\in\mathbb{C}_{+}.

Set
A=B+3
. For
0<v\leq 1
, define

\Delta=\sup_{|u|\leq A}|m_{\mu}(u+iv)-m_{\nu}(u+iv)|.

Then

\|\mu-\nu\|_{(-\infty,\infty)}\leq C_{B,M}(\Delta+\sqrt{v}).

Proof.

Let
P_{v}
have density
p_{v}(t)=v/(\pi(t^{2}+v^{2}))
, and write
Q_{\rho}=\rho*P_{v}
for
\rho=\mu,\nu
. Its density is
\pi^{-1}\Im m_{\rho}(u+iv)
. Since
\nu
is supported in
[-B,B]
, each tail of
Q_{\nu}
outside
[-A,A]
has mass at most
v/(3\pi)
. Integration of the Stieltjes comparison over
[-A,A]
gives

Q_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}\setminus[-A,A])\leq\frac{2v}{3\pi}+\frac{2A}{\pi}\Delta.

Anchor the two distribution functions at
-A
and integrate their density difference. On
[-A,A]
this yields

|F_{Q_{\mu}}(x)-F_{Q_{\nu}}(x)|\leq\frac{v}{\pi}+\frac{4A}{\pi}\Delta.

The same bound holds outside this interval by the tail estimates.

For
Y
with law
P_{v}
and any
h>0
, coupling gives

F_{Q_{\rho}}(x-h)-p_{h}\leq F_{\rho}(x)\leq F_{Q_{\rho}}(x+h)+p_{h},\qquad p_{h}=\mathbb{P}(Y>h)=\mathbb{P}(Y<-h)\leq\frac{v}{\pi h}.

Applying these inequalities to both measures and using the density bound for
\nu
, we obtain

\|\mu-\nu\|_{(-\infty,\infty)}\leq\frac{v}{\pi}+\frac{4A}{\pi}\Delta+2Mh+\frac{2v}{\pi h}.

Taking
h=\sqrt{v}
proves the claim.
∎

Proposition 4.4

(Small-product regime)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m_{n}}
be independent

matrices with
i.i.d. entries distributed as
\xi/\sqrt{n}
, and assume

m_{n}\leq n^{1/8-\epsilon}
for some fixed
\epsilon>0
.
Suppose
\mathbb{E}\xi=0
,
\mathbb{E}|\xi|^{2}=1
, and

\mathbb{E}|\xi|^{3}<\infty
.
Assume either that
\xi
is real with a density bounded by
L
,
or that its real and imaginary parts are independent and at least
one has a density bounded by
L
. Then the root empirical spectral
measure
\nu_{P_{n}}^{(m_{n})}
defined in Theorem
1.9

converges in probability to the circular law.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the cyclic linearization defined in
(

Consequently, the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}

multiplicity, are precisely all the
m_{n}

of
P_{n}
. In particular,

\mu_{\mathcal{L}}=\nu_{P_{n}}^{(m_{n})}

for every realization of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m_{n}}
.

Thus it suffices to prove the circular law for
\mathcal{L}
. Write
m=m_{n}
and
N=nm
, and let
G
be an
N\times N
complex Ginibre matrix with entry variance
N^{-1}
. Fix

. We first justify the singular-value comparison under the moment assumptions of this proposition, without invoking the subgaussian hypothesis of Theorem
3.6
.

Every row and column of
\mathcal{L}
has total entry variance one, its entries are independent, and its maximum entry variance is
n^{-1}
. Hence the finite-third-moment estimate (
3.11
) applies with ambient dimension
N
and bandwidth
n
. The associated free Stieltjes transform is the same as for

3.3
)–(
3.4
); denote it by
m^{o}_{z,\mathrm{free}}
. The corresponding probability measure

has compact support and bounded density, as in (
3.16
).

For any fixed
A>0
, take
v=n^{-1/32}
. Writing
m_{z}^{\mathcal{L}}
and
m_{z}^{G}
for the normalized Stieltjes transforms of the respective Hermitizations, (
3.11
) gives, for each
\eta=u+iv

\displaystyle|m_{z}^{\mathcal{L}}(\eta)-m^{o}_{z,\mathrm{free}}(\eta)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}\,v^{4}}+\frac{C}{nv^{5}}+\frac{C_{D}\sqrt{\log N}}{\sqrt{N}\,v}+\frac{C\sqrt{\log N}}{\sqrt{n}\,v^{2}}

\displaystyle=O(n^{-3/8})

with probability at least

m
. The same bound holds for
m_{z}^{G}
, whose bandwidth is
N\geq n
. For each deterministic
u

3.11
) are uniform on any fixed interval; the probability bound above is pointwise in
u

n^{-1}
-net of
[-A,A]

|m^{\prime}(\eta)|\leq v^{-2}
, and a sufficiently large fixed

, we obtain with probability
1-o(1)

(4.4)

\sup_{|u|\leq A}\left(|m_{z}^{\mathcal{L}}(u+iv)-m^{o}_{z,\mathrm{free}}(u+iv)|+|m_{z}^{G}(u+iv)-m^{o}_{z,\mathrm{free}}(u+iv)|\right)=O(n^{-3/8}).

Choose
B_{z}\geq 1
such that
\mu_{z}
is supported in
[-B_{z},B_{z}]
, and take
A=B_{z}+3
in (
4.4
). Lemma
4.3
applies separately to each Hermitization law with reference measure
\mu_{z}
, without imposing any support condition on the empirical measures. Thus each law is within
O(n^{-3/8}+\sqrt{v})=O(n^{-1/64})
of
\mu_{z}
in Kolmogorov distance. Passing to squared singular values as in (
3.14
) costs at most a fixed factor: the mass of
[-\sqrt{t},\sqrt{t}]
is the difference of a distribution function and its left limit. We obtain

(4.5)

\|\nu_{\mathcal{L}-zI_{N}}-\nu_{G-zI_{N}}\|_{[0,\infty)}=O(n^{-1/64})

with probability

. This supplies the required comparison for the growing block family under the stated moment assumptions.

We next control the logarithm near zero. Choose
0<\delta<\min(\epsilon,1/8)
and set
a=n^{-1/8+\delta}
. We spell out the small-value count underlying Corollary
3.5
for the present fixed
z
. Apply (
3.11
) at
\eta=ia
, with ambient dimension
N
and bandwidth
n
. Its first term is
O(n^{-4\delta})
, and the other terms also tend to zero. Since
\mu_{z}
has bounded density,
\Im m^{o}_{z,\mathrm{free}}(ia)=O_{z}(1)
. Thus, with probability
1-o(1)

\Im m_{z}^{\mathcal{L}}(ia)=O_{z}(1)

N\geq n
. By the spectral theorem, for either

or

\frac{1}{N}\#\{j:\sigma_{j}(M)\leq a\}\leq\frac{2a}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{a}{\sigma_{j}(M)^{2}+a^{2}}\leq C_{z}a.

Set
q=\min(|z|,|z|^{-1})

2.2
, first for the
m

n

Consequently, for either

or
M=G-zI_{N}

satisfies

. Combining the count and the lower bound gives

(4.6)

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\sigma_{j}(M)\leq a}|\log\sigma_{j}(M)|\leq C_{z}am\log n\leq C_{z}n^{\delta-\epsilon}\log n=o(1).

We only need a polynomial norm bound to compare the remaining logarithmic integrals. By Lemma
5.1
, with probability
1-O(n^{-2})

\|\mathcal{L}\|=\max_{j\in[m]}\|X_{j}\|\leq n^{2}.

Also,
\mathbb{E}\|G\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=N
, so Markov’s inequality gives
\mathbb{P}(\|G\|>N^{2})\leq N^{-3}
. Set
R_{n,z}=N^{2}+|z|+1
. On this common norm event, both squared singular-value measures are supported in
[0,R_{n,z}^{2}]

f_{a}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\log\max(t,a^{2})
for

4.2

\displaystyle\left|\int f_{a}\,d\nu_{\mathcal{L}-zI_{N}}-\int f_{a}\,d\nu_{G-zI_{N}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\log(R_{n,z}/a)\,\|\nu_{\mathcal{L}-zI_{N}}-\nu_{G-zI_{N}}\|_{[0,\infty)}

\displaystyle=O(n^{-1/64}\log n)=o(1).

Here
N\leq n^{9/8}
, so
\log(R_{n,z}/a)=O_{z}(\log n)
. For either matrix

, replacing

by
\int f_{a}\,d\nu_{M}
costs at most the left-hand side of (

\frac{1}{N}\log|\det(\mathcal{L}-zI_{N})|-\frac{1}{N}\log|\det(G-zI_{N})|\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{in probability}.

Finally,
\mathbb{E}[N^{-1}\|\mathcal{L}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[N^{-1}\|G\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}]=1
, so the norm tightness condition of Theorem
4.1
follows from Markov’s inequality. The exceptional point
z=0
has Lebesgue measure zero. The replacement principle and the circular law for
G
now imply the circular law for
\mathcal{L}
, and hence the claimed convergence of
\nu_{P_{n}}^{(m_{n})}
.
∎

5.
Least singular value of the cyclic linearization

In this section, we prove Theorem
2.2
. Write
m=m_{n}
,
N=nm
,
A=\mathcal{L}-zI_{N}
and
q=\min(|z|,|z|^{-1})
. Throughout the section,
1\leq m\leq n
and
0<|z|\leq K^{\prime}
. The probability estimates are for each deterministic
z
in this range, with constants uniform in
z
. We use cyclic indices, so that
X_{m+1}=X_{1}
and
u_{[m+1]}=u_{[1]}
. In particular, when
m=1
the linearization is
\mathcal{L}=X_{1}
.

Set

\mathcal{E}_{n}=\{\|X_{j}\|\leq n^{2},\ s_{min}(X_{j})\geq n^{-5}\text{ for every }j\in[m]\}.

We first record the probability estimate for this event, including its dependence on the number of blocks.

Lemma 5.1
.

There is a constant
C_{L}>0
, depending only on
L
, such that

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c})\leq C_{L}mn^{-3}\leq C_{L}n^{-2}.

Proof.

The normalization
\mathbb{E}|\xi|^{2}=1
gives
\mathbb{E}\|X_{j}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=n
. Thus Markov’s inequality yields

(5.1)

\mathbb{P}(\|X_{j}\|>n^{2})\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\|X_{j}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}}{n^{4}}=n^{-3}.

The deterministic distance reduction in Lemma
5.2
and the scalar small-ball estimate in Lemma
5.4
, applied to a single matrix

, give

\mathbb{P}(s_{min}(X_{j})\leq n^{-5})\leq n\,C_{L}\sqrt{n}\,(\sqrt{n}\,n^{-5})=C_{L}n^{-3}.

Indeed, for each deleted row, condition on the other rows and choose a unit vector in their right kernel; its coefficient vector has norm one. A union bound over the
m\leq n
blocks proves the claim, after increasing
C_{L}
.
∎

The target threshold in Theorem
2.2
allows us to use a union bound over all rows. No decomposition of the unit sphere is needed.

Lemma 5.2

(Reduction to row distances)
.

Let
B
be a complex
d\times d
matrix with rows
R_{1},\ldots,R_{d}
. Set

H_{i}=\operatorname{span}\{R_{j}^{*}:j\neq i\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{d},\qquad D_{i}=\operatorname{dist}(R_{i}^{*},H_{i}).

For any
s\geq 0
, if
s_{min}(B)\leq s
, then
D_{i}\leq\sqrt{d}\,s
for some
i\in[d]
.

Proof.

Choose a unit vector
v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
with
\|B^{*}v\|\leq s
, and an index
i
with
|v_{i}|\geq d^{-1/2}
. Projecting
B^{*}v=\sum_{j}v_{j}R_{j}^{*}
onto
H_{i}^{\perp}
gives
|v_{i}|D_{i}\leq s
.
∎

For a vector
u\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
, write
u=(u_{[1]},\ldots,u_{[m]})
with
u_{[j]}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Let
A^{(i)}
denote
A
with its
i
-th row deleted.

Proposition 5.3
.

On
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, for every
i\in[N]
and every unit vector

, we have

\min_{j\in[m]}\|u_{[j]}\|\geq H,\qquad H:=q^{m}n^{-10m}m^{-1/2}.

Proof.

Suppose that the deleted row belongs to block row
a
. All the other block rows are intact, and therefore

X_{j+1}u_{[j+1]}=zu_{[j]},\qquad j\neq a.

On
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, each such relation gives

\|u_{[j+1]}\|\geq\frac{|z|}{n^{2}}\|u_{[j]}\|,\qquad\|u_{[j]}\|\geq\frac{n^{-5}}{|z|}\|u_{[j+1]}\|.

Both ratios are at least
c:=qn^{-10}\leq 1
, since
|z|/n^{2}\geq qn^{-10}
and
n^{-5}/|z|\geq qn^{-10}
. Removing the relation indexed by
a
from the cycle leaves a path connecting all
m
blocks. Some block has norm at least
m^{-1/2}
, and every other block is reached along at most
m-1
edges. Thus

\|u_{[j]}\|\geq c^{m-1}m^{-1/2}\geq H

for every
j
. When

, the sole block has norm one and the same conclusion holds.
∎

Lemma 5.4

(Scalar small-ball estimate)
.

Let

be independent copies of an atom satisfying the density assumption of Theorem

. There is a constant

, depending only on

, such that for every nonzero

and every

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}\xi_{k}-w\right|\leq t\right)\leq\frac{C_{L}t}{\|a\|}.

For real atoms, one of the real coefficient vectors

and

. Project onto the corresponding real component and apply the one-dimensional case of
[

]
, Theorem 1.1. For complex atoms, choose a component with bounded density and condition on all copies of the other component. Then apply the same real projection argument. The independence of the two components permits this conditioning.
∎

.

6.
Product circular law without a restriction on the number of factors

We work under the assumptions of Theorem
1.9
and complete its proof by combining Proposition
4.4
with the singular-value law of Hanin and Jiang
[
27
, Theorem 1]
. Their main result states that, for a product
Q_{k,n}
of
k
independent
n\times n
matrices with i.i.d. entries distributed as
\xi/\sqrt{n}
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{\sigma_{j}(Q_{k,n})^{2/k}}\longrightarrow U[0,1]\quad\text{in probability}

as
k,n\to\infty
, with no restriction on their relative growth. Thus the squared singular values, after taking their
k
-th roots, have a universal uniform limit. Their theorem gives quantitative bounds in Kolmogorov distance under a finite fourth moment and a bounded-density assumption. In the complex case, the extension in Section 4 of their paper assumes independent real and imaginary parts, both with bounded densities, as in Theorem
1.9
.

This result is useful here because, after conditioning on all but the last factor, we can compare the shifted log determinant of
P_{n}
with a clipped logarithmic statistic of the singular values of the remaining product. The comparison error, after division by
nm_{n}
, is
O(\log n/m_{n})
. The Hanin–Jiang law identifies the limiting statistic as the logarithmic potential of the circular law; the cyclic linearization and the replacement principle then give the desired spectral limit. In fact, this argument applies whenever
m_{n}/\log n\to\infty
. The cutoff
m_{n}>n^{1/16}
below is only a convenient choice for joining it with Proposition
4.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.9
.

Proposition
4.4
, with
\epsilon=1/16
, treats the indices
for which
m=m_{n}\leq n^{1/16}
. We prove the assertion along the
remaining indices, so throughout this proof
m>n^{1/16}
. This convenient split joins the two proofs and ensures
\log n/m\to 0
, which controls the determinant comparison error and the rooted singular-value support below. Write

P_{n}=AX
, where
A=X_{1}\cdots X_{m-1}
and
X=X_{m}
.

We first compare the shifted determinant with a clipped singular-value
sum. For each deterministic
D\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
,
Lemmas
5.2
and
5.4
give

(6.1)

\mathbb{P}\bigl(s_{min}(X-D)\leq t\bigr)\leq C_{L}n^{2}t,\qquad t\geq 0.

The constant
C_{L}
is independent of
D
. Indeed, after conditioning on all rows except row
i
, choose a unit
vector in the kernel of the other rows. The scalar small-ball bound
for its inner product with row
i
is
C_{L}\sqrt{n}\,d
at distance

d
, irrespective of the deterministic shift. The row-distance
reduction takes
d=\sqrt{n}\,t
, and a union bound over
n
rows
proves (
6.1
). In particular, each factor and
hence
A
are almost surely invertible.

Fix
z\neq 0
and set
w=z^{m}
and
D=wA^{-1}
. Conditional on
A
, (
6.1
) applies to this

. Since
X
is independent of
A
and
\mathbb{E}\|X\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=n
, the following event has conditional probability at least
1-C_{L}^{\prime}n^{-3}
given
A
:

\|X\|\leq n^{2},\qquad s_{min}(X-D)\geq n^{-5}.

On this event, write
d_{j}=\sigma_{j}(D)
, in decreasing order.
Weyl’s inequality gives

\sigma_{j}(X-D)\leq d_{j}+n^{2}
and, when
d_{j}>2n^{2}
,

\sigma_{j}(X-D)\geq d_{j}/2
. For the remaining indices use the
least singular value bound. Thus, for

,

n^{-8}\max(1,d_{j})\leq\sigma_{j}(X-D)\leq n^{3}\max(1,d_{j}).

Using
\det(P_{n}-wI_{n})=\det A\,\det(X-D)
and the reciprocal
singular values of
A^{-1}
, we obtain

(6.2)

\left|\frac{1}{nm}\log|\det(P_{n}-z^{m}I_{n})|-\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\max\bigl(\sigma_{j}(A),|z|^{m}\bigr)\right|\leq\frac{8\log n}{m}=o(1).

For each deterministic
w
, the conditional failure probability is at most
C_{L}^{\prime}n^{-3}
, with a constant independent of
A
,
m
and
w
. This is a pointwise bound in
w
; no union over the factors is used.

Set
\ell=m-1
. By Theorem 1 of
[
27
]
and its
complex extension in Section 4 of the same paper,

\rho_{\ell,n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{\sigma_{j}(A)^{2/\ell}}\ \longrightarrow\ U[0,1]\quad\text{in probability},

since
\ell,n\to\infty
, with no restriction on their relative growth.
We next justify the use of a logarithmic test function. Independence and

\mathbb{E}[X_{j}X_{j}^{*}]=I_{n}
give

\mathbb{E}\|A\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=n
, for every
\ell
.
Therefore
\mathbb{P}(\|A\|>n^{2})\leq n^{-3}
, and outside this event
the support of
\rho_{\ell,n}
is contained in

[0,\exp(4\log n/\ell)]\subset[0,2]

for all sufficiently large
n
.
For fixed
r=|z|>0
, the functions

\frac{\ell}{2m}\log\max(t,r^{2m/\ell})
converge uniformly on

[0,2]
to
\frac{1}{2}\log\max(t,r^{2})
. They are continuous at zero
because the clipping threshold is positive. Consequently,

(6.3)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\max\bigl(\sigma_{j}(A),r^{m}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell}{2m}\int\log\max(t,r^{2m/\ell})\,d\rho_{\ell,n}(t)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\log\max(t,r^{2})\,dt=:\Phi(r)=\begin{cases}(r^{2}-1)/2,&0<r\leq 1,\\
\log r,&r\geq 1,\end{cases}

in probability. The function
z\mapsto\Phi(|z|)
is the logarithmic potential of the circular law.

Let
N=nm
and let
\mathcal{L}
be the cyclic linearization
(

, together with

\frac{1}{N}\log|\det(\mathcal{L}-zI_{N})|\longrightarrow\Phi(|z|)\quad\text{in probability}

for every fixed

N\times N

G_{N}

with entry variance

has the same shifted log-determinant

]

Moreover,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{N}\|\mathcal{L}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{N}\|G_{N}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\right]=1.

Markov’s inequality therefore gives the required Hilbert–Schmidt tightness, and the excluded point
z=0
has planar Lebesgue measure zero. Thus the replacement principle, Theorem
4.1
, applies along the dimensions

\mathcal{L}
. Since
\mu_{\mathcal{L}}=\nu_{P_{n}}^{(m_{n})}
