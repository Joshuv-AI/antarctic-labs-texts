Mind the Gap
Authors: Vivek Bhat, Subaina Khalid, Ankur Kalra
Published: 2025

Indigenous peoples around the world suffer a disproportionate burden of disease, with worse long-term health outcomes.1 These inequities are not random, reflecting the enduring legacy of colonial disenfranchisement that has undermined their health. In health care, this manifests as substantial barriers to access, and even when access is met, historical mistrust and systemic marginalization impair outcomes.

Heart failure (HF), one of the leading causes of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, is no exception. This is despite substantial progress in pharmacologic and device-based therapies that have improved outcomes worldwide. This viewpoint examines disparities in HF affecting Indigenous populations and outlines potential actionable strategies to address them.

Disparities in heart failure outcomes and care

The burden of HF among Indigenous populations is consistently greater than that of non-Indigenous populations across multiple regions. For example, Indigenous Australians reportedly have over twice the age-standardized prevalence of HF than non-Indigenous Australians, along with a substantially higher risk of mortality and hospitalization.2,3 Indigenous Canadians, specifically the Métis, continue to suffer an increasing incidence of HF, with an increase as high as 47% in males over the last decade.4,5 In New Zealand, incident HF hospitalization rates and HF-related mortality among the Maori and Pacific people have remained unchanged, despite overall population-level improvements.6,7 Finally, in the United States, similar HF burden differences between American Indians/Native Alaskans and other ethnicities persist.8,9

Contributors to these disparities extend from differences in baseline risk factors to inequities in management. Overall, Indigenous populations are often diagnosed with HF at a younger age. They carry a higher prevalence of comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, coronary artery disease, and renal disease.3, 4, 5 Additionally, conditions like rheumatic heart disease, considered part of bygone times in most high-income countries, remain prevalent.2,5

Access to care is markedly reduced, encompassing all components of the “Three Delays Framework”—seeking care, reaching care, and receiving care.5 Indigenous people often demonstrate less health-seeking behavior, even when medical attention is needed. For example, in Canada, Indigenous peoples are far more likely to have never had their blood pressure measured, and a tiny minority of known diabetic patients receive adequate cardiovascular screening.5 As a result, a significant proportion of HF cases remain undiagnosed. Geographic barriers compound these issues—Indigenous populations are more likely to live in rural, medically underserved regions with scarce cardiovascular services, requiring long travel to receive care.5,8 In the United States, the Indian Health Service, established explicitly to provide health care for American Indians, is maldistributed and often located far from areas with the highest density of American Indians, worsening access issues.8 These barriers limit continuity of care, which is essential to HF outcomes, and delay initiation of treatment after acute presentations. Furthermore, Indigenous peoples are disproportionately affected by adverse social determinants of health, including poverty, food insecurity, and limited education,2,5,8 which together contribute to delayed diagnosis and poorer adherence to care. Finally, Indigenous peoples more often suffer delays in receiving timely interventions, be it elective procedures after referral to specialist care, or even urgent ones, having arrived at a higher center. Often, this is attributable to institutional racism and a lack of culturally sensitive communication.5,7 The latter also contributes to the lack of health-seeking behavior—prior negative experiences discourage Indigenous peoples from seeking timely care. Overall, there is eroded trust, weakened patient-physician relationships, and undermined care.

Gaps in evidence

Despite increasing recognition of these disparities, significant lacunae remain in scientific literature. Existing literature has shed light on inequities in chronic conditions or cardiovascular disease broadly, but HF-specific work remains limited. Indigenous peoples are rarely included in HF clinical trials or large registries. Furthermore, existing research on Indigenous populations typically presents contemporary findings, while failing to acknowledge the historical context that shapes them.5 From a global perspective, most literature, including that cited in this work, is heavily skewed toward high-income countries, with almost no work on Indigenous populations from the Global South. Even these reports are not always representative, concentrating on a particular section of Indigenous peoples while missing others.5 HF care in the Indigenous populations is likely more complex, given the greater burden of comorbidities, as well as the need for culturally specific approaches. Potential biologic differences influencing the outcomes of established HF therapeutics also remain unexplored.

From acknowledgment to action: potential ways forward

Needless to say, these disparities represent a priority for decision-makers in HF care. Acknowledgment of the problem must progress to concrete action. Solutions are unlikely to be straightforward and would require a holistic review of existing systems to identify opportunities for improvement. Most solutions are likely not specific to HF care, but apply nonetheless.

Expanding access to care, supported by appropriate and sustained funding, is paramount. In the United States, for example, the Indian Health Service remains chronically underfunded and understaffed.8 Funding should aim to expand access to cardiology and HF services, including but not limited to greater telehealth access for elective consultations. This would facilitate timely referrals for procedures and advanced care. Funding should also address transportation barriers, so that residents of more remote regions can access services. Finally, it may be worth exploring the recruitment of Indigenous youth into medical training—this may facilitate long-term physician retention in more remote areas, while fostering cultural concordance with the local communities.5

Any interventions will fail without multifaceted, culturally sensitive approaches. Health care teams must receive training that meaningfully integrates Indigenous perspectives and customs, while also recognizing the enduring impact of historical trauma. Partnership between health care systems and local Indigenous communities can bridge the gap between Indigenous customs and modern medicine.

Social upliftment of these communities remains essential. Targeted initiatives addressing poverty and food insecurity in these communities would likely improve outcomes. Early educational efforts regarding cardiovascular risk factors, beginning at the school level, would also help address disparities in cardiovascular risk factors. These efforts should integrate Indigenous languages and cultural frameworks so that health messaging resonates with local knowledge, and communities understand social interactions with health care.

Finally, greater incentives are needed to support research on HF in Indigenous peoples. Targeted mentorship programs, dedicated grants, and incorporation of culturally sensitive, relevant education in researchers’ training would aid this goal. The representation of Indigenous peoples in HF-related trials and registries could be tracked as a quality metric, aimed at halting their historical exclusion from influential literature. This research must include Indigenous people at the decision-making table, not just as visiting advisors.10 This would ensure that research addresses local priorities and respects their sovereignty. Global coalitions, such as the Lancet-Lowitja Institute,1 can drive broader projects and shine light on common underpinnings of disparities in these populations globally.

Conclusions

Thus, Indigenous people worldwide are worse off at each point in the HF “pipeline”—they have a greater prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors exacerbated by sociodemographic disadvantages, they are more likely to develop HF, face reduced access to adequate care, and experience worse HF outcomes. Finally, they remain under-represented in HF literature, perpetuating inequities. Without targeted efforts to address underlying systemic inequities, advances in HF care will continue to bypass Indigenous peoples and perpetuate disparities. Addressing these lacunae is essential to achieving equitable HF care and outcomes for Indigenous peoples worldwide.
