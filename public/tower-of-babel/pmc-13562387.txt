Severe pulmonary vein stenosis presenting with infection-like pulmonary findings after radiofrequency ablation for atrial fibrillation: A case report
Authors: Qingdi Du, Zongsheng Tian, Jiahao Wang, Xueliang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report a case of severe pulmonary vein stenosis(PVS) with complete pulmonary vein occlusion(PVO) after radiofrequency ablation for atrial fibrillation(AF). A 51-year-old man presented to the respiratory department with chest tightness, dyspnea, and chest pain. Early chest computed tomography (CT) revealed inflammatory changes in the left lung and bilateral pleural effusions, and the patient responded poorly to antibiotic therapy. He had undergone radiofrequency catheter ablation for AF 5 months prior. On admission, inflammatory markers were not significantly elevated, procalcitonin was negative, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) culture and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) revealed no definitive pathogenic infection. Subsequent cardiac CTA showed non-visualization of the left inferior pulmonary vein and stenosis of the left superior pulmonary vein near the left atrium, leading to a diagnosis of post-ablation pulmonary vein occlusion and stenosis. The patient underwent pulmonary vein stenting at another hospital, with follow-up CTA demonstrating restored opacification distal to the stents without intraluminal filling defects. This case highlights the need for prompt evaluation of pulmonary vein patency when focal pulmonary infection-like changes, chest pain, or pleural effusion occur after atrial fibrillation ablation when infection is not confirmed.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Catheter ablation, Pulmonary vein occlusion, Pulmonary vein stenosis, Pulmonary infection, Bronchoscopy

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common type of cardiac arrhythmia, and its incidence increases with age. The Global Burden of Disease study estimated that AF affects approximately 33.5 million individuals worldwide [1]. AF can lead to heart failure, stroke, and cardiovascular-related hospitalization and mortality. Early intervention for paroxysmal AF may reduce the risk of these complications and alleviate the healthcare burden [2]. Current options for rhythm control in AF include pharmacological cardioversion, electrical cardioversion, radiofrequency catheter ablation, and surgical ablation; among these, radiofrequency catheter ablation has become a first-line treatment for rhythm control in paroxysmal AF [3].

Pulmonary vein stenosis (PVS) or pulmonary vein occlusion (PVO) is an uncommon but important complication following catheter ablation for AF, with an incidence of severe PVS reported at 0.5%–4% [4,5]. The development of this complication is typically associated with thermal injury to the pulmonary vein wall from radiofrequency energy [6]. Its clinical presentation lacks specificity and may manifest as chest tightness, dyspnea, chest pain, hemoptysis, pleural effusion, or recurrent pneumonia-like imaging changes, often leading patients to initially present to respiratory departments. Without active inquiry into the history of ablation and pulmonary vein pathology, patients are prone to misdiagnosis with pulmonary infection, pulmonary embolism, or interstitial lung disease, resulting in diagnostic delay. Such diagnostic delays are associated with stenosis progression, severe adverse events, and significant pulmonary parenchymal damage, and can be fatal if not treated in a timely manner [7,8].

We describe a patient who developed left inferior pulmonary vein occlusion and left superior pulmonary vein stenosis following radiofrequency catheter ablation for AF. The clinical presentation, diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of this complication are discussed to raise clinical awareness.

Case presentation

A 51-year-old man was admitted with a 20-day history of chest tightness and dyspnea. Twenty days prior to admission, the patient developed chest tightness, dyspnea, and chest pain. The patient was diagnosed with pulmonary infection and received piperacillin-tazobactam for 7 days with poor response at the local hospital. Two days prior to admission, he developed persistent left posterior back pain that worsened with deep inspiration, prompting presentation to our hospital. His past medical history was notable for cerebral infarction 7 months prior, for which he was taking aspirin and rosuvastatin. Five months before admission, he had undergone radiofrequency ablation for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. Pre-procedural cardiac CTA showed that the anteroposterior diameter of the left atrium was approximately 39 mm; the left and right superior and inferior pulmonary veins followed a natural course, showed no luminal stenosis, and drained separately into the left atrium (Fig. 1A and C). During the ablation procedure, power was delivered at 35–45 W with a temperature limit of 43°C and an irrigated saline flow rate of 17–30 mL/min.Circumferential pulmonary vein isolation was achieved with additional focal ablation at the anterior and posterior crossover regions, confirmed by pacing with the Pentaray electrode and ablation catheter at the end of the procedure. Post-procedurally, the patient was prescribed rivaroxaban, rosuvastatin calcium, and sotalol. On physical examination, breath sounds were coarse bilaterally without crackles or wheezes; heart rhythm was regular with no pathological murmurs at any valve areas; the abdomen was soft without tenderness or rebound tenderness; and there was no lower extremity edema.

Laboratory investigations revealed a white blood cell count of 7.24 × 109/L with a neutrophil ratio of 67.7%, C-reactive protein of 10.1 mg/L, and procalcitonin <0.04 ng/mL; D-dimer was 1.51 μg/mL FEU and fibrinogen was 4.47 g/L; troponin T was 0.004 ng/mL and NT-proBNP was 90.1 pg/mL. Tuberculosis infection T-cell test was positive; (1,3)-β-D-glucan test (G-test) and galactomannan antigen test (GM-test) were both negative. Electrocardiography demonstrated sinus rhythm. Echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 60% and an estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure of 31 mmHg, without evidence of significant pulmonary hypertension. These findings did not support typical bacterial, fungal, or tuberculous infection, and did not account for the poor response to antibiotic therapy. Chest CT showed pneumonia-like changes in the left lower lung with pleural effusion (Fig. 2A).

To further clarify the etiology, bronchoscopy was performed. The examination revealed scattered fresh bloody secretions in the trachea; the submucosal vessels of the left main bronchus were dilated, tortuous, and prominent, with marked mucosal hyperemia (Fig. 2B); the bronchial mucosa of the left lower lobe apical segment was hyperemic, with scant bloody secretions and white mucous plugs. Bronchoalveolar lavage was performed in the left lower lobe and samples were sent for testing. BALF culture demonstrated only normal oropharyngeal flora; mNGS detected low-level human herpesvirus 7 (RPTM 20) but no bacteria, fungi, parasites, drug resistance genes, or virulence genes. Given the patient's history of AF radiofrequency ablation and lack of convincing evidence of infection, cardiac CTA was performed. The results demonstrated a left atrial anteroposterior diameter of approximately 31 mm; the left inferior pulmonary vein was not visualized, consistent with complete occlusion, and the left superior pulmonary vein showed focal luminal stenosis near the left atrium (Fig. 1B and D), whereas the right superior and inferior pulmonary veins displayed a normal course without stenosis. In conjunction with the pre-ablation CTA demonstrating patent pulmonary veins bilaterally, the current findings were consistent with post-ablation left inferior pulmonary vein occlusion and left superior pulmonary vein stenosis.

The patient was subsequently transferred to another hospital for further treatment and underwent stenting of the left superior and left inferior pulmonary veins. Post-procedural follow-up CT demonstrated stent shadows at the origins of the left superior and inferior pulmonary veins with contrast opacification distally and no significant filling defects within the lumina; a left atrial appendage occluder was also visualized. Concurrent echocardiography revealed stent echogenicity within the left superior pulmonary vein with a pulsed-wave Doppler-estimated flow velocity of approximately 0.75 m/s; the left inferior pulmonary vein was poorly visualized but appeared to show stent echogenicity. The estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure was approximately 35 mmHg, and left ventricular systolic function was normal. The clinical course is summarized in Fig. 3.

Discussion

Radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) has become one of the commonly used treatments for AF. The core procedure of RFCA is pulmonary vein isolation (PVI), and PVS related to RFCA is one of the complications after AF ablation. Because of its non-specific symptoms, PVS carries a high misdiagnosis rate, and delayed treatment may lead to disease progression and even PVO [6]. The 2017 consensus statement on AF ablation classifies PVS based on the degree of luminal narrowing as mild (<50%), moderate (50%-70%), or severe (>70%) [9]. The severity of PVS symptoms depends on the degree of stenosis and the number of affected pulmonary veins. Early studies of PVS after PVI showed that patients with stenosis >60%-70% became symptomatic, particularly when multiple pulmonary veins were involved, whereas patients with moderate or lesser degrees of stenosis were asymptomatic [6]. In the present case, the patient had left inferior pulmonary vein occlusion and focal luminal stenosis of the left superior pulmonary vein, presenting with chest tightness, dyspnea, and chest pain.

With accumulated operator experience and advances in three-dimensional mapping, wide-area antral pulmonary vein isolation, and avoidance of ablation within the pulmonary veins, the incidence of AF ablation-related PVS has declined substantially compared with the early era [5,9]. Novel non-thermal energy sources such as pulsed field ablation may further reduce pulmonary vein narrowing compared with thermal ablation [10]. However, PVS can still result in serious clinical consequences. Its occurrence is often related to ablation performed too close to, or within, the pulmonary vein ostium [6,7,9]. Thermal injury to the pulmonary vein wall causes endothelial damage, coagulative necrosis, and local inflammation; these changes may promote fibroblast proliferation, collagen deposition, intimal hyperplasia, and scar contracture, ultimately resulting in progressive luminal narrowing or complete venous occlusion [6,7]. The risk of PVS may be increased by procedural factors including deep ablation sites, excessive energy delivery, high catheter contact force, and prolonged ablation at a single site, as well as by pulmonary venous anatomical variations and insufficient operator experience [[5], [6], [7],9].

Pulmonary vein CTA is the most commonly used first-line non-invasive imaging modality for the diagnosis of ablation-related PVS, as it can directly visualize the pulmonary vein ostia, degree of luminal stenosis, extent of occlusion, and distal pulmonary parenchymal changes. Other investigations such as transesophageal echocardiography, pulmonary ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) scintigraphy, and cardiac MRI may serve as adjunctive diagnostic tools. For patients being considered for interventional therapy or when non-invasive findings are discordant, selective pulmonary venography with trans-stenotic pressure gradient measurement can further define the severity of the lesion [6]. The incidence of PVS is higher on the left side than on the right, with the left inferior pulmonary vein carrying the greatest risk, which is consistent with our case. This may be attributed to the anatomical characteristics of the left pulmonary veins, including the smaller caliber of the left inferior pulmonary vein, the more cranial course of the left superior pulmonary vein, and the Coumadin ridge near the left atrial appendage, which may result in ablation lines being positioned closer to the pulmonary vein ostia [11,12].

A notable finding in this case was the bronchoscopic appearance of dilated, tortuous submucosal vessels with mucosal hyperemia in the left main bronchus. These changes reflect elevated pulmonary venous pressure transmitted retrogradely into the bronchial venous plexus, a phenomenon described in the setting of pulmonary venous hypertension [13,14]. Although bronchoscopy is not routinely performed in the diagnostic workup of PVS, these findings may serve as an important clue when initial presentation mimics pulmonary infection. Similarly, the focal opacity in the left lower lung on CT, initially interpreted as pneumonia, corresponds to the drainage territory of the occluded left inferior pulmonary vein. PVO elevates capillary hydrostatic pressure, producing exudative changes that mimic infectious pneumonitis on imaging. In the present case, the combination of antibiotic-refractory symptoms, unremarkable inflammatory markers (normal white blood cell count, low C-reactive protein, negative procalcitonin), and negative BALF culture and mNGS argued against infection, while the bronchoscopic findings and history of AF ablation prompted cardiac CTA, which established the diagnosis. Respiratory physicians should maintain a low threshold for pulmonary vein CTA when evaluating post-ablation patients with persistent respiratory symptoms and negative infectious workup.

For ablation-related PVS, prevention is essential. With the advancement of wide-area antral pulmonary vein isolation, three-dimensional mapping, contact force monitoring, and novel non-thermal energy ablation technologies, the incidence of PVS is expected to further decrease [5,10]. Once the diagnosis is established, the treatment strategy should be promptly evaluated based on symptoms, degree of stenosis, extent of pulmonary perfusion impairment, and disease duration. For symptomatic severe stenosis or progressive stenosis, balloon angioplasty and stent implantation are the primary percutaneous interventional approaches; stent implantation generally provides superior luminal maintenance, although long-term follow-up is required for restenosis and thrombosis-related complications. For a small subset of patients with chronic total occlusion in whom interventional therapy has failed or is not feasible, surgical resection may be considered following multidisciplinary evaluation [6,12,15].

Conclusion

Post-ablation pulmonary vein stenosis or occlusion poses a diagnostic challenge because of its non-specific presentation. When respiratory symptoms after AF ablation fail to respond to antibiotics, pulmonary vein patency should be evaluated early by cardiac CTA. Timely diagnosis and intervention can prevent irreversible pulmonary parenchymal damage and potentially fatal outcomes.
