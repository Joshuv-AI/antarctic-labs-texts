Non-elastic membrane dual-actuation liquid lens for continuous focus and zoom
Authors: Jing Zhang, Wen-xia Zhang, Hao Shen, Yu Lu, Ye-xi Jin, Yi-zhu Liu, Guang-yao Chen, Hao Wu, Li-wei Xue, Li-ning Sun, Li-guo Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMiniaturization is crucial for technological advancement and innovation. Simplifying systems, reducing device size, and integrating multiple functions remain limiting factors for the miniaturization of optical imaging systems. Liquid lenses offer compact structure, fast response, and no mechanical parts, demonstrating potential for miniaturized imaging and rapid focusing. However, existing designs focus primarily on single-focus function, failing to simultaneously achieve fast continuous focusing, zoom capability, and reliable sealing in practical applications. This has resulted in limitations to the full realisation of its unique advantages, significantly restricting its application prospects in scenarios such as micro-visualisation and mobile imaging terminals. To address the issue, this study presents a compact dual-actuation multifunctional liquid lens. For the first time, a non-membrane, sealed dual-cavity design is employed to integrate both focusing and zoom functions into a single device. The lens employs two actuation modes—electrowetting-on-dielectric and electromagnetic induction—to adjust the curvature and axial position of the liquid–liquid interface, enabling an integrated compact lens that can be controlled entirely by electrical signals. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed lens achieves continuous focusing with 100 ms response time and provides smooth zoom from 1× to 1.5×, together with high resolution and stable imaging quality. With its simple architecture and integrated functions, the proposed liquid lens overcomes the functional and packaging limitations of traditional non-membrane liquid lenses. By combining focusing and zooming in one element, it further reduces the complexity of imaging systems and shows strong potential for applications in microscopy, optical inspection, and intelligent vision devices. Optical imaging systems serve as essential instruments for observation and exploration of the world, finding extensive application across fields such as biomedical imaging, consumer electronics, and industrial inspection. To obtain clear images, the imaging system requires effective zoom and rapid focusing capabilities. However, the system complexity and bulkiness resulting from the integration of these functions severely limit miniaturisation and deployment across multiple scenes. Consequently, the miniaturisation, lightweight design and functional integration of optical systems have been long-standing objectives, aimed at continually enhancing convenience and application flexibility.

To enhance the integration of optical imaging systems, the limited assembly space imposes greater demands on optical components. However, traditional optical lenses typically require complex systems with mechanical displacement to achieve focusing and zoom functionality. This leads to inherent issues such as inefficient adjustment and bulky dimensions1,2. In contrast, liquid lens technology, as a novel adaptive optical component, enables rapid focusing without mechanical parts by utilising the adjustable properties of liquid interfaces. It offers advantages such as compact structure and high dynamic performance, and low manufacturing costs3–10. Consequently, by incorporating multifunctional, compact liquid lenses, it is a promising prospect to enhance efficiency while reducing system complexity and physical dimensions.

According to different driving mechanisms, liquid lenses can be mainly classified into two types: directly driven and indirectly driven. Directly driven liquid lenses are typically represented by electrowetting-on-dielectric (EWOD)11–16 and dielectrophoresis17, which directly act on the liquid interface without mechanical transmission structures. This type provides advantages such as a simple structure, fast response, and easy integration with miniaturized systems. In particular, EWOD-based liquid lenses adjust the wetting properties of a liquid on a solid surface by applying a voltage, enabling fast and continuous tuning of the focal length. However, EWOD actuation can only modify the curvature of the liquid–liquid interface. As a result, it is mainly used for focal length tuning and has difficulty in achieving independent magnification variation. Indirectly driven liquid lenses are mainly represented by elastomer-membrane pressure-driven systems18–22. In these devices, an electromagnetic actuator or a syringe-based actuation applies pressure to the liquid in a sealed cavity, deforming an elastic membrane to achieve zooming. This approach offers a large tuning range and allows for larger apertures. However, the introduction of an elastic membrane also brings inherent limitations, including fabrication variability (such as non-uniform membrane thickness and residual stress) and mechanical fatigue under long-term cyclic deformation.

As the demand for multifunctional capabilities in applications such as microscopic imaging23–27 and optical sensing continues to increase, single function is no longer sufficient. Consequently, a limited number of studies have begun to explore the integration of both focusing and magnification functions within a single liquid lens device. Zhang et al.28,29 demonstrated a method in which liquid column height and the effective aperture position are modified by injecting or withdrawing liquid, thereby enabling both focusing and discrete magnification changes within a single lens. This approach overcomes the limitation of conventional EWOD systems in terms of magnification control. However, the magnification adjustment is stepwise rather than continuous. More importantly, the injection and withdrawal of liquid rely on an external pump, meaning that the system is difficult to be fully sealed, which limits its integration level and reliability in practical applications. Park et al.30 combined electrowetting with an elastic membrane to achieve both continuous focal length tuning and scaling. However, the open cylindrical cavity used in their design exposes the liquid directly to the environment, failing to ensure proper sealing and introducing risks such as evaporation, contamination, and leakage. In addition, the structure still depends on an elastic membrane, which inherits the aforementioned membrane-related limitations. Furthermore, its cylindrical cavity lacks a self-centering design, and the optical axis stability during both focusing and zooming operations is not well guaranteed. It should be noted that fast zoom capability is also of great importance in other emerging optical systems, such as liquid lens–based sparse compound-eye cameras31 and multi-wavelength metasurface holographic systems with zoom functionality32. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop easily integrable liquid lenses that simultaneously enable both focusing and zooming functions to meet the broad requirements of advanced imaging applications.

In summary, although previous studies have attempted to integrate focusing and zooming functions, achieving continuous and reliable control of both functions using purely electrical signals within a single membrane-free and fully sealed compact device remains an unresolved key challenge in this field, which significantly limits the application of liquid lenses in integrated optical systems.

To address this issue, this work proposes a membrane-free and fully sealed dual-actuation liquid lens, which, for the first time, integrates EWOD-based continuous focusing and electromagnetic-induction-based continuous zooming within a single device (Fig. 1). The proposed design enables focal length adjustment with a response time of 100 ms and achieves continuous zooming from 1× to 1.5×. Additionally, a unique dual-cavity design has been implemented to reduce system complexity and ensure stable imaging, facilitating effective integration with microscopes, optical inspection systems, and portable imaging systems. We believe that this liquid lens provides a novel technical pathway for achieving multifunctional integration within a compactly packaged device, attaining a balance between structural compactness, optical performance, and actuation efficiency. The proposed system, which is characterised by small volume, rapid response and ease of integration, has broad potential for applications in microscopic imaging, consumer optics, endoscopic systems and other fields requiring adaptive multifunctional optical solutions.

Theory and principles

The proposed multifunctional liquid lens employs both the EWOD principle and electromagnetic induction for actuation through its dual inner and outer cavities. Figure 2 illustrates its structure and operating principle. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, the liquid lens is composed of upper and lower viewing windows, a truncated conical inner cavity, a coil outer cavity, a support ring, an internal magnetic ring between the inner and outer cavities, and polar and non-polar liquids filling the cavity. The inner cavity is electrically connected to the upper viewing window. To ensure fluid communication between the interior and exterior, the groove machined into its top, combined with the space between the bottom and the lower viewing window, consequently enables the formation of a communicating cavity with the outer cavity. The curved surface formed by surface tension between the two liquid interfaces within the inner cavity serves as the primary active surface. Light focusing and defocusing are achieved via alterations to its curvature (Fig. 2b, d), while image zooming is realized by adjusting its axial position (Fig. 2b, c). These two processes are achieved through two distinct drive mechanisms: The first employs the principle of EWOD to alter the contact angle of the fluid within the inner cavity, thereby modifying the curvature of the interface. The second utilises electromagnetic induction and the principle of communicating vessels to adjust the height of the liquid column within the inner cavity, thus changing the axial position of the interface.

Continuous zoom mechanism driven by electromagnetic induction

The proposed liquid lens achieves continuous zoom primarily through the combined action of a coil-based outer cavity and magnetic rings, operating based on the principles of communicating vessels (Fig. 2h) and electromagnetic induction (Fig. 2c). In geometric optics, image magnification can be adjusted by moving the lens along the optical axis while keeping the object stationary. In a liquid lens, this adjustment is achieved by controlling the axial position of the liquid interface. In a communicating-vessel configuration (Fig. 2h), for a fixed liquid volume, an increase in the volume of the lower cavity leads to a reduction in the height of the liquid column. Accordingly, the axial position of the liquid interface can be modulated by designing the lens to vary the effective volume of the communicating region. In the proposed design, this functionality is achieved by introducing an axially movable magnet ring between the inner and outer cavities.

Initially (Fig. 2b), the internal magnet ring resides at the bottom of the lens, repelled by the opposite polarity of the top magnet ring. Occupying a larger portion of the space containing the polar liquid, it maintains a relatively high liquid column in the inner cavity. The axial movement of the internal magnet ring is controlled via electromagnetic induction. When the solenoid coil is energized (Fig. 2c), the generated magnetic field exerts an upward force on the magnet ring, increasing the effective volume of the lower communicating region. As a result, additional polar liquid flows into this region, lowering the liquid interface in the inner cavity and thereby enabling zoom adjustment. As the current increases, the electromagnetic force on the magnet ring grows, causing it to rise until it reaches a new equilibrium under the repulsive force exerted by the top magnet, thereby enabling continuous adjustment of the lens magnification. Inner cavity with self-centering functionality

The imaging stability of a liquid lens largely depends on the axial symmetry and stability of the liquid–liquid interface. By designing the geometry of the inner cavity, a natural “potential well” can be created, ensuring that the liquid droplet returns to the cavity center. To maintain optical axis stability, the present study incorporates a truncated-conical geometry in the inner cavity, with the taper angle denoted as α. As shown in Fig. 3a, the liquid–liquid interface is constrained by the conical cavity to preserve axial symmetry. Even under external perturbations, such as vibrations induced by interface deformations during voltage adjustment, the interface generates a restoring force, thereby enabling self-centering of the optical axis34. Nevertheless, by combining COMSOL and Zemax simulations (as shown in “Materials and Methods”), the variations in diopter and focal length under multiple driving voltages for cavities with different inclination angles were obtained (Figs. 3c, d). The results reveal that, under the same aperture condition, a smaller inclination angle—despite its lower self-centering energy and weaker stability—exhibits higher focusing sensitivity at low voltages, as it produces larger changes in diopter and focal length per unit voltage. In addition, as shown in Fig. 3e, the variation of the liquid interface vertex height under different inclination angles indicates that a smaller inclination angle leads to a smaller amplitude of height change during actuation, thereby exerting a weaker influence on the lens magnification. Furthermore, the normalized response-time heatmap in Fig. 3f demonstrates that a larger inclination angle significantly prolongs the lens response time. Taking both optical-axis stability and driving efficiency into consideration, an inclination angle of α = 15° was ultimately selected to achieve a balance between imaging stability and response speed.

Electromagnetic structure for zooming

The zooming capability of the proposed liquid lens is achieved by electromagnetically actuating the internal magnetic ring, which reduces the liquid column height in the inner cavity. To determine the effective driving range and evaluate thermal effects, electromagnetic and thermal simulations of the zoom mechanism were performed (see Supplementary Information S1). The thermal simulation results show that the coil current should be limited to below 350 mA to avoid excessive heating. The electromagnetic simulation further shows that the coil produces only an axial attractive force on the magnetic ring, which increases monotonically with current at a fixed position.

To determine the effective working range of the magnetic ring, Δz is defined as the axial offset of the magnetic ring center relative to the coil center, where Δz<0 indicates that the ring center is located below the coil center (Fig. 4a). As shown in Fig. 4b, since the magnetic ring moves axially inside the coil, the attractive forces from above and below cancel symmetrically when Δz=0, resulting in a net force of zero. Within the range ∣Δz∣<5 mm, the axial force increases monotonically with ∣Δz∣. To ensure that the driving force remains monotonically controllable throughout operation, the working range was therefore limited to ∣Δz∣<5 mm.

Electromagnetic actuation design and validation. a Schematic illustration of the axial offset Δz between the coil and the magnetic ring centers. b Simulated electromagnetic force on the magnetic ring as a function of Δz at an excitation current of 350 mA. c–f Experimental results: c Electromagnetic force on the magnetic ring as a function of current at different axial offsets. d Lifting height of the magnetic ring as a function of current, showing saturation beyond 200 mA. e Coil resistance as a function of current in the range of 0–200 mA. f Coil temperature as a function of operating time at an excitation current of 200 mA

In the present design, the magnetic ring is initially positioned with its bottom face aligned with the lower window. A 2 mm support ring is added beneath the coil, setting the initial center offset to ∣Δz∣=2.5 mm. Figure 4c shows the experimentally measured electromagnetic attractive force as a function of current at different ring positions. The attractive force increases approximately linearly with current, and decreases as ∣Δz∣ decreases (i.e., as the ring moves closer to the coil center) under the same current, consistent with the simulation results. Accordingly, when the magnetic ring is held at a given position, increasing the current allows the electromagnetic force to exceed gravity, driving the ring upward. As the ring rises, ∣Δz∣ decreases and the electromagnetic force under the same current is reduced, so the ring eventually reaches a new equilibrium where the electromagnetic force balances gravity. Figure 4d shows the variation of the axial offset Δz of the magnetic ring relative to the coil center as a function of current. The ring position rises progressively with increasing current, showing a trend of rapid initial rise followed by a gradual slowdown, and approaches approximately −0.2 mm at 180 mA. These results are consistent with the theoretical analysis and simulation predictions.

Figure 4e shows the variation of coil resistance with current. The resistance remains at approximately 5 Ω, with an increase of no more than 1.2 Ω, indicating that changes in current do not cause significant coil heating. Figure 4f shows the coil temperature as a function of energization time under the maximum operating current. The temperature remains below 30 °C throughout, satisfying the operating requirements of the liquid lens.

Fabrication process

Figure 5 illustrates the fabrication process and structural integration of the liquid lens. As shown in Fig. 5a, the fabrication process mainly consists of four steps: fabrication of the optical windows, functional layer coating of the inner cavity, construction of the outer cavity, and magnetic ring treatment. The optical window surface is coated with an anti-reflection layer and a transparent electrode to improve imaging performance and ensure electrical connectivity. The inner cavity is coated with dielectric and hydrophobic layers to regulate interfacial electrical insulation and wettability. The magnetic ring and the inner wall of the outer cavity are surface-modified to enhance corrosion resistance and reduce friction during actuation. These components are then integrated through assembly, liquid filling, and encapsulation, enabling reliable electrode routing, stable mechanical confinement, and long-term sealing. Detailed fabrication procedures and encapsulation strategies are provided in the “Materials and Methods” section and the Supplementary Information.

To enhance the EWOD driving performance of the liquid lens, experimental investigations were conducted using the setup shown in Fig. 2f (details see Materials and Methods section). The contact angle variation of the liquid droplet during actuation follows the Young–Lippmann equation given in Eq. (2), where the dielectric constant ε and thickness d of the dielectric layer are the key factors determining the relationship between driving voltage and contact angle modulation. To identify a suitable dielectric material, Fig. 5b compares the electrowetting performance (driving voltage and contact angle range) of SU-8, Parylene C, PDMS, and Acrylic Acid. The results indicate that, at the same thickness, PDMS provides the largest contact angle range but requires excessively high voltages, leading to increased power consumption. In contrast, both SU-8 and Parylene C exhibit significantly lower voltages with comparable contact angle ranges. However, the relatively narrow operating voltage window of SU-8 results in greater variation in contact angle per unit voltage change, which is unfavourable for precise control. Considering both driving efficiency and control accuracy, Parylene C was selected as the dielectric material. Furthermore, Fig. 5c presents the operational voltage ranges for different thicknesses of Parylene C. As the thickness increases, the dielectric breakdown voltage is significantly enhanced, thereby enabling a broader tuning range. Nevertheless, excessive thickness leads to higher initial driving voltages and longer response times, while insufficient thickness poses a risk of dielectric breakdown. Based on a comprehensive evaluation of voltage range, controllability, and stability, the optimized dielectric parameter was determined to be Parylene C with a thickness of 5 μm.

Focusing performance characterization

To characterize the intrinsic response of the liquid lens, a laser–photodetector system was used, as detailed in the Materials and Methods section. The dynamic response can be described by two metrics: (1) the rise/fall time, which characterizes the time for the initial signal transition, and (2) the settling time, which characterizes the time required to suppress the residual oscillation. Five repeated measurements show an actuation (rise) time of ~100 ms with a ± 5% settling time of ~150 ms (Fig. 6a), and a recovery (fall) time of ~230 ms with a ± 5% settling time of ~350 ms (Fig. 6b). The damped oscillation after the initial transition mainly arises from the viscous damping associated with the large fluid volume, which is necessary for the lens to accommodate the magnetic ring mechanism. The imaging resolution was quantified by measuring the modulation transfer function (MTF) using a USAF 1951 target and the contrast transfer method. As shown in Fig. 6c, all curves exhibit highly consistent trends, with MTF remaining above 0.8 below 15 lp/mm (Table S1). The negligible variation across voltages indicates that curvature changes of the liquid–liquid interface do not introduce significant additional aberrations. Figure 6d illustrates the focal lengths of the liquid lens at different microscope magnifications, indicating that the lens provides a focal range sufficient to meet the requirements of microscopic observation. To evaluate repeatability and stability, image clarity was measured over 30–80 V under both increasing and decreasing voltage sweeps, with five repeated measurements at each point. As shown in Fig. 6e, f, within 30–65 V, the coefficient of variation (CV) remains below 5% for most conditions. At 70–80 V, increased CV (15–44%) is mainly due to severe defocus, where the clarity metric approaches its noise floor, leading to higher relative fluctuations. The overlap between forward and reverse curves indicates good reversibility of the liquid–liquid interface. No leakage, bubble formation, or dielectric degradation was observed during testing.

Zooming performance characterization

Figure 7 shows the zooming performance of the microscope achieved by adjusting the current applied to the coil. Figure 7a presents the image of a resolution target without the current application, revealing a relatively wide field of view accompanied by pronounced vignetting at the periphery. When a current of 180 mA is supplied to the coil, the magnetic ring inside the liquid lens is drawn upward by the electromagnetic force, reducing the height of the central liquid column. Consequently, a magnified image is obtained (Fig. 7c), where the finer resolution lines become more discernible, and the peripheral vignetting is alleviated. To quantitatively evaluate imaging quality, three representative regions (large-, medium-, and small-scale features) were analyzed before and after magnification. As shown in Fig. 7b, red, blue, and green lines denote the selected regions, which are schematically illustrated from the orange-boxed area in Fig. 7a, c. For each region, the contrast profiles along the marked lines were extracted from both the initial and the magnified images, and the resulting curves were superimposed for comparison, as shown in Fig. 7d–f. The results demonstrate that the microscope equipped with the liquid lens maintains consistent imaging quality across various areas of the field of view under different driving currents.

From the perspective of spatial frequency, the large-feature region (Fig. 7d) exhibits contrast curves with pronounced amplitudes corresponding to bright and dark stripes, suggesting that the system maintains stable imaging performance at low spatial frequencies. In the small and medium regions (Fig. 7e, f), the contrast amplitude decreases as the spatial frequency increases. However, the overall trends of the curves before and after magnification remain closely aligned, allowing the stripe structures to be clearly distinguished. This demonstrates that the proposed liquid lens introduces no significant imaging distortion during zooming, thereby ensuring the reliability of the variable-magnification imaging process. From the viewpoint of spatial distribution, the contrast deviations between different magnifications remain small in the left (Fig. 7d) and central (Fig. 7e) areas, further confirming the consistency of image quality. Although a slightly larger discrepancy before and after magnification is observed in the right-hand region (Fig. 7f), it remains within acceptable limits. This phenomenon may be attributed to the quasi-spherical imaging characteristics of the liquid lens, as this region is located farther from the optical axis.

Imaging performance and applications

To evaluate the imaging performance of the fabricated liquid lens in microscopic applications, the experimental system was established as illustrated in Fig. 8a. The liquid lens was inserted into the optical path between the objective lens and the camera, acting as an electrically tunable element for both focusing and zooming. Figure 8b shows the assembled liquid lens and the auxiliary components forming the imaging module. To demonstrate the focusing and zooming capabilities of the system, microscopic imaging experiments were conducted on a sample featuring the words “Liquid” and “Lens.” Figs. 8c, d show images focused on the foreground (“Liquid”) and background (“Lens”), respectively, corresponding to a depth difference of 3 cm. Figure 8e–g present images acquired at driving currents of 0 A, 100 mA, and 180 mA, respectively, with the lens focused on the foreground. As the driving current increases, the zooming capability becomes progressively more pronounced, achieving an optical magnification of approximately 1.5×. To further demonstrate practical applicability, a multi-depth PCB imaging experiment was conducted. As shown in Fig. 8h–j, the liquid lens was used to image electronic components with different height levels on a printed circuit board. Under the control of a depth-aware focusing strategy, the system was able to rapidly switch focus between low-profile and high-profile components without any mechanical movement. In addition, the zooming function enabled enhanced visualization of selected regions, improving the visibility of fine circuit features. This result demonstrates the capability of the proposed system for fast, multi-depth microscopic imaging in real-world inspection scenarios.

Discussion

In this study, a novel multifunctional liquid lens was developed, featuring an innovative dual-cavity design that, for the first time, integrates EWOD-based continuous focusing and electromagnetic-driven continuous zooming within a single sealed device without the use of an elastic membrane. The inner cavity provides EWOD-based focusing, while the outer cavity enables electromagnetic-driven zooming. Experimental characterization confirmed that the lens achieves a focusing response time of approximately 100 ms under a 50 V driving voltage, continuous magnification tuning from 1× to 1.5× under a driving current of 0–180 mA, and stable MTF performance. To contextualize the contribution of this work, Table 1 compares the architectural characteristics of the proposed liquid lens alongside representative state-of-the-art tunable liquid lenses. The proposed design is the only solution that simultaneously satisfies membrane-free structure, fully sealed enclosure, continuous focusing, and continuous zooming within a single self-contained device.

Despite these advantages, several limitations of the current prototype should be acknowledged, which also point to directions for future work. First, the current 1×–1.5× zoom range under a 1× objective configuration is mainly limited by the magnetic ring’s geometric travel (~2.3 mm). The range can be expanded through three pathways: (1) modifying the device structure to increase the liquid column height variation range Δh; (2) using higher-magnification objectives to amplify the magnification effect of the liquid lens; and (3) exploiting the additional magnification modulation from voltage-tuned focal length fL(U), enabling voltage–current cooperative zoom control. Second, the electromagnetic zoom mechanism relies on gravity to establish the magnetic ring equilibrium position, making the zoom function orientation-dependent. For applications requiring non-vertical observation, an optical folding element (e.g., mirror or prism) can redirect the imaging path to a vertical orientation without modifying the lens design. Third, contact angle hysteresis, charge trapping, and dielectric memory effects are inherent electrohydrodynamic challenges in EWOD systems. In the present design, contact line pinning and contact angle hysteresis together produce an apparent threshold voltage of approximately 30 V. Long-term contact angle drift, associated with Teflon AF surface degradation and dielectric fatigue, has important implications for the operational lifetime of the device and will be systematically investigated in future studies. In addition, a comprehensive dynamic model of the electromagnetic actuation, including magnetic force, inertia, viscous damping, and fluid–interface coupling, will also be developed to support high-frequency dynamic applications. Moreover, it should be noted that, although the theoretical decoupling analysis based on the two-group optical model indicates that focusing and zooming can be approximately independently controlled under a “focus-first, then zoom” sequential control strategy, direct experimental verification has not yet been carried out. Measuring the focal length and magnification under different driving voltage–current combinations would allow the residual cross-coupling to be quantitatively evaluated under practical operating conditions. This direction, together with the further development of a closed-loop calibration model that compensates for the residual coupling in real time, will be pursued in future work.

The optical applicability of the proposed liquid lens has also been evaluated through spectral characterization. Spectral transmittance measurements (Fig. S6) confirm >99% transmittance in the 450–700 nm range, making the device suitable for white-light microscopy, machine vision, and most visible-band fluorescence imaging applications. Transmittance below 400 nm is limited by the phenyl group absorption of the phenylmethyl silicone oil, restricting direct use in near-UV-excited fluorescence channels such as DAPI; replacement with low-UV-absorption non-polar liquids is identified as a future improvement to extend the spectral operating range. Additionally, three-wavelength chromatic aberration measurements (Fig. S8) indicate minimal chromatic difference between 658 nm and 515 nm within the 30–60 V non-saturated range, while the blue channel (450 nm) shows increasing focal position deviation at higher voltages, consistent with the stronger dispersion of phenylmethyl silicone oil at shorter wavelengths.

Looking forward, our prior research has demonstrated the potential of artificial intelligence to achieve adaptive autofocus through software-level optimization35. Accordingly, the proposed liquid lens holds promise as a critical hardware foundation for integrating compact optical systems with intelligent control algorithms, thereby enhancing the performance and application scope of adaptive optical systems.

Materials and methods

Experimental details for dielectric materials

The experimental setup for evaluating dielectric material performance is shown in Figs. 2f. The tests were conducted using droplet experiments on ITO-coated glass substrates coated with a dielectric layer and a hydrophobic layer. Specifically, the dielectric materials SU-8, PDMS, and acrylic acid were prepared via spin coating. The spin speed was adjusted, or multiple coatings were applied according to the material viscosity, to achieve a thickness of approximately 5 μm. Parylene C was deposited using chemical vapor deposition (CVD). A hydrophobic layer of Teflon, approximately 180 nm thick, was further spin-coated on top of the dielectric layer. A voltage was applied between the droplet and the ITO glass. As the voltage increased to the threshold level, the contact angle of the droplet changed; further increasing the voltage eventually led to dielectric breakdown. By recording the corresponding voltage and contact angle values during the process, the results shown in Figs. 5b, c were obtained.

Working liquid parameters

The liquid lens employs a pair of density-matched immiscible liquids. The polar liquid is a 0.1% (w/v) KCl aqueous solution (refractive index n1≈1.33, viscosity η1≈1.0 mPa s), selected for its superior electrowetting efficiency and lower driving voltage compared with deionized water and NaCl solutions. The non-polar liquid is phenylmethyl silicone oil (refractive index n2≈1.50, viscosity η2≈70 mPa s), chosen based on simulation analysis of the trade-off between response speed and oscillation damping. The interfacial tension between the two liquids is γ2≈50 mN/m. Spectral transmittance of the two liquids was characterized by UV-Vis spectrophotometry (190–1100 nm); results are provided in Supplementary Fig. S6.

Electrical driving parameters

The EWOD focusing mechanism is driven by a 60 Hz AC sinusoidal voltage signal with amplitude ranging from 0 to 90 V (peak-to-peak). AC driving is adopted to suppress electrolytic decomposition of the polar liquid at the electrode–liquid interface and to mitigate charge trapping effects in the Parylene C dielectric layer. The electromagnetic zoom mechanism is driven by a DC current applied to the electromagnetic coil, continuously variable from 0 to 180 mA. Response time measurement

To precisely characterize the intrinsic response of the liquid lens, a laser–photodetector measurement system was constructed. A 658 nm laser was transmitted through the liquid lens and directed onto a photodetector connected to an oscilloscope. In the initial state (no voltage applied), the laser beam diverges after passing through the lens and is not fully captured by the detector. Upon application of a 50 V step voltage, the deforming liquid–liquid interface converges the laser onto the detector, producing a sharp intensity rise recorded by the oscilloscope. The focusing response time is defined as the 10–90% rise time and the recovery time as the 90%–10% fall time. To further characterize the complete settling behavior of the device, the settling time is additionally reported, defined as the time required for the signal to enter and remain within a specified tolerance range (±5%) around the steady-state value. Five repeated measurements were performed; results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (shaded band in Fig. 6a, b).
