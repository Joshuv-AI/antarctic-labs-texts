Beyond Preferences: Learning Alignment Principles Grounded in Human Reasons and Values
Henry Bell, Lara Neubauer da Costa Schertel, Bochu Ding, Brandon Fain

Abstract




A crucial consideration when developing and deploying Large Language Models (LLMs) is the human values to which these models are aligned. In the constitutional framework of alignment models are aligned to a set of principles (the constitution) specified in natural language. However, it is unclear how to fairly determine this constitution with widespread stakeholder input. In this work we propose Grounded Constitutional AI (GCAI), a unified framework for generating constitutions of principles that are representative of both users’ general expectations toward AI (general principles) and their interaction-time preferences (contextual principles). We extend the Inverse Constitutional AI (ICAI) approach to generate contextual principles from human preference annotation data by leveraging human-provided
reasons
for their preferences. We supplement these contextual principles with general principles surfaced from user statements of
values
regarding AI. We show that a constitution generated by GCAI is preferred by humans over one generated through ICAI both personally, and for widespread use in governing AI behavior. Additionally participants consider the GCAI constitution to be more morally grounded, coherent, and pluralistic.

Individuals may express thousands of diverse views, general or specific, redundant, conflicting, or overlapping. To address this need, we propose
GCAI

Grounded Constitutional AI

Rather, GCAI is a scalable unsupervised machine learning framework that can synthesize large volumes of human preference annotations with reasons and general human descriptions of AI alignment values into a compact set of representative candidate principles (a constitution).

Prior work has similarly attempted to learn principles purely from human preference annotations, a method coined as Inverse Constitutional AI (ICAI)
(
11
)
. We surface the need of further grounding principle learning using: (i)
reasons
(or justifications) for human preference annotations, written in natural language, and (ii) general
values
humans report for LLM alignment, also written in natural language. At a high level, GCAI works in four main steps, the details of which vary slightly between (i) and (ii) and are specified in Section
4
with full prompts available in the Appendix.

(1)

Candidate Generation.
Using an LLM, we automatically rephrase individual reasons for preference annotations or general statements of values into one or more correctly formatted and generally applicable principles. This step is necessary as many human-written reasons or values are too narrow and specific (e.g., referring to a particular prompt), involve many semantically distinct concepts, or are simply written informally and idiomatically.

(2)

Clustering.
We embed each candidate principle as a numerical vector using a sequence embedding model. We then cluster the resulting embedded candidates. Clustering serves two purposes: Deduplication of many repeated candidates and identification of broadly relevant themes.

(3)

Summarization.
Using an LLM, we automatically summarize the most central candidates within each cluster (which often include somewhat different wordings of similar concepts) as new candidate principles.

(4)

Scoring and Selection.
The reduced set of summarized candidates are scored, either by their ability to predict relevant preferences (for preference annotations with reasons) or by their centrality within the embedding space as a soft proxy for consensus (for general statements of values). The top scoring candidates (up to the desired number) are selected.

2.
Grounding in Human Reasons and Values: Contextual and General Principles

We distinguish between two kinds of alignment principles the differ in their origin:

•

Contextual
principles arise from actual interaction experience with models. They may depend heavily on the prompt distribution, pretraining, and fine-tuning of the particular model with which a human interacts.

•

General
principles arise from anticipated concerns about hypothetical or possible model behavior. They represent prior commitments and do not depend on particular observed behaviors of a given LLM but rather on the broad values and concerns about possible LLM use.

Inverse Constitutional AI (ICAI)
(
11
)
tries to learn constitutional principles directly from preference annotations, a characteristic source for contextual principles. A human preference annotation includes a prompt, two example responses, and a selection of the preferred response. However, we emphasize that

principles are underdetermined by preference annotations
, in the sense that a given preference annotation might be grounded in multiple unrelated or even conflicting reasons for that preference. For example, consider the following preference annotation drawn from the Helpsteer 2 Dataset
(
41
)
.

Prompt

User:
I have a flurk in my glooben!

Response 1

LLM:
I’m sorry, I don’t speak whatever language that is. Can you please rephrase your concern in English? I’m here to help!

Response 2 (preferred
\checkmark
)

LLM:
I’m here to help answer your questions and engage in positive, respectful, and ethical conversations. However, I’m afraid I don’t understand the terms “flurk” and “glooben.” Could you please provide more context or clarify what you mean? I’ll do my best to assist you once I have a better understanding of your question.

The user prompt uses nonsense words, and both responses politely express a need for clarification and a willingness to help. What might explain the preference for Response 2? Multiple plausible reasons come to mind that would result in very different candidate principles. For example, perhaps Response 2 is preferred because it is more complete and detailed, fully clarifying its intended purpose and precisely highlighting the exact terms it cannot interpret. While initially plausible, this would completely miss the mark of the actual annotator reasons.

Annotator’s Reasons:

Response 2 is slightly better than Response 1 because it asks questions in a considerate way…While both responses are polite, Response 2’s tone is more open and accommodating, reflecting a better alignment with the potential for creative or playful engagement suggested by the original prompt. Response 1 comes across as a little snarky…Followed up by asking the user to rephrase the question *ïn English,*̈ which seems dismissive…

The actual reasons serve to ground principles of openness and non-dismissiveness in tone, having nothing to do with a preference for a more detailed response. An LLM (or human, for that matter) attempting to derive principles from the preference annotations alone might come to entirely the wrong conclusion based on a spurious inference.

Contextual principles are invaluable as a source of nuance about style, tone, and complex interpretations of helpfulness. But they are also insufficient. In particular,
principles to which the model is already well aligned will not surface regularly in contextual interaction.
In other words, contextual principle elicitation is heavily biased by the model and prompt distribution used for collecting the relevant preference data. Related,
principles regarding safety and harm
that are rarely relevant to normal interaction (but represent critical risks in rare interactions), will be underemphasized.

We find evidence for this concern empirically by a comparative analysis of themes in the PRISM dataset
(
25
)
compared to the Helpsteer 2 Dataset
(
41
)
. Specifically, we compare the human written reasons for preferences in the Helpsteer 2 dataset (the contextual case) with the general statements of alignment values contributed by human annotators in the PRISM survey data before and apart from their actual interactions with models (the general case).

3.2.
Alignment Goals and Learning from Stakeholder Input

Prior work has argued that the normative aspects of alignment should be confronted explicitly through a principle-based approach
(
16
)
. Helpfulness, harmlessness, and honesty have emerged as leading contenders
(
2
;
4
;
31
)
but may be too general and in need of interpretation to adequately inform model alignment. At a more detailed level, there is substantive disagreement over the relevant values and principles for alignment in a pluralistic society
(
42
;
35
;
34
)
. For this reason, some have argued to focus on a fair process for determining alignment goals rather than a particular fixed set of principles
(
15
)
. Constitutional AI is promising insofar as the principles written in natural language are directly used for model alignment. This enables transparency and collective participation
(
18
;
38
)
in the alignment process apart from the provision of preference annotations for training black-box reward models. How then can we elicit constitutions from participating stakeholders?

Inverse Constitutional AI (ICAI)
(
11
)
attempts to learn principles inferred from human preference annotations. ICAI uses an LLM to generate conjectural principles that could explain a preference annotation, then scores the resulting principles by their ability to broadly predict the preference dataset as a whole. In the language of GCAI, this amounts to learning contextual principles from preference annotations without reasons. Huang et al. (2024) proposed Collective Constitutional AI, wherein participants propose principles directly and then vote on a subset of the candidate principles
(
22
)
. This approach focuses on general principles. In contrast, GCAI learns
both
general principles directly from human statements of values
and
contextual principles principles from preference annotations corroborated by human-written reasons or justifications for those preferences, disambiguating and reducing the reliance on conjectural LLM interpretation of preferences.

4.1.
Candidate Generation

For the contextual principles, we use a subset of the HelpSteer2
(
41
)
dataset. We then use the model
gpt-4.1-mini-2025-04-14
and the prompt in Appendix
C.1
to infer candidate principles from the justification text, as in the following example:

Prompt:

I want you to act as an explainer and go in depth about a topic.

Response 1:

Sure, I can help. What topic would you like me to explain in depth?

Response 2:

Sure, I would be happy to help explain a topic in depth. What topic would you like me to explain?
Some things to keep in mind as I am explaining a topic: I will use simple language and avoid technical jargon as much as possible; I will provide examples and analogies to help make the topic more relatable and understandable; I will break down complex concepts into smaller, more manageable pieces; and I will provide a clear and concise summary of the topic at the end.
Let me know what topic you would like me to explain, and I will do my best to provide a thorough and understandable explanation.

Preference justification:

Response 2 is slightly better than Response 1.
Helpfulness: Response 2 provides a more structured and detailed plan for explaining the topic, making it slightly more helpful than Response 1, which lacks specificity.
Clarity: Response 2 presents a more coherent and clear outline of how the topic will be explained, reducing confusion.

Generated principles:

Provide a clear and detailed plan or outline to enhance helpfulness; use structured and specific information to improve clarity and reduce confusion.

This exemplifies how the justification adds context and value to the preference, allowing the LLM to extract or infer principles that better align with the reasons why the user preferred one response, rather than simply inferring them from the preference label alone.

For the general principles, we use the free-text answers from PRISM
(
25
)
that answer the question of which set of principles stakeholders think AI should follow, independent of what task it is performing. These responses were not single isolated statements, but descriptions of values that often expressed multiple distinct principles. To obtain individual principle candidates, we decompose each constitution into a set of principles by feeding a structured prompt and few-shot examples to the
gpt-4.1-2025-04-14
model (See §
C.2
). The resulting decompositions can be seen in the following example:

Stakeholder-Written Constitution:

Be friendly and uplifting in conversation. Do not show me any vile or unethical responses. Remember past conversations and learn from them what I like and don’t like. Always be respectful.

Decomposed principles:

AI should be friendly and uplifting in conversation; AI should not provide vile or unethical responses; AI should remember past conversations and learn user preferences.

In this example, we can see that some values arise that would not likely be observed during the interaction of a user with a previously aligned LLM, given that such models are already tuned not to provide vile or unethical responses.

4.2.
Clustering

The initial list of candidate principles is embedded using OpenAI’s
text-embedding-3-small
model
2
2

2

The specific choice of embedding model is not critical, as any model with comparable semantic similarity performance would be suitable.
.
For contextual principles, we use hierarchical clustering to group similar embeddings based on cosine similarity. Compared to the
k
-means clustering used in ICAI, hierarchical clustering better preserves unique principles, since
k
-means requires the number of clusters
K
to be fixed in advance. Because the true number of distinct principles generated in the first stage is unknown, specifying
K
is difficult: if
K
is too large, de-duplication is ineffective, and if
K
is too small, unrelated principles are forced together. Hierarchical clustering instead allows us to specify a similarity threshold
3
3

3

We found
0.42
to be appropriate in our experiments
in the sequence embedding space, rather than a fixed number of clusters.

When principles originate from stakeholder-written constitutions, we instead apply proportionally fair clustering
(
6
)
. In this setting, the objective is not only to remove redundancy but also to ensure that different value groups expressed in the data remain represented in the resulting set of clusters. Note that this is appropriate for the PRISM survey data where humans directly contribute constitutions and can be seen as represented by their contribution. By contrast, we do not recommend proportionally fair clustering for contextual principles because the distribution is strongly biased by the prompt distribution human annotators considered (i.e., as an annotator you may have been given prompts that did not reflect your most serious concerns).

The maximal number of clusters is chosen based on the assumption that each proportionally fair cluster should represent at least three data points, which is consistent with the fairness assumptions of the method while still allowing for a relatively fine-grained representation of human values. For each such cluster, the most central principle is selected as the one whose embedding lies closest to the cluster centroid, following the representative-selection procedure of
(
6
)
.
After clustering, we apply an initial de-duplication step using hierarchical clustering, as above, to merge clusters that are extremely similar in embedding space, reducing trivial redundancy while preserving proportional representation.

4.3.
Summarization

To generate a list of de-duplicated principles, we prompt an LLM (
gpt-4.1-2025-04-14
) to summarize the central principles within each cluster. We compute the mean embedding of all principles in a cluster and select the five nearest principle embeddings, which are then summarized. This deviates from ICAI, where a single principle is randomly selected from each cluster. Candidate principles within a given cluster tend to focus on a central idea, with slight variation in their precise wording. Summarizing a subset of these principles helps to faithfully represent more of the values present in a cluster
4
4

4

On the other hand, summarizing the
entire
cluster, for large clusters, resulted in some overly long principles which simply enumerated the details from the individual items within a cluster.
.

4.4.
Scoring and Selection

During scoring for contextual principles we consider how accurately candidate principles predict the original preference annotations. For each candidate principle and each annotation (prompt, two responses, and a preference), we prompt an LLM to select the response that better conforms to the given principle, with the option to abstain when the principle is irrelevant. A principle’s accuracy is computed as the number of times it correctly predicts the human preference when relevant, divided by the total number of relevant annotations plus a smoothing factor
\alpha
5
5

5

We use
\alpha=9
, proportional to the dataset size, to penalize principles that are extremely rarely relevant.
.

This scoring procedure is not applicable to principles derived from stakeholder-written constitutions, since these are not grounded in response-level preference data and therefore cannot be evaluated by their ability to predict pairwise response choices.

Therefore, we instead compute a mean squared distance (MSD) score for each cluster and rank clusters by this score, which provides a notion of internal agreement within a cluster and thus serves as a proxy for consensus over the represented principle. Because some clusters may still express closely related concepts, we perform a final de-duplication step using hierarchical clustering
6
6

6

We use a lower similarity threshold of 0.3 to conservatively merge only near-identical principles while retaining semantically distinct ones.
, merging semantically equivalent clusters and keeping the cluster with the lowest MSD as the representative.

Finally, we form a constitution by selecting the top
K/2
principles from each of the two elicitation sources, where
K
is the desired size. For ease of evaluation we use a value
K=10
in our experiments, though in practice one may wish to use a much larger value of
K
.

5.2.
Evaluative Framework

Our framework for evaluation was informed by previous AI constitution research
(
29
)
, contemporary literature on constitutional design
(
19
;
30
)
, and foundational legal and political theory
(
14
;
27
)
.

Evaluation Dimension

Definition

Moral Grounding

The extent to which the principle is informed by ethical rigor.

Fairness

The principle treats all affected parties equally and without bias.

Consensus

The constitution could be reasonably accepted by a pluralistic community, even if not endorsed unanimously by each individual.

Generality

The principle is broadly applicable across different contexts.

Clarity

The wording and intent of the principle are interpretable and unambiguous.

Coherence

Principles within the constitution can exist simultaneously without contradictions.

Feasibility

The AI can feasibly act on the principle in a real-world context.

Constancy

The principle is designed to be relevant and stable across time.

Faithfulness

The principle resists being distorted, co-opted, or warped in ways antithetical to its original intent.

Criteria used for principle and constitution evaluation

This framework is designed to engage in three distinct assessments:
legitimacy
,
conceptual integrity
, and
applicability
. The first set of criteria answers the meta-question, "Why should the system be bound by these rules?" as part of addressing the question of credibility and legitimacy. One source is its
moral grounding
, a foundational criterion that threads previous literature, both canonical and contemporary. For example, Lon Fuller notes in
The Morality of Law
that "legality is not a matter of form alone, but of fidelity to a moral enterprise."
(
14
)
Moral grounding as an evaluative dimensions, thus, challenges whether principles and constitutions are anchored in moral traditions that inculcate values-driven standards.

Political philosopher John Rawls, in his concept of Public Reason
(
27
)
, argues that justifiable constitutions must also be acceptable to all of its constituents, a position we capture in the dimensions of
fairness
and
consensus
.

The criteria of
generality
,
clarity
, and
coherence
assess conceptual integrity, the logical and semantic quality of principles and constitutions. In other words, they pose the question, "Are the principles (and constitutions) well-designed to guide behavior?" Generality addresses whether principles bear adequate conceptual breadth for situational applications and offer an architecture for predictable decision-making. Clarity assesses whether a principle can be meaningfully interpreted; it counterweights generality in ensuring that abstractions still offer understandable instruction. Finally, coherence measures at a constitution level the assurance of cogency such that principles can co-exist without significant conflict.

Finally, this evaluative framework asks, "Do these standards hold water in real-world applications?" The
prima facie
criterion here is
feasibility
, which examines how principles might intersect with technological, social, and economic constraints. With AI safety in mind, we raise an additional test of
faithfulness
, taking into account how human- or machine-based actions may distort the intent of a principle for malicious purposes. Finally, this framework considers
constancy
, a resistance to temporal brittleness, which cautions against transient assumptions encoded into principles written today and anticipates how shifting contexts may invalidate principles in ways orthogonal to original moral intent, logic, and application.

5.3.
Survey Methodology

We evaluated constitutions generated by both algorithms through two user surveys: 1) constitution-level, and 2) principle-level. 101 participants were recruited through the Prolific platform and randomly assigned the one of the two surveys. Our protocol was approved by our institutional review board (IRB) and all materials are included in Appendix
F
.

51 participants completed the constitution-level survey. These participants were shown both constitutions (in randomized order) (See Figure
2
and Figure
3
in Appendix
A
). We first asked participants to indicate which constitution they would prefer both 1) personally, and 2) for governing AI behavior for the U.S. population. After each of these questions we also asked participants to elaborate on what factors informed their preference in free text. Lastly, we asked participants to rate the constitutions along three dimensions: 1)
moral grounding
, 2)
consensus
, and 3)
coherence
on a 5-point Likert scale.

The remaining 50 participants completed the principle-level survey. Participants were randomly shown six individual principles, three from each of the two constitutions. First, they were asked if they personally agreed with the principle, and if the principle would be appropriate for governing AI behavior for the general U.S. population. They were then asked to evaluate each principle according to its:
1) moral grounding
, 2)
fairness
, 3)
generality
, 4)
clarity
, 5)
feasibility
, 6)
constancy
, and 7)
faithfulness
. Participants indicated on a five-point Likert scale their level of agreement that the principle embodied each dimension.

5.5.
Qualitative Data Analysis

We conducted qualitative data analysis of the free responses collected in the constitution survey, and the responses generated by our finetuned models. For both analyses researchers double coded a sample of responses (38 and 32 for the constitution survey and finetuned model generations, respectively) to establish an initial code book. Researchers then met to resolve any disagreements between their code books and consolidate a single set of agreed upon themes. The remaining data was then coded individually by researchers. No new themes emerged in the constitution survey data, however three new themes emerged while coding the finetuned model generations. 8.
Discussion

8.1.
Constitutions Are More Than The Sum of Their Parts

When evaluating standalone principles from the GCAI and ICAI constitutions, participants expressed little preference for principles from one constitution over the other (See §
6.6
). This was not the case when participants viewed the constitutions holistically. In a head-to-head comparison of constitutions, participants preferred the GCAI constitutions on all evaluation dimensions (See §
6.1
). This highlights an underappreciated dynamic of constitutions for AI alignment: that the
synergistic
composition of a constitution is an important factor in it’s approval from stakeholders. To further illustrate this point, we note that while factuality was the most important factor in making constitution-level preferences (See
3
), it was evaluated similarly to other principles in the principle-level evaluation (See
14
).

8.2.
Constitutions from GCAI Are Only Candidates and Should Be Discussed and Ratified

GCAI is notably a framework for eliciting
candidate
constitutions from stakeholder reasons and values. GCAI is designed to generate constitutions grounded in the values of a stakeholder population, which could reasonably garner widespread support. While the GCAI framework employs various scoring metrics during final principle selection, these metrics are far removed from direct human input, and are applied to principles that stakeholders have never actually seen. These scoring metrics should not be interpreted as valid expressions of support from stakeholders. Crucially GCAI does not have a method for ratifying the constitutions it generates. Before employing a GCAI constitution for model alignment developers should verify with relevant stakeholders that the constitution accurately reflects the pluralistic values of the population.

8.3.
Justifications Increase Transparency and Faithfulness

One of the benefits described in standard ICAI
(
11
)
is the ability to better interpret the principles guiding a preference annotation dataset. By incorporating justifications into our principle generation pipeline we further increase the transparency of preference data by more faithfully adhering to the true underlying reasoning of the human annotators. One limitation described by the original ICAI authors is that multiple constitutions may explain data equally well, so ICAI cannot claim to surface the exact principles used by the original data annotators. While this remains a limitation of our approach (and is, we believe, an inherent limitation in inferring principles from annotation data) we believe that incorporating justifications allows GCAI to more closely reflect the principles used during the original annotation task.

Limitations and Future Work

In this work we combine human reasons and values data from two different datasets. In practice GCAI is meant to elicit both general and contextual principles from the same stakeholder population. Future work should validate GCAI in more realistic deployment settings with a unified data source and large scale deployment for testing.

LLMs are used for generation and summarization within GCAI. While grounding candidate in the justifications and values written explicitly improves faithfulness, GCAI cannot guarantee perfect faithfulness and is subject to any biases of the LLM used within the technique.

Additionally, while GCAI generates constitutions of principles, the technique itself does not automatically guarantee the appropriateness of every principle that may be generated. In addition, no technique can guarantee perfect representation of every input perspective when multiple stakeholders disagree. Thus the final results require human oversight. As importantly, the final results require discussion and ratification from the relevant stakeholders for any given deployment. Future work should 1) investigate stakeholder attitudes towards GCAI constitutions, and 2) formalize a fair ratification process that incorporates feedback from the entire stakeholder population.
