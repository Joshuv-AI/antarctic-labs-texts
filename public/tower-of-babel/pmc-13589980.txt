Factors Influencing Patient Safety Incident Reporting and Evidence-Based Improvement Strategies Among Healthcare Professionals in Saudi Arabia: A Systematic Review Aligned with Vision 2030
Authors: Nouf Ahmed Banakhar, Ranad Abdulkareem Al-Ghamdi, Saleh Alzughaibi, Eman M Alanazi, Ahmed Alqheedan, Samiha Hamdi Sayed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo systematically review and synthesize existing evidence on the factors influencing patient safety incident (PSI) reporting among healthcare professionals in Saudi Arabia, and to identify context-specific strategies to strengthen reporting culture in alignment with Saudi Vision 2030.Participants and MethodsThis systematic review followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines. A comprehensive search of three electronic databases (PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar), along with grey literature sources and citation tracking, was conducted to identify studies published between January 2020 and December 2025. Fifteen studies met the inclusion criteria. Quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal tools. Due to methodological heterogeneity, a narrative synthesis was adopted, guided by the Population-Exposure-Outcome (PEO) framework and a four-domain conceptual model (cultural, organizational, technological, and individual factors). The protocol was registered a priori in PROSPERO (CRD420261334705).ResultsPersistent underreporting was observed across the included studies, with 44–50% of healthcare professionals reporting zero incidents, and rates reaching up to 90% in primary healthcare settings. Medication errors and near misses were the most frequently reported incident types. Barriers to reporting were systematically categorized across four interconnected domains: cultural (pervasive blame culture), organizational (heavy workloads, inadequate feedback), technological (poor system usability), and individual (low psychological safety and interpersonal concerns).ConclusionImproving PSI reporting requires a multilevel approach addressing these four domains. Implementing a Just Culture framework, strengthening leadership accountability, enhancing system usability, and improving interprofessional communication are essential to achieving Saudi Vision 2030’s patient safety goals.Keywords: incident reporting, barriers, enablers, systematic review, Saudi Arabia

Introduction

Incident reporting is a fundamental pillar of modern healthcare, essential for ensuring patient safety and fostering a culture of quality, accountability, and continuous improvement. Beyond mere data capture, it serves as a critical mechanism for analyzing safety incidents, identifying systemic weaknesses, and preventing recurrence.1,2 The effectiveness of these systems depends on a complex interplay of factors that either encourage or hinder healthcare professionals from disclosing safety incidents.3,4 Indeed, a punitive organizational culture is universally recognized as one of the strongest predictors of underreporting in healthcare settings worldwide.5

For this review, a patient safety incident (PSI) is defined as any event or circumstance that could have resulted in, or did result in, unnecessary harm to a patient, including near misses, no-harm incidents, and harmful incidents.6,7 Incident reporting refers to the structured documentation and submission of such events through formal reporting systems,4,5 and underreporting is defined as the failure to report PSI events that occur in clinical practice.4 This review addresses the full spectrum of reporting issues, including reporting behaviors, the types of incidents reported, and the barriers and enablers shaping disclosure.3,4

Despite global advancements in patient safety protocols, underreporting remains a persistent challenge.6,8–10 Global evidence indicates that adverse events occur in approximately 10% of hospital admissions, including falls, medication errors, surgical complications, and diagnostic delays.9 However, international studies from high-income countries consistently show that actual incident reporting rates fail to capture this burden, with estimates suggesting that 50% to 95% of safety incidents go unreported in Western healthcare systems.8,11 These events not only compromise patient outcomes but also erode public trust and increase healthcare costs.10

Saudi Arabia represents a particularly compelling context for examining this issue. While Western nations have spent decades organically evolving their safety cultures, Saudi Arabia is undertaking a unique, rapid, top-down, systemic overhaul driven by Vision 2030. Under this mandate, the Ministry of Health and the Saudi Patient Safety Center (SPSC) have prioritized patient safety as a strategic national imperative and have established landmark tools, including the Saudi Healthcare Sentinel Event Manual and the Saudi Patient Safety Taxonomy, to align national standards with international best practices.7,12 The Health Sector Transformation Program (HSTP 2021–2025) has further embedded safety culture within institutional reform. Yet despite this strong national framework, implementation at the hospital level remains inconsistent. Studies report moderate perceptions of safety culture alongside pronounced deficiencies in non-punitive responses to errors and in leadership expectations, suggesting a meaningful gap between policy intent and operational reality.10,13

This gap stems from barriers that manifest differently in Saudi Arabia than in global benchmarks. Culturally, while international literature predominantly identifies fear of malpractice litigation as the primary deterrent in the US and UK,11 the Saudi context shows that concerns about institutional sanctions and damage to professional reputation are so widespread that nearly 75% of healthcare workers in certain settings have never submitted a single report.1,14,15 This is further exacerbated in high-power-distance cultures, where the desire to maintain interpersonal harmony overrides the willingness to communicate openly.16 Organizationally, international studies show that providers view reporting as pointless when leadership fails to provide meaningful feedback,11,17 a perception compounded in Saudi Arabia by insufficient staffing and technological usability gaps.18–21 Cutting across all these structural domains is a profound human element, where healthcare professionals often experience “second victim” phenomena characterized by anxiety and moral burden.22 Consequently, what institutional frameworks frame as a routine administrative task is frequently perceived by frontline staff as a high-stakes choice between advocating for patient safety and protecting their professional reputation or collegial relationships.17

Despite a growing body of research, the literature specifically focused on incident reporting behaviors in Saudi Arabia remains fragmented. Most studies are confined to single institutions or specific professional groups, limiting their generalizability and obscuring the systemic, nationwide dynamics that drive reporting behavior.23–29 While a previous systematic review by Albalawi et al (2020)3 broadly examined factors contributing to general patient safety culture in Saudi Arabia, it did not specifically isolate the act of incident reporting, nor did it capture evidence from the active implementation phase of the Health Sector Transformation Program (2021–2025). Critically, no comprehensive synthesis has yet examined how cultural, organizational, technological, and individual factors interact specifically to shape PSI reporting behaviors and underreporting patterns within the distinctive, rapidly evolving context of Saudi Arabia’s recent healthcare reforms, leaving a significant evidence gap that hinders the development of targeted, context-specific interventions.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study adopted a systematic review approach, following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines to ensure that the processes of study identification, selection, evaluation, and synthesis were conducted in a clear, replicable, and thorough manner.30 The protocol for this systematic review was registered a priori in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number CRD420261334705), and no amendments were made to the registered protocol. This review is reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement, and a completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided as Supplementary Material (Supp.1. No member of the study team has previously conducted a systematic review on this topic, and the present work does not overlap with any prior published review by the authors. To ensure methodological rigor and full reviewer control over data interpretation, all processes (screening, appraisal, extraction, and synthesis) were conducted manually without the use of automation tools. A standard consensus protocol was applied throughout the review: two independent reviewers performed all tasks, resolving any discrepancies through discussion or, when consensus could not be reached, by consulting a third reviewer.

Conceptual Framework

To ensure a focused and systematic approach, this review was guided by the PEO (Population–Exposure–Outcome) framework, which is the recommended standard for systematic reviews examining factors, barriers, or etiology rather than clinical interventions. The primary review question and its components were defined as follows:

This PEO framework guided the search strategy, eligibility criteria, and data extraction. It was complemented by a four-domain conceptual model encompassing cultural, organizational, technological, and individual factors. This four-domain model provided the analytic structure for organizing and synthesizing the extracted data, ensuring that the barriers and enablers of patient safety incident reporting were examined systematically across each domain.

Information Sources

Data were collected from three major databases: PubMed, Google Scholar, and Web of Science. To ensure comprehensive coverage, reference lists of included articles were screened. Additional evidence was drawn from grey literature sources, including official publications from the Saudi Patient Safety Center (SPSC) and the Ministry of Health (MOH). The search was limited to studies published between January 2020 and December 2025 to capture recent evidence within the context of Saudi Arabia’s ongoing healthcare reforms.

Search Strategy

Database-specific search strategies were developed to reflect the syntax and indexing capabilities of each source. For PubMed, the search combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords using Boolean operators: (Patient safety [MeSH] OR safety incidents OR adverse events OR medical errors) AND (incident reporting OR event reporting OR error reporting) AND (health personnel [MeSH] OR healthcare professionals OR nurses OR physicians) AND (Saudi Arabia [MeSH] OR “Kingdom of Saudi Arabia” OR KSA). An analogous Boolean string using Topic-field tags was applied in Web of Science. Because Google Scholar does not support nested Boolean/MeSH syntax and applies a proprietary, non-transparent relevance-ranking algorithm, a simplified free-text query (“patient safety incident reporting” AND “Saudi Arabia”) was used. All returned records were screened to ensure comprehensive coverage, given the absence of precise Boolean filtering on this platform. The Google Scholar search was conducted on [15 January 2026], and the results were exported immediately to create a fixed, auditable record, since the platform’s output is not guaranteed to be reproducible later.

Eligibility Criteria and Selection Process

Studies were selected based on the Population-Exposure-Outcome (PEO) framework, which guided both the search strategy and the eligibility criteria.31 Eligible study designs included quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods studies published in English between January 2020 and December 2025. Studies were excluded if they were conducted outside Saudi Arabia, lacked data on incident reporting behavior, or were opinion papers, editorials, or non-peer-reviewed publications.3,14,28 Titles and abstracts were screened, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible studies, using the PEO-based inclusion criteria. The entire screening and selection process was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines.30,31

Assessment of Quality and Risk of Bias

The methodological quality and risk of bias of all included studies were assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for cross-sectional studies, as this design predominated across the included literature. For each appraisal item, studies were rated as Yes, No, Unclear, or Not Applicable. Only Yes responses were scored as 1; all other responses were scored as 0. The overall quality score for each study was calculated as the percentage of applicable criteria rated Yes, and studies were subsequently categorized into three quality levels: high (80–100% of criteria met), moderate (50–79%), and low (less than 50%), consistent with JBI guidance.31 A brief calibration exercise was conducted beforehand to ensure alignment of judgments between reviewers.30 Risk of bias was evaluated across five specific domains: selection bias, performance bias, detection bias, attrition bias, and reporting bias. These domain-level ratings were synthesized to derive an overall risk-of-bias judgment for each study.

Data Extraction

Data extraction was performed using a standardized form developed based on the PEO framework. Extracted data items encompassed seven categories: study characteristics (author, year, location, study design, sample size, and healthcare setting); participant characteristics (professional group and demographics); measurement tools and data collection methods; key factors influencing PSI reporting, classified under cultural, organizational, technological, and individual domains; PSI reporting behavior (including reporting rates, willingness to report, and underreporting patterns); common types of PSIs reported; and evidence-based strategies to enhance PSI reporting culture.

Data Synthesis and Analysis

Statistical meta-analysis was not feasible due to profound clinical, methodological, and statistical heterogeneity across the included studies. Specifically, the included literature measured fundamentally different constructs, ranging from subjective perceptions of safety culture using the AHRQ HSOPSC tool to objective counts of incident reporting rates, utilized non-comparable measurement instruments, and reported outcomes in formats that could not be statistically pooled. Consequently, calculating the I2 statistic to quantify heterogeneity was not applicable, and a narrative synthesis approach guided by the PEO framework was adopted to ensure a systematic and transparent integration of the evidence.30,31

Coding of the included studies was performed by two independent reviewers using a combination of deductive and inductive approaches. Deductive coding was structured around the four-domain conceptual framework, while inductive coding was applied to capture emergent themes not anticipated by the model. A codebook was developed and piloted before formal coding. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved by the standard consensus protocol established for this review.31

The synthesis process involved three stages. First, study findings were compiled using a structured data-extraction framework to provide a comprehensive overview of each study’s characteristics, outcomes, and key findings. Second, findings were thematically categorized according to the conceptual model to identify consistent patterns and relationships influencing PSI reporting behavior across the Saudi healthcare context. Third, convergent and divergent findings across studies were examined and interpreted, considering each study’s methodological quality and risk of bias, as assessed using the JBI critical appraisal checklists.31

Reporting Bias Assessment

Reporting bias, including publication bias and selective outcome reporting, was assessed through several strategies. To minimize publication bias, the search strategy was deliberately broad, encompassing three major electronic databases, grey literature sources (including the SPSC and MOH official publications), and backward and forward citation tracking. Given the narrative synthesis design and the nature of the included studies, statistical methods such as funnel plots were not feasible; consequently, the comprehensiveness of the search strategy served as the primary safeguard against publication bias.30,31

Selective outcome reporting was assessed by systematically comparing the methods and results sections of each included study to verify that all prespecified outcomes were adequately reported. Discrepancies between stated objectives and reported findings were noted and considered during the narrative synthesis and certainty assessment. When multiple reports of the same study were identified, the most methodologically complete report was retained for analysis.30,31

Certainty Assessment

Because a statistical meta-analysis was not conducted, the standard GRADE domains were operationalized for narrative synthesis of quantitative data as follows: (1) Risk of bias was assessed based on the aggregated JBI critical appraisal and risk-of-bias ratings of the studies contributing to each outcome domain, with downgrading applied when the majority of contributing studies were rated moderate or low quality; (2) Inconsistency was assessed by examining the directional consistency of findings across studies and settings, rather than statistical heterogeneity (I2). If most studies pointed to the same barrier or enabler despite varying study contexts, inconsistency was not considered serious; (3) Imprecision was evaluated based on the adequacy of sample sizes and the diversity of settings across the included studies, rather than the width of confidence intervals around a pooled estimate. If findings were drawn from small, single-center samples with narrow populations, imprecision was rated as serious; (4) Indirectness and (5) Publication bias were assessed using standard GRADE criteria. Two independent reviewers evaluated the certainty for each key outcome domain (eg, cultural, organizational, and technological factors). The overall certainty for each outcome was categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low. Downgrades were applied when necessary, and the justification for each rating was documented.32 Disagreements were resolved through consensus or consultation with a third reviewer to maintain robustness of the evidence.

Results

Study Selection

Figure 1 illustrates the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram of the study selection process. A total of 3760 records were initially identified through database searching: PubMed (n = 172), Google Scholar (n = 1095), and Web of Science (n = 2493). Additional searches of grey literature sources, including publications from the SPSC and MOH, and backward and forward citation tracking were conducted; however, no additional records were identified through other sources. Following the removal of 617 duplicate records and 113 non-open-access, non-peer-reviewed, and non-English records, 3030 records were screened at the title and abstract stage, of which 2840 were excluded as irrelevant. Of the 190 reports sought for retrieval, 20 could not be retrieved (full text unavailable despite database and grey literature searches). Full-text assessment was therefore conducted for the remaining 170 studies. Of these, 155 were excluded for the following reasons: outside the date range 2020–2025 (n = 75), outside Saudi Arabia (n = 19), and out of scope (n = 61). Ultimately, 15 peer-reviewed studies were included in the narrative synthesis. No selective outcome reporting was identified across the included studies.

Study Characteristics

The synthesis reveals a predominance of cross-sectional designs (n = 12), with three retrospective analyses.12,14,33 The studies vary significantly in scope, ranging from single-center investigations in specialized units—such as dental clinics, emergency departments, and oncology centers.15,29,33,34—to large-scale national surveys that encompass hundreds of thousands of healthcare providers across Saudi Arabia.12,14,26 Regarding settings, the research covers a broad spectrum of the healthcare system, including primary healthcare centers, tertiary care hospitals, and both the Ministry of Health (MOH) and private facilities.34–36 The sample populations are diverse, involving physicians, nurses, pharmacists, and administrative staff. Methodological Quality and Risk of Bias

Overall, the included studies demonstrate satisfactory methodological rigor, with the majority (n = 11) classified as high quality (meeting 100% of applicable JBI criteria) and four classified as moderate quality (meeting 75% of criteria)12,23,28,33 [Table 2]. The primary methodological limitations across the moderate-quality studies included unclear inclusion criteria,28 a lack of standardized measurement thresholds,23 and—most critically—inadequate identification and adjustment of confounding variables.12,23,28,33 Consequently, risk-of-bias assessments revealed a moderate risk of selection, information, and confounding bias in these four studies [Table 3]. To maintain analytical rigor, findings from these moderate-risk studies were interpreted with appropriate caution during the narrative synthesis and were not relied upon heavily to draw definitive conclusions.

Risk of Bias Assessment by Domain

PSI Reporting Behaviors and Underreporting

A pervasive culture of underreporting was consistently observed across the synthesized evidence, though the magnitude varied significantly by healthcare setting [Table 4]. In national and tertiary care samples, approximately 44–50% of healthcare professionals reported zero incidents.12,23 However, a critical analysis of the data reveals that underreporting is substantially amplified in specialized and primary care contexts—reaching 56% in dental clinics,29 64% among hospital nursing staff,26 and peaking at nearly 90% in primary healthcare centers.36 This gradient suggests that frontline, high-volume, or specialized settings face distinct, heightened barriers to reporting that go beyond the national average. Regarding incident types, medication errors and sentinel events were the most reported PSIs, with communication breakdowns and staff competency issues identified as their primary root causes.16,28,33–35

Barriers and Enablers to Reporting

Rather than existing in isolation, barriers to reporting operated synergistically across the four predefined domains [Table 4]. Cultural factors were the most dominant deterrents universally identified across the literature; a pervasive blame culture and fear of punitive consequences (including legal action and reputational damage) severely suppressed reporting behavior across all professional groups.1,13–15,21,23,26,29,36

These cultural barriers were frequently compounded by organizational deficiencies. High workloads and staffing shortages were consistently cited,10,14,21,28,33–36 but critically, the synthesis revealed that the perception of inadequate leadership support and a lack of feedback following report submission were equally damaging to reporting morale. Technologically, while electronic occurrence variance reporting (eOVR) systems are standard, poor usability and time-consuming interfaces were significant deterrents, particularly among staff with lower digital confidence.1,21,34 Finally, at the individual level, interpersonal concerns—specifically the fear of damaging collegial relationships and low psychological safety during critical transitions such as handovers- served as a distinct barrier that intersected with both cultural and organizational domains.15,29,34

Conversely, enablers of reporting were consistently identified as the inverse of these barriers: strong unit-level teamwork, visibly supportive leadership, and institutional non-punitive responses to errors were key facilitators for encouraging disclosure.10,13,26

Evidence-Based Strategies and Recommendations

Synthesis of the proposed strategies across the included studies revealed a strong consensus that isolated technical or structural fixes are insufficient to improve reporting. Instead, a multilevel approach is required. A critical analytical theme that emerged from the literature was the conditional effectiveness of interventions: multiple studies argued that implementing new electronic reporting systems or organizational feedback loops will yield minimal returns unless preceded by a fundamental cultural shift toward a Just Culture.12,35 Consequently, interventions targeting legal protections, anonymity, and non-punitive leadership responses were consistently positioned not merely as parallel recommendations, but as foundational prerequisites for all other domains.1,1–10,10–12,21,23,26,36 Beyond this cultural foundation, the synthesized evidence clustered around optimizing system usability to address technological barriers, and standardizing handoff communication to mitigate the individual-level fears that peak during clinical transitions.1,13,14,21,26,34 The specific mapping of these synthesized strategies across the four domains is detailed in Table 5.

Certainty Assessment

Table 6 presents the GRADE certainty assessment for the four key outcome domains synthesized in this review. The overall certainty of the evidence ranged from Low to Very Low, primarily reflecting the inherent limitations of cross-sectional study designs. The cultural, organizational, and technological domains were all rated as Low certainty. This rating was maintained because, despite the observational design, these domains demonstrated strong directional consistency across settings (no serious inconsistency) and were supported by studies with adequate sample sizes and diverse settings (no serious imprecision). However, the individual/interpersonal domain was further downgraded to Very Low certainty. This downgrade was driven by serious risk of bias (due to inadequate adjustment for confounding variables) and serious imprecision (due to reliance on small, single-center samples) in the primary studies driving this specific theme. Across all domains, indirectness and publication bias were not flagged as serious concerns.

Discussion

This systematic review aimed to synthesize the existing evidence on factors influencing patient safety incident (PSI) reporting among healthcare professionals in Saudi Arabia. The findings confirm that, despite robust national frameworks under Saudi Vision 2030 and the Health Sector Transformation Program, underreporting of PSIs remains a persistent challenge. The synthesized evidence reveals that reporting behavior is not determined by a single factor but rather by a complex, interplaying matrix of cultural, individual, organizational, and technological barriers; a pattern consistent with the theoretical framework established by Archer et al4 and corroborated by the growing body of international literature on this topic.

PSI Reporting Behavior and Common Types of Incidents

The synthesized evidence reveals a pervasive culture of underreporting across Saudi healthcare settings. International evidence indicates that, despite high rates of patient safety concerns and staff familiarity with reporting systems, submission rates remain critically low, with many healthcare workers reporting only a single incident throughout their entire careers.6 A scoping review by Smit and Peddle37 specifically examining nurses’ experiences in acute care settings found that nurses across multiple international contexts consistently reported fear of blame, uncertainty about what constitutes a reportable event, and a perceived lack of managerial follow-up as the dominant reasons for non-submission of reports, findings that closely mirror the Saudi experience. The findings from Saudi Arabia align with this global phenomenon, with 44–50% of healthcare workers reporting zero incidents in the preceding year despite frequent exposure to safety events.12–14

Regarding the types of incidents, global meta-analyses indicate that adverse events occur in approximately 10% of hospital admissions worldwide, with medication errors and surgical complications being the most prevalent.9 The Saudi data mirror this international trend, with medication errors, near misses, and sentinel events being the most identified PSI types. Medication errors were particularly prevalent at the prescribing and dispensing stages, with dispensing errors accounting for the largest share of reported incidents.28,33 The high proportion of near misses—accounting for 69.3% of all reported events in one study—suggests that detection systems are functioning at some level; however, the persistent underreporting of actual adverse events indicates that near-miss data alone may present an incomplete picture of the true safety burden in Saudi healthcare settings.33 Furthermore, the disproportionate involvement of high-alert medications in 45% of reported medication errors underscores the need for specialized reporting practices in complex care pathways such as oncology.1,33

Factors Affecting PSI Reporting Among Healthcare Providers

The review identified that underreporting is driven by a complex interplay of cultural, individual, organizational, and technological factors—many of which transcend national borders.

Cultural factors emerged as the most pervasive and deeply entrenched barriers. A blame culture, fear of punitive consequences, and concerns about legal repercussions were consistently identified as the primary deterrents.14 These findings are consistent with the theoretical framework proposed by Archer et al4 and recent international literature, which identify punitive organizational cultures as the strongest predictors of underreporting globally.5 International qualitative evidence further confirms that blame culture and fear of punishment are universal impediments to reporting across diverse healthcare systems. A qualitative systematic review by Hamed and Konstantinidis22 found that nurses across multiple international settings consistently cited fear of blame as the dominant barrier. Smit and Peddle37 similarly identified perceived punitive responses and a lack of psychological safety as the primary deterrents to reporting among acute care nurses globally. Furthermore, this cultural barrier directly stifles vital communication. In hierarchical, high-power-distance environments, the desire to maintain interpersonal harmony overrides the willingness to communicate openly—as evidenced by data showing that staff actively avoid reporting to prevent disruption of collegial relationships and that low psychological safety results in only half of clinicians speaking up when they witness safety concerns.21,29

At the individual level, personal fear of consequences further suppressed reporting behavior, though the specific nature of these concerns varied internationally. Western literature highlights fear of malpractice litigation as the primary individual deterrent among US and UK healthcare professionals.11 Similarly, Oweidat et al16 found that nurses across Middle Eastern settings reported low awareness of reporting procedures and fear of disciplinary consequences as dominant individual barriers. In the Saudi context specifically, individual fear was heavily tied to formal sanctions and damage to professional reputation. Unlike in Western contexts, where malpractice litigation is the primary individual deterrent,11,16 evidence from Saudi Arabia suggests that formal institutional penalties and concerns about professional reputation pose distinct barriers to incident reporting.1 A Just Culture framework addresses this directly through legal protections and guarantees of anonymity, which are essential for effectiveness in this environment.3

Organizational factors, including heavy workloads, staffing shortages, weak leadership support, and inadequate feedback mechanisms, were consistently reported as significant barriers across the included studies. Healthcare professionals frequently perceived reporting systems as time-consuming and insufficiently supported by leadership.10,34,35 This mirrors international evidence; Gampetro et al11 noted that providers in Western settings also view reporting as ineffective and time-consuming, particularly when hospital leaders rarely acknowledge or implement improvements based on submitted reports. Thull-Freedman and Liem-Beckett17 further observed that organizational reporting tools often fail to meet frontline needs, creating a disconnect between institutional reporting requirements and clinical realities. Critically, a qualitative study by Koskiniemi et al38 found that healthcare managers themselves identified inadequate managerial engagement, unclear feedback pathways, and insufficient staff support as the key organizational failures undermining reporting culture—suggesting that organizational barriers operate at both the frontline and managerial levels simultaneously. Equally important are organizational communication structures and feedback mechanisms.18 Poor interprofessional collaboration and unsafe handoff practices constitute systemic organizational failures that directly contribute to both incidents and their underreporting. The loss of critical patient information during shift changes highlights a breakdown in organizational processes and indicates an urgent need for structured communication interventions, such as standardized handoff protocols.21,28,34 International evidence from a systematic review of maternity care settings further corroborates these findings, identifying poor communication and inadequate feedback as the most consistent organizational barriers to reporting across diverse global contexts.18 Strengthening leadership accountability, establishing robust feedback loops, and standardizing handoff procedures are, therefore, critical organizational priorities.

Technologically, the transition from paper-based to electronic reporting is a global trend, with system usability recognized as a determinant of reporting success internationally.18,19 The Saudi experience mirrors this global pattern; Al-Rayes et al20 found that perceived ease of use was the strongest predictor of reporting intention, and Abu Alrub et al21 reported a 33% increase in reporting following eOVR implementation. Koskiniemi et al38 provide further international evidence on this dimension, finding that healthcare managers and nurses consistently recommended developing real-time, user-friendly reporting software as a priority intervention—emphasizing that systems must be integrated seamlessly into existing clinical workflows rather than treated as separate administrative tasks. A scoping review by Olakotan et al19 reinforced these findings, demonstrating that usability challenges in electronic health record systems significantly hinder healthcare professionals’ engagement with digital reporting platforms across multiple global settings.

Evidence-Based Strategies to Enhance PSI Culture in Alignment with Vision 2030

Globally, the shift toward a “Just Culture”—a concept originally popularized in Western safety-critical industries and later adapted for healthcare—is the gold standard for improving reporting.39 A just culture balances individual accountability with system-level accountability by distinguishing among human error, at-risk behavior, and reckless conduct, and responding to each proportionately rather than punitively. International evidence consistently demonstrates that implementing just culture principles is associated with higher reporting rates, improved psychological safety, and stronger organizational learning cultures.5,11,22,37

To achieve Vision 2030’s goal of a transparent, learning-oriented healthcare system, Saudi Arabia must adapt this international concept to its local context. While the Kingdom has successfully established centralized, top-down structural frameworks through the Saudi Patient Safety Center (SPSC) and tools like the Saudi Patient Safety Taxonomy—a level of centralized coordination often envied in fragmented Western systems—these policies must now be translated into ward-level culture.11 International experience suggests that policy-to-practice translation requires sustained leadership engagement, frontline staff empowerment, and context-specific implementation strategies that account for local cultural dynamics.11,17,27,38

The synthesized evidence highlights four main strategies, each linked to the cultural, organizational, technological, and individual areas discussed in this review: (1) Culturally tailored communication training is essential. Data from Saudi Arabia, which align with international findings in hierarchical healthcare settings, show that collegial relationships and power differences greatly affect reporting decisions.16,27,37 This means standard communication training must change to empower frontline staff to speak up across hierarchical levels and implement safe handoff protocols without fear of relational damage.7,28 (2) Leadership interventions should model principles of a just culture and provide timely feedback on reported incidents. This directly addresses the persistently low scores for non-punitive responses to errors and for psychological safety, as shown in Saudi national surveys.12 Koskiniemi et al38 further stress that managers need active training and support to become facilitators of a reporting culture rather than passive recipients of incident data. (3) Technological improvements are necessary to make electronic reporting systems user-friendly, protect anonymity, and fit seamlessly into clinical workflows. Evidence suggests that well-implemented digital systems can boost reporting rates by up to 33%.19–21,38 (4) Individual-level interventions should tackle personal barriers that remain even when organizational and cultural conditions improve. This includes providing legal protection for reporters, ensuring confidentiality and anonymity, offering targeted education on what constitutes a reportable event, and enhancing psychological safety—especially among professional groups such as radiation oncologists and dental practitioners, who often underreport.1,15,29 Together, these four strategies reflect the multilevel barriers outlined in this review and align with the recommendations most frequently supported across the studies reviewed.

While the synthesized evidence consistently identifies clear patterns across the four domains, it is essential to contextualize the resulting recommendations within the GRADE certainty assessment framework. The overall certainty of the evidence underpinning these strategies ranges from Low to Very Low. Consequently, while the proposed multilevel interventions are highly consistent with international best practices and the goals of Vision 2030, they should be interpreted as robust, directional guidance rather than definitive causal mandates.

Study Limitations

The most significant limitation of this review stems from the inherent constraints of the available evidence base. Foremost is the overwhelming predominance of cross-sectional study designs, which preclude establishing causal relationships or temporal sequences between identified barriers (eg, blame culture) and actual reporting behaviors. Furthermore, the reviewed literature is characterized by substantial methodological heterogeneity, not only in study designs but also in the measurement tools used to assess safety culture and the varying definitions of reporting behavior across institutions. This fragmentation prevented statistical meta-analysis and limited the ability to draw unified conclusions across different healthcare sectors. Additionally, despite some large-scale national surveys, a notable portion of the evidence was derived from single-center studies with inadequate adjustment for confounding variables, contributing to the overall Low-to-Very Low-GRADE certainty of the evidence.

At the review level, restricting the search to English-language publications between 2020 and 2025 across three databases may have excluded relevant Arabic-language literature or earlier foundational research. While the single-country focus was analytically necessary to capture the nuances of Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 reforms, it inherently limits the generalizability of the findings.

To overcome these limitations and mature the evidence base, future research in Saudi Arabia must pivot away from single-center, cross-sectional surveys. There is a critical need for higher-quality prospective and longitudinal study designs that can track how shifts in organizational policy impact reporting rates over time. Furthermore, large-scale multicenter studies using mixed-methods approaches are essential to move beyond identifying barriers and to evaluate the actual implementation and effectiveness of targeted interventions, such as Just Culture frameworks, across diverse healthcare settings.

Conclusion

This systematic review provides a detailed examination of the factors influencing PSI reporting among healthcare professionals in Saudi Arabia. Despite considerable national investments in patient safety infrastructure under Saudi Vision 2030, underreporting remains a widespread and persistent challenge driven by a complex interplay of cultural, organizational, technological, and individual barriers.

This review makes three distinct contributions to the existing evidence base. First, unlike prior systematic reviews that broadly examined patient safety culture in Saudi Arabia, this is the first synthesis to specifically isolate and systematically evaluate factors that affect incident reporting behaviors. Second, it applies a structured four-domain analytical model that moves beyond simply listing barriers, instead demonstrating how these domains interact to suppress reporting. Third, by capturing evidence published between 2020 and 2025, this review provides the first consolidated evaluation of incident reporting during the active implementation phase of the Health Sector Transformation Program, offering a highly current baseline for policymakers.

To meet the goals of Vision 2030, Saudi Arabia must adapt international “Just Culture” principles to its unique local context—particularly addressing the fear of disrupting workplace relationships and hierarchical harmony. Translating these findings into practice requires concurrent, multilevel action: implementing culturally tailored communication training, embedding leadership accountability within a Just Culture framework, improving the usability of e-reporting systems, and enhancing individual protections through legal safeguards and anonymity guarantees.

However, given the Low to Very Low certainty of the current evidence, these strategies should be interpreted as robust, directional guidance rather than definitive causal mandates. To elevate the quality of the evidence base, future research must pivot away from single-center, cross-sectional surveys. There is a critical need for prospective, longitudinal, large-scale, multicenter studies using mixed methods designs to track the actual effectiveness of these multilevel interventions over time.
