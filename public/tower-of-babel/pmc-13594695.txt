Isolated medial cuneiform fracture: Rare avulsion of atypical tibialis posterior tendon insertion – A case report and review of literature
Authors: Ranjith Kumar Yalamanchili, Syed Ifthekar, Deepak Kumar Maley, Christina Boda, Deepankar Satapathy, Sreekanth Eppakayala, Maheshwar Lakkireddy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We describe a case of an isolated medial cuneiform avulsion fracture in a 38-year-old man following a motorcycle fall. The patient underwent open reduction and internal fixation, regaining full function by four months postoperatively. An isolated avulsion fracture of the medial cuneiform can result from an external rotation force applied to a plantar flexed foot, coupled with a forceful active contraction of the Posterior Tibial Tendon (PTT), particularly in Olewnik's Type-I anatomical variant. Awareness of anatomical variants of PTT insertion and understanding the fracture morphology and displacement, helps in selecting appropriate fixation strategies in an isolated medial cuneiform fracture.

Introduction

Medial cuneiform fractures usually occur in conjunction with associated injuries of the mid foot and account for approximately 1.7% of all the midfoot injuries [1], [3]. Isolated fractures of the cuneiform bones without any associated injury are rare, with only a few scattered reports of medial and lateral cuneiform fractures, and just a single documented case involving the intermediate cuneiform are reported in the literature [1]. This rarity is attributed to the anatomical location of cuneiforms, which are structurally reinforced within the midfoot, making it unusual for any force to act solely on a single cuneiform without concurrently affecting adjacent metatarsals or the Lisfranc joint [2]. Nevertheless, isolated cuneiform fractures can be a result of unique and intriguing phenomenon that may result from a direct blow or by a combination of axial and rotational forces [1].

Most of the reported isolated fractures of medial cuneiform are un-displaced, that healed well with conservative management. To date, only three cases of non-union of medial cuneiform fractures have been reported, with the cause of non-union attributed to misdiagnosis or delayed identification of displaced fragments [4], [5], [6]. Considering the multipennate insertion of the Posterior Tibialis Tendon [PTT], it is interesting to understand the injury mechanism in a case of an isolated avulsion fracture of the medial cuneiform. We report a case of a 38 year old man who sustained an isolated medial cuneiform avulsion fracture following a motor cycle accident.

Case description

A 38-year-old man sustained injury to his right mid foot, following a fall from motorcycle with his plantar flexed foot loaded in abduction. Despite the injury, he was able to ambulate and presented to us with complaints of progressive swelling associated with severe pain over right mid foot while walking. Clinical examination revealed localized tenderness and swelling over the medial aspect of mid foot. Radiographic evaluation with an antero-posterior and oblique X-ray of foot revealed a displaced isolated fracture of medial cuneiform. To better characterize the fracture morphology and exclude associated injuries of mid foot, a computed tomography (CT) scan of the foot was done. Imaging revealed an isolated fracture of the medial cuneiform, with the fragment displaced plantar and flipped medially [Fig. 1A&B]. After the swelling over mid foot decreased as evident by appearance of wrinkle sign, the patient was operated under regional anaesthesia and under tourniquet control. With the patient in supine position, a 5-cm skin incision was made over the medial cuneiform. After the subcutaneous dissection, the fractured fragment was noted to be flipped and could not be reduced by rotation of the fragment with the foot in pronation. Anatomical reduction was achieved only by rotating the fragment while maintain the foot in supination [Fig. 2]. The fractured fragment was wedge-shaped, with a thin distal medial wall tapering toward the apex and a broader base. A single K-wire of size 1.8 mm was inserted distally near to the apex of the fragment to prevent toggling, as the base was well-supported by the adjacent navicular bone. Additionally, the fracture surface demonstrated good frictional resistance, contributing to overall stability. After the fixation, it was noted that the majority of the flipped bony fragment bore an insertion band of the PTT. The wound was closed in layers after bending and cutting the K-wire. Postoperatively, the foot was immobilized for six weeks, following which the K-wire was removed. The patient was mobilised with partial weight bearing protocol beginning at six weeks and progressed to full weight bearing by twelve weeks postoperatively. Fracture union was noted radiographically at 12 weeks [Fig. 3]. The patient resumed his daily activities by 16 weeks postoperatively. Discussion

To date, only fourteen studies comprising 21 cases of isolated cuneiform fractures of the foot were reported which includes three cases of non-union [Table 1]. Among them, the majority are un-displaced and often went undetected on initial radiographs, necessitating Computerized Tomography [CT] or Magnetic Resonance imaging [MRI] for further evaluation in patients with persistent pain over the medial cuneiform [2], [4], [6], [9], [11], [15], [16]. All the un-displaced fractures were effectively managed with conservative methods by splinting. Displacement of fracture fragments requiring surgical intervention were most commonly attributed to direct blow injuries, often resulting in comminution or avulsion mechanisms. The fracture morphology in most reported cases were not documented clearly, making it difficult to understand the precise mechanisms causing these injuries. Choi et al. categorized the location of single cuneiform fractures and distinguished the terminology ‘single’ as a fracture associated with surrounding injuries, and the term ‘isolated’ as solitary cuneiform fractures without any associated injuries [1]. The AO/OTA 2018 classification compendium categorizes medial cuneiform fractures based on articular surface involvement [avulsion −85.1.A, partial articular-85.1.B, and complete articular-85.1.C] [17]. Choi et al. reported a total of 21 ‘single’ medial cuneiform fractures, among which five were ‘isolated’ which included three extra-articular fractures resulting from supination injuries and two due to direct blow mechanism [1]. Detailed fracture morphology and possible avulsion mechanism was not discussed in their report. Medial cuneiform avulsion fractures are generally considered less likely to result from eccentric loading of the PTT due to its typical multipennate insertion. However, Olewnik et al. classified anatomical variants of PTT insertion, with Type I characterized by a single tendon slip inserting postero-medially on to both the navicular and medial cuneiform bones [18]. This focused insertion may increase stress concentration on the medial cuneiform during forceful foot inversion, offering a plausible explanation for the avulsion pattern observed in this case. We observed a unique plantar and medial flipping avulsion fracture of the medial cuneiform and suggest that an anatomical variant of tibialis posterior tendon insertion may account for this injury pattern in a plantar flexed foot subjected to external rotation force. These anatomical variations in tendon insertion foot prints may influence not only the fracture morphology but also the displacement patterns, particularly in avulsion-type injuries [19].

In the present case, the authors were initially unaware of the possibility of an atypical tibialis posterior tendon (PTT) insertion and, therefore, did not specifically evaluate for this anatomical variation during the preoperative assessment and hence a preoperative MRI was not obtained. The unexpected intraoperative findings prompted a detailed review of the literature, which highlighted the existence of such atypical insertion patterns and their clinical significance that corelated with intraoperative findings. This experience emphasizes that awareness of these anatomical variations can facilitate appropriate preoperative evaluation, with MRI serving as a valuable tool for identifying atypical PTT insertions and aiding surgical planning and management in such isolated injury patterns.

Identifying the morphology and displacement patterns guide the implant of choice in displaced fractures, that may involve K-wire, bone staple, headless screws, mini-plate fixation or a combination of them. In avulsion-type fractures, larger fragments can effectively be stabilized with a headless screw, whereas those with a thin distal medial cortex may be better suited for K-wire or staple fixation. Comminuted avulsion patterns typically require a mini-plate buttress for optimal stability and favourable outcomes. In patients with localized medial midfoot pain following any mode of trauma, a high index of clinical suspicion should prompt further evaluation with CT or MRI, even when plain radiographs show no evidence of bony injury.

Conclusion

Although an isolated avulsion fracture of the medial cuneiform is rare, it can result from an external rotation force applied to a plantar flexed foot, coupled with an active contraction of the posterior tibial tendon, especially in Olewnik's Type I anatomical variant. Awareness of such anatomical variants in PTT insertion can aid in recognizing rare avulsion injury pattern that may require open reduction over closed techniques. In the management of isolated cuneiform fractures, a clear understanding of fracture morphology, displacement patterns, and potential associated injuries is essential for selecting the appropriate fixation strategy.
