Surface microstructures of tricalcium phosphate regulate the autophagy of osteoclasts through the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway
Authors: Yuchen Yang, Kairui Chen, Huadong Wu, Tao Li, Lihao Wang, Hong Li, Qiang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ceramics, including tricalcium phosphate (TCP), are widely used in bioengineering medicine due to their biocompatibility and bone conductivity. While calcium phosphate materials can induce bone formation without exogenous factors, the specific physical structures of these materials play a crucial role in their biological effects. Autophagy is involved in osteoclast proliferation, differentiation, and activation, with the MAPK signaling pathway being a key regulator of these processes. This study aimed to explore how the surface microstructures of TCP affect osteoclast function and illustrate the underlying mechanism related to autophagy. Three types of TCP disks with different surface microstructures (TCPc, TCPm, and TCPp) were prepared and characterized using scanning electron microscopy. Mouse mononuclear macrophage RAW264.7 cells were induced to differentiate into osteoclasts and cocultured with the TCP disks. Various experiments, including CCK-8 assays, MDC autophagy staining, quantitative real-time PCR, and Western blot analysis, were conducted to evaluate cell viability, autophagy activity, gene expression, and protein expression related to osteoclast function and the MAPK signaling pathway. The surface microstructures of the TCP disks significantly influenced osteoclast activity. TCPc promoted osteoclast proliferation and survival, while TCPm and TCPp inhibited osteoclast activity. The autophagosomes in the TCPm and TCPp groups were reduced, and the expression of autophagy-related proteins (Atg5, Atg7, LC3A, LC3B, Beclin-1) was lower compared to the TCPc and blank groups. The expression of osteoclast-related genes (TRAP, CTSK) was highest in the blank group, followed by the TCPc group, and lowest in the TCPm and TCPp groups. The phosphorylation levels of ERK, p38, and JNK in the TCP groups were lower than those in the blank group, indicating that TCP surface structures inhibit the MAPK signaling pathway. The study highlights the importance of material surface microstructures in bone tissue formation and suggests potential strategies for regulating bone metabolism through material design.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Ceramics have been widely applied in bioengineering medicine and clinical work. As biodegradable polymers, ceramics can be either natural or synthetic, such as coralline hydroxyapatite and β-tricalcium phosphate, which can be synthesized with different morphisms, porosity, pore sizes, or surface structures [1, 2]. Tricalcium phosphate (TCP) is often used in clinical practice because it has a similar inorganic composition, biological activity, biocompatibility, and bone conductivity as that of bone tissue. In the past two decades, researchers have found that calcium phosphate materials exhibit heterotopic osteoinductive properties without the engagements of exogenous osteogenic inducible factors or cells, leading to the consideration that calcium phosphate might have some special chemical or physical properties that make it osteoinductive [3–5]. However, not all types of calcium phosphate ceramics have the same biological effects in vivo [6–8] and are capable of bone induction [9]. Various studies have shown that calcium phosphate ceramics with specific physical structural characteristics can exhibit bone induction [10–12]. Oliver-Urrutia et al. prepared a porous composite hydroxyapatite material and found that its porous structure not only has good mechanical properties, but also can be well combined with biological tissues to promote the formation of new bone [5]. Skliarenko et al. treated calcium phosphate materials with H2O2, resulting in surface microstructure changes and increased porosity of the materials, making them osteoinductive even in the absence of osteogenic stimulator induction [12]. Therefore, it was proposed that biological implant materials endorsed with microporous structures enhance the absorption of proteins, and that cell adhesion enhance bone induction [13]. The role of the surface microstructure of calcium phosphate ceramics in inducing bone tissue formation has not been identified; however, some previous researchers have hypothesized that the surface microstructure of calcium phosphate ceramics play a key role in the proliferation, adhesion, and differentiation of osteoclasts, as well as osteoblast.

Autophagy is usually maintained at a low level of stability in normal cells for a long time, but is heavily activated under stress conditions. Many studies suggest that autophagy be involved in osteoclast proliferation, differentiation, and functional activation, and that abnormal autophagy activity of osteoclasts may lead to certain bone diseases. The absorbency of osteoclasts decreased sharply when the exogenous autophagy inhibitor bafilomycin was added to the culture medium of osteoclasts. The expression of RANKL was significantly up-regulated after TGF-β3 treatment, suggesting that RANKL was involved in clock-controlled autophagy in bone metabolism. TGF-β3 stimulates autophagy by increasing the expression of Microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3-II (LC3-II), thereby inhibiting osteoclast resorption. Autophagy-related protein 5 (ATG5), autophagy-related protein 7 (ATG7), autophagy-related protein 12 (ATG12), autophagy-related protein 4β (ATG4β), and Microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3) are the primary proteins formed by autophagosomes [14, 15].

Mitogen activated protein kinase (MAPK) is a kind of signal transduction protease, which can transform various extracellular stimuli into specific cell responses in eukaryotic cells, and act as a signal hub to regulate cell proliferation, differentiation, stress response, autophagy, and apoptosis [15]. The MAPK signaling pathway mainly includes 4 subfamilies of ERK, JNK, p38 kinases, and ERK5-activated kinases, among which 2 subtypes of ERK, ERK1/2 and 3 subtypes of JNK, JNK1/2, are related to the proliferation of osteoclast precursor cells and apoptosis of osteoclasts. The four subtypes of p38, P38α, p38β, P38γ, p38δ, are highly expressed in the precursor cells of osteoclasts and mature osteoclasts, and play a key role in osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption. MAPK has been found to participate in osteoclast autophagy in many animal and cell experiments [16, 17]. Bone homeostasis is a dynamic balance maintained by osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Bone tissue is continuously absorbed by osteoclasts and rebuilt by osteoblasts throughout life [18]. During bone resorption, the cytoskeleton of osteoclasts rearranges to form a closed zone. After the completion of bone resorption, osteoclast apoptosis occurred and the process of bone resorption stopped. Therefore, osteoclasts play a key role in maintaining bone homeostasis. [19] However, current research on materials in bone formation tends to focus on osteoblasts, with less research on the role of osteoclasts. Therefore, in this study, we attempted to explore the effects of microstructures on the function of osteoclasts and its specific mechanism relating to autophagy.

Experimental sections

Preparation of TCP disks with different surface structures

In the experiment, 50 g of calcium phosphate powder was accurately weighed and placed in a plastic container. Then, 40 mL of 30% volume fraction deionized water and hydrogen peroxide, 30 mL of 5% polyethylene glycol solution, and 20 mL of methylcellulose solution were added and mixed thoroughly, and the mixed slurry was oscillated ultrasonically for 20 min. Subsequently, a proper proportion of hydrogen peroxide was added to cause the slurry to foam, and the heating time and stirring rate were strictly controlled to obtain different porosities and pore sizes, so as to prepare TCP cylinder with different microstructures. The paste was added to the mold and dried to obtain a good shape. The TCP cylinder was sintered into porcelain, and the sintering temperature was strictly controlled at 1080 °C and 1000 °C. After cooling, ceramic pieces of the same diameter and thickness were cut, polished, and dried after full washing, and contaminating microorganisms were removed by Co60 irradiation with a 25 Gy radiation dose for 2 h. Three TCP disks with different surface microstructures were eventually acquired. They were called TCP-compact (TCPc), TCP-middle(TCPm), and TCP-porous (TCPp) according to the porosity and pore size.

Characterization of the TCP disks

The surface morphology of the TCP disks was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Hitachi, SU8100). Image-Pro Plus 6.0 (Media Cybernetics, Inc., Rockville, MD, USA) was used to analyze the pore size and grain size of different TCP disks.

RAW264.7 cells cocultured with TCP disks

The RAW264.7 cells used in this experiment were purchased from iCell Bioscience Inc(ShangHai, China).

Mouse mononuclear macrophage RAW264.7 cells were used to inducing osteoclast for the cell study. The culture medium of RAW264.7 was Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Solarbio, China) with 1% penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin, 10 mg/mL streptomycin), 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Australia), and 50 ng/mL receptor activator of nuclear factor-κb ligand (RANKL R&D, USA). Before cell seeding, TCP was loaded into 24-well plates and pretreated with DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum for at least 24 h. Owing to its hydrophilicity, TCP must be fully soaked and absorbed by the medium and protein. Then, cells were seeded on the TCP disks placed in the 24-well plates with a cell density of 5.0*104 per sample. Separately cultured RAW264.7 cells on plastic disk were used as the blank group. Finally, 2 mL of culture medium was added to the wells of the plates, and the samples were placed in an incubator at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 and 95% air. The culture medium was refreshed once every two days.

Samples were harvested on days 2, 4, and 6 for the CCK-8 assay. For the monodansyl cadaverine (MDC) autophagy staining assays, gene expression assays, and for Western blot assays, the samples were harvested on day 7.

CCK-8 assay

RAW264.7 cells were seeded on TCP disks (2.5⊕103/cm2) and stimulated with 50 ng/mL of RANKL. The proliferation activity of RAW264.7 cells on days 2, 4, and 6 was measured using the CCK-8 kit (Bioss, China). According to the manufacturer’s protocol, the CCK-8 reagent was mixed with the complete medium at a ratio of 1:10 and added to the well plate. The same amount of the mixed solution of the complete medium and CCK-8 was used as control. It was then placed in an incubator with a concentration of 5% carbon dioxide at 37 °C for 1–2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate analyzer.

MDC autophagy staining assay

An MDC autophagy staining kit (Solarbio, China) was used to detect autophagy activity during osteoclast differentiation. According to the protocol, the cells were seeded on TCP disks and stimulated with a complete medium containing 50 ng/mL of RANKL for seven days. The reagents in the kit were proportionally and stained for 15-45 min without light. Images were taken using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Quantitative real-time PCR

To investigate osteoclast-related gene expression profiles in RAW264.7 cells according to the surface structure of the different materials, a quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assay was performed.

After seven days, total RNA was isolated from RAW264.7 cells cultured on each type of material using an RNA isolation kit (Axygen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, USA). The RNA was used to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA) with a PrimeScript RT reagent kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The osteoclast-related markers tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP), cathepsin K (CTSK), IntegrinαⅤ (ITGα5), and Integrinβ3 (ITGβ3) were measured using the Applied Biosystems Real-Time PCR System. Glycer-aldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was the mouse house-keeping gene. All primer sequences used for the amplification were as follows: forward 5′-CACTCCCACCCTFAFATTTGT-3′ and reverse 5′-CCCCAGAGACATGATGAAGTCA-3′ for TRAP; forward 5′-TCCGAAAAGAGCCTAGCGAA-3′ and reverse 5′-CCGAGAGATTTCATCCACCTTG-3′ for CTSK; forward 5′-TCAGATGCTATGGGCTGAGG-3′ and reverse 5′-CGGAAAGAGGAGCAGAAAGC-3′ for ITGβ3; forward 5′-GCACAGGCACTTAGAACAGG-3′ and reverse 5′-CAGGATTGAACCCAGGGAGT-3′ for ITGα5; forward 5′-AACGGATTTGGCCGTATTG-3′ and reverse 5′-CATTCTCGGCCTTGACTGTG-3′ for GAPDH. Finally, the real-time PCR reaction was run at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 34 s, and the dissociation curve was taken at 95 °C for 15 s, 60°C for 1 min, and 95 °C for 15 s. Data were analyzed using Applied Biosystems StepOne Software v2.3 (URL link: https://downloads.thermofisher.cn/Instrument_Software/qPCR/Step-1/SOP23_4482515.zip). The relative amounts of target genes normalized by GAPDH were calculated by the double delta Ct method and subsequently normalized to the gene expression level of each type of TCP disk determined after RAW264.7 cells were cultured for 7 days without TCP disks. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Western blot analysis

RAW264.7 cells were seeded on TCP disks (2.5⊕103 cells/cm2) and incubated with a complete medium for 24 h. Then, 50 ng/mL of RANKL was added to the culture medium. After RANKL stimulation for seven days, cells were harvested and lysed using RIPA lysate (Solarbio, China) containing 1% PMSF and phosphatase inhibitors (Bioss, China). The supernatant was then harvested after centrifugation. Subsequently, equal amounts of samples were boiled in SDS sample buffer containing β-mercaptoethanol and subjected to electrophoresis on 10% SDS-PAGE (Bio-Rad, USA). Proteins on the gel were transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane using a wet blot (Bio-Rad, USA) and incubated with 5% bovine serum albumin for 2 h. Membranes were probed with primary antibodies for ERK1/2 (Bioss, China), p-ERK1/2 (Abclonal, China), p38 (Bioworld Technology, USA), p-p38 (Affinity Biosciences, USA), JNK (Bioworld Technology, USA), p-JNK (Affinity Biosciences, USA), ATG5 (Proteintech, USA), ATG7 (Bioss, China), Beclin-1 (Bioss, China), LC3-A (Bioss, China), LC3B (Sigma, USA), p62 (Proteintech, USA), and GAPDH (Proteintech, USA). After washing, the membranes were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence system (US Everbright, China) in an iBright FL1500 Imaging System. Experiments were repeated independently at least three times.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3). Two-way analysis of variance followed by Bonferroni posttest comparisons was performed for multiple comparisons. Statistically significant differences were marked by *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.

Effect of TCP surface microstructure on cell viability and proliferation

The survival rates of adherent cells of the three TCP disks on the second day after seeding were approximately same, indicating that the initial cell adhesion efficiencies were similarly equal. In addition, after four days, the cell survival rates of all three materials increased, indicating that none of the materials were cytotoxic and the cells were able to proliferate. However, the viability of RAW264.7 cells cocultured with TCP disks was significantly lower than that of the blank group. On the sixth day, the cell survival rate in the TCPc group was similar to that in the blank group but significantly higher than that in the TCPm and TCPp groups (P < 0.05). These data indicate that, although initial cell attachment and proliferation were not impeded, long-term cell survival was reduced in the TCPm and TCPp groups with apparent microstructures.

Discussion

In the natural state, bone homeostasis is primarily maintained by the dynamic balance between osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Most of the previous studies focused on the osteogenic function of osteoblasts, but ignored the dynamic balance between osteoblasts and osteoclasts in the process of bone tissue formation. And osteoclasts may be the key to initiating osteogenesis. Osteoclasts, as a kind of multinucleated giant cell, are differentiated from osteoclast precursors under the stimulation of certain cytokines, such as the receptor activator of NF-κB ligand (RANKL) [20]. Osteoclasts have attracted increasing attention in recent years because of their important roles in bone growth and bone tissue renewal. An increasing number of studies have shown that the surface microstructure of materials can affect the bone induction of calcium phosphate, and osteoclasts may play a key role in this process, as well as osteoblasts. In bone tissue engineering, the surface microstructure of implanted materials can influence the formation of bone tissue by enhancing the differentiation and adhesion of adherent cells in bone tissue [21]. The maintenance of bone homeostasis requires a balance between bone formation and resorption. The importance of osteoclast-mediated bone resorption in the process of bone formation has not been widely recognized. In the initial process of bone formation, osteoclasts can absorb adjacent bone tissue, interact with the surface of the implant material, and participate in the remodeling of the interface between the implant material and bone during the entire osteoinduction function of the implant material [22]. In this study, we focused on the effect of TCP surface structures on osteoclasts in bone formation and the mechanisms.

The surface micromorphology of the material is in direct contact with the host tissue. Bone tissue forms on the outer surface of the material microstructure, not just inside the material. The porous structure of the disks not only provides space for cell adhesion and subsequent growth but also ensures the transport of nutrients and metabolites. It’s like a porous structure in a bone marrow cavity. At the same time, the porous structure can improve biocompatibility [23]. In addition, the roughness and other surface micromorphologies directly affect the adhesion and migration of cells. Therefore, the microstructure of the material surface plays a key role in bone formation. In this study, in addition to the differences in the submicron structure of the three TCP disks, there were significant differences in the micromorphology of the material surface, such as micropores. The surface microstructure of the TCPc group promoted the proliferation and survival of osteoclasts, while the surface microstructure of the TCPm and TCPp groups seemed to inhibit the activity of osteoclasts. Sukasa et al. found that suitable surface microstructure of materials can promote osteoclast activity and ITGB3 may play a central role in its process [24]. This is consistent with our experimental result 3.4. Davison et al. found that the surface microstructure of biphase calcium phosphate ceramics could enhance osteoclast activity more than the macrostructure, such as the sizes of large pores and pits [25]. Small surface microstructures (~1 μm) of calcium biphasic phosphate and titanium biphasic calcium phosphate promoted the formation of osteoclast-like cells and new bone formation, while larger surface structures (2–4 μm) inhibited these effects. In addition, β-TCP with a submicron surface structure (≤1 μm) was more favorable for osteoclast formation than β-TCP with a surface structure of microns (4 μm) [26, 27]. This is consistent with the inhibition of cell proliferation activity in the TCPm and TCPp groups in this experiment.

TRAP is related to the biology of osteoclasts, both in terms of physiological function and as a biomarker of bone resorption related to osteoclast quantity. In addition, the activity of TRAP is closely related to the number and morphological changes of osteoclasts, and the level of its activity can reflect the functional status of osteoclasts [28, 29]. TRAP and CTSK have a synergistic effect on osteoclast function. TRAP is one of the markers of osteoclast differentiation, and its high expression is closely related to the formation and function of osteoclasts. CTSK is highly selectively expressed in osteoclasts and is mainly involved in the degradation of type I collagen in bone matrix protein. Studies have shown that TRAP can enhance the activity of CTSK, thereby promoting the bone absorption function of osteoclasts [30]. Osteoclasts express many proteases, including cathepsin and MMPs, but CTSK is generally considered to be the main bone-degrading enzyme [31, 32]. In this study, the expression of TRAP and CTSK genes was detected by qPCR to reflect the influence of the TCP disk surface microstructure on osteoclast function. The results showed that the osteoclast function of the TCPm and TCPp groups was inhibited to varying degrees, but the TCPc group with a small crystal size had the highest expression of TRAP and CTSK, the largest number of osteoclasts, and the strongest bone resorption function, indicating that the crystal size and micropore size of materials can affect the number and function of osteoclasts. Therefore, we hypothesize that the surface microstructure of bone-inducing materials affects the signaling between osteoclasts and the transport of nutrients and metabolites, thereby regulating the activity and function of osteoclasts, and ultimately affecting the balance of bone homeostasis.

Autophagy is a self-protection mechanism that is widely observed in eukaryotic cells. Degradation of the aging or damaged organelles and misfolded protein macromolecules in cells is beneficial to cell metabolism or organelle renewal, which is of great significance for maintaining cell homeostasis, including bone metabolism related cells. Recent studies have shown that autophagy was involved in osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption [33, 34]. Autophagy-related proteins, including Atg5, Atg7, Atg4B, and LC3, play key roles in the formation of frills and the promotion of osteoclastic polarization, which ultimately lead to bone tissue absorption [35]. However, whether autophagy is involved in the regulation of TCP surface microstructure on osteoclast formation and function requires further study. In this experiment, the formation of autophagosomes in the TCPm and TCPp groups was inhibited, and the key proteins of autophagy, such as Beclin1, LC3A, LC3B, Atg5, and Atg7, were significantly down-regulated, indicating that the surface microstructure of TCP regulate the autophagy of osteoclasts [36]. With the increase in micropore size of TCP disks, the autophagy inhibition of osteoclasts became more obvious. It is necessary to pay attention to the expression of p62. It used to be thought that the process of autophagy in the choice of substrate is nonselective. However, recent studies have shown that cells exhibit nonselective autophagy in the starvation state, but in order to maintain intracellular homeostasis, autophagy is highly specific in the selection of substrates, (a process known as “selective autophagy”). This is more obvious when the body is in a state of disease [37]. Substrates in the process of selective autophagy may include ubiquitinated proteins, peroxidases, and mitochondria. The ubiquitin binding protein SQSTM1 (p62 and equestome1) on the surface of autophagy can capture the ubiquitin protein and bind to LC3-II [38]. Because p62 is degraded while transporting the target substrate to the interior of the autophagosome, an increase in p62 expression usually indicates a decline in autophagy. In the process of RANKL-induced osteoclast formation, the ratios of Atg5, Atg7, and LC3A/LC3B increase with the degradation of p62, which plays an important role in the formation of filamentous actin rings of osteoclasts. The surface microstructure of TCP can also regulate autophagy in osteoclasts. In this study, the expression of the p62 protein in the blank group was the highest, and it was significantly higher than that in the three TCP groups. A possible reason is that the surface microstructure of TCP disks affected the formation of the p62 protein, and TCP with a large crystal size reduced the total amount of p62. This is because p62 downregulation can also reduce RANKL-induced autophagy, expression of osteoclast-related genes, formation of TRAP-positive multinucleated cells, LC3 accumulation, and formation of actin rings [39].

At least five different signaling pathways are known to be involved in RANKL-induced osteoclast formation: the Src/PI3K/Akt, IKK/NF-κB, ERK, JNK, and p38 pathways [40, 41]. The results showed that TCP with different surface microstructures upregulated the phosphorylation of ERK1/2 and p38, but the effect on the phosphorylation level of JNK was not as obvious as that of the ERK1/2 and p38 signaling pathways. Therefore, the ERK1/2 and p38 signaling pathways might play an important role in the regulation of osteoclast formation by the surface microstructure of materials — in particular, the ERK1/2 signaling pathway. The ERK signaling pathway is related to the activity, proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation, and pseudopod decomposition of osteoclasts. The activated ERK signaling pathway not only promotes autophagy itself, but also induces autophagy by upregulating autophagy-related proteins, such as LC3 and p62 [42]. The ERK1 gene knockedout experiment in mice also demonstrate it play an important role in regulating the differentiation, migration, and bone resorption of osteoclasts [43]. In this study, we examined the expression of ERK, p38 and JNK and their phosphorylation levels in RANKL-induced osteoclasts and found that the phosphorylation of ERK, p38, and JNK was significantly inhibited after the cells were co-cultured with TCP. This suggests that TCP surface structure regulate the MAPK/ERK pathway in osteoclasts during bone formation. We will further validate the mechanism by which TCP microstructure regulates osteoclast autophagy by inhibiting relevant signaling pathways and detecting osteoclast autophagy.

Conclusion

The surface microstructures are essential for osteogenesis induced by TCP, however, the role of microstructure on osteoclasts has been thoroughly elucidated. Our study suggested the surface microstructure of TCP exert an important regulatory role for osteoblasts in bone tissue formation progress. TCP with different surface microstructures affects osteoclast activity, especially differentiation and bone resorption function and may regulate RANKL-induced autophagy in osteoclasts via the MAPK/ERK pathway. Our study found that materials with different surface microstructure may affect the signaling and nutrient transmission between osteoclasts, thus affecting the activity and proliferation of osteoclasts. This may lead to the activation of MAPK/ERK signaling pathway, which in turn regulates osteoclast autophagy, thereby affecting the function of osteoclasts.

Our research currently uses cell lines rather than primary cells, which is a major shortcoming of our experiments. At present, only a single osteoclast is studied in the experiment, and the co-culture level of osteoclast and osteoblast is not involved. We hope to continue to refine follow-up experiments to further focus on the key role of osteoclasts in bone homeostasis induced by materials. In addition, the material surface microstructure affects osteoclast behavior through integrin-mediated mechanical transduction, chemical characteristic coordination and immune regulation. The underlying mechanism of this process may be the regulation of osteoclast behavior by autophagy mediated by MAPK/ERK. Future studies could combine high-throughput screening (such as combinations of different microstructures) with single-cell sequencing techniques to analyze spatio-temporal dynamic regulatory networks.
