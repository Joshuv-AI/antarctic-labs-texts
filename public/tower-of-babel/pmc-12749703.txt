Public sector innovation in transition economies through hybrid entrepreneurship and the techno industry nexus
Authors: Mozhdeh Maleki, Mahsa Fatemi, Kurosh Rezaei-Moghaddam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Transitional economies face growing pressure to foster public sector innovation amid economic challenges. Hybrid entrepreneurship offers a pathway to enhance adaptability, efficiency, and service delivery. This study applies a multi-theoretical framework, integrating cognitive theory, the theory of planned behavior, social network theory, and the technology acceptance model (TAM), to examine factors influencing hybrid entrepreneurship adoption in Iran’s public organizations. In addition, Self-Determination Theory (SDT) is employed to understand how organizational support mechanisms, such as job promotion opportunities and a positive work culture, enhance employees’ intrinsic motivation and entrepreneurial intention. Based on a descriptive survey of 234 public sector experts in Fars province, structural equation modeling using SPSS26 and SmartPLS3 revealed that entrepreneurial motivation, perceived usefulness, and supportive organizational environments significantly drive adoption. The techno-industry nexus—linking scientific expertise with industrial application—emerged as a key innovation enabler. Findings also highlight the role of professional networks in facilitating knowledge access and collaboration. Policy suggestions include reducing regulatory barriers, enhancing technological infrastructure, and incentivizing innovation through academia–industry partnerships, contributing both theoretical and practical insights into public sector transformation in transitional economies.

Introduction

In recent years, Iran has grappled with significant economic challenges and persistently high unemployment rates, particularly among youth and university graduates1. While the expansion of university admissions offered a temporary solution, it inadvertently increased the number of highly educated job seekers, thereby intensifying pressures on the labor market2. These challenges have been compounded by economic crises driven by international sanctions and volatile oil prices, resulting in diminished employment opportunities, growing social dissatisfaction, and heightened migration trends3. According to the Statistical Center of Iran, the national youth unemployment rate (ages 15–24) reached approximately 23.7% in 2023—significantly higher than the national average—and in some provinces, this figure exceeded 30%. Moreover, the situation for university graduates is particularly concerning, with over 40% of the unemployed holding higher education degrees4. These alarming statistics reflect a substantial mismatch between the higher education system and the labor market, contributing to widespread disillusionment among young Iranians and undermining the country’s human capital potential.

These pressing challenges underscore the urgent need for innovative and entrepreneurial solutions. Entrepreneurship is widely recognized as an effective mechanism for alleviating unemployment, fostering innovation, and promoting sustainable economic development5,6. For Iran, empowering youth through entrepreneurship could play a pivotal role in reversing current employment trends by transforming job seekers into job creators and revitalizing the national economy amid ongoing structural constraints7,8.

In this context, hybrid entrepreneurship—defined as the process of initiating and managing a business venture while simultaneously maintaining traditional employment—emerges as a dynamic and increasingly relevant model, especially in environments characterized by economic uncertainty and labor market rigidity9,10. By combining the stability of salaried employment with entrepreneurial experimentation, hybrid entrepreneurs can mitigate the financial risks typically associated with full-time entrepreneurship while fostering gradual innovation, experiential learning, and resource accumulation11,12. This dual engagement allows individuals to assess market viability, acquire entrepreneurial competencies, and develop social capital without becoming entirely financially dependent on a new venture. Particularly in transitional and emerging economies, where formal employment is scarce and entrepreneurial barriers—such as regulatory burdens, limited capital access, and market instability—are prevalent, hybrid entrepreneurship offers a viable and strategic path to employment generation, economic diversification, and long-term resilience13.

Given Iran’s prevailing labor market conditions—characterized by high youth unemployment, underemployment of university graduates, and structural misalignments—hybrid entrepreneurship presents a promising and contextually appropriate strategy for promoting inclusive growth and socio-economic empowerment. Encouraging entrepreneurial activity alongside traditional employment holds the potential to mobilize untapped human capital, reduce dependence on saturated public sector employment, and contribute to the development of a more flexible and innovation-driven economy (Maleki et al., 2025).

The theoretical foundation of this study is built upon several interrelated conceptual frameworks. Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory posits that human behavior is shaped by reciprocal interactions among personal cognition, behavior, and environmental factors14. At its core is the concept of social learning, whereby individuals acquire knowledge and form perceptions through observation and interaction with others15. These cognitive and social processes shape how entrepreneurs perceive and respond to risks and opportunities, thus influencing their willingness to engage in hybrid entrepreneurship16. Moreover, prevailing social and cultural attitudes toward entrepreneurship can significantly influence these perceptions, acting as either enablers or barriers17.

Complementing this perspective, Ajzen’s Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) offers a robust psychological framework for analyzing entrepreneurial intention. TPB suggests that entrepreneurial behavior is guided by three key factors: attitude toward the behavior, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control18,19. Attitude reflects an individual’s overall evaluation of entrepreneurship; subjective norms pertain to perceived social pressures from family, peers, or society; and perceived behavioral control refers to the individual’s confidence in their ability to successfully initiate and manage a venture. Widely validated across diverse socio-economic contexts, TPB provides strong explanatory power for understanding entrepreneurial intention20,21. In Iran’s context—marked by labor market constraints and economic instability—TPB serves as a valuable lens through which to examine how psychological and social factors influence individuals’ willingness to pursue hybrid entrepreneurship22,23. It enables researchers and policymakers to identify not only structural barriers but also the cognitive and normative dynamics that affect entrepreneurial behavior, particularly among educated youth. When positive attitudes toward hybrid entrepreneurship are supported by encouraging social norms and access to critical resources—such as financial capital, skills, and networks—entrepreneurial intentions are significantly strengthened24,25. A strong sense of control over entrepreneurial conditions further reinforces individuals’ likelihood of initiating ventures26,27.

Social Network Theory adds another critical dimension by emphasizing the role of interpersonal relationships and resource exchanges in entrepreneurial ecosystems28. Through social networks, entrepreneurs can access vital information, technical expertise, funding opportunities, and collaborative partnerships—each essential for the success of hybrid ventures29,30. These networks also facilitate market entry, innovation, and knowledge dissemination, thereby enhancing the overall viability of hybrid entrepreneurship26.

In addition to these theoretical foundations, Self-Determination Theory (SDT) provides a valuable psychological lens for understanding how organizational support influences hybrid entrepreneurial motivation. SDT posits that individuals’ intrinsic motivation is strengthened when their needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness are fulfilled. In the context of hybrid entrepreneurship, organizational mechanisms such as job promotion opportunities, a positive work culture, and access to supportive resources can enhance employees’ sense of competence and autonomy, thereby fostering stronger entrepreneurial motivation and intention. Recent studies integrating SDT with technology adoption frameworks also show that institutional support enhances users’ self-efficacy and engagement, which parallels the mechanisms shaping hybrid entrepreneurship in organizational settings31.

Furthermore, the techno-industry nexus—which represents the connection between scientific or technical knowledge and its application in real organizational contexts—can be better understood through the lens of emerging digital technologies. Generative artificial intelligence (AI), for example, can translate organizational data and technical insights into practical outputs such as marketing content, product prototypes, strategic reports, or operational guidelines. By enabling employees to convert knowledge into usable artifacts rapidly, generative AI strengthens the bridge between technological expertise and industrial application, thereby operationalizing the techno-industry nexus in a concrete and contemporary manner. Recent studies highlight that generative AI significantly enhances employee creativity and performance within organizational functions, reinforcing its role as a catalyst for innovation and entrepreneurship32.

Additionally, the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) offers a useful framework for analyzing how individuals adopt and utilize new technologies. Developed by Davis33, TAM posits those two primary cognitive beliefs—Perceived Usefulness (PU) and Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU)—influence an individual’s intention to adopt a specific technology. PU refers to the belief that a technology will improve one’s effectiveness or performance, while PEOU reflects the perceived effortlessness associated with its use34,35. Widely applied across various domains—including education, healthcare, and entrepreneurship—TAM is particularly relevant in the context of hybrid entrepreneurship, where success often hinges on the effective use of digital platforms, social media, and online marketplaces. In countries like Iran, where access to conventional business infrastructure is constrained, digital entrepreneurship is emerging as a practical and scalable alternative36. Understanding the perceptual and psychological factors driving technology adoption is thus crucial. Integrating TAM into this study enables a deeper exploration of how young entrepreneurs’ attitudes toward digital technologies shape their entrepreneurial engagement, innovation potential, and scalability—critical considerations for tech-savvy yet economically constrained youth populations.

Hybrid entrepreneurs who perceive technology as a means to enhance competitiveness and operational efficiency are more likely to embrace innovation37. Moreover, previous job experience and organizational support contribute to shaping positive attitudes toward technology, reinforcing adoption behavior38,39. Financial motivations and family support also play a key role in accelerating the acceptance and effective implementation of technology40.

As recent TAM studies illustrate, technology adoption is strongly affected by users’ familiarity with existing systems and their reliance on convenient, intuitive workflows. Contemporary studies show that individuals—particularly younger, digitally active users—tend to value familiarity and convenience over technological novelty when evaluating new tools41. This suggests that employees’ previous experience with organizational software may shape their perceptions of new entrepreneurial technologies, strengthening both PEOU and PU. In contexts such as government organizations, where standardized digital systems are commonly used, the alignment between new entrepreneurial technologies and existing digital routines may significantly enhance acceptance and reduce perceived complexity. These insights highlight the need to consider familiarity-based and experience-driven moderators when examining technology-driven pathways to hybrid entrepreneurship42.

Building upon these theoretical perspectives, the present study investigates the factors influencing the adoption of hybrid entrepreneurship within government organizations in Iran’s agricultural sector, specifically in Fars Province. By analyzing variables such as risk-taking propensity, entrepreneurial motivation, and networking within the context of organizational culture and the broader business environment, the study seeks to uncover how these elements interact to foster a supportive ecosystem for technology adoption. Mediating factors—including perceived ease of use, techno-industry nexus, and perceived usefulness—are also examined for their roles in enhancing entrepreneurial intention and technology adoption. Collectively, these variables illuminate the complex interplay between social theories and individual behavior in the entrepreneurial ecosystem. Research method

This study was conducted using a descriptive approach, and data were collected through a survey method. The statistical population comprised experts, faculty members, and specialized staff from various governmental institutions in Fars Province, including the Agricultural Jihad Organization, the Department of Natural Resources and Watershed Management, the Department of Environmental Protection, and the Science and Technology Park. These individuals possessed valuable scientific and practical experience in the field of hybrid entrepreneurship, making these organizations key players in fostering entrepreneurship and innovation. The sample consisted of experienced professionals working within these organizations who were engaged in salaried employment as well as hybrid entrepreneurship. Stratified random sampling was used to select hybrid entrepreneurs for the study. Based on the total population of 600 hybrid entrepreneurs employed across the targeted governmental organizations, a sample of 234 individuals was randomly chosen using Krejcie and Morgan43 and subsequently interviewed. The questionnaires were distributed proportionally among the four organizations, reflecting the number of active hybrid entrepreneurs in each as 71 individuals from Agricultural Jihad Organization, 53 of them from the Department of Natural Resources and Watershed Management, 50 individuals from the Department of Environmental Protection, and 60 from the Science and Technology Park.

Although the study employed the traditional Krejcie and Morgan43 table, contemporary SEM literature suggests that a sample of 234 is sufficient for medium-complexity structural models. For variance-based SEM, the “10-times rule” and other minimum sample recommendations indicate that this sample size is adequate to detect significant relationships among latent variables44,45. Furthermore, post-hoc power analysis using G*Power confirmed that with 234 respondents, the study achieves adequate statistical power to detect medium effect sizes (f² = 0.15) at an alpha level of 0.05 for the estimated SEM paths, providing confidence in the reliability and robustness of the results. Moreover, the selection of the four organizations is justified as they represent the primary governmental bodies in Iran responsible for agriculture, natural resources, environmental management, and public-sector innovation. This ensures a relevant and representative sampling frame for studying hybrid entrepreneurship adoption.

The questionnaire design was grounded in an extensive review of relevant literature and the conceptual framework of the research. The questionnaires were distributed in person, with necessary explanations provided to the participants. The data collection process lasted for four months. This study was reviewed and approved by the Higher Education Committees at both the Agricultural School and Shiraz University levels, ensuring adherence to ethical considerations. All participants provided informed consent before taking part in the study. We confirm that all research was conducted in accordance with the relevant ethical guidelines and regulations governing research with human participants, as outlined by the Higher Education Committees at Shiraz University, and in compliance with internationally recognized ethical principles.

The study examined various variables that play crucial roles in the process of hybrid entrepreneurship adoption. Among the independent variables, risk-taking refers to the willingness to embrace entrepreneurial risks through a rational assessment of possible outcomes and benefits46–48. Hybrid entrepreneurial motivation encompasses the reasons and driving forces behind individuals’ engagement in hybrid entrepreneurship49,50. Networking captures the interactions and relationships entrepreneurs build within their professional circles, which facilitate resource and information exchange51. The business environment includes economic and social conditions that impact entrepreneurial activities, while organizational culture relates to the shared values and beliefs within an organization that influence employee behaviors52,53. Job promotion refers to opportunities for career advancement, and time management denotes the ability to efficiently plan and utilize time resources. Several mediating variables were also considered, including perceived ease of use, which relates to how effortlessly entrepreneurs can adopt new tools and technologies necessary for launching and managing businesses54–56. The techno-industry nexus reflects the connection between scientific knowledge and its practical application within the industry. Perceived usefulness involves individuals’ evaluations of the potential benefits associated with hybrid entrepreneurship57,58, and hybrid entrepreneurial intention indicates their willingness and resolve to initiate hybrid entrepreneurial ventures59,60. Ultimately, the dependent variable, hybrid entrepreneurship adoption, signifies individuals’ readiness and commitment to embracing and implementing hybrid entrepreneurship models. These variables collectively illustrate the complex interplay between social, organizational, and individual factors influencing the adoption of hybrid entrepreneurship, underscoring the importance of understanding these dynamics for success in this field.

After the initial design of the questionnaire, its face validity was confirmed through a review by a group of university professors. To assess the reliability of the questionnaire, a pilot study was conducted with a sample of 30 participants outside the main sample, in Kermanshah province. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated as shown in Table 1. As observed, the Cronbach’s alpha values for the research variables indicate a high level of reliability for the measurement instrument.

To address potential common method bias (CMB), given that the study relied on self-reported data collected from a single source at a single point in time, several procedural and statistical measures were implemented to minimize its impact. Procedural remedies included assuring respondents of anonymity and confidentiality, presenting items in a randomized order, using different scale anchors across constructs, and phrasing questions in clear, neutral, and non-leading wording to mitigate social desirability effects and reduce interpretive ambiguity. To assess CMB statistically, Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values were examined for all constructs using SPSS. According to Ringle et al.61, VIF values below 3.3 indicate that CMB is unlikely to pose a serious threat to the validity of the results. In the present study, all VIF values fell well below the 3.3 threshold, as reported in Table 1 alongside the reliability results from the pilot study, suggesting that CMB was not a significant concern.

After the questionnaire was approved, the research data were collected and subsequently analyzed using SPSS 16 and SmartPLS3 software. Descriptive statistics, one-way ANOVA, and Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) were employed for data analysis. SEM is a statistical technique that helps researchers examine the relationships among multiple variables within a model. Its ability to develop and test theories has led to its widespread application across various fields, including human resource management, marketing, strategic management, and information systems45.

Results

Overview of respondents

The study sample consisted of 234 hybrid entrepreneurs working in organizations including the Agricultural Jihad Organization, the Department of Natural Resources and Watershed Management, the Department of Environmental Protection, and the Science and Technology Park. Among the respondents, 86 individuals (36.8%) held a doctoral degree, while 61 respondents (26.1%) had a master’s degree. Additionally, 38 respondents (16.2%) had a bachelor’s degree. According to these statistics, approximately 62.9% of the respondents possessed postgraduate education. 71 respondents (30.3%) were female, and 163 respondents (69.7%) were male (Table 2).

Findings from Table 2 also reveal that 100 respondents (42.7%) have more than 19 years of work experience, while 59 respondents (25.2%) have 12 to 19 years of experience. Moreover, 75 respondents (32.1%) reported having less than 12 years of work experience. In terms of entrepreneurial activity, 64 respondents (27.4%) reported 4 to 9 years of experience in hybrid entrepreneurship, 113 respondents (48.3%) had more than 9 years of entrepreneurial experience, and 57 respondents (24.3%) had less than 4 years of experience in this field.

Comparison of hybrid entrepreneurs employed in the target organizations

Table 3 presents the results of a one-way ANOVA conducted to compare hybrid entrepreneurs across four organizations: The Agricultural Jihad Organization, the Department of Natural Resources and Watershed Management, the Department of Environmental Protection, and firms located in the Fars Province Science and Technology Park. The findings indicate statistically significant differences among respondents in the variables of risk-taking (F = 2.90, sig = 0.03), hybrid entrepreneurial motivation (F = 3.29, sig = 0.02), time management (F = 2.75, sig = 0.04), job promotion (F = 4.16, sig = 0.0001), perceived ease of use (F = 3.15, sig = 0.02), networking (F = 5.34, sig = 0.0001), techno-industry nexus (F = 5.72, sig = 0.0001), perceived usefulness (F = 4.89, sig = 0.0001), and hybrid entrepreneurial intention (F = 3.26, sig = 0.02).

Post hoc analysis using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test revealed significant differences in mean scores among hybrid entrepreneurs employed at the Science and Technology Park compared to those in the other three organizations. Specifically, significant differences were found in risk-taking (25.36), hybrid entrepreneurial motivation (51.75), time management (32.75), techno-industry nexus (28.06), and perceived usefulness (24.84). Moreover, the Tukey HSD results indicated that the mean score for job promotion (23.00) among entrepreneurs in the Science and Technology Park significantly differed from those in the Department of Environmental Protection and the Natural Resources and Watershed Management. Similarly, a significant difference in perceived ease of use (17.48) was observed between entrepreneurs at the Science and Technology Park and those at the Agricultural Jihad Organization and the Department of Natural Resources and Watershed Management. Additionally, significant differences were noted in the mean scores of networking (31.60) and hybrid entrepreneurial intention (25.96) between hybrid entrepreneurs in the Science and Technology Park and those employed in the Agricultural Jihad Organization and the Department of Environmental Protection.

Given that the majority of respondents in the Science and Technology Park hold postgraduate degrees and are members of academic staff, they enjoy access—within their primary organizations—to a variety of institutional supports and incentives. These include financial instruments such as loans and grants, access to social networks, the development of business ideas and commercial plans, capabilities in networking and consultation, opportunities to build and expand national and international collaborations, the ability to identify and exploit entrepreneurial opportunities, as well as access to advanced entrepreneurial education and financial management skills. The presence of knowledge-seeking individuals constitutes a fundamental pillar of competitiveness in the globalized environment. The economic success or failure of contemporary firms is closely tied to the quantity and quality of their intellectual capital and specialized expertise. Many organizations now acknowledge that sustainable competitive advantage lies in human capital, and thus prioritize the enhancement of employee knowledge, skills, and capabilities.

Such an organizational environment serves as an incubator for testing entrepreneurial ideas, effectively reducing the risk-taking burden associated with business failure by facilitating access to relevant information and resources. These conditions place hybrid entrepreneurs in the Science and Technology Park at a comparative advantage relative to their counterparts in other organizations, rendering them more predisposed to hybrid entrepreneurial adoption and eventual success. These individuals are actively engaged in techno-industry nexus domains, leveraging their specialized knowledge to outperform competitors. They also employ up-to-date technological knowledge and digital tools, including social networks, to reduce transaction costs, identify business environment opportunities, and facilitate knowledge spillovers, all of which contribute to business growth and innovation. The emphasis on knowledge as a key asset in expanded commercial networks has endowed the hybrid entrepreneurs located in the Fars Province Science and Technology Park with a distinct competitive edge. In line with this, the importance of the study’s variables has been corroborated in previous research. For instance, Zandazar et al.62 in their exploratory study on the benefits of locating knowledge-based firms in Fars Province’s science and technology incubators, demonstrated that access to diverse services (consulting, training, specialized workspace, financial facilities, etc.), physical security, funding, synergy, and entrepreneurship are major advantages afforded to these firms by their location within such ecosystems.

Similarly, Kurczewska and Mackiewicz63 highlighted that individual with diverse educational and professional backgrounds, coupled with managerial experience, are more likely to succeed in establishing hybrid ventures. Another report showed that hybrid entrepreneurs who enjoy job security, stable income, and accumulated experience in their primary employment develop higher levels of capability, and hybrid entrepreneurial motivation, thereby increasing their likelihood of entrepreneurial success. Drescher64 further observed that professional experience in managing large firms significantly contributes to hybrid entrepreneurial intention and performance within small and private enterprises.

Construct validation and reliability analysis

The path analysis and causal model of constructs affecting the adoption of hybrid entrepreneurship within public sector organizations in Fars Province are illustrated in Fig. 2. In this analysis, the impact of a set of variables was assessed using SmartPLS3 software. The software output, as depicted in Fig. 2, presents the factor loadings for each item. To evaluate the measurement model’s fit, three criteria were employed: reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity, based on the Fornell-Larcker approach65.

Reliability

Reliability refers to the extent to which a questionnaire yields consistent results when administered under similar conditions. In this study, reliability was assessed through the evaluation of factor loadings, Cronbach’s alpha, and composite reliability.

Factor loadings

A critical step in the evaluation of measurement models is the calculation of factor loadings, which reflect the strength of the relationship between latent constructs and their observed items. According to Marar et al.66, factor loadings should be equal to or greater than 0.40 to ensure acceptable reliability and construct validity. Factor loadings indicate how strongly each individual item is associated with its underlying construct, based on the correlation between the item and the latent variable it is intended to measure. Loadings equal to or above the 0.40 threshold indicate that the variance shared between the construct and its items exceeds the measurement error variance, thereby confirming the reliability of the measurement model. As presented in Tables 4, 5 and 6, all constructs exhibit factor loadings exceeding 0.40, suggesting a satisfactory level of reliability for this component of the model.

Cronbach’s alpha

Following the evaluation of factor loadings, Cronbach’s alpha was assessed to determine internal consistency reliability. It assesses internal consistency by measuring the overall correlation among all items within a construct, indicating how reliably they collectively represent the same underlying concept. A Cronbach’s alpha value above 0.7 is indicative of acceptable reliability. This threshold is clearly met, as shown in Tables 4, 5 and 6.

Discriminant validity

Discriminant validity is a subset of construct validity. This criterion assesses the extent to which a test measures the concept it is intended to measure. Specifically, discriminant validity evaluates whether constructs that are theoretically expected to be unrelated are indeed distinct. The Fornell-Larcker criterion was employed to assess discriminant validity. According to Fornell and Larcker67, discriminant validity is considered acceptable when the AVE for each construct is greater than the shared variance between that construct and any other constructs in the model. This evaluation is performed using a matrix in which the cells contain the correlation coefficients between constructs, while the diagonal elements represent the square roots of the AVE values for each construct. A model demonstrates acceptable discriminant validity if the diagonal values exceed all off-diagonal values in the corresponding rows and columns. Essentially, the Fornell-Larcker matrix is derived from the software output, with the difference that the diagonal is replaced by the square root of the AVE for each construct. As shown in Table 7, the values along the main diagonal are greater than all other values in their respective columns, confirming that the model possesses satisfactory discriminant validity.

Structural equation model fit

Following the assessment of the measurement model, the structural model is evaluated. The Partial Least Squares (PLS) model fit indicates the degree of alignment between the proposed theoretical model and the empirical data collected in the study. Various model fit indices are used in structural equation modeling to assess the adequacy of the structural model. In SmartPLS3 software, the PLS model fit indices include the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), the exact fit measures d_ULS and d_G, the Goodness of Fit (GOF) index, path coefficients’ significance, and the coefficient of determination (R²). The SRMR index is defined as the difference between the observed correlation matrix and the model-implied correlation matrix. It provides an absolute measure of model fit by evaluating the average magnitude of discrepancies between observed and expected correlations. An SRMR value below 0.10 is indicative of a good model fit. As shown in Table 8, the standardized root mean square residual is 0.073, confirming a strong model fit. Additionally, Euclidean Distance (d_ULS) and Geodesic Distance (d_G), are used for evaluating model fit in PLS and are known as exact fit criteria. Researchers employ these indices to assess the discrepancy between covariance matrices, where acceptable thresholds are d_G > 0.05 and d_ULS > 0.05. The results presented in Table 8 demonstrate the validity of the model fit based on the satisfactory alignment of estimated parameters with these benchmark values.

According to Table 14, hybrid entrepreneurial intention is positively and significantly associated with the adoption of hybrid entrepreneurship (β = 0.378, p = 0.0001), thereby supporting H27. This finding corresponds with Petrova’s79 argument that hybrid entrepreneurs rely on perceived usefulness factors such as opportunity recognition, risk minimization, creative problem-solving, resource availability, and flexibility. Developing hybrid entrepreneurial intentions may enable individuals to test and refine these competencies within a secure environment before committing to full-time entrepreneurship. In this sense, intention appears to represent a pivotal cognitive mechanism associated with the movement from motivation and perceived capability toward the adoption of entrepreneurial behavior.

Beyond this direct association, the results indicate that several other variables are indirectly related to the adoption of hybrid entrepreneurship. Variables such as job promotion, business environment, time management, hybrid entrepreneurial motivation, networking, risk-taking, organizational culture, perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and the techno-industry nexus show positive indirect associations through the mediating role of hybrid entrepreneurial intention. These relationships resonate with research by Jonbekova et al.80 and Singh81, which emphasize that universities, organizations, and industries form interconnected systems that collectively contribute to economic development and the creation of innovative ideas. In this context, hybrid entrepreneurs often serve as intermediaries bridging research centers and industry, facilitating the transfer and application of scientific knowledge within business environments.

Technological advancement and the globalization of knowledge have heightened the need for stronger collaboration between industry and academia. Hybrid entrepreneurs, through their dual occupational roles, often access strategic relationships that allow them to draw on both institutional and industrial resources when initiating ventures. Supportive and flexible organizational environments—characterized by openness to new ideas, creativity, and risk-taking—further encourage employees to engage in hybrid entrepreneurship by strengthening their competencies and expanding opportunities for professional growth.

Conversely, rigid hierarchies, heavy regulation, and limited autonomy tend to suppress innovative behavior. In line with Schumpeter’s notion of “creative destruction,” entrepreneurial activity drives transformation through the development of new products, technologies, and organizational forms82–84. Innovation is therefore central to hybrid entrepreneurship49,85, suggesting that individuals with higher innovative capacity are more likely to pursue hybrid ventures. Consistent with Maleki et al.12, our findings also indicate that the techno-industry nexus strengthens links between universities and industry, supporting the conditions necessary for hybrid entrepreneurship to flourish.

Discussion

Consistent with Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory, which emphasizes the reciprocal interactions between personal cognition, behavior, and environment, this study found that job promotion and time management significantly enhance perceived usefulness. Hybrid entrepreneurs’ ability to leverage knowledge and skills from their dual roles contributes to improved job performance and organizational innovation, reflecting the theory’s premise that behavior is shaped by both internal and external influences. The positive effect of job promotion underscores how organizational rewards and career advancements reinforce entrepreneurial cognition and motivation, thereby promoting technology adoption and entrepreneurial engagement. This aligns with Husain et al.68, who highlighted flexible organizational structures as enablers of creativity and employee empowerment.

From the perspective of Self-Determination Theory (SDT), these organizational support mechanisms can also be understood as fulfilling employees’ basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness, which are known to enhance intrinsic motivation86. When employees perceive that their organization provides fair promotion opportunities, values their contributions, and offers a supportive culture, they experience greater feelings of competence and autonomy. These experiences, in turn, strengthen their internal drive to pursue entrepreneurial activities within and beyond the organization. This SDT-based explanation provides a deeper psychological rationale for why supportive organizational environments are effective in fostering hybrid entrepreneurial motivation and intention. Recent research has similarly demonstrated that institutional support enhances users’ sense of efficacy and intrinsic motivation in technology adoption contexts, reinforcing the critical role of organizational encouragement in shaping adoption behaviors31.

The Theory of Planned Behavior is strongly supported by findings related to hybrid entrepreneurial motivation and intention. The direct effect of motivation on entrepreneurial intention underscores the importance of attitudes and perceived behavioral control in shaping entrepreneurial decisions. Motivated individuals are more likely to form intentions toward hybrid entrepreneurship as a means of securing financial stability and personal fulfillment, consistent with Demir et al.87. Moreover, the significant role of the business environment in enhancing entrepreneurial intention emphasizes the influence of subjective norms and perceived ease of access to resources, reinforcing Ajzen’s theory that external social factors and control perceptions are pivotal in the decision-making process.

Social Network Theory’s relevance is evident in the significant positive impact of networking on both the techno-industry nexus and entrepreneurial intention. The findings demonstrate how interpersonal connections and resource exchanges facilitate access to critical financial, technical, and informational support, thereby fostering innovation and venture development. Hybrid entrepreneurs act as boundary spanners between organizational knowledge and market demands, highlighting the role of networks in bridging science and industry, consistent with Goledzinowski and Blocki28. This is critical in a context like Iran’s, where collaborative ecosystems can mitigate economic challenges by promoting knowledge diffusion and opportunity recognition.

The Technology Acceptance Model is validated through the observed effects of perceived usefulness and ease of use on techno-industry nexus formation, entrepreneurial intention, and adoption of hybrid entrepreneurship. Entrepreneurs who perceive technology as beneficial and easy to use are more likely to integrate emerging technologies into their ventures, thus improving performance and competitiveness. This supports TAM’s premise that cognitive evaluations of technology drive adoption behaviors88,89. Importantly, the mediating role of techno-industry linkage illustrates how perceptions of technology translate into tangible industry outcomes, supporting the assertion by Rasumov and Markaryan90 that technological adoption is fundamental for hybrid entrepreneurship success.

To further clarify the operational mechanisms of the techno-industry nexus, emerging technologies such as generative artificial intelligence (AI) offer a concrete example of how scientific and technical knowledge is transformed into practical industrial outputs. Generative AI systems can rapidly convert organizational data and domain-specific knowledge into marketing content, product prototypes, technical documentation, or strategic analyses, thereby directly bridging scientific insight with real-world application. These tools enhance employees’ creative capacity, reduce the time required to translate ideas into actionable outputs, and enable individuals to experiment with innovative solutions—capabilities that are central to hybrid entrepreneurship. Recent evidence shows that generative AI significantly enhances employee creativity and performance within organizational functions, reinforcing its role as a key facilitator of the techno-industry connection32. Incorporating generative AI into this perspective helps make the techno-industry nexus more tangible and illustrates how modern technologies strengthen the pathways linking knowledge production to entrepreneurial action.

At the same time, recent extensions of TAM suggest that perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness operate not only as evaluative beliefs but also as reflections of employees’ prior familiarity with existing technological systems and their preference for convenient, well-established workflows. Research indicates that users often rely on familiarity and convenience rather than novelty when adopting new technologies41. In this regard, employees who already have experience with organizational software may evaluate emerging entrepreneurial technologies more positively because these systems feel more intuitive and less disruptive to their routines. This implies that the PEOU–intention relationship observed in this study may be partially shaped by the degree to which new entrepreneurial tools resemble existing information systems used within government organizations.

Similarly, perceived usefulness may be strengthened when new technologies complement familiar processes, reduce cognitive load, and fit seamlessly into employees’ established task structures. Thus, technology acceptance in the hybrid entrepreneurship context may depend not only on design characteristics but also on accumulated user experience and expectations regarding convenience12. These insights enrich the theoretical interpretation of TAM in this study and highlight the need to consider familiarity- and experience-based moderators in future research.

Furthermore, the study’s results highlight the dynamic and multifaceted nature of hybrid entrepreneurship in government organizations. The positive direct and indirect effects of variables such as job promotion, business environment, and networking on entrepreneurial intention and adoption illustrate a systemic interdependence. These findings resonate with Ajzen’s notion that behavior is not only influenced by personal attitudes but also by environmental facilitators and social support. Hybrid entrepreneurship emerges as a viable strategy for navigating the challenges of Iran’s labor market, enabling individuals to supplement income and innovate within organizational and technological frameworks.

Conclusion

This study investigated the determinants influencing the adoption of hybrid entrepreneurship among public sector employees in Iran’s agricultural sector, drawing upon an integrative theoretical foundation that includes Social Cognitive Theory, the Theory of Planned Behavior, Social Network Theory, and the Technology Acceptance Model. The findings provide a nuanced understanding of how individual cognition, organizational conditions, social networks, and technology perceptions jointly shape hybrid entrepreneurial behaviors within public sector contexts.

Hybrid entrepreneurship—where individuals develop business ventures while retaining salaried employment—has emerged as a significant model in environments characterized by economic uncertainty and labor market constraints. This study identified key variables that support or inhibit the adoption of hybrid entrepreneurship, thereby offering a comprehensive perspective on the dynamics underpinning this entrepreneurial pathway.

Hybrid entrepreneurial motivation emerged as a central factor, encompassing financial incentives, family security, collaboration, and intrinsic passion. These motivations not only influence individual engagement but also contribute to organizational innovation by enabling employees to address challenges creatively and elevate service quality. Perceived usefulness also plays a vital role, as entrepreneurs who recognize the tangible benefits of hybrid entrepreneurship—financial, social, or psychological—are more inclined to adopt and persist in hybrid ventures.

The business environment was found to be a critical contextual factor, shaping entrepreneurial intention through economic, social, legal, and cultural conditions. Supportive environments reduce entry barriers and perceived risks, facilitate access to financial and informational resources, and strengthen collaborative networks that ease adoption processes. The techno-industry nexus further reinforces this process by enabling scientifically trained individuals to apply their expertise to industrial challenges, fostering innovation and strengthening connections between universities, governmental bodies, and industry. Networking amplifies these benefits by facilitating resource exchange, knowledge flow, and collaboration across institutional boundaries.

Taken together, the findings demonstrate that hybrid entrepreneurship in the public sector is influenced by a constellation of individual, organizational, and technological factors. As hybrid entrepreneurs gain experience and access to scientific and technological resources, they are better positioned to generate innovative ideas, improve productivity, and contribute to organizational resilience. These insights enrich the broader understanding of hybrid entrepreneurship and underscore the importance of designing policies and organizational practices that support employees in pursuing hybrid entrepreneurial initiatives.

Practical implications

The results of this study offer several actionable insights for policymakers, public sector managers, and institutional leaders seeking to strengthen hybrid entrepreneurship within Iran’s agricultural sector. A central implication is the need to reduce administrative and regulatory barriers that hinder employees from initiating side ventures. This includes simplifying licensing procedures for agricultural and agri-technology micro-businesses, minimizing bureaucratic delays in permit issuance, and providing clear and accessible guidance on tax obligations for employees with dual income streams. These targeted reforms directly address some of the most significant constraints faced by hybrid entrepreneurs in rural and semi-rural regions.

Equally important is the promotion of innovation-driven collaboration between government bodies and academic institutions. Public agencies can operationalize such collaboration through mechanisms like co-funded research grants between agricultural organizations and universities, the creation of technology transfer offices specifically equipped to support public employees seeking to commercialize innovative ideas, and the implementation of “innovation leave” policies that allow employees temporary time away from routine duties to develop their entrepreneurial projects while retaining job security. These steps can significantly strengthen employees’ capacity to translate scientific knowledge into practical applications for the agricultural sector.

Additional organizational support is crucial for enhancing employees’ competence, autonomy, and readiness to pursue hybrid entrepreneurship. Government organizations could establish dedicated support units offering mentoring, legal and financial counseling, and market intelligence services, while also streamlining internal procedures that provide employees easier access to digital tools and organizational resources. Recognition and reward programs for employee-driven innovation would further encourage entrepreneurial engagement and align with the motivational principles emphasized by Self-Determination Theory.

Finally, fostering stronger academia–industry partnerships can build a more adaptive and knowledge-driven entrepreneurial ecosystem. This can be achieved by creating joint incubators, offering university-led training programs for public-sector employees, and supporting applied research projects that align with the strategic priorities of Iran’s agricultural sector. Such initiatives reinforce the techno-industry nexus and contribute to sustainable innovation and economic resilience.

Research limitations and future directions

The study primarily focused on key variables identified through existing literature and empirical analysis, which may have excluded other potentially influential factors such as psychological traits or sector-specific dynamics. Moreover, the reliance on self-reported measures may introduce biases related to respondents’ perceptions and social desirability, which can affect the accuracy of the results. Although procedural and statistical remedies were applied to minimize CMB—including anonymity assurances, randomized item ordering, and the evaluation of VIF values—CMB cannot be fully ruled out due to the single-source, cross-sectional nature of the data. Future studies should adopt multi-source designs, time-lagged data collection, or mixed-method approaches to further mitigate this limitation and strengthen causal inferences. Future research could expand by exploring hybrid entrepreneurship adoption in diverse geographic and industry contexts to enhance the external validity of findings. Further investigation into the role of individual psychological factors, such as risk tolerance and resilience, could enrich the explanatory power of existing models. Additionally, qualitative approaches, including case studies and interviews, may uncover nuanced insights into the decision-making processes and contextual challenges faced by hybrid entrepreneurs.
