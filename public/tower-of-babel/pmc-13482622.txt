An effective BiLSTM-CNN model for predicting large-scale temporal-spatial dynamics of normalized difference vegetation index
Authors: Arbia Soula, Almudena Díaz Zayas, Riadh Ksantini, Pedro Merino Gomez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVegetation, though central to terrestrial ecosystems, remains highly vulnerable to fluctuations in climate and human-induced activities. Such combined influence on vegetation health dynamics necessitates the application of robust remote sensing-based vegetation indices, such as the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI). The latter enables the detection of subtle structural and functional changes, offering an early warning of plant stress before visible symptoms appear. It is therefore crucial to predict vegetation activities by modelling NDVI that is able to detect and attribute the climate change impacts on vegetation growth through its temporal and spatial variations. For this reason, in this paper we introduce an advanced combined deep learning method (bidirectional long short-term memory and convolutional neural network model, BiLSTM-CNN) for temporal-spatial modelling of NDVI informed by meteorological and soil moisture data. BiLSTM-CNN is a composite progressive processing model that can investigate potential trends of vegetation alterations that may be abrupt and barely obvious, localized and extensive, happening over short or long-time scales. Our proposed BiLSTM-CNN forecasting method has been evaluated and compared with state-of-the-art techniques, and the experimental results have shown clearly that our proposed method is competitive with the existing relevant NDVI deep learning predicting models.Keywords: Deep learning, Normalized difference vegetation index, Meteorological data, Remote sensing data, Bidirectional long short-term memory (BiLSTM), Convolutional neural network (CNN)Subject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Not only do continued global climate change and anthropogenic activities pose a substantial risk to global water safety, but they also heighten vulnerabilities within vegetation dynamics1. While Vegetation is necessarily pivotal to the hydrological system, it is as a matter of fact imperatively essential to assess the correlation between the global vegetation status and environmental specifications. Such an operation helps improve climate adaptation strategies, optimize water use efficiency, and maximize accurate, sustainable crop production by qualifying farmers to monitor plant health and crop water stress. Therefore, to come to terms with the reciprocal reactions that exist in vegetation dynamics and climate change, monitoring vegetation phenology is essential.

If advancements in satellite imaging have substantially enhanced vegetation monitoring and assessment across spatial and temporal scales, Satellite-derived vegetation indices (VIs) have provided valuable indicators of vegetation functioning across the land surface. More importantly, NDVI – a quantitative metric used to study and model the physiological process of vegetation dynamics in their interaction with climate and environmental variations – exploits the divergence between red and near-infrared reflectance patterns linked to plant photosynthesis to estimate and assess vegetation status.

Indeed, research shows that the relationship between vegetation dynamics and climatic factors has been explored through three main groups of approaches. The first group encompasses empirical as well as statistical methods such as the Geodetector technique2, Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) techniques3, Empirical Orthogonal Function (EOF)4, and the Residual method5. However, if the aforementioned approaches premise that the NDVI time series data are linear and stationary, they are, as a matter of fact, not able to learn non-linear and non-stationary information hidden in the time series data, leading as such to a poor performance in NDVI predictions.

Besides, the second group consists of process-based dynamic vegetation models6, including Dynamic Global Vegetation Models (DGVMs) and their variants, which explicitly simulate fundamental ecological processes, including photosynthesis, respiration, and carbon allocation7,8. Nevertheless, these models cannot accurately capture key ecosystem processes driving vegetation growth. But unlike empirical or process-based methods, machine learning and deep learning techniques, constituting the third group, enhance the capacity to assess, analyse, and understand vegetation life cycle patterns at both spatial and temporal scales.

In particular, commonly used shallow machine learning models include Support Vector Machines (SVM), Artificial Neural Networks (ANN)9 and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)10. Indeed, deep learning models can learn hierarchical representations from raw data and capture complex temporal and spatial patterns, involving Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)11, Long Short-Term Memory networks (LSTM)12, and Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM)13.

As regards vegetation activity monitoring, the BiLSTM model effectively captures long-term temporal dependencies in NDVI dynamics driven by meteorological factors, making it highly suitable for accurate forecasting of individual vegetation types that range from deciduous and evergreen forests, mixed forests, shrublands, grasslands, savannas, to croplands. But, while BiLSTM excels at modelling temporal patterns, it has limited capacity to capture spatial structures and local heterogeneity, accordingly restricting its applicability in regions with diverse vegetation types and complex climate conditions.

Moreover, in land surface studies modelling vegetation activity through climate variables is primordial, given that these variables are the primary drivers of vegetation dynamics. Therefore, analysing high-resolution meteorological data across both spatial and temporal scales is critical inasmuch as it accurately simulates and predicts vegetation activity patterns. But where satellite-based vegetation indices can be intermittent or unavailable at certain times, the ability to continuously monitor vegetation becomes subject to limitations. Provided that climate variables and soil moisture can be measured on a daily basis without missing data makes it possible to track vegetation activity on a regular basis. Incorporating such climatic and soil variables into vegetation models allow for a reliable simulation and prediction of vegetation dynamics, even when satellite-derived observations are absent.

As an improvement of BiLSTM architecture, hybrid models have been developed. The most recent being the CNN-BiLSTM model proposed in14, which capitalizes on Convolutional Neural Networks’(CNN) capabilities for spatial feature extraction and BiLSTM’s capacities to detect temporal patterns of the vegetation indices. As such, it effectively makes up for the shortcomings of standalone models to capture the indices of vegetation’s spatiotemporal patterns defined on multi-dimensional and grid-based data. In the CNN-BiLSTM model, the extraction of spatial characteristics, through convolutional layers, will be done prior to the application of BiLSTM’s temporal modelling. However, early dimensionality reduction of the earlier layers employing convolution and pooling can cause a loss of detailed spatial characteristics, which would hamper the ability of BiLSTM layers to capture the temporal features in high-resolution vegetation dynamics monitoring.

To address this gap, in this study, we develop and evaluate a deep machine learning model to simulate vegetation growth in Mainland Spain. Rich with diverse ecosystem types and climate zones, Spain offers itself as a perfect fit to examine the applicability of our BiLSTM-CNN model. In this, we propose an architecture in which BiLSTM layers are applied prior to CNN layers. First, the BiLSTM models the temporal dependencies within the multivariate time series, capturing forward and backward contextual information across time. The resulting temporally encoded representations are then processed by CNN layers to extract higher-level feature interactions together with local patterns from the BiLSTM outputs, thereby enabling effective integration of temporal dynamics with feature-level abstraction.

Indeed, we posit that the BiLSTM–CNN hybrid framework is more suitable for NDVI forecasting in environments with large spatial variability and complex climatic influences. Not only will this model capture temporal sequential data, but it will also extract the most important hierarchical spatial features on board diverse data as well across long time intervals. Such are pivotal for a scalable and efficient NDVI time series prediction. To support the efficacy of our model, we will analyze and forecast the vegetation dynamic index in mainland Spain in the timeframe that runs from 2015 to 2024.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Sect. 2, we review and discuss related studies focusing on NDVI prediction models. Section 3 describes the proposed BiLSTM-CNN based NDVI time series forecasting model. In Sect. 4, we first provide a detailed description of the dataset and preprocessing procedures, followed by an extensive comparative evaluation of the proposed BiLSTM–CNN model against existing NDVI simulation models. Section 5 concludes the manuscript, gives a summary of the main findings achieved, and identifies aspects that might be approached in future research attempts.

Related work

Numerous studies indicate that continuous monitoring and prediction of the vegetation index NDVI are essential for regional ecosystem restoration and environmental management. Such analyses contribute significantly to vegetation stability, sustainable food production, informed ecological restoration strategies, and comprehensive assessment of ecosystem conditions and functions. Liu et al.15 proposed an NDVI time-series reconstruction method for Arctic regions to address data gaps caused by cloud contamination and sensor limitations. As such, their method helped improve the continuity of satellite-derived vegetation observations for spatial-temporal monitoring of vegetation structure. Their approach primarily focuses on restoring missing NDVI values to ensure consistent observation records. In contrast to reconstruction-based approaches, several studies have extended this direction by modeling NDVI – as a function of meteorological drivers – to enable predictive estimation of vegetation dynamics under varying environmental conditions. Subsequently, many studies, in response to the recent climatic changes, have investigated NDVI dynamics and prediction using climate-driven and environmental factors.

Indeed, research on NDVI prediction can be broadly categorized into three main methodological approaches. The first approach adopts an empirical model to examine the way vegetation indices interact with climatic variables across time and space. Wei et al.2 employed a linear regression approach and the Geodetector tool, alongside non-parametric methods such as the Mann–Kendall test and Theil–Sen estimator, to detect and quantify trends in NDVI time-series data across China and its seven regions. Their study evaluated inter-annual and seasonal NDVI growth, identified significant regional or seasonal differences, and examined the relationships between NDVI spatiotemporal variations and climatic factors, including temperature and precipitation.

Cao et al.16 studied the sensitivity of vegetation growth dynamics, in various vegetation types (forest, shrub, and grass), to climate change in Northern China in the period running from 2000 to 2020. In connection, the Breaks for Additive Seasonal and Trend (BFAST) technique was applied to deconstitute not only the NDVI time series into seasonal, trend, and residual constituents, but also to model the trend component through piecewise multiple linear regression for the purpose of detecting sudden behavioural changes. This study indicates that different climatic factors dominate vegetation growth in different areas: precipitation primarily promotes growth, while rising temperatures can slow it down. Moreover, forests and shrubs show distinct responses to solar radiation, reflecting spatial variability in vegetation–climate interactions.

Li et al.5 studied the influence of climate change and human activities on vegetation growth. They used reconstructed NDVI time series for China (1982–2018) from the GIMMS NDVI3g and MOD13A2 datasets, combined with temperature and precipitation observations from over 2000 meteorological stations. Long-term vegetation trends and their uncertainties were analysed using Theil–Sen trend analysis, Mann–Kendall tests, Pearson correlation, and residual trend analysis. The results revealed significant spatial and temporal heterogeneity in vegetation changes. However, by assuming that NDVI time series data are linear and stationary, these models, as a matter of fact, fall short of capturing non-linear and non-stationary features embedded in the data, leading as such to a poor performance in NDVI predictions.

The second group of approaches involves process-based dynamic vegetation models6. These models rely primarily on mathematical representations of terrestrial biospheric processes, allowing the simulation of vegetation dynamics and land–atmosphere interactions since the vegetation is influenced by multiple pressures, including climate change, altered atmospheric composition, and human land use. Changes in vegetation type, structure, and function also provide feedback to the climate by affecting surface-atmosphere carbon and water fluxes. Dynamic Global Vegetation Models (DGVMs) have been applied across different vegetation types and environmental conditions to quantify the impacts of climate change on vegetation and soil processes7.

Nagy et al.8 proposed a network of mountain research sites representing diverse climates, biogeography, and land use histories to fill data gaps and provide baseline knowledge for improving DGVMs. This network enables coordination, integration, and multi-site analyses, facilitating regional and international synthesis of mountain ecosystem responses to global change. It aims to enhance modelling capabilities by informing DGVM parameters, define plant functional types, and improve process representations for mountain and alpine vegetation.

Consequently, an effective modelling of global vegetation growth calls for a rigorous representation of several ecosystem processes, including, but not limited to, vegetative ecosystem carbon dynamics through photosynthesis and respiration. However, this kind of method fails to accurately illustrate the crucial functioning of ecosystem processes that contribute to vegetation growth. This leads to uncertainties in reproduced vegetation status. As well, it requires larger datasets so as to generate a powerful model parameterization.

Machine learning models with shallow and deep architecture methods have notably enhanced the capacity to assess, analyze, and understand vegetation life cycle patterns at both the spatial and the temporal levels. Several machine learning techniques take vegetation development monitoring as a supervised learning problem, where temperature and precipitation, among many other factors, are input variables, while the vegetation indicator is the target variable. For example, Huang et al.9 utilized a blend of three forecasting models – the Multiple Linear Regression (MLR), Artificial Neural Network (ANN), and Support Vector Machine (SVM) – in order to predict NDVI in China’s Yellow River Basin, while using the entropy weight method to measure the uncertainties of predicted outcomes of each individual model with the intention to improve prediction reliability. If ANN and SVM have the best fitting ability in the calibration phase, they, however, have the lowest performance in the validation phase.

In contradistinction with separate, non-combined prediction models, the combined forecasting model (CFM) shows superior predictive performance when it comes to calibration and validation. In connection, Li et al.10 presented a machine learning model based on extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) to simulate vegetation growth in different Chinese regions that showcase various ecosystem types and climate characteristics. To this end, they accounted for six environmental variables that bear upon vegetation development such as mean air temperature, precipitation, vapor pressure deficit, wind speed, solar radiation, and atmospheric CO2 concentration.

While Li et al’s model effectively anticipates the spatial and seasonal variations of NDVI with a coefficient of determination equal to 0.83; however, the gradient boosting machine falls short of dealing with time series data and hence does not accurately predict vegetation growth cycle. This means that it cannot figure out the temporal sequence history or trends that are in direct relation to climate and NDVI sequential data. Thus, such a model is essentially unable to simulate sequential relationships.

Gao et al.12 presented a deep learning model based on the long-short-term memory (LSTM) technique. In order to model the NDVI time series values, they used the prominent weather conditions that are able to impact the vegetation in the YRB area. In the primary phase, they used the geographical detector diagnosis tool that is robust enough to determine the fundamental climatic circumstances pertinent to vegetation’s growth and life. Next, they developed a multilayer multivariate Long-Short Term Memory (LSTM) to predict NDVI change using as input climatic data obtained in the first phase. They concluded that the three-layered LSTM provides better prediction results when compared to the other LSTM prediction models – with one, two and four layers – by capturing the differences in the data. Besides, it helps in learning the patterns and relationships within the climatic data. Consequently, the three-layered LSTM technique is efficient in tracking the development of YRB’s ecology in as far as climate change and human land use are concerned. Therefore, to optimize and select the best LSTM, hyperparameters computational skills and expertise are required.

In line with the recent advancements in remote sensing technologies, being the paramount provider of high temporal and spatial resolution vegetation indices, coupled with the stochastic models including that of Holt-Winters (HW), Box-Jenkins (BJ) and Box–Jenkins–Tiao (BJT), the choice of model coefficients and parameters has become more flexible and systematic1217,. Such developments will perforce improve drought assessment altogether with trend and seasonality detection of vegetation across various biomes. Gowri et al.18 developed a vegetation and climatic drought assessment system that predicts future cases of drought based on the temporal as well as the spatial scale. Such a study was applied on different time series data sets like MODIS sensors 13Q1, 11A1, and CHIRPS out of which the vegetation and climatological indicators are extracted. Drawing upon such indicators, the Vegetation and Temperature Condition Indices represent the intensity and frequency of droughts.

But while the Vegetation Health Index, which is based on temperature and humidity, gives insights into crop yield and conditions, the Soil Moisture Index describes the interrelation of Surface Temperature with NDVI. In this regard, with an eye on providing an effective predictor for vegetation drought conditions trend, they employed the HW model to sort out the correlation between the derived indices as a preparation for the training of the Convolutional 2D-LSTM model.

Mutti et al.19 introduced a prediction model for vegetation trend based on remote sensed vegetation index NDVI and different stochastic methods including HW, BJ and BJT at six desertification hotspots in the Brazilian semi-arid area. They modelled the mean and variance metrics of NDVI time series data through HW, BJ and BJT techniques so as to estimate their pertinence to the prediction of vegetation dynamics across this particular region. The comparison in all hotspots shows that BJ and BJT models performed slightly better than HW in simulating dry and degraded vegetation states during wet periods. This technique is an effective short-term NDVI prediction for seasonal vegetation changes in arid regions. Nonetheless, it is inefficient for long term vegetation status prediction capacity. As well, it is inadequate for regions characterized by high rainfall irregularity and intense climatic transformations.

Cui et al.11 presented a vegetation forecasting technique based on SF-CNN, which incorporates both a combination of image specificities learned from CNN and statistic attributes derived from historical data. In consequence, the derived statistical features are used with raw data as a CNN input to provide the latter with additional significant details and features and hence improve the accuracy and stability of the model. In reality, this model expresses the trend and changes in the recurrent data for the study period of four regions with various complex conditions. While retaining the same parameters in the training phase, the SF-CNN, compared to CNN, effectively improves the reliability and robustness in NDVI short-term predictions with notable generalization ability. However, it is challenging to incorporate statistical features with extracted image traits, which in turn demands effective preprocessing, high computational capabilities and advanced parameter tuning techniques.

Gao et al.12 developed an NDVI prediction model, called TSD-CNN-LSTM, that relies on both time series decomposition for long-term correlation and average cycles of the trend in time series data using X-12-ARIMA technique. Six forecasting models were designed to assess the effectiveness and the generalization of the model in climatic factors-based NDVI forecasting. This research has revealed that TP-N (Temperature & Precipitation-NDVI) model has higher performing assets than the other two models T-N (Temperature & NDVI) and P-N (Precipitation & NDVI) models. This explains the influence of the temperature-precipitation parameter on NDVI. Although CNN and LSTM are widely used to explore nonlinear relationships among climate change factors and vegetation growth index, the intricate integrative model that joins CNN and LSTM demands important computational resources and expertise.

Khodadadi et al.20 proposed a deep-learning NDVI forecasting model, WWPASFS-BiGRU, which integrates a Bidirectional GRU with attention mechanisms and a hybrid optimization combining the waterwheel plant algorithm and statistical fractal search within the case study in the region of Saudi Arabia.

Sun et al.13 developed a bidirectional long short-term memory BiLSTM which studied the rapport between meteorological factors and vegetation activities by simulating NDVI for each vegetation type existing in the studied region. The area under study presents various climate trends and diverse topographies. China’s northern hemisphere consists of different vegetation species where the weather can vary considerably. Thus, atmospheric pressure, precipitation, incoming shortwave radiation, maximum air temperature, minimum air temperature are used as the model input. Indeed, this method examined the prediction of vegetation drought at random time scale for six regional vegetation types. In this, it established the fact that only meteorological parameters can potentially determine vegetations stress.

Models like the bidirectional GRU, BiLSTM, and the LSTM model, which are highly accurate in analyzing temporal series but call for high computational powers, are extremely sensitive the setting of parameters and are not suited to model vegetation processes at an aggregated spatiotemporal resolution. The implementation of straightforward regression-based models can result in reduced robustness under severe environmental conditions. While BiLSTM models have been widely applied for NDVI prediction, hybrid CNN–BiLSTM architectures have been developed to further enhance predictive performance. By combining CNN-based spatial feature extraction with BiLSTM temporal modeling, these hybrid models capture more effectively spatiotemporal variations in vegetation indices, leading to the improvement of their prediction accuracy.

For instance, Wang et al.14 developed a hybrid CNN–BiLSTM model to predict vegetation indices (NDVI, EVI, and kNDVI) in the Yangtze River Basin using climate variables and analyse the corresponding spatiotemporal variation trends. Such a hybrid architecture demonstrated strong effectiveness in capturing vegetation index dynamics across both space and time. Yet, in such a CNN–BiLSTM architecture, convolutional layers are initially utilized to obtain spatial features, which are then followed by BiLSTM layers to extract temporal dependencies. Employing CNN layers at the beginning part of the network would diminish the dimensionality by performing convolution alongside pooling operations on the input. This may result in the loss of some spatial properties, restricting as such BiLSTM layers’s representational capacities, while providing a simplified feature set at the risk of excluding spatial cues vital to modelling temporal dynamics accurately. and potentially discarding spatial details that are crucial.

Notably, this represents a structural limitation of the CNN–BiLSTM architecture, where early spatial feature extraction can inadvertently reduce the effectiveness of subsequent temporal modelling. In response to these limitations, we develop a BiLSTM-CNN temporal-spatial predictive model to better capture the complex relationships between temporal dynamics and spatial heterogeneity, extracted from multisource remote sensing data including climate and soil factors. In the proposed BiLSTM–CNN, the BiLSTM layers first capture temporal dependencies from the full input sequence, providing enriched features for the subsequent CNN layers to extract spatial patterns effectively. This ordering preserves temporal information and avoids the loss of important features caused by early dimensionality reduction in CNN-BiLSTM.

Furthermore, our BiLSTM-CNN model improves modelling of complex spatiotemporal dynamics of NDVI by extracting key temporal and spatial features efficiently: firstly, we apply BiLSTM to mitigate vanishing and exploding gradient problems while simultaneously capturing bidirectional dependencies in sequential data by way of combining the outputs of forward and backward LSTM networks. Secondly, the CNN and pooling layers are employed to reduce the number of parameters and extract fine-grained spatial features such as subtle differences in vegetation structure, which are important for accurate modeling of NDVI variability across heterogeneous landscapes.

Methodology

In this section, we present in detail our proposed BiLSTM-CNN prediction model which is a blend of Bidirectional long short-term memory (BiLSTM), in itself an improved version of LSTM network, with a convolutional neural network (CNN). Such a model incorporates temporal and then spatial convolution operations that address a valuable insight feature in multisource remote sensing datasets. It consists of climate factors and soil moisture time series data.

Modelling of NDVI time series based BiLSTM-CNN model

The proposed NDVI forecasting model BiLSTM-CNN amalgamates BiLSTM´s temporal, sequential strengths and CNN´s strong spatial feature extraction capabilities. This considered mixture allows for capturing the temporal-spatial complex, nonlinear relationship in climate activity and soil moisture time series data. The BiLSTM-CNN model comprises an Input module, BiLSTM module, CNN module, Feature Fusion module and Output module as shown in Fig. 1. In this respect, to simulate the complex relationship between NDVI and environmental factors over a long-term period, our model relies primarily on a BiLSTM module composed of three stacked BiLSTM layers to recover time series of dynamic patterns on historical input data.

The BiLSTM_CNN Model.

This architecture’s depth allows the model to capture both short- and long-range temporal dependencies information from input sequence data, balance model complexity along with learning capacity while mitigating model overfitting through regularization techniques such as dropout layers. This design leverages the temporal patterns at multiple frequencies present in NDVI, meteorological and soil moisture time series data which provides better NDVI simulation benefits from this number of layers that influences the simulation accuracy.

Through bidirectional running, the back-and-forth LSTM networks help to examine the most pertinent features across time of the input sequence data. The first BiLSTM layer computes all the bidirectional hidden states along the time axis, extracting features in both the forward and backward directions of the sequence data and then concatenates them to constitute the final bidirectional hidden state that will be transferred to the dropout layer. In point of fact, the dropout layer as theorized by Srivastava et al.21 is an efficient technique commonly used to reduce parameters and overfitting by removing neurons units from the neural network with a fixed temporary probability.

Next, the Batch Normalization layer is applied to normalize received features from the previous layer. Not only does Batch Normalization prevent the internal covariate shift, it also thwarts the vanishing and exploding gradient issues in sequential data. The second BiLSTM layer coupled with the third one, while reiterating the same steps, process the normalized features obtained so far along opposite time directions to compute the components hidden states .

The LSTM networks is the key element in BiLSTM-CNN model. It comprises cell states and distinctive gate units: forget, input and output gate indicated by four blue boxes from left to right in the forward LSTM cell as presented in Fig. 2. These internal dynamics can control flux information among different gates employing sigmoid function. In the context of forward LSTM at tth day in sequence n, the forget gate learns to forget certain features prior hidden state for the present cell state Eq. (1). Besides, the input gate and the current memory acquire useful features in the present input for the current cell state as defined in Eqs. (2) and (3). The output gate learns to refine the new hidden state guided by the current cell state that are expressed by Eqs. (4), (5) and (6). Mountains that make part of the study area involve the Pyrenees Mountain range to the north, Cantabrian mountains in Northwest, Iberian System in Central-East, Baetic System in the Southeast part, and Catalan Coastal Ranges in Northeast as shown in Fig. 4 (Generated using QGIS version 3.44). While winters are cold, the rain season comes mainly in spring and autumn. Data sources and preprocessing

MODIS

Mindful of our research goals, we selected the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) datasets series from January 2015 to December 2023. It is collected from Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) onboard the Terra satellite from the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) with the 16-day composite land surface reflectance product MOD13A2 L3 Global with 1 km. 1 km spatial resolution. It covers the study area and provides quality and condition information from the layer VI-Quality (QA) to ensure reliable data. The MODIS NDVI product is more consistent and appropriate for identifying the long-term vegetation dynamics over a large spatial–temporal resolution. Since MODIS has been in orbit from March 2000 to the present time and publicly accessible. Each pixel accorded the best value every 16-day NDVI corresponding to influences including cloud cover, snow, shadow and solar azimuth.

The Data is derived from the differences between the surface reflectance data in red and near-infrared fields of the electromagnetic spectrum of the level 2 MOD09 product according to Eq. (11). The regular NDVI values ranges from − 1 to 1 in vegetated zones with negative index value which signifies clouds, water and snow. In contrast, positive value around 0 characterizes rocks and bare soil components, while larger positive NDVI value varying from sparse vegetation (0.1–0.5) to dense green vegetation (0.6 and over) express expanding of vegetation.

The MOD13A2 data was downloaded in GeoTIFF format drawn from study-area GeoJSON file. The NDVI Raster tiles from MOD13A2 were mosaicked and clipped to the region of interest using open-source Python libraries including rasterio and geopandas.

Such a dataset was re-projected to the WGS-84 geodetic datum and the EPSG:4326 coordinate reference system (CRS) was used for geographic coordinates in the research. The processing of data integrated the vegetation index (VI) quality information warrants that only high-quality observation is utilized. We employed spatial and temporal interpolation to fill the missing data in NDVI time series along with the Savitzky Golay Filter (S-G) to smooth the image. The min-max normalization technique is used to scale the NDVI values to range [0,1] using Eq. (12). Then, we apply linear interpolation to obtain daily NDVI data for the entire study period.

Due to the challenges posed by the processing of such a great data, the study area accounts for 204.426.369-pixel values for all time steps at 1 km. 1 km NDVI datum. To investigate the key climate-driven factors influencing vegetation growth, pixels were further classified into seven vegetation types (e.g., evergreen forests, deciduous forests, and shrublands) based on the Plant Functional Types scheme derived from the MODIS Land Cover Type Product (MCD12Q1; 500 m spatial resolution). This dataset provides annual global land cover information. Land cover classes for the period 2015–2023 were defined using the International Geosphere–Biosphere Programme (IGBP) classification system. Pixels with a 9-year mean NDVI below 0.1 were excluded from the analysis to minimize the influence of bare soil and sparsely vegetated areas. To ensure consistency between model inputs and outputs, the pre-processed NDVI and land cover datasets were reprojected and resampled to match the spatial resolution, projection, and grid structure of the meteorological data. The precipitation, temperature, humidity and soil moisture data were spatially masked according to the six predefined vegetation types and subsequently used as inputs to the BiLSTM-CNN model.

Precipitation data

The daily precipitation data used in this study comes from MERRA-2 tavg1_2d_flx_Nx (Modern-Era Retrospective analysis for Research and Applications version 2, 2 d,1-Hourly, Time-Averaged, Single-Level, Assimilation, Surface Flux Diagnostics V5.12.4). It provides hourly total precipitation data around 60 m high above the ground and the spatial resolution is . The precipitation data set is originally in units of kilograms per square meter per second (kg m⁻² s⁻¹). But for the objectives of this research, the unit is converted to millimetres per day (mm/day) to facilitate the interpretation in climatological units utilizing the equation defined in Eq. (13).

To ensure consistent spatial resolution, the precipitation data set is resampled into grids with size of with the same resolution as NDVI. In the meantime, it is projected to Coordinate system WGS-84, clipped according to the particularities of the study area, while other pre-processing steps were performed using the image-based on Python script. The sum of the hourly precipitation data values is employed on purpose of obtaining the daily spatial and temporal aggregation.

Temperature and humidity data set

The hourly air temperature and humidity data set are provided by M2T1NXSLV. It is an hourly time-averaged 2-dimensional Single-Level assimilation and diagnostics. It is the latest version of global atmospheric reanalysis for the satellite era produced by NASA Global Modeling and Assimilation Office (GMAO). They utilize the Goddard Earth Observing System Model (GEOS) version 5.12.4. It consists of meteorological diagnostics at standard used vertical levels. It includes air temperature and humidity which is restored to 2 m above the ground. The data set covers the study period from January 2015 to December 2023. The raw data is resampled using bilinear interpolation with a spatial resolution of 1 km. The Coordinates References System (CRS) is projected to WGS-84 and the data is cropped to fit into the research area contours. To address the data time alignment problem, the hourly data is used to estimate the mean daily temperature and humidity for the duration of the study.

Soil moisture data

The soil moisture time series data set is derived from M2T1NXLND. It is an hourly time-averaged 2-dimensional data collection in MERRA-2. Such a collection involves land surface diagnostics, including surface-soil-wetness, root zone soil wetness, surface layer and root zone layer water with a data of spatial resolution that is equal to . The Goddard Earth Observing System Model (GEOS) version 5.12.4, covering the measurement period from January 2015 to December 2023, is used here. To guarantee spatial alignment and computational efficiency, the preprocessing of soil moisture data goes through cropping, reprojecting the CRS of data to WGS-84, and resampling the data to 1 km using bilinear technique. To guarantee temporal alignment with other datasets, the mean of hourly data is geared to extract the daily soil moisture data of mainland Spain.

Experimental protocol

This section provides a detailed account of the experimental protocol followed to gauge the efficiency of our hybrid BiLSTM-CNN forecasting model, while ascertaining that the obtained results are neither biased nor attributable to chance. To showcase the benefits of combining various dynamic, enhanced, temporal, and spatial modelling for monitoring and predicting dynamic vegetation, we have compared the proposed BiLSTM-CNN model to different advanced learning models namely, CNN-GRU22, CNN-LSTM12, BiLSTM13 and CNN-BiLSTM23 models. To further investigate, separately, the vegetation dynamics for each vegetation type, the proposed model BiLSTM-CNN is applied to predict the NDVI time series for each vegetation type based on meteorological data. In subsection 4.3.1, we detail the hyperparameters configuration for training the BiLSTM-CNN model and in Subsection 4.3.2 we present the evaluation metrics used for model performance assessment.

Hyperparameters settings

Our BiLSTM-CNN architecture is made up of two key elements. The BiLSTM module – the first block – is structured around three hidden layers, each containing 200 units, dropout layers with a ratio 0.3 and batch-normalization layers are applied after each BiLSTM layer. The CNN module – the second block – incorporates two convolutional layers (1D CNN) with different filter dimensions 128 and 64, and kernel sizes of 5 and 3 respectively. Following the convolutional layers, a 1D max-pooling layer with a pool size of 2 is applied to down-sample the feature maps and retain the essential spatial patterns over multiple scales. This model closes off with a fully connected (Dense) layer to form a one-dimensional vector and is followed by a non-linear soft sign activation layer. Analysing NDVI fluctuations over the complete seasonal vegetation pattern provides valuable insights into seasonal growth patterns, while capturing both phenological dynamics and vegetation responses to environmental conditions. Predicting NDVI at the full vegetation-dynamics sequence is therefore essential, as it enables the assessment of complete growth cycles, identification of peak productivity periods, and evaluation of vegetation stress or anomalies caused by climate and soil moisture variations.

Accordingly, our objective is to forecast NDVI time series over a full phenological cycle, capturing the influence of climatic and soil factors on vegetation growth and activity by modelling the relationship between meteorological and soil moisture drivers and vegetation index NDVI, which allows for a comprehensive understanding the major vegetation activity. For this reason, we have trained the model on 80% of the data set, envisaging to trace the correlations between the climate, soil moisture data and NDVI. To ensure that the achieved results are not biased or coincidental, we performed paired t-tests on the , , , metrics24 by pairing up the BiLSTM-CNN model with each baseline model individually. The paired t-test assesses whether the differences in metric values between two models are statistically significant. To support this analysis, each model was executed over five runs using different five 20% data test split, maintaining identical evaluation conditions. This setup ensures that performance metrics are paired across runs, thereby justifying the use of a paired t-test for statistical comparison. Each model was independently executed five times, yielding repeated measurements of R², RMSE, MAE, and MAPE. The mean of R², MAE, MAPE, and RMSE over 5 runs were computed for each model to summarize overall performance and variability. Then, for each pair of models, differences in performance metrics were calculated across corresponding runs, forming paired observations, from which the mean difference between paired samples was computed. Then, a t-test is applied to assess whether the observed differences are statistically significant. Vegetation activity is driven by dynamic environmental conditions exhibiting both spatial and temporal variability, while annual NDVI fluctuations show marked differences across vegetation types25. Accordingly, the BiLSTM-CNN model is applied separately to different vegetation types to predict NDVI time series. For each vegetation type, the objective is to model the relationship between meteorological variables and vegetation activity so as to enable accurate NDVI prediction. Indeed, 500 training samples were randomly selected for each vegetation type, resulting in a total of 1,500 samples used for model training. Two evaluation strategies were employed to assess BiLSTM-CNN model performance. First, 20 test samples per vegetation type were randomly selected from the remaining pixels excluding the training data, forming a balanced test dataset. Second, all remaining pixels across mainland Spain were used for large-scale model evaluation.

As we can see, all the confidence intervals are higher than 95%, which shows that BiLSTM-CNN indeed provides statistically significant accuracy improvements. This strengthens our claim that the proposed architecture leverages a sequential processing strategy, where bidirectional temporal modeling is first used to capture complex temporal dependencies within the data. The output of this stage is then utilized as input to the CNN, which performs hierarchical local feature extraction. By learning from temporally enriched representations, the CNN can better identify local patterns and nonlinear relationships at multiple levels of abstraction. This synergy between temporal modeling and hierarchical spatial feature learning results in improved prediction performance, reduced error metrics, and increased model stability compared to other architectures.

We observe that the performance of CNN-GRU is lower than LSTM-based models, as it primarily focuses on extracting short-term patterns from meteorological time series data. This can limit its ability to capture complex long-term dependencies that are essential for modeling vegetation growth cycles. Consequently, the model may not fully learn long-range temporal relationships, including cumulative climatic influences and delayed vegetation responses to environmental conditions, which are critical for accurate NDVI prediction. The CNN-LSTM and CNN-BiLSTM models assume that temporal dependencies can be learned from locally extracted CNN features. However, this may lead to the loss of fine-grained temporal information and distortion of sequential dependencies. Since meteorological and NDVI data are non-stationary, CNN overemphasizes short-term patterns while it fails to capture long-term trends and regime shifts by discarding subtle or long-range temporal information that is crucial for time-series prediction. Accordingly, LSTM/BiLSTM operates on CNN-transformed feature representations rather than the original time-series data, which does not fully preserve accurate temporal dependencies and can potentially affect NDVI prediction accuracy. In comparison, the BiLSTM-CNN model first captures long-range bidirectional temporal dependencies using BiLSTM and then applies CNN to extract higher-level patterns from enriched temporal representations, leading to better preservation of sequence information and improved prediction performance.

Compared with the CNN–LSTM model, the proposed BiLSTM–CNN architecture demonstrates superior performance in capturing nonlinear relationships between climate, soil moisture, and NDVI data. The CNN–LSTM model achieves R² = 0.97, RMSE = 0.0284, MAE = 0.018, and MAPE = 10.29, whereas the BiLSTM–CNN model attains improved accuracy with R² = 0.99, RMSE = 0.008, MAE = 0.020, and MAPE = 3.944. In contrast, CNN–LSTM operates on post-convolutional representations, where feature abstraction may smooth temporal signals and obscure fine-grained variations, limiting its ability to capture raw time-series dynamics.

In contrast, CNN–GRU and standalone BiLSTM models exhibit lower performance due to limitations in capturing long-term dependencies and spatial variability. The CNN–GRU model achieves R² = 0.86, RMSE = 0.0819, MAE = 0.066, and MAPE = 59.21, reflecting its focus on short-term sequence modeling and reduced capability for long-term memory retention. Overall, the BiLSTM–CNN architecture demonstrates superior capability in modeling NDVI time series by jointly learning long-term temporal dependencies and spatial features, making it well-suited for large-scale vegetation monitoring across diverse environmental conditions. Conclusion

In this paper we have proposed an efficient BiLSTM-CNN model to predict temporal-spatial dynamics of NDVI and its response to meteorological and soil factors in Mainland Spain. The latter is characterized by various vegetation conditions that are highly influenced by climate change and soil moisture variability. This model has been developed to investigate the main patterns of NDVI variation and predict vegetation activity dynamics incorporating multisource datasets for instance satellite data, climate data, soil data. Composed of two main phases – the advantage of long-term time series memory of the BiLSTM and powerful local spatial features expression ability of CNN layers – our BiLSTM-CNN model outperformed CNN-GRU, CNN-LSTM, BiLSTM, CNN-BiLSTM models achieving the highest NDVI prediction accuracy with an of 0.99, an of 0.008, of 0.020 and of 3.944. Subsequently, the BiLSTM-CNN model has displayed a strong capability to monitor and reproduce the behaviour of vegetation dynamics that are closely linked to atmospheric conditions and soil factor.

More importantly, our study demonstrates the capability of using climate and soil moisture data to simulate NDVI time series through deep learning models. This highlights that BiLSTM-CNN architecture can be applied for each vegetation type separately to predict NDVI time series by establishing the relationship between meteorological and soil variables and vegetation activities, enabling broader applications in NDVI prediction and vegetation monitoring across diverse vegetation types. The BiLSTM-CNN model can accurately reproduce vegetation dynamics and stress responses under varying climatic and soil-moisture conditions. However, its performance may still be influenced by the study region’s geographic attributes—including elevation, slope, aspect, land cover, and the complex climatic variability—that shape vegetation dynamics and NDVI patterns.

In future studies, the model could be applied to regions with geological climatic and soil characteristics different from those of mainland Spain to evaluate its broader applicability. The combination of the two deep learning models involves a large number of hyperparameters that potentially will increase theirs tuning complexity. For this reason, in future work we aim to investigate the effectiveness of transformers for NDVI prediction based on complex dependencies with climate and soil factors, capturing long-range dependencies efficiently, through the attenuation mechanism for multivariate and missing times series data.
