MicroRNA-mediated autophagy and drug resistance in cancer: mechanisms and therapeutic strategies
Authors: Jinxing Wei, Xianghui Wang, Duo Yu, Yanyang Tu, Yaoyu Yu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This paper provides an exhaustive overview of the intricate interplay between microRNAs (miRNAs) and autophagy in the context of human cancers, underscoring the pivotal role these non-coding RNAs play in modulating autophagic pathways and their implications for cancer development, progression, and resistance to therapy. MiRNAs, as critical regulators of gene expression post-transcription, influence various biological processes, including autophagy, a catabolic mechanism essential for cellular homeostasis, stress response, and survival. The review meticulously delineates the mechanisms through which miRNAs impact autophagy by targeting specific genes and signaling pathways, thereby affecting cancer cell proliferation, metastasis, and response to chemotherapy. It highlights several miRNAs with dual roles, acting either as oncogenes or tumor suppressors based on the cellular context and the specific autophagic pathways they regulate. The paper further explores the therapeutic potential of targeting miRNA-autophagy axis, offering insights into novel strategies for cancer treatment through modulation of this axis. Emphasizing the complexity of the miRNA-autophagy relationship, the review calls for more in-depth studies to unravel the nuanced regulatory networks between miRNAs and autophagy in cancer, which could pave the way for the development of innovative therapeutic interventions and diagnostic tools.

Introduction

The family of single-stranded, non-coding RNA molecules known as microRNAs (miRNAs) is one that has been conserved throughout the course of evolution. 17–25 nucleotides are the length of these short RNAs. Through the process of post-transcriptional gene silencing, they exert control over biological processes [1]. The fact that microRNAs are present in a diverse array of living creatures, ranging from plants to mammals, provides evidence that the regulation of gene expression by miRNAs is an old process [2]. More than 60% of human genes have potential miRNA-binding sites, according to computational projections; hence, it’s conceivable that all of these genes are susceptible to control by these short RNAs [3]. MiRNA genes and gene clusters may be identified in the genome in both intergenic and intronic areas [4, 5]. All of these areas are situated at the same time. When microRNAs are clustered together, they may have common transcriptional regulatory groups. Thus, miRNAs can be expressed as polycistronic transcripts, which opens the door to the possibility of a coordinated expression pattern for functionally related miRNAs [6]. Typically, the expression of the host protein-coding gene is what determines the quantities of intronic microRNAs that are present in the cell. Furthermore, there are isolated microRNA genes, which are characterized by the presence of their own promoters and the ability to express themselves independently (18). Long primary microRNAs, also known as pri-miRNAs, are RNA transcripts that range in length from sixty to seventy nucleotides and are typically transcribed from miRNA genes in a way that is reliant on RNA polymerase II (pol II) [7]. The transcription of certain forms of microRNA, on the other hand, could be dependent on RNA polymerase III (pol III) (20). In the same way as protein-coding mRNAs do, primary miRNA transcripts have a 5′ cap and a 3′ poly-A tail. A process known as splicing can also occur with miRNA. It is possible for RNA pol II to create several functional miRNAs from a single pri-miRNA transcript [7]. This is because RNA pol II is responsible for the transcription of pri-miRNAs. In order to process intermediary RNA oligonucleotides and form mature and functional microRNAs, a series of RNAse-dependent processes must be carried out in a sequential manner once transcription has been completed. In the nucleus, they are subsequently processed by a core ribonuclease complex that consists of Drosha and its regulatory component DGCR8. This complex is responsible for the generation of hairpin-structured premature-miRNAs (pre-miRNAs) that are between 60 and 70 nucleotides in length. It is possible for exportin-5 to detect them after they have been cleaved, and then transfer them from the nucleus to the cytoplasm. In the cytoplasm, the DICER protein undergoes further cleavage of the hairpin structure of pre-miRNAs, resulting in the creation of miRNA duplexes that are around 21–22 nucleotides in length. Afterwards, these duplexes were loaded onto the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC). The RISC complex cannot function without the Argonaute (AGO) proteins. These proteins dock with specific mRNAs and guide mature single-stranded miRNAs to their targets. The fate of the mRNA affected the level of complementarity between the mature miRNA seed sequences (about 8 nucleotides in the miRNA’s core region) and the “miRNA response elements (MRE)” on the target mRNA sequences. After base-pairing with the guide miRNA, the slicer-dependent approach cleaves the target mRNA through an endonuclease-dependent cleavage. The target mRNA may be de-cappingand/or de-adenylated by miRNAs, which could speed up the breakdown process, while translation machinery could be interrupted by partial complementarity [4, 8]. In the recent years, the changes in the levels of miRNAs have been associated with the development of various human diseases in which miRNAs and cancer progression demonstrate significant correlation [9–13]. miRNAs in tumorigenesis

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) can be classified into families, with members differing in nucleotide sequences outside the seed region [14]. Certain chromosomal locations contain two or more copies of these miRNA families, which are unique to those regions [15]. miRNAs exhibit diverse expression patterns, ranging from spatial–temporal localization to ubiquitous distribution [16, 17]. There is an increasing body of evidence supporting the significant role of miRNAs in both physiological and pathological processes, including cell proliferation and differentiation, apoptosis, and metabolism. Furthermore, miRNAs can influence the tumor microenvironment, as well as immunity, hypoxia, and inflammation associated with tumors [18–21]. Each miRNA is believed to target multiple mRNAs, and alterations in miRNA expression are linked to the onset of various pathological disorders, particularly cancer [22–27]. Notably, miRNAs have emerged as valuable diagnostic and prognostic markers in cancer due to their association with aberrant expression patterns [28–30]. Moreover, it has been established that miRNAs can contribute to the resistance of cancer cells to chemotherapy, an area that has garnered considerable interest [31–33]. Various types of autophagy

Autophagy can be classified into three distinct categories: macroautophagy, microautophagy, and chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) [34]. Macroautophagy is a well-conserved and widely prevalent form of autophagy that involves the sequestration of part of a cellular organelle to form an autophagosome [35]. The process of macroautophagy, which requires the sequestration of a part of a cellular organelle in order to create an autophagosome [36], is a kind of autophagy that has been well preserved over the course of evolution and is rather widespread. After this, the lysosome will merge with the autophagosome to create an autolysosome. Within the autolysosome, the macromolecules that are to be destroyed, such as proteins and organelles, will be digested [37]. Within this context, selective macroautophagy is characterized by the recognition and selection of specific defective cellular organelles or substrates for degradation [38]. Mitophagy, which contains mitochondria, lysophagy, which contains lysosomes, pexophagy, which contains peroxisomes, ribophagy, which contains ribosomes, reticulophagy, which contains endoplasmic reticulum, and nucleophagy, which contains nuclear components are all examples of selective macroautophagy [38]. In the process of microautophagy, the lysosomes engulf several tiny vesicles by a process known as lysosomal cytoplasmic invagination [35]. Microautophagy is a kind of autophagy. The precise mechanism of microautophagy in lysosomes is still not fully understood [39], but for endosomal microautophagy, it has been reported that a number of proteins, such as endosomal sorting complexes required for transport I and III (ESCRT/III) and heat shock cognate 71 kDa protein (HSC70), play crucial roles in initiating the electrostatic interaction between the protein substrates and endosomes, which is necessary for autophagy to take place [35]. In contrast, CMA is an extremely selective process whereby a chaperone protein like HSC70 recognizes only the cytoplasmic components that have been tagged with a certain C-terminal KFERQ motif. By binding the motif to the surface-located lysosome-associated membrane protein type 2a (LAMP-A2) protein, this chaperone protein will subsequently guide the components to the lysosome [40]. The protein GFAP and the protein HSP90 are responsible for the stabilization of LAMP-2A [39]. To facilitate the trafficking of substrates into the lysosome for degradation, a multimeric complex involving the monomeric protein LAMP-2A is essential. This dynamic process assembles monomers to effectively translocate substrates. Once the translocation is completed, the multimeric complex disassembles and is subsequently recycled [35]. Mammalian autophagy

In cells that are undergoing autophagy, the development of a double membrane vesicle, which is referred to as an autophagosome, and its fusion with lysosomes are the most important processes that take place [41–43]. Autophagy is a process that facilitates the transport of cargo across membranes. Genes associated with autophagy play a crucial role in regulating this process. Various stress conditions, including nutrient deprivation, protein aggregation, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and hypoxia, can trigger the initiation of autophagy [44, 45]. Initiation signals are frequently incorporated into the mTOR complex [46, 47]. These signals are detected by conserved metabolic sensors. In response to signals that induce autophagy, mTOR is rendered inactive, leading to the formation of the ULK1 complex. The ULK1 complex is composed of ULK1, ATG13, RB1 inducible coiled-coil 1 (RB1CC1, also known as FIP200), and ATG101 [48, 49]. In addition, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) can sense the levels of AMP and ATP within the cell. This enzyme also plays a role in the formation of the ULK1 complex [50–52]. Following the activation of the ULK1 complex, the autophagic cargo is engulfed by a double membrane, leading to the formation of a cup-shaped structure known as a phagophore. Once mature, the phagophore evolves into an autophagosome. In contrast to selective autophagy, which involves the targeted delivery of specific cargoes to initiation sites via autophagic receptors, bulk autophagy encompasses the transport of small sections of cytoplasm as the cargo [53, 54]. An example of this process is the ability of calcium-binding and coiled-coil domain 2 (CALCOCO2, also known as NDP52), optineurin (OPTN), and sequestosome 1 (SQSTM1, also known as p62) to capture and transport defective mitochondria [55, 56]. In most cases, these autophagic receptors are able to bind membrane-bound LC3 through their LC3-interacting motifs (LIR), which is the mechanism by which cargos are transported to start sites. The components of the PI3K complex, which include PI3K, Beclin 1 (BECN1), ATG14 L, autophagy and Beclin 1 regulator 1 (AMBRA1), and vacuolar protein sorting-associated protein 35 (VPS35), are phosphorylated by the ULK1 complex once it has been activated [57]. An autophagy-specific PI3P pool is produced by an activated PI3K complex at a particular area of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) that is referred to as the omegasome [58]. PI3P molecules localized in omegasomes play a crucial role in recruiting effector proteins such as WIPI2 and DFCP1 by binding to their PI3P-binding domains. Additionally, WIPI2 interacts with ATG16L1, facilitating the recruitment of the ATG12-ATG5-ATG16L1 complex to the initiation site [59, 60]. Additionally, the ATG12-ATG5-ATG16L1 complex is responsible for the enhancement of the LC3 conjugation with membrane-resident phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), which ultimately leads to the transformation of LC3 from form I to form II [61]. It is the typical characteristic of autophagy that this transformation takes place [62]. Furthermore, gamma-aminobutyric acid receptor-associated proteins (GABARAPs), members of the ATG18 family, can be conjugated to phosphatidylethanolamine (PE). The localization of LC3 to initiation sites is a crucial step during selective autophagy, as it allows autophagic receptors to bind to membrane-bound LC3 through their LC3-interacting region (LIR). This interaction facilitates the proper delivery of selected cargo to autophagosomes [63]. The growth of phagophores can be attributed to a number of different intracellular membrane sources, including the plasma membrane, recycling endosomes, the Golgi complex, and mitochondria [64, 65]. Some of these membrane donation processes are mediated by vesicles that contain ATG9 [66, 67], but the method by which other membranes are delivered is yet unclear. The final stage before the formation of the autophagosome, which is directed by the ATG8 family of proteins [68–71], is the sealing of the phagophore. After some time, the adult autophagosome will eventually merge with the lysosome and the autolysosome. Acidic hydrolases in the lysosome are responsible for the degradation of inner cargos, which results in the release of nutrients into the cytoplasm for implementation in other intracellular processes [72–74].

Autophagy in cancer metastasis and drug resistance

The functions that autophagy plays in the processes of cancer metastasis and chemoresistance have been validated by research that has recently come to light [75]. Cancer cells may be able to successfully metastasize through the process of tumor-promoting autophagy [76], and they may also be able to survive chemotherapy through the process of protective autophagy [77–79]. The activation of autophagy is responsible for the promotion of chemoresistance and implies poor survival in the majority of malignancies. The cytotoxic chemicals that are used in chemotherapy cause stress, which in turn activates autophagy as a cellular adaptive response [80, 81]. Recently, it has been proven that DNA damage may cause the process of autophagy to occur and can also cause associated genes to be upregulated, which ultimately leads to chemoresistance [82]. As a result, the activation of autophagy establishes a distinct molecular mechanism for chemoresistance in cancer cells, mediated by specific long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs). For instance, GBCDRlnc1 has been implicated in gallbladder cancer [83], while MALAT1 is involved in gastric cancer [84]. Under certain conditions, the suppression of autophagy can restore chemotherapeutic sensitivity. For example, reducing autophagy through the knockdown of Atg7 has been shown to enhance chemosensitivity and prolong overall survival in animal models of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [80]. It is important for pancreatic cancer stem cells to undergo autophagy, which is regulated by OPN/NF-κB signaling. On the other hand, pharmacological suppression of autophagy has been shown to boost drug sensitivity [85]. Based on these data, it appears that there is a connection between autophagy and cancer stem cells, which are the fundamental reason for chemoresistance and illness retreatment. Furthermore, the inhibition of autophagy has the potential to improve the response to chemotherapy in triple-negative breast tumors (TNBCs) [81]. The inhibition of autophagy by miR-541 in HCC cells resulted in an increase in the cells’ susceptibility to sorafenib [86]. Additionally, N6-isopentenyladenosine increased chemotherapy sensitivity in melanoma by inhibiting autophagy [87]. This was accomplished by treating the cancer. The process of colonization at the new location, migration into the pre-metastatic niche, and invasion into the circulation are all experiences that cancer cells go through [88]. A vast body of data has demonstrated that autophagy plays a significant role in the process of cancer metastasis. This is because autophagy regulates a number of essential processes in the process, including tumor invasion, migration, extracellular matrix (EMT), the interaction between stromal cells and tumor cells, and immune surveillance [76, 89–91]. The precise role of autophagy in cancer metastasis remains a contentious issue, with most research suggesting that it promotes tumor growth, while a few studies indicate that it may also suppress tumor growth [92]. It has been demonstrated that an elevated level of LC3B is related with invasion and metastasis in solid tumors [93], whereas a high level of ATG5 is necessary for the development of metastasis in animal models of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [94]. Through the inhibition of autophagy, the ubiquitin-conjugating E2 enzyme variation known as Ube2v1 can play a role in the facilitation of metastasis in colorectal cancer [95]. It is important to note that therapy with rapamycin and trehalose has the potential to reverse Ube2v1-mediated metastasis in mice models. Through its direct binding to ATG13 and its enhancement of its expression, circMUC16-mediated autophagy is responsible for the promotion of metastasis in ovarian cancer. In addition to this, cirMUC16 has the potential to act as a ceRNA for miR-199a-5p, therefore alleviating the inhibitory effect that it has on Beclin-1 and RUNX1 [96]. On the other hand, the expression of autophagy-related genes in breast cancer has been shown to have a negative correlation with pre-metastasis signatures, which in turn limits the spread of tumors [97, 98].

Autophagy modulation by miRNAs: a general perspective of molecular interactions

A high number of miRNAs have been reported to regulate autophagy in human cancers [99]. There was an initial study by Zhu et al. [100] that miR-30a has the ability to adversely affect autophagic activity. According to the results of a dual luciferase reporter assay, miR-30a can bind to the 3′-untranslated region (UTR) of Beclin-1, leading to a reduction in Beclin-1 production. Additionally, the transfection of miR-30a mimics has been shown to suppress rapamycin-induced autophagy in T98G cells. Various other microRNAs have also been implicated in regulating specific autophagy-related proteins (ATGs) and their regulators at multiple stages of autophagy, including induction, vesicle nucleation, elongation, retrieval, and fusion. The activation of the ULK complex, comprising ULK1/2, ATG13, FIP200, and ATG101, represents the initial step in the process of autophagy induction [101, 102]. An essential component in the initial stages of the autophagic process is the ULK1 protein kinase. When nutrients are abundant, mTOR phosphorylates both ULK1 and mammalian ATG13 (mATG13), thereby limiting the activity of ULK1 kinase. Conversely, during starvation conditions, the inactivation of mTOR allows ULK1 to phosphorylate itself, mATG13, and FIP200. This cascade of events leads to the recruitment of other autophagy-related complexes, such as the class III phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3KCIII), thereby initiating autophagy. In melanoma cells, the miR-290–295 cluster has been shown to reduce autophagic cell death induced by glucose deprivation [103]. This is achieved by inhibiting the expression of ULK1 and ATG1. The downregulation of miR-20a and miR-106b expression in the absence of leucine is attributed to the inhibition of the transcription factor c-Myc. Both miR-20a and miR-106b mimics can effectively suppress autophagy induced by leucine deprivation in C2C12 myoblasts through transfection. Furthermore, mechanistic analyses reveal that miR-20a and miR-106b specifically target ULK1, leading to its inhibition [104]. Chemosensitization, cell cycle arrest, and autophagy were induced in multi-drug-resistant MCF7 cells by isoliquiritigenin, a chalcone-type flavonoid derived from licorice. Mechanistic investigations revealed that miR-25 is the primary target of isoliquiritigenin. Moreover, the suppression of miR-25 resulted in autophagic cell death by directly increasing the expression of ULK1 [105]. A direct target of miR-885-3p, ULK2 is another upstream autophagy initiator, according to current studies [106]. This suggests that miR-885-3p may potentially play a role in the control of autophagy.

Second, for vesicle nucleation to become active, the class III PI3K/Beclin-1 complex must be activated. This complex is associated with numerous binding partners, including Rubicon, UVRAG, ATG14L, hVPS34, and Bax-interacting factor-1 (BIF-1), among others. Evidence suggests that reducing Beclin-1 synthesis inhibits vesicle nucleation; specifically, miR-30a/b, miR-376b, miR-216a, and miR-17-5p have been shown to achieve this effect [107–110]. The researchers Huang et al. [111] discovered that miR-519a is also capable of targeting Beclin-1. Furthermore, they came to the conclusion that miR-630 and miR-374a have the ability to suppress UVRAG, which interacts with Beclin-1 and ultimately results in the activation of autophagy. It has been determined that the target of microRNA-195 is ATG14, which is an essential component of the class III PI3K/Beclin-1 complex that is responsible for the nucleation of the autophagosomal membrane according to [112]. A small GTPase known as RAB5A can stimulate the production of autophagosomes through its interaction with hVPS34 and Beclin-1. Notably, miRNA-101 can suppress autophagy by targeting RAB5A, indicating that miR-101 influences autophagy at the vesicle nucleation stage [113, 114]. Furthermore, two ubiquitin-like conjugation systems—the ATG12-ATG5-ATG16L complex and the ATG8-phosphatidylethanolamine conjugation—are essential for vesicle elongation. During this stage, various components, including microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3), ATG3, ATG4, ATG5, ATG7, ATG10, ATG12, and ATG16L, are involved. Recent studies have indicated that miR-101 and miR-376b can suppress the expression of the ATG4C and ATG4D genes [108, 113]. The seed sequence and consistency targets of miR-376a were identical to those of miR-376b, including ATG4C and Beclin-1. Through its targeting of ATG7, miR-375 was able to block the conversion of LC3-I to LC3-II in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells [115]. In glioblastoma cell lines, miR-17 has the ability to inhibit the production of ATG7 [116]. RAB5A has also been shown to have a role in the conjugation of ATG5 and ATG12 [113]. Through its targeting of RAB5A, microRNA-101 has the potential to influence both the nucleation and elongation stages of the vesicle process. There is evidence that microRNA-204 has the ability to modulate autophagy in renal clear cell carcinoma (RCC) by regulating LC3B [117]. ATG16L is the target of miR-106B, miR-93, and miR142-3p, which all have the ability to control autophagy [118, 119]. By focusing on the ATG5-ATG12 conjugation, miR-30a/c, miR-130a, miR-519a, miR-181a, miR-374a, miR-885-3p, and miR-630 may all hinder autophagy [111, 120, 121].

In conclusion, ATG2, ATG9, UVRAG, and ATG18 are the individuals responsible for regulating the process of retrieval and fusing. In this relatively late stage of autophagy, a large number of microRNAs are involved. It was discovered that miR-130a has a direct target in the form of ATG2B [122]. MiR-34 inhibits autophagy in mammalian cells by lowering the amount of ATG9 that is expressed [123]. Jegga et al. [124] investigated the post-transcriptional control of ATGs through the use of transcriptional and miRNA-mediated mechanisms. They discovered that miR-130, 98, 124, 204, and 142 are participating in the regulation of genes that are involved in the autophagy-lysosomal pathway. UVRAG is a molecule that plays a significant role in the fusion process. In light of this, microRNAs that target UVRAG, such as miR-630 and miR-374a, could play a role in the control of the process of autophagosome–lysosome fusion [111]. There are more miRNAs that are involved in the control of autophagy which are in addition to the miRNAs and their targets that were detailed before. It has been determined that the protein known as apoptosis repressor with caspase recruit domain (ARC) exhibits anti-autophagy properties. ARC knockout mice have been shown to have higher levels of autophagic activity, whereas ARC transgenic mice have reported lower levels of autophagy activity. It has been demonstrated that miR-325 has the ability to inhibit the translational activity of ARC, which suggests that ARC is a target of miR-325 [125]. The inhibition of ARC by miR-325 has the potential to induce an increase in autophagic activity in the mouse model. pertaining to immunity by controlling autophagy, the GTPase family M gene, also known as IRGM, has the ability to modulate the innate immune response. According to the findings of a bioinformatics study [126], the IRGM has the potential to be a target of miR-196. There is a correlation between the control of IRGM by miR-196 and the effectiveness of autophagy in patients with Crohn’s disease [127]. Histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and histone deacetylases (HDACs) are two enzymes that are essential to the process of acetylation of proteins. Both miR-206 and miR-9 have the potential to play a role in the regulation of HDAC and HAT expression in Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia (WM) cells, which might ultimately lead to autophagy-dependent cellular toxicity [128]. BCL-2 has the ability to bind to Beclin-1 and suppress autophagy that is dependent on Beclin-1. Therefore, miR-182, miR-34a, miR-210, miR-205, and miR-21 [129–133] possibly control autophagy through the BCL-2/Beclin-1-PI3KIII pathway. This is because they target BCL-2. When it comes to autophagy, the protein of p62, which is also referred to as sequestosome 1 (SQSTM1), is not only a scaffold in autophagosomes but also one of the selective substrates available. The miR-17/20/93/106 sequences all contain the same AAGUGC’seed’ region and have the ability to directly control p62 expression [134], which suggests that they may play a role in the regulation of autophagy. miR-155, which is generated by hypoxia, has the ability to boost autophagy by targeting numerous genes involved in the mTOR signaling pathway [135]. These genes include RHEB, RICTOR, and RPS6KB2. Through its targeting of mTOR and IGF-1R, miR-100 has the ability to cause autophagy to occur in hepatocellular carcinoma cells [136].

The miRNAs, are a class of short non-coding RNAs that range in length from 18 to 22 nucleotides and act as an epigenetic regulator by targeting messenger RNAs (mRNAs) during post-transcriptional processing [137]. There is a growing body of data that suggests that epigenetic regulation of certain microRNA genes has a direct role in the process of carcinogenesis and is associated with negative outcomes of cancer treatment [138, 139]. In the process of regulating autophagy, a significant number of microRNAs have been taken into consideration. ULK2 was directly controlled by miR-885-3p during the early stage of autophagy induction in squamous cell carcinoma cells that were exposed to cisplatin [106]. Zhu et al. found that Beclin 1 was a possible target for miR-30a, and that this miRNA had the ability to negatively control the expression of Beclin 1, which led to a reduction in autophagic activity [100]. Under hypoxic circumstances, miR-375 was able to limit autophagy by inhibiting the conversion of LC3I to LC3II as well as autophagic flux [115]. miR-376b was able to inhibit autophagy that was associated with hunger by targeting two important autophagy proteins, namely Atg4C and Beclin 1 [108]. It has been demonstrated that microRNA-101 is a powerful inhibitor of basal, etoposide- and rapamycin-induced autophagy, which has the potential to make breast cancer cells more susceptible to the cell death caused by 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT) [113]. The suppression of LC3B-mediated autophagy by miR-204 was found to be effective in inhibiting the development of tumors in renal clear cell carcinoma [117]. There are a number of additional microRNAs that have been linked to the control of autophagy, including miR-130a [122], miR-34 [123], and a number of others. An increase in the proliferation of gastric epithelium was seen during chronic Helicobacter pylori infection [140], which was caused by the methylation of miR-210. The suppression of microRNA-373 through epigenetic mechanisms led to an increase in cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [141]. The expression of lysine (K)-specific demethylase 6B was increased in hepatocellular carcinoma cells as a result of methylation-mediated suppression of miR-941 [142] epigenetically inactivated and functioning as a tumor suppressor in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [143], miR-615-5p was involved in the development of the disease. In human pancreatic cancer PANC-1 cells, the presence of miR-1291 was related with an increase in the amount of N-methylnicotinamide [144]. This resulted in a decreased potential for migration and invasion. Long noncoding RNA ANRIL knockdown was found to strongly inhibit the proliferation of gastric cancer cells by epigenetically silencing miR-99a/miR-449a, which suggests that ANRIL may be able to interact with miRNAs at the epigenetic level [145]. Among the factors that led to the development of acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) leukemogenesis was the epigenetic inactivation of mir-34a and -34b/c [146]. On the other hand, the connection between autophagy and microRNAs that are controlled by epigenetics has not been particularly well understood. There are a few microRNAs that have been identified to influence autophagy through an epigenetic mechanism. These microRNAs are either oncogenic or tumor suppressive. The well-known epigenetic regulator YY1 was responsible for the promotion of SQSTM1 expression and the consequent activation of autophagy [147]. This was accomplished by epigenetically inhibiting the expression of MiR372 [128]. Research has shown that the induction of autophagy is significantly influenced by the modification of histone acetylation that is reliant on miRNA-9. Based on these findings, it appears that epigenetic modification is a common mechanism via which microRNAs operate as drivers of tumor-suppression or tumor-promotion in a wide variety of cancer types [148]. It will be necessary in the future to get a deeper understanding of the relationship that exists between autophagy and microRNAs that are controlled by epigenetics.

Tumor-suppressor and tumor-promoting miRNAs in autophagy regulation

Tumor-promoting miRNAs

Recently, it was discovered that miRNA-106a, which is a member of a family of miRNAs that are highly carcinogenic, is overexpressed [149]. Furthermore, it has been reported to directly downregulate the levels of ULK1 mRNA in NB4 acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cells, which suggests that ULK1 is a possible tumor suppressor [150]. Similarly, while inducing autophagy, miRNA-106a may potentially target other ULK1 complex components such mAtg13 and FAK family-interacting protein of 200 kDa (FIP200). The ULK1 complex becomes unstable when the autophagic negative regulator mTORC1 phosphorylates mAtg13, disrupting their binding [151]. This occurs when putative oncomiRNAs are focusing on the transcriptional levels of tumor suppressors. Not only that, miRNA-221/222, which could function as an oncogene, inhibits the cell cycle inhibitor p27Kip1, which is a downstream regulator of PI3KCI/Akt. In HER2/neu-positive primary human breast cancer, this leads to cell death by autophagy cell line MCF-7 [152]. Therefore, miRNA-376b attenuates rapamycin-induced autophagy, which in turn directly downregulates the translational levels of Beclin-1 and Atg4C in Hun-7 and MCF-7 cells [108]. This is an oncogenic role that miRNA-376b performs. It is important to highlight that Beclin-1 promotes autophagy by cooperating with PI3KCIII in the nucleation of vesicles. This process is controlled by a number of positive mediators, such as the UV irradiation resistance-associated gene (UVRAG) and Bax-interacting factor-1 (Bif-1) [153]. It is possible that the levels of Atg4C might serve as an important autophagic biomarker in the process of vesicle elongation and completion [154]. This is due to the fact that Atg4C is involved in the conversion of LC3-I to LC3-II. In addition to miRNA-182 and miRNA-96, miRNA-183 is frequently overexpressed, positioning itself as a powerful oncogene in several types of cancer. This overexpression allows miRNA-183 to actively participate in cancer progression by regulating autophagy-related pathways. Specifically, it has been shown that blocking miRNA-183 expression directly influences autophagic processes. In medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), inhibition of miRNA-183 leads to a marked increase in LC3B1 (LC3-I) expression, a key protein involved in autophagosome formation, triggering the death of autophagic cells. This discovery underscores miRNA-183’s potential as a therapeutic target. By modulating its expression, it may be possible to restore the autophagic machinery that promotes cancer cell survival, effectively turning this process into a mechanism for inducing cancer cell death. This ability to shift the balance between autophagy and apoptosis offers a promising avenue for therapeutic intervention, particularly in aggressive cancers like MTC where conventional treatments often fall short. Targeting miRNA-183 could unlock new strategies in cancer therapy by disrupting the cellular processes that sustain tumor growth [155]. Furthermore, research found that miRNA-31 and miRNA-34c have the ability to activate cytoprotective autophagy (mitophagy) in the presence of oxidative stress. Consequently, in a tumor stroma model, they have a positive regulatory role in cancer growth and spread [156]. Tumor-suppressive effects, or they can promote tumor growth by inhibiting autophagy. These mechanisms reveal the dual role of the autophagy pathway in cancer, making it a complex and critical therapeutic target. Future research could further explore the interactions between these miRNAs and other autophagy-related proteins, particularly in the context of different cancer types and therapeutic settings, to understand how autophagy regulation affects tumor progression. Moreover, miRNA-targeted therapeutic strategies based on these findings hold promise for providing new approaches to cancer treatment.

Tumor-suppressor miRNAs

Zhu et al. (2009) states that miRNA-30a is the first microRNA associated with autophagy to be reported to have a tumor suppressing impact. Autophagic activity in T98G cells treated with predatory drugs is decreased because this microRNA negatively regulates the production of Beclin-1. Also, many malignancies, such as breast, liver, and prostate tumors, lose miRNA-101, which is another example of a tumor suppressive microRNA. It is possible that miR-101 expression in MCF-7 cells could be enhanced by mTORC1 signaling. The level of mTORC1 inactivation can be indicated by the expression of miRNA-101 because mTORC1 activation status affects autophagy and the activity of its direct target phospho-S6-kinase (pS6K) [113, 154]. Additionally, miRNA-183 exerts its oncogenic influence by suppressing autophagy through the targeted regulation of key autophagy-related genes, including Atg4D, STMN1, and RAB5A. Similar to the role of Atg4C in converting LC3-I to LC3-II, Atg4D is critically involved in this conversion process, facilitating the elongation and completion of the autophagosome. By targeting and downregulating Atg4D, miRNA-183 interferes with this essential step of autophagy, ultimately diminishing the cell’s ability to engage in autophagic processes that might otherwise lead to cancer cell death. Moreover, STMN1 (Stathmin 1) and RAB5A are crucial mediators in autophagy that perform specific regulatory functions. STMN1 is involved in microtubule dynamics, which are essential for the formation and trafficking of autophagosomes, while RAB5A, a member of the RAB GTPase family, plays a vital role in vesicle trafficking, including autophagosome maturation. By directly targeting these autophagy regulators, miRNA-183 not only suppresses the proper functioning of the autophagic machinery but also creates an environment that favors tumor growth and survival, as cancer cells often exploit impaired autophagy to evade cell death and continue proliferating. The contrasting effects of these miRNAs underscore the complexity of autophagy regulation in cancer, revealing opportunities for therapeutic intervention by either enhancing or suppressing autophagy depending on the specific miRNA involved and the cancer type. Targeting miRNAs like miRNA-183 that inhibit autophagy could restore the cell’s natural death mechanisms, providing a novel approach to treating cancers where autophagy suppression contributes to tumor progression [128]. This is accomplished by the downregulation of histone deacetylase 4 (HDAC4) and HDAC5, as well as the overexpression of acetyl-histone-H3 and -H4. However, the microRNAs that were previously listed as being either oncogenic or tumor suppressive have the potential to influence either cytoprotective autophagy or autophagic cell death. These two types of autophagy may play different functions in biological situations that are comparable. How is it possible for autophagy to perform the function of Janus in cancer cells? The question is, how exactly do microRNAs participate in the control of autophagy as either oncogenes or tumor suppressors? Two different ideas have been proposed in order to provide answers to these problems. One theory proposes that the Janus role of autophagy could be contingent on the many phases of cancer, such as stem cell, initiation and development, invasion and metastasis, and dormancy. The autophagy has the ability to inhibit the creation of tumors during the first stages of cancer, but it may also promote the invasion and metastasis of cancer once the disease has already developed [151, 154]. The alternative theory proposes that tumor development can result from an increase in oncogenic microRNAs and a decrease in miRNA-targeted tumor suppressors. Similarly, oncogenes that are miRNA-targeted might be overexpressed while tumor suppressive microRNAs are underexpressed [157, 158]. The ULK1 complex, the Beclin-1 interactome, and the Atg4 signaling pathway are among the tumor suppressive signaling pathways that oncogenic microRNAs may influence during autophagy. However, other oncogenic signaling pathways, such the mTORC1 signaling pathway, can be impacted by tumor suppressive miRNAs. Notably, Beclin-1 expression can be inhibited by both the oncogenic miRNA-376b and the tumor suppressive miRNA-30a. In a similar vein, two oncogenic miRNAs—miRNA-376b and miRNA-101—are able to block Atg4 in human cancer. Thus, the possibility that Beclin-1 and Atg4 are oncosuppressors with distinct cellular or tissue-specific roles could account for this apparent contradiction [159–161]. This idea has been proposed by a number of researchers.

miRNA/autophagy axis regulation by natural products and nanoparticles in cancer therapy

Small molecules, like urea, and complex compounds, like taxol, are examples of natural products [162]. Natural products are often found in nature. Several natural compounds have been the subject of recent research that has uncovered their chemical structures and biological activities. Natural products have been the subject of several research [163–165] that have studied their potential to protect against cancer. Resveratrol is a polyphenol that is mostly found in the skin of berries such as blueberries, raspberries, grapes, and mulberries. It is thought to have antioxidative properties. According to the findings of Tili and colleagues, the administration of resveratrol to colon cancer cells has the potential to inhibit the production of a number of oncogenic microRNAs, including miR-21 [166]. Fatty acids with omega-3 fatty acids have been shown to have a preventive impact against inflammatory disorders like cancer. In their study, Davidson and colleagues discovered that fish oil, which is a large source of omega-3 fatty acids, had the ability to inhibit the down-regulation of microRNAs such as miR-15b, miR-107, let-7d, miR-191, and miR-324-5p in the colon of rats that were modeled with colon cancer. This resulted in a significant reduction in the development of colon tumors [167]. One of the most common sources of the phytochemical curcumin is turmeric. Anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-cancer capabilities are all things that curcumin possesses. By treating human colon cancer cells with curcumin, Mudduluru et al. discovered that the activity of miR-21 was decreased in a dose-dependent way. This resulted in the cell cycle being arrested in the G2/M phase, as well as a reduction in tumor development and cell proliferation [168]. Among the phenolic compounds that may be found in licorice is isoliquiritigenin. A new function of isoliquiritigenin was revealed by Wang et al., which was that it acts as a natural inhibitor of autophagy-related miR-25, which is responsible for killing drug-resistant cancer cells. The researchers were able to demonstrate that isoliquiritigenin has the ability to produce cell cycle arrest, chemosensitization, and autophagy in cancer cells, but it did not trigger apoptosis. During the process of boosting autophagy flux, they discovered that miR-25 is the primary target of isoliquiritigenin. The process of autophagy was brought about by the inhibition of miR-25, which was accomplished by directly boosting the expression of an early regulator during the death autophagy induction phase (ULK1). It was also demonstrated that the overexpression of miR-25 inhibited the autophagy and chemosensitization that was produced by isoliquiritigenin [105].

In the recent years, the application of nanoparticles for purpose of cancer therapy has been increased and the increasing evidences have shown that nanoparticles can provide targeted delivery of drugs and genes in cancer therapy [169–173]. For the purpose of encapsulating a wide variety of medications and ensuring their efficient distribution, nanocarriers have been created. In this way, they have the potential to enhance the effectiveness of therapies. A wide variety of nanocarriers have been developed with the purpose of delivering a variety of substances, including medicines and DNA. PLGA particles demonstrated high levels of biodegradability and biocompatibility, both of which have the potential to be beneficial in the delivery of miRNA [174]. PLGA/PEI nanoparticles were utilized by Liang et al. for the purpose of delivering microRNA to HePG2 cells, which represented human hepatocellular carcinoma cells. PLGA- nanoparticle delivery of miR-26a was observed to boost the inhibitory impact of miRNA in HePG2 cells, according to the findings of the researchers [175]. The delivery of miRNA-203 into esophageal cancer cells was accomplished by Cao et al. by the utilization of protamine sulphate–nanodiamond nanoparticles. This delivery strategy was shown to have the potential to enhance the effect of miRNA-203 on esophageal cancer cells, as revealed by the researchers [176]. A surface-functionalizing strategy was utilized by Zheng et al. to generate poly(d,l-lactide-co-glycolide)/poly(l-lactide)-block-poly(ethylene glycol)-folate polymer nanoparticles (PLGA/PLA-PEG-FA) for the purpose of delivering miR-204-5p for the treatment of colon cancer. They observed that cancer cells were able to take up FA-NPs-miR-204 in an effective manner, and they examined the promotional impact of miR-204 on cell death as well as the inhibitory effect of miR-204 on cell proliferation [177]. Nanoparticles as delivery vehicles for microRNAs for the purpose of modulating autophagy have the potential to be an effective therapeutic method for the treatment of gastrointestinal cancer. In addition, research has been concentrated on the promotion of autophagy by the internalization of nanomaterials, which implies a unique method in the treatment of cancer [178–180].

miRNA/autophagy axis as therapeutic target

When cancer cells are subjected to metabolic or therapeutic challenges, new research has shown that autophagy that is driven by microRNAs may either promote the death of the cancer cells or promote their survival [181]. When it comes to cancer treatment, targeting autophagy might therefore provide a fresh and potentially effective therapeutic technique. As a result of the transfection of MiR-101, celastrol-induced autophagy is inhibited, which in turn leads to an increase in the cytotoxicity of celastrol in prostate cancer cells [182]. Additionally, it has been demonstrated that miR-101 is an effective inhibitor of autophagy that is activated by basal, etoposide, and rapamycin. The suppression of autophagy by microRNA-101 can also make breast cancer cells more susceptible to the cell death caused by 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT), according to research published [113]. Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) has been identified as a direct target of microRNA-212 in prostate cancer. The loss of microRNA-212 has been shown to efficiently increase autophagic flux and improve cell survival. Accordingly, MiR-212 has the potential to evolve into a therapeutic potential treatment for individuals suffering from prostate cancer [183]. While ectopic expression levels of MiR-200c prevent irradiation-induced autophagy and boost the therapeutic efficacy of radiotherapy in breast cancer, overexpression of MiR-200c lowers the amount of autophagy that is caused by irradiation [184]. Restoring LC3-II development is possible with miR-22 targeting either high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) in osteosarcoma or B-cell translocation gene 1 (BTG1) in colon cancer. Furthermore, in the cancer cell lines under study, overexpression of MiR-22 suppresses autophagy and promotes cell death. These results suggest that microRNA-22 may prove to be a useful therapeutic target for cancer patients and a predictor of chemotherapy susceptibility [185, 186]. Through the regulation of autophagy, microRNA-214 has the ability to enhance the sensitivity of estrogen receptor-positive (ER (+)) breast cancer cells to anticancer medicines. This suggests that MiR-214 may play a role as a new therapeutic method for overcoming endocrine resistance in ER (+) breast malignancies [187]. It is possible that miR-375 inhibits autophagy by lowering the expression of ATG7, which in turn reduces the survival of HCC cells when they are exposed to hypoxic circumstances. Due to the fact that MiR-375 has a direct inhibitory impact on autophagy, it has the potential to serve as a therapeutic target for the treatment of cancer [188]. In renal cell carcinoma, liver cancer, and cervical cancer, the overexpression of MiR-30a suppresses the creation of autophagosomes that are controlled by Beclin 1 or ATG5, and it also raises the proportion of tumor cells that undergo apoptosis as a result of therapy with anticancer drugs. Therefore, MiR-30a has the potential to be a biomarker that can boost the sensitivity of chemotherapy by inhibiting the autophagy that is dependent on Beclin 1 in cancer cells [189, 190]. The ability to upregulate anticancer drug-induced autophagy and relieve anticancer drug-induced cell proliferation in osteosarcoma cells was found by Wei et al. (2016) to be associated with increased expression of MiR-140-5p. These results indicate that MiR-140-5p may elicit autophagy as a mechanism by which osteosarcoma cells resist drugs. In addition to the autophagy that promotes survival, microRNAs have the ability to govern autophagic cell death as well. Multiple studies have shown that cancer cells cannot survive without autophagy, which is dependent on the phosphotase and tensin homologue deleted on chromosome 10 (PTEN) gene. The radioresistance of cervical cancer and the sorafenib resistance of head and neck cancer can both be increased by miR-21. Furthermore, because MiR-21 is responsible for reduced autophagy, it has been demonstrated that decreased autophagy is one of the probable pathways that contribute to enhanced radioresistance or drug-resistance. MiR-21 has the potential to be a therapeutic target in gene therapy interventions for the treatment of cervical cancer and head and neck cancer [191–193]. A genetically modified telomerase-specific oncolytic adenovirus has the ability to trigger autophagic cell death in human cancer cells by way of an E2F1-MiR-7-EGFR pathway. This finding provides a potential molecular mechanism for an anticancer virotherapy [194]. MiR-100 has the capacity to decrease the expression of MTOR or IGF1R, which presents a potential therapeutic target for HCC [136]. This might lead to the promotion of autophagy that is dependent on ATG7 and consequent apoptotic cell death in HCC cells.

miRNA/autophagy axis in cancer drug resistance

The process of autophagy is closely regulated by ATGs; hence, targeting ATGs is a viable technique for reversing drug resistance because of the extent to which they govern the process [195]. A great number of ATGs have been identified as direct targets of miRNAs. For instance, the 3’-untranslated region (UTR) of ATG5 mRNA can be bound by miR-137 [196], miR-181a [197], miR-216b [198], miR-30a [199], and miR-153-3p [200]. Many malignancies become more responsive to chemotherapeutic drugs as a result of this, since it aids in the breakdown of ATG5 mRNA. Among these malignancies include NSCLC, pancreatic, gastric, melanoma, chronic myelogenous leukemia, and others. Bcl-2 is directly targeted by miR-153-3p in the fight against imatinib resistance in chronic myeloid leukemia [201]. However, ATG5 is also a direct target of miR-153-3p. In a similar manner, miR-375 targets ATG7 [202] and ATG14 [203] in order to mediate the increased chemosensitivity of HCC to sorafenib. According to the research that were discussed before, a single microRNA has the ability to target several genes, and similarly, a single gene can be controlled by many microRNAs. One of the components of the PI3K complex that is responsible for mediating vesicle-trafficking events in autophagy is called Beclin-1, which is also referred to as ATG6 [204]. Through the inhibition of autophagy, it appears that targeting Beclin-1 with miR-409-3p [205], miR-17 [206], miR-216b [198], miR-17-5p [110], and miR-199a-5p [207] can cure chemoresistance in several forms of cancer. Research conducted in recent years has shed light on the interaction that occurs between the miR-30 family and Beclin-1. MiR-30 or its homologue miR-30a, miR-30a-5p has been shown to bind to the mRNA of Beclin-1 in order to inhibit autophagy-induced chemoresistance in a number of malignancies, including chronic myeloid leukemia [199, 208], gastric cancer [209], osteosarcoma [210], small cell lung cancer (SCLC) [211], and other types of cancer [190]. This conclusion was also verified by a clinical investigation that was conducted on Egyptian patients who were diagnosed with chronic myeloid leukemia [212]. It has been shown that a single microRNA appears to be responsible for modulating chemoresistance and chemosensitivity in a manner that is shared by several forms of cancer. miRNAs also target additional ATGs directly, such as miR-541 targeting ATG 2A [86], miR-24-3p targeting ATG4A [213], miR-1 targeting ATG3 [214], miR-23b-3p and miR-200b targeting ATG12 [215, 216], miR-874 targeting ATG16L1 [217], and miR-34a targeting ATG4B [218]. They are all examples of miRNAs that target ATGs. As a consequence, the overexpression of these microRNAs may lead to the sensitivity of chemotherapeutic medicines in the treatment of cancer. Not only that, but microRNAs are also capable of indirectly regulating ATGs. MiR-29c-3p, for example, inhibits autophagy by targeting FOXP1 in order to downregulate ATG14, which in turn causes ovarian cancer cells to become more sensitive to the treatment with cisplatin [219].

HMGB1, also known as high mobility group box 1, is a nuclear protein that binds to DNA and is extremely conserved. It is responsible for regulating a variety of functions connected to DNA, including replication, transcription, and repair [220]. A large number of studies have demonstrated that HMGB1 is involved in a number of characteristics that are characteristic of malignancies. As a result, HMGB1 is a viable target for the treatment of tumor growth, invasion, metastasis, and resistance to chemotherapy [221]. An important regulator of autophagy, HMGB1 is responsible for the development of drug resistance in a variety of malignancies, such as osteosarcoma [222], lung adenocarcinoma [223], and leukemia [224]. This is achieved by triggering the protective process of autophagy following the administration of medication. A small number of researchers have sought to establish a link between miRNAs and HMGB1. To make osteosarcoma, endometrial carcinoma, melanoma, retinoblastoma, breast cancer, and non-small cell lung cancer more sensitive to chemotherapeutic treatments, they found that miR-22, miR-218, miR-26a, miR-34a, miR-129-5p, and miR-142-3p targeted HMGB1 [185, 225–229]. HMGN5 is a further member of the HMG box family that has a role in the growth of tumors and the development of cancer. Meng and his colleagues carried out a number of tests in order to gain a better understanding of the chemoresistance that is connected with HMGN5. Through their research, they discovered that HMGN5-mediated autophagy plays a role in the development of chemoresistance in osteosarcoma. It has been demonstrated that miR-140-5p may sensitize osteosarcoma cells to chemotherapy by targeting HMGN5, which suggests that miR-140-5p may have a potential utility in the diagnosis and treatment of malignancies that are resistant to chemotherapy [230].

There are more key autophagic components, regulators, or signaling pathways that are connected with the processes of chemosensitivity that are mediated by miRNA. An example of this would be the RAB family, which is the biggest subfamily in the Ras family and contains more than sixty small GTPases. RABs are involved in a variety of different membrane trafficking processes, including the creation of autophagosomes [231]. According to the findings of Xu and colleagues, a high expression of miR-541 enhances the response of HCC to sorafenib therapy by targeting RAB1B [86]. In addition, miR-148a-3p suppresses RAB12, which in turn reduces cytoprotective autophagy, which in turn increases the cytotoxicity of cisplatin in gastric cancer [232]. Because of its central role in autophagy activation, ULK1 is an attractive target for cancer treatment. Newly identified miR-26a/b and miR-106a binding sites were located in the 3′-untranslated region (UTR) of ULK1. By increasing doxorubicin (Dox) sensitivity, autophagy suppression, and apoptosis in vitro and in vivo are all outcomes of miR-26a/b overexpression in HCC [233]. Similar to the previous example, ectopic expression of miR-106a led to a considerable increase in the amount of tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI)-induced cell death in lung adenocarcinoma cells when compared to control transduced cells [234]. Furthermore, the overexpression of miR-489 decreases the activity of ULK1, which in turn suppresses autophagy and makes breast cancer cells more sensitive to DOX [235]. FOXO3a is a transcription factor that has several functions and may either directly or indirectly direct autophagy [236]. According to a study conducted by Zhou and colleagues, FOXO3a is a direct downstream target of miR-223. The research demonstrated that the overexpression of miR-223 or agomiR-223 is linked to an increased susceptibility of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) to doxorubicin [237]. Additionally, Wingless-type MMTV integration site family member 2 (WNT2) is a member of the WNT family, which is a family that has been preserved throughout the course of evolution [238]. It has been well documented that the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway has a significant role in the development of tumors and the progression of tumors, particularly in the areas of cancer invasion and migration. However, there is a lack of knowledge regarding the involvement of WNT and autophagy [239]. Imatinib therapy was shown to be more effective in treating chronic myeloid leukemia [240] as a consequence of the findings of Chen et al., who discovered that overexpression of miR-199a/b-5p suppresses WNT2 and downstream signaling to alter autophagy development. In general, the activation of autophagy following chemotherapy provides cancer cells with a survival advantage. However, by overexpressing specific tumor-suppressor microRNAs, a beneficial strategy can be employed to inhibit autophagy, thereby enhancing chemosensitivity through various molecular pathways.

miRNA/autophagy axis in cancer diagnosis

An increasing body of research highlights microRNAs as promising biomarkers associated with tumors. Beyond their involvement in early-stage cancers, these biomarkers also play a role in tumor sensitivity, recurrence, and advanced-stage tumors. As a result, microRNAs hold potential as indicators for disease diagnosis, prognosis, and even treatment options for certain cancers [241, 242]. Understanding the role of miRNAs in regulating autophagy is crucial for cancer diagnosis. The function of autophagy can either promote survival or cell death, depending on factors such as the type of cancer and the intensity of the stress signals involved. For instance, microRNA-372 has the potential to directly target sequestosome 1 (SQSTM1), which can inhibit autophagy activation and subsequently affect the growth of breast cancer [147]. This could provide a valuable histological marker for diagnosing breast cancer. Additionally, microRNA-23b-3p has the potential to predict and treat drug resistance due to its ability to suppress autophagy by regulating ATG12 and HMGB2, as well as enhancing the sensitivity of multidrug-resistant cells to chemotherapeutics in gastric cancer [243]. One of the roles of microRNA-regulated autophagy is to promote survival, whereas another is to promote death. A study that just came to a close discovered that antiproliferative autophagy directly targets MiR-34a. In prostate cancer cells, miR-34a expression is significantly downregulated, and its transfection induces autophagy, which in turn prevents tumor cell proliferation. Because of these effects, miR-34a shows clinical promise as a biomarker for diagnosis or treatment [244].

Conclusion and perspectives

This review provides evidence that microRNAs are intricately linked to the primary autophagy machinery. While many studies have demonstrated that microRNAs (miRNAs) control autophagy pathway proteins, far fewer have investigated how miRNAs affect autophagic flux or attempted to identify the physiological context in which miRNAs may have a role in autophagy [245]. Consequently, further comprehensive functional studies are required to fully understand the role of microRNA regulators in autophagy activity control. There are important and intriguing parallels between the autophagy and miRNA systems, both of which help cells respond to stresses. Despite the obvious differences between a genome-wide regulatory network and a central catabolic route, this remains true. Curiously, several microRNAs with anti-autophagy effects have also been found to suppress tumor growth; these include miR-30a, miR-34a, and miR-101. It appears that certain tumor types are required to activate autophagy, and this may be related to the downregulation of certain miRs. Studies demonstrating the loss of Dicer have provided evidence that the miRNA regulatory network alone may play a crucial role in controlling autophagy [122]. Nevertheless, the scope of these results is limited at the moment. Furthermore, comprehensive genetic studies are needed, which should investigate autophagy activity following tissue-specific ablation of miRNA production pathway enzymes. Within this context, studying the functional relationships between microRNA regulation and autophagy’s involvement in neuronal degeneration would be quite interesting. It is worth mentioning that the microRNAs that have been demonstrated to influence autophagy proteins thus far mainly aim at the beginning of the pathway, when vesicles are being formed and stretched out. An evolutionary adaptation to prevent autophagosome accumulation and total lysosomal function blockage could explain this phenomenon. Nevertheless, it cannot be denied that this highlights the need for research to investigate how autolysosome function and autophagosome fusion control via miRNA. Multiple investigations point to microRNAs as a potential therapeutic target for inhibiting autophagy in cancer. To target the autophagy pathway at different phases, miRNA mimetics or inhibitors may be an advantage, even though there are several modest pharmacologic inhibitors of autophagy or lysosome function.

Over the course of the last several decades, there has been a growing interest in determining the regulatory function that microRNAs play in many aspects of cancer, such as resistance to chemotherapeutic medicines. There is significant evidence that certain microRNAs affect autophagy in a variety of malignancies, hence facilitating chemoresistance. This is due to the paradoxical function that autophagy plays in the process of defending against chemotherapy. As a result, gaining a knowledge of the crosstalk and interaction that occurs between microRNAs and autophagy in the context of chemoresistance may result in the development of a novel strategy to miRNA-based therapy, which will assist in overcoming a significant barrier to the development of effective therapeutics. Nevertheless, there is a need for more study that is both more in-depth and more extensive about the relationship between microRNAs, autophagy, and chemoresistance. Moreover, because of the regulation of mitophagy that occurs during chemotherapy and the function that it plays in the acquisition of resistance, it is vital to conduct additional mechanistic investigations in order to comprehend the role that miRNAs play as an additional participant in the development of resistance to chemotherapeutic treatments [246]. MicroRNAs can be classified into two categories: those that induce chemoresistance and those that inhibit it. This classification impacts various molecular pathways, including PTEN, PI3K/Akt, STAT3, and others. During cancer chemotherapy, microRNAs target genes involved in autophagy regulation, such as ATG and ULK1, among others. Additionally, further research conducted in databases like PubMed and Google Scholar has revealed that upstream biological pathways can modulate the miR/autophagy axis during chemotherapy. For this reason, we have dedicated substantial discussion to the influence of upstream mediators on the miR/autophagy relationship. Notably, long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) emerge as primary upstream mediators of the microRNA/autophagy axis, capable of either activating or inhibiting chemoresistance by influencing various microRNAs, which subsequently target the autophagy process. In contrast, the role of circular RNAs (circRNAs) as upstream mediators of the miR/autophagy axis has been explored in fewer studies. Consequently, significant work remains to identify circRNAs that could contribute to cancer treatment by influencing the miR/autophagy axis. In conclusion, we have found that beyond lncRNAs and circRNAs, additional upstream mediators can modulate the miR/autophagy axis, as discussed in Sect. 6.3. This group includes various molecular pathways, such as HIF-1α, c-Myc, and Nrf2. It is noteworthy that we have devoted a section to examining anti-tumor drugs as chemosensitizers through their effects on the miR/autophagy axis. These drugs can upregulate or downregulate microRNAs, subsequently affecting autophagy and enhancing chemotherapy effectiveness. Future research may further illuminate the benefits of regulating the microRNA/autophagy axis to improve chemotherapy outcomes in cancer patients [247].

The recycling of biological macromolecules and organelles is an example of autophagy, which is a system that evolved throughout time. MicroRNAs are involved in a wide variety of biological activities and are of critical importance in the control of autophagy in instances of cancer. There has been a continuous rise in the number of research that have been conducted on the role of autophagy and microRNAs in cancer [248]. However, further research is essential in the realm of autophagy and microRNAs, as the field is still in its nascent stages. This exploration should encompass the investigation of the seemingly contradictory roles of autophagy and miRNAs in cancer, as well as the development of new research methodologies and tools. The role of miRNA-mediated autophagy in cancer remains a topic of debate, with uncertainty regarding whether miRNA-regulated autophagy serves as a mechanism for cancer cell survival or death. Moreover, microRNAs appear to play both direct and indirect roles in cancer regulation. As a result, the multifaceted functions of microRNAs in cancer and the ambiguous role of autophagy further complicate the intricate regulatory mechanisms at play. MicroRNAs associated with autophagy present a potential alternative to LC3 and SQSTM1 as early markers of autophagy, given that miRNAs can induce changes in gene expression prior to detectable alterations in protein levels. In recent years, numerous high-resolution methods for miRNA detection have been developed. For instance, Guk et al. introduced a novel detection method based on miRNA hybridization and the self-circulation of molecular beacon circuits. Additionally, Li and colleagues developed a highly sensitive fluorescence polarization method for miRNA detection, boasting a detection limit of approximately 0.001 nM [249]. Furthermore, Jin et al. proposed a highly sensitive miRNA detection method utilizing Chlorella virus DNA ligase. This technique demonstrates a sensitivity that is at least forty times greater than current standard methods, enabling the detection of individual miRNAs that differ by as little as one nucleotide [250]. Additionally, studies examining the effects of individual microRNAs on autophagy genes often exhibit bias. Therefore, it is crucial to utilize large, multidisciplinary public databases to evaluate how microRNAs regulate all the genes and processes within the complex autophagy network. MicroRNAs can modulate cancer sensitivity to radiation and chemotherapy by influencing autophagy. However, it would be unwise to prioritize cell death over cell survival, particularly since autophagy is the mechanism that leads to treatment-induced cell resistance. Dysregulated microRNAs associated with autophagy may present potential therapeutic targets for anti-cancer strategies, as autophagy serves as a critical link between cell survival and death. Thus, significant attention must be given to the biosafety and reliability of miRNA-based therapeutic approaches. This includes determining effective methods for delivering miRNA mimics in vivo, clarifying the roles of miRNAs in specific autophagy steps, and understanding how off-target effects of miRNAs can inform treatment strategies. MicroRNAs related to autophagy show great promise for use in cancer diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis assessment. Consequently, one of the primary objectives for researchers should be to conduct a comprehensive investigation into the interplay between autophagy and miRNAs, as well as the applications associated with these topics.
