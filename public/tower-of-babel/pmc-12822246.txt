Prevalence and determinants of caesarean section delivery in Ethiopia: analysis of the 2019 EMDHS using Poisson regression with robust standard errors
Authors: Temesgen Gebeyehu Wondmeneh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies in Ethiopia have examined factors associated with caesarean section delivery, but all reported odd ratios from logistic regression. Prevalence ratios estimated using Poisson regression with robust standard errors is more directly interpretable for public health and policy audience than odd ratios. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence and associated factors of caesarean section delivery in Ethiopia using Poisson regression with robust standard errors.MethodsThis study analyzed secondary data from 5753 reproductive-age women using 2019 Ethiopian Mini Demographic and Health Survey. Poisson regression with robust standard errors was performed using Stata version 17. Statistical significance was declared at p-value < 0.05, and Adjusted prevalence ratios (APR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.ResultsThe prevalence of caesarean section delivery in Ethiopia was 6.1% (95% CI: 5.5–6.7). Women aged 35–49 years (APR = 1.79; 95% CI: 1.20–2.60, p = 0.004), those of Christian religion (APR = 1.70; 95% CI: 1.25–2.30, p = 0.001), and women with secondary (APR = 1.92; 95% CI: 1.40–2.70, p < 0.001) or higher education (APR = 2.20; 95% CI: 1.54–3.20, p < 0.001) had a significantly higher likelihood of caesarean delivery. Similarly, women with three ANC visits (APR = 4.70; 95% CI: 2.50–8.70, p < 0.001) or four or more visits (APR = 3.00; 95% CI: 1.67–5.40, p < 0.001), and those residing in Amhara region (APR = 2.03; 95% CI: 1.09–3.86, p = 0.026), Harari (APR = 2.04; 95% CI: 1.12–3.70, p = 0.019), Addis Ababa (APR = 2.25; 95% CI: 1.26–4.01, p = 0.006), and Dire Dawa (APR = 3.00; 95% CI: 1.70–5.50, p < 0.001) also had a significantly higher likelihood of undergoing caesarean delivery. In contrast, women residing in rural areas (APR = 0.68; 95% CI: 0.45–0.99, p = 0.045) and those with a high birth order (APR = 0.76; 95% CI: 0.58–0.98, p = 0.032) had a significantly lower prevalence ratio of caesarean delivery compared with their counterparts.ConclusionCaesarean section delivery is rare in Ethiopia, with higher rates among older, educated, Christian women and those receiving more antenatal care, especially in urban areas like Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa. To address disparities, targeted efforts are needed to improve access in rural areas, strengthen antenatal care, and raise awareness among less-educated women and non-Christian women.Keywords: Prevalence, Caesarean section, Delivery, Ethiopia, Poisson regression

Background

Cesarean delivery is the surgical birth of a baby via incisions in the abdomen and uterus [1, 2]. Despite the risk of postoperative infection [3, 4], it remains a lifesaving procedure for deliveries that endanger the mother or newborn. The WHO recommends a population-level CS rate of 10–15%, as lower rates may indicate unmet need and higher rates may suggest overuse [5]. Ethiopia continues to experience high maternal [6] and neonatal [7, 8] mortality, some of which could be prevented by timely and appropriate cesarean delivery [9–11].

A meta-analysis in sub-Saharan Africa reported cesarean delivery prevalence from 1.3% in Chad to 24.5% in South Africa [12]. Population-based surveys such as the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) provide lower estimates, with an overall prevalence of 6.04%, including 10.37% in urban areas and 3.78% in rural areas [13]. Similarly, a meta-analysis of East African studies found an overall prevalence of 24%, with the highest in Ethiopia at 28.3% and the lowest in Uganda at 11.9% [14]. Nationally representative surveys reported a cesarean delivery prevalence of 10.4% in the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey [15] and 1.2 to 5.4% in the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS) [16, 17].

In sub-Saharan Africa, caesarean section delivery was significantly associated with several factors, including urban residence, higher maternal education, greater wealth, and more ANC visits [12, 13, 15]. A meta-analysis of East African studies identified advanced maternal age, higher education, pregnancy-induced hypertension, previous cesarean, and antepartum hemorrhage as risk factors for cesarean delivery [14, 16], while rates declined with higher birth order [18, 19]. In Ethiopia, cesarean delivery was more common in urban areas such as Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa, in female-headed households, and in cases of multiple births [17].

Although some studies in Ethiopia have examined factors associated with caesarean section delivery, all reported odds ratios from logistic regression. Prevalence ratios estimated using Poisson regression with robust standard errors is more directly interpretable for public health and policy audience. Therefore, this study uses nationally representative data to assess the prevalence and determinants of caesarean delivery, providing evidence to inform equitable, context-specific maternal health interventions and policies.

Methods

Study setting and design

A cross-sectional secondary dataset from the 2019 Ethiopian Mini Demographic and Health Survey (EMDHS) was utilized. The survey was conducted by the Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI) and the Central Statistical Agency (CSA), under the auspices of the Ministry of Health. Ethiopia comprises ten regional states (Tigray, Amhara, Afar, Oromia, Southern Nations, Nationalities and Peoples’ Region [SNNPR], Sidama, Harari, Somali, Benishangul-Gumuz, and Gambela) and two city administrations (Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa). Data collection was carried out from March to June 2019.

Study population and sampling procedure

This study included reproductive-aged women who had given birth within the five years preceding the survey, based on data from the individual record (IR) file of the 2019 Ethiopian Mini Demographic and Health Survey (Mini-EDHS). A total of 5,753 reproductive-aged women were included in the analysis. The sample was selected using a two-stage stratified sampling technique. In the first stage, stratification was performed by region, and each region was further stratified into urban and rural areas. In the second stage, enumeration areas (EAs) were selected using a probability proportional to size (PPS) allocation method. A total of 305 EAs were selected for the 2019 Mini-EDHS [20] (Fig. 1).

Data collection methods, tools and data quality assurance

The 2019 Ethiopia Mini Demographic and Health Survey (EMDHS) interviewers used tablet computers to record responses during data collection. The tablets were equipped with Bluetooth technology to facilitate the remote electronic transfer of files within the computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI) system. This included the transfer of assignments from supervisors to interviewers and the return of completed questionnaires from interviewers to supervisors. The electronic data collection system used in the 2019 EMDHS was developed by The DHS Program, utilizing the mobile version of the Census and Survey Processing System (CSPro). Five questionnaires were employed in the 2019 EMDHS: [1] the Household Questionnaire [2], the Woman’s Questionnaire [3], the Anthropometry Questionnaire [4], the Health Facility Questionnaire, and [5] the Fieldworker’s Questionnaire. These instruments, based on the DHS Program’s standard questionnaires, were adapted to address population and health issues specific to the Ethiopian context. The Woman’s Questionnaire was used to collect information from all eligible women aged 15–49 years. These women were asked questions covering the following key topics: background characteristics, reproduction, contraception, pregnancy and postnatal care, child nutrition, childhood immunizations, and health facility information. The main training for the 2019 EMDHS was conducted from February 27 to March 19, 2019. The Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI) recruited and trained 151 health professionals as field staff for the main data collection. The training included instruction on interviewing techniques and field procedures, a detailed review of the questionnaire content, guidance on administering both paper-based and computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI) questionnaires, mock interviews among trainees, and practice interviews with actual respondents in areas outside the survey sample [20].

Study variables

The outcome variable in this study was caesarean section delivery, derived from a dichotomous question in the DHS survey that asked whether the birth was delivered by caesarean section. The responses were categorized as “No” (coded as 0) or “Yes” (coded as 1), consistent with the original classification [20].

Independent variables: The variables included based on the literature were age, residence, region, marital status, religion, educational status, wealth index, media exposure, birth interval, birth order, and ANC visits.

Data management and statistical analysis

After receiving authorization from the DHS Program website to use the DHS dataset, the data were downloaded from the site. Stata version 17 was used to extract, recode, and analyze the data. Sample weight was applied to ensure representativeness and to obtain reliable statistical estimates. Descriptive statistics, including frequency distributions and proportions for categorical variables, were performed. The overall prevalence of caesarean section, along with the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI), was reported. Poisson regression with robust standard errors was employed to estimate adjusted risk ratios (ARRs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). This approach provides directly interpretable measures of association for binary outcomes and is particularly well suited for cross-sectional survey data. It also avoids the convergence issues that can occur with log-binomial regression and follows the modified Poisson framework proposed by Zou (2004) [21]. Based on this rationale, Poisson regression with robust standard errors was applied in the present analysis to estimate prevalence ratios, offering measures of association that are more easily interpretable for public health and policy audiences. Although the prevalence of caesarean section was low (6.1%), Poisson regression was selected primarily to enhance interpretability and consistency of the estimates rather than to address the overestimation concerns associated with logistic regression. Guided by this modeling strategy, variables with a p-value less than 0.2 in the bivariate Poisson regression with robust standard errors were retained for inclusion in the final multivariate model. Because the Poisson model assumes equidispersion—where the mean and variance of the outcome are approximately equal—the Pearson goodness-of-fit test was used to evaluate this assumption. While robust standard errors help address potential violations of equidispersion, formally assessing dispersion provides additional assurance of the validity of model estimates.

Model selection

A stepwise modeling approach using Poisson regression with robust standard errors was employed to identify factors associated with cesarean section delivery. Four models were constructed to progressively assess the effects of individual- and community-level factors: Model 0 (Null Model): Included no predictors and served as a baseline to assess variation in cesarean section prevalence without explanatory variables. Model I (Individual-Level Model): Included only individual-level variables. Model II (Community-Level Model): Included only community-level characteristics. Model III (Full Model): Combined both individual- and community-level predictors to evaluate their joint effects on cesarean section prevalence. This sequential approach allows assessment of how associations change as variables from different levels are added and highlights the potential confounding effects of individual versus community factors. Although no multilevel model was performed, this method provides insight into the relative contributions of factors at each level. While conventional model fit statistics such as the deviance (likelihood ratio test), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) are typically used in multilevel analyses, they are not applicable in Poisson regression with robust standard errors. Therefore, model comparison was based on theoretical relevance, statistical significance of predictors, conceptual completeness, and consistency of significance across models [21, 22]. In Poisson regression with robust standard errors, the coefficient (β₁) was reported, and the prevalence ratio (PR) was estimated using the formula: PR = eβ1 [21, 22], where β₁ represents the estimated coefficient. Finally, prevalence risk ratios along with their corresponding 95% CI were reported. It is important to note that, although the null model and Models I–III were labeled to reflect the sequential inclusion of covariates from different levels, they should not be interpreted as multilevel models. Instead, single-level Poisson regression with robust standard errors was applied and incorporated the EMDHS sampling weights. Robust variance estimation provides some protection against intra-cluster correlation; however, no explicit multilevel structure or cluster-level random effects were modeled, and clustering at the enumeration area or regional level was not directly accounted for. Variance inflation factors (VIF) diagnostics showed no multicollinearity (all VIFs < 5, mean = 1.94). The final reduced model included the most relevant individual- and community-level factors.

Ethical approval

This study did not require ethical clearance since the DHS dataset is publicly accessible. The data were accessed from the Monitoring and Evaluation to Assess and Use Results Demographic and Health Survey (MEASURE DHS) following registration and approval. All ethical guidelines related to the use of secondary data in publications were fully observed. Further details on DHS data usage and ethical standards can be found at https://dhsprogram.com/methodology/Protecting-the-Privacy-of-DHS-Survey-Respondents.cfm.

Results

Characteristics of study participants

In this study, a total of 5,753 women of reproductive age (15–49 years), with a weighted sample of 5,527, were included. More than half (53.8%) of women were in the age group of 25–34 years. Approximately three-fourths (76.9%) of women resided in rural areas. More than ninety percent (93.8%) of women were married. Nearly half (51.7%) of the women were Muslim, while the remainder were Christian. Women with no formal education accounted for 54.7%. Those with a poor wealth index made up 51.4%. Two-thirds of women were not exposed to any media. Nearly one-fifth of the women had a preceding birth interval of less than 24 months. First birth order women constituted 21.9%. Women with no antenatal care (ANC) visits comprised 18.4% (Table 1).

Model selection and its justifications

Four sequential models were fitted using Poisson regression with robust standard errors to identify factors associated with caesarean section among women in Ethiopia using EDHS data. This stepwise approach allowed us to examine how associations changed with the inclusion of variables from different levels, providing insight into the relative influence of individual and community factors. Model 0 was an empty model with no covariates, used to assess baseline variation. However, since it did not provide substantive results, it was not reported. Model I included individual-level variables, while Model II incorporated community-level variables. Model III, the full model, included both individual- and community-level factors. In Model III, several factors remained statistically significant; including women aged 35–49 years, rural residence, Christian religious affiliation, secondary and higher education, higher birth order, utilization of three or more antenatal care (ANC) visits, and residence in specific regions such as Amhara, Addis Ababa, and Dire Dawa. These variables showed consistent significance across the nested models, highlighting their robust association with the outcome. As conventional model fit statistics (e.g., AIC, BIC) are not applicable in Poisson regression with robust standard errors, model selection was based on theoretical relevance, statistical significance of predictors, and conceptual completeness. Accordingly, Model III was selected as the best-fitting and most informative model for interpretation and reporting. Model assumptions/dispersion check

The Pearson goodness-of-fit test indicated a dispersion ratio of 0.915, suggesting slight underdispersion. Robust standard errors were used to account for potential violations of the equidispersion assumption. The use of robust standard errors ensured that the reported confidence intervals and p-values remained valid despite minor deviations from the Poisson model assumptions.

Associated factors of caesarian section among women in Ethiopia

To identify the key determinants of caesarean section delivery, multicollinearity among the predictors was first assessed. All variables had VIFs ranging from 1.04 to 3.14, with a mean VIF of 1.94, indicating no significant multicollinearity. The final reduced model identified maternal age, residence, marital status, religion, wealth index, media exposure, education, birth order, antenatal care attendance, and geographic region as independent predictors, providing precise estimates of prevalence rate ratios and their confidence intervals.

In the multivariable Poisson regression analysis with robust standard errors, several individual and community characteristics were associated with caesarean section (CS) delivery. Women aged 35–49 years had a higher prevalence of CS than younger women (APR = 1.79; 95% CI: 1.20–2.60, p=0.004), while rural residence was associated with a lower prevalence (APR = 0.68; 95% CI: 0.45–0.99, p=0.045). Religious affiliation showed an association, with Christian women reporting a higher prevalence of CS compared with Muslim women (APR = 1.70; 95% CI: 1.25–2.30, p=0.001). Education demonstrated a positive gradient: secondary education (APR = 1.92; 95% CI: 1.40–2.70, p< 0.001) and higher education (APR = 2.20; 95% CI: 1.54–3.20, p< 0.001) were linked to greater CS prevalence compared with no formal education. Antenatal care (ANC) attendance had the strongest association, as women with three visits (APR = 4.70; 95% CI: 2.50–8.70, p< 0.001) or four or more visits (APR = 3.00; 95% CI: 1.67–5.40, p< 0.001) showed markedly higher prevalence than those with no visits. Higher birth order was associated with a lower prevalence of CS (APR = 0.76; 95% CI: 0.58–0.98, p=0.032). Regionally, higher prevalence was observed in Amhara (APR = 2.03; 95% CI: 1.09–3.86, p= 0.026), Addis Ababa (APR = 2.25; 95% CI: 1.26–4.01, p= 0.006), and Dire Dawa (APR = 3.00; 95% CI: 1.70–5.50, p< 0.001) compared with Tigray (Table 3).

Discussion

Although caesarean delivery carries a risk of postoperative infection [3, 4], it is a lifesaving procedure that reduces maternal and neonatal mortality [5–8]. In Ethiopia, there is a shortage of recent studies employing suitable statistical methods for accounting for low-prevalence outcomes. This study aimed to assess the prevalence and associated factors of caesarean delivery in Ethiopia using Poisson regression with robust standard errors.

Only 6.1% of live births in Ethiopia were delivered by caesarean section, a rate significantly lower than the World Health Organization’s recommended threshold of 10–15%, which is considered necessary to meet the obstetric needs of a population [5]. The findings demonstrate substantial regional disparities in caesarean section delivery, ranging from 0.8% in the Somali region to 24% in Addis Ababa, which represents a 30-fold difference between the lowest and highest regions. Rates were significantly higher in urban centers such as Addis Ababa (24.1%), Dire Dawa (13.7%), and Harari (9.2%). In contrast, markedly lower rates were observed in emerging regions including Somali (0.8%), Afar (2.5%), and Gambela (3.3%). The prevalence ranged from 5.3% to 7.8% in Tigray, SNNP, and Amhara regions, while Oromia and Benishangul Gumuz reported rates of 3.5% and 4.2%, respectively. When compared with the WHO recommended population level caesarean section rate of 10 to 15%, the results indicate both underuse and possible overuse across regions. Most regions, including Somali, Afar, Gambela, Oromia, Benishangul Gumuz, Tigray, SNNP, and Amhara, fall below the recommended range, which suggests substantial unmet need for essential caesarean services. Addis Ababa is above the upper recommended threshold, which indicates possible overuse. Dire Dawa is within the recommended range, while Harari is slightly below the lower threshold. The current prevalence is lower than that reported in a previous meta-analysis conducted in sub-Saharan Africa [12], but it aligns with the overall prevalence of caesarean delivery found in other DHS-based studies from the region [13]. A previous DHS study in sub-Saharan Africa also reported disparities in caesarean delivery between urban (10.37%) and rural (3.78%) areas. Moreover, the current magnitude of caesarean delivery is lower than that reported in earlier studies conducted in East Africa [14] and Tanzania [15]. For some studies, differences in study design (meta-analysis vs. DHS data) or study settings (community-based vs. facility-based) help explain the variation in reported caesarean delivery prevalence. Specifically, pooled meta-analysis estimates are generally higher because they often include facility-based studies or selected populations, whereas DHS surveys are population-based and capture both facility and community births, typically resulting in lower overall estimates. Therefore, the observed discrepancies largely reflect differences in study populations and data sources rather than true differences in caesarean delivery rates. The low national prevalence of caesarean delivery indicates underutilization of this lifesaving procedure where access to comprehensive emergency obstetric care is limited. In contrast, the utilization of other maternal and child health services such as antenatal care and routine childhood vaccination remains considerably higher, revealing a clear gap in service coverage. The disparity is even more pronounced in rural and low-resource areas, where geographical, financial, and systemic barriers hinder timely access to surgical interventions [12–17]. As a result, many women may face increased risks of maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality [7, 8] due to the lack of medically indicated caesarean deliveries. This underscores the urgent need to investigate the determinants of caesarean section use and to identify gaps in service provision and health system readiness to improve maternal health outcomes in Ethiopia. These regional and urban–rural differences likely reflect structural and systemic barriers to accessing obstetric care. Urban areas and higher-resource regions typically have greater health system capacity, including better-equipped facilities, a higher concentration of skilled health personnel, and more private-sector services capable of providing caesarean delivery. In contrast, rural and under-resourced regions face challenges such as long distances to health facilities, limited availability of surgical services, and shortages of trained staffs, which restrict access to caesarean delivery. Expanding health system capacity and addressing geographic and infrastructural barriers are essential to reducing these stark regional disparities. The magnitude of caesarean section in the present study is slightly higher than that reported in previous EDHS studies in Ethiopia [16, 17]. This difference may be attributed to the time gap between the studies, which reflects changes in healthcare infrastructure, policy emphasis, and maternal health service coverage over time. In recent years, Ethiopia has made significant investments in expanding access to maternal health services, including the upgrading of health facilities to provide basic and comprehensive emergency obstetric care [23–25]. Additionally, increased awareness among women about the importance of facility-based deliveries and improvements in referral systems may have contributed to the gradual rise in caesarean section rates. Furthermore, urbanization, rising institutional delivery rates and a growing private healthcare sector may also explain the observed increase.

This study identified several individual- and community-level factors significantly associated with caesarean section (CS) delivery using Poisson regression with robust standard errors. Women aged 35–49 years were significantly more likely to have a caesarean delivery compared with younger women, indicating a positive association between older maternal age and caesarean delivery. This finding aligns with existing literature, which indicates that advanced maternal age is linked to increased obstetric risks [14, 16], such as hypertensive disorders, gestational diabetes, and a higher likelihood of previous caesarean deliveries—all contributing to higher caesarian section rates. The elevated risk of complications among older mothers may lead healthcare providers to choose caesarean delivery as a precautionary measure. Women with higher educational attainment had a greater likelihood of undergoing caesarean section. This finding is consistent with earlier studies [14, 16]. Education may improve women’s health literacy, enabling them to better understand and utilize maternal health services. Educated women are also more likely to reside in urban areas, have greater financial resources, and exercise more autonomy in decision-making regarding their mode of delivery [17]. This highlights the influence of socioeconomic factors on maternal healthcare choices and access. Religious affiliation was significantly associated with caesarean section use, with Christian women more likely to deliver by caesarean than Muslim women. This difference likely reflects contextual factors such as geographic settlement, access to health services, and socioeconomic conditions rather than individual religious precepts. In Ethiopia, Christian populations are more concentrated in urban and higher-resource areas, where caesarean services are more readily available, suggesting that geographic and socioeconomic factors may confound the observed association. Additionally, religion in the DHS is self-reported, and individuals may not consistently adhere to the practices associated with their declared religion, introducing potential heterogeneity and misclassification. Overall, these findings suggest that religion serves primarily as a proxy for broader contextual and structural influences on delivery practices rather than a causal determinant. Antenatal care (ANC) utilization was also strongly associated with caesarean section, with women attending more than three ANC visits showing a higher prevalence of caesarean birth than those with fewer visits. This pattern, consistent with previous studies [12, 13, 15], underscores the role of ANC in identifying and managing pregnancy complications as well as providing counseling and support for informed decision-making about delivery options. However, this association should be interpreted with caution, as women experiencing pregnancy complications are more likely to attend frequent ANC visits and simultaneously have a higher likelihood of requiring caesarean delivery. Because our analysis could not fully adjust for this confounding by indication, the observed strong association likely reflects underlying pregnancy risk rather than a direct causal effect of ANC attendance on the mode of delivery. Conversely, rural residence was associated with a lower likelihood of caesarean delivery, highlighting persistent geographic disparities in access to obstetric services. In urban areas—particularly in major cities such as Addis Ababa, Dire Dawa, and Harari—caesarean delivery was more common. Previous studies have also reported higher caesarean section rates in urban settings [12, 13, 15, 17]. This disparity may reflect limited access to healthcare facilities equipped to perform surgical deliveries, financial constraints favoring vaginal delivery in rural areas. The gap between urban and rural caesarean section rates highlights the need to improve access to emergency obstetric care in underserved regions to ensure equitable maternal health outcomes. Caesarean delivery was less likely to occur among women with higher birth orders. Previous studies have also indicated that caesarean delivery is less common among women with higher birth orders [18, 19]. This may be because women with multiple previous vaginal births are more likely to experience uncomplicated deliveries, reducing the need for surgical intervention.

Strength and limitation of the study

In this study, Poisson regression with robust standard errors was used to present prevalence ratios, which are more intuitive for policy and program interpretation. Given the low prevalence of caesarean section, however, odds ratios from logistic regression would have closely approximated risk ratios. The choice of method was therefore driven more by clarity of interpretation than by an expectation of substantial numerical differences. Although survey weights and robust standard errors were applied, an explicit multilevel model was not used to capture potential variation in caesarean delivery across enumeration areas or regions. Delivery practices are known to vary across clusters, and studies employing multilevel approaches may better quantify such contextual influences. This study relied on self-reported region of residence, which may be inconsistently reported and does not necessarily reflect cultural or behavioral homogeneity. Such variability introduces the possibility of misclassification and bias. Consequently, regional differences observed here should be interpreted with caution and not as causal relationships or indicators of religious or cultural adherence. As a cross-sectional study, it is also unable to establish causality. Nonetheless, the findings are based on a nationally representative sample.

Conclusion

Caesarean section delivery remains uncommon in Ethiopia, with notable disparities by age, education, religion, birth order, antenatal care (ANC) attendance, and geographic location. The findings highlight that women who attend fewer ANC visits or belong to certain religious groups are less likely to access caesarean delivery, emphasizing the need to interpret these results in the context of cultural, socioeconomic, and health system factors. To address these gaps, interventions should focus on improving equitable access to safe caesarean delivery, particularly in rural and underserved regions. Strengthening ANC services and providing targeted education on the benefits and appropriate indications of caesarean delivery are critical, especially for women with lower education levels and first-time mothers. Engaging community and religious leaders in culturally sensitive initiatives may help overcome sociocultural barriers. These strategies are essential for enhancing both the scientific understanding and the policy relevance of efforts to improve maternal and neonatal health outcomes nationwide.
