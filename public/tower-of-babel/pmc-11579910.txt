Convexity dural hemangioma: illustrative case
Authors: Ellery A. Hayden, Jack A. Leoni, Frank Culicchia
Published: 2024

Dural hemangiomas are a rare form of intracranial tumor and appear infrequently in the literature, particularly at the convexity.1 Cavernous hemangiomas tend to present as intraparenchymal lesions, and only around 5%–13% have been reported in the current literature to originate from the dura mater.2 Furthermore, those that are of dural origin most often occur in the middle cranial fossa, associated with the cavernous sinus, and their symptomology is usually attributable to mass effect depending on tumor location.3 This article highlights hemangioma as an important part of the differential diagnosis of dural-based convexity masses, even when imaging suggests a meningioma.

Illustrative Case

Here, we present the case of a 46-year-old female patient with a left frontal intracranial mass measuring 11 mm × 12 mm × 8 mm, which was found incidentally during the evaluation of chronic headache and followed with serial imaging over a period of 4 years (Fig. 1). The mass was located 0.87 cm anterior to the central sulcus and 2.23 cm lateral to the midline and displayed growth to 15 mm × 11 mm × 11 mm over the 4-year period of observation. The mass was suspected to be a meningioma, as it was a homogeneously enhancing, extra-axial broad-based mass that exhibited findings of a dural tail along the dura of the superior frontal lobe on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). It is unclear if her chronic headache was caused in part by this mass, as it was small without significant mass effect. However, the decision was made to perform left frontal craniotomy for excision, given the patient’s young age and the rate at which the lesion grew in depth over 4 years. Coronal T1-weighted postcontrast MRI sequence demonstrating a homogeneously enhancing left frontal dural broad-based mass with enhancement of adjacent dura and a dural tail sign.

A left frontal craniotomy was performed under general anesthesia with skeletal fixation. Intraoperative image navigation was used to localize the lesion and plan the craniotomy to facilitate minimal bone removal. The skin, pericranium, and bone flap appeared grossly normal, without evidence of vascular congestion. The dura was subsequently opened, and the underlying mass was noted to have a broad base and very firm attachment to the dura mater. The mass also appeared purple-red and vascular, which was grossly atypical of a convexity meningioma. As the dura was reflected, the tumor separated from the underlying brain easily, with very little dissection required. The tumor was removed en bloc via circumferential excision with normal-appearing dural margins. The frozen specimen was not collected intraoperatively. The final pathology of the permanent specimen revealed a dural hemangioma with many congested capillaries and no evidence of meningothelial cells to suggest an angiomatous meningioma (Figs. 2 and 3). Follow-up imaging 6 months later revealed some residual dural thickening but no evidence of a recurrent mass lesion (Fig. 4). The patient was doing well clinically at follow-up with no complaints. Discussion

Observations

This is a particularly rare case of an asymptomatic convexity dural capillary hemangioma that was thought to be a meningioma on imaging for a period of 4 years. A literature review of 8000 patients found an incidence rate of cavernous angioma in the general population of approximately 0.075%.4 Extra-axial cavernous angiomas (or hemangiomas) account for only 0.4%–2% of all intracranial vascular malformations, and only 5%–13% of these lesions are reported to originate from the dura mater.1, 2 Given the infrequency of this pathology, the tendency for these lesions to be definitively diagnosed only intraoperatively, and the relatively recent advent of advanced MRI, the natural history of the cavernous angioma is not fully understood.4 Furthermore, the current literature reports only 17 cases of dural-based hemangiomas located at the convexity.1, 2, 5–19 This illustrative case reaffirms and highlights a place for hemangiomas in the differential diagnosis of dural-based masses, as they are curable with curettage or en bloc resection and respond well to radiation if they are operatively inaccessible in part or in whole.20, 21 Misdiagnosis of dural hemangiomas as meningiomas is documented infrequently in the literature and is often attributed to the exhibition of a dural tail sign on MRI, which is a thickening and enhancement of the dura mater in continuity with the mass.21, 22 Our case exhibited both a dural tail sign and a broad-based attachment to the dura on imaging with a growth rate typical of meningiomas, highlighting these similarities on imaging.

Lessons

This case illustrates the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis when considering a dural-based mass on imaging. Furthermore, it is important that this differential includes a dural hemangioma to be prepared intraoperatively to attain gross-total resection and treatment of the lesion. While this specific case illustrates a dural hemangioma initially diagnosed as a meningioma, this observation serves as a reminder to consider a wide differential when diagnosing dural-based masses. For example, a study of 1000 patients by Ghosal et al. found that 2% of resected extra-axial dural masses initially diagnosed as meningiomas were found to be other pathologies.23 In this adult patient population, these included metastasis, solitary fibrous tumor, undifferentiated sarcoma, osteosarcoma, Rosai-Dorfman disease, and medulloblastoma.23 Additional pathologies that have been reported in the literature to mimic meningiomas include, but are not limited to, dural hemangiomas, melanocytic tumors, Epstein-Barr virus–associated smooth muscle tumors, sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, lymphoma, and autoimmune immunoglobulin G4–related disease.24 Our case serves to illustrate the importance of considering a diverse differential when planning operative resection of a suspected meningioma. While some of these pathologies are treated similarly to meningiomas, other pathologies such as sarcoidosis and lymphoma require vastly different treatment regimens and extent of recommended resection.
