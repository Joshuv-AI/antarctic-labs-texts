1.
introduction

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be an Artinian local complete intersection of complexity
c\geq 2
. For simplicity we assume that the residue field
k
of
A
is algebraically closed. Let
\ell(M)
denote length of an
A
-module and let
\mu(M)
denote the number of its minimal generators. If
c=2
and if the Hilbert series of
A
is
1+2z+z^{2}
then it is not difficult to prove that the length of any module with complexity
\leq 1
is even. Note that complexity
\leq 1
is a homological notion having to do with boundedness of betti-numbers. However length is a decidedly a non-homological notion. The purpose of this paper is to investigate whether analogues of this result holds to complete intersections of higher codimension.

It turns out that the theory of support varieties of modules is very fruitful for us. This notion was introduced by Avramov
[
1
]
and extended to pairs of modules by Avramov and Buchweitz, see
[
2
]
.
Basically to every module
M
an algebraic variety
\mathcal{V}(M)\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{c-1}(k)
is attached. Support varieties encode homological information of a module.
It also turns out (when
A
is Artinian) that existence of an indecomposable module
H
with
\Omega(H)\cong H
and
\mu(H)
odd is also important (here
\Omega(H)
denotes the first syzygy of
H
and
\mu(H)
is the minimal number of generators of
H
). We note that support variety of an indecomposable periodic module is
\{a\}
for some
a\in\mathbb{P}(k)^{c-1}
, see
[
3
, 3.2]
. Our first result is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be an Artin local complete intersection of complexity
c\geq 2
with algebraically closed residue field
k
. Assume
\ell(A)
is even. Also assume that there exists an indecomposable
A
-module
H
with
\Omega(H)\cong H
and
\mu(H)
odd. Let
\{\alpha\}=\mathcal{V}(H)
. Then there exists integer
m_{A}\geq 2
(depending only on
A
) such that
m_{A}
divides
\ell(M)
for every
M
with
\alpha\notin\mathcal{V}(M)
.

Next we turn to the question,
whether there exists complete intersection rings satisfying hypothesis of Theorem
1.1
. It turns out there is a very large family of complete intersections which satisfy the hypothesis of Theorem
1.1
.

1.2
.

Example:
Let
(Q,\mathfrak{n})
be a regular local ring of dimension
c\geq 2
with algebraically closed residue field
k=Q/\mathfrak{n}
.
Let
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots f_{c-1},g
be a regular sequence in
Q
such that
f_{i}\in\mathfrak{n}^{2}
for all
i
and
g\in\mathfrak{n}
.
Set
A=Q/(f_{1},\ldots,f_{c-1},g^{2})
.
Notice
A
is an Artin complete intersection of complexity
c
. Set
H=A/(g)=Q/(f_{1},\ldots,f_{c-1},g)
.
Clearly
H
is an
A
-module. It is indecomposable since
\mu(H)=1
.
Set
B=Q/(f_{1},\ldots,f_{c-1})
. Then note that
g
is
B
-regular. We have an exact sequence

0\rightarrow B/(g)\rightarrow B/(g^{2})\rightarrow B/(g)\rightarrow 0.

Notice

and

. It follows that

. Also

is even. Thus

satisfies the conditions of Theorem

.

We might wonder about complete intersection rings

where

is odd. It turns out we can prove a similar result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(Q,\mathfrak{n})
be a regular local ring of dimension

with algebraically closed residue field

.
Let
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots f_{c-1},g

. Set

. Then

is an indecomposable periodic

-module. Let
\{\alpha\}=\mathcal{V}(H)
. Then there exists integer

(depending only on

) such that
m_{A}
divides
\ell(M)
for every

Remark 1.4
.

Theorem
1.3
implies the assertion for the example following Theorem

. However Theorem

The original question which motivated me was whether there exists a number

such that

for all

Let

1.6
.

Technique used to prove our result:

The technique to prove our result, to the best of our knowledge has not been used earlier in commutative algebra.
Let
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
denote the stable category of
A
-modules. Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{P}^{c-1}(k)
. We let
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(A)
denote the subset of
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
consisting of all modules
M
with
\alpha\notin\mathcal{V}(M)
. It is easily shown that
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(A)
is a thick subcategory of
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
, see
2.10
. Let
\mathcal{T}
denote the Verdier quotient
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)/\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(A)
.
We have an exact sequence of Grothendieck groups

G_{0}(\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(A))\xrightarrow{\xi}G_{0}(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A))\rightarrow G_{0}(\mathcal{T})\rightarrow 0.

Now we have
\epsilon\colon G_{0}(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A))\cong\mathbb{Z}/\ell(A)\mathbb{Z}
where
\epsilon([M])=\ell(M)\mod\ell(A)
. It suffices to show
\xi
is NOT surjective. Equivalently we have to prove
G_{0}(\mathcal{T})\neq 0
. We recall a result due to Thomason
[
9
]
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a skeletally small triangulated category. Recall a subcategory
\mathcal{D}
is dense in
\mathcal{C}
if the smallest thick subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
containing
\mathcal{D}
is
\mathcal{C}
itself. In
[
9
]
a one-to one correspondence between dense subcategories of
\mathcal{C}
and subgroups of the Grothendieck group
G_{0}(\mathcal{C})
is given. In particular if
G_{0}(\mathcal{C})=0
then any dense subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
is
\mathcal{C}
itself. Let
\mathcal{A}
be the dense subcategory of
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
corresponding to the subgroup
\{0\}
of
G_{0}(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A))
. By a general construction we construct a dense subcategory
\mathcal{A}^{*}
in
\mathcal{T}
which is closely related to
\mathcal{A}
, see section
3
. So if
G_{0}(\mathcal{T})=0
then
\mathcal{A}^{*}=\mathcal{T}
. In particular
k\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
. We use this fact to get a contradiction.

We now describe in brief the contents of this paper. In section two we discuss some preliminaries that we need.
In section three we describe a dense category of a Verdier quotient of
\mathcal{C}
corresponding to a dense sub category of
\mathcal{C}
(here
\mathcal{C}
is a triangulated category).
In section four we prove Theorem
1.1
following the strategy discussed in

1.6
. In section five we prove Theorem
1.3
. Finally in section six we prove Theorem
1.5
.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout this paper all rings are Noetherian and all modules considered are finitely generated.

2.1
.

Triangulated categories:

We use
[
8
]
for notation on triangulated categories. However we will assume that if
\mathcal{C}
is a triangulated category then
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)
is a set for any objects
X,Y
of
\mathcal{C}
.

2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a triangulated category with shift functor
\sum
. A full subcategory
\mathcal{D}
of
\mathcal{C}
is called a
triangulated subcategory
of
\mathcal{C}
if

(1)

X\in\mathcal{D}
then
\sum X,\sum^{-1}X\in\mathcal{D}
.

(2)

If
X\rightarrow Y\rightarrow Z\rightarrow\sum X
is a triangle in
\mathcal{C}
then if
X,Y\in\mathcal{D}
then so does
Z
.

(3)

If
X\cong Y
in
\mathcal{C}
and
Y\in\mathcal{D}
then
X\in\mathcal{D}
.

2.3
.

A triangulated subcategory
\mathcal{D}
of
\mathcal{C}
is said to be
thick
if
U\oplus V\in\mathcal{D}
then
U,V\in\mathcal{D}
.
A triangulated subcategory
\mathcal{D}
of
\mathcal{C}
is
dense
if for any
U\in\mathcal{C}
there exists
V\in\mathcal{C}
such that
U\oplus V\in\mathcal{D}
.

2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a skeletally small triangulated category. The
Grothendieck group

G_{0}(\mathcal{C})
is the quotient group of the free abelian group
on the set of isomorphism classes of objects of
\mathcal{C}
by the Euler relations:
[Y]=[X]+[Z]
whenever
X\rightarrow Y\rightarrow Z\rightarrow\sum X
is a triangle in
\mathcal{C}
.
We always have a triangle
X\rightarrow 0\rightarrow\sum X\xrightarrow{1}\sum X
. So we have
[X]+[\sum X]=[0]=0
in
G_{0}(\mathcal{C})
. Therefore
[\sum X]=-[X]
in
G_{0}(\mathcal{C})
. It follows that
every

element of
G_{0}(\mathcal{C})
is of the form
[X]
for some
X\in\mathcal{C}
.

2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be skeletally small triangulated category and let
\mathcal{D}
be a thick subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
. Set
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{D}
be the Verdier quotient. There exists an exact sequence (see
[
7
, VIII, 3.1]
),

G_{0}(\mathcal{D})\rightarrow G_{0}(\mathcal{C})\rightarrow G_{0}(\mathcal{T})\rightarrow 0.

2.6
.

(with setup as in
2.4
). Thomason
[
9
, 2.1]
constructs a one-to-one correspondence between dense subcategories of
\mathcal{C}
and subgroups of
G_{0}(\mathcal{C})
as follows:

To
\mathcal{D}
a dense subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
corresponds the subgroup which is the image of
G_{0}(\mathcal{D})
in
G_{0}(\mathcal{C})
. To
H
a subgroup of
G_{0}(\mathcal{C})

corresponds the full subcategory
\mathcal{D}_{H}
whose objects are those
X
in
\mathcal{C}
such that

[X]\in H
.

2.7
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be Gorenstein local ring. Let
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
be the stable category of maximal Cohen-Macaulay
A
-modules. Then
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
is a triangulated category with shift functor as
\Omega^{-1}
(the co-syzygy functor), see
[

. We will need the following fact that if

is a triangle then we have an exact sequence

2.8
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be Artinian Gorenstein local ring. Let
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
be the stable category of
A
-modules. Then we have an isomorphism
\epsilon\colon G_{0}(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A))\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}/\ell(A)\mathbb{Z}

2.9
.

Support Varieties of modules over complete intersections:

Let
(Q,\mathfrak{n})
be a regular local ring and let
A=Q/(f_{1},\ldots,f_{c})
where
f_{1},\ldots,f_{c}\in\mathfrak{n}^{2}
is a
Q
-regular sequence. Assume
k=Q/\mathfrak{n}
is algebraically closed.
Gulliksen,
[
6
, 3.1]
, proved that
\operatorname{Ext}^{*}(M,k)
is a finitely generated graded module over
T=k[t_{1},\ldots,t_{c}]
. Here
t_{1},\ldots,t_{c}
can be taken to be the Eisenbud operators acting on a minimal free resolution of
M
, see
[
5
]
. Define

\mathcal{V}(M)=Z(\operatorname{ann}_{T}(\operatorname{Ext}^{*}(M,k))
in the projective space
\mathbb{P}^{c-1}
. We call

\mathcal{V}(M)
the support variety of a module
M
.
Note that in
[
2
]
support varieties are defined as
Z(\operatorname{ann}_{T}(\operatorname{Ext}^{*}(M,k)))
in

k^{c}
which is a cone as the ideal involved is homogeneous. Thus our notion of support variety is equivalent to that in
[
2
]
.
In our case
A
is also Artinian. The following facts follow:

(1)

(see
[
2
, 5.6 (6), (8), (9)]
) If
0\rightarrow M\rightarrow N\rightarrow L\rightarrow 0
is a short exact sequence of
A
-modules then

\mathcal{V}(N)\subseteq\mathcal{V}(M)\cup\mathcal{V}(N).

(2)

\mathcal{V}(M)\cap\mathcal{V}(N)=\emptyset
if and only if
\operatorname{Tor}^{A}_{i}(M,N)=0
for all
i\geq 1
. This follows from the fact that
A
is Artin and
[
2
, Theorem IV, 4.9(iii)]
.

(3)

\mathcal{V}(\Omega(M))=\mathcal{V}(M)
and if
M
is MCM
A
-module then
\mathcal{V}(\Omega^{-1}(M))=\mathcal{V}(M)
, see
[
2
, 5.6(5), (9)]
.

We will need the following result:

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be an Artin local complete intersection of codimension
c\geq 2
and with algebraically closed residue field
k
. Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{P}^{c-1}(k)
. Let
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(A)
denote the subset of
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
consisting of all modules
M
with
\alpha\notin\mathcal{V}(M)
. Then
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(A)
is a thick subcategory of
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
.

Proof.

We first show that
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(A)
is a triangulated sub-category of
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
. See
2.2
for the definition of a triangulated sub-category. Conditions (1), (3) are trivially satisfied by
\mathcal{S}
.
Let
M\rightarrow N\rightarrow L\rightarrow\Omega^{-1}(M)
be a triangle in
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
with
M,N\in\mathcal{S}
. Then we have an exact sequence
0\rightarrow N\rightarrow L\oplus F\rightarrow\Omega^{-1}(M)\rightarrow 0
where
F
is a free
A
-module. Then

\mathcal{V}(L)=\mathcal{V}(L\oplus F)\subseteq\mathcal{V}(N)\cup\mathcal{V}(\Omega^{-1}(M)).

Therefore
\alpha\notin\mathcal{V}(L)
. So
L\in\mathcal{S}
. Thus
\mathcal{S}
is a triangulated subcategory of
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
.
Let
M=U\oplus V\in\mathcal{S}
. Then by
[
2
, 5.6 (7), (9)]
we get

\mathcal{V}(M)=\mathcal{V}(U)\cup\mathcal{V}(V).

So
\alpha\notin\mathcal{V}(U)
and
\alpha\notin\mathcal{V}(V)
. Therefore
U,V\in\mathcal{S}
. Thus
\mathcal{S}
is thick.
∎
