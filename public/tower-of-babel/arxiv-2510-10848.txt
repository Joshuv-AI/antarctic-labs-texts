On Sets of Periodic Orbit Lengths in Finitely Presented Dynamical Systems
Huub de Jong

Abstract
We classify the sets of natural numbers n n for which certain dynamical systems ( X , f ) (X,f) on a compact metric space X X have a periodic point of (least) period n n . Interest in this question dates back to Sharkovskii’s theorem for continuous maps on intervals of the real line, but it also ties to checkable conditions for Krieger’s embedding theorem for symbolic dynamical systems. Given a system for which the logarithmic derivative of the Artin-Mazur zeta function is rational, we use the Skolem-Mahler-Lech theorem to classify for which n n the system has a periodic point of (not necessarily least) period n n . Moreover, we build on work on finitely presented (FP) systems and their relationship to symbolic dynamics to classify the set of least periods, that is periodic orbit lengths, for arbitrary FP systems, extending a known classification for shifts of finite type. We also provide several constructions to realize any such least period sets.

Introduction

Let
X
be a nonempty compact metric space, and
f:X\to X
a continuous surjective map. Moreover, denote
\mathbb{N}:=\{1,2,3,...\}
. Our primary interest in this paper is in the collection of
periodic points
of period
n\in\mathbb{N}
, denoted

P_{n}(f):=\{x\in X:f^{n}(x)=x\},

and the periodic points of
least period

n
, which we denote

Q_{n}(f):=\{x\in X:f^{n}(x)=x,\text{ and }f^{k}(x)\neq x\text{ for }1\leq k<n\}.

For convenience, we will also write
p_{n}(f):=|P_{n}(f)|
, and
q_{n}(f):=|Q_{n}(f)|.

It is clear that

p_{n}(f)=\sum_{k|n}q_{k}(f).

In particular, we are interested in characterizing the
period set
of
f
,

\operatorname{PS}(f):=\operatorname{supp}(p_{n}(f))=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:p_{n}(f)>0\},

and the
least period set
, that is the set of periodic orbit lengths, of
f

\operatorname{LPS}(f):=\operatorname{supp}(q_{n}(f))=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:q_{n}(f)>0\}.

This question is of interest in various situations, two of which we will briefly explain.

First, in 1964, Sharkovskii completely classified
\operatorname{LPS}(f)
when
f
is a continuous selfmap of a (possibly infinite) interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
. In his proof, he first constructs what is sometimes known as the

\triangleleft

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}&3&\triangleleft&5&\triangleleft&7&\triangleleft&9&\triangleleft&\cdots\\
\triangleleft&2\cdot 3&\triangleleft&2\cdot 5&\triangleleft&2\cdot 7&\triangleleft&2\cdot 9&\triangleleft&\cdots\\
\triangleleft&2^{2}\cdot 3&\triangleleft&2^{2}\cdot 5&\triangleleft&2^{2}\cdot 7&\triangleleft&2^{2}\cdot 9&\triangleleft&\cdots\\
&\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots&\\
&\cdots&\triangleleft&2^{3}&\triangleleft&2^{2}&\triangleleft&2&\triangleleft&1\end{matrix}

A

in a total ordering is a set

such that whenever

. The result known as

Theorem 1.1

(Sharkovskii
[

]
)

A set

is the LPS of a continuous selfmap

if and only if
S
is a tail in the Sharkovskii ordering.

We say that a shift space

Stated otherwise, an SFT is a shift space that can be described by “forbidding” finitely many words from occurring in any point in the shift space.

By recoding to what is known as a

Theorem 1.3
.

Let

is of the form

F\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}S_{i},

is finite, each

Moreover, if

.

Finally, if

is either
\{1\}

2
Period Sets

In this section, we will prove Theorem
1.3
, which in particular classifies the period set of dynamical systems with a rational zeta function. Systems with this property include rational maps of the complex plane
[
Hin94
]
and FP Systems
[
Fri87
]
, which include Axiom A diffeomorphisms
[
Man71
]
and SFTs
[
LM21
]
, fall in this category.

Recall that we are trying to show that if
(X,f)
is a dynamical system whose zeta function with positive radius of convergence such that
\frac{d}{dt}\ln\zeta_{f}(t)
is rational, then the period set of
X
is of the form

\operatorname{PS}(X)=\bigcup_{i=0}^{n}d_{i}(\mathbb{N}+k_{i}),

where the union is possibly empty.

We will require a lemma due to Schützenberger, which we modify slightly to suit our purposes. A sequence of complex numbers
p_{n}
satisfies a
linear recurrence (of order
k
)
if there exist a finite collection of complex numbers
b_{i}
for
0\leq i\leq k
, not all 0, such that for all
n
larger than some
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

\sum_{i=0}^{k}b_{i}p_{n-i}=0.

The proof of the lemma is fairly elementary and we include it here for completeness.

Lemma 2.1

(Schützenberger)
.

Consider a sequence
(p_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
of complex numbers such that on some open disk in
\mathbb{C}
, centered at 0 we have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}t^{n}=\frac{A(t)}{B(t)},

with
A(t),B(t)\in\mathbb{C}[t]
. Then for large enough
n
,
(p_{n})
satisfies a linear recurrence. Moreover, if
p_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}
for all
n
, then the coefficients of
A(t),B(t)
are rational.

Proof.

Since the series does not have a pole at 0, we may write

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}t^{n}=\frac{a_{k}t^{k}+a_{k-1}t^{k-1}+\dots+a_{0}}{b_{m}t^{m}+\dots+1},

so that the numerator and denominator are relatively prime in
\mathbb{C}[t]
. Then we have

\displaystyle({b_{m}t^{m}+\dots+1})\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}t^{n}\right)={a_{k}t^{k}+a_{k-1}t^{k-1}+\dots+a_{0}},

(1)

and so for
n>k,

p_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}-p_{n-i}b_{i}.

The linear recurrence above is in fact of minimal order, since otherwise
\operatorname{gcd}(A(t),B(t))\neq 1
. Let now

P_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}p_{n}&\dots&p_{n-m+1}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
p_{n-m+1}&\dots&p_{n-2m+2}\end{pmatrix}

so that

P_{n+1}=\begin{pmatrix}{-b_{1}}&{-b_{2}}&\dots&{-b_{m}}\\
1&0&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&\dots&1&0\end{pmatrix}P_{n}.

Since
(p_{n})
does not satisfy any shorter linear recurrence,
P_{n}
is invertible. It follows that

P_{n+1}P_{n}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}{-b_{1}}&{-b_{2}}&\dots&{-b_{m}}\\
1&0&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&\dots&1&0\end{pmatrix},

hence
B(t)\in\mathbb{Q}[t]
. By (
1
),
A(t)\in\mathbb{Q}[t]
, and the proof is complete.
∎

The second part of the lemma above implies that if a system has a rational zeta function, its poles and zeroes must be algebraic numbers.

We will also use the following powerful theorem, which is much broader than our use case. We refer the reader to
[
Han85
]
for a comprehensive proof. For a sequence
(a_{n})
, we denote its
zero set
by

Z(a_{n}):=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:a_{n}=0\}.

Theorem 2.2

(Skolem-Mahler-Lech)
.

Let
(a_{n})
be a sequence of complex numbers that satisfies a linear recurrence. Then there exist
r,b_{1},\dots,b_{k}
with
k
possibly
0
such that the zero set of
(a_{n})
is

Z(a_{n})=F\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}(b_{i}+r\mathbb{N})

where F is finite.

We have now equipped ourselves with all the tools required to prove our main result of this section.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
\zeta(t)
denote the dynamical zeta function of
(X,f)
. By definition of the zeta function, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\ln(\zeta(t))=\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{p_{n}}{n}t^{n}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{n}t^{n-1}.

Since
\frac{d}{dt}\ln\zeta(t)
is assumed to be rational, by Lemma
2.1
, the sequence
(p_{n})
satisfies a linear recurrence. By Theorem
2.2
, this means that for some
r,K\in\mathbb{N}
and some
B\subset\{0,1,\dots,r-1\}
and finite
F\subset\mathbb{N}
,

Z(p_{n})=F\cup\bigcup_{i\in B}\{m=i\mod r,m\geq K\},

and thus

\operatorname{PS}(X)=Z(p_{n})^{c}=F^{\prime}\cup\bigcup_{i\in B^{c}}\{m=i\mod r,m\geq K\},

(2)

where
F^{\prime}=\{1,\dots,K-1\}\setminus F
.

We shall write
i|j\mod r
to mean that for some
a\in\mathbb{N}
,
ai=j\mod r
, or equivalently that
j
is an integer combination of
i
and
r
. We note further that by definition of
(p_{n})
, for all
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle n\in\operatorname{PS}(X)\implies kn\in\operatorname{PS}(X).

(3)

This means that if
i\in B^{c}
, and
i|j\mod r
, then
j\in B^{c}
. Indeed, suppose
K\leq i+kr\in\operatorname{PS}(X)
and
a(i+kr)=j+br
for some constants
a,b,
and
k
. Then
K\leq j+br\in\operatorname{PS}(X)
, so
j\in B^{c}
.

We now show that

\displaystyle\bigcup_{i\in B^{c}}\{m=i\mod r\}=\bigcup_{i\in B^{c}}i\mathbb{N}.

(4)

On the one hand, for fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
i|ni\mod r
. Hence
ni=j+kr
for some
j\in B^{c}
, implying

\bigcup_{i\in B^{c}}\{m=i\mod r\}\supseteq\bigcup_{i\in B^{c}}i\mathbb{N}.

On the other hand, if
m=i+kr
, we can write
m=k^{\prime}\gcd(i,r)
for some
k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
. Bézout’s Lemma, Theorem 2.10 in
[
Jud24
]
, tells us that
i|\gcd(i,r)\mod r
, and so
\gcd(i,r)\in B^{c}
. This means

\bigcup_{i\in B^{c}}\{m=i\mod r\}\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in B^{c}}i\mathbb{N},

establishing (
4
).

Applying this equality to (
2
), we get that

\displaystyle\operatorname{PS}(X)

\displaystyle=F^{\prime}\cup\bigcup_{i\in B^{c}}\{m=i\mod r,m\geq K\}

\displaystyle=F^{\prime}\cup\bigcup_{i\in B^{c}}\{m=i\mod r\}\cap\{m\geq K\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{divisors})}}{{=}}F^{\prime}\cup\bigcup_{i\in B^{c}}i\mathbb{N}\cap\{m\geq K\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{monotonicity})}}{{=}}\bigcup_{d\in F^{\prime}}d\mathbb{N}\cup\bigcup_{i\in B^{c}}i\left(\mathbb{N}+\left\lfloor\frac{K}{i}\right\rfloor\right)

Both
F^{\prime}
and
B^{c}
are finite, giving the desired expression.

Finally, if
\zeta(t)=\frac{R(t)}{S(t)}
for some polynomials
R,S\in\mathbb{Q}[t]
, then

\frac{d}{dt}\ln\zeta(t)=\frac{R^{\prime}(t)}{R(t)}-\frac{S^{\prime}(t)}{S(t)}.

This expression is rational, so
\zeta(t)
satisfies the assumptions of the theorem, and the conclusion holds.
∎

3
Least Period Sets

3.1
Entropy and Finitely Presented Systems

For our result on least period sets, we will need some tools from topological dynamics, which we develop here. For a detailed introduction, we refer to
[
Wal00
]
.

Let
(X,d)
be a compact metric space and
f:X\to X
our transformation of interest. The transformation
f
induces a new metric

d_{n}(x,y)=\max_{0\leq i<n}d(f^{i}x,f^{i}y)

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We say that a set of points
E
is
(n,\varepsilon)
-
separated
if for
x,y\in E
,
x\neq y
, we have
d_{n}(x,y)>\varepsilon
.
Let

s_{n}(\varepsilon):=\max_{E\subset X}\{|E|:E\text{ is $(n,\varepsilon)$-separated}\},

which is finite for fixed
n,\varepsilon
by compactness of
X
.

We can now state one way of defining the topological entropy of a dynamical system. This particular definition is due to Bowen, see Chapter 7.2 in
[
Wal00
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

The topological entropy of a dynamical system
(X,f)
as above is

h(f)=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log s_{n}(\varepsilon).

The first half of the next lemma is well-known and follows for example from combining Theorem 7.2 and Theorem 7.8 in
[
Wal00
]
. A purely topological proof of the second half can be found in chapter IX, Theorem 1.8 of
[
Rob95
]
, which uses Theorem 17 from
[
Bow71
]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
\pi:(X,f)\to(Y,g)
is a factor map between dynamical systems, then

h(g)\leq h(f).

Moreover, if
\pi
is also bounded-to-one, then

h(g)=h(f).

We will also use a property called
entropy-minimality
, which says that any proper subsystem must have strictly smaller entropy than the whole system.

Definition 3.3
.

A dynamical system
(X,f)
is entropy-minimal if, whenever
Y
is a closed invariant proper subset of
X
, we have

h(f|_{Y})<h(f).

Irreducible SFTs, for example, have this property. The following lemma is Theorem 4.4.7 in
[
LM21
]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose
(X,\sigma)
is an irreducible SFT. Then
(X,\sigma)
is entropy-minimal.

Combining this result with Lemma
3.2
immediately shows that entropy-minimality holds for any bounded-to-one factor of an irreducible SFT.

Corollary 3.5
.

Suppose
\pi:X\to\Omega
is a bounded-to-one factor map from an irreducible SFT
X
to some dynamical system
(\Omega,f)
. Then
(\Omega,f)
is entropy-minimal. In particular, topologically transitive FP systems are entropy-minimal.

Proof.

Let
Z\subsetneq\Omega
be a proper subsystem. Since
Z

Finally, the following well-known lemma on entropy for expansive homeomorphisms is in

Lemma 3.6

When

These

have a long history dating back to Sinai in his work on

Loosely speaking, the idea is to partition

into finitely many closed “rectangles”

with disjoint interiors, such that a specific SFT gives a symbolic representation of

.

Given a Markov partition, we define a transition matrix

and denote the shift space generated by this matrix

.
We then obtain a factor map
\pi:X_{A}\to\Omega
defined by

\pi(...x_{-1}x_{0}x_{1}...)=\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}f^{-i}(R_{x_{i}}).

This map is well-defined and continuous when the rectangles of the partition are “small enough,” and the following diagram commutes.

In what follows, we will not explicitly need Markov partitions, so we refrain from defining them rigorously here. However, thanks to Theorem

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\pi:X\to\Omega
be a factor map arising from a Markov partition of

. Then the following hold.

(A)

(D)

If

Properties (

One can also find (

The forward direction of (

shows in particular that the global periods of all irreducible SFT covers of a sofic shift for which there exist a point with a unique preimage must be equal. While their result is stated for sofic shifts, the proof carries over to the FP case directly. Combining this with Proposition
3.8
(
C
) shows that the value of

is independent of the choice of Markov partition.

Let us continue with the main proof. Recall that

\tilde{p}_{n,i,j}:=\left|\tilde{P}_{n}\left(f,\pi_{i}^{j}\right)\right|\quad\text{ and }\quad\tilde{q}_{n,i,j}:=\left|\tilde{Q}_{n}\left(f,\pi_{i}^{j}\right)\right|.

We have just shown that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{dn}\log\left(\tilde{p}_{dn,i,j}\right)=\log(\lambda),

where
\lambda
is such that
h(X_{i}^{j})=\log(\lambda)
and
d
is the global period of
X_{i}^{j}
. We apply this fact in conjunction with a technique from the proof of Corollary 4.3.8 in
[
LM21
]
.

First observe that from Equation (
5
), we get that

\tilde{q}_{n,i,j}\geq\tilde{p}_{n,i,j}-\sum_{k|n,k<n}\tilde{p}_{k,i,j}\geq\tilde{p}_{n,i,j}-\sum_{k<n/2}\tilde{p}_{k,i,j}.

(7)

Moreover, we note that
\tilde{P}_{n}(f,\pi_{i}^{j})
is empty whenever
n
is not a multiple of

, since a periodic point

with a unique preimage has period

if and only if its preimage has period
n
. Hence
\tilde{Q}_{n}(f,\pi_{i}^{j})
is empty if
n\notin d\mathbb{N}
.

Since also
\tilde{p}_{dn,i,j}\sim\lambda^{dn}
, we conclude from equation (
7
) that
\tilde{Q}_{dn}(f,\pi_{i}^{j})
is nonempty for all large enough

. That is,

\operatorname{supp}\left(\tilde{q}_{n,i,j}\right)=dS,

where
S
is cofinite.

We have now shown that

\displaystyle\operatorname{LPS}(f)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{G}}\operatorname{supp}\left(\left|\tilde{Q}_{n}\left(f,\pi_{i}^{j}\right)\right|\right)

\displaystyle=F\cup\bigcup_{l=1}^{L}d_{l}S_{l}

where
F
is finite and each
S_{l}
is cofinite. Since
X=X_{1}
is an SFT, it has at least one periodic point, and therefore
\Omega
has at least one periodic point. Hence, either
F
or the big union may be empty, but not both. This concludes the general case.

Recall from Proposition
3.8
(
D
) that if
(\Omega,f)
is FP and topologically transitive,
X
can be taken to be irreducible. Such an SFT

must either consist of a single finite orbit, or have infinitely many periodic points. Consequently, the LPS of a topologically transitive FP system must be of the form above, but if it is finite, it must be a singleton.

Moreover, if
(\Omega,f)
is topologically mixing, Proposition
3.8
(
D
) says that
X_{1}
is mixing, hence
X_{1}
has period 1 by Proposition 4.5.10 in
[
LM21
]
. In the notation above, this means that
d_{l}=1
for some
l
. The union of a cofinite set with any other subset of the natural numbers is still cofinite, so the contribution of
\tilde{q}_{n,i,j}
for
i>1
, the “deeper” layers of the system, to the LPS is effectively hidden. With that, we have proven Theorem
1.4
.

Suppose now that
\Omega
is an SFT. Then the alphabet of
\Omega
forms a Markov partition, and
\pi
will be the identity map. That is,
X=\Omega
. We thus only need to consider the irreducible components
X_{1}^{1},...,X_{1}^{k_{1}}
. By the above analysis, the LPS of each component is either a singleton

or
dS
for

cofinite. Thus,

where

is finite and each

cofinite. Again, either

or the big union may be empty, but not both. This classification was first proved in
[
DP19
]
by a direct combinatorial argument.

The observations we made with respect to the irreducibility hold in the same way. If

is irreducible, then we only have one component

, and

is either a singleton
\{d\}
or
dS
for

cofinite. If

, and

is either
\{1\}
or a cofinite set

. This concludes the proof of Theorem

.

3.2.1
Receptive points

In the introduction, we mentioned that our main results are motivated by Krieger’s famed embedding theorem. It is natural to ask for generalizations of this theorem. In
[
Boy83
]
, Boyle proves one direction of Krieger’s theorem in the case of mixing sofic shifts, and recent work by Marcus, Meyerovitch, Thomsen, and Wu in
[
MMTW25
]
explores the irreducible case for both sofic shifts and SFTs. In both cases, the main ingredient is the set of
receptive periodic points
.

Let
Y
be a sofic shift. A word
v
is
synchronizing
if for any words
w
,
w^{\prime}
, such that
wv
and
vw^{\prime}
are both allowed in the shift, then
wvw^{\prime}
is also allowed. A periodic point
w^{\infty}
in
Y
with least period
|w|
is then said to be
receptive
if there exist synchronizing words
v_{1},v_{2}
such that the word
v_{1}w^{k}v_{2}
is allowed in
X
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Briefly, the intuition behind the utility of receptive periodic points is as follows. Recall that in order to embed a shift space
X
into a mixing SFT
Y
, Krieger’s embedding theorem requires that
h(X)<h(Y)
and
q_{n}(X)\leq q_{n}(Y)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Both conditions force our embedding to behave a certain way on the set of all periodic points: The former gives rise to an embedding into
P_{n}(Y)
for all but finitely many values of
n
, while the latter condition forces an embedding on these remaining values of
n
. A priori, these two embeddings need not cooperate nicely if
X
is a mixing sofic shift. Receptive periodic points are the key element that allow us to “glue together” the two embeddings that arise from the two conditions, which is precisely what is done in Theorem 3.6 of
[
Boy83
]
.

For our purposes, it will be convenient to use the following definition of receptive periodic points, which is equivalent to the definition above by Theorem 4.7 in
[
MMTW25
]
.

Definition 3.13
.

Let
Y
be a sofic shift and
w\in Y
be periodic. We say
w
is receptive if there exists an irreducible SFT
X
and a factor map
\pi:X\to Y
such that
w
has a preimage under
\pi
with the same least period. In particular, periodic points with a unique preimage under some such factor map
\pi
are receptive.

We also extract from Proposition 5.1 of the same reference the equivalence of two ways in which one might define the “period” of a sofic shift. Recall that the global period of an irreducible SFT
X
is defined to be
\gcd(\operatorname{PS}(X)),
which is equal to the period of any graph presentation of
X
.

Proposition 3.14

(
[
MMTW25
]
)
.

Let
Y
be an irreducible sofic shift. Then the global period of any irreducible SFT
X
for which there exists a factor map
\pi:X\to Y
which is one-to-one on at least one point in
Y
is equal to the gcd of the periods of all receptive periodic points.

This leads us to the following. Suppose
Y
is an irreducible sofic shift that is not a finite orbit.
Y
is a topologically transitive FP system, and hence admits a Markov partition. Let
X
be the SFT cover arising from this Markov partition for
Y
and denote the corresponding factor map
\pi:X\to Y
. Denote also by
\tilde{Q}_{n}(Y)
the set of points of least period
n
which have a unique preimage under
\pi
.

By Proposition
3.8
(
C
) there is at least one such point, so we know from the proof of Theorem
1.4
that

\operatorname{supp}(\{|\tilde{Q}_{n}(Y)|\})=dS^{\prime},

where
S^{\prime}
is some cofinite set, and
d
is the global period of
X
. By Definition
3.13
, any point with a unique preimage in this particular cover of
Y
is receptive, and thus

\operatorname{LPS}(\{y\in Y:y\text{ is periodic and receptive}\})\supset dS^{\prime}.

However, by Proposition
3.14
, we also know that

\operatorname{LPS}(\{y\in Y:y\text{ is periodic and receptive}\})\subset d\mathbb{N}.

Of course, in the case that
Y
is a finite orbit, all of its points are receptive and of the same period. Combining all of the above, we immediately get the following result.

Theorem 3.15
.

Let
Y
be an irreducible sofic shift. Then

\operatorname{LPS}(\{y\in Y:y\text{ is periodic and receptive}\})

is either a singleton
\{d\}
or
dS
,
where
S
is a cofinite set.

We point out that Definition
3.13
is not symbolic, and hence a priori can be used to define receptive points in an FP system. In fact, although
[
MMTW25
]
focuses on sofic systems, it seems many of their results carry over directly to the FP case. We thus conjecture that Theorem
3.15
also holds for FP systems.

3.3
Gap shifts

We shall obtain a more precise characterization of the LPS of a subclass of shift spaces called
gap shifts
. We denote
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.

Definition 3.16
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then the
S
-gap shift, denoted
X(S)
, is the shift on
\{0,1\}
such that the word
10^{m}1
is only allowed if
m\in S
.

Note that if
S
is finite, the symbol 1 occurs infinitely many times to the right and to the left in any point
x\in X(S)
. Moreover, if
S
is infinite, infinitely many consecutive 0’s are allowed, and hence the point
0^{\infty}\in X(S)
.

The intersection of
S
-gap shifts and sofic shifts (respectively SFTs) is easily described, due to the following result due to Dastjerdi and Jangjoo. We say a set
S\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
is
eventually periodic
if, up to some finite number of elements, it consists only of tails of infinite arithmetic progressions.

Proposition 3.17

(
[
DJ12
]
)
.

An
S
-gap shift is sofic if and only if
S
is eventually periodic. Moreover, an
S
-gap shift is an SFT if and only if
S
is finite or cofinite.

A subset
R
of the natural numbers is
almost sum-closed
if for any pair of
distinct
elements of
R
, their sum is also in
R
. Given a set
S
, we denote its
almost sum-closure
by

\Sigma_{S}^{*}:=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}:k\in\mathbb{N},n_{i}\in S\text{ with }k=1\text{ or }\exists i,j\in\{1,...,k\}\text{ s.t. }n_{i}\neq n_{j}\right\}.

The following properties of almost sum-closures are immediate from the definition and Bézout’s Lemma, Theorem 2.10 in
[
Jud24
]
.

Proposition 3.18
.

If
S\subset\mathbb{N}
is a singleton, then

\Sigma_{S}^{*}=S.

More generally, if
S\subset\mathbb{N}
is almost sum-closed, then

\Sigma_{S}^{*}=S.

Finally, if
S
contains at least two distinct elements, then

\Sigma_{S}^{*}=\gcd(S)\cdot S^{\prime},

where
S^{\prime}
is cofinite.

Recall that a word
w
is
not purely periodic
if there is no other word
v
and integer
k>1
such that
w=v^{k}
. In other words, if
w
is not purely periodic and
w^{\infty}
is allowed in a shift, then the least period of
w^{\infty}
is
|w|
. Our classification of the LPS of gap shifts is then as follows.

Theorem 3.19
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
be nonempty and let
X(S)
be the
S
-gap shift. Then

\operatorname{LPS}(X(S))=\Sigma_{S+1}^{*}

if
S
is finite, and

\operatorname{LPS}(X(S))=\Sigma_{S+1}^{*}\cup\{1\}

if
S
is infinite. In particular,
\operatorname{LPS}(X(S))
is either

•

A singleton
\{d\}
, or

•

An almost sum-closed set
dR
with
R
cofinite, or

•

An almost sum-closed set
dR
with
R
cofinite, together with the singleton
\{1\}
.

Proof.

We will consider three cases.
First, if
S=\{d\}
, then

X(S)=\{\sigma^{i}((10^{d})^{\infty}):i\in\mathbb{Z}\},

which is a finite orbit of length
d+1
. Hence,

\operatorname{LPS}(X(S))=\{d+1\}=\Sigma_{\{d+1\}}^{*}.

Suppose now that
S
is finite with
|S|>1
, and let
x\in X(S)
be periodic. Since
S
is finite,
x\neq 0^{\infty}
. Then we either have

x=\sigma^{i}((10^{n})^{\infty})

for some
i\in\mathbb{Z}

n\in S
, or

x=\sigma^{i}((10^{n_{1}}10^{n_{2}}1\cdots 10^{n_{k}})^{\infty}),

where
i\in\mathbb{Z}
,
k>1
,
n_{j}\in S
for all
1\leq j\leq k
, and
w:=10^{n_{1}}10^{n_{2}}1\cdots 10^{n_{k}}
is not purely periodic.
In the first case, the least period of
x
is

|10^{n}|=n+1\in\Sigma^{*}_{S+1}.

In the second case, at least two
n_{j},n_{j^{\prime}}
are distinct, so the least period of
x
is

|10^{n_{1}}1\cdots 10^{n_{k}}|=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(n_{j}+1)\in\Sigma^{*}_{S+1}.

Thus,
\operatorname{LPS}(X(S))\subset\Sigma^{*}_{S+1}.

Conversely, let
\tilde{n}\in\Sigma_{S+1}^{*}
. Then there exists a set
\{n_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
such that
n_{j}\in S
for all
1\leq j\leq k
and at least two
n_{j}
,
n_{j^{\prime}}
are distinct, and

\sum_{j=1}^{k}(n_{j}+1)=\tilde{n}.

Then, after sorting the
n_{j}
in weakly increasing order, the word

10^{n_{1}}1\cdots 10^{n_{k}}

is not purely periodic, so

x=(10^{n_{1}}1\cdots 10^{n_{k}})^{\infty}

is in
X(S)
and has least period
\tilde{n}
. Using Proposition
3.18
, we conclude that

\operatorname{LPS}(X(S))=\Sigma_{S+1}^{*}=\gcd(S+1)\cdot R

for some cofinite set
R
.

Finally, suppose
S
is infinite. We may then repeat exactly the argument above, but must also note that
0^{\infty}\in X(S)
. This yields a point of period 1. In this case, we thus get

\operatorname{LPS}(X(S))=\Sigma_{S+1}^{*}\cup\{1\}=\left(\gcd(S+1)\cdot R\right)\cup\{1\},

where again
R
is cofinite.

∎

In the next section, we will show that any set of the form in Theorem
3.19
can in fact be realized by a sofic gap shift, or even a gap shift of finite type.

4

This section is dedicated to explicit constructions that prove the sufficiency in our main results. In fact, each construction will be a shift space.

Remark 4.1
.

Our constructions in this section can also be used to realize period sets

In particular, Theorem

.

4.2
Sofic shifts

At the end of the previous section, we noted how to construct a certain SFT to realize more complicated LPSs than the ones in Theorem

We will construct a graph

\mathcal{G}=(G,\Phi)

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose
S

F\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}S_{i}

If

Our construction is such that the shifts on all

4.3
Gap shifts

The inherent structure of gap shifts allows us to realize their LPS with relative ease. In fact, we obtain the desired LPS via a
sofic
gap shift. The majority of the proof below follows from Proposition
3.18
and our observations during the proof of Theorem
3.19
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
Q
be a set as outlined in the conclusion of Theorem
3.19
. Then there exists a sofic
S
-gap shift whose LPS is
Q
. Moreover, if
Q
is almost sum-closed, then there exists an
S
-gap SFT with least period set
Q
.

Proof.

Let
Q
be a set as indicated and for
S\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
, let
X(S)
be the
S
-gap shift. We will first prove the second half of the theorem. If
Q
is a singleton
\{n\}
, we take
S=\{n-1\}
. Then
X(S)
is a finite orbit of length
n
. Singletons are trivially almost sum-closed, and
X(S)
is an SFT, so we are done.

Next, suppose
Q
is an almost sum-closed set
dR
with
R
cofinite. We will find a finite set of elements whose almost sum-closure is
Q
. Write first

Q=F\cup\{dN,d(N+1),d(N+2),...\},

where every element of
F
is divisible by
d
and strictly smaller than
dN
. Now by assumption
Q
is almost sum-closed, so for some
F^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{N}
,

\Sigma_{F}^{*}=F\cup F^{\prime}\subset Q.

Moreover, if we set

B=\{dN,d(N+1),d(N+2),...,d\cdot 2N\},

then

\Sigma^{*}_{B}=\{dN,d(N+1),d(N+2),...\}.

Thus, in this particular case,

\Sigma^{*}_{F\cup B}=F\cup\{dN,d(N+1),d(N+2),...\}=Q.

We noted in the proof of Theorem
3.19
that if
S
is finite,

\operatorname{LPS}(X(S))=\Sigma^{*}_{S+1}.

Thus, if we set
S=(F\cup B)-1
, then
X(S)
is an SFT by Proposition
3.17
, and

\operatorname{LPS}(X(S))=Q.

Finally, suppose

Q={dR}\cup{\{1\}}

with
R
cofinite and
dR
almost sum-closed. Then
dR
is eventually periodic, so
dR-1
is also eventually periodic. Thus, setting
S=dR-1
, the
S
-gap shift
X(S)
is sofic by Proposition
3.17
.
Now, we recall from the proof of Theorem
3.19
that if
S
is infinite,

\operatorname{LPS}(X(S))=\Sigma_{S+1}^{*}\cup\{1\}.

But by Proposition
3.18
, we have

\Sigma_{(dR-1)+1}^{*}=\Sigma_{dR}^{*}=dR,

and hence for
S=dR-1
,

\operatorname{LPS}(X(S))=dR\cup\{1\},

completing the proof.
∎

4.4
Arbitrary LPS

We conclude our collection of realizations by showing that
any
subset of the natural numbers is the LPS of some subshift of the full 2-shift.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{N}
be arbitrary. Then there exists a shift space
X\subset\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
whose LPS is
S
.

Proof.

If
S
is finite, take
X=\{\sigma^{n}((10^{k-1})^{\infty}):k\in S,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. Then
X
is a finite shift space.

Suppose
S=\{k_{1},k_{2},\dots\}
is infinite and written in strictly increasing order. We consider two cases.

If
1\in S
, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, take
u_{i}=10^{k_{i}-1}
, which is not purely periodic. Then the LPS of

X=\overline{\{\sigma^{n}(u_{i}^{\infty}):i\in\mathbb{N},n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}

certainly contains
S
. We will show that it contains no other periods.

Let
X
be as above, and take
x\in X
periodic with least period
k
, where
x=w^{\infty}
for
w
not purely periodic. If there are no 1s in
w
, then
x=0^{\infty}
, which has least period 1. This period is in
S
by assumption, and we are safe.

Otherwise, take
x^{(n)}
to be a sequence of points such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

x^{(n)}=\sigma^{j}(u_{i}^{\infty})

for some
i\in\mathbb{N},j\in\mathbb{Z}
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}x^{(n)}=x.

Now there exists an
N
such that for all
n\geq N
, we have that

x^{(n)}_{[-k,k-1]}=x_{[-k,k-1]}=w^{2}.

Since there is a 1 somewhere in
w
, we know that the word
10^{l}1
is in
w^{2}
for some
l
. But if this word is contained in
x^{(n)}
, then
x^{(n)}
must be some shift of
(10^{l})^{\infty}
, and therefore
l=k_{i}-1
for some
k_{i}\in S
. We conclude that for some
i\in\mathbb{N},j\in\mathbb{Z}
.

x^{(n)}=\sigma^{j}(u_{i}^{\infty})

for all
n\geq N
. Hence,

x=\sigma^{j}(u_{i}^{\infty}),

and the least period of
x
is in
S
by construction.

Suppose now that
1\notin S
. Set
u_{1}=10^{k_{1}-1}
. For
i>1
, take

u_{i}=1u_{1}^{g_{i}}u_{i}^{\prime},

where
g_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
u_{i}^{\prime}
is a strict prefix of
u_{1}
such that
|u_{1}^{g_{i}}u_{i}^{\prime}|=k_{i}-1
. This way
u_{i}
is not purely periodic, since the subword 110 only occurs at the very start. At the same time,
u_{i}
contains the subword 110 for any
i>1
, since
k_{1}\neq 1.

Now the LPS of the shift space

X=\overline{\{\sigma^{n}(u_{i}^{\infty}):i\in\mathbb{N},n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}

again contains
S
. We show that no other least periods are created, using a similar, but slightly more subtle, argument as in the previous case.

Let again
X
be as above, and take
x\in X
periodic with least period
k
, where
x=w^{\infty}
for
w
not purely periodic.

Moreover, take once again
x^{(n)}
to be a sequence of points such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

x^{(n)}=\sigma^{j}(u_{i}^{\infty})

for some
i\in\mathbb{N},j\in\mathbb{Z}
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}x^{(n)}=x.

Slightly altering our previous argument, there exists an
N
such that for all
n\geq N
, we have that

x^{(n)}_{[-k,2k-1]}=x_{[-k,2k-1]}=w^{3}.

Suppose that the word 110 appears in
x
. Then
w^{3}
contains a word of the form
110\tilde{u}110
for some subword
\tilde{u}
. For all
n
large enough,
x^{(n)}
then also contains
110\tilde{u}110
. This is only possible if

x^{(n)}=\sigma^{j}(u_{i}^{\infty})

for some
i\in\mathbb{N},j\in\mathbb{Z}
for all large
n
, and hence
x=\sigma^{j}(u_{i}^{\infty})
with least period
k_{i}\in S
.

If the word 110 does
not
appear in
x
, take a subsequence
n_{m}
such that

x^{(n_{m})}_{[-mk,mk-1]}=x_{[-mk,mk-1]}=w^{2m}

for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Since the word 110 does not appear in
w^{2m}
for any
m
, we know that if 110 appears in
x^{(n_{m})},
it must be outside of the window
[-mk,mk-1]
. In other words, for all
m
,

x^{(n_{m})}_{[-mk,mk-1]}=\sigma^{j}(u_{1}^{\infty})_{[-mk,mk-1]}

for some
j\in\mathbb{Z}
.
We then conclude that
x=\sigma^{j}(u_{1}^{\infty})
, which has least period
k_{1}\in S
.

∎
