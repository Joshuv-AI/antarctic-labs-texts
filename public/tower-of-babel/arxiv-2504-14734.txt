Apples Falling, Buckets Rolling, and Why Inertia Keeps Trolling
Nicola Bamonti

Abstract
Inertia is the most fundamental and yet elusive concept in physics. Modern geniuses such as Newton and Einstein have wondered about its nature, but the last word has yet to be said and ‘the question of questions’ still remains: what is inertia ? In the paper, analysing the properties and criticalities of the concept of geodesic, I propose to separate the notion of geodesic inertial motion from that of natural motion , which I identify as motion characterised by a generalised version of the geodesic deviation. This strategy succeeds in shedding new light on the motion of bodies.

Introduction

It is 1666 and England is facing the Great Plague in London. In that year, Cambridge University was closed because of the epidemic, and Newton, who was then a young student, retired to his home at Woolsthorpe Manor. It is a spring morning and young Newton is enjoying the light breeze and early sunshine beneath an apple tree.
Immersed in thought, between infinitesimal calculations and prisms an apple fell on his head.

Eureka!
– he exclaimed.

The apple would never have fallen if a force had not caused it to do so, the same force that keeps the Earth in orbit around the Sun
, Newton thought.

And that’s not all
— he continued —
the natural tendency of a body to resist changes in its state of motion is why the free surface of water within a bucket rotating with respect to absolute space takes on a concave shape.

Suddenly, the qualitative ideas of Galileo and Descartes crystallised in a rigorous form in the mind of Newton, who conceived his three laws of dynamics, including the first: the law (or principle) of inertia.

This, of course, is just a tale.

Newton stands in continuity with a long tradition of thinkers in the West, known as
natural philosophers
, who questioned Nature and its laws.
Among these questions is that concerning the motion of bodies.

The motion of bodies is a topic over 2,000 years old.

How
bodies move in nature?
This question is accompanied by another, perhaps more profound question:
why
do bodies move in the way they do? What is the nature of their motion?

These questions, when they arose, were often posed in terms of a division: There are motions caused by agents external to the bodies and motions intrinsic to the bodies themselves when no external cause acts.
So the questions become:
how
do bodies move when no external cause acts on them? And how do they move when there is such a cause?
And
why
do they assume such and such a configuration of motion?

The first investigator of inertia was Aristotle with his distinction between ‘
natural motions
’, typical of bodies ‘left to themselves’, and ‘
violent motions
’, typical when an external influence is present
(
Sachs 1995
)
.

In the late Middle Ages, Buridan advanced the
impetus theory
, positing that a body in motion carries an internal ‘impetus’ that leads it to continue its motion even in the absence of external causes
(
Jung 2011
)
. This is historically the first definition of inertia similar to the one that would later be refined in modern times by Galileo and Descartes and then rigorously formalised by Newton in his ‘first law’.

The recent literature on whether and in what sense GR has the power to

In this paper, drawing on the work of Galileo, Newton and Einstein, I aim to shed light on what inertia is, with a particular focus on the concept of inertial motion in GR.
My analysis will reveal that inertia is a useful fantasy and that the natural motion of bodies follows other laws.

2.1
Principle or Law?

We should ask: is inertia an empirical law that describes observed regularities, or a principle taken as a starting point to account for those regularities?
8
8

8

In the first case scenario, another topic of debate is whether the
Law of inertia
is a separate law or a corollary to the Newton’s Second Law. In essence, the answers depends on the formulation of the Newtonian theory.
In a four‐dimensional (covariant) formulation, once inertial frames are properly defined as adapted to the assumed flat affine structure, the first law follows from the second law since it merely encodes that force-free particles follow geodesics (i.e. “straight lines” in space–time).
On the other hand, in the standard three-dimensional formulation, “the first law asserts that there exist certain frames with respect to which the second law is supposed to hold. The second law thus does not even make sense without the first law to define those frames”
(
Jacobs 2024
, p.7)
. See the discussion below for details.

Until now, I referred to the ‘principle of inertia’ and not the ‘law of inertia’.
Let me therefore clarify the two terms by introducing a
law-like terminology
and a

The Law of Inertia: The Law-like Terminology.

In Newtonian mechanics the “law of inertia” is traditionally expressed by Newton’s first law – a statement that free (force‑free) bodies continue in uniform straight‑line motion (cf, Def. (
2
)) – and is regarded as an
empirical law
on a par with Newton’s other laws.
In more detail, in the law‑like terminology, Newton’s first law is an empirical statement: free bodies move uniformly in straight lines.

The common ground for the law‑like terminology is the standard
three-dimensional framework
, where inertial frames are defined as those relative to which the simplest form of Newton’s laws holds; that is, they are frames where free particles appear to move uniformly
(v=const)
and forced particles follow
F=ma
(see
Weatherall 2020b
for a critique on the use of simplicity as a criterion).
The three-dimensional framework is in line with what
Jacobs 2024
call the
law-based approach
on inertia where the first law is used to
define
inertial frames (presupposing they exists).
9
9

9

This need to presuppose the existence of what one defines not only is methodologically problematic, but also undermines the definition of inertia as an empirical law in the same way as Newton’s other laws. See
Bergmann 1976
, p.8
. To be clear, this was not a problem for Newton himself, since he defined its first law with respect to Absolute space. Thus, according to Newton its first law was an empirical statement about the motion of bodies and not a definition of inertial frames, nor a statement for their existence.

However, as already stressed above, a law‑based account is problematic because it tends to be circular: one identifies inertial frames by the very behaviour (inertial motion) that the law is meant to explain. Furthermore, arbitrary coordinate transformations can always “hide” or “recover” the formal simplicity of the laws, making the definition too liberal.

In response,
the four-dimensional (covariant) framework
can be adopted, which is more in line with what
Jacobs 2024
call the
structure‑based approach
. Here, inertial frames are those adapted to the underlying flat affine structure of spacetime (
Galilean Spacetime
in the case of Newtonian physics
Earman 1992
), thus grounding the Law of Inertia in the geometry of the world rather than in particle dynamics. In this setting, the law of inertia does note
define
the inertial frames, which are instead defined independently in terms of the presupposed flat-affine structure.
10
10

10

Rigorously, an affine structure is provided by an affine connection (not necessarily the
unique Levi-Civita connection
of a metric). This connection tells you how to parallel-transport vectors and defines the geodesics of the manifold.
In certain coordinates, called affine or Riemann-normal coordinate, we can define a
flat
affine spacetime that has
all
Levi-Civita coefficients equal to zero.
Importantly, if it is possible to find a coordinate system where all Levi-Civita coefficients vanish everywhere, then the Riemann curvature tensor must be identically zero, and this fact remains true in all coordinate systems. However, in a different coordinate system, some Levi-Civita coefficients may become non-zero, even though the affine structure remains flat. Thus, in Newtonian spacetime this structure allows for the existence of
global
inertial frames related by Galilean transformations (rotations, spatial translations, time translations, and boosts). For completeness, let me stress that another important geometric structure is the
conformal structure
, which in GR is governed by the Weyl tensor. The conformal structure in Newtonian spacetime is less straightforward than in relativistic settings due to its degenerate metrics. While the Riemann tensor in Newtonian gravity shares algebraic symmetries with its relativistic counterpart, the Weyl tensor has no a direct analogue, as Newtonian curvature lacks vacuum/conformal degrees of freedom.
This reflects the fundamental distinction between Newtonian gravity (elliptic, non-radiative) and GR (hyperbolic, radiative). Advanced extensions (e.g.,
teleparallel Newton-Cartan

(
Schwartz 2023

However, for lovers of twists and turns, in Newtonian gravity it is possible to define the ‘

E_{ij}=\cfrac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial x^{i}\partial x^{j}}-\cfrac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}\nabla^{2}\Phi.

This object serves as the Newtonian analogue of the electric part of the relativistic Weyl tensor since it describes gravity when
\nabla^{2}\Phi=0
(i.e. in vacuum). In GR the Weyl tensor

However, Newtonian gravity lacks a counterpart to the magnetic Weyl tensor, as there are no propagating degrees of freedom (e.g., gravitational waves) or frame-dragging effects. Furthermore,

In particular—as emphasised in

The Einstein’s Law of Inertia

Let me now turn the discussion to the relativistic case of GR. This turn does not mean abandoning the classical case, in fact the results I will obtain in GR regarding the concept of inertia will be more general and naturally extendable to the classical Newtonian case under appropriate limits.

In GR, one naturally adopts a
structural
view: gravity is not a force but rather encoded in the curvature of spacetime. Consequently, departures from inertial motion cannot be ascribed to gravitational forces, unlike in Newtonian formulations (whether in three‑ or four‑dimensional formulation).
For completeness, I point out that this geometrical understanding is not exclusive to GR, but already appears in non-relativistic settings like Newton–Cartan theory (NCT), where one “geometrises” the gravitational interaction (see
Earman and Friedman 1973

Trautman 1965

Trautman 1967

We may still have an ace up our sleeve.

In certain spacetimes—specifically, those with torsion—the notion of geodesics (often called
self-parallel curves
) does not necessarily coincide with the notion of curves that extremise the spatiotemporal distance (often called
extremal curves
).
This discrepancy arises because, unlike in ‘standard GR’, the Levi-Civita connections and the more general affine connections are distinct in these settings.

Levi-Civita connections
are defined in terms of the gravitational field and are used to express the geodesic equation in spacetimes without torsion; they are symmetric in their lower indices.

In contrast,
(general) affine connections
are defined without reference to a gravitational field and need not be symmetric. The antisymmetric part of an affine connection defines the
torsion field
.
In spacetimes with torsion, the affine connection is used to define extremal curves, which may differ from the self-parallel (geodesic) curves determined by the Levi-Civita connections in the torsion-free case.
21
21

21

Levi-Civita connections, respecting the compatibility condition
\nabla_{c}g_{ab}=0
(also known as
Ricci Theorem
in tensor analysis), are related to the derivatives of the metric. Their coefficients are also sometimes referred to as
Christoffell’s symbols

\Gamma^{a}_{bc}=\frac{1}{2}\,g^{ad}\left(\partial_{b}g_{dc}+\partial_{c}g_{bd}-\partial_{d}g_{bc}\right)
. Affine connections and Christoﬀell symbols (i.e. Levi-Civita coefficients) in principle represent different objects. Affine connections are defined as the components of the derivative of the basis vectors along the tangent directions of the manifold itself (the component normal to the manifold is called the
extrinsic curvature
). Thus, to define a generic affine connection, we do not need a metric structure on the manifold.
Christoﬀell symbols, on the other hand, are defined from metric properties of the manifold by introducing the metric tensor.

In the case of a free-falling

Does it extremise the distance between two points or does it keep the covariant derivative of the tangent vector equal to zero?
This question could lead us to understand inertial motion as motion along extremal curves rather than along geodesics, which would also remove triviality from the Definition (

Unfortunately, this is not how things work

3.1
The Link With the Equivalence Principle: What is Local?

According to Definition (

24

24

In this work I will always refer to what in philosophical literature is called the

Earman and Friedman wryly observed that “there are almost as many interpretations of this principle as there are authors of books on relativity theory”

One of the most influential formulations—often attributed to

It could also be put it in the following way (see

‘Locally’, the effects of a gravitational field are equivalent to those experienced in a non-inertial reference frame. Thus, gravity can be cancelled ‘locally’ and this possibility defines ‘local’ inertial frames where
\Gamma^{a}_{bc}=0

25
25

25

I include scare quotes on the term

This principle, rooted in Einstein’s original insights towards the construction of GR (his ‘happiest thought’), was initially proposed as a

This led to the ‘

However, Einstein in the inception of the principle considered the case of a

26
26

26

As a historical note that I think is relevant and not emphasised enough, the first (rough) idea of the Einsteinian global version of the equivalence principle dates back to

Corollary VI to the laws of motion

If bodies are moved in any way among themselves, and are urged
by equal accelerative forces along parallel lines, they will all
continue to move among themselves in the same way as if they
were not acted on by those forces (ivi, p. 20.)

Pauli subsequently generalised the argument, claiming that

Thus, in a sense reversing Einstein’s argument, the infinitesimal EP became the standard version adopted, spreading the belief that one can always transform away an arbitrary gravitational field in GR within an

Norton 1985

A closer examination reveals that the very notion of ‘local’ warrants scrutiny, as it can be interpreted in two distinct ways:

•

Local at a point:
This refers to an infinitesimal neighbourhood around a single manifold point on the manifold. In the limit, this neighbourhood reduces to the point itself, which has Lebesgue measure zero in the ambient spacetime. In coordinate-based terminology, this notion corresponds to
Riemann normal coordinate
s (adapted to a point).

•

Local along a geodesic:
This pertains to an infinitesimal ‘tubular’ neighbourhood surrounding a timelike geodesic. While the neighbourhood itself has Lebesgue measure zero as a subset of spacetime, the geodesic admits a well-defined affine parameterisation with a length (or proper time) that may be finite or unbounded. In coordinate-based terminology, this notion corresponds to
Fermi normal coordinates
(adapted to a geodesic)
(
Fermi 1922
)
.

As I shall argue in §
5
, in both interpretations the EP remains a ‘useful fantasy’.
Realistic physical systems are extended in space
27
27

27

For some authors, objects are also extended in time. See, for example, the case of four-dimensionalism
(
Sider 2001
;
Hawley 2024
)
. Personally, I consider this view to be intrinsically flawed in GR, given the local nature of time and at most limited to special relativity.
, making strict pointwise or ‘tubular’ localisation inapplicable.
28
28

28

This fact is the other side of the coin of

Thus, although ‘morally’, the standard story that special relativity is locally valid in GR is certainly correct, appealing to the local validity of special relativity or the pointwise vanishing of
\Gamma^{a}{}_{bc}

I contend that a more robust approach demands questioning the ontological status of geodesics themselves, which are inherently infinitesimal—and thus singular—regions of spacetime.

4
A Useful Fantasy: The Test Particle Approximation

If you wish to learn from the theoretical physicist anything about the methods which he uses, I would give you the following piece of advice: Don’t listen to his words, examine his achievements. For to the discoverer in that field, the constructions of his imagination appear so necessary and so natural that he is apt to treat them not as the creations of his thoughts but as given realities.

Einstein 1934

To recap, in §
2
I reviewed the standard definitions of inertial motion and the Principle of Inertia in classical mechanics—formulated in terms of force‑free motion—and highlighted their inherent circularity. In §
3
I then adopted the notion of geodesic motion to define

conflicts with the finite spatial extension of all realistic bodies, from electrons to planets.

29

29

Even an electron, though often idealised as a “point charge”, cannot truly be a geometric point: classical electrodynamics predicts an infinite self‑energy for a genuine point charge, rendering the model unphysical. In quantum field theory, renormalisation is required to absorb such infinities, suggesting that the point‑like description may only be effective, with a more fundamental theory assigning particles a finite size or internal structure.

In this section I examine additional difficulties that arise when one interprets geodesic motion as the criterion of local inertial motion for
actual

30
30

30

Though I restrict attention to GR here, analogous remarks apply to Newton–Cartan theory (cf. fn.

A geodesic typically describes the motion of a
test particle

So, the geodesic equation —
\frac{Du^{a}}{D\tau}\equiv u^{b}\nabla_{b}u^{a}=0
, where
u^{a}
is the four-velocity of the test particle— provides the “law” for how gravitating bodies
hover
the given metric field, without affecting it.
This approximation provides a mathematically tractable approximation, but it diverges from physical reality: every body exerts its own gravitational influence, inducing
backreaction

Anderson 1967

The motion of an otherwise free particle in a gravitational field will be governed by the geodesic equation […] The gravitational field appearing in these equations is the
total
gravitational field, which is influenced by the presence of the particle both directly through the contribution of its own field and through its effect on whatever other sources of gravitational fields are present. Because of the intrinsic nonlinearity of the Einstein equations, these two contributions are inextricably intertwined in the total field so that there is no simple way that we can eliminate self-interactions […] Only for the
limiting case in which we can neglect the influence of the particle on the other
sources can we subtract off this self-interaction and take [the geodesic equation] to describe the motion of a free particle in a gravitational field (ivi, p.353).

So, following

tolerably accurate approximation

(
Thébault 2021
)
.

The test-particle approximation, though a cornerstone of theoretical physics, represents a model that simplifies complex interactions.
This perspective highlights the broader philosophical implications of my exploration of the difficulties in defining a realistic concept of inertia, started in §
3.1
.
Embracing this challenge, I aim to reconcile the theoretical elegance of GR with the practical limitations imposed by real-world physical systems.

A particularly incisive critique of the geodesic principle in GR is provided by Tamir
(
Tamir 2012
)
. He formulates a three‑part argument:

Specifically, I argue for the following three claims.
First, […] massive bodies are [n]ever guaranteed to follow geodesic paths.
Second, […] extended massive bodies generically deviate from uniformly geodesic paths.
[…] Third, thanks to certain mathematical theorems concerning distribution theory, alternative representations of massive bodies as unextended ”point” particles must result either in precluding the possibility of coupling the particle to the spacetime metric in a way that is coherent with Einstein’s field equations or in having to excise the particle (and its would-be path) from spacetime entirely.
This three-pronged argument reveals that […] the geodesic principle in such a way requires that either the gravitating body is not massive, its existence violates Einstein’s field equations, or it does not exist within the spacetime manifold at all (let alone along a geodesic) (ivi, p.137-138).

In what follows, I concentrate on his first and third criticisms; the second will be addressed in §
5
.

Tamir’s analysis suggests that the geodesic principle applies only to
unextended test particles
, which are manifestly highly approximated models of realistic bodies.
Yet, almost contradictorily, in GR, unlike other classical theories, the geodesic equations are not logically independent from Einstein’s equations (EFEs) which take into account the stress-energy content of matter.
How can it work for test particles? Did I not just state that
only
test bodies follow geodesics and that, by definition, test bodies do not contribute to the dynamics of the metric, having no backreaction?

Let me untangle what is going on.
The derivability of the geodesic equation from the EFEs hints

31

32

This means first knowing the form of the tensor

I point out some perplexities concerning this inference guided by the Bianchi’s identities.
The first is that, strictly speaking, this inference concerns only matter that appears as a source in EFEs and not
test
— as opposed to sourcing —- matter.
Secondly, as stated in
Weatherall 2016

The usual response is to regard test particles as “small bits” of extended matter and to show that, under suitable approximations, these “bits” follow geodesics. Yet this, too, is merely
a useful fantasy
.

Moreover, note that timelike (that is,
sub-luminal

What I mean is that theorem (
4.1
) requires the strengthened dominant energy condition to guarantee
timelike

\nabla_{a}T^{ab}=0
does not imply geodesic motion.

However, in the special case of a perfectly homogeneous, pressureless (dust) fluid—often used as an idealisation for non-interacting matter—the absence of pressure ensures that no forces other than gravity act on the freely falling fluid elements. As a result, each element follows a geodesic, despite its backreaction contribution being non-zero.
33
33

33

The pressureless dust model represents a continuous, uniform distribution of matter rather than a realistic extended body. In reality, every body described by rigorous physical theories possesses angular momentum distributions and internal properties (e.g., internal stresses or electromagnetic charges) that lead to deviations from geodesic motion.

An example of such a special case is the
FLRW cosmic fluid
, where non-relativistic matter is modelled as pressureless dust.
34
34

34

It is important to note that the FLRW framework is not limited to a single “dust” component, but it allows the total cosmic fluid to be a mixture of several components, each with its own equation of state. These include non-relativistic matter (e.g., cold dark matter or baryonic matter), radiation, and dark energy.
In the FLRW model, the geodesic motion of fluid elements applies only to pressureless dust (non-relativistic matter). Relativistic components like radiation or dark energy exhibit pressure/stress terms in their energy-momentum tensors, causing deviations from geodesic motion of the fluid. Individual photons, however, always follow null geodesics in GR, independent of the fluid description.

For the cosmic fluid, the energy–momentum tensor is given by

T^{ab}=\rho\,u^{a}u^{b},

(8)

\rho>0

u^{a}

The absence of pressure terms ensures that:

Furthermore, as seen above in the cosmological case, derivations that assume geodesic motion for extended bodies often require significant symmetry assumptions.

According to this strategy, geodesics represent singular trajectories in vacuum spacetime, where the stress-energy content of bodies is ‘

In this way, they reconciled the fact that the geodesic equation is not independent on the EFEs (as per the geodesic theorem) with the claim that backreacting bodies likewise follows geodesic motion.
In particular, by adopting this strategy, it is possible to construct a formal proof to derive geodesic motion even for bodies with significant mass and not only for test particles.
This kind of proof is commonly referred to as the

In more detail, the procedure is as follows:

•

One firstly excises the singular curves from the spacetime manifold.

•

Once excised, these curves no longer contribute to the source terms in the EFEs. The bodies are then ‘confined’ to these curves, placing them ”outside” the spacetime manifold and thus eliminating their role as sources in the EFEs, becoming for all practical purposes as
test
bodies. However, differently from test bodies, the stress-energy content is not ‘thrown away’
a priori
, but managed in such a way that it is not taken into account (a similar move is adopted in

•

These excised curves and bodies are then reintroduced and they align with the geodesics of the vacuum solutions in the reconstructed spacetime.

As stated in

Thus, the geodesic principle not only does not derive logically from the EFEs, but an evolution that is precisely geodesic would typically contravene the EFEs in cases involving non-negligible massive bodies.

From the discussion conducted, it can therefore be concluded that the geodesic principle (or theorem) applies

Since every realistic object has finite spatial extension—including those approximated as “test” bodies—the notion of geodesics is best regarded as a

Accordingly, in the next section I shall argue that if the locally inertial motion is defined as geodesic motion (that is, the motion along a geodesic in a Fermi normal inertial frame), then the geodesic, inertial motion cannot be the

5
Natural Motion is Not Inertial Motion: The Geodesic Deviation

The journey thus far has highlighted the difficulties in defining inertial motion from both classical and relativistic perspectives—ultimately revealing the role of geodesics in the real world as, at best, a
useful fantasy
. What remains? How can we meaningfully speak of inertial motion for realistic material bodies? At the terminus of our main road, a side lane emerges that separates
natural motion
from
inertial motion
. Since Aristotle profound distinction between natural and forced motion, these notions have been treated as two sides of the same coin—natural motion being inertial, and vice versa—but I propose that they instead be distinguished.

In this section I argue that the quest to define the “inertial motion of real bodies” is misconceived. If one begins with an erroneous question, one should not be surprised to find no satisfactory answer. What does make sense is to speak, on the one hand, of the
natural motion of real bodies
, and on the other, of the
inertial motion of fictitious bodies
.

If—in line with §
3
and §
4
—one identifies inertial motion with the local geodesic motion of test particles, one must then ask: what is the
natural
motion of bodies that are both extended and backreacting? The side lane leads to the notion of
geodesic deviation motion
.

I now introduce a crucial working hypothesis. For the present paper, I restrict attention to bodies that are
extended
but
non‑backreacting
, which I term
quasi‑realistic bodies
. The goal is to define a notion of natural motion solely for these bodies; the more complicated case of genuine backreaction will be addressed in more details in subsequent work. As in any long voyage, pausing at intermediate waypoints clarifies the path forward.

Accordingly, the remainder of this section is organised as follows:

In §
5.1
I introduce the standard geodesic deviation formalism, which comprehensively formalises the dynamics of extended but not backreacting bodies: what are called here ‘quasi-realistic bodies’.

In §
5.2
I sketch how this formalism might be modified to include backreaction effects—thus addressing the true case of natural motion, which however will be analysed in more details elsewhere.

In §
5.3
I present a cosmological example that illustrates and supports the distinction between inertial and natural motion.

5.1
Extended
Test
Body: Tidal Effects

\xi^{a}

As already stressed in §

Conversely, in a flat spacetime, one can introduce a reference frame that locally mimics the presence of an arbitrary gravitational field. Within such a frame, the
apparent

If two freely falling point-like test particles carry (massless and infinitesimal, idealised) accelerometers, their accelerometers would read
zero
individually (they are in free fall).
However, each of them would measure a (relative) acceleration caused by spacetime curvature encoded by the Riemann tensor.
Crucially, the relative acceleration (expressed here in some coordinates for ease of understanding)
A^{\mu}:=\frac{D^{2}\xi^{\mu}}{D\tau^{2}}
is ‘intrinsic’ to spacetime curvature and it is measured
by comparing the motion of the geodesics of the couple with respect to the local inertial frame established along one of them
.
Relative acceleration is intrinsic in the sense that (per equation (
13
)) reflects only the intrinsic curvature. Intrinsic curvature in GR is captured by the Riemann tensor and refers to the curvature that is determined entirely by measurements made within the manifold itself—without any reference to how the manifold might be embedded in a higher-dimensional space.
The Riemann tensor that cannot be cancelled at a point or along a geodesic by choosing an appropriate reference frame, thus the effects of intrinsic curvature in terms of the relative acceleration of locally inertial systems along geodesics are also present in a locally inertial reference frame.
Thus, the
relative

In other words, if one attaches a local inertial frame to one of the geodesics, the second covariant derivative of the separation vector gives the acceleration of the second geodesic relative to that first one.

The geodesic deviation formalism underpins our understanding of tidal effects in gravitational fields, as it links local geometric properties of spacetime with observable phenomena such as the stretching and squeezing experienced by extended test bodies in a gravitational field: for example the gravitational wave strain detected by interferometers like LIGO
(
Abbott et al. 2016

For extended

In relativity, the concept of a rigid body is fundamentally unviable because any instantaneous transmission of force across an object would require signals to travel faster than light. Thus, special relativistic frameworks often invoke the concept of
Born rigidity
, a much more restrictive condition that only applies to very specific motions and which requires that internal structure of an object remains unchanged under motion in its own frame. In GR, Born rigidity becomes even more problematic to maintain due to tidal effects. For discussions see
Giulini 2006

Using a perturbative approach, one can also describe the extended structure of the body using a
multipole expansion
of its stress-energy tensor. This is much like how we describe extended charge distributions in electrodynamics. The lowest order (monopole) gives the total mass-energy of the body; the next order (dipole) does not contribute to gravitational radiation since I am assuming
zero spin

Alternatively, at the ‘collective level’ of the whole extended body, the effects of the geodesic deviation are encoded by the ‘
quadrupole tidal force

F^{a}_{\text{tidal}}\propto-\nabla^{a}R_{bcde}Q^{bcde}

is the

The quadrupole encodes the body’s

generalised
Mathisson-Papapetrou-Dixon formalism, truncated at quadrupole order.
Assuming zero spin

\frac{Dp^{a}}{D\tau}=F^{a}_{\text{tidal}}
(see fn.
22

\frac{Dp^{a}}{D\tau}=0

To clarify, at the dynamical level, the geodesic deviation equation specifies the

within

(via

5.2
A Sketch For Extended and
Backreacting
Body: Self-Force Effects

When one considers an extended, spinless body whose gravitational backreaction on the surrounding spacetime is non‑negligible, the simple picture provided by the standard geodesic deviation must be refined. The trajectory of such a body is governed not only by its internal multipolar structure—arising from its finite size—but also by the way its own stress–energy perturbs the geometry through which it moves.

In particular, two key complications arise which are known as
Self-Force effects
. These effects, often relevant in the context of
extreme mass-ratio inspirals

(
Barack 2009
;
Amaro-Seoane 2018
)
, introduce additional contributions to the geodesic deviation equation, arising from:

Metric perturbations

: the body’s own gravitational field induces a perturbation
h_{ab}
on an assumed background metric
g^{0}_{ab}
.

Tail effects

: the gravitational field propagates in a curved spacetime in a non-local manner (the introduction of these effects is due to the work of
DeWitt and Brehme 1960
).

Once self-force effects are included, the relative acceleration between nearby geodesics receives extra contributions that depend on the body’s own gravitational field propagation.

These phenomena are treated perturbatively within the
MiSaTaQuWa
formalism after Mino, Sasaki, Tanaka, Quinn, and Wald
(
Mino et al. 1997
;
Quinn and Wald 1997
)
. The spacetime metric is split into a background and a perturbation sourced by the body’s stress-energy tensor:

g_{ab}=g^{0}_{ab}+h_{ab}.

(16)

This perturbative formalism assumes
h_{ab}<<g^{0}_{ab}
and truncates multipole expansions at quadrupole order, avoiding the intractable non-linearities of the full EFEs. In fact, an exact approach is mathematically infeasible for general matter distributions and is only tractable under strong symmetry assumptions or in special cases.

Within this framework, the collective dynamics of the body are governed by a generalised Mathisson–Papapetrou–Dixon system, incorporating both:

F^{a}_{\mathrm{tidal}}\;\propto\;-\,\nabla^{a}R_{bcde}\;Q^{bcde},\quad F^{a}_{\mathrm{self}}\;\propto\;-\,\nabla^{a}h^{\mathrm{tail,R}}_{ab},

(17)

where
Q^{bcde}
is the quadrupole tensor of the body’s stress–energy distribution, and
h^{\mathrm{tail,R}}_{ab}
is the regularised tail part of the metric perturbation, obtained via a Green’s function approach that removes divergences
(
Dixon 1970
;
Poisson 2004
;
Pound et al. 2020
;
Mathews et al. 2022
)
.
51
51

51

This formalism underpins gravitational–wave modelling for missions such as
LISA
gravitational-wave modelling, in which tail effects produce measurable phase shifts in the observed waveform.

Crucially, the self–force depends on these
non‑local tail terms
, which rely on the body’s entire past history.
A wave emitted by the body at some earlier time (given a foliation) can get scattered, then act back on the body at a later time.
This ‘echo’ of the body’s past gravitational activity, returning to influence it, is called the ‘
tail effect
’.
These terms arise because in curved spacetime the retarded Green’s function has support
inside
the light cone, allowing the body to ‘feel’ the influence of its past gravitational field
(
Caldwell 1993
)
.
In other words, unlike flat spacetime, where interactions propagate strictly
on
the light cone, in curved spacetime waves can also propagate
inside
the light cone, giving rise to genuinely non‑local dynamics.
Think of it like a body ”feeling its own field” after it has scattered off the background.

5.3
A cosmological example for the natural motion

Cosmology provides a particularly illuminating context in which to distinguish
natural
from
inertial
(geodesic) motion.
Unlike terrestrial examples—falling apples, fictitious lifts in
gedankenexperiment
, or objects resting on tables, which introduce complicated frictional and environmental forces—the large-scale universe offers systems whose only significant interaction is gravity.2

Consider dark matter, often modelled as a
dark fluid
, whose constituent particles interact (to very high accuracy) only gravitationally.
What is the
natural
motion of dark matter? Clearly, it is determined by gravity, yet it is
not
geodesic in any physically realistic sense. Dark matter forms filaments, halos, aggregates, it is influenced (gravitationally) by the surrounding matter and the (ever-present) gravitational field
(
Navarro et al. 1996
;
Bertone and Hooper 2018
)
.

(

In fact, remember that there is no such thing as ’gravity-free spacetime’ out there.

One might object that while it is true that on a galactic scale dark matter aggregates around the visible matter of a galaxy forming a dark halo, it is also true that on cosmological scales dark matter follows the
Hubble flow
, which is the
geodesic motion
of the homogeneous and isotropic dust fluid of the perfectly homogeneous and isotropic Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) model.

53

53

This flow describes the large-scale motion of matter, driven by the expansion of spacetime itself. More formally, Hubble flow is the component of recessional velocity of matter due to the expansion, separating it from peculiar velocities caused by local gravitational interactions
(
Ryden 2016

Bamonti and Thébault 2024

.

A closer look reveals that this objection assumes an approximation of
perfect
homogeneity and isotropy, which the real universe only approximately satisfies.

Nothing
in the universe follows the Hubble flow because, as already explained, nothing in the universe can follow a geodesic motion.

This is a useful fantasy
(see also
Bamonti and Thébault 2024
on this point).

In reality, dark matter—and indeed all matter—
deviates
from perfect geodesic motion because the universe contains inhomogeneities and anisotropies
at all scales
. Structure formation proceeds through the growth of small
perturbations
, leading to the cosmic web of filaments and voids. These perturbations break the smooth congruence of FLRW geodesics and induce relative accelerations governed by the geodesic deviation equation (and its generalisations with backreaction) rather than by the geodesic equation alone.

54

Thus, the “natural motion” of dark matter is its relative motion within the perturbed spacetime, determined by tidal forces and self‑gravitational backreaction. This example vividly supports the thesis that
natural
motion is governed by geodesic deviation (plus backreaction terms), whereas
inertial
motion, in the strict geodesic sense, remains a useful but ultimately unrealisable idealisation.

6
Conclusion

Our journey ends here, for now. On the 2,000-year-old road to inertia, I have explored only a few passages alredy marked by the ideas of Newton and Einstein, and then begun to walk down a hitherto unexplored side lane.

I have led you in a direction where inertial motion and natural motion represent two different concepts, formalised by different equations of motion: geodesic vs. (generalised) geodesic deviation motion, respectively.

This result is not intended to be the end point of the long road to understanding inertia, but the results obtained may be useful in illuminating an uphill road that is difficult to traverse and full of side roads yet to be explored.

In summary, my investigation reveals that the standard picture of inertial motion—as exemplified by geodesic motion—is, at best, a “useful fantasy” when applied to realistic, extended bodies.
The analysis shows that while the test particle approximation serves as a mathematically tractable tool, it fails to capture the complex backreaction effects and internal structure inherent to real physical systems. Consequently, the longstanding identification of inertial and natural motion needs to be revisited.
By disentangling these concepts and formalising natural motion via generalised deviation equations, I removed the circularity and triviality that afflict conventional definitions of inertia.
This perspective not only clarifies the status of the Equivalence Principle as an operational approximation, but also suggests new directions for unifying gravity with natural, rather than inertial, motion

Future research might extend this framework to field theories: what does it mean for a field configuration, governed by Euler–Lagrange equations, to “move naturally” rather than “inertially”? How can we formally distinguish between inertial and natural motion within a field-theoretic framework? How do backreaction and multipolar structure influence field dynamics?
Addressing these questions could lead to a deeper understanding of the interplay between symmetry and dynamics.

Additionally, it would be intriguing to extend the conceptual framework introduced in this paper to explore its applicability in the context of gravitational wave physics and Newton-Cartan theories and other (non) relativistic alternative theories of gravity
(
Beltrán Jiménez et al. 2019
;
Wolf et al. 2024
)
.

Ultimately, my invitation is to acknowledge the limitations of our traditional concepts and to explore new models that more accurately reflect the nuances of physical reality. By doing so, we can bridge the gap between elegant theoretical constructs and the messy complexity of the real world.
