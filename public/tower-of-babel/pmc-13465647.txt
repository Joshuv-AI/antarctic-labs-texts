Ethical challenges in aesthetic dentistry: a qualitative exploration
Authors: İlkim Karakapıcı, Nüket Örnek Büken
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe rapid expansion of aesthetic dentistry has intensified ethical tensions between professional obligations and patient demands shaped by consumer culture and social media. This conflict is further exacerbated by market-driven healthcare services. Although ethical discussions on aesthetic dentistry are largely theoretical, empirical evidence on everyday ethical dilemmas in clinical practice remains limited. This study aimed to explore ethical problems in aesthetic dentistry, dentists’ attitudes toward these challenges.MethodsA qualitative research design was adopted, utilizing in-depth semi-structured interviews with 19 dentists—including both specialists from various disciplines and general practitioners—in Ankara, Turkey. Participants were selected through purposive and snowball sampling methods. The data were analyzed using an inductive thematic analysis approach, with the entire systematic process managed and organized via MAXQDA Analytics Pro 2024.ResultsThematic analysis identified four main themes: Ethical Responsibility, Perceptions Regarding Aesthetic Dentistry, Transition from Healthcare Provision to Commercial Practices, and Reflections of Systemic Dynamics on Dental Service Delivery. Each of these themes consisted of several subthemes.ConclusionIn conclusion, this study identifies that the primary ethical tension in aesthetic dentistry stems from the interaction between non-maleficence and autonomy. The findings highlight the emergence of a potentially vulnerable patient profile and suggest that increasing commercialization—particularly within the context of health tourism—poses significant ethical and professional challenges; therefore, these issues may require more extensive discussion, as well as the development of more comprehensive ethical codes and policies in the field.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-026-01478-4.Keywords: Aesthetic dentistry, Ethical issues, Overtreatment, Commercialization, Marketing

Background

Aesthetic dentistry has become increasingly popular within dental practice in recent years and it is characterized by a growing demand from patients. Advancements in technology, dental materials, and clinical procedures have facilitated the more frequent implementation of elective interventions such as aesthetic dentistry [1]. However, behind the desire to have a “perfect” smile lies a complex structure that includes the interplay of biology, culture and society [2]. Oral aesthetics has been linked to increased self-confidence, social popularity, enhanced career opportunities, academic success, and improved social competencies [3].

Furthermore, the appearance of the mouth and teeth is considered an aspect of the body used by society in evaluating individuals [4]. Media, particularly social media, are recognized as key contributors to the rising demand for aesthetic dentistry. Research indicates that most dental practitioners perceive social media as a major factor influencing patients’ interests, with many patients requesting some treatments because they are trending online [1]. These factors render the growing prominence of aesthetic dentistry understandable.

The role of aesthetic procedures in general dentistry remains a subject of ongoing debate and critical scrutiny within the dental profession. Holden evaluates the integration of the cosmetic concept within professional dental practice, discussing how Foucault’s concept of ‘care of the self’ has normalized cosmetic interventions by incorporating self-improvement into the definition of health. Drawing on the World Health Organization’s (WHO) definition of oral health and the World Dental Federation’s (FDI) recognition of oral health as a fundamental component of both physical and mental well-being, Holden suggests that smiling is increasingly recognized as a component of oral health [4]. It is no longer meaningful to claim that the rapidly evolving field of cosmetic dentistry is not among the professional aims of dentistry [4].

However, following this acknowledgement, it is necessary to discuss the ethical concerns and potential risks associated with aesthetic dentistry. There are inherent characteristics of aesthetic dentistry, such as the debate over its medical necessity and its elective nature that needs clarification [5]. These issues can be examined through established bioethical frameworks, particularly the principles of autonomy, nonmaleficence, and professionalism. In this context, autonomously requested aesthetic dental procedures that may simultaneously cause harm to the patient may create a conflict between the principles of autonomy and nonmaleficence [6]. Moreover, increasing patient demands place pressure on dentists, challenging their professional responsibility to act in the patient’s best interest and adhere to standards of professionalism. The growing commercialization of dentistry is also closely linked to aesthetic dentistry. This shift reflects the broader commercialization of healthcare, where market dynamics may influence clinical decision-making. In a competitive market, unethical practices such as producing misleading content on social media or engaging in overtreatment—defined as unnecessary dental procedures— are increasingly debated, particularly in relation to violations of nonmaleficence [5]. It has further been suggested that this commercialization may undermine the professional integrity of dentistry and weaken trust in the profession [6].

The aim of this qualitative study is to identify the ethical issues in the field of aesthetic dentistry and to explore the underlying causal factors contributing to their emergence. The existing literature largely consists of theoretical debates and professional opinions; therefore, this study seeks to contribute to the field through empirical research.

Method

Study design

A qualitative research design was chosen because the aim of this study was to explore dentists’ ethical issues in aesthetic dentistry in depth. This approach enabled the researchers to capture participants’ experiences, perspectives, and contextual factors shaping these ethical issues. The study is based on an interpretivist paradigm. This paradigm assumes that the social world is not something to be discovered externally but is constructed by individuals through experiences, interactions, and meaningful social actions. Rather than aiming to produce generalizable conclusions, our study sought to understand the context and mechanisms underlying various experiences [7]. Based on this paradigm, an exploratory qualitative design was adopted. The study conformed to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Hacettepe University Non Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Board. (Project No: GO 22/1208). To ensure the methodological integrity of the study, the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) was followed [8].

Research team and reflexivity

This study is derived from the doctoral dissertation of İlkim Karakapıcı, a dentistry graduate and PhD candidate in medical ethics. She conducted all interviews, transcription, and coding. İlkim Karakapıcı has completed courses in qualitative research and possesses expertise in both dentistry and qualitative methods, which supported her ability to conduct the study rigorously. Her supervisor, Professor Nüket Örnek Büken, a medical doctor specializing in medical ethics, was regularly consulted throughout the study, providing active feedback during analysis and interpretation. Prior to the study, no personal relationships were established with participants. Participants were informed that the researcher was a PhD candidate investigating ethical issues in aesthetic dentistry. To ensure reflexivity, a research journal was maintained to document assumptions and potential biases, alongside reflections on the study’s progress and emerging findings. This journal provided a dedicated space for recording the researcher’s intellectual and emotional responses to the data, as well as the justifications for critical decisions made during the research journey.

Participant selection

The study sample was established through purposive sampling strategies, specifically utilizing the snowball sampling technique to reach participants with in-depth knowledge and experience in aesthetic dentistry. Since aesthetic dentistry is not a formally recognized specialty, the inclusion criteria were structured based on the participants’ clinical focus and professional orientation. Accordingly, the inclusion criteria were defined as: (1) holding a specialty or doctorate degree (or being currently enrolled in such a program) in fields directly related to aesthetics, such as Prosthodontics, Restorative Dentistry, Orthodontics, or Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery/Periodontology; (2) for general dentists, professionally identifying as an ‘aesthetic dentist’, and (3) having a minimum of two years of active clinical experience in aesthetic dental procedures. To enhance data credibility through the snowball method, each participant was required to nominate colleagues performing aesthetic dentistry procedures.

Conversely, practitioners in fields where aesthetic applications remain outside the primary clinical scope, such as Endodontics and Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology, as well as dentists who perform aesthetic procedures only as a limited part of their routine practice or provide only general dentistry services, were excluded from the study. During the recruitment process, candidates practicing in private clinics or owning their own clinics were identified as a specific group of interest. Additionally, particular emphasis was placed on contacting candidates who publicly presented themselves as aesthetic dentists through an active social media presence or online advertising. When such candidates were identified through the snowball process, they were prioritized for interview invitations; however, this characteristic was not required for inclusion. During the data collection phase, seven participants declined the invitation for an interview, and the reasons for their refusal remain unknown.

Interview guide

Based on the study aims and the relevant literature, a semi-structured interview guide consisting of 15 open-ended questions was developed for this study. The guide was not formally pilot-tested prior to data collection. The interview guide began with four questions aimed at collecting basic personal and professional information about the participants. These questions addressed the dentists’ workplace settings, years of professional experience, and whether they held a specialty qualification.

The guide then included questions exploring participants’ observations regarding aesthetic dentistry in Türkiye, such as perceived changes in patient demand, characteristics of the patient profile seeking aesthetic procedures, and potentially problematic areas within aesthetic dental practice. Participants were also asked about their views on professional promotion and whether dentists engage in advertising practices.

A further group of questions focused on the ethical dynamics of aesthetic dental practice. These questions explored how dentists respond to patient requests, how they balance patient expectations with the principle of non-maleficence, how they approach patients with unrealistic expectations or poor oral health who are primarily concerned with appearance, and how the informed consent process operates in their practice. Participants were also asked whether aesthetic procedures are typically requested by patients or recommended by dentists, and whether they use social media platforms or share before-and-after images of treatments.

Finally, a set of questions aimed to understand participants’ broader perspectives on aesthetic dentistry, including how they became involved in the field, their motivations for practicing aesthetic dentistry, and their views on the meaning and role of aesthetic dentistry within the wider field of dentistry.

Data collection

Potential participants were contacted either by telephone or through in-person visits to their workplaces. A brief explanation of the study was provided, and participants were invited to schedule an interview at a convenient time. Interviews were conducted with 20 dentists. All interviews were audio-recorded.

In one case, although an interview appointment had been arranged, the participant withdrew consent for audio recording at the time of the meeting. The researcher proceeded with the interview by taking written notes; however, this interview was later excluded from the analysis to maintain consistency in the data collection process.

With the exception of one interview conducted online due to the participant’s limited availability and preference for a remote format, all interviews were conducted face-to-face. No notable differences were observed between the online and in-person interviews in terms of the depth or content of the responses.

Each interview lasted approximately one hour. The study was limited to Ankara. Interviews generally took place at the participants’ workplaces; however, some preferred not to be interviewed at their workplace due to confidentiality concerns. In such cases, nearby quiet locations suitable for recording were used. All interviews were conducted in Turkish. No one else was present during the interviews besides the researcher and the participants.

During interviews, the researcher recorded observations of the workplace and participants in field notes. Before starting, participants were informed about the study, assured of confidentiality and anonymity, and provided informed consent, with verbal consent confirmed at the start of audio recording. Interviews were conducted individually with participants previously unknown to the researcher. No repeat interviews were conducted, and transcripts were not returned to participants. Immediately after each interview, observations were recorded and audio recording was transcribed. Transcripts and field notes were reviewed both interpretively and reflexively to guide analysis and prepare for subsequent interviews.

Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently. Interviews continued until thematic saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new codes or themes emerged from the data and similar patterns began to recur across interviews. Saturation was monitored through ongoing coding and comparison across interviews, and determined through research team consensus when no new codes emerged in consecutive interviews. This decision was also reviewed and approved by the thesis monitoring committee.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using an inductive thematic analysis approach. This systematic process was managed and organized using MAXQDA Analytics Pro 2024. Themes were generated from the data without the use of a predefined code list. This inductive, open-coding process —which also utilized in vivo codes to preserve the participants’ authentic voices and unique terminology— was conducted by taking into account the research questions, key concepts in the literature, participants’ expressions within their specific social contexts, and the researcher’s professional interpretations. The analysis moved from descriptive coding to analytical abstraction through a constant comparative method; initial codes were iteratively compared and grouped into broader sub-themes based on their conceptual similarities and underlying patterns. Explanations were recorded for each code to clarify meanings and prevent definitional drift—changes in meaning over time for the same code [9]. The findings were not returned to the participants for comment or correction, and no participant feedback was incorporated into the final analysis.

To enhance the rigour of the analysis, a collaborative coding process was employed. While the primary researcher performed the initial coding and thematic mapping, the supervisor independently cross-checked the categories. Final themes were established through a consensus-building process, where differing viewpoints were discussed and reconciled.

Data representation

The findings of the study are presented in a narrative format, structured around the identified themes and sub-themes. To illustrate and ground the findings, direct participant quotes are provided, with each participant identified by a unique code number (e.g., K-1, K-2) to ensure anonymity. The thematic descriptions include data-driven interpretations that provide context to the practitioners’ experiences. While the primary focus is on the prevailing patterns within the data, notable exceptions, divergent perspectives, or unique cases were also integrated into the findings.

The selection of representative evidence and the translation of quotes from the original language (Turkish) into English were conducted through a collaborative, consensus-based process among the authors. Quotes have been edited for clarity and brevity; omissions are indicated by ellipses in brackets […]. Care has been taken to preserve the original meaning and the participants’ intent.

This manuscript is derived from an extensive qualitative dissertation that explored the broader ethical landscape of aesthetic dentistry. Given the comprehensive nature of the original study, a purposeful and strategic selection of sub-themes and codes was undertaken for this article. These sub-themes were prioritized for their analytical significance, empirical richness, and conceptual interconnectedness, collectively providing a holistic understanding. The selection was strictly guided by the specific objective of this study. The strategically selected sub-themes and codes focus on the ethical tension between non-maleficence and autonomy, the causes of overtreatment, and marketing practices, which were identified as the most salient conflict areas during the analysis. To maintain structural consistency and evidential integrity between the dissertation and this publication, the thematic framework presented here directly adheres to the original.

Trustworthiness and rigour

Throughout the entire research process, a collaborative approach was maintained with the supervisor, involving continuous peer debriefing and investigator triangulation. The development of the semi-structured interview guide, the assessment of thematic saturation, and the coding process were initially conducted by the primary researcher and subsequently reviewed, discussed, and refined through consensus-based evaluations with the supervisor.

Furthermore, the research was subject to a formal and systematic external audit process through a Thesis Monitoring Committee at Hacettepe University. The study’s methodology, data collection procedures, and analytical progress were evaluated through six formal progress review sessions held at six-month intervals over a three-year period by a Thesis Monitoring Committee. During these sessions, the committee members—comprising independent subject-matter experts—provided critical feedback, suggested refinements, and monitored the analytical rigour of the work. Detailed progress reports were documented for each of these intervals, serving as a comprehensive audit trail. Accordingly, member checking was not employed, as credibility and dependability were ensured through these multilayered and continuously applied validation strategies.

It is necessary to define the expression ‘itinerant’ (çantacı) dentistry, as it is a specific professional construct identified within the scope of this research. In the Turkish dental community, the term ‘çantacı’ (literally ‘suitcase’ or ‘bag-carrier’ dentistry) refers to an unregistered itinerant practice model. Although the term lacks a formal definition in academic literature, it remains a prevalent concept in routine clinical discourse. This involves specialist dentists who travel between various private clinics to perform specific procedures without a formal employment contract or work permit. Although these individuals hold valid dental degrees and specialist certifications, they operate as mobile, off-the-record practitioners who receive commission-based payments and bear no long-term legal or tax responsibility for the clinic or patient follow-up.

Qualitative findings

The thematic analysis revealed four interconnected themes. The first theme, ‘Ethical Responsibility’, covers the core issues addressing aesthetic dentistry from an ethical perspective in line with the study’s primary objective; it encompasses both the participants’ general medical ethics understanding and their fundamental ethical responsibilities in professional practices as a whole. The theme ‘Perceptions regarding Aesthetic Dentistry Patients’ is designed as a supportive theme; by examining physicians’ observations of patients and the social factors driving patients toward these procedures, it helps to understand the social grounds on which ethical issues such as patient autonomy and informed consent arise.

Another primary focus of the study, the theme ‘Transition from Healthcare Provision to Commercial Practices’, focuses on commercial practices specific to aesthetic dentistry and analyzes the direct and mutual interaction between commercialization and aesthetic applications. Finally, the theme ‘Reflections of Systemic Dynamics on Dental Service Delivery’ is another supportive structure that provides the systemic background for the phenomenon of commercialization. The purpose of this theme is to clarify the position of aesthetic dentistry within this big picture by reflecting the commercialization trend in the general dental service atmosphere. Ethical responsibility

This theme explores ethical challenges in aesthetic dentistry, centering on the tension between non-maleficence and patient autonomy. It examines how practitioners navigate and justify these conflicts, particularly when determining clinical indications for elective procedures. Furthermore, in addition to these core discussion points, professional competence was addressed as it constitutes a fundamental ethical responsibility.

Non-maleficence

Dentists generally (15/19) tend to prioritize non-maleficence when it comes to aesthetic procedures. As an example of this, they adopt approaches such as preferring less invasive treatments and prioritizing the improvement of patients’ oral health and function, with aesthetics considered a secondary goal. They primarily focus on preserving patients’ health and prefer to avoid performing interventions that could cause significant harm.

“We try to meet the patient’s expectations as long as we can do so while preserving their health. But as I said, prioritizing ‘do no harm,’ if fulfilling that expectation would compromise their health baseline, we try to explain that I wouldn’t be able to do it—or that no doctor would be able to do it.” (K-10)

A few participants, when refusing to perform the procedure, tried to sincerely persuade patients by giving examples that they would not perform such procedures on their own family members.

“I say something like this to the patient: if you were my mother, father, or sibling, I wouldn’t perform this treatment on you.” (K-3)

Autonomy

In contrast to all other participants, only one prioritized the principle of autonomy over non-maleficence. This participant argued that when patients autonomously choose to undergo aesthetic procedures, they are, in effect, not choosing to remain completely healthy. They described two distinct paths, emphasizing that one must choose between aesthetics and maintaining full health.

“But this is how it is—what we call aesthetics has nothing to do with health. So, if we are naturally supposed to age, and you delay that with Botox or any other procedure, you are already overlooking health, and this is exactly the case here […] If you understand and analyze the patient’s request, and if you can truly give the patient what they want, I see no objection to performing it […] After providing the patient with all kinds of information, the decision is ultimately the patient’s.” (K-15)

A minority of participants (5/19) reported that, although they aim to avoid causing harm, they are unable to resist patients’ persistent demands. They stated that they inform patients about the potential risks of the procedures; however, if the patient remains insistent, they proceed with the requested intervention. One participant describes both the persistence of patients influenced by social media and their own decision to agree to a procedure following a patient’s intense insistence.

“In my opinion, the real harm is that what we call ‘indication’ is being completely forgotten. A patient sees it on Instagram, for example. ‘Oh, they had it done, so do it for me too,’ they say, but maybe that indication isn’t right for them at all […] patients for whom it’s absolutely not indicated just keep pushing for it […] for instance, we had a patient who didn’t have an indication for Zirconia but specifically wanted Zirconia […] They were so persistent—I mean, really pushy. They said they would take great care of it and that they just wanted something aesthetically pleasing. We said okay, let’s get [informed] consent.” (K-13)

Indication based on benefit-harm balance

Another important issue in aesthetic procedures is the process of establishing indications for treatment. While attempting to establish indications for treatment, nearly half of the participants referred to psychosocial indications. A majority of the participants (10/19) reported that aesthetic interventions can be highly beneficial for patients and contribute positively to their well-being, highlighting their ability to personally witness these improvements. However, they emphasized that patients need to be carefully evaluated in this respect. Four out of nineteen participants expressed a sense of moral ease regarding the provision of these treatments. Some participants reported refusing such procedures when they believed that the patient would not benefit psychologically and that the potential harms would outweigh the benefits. An ethical concern expressed by one participant—questioning whether patients seek aesthetic procedures based on their own autonomous will, and whether this may result in harm rather than benefit—is illustrated in the following excerpt.

“Am I doing harm while thinking I’m doing good?… You think you’re doing the very best, yet the patient isn’t satisfied or keeps coming back with new demands… with some patients, I feel like we’re in dangerous waters. It becomes difficult to know where to draw the line […] They haven’t come with their own will or based on a real need […] In fact, they’ve seen someone’s smile on social media and think it would be better if they looked like that […] And before coming to us, they should actually see a psychiatrist. In such cases, any intervention we make […] may end up serving a much greater harm rather than doing good.” (K-5)

Professional competence

Ensuring professional competence in terms of knowledge and skills is inherently linked to the principle of non-maleficence, as continuous self-improvement is essential to avoid patient harm. In this regard, a few participants highlighted that a lack of professional development regarding current aesthetic techniques constitutes a cause of overtreatment.

“Yes, but this is also about whether the practitioner follows current treatment protocols or contemporary techniques. […] A dentist who has never even performed a laminate—or whose laminate veneer has never actually had the chance to fall off—claims, ‘These fall off, so I do zirconia instead.’ No, the truth is, the person has never done laminates and doesn’t know how to. Consequently, they perform excessive tooth reduction.” (K-19).

Perceptions regarding aesthetic dentistry patients

This supportive theme focuses on patients’ perspectives on treatment, and their underlying expectations. By examining these patient-centered factors, the findings offer a more comprehensive understanding of the ethical landscape beyond purely clinical observations.

Patient expectations

A significant majority of the participants (12/19) emphasized a substantial rise in patients’ aesthetic expectations, noting that these often lead to communication challenges when expectations become unrealistic. Among these, nine participants specifically noted that social media platforms significantly heighten patient expectations, often contributing to the development of unattainable aesthetic goals. Furthermore, 15 out of 19 participants identified social media/ media as the most prominent factors driving the increased demand for aesthetic procedures.

“There’s a very demanding group of patients whom I explain in great detail that their own teeth wouldn’t look like that and that arranging their own teeth won’t result in that appearance, but I still can’t convince them.” (K-14)

Another key topic, emphasized by nearly half of the participants (11/19), was the patients’ desire for rapid results and their preference for completing the treatment in the shortest possible timeframe. One respondent recounted a scenario in which a treatment was performed, triggered by the patient’s pressure and the explicit threat of consulting a competing clinic.

“Some patients [say], ‘Is it supposed to take this long? No, I can’t wait,’ and so on. For instance, I explained everything and we started her orthodontic treatment […] three months passed. But the progress felt too slow for her; she wanted a rapid change. She had her braces removed (expression of surprise). She said, ‘Doctor, remove them, or I’m leaving.’ So I removed them and crowned the teeth.” (K-4)

Health literacy

Seven respondents reported that patients frequently place a higher priority on aesthetic outcomes than on oral hygiene, showing a lack of appreciation for the biological value of their teeth.

“The patient doesn’t really know. They just think, ‘It’s just a tooth, what’s the harm?’ Actually, my own teeth were very crooked. When I was in high school, I absolutely didn’t want braces […] As a teenager, I was like, ‘I’ll just pull my very crooked tooth and get a crown.’ That’s what I thought in high school. If I had gone anywhere back then and they just said, ‘Sure, we’ll do it,’ and put it in, I would have sat there happy, gotten it done, and left. Later, I would have regretted it a lot.” (K-18)

Moreover, several participants observed that basic cleaning procedures achieved patient satisfaction even when invasive treatments were initially sought. Conversely, one practitioner recounted experiences with patients whose primary motivation was cosmetic, despite a significant lack of awareness regarding their basic dental requirements.

“There are too many processes that need to be our priority. For example, teeth that require root canals, teeth that need fillings, deep cavities, wisdom teeth that need extraction.… like tartar removal. Some patients don’t want tartar removal but want whitening instead. Often, instead of addressing urgent or necessary treatments first, they come with aesthetic concerns as the priority […] In these cases, the only thing we can do is try to establish proper communication—explain to the patient why the other treatments are more important and what might happen if those aren’t prioritized. […] In the end, it’s the patient’s call; we just end up doing whatever they want.” (K-8)

Transition from healthcare provision to commercial practices

Focusing on the shift toward commercial practices in aesthetic dentistry, this theme explores how ethical breaches often emerge as cases of overtreatment within an increasingly market-driven healthcare environment. Additionally, it incorporates an analysis of advertising and marketing strategies as key components of this commercialized landscape.

“Money enters the picture”

A significant majority of participants highlighted the nexus between aesthetic procedures and the increasing commercialization of dentistry. Aesthetic dentistry was frequently characterized as a highly lucrative avenue for revenue generation within the profession.

“Basically, in aesthetics, most people approach it with the mentality of ‘let me just do a full mouth cases and get paid.’” (K-1)

Furthermore, an academic participant described how commercialization, through the medium of aesthetic dentistry, shapes the professional outlook and aspirations of the next generation.

“However, the younger generation’s perspective on the profession is not like mine […] they don’t think about contributing to the patients’ psychological well-being. There is a massive market for this, and quite frankly, they want to learn it because it is highly profitable […] But we find it difficult to instill in the new generation that being a doctor is not about commerce. This is because aesthetics has a commercial foundation, and when utilized as such, it is highly lucrative.” (K-5)

Six participants with prior experience in private clinics disclosed that they had performed procedures they deemed clinically unnecessary due to an inability to dissent from their employers. These participants acknowledged that they felt compelled to prioritize administrative mandates over their own clinical judgment. One participant illustrated the compromise between clinical ethics and commercial survival by describing a ‘forced’ satisfaction of all parties involved:

“Even if I don’t want to meet [the patient’s expectations], the clinic owner questions me, asking, ‘Why didn’t you do it? Why didn’t you perform that procedure for the patient?’ I am pushed into a situation where I feel obligated to comply. It makes it a bit harder to survive in the market […] I’m making an effort to satisfy the employer’s expectations; therefore, I try not to lose patients. […] I aim for a middle ground where I perform the least invasive procedure possible while still satisfying the request. Then the clinic owner is happy, I am happy, the patient is happy […] but if it were up to me, I wouldn’t even do that.” (K-12)

Two participants who own clinics rationalized their participation in these procedures by asserting that patients were already resolute in their decision. They argued that their refusal would only result in the patient consulting a competitor, thus using this ‘inevitability’ to rationalize their actions.

“If someone has their mind set on it, they will absolutely get it done. Let me tell you a story: I had just opened my private practice. Two young girls came to see me. I spent hours talking to them about why they shouldn’t have their teeth cut or prepared. Two weeks later, I saw them at a mall, and both of them had already had their teeth done by someone else.” (K-15)

Moreover, one respondent highlighted how patient autonomy is used as a justification to prioritize profit over clinical utility. They stated that practitioners may perform aesthetic treatments under the guise of honoring the patient’s will, while the underlying motive is purely financial.

“It’s not about aesthetic value, but rather: ‘The patient is fine with it, so let’s do it, earn the money, and be done with it.’ It’s essentially just ‘getting it over with’.” (K-14)

“Selling the treatment”

A significant proportion of participants (9/19) observed that aesthetic dental treatments are actively promoted to patients by practitioners. This phenomenon is distinguished from professional recommendations based on clinical beneficence; rather, it entails influencing patients by inducing a perceived ‘deficiency’ or ‘aesthetic flaw.’ One respondent explicitly characterized this practice as ‘selling the treatment.’

" I think that currently, many aesthetic treatments in Turkey are simply being ‘sold’. […] ‘Selling the treatment’ is when you visit me for, say, a tartar cleaning, and I suggest, ‘We should do laminates, it would look great.’ No, that’s not how it should be; the patient must be the one to request that. […] Do practitioners promote it? Certainly. I know ‘angelic-looking’ salespeople who are incredibly skilled at what they do.” (K-20)

Furthermore, participants described certain techniques specific to aesthetic dentistry that function as subtle marketing strategies or methods of influence. Although these are not framed as overt advertising, they are identified as methods that facilitate the ‘selling’ of aesthetic procedures to patients. These strategies encompass the strategic selection of clinic locations (such as opening clinics in affluent neighborhoods or high-rise skyscraper plazas), interior design aesthetics, and the creation of a ‘warm’ or ‘intimate’ clinical ambiance to shape patient perceptions. Furthermore, the ‘presentability’ of the dentist—including the display of their personal appearance, lifestyle, affluence, and even their own smiles—serves as a core marketing tool. Additionally, participants noted the use of evocative naming strategies for aesthetic procedures designed to stimulate a consumerist impulse in patients. Examples of these strategies, supported by patient quotes, are provided below.

“There is no such department as ‘Aesthetic Dentistry’ in the faculties; this is a bit of populism, maybe a bit of a literal translation […] but I’m aware that such a trend and expectation has emerged. More accurately, let’s call it a market, I mean […] to put it bluntly, I guess ‘winning over’ patients happens with this title. It might be creating a perception for the patient that it’s a different specialty from a regular dentist. The patient might be coming with the thought, the belief, that such specific training has been received […] Generally, on Instagram profiles of dentists who do this, I see things like expensive watches, luxurious places, confident postures, and that kind of puts me off a bit.” (K-11)

“For someone in this profession, having poor dental aesthetics is not viewed favorably by patients. It is akin to consulting an overweight dietitian or visiting a hairdresser whose own hair is in terrible condition.” (K-7)

“I think many of my colleagues who focus on aesthetics are moving away from being dentists. They behave more like aestheticians or aesthetic technicians and continue with a business-oriented mindset. When we talk about the importance of the white coat and the professional oath—first, do no harm, show respect, etc. we’re trying to instill a very heavy responsibility. But when aesthetics enters the picture, it seems to a bit messy. Most of my colleagues working in aesthetics don’t even wear a white coat.” (K-5)

“I mean, I don’t know. ‘Smile design,’ ‘Hollywood smile’… It feels a bit overhyped to me, but I use them too. I say, ‘Let’s do a smile design.’ To be honest, I use these terms because that’s how the patient understands what is going to be done. Among us doctors, when we say ‘smile design,’ it might feel like marketing fluff. For a while—and it probably still is—composite laminates were referred to as ‘bonding.’ I used to think, ‘What does bonding even mean?’ It doesn’t really make sense; it’s not logical. But a patient can say, ‘I had bonding done.’ In reality, what they had was perhaps just an aesthetic filling, but calling it ‘bonding’ sounds more attractive to them. Yes, such things do exist.” (K-9)

Advertising and social media

Nearly half of the participants (10/19) observed that dentists engage in advertising, while four respondents stated that they perceived no ethical breach in advertising. For the majority of participants, the concept of ‘advertising’ was synonymous with sharing ‘before-and-after’ clinical photographs on social media. However, the form of advertising described by one participant serves as an intriguing example of direct commercial campaigning.

“This is even worse: those who say, ‘I’m running a New Year’s campaign,’ like offering a free scaling to anyone who gets teeth whitening. There are clinics that reduce the profession almost to the level of a lottery or a raffle. I find this even more distasteful […] Exactly—campaigns for implants, campaigns for prosthetics… I’ve even heard advertisements for them on the radio.” (K-18)

The same respondent, along with three other participants, argued that before-and-after photographs shared on social media are frequently digitally manipulated, asserting that such altered images constitute misleading content by distorting clinical reality.

“Yes, they use Photoshop extensively; there are even Telegram groups dedicated to these Photoshop techniques—teaching how to edit photos […]. Patients are unable to discern the difference anyway. They assume that what they see is directly applicable to their own case, exactly as it appears.” (K-18)

Furthermore, two clinic-owning participants argued that economic survival in the contemporary dental market is contingent upon advertising. These individuals admitted to advertising reluctantly, framing it as an unavoidable necessity for professional survival.

“The world is heading in that direction now; they will do it—everyone will. Those who don’t will be wiped out from the market […] No matter how good your work is, if you’re not present in that dimension of the business, you will simply disappear.” (K-4)

Reflections of systemic dynamics on dental service delivery

The secondary supporting theme focuses on broader systemic issues, such as the over-commercialization of dental health service delivery, rather than being limited to aesthetic dentistry alone. While this theme is not elaborated in detail within this study, it provides a critical framework for evaluating aesthetic practices. This theme addresses only health tourism, which we believe yields significant findings.

Structural conditions of dental service delivery

Fulfilling insistent patient demands, when it occurs in the context of health tourism, represents a complete violation of the principle of non-maleficence and demonstrates that only patient autonomy is being considered, as expressed by a few participants. One participant underscored that the rigid and pre-determined demands of patients often render professional clinical guidance ineffective.

“For example, for dentists working in health tourism by the seaside, both Turkish and foreign patients are extremely demanding. Clinics attract even Turkish patients because of the full-mouth anterior procedures they perform for foreign patients. As a result, patients in such settings are very clear and insistent about what they want. Talking to them is basically meaningless. There’s no point in trying to explain what would be better for them.” (K-12).

Additionally, it was observed that two participants described health tourism as a ‘factory-style’ process.

“They apply the same treatment to everyone, turning it into a factory-like operation.” (K-20)

Ethical issues arising from systemic problems

Within this systemic framework, the most direct link to aesthetic dentistry is a few participants’ contention that the health tourism model significantly erodes professional prestige.

“Our profession is being tarnished. You know, this ‘Turkey teeth’ phenomenon emerged—those unnaturally white teeth that became famous worldwide. In the past, when I went abroad, I would proudly say I was a dentist, both in general society and among colleagues. Now, at international congresses, I am ashamed to say I am a Turkish dentist. Especially as a prosthodontist, I feel this shame deeply because they look at you directly as if you are a ‘butcher’.” (K-15)

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the ethical problem areas encountered by dentists in aesthetic dentistry practices. When analyzing the ‘Ethical Responsibility’ theme, it is observed that the most prominent conflict area focuses on the delicate balance between the duty to avoid harm to patients and respect for patient autonomy. Kovács et al. [3] also noted in their study that one of the causes of recurring ethical dilemmas between oral health and patient autonomy is aesthetic procedures. The literature generally recommends the use of minimally invasive techniques, and dentists tend to regard more invasive interventions as less ethical [6]. The findings of our study are consistent with the literature; it is observed that participants prioritize the non-maleficence principle while considering the autonomous requests of the patients. Practitioners attempt to establish this balance by adopting minimally invasive approaches and positioning aesthetics as a secondary outcome of the treatment rather than its primary goal. In cases where the probability of harm to the patient was high, practitioners sometimes refused to perform the procedure; in some instances, the ‘daughter test’ was employed as a persuasive argument. The ‘daughter test’ in aesthetic dentistry prompts the practitioner to reflect briefly: Would I perform this procedure on my own daughter [10]? . In our research, this example represents the most extreme stance within the framework of the principle of non-maleficence.

On the other hand, a single participant prioritized autonomy over all other principles, arguing that the final decision must rest with the patient once they are fully informed. This perspective aligns with Glick [11], who asserts that patients are the best judges of their own interests, noting that we are now encountering patients who are more educated than ever, fully aware of their needs and desires, and have proactively researched the benefits and risks of treatments; thus, they should not be viewed as ‘gullible’. This minority view in our study represents a radical departure from traditional paternalistic approaches.

Another critical issue identified in our study regarding the tension between non-maleficence and autonomy is the attitude of participants towards persistent patient requests stemming from certain reasons. In this context, it is essential to address the supporting theme, ‘Perceptions regarding Aesthetic Dentistry Patients.’ The observations provided by the participants in our study suggest that the patient profile described by Glick [11] may not always be encountered in clinical practice. The vast majority of our participants emphasized that the profound influence of social media was a primary concern. Consistent with this observation, there is evidence indicating that dentists attribute the rapidly increasing demand for aesthetic dentistry primarily to the influence of social media [1]. In addition to the role of social media in raising patient expectations [5], research shows that television programs featuring rapid aesthetic transformations also play a significant part in heightening these expectations [12]. This challenge is further underscored by a survey from the American Academy of Esthetic Dentistry (AAED), in which dentists identified unrealistic patient expectations as the most significant hurdle they face in the practice of aesthetic dentistry [13]. A number of participants in our study provided specific examples of patients prioritizing aesthetic appearance over general oral health. The perception of the dental profession and its field is largely constructed around an emphasis on the smile. This emphasis in dentists’ advertisements and clinical settings—rather than on maintaining overall oral health—conveys to patients the impression that the primary purpose of dental treatments is to enhance their smiles [14]. Moreover, it has been argued that patients tend to adopt an outcome-oriented perspective in cosmetic procedures, often downplaying the associated risks [6]. Additionally, it is stated that the primary objective of aesthetic procedures is to provide psychological benefits by addressing patient dissatisfaction. The literature highlights a potential risk of dissatisfaction among certain vulnerable groups and raises concerns regarding whether these sensitive populations can realistically assess benefits and risks [6]. Our study also encountered cases where practitioners faced challenges in determining psychosocial indications.

In light of all the aforementioned factors, these situations, which are directly linked to patient autonomy, must be carefully evaluated by practitioners. Patient autonomy should be empowered through the provision of objective information regarding risks and potential harms. After assisting patients in making informed decisions, a balance must be struck with the obligation to act in the patient’s best interest [5].

The theme ‘Transition from Healthcare Provision to Commercial Practices’ addresses the commercial nature of aesthetic dentistry; in this context, our study identified instances of overtreatment arising from various factors. Rostamzadeh et al. [5] have noted that overtreatment represents a significant ethical concern in aesthetic dentistry. Similar to the study on overtreatment conducted by Holden et al. [15], the vast majority of participants in our study mentioned that their colleagues were focused on making money and the commercial aspect of practice. The key point we wish to emphasize here is the use of aesthetic dentistry as a means to legitimize the prioritization of commercial profit.

One participant illustrated how patient autonomy is misused to justify overtreatment for financial gain. A minority avoided professional responsibility by arguing ‘someone else will do it anyway,’ framing the choice as the patient’s alone. However, the FDI explicitly states that when a dentist accepts a patient’s request based on the rationale that the patient would otherwise seek treatment elsewhere, it constitutes a financial decision rather than a clinical one based on health concerns [16].

Nearly half of the participants in our study reported instances in which aesthetic procedures are offered to patients who originally visited the dentist for reasons unrelated to aesthetic treatment. The ethical concerns highlighted by Rostamzadeh et al. [5] regarding “the exploitation of patients’ psychological vulnerabilities for commercial gain” appear to be substantiated by the examples observed in our study. Another point highlighted in the study by Holden et al. [15] is the pressure experienced in the workplace and the influence of financial incentives. Some participants reported feeling compelled to perform treatments they deemed unnecessary. In addition to attempting to meet patient expectations and the difficulty of refusing their requests, a lack of specialized knowledge or professional competence is also identified in the literature as an underlying cause of overtreatment [5, 17]. We have excerpts from participants indicating that they engaged in overtreatment for these reasons. Professional inadequacy not only poses a direct risk of harm to the patient but also constitutes a failure to uphold fundamental ethical responsibilities.

It was found that the most common marketing practice was the sharing of “before–after” photographs on social media. Several participants highlighted the misleading nature of a majority of these social media posts. Consistent with these statements, previous studies have found that nearly half of dentists use before–after photographs for advertising purposes and the literature has highlighted concerns regarding misleading advertising and manipulative content [1, 5]. Social media, one of the most frequently employed marketing strategies among dentists, can indeed serve an educational purpose for patients; however, it also contributes to misinformation and misleading advertising [5]. In one study, more than half of the patient participants reported that they trusted content about dental treatments on social media and stated that they could be influenced by a dentist’s or dental clinic’s account [18]. This situation renders the use of social media as a marketing tool even more concerning.

Furthermore, it is worth noting that two participants expressed a feeling of being compelled to use social media; despite their personal reluctance, they believed that failing to engage with these platforms would jeopardize their professional market presence.

Other marketing strategies identified in our study correspond to those described by Doughty et al. [6], including “offering financing options, providing the dental team with ‘smile-makeovers,’ improving the décor of a practice, and creating sales literature. Participants also noted that aspects such as dentists’ attire and the exhibition of a “luxurious” lifestyle serve as functioned as marketing strategies. Finally, attention was drawn to the fact that cosmetic dentistry is not a formally recognized specialty and that the absence of such a status creates uncertainty [6]. Our study includes quotation indicating that this ambiguity is exploited as a marketing tool. As demonstrated in our study, patients are surrounded by multiple forms of marketing that extend well beyond social media.

The final theme, ‘Reflections of Systemic Dynamics on Dental Service Delivery’, centers on the evolving nature of dentistry within the framework of health tourism. The transformation of dental treatments from a fundamental healthcare service into a commercial commodity poses a significant threat to the social contract between the dental profession and society. In a paradigm where the patient is reduced to a consumer seeking the lowest-cost product and the dentist acts as a seller driven by profit maximization, the foundational principles of ethics and professionalism are effectively marginalized [4]. In our study, several dentists emphasized that health tourism mirrors this highly commercialized landscape, illustrating the extreme end of consumer/patient autonomy in aesthetic dentistry. The transformation of healthcare into an ‘factory-style’ operation is particularly concerning, as it prioritizes standardized commercial output over individualized ethical care.

Doughty et al. [19] highlight that dental tourism in the UK has surged, with Turkey emerging as a primary destination, and note that the ‘#TurkeyTeeth’ hashtag serves as a social media conduit for both accurate and inaccurate health information regarding aesthetic treatments sought abroad. Furthermore, the authors argue that collectively placing all negative outcomes of overseas dentistry under the “#TurkeyTeeth” label is unfair and inappropriate, as it overlooks the complexity of the phenomenon. Parallel to these findings, our research includes accounts from participants who expressed that such global perceptions and overgeneralizations have led to a significant loss of professional prestige. It is crucial to avoid a reductionist approach that characterizes all dental tourism activities in Turkey through a single lens of failure. While the potential for ethical violations remains a risk in certain cases where commercial concerns take precedence, the capacity of social media to dramatize these isolated incidents and portray them as far more widespread than they truly are should not be overlooked.

At this point, the importance of ethics education in dentistry has been emphasized and to develop dedicated guidelines and ongoing training. From an ethical perspective, prioritizing the patient’s welfare, overall health, and best interests, as well as strengthening patient autonomy, is recommended because of commercialization and patient vulnerability [5].

The literature highlights ethical concerns related to social media, aggressive marketing, and commercialization in the context of aesthetic dentistry [5]. As a result, worries persist about patient safety and the diminishing professional standing of dentists [6]. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that an academic participant in our study highlighted how the new generation of dentists is significantly influenced by this climate of commercialization. As observed in some aspects of our study, market mechanisms exert a significant influence on dentists’ professional lives, and dentists often find themselves navigating the challenges of a commercial world [20, 21]. As Holden notes, aesthetic dentistry is not inherently harmful to the profession; the harm arises when it is exploited for commercialization and consumer culture [4]. One of the core values of dentistry is professional autonomy. As dental practices shift toward a market-driven model, the pressure to prioritize financial returns over clinical necessity threatens the dentist’s independent judgment. Dentists can overcome these commercial constraints and create the conditions necessary to benefit their patients only by maintaining effective self-regulation [22].

Limitations and future research directions

This study has several limitations. First, as the research was restricted to Ankara, data were not collected from Istanbul or Turkey’s coastal cities, where cosmetic dentistry is more predominantly practiced as a major component of health tourism. Second, the participants were not practitioners specializing exclusively in health tourism; instead, they provided general observations and perspectives regarding this healthcare model. Future studies could focus specifically on cosmetic health tourism in Turkey. Additionally, some areas of discussion may be underrepresented because a few dentists in Ankara who advertised most extensively declined to participate in the interviews. The sample was distributed across multiple dental specialties, resulting in relatively small subgroup sizes. This may limit the ability to draw robust comparisons between specialties. Furthermore, this study exclusively reflects the perspectives of dentists; therefore, future research incorporating patient experiences is essential to provide a more holistic understanding of the field.

Conclusion

In this study, we sought to identify the main areas of ethical concern in aesthetic dentistry. This results highlights that the most significant ethical tension in aesthetic dentistry arises from the interaction between non-maleficence and autonomy. Our findings suggest the potential for a vulnerable patient profile to emerge. Furthermore, our results suggest that increasing commercialization within aesthetic dentistry has the potential to generate significant ethical and professional challenges. This is particularly evident in the context of health tourism.

These findings highlight the need for stronger communication strategies in clinical practice to support informed consent and manage socially influenced patient expectations. At the policy level, stricter regulation of misleading marketing and social media content in aesthetic dentistry may help protect patient autonomy. Furthermore, professional guidelines should more explicitly address the ethical challenges arising from the commercialization of dentistry and health tourism, providing clearer frameworks.
