Testing Models for Angular Power Spectra: A Distribution-Free Approach
Sara Algeri, Xiangyu Zhang, Erik Floden, Hongru Zhao, Galin L. Jones, Vuk Mandic, Jesse Miller

Abstract
A novel goodness-of-fit strategy is introduced for testing models of angular power spectra with unknown parameters. Using this strategy, it is possible to assess the validity of such models without specifying the distribution of the angular power spectrum estimators. This holds under general conditions, ensuring the method’s applicability in diverse applications. Moreover, the proposed solution overcomes the need for case-by-case simulations when testing different models, leading to notable computational advantages.



.

Let
T(\widehat{\Omega},\mathbf{x})
be a sky map with
\widehat{\Omega}
being the sky direction and
\mathbf{x}
denoting other

, such as redshift or frequency bin of the observed light or GWs. For example,

may represent the number of galaxies observed over different pixels and at a given redshift, or the CMB temperature measured across the sky and at a given CMB frequency bin.
Note that
\mathbf{x}

\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}

Consider the decomposition of

given by

\displaystyle T(\widehat{\Omega},\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}a_{\ell m}(\mathbf{x})Y_{\ell m}(\widehat{\Omega})

(1)

\displaystyle a_{\ell m}(\mathbf{x})=\int T(\widehat{\Omega},\mathbf{x})Y_{\ell m}(\widehat{\Omega})d\widehat{\Omega}.

(2)

The angular power spectrum is defined as

\displaystyle C_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}|a_{\ell m}(\mathbf{x})|^{2}.

(3)

In practical applications, the sky map
T(\widehat{\Omega},\mathbf{x})
is often directly measured in the pixel basis and can then be converted into estimates
\widehat{a}_{\ell m}(\mathbf{x})

\widehat{a}_{\ell m}(\mathbf{x})
estimators at low
\ell

a_{\ell m}(\mathbf{x})

[
39

27

In any case, by replacing

\widehat{C}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})

for all
\ell=1,\dots,L
(see

Note that while
\ell=0
can be included, we choose not to do so since often one is interested only in fluctuations of
T(\widehat{\Omega},\mathbf{x})
around its mean, implying
C_{0}(\mathbf{x})=0
.

Let
\mathbf{C}^{M}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\theta})
be a theoretical model with components
C^{M}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\theta})
aiming to predict
C_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})
in (
3
). Denote with
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}(\mathbf{x})
the vector with components
\widehat{C}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})
. Once an estimate of the unknown parameter
\mathbf{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is obtained, our goal is to assess the validity of
\mathbf{C}^{M}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\theta})
based on the information carried by
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}(\mathbf{x})
and its covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}(\mathbf{x})
.

Various methods for estimating
\mathbf{C}^{M}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\theta})
have been proposed and often rely on the assumption that, for each
\mathbf{x}
fixed,
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}(\mathbf{x})
is a Gaussian random vector
[
40
,
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
.
However, while the estimates
\widehat{a}_{\ell m}(\mathbf{x})
are usually obtained by averaging large amounts of data and are asymptotically distributed as a complex multivariate Gaussian, the
\widehat{C}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})
are averages of the squares of
\widehat{a}_{\ell m}(\mathbf{x})
. It follows that their distribution is that of a generalized
\chi^{2}
whose likelihood function does not have a closed-form expression
[
1
]
and, in general, cannot be considered approximately Gaussian.
Solutions that aim to account for non-Gaussianity typically involve likelihoods that are combinations of a Gaussian and offset log-normal distribution
[
45
,
46
]
. However, those have also been shown to be unsatisfactory in some settings
[
47
]
.
Unfortunately, when the distribution of
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}(\mathbf{x})
is not adequately modeled, constructing a goodness-of-fit test for
\mathbf{C}^{M}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\theta})
is particularly challenging.
Nonetheless, such a test is required to ensure the reliability of the resulting estimates of
\mathbf{\theta}
and subsequent conclusions.

Motivated by this challenge, we discuss a novel distribution-free approach to testing theoretical models for angular power spectra. Traditionally, distribution-free goodness-of-fit tests are defined as tests that rely on test statistics with an asymptotic null distribution independent of the model or distribution being tested.
Here, however, it acquires a broader meaning: the null distribution of the test statistics used does not depend on the model. Additionally, to derive this distribution, one does not need to know the distribution of the random variables whose mean is being tested, in our case,
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}(x)
. This is made possible by reformulating the estimation problem of
\mathbf{C}^{M}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\theta})
as a regression problem and implementing the testing procedure proposed by

Khmaladze [48]
. This letter is the first work applying such a technique to enable distribution-free testing of models for the angular power spectrum.

A significant computational advantage is that a distribution-free testing solution avoids the need for case-by-case simulations when testing different models. It is, therefore, especially desirable when testing models with a complicated structure, as in the case of astrophysical anisotropic stochastic GW backgrounds or when
cross-correlating different sky maps such as galaxy count maps and GW background sky maps
[
19
,
20
,
22
,
21
]
.

This letter provides a self-contained exposition of the method in general settings.
In a companion paper
[
1
]
, we present all of the technical details for the main results, discuss their extensions, and illustrate the implementation of the methodology to study the anisotropic stochastic gravitational-wave background. Finally, while the proposed procedure is motivated by the need for distribution-free inference for autocorrelation angular power spectra, the statistical tools described here also apply to the analysis of cross-correlation angular power spectra.

II
Estimation via Generalized Least Squares

Let
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
be the vectors collecting the values of the independent variables. We assume that such values are linearly ordered so that, if
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbf{x}_{1}\leq\mathbf{x}_{2}\leq\dots\leq\mathbf{x}_{n}
. When
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
, we assume that the
D
-dimensional vectors
\mathbf{x}_{i}=(x_{i1},\dots,x_{iD})
are ordered by increasing one dimension at a time. For example, if
D=2
and
n=n_{1}\cdot n_{2}
, then:

\begin{split}\mathbf{x}_{1}&=(x_{11},x_{12}),\mathbf{x}_{2}=(x_{21},x_{12}),\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n_{1}}=(x_{n_{1}1},x_{12}),\\
&\mathbf{x}_{n_{1}+1}=(x_{11},x_{22}),\dots,\mathbf{x}_{2n_{1}}=(x_{n_{1}1},x_{22}),\text{ etc.}\end{split}

(4)

Denote with
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}
the vector of components
\widehat{C}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
, with
\ell=1,\dots,L
and
i=1,\dots,n
, i.e., if
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}^{T}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=[\widehat{C}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),\ldots,\widehat{C}_{L}(\mathbf{x}_{i})]
, then
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}
is a vector of dimension
N=L\cdot n
and defined as

\ \widehat{\mathbf{C}}=[\widehat{\mathbf{C}}^{T}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\ldots,\widehat{\mathbf{C}}^{T}(\mathbf{x}_{n})]^{T}.

(5)

For example,
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
may represent the estimated angular power spectrum up to the
L
-th multipole for the GW energy density at the GW frequency
\mathbf{x}_{i}
. Hence,
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}
collects the estimated angular power spectra at frequencies
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
.
The vector
\mathbf{C}^{M}(\mathbf{\theta})
of elements
C^{M}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{\theta})
are specified similarly.

Let
\bm{\Sigma}
be the covariance matrix of
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}
. We assume the random vectors
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
at different
\mathbf{x}_{i}
values to be independent so that, for all
\ell,\ell^{\prime}=1,\dots,L
and
i\neq i^{\prime}
,

\text{cov}\bigl(\widehat{C}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),\widehat{C}_{\ell^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x}_{i^{\prime}})\bigl)=0.

(6)

Therefore,
\bm{\Sigma}
is a block diagonal matrix with blocks
\bm{\Sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
.
We further assume that
\bm{\Sigma}
is either known exactly, well approximated, or estimated by means of a consistent estimator
[
1
]
. As an example, in the context of the anisotropic stochastic GW background, the covariance matrix for the
\widehat{a}_{\ell m}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
estimators can be estimated directly from the time-series strain data for each frequency bin
\mathbf{x}_{i}

[
39
,
24
,
27
]
and the estimators in different frequency bins are independent to a very good approximation
[
24
,
39
]
. This covariance matrix can then be used to construct the block diagonal matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
, with each block corresponding to a different GW frequency
\mathbf{x}_{i}
.

Estimating the astrophysical parameter
\mathbf{\theta}
characterizing
\mathbf{C}^{M}(\mathbf{\theta})
can be accomplished using least squares estimation.
That is, by solving

\begin{split}\widehat{\mathbf{\theta}}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{\theta}}(\widehat{\mathbf{C}}-\mathbf{C}^{M}(\mathbf{\theta}))^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\widehat{\mathbf{C}}-\mathbf{C}^{M}(\mathbf{\theta})).\end{split}

(7)

Notice that the optimization problem in (
7
), known in the statistical literature as ‘Generalized Nonlinear Least Squares’
[e.g.,
49
, Ch. 6-7]
is equivalent to using maximum likelihood for estimating
\mathbf{\theta}
when the likelihood of
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}
is approximately Gaussian
[
50
,
51
,
47
, e.g.,]
. However, the rationale behind (
7
) is simply that of minimizing the sum of the
squares of the decorrelated residuals collected in the vector

\widehat{\mathbf{\epsilon}}=[\widehat{\epsilon}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\epsilon}_{N}]^{T}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}(\widehat{\mathbf{C}}-\mathbf{C}^{M}(\widehat{\mathbf{\theta}}))

(8)

where
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}
is the inverse square-root matrix of
\bm{\Sigma}
obtained, for example, via Jordan or Schur decomposition
[
52
, e.g.,]
.

The estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{\theta}}
is consistent based on the classical theory of
M
-estimation
[
53
]
. That is, regardless of the distribution of
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}
, as
N\rightarrow\infty
,
\widehat{\mathbf{\theta}}
converges to the true value of
\theta
when
\mathbf{C}^{M}(\theta)
is correctly specified. Such a property is also necessary to enable the validity of the testing approach described in
III
(Cf.
[
1
]
for the technical details).

It follows that what is relevant to ensure the consistency of
\widehat{\mathbf{\theta}}
as an estimator of
\mathbf{\theta}
is not the Gaussianity of
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}
but rather the validity of the postulated model
\mathbf{C}^{M}(\mathbf{\theta})
. Therefore, our goal is to test the hypothesis:

H_{0}:\langle\widehat{\mathbf{C}}\rangle=\mathbf{C}^{M}(\mathbf{\theta}).

(9)

III
Distribution-free goodness-of-fit

The
N\times 1
vector of decorrelated residuals,
\widehat{\mathbf{\epsilon}}
in (
8
), allow us to quantify the discrepancy between the estimated angular power spectrum,
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}
, and the proposed model
\mathbf{C}^{M}(\mathbf{\theta})
. To test the hypothesis in (
9
), we consider the process of partial sums of the decorrelated residuals

\displaystyle w_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\widehat{\epsilon}_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\widehat{\mathbf{\epsilon}}^{T}\mathbb{I}_{t}

(10)

where
t=1,\dots,N
indexes the elements of the process and

\mathbb{I}_{t}=[\mathbb{1}_{\{1\leq t\}},\mathbb{1}_{\{2\leq t\}},\dots,\mathbb{1}_{\{N\leq t\}}]^{T}
.
Hence,

w_{{}_{N}}(1)=\frac{\widehat{\epsilon}_{1}}{\sqrt{N}},w_{{}_{N}}(2)=\frac{\widehat{\epsilon}_{1}+\widehat{\epsilon}_{2}}{\sqrt{N}},\dots,w_{{}_{N}}(N)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\widehat{\epsilon}_{k}.

(11)

A family of test statistics for (
9
) is easily constructed by
considering functionals of
w_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t)
. For example, by choosing

\begin{split}h_{\text{KS}}\bigl[w_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t)\bigl]&=\max_{t}|w_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t)|\end{split}

(12)

we obtain the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic
[
54
]
adapted to our context.

While the elements of
\widehat{\mathbf{\epsilon}}
are uncorrelated, it is possible that some form of nonlinear dependence may persist among them when
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}
is not approximately Gaussian. In most cases, however, this does not represent an impediment to deriving the limiting distribution of
w_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t)
. In particular, if the dependence is ‘local’ as is the case, for example, when the vectors

\widehat{\mathbf{C}}^{T}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
, introduced in Section
II
, are independent from one another, under
H_{0}
and as
N\rightarrow\infty
, the process
w_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t)
can be shown to converge to a Gaussian process with mean zero and covariance function approximately equal to:

\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{I}^{T}_{t}[\mathbf{I}_{N}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbf{\mu}_{j}\mathbf{\mu}^{T}_{j}\bigl]\mathbb{I}_{t^{\prime}}

(13)

with
\mathbf{I}_{{}_{N}}
denoting the
N\times N
identity matrix and the set of vectors
\{\mathbf{\mu}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
is such that:

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{\mu}^{T}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{\mu}^{T}_{p}\end{array}\right]=\left[\dot{\mathbf{C}}^{M}(\mathbf{\theta})^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\dot{\mathbf{C}}^{M}(\mathbf{\theta})\right]^{-1/2}\left[\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\dot{\mathbf{C}}^{M}(\mathbf{\theta})\right]^{T},

(14)

where
p
is the size of
\mathbf{\theta}
and
\dot{\mathbf{C}}^{M}(\mathbf{\theta})=\frac{\partial\mathbf{C}^{M}(\mathbf{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{\theta}}
.
The structure of the vectors
\mathbf{\mu}_{j}
strictly depends on the type of estimator employed (see
[
1
]
for more details).

From (
13
), it follows that the asymptotic null distribution of
w_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t)
depends on the model being tested through its covariance. As a result, one must simulate the null distribution of the desired test statistic on a case-by-case basis for each of the models being tested with Monte Carlo methods or the parametric bootstrap
2
2

2

The parametric bootstrap is a resampling method that
differs from classical Monte Carlo methods in that various samples are simulated by replacing the true value of
\mathbf{\theta}
with its estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{\theta}}

[
55
]
.
.
Unfortunately, this may be computationally expensive when testing a number of different, and possibly complicated, models.
Nevertheless, it is possible to overcome this limitation by constructing a new set of residuals and a corresponding process of partial sums, with limiting null distribution independent of
\mathbf{C}^{M}(\mathbf{\theta})
.

The main building block is the operator

U_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{q}-\frac{\langle\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b},\mathbf{q}\rangle}{1-\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle}(\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}),

(15)

originally introduced in the context of distribution-free goodness-of-fit for discrete distributions
[
56
]
. Hereafter, we refer to the map entailed by the operator
U_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
as
Khmaladze-2
(or
K2
)
transformation

3
3

3

The nomenclature
Khmaladze-2
(or
K2
)
transformation
is used to distinguish the map entailed by the operator
U_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
from the “Khmaladze transformation” or Khmaladze-1 (K1) transformation
[
57
]
.
.

Given two vectors,
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
, with
\|\mathbf{a}\|=\|\mathbf{b}\|=1
, the unitary operator
U_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
maps
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{a}
into
\mathbf{b}
,
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{b}
into
\mathbf{a}
, and leaves vectors
\mathbf{q}
orthogonal to
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
unchanged.
In our setup,
U_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
is employed to map the model-dependent vectors
\{\mathbf{\mu}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
, characterizing the covariance of the process
w_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t)
, into an arbitrarily chosen, model-independent, orthonormal set of vectors
\{\mathbf{r}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
.

By adapting the testing framework of
Khmaladze [48]
to our context, we can construct residuals with limiting null distribution depending solely on the vectors
\{\mathbf{r}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
and which is, consequently, independent of the model
\mathbf{C}^{M}(\mathbf{\theta})
being tested.
The main steps of this approach are now described.

Step 1.
Estimate
\mathbf{\theta}
via (
7
) and construct the vectors
\mathbf{\mu}_{j}
in (
14
) with
\mathbf{\theta}
replaced by
\widehat{\mathbf{\theta}}
.

Step 2.
Given an arbitrarily chosen orthonormal set
\{\mathbf{r}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
, construct a new set of vectors
\{\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
orthogonal to each
\mathbf{\mu}_{j^{\prime}}
, with
j^{\prime}<j
, by setting
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{1}=\mathbf{r}_{1}
and constructing subsequent vectors
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{j}
as

\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{j}=U_{\mathbf{\mu}_{(j-1)},\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{(j-1)}}\dots U_{\mathbf{\mu}_{1}\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{1}}\mathbf{r}_{j}

(16)

for example,

\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{2}&=U_{\mathbf{\mu}_{1}\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{1}}\mathbf{r}_{2}\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{3}&=U_{\mathbf{\mu}_{2}\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{2}}U_{\mathbf{\mu}_{1}\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{1}}\mathbf{r}_{3},\text{ etc.}\end{split}

(17)

Notice that each operator
U_{\mathbf{\mu}_{j},\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{j}}
is applied to everything on its right-hand side.

Step 3.
Consider the operator

\mathbf{U}_{p}^{T}\mathbf{q}=U_{\mathbf{\mu}_{1}\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{1}}\dots U_{\mathbf{\mu}_{p},\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{p}}\mathbf{q}

(18)

mapping vectors
\mathbf{\mu}_{j}
into vectors
\mathbf{r}_{j}

and construct the vector of
K2 residuals

\widehat{\mathbf{e}}=\mathbf{U}_{p}^{T}\Bigl[\widehat{\mathbf{\epsilon}}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbf{\mu}_{j}\mathbf{\mu}^{\sf T}_{j}\widehat{\mathbf{\epsilon}}\Bigl]=\mathbf{U}_{p}^{T}\widehat{\mathbf{\epsilon}}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbf{r}_{j}\mathbf{r}^{\sf T}_{j}\mathbf{U}_{p}^{T}\widehat{\mathbf{\epsilon}}

(19)

Step 4.
Let the partial sums of the K2 residuals be

v_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\widehat{\mathbf{e}}^{T}\mathbb{I}_{t}.

(20)

Similarly to (
12
), test statistics for (
9
) can be constructed by taking functionals
h[\cdot]
of
v_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t)
.

Step 5.
Compute the value of the desired test statistic,
h[v_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t)]
, and compare it with the distribution of the same functional taken with respect to a zero-mean Gaussian process,
u_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t)
, with covariance function

\begin{split}\langle u_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t),u_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t^{\prime})\rangle=\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbb{I}_{t}^{T}\biggl[I-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbf{r}_{j}\mathbf{r}_{j}^{T}\biggl]\mathbb{I}_{t^{\prime}}.\\
\end{split}

(21)

The distribution of
h[u_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t)]
can be derived directly by simulating realizations of

u_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbb{I}_{t}^{T}[\mathbf{e}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbf{r}_{j}\mathbf{r}^{T}_{j}\mathbf{e}]

(22)

with
\mathbf{e}
drawn from a multivariate standard normal.

Step 6.
Reject the model
\mathbf{C}^{M}(\mathbf{\theta})
if the probability
P(h[u_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t)]>h[v_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t)])
is smaller than the desired significance level.

The construction of the
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{j}
vectors in Step 2 is an intermediate step necessary to map vectors
\mathbf{\mu}_{j}
’s into
\mathbf{r}_{j}
using the operator
\mathbf{U}_{p}^{T}

[
1
]
. This enables the construction of the K2 transformed residuals,
\widehat{\mathbf{e}}
, as described in Step 3.
In contrast to the process
w_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t)
in (
10
), under
H_{0}
, the process of partial sums of the K2 residuals,
v_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t)
, constructed in Step 4, has the same limiting distribution as the Gaussian process
u_{{}_{N}}\hskip-0.71114pt(t)
. The covariance function of the latter is given in (
21
) and depends solely on the
-
arbitrarily chosen
-
orthonormal set
\{\mathbf{r}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
. This is true regardless of the distribution of
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}

[
1
]
.
It follows that, in the limit, test statistics constructed as outlined in Step 5 have a limiting null distribution that is independent of both the distribution of
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}
and the model
\mathbf{C}^{M}(\mathbf{\theta})
being tested.
Finally, for sufficiently large
N
, the probability in Step 6 provides an asymptotic approximation of the p-value for testing the hypothesis in (
9
).

An illustrative example

Let
\mathbf{C}^{M_{1}}(\mathbf{\theta})
and
\mathbf{C}^{M_{2}}(\mathbf{\theta})
be two candidate models for
E[\widehat{\mathbf{C}}]
with components

\begin{split}&C^{M_{1}}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{\theta})=\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}\ell+\theta_{2}\mathbf{x}_{i},\\
&C^{M_{2}}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{\theta})=\exp\{\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}\mathbf{x}_{i}+\theta_{2}\mathbf{x}_{i}\ell\}.\end{split}

(23)

The
\mathbf{x}_{i}

In summary, these results suggest that one can simulate the null distribution of
