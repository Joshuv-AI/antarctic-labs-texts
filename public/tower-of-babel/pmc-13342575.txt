Evaluation of large language models and retrieval-augmented generation for clinical reasoning in pediatric myopia: a 50-case real-world study
Authors: Daohuan Kang, Kaikai Zhao, Deji Cheng, Lu Yuan, Wen Sun, Kai Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To evaluate the clinical reasoning ability of large language models (LLMs) and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems in pediatric myopia management using a real-world, expert-annotated case set covering diverse refractive, pathological, and high-risk scenarios. Six models were tested: baseline LLMs (GPT Base, Gemini Base, Grok Base) and their RAG variants (GPT-RAG, Gemini-RAG, Grok-RAG). RAG was augmented with 41 authoritative guidelines, including IMI white papers and the LAMP study. Performance was evaluated through automated scoring by Claude 4 Opus and blinded adjudication by three senior ophthalmologists, focusing on Accuracy, Utility, and Safety. RAG-enhanced models significantly outperformed baseline models across all metrics. Notably, GPT-RAG achieved the highest weighted automated score (7.46), surpassing GPT Base (7.37). Human adjudication revealed that RAG models achieved 90–94% consensus alignment compared to 68–82% for baselines. Crucially, the probability of high-risk recommendations—those capable of causing severe vision loss—was eliminated (0%) in all RAG models, whereas baseline models exhibited high-risk error rates of 6–14%. LLM + RAG integration boosts reliability and safety in pediatric myopia care, particularly for high-risk decisions. RAG’s domain knowledge incorporation advances AI clinical tools in ophthalmology, though ophthalmologist-in-the-loop refinement is essential pre-deployment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51205-7.

Introduction

Childhood myopia has become a significant global public health issue, with projections indicating that by 2050 approximately half of the world’s population will be affected and that high myopia will become increasingly prevalent, leading to serious long-term visual impairment risks including retinal detachment, myopic maculopathy, glaucoma, and early cataracts1,2. Early-onset myopia is a strong predictor of greater refractive errors in adulthood. Consequently, the management of myopia has evolved from simple optical correction to a comprehensive approach that emphasizes controlling axial elongation and reducing the risk of vision loss3,4.

The decision-making process for managing pediatric myopia is complex and requires ophthalmologists to consider multiple factors, including cycloplegic refraction, axial length progression, corneal curvature, binocular visual function parameters, and genetic and environmental risk factors5,6. Treatment options are diverse, including orthokeratology (OK lenses), low-concentration atropine eye drops, peripheral defocus lenses, and soft contact lenses. The selection of appropriate interventions often depends on the latest clinical evidence, such as the LAMP study comparing the efficacy of different atropine concentrations7. However, managing the growing volume of patient data and rapidly evolving clinical guidelines poses a significant challenge to traditional decision-making models, impacting both efficiency and accuracy8.

In recent years, advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have made substantial contributions to ophthalmology9–12. AI models have shown performance on par with that of expert ophthalmologists, highlighting their potential to enhance diagnostic accuracy and efficiency in this field13–15. However, clinical practice extends beyond image recognition and increasingly relies on natural language for tasks like medical history taking, logical reasoning, and doctor-patient communication16. LLMs have emerged as promising tools in this regard, showing exceptional performance on general medical exams (USMLE)17 and achieving near-resident-level proficiency in ophthalmology-related tasks18. Despite their promise, LLMs face substantial challenges in clinical settings, particularly the issue of “hallucinations,” where models generate incorrect or fabricated information with high confidence19,20. In pediatric myopia management, such errors can have catastrophic consequences, such as confusing atropine concentrations or referencing outdated treatment guidelines. Moreover, LLMs are often limited by a knowledge cutoff, meaning they cannot incorporate the most recent clinical consensus, leading to outdated recommendations21.

To address these limitations, the Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) framework has been developed. RAG improves model performance by retrieving relevant information from authoritative external knowledge sources before generating responses. This approach enables models to base their answers on up-to-date and contextually relevant evidence. RAG has distinct advantages over traditional fine-tuning methods, including the ability to update knowledge without retraining the model and providing traceability by citing specific guideline sections. These features make RAG an essential tool for medical AI, especially in ophthalmology, where accurate and timely recommendations are crucial.

However, the effectiveness of RAG in ophthalmology, particularly in managing complex, long-term conditions like pediatric myopia, has not been thoroughly validated. Existing studies have predominantly focused on simpler tasks, such as answering multiple-choice questions, rather than the nuanced decision-making required in real-world clinical practice22. This study aims to address this gap by constructing a high-quality evaluation dataset consisting of 50 real-world clinical cases. These cases will be used to compare the performance of general LLMs with RAG-enhanced models in pediatric myopia clinical reasoning. The key contributions of this research include the development of real-world evaluation benchmarks that reflect the complexities and uncertainties of clinical decision-making, quantifying the impact of RAG on accuracy, utility, and safety, and introducing a novel multidimensional framework to assess the safety of medical AI systems.

Methods

Study design and ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Approval No. 2024-IRB-0365) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The dataset was constructed from retrospective clinical records; all patient data was strictly anonymized and de-identified prior to inclusion. Informed consent for the use of clinical data had been obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardians during original patient care. The study focused on the technical evaluation of AI models and did not involve direct patient interaction or intervention (Fig. 1).

Dataset curation: the 50-case real-world benchmark

Unlike synthetic “textbook” cases often used in AI evaluation, these cases preserved the ambiguity, noise, and comorbidities found in real-world ophthalmology.

The RAG architecture and corpus construction

To isolate the effects of the RAG framework, we evaluated six model configurations: three baseline large language models and their corresponding RAG-enhanced variants.

The baseline models were GPT-4o (OpenAI, accessed October 2025), Gemini 1.5 Pro (Google, accessed October 2025), and Grok-2 (xAI, accessed October 2025). All models were queried with a temperature setting of 0 to ensure deterministic and reproducible outputs.

The RAG variants were GPT-RAG, Gemini-RAG, and Grok-RAG, each augmented using the same specialized knowledge corpus.

Implementation

The RAG system used a vector database to handle semantic similarity searches. We created embeddings for the documents with OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-large model, breaking them into 512-token chunks that overlapped by 128 tokens. For every query, we pulled the top 10 most relevant chunks (setting k = 10), skipping any extra reranking. When a clinical case was input, the system:

Generated an embedding of the query;Searched the vector database for the top-k most relevant text chunks from the 41-document corpus;Fed these chunks into the LLM context window with a system prompt instructing the model to “Answer based strictly on the provided context.”

The top 10 chunks were retrieved using semantic cosine similarity, without source weighting or secondary reranking. To resolve conflicting guidelines, the models either presented both clinical perspectives or defaulted to the dominant consensus within the retrieved text, such as the IMI white papers.

Statistical methods

Automated scores were reported descriptively as means and weighted totals.

Proportions were reported as percentages with ranges. Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) with a robust variance estimator were utilized to compare categorical outcomes (e.g., consensus alignment rates, incidence of high-risk errors) between RAG-enhanced and baseline models. Pairwise comparisons between specific provider models (e.g., GPT-RAG vs. GPT Base) were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method. Statistical significance was defined as an adjusted two-sided P-value < 0.05. Inter-rater reliability was quantified using Fleiss’ kappa per axis, with 95% CI via standard variance estimator. Interpretation followed Landis and Koch criteria (substantial: 0.61–0.80; almost perfect: >0.80)23. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 17.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Human adjudication results

The human adjudication provided a granular view of model performance, particularly highlighting the impact of RAG on clinical safety and accuracy.

Accuracy (Fig. 2)

Fig. 2Human evaluation results of responses generated by LLMs and LLM-RAG in terms of accuracy.

Human evaluation results of responses generated by LLMs and LLM-RAG in terms of accuracy.

RAG models showed a marked improvement in aligning with established clinical consensus. Gemini-RAG achieved the highest consensus alignment at 94%, followed closely by Grok-RAG (92%) and GPT-RAG (90%). In contrast, baseline models showed a higher rate of recommendations Opposed to consensus (up to 20% for Grok Base). Furthermore, RAG significantly reduced the incidence of Missing Content of great clinical significance, with all RAG models scoring 2–4% in this category, compared to 14–22% for baseline models. The difference in consensus alignment rates between RAG models and baseline models was statistically significant (P < 0.001).

Utility (Fig. 3)

Fig. 3Human evaluation results of responses generated by LLMs and LLM-RAG in terms of utility.

Human evaluation results of responses generated by LLMs and LLM-RAG in terms of utility.

The RAG models demonstrated superior clinical utility, primarily by enhancing the ability to retrieve and reason with domain-specific knowledge. Correct Retrieval was significantly higher for RAG models (90–96%) compared to baseline models (54–68%). This enhanced retrieval directly translated to better Correct Reasoning, with RAG models scoring 88–96% in this sub-aspect, confirming that the RAG mechanism effectively grounds the LLM’s logic in the provided corpus. The improvement in Correct Retrieval rates was highly significant (P < 0.001).

Safety (Fig. 4)

Fig. 4Human evaluation results of responses generated by LLMs and LLM-RAG in terms of safety.

Human evaluation results of responses generated by LLMs and LLM-RAG in terms of safety.

Safety was the most critical dimension, and RAG showed its most profound impact here. The incidence of Inappropriate/Wrong Content of great clinical significance was dramatically reduced by RAG (0–4%) compared to baseline models (10–18%). Most importantly, RAG models completely eliminated the possibility of a High-Risk recommendation (0%, 95% CI: 0.0% − 2.4% per-response) across the 150 total outputs evaluated for all RAG configurations. In contrast, baseline models carried a high-risk error rate ranging from 6% to 14% (with a pooled overall risk of 10%, 95% CI: 5.7% − 15.9% across 150 outputs). A total of 300 model outputs (50 cases × 6 models) were blindly adjudicated by the expert panel. The reduction in high-risk recommendations between RAG and baseline models was statistically significant (P < 0.001). This suggests that RAG acts as a vital safety filter, preventing the generation of potentially harmful clinical advice.

Discussion

The results from this study clearly demonstrate that RAG systems significantly enhance the clinical reasoning and decision-making abilities of LLMs in pediatric myopia management. By incorporating external authoritative guidelines and medical literature, RAG models showed superior performance compared to baseline LLMs, particularly in the critical domains of accuracy, utility, and most importantly, safety.

Enhanced accuracy and consensus alignment

The RAG-enhanced models exhibited substantial improvements in clinical accuracy when compared to baseline models, aligning more closely with established clinical consensus. Specifically, the RAG models achieved higher consensus alignment rates of 90–94%, whereas baseline models only reached 68–82%. This is particularly critical in pediatric myopia, a condition where accurate clinical decision-making is essential to prevent long-term vision impairment, including retinal detachment, glaucoma, and myopic maculopathy24. The integration of up-to-date clinical guidelines, such as the IMI White Papers25 and LAMP Study26, allowed RAG models to base their decisions on the latest evidence, significantly reducing the chances of generating outdated or incorrect recommendations27.

This improvement in accuracy supports the growing body of literature suggesting that integrating domain-specific knowledge into AI models can enhance their reliability in real-world clinical applications, particularly in complex fields such as pediatric ophthalmology28.

Clinical utility of RAG models

In addition to accuracy, the RAG models demonstrated remarkable improvements in clinical utility. The enhanced retrieval mechanism in RAG systems ensured that relevant and contextually appropriate information was efficiently incorporated into clinical reasoning. This led to better performance in key areas such as “Correct Retrieval” and “Correct Reasoning,” where RAG models consistently outperformed their baseline counterparts29. These improvements suggest that RAG systems could provide significant value in clinical decision-making, especially in pediatric myopia management, where treatment decisions must consider multiple factors such as axial length progression, refractive error, and potential for treatment complications30.

The ability of RAG models to retrieve and reason with the most pertinent clinical knowledge ensures that healthcare professionals can make more informed, evidence-based decisions. This ability to incorporate complex, up-to-date clinical data directly into the decision-making process is crucial in managing the multifaceted nature of pediatric myopia, where personalized care is often required31.

Safety enhancements: preventing harmful recommendations

One of the most significant contributions of this study is the improved safety associated with RAG-enhanced models. Safety is a critical factor in medical AI applications, particularly in ophthalmology, where missteps in treatment decisions can lead to irreversible damage. RAG models demonstrated a dramatic reduction in the generation of inappropriate or high-risk recommendations. In contrast to baseline models, which exhibited a high risk of generating potentially harmful advice (6–14%), the RAG models completely eliminated the possibility of high-risk recommendations (0%).

This finding is particularly notable given the potential for severe outcomes in pediatric myopia management. Errors in prescribing inappropriate interventions, such as the wrong concentration of atropine or incorrect management of ocular surface diseases, can lead to significant visual impairments. The safety improvements observed in this study confirm the value of RAG systems in mitigating such risks, acting as a safeguard that enhances the safety of clinical decisions32.

Limitations and future directions

While the results of this study are promising, several limitations must be addressed in future research. First, the study evaluated only a relatively small set of 50 pediatric myopia cases. Expanding the dataset to include a more diverse range of clinical scenarios, particularly rare conditions or more severe manifestations of myopia, could provide a better understanding of the capabilities and limitations of RAG-enhanced models in real-world settings33.

Additionally, the study relied primarily on expert-adjudicated evaluations of simulated cases. Although this approach yields valuable performance metrics, deploying RAG systems in actual clinical environments is essential to gauge their broader utility. Future investigations should prioritize live testing in clinical settings, considering factors like clinician trust, user interface design, and seamless integration with electronic health records, which are pivotal for assessing practical viability34. Clinical deployment of RAG models requires an ‘ophthalmologist-in-the-loop’ decision support framework, where specialists authorize AI-generated drafts. This collaborative approach has already proven successful in improving accuracy and workflow efficiency for complex tasks like ultrasound interpretation35. Furthermore, patient safety relies on technical firewalls: high-risk triggers (e.g., ‘suspected CNV’ or ‘retinal break’) must bypass AI processing entirely and alert clinicians directly to prevent emergency diagnostic delays.

Moreover, RAG’s effectiveness hinges on the quality of its knowledge corpus; in this study, the corpus comprised just 41 authoritative documents. Future work could extend this to include a wider array of multilingual guidelines, enhancing comprehensiveness and cross-cultural applicability. Currently, single-pass retrieval limits RAG’s capacity for intricate cases. Exploring iterative RAG or agentic workflows could resolve this, advancing ophthalmic care toward autonomous, adaptive agents capable of multi-step reasoning36.

Finally, the rapid evolution of clinical guidelines and AI models poses an ongoing challenge. Although this study drew from a carefully curated set of resources, maintaining RAG systems with regular updates to incorporate the latest medical research and protocols will be crucial for sustaining their clinical relevance and accuracy37.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the significant potential of RAG-enhanced LLMs in improving clinical decision-making and reasoning in pediatric myopia management. The combination of large-scale knowledge retrieval and model generation has proven to be a powerful tool in enhancing the accuracy, utility, and safety of AI-powered clinical decision support systems. As AI continues to evolve, the integration of domain-specific knowledge via RAG will be a crucial step in developing safe, effective, and reliable decision-support tools for complex medical conditions like pediatric myopia. While further refinement and validation are necessary, the results from this study provide a strong foundation for the future development and deployment of AI tools in ophthalmology, with the potential to improve patient outcomes and assist clinicians in making informed, evidence-based decisions.
