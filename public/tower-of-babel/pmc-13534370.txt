Prognostic implications and precision selection criteria of primary tumor resection in metastatic gallbladder adenocarcinoma
Authors: Xun Guo, Wei Li, Zhou Xu, Qinglin Yang, Zhouyuan Du, Tao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGallbladder cancer is recognized as one of the most aggressive malignancies within the biliary system. Usually, the substantial part of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, often with metastases to distant organs. In cases of metastatic gallbladder adenocarcinoma, the potential benefit of primary tumor resection (PTR) in improving patient prognosis remains an unresolved issue that warrants further investigation.Patients and methodsWe collected data to identify patients diagnosed with gallbladder cancer and concurrent metastases to the liver, lungs, bones, brain, or distant lymph nodes between 2004 and 2021 from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. The impact of primary tumor resection (PTR) on overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) was evaluated using the Cox proportional hazards model. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated for additional clarity. Subgroup analyses were also conducted to identify patient groups that might have a longer survival period from PTR.ResultsA total of 1968 patients diagnosed with metastatic gallbladder adenocarcinoma at the time of initial presentation between 2004 and 2021 were included in the analysis. Among these, liver metastasis was the most frequent site of metastasis (n = 1695, 86.12%). Of the total cohort, 600 patients (30.49%) underwent PTR, and these patients demonstrated a significant improvement in both OS (0.679 [0.612–0.752], P < 0.001) and CSS (0.690 [0.619–0.768], P < 0.001). Notably, PTR was associated with reduced overall mortality in patients with isolated liver or distant lymph node metastases (liver metastasis: 0.690 [0.612–0.778], P < 0.001; distant lymph node metastasis: 0.498 [0.324–0.763], P < 0.001), as well as reduced cancer-specific mortality (liver metastasis: 0.698 [0.616–0.791], P < 0.001; distant lymph node metastasis: 0.522 [0.332–0.820], P = 0.003). In addition, patients with one or two metastatic organs who underwent PTR experienced a similar reduction in both overall mortality (1 metastatic organ: 0.691 [0.619–0.771], P < 0.001; 2 metastatic organs: 0.623 [0.434–0.895], P = 0.005) and cancer-specific mortality (1 metastatic organ: 0.700 [0.624–0.785], P < 0.001; 2 metastatic organs: 0.643 [0.439–0.940], P = 0.013).ConclusionThese results demonstrate that primary tumor resection (PTR) may prolong life expectancy of patients with certain subtypes of metastatic gallbladder adenocarcinoma. Consequently, these findings offer valuable insights and guidance for the management of metastatic gallbladder adenocarcinoma, particularly in selecting patients who may benefit from surgical intervention.Keywords: Primary tumour resection, Overall survival, Cancer-specific survival, Gallbladder adenocarcinoma, SEER database

Introduction

Gallbladder cancer is a relatively rare yet highly malignant tumor of the gastrointestinal system [1], accounting for approximately 0.5%-1.2% of all gastrointestinal cancers and ranking as the most common malignancy of the biliary system [2]. Epidemiological data indicate that there are approximately 170,000 new cases of gallbladder cancer worldwide each year [3], with significantly higher incidence rates in East Asia and South America compared to other regions. This discrepancy may be linked to pre-cancerous conditions such as chronic cholecystitis, cholelithiasis, and adenomatous polyps of the gallbladder. [4]. The pathological types of gallbladder cancer include adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, undifferentiated carcinoma, and neuroendocrine tumors, with gallbladder adenocarcinoma being the most prevalent, accounting for over 90% of cases [5]. The clinical presentation of gallbladder adenocarcinoma is often non-specific. Early-stage symptoms are typically vague, including right upper abdominal pain, dyspepsia, and other signs resembling those of cholelithiasis [6]. These nonspecific manifestations are largely due to the tumor’s concealed anatomical location, the absence of distinct biomarkers, and its aggressive growth pattern. As the disease progresses, tumor cells often invade the liver parenchyma directly, leading to metastasis to hepatic hilar lymph nodes and distant organs through the peritoneum and bloodstream. Most patients are diagnosed at stages III or IV [7], at which point the disease has already advanced significantly. As a result, treatment outcomes are generally poor, with a 5-year survival rate of less than 20% [8].

Recent advances in therapeutic strategies for gallbladder adenocarcinoma include immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as pembrolizumab and divalizumab, which, when combined with chemotherapy, have shown promise in prolonging survival for patients with advanced disease, providing a new avenue for translational therapy [9]. Additionally, the development of molecular targeted therapies, such as anti-HER2 and anti-VEGF agents, along with the advent of gene editing technologies, marks a shift toward more individualized and precise treatment approaches for gallbladder cancer [10]. It is indisputable that most cases of gallbladder adenocarcinoma, even those receiving immunotherapy and targeted therapy, still require surgical intervention. While surgical techniques for gallbladder cancer continue to evolve, the fundamental goal remains the removal of the primary tumor. Primary tumor resection (PTR) continues to be the cornerstone of treatment, as it not only directly excises tumor tissue but also reduces the risk of local recurrence and distant metastasis through lymph node dissection. This approach improves patient outcomes and enhances the quality of life. Furthermore, PTR has been shown to improve survival rates in patients with certain metastatic solid tumors, such as gastric cancer, medullary thyroid cancer, lung cancer, and pancreatic cancer [11–14]. Thus, surgical resection remains the key strategy for achieving a radical treatment for gallbladder adenocarcinoma [15]. However, the effectiveness of surgery is limited by factors such as tumor stage, the extent of surgical resection, and the presence of lymph node metastasis [16].

The treatment of advanced or unresectable gallbladder cancer presents significant challenges. For patients with metastatic gallbladder adenocarcinoma, PTR holds potential to prolong the life expectancy; however, the site and number of metastases may influence the efficacy of surgery. Additionally, the clinical effectiveness of PTR in these patients, and the identification of specific subgroups who would benefit most from this procedure, require further clinical evidence. To resolve this challenge, we undertook a study utilizing the SEER database, which includes a large cohort of patients with metastatic gallbladder adenocarcinoma. Our analysis focused on the prognostic impact of different metastatic sites and the number of metastatic organs, as well as the role of PTR in treatment. Methods

Data sources and patient selection

Patient data for this study were sourced from the SEER database. The main inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a definitive pathological diagnosis of gallbladder adenocarcinoma; (2) the presence of metastases to distant organs; (3) the gallbladder is the primary tumor site; (4) availability of comprehensive treatment data (including surgical interventions and radiotherapy), as well as information on the year of diagnosis and duration of follow-up. The exclusion criteria included: (1) patients diagnosed at age less than 18 years; and (2) unclear information regarding the site of distant metastasis.

Data collection and definition of variables

The SEER database is a population-based national reporting system that collects comprehensive data on patients with cancer, including information on incidence, treatment modalities, mortality, and other demographic factors, representing approximately 28% of the U.S. population [17]. For our study, we extracted cases of gallbladder adenocarcinoma diagnosed between 2004 and 2021. This time frame was selected because the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging and Collaborative Staging (CS) data have been available since 2004. From the SEER database, we collected the following variables for each patient: year of diagnosis, age, sex, race, tumor grade, tumor size, regional lymph node status, and metastatic status for the lungs, bones, liver, brain, and distant lymph nodes. Additionally, treatment information, including whether patients underwent primary tumor resection (PTR), radiotherapy, or chemotherapy, was extracted. We also obtained survival data, including the patient’s vital status and cancer-specific mortality classification. PTR was defined as direct surgical resection of the primary tumor, excluding procedures such as cryosurgery, excisional biopsy, electrocautery, and photodynamic therapy.

Statistical analyses

The study cohort was categorized into two groups based on whether primary tumor resection (PTR) was performed: the PTR group (n = 600, 30.49%) and the non-PTR group (n = 1368, 69.51%). To assess differences in categorical variables between the groups, the chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were utilized, while continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t-test. The primary endpoints of the study were overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS). OS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause, with all-cause mortality considered an event and survivors treated as censored. In contrast, CSS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death specifically attributable to cancer, with cancer-related deaths considered events, and deaths from other causes or survivors treated as censored.

Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to identify independent prognostic factors, with hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) calculated for each factor. Overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) were estimated using Kaplan-Meier (K-M) curves, and group comparisons were made using the log-rank test. Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 1,968 patients diagnosed with metastatic gallbladder adenocarcinoma were included in this study. Of these, 600 patients (30.49%) were male and 1,368 patients (69.51%) were female. Regarding age, 426 patients (21.65%) were under 60 years old, while 1,542 patients (78.35%) were aged 60 or older. Among the cohort, approximately one-third of patients underwent primary tumor resection (PTR) (n = 600, 30.49%), while the remaining two-thirds did not (n = 1,368, 69.51%).

Statistically significant differences between the PTR and non-PTR groups were observed in several clinical characteristics, including brain metastasis, distant lymph node (DLN) metastasis, widespread metastasis(defined as involvement of more than five organs), age, tumor grade, T stage, N stage, radiation treatment, tumor size, race, and the number of metastatic sites (P < 0.05). Notably, compared to the non-PTR group, a higher proportion of patients in the PTR group were under 60 years of age (24.50% vs. 20.39%), had brain metastasis (0.5% vs. 0%), and exhibited DLN metastasis (8.83% vs. 3.07%). However, fewer patients in the PTR group had widespread metastasis (1.00% vs. 2.56%).

In terms of tumor characteristics, patients in the PTR group had earlier pathological grades, with a greater proportion classified as G1-2 (41.50% vs. 6.49%) and G3-4 (51.00% vs. 11.48%). Similarly, T and N stages were more favorable in the PTR group, with the majority of patients in T1-2 (41.33% vs. 11.70%) and T3-4 (54.33% vs. 41.59%) stages, as well as N+ (33.50% vs. 23.46%) and N0 (48.83% vs. 39.55%) stages. Furthermore, a higher proportion of patients in the PTR group received radiotherapy (8.50% vs. 5.85%), had tumor size determination (65.00% vs. 38.74%), (79.17% vs. 71.64%) were of Caucasian descent, and had only one metastatic site (91.17% vs. 80.63%), as detailed in Table 1.

After PSM, the PTR group and the non-PTR therapy group each comprised 219 matched patients. Apart from T stage (P = 0.006), no significant associations were observed between variables in the two groups after PSM (Table 4).

Metastatic patterns across the overall cohort

To visualize the distribution of metastatic organs among the patients, a Venn diagram was constructed using the collected data (Fig. 1). As depicted, the liver was the most commonly affected organ, with metastases present in 1,695 patients (86.12%), followed by the lungs (n = 281, 14.28%), bones (n = 149, 7.58%), distant lymph nodes (n = 96, 4.88%), and brain (n = 16, 0.81%). The overlapping areas of the Venn diagram represent patients with metastases to multiple organs. In total, 1,650 patients (83.84%) had a single metastatic site, while 246 patients (12.5%) had two metastatic sites. A smaller number of patients had three (n = 29, 1.47%) or four (n = 2, 0.1%) metastatic sites. Notably, no patients were found to have metastases to five organs.

Prognostic impact of PTR in the overall cohort

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to assess factors influencing patients’ overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS). In the univariate Cox regression analysis, several variables were found to be significantly associated with OS, including distant lymph node (DLN) metastasis, age, tumor grade, T stage, N stage, primary tumor resection (PTR), radiation recode, chemotherapy recode, tumor size, and the number of involved sites (P < 0.05).Subsequent multivariate Cox regression analysis identified that tumor grade (G3-4: HR 1.41, 95% CI 1.22–1.64, P < 0.001), PTR (HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.59–0.80, P < 0.001), chemotherapy (HR 0.38, 95% CI 0.35–0.42, P < 0.001), race (White: HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.72–0.94, P = 0.003), and the number of involved sites (2 sites: HR 1.19, 95% CI 1.03–1.37, P = 0.016) were independently associated with improved OS (Table 2).

Similarly, in univariate Cox regression analysis for CSS, variables such as DLN metastasis, age, tumor grade, T stage, N stage, PTR, radiation recode, chemotherapy recode, tumor size, and number of involved sites were significantly correlated with CSS (P < 0.05). Multivariate analysis further revealed that tumor grade (G3-4: HR 1.43, 95% CI 1.23–1.68, P < 0.001), PTR (HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.60–0.81, P < 0.001), and chemotherapy (HR 0.39, 95% CI 0.35–0.43, P < 0.001) were significant predictors of improved CSS (Table 3). To further evaluate the impact of PTR on patients’ OS and CSS, we divided the 1,968 patients into two groups: those who underwent PTR and those who did not. The log-rank test revealed a significant association between PTR and improved OS and CSS (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Optimal candidates for undergoing PTR in patients with metastatic gallbladder adenocarcinoma

Survival analyses, Kaplan-Meier curves, and log-rank tests were conducted to assess the impact of primary tumor resection (PTR) on survival outcomes, taking into account the different metastatic sites and the number of metastases. The results demonstrated that the efficacy of PTR varied depending on the type and extent of metastasis.

Patients with liver metastases showed a survival benefit following PTR, with improved overall survival (OS) (P < 0.001) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4E and F). We used the official SEER definition, according to which “Mets at DX-liver = Yes” includes only distant liver metastases confirmed by coding (CS Mets at DX: Liver = 1) and excludes cases where liver metastases result solely from direct invasion (T3). Similarly, PTR also improved the prognosis for patients with distant lymph node metastases, extending both OS (P < 0.001) and CSS (P = 0.003) (Fig. 4G and H). In contrast, no significant survival advantage was observed for patients with bone metastases (P = 0.960 for OS; P = 0.760 for CSS) (Fig. 4A and B) or lung metastases (P = 0.954 for OS; P = 0.714 for CSS) (Fig. 4C and D). Notably, three patients with isolated brain metastases were excluded from the analysis, as all of them underwent PTR.

Kaplan-Meier curves illustrating overall and cancer-specific survival based on the performance of PTR in patients with bone (A and B), lung (C and D), liver (E and F), and distant lymph node (G and H)

Further survival analysis based on the number of metastatic organs revealed that patients with only one metastasis, regardless of the organ involved, experienced survival benefits from PTR, with longer survival times observed for both OS (P < 0.001) and CSS (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5A and B). This survival advantage persisted in patients with two metastases (P = 0.005 for OS; P = 0.013 for CSS) (Fig. 5C and D). However, for patients with three metastases (P = 0.463 for OS; P = 0.395 for CSS) (Fig. 5E and F), four metastases (P = 0.317 for OS; P = 0.317 for CSS) (Fig. 5G and H), or extensive metastasis (P = 0.553 for OS; P = 0.379 for CSS) (Fig. 5I and J), PTR did not result in any significant improvement in survival outcomes.

Kaplan-Meier curves illustrating overall and cancer-specific survival based on the performance of PTR in patients with 1 (A and B), 2 (C and D), 3 (E and F), and 4 (G and H) metastatic organs and widespread metastases (I and J)

In summary, our findings suggest that PTR offers the most benefit to patients with metastatic gallbladder adenocarcinoma who have isolated liver or distant lymph node metastases, as well as those with one or two metastatic sites. These patients are more likely to achieve improved treatment outcomes following PTR.

The impact of PTR on subgroups with different metastatic sites

We conducted a subgroup analysis of all patients and created a forest plot to summarize the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for overall mortality (OM) and cancer-specific mortality (CSM) in the PTR and non-PTR groups across various subgroups (Fig. 6).

Forest plots summarizing the HRs and 95% CIs of overall (A) and cancer-specific mortality (B) in subgroup analyses comparing PTR vs. no-PTR

In patients with different metastatic sites, surgical resection of the primary tumor significantly reduced the risk of mortality in those with isolated liver metastases (PTR vs. non-PTR: 0.673 (0.597–0.759), P < 0.001 for OM; 0.683 (0.603–0.774), P < 0.001 for CSM) and distant lymph node metastases (PTR vs. non-PTR: 0.482 (0.314–0.739), P = 0.001 for OM; 0.508 (0.324–0.798), P = 0.003 for CSM). Similarly, PTR was associated with improved survival outcomes in patients with one metastasis (PTR vs. non-PTR: 0.675 (0.605–0.753), P < 0.001 for OM; 0.686 (0.611–0.769), P < 0.001 for CSM) and two metastases (PTR vs. non-PTR: 0.595 (0.414–0.855), P = 0.005 for OM; 0.618 (0.423–0.904), P = 0.013 for CSM), reducing both OM and CSM in these patient groups.

However, for patients with bone metastases (PTR vs. non-PTR: 0.984 (0.527–1.84), P = 0.961 for OM; 1.094 (0.566–2.116), P = 0.790 for CSM), lung metastases (PTR vs. non-PTR: 1.024 (0.594–1.763), P = 0.933 for OM; 1.122 (0.628–2.005), P = 0.698 for CSM), or extensive metastases (PTR vs. non-PTR: 0.741 (0.26–2.113), P = 0.575 for OM; 0.599 (0.181–1.979), P = 0.401 for CSM), PTR did not demonstrate any significant survival advantage. This trend was also observed in patients with three metastases (PTR vs. non-PTR: 1.428 (0.471–4.332), P = 0.529 for OM; 1.559 (0.509–4.771), P = 0.437 for CSM), confirming the conclusions drawn from previous analyses.It is important to note that the number of patients with four metastases was too small to draw any conclusions regarding the potential benefits of PTR, and patients with brain metastases were excluded from this analysis since all of them underwent PTR.

Discussion

We analyzed data from the SEER database and confirmed that primary tumor resection (PTR) significantly improved survival outcomes and prognosis in patients with metastatic gallbladder adenocarcinoma. Further evaluation of PTR’s efficacy was conducted across different metastatic sites and varying numbers of metastases. The results demonstrate that PTR prolong the life expectancy in patients with isolated liver metastases (hazard ratio [HR] 0.690 [0.612–0.778], P < 0.001 for overall survival [OS]; HR 0.698 [0.616–0.791], P < 0.001 for cancer-specific survival [CSS]), distant lymph node metastases (HR 0.498 [0.324–0.763], P < 0.001 for OS; HR 0.522 [0.332–0.820], P = 0.003 for CSS), as well as those with one metastasis (HR 0.691 [0.619–0.771], P < 0.001 for OS; HR 0.700 [0.624–0.785], P < 0.001 for CSS) and two metastases (HR 0.623 [0.434–0.895], P = 0.005 for OS; HR 0.643 [0.439–0.940], P = 0.013 for CSS). Both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses demonstrated that PTR was significantly correlated with both overall mortality and cancer-specific mortality, highlighting its role as a strong predictor of improved survival outcomes. Overall, patients who underwent PTR had a better prognosis and longer survival compared to those who did not undergo PTR. These findings underscore the potential of PTR in delivering personalized treatment strategies for patients with metastatic gallbladder adenocarcinoma.

Gallbladder adenocarcinoma is characterized by its insidious onset and invasiveness, which makes it highly susceptible to metastasis, often resulting in a poor prognosis for affected patients. Despite the generally unfavorable outcomes for patients with distant metastases, the utility of surgery remains a topic of debate in this patient population [18]. Additionally, the therapeutic efficacy of surgical interventions for gallbladder adenocarcinoma is influenced by the specific sites of metastasis. As a result, individualized treatment strategies are essential for optimizing patient outcomes [19].

Previously, Gao Tianming et al. investigated whether patients with liver metastases from gallbladder adenocarcinoma could achieve a survival benefit from surgical intervention, and their conclusions were consistent with those of the present study. However, their analysis was limited exclusively to patients with liver metastases and did not consider other metastatic sites. In contrast, the current study includes patients with various types of metastatic gallbladder cancer, thereby offering findings that are more broadly applicable to clinical practice. Similarly, Xie Jiang et al. explored the impact of primary tumor resection on survival outcomes in patients with metastatic gallbladder cancer [20], focusing primarily on whether such patients could achieve prolonged survival through the procedure. In comparison, the present study aims to identify the specific patient population that derives the greatest benefit from primary tumor resection, highlighting a fundamental difference in research objectives between the two studies.

The role of primary tumor resection (PTR) in the management of gallbladder cancer has been a focal point of research [21]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that surgical resection in the early stages of gallbladder cancer can substantially improve patient prognosis [22]. However, as previously noted, surgical treatment must be tailored to the individual patient, with the extent of resection determined by factors such as tumor characteristics, location, and the stage of the disease [23]. These factors play a critical role in influencing the appropriate surgical approach, as the resection range varies between early and advanced stages of the disease. Consequently, the efficacy of PTR in patients with gallbladder cancer is highly dependent on the presence and location of metastases.

Among metastatic sites, the liver is the most common organ to be affected by gallbladder adenocarcinoma. PTR proves to be particularly effective in patients with isolated liver metastases. This efficacy may be attributed to several factors. Liver metastases from gallbladder cancer often present as small, relatively isolated nodules with limited distribution, which makes surgical resection feasible, provided there is no invasion of critical structures such as blood vessels and bile ducts [24]. Furthermore, because the liver is adjacent to the gallbladder, metastatic foci located in surgically accessible areas can be effectively resected alongside the primary tumor. Additionally, the liver’s regenerative capacity contributes to recovery after surgery, which further enhances the likelihood of improved survival outcomes in these patients [25].

Our findings suggest that primary tumor resection (PTR) may positively impact the prognosis of patients with distant lymph node metastases. The significance of comprehensive lymph node dissection has been well-established in previous studies [26]. Consequently, we propose that for patients who have developed lymph node metastases, performing PTR alongside appropriate lymph node dissection could enhance therapeutic efficacy [27]. This approach makes PTR particularly beneficial for patients with lymph node metastasis, as compared to those with metastases in other distant organs.

Through univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, we identified that, in addition to PTR, chemotherapy and radiotherapy also contribute to improved patient prognosis. In clinical practice, a variety of treatment options—such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy—have emerged for managing this aggressive biliary malignancy. While systemic chemotherapy is considered by some to be more effective than surgery for patients with metastatic gallbladder cancer [28], targeted therapies, such as those targeting HER-2 (a mutated target in gallbladder cancer) [29], and immunotherapy with pembrolizumab (a PD-1 inhibitor) [30], offer additional therapeutic avenues for patients with gallbladder adenocarcinoma [31]. However, these treatments demonstrate limited efficacy in cases of metastatic gallbladder adenocarcinoma [32], underscoring the continued importance of radical surgery as the cornerstone of treatment [33].

Analyzing the impact of primary tumor resection (PTR) on the prognosis of patients with metastatic gallbladder adenocarcinoma is crucial for providing more informed recommendations and helping patients identify the most suitable treatment strategies. However, our study does have several limitations. Firstly, the SEER database includes only five specific metastatic sites, and the majority of patients in our cohort had isolated liver metastases. The sample size for patients with isolated brain metastases was too small, which hindered our ability to make robust recommendations for this subgroup. Furthermore, in the SEER database, metastatic sites are usually identified through imaging rather than biopsy. Such reliance may lead to diagnostic inaccuracies, which could consequently influence the final results.Secondly, our study is subject to immortal time bias, as patients who underwent surgery were required to survive long enough to receive the procedure, which inherently biases the comparison in their favor. This limitation reduces the validity of our conclusions.Thirdly, the SEER population over-represents white patients while under-representing Asian-Pacific patients and African-American cohorts known to have higher gallbladder-cancer incidence. This skew limits external validity. Lastly, as a retrospective study, the analysis remains subject to selection bias and confounding by indication, and further prospective controlled studies are needed to identify highly selective patient subgroups that may benefit most from PTR.

Conclusion

The study suggests that primary tumor resection (PTR) may improve prognosis and prolong survival in patients with metastatic gallbladder adenocarcinoma. Patients with isolated liver metastases, distant lymph node metastases, or a limited number of metastases (one or two) are particularly likely to benefit from this intervention.
