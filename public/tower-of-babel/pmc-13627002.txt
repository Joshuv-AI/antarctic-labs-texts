Identification of CCSER1 missense variants and diplotypes as molecular markers for growth and lambing-related traits in Junken meat sheep
Authors: Sanchuan Zhao, Binpeng Xi, Qian Yu, Wenzhe Zhang, Yan Chen, Junlin Lan, Zongsheng Zhao, Hua Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To validate the potential role of CCSER1, a candidate gene for litter size and yearling weight identified by selection signature analysis, in lambing performance and growth development of Junken meat sheep, CCSER1 polymorphisms were genotyped using the Sequenom MassARRAY® SNP assay. Association analysis was conducted to assess the associations of CCSER1 polymorphisms and SNP2–SNP3 diplotypes with growth and lambing-related traits, and reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was used to determine the expression profile of CCSER1 in the ewes' digestive, developmental and reproductive tissues. The results showed three missense mutations in the CCSER1 gene of Junken meat sheep were identified, including SNP1 (XM_042251242.2: c.2557C>T) in exon 11, SNP2 (XM_042251242.2: c.1206A>G) and SNP3 (XM_042251242.2: c.16T>C) in exon 2. Three SNPs were exploratorily predicted to alter the secondary and tertiary structures of the CCSER1 protein. A single-stage mixed model was applied to evaluate the associations between individual SNP genotypes and phenotypic variation, with Bonferroni correction subsequently performed for multiple testing within each trait. The results showed that the associations of SNP2 with ABW (adjusted P = 0.0024) and SNP3 with YW (adjusted P = 0.022) and ADG2 (adjusted P = 0.039) remained statistically significant after Bonferroni correction. Phasing analysis of SNP2 and SNP3 identified four chromosome-level haplotypes and six corresponding diplotypes. A significant diplotype association was observed for YW (adjusted P = 0.039). Tukey's HSD test indicated that individuals carrying the T-A/T-A or T-A/T-G diplotypes exhibited significantly higher YW compared with those carryingT-G/T-G diplotype. The RT-qPCR analysis revealed a tissue-specific expression profile of CCSER1, with the highest expression detected in the abomasum (P < 0.01). In addition, CCSER1 expression levels in the lung and ovary were significantly higher than those in the liver, kidney, skin, brain, and pituitary gland (P < 0.01). These findings suggest that CCSER1 is a candidate gene for growth and lambing-related traits in Junken meat sheep, and that SNP2, SNP3, T-A/T-A and T-A/T-G may serve as useful molecular markers for marker-assisted selection to improve lambing performance and growth development in sheep.

Introduction

Sheep farming is an important source of global meat production, and lambing performance and growth rate are core economic traits that directly affect the profitability of sheep production (1). In recent years, with the rapid development of modern intensive farming and the continuous advancement of precision breeding, the development of new meat sheep breeds combining high fecundity with greater body weight has become an important direction in modern animal breeding (2). However, litter size and body weight often show a negative correlation, as multiple births are frequently accompanied by lower lamb birth weight and slower growth and development (3). Therefore, identifying key genetic markers that can simultaneously contribute to increased prolificacy and greater body weight has become a major focus in sheep breeding.

In our previous studies, genome-wide association study (GWAS) and selection signature analysis identified the coiled-coil serine-rich protein 1 gene (CCSER1) as a candidate gene associated with litter size (4) and yearling weight (5) in sheep. The CCSER1 gene is located on chromosome 6 in sheep, consists of 11 exons, and encodes 899 amino acids (6). Cell biological studies have shown that the protein encoded by CCSER1 is mainly localized to key structures such as the midbody during late mitotic cytokinesis and the γ-tubulin ring complex, and serves as an important structural or regulatory component required for normal cell division and the maintenance of chromosomal and genomic stability (7, 8). This fundamental role in cell proliferation regulation may confer pleiotropic potential on CCSER1 in economically important traits of livestock. Previous studies have shown that CCSER1 is not only significantly associated with diverse phenotypes, including coat characteristics in Northwest Tibetan cashmere goats (9), disease resistance in Merino sheep (10), and milk yield in Yuansheng Aite dairy sheep (11), but also represents a key gene involved in the regulation of growth and reproductive performance in sheep. Liu et al. (12) identified CCSER1 as an important selected gene related to reproduction and development in the Xiangdong black goat population. Li et al. (13) confirmed, using GWAS, that this gene has a significant genetic effect on body weight traits in Hu sheep from 3 to 7 months old. Although multiple studies have demonstrated the important role of CCSER1 in reproduction, growth, and development in sheep and goats, whether the genetic polymorphisms exist in CCSER1 gene of Junken meat sheep, a newly developed prolific meat sheep breed (14), and how these polymorphisms regulate growth and ewe reproductive traits remain unclear.

Therefore, in this study, candidate gene analysis and bioinformatics approaches were used to identify the mutation sites in the CCSER1 gene and predict the effects of SNPs on changes in CCSER1 protein structure. CCSER1 polymorphisms were genotyped using the Sequenom MassARRAY® SNP assay. Genotype-phenotype association analyses were performed to evaluate the effects of CCSER1 SNPs on growth and lambing-related traits. In addition, SNP2 and SNP3 within the same LD block were phased to reconstruct chromosome-level haplotypes and individual diplotypes, followed by diplotype-phenotype association analysis. In addition, reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was used to analyze the transcriptional profile of CCSER1 in digestive, developmental, and reproductive organs of ewes. This study aimed to evaluate population-specific associations between CCSER1 variants and economically relevant traits. These results may provide a basis for the application of marker-assisted selection in improving growth and lambing-related traits in sheep.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

This study was carried out in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee of Xinjiang Academy of Agricultural and Reclamation Sciences (Shihezi, China, ethic committee approval number: XJNKKXY-2020–34, 30 December 2020).

Sample collection

A total of 265 healthy Junken mutton sheep aged 3–6 years were selected from the Sheep Breeding Farm of Xinjiang Academy of Agricultural and Reclamation Science, comprising 235 ewes and 30 rams. All individuals were born and raised within the original breeding population without introgression from other sheep breeds. The animals were maintained under the same environmental and management conditions and had free access to feed. Before sample collection, all sheep were fasted for 12 h with free access to water to ensure sampling standardization and data accuracy. Blood samples were collected from the jugular vein into EDTA anticoagulant tubes, gently inverted to ensure complete mixing, and stored at −20 °C until genomic DNA extraction.

Three 1-year-old female Junken meat sheep with similar body weights and same genotypes of SNP1, SNP2, and SNP3 were sampled, and tissues were collected following standardized procedures. Briefly, anesthesia was induced by sequential intramuscular administration of diazepam (0.5–1.0 mg/kg; Gansu Ankang Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China) and scopolamine (0.01–0.04 mg/kg; Kaimei Gene, USA), followed by intravenous administration of thiopental sodium (10–20 mg/kg; Shanghai Xinya Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China). After anesthesia, the sheep were euthanized by jugular exsanguination, and tissue samples were immediately collected, including the heart, liver, lung, kidney, skin, skeletal muscle, brain, pituitary gland, rumen, abomasum, duodenum, ileum, uterus, and ovary. The collected tissues were rapidly transferred into RNase-free cryopreservation tubes and immediately immersed in liquid nitrogen to prevent RNA degradation. These samples were subsequently used for RNA extraction.

Phenotypic data of the experimental sheep population were collected and processed, including the litter size of each parity (LS) and lamb birth weight of ewes from 2020 to 2024, as well as the birth weight (BW), weaning weight at 3 months old (WW) and yearling weight (YW) in the experimental population. Microsoft Excel 2019 was used to calculate the average birth weight per litter (ABW), average daily gain from birth to weaning (ADG1), average daily gain from birth to 12 months old (ADG2) and average daily gain from weaning to 12 months old (ADG3) for each sheep. Genomic DNA was extracted from blood samples using the RelaxGene Blood DNA System (TIANGEN Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). DNA quality was assessed by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop™ 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Single-base extension primers for the three target loci were designed using Assay Design software (version 3.1) (Sequenom, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) based on the ovine CCSER1 reference transcript XM_042251242.2 annotated on the ARS-UI_Ramb_v3.0 reference genome assembly. The experimental procedure included PCR amplification, shrimp alkaline phosphatase (SAP) treatment, single-base extension reaction, mass spectrometric detection, and genotype data acquisition.

Function prediction

The wild-type and mutant sequences of the CCSER1 gene were retrieved from the Ensembl database (https://www.ensembl.org/index.html; accessed on 7 May 2026). The secondary and tertiary structures of the target protein before and after missense mutations were predicted using SOPMA (https://npsa.lyon.inserm.fr/cgi-bin/npsa_automat.pl?page=/NPSA/npsa_sopma.html; accessed on 7 May 2026) and SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/; accessed on 7 May 2026), respectively. All parameters were set to default values.

To elucidate the biological function of the protein encoded by the candidate gene CCSER1 and identify its potential interacting molecules, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins database, STRING v12.0 (https://string-db.org/; accessed on 8 May 2026), with Ovis aries selected as the reference organism.

Population genetic analysis

R software (version 4.5.3) (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used to calculate the genotype frequency, allele frequency, minor allele frequency (MAF), observed heterozygosity (Ho), expected heterozygosity (He), effective number of alleles (Ne), and polymorphism information content (PIC) for each SNP locus. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) tests of the SNP loci were performed using Haploview software (version 4.2) (16).

Haplotype reconstruction and diplotype association analysis

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis was first used to identify linked CCSER1 variants. SNP2 and SNP3, which were located within the same LD block, were subsequently phased using Beagle software (version 5.5). For each individual, phased alleles at SNP2 and SNP3 located on the same chromosome were combined to define chromosome-level haplotypes. The two phased haplotypes carried by each individual were then paired to define the corresponding diplotype. Haplotype frequencies were calculated using the total number of phased chromosomes (2N), whereas diplotype frequencies were calculated using the number of individuals (N). Diplotypes with frequencies below 5% were excluded from phenotype association analyses.

Diplotype–phenotype associations were analyzed in JMP Pro software (version 18.0.0) using the same single-stage statistical models described above for the SNP genotype analyses, with diplotype replacing genotype as the fixed genetic effect. For LS and ABW, ewe identity was included as a random effect to account for repeated records. For BW, WW, YW, ADG1, ADG2, and ADG3, the same systematic fixed effects and continuous covariates used in the corresponding SNP models were retained. Adjusted least-squares means and standard errors were obtained directly from these models.

The P values derived from diplotype–phenotype association analyses were subjected to separate multiple-testing correction using the Bonferroni method. Associations with an adjusted (Bonferroni-adjusted P-value < 0.05) were considered statistically significant. For associations remaining significant after Bonferroni correction, pairwise comparisons among genotypes were performed using Tukey's HSD test.

Total RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted using an RNA/protein extraction kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop™ 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), and RNA integrity was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using the EasyScript One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) and stored at −20 °C until further use.

Detection of CCSER1 mRNA expression by qPCR

GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene, and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed to determine the expression profile of CCSER1 in 14 tissues of Junken meat sheep. The relative expression level of CCSER1 was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (17). Three biological replicates were included for each sample.

Based on the ovine CCSER1 and GAPDH reference transcript sequences (XM_042251242.2 and NM_001190390.1, respectively), primers were designed using the NCBI Primer-BLAST tool. All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and the primer information is shown in Table 2. The qPCR reaction was performed using LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master mix (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) strictly according to the manufacturer's instructions. qPCR detection was conducted using a LineGene 9600 Plus FQD-96A real-time PCR system (Bioer Technology, Hangzhou, China).

Results

Analysis of SNP loci in the CCSER1 gene

In this study, nonsynonymous mutations in the coding region of the CCSER1 gene were analyzed, and three missense variants were identified. According to the ARS-UI_Ramb_v3.0 reference genome, SNP1 was located at Chr6:33,699,675 in exon 11 with a genomic REF>ALT change of G>A. Relative to the reference transcript XM_042251242.2, this variant was annotated as c.2557C>T and p.Arg853Trp. SNP2 was located at Chr6:35,012,842 in exon 2 with a genomic T>C substitution and was annotated as c.1206A>G and p.Ile402Met. SNP3 was located at Chr6:35,014,032 in exon 2 with a genomic A>G substitution and was annotated as c.16T>C and p.Ser6Pro. Because CCSER1 is located on the reverse strand, the genomic REF>ALT alleles are complementary to the corresponding transcript-level HGVS nucleotide changes (Figure 1; Table 3).

Predicted effects of missense mutations on CCSER1 protein structure and PPI analysis

For the three missense mutation loci, the secondary and tertiary structures of the CCSER1 protein before and after mutation were visually predicted using SOPMA and SWISS-MODEL. The results showed that the protein encoded by the CCSER1 gene was mainly composed of three types of secondary structures, namely random coil, α-helix and extended strand (Figures 2A, B; Table 4). The SNP1 mutation decreased the proportions of α-helix and extended strand by 0.44% and 0.78%, respectively, while increasing the proportion of random coil by 1.22% (Figure 2C). The SNP2 mutation reduced the proportion of extended strand by 0.78% (Figure 2D), which was consistent with the predicted effect of the SNP1 mutation. In addition, the SNP2 mutation increased the proportions of α-helix and random coil by 0.67% and 0.11%, respectively (Figure 2D). The SNP3 mutation decreased the proportion of extended strand by 0.56% and increased the proportion of random coil by 0.56%, but did not affect the proportion of α-helix (Figure 2E). Collectively, all three mutations reduced the proportion of extended strand, which generally contributes to protein rigidity and structural stability, and increased the proportion of random coil, a structure characterized by high flexibility and disorder.

Comparison of the predicted tertiary structures before and after mutation showed that the three mutations had relatively minor effects on the overall tertiary structure of the CCSER1 protein. However, local structural changes were observed at the mutation sites. Specifically, SNP1 changed the side chain of the corresponding amino acid residue from an originally linear-like structure to a ring-like structure; SNP2 led to substitution of the local amino acid side-chain group; and SNP3 changed the side chain from an originally non-cyclic structure to a cyclic structure (Figure 3A). The predicted models showed minor local differences in side-chain conformation around the substituted residues, whereas the overall predicted protein structure remained largely similar.

Furthermore, to explore the potential interacting molecules and regulatory network of CCSER1 in biological processes, protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis was performed using the STRING database. The results showed that, under the low-confidence score threshold, CCSER1 was connected with several candidate proteins, including SHOC1, CRYBG1 and CNKSR1 (Figure 3B). Analysis of the network edge evidence indicated that the associations between CCSER1 and the above proteins were mainly supported by gene co-occurrence and text-mining evidence, whereas strong evidence for direct physical complex interactions was not detected.

Population genetic analysis of SNP loci in the CCSER1 gene

Based on the genotyping data, population genetic analysis of the SNP loci in the CCSER1 gene was further performed (Table 5). The results showed that three genotypes, GG, GA, and AA, were detected at SNP1, among which GG and GA were the predominant genotypes, and the G allele was the predominant allele. For SNP2, two genotypes, TT and TC, were detected, with the TT genotype and T allele being the predominant genotype and allele, respectively. For SNP3, three genotypes, AA, AG, and GG, were identified, among which the AG genotype and A allele were predominant.

Descriptive statistics of phenotypic traits

Following phenotypic quality control, 287 litter-size (LS) records and 283 average birth weight per litter (ABW) records were retained for the reproductive traits, whereas the numbers of valid records for BW, WW, YW, ADG1, ADG2, and ADG3 were 234, 212, 209, 199, 204, and 205, respectively (Figure 4; Table 6). The numbers of individuals and record count for each genotype of the three SNPs are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Mean LS was 1.93 ± 0.80 lambs per parity, with litter sizes of two lambs being the most frequent (44.2%), followed by one (32.7%), three (20.4%), and four lambs (2.7%). Mean ABW was 3.17 ± 0.62 kg, and 44.1% of the records were distributed within the 3.0–3.5 kg interval, followed by 23.4% within the 2.5–3.0 kg interval. For growth traits, mean BW, WW, and YW were 3.35 ± 0.75, 28.41 ± 6.87, and 53.71 ± 11.27 kg, respectively. The corresponding mean values for ADG1, ADG2, and ADG3 were 278.04 ± 72.06, 136.65 ± 32.77, and 92.14 ± 36.53 g/d, respectively. These phenotypic records were within the expected range for production performance and provided a data basis for subsequent phenotype association analysis.

Tissue-specific expression profile of the CCSER1 gene

The relative expression levels of CCSER1 in 14 tissues were determined using RT-qPCR. The results showed that CCSER1 expression levels in the lung, abomasum, and ovary were highly significantly higher than those in the liver, kidney, skin, brain, and pituitary gland (P < 0.01). Among all detected tissues, CCSER1 showed the highest relative expression level in the abomasum, which was highly significantly higher than those in the lung and ovary (P < 0.01). No significant difference in CCSER1 expression was observed between the lung and ovary (P > 0.05; Figure 7). In addition, the expression levels of CCSER1 in the heart, skeletal muscle, rumen, duodenum, ileum, and uterus were below the limit of detection. These results provide a preliminary descriptive profile of the tissue distribution of CCSER1 expression in Junken meat sheep.

Discussion

To date, extensive research has focused on molecular markers associated with growth and lambing traits in sheep. Previous investigations employing selection signature analysis, quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping, and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have yielded valuable evidence for screening candidate genes and advancing molecular breeding targeting reproductive and growth traits in sheep (18–21). However, sheep breeds vary greatly in genetic background, linkage disequilibrium patterns, allele frequencies and long-term selection objectives. Therefore, genome-wide candidate regions and genes cannot be directly utilized as molecular markers for marker-assisted selection within a single breed, necessitating further validation in the target experimental population (22, 23). Based on CCSER1, a candidate gene for litter size and yearling weight identified in our previous selection signature analysis, the present study identified polymorphisms in this gene in the Junken meat sheep population and systematically analyzed the associations of its genotypes and diplotypes with growth and lambing traits.

CCSER1 encodes a protein containing coiled-coil domains and serine-rich regions and is mainly involved in biological processes such as cytoskeletal organization, cell migration, and cell division (7, 8). In recent years, the genetic regulatory potential and pleiotropic effects of CCSER1 on economically important traits in livestock have gradually been revealed. Previous studies have shown that SNP variation in this gene significantly affects milk quality traits in Gannan yaks (24), that its copy number variation is associated with growth traits such as body weight and chest circumference in Chinese goat populations (25), and that CCSER1 has been identified as an important candidate gene in a GWAS of growth traits in Red Angus cattle (26). Notably, Lukonina et al. (27) detected strong positive selection signatures in the genomic region harboring CCSER1 in several sheep populations, including Salsk sheep, Suffolk sheep, and Texel sheep, using three within-population selection signature methods: integrated haplotype score (iHS), number of segregating sites by length (nSL), and integrated haplotype homozygosity pooled (iHH12). Collectively, these findings suggest that CCSER1 may be involved in the genetic regulation of reproductive performance and growth rate in sheep. However, its genetic variation and phenotypic effects in Junken meat sheep remain to be further validated.

In this study, three missense SNPs in the CCSER1 gene were identified using a candidate gene approach, including SNP1 ((XM_042251242.2: c.2557C>T) in exon 11 and SNP2 ((XM_042251242.2: c.1206A>G) and SNP3 ((XM_042251242.2: c.16T>C) in exon 2. Exploratory prediction analysis using SOPMA and SWISS-MODEL showed that the amino acid substitutions corresponding to these three SNPs may decrease the proportion of extended strand and increase the proportion of random coil, accompanied by structural changes in amino acid side chains. Similar missense mutation-induced shifts in protein secondary structure toward a more disordered and flexible state have been reported in studies of structural polymorphisms in breeds such as Subo Merino sheep, and such changes have been considered an important mechanism underlying alterations in conformational stability and substrate-binding affinity of target proteins (28). In addition, Schaefer and Rost reported, based on machine learning analyses, that side-chain alterations caused by single amino acid substitutions may change local protein conformation and thereby affect the structure and function of the encoded protein, as well as its physical binding capacity with cofactors (29, 30).

We further conducted population genetic analysis of the three SNP loci in Junken meat sheep. Junken meat sheep is a newly developed meat sheep breed independently bred in Xinjiang, China, in 2025, and has undergone long-term directional selection to improve lambing performance and growth performance (6). Population genetic parameters showed that SNP1 and SNP3 exhibited moderate polymorphism, whereas SNP2 showed low polymorphism. SNP2 and SNP3 conformed to Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE), while SNP1 deviated from HWE. Previous studies have demonstrated that deviations from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) at specific loci in livestock populations may arise from multiple biological and demographic factors, including long-term artificial selection, non-random mating, family structure, reduced effective population size, and population subdivision (31, 32). In livestock breeding populations, intensive selection programs and unequal reproductive contributions of elite individuals can reshape allele-frequency distributions and genetic relationships among individuals, thereby influencing genotype equilibrium at particular loci (33). Therefore, the deviation of SNP1 from HWE observed in the Junken meat sheep population may reflect the combined effects of breeding history, selection practices, and population genetic characteristics. Considering that the association signals of SNP1 did not remain significant after Bonferroni correction, SNP1 was not regarded as a reliable molecular genetic marker for growth or lambing-related traits in the current population. Interestingly, although SNP2 showed low polymorphism and a minor allele frequency (MAF) lower than 0.05 in the present population, individuals with the heterozygous genotype had highly significantly higher ABW than those with the wild-type genotype. Manolio et al. (34) reported that some low-frequency variants with significant phenotypic effects are important for explaining complex quantitative traits. In livestock populations, the presence of low-frequency favorable alleles may be associated with recent mutation, introgression, genetic drift, population-specific selection, or an ongoing breeding process in which such alleles have not yet been sufficiently fixed (35). Therefore, SNP2 and SNP3was retained in this study as potentially favorable variants and included in subsequent diplotype and phenotype association analyses to further evaluate its genetic effect and potential breeding value.

To further evaluate the robustness of the associations between CCSER1 variants and phenotypic traits in Junken meat sheep, genotype–phenotype and diplotype–phenotype associations were systematically assessed, followed by Bonferroni correction for multiple testing. Different mutation loci in CCSER1 showed significant effects on growth and lambing-related traits. Specifically, the TC genotype at SNP2 were identified as favorable genotypes for ABW, whereas the AA and AG genotypes at SNP3 showed significant positive effects on YW and ADG2. ABW is a key indicator reflecting maternal nutrient allocation during gestation and fetal developmental potential, and it may further affect postnatal health status, growth and development, and subsequent production performance of lambs (36). Meanwhile, body weight at 12 months old and average daily gain from birth to 12 months old are important reference indicators for evaluating the economic performance of commercial meat sheep (37). Therefore, the results of this study suggest that CCSER1 variants may be more closely involved in biological processes related to fetal developmental quality and postnatal growth and development.

Notably, no significant differences in BW were observed among individuals with different genotypes, whereas significant phenotypic divergence was observed in YW and ADG2. Khazaei-Koohpar et al. (38) reported that, during the early postnatal stage in sheep, maternal genetic effects and maternal environmental effects have additive effects on lamb body weight. After weaning, as lambs enter the stage of independent feeding, individual additive genetic effects gradually increase, leading to more pronounced differentiation in growth phenotypes. This finding not only suggests that CCSER1 may be involved in mid- to late-stage postweaning growth and development in sheep, but is also consistent with the study by Li et al. (11), who identified CCSER1 as a growth-related candidate gene in a GWAS of body weight traits at different ages in Hu sheep. To evaluate the cumulative genetic effects generated by multiple loci, haplotype analysis was performed based on the coordinated inheritance of linked variants within the gene (39). To evaluate the joint inheritance pattern of SNP2 and SNP3, chromosome-level haplotypes and individual diplotypes were reconstructed from phased genotype data. The two major haplotypes, T-A and T-G, together accounted for more than 95% of phased chromosomes, whereas C-containing haplotypes occurred at low frequencies. Among the six reconstructed diplotypes, T-A/T-G, T-A/T-A, and T-G/T-G were the three common classes retained for association analysis. The results showed that individuals carrying T-A/T-A and T-A/T-G showed significantly higher YW than those carrying T-G/T-G. This diplotype evidence not only helps overcome the limitation of weak effects from individual SNPs but also further supports the potential breeding value of CCSER1 as a molecular marker for improving meat sheep performance.

To further explore the potential biological basis underlying the association between CCSER1 and phenotypic differences in Junken meat sheep, a tissue expression profile analysis was performed in this study. The results showed that CCSER1 was predominantly expressed in the abomasum, ovary, and lung, with the highest expression level observed in the abomasum. The abomasum is the true stomach of ruminants and is the only one of the four gastric compartments that retains the ancestral mammalian glandular secretory function and performs the core function of protein digestion. It secretes large amounts of hydrochloric acid and pepsin (40, 41). Therefore, the high expression of CCSER1 in the abomasum may be associated with the maintenance of abomasal tissue function, epithelial cell renewal, or digestion-related physiological processes. Previous studies have reported that CCSER1 may participate in processes such as cytoskeletal organization, cell migration, and cell division (8). Ramljak et al. (42) found that CCSER1 was one of the genes under strong positive selection in local sheep breeds with high feed intake, suggesting a potential link between this gene and traits related to feed intake, digestion, or growth and development. The increased mechanical friction and feeding load caused by high feed intake may keep abomasal epithelial cells in a long-term state of shedding and renewal (43), accompanied by increased expression of many genes related to cell growth and proliferation in abomasal tissue (44).

Interestingly, CCSER1 also showed a certain level of expression in the ovary. Combined with the significant positive effect of the SNP2 mutation on ABW in the association analysis, this finding suggests that CCSER1 may have a potential relationship with ovarian function or early fetal developmental processes. ABW is jointly affected by multiple factors, including maternal nutrient supply, the gestational environment, placental function, and fetal developmental status (45, 46). Therefore, the association between CCSER1 and ABW may reflect its potential involvement in reproductive tissues or physiological processes related to fetal development. This is biologically consistent with the recent finding by Wang et al. (47), who reported that MMP16 may improve lambing performance in breeds such as Lop sheep by regulating the microenvironment of follicular development.

In addition, complex reproductive and growth-related traits such as ABW, YW, and ADG2 are often difficult to fully explain solely by the local expression pattern of a single gene (48–50). Previous studies based on the omnigenic model and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) in livestock have suggested that complex animal traits are not determined only by isolated major-effect genes, but are also shaped by the coordinated and integrated regulation of numerous small-effect genes within large and highly interconnected regulatory networks (51–54). In the present study, PPI analysis indicated that CCSER1 occupied a node within the interaction network and was connected with 10 proteins, including SHOC1, suggesting that CCSER1 may participate in the coordinated regulation of complex production traits and serve as a candidate gene associated with these traits in Junken meat sheep. This potential indirect cooperative mechanism requires further investigation through functional validation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, three missense SNPs, namely SNP1 (XM_042251242.2: c.2557C>T), SNP2 (XM_042251242.2: c.1206A>G), and SNP3 (XM_042251242.2: c.16T>C), were identified in the coding region of the CCSER1 gene. After single-stage association analysis and correction for multiple testing, SNP2 and SNP3 were significantly associated with growth- and early development-related traits, including ABW, YW, and ADG2. In addition, sheep carrying the T-A/T-A or T-A/T-G diplotypes formed by SNP2 and SNP3 exhibited significantly higher YW than those carrying the T-G/T-G diplotype. The high expression of CCSER1 in the abomasum and its moderate expression in the ovary further suggest that this gene may be involved in cellular renewal processes in ruminant digestive tissues and maternal reproductive organs. SNP2, SNP3, and the T-A/T-A and T-A/T-G diplotypes within the CCSER1 gene may therefore represent promising candidate markers for marker-assisted selection of lambing- and growth-related traits in Junken meat sheep, potentially providing a basis for improving average birth weight per litter, yearling weight, and average daily gain.
