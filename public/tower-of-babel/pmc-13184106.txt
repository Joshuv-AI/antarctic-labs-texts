Development and validation of a stability-indicating high-performance liquid chromatography method for iomeprol
Authors: Tingting Lou, Jiaqi He, Guoqing Wu, Luwei Wang, Hangyu Zhao, Yuchen Su, Yifu Kong, Huina Zhang, Yonghua Yu, Jie Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to develop and validate a reliable reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) method for the simultaneous separation and quantification of iomeprol and its related impurities in contrast agent injections. The method employed a Nanochrom ChromCore AQ C18 column with gradient elution using a mobile phase consisting of phosphate buffer and a mixture of water, acetonitrile, and 2-butanol. Through systematic optimization, the method effectively addressed the limitations of existing techniques, such as LC-MS/MS and online SPE-POPLC, including insufficient impurity resolution and high detection costs. Validated in accordance with ICH Q2(R1) guidelines, the method demonstrated excellent performance in all parameters: high specificity with baseline separation of all impurities and no interference from excipients; linearity over the range from LOQ to 200% (R² > 0.999); accuracy with recovery rates of 96%–101% (RSD < 3%); precision with RSD values below 2.0% for both repeatability and intermediate precision; and robustness under varied chromatographic conditions, with solutions remaining stable for up to 35 h. Forced degradation studies revealed that iomeprol is particularly susceptible to degradation under alkaline and photolytic conditions, highlighting the importance of pH control and light protection during formulation and storage. This RP-HPLC method provides a sensitive, accurate, precise, and robust analytical tool for quality control of iomeprol in both active pharmaceutical ingredients and finished products, offering significant technical support for enhancing drug quality and ensuring clinical medication safety.Keywords: HPLC, Iomeprol, Method Validation, Contrast AgentSubject terms: Chemistry, Drug discovery

Introduction

Iomeprol, as a non-ionic, low-osmolar contrast agent, has become a cornerstone in various radiological examinations—including contrast-enhanced CT scans, angiography, urography, and conventional selective coronary angiography—due to its high X-ray attenuation characteristics and osmolarity close to physiological levels1–4. In recent years, its clinical applications have continued to expand. Multiple studies have focused on improving its safety profile and diagnostic efficacy across different patient populations. These efforts include enhancing imaging safety in patients with renal impairment, optimizing contrast enhancement for early tumor diagnosis, systematically reducing the incidence of anaphylactoid reactions, and further exploring its applications in interventional radiology5–7.

This substantial market size and broad clinical use reflect the extensive global consumption of the agent. However, with the expanding patient base, safety concerns have attracted increasing attention. A large meta-analysis encompassing more than 1.13 million patients revealed that the overall incidence of acute adverse reactions with iomeprol was as high as 1.38%, significantly higher than that of other non-ionic low-osmolar contrast agents such as ioversol (0.34%). Moderate and severe reactions accounted for 0.27% and 0.040%, respectively8. Common adverse reactions include allergies, nausea, vomiting, headache, dizziness, and bronchospasm. There have also been case reports of severe hypersensitivity reactions (including anaphylactic shock). The carbamoyl side chain in its molecular structure is thought to be associated with its higher immunogenicity and increased risk of adverse reactions. In the impurity structures of iomeprol studied in this paper, all contain this side chain structure, which may increase the risk of adverse reactions to some extent. Therefore, controlling the level of impurities can further enhance the clinical safety of the drug9–12.

Among these adverse reactions, hypersensitivity or anaphylaxis represents one of the most severe risks associated with iodine-based contrast agents. Once systemic anaphylaxis occurs, it may lead to serious consequences including death. Therefore, improving the quality of the drug product is of paramount importance. Iomeprol Injection (Iomeron® 400) contains 81.65 g of iomeprol per 100 mL, which is a very high concentration among iodine-based contrast agents13. Inadequate control of any impurity may provoke adverse reactions in patients. Hence, the development of a reliable impurity detection method is urgently needed.

Currently, published methods for iomeprol detection primarily include liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) and online solid-phase extraction coupled with polarity-optimized liquid chromatography (SPE-POPLC)14,15. However, these methods exhibit significant limitations in terms of impurity resolution and/or simultaneous quantification of iomeprol and its impurities, in addition to high detection costs. For instance, the LC-MS/MS method developed by Rama Rao R et al.14 could detect three impurities in iomeprol, but the impurity peaks showed poor chromatographic shape (peak splitting) and high baseline noise, making the impurity peaks susceptible to baseline interference and compromising accuracy. Similar to other LC-MS/MS methods reported in the literature—such as the ultra-sensitive LC-MS/MS method for elobixibat determination in human plasma16 and the UPLC-ESI-MS/MS method for simultaneous quantification of amlodipine, benazepril and benazeprilat—the LC-MS/MS method for iomeprol analysis also faces inherent challenges in routine quality control: high instrument purchase and maintenance costs, strict operational requirements (needing professional technicians for mass spectrometer calibration, ion source cleaning, and parameter optimization), and potential matrix effects that may affect quantitative accuracy16. The UPLC method developed by Zhou T et al.15 was only capable of quantifying iomeprol content without effectively excluding interference from impurities, and its application was context-limited.

Hence, the development of a reliable impurity detection method is urgently needed. Stability-indicating analytical methods, which can simultaneously separate and quantify APIs and their degradation products, are essential for pharmaceutical quality control throughout the drug lifecycle17,18. For example, selective stability-indicating HPLC methods have been successfully developed for ertapenem and imipenem, enabling accurate determination of the APIs in the presence of their degradants generated under various stress conditions17. Similarly, a robust stability-indicating method was established for the simultaneous analysis of cyclopentolate hydrochloride and phenylephrine hydrochloride, effectively distinguishing the APIs from potential degradation products and ensuring product quality18. In light of these successful applications and the degradation risks of iomeprol (e.g., alkaline and photolytic degradation), the development of a stability-indicating HPLC method for iomeprol and its impurities is of great practical significance.

In light of these limitations, this study developed and validated a reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) method for the detection of iomeprol and its impurities. This method demonstrates high specificity, sensitivity, accuracy, and robustness, with well-resolved impurity peaks. The mobile phase consists primarily of phosphate salt, water, acetonitrile, and 2-butanol, without the need for special reagents or pH adjustment. When compared with the LC-MS/MS method by Rama Rao R (2021) and the UPLC method by Zhou T (2015), our method shows clear advantages: in terms of separation capacity, our method enables the simultaneous separation of 5 known impurities and unknown degradation impurities, achieving baseline separation between all impurities and the main component, whereas the former method can only detect 3 impurities and suffers from peak splitting and high baseline noise, and the latter can only quantify the main component without any data on impurity separation; in terms of sensitivity, our method has a limit of quantification (LOQ) as low as 0.067 µg/mL, which is far superior to the 0.1 µg/mL of the former method and 0.5 µg/mL of the latter; in terms of equipment requirements, our method can be implemented using a conventional Agilent 1260 HPLC (DAD) without the need for high-end mass spectrometry or UPLC equipment, whereas the former requires a professionally operated high-end LC-MS/MS system and the latter also has high instrument demands; in terms of application scenarios, our method is suitable for impurity QC testing of APIs and injection solutions, while the former is only applicable to laboratory-based qualitative studies of trace impurities and the latter is limited to pharmacokinetic analysis of the main component in human plasma.This method not only facilitates quality control of iomeprol but also provides a reliable tool for pharmacokinetic studies and therapeutic drug monitoring. By enabling accurate quantification of iomeprol and its related impurities, it enhances patient medication safety and supports therapeutic efficacy across various clinical scenarios13.

Chromatographic parameters

HPLC analysis was performed using a Nanochrom ChromCore AQ C18 column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm). The detection wavelength was set at 245 nm (for impurities A, B, C, D, and E). The injection volume was 10 µL, the flow rate was 1.0 mL/min, and the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C. Mobile phase A consisted of a 0.01 mol/L aqueous solution of potassium dihydrogen phosphate. Mobile phase B was composed of water/acetonitrile/2-butanol (50/45/5, v/v/v). Forced degradation study

Forced degradation studies were conducted by subjecting the drug to harsh stress conditions (such as strong light, high temperature, high humidity, acid, base, and oxidation) to accelerate degradation. This approach provided critical information on drug stability within a short period, offering scientific support for the entire drug lifecycle from development to market approval.

The forced degradation studies were conducted in accordance with the ICH Q1A(R2) guideline on Stability Testing of New Drug Substances and Products, as well as the ICH Q1B guideline on Photostability Testing of New Drug Substances and Products. These guidelines provide a scientific framework for stress testing to understand the intrinsic stability of the drug substance and to support the validation of analytical methods. The experimental conditions (acid, base, oxidation, thermal, and photolytic) were designed to achieve an appropriate level of degradation to assess the method’s ability to separate and quantify degradation products without interference.

Acid degradation

Test solution: 1 mL of iomeprol injection was accurately transferred into a 200 mL volumetric flask. Then, 1 mL of 1 mol/L hydrochloric acid solution was added and mixed well. The mixture was kept at room temperature for 4 h. Afterward, 1 mL of 1 mol/L sodium hydroxide solution was added to neutralize the acid, and the solution was diluted to volume with water and mixed well. Then, 5 mL of this solution was accurately transferred into a 20 mL volumetric flask, diluted to volume with water, and mixed well.

Alkaline degradation

Test solution: 1 mL of iomeprol injection was accurately transferred into a 200 mL volumetric flask. Then, 1 mL of 1 mol/L sodium hydroxide solution was added and mixed well. The mixture was placed in an 80 °C water bath for 1 h. Afterward, 1 mL of 1 mol/L hydrochloric acid solution was added to neutralize the base, and the solution was diluted to volume with water and mixed well. Then, 5 mL of this solution was accurately transferred into a 20 mL volumetric flask, diluted to volume with water, and mixed well.

Oxidative degradation

Test solution: 1 mL of iomeprol injection was accurately transferred into a 200 mL volumetric flask. Then, 1 mL of 30% hydrogen peroxide solution was added and mixed well. The mixture was kept at room temperature for 4 h, then diluted to volume with water and mixed well. Then, 5 mL of this solution was accurately transferred into a 20 mL volumetric flask, diluted to volume with water, and mixed well.

Photolytic degradation

Test solution: One bottle of iomeprol injection was placed in a light chamber for 48 h. Then, 1 mL was accurately transferred into a 200 mL volumetric flask, diluted to volume with water, and mixed well. Then, 5 mL of this solution was accurately transferred into a 20 mL volumetric flask, diluted to volume with water, and mixed well.

Thermal degradation

Test solution: One bottle of iomeprol injection was placed in an oven at 105 °C for 4 h. After cooling to room temperature, 1 mL was accurately transferred into a 200 mL volumetric flask, diluted to volume with water, and mixed well. Then, 5 mL of this solution was accurately transferred into a 20 mL volumetric flask, diluted to volume with water, and mixed well.

Unstressed sample

Test solution: 1 mL of iomeprol injection was accurately transferred into a 200 mL volumetric flask, diluted to volume with water, and mixed well. Then, 5 mL of this solution was accurately transferred into a 20 mL volumetric flask, diluted to volume with water, and mixed well.

Placebo and blank solvent groups

The degradation procedures for the placebo and blank solvent were consistent with those applied to the sample.

Method optimization

During the initial method development process, under the detection wavelength of 245 nm, with an injection volume set at 10 µL, a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, and a column temperature of 40 °C, Mobile Phase A was a 0.01 mol/L aqueous potassium dihydrogen phosphate solution. Mobile Phase B consisted of water/acetonitrile in a volume ratio of 50/50. Several brands of chromatographic columns were evaluated, such as Thermo Hypersil ODS-2, SHIMADZU VP-ODS, Agilent 5 HC-C18, ChromCore AQ C18, and AmethSeP T3. However, regardless of the column used, the resolution between impurity B and the main peak was insufficient. Consequently, increasing the column temperature to 60 °C was attempted, which significantly improved the separation of impurities. Nevertheless, such a high column temperature led to a sharp decline in column efficiency, and currently, very few commercially available columns can withstand 60 °C, making this temperature unsuitable for routine analysis.

Without altering the column temperature, 2-butanol was introduced to enhance the elution capability of the impurities in the organic phase (typically, in reversed-phase systems, the elution strength of 2-butanol is greater than that of acetonitrile and methanol). The results showed that when the proportion of 2-butanol in Mobile Phase B reached 5%, baseline separation from the main peak was achieved (resolution reached 1.7). When the proportion was below 5%, the retention time of impurity B gradually shifted earlier, merging with the main peak. When the proportion exceeded 5%, the separation capability did not improve significantly. Therefore, the final composition selected for Mobile Phase B was water/acetonitrile/2-butanol in a volume ratio of 50/45/5.

Method validation

The method validation experiments in this study, including the assessments of specificity, linearity, accuracy, precision (repeatability and intermediate precision), detection limit (LOD), quantitation limit (LOQ), and robustness, were designed and performed in strict accordance with the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) guideline Q2(R1): Validation of Analytical Procedures: Text and Methodology.

System suitability

Approximately 80 mg of iomeprol reference standard was accurately weighed and placed in a 20 mL volumetric flask. An appropriate volume of purified water was added, and the mixture was sonicated to dissolve the standard completely before being cooled to room temperature. Then 0.2 mL of Impurity A stock standard solution (200 µg/mL), 0.2 mL of Impurity B stock standard solution (400 µg/mL), 2.0 mL of Impurity C stock standard solution (160 µg/mL), 0.2 mL of Impurity D stock standard solution (400 µg/mL) and 0.2 mL of Impurity E stock standard solution (400 µg/mL) were precisely pipetted into the aforementioned 20 mL volumetric flask. The flask was diluted to the mark with purified water and shaken thoroughly to mix uniformly, yielding the system suitability solution with a concentration of approximately 4 mg/mL for iomeprol, 2 µg/mL for Impurity A, 4 µg/mL for Impurity B, 16 µg/mL for Impurity C, 4 µg/mL for Impurity D and 4 µg/mL for Impurity E.At least one injection of the blank solvent was performed, followed by five consecutive injections of the system suitability solution. One injection each of the blank excipient solution and the test sample solution was also carried out.

Specificity

By analyzing the blank solvent, blank placebo, and system suitability solution, it was confirmed that no interference from the blank solvent or blank placebo was observed for the impurities and the main component (ensuring no interference at the peak positions of the impurities and the main component). Simultaneously, the resolution between known impurities, the main component, and unknown impurities in the chromatogram was examined. Through the analysis of forced degradation samples, the separation between impurities generated under stress conditions and known impurities was further verified to evaluate the specificity of the method.

Limit of quantification (LOQ) and limit of detection (LOD)

Appropriate volumes of the iomeprol and impurity standard stock solutions were accurately measured and serially diluted with water to achieve a signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) ≥ 10 for the determination of the LOQ concentration, and diluted to S/N ≥ 3 for the determination of the LOD concentration. Subsequently, six replicates of the LOQ solution were prepared in parallel and injected into the HPLC system. The relative standard deviation (RSD) of the peak areas for the six LOQ injections was calculated.

Linearity and range

Iomeprol and its Impurities A–E linear solutions at the L1 to L5 levels were prepared using water as the diluent. The reference substances were accurately weighed to prepare 100 mL of individual standard stock solutions with the concentrations as follows: iomeprol at 1000 µg/mL, Impurities A, B, D and E each at 200 µg/mL, and Impurity C at 1600 µg/mL. Each of the above stock solutions was then diluted with water at a volume ratio of 1:9 to prepare 10-fold intermediate dilutions, which were exclusively used for the preparation of the L1 linear solution. Five 1000 mL Class A volumetric flasks were taken and labeled L1 to L5, respectively. 1 mL of each 10-fold intermediate dilution was transferred into the volumetric flask labeled L1. For the volumetric flasks labeled L2 to L5, the corresponding volumes of the individual standard stock solutions were transferred successively, with the volumes being 0.2, 0.4, 0.6 and 0.8 mL for iomeprol, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0 mL for Impurity A, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0 and 4.0 mL for each of Impurities B, D and E, and 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0 mL for Impurity C, respectively. Water was added to each volumetric flask to near the graduation mark; the solutions were allowed to cool to room temperature and then the volume was made up to the 1000 mL graduation mark with water. All the resulting solutions were mixed thoroughly.These solutions were injected into the HPLC system for analysis. The linear correlation coefficients of each impurity were confirmed, and the correction factors for each impurity were evaluated within the concentration range from the limit of quantification (LOQ) to 200%.

Accuracy(spiked sample preparation)

For the preparation of accuracy spiked solutions of Impurities A–E in iomeprol at the spiking levels of 10%, 50%, 100% and 150%, the reference substances of each impurity were accurately weighed to prepare 100 mL of individual standard stock solutions separately, with the concentrations of 200 µg/mL for Impurities A, B, D and E and 1600 µg/mL for Impurity C. The reference substances were ultrasonically dissolved in water, allowed to cool and then made up to the marked volume, followed by thorough mixing. Four 1000 mL Class A volumetric flasks were taken and labeled corresponding to the above spiking levels, and the respective volumes of each stock solution were accurately pipetted into the flasks as follows: 10% level (Impurity A: 1.0 mL, Impurity B: 2.0 mL, Impurity C: 0.1875 mL, Impurity D: 2.0 mL, Impurity E: 2.0 mL); 50% level (Impurity A: 5.0 mL, Impurity B: 10.0 mL, Impurity C: 5.0 mL, Impurity D: 10.0 mL, Impurity E: 10.0 mL); 100% level (Impurity A: 10.0 mL, Impurity B: 20.0 mL, Impurity C: 10.0 mL, Impurity D: 20.0 mL, Impurity E: 20.0 mL); 150% level (Impurity A: 15.0 mL, Impurity B: 30.0 mL, Impurity C: 15.0 mL, Impurity D: 30.0 mL, Impurity E: 30.0 mL). Finally, water was added to each flask to near the graduation mark, the solutions were allowed to cool and then made up to the 1000 mL graduation mark with water, and thorough mixing was performed to obtain the accuracy spiked solutions at all spiking levels.Three replicate samples shall be prepared for each recovery solution to evaluate the accuracy of the method.

Precision

Repeatability

The reference substances of Impurities A, B, C, D and E were accurately weighed to prepare individual standard stock solutions with a constant volume of 100 mL respectively, among which the concentration of the stock solutions for Impurities A, B, D and E was 200 µg/mL and that for Impurity C was 1600 µg/mL. Each reference substance was ultrasonically dissolved in water, allowed to cool to room temperature, then made up to the marked volume and mixed thoroughly. Six 1000 mL Class A volumetric flasks were taken and labeled as Repeatability Sample 1 to 6 in sequence. 10.0 mL of Impurity A stock solution, 20.0 mL of Impurity B stock solution, 10.0 mL of Impurity C stock solution, 20.0 mL of Impurity D stock solution and 20.0 mL of Impurity E stock solution were accurately pipetted into each volumetric flask respectively. Subsequently, water was added to each volumetric flask to near the graduation mark, the solutions were allowed to cool to room temperature and then made up to the 1000 mL graduation mark with water and mixed thoroughly, thus obtaining six replicate test solutions at the 100% spiking level.

Intermediate precision

Following the procedure described in Section “Intermediate precision”, test samples were prepared and analyzed under different conditions (different days, analysts, and instruments) to evaluate intermediate precision. The resulting data were collectively analyzed and compared with the repeatability data.

Robustness

This study conducted a systematic robustness evaluation of the established chromatographic analysis method to demonstrate its ability to maintain analytical performance when intentional, minor variations were introduced to key method parameters. These deliberate changes included adjustments to the flow rate of the mobile phase (± 0.1 mL/min), column temperature (± 2 °C), column brand, analyst, and instrument. Additionally, the stability of the solutions themselves was investigated to ensure the accuracy of injection results and to help reduce associated testing costs for both enterprises and researchers. This evaluation is crucial for ensuring the reliability and reproducibility of the method in real-world pharmaceutical quality control environments.

Forced degradation results

As shown in Table 4; Fig. 3, the forced degradation data demonstrated that baseline separation was achieved between all impurities. Neither the blank solvent nor the blank excipient interfered with the peaks of the impurities or iomeprol. The purity of the main peak met the specified requirements, and the mass balance ranged between 90% and 110%, indicating excellent specificity of the method.

Forced degradation chromatogram of the sample.

Based on the degradation behavior observed, iomeprol was highly susceptible to degradation under alkaline conditions, with rapid increases in impurity C and total impurities. Although iomeprol contains primary/secondary alcoholic groups (prone to oxidation) and an amide group (prone to acidic hydrolysis) in its molecular structure, the API of iomeprol showed no significant degradation under the ICH-compliant mild acidic stress conditions (1 mol/L HCl, room temperature, 4 h) in this study, which is attributed to the steric hindrance of bulky iodine-substituted benzene rings and alkyl groups around the amide group and the reduced electron density of hydroxyl groups caused by electron-withdrawing iodine-containing aromatic groups. This result suggests that the API of iomeprol should be maintained under acidic pH conditions, and its formulated aqueous solution should be controlled at approximately pH 7.0 to ensure stability.

A slight increase in impurities was observed under photolytic conditions, indicating that iomeprol exhibits certain sensitivity to light. Therefore, protection from light should be emphasized during daily storage and transportation. Under the controlled oxidative stress conditions (30% H₂O₂, room temperature, 4 h) and thermal stress conditions (105℃, 4 h) in this study, iomeprol showed no significant degradation, which is the combined result of the electronic effect of its molecular functional groups and the mild stress conditions in accordance with ICH guidelines for method validation.

These degradation studies demonstrate that iomeprol injection and its API show relative stability under routine production and storage conditions (room temperature, neutral pH, light protection) and mild stress conditions compliant with ICH guidelines, while being highly susceptible to alkaline degradation and slightly sensitive to light, with light protection and strict pH control being the key precautions required.

LOD and LOQ

The results for the limit of quantitation (LOQ) and limit of detection (LOD) were calculated using Agilent Data Analysis OpenLab CDS 2.7 software. Tables 5 and 6 present the validation data for LOD and LOQ. The results demonstrate that under the current method, the LOQ concentrations for impurity A, impurity B, impurity C, impurity D, impurity E, and iomeprol are 0.067 µg/mL, 0.101 µg/mL, 0.135 µg/mL, 0.101 µg/mL, 0.108 µg/mL, and 0.101 µg/mL, respectively, which correspond to 0.002% of the sample concentration. The signal-to-noise ratios at the LOQ level were all above 10. Moreover, the low coefficient of variation among the peak areas of six consecutive LOQ samples indicates that the method exhibits sensitive and stable detection capabilities for trace impurities. Discussion

This study established an HPLC method for the analysis of impurities in iomeprol injection and demonstrated satisfactory analytical performance across multiple validation parameters. A comprehensive discussion regarding the overall performance, practical applicability, and existing limitations of the method is provided below.

Forced degradation studies revealed that iomeprol is highly susceptible to degradation under alkaline conditions, which is consistent with the presence of alkali-labile carbamoyl side chains and ester linkages in its molecular structure. Although iomeprol has inherent chemical characteristics of containing oxidation-prone primary/secondary alcoholic groups and hydrolysis-prone amide groups, it showed no significant degradation under the mild acidic and oxidative stress conditions designed in this study (in accordance with ICH Q1A (R2) and Q1B guidelines). This is due to the steric hindrance effect of the bulky groups around the amide group (reducing the accessibility of H⁺ to the carbonyl group) and the electronic effect of the electron-withdrawing iodine-containing aromatic groups (lowering the electron density and reactivity of alcoholic hydroxyl groups). The development of such a stability-indicating method aligns with the principles of quality by design (QbD) in pharmaceutical analysis, as demonstrated by a recent QbD-driven RP-HPLC method for dihydropyridines calcium channel blockers, which emphasized systematic optimization and robustness to ensure method reliability19. Furthermore, stability-indicating methods are not only limited to small-molecule drug analysis but also play a crucial role in biosimilarity assessment, as shown in the orthogonal biosimilarity testing of etanercept, where green and white analytical approaches were integrated to ensure product consistency20. These studies collectively highlight the broad application and critical importance of stability-indicating HPLC methods in pharmaceutical quality assurance, which further confirms the practical value of our established method.

Firstly, the method proved to be robust in terms of system suitability, specificity, and stability. The resolution between each impurity and the main component met analytical requirements, and the retention times and peak areas showed good reproducibility. Neither the blank solvent nor the excipient blank interfered with the detection of the impurities or the main component, indicating high stability and anti-interference capability of the chromatographic system. Forced degradation studies revealed that iomeprol is highly susceptible to degradation under alkaline conditions, which is consistent with the presence of alkali-labile carbamoyl side chains and ester linkages in its molecular structure. Although iomeprol has inherent chemical characteristics of containing oxidation-prone primary/secondary alcoholic groups and hydrolysis-prone amide groups, it showed no significant degradation under the mild acidic and oxidative stress conditions designed in this study (in accordance with ICH Q1A (R2) and Q1B guidelines). This is due to the steric hindrance effect of the bulky groups around the amide group (reducing the accessibility of H⁺ to the carbonyl group) and the electronic effect of the electron-withdrawing iodine-containing aromatic groups (lowering the electron density and reactivity of alcoholic hydroxyl groups). This highlights the need for strict pH control and careful selection of packaging materials during formulation development and storage—particularly for injections—where compatibility tests of primary packaging must be conducted to ensure no alkaline leachables migrate into the drug product. Additionally, a slight increase in impurities was observed during photodegradation studies, indicating that the product should be protected from light to ensure clinical safety. The use of amber glass vials or light-protective secondary packaging is recommended, and harsh acidic/oxidative conditions should be avoided during the production and transportation process.

In terms of linearity and accuracy, all impurities exhibited good linear responses over a wide concentration range. Recovery rates were consistent and close to 100%, demonstrating high quantitative accuracy and reliability of the method for precise impurity monitoring. Precision studies, including intermediate precision involving different analysts, instruments, and time points, showed highly consistent results, confirming that the method is reproducible and transferable across different laboratories. The LOQ validation demonstrated that the lowest concentration accurately quantified was 0.067 µg/mL, ensuring that no impurities would go undetected during drug analysis.

Solution stability studies indicated that the major analytical solutions remained stable for up to 35 h, sufficiently covering the duration of routine testing. Robustness testing confirmed that minor variations in chromatographic conditions did not affect the results, reducing operational stringency for analysts and further enhancing the method’s practicality in quality control workflows.

Compared with existing methods reported in the literature, the present method offers higher impurity recovery rates (close to 96%–101%), improved separation capability (simultaneously resolving five or more impurities), and higher sensitivity. Moreover, it does not require special reagents or materials, reducing potential health risks to operators, and can be performed using conventional HPLC systems. In contrast to LC-MS or other techniques, this method is more straightforward and cost-effective. As demonstrated by the HPLC-ESI-MS/MS method for antimicrobial multi-residue determination in fish tissues, LC-MS/MS often requires complex sample pretreatment (e.g., solid-phase extraction, derivatization) to eliminate matrix interference and ion suppression effects, which increases operational complexity and analysis time. In contrast, our HPLC method only requires simple dilution of iomeprol injection samples (1 mL sample diluted to 200 mL) without additional pretreatment, and the mobile phase (0.01 mol/L potassium dihydrogen phosphate solution, water/acetonitrile/2-butanol = 50/45/5, v/v/v) is easy to prepare and low-cost. Additionally, our method avoids the ion source contamination and periodic maintenance issues of LC-MS/MS, and the solution stability of up to 35 h further adapts to the needs of batch sample testing in routine quality control.

Furthermore, compared with LC-MS/MS methods that rely on specific mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) detection16, our HPLC method based on ultraviolet absorption detection (245 nm) has better compatibility with different matrix samples, and there is no risk of false negative results caused by ion suppression or compound fragmentation. This advantage is particularly important for the detection of unknown degradation impurities (e.g., new impurities generated under alkaline stress), as HPLC can effectively separate all impurities from the main component based on retention time differences, while LC-MS/MS may miss some unknown impurities that are difficult to ionize.

This study conducted a systematic robustness evaluation of the established chromatographic method. The results demonstrate that the method exhibits excellent compatibility with different brands of chromatographic columns. This is particularly significant in the context of potential global supply chain instability, as it provides laboratories with procurement flexibility. It allows for switching between columns from different suppliers without the need for re-validation, significantly reducing both costs and time. Additionally, minor fluctuations in flow rate and column temperature were shown to have no impact on the results. This indicates that the method can generate reliable data even with slight instrumental variations or when used in laboratories operating under different climatic conditions, thereby ensuring the continuity and consistency of product quality assessment.

Furthermore, the consistent method performance across different analysts and instruments is a critical prerequisite for its successful transfer to other quality control laboratories—for instance, from R&D departments to production quality control units, or between different manufacturing sites. The findings strongly confirm that the method exhibits low dependency on both the operator and the equipment, which substantially reduces the risk of transfer failure. This ensures the comparability of test results across different locations and provides a solid foundation for product consistency and regulatory compliance.

Moreover, the robustness of an analytical method is vital for managing a drug’s lifecycle, which can span several decades. During this period, instruments may be upgraded, reagent batches will change, and personnel will turnover. A robust method is capable of withstanding these inevitable minor variations, ensuring that the impurity profile data collected throughout the product’s entire lifecycle remains stable and comparable. This is crucial for tracking impurity trends, evaluating the impact of process changes, and fulfilling long-term regulatory compliance requirements for drug registration.

Although the method performs excellently in many aspects, there is still room for improvement. Firstly, the current run time of 55 min may not be ideal for high-throughput routine quality control. It is important to note that this duration was deliberately designed based on the unique characteristics of iomeprol injection rather than an inherent inability to shorten it: iomeprol Injection (Iomeron® 400) has an ultra-high concentration of 81.65 g/100 mL, leading to strong adsorption of the analyte and its impurities on the chromatographic column packing. Thus, an extended post-run re-equilibration time of nearly 20 min was incorporated to ensure complete elution of all components and prevent carryover between consecutive injections, which is a critical prerequisite for the accurate quantification of trace impurities (LOQ as low as 0.067 µg/mL). Nevertheless, we propose feasible optimization strategies for shortening the run time in subsequent research to adapt to high-throughput analysis needs: (1) Optimize the gradient elution program by appropriately increasing the initial proportion of mobile phase B and accelerating the gradient ramp rate, while shortening the re-equilibration time; (2) Dilute the sample solution from 4 mg/mL to 1 mg/mL (ensuring impurity concentration above LOQ) to reduce column adsorption and shorten the re-equilibration period; (3) Employ a short chromatographic column (e.g., 4.6 mm × 100 mm/150 mm) to significantly reduce the retention time of all components. These strategies are dedicated to routine fast quality control scenarios (e.g., batch release testing) where high-throughput analysis is the main demand, and we will conduct full validation of the optimized short-run-time method in accordance with ICH Q2(R1) guidelines.

Secondly, the stability results of iomeprol in this study are based on mild stress conditions compliant with ICH Q1A(R2) and Q1B guidelines for analytical method validation. To supplement the complete stability profile of iomeprol, further in-depth studies will be conducted to investigate its degradation behavior under harsher acidic/oxidative conditions (e.g., higher acid/oxidant concentrations, elevated temperatures, longer reaction times), which will provide more comprehensive theoretical support for its industrial production, long-term storage, and transportation.

In the future, this stability-indicating HPLC method may be further applied to long-term stability monitoring of actual iomeprol API and formulation samples. It can also be combined with other analytical techniques (e.g., LC-MS/MS) to identify unknown degradation products generated under extreme stress conditions, thereby more comprehensively evaluating the quality attributes of iomeprol products and providing stronger technical support for drug safety assurance.

Conclusion

The stability-indicating HPLC method developed in this study has been comprehensively validated, with all metrics complying with the requirements of the ICH Q2 (R1) guideline. It demonstrates excellent specificity, linearity, accuracy, precision, and robustness, and can effectively separate and quantify iomeprol and its degradation products under various stress conditions (alkaline, photolytic, acidic, oxidative, thermal). The method also takes into account the inherent chemical characteristics of iomeprol (containing primary/secondary alcoholic groups and amide groups), and the stability results obtained under mild stress conditions provide a scientific basis for the routine quality control of iomeprol, indicating high practical value and broad application potential in the analysis of iomeprol drug substances and related formulations. Although there remains room for improvement in certain aspects, the method already provides a reliable analytical tool for quality control and safety assessment of this drug. It offers strong support for clinical medication safety and also establishes a foundation for pharmacokinetic studies and formulation development.
