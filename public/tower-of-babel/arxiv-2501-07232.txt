Protecting collective qubits from non-Markovian dephasing
Antoine Covolo, Valentin Magro, Mathieu Girard, Sébastien Garcia, Alexei Ourjoumtsev

Abstract
In quantum technologies, qubits encoded as collective atomic or solid-state excitations present important practical advantages, such as strong directional coupling to light. Unfortunately, they are affected by inhomogeneities between the emitters, which make them decay into dark states. In most cases, this process is non-Markovian. Through a simple formalism, we unveil a regime where this decay is suppressed by a combination of driving and non-Markovianity. We experimentally demonstrate this “driving protection” using a Rydberg superatom, making its coherent Rabi oscillations last about 14 14 times longer than the free lifetime of the collective qubit.



I
Introduction

Quantum systems are never fully closed. Describing their interactions with their classical environment in an accurate and tractable way is essential in various contexts, in particular in quantum technologies. The textbook scenario assumes that these interactions are constant, and that the resulting correlations between the system and the environment instantly vanish without affecting the latter. For a qubit carried by a two-level atom, these interactions lead to three effects: the decay of the excited state, its excitation by thermal photons, and pure dephasing. In this Born-Markov approximation, the coherence of the system always decays exponentially.

Qubits can also be encoded as collective excitations of many emitters, acting together like a quantum phased array antenna. The excited state of the qubit is then an exchange-symmetric Dicke state
[
1
]
, with a single excitation delocalized over the whole ensemble. This encoding is the cornerstone of heralded quantum information processing with atomic ensembles
[
2
]
, of quantum memories using rare-earth ion-doped crystals or atomic gases
[
3
]
, and of recent quantum optics experiments using Rydberg superatoms
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
. Among other practical advantages, it offers a directional coupling to light enhanced with respect to that of a single emitter
[
7
]
. However, collective encoding suffers from an inherent drawback: crystalline defects, inhomogeneous external fields, or thermal atomic motion randomly shift the transition frequencies of the emitters. With time, the phases between the elements of the “antenna” become random, scrambling its emission. In other words, inhomogeneous dephasing makes the qubit’s excited state decay in a quasi-continuum of dark, asymmetric Dicke states.

The Born-Markov approximation generally fails to describe this effect. To see this, let us consider

N\gg 1
two-level emitters with angular frequencies
\omega_{n}
, carrying a qubit encoded in the states
\left|G\right\rangle=\prod_{n=1}^{N}|g^{(n)}\rangle
where all the emitters are in the ground state
g
, and
\left|E_{0}\right\rangle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\hat{\sigma}}_{eg}^{(n)}\left|G\right\rangle/\sqrt{N}
where
{\hat{\sigma}}_{eg}^{(n)}=|e^{(n)}\rangle\langle g^{(n)}|
promotes the emitter
n
into the excited state
e
, such that the excitation is fully delocalized. After a time
t
, each term in this sum accumulates a phase factor
{\textrm{e}}^{-i\omega_{n}t}
. If the frequencies
\omega_{n}
follow a probability distribution
P(\omega)
, an ergodic approximation shows that the overlap between the initial state
\left|E_{0}\right\rangle
and the final state
\left|E(t)\right\rangle
obeys

s(t)=\overline{\left\langle E_{0}\middle|E(t)\right\rangle}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\overline{{\textrm{e}}^{-i\omega_{n}t}}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}\omega P(\omega){\textrm{e}}^{-i\omega t}.

(1)

Unless
P(\omega)
is a Lorentzian, the decay of
s(t)
is not exponential: the quasi-continuum of dark states has a memory, allowing some information to flow back into the system.

Equation
1
is sometimes used as-is
[
8
,
9
]
. However, it assumes that the system is excited at a well-known moment, quasi-instantaneously with respect to the dephasing. While reasonable for heralded collective atomic spin qubits
[
2
]
, this assumption fails for temporally multimode quantum memories
[
10
]
, where the preparation time is undefined, and for Rydberg superatoms, where the dephasing is currently not negligible at the timescales of single-qubit gates. Moreover, as shown below, this decay can dramatically change in presence of driving. One is then left with two unappealing choices: keeping
N-1
irrelevant states in the density matrix, or using a generalized master equation
[
11
]
to describe the evolution. Both are resource-intensive, and obscure much of the relevant physics.

\Omega

\left|\psi_{\pm}\right\rangle
and the

Here, we show that the non-Markovianity of this decay process can not only be efficiently modeled, but also used to suppress it with remarkably little experimental effort. By mapping the inhomogeneous dephasing onto a displacement in time-frequency phase space, we construct a ladder of sequentially coupled Dicke states (Fig.
1
(a),(b),(c)) and show that they can be determined analytically in many physically-relevant cases. Including the first few of them in the system’s Hilbert space and treating the others as a broad continuum provides a precise and resource-efficient description of the non-Markovian dynamics. In an analytically-tractable limit, we identify a process akin to cavity protection
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]

where the qubit’s decay is inhibited by a combination of non-Markovianity and strong driving (Fig.
1
(d-g)). We experimentally demonstrate this effect by using a Rydberg superatom undergoing two spectrally different non-Markovian processes. Consistently with the predictions of our model, we observe that its coherent Rabi oscillations can last up to
\sim 14
times longer than the characteristic inhomogeneous dephasing time.

II
Theoretical model

Using the definitions above, let us consider the effect of the inhomogeneous dephasing Hamiltonian
{\hat{H}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}{\hat{\sigma}}_{ee}^{(n)}
on a single excitation in the ensemble (
\hbar=1
in the following). We rewrite
\omega_{n}=\sqrt{2}\omega_{\mathrm{S}}\,x_{n}
where
x_{n}
is a dimensionless random variable governed by a probability distribution
\rho(x)=\sqrt{2}\omega_{\mathrm{S}}P(\sqrt{2}\omega_{\mathrm{S}}\,x)
. The scaling rate
\omega_{\mathrm{S}}
leaves some freedom for optimizing the latter.
Assuming ergodicity with
N\gg 1
, we approximate the sum over
n
by
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}x
written as
\int\hbox{{d}}x
to simplify notations, and replace the discrete operators
{\hat{\sigma}}_{ij}^{(n)}
with
{\hat{\sigma}}_{ij}(x)
satisfying

[{\hat{\sigma}}_{ij}(x),{\hat{\sigma}}_{kl}(x^{\prime})]=\delta(x-x^{\prime})(\delta_{jk}{\hat{\sigma}}_{il}(x)-\delta_{il}{\hat{\sigma}}_{kj}(x)).

We then rewrite
{\hat{H}}=\sqrt{2}\omega_{\mathrm{S}}\int\hbox{{d}}x\,x\,{\hat{\sigma}}_{ee}(x)
and
\left|E_{0}\right\rangle=\int\hbox{{d}}x\,\psi_{0}(x){\hat{\sigma}}_{eg}(x)\left|G\right\rangle
where the wavefunction
\psi_{0}(x)=\sqrt{\rho(x)}{\textrm{e}}^{i\phi(x)}
satisfies
|\psi_{0}(x)|^{2}=\rho(x)
, the arbitrary phase
\phi(x)
leaving the choice of the most convenient gauge.

In the single excitation regime considered here, the dimensionless frequency
x
and its conjugate dimensionless time
p
can be treated as operators equivalent to position and momentum, with
[{\hat{x}},{\hat{p}}]=i

[
16
]
. Then
{\hat{\sigma}}_{eg}(x)\left|G\right\rangle=\left|x\right\rangle
is formally equivalent to a usual continuous-variable position eigenstate, giving
\left|E_{0}\right\rangle=\int\hbox{{d}}x\,\psi_{0}(x)\left|x\right\rangle
and
{\hat{H}}=\sqrt{2}\omega_{\mathrm{S}}\int\hbox{{d}}x\,x\left|x\middle\rangle\middle\langle x\right|=\sqrt{2}\omega_{\mathrm{S}}\,{\hat{x}}
, which allows us to interpret the evolution
\hat{U}(t)=\exp(-i{\hat{H}}t)
as a phase-space displacement in the direction
-p
by a distance
\sqrt{2}\omega_{\mathrm{S}}\,t
. Eq.
1
is recovered via

s(t)=\left\langle E_{0}\right|\hat{U}(t)\left|E_{0}\right\rangle=\int\hbox{{d}}x\rho(x){\textrm{e}}^{-i\sqrt{2}\omega_{\mathrm{S}}\,x\,t}.

(2)

We consider three physical examples:

•

A Markovian process, with a Lorentzian spectrum
\rho_{\mathrm{M}}(x)=2/(\pi(1+4x^{2}))
and a population decay rate
\sqrt{2}\omega_{\mathrm{M}}
, where we can choose
\psi_{\mathrm{M},0}(x)=\sqrt{2/\pi}/(1+i2x)
. As expected, Eq.
2
leads to an exponential decay of the amplitude,
s_{\mathrm{M}}(t)={\textrm{e}}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{M}}t/\sqrt{2}}
.

•

Gaussian inhomogeneous dephasing, with
\rho_{\mathrm{G}}(x)={\textrm{e}}^{-x^{2}}/\sqrt{\pi}
and
\psi_{G,0}(x)=\sqrt{\rho_{\mathrm{G}}(x)}
, encountered for example in atomic ensembles where the velocities
v_{n}
obey Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics. The Doppler shifts
\omega_{n}=k\,v_{n}
then follow a Gaussian distribution where the width
\omega_{\mathrm{G}}=k\sqrt{k_{\mathrm{B}}T/m}
depends on the effective spin-wave vector
k
, the Boltzmann constant
k_{\mathrm{B}}
, the temperature
T
and the atomic mass
m
. The state
\left|E_{0}\right\rangle
, which corresponds to a vacuum state
\left|0\right\rangle
in this phase space, is displaced by
\hat{U}(t)
into a coherent state
\left|-i\omega_{\mathrm{G}}t\right\rangle
, with a Gaussian decay of the amplitude
s_{\mathrm{G}}(t)=\left\langle 0\middle|-i\omega_{\mathrm{G}}t\right\rangle={\textrm{e}}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{G}}^{2}t^{2}/2}
.

•

Differential light shifts between ground and excited atomic states in a three-dimensional harmonic trap, where the Gaussian thermal distribution of atomic positions and the quadratic position-dependance of the light-shift potential lead to
\rho_{\mathrm{L}}(x)=\sqrt{x}{\textrm{e}}^{-x}\theta(x)2/\sqrt{\pi}
and
\psi_{L,0}(x)=\sqrt{\rho_{\mathrm{L}}(x)}
, where
\theta
is the Heaviside step function. The scaling rate is
\omega_{\mathrm{L}}=\delta\alpha_{\mathrm{r}}\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T/\sqrt{2}\hbar
where
\delta\alpha_{\mathrm{r}}=(\alpha_{e}-\alpha_{g})/\alpha_{g}
is the relative difference of polarizabilities of the excited and ground states. The amplitude
s
decays here as
s_{\mathrm{L}}(t)=(1+i\sqrt{2}\omega_{\mathrm{L}}t)^{-3/2}
.

Unless stated otherwise, we set
\omega_{\mathrm{S}}=1
as a unit for angular frequencies and rates, and
1/\omega_{\mathrm{S}}
as a unit for time.

The interest of this mapping becomes apparent when the qubit experiences classical or quantum driving and Eqs.
1
or
2
no longer hold. As shown below, it allows one to explicitly construct the ladder of Dicke states sequentially coupled by the inhomogeneous dephasing. By truncating this ladder at a given level and treating its coupling with higher-order states as an irreversible decay, one can use a standard Gorini - Kossakowski - Sudarshan - Lindblad (GKSL) master equation
[
17
,
18
]
to describe the non-Markovian dynamics via a small extension of the Hilbert space.

We start by constructing the ladder of Dicke states
\{\left|E_{k}\right\rangle\}
from
\left|E_{0}\right\rangle
by successive applications of
{\hat{H}}
. As
{\hat{H}}\propto\hat{x}
, the corresponding wavefunctions have the form
\psi_{k}(x)=Q_{k}(x)\psi_{0}(x)
where
Q_{k}
is a polynomial of degree
k
. The problem then reduces to finding a family of orthogonal polynomials for the weight function
\rho
:
\langle E_{k}|E_{l}\rangle=\int\rho(x)Q_{k}^{*}(x)Q_{l}(x)=\delta_{k,l}
.
This problem is generically solved by Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization but, given its importance in mathematics, engineering or data science, explicit solutions are known in many cases
[
19
]
. By construction,
{\hat{H}}
is tridiagonal in this basis, with nonzero elements
H_{k}=\left\langle E_{k}\right|{\hat{H}}\left|E_{k}\right\rangle
and
V_{k}=\left\langle E_{k-1}\right|{\hat{H}}\left|E_{k}\right\rangle
. For the Gaussian
\rho_{\mathrm{G}}
,
Q_{k}
is a Hermite polynomial up to a normalization factor and
\left|E_{\mathrm{G},k}\right\rangle
is the Fock state
\left|k\right\rangle
, which directly gives
H_{\mathrm{G},k}=0
and
V_{\mathrm{G},k}=\sqrt{k}
. For the light shift
\rho_{\mathrm{L}}
,
Q_{k}(x)=\sqrt{2^{k}k!/(2k+1)!!}L_{k}^{(1/2)}(x)

where
L_{k}^{(1/2)}
is a generalized Laguerre polynomial. Their recurrence relation
[
19
]
gives
H_{\mathrm{L},k}=\sqrt{2}(2k+3/2)
and
V_{\mathrm{L},k}=-\sqrt{k(2k+1)}
. For a generic
\rho(x)
we initialize the recurrence with
\psi_{-1}(x)=0
and
\psi_{0}(x)
, calculate

a_{k}\psi_{k+1}(x)=(\sqrt{2}x-H_{k})\psi_{k}(x)-V_{k}\psi_{k-1}(x)
,
and determine
a_{k}
by normalization.

If
\rho(x)\sim x^{-d}
at infinity, the ladder of Dicke states terminates at step
n=\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor
as
x\,\psi_{n}(x)
becomes non-normalizable: the effect of
{\hat{H}}
on
\left|E_{n}\right\rangle
is then treated in a Born-Markov approximation as an irreversible decay into a spectrally-broad continuum. For the Lorentzian
\rho_{\mathrm{M}}
this happens at
n=0
. If this “quantum Markov order”
[
20
]
is impractically large or infinite, as it is the case for
\rho_{\mathrm{G}}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{L}}
, one can choose
n
as a compromise between the precision and the size of the simulations and approximate the coupling of
\left|E_{n}\right\rangle
to higher-lying states by a Markovian process.

To terminate this ladder, we thus need to calculate the amplitude decay rate
\gamma_{n}
and the Lamb-like energy shift
\delta_{n}
due to the coupling of
\left|E_{n}\right\rangle
with the “broad continuum” of states

\{\left|E_{k>n}\right\rangle\}
. A straightforward use of the resolvent
{\hat{G}}(z)=(z-{\hat{H}})^{-1}

[
21
]
, detailed in Appendix
A
, shows that
\Delta_{n}=\delta_{n}-i\gamma_{n}
obeys the recurrence relation

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=

\displaystyle H_{0}-H_{n}-\frac{|V_{n}|^{2}}{\Delta_{n-1}},

(3)

the initial value
\Delta_{0}
being given by
\Delta_{0}=-1/G_{0}(H_{0})
where, in the limit
\text{Im}(z)\rightarrow 0^{+}
,

G_{0}(z)=\left\langle E_{0}\right|{\hat{G}}(z)\left|E_{0}\right\rangle\\
=\int\frac{\rho(x)\hbox{{d}}x}{z-\sqrt{2}x}=-i\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\textrm{e}}^{izt}s(t)\hbox{{d}}t

(4)

For the Markovian case
\rho_{\mathrm{M}}
,
G_{\mathrm{M},0}(z)=1/(z+i/\sqrt{2})
and
H_{\mathrm{M},0}=0
gives, self-consistently,
\delta_{\mathrm{M},0}=0
and
\gamma_{\mathrm{M},0}=1/\sqrt{2}
.
For the Gaussian
\rho_{\mathrm{G}}
with
H_{\mathrm{G},0}=0
and

G_{\mathrm{G},0}(z)=-i\sqrt{\pi/2}\,w(z/\sqrt{2})
where
w(z)={\textrm{e}}^{-z^{2}}\hbox{{e}rfc}(-iz)
is the Faddeeva function,
\delta_{\mathrm{G},n}=0
and

\gamma_{\mathrm{G},n}=(2/\pi)^{(-1)^{n}/2}n!!/(n-1)!!
. For the light-shift spectrum
\rho_{\mathrm{L}}
,
G_{\mathrm{L},0}(z)=-\sqrt{2}-i\sqrt{\pi z}\sqrt[4]{2}\,w(\sqrt{z}/\sqrt[4]{2})
and
H_{\mathrm{L},0}=3/\sqrt{2}
; Eq.
3
becomes
\Delta_{\mathrm{L},n}=-2^{3/2}n-n(2n+1)/\Delta_{\mathrm{L},n-1}
and gives
\delta_{\mathrm{L},n}
and

by induction.

As shown in Fig.

, numerical simulations in a basis of size

rapidly converge to the analytic solutions of Eq.
2
with increasing

. At long times, approximating the higher-lying states by a Markovian continuum significantly improves the precision compared to a simple phase-space truncation. This approach enables resource-efficient numerical simulations in more complex situations where several collective qubits are coupled to time-dependent classical or quantum light.

III
Driving protection effect

This formalism predicts an interesting effect when the qubit is resonantly driven with a constant Rabi angular frequency
\Omega
. A similar resolvent-based approach, detailed in Appendix
B
together with the derivations of the expressions below, shows that Eq.
2
, describing the excited-state amplitude dynamics, is then replaced with

s_{\Omega}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{i\hbox{{d}}z}{2\pi}\frac{zG_{0}(z+H_{0}){\textrm{e}}^{-itz}}{z-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}G_{0}(z+H_{0})}.

(5)

In the following we focus on the strong-driving regime where
\Omega\gg 1
.

For the Lorentzian spectrum
\rho_{\mathrm{M}}
,
H_{\mathrm{M},0}=0
and the simple form of
G_{\mathrm{M},0}(z)
gives the exact solution

s_{\mathrm{M},\Omega}(t)={\textrm{e}}^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{M}}t/2}\left(\cos(\Omega_{\mathrm{M}}t/2)-\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{M}}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{M}}}\sin(\Omega_{\mathrm{M}}t/2)\right)

(6)

with
\Omega_{\mathrm{M}}=(\Omega^{2}-\gamma_{\mathrm{M}}^{2})^{1/2}
. In this Markovian case, a conventional master equation approach leads to the same result. For
\Omega\geq\gamma_{\mathrm{M}}=1/\sqrt{2}
the decay rate
\gamma_{\mathrm{M}}/2
, reduced by half by the driving, becomes independent on
\Omega
: no matter the driving strength, the decay persists.

The situation radically changes for the Gaussian spectrum
\rho_{\mathrm{G}}
. The asymptotic expansion
\text{Re}(G_{\mathrm{G},0}(z))=z^{-1}+z^{-3}+O(z^{-5})
gives

\displaystyle\mathrlap{s_{\mathrm{G},\Omega}(t)\approx{\textrm{e}}^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{G},\Omega}t}\cos(\Omega_{\mathrm{G}}t/2),}

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{G}}\approx

\displaystyle~\Omega+\frac{2}{\Omega},

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathrm{G},\Omega}\approx

\displaystyle~\frac{\Omega^{2}}{8}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2{\textrm{e}}}}{\textrm{e}}^{-\Omega^{2}/8}.

(7)

The decay profile changes from Gaussian to exponential, its rate
\gamma_{\mathrm{G},\Omega}
vanishing exponentially with
\Omega^{2}
. Here, strong driving combined with non-Markovianity allows high-visibility Rabi oscillations to occur over times strongly exceeding the natural dephasing time, as shown in Fig.
3
(a). This “driving protection” of the collective qubit is closely related to cavity protection
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
, the collective Rabi frequency
\Omega
replacing the collective cavity coupling.

For the light-shift spectrum
\rho_{\mathrm{L}}
, the expansion
\text{Re}(G_{\mathrm{L},0}(z+H_{\mathrm{L},0}))=z^{-1}+3z^{-3}+O(z^{-4})
leads to

\displaystyle\mathrlap{s_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}(t)\approx\frac{1}{2}\left({\textrm{e}}^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}t}{\textrm{e}}^{-i\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}t/2}+{\textrm{e}}^{i\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}t/2}\right),}

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}\approx

\displaystyle~\Omega+\frac{6}{\Omega},

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}\approx

\displaystyle~\frac{\Omega^{2}}{8}\sqrt{\frac{\pi\Omega}{\sqrt{2}{\textrm{e}}^{3}}}{\textrm{e}}^{-\Omega/\sqrt{8}}.

(8)

As visible in Fig.
3
(b), the driving protection persists but, since
\gamma_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}
decreases slower than
\gamma_{\mathrm{G},\Omega}
with
\Omega
, it is weaker than for a Gaussian spectrum. While in the Gaussian case the system fully decays in the continuum, here the population
|s_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}(t)|^{2}
tends to
1/4
at long times. In fact, the two terms in the expression of
s_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}(t)
respectively correspond to the propagators
\left\langle\psi_{\pm}\right|{\hat{U}}_{\Omega}(t)\left|\psi_{\pm}\right\rangle
with
\left|\psi_{\pm}\right\rangle=(\left|G\right\rangle\pm\left|E_{0}\right\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
: the population in
\left|\psi_{+}\right\rangle
decays with a rate
2\gamma_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}
while
\left|\psi_{-}\right\rangle
is very efficiently protected by the drive.

IV
Physical interpretation

While this “driving protection” qualifies as dynamical decoupling, it is a fundamentally collective effect, different from the techniques generally used to counteract dephasing in two-level systems. The simplest of them is spin echo
[
22
]
, where the dephasing due to an unknown shift
\omega
of the resonance frequency can be compensated by periodically swapping the ground and excited amplitudes via
\pi
pulses. This mitigation persists when the qubit is driven continuously with a Rabi frequency
\Omega_{0}\gg\omega

[
23
]
.

The process considered here is not a mere sum of such effects acting on every emitter in the ensemble. Indeed, the Rabi frequency
\Omega=\Omega_{0}\sqrt{N}
is collectively enhanced with respect to the individual Rabi frequency
\Omega_{0}
. The latter can be much smaller than
\omega_{\mathrm{S}}
, and thus insufficient to mitigate dephasing in a single emitter. Moreover, discrete or continuous spin echo fundamentally relies on swapping the roles of the ground and the excited states. Here the excited population of each emitter remains below
1/N
. For Rydberg superatoms, single-atom spin echo requires bringing
\sim N-1
atoms in a Rydberg state which is prevented by dipole blockade. Finally, unlike discrete or continuous spin echo, driving protection crucially relies on non-Markovianity.

The mechanism behind driving protection can be sketched in the following way. In the rotating frame, the eigenstates of the driven collective qubit are
\left|\psi_{\pm}\right\rangle=(\left|G\right\rangle\pm\left|E_{0}\right\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
, with eigenenergies
\pm\Omega/2
. The dephasing
\sqrt{2}{\hat{x}}
couples them to the continuum of states
\{\left|x\right\rangle\}
via
g(x)=\left\langle x\right|\sqrt{2}{\hat{x}}\left|\psi_{\pm}\right\rangle=\pm x\,\psi_{0}(x+H_{0}/\sqrt{2})
, the offset
H_{0}
accounting for the resonant driving. For a Lorentzian spectrum,
g_{\mathrm{M}}(x)\propto x/(1+i2x)
is spectrally broad [Fig.
4
]: for any
\Omega
, the states
\left|\psi_{\pm}\right\rangle
are resonantly coupled to the continuum, which results in irreversible decay. In contrast, for a Gaussian spectrum,
g_{\mathrm{G}}(x)\propto x\,{\textrm{e}}^{-x^{2}/2}
is spectrally narrow and localized around
0
. Strong driving makes the dephasing-induced coupling off-resonant and thus inhibits the decay of the qubit. For the light-shift spectrum,
g_{\mathrm{L}}(x)\propto x\,\psi_{\mathrm{L},0}(x+3/2)
is asymmetric, and
\left|\psi_{-}\right\rangle
is more strongly protected than
\left|\psi_{+}\right\rangle
.

V
Experimental methods

To test our predictions experimentally, we use a Rydberg superatom: a small cold atomic ensemble where interactions between Rydberg-state atoms block the number of excitations to one at most, thus defining a collectively-encoded qubit
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
. As shown in Fig.
5
, the superatom is coupled to a running-wave optical cavity enabling an efficient mapping of its excited state
\left|E_{0}\right\rangle
to a single-photon Fock state
\left|1\right\rangle
. Therefore, by driving the superatom to its excited state, waiting for a storage duration
t_{\mathrm{s}}
, mapping
\left|E_{0}\right\rangle
to a photon, and measuring the probability to detect the latter with a photon counter, we obtain a signal proportional to
|s(t_{\mathrm{s}})|^{2}
.

The superatom is produced in
0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
through the sequential use of two-dimensional (2D) magneto-optical trapping (MOT), 3D MOT, optical molasses cooling, transporting in a one-dimensional optical lattice, and degenerate Raman sideband cooling, as described in detail in Refs.
[
24
,
25
]
. It is made of
N\simeq 500
atoms with a Gaussian spatial distribution, loaded in a crossed dipole trap. In the last initialization stage, the atoms are optically pumped in the state
\left|g\right\rangle:5S_{1/2},F\!\!=\!\!1,m_{F}\!\!=\!\!1
. By adjusting the depth of the trapping potential during either the preparation or the experimental run, we can control the light-shifts in our system, as well as increase the compression and modify the temperature of the atomic cloud. This allows us to prepare inhomogeneous broadening spectra with similar characteristic widths but with different shapes. To obtain a purely Gaussian Doppler broadening, we switch off the trap during the experimental protocol and increase the confinement potential during the preparation, creating a cloud with a root-mean-square radius
\sigma_{r}=$3.7(2)\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
and a temperature
T=$6.2(3)\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro K}$
. To probe a combination of light-shift and Doppler broadening mechanisms, we do not compress the cloud, which makes it colder (
\sigma_{r}=$4.4(2)\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
and
T=$2.5(1)\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro K}$
), but apply an average light-shift of
4.8(6)10^{2}\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
during the experimental protocol. We adjust the temperature and the light shift to achieve similar coherence times for both configurations. Then, we repeat
10
times the excitation and measurement protocol described below. To remove any residual Rydberg-excited atoms, we apply an electric field pulse using in-vacuum electrodes before each repetition.

VI
Experimental results

The results of the experiment are presented in Fig.
6
. Panels (a) and (b) show the decay of
\left|E_{0}\right\rangle
without driving, fitted with the solutions of Eq.
2
. The Gaussian decay in panel (a), with a mean lifetime
\sqrt{\pi}/(2\omega_{\mathrm{G}})=$2.33(7)\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}$
, results from pure Doppler thermal broadening. Panel (b) shows a decay with a similar mean lifetime of
2.03(9)\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
, but with a different shape, arising from a convolution between a Gaussian, due to residual thermal motion, and a light-shift spectrum slightly modified by the anharmonicity of our optical dipole trap [details in Appendix
C
].

Panels (c) and (d) correspond to the same configurations in presence of Rabi flopping. They confirm the predicted driving protection: the visibility of Rabi oscillations increases with their frequency and reaches coherence times strongly exceeding the free decay lifetimes measured above. The observed dynamics are reproduced by numerical simulations on a desktop computer, using a truncated basis of Dicke states [details in Appendix
C
]. By fitting an exponential envelope
{\textrm{e}}^{-\Gamma t_{\mathrm{d}}}
on the measured oscillations, with a fit function accounting for shot-to-shot fluctuations of the number of atoms in the prepared superatom [details in Appendix
C.1
], we obtain the decay rate
\Gamma
presented in panel (e). It decreases rapidly with the Rabi frequency, down to a factor
14(5)
below the characteristic free decay rate of
\left|E_{0}\right\rangle
. As expected from Sec.
III
, at intermediate values
5<\Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{G}}<10
(i.e
$0.3\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$<\Omega/(2\pi)<$0.6\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
), the driving protection is less efficient in the light-shifted case than in the Gaussian one. For the latter, the data points are close to the analytic result of Eq.
7
, valid in the strong driving limit and plotted for
\Omega>3\omega_{\mathrm{G}}
. At very strong driving,
\Gamma
is ultimately limited by experimental effects compatible with a form
\Gamma_{\Omega}\,\Omega^{2}+\Gamma_{0}

[
28
]
.
\Gamma_{\Omega}
accounts for decoherence mechanisms proportional to the power of the driving beam, including spontaneous emission of the intermediate level of the two-photon transition between
\left|G\right\rangle
and
\left|E_{0}\right\rangle
and imperfect Rydberg blockade; while
\Gamma_{0}
stems from defects such as the finite lifetime of the Rydberg state, imperfect optical pumping, impure laser polarizations, or residual electric fields.

Conclusion

We unveiled, theoretically and experimentally, a dynamical decoupling effect for collective qubits, which enhances coherence times by a factor
14
in our experimental system.
The demonstration of this driving protection effect was enabled by the simplicity of the underlying theoretical framework. Among existing descriptions of non-Markovian quantum dynamics
[
29
,
30
]
, it has some resemblance with the formalism of pseudomodes
[
31
]
. Applicable to a large class of physical systems, it gives access to an easy implementation, to efficient numerics, and to intuitive physical interpretations exemplified here.

It is worth comparing this protection mechanism with other ways of counteracting inhomogeneous dephasing, all of which come at some practical cost. Firstly, one can act on the dephasing’s causes. For the Doppler and the light-shift broadenings, it means cooling the atoms further, which significantly degrades experimental duty cycles, and switching off the trapping light, which eventually leads to atomic loss. Alternatively, the system can be made less sensitive to dephasing, by using Doppler-free excitation or state-insensitive dipole traps. For Rydberg superatoms, such traps operate close to atomic transitions
[
32
]
, while Doppler-free excitation requires three laser beams with specific angles
[
33
]
, putting significant constrains on optical access. Other mitigation schemes for Doppler broadening in Rydberg gases involve a continuous off-resonant coupling
[
28
]
or a transient transfer
[
34
]
to an auxiliary state. Compared to these schemes, the strengths and weaknesses of driving protection are similar to those of spin echo, applicable to collectively-encoded qubits
[
35
]
but, as mentioned earlier, incompatible with strong Rydberg blockade. It leads to unwanted phase accumulations, sensitive to fluctuations of the Rabi frequency, but it is not restricted to specific ranges of atomic levels and it requires no resources other than those already available to manipulate the qubit.

Besides its potential applications in quantum information processing with collective qubits, the driving protection process is interesting in its own right. Compared to cavity protection, where the collective coupling between the cavity and the emitters is generally fixed
[
14
]
, the temporal control of the collective Rabi frequency gives access to dynamics yet to be explored, such as the active stabilization of the eigenstates of the coupled system.

Appendix A
Lamb-like shift and decay rate

In the model described in the
manuscript, we identify a ladder of Dicke states
\{\left|E_{k}\right\rangle\}
sequentially coupled by the inhomogeneous-dephasing Hamiltonian
{\hat{H}}=\sqrt{2}{\hat{x}}
, and calculate the diagonal terms
H_{k}=\langle E_{k}|{\hat{H}}|E_{k}\rangle
and the coupling terms
V_{k}=\langle E_{k-1}|{\hat{H}}|E_{k}\rangle
. We then restrict the system’s Hilbert space to the finite subspace
\{\left|E_{k\leq n}\right\rangle\}
formed by the lowest
n+1
states, and treat the orthogonal subspace spanned by
\{\left|E_{k>n}\right\rangle\}
as a broad continuum. Here we calculate the amplitude decay rate
\gamma_{n}
and the Lamb-like energy shift
\delta_{n}
of
\left|E_{n}\right\rangle
induced by the coupling to this continuum. For this, we define the
projectors
{\hat{P}}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left|E_{k}\middle\rangle\middle\langle E_{k}\right|
and
{\hat{Q}}=1-{\hat{P}}
into the two subspaces of interest, and derive an effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
{\hat{H}}_{\mathrm{e}}
from the resolvent
{\hat{G}}(z)=(z-{\hat{H}})^{-1}
which obeys
[
21
]

\displaystyle{\hat{P}}{\hat{G}}(z){\hat{P}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z{\hat{P}}-{\hat{H}}_{\mathrm{e}}(z)},

\displaystyle{\hat{H}}_{\mathrm{e}}(z)

\displaystyle={\hat{P}}{\hat{H}}{\hat{P}}+{\hat{P}}{\hat{H}}{\hat{Q}}\frac{1}{z{\hat{Q}}-{\hat{Q}}{\hat{H}}{\hat{Q}}}{\hat{Q}}{\hat{H}}{\hat{P}}.

(9)

To obtain a parameter-free
{\hat{H}}_{\mathrm{e}}
, the energy
z
is fixed to a physically-relevant value. As
\left|E_{0}\right\rangle
is the only experimentally-accessible state, we set
z
to
H_{0}
. Since
{\hat{H}}
is tridiagonal in this basis,

\displaystyle{\hat{H}}_{\mathrm{e}}(z)

\displaystyle={\hat{P}}{\hat{H}}{\hat{P}}+\Delta_{n}\left|E_{n}\middle\rangle\middle\langle E_{n}\right|,

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}

\displaystyle=\delta_{n}-i\gamma_{n}=|V_{n+1}|^{2}\left\langle E_{n+1}\right|{\hat{G}}_{n+1}(z)\left|E_{n+1}\right\rangle

(10)

where
{\hat{G}}_{n+1}(z)=(z{\hat{Q}}-{\hat{Q}}{\hat{H}}{\hat{Q}})^{-1}
is the resolvent of
{\hat{Q}}{\hat{H}}{\hat{Q}}
.
\Delta_{n}
can be calculated inductively by replacing
G
with
{\hat{G}}_{n}
and
{\hat{P}}
with
{\hat{P}}_{n}=\left|E_{n}\middle\rangle\middle\langle E_{n}\right|
in Eq.
9
:

\displaystyle{\hat{P}}_{n}{\hat{G}}_{n}(z){\hat{P}}_{n}=

\displaystyle{\hat{P}}_{n}\frac{\Delta_{n-1}}{|V_{n}|^{2}}=\frac{{\hat{P}}_{n}}{z-H_{n}-\Delta_{n}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\Delta_{n}=

\displaystyle z-H_{n}-\frac{|V_{n}|^{2}}{\Delta_{n-1}}.

(11)

The initial value
\Delta_{0}
is given by the limit
\text{Im}(z)\rightarrow 0^{+}
of

\displaystyle G_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{z-H_{0}-\Delta_{0}}=\left\langle E_{0}\right|{\hat{G}}(z)\left|E_{0}\right\rangle=\int\frac{\rho(x)\hbox{{d}}x}{z-\sqrt{2}x}=-i\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\textrm{e}}^{izt}s(t)\hbox{{d}}t,

(12)

the noise spectral density
\rho(x)
and the amplitude
s(t)=\langle E_{0}|{\textrm{e}}^{-i{\hat{H}}t}|E_{0}\rangle=\int\hbox{{d}}x\rho(x){\textrm{e}}^{-i\sqrt{2}xt}
being defined in the main text.

We can now apply this procedure to the three specific cases discussed in the manuscript.

•

For the Markovian case
\rho_{\mathrm{M}}
where
H_{\mathrm{M},0}=0
,

\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{M}}(t)=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}x\frac{2{\textrm{e}}^{-i\sqrt{2}xt}}{\pi(1+4x^{2})}=\frac{1}{2i\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}x{\textrm{e}}^{-ixt}\left(\frac{1}{x-i/\sqrt{2}}-\frac{1}{x+i/\sqrt{2}}\right)={\textrm{e}}^{-t/\sqrt{2}}

(13)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow G_{\mathrm{M},0}(z)=

\displaystyle-i\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}t{\textrm{e}}^{-t/\sqrt{2}+itz}=\frac{1}{z+i/\sqrt{2}}.

(14)

Self-consistently, this gives
\delta_{\mathrm{M},0}=0
and
\gamma_{\mathrm{M},0}=1/\sqrt{2}
independently on
z
.

•

For the Gaussian spectrum
\rho_{\mathrm{G}}
,

\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{G}}(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}x{\textrm{e}}^{-x^{2}-i\sqrt{2}xt}={\textrm{e}}^{-t^{2}/2},

(15)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow G_{\mathrm{G},0}(z)=

\displaystyle-i\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}t\;{\textrm{e}}^{-t^{2}/2+itz}=-i\sqrt{2}{\textrm{e}}^{-z^{2}/2}\int_{-iz/\sqrt{2}}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}t\;{\textrm{e}}^{-t^{2}}=-i\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}w\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right),

(16)

where
w(z)={\textrm{e}}^{-z^{2}}\hbox{{e}rfc}(-iz)
is the Faddeeva function. With

, we get
\Delta_{\mathrm{G},0}=-1/G_{\mathrm{G},0}(0)=-i\sqrt{2/\pi}
and, from Eq.
11

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{G},n}=\frac{n}{\Delta_{\mathrm{G},n-1}}=\frac{n}{n-1}\Delta_{\mathrm{G},n-2}=...

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathrm{G},n}=0,

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathrm{G},n}=\frac{(n)!!}{(n-1)!!}\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{(-1)^{n}/2}.

(17)

•

For the light-shift spectrum
\rho_{\mathrm{L}}
, assuming
\text{Im}(z)\rightarrow 0^{+}
,

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{L},0}(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}x\frac{\sqrt{x}{\textrm{e}}^{-x}}{z-\sqrt{2}x}=-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}u\frac{u^{2}{\textrm{e}}^{-u^{2}}}{u^{2}-z/\sqrt{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{2}-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}u{\textrm{e}}^{-u^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}}\left(\frac{1}{u-\sqrt{z/\sqrt{2}}}-\frac{1}{u+\sqrt{z/\sqrt{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{2}-i\sqrt{z\sqrt{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}u\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}t{\textrm{e}}^{-u^{2}-it(u-\sqrt{z/\sqrt{2}})}=-\sqrt{2}-i\sqrt{z\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}t{\textrm{e}}^{-t^{2}/4+it\sqrt{z/\sqrt{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{2}-i\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{z\sqrt{2}}w\left(\sqrt{\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}}\right).

(18)

With
z=H_{\mathrm{L},0}=3/\sqrt{2}
,
\Delta_{\mathrm{L},0}=1/(\sqrt{2}+i\sqrt{3\pi}w(\sqrt{3/2}))
. Eq.

becomes

and gives
\delta_{n}
and

by induction.

Let us consider here that the collective qubit is resonantly driven between
\left|G\right\rangle
and
\left|E_{0}\right\rangle
with a constant Rabi frequency

, assumed to be real. In the rotating frame, writing

\displaystyle{\hat{H}}_{\Omega}=

\displaystyle{\hat{H}}_{\mathrm{r}}+{\hat{D}}

\displaystyle{\hat{H}}_{\mathrm{r}}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}{\hat{x}}-H_{0}(1-{\hat{\sigma}}_{GG}),

\displaystyle{\hat{D}}=\frac{\Omega}{2}({\hat{\sigma}}_{G0}+{\hat{\sigma}}_{0G})

(19)

where the dephasing term
{\hat{H}}_{\mathrm{r}}
is offset by
H_{0}
to make the driving
{\hat{D}}
resonant.
We define the resolvents
{\hat{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}
and
{\hat{G}}_{\Omega}
of
{\hat{H}}_{\mathrm{r}}
and
{\hat{H}}_{\Omega}
,
and use the general relation
\hat{B}^{-1}=\hat{A}^{-1}+\hat{A}^{-1}(\hat{A}-\hat{B})\hat{B}^{-1}

[

]

\displaystyle{\hat{G}}_{\Omega}(z)=

\displaystyle{\hat{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(z)+{\hat{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(z){\hat{D}}{\hat{G}}_{\Omega}(z)={\hat{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(z)+{\hat{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(z){\hat{D}}{\hat{G}}^{\prime}(z)+{\hat{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(z){\hat{D}}{\hat{G}}^{\prime}(z){\hat{D}}{\hat{G}}_{\Omega}(z)=...=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\left({\hat{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(z){\hat{D}}\right)^{n}{\hat{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(z).

\displaystyle{\hat{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(z){\hat{D}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega}{2}\left(\frac{1}{z}{\hat{\sigma}}_{G0}+\frac{1}{z-\sqrt{2}{\hat{x}}+H_{0}(1-{\hat{\sigma}}_{GG})}{\hat{\sigma}}_{0G}\right),

(21)

(22)

\displaystyle\left({\hat{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(z){\hat{D}}\right)^{3}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\Omega}{2}\right)^{3}\frac{G_{0}(z+H_{0})}{z}\left(\frac{1}{z}{\hat{\sigma}}_{G0}+\frac{1}{z-\sqrt{2}{\hat{x}}+H_{0}(1-{\hat{\sigma}}_{GG})}{\hat{\sigma}}_{0G}\right)=\frac{\Omega^{2}G_{0}(z+H_{0})}{4z}{\hat{G}}_{0}(z){\hat{D}}

(23)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\left({\hat{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(z){\hat{D}}\right)^{2n+k}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\Omega^{2}G_{0}(z+H_{0})}{4z}\right)^{n}\left({\hat{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(z){\hat{D}}\right)^{k},

(24)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow{\hat{G}}_{\Omega}(z)=

\displaystyle{\hat{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(z)+\frac{{\hat{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(z){\hat{D}}{\hat{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(z)+({\hat{G}}^{\prime}(z){\hat{D}})^{2}{\hat{G}}_{\mathrm{r}}(z)}{1-\Omega^{2}G_{0}(z+H_{0})/(4z)}

where

is given by Eq.

(27)

In particular, the amplitude of the qubit’s excited state now evolves as

,

s_{\mathrm{M},\Omega}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{i\hbox{{d}}z}{2\pi}\frac{{\textrm{e}}^{-itz}}{2}\left(\frac{1-i\gamma_{\mathrm{M}}/\Omega_{\mathrm{M}}}{z-\Omega_{\mathrm{M}}/2+i\gamma_{\mathrm{M}}/2}+\frac{1+i\gamma_{\mathrm{M}}/\Omega_{\mathrm{M}}}{z+\Omega_{\mathrm{M}}/2+i\gamma_{\mathrm{M}}/2}\right)\\
={\textrm{e}}^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{M}}t/2}\left(\cos\left(\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{M}}t}{2}\right)-\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{M}}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{M}}}\sin\left(\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{M}}t}{2}\right)\right).

(30)

When
\Omega>\gamma_{\mathrm{M}}
, the decay rate
\gamma_{\mathrm{M}}/2
, two times smaller than without driving, becomes independent on the driving strength.

We can verify these results using standard Bloch equations. For consistency with the model above, let us assume that the qubit oscillates between the states
\left|g\right\rangle
and
\left|e\right\rangle
and decays into a different state
\left|d\right\rangle
. The master equation

\displaystyle\frac{\hbox{{d}}}{\hbox{{d}}t}{\hat{\rho}}=

\displaystyle-i\frac{\Omega}{2}\left[{\hat{\sigma}}_{ge}+{\hat{\sigma}}_{eg},{\hat{\rho}}\right]+\gamma\left(2{\hat{\sigma}}_{de}{\hat{\rho}}{\hat{\sigma}}_{ed}-{\hat{\sigma}}_{ee}{\hat{\rho}}-{\hat{\rho}}{\hat{\sigma}}_{ee}\right)

(31)

leads to

\frac{\hbox{{d}}}{\hbox{{d}}t}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\hat{\rho}}_{ee}\\
\text{Im}{\hat{\rho}}_{ge}\\
{\hat{\rho}}_{dd}\end{array}\right)=-\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}2\gamma&-\Omega&0\\
\Omega&\gamma&\Omega/2\\
-2\gamma&0&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\hat{\rho}}_{ee}\\
\text{Im}{\hat{\rho}}_{ge}\\
{\hat{\rho}}_{dd}\end{array}\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
\Omega/2\\
0\end{array}\right).

(32)

For
\Omega>\gamma
, the real parts of the eigenvalues
\{\gamma,\gamma\pm i\sqrt{\Omega^{2}-\gamma^{2}}\}
of the matrix are all equal to
\gamma
, independently on
\Omega
. Setting
\gamma=\gamma_{\mathrm{M}}
allows one to recover the results above.

B.2
Gaussian process

For a Gaussian spectrum,
H_{\mathrm{G},0}=0
and
G_{\mathrm{G},0}(z)
is given by Eq.
16
, which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{G},0}(z)=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}D\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)-i\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}{\textrm{e}}^{-z^{2}/2},

\displaystyle D(z)=

\displaystyle{\textrm{e}}^{-z^{2}}\int_{0}^{z}{\textrm{e}}^{t^{2}}\hbox{{d}}t,

(33)

where
D(z)
is the Dawson integral. The even function
D(z)/z
monotonically decreases from
1
to
0
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, which means that for
\Omega\gg 1
the real part of the denominator in the integrand of Eq.
28
vanishes in
z_{\pm}=\pm\Omega_{\mathrm{G}}/2
with
\Omega_{\mathrm{G}}\gg 1
, where its imaginary part becomes very small. We can recursively user the asymptotic expansion

\text{Re}(G_{\mathrm{G},0}(z))=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(2k-1)!!}{z^{2k+1}}+O\left(\frac{1}{z^{2N+3}}\right),

(34)

to solve
\Omega_{\mathrm{G}}/2=(\Omega^{2}/4)\text{Re}(G_{\mathrm{G},0}(\Omega_{\mathrm{G}}/2))

and find
\Omega_{\mathrm{G}}
to an arbitrary order in
\Omega
. To the lowest non-trivial order,

\Omega_{\mathrm{G}}=\Omega+2/\Omega+O(\Omega^{-3})
. Near
z_{\pm}
, to the first order in
z-z_{\pm}
and in
1/\Omega
,

z-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}\text{Re}((G_{\mathrm{G},0}(z))\approx(z-z_{\pm})\left(1-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}\text{Re}((G_{\mathrm{G},0}^{\prime}(z_{\pm}))\right)\approx(z-z_{\pm})\left(1+\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}\frac{1}{z_{\pm}^{2}}\right)\approx 2(z-z_{\pm}).

(35)

As the imaginary part of the integrand’s denominator is suppressed exponentially in
\Omega^{2}
by
\text{Im}(G_{\mathrm{G},0}(z_{\pm}))=-\sqrt{\pi/(2e)}{\textrm{e}}^{-\Omega^{2}/8}(1+O(\Omega^{-2}))
, we find

\displaystyle\frac{zG_{\mathrm{G},0}(z)}{z-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}G_{\mathrm{G},0}(z)}\approx\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{z-\Omega_{\mathrm{G}}/2+i\gamma_{\mathrm{G},\Omega}}+\frac{1}{z+\Omega_{\mathrm{G}}/2+i\gamma_{\mathrm{G},\Omega}}\right),

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{G}}\approx\Omega+\frac{2}{\Omega},

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathrm{G},\Omega}\approx\frac{\Omega^{2}}{8}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2e}}{\textrm{e}}^{-\Omega^{2}/8}.

(36)

Eq.
28
then integrates into
s_{\mathrm{G}}(t)\approx{\textrm{e}}^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{G},\Omega}t}\cos(\Omega_{\mathrm{G}}t/2)
, showing that strong driving turns the Gaussian decay into an exponential one, and that the rate
\gamma_{\mathrm{G}}
of this exponential decay decreases extremely fast when
\Omega
increases. Even for a moderate
\Omega=10
,
\gamma_{\mathrm{G}}
drops below
10^{-4}
, allowing Rabi oscillations to persist for much longer that the decay time of he undriven qubit.

B.3
Light shift in a harmonic trap

The case of the harmonic light shift spectrum
\rho_{\mathrm{L}}
resembles the Gaussian one. Here
H_{\mathrm{L},0}=3/\sqrt{2}
and the asymptotic expansion of the real part of Eq.
18
gives

\displaystyle\text{Re}(G_{\mathrm{L},0}(z))=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{z}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(2k+1)!!}{(\sqrt{2}z)^{k}}+O\left(\frac{1}{z^{N+2}}\right)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\text{Re}(G_{\mathrm{L},0}(z+H_{\mathrm{L},0}))=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{z}+\frac{3}{z^{3}}+O\left(\frac{1}{z^{4}}\right).

(37)

This shows that real part of the denominator of the integrand in Eq.
28
vanishes in two nearly opposite points
z_{\pm}=\pm(\Omega/2+3/\Omega)+12\sqrt{2}/\Omega^{2}+O(1/\Omega^{3})
. Using
w(iz)={\textrm{e}}^{z^{2}}\hbox{{e}rfc}(z)
to rewrite

G_{\mathrm{L},0}(-z)=-\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{\pi z\sqrt{2}}{\textrm{e}}^{z/\sqrt{2}}\hbox{{e}rfc}\left(\sqrt{\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}}\right),

(38)

we see that, for
z>0
,
\text{Im}(G_{\mathrm{L},0}(z))=-\sqrt[4]{2}\sqrt{\pi z}{\textrm{e}}^{-z/\sqrt{2}}
but
\text{Im}(G_{\mathrm{L},0}(-z))=0
. With
G_{\mathrm{L},0}^{\prime}(z+H_{\mathrm{L},0})=-z^{-2}+O(z^{-4})
, the same approximations as in the Gaussian case give again

\displaystyle z-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}\text{Re}((G_{\mathrm{L},0}(z+H_{\mathrm{L},0}))\approx

\displaystyle(z-z_{\pm})\left(1-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}\text{Re}((G_{\mathrm{L},0}^{\prime}(z_{\pm}))\right)\approx(z-z_{\pm})\left(1+\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}\frac{1}{z_{\pm}^{2}}\right)\approx 2(z-z_{\pm}).

(39)

Near
z_{+}
, the imaginary part of the integrand’s denominator now decreases exponentially with
\Omega
, while near
z_{-}
it vanishes:

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}\text{Im}((G_{\mathrm{L},0}(z_{+}+H_{\mathrm{L},0}))=\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}\sqrt{\frac{\pi\Omega}{\sqrt{2}e^{3}}}{\textrm{e}}^{-\Omega/\sqrt{8}}(1+O(\Omega^{-2})),

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}\text{Im}((G_{\mathrm{L},0}(z_{-}+H_{\mathrm{L},0}))=0

(40)

This gives

\displaystyle\frac{zG_{\mathrm{L},0}(z)}{z-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}G_{\mathrm{L},0}(z)}\approx\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{z-\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}/2+i\gamma_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}}+\frac{1}{z+\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}/2}\right),

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}\approx\Omega+\frac{6}{\Omega},

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}\approx\frac{\Omega^{2}}{8}\sqrt{\frac{\pi\Omega}{\sqrt{2}e^{3}}}{\textrm{e}}^{-\Omega/\sqrt{8}},

(41)

and integrates into

s_{\mathrm{L}}(t)=\left\langle E_{0}\right|{\hat{U}}_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}(t)\left|E_{0}\right\rangle\approx({\textrm{e}}^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}t}{\textrm{e}}^{-i\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}t/2}+{\textrm{e}}^{i\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}t/2})/2.

(42)

Using Eqs.
26
and
27
, we can apply the same procedure to the three other elements of the restricted propagator
{\hat{U}}_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}(t>0)
:

\displaystyle\left\langle G\right|{\hat{U}}_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}(t)\left|G\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{i\hbox{{d}}z}{2\pi}\frac{{\textrm{e}}^{-itz}}{z-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}G_{\mathrm{L},0}(z+H_{\mathrm{L},0})}\approx\frac{{\textrm{e}}^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}t}{\textrm{e}}^{-i\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}t/2}+{\textrm{e}}^{i\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}t/2}}{2}=\left\langle E_{0}\right|{\hat{U}}_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}(t)\left|E_{0}\right\rangle,

(43)

\displaystyle\left\langle E_{0}\right|{\hat{U}}_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}(t)\left|G\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{i\hbox{{d}}z}{2\pi}\frac{\frac{\Omega}{2}G_{\mathrm{L},0}(z+H_{\mathrm{L},0}){\textrm{e}}^{-itz}}{z-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}G_{\mathrm{L},0}(z+H_{\mathrm{L},0})}\approx\frac{{\textrm{e}}^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}t}{\textrm{e}}^{-i\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}t/2}-{\textrm{e}}^{i\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}t/2}}{2}=\left\langle G\right|{\hat{U}}_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}(t)\left|E_{0}\right\rangle.

(44)

Thus, in the strong driving limit, the propagator restricted to the qubit’s subspace can be written as

{\hat{U}}_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}(t)\approx{\textrm{e}}^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}t}{\textrm{e}}^{-i\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}t/2}\left|\psi_{+}\middle\rangle\middle\langle\psi_{+}\right|+{\textrm{e}}^{i\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}t/2}\left|\psi_{-}\middle\rangle\middle\langle\psi_{-}\right|

(45)

where
\psi_{\pm}=(\left|G\right\rangle\pm\left|E_{0}\right\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
are the eigenstates of the Rabi flopping Hamiltonian
{\hat{D}}
. The “driving protection” of
\left|\psi_{+}\right\rangle
is weaker than for the Gaussian spectrum, as
\gamma_{\mathrm{L},\Omega}
decreases exponentially with
\Omega
instead of
\Omega^{2}
, while
\left|\psi_{-}\right\rangle
does not decay at all.

Simulations

The simulations presented together with our experimental results in the main manuscript use the Python package QuTiP
[

to integrate the GKSL master equation

\frac{\hbox{{d}}\hat{\rho}}{\hbox{{d}}t}=-i\left[\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Drive}}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Deph}},\hat{\rho}\right]+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Deph}}[\hat{\rho}]+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Loss}}[\hat{\rho}],

(46)

incorporating the following terms.

C.1
Driving term
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Drive}}
and fluctuations of
\Omega
.

In
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Drive}}=(\Omega/2)(\left|E_{0}\right\rangle\left\langle G\right|+\left|G\right\rangle\left\langle E_{0}\right|)
, describing the Rabi flopping of the qubit, the temporal profile of the collective Rabi frequency
\Omega=\sqrt{N}\Omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}/(2\Delta)
is obtained from “red drive” and “blue drive” laser pulse shapes measured with photodiodes and plotted in Fig. 5(b) in the main manuscript. The detuning
\Delta
and the “blue drive” Rabi frequency
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}
being fixed (see “Experimental Methods” in the manuscript), the proportionality factor between
|\Omega|^{2}
and the controlled power of the “red drive” beam is extracted from experimental Rabi oscillations in the regime where
\Omega\gg\omega_{\mathrm{G}},\omega_{\mathrm{L}}\sim$0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
. The Poissonian shot-to-shot fluctuations of the atomic number
N
, which can be approximated by a Gaussian as
N
is large, result in fluctuations of
\Omega
with a relative standard deviation
\sigma_{\Omega}\approx 0.024\Omega
, increased to the experimentally-measured value
\sigma_{\Omega}\approx 0.028\Omega
by residual fluctuations of laser power. We account for these fluctuations by averaging the results of each simulation over 500 runs, drawing
\Omega
from a Gaussian distribution.

We characterize these fluctuations by fitting the experimental Rabi oscillations in the regime
\Omega\gg\omega_{\mathrm{G}},\omega_{\mathrm{L}}
, where the oscillation envelope is primarily governed by Rabi frequency fluctuations. Gaussian-distributed fluctuations in
\Omega
are incorporated through the following fit function:

f(t)=\frac{A}{2}\left(1-\cos(\Omega t){\textrm{e}}^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\Omega}^{2}t^{2}}\right){\textrm{e}}^{-\Gamma t},

(47)

where
A
is an amplitude scaling factor and
\Gamma
the exponential decay rate represented in Fig. 6(e). Given the linear dependence of
\sigma_{\Omega}
on
\Omega
, we fix the fluctuation parameter in the fit to extract
\Gamma
for any Rabi frequencies. However, when the decay rate
\Gamma
becomes small, due to to the protection mechanism, the envelope becomes dominated by fluctuations of
\Omega
. In this case,
\Gamma
is difficult to estimate, leading to increased uncertainties for low values of
\Gamma
visible in Fig. 6(e) of the main text.

C.2
Inhomogeneous dephasing terms
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Deph}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Deph}}

The Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Deph}}
and the Liouvillian decay term
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Deph}}

A
above. We account for non-Markovianity by including in
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Deph}}

n=15
Dicke states for the purely Gaussian Doppler broadening, and
n=20
for the light-shifted one.

For Gaussian dephasing, we extract
\omega_{\mathrm{G}}=2\pi\times$61(8)\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}$
from the fit of the measured population decay
|s_{\mathrm{G}}(t)|^{2}
presented in “Experimental results” section of the main manuscript. This value is consistent with the temperature obtained by time-of-flight measurement with absorption imaging, yielding
\omega_{\mathrm{G}}=2\pi\times$66(1)\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}$
.

For the light-shifted case, the scaling angular frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{S}}
is obtained by independently measuring the temperature, the average light shift, and the width of the Gaussian atomic density profile in the trap. The anharmonicity of the trap, formed by crossed Gaussian beams, leads to a noticeable deviation between
\rho_{\mathrm{L}}
and the actual light-shift distribution
\rho_{\mathrm{L^{\prime}}}
, shown in Fig.

via Monte Carlo sampling, by randomly drawing a large number of atoms from the spatial distribution obtained by absorption imaging, calculating the light shift distribution knowing the trap’s geometry, and rescaling it using the measured mean value of the light shift. The velocity-dependent Doppler shifts being statistically independent on the position-dependent light shifts in a thermal cloud, the total inhomogeneous broadening distribution
\rho_{\mathrm{GL^{\prime}}}
is obtained either by a convolution of
\rho_{\mathrm{L^{\prime}}}
with a Gaussian
\rho_{\mathrm{G}}
or by an additional Monte Carlo sampling of Gaussian-distributed atomic velocities. We center
\rho_{\mathrm{GL^{\prime}}}

\psi_{\mathrm{GL^{\prime}},0}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{4}\beta_{k}\frac{1}{\pi^{1/4}\sqrt{2^{k}k!}}\mathcal{H}_{k}(x)e^{-x^{2}/2},

(48)

where
\beta_{k}
are the coefficients and
\mathcal{H}_{k}
are the Hermite polynomials. The procedure described in the theoretical section of the main manuscript is then applied algebraically, involving only the list of coefficients
\{\beta_{k}\}
.

C.3
Additional loss term
\mathbf{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Loss}}}

Additional losses from the state
\left|E_{0}\right\rangle
, described by the Liouvillian term
\mathbf{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Loss}}}
, arise from a combination of several effects left aside in the idealized model above. Among them, imperfections in the Rydberg blockade or a residual population of the short-lived intermediate state
\left|f\right\rangle
stemming from the finite detuning
\Delta
are expected to induce loss rates scaling as
\Omega^{2}
, making the overall additional damping rate evolve as
\Gamma_{\Omega}\Omega^{2}+\Gamma_{0}
. We estimate this rate in the strong driving regime with no light shifts, such as
\Omega\gg\omega_{\mathrm{G}}
, where driving protection makes inhomogeneous dephasing negligible. We fit the experimental oscillations to extract
\Gamma_{\Omega,\mathrm{fit}}\times 2\pi=$4.6(1)10^{-3}\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$^{-1}
and
\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{fit}}=$2\pi\times 2.6(1.5)\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}$
.
