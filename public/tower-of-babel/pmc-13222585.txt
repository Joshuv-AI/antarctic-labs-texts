Immunosenescence in Older Patients with Psoriasis: Mechanistic Insights and Opportunities for Biologic Therapy
Authors: Wenjie Wang, Shuying Zha, Liyun Sun, Dongmei Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPsoriasis is a chronic systemic inflammatory disease that is increasingly seen in older patients. As the immune system ages, the mechanisms that normally keep immune responses in balance become less precise. The adaptive immune system is reshaped both quantitatively and structurally, whereas innate immunity becomes less well regulated rather than simply less active. Age-related changes in the skin microenvironment may also help sustain local inflammatory signaling. In older patients with psoriasis, these age-related immune changes intersect with the pathways already known to drive disease. Th17 and cytotoxic CD8⁺ T (Tc17) responses remain prominent, control of antigen-presenting cells (APCs) is less tightly regulated, and natural killer (NK) cell function is also altered. These changes help sustain inflammation and further compromise the epidermal barrier. Treatment decisions are made more complex by common comorbidities, especially cardiovascular disease and metabolic syndrome. Biologic agents targeting TNF-α, IL-12/23, IL-23, IL-17, and IL-36 have markedly expanded the therapeutic options for psoriasis. Direct evidence in older patients with psoriasis remains limited, but the studies available so far suggest that treatment efficacy is broadly comparable to that seen in younger populations. Even so, immune aging may still affect both susceptibility to infection and the likelihood of remaining on treatment over time. TNF inhibitors remain particularly useful in patients with systemic inflammatory comorbidities, although infection risk requires close attention. IL-12/23 inhibitors offer the advantage of dual cytokine blockade. Selective IL-23 inhibitors allow more focused control of Th17-driven inflammation while leaving Th1 function relatively preserved. IL-17 inhibitors are often associated with rapid clinical improvement, whereas IL-36 inhibitors may be particularly promising in pustular disease. Clarifying how immunosenescence shapes disease behavior and treatment response in older patients should make it easier to individualize therapy.Keywords: psoriasis, immunosenescence, older patients, biologic therapy, adaptive immunity, innate immunity

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory disease with systemic involvement and a well-established immunologic basis.1 It may arise at any age, but its onset tends to follow a bimodal distribution, occurring most commonly between 30 and 39 years of age and again between 60 and 69 years.2 The latter pattern is increasingly important in the setting of population aging. Epidemiologic data support a meaningful burden of disease in older adults. An Italian study reported a lifetime prevalence of 3.5% in individuals 60–64 years old,3 and a German cohort found a prevalence of 5.3% among those 70–95 years of age.4 For many older adults, psoriasis does not occur in isolation. Comorbid cardiovascular disease and metabolic syndrome are common and often make management more complex. In addition, aging is accompanied by broad changes in immune function that may shape both the onset and the chronicity of psoriasis.5,6 Among these, immunosenescence has emerged as a particularly relevant concept. Coined by Roy Walford in 1960, the term refers to age-associated remodeling of the immune system, including impaired adaptive immunity and sustained activation of innate immune responses.1,2 Such changes may promote persistent inflammation and may partly explain the distinct presentation and treatment challenges of psoriasis in older adults.

Biologic therapy has transformed the management of psoriasis. Agents that target tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-17, and IL-23 can markedly reduce disease activity and often produce substantial improvements in quality of life.7 By interrupting inflammatory pathways that drive psoriatic disease, these therapies have become a mainstay of current clinical practice. In older patients, however, treatment decisions are often more complex. Aging affects both immune function and the cutaneous microenvironment, which may influence not only the clinical expression of psoriasis but also response to biologic therapy. These factors may also affect treatment safety and long-term persistence. Current evidence suggests that older patients with psoriasis generally achieve clinical responses similar to those seen in younger adults. Even so, serious adverse events appear to occur more often in this group, and treatment is discontinued more frequently because of safety concerns.7,8

Older patients with psoriasis often show changes in both innate and adaptive immunity and frequently have cardiovascular, metabolic, and other age-related comorbidities. Together, these factors may modify the inflammatory networks that drive psoriasis and may influence both disease course and response to biologic therapy. In clinical practice, this added complexity can make treatment selection more difficult and heighten concern about adverse events. Although targeted therapies are increasingly used in this population, the relationship between immunosenescence and psoriasis remains incompletely understood, particularly with regard to treatment response and safety. A better understanding of age-related immune changes may help explain differences in disease behavior and support more informed decisions about patient selection, baseline risk assessment, and treatment monitoring. In this review, we examine the evidence linking immunosenescence to psoriasis in older patients and discuss how these insights may inform biologic treatment strategies in routine clinical practice.

The Role of Immunosenescence in the Pathogenesis of Psoriasis in Older Patients

Psoriasis results from persistent crosstalk between innate and adaptive immunity.9 In psoriatic skin, keratinocytes work together with dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, and other innate immune cells to generate a local inflammatory environment characterized by TNF-α, IL-23, and IL-1 family cytokines.10 These signals, in turn, promote pathogenic T-cell responses, particularly T helper (Th)17 and cytotoxic CD8⁺ T (Tc17) cell responses, with increased production of IL-17, IL-22, and related cytokines.11 These processes contribute to persistent keratinocyte activation, epidermal hyperplasia, progressive barrier dysfunction, and other pathologic changes characteristic of psoriasis.11 In older patients, age-related changes in both innate and adaptive immunity may further shape these processes and may help explain persistent inflammation, differences in clinical presentation, and possible variation in response to biologic therapy.

Functional Remodeling of Adaptive Immunity

Older patients show a marked reduction in thymic activity, which limits the ongoing generation of naive T cells. Analyses of peripheral blood have demonstrated an accumulation of effector and terminally differentiated T cell populations together with a contraction of the naive T cell pool and loss of clonal diversity, changes widely recognized as hallmarks of immune senescence.12–14 In older patients with psoriasis, these age-associated immune changes interact with chronic inflammatory signaling pathways. Within chronically inflamed psoriatic skin and circulation, a narrowed and functionally biased adaptive immune repertoire may contribute to persistent immune activation.

In older individuals, both circulating and skin infiltrating Th17 and Tc17 cells are detected at higher frequencies in immune phenotyping studies of aging cohorts.15 This expansion appears sustained rather than transient. The lineage-defining transcription factor retinoic acid receptor–related orphan receptor gamma t (RORγt) maintains chromatin accessibility at effector loci such as IL-17A and IL-23 receptor (IL-23R), enabling differentiated Th17 cells to retain cytokine production even in the absence of strong antigenic stimulation.16 In older patients with psoriasis, the chronic inflammatory environment is shaped in part by IL-23 produced by antigen-presenting cells (APCs). IL-23 drives Th17 differentiation and increases IL-23R expression through the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling pathway, forming a positive feedback loop. As a result, levels of IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-22, and granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) rise in lesional tissue. These cytokines directly contribute to keratinocyte activation and help maintain psoriatic plaques.11,17 High levels of IL-6 in psoriatic lesions also have been linked to reduced stability of regulatory T (Treg) cells. Under inflammatory conditions, some Treg cells start to express RORγt and produce IL-17.18 Persistent Th17 activity, together with impaired regulatory control, can contribute to the chronic inflammatory state in older patients with psoriasis.

Aging is also associated with measurable alterations in the T cell receptor (TCR) repertoire. High-throughput sequencing of peripheral blood samples has revealed an age-associated reduction in diversity within the TCR complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3).19,20 In the skin, cutaneous lymphocyte–associated antigen (CLA)–positive T cells often expand in an oligoclonal manner. This pattern is likely due to reduced thymic output and long-term antigenic stimulation.21,22 Functional changes accompany these alterations in the TCR repertoire. After activation, clusters of differentiation 4 (CD4) memory T cells from older individuals tend to rely more on oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation, which may reflect age-associated metabolic remodeling.23 This metabolic bias appears to increase oxidative stress and cumulative cellular damage. It can promote short-lived effector T cell differentiation while limiting the persistence and survival of long-term memory T cells.24 These changes may help sustain chronic inflammation and limit the ability of the immune system to adapt during treatment.

Alterations in Innate Immunity and the Cutaneous Microenvironment

As people age, immune senescence affects not only adaptive immunity but also markedly changes innate immune cells, especially macrophages, DCs and natural killer (NK) cells. These cells often show differences in number, distribution, and functional activity, and this remodeling forms a key basis for the chronic low-level inflammation observed with aging. Such persistent inflammation increases vulnerability to infections and autoimmune diseases.25,26 In older patients with psoriasis, the age-associated decline in innate immune function occurs alongside changes in the local skin microenvironment, which together are thought to drive disease chronicity and reduce the response to standard treatments.

Macrophages

In older patients with psoriasis, studies have documented multiple alterations in macrophage function that influence both disease progression and therapeutic responsiveness. Macrophages, as important effectors of innate immunity, contribute to keratinocyte proliferation and inflammatory cell infiltration through proinflammatory signaling pathways, while also playing essential roles in tissue repair and resolution of inflammation.27,28 Aging is associated with progressive dysregulation of macrophage polarization and functional homeostasis. Although increased expression of alternatively activated (M2) macrophage-associated markers has been observed in some aging-associated studies,29,30 functional polarization toward a fully reparative M2 macrophage phenotype appears to be impaired. The paracrine microenvironment shaped by senescent fibroblasts and mesenchymal stem cells further limits effective M2 macrophage differentiation and sustains a proinflammatory profile.31 Age-associated disruption of intercellular communication attenuates responsiveness of regulatory pathways such as transforming growth factor beta/Sma- and Mad-related protein (TGF-β/Smad) signaling, which normally promotes M2 macrophage polarization and anti-inflammatory activity.32,33 Concurrently, extracellular vesicles released by senescent cells activate macrophages through the microRNA-30b-5p (miR-30b-5p)/sirtuin 1 (SIRT1)/nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) axis, thereby sustaining production of IL-1 beta (IL-1β), IL-6, and other proinflammatory mediators.34,35 Because direct evidence from older patients with psoriasis remains limited, several of the following mechanistic features are drawn in part from broader immunosenescence studies and non-psoriasis models. Together, these mechanisms may drive a macrophage phenotype characterized by impaired tissue repair capacity and sustained proinflammatory activity, reinforcing the chronic inflammatory environment in older patients with psoriasis.

In aging more broadly, macrophages gradually lose efficiency in both phagocytosis and antigen presentation. Surface expression of Fc gamma receptors, complement receptors, and major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II) molecules decreases. This decline weakens T-cell activation and reduces pathogen clearance, which may allow cutaneous inflammation to persist.36,37 Macrophage function is also altered with age. As mitochondrial integrity deteriorates, mitochondrial deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) damage and loss of membrane potential can increase reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, thereby promoting activation of the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and enhancing the release of IL-1β and IL-18.36,38 These changes can drive cells toward senescence or apoptosis. Impaired autophagy may further sustain inflammation by allowing damaged organelles and aggregated proteins to accumulate, which promotes inflammasome assembly and ongoing cytokine production.39,40 Epigenetic regulation is also disrupted, with promoter hypomethylation of proinflammatory genes and abnormal histone modifications contributing to continued expression of tumor necrosis factor and IL-6, while anti-inflammatory pathways remain relatively suppressed.41 Taken together, these age-related changes promote a macrophage phenotype that favors ongoing inflammation. In older patients with psoriasis, this imbalance may help sustain plaque activity, weaken treatment response, and increase the burden of comorbid disease. Targeting macrophage aging and the mechanisms that maintain this proinflammatory state may therefore offer additional therapeutic opportunities in this population.

DCs

Several studies have shown that aging is accompanied by changes in dendritic cell (DC) function that may weaken immune tolerance.42,43 In older individuals, DCs are less effective at inducing regulatory T (Treg) cells, produce more proinflammatory cytokines, and express lower levels of inhibitory molecules such as programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1).43 At the same time, costimulatory signaling appears to outweigh inhibitory signaling, which may reduce peripheral tolerance and permit expansion of autoreactive T cells.44 Experimental studies have also identified signaling pathways within DCs, including the mammalian sterile 20-like kinase 1 (MST1) and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, as regulators of IL-6 and related cytokine production.45 Although direct evidence in older patients with psoriasis remains limited, findings from aging research and other disease settings suggest that these DC abnormalities may favor Th17 differentiation and support an IL-17 dominant inflammatory milieu.45 In older patients with psoriasis, such changes may contribute to impaired immune regulation and may help explain the greater tendency toward autoimmunity seen in this population.46

In aging more broadly, DC populations are reshaped in both number and function. In older patients, immune cells often show signs of mitochondrial decline.47 These changes include a reduced membrane potential, decreased adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, and diminished oxidative phosphorylation.47,48 When energy supply is limited, DCs may struggle to meet the high metabolic demands needed for activation and antigen processing. This limitation can reduce the efficiency of MHC-II-dependent antigen presentation.49,50 Circulating plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), which participate in cutaneous immunity, decline with age. Their production of type I interferon (IFN-α) also decreases.51 This weakens early responses to viral infection and tissue injury and alters downstream adaptive immune regulation. At the same time, the distribution of conventional dendritic cell (cDC) subsets shifts. CD141+ cDC1, known for cross-presentation, tend to become less abundant, whereas CD1c+ cDC2 cells, with a more inflammatory profile, become relatively more prominent.51,52 These inflammatory DCs express higher levels of human leukocyte antigen DR (HLA-DR), and IL-1β, which may increase their capacity to activate naive T cells.51 In psoriatic lesions, expansion of Th1 and Th17 cells and a clear shift toward a proinflammatory environment have been described,53 and these findings may be relevant to chronic inflammation and disease activity in older patients with psoriasis.

NK Cells

Peripheral blood testing in older patients with psoriasis shows clear changes in lymphocyte composition. NK cells are increased in both proportion and absolute number, while cluster of CD8+ T cells are reduced, and the ratio of Th to Treg cells is higher.54 When age and disease are considered together using generalized linear models, NK cell expansion seems to reflect the combined influence of Psoriasis and aging rather than age alone.54 Examining immune networks in the same population reveals weaker links among cell subsets, suggesting that regulatory interactions become less effective.54 These observations suggest that immunosenescence actively shapes the immune environment in late-onset Psoriasis instead of acting as a passive background.

Cell number does not always correlate with function. In older people, NK cells may rise in number but often show a reduced ability to kill target cells, which emphasizes the importance of activating and inhibitory receptors on the cell surface.55 Even small changes in the balance of these receptors can alter NK cell responsiveness. In individuals carrying the human leukocyte antigen C (HLA-C) allele C*06:02, both CD8+ T cells and NK cells can recognize the same antigens, which may link genetic susceptibility to innate immune activity.56 In an aging immune system with weaker regulatory control, this overlap could promote inappropriate NK cell activation. Chronic inflammatory stress may further change receptor patterns, increasing the proportion of the inhibitory receptor natural killer group 2, member A (NKG2A) and reducing cytotoxic signaling.11,52 As a result, recognition and killing of target cells through NKG2D may be impaired. To our knowledge, large-scale studies confirming consistent NKG2A upregulation and natural killer group 2, member D (NKG2D) downregulation in blood and skin lesions from older psoriasis patients remain limited, and additional research is needed to clarify the NK cell phenotype in aging-associated psoriasis.

NK cell function depends heavily on metabolic reprogramming.57 In psoriasis lesions, NK cells display altered metabolic states that are closely associated with their pro-inflammatory activity. Immune senescence is accompanied by systemic declines in metabolic homeostasis and mitochondrial function, which likely worsen these metabolic impairments in NK cells.55 Consequently, NK cells produce higher levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interferon gamma (IFN-γ) and TNF-α, while their cytotoxic activity is reduced. The cumulative decline in metabolism and function may reduce the ability of NK cells to eliminate abnormal keratinocytes. This could contribute to the persistence of the inflammatory environment.

Skin Barrier

In older patients, the skin barrier shows multiple functional changes rather than a single structural defect.58 Lipid composition, junction organization, and immune balance are all affected. Studies consistently report lower ceramide levels in the stratum corneum of aged and inflamed skin, along with declines in other essential lipids. With fewer lipids, the barrier loses some of its hydrophobic properties and becomes less able to retain moisture. And then, transepidermal water loss increases, and external irritants penetrate more easily.58 Analyses of epidermal tissue reveal that tight junction proteins, such as occludin and zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), are reduced, particularly in the lower epidermis.59,60 These proteins, normally limited to the upper granular layer, extend into the mid and lower spinous layers in a disorganized pattern, weakening junctional integrity.59,60 At the same time, IL-17A and IL-22 suppress tight junction protein expression, which may worsen inflammation in the epidermis. As immune function declines with age, the skin’s repair capacity decreases, leaving transepidermal water loss elevated.

The skin barrier disruption also affects the epidermal microenvironment. In psoriatic lesions, human cathelicidin antimicrobial peptide (LL-37) is upregulated.61,62 LL-37 can bind to the exposed self-DNA and form immune complexes. These immune complexes can trigger pDCs through receptors such as toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9). Activation of pDCs results in the production of IFN-α.61,62 This can lead to the amplification of autoimmune and proinflammatory signaling pathways, providing a platform for the early activation of the immune system in Psoriasis. Non-invasive imaging techniques, such as high-frequency ultrasound and optical coherence tomography, can detect these changes.63 During the plaque stage, epidermal thickening, irregularity of the dermal-epidermal interface, and abnormal ultrasound echoes are detectable, and these features serve as practical indicators of disease activity and barrier status.63 The relationship between immunosenescence and psoriasis in older patients is shown in Figure 1.

Biologic Therapy in Older Patients with Psoriasis

TNF-α Inhibitors (TNFi)

TNF-α was recognized early on as a proinflammatory cytokine involved in psoriasis. It induces keratinocyte proliferation and is also involved in dermal vascular alterations. Experimental studies also revealed its role in the activation of the IL-23 and IL-17 pathway.64 Unlike IL-17 and IL-23, which are more downstream, TNF-α is upstream in the inflammatory pathway, and TNFi have the potential to improve cutaneous lesions while also reducing systemic inflammatory markers. As one ages, there are changes in the immune system. Circulating proinflammatory cytokines and acute phase proteins gradually increase, leading to a chronic low-grade inflammatory condition, which is often referred to as inflammaging.65,66 Observational studies in healthy subjects have demonstrated an increase in the levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and high sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) over time.67 An increase in these biomarkers has been linked to cardiovascular events and metabolic disorders in the elderly.68 TNF-α induces IL-6 expression and indirectly induces CRP production, making it more upstream in this inflammatory cascade and thus playing a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of inflammaging.69 In older psoriasis patients, TNF-α is implicated not only in the cutaneous inflammatory process but also, importantly, in the systemic chronic inflammation and associated conditions such as cardiovascular disease and metabolic disorders. In patients with a greater inflammatory burden and associated metabolic and cardiovascular risk factors, the use of TNFi may potentially be beneficial in decreasing overall inflammatory processes, in addition to controlling cutaneous disease. Currently available TNF inhibitors include adalimumab, infliximab, golimumab, certolizumab pegol, and etanercept, a fusion protein.70 The effectiveness of these drugs in psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis has been established based on accumulated clinical experience.71 A similar response to treatment has been observed across most patient subgroups,72,73 including those with associated conditions such as liver cirrhosis,74 indicating that treatment response is not strictly limited by demographic factors or specific associated diseases. To our knowledge, available evidence has not shown a clear difference in response between older and younger patients, which, to some extent, supports the use of TNFi in appropriately selected older patients with Psoriasis.

In older patients with psoriasis, adverse events from TNFi may become more apparent in the context of immunosenescence. Clinical evidence reveals that the rate of patients stopping TNFi therapy because of adverse events increases with time, and infections are the most common reason, especially upper respiratory tract infection (URTI) and skin or soft tissue infection (SSTI).73,74 Age-associated changes in immunity, such as decreased T-cell repertoire, impaired macrophage and natural killer NK cell effector function, and impaired skin repair, may play a role in this increased susceptibility. TNF-α is involved in macrophage activation and the preservation of granuloma architecture, and systemic blockade might theoretically pose a risk for respiratory infections, skin or soft tissue infections, and reactivation of latent tuberculosis (TB). An observational study also indicates that older patients on TNFi have a higher incidence of severe infections than younger patients, although it is hard to distinguish this risk from the influence of age, comorbidities, and underlying immune system decline.75 Regarding cancer risk, there seems to be no strong evidence at present that TNFi increase the incidence of solid malignancies. However, in older patients with impaired immune surveillance, skin malignancies and hematologic malignancies still need to be monitored in the long term. TNFi may have potential benefits in cardiovascular diseases due to the reduction of systemic inflammation, although the use of these drugs in older patients with moderate to severe congestive heart failure should be carefully considered.76

Although some studies have found that drug survival may be shorter in older patients with psoriasis. Nevertheless, there is no evidence that directly connects this to the mechanisms of immunosenescence, such as the expansion of pathogenic T cell clones or irreversible activation of the inflammatory pathway. Rather, this is most likely due to a variety of factors, including adverse events, the progression of comorbidities, and changes in treatment approach. In this regard, TNFi remain a viable treatment option for older patients with psoriasis, but they should be used with caution and may not be suitable as a universal first-line therapy. Rather, individualized treatment decisions that consider the risk of infection, comorbidity profile, and overall inflammatory burden are probably more appropriate than those based solely on age. As the understanding of immune aging improves, the treatment of older patients with TNFi may gradually evolve to a more refined approach based on patient phenotype and inflammatory profile.

IL-12/23 Inhibitors (IL-12/23i)

IL-12/23i, with ustekinumab as a representative, specifically target the p40 subunit shared by IL-12 and IL-23.77,78 Recent cellular studies show that C-C chemokine receptor type 7 positive (CCR7+) DCs, the main producers of IL-23 in psoriasis, can express both the p19 subunit of IL-23 and the p40 subunit of IL-12, revealing these cells serve as a key link between the Th17 and Th1 inflammatory axes.79 Targeting p40 with ustekinumab may directly affect this pathogenic DCs subset, simultaneously reducing downstream signaling from both the IL-23/Th17 and IL-12/Th1 pathways. In older patients with psoriasis, the IL-23/IL-17 axis remains central to disease initiation and maintenance, but Th1 -associated responses continue to contribute to inflammation regulation and host defense.80,81 IL-12, a classic Th1 cytokine, is essential for activating cytotoxic T cells and NK cells, as well as mediating antiinfection and antitumor immune responses.81 Clinical data support stable efficacy of ustekinumab in older patients. In one cohort of older patients and another study primarily involving younger patients, psoriasis area and severity index (PASI)75 response rates at 16 weeks were 56.5% and 57.6%, respectively, indicating that ustekinumab maintains consistent effectiveness in older psoriasis patients.82,83

From the perspective of immunosenescence and long-term safety, IL-12/23i raise theoretical concerns that their dual-pathway blockade might cause nonselective immunosuppression, potentially increasing the risk of infections, particularly those caused by intracellular pathogens, as well as malignancies and cardiovascular events. But, the real-world evidence showed that ustekinumab, an IL-12/23i, is generally associated with a lower risk of serious infections compared with TNFi.81 A single case report described chronic lymphocytic leukemia in a patient with psoriatic arthritis treated with ustekinumab, but this isolated case is insufficient to prove a causal link.84 Cohort studies also indicate that IL-12/23i do not raise the overall risk of malignancy compared with TNFi.85 Earlier concerns suggested that ustekinumab might increase cardiovascular events, yet recent data do not support this worry.71,86 Analyses using the TriNetX database showed that in patients starting biologics for psoriasis or psoriatic arthritis, IL-12/23i did not greatly raise the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events or venous thromboembolism.87 A nationwide cohort study from Korea found no significant difference in the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events between IL-12/23 inhibitors and TNF inhibitors. The same study reported lower all-cause mortality with IL-12/23 inhibitors, with this association appearing more pronounced in women and older patients.88 Retrospective studies in inflammatory bowel disease have likewise suggested that advanced biologic therapies, including IL-12/23 inhibitors, may reduce the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular events.89 Among the agents examined, IL-12/23 inhibitors appeared to show the most favorable cardiovascular profile. Taken together, these findings are reassuring with respect to the long-term safety of IL-12/23 inhibitors. Even so, in older patients with immunosenescence, their overall benefit-risk profile still needs to be defined more clearly in prospective studies.

Long-term management of psoriasis involves more than safety alone. Data from a German outpatient registry showed lower drug-retention rates with IL-12/23 inhibitors than with TNF inhibitors and IL-17 inhibitors, suggesting that these agents may be discontinued more often because of limited efficacy or nonserious adverse events.90 In older patients, this may be reflected clinically in a slower treatment response or incomplete clearance of skin lesions. The persistence of IL-23-producing CCR7+ dendritic cells in lesional skin, which has been linked to disease recurrence,79 further suggests that inflammation in some patients remains strongly dependent on the IL-23 axis. In these cases, broad p40 inhibition may not fully address the underlying pathogenic mechanisms, and additional IL-12 blockade does not always yield proportional clinical benefit.81 Lower retention rates therefore appear consistent with the idea that limited efficacy, together with adverse events unrelated to tumors or cardiovascular disease, may restrict the long-term use of IL-12/23 inhibitors in practice.90 In patients with age-associated decline of Th1 function,91 the value of additional IL-12 inhibition remains uncertain. IL-12/23i may be most appropriate for those with manageable infection risk who do not require full lesion clearance but need stable long-term disease control. Conversely, in subgroups with pronounced markers of immune senescence and strong activation of the IL-23/IL-17 axis, selective IL-23 inhibitors (IL-23i) or IL-17i may better match the underlying pathogenic mechanisms. Ongoing post-marketing surveillance and extended follow-up studies will be essential to clarify safety and to identify the patients most likely to benefit.

IL-23 Inhibitors (IL-23i)

In psoriatic lesions, IL-23 is highly expressed within the epidermis,92 and its activity extends beyond the skin. Work exploring the skin-gut joint axis has placed IL-23 at the center of the inflammatory network that links cutaneous, nail, and peripheral joint involvement.93 There is clinical evidence supporting the role of IL-23i in patients with psoriasis involving the skin, nails, and peripheral joints.71,94 At the cellular level, IL-23 maintains the integrity of pathogenic Th17 cells, enhances their effector functions, and prevents their conversion to a nonpathogenic phenotype.93 In older patients, immune senescence is characterized by decreased thymic function, reduction in the naive T cell compartment, and reduced immune diversity. As regulatory homeostasis declines, chronic inflammation becomes increasingly reliant on the constant cytokine signaling input, especially from IL-23.93 Under these conditions, IL-23 remains an active driver of pathogenic immune circuits. Blocking IL-23 therefore targets this persistent pathway. Guselkumab, risankizumab, and tildrakizumab selectively target the p19 subunit unique to interleukin 23, thus inhibiting IL-23-mediated signaling while preserving IL-12 function, which is critical for protective Th1 immunity.95 By doing so, these drugs avoid nonselective interference with host immunity.95,96 The high level of specificity makes anti-p19 monoclonal antibodies highly targeted biologic agents for the treatment of psoriasis. They are therefore an important component of long-term management in older patients.71

In clinical practice, physicians must still weigh safety and the feasibility of maintaining disease control over time. The issue becomes even more relevant in patients with multimorbidity, where age-associated changes in immune function may influence both efficacy and tolerability. In this context, agents that avoid broad immunosuppression are generally preferred. IL-23i are therefore of particular interest, as they target the IL-23 and Th17 pathway in a more selective manner. Evidence from one real-world cohort, which included a meaningful proportion of older patients, demonstrated that tildrakizumab achieved sustained clinical responses in psoriasis, even among individuals with multiple comorbid conditions.97 PASI75 and PASI90 rates were largely similar across age groups, and the durability of response was maintained during follow-up. Although observational data have inherent limitations, these findings reveal that the efficacy of IL-23i does not seem substantially reduced by immune remodeling associated with aging.97 In this study, approximately 31% of patients reached rapid and near complete clearance, defined as PASI less than 1, by week 12.97 Patients achieving early deep responses were more likely to have lower body mass index, less prior exposure to biologic therapy, and no concomitant psoriatic arthritis. These characteristics may reflect a relatively preserved immune state, which reveals the possibility that baseline immune status at treatment initiation could influence the depth and speed of response.97

Beyond efficacy, safety remains an important consideration in older patients. In this cohort, no cases of tumor recurrence or newly diagnosed malignancy were reported, despite the fact that 9.9% of patients had a prior history of cancer.97 This matters in older patients, where tumor surveillance is often a concern, and also reveals that the IL-23 pathway may play a limited role in tumor immunosurveillance.97,98 Besides, severe infections were not more frequent, and no signal of TB reactivation emerged. Most reported infections were mild to moderate upper respiratory tract events, similar to what would be expected in the general population.97 These findings are relevant in older patients, whose innate and cellular immune reserves are often reduced. Practical aspects also matter in routine care. Dosing once every 12 weeks reduces treatment burden, particularly for patients already taking multiple medications. Treatment persistence reached 93.4% among middle-aged and older participants, suggesting that adherence can be sustained in patients with multimorbidity.97 Overall, IL-23i represent a practical option for older patients with psoriasis, particularly in the setting of immune aging and comorbidity.

IL-17 Inhibitor (IL-17i)

In clinical studies, IL-17 inhibitors, including secukinumab, ixekizumab, and brodalumab, have achieved higher rates of skin clearance than other biologic agents during the early treatment phase, such as at 12 to 16 weeks.99 This is consistent with the known role of IL-17 in psoriasis. IL-17 sits close to the end of the inflammatory cascade in psoriasis and acts directly on keratinocytes. It promotes their proliferation, drives the expression of antimicrobial peptides and chemokines, and interferes with tight junction integrity in the epidermis. Blocking IL-17A or its receptor therefore shuts down a pathway that is immediately relevant to plaque formation, which likely accounts for the rapid improvement and the depth of clearance observed with this group of agents.100,101 The situation in older patients is somewhat different from that in younger adults. In the context of immune senescence, inflammatory circuits in older patients with psoriasis often depend heavily on downstream effector cytokines to sustain disease activity. Direct targeting of IL-17 can result in a rapid reduction in lesion burden and inflammatory status. This characteristic may be particularly relevant for older patients with high disease activity who require prompt control of symptoms. Anti-drug antibodies have been reported across the IL-17i class, although the frequency differs among individual agents. Bimekizumab, for example, has shown a relatively higher incidence of antibody formation. However, antibody positivity does not consistently translate into reduced efficacy.102 In routine care, when one IL-17i does not provide adequate control, clinicians often switch within the same class, such as from secukinumab to brodalumab, rather than abandoning the pathway altogether.103 Real-world data indicate that a proportion of patients can recapture disease control after such a change.103 In resource limited environments, this sequential approach within the IL-17i may also represent a more feasible and cost conscious option for patients who fail standard regimens.104

Drug retention is an important measure of long-term efficacy and tolerability. In real-world studies, drug retention at month 24 was lower for IL-17i than for IL-23i.99 Data from the UK registry also found that IL-23i were associated with the lowest risk of discontinuation for lack of efficacy and with longer drug survival compared with IL-17i.105 These findings indicate that although IL-17i act rapidly, they do not appear to offer an advantage in long-term treatment maintenance. From the perspective of immune senescence and its interaction with the skin barrier, the physiological role of IL-17 also warrants attention. IL-17 contributes to the maintenance of cutaneous and mucosal barrier immunity and participates in host defense against fungi and certain bacteria, largely through induction of antimicrobial peptides such as human β-defensins (hBDs), S100 calcium-binding proteins, and LL-37.106 Immune senescence itself is associated with impaired barrier repair, altered tight junction protein expression, and reduced innate immune cell function. Further inhibition of IL-17 in this context may increase susceptibility of the skin and mucosa. Clinical studies indicate that adverse events related to IL-17i are mainly mucosal Candida infections, and URTI is also relatively common, although usually mild to moderate.60,71 In older patients, particularly those with diabetes, chronic lung disease, or long-term antibiotic use, closer monitoring is advisable. IL-17i may also exacerbate inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) in susceptible individuals.107 Caution is required when prescribing these agents to elderly patients with active IBD.

IL-17i have the advantage of rapidly alleviating diseases, but compared with TNFi, they have a lower degree of systemic immunosuppression. Compared with IL-12/23i, their inflammation blockade is more direct, but it lacks the regulatory ability of upstream pathogenic signals. Under the background of immunosenescence, although blocking IL-17 alone can greatly reduce the inflammatory response in the short term, since the continuous activation of the upstream IL-23/Th17 axis has not been regulated, the pathogenic Th17 cell population and its immune memory are retained. After drug withdrawal, inflammation may recur, leading to disease relapse. Due to this, IL-17i seem to be more suitable for older patients who need rapid symptom relief, have a heavy skin lesion burden, and have a controllable infection risk. However, in the context of pursuing long-term stable immune remodeling and minimizing infection risk, their priority may be lower than that of selective IL-23i.

IL-36 Inhibitor

IL-36 has recently attracted attention as an important inflammatory pathway in psoriasis, with a key role in generalized pustular psoriasis (GPP) and inflammatory burst phenotypes.108–110 Cytokines IL-36α, β, and γ produced by keratinocytes activate the IL-36 receptor. This stimulates keratinocytes, DCs, and neutrophils, forming a feedback loop characterized by neutrophil infiltration and amplified acute inflammation.109,111 In GPP, certain mutations in the IL-36 receptor antagonist gene (IL36RN) reduce the activity of the IL-36 receptor antagonist (IL-36RA), while mutations in caspase recruitment domain family member 14 (CARD14) enhance its function. These changes may disrupt IL-36 signaling and cause excessive cytokine release along with neutrophil accumulation in the epidermis.112,113 This process drives local skin inflammation and is often accompanied by systemic symptoms such as fever and multiorgan involvement. In some cases, the disease progresses to complications that can threaten life.110,114

In older patients, declining innate immune regulation and impaired resolution of inflammation may further intensify responses mediated by IL-36 and contribute to systemic inflammation.110,114 This biological background provides a rationale for therapeutic intervention. Clinical trials have found that the rapid efficacy of spesolimab in controlling acute flares of GPP and preventing recurrence.115,116 In the active phase, this drug, the first biologic licensed in Europe for this indication, prevented disease severity and improved clinical outcomes by inhibiting the IL-36 receptor.117 By interrupting this signaling pathway early, IL-36 inhibition seems particularly effective in older patients with acute presentation of high fever, leukocytosis, and very high levels of CRP.113,117 Unlike TNFi, IL-23i, or IL-17i, which primarily target adaptive immunity, this approach specifically targets the keratinocyte and neutrophil interaction.113,117 Even in the setting of immune senescence, in which adaptive immunity is dampened, targeting this pathway can still provide effective control of inflammation because its primary mechanism is focused on innate immunity.

Clinical trials and initial real-world experience have, to date, offered only a short-term but generally reassuring safety profile for IL-36 inhibitors. Severe infections have been rare in the literature, and most therapies have been used for a short duration or intermittently, which may help to explain the low rate of infectious complications.118 To date, most trials have been conducted in patients with the pustular or inflammatory flare variants of psoriasis, and not in those with the more stable plaque variant. As such, there is little information on long-term use. The effects of chronic therapy on infection risk and tumor immune surveillance, especially in the context of immune senescence, have not been well established. The introduction of spesolimab, an IL-36 inhibitor, is an important step forward in targeting this pathway. It also provides patients with GPP a new therapeutic option, although treatment of this disease is not yet standardized worldwide.113 From a clinical standpoint, the use of IL-36 inhibitors seems better suited to the short-term management of acute inflammation or for the management of generalized pustular flares in older patients. The most appropriate timing and duration of use in older patients with psoriasis require further study. Other Biological Agents

In addition to the TNF-α, IL-12/23, IL-23, IL-17, and IL-36 inhibitors that are currently in widespread clinical use, other biologic agents targeting different components of immune regulation have been explored in psoriasis and related inflammatory disorders in recent years. However, none of the agents discussed below, including PCSK9 inhibitors, IL-1-targeted agents, IL-22-targeted agents, and CTLA-4-based strategies, is currently approved for psoriasis. Accordingly, the following discussion is exploratory and is intended to highlight potential mechanistic or future therapeutic relevance rather than current clinical availability for psoriasis. Although these agents have not become part of mainstream psoriasis therapy, their potential value deserves consideration from the perspective of immunosenescence and psoriasis in older patients, particularly in patients with multiple systemic comorbidities, or in those for whom conventional targeted therapies are limited or carry a higher risk.

Circulating levels of proprotein convertase subtilisin kexin type 9 (PCSK9) are greatly elevated in patients with psoriasis and show a positive correlation with PASI scores as well as systemic inflammatory markers.120 In lesional epidermis, keratinocytes display a gradient pattern of PCSK9 expression that inversely correlates with IL-36 gamma levels,121 linking PCSK9 to the inflammatory milieu within psoriatic skin. Experimental and clinical observations further indicate that PCSK9 can impair endothelial function and enhance monocyte-macrophage activation,122 providing a biologic basis for the connection between cutaneous inflammation and atherosclerosis. Although PCSK9 was originally characterized as a regulator of lipid metabolism that increases circulating low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) through promotion of low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) degradation, its function is not limited to lipid regulation and extends into immune modulation.121

In older patients with psoriasis, persistent inflammation and gradual changes in both innate and adaptive immunity appear to increase the risk of cardiovascular comorbidities.123–125 There is increasing interest in whether PCSK9 inhibitors may do more than lower cholesterol. In addition to reducing LDL-C, these medications could influence inflammation in ways that protect against atherosclerosis. In patients with a high inflammatory burden, this reduction may help limit plaque formation and destabilization.126 Evidence to date is derived largely from cardiovascular outcome trials rather than psoriasis-specific studies.127 Therefore, any role for PCSK9 inhibitors in psoriasis itself remains speculative. Long-term treatment in older patients also requires attention to polypharmacy and financial burden.128 Even so, PCSK9 inhibitors may still have a role in the broader management of cardiovascular comorbidities in older patients with psoriasis when otherwise clinically indicated. Recent experimental work has also explored direct targeting of PCSK9 in psoriasis using a biomimetic nanocapsule microneedle system delivering PCSK9 small interfering RNA (siRNA), which alleviated psoriasis-like dermatitis in an animal model through suppression of the NF-κB pathway and modulation of Th cell subsets.129

IL-1 pathway-targeted biologics also merit attention. IL-1a and IL-1β act at the early stage of the inflammatory cascade and represent important drivers of inflammatory aging.130 Persistent activation of IL-1 signaling in older patients has been closely linked to atherosclerosis, insulin resistance, and chronic organ inflammation.131–133 From a mechanistic standpoint, inhibition of IL-1 may help reduce the background level of systemic inflammation and may have clinical relevance in older patients with psoriasis who carry a substantial burden of comorbid disease. However, IL-1 is integral to host defense against infection. Prolonged systemic suppression therefore requires caution in individuals with diminished immune reserve and may be more appropriate in selected patients with inflammatory flares or specific clinical phenotypes. At present, however, the use of IL-1-targeted therapy in psoriasis remains investigational rather than established clinical practice.

In patients with psoriasis, IL-22 levels are consistently elevated in plasma, serum, and lesional tissue, and these increases track with clinical activity.134–136 IL-22 acts directly on keratinocytes, driving their proliferation and epidermal thickening while restraining terminal differentiation, changes that compromise barrier integrity.134 In older patients, the skin’s ability to repair itself often declines with immune aging. This environment may allow persistent IL-22 activity to keep lesions chronic by promoting keratinocyte overgrowth and slowing their maturation. Targeting IL-22 or its receptor with biologic therapies provides a more precise way to influence this pathway. Since IL-22 mainly affects epithelial cells rather than the broader immune system, this strategy could reduce the risk of widespread immune suppression. Even so, clinical data supporting IL-22 targeted therapy in psoriasis remain sparse, and no such agents are currently approved for psoriasis.134–136

Checkpoint pathways such as cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4) have been discussed as a possible direction in the management of psoriasis in older patients.71 These agents do not function by blocking a single inflammatory cytokine. Instead, they influence T cell activation through co-stimulatory signaling, thereby adjusting the overall immune threshold. In the setting of immune aging, the problem is often dysregulation rather than simple hyperactivation. For this reason, restoring balance may be more relevant than intensifying suppression.71 At present, no established agents are available for psoriasis. This concept may be reasonable in selected older patients with relatively stable disease, particularly when long-term safety and preservation of immune stability are central considerations. While there are currently no established clinical agents available, such strategies may be particularly relevant for older patients with relatively stable disease, where the focus is on long-term safety and the preservation of immune stability.

Conclusion

Aging reshapes the immune system, and those changes have direct consequences for psoriasis. In older patients, weaker adaptive immune control, persistent innate immune activation, and slower barrier repair can work together to keep cutaneous inflammation active. This may help explain why psoriasis in later life may have distinct clinical features, why infection risk may be higher, and why responses to biologic therapy are sometimes less predictable For that reason, biologic treatment in older patients should be chosen with particular care, with attention to expected efficacy, infection risk, comorbidities, and the patient’s overall inflammatory profile.

TNFi remain effective for controlling systemic inflammation, but in older patients they require close monitoring for infection. IL-12/23i are generally well tolerated; however, in the setting of immune senescence, concurrent blockade of both pathways may not always be necessary, and their long-term benefit in older patients remains uncertain. By contrast, selective IL-23i may be particularly useful in this population because they combine targeted cytokine inhibition with durable efficacy and a favorable safety profile overall, features that support long-term disease control. IL-17i can produce rapid clinical improvement, but concerns about mucosal host defense and the risk of relapse may make them less attractive for long-term maintenance. IL-36 inhibitors appear most relevant in the setting of acute disease flares, especially GPP. Future treatment decisions should not rely on chronological age alone. A better approach may be to consider evidence of immune senescence together with each patient’s inflammatory phenotype when selecting biologic therapy. Prospective studies designed specifically for older patients with psoriasis are still needed to compare biologic agents with respect to efficacy, safety, infection risk, and long-term treatment persistence. Better biomarkers of immune senescence would also help guide more individualized treatment in this population.
