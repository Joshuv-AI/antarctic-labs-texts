Obturator hernia misdiagnosed as inguinal incarceration: a case of postoperative small-bowel perforation and subcutaneous fecal emphysema
Authors: Anas Derkaoui, Tarik Deflaoui, Intissar Aissi, Abdelali Guellil, Rachid Jabi, Mohammed Bouziane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:An obturator hernia is a rare condition accounting for less than 1% of abdominal wall hernias. Due to its deep pelvic location and nonspecific clinical presentation, it is frequently misdiagnosed, most often as an inguinal hernia. Delayed or incorrect diagnosis may result in bowel ischemia and perforation.Case presentation:We report the case of a 61-year-old man who underwent emergency open surgery at another institution for a presumed strangulated right inguinal hernia with placement of prosthetic mesh. No operative report was available regarding intraoperative findings, bowel assessment, or mesh positioning. Eight days later, the patient presented with progressive abdominal distention, respiratory distress, and extensive subcutaneous emphysema involving the cervical and thoracic regions. A CT scan demonstrated pneumoperitoneum, bilateral pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, and an incarcerated obturator hernia with small-bowel ischemia and a suspected perforation. Emergency laparotomy revealed a perforated ileal loop associated with extensive fecal contamination tracking through fascial planes in proximity to the previously placed mesh. Segmental bowel resection with double stoma, obturator defect closure, and drainage were performed. The postoperative course was favorable.Clinical discussion:This case illustrates several rare and clinically relevant issues: (1) an obturator hernia mimicking an inguinal hernia; (2) a complex postoperative course with bowel perforation in the context of recent mesh repair; and (3) extensive subcutaneous fecal emphysema with thoracic extension. However, in the absence of operative documentation from the initial surgery, it remains impossible to determine with certainty whether the obturator hernia was initially unrecognized, whether ischemia evolved secondarily, or whether the perforation occurred independently in the postoperative period. This uncertainty highlights the importance of caution in attributing postoperative complications to a single causal mechanism when complete clinical data are unavailable.Conclusion:Obturator hernia remains a diagnostic challenge in emergency surgery. In cases of persistent postoperative obstruction after groin hernia repair, alternative diagnoses should be considered. This case also underlines the importance of complete operative documentation and close postoperative follow-up. Subcutaneous fecal emphysema represents an exceptionally rare but severe manifestation of gastrointestinal perforation.

Introduction

Obturator hernia is a rare entity, representing less than 1% of all abdominal wall hernias, and is often diagnosed late due to nonspecific symptoms[1,2]. It predominantly affects elderly, thin patients and may present with bowel obstruction or mimic more common groin hernias[3].

Diagnostic delay can lead to bowel ischemia, necrosis, or perforation[4]. Cross-sectional imaging, particularly CT, plays a central role in diagnosis[5–7].

We report a complex case, initially managed as a strangulated inguinal hernia, later complicated by bowel perforation and extensive subcutaneous fecal emphysema. Case report

A 61-year-old man with no significant medical history was referred for worsening abdominal distension and respiratory distress. He had previously undergone an emergency open right inguinal hernia repair with prosthetic mesh placement at another institution for a presumed strangulated inguinal hernia.

Importantly, no operative report was available regarding intraoperative findings, bowel viability, or prosthetic positioning.

Postoperatively, the patient reportedly experienced persistent intestinal obstruction without full clinical resolution.

Eight days after surgery, he presented to our emergency department with progressive abdominal distension, dyspnea, and rapidly extending subcutaneous emphysema involving the thoracic and cervical regions. On admission, he was hemodynamically unstable and hypoxemic.

An urgent CT scan revealed pneumoperitoneum, pneumomediastinum, bilateral pneumothoraces, and diffuse subcutaneous emphysema. A right obturator hernia containing ischemic small bowel with signs suggestive of perforation was also identified (Fig. 1). An emergency midline laparotomy was performed. Intraoperatively, a perforated ileal loop was found with extensive fecal contamination spreading through fascial planes in close proximity to the previously placed mesh. These findings suggested a complex intra-abdominal evolution, although a definitive causal relationship could not be established.

The postoperative course was favorable, with progressive respiratory and hemodynamic stabilization. No signs of necrotizing soft tissue infection (including Meleney’s gangrene) developed during close clinical surveillance and broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy.

The patient was discharged after a full recovery. At the 3-month follow-up, he had a good clinical course, with a satisfactory general condition, no infectious complications, and no evidence of recurrent hernia.

Discussion

Obturator hernia remains one of the most difficult abdominal wall hernias to diagnose clinically. Its deep location and nonspecific symptoms often lead to misdiagnosis, particularly as an inguinal hernia[9].

In this case, initial surgical management was performed for a presumed strangulated inguinal hernia. However, due to the absence of operative documentation, the initial intraoperative findings could not be verified, limiting definitive interpretation of the clinical course.

It therefore remains uncertain whether the obturator hernia was present but unrecognized during the first intervention or whether the clinical picture evolved in the postoperative period.

This limitation highlights an important issue in emergency surgery: the absence of detailed operative reports may significantly compromise postoperative clinical interpretation and medico-surgical analysis.

CT imaging is essential in suspected bowel obstruction, particularly in elderly patients with atypical presentations[5–7]. Early imaging might have clarified the diagnosis and potentially modified management.

The presence of bowel perforation adjacent to prosthetic mesh raises concerns about surgery in potentially contaminated or uncertain fields. While mesh repair is widely accepted in elective hernia surgery, its use in emergency settings remains debated[10–12].

Although a direct causal relationship cannot be established, the anatomical proximity between the perforation and the mesh suggests a possible contribution of local inflammatory changes or altered tissue planes to disease progression.

Finally, subcutaneous fecal emphysema is an extremely rare manifestation of gastrointestinal perforation, and its extension to the cervical and thoracic regions represents an exceptional clinical presentation.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the diagnostic complexity of obturator hernia and the challenges of interpreting postoperative complications in the absence of complete surgical documentation. Persistent postoperative obstruction should prompt early imaging to exclude alternative or missed pathology. Subcutaneous fecal emphysema remains a rare but severe manifestation of bowel perforation. This case emphasizes the importance of meticulous operative reporting and close postoperative surveillance in emergency hernia surgery.
