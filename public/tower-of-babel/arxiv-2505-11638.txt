Accelerating Natural Gradient Descent for PINNs with Randomized Numerical Linear Algebra Thanks: Corresponding author: Ivan Bioli
Ivan Bioli †,‡, Carlo Marcati §, Giancarlo Sangalli †,¶

Abstract
. Natural Gradient Descent (NGD) has emerged as a promising optimization algorithm for training neural network-based solvers for partial differential equations (PDEs), such as Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs). However, its practical use is often limited by the high computational cost of solving linear systems involving the Gramian matrix. While matrix-free NGD methods based on the conjugate gradient (CG) method avoid explicit matrix inversion, the ill-conditioning of the Gramian significantly slows the convergence of CG. In this work, we extend matrix-free NGD to broader classes of problems than previously considered and propose the use of Randomized Numerical Linear Algebra (RandNLA) techniques for efficient preconditioning of the inner CG solver. The resulting algorithms demonstrate substantial performance improvements over existing NGD-based methods on a range of PDE problems discretized using neural networks, and offer competitive results compared to other state-of-the-art optimizers. Keywords. Natural Gradient, Randomized Numerical Linear Algebra, Preconditioning, Neural Networks, PDEs.



1.
Introduction

Neural network (NN) based approaches for solving partial differential equations
(PDEs) have recently attracted significant attention as promising alternatives
to traditional numerical methods for both forward and inverse problems. A common
strategy is to incorporate the governing PDE directly into the loss function, as
in Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)

[
30
,
72
,
78
]
,
Variational PINNs
[
51
,
52
]
, the
Deep Ritz method
[
31
]
, and various subsequent extensions

[
8
,
42
,
9
,
25
,
75
]
.
These methods hold the promise of providing mesh-free approximations of PDE
solutions and of exploiting the approximation power of neural networks

[
28
,
24
]
, particularly in high
dimensions
[
20
]
, positioning them as promising tools for
high-dimensional, inverse, or complex domain problems.
Despite this theoretical promise, the practical adoption and reliability of
NN-based PDE solvers are often hindered by significant optimization challenges.
Standard first-order algorithms, notably Adam
[
53
]
, have been
reported to often perform poorly, stagnating at solutions with low accuracy

[
58
,
86
,
73
,
87
]
.
This issue is generally attributed not to the expressiveness of neural networks,
but to the ill-conditioning of the loss landscape

[
73
,
23
,
58
]
.
Consequently, specialized optimization strategies tailored to PDE-related
loss functions are increasingly being advocated

[
21
,
65
,
76
]
.

To counteract these training pathologies, various strategies have been proposed.
A body of work focuses on loss weighting schemes or adaptive selection of
collocation points to balance contributions from different terms in the loss and
reduce quadrature errors

[
86
,
15
,
84
,
85
,
89
]
.
While these techniques can mitigate some failure modes, they do not fully
resolve the underlying ill-conditioning introduced by discretizing a PDE
operator. Only recently, more principled second-order (or curvature-based) optimization algorithms
have begun to emerge

[
64
,
65
,
23
,
91
,
22
,
73
,
43
,
49
,
48
]
,
often derived from preconditioning the underlying infinite-dimensional PDE
operator

[
23
,
65
,
64
,
43
]
.
Among them, Natural Gradient Descent (NGD) has emerged as a particularly
promising and unifying framework, as several earlier approaches can be
interpreted as NGD with different choices of the optimization metric

[
68
,
65
]
.

NGD has demonstrated remarkable performance, achieving high accuracy in few
iterations by using a search direction of the form
\mathbf{d}=-(\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\mu\mathbf{I})^{-1}\nabla L(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
denotes the neural network
parameters,
L
is the loss function,
\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
the positive semidefinite
Gramian matrix (see
Section

3.1
), and
\mu>0
a regularization parameter.
However, solving linear systems with
(\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\mu\mathbf{I})
via direct
methods incurs a cubic cost
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
in the number of network parameters,
making NGD impractical for large-scale networks. To alleviate this, matrix-free
strategies have been proposed

[
49
,
91
]
, where the system is solved
approximately using the conjugate gradient (CG) method using only matrix-vector
products (matvecs) with
\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, without assembling the matrix. In
practice, however,
\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is often ill-conditioned, as shown in

Figure

1
, leading to slow CG convergence and motivating the need
for preconditioning. In this work, we address this issue using tools from
Randomized Numerical Linear Algebra (RandNLA)

[
63
,
66
]
.

1.1.
Contributions

The core contributions of this work are as follows:

(1)

We provide a general
methodology for efficiently computing matrix-vector products with the Gramian
using automatic differentiation, generalizing beyond the specific metrics
considered in prior works

[
65
,
49
,
91
]
.

(2)

We propose employing techniques from RandNLA
to construct a preconditioner for
(\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\mu\mathbf{I})
. In particular,
we focus on the randomized Nyström approximation

[
35
,
81
]
and the Randomly Pivoted
Partial Cholesky (RPCholesky) decomposition

[
18
,
32
]
. By relying on a low-rank
approximation of the Gramian, these preconditioners leverage the empirically
observed strong spectral decay (see
Figure

1
), turning it from a computational challenge into
an asset.

(3)

We present a modular and scalable framework for efficient NGD
that decouples the underlying NGD implementation (whether full or matrix-free)
from the preconditioner construction, enabling flexible algorithmic
combinations.

Consequently, we introduce two novel NGD variants for optimizing neural
network-based PDE solvers, termed
NyströmNGD
and
RPCholNGD
.
These methods combine matrix-free NGD with randomized Nyström and RPCholesky
preconditioning, respectively. Finally, we present extensive numerical
experiments demonstrating that our proposed methods achieve significant
acceleration compared to existing NGD-based approaches, which serve as our
primary baseline, while also exhibiting competitive performance against other
state-of-the-art optimizers.

1.2.
Related work and comparison with existing optimization methods

Compared to full-memory quasi-Newton methods such as BFGS and SSBroyden

[
88
,
83
,
55
]
, our
approach differs fundamentally both conceptually and computationally.
Conceptually, NGD is rooted in a first-optimize-then-discretize paradigm: one
chooses a metric in function space, possibly tailored to the underlying PDE, and
then pulls it back to parameter space through the neural network ansatz.
Consequently, the Gramian need not coincide with an approximation of the Hessian
of the parameter-space loss. In contrast, quasi-Newton methods operate directly
in parameter space and iteratively approximate the Hessian (or inverse Hessian)
of
L(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, thereby losing the functional interpretation associated with
the underlying infinite-dimensional problem. Computationally, NyströmNGD and
RPCholNGD combine matrix-free Gramian matrix-vector products (matvecs) with
rank-
\ell
preconditioners. This yields a tunable memory footprint of order

\mathcal{O}(p\ell)
, where
p
is the number of neural network parameters, in stark
contrast to the
\mathcal{O}(p^{2})
storage required by full-memory quasi-Newton
methods. This low-rank and matrix-free structure also distinguishes our methods
from standard NGD implementations that rely on explicitly assembling or
factorizing the full Gramian.

While matrix-free NGD implementations have been explored for specific metrics

[
49
,
91
]
, none of these prior works
address the inherent ill-conditioning of the Gramian, which severely hampers CG
convergence in practice. To overcome this bottleneck, our work bridges NGD with
RandNLA, whose use in Machine Learning is well-established

[
34
,
27
,
82
]
.
Randomized Nyström preconditioning
[
35
]
has been
applied to PINNs in
[
73
]
, but only as a final stage
after Adam and L-BFGS, applying the Nyström approximation, designed for
symmetric positive semidefinite matrices, to the Hessian, which may be
indefinite. Randomized techniques have been applied to NGD

[
12
,
40
]
in the so-called sketch-and-solve paradigm,
whereas we employ them to construct a preconditioner. Alternative notions of preconditioning, where the
preconditioner is embedded into the loss function, are explored in

[
59
,
8
]
. Kronecker-Factored
Approximate Curvature (K-FAC) has also
been used to approximate the Gramian

[
22
]
, but not as a preconditioner, and it
requires problem-specific adaptations.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

Hilbert and Banach spaces are indicated using roman capital letters. Linear
operators are denoted with calligraphic letters. Vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are in
boldface, and matrices use boldface capital letters. The Euclidean gradient of a
function
L:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is denoted by
\nabla L
.

Let
\mathrm{V}
be a real Hilbert space. Its inner product and norm are denoted by

\left({\cdot},\,{\cdot}\right)_{\mathrm{V}}
and
\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{\mathrm{V}}
. Its (topological) dual
is indicated
\mathrm{V}^{\prime}
, and the duality pairing by

\left\langle{\cdot},\,{\cdot}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{V}^{\prime}\times\mathrm{V}}
. The space of bounded linear operators from

\mathrm{V}
to another Hilbert space
\mathrm{W}
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{V},\mathrm{W})
. We say
that
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{V},\mathrm{V}^{\prime})
is symmetric positive semidefinite
(SPSD) if the associated bilinear form
a(u,v)=\left\langle{\mathcal{A}u},\,{v}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{V}^{\prime}\times\mathrm{V}}
is
symmetric and satisfies
a(u,u)\geq 0
for all
u\in\mathrm{V}
. It is symmetric
positive definite (SPD) if
a
is also coercive, i.e., if
\exists\,\alpha>0

such that
a(u,u)\geq\alpha\left\|{u}\right\|_{\mathrm{V}}^{2}
for all
u\in\mathrm{V}
.

2.2.
Abstract Setup

Let
\mathrm{V}
be a Hilbert space equipped with the inner product

\left({\cdot},\,{\cdot}\right)_{\mathrm{V}}
. We endow
\mathrm{V}
with a potentially
point-dependent metric
g_{u}\left({\cdot},\,{\cdot}\right)
, giving
\mathrm{V}
the
structure of a Riemannian manifold. Specifically, the map
u\mapsto g_{u}
is
assumed to be smooth from
\mathrm{V}
to
\mathcal{L}^{2}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\mathrm{V},\mathbb{R})
, the
space of continuous, symmetric, bilinear forms on
\mathrm{V}
. We denote
\left\|{w}\right\|_{g_{u}}=\sqrt{g_{u}\left({w},\,{w}\right)}
. Representative examples of these spaces and
metrics are provided in
Remark

3.3
. We consider a general
minimization problem of the form

(1)

\inf_{u\in\mathrm{V}}E(u),

where
E:\mathrm{V}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function (with respect to the Riemannian metric

g
), referred to as the
energy
. The Riemannian gradient and Hessian of

E
(in the metric
g
) are denoted by
\mathrm{grad}^{g}E
and
\mathrm{Hess}^{g}E
, respectively.

To approximate the solution to (
1
), we introduce a parametrized ansatz

\mathcal{N}=\left\{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\coloneqq\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,:\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta\right\}\subseteq\mathrm{V},

where
\rho:\Theta\to\mathrm{V}
is a smooth map and
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
is open.
We then consider the finite-dimensional optimization problem

(2)

\inf_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}L(\boldsymbol{\theta})\coloneqq\inf_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}E(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\inf_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}(E\circ\rho)(\boldsymbol{\theta}).

Here,
u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
could be a neural network with parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
,
though the framework applies more generally. We will refer to

\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
as the
parameter space
, and to
\mathrm{V}
as the

function space
. Note that while the original optimization problem
(
1
) may be infinite dimensional, the ansatz is
parametrized by a finite-dimensional
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

2.2.1.
Discretization of PDEs

The focus of this article is on problems arising from the discretization of
PDEs. In this case,
\mathrm{V}
is a Hilbert space of functions defined on a domain

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, e.g.,
\mathrm{V}=\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
, and
E
is derived from
the strong or weak formulation of a PDE on
\Omega
. This
abstract setting (
2
) encompasses, for
instance, the Deep Ritz method
[
31
]
, PINNs

[
72
,
30
]
,
Variational PINNs (VPINNs)
[
51
]
, and other PINN
variants

[
52
,
8
,
42
,
25
,
75
]
.

As a general framework for PDEs we consider the following. Let
\mathrm{V}
be a
Hilbert space and let
\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}
be Banach spaces. We seek
u\in\mathrm{V}
such that

(3)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{F}(u)=f,&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\mathcal{B}(u)=g,&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\mathcal{F}:\mathrm{V}\to\mathrm{X}
is a differential operator,
f\in\mathrm{X}
is the source
term,
\mathcal{B}:\mathrm{V}\to\mathrm{Y}
is a boundary operator, and
g\in\mathrm{Y}
is the boundary
data. The energy can then be defined as

E(u)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\mathcal{F}(u)-f}\right\|_{\mathrm{X}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\mathcal{B}(u)-g}\right\|_{\mathrm{Y}}^{2},

where possibly weighting factors can be included, and
data misfit terms can be added.

The choice of function spaces and well-posedness depend on the specific PDE and
on whether a strong or weak formulation is used. For example, consider the Poisson
equation with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions:

(4)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=f,&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u=g,&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

For a strong formulation, one can employ the spaces
\mathrm{V}=\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega)
,
\mathrm{X}=\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, and
\mathrm{Y}=\mathrm{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega)
, with
energy

E(u)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\Delta u+f}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{u-g}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}.

With weak formulations, boundary conditions can be imposed either weakly (with
\mathrm{Y}=\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
) or strongly.
We adopt the latter approach, for which the natural spaces are
\mathrm{V}=\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
,
\mathrm{X}=\left(\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\right)^{\prime}=\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)
.
Given a lifting
\tilde{g}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
of the Dirichlet data, the solution can be written as
u=u_{0}+\tilde{g}
, where the energy is

E(u_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\mathcal{A}u_{0}-\tilde{f}}\right\|_{\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2},\qquad\text{with}\qquad\left\langle{\mathcal{A}u},\,{v}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{H}^{-1}\times\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}}=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v,\quad\tilde{f}=f-\mathcal{A}\tilde{g}.

2.3.
Neural Networks

Neural networks (NNs) serve as the primary parametrized ansatz in this work. For
simplicity, we focus on feedforward networks, though our methods are
architecture-agnostic and apply to other network types as well. A neural network
with input dimension
d
and output
dimension
d^{\prime}
is a function
u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathcal{NN}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
defined as the composition of
affine and nonlinear transformations. Let
L
be the number of hidden layers,
let
n_{0}=d,n_{L+1}=d^{\prime}
, and
n_{1},\dots,n_{L}
be the number of neurons in
each hidden layer, and let
\sigma
be a nonlinear activation function. The
mapping induced by the NN
is defined as:

\begin{split}\mathbf{x}_{0}&=\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
\mathbf{x}_{\ell}&=\sigma(\mathbf{W}_{\ell}\mathbf{x}_{\ell-1}+\mathbf{b}_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}},\qquad\text{for }\ell=1,\dots,L,\\
u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathcal{NN}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}_{L+1}&=\mathbf{W}_{L+1}\mathbf{x}_{L}+\mathbf{b}_{L+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}},\end{split}

where
\mathbf{W}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}\times n_{\ell-1}}
and
\mathbf{b}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}}
are the weights and biases of layer
\ell
, respectively. The
parameters of the network are
\boldsymbol{\theta}=[\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{W}_{1});\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{b}_{1});\dots;\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{W}_{L+1});\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{b}_{L+1})]\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, stacked into a single vector, with
p=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L+1}(n_{\ell}n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell})
. No activation function is applied
in the last layer.

2.3.1.
Finite Element Interpolated Neural Networks (FEINNs)

While standard NNs are typically used with collocation-based methods like PINNs,
in order to deal with weak formulations of PDEs,
we consider Finite Element
Interpolated Neural Networks (FEINNs)

[
8
,
7
,
11
]
, where a
neural network is interpolated onto a finite element (FE) space.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Lipschitz polygonal/polyhedral domain with a conforming mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, and let
\mathrm{V}_{h}\subset\mathrm{V}=\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
be the
FE space with
\mathrm{V}_{h,0}=\mathrm{V}_{h}\cap\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Denote the Lagrangian
basis of
\mathrm{V}_{h}
by
\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where
\varphi_{i}
is
associated to node

\mathbf{x}_{i}
, and let
\Pi_{h}:C^{0}(\Omega)\to\mathrm{V}_{h,0}
be the FE interpolation
operator obtained by evaluation at interior nodes. Given a discrete lifting

\bar{u}_{h}
of Dirichlet data, the FEINN is defined as

u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\bar{u}_{h}+\Pi_{h}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathcal{NN}}=\bar{u}_{h}+\sum_{i\,:\,\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathring{\Omega}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathcal{NN}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\varphi_{i}.

For simplicity, we assumed that Dirichlet boundary conditions are imposed on the
whole boundary, but mixed boundary conditions can also be accommodated in the
framework. Further details are provided in

[
8
,
7
]
.

This approach offers several advantages. First, Dirichlet boundary conditions
can be enforced by applying them to the FE interpolant using
standard FE techniques, avoiding the need to impose them directly on the neural
network output
[
11
,
10
]
. Second,
it alleviates integration challenges, a known issue in neural network-based PDE
solvers
[
74
]
, especially in the Deep Ritz method

[
80
]
. Finally, spatial derivatives can be computed at
the FE level, eliminating the need for automatic differentiation with respect to
the input of the neural network.

3.
Matrix-free Natural Gradient Descent

3.1.
Natural Gradient Descent

The Natural Gradient Descent (NGD) method

[
3
,
62
,
68
,
65
]

targets optimization problems of the form (
2
) following the
first-optimize-then-discretize paradigm. Within the functional setting
established in
Section

2.2
, NGD moves along the steepest descent
direction according to the metric
g
on
\mathrm{V}
, the “natural” one for the
function
u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, and pulls it back to the parameter space.

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}
be the current iterate. The steepest descent direction in function
space is
d=-\mathrm{grad}^{g}E(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, the Riemannian gradient of
E
in
the metric
g
, and the corresponding update

u\leftarrow u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\alpha\mathrm{grad}^{g}E(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}).

In NGD, the parameter update direction is
-\mathbf{d}
, where the
natural
gradient

\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is such that the first-order expansion

u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}-\alpha\mathbf{d}}\approx u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\alpha\mathrm{D}\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})[\mathbf{d}]=u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{p}d_{j}\cdot\partial_{\theta_{j}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},

best approximates (in the metric
g
) the update in function space, i.e.,

\mathbf{d}=\argmin_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\|{\mathrm{grad}^{g}E(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\mathrm{D}\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})[\mathbf{w}]}\right\|_{g_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}.

This corresponds to the
g_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
-orthogonal projection of

\mathrm{grad}^{g}E(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
onto the model’s
generalized tangent space

\mathrm{T}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{N}=\mathrm{Span}\left(\left\{\partial_{\theta_{1}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\dots,\partial_{\theta_{p}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\}\right)\subset\mathrm{V}.

Specifically, the projection
v=\sum_{i=1}^{p}d_{i}\partial_{\theta_{i}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
satisfies

g_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\left({\sum_{j=1}^{p}d_{j}\partial_{\theta_{j}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},\,{\partial_{\theta_{i}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)=g_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\left({\mathrm{grad}^{g}E(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})},\,{\partial_{\theta_{i}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)=\mathrm{D}E(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})[\partial_{\theta_{i}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]=\partial_{\theta_{i}}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

or, in compact form,

(5)

\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{d}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\qquad\left[\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]_{ij}=g_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\left({\partial_{\theta_{i}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},\,{\partial_{\theta_{j}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right),

where
\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is often referred to as the Gramian

[
90
,
65
]
or (Fisher) information matrix

[
3
,
62
,
68
]
.
If

\partial_{\theta_{1}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\dots,\partial_{\theta_{p}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

are not linearly independent, the minimizer is not unique, and the
minimum-norm solution is selected, namely,

(6)

\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\dagger}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

where
\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\dagger}
denotes the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of

\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.
As observed in the literature

[
90
,
14
]
, it is often beneficial
to add a damping term to improve numerical stability and convergence, yielding
the regularized direction

(7)

\mathbf{d}=\left(\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\mu\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

where
\mu>0
is a regularization parameter, typically adapted during the
optimization process. Once the (regularized) natural gradient descent is
computed, the parameters are updated as

\boldsymbol{\theta}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}-\alpha\mathbf{d}=\boldsymbol{\theta}-\alpha(\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\mu\mathbf{I})^{-1}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

where the step-size
\alpha
is typically chosen via a line-search procedure.

3.2.
A matrix-free formulation

Leaving aside the resolution of (
7
), even assembling the
Gramian
\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
in (
5
) can be computationally
prohibitive in both floating-point operations (flops) and memory. Indeed,
\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is a
p\times p

matrix, where
p
is the number of network parameters,
which can be on the order of tens of thousands even for relatively small
neural networks. To mitigate this issue, we consider a matrix-free approach:

\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is never explicitly assembled, and only
matrix-vector products are computed as needed. This is particularly efficient when
solving linear systems with
(\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\mu\mathbf{I})
using iterative methods
such as Conjugate Gradient (CG), which require only matvecs.

Prior work has demonstrated that matrix-vector products with the Gramian
(
5
) can be computed via automatic differentiation for
specific metrics
[
49
,
65
,
91
]
.
Here, we show that autodiff-based matvecs extend to broader metric classes.
Specifically, we show that matrix-vector products with the Gramian
(
5
) can be computed via automatic differentiation,
provided the metric satisfies the following assumption and it is discretized via
quadrature.

Assumption 3.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a domain, and let
\mathrm{V}
be a Hilbert space of functions on
\Omega
. Assume that the metric

g_{u}
takes the form

(8)

g_{u}\left({v},\,{w}\right)=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{F}_{u}v(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathcal{F}_{u}w(\mathbf{x})\,\varphi_{u}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

for all
v,w\in\mathrm{T}_{u}\mathrm{V}\subseteq\mathrm{V}
, where
\mathcal{F}_{u}:\mathrm{V}\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;\varphi_{u})^{k}
is linear and continuous, and
\varphi_{u}
is a positive function.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
Assumption

3.1
hold, and let
\mathtt{sg}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
denote
the stop-gradient operator, formally defined by
\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
\nabla\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbf{0}
. If the integral
(
8
) is approximated using a quadrature rule with
points
\{\mathbf{x}_{r}\}_{r=1}^{q}
and weights
\{w_{r}\}_{r=1}^{q}
, then

(9)

\begin{split}\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})&=\sum_{r=1}^{q}w_{r}\,\varphi_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x}_{r})\,\left(\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{r})\right)^{\top}\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{r})\\
&=\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}\varphi_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\mathbf{I}_{k}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&w_{q}\varphi_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x}_{q})\mathbf{I}_{k}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\quad\,\,\,\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{q})\end{bmatrix},\end{split}

where
\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
denotes the Jacobian with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{e}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
denote the
h
-th vector of the canonical basis, i.e.,
the vector that has a
1
in the
h
-th entry and
0
in all other entries.
Using the linearity and continuity of
\mathcal{F}_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
,

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\partial_{\theta_{h}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}&=\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}\partial_{\theta_{h}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}\left(\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}+t\mathbf{e}_{h}}-u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{t}\right)\\
&=\lim_{t\to 0}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}\left(\frac{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}+t\mathbf{e}_{h}}-u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{t}\right)=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}+t\mathbf{e}_{h}}-\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{t}\\
&=\partial_{\theta_{h}}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}.\end{split}

Note that the use of the stop-gradient operator is crucial to ensure that we
do not differentiate
\mathcal{F}_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. Hence,

\begin{split}[\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]_{ij}&=g_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\left({\partial_{\theta_{i}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},\,{\partial_{\theta_{j}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\partial_{\theta_{i}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\cdot\mathcal{F}_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\partial_{\theta_{j}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\,\varphi_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{\theta_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\cdot\partial_{\theta_{j}}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,\varphi_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},\end{split}

or, in matrix form,

\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\left(\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)^{*}\,\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},

where the Hilbert adjoint
\left(\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)^{*}
is taken with respect
to the inner product on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\varphi_{u})^{k}
, and we identify a
matrix with the induced linear map.

Applying a quadrature formula with nodes
\{\mathbf{x}_{r}\}_{r=1}^{q}
and weights

\{w_{r}\}_{r=1}^{q}
, we get

\begin{split}[\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]_{ij}&=\sum_{r=1}^{q}w_{r}\,\partial_{\theta_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{r})\cdot\partial_{\theta_{j}}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{r})\,\varphi_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x}_{r})\end{split}

and thus, in matrix form:

\begin{split}\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})&=\sum_{r=1}^{q}w_{r}\,\varphi_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x}_{r})\,\left(\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{r})\right)^{\top}\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{r})\\
&=\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}\varphi_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\mathbf{I}_{k}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&w_{q}\varphi_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x}_{q})\mathbf{I}_{k}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta}).\end{split}

∎

As a consequence of
Theorem

3.2
, matrix-vector products with

\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
can be computed using only products with the Jacobian and the
transpose Jacobian of
\mathbf{F}
. These operations can be efficiently implemented via
automatic differentiation, using forward-mode autodiff for Jacobian-vector
products (JVPs) and backward-mode autodiff for vector-Jacobian products (VJPs).
Note that most autodiff frameworks include a stop-gradient function.

Remark 3.3
.

Assumption

3.1
encompasses standard metrics used in PDE-related
function spaces. For instance, the
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
and
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)

metrics correspond to the choices
\mathcal{F}_{u}v=v
and
\mathcal{F}_{u}v=(\nabla v,v)
, respectively. It also includes the Gauss–Newton metric on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, for which matrix-free NGD implementations have been
proposed in
[

,
65

for specific

the metrics relevant in the context of PDEs, since these typically need to

3.2.1.
A link with Operator Preconditioning and the case of Finite Element Interpolated Neural Networks

The Gramian (
5
) can be interpreted in the context of
operator preconditioning

[
47
,
60
,
54
,
38
]
.
Specifically, NGD can be seen as a preconditioned gradient descent method, with
the Gramian serving as the preconditioner
[
23
]
. For a

linear
parametrized model
u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\theta_{i}\,\varphi_{i}
, the Gramian reduces to
\left[\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]_{ij}=g_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\left({\varphi_{i}},\,{\varphi_{j}}\right)
. If
(\mathrm{V},\left({\cdot},\,{\cdot}\right)_{\mathrm{V}})
is a Hilbert space and
g
is induced by a positive
definite linear operator
\mathcal{G}:\mathrm{V}\to\mathrm{V}^{\prime}
, then

[\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]_{ij}=[\mathbf{G}]_{ij}=\left\langle{\mathcal{G}\varphi_{i}},\,{\varphi_{j}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{V}^{\prime}\times\mathrm{V}},

which is independent of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. This coincides exactly with preconditioners
arising from operator preconditioning in Galerkin discretizations of PDEs

[
47
]
.

The connection with operator preconditioning highlights that the choice of
metric crucially influences the convergence speed of NGD. In general, the link
between NGD and Riemannian gradient descent
[
64
]

suggests selecting the metric
g
to reduce the condition number of the
Riemannian Hessian. Further details are provided in
Appendix

D
, where
we present a unified framework for metric selection.

In the case of FEINNs,
Theorem

3.2
can be specialized to connect with
operator preconditioning for finite elements. Assume, for simplicity, that the
interior nodes of the mesh are the first
N_{I}
,
so that the FEINN can be written as
u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\bar{u}_{h}+\sum_{i=1}^{N_{I}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathcal{NN}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\varphi_{i}
. Then the Gramian (
5
) takes the form

(10)

\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\left(\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbf{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{h}\left(\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbf{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right),\qquad\text{where}\qquad\mathbf{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\begin{bmatrix}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathcal{NN}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\\
\vdots\\
u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathcal{NN}}(\mathbf{x}_{N_{I}})\end{bmatrix},

where
\mathbf{G}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{I}\times N_{I}}
with
[\mathbf{G}_{h}]_{ij}=\left\langle{\mathcal{G}\varphi_{i}},\,{\varphi_{j}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{V}^{\prime}\times\mathrm{V}}
is the FEM operator preconditioner. Note that
computing matvecs with the Gramian requires only matvecs with
\mathbf{G}_{h}
,
but not with its inverse, and JVPs and VJPs with
\mathbf{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, which
is an evaluation of the neural network at the interior nodes of the
mesh.

4.
A modular and scalable framework for Natural Gradient Descent

In this section, we present a framework for efficiently computing the Natural
Gradient Descent (NGD) direction. The framework is inherently modular: we break
the computation down into subtasks and propose several efficient methods for
each, allowing the optimal choice to be tailored to the specific problem at
hand. The computational cost estimates and the formulation of NGD in this
section are based on the following assumption.

Assumption 4.1
.

We consider the regime where a highly accurate quadrature rule is employed and the number of quadrature points
q
exceeds the number of neural network parameters
p
.

When
q\ll p
(for instance, if mini-batching is used at each
NGD iteration), techniques based on the Woodbury matrix identity can be employed
to reduce the computational cost
[
40
]
.

To compute the natural gradient
\mathbf{d}=\left(\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\mu\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}L(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, a primary distinction lies in how
the linear system associated with the matrix
\left(\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\mu\mathbf{I}\right)
is solved: directly or iteratively. When using a direct solver
approach, the required tasks at each step are:

(1)

Assemble the full Gramian
\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
;

(2)

Compute the regularization parameter
\mu>0
;

(3)

Solve the regularized linear system
\left(\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\mu\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{d}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}L(\boldsymbol{\theta})
using a direct
solver. Because the regularized Gramian is symmetric positive
definite and dense, the preferred direct solver is the Cholesky
decomposition followed by triangular solves.

Alternatively, when employing an iterative approach, the solver of choice is
the preconditioned Conjugate Gradient (pCG) method. The corresponding steps are:

(1)

Define a matrix-vector product function
\mathbf{v}\mapsto\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\mathbf{v}
;

(2)

Compute the regularization parameter
\mu>0
;

(3)

Define a suitable preconditioner (if any);

(4)

Solve the regularized linear system
\left(\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\mu\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{d}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}L(\boldsymbol{\theta})
using pCG.

Figure

appropriate based on the specific problem and available computational

4.1.
Assembly of the Gramian and matvecs

For simplicity, we present the case where
k=1
, i.e.,

\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
is a scalar. The vector
case follows in a completely analogous manner by summing the contributions of
the
k
components.

In the subsequent complexity analyses, we let
[FP]
denote the flops cost of a single forward pass

\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
. We recall that when using
automatic differentiation (AD), the computational cost of each
Jacobian-Vector-Product (JVP) and Vector-Jacobian-Product (VJP) is a small
constant multiple of the forward pass cost
[
37
,
61
]
.
However, the memory requirements differ between reverse-mode AD (VJP) and
forward-mode AD (JVP). For a feedforward neural network, the cost of a forward
pass scales as
\mathcal{O}(p)
, linearly with the number of network parameters. Note,
however, that when executed on modern GPUs, batch evaluation across
q
points
benefits significantly from parallelization.

4.1.1.
Computing the full Gramian

Evaluating the Gramian as in (
9
) requires computing

\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{q})^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}\quad\text{where}\quad\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\text{ for all }i=1,\dots,q.

Using reverse-mode AD, this operation requires
\mathcal{O}(q\texttt{[FP]})
flops

[
67
]
, which is essentially equivalent to the cost of computing
the loss gradient
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}L(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. Subsequently, the tensor
contraction to form the Gramian via (
9
) requires

\mathcal{O}(p^{2}q)
flops. The memory footprint is approximately
\mathcal{O}(pq)
,
dominated by the storage of the full Jacobian. If this exceeds available memory,
the quadrature points can be partitioned into
N_{B}
batches of size
B
. The
Jacobian and the contraction can then be computed
B
rows at a time, as
illustrated in
Figure

3
. More formally, assuming
q=B\cdot N_{B}
, we rewrite (
9
) as

(11)

\begin{split}&\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{b=1}^{N_{B}}\left(\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbf{F}_{b}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}w_{(b-1)B+1}\varphi_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x}_{(b-1)B+1})&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&w_{bB}\varphi_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x}_{bB})\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbf{F}_{b}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\\
&\mathbf{F}_{b}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{(b-1)B+1})\\
\vdots\\
\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{bB})\end{bmatrix},\end{split}

and compute and add the contribution of each batch sequentially. This approach
restricts the memory requirement to
\mathcal{O}(p^{2}+pB)
, while the overall FLOP
count remains largely unchanged, though the degree of parallelization is
constrained by the batch size.

4.1.2.
Matvecs with the Gramian

We now discuss strategies for computing matrix-vector products (matvecs) with the
Gramian
\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. We evaluate these strategies based on three metrics:
setup cost (precomputing the auxiliary quantities required to define the
mapping
\mathbf{v}\mapsto\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{v}
), matrix-matrix product (MMP) cost
(evaluating
\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{V}
for
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times\ell}
), and memory footprint.
Note that the cost of a single matvec is simply the MMP cost evaluated at
\ell=1
.

Precompute the Gramian

The first strategy simply consists of assembling the full Gramian as detailed in

Section

4.1.1
and subsequently performing MMPs directly. The setup
cost is
\mathcal{O}(q\texttt{[FP]}+p^{2}q)
, while each MMP requires
\mathcal{O}(p^{2}\ell)
flops. By
processing the quadrature points in batches of size
B
, the memory cost can be
bounded by
\mathcal{O}(p^{2}+pB)
.

Precompute the Jacobian only

A second strategy computes the Jacobian
\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
but avoids
the explicit contraction required to assemble the Gramian. Instead, MMPs are
evaluated in a matrix-free fashion according to (
9
): one
sequentially multiplies by
\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, the diagonal matrix

\mathrm{diag}\left(w_{1}\varphi_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\dots,w_{q}\varphi_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x}_{q})\right)
, and finally
\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}
.
The setup requires computing
\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
at a cost of

\mathcal{O}(q\texttt{[FP]})
, and each MMP costs
\mathcal{O}(pq\ell)
. However, memory usage cannot
be reduced below
\mathcal{O}(pq)
because the entire Jacobian must be stored.

Batch-wise Jacobian recomputation on the fly

A third strategy avoids precomputation entirely by evaluating the Jacobian
batch-wise and on the fly during each MMP computation. Following
(
11
), for each batch
b=1,\dots,N_{B}
, we evaluate
the partial Jacobian
\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbf{F}_{b}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, compute its matrix-free
contribution to the MMP, and accumulate the result sequentially. This approach
is mathematically equivalent to performing Vector-Jacobian Products (VJPs) and
Jacobian-Vector Products (JVPs) on the batched maps

\boldsymbol{\theta}\mapsto\mathbf{F}_{b}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{B}
. The setup cost is strictly negligible
since no precomputation occurs. While each MMP requires

\mathcal{O}(q\texttt{[FP]}+pq\ell)
FLOPs, the memory footprint is substantially reduced to

\mathcal{O}(pB)
.

. If memory is not a bottleneck and the number of
simultaneous matvecs
\ell

p
(which is
typically the case), precomputing only the Jacobian is the most efficient choice
in terms of combined setup and MMP costs. Conversely, if storing the full
Jacobian exceeds available memory, recomputing it batch-wise and on the fly
for each MMP becomes the most viable alternative. For the sake of completeness, we
note that assembling the full Gramian can still be advantageous in the specific
edge case where out-of-memory errors are driven by an exceptionally large number
of quadrature points
q
, provided the parameter count
p
is small enough that
the
\mathcal{O}(p^{2})
storage requirement remains easily feasible.

4.1.3.
Computing the diagonal of the Gramian

Computing the diagonal of the Gramian
\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is often useful. While this
is trivial if the full Gramian has already been assembled, it can also be
computed efficiently without forming the full matrix by evaluating only the
diagonal entries of (
9
). Specifically, it holds that

(12)

\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{q}w_{i}\,\varphi_{u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\,\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{F}_{u_{\mathtt{sg}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\right)^{\odot 2},

where
⊙2
denotes the element-wise square. The accumulation in
(
12
) requires
\mathcal{O}(pq)
flops and can be straightforwardly
executed in batches, thereby reducing the memory footprint to
\mathcal{O}(pB)
.

4.1.4.
The case of Finite Element Interpolated Neural Networks

As described in
Section

2.3.1
, in FEINNs the neural network is interpolated
onto the finite element (FE) space, and integration is performed at the FE
level. Consequently, the Gramian assumes the form given in
(
10
). Therefore, in the computational cost analyses
presented above for assembling the Gramian and performing associated matvecs,
the number of quadrature points
q
is replaced by the number of interior mesh
nodes
N_{I}
, and
[FP]
denotes the cost of a single forward pass
u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathcal{NN}}(\mathbf{x})
.
Furthermore, we must account for the cost of multiplying by the FEM operator

\mathbf{G}_{h}
. Letting
\mathtt{mv}_{h}=\mathtt{mv}_{h}(N_{I})
denote the cost of a matvec with
\mathbf{G}_{h}
,
the additional cost to compute the full Gramian is
\mathcal{O}(p\,\mathtt{mv}_{h})
. For each
matrix-free MMP when only the full Jacobian is precomputed, the additional cost
is
\mathcal{O}(\ell\,\mathtt{mv}_{h})
.

Regarding batch processing, the FEM operator
\mathbf{G}_{h}
is typically very sparse
but not diagonal. Consequently, batching cannot be performed as trivially as
before, since the interactions between different rows must be addressed. Let

\{I_{b}\}_{b=1}^{N_{B}}
represent the index sets for the batches of interior
nodes. For each batch
b=1,\dots,N_{B}
, we identify the set of interacting
indices,
J_{b}=\{j:[\mathbf{G}_{h}]_{ij}\neq 0\text{ for some }i\in I_{b}\}
, and
sequentially accumulate the batch contributions as follows:

(13)

\begin{split}&\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{b=1}^{N_{B}}\left(\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbf{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta})[I_{b},:]\right)^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{h}[I_{b},J_{b}]\left(\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbf{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta})[J_{b},:]\right),\\
&\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbf{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta})[I,:]=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathcal{NN}}(\mathbf{x}_{i_{1}})^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathcal{NN}}(\mathbf{x}_{i_{|I|}})^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{|I|\times p},\qquad I=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{|I|}\}.\end{split}

To minimize redundant row computations in the Jacobian, it is advantageous to
group spatially proximate interior nodes into the same batch, ensuring that the
set of interacting indices
J_{b}
remains comparable in size to
I_{b}
. By
defining
B=\max_{b}|J_{b}|
, the memory footprint is constrained to
\mathcal{O}(pB)
.
The overall FLOP count is more complex to analyze, as it depends heavily on the
sparsity pattern of
\mathbf{G}_{h}
and the specific batching strategy; however, it is
expected to be on the order of
\mathcal{O}(N_{I}\texttt{[FP]}+pN_{I}\ell+\ell\,\mathtt{mv}_{h})
for MMPs,
and
\mathcal{O}(p\,\mathtt{mv}_{h})
for computing the Gramian diagonal.

4.2.
Preconditioning matrix-free Natural Gradient Descent

When iteratively solving the regularized linear system
\left(\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\mu\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{d}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}L(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, the Conjugate Gradient (CG) method
is typically employed. In practice, however,
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is often
ill-conditioned, leading to slow convergence unless a suitable preconditioner is
applied. Furthermore,
\mathbf{G}
lacks the sparsity or grid-based structure typical
of FEM and FD matrices, which are essential for classical preconditioners such
as Incomplete Cholesky, multigrid/multilevel methods, and domain decomposition
techniques. Instead, the main characteristics of
\mathbf{G}
are that it is symmetric
positive semidefinite, it typically exhibits rapid singular value decay (see

Figure

1
), and it allows for efficient matvecs and efficient
access to its diagonal. Given these properties, Randomized Nyström

[
81
,
35
]
and Randomly Pivoted
Partial Cholesky (RPCholesky)

[
18
,
33
,
32
,
79
]

represent, to the best of our knowledge, the most effective methods currently
available for constructing a preconditioner. In this section, we review both
Randomized Nyström preconditioning and RPCholesky, explaining how to
integrate them into our framework to accelerate matrix-free NGD.

4.2.1.
Randomized Nyström

Let
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
be a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix.
The
randomized
Nyström approximation
[
63
, Section
14]
with a random test matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times\ell}
is defined as

(14)

\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathrm{nys}}\coloneqq(\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{\Omega})\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\top}\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right)^{\dagger}(\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{\Omega})^{\top}.

The randomness in
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
ensures that
\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathrm{nys}}
approximates

\mathbf{G}
well with high probability
[
63
, Section
14.4]
. In this work, we choose
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
to be
either a standard normal matrix (i.e., with i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries) or
a sampling matrix
[
2
,
6
]
(i.e., with columns
sampled uniformly at random from the standard basis). While other choices for

\boldsymbol{\Omega}
are possible, such as structured random matrices

[
5
,
63
,
66
,
41
]
,
they are beyond the scope of this study.
For a standard Gaussian test matrix, the randomized Nyström approximation
admits strong theoretical guarantees

[
63
, Theorem 14.1]
. In particular, for any target
rank
k<\ell-1
, it provides a good low-rank approximation of
\mathbf{G}
in
expectation, with error close to the optimal rank-
k
error up to an additive
term depending on the eigenvalue tail, which decays as the sketch size

\ell
increases. Consequently, the Nyström approximation is especially
effective when
\mathbf{G}
exhibits strong spectral decay, as is the case in our
setting.

We emphasize that the formula (
14
) is
numerically unstable and should not be used directly. A more robust and
efficient implementation, which also returns the eigendecomposition of

\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathrm{nys}}
, is provided in
[
63
, Algorithm
16]
and reported in

Section

A.1
.

Randomized Nyström preconditioning

Given an eigendecomposition
\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathrm{nys}}=\mathbf{U}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\mathbf{U}^{\top}
with
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}=\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\lambda}_{1},\dots,\hat{\lambda}_{\ell})
and
\hat{\lambda}_{1}\geq\dots\geq\hat{\lambda}_{\ell}
,
the randomized Nyström preconditioner
[
35
]
for

(\mathbf{G}+\mu\mathbf{I})
with
\mu\geq 0
is constructed as

(15)

\mathbf{P}^{-1}=\mathbf{U}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}+\mu\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{U}^{\top}+\frac{1}{\hat{\lambda}_{\ell}+\mu}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}).

To ensure good performance in expectation, the sampling parameter
\ell
should
be scaled proportionally to the effective dimension
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mu)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{G})/(\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{G})+\mu)
. This metric acts as a
smoothed count of the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{G}
that exceed
\mu

[
35
, Theorem
1.1]
. When
\mathbf{G}
exhibits strong spectral decay,

d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mu)\ll p
, making the randomized Nyström preconditioner
particularly effective
[
35
, Theorem 5.1]
.

Computational complexity

Each application of the randomized Nyström preconditioner costs

\mathcal{O}(p\ell)
flops. Beforehand, one must compute the Nyström approximation
itself. Let
\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{mv}}(\ell)
denote the cost of computing the MMP

\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, which varies depending on the selected strategy from

Section

4.1.2
. The overall cost to construct the randomized
Nyström approximation is
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{mv}}(\ell)+p\ell^{2})
flops,
with a memory requirement of
\mathcal{O}(p\ell)
. This overhead is in addition to the
specific setup costs and memory footprint of the chosen matvec strategy.

4.2.2.
Randomly Pivoted Partial Cholesky (RPCholesky)

Randomly Pivoted Partial Cholesky (RPCholesky)

[
18
,
32
,
33
]
is a randomized
variant of the standard Pivoted Partial Cholesky algorithm

[
46
]
. It computes a rank-
\ell
approximation
\mathbf{G}\approx\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathrm{RPChol}}\coloneqq\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{\top}
, where
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times\ell}
, for a symmetric positive
semidefinite matrix
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
. This is achieved by performing

\ell
steps of the pivoted Cholesky decomposition, where pivots are selected by
sampling proportionally to the diagonal entries of the residual matrix. Starting
with the initial residual
\mathbf{G}^{(0)}=\mathbf{G}
and its diagonal
\mathbf{d}^{(0)}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{G}^{(0)})
, RPCholesky iterates the following steps for
i=1,\dots,\ell
:

(1)

Sample the
i
-th pivot
s_{i}
according to the current diagonal:

s_{i}\sim\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{G}^{(i-1)})=\mathbf{d}^{(i-1)},

where
s\sim\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}
denotes the probability distribution
\mathbb{P}(s=j)=d_{j}/\sum_{k}d_{k}
.

(2)

Extract and rescale the selected pivot column:

\begin{split}\mathbf{f}^{(i)}&=\mathbf{G}^{(i-1)}[:,s_{i}]=\mathbf{G}[:,s_{i}]-\mathbf{F}[:,1:i-1]\mathbf{F}[s_{i},1:i-1]^{\top},\\
\mathbf{F}[:,i]&=\mathbf{f}^{(i)}/\sqrt{\mathbf{f}^{(i)}_{s_{i}}}.\end{split}

(3)

Update the residual matrix and its diagonal:

\begin{split}\mathbf{G}^{(i)}&=\mathbf{G}^{(i-1)}-\mathbf{F}[:,i]\mathbf{F}[:,i]^{\top},\\
\mathbf{d}^{(i)}&=\mathbf{d}^{(i-1)}-\left(\mathbf{F}[:,i]\right)^{\odot 2}.\end{split}

As the update equations demonstrate, the algorithm can be executed without ever
explicitly forming the full residual matrices
\mathbf{G}^{(i)}
. Instead, it
incrementally builds the approximation factor
\mathbf{F}
one column at a time while
updating only the diagonal vector
\mathbf{d}^{(i)}
. A block variant of the algorithm
selects
b>1
pivots simultaneously, performing the same operations on a batch
of
b
columns at once. Detailed pseudocode for both the standard and block
versions is provided in
Algorithms

7
and
8
. Notably, the
approximation error in the trace norm (i.e., the Schatten 1-norm) can be easily
monitored, since

\left\|{\mathbf{G}-\mathbf{F}[:,1:i]\mathbf{F}[:,1:i]^{\top}}\right\|_{*}=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{G}^{(i)})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbf{d}^{(i)}_{j}.

This allows the decomposition to be terminated early once a desired accuracy is
reached.

RPCholesky is mathematically equivalent to the Nyström approximation when
the sampling matrix is constructed from the selected pivots
[
18
, Property
2.1]
. In RPCholesky, however, the indices are sampled
adaptively based on the residual at the current iteration. Trace-norm error bounds show that this adaptive approximation is particularly
effective when
\mathbf{G}
exhibits strong spectral decay, as is the case in our
setting
[
32
, Theorem 4.2]
.

RPCholesky preconditioning

Once the RPCholesky approximation
\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathrm{RPChol}}=\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{\top}

has been constructed, the corresponding RPCholesky preconditioner

[
29
]
for the regularized matrix
(\mathbf{G}+\mu\mathbf{I})
with
\mu>0
is defined as

\mathbf{P}=\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathrm{RPChol}}+\mu\mathbf{I}=\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{\top}+\mu\mathbf{I}.

Note that
\mathbf{P}
can be efficiently inverted by computing a thin singular value
decomposition (SVD)
\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\top}
as

(16)

\mathbf{P}^{-1}=\frac{1}{\mu}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top})+\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}+\mu\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{U}^{\top}.

Alternatively, one can compute the
\ell\times\ell
Cholesky decomposition

\mathbf{F}^{\top}\mathbf{F}+\mu\mathbf{I}_{\ell}=\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}
and use it to efficiently invert

\mathbf{P}
via the Woodbury matrix identity:

(17)

\mathbf{P}^{-1}=\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{I}-\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{R}^{-\top}\mathbf{F}^{\top}.

Although this second alternative is theoretically less numerically stable, we
did not encounter stability issues in our experiments. Because it is
significantly faster on GPUs than the SVD-based approach, we adopt this second
formulation.

The performance of the RPCholesky preconditioner is governed by the

\mu
-tail rank
of
\mathbf{G}
, defined as
\operatorname{rank}_{\mu}(\mathbf{G})\coloneqq\min\left\{r\geq 0:\sum_{i>r}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{G})\leq\mu\right\}
. When
\mathbf{G}
exhibits
rapid spectral decay, we have
\operatorname{rank}_{\mu}(\mathbf{G})\ll p
, making RPCholesky
preconditioning particularly efficient
[
29
, Theorem 2.2]
.

Computational complexity

Each application of the RPCholesky preconditioner requires
\mathcal{O}(p\ell)
flops.
However, one must also account for the initial cost of computing the RPCholesky
approximation. Let
\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{col}}(b)
denote the cost of extracting
b

columns of the matrix
\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. This operation is essentially equivalent
to a matrix-matrix product (MMP) with a
p\times b
matrix, and its cost varies
depending on the strategy selected from
Section

4.1.2
. Furthermore,
let
\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{diag}}
denote the cost of computing the diagonal of
\mathbf{G}
,
which is necessary for pivot selection and can be evaluated efficiently as
detailed in
Section

4.1.3
. The total cost to construct an RPCholesky
approximation of rank
\ell=tb
is thus
\mathcal{O}\left(\ell\cdot\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{col}}(b)/b+\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{diag}}+p\ell^{2}\right)
flops, with a memory footprint of

\mathcal{O}(p\ell)
. This overhead is in addition to the specific setup costs and
memory requirements of the chosen matvec strategy. Notably, block processing
yields speedups both in column extraction (by evaluating MMPs in batches) and
in the execution of the algorithm itself (by casting computations as efficient
matrix-matrix operations).

4.2.3.
A comparison of Randomized Nyström and RPCholesky
preconditioning

Randomized Nyström and RPCholesky are both effective when
\mathbf{G}
has strong
spectral decay, but they differ in how the low-rank approximation is
constructed and controlled. Randomized Nyström builds a global sketch

\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{\Omega}
and, for Gaussian test matrices, enjoys near-optimal low-rank
approximation guarantees in expectation. Moreover, the quality of the resulting
preconditioner is governed by the effective dimension
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mu)
,
which acts as a smoothed count of the eigenvalues above the regularization
threshold
\mu
. RPCholesky instead incrementally selects columns by sampling
from the diagonal of the current residual. It is therefore mathematically
equivalent to a Nyström approximation whose sampling matrix is formed by the
selected pivots, but with the important distinction that these pivots are chosen
adaptively from the residual at each iteration. This makes RPCholesky more
directly controllable: the trace-norm approximation error can be monitored
during the factorization, the rank can be selected adaptively rather than fixed
in advance, and the quality of the preconditioner is governed by the

\mu
-tail rank of
\mathbf{G}
.

For a rank-
\ell
approximation, Nyström requires
\ell
matvecs, whereas
RPCholesky needs access to
\ell
columns of
\mathbf{G}
. Their computational costs
are
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{mv}}(\ell)+p\ell^{2})
for Nyström and

\mathcal{O}\left(\ell\cdot\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{col}}(b)/b+\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{diag}}+p\ell^{2}\right)
for blocked RPCholesky with block size
b
. These
complexity estimates highlight the main practical differences between the two
approaches. First, RPCholesky must compute the diagonal of
\mathbf{G}
to define the
sampling probabilities. Second, and more importantly, RPCholesky accesses the
columns of
\mathbf{G}
sequentially in blocks of size
b
, whereas Nyström forms

\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{\Omega}
by performing the required
\ell
matvecs in a single batched
operation. This makes Nyström easier to parallelize and particularly
advantageous for large-scale problems in which batching is essential, since it
avoids the
\mathcal{O}(q\texttt{[FP]})
overhead of recomputing the Jacobian for each batch in
every MMP; see
Table

1
. RPCholesky, on the other hand, is
preferable when efficient access to the diagonal and to selected columns is
available, and when one wants finer online control of the approximation quality
through adaptive rank selection and residual monitoring.

4.3.
Computing the regularization parameter
\mu

The regularization parameter
\mu>0
is crucial for ensuring numerical
stability and mitigating the slow initial convergence typical of
quasi-second-order methods like NGD. Prior works have proposed heuristics that
adapt
\mu
dynamically

[
14
,
36
]
or decrease it
proportionally with the loss
[
90
]
, allowing early
iterations to mimic gradient descent before gradually shifting to NGD near a
local minimum. We instead propose a heuristic based on the numerical rank of

\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.

Let
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{p}
denote the eigenvalues of the Gramian

\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. We aim at setting
the regularization parameter to an approximation of
\mu\approx\gamma\cdot\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}\cdot\lambda_{1}
, with
\gamma=10
as a reasonable
default. This corresponds to the threshold commonly employed to determine the
numerical rank, i.e., the cut-off below which eigenvalues are considered to be
numerically zero
[
70
]
. Although the exact
largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
is typically not directly available, an estimate

\hat{\lambda}_{1}
is often readily accessible, for instance, from the
Nyström approximation. Alternatively, it can be efficiently computed using a
few iterations of the power method (we found 2 to 4 iterations to be
sufficient). Note that more advanced strategies for the scheduling of
\mu
, based on the
knowledge of all eigenvalues of the Gramian, have recently appeared in the
literature
[
77
]
. The inclusion of those strategies in
the framework considered in this paper is a direction of future work.

Finally, we observed that it can be beneficial to enforce stronger
regularization in early iterations, when the optimization is still far from a
local minimum. This can be achieved using a heuristic of the form
\mu=\max(\gamma\cdot\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}\cdot\hat{\lambda}_{1},c\cdot\left\|{\nabla L(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right\|^{\alpha})
or
\mu=\max(\gamma\cdot\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}\cdot\hat{\lambda}_{1},c\cdot|L(\boldsymbol{\theta})|^{\alpha})
,
where the scaling factor
c
and the exponent
\alpha
are problem-dependent.

4.4.
Putting it all together: Preconditioned Matrix-Free NGD Algorithms

We are finally ready to combine the ingredients described so far
and introduce
the two primary algorithmic contributions of this paper: matrix-free NGD with
randomized Nyström preconditioning (NyströmNGD) and matrix-free NGD with
RPCholesky preconditioning (RPCholNGD). To emphasize the modularity of our
framework, we first formulate Natural Gradient Descent as a meta-algorithm
(
Algorithm

1
) that delegates the computation of the descent
direction to a specific solver routine. This allows us to seamlessly swap
between a direct solver based on the full Gramian (
Algorithm

2
)
and a matrix-free iterative solver (
Algorithm

3
). The matrix-free
solver, in turn, accepts a preconditioner builder, enabling the construction of
NyströmNGD (
Algorithm

4
) and RPCholNGD (
Algorithm

5
)
simply by exchanging the preconditioning strategy.

A key advantage of NyströmNGD and RPCholNGD over standard NGD is their
reduced and tunable memory footprint. Excluding the memory required for the
matvec function, which depends on the strategy chosen from

Section

4.1.2
but can be bounded by
\mathcal{O}(pB)
using a batch
size

2\ell
vectors of length

history size of
\ell
. Consequently, the parameter
\ell_{\max}
provides
explicit control over memory consumption, allowing the method to scale to larger
networks. Naturally, smaller values of
\ell_{\max}
may result in a weaker
preconditioner and a higher number of inner pCG iterations, but the algorithm
remains fully operational.

5.
Numerical Experiments

We evaluate the performance of NyströmNGD and RPCholNGD on a diverse set of
PDE problems with known analytical solutions. Our benchmarks encompass both
strong formulations, discretized via Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs),
and weak formulations, discretized using Finite Element Interpolated Neural
Networks (FEINNs). The complete codebase to reproduce the results will be made
publicly available upon publication at

https://github.com/IvanBioli/preconditioned-natural-gradient.git
, and is
currently available upon reasonable request.

Evaluated methods and baselines

In
Section

5.1
, we compare several algorithmic configurations within our modular NGD framework
(legend names in parentheses): NGD with a direct solver (NGD full); NGD with a
matrix-free solver and no preconditioning (NGD matrix-free no prec.);
NyströmNGD using a Gaussian random test matrix (NyströmNGD Gaussian);
NyströmNGD with a random sampling test matrix with sampling probability
proportional to
\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))
(NyströmNGD Sampling); and finally,
RPCholNGD.

Furthermore, in
Section

5.2
, we benchmark our preconditioned matrix-free NGD methods against state-of-the-art first- and quasi-second-order optimizers commonly employed in the literature. We include SSBroyden due to its recently reported exceptional performance on PINNs
[
83
,
55
]
. We also evaluate Adam
[
53
]
, BFGS
[
88
]
, and L-BFGS
[
88
]
, as they are the most standard first-order, quasi-Newton, and limited-memory quasi-Newton algorithms for PINNs, respectively. Additional optimizers, such as Shampoo
[
39
,
4
]
and Muon
[
50
]
, were also tested but are omitted from the final comparison as they did not yield improvements over SSBroyden. Because the
per-iteration computational cost varies significantly across these methods, we
evaluate performance based on both iteration count and wall-clock time.
We remark that our choice of numerical examples is motivated by the goal of
comparing with those present in the NGD
literature, as our primary focus is on improving NGD methods rather than
outperforming all optimizers for all classes of PDEs.

All experiments are conducted in double precision on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090
GPU (24 GB), with results averaged over 10 independent runs. In the resulting
plots, solid lines indicate medians while shaded regions represent the
interquartile range (
25
th to
75
th percentiles).

General setup and hyperparameters

Unless specified otherwise, experiments employ a fully connected neural network
with three hidden layers of width 64 and
\tanh
activations. Except in the
scalability experiments in
Section

5.3
, in all NGD variants we
compute the full Jacobian without batching, and for matrix-free implementations,
we employ the “Precompute Jacobian only” strategy (see

Section

4.1.2
).
For the preconditioned NGD methods, NyströmNGD and RPCholNGD, we cap the
maximum preconditioner rank at
\ell_{\max}=500
and run pCG for a maximum of

\mathtt{maxit}=20
iterations. Within NyströmNGD, the rank increase
parameter is fixed to
\delta\ell=20
. In RPCholNGD, we use the block variant
of RPCholesky with a block size of
b=20
. To ensure a fair comparison across
methods, the history size for L-BFGS is set to
500
to match the memory
footprint of the preconditioned NGD methods. Similarly, for the
un-preconditioned matrix-free NGD, we allocate a maximum of
520
pCG
iterations, matching the total maximum number of matrix-vector products
(matvecs) permitted in the preconditioned variants.

To enforce stronger regularization during the early stages of optimization, if not stated otherwise, all
NGD methods utilize the adaptive heuristic
\mu=\max(\gamma\cdot\hat{\lambda}_{1}\cdot\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}},10^{-4}\cdot L(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{2})
.
The largest eigenvalue estimate,
\hat{\lambda}_{1}
, is computed using four
iterations of the power method, except in NyströmNGD where the estimate is
naturally provided by the Nyström approximation. The parameter
\gamma
is
set to
\gamma=10
.

A comprehensive description of the problem-specific configurations, including
neural architectures, spatial discretizations, iteration budgets, and baseline
optimizer hyperparameters, as well as an extended sensitivity analysis of
NyströmNGD and RPCholNGD, is provided in
Appendix

B

and
Appendix

C
.

5.1.1.
Poisson problem in 3D – PINNs

We consider the Poisson problem on the 3D unit cube

[
72
,
21
]

(18)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=f&\Omega=(0,1)^{3},\\
u=g_{D}&\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

with exact solution
u(x,y,z)=\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)\sin(\pi z)
, discretized
via Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) as in

[
90
]
. Problem (
18
) is linear and is
considered in strong form. Using the notation of

Section

2.2.1
, the function spaces are
\mathrm{V}=\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega)
,
\mathrm{X}=\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, and
\mathrm{Y}=\mathrm{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega)
,
with operators

\mathcal{F}(u)=-\Delta u,\qquad\mathcal{B}(u)=\left.{u}\right|_{\partial\Omega}.

The corresponding least-squares loss is

(19)

E(u)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\Delta u+f}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{u-g_{D}}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2},

and the induced metric reads

(20)

g\left({u},\,{v}\right)=\left({\Delta u},\,{\Delta v}\right)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\left({u},\,{v}\right)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}\Delta u\,\Delta v\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}u\,v\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}.

Remark 5.1
.

The metric (
20
) is coercive only in the

\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\Omega)
norm, but not in
\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega)
. This is due to the
use of the
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega)
norm for the Dirichlet boundary term.
Coercivity in
\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega)
can be restored by enforcing the boundary
conditions strongly
[
90
, Remark 6]
or by penalizing
in the
\mathrm{H}^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
norm
[
15
]
.
However, in line with standard PINN practice, we retain the

\mathrm{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega)
penalty for simplicity.

Results

As shown in
Figures

4(a)
and
4(b)
, NGD with a direct
solver (NGD full), both variants of NyströmNGD, and RPCholNGD achieve
similar accuracy in a comparable number of iterations; however, the matrix-free
preconditioned NGD variants do so in significantly less time. Conversely,
unpreconditioned matrix-free NGD fails to reach the accuracy of the
preconditioned variants, despite being allocated the same budget of Gramian
matvecs. This underscores the crucial role of preconditioning: given a fixed
matvec budget, it is more effective to allocate the majority of it toward
constructing a robust preconditioner. Without preconditioning, the linear system
is solved inaccurately, yielding a poor approximation of the natural gradient
descent direction and ultimately degrading the final accuracy. Furthermore, per
iteration, the preconditioned NGD variants are significantly faster than
unpreconditioned matrix-free NGD. This speedup occurs because assembling the
preconditioner allows for the parallel execution of multiple matvecs with the
Gramian, unlike the sequential matvecs required by CG. Finally,

Figure

4(c)
shows that the rank parameter
\ell
increases
rapidly during early iterations, consistent with
Figure

4(d)
,
before stabilizing near the maximum allowed rank
\ell_{\max}=500
. Note,
however, that despite reaching this maximum rank, the method still achieves a
compression factor of approximately
17\times
relative to the total number of
parameters
p=8641
.

) in strong form discretized via PINNs, with function spaces

(22)

E(u)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\partial_{t}u-\Delta u-f}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I,\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{u-g_{D}}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I,\mathrm{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega))}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{u(0,\cdot)-u_{0}}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

5.1

5.1.3.
Kovasznay Flow (2D steady-state Navier–Stokes flow) – PINNs

We consider the two-dimensional steady Navier–Stokes flow as originally
described in
[
56
]
, with Reynolds number
\mathrm{Re}=40
, following the setup in
[
49
, Section 4.1]
. The
system of PDEs reads

(24)

\begin{cases}-\nu\Delta u+(u\cdot\nabla)u+\nabla p=f&\Omega=(-\frac{1}{2},1)\times(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}),\\
\nabla\cdot u=0&\Omega,\\
u=g_{D}&\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

with exact solution

\begin{split}u(x,y)&=\begin{bmatrix}1-e^{\lambda x}\cos(2\pi y)\\
\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}e^{\lambda x}\sin(2\pi y)\end{bmatrix},\\
p(x,y)&=\frac{1}{2}(1-e^{2\lambda x})-c,\end{split}

where
\lambda=\frac{1}{2\nu}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{4\nu^{2}}+4\pi^{2}}
,
\nu=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Re}}=\frac{1}{40}
, and
c
is chosen such that
\int_{\Omega}p=0
. Following
[
49
]
, we consider the function
spaces
\mathrm{V}=\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega)\times\{p\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega):\int_{\Omega}p=0\}

and
\mathrm{X}=\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{2}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega)
. Problem (
24
) is written in
strong form as a nonlinear least-squares problem with residual

(25)

\mathcal{R}(u,p)=\begin{bmatrix}-\nu\Delta u+(u\cdot\nabla)u+\nabla p-f\\
\nabla\cdot u\\
u-g_{D}\end{bmatrix},

and corresponding energy functional

(26)

E(u,p)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{-\nu\Delta u+(u\cdot\nabla)u+\nabla p-f}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\nabla\cdot u}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{u-g_{D}}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega)}.

As a metric, we consider the Gauss–Newton metric

g_{(u,p)}\left({(v,h)},\,{(w,t)}\right)=\left({\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}(u,p)[(v,h)]},\,{\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}(u,p)[(w,t)]}\right).

Since the residual (
25
) is nonlinear, the metric is
point-dependent. We refer to
[
49
]
for a discussion on
coercivity.

To discretize (
24
), we parameterize the velocity and
pressure fields using two separate neural networks,
(u,p)=(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}},p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}})
, and concatenate their weights into a single
parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}=[\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}]
. To ensure the total parameter
count remains comparable to the other experiments, both networks are configured
with three hidden layers of
45
neurons each. Furthermore, because we utilize
the Gauss–Newton metric for this problem, we adapt the regularization parameter

\mu
using the standard Levenberg–Marquardt heuristic
[
88
]
.

Results

In this example, NyströmNGD with Gaussian test matrices converges in fewer iterations compared to the other methods, as shown in
Figure

6(a)
. However, NyströmNGD with sampling test matrices and RPCholNGD benefit from a lower per-iteration cost, allowing them to achieve comparable overall convergence times (see
Figure

exhibits a pronounced peak during intermediate iterations, hitting the maximum rank
\ell_{\max}=500
before decreasing and stabilizing around
\ell=400
(see

[
13
]
. Conversely, RPCholNGD steadily increases the rank to approximately
\ell=220
, well below the maximum
\ell_{\max}=500
, and achieves similar accuracy with a reduced memory footprint. This demonstrates that the choice of preconditioner can impact both the convergence speed (in iterations and wall-clock time) and the memory requirements of NGD.

5.1.4.
Poisson problem in 2D – FEINNs Energy minimization (Deep Ritz-like) formulation

We consider the Poisson problem on the 2D unit square

(27)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=f&\Omega=(0,1)^{2},\\
u=g_{D}&\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
f\in\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)
and
g_{D}\in\mathrm{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega)
. As exact solution we take
u(x,y)=\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)
.

All equalities in (
27
) are understood in the
variational sense. That is, given a lifting
\tilde{g}_{D}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)

such that
\left.{\tilde{g}_{D}}\right|_{\partial\Omega}=g_{D}
, we seek
u_{0}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
satisfying

(28)

a(u_{0},v)=\int_{\Omega}fv\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}-a(\tilde{g}_{D},v)\quad\forall v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\qquad\text{where}\quad a(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

The solution is then
u=u_{0}+\tilde{g}_{D}
. The discretization spaces are
\mathrm{V}=\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
,
\mathrm{X}=\mathrm{V}^{\prime}=\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)
, with linear operator

\mathcal{F}:\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega),\qquad\left\langle{\mathcal{F}u},\,{v}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{H}^{-1}\times\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}}=a(u,v).

Since
a
is symmetric and coercive on
\mathrm{V}\times\mathrm{V}
, we adopt an energy
minimization formulation, akin to the Deep Ritz method:

(29)

\min_{u_{0}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}E(u_{0})\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\nabla u_{0}}\right\|^{2}-fu_{0}+\nabla\tilde{g}_{D}\cdot\nabla u_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

with corresponding metric
g=a
.

Since the energy (
29
) may take negative values and does not vanish at the minimum, we cannot employ a regularization term of the form
\mu=\max(\gamma\cdot\hat{\lambda}_{1}\cdot\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}},10^{-4}\cdot L(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{2})
to enforce strong regularization during early training iterations. Instead, at each iteration
k
, we utilize
\mu=\max(\gamma\cdot\hat{\lambda}_{1}\cdot\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}},2^{-k})
.

Results

NyströmNGD and RPCholNGD exhibit almost identical convergence in iterations compared to NGD full, reaching the same accuracy at a fraction of the computational cost, as shown in
Figure

7(b)
. The performance gap between the preconditioned NGD variants and NGD full is more pronounced in this example than in previous ones because a rank of
\ell\approx 150
suffices for effective preconditioning (see
Figure

7(c)
). This is consistent with the significantly sharper singular value decay observed in
Figure

7(d)
compared to earlier examples. Conversely, unpreconditioned matrix-free NGD remains uncompetitive, further underscoring the critical importance of preconditioning.

5.1.5.
Diffusion-reaction-transport problem in 2D – FEINNs

We consider the diffusion-reaction-transport problem

(30)

\begin{cases}-\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla u)+\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla u+\sigma u=F&\Omega=(0,1)^{2},\\
u=g&\Gamma_{D}\subset\partial\Omega,\\
\kappa\nabla u\cdot\mathbf{n}=\psi&\Gamma_{N}\subset\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\kappa(x,y)=2+\sin(x+2y)
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{x-y^{2}+5}&\sqrt{y-x^{2}+5}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
, and
\sigma(x,y)=e^{\frac{x}{2}-\frac{y}{3}}+2
. The source term
F
is chosen so that the
exact solution is

u(x,y)=\sin(3.2x(x-y))\cos(4.3y+x)+\sin(4.6(x+2y))\cos(2.6(y-2x)),

as in
[
11
,
8
]
. The Dirichlet boundary

\Gamma_{D}
consists of the left and right sides of the domain, while the Neumann
boundary
\Gamma_{N}
corresponds to the top and bottom edges.

The function spaces are
\mathrm{V}=\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0,\Gamma_{D}}(\Omega)
,
\mathrm{X}=\mathrm{V}^{\prime}=\mathrm{H}^{-1}_{\Gamma_{D}}(\Omega)
, and the weak formulation of
(
30
) is defined as
\mathcal{F}u=f
where

\begin{split}\left\langle{\mathcal{F}u},\,{v}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{H}^{-1}_{\Gamma_{D}}\times\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0,\Gamma_{D}}}&=a(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}(\kappa\nabla u)\cdot\nabla v+(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla u)\,v+\sigma\,u\,v\,\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\\
\left\langle{f},\,{v}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{H}^{-1}_{\Gamma_{D}}\times\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0,\Gamma_{D}}}&=\int_{\Omega}F\,v\,\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\int_{\Gamma_{N}}\psi\,v\,\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}.\end{split}

Since
\mathcal{F}
is in general not symmetric positive definite, we adopt the
least-squares formulation

(31)

\min_{u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}E(u)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\mathcal{F}u-f}\right\|_{\mathrm{H}^{-1}_{\Gamma_{D}}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle{\mathcal{F}u-f},\,{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{V}}^{-1}(\mathcal{F}u-f)}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{H}^{-1}_{\Gamma_{D}}\times\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0,\Gamma_{D}}}.

Here,
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{V}}^{-1}=(-\Delta)^{-1}:\mathrm{H}^{-1}_{\Gamma_{D}}\to\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0,\Gamma_{D}}
denotes the inverse Riesz operator, which involves
solving a Poisson equation. The least-squares metric would be
\left({v},\,{w}\right)=\left\langle{\mathcal{F}w},\,{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{V}}^{-1}\mathcal{F}v}\right\rangle
. To reduce the computational
cost, we consider the
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0,\Gamma_{D}}
norm
g\left({u},\,{v}\right)=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
. This choice is motivated by the fact
that the
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0,\Gamma_{D}}
norm is spectrally equivalent to the least-squares metric
due to the inf-sup condition, and it allows us to avoid the costly
application of
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{V}}^{-1}
for each matvec with the Gramian.

When discretizing (
31
) with FEINNs, applying the discrete
Riesz operator
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{V}_{h}}^{-1}
corresponds to solving a Poisson problem
with FEM. To reduce computational cost, one could instead use a spectrally
equivalent approximation, such as a few cycles of a geometric multigrid
preconditioner, as done in
[
8
]
. However, for simplicity, we
solve the Poisson problem directly using FEM, computing a Cholesky factorization
of the Laplacian matrix once.

Results

Figure

5.2.
Comparison with other optimizers

In this section, we compare the optimizers from the NGD framework, and with more
care NyströmNGD and RPCholNGD, against a suite of standard and
state-of-the-art optimizers (Adam, L-BFGS, BFGS, and SSBroyden) on all five test
problems. Since each optimizer is run for a deliberately large number of
iterations, often well beyond the point of practical convergence, we quantify
performance at the iteration where each optimizer effectively plateaus.
Specifically, we define a
plateau
as the first iteration after which the
relative error fails to improve by more than
10\%
within a
trailing window covering
10\%
of the total number of iterations.

Figure

10
shows the full convergence history for all
optimizers on each problem; a dot (
\bullet
) on each curve marks the detected
plateau iterate, confirming that the criterion reliably captures the end of
productive convergence.
Table

reports, for each
optimizer and test problem, the median error at the plateau. The following
line reports, in smaller font and in parentheses, the
25
th and
75
th
percentiles in compact form as
(a-b\,\times 10^{k})
, while the last line reports
the corresponding cumulative wall-clock time in seconds.

As reported in
Table

2
, the final accuracy achieved by
NGD with a direct solver (NGD full) and the matrix-free preconditioned NGD
variants (NyströmNGD and RPCholNGD) is highly consistent across all test
problems. This shows that the primary objective of the paper is achieved in all
examples: improving the computational efficiency of existing NGD approaches
while preserving essentially the same final accuracy. On most problems, this
accuracy is also comparable to that of SSBroyden, which remains a very strong
baseline and, in some cases, attains a slightly better final error. The runner-up among
the baseline methods is BFGS; however, its final accuracy on the PINN examples
remains more than an order of magnitude worse. Crucially, both SSBroyden and
BFGS entail memory requirements of
\mathcal{O}(p^{2})
, whereas the
preconditioned NGD methods operate with a memory footprint of

\mathcal{O}(p\ell_{\max})
. When comparing our approaches to L-BFGS, which
possesses similar memory requirements given history size
\ell_{\max}
, the
final accuracy of NyströmNGD and RPCholNGD remains clearly better on the
considered benchmarks. Adam, being a first-order method, consistently yields the
poorest accuracy across all benchmarks.

In terms of computational efficiency, RPCholNGD and NyströmNGD are often
faster than SSBroyden while using less memory, although this behavior is not
uniform across all evaluated problems. Among the proposed variants,
NyströmNGD often offers the best trade-off between final accuracy and
computational time, with Gaussian test matrices serving as a robust default
choice. The main exception is the Kovasznay problem, where SSBroyden is faster
and reaches a lower error than the NGD-based methods. We deliberately retained
this example for fairness, as it shows that NGD methods are not uniformly
superior to SSBroyden on all possible benchmarks. At the same time, comparing
the preconditioned variants with NGD full on this problem indicates that the
accuracy bottleneck lies in the underlying NGD formulation rather than in the
randomized preconditioner, since the preconditioned methods recover essentially
the same accuracy in significantly less time. Overall, these comparisons should
be read primarily as evidence that the proposed methods improve
baseline NGD, while remaining competitive with strong state-of-the-art
optimizers on many problems; see
Figure

5.3.
Scalability Experiments – Poisson problem in 3D

We consider the 3D Poisson problem (
18
), discretized via PINNs
as in
Section

5.1.1
, and investigate the scalability of the
preconditioned NGD methods with respect to the number of parameters
p
and the
number of quadrature points
q
. First, we keep
p
fixed and increase the total
number of quadrature points to
q=10^{5}
, such that the full Jacobian

\mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
exceeds memory capacity, requiring batching over the quadrature
points (i.e., the “Recompute batch Jacobian on the fly” strategy from

Table

1
). Second, we also increase the number of parameters to

p\approx 3.3\times 10^{4}
by using a wider network with three hidden layers of

128
neurons each, rendering the
\mathcal{O}(p^{2})
memory requirement of full NGD
and SSBroyden prohibitive under our hardware constraints. In both experiments,
we employ a batch size of
B=1000
quadrature points. The results are reported
in
Table

3
.

When increasing the number of quadrature points from
q=10^{4}
to
q=10^{5}
,
NyströmNGD (Gaussian) and NyströmNGD (Sampling) exhibit a per-iteration
cost that grows almost linearly in
q
, with only a slight overhead due to
batching, which requires recomputing the (batched) Jacobian at each CG
iteration. In contrast, the per-iteration cost grows significantly more for
RPCholNGD. As discussed in
Section

4.2.3
, constructing
the Nyström preconditioner performs all matvecs in a single batched
operation, whereas RPCholesky accesses the column blocks of the Gramian
sequentially, thereby incurring the
\mathcal{O}(q\texttt{[FP]})
overhead of recomputing the
Jacobian for each block.

Furthermore, our preconditioned matrix-free NGD methods can also be run on the
larger
3\times 128\times 128\times 128\times 1
architecture, for which NGD full
and SSBroyden become prohibitive due to their
\mathcal{O}(p^{2})
memory footprint. The
wider network also yields a more accurate final approximation. Finally, comparing
the two architectures at
q=10^{5}
, the per-iteration cost scales approximately
linearly with the number of parameters, as expected.

6.
Discussion and Conclusion

We presented a modular framework for efficient natural gradient descent (NGD)
and proposed the use of techniques from Randomized Numerical Linear Algebra
(RandNLA), specifically the Nyström approximation and the Randomly Pivoted
Partial Cholesky (RPCholesky) factorization, to construct a preconditioner for
the inner conjugate gradient (CG) solver in matrix-free NGD. This led to

NyströmNGD
and
RPCholNGD
, two novel variants of NGD for
neural-network-based PDE solvers. Moreover, our work extends matrix-free NGD to
a broader class of metrics relevant to PDE-related problems. Our approach
significantly reduces the cost of the inner solver, resulting in substantial
wall-clock savings compared to NGD with a direct solver and unpreconditioned
matrix-free NGD.

We validated NyströmNGD and RPCholNGD on various PDE problems, both in
strong form discretized with PINNs and in weak form discretized with FEINNs. In
all considered examples, the primary objective of the paper is achieved: the
preconditioned variants improve existing NGD approaches by matching or improving
the accuracy of NGD full and unpreconditioned matrix-free NGD at a lower
computational cost. We also compared them with strong baselines such as
SSBroyden to better position the methods within the broader optimization
literature. On most of the analyzed problems, our methods attain an accuracy comparable to
SSBroyden with lower memory requirements and often shorter wall-clock times;
however, this behavior is not uniform, and the Kovasznay example shows that
NGD-based methods are not universally superior to all state-of-the-art
optimizers. This does not affect the main conclusion of the paper, since our
goal is to improve the efficiency and scalability of NGD itself rather than to
outperform every competing optimizer across all PDEs.

The use of Randomized NLA low-rank approximations for preconditioning builds
on the empirically observed low-rank structure of the Gramian. Our results
confirm that the Gramian often admits strong low-rank compression, and low-rank
preconditioners remain highly effective even when they do not fully capture the
Gramian. Furthermore, the core idea of preconditioning the inner CG solver
remains effective even when the Gramian does not admit a low-rank approximation,
and we plan to investigate alternative Gramian approximations for
preconditioning purposes. Moreover, NyströmNGD and RPCholNGD are
particularly promising for neural-network-based solvers targeting weak
formulations of PDEs, where the underlying variational structure can naturally
guide the choice of metric. Our results in

Sections

5.1.4
and
5.1.5
strongly support this potential and
motivate further research in this direction. Finally, it would be valuable to
extend these methods to operator learning, which requires accommodating the
structure of operator spaces rather than just function spaces, and to broader
large-scale machine learning tasks where NGD has already shown promise.

Sensitivity analysis

We conduct a sensitivity analysis of NyströmNGD and RPCholNGD with respect to
their key hyperparameters. Specifically, we examine the impact of the
regularization parameter
\mu
and the rank adaptivity strategy on the
convergence behavior and computational efficiency. For simplicity, we focus on
NyströmNGD with Gaussian test matrices, as we found the behavior to be
similar when using sampling test matrices.

Regularization parameter
\mu

\mu

\gamma=1
on the strong-form problems (Poisson 3D, Heat 3+1D). NyströmNGD is more
robust:
\gamma=1
still yields good accuracy, but
\gamma=0.1
causes
divergence on the variational problems and also increases the Nyström rank
parameter
\ell
, raising per-iteration computational cost. Results are reported

and

Appendix D
Choice of the Metric

The link with operator preconditioning in
Section

3.2.1
makes it evident that the choice of metric has a crucial influence on the convergence speed of NGD. In some cases, the problem’s structure naturally suggests a metric, as in Variational Monte Carlo
[
69
,
45
,
65
]
or Sobolev gradient flows
[
44
,
19
]
. In general, the link between NGD and Riemannian gradient descent
[
64
]
suggests selecting the metric
g
to reduce the condition number of the Riemannian Hessian
[
16
]
. Indeed, it can be shown that NGD corresponds to Riemannian gradient descent
[
16
]
on the manifold
(\mathrm{V},g)
, up to the
g
-orthogonal projection of the Riemannian gradient onto the model’s generalized tangent space
\mathrm{T}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{N}
, and a second-order correction; see
[
65
, Theorem 1]
. More precisely, the NGD update
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}-\alpha_{k}\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})^{\dagger}\nabla L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})
satisfies

u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k+1}}=u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}-\alpha_{k}\Pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}^{g}\left(\mathrm{grad}^{g}E(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}})\right)+\varepsilon_{k},

where
\Pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}^{g}
denotes the
g
-orthogonal projection onto the generalized tangent space
\mathrm{T}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}\mathcal{N}
, and
\varepsilon_{k}=\mathcal{O}\big(\alpha_{k}^{2}\left\|{\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})^{\dagger}\nabla L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})}\right\|^{2}\big)
. A principled strategy for selecting the metric
g
is thus to adopt criteria from Riemannian optimization, choosing
g
to reduce the condition number of the Riemannian Hessian
[
16
,
1
]
. Although convergence may differ from standard Riemannian gradient descent due to the projection onto
\mathrm{T}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{N}
, whose effect depends on the geometry of
\mathcal{N}
and the parametrization, this strategy can be expected to remain effective if the ansatz
\mathcal{N}
is sufficiently expressive.

D.1.
Linear problems

Setting D.1
.

Let
\mathrm{V},\mathrm{W}
be Hilbert spaces, and let
\mathcal{A}:\mathrm{V}\to\mathrm{W}^{\prime}
be a linear isomorphism. Given
f\in\mathrm{W}^{\prime}
, we aim at solving

(32)

\mathcal{A}u=f\in\mathrm{W}^{\prime}.

A natural approach is to consider the least-squares problem

(33)

\inf_{u\in\mathrm{V}}E(u)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\mathcal{A}u-f}\right\|_{\mathrm{W}^{\prime}}^{2}.

Choosing on
\mathrm{V}
the (point-independent) metric

(34)

g\left({v},\,{w}\right)\coloneqq\left({\mathcal{A}v},\,{\mathcal{A}w}\right)_{\mathrm{W}^{\prime}},

yields
\mathrm{Hess}^{g}E=\mathrm{Id}
. Riemannian gradient descent on
(\mathrm{V},g)

thus converges in a single iteration, as the Riemannian gradient is given by

\mathrm{grad}^{g}E(u)=u-\mathcal{A}^{-1}f
, i.e., computing it is equivalent to solving
(
32
). NGD does not require applying
\mathcal{A}^{-1}
:
computing the Gramian
\left[\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]_{ij}=\left({\mathcal{A}\partial_{\theta_{i}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},\,{\mathcal{A}\partial_{\theta_{j}}u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)_{\mathrm{W}^{\prime}}
involves only applications of
\mathcal{A}
.

An alternative is to choose the metric

(35)

g\left({v},\,{w}\right)\coloneqq\left({v},\,{w}\right)_{\mathrm{V}},

which corresponds to the standard
\mathrm{V}
-inner product. In this case, the
Riemannian gradient is
\mathrm{grad}^{g}E(u)=\mathcal{A}^{*}(\mathcal{A}u-f)
, and the
Riemannian Hessian is
\mathrm{Hess}^{g}E=\mathcal{A}^{*}\mathcal{A}
. If an inf-sup condition with
constant
\beta>0
holds, i.e.,
\inf_{v\in\mathrm{V}}\sup_{w\in\mathrm{W}}\frac{\left\langle{\mathcal{A}v},\,{w}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{W}^{\prime}\times\mathrm{W}}}{\left\|{v}\right\|_{\mathrm{V}}\left\|{w}\right\|_{\mathrm{W}}}\geq\beta
,
then the condition number of the Hessian is bounded by
\left\|{\mathcal{A}}\right\|^{2}/\beta^{2}
.

Remark D.2
.

We formulate the linear problem from
\mathrm{V}
to the
dual
of
\mathrm{W}
because one of our target applications involves variational formulations of PDEs, which naturally take the form (
32
). For PINNs, which are based on strong formulations,
\mathrm{W}
is typically a (product of)
\mathrm{L}^{2}
spaces, so that
\mathrm{W}^{\prime}\cong\mathrm{W}
and the formulation recovers that of
[
90
]
, which does not employ the dual.

Remark D.3
.

In Setting
D.1
, it is typically straightforward to approximate numerically the inner product
\left({\cdot},\,{\cdot}\right)_{\mathrm{W}}
and the duality pairing
\left\langle{\cdot},\,{\cdot}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{W}^{\prime}\times\mathrm{W}}
, but not the dual inner product
\left({\cdot},\,{\cdot}\right)_{\mathrm{W}^{\prime}}
. Let
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{W}}:\mathrm{W}\to\mathrm{W}^{\prime}
denote the Riesz map, defined by

\left\langle{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{W}}v},\,{w}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{W}^{\prime}\times\mathrm{W}}=\left({v},\,{w}\right)_{\mathrm{W}}\qquad\forall v,w\in\mathrm{W}.

Then, for
\ell,h\in\mathrm{W}^{\prime}
, we have

\left({\ell},\,{h}\right)_{\mathrm{W}^{\prime}}=\left\langle{\ell},\,{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{W}}^{-1}h}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{W}^{\prime}\times\mathrm{W}}.

For example, when
\mathrm{W}=\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, the dual space is
\mathrm{W}^{\prime}=\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)
, and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{W}}=-\Delta
, the negative Laplace operator. Computing the dual inner product thus requires applying
(-\Delta)^{-1}
, i.e., solving a (discretized) Laplace problem. In contrast, for
\mathrm{W}=\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, one has
\mathrm{W}^{\prime}\cong\mathrm{W}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{W}}=\mathrm{Id}
.

Symmetric positive definite linear operators

If
\mathrm{V}=\mathrm{W}
and
\mathcal{A}
is symmetric positive definite, one can endow
\mathrm{V}
with the
\mathcal{A}
-induced inner product

\left({v},\,{w}\right)_{\mathcal{A}}=\left\langle{\mathcal{A}v},\,{w}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{V}^{\prime}\times\mathrm{V}}\qquad\forall v,w\in\mathrm{V}.

This corresponds to choosing
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{V}}=\mathcal{A}
, and the loss (
33
) becomes

(36)

E(u)=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle{\mathcal{A}u},\,{u}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{V}^{\prime}\times\mathrm{V}}-\left\langle{f},\,{u}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{V}^{\prime}\times\mathrm{V}}+\mathrm{const},

where the constant is irrelevant for gradient-based optimization. This is precisely the variational form used in the Deep Ritz method
[
31
]
. The corresponding natural metric is
g=\left({\cdot},\,{\cdot}\right)_{\mathcal{A}}
.

D.2.
Nonlinear problems

For general nonlinear problems, there is no canonical choice of metric as in the linear case. In some instances, the structure of the problem suggests multiple metrics; the choice of the metric impacts the convergence and the computational cost of the optimization schemes. Examples include Variational Monte Carlo (VMC)
[
69
,
45
,
65
]
or Sobolev gradient flows
[
44
,
19
]
. Below we outline some general strategies that can be used in nonlinear problems and are employed in our numerical experiments.

Newton’s method

Setting D.4
.

Let
(\mathrm{V},a)
be a Riemannian manifold and
E:\mathrm{V}\to\mathbb{R}
a smooth function. We aim to solve
\inf_{u\in\mathrm{V}}E(u)
.

If
\mathrm{Hess}^{a}E(u)
is symmetric positive definite for all
u\in\mathrm{V}
, a natural metric leading to Riemannian Newton’s method
[
16
]
is

(37)

g_{u}\left({v},\,{w}\right)=\mathrm{Hess}^{a}E(u)[v,w].

In the case
(\mathrm{V},a)=(\mathrm{V},\left({\cdot},\,{\cdot}\right)_{\mathrm{V}})
for a Hilbert space
\mathrm{V}
, this yields the Energy Natural Gradient Descent (ENGD) method
[
64
,
65
]
. Note that to interpret ENGD as a Natural Gradient Descent, it is necessary that
\mathrm{Hess}^{a}E(u)
is SPD for all
u\in\mathrm{V}
, as otherwise (
37
) does not define a valid metric.

Nonlinear
least-squares
problem: Gauss–Newton

Setting D.5
.

Let
\mathrm{V},\mathrm{W}
be Hilbert spaces, and let
\mathcal{R}:\mathrm{V}\to\mathrm{W}
be a (possibly nonlinear) residual. We aim at finding
u\in\mathrm{V}
such that
\mathcal{R}(u)=0
.

A natural approach is to minimize the nonlinear least-squares functional

(38)

\inf_{u\in\mathrm{V}}E(u)\coloneqq\inf_{u\in\mathrm{V}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\mathcal{R}(u)}\right\|_{\mathrm{W}}^{2}.

The Hessian of
E
reads

\mathrm{D}^{2}E(u)[v,w]=\left({\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}(u)[v]},\,{\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}(u)[w]}\right)_{\mathrm{W}}+\left({\mathcal{R}(u)},\,{\mathrm{D}^{2}\mathcal{R}(u)[v,w]}\right)_{\mathrm{W}},

and a symmetric positive semidefinite approximation is given by the Gauss–Newton method
[
88
]

(39)

g_{u}\left({v},\,{w}\right)=\left({\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}(u)[v]},\,{\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}(u)[w]}\right)_{\mathrm{W}}.

This choice of the metric leads to the Gauss–Newton NGD method

[
49
,
43
]
. Note that
g_{u}
is, in general, only semidefinite. While this does not pose numerical issues when using the pseudoinverse or damping, the positive definiteness of
g_{u}
is necessary to interpret it as a Riemannian metric. Whether this condition holds depends on the structure of the underlying nonlinear PDE.
