Elucidating the photo-induced fading mechanism in green tea beverages: Metabolomics-guided discovery, quantification, and spiking validation of critical metabolites
Authors: Shanshan Wang, Sixu Li, Zheng Tu, Meng Tao, Qinyan Yu, Anan Xu, Yanyan Cao, Zhengquan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The color of tea beverages is a critical quality determinant, yet photo-induced fading has received little attention compared to browning reactions. This study investigated riboflavin photodegradation in unsweetened green tea beverages under both retail-shelf lighting and simulated solar radiation. Under retail-shelf lighting, approximately 90% of riboflavin degraded within the first 5 days of a 20-day exposure period (p < 0.05), confirming its high susceptibility under realistic retail conditions. Under simulated solar radiation, a comparable 90% degradation occurred within only 8 h. Chlorophyll also degraded by approximately 37.5% after 8 h of sunlight exposure. While riboflavin serves as the primary driver of chromatic deterioration, chlorophyll contributes to a lesser extent in the photo-induced fading process. These findings establish riboflavin as a key target for intervention, with chlorophyll as a secondary contributor, providing a theoretical foundation for developing targeted light-protective strategies to preserve the color quality of green tea beverages.

Introduction

Tea stands as one of the world's three major non-alcoholic beverages. In recent years, driven by rising living standards and accelerating lifestyles, ready-to-drink (RTD) sugar-free tea beverages have experienced rapid development due to their inherent naturalness, health benefits, and convenience (Xu, 2024). Green tea, the predominant tea category produced and consumed in China, occupies a central position within both Chinese and global tea culture. Its non-fermentation process preserves a rich profile of natural active compounds inherent in fresh tea leaves, resulting in unique health-functional properties. These include, but are not limited to, antioxidant (Wan et al., 2023; White et al., 2025) and anti-aging effects (Liang, Qu, Ang, Li, & He, 2025; Shaukat et al., 2023), cardiovascular protection (Guo, Li, Lin, & Liu, 2023), metabolic regulation and weight management (Song, Ho, & Zhang, 2024), antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory activities (Song et al., 2023). Consequently, as a significant segment within the global market for health-oriented beverages, sugar-free green tea beverages have exhibited robust growth in recent years. This expansion is propelled by the dual drivers of consumer upgrading and technological innovation.

The rapid development of ready-to-drink (RTD) tea beverages has been accompanied by growing attention to product quality. Key evaluation metrics for tea beverage quality encompass taste, flavor, and color (Luo & Gao, 2022). The chromatic properties of tea infusions vary significantly across beverage categories, arising from the collective contribution of phytochemical constituents including catechins and their oxidative derivatives, flavonol glycosides, and anthocyanins. Therefore, the color of tea beverages is highly susceptible to deterioration due to multiple factors, including raw material properties, water quality, processing parameters, light exposure, oxygen presence, temperature fluctuations, and storage duration (Cao et al., 2025; Chen, Wu, & Liang, 2018; Fan et al., 2016). This deterioration significantly compromises sensory quality. Chromatic deterioration in tea beverages constitutes a critical technical bottleneck constraining industrial advancement within this sector.

Research indicates that sugar-free tea beverages across various product categories universally exhibit quality deterioration characterized by non-enzymatic browning, colloidal turbidity, and sensory degradation. These defects manifest with particular prominence in green tea-based formulations (Wang, Zhang, Chen, Shi, & Yin, 2025). To address the prevalent issue of browning in tea beverages, researchers worldwide have conducted extensive studies. Various color stabilization strategies have been proposed based on research findings, including: Optimization of primary tea processing techniques (Dong et al., 2018), Refinement of beverage manufacturing processes (Dai, 2007), Enzymatic treatment methods (Wang et al., 2020), Ion-based color stabilization approaches (Mossion, Potin-Gautier, Delerue, Le Hécho, & Behra, 2008; Yasuda, Matsuda, Ohshiro, Inouye, & Tabata, 2012), Incorporation of antioxidants (Chen et al., 2021; Hung et al., 2018). While these interventions demonstrate some efficacy in color preservation, their performance exhibits notable inconsistency. Furthermore, research has identified a distinct phenomenon: green tea beverages undergo significant photo-induced fading under supermarket display lighting or direct sunlight. This light-dependent color degradation poses a substantial risk to product aesthetic appeal and consumer experience. Light exposure may not only induce chromatic deterioration but also affect the volatile profile of tea beverages. Recent studies have demonstrated that certain ppt-level aroma compounds, such as 3-alkyl-2-methoxypyrazines, exhibit extremely low odor thresholds and contribute significantly to the overall aroma quality of green tea, with their concentrations being influenced by processing and agronomic practices (Tao et al., 2026). Consequently, it can diminish market competitiveness, potentially leading to unnecessary economic losses and erosion of brand trust.

This study focuses on elucidating the molecular basis of light-induced discoloration in tea beverages. Employing an innovative untargeted metabolomics approach, we conducted comprehensive and unbiased profiling of small-molecule metabolites in tea beverage samples before and after simulated light exposure. This high-throughput screening strategy aimed to identify differentially expressed metabolites significantly associated with the discoloration process. Subsequently, high-sensitivity and high-specificity targeted quantitative methodologies (e.g., LC-MS/MS) were utilized to precisely quantify these key differential metabolites. This enabled the validation of the correlation between their dynamic changes and the degree of color deterioration, ultimately facilitating the identification of pivotal compounds playing a decisive role in the process. This work lays a crucial scientific foundation for developing color protection technologies, including raw material selection, novel light-shielding packaging materials, screening of efficient photostabilizers, and optimization of processing techniques. The findings possess practical significance for enhancing the shelf-life stability of tea beverages and ensuring the consistency of product sensory quality.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Ferrous sulfate, sodium potassium tartrate, disodium hydrogen phosphate, potassium dihydrogen phosphate, acetone and riboflavin were purchased from Aladdin-Reagent (Shanghai, China). All reagents were of analytical grade unless otherwise specified. Acetic acid (≥ 99.9% HPLC) and ammonium formate (HPLC) were purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China). Acetonitrile (99.9% HPLC) was purchased from J&K Scientific (Beijing, China). Epicatechin (EC, 96% HPLC), epigallocatechin (EGC, 90% HPLC), and EGCG (98%) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Caffeine (98% HPLC) and theaflavin (80%) were purchased from Desite (Chengdu, China). Optima TM mass spectrometry grade methanol, acetonitrile, ammonium acetate, isopropyl alcohol and formic acid were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Fair Lawn, USA). Formic acid ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ·cm) was prepared using a Milli-Q water purification system (Merck Millipore, USA).

Sample preparation

Solar radiation-treated group: The same batch of commercially available green tea beverages was exposed to sunlight for 0 min, 15 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h and 8 h respectively; the negative control was placed in a tin foil bag and exposed to sunlight for 8 h simultaneously; the number of bottles exposed in each group was 5 and the intensity of light from sunlight is 80,000 to 100,000 lx.

Light treatment group: The commercially available green tea beverages were placed in the supermarket shelves for light treatment with the same batch. The supermarket shelf lighting corresponds to typical indoor LED or fluorescent lighting commonly used in retail displays, with a correlated color temperature of approximately 4000 K. The treatment duration was 20 days, and 5 bottles were sampled each day. The light intensity of the beverage refrigeration cabinet is 200–5000 Lux.

To arrest further chromatic evolution, processed samples were immediately transferred to light-impermeable containers and preserved at 4 °C under dark conditions.

The tea processing techniques are as follows:

Black tea:the fresh leaves were withered, rolled, fermented, first-dried and second-dried; Yellow tea: the fresh leaves were spreaded, fixed, rolled, yellowed, first-dried and second-dried; Green tea: the fresh leaves were spreaded, fixed, rolled, first-dried and second-dried.

Determination of routine indices

Routine quality indicators, including color, turbidity, pH, and total polyphenols (TPs), were measured for all samples. Colorimetric analysis was performed using a Konica Minolta Spectrophotometer CM-5, calibrated according to the CIE L*a*b* color space standard (ISO 11664-4:2019). The L* value represents lightness, with higher values indicating clearer beverages and lower values corresponding to darker or more turbid samples. The a* value denotes the red–green axis, where positive values indicate redness and negative values indicate greenness. The b* value denotes the yellow–blue axis, where positive values indicate yellowness and negative values indicate blueness. The total color difference (ΔE) was calculated as the overall deviation from the reference sample using the formula described by Cao et al. (Cao et al., 2025):ΔE=Lsample∗−Lcontrol∗2+asample∗−acontrol∗2+bsample∗−bcontrol∗21/2

Turbidity was measured using a WGZ-2000 turbidity meter (Shanghai Yidian Physical and Optical Instruments), with pure water as the blank control. Total polyphenol content was determined following the ferrous tartrate colorimetric method at 540 nm according to the Chinese national standard GB/T 21733–2008, using a U-6100 spectrophotometer (Shanghai Metash Instruments Co., Ltd., China).

Nontargeted metabolomic analysis using UPLC-HRMS

After the tea water samples were completely frozen and shaken to mix, 900 μL of each sample was precisely aspirated and evenly placed in 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes for freeze-drying (Labconco Corporation, Kansas City, USA). After freeze-drying, 120 μL of 70% methanol solution was used to extract the samples, and the samples were fully vortexed for 3 min. Then, they were transferred to a centrifuge at 3500 r/min for 15 min. 100 μL of the supernatant was taken and transferred to the sample vial for metabolomics analysis using liquid chromatography-high-resolution orbitrap mass spectrometry.

The detection was performed by IPhenome Biotechnology (Yun Pu Kang) Inc. (Dalian, China). Untargeted metabolomics analysis was conducted on an Ultimate 3000 ultra-high performance liquid chromatograph coupled with Q ExactiveTM quadrupole-Orbitrap high resolution mass spectrometer UPLC-HRMS system (Thermo Scientific, USA). Metabolites were separated by using an AcquityTM HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm, Waters Corporation, USA) maintaining at 50 °C and eluted by 0.1% formate/water and 0.1% formate/acetonitrile using linear gradient ramping from 5% organic mobile phase to 90% in 20 min. The quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer was all operated under identical ionization parameters with a heated electrospray ionization source except ionization voltage including sheath gas 45 arb, aux gas 10 arb, heater temperature 355 °C, capillary temperature 320 °C and S-Lens RF level 55%. Stepped normalized collision energy was employed for collision induced disassociation of metabolite using ultra-pure nitrogen as fragmentation gas. All the data acquired as profile format.

The targeted metabolomics results of the sample

Take 1 mL of the homogenized tea sample and aliquot it into 1.5 mL EP tubes in portions of 300 μL for lyophilization. Three lyophilized aliquots were subjected to triplicate parallel experiments. Reconstitute the samples with 80 μL of 70% methanol, then precisely transfer 60 μL of the supernatant to a 96-well plate (Thermo Scientific, USA) for targeted metabolomics analysis.

Targeted metabolite detection was performed using a Thermo Ultimate 3000 ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system coupled to a TSQ Endura MD Plus triple quadrupole mass spectrometer, with data acquisition conducted in selected reaction monitoring mode (Tables 1, S2). Acquity UPLC HSS C18 column (1.8 μm, 2.1*100 mm) was employed. The mobile phase for UPLC-MS/MS determination comprised 0.1% aqueous solution of formic acid (A) and Acetonitrile (ACN) (B) for determination of flavonoids and phenolic substances, while ammonium formate aqueous solution (2 mM, A) and Acetonitrile (ACN) (B) were used for determination of riboflavin. The mobile phase was delivered at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The mobile phase was delivered at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min, and signals were monitored at 220 nm with DAD detection. Heated ESI ion source parameters: Positive ion mode voltage, 3.5 kV; Negative ion mode voltage, −3.0 kV; Ion source temperature, 350 °C; Sheath Gas, 40 Arb (arbitrary units); Aux Gas, 15 Arb (arbitrary units); Collision Gas pressure, 2.0 mTorr.

Determination of riboflavin content in different tea varieties

Accurately weigh 100 mg of powdered tea sample into a 15 mL centrifuge tube. Add 10 mL of 70% methanol-water solution. Following vortex mixing at 1300 rpm for 30 min, perform ultrasonication-assisted extraction in a 70 °C water bath for 20 min. Repeat this extraction procedure twice. Between each ultrasonication step, briefly vortex-mix the tube for 2 min to ensure complete resuspension of the tea powder. After the two extraction cycles, transfer 1 mL of the suspension to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube and centrifuge at 10,000 ×g for 10 min. Precisely transfer 180 μL of the supernatant to a 96-well microplate (Thermo Scientific, USA) for riboflavin quantification. Targeted metabolite detection was performed using a Thermo Ultimate 3000 ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system coupled to a TSQ Endura MD Plus triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. Data were acquired in Selected Reaction Monitoring (SRM) mode. Mass spectrometric detection conditions were as described in Section 2.5 (Tables 2.).

Spike recovery validation for riboflavin and theaflavin

Green tea leaves were subjected to hot water extraction (65 °C，solid-to-water ratio 1:30, tea concentration 6 g/L). Following cooling and multi-stage filtration, the extract was adjusted to volume. Color protection agents (vitamin C and sodium bicarbonate) were added according to the formulation. Riboflavin and theaflavins were incorporated into the infusion at specified ratios. The infused solutions were transferred into polyethylene terephthalate (PET) bottles and subjected to accelerated light exposure. Comparative analysis of the degree of color change was subsequently performed.

Statistical analysis

All the experiments were performed in triplicate, and the data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The least significant difference (LSD) in one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to analyze the differences among the results applying SPSS version 24.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Differences with a p value less than 0.05 were considered significant. Mass spectrometric data were processed using TraceFinder software and quantified via the external standard calibration curve method. Multivariate statistical analyses, including Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Orthogonal Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA), among others, were performed using Rstudio.

Results and discussion

Determination of routine indices

Green tea beverages stored on supermarket shelves undergo progressive chromatic alterations upon prolonged exposure to artificial light. Notably, these changes are characterized by photo-induced fading, a phenomenon that contrasts with the conventional oxidative browning typically observed during tea storage, which deepens the color (Luo & Gao, 2022). Despite its practical relevance, no systematic investigation has yet addressed this photo-induced fading phenomenon.

To systematically examine this effect, we collected samples daily from green tea beverages displayed under supermarket shelf lighting for a continuous period of 20 days. As shown in Fig. 1B, the color of the beverages gradually faded with increasing daylight exposure hours, while light-shielded controls maintained chromatic stability throughout the entire experiment. To further validate the role of light irradiation, we exposed the beverages to intense simulated solar radiation. As demonstrated in Fig. 1A-L*, bottled beverages already exhibited detectable photo-induced fading after merely 15 min of direct solar exposure. At the 8-h irradiation endpoint, pronounced chromatic alteration was observed (Fig. 1A), while light-shielded controls have no significant change in the color. These results independently corroborate that light is the driving factor for the fading observed in commercial green tea beverages.

Colorimetric measurement results and content of tea polyphenols of samples was shown in Fig. 1. The chromaticity values showed a decrease in brightness (L*) and greenness (-a*), while the yellow (b*) hues increased. The ΔE values indicated that the average color difference gradually increased compared to the freshest sample (0 h or 0d). Within the light-exposure treatment group, a statistically significant chromatic shift (ΔE > 1) was observed after only 0.25 h of irradiation, and this color alteration progressively intensified with extended exposure time. In contrast the light-shielded controls showed minimal chromatic deviation (ΔE < 1) relative to fresh preparations. Under sustained irradiation, the tea infusions exhibited time-dependent chromatic fading, with ΔE values exceeding the perceptibility threshold (ΔE >1) within 24 h. The degradation kinetics displayed significant deceleration starting from the 9-day exposure point (p < 0.05).

To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we concurrently quantified fundamental physicochemical parameters using standardized protocols, including total tea polyphenol content (TPC), pH, and turbidity. However, there were no significant differences in the tea polyphenol content (Fig. 1), pH value and turbidity of the samples (Fig.S1.) among the light-exposed and control groups. To identify potential photodegradation markers, untargeted metabolomics was subsequently performed on light-stressed samples (from both the solar radiation and artificial light cohorts).

High-resolution mass spectrometry-based profiling identified 296 metabolites across multiple compound classes, notably encompassing amino acid derivatives, flavonoid glycosides, phenolic acids, glycerophospholipids, and short-chain organic acids. 1899, British chemist Wynter Blyth isolated a bright yellow pigment from milk, designating it as “lactochrome”, now recognized as riboflavin (Remucal & McNeill, 2011). As a water-soluble B-vitamin, riboflavin exhibits limited aqueous solubility while displaying intense yellow-green fluorescence. It primarily occurs in vegetable and animal-derived foods, functioning as a coenzyme for multiple oxidoreductases. Riboflavin demonstrates anti-inflammatory, anti-carcinogenic, and antioxidant activities, with notable thermal stability but extreme photosensitivity. It remains unstable in strong alkaline conditions or under visible / UV irradiation, undergoing irreversible photodegradation upon light exposure (Wilson, Alsalem, Berden, Oomens, & Dessent, 2025). As presented in Table 1, initial baseline riboflavin concentrations were 18.2 ± 1.4 ng/mL in the light-exposure cohort (Day 0) and 20.7 ± 0.2 ng/mL in the solar radiation-treated group (0 h). This inter-group variation is attributable to intrinsic compositional differences between production batches. Riboflavin exhibited marked photosensitivity, with no significant loss in dark controls (8 h; p > 0.05). Dose-dependent photolysis under solar radiation yielded >90% depletion at 8 h. Artificial illumination replicated this degradation profile (90% loss by D 5), followed by stabilization during extended exposure-suggesting near-complete photoconversion or depletion of photosensitive fractions. FMN — the phosphorylated bioactive derivative of riboflavin — demonstrated significant irradiation-dependent decay. Its attenuation kinetics showed strong concordance with riboflavin depletion, indicating potential shared photolytic mechanisms within the flavin redox system.

Theaflavins, first isolated from black tea by Roberts (1957), constitute a class of orange-yellow benzotropolone-structured compounds exhibiting high solubility in ethyl acetate. Characteristically, theaflavins display multiple absorption maxima at 280 nm, 380 nm, and 460 nm (Stodt & Engelhardt, 2013), typically crystallizing as orange-yellow needles. Their molecular architecture features multiple phenolic hydroxyl groups, ester linkages, and interconnected benzotropolone moieties connected via single bonds (Li, Wan, Yang, & Wang, 2006). This structural complexity confers inherent instability, rendering theaflavins susceptible to degradation by temperature, pH fluctuations, ionic strength, and atmospheric oxygen (Yu, et al., 2023). Elevated temperatures accelerate cleavage of single bonds within the extended conjugated system, significantly compromising theaflavin integrity in a temperature-dependent manner (higher temperatures → lower stability) (Yu, Qin, Sun, Sun, & Gao, 2022; Yu, Sun, Qin & Gao, 2023). The weakly acidic phenolic hydroxyl groups stabilize theaflavins under acidic-to-neutral conditions (pH 4–7). Conversely, alkaline environments (pH > 8) promote enol-to-keto tautomerization through destabilization of the enol structure, thereby reducing theaflavin content. Metal ions (e.g., Fe3+) induce chromogenic reactions with phenolic groups, disrupting native molecular conformation. While stable under ambient light across visible wavelengths, theaflavins undergo rapid decomposition in both strongly acidic (pH < 3) and alkaline conditions (pH > 9) (Cai, 2011). UPLC-MS/MS quantification (Thermo Ultimate 3000/TSQ Endura MD Plus) measured mean theaflavin concentrations of 33.34 ± 2.33 ng/mL in commercial products. Non-detection in light-stressed samples suggests either: i) storage-dependent depolymerization, ii) photolytic cleavage of the benzotropolone moiety, or iii) matrix interference from light-generated chromophores obscuring detection. Consensus metabolomic profiling identified riboflavin and theaflavin derivatives as dominant photolabile chromophores governing ΔE trajectories. Validation protocols were established through factorial supplementation of chromatographically purified standards into accelerated light-stability models, enabling deconvolution of their individual photolytic contributions via time-resolved colorimetry.

The riboflavin content in various types of teas

To investigate variations in riboflavin content among tea varieties, the riboflavin levels in ten distinct tea types were quantified. The analyzed samples comprised: BKH (Kaihua black tea), BS (Sri Lankan black tea), GKH (Kaihua green tea), YKH (Kaihua yellow tea), GDF (Green tea), GLJ (Longjing tea), SP (Raw Pu-erh), WL (Light-scented oolong), GJS (Jingshan green tea), and YKHZ (Kaihua yellow tea Z). Riboflavin (RF), also designated as vitamin B₂, constitutes an essential water-soluble micronutrient in living organisms. Its molecular architecture comprises an isoalloxazine ring system linked to a ribitol moiety. Within biological systems, RF primarily functions as the enzymatic cofactor precursors flavin mononucleotide (FMN) and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), which mediate pivotal electron transfer reactions in diverse metabolic redox pathways. The riboflavin content ranged from 762.26 ± 7.63 to 5754.60 ± 10.49 ng/g. Among the tested varieties, Kaihua black tea (BKH) exhibited the highest riboflavin concentration, followed sequentially by raw Pu-erh (SP), Kaihua yellow tea Z (YKHZ), and Kaihua yellow tea (YKH). Notably, no statistically significant difference (p > 0.05) was observed between Sri Lankan black tea (BS) and light-scented oolong (WL). Both demonstrated significantly elevated riboflavin levels compared to the four green tea varieties (GDF, GLJ, GJS, GKH) analyzed in this study. The addition rate of tea leaves in tea beverages is approximately 5 g/L. Correspondingly, the riboflavin content in beverages prepared from various tea types ranges from 3.80 to 28.77 ng/mL. This finding aligns with the theaflavin content in the reference samples provided with the tested specimens: light-exposed group (18.90 ± 0.97 ng/mL) and sun-dried group (20.68 ± 0.64 ng/mL). BKH exhibited the highest FMN concentration, with no statistically significant differences observed among other groups (p > 0.05). Similarly, FAD levels in GKH, YKH, GJS, YKHZ, and GDF were significantly elevated compared to remaining cohorts, which showed homogeneous FAD distribution without significant inter-group variations.

The observed differences in riboflavin content among tea types raise an important question regarding whether processing methods or tea cultivars play a larger role in determining initial riboflavin levels. Riboflavin is known to be relatively stable during thermal and non-thermal food processing, but highly sensitive to light. During green tea processing, the fixation step employs high temperatures to inactivate endogenous enzymes; while this thermal treatment may cause some riboflavin loss, the extent is limited given riboflavin‘s moderate thermal stability. Studies on green tea manufacturing have reported gradual decreases in riboflavin content after the steaming step, suggesting that thermal processing does contribute to riboflavin reduction, though the losses appear moderate compared to the severe photodegradation observed in finished beverages. For black tea, the fermentation step involves enzymatic oxidation of polyphenols. Riboflavin itself is not a direct substrate of polyphenol oxidase, and microbial fermentation of tea has been shown to accumulate B vitamins, including riboflavin. Therefore, the fermentation process is unlikely to cause substantial riboflavin degradation. In fact, the relatively higher riboflavin content observed in black tea compared to green tea in this study (e.g., Kaihua black tea at 5754.60 ± 10.49 ng/g vs. green tea varieties ranging from 762.26 to 1497.62 ng/g) may reflect cultivar-dependent differences rather than processing-induced losses. Yellow tea, which undergoes a post-fermentation “yellowing” step, also retained considerable riboflavin levels. Collectively, these observations suggest that tea cultivar—which determines the baseline riboflavin biosynthesis and accumulation in fresh leaves—likely plays a more dominant role than processing methods in determining the initial riboflavin content of tea infusions. Processing steps such as fixation and fermentation may cause moderate losses, but the magnitude of these losses appears smaller than the inherent variation among cultivars. This interpretation is consistent with the wide inter-cultivar variation in riboflavin content observed across the ten tea types analyzed in this study, and with previous reports that riboflavin content in tea infusions can range from 0.34 to 10.36 μg/g depending on the sample. Future studies employing the same cultivar processed into different tea types would be necessary to definitively disentangle the contributions of cultivar versus processing method.

To validate the impact of riboflavin on solar-induced discoloration of tea beverages, three tea cultivars (GKH, GLJ and SP) were formulated into experimental infusions. The beverages contained 6 g / L tea solids and incorporated standardized stabilizers: 0.03% (w/v) ascorbic acid and 0.02% (w/v) sodium bicarbonate, consistent with commercial preservation protocols for chromatic stability maintenance. Bottled tea beverages were subjected to direct solar irradiation for 1 h and 2 h (as illustrated in Fig. 4). Post-exposure samples were analyzed for chromatic variation using a calibrated colorimeter with CIE L*a*b* color space quantification. Results demonstrated significant chromatic alterations (ΔE > 1) in GLJ infusions after merely 1 h of solar exposure. SP exhibited measurable discoloration post 2-h irradiation, whereas GKH maintained remarkable photostability with negligible ΔE changes at this timepoint. Consequently, photosensitivity decreased sequentially: GLJ > SP > GKH. This inverse correlation aligned with total flavin content (riboflavin + FMN + FAD), quantified at 12.0 ± 0.37, 14.3 ± 0.44, and 15.3 ± 0.46 μg/g in GLJ, SP, and GKH respectively.

Spike recovery validation for riboflavin and theaflavin

Based on the experimental findings, the approximate contents of theaflavins and riboflavin in the tested tea beverages were 20 ng/mL and 30 ng/mL, respectively. LAB color values were measured after supplementing purified water with 1×, 2×, 10×, and 50× concentrations of: a) Theaflavins alone (WT); b) Riboflavin alone (WR); c) Theaflavin-riboflavin mixtures (WM). In Fig. 5B, the samples from left to right correspond to: (1) Ultrapure water (negative control), (2) 1000 μg/mL riboflavin aqueous solution (50× concentrated), (3) 1500 μg/mL theaflavin aqueous solution (50× concentrated), (4) Equimolar mixture of riboflavin and theaflavin (both 50× concentrated). The experimental findings demonstrate that supplementation with 2× concentration of riboflavin (40 ng/mL) induces measurable chromatic alterations in aqueous systems, whereas theaflavins supplementation alone exhibits negligible impact across all tested concentrations (ΔE < 1.0) (Fig. 5 C and 5 E). Paradoxically, theaflavin-riboflavin co-supplementation produces significant synergistic color shifts. To further validate the chromatic contributions of riboflavin and theaflavin, Kaihua green tea (GKH) was formulated into infusions. All samples subsequently underwent controlled solar irradiation for 2 h, with chromatic evolution quantified via CIE L*a*b* colorimetry. Notably, riboflavin fortification at 40 μg/mL induced significant chromatic deviation (ΔE > 1) in tea infusions, whereas equivalent theaflavin supplementation exhibited negligible impact on beverage colorimetry, as quantified in Fig. 5D. As evidenced in Fig. 4B, riboflavin-fortified aqueous solutions exhibited marked chromatic alterations after merely 1 h of solar irradiation, directly verifying the pronounced photosensitivity of riboflavin. Conversely, theaflavin-supplemented solutions maintained chromatic stability (ΔE < 0.8), indicating negligible photolytic impact of theaflavin on infusion colorimetry.

The photolytic degradation of riboflavin (vitamin B₂) proceeds via a light-driven self-oxidation cascade. Upon absorption of blue or ultraviolet light, the molecule enters an excited state and acts as a photosensitizer, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as singlet oxygen and hydroxyl radicals through electron or energy transfer (Khudyakov, 2025; Moriones et al., 2025). These highly oxidizing species then attack the molecule's own isoalloxazine ring structure, leading to side-chain cleavage and ultimate degradation into photoinactive products like lumichrome and lumiflavin. Critically, riboflavin readily forms dimers or aggregates in solution. These aggregated states exhibit a spectral red-shift, resulting in enhanced light absorption and significantly faster reaction rates, thereby functioning as “high-efficiency reaction centers” that amplify overall photolytic degradation. This insight fundamentally explains the pronounced environmental sensitivity of riboflavin's photostability at the molecular level. Consequently, riboflavin represents a primary factor driving the color fading observed in green tea beverages.

Although the main substances that contribute to the color of green tea are water-soluble pigment substances, chlorophyll, as a lipid-soluble pigment, may also be present in small amounts in the tea soup. Therefore, we further measured the chlorophyll content in the tea beverage samples. We concentrated the samples that were exposed to sunlight for 0 h, 8 h, and those exposed to light for 8 h in the dark, and determined the chlorophyll content according to the methods in the literature (Yu, 2020). The results are shown in Fig. 5F. After 8 h of sunlight exposure, chlorophyll content decreased by approximately 37.5%, whereas no significant reduction was observed in the dark-stored samples compared to the control, indicating that light directly promotes chlorophyll degradation.

The photobleaching of chlorophyll under light irradiation is fundamentally a degradation process in which its core structure is disrupted by ROS generated via photo-driven reactions (Lingvay, Akhtar, Sebok-Nagy, Pali, & Lambrev, 2020). Upon photon absorption, chlorophyll molecules are excited to higher energy states and subsequently transfer energy to ambient triplet oxygen (3O₂), producing highly reactive singlet oxygen (1O₂), which directly oxidizes the porphyrin ring system and leads to irreversible chemical modifications (Fig. 6B). These modifications include the replacement of the central magnesium ion with hydrogen ions (yielding pheophytin) and the cleavage of the conjugated double-bond system of the porphyrin ring, generating a series of hydroxylated and ring-opened small-molecular compounds that macroscopically manifest as gradual green color fading (Petrovic, Zvezdanovic, & Markovic, 2017). This process is modulated by light intensity, oxygen concentration, and the local molecular environment of chlorophyll—particularly its incorporation into lipid membranes, which affects its photostability. Given its susceptibility to photo-oxidative degradation, chlorophyll may also participate in the color changes observed in tea infusions, although its quantitative contribution requires further investigation, as only a single time-point measurement (8 h sunlight) was available in this study, and no spiking validation experiments were conducted to confirm its causal role.Fig. 6Proposed Mechanism Underlying Light-Induced Fading in Green Tea Beverages. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study systematically demonstrates that photo-induced chromatic deterioration significantly impairs the sensory quality and commercial value of green tea beverages. Compared with light-shielded controls, light exposure caused marked declines in riboflavin and theaflavin contents. Spiking experiments confirmed that riboflavin produced a pronounced synergistic color-enhancing effect with soluble tea compounds, whereas theaflavin addition showed negligible influence on liquor color. Chlorophyll, though present only in trace amounts, also underwent partial degradation under light. While riboflavin plays a central role as the primary driver of chromatic deterioration, chlorophyll may also participate in the photo-induced fading process. These findings highlight riboflavin as a key target, with chlorophyll contributing to a lesser extent, providing a theoretical basis for developing targeted color-protection strategies, including riboflavin stabilization (e.g., with antioxidants such as ascorbic acid) and chlorophyll microencapsulation. Complementary practical measures, such as optimizing raw leaf tea selection, employing light-blocking PET bottles, utilizing light-filtering labels, and refining shelf-lighting conditions, may further improve storage stability.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although preliminary experiments confirmed the universal occurrence of photo-induced fading across multiple brands of unsweetened green tea beverages and in laboratory-prepared samples, the quantitative degradation rates and the specific contributions of individual formulation variables (e.g., pH, antioxidant additives, polyphenol concentrations) have not been systematically characterized. Moreover, the photosensitivity of tea pigments may vary across different tea types (e.g., oolong tea, black tea), and the proposed reaction pathways were inferred from metabolomic data and literature, requiring direct experimental validation. Future research should systematically examine how formulation parameters modulate photodegradation kinetics, develop nano-encapsulation for chlorophyll stabilization, compare photosensitivity across tea types, and elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying riboflavin's synergistic color-enhancing effect, thereby facilitating the development of more robust and broadly applicable color-protection strategies for the tea beverage industry.
