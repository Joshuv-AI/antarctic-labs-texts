Lorentzian polynomials and matroids over triangular hyperfields Part 1: Topological aspects
Matthew Baker, June Huh, Mario Kummer, Oliver Lorscheid

Abstract
. Lorentzian polynomials serve as a bridge between continuous and discrete convexity, connecting analysis and combinatorics. In this article, we study the topology of the space ℙ ​ L J {\mathbb{P}}\upL_{J} of Lorentzian polynomials on J J modulo ℝ > 0 \mathbb{R}_{>0} , which is nonempty if and only if J J is the set of bases of a polymatroid. We prove that ℙ ​ L J {\mathbb{P}}\upL_{J} is a manifold with boundary of dimension equal to the Tutte rank of J J , and more precisely, that it is homeomorphic to a closed Euclidean ball with the Dressian of J J removed from its boundary. Furthermore, we show that ℙ ​ L J {\mathbb{P}}\upL_{J} is homeomorphic to the thin Schubert cell Gr J ⁡ ( 𝕋 q ) \Gr_{J}({\mathbb{T}}_{q}) of J J over the triangular hyperfield 𝕋 q {\mathbb{T}}_{q} , introduced by Viro in the context of tropical geometry and Maslov dequantization, for any positive real number q q . This identification enables us to apply the representation theory of polymatroids developed in the companion paper [ 4 ] , as well as earlier work by the first and fourth authors on foundations of matroids, to give a simple explicit description of ℙ ​ L J {\mathbb{P}}\upL_{J} up to homeomorphism in several key cases. Our results show that ℙ ​ L J {\mathbb{P}}\upL_{J} always admits a compactification homeomorphic to a closed Euclidean ball. They can also be used to answer a question of Brändén in the negative by showing that the closure of ℙ ​ L J {\mathbb{P}}\upL_{J} within the space of all polynomials modulo ℝ > 0 \mathbb{R}_{>0} is not homeomorphic to a closed Euclidean ball in general. In addition, we introduce the Hausdorff compactification of the space of rescaling classes of Lorentzian polynomials and show that the Chow quotient of a complex Grassmannian maps naturally to this compactification. This provides a geometric framework that connects the asymptotic structure of the space of Lorentzian polynomials with classical constructions in algebraic geometry.

Introduction

In
[
15
]
, Brändén and the second author introduced the notion of
Lorentzian polynomials
and used it to establish the log-concavity of various sequences of combinatorial origin.
Lorentzian polynomials serve as a bridge between continuous and discrete convexity, connecting analysis and combinatorics.
Among their many applications to combinatorics, Lorentzian polynomials were used by Brändén–Huh
[
15
]
, and independently Anari et al.

[

If

[n]

If we assume the support of the polynomials in question to be
square-free

The

. In general, a homogeneous polynomial

with nonnegative coefficients is Lorentzian if and only if the partial derivative

is identically zero or log-concave on

for all
\myalpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0}
. See
Section

4.1
for an extended discussion in the general setting. Our primary goal in this paper is to study the topology of the space of Lorentzian polynomials with a given support. Throughout this introduction, we focus on the case of square-free support for expositional simplicity.

.

A fundamental result on the space of Lorentzian polynomials is the following statement due to Brändén

Theorem 1.1

The spaces

Our main goal is to relate spaces of Lorentzian polynomials to various Grassmannians. A first instance of such a connection goes back to
[

Let

Therefore, we obtain a continuous map

(1)

\Gr(d,n)({\mathbb{C}})\longrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\upL(d,n)_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boxtimes$}},\qquad(p_{S})\longmapsto\sum_{S}|p_{S}|^{2}\,x^{S}.

We study this map in detail in

. For example, when

\Gr(2,4)({\mathbb{R}})\longrightarrow\partial{\mathbb{P}}\upL(2,4)_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boxtimes$}}

1.1.
Thin Schubert cells

Let
{\mathcal{B}}(M)
be a collection of
d
-element subsets of
[n]
, viewed as a collection of degree
d
square-free monomials in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
.
We set

\upH_{M}\coloneq\big\{f\in\upH(d,n)\mid\text{the support of $f$ is ${\mathcal{B}}(M)$}\big\}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \upL_{M}\coloneq\upL(d,n)\cap\upH_{M}.

One of the basic results proved in
[
15
]
hints at a surprising link between the continuous and the discrete world: The set
\upL_{M}
is nonempty if and only if
{\mathcal{B}}(M)
is the set of bases of a
matroid
of rank
d
on
[n]
.
2
2

2

A matroid
M
on
[n]
is given by a nonempty collection
{\mathcal{B}}(M)
of subsets of
[n]
satisfying the
symmetric exchange property
: For any
B_{1},B_{2}\in{\mathcal{B}}(M)
and any
b_{1}\in B_{1}\backslash B_{2}
, there is
b_{2}\in B_{2}\backslash B_{1}
such that both
B_{1}\backslash\{b_{1}\}\cup\{b_{2}\}
and
B_{2}\backslash\{b_{2}\}\cup\{b_{1}\}
belong to
{\mathcal{B}}(M)
. Members of the collection
{\mathcal{B}}(M)
are called
bases
of the matroid
M
. The symmetric exchange property implies that any two bases have the same cardinality, called the
rank
of the matroid. For general introduction to matroids, we refer to
[
45
]
.

We regard
{\mathbb{P}}\upL_{M}
as the analogue of a
thin Schubert cell
(or
matroid stratum
) in
{\mathbb{P}}\upL(d,n)_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boxtimes$}}
. While a thin Schubert cell in a Grassmannian over a field can be empty, depending on whether or not the matroid is representable over that field, every matroid occurs as the support of some Lorentzian polynomial. In fact, the generating polynomial
\sum_{B\in{\mathcal{B}}(M)}x^{B}
is a Lorentzian polynomial if and only if
{\mathcal{B}}(M)
is the set of bases of a matroid
[
15
]
*Theorem 3.10. Our first result is the following:

Theorem 1.3

(
Section

4.4
)
.

For every matroid
M
, the space
{\mathbb{P}}\upL_{M}
is a manifold with boundary.

This should be contrasted with the various
universality theorems
for thin Schubert cells in Grassmannians over a field, which roughly state that such thin Schubert cells can exhibit arbitrary singularities
[
38
,
53
,
39
]
.
In fact, we can explicitly describe
{\mathbb{P}}\upL_{M}
up to homeomorphism. In order to formulate the result, we recall from
[
15
]
the connection between Lorentzian polynomials and the
Dressian
, the piecewise-linear space of all rank
d
valuated matroids on
[n]
which plays the role of the Grassmannian in tropical geometry.

Example 1.4
.

Let
M
be a matroid of rank
d
on
[n]
with the set of bases
{\mathcal{B}}(M)
. A function
\mynu\colon{\mathcal{B}}(M)\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a
valuated matroid
if for any
B_{1},B_{2}\in{\mathcal{B}}(M)
and any
b_{1}\in B_{1}\backslash B_{2}
, there is
b_{2}\in B_{2}\backslash B_{1}
such that

\mynu(B_{1})+\mynu(B_{2})\leqslant\mynu(B_{1}\backslash\{b_{1}\}\cup\{b_{2}\})+\mynu(B_{2}\backslash\{b_{2}\}\cup\{b_{1}\}).

The
Dressian

\Dr_{M}
of
M
is the set of all valuated matroids
\mynu\colon{\mathcal{B}}(M)\to{\mathbb{R}}
modulo addition of constant functions on
{\mathcal{B}}(M)
.
If
\mynu\in\Dr_{M}
, then
f_{\mynu}\coloneq\sum_{B\in{\mathcal{B}}(M)}e^{-\mynu(B)}\,x^{B}
is a Lorentzian polynomial
[
15
]
*Theorem 3.14. Therefore, we obtain a continuous injective map

(2)

\exp\colon\Dr_{M}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\upL_{M},\qquad\mynu\longmapsto f_{\mynu}.

Clearly, we have
t\mynu\in\Dr_{M}
for any
t>0
and any
\mynu\in\Dr_{M}
. Moreover, if
\mynu
is not constant, then the limit of
f_{t\mynu}
as
t
goes to infinity is a Lorentzian polynomial with support strictly smaller than
{\mathcal{B}}(M)
, and hence it is not an element of
{\mathbb{P}}\upL_{M}
. In a sense, this the only obstruction to
{\mathbb{P}}\upL_{M}
being compact:

Theorem 1.5

(
4.19
)
.

The space
{\mathbb{P}}\upL_{M}
can be compactified to a closed Euclidean ball by adding a point for each ray contained in the Dressian
\Dr_{M}
.

We also compute the dimension of
{\mathbb{P}}\upL_{M}
in terms of
M
. An obvious upper bound is the ambient dimension
|{\mathcal{B}}(M)|-1
, which is not tight in general. The following constraint is analogous to the corresponding statement of Brändén on stable polynomials
[
16
]
*Lemma 6.1:

Lemma 1.6

(
Section

4.1
)
.

Let
f=\sum_{B}p_{B}\,x^{B}
be a degree
d
Lorentzian polynomial with square-free support.
Let
S
be a set of cardinality
d-2
in
[n]
, and let

be pairwise distinct elements of
[n]
not in
S
. If one of the three terms

(3)

p_{S\cup\{i,j\}}p_{S\cup\{k,l\}},\,\,p_{S\cup\{i,k\}}p_{S\cup\{j,l\}},\,\,p_{S\cup\{i,l\}}p_{S\cup\{j,k\}}

is zero, then the other two are equal.

Note that the three terms in

correspond to the monomial terms in the

for Grassmannians, which shows immediately that

1.1

1
.
We also note that the conditions from

Theorem 1.7

(

.

Let
\upB\subseteq V_{M}/\mathbf{1}

is homeomorphic to the space

All results outlined here can be straight-forwardly extended to the general case of Lorentzian polynomials with a support that is not necessarily square-free,

1.2.
Grassmannians over triangular hyperfields

In order to make precise the intuition that the space of Lorentzian polynomials is like a Grassmannian, we recall from
[
3
]
that there is a natural way to generalize the notion of a field by relaxing the requirement that addition is a binary operation; such generalized fields are called
tracts
.

The definition of a tract will be given in
Section

3.1
. For now it is enough to know that a tract
F
consists of a multiplicative abelian group
F^{\times}
, together with the
null set
of “additive relations” of the form
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{k}=0
with
a_{i}\in F=F^{\times}\cup\{0\}
.

Given nonnegative integers
d\leq n
and a tract
F
, we define the
F
-
Grassmannian

\Gr(d,n)(F)
as the set of solutions in
F^{\binom{n}{d}}/F^{\times}
to the
Plücker equations
: For any set
S_{1}
of cardinality
d-1
in
[n]
and any set
S_{2}
of cardinality
d+1
in
[n]
, we have

\sum_{x\in S_{2}\backslash S_{1}}\textrm{sign}(x)p_{S_{1}\cup\{x\}}p_{S_{2}\backslash\{x\}}=0,

where
\textrm{sign}(x)=\pm 1
is determined by the parity of
\#\{x<s\in S_{1}\}+\#\{x<s\in S_{2}\}
, see
Section

3.3
.
When
F=K
is a field, this recovers the usual Grassmannian
\Gr(d,n)(K)
over
K
, parametrizing
d
-dimensional subspaces of
K^{n}
. Similarly, for a matroid
M
of degree
d
on
[n]
, one defines the
thin Schubert cell

\Gr_{M}(F)
to be the subset of
\Gr(d,n)(F)
consisting of points with support
{\mathcal{B}}(M)
, which is naturally embedded into
(F^{\times})^{{\mathcal{B}}(M)}/F^{\times}
.
If the tract
F
carries a topology, this induces a natural topology on
\Gr(d,n)(F)
and
\Gr_{M}(F)
, see
[
4
]
*Section 12 and
[
6
]
.

As an algebraic framework for Maslov dequantization, Viro defines in
[
55
,
56
]
a family
{\mathbb{T}}_{q}
of tracts indexed by a positive real number
q
, called
triangular hyperfields
. See
Section

3.5
for their relation to amoebas and tropicalizations. The multiplicative group
{\mathbb{T}}_{q}^{\times}
of
{\mathbb{T}}_{q}
is
({\mathbb{R}}_{>0},\cdot)
for all
q>0
, and for
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\geqslant 0
, we have
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{k}=0
in
{\mathbb{T}}_{q}
if and only if
a_{1}^{1/q},\ldots,a_{k}^{1/q}
form the side lengths of a (possibly degenerate) convex
k
-gon. So, for example,
a+b+c=0
in
{\mathbb{T}}_{1}
if and only if
a,b,c
form the side lengths of a possibly degenerate Euclidean triangle (hence the name “triangular hyperfield”), and, up to a positive multiple,
\Gr(2,4)({\mathbb{T}}_{1})
is
the set of nonnegative real numbers
(p_{12},p_{13},p_{14},p_{23},p_{24},p_{34})
such that

p_{ij}p_{kl}+p_{ik}p_{jl}\geq p_{il}p_{jk}\ \ \text{for any $i,j,k,l$.}

This family of tracts has two limit objects
{\mathbb{T}}_{0}
(the
tropical hyperfield
) and
{\mathbb{T}}_{\infty}
(the
degenerate triangular hyperfield
), whose ground sets are also
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
with the usual multiplicative structure. The additive relations are given as follows:

(1)

a_{1}+\cdots+a_{k}=0
holds in
{\mathbb{T}}_{0}
if and only if it holds in all
{\mathbb{T}}_{q}
with
q>0
.

(2)

a_{1}+\cdots+a_{k}=0
holds in
{\mathbb{T}}_{\infty}
if and only if it holds in some
{\mathbb{T}}_{q}
with
q>0
.

See
Section

3.2
for alternative descriptions of the null sets of
{\mathbb{T}}_{0}
and
{\mathbb{T}}_{\infty}
.
The topology on
{\mathbb{T}}_{q}
is defined as the Euclidean topology on
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
.

In the previous subsection, we have already seen the Grassmannians over
{\mathbb{T}}_{0}
and
{\mathbb{T}}_{\infty}
in disguise. Namely, the coordinate-wise logarithm map

{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathcal{B}}(M)}_{>0}/{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathcal{B}}(M)}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
takes
\Gr_{M}({\mathbb{T}}_{0})
to

and
\Gr_{M}({\mathbb{T}}_{\infty})
to the vector space

. The statements of

1.1
and
Section

1.1

Theorem 1.8
.

For every matroid
M
, there are natural inclusions

(4)

\Gr_{M}({\mathbb{T}}_{0})\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\upL_{M}\hookrightarrow\Gr_{M}({\mathbb{T}}_{\infty}).

. We will explain some consequences in the next subsection.

Theorem 1.9

(

)
.

For every positive real number

We have
{\mathbb{P}}\upL(d,n)_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boxtimes$}}=\mycoprod_{M}{\mathbb{P}}\upL_{M}
and

.

For every matroid

on

2.
Elementary properties of star-shaped sets

We fix a finite dimensional
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space
V
equipped with a norm
\|\cdot\|
.

Recall that a triple
(x_{*},X,V)
, where
X\subseteq V
and
x_{*}\in X
, is called
star-shaped
if for every
x\in X
and every
t\in[0,1]
, the point
x_{*}+t\cdot(x-x_{*})
lies in
X
.

Definition 2.1
.

A triple
(x_{*},X,V)
, where
X\subseteq V
and
x_{*}\in X
, is called
strongly star-shaped
if
X
is closed in
V
and if for every
x\in X
and every
t\in[0,1)
, the point
x_{*}+t\cdot(x-x_{*})
lies in the interior of
X
(with respect to the topology of
V
).

Note that
(x_{*},X,V)
being strongly star-shaped implies in particular that
x_{*}
is in the interior of
X
. Our first goal is to show that if
(x_{*},X,V)
is strongly star-shaped, then it is homeomorphic to an open subset of a Euclidean ball.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(x_{*},X,V)
be strongly star-shaped.
The following map is continuous:

\mypsi\colon V\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0},\qquad x\longmapsto\inf\big\{t>0\,\big|\,x_{*}+\frac{x-x_{*}}{t}\in X\big\}.

Furthermore, we have for all
x\in X
:

(1)

\mypsi(x)\leqslant 1
.

(2)

If
\mypsi(x)>0
, then
x_{*}+\frac{x-x_{*}}{\mypsi(x)}\in X
.

(3)

If
\mypsi(x)=0
, then
x_{*}+s\cdot(x-x_{*})
is in the interior of
X
for all
s\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
x_{*}=0
is the origin.
In order to prove that
\mypsi
is continuous, let
(x_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subseteq V
be a sequence converging to
x_{0}\in V
. We first let
t_{0}>\liminf_{i\to\infty}\mypsi(x_{i})
. Then there is a subsequence
(y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of
(x_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and a sequence
(t_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
with
t_{i}>\mypsi(y_{i})
for all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
which converges to
t_{0}
. Then the sequence
(\frac{y_{i}}{t_{i}})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is in
X
and converges to
\frac{x_{0}}{t_{0}}
, which is therefore also in
X
. Thus
t_{0}\geqslant\mypsi(x_{0})
, which implies
\liminf_{i\to\infty}\mypsi(x_{i})\geqslant\mypsi(x_{0})
.
Next we prove that
\limsup_{i\to\infty}\mypsi(x_{i})\leqslant\mypsi(x_{0})
. Let
t_{0}>\mypsi(x_{0})
. Then
\frac{x_{0}}{t_{0}}
is in the interior of
X
. If
U\subseteq X
is an open neighbourhood of
\frac{x_{0}}{t_{0}}
, then
U^{\prime}=t_{0}\cdot U
is an open neighbourhood of
x_{0}
such that
\mypsi(x)\leqslant t_{0}
for all
x\in U^{\prime}
. This implies that
\limsup_{i\to\infty}\mypsi(x_{i})\leqslant\mypsi(x_{0})
.

The first of the three additional statements is trivial, the second follows because
X
is closed, and the third follows from the definition of a strongly star-shaped set.
∎

We denote by
S
the unit sphere in
V
around the origin. Let
(x_{*},X,V)
be strongly star-shaped and consider the following set of directions:

S(x_{*},X)=\{x\in S\mid\mypsi(x_{*}+x)=0\}.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
\upB\subseteq V
be the closed unit ball. Then
X
is homeomorphic to
\upB\backslash S(x_{*},X)
.

Proof.

After replacing
X
by
X-x_{*}
we can assume without loss of generality that
x_{*}
is the origin.
We consider the map

\myvarphi\colon\upB\backslash S(0,X)\longrightarrow X,\qquad x\longmapsto\frac{x}{1+\mypsi(x)-\|x\|},

which is continuous by
Section

2
. Its inverse is given by the continuous map

\myphi\colon X\longrightarrow\upB\backslash S(0,X),\qquad x\longmapsto\frac{x}{1-\mypsi(x)+\|x\|}.\qed

Corollary 2.4
.

Every strongly star-shaped set is a topological manifold with boundary.

Proof.

Let
(x_{*},X,V)
be a strongly star-shaped. The set
S(x_{*},X)
is closed because
\mypsi
is continuous. Therefore,
Section

2
shows that
X
is homeomorphic to an open subset of the closed unit ball, and thus to a manifold with boundary.
∎

Finally, we provide some criteria to show that a set is strongly star-shaped.

Lemma 2.5
.

Assume that
V
is contained in a finite dimensional
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space
U
. Let
U_{i}
for
i\in[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
be some further finite dimensional
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector spaces. Let
\mypi_{i}\colon U\to U_{i}
be linear maps and
V_{i}\subseteq U_{i}
linear subspaces such that
V=\cap_{i=1}^{n}\mypi_{i}^{-1}(V_{i})
. Furthermore, let
X_{i}\subseteq V_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
be subsets and set
X=\cap_{i=1}^{n}\mypi_{i}^{-1}(X_{i})
. Finally, let
x_{*}\in V
be such that
(\mypi_{i}(x_{*}),X_{i},V_{i})
is strongly star-shaped for all
i\in[n]
. Then
(x_{*},X,V)
is strongly star-shaped.

Proof.

Let
x\in X
and
t\in[0,1)
. Because
(\mypi_{i}(x_{*}),X_{i},V_{i})
is strongly star-shaped, it follows that
\mypi_{i}(x_{*}+t\cdot(x-x_{*}))
lies in the interior (relative to
V_{i}
) of
X_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
. Since
V=\cap_{i=1}^{n}\mypi_{i}^{-1}(V_{i})
and
X=\cap_{i=1}^{n}\mypi_{i}^{-1}(X_{i})
, this implies that
x_{*}+t\cdot(x-x_{*})
lies in the interior (relative to
V
) of
X
.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Consider the triple
(x_{*},X,V)
consisting of a closed subset
X\subseteq V
which is star-shaped with respect to a point
x_{*}
in the interior of
X
. Assume that for every
x\in X
, there exists
0<t_{0}<1
such that for every
t\in(t_{0},1)
, the point
x_{*}+t\cdot(x-x_{*})
lies in the interior of
X
. Then
(x_{*},X,V)
is strongly star-shaped.

Proof.

Let
x\in X
and
t\in[0,1)
. We need to prove that the point
x_{1}=x_{*}+t\cdot(x-x_{*})
lies in the interior of
X
. If
t=0
, then we are done because
x_{*}
is in the interior of
X
. If
0<t<1
, then by assumption there exists
c\geqslant 1
such that
x_{c}=x_{*}+c\cdot t\cdot(x-x_{*})
lies in the interior of
X
. Let
U\subseteq X
be an open neighbourhood of
x_{c}
. Then the set

\Big\{x_{*}+\frac{1}{c}(y-x_{*})\mid y\in U\Big\}

is an open neighbourhood of
x_{1}
which is contained in
X
(because
X
is star-shaped).
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
X\subseteq V
be a closed subset and
W\subseteq V
a linear subspace such that
W+X=X
. For every
x_{*}\in X
and
w\in W
,
(x_{*},X,V)
is strongly star-shaped if and only if
(w+x_{*},X,V)
is strongly star-shaped.

Proof.

This follows directly from the definitions.
∎

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
X\subseteq V
be a closed subset and
W\subseteq V
a linear subspace such that
W+X=X
, and consider the projection
\mypi\colon V\to V/W
. We choose some norm on
V/W
. Let
x_{*}\in X
be such that
(x_{*},X,V)
is strongly star-shaped. Then:

(1)

(\mypi(x_{*}),\mypi(X),V/W)
is strongly star-shaped.

(2)

The set of directions
S(\mypi(x_{*}),\mypi(X))
is equal to

\big\{\mypi(x)\in S\subseteq V/W\mid\forall t\geqslant 0:x_{*}+tx\in X\big\},

where
S
is the unit sphere in
V/W
.

Proof.

It follows from
W+X=X
that
\mypi(X)
is closed. Let
x\in X
and
0\leqslant t<1
. Then
x_{*}+t(x-x_{*})
is in the interior of
X
. Thus
\mypi(x_{*})+t(\mypi(x)-\mypi(x_{*}))
is in the interior of
\mypi(X)
, since
\mypi
is open. This establishes part (1).

For part (2), the inclusion
\supset
is clear. For the other inclusion, let
x\in V
such that
\mypi(x)\in S(\mypi(x_{*}),\mypi(X))
. Then for all
t\geqslant 0
, there is
x_{t}\in X
such that
\mypi(x_{*})+t\mypi(x)=\mypi(x_{t})
. This is equivalent to
x_{*}+tx=x_{t}+w_{t}
for some
w_{t}\in W=\ker(\mypi)
. Because
W+X=X
, this implies the claim.
∎

3.
The topology of representation spaces over triangular hyperfields

We first recall some definitions and results on polymatroid representations over tracts.

3.1.
Definition of tracts

A
pointed monoid
is a multiplicatively written commutative monoid
F
with unit
1
and a distinguished element
0
that satisfies
0\cdot a=0
for all
a\in F
. The
unit group of
F
is the group

F^{\times}\ =\ \{a\in F\mid ab=1\text{ for some }b\in F\}

of all invertible elements in
F
. A
pointed group
is a pointed monoid
F
such that
F^{\times}=F-\{0\}
. The
ambient semiring
of a pointed group
F
is the group semiring

F^{+}\ =\ {\mathbb{N}}[F^{\times}].

We denote its elements by
\sum n_{a}.a
(where
n_{a}\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
n_{a}=0
for all but finitely many
a\in A
),
n_{1}.a_{1}+\dotsb+n_{r}.a_{r}
, or
\sum a_{i}
(where
a
appears
n_{a}
times as a summand). The pointed group
F
embeds into
F^{+}
by sending
0
to the additively neutral element of
F^{+}
and
a\in F^{\times}
to
a=1.a\in F^{+}
. An
ideal of
F^{+}
is a subset that contains
0
and is closed under addition and under multiplication by elements of
F^{+}
.

Definition 3.1
.

A
tract
3
3

3

What we call a tract in this text is, in the language of
[
9
]
, an
ideal tract
or an
idyll
.
is a pointed group
F
together with an ideal
N_{F}
of
F^{+}
, called the
null set of
F
, such that for every
a\in F
there is a unique
b\in F
with
a+b\in N_{F}
.
A
homomorphism of tracts
is a multiplicative map
f\colon F_{1}\to F_{2}
with
f(0)=0
and
f(1)=1
such that the induced map
F_{1}^{+}\to F_{2}^{+}
sends every element of
N_{F_{1}}
to an element of
N_{F_{2}}
.

We write
-a
for the unique element
b
with
a+b\in N_{F}
and call it the
additive inverse of
a
, and we use
a-b
for
a+(-b)
. Typically, we denote a tract by
F
and suppress its null set
N_{F}
from the notation.

Example 3.2
.

The most basic example of a tract is a field
F
, which is a pointed group with respect to multiplication, with null set
N_{F}
consisting of all formal sums of elements that sum to zero in
F
.

3.2.
Triangular hyperfields

Viro introduces triangular hyperfields in
[
55
,
56
]
as an algebraic framework for Maslov dequantization, which we review in
Section

3.5
. In this text, we consider triangular hyperfields (whose definition we give in
Section

3.2
) as tracts.

For
a,b\geqslant 0
, we define

a\boxplus_{q}b=\big\{c\geqslant 0\mid|a^{1/q}-b^{1/q}|\leqslant c^{1/q}\leqslant a^{1/q}+b^{1/q}\big\}.

In other words,
c\in a\boxplus_{q}b
if and only if
a^{1/q}
,
b^{1/q}
and
c^{1/q}
are the side lengths of a Euclidean triangle.

If
A,B
are subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
, we define

A\boxplus_{q}B=\bigcup_{a\in A,\,b\in B}a\boxplus_{q}b.

It was shown in
[
55
]
*§5 that
\boxplus_{q}
, together with the usual multiplication on
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
, defines a so-called
hyperfield
. We will not define hyperfields here, but only note the following consequence of the definition:

Lemma 3.3
.

The pointed group
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
, together with the null set

N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{q}}=\big\{0\big\}\cup\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\mid n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{\geqslant 2}\mathrm{\;and\;}0\in a_{1}\boxplus_{q}\cdots\boxplus_{q}a_{n}\big\},

is a tract, called the
triangular hyperfield

{\mathbb{T}}_{q}
.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that
\boxplus_{q}
, together with the usual multiplication on
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
, defines a hyperfield (cf.
[
55
]
*Section 5,
[
3
]
*Section 2.3, and
[
9
]
*Theorem 2.21). It can also be verified directly without any difficulties.
∎

In order to avoid confusion with the usual addition of nonnegative real numbers, we will from now on denote addition in the group semiring
{\mathbb{T}}_{q}^{+}
by
+_{q}
.

Remark 3.4
.

For all
a\geqslant 0
and
q>0
, we have
a+_{q}a\in N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{q}}
. Furthermore, we have
a_{1}+_{q}\cdots+_{q}a_{n}\in N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{q}}
if and only if
a_{1}^{1/q}+_{1}\cdots+_{1}a_{n}^{1/q}\in N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{1}}
.

We will also make use of the following description of elements of length larger than three in the null set of
{\mathbb{T}}_{1}
. Recall that a
cyclic
n
-gon
is an
n
-gon whose vertices lie on a circle in the Euclidean plane.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

a_{1}+_{1}\cdots+_{1}a_{n}\in N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{1}}
;

(2)

a_{i}\leqslant\sum_{j\neq i}a_{j}
for all
i=1,\dotsc,n
;

(3)

a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n}
are the side-lengths (proceeding clockwise from a fixed vertex) of a (possibly degenerate)
n
-gon in the Euclidean plane;

(4)

a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n}
are the side-lengths (proceeding clockwise from a fixed vertex) of a (possibly degenerate) convex
n
-gon in the Euclidean plane;

(5)

a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n}
are the side-lengths (proceeding clockwise from a fixed vertex) of a (possibly degenerate) cyclic
n
-gon in the Euclidean plane.

Proof.

It is clear that (
5
) implies (
4
), which implies (
3
).
And, since the shortest distance between two points is a straight line, (
3
) implies (
2
).
Moreover, it is proved in
[
47
]
*Theorem 6.2 (see also
[
51
]
) that (
5
) is equivalent to (
2
). Therefore (
2
), (
3
), (
4
), and (
5
) are all equivalent.
(We note that one can show in a much simpler and more direct way that (
2
) implies (
3
), and with a bit of additional work that (
2
) implies (
4
).)

So it suffices to show that (
1
) is equivalent to (
2
). For this, we use the elementary observation (cf.
[
11
]
*Proposition 1.10 and Lemma 2.3) that if
H
is a hyperfield with hyperaddition
\boxplus
, we have
0\in a_{1}\boxplus\cdots\boxplus a_{n}
iff there exists
a\in H
such that
0\in a_{1}\boxplus a_{2}\boxplus\cdots\boxplus a_{n-2}\boxplus a
and
0\in a_{n-1}\boxplus a_{n}\boxplus(-a)
.
Since
-1=1
in
{\mathbb{T}}_{1}
, it follows that
a_{1}+_{1}\cdots+_{1}a_{n}\in N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{1}}
iff there exists
a\in{\mathbb{T}}_{1}
such that
a_{1}+_{1}a_{2}+_{1}\cdots+_{1}a_{n-2}+_{1}a\in N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{1}}
and
a_{n-1}+_{1}a_{n}+_{1}a\in N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{1}}
. (A tract with this property is called a

in
[
11
]
.)

We know that (
1
) is equivalent to (
2
) when
n=3
, so we may assume that

. Assume (
1
) holds. Then there exists
a\in{\mathbb{T}}_{1}
such that
a_{1}+_{1}a_{2}+_{1}\cdots+_{1}a_{n-2}+_{1}a\in N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{1}}
and
a_{n-1}+_{1}a_{n}+_{1}a\in N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{1}}
. By induction, we may assume that
a_{i}\leqslant\sum_{j\neq i,j\leqslant n-2}a_{j}+a
for all

, and
a\leqslant a_{n-1}+a_{n}
, and therefore (
2
) holds.

Conversely, assume (
2
) holds. One can give an elementary direct algebraic proof of (
1
), but it is perhaps more enlightening to proceed as follows. Since (
2
) implies (
4
), there is a convex
n
-gon

with side lengths

.
Then
a_{n-1}
and
a_{n}
are consecutive side lengths in

, and we draw a diagonal of

of length

which splits

into a triangle with side lengths
a,a_{n-1}
, and
a_{n}
and a convex
(n-1)
-gon

with side lengths
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-2}
and
a
. Since these side lengths satisfy (
2
), it follows by induction that both
a_{n-1}+_{1}a_{n}+_{1}a
and
a_{1}+_{1}a_{2}+_{1}\cdots+_{1}a_{n-2}+_{1}a
belong to

. Thus
a_{1}+_{1}\cdots+_{1}a_{n}\in N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{1}}
as desired.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Penner also proves in

Lemma 3.7
.

Let

,
0\leqslant q<1
, and

. Then

(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n})^{q}<x_{1}^{q}+\cdots+x_{n}^{q}.

Proof.

By induction, it suffices to prove the case

. We have

(x_{1}+x_{2})^{q}\ =\ \frac{x_{1}}{x_{1}+x_{2}}\cdot(x_{1}+x_{2})^{q}\ +\ \frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}+x_{2}}\cdot(x_{1}+x_{2})^{q}\\[5.0pt]
=\ \Big(\frac{x_{1}}{x_{1}+x_{2}}\Big)^{1-q}\cdot x_{1}^{q}\ +\ \Big(\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}+x_{2}}\Big)^{1-q}\cdot x_{2}^{q}\ <\ x_{1}^{q}+x_{2}^{q},

where the last the inequality follows because

∎

Corollary 3.8
.

Let

. Then
N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{p}}\subseteq N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{q}}
as subsets of
{\mathbb{N}}[{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}]
.

Proof.

The claim is clear for

, so we may assume that

. Consider
\sum a_{i}\in N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{p}}
, i.e.
a_{j}^{1/p}\leqslant\sum_{i\neq j}a_{i}^{1/p}
for all

, i.e.,

and

We define the

as the tract

with null set

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\geqslant 0
and let

. Then:

(1)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\in N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{0}}
if and only if the sum is identically zero or the maximum among
a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n}
appears at least twice.

(2)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\in N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{\infty}}
if and only if the sum is identically zero, the maximum among
a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n}
appears at least twice, or at least three of the

are nonzero.

Proof.

For part (
1
), assume that
a_{1}>a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}
. Then
\frac{a_{2}}{a_{1}},\cdots,\frac{a_{n}}{a_{1}}<1
and there exists
p>0
small enough such that
\left(\frac{a_{i}}{a_{1}}\right)^{1/p}<\frac{1}{n}
for all

. It follows that
a_{1}^{1/p}>a_{2}^{1/p}+\cdots+a_{n}^{1/p}
, contradicting (1) for

by
Section

3.2
. The other direction of (
1
) follows directly from
Section

3.2
.

The “only if” direction of part (
2
) is clear. For the other direction, it suffices to prove
a_{1}^{1/p}\leqslant a_{2}^{1/p}+\cdots+a_{n}^{1/p}
for a suitable

. We can further assume that
a_{1}>a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}
. Again, this implies
\frac{a_{2}}{a_{1}},\cdots,\frac{a_{n}}{a_{1}}<1
and there exists
p>0
large enough such that
\left(\frac{a_{i}}{a_{1}}\right)^{1/p}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
for all

with

. Since, by assumption, this is the case for at least two

, the claim follows.
∎

Finally, we study tract homomorphisms into triangular hyperfields. The situation is particularly simple for the degenerate triangular hyperfield

:

Example 3.10
.

Let
F
be a tract and

a group homomorphism. We claim that the induced map

) of

, this follows from

being torsion-free. Therefore, tract homomorphisms

Next we study tract homomorphisms

Lemma 3.11
.

For

, define

. The following holds for all

(1)

If

, then

(2)

Conversely, if

with

\mypsi_{t}(a_{1})+_{q}\mypsi_{t}(a_{2})+_{q}\mypsi_{t}(a_{3})\not\in N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{q}}

The case

.
It is clear that

3.3.
Polymatroid representations

There are several cryptomorphic definitions of an (integral) polymatroid
[
57
,
44
,
24
]
. For us, a polymatroid will always be given by its set of bases: A subset
J\subseteq\Delta_{n}^{d}=\{\myalpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}\mid\myalpha_{1}+\dotsb+\myalpha_{n}=d\}
is the set of bases of a polymatroid if and only it is M-convex in the following sense.

Definition 3.12
.

A subset
J
of
\Delta^{d}_{n}
is
M-convex
if
J\neq\varnothing
and for all
\myalpha,\mybeta\in J
and every
i\in[n]
with
\myalpha_{i}<\mybeta_{i}
, there exists
j\in[n]
such that
\myalpha_{j}>\mybeta_{j}
and
J
contains both
\myalpha+e_{i}-e_{j}
and
\mybeta-e_{i}+e_{j}
. The
rank
of the M-convex set
J
is
r
.

Example 3.13
.

If we identify a subset of
[n]
with its indicator function, regarded as an element of
\{0,1\}^{n}
, then the set of bases of a rank
d
matroid on
[n]
is an M-convex set in
\Delta_{n}^{d}
.

Recall the following definitions from
[
4
]
. We consider
{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
as a partially ordered set with respect to the partial order
\myalpha\leqslant\mybeta
if and only if
\myalpha_{i}\leqslant\mybeta_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
.

Definition 3.14
.

Let
J\subseteq\Delta_{n}^{d}
be an M-convex set. We define
\mydelta^{-}_{J}=\inf(J)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
to be the vector whose
i
-th coordinate is
\textup{min}\{\myalpha_{i}\mid\myalpha\in J\}
. Similarly, we define
\mydelta^{+}_{J}=\sup(J)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
to be the vector whose
i
-th coordinate is
\textup{max}\{\myalpha_{i}\mid\myalpha\in J\}
.

Definition 3.15
.

Let
F
be a tract and
J\subseteq\Delta_{n}^{d}
an M-convex set.
A function
\myrho\colon\Delta^{d}_{n}\to F
is a
strong
F
-representation
of
J
if its support is
J
and if it satisfies the Plücker relations

(7)

\sum_{k=0}^{s}(-1)^{k+\myepsilon(k)}\cdot\myrho(\myalpha+e_{i_{0}}+\dotsb\widehat{e_{i_{k}}}\dotsb+e_{i_{s}})\cdot\myrho(\myalpha+e_{i_{k}}+e_{j_{2}}+\dotsb+e_{j_{s}})\in N_{F}

for all
2\leqslant s\leqslant d
, all
\myalpha\in\Delta^{d-s}_{n}
, all
1\leqslant i_{0}\leqslant\dotsc\leqslant i_{s}\leqslant n
and all
1\leqslant j_{2}\leqslant\dotsc\leqslant j_{s}\leqslant n
with

\mydelta_{J}^{-}\ \leqslant\ \myalpha\qquad\text{and}\qquad\myalpha+e_{i_{0}}+\cdots+e_{i_{s}}+e_{j_{2}}+\cdots+e_{j_{s}}\ \leqslant\ \mydelta_{J}^{+},

where
\myepsilon(k)
is the number of
k\in\{2,\ldots,s\}
with
i_{k}<j_{s}
.

It is a
weak

-representation
of

if its support is
J
and if it satisfies the

-term Plücker relations

\myrho(\myalpha+e_{j}+e_{k})\cdot\myrho(\myalpha+e_{i}+e_{l})\ -\ \myrho(\myalpha+e_{i}+e_{k})\cdot\myrho(\myalpha+e_{j}+e_{l})\\
+\ \myrho(\myalpha+e_{i}+e_{j})\cdot\myrho(\myalpha+e_{k}+e_{l})\in N_{F}

for all
\myalpha\in\Delta^{d-2}_{n}
and
1\leqslant i\leqslant j\leqslant k\leqslant l\leqslant n
with
\mydelta_{J}^{-}\leqslant\myalpha
and
\myalpha+e_{i}+e_{j}+e_{k}+e_{l}\leqslant\mydelta_{J}^{+}
.

If

Proposition 3.24

Let

We first prove the case of strong representations.
It is clear from the definitions that
\upR_{J}({\mathbb{T}}_{q})\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{J}_{>0}

\myrho\in\upR_{J}({\mathbb{T}}_{q})

\myrho_{t}\colon J\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{>0},\qquad\myalpha\longmapsto\myrho(\myalpha)^{t}

\upR_{J}({\mathbb{T}}_{q})

\upR_{J}({\mathbb{T}}_{\infty})

\mydelta_{J}^{-}\leqslant\myalpha\in\Delta^{r-s}_{n}

Assume otherwise. We have, for all

\displaystyle\myrho_{t}(\myalpha+e_{i_{0}}+\dotsb\widehat{e_{i_{l}}}\dotsb+e_{i_{s}})^{1/q}\cdot\myrho_{t}(\myalpha+e_{i_{l}}+e_{j_{2}}+\dotsb+e_{j_{s}})^{1/q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\myrho(\myalpha+e_{i_{0}}+\dotsb\widehat{e_{i_{l}}}\dotsb+e_{i_{s}})^{t/q}\cdot\myrho(\myalpha+e_{i_{l}}+e_{j_{2}}+\dotsb+e_{j_{s}})^{t/q}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k=0,k\neq l}^{s}\myrho(\myalpha+e_{i_{0}}+\dotsb\widehat{e_{i_{k}}}\dotsb+e_{i_{s}})\cdot\myrho(\myalpha+e_{i_{k}}+e_{j_{2}}+\dotsb+e_{j_{s}})\right)^{t/q}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0,k\neq l}^{s}\myrho_{t}(\myalpha+e_{i_{0}}+\dotsb\widehat{e_{i_{k}}}\dotsb+e_{i_{s}})^{1/q}\cdot\myrho_{t}(\myalpha+e_{i_{k}}+e_{j_{2}}+\dotsb+e_{j_{s}})^{1/q},

3.5.
Maslov dequantization

The realization of triangular hyperfields as tracts allows for the following reformulation of Maslov dequantization (cf. Viro’s papers
[
55
,
56
]
). In a nutshell, the idea behind Maslov dequantization is to deform the usual addition
(a,b)\mapsto a+b
of nonnegative real numbers via the rule

(a,b)\mapsto\big(a^{1/q}+b^{1/q}\big)^{q},

which in the limit as
q
goes to zero becomes the tropical sum:

\ \lim\limits_{q\to 0}\big(a^{1/q}+b^{1/q}\big)^{q}\ =\ \textup{max}\{a,b\}.

In the language of tracts, Maslov dequantization is reflected in the fact that the tropical hyperfield
{\mathbb{T}}_{0}
is the limit as
q\to 0
of the triangular hyperfields
{\mathbb{T}}_{q}
for
q>0
, in the sense that
N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{0}}=\bigcap_{q>0}N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{q}}
. This limiting process extends to
{\mathbb{T}}_{q}
-rational points of varieties, leading to a novel framework for amoebas and their “tropical” limit, as explained in the following.

Let
X
be a complex variety embedded into the torus
({\mathbb{C}}^{\times})^{n}
. The
q
-amoeba of
X
is the image
{\mathcal{A}}_{q}(X)
of
X
under the map
({\mathbb{C}}^{\times})^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
given by

(a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n})\ \longmapsto\ (\log|a_{1}|^{q},\dotsc,\log|a_{n}|^{q}).

The
tropicalization of
X
is the Hausdorff limit

X^{\textup{trop}}\ \coloneq\ {\mathcal{A}}_{0}(X)\ \coloneq\ \lim_{q\to 0}\ {\mathcal{A}}_{q}(X)

as a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

These spaces are controlled by a certain tract
F={\mathbb{C}}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n})\!\sslash\!\mypi^{-1}(I)
associated with
X\subseteq({\mathbb{C}}^{\times})^{n}
, which we define in the following. Let
R={\mathbb{C}}[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\dotsc,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]
be the ring of Laurent series over
{\mathbb{C}}
and let
I\subseteq R
be the vanishing ideal of
X
. As a pointed monoid,
F
is the submonoid
\{\overline{ax^{n}}\mid a\in{\mathbb{C}},\,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\}
of
R/I
, where
\overline{p}
is the class of
p\in R
in
R/I
. The inclusion
F\to R
extends linearly to a semiring homomorphism
\mypi\colon F^{+}\to R
, where
F^{+}={\mathbb{N}}[\overline{ax^{n}}\mid a\in{\mathbb{C}}^{\times},\,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}]
is the ambient semiring of
F
. The null set of
F
is the pullback
N_{F}=\mypi^{-1}(I)
of the vanishing ideal of
X
.

For the purposes of the following construction, we define a complex tract to be a tract
T
together with a tract morphism
\myalpha_{T}\colon{\mathbb{C}}\to T
. For example,
{\mathbb{T}}_{q}
is a complex tract (for
q\geqslant 0
) with respect to the map
z\mapsto|z|^{q}
, which is a tract morphism
\myalpha_{{\mathbb{T}}_{q}}\colon{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{T}}_{q}
since whenever
\sum a_{i}\in N_{\mathbb{C}}
(i.e.,
\sum a_{i}=0
in
{\mathbb{C}})
, the
|a_{i}|
form the side lengths of a (possibly degenerate)
n
-gon in the Euclidean plane, and thus
\sum|a_{i}|^{q}\in N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{q}}
by
Section

3.2
. A
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear map between complex tracts
S
and
T
is a tract morphism
f\colon S\to T
such that
\myalpha_{T}=f\circ\myalpha_{S}
. We denote by
\Hom_{\mathbb{C}}(S,T)
the set of
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear maps from
S
to
T
. The natural inclusion
\myalpha_{F}\colon{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n})\!\sslash\!\mypi^{-1}(I)=F
provides
F
with the structure of a complex tract.

Since
F^{\times}
is generated by
\overline{x_{1}},\dotsc,\overline{x_{n}}
, the evaluation map

\begin{array}[]{cccl}\myiota_{F,T}\colon&\Hom_{\mathbb{C}}(F,T)&\longrightarrow&(T^{\times})^{n}\\[5.0pt]
&{}[f\colon F\to T]&\longmapsto&\big(f(x_{1}),\dotsc,f(x_{n})\big)\end{array}

is an injection for every complex tract
T
. Also, by construction, the image of the map
\myiota_{F,{\mathbb{C}}}\colon\Hom_{\mathbb{C}}(F,{\mathbb{C}})\to({\mathbb{C}}^{\times})^{n}
is equal to
X
.

Proposition 3.35
.

Let
X\subseteq({\mathbb{C}}^{\times})^{n}
be a subvariety. Let
{\mathcal{A}}_{q}(X)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be its
q
-amoeba (for
q>0
) and let
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}(X)=X^{\textup{trop}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be its tropicalization. Let

\begin{array}[]{cccl}\log(\myiota_{X,{\mathbb{T}}_{q}})\colon&\Hom_{\mathbb{C}}(F,{\mathbb{T}}_{q})&\longrightarrow&{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\\[5.0pt]
&{}[f\colon F\to{\mathbb{T}}_{q}]&\longmapsto&\big(\log f(x_{1}),\dotsc,\log f(x_{n})\big)\end{array}

for all
q\geqslant 0
, and

{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Proof.

The inclusions
\myiota_{F,T}
are evidently functorial in
T
, and thus

induces the commutative diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X\ =\ \Hom(F,{\mathbb{C}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces({\mathbb{C}}^{\times})^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\qquad\Hom(F,{\mathbb{T}}_{q})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces({\mathbb{T}}_{q}^{\times})^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\myiota_{F,{\mathbb{C}}}}

Thus

We turn to the claim that

The topology of spaces of Lorentzian polynomials

4.1.
Definitions

We denote by
\upH(d,n)
the space of homogeneous polynomials of degree
d
in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
with nonnegative real coefficients.
Let
\mathring{\upL}^{2}_{n}
be the space of homogeneous quadratic polynomials
f\in\upH(2,n)
with strictly positive coefficients whose Hessian
{\mathcal{H}}_{f}
has
Lorentzian signature
, that is,
{\mathcal{H}}_{f}
has one positive eigenvalue and
n-1
negative eigenvalues, counting multiplicities.
A
strictly Lorentzian polynomial
of degree
d
in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
is a polynomial
f\in\upH(d,n)
with strictly positive coefficients such that
\partial^{\myalpha}f\in\mathring{\upL}^{2}_{n}
for all
\myalpha\in\Delta^{d-2}_{n}
. A
Lorentzian polynomial
is a limit of strictly Lorentzian polynomials. It was shown in
[
15
]
that a nonzero polynomial
f\in\upH(d,n)
with nonnegative coefficients is Lorentzian if and only if it satisfies one of the following equivalent conditions:

(1)

The support of
f
is an M-convex set and the Hessian of
\partial^{\myalpha}f\in\upH(2,n)
has at most one positive eigenvalue for all
\myalpha\in\Delta^{d-2}_{n}
.

(2)

The partial derivative
\partial^{\myalpha}f
is identically zero or log-concave on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
for all
\myalpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0}
.

For an M-convex set
J
, we denote by
\upL_{J}
the set of Lorentzian polynomials with support
J
.

Example 4.1
.

A polynomial
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is called
(real) stable
if
f=0
or
f(w)\neq 0
for all
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
, where
{\mathbb{H}}\coloneq\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\;|\;{\rm Im}(z)>0\}
is the complex upper half-plane. Homogeneous stable polynomials with nonnegative coefficients are Lorentzian by
[
15
]
*Proposition 2.2. Moreover, in degree two the notions of stable and Lorentzian coincide by
[
15
]
*Lemma 2.5. We denote the set of stable polynomials with nonnegative coefficients and support
J
by
\upS_{J}
.

The multiplicative group
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
acts on
\upL_{J}
by scalar multiplication, and we denote the quotient space by
{\mathbb{P}}\upL_{J}
. Similarly, the multiplicative group
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{>0}
acts on
\upL_{J}
by scaling each variable, and we denote by

the quotient space. The same notation applies to spaces of stable polynomials.
If

The space of Lorentzian polynomials with support

over

Lemma 4.2
.

If

is a Lorentzian polynomial with support

, then
\myrho_{f}\in\upR^{\rm w}_{J}({\mathbb{T}}_{\infty})
.

Proof.

Let
J\subseteq\Delta^{d}_{n}
be an M-convex set, let

, and let
\myrho=\myrho_{f}
. Let
\myalpha\in\Delta_{n}^{d-2}
and choose

such that one of the three terms

To show that

If

The multi-affine part of

is Lorentzian, cf.

*Corollary 3.5. Because Lorentzian polynomials of degree two are real stable, we can apply

∎

Remark 4.3
.

As a consequence of

4.1
and the equality
\upR^{\rm w}_{J}({\mathbb{T}}_{\infty})=\upR_{J}({\mathbb{T}}_{\infty})
(see

3.3
), if

is Lorentzian then
\myrho_{f}
belongs to
\upR_{J}({\mathbb{T}}_{\infty})
, which means that

satisfies

degenerate Plücker relations.
More precisely,

c_{\mybeta+e_{i_{0}}}\ \cdot\ c_{\mygamma+e_{i_{1}}}\ =\ c_{\mybeta+e_{i_{1}}}\ \cdot\ c_{\mygamma+e_{i_{0}}}

for all

such that

c_{\mybeta+e_{i_{0}}+e_{i_{1}}-e_{k}}\cdot c_{\mygamma+e_{k}}=0

whenever

and
\mybeta_{k}>\mygamma_{k}
.

By
[

]
*Lemma 6.1, this fact is known for stable polynomials when
\mybeta-e_{k}=\mygamma-e_{l}
for some
k,l\in[n]
, i.e., stable polynomials satisfy the degenerate
3
-term Plücker relations. That Lorentzian polynomials, or even stable polynomials, satisfy all degenerate Plücker relations appears to be a new observation.

An example of a degenerate Plücker relation with more than
3
terms is

c_{123}\cdot c_{456}\ +\ c_{124}\cdot c_{356}\ +\ \underbrace{c_{134}\cdot c_{256}}_{=0}\ +\ \underbrace{c_{234}\cdot c_{156}}_{=0}\quad\in\quad N_{{\mathbb{T}}_{\infty}}

for the matroid
J=\{\myalpha\in\Delta^{3}_{6}\mid\myalpha_{i}\leqslant 1\text{ for all $i\in[n]$ and }e_{3}+e_{4}\not\leqslant\myalpha\}
, which is a parallel extension of
U_{3,5}
with parallel elements
3
and
4
. Consequently, we have
c_{123}\cdot c_{456}=c_{124}\cdot c_{356}
for any Lorentzian polynomial
f=\sum_{\myalpha\in\Delta^{d}_{n}}c_{\myalpha}\frac{x^{\myalpha}}{\myalpha!}
with support
J
.

For
f=\sum_{\myalpha\in\Delta^{d}_{n}}c_{\myalpha}\frac{x^{\myalpha}}{\myalpha!}
and

, we define

.

4.2.
Simplification of Lorentzians in degree two

We first recall the following property of M-convex sets
J\subseteq\Delta_{n}^{2}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
J\subseteq\Delta_{n}^{2}
be an M-convex set. Let
V\subseteq[n]
be the set of all
i\in[n]
such that
e_{i}+e_{j}\in J
for some
j\in[n]
, and let
E
be the set of all two-element subsets
\{i,j\}\subseteq V
such that
e_{i}+e_{j}\in J
. Finally, let
D
the set of all
i\in J
such that
2e_{i}\in J
. Then:

(1)

We have
e_{i}+e_{j}\in J
for all
i\in D
and
j\in V
.

(2)

The graph
G=(V,E)
is a complete multipartite graph where
V
is partitioned into the equivalence classes of the equivalence relation
\sim
generated by
i\sim j
if
e_{i}+e_{j}\notin J
.

Proof.

Let
i\in V
such that
2e_{i}\in J
and let
j\in V
. Because
j\in V
, there exists
k\in V
such that
e_{j}+e_{k}\in J
. If
k=i
, then we are done. Otherwise, it follows from M-convexity that
e_{i}+e_{j}\in J
. This proves part (1). For part (2), we note that
E
is the set of bases of a rank
2
matroid on the ground set
V
, which is a parallel extension of a uniform matroid
U_{2,r}
. The claim then follows from
[
22
]
*Corollary 5.4, noting that the equivalence class of
i\in V
is the flat (or parallel class) of
i
.
∎

Definition 4.6
.

Let
J\subseteq\Delta_{n}^{2}
be an M-convex set. We use the notation from
Section

4.2
. By part (2) of
Section

4.2
, there is a partition

V=\mycoprod_{i=1}^{r}V_{i}

such that
\{i,j\}
is an edge of
G
if and only if
i
and
j
are contained in two different
V_{l}
. For
i\in V
, we let
p(i)\in[r]
be such that
i\in V_{p(i)}

J^{\rm simp}=\{2e_{p(i)}\mid i\in D\}\cup\{e_{i}+e_{j}\mid i,j\in[r]\textrm{ and }i\neq j\}\subseteq\Delta_{r}^{2}.

Theorem 4.7

(Simplification)
.

Let
J\subseteq\Delta_{n}^{2}
be an M-convex set and
J^{\textup{simp}}
as above.
Let
f\in\upH(d,n)
with
\supp(f)=J
such that
\myrho_{f}\in\upR_{J}({\mathbb{T}}_{\infty})
. Then:

(1)

There exists a unique polynomial
g\in\upH(d,r)
with
\supp(g)=J^{\rm simp}
and unique
\mylambda_{1},\ldots,\mylambda_{n}>0
such that:

(1)

For all
j\in[r]
we have
\sum_{i\in V_{j}}\mylambda_{i}=1
.

(2)

As polynomials in
H(d,n)
, we have

f(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n})\ =\ g\Big(\sum_{i\in V_{1}}\mylambda_{i}x_{i},\ldots,\sum_{i\in V_{r}}\mylambda_{i}x_{i}\Big).

(2)

f
is Lorentzian if and only if
g
is Lorentzian.

Proof.

Let
k,l\in[r]
be two different indices, and let
f_{kl}
the polynomial obtained from
f
by setting all variables to zero that are not in
V_{k}\cup V_{l}
. We claim that there are
a_{kk},a_{ll}\geqslant 0
,
a_{kl}>0
, and
\mymu^{k,l}_{i}>0
,
i\in V_{k}\cup V_{l}
, such that

\sum_{i\in V_{k}}\mymu^{k,l}_{i}=\sum_{i\in V_{l}}\mymu^{k,l}_{i}=1

and

(10)

f_{kl}=a_{kk}\cdot\sum_{i\in D\cap V_{k}}x_{i}^{2}+a_{ll}\cdot\sum_{i\in D\cap V_{l}}x_{i}^{2}+a_{kl}\cdot(\sum_{i\in V_{k}}\mymu^{k,l}_{i}x_{i})\cdot(\sum_{i\in V_{l}}\mymu^{k,l}_{i}x_{i}).

We further require
a_{kk}=0
(resp.
a_{ll}=0
) if
D\cap V_{k}=\varnothing
(resp.
D\cap V_{l}=\varnothing
). Then all
a_{kk},a_{ll},a_{kl},\mymu^{k,l}_{i}
are uniquely determined by
Equation

10
if they exist. It follows from part (1) of
Section

4.2
that
D\cap V_{k}\neq\varnothing
implies
|V_{k}|=1
, and the same holds true for
V_{l}
. Therefore, the claim is clear when
D\cap(V_{k}\cup V_{l})\neq\varnothing
, and for the remainder of the proof we assume that
D\cap(V_{k}\cup V_{l})=\varnothing
. In this case, the claim is equivalent to the matrix
(\myrho_{f}(e_{i}+e_{j}))_{i\in V_{k},j\in V_{l}}
having rank one. However, this is true because
\myrho_{f}\in\upR_{J}({\mathbb{T}}_{\infty})
implies that every
2\times 2
minor of this matrix vanishes.

In order to prove part (1), it remains to show that for all pairwise different
k,l,l^{\prime}\in[r]
and
i\in V_{k}
, we have

\mymu^{k,l}_{i}=\mymu^{k,l^{\prime}}_{i}
.
To this end, let
i_{1},i_{2}\in V_{k}
,
j_{1}\in V_{l}
, and
j_{2}\in V_{l^{\prime}}
. Then

\underbrace{\myrho_{f}(e_{i_{1}}+e_{i_{2}})}_{=0}\cdot\myrho_{f}(e_{j_{1}}+e_{j_{2}})=0.

Because
\myrho_{f}
is a representation over
{\mathbb{T}}_{\infty}
, this implies that

\myrho_{f}(e_{i_{1}}+e_{j_{1}})\myrho_{f}(e_{i_{2}}+e_{j_{2}})=\myrho_{f}(e_{i_{1}}+e_{j_{2}})\myrho_{f}(e_{i_{2}}+e_{j_{1}}).

If we plug in the expressions from
Equation

10
for the coefficients of
f
, we obtain

a_{kl}a_{kl^{\prime}}\mymu^{k,l}_{i_{1}}\mymu_{j_{1}}^{k,l}\mymu_{i_{2}}^{k,l^{\prime}}\mymu_{j_{2}}^{k,l^{\prime}}=a_{kl}a_{kl^{\prime}}\mymu^{k,l^{\prime}}_{i_{1}}\mymu_{j_{2}}^{k,l^{\prime}}\mymu_{i_{2}}^{k,l}\mymu_{j_{1}}^{k,l}

which shows that
\mymu_{i_{1}}^{k,l}\mymu_{i_{2}}^{k,l^{\prime}}=\mymu_{i_{1}}^{k,l^{\prime}}\mymu_{i_{2}}^{k,l}
. This means that the vectors
(\mymu^{k,l}_{i}\mid i\in V_{k})
and
(\mymu^{k,l^{\prime}}_{i}\mid i\in V_{k})
are linearly dependent. Because both are in the standard simplex, these vectors must be equal. This implies the claim of part (1).

For part (2), assume first that
g
is Lorentzian. Then
f
is Lorentzian by
[
15
]
*Theorem 2.10. Conversely, assume that
f
is Lorentzian. We obtain
g
by setting all but one variable from each group equal to zero and appropriately scaling the remaining one. This operation also preserves the Lorentzian property by
[
15
]
*Theorem 2.10.
∎

.

.

Lemma 4.11
.

If
f
is in the interior of
\upL_{M}
for
M=U_{2,n}
, then the Hessian of
f
has full rank.

Proof.

Let
f\in\upL_{M}
be such that the Hessian
A
of
f
has rank less than
n
. Note that this implies
n>2
. Let
0\neq v\in\ker(A)
. Because every off-diagonal entry of
A
is positive, there are at least two indices
j,k\in[n]
such that
v_{j}
and
v_{k}
are nonzero. Because
n>2
, there exists
i\in[n]\backslash\{j,k\}
. Now let
B=(b_{rs})_{r,s}
be the symmetric
n\times n
matrix such that
b_{ik}=b_{ki}=1
,
b_{jk}=b_{kj}=-\frac{v_{i}+v_{k}}{v_{j}}
, and all other entries are zero. Then

v^{t}Bv=2\cdot\left(v_{i}v_{k}-v_{j}v_{k}\frac{v_{i}+v_{k}}{v_{j}}\right)=-2v_{k}^{2}.

Furthermore, letting
e=e_{i}+e_{k}
, we have
e^{t}Be=2
and

e^{t}Bv=v^{t}Be=v_{i}+v_{k}-v_{j}\cdot\frac{v_{i}+v_{k}}{v_{j}}=0.

Finally, we compute

(\mylambda\cdot e+\mymu\cdot v)^{t}(A-\myepsilon\cdot B)(\mylambda e+\mymu v)=\mylambda^{2}(e^{t}Ae-2\myepsilon)+\mymu^{2}\cdot\myepsilon\cdot v_{k}^{2}.

Therefore, for small enough
\myepsilon>0
, the matrix
A-\myepsilon\cdot B
is positive definite on the two dimensional span of
e
and
v
. This shows that
f
is not in the interior of
\upL_{M}
.
∎

4.3.
Strong star-shapedness in degree two

For
J\subseteq\Delta_{n}^{2}
an M-convex set, we now examine the image
\log(\upL_{J})
of
\upL_{J}
under the coefficient-wise logarithm map.
Note that we take coefficients with respect to the normalized monomial basis, i.e.,

\log(\upL_{J})=\Big\{(\log c_{\myalpha})_{\myalpha\in J}\;|\;\sum_{\myalpha\in J}c_{\myalpha}\frac{x^{\myalpha}}{\myalpha!}\in\upL_{J}\Big\}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{J}.

By
Section

4.1
,
\log(\upL_{J})
is contained in the linear space
V_{J}=\log(\upR_{J}({\mathbb{T}}_{\infty}))
. Furthermore, it follows from
4.4
that

\log(\upL_{J})
is star-shaped with respect to the origin. However, in general the origin does not lie in the interior of
\log(\upL_{J})
. In particular, the triple
(0,\log(\upL_{J}),V_{J})
is not strongly star-shaped in general. However, we will prove that
\log(\upL_{J})
is a strongly star-shaped subset of
V_{J}
with respect to another point.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
A=(a_{ij})_{ij}
be a symmetric
n\times n
matrix with entries in
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
which has exactly one positive eigenvalue. If
0\leqslant d_{i}\leqslant a_{ii}
for all
i\in[n]
, then the matrix

A^{\prime}=A-\textnormal{Diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})

has exactly one positive eigenvalue or is the zero matrix.

Proof.

Assume that the matrix
A^{\prime}
is not the zero matrix. Then
A^{\prime}
has at least one positive eigenvalue, because it has only nonnegative entries. It cannot have more than one positive eigenvalue, because then the same would be true for the matrix

A=A^{\prime}+{\textnormal{Diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})}

as
{\textnormal{Diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})}
is positive semidefinite.
∎

Theorem 4.13
.

Let
J\subseteq\Delta_{n}^{2}
be an M-convex set. For every
i\in[n]
, let
a_{ii}\in[0,1]
, and consider the polynomial

f=\sum_{2e_{i}\in J}a_{ii}\cdot\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{2}+\sum_{e_{i}+e_{j}\in J,\,i\neq j}x_{i}x_{j}.

(1)

The set
\log(\upL_{J})
is star-shaped with respect to
\log(f)
.

(2)

If
a_{ii}<1
for every
i\in[n]
, then the Hessian of the simplification of
f
has full rank.

(3)

If
a_{ii}<1
for every
i\in[n]
, then
(\log(f),\log(\upL_{J}),V_{J})
is strongly star-shaped.

Proof.

For part (1), we need to prove that for all
g\in\upL_{J}
and
p\in[0,1]
we have

(11)

\log(f)+p\cdot(\log(g)-\log(f))\in\log(\upL_{J}).

Let
(a_{ij})_{ij}
and
(b_{ij})_{ij}
be the Hessian matrices of
f
and
g
, respectively. Then (
11
) is equivalent to the matrix

\left(a_{ij}\cdot\left(\frac{b_{ij}}{a_{ij}}\right)^{p}\right)_{ij}=\left(b_{ij}^{p}\right)_{ij}-\textnormal{Diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})

having exactly one positive eigenvalue, where
d_{i}=b_{ii}^{p}\cdot(1-a_{ii}^{1-p})
. This follows from
Section

4.3
together with
[
15
]
*Lemma 3.24.

For part (2), we observe that the Hessian of the simplification of
f
has the form

H=\textbf{1}_{r}-D,

where
\textbf{1}_{r}
is the
r\times r
all-ones matrix and
D
is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal
v
has strictly positive entries that are at most
1
. Furthermore, if
r=1
, then the entry of
v
is strictly less than
1
. Letting
w
be the vector whose
i
-th entry is
v_{i}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, the matrix
H
has the same rank as

H^{\prime}=w\cdot w^{t}-I_{r}.

The matrix
w\cdot w^{t}
has only one nonzero eigenvalue, which is the norm of
w
. Because
0<v_{i}\leqslant 1
and
0<v_{1}<1
if
r=1
, the norm of
w
is strictly larger than
1
, which implies that
H^{\prime}
has full rank.

In order to prove part (3), we first note that
\log(\upL_{J})
is closed because
\upL_{J}
is closed. By part (2) and
Section

4.2
, the point
\log(f)
is in the interior of
\log(\upL_{J})
relative to
V_{J}
. In order to conclude that
(\log(f),\log(\upL_{J}))
is strongly star-shaped as a subset of
V_{J}
, we would like to apply
Section

2
. To this end, we need to prove that for every
g\in\upL_{J}
there exists
0<p_{0}<1
such that for every
p\in(p_{0},1)
, the point

x_{p}=\log(f)+p\cdot(\log(g)-\log(f))

lies in the interior of
\log(\upL_{J})
relative to
V_{J}
. We can write
x_{p}=\log(h_{p})
for some polynomial
h_{p}
. The determinant of the Hessian of the simplification of
h_{p}
is an analytic function in
p
by
Section

4.2
. It does not vanish for
p=0
by part (2). Therefore, it has only isolated zeros. This implies that there exists
0<p_{0}<1
such that for every
p\in(p_{0},1)
, the Hessian of the simplification of
h_{p}
has full rank. By
Section

4.2
, this implies the desired statement.
∎

4.4.
Conclusion about the topology

Now we are ready to complete the proof of
1.7
.
Consider the linear operator
\upN
on polynomials defined by the condition
\upN(x^{\myalpha})=\frac{x^{\myalpha}}{\myalpha!}
. (We call
N
the
normalization operator
.)
If a polynomial
f
is Lorentzian, then
\upN(f)
is also Lorentzian by
[
15
]
*Corollary 3.7. We will prove that
(\log(\upN(f_{J})),\log(\upL_{J}),V_{J})
is strongly star shaped for every M-convex
J\subseteq\Delta^{d}_{n}
, where
V_{J}=\log(\upR_{J}({\mathbb{T}}_{\infty}))
, as above, and
f_{J}=\sum_{\myalpha\in J}\frac{x^{\myalpha}}{\myalpha!}
is the (exponential) generating function of
J
, i.e.,

\upN(f_{J})=\sum_{\myalpha\in J}\frac{x^{\myalpha}}{(\myalpha!)^{2}}.

More generally, for any real number
t
, we write
\upN_{t}
for the linear operator on polynomials defined by the condition

\upN_{t}(x^{\myalpha})=\frac{x^{\myalpha}}{(\myalpha!)^{t}}.

The operator
\upN_{0}
is the identity and the operator
\upN_{1}
is the normalization operator
\upN
. Note furthermore that
\upN_{s}\circ\upN_{t}=\upN_{s+t}
. The following lemma generalizes
[
15
]
*Corollary 3.5 and
[
15
]
*Corollary 3.7 by interpolating between them.

Lemma 4.14
.

The operator
\upN_{t}
preserves the Lorentzian property for all
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

The proof of
[
15
]
*Corollary 3.7 admits a straightforward generalization and shows that the symbol of
\upN_{t}
is a Lorentzian polynomial in
2n
variables.
∎

We will need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
f=\sum_{\mybeta\in\Delta^{d}_{n}}\frac{c_{\mybeta}}{\mybeta!}\cdot x^{\mybeta}
,

, and
g=\partial^{a}\upN_{t}(f)
for some

.
Then

Proposition 5.10

with

where

is the rank of the Hessian of

.

Assume that

is in the image
\upR_{M}({\mathbb{C}})\to\upL_{M}
. Then, by

5.1
, there are Hermitian

6.2.
Compactly supported Euler characteristic

Building upon the results from the previous subsection, we will compute the Euler characteristic of
\overline{{\mathbb{P}}\upL}_{M}
for some matroids
M
. In particular, we will see an example of a matroid
M
for which
\mychi(\overline{{\mathbb{P}}\upL}_{M})\neq 1
, which shows in particular that
\overline{{\mathbb{P}}\upL}_{M}
is not homeomorphic to a ball.

To this end, for any M-convex
J
, we consider the decomposition

\overline{{\mathbb{P}}\upL}_{J}=\bigsqcup_{J^{\prime}}(\overline{{\mathbb{P}}\upL}_{J}\cap{\mathbb{P}}\upL_{J^{\prime}}),

where the disjoint union is over all initial subsets
J^{\prime}
of
J
.

For computing
\mychi(\overline{{\mathbb{P}}\upL}_{J})
in terms of the cells
\overline{{\mathbb{P}}\upL}_{J}\cap{\mathbb{P}}\upL_{J^{\prime}}
, we will make use of some concepts and results from tame topology (see
[
54
]
for an introduction to this topic).

We say that a subset
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is
definable
if it is of the form

\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid\exists y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}:P(x,y,e^{x},e^{y})=0\},

where
P
is a real polynomial in
2(n+k)
variables, and where
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
,
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
,
e^{x}=(e^{x_{1}},\ldots,e^{x_{n}})
,
e^{y}=(e^{y_{1}},\ldots,e^{y_{k}})
. A map
f\colon S\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
from a definable set
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is
definable
if its graph is definable. It was shown by Wilkie
[
58
]
that the definable sets form an
o-minimal structure
. This implies in particular that:

(1)

Every semi-algebraic set and every semi-algebraic map is definable.

(2)

Boolean combinations of definable sets are definable.

(3)

If
A
is definable, then so are
{\mathbb{R}}\times A
and
A\times{\mathbb{R}}
.

(4)

The image of a definable set under a definable map is definable.

Example 6.17
.

Note that the map
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\,x\mapsto e^{x}
is definable but not semi-algebraic.

Remark 6.18
.

Let
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
be a definable set. Then the set

\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid\exists t\in{\mathbb{R}}:(x,t)\in S\}

is the image of
S
under the definable map

\mypi\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\qquad(x,t)\mapsto x,

and thus is again definable by property (4) of o-minimality. Similarly, the set

\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}:(x,t)\in S\}={\mathbb{R}}^{n}\backslash\mypi({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\backslash S)

is definable. We say that
definable sets satisfy quantifier elimination
.

Theorem 6.19

(§4.2 in
[
54
]
)
.

There is a unique integer-valued map
\mychi_{*}
on the set of definable sets that satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

\mychi_{*}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})=(-1)^{n}
,

(2)

If
S,T\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
are disjoint definable sets, then
\mychi_{*}(S\cup T)=\mychi_{*}(S)+\mychi_{*}(T)
,

(3)

If
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a definable set and
f\colon S\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is an injective definable map, then
\mychi_{*}(S)=\mychi_{*}(f(S))
.

(4)

If
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
T\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
are definable sets, then
\mychi_{*}(S\times T)=\mychi_{*}(S)\cdot\mychi_{*}(T)
.

Remark 6.20
.

(1)

Let
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a compact definable set.
Because
S
can be triangulated by means of definable homeomorphisms, see
[
54
]
*§8.2, it follows that
\mychi_{*}(S)
is equal to the topological Euler characteristic
\mychi(S)
of
S
, defined as the alternating sum

\mychi(S)=b_{0}-b_{1}+b_{2}-b_{3}+\cdots,

where
b_{i}
is the rank of the
i
th singular homology group of
S
.

(2)

If
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is definable but not compact, then
\mychi_{*}(S)
might differ from
\mychi(S)
. For example
\mychi({\mathbb{R}}^{n})=1
because
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is contractible, but
\mychi_{*}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})=(-1)^{n}
by part (1) of
6.19
.

Applying these considerations to our situation, we obtain the following formula.

Lemma 6.21
.

Let
J
be an M-convex set. Then

\mychi(\overline{{\mathbb{P}}\upL}_{J})=\sum_{J^{\prime}}\mychi_{*}(\overline{{\mathbb{P}}\upL}_{J}\cap{\mathbb{P}}\upL_{J^{\prime}})=-\sum_{J^{\prime}}\mychi_{*}(\overline{\upL}_{J}\cap\upL_{J^{\prime}})=-\sum_{J^{\prime}}\mychi_{*}(\log(\overline{\upL}_{J}\cap\upL_{J^{\prime}})),

where the sums are over all initial subsets
J^{\prime}
of
J
.

Proof.

The first equality follows from part (2) of
6.19
. For proving the second equality, we first note that
\overline{{\mathbb{P}}\upL}_{J}\cap{\mathbb{P}}\upL_{J^{\prime}}
can be identified with the subset of
\overline{\upL}_{J}\cap\upL_{J^{\prime}}
consisting of all polynomials for which the sum of the coefficients is equal to one. Under this identification we obtain the homeomorphism

\left(\overline{{\mathbb{P}}\upL}_{J}\cap{\mathbb{P}}\upL_{J^{\prime}}\right)\times{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\to\overline{\upL}_{J}\cap\upL_{J^{\prime}},\qquad(f,\mylambda)\mapsto\mylambda\cdot f.

Because
\mychi_{*}({\mathbb{R}}_{>0})=\mychi_{*}({\mathbb{R}})=-1
by part (1) of
6.19
, the second equality of the claim now follows from part (4) of
6.19
. Finally, the third equality follows from part (3) of
6.19
.
∎

The sets
\log(\overline{\upL}_{J}\cap\upL_{J^{\prime}})
are star-shaped by
Section

6.1
. Thus we can use
Section

6.2
and the following lemma to compute the Euler characteristic of
\mychi(\overline{{\mathbb{P}}\upL}_{J})
.

Lemma 6.22
.

Let
X\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a closed definable set that is star-shaped with respect to the origin. Let
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be the unit sphere. Consider the set of rays contained in
X
:

S(X)=\{\mynu\in S\mid\forall t\geqslant 0:t\cdot\mynu\in X\}.

Then
S(X)
is a definable set satisfying
\mychi_{*}(S(X))+\mychi_{*}(X)=1
.

Proof.

By
Section

6.2
, definable sets satisfy quantifier elimination, and thus the set
S(X)
is definable. For the same reason, the following map is definable:

\mypsi\colon X\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0},\qquad x\mapsto\inf\{t>0\mid\frac{x}{t}\in X\}.

Furthermore, by
Section

2
, we have for all
x\in X
:

(1)

\mypsi(x)\leqslant 1
.

(2)

If
\mypsi(x)>0
, then
\frac{x}{\mypsi(x)}\in X
.

(3)

\mypsi(x)=0
if and only if
x=0
or
x\neq 0
and
\frac{x}{\|x\|}\in S(X)
.

The proof of
Section

2
also shows that
\mypsi
is lower semi-continuous, as this part of the proof only uses that
X
is closed and star-shaped.

Let
\upB\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be the closed unit ball, and consider the map

\myphi\colon X\to\upB\backslash S(X),\qquad x\mapsto\frac{x}{1-\mypsi(x)+\|x\|}.

The map is well-defined because for all
x\in X
we have
0\leqslant\mypsi(x)\leqslant 1
and
x\neq 0
whenever
\mypsi(x)=1
. Its norm is lower semi-continuous, because
\mypsi
is. For fixed
x\in X
, the map

\myphi_{x}\colon[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}x,\qquad\mylambda\mapsto\myphi(\mylambda\cdot x)

is continuous, its norm is monotonically increasing, and it satisfies
\myphi_{x}(0)=0
and
\myphi_{x}(1)=x
. This shows that the image
\myphi(X)
is star-shaped with respect to the origin. Furthermore,
\myphi
is definable, and a short calculation shows that it is injective. Thus
\mychi_{*}(X)=\mychi_{*}(\myphi(X))
.

We claim that the closure
Y
of
\myphi(X)
is the disjoint union of
\myphi(X)
and
S(X)
. A point
x\in X
is mapped by
\myphi
to
S
if and only if
\mypsi(x)=1
. Thus, by (3), we cannot have
\myphi(x)=\frac{x}{\|x\|}\in S(X)
. This shows that
\myphi(X)
and
S(X)
are disjoint. Now let
x\in S(X)
, meaning that
\|x\|=1
and
t\cdot x\in X
for all
t\geqslant 0
. We have

\lim_{t\to\infty}\myphi(tx)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{tx}{1+{t}}=x.

This shows that
S(X)
is contained in the closure of
\myphi(X)
.

Conversely, let
(x_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence in
X
such that
(\myphi(x_{i}))_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges. If it is unbounded, then
(\myphi(x_{i}))_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges to a point in
S(X)
. Otherwise, it converges to a point in
\myphi(X)
by semi-continuity and star-shapedness. Thus we have shown that the closure
Y
of
\myphi(X)
is the disjoint union of
\myphi(X)
and
S(X)
. As
Y
is a compact star-shaped definable set, we have

1=\mychi_{*}(Y)=\mychi_{*}(\myphi(X))+\mychi_{*}(S(X))=\mychi_{*}(X)+\mychi_{*}(S(X)).\qed

The next goal is to provide a natural compactification of the orbit spaces

6.3.1.
The Hausdorff quotient

Let
X
be a compact metrizable space.
Recall that for any choice of metric on
X
, the set
H(X)
of compact subsets of
X
equipped with the Hausdorff metric is again a compact metric space. The topology of
H(X)
does not depend on the metric on
X
. A continuous map
f\colon X\to Y
of compact metrizable spaces induces a continuous map
H(f)\colon H(X)\to H(Y)
. We will make frequent use the following easy lemma:

Lemma 6.38
.

Let
(K_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of compact subsets
K_{i}
of
X
that converges to the compact set
K\subseteq X
in the Hausdorff metric. For each
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
U_{i}
be a dense subset of
K_{i}
. Then
K
is the set of all
x\in X
for which there is a sequence
(x_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
x_{i}\in U_{i}
that converges to
x
.

Given a topological group
G
acting continuously on
X
, we recall from
[
28
]
the construction of the Hausdorff quotient
X/_{H}G
. We consider the map

e\colon X\longrightarrow H(X),\qquad x\longmapsto\overline{Gx}.

Let
\mathbb{U}
be the set of open, dense, and
G
-invariant subsets of
X
. Then the
Hausdorff quotient
of
X
by
G
, denoted
X/_{H}G
, is defined as

X/_{H}G=\bigcap_{U\in\mathbb{U}}\overline{e(U)}.

Because
X/_{H}G
is a closed subspace of
H(X)
, it is compact and metrizable.

Definition 6.39
.

A set
U\in\mathbb{U}
is
stable
if
e|_{U}\colon U\to H(X)
is continuous. A set
U\in\mathbb{U}
is
semi-stable
if
X/_{H}G=\overline{e(U)}
.

Remark 6.40
.

Being stable implies being semi-stable
[
28
]
*Remark 1.2.

In certain situations, the Hausdorff limit interacts nicely with the Hilbert quotient of a projective variety.

(

6.3.3.
Interlude on the
\aleph_{1}
-saturation

We first recall some basic properties of the so-called
\aleph_{1}
-saturation

of
{\mathbb{R}}
, see
[
48
]
*§2.2. Let
{\mathcal{F}}
be a non-principal ultrafilter on
{\mathbb{N}}
, i.e., an ultrafilter containing the filter of cofinite subsets of

, and let
{\mathbb{R}}^{*}
be the ultrapower
{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathbb{N}}}/{\mathcal{F}}
of
{\mathbb{R}}
with respect to

. This is the set of equivalence classes of sequences in
{\mathbb{R}}
, where two sequences
(x_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and

are defined to be equivalent if the set of indices

for which

is in

.
By
[
48
]
*Theorem 2.2.7 and 2.2.8, the ultrapower

is a real closed field. Here sum and product are defined as the equivalence class of the component-wise sum and product of two representing sequences, respectively.
Moreover, one can consider
{\mathbb{R}}
as a subfield of
{\mathbb{R}}^{*}
via the map
{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{*}
that sends
a
to the class of the constant sequence
(a)_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. The total order on
{\mathbb{R}}^{*}
is given by

(19)

[(x_{i})_{\in{\mathbb{N}}}]\geqslant 0\Longleftrightarrow\{i\in{\mathbb{N}}\mid x_{i}\geqslant 0\}\in{\mathcal{F}}.

Next recall, for example from
[
34
]
, that the
convex hull

{\mathfrak{o}}
of

in
{\mathbb{R}}^{*}
is the set of all elements

such that there are

with
a\leqslant x\leqslant b
. It is clear that
{\mathfrak{o}}
is a valuation ring of
{\mathbb{R}}^{*}
which contains
{\mathbb{R}}
. The maximal ideal
{\mathfrak{m}}
of

consists of all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{*}
such that for all
\myepsilon\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
\myepsilon>0
, we have

. Finally, we denote by
\mykappa={\mathfrak{o}}/{\mathfrak{m}}
the corresponding residue field, and by

the natural homomorphism.

Lemma 6.51
.

The restriction of
\mypi
to

is an isomorphism onto

Proof.

This follows from
[

]
*Proposition 2.5.3 and the fact that

has no free Dedekind cut, see

]

In light of

, we may identify

with
{\mathbb{R}}
. The

of

is the abelian group

defined by

for

makes

a totally ordered group. Since

is real closed,

is divisible. We will use additive notation for the group

By Hahn’s embedding theorem

, we can embed

into the additive group of a real closed field

, whose value group is the divisible closure of the group of Archimedean equivalence classes of

is divisible, it is a

-linear subspace of

Remark 6.52
.

Let

be a sequence in

and let

Lemma 6.53
.

Let

be an M-convex set.
Let

be a sequence in

In order to prove that

]
*Theorem 6.4: A function

\myalpha\in\Delta^{d-2}_{n}
and all

such that

,

(20)

f(\myalpha+e_{i}+e_{k})+f(\myalpha+e_{j}+e_{l})\\
\geqslant\ \textup{min}\big\{f(\myalpha+e_{i}+e_{j})+f(\myalpha+e_{k}+e_{l}),\ f(\myalpha+e_{i}+e_{l})+f(\myalpha+e_{j}+e_{k})\big\}.

Now consider a sequence

in

for some

\leqslant\ y_{m}(\myalpha+e_{i}+e_{j})^{1/q}\cdot y_{m}(\myalpha+e_{k}+e_{l})^{1/q}+\ y_{m}(\myalpha+e_{i}+e_{l})^{1/q}\cdot y_{m}(\myalpha+e_{j}+e_{k})^{1/q}.

6.3.5.
Examples

In some examples, we can show that
X/_{H}G
is homeomorphic to a closed Euclidean ball.

Example 6.55

(Rigid polymatroids)
.

If
J
is rigid, i.e., the space
\ulineGr_{J}({\mathbb{T}}_{0})
is a singleton, then
U/G
is homeomorphic to a closed Euclidean ball whose dimension is the rank of the multiplicative group of the foundation of
J
, see
3.26
and
4.22
. Moreover, since
U/G
is compact, we have
X/_{H}G=U/G
by
Section

6.3.2
.

Examples of rigid matroids are binary matroids and projective geometries over finite fields, for which
X/_{H}G
is a point, and the Betsy Ross matroid, for which
X/_{H}G
is homeomorphic to a closed interval.

The next simplest case is when
\log\ulineGr_{J}({\mathbb{T}}_{0})
is one-dimensional and consists of a finite number
r
of rays. In this case, by
3.26
and
4.22
,
U/G
is homeomorphic to a closed Euclidean ball with
r
points removed from its boundary — one for each ray of
\log\ulineGr_{J}({\mathbb{T}}_{0})
. These rays correspond to pairwise different regular polymatroid subdivisions. If we further assume that every proper initial matroid of
M
has a finite foundation, then
6.49
implies that
X/_{H}G
is obtained from
U/G
by adding
r
points. Hence
X/_{H}G
is homeomorphic to a closed Euclidean ball.

Example 6.56
.

Every proper initial matroid of
U_{2,4}
is binary. Thus
X/_{H}G
is homeomorphic to a two dimensional closed disc.

Example 6.57
.

For the non-Fano matroid
J=F_{7}^{-}
, the space
\log\ulineGr_{J}({\mathbb{T}}_{0})
consists of exactly one ray. The two maximal cells of the corresponding regular matroid subdivision are the base polytopes of the Fano matroid and another matroid which is graphic by
[
25
]
*Theorem 5.4. In particular, both are binary. The space
X/_{H}G
is homeomorphic to a closed interval, with one endpoint corresponding to the non-trivial matroid subdivision.

Example 6.58
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}_{11}
be the matroid from
Section

6.2
. We have already seen that
\log\ulineGr_{J}({\mathbb{T}}_{0})
consists of five rays. A computer calculation shows that every initial matroid of
{\mathcal{T}}_{11}
is binary. Therefore, the space
X/_{H}G
is homeomorphic to a four-dimensional closed ball.

6.3.6.
The Grothendieck–Knudson moduli space of stable rational curves

Recall that the Chow quotient of
{\Gr}(2,n)({\mathbb{C}})
is isomorphic to the Grothendieck–Knudson moduli space
\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}_{0,n}
of stable rational curves with
n
marked points
[
33
]
*Chapter IV. By
6.48
, there is a natural continuous map

\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}_{0,n}\longrightarrow\textnormal{HC}({\ulineL}(2,n)_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boxtimes$}}),

where
\textnormal{HC}({\ulineL}(2,n)_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boxtimes$}})=\textnormal{HC}({\ulineL}_{U_{2,n}})
.
We discuss this map for
n=4
and
n=5
. (These are the first interesting cases, as for
n<4
source and target are a point.) We further observe that, by construction, the map is constant on orbits of the action of complex conjugation on
\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}_{0,n}
.

The space
\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}_{0,4}
is the complex projective line, and by
Section

6.3.5
the space
\textnormal{HC}({\ulineL}(2,4))
is homeomorphic to a closed disc.

Lemma 6.59
.

The map
\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}_{0,4}\to\textnormal{HC}({\ulineL}(2,4)_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boxtimes$}})
is the quotient by the action of complex conjugation.

Proof.

Since the map is continuous and both source and target are compact Hausdorff spaces, it suffices to show that the map is surjective and its fibers are exactly the orbits under complex conjugation. By
Section

5.1
, the map
{{\mathcal{M}}}_{0,4}\to{\ulineL}_{U_{2,4}}
is surjective. This establishes the surjectivity of our map, because
\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}_{0,4}
and
\textnormal{HC}({\ulineL}(2,4)_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boxtimes$}})
are compactifications of
{{\mathcal{M}}}_{0,4}
and
{\ulineL}_{U_{2,4}}
. Fibers of points in
{\ulineL}_{U_{2,4}}
are orbits by
Section

5.1
. The compactification
\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}_{0,4}
has three additional points, all of them are real, and these are mapped injectively to the three additional points in the compactification of
{\ulineL}_{U_{2,4}}
.
∎

The space
\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}_{0,5}
is the complex projective plane blown-up at four general points. By
Section

5.1
, the image of the map
\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}_{0,5}\to\textnormal{HC}({\ulineL}(2,5)_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boxtimes$}})
is the closure of
\partial\ulineL_{U_{2,5}}
inside
\textnormal{HC}({\ulineL}(2,5)_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boxtimes$}})
, which we denote by
\partial\textnormal{HC}({\ulineL}(2,5)_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boxtimes$}})
.

Lemma 6.60
.

The map
\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}_{0,5}\to\partial\textnormal{HC}({\ulineL}(2,5)_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boxtimes$}})
is the quotient by the action of complex conjugation.

Proof.

As in
Section

6.3.6
, we need to show that each fiber of the map is a single orbit under complex conjugation.
By the description in
[
33
]
*Section 1.2, for every cycle
Z\in\Gr(2,5)({\mathbb{C}})/\!\!/({\mathbb{C}}^{\times})^{5}
there is a regular matroid subdivision of the base polytope of
U_{2,5}
, corresponding to matroids
M_{1},\ldots,M_{r}
, such that

Z=Z_{1}\cup\cdots\cup Z_{r},

where each
Z_{i}
is the orbit closure of a point from
\Gr_{M_{i}}({\mathbb{C}})
. The fiber over the image of
Z
is equal to

\{\mysigma^{e_{1}}(Z_{1})\cup\cdots\cup\mysigma^{e_{r}}(Z_{r})\mid e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}\in\{0,1\}\},

where
\mysigma
denotes complex conjugation. However, for every regular matroid subdivision of the base polytope of
U_{2,5}
, at most one of the
Z_{i}
is not mapped to itself by
\mysigma
. Thus the fiber over the image of
Z
is just the orbit of
Z
.
∎

Remark 6.61
.

The quotient map of
\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}_{0,5}
by complex conjugation was studied in detail in
[
52
]
, and it exhibits some beautiful combinatorics. For example, the authors define cell structures on
\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}_{0,5}
and its quotient which are, in a certain precise sense, dual to the Desargues graph and the Petersen graph, respectively.

For
n\geqslant 6
, fibers of
\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}_{0,n}\to\textnormal{HC}({\ulineL}(2,n)_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boxtimes$}})
can consist of more than one complex conjugate pair:

Example 6.62
.

Let
n\geqslant 6
, let
a,b\in{\mathbb{C}}
be distinct non-real complex numbers, and let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
be pairwise distinct real numbers. We construct a stable curve
X_{a,b}
with
n
marked points that has two irreducible components. On the first component, we mark the three real points
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
, while the remaining
n-3
points
c_{4},\ldots,c_{n}
are marked on the second component. Finally, the point
a
on the first component is identified with the point
b
on the second one. Then the four stable curves
X_{a,b}
,
X_{\bar{a},b}
,
X_{a,\bar{b}}
, and
X_{\bar{a},\bar{b}}
represent four distinct points in
\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}_{0,n}
which are all mapped to the same point under the map
\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}_{0,n}\to\textnormal{HC}({\ulineL}(2,n)_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boxtimes$}})
.
