Integrated LC-MS-based lipidomics and transcriptomics reveal lipid heterogeneity and flavour variation in different cuts of muscles from Sadumont lamb
Authors: Tao Guo, Baiming Xing, Ruixue Nie, Wenwen Wang, Na Liu, Xiaoping An, Jingwei Qi, Yuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Substantial improvements in lamb production have been achieved through genetic selection and advanced husbandry practices; however, whether flavour heterogeneity among different muscle anatomical regions is driven by region-specific metabolic and lipid regulatory patterns remains unclear. We hypothesised that distinct muscle cuts exhibit characteristic flavour profiles associated with differential amino acid composition, lipid molecules, and gene profiles. To verify this hypothesis, the longissimus dorsi muscle (LDM), front leg muscle (FLM), and hind leg muscle (HLM) of Sadumont lamb were systematically compared using integrated omics.Significant differences in amino acid composition were observed among muscles, with glycine and valine showing pronounced variation. The FLM exhibited higher levels of delicious amino acids than LDM and HLM. Volatile profiling revealed distinct aroma signatures, with hexanal predominating in LDM, 1-octen-3-ol and d-limonene characterising FLM, and octanal, nonanal, and benzaldehyde enriched in HLM. Lipidomic analysis showed that LDM was dominated by triacylglycerols, whereas FLM was enriched in phospholipids, particularly phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylinositol, which are associated with lipid oxidation and flavour precursor generation. The HLM displayed an intermediate lipid profile with slightly elevated C22:6 and C20:5n3. Transcriptomic analysis identified 2595 differentially expressed genes, which were enriched in lipid-related pathways, including fatty acid elongation and metabolism. Notably, divergent expression of PPARA and ACAA2 supported differential regulation of fatty acid oxidation across muscle cuts.These findings support our hypothesis that flavour heterogeneity among muscle cuts is closely linked to cut-specific lipid components and gene expression. Additionally，these molecular markers could be utilised in precision breeding and meat quality optimisation.

Introduction

Muscles from different parts of the carcass perform distinct physiological functions, resulting in marked differences in muscle metabolic activity and lipid deposition patterns (Dashdorj et al., 2015; Qi et al., 2023). Lamb is a notable choice among various meats, primarily due to its unique volatile organic compounds and fatty acids, particularly its high contents of unsaturated fatty acids (Li et al., 2022; Realini et al., 2021). Among commercial lamb breeds, Sadumont lamb, a hybrid breed produced by crossing Suffolk (sire) and Dumont (dam) sheep, has attracted increasing attention because of its favourable growth performance, carcass traits, and meat production potential. However, despite its growing importance in lamb production systems, the molecular mechanisms underlying meat quality traits in this breed remain poorly understood.

Muscle tissue in lamb carcasses exhibits substantial heterogeneity, and different cuts demonstrate variations in lipid composition, flavour profile, and market value (Wang et al., 2024). Sensory studies have demonstrated that muscles such as the loin, rib, brisket, and leg differ significantly in flavour intensity and aromatic complexity, with fat-rich tissues often contributing more pronounced and diverse volatile compounds to the flavour profile (Qi et al., 2023). These observations highlight the importance of muscle-specific lipid metabolism in shaping lamb meat quality.

Lipid content and fatty acid composition exhibit significant variation across muscles within various species. For instance, studies on cattle have shown that intramuscular fat (IMF) content is higher in rib and ribeye muscles compared to striploin and tenderloin (Yu et al., 2023). Similarly, in lamb, the Longissimus lumborum has lower total fat and saturated fatty acid content but higher levels of long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids compared to other muscles (Gruffat et al., 2021). These variations in fatty acid profiles are also observed in pigs, where hind leg muscles contain higher proportions of polyunsaturated fatty acids compared to other cuts (Duan et al., 2024). However, the molecular mechanisms underlying muscle-specific lipid deposition and metabolism in lamb remain poorly understood.

Ruminants, exhibit complex lipid metabolism due to microbial biohydrogenation in the rumen, which alters dietary fatty acids into bioactive compounds before deposition in tissues (Sato et al., 2021; Schumacher et al., 2022). These processes contribute to distinct lipid profiles in various tissues, impacting both sensory traits and nutritional quality. However, traditional biochemical or single-gene approaches are limited in their ability to comprehensively elucidate the regulatory networks underlying these complex traits.

Recent advances in high-throughput omics technologies have resulted in the development of powerful tools to investigate meat quality at a systems level. Transcriptomics has been extensively used to investigate genes and pathways involved in muscle development and lipid metabolism in various livestock species (Albuquerque et al., 2021, Meng et al., 2020, Zhang et al., 2014). Lipidomics enables detailed characterisation of lipid species that directly affect flavour development, juiciness, oxidative stability, and nutritional quality, including triglycerides, phospholipids, and polyunsaturated fatty acids (Wang et al., 2019; Cao & Xu, 2024). Volatilomics, when integrated with these omics approaches, allows for a comprehensive understanding of flavour compounds in meat and their interaction with lipid composition. Increasing evidence suggests that integrating transcriptomics with lipidomics provides deeper insights into the molecular regulation of intramuscular fat deposition and lipid remodelling than either approach alone (Zhao et al., 2025).

Despite these advances, little is known about the muscle-specific molecular mechanisms underlying lipid metabolism and flavour formation in Sadumont lamb. Therefore, We hypothesised that different muscle cuts of Sadumont lamb exhibit distinct lipidomic and transcriptional signatures that collectively contribute to variations in flavour-related volatiles and nutritional attributes. Therefore, this study employed an integrated volatilomic, transcriptomic, and lipidomic approach to comprehensively characterize the molecular and biochemical differences among multiple muscle tissues in Sadumont lamb. This study contributes to elucidating the biological basis of phenotypic differences observed in different cuts of lamb, advancing our understanding of lamb meat quality.

Materials and methods

Animals and sample collection

Approval No. NND2023056 was granted by the Inner Mongolia Agricultural University Research Ethics Committee for the protocols involving the experimental animals in this study. Sadumont lambs were obtained from farm in Wulanhua Town, Siziwang Banner, Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region. Under identical feeding conditions (group housing with fully slatted flooring systems) and the same diet formulation (the ingredients and chemical composition of the basal diet are listed in Table S1.) the lambs were fattened until reaching slaughter weight. Six Sadumont lambs with similar body weights (45.62 ± 2.31 kg) were randomly selected from the flock and transported to a slaughterhouse in the same town within approximately 30 min. Upon arrival, the lambs were allowed to rest in pens for 12 h, after which humane slaughter was carried out following standardized procedures (animals were rendered unconscious prior to slaughter by intravenous injection of an overdose of sodium pentobarbital). Muscle samples were collected in the order of front leg muscle (FLM), longissimus dorsi (LDM), and hind leg muscle (HLM). A small piece of tissue from each muscle cut was collected and quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen. After the samples were collected, the next individual was slaughtered, and this procedure was repeated sequentially until muscle tissues from 6 sheep were collected. All samples were subsequently stored at −80 °C for further experimental analyses. In this study, blinding was not applied during either sample processing or data analysis.

Amino acid composition

The amino acid composition for FLM, HLM, and LDM samples (n = 6) was analysed using the method described by Ji et al. (2025), in accordance with the guidelines provided in Chinese Standard GB 5009.124–2016 (Determination of Amino Acids in Food under National Standards for Food Safety). The FLM, LDM, and HLM samples were subjected to acid hydrolysis for sample preparation. In brief, the muscle samples were first freeze-dried at −80 °C for 48 h and then 50 mg of muscle tissue was placed into a digestion tube. Subsequently, 10.0 mL of 6.0 mol/L HCl was added to the tube, and the sample was subjected to hydrolysis in an oven at 110 °C for 24 h, with precautions taken such as keeping the tube in an ice bath and filling it with nitrogen gas before sealing. After hydrolysis, the solution was transferred to a volumetric flask and made up to a constant volume of 25 mL. A portion (0.5 mL) of this solution was taken, concentrated to dryness using N2 in a dry bath at 50 °C, and reconstituted to 2.5 mL with a 0.02 mol/L hydrochloric acid solution. Approximately 1.5 mL of this solution was then filtered using a 0.22-μm filter membrane and subsequently analysed for amino acid content using an amino acid analyser (L8900, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Fatty acid composition

The fatty acid composition of FLM, HLM, and LDM (n = 6) was analysed according to Chinese Standard GB 5009.168–2016 (China National Food Safety Standard - Determination of Fatty Acids in Foods), with some modifications. Briefly, 50 mg samples were homogenised in 10 mL tubes with 3 mL of n-hexane, followed by shaking at 50 °C for 30 min using a MIX-3000 oscillator (Miou Instrument Co., Hangzhou China). After adding 3 mL of 0.4 mol/L KOH-methanol, the mixture was shaken under the same conditions, allowed to cool to room temperature, and then mixed with 1 mL ultrapure water. A 90 μL aliquot of the supernatant was combined with 10 μL of an internal standard (methyl nonadecanoate, 125 μg/mL, Sigma Co., St. Louis, Mo, USA) in a 2 mL vial for subsequent Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis.

GC analysis (Agilent 8860, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was performed using a 30 m × 0.32 mm × 0.25 μm polysiloxane column with helium carrier gas, employing a split ratio of 10:1. The temperature of the injector and detector was maintained at 250 °C and 230 °C, respectively. The temperature program was initiated at 50 °C for 1 min, then increased at a rate of 25 °C/min to 175 °C, where it was held for 3.5 min. It was then ramped up at a rate of 4 °C/min to a final temperature of 230 °C, holding for 1 min. For the GC–MS analysis, an Agilent 5977C (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used with the ion source set at 230 °C and the interface at 280 °C, with the ionisation energy of 70 eV. Fatty acids were quantified based on peak area and expressed as g/100 g total fatty acids.

Volatile compound profiles

Volatile compounds in FLM, HLM, and LDM (frozen tissue samples, n = 6) were analysed using headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) coupled with GC–MS/MS. For this analysis, 2 g of each sample was homogenised and extracted with a manual SPME injector (57382-U, Sigma Co., St. Louis, MO, USA) fitted with a 50/30 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS fiber for 40 min at 55 °C.

For the GC analysis, it was conducted using an Agilent 7890B system with a DB-WAX column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm), and helium was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1 mL/min under splitless mode. The oven temperature program was set to 50 °C for 7 mi, nutesfollowed by a ramp of 10 °C/min to 100 °C, and then a ramp of 5 °C/min to 230 °C with a 10-min hold at this final temperature. The temperature of the injector was maintained at 220 °C.

For MS analysis, a QP2010 (Shimadzu Co., Kyoto, Japan) was used, with electron ionisation set at 70 eV, and the ion source temperature was set to 230 °C. It was conducted in full scan mode, covering a mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) range of 20 to 450.

The mass spectrum obtained from the GC–MS analysis was compared with the NIST standard spectral database on the computer, and the top three substances with a matching degree of 80% or higher were selected as reference flavour compounds. The relative content of each component in the sample was then calculated using the peak area normalisation method.

The volatile flavour compounds among the three muscle cuts were first analysed using a general linear mixed model, followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. Compounds with a P-value <0.05 were considered significantly different volatile flavour compounds.

Lipid extraction and lipidomic assay

Lipid extraction: Tissue samples (100 mg, n = 5) were deactivated with 750 μL methanol in Teflon-capped glass tubes. The samples were vortexed and then extracted with 2.5 mL of MTBE for 1 h with shaking. Phase separation was induced by adding 0.625 mL of MS-grade water and incubation for 10 mins, followed by centrifugation at 1000 ×g for 10 min. The organic phase was collected, and the aqueous phase was re-extracted with 1 mL of MTBE/methanol/water (10:3:2.5, v/v/v). The combined organic phases were dried using N2 gas, reconstituted in 100 μL of isopropanol, and stored for LC-MS analysis.

LC-MS analysis: A Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with an Accucore C30 column (150 × 2.1 mm, 2.6 μm) was coupled to a Q Exactive™ HF mass spectrometer. The separation utilised a 20-min gradient at a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min, maintained at 40 °C with mobile phase A (acetonitrile/water, 6:4, 10 mM ammonium acetate, 0.1% formic acid) and B (acetonitrile/isopropanol, 1:9, 10 mM ammonium acetate, 0.1% formic acid). The gradient was as follows: 30% B (0–2 min), 43% B (5 min), 55% B (5.1 min), 70% B (11 min), 99% B (16 min), and returning to 30% B (18.1 min).

MS parameters: The analysis was conducted in both positive and negative polarity modes: sheath gas, 138 KPa; auxiliary gas, 5 L/min (positive)/7 L/min (negative); sweep gas, 1 L/min; spray voltage, 3 kV; capillary, 350 °C; heater, 400 °C; S-Lens RF, 50; scan range, 114–1700 m/z; AGC target, 1e6 (MS1)/1e5 (MS2); collision energy, 25/30 eV (positive)/20/24/28 eV (negative); isolation window, 1 m/z; dynamic exclusion, 15 s.

Data processing: The raw data were analysed using Compound Discoverer 3.1 (Thermo Fisher) for peak alignment, picking, and quantitation. The main parameters were set as follows: retention time tolerance, 0.2 min; actual mass tolerance, 5 ppm; signal intensity tolerance, 30%; signal/noise ratio, 3; and minimum intensity, 100000. After that, peak intensities were normalised to the total spectral intensity. The normalised data were used to predict the molecular formula based on additive ions, molecular ion peaks and fragment ions. Peaks were then matched with the Lipidmaps and Lipidblast database to obtained the accurate qualitative and relative quantitative results. Lipids with a coefficient of variation (CV) < 30% in quality control (QC) samples were retained for further analysis. Lipid identification was performed based on precursor ion information, MS/MS fragmentation patterns, and database matching. Therefore, the identified lipids were considered as putatively annotated compounds (Level 2 identification).

Identification of the major and differential lipid molecules

Principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were performed by metaX (metaX 1.4.16, BGI Genomics Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China), a flexible and comprehensive software for metabolomics data processing. Statistical significance (P-value) was determined using Student's t-test. Metabolites with VIP > 1, P-value <0.05, and a fold change (FC) ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.667 were classified as differential metabolites. Volcano plots, generated based on Log2(FC) and -log10(P-value), were employed to visualise and screen metabolites of interest.

PLS-DA was used to construct a model describing the relationship between lipid compound abundances and sample categories based on partial least squares regression, thereby enabling prediction of sample classes. PLS-DA models were established for each comparison group, and model evaluation parameters (R2 and Q2) were obtained through 7-fold cross-validation (when the number of biological replicates n ≤ 3, k-fold cross-validation was applied, where k = 2n). Values of R2 and Q2 closer to 1 indicate a more stable and reliable model.

To further evaluate the model quality, permutation tests were performed to assess potential overfitting. Overfitting reflects whether the model construction is reliable: a non-overfitted model can effectively describe the samples and provides a valid basis for identifying potential biomarker sets, whereas an overfitted model is not suitable for describing the samples or for subsequent analyses. In this test, the class labels of each sample were randomly permuted and the model was rebuilt and evaluated. Each permutation generated a pair of R2 and Q2 values. After 200 permutations, regression lines of R2 and Q2 were obtained. The model is considered not overfitted when the R2 values are higher than the Q2 values and the intercept of the Q2 regression line on the Y-axis is below zero.

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA, n = 4). RNA quality and quantity were evaluated by gel electrophoresis, and the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (Novogene Bioinformation Science and Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Raw RNA-seq data were deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database under the Accession Number PRJNA1336618. Using the fastp software platform (Chen et al., 2018), adapters and low-quality reads were removed to obtain the clean reads. Clean reads were then mapped to the reference genome, ARS-UI_Ramb_v3.0, utilising HISAT2 v2.0.5 (Kim et al., 2015).

Genes were counted using featureCounts v1.5.0-p3 (Liao et al., 2014). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 v1.20.0 (Love et al., 2014) based on the original read counts, with the thresholds of |log2(fold change)| > 1 and adjusted P-value (P_adj) < 0.05. For the presentation of gene expression levels, FPKM values were calculated separately using StringTie v1.3.3b (Pertea et al., 2015) and were only used for visualisation and descriptive analysis. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomics (KEGG) enrichment analyses of DEGs were performed using the R package clusterProfiler v 3.8.1 (Yu et al., 2012) at a significance level of P-value <0.05. Gene Set Enrichment Analyses (GSEA) of all gene expression data were based on the local GSEA software v3.0.0 (Mootha et al., 2003; Subramanian et al., 2005). The significantly enriched functional pathways were screened according to the criteria of the absolute value of normalised enrichment score (NES) >1, P-value <0.05, and FDR < 0.25.

Quantitative real-time PCR assay (qRT-PCR)

Ten DEGs, namely, LDHA, PGM1, MDH2, PGK1, GPI, ACTN3, PLINS, ADIPOR2, ACAA2, and ACAT1, were randomly selected to validate expression profiles using qRT-PCR. The qRT-PCR assay was conducted as described by Kong et al. (Kong et al., 2023). Briefly, utilising FastKing gDNA Dispelling RT SuperMix (Tiangen, Beijing, China), 2 μg of RNA from each group (n = 4) were converted into cDNA through reverse transcription. qRT-PCR was performed on a Roche LightCycler® 480 (Roche Diagnostics Ltd., Rotkreuz, Switzerland) using 2 × SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (Abclonal, Wuhan, China). The primers used to quantify gene expression were designed using NCBI Primer-BLAST (Ye et al., 2012) and synthesised by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The 2−△△Ct method was used to calculate relative gene expression levels, with β-actin was used as a housekeeping gene (Table S2, Schmittgen & Livak, 2008).

Statistical analysis and data visualisation

All data were analysed using the SAS (Version 9.2, SAS Inst. Inc., Cary, NC, USA) software to perform general linear mixed models. The model incorporated lamb cuts (FLM, LDM, and HLM) as fixed effects, while animal was considered as the random effect. Model residuals were checked for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the data were transformed accordingly. The results were expressed as least squares mean (LSM) with standard deviation (SD). All tests were two-sided and were conducted at the 0.05 level. That is, P < 0.05 indicates a significant difference. Tukey's multiplicity adjustment was applied for pairwise comparisons among lamb cuts. Correlation analyses between lipid flavour precursors and volatile flavour compounds, as well as between lipidomics and transcriptomics data, were performed using Pearson's method in GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software), and P < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant. A Pearson correlation coefficient > 0.8 or < −0.8 was taken to indicate a strong correlation between the two compounds (Schober et al., 2018). Visualisation of the final result was performed using GraphPad Prism v8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and using the R package ggplot2 (Ginestet, 2011), SRplot (Accessed on 9 November 2023, https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn), EVenn (Chen et al., 2021), TBtools (Chen et al., 2020).

Discussion

Compared with other meats such as pork and beef, lamb has increasingly been favoured by consumers due to its dense nutrient profile and distinctive flavour (Teixeira et al., 2020). In this study, the overall nutritional composition of Sadumont lamb showed moderate variation across muscle cuts. Amino acid profiles differed among FLM, LDM, and HLM, with FLM exhibiting higher levels of glycine (Gly) and DAA. Amino acids are known precursors of flavour compounds generated during cooking, and some (e.g., Gly, Ser, and Ala) are associated with sweet and umami taste (de Araújo Cordeiro et al., 2020; Madruga et al., 2010; Ramalingam et al., 2019). However, their contribution to sensory perception depends on cooking conditions, concentration thresholds, and interactions with other compounds. Therefore, the observed differences primarily indicate compositional variation rather than confirmed flavour enhancement. In contrast, LDM and HLM contained relatively higher levels of essential amino acids, suggesting potential differences in nutritional value among cuts.

Notably, FLM exhibited a distinct lipid profile compared with the other muscle cuts. Fatty acid analysis showed that FLM had a higher UFA:SFA ratio and a higher relative content of linoleic acid (C18:2n6c), whereas LDM and HLM contained relatively higher levels of saturated fatty acids, including palmitic acid (C16:0). Given that lipid oxidation is a major pathway in meat flavour formation (Wood et al., 2008), these compositional differences may influence the generation of volatile compounds. Consistent with this, volatile compound analysis revealed variation in lipid-derived compounds among cuts, with higher levels of 1-octen-3-ol detected in FLM, while hexanal was more abundant in LDM and HLM (Duan et al., 2024; Van Ba et al., 2013). These findings suggest that FLM may possess a distinct lipid/VOC profile. However, the relationship between lipid composition, VOC formation, and sensory perception is complex and influenced by compound thresholds, interactions, and cooking conditions. Therefore, the sensory implications of these differences remain uncertain and require validation through controlled sensory studies.

The main fatty acids in PC and PE are UFA (C18:2n6, C20:4n6), while the ratio of saturated to unsaturated fatty acids in PI is equal (Bermingham et al., 2018). The present study shows that the relative content of PC in the FLM of Sadumont lamb is significantly higher than that in the other two muscle parts. This observation is consistent with, and may partly contribute to, the higher UFA content detected in the FLM compared with the other two muscle parts. In addition, the determination results of the molecular level of PC show that the relative content of PC (18:0/18:2) in the FLM is significantly higher than that in the other two muscle parts, and the relative content of PC (18:2/18:2) in the FLM is markedly higher than that in LDM. PA is an important intermediate product of lipid metabolism and a precursor for the synthesis of triglycerides and PI. This experiment shows that the relative content of PA and PI in the FL muscle is significantly higher than that in the other two muscle parts. Previous studies have suggested that phospholipids containing unsaturated acyl chains (e.g., C18:1, C18:2, C16:1, and C20:4) may participate in flavour formation through oxidation processes (Xu et al., 2022). Consistent with this, the present results show that the relative contents of PC (18:0/18:2) and PI (18:0/20:4) in the FLM were significantly higher than those in the other muscle parts, whereas PE (18:0/18:2) was significantly lower. Taken together, these findings suggest that differences in flavour characteristics among the three muscle parts of Sadumont lamb may be associated with variations in phospholipids containing unsaturated acyl chains. However, this relationship should be interpreted with caution, as the underlying mechanisms remain unclear and require further experimental validation.

Fat accumulation is a complex biological process that can be influenced at the transcriptional level, contributing to differences in adipose distribution among tissues. In the FLM vs. HLM comparison, a total of 2147 DEGs were identified. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that the sphingolipid signalling pathway was significantly enriched and associated with lipid metabolism. In addition, genes such as PRKZC, PRKZA, ADORA1 involved in this pathway were predominantly expressed in HLM. However, the specific roles of these genes in lipid metabolism remain unclear, and their functional significance requires further investigation. In the comparison of FLM vs. LDM, 228 DEGs were identifiedand grouped into four clusters based on their expression patterns. Genes in clusters C2 (n = 53) and C4 (n = 31) were highly expressed in FLM. Functional enrichment analysis indicated that genes in C2 were associated with pathways such as regulation of adipocyte lipolysis, PPAR signalling, and PI3K–Akt signalling, while genes in C4 were enriched in the AMPK signalling pathway (Gandhi et al., 2014; Homan et al., 2021; Hu et al., 2016; Świderska et al., 2021). Although previous studies have demonstrated that AMPK and PI3K–Akt signalling pathways play important roles in metabolic regulation, it should be noted that pathway enrichment and gene expression data alone are insufficient to establish direct regulatory mechanisms in the present context. Therefore, the involvement of these pathways in muscle-specific lipid metabolism in Sadumont lamb remains to be further validated. In addition, phospholipid-related signalling molecules, such as PIP2 and PIP3, are known to participate in PI3K-Akt pathway activation (Huang et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2025). In this study, the relative contents of PC, PI, and PA in FLM were significantly higher than those in LDM, which may be associated with differences in glycerophospholipid metabolism. However, the potential link between lipid composition and transcriptomic changes should be interpreted with caution, as no direct causal relationship can be established based on the current data. For the HLM vs. LDM comparison group, we found that the KEGG enrichment analysis identified many pathways, among which fatty acid degradation, fatty acid elongation, and fatty acid metabolism were significantly activated in the HLMs, and ACAA2 and HADH were both enriched in the above pathways. Acetyl-CoA acyltransferase 2 (ACAA2) is known to be involved in the final step of mitochondrial β-oxidation and plays an important role in fatty acid metabolism (Zhang et al., 2018). Similar expression patterns have been reported in previous studies comparing different muscle types (Liu, 2009). Although studies have suggested that ACAA2 may be associated with adipocyte differentiation, such findings are primarily based on in vitro experiments (Zhang et al., 2018). Therefore, its role in muscle lipid deposition and meat quality traits in vivo remains unclear. Overall, the present transcriptomic results provide preliminary insights into biological processes potentially related to lipid metabolism in different muscle tissues; however, interpretations regarding regulatory mechanisms, adipose lineage development, or applications in precision breeding and meat quality improvement require further experimental validation.

Despite these findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sensory implications of the observed lipid and VOC differences were not directly evaluated, limiting the ability to link molecular composition to consumer perception. Second, the study was conducted in a single breed (Sadumont lamb), and the generalizability of the findings to other breeds remains uncertain. Future studies should incorporate sensory evaluation, targeted metabolite validation, and functional experiments to confirm the biological relevance of the identified lipid species and genes. In addition, validation across diverse ovine populations (e.g., Texel, Dorper) would strengthen the applicability of these findings and support the development of precision breeding strategies for meat quality improvement.

Conclusions

This study conducted a comparative analysis of amino acid composition, fatty acid profiles, and volatile flavour compounds in Sadumont lamb muscles (FLM, LDM, and HLM). The results revealed distinct differences in lipid composition, volatile compounds, and gene expression profiles across muscle cuts. Transcriptomic analysis identified differentially expressed genes and pathways associated with lipid metabolism, while lipidomic results highlighted variations in phospholipid composition. Integrated multi-omics analysis suggested potential associations between gene expression patterns, lipid metabolism, and flavour characteristics. However, these findings remain preliminary, and the underlying mechanisms require further validation. Future studies are needed to clarify these relationships.
