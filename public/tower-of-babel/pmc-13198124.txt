Conduction System Disease
Authors: Syeda Atiqa Batul, Pugazhendhi Vijayaraman
Published: 2026

The conduction system comprises specialized electrical impulse–generating and propagating cells that function in connection with the autonomic nervous system to maintain an efficient synchronized cardiac pump function. The sinoatrial node, atrioventricular (AV) conduction axis (AV node/His bundle), and intraventricular conduction (right and left bundle branches and Purkinje network) work in a hierarchical manner. The cardiac pacemaker cells have the unique property of phase 4 automaticity generated by the balance of the decay of outward potassium repolarizing current (Ik) and the depolarizing sodium channel current often termed as the “funny” current (If). The action potentials in the AV nodal area are similar to the sinus node albeit with a slower conduction velocity (0.03 m/s). Conduction in the His-Purkinje network on the other hand is considerably faster (2.3 m/s) due to abundant high capacitance gap junction (connexins 43 and 40) and sodium-dependent action potentials.1

Dysfunction may occur at any level and may be a manifestation of a wide range of hereditary and acquired disorders often in association with concomitant structural heart disease. Acquired conduction system disease in the aging population mirrors concurrent comorbidities, ischemic or infiltrative cardiomyopathy, valvular dysfunction such as aortic stenosis, heart failure, and atrial fibrillation. Electrocardiography (ECG)-based studies suggest the prevalence of first-degree AV block to be 1% to 2% in the general population, increasing to 5% to 10% in the elderly.2,3

AV block as a result of surgical trauma, particularly in the era of percutaneous valvular interventions such at transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) and transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement, is increasing in incidence and requires complex decision making. The incidence of high-grade or complete heart block necessitating pacemaker implantation is estimated to be 1% to 5% in open-heart surgeries (particularly valve replacement surgery), between 5% and 33% in TAVR depending on the type of valve, and between 12% and 25% after percutaneous tricuspid valve replacement.4,5 Congenital heart disease and genetic disorders, although rare, are important, as they may be syndromic and may affect additional family members. Epidemiological data suggest that cardiac conduction disturbances in young adults (<50 years of age) represent about 13% of all conduction disorders.6 These studies also indicate an increased mortality, with second-degree AV block and distal conduction disease manifesting as bundle branch block or hemiblocks due to higher rates of progression to third-degree AV block with resultant complications such as syncope, heart failure, and sudden cardiac death.7

Risk stratification of patients based on symptom assessment, clinical history, and most importantly timely acquisition and accurate interpretation of ECG allows for appropriate lifesaving interventions. Analyzing the PR interval, QRS duration, Wenckebach phenomenon, and ventricular rates on surface ECG provides important clues to determine the level of block. The presence of certain pathognomonic and ominous features such as alternating bundle branch block and evidence of bifascicular or multilevel block in the relevant clinical setting should alert the treating physicians to impending complete heart block, asystole, and circulatory collapse. Patient outcomes in these instances can be greatly improved by early referral for cardiac pacing. In patients with infranodal AV block, the site of block is often discrete and most commonly intra-Hisian (89%). Pacing distal to the level of block can rectify these abnormalities and these patients are ideal candidates for conduction system pacing (CSP). Like AV block, bundle branch block (BBB) can exhibit first-, second- and third-degree block that may be unmasked by incremental atrial pacing. An ECG showing RBBB, left-axis deviation due to associated left anterior fascicular block and absent S-waves in leads I and aVL (noted in LBBB) is termed as masquerading bundle branch block (MBBB). Unequal activation of both bundle branches may generate this pattern that does not meet the absolute criteria for either left or right bundle branch block. Diffuse, distal Purkinje network disease due to scarring or fibrosis is less common and typically seen in elderly with underlying coronary artery disease and ischemic cardiomyopathy.8,9

In younger patients without evidence of overt structural heart disease, who present with symptoms suggestive of bradycardia or chronotropic incompetence, decision making may not be as straight forward. Additional testing such as exercise treadmill stress test may be needed to uncover concealed conduction abnormalities or rule out myocardial ischemia. Cardiac imaging such as computed tomography, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging and genetic testing should be considered where appropriate. It is prudent to exclude reversible causes such as infection (eg, Lyme's myocarditis), effect of drugs and electrolyte imbalance (hyperkalemia) before committing to device therapy.10

The cases presented in this special issue of JACC: Case Reports have been selected meticulously to highlight the patterns of conduction disease on ECG for the benefit of our readers. The clinical vignettes have been structured to facilitate a stepwise exclusion process to reach the correct diagnosis.
