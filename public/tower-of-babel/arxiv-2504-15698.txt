Shallow randomized measurement in noisy quantum devices
Gyungmin Cho, Dohun Kim

Abstract
Quantum hardware is steadily improving, but near-term quantum devices remain limited by noise and circuit depth. This motivates measurement protocols that can use shallow-depth circuits while remaining robust to experimental errors. In this work, we study the advantages of shallow randomized measurements over non-entangling single-qubit measurements for learning properties of quantum states. Although shallow measurements have shown advantages in selected applications, their usefulness across different learning tasks has not been systematically understood. Here, we develop a theoretical framework based on Clifford ensembles that incorporates shallow measurements into derandomized measurements, multi-shot protocols, common randomized measurements, error-mitigated estimators, and hybrid quantum-classical learning. Finally, we validate these results on IBM quantum hardware in experiments with up to 40 qubits and 46 layers of two-qubit gates. These results indicate that shallow-depth measurements can provide practical benefits on noisy quantum devices.

Introduction

Quantum computers are anticipated to demonstrate advantages over classical computers in tasks such as factorization
[
56
]
, machine learning
[
30
]
, and optimization
[
39
]
. Beyond these applications, quantum computers also serve as a new experimental tool in physics, facilitating the exploration of highly entangled quantum states
[
23
]
. Classically simulating such states is generally constrained by memory and computational costs that grow exponentially with system size; however, quantum computers enable the preparation of complex quantum states
[
55
,
53
]
and measurement of various physical quantities
[
9
,
17
]
, creating new pathways to study unexplored physics. Nevertheless, in cases where theoretical frameworks are incomplete, it may be unclear which physical quantities to measure, potentially requiring quantum memory or additional experiments.

One solution is to store quantum information classically via quantum state tomography
(QST). However, without further assumptions, its memory and computational costs grow exponentially with the system size
n

[
26
,
46
]
. To avoid full state reconstruction, shadow tomography
[
2
]
and, more recently, classical shadows
[
32
]
were proposed for predicting expectation values of many observables. These methods have been implemented on current noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices
[
60
,
68
,
49
]
.

Subsequent work has developed several variants of classical shadows.
Derandomized classical shadows
[
33
]
improve the estimation of predetermined observables by selecting measurement bases deterministically.
Robust shadows
[
12
]
mitigate errors in randomized measurements, while multi-shot shadows
[
27
,
71
]
reduce the need to sample a new unitary for every shot.
Other extensions include fermionic shadows
[
70
]
, tailored to fermionic systems, shallow shadows based on brickwork circuits
[
6
,
28
,
29
]
, and methods that incorporate prior information about the prepared state
[
64
]
.

1.1
Contributions and scope

The developments reviewed above motivate a unified treatment of shallow
randomized measurements that can accommodate the main extensions of classical
shadows. In this work, we develop such a framework based on Clifford measurement ensembles. We first combine derandomization with shallow measurements, obtaining deterministic measurement bases whose confidence bound is no larger than the corresponding randomized average. While previous studies of shallow shadows primarily focused on brickwork circuits, we study block-shadow circuits, for which the shadow channel and its inverse can be analyzed explicitly. This structure enables extensions to multi-shot protocols, common randomized measurements with an approximate bias state, and error-mitigated estimators. We also show that classical machine-learning models trained on data from shallow measurements can outperform those trained on single-qubit measurement data in a many-body physics task. Finally, we validate these results on IBM quantum hardware and observe that the predicted advantages can persist in the presence of experimental noise.

1.2
Related work

Classical shadows
[
32
]
based on random Pauli
measurements and random Clifford measurements provide two standard reference
points. Random Pauli measurements are experimentally simple and are well suited
for local observables, whereas random Clifford measurements are effective for
low-rank observables but require global Clifford circuits with

\mathcal{O}(n)
depth in standard implementations without ancilla
qubits
[
8
]
. Shallow shadows

[
6
,
28
,
29
]
aim to interpolate between these two limits using
entangling measurement circuits of depth much smaller than
\mathcal{O}(n)
;
in this work, we focus on the
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
-depth regime. Most
previous analyses of shallow shadows considered brickwork random circuits. Here,
we use Clifford measurement ensembles as a common framework for shallow
randomized measurements, and focus on block Clifford measurements when
closed-form expressions for the shadow channel, its inverse, and the moments
entering the variance analysis are needed. These explicit formulas allow us to
derive sample-complexity bounds and track post-processing costs without
introducing an additional approximation in the inverse channel.

Our setting is also related to recent approaches based on local random circuits
that form approximate unitary designs
[
54
]
. These works
give worst-case guarantees by showing that the moment channel of the implemented
ensemble approximates the corresponding Haar moment channel in relative error.
In principle, one can define an unbiased estimator by using the exact inverse
channel of the implemented measurement ensemble. In practice, however, this
inverse channel may be difficult to compute or use directly. Moreover, since the
inverse shadow map is not a physical quantum channel, order-type inequalities
for moment channels do not directly translate into comparable inequalities
after applying the inverse map. For this reason, existing protocols often use
the inverse Haar shadow channel as a tractable substitute. This gives a
controllable weak bias for some observable-prediction tasks when the implemented
ensemble is sufficiently close to the target design, but it is not directly
applicable to tasks, such as single-shot purity estimation and quantum state
tomography, where a more refined inverse-channel treatment is needed. In the
block-shadow setting considered here, this issue is avoided because a
closed-form expression for the exact inverse channel is available.

Most of the favorable scaling results in this work should be understood as typical-case statements, except where worst-case bounds are stated explicitly. This viewpoint is close in spirit to previous analyses of brickwork shallow shadows
[
6
,
28
,
29
]
, which establish logarithmic-depth scaling for fixed, state-independent observables. The block Clifford structure allows us to extend this type of typical-case analysis to correlated state–observable settings, in which the observable is not fixed independently of the state being averaged over. Such settings are more difficult to treat for brickwork circuits. This situation arises, for example, in state verification, where the target observable may be the prepared state itself. In this correlated setting, we average the full state-dependent variance directly and show that shallow-depth block Clifford measurements achieve, in the typical-case regime, the same scaling as global random Clifford measurements. Similar moment calculations are also used in our analysis of purity estimation.

These typical-case results do not imply a worst-case guarantee. Indeed, we identify an explicit block-product family for which the relevant block-shadow norm scales as
3^{n/k}
. These counterexamples are highly aligned with the chosen block partition and illustrate a structured failure mode of block shadows. Thus, logarithmic-depth block measurements should not be viewed as worst-case replacements for global random Clifford measurements. To probe the relevance of the typical-case analysis beyond random states, we perform numerical simulations for physically motivated many-body states, including ground states and states generated by quench dynamics.

Block shadows also have a practical advantage in post-processing. For brickwork shallow shadows, the inverse shadow channel is generally not available in closed form and is often approximated numerically
[
6
,
28
,
29
]
. This requires an additional optimization or tensor-network approximation step. If an estimator is built from this approximate inverse, bounds derived for the exact inverse channel do not directly apply without controlling the additional approximation error.
For block shadows, the inverse channel is exact and has a product structure. This makes the post-processing more efficient than the corresponding brickwork shallow-shadow calculation in the settings considered here; see Appendices
C.2.3
and
C.3.2
for a detailed comparison.

Product-structured randomized measurements have also appeared in related contexts, including fidelity estimation
[
35
]
, quantum state tomography
[
58
]
, and measurements with analog quantum devices
[
62
]
. These works suggest that product structure can be useful in practice, but a general statistical explanation of the regimes in which such methods perform well has been less developed. Our analysis helps fill this gap by giving explicit variance and moment bounds for block Clifford measurements, and by identifying both typical-case advantages and structured worst-case counterexamples.

2
Classical shadow

The classical shadow is a method for predicting many properties of a quantum state without QST
[
32
]
. This method involves applying a unitary
U
, sampled from a random unitary ensemble
\mathbb{U}
, to the quantum state
\rho
, followed by a measurement that yields a bitstring
b\in\{0,1\}^{n}
as the outcome. By repeating the process
T
times, we can obtain the classical shadow
{S_{T}}(\rho)=\{{({U_{i}},{b_{i}})}\}_{i=1}^{T}
. Using the shadow channel
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{U}}
defined by

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{U}}(\rho)=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathbb{U}}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}U^{\dagger}\ket{b}\bra{b}U\,\operatorname{Tr}\!(U\rho U^{\dagger}\ket{b}\bra{b}).

(1)

Using this channel, we obtain an unbiased estimator of the quantum state
\rho
as
\hat{\rho}=\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{U}}^{-1}(U^{\dagger}\ket{b}\bra{b}U)
. To estimate the expectation value of
O
, we can use
\hat{o}=\text{Tr}(O\hat{\rho})
. The number of experiments required to predict with an additive error
\epsilon
is determined by the state-dependent shadow norm

\lVert O\rVert_{\mathrm{sh,\rho,\mathbb{U}}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathbb{U},b}\text{Tr}(O\hat{\rho})^{2}

(2)

Then, state-independent shadow norm is
\lVert O\rVert_{\mathrm{sh,\mathbb{U}}}=\max_{\rho}\lVert O\rVert_{\mathrm{sh,\rho,\mathbb{U}}}
and according to Chebyshev’s inequality, the required number of experiments is
T=\mathcal{O}(\text{Var}[\hat{o}]/\epsilon^{2})=\mathcal{O}(\lVert O\rVert_{\text{sh},\mathbb{U}}^{2}/\epsilon^{2})
.
In the case of
\mathbb{U}=\text{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}
, where
\text{Cl}(m)
denotes a
m
-qubit Clifford group, this corresponds to random Pauli measurement (RPM) suitable for measuring local observables. RPM offers practical advantages as it requires only single-qubit gates, making it implementable on noisy quantum hardware. In contrast,
\mathbb{U}=\text{Cl}(n)
, termed random Clifford measurement (RCM), is suitable for low-rank observables. However, implementing a
n
-qubit random Clifford gate requires a circuit depth proportional to
\mathcal{O}(n)
without ancilla qubits
[
8
]
, posing challenges for current noisy hardware. Shallow shadow
[
6
,
28
]
serves as an intermediate approach between these unitary ensembles, providing a smooth transition from RPM to RCM.

Figure 1:
(a) Structure of single-qubit measurement. Each single-qubit gate is uniformly sampled from the set of single-qubit Clifford gates. (b) Structure of a brickwork circuit. Each two-qubit gate is uniformly sampled from the set of two-qubit Clifford gates. (c) Structure of a block circuit. Each block is uniformly sampled from the set of Clifford gates acting on
k
qubits.

Many previous studies
[
17
,
32
,
60
,
68
,
33
]
have been conducted using RPM (Fig.
1
a). In shallow shadow, measurement circuits can be constructed using two different approaches: brickwork (shallow) shadow (Fig.
1
b) and block (shallow) shadow (Fig.
1
c). Brickwork measurements are composed of two-qubit gates acting on nearest-neighbor qubits. In the block measurements, we apply gates only within blocks of size
k
qubits. For convenience, we define the block weight
w_{k}(P)
for Pauli strings
P
(
P
is a tensor product of
n
Pauli matrices from
\{I,X,Y,Z\}
) as the number of blocks where
P
acts non-trivially. For example,
w_{1}(P)(=|\{i\in[n]:P_{i}\neq I\}|)
is a widely used weight and
0\leq w_{k}(P)\leq\lceil n/k\rceil
. Throughout,
\|A\|_{p}=(\operatorname{Tr}|A|^{p})^{1/p}
denotes the Schatten
p
-norm.

3
Block shadow

Previously, studies
[
6
,
28
,
36
]
have focused on random circuits with brickwork structures. In contrast, less is known about block shadow
[
35
,
61
]
, which also interpolates between RPM and RCM. Both are expected to behave similarly; however, unlike brickwork shadow, block shadow has a structure suitable for computing the shadow channel and its inverse, thereby facilitating post-processing and allowing straightforward extensions to the previously introduced robust shadow
[
12
]
. We assume that, for block size
k
, a unitary gate for each block is sampled uniformly from Cl(
k
). The depth
d
required to implement the unitary from Cl(
k
) is
d=ck
, where
c
depends on hardware connectivity. Below, we set
\mathbb{U}=\text{Cl}(k)^{\otimes n/k}
and omit for simplicity.

First, we obtain the shadow channel of block shadow
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{k}^{\otimes n/k}
where
\mathcal{M}_{k}(A)=(A+\operatorname{Tr}(A)I_{k})/(2^{k}+1)
and
I_{k}
is
2^{k}\times 2^{k}
identity matrix. Following this, the shadow norm of Pauli strings
P
can be written as

\lVert P\rVert_{\mathrm{sh}}^{2}=(2^{k}+1)^{w_{k}(P)}.

(3)

Eq. (
3
) indicates that the shadow norm of a fully-packed Pauli string
P
becomes
\lVert P\rVert_{\mathrm{sh}}^{2}=(2^{k}+1)^{n/k}
in case
k
divides
n
. This allows us to observe a transition of
\lVert P\rVert_{\mathrm{sh}}^{2}/2^{n}
from exponential to constant dependence on
n
at
k2^{k}=\Omega(n)
consistent with the previous findings on brickwork circuits
[
6
]
. These results indicate that the shadow norm of observables with the bounded
\|\cdot\|_{2}
-norm is constant in typical cases
[
6
]
.

However, this fixed-observable argument does not directly apply when the target observable is correlated with the prepared state, as in state verification where the observable may be the ideal target state.
The average over
\rho
then involves correlations between the observable and the prepared state, rather than only the fixed-observable shadow norm.
Using the block structure of the unitary ensemble, for
k2^{k}=\Omega(n)
,

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\!\left[\|\rho\|_{\mathrm{sh},\rho}^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}(1),

(4)

where the average is over Haar-random pure states and
\rho
is used both as the prepared state and as the rank-one target observable (Appendix
B.5
).

More generally, for any observable
O
, block shadows with
k2^{k}=\Omega(n)
satisfy

\|O\|_{\mathrm{sh,\rho}}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(3^{n/k}\|O\|_{2}^{2}).

(5)

This scaling is tight, for instance, when

O=\rho=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n/k}\ket{\Psi_i}\!\bra{\Psi_i}
.
Thus, without additional structure, a constant shadow norm—and hence

T=\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{2})
—requires
k=\Omega(n)
(Appendix
B.6
).

A recent approach
[
54
]
can address this worst-case regime by doubling the block size and applying an additional staggered block layer, at the cost of a deeper measurement circuit. Our result is complementary: it shows that the simpler block circuit in Fig.
1
c already achieves constant average-case scaling for fidelity estimation, and we numerically observe this behavior for states arising in several many-body settings, including ground states and states after quenched dynamics (Appendix
C.2.5
).

Besides Pauli strings and low-rank observables, purity estimation via classical shadows has been used as a subroutine in many studies
[
17
,
63
]
.
To explore the potential advantages of block shadows for purity estimation, we prove that, in the single-shot setting, the number of experiments
T
required to estimate
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^{2})
within additive error
\epsilon
satisfies

T=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\max\left\{3^{n/k}/\epsilon^{2},\,2^{n}/\epsilon\right\}\right)

(6)

when
k2^{k}=\Omega(n)
.
The proof is provided in Appendix
B.8
.
This extends the existing analysis for RPM
[
17
,
59
,
50
]
.
For constant
\epsilon
, block shadows with
k2^{k}=\Omega(n)
and
k\geq 2
achieve the same sample-complexity scaling as RCM.
A similar trend also appears in probing quantum chaos via the spectral form factor
[
40
]
, as detailed in Appendix
B.22
.

For single-shot purity estimation, applying the estimator used for worst-case fidelity estimation in Ref.
[
54
]
does not by itself guarantee the same sample-complexity scaling as RCM with logarithmic-depth measurement circuits.
This is consistent with the numerical observations reported in Ref.
[
15
]
.

Derandomized block shadow

Randomized measurement (RM) is an efficient measurement strategy when the physical observables are unknown in advance
[
32
]
. However, in cases like variational quantum algorithms
[
47
]
, where the observables to be measured are predetermined, RM may not be optimal. To overcome this, recent studies introduced a derandomization method, consistently outperforming RM on average
[
33
]
. We aim to extend this to a multi-qubit measurement. Although there has been an attempt
[
67
]
to derandomize RCM, the need to implement an
\mathcal{O}(n)
depth unitary on NISQ devices poses a challenge. To address this, we used block shallow measurements and implemented the derandomization algorithm based on the following two key facts.

Fact 1
.

Let
\mathbb{U}_{\mathrm{MUB}}=\{U_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{2^{n}+1}
be a set of Clifford unitaries such that
\{U_{\ell}^{\dagger}\ket{b}:b\in\{0,1\}^{n}\}
, for
\ell=1,\ldots,2^{n}+1
, forms a complete set of mutually unbiased bases on
n
qubits. Then
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{U}_{\mathrm{MUB}}}=\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{Cl}(n)}

[
67
]
(Appendix
B.1
).

Fact 2
.

There exists a Clifford ensemble
\mathbb{U}_{0}\subseteq\mathrm{Cl}(n)
with
|\mathbb{U}_{0}|\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}(2^{i}+1)
such that, for every observable
O
,
\lVert O\rVert_{\mathrm{sh},\mathbb{U}_{0}}=\lVert O\rVert_{\mathrm{sh},\mathrm{Cl}(n)}
(Appendix
B.2
).

Let
\mathbf{P}=\{P_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
be a set of Pauli strings and
\mathbf{B}=\{U_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
be a set of measurement bases. Define
M_{l}(\mathbf{B})=\sum_{m}\mathbf{1}\{U_{m}P_{l}U_{m}^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\}
and

\text{CONF}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{P};\mathbf{B})\coloneqq\sum_{l=1}^{L}\exp(-\epsilon^2M_l(\mathbf{B})/2).

(7)

If
\text{CONF}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{P};\mathbf{B})\leq\delta/2
, then the expectation values of all
\{P_{l}\}_{l}
can be estimated within additive error
\epsilon
with failure probability at most
\delta

[
33
]
.
By applying the derandomization procedure, we obtain an output set of measurement bases
\mathbf{B}^{\#}
satisfying

\text{CONF}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{P};\mathbf{B}^{\#})\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}[\text{CONF}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{P};\mathbf{B})].

(8)

At each step of the derandomization, we choose a measurement basis that minimizes the conditional average confidence.
Using Fact
1
with block size
k
, the average confidence is

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}[\text{CONF}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{P};\mathbf{B})]=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left(1-\nu(2^{k}+1)^{-w_{k}(P_{l})}\right)^{M},

(9)

where
\nu=1-\exp(-\epsilon^2/2)
(Appendix
B.3
).
Thus, when Fact
1
is applied blockwise, only
2^{k}+1
candidate bases need to be searched for each block.

In the single-qubit case, the ensemble
\mathbb{U}=\{I,R_{Y}(-\pi/2),R_{X}(\pi/2)\}
already gives the same shadow norm as
\mathrm{Cl}(1)
for arbitrary observables.
For
k>1
, however, the ensemble in Fact
1
reproduces the shadow channel but does not necessarily reproduce the full Clifford shadow norm for arbitrary observables.
Fact
2
gives a sufficient, but larger, Clifford ensemble for this stronger property.
In practice, when implementing the derandomization algorithm, it may be useful to use an ensemble whose size lies between the MUB construction in Fact
1
and the sufficient construction in Fact
2
.

To assess the practicality of derandomized block shadows, we performed numerical and hardware experiments.
We used block shadows with block size
k=2
and considered the task of estimating the energy uncertainty
\langle H^{2}\rangle-\langle H\rangle^{2}
for the cluster-Heisenberg model

H_{\mathrm{CH}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}Z_{i}X_{i+1}Z_{i+2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(X_{i}X_{i+1}+Y_{i}Y_{i+1}+Z_{i}Z_{i+1}\right),

(10)

with open boundary conditions. In the numerical experiments, since
\langle H_{\mathrm{CH}}\rangle
can be measured using a small number of measurement bases, we focus on the number of experiments required to estimate
\langle H_{\mathrm{CH}}^{2}\rangle
.
The operator
H_{\mathrm{CH}}^{2}
contains many nonlocal Pauli strings, making it difficult to manually group simultaneously measurable terms.
We estimated the total number of experimental runs required to measure each Pauli string appearing in
H_{\mathrm{CH}}^{2}
at least 100 times, for system sizes up to
n=160
.
As shown in Fig.
2
a, derandomized block shadows require fewer measurements than single-qubit derandomization
[
33
]
across all system sizes considered.

On current noisy quantum computers, however, errors from the two-qubit gates used in shallow measurements may offset the sample-complexity reduction predicted by the noiseless analysis.
To test whether the advantage persists under hardware noise, we prepared an approximate ground state of
H_{\mathrm{CH}}
on a quantum computer with
n=20
qubits and estimated the Pauli strings appearing in
H_{\mathrm{CH}}^{2}
.
As shown in Fig.
2
b, derandomized block shadows still reduce the number of measurements required to achieve a given accuracy, even in the presence of hardware noise.
Experimental details are provided in Appendix
C.1
.

5
Clifford ensemble

Assuming a Pauli-invariant unitary ensemble
\mathbb{U}
, the following result holds
[
10
]
.

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{U}}(P)=m_{P}P,\quad\lVert P\rVert_{\text{sh}}^{2}=m_{P}^{-1}

(11)

where
m_{P}
is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{U}}
, and the Pauli string
P
is its eigenoperator. Here, we show that the above results still hold when the unitary ensemble is composed only of Clifford gates instead of requiring Pauli-invariance (Appendix
A.2
). There are three motivations for using the Clifford ensemble. First, because Clifford circuits can be simulated efficiently classically.
[
1
]
, post-processing after RM becomes more straightforward, and they can be further combined with tensor network formalism. In particular, we utilize a probabilistic interpretation of
m_{P}=\mathbb{E}_{U}[\mathbf{1}\{UPU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\}]=\text{Pr}_{U}(UPU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z})
, where
1\{\cdot\}
is the indicator function
[
6
]
. Second, through Pauli twirling, also known as randomized compiling
[
66
]
, the error channels arising from RM can be transformed into Pauli error channels. Finally, in many quantum error correction codes, Clifford gates are easier to implement than non-Clifford gates
[
22
,
48
]
. Building on this, we further demonstrate that it is possible to achieve various advantages of existing variants of classical shadows simultaneously.

For convenience in this section, we define the number of experiments
T=N_{U}N_{S}
, where
N_{U}
is the number of unitaries sampled from the unitary ensemble, and
N_{S}
represents the number of repeated measurements in the same basis.

First, we calculated the variance for a Pauli string
P
using a (brickwork or block) shallow shadow in the multi-shot case (
N_{S}>1
) as follows:

\text{Var}(\text{Tr}(\hat{\rho}P))=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\left(m_{P}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{N_{S}}+\frac{N_{S}-1}{N_{S}}\text{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}\right)-\text{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}\right).

(12)

This is consistent with previous results when
\mathbb{U}=\mathrm{Cl}(n)

[
27
,
71
]
and extends the multi-shot analysis to shallow shadows (Appendix
B.9
).

When estimating the expectation value of an observable

O=\sum_{P}\alpha_{P}P
using a shallow shadow in the multi-shot setting, the variance can be written as

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}(O\hat{\rho})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\left(\frac{1}{N_{S}}V_{2}(O,\rho)+\frac{N_{S}-1}{N_{S}}V_{1}(O,\rho)-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho O)^{2}\right)

(13)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N_{U}}\left(V_{1}(O,\rho)+\frac{V_{2}(O,\rho)}{N_{S}}\right),

(14)

where

V_{1}(O,\rho)=\sum_{P,Q}\alpha_{P}\alpha_{Q}\,\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)\operatorname{Tr}(\rho Q)\,f(P,Q),\quad V_{2}(O,\rho)=\sum_{P,Q}\alpha_{P}\alpha_{Q}\,\operatorname{Tr}(\rho PQ)\,f(P,Q),

(15)

and

f(P,Q)=\Pr_{U}\!\left(UPU^{\dagger},UQU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\right)/(m_{P}m_{Q})
(Appendix
B.10
).
Since
V_{1}(O,\rho)\leq V_{2}(O,\rho)
, increasing
N_{S}
does not increase the variance.
Moreover, for block shadows and fixed
O
, the Haar average over
\rho
satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[V_{1}(O,\rho)]=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\|O\|_{2}^{2}/D\right),\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[V_{2}(O,\rho)]=\mathcal{O}(\|O\|_{2}^{2})

(16)

when
k2^{k}=\Omega(n)
(Appendix
B.13
).
Thus, for typical states and bounded
\|O\|_{2}
, the
V_{2}/N_{S}
term can be suppressed by reusing each measurement basis, while the limiting term
V_{1}
remains small.
This makes multi-shot measurements beneficial for fidelity estimation in the average-case regime, even with logarithmic-depth circuits.
This contrasts with worst-case examples
[
27
,
71
]
, where
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
are comparable and increasing
N_{S}
gives only little improvement (Appendix
C.2.2
).

We next construct a purity estimator compatible with shallow shadows in the multi-shot setting.
For each sampled measurement basis
U_{i}
, we reuse the same basis
N_{S}
times and define

\hat{p}_{2}=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{U}}\hat{p}_{2}(U_{i}),\qquad\hat{p}_{2}(U)=\binom{N_{S}}{2}^{-1}\sum_{i>j}^{N_{S}}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}\ket{b_i}\!\bra{b_i}U\,\mathcal{M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}\ket{b_j}\!\bra{b_j}U\right)\right).

(17)

This estimator is unbiased,
\mathbb{E}[\hat{p}_{2}]=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^{2})
.
Moreover,

\operatorname{Var}(\hat{p}_{2})\leq\frac{1}{N_{U}}\left(V_{1}(\rho,\rho)+\frac{4V_{2}(\rho,\rho)}{N_{S}}+\frac{2V_{3}(\rho)}{(N_{S}-1)^{2}}\right),

(18)

where
V_{3}(\rho)=\sum_{P,Q}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho PQ)^{2}f(P,Q)/D^{2}
(Appendix
B.11
).

For block shadows with
k2^{k}=\Omega(n)
, the typical-case bounds
V_{3}(\rho)=\mathcal{O}(2^{n})
and
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[V_{1,2}(\rho,\rho)]=\mathcal{O}(1)
(Appendix
B.14
) imply that
N_{S}=\mathcal{O}(2^{n/2})
is sufficient for constant-accuracy (single-shot) purity estimation.
The same analysis extends to the distributed setting with shared randomness
[
4
]
, yielding analogous bounds for estimating the inner product
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\sigma)
between quantum states prepared by two parties
[
18
]
(Appendix
B.12
).

Although
N_{S}
still grows exponentially and is therefore not scalable to large
n
, it remains accessible for near-term experiments on systems of around 30 qubits.
This is useful in settings where purities of local patches are sufficient to infer global properties of interest
[
63
]
.
These results suggest that, in typical cases, local random Clifford circuits can approach the information-theoretically optimal sample complexity without requiring approximate unitary 4-designs
[
54
,
4
,
24
]
.

For explicit estimates, we focus on block shadows. While brickwork shadows may have comparable sample complexity, block shadows offer a post-processing advantage through their tensor-product structure and exact inverse shadow channel; see Appendices
C.2.3
and
C.3.2
for a detailed comparison.

6
Common randomized measurements using shallow shadows

When
\Tr(\rho P)
is close to
1
in Eq. (
12
), increasing
N_{S}
provides little benefit.
If prior information about the prepared state
\rho
is available, this limitation can be mitigated using common randomized measurements (CRM)
[
64
]
.
CRM replaces the standard estimator
\hat{\rho}
by

\hat{\rho}_{\sigma}=\hat{\rho}-\hat{\sigma}+\sigma,

(19)

where
\sigma
is a bias state approximating
\rho
, and
\hat{\sigma}=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{U}}\hat{\sigma}^{(U_{i})}

is estimated using the same measurement bases as
\hat{\rho}
.
This shared-basis construction can reduce the variance even when multi-shots alone are ineffective.

Here, we combine CRM with shallow shadows.
In the original CRM protocol, the bias contribution for a fixed measurement basis
U
is evaluated as
[
64
]

\hat{\sigma}_{\text{old}}^{(U)}=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\bra{b}U\sigma U^{\dagger}\ket{b}{\cal M}^{-1}(U^{\dagger}\ket{b}\bra{b}U).

(20)

We instead allow the bias contribution to be estimated from measurement outcomes of
\sigma
obtained in the same basis:

\hat{\sigma}_{{\rm{new}}}^{(U)}=\frac{1}{N_{\sigma}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\sigma}}{{\cal M}^{-1}}({U^{\dagger}}|{b_{j}}\rangle\langle{b_{j}}|U),

(21)

where
b_{j}
is sampled from measuring
U\sigma U^{\dagger}
.
With this estimator, the variance for a Pauli string
P
satisfies

\operatorname{Var}\!\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{\sigma}P)\bigr)\leq\frac{m_{P}^{-1}}{N_{U}}\left(\frac{1-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}}{N_{\rho}}+\frac{1-\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma P)^{2}}{N_{\sigma}}+\operatorname{Tr}((\rho-\sigma)P)^{2}\right),

(22)

where
N_{\rho}
and
N_{\sigma}
are the numbers of repeated measurements per basis for
\rho
and
\sigma
, respectively (Appendix
B.16
).
When
N_{\sigma}
is large, Eq. (
22
) reduces to the previous CRM bound
[
64
]
.

The extended estimator in Eq. (
21
) is useful when the bias state (
\sigma
) is available on another quantum device but does not admit an efficient classical description.
In this case, the bias contribution can be incorporated directly through measurement outcomes of (
\sigma
), without requiring a classical evaluation of the probabilities appearing in Eq. (
20
). Explicit comparisons of post-processing times are provided in Appendix
C.4.1
.

On the other hand, when the estimator in Eq. (
20
) is used together with a Clifford ensemble, its expectation value for a Pauli string
P
simplifies to

{\rm{Tr}}({\hat{\sigma}_{{\rm{old}}}}P)=m_{P}^{-1}{\rm{Tr}}(\sigma P){\bf{1}}\{UP{U^{\dagger}}\in\pm{\cal Z}\}.

(23)

Thus, for Pauli observables, the contraction over all bit strings in Eq. (
20
) can be bypassed (Appendix
B.17
).
In particular, this allows us to incorporate recently introduced Pauli propagation methods for approximately computing
\text{Tr}(\sigma P)

[
3
,
21
,
5
,
45
,
52
]
into randomized measurements.
Using this Clifford structure, efficient classical post-processing is possible for observables
O=\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{i}P_{i}
where
r=\mathrm{poly}(n)
, including many local Hamiltonians.

In the above analysis, we assume that the unitary ensemble consists only of Clifford gates. Consequently, the above results are valid for ensembles that fail to be Pauli-invariant but are composed solely of Clifford gates; one such example
[
70
]
is
\mathbb{U}=\text{Cl}(n)\cap\text{FGU}(n)
, where
\text{FGU}(n)
is the set of Fermionic Gaussian Unitaries for
n
fermionic particles.

7
Impact of noise on shallow shadows

We now analyze how errors affect the above results.
Two noise models for RMs have been studied in previous work
[
12
,
51
]
.
The first model assumes a noise channel applied after the random unitary
U
,
\Lambda_{U}=\Lambda\mathcal{U}
.
The second model describes layer-wise noise,

\Lambda_{U}=\Lambda_{t}\mathcal{U}_{t}\cdots\Lambda_{1}\mathcal{U}_{1}
,
where
\mathcal{U}_{l}
is the unitary channel corresponding to the
l
-th layer
U_{l}
,
\Lambda_{l}
is the noise channel at that layer, and
U=U_{t}\cdots U_{1}
.
In both cases, we assume that the noise channels are fixed and independent of the sampled unitary gates.

As mentioned above, when the measurement ensemble consists of Clifford gates, Pauli twirling converts the effective noise into a Pauli channel, which is unital
[
66
]
.
Thus, for a Pauli string
P
, we can write

\Lambda_{U}(P)=\lambda_{U,P}UPU^{\dagger}.

Using this fact, the noisy shadow channel

\widetilde{\cal M}(A)={\mathbb{E}_{U}}\left[{\sum_{b}}{U^{\dagger}}|b\rangle\langle b|U\,{\rm{Tr}}({\Lambda_{U}}(A)|b\rangle\langle b|)\right]

(24)

still has Pauli strings as eigenoperators (Appendix
B.18
).
Indeed,

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(P)=\widetilde{m}_{P}P,\qquad{\widetilde{m}_{P}}={\mathbb{E}_{U}}\left[{\lambda_{U,P}}{\bf{1}}\{UP{U^{\dagger}}\in\pm{\cal Z}\}\right].

(25)

Therefore, an error-mitigated estimator can be obtained by using
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}
instead of
\mathcal{M}^{-1}
.
Extending Eq. (
12
) to the noisy setting gives, for a Pauli string
P
,

{\rm{Var}}({\rm{Tr}}(\hat{\rho}P))=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\left[\widetilde{m}_{P}^{-2}\left(\frac{m_{P}}{N_{S}}+\frac{N_{S}-1}{N_{S}}{\rm{Tr}}(\rho P)^{2}{\mathbb{E}_{U}}\!\left[\lambda_{U,P}^{2}{\bf{1}}\{UP{U^{\dagger}}\in\pm{\cal Z}\}\right]\right)-{\rm{Tr}}(\rho P)^{2}\right],

(26)

as derived in Appendix
B.19
.
In the noiseless limit, where
\lambda_{U,P}=1
and
\widetilde{m}_{P}=m_{P}
, Eq. (
26
) reduces to Eq. (
12
).
The same analysis can also be applied to CRM in the presence of errors (Appendix
C.4.4
).

8
Entanglement-enhanced classical ML

Classical shadows were originally developed for predicting many properties of a quantum state.
Recent studies
[
31
]
have shown that classical ML models can also learn from quantum experimental data provided in the form of classical shadows, even when classical simulation of the underlying quantum system is computationally difficult.

Since the ML procedure is performed entirely on a classical computer after data acquisition, this approach is well suited for current NISQ devices.
Moreover, because it does not require specifying observables in advance, it can be useful for studying quantum systems whose relevant physical quantities are not fully known.
Applications have been demonstrated in theoretical and experimental studies of quantum phase recognition and ground-state learning in many-body systems
[
34
,
42
,
14
]
.

Previous studies
[
34
,
42
,
14
]
have mainly used classical shadows obtained from single-qubit measurements.
Here, we extend this approach to shallow measurements and study whether the additional information obtained from entangling measurement circuits can improve classical ML performance.
We use kernel principal component analysis (PCA)
[
7
]
as the classical ML algorithm, employ block shadows with block size
k=2
, and extend the previously introduced shadow kernel
[
34
]
as follows:

k^{(\text{shadow})}(S_{T}(\rho),S_{T}(\sigma))=\exp\left({\frac{\tau}{{{T^{2}}}}\sum_{t,t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\exp\left({\frac{\gamma}{{n/k}}\sum_{i=1}^{n/k}{\rm{Tr}}\left(\hat{\rho}_{i}^{(t)}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{(t^{\prime})}\right)}\right)}\right),

(28)

where
\hat{\rho}_{i}^{(t)}
is the
i
-th block of the
t
-th shadow sample for
\rho
,
\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{(t^{\prime})}
is defined similarly, and
\tau
and
\gamma
are hyperparameters.
The computational time for evaluating
k^{(\text{shadow})}
is
\mathcal{O}(4^{k}nT^{2})
.

As in previous work
[
34
]
, the performance guarantee assumes the existence of a local order parameter that distinguishes the phases.
When this order parameter is geometrically local, shallow shadows can provide an advantage over single-qubit shadows by capturing correlations within each block (Appendix
B.21
).

where the system is in a trivial phase for

and in a topological phase for
v<w
.
We prepared ground states of

for

with

.

to

.

9
Discussion

We have shown that shallow measurements allow predicting many physical quantities with fewer measurements and obtaining more refined classical data about a quantum state than single-qubit measurements. We developed a new theoretical framework that combines the advantages of various classical shadow variants
[
33
,
12
,
27
,
71
,
70
,
6
,
28
,
29
,
64
]
and analyzed the impact of errors. We also experimentally confirmed that our approach works effectively on current noisy quantum devices. Ultimately, by leveraging improved gate fidelities, we reduce the required number of experiments and continuously extend the applicability of NISQ devices. Our work opens several pathways for future research. For instance, building on our results, exploring potential enhancements in applications
[
35
,
34
,
42
,
58
]
using classical shadows could be a valuable direction for future research. Furthermore, integrating restricted quantum memory
[
13
]
into our approach is feasible on near-future quantum hardware, offering another promising avenue for follow-up studies.

Appendix A
Preliminaries

In this section, we collect the notation and elementary identities used in the
appendix.
Throughout,
n
denotes the number of qubits and
D=2^{n}
.
We write
\mathcal{H}^{D}
for the
D
-dimensional Hilbert space and

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{D})
for the space of linear operators on
\mathcal{H}^{D}
.
The Schatten
p
-norm of an operator
X
is denoted by
\|X\|_{p}
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}=\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
be the set of
n
-qubit Pauli
strings, with phases omitted.
For
A\subseteq[n]
, let
D_{A}=2^{|A|}
, and for a block size
k
, let

D_{k}=2^{k}
.
We denote by
\mathrm{Cl}(m)
the Clifford group on
m
qubits.
For block shadows,
k
denotes the block size, and for brickwork shadows,

d_{\mathrm{RM}}
denotes the two-qubit circuit depth of the randomized
measurement circuit.

We also define

\mathcal{Z}=\{I,Z\}^{\otimes n},\qquad(U,P,b):=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(UPU^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|\right),

where
P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
,
U
is a measurement unitary, and

b\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is a computational-basis outcome.

A.1
Projective state designs

We use standard moment identities for Haar-random pure states.
Let
\rho=\ket{\psi}\!\bra{\psi}
be a Haar-random pure state on
\mathcal{H}^{D}
.
For a positive integer
t
, its
t
-th moment is

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\rho^{\otimes t}]=\frac{1}{D(D+1)\cdots(D+t-1)}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{t}}U_{\sigma}.

(30)

Here,
S_{t}
is the symmetric group on
t
elements, and

U_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{L}((\mathcal{H}^{D})^{\otimes t})
is defined by

U_{\sigma}\ket{i_1}\ket{i_2}\cdots\ket{i_t}=\ket{i_{\sigma^{-1}(1)}}\ket{i_{\sigma^{-1}(2)}}\cdots\ket{i_{\sigma^{-1}(t)}}.

(31)

Equivalently,
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\rho^{\otimes t}]
is the normalized projector
onto the symmetric subspace of
(\mathcal{H}^{D})^{\otimes t}
.

More generally, an ensemble of pure states is called a projective
t
-design
if its
t
-th moment agrees with the Haar moment above.
Therefore, every Haar-average calculation below that only involves

\rho^{\otimes t}
remains valid if the average over
\rho
is replaced by an exact projective
t
-design.

We will use the following trace identity repeatedly.
For operators
A_{1},\ldots,A_{t}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{D})
,

\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[U_{\sigma}(A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{t})\right]=\prod_{(i_{1}\cdots i_{\ell})\in\sigma}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(A_{i_{\ell}}\cdots A_{i_{1}}\right),

(32)

where the product is taken over the disjoint cycles of
\sigma
.
For example, the second moment gives

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\rho^{\otimes 2}]=\frac{1}{D(D+1)}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{2}}U_{\sigma},

(33)

and hence

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\operatorname{Tr}(\rho A)\operatorname{Tr}(\rho B)]=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(A)\operatorname{Tr}(B)+\operatorname{Tr}(AB)}{D(D+1)}.

(34)

Similarly, the third moment used below is

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\rho^{\otimes 3}]=\frac{1}{D(D+1)(D+2)}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{3}}U_{\sigma},

(35)

and the fourth moment is

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\rho^{\otimes 4}]=\frac{1}{D(D+1)(D+2)(D+3)}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{4}}U_{\sigma}.

(36)

We can also consider state averages at the density-matrix level by using the
standard induced ensemble of random density matrices.
Let
E
be an environment with
\dim E=K
, and let

\ket{\Psi}\in\mathcal{H}^{D}\otimes E
be a Haar-random pure state.
The reduced state

\rho=\operatorname{Tr}_{E}\!\left(\ket{\Psi}\!\bra{\Psi}\right)

(37)

defines an induced ensemble on
\mathcal{H}^{D}
.
Its moments are obtained by applying the Haar moment formula on the enlarged
Hilbert space and tracing out the environment:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{(K)}[\rho^{\otimes t}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{E^{\otimes t}}\!\left[\mathbb{E}_{\Psi}\left[\left(\ket{\Psi}\!\bra{\Psi}\right)^{\otimes t}\right]\right]

(38)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{DK(DK+1)\cdots(DK+t-1)}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{t}}K^{c(\sigma)}U_{\sigma},

(39)

where
c(\sigma)
is the number of cycles in
\sigma
.
Here
U_{\sigma}
acts on the
t
copies of the system Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}^{D}
.

A.2
Probabilistic interpretation of the shadow norm

For a random unitary ensemble
\mathbb{U}
, the shadow channel is defined by

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{U}}(\rho)=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathbb{U}}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}U^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|U\,\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U\rho U^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|\right).

(40)

Given an outcome
b
obtained after applying
U
, the corresponding
classical-shadow estimator is

\hat{\rho}=\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{U}}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|U\right).

(41)

For an observable
O=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\alpha_{P}P
, the single-shot
estimator of
\operatorname{Tr}(O\rho)
is

\displaystyle\hat{o}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}(O\hat{\rho})

(42)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{U}}^{-1}(O)U^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|U\right)

(43)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\alpha_{P}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{U}}^{-1}(P)U^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|U\right).

(44)

Thus, for Pauli observables, it is enough to understand the action of

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{U}}
on Pauli strings.

Assume now that
\mathbb{U}
consists of Clifford unitaries.
Then the following identities hold for any fixed
U\in\mathbb{U}
.

•

For any Pauli string
P
,

(U,P,b)=(U,P,b)\,\mathbf{1}\{UPU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\}.

(45)

•

For Pauli strings
P_{1},\ldots,P_{r}
,

\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{r}(U,P_{\ell},b)=D\,\mathbf{1}\!\left\{\prod_{\ell=1}^{r}P_{\ell}=\pm I\right\}\prod_{\ell=1}^{r}\mathbf{1}\{UP_{\ell}U^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\}.

(46)

The first identity simply states that only Pauli strings diagonal in the
computational basis can contribute to a computational-basis measurement.
The second identity is the corresponding orthogonality relation after conjugation
by
U
.

Using these properties, we obtain, for any
P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{U}}(P)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\sum_{b}U^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|U\,\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(UPU^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|\right)

(47)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\sum_{b}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\frac{1}{D}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(UQU^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|\right)Q\,\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(UPU^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|\right)

(48)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\left[\mathbf{1}\{UPU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\}\right]P.

(49)

In the last line, we used Eq. (
46
).
Hence
P
is an eigenoperator of the shadow channel with eigenvalue

m_{P}:=\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\left[\mathbf{1}\{UPU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\}\right]=\Pr_{U}(UPU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}).

(50)

In particular,

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{U}}^{-1}(P)=m_{P}^{-1}P

(51)

whenever
m_{P}\neq 0
.

The state-dependent shadow norm of a Pauli string is then

\displaystyle\|P\|_{\mathrm{sh},\rho}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U,b}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{U}}^{-1}(P)U^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|U\right)^{2}

(52)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\sum_{b}m_{P}^{-2}(U,P,b)^{2}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U\rho U^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|\right)

(53)

\displaystyle=m_{P}^{-1}.

(54)

Thus, the usual Pauli-eigenoperator formula for Pauli-invariant ensembles also
holds for Clifford ensembles. For the block Clifford ensemble
\mathbb{U}=\mathrm{Cl}(k)^{\otimes n/k}
,
m_{P}^{-1}
is upper-bounded as follows:

m_{P}^{-1}=(2^{k}+1)^{w_{k}(P)}\leq(2^{k}+1)^{n/k}=2^{n}(1+2^{-k})^{n/k}\leq 2^{n}e^{n/(k2^{k})}.

(55)

In particular, when
k2^{k}=n/\alpha
, we have

m_{P}^{-1}\leq 2^{n}e^{\alpha}.

(56)

For Pauli string
P
with contiguous support of length
\ell
, the shadow norm is
\|P\|_{\text{sh}}^{2}/2^{n}\leq(2^{k}+1)^{\ell/k+1}=\mathcal{O}(\ell 2^{\ell})
where
k2^{k}=\Omega(\ell)
and
k\geq 2
, confirming the result predicted by the mean-field approximation in the brickwork shadow case
[
36
]
.

For a general observable
O=\sum_{P}\alpha_{P}P
, the same calculation gives

\displaystyle\|O\|_{\mathrm{sh},\rho}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\alpha_{P}\alpha_{Q}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho PQ)f(P,Q),

(57)

where

f(P,Q):=\frac{\Pr_{U}\!\left(UPU^{\dagger},\,UQU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\right)}{m_{P}m_{Q}}.

(58)

The function
f(P,Q)
depends on the unitary ensemble and will be the main quantity controlling the variance calculations below.

For the global Clifford ensemble
\mathbb{U}=\mathrm{Cl}(n)
, one obtains

f(P,Q)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }P=I\text{ or }Q=I,\\[4.0pt]
D+1,&\text{if }P=Q\neq I,\\[4.0pt]
\dfrac{2(D+1)}{D+2},&\text{if }P\neq I,\ Q\neq I,\ P\neq Q,\ PQ=QP,\\[6.0pt]
0,&\text{if }P\neq I,\ Q\neq I,\ P\neq Q,\ PQ\neq QP.\end{cases}

(59)

For product ensembles,
f(P,Q)
factorizes.
For example, for
\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}
,

f(P,Q)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(P_{i},Q_{i}),

(60)

where the same symbol
f
is used for the corresponding single-qubit function.
For block shadows, we assume for simplicity that the
n
qubits are partitioned
into
n/k
blocks of size
k
. Then

f(P,Q)=\prod_{i=1}^{n/k}f(P_{i},Q_{i}),

(61)

where
P_{i},Q_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}
denote the restrictions of
P,Q
to the

i
-th block.
This factorization is one of the main technical advantages of block shadows:
although
f(P,Q)
is difficult to compute explicitly for general brickwork
circuits, it is directly computable for block shadows.

We will also use the following elementary sums for block shadows.
Assume
\mathbb{U}=\mathrm{Cl}(k)^{\otimes n/k}
and
k2^{k}=n/\alpha
. Then

\displaystyle\bullet\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}f(P,I)=D^{2},

(62)

\displaystyle\bullet\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}f(P,P)=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}m_{P}^{-1}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n/k}\binom{n/k}{\ell}(2^{k}+1)^{\ell}(4^{k}-1)^{\ell}

\displaystyle\quad=(8^{k}+4^{k}-2^{k})^{n/k}\leq D^{3}\left(1+\frac{1}{2^{k}}\right)^{n/k}\leq D^{3}e^{\alpha},

(63)

\displaystyle\bullet\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}f(P,Q)=(16^{k})^{n/k}=D^{4}.

(64)

Finally, we define the variance quantities used in the following sections.
For
O=\sum_{P}\alpha_{P}P
, set

\displaystyle V_{1}(O,\rho)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\alpha_{P}\alpha_{Q}\,\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)\operatorname{Tr}(\rho Q)\,f(P,Q),

(65)

\displaystyle V_{2}(O,\rho)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\alpha_{P}\alpha_{Q}\,\operatorname{Tr}(\rho PQ)\,f(P,Q).

(66)

For purity and inner-product estimators, we also use

V_{3}(\rho):=\sum_{P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho PQ)^{2}}{D^{2}}\,f(P,Q).

(67)

When
O=\rho
, we use the shorthand

V_{1}(\rho):=V_{1}(O=\rho,\rho)
and

V_{2}(\rho):=V_{2}(O=\rho,\rho)
.
Equivalently, using

\rho=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)P,

(68)

we have

\displaystyle V_{1}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho Q)^{2}}{D^{2}}f(P,Q),

(69)

\displaystyle V_{2}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)\operatorname{Tr}(\rho Q)\operatorname{Tr}(\rho PQ)}{D^{2}}f(P,Q).

(70)

For later use, we record the relation

V_{1}(\rho)\leq V_{2}(\rho)\leq V_{3}(\rho).

(71)

Indeed, the three quantities can be expressed in terms of the same
two-shot overlap.
For a fixed
U
, let
b_{1},b_{2}
be independent measurement outcomes sampled
from
U\rho U^{\dagger}
. Then

\displaystyle V_{1}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\mathbb{E}_{b_{1},b_{2}|U}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,\mathcal{M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{2}\rangle\langle b_{2}|U\right)\right)\right]^{2},

(72)

\displaystyle V_{2}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\mathbb{E}_{b_{1}|U}\left[\mathbb{E}_{b_{2}|U}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,\mathcal{M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{2}\rangle\langle b_{2}|U\right)\right)\right]^{2},

(73)

\displaystyle V_{3}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\mathbb{E}_{b_{1},b_{2}|U}\left[\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,\mathcal{M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{2}\rangle\langle b_{2}|U\right)\right)^{2}\right].

(74)

The first two identities differ only in the order in which the square and the
average over
b_{1}
are taken. Hence, by Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\left[\mathbb{E}_{b_{1}|U}\mathbb{E}_{b_{2}|U}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,\mathcal{M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{2}\rangle\langle b_{2}|U\right)\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{b_{1}|U}\left[\mathbb{E}_{b_{2}|U}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,\mathcal{M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{2}\rangle\langle b_{2}|U\right)\right)\right]^{2}.

(75)

Averaging over
U
gives

V_{1}(\rho)\leq V_{2}(\rho).

(76)

Similarly, for each fixed
U
and
b_{1}
, Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

\displaystyle\left[\mathbb{E}_{b_{2}|U}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,\mathcal{M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{2}\rangle\langle b_{2}|U\right)\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{b_{2}|U}\left[\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,\mathcal{M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{2}\rangle\langle b_{2}|U\right)\right)^{2}\right].

(77)

Averaging over
b_{1}
and
U
gives

V_{2}(\rho)\leq V_{3}(\rho).

(78)

Therefore,

V_{1}(\rho)\leq V_{2}(\rho)\leq V_{3}(\rho).

(79)

With the same procedures as above, we can prove
V_{1}(O,\rho)\leq V_{2}(O,\rho)
.

Proofs of key results

B.1
Proof of the Fact
1

Fact
.

Let
\mathbb{U}_{\mathrm{MUB}}=\{U_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{2^{n}+1}
be a set of Clifford unitaries such that
\{U_{\ell}^{\dagger}\ket{b}:b\in\{0,1\}^{n}\}
, for
\ell=1,\ldots,2^{n}+1
, forms a complete set of mutually unbiased bases on
n
qubits. Then
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{U}_{\mathrm{MUB}}}=\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{Cl}(n)}

[
67
]
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to prove that
\mathcal{M}_{\text{MUB}}(P)=\mathcal{M}_{\text{Cl}(n)}(P)
holds for all Pauli strings
P
. For
P=I
, it is trivial. For
P\neq I
, we have

\mathcal{M}_{\text{Cl}(n)}(P)=(2^{n}+1)^{-1}P.

(80)

Because
UPU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}
must be satisfied by exactly one
U\in\mathbb{U}_{\text{MUB}}
, the
m_{P}
from
\mathbb{U}_{\text{MUB}}
becomes

m_{P}=1/|\mathbb{U}_{\text{MUB}}|=1/(2^{n}+1),

(81)

where
|\mathbb{U}_{\text{MUB}}|=2^{n}+1
. Consequently,
\mathcal{M}_{\text{MUB}}(P)=\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{Cl}(n)}(P)
holds for all
P
, then
\mathcal{M}_{\text{MUB}}=\mathcal{M}_{\text{Cl}(n)}
.
∎

B.2
Proof of Fact
2

Fact
.

There exists a Clifford ensemble
\mathbb{U}_{0}\subseteq\mathrm{Cl}(n)
with
|\mathbb{U}_{0}|\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}(2^{i}+1)
such that, for every observable
O
,
\lVert O\rVert_{\mathrm{sh},\mathbb{U}_{0}}=\lVert O\rVert_{\mathrm{sh},\mathrm{Cl}(n)}
.

Proof.

It is enough to construct an ensemble
\mathbb{U}_{0}
for which
f(P,Q;\mathbb{U}_{0})=f(P,Q;\mathrm{Cl}(n))
for all Pauli strings
P,Q
, where
f(P,Q;\mathbb{U})
denotes the function
f(P,Q)
associated with the ensemble
\mathbb{U}
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the set of maximal commuting Pauli subgroups, with phases omitted.
Equivalently, each
L\in\mathcal{L}
specifies one stabilizer measurement basis.
The number of such subgroups is

|\mathcal{L}|=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(2^{i}+1).

(82)

For each
L\in\mathcal{L}
, choose one Clifford unitary
U_{L}
satisfying

U_{L}^{\dagger}\mathcal{Z}U_{L}=L,

(83)

and define

\mathbb{U}_{0}=\{U_{L}:L\in\mathcal{L}\}.

(84)

Then
|\mathbb{U}_{0}|=|\mathcal{L}|\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}(2^{i}+1)
.

We now note that the events entering
f(P,Q)
depend on
U
only through the subgroup
U^{\dagger}\mathcal{Z}U
.
Indeed,

\displaystyle UPU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\quad

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad P\in\pm U^{\dagger}\mathcal{Z}U,

(85)

\displaystyle UPU^{\dagger},\,UQU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\quad

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad P,Q\in\pm U^{\dagger}\mathcal{Z}U.

(86)

Therefore, the probabilities appearing in
f(P,Q)
are determined only by the induced distribution of
U^{\dagger}\mathcal{Z}U
over
\mathcal{L}
.

For
U\sim\mathbb{U}_{0}
, the subgroup
U^{\dagger}\mathcal{Z}U
is uniform over
\mathcal{L}
by construction.
For
U\sim\mathrm{Cl}(n)
, the same distribution is obtained because the Clifford group acts transitively on stabilizer measurement bases, and each
L\in\mathcal{L}
has the same number of Clifford preimages.
Hence the probabilities defining
f(P,Q)
are identical for
\mathbb{U}_{0}
and
\mathrm{Cl}(n)
.
Thus,

f(P,Q;\mathbb{U}_{0})=f(P,Q;\mathrm{Cl}(n))

(87)

for all Pauli strings
P,Q
.

Using Eq. (
57
), we obtain

\|O\|_{\mathrm{sh},\mathbb{U}_{0}}=\|O\|_{\mathrm{sh},\mathrm{Cl}(n)}

(88)

for every observable
O
, as claimed.
∎

B.3
An average confidence in block shadow

Let
{\bf{P}}=\{{P_{l}}\}_{l=1}^{L}
be a set of Pauli strings and
{\bf{B}}=\{{U_{m}}\}_{m=1}^{M}
be a set of measurement basis. Define
M_{l}(\textbf{B})={\sum_{m}}{\bf{1}}\{{U_{m}}{P_{l}}U_{m}^{\dagger}\in\pm{\cal Z}\}
and
{\rm{CON}}{{\rm{F}}_{\varepsilon}}({\bf{P}};{\bf{B}})\coloneqq\sum_{l=1}^{L}\exp\left({-\frac{{{\varepsilon^{2}}}}{2}{M_{l}}({\bf{B}})}\right)
, where
\mathcal{Z}=\{I,Z\}^{n}
. Then following holds

{\rm{CON}}{{\rm{F}}_{\varepsilon}}({\bf{P}}{\rm{;}}{{\bf{B}}^{\#}})\leq\mathbb{E}{{}_{\bf{B}}}[{\rm{CON}}{{\rm{F}}_{\varepsilon}}({\bf{P}}{\rm{;}}{\bf{B}})]=\sum_{l=1}^{L}{(1-\nu/{({2^{k}}+1)^{{w_{k}}({P_{l}})}})^{M}},

(89)

where
\nu=1-\exp(-\epsilon^2/2)
.

Proof.

Closed form expression for
\mathbb{E}{{}_{\bf{B}}}[{\rm{CON}}{{\rm{F}}_{\epsilon}}({\bf{P}}{\rm{;}}{\bf{B}})]
is as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}[\mathrm{CONF}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{P};\mathbf{B})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\!\left[\sum_{l=1}^{L}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}M_{l}(\mathbf{B})\right)\right]

(90)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\!\left[\sum_{l=1}^{L}\prod_{m=1}^{M}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\mathbf{1}\{U_{m}P_{l}U_{m}^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\}\right)\right]

(91)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\!\left[\sum_{l=1}^{L}\prod_{m=1}^{M}(1-\nu\,\mathbf{1}\{U_{m}P_{l}U_{m}^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\})\right]

(92)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\!\left[\prod_{m=1}^{M}(1-\nu\,\mathbf{1}\{U_{m}P_{l}U_{m}^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\})\right]

(93)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left(1-\frac{\nu}{(2^{k}+1)^{w_{k}(P_{l})}}\right)^{M}

(94)

∎

B.4
State-averaged variance for state-independent observables

Proof.

Let
O
be Hermitian,
\rho
be a pure quantum state,
k2^{k}=n/\alpha
, and the unitary ensemble

\mathbb{U}=\mathrm{Cl}(k)^{\otimes n/k}
. Let

O=\sum_{P}\alpha_{P}P
and define

\hat{o}=\mathrm{Tr}(O\hat{\rho})
, then

\displaystyle{\rm{Var}}(\hat{o})

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}_{U,b}}[{{\hat{o}}^{2}}]

(95)

\displaystyle={\sum_{P,Q}}{\alpha_{P}}{\alpha_{Q}}{\rm{Tr}}(\rho PQ)f(P,Q).

(96)

When taking the average over
\rho
, the resulting mean value becomes the same as the value for
\rho=I/2^{n}

[
6
]
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\mathrm{Var}(\hat{o})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\!\left[\sum_{P,Q}\alpha_{P}\alpha_{Q}\mathrm{Tr}(\rho PQ)\,f(P,Q)\right]

(97)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P}\alpha_{P}^{2}f(P,P)=\sum_{P}\alpha_{P}^{2}m_{P}^{-1}

(98)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(2^{k}+1)^{n/k}}{2^{n}}\,\|O\|_{2}^{2}\leq e^{\alpha}\|O\|_{2}^{2}

(99)

Because the circuit depth
d
required to implement a random Clifford gate from Cl(
k
) is
d=ck
, where
c
depends on the connectivity of the hardware and
k=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
, it follows that
d=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
. This is consistent with previously known results
[
6
]
.
∎

B.5
State-averaged variance for state-dependent observables

Proof.

Let
\rho
be a pure quantum state,
k2^{k}=n/\alpha
, and unitary ensemble
\mathbb{U}=\text{Cl}(k)^{\otimes n/k}
. In the case of
O=\rho
, one must exercise caution when computing the average over
\rho
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\hat{o})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{P,Q}\alpha_{P}\alpha_{Q}\mathrm{Tr}(\rho PQ)\,f(P,Q)

(100)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P,Q}\mathrm{Tr}(\rho P)\mathrm{Tr}(\rho Q)\mathrm{Tr}(\rho PQ)\,f(P,Q)/4^{n}

(101)

As shown in the above equation, unlike in
B.4
, we must use a state 3-design to compute the average instead of state 1-design. By employing the property of the projective 3-designs
[
43
]
,

{\mathbb{E}_{\rho}}[{\rho^{\otimes 3}}]=\frac{1}{{D(D+1)(D+2)}}{\sum_{\sigma\in{S_{3}}}}{U_{\sigma}}.

(102)

We can calculate the average as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\mathrm{Var}(\hat{o})]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{D^{3}(D+1)(D+2)}\sum_{P,Q}f(P,Q)\,\sum_{\sigma\in S_{3}}\mathrm{Tr}\!\big(U_{\sigma}(P\otimes Q\otimes PQ)\big)

(103)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D^{3}(D+1)(D+2)}\sum_{P,Q}f(P,Q)\big[\mathrm{Tr}(P)\mathrm{Tr}(Q)\mathrm{Tr}(PQ)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\mathrm{Tr}(PQ)^{2}+\mathrm{Tr}(P)^{2}+\mathrm{Tr}(Q)^{2}+2\mathrm{Tr}(P^{2}Q^{2})\big]

(104)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{D^{3}(D+1)(D+2)}\big(D^{3}+2D^{4}+D^{5}e^{\alpha}+2D^{5}\big)

(105)

\displaystyle\leq 2+e^{\alpha}+\mathcal{O}(1/D).

(106)

If the quantum state undergoes a state-independent unital channel
C
, the same bound still holds.
Indeed, the above calculation is repeated after replacing each Pauli string by its image under
C^{\dagger}
.
Since
C^{\dagger}
is also unital, we have
\|C^{\dagger}(P)\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{D}
for every Pauli string
P
.
Thus, each term is bounded in the same way as above, and the averaged variance remains upper-bounded by Eq. (
106
).
∎

B.6
Proof of the
\left\|O\right\|_{{\rm{sh},\rho}}^{2}={\cal O}(3^{n/k}\|O\|_{2}^{2}
)

Proof.

Consider the block Clifford ensemble

\mathbb{U}=\text{Cl}(k)^{\otimes n/k},\qquad\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{k}^{\otimes n/k}.

(107)

We prove that, for every state
\rho
and every Hermitian observable
O
,

\|O\|_{\mathrm{sh,\rho}}^{2}\leq 3^{n/k}\|O\|_{2}^{2}.

(108)

We first recall the standard Clifford shadow-norm bound from
Ref.
[
32
]
. For a single
k
-qubit block, for
every Hermitian
X\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{2^{k}})
and every
state
\sigma\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}_{U}\sum_{b}{\rm Tr}\!\left(\sigma\,U^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|U\right){\rm Tr}\!\left[X\,\mathcal{M}_{k}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|U\right)\right]^{2}\leq 3\,{\rm Tr}(\sigma)\,\|X\|_{2}^{2}.

(109)

We now prove the desired result by induction on the number of blocks.
The case
n/k=1
follows from
Eq. (
109
) with
\sigma=\rho
.

Assume the claim holds for
n/k-1
blocks. Separate the first block from
the remaining blocks. For the remaining blocks, write

\Phi_{R}=\bigotimes_{i=2}^{n/k}\phi_{i},\qquad Y_{R}=\bigotimes_{i=2}^{n/k}\mathcal{M}_{k}^{-1}(\phi_{i}),

(110)

where
\phi_{i}=U_{i}^{\dagger}\ket{b_i}\bra{b_i}U_{i}
. Conditioned on
\Phi_{R}
, define the subnormalized state on the first block
by

\rho_{1}(\Phi_{R})={\rm Tr}_{R}\!\left[(I_{k}\otimes\Phi_{R})\rho\right].

(111)

Then

{\rm Tr}[\rho(\phi_{1}\otimes\Phi_{R})]={\rm Tr}[\rho_{1}(\Phi_{R})\phi_{1}].

(112)

For fixed
Y_{R}
, define

O_{1}(Y_{R})=\mathrm{Tr}_{R}(O(I_{1}\otimes Y_{R}))=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{P}_{k}}{\rm Tr}[O(S\otimes Y_{R})]S.

(113)

Then, with
Y_{1}=\mathcal{M}_{k}^{-1}(\phi_{1})
,

{\rm Tr}[O_{1}(Y_{R})Y_{1}]={\rm Tr}[O(Y_{1}\otimes Y_{R})].

(114)

Applying Eq. (
109
) to the first block,
we obtain

\displaystyle\|O\|_{\mathrm{sh,\rho}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\,\mathbb{E}_{U_{R}}\sum_{b_{R}}{\rm Tr}(\rho_{R}\Phi_{R})\|O_{1}(Y_{R})\|_{2}^{2},

(115)

where
\rho_{R}={\rm Tr}_{1}(\rho)
.

By Pauli orthogonality on the first block,

\|O_{1}(Y_{R})\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{P}_{k}}{\rm Tr}[O(S\otimes Y_{R})]^{2}.

(116)

Define

O_{S}={\rm Tr}_{1}[(S\otimes I_{R})O].

(117)

Then

{\rm Tr}[O(S\otimes Y_{R})]={\rm Tr}(O_{S}Y_{R}).

(118)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|O\|_{\mathrm{sh,\rho}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2^{k}}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{P}_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{U_{R}}\sum_{b_{R}}{\rm Tr}(\rho_{R}\Phi_{R}){\rm Tr}(O_{S}Y_{R})^{2}

(119)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2^{k}}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{P}_{k}}\|O_{S}\|_{\mathrm{sh,\rho_{R}}}^{2}.

(120)

By the induction hypothesis applied to the remaining
n/k-1
blocks,

\|O_{S}\|_{\mathrm{sh,\rho_{R}}}^{2}\leq 3^{n/k-1}{\rm Tr}(\rho_{R})\|O_{S}\|_{2}^{2}=3^{n/k-1}\|O_{S}\|_{2}^{2}.

(121)

Hence

\|O\|_{\mathrm{sh,\rho}}^{2}\leq 3^{n/k}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{P}_{k}}\|O_{S}\|_{2}^{2}.

(122)

Finally, from the Pauli expansion on the first block,

O=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{P}_{k}}S\otimes O_{S},

(123)

we have

\|O\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{P}_{k}}\|O_{S}\|_{2}^{2}.

(124)

Therefore

\|O\|_{\mathrm{sh,\rho}}^{2}\leq 3^{n/k}\|O\|_{2}^{2}.

(125)

∎

B.7
Tightness of
\left\|O\right\|_{{\rm{sh},\rho}}^{2}={\cal O}(3^{n/k})

Proof.

Let unitary ensemble
\mathbb{U}
be
\text{Cl}(k)^{\otimes n/k}
,
k2^{k}=\Omega(n)

In the case of the

B.8
Variance bound for purity estimation in the single-shot setting

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{U}=\mathrm{Cl}(k)^{\otimes m}
, where
m=n/k
, and assume

k2^{k}=n/\alpha
. Consider

\hat{p}_{2}=\frac{2}{T(T-1)}\sum_{i>j}^{T}{\rm Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{i}\hat{\rho}_{j}),

(130)

where the
\hat{\rho}_{i}
’s are independent single-shot shadow estimators.
The standard variance identity for a second-order
U
-statistic gives

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(\hat{p}_{2})

\displaystyle=4\,\frac{T-2}{T(T-1)}{\rm Var}\!\left({\rm Tr}(\rho\hat{\rho})\right)+\frac{2}{T(T-1)}{\rm Var}\!\left({\rm Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{1}\hat{\rho}_{2})\right).

(131)

The first term is bounded by the worst-case block-shadow norm:

{\rm Var}\!\left({\rm Tr}(\rho\hat{\rho})\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\rm Tr}(\rho\hat{\rho})^{2}\right]=\|\rho\|_{\rm sh,\rho}^{2}\leq e^{\alpha/3}3^{m}\|\rho\|_{2}^{2}\leq e^{\alpha/3}3^{m}.

(132)

It remains to bound the second term. Using the Pauli expansion and

\mathcal{M}^{-1}(P)=m_{P}^{-1}P
, we have

{\rm Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{1}\hat{\rho}_{2})=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}m_{P}^{-2}(U_{1},P,b_{1})(U_{2},P,b_{2}).

(133)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\rm Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{1}\hat{\rho}_{2})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D^{2}}\sum_{P,Q}m_{P}^{-2}m_{Q}^{-2}\mathbb{E}_{U_{1},b_{1}}\!\left[(U_{1},P,b_{1})(U_{1},Q,b_{1})\right]\mathbb{E}_{U_{2},b_{2}}\!\left[(U_{2},P,b_{2})(U_{2},Q,b_{2})\right].

(134)

For one copy,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U,b}\!\left[(U,P,b)(U,Q,b)\right]

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}(\rho PQ)\,p(P,Q),

(135)

where

p(P,Q):=\Pr_{U}\!\left(UPU^{\dagger},UQU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\right).

(136)

Thus

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\rm Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{1}\hat{\rho}_{2})^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{D^{2}}\sum_{P,Q}m_{P}^{-2}m_{Q}^{-2}p(P,Q)^{2}\left|{\rm Tr}(\rho PQ)\right|^{2}.

(137)

We now keep the factor
p(P,Q)^{2}
and group the sum by the
phase-free Pauli string
R
determined by
PQ=\pm R
on the support of

p(P,Q)
. Define

C_{R}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
PQ=\pm R\end{subarray}}m_{P}^{-2}m_{Q}^{-2}p(P,Q)^{2}.

(138)

Since
p(P,Q)=0
unless
P
and
Q
commute blockwise,
Eq. (
137
) implies

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\rm Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{1}\hat{\rho}_{2})^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{D^{2}}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}C_{R}\,{\rm Tr}(\rho R)^{2}.

(139)

It remains to bound
C_{R}
. By the block product structure,
C_{R}

factorizes over the
m
blocks. For a single block, define

c(P,Q):=f(P,Q)^{2}.

(140)

By Eq. (
59
),

c(P,Q)=\begin{cases}1,&P=I\text{ or }Q=I,\\[4.0pt]
(D_{k}+1)^{2},&P=Q\neq I,\\[4.0pt]
\dfrac{4(D_{k}+1)^{2}}{(D_{k}+2)^{2}},&P,Q\neq I,\ P\neq Q,\ PQ=QP,\\[8.0pt]
0,&PQ\neq QP.\end{cases}

Let

C_{r}^{\rm loc}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{k}\\
PQ=\pm r\end{subarray}}c(P,Q).

Then

\displaystyle C_{I}^{\rm loc}

\displaystyle=1+(D_{k}^{2}-1)(D_{k}+1)^{2}\leq D_{k}^{4}\left(1+\frac{2}{D_{k}}\right),

(141)

\displaystyle C_{r(\neq I)}^{\rm loc}

\displaystyle=2+\left(\frac{D_{k}^{2}}{2}-2\right)\frac{4(D_{k}+1)^{2}}{(D_{k}+2)^{2}}\leq 2D_{k}^{2}.

(142)

Consequently, if
A=\operatorname{supp}_{k}(R)\coloneq\{i\in[n/k]:R[i]\neq I_{k}\}
is the block support of

R
, then

C_{R}\leq\left[D_{k}^{4}\left(1+\frac{2}{D_{k}}\right)\right]^{m-|A|}(2D_{k}^{2})^{|A|}.

(143)

For fixed
A
, using
R=R_{A}\otimes I_{A^{c}}
and Pauli orthogonality,

\sum_{\operatorname{supp}_{k}(R)=A}{\rm Tr}(\rho R)^{2}\leq\sum_{R_{A}}{\rm Tr}(\rho_{A}R_{A})^{2}=D_{k}^{|A|}{\rm Tr}(\rho_{A}^{2})\leq D_{k}^{|A|}.

(144)

Combining Eqs. (
139
),
(
143
), and (
144
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\rm Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{1}\hat{\rho}_{2})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{D^{2}}\sum_{A\subseteq[m]}\left[D_{k}^{4}\left(1+\frac{2}{D_{k}}\right)\right]^{m-|A|}(2D_{k}^{2})^{|A|}D_{k}^{|A|}

(145)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D^{2}}\left[D_{k}^{4}\left(1+\frac{2}{D_{k}}\right)+2D_{k}^{3}\right]^{m}

(146)

\displaystyle\leq D^{2}\left(1+\frac{4}{D_{k}}\right)^{m}\leq D^{2}e^{4\alpha}.

(147)

Therefore,

{\rm Var}\!\left({\rm Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{1}\hat{\rho}_{2})\right)\leq D^{2}e^{4\alpha}.

(148)

Substituting Eqs. (
132
) and
(
147
) into Eq. (
131
) gives

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(\hat{p}_{2})

\displaystyle\leq 4e^{\alpha/3}\frac{T-2}{T(T-1)}3^{m}+\frac{2D^{2}e^{4\alpha}}{T(T-1)}

(149)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{3^{n/k}}{T}+\frac{D^{2}}{T^{2}}\right).

(150)

By Chebyshev’s inequality, it is sufficient to take

T=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\max\left\{\frac{3^{n/k}}{\epsilon^{2}},\frac{2^{n}}{\epsilon}\right\}\right)

(151)

to estimate
{\rm Tr}(\rho^{2})
within additive error
\epsilon
.
∎

B.9
Multi-shot variance for a Pauli observable

For the discussion in this section and below, write
T=N_{U}N_{S}
, where

N_{U}
is the number of sampled measurement bases and
N_{S}
is the number of
repeated measurements in each basis.

Proof.

Let

\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{U}}\hat{\rho}_{U_{i}}(N_{S}),\qquad\hat{\rho}_{U}(N_{S})=\frac{1}{N_{S}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{S}}\hat{\rho}_{U,j},

(152)

where

\hat{\rho}_{U,j}=\mathcal{M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{j}\rangle\langle b_{j}|U\right).

(153)

For a Pauli string
P
, define

\hat{o}_{P}=\operatorname{Tr}(P\hat{\rho}).

Since the
N_{U}
measurement bases are independent,

\operatorname{Var}(\hat{o}_{P})=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}(P\hat{\rho}_{U}(N_{S}))\right).

(154)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}(P\hat{\rho}_{U}(N_{S}))\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{Tr}(P\hat{\rho}_{U}(N_{S}))^{2}\right]-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}.

(155)

Using
\mathcal{M}^{-1}(P)=m_{P}^{-1}P
, we have

\operatorname{Tr}(P\hat{\rho}_{U,j})=m_{P}^{-1}(U,P,b_{j}),

(156)

where
(U,P,b)\coloneqq\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(UPU^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|\right)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{Tr}(P\hat{\rho}_{U}(N_{S}))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{S}^{2}}\sum_{j,\ell=1}^{N_{S}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{Tr}(P\hat{\rho}_{U,j})\operatorname{Tr}(P\hat{\rho}_{U,\ell})\right]

(157)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{S}}m_{P}^{-2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[(U,P,b_{1})^{2}\right]+\frac{N_{S}-1}{N_{S}}m_{P}^{-2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[(U,P,b_{1})(U,P,b_{2})\right].

(158)

The diagonal term is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[(U,P,b_{1})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\sum_{b}(U,P,b)^{2}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U\rho U^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|\right)

(159)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\left[\mathbf{1}\{UPU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\}\right]

(160)

\displaystyle=m_{P}.

(161)

The off-diagonal term is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[(U,P,b_{1})(U,P,b_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\sum_{b}(U,P,b)\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U\rho U^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|\right)\right]^{2}

(162)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\left[\mathbf{1}\{UPU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}\right]

(163)

\displaystyle=m_{P}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}.

(164)

Combining these identities gives

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}(P\hat{\rho}_{U}(N_{S}))\right)

\displaystyle=m_{P}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{N_{S}}+\frac{N_{S}-1}{N_{S}}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}\right)-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}.

(165)

Hence,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}(P\hat{\rho})\right)=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\left[m_{P}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{N_{S}}+\frac{N_{S}-1}{N_{S}}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}\right)-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}\right].

(166)

This proves the claim.
∎

B.10
Multi-shot variance for a general observable

For the discussion in this section, write
T=N_{U}N_{S}
, where

N_{U}
is the number of sampled measurement bases and
N_{S}
is the number of
repeated measurements in each basis. Let
O=\sum_{P}\alpha_{P}P
.

Proof.

As in Appendix
B.9
, define

\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{U}}\hat{\rho}_{U_{i}}(N_{S}),\qquad\hat{\rho}_{U}(N_{S})=\frac{1}{N_{S}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{S}}\hat{\rho}_{U,j},

(167)

where

\hat{\rho}_{U,j}=\mathcal{M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{j}\rangle\langle b_{j}|U\right).

(168)

Since the
N_{U}
measurement bases are sampled independently,

\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}(O\hat{\rho})\right)=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}(O\hat{\rho}_{U}(N_{S}))\right).

(169)

Moreover, using
\mathcal{M}^{-1}(P)=m_{P}^{-1}P
, we have

\operatorname{Tr}(O\hat{\rho}_{U,j})=\sum_{P}\alpha_{P}m_{P}^{-1}(U,P,b_{j}),

(170)

where

(U,P,b)=\operatorname{Tr}(UPU^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|)
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{Tr}(O\hat{\rho}_{U}(N_{S}))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{S}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{Tr}(O\hat{\rho}_{U,1})^{2}\right]+\frac{N_{S}-1}{N_{S}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{Tr}(O\hat{\rho}_{U,1})\operatorname{Tr}(O\hat{\rho}_{U,2})\right].

(171)

We first compute the diagonal term. Expanding

\rho=D^{-1}\sum_{R}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho R)R
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{Tr}(O\hat{\rho}_{U,1})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\sum_{b}\sum_{P,Q}\alpha_{P}\alpha_{Q}m_{P}^{-1}m_{Q}^{-1}(U,P,b)(U,Q,b)\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U\rho U^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|\right)

(172)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\sum_{b}\sum_{P,Q,R}\alpha_{P}\alpha_{Q}m_{P}^{-1}m_{Q}^{-1}(U,P,b)(U,Q,b)(U,R,b)\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho R)}{D}

(173)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P,Q}\alpha_{P}\alpha_{Q}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho PQ)\frac{\Pr_{U}\!\left(UPU^{\dagger},\,UQU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\right)}{m_{P}m_{Q}}

(174)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P,Q}\alpha_{P}\alpha_{Q}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho PQ)f(P,Q)

(175)

\displaystyle=V_{2}.

(176)

In the third equality, we used the Pauli orthogonality identity in
Eq. (
46
). In particular, if
UPU^{\dagger}
and

UQU^{\dagger}
are both diagonal, then the only nonzero contribution comes
from
R=PQ
.

We next compute the off-diagonal term. Since
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
are
independent conditioned on the same
U
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{Tr}(O\hat{\rho}_{U,1})\operatorname{Tr}(O\hat{\rho}_{U,2})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\sum_{b_{1},b_{2}}\sum_{P,Q}\alpha_{P}\alpha_{Q}m_{P}^{-1}m_{Q}^{-1}(U,P,b_{1})(U,Q,b_{2})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U\rho U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|\right)\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U\rho U^{\dagger}|b_{2}\rangle\langle b_{2}|\right)

(177)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P,Q}\alpha_{P}\alpha_{Q}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)\operatorname{Tr}(\rho Q)\frac{\Pr_{U}\!\left(UPU^{\dagger},\,UQU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\right)}{m_{P}m_{Q}}

(178)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P,Q}\alpha_{P}\alpha_{Q}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)\operatorname{Tr}(\rho Q)f(P,Q)

(179)

\displaystyle=V_{1}.

(180)

Combining the diagonal and off-diagonal contributions gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{Tr}(O\hat{\rho}_{U}(N_{S}))^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{N_{S}}V_{2}+\frac{N_{S}-1}{N_{S}}V_{1}.

(181)

Since the estimator is unbiased,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{Tr}(O\hat{\rho}_{U}(N_{S}))\right]=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho O).

(182)

Hence,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}(O\hat{\rho})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\left(\frac{1}{N_{S}}V_{2}+\frac{N_{S}-1}{N_{S}}V_{1}-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho O)^{2}\right)

(183)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N_{U}}\left(V_{1}+\frac{V_{2}}{N_{S}}\right).

(184)

∎

B.11
Variance bound for purity estimation in the multi-shot setting

Proof.

Let

\hat{p}_{2}=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{U}}\hat{p}_{2}(U_{i})
,
where

\hat{p}_{2}(U)=\binom{N_{S}}{2}^{-1}\sum_{i>j}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{i}\rangle\langle b_{i}|U\,\mathcal{M}^{-1}\bigl(U^{\dagger}|b_{j}\rangle\langle b_{j}|U\bigr)\right).

Then,
\hat{p}_{2}
is an unbiased estimator, as shown below.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\hat{p}_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\hat{p}_{2}(U)\right]

(185)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U,b_{1},b_{2}}\!\left[{\rm Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,{\cal M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{2}\rangle\langle b_{2}|U\right)\right)\right]

(186)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U,b_{1},b_{2}}\!\left[\sum_{P}m_{P}^{-1}(U,P,b_{1})(U,P,b_{2})/D\right]

(187)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U,b_{1},b_{2}}\!\left[\sum_{b_{1},b_{2}}\sum_{P,Q,R}m_{P}^{-1}(U,P,b_{1})(U,Q,b_{1})(U,P,b_{2})(U,R,b_{2})\frac{{\rm Tr}(\rho Q)\,{\rm Tr}(\rho R)}{D^{3}}\right]

(188)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P}\frac{{\rm Tr}(\rho P)^{2}}{D}

(189)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}(\rho^{2})

(190)

Building on this,
\text{Var}[\hat{p}_{2}]
can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(\hat{p}_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\,{\rm Var}(\hat{p}_{2}(U))

(191)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\left(\mathbb{E}[\hat{p}_{2}(U)^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[\hat{p}_{2}(U)]^{2}\right)

(192)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\left(\mathbb{E}[\hat{p}_{2}(U)^{2}]-{\rm Tr}(\rho^{2})^{2}\right).

(193)

Then,
\mathbb{E}[{\hat{p}_{2}}{(U)^{2}}]
can be calculated as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{p}_{2}(U)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{(N_{S}-2)(N_{S}-3)}{N_{S}(N_{S}-1)}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[{\rm Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,{\cal M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{2}\rangle\langle b_{2}|U\right)\right){\rm Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{3}\rangle\langle b_{3}|U\,{\cal M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{4}\rangle\langle b_{4}|U\right)\right)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{N_{S}-2}{N_{S}(N_{S}-1)}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[{\rm Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,{\cal M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{2}\rangle\langle b_{2}|U\right)\right){\rm Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,{\cal M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{3}\rangle\langle b_{3}|U\right)\right)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2}{N_{S}(N_{S}-1)}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[{\rm Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,{\cal M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{2}\rangle\langle b_{2}|U\right)\right)^{2}\Bigr]

(194)

The first term in Eq. (
194
) can be calculated as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U,b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4}}\Bigl[\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,\mathcal{M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{2}\rangle\langle b_{2}|U\right)\right)\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{3}\rangle\langle b_{3}|U\,\mathcal{M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{4}\rangle\langle b_{4}|U\right)\right)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U,b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4}}\left[\sum_{P,Q}m_{P}^{-1}m_{Q}^{-1}(U,P,b_{1})(U,P,b_{2})(U,Q,b_{3})(U,Q,b_{4})\Big/D^{2}\right]

(195)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\Bigg[\sum_{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4}}\sum_{P,Q,R,S,T,L}m_{P}^{-1}m_{Q}^{-1}(U,P,b_{1})(U,R,b_{1})(U,P,b_{2})(U,S,b_{2})

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\times(U,Q,b_{3})(U,T,b_{3})(U,Q,b_{4})(U,L,b_{4})\,\operatorname{Tr}(\rho R)\operatorname{Tr}(\rho S)\operatorname{Tr}(\rho T)\operatorname{Tr}(\rho L)\Big/D^{6}\Bigg]

(196)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P,Q}\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho Q)^{2}}{D^{2}}\frac{\Pr_{U}\!\left(UPU^{\dagger},\,UQU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\right)}{m_{P}m_{Q}}

(197)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P,Q}\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho Q)^{2}}{D^{2}}f(P,Q)

(198)

\displaystyle=V_{1}(\rho).

(199)

The second term in Eq. (
194
) can be calculated as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U,b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}}\!\Bigl[{\rm Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,{\cal M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{2}\rangle\langle b_{2}|U\right)\right){\rm Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,{\cal M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{3}\rangle\langle b_{3}|U\right)\right)\Bigr]

(200)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U,b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}}\!\Bigl[\sum_{P,Q}m_{P}^{-1}m_{Q}^{-1}(U,P,b_{1})(U,P,b_{2})(U,Q,b_{1})(U,Q,b_{3})\Big/D^{2}\Bigr]

(201)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\Bigl[\sum_{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}}\sum_{P,Q,R,S,T}m_{P}^{-1}m_{Q}^{-1}(U,P,b_{1})(U,Q,b_{1})(U,R,b_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times(U,P,b_{2})(U,S,b_{2})(U,Q,b_{3})(U,T,b_{3})\frac{{\rm Tr}(\rho R)\,{\rm Tr}(\rho S)\,{\rm Tr}(\rho T)}{D^{5}}\Bigr]

(202)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P,Q}\frac{{\rm Tr}(\rho P)\,{\rm Tr}(\rho Q)\,{\rm Tr}(\rho PQ)}{D^{2}}\,f(P,Q)

(203)

\displaystyle=V_{2}(\rho).

(204)

The third term in Eq. (
194
) can be calculated as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U,b_{1},b_{2}}\!\Bigl[{\rm Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,{\cal M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{2}\rangle\langle b_{2}|U\right)\right)^{2}\Bigr]

(205)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U,b_{1},b_{2}}\!\Bigl[\sum_{P,Q}m_{P}^{-1}m_{Q}^{-1}(U,P,b_{1})(U,Q,b_{1})(U,P,b_{2})(U,Q,b_{2})\Big/D^{2}\Bigr]

(206)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\Bigl[\sum_{b_{1},b_{2}}\sum_{P,Q,R,S}m_{P}^{-1}m_{Q}^{-1}(U,P,b_{1})(U,Q,b_{1})(U,P,b_{2})(U,Q,b_{2})(U,R,b_{1})(U,S,b_{2})\Big/D^{4}\Bigr]

(207)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\Bigl[\sum_{b_{1},b_{2}}\sum_{P,Q,R,S}m_{P}^{-1}m_{Q}^{-1}(U,P,b_{1})(U,Q,b_{1})(U,P,b_{2})(U,Q,b_{2})(U,R,b_{1})(U,S,b_{2})\,{\rm Tr}(\rho R)\,{\rm Tr}(\rho S)/D^{4}\Bigr]

(208)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P,Q}\frac{{\rm Tr}(\rho PQ)^{2}}{D^{2}}\,f(P,Q)

(209)

\displaystyle=V_{3}(\rho).

(210)

Combining the three estimates above with Eq. (
194
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{p}_{2}(U)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{(N_{S}-2)(N_{S}-3)}{N_{S}(N_{S}-1)}V_{1}(\rho)+\frac{4(N_{S}-2)}{N_{S}(N_{S}-1)}V_{2}(\rho)+\frac{2}{N_{S}(N_{S}-1)}V_{3}(\rho)

(211)

\displaystyle\leq V_{1}(\rho)+\frac{4}{N_{S}}V_{2}(\rho)+\frac{2}{(N_{S}-1)^{2}}V_{3}(\rho).

(212)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(\hat{p}_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\left(\mathbb{E}[\hat{p}_{2}(U)^{2}]-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^{2})^{2}\right)

(213)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N_{U}}\left(V_{1}(\rho)+\frac{4}{N_{S}}V_{2}(\rho)+\frac{2}{(N_{S}-1)^{2}}V_{3}(\rho)\right).

(214)

This proves the claim.
∎

B.12
Inner product estimation in the distributed setting

Let
\hat{f}=\frac{1}{N_{U}}{\sum_{i=1}}\hat{f}({U_{i}})
and
\hat{f}(U)=\frac{1}{{N_{S}^{2}}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{{N_{S}}}{\rm{Tr}}({U^{\dagger}}|{b_{i}}\rangle\langle{b_{i}}|U{{\cal M}^{-1}}({U^{\dagger}}|{c_{j}}\rangle\langle{c_{j}}|U))
, where
b_{i}
and
c_{j}
are measurement outcomes of
U\rho U^{\dagger}
and
U\sigma U^{\dagger}
, respectively. Then, following the same procedures in
B.11
and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we can compute the following

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}]

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}(\rho\sigma),

(215)

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(\hat{f})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\Biggl(\left(\frac{N_{S}-1}{N_{S}}\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\Bigl[{\rm Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,{\cal M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|c_{1}\rangle\langle c_{1}|U\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times{\rm Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{2}\rangle\langle b_{2}|U\,{\cal M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|c_{2}\rangle\langle c_{2}|U\right)\right)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{N_{S}-1}{N_{S}^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\Bigl[{\rm Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,{\cal M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|c_{1}\rangle\langle c_{1}|U\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times{\rm Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,{\cal M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|c_{2}\rangle\langle c_{2}|U\right)\right)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{N_{S}-1}{N_{S}^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\Bigl[{\rm Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,{\cal M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|c_{1}\rangle\langle c_{1}|U\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times{\rm Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{2}\rangle\langle b_{2}|U\,{\cal M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|c_{1}\rangle\langle c_{1}|U\right)\right)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{N_{S}^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\Bigl[{\rm Tr}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|U\,{\cal M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|c_{1}\rangle\langle c_{1}|U\right)\right)^{2}\Bigr]-{\rm Tr}(\rho\sigma)^{2}\Biggr)

(216)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N_{U}}\Biggl(\frac{1}{N_{S}^{2}}\sqrt{V_{3}(\rho)V_{3}(\sigma)}+\frac{N_{S}-1}{N_{S}^{2}}\bigl(\sqrt{V_{1}(\rho)V_{3}(\sigma)}+\sqrt{V_{3}(\rho)V_{1}(\sigma)}\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left(\frac{N_{S}-1}{N_{S}}\right)^{2}\sqrt{V_{1}(\rho)V_{1}(\sigma)}\Biggr).

(217)

This gives the multi-shot variance bound for distributed inner-product estimation.
In the typical regime where
V_{1}(\rho),V_{1}(\sigma)=\mathcal{O}(1)
and

V_{3}(\rho),V_{3}(\sigma)=\mathcal{O}(2^{n})
, choosing

N_{S}=\mathcal{O}(2^{n/2})
suppresses the
V_{3}
-dependent term to a constant.

B.13
Typical values of
V_{1}(O,\rho)
and
V_{2}(O,\rho)

Let
O=\sum_{P}\alpha_{P}P
be a fixed observable independent of
\rho
.
Let
\mathbb{U}=\mathrm{Cl}(k)^{\otimes n/k}
, and assume that
k2^{k}=n/\alpha
.
For Haar-random pure states, the second moment identity gives

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)\operatorname{Tr}(\rho Q)]=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(P)\operatorname{Tr}(Q)+\operatorname{Tr}(PQ)}{D(D+1)}.

(218)

Using this identity in
V_{1}(O,\rho)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[V_{1}(O,\rho)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{P,Q}\alpha_{P}\alpha_{Q}\,\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)\operatorname{Tr}(\rho Q)]\,f(P,Q)

(219)

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(O)^{2}}{D(D+1)}+\frac{1}{D+1}\sum_{P}\alpha_{P}^{2}f(P,P)

(220)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\|O\|_{2}^{2}}{D}\right).

(221)

Here we used
f(P,P)=m_{P}^{-1}\leq De^{\alpha}
and

\sum_{P}\alpha_{P}^{2}=\|O\|_{2}^{2}/D
.

Similarly, since
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\rho]=I/D
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[V_{2}(O,\rho)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{P,Q}\alpha_{P}\alpha_{Q}\,\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\operatorname{Tr}(\rho PQ)]\,f(P,Q)

(222)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P}\alpha_{P}^{2}f(P,P)

(223)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\|O\|_{2}^{2}).

(224)

Therefore, for observables with bounded
\|O\|_{2}
, the typical values satisfy

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[V_{1}(O,\rho)]=\mathcal{O}(1/D),\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[V_{2}(O,\rho)]=\mathcal{O}(1).

(225)

B.14
Typical values of
V_{1}(\rho)
,
V_{2}(\rho)
, and
V_{3}(\rho)

Let
\mathbb{U}=\mathrm{Cl}(k)^{\otimes n/k}
, and assume that

k2^{k}=n/\alpha
.
Using the standard fourth-moment identity for Haar-random pure states,

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\rho^{\otimes 4}]=\frac{1}{D(D+1)(D+2)(D+3)}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{4}}U_{\sigma},

(226)

we can upper-bound
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[V_{1}(\rho)]
as follows.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\!\left[V_{1}(\rho)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\!\left[\sum_{P,Q}\frac{{\rm Tr}(\rho P)^{2}{\rm Tr}(\rho Q)^{2}}{D^{2}}\,f(P,Q)\right]

(227)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P,Q}\frac{{\rm Tr}\!\left(\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\!\left[\rho^{\otimes 4}\right](P\otimes P\otimes Q\otimes Q)\right)}{D^{2}}\,f(P,Q)

(228)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D^{3}(D+1)(D+2)(D+3)}\sum_{P,Q}f(P,Q)\sum_{\sigma\in S_{4}}{\rm Tr}\!\left(U_{\sigma}(P\otimes P\otimes Q\otimes Q)\right)

(229)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{D^{2}\sum_{P,Q}f(P,Q)+{\cal O}\!\left(D^{2}\sum_{P}f(P,P)+D^{2}\sum_{P}f(P,I)+D\sum_{P,Q}f(P,Q)\right)}{D^{3}(D+1)(D+2)(D+3)}

(230)

\displaystyle\leq 1+{\cal O}(1/D).

(231)

The same third-moment calculation used in Appendix
B.5
gives

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[V_{2}(\rho)]=\mathcal{O}(1).

(232)

It remains to bound
V_{3}(\rho)
.
Using the standard second-moment identity for Haar-random pure states,

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\rho^{\otimes 2}]=\frac{1}{D(D+1)}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{2}}U_{\sigma},

(233)

we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\sum_{P,Q}\frac{{\rm Tr}(\rho PQ)^{2}}{D^{2}}\,f(P,Q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P,Q}\frac{{\rm Tr}\!\left(\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\!\left[\rho^{\otimes 2}\right](PQ\otimes PQ)\right)}{D^{2}}\,f(P,Q)

(234)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D^{3}(D+1)}\left(\sum_{P,Q}{\rm Tr}(PQ)^{2}\,f(P,Q)+\sum_{P,Q}f(P,Q)\right)

(235)

\displaystyle\leq De^{\alpha}+{\cal O}(1).

(236)

In the calculation above, the average for the typical case was taken only for pure states. To include mixed states in the averaging, we can purify the system by introducing an environment
E
and work with the composite pure state
\rho_{\text{SE}}
. In that setting, every Pauli string
P
used in the proof is replaced by
P\otimes I_{E}
, where
I_{E}
is the identity operator in the environment
E
. Repeating the same steps under this extension shows that all resulting values are strictly lower (or at most equal) than those obtained in the pure state case.

B.15
Worst-case values of
V_{1}(\rho)
,
V_{2}(\rho)
, and
V_{3}(\rho)

Let
\mathbb{U}=\mathrm{Cl}(k)^{\otimes m}
, where
m=n/k
, and assume
k2^{k}=n/\alpha
. In the worst case, both
V_{1}(\rho)
and
V_{2}(\rho)
scale as
\mathcal{O}(3^{m})
. However, the conditions under which this scaling is attained are different. The upper bound for
V_{2}(\rho)
is already attained, up to constant factors, by block-product pure states

\rho=\ket{\Psi}\!\bra{\Psi},\qquad\ket{\Psi}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\ket{\Psi_i},\qquad\ket{\Psi_i}\in\mathcal{H}^{D_{k}}.

By contrast, for
V_{1}(\rho)
to attain the same worst-case scaling, the local
states
\ket{\Psi_i}
must additionally be stabilizer states.

We now bound
V_{3}(\rho)
. Recall that

V_{3}(\rho)=\frac{1}{D^{2}}\sum_{P,Q}{\rm Tr}(\rho PQ)^{2}f(P,Q).

Since
f(P,Q)=0
unless
P
and
Q
commute blockwise, the contributing
terms satisfy
PQ=\pm R
for a phase-free Pauli string
R
. As in the
single-shot purity calculation, we group the sum by
R=PQ
, but here the
coefficient is
f(P,Q)
rather than
f(P,Q)^{2}
. Define

B_{R}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
PQ=\pm R\end{subarray}}f(P,Q).

Then

V_{3}(\rho)=\frac{1}{D^{2}}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}B_{R}\,{\rm Tr}(\rho R)^{2}.

(237)

The coefficient
B_{R}
factorizes over the
m
blocks. For a single block,
let

B_{r}^{\rm loc}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{k}\\
PQ=\pm r\end{subarray}}f(P,Q).

Using Eq. (
59
),
we obtain

\displaystyle B_{I}^{\rm loc}

\displaystyle=1+(D_{k}^{2}-1)(D_{k}+1)=D_{k}^{3}+D_{k}^{2}-D_{k},

(238)

\displaystyle B_{r(\neq I)}^{\rm loc}

\displaystyle=2+\left(\frac{D_{k}^{2}}{2}-2\right)\frac{2(D_{k}+1)}{D_{k}+2}=D_{k}^{2}-D_{k}.

(239)

Therefore, if
A=\operatorname{supp}_{k}(R)
, then

B_{R}=(D_{k}^{3}+D_{k}^{2}-D_{k})^{m-|A|}(D_{k}^{2}-D_{k})^{|A|}.

(240)

Let
\mathcal{P}_{A}
be the phase-free Pauli basis on the blocks in
A
, and
let
D_{A}=D_{k}^{|A|}
. Since the exact block-support condition only restricts
the Pauli strings in
\mathcal{P}_{A}
, Pauli orthogonality gives

\sum_{\operatorname{supp}_{k}(R)=A}{\rm Tr}(\rho R)^{2}\leq\sum_{R_{A}\in\mathcal{P}_{A}}{\rm Tr}(\rho_{A}R_{A})^{2}=D_{A}{\rm Tr}(\rho_{A}^{2})\leq D_{k}^{|A|}.

(241)

Combining Eqs. (
237
),
(
240
), and (
241
), we obtain

\displaystyle V_{3}(\rho)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{D^{2}}\sum_{A\subseteq[m]}(D_{k}^{3}+D_{k}^{2}-D_{k})^{m-|A|}(D_{k}^{2}-D_{k})^{|A|}D_{k}^{|A|}

(242)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D^{2}}\left[D_{k}^{3}+D_{k}^{2}-D_{k}+D_{k}(D_{k}^{2}-D_{k})\right]^{m}

(243)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D^{2}}(2D_{k}^{3}-D_{k})^{m}

(244)

\displaystyle=\left(2D_{k}-D_{k}^{-1}\right)^{m}

(245)

\displaystyle\leq(2D_{k})^{m}=D\,2^{m}=2^{n}2^{n/k}.

(246)

Thus, in the worst case,

V_{3}(\rho)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(2^{n}2^{n/k}\right).

(247)

This upper bound is tight up to constant factors. For a block-product pure
state

\rho=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\ket{\psi_i}\!\bra{\psi_i},

the expression for
V_{3}(\rho)
factorizes over blocks. Using

\sum_{r\in\mathcal{P}_{k}}{\rm Tr}(\psi_{i}r)^{2}=D_{k}
and

{\rm Tr}(\psi_{i}I)^{2}=1
, the single-block contribution is

\frac{1}{D_{k}^{2}}\left[B_{I}^{\rm loc}+(D_{k}-1)B_{r\neq I}^{\rm loc}\right]=2D_{k}-1.

Hence

V_{3}(\rho)=(2D_{k}-1)^{m},

(248)

showing that the worst-case scaling is

V_{3}(\rho)=\Theta(D\,2^{m})
.

B.16
Upper bound on

\operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{\sigma}P))

Proof.

Let
U_{1},\ldots,U_{N_{U}}
be independently sampled measurement bases.
For each basis
U_{i}
, let
b_{ij}
and
c_{ij}
denote the
j
-th measurement
outcomes obtained from
\rho
and
\sigma
, respectively.
We define

\hat{\rho}_{\sigma}=\hat{\rho}-\hat{\sigma}+\sigma,

(249)

where

\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{U}}\hat{\rho}_{U_{i}}(N_{\rho}),\qquad\hat{\sigma}=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{U}}\hat{\sigma}_{U_{i}}(N_{\sigma}),

(250)

with

\hat{\rho}_{U}(N_{\rho})=\frac{1}{N_{\rho}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rho}}\mathcal{M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|b_{ij}\rangle\langle b_{j}|U\right),

(251)

and

\hat{\sigma}_{U}(N_{\sigma})=\frac{1}{N_{\sigma}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\sigma}}\mathcal{M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}|c_{j}\rangle\langle c_{j}|U\right).

(252)

For a fixed basis
U
, write

\hat{\rho}_{\sigma}(U)=\hat{\rho}_{U}(N_{\rho})-\hat{\sigma}_{U}(N_{\sigma})+\sigma.

(253)

Since the
N_{U}
measurement bases are independent,

\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{\sigma}P)\right)=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{\sigma}(U)P)\right).

(254)

Define

A_{U}=\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{U}(N_{\rho})P),\qquad B_{U}=\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\sigma}_{U}(N_{\sigma})P).

Then

\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{\sigma}(U)P)=A_{U}-B_{U}+\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma P).

(255)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{\sigma}(U)P)\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}(A_{U}-B_{U})

(256)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(A_{U}-B_{U})^{2}]-\operatorname{Tr}\!\left((\rho-\sigma)P\right)^{2}.

(257)

We now compute the second moment.
By the multi-shot variance calculation in Appendix
B.9
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[A_{U}^{2}]

\displaystyle=m_{P}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{N_{\rho}}+\frac{N_{\rho}-1}{N_{\rho}}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}\right),

(258)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[B_{U}^{2}]

\displaystyle=m_{P}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{N_{\sigma}}+\frac{N_{\sigma}-1}{N_{\sigma}}\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma P)^{2}\right).

(259)

It remains to compute the cross term. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[A_{U}B_{U}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\rho}N_{\sigma}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rho}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{\sigma}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[m_{P}^{-2}(U,P,b_{j})(U,P,c_{\ell})\right]

(260)

\displaystyle=m_{P}^{-2}\mathbb{E}_{U}\sum_{b,c}(U,P,b)(U,P,c)\,\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U\rho U^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|\right)\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(U\sigma U^{\dagger}|c\rangle\langle c|\right)

(261)

\displaystyle=m_{P}^{-2}\mathbb{E}_{U}\sum_{b,c}\sum_{Q,R}(U,P,b)(U,Q,b)(U,P,c)(U,R,c)\,\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho Q)\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma R)}{D^{2}}

(262)

\displaystyle=m_{P}^{-2}\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\left[\mathbf{1}\{UPU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\}\right]\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma P)

(263)

\displaystyle=m_{P}^{-1}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma P).

(264)

Combining the above identities gives

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{\sigma}P)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\Bigg[m_{P}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{N_{\rho}}+\frac{N_{\rho}-1}{N_{\rho}}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}+\frac{1}{N_{\sigma}}+\frac{N_{\sigma}-1}{N_{\sigma}}\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma P)^{2}-2\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma P)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\operatorname{Tr}\!\left((\rho-\sigma)P\right)^{2}\Bigg]

(265)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m_{P}^{-1}}{N_{U}}\left(\frac{1-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}}{N_{\rho}}+\frac{1-\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma P)^{2}}{N_{\sigma}}+\Tr((\rho-\sigma)P)^{2}\right).

(266)

This proves the desired bound.
∎

We now consider a variant in which the bias contribution is not evaluated exactly,
but is estimated using an additional independent classical shadow of
\sigma
.
Let

\hat{\rho}_{\sigma}=\hat{\rho}-\hat{\sigma}_{1}+\hat{\sigma}_{2},

(267)

where
\hat{\rho}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{1}
are constructed using the same

N_{U}
measurement bases, while
\hat{\sigma}_{2}
is constructed independently
from
N_{U}^{\prime}
additional measurement bases applied to
\sigma
.
For a Pauli string
P
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}(P\hat{\rho}_{\sigma})\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(P(\hat{\rho}-\hat{\sigma}_{1})\right)+\operatorname{Tr}(P\hat{\sigma}_{2})\right)

(268)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(P(\hat{\rho}-\hat{\sigma}_{1})\right)\right)+\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}(P\hat{\sigma}_{2})\right).

(269)

Here, the second equality follows from the independence of
\hat{\sigma}_{2}

from
\hat{\rho}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{1}
.
Since

B.18
Shadow channel
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
in the presence of errors.

In
A.2
, we have shown that, in the absence of errors, the eigenoperators of the shadow channel
\mathcal{M}
are the Pauli strings
P
, and their corresponding eigenvalues are
m_{P}=\mathbb{E}{{}_{U}}[{\bf{1}}\{UP{U^{\dagger}}\in\pm{\cal Z}\}]={\Pr_{U}}(UP{U^{\dagger}}\in\pm{\cal Z})
. In this section, we will extend this to the regime with the presence of errors. First, since the unitaries we used in RM consist solely of Clifford gates, we can transform any arbitrary error channel in RM into a Pauli error channel through Pauli Twirling
[
66
]
. Previously, two main models of error channel have been studied: the first
[
12
]
,
\Lambda_{U}=\Lambda\cdot\mathcal{U}
, and the second
[
51
]
,
\Lambda_{U}=\Lambda_{t}\cdot\mathcal{U}_{t}…\Lambda_{1}\cdot\mathcal{U}_{1}
where, where
\mathcal{U}_{(l)}
represents superoperator of
U_{(l)}
and
\Lambda_{(l)}
is the quantum error channel (at the
l
th layer) and
U=U_{t}...U_{1}
. Because two error channels are Pauli error channel, denote
\Lambda_{U}(P)=\lambda_{U,P}UPU^{\dagger}
(For the first error model
\lambda_{U,P}=\lambda_{UPU^{\dagger}}
and for the second error model
\lambda_{U,P}={\lambda_{{U_{1}}P{U_{1}}^{\dagger}}}...{\lambda_{{U_{t}}...{U_{1}}P{U_{1}}^{\dagger}...{U_{t}}^{\dagger}}}
).

\displaystyle\widetilde{\cal M}(P)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\left[\sum_{b}U^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|U\cdot{\rm Tr}\!\left(\Lambda_{U}(P)|b\rangle\langle b|\right)\right]

(277)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\left[\sum_{b}U^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|U\cdot\lambda_{U,P}\,{\rm Tr}\!\left(UPU^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|\right)\right]

(278)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\left[\sum_{b}\sum_{Q}\lambda_{U,P}\,{\rm Tr}\!\left(UPU^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|\right){\rm Tr}\!\left(UQU^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|\right)Q/D\right]

(279)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\left[\sum_{b}\sum_{Q}\lambda_{U,P}\,{\rm Tr}\!\left(UPU^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|\right){\rm Tr}\!\left(UQU^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.\cdot\mathbf{1}\{UPU^{\dagger}\in\pm{\cal Z}\}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{UQU^{\dagger}\in\pm{\cal Z}\}Q/D\right]

(280)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\left[\sum_{Q}\lambda_{U,P}\,\delta_{P,Q}\,\mathbf{1}\{UPU^{\dagger}\in\pm{\cal Z}\}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{UQU^{\dagger}\in\pm{\cal Z}\}\right]Q

(281)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\left[\lambda_{U,P}\,\mathbf{1}\{UPU^{\dagger}\in\pm{\cal Z}\}\right]P

(282)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{m}_{P}P

(283)

Because
\lambda_{U,P}\leq 1
,
\widetilde{m}_{P}
is always less than or equal to
m_{P}
.

B.19
Upper bound on

\operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}P))
in the presence of errors

We consider the noisy shadow channel

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(A)=\mathbb{E}_{U}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}U^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|U\,\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(\Lambda_{U}(A)|b\rangle\langle b|\right).

(284)

After Pauli twirling, we assume that the effective noisy measurement channel
acts on Pauli operators as

\Lambda_{U}(P)=\lambda_{U,P}UPU^{\dagger}.

(285)

In particular, the channel is unital, so
\lambda_{U,I}=1
.
As shown in the previous section, each Pauli string
P
remains an eigenoperator of

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
, with eigenvalue

\widetilde{m}_{P}=\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\left[\lambda_{U,P}\mathbf{1}\{UPU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\}\right],

(286)

where
\mathcal{Z}=\{I,Z\}^{n}
.

For the multi-shot estimator, let
U_{1},\ldots,U_{N_{U}}
be independently sampled
measurement bases.
For each basis
U_{i}
, let
b_{ij}
be the
j
-th measurement outcome from
the noisy measurement of
\rho
.
We define

\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{U}}\hat{\rho}_{U_{i}}(N_{S}),\qquad\hat{\rho}_{U_{i}}(N_{S})=\frac{1}{N_{S}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{S}}\hat{\rho}_{U_{i},j},

(287)

where

\hat{\rho}_{U_{i},j}=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}\!\left(U_{i}^{\dagger}|b_{ij}\rangle\langle b_{ij}|U_{i}\right).

(288)

Then

\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{U,j}P)=\widetilde{m}_{P}^{-1}(U,P,b_{j}).

(289)

Since the
N_{U}
measurement bases are independent, we have

\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}P)\right)=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{U}(N_{S})P)\right).

(290)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{U}(N_{S})P)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{U}(N_{S})P)^{2}\right]-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}.

(291)

The first term is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{U}(N_{S})P)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{S}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{U,1}P)^{2}\right]+\frac{N_{S}-1}{N_{S}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{U,1}P)\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{U,2}P)\right].

(292)

For the diagonal contribution,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{U,1}P)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\widetilde{m}_{P}^{-2}\mathbb{E}_{U}\sum_{b}(U,P,b)^{2}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(\Lambda_{U}(\rho)|b\rangle\langle b|\right)

(293)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{m}_{P}^{-2}\mathbb{E}_{U}\sum_{b}\sum_{Q}\lambda_{U,Q}(U,P,b)^{2}(U,Q,b)\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho Q)}{2^{n}}

(294)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{m}_{P}^{-2}\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\left[\mathbf{1}\{UPU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\}\right]

(295)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{m}_{P}^{-2}m_{P}.

(296)

Here we used
\lambda_{U,I}=1
in the third line.
For the off-diagonal contribution,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{U,1}P)\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{U,2}P)\right]

\displaystyle=\widetilde{m}_{P}^{-2}\mathbb{E}_{U}\sum_{b_{1},b_{2}}(U,P,b_{1})(U,P,b_{2})\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(\Lambda_{U}(\rho)|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|\right)\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(\Lambda_{U}(\rho)|b_{2}\rangle\langle b_{2}|\right)

(297)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{m}_{P}^{-2}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\left[\lambda_{U,P}^{2}\mathbf{1}\{UPU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\}\right].

(298)

Combining these identities gives

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}P)\right)=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\Bigg[\widetilde{m}_{P}^{-2}\Bigg(\frac{m_{P}}{N_{S}}+\frac{N_{S}-1}{N_{S}}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\left[\lambda_{U,P}^{2}\mathbf{1}\{UPU^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\}\right]\Bigg)-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}\Bigg].

(299)

In the noiseless case,
\lambda_{U,P}=1
and
\widetilde{m}_{P}=m_{P}
, so this reduces to

\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}P)\right)=\frac{1}{N_{U}}\left[m_{P}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{N_{S}}+\frac{N_{S}-1}{N_{S}}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}\right)-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)^{2}\right],

(300)

which agrees with the noiseless multi-shot formula.

B.21
Matrix Bernstein inequality for block shadow

Although block shadow does not always provide more accurate measurements than random Pauli measurement (RPM) for every physical observable—for instance, consider a Pauli string in which, within each block, only one Pauli matrix is nontrivial while all others are identities; in this case, the variance under RPM is
\mathcal{O}(3^{n/k})
, whereas for block shadow, it reaches the maximum value of
(2^{k}+1)^{n/k}
—using shallow shadow can offer favorable scaling compared to RPM if the region of interest is contiguous. For example, to analyze how few measurements are required for quantum state tomography on a contiguous region A of
r(\leq n)
qubits using block shadow, one can invoke the following matrix Bernstein inequality
[
25
]
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},...X_{T}
defined by

{X_{t}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r/k}\left(({2^{k}}+1){\rm{}}U_{i}^{\left(t\right)\dagger}|b_{i}^{(t)}\rangle\langle b_{i}^{(t)}|U_{i}^{(t)}-I\right)

(325)

be independent random
D_{r}
-dimensional matrices with
\mathbb{E}[{X_{t}}]={\rho_{A}}
and
\|{X_{t}}-E[{X_{t}}]\|_{\infty}\leq R
almost surely, then the following holds

\Pr\!\left[\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}\bigl(X_{t}-\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]\bigr)\right\|_{\infty}\geq\epsilon\right]\leq 2D_{r}\exp\!\left(-\frac{T\epsilon^{2}/2}{\sigma^{2}+R\epsilon/3}\right)

(326)

where
{\sigma^{2}}={\rm{}}||1/T{\sum_{t}}\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{2}]|{|_{\infty}}
. First, it is straightforward to compute
R=2^{r}+1
.
\sigma^{2}
can be determined as follows:
Let
X=\otimes_{i=1}^{r/k}[({2^{k}}+1)U_{i}^{\dagger}|{b_{i}}\rangle\langle{b_{i}}|{U_{i}})-I]
, then
{X^{2}}=\otimes_{i=1}^{r/k}[({4^{k}}-1)U_{i}^{\dagger}|{b_{i}}\rangle\langle{b_{i}}|{U_{i}})+I]

{\sigma^{2}}={\left\|{\mathbb{E}[{X^{2}}]}\right\|_{\infty}}\leq\sum_{l=0}^{r/k}\binom{r/k}{l}{2^{kl}}{({2^{k}}-1)^{r/k-l}}={({2^{k+1}}-1)^{r/k}}

(327)

When

\sigma^{2}\leq 3^{r}
, which is consistent with previous results
[
25
,
34
]
. For
k=2

, and as

increases, it gradually approaches to
\mathcal{O}(2^{r})

\displaystyle\Pr\!\left[\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}\bigl(X_{t}-\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]\bigr)\right\|_{1}\geq\epsilon\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2D_{r}\exp\!\left(-\frac{T\epsilon^{2}/2}{4^{r}(\sigma^{2}+R\epsilon/3)}\right)

(328)

\displaystyle\leq 2D_{r}\exp\!\left(-\frac{3T\epsilon^{2}}{8\times 4^{r}\bigl((2^{k+1}-1)^{r/k}\bigr)}\right)

(329)

Based on these results, setting

ensures that the empirical average converges to

with high probability. Moreover, by following the remaining steps outlined in the Appendix of
[
34
]
, one can prove that the required sample complexity

for training can be reduced accordingly.

B.22
Estimation of spectral form factor

It has been suggested that the spectral form factor (SFF), denoted as

K(t)=\frac{1}{4^{n}}\mathbb{E}_{T}[\text{Tr}(T(t))\text{Tr}(T(t)^{\dagger})],

(330)

can be utilized to distinguish whether quantum dynamics driven by a Hamiltonian is chaotic, consistent with predictions from random matrix theory, or many-body localized. Most of the previous analyses in our work involved calculations dependent on specific quantum states, whereas the SFF differs in that it is determined not by particular quantum states
\rho
but by the underlying quantum dynamics
U=\exp(-iHt)
. Nevertheless, we show that by employing the block shadow method introduced in the main text, it is possible to achieve the same sample complexity scaling as obtained using global Clifford circuits, even with circuits of
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
depth. We used the same experimental sequences as described in
[
40
]
. The key difference is that, instead of
U\sim\text{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}
, we used
U\sim\text{Cl}(k)^{\otimes n/k}
. For further details regarding the measurement protocol, we refer the reader to
[
40
]
. We can use an unbiased estimator for
K(t)
as follows:

\hat{K}(t)=\frac{1}{M}\sum\limits_{r=1}^{M}{{{(-{2^{k}})}^{-|{s^{(r)}}{|_{k}}}}},

(331)

where
|s|_{k}
is the number of blocks for which the measurement bitstrings of that block differs from the all-zero string 0…0. It can be readily shown that Eq. (
331
) is an unbiased estimator, and its variance is calculated as follows:

\displaystyle{\rm Var}[\hat{K}(t)]

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm Var}\!\left[(-2^{k})^{-\,|s|_{k}}\right]}{M}

(332)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left(1+\frac{1}{2^{k}}-\frac{1}{4^{k}}\right)^{n/k}-K(t)^{2}\right)

(333)

Thus, from the above calculation, we see that when
k2^{k}=\Omega(n)
, the scaling matches that of the global Clifford case (
k=n
).

C.1.2

1

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\!\left[\left.\mathrm{CONF}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{P};\mathbf{B})\,\right|\,\mathbf{B}^{\#}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\!\left[\sum_{l=1}^{L}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}M_{l}(\mathbf{B})\right)\,\middle|\,\mathbf{B}^{\#}\right]

(336)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\!\left[\sum_{l=1}^{L}\prod_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\mathbf{1}\!\left\{U_{m^{\prime}}P_{l}U_{m^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\in\pm\mathcal{Z}\right\}\right)\,\middle|\,\mathbf{B}^{\#}\right]

(337)

(338)

C.2
Fidelity estimation (Fig.
3

Estimating the fidelity with respect to a target quantum state has been extensively studied
[
32
,
20
]
. Such estimations are essential in evaluating how accurately desired quantum states are prepared on noisy quantum computers. Previous studies
[
32
]
have established that the RCM effectively estimates expectation values of low-rank observables, including fidelity. Here, we investigate the advantages and limitations of employing block shadow with block size
k
, where
k=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
.

In the worst case, block shadow requires a sample complexity that increases exponentially
\mathcal{O}(3^{n/k})
for system size
n
. This worst-case saturation occurs when the quantum state has a simple block tensor product structure
|\Psi\rangle=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n/k}|{\Psi_{i}}\rangle
where all blocks are not entangled with each other, which might differ from the quantum states typically encountered in practice. In contrast, we proved that, for typical cases, the block shadow allows for sample complexity scaling similar to that of RCM
[
32
]
. To support this, we conducted numerical experiments presented in the main text, showing that accurate fidelity estimation is possible even for approximately 50 qubits.

C.2.1

We prepared a random quantum state for a system of size
n
= 48 qubits by applying 8 CZ layers and interleaving random single-qubit gates sampled from
\mathbb{U}(2)
before and after each CZ gate.

C.2.2
Comparison with RCM

Previous analyses of multi-shot classical shadows with RCM reported that reusing the same measurement basis gives little improvement in certain regimes
[
27
,
71
]
.
This behavior can be understood from the variance decomposition for fidelity estimation.
For a rank-one target observable
\Psi=\ket{\Psi}\!\bra{\Psi}
, the multi-shot variance is

{\rm{Var}}({\rm{Tr}}(\hat{\rho}\Psi))=\frac{1}{{N_{U}}}\left(\frac{1}{{N_{S}}}V_{2}(\Psi,\rho)+\frac{{N_{S}}-1}{{N_{S}}}V_{1}(\Psi,\rho)-{\rm{Tr}}(\rho\Psi)^{2}\right).

(347)

Thus, increasing
N_{S}
is useful only when the limiting term
V_{1}(\Psi,\rho)
is small compared with
V_{2}(\Psi,\rho)
.

For
\mathbb{U}=\mathrm{Cl}(n)
, one has

V_{1}(\Psi,\rho)\leq 3+\mathcal{O}(1/D),\qquad V_{2}(\Psi,\rho)\leq 3+\mathcal{O}(1/D).

(348)

In the worst case, this bound is saturated.
For example, when
\rho=\Psi
is a stabilizer state, both

and

are
3+\mathcal{O}(1/D)

does not reduce the variance beyond the

floor, which explains why multi-shot measurements give little benefit in such worst-case examples
[
27
,
71
]
.

By contrast, the typical-case behavior is different.
As shown in Appendix
B.13
, for a fixed rank-one target
\Psi
and Haar-random
\rho

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[V_{1}(\Psi,\rho)]=\mathcal{O}(1/D),\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[V_{2}(\Psi,\rho)]=\mathcal{O}(1).

(349)

Therefore, in the typical-case regime, the term
V_{2}/N_{S}
can be suppressed by reusing each measurement basis, while the remaining term
V_{1}
stays small.
This shows that multi-shot measurements can be beneficial for fidelity estimation, even though they may provide little improvement in worst-case stabilizer examples. Importantly, this typical-case advantage persists even for logarithmic-depth block-shadow circuits.

C.2.3
Post-processing procedure using a tensor network

In many cases, shallow shadow methods offer the advantage of estimating fidelity with a measurement circuits of depth
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
and sample complexity scaling comparable to RCM; however, they introduce additional complexity in the post-processing step. In this section, we describe the post-processing procedure and compare the computational costs associated with brickwork and block circuits.

\chi_{\mathrm{inv}}

6

C.3.2
Post-processing procedure for purity estimation

A similar distinction appears in the post-processing cost for purity estimation.
In the shallow regime considered here, we take
d_{\mathrm{RM}}=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
for brickwork circuits and
k=\mathcal{O}(\log n)

\chi_{\mathrm{inv}}

\Tr\!\left(U^{\dagger}\ket{b}\!\bra{b}U\,\mathcal{M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}\ket{c}\!\bra{c}U\right)\right)

\mathrm{Time_{\mathrm{brickwork}}}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\chi_{\mathrm{inv}}^{2}2^{8d_{\mathrm{RM}}}n\right).

\mathrm{Time_{\mathrm{block}}}=\mathcal{O}(n).

(356)

Indeed, for
U=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n/k}U_{i}

\Tr\!\left(U^{\dagger}\ket{b}\!\bra{b}U\,\mathcal{M}^{-1}\!\left(U^{\dagger}\ket{c}\!\bra{c}U\right)\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{n/k}\left[(2^{k}+1)\delta_{b_{i},c_{i}}-1\right],

where

C.5
Entanglement-enhanced machine learning

So far, we have studied how classical shadows with shallow measurements improve the estimation of physical observables.
Beyond observable estimation, recent theoretical
[
34
,
42
]
and experimental
[
14
]
work has used data obtained from randomized measurements (RM) as input to classical machine learning (ML) algorithms.
Most prior studies used randomized measurements consisting only of single-qubit gates.
Here, we extend this setting to shallow measurements with entangling gates and investigate whether the resulting data can improve classical ML performance.

C.5.2
Error mitigation

If
\mathbb{U}=\text{Cl}(k)
, then the inverse of the noise shadow channel
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
can be expressed as

{\widetilde{\cal M}^{-1}}={\sum_{\lambda\in R}}\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}^{-1}{\Pi_{\lambda}}={\widetilde{f}_{0}}^{-1}{\Pi_{0}}+{\widetilde{f}_{1}}^{-1}{\Pi_{1}}={\Pi_{0}}+\alpha({D_{k}}+1){\Pi_{1}},

(378)

where
{\Pi_{0}}={\rm{}}|I\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle I|,{\rm{}}{\Pi_{1}}={\sum_{P\neq I}}|P\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle P|
, and
\alpha\geq 1
. When expressed in the form of a standard quantum channel, it is written as

{\widetilde{\cal M}^{-1}}(A)=\alpha({D_{k}}+1)A-\frac{{\alpha({D_{k}}+1)-1}}{{{D_{k}}}}I.

(379)

In the noiseless case,
\alpha=1
, turning it into
\mathcal{M}^{-1}
. When computing the shadow kernel defined by

{k^{{\rm{(shadow)}}}}({S_{T}}(\rho),{S_{T}}(\sigma))=\exp\left({\frac{\tau}{{{T^{2}}}}\sum_{t,t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\exp\left({\frac{1}{{n/k}}\sum_{i=1}^{n/k}{\gamma_{i}}{\rm{Tr}}(\hat{\rho}_{i}^{(t)}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{(t^{\prime})})}\right)}\right).

(380)

we need to use the noise shadow channel
\cal\widetilde{M}
for
\hat{\rho}_{i}^{(t)}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{(t)}
, which requires additional calibration experiments. However, if we assume the error channel is translationally invariant and choose appropriate hyperparameters
\tau
and
\{\gamma_{i}\}_{i}
, we can compute the error-mitigated (EM) shadow kernel without conducting further calibration experiments.

Given that

{\rm{Tr}}({\widetilde{\cal M}^{-1}}({A_{i}}){\widetilde{\cal M}^{-1}}({B_{i}}))=\alpha_{i}^{2}{\rm{Tr}}({{\cal M}^{-1}}({A_{i}}){{\cal M}^{-1}}({B_{i}}))+{\beta_{i}},

(381)

where
{\beta_{i}}:=({D_{k}}+2)\alpha_{i}^{2}-\frac{{{\alpha_{i}}({D_{k}}+1)-1}}{{{D_{k}}}}
, the following holds accordingly

{k^{{\rm{(shadow)}}}}({\rm{EM}};{\{{\gamma_{i}}\}_{i}},\tau)={k^{{\rm{(shadow)}}}}({\rm{Raw}};{\{{\gamma_{i}}\alpha_{i}^{2}\}_{i}},\tau{e^{\bar{\beta}}}),

(382)

where
\bar{\beta}=\frac{{{\sum_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}{\beta_{i}}}}{{n/k}}
. If the error channel is translationally invariant, then all
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i}
values are the same. Consequently, we can redefine the hyperparameters to obtain the error-mitigated shadow kernel

{k^{{\rm{(shadow)}}}}({\rm{EM}};\gamma,\tau)={k^{{\rm{(shadow)}}}}({\rm{Raw}};\gamma{\alpha^{2}},\tau{e^{\bar{\beta}}})

(383)

without conducting additional calibration experiments. To approximate a uniform error channel in experiments, one could randomly sample the qubit layouts. However, this introduces additional errors due to swap gate overhead in restricted qubit connectivity. Because we employed a one-dimensional open-chain qubit layout, we altered the direction of the chain during the experiment to avoid using additional gates. For the experiments presented in the main text of Fig.
5

(384)

where
\hat{\rho}_{i}^{(t,s)}={{\cal M}^{-1}}(U_{t}^{\dagger}|{b_{s}}\rangle\langle{b_{s}}|{U_{t}})

(\tau,\gamma)=(1,1)
when
k
