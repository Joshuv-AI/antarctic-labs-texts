Convergence of two-timescale gradient descent ascent dynamics: finite-dimensional and mean-field perspectives
Jing An, Jianfeng Lu

Abstract
The two-timescale gradient descent-ascent (GDA) is a canonical gradient algorithm designed to find Nash equilibria in min-max games. We analyze the two-timescale GDA by investigating the effects of learning rate ratio on convergence behavior in both finite-dimensional and mean-field settings. In particular, for finite-dimensional quadratic min-max games, we obtain long-time convergence in near quasi-static regimes through the hypocoercivity method. For mean-field GDA dynamics, we investigate convergence under a finite-scale ratio using a mixed synchronous-reflection coupling technique.



1
Introduction

Multi-objective optimization problems, which aim to optimize multiple objectives simultaneously, have gained significant attention due to their wide range of applications, including economics and finance
[
50
]
, engineering design
[
2
]
, and control systems
[
26
]
. In this paper, we focus on a specific bi-objective problem, which is the classical min-max optimization

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}K(x,y).

(1.1)

Here
K
can be considered as the game objective for min and max players, and
x,y
are player strategies from the strategy space
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
.

Min-max optimization problems are important as they arise naturally when competing objectives or adversarial dynamics are involved in machine learning tasks. Such tasks include generative adversarial networks
[
28
]
, multi-agent reinforcement learning
[
8
]
, distance computation in optimal transport
[
40
]
. The goal of min-max optimization problem (
1.1
) is to find the pure Nash equilibria
[
45
]
, or saddle points. However, finding the Nash equilibrium (NE) is noticeably subtle, as it may not even exist.

In terms of learning algorithms to solve min-max optimization problems (
1.1
), the most standard first-order method is the gradient descent-ascent (GDA) algorithm. At each iteration, the GDA performs gradient descent over
x
and gradient ascent over
y
. With equal stepsizes for descent and ascent updates, there has been massive literature establishing both asymptotic and non-asymptotic convergence results of the average iterates
[
35
,
47
,
46
]
. For finite-dimensional min-max problems, under the assumption that
K
is convex-concave, GDA can achieve linear convergence
[
14
,
1
,
39
]
.
However, in more general settings where
K
lacks convex-concave structure, GDA with equal stepsizes may converge to limit cycles or even diverge
[
5
,
31
,
43
,
18
,
4
]
.

To tackle the difficulties caused by nonconvexity-nonconcavity, the
two-timescale GDA algorithm, which takes different learning rates
for gradient descent and ascent updates, has
become a popular alternative. As
[
30
]
shows, the
two-timescale GDA empirically has better convergence
performance. Regarding convergence results of the two-timescale GDA in
finite dimensional Euclidean spaces, we mention that the best
last-iterate convergence results that we are aware of assume
f
only
satisfies weak convexity-concavity, such as two-sided
Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL) condition
[
20
,
55
]
, under the assumption that a saddle point exists.

Beyond finite-dimensional min-max problems, there has been significant recent progress on min-max problems in the mean-field regime
[
32
,
22
,
42
,
41
,
52
,
33
,
37
,
15
,
9
]
. These problems, also known as zero-sum games in the space of probability distributions, can be formulated as

\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}\max_{q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}K(x,y)p(x)q(y)\,dxdy,

(1.2)

Compared with the finite dimensional case, the pure strategies
x,y
are replaced by mixed strategies
p,q
which are probability distributions over the set of strategies.
The solution
(p^{*},q^{*})
to such infinite-dimensional min-max problems is called mixed Nash equilibrium (MNE). Compared to the existence of NE for pure strategies, existence theory of MNE is better established. For example, by Glicksberg’s minimax theorem for continuous games
[
27
]
, such a MNE exists if
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
are compact manifolds
without boundary. However, finding the MNE is in general difficult. Recent works such as
[
22
]
suggest an infinite-dimensional entropy-regularized min-max problem, so that a unique Nash equilibrium is guaranteed to exist and has an explicit form. The mean-field counterpart of GDA, known as Wasserstein gradient descent-ascent flows (or mean-field GDA), has become a popular topic of study, particularly regarding its convergence guarantees and rates
[
54
]
.

In terms of whether the two-timescale mean-field GDA with a fixed finite scale ratio
\eta>0
converges to the unique MNE
[
22
,
54
]
, previous established results are unfortunately limited: For compact manifold domains,
[
42
]
obtained convergence guarantees in the quasi-static regime (where
\eta=+\infty
or
0
). This was later extended by
[
41
]
through a novel Lyapunov construction under log-Sobolev inequality assumptions, but still the convergence is limited near the quasi-static regime where
\eta\gg 1
or
\eta\ll 1
. For the whole Euclidean spaces, a recent work
[
9
]
provides exponential convergence guarantees for mean-field GDA with
\eta=1
, assuming that the objective function
K
is smooth and globally strongly convex-concave.

In this work, we investigate the two-timescale GDA in the continuous-time limit, and conduct convergence analysis from new perspectives in partial differential equations and stochastic analysis. In particular, if we take
\eta=\eta_{y}/\eta_{x}
as the ratio of learning rates, we want to answer the following question:

How does the two-timescale GDA converge depending on the ratio of learning rates
\eta
?

We will provide quantitative answers for both finite-dimensional and infinite-dimensional cases.

1.1
Our contributions

We highlight the major contributions as follows.

•

In the finite-dimensional setting, we analyze the dynamics of the two-timescale GDA algorithm applied to the classical quadratic game
[
38
]
. Through rescaling, we identify that the learning dynamics exhibit a hypocoercive structure
[
51
]
. Leveraging this framework, we construct a Lyapunov function to quantitatively estimate the convergence rate with respect to ratio of learning rates
\eta
(Theorem
3.1
). For completeness, we further discuss how to design a preconditioner for the two-timescale dynamics to reduce sensitivity to extreme values of
\eta
.

•

In the infinite-dimensional / mean-field setting, we examine the entropy-regularized min-max problem introduced by
[
22
]
and study the convergence of the two-timescale mean-field GDA for a finite range of
\eta
using the mixed synchronous-reflection coupling method
[
24
]
. Our convergence analysis accommodates
locally nonconvex-nonconcave
objective functions
K
and highlights how feasibility of contraction depends on the choice of the ratio
\eta
, the geometric properties of
K
, and the strength of the entropy regularization (Theorem
5.1
).

Moreover, in Appendix
A
, we present an alternative approach using the averaging method to derive the leading term of the convergence rate in the finite-dimensional case when the interaction matrix dominates, a topic previously studied in
[
53
]
through spectral analysis.

Related works

Two-timescale methods.

Including the classical two-timescale stochastic approximation
[
7
,
34
]
, there have been many types of two-timescale learning methods applied across a variety of problems such as distributed optimization
[
36
,
11
]
, finding fixed points in reinforcement learning
[
17
,
16
,
3
,

, as well as finding global optimality in optimization as we mentioned previously. Separation of timescales often serves to enhance convergence behavior, facilitate empirical simulations

]
, or even enable the discovery of distinct solutions by appropriately tuning learning rates

]
.

Hypocoercivity.

Originally developed for the analysis of kinetic equations such as the Boltzmann and Fokker-Planck equations, the hypocoercivity method

has become a powerful tool in the study of stochastic and partial differential equations. It provides a framework for estimating energy dissipation by capturing the interplay between degenerate dissipation and mixing effects
[

,
29
,
48
,
10

. This variational approach serves as an alternative to conventional methods based on spectral analysis, and we employ it to establish Theorem

.

Coupling methods.

Coupling is a powerful method in probability theory through which random variables can be compared with each other. Recently, variants of coupling methods have been developed to estimate contraction rates for Langevin dynamics
[
24
,
25
,
49
]
regarding different types of drifts, and later extended to sampling and optimization
[
23
,
12
,
13
,
44
]
.

Organization

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the problem setup, notations, and informal statements of the main theorems. Section
3
investigates the convergence of the two-timescale GDA dynamics for the finite-dimensional quadratic game using a hypocoercivity approach. In Section

, we discuss preconditioned saddle point problems, which help mitigate the effects of extreme values of the ratio

presents a detailed convergence analysis of the two-timescale mean-field GDA for a finite range of
\eta
using the coupling method.

2
Setup and main results

This section provides preliminary calculations, definitions, and notations used throughout the paper. Let
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
denote the space of probability distributions over a compact manifold
\mathcal{X}
.
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm for vectors, and
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm of a matrix. We use
\kappa^{-}(r)=\max\{0,-\kappa(r)\}
to denote the negative part of the function. Sometimes we use the notation
\dot{f}(t)=df/dt
.

2.1
Finite dimensional quadratic games

In the first part, we consider solving the quadratic game in the finite dimension.

\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\max_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}K(x,y)=\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\max_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\Big\{\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}Qx+x^{\top}Py-\frac{1}{2}y^{\top}Ry\Big\}.

(2.1)

The quadratic game, which arises from control problems such as linear quadratic regulators (LQR), is a widely-studied subject. If we solve this zero-sum game by two-timescale gradient descent-ascent algorithm in the continuous time, then the dynamics would follow

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{x}K(x,y)=-Qx-Py,

(2.2a)

\displaystyle\dot{y}(t)

\displaystyle=\eta\nabla_{y}K(x,y)=-\eta Ry+\eta P^{\top}x.

(2.2b)

We may introduce a rescaled function
z(t)=\sqrt{\eta}x(t)
, so that the equations can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\dot{z}(t)

\displaystyle=-Qz-\sqrt{\eta}Py,

(2.3a)

\displaystyle\dot{y}(t)

\displaystyle=-\eta Ry+\sqrt{\eta}P^{\top}z.

(2.3b)

Taking
\phi(t)=[z(t),y(t)]^{\top}
, we have that

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}(t)=-\begin{bmatrix}Q&\sqrt{\eta}P\\
-\sqrt{\eta}P^{\top}&\eta R\end{bmatrix}\phi(t)=-D\phi(t)-\sqrt{\eta}L\phi(t),

(2.4)

with the symmetric matrix
D=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}Q&0\\
0&\eta R\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
(while strictly speaking we shall use
D_{\eta}
, we suppress the subscript to keep notation simpler) and the skew-symmetric matrix
L=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&P\\
-P^{\top}&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
.
It is clear from linear ODE theory that the flow converges locally to a local saddle point
(x^{*},y^{*})
if all eigenvalues of the matrix
D+\sqrt{\eta}L
have strictly positive real parts:

\mu_{\eta}:=\min_{\lambda\in\text{Sp}(D+\sqrt{\eta}L)}\text{Re}(\lambda)>0,

(2.5)

where Sp
(\cdot)
denotes the spectrum of a matrix. However, it is not obvious how the condition
\mu_{\eta}>0
is affected by the two-timescale ratio
\eta
. While it is possible to conduct detailed spectral analysis as in
[
53
]
, we choose to investigate the convergence rate dependence on
\eta
from the hypocoercivity perspective
[
51
,
21
]
by constructing a Lyapunov function based on the interplay of symmetric and skew-symmetric matrices. Compared to spectral analysis, the analysis based on hypocoercivity is relatively simpler.

With the rescaled time
s=\sqrt{\eta}t
, we are able to obtain the following convergence result under appropriate assumptions (see Theorem
3.1
for rigorous statement), with some constants
C,\Lambda>0
,

\|\phi(s)\|^{2}\leq C\exp\Big(-\Lambda\min\Big\{\sqrt{\eta},\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}\Big\}s\Big)\|\phi_{0}\|^{2}.

In particular, the convergence result suggests that optimal choice of
\eta
is of order
1
, so that the timescales of dynamics of two components are comparable, rather than using
\eta\ll 1
or
\eta\gg 1
as in the quasi-static regime.

Returning to original variables of dynamics (
2.2
), we get

\eta\lVert x(t)\rVert^{2}+\lVert y(t)\rVert^{2}\leq Ce^{-\Lambda\min\{1,\eta\}t}\bigl(\eta\lVert x(0)\rVert^{2}+\lVert y(0)\rVert^{2}\bigr).

(2.6)

While this seems to suggest choosing
\eta\gg 1
to accelerate convergence, it is somewhat deceiving since this is only for the continuous-time dynamics, while usual discretization of (
2.2
) would require step size
O(1/\eta)
when
\eta\gg 1
due to the stiffness. The convergence of a preconditioned saddle point algorithm suggested by our analysis would be discussed in more details, see Section
4
.

2.2
Infinite dimensional continuous games

The infinite-dimensional entropy-regularized min-max problem is of the form

\displaystyle\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}\max_{q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})}E_{\beta}(p,q):=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}K(x,y)p(x)q(y)~dxdy+\beta^{-1}H(p)-\beta^{-1}H(q)

(2.7)

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad H(p):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\log\Big(\frac{dp}{dx}\Big)~dp,\quad H(q):=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\log\Big(\frac{dq}{dy}\Big)~dq,

which is obtained from (
1.2
) by adding entropy regularization terms, where

\beta>0
is a regularization parameter. Thanks to the entropy
regularization, the objective energy functional
E_{\beta}(p,q)
is
strongly convex in
p
and strongly concave in
q
, thus by von
Neumann’s minimax theorem

\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}\max_{q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})}E_{\beta}(p,q)=\max_{q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})}\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}E_{\beta}(p,q).

(2.8)

Theorem 4 in
[
22
]
establishes that if in addition

K
is continuous on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, a unique Nash
equilibrium
(p^{*},q^{*})
in the sense that

E_{\beta}(p^{*},q)\leq E_{\beta}(p^{*},q^{*})\leq E_{\beta}(p,q^{*}),\quad\text{for all}~p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}),~q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})

exists and is the unique fixed point of

\displaystyle p(x)=\frac{1}{Z_{p}}\exp\Big(-\beta\int_{\mathcal{Y}}K(x,y)q(y)~dy\Big),\quad q(y)=\frac{1}{Z_{q}}\exp\Big(\beta\int_{\mathcal{X}}K(x,y)p(x)~dx\Big),

(2.9)

where
Z_{p},Z_{q}
are normalizing constants to make
p,q
probability distributions.

We focus on analyzing the dynamics of the entropy-regularized two-timescale gradient descent-ascent flow under the Wasserstein metric:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p_{t}=\nabla_{x}\cdot\big(p_{t}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\nabla_{x}K(x,y)q_{t}(y)~dy\big)+\beta^{-1}\Delta_{x}p_{t}

(2.10a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}q_{t}=\eta\Big(-\nabla_{y}\cdot\Big(q_{t}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\nabla_{y}K(x,y)p_{t}(x)~dx\Big)+\beta^{-1}\Delta_{y}q_{t}\Big),

(2.10b)

which can be viewed as the mean-field limit (
N\to\infty
) of the two-timescale Langevin descent-ascent gradient flow

\displaystyle dX_{t}^{i}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla_{x}K(X_{t}^{i},Y_{t}^{j})dt+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}dW_{t}^{i}

(2.11a)

\displaystyle dY_{t}^{i}=\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla_{y}K(X_{t}^{j},Y_{t}^{i})dt+\sqrt{2\eta\beta^{-1}}dB_{t}^{i}

(2.11b)

via
p_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{t}^{i}},~q_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{Y_{t}^{i}}
, by taking the number of strategies
N
to infinity.

In the above dynamics, the parameter
\eta
can be viewed as a ratio between the rate of the two dynamics. We will adopt a mixed synchronous-reflection coupling approach, inspired by
[
24
]
, to establish a Wasserstein-1 convergence result. Given that the objective function is strongly convex-concave outside a local ball with radius
R
, we have

W_{1}((p_{t},q_{t}),(p^{*},q^{*}))\leq Ce^{-c\min\{1,\eta\}t}W_{1}((p_{0},q_{0}),(p^{*},q^{*})).

We defer the details of the statement and required assumptions to Theorem
5.1
. Notably, we obtain qualitative the same decay rate as the finite dimensional GDA dynamics (
2.6
).

This result, compared to
[
15
,
9
]
, accommodates the scenario where
K
is locally nonconvex-nonconcave, and extends the range of allowed
\eta
value compared with
[
41
]
which only addressed near quasi-static regime.

3
Convergence of quadratic zero-sum games

In this section, we focus on the two-timescale GDA dynamics (
2.2
) for solving the quadratic zero-sum game (
2.1
). In particular, without using any spectral analysis tools, we want to investigate how the symmetric matrix
D
together with the skew-symmetric matrix
L
impact the convergence behavior as
\eta\ll 1
or
\eta\gg 1
. By rescaling the equation (
2.4
) with time
s=\sqrt{\eta}t
, we have

\frac{d}{ds}\phi=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}D\phi-L\phi.

(3.1)

The observation that

D=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}Q&0\\
0&\eta R\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\sim\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}Q&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
when
\eta\ll 1
and
\frac{1}{\eta}D=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}Q/\eta&0\\
0&R\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\sim\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
0&R\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
when
\eta\gg 1
mimics the degenerate diffusion in the kinetic equation, which motivates us to take the hypocoercivity approach as in
[
21
]
for convergence analysis.

We start with a modified norm, which can be viewed as a Lyapunov function

H(\phi)=\frac{1}{2}\|\phi\|^{2}-\epsilon\langle M\phi,\phi\rangle

(3.2)

with small
\epsilon\in(0,1)
to be chosen, and the matrix
M
is chosen to be

M:=-(I+(L\Pi)^{\top}L\Pi)^{-1}(L\Pi)^{\top}.

(3.3)

Here
I\equiv I_{n+m}
is an identity matrix, and
\Pi
is a projection matrix onto kernel of a selected symmetric semi-definite matrix and satisfies the algebraic assumption

Assumption 1
.

The projection operator
\Pi
satisfies

\Pi L\Pi=0.

(3.4)

We specify what projection matrix
\Pi
we will use depending on the cases. We write
\Pi_{Q}
as projection onto the kernel of
Q
, so it functions as, for any
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+m}
,

\begin{bmatrix}Q&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\Pi_{Q}v=0.

(3.5)

On the other hand, we write
\Pi_{R}
to be projection onto the kernel of
R
, so that for any
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+m}
,

\begin{bmatrix}Q&0\\
0&R\end{bmatrix}\Pi_{R}v=0.

(3.6)

Based on the choice of
\Pi
, we have different lower bound assumptions for the symmetric matrix.

Assumption 2

(microscopic coercivity)
.

Given
S=\Bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}Q&0\\
0&R\end{smallmatrix}\Bigr]
, let
\lambda_{Q}
and
\lambda_{R}
be the least eigenvalues of
Q
and
R
respectively. If
\Pi=\Pi_{Q}
, we have

\langle S\phi,\phi\rangle\geq\lambda_{Q}\|(I-\Pi_{Q})\phi\|^{2},

(3.7)

and if
\Pi=\Pi_{R}
, we have

\langle S\phi,\phi\rangle\geq\lambda_{R}\|(I-\Pi_{R})\phi\|^{2}.

(3.8)

Regarding the skew-symmetric matrix, we assume a universal lower bound for either
\Pi=\Pi_{Q}
or
\Pi=\Pi_{R}
.

Assumption 3

(macroscopic coercivity)
.

Let
\lambda_{L}
be the minimum eigenvalue of
L^{\top}L=\Bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}PP^{\top}&0\\
0&P^{\top}P\end{smallmatrix}\Bigr]
, then

\|L\Pi\phi\|^{2}\geq\lambda_{L}\|\Pi\phi\|^{2}.

(3.9)

Lemma 3.1
.

Given the operator
M
in (
3.3
) and Assumption
1
, we have the bounds

\|M\phi\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|(I-\Pi)\phi\|,\quad\|LM\phi\|\leq\|(I-\Pi)\phi\|.

(3.10)

Proof.

For any
\phi
, we denote
f:=M\phi
. Then we get

(L\Pi)^{\top}\phi=-(I+(L\Pi)^{\top}L\Pi)f,

(3.11)

which implies that

f=\Pi L\phi-\Pi L^{\top}L\Pi f.

(3.12)

Because both sides project onto the same vector space, we have
\Pi M=M
.

Now taking the inner product of (
3.11
) with
f
, we obtain that

\|f\|^{2}+\langle L\Pi f,L\Pi f\rangle=\langle\phi,L\Pi f\rangle=-\langle(I-\Pi)\phi,L\Pi f\rangle,

(3.13)

as
\langle\Pi\phi,L\Pi f\rangle=0
due to Assumption
1
. We apply the Young’s inequality to the right side of (
3.13
) to obtain the first bound,

\|f\|^{2}+\|L\Pi f\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\|(I-\Pi)\phi\|^{2}+\|L\Pi f\|^{2}.

(3.14)

Moreover, to get the second bound in the result, we apply the Young’s inequality to the right side of (
3.13
) again (with different constants), and drop
\|f\|^{2}
on the left side, to arrive at

\|L\Pi f\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|(I-\Pi)\phi\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|L\Pi f\|^{2}.

(3.15)

Combining with
L\Pi f=L\Pi M\phi=LM\phi
, we then obtain the claim of the lemma.
∎

Furthermore, we also need some upper bound assumptions.

Assumption 4
.

Given
S=\Bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}Q&0\\
0&R\end{smallmatrix}\Bigr]
, if
\Pi=\Pi_{Q}
, there exists
\Lambda_{Q}>0
such that

\langle MS\phi,\phi\rangle\leq\Lambda_{Q}\|(I-\Pi_{Q})\phi\|\|\phi\|

(3.16)

and if
\Pi=\Pi_{R}
, then there exists
\Lambda_{R}>0
such that

\langle MS\phi,\phi\rangle\leq\Lambda_{R}\|(I-\Pi_{R})\phi\|\|\phi\|.

(3.17)

Moreover, we can find a constant
C_{M}>0
such that

\langle ML(I-\Pi)\phi,\phi\rangle\leq C_{M}\|(I-\Pi)\phi\|\|\phi\|.

(3.18)

By Lemma
3
, we may choose small
\epsilon\in(0,1)
to have norm equivalence between
H(\phi)
and
\|\phi\|^{2}
:

\frac{1-\epsilon}{2}\|\phi\|^{2}\leq H(\phi)\leq\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}\|\phi\|^{2}.

(3.19)

With all assumptions stated, we are ready to state and prove the main result in this section.

Theorem 3.1
.

With Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
, and
4
, we can find explicit constants
\Lambda,C>0
, depending on
\epsilon
, to get that for all
s\geq 0
,

\|\phi(s)\|^{2}\leq C\exp\Big(-\Lambda\min\Big\{\sqrt{\eta},\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}\Big\}s\Big)\|\phi_{0}\|^{2}.

(3.20)

Proof.

Case 1:
\eta\ll 1
.
In this case, we consider

\tilde{D}:=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}Q&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]

as an approximation to the diffusion matrix
D=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}Q&0\\
0&\eta R\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
,
We can find a constant
C_{R}>0
such that

\|\tilde{D}-D\|_{F}\leq C_{R}\eta.

(3.21)

With
\tilde{D}
in mind, we choose
\Pi=\Pi_{Q}
satisfying Assumption
1
that projects onto kernel of
\tilde{D}
, and write
H(\phi)=H_{Q}(\phi)
accordingly. Taking the derivative of
H(\phi)
in time
s
, by the dynamics (
3.1
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}H_{Q}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\langle\phi,\phi_{s}\rangle-\epsilon\langle M\phi_{s},\phi\rangle-\epsilon\langle M\phi,\phi_{s}\rangle

(3.22)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}\langle\phi,D\phi\rangle+\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\eta}}\langle MD\phi,\phi\rangle+\epsilon\langle ML\phi,\phi\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\eta}}\langle M\phi,D\phi\rangle+\epsilon\langle M\phi,L\phi\rangle.

For
\langle\phi,D\phi\rangle
, we utilize Assumption
2
and (
3.21
) to obtain that

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}\langle D\phi,\phi\rangle

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}\langle\tilde{D}\phi,\phi\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}\big|\langle(D-\tilde{D})\phi,\phi\rangle\big|

(3.23)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\lambda_{Q}}{\sqrt{\eta}}\|(I-\Pi_{Q})\phi\|^{2}+C_{R}\sqrt{\eta}\|\phi\|^{2}.

For
\langle MD\phi,\phi\rangle
, by the property that
\Pi_{Q}M=M
and Assumption
1
, we observe that

\langle M\tilde{D}\phi,\phi\rangle=\langle M\phi,\tilde{D}\phi\rangle=\langle\Pi_{Q}M\phi,\tilde{D}\phi\rangle=\langle\tilde{D}\Pi_{Q}M\phi,\phi\rangle=0.

(3.24)

Thus

\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\eta}}\langle MD\phi,\phi\rangle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\eta}}\big|\langle M(D-\tilde{D})\phi,\phi\rangle\big|\leq\epsilon C_{R}\sqrt{\eta}\|\phi\|^{2}.

(3.25)

As for
\langle ML\phi,\phi\rangle
, we may split

\displaystyle\langle ML\phi,\phi\rangle=\langle ML\Pi\phi,\phi\rangle+\langle ML(I-\Pi)\phi,\phi\rangle,

(3.26)

and treat the first part by using Assumption
3
:

\langle ML\Pi\phi,\phi\rangle=-\langle(I+(L\Pi)^{\top}L\Pi)^{-1}(L\Pi)^{\top}(L\Pi)\phi,\phi\rangle\leq-\frac{\lambda_{L}}{1+\lambda_{L}}\|\Pi\phi\|^{2}=-\frac{\lambda_{L}}{1+\lambda_{L}}\|\Pi_{Q}\phi\|^{2}.

(3.27)

As for the second part, we have the bound by Assumption
4
,

\langle ML(I-\Pi_{Q})\phi,\phi\rangle\leq C_{M}\|(I-\Pi_{Q})\phi\|\|\phi\|.

(3.28)

For
\langle M\phi,D\phi\rangle
, we then use bounds in Lemma
3
and (
3.21
) to get that

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\eta}}\langle M\phi,D\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\eta}}\langle M\phi,\tilde{D}\phi\rangle+\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\eta}}\langle M\phi,(D-\tilde{D})\phi\rangle

(3.29)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\eta}}\|M\phi\|\Big(\|\tilde{D}\phi\|+\|(D-\tilde{D})\phi\|\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon\Lambda_{Q}}{2\sqrt{\eta}}\|(I-\Pi_{Q})\phi\|\|\phi\|+\frac{\epsilon C_{R}\sqrt{\eta}}{2}\|(I-\Pi_{Q})\phi\|\|\phi\|.

Lastly, for
\langle M\phi,L\phi\rangle
, by Lemma
3
, we obtain that

\langle M\phi,L\phi\rangle=-\langle LM\phi,\phi\rangle\leq\|(I-\Pi_{Q})\phi\|\|\phi\|.

(3.30)

Combining all bounds above together, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}H_{Q}(\phi)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\lambda_{Q}}{\sqrt{\eta}}\|(I-\Pi_{Q})\phi\|^{2}-\frac{\epsilon\lambda_{L}}{1+\lambda_{L}}\|\Pi_{Q}\phi\|^{2}

(3.31)

\displaystyle+\epsilon\Big(\frac{\Lambda_{Q}}{2\sqrt{\eta}}+\frac{C_{R}\sqrt{\eta}}{2}+1+C_{M}\Big)\|(I-\Pi_{Q})\phi\|\|\phi\|+(1+\epsilon)C_{R}\sqrt{\eta}\|\phi\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\begin{bmatrix}\|(I-\Pi_{Q})\phi\|\\
\|\Pi_{Q}\phi\|\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}S_{++}&S_{+-}/2\\
S_{+-}/2&S_{--}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\|(I-\Pi_{Q})\phi\|\\
\|\Pi_{Q}\phi\|\end{bmatrix}+(1+\epsilon)C_{R}\sqrt{\eta}\|\phi\|^{2},

with

S_{++}=\frac{\lambda_{Q}}{\sqrt{\eta}},\quad S_{--}=\frac{\epsilon\lambda_{L}}{1+\lambda_{L}},\quad S_{+-}=-\epsilon\Big(\frac{\Lambda_{Q}}{2\sqrt{\eta}}+\frac{C_{R}\sqrt{\eta}}{2}+1+C_{M}\Big)

(3.32)

and the smallest eigenvalue of the matrix
\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}S_{++}&S_{+-}/2\\
S_{+-}/2&S_{--}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
is

\lambda_{\min}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(S_{++}+S_{--}-\sqrt{(S_{++}-S_{--})^{2}+(S_{+-})^{2}}\Big).

(3.33)

Note that we consider the regime where
\eta\ll 1
. In order to make
\lambda_{\min}>0
, we take
\epsilon\sim\sqrt{\eta}
so that for sufficiently small
\eta
, we can find a constant
c>0
to get

\frac{d}{ds}H_{Q}(\phi)\leq-c\sqrt{\eta}\|\phi\|^{2}\leq-\frac{2c\sqrt{\eta}}{1+\epsilon}H_{Q}(\phi)

(3.34)

by the norm equivalence (
3.19
), which also implies that for
\eta\ll 1
,

H_{Q}(\phi(s))\leq\exp\Big(\frac{-2c\sqrt{\eta}s}{1+\epsilon}\Big)H_{Q}(\phi_{0})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\|\phi(s)\|^{2}\leq\frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}\exp\Big(\frac{-2c\sqrt{\eta}s}{1+\epsilon}\Big)\|\phi_{0}\|^{2}.

(3.35)

Case 2:
\eta\gg 1
.

We first rewrite the dynamics (
3.1
) as

\phi_{s}=-\sqrt{\eta}B\phi-L\phi

(3.36)

with the re-scaled diffusion matrix

B=\frac{1}{\eta}D=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}Q/\eta&0\\
0&R\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
. We take

\tilde{B}:=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
0&R\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
to compare with
B
, and one can find a constant
C_{Q}>0
such that

\|\tilde{B}-B\|_{F}\leq C_{Q}\eta^{-1}.

(3.37)

With
\tilde{B}
in mind, we choose
\Pi=\Pi_{R}
satisfying Assumption
1
that projects onto kernel of
\tilde{B}
, and write
H(\phi)=H_{R}(\phi)
accordingly. Taking derivative of
H(\phi)
in time
s
, we then get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}H_{R}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\langle\phi,\phi_{s}\rangle-\epsilon\langle M\phi_{s},\phi\rangle-\epsilon\langle M\phi,\phi_{s}\rangle

(3.38)

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{\eta}\langle\phi,B\phi\rangle+\epsilon\sqrt{\eta}\langle MB\phi,\phi\rangle+\epsilon\langle ML\phi,\phi\rangle

\displaystyle+\epsilon\sqrt{\eta}\langle M\phi,B\phi\rangle+\epsilon\langle M\phi,L\phi\rangle.

The estimates of each term are similar to what we have done in Case 1. Omitting those details, we will eventually get the bound that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}H_{R}(\phi)

\displaystyle\leq-\lambda_{R}\sqrt{\eta}\|(I-\Pi_{R})\phi\|^{2}-\frac{\epsilon\lambda_{L}}{1+\lambda_{L}}\|\Pi_{R}\phi\|^{2}

(3.39)

\displaystyle+\epsilon\Big(\frac{\Lambda_{R}\sqrt{\eta}}{2}+\frac{C_{R}}{2\sqrt{\eta}}+1+C_{M}\Big)\|(I-\Pi_{R})\phi\|\|\phi\|+\frac{(1+\epsilon)C_{Q}}{\sqrt{\eta}}\|\phi\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\begin{bmatrix}\|(I-\Pi_{Q})\phi\|\\
\|\Pi_{Q}\phi\|\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}S_{++}&S_{+-}/2\\
S_{+-}/2&S_{--}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\|(I-\Pi_{Q})\phi\|\\
\|\Pi_{Q}\phi\|\end{bmatrix}+\frac{(1+\epsilon)C_{Q}}{\sqrt{\eta}}\|\phi\|^{2},

with

S_{++}=\lambda_{R}\sqrt{\eta},\quad S_{--}=\frac{\epsilon\lambda_{L}}{1+\lambda_{L}},\quad S_{+-}=-\epsilon\Big(\frac{\Lambda_{R}\sqrt{\eta}}{2}+\frac{C_{R}}{2\sqrt{\eta}}+1+C_{M}\Big)

(3.40)

and the smallest eigenvalue of this matrix is

\lambda_{\min}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(S_{++}+S_{--}-\sqrt{(S_{++}-S_{--})^{2}+(S_{+-})^{2}}\Big).

(3.41)

Since we consider the regime where
\eta\gg 1
, in order to make
\lambda_{\min}>0
we take
\epsilon\sim 1/\sqrt{\eta}
so that there exists a constant
c^{\prime}>0
,

\frac{d}{ds}H_{R}(\phi)\leq-\frac{c^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\eta}}\|\phi\|^{2}\leq-\frac{2c^{\prime}}{(1+\epsilon)\sqrt{\eta}}H_{R}(\phi).

(3.42)

Accompanied with the norm equivalence (
3.19
), we conclude that for
\eta\gg 1
,

H_{R}(\phi(s))\leq\exp\Big(\frac{-2c^{\prime}s}{(1+\epsilon)\sqrt{\eta}}\Big)H_{R}(\phi_{0})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\|\phi(s)\|^{2}\leq\frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}\exp\Big(\frac{-2c^{\prime}s}{(1+\epsilon)\sqrt{\eta}}\Big)\|\phi_{0}\|^{2}.

(3.43)

∎

, we numerically verify Theorem
3.1
by demonstrating that the optimal choice of

\eta
for minimizing the least eigenvalue is of order
1
, away from the quasi-static regimes.

4
Preconditioned saddle point problem

Theorem
3.1
establishes the convergence behavior of continuous-time dynamics (
2.4
), while in practice, we would need a numerical discretization to (
2.4
) of the form

\phi_{k+1}-\phi_{k}=-\rho\Big(D+\sqrt{\eta}L\Big)\phi_{k},

(4.1)

where
\rho
is the step size. The condition number of the matrix
D+\sqrt{\eta}L
can significantly limit the choice of step size
\rho>0
for convergence, especially in the case where
\eta\gg 1
or
\eta\ll 1
. The condition number, defined as the ratio of the maximal to minimal singular values of the matrix, is at least of the order

\kappa\Big(D+\sqrt{\eta}L\Big)=\kappa\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}D+L\Big)\geq O(\max\{\eta,1/\eta\}).

(4.2)

This is because
\sigma_{\max}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}D+L\Big)=\Big\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}D+L\Big\|_{2}\geq\max\{\xi^{\top}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}D+L\Big)\xi,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m+n}\}=O(\max\{\sqrt{\eta},1/\sqrt{\eta}\})
, and
\sigma_{\min}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}D+L\Big)=O(\min\{\sqrt{\eta},1/\sqrt{\eta}\})
, as inferred from Theorem
3.1
. Thus, In practice, it is crucial to include a preconditioner for the matrix
D+\sqrt{\eta}L
to mitigate the impact of extreme values of
\eta
. Specifically, we aim to find a preconditioner
\mathcal{P}
such that the numerical updates, with
\xi
being the transformed vector
\phi
, follow as

\xi_{k+1}-\xi_{k}=-\rho\mathcal{P}\Big(D+\sqrt{\eta}L\Big)\xi_{k},

(4.3)

so that the choice of
\rho
does not depend on
\eta
.

Suppose
Q
is symmetric positive definite, ensuring that it is invertible. We then consider the following equation via block Gaussian elimination:

\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
\sqrt{\eta}P^{\top}Q^{-1}&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}Q&\sqrt{\eta}P\\
-\sqrt{\eta}P^{\top}&\eta R\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&-\sqrt{\eta}Q^{-1}P\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}Q&0\\
0&\eta(R+P^{\top}Q^{-1}P)\end{bmatrix}.

(4.4)

The condition number on the right-hand side will not depend on
\eta
if we further multiply it by
S=\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
0&I/\eta\end{bmatrix}
.
We define
M=\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
\sqrt{\eta}P^{\top}Q^{-1}&I\end{bmatrix},N=\begin{bmatrix}I&-\sqrt{\eta}Q^{-1}P\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}
, and introduce
\xi_{k}=N^{-1}\phi_{k}
. Thus the numerical update (
4.1
) with a left preconditioner
SM
becomes

N\xi_{k+1}-N\xi_{k}=-\rho SM(D+\sqrt{\eta}L)N\xi_{k}=-\rho\begin{bmatrix}Q&0\\
0&R+P^{\top}Q^{-1}P\end{bmatrix}\xi_{k},\quad

(4.5)

Therefore, we take
\mathcal{P}=N^{-1}SM
, so that (
4.3
) is

\xi_{k+1}-\xi_{k}=-\rho N^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}Q&0\\
0&R+P^{\top}Q^{-1}P\end{bmatrix}\xi_{k},\quad N^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}I&\sqrt{\eta}Q^{-1}P\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}.

(4.6)

From the above, we conclude that by taking the preconditioner
\mathcal{P}=N^{-1}SM
, the condition number

\kappa\Big(N^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}Q&0\\
0&R+P^{\top}Q^{-1}P\end{bmatrix}\Big)\leq\kappa\Big(\begin{bmatrix}Q&0\\
0&R+P^{\top}Q^{-1}P\end{bmatrix}\Big)

(4.7)

does not depend on
\eta
.

Before moving toward convergence results, we note that
\phi=[\sqrt{\eta}x,y]^{\top}
, and
\xi=N^{-1}\phi=[\sqrt{\eta}(x+Q^{-1}Py),y]^{\top}
. Throughout the remaining content, we denote
T=\begin{bmatrix}Q&0\\
0&R+P^{\top}Q^{-1}P\end{bmatrix}
.

Claim 4.1
.

All eigenvalues of
N^{-1}T
are real and nonnegative.

Proof.

Note that
N^{-1}T=\begin{bmatrix}Q&\sqrt{\eta}Q^{-1}P(R+P^{\top}Q^{-1}P)\\
0&R+P^{\top}Q^{-1}P\end{bmatrix}
. We consider its eigenvalue
\lambda
with associated eigenvector
[u;v]^{\top}
, which gives equations

\displaystyle Qu+\sqrt{\eta}Q^{-1}P(R+P^{\top}Q^{-1}P)v

\displaystyle=\lambda u,

(4.8)

\displaystyle(R+P^{\top}Q^{-1}P)v

\displaystyle=\lambda v.

(4.9)

The second equation, since
R+P^{\top}Q^{-1}P
is symmetric, implies that if
v\neq 0
, then
\lambda
must be real and nonnegative. Otherwise, if
\lambda
is complex, then
v=0
. The second case cannot be true because if
v=0
, we have
Qu=\lambda u
, which implies either
u=0
, or
\lambda
is real and nonnegative. Therefore, only the first case exists for nontrivial eigenvectors.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

By choosing
\rho=\frac{\lambda_{\min}(N^{-1}T+(N^{-1}T)^{\top})}{2\sigma_{\max}^{2}(N^{-1}T)}
, the iterates of (
4.3
) shrink as

\|\xi_{k+1}\|\leq\sqrt{1-\frac{\lambda_{\min}^{2}(N^{-1}T+(N^{-1}T)^{\top})}{4\sigma_{\max}^{2}(N^{-1}T)}}\|\xi_{k}\|.

(4.10)

Proof.

From (
4.3
), we have

\displaystyle\|\xi_{k+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(I-\rho\mathcal{P}(D+\sqrt{\eta}L))\xi_{k}\|^{2}=\|(I-\rho N^{-1}T)\xi_{k}\|^{2}

(4.11)

\displaystyle=\|\xi_{k}\|^{2}-\rho\langle\xi_{k},N^{-1}T\xi_{k}\rangle-\rho\langle N^{-1}T\xi_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle+\rho^{2}\|N^{-1}T\xi_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\rho^{2}\sigma_{\max}^{2}(N^{-1}T))\|\xi_{k}\|^{2}-\rho\lambda_{\min}(N^{-1}T+(N^{-1}T)^{\top})\|\xi_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{\lambda_{\min}^{2}(N^{-1}T+(N^{-1}T)^{\top})}{4\sigma_{\max}^{2}(N^{-1}T)}\Big)\|\xi_{k}\|^{2},

and the last line is achieved by taking
\rho=\frac{\lambda_{\min}(N^{-1}T+(N^{-1}T)^{\top})}{2\sigma_{\max}^{2}(N^{-1}T)}
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

We observe that

\frac{\lambda_{\min}(N^{-1}T+(N^{-1}T)^{\top})}{2\sigma_{\max}(N^{-1}T)}\leq\frac{\sigma_{\min}(N^{-1}T)}{\sigma_{\max}(N^{-1}T)}=\frac{1}{\kappa(N^{-1}T)},

(4.12)

Thus, for the iteration
\|\xi_{k+1}\|\leq c\|\xi_{k}\|
, the convergence rate
c=\sqrt{1-\frac{\lambda_{\min}^{2}(N^{-1}T+(N^{-1}T)^{\top})}{4\sigma_{\max}^{2}(N^{-1}T)}}\geq\sqrt{1-1/\kappa^{2}(N^{-1}T)}
. Therefore, with the preconditioner
\mathcal{P}
, the condition number of the resulting matrix
N^{-1}T
does not depend on the learning rate ratio
\eta
, and allows for a faster convergence rate when
\eta
is extreme.

Lastly, for completeness, we examine the convergence rate of the preconditioned continuous dynamics. With the properly chosen preconditioner
\mathcal{P}=N^{-1}SM
, the convergence rate of the dynamics
\dot{\xi}(t)=-N^{-1}T\xi(t)
is also improved for extreme
\eta
, compared with Theorem
3.1
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose that both
Q
and
R
are symmetric positive definite with least eigenvalues of order
O(1)
, then for all choices of
\eta
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that
\lambda_{\min}(N^{-1}T)\geq C\max\{1,\sqrt{\eta}\}
. Therefore, for the continuous-time equation
\dot{\xi}(t)=-N^{-1}T\xi(t)
,

\|\xi(t)\|\leq\|\xi_{0}\|e^{-C\max\{1,\sqrt{\eta}\}t}.

Proof.

All we need to show is that when
\eta
is extreme, the least eigenvalue of
N^{-1}T
remains at least of order
O(1)
.

We first consider the case
\eta\ll 1
. Note that

N^{-1}T=\begin{bmatrix}Q&\sqrt{\eta}Q^{-1}P(R+P^{\top}Q^{-1}P)\\
0&R+P^{\top}Q^{-1}P\end{bmatrix}=T+E,\quad E=\begin{bmatrix}0&\sqrt{\eta}Q^{-1}P(R+P^{\top}Q^{-1}P)\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},

and by Weyl’s inequality,
|\sigma_{\min}(N^{-1}T)-\sigma_{\min}(T)|\leq|\sigma_{\max}(E)|=O(\sqrt{\eta})
, so we have
\sigma_{\min}(N^{-1}T)\geq O(1)
for sufficiently small
\eta
.

In the case
\eta\gg 1
,
\sigma_{\max}(N^{-1}T)=\max\{\|N^{-1}T\xi\|_{2}:\|\xi\|=1\}=O(\sqrt{\eta})
. The
O(1)
order condition number of
N^{-1}T
implies that
\sigma_{\min}(N^{-1}T)=O(\sqrt{\eta})
.

By the relation
\lambda_{\min}(N^{-1}T)\geq\sigma_{\min}(N^{-1}T)
, we conclude that there exists a constant
C>0
such that
\lambda_{\min}(N^{-1}T)\geq C\max\{1,\sqrt{\eta}\}
.
∎

5
Convergence of the continuous game

In this section, we analyze the convergence of the two-timescale gradient descent-ascent flow under the Wasserstein metric (
2.10
) for finite
\eta
. We start with assumptions on
K
. The following assumes that the gradients of
K
are Lipschitz continuous, and
K
is strongly convex-concave outside a ball (when two points are more than
R>0
apart). Note that
K
is allowed to be locally nonconvex-nonconcave.

Assumption 5
.

There exist
\kappa_{x},\kappa_{y},m_{x},m_{y}>0
such that for all
x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\mathcal{Y}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{x}K(x_{1},y)-\nabla_{x}K(x_{2},y),x_{1}-x_{2}\rangle\geq\kappa_{x}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2},~\text{for all}~\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|\geq R,y\in\mathcal{Y},

(5.1)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{y}K(x,y_{1})-\nabla_{y}K(x,y_{2}),y_{1}-y_{2}\rangle\leq-\kappa_{y}\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|^{2},~\text{for all}~x\in\mathcal{X},\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|\geq R,

and

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{x}K(x_{1},y)-\nabla_{x}K(x_{2},y),x_{1}-x_{2}\rangle\geq-m_{x}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2},~\text{for all}~\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|<R,y\in\mathcal{Y},

(5.2)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{y}K(x,y_{1})-\nabla_{y}K(x,y_{2}),y_{1}-y_{2}\rangle\leq m_{y}\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|^{2},~\text{for all}~x\in\mathcal{X},\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|<R,

Moreover, there exist positive constants
L_{X},L_{Y},l_{X},l_{Y}
such that

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{x}K(x_{1},y_{1})-\nabla_{x}K(x_{2},y_{2})\|\leq l_{X}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|+L_{Y}\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{y}K(x_{1},y_{1})-\nabla_{y}K(x_{2},y_{2})\|\leq L_{X}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|+l_{Y}\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|,

for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X},y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathcal{Y}
.

We look at the two-timescale gradient descent-ascent Langevin dynamics, by taking the realizations
X_{t}
from the marginal distribution
p_{t}=\text{Law}(X_{t})
and
Y_{t}\sim q_{t}=\text{Law}(Y_{t})
, and they follow

\displaystyle dX_{t}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\nabla_{x}K(X_{t},y)q_{t}(y)dy~dt+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}dW_{t},

(5.4a)

\displaystyle dY_{t}

\displaystyle=\eta\int_{\mathcal{X}}\nabla_{y}K(x,Y_{t})p_{t}(x)dx~dt+\sqrt{2\eta\beta^{-1}}dB_{t},

(5.4b)

where
W,B
are independent Brownian motions. The motivation for us to look into the coupled overdamped Langevin equations is to take the reflection-synchronous coupling approach introduced by
[
24
]
. Let us consider the coupling for diffusion processes
(X_{t},Y_{t})
and
(\tilde{X}_{t},\tilde{Y}_{t})
, and we use the notation
Z_{t}=X_{t}-\tilde{X}_{t},Q_{t}=Y_{t}-\tilde{Y}_{t}
.

Let us fix
\delta>0
and suppose that we have Lipschitz continuous functions
\text{rc}_{i}(Z_{t},Q_{t})
and
\text{sc}_{i}(Z_{t},Q_{t}),i=1,2
satisfy

\displaystyle\text{rc}_{i}(Z_{t},Q_{t})^{2}+\text{sc}_{i}(Z_{t},Q_{t})^{2}=1,

(5.5)

\displaystyle\text{rc}_{1}(Z_{t},Q_{t})=1~\text{if}~\|Z_{t}\|\geq\delta,\quad\text{rc}_{1}(Z_{t},Q_{t})=0~\text{if}~\|Z_{t}\|\leq\delta/2,

\displaystyle\text{rc}_{2}(Z_{t},Q_{t})=1~\text{if}~\|Q_{t}\|\geq\delta,\quad\text{rc}_{2}(Z_{t},Q_{t})=0~\text{if}~\|Q_{t}\|\leq\delta/2.

We will often write
\text{rc}\equiv\text{rc}(z,q),\text{sc}\equiv\text{sc}(z,q)
for notation simplicity. Then over
(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{2}
, a coupling of two solutions to (
5.4
) is given by

\displaystyle dX_{t}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\nabla_{x}K(X_{t},y)q_{t}(y)dy~dt+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\text{rc}_{1}dW_{t}^{\text{rc}}+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\text{sc}_{1}dW_{t}^{\text{sc}},

(5.6)

\displaystyle dY_{t}

\displaystyle=\eta\int_{\mathcal{X}}\nabla_{y}K(x,Y_{t})p_{t}(x)dx~dt+\sqrt{2\eta\beta^{-1}}\text{rc}_{2}dB_{t}^{\text{rc}}+\sqrt{2\eta\beta^{-1}}\text{sc}_{2}dB_{t}^{\text{sc}},

\displaystyle d\tilde{X}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\nabla_{x}K(\tilde{X}_{t},\tilde{y})\tilde{q}_{t}(\tilde{y})d\tilde{y}~dt+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\text{rc}_{1}(I-2e_{t}^{1}e_{t}^{1,\top})dW_{t}^{\text{rc}}+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\text{sc}_{1}dW_{t}^{\text{sc}},

\displaystyle d\tilde{Y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\eta\int_{\mathcal{X}}\nabla_{y}K(\tilde{x},\tilde{Y}_{t})\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{x})d\tilde{x}~dt+\sqrt{2\eta\beta^{-1}}\text{rc}_{2}(I-2e_{t}^{2}e_{t}^{2,\top})dB_{t}^{\text{rc}}+\sqrt{2\eta\beta^{-1}}\text{sc}_{2}dB_{t}^{\text{sc}},

with independent Brownian motions
W^{\text{rc}},W^{\text{sc}},B^{\text{rc}},B^{\text{sc}}
, and initial distribution
(X_{0},Y_{0})\sim\mu,(\tilde{X}_{0},\tilde{Y}_{0})\sim\nu
. Here
e_{t}e_{t}^{\top}
is the orthogonal projection onto the unit vector as

e_{t}^{1}=\begin{cases}Z_{t}/\|Z_{t}\|,~\text{if}~Z_{t}\neq 0,\\
u_{1},\quad\quad\quad\text{if}~Z_{t}=0,\end{cases}e_{t}^{2}=\begin{cases}Q_{t}/\|Q_{t}\|,~\text{if}~Q_{t}\neq 0,\\
u_{2},\quad\quad\quad\text{if}~Q_{t}=0,\end{cases}

(5.7)

and
u_{1}\in\mathcal{X},u_{2}\in\mathcal{Y}
are some arbitrary fixed unit vectors.

Here, we emphasize that applying a reflection coupling individually to each process may not work, as the realization and its reflection can move apart after the coupling time for each process due to the interaction between the two processes. To address this, we employ a mixed reflection-synchronous coupling with regularized reflection functions, avoiding reliance on the coupling time in the local regime. Eventually, we will pass the distance constant
\delta
to zero to remove the additional error term arises from this construction, to obtain the main result, Theorem
5.1
.

In order to construct an appropriate metric, we define

\displaystyle\kappa_{x}(r,t)

\displaystyle:=r^{-2}\inf\Bigl\{\int_{\mathcal{Y}}(x-x^{\prime})\cdot\bigl(\nabla_{x}K(x,y)-\nabla_{x}K(x^{\prime},y)\bigr)q_{t}(y)dy~\text{s.t.}~\|x-x^{\prime}\|=r\Bigr\},

(5.8)

\displaystyle\kappa_{y}(r,t)

\displaystyle:=r^{-2}\inf\Bigl\{-\int_{\mathcal{X}}(y-y^{\prime})\cdot\bigl(\nabla_{y}K(x,y)-\nabla_{y}K(x,y^{\prime})\bigr)p_{t}(x)dx~\text{s.t.}~\|y-y^{\prime}\|=r\Bigr\}.

For all

\lim_{r\to 0}r\kappa(r,t)=0

\lim_{r\to+\infty}r\kappa(r,t)>0

ensures that

stay bounded for all

We now state the main convergence result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Under Assumption
5
, suppose that

\displaystyle L_{X}\leq\frac{c_{1}\varphi_{1}(R)}{2\gamma\eta}~~\text{and}~~L_{Y}\leq\frac{1}{2}c_{2}\gamma\eta\varphi_{2}(R),

(5.9)

Here, constants are set as

\varphi_{2}(R)=\exp(-\frac{\beta}{4}\int_{0}^{R}\tilde{\kappa}_{y}(s)sds)

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=4\beta^{-1}\Big((e-1)R^{2}/2+\sqrt{\frac{8}{\beta\kappa_{x}}}Re^{\pi/4}+\frac{4}{\beta\kappa_{x}}\Big)^{-1},

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=4\beta^{-1}\Big((e-1)R^{2}/2+\sqrt{\frac{8}{\beta\kappa_{y}}}Re^{\pi/4}+\frac{4}{\beta\kappa_{y}}\Big)^{-1},

which are independent of
\eta

Remark 5.1

From the convergence result above, we observe that a higher temperature

(\kappa_{x})
and concavity

6
Discussion

In this paper, we analyzed the convergence behavior of two-timescale GDA dynamics in both finite-dimensional and infinite-dimensional settings. Leveraging tools such as hypocoercivity and coupling methods, we provided rigorous results on the interplay between the learning rate ratio and convergence properties in continuous time. Specifically, we derived quantitative estimates of convergence rates for finite-dimensional quadratic games and established Wasserstein-1 convergence guarantees for mean-field GDA dynamics under reasonable assumptions.

Our results address the importance of the learning rate ratio in determining the dynamics’ behavior. Notably, for the mean-field min-max optimization problem, we addressed an open question posed by
[
54
]
, providing convergence guarantees of mean-field GDA for locally nonconvex-nonconcave objective functions when high temperature is considered.

Future research could explore extending the hypocoercivity approach to analyze mean-field GDA, particularly by tackling the challenges posed by its nonlinear drift structure, which is a promising direction for further study.
