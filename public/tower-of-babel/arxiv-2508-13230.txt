1.
Introduction

In this paper, we consider the following eikonal equation with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary value condition

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} |Du|&=V\quad\text{in }\mathcal{B}(0,1),\\
u&=0\quad\text{on }\partial\mathcal{B}(0,1),\end{aligned}\right.

where
\mathcal{B}(0,1)
is the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
V:\mathcal{B}(0,1)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is a nonnegative Lipschitz continuous function.

The eikonal equation arises as a fundamental model in the theory of first-order Hamilton-Jacobi equations. It represents the stationary Hamilton-Jacobi equation with a Hamiltonian of the form
H(p,x)=|p|-V(x)
for
(p,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and plays a central role in both the analysis of nonlinear PDEs and in applications such as geometric optics, front evolution, and optimal control.

To study the solutions of (
1.1
), we use the vanishing viscosity procedure. For
\epsilon>0
, we consider the equation

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} |Du^{\epsilon}|-V&=\epsilon\Delta u^{\epsilon}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{B}(0,1),\\
u^{\epsilon}&=0\quad\text{on }\partial\mathcal{B}(0,1).\end{aligned}\right.

To simplify our problem, we always assume that
V
is radially symmetric and consider the radially symmetric solution of (
1.1
).
With a slight abuse of notation, we suppose
V(x)=V(|x|)=V(r)
, where
V:[0,1)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is Lipschitz continuous.

The vanishing viscosity method is a classical regularization technique for studying first-order Hamilton-Jacobi equations (see
[
6
,
7
,
15
]
and the references therein). By adding a small diffusive term
\epsilon\Delta u^{\epsilon}
to the original equation, the new equation admits smooth solutions for each
\epsilon>0
. Classically, this process selects a correct viscosity solution out of many possible almost everywhere solutions. If the limiting equation has the comparison principle, then it has a unique viscosity solution, and hence, the unique viscosity solution is selected.

It is important to emphasize that when studying the convergence of solutions to (
1.2
) as
\epsilon\to 0
, a so-called “selection problem”arises. Specifically, for our problem, the limiting PDE (
1.1
) admits multiple viscosity solutions as
V
can be zero at some locations, which is discussed in detail in Section 5. It is not clear at all what solution that
\{u^{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon>0}
converges to, and what is the underlying selection principle. In general, for such a selection problem, there has been a lot of related research (see
[
2
,
9
,
8
,
1
,
17
,
10
,
3
,
11
,
12
,
16
]
and the references therein), but this remains a widely open field. For further discussions and a broader view of related open problems, we refer the reader to
[
13
, Section 6.6]
, where these issues are explored in more detail.

In this paper, we establish the following results in the radially symmetric setting of the eikonal equation:

(1)

Selection principle:
We prove that the maximal solution among all radially symmetric viscosity solutions is selected by the vanishing viscosity process (Theorem 3.1).

(2)

Quantitative convergence rate:
We derive an explicit estimate for the convergence as
\epsilon\to 0
:

|u^{\epsilon}(x)-u(x)|\leq C\epsilon|\log\epsilon|\,(1-|x|-\log|x|),\quad x\in\mathcal{B}(0,1)\setminus\{0\},

as stated in Theorem 4.1.
To the best of our knowledge, the results of Theorems 3.1 and 4.1 are new in the literature.

(3)

Uniqueness discussion:
We analyze the uniqueness of viscosity solutions for the eikonal equation when
V
vanishes at certain points, and provide illustrative examples in Section 5.
The result in this part is well known; see
[
13
]
for example.

2.
Preliminaries

As
V
is radially symmetric, it is natural to look for radially symmetric solutions to (
1.2
) where
u^{\epsilon}(x)=u^{\epsilon}(|x|)
for
x\in\overline{\mathcal{B}(0,1)}
. Now (
1.2
) has been simplified into an ODE:

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} |(u^{\epsilon})^{{}^{\prime}}|-V&=\epsilon\left((u^{\epsilon})^{{}^{\prime\prime}}+\frac{n-1}{r}(u^{\epsilon})^{{}^{\prime}}\right)&\quad\text{in}\ (0,1),\\
u^{\epsilon}(1)&=0&r=1.\end{aligned}\right.

To solve (
2.1
), we first assume
(u^{\epsilon})^{{}^{\prime}}(r)\leq 0
for
r\in(0,1)
. We will check our assumption after we get
u^{\epsilon}
.

Let
p^{\epsilon}(r)=(u^{\epsilon})^{{}^{\prime}}(r)
, then
p^{\epsilon}
satisfies a nonhomogeneous first order ODE:

(2.2)

(p^{\epsilon})^{{}^{\prime}}+\left(\frac{n-1}{r}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)p^{\epsilon}=-\frac{V}{\epsilon}.

Using the integrating factor method, we get the solution
p^{\epsilon}(r)
to (
2.2
):

p^{\epsilon}(r)=-\frac{1}{\epsilon}r^{-n+1}e^{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}}\int_{0}^{r}s^{n-1}e^{\frac{s}{\epsilon}}V(s)\,ds+Cr^{-n+1}e^{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}}

where
C
is a constant to be chosen.

Since
u^{\epsilon}
is radially symmetric, we require
p^{\epsilon}
to satisfy

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}p^{\epsilon}(r)=0
so that
(u^{\epsilon})^{{}^{\prime}}(0)=0
and
u^{\epsilon}
is smooth enough at
r=0
.
Therefore
C=0
, and
p^{\epsilon}
becomes:

(u^{\epsilon})^{{}^{\prime}}(r)=p^{\epsilon}(r)=-\frac{1}{\epsilon}r^{-n+1}e^{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}}\int_{0}^{r}s^{n-1}e^{\frac{s}{\epsilon}}V(s)\,ds.

Recalling our boundary condition
u^{\epsilon}(1)=0
, we now can get
u^{\epsilon}
: For
r\in[0,1]
, set

u^{\epsilon}(r)=\int_{1}^{r}p^{\epsilon}(s)\,ds.

However, according to the process above,
u^{\epsilon}
we found satisfies (
2.1
) and hence (
1.2
) classically in the open interval
(0,1)
. We still need to check whether
u^{\epsilon}
satisfies (
1.2
) at 0.

To verify this, we compute the partial derivatives of
u^{\epsilon}
at 0.
Let
\{e_{1},e_{2},\cdots,e_{n}\}
be the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Since

u^{\epsilon}(0)=\int_{1}^{0}(u^{\epsilon})^{{}^{\prime}}(s)\,ds,

by L’Hopital’s rule,

\displaystyle u_{x_{i}}^{\epsilon}(0)

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{u^{\epsilon}(he_{i})-u^{\epsilon}(0)}{h}

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\int_{0}^{h}(u^{\epsilon})^{{}^{\prime}}(s)\,ds}{h}

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}(u^{\epsilon})^{{}^{\prime}}(h)

\displaystyle=(u^{\epsilon})^{{}^{\prime}}(0)

\displaystyle=0,

which implies that
u^{\epsilon}\in C^{1}(\mathcal{B}(0,1))
. Next, by the L’Hopital rule again,

\displaystyle u_{x_{i}x_{i}}^{\epsilon}(0)

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{u^{\epsilon}_{x_{i}}(he_{i})-u^{\epsilon}_{x_{i}}(0)}{h}=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{p^{\epsilon}(h)}{h}

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{-\frac{1}{\epsilon}h^{-n+1}e^{-\frac{h}{\epsilon}}\int_{0}^{h}s^{n-1}e^{\frac{s}{\epsilon}}V(s)\,ds}{h}

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}-\frac{1}{\epsilon}e^{\frac{-h}{\epsilon}}\cdot\lim_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\int_{0}^{h}s^{n-1}e^{\frac{s}{\epsilon}}V(s)\,ds}{h^{n}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\lim_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\int_{0}^{h}s^{n-1}e^{\frac{s}{\epsilon}}V(s)\,ds}{h^{n}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\lim_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{h^{n-1}e^{\frac{h}{\epsilon}}V(h)}{nh^{n-1}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{V(0)}{n\epsilon}.

In this way,

Du^{\epsilon}(0)=(u^{\epsilon}_{x_{1}}(0),u^{\epsilon}_{x_{2}}(0),\cdots,u^{\epsilon}_{x_{n}}(0))=(0,0,\cdots,0),

and

\epsilon\Delta u^{\epsilon}(0)=\epsilon\sum_{i=1}^{n}u^{\epsilon}_{x_{i}x_{i}}(0)=-V(0).

Therefore,

|Du^{\epsilon}(0)|-V(0)=0-V(0)=-V(0)=\epsilon\Delta u^{\epsilon}(0).

Hence, we know
u^{\epsilon}
solves (
1.2
) classically at
x=0
.
At the same time, since
V(s)\geq 0
on
[0,1)
, then

(u^{\epsilon})^{{}^{\prime}}(r)=p^{\epsilon}(r)=-\frac{1}{\epsilon}r^{-n+1}e^{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}}\int_{0}^{r}s^{n-1}e^{\frac{s}{\epsilon}}V(s)\,ds\leq 0,

which confirms that our assumption is correct.

We thus have the formulas for
u^{\epsilon}
: For
|x|=r\in[0,1]
,

(u^{\epsilon})^{{}^{\prime}}(r)=p^{\epsilon}(r)=-\frac{1}{\epsilon}r^{-n+1}e^{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}}\int_{0}^{r}s^{n-1}e^{\frac{s}{\epsilon}}V(s)\,ds,

(2.3)

u^{\epsilon}(r)=\int_{1}^{r}p^{\epsilon}(s)\,ds=\int_{r}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{s}\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)^{n-1}e^{\frac{t-s}{\epsilon}}V(t)\,dt\right)\,ds.

And we set, for
|x|=r\in[0,1]
,

(2.4)

u(r)=\int_{r}^{1}V(s)\,ds.

We remark that
u
set in (
2.4
) is the maximal viscosity solution to (
1.1
) among all possible radially symmetric viscosity solutions to (
1.1
).

3.
Qualitative convergence argument of the vanishing viscosity process

In this section, we will show that
u^{\epsilon}
converges to
u
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
and discuss the convergence rate in the next section. We begin by stating the main result and then provide a proof.

Theorem 3.1
.

As in (
2.3
)–(
2.4
), let
\{u^{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon>0}
and
u
be solutions to (
1.2
) and (
1.1
) respectively under the radially symmetric setting. Uniformly for
0\leq r\leq 1
, we have

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}u^{\epsilon}(r)=u(r).

Proof.

We first show
-p^{\epsilon}(s)\rightarrow V(s)
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
for
s>0
.

Recall our formula for
-p^{\epsilon}
:

-p^{\epsilon}(s)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{s}\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)^{n-1}e^{\frac{t-s}{\epsilon}}V(t)\,dt.

Let
t=s+\epsilon y
, then
p^{\epsilon}
becomes:

\displaystyle p^{\epsilon}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{-s/\epsilon}^{0}\left(\frac{s+\epsilon y}{s}\right)^{n-1}e^{y}V(s+\epsilon y)\,dy

\displaystyle=\int\left(\frac{s+\epsilon y}{s}\right)^{n-1}e^{y}V(s+\epsilon y)\mathbbm{1}_{(-s/\epsilon,0)}(y)\,dy.

Also, we can write
V
as:

V(s)=\int e^{y}V(s)\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(y)\,dy.

We see that the integrand

\left(\frac{s+\epsilon y}{s}\right)^{n-1}e^{y}V(s+\epsilon y)\mathbbm{1}_{(-s/\epsilon,0)}(y)\rightarrow e^{y}V(s)\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(y)

for
s>0
, as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
, since
V
is continuous.

We also see that

\left|\left(\frac{s+\epsilon y}{s}\right)^{n-1}e^{y}V(s+\epsilon y)\mathbbm{1}_{(-s/\epsilon,0)}(y)\right|\leq Me^{y}\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(y)

for some
M>0
and fixed
s>0
, as
V
is bounded.

Since
Me^{y}\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(y)\in

L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, by the dominated convergence theorem,

\int\left(\frac{s+\epsilon y}{s}\right)^{n-1}e^{y}V(s+\epsilon y)\mathbbm{1}_{(-s/\epsilon,0)}(y)\,dy\rightarrow\int e^{y}V(s)\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(y)\,dy,

i.e., we have
p^{\epsilon}(s)\rightarrow V(s)
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
, for
s>0
.
Notice that

|p^{\epsilon}(s)|=\left|\int_{-s/\epsilon}^{0}\left(\frac{s+\epsilon y}{s}\right)^{n-1}e^{y}V(s+\epsilon y)\,dy\right|\leq M\int_{-s/\epsilon}^{0}e^{y}\,dy\leq M\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{y}dy=M

since
V
is bounded.

Applying the dominated convergence theorem again, we get

\int_{r}^{1}-p^{\epsilon}(s)\,ds\rightarrow\int_{r}^{1}V(s)

as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
, as desired.
Hence we proved
u^{\epsilon}(r)\rightarrow u(r)
for
r\in[0,1]
, as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
.

As
|p^{\epsilon}|\leq M
, we have
\{u^{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon\in(0,1)}
is equi-continuous. By the Azelà-Ascoli theorem and the above, we imply that
u^{\epsilon}\rightarrow u
uniformly on
[0,1]
.
∎

4.
rate of convergence of the vanishing viscosity process

As in the previous section, we first present the main result, namely the convergence rate, and then provide the proof.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\{u^{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon>0}
and
u
be the radially symmetric solutions to (
1.2
) and (
1.1
), respectively, as defined in (
2.3
)–(
2.4
). Then, for all
0<r\leq 1
, we have

|u^{\epsilon}(r)-u(r)|\leq C\epsilon|\log\epsilon|(1-r-\log r)

for some positive
C>0
independent of
\epsilon,r\in(0,1]
.

We note that a similar convergence rate
\epsilon|\log\epsilon|
appears in the recent works
[
14
,
4
,
5
]
for the vanishing viscosity process with uniformly convex Hamiltonians.
Our result is different as it is for the eikonal equation with
H(x,p)=|p|-V(x)
, which is convex but not uniformly convex in
p
. This resemblance might hint at underlying structural connections worth investigating further.

Proof.

To find the rate of convergence for
u^{\epsilon}\rightarrow u
, we first turn to focus on the convergence rate of their derivatives.

In the following part, we study the rate of convergence for
-p^{\epsilon}(r)\rightarrow V(r)
.

From the discussion above, we have

-p^{\epsilon}(r)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{r}\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{n-1}e^{\frac{s-r}{\epsilon}}V(s)\,ds.

Let
\frac{s-r}{\epsilon}=t
, then

-p^{\epsilon}(r)=\int_{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}}^{0}(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1}e^{t}V(r+\epsilon t)\,dt.

Now we consider
|V(r)-(-p^{\epsilon}(r))|
. We have

\displaystyle|V(r)-(-p^{\epsilon}(r))|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{t}V(r)dt-\int_{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}}^{0}(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1}e^{t}V(r+\epsilon t)\,dt\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{-\infty}^{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}}e^{t}V(r)\,dt+\int_{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}}^{0}e^{t}V(r)dt-\int_{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}}^{0}(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1}e^{t}V(r+\epsilon t)\,dt\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{-\infty}^{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}}e^{t}V(r)\,dt\right|+\int_{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}}^{0}e^{t}|V(r)-(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1}V(r+\epsilon t)|\,dt

\displaystyle=\text{I}+\text{II}.

At this point, we bound I and II separately. We first control I,

\displaystyle\text{I}

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{-\infty}^{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}}e^{t}V(r)\,dt\right|

\displaystyle=|V(r)e^{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}}|

\displaystyle=|V(r)|e^{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}},

where we use
V(r)
is bounded.

As for II, we separate it into integrals on two different intervals and bound them respectively.

\text{II}=\left(\int_{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}}^{\log\epsilon}+\int_{\log{\epsilon}}^{0}\right)e^{t}|V(r)-(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1}V(r+\epsilon t)|\,dt=\text{III}+\text{IV}.

We compute

\displaystyle\text{III}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}}^{\log\epsilon}e^{t}|V(r)-(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1}V(r+\epsilon t)|\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}}^{\log\epsilon}e^{t}\,dt

\displaystyle=\left.Ce^{t}\right|_{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}}^{\log{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle=C(\epsilon-e^{-\frac{r}{\epsilon}})

\displaystyle\leq C\epsilon

for
r>0
.
Also,

\displaystyle\text{IV}

\displaystyle=\int_{\log\epsilon}^{0}e^{t}|V(r)-(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1}V(r+\epsilon t)|\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\log\epsilon}^{0}e^{t}|V(r)-(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1}V(r)+(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1}V(r)-(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1}V(r+\epsilon t)|\,dt.

Now we focus on the integrand

\displaystyle|V(r)-(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1}V(r)+(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1}V(r)-(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r})^{n-1}V(r+\epsilon t)|

\displaystyle\leq|V(r)|(1-(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1})+(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1}|V(r)-V(r+\epsilon t)|.

For the term
1-(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1}
, we see that

\displaystyle 1-(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1}=1-(1+(n-1)\frac{\epsilon}{r}t+\cdots+(\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1}).

Noticing that
-\frac{r}{\epsilon}\leq\log{\epsilon}\leq t\leq 0
, we have
|\frac{\epsilon}{r}t|\leq 1
.
Thus,

|1-(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1}|\leq C\frac{\epsilon}{r}|t|.

For the other term
(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1}|V(r)-V(r+\epsilon t)|
,

\displaystyle(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r}t)^{n-1}|V(r)-V(r+\epsilon t)|

\displaystyle\leq|V(r)-V(r+\epsilon t)|

\displaystyle\leq C\epsilon|t|

where we use
V
is Lipschitz continuous.

Finally, we add these bounds together to get

\displaystyle\text{IV}

\displaystyle=\int_{\log\epsilon}^{0}e^{t}|V(r)-(1+\frac{\epsilon}{r})^{n-1}V(r+\epsilon t)|\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\log\epsilon}^{0}e^{t}(C_{1}\frac{\epsilon}{r}|t|+C_{2}\epsilon|t|)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\log\epsilon}^{0}e^{t}(C_{1}\frac{\epsilon}{r}|\log{\epsilon}|+C_{2}\epsilon|\log{\epsilon}|)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq(C_{1}\frac{\epsilon}{r}|\log{\epsilon}|+C_{2}\epsilon|\log{\epsilon}|)(1-\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\frac{1}{r})(\epsilon|\log{\epsilon}|).

Combining all these estimations together and keeping track with
r
, we have

|-p^{\epsilon}(r)-V(r)|\leq C\epsilon|\log{\epsilon}|(1+\frac{1}{r}).

Then,

\displaystyle|u^{\epsilon}(r)-u(r)|

\displaystyle=|\int_{1}^{r}p^{\epsilon}(s)\,ds-\int_{1}^{r}(-V(s))\,ds|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{r}^{1}|p^{\epsilon}+V(s)|\,ds

\displaystyle\leq C\epsilon|\log\epsilon|\int_{r}^{1}(1+\frac{1}{s})\,ds

\displaystyle\leq C\epsilon|\log\epsilon|(1-r-\log r).

∎

Remark
.

The bound in Theorem 4.1 blows up as
r\rightarrow 0^{+}
. As of now, we do not know the convergence rate of
u^{\epsilon}(0)-u(0)
.

5.
A discussion on the uniqueness of viscosity solutions

In this section, we start with the following eikonal equation with Dirichlet boundary value condition

(5.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} |Du|&=f(x)\quad\text{in }U,\\
u&=0\quad\text{on }\partial U,\end{aligned}\right.

where
U
is an open, bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
f:U\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is a nonnegative continuous function.

We give the definitions of viscosity subsolutions, supersolutions, and solutions to (
5.1
).
See
[
15
]
.

Definition 5.1

(viscosity solutions of (
5.1
))
.

A function
u\in{\rm BUC\,}(U)
, where
{\rm BUC\,}(U)
denotes the space of bounded, uniformly continuous functions on
U
, is called

(a) a viscosity subsolution of (
5.1
) if for any
\varphi\in C^{1}(U)
such that
u-\varphi
has a local max at
x_{0}\in U
, then

|D\varphi(x_{0})|-f(x_{0})\leq 0,

and
u(x)\leq 0
on
\partial U
;

(b) a viscosity supersolution of (
5.1
) if for any
\psi\in C^{1}(U)
such that
u-\psi
has a local min at
x_{0}\in U
, then

|D\psi(x_{0})|-f(x_{0})\geq 0,

and
u(x)\geq 0
on
\partial U
;

(c) a viscosity solution of (
5.1
) if it is both a viscosity subsolution and a supersolution.

Now we give the comparison principle for viscosity solutions of (
5.1
) and the proof of it. This result is well known; see
[
13
]
. We include it here for completeness.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{x\in U|\;f(x)=0\}
and
u
,
v
be two viscosity solutions of (
5.1
). Then, if
u\leq v
on
\mathcal{A}
, we have
u\leq v
in
U
.

Proof.

Using the same notation as in the theorem. We suppose
u,v
to be two viscosity solutions to (
5.1
) and
u\leq v
on
\mathcal{A}
.

First, fix
\epsilon>0
. We consider the set
\mathcal{P}=\bigcup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{B}(a,\delta)
, i.e., the union of balls with radius
\delta
centered at points in
\mathcal{A}
. Since
u\leq v
in
\mathcal{A}
, we are able to choose
\delta_{\epsilon}>0
small enough such that

su-(v+\epsilon)<-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\quad\text{in}\ \overline{\mathcal{P}}

for
s<1
and
s
close enough to 1.

For convenience, we denote
u^{s}=su
and
v^{\epsilon}=v+\epsilon
and our goal is to show
u^{s}\leq v^{\epsilon}
in
U
for
\epsilon>0
small and
s<1
close to 1.

We continue our proof by using the classical “doubling variable” method. Assume by contradiction that

\sup_{x\in\bar{U}\setminus\mathcal{P}}(u^{s}(x)-v^{\epsilon}(x))=u^{s}(\bar{x})-v^{\epsilon}(\bar{x})=\sigma>0\;\text{for some}\ \bar{x}\in\bar{U}\setminus\mathcal{P}.

We see that:

u^{s}(x)-v^{\epsilon}(x)=su(x)-v(x)-\epsilon=0-\epsilon<0\;\text{on}\;\partial U\\

and

u^{s}(x)-v^{\epsilon}(x)=su(x)-v(x)-\epsilon<-\frac{\epsilon}{2}<0\;\text{on}\;\partial\mathcal{P}.

Hence,
\bar{x}
can only belong to
U\setminus\mathcal{\bar{P}}
.

Here, we tried to avoid the case that
\bar{x}
appears on the boundary because we will apply the viscosity subsolution and supersolution test later.
To be more precise, we need a local max or a local min near
\bar{x}
, which may not be true when
\bar{x}
is on the boundary.
We consider an auxiliary function
\Psi(x,y)
:

\Psi^{\alpha}(x,y)=u^{s}(x)-v^{\epsilon}(y)-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{2\alpha}

and

\sup_{x,y\in\bar{U}\setminus\mathcal{P}}=\Psi^{\alpha}(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha})\;\text{for some}\;x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha}\in\bar{U}\setminus\mathcal{P}.

Since
(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha})\rightarrow(\bar{x},\bar{x})
as
\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}
, we have
x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha}\in U\setminus\bar{\mathcal{P}}
for sufficiently small
\alpha
.
Since
u
is a viscosity solution to the (
5.1
), we see that
u^{s}=su
is a viscosity solution to the following equation:

(5.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} |Du^{s}|&=sf(x)\quad\text{in }U\\
u^{s}&=0\quad\text{on }\partial U\end{aligned}\right.

.

From the above, we know that the auxiliary function
\Psi^{\alpha}(x,y)
has a max at
(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha})
. Therefore,
x\mapsto\Psi^{\alpha}(x,y_{\alpha})
has a max at
x_{\alpha}
, which means

x\mapsto u^{s}(x)-\frac{|x-y_{\alpha}|^{2}}{2\alpha}\quad\text{has a max at}\;x_{\alpha}.

As
u^{s}
is a viscosity solution of (
5.2
), by the viscosity subsolution test, we have

\frac{1}{\alpha}|x_{\alpha}-y_{\alpha}|-sf(x_{\alpha})\leq 0.

Similarly,
y\mapsto\Psi^{\alpha}(x_{\alpha},y)
has a min at
y_{\alpha}
, which means

y\mapsto v(y)-(-\frac{|x_{\epsilon}-y|^{2}}{2\alpha})\quad\text{has a min at}\;y_{\alpha}.

As
v
is a viscosity solution of (
5.1
), by the viscosity supersolution test, we have

\frac{1}{\alpha}|x_{\alpha}-y_{\alpha}|-f(y_{\alpha})\geq 0.

Combine these two inequalities together, we get

f(y_{\alpha})\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}|x_{\alpha}-y_{\alpha}|\leq sf(x_{\alpha}).

Take
\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}
, we get

f(\bar{x})\leq sf(\bar{x}).

Since
f(x)
is nonnegative and
\mathcal{A}=\{x\in U|f(x)=0\}
, then
f(x)>\gamma>0
for some positive
\gamma
in
U\setminus\bar{\mathcal{P}}
. Notice that
\bar{x}\in U\setminus\bar{\mathcal{P}}
,
f(\bar{x})>0
. Recall that
s<1
, then the inequality

f(\bar{x})\leq sf(\bar{x})

cannot be true. We get a contradiction. Hence we proved
u^{s}\leq v^{\epsilon}
in
U\setminus{\bar{\mathcal{P}}}
.
Also, according to our construction at the beginning of the proof, we have
u^{s}\leq v^{\epsilon}
in
\mathcal{\bar{P}}
, too. As a result,
u^{s}\leq v^{\epsilon}
in the whole
U
.

Moreover,
u^{s}\leq v^{\epsilon}
in
U
is true for
s<1
close to 1 and
\epsilon>0
small. Then take
s\rightarrow 1^{-}
and
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
, we have

u\leq v\quad\text{in}\;U.

∎

From this theorem, we can directly derive the following two corollaries.

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
u,v
be two viscosity solutions of (
5.1
) and
u=v
on
\mathcal{A}
. Then
u=v
on
U
.

Corollary 5.4
.

For strictly positive
f
, if a viscosity solution to equation (
5.1
) exists, then it is unique.

The proof of this corollary is quite simple. Notice that for strictly positive
f
, the set
\mathcal{A}
is empty. Then for any two viscosity solutions
u,v
, the assumption that two solutions
u,v
coincide on
\mathcal{A}
holds vacuously, and hence the uniqueness follows from the extension property.
