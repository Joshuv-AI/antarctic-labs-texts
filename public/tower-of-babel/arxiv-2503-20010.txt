A truncated inner product formula in the geometry of numbers
Seokho Jin, Seungki Kim

Abstract
. We explore the theory of truncations of automorphic forms to study the fine-scale distribution of rational points on Grassmannians. Previous approaches based on variance estimates were hindered by the divergence of the L 2 L^{2} -norm of the counting function for sublattices of intermediate rank. We resolve this issue by developing an asymptotic formula for the truncated inner product of pseudo-Eisenstein series E f ​ ( g ) = ∑ L ⊆ ℤ n rank ​ k , prim . f ⁡ ( det L ​ g ) E_{f}(g)=\sum_{L\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}\atop\mathrm{rank\,}k,\ \mathrm{prim.}}f(\det Lg) on SL ⁡ ( n , ℤ ) \ SL ⁡ ( n , ℝ ) \mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{R}) . Our analysis relies on several technical results of independent interest, including a proof of the uniform moderate growth property for pseudo-Eisenstein series, a comparison of the Arthur and harsh truncations, and a method for resolving singularities in the Maass-Selberg relations. 1.
Introduction

1.1.
Automorphic methods in the geometry of numbers

It may be said that the automorphic approach to the geometry of numbers started with the following celebrated theorem of Siegel
[
Sie45
]
. Let
G=\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{R}),\Gamma=\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
, and
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function. Then

(1.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{vol}(\Gamma\backslash G)}\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\atop v\neq 0}f(vg)dg=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)dx.

Siegel
[
Sie45
]
also stated without proof that, for
1\leq k<n
and measurable
f:(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{vol}(\Gamma\backslash G)}\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}\sum_{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\atop\mbox{\tiny lin. indep.}}f(v_{1}g,\ldots,v_{k}g)dg=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{k}}f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})dx_{1}\ldots dx_{k},

which was later shown by Rogers
[
Rog55
]
and Schmidt
[
Sch57
]
. These formulas, and their numerous extensions (e.g.,
[
Vee98
,
AGH24
,
GKY22
,
Kim24
]
), have been among the most powerful tools in the geometry of numbers to date, as evidenced by the records on sphere packing
[
Rog47
,
Ven13
,
Kla25
]
and covering
[
Rog59
]
by lattices. Moreover, nowadays they have become one of the standard methods in homogeneous dynamics as well, playing roles in a number of important works, such as
[
EMM98
,
KM99
,
AM09
,
MS10
,
AM15
]
.

Recently, the field has seen several more fruitful applications of automorphic methods. For example, the second-named author and Venkatesh
[
KV18
]
used the full force of the spectral theory of the
\mathrm{GL}(n)
Eisenstein series to show that (on the Riemann Hypothesis) all but arbitrarily small percentage of lattices have about the same number of Siegel-reduced bases, a result that helps shape one’s understanding of lattice reduction algorithms such as LLL
[
LLL82
]
. Viazovska
[
Via17
]
and Cohn-Kumar-Miller-Radchenko-Viazovska
[
CKMRV
]
, with brilliant uses of the theory of modular forms, found the “miracle functions” whose existences were conjectured by Cohn-Elkies
[
CE03
]
, proving that the
E_{8}
and the Leech lattices provide the best sphere packings in dimensions 8 and 24, respectively. Gargava-Viazovska
[
GV24
]
recently gave a new lower bound on the lattice packing densities in infinitely many dimensions, replacing the record of Venkatesh
[
Ven13
]
, by adopting part of the techniques of Einsiedler-Lindenstrauss-Michel-Venkatesh
[
ELMV
]
as well as the new subconvexity bound due to Petrow-Young
[
PY23
]
.

1.2.
This paper

The purpose of the present paper is to explore yet another aspect of the theory of automorphic forms for its uses in the geometry of numbers: the Arthur truncation and the inner product formula for the truncated Eisenstein series, known as the Maass-Selberg relations. Specifically, our goal is to develop the methods that facilitate their applications, as well as to demonstrate their power, in the field of lattice-point counting.

The very idea of applying the Arthur truncation and the related machinery to the geometry of numbers originates from S.D. Miller, who, in his lecture notes
[
Mil20
]
, demonstrated an alternative proof of the Siegel integral formula (
1.1
) via the Maass-Selberg relations. The Ph.D. thesis of Thurman
[
Thu24
]
generalizes Miller’s argument to evaluate the integrals of the form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{vol}(\Gamma\backslash G)}\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}\sum_{L_{1}\subseteq_{p}\ldots\subseteq_{p}L_{k}\subseteq_{p}\mathbb{Z}^{n}\atop\mathrm{rk\,}L_{j}=i_{j}}f(\det L_{1}g,\ldots,\det L_{k}g)dg

for given
1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}<n
; here,
L\subseteq_{p}M
means that
L
is a primitive sublattice of
M
. For
G=\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})
and
\Gamma=\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
, this was previously announced by
[
Kim22
, Theorem 5]
; but Thurman’s formula also covers the case in which
G
is any Chevalley group. Both
[
Mil20
]
and
[
Thu24
]
work with the truncated inner product of a “lattice-point counting function” — such as
\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}f(vg)
— against the constant function
1
. The present paper, on the other hand, is concerned with the product of two nontrivial lattice-point counting functions. More precisely, we are interested in the integrals of the form

(1.2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{vol}^{C}(\Gamma\backslash G)}\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}\Lambda^{C}\sum_{L_{1}\subseteq_{p}\mathbb{Z}^{n}\atop\mathrm{rk\,}L_{1}=k_{1}}f_{1}(\det L_{1}g)\sum_{L_{2}\subseteq_{p}\mathbb{Z}^{n}\atop\mathrm{rk\,}L_{2}=k_{2}}f_{2}(\det L_{2}g)dg,

where
\Lambda^{C}
is the truncation operator (see Sec.
2.6
below),
\mathrm{vol}^{C}(\Gamma\backslash G)=\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}\Lambda^{C}1dg
is the volume of the truncated fundamental domain,
1\leq k_{1},k_{2}\leq n
, and
f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are smooth functions of compact support.

Truncated versions of the mean value formulas over lattices, such as (
1.1
), are naturally of interest, since they allow us to dismiss from consideration those lattices sitting near the cusp of
\Gamma\backslash G
, which are expected to behave badly in terms of packing and covering. There also exists a more concrete motivation, arising from the fact
[
Kim22
, Corollary to Theorem 4]
that, for
2\leq k\leq n-2
and any measurable
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of nonzero mass,

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}\left(\sum_{L\subseteq_{p}\mathbb{Z}^{n}\atop\mathrm{rk\,}L=k}f(\det Lg)\right)^{2}dg=\infty.

As explained in
[
Kim22
]
, this stymies the attempt to study the metric properties of the rank
k
sublattices of a random lattice, or equivalently the rational points of the Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}(n,k)
with respect to a random twisted height (cf.
[
Thu93
]
), via the standard methods that have been immensely successful in the case
k=1
. Truncation provides an appealing method for circumventing this problem, since it forces the above integral to become finite.

Our main result is the following formula for (
1.2
). Here we give a rough version of the actual statement Theorem
5.2
, in order to quickly present the idea without laying out the technical details.

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem
5.2
, roughly stated)
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be smooth functions of compact support.
The truncated inner product (
1.2
) is equal to

\displaystyle Mf_{1}(n)Mf_{2}(n)\xi(n,k_{1})\xi(n,k_{2})+O_{n,f_{1},f_{2}}(T^{\kappa}),

where
Mf
denotes the Mellin transform of
f
,

\displaystyle\xi(n,k)=\frac{\xi(2)\cdots\xi(n-k)}{\xi(k+1)\cdots\xi(n)},

\kappa<n(n^{2}-1)/6
is an explicit constant, and
T\gg 0
is a parameter that determines the truncated region.
1
1

1

The higher
T
is, the less we truncate; see Sec.
2.6
,
2.7
for details.

The main term above is precisely what one would expect, based on the existing literature on the problem of counting sublattices and the rational points of a flag variety, e.g.,
[
Sch68
,
Thu93
,
Kim22
,
Kim23
]
. For instance, the notation
a(n,d)H^{n}
in those works is precisely
Mf(n)\xi(n,k)
when
f=\mathbf{1}_{[0,H]}
, the characteristic function of
[0,H]
.

Of course, for Theorem
1.1
to be useful, the error term must be manageable. It is somewhat inconvenient to state it precisely in the introduction, but for certain important applications, it takes the form

(1.3)

\displaystyle O_{n}\left(Mf_{1}(n)Mf_{2}(n-\eta)T^{-\frac{\eta k_{2}(n-k_{2})}{2}}\right)+O_{n}\left(Mf_{1}(n-\eta^{\prime})Mf_{2}(n-\eta)T^{\kappa}\right)

for
0<\eta^{\prime}<\eta<1
. If
f_{1}=f_{2}=\mathbf{1}_{[0,H]}
, say, then
Mf_{1}(s)=Mf_{2}(s)=H^{s}/s
, so the error is indeed less than the main term for all sufficiently large
H
.

We also provide a version of the main theorem (Theorem
5.3
) in which the truncation is “harsh,” at the cost of an additional error term
O(T^{-p})
for any choice of
p>0
. The “harsh truncation” of an automorphic form causes it to vanish in the truncated region, which is a desirable trait from the perspective of the geometry of numbers, since it enables one to study the statistical properties of lattices contained in a compact subset of
\Gamma\backslash G
. In fact, we prove the following.

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem
3.7
, roughly stated)
.

Let
\phi
be a function on
\Gamma\backslash G
of uniform moderate growth. Then its harsh and Arthur truncations differ by at most
O(T^{-p})
pointwise in the truncated region parametrized by
T>0
. They coincide on the rest of
\Gamma\backslash G
.

We also show (Proposition
3.2
) that functions of the form
\sum_{L\subseteq_{p}\mathbb{Z}^{n}}f(\det Lg)
are indeed of uniform moderate growth; so the above theorem is applicable to those.

Finally, as a demonstration of the power of the automorphic machinery in the geometry of numbers, we showcase an application of our results that extends the famous lattice-point counting discrepancy bound of Schmidt
[
Sch60
]
to the problem of counting sublattices.

Theorem 1.3

(Theorem
7.7
restated)
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
small. Let
h_{p}:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the characteristic function of
[0,p]
. Then for almost every
g\in\Gamma\backslash G
and
p\gg_{g}0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{L\subseteq_{p}\mathbb{Z}^{n}\atop\mathrm{rk\,}L=k}h_{p}(\det Lg)-Mh_{p}(n)\xi(n,k)=O_{n,\epsilon,g}(p^{n-\frac{1}{7}+\epsilon}).

For
\min(k,n-k)\geq 8
, this error term is substantially lower than the currently known estimates (e.g.
[
Sch68
,
Thu93
,
Kim23
]
)

\displaystyle\sum_{L\subseteq_{p}\mathbb{Z}^{n}\atop\mathrm{rk\,}L=k}h_{p}(\det Lg)-Mh_{p}(n)\xi(n,k)=O_{n,g}\left(p^{n-\max(\frac{1}{k},\frac{1}{n-k})}\right),

as well as the conjecture of Franke-Manin-Tschinkel
[
FMT89
, Corollary to Theorem 5]
, which is somewhat better than the above but still diminishes as
\min(k,n-k)
grows.

Our work leaves much room for further developments. In particular, it seems that our techniques can be extended to generalized flag varieties, which we hope to return to in near future. It is also likely that certain parts of our analyses could be improved for quantitatively tighter results. Looking for applications in which truncation plays an essential role — such as in Theorem
1.3
above — would of course be especially interesting. To that end, it may be worth trying to strengthen our formula so that it takes into account the “shapes” of sublattices as well.

1.3.
A few words about the proof

Let us briefly discuss the main technical challenges faced and resolved in the course of our proof of Theorem
1.1
.

UMG property of pseudo-Eisenstein series

It is well-known (see e.g.,
[
MW95
, I.2]
) that an automorphic form, in the strict sense of the word (see
[
MW95
, I.2.17]
), is of uniform moderate growth (UMG), from which it can be shown that its Arthur truncation is of rapid decay. In particular, this applies to every Eisenstein series. However, a pseudo-Eisenstein series such as
\sum_{L\subseteq_{p}\mathbb{Z}^{n}}f(\det Lg)
is not an automorphic form in the narrow sense; rather, it is a direct integral of a certain family of Eisenstein series. To show that it also has the UMG property, one needs to
uniformly
bound the rate of growth of all Eisenstein series in the family. Curiously, it appears that this natural problem has not been considered previously, at least in writing. We supply the argument for what we need below (Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
), provided
f
is smooth.

Harsh versus Arthur truncations

As discussed earlier, from the geometry of numbers perspective, it is expedient for the truncations of lattice-point counting functions to have a property strictly stronger than rapid decay, namely that it is arbitrarily small everywhere in the truncated region. To achieve this, we refine the argument of Arthur
[
Art05
, Proposition 13.2]
by further exploiting his theory of truncation as well as the “UMG trick” of Harish-Chandra
[
Har76
]
. As a result, we obtain Theorem
1.2
, and the harsh-truncated version of Theorem
1.1
.

Singularities in the Maass-Selberg relation

The Maass-Selberg relation for the Borel Eisenstein series

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}\Lambda^{C}E_{B}(\lambda_{1},g)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{C}E_{B}(\lambda_{2},g)dg

\displaystyle=\sum_{w_{1},w_{2}\in W}\frac{e^{\langle C,w_{1}\lambda_{1}+w_{2}\lambda_{2}\rangle}}{\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta}\langle\alpha^{\vee},w_{1}\lambda_{1}+w_{2}\lambda_{2}\rangle}M(w_{1},\lambda_{1})M(w_{2},\lambda_{2}),

(see Section 2 for the notations) is highly singular in general, e.g., when
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
, as can also be seen in the simpler formula for
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
. It is expected that most singularities on the right cancel with one another, and the genuine singularities, if any, only arise from the intertwining operator
M(w,\lambda)
. However, to the best of our knowledge, no concrete computations have been carried out in this regard, except perhaps for groups of low rank. We are able to resolve this problem for the cases needed by our proof of Theorem
1.1
, by first perturbing one of the
\lambda_{i}
’s to separate the singularities, and then resolving each of them via a combinatorial argument. It seems plausible that our method may extend to any pair of Borel Eisenstein series.

1.4.
Organization

Section 2 lays out the background knowledge and the notations used throughout the paper. In Section 3, we show that the pseudo-Eisenstein series of our interest are of uniform moderate growth, and study the decay properties of the Arthur truncation, proving Theorem
1.2
. In Section 4, we prove Theorem
4.1
, a truncated integral formula for a pseudo-Eisenstein series. It serves as a slight refinement of Miller
[
Mil20
]
and parts of Thurman
[
Thu24
]
, as well as a gentle introduction to the more complicated proof of Theorem
1.1
, which is handled in Section 5. The singularity removal is delegated to Section 6. Finally, Section 7 proves Theorem
1.3
.

2.2.
The spaces
\mathfrak{a}_{P_{1}}^{P_{2}}

Let

\mathfrak{a}:=\mathfrak{a}_{0}:=\Big\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{i}a_{i}=0\Big\}.

n
elements, that acts on
\mathfrak{a}
by permuting its entries. More precisely, for
w\in W
and
a\in\mathfrak{a}
,
(wa)_{i}=a_{w(i)}
; note that this is a right group action.
W
also acts on
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
as well:
for
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
,
(w\lambda)_{w(i)}=\lambda_{i}
; this is a left group action. Observe that
w\lambda(a)=\lambda(wa)
. The notion of the Weyl group generalizes to
\mathfrak{a}_{P_{1}}^{P_{2}}
and so on, but it is not needed for the present paper.

2.3.
Roots, coroots, and weights

\mathfrak{a}^{*}
possesses a few distinguished elements that play an important role in Lie theory. A
root
is an element of the form
\alpha_{ij}\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
,
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
, defined by
\alpha_{ij}(a)=a_{i}-a_{j}
for
a\in\mathfrak{a}
.
\alpha_{ij}
is said to be
positive
if
i<j
, and
negative
if
i>j
. The
Weyl element

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha_{ij}>0}\alpha_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(n-1,n-3,\ldots,-n+1)\equiv(n,n-1,\ldots,1)
is defined to be half the sum of all positive roots.

For
1\leq i\leq n-1
, a root of the form
\alpha_{i,i+1}
is said to be
simple
, which we write
\alpha_{i}
for short. We denote by
\Delta=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1}\}
the set of all simple roots;
\Delta
forms a basis of
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
. We endow
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
with an inner product defined by
\langle\lambda,\alpha_{ij}\rangle=\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}
. Note that the action of
W
is unitary with respect to this inner product.

We define the
coroot

\alpha_{i}^{\vee}\in\mathfrak{a}
of
\alpha_{i}\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
to be the
n
-vector with
1
in its
i
-th entry,
-1
in the
(i+1)
-st, and
0
elsewhere. We write
\Delta^{\vee}=\{\alpha_{1}^{\vee},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1}^{\vee}\}
;
\Delta^{\vee}
forms a basis of
\mathfrak{a}
. Let
\widehat{\Delta}=\{\varpi_{1},\ldots,\varpi_{n-1}\}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^{*}
be the dual basis of
\Delta^{\vee}
; the elements
\varpi_{i}(=\varpi_{\alpha_{i}})
are in fact
\sum_{k=1}^{i}\textbf{e}_{k}
, and are referred to as
fundamental weights
.
Analogously, let
\widehat{\Delta}^{\vee}=\{\varpi_{1}^{\vee},\ldots,\varpi_{n-1}^{\vee}\}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}
be the dual basis of
\Delta
; here
\varpi_{i}^{\vee}=\sum_{k=1}^{i}\textbf{e}_{k}-\frac{i}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\textbf{e}_{k}
. This is the set of the
fundamental coweights
.

Fix a standard parabolic
P
stabilizing a flag
0\subsetneq E_{i_{1}}\subsetneq\ldots\subsetneq E_{i_{k}}\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We define

\displaystyle\Delta^{P}=\{\alpha_{i}:i\neq i_{j}\mbox{ for any $j=1,\ldots,k$}\},

a basis of
(\mathfrak{a}^{P})^{*}
, and also define

\displaystyle\Delta_{P}

\displaystyle=\{\alpha\Big|_{\mathfrak{a}_{P}}:\alpha\in\Delta-\Delta^{P}\},

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}_{P}

\displaystyle=\{\varpi_{\alpha}\Big|_{\mathfrak{a}_{P}}:\alpha\in\Delta-\Delta^{P}\},

which are bases of
(\mathfrak{a}_{P})^{*}
.
These have dual bases

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}_{P}^{\vee}

\displaystyle=\{\varpi_{\alpha}^{\vee}:\alpha\in\Delta_{P}\},

\displaystyle\Delta_{P}^{\vee}

\displaystyle=\{\alpha^{\vee}:\alpha\in\Delta_{P}\}

of
\mathfrak{a}_{P}
, respectively.

In general, for parabolics
P_{1}\subseteq P_{2}
, we define

\displaystyle\Delta_{P_{1}}^{P_{2}}

\displaystyle=\{\alpha\Big|_{\mathfrak{a}_{P_{1}}^{P_{2}}}:\alpha\in\Delta^{P_{2}}-\Delta^{P_{1}}\},

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}_{P_{1}}^{P_{2}}

\displaystyle=\{\varpi\Big|_{\mathfrak{a}_{P_{1}}^{P_{2}}}:\varpi\in\widehat{\Delta}_{P_{1}}-\widehat{\Delta}_{P_{2}}\},

both bases of
(\mathfrak{a}_{P_{1}}^{P_{2}})^{*}
, and we define
(\widehat{\Delta}_{P_{1}}^{P_{2}})^{\vee},(\Delta_{P_{1}}^{P_{2}})^{\vee}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}_{P_{1}}^{P_{2}}
to be their dual bases, respectively.

2.4.
The pseudo-Eisenstein series
E_{f}

From now on, fix
k
and the corresponding maximal parabolic
P=P(k)
stabilizing the flag
0\subseteq E_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The right action of
G
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
extend naturally to
\wedge^{k}\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{e}_{n-k+1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\mathbf{e}_{n}\in\wedge^{k}\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

To a function
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for which the Mellin transform exists and the Mellin inversion formula applies,
we associate a pseudo-Eisenstein series

(2.2)

E_{P,f}(g)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma\cap P\backslash\Gamma}f(\|\mathbf{e}\gamma g\|).

If
P
is clear from the context, we write
E_{P,f}=E_{f}
.
Using the Mellin inversion formula

f(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathrm{Re}\,s=c}Mf(s)x^{-s}ds

for any appropriate
c
, we can rewrite (
2.2
) as

(2.3)

E_{f}(g)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathrm{Re}\,s=c}Mf(s)E_{P}(s,g)ds,

where

(2.4)

E_{P}(s,g)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma\cap P\backslash\Gamma}\|\mathbf{e}\gamma g\|^{-s}.

By e.g.,
[
Kim23
, Lemma 4.1]
,
E_{P}(s,g)
converges absolutely whenever
\mathrm{Re}\,s>n
.
E_{P}(s,g)
in fact admits meromorphic continuation to
\mathbb{C}
, by Proposition
2.1
below.

2.5.
Borel Eisenstein series

For
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
in the
Godement range
of absolute convergence i.e. satisfying
\alpha^{\vee}_{i}(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda)>1
for all
1\leq i<n
, the Borel Eisenstein series
E_{B}(\lambda,g)
is defined to be

E_{B}(\lambda,g)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma\cap B\backslash\Gamma}a(\gamma g)^{\lambda+\rho}.

The right-hand side converges absolutely in the Godement range.
The theory of Eisenstein series shows that
E_{B}
admits meromorphic continuation to
\mathfrak{a}_{\mathbb{C}}
, with finitely many hyperplane singularities. In particular, it can be shown by the Langlands constant term formula that
E_{B}(-\rho,g)=1
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For
\mathrm{Re}\,s>n
, the equality
E_{P}(s,g)=E_{B}(\lambda,g)
holds with the choice

(2.5)

\lambda=\lambda(s)\equiv(-n,-n+1,\ldots,-k-1;-s-k,-s-k+1,\ldots,-s-1).

Proof.

For
\lambda\equiv(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
in the Godement range, we have

E_{B}(\lambda,g)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma\cap P\backslash\Gamma}\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma\cap B\backslash\Gamma\cap P}a(\gamma^{\prime}\gamma g)^{\lambda+\rho}.

Write
a^{(k)}=a_{1}\cdots a_{k},a^{(n-k)}=a_{k+1}\cdots a_{n}
. Let
\lambda^{(k)}\equiv(\lambda^{(k)}_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{(k)}_{k},0,\ldots,0)
and
\rho^{(k)}\equiv(k,k-1,\ldots,1,0,\ldots,0)
. Similarly, let
\lambda^{(-k)}\equiv(0,\ldots,0,\lambda^{(-k)}_{k+1},\ldots,\lambda^{(-k)}_{n})
and
\rho^{(-k)}\equiv(0,\ldots,0,n-k,\ldots,1)
.
Then we can rewrite

(2.6)

E_{B}(\lambda,g)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma\cap P\backslash\Gamma}\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma\cap B\backslash\Gamma\cap P}a^{(k)}(\gamma^{\prime}\gamma g)^{\lambda^{(k)}+\rho^{(k)}}a^{(n-k)}(\gamma^{\prime}\gamma g)^{\lambda^{(-k)}+\rho^{(-k)}}a(\gamma^{\prime}\gamma g)^{\aleph}

for
\aleph=(\lambda+\rho)-(\lambda^{(k)}+\rho^{(k)})-(\lambda^{(-k)}+\rho^{(-k)})
.

Choose
\lambda,\lambda^{(k)},\lambda^{(-k)}
so that the following conditions are satisfied: (i)
\lambda
,
\lambda^{(k)}
and
\lambda^{(-k)}
are in the Godement range of the absolute convergence of
E_{B}
,
E_{B}^{(k)}
and
E_{B}^{(n-k)}
, the Borel Eisenstein series in
\mathrm{SL}(n)
,
\mathrm{SL}(k)
and
\mathrm{SL}(n-k)
, respectively (ii)
\aleph\equiv(0,\ldots,0,-s,\ldots,-s)
. It is easy to see that such choices can indeed be made for
\mathrm{Re}\,s>n
. Then the inner sum over
\gamma^{\prime}
in (
2.6
) is equal to

a(\gamma g)^{\aleph}E_{B}^{(k)}(\lambda_{k},\gamma g)E_{B}^{(n-k)}(\lambda_{n-k},\gamma g),

since
a(\gamma^{\prime}\gamma g)^{\aleph}=a(\gamma g)^{\aleph}
. Invoking the meromorphic continuation of the Eisenstein series, and setting
\lambda^{(k)}=-\rho_{k}
and
\lambda^{(-k)}=-\rho_{n-k}
, this is equal to
a(\gamma g)^{\aleph}=\|\mathbf{e}\gamma g\|^{-s}
, and thus (
2.6
) coincides with
E_{P}(s,g)
.
∎

2.6.
Truncation

For a locally bounded function
\phi
on
\Gamma\backslash G
and a standard parabolic
P
, we define
\phi_{P}
, the
constant term
of
\phi
along
P
by

\phi_{P}(g)=\int_{\Gamma\cap{N_{P}}\backslash N_{P}}\phi(ng)dn

where
dn
is the Haar measure on
N_{P}
with unit volume on
\Gamma\cap{N_{P}}\backslash N_{P}
. Then for sufficiently regular
C\in\mathfrak{a}
, i.e.
\alpha_{i}(C)\gg 0
for all
1\leq i<n
, the
Arthur truncation

\Lambda^{C}\phi
of
\phi
is given by

(2.7)

\Lambda^{C}\phi(g)=\sum_{P\supseteq P_{0}}(-1)^{r(P)}\sum_{\delta\in\Gamma\cap P\backslash\Gamma}\phi_{P}(\delta g)\widehat{\tau}_{P}(H_{P}(\delta g)-C),

where
\widehat{\tau}_{P}:\mathfrak{a}_{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is the characteristic function of the obtuse Weyl chamber

(2.8)

\{t\in\mathfrak{a}_{P}:\varpi(t)\geq 0\mbox{ for all }\varpi\in\widehat{\Delta}_{P}\}.

\Lambda^{C}
is a self-adjoint and idempotent operator on the space of functions on
\Gamma\backslash G
.

Our later argument necessitates a deeper understanding of the Arthur truncation. Let us recall the parts of the theory that are relevant to us.

Following Arthur
[
Art05
, Sec. 8]
, for a standard parabolic
P
and elements
C_{1}^{P},C^{P}
of
\mathfrak{a}_{0}^{P}
, and a compact subset
\omega
of
N_{B}
, define

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}^{P}(C_{1}^{P})

\displaystyle=\{g=nak:n\in\omega,a\in\exp(\mathfrak{a}_{0}),k\in\mathrm{O}(n,\mathbb{R}),

\displaystyle\hskip 19.91692pt\alpha(H_{0}^{P}(g)-C_{1}^{P})>0\ \forall\alpha\in\Delta_{0}^{P}\},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}^{P}(C_{1}^{P},C^{P})

\displaystyle=\{g\in\mathfrak{S}^{P}(C_{1}^{P}):\varpi(H_{0}^{P}(g)-C^{P})<0\ \forall\varpi\in\widehat{\Delta}_{0}^{P}\}.

By reduction theory, we can choose
\omega
and
C_{1}
so that
\mathfrak{S}^{G}(C_{1})
contains a fundamental domain for
\Gamma\backslash G
; let us fix these once and for all throughout the paper. In the discussion below, we take
C_{1}^{P}=(C_{1})_{0}^{P}
and
C^{P}=(C)_{0}^{P}
; we often omit these subscripts, as per the convention we adopted in Section
2.2
.

Let
F^{P}(g,C)
be the characteristic function of the projection of
\mathfrak{S}^{P}(C_{1},C)
onto
\Gamma\cap P\backslash G
. Also for
H\in\mathfrak{a}_{P_{1}}
let

(2.9)

\sigma_{P_{1}}^{P_{2}}(H)=\sum_{Q:Q\supseteq P_{2}}(-1)^{r(Q)-r(P_{2})}\tau_{P_{1}}^{Q}(H)\widehat{\tau}_{Q}(H),

where
Q
runs over the standard parabolics, and
\tau_{P_{1}}^{Q}
is defined as in (
2.8
) by replacing
\mathfrak{a}_{P}
by
\mathfrak{a}_{P_{1}}^{Q}
and
\varpi\in\widehat{\Delta}_{P}
by
\alpha\in\Delta_{P_{1}}^{Q}
. It turns out that
\sigma
is a characteristic function of a set in
\mathfrak{a}_{P_{1}}
; more precisely, by the Möbius inversion formula, it holds that for
P\supseteq P_{1}

\tau_{P_{1}}^{P}(H)\widehat{\tau}_{P}(H)=\sum_{P_{2}:P_{2}\supseteq P}\sigma_{P_{1}}^{P_{2}}(H)

(see
[
Art05
, (8.2)]
). In particular, for each
P_{1}
, the supports of
\sigma_{P_{1}}^{P_{2}}
, for
P_{2}\supseteq P_{1}
, form a partition of the obtuse Weyl chamber (
2.8
) in
\mathfrak{a}_{P_{1}}
.

Then, for locally bounded
\phi:\Gamma\backslash G\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, its truncation
\Lambda^{C}\phi(g)
can be written as

\sum_{P_{1}\subseteq P_{2}}\sum_{\delta\in\Gamma\cap P_{1}\backslash\Gamma}F^{P_{1}}(\delta x,C)\sigma_{P_{1}}^{P_{2}}(H_{P_{1}}(\delta x)-C)\phi_{P_{1},P_{2}}(\delta x),

where
P_{1}\subseteq P_{2}
run over standard parabolic subgroups, and

\phi_{P_{1},P_{2}}(y)=\sum_{\{P:P_{1}\subseteq P\subseteq P_{2}\}}(-1)^{r(P)}\phi_{P}(y).

(See Chs. 8 and 13 of
[
Art05
]
for proof, especially pages 37-43.) Let us write
\chi_{P_{1},P_{2}}^{C}(x)=F^{P_{1}}(x,C)\sigma_{P_{1}}^{P_{2}}(H_{P_{1}}(x)-C)
, so that

(2.10)

\Lambda^{C}\phi(x)=\sum_{P_{1}\subseteq P_{2}}\sum_{\delta\in\Gamma\cap P_{1}\backslash\Gamma}\chi_{P_{1},P_{2}}^{C}(\delta x)\phi_{P_{1},P_{2}}(\delta x).

In particular,
\chi_{G,G}^{C}
is the characteristic function of the “untruncated region,” on which
\Lambda^{C}\phi(x)=\phi(x)
.

This reformulation of the Arthur truncation can in particular be used to prove that, if
\phi
is of uniform moderate growth, then
\Lambda^{C}\phi
is of rapid decay — see e.g.,
[
Art05
, Proposition 13.2]
for a proof.

Let us also define the
harsh truncation

\mathrm{H}^{C}\phi(g)=\begin{cases}\phi(g)&g\in\mathrm{supp}\,\chi^{C}_{G,G}\\
0&g\not\in\mathrm{supp}\,\chi^{C}_{G,G}.\end{cases}

Intuitively, this may be the more useful form of truncation for applications to the geometry of numbers. The major drawback, however, is that it lacks a tractable inner product formula, unlike the Arthur truncation.

2.7.
Maass-Selberg relations

For Borel Eisenstein series
E_{B}(\lambda_{1},g)
and
E_{B}(\lambda_{2},g)
, the Maass-Selberg relation is given by

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}\Lambda^{C}E_{B}(\lambda_{1},g)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{C}E_{B}(\lambda_{2},g)dg

\displaystyle=\sum_{w_{1},w_{2}\in W}\frac{e^{\langle C,w_{1}\lambda_{1}+w_{2}\lambda_{2}\rangle}}{\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta}\langle\alpha^{\vee},w_{1}\lambda_{1}+w_{2}\lambda_{2}\rangle}M(w_{1},\lambda_{1})M(w_{2},\lambda_{2}),

where

M(w,\lambda)=\prod_{\alpha>0\atop w\alpha<0}c(\langle\alpha^{\vee},\lambda\rangle),\ c(z):=\frac{\xi(z)}{\xi(1+z)}.

This formula is initially stated for sufficiently regular
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
, but it can be extended further by meromorphic continuation.
We note that
c(z)
has a simple zero at
z=-1
and a simple pole at
z=1
. Also,
c(z)=(c(-z))^{-1}
.

In our later computation, we choose

C=\log T\cdot\rho^{\vee}:=\log T\cdot\left(\frac{n-1}{2},\frac{n-3}{2},\ldots,\frac{1-n}{2}\right)

for a sufficiently large
T\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, so that

e^{\langle C,w_{1}\lambda_{1}+w_{2}\lambda_{2}\rangle}=T^{\langle\rho^{\vee},w_{1}\lambda_{1}+w_{2}\lambda_{2}\rangle}.

3.
Growth properties of
\Lambda^{C}E_{f}

3.1.
Uniform moderate growth and rapid decay

Recall that a
N_{B}\cap\Gamma
-invariant complex-valued function
\phi
on
G
is said to be
of moderate growth of exponent
\lambda
if it holds that
|\phi(g)|\ll_{C_{1}}a(g)^{\lambda}
for all
g\in\mathfrak{S}^{B}(C_{1})
. Recall also that
\phi
is said to be
of rapid decay
if it is of moderate growth of any exponent
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
, and is said to be of
uniform
moderate growth of exponent
\lambda
, or
\lambda
-UMG for short, if
\phi
is smooth and
L\phi
is of moderate growth of the same exponent
\lambda
for any
L\in U(\mathfrak{g})
.

In this section, and the next, we study the growth properties of the truncated function
\Lambda^{C}E_{f}
, with the goal of showing that it does not differ much from the “harsh truncation” (Theorem
3.7
below). In doing so we need to control the growth of
E_{P}(s,g)
simultaneously for all
s
lying on a vertical line. The following proposition accomplishes this goal.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
\chi\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
and
L\in U(\mathfrak{g})
. Then

|La(g)^{s\chi}|\leq|p(s)a(g)^{s\chi}|

for some polynomial
p(s)
whose degree is equal to the degree of
L
.
p(s)
depends on the choice of
\chi
and
L
.

Proof.

Let us first consider the simplest case, in which
L=X\in\mathfrak{g}
. The chain rule implies that

Xa(g)^{s\chi}=-sa(g)^{(s-1)\chi}\cdot Xa(g)^{\chi}.

Taking the NAK decomposition
g=nak
, we can write

Xa(g)^{\chi}=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg\rvert_{t=0}a(nae^{tkXk^{-1}}k)^{\chi}=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg\rvert_{t=0}a(ae^{tkXk^{-1}})^{\chi}.

Again taking the NAK decomposition
n(t)a(t)k(t)
of
e^{tkXk^{-1}}
, we find that the above equals

\frac{d}{dt}\bigg\rvert_{t=0}(a\cdot a(t))^{\chi}=a^{\chi}\cdot\frac{d}{dt}\bigg\rvert_{t=0}a(t)^{\chi}.

This quantity, of course, depends on
X
and
k
. However, since
k
lies in a compact subset of
G
(namely
\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{R})
), its size can be bounded independently of
k
. Hence we conclude

\left|Xa(g)^{s\chi}\right|\ll_{X}\left|sa(g)^{s\chi}\right|.

We proceed similarly for general
L\in U(\mathfrak{g})
.
It suffices to consider the case
L=X_{d}\cdots X_{1}
,
X_{i}\in\mathfrak{g}
, and to prove that
|La(g)^{\chi}|\ll_{L}|a(g)^{\chi}|
. Again write
g=nak
for the NAK decomposition of
g
, and recursively take, for
i=d,\ldots,1
and
t_{d},\ldots,t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
,
n_{i}(t_{d},\ldots,t_{i})a_{i}(t_{d},\ldots,t_{i})k_{i}(t_{d},\ldots,t_{i})
to be the NAK decomposition of
\exp(t_{i}l_{i}X_{i}l_{i}^{-1})
, where

l_{i}=k_{i+1}(t_{i+1})\cdots k_{d}(t_{d})\cdot k.

Arguing similarly as in
d=1
case above, we obtain

\displaystyle X_{d}\cdots X_{1}a(g)^{\chi}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt_{d}}\bigg\rvert_{t_{d}=0}\cdots\frac{d}{dt_{1}}\bigg\rvert_{t_{1}=0}(a\cdot a_{d}\cdots a_{1})^{\chi}

\displaystyle=a^{\chi}\cdot\frac{d}{dt_{d}}\bigg\rvert_{t_{d}=0}\cdots\frac{d}{dt_{1}}\bigg\rvert_{t_{1}=0}(a_{d}\cdots a_{1})^{\chi}.

Each of the derivatives in the last line depends on
L
and
k
, but again, since
k
lies in a compact set, its size is bounded independently of
k
, and thus of
g
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark
.

From Proposition
3.1
and the Leibniz integral rule, it follows that, for any
L\in U(\mathfrak{g})
and
s\in\mathbb{C}
with
\mathrm{Re}\,s=c>n
,

|LE_{P}(s,g)|\leq|p_{L}(s)E_{P}(c,g)|,

where
p_{L}(s)
is some polynomial whose degree is equal to that of
L
. Since a Borel Eisenstein series, in particular
E_{P}(c,g)
, is of moderate growth, say of exponent
\chi
, by well-known results
2
2

2

see e.g.,
[
MW95
, I.2.17]
or
[
GMP17
, Appendix A]
; it can also be derived from the result of
[
Kim23
]
, with a specific value for
\chi
.
, this implies that

(3.1)

|LE_{P}(s,g)|\ll|p_{L}(s)a(g)^{\chi}|

for any
s
on the line
\mathrm{Re}\,s=c>n
. This provides the desired uniform control over all
E_{P}(s,g)
on a vertical line.

In fact, we will later need a slightly stronger statement than (
3.1
): namely

(3.2)

|LE_{B}(\lambda(s)+\varepsilon\rho,g)|\ll|p_{L}(s,\varepsilon)a(g)^{\chi}|,

where
\lambda(s)
is as in the statement of Proposition
2.1
,
\mathrm{Re}\,s=c>n
as before,
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
lies in a small neighborhood of
0
, and
\chi
is some element of
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
depending on
c
. This follows from the fact that
|La(g)^{\lambda(s)+\varepsilon\rho}|\ll|p_{L}(s,\varepsilon)a(g)^{\lambda(s)+\varepsilon\rho}|
, which in turn follows from Proposition
3.1
.

The uniform moderate growth of
E_{f}
and the rapid decay of
\Lambda^{C}E_{f}
follow quickly from (
3.1
).

Proposition 3.2
.

For
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
Mf(s)
is of rapid decay on the vertical line
\mathrm{Re}\,s=c>n
,
E_{f}(g)
is of uniform moderate growth of the same exponent as
E_{P}(c,g)
. In addition,
\Lambda^{C}E_{f}
is of rapid decay.

Proof.

By the Leibniz integral rule and (
3.1
),

\displaystyle\left|LE_{f}(g)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathrm{Re}\,s=c}Mf(s)LE_{P}(s,g)ds\right|

\displaystyle\leq|E_{P}(c,g)|\int_{\mathrm{Re}\,s=c}\left|p_{L}(s)Mf(s)\right|ds.

The integral converges by the rapid decay of
Mf(s)
. Thus
E_{f}
is of uniform moderate growth of the same exponent as
E_{P}(c,g)
.

The rapid decay statement follows from the fact that the Arthur truncation of a function of uniform moderate growth is of rapid decay, as mentioned in Section
2.6
.
∎

3.2.
Size in the truncated region

The main goal of this section is to prove Theorem
3.7
below, which asserts that, for all
\phi
of uniform moderate growth, and for all sufficiently large
T
,
\Lambda^{C}\phi
is arbitrarily small outside
\mathrm{supp}\,\chi_{G,G}^{C}
, in which
\Lambda^{C}\phi(g)=\phi(g)
. It is a refinement of
[
Art05
, Proposition 13.2]
— which shows that
\Lambda^{C}\phi
is of rapid decay, but does not guarantee it is small everywhere in the truncated region — with applications to the geometry of numbers in mind. In addition, we track the constants implicit in our estimates in case
\phi=E_{P}(s,g)
or
E_{B}(\lambda(s)+\varepsilon\rho,g)
with varying values of
\mathrm{Im}\,s
.

We need some lemmas to proceed. Recall that we are taking

C=C(T)=\log T\cdot\left(\frac{n-1}{2},\frac{n-3}{2},\ldots,\frac{1-n}{2}\right)

for our computations, though any sufficiently regular element of
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
will do. We also assume
P_{1}\subseteq P_{2}
, not both

M(w_{1}w_{2},\lambda)=M(w_{1},w_{2}\lambda)M(w_{2},\lambda)

for any
w_{1},w_{2}\in W
.
Let
w_{*}\in W
be the unique element such that

\displaystyle w_{*}(n-k+1)

\displaystyle<w_{*}(n-k+2)<\ldots<w_{*}(n)

\displaystyle<w_{*}(1)<w_{*}(2)<\ldots<w_{*}(n-k).

Also let
\tilde{\rho}
be such that
w_{*}\tilde{\lambda}(n)=-\tilde{\rho}
; observe that
\tilde{\rho}=\rho+O(\varepsilon)
indeed. (
4.4
) implies

M(ww_{*},\tilde{\lambda})=M(w,-\tilde{\rho})M(w_{*},\tilde{\lambda})

for any
w\in W
, and thus the left-hand side of (

) is equal to

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{M(w_{*},\tilde{\lambda}(n))}\sum_{w\in W}\frac{e^{\langle C,w\tilde{\lambda}(n)-\rho\rangle}}{\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta}\langle\alpha^{\vee},w\tilde{\lambda}(n)-\rho\rangle}M(w,\tilde{\lambda}(n)).

Here, one computes that

M(w_{*},\tilde{\lambda}(n))=\xi(1+(n-1)\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{\xi(2+O(\varepsilon))\cdots\xi(n-k+O(\varepsilon))}{\xi(k+1+O(\varepsilon))\cdots\xi(n+O(\varepsilon))}.

Therefore, it suffices to show that, for each
w\in W
,

(4.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}(n-1)\varepsilon\cdot\frac{e^{\langle C,w\tilde{\lambda}(n)-\rho\rangle}}{\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta}\langle\alpha^{\vee},w\tilde{\lambda}(n)-\rho\rangle}M(w,\tilde{\lambda}(n))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\mathrm{Res}_{s=n+O(\varepsilon)}\frac{e^{\langle C,w\tilde{\lambda}-\rho\rangle}}{\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta}\langle\alpha^{\vee},w\tilde{\lambda}-\rho\rangle}M(w,\tilde{\lambda}).

Before we start, let us remark that, in the expression

\frac{e^{\langle C,w\tilde{\lambda}-\rho\rangle}}{\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta}\langle\alpha^{\vee},w\tilde{\lambda}-\rho\rangle}M(w,\tilde{\lambda}),

any potential singularity in the
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
limit that is independent of
s
is a removable one. First of all,
M(w,\tilde{\lambda})
induces no such singularity, since there exists no positive root
\alpha
such that
\langle\alpha^{\vee},\lambda\rangle=1
. As for the denominator terms, if
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w\tilde{\lambda}-\rho\rangle=O(\varepsilon)
for some
j
, then
w^{-1}(j)
and
w^{-1}(j+1)
must be adjacent and in the same block, and thus
w^{-1}(j)>w^{-1}(j+1)
; this causes
c(\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{w^{-1}(j+1)},\tilde{\lambda}\rangle)=c(-1+O(\varepsilon))=O(\varepsilon)
to appear as a factor of
M(w,\tilde{\lambda})
, which offsets the size of
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w\tilde{\lambda}-\rho\rangle
.

Therefore, for either side of (
4.5
) to yield a nonzero value, there are only two mutually exclusive possibilities:
w(1)+1=w(n)
or
w(1)>w(n)
— note that this also implies that all poles of (
4.2
) at
s=n+O(\varepsilon)
are simple.

In case
w(1)+1=w(n)
, the denominator on the left-hand side of (
4.5
) has a factor
(n-1)\varepsilon
; in case
w(1)>w(n)
,
M(w,\tilde{\lambda})
contains the factor
c(\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{1n},\tilde{\lambda}(n)\rangle)
, which has the factor
\xi(1+(n-1)\varepsilon)=1/((n-1)\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2}))
. On the right-hand side of (
4.5
), the counterpart of these corresponding terms are
s-n+(n-1)\varepsilon
and
\xi(s-n+1+(n-1)\varepsilon)
, respectively. From these observations, it is clear that the equality (

) holds.
∎

Proposition
4.4
accounts for the main term appearing in the statement of Theorem
4.1
. The error term arises from the integral

(4.6)

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathrm{Re}\,s=n-\eta}Mf(s)\sum_{w\in W}\frac{e^{\langle C,w\tilde{\lambda}-\rho\rangle}}{\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta}\langle\alpha^{\vee},w\tilde{\lambda}-\rho\rangle}M(w,\tilde{\lambda})ds

that remains after the contour shift. The only obstacle to bounding this integral is the possibility that
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w\tilde{\lambda}-\rho\rangle=O(\varepsilon)
for some
j
. This means that
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w\lambda\rangle=1
, and thus
w^{-1}(j)=w^{-1}(j+1)+1
and

. Hence the factor
c(\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{w^{-1}(j+1)},\tilde{\lambda}\rangle)=O(\varepsilon)
appears in
M(w,\tilde{\lambda})
, which balances out the denominator term
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w\tilde{\lambda}-\rho\rangle
. Hence the above integral converges.

It is easy to show that
\langle\rho^{\vee},w\lambda\rangle
is maximized when the entries of
w\lambda
are sorted in descending order. Thus the (real part of)
T
-degree of the above integral is maximized when

w\lambda(n-\eta)=\rho+(0,\ldots,0;\underbrace{\eta,\ldots,\eta}_{\mbox{\tiny$k$ times}}),

i.e.
w
“reverses order in each block,” to

\eta\left(\frac{-n+1}{2}+\cdots+\frac{-n+2k-1}{2}\right)=-\frac{\eta k(n-k)}{2}.

Therefore (

O_{n,f}(T^{-\frac{\eta k(n-k)}{2}})
. This completes the proof of Theorem
4.1
.

4.3.
Proof of Proposition
4.2

Recall from (
4.3
) that

\mathrm{vol}^{C}(\Gamma\backslash G)=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\sum_{w\in W}\frac{e^{\langle C,-w\tilde{\rho}-\rho\rangle}}{\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta}\langle\alpha^{\vee},-w\tilde{\rho}-\rho\rangle}M(w,-\tilde{\rho}).

Suppose that
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},-w\tilde{\rho}-\rho\rangle=O(\varepsilon)
. Then
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w\rho\rangle=-1
, so
w^{-1}(j)=w^{-1}(j+1)+1
. This causes
c(\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{w^{-1}(j+1)},-\tilde{\rho}\rangle)=O(\varepsilon)
to appear as a factor of
M(w,-\tilde{\rho})
, balancing out the growth of
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},-w\tilde{\rho}-\rho\rangle
. In particular, the above limit converges.

The flip side of this is that, if
c(\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{h},-\tilde{\rho}\rangle)=O(\varepsilon)
appears as a factor of
M(w,-\tilde{\rho})
, then the summand corresponding to
w\in W
vanishes as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
unless
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{w(h+1)},-w\tilde{\rho}-\rho\rangle=O(\varepsilon)
. This greatly limits the number of
w\in W
that contribute nontrivially to the formula (
4.3
): if
w(h+1)<w(h)
, then
w(h+1)+1=w(h)
. Let us say that
w\in W
is
permissible
if it satisfies this condition.

Permissible permutations lead to the following combinatorial consideration. Partition an ordered set
A=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
into
p
ordered contiguous blocks
A_{1},\ldots,A_{p}
, so that
A=A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{p}
; here, taking union is order-sensitive too. Also, for an ordered set
S
, write
S^{r}
for the set consisting of the same elements but in reversed order; for example, if
S=\{1,2,3\}
, then
S^{r}=\{3,2,1\}
.

As an ordered set,
-\rho
can be naturally identified with
A
. Each permissible
w\in W
must take
-\rho
into an ordered set
\varphi
of the form

\varphi=A_{1}^{r}\cup A_{2}^{r}\cup\cdots\cup A_{p}^{r}.

If
\varphi_{j}<\varphi_{j+1}
, then
\langle\rho^{\vee},(j\ j+1)\varphi\rangle>\langle\rho^{\vee},\varphi\rangle
— the difference is precisely
\varphi_{j+1}-\varphi_{j}
. From this, we see that
\langle\rho^{\vee},\varphi\rangle
increases whenever we move
A_{i}^{r}
to the left of
A_{i-1}^{r}
, and then concatenate the resulting
A_{i}^{r}\cup A_{i-1}^{r}
into a single block. In particular, for a given
\varphi
, there exists
\varphi^{\prime}
with a smaller number of blocks, also coming from a permissible permutation, such that
\langle\rho^{\vee},\varphi^{\prime}\rangle>\langle\rho^{\vee},\varphi^{\prime}\rangle
.

Therefore, the highest
T
-degree
\langle\rho^{\vee},-w\tilde{\rho}-\rho\rangle
occurs when
p=1
with
-w\tilde{\rho}=\rho
, and the degree is zero. Clearly, the next highest
T
-degree occurs when
p=2
, and a moment’s reflection shows that it happens when

\varphi=(n-1,\ldots,2,1,n)\mbox{ or }(1,n,\ldots,3,2),

and the corresponding
T
-degree is
-n(n-1)/2
. In fact, it can also be shown that the contributions from these two subleading
\varphi
’s are identical and thus do not cancel each other out; hence this error estimate is sharp.

5.1.
Formulation

Let

\displaystyle\equiv(-n,\ldots,-k_{1}-1;-s_{1}-k_{1},\ldots,-s_{1}-1),

\displaystyle\equiv(-n,\ldots,-k_{2}-1;-s_{2}-k_{2},\ldots,-s_{2}-1),

The goal of this section is to estimate the truncated correlation

\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}\Lambda^{C}E_{P_{1},f_{1}}(g)E_{P_{2},f_{2}}(g)dg.

The statement below provides our starting point.

Proposition 5.1
.

Continue with the above notations.
Then (

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{2}}\int_{\mathrm{Re}\,s_{1}=c_{1}}\int_{\mathrm{Re}\,s_{2}=c_{2}}Mf_{1}(s_{1})Mf_{2}(s_{2})

\displaystyle\sum_{w_{1},w_{2}\in W}\frac{e^{\langle C,w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\lambda_{2}\rangle}}{\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta}\langle\alpha^{\vee},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\lambda_{2}\rangle}M(w_{1},\tilde{\lambda}_{1})M(w_{2},\lambda_{2})ds_{2}ds_{1}.

Proof.

First we claim that, for any maximal parabolic
P

By (

In the last expression, the order of the two integrals can be exchanged, since

We now expand the square in (

w_{2}\lambda_{2}(n-\eta_{2})=\rho+(0,\ldots,0;\underbrace{\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{2}}_{\mbox{\tiny$k_{2}$ times}}).

From this, it can be computed that (
5.7
) in the
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
limit is bounded by
O_{n,f_{1},f_{2},\eta_{2}}(T^{-\eta_{2}k_{2}(n-k_{2})/2})
.

5.4.
The remaining integral

We conclude the estimate of (
5.1
) by proving that the integral

(5.8)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{2}}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{Re}\,s_{1}=c_{1}}\int_{\mathrm{Re}\,s_{2}=c_{2}}Mf_{1}(s_{1})Mf_{2}(s_{2})

\displaystyle\sum_{w_{1},w_{2}\in W}\frac{e^{\langle C,w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}}{\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta}\langle\alpha^{\vee},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}M(w_{1},\tilde{\lambda}_{1})M(w_{2},\tilde{\lambda}_{2})ds_{2}ds_{1}

converges, where
c_{1}=n-\eta_{1},c_{2}=n-\eta_{2}
. Since
M(w_{i},\tilde{\lambda}_{i})
cannot diverge by our choice of
c_{1},c_{2}
, the only obstacle to its convergence is the possibility that
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle
equals a multiple of
\varepsilon
for some
j=1,\ldots,n-1
. For this to happen, it is necessary that
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle
is independent of both
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
; otherwise, its real part is away from zero by at least
\min(\eta_{1},1-\eta_{2},\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})
.

Suppose first that
w_{2}^{-1}(j)+1\neq w_{2}^{-1}(j+1)
.
Then there exists
j^{\prime}\neq n-k_{2}
such that
w_{2}(j^{\prime})>w_{2}(j^{\prime}+1)
, implying that
M(w_{2},\tilde{\lambda}_{2})=0
, since
c(\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j^{\prime}},\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle)=c(-1)=0
appears as its factor. Thus the summand under question is zero, regardless of
\varepsilon
.

Next suppose that
w_{2}^{-1}(j)+1=w_{2}^{-1}(j+1)
. Then we must have
w_{1}^{-1}(j+1)+1=w_{1}^{-1}(j)
; in other words,
w_{1}
flips the sign of
\alpha_{w_{1}^{-1}(j+1)}
. Hence
M(w_{1},\tilde{\lambda}_{1})
begets the factor
c(\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{w_{1}^{-1}(j+1)},\tilde{\lambda}_{1}\rangle)=c(-1+O(\varepsilon))=O(\varepsilon)
, which balances out the growth of
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. This proves that for every
j
such that
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle=O(\varepsilon)
, there corresponds a factor of
M(w_{1},\tilde{\lambda}_{1})
that cancels it out. This proves that (
5.8
) converges as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.

As for the size of (
5.8
) in terms of
T
, first off we know that it increases with
T
in general, by
[
Kim22
, Corollary to Theorem 4]
. (Of course, the cases
k_{1}=k_{2}=1
and
k_{1}=k_{2}=n-1
are well-known exceptions.) The trivial upper bound would be
O(T^{\langle\rho^{\vee},2\rho\rangle})=O(T^{n(n^{2}-1)/6})
. The exponent can be slightly improved by observing that, if
w_{2}(j)>w_{2}(j+1)
for
j\neq n-k_{2}
, then the corresponding term in (
5.8
) equals zero. Taking this into account, the
w_{2}
yielding the maximum
T
-degree is in fact the trivial permutation, so that

w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}(n-\eta_{2})=-\rho+(\underbrace{n,\ldots,n}_{n-k_{2}};\underbrace{\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{2}}_{k_{2}}).

On the other hand, the candidate
w_{1}
yielding the maximum
T
-degree is

w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}(n-\eta_{1})=\rho+(0,\ldots,0;\underbrace{\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{1}}_{k_{1}}).

With these choices of
w_{1},w_{2}
, it may be said that (
5.8
) is of order
O(T^{\kappa})
, where

\displaystyle\kappa:=\langle\rho^{\vee},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}(n-\eta_{1})+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}(n-\eta_{2})\rangle.

For instance, when
k_{1}=k_{2}=k
, we can compute

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=\frac{k(n-k)}{2}(n-\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})<\frac{n^{3}}{8},

which is indeed smaller than
n(n^{2}-1)/6
for all
n\geq 3
and
1\leq k\leq n-1
. This completes the proof that (
5.8
) contributes
O_{n,f_{1},f_{2},\eta_{1},\eta_{2}}\left(T^{\kappa}\right)
.

6.
The cancellation of the poles

6.1.
The simplest case

We continue from the context of the discussion in Section
5.3
. Recall that we are considering the
s_{2}
-residues of of Type I and Type II, where
s_{1}
and
\varepsilon\neq 0
are fixed. We write
z=s_{2}-n-a\varepsilon
for a residue of Type I, and
z=s_{2}-s_{1}+1-a\varepsilon
for a residue of Type II.

Let us first discuss the simplest case in which
\langle\alpha_{j}^{\vee},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle=\pm z
for precisely one value of
j
. Write

(6.1)

\tilde{I}(w_{1},w_{2})=\frac{e^{\langle C,w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}}{\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta}\langle\alpha^{\vee},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}M(w_{1},\tilde{\lambda}_{1})M(w_{2},\tilde{\lambda}_{2}),

and
w_{i}^{\prime}=(j\ j+1)w_{i}
. We will show that the poles of
\tilde{I}(w_{1},w_{2})
and
\tilde{I}(w_{1}^{\prime},w_{2}^{\prime})
at
z=0
add up to zero.

Let us examine how each factor of
\tilde{I}(w_{1},w_{2})
changes upon replacing
w_{i}
by
w_{i}^{\prime}
. Clearly

(6.2)

\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w^{\prime}_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w^{\prime}_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle=-\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle.

Also,

\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j\pm 1},w^{\prime}_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w^{\prime}_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle=\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j\pm 1}+\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle,

and other
\langle\alpha^{\vee},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle
remain unchanged. Using these, one can also compute that

e^{\langle C,w^{\prime}_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w^{\prime}_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}=e^{\langle C,w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}\cdot T^{-\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}.

On the other hand, from the functional equation (
4.4
) of the intertwining operator
M
, we have

M(w_{1}^{\prime},\tilde{\lambda}_{1})M(w_{2}^{\prime},\tilde{\lambda}_{2})=c(\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}\rangle)c(\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle)M(w_{1},\tilde{\lambda}_{1})M(w_{2},\tilde{\lambda}_{2}).

For
z
near zero, one observes that

\displaystyle c(\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}\rangle)c(\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle)

\displaystyle=1+O(z),

\displaystyle T^{\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}

\displaystyle=1+O(z),

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j\pm 1},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}{\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j}+\alpha^{\vee}_{j\pm 1},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\pm z}{\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j\pm 1},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}\right)^{p}=1+O(z).

Therefore every individual factor of
\tilde{I}(w^{\prime}_{1},w^{\prime}_{2})
, with the exception of (
6.2
), differs from the corresponding factor of
\tilde{I}(w_{1},w_{2})
by a multiplicative factor of size
1+O(z)
.

It follows that

\tilde{I}(w_{1},w_{2})+\tilde{I}(w_{1}^{\prime},w_{2}^{\prime})=O(z\tilde{I}(w_{1},w_{2})),

which converges to a constant as
z\rightarrow 0
. This implies that the poles of
\tilde{I}(w_{1},w_{2})
and
\tilde{I}(w_{1}^{\prime},w_{2}^{\prime})
at
z=0
indeed cancel with each other, as claimed.

6.2.
Multiple poles

We proceed to consider the general case, in which
\langle\alpha_{j}^{\vee},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle=\pm z
for more than one value of
j
. We first observe that it cannot be the case for two consecutive values, say
j
and
j+1
. If this were the case, then
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle=\pm z
and
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j+1},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle=\mp z
; however, this implies that
(w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1})_{j}=(w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1})_{j+2}
, which is impossible. Thus if we write
j_{1}<\ldots<j_{r}
for all the indices such that
\langle\alpha_{j_{i}}^{\vee},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle=\pm z
, then
j_{i}-j_{i-1}\geq 2
.

Write
\tau_{i}=(j_{i}\ j_{i}+1)
, and for
e\in\{0,1\}^{r}
, write
\tau^{e}=\prod_{i}\tau_{i}^{e_{i}}
. We will show that

\sum_{e\in\{0,1\}^{r}}\tilde{I}(\tau^{e}w_{1},\tau^{e}w_{2})=O(z^{r}\tilde{I}(w_{1},w_{2})),

which immediately implies that it has zero residue at
z=0
, or equivalently
s_{2}=n+a\varepsilon
.

To this end, let us introduce a few additional notations. We write

\displaystyle\mu_{j_{i}}

\displaystyle=c(\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i}},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}\rangle)c(\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i}},w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle)\cdot T^{-\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i}},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle},

\displaystyle\underline{\Delta}_{j_{i}-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i}-1},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}{\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i}-1}+\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i}},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}\cdot{T^{\frac{1}{2}(j_{i}-1)(n-j_{i}+1)\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i}},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}},

\displaystyle\bar{\Delta}_{j_{i}+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i}+1},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}{\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i}}+\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i}+1},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}\cdot{T^{\frac{1}{2}(j_{i}+1)(n-j_{i}-1)\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i}},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}},

and
\mathcal{E}_{i}=\mu_{j_{i}}\underline{\Delta}_{j_{i}-1}\overline{\Delta}_{j_{i}+1}
. Arguments from the previous section show that
\mu_{j_{i}},\underline{\Delta}_{j_{i}-1},\overline{\Delta}_{j_{i}+1}
, and
\mathcal{E}_{i}
are all terms of size
1+O(z)
, and that

\tilde{I}(\tau_{i}w_{1},\tau_{i}w_{2})=-\tilde{I}(w_{1},w_{2})\cdot\mathcal{E}_{i}.

Informally speaking,
\underline{\Delta}_{j_{i}-1}
account for the change in the “(
j_{i}-1
)-st term” of
\tilde{I}(w_{1},w_{2})
caused by composing
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
by
\tau_{i}
; similarly for
\overline{\Delta}_{j_{i}+1}
and
\mu_{j_{i}}
.

6.2.1.
All zeros are apart

The easiest case to handle is in which
j_{i+1}-j_{i}>2
for all
i=1,\ldots,r-1
. Then we have

\tilde{I}(\tau^{e}w_{1},\tau^{e}w_{2})=\tilde{I}(w_{1},w_{2})\prod_{i:e_{i}\neq 0}(-\mathcal{E}_{i}),

that is, the errors are multiplicative. This implies that

\sum_{e\in\{0,1\}^{r}}\tilde{I}(\tau^{e}w_{1},\tau^{e}w_{2})=\tilde{I}(w_{1},w_{2})\prod_{i=1}^{r}(1-\mathcal{E}_{i})=O(z^{r}\tilde{I}(w_{1},w_{2})),

which is
O(1)
as
z\rightarrow 0
, showing that the residues indeed cancel to zero.

6.2.2.
All zeros are together

We next consider the polar opposite case in which
j_{i+1}-j_{i}=2
for all
i=1,\ldots,r-1
. The errors are no longer strictly multiplicative, which comes from the fact that both
\tau_{i}
and
\tau_{i+1}
alter
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i}+1},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle
. Still, it turns out that an analogous principle continues to hold.

For
e\in\{0,1\}^{r}
, we can write

\tilde{I}(\tau^{e}w_{1},\tau^{e}w_{2})=(-1)^{|\{i:e_{i}=1\}|}\tilde{I}(w_{1},w_{2})\cdot\mathcal{E}_{e},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{e}=\prod_{\ell=j_{1}-1}^{j_{r}+1}\mathcal{L}_{\ell},

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\ell}=\mathcal{L}_{e,\ell}=\begin{cases}\mu_{j_{i}}&\mbox{if $\ell=j_{i}$ and $e_{i}=1$}\\
\underline{\Delta}_{j_{i}-1}&\mbox{if $\ell=j_{i}-1$, and $e_{i}=1$ and $e_{i-1}$=0}\\
\overline{\Delta}_{j_{i}+1}&\mbox{if $\ell=j_{i}+1$, and $e_{i}=1$ and $e_{i+1}=0$}\\
\underline{\overline{\Delta}}_{j_{i}+1}&\mbox{if $\ell=j_{i}+1$, and $e_{i}=1$ and $e_{i+1}=1$.}\end{cases}

Here we understand
e_{0}=e_{r+1}=0
always,
\mu_{j_{i}},\underline{\Delta}_{j_{i}-1},\overline{\Delta}_{j_{i}+1}
are as earlier, and

\displaystyle\underline{\overline{\Delta}}_{j_{i}+1}=\frac{\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i}+1},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}{\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i}}+\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i}+1}+\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i+1}},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}\cdot T^{\frac{1}{2}(j_{i}+1)(n-j_{i}-1)\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i}}+\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i+1}},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle}.

Lemma 6.1
.

\underline{\overline{\Delta}}_{j_{i}+1}=\overline{\Delta}_{j_{i}+1}\underline{\Delta}_{j_{i+1}-1}+O(z^{2}).

Proof.

Suppose first that the signs of
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i}},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle
and
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i+1}},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle
are equal; without loss of generality, we assume both are equal to
z
.
One then observes that

\displaystyle\underline{\overline{\Delta}}_{j_{i}+1}-\overline{\Delta}_{j_{i}+1}\underline{\Delta}_{j_{i+1}-1}=T^{(j_{i}+1)(n-j_{i}-1)z}\left(\frac{u}{2z+u}-\left(\frac{u}{z+u}\right)^{2}\right),

where we temporarily denote

\displaystyle u=\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i}+1},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle.

It can be easily seen that

\displaystyle\frac{u}{2z+u}-\left(\frac{u}{z+u}\right)^{2}=O(z^{2}),

which is the desired result. In case the signs of
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i}},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle
and
\langle\alpha^{\vee}_{j_{i+1}},w_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+w_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\rangle
differ, we have

\displaystyle\underline{\overline{\Delta}}_{j_{i}+1}-\overline{\Delta}_{j_{i}+1}\underline{\Delta}_{j_{i+1}-1}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{u}{z+u}\cdot\frac{u}{-z+u}

\displaystyle=O(z^{2})

again, as desired.
∎

The point of this lemma is that the errors are “almost multiplicative” in a certain sense.
We will use this to prove

\sum_{e\in\{0,1\}^{r}}\tilde{I}(\tau^{e}w_{1},\tau^{e}w_{2})=\tilde{I}(w_{1},w_{2})\sum_{e\in\{0,1\}^{r}}(-1)^{|\{i:e_{i}=1\}|}\mathcal{E}_{e}=O(z^{r}\tilde{I}(w_{1},w_{2})).

The base case
r=1
is precisely the simplest case proved above.
In general, we write

(6.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\{0,1\}^{r}}(-1)^{|\{i:e_{i}=1\}|}\mathcal{E}_{e}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\{0,1\}^{r}\atop e_{r-1}=0,e_{r}=0}(-1)^{|\{i:e_{i}=1\}|}\prod_{\ell=j_{1}-1}^{j_{r-2}+1}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}\cdot(1-\mu_{r}\overline{\Delta}_{j_{r}+1}\underline{\Delta}_{j_{r}-1})

\displaystyle\hskip 11.38109pt+\sum_{e\in\{0,1\}^{r}\atop e_{r-1}=1,e_{r}=0}(-1)^{|\{i:e_{i}=1\}|}\prod_{\ell=j_{1}-1}^{j_{r-2}+1}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}\cdot(\mu_{r-1}\overline{\Delta}_{j_{r-1}+1}-\mu_{r-1}\mu_{r}\overline{\underline{\Delta}}_{j_{r-1}+1}\overline{\Delta}_{j_{r}+1}).

By the induction hypothesis applied to
r-2
,

(6.4)

\sum_{e\in\{0,1\}^{r}\atop e_{r-1}=e_{r}=0}(-1)^{|\{i:e_{i}=1\}|}\prod_{\ell=j_{1}-1}^{j_{r-2}+1}\mathcal{L}_{\ell},\sum_{e\in\{0,1\}^{r}\atop e_{r-1}=1,e_{r}=0}(-1)^{|\{i:e_{i}=1\}|}\prod_{\ell=j_{1}-1}^{j_{r-2}+1}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}

are both of order
O(z^{r-2})
.
For the first sum, this is clear.
As for the second sum, by two applications of the induction hypothesis,

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\{0,1\}^{r}\atop e_{r-2}=0,e_{r-1}=1,e_{r}=0}(-1)^{|\{i:e_{i}=1\}|}\prod_{\ell=j_{1}-1}^{j_{r-2}}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}\cdot\underline{\Delta}_{j_{r-2}+1}

\displaystyle\hskip 11.38109pt+\sum_{e\in\{0,1\}^{r}\atop e_{r-2}=1,e_{r-1}=1,e_{r}=0}(-1)^{|\{i:e_{i}=1\}|}\prod_{\ell=j_{1}-1}^{j_{r-2}}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}\cdot(\overline{\Delta}_{j_{r-2}+1}\underline{\Delta}_{j_{r-2}+1}+O(z^{2}))

\displaystyle=O(z^{r-2}\underline{\Delta}_{j_{r-2}+1})+\sum_{e\in\{0,1\}^{r}\atop e_{r-2}=1,e_{r-1}=1,e_{r}=0}(-1)^{|\{i:e_{i}=1\}|}\prod_{\ell=j_{1}-1}^{j_{r-2}}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}\cdot O(z^{2})

\displaystyle=O(z^{r-2})+O(z^{r-1})=O(z^{r-2})

for
z
near zero.

Moreover, recall that the first sum in (
6.4
) equals

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\{0,1\}^{r}\atop e_{r-2}=e_{r-1}=e_{r}=0}(-1)^{|\{i:e_{i}=1\}|}\prod_{\ell=j_{1}-1}^{j_{r-2}}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}+\sum_{e\in\{0,1\}^{r}\atop e_{r-2}=1,e_{r-1}=e_{r}=0}(-1)^{|\{i:e_{i}=1\}|}\prod_{\ell=j_{1}-1}^{j_{r-2}}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}\cdot\overline{\Delta}_{j_{r-2}+1}.

It follows by adding this expression to the previous equality that the two sums in (
6.4
) add to
O(z^{r-1})
.

Thus (
6.3
) becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\{0,1\}^{r}\atop e_{r-1}=e_{r}=0}(-1)^{|\{i:e_{i}=1\}|}\prod_{\ell=j_{1}-1}^{j_{r-2}+1}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}\cdot(1-\mu_{r}\overline{\Delta}_{j_{r}+1}\underline{\Delta}_{j_{r}-1}-\mu_{r-1}\overline{\Delta}_{j_{r-1}+1}

\displaystyle\hskip 11.38109pt+\mu_{r-1}\mu_{r}\overline{\underline{\Delta}}_{j_{r-1}+1}\overline{\Delta}_{j_{r}+1})+O(z^{r-1}(\mu_{r-1}\overline{\Delta}_{j_{r-1}+1}-\mu_{r-1}\mu_{r}\overline{\underline{\Delta}}_{j_{r-1}+1}\overline{\Delta}_{j_{r}+1}))

\displaystyle=O(z^{r-2}(1-\mu_{r}\overline{\Delta}_{j_{r}+1}\underline{\Delta}_{j_{r}-1}-\mu_{r-1}\overline{\Delta}_{j_{r-1}+1}+\mu_{r-1}\mu_{r}\overline{\underline{\Delta}}_{j_{r-1}+1}\overline{\Delta}_{j_{r}+1}))

\displaystyle\hskip 11.38109pt+O(z^{r-1}(\mu_{r-1}\overline{\Delta}_{j_{r-1}+1}-\mu_{r-1}\mu_{r}\overline{\underline{\Delta}}_{j_{r-1}+1}\overline{\Delta}_{j_{r}+1})).

By Lemma
6.1
, this is

\displaystyle O(z^{r-2}(1-\mu_{r}\overline{\Delta}_{j_{r}+1}\underline{\Delta}_{j_{r}-1})(1-\mu_{r-1}\overline{\Delta}_{j_{r-1}+1}))+O(z^{r})

\displaystyle+O(z^{r-1}\mu_{r-1}\overline{\Delta}_{j_{r-1}+1}(1-\mu_{r-1}\underline{\Delta}_{j_{r}-1}\overline{\Delta}_{j_{r}+1}))+O(z^{r+1})

\displaystyle=O(z^{r}).

6.2.3.
Mixed case

Lastly, consider the case in which there are
p>1
“consecutive zeros,” each of length
r_{\ell}
, so that
j_{i+1}-j_{i}=2
if and only if
i\neq r_{1}+\ldots+r_{\ell}
for
\ell=1,\ldots,p-1
. Write

\displaystyle\{0,1\}^{r}=\{0,1\}^{r_{1}}\times\cdots\times\{0,1\}^{r_{p}},

and identify
\{0,1\}^{r_{\ell}}
with the
\ell
-th component of this expression, with all other components being zero. By the work of the previous section, for any
e^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{r}
whose
\ell
-th component is zero,

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\{0,1\}^{r_{\ell}}}\tilde{I}(\tau^{e+e^{\prime}}w_{1},\tau^{e+e^{\prime}}w_{2})=\tilde{I}(\tau^{e^{\prime}}w_{1},\tau^{e^{\prime}}w_{2})\mathcal{F}_{\ell}

for some expression
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
of order
O(z^{r_{\ell}})
. This implies

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\{0,1\}^{r}}\tilde{I}(\tau^{e}w_{1},\tau^{e}w_{2})=\tilde{I}(w_{1},w_{2})\mathcal{F}_{1}\cdots\mathcal{F}_{p}=O(z^{r}\tilde{I}(w_{1},w_{2})),

as claimed.

7.
An application

7.1.
Settings

Fix
0<\eta_{2}<1
, and integers
n\geq 4
and
2\leq k\leq n-2
. Let
P=P(k)
the maximal parabolic subgroup of
G
, and
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a nonnegative function of compact support whose Mellin transform
Mh(s)
and its inverse transform exist on the strip
\mathrm{Re}\,s\in[n-\eta_{2},n+2\eta_{2}-\eta_{2}^{2}]
.

Since our main formula Theorem
5.2
requires that the input function be smooth, we need to convolve
h
by a bump function
g
. Define

\sigma(x)=\begin{cases}Ae^{-1/(1-x^{2})}&\mbox{if $|x|<1$},\\
0&\mbox{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
A\in\mathbb{R}
is defined so that
\int\sigma(x)dx=1
. For a (small) parameter
0<\delta<1
, let
B_{\delta}(x)=\delta^{-1}\cdot\sigma(\delta^{-1}x)
and
\beta_{\delta}(x)=B_{\delta}(\log x)
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Write
s=c+it
. The following statements hold.

(i)

|\widehat{\sigma}(z)|\ll|(\mathrm{Im}\,z)^{-3/4}e^{-\sqrt{\mathrm{Im}\,z}}|
for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
with bounded
\mathrm{Re}\,z
.

(ii)

|M\beta_{\delta}(s)|\ll|(\delta t)^{-3/4}e^{-\sqrt{\delta t}}|

(iii)

M\beta_{\delta}(c)=1+O(c\delta)
for
\delta
in a neighborhood of zero.

Proof.

(i) is well-known, see e.g.,
[
Joh15
, Section 2]
; we also added a more elaborate computation in the appendix below. (ii) follows from (i) and

\displaystyle M\beta_{\delta}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta_{\delta}(x)x^{s-1}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}B_{\delta}(x)e^{(c+it)x}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}\sigma(\delta^{-1}x)e^{i(t-ci)x}dx

\displaystyle=\widehat{\sigma}(\delta(t-ci)).

The above calculation also yields that

M\beta_{\delta}(c)=\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}\sigma(\delta^{-1}x)e^{cx}dx.

Since
e^{cx}=1+O(c\delta)
in the range of integration, this proves (iii).
∎

For a subset
A
of
\mathbb{R}
, define

h_{A}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $x\in A$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

For
N\in\mathbb{R}
, we write
h_{N}=h_{[0,N]}
. It may be helpful to note that
Mh_{N}(s)=N^{s}/s
. Let
f_{A,\delta}=h_{A}*\beta_{\delta}
, and similarly for
f_{N,\delta}
.

7.2.
Second moment estimate

For our application Theorem
1.3
, we are interested in the second moment

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{vol}^{C}(\Gamma\backslash G)}\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}\left|\mathrm{H}^{C}E_{f_{A,\delta}}(g)\right|^{2}dg.

for suitable choices of intervals
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and
\delta
.
Since
\Lambda^{C}E_{f_{A,\delta}}(g)
is real-valued, and
|\mathrm{H}^{C}E_{f_{A,\delta}}(g)|\leq|\Lambda^{C}E_{f_{A,\delta}}(g)|
for all
g\in\Gamma\backslash G
, this is bounded above by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{vol}^{C}(\Gamma\backslash G)}\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}\left(\Lambda^{C}E_{f_{A,\delta}}(g)\right)^{2}dg.

We apply Theorem
5.2
to estimate this integral. Clearly, the main term is given by

\displaystyle\left(Mf_{A,\delta}(n)\xi(n,k)\right)^{2}.

For a more precise error estimate, we revisit the expressions (
5.7
) and (
5.8
). First, consider the integral

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{Re}\,s_{2}=n-\eta_{2}}Mf_{2}(s_{2})\sum_{w_{2}\in W}\frac{e^{\langle C,w_{2}\lambda_{2}-\rho+O(\varepsilon)\rangle}}{\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta}\langle\alpha^{\vee},w_{2}\lambda_{2}-\rho+O(\varepsilon)\rangle}M(w_{2},\lambda_{2})ds_{2}

in (
5.7
). By Lemma
4.3
,
|M(w_{2},\lambda_{2})|\ll_{n}|\mathrm{Im}\,s_{2}|^{0.501\eta_{2}}
. On the other hand, none of the denominator terms
\langle\alpha^{\vee},w_{2}\lambda_{2}-\rho\rangle
vanish on the line
\mathrm{Re}\,s_{2}=n-\eta_{2}
; moreover, at least one of them is linear in
\mathrm{Im}\,s_{2}
. Therefore, for
f_{2}=f_{A,\delta}
, this integral is bounded by a constant in
n,\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
times

(7.1)

\displaystyle T^{-\eta_{2}\frac{k_{2}(n-k_{2})}{2}}\int_{\mathrm{Re}\,s_{2}=n-\eta_{2}}\left|Mh_{A}(s_{2})M\beta_{\delta}(s_{2})(1+\mathrm{Im}\,s_{2})^{-0.499\eta_{2}}\right|ds_{2}.

Let us also consider the integral (
5.8
). Again, we can use Lemma
4.3
to bound the growths of
M(w_{1},\lambda_{1})
and
M(w_{2},\lambda_{2})
. The denominator terms again does not vanish in the region of integration. Hence, for
f_{1}=f_{2}=f_{A,\delta}
, (
5.8
) is bounded by a constant in
n,\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
times

(7.2)

\displaystyle T^{\kappa}\prod_{i=1,2}\int_{\mathrm{Re}\,s_{i}=n-\eta_{i}}\left|Mh_{A}(s_{i})M\beta_{\delta}(s_{i})(\mathrm{Im}\,s_{i})^{0.501\eta_{i}}\right|ds_{i}.

At this point, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 7.2
.

Suppose
s=c+it
,
c\geq 1
, and
A=(N_{1},N_{2}]
, so that either
N_{1}=0
or
N_{2}^{c}-N_{1}^{c}\leq N_{1}^{c}/2
. Then
|Mh_{A}(s)|\ll|Mh_{A}(c)|
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

First note that

\displaystyle Mh_{A}(s)=\frac{N_{2}^{s}}{s}-\frac{N_{1}^{s}}{s}.

Thus the case
N_{1}=0
is trivial, and we assume
N_{1}>0
for the rest of the proof.
Rewriting this as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}\left(N_{2}^{c}e^{it\log N_{2}}-N_{1}^{c}e^{it\log N_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\left((N_{2}^{c}-N_{1}^{c})e^{it\log N_{2}}+N_{1}^{c}e^{it\log N_{2}}(1-e^{it\log\frac{N_{1}}{N_{2}}})\right),

it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{c+it}N_{1}^{c}(1-e^{it\log\frac{N_{1}}{N_{2}}})\right|\ll\frac{1}{c}(N_{2}^{c}-N_{1}^{c})

for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

When
|t\log(N_{2}/N_{1})|\leq 1/2
, the left-hand side of the above inequality is bounded by a constant times

\displaystyle\left|\frac{t}{c+it}N_{1}^{c}\log\frac{N_{2}}{N_{1}}\right|\ll N_{1}^{c}\log\frac{N_{2}}{N_{1}}.

If
|t\log(N_{2}/N_{1})|>1/2
, then it is bounded by

\displaystyle\left|\frac{2N_{1}^{c}}{c+it}\right|\ll\frac{N_{1}^{c}}{t}\ll N_{1}^{c}\log\frac{N_{2}}{N_{1}}.

On the other hand, the assumption implies that

\displaystyle c\log\frac{N_{2}}{N_{1}}=\log\left(1+\frac{N_{2}^{c}-N_{1}^{c}}{N_{1}^{c}}\right)\ll\frac{N_{2}^{c}-N_{1}^{c}}{N_{1}^{c}},

which completes the proof.
∎

Thanks to Lemma
7.2
above, the integrals in (
7.1
) and (
7.2
) are bounded by

\displaystyle Mh_{A}(n-\eta_{2})\int_{\mathrm{Re}\,s_{2}=n-\eta_{2}}\left|M\beta_{\delta}(s_{2})(1+\mathrm{Im}\,s_{2})^{-0.499\eta_{2}}\right|ds_{2}

and

\displaystyle Mh_{A}(n-\eta_{i})\int_{\mathrm{Re}\,s_{i}=n-\eta_{i}}\left|M\beta_{\delta}(s_{i})(\mathrm{Im}\,s_{i})^{0.501\eta_{i}}\right|ds_{i},

respectively. Plugging in Lemma
7.1
(ii) and performing the change of variables
\delta\cdot\mathrm{Im}\,s_{i}=u
, it turns out that they are of order
O(\delta^{-1}Mh_{A}(n-\eta_{2}))
and
O(\delta^{-1-0.501\eta_{i}}Mh_{A}(n-\eta_{i}))
, respectively. This completes the error estimate. We summarize our discussion in the form of a proposition.

Proposition 7.3
.

Fix
T>0
. Let
A=(N_{1},N_{2}]
, so that either
N_{1}=0
or
N_{2}^{c}-N_{1}^{c}\leq N_{1}^{c}/2
for
c=n-\eta_{1}
and
n-\eta_{2}
. Assume in addition that

\displaystyle Mh_{A}(n)T^{-\eta_{2}\frac{k_{2}(n-k_{2})}{2}}>Mh_{A}(n-\eta_{1})T^{\kappa}.

This is satisfied, for instance, if
Mh_{A}(n-\eta_{1})
is sufficiently large relative to
T
.
Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{vol}^{C}(\Gamma\backslash G)}\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}\left|\mathrm{H}^{C}E_{f_{A,\delta}}(g)\right|^{2}dg

\displaystyle=\left(Mf_{A,\delta}(n)\xi(n,k)\right)^{2}+O(\delta^{-\alpha}Mh_{A}(n)Mh_{A}(n-\eta_{2})),

where the implicit constant depends on
n,\eta_{1},\eta_{2},
and
T
, and
\alpha=2+0.501(\eta_{1}+\eta_{2})
.

Remark
.

By the remark under Lemma
4.3
, and setting
\eta_{1}\approx 0
and
\eta_{2}\approx 1
,
\alpha
can in fact be made arbitrarily close to (but greater than)
2.5
.

Proof.

The only additional work done here is the fusion of the two error terms (
7.1
) and (
7.2
): recall that they are

\displaystyle O\left(\delta^{-1}Mh_{A}(n)Mh_{A}(n-\eta_{2})T^{-\eta_{2}\frac{k_{2}(n-k_{2})}{2}}\right)

and

\displaystyle O\left(\delta^{-\alpha}Mh_{A}(n-\eta_{1})Mh_{A}(n-\eta_{2})T^{\kappa}\right),

respectively.
∎

7.3.
Adaptation of Schmidt’s argument

In this section, we adapt the famous Borel-Cantelli argument of Schmidt
[
Sch60
]
to the problem of counting sublattices. We closely follow especially pages 520-521 of
[
Sch60
]
. We will first derive a result for the smoothed function
f_{A,\delta}
first (Theorem
7.6
below), and then finesse a result for
h_{A}
from it (Theorem
7.7
).

From now on, take
\eta_{1}=1-\eta_{2}
, and write
\eta=\eta_{2}
. We intend
\eta
to be close to
1
from the left. Fix
T\gg 0
and
H_{0}\gg_{T}0
. Choose a sequence
\{p_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
so that
p_{0}=0
,
p_{1}=(2H_{0})^{2n-\eta}
, and
p_{j+1}^{2n-\eta}-p_{j}^{2n-\eta}=H_{0}^{2n-\eta}
. For integers
k>j\geq 0
, write

\displaystyle V_{j}^{k}=\mathrm{Var}(\Lambda^{C}E_{f_{(p_{j},p_{k}],\delta}}).

Note that
p_{k}^{a}-p_{j}^{a}\leq p_{j}^{a}/2
for all
a<2n-\eta
, so that the condition of Proposition
7.3
is satisfied.

We claim
V_{j}^{k}\leq\delta^{-\alpha}H_{1}(k-j)
for some constant
H_{1}=H_{1}(n,\eta,T)
, where
\alpha
is as in the statement of Proposition
7.3
. By Proposition
7.3
,

\displaystyle\delta^{\alpha}V_{j}^{k}

\displaystyle\ll Mh_{(p_{j},p_{k}]}(n)Mh_{(p_{j},p_{k}]}(n-\eta)

\displaystyle\ll(p_{k}^{n}-p_{j}^{n})(p_{k}^{n-\eta}-p_{j}^{n-\eta})

\displaystyle<p_{k}^{2n-\eta}-p_{j}^{2n-\eta}\leq 2H_{0}^{2n-\eta}(k-j),

where the implicit constants depend on
n,\eta
, and
T
.
This verifies the claim.

Following Schmidt
[
Sch60
]
, define for
S\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}

\displaystyle K_{S}=\{(j,k):0\leq j<k\leq 2^{S},j=u2^{s},k-j=2^{s}\mbox{ for $u,s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$}\}.

Lemma 7.4
.

In the above context, it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{(j,k)\in K_{S}}V_{j}^{k}\leq\delta^{-\alpha}H_{1}(S+1)2^{S}.

Proof.

For each
s=0,\ldots,S
, their are precisely
2^{S-s}
elements
(j,k)\in K_{S}
such that
k-j=2^{s}
. These elements contribute at most
H_{1}2^{S-s}\cdot 2^{s}=H_{1}2^{S}
to the sum. Since there are
(S+1)
possible choices for
s
, this completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 7.5
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a nondecreasing function such that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{\psi(x)}<\infty.

Then for almost every
g\in\Gamma\backslash G
, there exists a constant
H_{2}=H_{2}(n,\eta,g)>0
such that

\displaystyle\left(E_{f_{p_{N},\delta}}(g)-Mf_{p_{N},\delta}(n)\xi(n,k)\right)^{2}<\delta^{-\alpha}H_{2}N(\log N)^{2}\psi(\log N)

for all integers
N\gg_{g}0
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove this for almost every
g
in the truncated domain
\mathrm{supp}\,\chi^{C}_{G,G}
for all sufficiently large
T
.

Write
\tilde{\psi}(S)=\psi((S-1)\log 2)
. By Lemma
7.4
above and Markov’s inequality, the set of
g\in\mathrm{supp}\,\chi^{C}_{G,G}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{(j,k)\in K_{S}}\left(E_{f_{(p_{k}-p_{j}],\delta}}(g)-Mf_{(p_{k}-p_{j}],\delta}(n)\xi(n,k)\right)^{2}>\delta^{-\alpha}H_{1}(S+1)2^{S}\tilde{\psi}(S)

has measure
O(1/\tilde{\psi}(S))
. Since
\sum_{S\gg 0}1/\tilde{\psi}(S)
converges by assumption, the Borel-Cantelli lemma implies that almost every
g\in\mathrm{supp}\,\chi^{C}_{G,G}
satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{(j,k)\in K_{S}}\left(E_{f_{(p_{k}-p_{j}],\delta}}(g)-Mf_{(p_{k}-p_{j}],\delta}(n)\xi(n,k)\right)^{2}\leq\delta^{-\alpha}H_{1}(S+1)2^{S}\tilde{\psi}(S)

for all
S\gg_{g}0
. On the other hand, for any
2^{S-1}<N\leq 2^{S}
,

\displaystyle E_{f_{p_{N},\delta}}(g)-Mf_{p_{N},\delta}(n)\xi(n,k)

is the sum of at most
S
elements of the form

\displaystyle E_{f_{(p_{k}-p_{j}],\delta}}(g)-Mf_{(p_{k}-p_{j}],\delta}(n)\xi(n,k)

for
(j,k)\in K_{S}
. Therefore, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\left(E_{f_{p_{N},\delta}}(g)-Mf_{p_{N},\delta}(n)\xi(n,k)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{-\alpha}H_{1}S(S+1)2^{S}\tilde{\psi}(S)

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{-\alpha}H_{2}N(\log N)^{2}\psi(\log N)

for some constant
H_{2}>0
.
H_{2}
depends on
n,\eta,
and
T
; but the dependence on
T
can be replaced by the dependence on
g
.
∎

By taking
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that
\eta>1-2\epsilon_{0}
, the above two lemmas readily imply the following result.

Theorem 7.6
.

Choose any
\epsilon_{0}>0
. Then for almost every
g\in\Gamma\backslash G
and
p\gg_{g}0
,

\displaystyle E_{f_{p,\delta}}(g)-Mf_{p,\delta}(n)\xi(n,k)=O_{n,\epsilon_{0},g}\left(\delta^{-\alpha}p^{n-\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon_{0}}\right).

Remark
.

Notice that the error term here is much better than what is suggested by the previous works on the topic
[
Sch68
,
Thu93
,
Kim23
]
. In fact, if the bound on
\kappa
can be refined to be slightly better than
\kappa<k(n-k)/2
, it is possible to improve it even further: it allows one to make different choices of
T
and
\eta_{1}
so that (
5.8
) becomes smaller than (
5.7
).

Proof.

We continue from the proof of Lemma
7.5
. Take a number
p
between
p_{N}
and
p_{N+1}
. Then

\displaystyle E_{f_{p_{N}}}(g)-Mf_{p_{N+1}}(n)\xi(n,k)

\displaystyle\leq E_{f_{p}}(g)-Mf_{p}(n)\xi(n,k)

\displaystyle\leq E_{f_{p_{N+1}}}(g)-Mf_{p_{N}}(n)\xi(n,k).

Thus, in the light of Lemma
7.5
, it suffices to show that
Mf_{p_{N}}(n)=p_{N}^{n}/n
and
Mf_{p_{N+1}}(n)=p_{N+1}^{n}/n
differ by at most
O_{n}(p^{n-\eta/2})
for
N
sufficiently large. In fact, they differ by a much smaller amount, since by assumption
p_{N+1}^{2n-\eta}-p_{N}^{2n-\eta}
is a constant.
∎

The freedom over the choice of
\delta
allows us to do without smoothing, with a slightly worse error term.

Theorem 7.7
.

Choose any
\epsilon>0
. Then for almost every
g\in\Gamma\backslash G
and
p\gg_{g}0
,

\displaystyle E_{h_{p}}(g)-Mh_{p}(n)\xi(n,k)=O_{n,\epsilon,g}(p^{n-\frac{1}{7}+\epsilon}).

Proof.

One can check that, for every
g\in\Gamma\backslash G
,

\displaystyle E_{f_{pe^{-\delta},\delta}}(g)\leq E_{h_{p}}(g)\leq E_{f_{pe^{\delta}},\delta}(g).

By Lemma
7.1
(iii) and Theorem
7.6
, this implies that for almost every
g\in\Gamma\backslash G
and
p\gg_{g}0

\displaystyle E_{h_{p}}(g)-Mh_{p}(n)\xi(n,k)=O_{n,\epsilon_{0},g}\left(\delta p^{n}+\delta^{-\alpha}p^{n-\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon_{0}}\right).

As remarked after the statement of Proposition
7.3
,
\alpha>5/2
can be made arbitrarily close to
5/2
; and of course,
\epsilon_{0}
can be set to be arbitrarily small. Hence if we choose
\delta=p^{-1/7}
, the right-hand side above becomes
O_{n,\epsilon,g}(p^{n-1/7+\epsilon})
for an arbitrarily small
\epsilon
.
∎

Appendix A
The saddle point method

The purpose of this appendix is to elaborate on the computations of
[
Joh15
]
, for completeness of our paper.
For
k\in\mathbb{C}
, let
F(k)=\int_{-1}^{1}e^{ikx-\frac{1}{1-x^{2}}}dx
. Because for
\mathrm{Re}\,k\leq 0
, we have
F(k)=F(-k)
, it is sufficient to consider the case that
\mathrm{Re}\,k\geq 0
. Letting
t=1-x
, this equals to
\int_{0}^{2}e^{ik(1-t)-\frac{1}{t(2-t)}}dt
, so

F(k)=e^{ik}\int_{0}^{2}e^{-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4-2t}}dt.

Note that on
t\in(0,\frac{1}{\mathrm{Re}\,k})
,
e^{-\frac{1}{2t}}<e^{-\frac{\mathrm{Re}\,k}{2}}
, and similarly on
t\in(2-\frac{1}{\mathrm{Re}\,k},2)
,
e^{-\frac{1}{4-2t}}<e^{-\frac{\mathrm{Re}\,k}{2}}
, hence the integrand near
0
or
2
is negligible.

Letting
h(t)=-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4-2t}
,
h^{\prime}(t)=-ik+\frac{1}{2t^{2}}-\frac{2}{(4-2t)^{2}}=-ik+\frac{-16(t-1)}{8t^{2}(2-t)^{2}}
. Solving
h^{\prime}(t)=0
is not easy. Instead, we consider expansions of it near
0
and
2
respectively: for example, near
0
we have

-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4-2t}=-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4}+O(t).

Then the integral
\int_{0}^{2}e^{-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4}+O(t)}dt=\int_{0}^{2}e^{-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4}}(1+O(t))dt
near
0
.

Let

g(t)=-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4}.

Then
g^{\prime}(t)=-ik+\frac{1}{2t^{2}}
and solving
g^{\prime}(t)=0
gives

t_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2ik}}=\frac{1-i}{2\sqrt{k}},

at which
e^{-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4}}
is maximal. Similarly, near
2
the same reasoning gives us

t_{2}=2-\frac{1+i}{2\sqrt{k}}.

We want to move the path of integral to another path through these saddle points and apply the saddle point method to find the main term, and do an error analysis to bound error. Let
k=re^{i\theta}
, where
r=|k|
and
\theta\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then
\sqrt{k}=\sqrt{r}e^{\frac{i\theta}{2}}
,
t_{1}=\frac{e^{-i(\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4})}}{\sqrt{2r}}
and
t_{2}=2-\frac{e^{-i(\frac{\theta}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4})}}{\sqrt{2r}}
.

Let
C
be the path consisting of the line
C_{1}(\theta)\subset\{x+yi\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ y=-\tan(\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4})x\}
joining
0
and
1-ie^{-i\theta}
, and the line
C_{2}(\theta)\subset\{x+yi\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ y=\cot(\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4})(x-2)\}
joining
1-ie^{-i\theta}
and
2
— one can easily check that the two lines indeed meet at
1-ie^{-i\theta}
.
Then it holds that

\displaystyle F(k)=e^{ik}\left(\int_{C_{1}(\theta)}e^{-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4-2t}}dt+\int_{C_{2}(\theta)}e^{-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4-2t}}dt\right).

We will work on one integral at a time.

For the integral over
C_{1}(\theta)
, first consider, for
\alpha\in(r^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon},1)
, the quantity
t=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2k}}e^{-\frac{i\pi}{4}}(1-\alpha)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2r}}e^{-i(\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4})}(1-\alpha)=t_{1}(1-\alpha)
in the line connecting
0
and
t_{1}
. We have

|e^{-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4-2t}}|=|e^{-ikt_{1}-\frac{1}{2t_{1}}-\frac{1}{4}}||e^{ikt_{1}\alpha-\frac{\alpha}{2t_{1}(1-\alpha)}}e^{O(t_{1})}|

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,\left(ikt_{1}\alpha-\frac{\alpha}{2t_{1}(1-\alpha)}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\,\left(ire^{i\theta}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2r}}e^{-i(\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4})}\alpha-\frac{\alpha}{2\frac{1}{\sqrt{2r}}e^{-i(\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4})}(1-\alpha)}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha\sqrt{r}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)+\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha^{2}\sqrt{r}}{\sqrt{2}(1-\alpha)}\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\leq-\frac{r^{2\epsilon}}{\sqrt{2}}\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right),

which imply

\displaystyle|e^{-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4-2t}}|\ll|e^{-ikt_{1}-\frac{1}{2t_{1}}-\frac{1}{4}}|e^{-\frac{r^{2\epsilon}}{\sqrt{2}}\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}.

Similarly for
t=t_{1}(1+\beta)\in C
, where
\beta\geq r^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon}
, we have

|e^{-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4-2t}}|=|e^{-ikt_{1}-\frac{1}{2t_{1}}-\frac{1}{4}}||e^{-ikt_{1}\beta+\frac{\beta}{2t_{1}(1+\beta)}}e^{O(t_{1})}|

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,\left(-ikt_{1}\beta+\frac{\beta}{2t_{1}(1+\beta)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta\sqrt{r}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)+\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\frac{1}{1+\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\beta^{2}\sqrt{r}}{\sqrt{2}(1+\beta)}\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{r^{2\epsilon}}{\sqrt{2}(1+r^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon})}\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right),

and hence

\displaystyle|e^{-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4-2t}}|\ll|e^{-ikt_{1}-\frac{1}{2t_{1}}-\frac{1}{4}}|e^{-\frac{r^{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}}{\sqrt{2}(1+r^{-\epsilon})}\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}.

From these estimations, we see that the main contribution comes from
t=t_{1}(1+\gamma)\in C
with
\gamma\in(-r^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon},r^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon})
.
We have for
g(t)=-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4}
,

\displaystyle g(t)

\displaystyle=g(t_{1})+\frac{g^{\prime}(t_{1})}{1}(t-t_{1})+\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(t_{1})}{2!}(t-t_{1})^{2}+\cdots

\displaystyle=g(t_{1})+\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(t_{1})}{2!}(t-t_{1})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}\left(\frac{-1}{t_{1}}\right)^{m+1}(t-t_{1})^{m}

\displaystyle=-ikt_{1}-\frac{1}{2t_{1}}-\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2t_{1}}-\frac{1}{2t_{1}}\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}(-\gamma)^{m}

\displaystyle=-ikt_{1}-\frac{1}{2t_{1}}-\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2t_{1}}+O(|t_{1}|^{-1}\gamma^{3}).

Also, writing
h(t)=e^{-\frac{2t}{4(4-2t)}}
,

\displaystyle\int_{t_{1}(1-r^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon})}^{t_{1}(1+r^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon})}e^{-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4-2t}}dt=\int_{t_{1}(1-r^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon})}^{t_{1}(1+r^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon})}h(t)e^{g(t)}dt

\displaystyle=t_{1}\int_{-r^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon}}^{r^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon}}h(t_{1}(1+\gamma))e^{g(t_{1}(1+\gamma))}d\gamma

\displaystyle=t_{1}e^{-ikt_{1}-\frac{1}{2t_{1}}-\frac{1}{4}}\int_{-r^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon}}^{r^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon}}h(t_{1}(1+\gamma))e^{-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2t_{1}}-\frac{1}{t_{1}}\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}(-\gamma)^{m}}d\gamma

\displaystyle=t_{1}\sqrt{2t_{1}}e^{-ikt_{1}-\frac{1}{2t_{1}}-\frac{1}{4}}\int_{-\frac{r^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon}}{\sqrt{2t_{1}}}}^{\frac{r^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon}}{\sqrt{2t_{1}}}}h(t_{1}(1+\sqrt{2t_{1}}\gamma))e^{-\gamma^{2}-2\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}(2t_{1})^{\frac{m}{2}-1}(-\gamma)^{m}}d\gamma

\displaystyle=t_{1}\sqrt{2t_{1}}e^{-ikt_{1}-\frac{1}{2t_{1}}-\frac{1}{4}}\int_{-\frac{r^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon}}{\sqrt{2t_{1}}}}^{\frac{r^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon}}{\sqrt{2t_{1}}}}e^{-\gamma^{2}-\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}t_{1}^{\frac{m}{2}-1}(-\gamma)^{m}}d\gamma+O\left(|t_{1}|^{\frac{5}{2}}|e^{-ikt_{1}-\frac{1}{2t_{1}}-\frac{1}{4}}|\right).

Here, it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{-\frac{r^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon}}{\sqrt{2t_{1}}}}^{\frac{r^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon}}{\sqrt{2t_{1}}}}e^{-\gamma^{2}-2\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}(2t_{1})^{\frac{m}{2}-1}(-\gamma)^{m}}d\gamma

\displaystyle=\int_{-r^{\epsilon}}^{r^{\epsilon}}e^{-\gamma^{2}-2\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}(2t_{1})^{\frac{m}{2}-1}(-\gamma)^{m}}d\gamma+O(e^{-\frac{1}{2}r^{\epsilon}})

\displaystyle=\int_{-r^{\epsilon}}^{r^{\epsilon}}e^{-\gamma^{2}}d\gamma+O(|t|^{\frac{1}{2}}r^{3\epsilon})+O(e^{-\frac{1}{2}r^{-\epsilon}(2r)^{\frac{1}{4}}})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\gamma^{2}}d\gamma+O(r^{-\frac{1}{4}+3\epsilon})=\sqrt{\pi}+O(r^{-\frac{1}{4}+3\epsilon}).

Summing up everything together, we conclude that

\displaystyle e^{ik}\int_{C_{1}(\theta)}e^{-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4-2t}}dt

\displaystyle=e^{ik}t_{1}\sqrt{2t_{1}}e^{-ikt_{1}-\frac{1}{2t_{1}}-\frac{1}{4}}\left(\sqrt{\pi}+O(r^{-\frac{1}{4}+3\epsilon})\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{-i\pi}{\sqrt{2i}k^{\frac{3}{2}}}}e^{ik-\sqrt{2ik}-\frac{1}{4}}\Big(1+O(|k|^{-\frac{1}{4}+3\epsilon})\Big).

For the integral over
C_{2}(\theta)
, we first substitute
t
by
2-t
to rewrite

\displaystyle e^{ik}\int_{C_{2}(\theta)}e^{-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4-2t}}dt=e^{-ik}\int_{C_{2}^{\prime}(\theta)}e^{ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4-2t}}dt,

where
C_{2}^{\prime}(\theta)\subset\{x+yi\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ y=\cot(\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4})x\}
joining
0
and
1+e^{-i\theta}
.
Note that
\cot(\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4})=-\tan(\frac{\pi+\theta}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4})
and and
1+ie^{i\theta}=1-ie^{i(\pi+\theta)}
.
Therefore we see that

\displaystyle e^{ik}\int_{C_{2}(\theta)}e^{-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4-2t}}dt=e^{i(-k)}\int_{C_{1}(\pi+\theta)}e^{-i(-k)t-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4-2t}}dt.

Similarly as in the integral over
C_{1}(\theta)
earlier, we obtain

\displaystyle e^{ik}\int_{C_{2}(\theta)}e^{-ikt-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4-2t}}dt

\displaystyle=e^{i(-k)}\int_{C_{1}(\pi+\theta)}e^{-i(-k)t-\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{4-2t}}dt

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{-i\pi}{\sqrt{2i}(-k)^{\frac{3}{2}}}}e^{i(-k)-\sqrt{2i(-k)}-\frac{1}{4}}\Big(1+O(|k|^{-\frac{1}{4}+3\epsilon})\Big)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{i\pi}{\sqrt{-2i}k^{\frac{3}{2}}}}e^{-ik-\sqrt{-2ik}-\frac{1}{4}}\Big(1+O(|k|^{-\frac{1}{4}+3\epsilon})\Big)

Our computations so far yield the conclusion that

\displaystyle F(k)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{-i\pi}{\sqrt{2i}k^{\frac{3}{2}}}}e^{ik-\sqrt{2ik}-\frac{1}{4}}\Big(1+O(|k|^{-\frac{1}{4}+3\epsilon})\Big)

\displaystyle\ \ +\sqrt{\frac{i\pi}{\sqrt{-2i}k^{\frac{3}{2}}}}e^{-ik-\sqrt{-2ik}-\frac{1}{4}}\Big(1+O(|k|^{-\frac{1}{4}+3\epsilon})\Big).

In particular, for real
k
,

F(k)=2\mathrm{Re}\,\left(\sqrt{\frac{-i\pi}{\sqrt{2i}k^{\frac{3}{2}}}}e^{ik-\sqrt{2ik}-\frac{1}{4}}\Big(1+O(|k|^{-\frac{1}{4}+3\epsilon})\Big)\right).
