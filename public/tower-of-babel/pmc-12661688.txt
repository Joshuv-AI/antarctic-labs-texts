Validation of an AI-Based platform for structured diagnosis of headache disorders using ICHD-3 criteria
Authors: João Brainer Clares de Andrade, Thiago Bulhões da Silva Costa, Júlia Lima Vasconcelos, Thiago Luís Marques Lopes, Mateus Dutra Balsells, Vinícius Luiz Cristofolini, Sophia Oliveira Querobin, Flavio Moura Rezende Filho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe diagnosis of headache disorders remains a clinical challenge, particularly for non-specialists, due to the complexity of the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3), and the absence of biomarkers. Large language models (LLMs) represent a promising tool to support accurate and scalable diagnostic classification, especially in resource-limited settings.ObjectiveTo validate the performance of a free, multilingual clinical decision support platform—Head.AI—designed to classify headache cases using GPT-4o and a structured implementation of ICHD-3.MethodsWe conducted an independent validation using 315 expert-generated vignettes representing 215 ICHD-3 diagnoses, input into Head.AI and three other platforms (Claude Sonnet 4.0, Grok 3.0, and Gemini 2.5). Outcomes included diagnostic accuracy (rank of correct diagnosis), calibration, and citation rate.ResultsThe algorithm correctly identified the top diagnosis in 89.5% of cases (vs. 74–80% in comparators), with a citation rate >97% and calibration (Brier score 0.153). It maintained consistent performance across primary and secondary headaches and achieved first-hypothesis accuracy >74% in difficult cases. Logistic regression confirmed Head.AI had significantly higher odds of correct classification (ORs vs. comparators: 2.04–2.86; all p < 0.01).ConclusionOur algorithm demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy across a broad spectrum of headache disorders, exceeding the performance reported in prior studies, though direct comparison should be interpreted with caution due to methodological differences. Its public availability, structured knowledge base, and educational potential make it a valuable contribution to AI-assisted headache care. Introduction

Headache disorders are among the most frequent complaints in both general practice and neurological outpatient settings, with a global impact on health systems and individual quality of life [1, 2]. Despite their prevalence, headache disorders remain underdiagnosed and frequently misclassified, partly due to their reliance on clinical history and the absence of definitive biomarkers [3, 4].

The current gold standard for diagnosis is the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3) [5]. While comprehensive, the ICHD-3 requires significant clinical expertise and familiarity to apply accurately. Studies have shown that even among trained professionals, interrater agreement in headache classification can be modest, especially when evaluating complex or atypical cases [6]. Moreover, diagnostic accuracy can be influenced by factors such as time constraints in clinical settings, patient communication challenges, and limited specialist availability, particularly in primary care environments [1, 7].

These diagnostic challenges present an opportunity to incorporate decision-support tools into clinical practice. Headache disorders, unlike many other neurological conditions, are diagnosed almost entirely through structured history-taking. This unique characteristic makes them particularly amenable to classification by artificial intelligence (AI) models based on natural language processing (NLP) and large language models (LLMs) [7–9]. Recent efforts using AI to classify headaches have demonstrated promising results [10]. However, most existing tools are limited in scope, often restricted to differentiating between a small number of headache types or relying on supervised datasets not grounded in the full structure of the ICHD-3 classification [11–13]. Furthermore, previous AI applications frequently lack transparent documentation of their underlying ontologies or expert-driven input. This limits their interpretability, scalability, and utility in educational contexts, especially for medical students, residents, and general practitioners who are still mastering the nuances of headache diagnosis [14, 15].

In this scenario, we aimed to design and validate a comprehensive and expert-curated AI-based platform to assist in the classification of headache disorders using the full ICHD-3 taxonomy. To our best knowledge, this is the first AI-based implementation that couples a full ICHD-3 structured ontology with a multilingual LLM engine.

Methods

Platform Development

Head.AI is a natural language processing (NLP) system specifically designed to classify clinical headache cases according to the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3). The platform was developed using the GPT-4o API (OpenAI), incorporating a dual-source knowledge base: the complete ICHD-3 classification in its original structure and a structured JSON repository created by a panel of headache experts. This JSON file contained detailed metadata for each diagnosis, including codes, diagnostic criteria, and minimum episode duration, mapped directly to ICHD-3 taxonomy.

The prompt engineering process involved fine-tuning the API’s behavior to ensure that it adhered strictly to the hierarchical logic and diagnostic rules of ICHD-3. Although the initial implementation was designed in Brazilian Portuguese, the underlying GPT-4o architecture enabled multilingual input-output processing. As such, clinical queries could be presented in Portuguese, but the system was configured to consistently return structured diagnostic responses in English, facilitating broader applicability and interoperability with international clinical research standards.

No fine-tuning or reinforcement learning with human feedback was performed on the model itself; instead, performance optimization relied exclusively on prompt design and expert-curated reference materials.

System architecture and technical infrastructure

The Head.AI platform (www.head-ai.com.br) is a web-based clinical decision support system built upon the GPT-4o architecture via OpenAI’s API interface. All inference requests are routed through a secure backend server that handles user input formatting, session management, and real-time API calls to the language model. The infrastructure is hosted on a HIPAA-compliant cloud environment, with encrypted communication protocols and no user data retention.

Prompt templates were engineered to ensure adherence to ICHD-3 diagnostic logic, and internal error handling routines were implemented to intercept incomplete or malformed outputs. When necessary, Head.AI automatically re-queries the model using a fallback strategy to enhance response reliability.

Latency per inference remained below 2 s on average, enabling very fast interaction for educational use. The platform supports multilingual input, but the diagnostic outputs are standardized in English for consistency and international applicability. No fine-tuning was applied to the base model; the platform’s accuracy derives from prompt design and a structured expert-defined knowledge layer embedded in the system.

Although prompts were entered in Brazilian Portuguese, the diagnostic outputs were standardized in English. Given the language flexibility of GPT-4o, which was pretrained on multilingual corpora, no performance degradation was noted, but future cross-lingual validation is warranted.

Head.AI does not rely on fine-tuning the LLM itself. Instead, it uses a structured prompt template that dynamically references a JSON-based ontology aligned with ICHD-3 diagnostic criteria. Internal validation routines and a fallback query system were implemented to manage incomplete outputs. This architecture distinguishes Head.AI from generic prompt-based applications of LLMs.

Validation step

An independent validation protocol was implemented to assess diagnostic performance across platforms. Two board-certified neurologists with expertise in headache medicine developed a set of clinical vignettes based on the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3). Each vignette corresponded to a single ICHD-3 diagnosis, including variable symptom presentations and overlapping features. The experts were explicitly instructed not to use any generative AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Grok, or Gemini) during the case creation process, to prevent potential inadvertent reinforcement of the platforms’ pretrained knowledge.

For each case, the corresponding ICHD-3 code was assigned, and the diagnostic difficulty was independently classified by the authors as easy, moderate, or difficult. No restriction was imposed on the number of cases per diagnostic code, allowing for denser sampling in clinically diverse or diagnostically challenging categories.

The final dataset was constructed to ensure comprehensive representation of all diagnostic groups in the ICHD-3, including rare secondary headache syndromes. This sampling strategy was intentional, allowing performance assessment across the entire classification system rather than mirroring real-world prevalence.

The clinical vignettes were subsequently entered into each of the four evaluated platforms—Head.AI, Gemini 2.5, Grok 3.0, and Claude Sonnet 4.0—by a third board-certified neurologist who had not participated in case development and was blinded to the reference diagnosis. This step ensured unbiased input processing and output retrieval across platforms. All case submissions and diagnostic outputs were collected between May and June 2025, using standardized prompts.

We opted for simulated clinical vignettes to guarantee coverage of the full ICHD-3 taxonomy. A real-world dataset would likely be heavily skewed toward common primary headache disorders, severely limiting our ability to evaluate algorithmic performance in rare but clinically relevant conditions.

Outcome definition and evaluation metrics

The primary outcome was defined as whether the correct ICHD-3 diagnosis appeared among the top hypotheses returned by each platform in response to a clinical vignette. Each platform was prompted to generate up to three diagnostic hypotheses for each case.

Classification outcomes were grouped into four mutually exclusive categories:

1 st Hypothesis: The correct diagnosis was returned as the top-ranked result.

1 st Hypothesis: The correct diagnosis was returned as the top-ranked result.

2nd Hypothesis: The correct diagnosis was listed in second position.

2nd Hypothesis: The correct diagnosis was listed in second position.

3rd Hypothesis: The correct diagnosis was listed in third position.

3rd Hypothesis: The correct diagnosis was listed in third position.

No Cited: The correct ICHD-3 diagnosis was not cited among the top three responses.

No Cited: The correct ICHD-3 diagnosis was not cited among the top three responses.

Diagnostic accuracy was quantified using the proportion of cases correctly classified in each rank category. Logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios for returning the correct diagnosis as the first hypothesis, both in unadjusted and adjusted frameworks. Adjustments were made for diagnostic category (primary vs. secondary headaches) and expert-assigned case difficulty.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the dataset in terms of diagnostic categories (primary vs. secondary headaches), ICHD-3 classification groups, and expert-assigned case difficulty. Continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and proportions.

To compare diagnostic performance across platforms, we conducted a Chi-square test evaluating the distribution of classification outcomes—defined as the rank position (1st, 2nd, 3rd hypothesis, or no citation) in which the correct ICHD-3 diagnosis was returned. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

We modeled the likelihood of platforms returning the correct diagnosis as the 1 st hypothesis using logistic regression. Unadjusted models included only the platform as a predictor, while adjusted models incorporated additional covariates such as diagnostic category (primary vs. secondary headache) and case difficulty (easy, moderate, or difficult). Model calibration was assessed using Brier scores and visually through calibration curves. The diagnostic citation rate—defined as the percentage of cases in which the correct diagnosis appeared anywhere among the top three hypotheses—was computed overall and stratified by ICHD-3 group. To assess systematic failure patterns, a Poisson regression model was implemented to evaluate omission rates (No Cited outcomes), with adjustments for diagnostic group prevalence and case count per platform.

Platform performance was also stratified by headache classification (primary vs. secondary) and ICHD-3 diagnostic groups. Interaction effects between platform and diagnostic subgroup were tested using likelihood-ratio tests.

All analyses were conducted in Python (v 3.11.11), using the statsmodels, scikit-learn, and matplotlib libraries.

Compliance with reporting standards

Our study adheres to key principles of two internationally recognized frameworks for the transparent reporting of artificial intelligence–based clinical tools: the MINimum Information for Medical AI Reporting (MINIMAR) checklist and the Developmental and Exploratory Clinical Investigation of Decision-support systems driven by AI (DECIDE-AI) guideline for early-phase evaluation of AI-driven decision-support systems. In accordance with MINIMAR, the platform’s architecture, prompt structure, input/output format, and privacy safeguards are fully documented, with no storage of personal data and all results made publicly available. Aligned with DECIDE-AI, the study followed a structured early-phase validation protocol, using expert-generated vignettes, independent blinded evaluation, and comprehensive performance metrics including accuracy, calibration, and omission rates.

The checklists and a methodological flowchart (Figure S1) are available in Supplementary Material 2.

Ethical statement

This study did not involve human participants, identifiable personal data, or interventions, and therefore did not require submission to an institutional ethics committee. All clinical vignettes were synthetically generated by medical experts for research and educational purposes, without referencing real patients or medical records.

The Head.AI platform operates in full compliance with international data privacy regulations, including the Brazilian General Data Protection Law and the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). No user data are stored, retained, or processed beyond the immediate inference session. Users are presented with a clear disclaimer outlining the educational and non-diagnostic nature of the platform prior to use.

Head.AI is freely accessible for educational and research use. All clinical vignettes, corresponding ICHD-3 classifications, and diagnostic outputs from the evaluated platforms are available through an open-access repository to ensure transparency, reproducibility, and ongoing evaluation by the scientific community.

The code is fully available at: https://github.com/joaobrainer/head.ai; and the dataset with vignettes and JSON file are fully available in Mendley Data repository [Andrade, Joao (2025), “Head.AI Platform”, Mendeley Data, V2, doi: 10.17632/4fms6cw2s5.2. A strength of this work is the transparency of our approach – we adhered to emerging reporting standards for AI in healthcare and have made all case vignettes and outputs publicly available for reproducibility.

Results

A total of 315 vignettes derived from 215 distinct headache classifications were included in the final analysis. The distribution of headache disorders according to the main diagnostic categories of the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3), revealed a predominance of vascular headaches (Group 6), accounting for 21.6% of cases. This was followed by headaches attributed to substance use or withdrawal (Group 8, 12.1%) and non-vascular intracranial disorders (Group 7, 10.5%). Migraine (Group 1), although highly prevalent in population-based studies, accounted for 7.9% of classified cases in our sample. Detailed comparisons are presented in Tables S1–S3 (Supplementary Material 3). Overall, primary headaches represented 29.2% of our dataset, while secondary headaches were more frequent (70.8%). This distribution reflects our study design rather than clinical prevalence, as we aimed to include cases from all ICHD-3 diagnostic groups, including rare secondary headaches. The diagnostic difficulty, as judged by expert reviewers, was predominantly classified as moderate (57.1%), with 34.3% considered easy and only 8.6% rated as difficult to classify. Although some variation from ICHD-3 reference proportions was observed—for example, a higher representation of cranial/facial structure-related headaches (Group 11: 7.9% vs. 5.4%) and a lower proportion of trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias (Group 3: 4.8% vs. 6.9%).

The distribution of outcomes varied among platforms. Head.AI yielded the highest number of 1 st Hypothesis classifications (n = 282/315), followed by Grok 3.0 (n = 253/315), Claude Sonnet 4.0 (n = 234/315), and Gemini 2.5 (n = 211/315). For 2nd Hypothesis outcomes, Head.AI and Claude Sonnet 4.0 each returned 20 cases, Grok 3.0 returned 12, and Gemini 2.5 returned 12. As for 3rd Hypothesis classifications, Head.AI returned 9 cases, Claude Sonnet 4.0 returned 5, Gemini 2.5 returned 4, and Grok 3.0 returned only 1 case. The No Cited outcome—indicating failure to identify the correct diagnosis among the top three hypotheses—was more frequent in Claude Sonnet 4.0 (n = 59/315), Grok 3.0 (n = 52/315), and Gemini 2.5 (n = 43/315), and occurred least often with Head.AI (n = 7/315). These differences were statistically significant (χ² = 54.32, df = 9, p < 0.001), confirming that the classification performance profile is strongly associated with the diagnostic platform used (Fig. 1). Head.AI returned the correct diagnosis as the top hypothesis in 89.5% of cases, compared to 80.3% for Grok 3.0, 77.1% for Gemini 2.5, and 74.3% for Claude Sonnet 4.0 (Fig. 2).

Compared to Head.AI, all other platforms showed significantly lower odds of returning the correct diagnosis as the 1 st Hypothesis, in a bivariate regression model. Gemini 2.5 had an odds ratio of 0.41 (95% CI: 0.27–0.64, p < 0.001), Grok 3.0 had an OR of 0.50 (95% CI: 0.32–0.77, p = 0.002), and Claude Sonnet 4.0 showed the lowest odds, with an OR of 0.36 (95% CI: 0.23–0.55, p < 0.00001), Table 1. Thus, this unadjusted logistic regression model shows Head.AI with substantial advantage over the other platforms. Compared to Gemini 2.5, the odds of Head.AI returning the correct diagnosis as the top hypothesis were 143.9% higher. When compared to Grok 3.0, this advantage was 100%, and relative to Claude Sonnet 4.0, Head.AI improved the odds by 177.8%. In this unadjusted model, the Brier score was 0.159.

In a logistic regression model adjusted for both diagnostic classification (primary vs. secondary headaches) and case difficulty, Head.AI demonstrated significantly superior performance compared to the other diagnostic platforms in correctly identifying the diagnosis as the first hypothesis. When compared to Gemini 2.5, Head.AI increased the odds of a correct top-ranked diagnosis by 132.6%. Relative to Grok 3.0, the odds were 104.1% higher, and the most pronounced improvement was seen when compared to Claude Sonnet 4.0, with Head.AI yielding a 185.7% increase in odds. The Brier score for this fully adjusted model was 0.153, indicating good calibration and probability accuracy (Table 1).

The unadjusted logistic regression model, which included only the diagnostic platform as a predictor, was used to estimate the probability of each platform correctly identifying the diagnosis as the first hypothesis. Predicted probabilities ranged from 0.74 for Claude Sonnet 4.0 to 0.90 for Head.AI, with Grok 3.0 and Gemini 2.5 yielding intermediate values of 0.80 and 0.77, respectively. The calibration curve indicated that the model’s predicted probabilities aligned reasonably well with observed frequencies across six probability bins. The observed proportion of correct classifications ranged from 67% to 91%, with a tendency toward slight underestimation in the middle ranges and high calibration at higher predicted values, Figure S1 (Supplementary Material 3).

The analysis of Cited Rates—defined as the proportion of cases in which a correct diagnosis appeared among the top three hypotheses (1st, 2nd, or 3rd)—revealed differences across platforms and ICHD-3 diagnostic groups (Fig. 3). Head.AI consistently demonstrated the highest coverage across nearly all categories, with cited rates exceeding 90% in several groups. In contrast, Claude Sonnet 4.0 and Grok 3.0 showed significantly lower citation performance, particularly within less prevalent or more complex diagnostic categories such as groups 10 through 14. Gemini 2.5 exhibited intermediate performance, with modest citation rates across most ICHD groups.

In the stratified analysis by headache classification, platforms displayed variable performance between primary and secondary headache cases. Head.AI achieved a first-hypothesis accuracy of 88.0% in primary cases and 89.3% in secondary cases. In contrast, Gemini 2.5 showed a decline from 85.0% in primary to 72.7% in secondary headaches. Claude Sonnet 4.0 decreased from 82.9% to 71.7%, and Grok 3.0 from 84.2% to 78.1%, Figure S1 (Supplementary Material 3).

In a Poisson regression, using Head.AI as the reference platform, the Poisson regression model showed that all other platforms had significantly higher omission rates—measured as the log rate ratio of failing to cite the correct diagnosis. Grok 3.0 exhibited a log rate ratio of 2.01 (95% CI: 1.96 to 2.05; p < 0.001), Gemini 2.5 had a log rate ratio of 2.07 (95% CI: 2.03 to 2.11; p < 0.001), and Claude Sonnet 4.0 showed the highest omission rate with a log rate ratio of 2.13 (95% CI: 2.09 to 2.18; p < 0.001), Figure S3 (Supplementary Material 3).

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that a large language model (LLM)-based tool, structured upon the full ICHD-3 classification and guided by expert-validated inputs, can achieve high accuracy in classifying a wide sample of clinical headache vignettes. Our algorithm correctly identified the diagnosis as its first hypothesis in 90% of cases, outperforming Claude Sonnet 4.0 (74%), Gemini 2.5 (77%), and Grok 3.0 (80%). This performance was consistent across headache types, and robust even in clinically challenging cases, with first-hypothesis accuracy reaching 74% in the subgroup labeled as “difficult”. These results are higher than those reported in prior work; however, direct comparisons are limited by differences in data sources and scope [16], where diagnostic AI performance was constrained to narrower subsets (e.g., only migraine, tension-type, and cluster headache) and achieved lower overall agreement with specialists.

Previous studies have explored diverse approaches to automated headache diagnosis. Compared to prior headache diagnostic studies, our platform approach employs distinct methodological choices. Katsuki et al. (2023) [3] trained a supervised machine learning model on a real-world clinical dataset but limited its scope to primary headache types and acknowledged the need for external validation. Cowan et al. (2022) [17] validated a rule-based engine focused exclusively on migraine in a prospective study. Costabile et al. (2020) [18] developed a logic-based system (HEAD-ASP) covering the full ICHD-3 spectrum using formal rules but did not evaluate its diagnostic performance against clinical or AI benchmarks. Although HEAD-ASP encoded ICHD-3 criteria using formal logical rules, it did not undergo performance evaluation against clinical benchmarks or comparative assessment with other AI platforms. Our study provides rigorous validation through blinded evaluation across multiple LLM platforms. Zhang et al. (2023) [19] encoded ICHD-3 criteria using prime number logic to explore concurrent diagnoses, offering conceptual insights but no practical tool. In contrast, Head.AI combines an expert-curated ICHD-3 ontology with a general-purpose large language model (GPT-4o). While their prime number logic encoding explored concurrent diagnoses conceptually, it did not result in a practical diagnostic tool. Our platform provides an immediately accessible, clinically applicable solution. While these tools offer general medical consultation capabilities, our platform provides specialized, comprehensive coverage of the complete ICHD-3 taxonomy with expert-curated diagnostic criteria specifically designed for headache classification. Moreover, we implemented a rigorous evaluation wherein the AI-generated diagnoses were assessed in a blinded fashion across multiple LLM platforms, an approach that ensures an unbiased comparison of performance and was not explored in previous studies. We believe this combination of a knowledge-base ontology with multi-LLM blind testing represents a novel methodological balance between symbolic rigor and generative AI flexibility in headache classification.

One important distinction in our approach lies in the structured knowledge foundation used to support the model. While earlier works [15, 16] relied on training AI models using labeled patient data or fixed questionnaires, our model was grounded in an explicit ontology derived from the full ICHD-3 and curated manually by headache specialists. This strategy aligns with recent recommendations [14] for explainable and verifiable AI in medicine and appears to have contributed to the superior calibration observed in our model. Indeed, the calibration curve of our algorithm across 20 adaptive bins revealed that predicted probabilities closely matched observed frequencies, with particularly accurate calibration above 85% predicted probability—demonstrating the model’s reliability not just in classification, but in expressing diagnostic confidence.

Our model’s performance in classifying both primary and secondary headaches is also clinically relevant. While tools such as those by some authors [20, 21] have explored AI-assisted headache classification, they have often done so in restricted language contexts or within pediatric cohorts. Moreover, few systems [16, 22] have tackled secondary headaches, which often require nuanced interpretation of warning signs and exclusion criteria. By including 45 secondary headache vignettes and demonstrating strong diagnostic performance in that group, our results suggest that LLM-based systems can transcend the diagnostic scope of current narrow-focused models. Importantly, less than 2% of cases were left uncited by our model—far below the omission rates described in prior unsupervised models. The use of simulated vignettes enabled structured sampling across the complete ICHD-3 diagnostic taxonomy. Real-world datasets, while valuable, often underrepresent rare conditions, which would prevent comprehensive evaluation of classification performance.

In addition to its diagnostic performance, our algorithm holds promise for medical education. Clinical reasoning around headache classification is notoriously difficult for students and generalists [6] and inter-rater agreement even among neurologists has been shown to be modest. A recent cross-sectional study [15] demonstrated that structured tools can improve accuracy and confidence among non-specialists managing headache cases. Our model, which provides structured, ICHD-3-aligned feedback, could serve a dual role as both a support system and a teaching resource—particularly in low-resource settings or during neurology rotations. This reflects recent calls in the literature for AI models that combine technical performance with interpretability and educational value [13, 14, 22].

Future work will focus on three key areas. First, we will conduct prospective validation studies using de-identified patient records from multiple centers, allowing assessment of model performance under real-world variability. Second, we intend to expand language support beyond Portuguese and English to include Spanish, French, Italian and Mandarin, thereby increasing global applicability and supporting deployment in diverse healthcare environments. Third, we plan to evaluate the platform’s generalizability, safety, and usability in clinical practice through structured implementation studies in the context of primary healthcare in rural areas.

Nonetheless, this study has some limitations. The model was developed and validated using clinical vignettes written in Brazilian Portuguese, which may limit its direct generalizability. Although the GPT-based API performs translation and semantic processing across languages, prospective testing in English and other languages is important. Second, all test cases were synthetic and created by neurologists, which, while improving internal validity, may not fully reflect the linguistic variability, comorbidity, or ambiguity present in real-world encounters. Fourth, we did not assess user experience, interface usability, or integration into live clinical environments. The intended users of Head.AI are healthcare professionals and medical trainees. While the platform is freely accessible, its results should be interpreted within a clinical context, and not as a substitute for professional judgment. These aspects should be addressed in future research phases, along with prospective validation using real patient data, in both hospital and outpatient settings. Finally, each vignette was linked to a single ICHD-3 diagnosis. We acknowledge that in real-world clinical scenarios, multiple concurrent headache diagnoses can occur. This is a known limitation of both the ICHD-3 system and current AI implementations.

Conclusion

Head.AI is a free, user-friendly, and publicly accessible clinical diagnostic support platform (www.head-ai.com.br) that leverages a GPT-4o-based architecture and an expert-curated implementation of the full ICHD-3 classification to classify headache disorders from unstructured clinical text. In our study, it achieved high first-hypothesis diagnostic accuracy, demonstrated strong performance across both primary and secondary headache types, and maintained robust calibration across probability ranges. These results surpass those reported by previously published models, particularly due to the platform’s broader diagnostic scope, structured knowledge base, and reliable performance in complex cases. By combining high technical accuracy with open access, multilingual processing, and transparent outputs, Head.AI may represent a significant step forward in the democratization of headache education—and potentially, in diagnostic support as well.
