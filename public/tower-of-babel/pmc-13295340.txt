Association between preeclampsia and eclampsia in Black women with endometriosis: a scoping review
Authors: Calbeth C. Alaribe, Kimberly D. Sapre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWomen with endometriosis are at increased risk of preeclampsia and eclampsia, leading causes of maternal mortality among Black women in the United States. Despite this, Black women are significantly underdiagnosed with endometriosis. This scoping review examines how peer-reviewed literature addresses the association between endometriosis and preeclampsia/eclampsia in Black women in the US.MethodA comprehensive literature search was conducted across nine databases and PROSPERO for original articles published from inception through March 2024. Eligible studies were required to be US-based, include reproductive-aged Black women (15–49 years) with laparoscopically confirmed endometriosis, and report outcomes of preeclampsia and/or eclampsia.ResultsThe search identified 370 records; 19 full-text articles were reviewed. None met all inclusion criteria, primarily because most studies were conducted outside the US or lacked race-stratified outcomes. As a result, no eligible studies investigated the association between endometriosis and preeclampsia or eclampsia in Black women in the US.ConclusionsThis review reveals a significant gap in research examining endometriosis in Black women and its association with adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preeclampsia and eclampsia. The findings emphasize the need for inclusive, race-conscious research to inform clinical care and reduce maternal health disparities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27739-w.

Background

Endometriosis is a chronic, inflammatory gynecologic condition affecting an estimated 10% of reproductive-aged women in the United States (US) and over 190 million women worldwide [1–3]. Although its etiology remains unclear, neurological, immunological, genetic, and hormonal factors are thought to contribute to disease development [4, 5]. The condition manifests with a wide range of debilitating symptoms, including pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, menorrhagia, and infertility [4]. Despite its high prevalence, the condition is underdiagnosed, particularly among Black women [6]. This diagnostic gap stems from several factors: symptom overlap with other conditions [7], reliance on invasive diagnostic procedures such as laparoscopy [8], and implicit bias in clinical care [1]. For many women, diagnosis is significantly delayed. Globally, the average time from symptom onset to diagnosis is approximately seven years [9]. In the US, Black women experience even longer delays, often influenced by structural and institutional racism in healthcare [1, 8]. A study by Bougie et al. [6] found that Black women are nearly 50% less likely to be diagnosed with endometriosis compared to White women.

Endometriosis is associated with severe comorbidities, including preeclampsia and eclampsia—hypertensive disorders of pregnancy that are among the leading causes of maternal morbidity and mortality and disproportionately affect Black women [10–16]. Prior studies have demonstrated a modest but statistically significant association between these disorders and endometriosis, with reported odds ratios ranging from 1.19 to 1.20 (95% CI 1.01–1.39) compared to those without the condition [12, 13]. Evidence suggests that immunologic and vascular alterations in endometriosis may contribute to preeclampsia through overlapping pathophysiological mechanisms [10, 11]. Endometriosis triggers immune activation, with elevated inflammatory markers such as B and T lymphocytes, natural killer cells, and neutrophils [11]. This systemic inflammation promotes oxidative stress, which has been implicated in the development of preeclampsia [11]. Preeclampsia itself is characterized by impaired spiral artery remodeling in the myometrium, resulting in increased vascular resistance and hypertension during pregnancy [11, 17]. Although research has documented correlations between endometriosis and adverse pregnancy outcomes such as preeclampsia and eclampsia [10–14], very few studies have examined these relationships specifically in Black women. Currently, there are no established clinical guidelines specifically addressing the management of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy in patients with a history of endometriosis. Furthermore, evidence demonstrating that treatment of endometriosis reduces the risk of preeclampsia or related outcomes remains limited and warrants further investigation. As a result, there remains a critical need to better understand how endometriosis relates to hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, particularly among populations at highest risk, including Black women.

Laparoscopy with histologic confirmation remains the gold standard for diagnosing endometriosis [18] and is a key reason for delays in diagnosis among Black women [19]. In a retrospective study of 124 reproductive-aged women, Li et al. [20] found that Black women, even when undergoing laparoscopy for secondary indications such as fibroids or abnormal imaging, were diagnosed with endometriosis an average of 15.2 months after initial presentation with pelvic pain, compared with 13.6 months for White women. These delays are compounded by provider bias in assessing pain thresholds, which influences the likelihood and timing of confirmatory diagnostic procedures [20].

Racial bias in healthcare, particularly in the assessment and treatment of pain, continues to shape clinical decision-making and perpetuate disparities. Medical education and practice often reinforce misconceptions rooted in the legacy of slavery [21, 22]. For example, a 2016 study found that nearly half of medical students and residents endorsed at least one false belief about biological differences in pain perception between Black and White patients, such as the assumption that Black individuals have thicker skin or less sensitive nerve endings [23]. These misconceptions contribute to the systematic underestimation of pain in Black patients, resulting in delayed or inadequate treatment [24]. In the context of endometriosis, such biases may delay symptom recognition, reduce referrals for diagnostic evaluation, and worsen outcomes. Supporting this, a retrospective study by Hen et al. [25] found that Black patients were significantly less likely than non-Black patients to be referred to pelvic pain specialists (14.5% vs. 17.8%, P = .001).

Delayed screening and diagnosis of endometriosis postpone treatment and may increase the risk of preeclampsia and eclampsia. Data from the 2016–2017 Enhanced Vital Records show that Black women in the US are five times more likely to die from preeclampsia and eclampsia than White women [15]. Amid such heightened vulnerability, many studies examining the relationship between endometriosis and preeclampsia fail to account for racial disparities. For instance, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Lalani et al. [12] found that women with endometriosis had higher odds of adverse maternal outcomes, including preeclampsia (odds ratio [OR] 1.18, 95% CI 1.01–1.39). Similarly, Drummond et al. [26] reported that women with endometriosis who conceived spontaneously were at increased risk of preeclampsia compared with women without endometriosis (risk ratio [RR] 1.47, 95% CI 1.13–1.89, P = .003). Neither study, however, stratified outcomes by race. A scoping review is therefore needed to identify existing research that evaluates the intersection of endometriosis, preeclampsia, and eclampsia in Black women in the US.

Methods

This scoping review followed the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines. The study protocol was registered with the Open Science Framework on August 6, 2025 (https://osf.io/egu38). The review aimed to evaluate study design, sample size, participant selection, validity, and whether race or ethnicity was included as a variable. The primary research question was: How does peer-reviewed literature inform the association between endometriosis, preeclampsia, and eclampsia in Black women in the US?

Search engines

A systematic search of peer-reviewed literature published in English was conducted across nine databases and PROSPERO: PubMed, CINAHL Plus with Full Text, ScienceDirect, Cochrane Library, Academic Search Complete, eBook Medical Collection (EBSCOhost), Health Source – Consumer Edition, Health Source: Nursing/Academic Edition, and Google Scholar. The search included articles from the database inception through March 2024.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were eligible if they met all the following criteria: (1) peer-reviewed original research conducted in the US; (2) included reproductive-aged Black women (15–49 years) with laparoscopically confirmed endometriosis; and (3) reported preeclampsia and/or eclampsia as an outcome. Only studies with race-stratified results were included. Surgical confirmation was required to minimize misclassification bias, as reliance on administrative coding (e.g., ICD-9/10) may result in variable diagnostic accuracy and underascertainment of endometriosis. Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) reviews, meta-analyses, or overviews; (2) studies conducted outside the US; (3) studies without race or ethnicity data; (4) studies that did not assess both endometriosis and preeclampsia/eclampsia; or (5) populations outside the reproductive age range. The review was restricted to full-text articles published in English. Reference lists of included articles were manually screened for additional relevant studies.

Study selection was conducted in two stages. First, titles and abstracts were screened for relevance based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Articles that met initial screening criteria underwent full-text review. Screening and full-text review were conducted by a single reviewer. While this approach ensured consistency in study selection, it may introduce selection bias and limit reproducibility, and is therefore acknowledged as a limitation.

Search strategy

The search strategy combined keywords related to ‘endometriosis’, ‘preeclampsia’, and ‘eclampsia’ in article titles or abstracts. Full search strategies, including MeSH terms for all databases and PROSPERO, are provided in Supplementary Appendix (B) Race and ethnicity were not included as primary search terms due to variability and inconsistency in how these variables are reported and indexed across studies [27]; instead, race and ethnicity data were assessed during full-text screening to determine eligibility based on predefined inclusion criteria.

Results

The search initially identified 370 records across nine databases and PROSPERO. After removing duplicates and non-peer-reviewed articles, 209 titles and abstracts were screened. Nineteen full-text articles were reviewed, but none met all the inclusion criteria. Three appeared relevant but were excluded because they either (1) lacked laparoscopically confirmed diagnoses of endometriosis, (2) or did not stratify outcomes by race or ethnicity. Consequently, no eligible studies specifically examined the association between endometriosis and preeclampsia or eclampsia in Black women in the US. Discussion

Main findings

This scoping review identified no peer-reviewed studies that directly investigated the association between endometriosis and preeclampsia or eclampsia among Black women in the US. Three US-based studies were excluded because they did not stratify outcomes by race or ethnicity [28, 33, 34]. Although all three studies collected race data [28, 33, 34], it was used only as a covariate in multivariable models without presenting race-stratified outcomes. While adjustment for race is common in epidemiologic research, this approach may obscure disparities by treating race as a nuisance variable rather than a construct through which systemic racism shapes health outcomes [45]. In doing so, it implicitly models a context in which racism does not exist, thereby masking meaningful differences across racial groups [45]. For hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, leading contributors to maternal mortality among Black women, this limitation is particularly consequential. Without stratified analyses or explicit consideration of structural mechanisms, such approaches limit the interpretability and equity relevance of existing evidence.

Building on this limitation, a key unanswered question is whether race/ethnicity modifies the association between endometriosis and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. We hypothesize that race/ethnicity functions as an effect modifier, such that both the magnitude and clinical expression of this association differ across racial groups. Several mechanisms may underlie this relationship. Black women experience well-documented delays in the diagnosis of endometriosis [6, 46], often presenting with more advanced or untreated disease [47]. Advanced-stage endometriosis is associated with increased pelvic adhesions and fibrosis, contributing to heightened systemic inflammation and vascular dysfunction, pathways implicated in the pathogenesis of preeclampsia [11, 48, 49]. These delays may result in more severe disease during pregnancy, thereby amplifying these pathophysiologic processes. In addition, structural inequities, including reduced access to specialty care [20], delays in referral [25], and cumulative exposure to chronic stressors associated with systemic racism [50], may contribute to increased allostatic load [51], consistent with the weathering hypothesis, which describes accelerated physiological deterioration due to chronic social disadvantage [52]. These processes may exacerbate both disease progression and pregnancy-related complications. Black women also have a higher prevalence of chronic hypertension [53], a known risk factor for preeclampsia [54], which may further increase susceptibility to preeclampsia.

Importantly, these pathways suggest that disparities are not solely attributable to differences in exposure prevalence but may reflect differences in how endometriosis translates into adverse maternal outcomes. As such, analytic approaches that rely solely on race adjustment may obscure these dynamics. Future research should explicitly assess effect measure modification (EMM) and incorporate complementary approaches, including mediation, decomposition, and quantitative bias analyses, to disentangle the contributions of diagnostic delay, disease severity, and structural determinants of health.

Beyond the lack of race-stratified analyses, additional patterns across the excluded literature further clarify the nature of this evidence gap. The majority of studies were conducted outside the US, primarily in Europe and Canada [10, 14, 29–32, 35, 37, 42–44], with additional studies from Asia and Oceania, including Taiwan [41], South Korea [39], Turkey [38, 40], and Australia [36]. This geographic distribution limits the applicability of findings to populations most affected by maternal health disparities, particularly given that the US has the highest maternal mortality rate among developed nations [55]. Across all reviewed studies, none performed race-stratified analyses of the association between endometriosis and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. Furthermore, most studies did not report race or ethnicity descriptively, if at all. Among the few US-based studies that collected race data, race was typically included only as a covariate in adjusted models, without reporting participant distribution by race or presenting race-specific outcomes. Notably, even when race data were collected, the proportion of Black participants was either not reported or represented a minute percentage of the study population, further limiting the ability to assess disparities. Together, these patterns highlight a striking shortfall in US-based, equity-focused research and demonstrate that the current evidence base is constrained by fundamental methodological limitations, including insufficient representation of Black women, the absence of race-conscious analytic frameworks, and inconsistent reporting of demographic data.

The absence of eligible studies underscores a critical gap in the literature and raises concerns that the exclusion of Black women from research may contribute to misdiagnosis, undertreatment, and adverse maternal outcomes. Given the limited evidence, it is critical to examine how existing literature on endometriosis, preeclampsia, eclampsia, and race frames these associations and what methodological limitations may explain the persistent disparities.

Comparison with existing literature

The lack of eligible studies likely reflects broader structural gaps in the research landscape and several factors that contribute to them. First, Black women remain underrepresented in many endometriosis studies, often driven by the persistent misconception that the condition is less prevalent in Black women [1], despite prior literature identifying endometriosis as a significant contributor to gynecologic surgery and hospitalization among Black women in the US [56]. This results in insufficient sample sizes to support meaningful race-stratified analyses. Second, endometriosis is underdiagnosed in Black women [6], which may limit their inclusion in clinical studies. Third, endometriosis research has historically not prioritized racial and ethnic disparities [1, 57, 58], leading to a lack of intentional study designs that incorporate equity-focused analyses. Finally, publication practices may further contribute to this gap, as studies that do not include diverse populations or fail to report race-specific findings may still be published without addressing these limitations. Together, these factors suggest that the absence of evidence reflects systemic gaps in the design, conduct, and reporting of research.

Despite the known association between endometriosis and preeclampsia and eclampsia, most studies either misguidedly adjust for race [28, 33, 34], fail to collect race data, or stratify outcomes by race [10, 14, 29–32, 37, 42–44, 59]. For example, Vélez et al. [59] conducted a population-based retrospective analysis of endometriosis and adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preeclampsia, placental disorders, and postpartum hemorrhage in Ontario, Canada. Although the study adjusted for maternal age, socioeconomic status, and fibroid history, race was excluded as a primary variable. Only a secondary bias analysis, based on population-level data, suggested a slightly increased risk of preeclampsia among Black women with endometriosis [59]. Similarly, a retrospective study conducted by Lafluer et al. [10] examined endometriosis and severe maternal morbidity outcomes, such as preeclampsia, and adjusted for comorbidities, socioeconomic status, and place of residence, yet race was not collected as a variable. This reflects a broader methodological problem: race is often excluded or relegated to secondary analyses, which limits the interpretability and applicability of findings for populations most at risk.

Although the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) Revitalization Act of 1993 mandated the inclusion and analysis of sex, gender, racial, and ethnic data in clinical research [60, 61], endometriosis studies continue to focus primarily on White populations. A 2021 retrospective study by Christ et al. examining endometriosis diagnoses in the US (2006–2015) reported higher rates among White women compared with non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and Asian women [62]. These findings may be confounded by selection bias but are often misinterpreted as evidence of lower prevalence in non-White populations. Of the 332,056 women included, 136,933 were non-Hispanic White, and only 11,421 were non-Hispanic Black. Among the 2,863 women diagnosed with endometriosis, 1,304 were White and only 85 were Black [62]. The study did not address these stark disparities in sample size. Such disproportion undermines the generalizability of results and perpetuates disparities in diagnosis, treatment, and outcomes for Black women.

When research continues to suggest that endometriosis is less prevalent in Black women, without accounting for sampling or diagnostic bias, clinicians may be less likely to suspect or diagnose the condition in this population. Furthermore, when endometriosis is suspected, Black women are less likely to be offered minimally invasive procedures such as laparoscopic surgery. In a scoping review by Westwood et al. [63], White women were significantly more likely to undergo minimally invasive procedures, while Black women were more often subjected to open abdominal surgeries, which carry greater surgical risks and higher mortality rates.

Further implications

While race is a social construct, it remains a powerful driver of health disparities and must be consistently captured in clinical research alongside other demographic variables such as age, sex, and socioeconomic status. Addressing the persistent underrepresentation of Black women in endometriosis research is essential for advancing reproductive health equity. Future research should leverage existing datasets, including vital statistics and electronic medical records (EMRs), to investigate racial disparities in endometriosis diagnosis, treatment, and pregnancy outcomes. The 2023 Joint Commission mandate [64], which requires race and ethnicity data collection, presents a timely opportunity to embed equity into research infrastructure.

Building on these infrastructure changes, research strategies should prioritize approaches most likely to generate meaningful and actionable insights. Retrospective analyses of EMRs can shed light on diagnostic delays, treatment gaps, and maternal outcomes across diverse populations, including underserved groups often excluded from prospective studies. Such analyses may also clarify whether disparities are driven by differences in healthcare access, insurance coverage, or comorbidities. In addition, analytic approaches such as EMM can further distinguish whether associations differ by race, avoiding the limitations of simple adjustment [45]. Furthermore, prospective cohort studies with intentional oversampling of Black women would yield race-stratified data on endometriosis and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, including preeclampsia and eclampsia. Incorporating qualitative research, such as patient interviews, focus groups, and community-based participatory methods, can further contextualize these disparities and ensure that research priorities align with patient experiences. Taken together, these strategies would strengthen the research foundation and promote more equitable care for women who have historically been overlooked in reproductive health research.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this scoping review is the breadth and rigor of its search strategy. The protocol incorporated nine major databases and one prospective register, developed in collaboration with an expert health sciences librarian. In addition, the methodology adhered to the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines [65], promoting transparency and reproducibility.

This review also has limitations. First, study selection and screening were performed by a single reviewer, which may have introduced bias and precluded assessment of inter-rater reliability. Second, the requirement for laparoscopic confirmation of endometriosis may have limited the number of eligible studies, particularly as many population-based datasets rely on ICD-9/10 codes to define endometriosis. While this criterion was selected to improve diagnostic specificity and reduce misclassification bias, it may have reduced generalizability and excluded relevant studies, particularly among underserved populations with limited access to surgical diagnosis. Importantly, misclassification bias in administrative data may not be uniform across populations; therefore, reliance on ICD-based definitions may obscure disparities rather than clarify them. Future research should consider sensitivity analyses using administrative datasets while accounting for potential differential misclassification.

Conclusion

This scoping review revealed a critical gap in the existing literature: the absence of studies examining the association between endometriosis and preeclampsia or eclampsia in Black women in the US. This omission reflects systemic patterns of exclusion and highlights the urgent need to develop racially inclusive research frameworks that intentionally prioritize representation from historically marginalized populations. Future studies should focus on the relationship between endometriosis and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, with particular attention to how race influences risk, diagnosis, and outcomes. Additionally, given emerging evidence linking endometriosis [66, 67] to an increased risk of long-term cardiovascular disease, future studies should adopt a life-course perspective to understand how early gynecologic conditions contribute to later cardiovascular risk. Addressing these gaps is essential for advancing women’s health equity, guiding clinical practice, and shaping policies to reduce preventable maternal deaths among Black women.
