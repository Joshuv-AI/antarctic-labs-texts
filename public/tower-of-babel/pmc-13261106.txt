Single-nucleus brain transcriptomics reveals microglia dysfunction in multiple system atrophy

ABSTRACT
Multiple system atrophy (MSA) is a rare, age-related neurodegenerative disease that shares clinical and pathological features with Parkinson’s disease (PD) but presents a more devastating disease course. To elucidate the distinct cellular pathophysiology, we performed single-nucleus RNA sequencing on postmortem striatal brain tissue from 7 MSA and 12 PD patients, and 10 non-neurological cases. Here, we show significant compositional differences in astroglia and microglia subtypes, while oligodendroglia and neurons are comparable. PD brains show abundant microglia expressing MHC class II HLA haplotypes, indicative of a proinflammatory state, alongside more homeostatic astrocytes. In contrast, MSA lack activated microglia but has more reactive astrocytes compared to PD. Transcriptomic analysis suggests compromised oligodendrocyte signaling in MSA, with microglia being in a state of immune tolerance or exhaustion. Microglia derived from iPSC exposed to patient cerebrospinal fluid exhibit reduced phagocytic activity, especially in MSA. These findings underscore a dysfunctional immune response in MSA as a potential contributor to the more severe pathophysiology of MSA.

Introduction

Multiple system atrophy (MSA) is a rare neurodegenerative disease characterized by progressive motor dysfunction. Currently, there is no cure, and only alleviating care is available, ultimately leading to a fatal outcome typically within 6 to 10 years of diagnosis1. Clinically, MSA is categorized as an atypical Parkinsonian disorder as it shares many of the motoric clinical manifestations with Parkinson’s disease (PD). However, MSA differs from PD as patients do not respond equally well to dopamine replacement treatments, and they present a clinical picture marked by autonomic failure and cerebellar involvement such as ataxia2. Neuropathologically, both MSA and PD are classified as alpha(α)-﻿synucleinopathies, with the distinction that aggregated α-synuclein is enriched in oligodendroglia in MSA and in neurons in PD3. Unlike PD, no familial genes for MSA have yet been identified despite the severity of the condition, but a recent genome-wide association analysis identified possible risk loci, highlighting the complex, multifactorial pathogenesis at the core of MSA4. Recent studies have also implicated environmental factors, such as infections, in the development of MSA5. Notably, MSA shows signs of strong neuroinflammation in the form of increased microglial/macrophage numbers6–8, as well as immune system activation9–11, pointing to a significant role of microglia in MSA pathology. However, microglia cells are dynamically transitioning across multiple states depending on the conditions, with this change not necessarily being reflected by a change in microglia numbers but rather with a change in the transcriptome12,13. Therefore, unraveling microglia transcriptional phenotypes in MSA in interaction with other cellular changes can provide us with a deeper understanding of disease states.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) technology offers a unique opportunity to examine pathological changes at the cellular level and identify specific cell subpopulations affected by neurodegenerative diseases. Importantly, snRNA-seq has been applied to postmortem brain samples of Alzheimer’s disease and revealed novel molecular mechanisms that dramatically improved our understanding of disease pathogenesis14–17. Thus, to uncover MSA-specific disease mechanisms at high resolution, we conducted snRNA-seq analysis of the striatum from brains obtained from MSA and PD patients and non-neurological controls (CTRLs). We specifically focused on the striatum, the largest region within the basal ganglia that is equally affected in both PD and MSA18, due to the progressive dopaminergic denervation resulting from striatonigral degeneration19. We report compositional and transcriptional changes in specific subpopulations of neurons and glia cells in both MSA and PD. Importantly, we show that microglia in MSA appear to be in an immunosuppressive or tolerant state compared to PD, implicating microglia responses in the distinct pathophysiology of these diseases.

Results

The single-nucleus transcriptomics of the striatum in MSA, PD and control brains

To analyze cellular changes in MSA and PD and their transcriptomic differences, we selected the striatum from postmortem brains of 7 patients with MSA, 12 patients with PD and 10 CTRL cases matched for post-mortem interval (PMI). The presence of pathology was confirmed for both PD and MSA brains. The age was slightly lower in the MSA group compared to the PD group, driven by the younger age of onset and faster disease progression in MSA patients (Table 1 and Supplementary Dataset 1). The sample processing workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1a. In total, we sequenced 130,782 nuclei from 29 samples with a mean of 115,298 [25,811-596,564] reads per nucleus (Supplementary Fig. 1a–g). Following filtering for sequencing depth, doublet removal (Supplementary Fig. 1h, i) and ambient RNA removal (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b), we were left with 117,191 nuclei and 29 samples with a median of 4688 [514–159,826] UMIs per nucleus and a median of 2045 [305–11,876] genes per nucleus. The cells were embedded in a UMAP (Fig. 1b), and major cell types were identified based on expression of known markers (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 2c and Supplementary Dataset 2). Notably, samples were well integrated (Fig. 1d, visualization of covariates on UMAPs in Supplementary Fig. 3). Statistical analyses of covariate impact did not show any significant association (Supplementary Fig. 4a–c and Supplementary Dataset 3). To provide robust statistical confirmation for the lack of large covariate-mediated effects on our dataset, we implemented the single-cell Interpretable Tensor Decomposition (scITD) package that uncovers variable gene programs across samples in a sc/snRNA-seq dataset20. Importantly, none of the major cell types showed covariate-associated gene expression signatures (sex, postmortem interval, age, brain bank, flowcell and cDNA processing batches), except OPCs showing potential and rather minor impact of age, flowcell, and cDNA processing batches (Supplementary Fig. 5). To get an overview of compositional changes across conditions, we compared the abundance of all major cell classes identified in the striatum (Fig. 1e–g). To avoid using cell proportions that bias compositional analysis, i.e., since proportions are always 100% - increase in one subtype will lead to relative decrease in all others, we implemented dedicated tools for compositional analysis of snRNA-seq data. To this end, we used Compositional Data Analysis (CoDA) as in the Cacoa package21,22 that allows for independent analysis of cellular proportions and was successfully implemented to identify differences in cell abundance in schizophrenia postmortem brains21. As expected, there was a significant general increase in astrocyte and microglia cell numbers in PD when compared to controls (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Datasets 4 and 5). Surprisingly, when MSA was compared to CTRLs, there were no such changes (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Datasets 4 and 5), whereas when MSA was compared to PD, MSA brains had significantly fewer microglia cells (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Datasets 4 and 5). Furthermore, MSA brains presented increased numbers of pericytes/endothelial cells, GABAergic interneurons (later just GABAergic neurons), medium spiny neurons (MSN) and peripheral immune cells (Fig. 1g). Using an alternative tool for identification of compositional changes, scCODA23, in PD vs MSA comparison, we were able to validate increase in microglia in PD, as well as increase in GABAergic neurons and MSNs in MSA (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Importantly, these changes were not associated with donor age (Supplementary Fig. 6e). The lower microglial proportion in MSA vs PD is of particular interest and may suggest a subdued microglia response that is accompanied by an increased immune cell infiltration and regulation of neurovascular coupling in MSA. The relative higher MSN and GABAergic neuronal proportions may indicate a different vulnerability of these neuronal subtypes in MSA compared to PD, as suggested by a topographic susceptibility of only certain MSN subpopulations to neurodegeneration in MSA24.

To compare the magnitude of transcriptomic changes across various cell types, we measured overall expression changes for each annotated cell type between conditions using expression distances as before21 (Fig. 1h–j). Interestingly, only few significant changes were detected, and in general the changes were rather minor, especially for MSA, where only oligodendrocytes presented significant changes (Fig. 1h). For PD, the only large change was in neurons, which was not significant due to high variability (Fig. 1i). However, significant gene expression changes were observed in glial cell types - oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), astrocytes and oligodendroglia (Fig. 1i). When comparing MSA to PD, expression distances were significant for oligodendrocytes and astroglia (Fig. 1j). Since in transcriptomic space, samples belonging to CTRL, MSA and PD were well-integrated (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 3), it is likely that cell type-specific transcriptional changes in MSA or PD do not extend to large transcriptomic effects and are rather focused on specific cellular processes.

Minor compositional changes in neuronal subtypes across PD and MSA

To gain deeper insight into how specific cell subtypes and/or cell states are affected in MSA, we performed high-level annotations for cell subtypes/states for each major cell class (markers are shown in Supplementary Dataset 6). For the neuronal lineage, we identified 14 subtypes: 11 GABAergic, 2 GLUergic, and 3 MSN subtypes. GABAergic neurons were subdivided based on cardinal subtype markers (PVALB, SST, VIP, ID2, PAX6), and, additionally, we named a few minor clusters using lineage-derived markers (such as NKX2-1, NR2F2), classical GABAergic interneuron protein markers (for instance, CALB2 – for calretinin protein), or cluster-specific markers (for instance, DCC or ST18). GLUergic subtypes were subdivided based on expression of SATB2 (common marker of excitatory neurons) and RELN (labeling a unique population of excitatory neurons, see below). MSNs were subdivided according to those expressing dopamine D1 (MSN_DRD1) or D2 receptor (MSN_DRD2), as well as a third MSN type (MSN_FOXP2) characterized by high expression of forkhead box protein P2 (FOXP2) (Fig. 2a, b). To cross-validate our annotations with other studies, we used a recently published striatum GABAergic interneuron dataset25. Using a cosine similarity approach, we showed that most of our GABAergic neuron subtypes had clear counterparts in the Garma et al. dataset (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b), while a few others exhibited unique molecular signatures in our dataset, which might be explained by regional differences in sampling between our and Garma et al. studies. Using CoDA to identify compositional changes in subtypes of neurons (ranges are shown in Supplementary Dataset 4), we observed the only major difference - for the GABAergic_MEIS2_ST18 subpopulation, with a significant decrease in PD but not in MSA when compared to controls (Fig. 2c–e). No major changes in any other subtype were observed (Fig. 2c–e), which was confirmed by scCODA (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Likewise, there were no notable expression shifts, indicating no major cell-type or disease-specific transcriptional changes in the neuronal population (Fig. 2f). Interestingly, when analyzing compositional changes using densities of cells on UMAP by Cacoa, which can be called cluster-free method of compositional data analysis (an alternative to more usual cluster-based methods), we detected a visible decrease in two neuronal populations in both PD and MSA, namely GABAergic_PVALB and GLUergic_RELN subtypes (Fig. 2g). The former was much diminished in PD and MSA, and thus the changes in abundance could not be measured by the cluster-based analysis provided by CoDA which requires presence of cells in both conditions for comparison, not allowing for meaningful statistical validation. Nevertheless, when looking at cell type distribution stratified per sample, PVALB cells are present across multiple control brains and completely absent in the disease brains (Supplementary Dataset 7). The identification of GLUergic_RELN neurons in the striatum is a noteworthy finding in itself, since the only non-GABAergic neuron subtype described in the striatum so far are cholinergic neurons27, and GLUergic_RELN are not cholinergic (Fig. 2b). Further, RELN is a key secreted extracellular matrix protein that modulates synaptic plasticity28, and given its wide-spread expression in GABAergic neurons, including GABAergic_PVALB (Fig. 2b), local Reelin signaling might have an important role for neuronal networks in the striatum. Importantly, we searched through human brain snRNA-seq datasets for cells resembling our GLUergic_RELN subtype and found that CADPS2+ cluster of neurons in the midbrain29 also co-expresses RELN and has high similarity to GLUergic_RELN neurons (Supplementary Fig. 9), further substantiating the existence of RELN + excitatory neurons in subcortical regions of the human brain.

Higher proportion of homeostatic astroglia in PD

We next analyzed glial cell types and annotated subpopulations of astrocytes, oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) and oligodendrocytes (microglia was analyzed separately, see below). Based on markers from previous studies, we identified two astroglial subpopulations, a reactive (AS_reactive) and a homeostatic (AS_homeostatic)30, three oligodendroglia subpopulations31, and OPCs and differentiation-committed OPCs (COPs) (Fig. 3a, b, markers shown in Supplementary Dataset 6). For astroglia, we observed a decrease in AS_reactive in PD vs CTRLs, and a decrease in AS_homeostatic in MSA vs PD (Fig. 3c–e and Supplementary Datasets 4 and 7). These compositional changes were cross validated with scCODA (Supplementary Fig. 6c). The significant different expression distance of AS_reactive and AS_homeostatic astroglia in PD and not MSA supports a more dynamic transition of astroglial states in PD compared to MSA (Fig. 3f). Homeostatic astrocytes typically function as primary energy suppliers to neurons32, thus suggesting an increased astroglial supportive role in PD but not in MSA. Consistent with this, we observe in PD astroglia (both homeostatic and reactive) upregulations in metabolic pathways, including those linked to electron transport chain, mitochondrial function, aerobic respiration, and ATP synthesis (Fig. 4a, b). Conversely, in MSA, we observe downregulation of stress response-related pathways and metabolic processes, together with upregulation of pathways including, but not limited to, those associated with platelet activation, regulation of epithelial to mesenchymal transition and interleukin (IL)-6 production, pointing to blood-brain barrier (BBB) defects and inflammatory responses (Fig. 4a, b). Compromised oligodendrocytes in MSA signal to microglia and perivascular macrophages

When analyzing oligodendroglia, a compositional change towards lower numbers of the oligodendroglia population expressing SLC5A11 (OL-SLC5A11) was observed in both MSA and PD (Fig. 3c, d, respectively, and Supplementary Datasets 4 and 7). These cells are considered homeostatic white matter tract oligodendrocytes31. These compositional changes were cross-validated with scCODA (Supplementary Fig. 6c). The MSA OL-SLC5A11 subtype showed an upregulation of pathways related to viral processes, suggesting innate immune responses of this cell subtype in MSA (in Fig. 4c, pathways are selected based on their relevance to disease etiology from the full list of significant pathways in Supplementary Dataset 8). Further support for this notion was an upregulation of Toll-Like receptor 1 (TLR1) gene expression in the MSA OL-LINC01608 and OL_SLC5A11 subpopulations (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Dataset 9), which aligns with prior findings of TLR1 overexpression in the striatal regions of MSA brains33.

To gain better insight into potential immune signaling originating from the MSA oligodendroglia, we performed a cell-to-cell signaling analysis. Several prominent APOE ligand-receptor pairs previously associated with AD pathogenesis and inflammation34–36 were upregulated in both MSA and PD (Supplementary Fig. 10a,b). One of the most represented genes was LRP1 encoding low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1, a transmembrane protein previously shown to be related to clearance of beta-amyloid, myelin debris and apoptotic cells37–39. Moreover, LRP1 has also been related to PD ﻿α-synuclein cell transmission40,41. First, we identified much stronger upregulation of LRP1 expression in MSA oligodendrocytes than in PD oligodendrocytes compared to CTRLs (Fig. 4e). Next, we observed a massive increase in LRP1-dependent signaling from multiple cell types, including neurons, microglia, perivascular macrophages (PVMs) and astrocytes towards oligodendroglia (Fig. 4f).

The decrease in oligodendrocytes was reflected in a diminished OPC pool, but only in PD (Fig. 3d), and no compositional changes were detected for COPs (Fig. 3c–e and Supplementary Datasets 4 and 7). This suggests that in PD the reduction (Fig. 3c-e) and potential dysfunction (e.g., based on gene expression changes, Fig. 3f and Supplementary Datasets 8 and 9) of oligodendrocytes might derive from a decline/dysfunction of OPCs, whereas in MSA, OPCs are relatively unchanged and changes may be directly related to oligodendroglia disturbances. When analyzing the expression of genes previously associated with MSA4,42 we notably observed Tubulin Promoting Protein (p25alpha/TPPP) overexpression in MSA oligodendroglia and differential expression of the phospholipase A2 (PLA2G4C) between MSA and PD in astrocytes, oligodendrocytes and OPCs (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Dataset 9).

Subdued microglial responses in MSA, in contrast to increased numbers of activated microglia in PD

We next analyzed subpopulations of resident immune cells in the striatum, which were mainly represented by microglia. Using previously established gene expression markers43,44, we identified four microglia subtypes/states and a subpopulation of perivascular macrophages (PVMs) (Fig. 5a, b, markers in Supplementary Dataset 6). Using pseudotime analysis, we identified two trajectories from steady-state microglia to activated microglia or PVMs, respectively (Fig. 5c, d). Interestingly, we also observed a transition from the intermediate1 state to PVMs (Fig. 5d). To identify pseudotime-related genes associated with each trajectory, we applied a generalized additive mixed model (see Methods) and plotted the smoothed expression (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 12). For both trajectories we identified cell subtype-specific markers described by pseudotime, e.g., AIF1 for the activated microglia trajectory (Supplementary Fig. 12b) or F13A1, CD163, MRC1 for the PVM trajectory (Fig. 5e). For the activated microglia trajectory, top 50 pseudotime genes were functionally related to antigen binding and more (Supplementary Dataset 10). For the PVM trajectory, we identified pseudotime-related repression of genes associated with microglia followed by induction of genes associated with PVMs (Fig. 5e), which were functionally related to GTPase activity among other things (Supplementary Dataset 11). At the same time, we noted a significant reduction in the intermediate2 microglia state in MSA (Fig. 5f, h), which is the primary microglia state transitioning from steady-state towards the activated microglia pool (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 12a). These compositional changes were also found when running the analysis with scCODA (Supplementary Fig. 6d). Stratification of samples into age bins excluded age as a major contributor to the observed differences (Supplementary Fig 6f). To further validate the pool of activated microglia, we cross-referenced them by applying the same markers on an available public dataset containing nuclei from PD patients from the anterior cingulate cortex45 and observed a nominally significant increased proportion of activated microglia in PD (Supplementary Fig. 15), supporting our findings.

Gene ontology analysis showed upregulation of several innate immune response terms linked to viral and interferon-alpha/ beta (IFN-I) signaling46 in MSA (Fig. 5k, l show pathways of interest selected from the full list in Supplementary Dataset 8), predominantly in PVMs and also in intermediate microglia subtypes. While this upregulation of innate immune response seems characteristic for MSA, PD intermediate microglia showed upregulated gene ontology terms associated with mitochondria metabolism, protein folding, and antigen processing and presentation (in Fig. 5l, pathways are selected based on their relevance to disease etiology from the full list in Supplementary Dataset 8), which are indicative of a primed microglia state47. In contrast, pathways for activated microglia in MSA related to mitochondria respiration were downregulated (in Fig. 5k pathways are selected based on their relevance to disease etiology from the full list in Supplementary Dataset 8). Further supporting differences in immune activation between MSA and PD, we observed an overall increased expression of class I HLA in MSA (Supplementary Fig. 16a) indicative of a general cytotoxic response, while PD microglia showed increased class II HLA expression (Supplementary Fig. 16a).

Detailed examination of cytokine expression showed a general decrease in the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as ILs 1A, 1B, and 6 (cluster 2), and an upregulation of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as ILs 4 and 13 (cluster 3) in MSA microglia (Supplementary Fig. 16b). On the other hand, PVMs in MSA showed upregulated ILs 5, 7, and 15 which are cytokines involved in host defense by regulating T and B lymphocytes and natural killer cells48,49, further supporting the idea of dysfunctional microglia in MSA with peripheral immune cells taking over in driving inflammation. Of interest, increased thrombopoietin (TPO) expression by PVMs in MSA suggests neurovascular thromboinflammation50 (Supplementary Fig. 16b). By analyzing gene regulons (see Methods), we found a large number of MSA-specific regulons in intermediate1 microglia, and most of these were also highly expressed in PVMs (Supplementary Fig. 17a, b). These regulons were functionally related to oxidative stress and immune processes (Supplementary Fig. 17c).

Finally, to assess whether our findings for perturbation of immune processes are supported by a genetic predisposition, we utilized single-cell disease-relevance scoring using GWAS datasets for PD51 (Supplementary Fig. 18a, b) and MSA4 (Supplementary Fig. 18c). Our findings indicate a significant cell-type disease association, particularly within cell types that are associated with immune response - microglia, PVMs and other immune cells, and pericytes (Supplementary Fig. 18a). Notably, this association was significantly heterogeneous within PVMs (Supplementary Fig. 18a), and specific for the intermediate2 microglia (Supplementary Fig. 18b). In contrast, no such association emerged for neurons except for GLUergic neurons, oligodendrocytes, or astrocytes, underscoring a genetic susceptibility that may be intricately linked to the immune response.

Taken together, the dramatic PD-specific increase in activated microglia indicates that, while in PD activated antigen-presenting microglia are part of the pathological conditions, MSA microglia seem unresponsive or having a blunted response. Instead of transitioning to an activated state, MSA microglia adopt a more PVM-like phenotype, which may activate infiltrating immune cells.

Reduced phagocytic activity in microglia exposed to cerebrospinal fluid from MSA patients

Based on these observations, we next investigated microglial responses determined by the disease conditions by exposing human iPSC (hiPSC)-derived microglia cultures to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from MSA or PD patients as a proxy of disease-specific brain environment. We used CSF samples from a cohort previously characterized by proteomics in a study that we previously published9. From that cohort, we selected all available CSF samples and pooled the samples by disease group, n total included 17 CTRLs, 28 MSA patients, and 34 PD patients. The microglia responses were assessed by measuring cytokine release and phagocytic activity towards pHrodo Red E. coli BioParticles (Fig. 6a). To ensure the robustness of our analysis, we performed the experiment twice using different hiPSC cell lines and different CSF pools, with the second pool comprising a sub-cohort of the first, selected based on sample availability (Table 3 and Supplementary Dataset 13). We successfully reproduced the results across different laboratories (see Methods). Microglia were incubated in CSF for 24 h, in the first experiment only in undiluted (1:1) CSF (Fig. 6b, c), and in the second experiment also in diluted (1:2 and 1:4) CSF (Fig. 6e, f and Supplementary Fig. 19) and live imaged for 24 h (Supplementary Videoes 1 and 2). As expected, exposure to CTRL CSF or microglia medium led to increase in phagocytic activity of microglia over time (Fig. 6b, e). However, microglia phagocytic activity was significantly reduced by exposure to PD or MSA CSF pools when compared to CTRL CSF. The reduction of phagocytic activity was the strongest with undiluted MSA/PD CSF, whereas the dilution of MSA/PD CSF to 1:4 abolished the effect and was similar to CTRL CSF or incubation with microglia medium (Fig. 6c, f and Supplementary Fig 19a). Importantly, at 1:2 dilution, MSA or PD CSF showed a marked differential impact on phagocytic activity, namely at this dilution MSA CSF exposure still reduced phagocytic activity of microglia, whereas exposure to PD CSF was similar to CTRL CSF (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 19c). This suggests that the factors affecting microglia phagocytosis are present in higher amounts in the MSA CSF pool than in the PD CSF pool. As potential microglia modulators, we measured levels of soluble CD16352 or α-synuclein in CSF of MSA, PD, or CTRL samples. Levels for both soluble CD163 (Fig. 6g) and α-synuclein (Fig. 6h) proteins were higher in MSA than PD patients, and an increase in soluble CD163 concentration correlated with an increase in α-synuclein (Fig. 6i). In order to investigate putative factors explaining the differences in the hiPSC responses following CSF exposure, we applied KEGG pathway analyses on the differentially expressed proteins identified in our previously published proteomics study on individual CSF samples9 (Supplementary Dataset 14). We observed pathways related to lysosome and phagosome activity as some of the most enriched pathways, albeit insignificant. Nevertheless, the proteins responsible for these enrichments differed between MSA and PD patients (Fig. 6j), possibly explaining the differences in phagosome activity of hiPSCs. Notably, Cathepsin S (CTSS), a microglia-associated protease53, was upregulated in MSA. CTSS has been reported to drive more toxic microglia activation signatures54,55. In addition, when comparing cytokines in patient and CTRL CSF pools after incubation, we observed a tendency towards elevated release of the immunomodulating IL-10 from hiPSCs exposed to the MSA CSF (Fig. 6d). No cytokines were significantly different following adjustment for multiple testing (Supplementary Dataset 15). These results support the conclusion that the less active microglia state in MSA may be a consequence of factors present in the diseased brain. Discussion

In this study, we performed snRNA-seq analysis to assess the transcriptomic profiles of cell populations in the striatal region of PD and MSA patients, following by validation using histology and functional experiments. Our aim was to reconstruct the temporal and dynamic cellular trajectories that distinguish MSA from PD, where MSA is a much more severe atypical Parkinsonian disorder. We identified compositional and transcriptional differences in both neuronal and glial cells between PD and MSA patients compared to CTRLs. We demonstrated that microglia exhibit divergent states in the two diseases. Our data show that PD microglia seemed more polarized towards a more pro-inflammatory and pro-active M1 macrophage phenotype, characterized by the expression of MHC-II involved in antigen presentation56–58, while MSA microglia closer resembled the M2 macrophage phenotype, or an ‘alternative activation’ state marked by the expression of ILs 4 and 13 associated with immunosuppression and tissue repair58–61. This divergent activation state may represent a disease-specific mechanism of pathogenesis and may be associated to the more acute progressing nigrostriatal degeneration described in MSA18.

The increased pool of activated microglia found in this study in PD brains supports the extensively described role of microglia activation in PD pathogenesis62. In this study, we performed snRNA-seq on the striatal region on PD postmortem brains. Other studies have investigated other regions, including SN26,63–65, midbrain29, frontal cortex66 and anterior cingulate cortex45. The study by Feleke et al.45, also reported transcriptomic differences in neuronal and oligodendroglial populations in PD compared to CTRLs, further aligning with our results.

MSA is a rarer and less-extensively studied disease, and to date only one other study by Nido et al. has applied snRNA-seq investigation on MSA brain tissue67. Nido et al. focused on the prefrontal cortex, an area not considered a primary site of pathology, unlike the striatum investigated in our study. In that study also, no significant microglia changes were observed in MSA, while clear increases were reported in PD; however, microglia subtypes were not further characterized as done in our analyses. Notably, previous data show extensive neuroinflammation and immune cell infiltration across regions in MSA brains9,68–70. Thus, it is believed that microglia play a role in the pathogenesis and progression of MSA from early stages71–73, which is further supported by PET-imaging studies demonstrating widespread microglia activity early in the disease74,75.

Our finding of an immunosuppressive microglia state in the striatal areas of MSA brains, although unexpected, may reflect a response to prolonged and sustained inflammation. Repeated exposure of microglia to inflammatory stimuli can lead to exaggerated microglial responses or loss of responsiveness47, especially when secondary inflammatory insult affects an already immune-activated brain76,77. This ‘immune tolerance’ in microglia might be a protective mechanism to limit further tissue damage78 or might result from energy depletion and metabolic dysfunction leading to exhaustion and phagocytosis dysfunction79–81. Here, we demonstrated that microglia unresponsiveness might be a direct consequence of prolonged exposure to the diseased brain environment, based on CSF exposure experiments. While one could argue that the striatum may be more pathologically affected in MSA than in PD, and that the differences we observe may simply be a consequence of this, examination of the extensively pathological affected substantia nigra still shows microglia expressing MHC-II in PD65, supporting the notion that the unresponsive microglia may be MSA-specific.

There is ongoing debate on whether microglial activation is beneficial or detrimental, particularly in the context of α-synuclein pathology62. While it is crucial for microglia to phagocytize and clear α-synuclein to prevent disease spread, α-synuclein accumulation in microglia leads to phagocytic exhaustion and elevated oxidative stress, compromising neuronal integrity79. Loss of microglia phagocytosis causes accumulation of α-synuclein and neurodegeneration82–85. When linked to our observations, this suggests a state of microglia exhaustion in MSA brains due to an initial microglia activation that could result in a more accelerated disease spreading. Considering that MSA has a shorter disease course, this supports a more extensive and detrimental neuroinflammation in MSA than in PD10,12. On the other hand, dysfunctional immune responses may already be present at the initial stages in MSA, caused by the presence of an MSA-specific α-synuclein strain86 believed to behave in a more virulent pattern87. Release of the immunomodulatory IL-10 cytokine is seen at early stages of prion infections88–90. Exposure to CSF from MSA patients increased the release of IL-10 by microglia, although this will need confirmation in a larger sample. Understanding the sequence and major triggers of the inflammatory and immune responses during the disease course could open a window for therapeutic interventions, where stimulation of phagocytosis by rapamycin might be a potential therapeutic target80.

Our observations of microglia dysfunction were accompanied by notable differences in astroglial states between MSA and PD brains. Astroglia, which respond to signals from activated microglia91, are key downstream effectors of the neuroinflammatory response92. Once considered passive and homogeneous, astrocytes are now understood to be heterogeneous and functionally dynamic, playing crucial roles in maintaining brain homeostasis and responding specifically to brain injury93,94. While we cannot determine if the astroglial states in MSA or PD are neurotoxic or compensatory, their distinct reactivity indicates different contributions to disease mechanisms in MSA vs PD. The increased mitochondrial ATP synthase in PD astroglia may represent a compensatory response to maintain neuronal health95. In contrast, MSA brains show astroglia directed towards modulating BBB function. This aligns well with previous reports of progressive BBB impairment in MSA9,96,97, indicating that BBB disruption is a significant pathological feature of MSA.

In addition, we found increased numbers of immune cells in MSA relative to PD, while PVMs were present in both PD and MSA brains. These PVMs expressed CD163 and F13A1, markers associated with perivascular inflammatory responses98,99 and tissue repair100,101, further supporting BBB disruptions. PVMs play an active role in neuroinflammation and immune cell infiltration in neurodegenerative conditions102,103. PVMs originate from erythromyeloid progenitor cells that migrate to the CNS during embryonic development104. According to pseudotime analyses, microglia in MSA may adopt a more similar phenotype to PVMs under extreme pathological conditions.

We found no major overall differences in the oligodendroglial and neuronal cell compositions between MSA and PD, despite PD typically being associated with neuronal dysfunction and MSA with oligodendroglial dysfunction3. This may be explained by the fact that oligodendroglia, specifically the subtype SLC5A11, were decreased not only in MSA but also in PD. For the neuronal population, we observed lower presence of GABAergic PVALB-expressing neurons in both conditions, and of a specific subtype, GABAergic_MEIS2_ST18, in PD. While previous studies have frequently reported differences in dopamine D2 receptor binding in PD and MSA, these findings are based on imaging data and may not directly reflect neuronal loss105. Instead, changes in binding may result from compensatory downregulation of D2 receptors due to dopaminergic denervation or dopamine agonist treatment106 rather than true differences in cell compositions. Lack of significant neuronal reduction is consistent with previous snRNA-seq studies in PD brains, where neither the prefrontal66 nor the anterior cingulate cortex45 showed neuronal loss. Even in the substantia nigra, reduction is modest63. Overall, our findings suggest that the distinctions between MSA and PD are more likely driven by functional alterations within these specific cell populations than by major changes in numbers.

Our findings of lower presence of GABAergic PVALB-expressing neurons, although not statistically validated, align with a well-documented particular vulnerability of this type of neuron due to dopaminergic denervation107. They are also more prone to intracellular α-synuclein accumulation108. Whether this could be related to a reduction in GLUergic neurons expressing RELN, an extracellular matrix protein that regulates striatal PVALB-expressing neuron numbers in adulthood109, could be of interest to investigate further. The plausible presence of RELN-expressing GLUergic neurons in the human striatum has not been reported before, and since these neurons are not present in mouse110 or marmoset111 striatum, this type of neurons might have novel human-specific roles in the regulation of striatal neuronal networks. However, to confirm the existence of RELN-expressing GLUergic neurons in the human striatum, these observations need to be further assessed by histological and functional assays in future studies.

Whether the pathogenesis of MSA starts in the oligodendroglia with secondary neuronal degeneration or begins as primary neuronal pathology leading to secondary α-synuclein accumulation in oligodendroglia, is still a matter of debate73,112,113. Even though neurons and oligodendroglia proportions are equally affected in both PD and MSA, our observations indicate that oligodendroglia are more compromised in MSA. Among other things, they show increased expression of TPPP/p25, which has been implicated in α-synuclein aggregation into glial inclusions113–115. Recent studies suggest TPPP/p25 may impair α-synuclein fibril autophagy and induce more aggressive α-synuclein strains116,117. The increased cell-to-cell communication toward oligodendroglia involving the lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1), a key regulator of α-synuclein uptake and spread118, could suggest that α-synuclein accumulation in oligodendroglia might be secondary to the disease onset and thereby comprome oligodendroglia. Furthermore, LRP1 expression is associated with the removal of myelin debris and OPC-mediated inflammatory responses119–121. As observed, the damaged oligodendroglia then express TLR1 to trigger an immune response and activate CD8+ T-cell mediated cell destruction122, which is further supported by the increased IL15 expression in MSA PVMs which is known to stimulate CD8 + T-cells infiltration123. We have previously reported elevated MHC-I expression in MSA brains, highlighting the presence of a cytotoxic immune response10. Together with the present findings, we believe that this cytotoxic response is unleashed by oligodendroglia. In addition, the overrepresentation of pathways related to viral defense and type 1 interferon in MSA microglia and PVMs points to a strong innate immune response that most likely contributes to the accelerated disease progression.

While our results provide insights into the involvement of microglia in MSA, several limitations should be considered, one of which is the limited availability of human tissue in tissue banks. For instance, PD and MSA samples were derived from different tissue banks, and since MSA samples were easier to access for this study, they were processed earlier with control samples, whereas PD samples were processed later. In addition, the PD samples were, on average, older than MSA, reflecting the longer lifespan of PD compared to the more devastating MSA. Due to limited tissue availability, such differences in covariates between sample groups are an inherent and often unavoidable factor. To address this, we conducted a comprehensive assessment of covariates, performed independent RNAscope validation on a second cohort of age-, PMI-, and brain bank-matched samples, and confirmed our findings in an external dataset, all supporting that the observed microglial differences reflect genuine biological variation. Further limitations are the size of the sample set not allowing us to stratify the snRNA-seq data by different MSA or PD subtypes, nor capture the clinical variability. Additionally, snRNA-seq lacks spatial resolution, which precludes spatial analyses of changes in microglia states. Future studies should focus on using specific microglial markers, immunofluorescence, and spatial transcriptomics to determine the extent to which inactive/unresponsive microglia are more prevalent in patients with different disease durations and in brains with varying types and degrees of α-synuclein pathology. Finally, comparisons with other brain regions, such as the frontal cortex or cerebellum, could help clarify whether these microglia responses precede oligodendroglial and neuronal changes.

In conclusion, our observations underscore the importance of understanding alterations in specific cell groups within the broader context of a pathological network, rather than occurring in isolation, as summarized in Fig. 6k. We believe that the identification of inactive, non-responsive microglia in MSA may help refine how we understand the immune response in this devastating disease and may provide important insights for developing therapies aimed at enhancing microglial responsiveness.

Methods

Human brain and CSF samples

The present project was approved by the regional ethical committee of Region Hovedstaden, Denmark (H-16025210 and H-18064394) and by the Danish Data Protection Agency (Datatilsynet) (P-2023-15234, P-2024-18019). Publication of patient demographic data was approved by Datatilsynet (2025-52-0199). All participants gave written consent according to the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. Clinically and neuropathologically well-characterized human brain tissue was collected from the Bispebjerg Brain Bank, Center for Neuroscience and Stereology, Bispebjerg Hospital, Copenhagen University Hospital, Denmark, and the Harvard Tissue Resource Center, Harvard Medical School Teaching Hospital, USA. Demographic and autopsy-related information are shown in Table 1 and Supplementary Dataset 1. CSF samples were collected from the Bispebjerg Movement Disorders Biobank, Bispebjerg Hospital, Copenhagen University Hospital, Denmark, and their details are shown in Table 3 and Supplementary Dataset 8. Patients were diagnosed based on the international criteria for clinical probable diagnosis124,125. The controls included non-parkinsonian symptomatic controls with isolated acute-onset headache, suspected of subarachnoid hemorrhage, a diagnosis which was later rejected. All CSF CTRLs underwent a CAT-scan and neurological examinations. All participants gave written consent according to the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.

Isolation of nuclei from frozen postmortem human brain tissue

Nuclei were isolated as described in detail elsewhere21,126 with some modifications. In short, app. 50-100 mg pieces of human tissue samples were excised from the striatum of the brain. The tissue was homogenized in a precooled Dounce homogenizer containing ice-cooled homogenization buffer (250 mM sucrose, 25 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM Tris buffer, pH 8.0, 1 μM DTT, 1x protease inhibitor (Roche, Switzerland), 0.4 U/μL RNAse inhibitor (Takara, USA), 0.2 U/μL Superasin inhibitor (Invitrogen, USA), and 0.1% Triton X-100) using both loose and tight pestles before filtering using a 70 µm filter (Millipore). Nuclei were collected (4 °C, 1000 rcf, 9 min) and the pellet resuspended in a blocking buffer (RNase-free PBS, 0.5% BSA (VWR, USA) and 0.2 U/μL RNase inhibitor (Takara, USA)) containing 2 µg/mL 7-amino actinomycin D (7-AAD; ThermoFisher Scientific) to stain nuclei before sorting on a BD FACSAria III sorter using a 75 μm nozzle and controlled by BD FACSDiva 8.0.1 software. Nuclei selection has been described by us in details previously, with representative flow cytrometric isolation plots shown in Fig. S2A by Batiuk, et al.21. Briefly, gating was performed using FSC-A/SSC-A to select nuclei, doublets were removed in a two-step manner using first FSC-W/FSC-H and then SSC-W/SSC-H gates, before final selection based on the 7-AAD staining using FSC-A/PE-Cy5-A. App. 20,000 single nuclei were collected per sample in a tube containing 5 µL blocking buffer. Concentrations were estimated using a Bürker-Türk counting chamber. Cells were spun at 500 × g for 1 min and resuspended in 40 µL blocking buffer. The nuclei were processed using the Chromium Single Cell 3’ v. 3.1 protocol (10x Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. We sought to recover 8000 nuclei per sample by mixing some of the nuclei suspension with RNase-free water containing the reverse transcription master mix. The mixture was loaded onto a Chromium Chip B together with Barcoded Gel Beads and Partitioning Oil for GEM creation using the 10x Chromium Controller. GEMs were slowly aspirated from the recovery wells of the chip and incubated for the reverse transcription step. First-strand cDNA was cleaned up using DynaBeads MyOne Silane beads and preamplified for 12 cycles. Post amplification, the cDNA was purified using SPRIselect and quantified using Qubit 3.0 (ThermoFisher Scientific). Bioanalyzer traces (Agilent Technologies) were used to assess the cDNA quality before sample storage at −20 °C.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing

Following the manufacturer’s instructions, cDNA was subjected to fragmentation, end-repair, and adapter ligation. Then, a sample index PCR was performed for 11 cycles before double-sided library purification using SPRIselect beads. Samples were diluted to 2 nM quantified using Qubit 3.0. Libraries were pooled and diluted to 400 pM and sequenced on a Novaseq 6000 or NextSeq 500 (Illumina, USA) using 28 cycles for read 1, 91 cycles for read 2, and 8 cycles for i7 index.

Bioinformatic analysis

Preprocessing

Raw sequencing data were aligned using 10x Genomics Cell Ranger v. 3.1.0127 with a GRCh38 Ensembl 93 reference. A pre-mRNA reference was used per instructions from 10x Genomics (https://www.10xgenomics.com/support/software/cell-ranger/latest/analysis/inputs/cr-3p-references). Initial QC and filtering was performed using CRMetrics128 v. 0.3.0 with the function filterCms setting flags depth.cutoff = 500 and mito.cutoff = 5% to remove cells with low UMI count and/or high mitochondrial gene count. Whenever possible throughout our workflow, mitochondrial and ribosomal gene counts were omitted to reduce bias from ambient signaling. Ambient RNA was removed using CellBender129 v. 0.2.2 within CRMetrics using the functions prepareCellbender and saveCellbenderScript using per sample custom expected droplets through the flag total_droplets. Doublets were detected using DoubletDetection130 v. 4.2 within CRMetrics using the function detectDoublets and setting method = “doubletdetection”.

Integration and annotation

Samples were normalized using Pagoda2131–133 v. 1.0.12 with the wrapper function basicP2proc. Integrations and embeddings were performed with Conos134 v. 1.5.2 using the functions buildGraph in a PCA space and embedGraph with flag method = “UMAP” or “largeVis” along with findCommunities and findSubcommunities that identifies (sub)clusters using Leiden clustering. For integration with external datasets, we used the flag alignment.strength to improve embeddings. Cell (sub)type markers were identified with getDifferentialGenes setting z.threshold = 1, upregulated.only = TRUE, append.specificity.metrics = TRUE, append.auc = TRUE and cross-correlated with known markers from the literature.

Covariate analysis

We investigated possible influence from covariates using scITD20 v. 1.0.4 using form_tensor setting donor_min_cells 1-5 depending on sparsity of cell subtypes per sample, var_scale_power 0.5-2 to check how different transformations influenced results, and aiming for around 1000 genes by adjusting vargenes_thresh. For each cell type, we ran determine_ranks_tucker to determine the optimal number of ranks. In addition, we utilized Cacoa22 v. 0.4.0 and estimateMetadataSeparation to assess covariate influence, setting space = “expression.shifts”. This constructs a sample–sample distance graph from per–cell-type expression-shift distances and then tests how strongly each metadata variable explains that structure via permutation testing.

Differentially expressed genes

In Cacoa, we used estimateDEPerCellType to calculate DEGs on per cell type pseudobulk counts using the Wald test in DESeq2. We set the flag covariates to account for covariate influence and resampling.method = “bootstrap” to get the most stable DEGs using 30 bootstrap iterations to estimate the variability in gene expression. The p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction.

Pathway enrichment

We used the estimateOntology function in Cacoa to calculate gene set enrichments setting type = “GSEA” and using the org.Hs.eg.db database with the previously calculated DEGs as input. In addition, for pathway enrichment of significant proteins from the soluble fraction of CSF pools, we used clusterProfiler135 to estimate enrichment of KEGG pathways using the enrichKEGG function that performs a hypergeometric test. In both cases, the p-value calculations were followed by Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment for multiple testing.

Cluster-free compositional changes

Cell densities were estimated with estimateCellDensity from Cacoa setting method = “graph”, thereby using a Chebyshev-polynomial approximation of heat-kernel smoothing on the graph. Then, we highlighted the differences by running estimateDiffCellDensity from Cacoa with type = “subtract” to show local enrichment or depletion across the graph.

Cluster-based compositional changes

For proportion changes, we used estimateCellLoadings from Cacoa that fits a linear model to cell-type proportions across samples and computes significant differences by bootstrap resampling to account for variability in sample composition. Raw p-values were adjusted using Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction. To validate significant changes in cell proportion, we ran scCODA23 v. 0.1.9 that applies a Bayesian model to estimate compositional differences between groups. We ran rel_abundance_dispersion_plot to select optimal reference cell types per comparison.

Cluster-based expression shifts

We used estimateExpressionShiftMagnitudes from Cacoa to quantify overall expression shifts between conditions. This is carried out by calculating pairwise distances between samples within each cell type on pseudo-bulk expression profiles, followed by normalization to the average distance within each condition. Significance is calculated using permutation testing followed by Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction.

Protein-protein interactions

Protein-protein interactions were calculated using STRINGdb136 v. 12.0 and plotted using ggraph137, where the input was differentially expressed proteins for MSA or PD vs CTRL in the soluble fraction of pooled CSF samples.

Cosine similarities

Cosine similarities were estimated by identifying overdispersed genes across our dataset and the external dataset that we compared to before we collapsed counts by cell type into pseudobulks for each dataset individually, and size-normalized these counts using DESeq2138. We then subsetted both matrices to the top 50 overdispersed genes and performed a PCA reduction on our dataset. Then, the external dataset was rotated into our PCA space, and cosine similarities were calculated between cell types in this shared low-dimensional space.

Cell-cell interactions

Cell-cell interaction analyses were performed using LIANA139 v. 1.0.4 that integrates multiple ligand-receptor inference methods. We applied the rank_aggregate function to combined ranks from these methods into a consensus score to improve robustness and reduce method-specific bias. The interaction network was visualized with circlize140.

Genetic risk factor vulnerability

ScDRS141 v. 1.0.2 was used to calculate GWAS enrichment in each cell by quantifying expression of disease-associated genes, enabling cell-level prioritization of disease relevance. This was accomplished through load_gs to load the munged dataset, followed by score_cell and downstream_group_analysis setting group_cols = “cell_type”. We used the GWAS summary statistics from Nalls et al.51.

HLA gene counts

HLA gene counts were assessed using scHLAcount142 v. 0.2.0 that quantifies allele-specific HLA gene expression in single-cell RNA-seq data. It counts the molecules per allele in each cell, allowing for the detection of HLA expression imbalances in different conditions.

Regulon activity

Regulons were identified using SCENIC143,144 v. 0.12.1 that reconstructs gene regulatory networks by inferring transcription factor (TF)-target relationships from co-expression patterns and motif enrichment. It then scores the activity of these TF-centered modules (regulons) in each cell using AUCell, enabling identification of cell states based on regulatory programs rather than only gene expression. We accomplished this by running pyscenic grn with -m grnboost2 followed by ctx with –mask-dropouts.

Pseudotime estimations

Slingshot145 was used to estimate pseudotime trajectories, which were plotted using TSCAN146. We used gamm4147 to identify pseudotime-relevant genes by fitting three generalized additive mixed models to each gene with donor as a random variable: a null model where gene expression (GEX) ~ 1, a pseudotime model where GEX ~ pseudotime, and an interaction model where GEX ~ pseudotime + condition. As the condition did not improve the models significantly, we selected genes described by pseudotime. Then, we fitted and smoothed the GEX based on pseudotime using tradeSeq148 before plotting.

Miscellaneous

Most plots were created using ggplot2149, heatmaps were made with ComplexHeatmap150. The R packages sccore151, dplyr152, and magrittr153 were used extensively throughout the analyses in R. All analysis reports are available at https://www.github.com/rrydbirk/MSAvsPD.

RNAscope fluorescent in situ hybridization target validation

To validate identified marker genes from the snRNA-seq analyses, we extracted putamen brain samples from six CTRL, five PD patients and four MSA patients from -80 °C stored whole brain slices. Samples were matched with regards to sex, age and PMI (Table 2 and Supplementary Dataset 7). A staining strategy was chosen where 15 slides from each sample were cut at 10 µm on a Leica cryostat at − 20 °C and transferred to Fisherbrand™ Superfrost™ Plus Microscope Slides (#22-037-246, ThermoFisher) and stored at − 80 °C until further processing. In order to target activated microglia, AIF1 was coupled with CSF1R for duplex target identification with the RNAscope multiplex fluorescent reagent kit v2 (#323100, ACDbio) pipeline. Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in 1x PBS at 4 °C for 15 min and washed twice in 1x PBS on a rocker. Slides were subsequently dehydrated in ethanol with an increasingly higher concentration gradient with 1 five min wash at 50% v/v, 1 70% v/v and 2 subsequent 100% v/v ethanol dehydrations. Slides were placed on absorbent paper to dry for 5 min. A barrier was drawn around tissue sections with a hydrophobic pen and put to dry for 5 min. Afterwards, samples were incubated with hydrogen peroxide for 10 min at RT and washed twice in 200 mL DEPC (diethyl pyrocarbonate (#ICNA0215090225, WVR)) treated water. Next, tissue slides were treated with RNAscope Protease IV for 30 min at RT and afterwards washed twice in 200 mL 1x PBS. Probes were prepared by warming C1 (AIF1) and C2 (CSF1R) probes at 40 °C for 10 min and C2 probes were mixed with C1 probes 1:50. 1x wash buffer was prepared by warming 2 × 60 mL 50x wash buffer at 40 °C for 10–20 min, and two bottles were diluted into 6 L with RNase-free water. C1 and C2 probes were hybridized to samples in a serial manner by incubation at 40 °C for two h and subsequently washed twice with 200 mL 1x wash buffer. Amplification reagents Multiplex FL v2 AMP 1 and 2 were hybridized by incubation at 40 °C for 30 min following two washes in 200 mL wash buffer. Fluorescent signals were developed with channel-specific horseradish peroxidase (HRP, RNAscope Multiplex FL v2 HRP-C1 for channel 1 and C2 for channel 2) by incubation for 15 min at 40 °C with two subsequent 200 mL washes with 1x wash buffer followed by addition of Opal dyes (Opal 520, #FP1487001KT and Opal 570, #FP1488001KT, Akoya Biosciences) with Opal 570 for C1 and Opal 520 for C2 and incubation at 40 °C for 30 min with two washes with 1x wash buffer afterwards. HRP activity was blocked with RNAscope multiplex FL v2 HRP blocker by incubation at 40 °C for 15 min followed by two 1x wash buffer washes. Slides were counterstained with DAPI by 30 sec incubation at RT, coverslipped with antifade mount and stored overnight at RT in darkness. Slides were imaged using a Zeiss Axioscan 7 and Axioscan Z1 at 20x magnification.

RNAscope analysis

Autofluorescence was identified and excluded using AFid154 in Fiji155 for ImageJ v. 1.54 f. Raw images were loaded and split into each capture channel. AFid was used on capture channels for cell marker or target antibody with Max area = 200,000. Then, channels were merged back into one picture. Cell and spot detection were performed in QuPath v. 0.5.0156. Cells were detected using the built-in function Cell detection with Min area = 20. Then, spots were detected using the built-in function Subcellular detection on capture channels for cell marker or target antibodies, with Detection threshold adjusted to 3-400 and Expected spot size = 2, Min spot size = 2. Areas were provided in QuPath by marking the entire tissue on the slide.

Phagocytic activity

Microglia induction and differentiation from hiPSC line. For experiment 1, we used a human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC) line derived from a healthy control male generated by episomal reprogramming of fibroblasts157,158. The hiPSC were maintained in Essential 8 (E8) media (A1517001, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and differentiated into microglia according to a modified protocol159,160. The hiPSCs were collected using Accutase (A1110501, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 3×10^6 cells were plated per well of a Aggrewell 800 24-well plate (34811, Stem Cell Technologies). Microglia induction was initiated via embryoid bodies (EBs) in E8 media supplemented with 50 ng/mL BMP4 (120-05ET, Peprotech), 50 ng/mL VEGF (100-20 A, Peprotech) and 20 ng/mL SCF (PEP-0860, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to direct differentiation towards mesoderm and hematopoiesis. EBs were then harvested and macrophage precursor cell (MPC) producing factories were established using X-VIVO 15 (BE02-060Q, Lonza), with 1% Glutamax (35050061, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0.1% Pen/Strep (P0781-100ML, Sigma), supplemented with 100 ng/mL M − CSF (300–25, Peprotech) and 25 ng/mL IL-3 (GFH80-100, Cell Guidance System). MPC factories were supplemented with 5-10 mL media every week for four weeks. Following, MPC were harvested for terminal microglia differentiation using a cell strainer. The cells were seeded in pre-coated Corning Cellbind cell culture dishes (CLS3294-210EA, Sigma) in microglia media (MM) consisting of Advanced DMEM/F12 (12634010, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1% Glutamax, 0.1% Pen/Strep, 1% N2 (17502048, Thermo Fisher Scientific), supplemented with 100 ng/mL IL-34 (200–34, Peprotech) and 10 ng/mL GM-CSF (300–03, Peprotech), with a seeding density of 10^5 cells/cm2. Microglia-like cells were generated within two weeks of maturation, with two media changes per week.

For experiment 2, the hiPSC-line 3411 (obtained from the MJF Foundation, healthy male donor, clone ID 11295.103) was used and microglia differentiated following Brownjohn et al.161. Briefly, iPSC colonies were disaggregated, counted, and resuspended in mTeSR plus media (100-0276, Stemcell Technologies) containing 50 ng/mL BMP-4 (314-BP-010, R&D Systems), 20 ng/mL SCF (130093991, Miltenyi Biotec), 50 ng/mL VEGF-121 (100-20 A, PeproTech), and 10 μM ROCK inhibitor Y-27632 (1254, Tocris) to form yolk-sack embryoid bodies (YS-EBs). A total of 10,000 cells per well were seeded in a 96 well-plate. On day 2, the same media was added (without ROCK inhibitor). On day 4, YS-EBs were moved to a 6 well plate (10-12 per well) in X-VIVO 15 media (LZBE02-060F, Lonza) containing 1% GlutaMax, 100 U/mL penicillin, 0.1% streptomycin (15140122, Thermo Scientific), 55 uM β-mercaptoethanol, 25 ng/mL IL-3 (203-IL-010, R&D Systems), and 100 ng/mL M-CSF (PHC9504, Thermo Scientific). Since day 10 in this media, primitive macrophage precursors (PMPs) were obtained from the conditioned media. PMPs were centrifuged at 300 x g and counted. A total of 52.000 cells /cm2 were seeded in a 6 well-plate and cultured in RPMI 1640 media (11875093, Thermo Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS (10082147, Thermo Scientific), 2 mM GlutaMax, 100 U/mL penicillin, 0.1% streptomycin, 100 ng/mL IL-34 (200-34, PeproTech), and 10 ng/mL GM-CSF (300-03, PeproTech). At 6 days in vitro, microglia were detached, and 31,000 cells/cm2 were seeded into a glass-bottom 96-well plate (P96-1.5H-N, Cellvis).

Exposure of microglia to CSF. For experiment 1, hiPSC-derived microglia were incubated for 24 h with 1.5 mL undiluted CSF pools collected from 17 CTRLs, 28 MSA patients or 34 PD patients. Control experiments were microglia incubated in microglia media (MM), as well as in MM added 100 ng/ml LPS (Sigma Aldrich, L2630) as a positive control. CSF was collected and replaced by MM with 75 μg/mL pHrodo Red E. coli BioParticles Conjugate for Phagocytosis (Thermo Fisher Scientific, P35361). The procedure was replicated in three independent microglia differentiations per condition. For experiment 2, microglia were exposed for 24 h to 100 µL CSF pools from 13 CTRLs, 21 MSA patients or 22 PD patients at different dilutions (1:1, 1:2 or 1:4 in MM) and MM with or without 100 ng/mL LPS (497693, Invitrogen) in three technical replicates. CSF was collected and replaced by MM with 150 µg/mL pHrodo BioParticles (P35361, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Phagocytic assay. In experiment 1, live imaging analysis was performed using the Biostation IM Cell S1/Cell S2 System (Nikon Instruments, 2022) for 24 h, and images were obtained every 10 min from five different sections of each replicate (Supplementary video 1). The Nikon Biostation Imaging Software was used for image processing, and data was obtained from 10^6 cells per replicate. For experiment 2, the cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and imaged using a Cell Voyager CV8000 (Yokogawa) (Supplementary video 2). Microglia were imaged in three fields of view per well every 8.6 min for each replicate, and the data was analyzed using the pipeline software162. For both experiments, the data was normalized to the cell count.

Mesoscale and ELISA measurements

Release of microglial cytokines into the culture medium/CSF was measured using the biomarker assay V-PLEX Proinflammatory Panel 1 (K15049D, MesoScale Discovery (MSD)) on a high-sensitivity multiplex platform for cytokine profiling (MSD). Data were normalized to total protein content. Total ɑ-synuclein was measured using the U-PLEX Human ɑ-Synuclein Kit (K151WKK, MSD) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, 25 µL of prepared capture antibody was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at RT. The plate was then washed three times with a wash buffer (PBS + 0.05% Tween-20). Next, 25 µL of prepared detection antibody and 25 µL of diluted (1:20) CSF or prepared standard curve were added to each well in duplicates. The plate was sealed with an adhesive plate sealer and incubated at RT with shaking for 2 h. After an additional wash cycle, 150 µL/well of 1 x Read Buffer T was added to each well and immediately read using the MESO QuickPlex SQ 120MM (MSD, USA).

ELISA sCD163 assessment

Soluble CD163 (sCD163) levels were measured using the Human CD163 uncoated ELISA Kit (88-50361-22, Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, after the plate was coated with the capture antibody for 24 h and subsequently blocked, 60 µg of total protein from each sample, along with the standard curve, was added to the plate in duplicates together with a 1:250 dilution of the biotin-conjugated detection antibody. After a 2 h incubation, the plate was washed five times with a wash buffer (PBS + 0.05% Tween−20). After an additional wash cycle, streptavidin-HRP was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at RT. After another wash step, 100 µL/well of TMB substrate was added and incubated for 15 min before 100 µL/well of sulfuric acid stop solution was added. The plate was then immediately read at 420 nm on a Multiscan™ FC Microplate Photometer (Thermo Scientific, USA).
