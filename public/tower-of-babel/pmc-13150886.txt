Effects of a multidisciplinary transitional care programme on self-management and quality of life in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention: a propensity score-matched analysis
Authors: Lei Tao, Dan Luo, Limin Tan, Cangyun Zhang, Yuci Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesAdequate self-management support following percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) remains a recognised challenge. This study evaluated the Integrated Psychocardiology Transitional Care (IPTC) programme’s effect on self-management and quality of life, and the mechanism of their interaction.DesignRetrospective cohort study.ParticipantsOutcomes were analysed for 697 patients after propensity score matching from an initial PCI registry cohort of 1148.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was change in Coronary Artery Disease Self-Management Scale Score over 12 months. Secondary outcomes included the Seattle Angina Questionnaire (SAQ), 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36), Generalised Anxiety Disorder 7 and Patient Health Questionnaire 9 scores. Mixed-effects models and time-lagged mediation analysis were used to examine longitudinal changes and the mediating role of self-management.ResultsNo between-group difference in self-management was observed at 1 month. Significant differences in self-management emerged at 3 months, 6 months and 12 months (all p<0.001). The mixed-effects model for repeated measures analysis revealed significant group × time interaction effects (F=183.1, p<0.001), indicating differential improvement trajectories favouring the IPTC group. The IPTC group showed significantly better SF-36 physical (45.6±9.6 vs 39.8±8.4, 95% CI 4.5 to 7.2, p<0.001) and mental (44.9±8.3 vs 41.7±8.1, 95% CI 2.0 to 4.4, p<0.001) scores at 12 months. Time-lagged mediation analysis confirmed self-management improvement mediated quality of life effects.ConclusionsThe IPTC programme is associated with improved quality of life in PCI patients, and this association appears to be partly explained by enhanced self-management. These findings suggest that integrating psychology-informed transitional care into post-PCI care may be beneficial.Keywords: Coronary intervention, Nursing Care, Self-Management, Quality of Life

Introduction

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is a cornerstone in the management of coronary artery disease, effectively relieving ischaemia and improving survival.1 However, the postdischarge period represents a critical and vulnerable phase for patients, who are often confronted with the challenges of managing a chronic condition, adhering to complex medication regimens, and navigating significant psychological distress.2 3 Effective self-management, encompassing symptom management, treatment adherence, and associated lifestyle and psychosocial adjustments, is widely recognised as a crucial determinant of long-term outcomes.4 Despite its importance, a significant gap exists between the need for comprehensive self-management support and the reality of standard post-PCI care. Conventional care models often prioritise physiological outcomes and are frequently fragmented, lacking structured, sustained support as patients transition from hospital to home.5 This gap is particularly pronounced in the domain of psychocardiology, which refers to the integration of psychological care into cardiac practice.6 7 However, systematic psychological assessment and support are not routinely embedded in transitional care pathways.

Transitional care programmes have emerged as a promising strategy to bridge this care continuum gap. While some programmes have shown benefits, many focus on a single dimension, such as education or nurse-led follow-up, and lack a truly integrated, multidisciplinary approach that simultaneously addresses the medical, behavioural and psychological needs of patients.8 The effectiveness of a structured transitional care programme that explicitly integrates psychocardiological principles to enhance self-management in PCI patients remains underexplored.9 To address this evidence gap, we developed and implemented the Integrated Psychocardiology Transitional Care (IPTC) programme. This structured, multidisciplinary intervention was designed to bridge the gap between inpatient care and community living by integrating core components often addressed in isolation: standardised predischarge education, comprehensive psychological assessment with tailored support, individualised self-management planning and a phased nurse-led follow-up protocol spanning from intensive early support to sustained monthly contacts, all facilitated by structured communication with primary care providers.

This study aimed to evaluate the effects of the IPTC programme on self-management behaviour over a 12-month period in patients undergoing PCI. Furthermore, although previous studies have found that certain interventions can improve self-management capability and quality of life in patients after PCI, no research to date has clearly established a causal relationship between self-management and quality of life.10,14 This study therefore employed a longitudinal design with mediation analysis to elucidate the causal relationship and underlying mechanisms between self-management improvement and quality of life enhancement, aiming to provide a theoretical basis for optimising future interventional strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study used data from the institutional PCI registry. The study consecutively enrolled adult patients (age ≥18 years) who had undergone successful PCI between September 2023 and September 2024. Patients were excluded if they had a documented history of severe cognitive impairment, dementia or a major psychiatric disorder that would preclude accurate assessment of patient-reported outcomes. Additional exclusion criteria included the presence of a terminal illness with a life expectancy of less than 1 year, severe sensory impairment preventing participation in standard care or the absence of at least one documented post-PCI assessment for the primary outcome within the follow-up period. Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

The IPTC programme

The intervention group received the IPTC programme, a structured, multidisciplinary intervention designed to bridge the gap between inpatient care and community living. The multidisciplinary team consisted of a cardiology nurse specialist, a cardiologist, a psychological counsellor and a cardiac rehabilitation specialist. The cardiology nurse specialist delivered the predischarge education and conducted all telephone follow-ups. The cardiologist provided medical oversight and medication counselling. The psychological counsellor provided psychological assessment and support. The cardiac rehabilitation specialist provided lifestyle and exercise advice. The core components included: (1) A standardised predischarge education session focusing on medication adherence, symptom recognition and lifestyle modification, initiated at the bedside during the index hospitalisation; (2) A comprehensive psychological assessment using the Generalised Anxiety Disorder 7 (GAD-7) and Patient Health Questionnaire 9 (PHQ-9) tools to identify anxiety and depression, conducted prior to discharge, followed by tailored psychological support or referral provided by the psychological counsellor;7 15 (3) The development of an individualised self-management plan in collaboration with the patient during hospitalisation; (4) Scheduled follow-up telephone calls by a dedicated cardiac nurse, with an intensive phase of calls at 1 week, 2 weeks, 4 weeks and 8 weeks postdischarge to reinforce education, address acute concerns and monitor progress, followed by a maintenance phase of monthly support calls until the 12-month follow-up; and (5) Facilitated communication with primary care providers, including the transmission of a structured summary of the patient’s care plan and psychological profile. The intervention components were delivered with standardised dosage as follows. The predischarge education session lasted approximately 30 min. The self-management plan took about 15 min to complete. Each telephone follow-up call lasted 10–15 min and was guided by a structured call checklist. The control group received standard post-PCI care, which consisted of routine discharge instructions and usual follow-up. This typically involved scheduled outpatient visits with their cardiologist and primary care physician at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months and 12 months postdischarge, but it lacked the structured, proactive and psychology-integrated components of the IPTC programme, such as the dedicated nurse-led telephone follow-ups, individualised self-management planning, and systematic psychological assessment and support. During the study period, formal, structured cardiac rehabilitation programmes were not yet fully operationalised at our institution; while some patients may have received brief advice on exercise or diet during outpatient visits, no systematic cardiac rehabilitation programme was in place to track or document such participation.

Data collection and measurements

Baseline assessments for all patient-reported outcomes were conducted for the majority of patients within 24–48 hours postprocedure. For elective PCI patients, assessments were performed either preoperatively or within the same 24–48 hours window postprocedure, depending on clinical status and feasibility. Clinical data, including left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), were extracted from medical records. Physical activity level was operationally assessed based on WHO guidelines and categorised into three distinct groups. An ‘Active’ classification required meeting the full criteria of at least 150 min of moderate-intensity or 75 min of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity per week, plus muscle-strengthening activities on at least 2 days. Those performing some sustained aerobic activity but failing to meet either the duration or strength-training criteria were categorised as ‘Insufficiently Active’. Individuals engaging in no moderate or vigorous aerobic activity of at least 10 min durations were classified as ‘Sedentary’.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was the change in the total score of the Coronary Artery Disease Self-Management Scale from baseline to 1 month, 3 months, 6 months and 12 months. The Self-Management Scale is a 27-item instrument that assesses self-management behaviours across three core aspects: daily life management, disease management and emotion management.10 16 Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert Scale, resulting in a total score range of 27–135, where a higher score indicates better self-management. Secondary outcomes at the 12-month follow-up included disease-specific quality of life measured by the Seattle Angina Questionnaire (SAQ) Summary Score and generic health-related quality of life assessed via the physical (PCS) and mental (MCS) component summaries of the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36). Anxiety and depressive symptoms were evaluated using GAD-7 and PHQ-9, respectively.17 18

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared by Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test as appropriate. The χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical variables. A two-sided value of p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. To control for potential confounding in this non-randomised study, propensity score matching (PSM) was employed. Variables for the propensity score model were selected based on clinical relevance and a univariate association with group assignment at p<0.20. The final model included the following covariates: age, sex, BMI, education level, marital status, employment status, cardiac diagnosis with five categories including STEMI, NSTE-ACS, stable angina, asymptomatic ischaemia and others, history of myocardial infarction, history of PCI, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, LVEF, number of diseased vessels, number of stents implanted and the Self-Management Scale total score at baseline. A 1:1 matching algorithm without replacement was used with a caliper width of 0.2 of the SD of the logit of the propensity score. Between-group comparisons of the Self-Management Scale Score over time were performed using a mixed-effects model for repeated measures (MMRM), which included the fixed effects of group, time and their interaction, and accounted for within-patient correlation. Between-group differences in secondary outcomes at 12 months were analysed using appropriate parametrical or non-parametrical tests. To establish temporal precedence, a time-lagged mediation analysis was conducted. The mediator was defined as the change in self-management score from baseline to 3 months, capturing early intervention-induced improvements. The outcomes were the SF-36 PCS and MCS scores at 12 months, each adjusted for their respective baseline scores. Mediation analyses were performed using a bootstrap approach with 5000 resamples (PROCESS macro V.4.0, model 4), and indirect effects were considered significant if the 95% bias-corrected CI did not contain zero. All analyses were performed after PSM using the matched cohort. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS (V.27.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, New York, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics and PSM

A total of 1253 patients were assessed for eligibility. After excluding 105 patients (41 due to dementia or psychiatric disorders and 64 due to severe sensory impairment), 1148 patients were included in the study. These participants were allocated to either the IPTC group (n=359) or the control group (n=789). During the follow-up period, 10 patients were lost from the IPTC group and 24 from the control group. Consequently, 349 patients in the IPTC group and 765 in the control group were analysed before PSM (table 1 and figure 1). Before PSM, the two groups were largely comparable across most baseline characteristics, including age, sex, body mass index, education level, marital status, cardiac diagnosis, comorbidities and psychological scores, with no statistically significant differences observed (all p>0.05) (table 1 and online supplemental table 1). Following PSM, 348 well-matched patients were selected for the control group, resulting in a balanced final cohort of 697 patients (IPTC: n=349; control: n=348) for the primary analysis. After matching, all baseline characteristics showed no significant differences between the groups (all p>0.05).

Changes in the Self-Management Scale Score

The longitudinal changes in the Self-Management Scale Total Score are presented in table 2 and figure 2. At baseline and the 1-month follow-up, there were no significant differences between the IPTC and control groups (67.0±11.5 vs 67.0±12.8, p=0.958; and 74.4±12.1 vs 73.4±13.0, p=0.269, respectively). However, a statistically significant divergence emerged at the 3-month assessment, where the IPTC group demonstrated a higher score than the control group (81.0±12.2 vs 76.7±13.1, p<0.001). This significant between-group difference further increased and was maintained at the 6-month (85.1±12.3 vs 78.4±13.3, p<0.001) and 12-month follow-ups (88.3±12.4 vs 79.8±13.5, p<0.001).

Secondary outcomes at 12-month follow-up

As shown in table 4, the analysis of secondary outcomes at 12 months revealed significant improvements in generic health-related quality of life for the IPTC group. There was no significant difference between the groups in the SAQ Summary Score (75.5±15.5 vs 74.6±16.8, p=0.433). In contrast, the IPTC group scored significantly higher on both the PCS (45.6±9.6 vs 39.8±8.4, 95% CI 4.5 to 7.2, p<0.001) and the MCS (44.9±8.3 vs 41.7±8.1, 95% CI 2.0 to 4.4, p<0.001) of the SF-36 Health Survey. To examine whether the intervention effects varied by clinical presentation, stratified analyses were performed according to PCI indication. Among the 697 patients in the matched cohort, 337 (48.4%) underwent emergent PCI for acute coronary syndrome and 360 (51.6%) underwent elective PCI for stable indications. In the emergent PCI subgroup, the IPTC group had significantly higher SF-36 PCS (45.8±9.3 vs 39.7±8.2, p<0.001) and SF-36 MCS (44.8±8.7 vs 41.6±8.3, p=0.001) scores compared with the control group. However, no significant difference was observed in the SAQ summary score (73.7±16.3 vs 76.0±16.1, p=0.199). In the elective PCI subgroup, the IPTC group showed significant improvements across all measures, with higher SF-36 PCS (45.5±9.8 vs 40.0±8.5, p<0.001), SF-36 MCS (45.0±7.9 vs 41.8±8.0, p<0.001) and SAQ summary scores (77.1±14.6 vs 73.2±17.4, p=0.019) compared with the control group. To investigate the mechanism through which the IPTC intervention improved health outcomes, mediation analyses were conducted, with the change in self-management score as the mediator.

Time-lagged mediation analysis of self-management improvement

Time-lagged mediation analyses were conducted to test whether early improvement in self-management (baseline to 3 months) mediated the intervention effects on the 12-month quality of life. As shown in table 5, the IPTC intervention significantly improved self-management at 3 months (Path A: coefficient=4.411, 95% CI 3.778 to 5.044, p<0.001). This early improvement in self-management significantly predicted higher SF-36 PCS scores at 12 months after adjusting for baseline PCS (Path B: coefficient=0.195, 95% CI 0.059 to 0.330, p=0.005). The indirect effect via early self-management improvement was significant (coefficient=0.858, 95% CI 0.234 to 1.512), accounting for approximately 15.4% of the total effect (total effect=5.583, 95% CI 4.429 to 6.738, p<0.001). The direct effect remained significant (coefficient=4.725, 95% CI 3.430 to 6.019, p<0.001), indicating that other mechanisms beyond self-management also contribute to the programme’s impact on physical health.

Discussions

This study suggests that the improvement of self-management may be a key mechanism through which the IPTC programme is associated with better physical and mental health outcomes in PCI patients. These benefits, which strengthened sustainably over time following a delayed onset, highlight the critical need for prolonged support to achieve lasting behavioural change. These findings advocate for the integration of this structured, psychology-informed model into standard post-PCI care.

The finding that the IPTC group showed significantly greater improvements in both the physical and mental components of the SF-36 at 12 months, but not in the SAQ summary score, offers valuable insights. This suggests that the intervention had a broader impact on generic health-related quality of life, encompassing overall physical functioning, vitality and mental well-being, rather than solely on disease-specific symptoms like angina frequency and stability. It indicates the programme’s success in addressing the patient’s holistic health experience beyond their cardiac condition. Our results align with and extend the existing literature on transitional care.11 12 While previous studies have shown the benefits of nurse-led follow-up or educational interventions,13 our IPTC programme’s novelty lies in its structured integration of continuous psychological assessment and support with self-management training. This multidisciplinary approach appears to be crucial for addressing the complex biopsychosocial needs of PCI patients, ultimately fostering greater patient empowerment and long-term adherence.

Zhang et al had primarily described the relationship between self-management and quality of life using basic correlational analyses, such as Pearson’s correlation.17 While this method establishes simple associations, it cannot test complex theoretical pathways or elucidate the psychological mechanisms underlying these relationships. The present study advances this field by employing a rigorous mediation analysis to test a theoretically grounded model. By employing a time-lagged mediation framework that established temporal precedence with the mediator measured at 3 months and outcomes at 12 months, this study provides evidence that early improvement in self-management is one plausible mechanism underlying the benefits of the IPTC programme. The significant indirect effects for both physical and mental health outcomes support the theoretical model that empowering patients with self-management skills early in the recovery process yields sustained health benefits.

Notably, the proportion of the total effect mediated was larger for mental health (32.4%) than for physical health (15.4%). This differential pattern is clinically meaningful. The self-management measure used in this study includes an ‘emotion management’ subdomain, which may directly capture skills relevant to psychological well-being. Patients who develop effective self-management strategies may experience greater perceived control over their condition, reduced health-related anxiety and improved coping—factors that directly influence mental health. For physical health, the relatively smaller mediation proportion suggests that other mechanisms, such as improved cardiac function, medication adherence or lifestyle modifications, may play more dominant roles. This interpretation aligns with the multicomponent nature of the IPTC programme, which includes direct psychological support (via GAD-7/PHQ-9 assessment and tailored interventions), structured nurse-led follow-up and enhanced communication with primary care providers.

The persistence of significant direct effects for both outcomes underscores that self-management is one of multiple pathways. The relatively modest proportion of variance explained by self-management (15%–32%) is consistent with findings from other complex intervention studies, where multifaceted programmes typically operate through several interconnected mechanisms rather than a single dominant pathway.

Stratified analyses by PCI indication revealed that the IPTC programme consistently improved generic health-related quality of life in both emergent and elective PCI patients. However, its impact on disease-specific quality of life differed by clinical presentation. Among elective PCI patients, the IPTC group showed significantly higher SAQ scores at 12 months compared with the control group, whereas no such difference was observed in the emergent PCI subgroup. This finding may reflect the distinct clinical characteristics of these populations. Patients undergoing emergent PCI for ACS often present with more severe baseline angina symptoms and higher levels of psychological distress, which may require a longer follow-up period to detect improvements in disease-specific outcomes beyond those achieved by revascularisation alone.19 Alternatively, the SAQ may be less sensitive to the holistic, biopsychosocial approach of the IPTC programme in the acute setting, whereas stable patients may derive more readily measurable benefits in angina-specific domains. These findings suggest that while the IPTC programme is broadly effective across clinical presentations, its effects on disease-specific quality of life may be more pronounced in elective PCI patients.

Our study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective observational design. First, the possibility of selection bias due to loss to follow-up cannot be ruled out. Second, despite the use of rigorous PSM to control for known confounders, the influence of unmeasured confounding factors such as income, health literacy, individual patient motivation, healthy user bias, Syntax Score and detailed socioeconomic status may persist, which PSM cannot fully address.13 20 Third, we did not collect hard clinical endpoints such as major adverse cardiovascular events or rehospitalisation. However, the SAQ summary score, SF-36 PCS, GAD-7 and PHQ-9 scores have been shown to correlate with clinical outcomes in post-PCI populations, providing indirect insights into patient clinical status. Future studies should directly track major adverse cardiovascular events to validate the clinical impact of the IPTC programme. At last, the generalisability of our findings may be limited to settings with similar healthcare infrastructures and patient populations. Future research should first focus on modifying the IPTC programme, for instance, by optimising follow-up intensity for each patient and integrating digital health tools.21 Following this optimisation phase, prospective randomised controlled trials should be conducted to validate the effectiveness of the refined intervention, and to investigate its cost-effectiveness and core active components.

Conclusions

The IPTC programme is associated with significant improvements in self-management and quality of life in PCI patients, with benefits emerging after the first month and strengthening over time. Time-lagged mediation analysis suggests that improvement in self-management may be a key mechanism through which the intervention is linked to positive effects on both physical and mental health. These findings indicate that integrating this structured, psychology-informed model into standard post-PCI care warrants further investigation.
