Study on Fluidity and Deposition Characteristics of Air-Containing Filling Slurry
Authors: Chunming Ai, Haichuan Lin, Ziqi Chen, Chao Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the continuous exploitation of global mineral resources, backfill technology for gob areas has become a crucial aspect of mine safety and sustainable development. As a primary method of gob area backfill, slurry backfill directly relates its flow properties and filling height to the efficiency and safety of mine extraction. To enhance the flow properties of the slurry and increase its filling height, a research study on the flow and deposition characteristics of a gas-containing filling slurry was conducted using a combination of theoretical analysis, laboratory experiments, and field tests. Experiments on the gas content were conducted using a concrete gas content tester, rheological property experiments were performed using a paddle rheometer, slump flow tests were carried out using a slump cone, and slurry deposition experiments were conducted using gob area similarity models. Analysis of viscosity characteristics revealed that the slurry gas content increased with the addition of air-entraining agents and the yield stress of the slurry decreased by 49.27% with an increase in the gas content at the same mix ratio, resulting in a 48% reduction in slurry viscosity. By analyzing the mechanism of gas phase enhancement on slurry flow, it was found that the anionic strength of bubbles generated by adding air-entraining agents was greater than the anionic strength within the slurry, changing the original particle arrangement and disrupting the tailings flocculation, thereby increasing the distance between floccules and enhancing the slurry’s fluidity. Based on field tests, a semiindustrial flow and deposition experimental platform was established based on similarity theory, which found that the settlement rate of filling slurry with air-entraining agents decreased, improving the expansion ratio. This research is of significant importance for improving the effectiveness of the slurry backfill.

Introduction

In recent years, as mining depths have increased, the harsh mining conditions of deep resources, characterized by high stress, high rock temperature, and deep well shafts, have placed increasingly stringent demands on the quality of filling materials.1−3 During actual filling operations, the flow and deposition characteristics of the slurry significantly impact its flow performance, filling quality, and support effectiveness for the roof.4−9 Poor roof contact during filling can lead to excessive exposure of the stope roof. When this reaches or exceeds the maximum allowable area for maintaining roof stability, it can result in hazards such as filling body collapse and roof fall, posing a safety risk to operators.10−12 The addition of air-entraining agents can enhance slurry flowability and improve roof contact effects,13−15 which has been applied in mine filling and achieved favorable results.

To investigate the impact of air-entraining agents on filling effectiveness, numerous scholars both domestically and internationally have conducted extensive research on the rheological properties of filling materials with air-entraining agents. Gan et al.16 compared the flow properties of filling slurries with different types of air-entraining agents and found that sodium rosinate (SA)-based agents had the most significant effect on enhancing slurry flow characteristics. Liu et al.17 proposed that as the content of air-entraining agents increased, the internal porosity of gypsum increased, with pore sizes initially enlarging and then shrinking, thereby improving the properties of the filling material. Li et al.18 suggested using air-entraining agents to enhance slurry rheological properties, as they function as ball bearings for lubrication and separate particles, enabling long-distance filling of the slurry. Zheng et al.19 studied the influence of the ratio of superplasticizer to pumping agent on various properties of full tailings paste cementitious filling slurry and found that maintaining the air content of the filling material between 7.5 and 9.5% could meet slurry transportation requirements. Li et al.20 examined the effect of pumping agent dosage on slump and discovered that a dosage of 2.5% resulted in the maximum slump of the paste. Wang et al.21 investigated the optimal mixing ratio of cement, asphalt emulsion, sand, additional water, and the air-entraining agent in the slurry composition, concluding that a dosage of 0.6–1.7% of the air-entraining agent produced good slurry flowability, with the hardened material achieving a 28-day compressive strength of 1.8–2.5 MPa. Pundienė et al.22 found that adding air-entraining agents improved slurry stability and viscosity, with the degree of improvement depending on the type and dosage of the agent. Yang et al.23 studied the effects of three types of air-entraining agents on paste flowability and strength properties, discovering that paste air content increased with the content of air-entraining agents, peaking at 0.9% agent content, which also increased the paste slump by 10–13 mm, reduced viscosity by 0.13–0.20 pa·s, and decreased yield stress by 81.47–93.7%. Lan et al.24 prepared an expansive filling slurry by adding hemihydrate phosphogypsum and air-entraining agents to the slurry, observing that compared to slurries without air-entraining agents, the slurry underwent expansion, significantly improving the roof contact effect of the filling material. Hefni et al.25 discovered that the bleeding rate of gas-bearing expansive slurry was significantly lower than that of the slurry without air-entraining agents, and that the entrapped gas in the slurry reduced porosity, making the filling material lighter and the goaf more stable. Struble et al.26 found that as the air content increases, the yield stress increases while the plastic viscosity decreases. He et al.27 discovered that the consistency of mortar increases slowly with the addition of air-entraining agents, but the overall consistency of the mortar remains relatively low. Yun et al.28 reported that the use of air-entraining agents can reduce flow resistance, and the torque viscosity decreases with an increase in air-entraining agent content, effectively improving the pumpability of high-performance wet-mix shotcrete. Carlswärd29 and Wallevik et al.30 found that the addition of air-entraining agents significantly reduces plastic viscosity but has a less significant impact on yield stress. Rahman et al.31 discovered that the bubbles generated by air-entraining agents are attracted to cement particles, forming bubble bridges that increase the binding force between particles. Once the bubble bridges break and the slurry can flow, the bubbles reduce plastic viscosity, acting as a lubricant.

Current research on the impact of air-entraining agents on filling effects has primarily focused on the rheological properties, slump, strength, and material mix proportions of filling slurries.32−35 However, there is limited research on how the gas content in filling slurries, introduced by air-entraining agents, affects their settlement rate, expansion ratio, and flow and deposition characteristics. Therefore, this paper conducts experiments on the influence of the gas content on the fluidity of filling slurries and establishes an experimental platform based on similarity theory to study the flow and deposition characteristics of gas-containing filling slurries. This research lays the foundation for improving the stability and filling distance of filling slurries. It is of significant importance for enhancing the filling effect of slurries, improving their fluidity, and reducing their settlement rate.

Air-Entraining Agent

The air-entraining agent used is sodium rosinate (SA). Sodium rosinate is a cost-effective and easy-to-use air-entraining agent. It has a benzene ring structure, which can form a large number of bubbles, and is soluble in water.

Rheological Property Experiment

Air-entraining agents alter the bubble structure within the slurry, thereby influencing its rheological parameters, such as viscosity, yield stress, and thixotropy. To determine the rheological parameters of the filling slurry under varying air-entraining agent dosages, a Brookfield RS-SST paddle rheometer was employed to measure the rheological properties of the slurry. During the testing process, the shear rate varied over a specified range, with a duration of 240 s. Preshear was not applied before measurement to avoid any impact on the experimental results from material flow and buoyancy-induced bubble migration.

Analysis and Discussion of Experimental Results

Effect of the Gas Content on Slurry Flow Characteristics

Relationship between the Gas Content and Fluidity of Slurry

Through the experiments of measuring the air content and the slump flow of the slurry, we obtained the relationship between the air content and the fluidity of the slurry, as shown in Figure 4.

Relationship between the gas content and flow performance of the slurry under the action of the air-entraining agent.

As can be seen from Figure 4, there is a strong linear correlation between the fluidity of the slurry and its air content. The air content increases with an increase in the dosage of the air-entraining agent. The increase in the air content reduces the friction between particles and decreases the adhesion of fine particles, which facilitates relative motion between particles and improves the fluidity of the slurry. When the air content reaches 26.15%, the fluidity of the slurry tends to stabilize, indicating that further increases in the air content no longer significantly improve the fluidity of the slurry.

Effect of the Gas Content on Slurry Deposition Characteristics

The deposition morphology of the filling slurry without and with the added air-entraining agent can be obtained through similar flume experiments, as shown in Figure 7.

Filling slurry deposition morphology.

As can be seen from Figure 7, the addition of an air-entraining agent results in a flatter deposition slope of the slurry and an increase in the expansion ratio. Based on the results of the slurry deposition experiment, the deposition surface is quantitatively characterized to obtain the deposition curve, as shown in Figure 8.

Slurry flow deposition curve.

The bubbles generated during the mixing process of the slurry without an air-entraining agent mainly come from the air entrained during mixing. However, these bubbles in the slurry tend to be unstable and easily escape or disappear. During deposition, the slurry without an air-entraining agent is prone to bleeding and segregation, where particles of different sizes in the slurry separate due to different deposition velocities, leading to the accumulation of water on the surface of the slurry. As a result of bleeding and segregation, the surface of the slurry without the air-entraining agent is often uneven after deposition, as shown in Figure 9a.

Comparison of the slurry settling surface.

The bubbles generated in the slurry with an added air-entraining agent mainly come from the chemical reactions between the components in the slurry. Additionally, the sodium rosinate air-entraining agent is an anionic surfactant whose molecular structure consists of hydrophobic and hydrophilic groups. In the slurry, these groups undergo directional adsorption on different interfaces, reducing the surface tension of the slurry. The resulting tiny and stable bubbles can fill the pores between slurry particles, reducing the water flow around the particles and minimizing bleeding and segregation. Consequently, the surface of the slurry with the added air-entraining agent is smoother and more even after deposition, as shown in Figure 9b.

Bubbles’ Impact Mechanism on Filling Slurry Flow and Deposition

Analysis of Flow Mechanism of the Slurry Strengthened by Bubbles

Destructive Effect of Bubbles on Tailing Floc

It is generally believed that tiny particles such as cement and tailings sand have a double electric layer structure on their surfaces. In the slurry, anions adsorb onto the particle surfaces. The sodium rosinate air-entraining agent belongs to the anionic class, and the bubbles it produces have a negative charge on their surfaces. There is an attractive force between the bubble surfaces and the fine particles (tailings, sand, cement) within the suspension slurry, which weakens the particle agglomeration effect within the suspension slurry. Since water is a polar molecule, when water molecules in the suspension encounter bubbles, the anionic strength in the bubbles is greater than that in the filling slurry, causing particles such as tailings sand to be attracted to the bubble surfaces. This disrupts the flocculation of tailings sand but improves the fluidity of the slurry. The mechanism of bubble disruption on tailings flocculation is illustrated in Figure 10.

Failure mechanism of air bubbles on tailings floc.

Dispersion Effect of Bubbles on Reconstituted Floc

After the addition of the air-entraining agent, the bubbles disrupt the flocculation of tailings sand through their various effects such as lubrication and dispersion, reducing the friction between the flocculated tailings particles in the slurry and improving its fluidity. The impact of bubbles on the spacing between reconstructed flocculations is shown in Figure 11.

Action mechanism diagram of bubbles on reconstituted tailings floc.

Figure 11 illustrates the effect of bubbles disrupting the flocculation of tailings. After the addition of the air-entraining agent, the generated bubbles are evenly distributed between particles, exerting a dispersing effect on the structure of tailings flocculation. This changes the original particle arrangement, increases the distance between floccules, and subsequently enhances the fluidity of the slurry.

Effect of the Gas Phase Factor on the Yield Surface of the Slurry

Some scholars have applied fluid dynamics theory to study the flow of the slurry36 and predicted the flow surface of the non-Newtonian fluid slurry based on the inclined plate flow model of viscoplastic fluid mechanics. The mechanical mechanism of this model is illustrated in Figure 12.

Gas-bearing slurry flow mechanism diagram.

In aerated slurry, when the yield stress of the slurry is greater than its shear stress (τy > τ), the slurry remains in a solid state and does not flow. As the gas content increases, when the yield stress of the slurry becomes less than its shear stress (τy < τ), the internal structure of the slurry is disrupted, enhancing the fluidity of the aerated slurry. Figure 12 illustrates that as the content of air-entraining agent increases, the flow length of the slurry with a high gas content is significantly longer than that of the slurry with a low gas content.

Analysis on the Characteristic Mechanism of Bubble Reducing Slurry Deposition

Bubble Shape of the Slurry Surface

During the preparation of the slurry, the addition of sodium rosinate as an air-entraining agent improved the microstructure of the slurry. Particles such as tailings and cement adhered to the surface of the anionic bubbles generated by the air-entraining agent. The morphology of the bubbles on the surface of the slurry is shown in Figure 13.

Bubble shape on the slurry surface.

Influence of Bubbles on Slurry Deposition

Due to the smooth surface of the bubbles generated by the air-entraining agent, when the particles in the slurry move around the bubbles, the frictional resistance between the particles is greatly reduced, thus slowing down the settling velocity of the slurry particles. The generation of bubbles disrupts the flocculated structure of the tailings, and the particles inside the slurry adsorb onto the bubbles, thereby increasing the volume of the slurry. Research has found that the deposition height of the slurry with the addition of an air-entraining agent (Figure 14b) is higher than that of the slurry without the addition of an air-entraining agent (Figure 14a). The mechanism of the effect of bubbles on the slurry deposition is shown in Figure 14.

Micrograph of the Backfill under an Electron Microscope Experiment

The microstructural images of the backfill without the air-entraining agent and with the air-entraining agent at the same location were obtained through SEM experiments, as shown in Figure 15.

Microstructure of the backfill under an electron microscope.

As shown in Figure 15a, the filling body has smaller particles and a higher degree of compactness, indicating that without the addition of an air-entraining agent, the cement matrix is relatively dense with fewer voids between particles. On the other hand, Figure 15b reveals that the filling body exhibits larger particle sizes and a more porous structure, suggesting that after adding the air-entraining agent, the presence of bubbles increases the distance between cement and tailings particles, altering the flocculation structure between particles. The generated bubbles occupy the space that may have originally formed larger voids, thereby reducing physical settlement caused by particle rearrangement or dehydration. Therefore, adding an air-entraining agent can increase the volume of the slurry and slow down the deposition rate of slurry particles. This study is of great significance for improving filling performance, enhancing slurry fluidity, reducing deposition height, and increasing slurry volume.
