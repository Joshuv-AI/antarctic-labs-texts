Giant hydronephrosis due to neglected congenital pelvi-ureteric junction obstruction: a case report and literature review

Introduction

Since Glass reported the first case of giant hydronephrosis in 1749 during a postmortem examination, other cases have been documented[1,2]. Notably, Tombari et al[1] reported the largest hydronephrosis in recent history, in which 52 L of urine were drained. Stirling generally defined this rare condition as the presence of more than 1.5 L of fluid, or fluid amounting to 1.6% of body weight, in the renal pelvis[2,3]. Grover and Crisp[4] also described giant hydronephrosis as a condition originating from the kidney, occupying at least half of the abdomen or extending across the midline, with the length of the kidney exceeding 5 vertebrae. It commonly presents on the left side and is more prevalent in men than in women (male-to-female ratio: 2.4:1)[5].

With few exceptions, the preponderance of giant hydronephrosis is attributable to congenital anomalies, mostly pelvi-ureteric junction obstruction (PUJO). The report by Yates-Bell and Packham[1] was notable for documenting the occurrence of a solitary crossed ectopic kidney. However, giant hydronephrosis could also be attributed to ureteric calculi[6], ureteric tumors, or trauma[7].

We report the case of giant hydronephrosis in a 14-year-old African female, with a review of the etiology and the peculiarities of management within an African context. Case presentation

A 14-year-old girl, the youngest of eight children, presented with a 1-year history of a left flank swelling with progressive distension, pain, and heaviness of the abdomen. The abdomen became grossly distended 2 months after she first noticed the swelling. She visited a peripheral facility for treatment but noticed progressive worsening of her condition and was therefore referred for further care. The patient denied any history of early-morning facial puffiness, pedal edema, nausea, vomiting, constipation, fever, chills, or hematuria.

On physical examination, the young adolescent female’s general condition was stable. The abdomen was grossly distended, extending from the left flank to the epigastric, right lumbar, and right iliac fossa regions (see Fig. 1). It was firm but non-tender. The liver, spleen, and right kidney were not palpable, but the left kidney was ballotable. Bowel sounds were present and normal. The remainder of the systemic examination was unremarkable. An abdominopelvic computed tomography (CT) scan showed a huge left-sided intra-abdominal cystic lesion with pelvicalyceal dilatation, severe cortical thinning, and features suggestive of chronic left PUJO, as well as a minimally dilated right renal pelvis and calyces (Fig. 2). The anteroposterior (AP) diameter measured approximately 165 mm in the axial plane, suggestive of severe hydronephrosis on the modified radiology grading (RG) scale[9]. As can be clearly seen in Figures 1 and 2, the mass had crossed the midline. Renal scintigraphy was not performed due to its unavailability at our facility. Intervention

The intra-abdominal mass was accessed through a midline transperitoneal incision. The main findings at surgery were a markedly enlarged left kidney with urine retained in the renal pelvis and a normally appearing left ureter distal to the PUJO. There was significant cortical thinning, with a flabby renal cortex and pelvis, and 10.4 L of amber-colored urine was drained. The mass, which had crossed the midline, displaced the descending colon and small bowel to the right side of the abdomen. The right kidney, liver, and spleen appeared essentially normal (Fig. 3 and Supplemental Digital Content Figure S1, available at: https://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A138). The body weight before surgery was 56.5 kg. Postoperatively, the body weight measured 45.3 kg; a reduction of 11.2 kg, constituting the combined weight of the drained urine (10.4 kg) and the nephrectomy specimen (0.8 kg). The weight of the 10.4 L of urine drained was 10.4 kg, representing 18.4% of the total pre-operative body weight. The patient was monitored postoperatively with no complications observed and was discharged on post-operative day 9 in stable condition. Three weeks postoperatively, the wound had healed, and the patient and their relatives were satisfied with the outcome (Fig. 1b). The histopathology confirmed a normal vein and renal artery, an atrophic kidney with chronic mononuclear interstitial inflammation, tubular atrophy with focal fibrosis, glomerular vascular congestion, and a cystic wall composed of fibrocollagenous tissue and lined by epithelial cells with squamous metaplasia. No malignancy was identified.

Follow-up and outcomes

The patient presented for review 3 weeks after surgery. The wound had healed, and repeat laboratory investigations (full blood count, renal function tests) were essentially normal.

Patient assessment of care

The patient and her parents were satisfied with the management plan offered by the urology team and with its outcomes.

Discussion

Giant hydronephrosis is a relatively uncommon surgical phenomenon. However, it often highlights the neglect of congenital renal anomalies. Studies in Africa and the Middle East show that these conditions result from neglected or unrecognized upper urinary tract obstructions[10,11]. Another reason for the delayed diagnosis is the gradual onset and the asymptomatic nature of the presentation[12]. In well-resourced countries, such congenital anomalies are detected via prenatal ultrasound or in newborns; however, due to limited access to quality obstetric and pediatric services in the African sub-region, most anomalies are diagnosed late, especially when they are asymptomatic. Therefore, the late presentation of our patient is common rather than unusual. She presented only a few months ago, despite having had this anomaly since birth. In a Tanzanian study, Mahenge and colleagues[13] identified limited access to antenatal ultrasound and the lack of trained personnel as the main factors contributing to late presentation and delayed diagnosis of congenital renal anomalies. With the advent of point-of-care ultrasound and other relatively affordable ultrasound devices in developing countries, it is now possible to detect these anomalies in utero or shortly after birth. In addition to making ultrasound services available, it is necessary to ensure that the personnel providing them are well-trained to identify these anomalies.

The most common cause of giant hydronephrosis is PUJO[3,10]. However, vesico-ureteric junction obstruction (VUJO), distal ureteric calculi, ureteric tumors, and trauma have also been reported[6,7]. PUJO and VUJO are known to cause chronic obstruction with progressive dilation of the pelvicalyceal system. As a result, they are often asymptomatic, especially if unilateral[10]. Rkik and colleagues[6] reported a case of giant hydronephrosis caused by a distal ureteric calculus in a 63-year-old man in Morocco, where approximately 8 L of urine was drained, compared with 10.4 L in the present case. In another report from Eastern Africa, giant hydronephrosis was identified in a 46-year-old with 22 L of urine drained[14]. Other similar reports across Africa reveal that the condition is usually diagnosed at relatively older ages rather than in the neonatal period, as might be expected[2,15,16]. The classic presentation of the disease is a protruding abdomen, as observed in this index case. This may or may not be linked to pain. In cases of a solitary kidney or bilateral renal anomalies, signs of chronic renal failure, such as bilateral pitting oedema, facial puffiness, and uremic frost, may develop, but our patient had none of these. These patients may appear chronically ill and malnourished due to the longstanding nature of the disease. Abdomino-pelvic ultrasonography is an effective tool in establishing the diagnosis; nevertheless, CT scans provide clearer, more detailed imaging to aid surgical planning[15]. Renal scintigraphy and intravenous urograms assess differential renal function (DRF) and prognosis, and guide management. Regarding specific care, there may be different approaches. Some surgeons may prefer to relieve the obstruction temporarily by the passage of a nephrostomy tube or stent as they prepare for surgery, as observed by Rkik et al[6] while others prefer definitive surgery ab initio, as seen in this index case. Although open surgery is commonplace in Africa, minimally invasive nephrectomy or pyeloplasty are suitable alternatives and are common elsewhere[17]. The nephrectomized patient must be counseled extensively to avoid over-the-counter analgesics and herbal preparations, which may worsen renal function, as excretion is now dependent on a solitary kidney. Thereafter, these patients require structured, multidisciplinary, and well-coordinated monitoring to ensure optimal renal function. In this respect, the nephrologist should be keenly involved in long-term care, as another potential cause of worsening renal function is hyperfiltration injury, which may occur in post-nephrectomy patients[18]. This index case will be reviewed regularly by the urology and nephrology teams, with repeated ultrasonography and renal function assessments to ensure renal function remains satisfactory.

The volume of urine drained (10.4 L) corresponds to an approximate mass of 10.4 kg, which is nearly 60–70 times the normal renal weight of 120–170 g. Furthermore, this constituted approximately 18.4% of the patient’s total body weight, far exceeding the classical definition of giant hydronephrosis (>1.5 L or 1.6% of body weight)[2–4]. These findings highlight the extreme chronicity and severity of the obstruction, resulting in massive pelvicalyceal dilatation and near-complete cortical atrophy. In such circumstances, renal salvage is rarely feasible, and nephrectomy may be the most appropriate and definitive management option. In patients with suspected PUJO, DRF of less than 40% on renal scintigraphy is considered an indication for surgery. Other indications for surgery include an anteroposterior diameter >20 mm on imaging, grade 3 or 4 postnatal hydronephrosis as defined by the Society for Fetal Urology or the modified RG scale, and persistent pain and infection[9,19,20]. In most cases, kidney preservation is preferred to extirpation. However, when renal function is significantly impaired with DRF less than 20%, the management becomes controversial. Under such circumstances, many surgeons may prefer a nephrectomy, since pyeloplasty may be associated with longer-term complications and morbidity[10,21]. Nevertheless, other surgeons recommend pyeloplasty even in poorly functioning kidneys, as they believe renal function may recover after reconstructive repair, especially in pediatric populations[22,23]. The European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines[20] suggest that in children with DRF less than 20%, pyeloplasty or nephrectomy should be discussed. They further state that a staged approach after a trial of urinary diversion has limited benefits and increases treatment burden. The guidelines recommend that while pyeloplasty should be considered in poorly functioning kidneys, interpretation of DRF results should be done cautiously due to inherent variability in DRF measurements, as changes may reflect methodological margins of error rather than true functional gains. As a result, pyeloplasty as a means of salvaging the kidney may not be tenable in all cases. In a retrospective analysis, Okoro and Ekeke[10] found that complication rates were much higher among patients who had pyeloplasties compared to those who had nephroureterectomies. Moreover, the massive dilatation and tortuosity of the renal pelvis make pyeloplasty repair technically challenging. Pyeloplasty repairs were more prone to anastomotic leaks, restenosis, recurrent hydronephrosis, and impaired renal function. Additionally, pyeloplasty requires more frequent follow-up with repeated ultrasound and renal scintigraphy, in contrast to nephrectomy[24]. After discussions with our patient, a simple nephrectomy was preferred.

The preference for simple nephrectomy rather than pyeloplasty is justified by the tortuosity and massive dilatation of the renal pelvis (10.4 L of urine drained), the severe thinning of the renal cortex, and the resultant cortical atrophy. The renal cortex and pelvis were palpably flabby and extremely weak on intraoperative examination. The AP diameter measured approximately 165 mm on CT, far exceeding the definition of severe hydronephrosis (AP diameter >35 mm)[9]. The AP diameter was also approximately 8 times the cutoff for surgery (AP > 20 mm)[19]. These factors, coupled with the persistent pain the patient presented with, make simple nephrectomy a more rational approach in our view.

Although our observations are valuable, the relatively short follow-up period is a clear limitation of the study. Long-term follow-up would better evaluate the drawn-out effect of nephrectomy in this patient. Secondly, the unavailability of renal scintigraphy is a significant limitation, as it would have provided a more objective measurement of DRF.

Conclusion

Giant hydronephrosis is mostly a consequence of neglected congenital renal anomalies in Africa. Pyeloplasty, as a means of salvaging the kidney, may not be feasible in all cases, making nephrectomy an alternative in selected advanced cases. The nephrectomized patient must avoid unprescribed medications, including herbal concoctions, as excretion is now dependent on a solitary kidney.
