Motor alignment and sensory outcomes in pediatric patients with basic-type intermittent exotropia after bilateral lateral rectus recession versus unilateral recession–resection
Authors: Merve Simsek, Mehmet Cem Sabaner, Nida Yunlu, Cem Simsek, Goksu Alacamli, Ozlem Barut Selver
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo compare motor alignment and sensory outcomes after bilateral lateral rectus recession (BLR) versus unilateral lateral rectus recession with medial rectus resection (R&R) in children with basic-type intermittent exotropia (IXT).MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 48 patients with basic-type IXT who underwent BLR (n = 26) or unilateral R&R (n = 22). Assessments were performed preoperatively and at 1 month and 1 year postoperatively. Deviation angles were measured in prism diopters (PD). Near stereopsis was analyzed after log transformation, and sensory status was evaluated using the Worth 4-dot test. The primary outcome was 1-year motor success, defined as a residual deviation ≤ 10 PD.ResultsBaseline characteristics were similar between groups (all p > 0.05). Motor success rates were comparable between BLR and R&R at 1 month (92% vs. 91%; p = 1.000) and 1 year (88% vs. 86%; p = 1.000). Orthophoria rates (77% vs. 68%; p = 0.497) and reoperation rates (12% vs. 14%; p = 0.827) were also similar. Deviation decreased significantly over time (p < 0.001). Stereopsis improved postoperatively and was maintained at 1 year (p < 0.001). In an exploratory multivariable analysis, higher preoperative deviation was associated with lower odds of motor success (aOR 0.71; p = 0.024); however, this estimate should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited number of failure events.ConclusionsBLR and unilateral R&R yielded comparable motor and sensory outcomes at 1 year postoperatively in basic-type IXT. The exploratory association between higher preoperative deviation and lower motor success requires confirmation in larger cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12886-026-05339-x.

Introduction

Intermittent exotropia (IXT) is the most common pathological form of exodeviation in childhood. It is characterized by intermittent exodeviation alternating with orthotropia, often exacerbated by fatigue or sleep deprivation. Fusion is typically present during periods of orthotropia; however, abnormal retinal correspondence, suppression, or diplopia may occur during exotropic episodes. Intermittent deviation usually manifests between 2 and 5 years of age, with increasing frequency over time, and may eventually progress to constant exotropia as the child matures [1, 2].

Intermittent exotropia can be classified into different subtypes according to the relationship between distance and near deviations. Basic-type IXT is characterized by similar exodeviation at distance and near fixation and represents one of the most common forms of IXT. When a disparity between distance and near deviation is present, monocular occlusion and assessment of the AC/A ratio may be used to distinguish basic-type IXT from other subtypes according to the Kushner classification [3, 4]. Accurate classification is important for identifying a clinically homogeneous basic-type IXT population.

In the management of intermittent exotropia, patients with deviations < 20 prism diopters (PD) may be managed conservatively with myopic overcorrection to stimulate convergence, occlusion therapy for antisuppression, and convergence exercises. In contrast, surgery is typically considered for larger deviations and poorer control (e.g., higher Newcastle control scores) [5]. Reported surgical success rates are approximately 80%; however, recurrence has been observed in up to 50% of cases within three years [6]. A definitive surgical standard has not been established, and procedure selection is generally guided by the degree of ocular deviation. Available surgical options include bilateral lateral rectus recession and unilateral lateral rectus recession combined with medial rectus resection [7].

Basic-type intermittent exotropia (IXT) is one of the most prevalent forms of IXT [4]. The choice of surgical technique in patients requiring operative management remains controversial, particularly between bilateral lateral rectus recession (BLR) and unilateral recession–resection (R&R). Given ongoing debate regarding BLR versus unilateral R&R in basic-type IXT, we compared 1-month and 1-year motor and sensory outcomes in a clinically homogeneous cohort with good alternation.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study included 48 patients who underwent surgery for basic-type intermittent exotropia between January 2022 and January 2025. To reduce the potential influence of age on surgical outcomes, inclusion was limited to patients between 4 and 10 years of age. Inclusion criteria were age between 4 and 10 years, a diagnosis of basic-type IXT, a Newcastle control score ≥ 6, good alternation, a normal AC/A ratio, measurable preoperative and postoperative visual acuity, and reliable cooperation with Worth 4-dot and stereopsis testing. Exclusion criteria were anisometropia or amblyopia, poor or absent alternation, A- or V-pattern deviation, oblique muscle dysfunction, additional ocular pathology, previous strabismus surgery, non–basic-type IXT according to the Kushner classification, and any previous treatment for IXT, including spectacles, myopic overcorrection, occlusion or anti-suppression therapy, orthoptic exercises, or prism correction.

Children presenting with intermittent exodeviation underwent surgery after baseline evaluation based on clinical indications (deviation magnitude and control), without prior strabismus surgery. Inclusion criteria also required a Newcastle control score ≥ 6, measurable preoperative and postoperative visual acuity, and reliable cooperation with Worth 4-dot and stereopsis testing. Sensory fusion status was assessed using the Worth 4-dot test and recorded as fusion, suppression, or diplopia. Near stereopsis levels (arc/seconds) were measured. Near point of convergence (NPC), and positive fusional vergence (PFV) were additionally assessed. To ensure that only patients with basic-type IXT were included, the magnitude of ocular deviation was measured at both distance and near fixation using the prism and alternate cover test. Patients with unequal distance and near deviations underwent repeat measurements after 60 min of monocular occlusion and were subsequently classified according to the Kushner classification. Only patients fulfilling the criteria for basic-type IXT were included in the study. Best-corrected and uncorrected visual acuity were measured using Snellen or tumbling E charts. Noncycloplegic and cycloplegic refraction and dilated fundus examination were performed. The participants were categorized into two groups according to the surgical technique applied. In patients for whom both BLR and R&R were considered clinically appropriate, surgical selection reflected an evolution in the surgeon’s practice over the study period. BLR was predominantly performed during the earlier period, whereas unilateral R&R was increasingly adopted later as the surgeon gained greater experience with this technique. Good alternation was defined as the ability to fixate with either eye without evidence of amblyopia; this did not preclude the presence of a clinically identifiable fixation preference. In patients undergoing R&R, surgery was performed on the eye with the weaker fixation preference.

Group 1 consisted of 26 patients treated with bilateral lateral rectus recession (BLR) performed, whereas Group 2 comprised 22 patients who underwent unilateral lateral rectus recession combined with medial rectus resection (R&R). The surgical amounts for both BLR and unilateral R&R were determined according to the preoperative deviation angle using the surgical dosage schedule reported by Kim and Choi [8], which was based on the modified surgical formula suggested by Parks. The exact surgical dosages used in the present study are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Postoperative evaluations were conducted at the first month and at one year following surgery.

Surgical Technique

All surgical procedures were performed under general anesthesia by the same experienced surgeon (M.S.). In the BLR group, a limbal conjunctival incision was performed in both eyes. The lateral rectus muscle was isolated using a muscle hook and secured with a 6–0 polyglactin (Vicryl) suture. The muscle was then disinserted from its insertion and recessed to the sclera according to the predetermined surgical dosage based on the angle of deviation. In the R&R group, surgery was performed on the non-dominant eye. Lateral rectus recession was carried out according to the deviation angle. The medial rectus muscle was subsequently isolated with a muscle hook and secured with a 6–0 polyglactin suture posterior to the planned resection site. The muscle was then transected, and the predetermined amount of muscle tissue was resected. Finally, the muscle was reattached to its original insertion site. Motor success was defined as a residual horizontal deviation of ≤ 10 PD (esodeviation or exodeviation) at distance fixation, as measured by the prism and alternate cover test. Secondary outcomes included orthophoria status, residual deviation (PD) at each follow-up, change in deviation from baseline (ΔPD), near stereopsis at each follow-up and its change from baseline (Δstereopsis on the log scale), sensory status distribution at 1 month and 1 year, and reoperation rates.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was carried out using SPSS software (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Because normal distribution was not observed, continuous variables are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Categorical variables were summarized as counts and proportions. Group differences were evaluated using Pearson chi-square analysis or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate. Longitudinal changes across the three evaluation periods (preoperative, postoperative month 1, and postoperative year 1) were examined with the Friedman test. When overall significance was detected, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were applied for post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment (significance threshold: 0.0167).

Associations between clinical variables and 1-year motor success were initially assessed using univariable logistic regression, and findings are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A multivariable logistic regression model including predetermined statistical meaningful parameters (including age, preoperative deviation, and Newcastle control score) was subsequently constructed to limit overfitting considering the relatively small number of failures. All analyses were two-sided, and statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05 unless otherwise specified.

Results

This retrospective comparative study included 48 patients with basic-type IXT who underwent BLR (n = 26) or unilateral R&R (n = 22). Mean age was 7.5 ± 1.9 years (BLR) and 7.3 ± 2.0 years (R&R). Baseline characteristics were comparable (all p > 0.05) (Table 1). The median Newcastle score was 7.5 (IQR, 6–9) in the BLR group and 7.0 (IQR, 6–9) in the unilateral R&R group, with no significant between-group difference (p = 0.789).

Motor alignment

Deviation decreased significantly over time in the overall cohort (preoperative vs. 1 month vs. 1 year; Friedman p < 0.001). Median deviation decreased from 35 PD preoperatively to 5 PD at 1 month and 0 PD at 1 year. Within-group analyses showed significant reduction from baseline to 1 month and from baseline to 1 year in both surgical groups (all p < 0.001). Deviation at 1 year was similar between groups (median 0 PD in both; Mann–Whitney p = 0.483), as was the change in deviation from baseline to 1 year (Mann–Whitney p = 0.762).

Motor success (|PD|≤10) did not differ between BLR and R&R at 1 month (92.3% vs. 90.9%; Fisher’s exact p = 1.000) or at 1 year (88.5% vs. 86.4%; Fisher’s exact p = 1.000). Orthophoria rates were also comparable between groups at 1 month (50.0% vs. 40.9%; Pearson χ² p = 0.529) and at 1 year (76.9% vs. 68.2%; Pearson χ² p = 0.497). Reoperation rates were low and similar (11.5% vs. 13.6%; Fisher’s exact p = 0.827). No postoperative esotropia was observed; diplopia was rare and occurred in 1 patient at the 1-year visit (Table 2).

Sensory outcomes

Near stereopsis improved significantly across follow-up in the overall cohort (Friedman p < 0.001), with improvement from baseline to 1 month and from baseline to 1 year, while change between 1 month and 1 year did not reach the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold. At 1 year, stereopsis (log scale) and stereopsis change from baseline were similar between BLR and R&R (p = 0.991 and p = 0.495, respectively). Sensory status distribution at 1 year (fusion/suppression/diplopia) did not differ between surgical groups (Monte Carlo Fisher p = 0.530) (Table 3).

Predictors of 1-year motor success

In univariable analyses, surgical type was not associated with 1-year motor success (OR 1.21; p = 0.827). Higher preoperative deviation was associated with lower odds of 1-year success (OR 0.69 per 1-PD increase; p = 0.009). In the multivariable model, higher preoperative deviation remained associated with lower odds of 1-year motor success (aOR 0.71 per 1-PD increase, 95% CI 0.53–0.96; p = 0.024). However, given the limited number of failure events, the adjusted estimates should be interpreted cautiously (Table 4).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of children aged 4–10 years with basic-type intermittent exotropia and good alternation, we compared BLR with R&R in terms of motor alignment and sensory outcomes at 1 month and 1 year. Both procedures produced a significant reduction in deviation and an improvement in near stereopsis over time, and the 1-year motor success and sensory outcomes were comparable between the two surgical approaches. Exploratory multivariable analysis suggested a possible association between a larger preoperative angle of deviation and lower odds of 1-year motor success. However, given the limited number of failure events and the consequent low events-per-variable ratio, this finding should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Together, these findings suggest that BLR and unilateral R&R can achieve comparable 1-year motor and sensory outcomes in a clinically homogeneous basic-type IXT cohort.

Multiple factors have been associated with surgical success in intermittent exotropia, including age, sex, age at surgery, amblyopia or anisometropia, preoperative angle of deviation, preoperative stereopsis and fusion status, prior prism, occlusion, or myopic overcorrection therapy, and surgical technique [6, 9]. To allow comparison of the two surgical techniques, potential confounding factors affecting surgical success were controlled. Patients with amblyopia, anisometropia, or additional ocular pathology were excluded. The two groups were comparable with respect to age, sex, preoperative angle of deviation, stereopsis, and fusion status.

Basic-type intermittent exotropia, one of the most frequently encountered forms of intermittent exotropia, lacks a standardized surgical treatment strategy. In patients who do not demonstrate alternating fixation, unilateral lateral rectus recession combined with medial rectus resection is typically performed on the nondominant eye. Conversely, in patients with alternating deviation and symmetrical ocular dominance, either bilateral lateral rectus recession or unilateral lateral rectus recession with medial rectus resection can be selected as the surgical option [10].

In addition to standard BLR and unilateral R&R, augmented BLR recession has been proposed as an alternative surgical strategy to reduce postoperative undercorrection and recurrence. Previous studies have reported higher long-term success and lower recurrence rates with augmented BLR compared with standard BLR, although increased early postoperative overcorrection remains a potential concern [11, 12].

Song et al. [13] demonstrated that, in patients with basic-type intermittent exotropia and symmetrical ocular dominance, bilateral lateral rectus recession achieved more effective exotropia correction compared with alternative surgical techniques at 18 months of follow-up. Similarly, Choi et al. [14] observed reduced recurrence rates following bilateral lateral rectus recession when compared with unilateral lateral rectus recession combined with medial rectus resection over a 2-year follow-up period. In addition, Maruo et al. [15] reported more favorable surgical outcomes with bilateral lateral rectus recession in a large series of 666 patients monitored for 4 years. These results have been attributed to a possible reduction in the tethering effect secondary to increased tension of the medial rectus muscle [16–18]. Additionally, earlier reports have indicated that the success of unilateral lateral rectus recession combined with medial rectus resection may diminish with advancing patient age [19]. In contrast, Kim et al. [20] demonstrated more favorable motor alignment and lower recurrence rates with unilateral lateral rectus recession combined with medial rectus resection compared with bilateral lateral rectus recession during a 9-year follow-up. Similarly, Wang et al. [21] and Kim H. et al. [22] reported enhanced surgical outcomes with unilateral lateral rectus recession combined with medial rectus resection in patients with intermittent exotropia.

In the present study, although early outcomes numerically favored unilateral R&R, differences were not statistically significant, and both procedures yielded comparable 1-year motor and sensory outcomes. At 1 year, both procedures produced comparable motor alignment and sensory fusion outcomes. Both surgical techniques provided favorable results in stereopsis, fusion, and angle of deviation. Similarly, the Pediatric Eye Disease Investigator Group demonstrated comparable effectiveness of bilateral lateral rectus recession and unilateral lateral rectus recession combined with medial rectus resection in intermittent exotropia in a cohort of 197 children [23]. Consistent with these findings, Wang et al. [24] reported similar efficacy between the two procedures in a 2021 meta-analysis including 967 patients.

This study has several limitations. This study has a retrospective design and the modest sample size, which may reduce power to detect small between-group differences. Because the number of 1-year failure events was limited, the multivariable logistic regression model had a low events-per-variable ratio and was therefore at increased risk of overfitting. Although the model was restricted to three prespecified clinically relevant covariates, the adjusted estimates should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. Surgical dosing details and potential inter-surgeon variability should be explicitly reported, as these may influence alignment stability. Surgical allocation was not randomized and was influenced by clinical judgment and parental preference regarding unilateral versus bilateral surgery; therefore, potential selection bias cannot be completely excluded despite the comparable baseline clinical characteristics of the two groups. Finally, follow-up was limited to 1 year; longer-term observation is warranted given the known tendency for recurrence in intermittent exotropia beyond the first postoperative year. The 1-year follow-up period represents an important limitation of the present study. Recurrence of intermittent exotropia may continue to develop beyond the first postoperative year, particularly during the subsequent 2–3 years. Therefore, the comparable outcomes observed between BLR and R&R in the present study should be interpreted within the 1-year postoperative period and should not be extrapolated to long-term surgical stability. Longer-term follow-up is required to determine whether differences in recurrence between the two procedures emerge over time.

Conclusion

Patients with larger preoperative angles of deviation demonstrated lower 1-year motor success rates independent of surgical technique. In conclusion, bilateral lateral rectus recession and unilateral lateral rectus recession combined with medial rectus resection produced comparable motor and sensory outcomes at 1 year in children with basic-type intermittent exotropia. Longer-term follow-up is required to determine the durability of these outcomes and to assess potential differences in recurrence between the two procedures.
