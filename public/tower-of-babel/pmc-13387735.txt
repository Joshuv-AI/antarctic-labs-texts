The “GABAergic Brake” on the Spinal Defecation Reflex: A Proposed Neurobiological Model Explaining Clozapine‐ and Benzodiazepine‐Induced Gastrointestinal Hypomotility and Ogilvie's Syndrome
Authors: Takahiko Nagamine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Synergistic “Double Brake” Model on the Spinal Defecation Center in Clozapine‐Induced Constipation. Real‐world data shows BZDRs increase laxative risk in schizophrenia. Mitigating the central GABAergic brake via sedative optimization and maintaining gut microbiota homeostasis prevents fatal Ogilvie's syndrome.

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the recent nationwide retrospective study by Ochi et al. demonstrating a significant association between benzodiazepine prescriptions and the risk of laxative use in patients with schizophrenia [1]. While the authors appropriately highlighted non‐specific behavioral factors such as sedation and reduced physical activity, we believe their findings carry profound mechanistic implications. Specifically, this real‐world clinical evidence from Japan provides a critical clinical foundation for re‐evaluating the neurobiological underpinnings of severe gastrointestinal dysfunction, particularly in patients receiving clozapine.

Clozapine remains the gold standard for treatment‐resistant schizophrenia, yet clozapine‐induced gastrointestinal hypomotility (CIGH) represents a severe, potentially fatal complication that can rapidly progress to megacolon or acute colonic pseudo‐obstruction, historically known as Ogilvie's syndrome [2]. Crucially, Ogilvie's syndrome is characterized by massive dilatation of part or all of the colon and rectum without any mechanical or intrinsic obstruction [3]. In psychiatric and emergency care, this condition represents a critical medical emergency: if unrecognized, cecal dilatation carries a high risk of bowel perforation, requiring high‐mortality surgical interventions like cecostomy or colectomy [3]. The pathophysiology is thought to involve severe autonomic dysregulation—specifically, an imbalance between suppressed sacral parasympathetic output and overactive sympathetic tone. Although CIGH and its progression to Ogilvie's syndrome are traditionally attributed to peripheral anticholinergic mechanisms, this conventional model alone appears insufficient to explain the profound treatment resistance and the high prevalence of constipation observed in daily psychiatric practice [4]. Based on established preclinical frameworks, we propose a synergistic central‐peripheral model wherein the nationwide data reported by Ochi et al. may reflect an overlooked pharmacological phenomenon: concomitant benzodiazepine receptor agonists (BZDRs) may act as a central “second hit,” potentially suppressing the spinal defecation reflex [5, 6].

To understand this plausible synergy, it is essential to consider the central regulation of colorectal motility, which shares anatomical and functional networks with the descending pain modulatory system. Preclinical tracing and physiologic animal models have established that rectal distension activates descending monoaminergic pathways projecting from the periaqueductal gray (PAG) to the sacral parasympathetic nucleus (SPN) in the spinal defecation center [7]. Within this network, descending dopaminergic and serotonergic signaling facilitates the activation of preganglionic parasympathetic neurons, thereby driving colorectal propulsion. Conversely, local GABAergic interneurons in the sacral cord exert a continuous tonic inhibitory influence on the SPN, serving as a physiological “GABAergic brake” to prevent premature evacuation [5, 6, 7]. Antipsychotics, especially clozapine with its potent, broad‐spectrum antagonism of dopamine (D2) and serotonin (5‐HT2A/5‐HT2C) receptors, significantly blunt this descending facilitatory monoaminergic drive [6]. When BZDRs are chronically co‐prescribed to manage residual anxiety, insomnia, or akathisia [5], they strongly potentiate GABA‐A receptor‐mediated inhibitory transmission within these same sacral circuits [6]. Preclinical rodent models have demonstrated that GABAergic signaling at the spinal cord level can directly inhibit or compete with the facilitative action of monoamines on the defecation reflex [6]. Therefore, we hypothesize that this dual burden—the loss of descending monoaminergic facilitation combined with BZDR‐induced intensification of local GABAergic inhibition—may synergistically immobilize the spinal defecation center, predisposing vulnerable patients to severe pseudo‐obstruction (Figure 1).

The Proposed Synergistic Neurobiological Model of Clozapine‐ and Benzodiazepine‐Induced Defecation Reflex Failure. Rectal distension normally triggers descending facilitatory pathways (dopaminergic and serotonergic) from the periaqueductal gray (PAG) to the sacral parasympathetic nucleus (SPN) to promote colorectal motility. Clozapine blunts this monoaminergic facilitation. Concomitant benzodiazepines potentially potentiate GABAergic interneurons (“GABAergic brake”) within the spinal defecation center, a pathway demonstrated to compete with monoaminergic drive in preclinical models. This hypothesized dual pharmacological burden may suppress sacral autonomic output, contributing to severe clozapine‐induced gastrointestinal hypomotility (CIGH) and escalating the risk of acute colonic pseudo‐obstruction (Ogilvie's syndrome).

This integrated framework also offers a rational, though still theoretical, explanation for the clinically observed sex differences in constipation severity. Experimental evidence indicates a notable sexual dimorphism in rodent defecatory circuits: while male circuits rely predominantly on robust monoaminergic facilitation, female sacral circuits exhibit a significantly higher baseline dependence on GABAergic gating mechanisms in response to colorectal stimulation [6, 8]. Consequently, female patients may possess an intrinsic neurobiological vulnerability to pharmacologically enhanced GABAergic suppression. This implies that the BZDR‐associated laxative risk captured by Ochi et al. [1] may be disproportionately pronounced or dangerous in women, potentially accelerating the transition from routine CIGH to acute Ogilvie's syndrome [5].

From a therapeutic perspective, disrupting this hypothesized central “GABAergic brake” represents a logical target in clozapine management. In patients suffering from refractory CIGH, withdrawing BZDRs or substituting them with dual orexin receptor antagonists (DORAs) should be cautiously considered as a potential non‐laxative approach. Because DORAs promote sleep by blocking orexin receptors without directly amplifying GABA‐A receptor signaling, their use theoretically avoids the chronic enhancement of sacral spinal inhibition, thereby preserving the physiological integrity of the defecation reflex [6]. While direct clinical evidence confirming that BZDR‐to‐DORA substitution reduces Ogilvie's syndrome is currently lacking, this approach warrants clinical investigation. Furthermore, this central reflex failure inevitably exacerbates peripheral dysfunction through the gut–brain axis. Prolonged colonic transit alters the intestinal microenvironment, driving severe dysbiosis characterized by the depletion of beneficial short‐chain fatty acid (SCFA)‐producing bacteria [9]. Paradoxically, standard intervention strategies in Japan, such as the ubiquitous use of magnesium oxide (MgO) as a first‐line osmotic laxative [4], may inadvertently alter the microbiome composition [10]. Chronic MgO administration elevates intraluminal pH, which can suppress butyrate‐producing microbiota [10]. Because SCFAs like butyrate are essential for modulating central dopaminergic signaling and transporter expression [6, 11, 12], MgO‐induced dysbiosis might further weaken the descending facilitatory pathways. Therefore, optimizing central reflexes via BZDR reduction should ideally be coupled with specific microbiota‐oriented strategies to disrupt this detrimental feedback loop [6, 10, 11]. Given that even alternative osmotic laxatives like polyethylene glycol may similarly disrupt the intestinal microenvironment, a more direct approach focusing on gut microbiota management appears highly preferable. Specifically, the administration of probiotics containing a combination of lactic acid bacteria and butyrate‐producing bacteria could potentially mitigate the laxative‐induced dysbiosis, thereby preserving enteric SCFA production and supporting the descending facilitatory pathways [6, 10].

In conclusion, the real‐world data by Ochi et al. [1] provide vital epidemiological support for the gastrointestinal risks of BZDRs in schizophrenia. Recognizing the potential overlapping pharmacological suppression of the spinal defecation reflex by clozapine and BZDRs may be essential to preventing severe CIGH and catastrophic Ogilvie's syndrome [2, 7]. Prospective clinical studies investigating colonic transit time and longitudinal microbiome profiles during BZDR withdrawal are highly warranted to validate this dual‐mechanism hypothesis.
