Numerical investigation of the flexural behaviour and composite action of reinforced concrete sandwich panels (RCSP) with EPS core: parametric study using FEA
Authors: Hibretu Kaske Kassa, Putul Haldar, Adil Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConventional unreinforced brick masonry walls pose significant sustainability and seismic vulnerability challenges due to excessive resource consumption and self-weight. Reinforced Concrete Sandwich Panels (RCSP) with Expanded Polystyrene (EPS) core present a promising alternative, offering similar functional benefits while being lightweight, sustainable, and exhibiting enhanced seismic performance. This study investigates flexural behaviour of RCSP using validated three-dimensional nonlinear finite element model developed in Abaqus/Explicit, demonstrating close agreement with experimental results in terms of ultimate load capacity, crack initiation and propagation, and failure mechanisms. A systematic parametric study evaluated the influence of shear connector type, spacing, diameter, Welded Wire Mesh (WWM) size, EPS core thickness, and longitudinal reinforcement. Among connector types, Double-Truss Shear Connectors (DSC) increased the ultimate load capacity by up to 12–18% and improved post-peak ductility compared to orthogonal and Single-Truss Shear Connectors (SSC). Reducing connector spacing from 150 to 75 mm enhanced the ultimate load capacity by approximately 8.9%, with negligible change in failure mode. Increasing WWM diameter from 3 to 6 mm resulted in significant increase in flexural strength of up to 102.8%, indicating strong dependence on reinforcement stiffness. Variations in EPS core thickness showed less than 3% change in flexural capacity, confirming its negligible structural contribution under bending. Inclusion of additional longitudinal reinforcement (6 mm Fe500 bars at 160 mm spacing) increased ultimate load capacity by 40.5% and significantly enhanced ductility. These findings provide critical insights into the governing parameters affecting composite action and offer design-oriented recommendations for optimizing RCSP systems as lightweight, sustainable, and seismic-resilient alternatives to conventional masonry walls.Keywords: Expanded polystyrene, Reinforced concrete sandwich panels, Flexure behaviour, Finite element modelling, Parametric studySubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Walls play vital roles in constructing structures by offering lateral stability, protection from the environment through thermal and acoustic insulation, and privacy for occupants. Among the available materials, unreinforced brick masonry walls are still widely used due to their affordability, ease of construction, and insulating properties. However, the inhomogeneous nature of brick masonry leads to complex and unpredictable failure modes when subjected to lateral loads 1–8. Furthermore, the considerable self-weight of masonry increases seismic demands on the structural frame, often leading to severe damage or collapse in earthquake-prone areas9–17.

The unequivocal need for an alternative wall panel solution that reduces seismic weight without compromising other beneficial properties has motivated the adoption of Reinforced Concrete Sandwich Panels (RCSP) with an Expanded Polystyrene (EPS) core. RCSP consists of two concrete wythes which is reinforced with Welded Wire Mesh (WWM) and separated by a lightweight EPS core, connected by shear connectors18–29 as presented in Fig. 1. These panels offer multiple advantages including reduced weight, enhanced thermal and acoustic insulation, improved seismic performance, and ease of handling and installation. However, the overall performance of RCSP composite is contingent upon several parameters, such as various types of shear connectors, spacing of shear connectors, thickness of concrete wythe, change in diameter of shear connector and wired mesh, EPS core thickness in the RCSP, and presence of longitudinal reinforcement in the wythe.

(a) Typical components of RCSP with EPS core; (b) EPS core with welded steel wire mesh, shear connectors, and cross-section of RCSP 66.

Despite increasing adoption of RCSP in modern construction, limited studies have addressed their behaviour under various loading conditions, particularly in flexure. Benayoune et al.30 and Joseph et al.31 conducted experimental investigations on precast RCSPs with EPS cores under four-point bending. Their results indicated that the panels demonstrated composite action up to failure and exhibited flexural response characteristics similar to conventional reinforced concrete one-way slabs. Hou et al.32 evaluated the flexural response of EPS-core sandwich panels under gradually increasing, uniformly distributed loading. The panels achieved full composite action; however, those with ribbed exterior wythes showed reduced flexural resistance at the onset of cracking compared to flat wythes. Ahmad and Singh19 explored the influence of edge beam configurations on flexural performance. Their findings highlighted that the presence and type of edge beam (meshed vs. reinforced) significantly influenced the failure mode and crack propagation patterns of RCSP specimens.

Other studies have focused on crack development and failure mechanisms. Fam33 reported that panels reinforced with Basalt Fiber-Reinforced Polymer (BFRP) exhibited a greater tendency for concrete wythe crushing compared to those with steel reinforcement. Mohamad et al.34 observed that cracking typically initiates at mid-span and propagates symmetrically toward both supports. Benayoune et al.30 further noted that initial cracking occurred at approximately 55–60% of the ultimate load. Importantly, they found that the stiffness and configuration of shear connectors play a critical role in enhancing the panel’s load capacity and overall composite action.

While RCSP with EPS core has attracted growing research interest due to its lightweight construction, energy efficiency, and sustainability advantages, significant knowledge gaps persist regarding its structural behaviour under different loading conditions, such as flexural loading.

Despite growing research interest, several critical knowledge gaps remain underexplored. First, there is a lack of comprehensive numerical investigations capable of simultaneously capturing global flexural response and local damage mechanisms such as crack initiation, propagation, and interfacial slip. Second, the influence of key governing parameters, including shear connector type, spacing, diameter, Welded Wire Mesh (WWM) characteristics, EPS core thickness, and additional longitudinal reinforcement in the concrete wythe, has not been systematically evaluated within a unified analytical framework. Third, the role of shear connector configuration in governing the degree of composite action and its direct impact on flexural strength, ductility, and failure mode remains insufficiently quantified. Furthermore, the contribution of additional longitudinal reinforcement within thin concrete wythes, particularly in enhancing ductility and post-peak behaviour, has received limited attention in existing literature.

To address these gaps, the present study develops a validated three-dimensional nonlinear finite element model of RCSP subjected to flexural loading using Abaqus. The model is calibrated against experimental results reported by Ahmad and Singh19, ensuring accurate representation of both global structural response and local failure mechanisms. A comprehensive parametric study is subsequently conducted to investigate the effects of shear connector type (orthogonal, Single-Truss, and Double-Truss), connector spacing and diameter, WWM diameter, EPS core thickness, and the inclusion of additional longitudinal reinforcement. The novelty of this study lies in: (i) the development of a high-fidelity numerical model capable of capturing both global and local response characteristics of RCSP systems; (ii) a systematic multi-parameter evaluation within a unified framework to quantify the influence of key design variables; (iii) explicit assessment of composite action through variation of connector configurations; and (iv) generation of quantitative, design-oriented insights into strength, ductility, and failure mechanisms. The outcomes of this study aim to support the rational design and wider structural adoption of RCSP systems as lightweight, sustainable, and seismically efficient alternatives to conventional masonry construction.

Development of FE model for RCSP

A detailed Finite Element (FE) model of the Reinforced Concrete Sandwich Panel (RCSP) was developed using Abaqus35, replicating the experimental configuration presented by Ahmad and Singh19 under flexural loading. The RCSP model consists of two concrete wythes, an Expanded Polystyrene (EPS) core, mesh-anchored edge beams (MEBs), and embedded shear connectors as depicted in schematic configuration in Fig. 2 (a). As shown in Fig. 2 (b), the experimental setup involved applying two-line loads at one-third of the effective span to evaluate the panel’s flexural capacity, mid-span deflection, crack propagation, and failure mechanism.

(a) Cross-section of 165 mm thick RCSP specimen; (b) Test setup for flexure test in loading frame19.

The RCSP specimen considered in this study, based on the experimental study by Ahmad and Singh19, has a rectangular shape with an overall thickness of 165 mm, width of 600 mm, and length of 1200 mm. The panel consists of two outer layers of concrete wythes, encasing a prefabricated EPS core, forming a layered composite cross-section. The top and bottom wythe have thickness of 50 mm and 35 mm, respectively, with one wythe surface exhibiting a corrugated profile conforming to the EPS core geometry. The pre-fabricated EPS core has an average thickness of 80 mm with longitudinally aligned corrugations of 15 mm height and 80 mm peak-to-peak spacing, providing mechanical interlocking at the wythe to core interface to enhance interfacial shear transfer. Each concrete wythe is reinforced with a galvanized steel WWM comprising 3 mm diameter wires arranged orthogonally at longitudinal and transverse spacings of 80 mm and 75 mm, respectively. Composite action between the wythes is achieved through orthogonal shear connectors of 127 mm length, penetrating the EPS core and anchored into both wythes at transverse and longitudinal spacings of 240 mm and 150 mm, respectively. The EPS core is removed from the edge beam zones to form Mesh anchored concrete Edge Beam (MEB), each 150 mm wide, where the WWM extends continuously into the plain micro-concrete edge beams, ensuring structural continuity at the panel boundaries.

To simulate the RCSP accurately, each component was modelled individually within the Abaqus part module. A total of four three-dimensional (3D) solid parts and three wire elements were defined to represent the concrete wythes, EPS core, shear connectors, wire mesh, and edge beams. Loading and boundary conditions

The FE model assembly integrated all primary RCSP constituents, such as concrete wythes, EPS core, Welded Wire Mesh (WWM), orthogonal shear connectors, and edge beams, with interfacial interactions defined to accurately replicate the load transfer mechanisms observed experimentally. The WWM was incorporated into the concrete wythes and edge beams using the embedded element constraint in Abaqus, enforcing full translational compatibility between reinforcement and host concrete nodes and assuming perfect bond throughout the loading. Similarly, the orthogonal shear connectors were modelled as embedded elements anchored within both concrete wythes at their respective ends, with the connector body traversing the EPS core to ensure effective interfacial shear transfer and composite action between the wythes (Fig. 3 (c)). A tie constraint was assigned at the interface of concrete wythe-edge beam, enforcing full displacement and rotational compatibility consistent with the monolithic micro-concrete casting of the edge beam in the experimental program. The interface between the EPS core and the concrete wythes and edge beams was modelled using a surface-to-surface contact formulation with hard contact in the normal direction to prevent interpenetration and simulate friction-governed interfacial shear transfer. The friction coefficient was assigned based on values reported in the literature, and a sensitivity study was conducted for EPS-concrete interfaces.

The RCSP was modelled as simply supported with an effective span of 1050 mm, consistent with the experimental setup. One end of the panel was restrained in both vertical and horizontal directions, while the other end was restrained only in vertical direction. Loading was applied through two-line loads positioned at one-third of the span from each support, using displacement-controlled loading at a rate of 0.5 mm/s (Fig. 3 (d)). Reference points were assigned to the load application locations and connected to rigid bodies to facilitate load transfer to the continuum parts. Given the quasi-static nature of concrete failure under bending, the Abaqus/Explicit dynamic solver was used to model the flexural response efficiently. This solver is particularly effective in capturing localized cracking, damage evolution, and post-peak softening behaviour in brittle materials. Mass scaling and/or artificial damping were not used; thus, the effect of inertia was mitigated only by controlling the load rate without altering material and inertial properties artificially.

EPS core

The EPS core was modelled using an elastic–plastic constitutive formulation. The initial response was defined as linear elastic with a Young’s modulus of 4 MPa37, consistent with reported values for EPS of similar density. Although EPS exhibits nonlinear behaviour under large compressive strains51,52, its contribution to the overall structural response of RCSP under flexural loading is negligible due to its significantly lower stiffness compared to concrete wythes. Under bending, the EPS core primarily functions as a lightweight filler and spacer, maintaining the separation between concrete wythes and facilitating interfacial shear transfer through contact and connector action. The stress levels developed within the EPS remain very low,therefore, the response is dominated by the elastic regime. However, plastic behaviour was included using experimentally derived stress–strain data36,53 which varies based on the density of EPS core, as shown in Fig. 6, to ensure numerical robustness in localized compression zones, particularly near shear connectors and contact interfaces. Thus, the EPS core does not contribute meaningfully to load-carrying capacity or stiffness, and its role remains primarily geometric and functional rather than structural.

Parameters influencing the FE model accuracy

To enhance the fidelity of the FE model of the Reinforced Concrete Sandwich Panel (RCSP), a comprehensive sensitivity analysis was conducted. Key modelling parameters-including dilation angle, shape factor, viscosity parameter, Poisson’s ratio of the EPS core, mesh size, and the friction coefficient at material interfaces were varied to assess their influence on the model’s moment-rotation response. The goal was to minimize discrepancies between the numerical and experimental results across parameters such as initial stiffness, yield, ultimate moment, and the softening behaviour.

Mesh convergence and element discretization

A mesh sensitivity analysis was checked for different mesh size of 35, 40, 45, 50, and 55 mm against experimental results in a response of initial axial stiffness in the pre-cracking elastic regime, ultimate load-carrying capacity, and post-peak softening behaviour. The optimal mesh size was identified as that producing the minimum percentage deviation from experimental values across all three stages.

Mesh sizes of 45 mm and 50 mm demonstrate satisfactory convergence, yielding moment-rotation responses that closely match the experimental results. Consequently, a mesh size of 45 mm is considered optimal for simulating the structural behaviour of RCSP, as illustrated in Fig. 8. The coarser mesh of 55 mm proved inadequate, as it failed to capture the post-peak strength degradation in the moment-rotation curve, producing significant deviations, particularly in regions where crack localization is highly sensitive to element size. Conversely, finer mesh sizes of 35 mm and 40 mm resulted in comparatively lower initial stiffness and ultimate load capacity, along with a less pronounced post-peak load drop.

Effect of mesh size on moment vs rotation curve of RCSP.

Dilation angle and plasticity parameters

In the Concrete Damaged Plasticity (CDP) model, the dilation angle represents the ratio of volumetric to shear strain during plastic flow. It varies in range of 30°- 40°54,57. Sensitivity analysis was performed over the same range, and convergence was achieved at a dilation angle of 33.5°. The shape factor (k), which defines the yield surface in the deviatoric plane, was varied between 0.5 and 1.0, and a value of 0.85 was found optimal. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the effect of the viscosity parameter on the numerical response of RCSP. Material models exhibiting stiffness degradation and strain-softening behaviour often encounter numerical convergence difficulties in finite element analysis. The viscosity parameter is the general technique to overcome some of these difficulties by permitting stresses outside the yield surface [35];58. Viscosity values of 0, 0.0005, 0.001, 0.0015, and 0.002 were examined. The results indicate that the influence of the viscosity parameter on the overall response of RCSP is negligible, with no significant differences observed in convergence behaviour and in the predicted results for initial stiffness, up to the yielding moment, the ultimate moment, and the softening part between the FEA and the experimental results which demonstrates that the computational model is free from any convergence issue. This is due to the combined effect of the selection of the tensile stress-displacement constitute model and the stiffening effect provided by the rebars embedded in concrete wythe.

EPS core properties and interface friction

The Poisson’s ratio of the EPS core, which ranges from 0.05 to 0.5 in the literature53,59,[36], was tested for its influence on panel behaviour. A value of 0.25 produced the best agreement with experimental results. However, its influence on global response was found to be minimal, confirming the limited structural role of the EPS core under flexural loading.

The friction coefficient at the EPS-concrete interface was determined through a systematic sensitivity analysis by varying its value between 0.27 and 1.2, as reported in the literature for similar material interfaces60–62. The numerical responses were compared with experimental moment-rotation behaviour in terms of initial stiffness, ultimate load capacity, and post-peak softening as shown in Fig. 9. As the coefficient of friction increases, a corresponding increase in the ultimate load capacity is observed, leading to notable changes in the post-peak response. A coefficient of friction between 0.8 and 1.0, the moment-rotation curve exhibits relative stability, indicating diminishing sensitivity. A friction coefficient of 0.96 provided the closest overall agreement with experimental results. This relatively high value is attributed to the corrugated configuration of the EPS core (15 mm height and 80 mm spacing), which enhances mechanical interlocking at the interface and increases resistance to interfacial slip compared to a smooth surface. The sensitivity analysis (Fig. 9) further indicated that the friction coefficient has limited influence on initial stiffness and peak load, but a more pronounced effect on post-peak behaviour and ductility, where interfacial slip governs the degradation of composite action. Therefore, the adopted friction coefficient is both physically justified and numerically validated, ensuring a realistic representation of interfacial behaviour in the RCSP system.

Verification of FE model with experimental observations

This section demonstrates the ability of the developed FE model in capturing the moment-rotation behaviour, crack initiation and propagation, and failure mechanism of the RCSP composite observed by Ahmad and Singh19 under flexural loading.

Moment-rotation behaviour and load-carrying capacity

The present study validates the FE model by comparing its numerical analysis results with experimental observations by Ahmad and Singh19. This validation involves analysing the FEA results against experimental findings obtained from three samples and their average to ensure accuracy and reliability. From the experiment, the average ultimate/peak moment of resistance is 15.31 kNm/m at an average rotation of 0.0277. Additional parameters encompass the initial stiffness and ductility of the curves, providing a comparative evaluation of the FE model’s accuracy and performance of RCSP. The area under the moment-rotation curve indicates the energy absorption capacity of the RCSP under flexural loading, and the percentage difference between the experimental and numerical results is less than 10%, demonstrating the accuracy and reliability of the developed FE model. In the early stage (before cracks occur), both curves closely match within the linear range. In the nonlinear range, the FEA curve falls between the experimental curves of individual samples and their average, showing a closer match with the average curve. The slightly higher maximum moment predicted by the FEA compared to the experimental result is an expected in nonlinear FE modelling of RCSP, attributable to the idealized material constitutive models, perfect bond assumptions between reinforcement and concrete as embedment, and the absence of geometric imperfections and material variability inherent in experimental specimens. The FEA results indicate that the applied load was mainly resisted by the catenary action of the concrete wythes, a phenomenon also seen in the experiment. The micro-concrete developed cracks early on, while the WWM mostly resisted the ultimate load through catenary action. When the ultimate load was reached, the resistance of the RCSP suddenly dropped, leading to failure. The sharp decline in resistance capacity after reaching the ultimate load was due to cracking in the concrete wythe and the rupture of the WWM beyond its yielding stress, which was also observed in the experiment.

The distinct responses of the top and bottom concrete wythes, along with the roles of shear connectors and WWM, contribute to the overall behaviour of the RCSP. Crack propagation and failure mechanism

From the experiment, Ahmad and Singh19 a crack in the specimen was initiated at the bottom of the span, near half of the length, and spread across the entire span. Early-stage cracking is observed in the micro-concrete on both sides of the RCSP. The WWM reinforcement primarily resists the ultimate load. The crack along the edge beam in the top wythe occurred later, as shown in Fig. 14 (a). The FEA results show a strong correlation with the experimental observations regarding crack initiation and progression, except that in the case of FEA, the crack occurs symmetrically due to the fact that the FE model does not consider voids in micro-concrete or cracks caused by temperature and shrinkage, as illustrated in Fig. 14 (b), (c), and (d) under flexural loading. Numerical results align with the experimental findings that the catenary action of the wythes primarily resisted the applied load. Notably, cracks in the micro-concrete wythe began to form at an early stage of loading. The WWM primarily resisted the ultimate load through catenary action. Figure 14 (b) and (c) illustrate the crack propagation in the RCSP at two critical stages: (b) at the peak load stage, and (c) immediately prior to the sudden drop in load-carrying capacity, which ultimately leads to failure.

Observations from both the FE model and the experimental test show that the final failure occurred due to the rupture of the WWM near the supports in the bottom wythe and near the loading lines in the top wythe, as shown in Fig. 15 (a), (b), and (c). The figure shows the stress in the WWM of the bottom and top wythe of the RCSP, and beyond this point, the load-carrying capacity of the panel suddenly decreases, leading to the failure of the RCSP. The WWM in the top and bottom wythe reached its yielding point early at a displacement of 11.86 mm and 13.05 mm, respectively, and resisted until failure. The EPS core experienced shear tension failure within the constant shear zone of the specimen. This failure pattern indicates a considerable transfer of shear forces through the EPS core. The failure mechanism is a combination of flexure and shear failure. The behaviour of the RCSP specimens is between the fully composite and non-composite actions of the two wythes in both experimental and FEA cases. The sequence of failure and yielding in each component highlights that the concrete section experienced yielding and failure first, followed by the shear connector, and ultimately, the WWM resisted the load until rupture occurred in it.

It is acknowledged that the validation is based on a single experimental study, which may limit the generality of the conclusions. However, the model demonstrates strong agreement with experimental results not only in terms of peak response but also in capturing the full moment–rotation behaviour, crack propagation, and failure mechanisms. This indicates that the model reliably represents the governing physical behaviour of RCSP systems under flexural loading. The observed deviations, which are within 10%, are attributed to modelling idealizations such as assumed perfect bond, simplified constitutive relationships, and the absence of geometric imperfections and material variability inherent in experimental specimens. Despite these limitations, the consistency of agreement across multiple response parameters supports the reliability and predictive capability of the developed FE model.

Parametric studies using the validated finite element model

The validated FE model is employed to investigate the influence of various parameters on the structural performance of Reinforced Concrete Sandwich Panels (RCSP) subjected to flexural loading. The parametric study evaluated the impact of (a) different types of shear connectors (SSC, DSC), (b) spacing of orthogonal shear connectors along the length (75 mm, 125 mm, 175 mm), (c) variations in the thickness of the EPS core (60 mm, 90 mm, 100 mm, 120 mm), (d) changes in the diameter of the WWM and orthogonal shear connectors, and (e) presence of longitudinal reinforcement in the RCSP wythe. It should be noted that the present parametric study evaluates the influence of each variable independently to establish its fundamental role in governing RCSP behaviour. Potential interaction effects between parameters, although important, are beyond the scope of this study and will be addressed in future work.

Influence of shear connector type

In this section, the parametric study was conducted to investigate the performance of RCSP using different types of shear connectors, specifically Single-Truss Shear Connectors (SSC) and Double-Truss Shear Connectors (DSC). Both the SSC and DSC shear connectors are modelled as truss-type element inclined at 60-degree angle, passing through the EPS core of RCSP, and tied to the WWM within the concrete wythes, as illustrated in Fig. 16.

(a) SSC arrangement; (b) SSC with RCSP; (c); DSC arrangement and (d) DSC with RCSP.

As shown in Fig. 17 (a), panels with DSC exhibited slightly higher initial stiffness and greater ultimate moment capacity than those with SSC or orthogonal connectors. This enhancement is attributed to the truss connectors’ ability to resist both shear and flexural deformations, thereby improving stiffness, delaying crack initiation, and enhancing energy dissipation. Both SSC and DSC configurations demonstrated improved ductility and delayed crack propagation compared to orthogonal connectors. Crack propagation in the RCSP with SSC and DSC is nearly identical to that of an orthogonal shear connector, as presented in Fig. 17 (b) and (c), except that the crack initiation and spreading times are relatively longer in the case of DSC. For the RCSP with SSC and DSC, the bottom part of the RCSP exhibits cracking. Conversely, the top wythe shows some cracks near the midpoint and at the applied line load. In all cases, the failure sequence began with yielding of the concrete section, followed by gradual failure of the shear connectors, indicating partially composite action.

(a) Moment-rotation curve of the panel with different types of orthogonal shear connectors; (b) Crack propagation and failure mode of RCSP with SSC; and (c) Crack propagation and failure mode of RCSP with DSC.

Among the shear connector types investigated, the DSC demonstrated higher flexural structural performance, with higher initial stiffness, greater ultimate load-carrying capacity, and more gradual post-peak softening than both the orthogonal shear connector and SSC. This is attributed to the geometric redundancy of the DSC arrangement, wherein two connector legs simultaneously resist interfacial slip over a larger bonded area at the wythe-core interface, promoting more effective composite action and sustained load transfer through the post-peak regime. Moreover, DSC and SSC showed better flexural behaviour in comparison with orthogonal shear connector, where ultimate load capacity for both types of shear connector was about 7.04% higher than in the case of orthogonal shear connector.

Effect of shear connector spacing along the length

The spacing of shear connectors along the span of RCSP influences the load-carrying capacity, composite action, and failure mechanism, especially under flexural loading. It is essential to investigate the optimal spacing of shear connectors during RCSP design to enhance their structural behaviour. In this section, three models with orthogonal shear connectors spaced at 75 mm, 125 mm, and 175 mm (longitudinally) were analysed, maintaining a constant transverse spacing of 240 mm. The results indicate that reducing the connector spacing has a minor impact on the ultimate load but significantly influences ductility and strain capacity.

As shown in Fig. 18 (a), the RCSP with a spacing of 75 mm along the span achieved a higher ultimate moment (18.40 kNm/m) and exhibited extended ductile response (rotation of 0.03345 rad). Crack propagation patterns for shear connector spacings of 75 mm, 125 mm, and 175 mm are illustrated in Fig. 18 (b), (c), and (d), respectively. Crack propagation was similar across all models; however, the panels with closer spacing (75 mm and 125 mm) demonstrated delayed cracking and longer failure durations (Fig. 18 (b-d)). These findings emphasize that closer shear connector spacing improves ductility without substantially affecting failure modes, which remained consistent due to partial composite action between the wythes and the EPS core. Variation in connector spacing has a minimal influence on the initial flexural stiffness, showing that the pre-cracking response of RCSP is mainly governed by the concrete wythe rather than connector density. Connector spacing plays a significant role in governing post-peak ductility and energy dissipation capacity, and closer spacing is recommended where post-peak structural integrity is a design consideration. A closer connector spacing provides the highest restraint points against interfacial slip and wythe separation during progressive damage, thus supporting composite action beyond the peak load and resulting in more gradual load degradation. Reducing the orthogonal shear connector spacing from the baseline configuration of 150 mm to 75 mm resulted in an approximately 8.9% increase in ultimate flexural load-carrying capacity, accompanied by a more gradual post-peak softening response.

Effect of orthogonal shear connector spacing on (a) moment vs rotation curve; (b) crack propagation of panel with 75 mm spacing; (c) crack propagation of panel with 125 mm spacing; and (d) crack propagation of panel with 175 mm spacing.

Effect of longitudinal steel reinforcement in bottom wythe of RCSP

The purpose of providing additional longitudinal reinforcement in the bottom wythe is to increase the flexural strength of the RCSP. A total of two models were developed with 6 mm diameter longitudinal rebar provided in the bottom wythe with a spacing of 160 mm and 240 mm. It is evident from the moment-rotation curve shown in Fig. 19 (a) that introducing additional longitudinal rebars at the bottom wythe significantly improves the flexural resistance capacity of the RCSP with the panel reinforced at 160 mm spacing, achieving a higher moment resistance and reduced deformation.

Effect of additional rebar in bottom wythe (a) moment vs rotation curve, (b) crack propagation of panel with 6 mm diameter rebar at 160 mm spacing, and (c) crack propagation of panel with 6 mm diameter rebar at 240 mm spacing.

The initial stiffness of RCSP with additional rebar remained largely unchanged; however, post-yield behaviour revealed a sharp drop in load capacity after peak resistance was achieved, attributed to plastic deformation of both WWM and additional reinforcement. This indicates that reinforcement contributes effectively up to the strain-hardening stage, after which structural degradation dominates. The sequence of a crack spreading was not significantly influenced by the presence or absence of additional rebars in the bottom wythe, and the cracking in the RCSP specimen was initiated in the bottom wythe and symmetric propagation over the span as shown in Fig. 19 (b) and (c). Nonetheless, reinforced panels showed delayed crack progression and higher strain absorption, enhancing energy dissipation. The use of additional longitudinal steel rebars in the concrete wythes of RCSP has shown significant improvement in the ultimate load-carrying capacity compared to those without additional longitudinal steel rebars, which improves crack control by reducing crack spacing and width, delaying tensile crack propagation through the wythe and preserving the concrete compression zone integrity up to higher loads. Consequently, a longitudinal rebar spacing of 160 mm is identified as the optimal configuration, which provides the most effective balance in reinforcement efficiency and performance enhancement. The use of additional longitudinally placed reinforcing steel bars of 6 mm diameter Fe500 grade in the tension zone wythe at a spacing of 160 mm led to an appreciable increase of about 40.71% in the ultimate flexural carrying capacity compared to that of the panel only reinforced with galvanized WWM.

Effect of WWM and shear connector diameter

This section assesses the influence of varying the diameter of galvanized WWM and orthogonal shear connectors (3 mm, 4 mm, 5 mm, and 6 mm) on the load-carrying capacity and failure mechanism of the panel under flexural loading. The validated RCSP model originally used 3 mm diameters of WWM.

Figure 20 (a) reveals that increasing the diameter of the WWM and shear connector from 3 to 6 mm results in a maximum moment capacity of 34.27 kNm/m at a rotation of 0.0511. As the diameter of the WWM increases or decreases, there is an insignificant change in the rotation at the ultimate moment. The ultimate moment capacity and corresponding rotation for RCSP panels reinforced with intermediate diameters (4 mm and 5 mm) are recorded as 25.78 kNm/m with 0.0564 and 29.87 kNm/m with 0.0434, respectively. Despite the increase in moment resistance, larger diameters led to more abrupt post-peak declines in load capacity. This behaviour is attributed to the higher stress concentrations developed within the WWM as it exceeds the yield strength. Following yielding, the residual load-carrying capacity of the panel is primarily governed by the strain-hardening behaviour of the WWM, which provides additional resistance until the final failure occurs. The RCSP suddenly fails when the other components of the panel, such as the top and bottom wythe and the shear connector, reach their yielding point early. The WWM carries the applied load until the ultimate load is reached, at which point the rupture and failure of the WWM cause a sudden drop in load-carrying capacity.

Effect of the diameter of WWM and shear connector on (a) moment-rotation; (b) crack propagation of the RCSP with 4 mm diameter at a rotation of 0.0614; (c) crack propagation of the RCSP with 5 mm diameter at a rotation of 0.0461; and (d) crack propagation of the RCSP with 6 mm diameter at a rotation of 0.0511.

As shown in Fig. 20 (b), (c), and (d), the crack initiation and propagation patterns for RCSP specimens reinforced with larger diameters of WWM and orthogonal shear connectors display a similar failure mechanism to that of panels reinforced with 3 mm diameter reinforcement. As the diameter of the WWM and shear connectors increases, the time for cracks to propagate across the panel extends, and the larger-diameter reinforcement offers increased stiffness and confinement. This effectively restrains crack widening and slows damage progression, which enhances the ductility and energy absorption capacity of the RCSP system. Although, cracking patterns were similar across all cases, but larger diameters extended the time to failure and improved confinement, as shown in Fig. 20 (b–d). The increase in WWM and shear connector diameter from 3 to 4 mm, 5 mm, and 6 mm results in consistent improvements in initial flexural stiffness, ultimate load-carrying capacity, and post-peak response. This is attributed to the increased cross-sectional area and bending stiffness of the reinforcement, which enhances resistance to shear-induced deformation at the interface between the concrete wythes and the EPS core. Consequently, interfacial slip is reduced, leading to improved load transfer efficiency and enhanced composite interaction between the wythes.

However, it is important to note that this improvement does not imply the attainment of fully composite action. The degree of composite action was quantitatively evaluated using analytical bounding cases, as discussed in Sect. "Degree of composite action at ultimate stage". Although increasing the reinforcement diameter significantly enhances the ultimate moment capacity (with increases of approximately 52.55%, 76.75%, and 102.75% for 4 mm, 5 mm, and 6 mm diameters, respectively, relative to the 3 mm case), the predicted values remain within, or approach but do not consistently exceed, the fully composite analytical limit. This confirms that the RCSP continues to exhibit partially composite action, with improved interaction between wythes due to enhanced connector efficiency. From a design perspective, while increasing the diameter of WWM and shear connectors substantially improves flexural capacity, stiffness, and energy absorption, it also leads to increased post-peak brittleness and material usage. This is primarily attributed to the shift toward an over-reinforced section behaviour, wherein the increased steel area elevates the neutral axis depth such that the concrete compression zone reaches its crushing strain prior to tensile reinforcement yielding, producing a compression-governed brittle failure. Therefore, a balance between structural performance and material economy should be considered in practical applications.

Effect of EPS core thickness

The validated model has an EPS core thickness of 80 mm. For this parametric study, a total of three models with EPS core thicknesses of 60 mm, 100 mm, and 120 mm were developed and compared to the validated 80 mm core model, keeping all other geometric and material properties unchanged. The results show that variations in EPS core thickness did not significantly affect flexural capacity, failure mode, or crack propagation, except for a slight change in post-peak behaviour as shown in Fig. 21. All panels displayed partial composite action with similar cracking sequences. These findings are consistent with the findings by Carbonari et al.64, suggesting that EPS thickness plays a negligible role in flexural performance. However, for design optimization, further investigation is necessary, considering other performance aspects such as thermal insulation, fire resistance, and acoustic performance.

Effect of the EPS core thickness on (a) moment-rotation; (b) crack propagation of the RCSP with 60 mm thickness; (c) crack propagation of the RCSP with 100 mm thickness; and (d) crack propagation of the RCSP with 120 mm thickness.

In a RCSP, flexural rigidity is predominantly governed by the bending stiffness of the concrete wythes, which acts as the tension and compression flanges of an equivalent composite section. The EPS core, equivalent to the web of an I-section, serves primarily to maintain wythe separation rather than resist flexural stresses, owing to its considerably lower elastic modulus relative to concrete. As the EPS core experiences negligible longitudinal stress under flexural loading, its thickness does not affect crack initiation, propagation, or ultimate flexural capacity.

Degree of composite action at ultimate stage

To quantify the degree of composite action in the RCSP, classical approaches for direct evaluation are limited due to the complexity of interlayer interaction and the difficulty in explicitly capturing slip and strain compatibility between wythes. Therefore, a rational approach based on analytical bounding conditions namely, fully composite and non-composite action can be analytically evaluated following the methodology proposed by30,65.

For the non-composite case, in which no shear transfer is assumed between the concrete wythes at ultimate strength (Fig. 22 (a)), each wythe is assumed to act independently as a separated flexural element; and their individual ultimate flexural capacities are computed independently and superimposed to obtain the total panel resistance. In contrast, the fully composite case (Fig. 22 (b) assumes complete shear transfer between the wythes, such that the panel behaves as a monolithic section. The neutral axis and ultimate flexural capacity are determined by considering the combined contribution of both wythes and their reinforcement.

Schematic diagram showing estimation of (a) Non-composite and (b) Fully composite action.

The analytical calculations show ultimate moment resistances of 7.47 kNm/m and 33.96 kNm/m for the non-composite and fully composite action bounding cases, respectively. The FEA-predicted ultimate moment capacity of the reference model is 16.9 kNm/m, which lies between these two bounds, thereby confirming partially composite action of the RCSP under flexural loading. This intermediate response reflects the limited but effective shear transfer provided by the combined action of orthogonal shear connectors and frictional resistance at the EPS-concrete interface. For all configurations, including variations in shear connector type, spacing, additional reinforcement, and reinforcement diameter, the FEA-predicted ultimate moment consistently lies within the analytical bounds, indicating partially composite action.

However, even at higher diameters (e.g., 6 mm), the results do not consistently exceed the fully composite analytical capacity. Overall, the degree of composite action in RCSP is governed by the combined efficiency of shear connectors and interfacial friction. While design parameters such as reinforcement diameter and connector configuration can significantly enhance composite interaction, complete composite action remains an idealized upper bound that is not fully attained under the studied conditions.

Conclusion

This study employed a validated FE model to conduct a detailed parametric analysis of RCSP subjected to flexural loading. The numerical study on the flexural behaviour of RCSP has provided valuable insights into its load-carrying capacity, cracking propagation, and failure mechanism. The key conclusions drawn are as follows:

Shear Connector Type: The use of truss-type shear connectors (SSC and DSC) improves both the ultimate load capacity and ductility of the RCSP compared to orthogonal connectors. DSC provides the highest initial stiffness and energy absorption.
