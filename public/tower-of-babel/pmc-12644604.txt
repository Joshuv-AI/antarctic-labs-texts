Semantic orientation for indoor navigation system using large language models
Authors: Marzena Halama, Sławomir Nowak, Konrad Połys
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutonomous robots play an important role in modern indoor navigation, but existing systems often struggle to achieve seamless human interaction and semantic understanding of environments. This paper presents an Artificial Intelligence (AI)-driven object recognition system enhanced by Large Language Models (LLMs), such as GPT-4 Vision and Gemini, to bridge this gap. Our approach combines vision-based mapping techniques with natural language processing and interactions to enable intuitive collaboration on navigation tasks. By leveraging multimodal input and vector space analysis, our system achieves enhanced object recognition, semantic embedding, and context-aware responses, setting a new standard for autonomous indoor navigation. This approach provides a novel framework for improving spatial understanding and dynamic interaction, making it suitable for complex indoor environments.Keywords: Large language models, GPT-4, Semantic orientation, Indoor navigationSubject terms: Information technology, Computer science, Applied mathematics, Software

Introduction

Indoor navigation systems are becoming increasingly important in complex environments such as hospitals, airports1, and shopping centers, often serving individuals with various disabilities2 or the elderly and increasingly relying on machine learning (ML) techniques3,4. Many modern systems leverage Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) techniques to provide spatial awareness5, mapping capabilities6, and object detection7, allowing robots and intelligent assistants to navigate autonomously. However, there is a growing demand for indoor navigation systems that do more than just compute routes – users want systems that understand their environment and interact in natural language. For example, a user might ask ’Where is the nearest printer?’ or ’Take me to Room 202’ instead of giving coordinates. Humans navigate indoor spaces by recognizing objects and landmarks, forming a mental map, and using language to share this knowledge (for example, ’turn left at the elevator, the restroom is beside the water fountain’). Inspired by this, our work integrates computer vision and large-language models to create a semantic map of the environment. This map encodes what and where things are – objects, rooms, and important features – in a format that an AI can use for navigation and discuss with the user.

Existing solutions also have limitations related to their overall performance in indoor environments8. As users demand systems that not only provide precise directions but also respond contextually to human-like queries and tasks, there is a clear need for innovative approaches. In short, this research is motivated by the vision of an indoor navigator that sees and talks: it sees the world through computer vision, builds an organized memory of the space, and talks to users through the reasoning power of an LLM. To address these challenges, we propose an advanced autonomous navigation framework that integrates state-of-the-art computer vision with the semantic capabilities of LLMs, as shown below (Fig. 1).

The diagram illustrates human-robot communication and semantic mapping, where user queries interpreted by the LLM are combined with a database of visually recognized objects, enabling semantic navigation to the appropriate destination.

This integration allows the navigation system to map and understand indoor spaces, including objects and their relationships with one another and the environment, allowing meaningful, context-aware dialogue with users. At the heart of our system is a dedicated object database that stores detailed information about the environment, such as the objects within it along with their attributes, including color, size, and purpose. Using this database, the system gains a deeper understanding of their surroundings and can respond accurately to user queries about the location or function of various objects. To evaluate the real-world performance of the complete system, and including its ability to interpret natural-language instructions, reason about visual-semantic context, and autonomously navigate, we conducted a structured simulation consisting of 30 representative navigation queries. The system achieved a Query Completion Success Rate of 76.7%, successfully completing most navigation queries, indicating its readiness for real-use applications. By leveraging multimodal capabilities, our system not only detects objects in real-time but also interprets their relationships and responds naturally to questions such as “Where can I find a coffee?” or “What objects are in this room?”. Additionally, this system dynamically adapts to situational changes through autonomous room-by-room mapping, increasing its effectiveness in environments such as shopping centers, airports, and medical facilities. It also enhances accessibility for individuals with special needs, such as the elderly or those with limited mobility, by offering personalized and intuitive guidance. At the core of our approach is a dual-system architecture.The first component focuses on vision-based object detection, where advanced techniques such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)9,10 and You Only Look Once (YOLO) models11 can be utilized. However, these models primarily enable object identification without providing detailed attributes (e.g., color, size, purpose). Therefore, if a high level of semantic analysis is not required, these models may be sufficient. However, when a richer understanding of spatial cognition is necessary, maintaining a high level of environmental awareness requires leveraging LLMs, which, while significantly enhancing contextual reasoning, also come with computational and resource-intensive requirements12. Our system allows for adaptive levels of semantic analysis, making it more versatile for real-time applications, as confirmed by an average response time of 13.37 seconds across diverse navigation queries.

In our experiments, we relied primarily on data from the simulator because it allowed us to create and test a comprehensive solution, which was an autonomous robot mapping space that can communicate with humans using natural language and, as a result, serve as a navigator or a guide that understands the surrounding space. However, the robotic part is a separate issue and this work is carried out by a separate team, and this article focuses only on the part in which we process the received visual data and the subsequent interaction with humans. The work described here focused on data from the simulator as the first stage of research. Without the participation of the robot, we captured a series of images of our office environment. The results using real photos and the GPT-4o model were very promising, but this will be the subject of further work, also combined with the work of the team dealing with the robot. The next stage will be research carried out in a real environment with a real robot, not just a simulator. The outcomes of this work are also published as part of the Sinotaic.com IoT cluster.

This paper presents our innovative approach, emphasizing its contributions to improving navigation accuracy, user accessibility, and robot-human collaboration in dynamic indoor environments. By integrating computer vision technology with LLMs, we aim to meet growing user demands, offering a novel and promising direction for the development of navigation systems. The remaining of this paper is organized as follows: we first review related work, outline the methodology, and detail the implementation of our dual-modality system. Finally, we present our experimental results and discuss future research directions.

Related work

Existing solutions often integrate machine learning and robotics, aiming to enhance spatial awareness and user interaction. However, the rapid development of technologies, including computer vision and LLMs, has introduced new possibilities and challenges. This section provides a short overview of the advancements in the area, focusing on the evolution of traditional and machine learning-based navigation methods, the growing role of LLMs in robotics, the importance of prompt design, and the integration of multimodal systems to bridge the gap between spatial understanding and human-like interaction13. We highlight existing limitations and establish the context for our proposed approach.

Evolution of indoor navigation systems

Indoor navigation systems have evolved significantly in recent years, leveraging a variety of technologies to improve positioning accuracy and user interaction14. Early solutions often relied on traditional algorithms with handcrafted features, but these approaches struggled with dynamic environments and user-specific needs. Recent advancements have introduced deep learning-based methods, including CNNs and real-time object detection models such as YOLO. These methods have demonstrated substantial improvements in object recognition and spatial mapping, but their integration with high-level semantic understanding remains limited15–18.

Role of large language models

LLMs such as GPT-4, LLaMA, and Galactica have recently shown exceptional capabilities in natural language understanding and multimodal processing19–21. Their ability to combine text and visual inputs enables more intuitive interactions with users, a critical aspect of indoor navigation. For example, ChatGPT and PaLM have demonstrated high performance in open-domain scenarios, while multimodal models like GPT-4 Vision and Lynx extend these capabilities to tasks involving visual context22–24. However, current implementations often lack robust integration with real-time navigation tasks or fail to address the complexities of dynamic indoor environments.

Prompt design

An essential component of leveraging LLMs in indoor navigation is the design of clear and precise prompts. Properly crafted prompts not only enhance the responsiveness of the system but also significantly improve its ability to provide contextually relevant answers25,26. For example, prompts that include spatial references or detailed instructions can guide the robot in understanding complex queries such as “Where is the nearest coffee desk with available seating?” or “What is the shortest accessible route to the elevator?”

Providing the right context within a prompt also supports semantic analysis and enables the system to capture nuanced user intentions, such as urgency or emotional tone27,28. This is particularly valuable in environments like hospitals, where users may require quick and accurate guidance in stressful situations. Moreover, the prompt design ensures consistency and adaptability, optimizing the interaction between users and the robot29, even in dynamically changing spaces.

Our approach incorporates tailored prompt structures that align with the dual-modality system, ensuring seamless integration between natural language understanding and real-time vision-based mapping. By doing so, we address existing challenges in using LLMs for autonomous navigation and establish a framework for efficient and intuitive robot-human collaboration.

Multimodal systems for indoor navigation

Multimodal systems, combining vision-based data with LLMs, present a promising direction for bridging this gap between human intent and robotic action30–32. Recent SLAM advances, such as MVS-SLAM33, and dynamic Object-Aware SLAM34, together with multimodal approaches leveraging semantic maps and LLMs as copilots35, highlight the value of integrating geometry, semantics, and dynamic object awareness for reliable perception. Further, 3D-Mem36, which introduces memory-based 3D scene representations for embodied reasoning, highlights the rapid evolution of this field. These approaches provide key insights for reliable robot perception in changing environments.

Despite these advances, the application of multimodal models in indoor navigation remains an underexplored area. Existing solutions often overlook the dynamic nature of real-world spaces or the need for personalized guidance, particularly for individuals with specific accessibility requirements. Our work builds on these developments by introducing a novel integration of advanced vision-based object recognition with the semantic capabilities of LLMs. By leveraging both recent SLAM advances and LLM-driven language understanding, our system can dynamically adapt to changes in the environment, enabling accurate spatial mapping and context-aware user interactions. This direction aligns with emerging research in embodied vision-and-language navigation, such as Visual Language Maps for Robot Navigation37 and NavGPT38, which highlight how spatial grounding and explicit reasoning can enhance collaboration between humans and robots. Our research contributes to the field by addressing the limitations of existing systems and providing a scalable solution for complex environments.

Methodology and implementation

Our method is divided into two main stages. Stage one focuses on creating a database of objects, rooms, and their attributes, recognized from image data. The second stage involves implementing an interactive user system that leverages this database to provide intelligent and context-aware navigation solutions.

Stage I: data collection and database creation

The process of collecting vision data begins with the robot recording a video of its environment. This step is fundamental for enabling autonomous robots to improve their spatial orientation and develop a deeper understanding of their surroundings. The goal is to create a robust database that supports intelligent and dynamic navigation. In this stage, an autonomous robot equipped with visual sensors maps the spatial layout using on-board camera systems. The video data collected by the robot are transmitted to a centralized processing unit. Specific frames, called keyframes, are extracted from the video sequence to construct an accurate panorama of the interior space. These frames are then input into the dual-modality LLM. The image dataset, combined with carefully designed prompts, is used to construct a dedicated database. This database contains a list of objects recognized by the vision-based system, enriched with their attributes (e.g., size, color, purpose). This ability of the LLM to comprehensively analyze the surrounding space enriches databases by providing detailed information about each recognized object. This sets language models apart from traditional deep learning models dedicated to object recognition tasks. Overall, this process emphasizes the integration of video input, image analysis, and LLM-generated responses, all of which contribute to building a database that helps the autonomous robot navigate and interact with its environment more efficiently. In addition to the solutions tested and described in this article, we used LLM from OpenAI, the GPT-4o model, to create a working demo (which was also presented in the form of a video39). The model revision is dated 2024-08-06. Apart from the prompts, the parameters included an experimentally selected temperature of 0.4, the number of response variants set to 1, and the response format was set to “json_object”.

The schema of the database in our case is shown in Fig. 2. The primary table is named Images and contains metadata about the image itself. It is linked to a table that holds a list of detected objects, which in turn links to a table describing their attributes. The completion_info table gathers only diagnostic data regarding the tokens used, the model, and other related parameters. The database also includes three auxiliary tables (with the suffix _embeddings) that store vector representations of words for optimization purposes - this allows avoiding repeated queries to the model for embeddings if they have already been obtained previously.

Object recognition and embedding

A crucial component of our system is the creation of a structured reference database using ML techniques (Fig. 3). Our system assumes a scenario where, in order to build this database, we use our own multimodal LLM modules with multiple inputs, which integrate object detection with contextualization.

The process of creating a database for object recognition and semantic comparison, and a visualisation simulation environment. Video is processed via key-frame extraction and object-recognition models (LLM, CNN, YOLO); semantic embeddings and results are then stored in a dedicated database.

This approach enables a broader range of capabilities compared to conventional object recognition models, allowing for richer semantic understanding and improved adaptability in dynamic environments. Rather than performing real-time object detection, we structured our evaluation around a reference list of pre-recognized objects, ensuring controlled and reproducible testing conditions. We evaluated the ImageNet40 and COCO41 datasets in terms of their suitability for object recognition tasks. These datasets provide a potential foundation for automated visual analysis in our system.

Instead of using all object classes, our study focuses on a reference list of semantically similar object pairs (Table 1), enabling precise comparisons in vector space. On the other hand, we also created a list with objects without semantic relations. This second list serves as a counterexample, providing a clear contrast and highlighting how the models deal with unrelated objects, thus offering a more comprehensive evaluation of their performance.

The semantic relationships between these objects were analyzed using four embedding methods: Word2Vec42, GloVe43, FastText44, and BERT45. Hierarchical clustering was applied to assess the semantic organization of objects. The accurate semantic clustering of objects allows the system to prioritize relevant objects during navigation, reducing response time and improving user satisfaction. To compute distances between embeddings, the Euclidean distance metric was used, while the connections between the closest clusters were formed using Ward’s linkage method, which minimizes the sum of squared differences within clusters, resulting in cohesive and compact groupings. Stage II: user interaction system

The second stage builds upon the database created in Stage 1 to implement an advanced indoor navigation system. This system processes user commands issued in natural language and provides context-aware responses. The user interaction system employs semantic embedding techniques to link textual commands with the corresponding vision data. For example, a query like “Where is the nearest coffee machine?” triggers a semantic search in the database to locate the requested object and provide its position relative to the user. This interaction is supported by LLMs, which enable the system to understand and interpret complex user queries, even in dynamically changing environments.

The proposed algorithm (Fig. 4) provides a structured process for object recognition, semantic embedding, and context-aware response generation in indoor navigation systems. The process begins with the user submitting a query, which is initially parsed into JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) format for standardized interpretation. The algorithm evaluates whether the query is valid; if the query fails validation, the process terminates, and no further action is performed. For valid queries, relevant objects are extracted from the user query to focus the subsequent analysis on specific entities. For each selected object, an object embedding is generated to represent it in a high-dimensional vector space, and a search is conducted within the object database to find the k most similar objects based on semantic similarity. Objects retrieved during the search are filtered to remove those deemed irrelevant to the original user query. The system then retrieves images containing the identified, relevant objects, which are used to enhance spatial understanding and generate a visual response. From the retrieved images, the algorithm selects the “best” picture according to user query criteria, ensuring the returned result aligns closely with user expectations and task requirements. Finally, the selected image and its spatial coordinates are integrated to generate a comprehensive response, providing the user with a detailed, contextually aware output. The process concludes, effectively improving the system’s human-robot interaction capabilities for indoor navigation tasks.

Results

This section presents the evaluation of the proposed framework. We analyze clustering quality using NMI and cosine similarity to assess semantic relationships among object pairs. The results are discussed in terms of their implications for robot-environment interaction and the overall system performance. Importantly, our system was tested on real-world images to ensure realistic feature extraction. However, the main aim of our experiments was to evaluate the reasoning capacity of the system and the performance of the LLM so it was decided to create a reference list of objects. In addition to semantic clustering, we also assessed overall system performance in a simulated navigation task.

Hierarchical clustering of semantic relations

Hierarchical clustering was performed for selected word embedding methods: Word2Vec, GloVe, FastText, and BERT. This clustering resulted in four dendrograms (Fig. 5), showing the hierarchical structure of the clusters formed by each embedding method. The dendrograms allowed a visual assessment of the extent to which semantic relations from the reference list were mapped. Observations indicated that semantically similar objects are often grouped into coherent clusters. It is worth noting that the differences between embedding methods are evident in the structure of the resulting clusters - some techniques, such as BERT, better represent deep semantic relations compared to traditional methods such as Word2Vec.

Dendrograms for hierarchical clustering based on different word embedding methods (Word2Vec, GloVe, FastText, BERT), illustrating how objects group semantically under each method.

Evaluation of clustering quality using NMI

The results (Table 2) of the qualitative clustering analysis were calculated using the NMI metric, which related to a reference list of words in which some semantic similarity was assumed. In this case, Word2Vec achieved the highest NMI score of 0.8843. This confirms that the embedding method successfully groups semantically related objects. FastText and GloVe also performed well. In contrast, BERT achieved a significantly lower NMI score, indicating limitations in its ability to cluster objects based on their semantic relationships. This suggests that such models, while powerful for natural language processing tasks, may not align as effectively with object-class representations. The overall results indicate the need to choose the right embedding method depending on the specific task.

Navigation task evaluation

To evaluate the system’s effectiveness in supporting natural-language-based navigation, we conducted a structured simulation involving 30 representative queries. The queries were divided into three categories: (1) spatial location of rooms or functional areas (e.g., ‘Where is the kitchen?’), (2) presence and placement of general objects (e.g., ‘Where is the table?’), and (3) objects described by specific attributes (e.g., ‘Where is the orange chair?’). This type of categorisation allowed us to assess the performance of the system at different levels of semantic complexity, from general spatial understanding to precise object attribute recognition. For each query, the system determined the most probable target location and autonomously navigated there. Upon arrival, the robot captured an image from its final viewpoint. An expert evaluator assessed the quality of the response based on this image using a 3-point relevance scale: 0–the queried object or location was not visible; 1-partially visible or ambiguous instance of the object; 2–correct and clearly visible instance. In addition to semantic accuracy, we measured system latency, defined as the total elapsed time from the moment a query was issued to the point at which the corresponding image was acquired. This metric captures both reasoning and navigation performance. Across all evaluated tasks, the system achieved an average response time of 13.37 s, demonstrating its potential for real-time deployment in practical scenarios.

These results (Table 4) indicate that the system shows high performance in terms of semantic understanding, especially when responding to general objects or room-level queries. The table includes the average response time for each query category to allow a more detailed analysis of the system’s performance depending on the complexity of the task. In addition, we calculated a weighted success score to capture the graded relevance of the system’s response. This indicator is defined as a weighted ratio in which answers rated as ’clearly visible’ (score 2) are assigned a weight of 2 and answers rated as ’partially visible’ (score 1) are assigned a weight of 1. Answers with a score of 0 have no impact on the score. The score is given as a fraction of the maximum achievable score, providing a proportion that reflects the effectiveness of the system for all query categories. The overall weighted score across all categories was 76.7, confirming the system’s robustness in natural-language-guided semantic navigation tasks.

Conclusion and discussions

The proposed workflow, combining vision-based object detection, semantic embedding, and natural language processing, ensures a seamless and dynamic integration of spatial understanding with user-centric interaction. The detailed study shows that, regardless of the method used to recognize objects at the robot space analysis stage, the database thus created is sufficient to support the next stage of the system. This stage is the improvement of robot-human interaction using LLM. Nevertheless, the main advantage of our proposed approach lies in the LLM’s ability to combine visual analysis with contextual semantic understanding, such as identifying specifically a ‘red pea cup’ against other objects. In our system, LLM automatically detects objects and attributes meaning to them in a manner similar to human perception. This ability is further enhanced by leveraging multimodal models to provide responses tailored to specific user queries in real time, addressing not only object recognition but also spatial relationships and contextual needs. As a result, our system significantly enhances the robot’s ability to navigate complex environments while meeting the high expectations of intuitive and meaningful interaction with users. Across all 30 navigation queries, the system achieved a Query Completion Success Rate of 76.7%, defined as the average of weighted success scores across all query categories, where each response is evaluated based on its semantic relevance using a graded relevance scale. This shows that the system correctly interpreted natural language instructions, navigated to the target location, and identified the object unambiguously in line with the query intent. Additionally, the system demonstrated strong performance in recognising object attributes—such as colour, size or shape, confirming its ability for deep semantic and contextual analysis. The performance gap between general object queries success and attribute-specific queries suggests a challenge in multimodal grounding. This indicates that future work should focus not only on the language model’s reasoning but also on improving the robustness of the underlying visual feature extraction.

The article demonstrates how our system can transform traditional, functionally limited indoor navigation systems into intelligent, context-aware tools. Future research will focus on further developing semantic understanding algorithms and testing the system in a variety of environments, which could open up new possibilities for more personalized and interactive indoor navigation. We plan to extend our indoor navigation system with voice interaction functionality so that users can interact with the system more naturally and directly. The work will focus on implementing speech recognition and natural language processing technology, allowing users to ask questions and receive answers via voice commands46. The addition of voice interaction will increase the system’s usability, particularly for individuals with mobility limitations, and improve the overall user experience. A limitation of the current approach is its reliance on a pre-generated semantic database. The system’s performance may degrade in highly dynamic environments where objects are frequently moved or replaced, as it currently lacks a mechanism for real-time map updates.

Additionally, we aim to improve the scalability of the system by optimizing its workflow for real-time applications in larger, more dynamic environments, such as hospitals or shopping centers while maintaining low response latency (averaging 13.37 s in our tests, for timely and effective user interaction). A focus will be also on enhancing the spatial mapping subsystem to integrate more detailed topological information obtained during the mapping process. By incorporating structural data, such as the layout and positioning of walls, doorways, and other architectural elements, the system will achieve more accurate and context-aware mapping of objects within the environment.
