The generalized Chebotarev problem in higher genus
M.

Abstract




We consider the extension to higher genus Riemann surfaces of the classical Chebotarev problem, with a view towards the development of the theory of Padé approximants on algebraic curves.
To this end we define an appropriate notion of capacity that mimics the standard one, following works of Chirka and of the author and collaborators.
The nontrivial topology of the Riemann surface requires further specification of the “homotopy class” of the continua in the solution of the Chebotarev problem. We also discuss the relationship of this problem to the theory of Jenkins-Strebel quadratic differentials.

Keywords:

Minimal Capacity,
S
-property, Green function, quadratic differentials, harmonic functions

Introduction

The theory of Padé approximation is a useful and beautiful branch of approximation theory, which touches upon several distant areas such as number theory, random matrices, integrable systems, to mention a few.
By its nature the classical theory is formulated as an approximation tool for functions on the complex plane (or subsets thereof), namely on the simplest of Riemann surfaces, the Riemann sphere
\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

In recent years we have started a program aimed at generalizing the theory and potentially its applications to the case where the Riemann sphere is replaced by a more general Riemann surface
{\mathcal{C}}
of arbitrary genus.
In
[
3
]
we introduced the notion of Padé approximants for an appropriate generalization of Stieltjes-Markov functions of measures on paths embedded in
{\mathcal{C}}
, showing how the denominators, meromorphic functions with prescribed poles, satisfy an orthogonality that mimics the relationship between orthogonal polynomials and measures in the complex plane. In particular it was shown that the denominators and suitable Cauchy transforms thereof can be arranged in a
2\times 2
matrix that satisfies a Riemann-Hilber problem, much like ordinary orthogonal polynomials do
[
12
]
. This allowed
[
5
]
to analyze their behaviour for large degree using the nonlinear steepest descent method much like
[
12
,
9
]
. Another application to matrix orthogonal polynomials was developed in
[
4
]
.

This paper is then born out of a necessity that arises in the theory of nonlinear steepest descent method, or Deift-Zhou method
[
10
]
, when generalized to Riemann-Hilbert problems on higher genus Riemann surfaces. Namely, that of constructing an appropriate equilibrium measure, which is then used to start the Deift-Zhou method for analyzing the large degree asymptotics of orthogonal rational functions.

It is well known
[
31
,
32
,
33
]
that the zeros of denominators of the diagonal Padé approximants for a germ of analytic function
F(z)
near
z=\infty
accumulate on a set
\mathfrak{F}
with the property that

•

the function
F
admits analytic extension to
\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus\mathfrak{F}
;

•

the set
\mathfrak{F}
is the unique set of minimal logarithmic capacity amongst all sets satisfying the previous condition.

The typical situation is that of a function
F
admitting unrestricted analytic continuation to the universal cover of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
minus a finite set,
E
, of branch-points. The reader can think of an algebraic function
F
for example. In this context the function
F
does not play a particularly prominent role; just the set of branchpoints
E
and the overall topology of the maximal set of analytic continuation do.

If, for example,
F
can only be extended to a simply connected domain containing
\infty
, then
\mathfrak{F}
must be a continuum (connected compact set) containing the branch-points
E
. Then this can be reformulated as the classical Chebotarev problem.

Problem 1.1
(Pólya in a letter to Chebotarev
[
26
]
, solved by Grotzsch
[
15
]
and Lavrentieff
[
22
]
)

For a given finite set
E=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{N}\}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
, find a continuum
\mathfrak{F}
containing
E
and of minimal logarithmic capacity amongst all such sets.

We will call
E
the
anchor set
and
e\in E
an
anchor
.
It may well be that
F
can be extended analytically to a set that is not necessarily simply connected. The “connectivity” of such a set then plays an important role. For example, the function

F(z)=\sqrt[4]{\frac{z-2}{z-1}}+\sqrt[3]{\frac{z+i}{z-i}}

clearly has four branchpoints
E=\{1,2,i,-i\}
but can be extended to any set where the branchcuts extends from
1
to
2
and from
i
to
-i
: in other words it is not necessary for the domain of analyticity to be simply connected. Thus the set of minimal capacity
\mathfrak{F}
in Stahl’s theory needs not be a continuum containing the anchors. It must however be a
poly-continuum
(finite union of continua) where
1,2
belong to the same connected component and so do
i,-i
, but not necessarily the same component for the two pairs. This leads to a
generalized
Chebotarev problem, where the class over which we minimize the capacity is that of poly-continua with the required “connectivity pattern”, see also
[
23
]
. A numerical approach to generalized Chebotarev poly-continua in the plane can be found in
[
2
]
.

Summary of results.

We can easily imagine the generalization of this problem to an arbitrary Riemann surface
{\mathcal{C}}
of genus
g
, and indeed this is the focus of this paper.

Suppose now
E\subset{\mathcal{C}}
is a finite set of anchors on
{\mathcal{C}}
and we are seeking, similarly to the previously described situation, a poly-continuum
\mathcal{K}
of minimal capacity that preserves an appropriate “connectivity pattern”,
\mathscr{P}
:
the precise meaning is given in Section
2
, see Definition
2.2
. Briefly, it is a prescription of the homotopy classes (in the relative homotopy
\pi_{1}({\mathcal{C}},E)
) that the poly-continuum defines by connecting the anchors together.

The definition of capacity is relatively simple and we follow ideas in
[
8
]
and
[
6
]
. Like the ordinary logarithmic capacity in the complex plane, the notion requires to specify a reference point relative to which the capacity is computed. In the standard case this is usually the point at infinity and in our setting it is just a point on
{\mathcal{C}}
which we denote by
\infty
as a reference to the standard case.

Let us fix a point
\infty\in{\mathcal{C}}
and consider a compact set
\mathcal{K}\subset{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\{\infty\}
which is regular for the Dirichlet problem. Let
G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)
denote the standard Green’s function of the complement of
\mathcal{K}
with logarithmic pole at the point
\infty
. Let us choose a local coordinate
z_{\infty}
near
\infty
and define the capacity as

\ln{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}(\mathcal{K}):=\lim_{p\to\infty}\left(\ln\frac{1}{|z_{\infty}(p)|}-G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)\right).

(1.1)

It has the property that if
w_{\infty}
is a different local coordinate then

{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}(\mathcal{K})={\rm Cap}_{w_{\infty}}(\mathcal{K})\left|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}w_{\infty}}{{\mathrm{d}}z_{\infty}}\right|_{p=\infty}.

(1.2)

The classical Frostman theorem relates the capacity of a set to a probability measure supported on its boundary, and Chirka
[
8
]
showed how to extend this result to Riemann surfaces. His very general results can be specialized also to the above notion of capacity. See Proposition
3.4
.

In Section
4
we get to the first result. We first review Chirka’s results that prove that the capacity
{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}
is continuous with respect to the Hausdorff topology on the class of poly-continua we are interested in, summarizing Chirka’s result in Lemma
4.1
. This continuity allows us to prove Theorem
4.3
which we rephrase below.

Theorem
For a given set of anchors
E
and any connectivity pattern, there exists a unique poly-continuum that realizes the minimum of the capacity within this class.

See Theorem
4.3
for more precise formulation. The existence is a simple consequence of the continuity of the capacity. The uniqueness is a slightly more delicate matter which leads us to the next section.

In Section
5
we show how sets of minimal capacity are related to trajectories of quadratic differentials. The core ideas here are not new and have been used in
[
25
,
14
,
23
]
. One of the main features which we can state here in simple terms is the
S
-property introduced by Gonchar-Rakhmanov and Stahl
[
14
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
.
In short, the minimal capacity sets
\mathcal{K}
are finite unions of analytic arcs, possibly meeting transversally at the endpoints, and the Green’s function
G_{\mathcal{K}}(p)=G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)
has the
S
-property

\frac{\partial G_{\mathcal{K}}(p)}{\partial{\bf n}_{+}}=\frac{\partial G_{\mathcal{K}}(p)}{\partial{\bf n}_{-}},

(1.3)

where
{\bf n}_{\pm}
denote the two opposite normals at a smooth point of
\mathcal{K}
.
Furthermore, using the tool of Schiffer variations
[
30
]
as in
[
28
,
23
,
21
,
6
]
we show that the expression
\mathfrak{Q}=(2\partial_{p}G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty))^{2}
(with
\partial_{p}
the Wirthinger operator (
3.5
)) extends to a meromorphic quadratic differential on
{\mathcal{C}}
such that:

•

\mathfrak{Q}
has a double pole at
\infty
with bi-residue
1
: the bi-residue of a quadratic differential at a double pole is the coefficient of the term
w^{-2}
in any local coordinate. Thus in any local coordinate
w
near
\infty
the differential has the expression

\mathfrak{Q}(p)=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}w^{2}}{w^{2}}(1+\mathcal{O}(w)).

(1.4)

•

\mathfrak{Q}
has at most simple poles at the anchor set
E
.

•

all contour integrals of
\sqrt{\mathfrak{Q}}
on the Riemann surface
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
defined by
v^{2}=\mathfrak{Q}
(the “canonical double cover”) are purely imaginary. This is the so–called
Boutroux condition
, (
5.22
).

See Theorem
5.3
, Corollary
5.5
.
This result borrows heavily on ideas from
[
6
]
.

We have also a converse to the above; namely (Theorem
5.6
) we show that any quadratic differential
\mathfrak{Q}
satisfying the same conditions in the above bullet-points, defines a set of minimal capacity obtained by collecting the graph of some of its
critical vertical trajectories
(for the terminology and background on quadratic differentials we refer to the monographs
[
34
,
18
]
).

In Section
6
we prove a result of a slightly different flavour. Namely we establish a criterion by which the capacity of two sets can be compared to each other. It is immediate to see from the definition and Frostman’s theorem (generalized to higher genus) that the capacity is monotonic with respect to inclusion. The comparison criterion we prove is a generalization of a result of Jenkins’
[
19
]
, where he proves that sets with the
S
-property
2
2

2

Jenkins in loc. cit. characterizes the sets in a different way but in the modern terminology his characterization is equivalent to the
S
-property.
are of minimal capacity in an appropriate class. The generalization consists in extending the result to sets without the
S
-property (we still assume that their boundaries are sufficiently smooth, however): here we will be using the ideas of
[
7
]
.
This leads to the interesting notion of
Jenkins Interception Property
which we outline here.
Let
\mathfrak{F}
be a poly-continuum with piecewise smooth boundary. Its Green’s function
G_{\mathfrak{F}}(p;\infty)
defines a measure on the foliation of its gradient lines.
The density of this measure can be expressed as a measure on the outer boundary of
\mathfrak{F}
with density given by the normal derivative of the Green’s function itself. If, from a smooth point
p
on the outer boundary, there are two emerging trajectories (from either sides) we call
dominant
the trajectory on the side where the above density is larger, the other being the
recessive
trajectory. If the density is equal on both sides, as is the case if
\mathfrak{F}
has the
S
–property, we say that both trajectories are dominant. If there is only one trajectory from
p
we still call it dominant. A second set
\mathcal{K}
has the Jenkins Interception Property (JIP) relative to
\mathfrak{F}
if it intersects all but finitely many of the dominant trajectories. Then the result is Theorem
6.3
, stating that

if the poly-continuum
\mathcal{K}
satisfies JIP relative to
\mathfrak{F}
then its capacity is larger.
Furthermore, under this assumption, the two capacities are equal iff
\mathcal{K}=\mathfrak{F}
.

This result perfectly illustrates the importance of the
S
-property, when all trajectories are dominant. Indeed, intuitively it clarifies that any deformation of a set with the
S
-property (at fixed anchors) has larger capacity because it intersects all the trajectories either on “one or the other side”.

In Corollary
6.4
and Proposition
6.5
we use this result to show the uniqueness of the minimizer in a given connectivity class.

We comment in Section
7
on the relationship between the Chebotarev problem and the theory of Jenkins-Strebel differentials, which are used in the combinatorial model of the moduli spaces of Riemann surfaces.

We conclude in Section
8
outlining in slightly greater detail how the present results fit into a future plan of study of large degree asymptotic behaviour of Padé approximation on a Riemann surface.

Setup: connectivity patterns

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a compact smooth Riemann surface of genus
g
with complex structure.
We will also need a complete metric,
\sigma
, in the same conformal class; any metric will do, e.g. the unique metric of constant negative curvature
-1
. When referring to the distance between points or sets, we will understand it in this metric and we denote the distance function by the symbol
d_{\sigma}:{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathcal{C}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Corresponding to this metric we have a Hausdorff metric, which we denote by
d_{H}
, on the class of compact subsets of
{\mathcal{C}}
. This is also a complete metric.

Let
E=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{N}\}\subset{\mathcal{C}}
be a finite nonempty subset which we call
anchor set
(with the elements being
anchors
).
Let
\pi_{1}({\mathcal{C}},E)
be the relative homotopy group; thus the elements of this group are homotopy classes of paths with endpoints at one or more anchors.
By
poly-continuum
we understand a finite union of
continua
(compact connected sets).

Decorated adjacency matrices.

In broad terms, we need to classify the poly-continua containing the anchor set
E
according to their “connectivity pattern”, which we now proceed to formalize.

Let
\gamma\in\pi_{1}({\mathcal{C}},E)
; we say that a poly-continuum
\mathcal{K}

contains the class
\gamma
if any
\epsilon
-fattening of
\mathcal{K}
contains a representative path in the class
\gamma
. It should be clear that the property ”continua containing the pre-assigned class
\gamma
” is Hausdorff continuous, namely, if a Hausdorff convergent sequence
(\mathcal{K}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
has the property that all
\mathcal{K}_{n}
’s contain
\gamma
, then so does the limiting continuum
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
.

Given any poly-continuum
\mathcal{K}
containing the anchor set
E
, we can associate a
connectivity pattern
that records which points of
E
belong to the same connected component of
\mathcal{K}
, as was done in
[
7
]
. In view of the nontrivial topology of
{\mathcal{C}}
, however, we want to retain a more granular information of how two points of
E
are connected by
\mathcal{K}
. For this reason we introduce the
decorated adjacency matrix
below.

Definition 2.1

Given a poly-continuum
\mathcal{K}
containing the anchor set
E
, the decorated adjacency matrix
M=M(\mathcal{K})
is a square matrix of size
|E|\times|E|
, indexed by elements of
E
. In the entry
M_{e,\widetilde{e}}
we place the set of all classes of paths
\gamma\in\pi_{1}({\mathcal{C}},E)
connecting
e
to
\widetilde{e}
and
\widetilde{e}
to
e
, and contained in
\mathcal{K}
.

\blacktriangle

We also define a partial order amongst these decorated adjacency matrices as follows; we say that
M(\widetilde{K})\prec M(\mathcal{K})
if the set in each entry of
M(\mathcal{K})
contains the set in the corresponding entry of
M(\widetilde{K})
. In words we will say that
the connectivity of
\mathcal{K}
exceeds that of
\widetilde{K}
.

Finally we want to define a class of poly-continua that all exceed a given connectivity pattern
\mathscr{P}
.

For each pair
(e,\widetilde{e})\in E\times E
we choose either the empty set or a pair
\{\gamma,\gamma^{-1}\}
with
\gamma
a choice of a unique class
\gamma_{e,\widetilde{e}}\in\pi_{1}({\mathcal{C}},E)
.
On the diagonal
(e,e)
there is no constraint and hence the choices are either the empty set, the singleton of the identity or a loop based at
e
together with its inverse.
We construct then a matrix
M^{\mathscr{P}}
containing in each entry the corresponding choice.

For our application later on we insist that every anchor
e\in E
should be the endpoint of a nontrivial class:

\forall e\in E\ \exists\ \widetilde{e}:\ M^{\mathscr{P}}_{e,\widetilde{e}}\neq\emptyset,{\rm Id}.

(2.1)

We briefly comment on this condition: its motivation is that we are going to consider minimal capacity poly-continua
\mathcal{K}
such that
M^{\mathscr{P}}\!\!\prec M(\mathcal{K})
, and if an element
e
does not belong to a nontrivial class in the pattern
\mathscr{P}
, then this point would be “ignored” by the minimal capacity set (isolated points have zero capacity), and we may as well have removed
e
altogether from consideration.

We thus come to the formal definition of our class of poly-continua:

Definition 2.2

Let
\mathscr{P}
be an admissible pattern as per condition (
2.1
).
Let
\mathbb{K}_{\mathscr{P}}
be the class of all poly-continua
\mathcal{K}
such that:

1.

the connectivity pattern of
\mathcal{K}
exceeds
\mathscr{P}
, namely,
M^{\mathscr{P}}\prec M(\mathcal{K})
;

2.

the number of connected components of
\mathcal{K}
does not exceed the number of homotopy classes in
\mathscr{P}
modulo inversion (hence at most
|E|(|E|+1)/2
).
\blacktriangle

Again, a comment is in order here. While the first condition in Definition
2.2
should be self-explanatory, the second condition is a minimality of sorts. Namely, we insist that all components of
\mathcal{K}
are “actively participating” in connecting anchors. This is convenient because in the minimization process of the capacity, we want to avoid the occurrence of components of small capacity.

The class
\mathbb{K}_{\mathscr{P}}
is closed in Hausdorff topology since we have already commented that the condition of containing a class is a Hausdorff continuous property and so is the uniform bound on the number of connected components.

Problem 2.3

For a given anchor set
E
and admissible connectivity pattern
\mathscr{P}
we are seeking the set of minimal capacity in
\mathbb{K}_{\mathscr{P}}
.

For Problem
2.3
to make sense we need do carefully define the notion of capacity on a Riemann surface, and we take most of what follows from
[
8
]
.

3
Capacity on a Riemann surface

As in the standard case where
{\mathcal{C}}
is the Riemann sphere, the capacity of a set is defined relative to a chosen point in its complement. In the case of the Riemann sphere this is usually taken as the point at infinity, but for a Riemann surface
{\mathcal{C}}
there are no distinguished points. We thus choose a point on
{\mathcal{C}}
which we denote
\infty
, where the notation is simply a nod to the standard case.

In all considerations in this paper we will assume
\mathcal{K}
to be a poly-continuum with finitely many connected components. It is easy to see that such a set satisfies the Wiener criterion for the well–posedness of the Dirichled problem (see
[
29
]
, App A.2, Theorem 2.1).

Definition 3.1

Given a proper poly-continuum
\mathcal{K}\subset{\mathcal{C}}
with
\infty\not\in\mathcal{K}
, the polar Green’s function of
\mathcal{K}
(with pole at
\infty
) is the unique function
G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)
such that

1.

G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)
is harmonic in the variable
p\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{\infty\}
;

2.

G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)=0
for all
p\in\mathcal{K}
;

3.

G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)-\ln\frac{1}{|z_{\infty}(p)|}
is harmonic and bounded in a full neighbourhood of
\infty
, where
z_{\infty}
is any local coordinate near
\infty
.
\blacktriangle

By the minimum principle
G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)
is a strictly positive function on
{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\mathcal{K}\cup\{\infty\}
.

Observe that
G_{\mathcal{K}}
is non-trivial only in the connected component,
\Omega
, of
{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\mathcal{K}
that contains
p=\infty
. We call this the
exterior region
and we call
\partial\Omega
the
outer boundary
of
\mathcal{K}
.
Different compact sets with the same
\Omega
will have the same Green’s function, namely, the Dirichlet problem is “blind” towards holes of
\mathcal{K}
. We will say that
\mathcal{K}
is
polynomially convex
if
\mathcal{K}={\mathcal{C}}\setminus\Omega
, although the adverb “polynomially” is here a bit out of place, since there are no polynomials on
{\mathcal{C}}
. To simplify the discussion we make the running assumption that

all poly-continua we consider will be polynomially convex.

(3.1)

3.1
Bipolar Green’s function

We need a substitute for the Green’s kernel
\ln|z-w|
on the Riemann sphere. Following
[
6
]
we introduce the notion below.
Let
G_{\infty}(p,q)
be the unique (up to additive constant)
bipolar Green function
with logarithmic pole at
\infty
. Namely

1.

G_{\infty}(p,q)
is harmonic w.r.t.
p
in
{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\{\infty,q\}
;

2.

in any local coordinate, for
p
near
q

G_{\infty}(p,q)=\ln{|z(p)-z(q)|}+\mathcal{O}(1).

(3.2)

Equivalently,
G_{\infty}(p,q)-\ln d_{\sigma}(p,q)
is bounded along the diagonal
p=q\neq\infty
.

3.

in a local coordinate near
\infty
,
z_{\infty}(\infty)=0
, we have

G_{\infty}(p,q)=\ln\frac{1}{|z_{\infty}(p)|}+\mathcal{O}(1).

(3.3)

Equivalently,
G_{\infty}(p,q)+\ln d_{\sigma}(p,\infty)
is bounded for
p
in a neighbourhood of
\infty
.

The bipolar Green’s function can be constructed as follows; let
\Omega^{i{\mathbb{R}}}_{\infty,q}
be the unique differential of the third kind with purely imaginary periods and with simple poles at
p=q,\infty
and residues
1,-1
, respectively
[
11
]
. Then

G_{\infty}(p,q)=\mathrm{Re}\,\left(\int^{p}\Omega^{i{\mathbb{R}}}_{\infty,q}(\zeta)\right).

(3.4)

up to additive constant.
Denote with
\partial_{p}
the Wirtinger operator that takes any local harmonic function
F(p)
and returns a local holomorphic differential given by

\partial_{p}F(p):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}-i\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}\right){\mathrm{d}}z,\ \ z=x+iy,

(3.5)

in a local coordinate
z=z(p)
. Then we have the converse of (
3.4
)

\Omega^{i{\mathbb{R}}}_{\infty,q}(p)=2\partial_{p}G_{\infty}(p,q).

(3.6)

It can also be shown either directly or by a consequence of the Riemann bilinear identities that
G_{\infty}(p,q)=G_{\infty}(q,p)
.
The arbitrariness of additive constant can be disposed of in several ways. Possibly the most expedient is to simply give an explicit formula for
G_{\infty}(p,q)
in terms of Riemann Theta functions:

G_{\infty}(p,q)=\ln\left|\frac{\Theta_{\Delta}\left(\int_{q}^{p}\boldsymbol{\omega}\right)}{\Theta_{\Delta}\left(\int_{\infty}^{p}\boldsymbol{\omega}\right)\Theta_{\Delta}\left(\int^{\infty}_{q}\boldsymbol{\omega}\right)}\right|-2\pi\mathrm{Im}\,\left(\int_{\infty}^{p}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{t}\right)\cdot(\mathrm{Im}\,{\boldsymbol{\tau}})^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{Im}\,\left(\int_{\infty}^{q}\boldsymbol{\omega}\right)

(3.7)

where
\boldsymbol{\omega}=[\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{g}]
is the vector of normalized holomorphic differentials and
\Theta_{\Delta}
denotes the Riemann theta function with an odd-nonsingular characteristic, see
[
11
]
, Ch. I and also Appendix
A
here.

Example 3.2

If
{\mathcal{C}}
is an elliptic curve (genus one) represented by
{\mathbb{C}}/{\mathbb{Z}}+\tau{\mathbb{Z}}
with
\mathrm{Im}\,\tau>0
and with the choice of
\infty
as
p=0
, then (see also
[
6
]
Example 2.3)

G_{\infty}(p,q)=\ln\left|\frac{\theta_{1}\left(p-q\right)}{\theta_{1}\left(q\right)\theta_{1}\left(p\right)}\right|-2\pi\frac{\mathrm{Im}\,\left(p\right)\mathrm{Im}\,\left(q\right)}{\mathrm{Im}\,{\tau}},

(3.8)

where
\theta_{1}
is the standard Jacobi elliptic theta function.

Alternatively one can choose the additive constant subordinate to the choice of a local coordinate
z_{\infty}
and so that
G_{\infty}(p,q)=\ln\frac{1}{|z_{\infty}(p)|}+\mathcal{O}(|z_{\infty}(p)|)
(namely, so that the constant term vanishes in the coordinate
z_{\infty}
).

3.2
Capacity of a set
\mathcal{K}

Recall the running assumption that
\mathcal{K}
is a poly-continuum with finitely many components.

Definition 3.3

Given the Green’s function
G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)
and a fixed local coordinate
z_{\infty}
we define the capacity as

\ln{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}(\mathcal{K}):=\lim_{p\to\infty}\left(\ln\frac{1}{|z_{\infty}(p)|}-G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)\right).

(3.9)

Alternatively and equivalently, in the local coordinate
z_{\infty}
the Green’s function has the expansion

G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)=\ln\frac{1}{|z_{\infty}(p)|}-\ln{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}(\mathcal{K})+\mathcal{O}(|z_{\infty}(p)|).

(3.10)

\blacktriangle

One could tie the choice of coordinate to the metric by choosing the local coordinate adapted to the metric in the sense that
\lim_{p\to\infty}\frac{|z_{\infty}(p)|}{d_{\sigma}(p,\infty)}=1
, but there is no discernible convenience in doing so.

It is useful to give several equivalent representations of this capacity and relate it to a bipolar Green’s function.

For the case
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{P}^{1}
we have the classical Frostman’s theorem
[
13
]
relating the Green’s function of the complement of a compact set to a probability measure on its boundary.
In particular, such a measure is also the unique minimizer of the logarithmic energy of probability measures supported on the compact set.

This has been generalized in
[
8
]
to an arbitrary Riemann surface with even greater generality than needed in our paper now.
We briefly review the key points, with the intention of simplifying the reader’s navigation.

In our setting, it follows that there is a probability measure
{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\mathcal{K}}
supported on the outer boundary of
\mathcal{K}

G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)=\mathcal{U}_{{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{{}_{\mathcal{K}}}}(p)-\ln{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}(\mathcal{K}),\qquad\mathcal{U}_{{\mathrm{d}}\mu}(p):=\int G_{\infty}(p,q){\mathrm{d}}\mu(q)

(3.11)

where the bipolar Green’s function is normalized so that
G_{\infty}(p,q)=\ln\frac{1}{|z_{\infty}(p)|}+\mathcal{O}(|z_{\infty}(p)|)
and the constant
{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}(\mathcal{K})
is precisely the previously defined capacity subordinated to the same choice of local coordinate.
Integrating (
3.11
) against
{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\mathcal{K}}
and recalling that by definition
G_{\mathcal{K}}
vanishes on
\mathcal{K}
, we obtain the expression of
{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}(\mathcal{K})
as follows

\ln{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}(\mathcal{K})=\iint G_{\infty}(p,q){\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\mathcal{K}}(p){\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\mathcal{K}}(q).

(3.12)

The analog of Frostman’s theorem, following
[
8
]
and adapting to our situation here, is summarized in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.4

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{K}}^{1}
denote the set of probability measures supported on
\mathcal{K}
. Then

\displaystyle{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}(\mathcal{K})

\displaystyle={\rm exp}\left(-\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{K})\right),\ \ \ \mathcal{I}(\mathcal{K}):=\min_{{\mathrm{d}}\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{K}}^{1}}\mathcal{E}[{\mathrm{d}}\mu]

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[{\mathrm{d}}\mu]:=-\int_{\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{K}}G_{\infty}(p,q){\mathrm{d}}\mu(p){\mathrm{d}}\mu(q)

(3.13)

The minimum of
\mathcal{E}
is achieved at a uniquely defined measure
{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\mathcal{K}}
, supported on its outer boundary. The Green’s function is related to the potential of the measure by (
3.11
), i.e., they differ by a constant equal to the logarithm of the capacity.

In the classical case, as mentioned, this is essentially Frostman’s theorem; in the case of Riemann surfaces instead, with reference to
[
8
]
and notation therein:

1.

The existence and uniqueness of
{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\mathcal{K}}
can be found in Section 2.
In order to adapt loc.cit. to our case, we should take
\phi=\delta_{\infty}
. See Lemmas 1,2 in section 2.3 in particular.

2.

The fact that the measure is supported on the outer boundary of
\mathcal{K}
(in our terminology) and that the potential is equal to the Green’s function (up to constant) follows then from Corollary 3, pag 319.

The quantity
\mathcal{E}[{\mathrm{d}}\mu]
can be termed the (electrostatic)
energy
of a probability measure, in analogy with the case of the Riemann sphere where the bipolar Green’s function would be
\ln{|z-w|}
.

3.3
The conformal map
W_{\mathcal{K}}
.

Let
g_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty):=G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)+iH_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)
, where
H_{\mathcal{K}}
is the harmonic conjugate function so that
g_{\mathcal{K}}
is analytic and defined on the universal cover of
{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\mathcal{K}\cup\{\infty\}
. It has a logarithmic branch point at
\infty
and with additive monodromy
2i\pi
, behaving like
\ln{\zeta_{\infty}(p)}
, where
\zeta_{\infty}(\infty)=0
is a local parameter.
Since
\mathrm{Re}\,g_{\mathcal{K}}=G_{\mathcal{K}}
is zero on
\mathcal{K}
the function

W(p)=W_{\mathcal{K}}(p):={\rm e}^{-g_{{}_{\mathcal{K}}}(p)}

(3.14)

maps the universal cover of
{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\mathcal{K}
onto the unit disk, with
\infty
being mapped to the origin in the
W
–plane and the boundary of
\mathcal{K}
onto the unit circle. In terms of a local coordinate
z_{\infty}
near
\infty
we have

W(p)={z_{\infty}(p)}{{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}(\mathcal{K})}\Big(1+\mathcal{O}(z_{\infty})\Big)={z_{\infty}(p)}{\rm e}^{-\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{K})}\Big(1+\mathcal{O}(z_{\infty})\Big).

(3.15)

Remark 3.5

When
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{P}^{1}
(the Riemann sphere), the bipolar Green’s function is simply
\ln{|z-w|}
and the subtleties related to the choices of coordinate near
\infty
are lost. When referring to the logarithmic energy, it is universally implied that the natural choice of coordinate
z
has been made near
\infty
.

\blacktriangle

Consider the map
W_{\mathcal{K}}
defined in (
3.14
). In general it is not one-to-one unless we perform some additional dissection that we now describe.
The Green’s function
G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)
must have a certain number
k\geq 0
of critical points (counted with multiplicity); these are the zeros of the meromorphic differential
\partial_{p}G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)
, where
\partial_{p}
is the Wirtinger operator (
3.5
). This differential is meromorphic on
{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\mathcal{K}
with a unique simple pole at
\infty
and residue
-\frac{1}{2}
. The number of these zeros (counted with multiplicity) depends on the Euler characteristic of
{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\mathcal{K}\cup\{\infty\}
thanks to elementary Morse theory.
Namely, the degree of the zero divisor of
\partial_{p}G_{\mathcal{K}}
on
{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\mathcal{K}
is

k=-\mathcal{X}({\mathcal{C}}\setminus\mathcal{K}\cup\{\infty\}).

(3.16)

Example 3.6

If
\mathcal{K}
is a single contractible component then
k=2g
.

The map
W=W_{\mathcal{K}}
determines the flat metric by the formula
|2\partial_{p}G_{\mathcal{K}}|=\left|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}W}{W}\right|
on
{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\mathcal{K}\cup\{\infty\}
, with conical singularities at the
k
critical points
\mathscr{B}=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{k}\}
.

In this metric both the level-sets and the gradient lines of
G_{\mathcal{K}}
provide two local foliations in mutually orthogonal geodesics. The dissection,
\Sigma
, is then constructed as follows;
from each critical point
b\in\mathscr{B}
there are
2\mu_{b}
steepest descent gradient lines of
G_{\mathcal{K}}
, where
\mu_{b}
is the multiplicity of the zero of
\partial_{p}G_{\mathcal{K}}
at
b
. Each of these gradient lines ends either on another critical points, or on the boundary of
\mathcal{K}
.

We define
\Sigma
as the union of all these steepest descent trajectories from all critical points in
\mathscr{B}
.

Lemma 3.7

The set
{\mathcal{C}}\setminus(\Sigma\cup\mathcal{K})
is simply connected.
The map
W_{\mathcal{K}}
maps
{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\Sigma\cup\mathcal{K}
conformally and bijectively to the unit disk with
2k
radial slits.

Proof.

The proposed set is geodesically star-like with respect to the flat metric
|{\mathrm{d}}W|/|W|
, with the point
\infty
at the center of the star; for each
p_{0}\not\in\Sigma\cup\mathcal{K}
we take the maximally extended steepest ascent gradient line,
\gamma
, of
G_{\mathcal{K}}
. Any steepest ascent trajectory of
G_{\mathcal{K}}
can end only at
\infty
or at a critical point, but since we have precisely removed all the steepest ascent geodesics ending at critical points, the geodesic
\gamma
above can only end at
\infty
, and hence it is mapped to a radial segment in the
W
–plane, ending at the origin. This proves the simple connectedness.

The second assertion now follows from the fact that
W_{K}
restricted to
{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\Sigma\cup\mathcal{K}
is an analytic map on a simply connected domain without any critical points, hence bijective.

\blacksquare

We point out that if
{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\mathcal{K}
is already simply connected, then
W_{\mathcal{K}}
will be the corresponding uniformizing map mapping
\infty
to
W_{\mathcal{K}}=0
.

4
Generalized Chebotarev problem in higher genus

In this section we prove the first main result of the paper, Theorem
4.3
.
We consider an admissible connectivity pattern
\mathscr{P}
and the corresponding class
\mathbb{K}_{\mathscr{P}}
as in Definition
2.2
.
We fix a local coordinate
z_{\infty}
and consider then the corresponding capacity as a functional

{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}:\mathbb{K}_{\mathscr{P}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

(4.1)

We will show that there exists a minimizer of the capacity within this class. We then investigate the properties of such minimal sets in the subsequent sections, including the uniqueness of the minimizer.

This problem can be considered as a generalized Chebotarev problem in higher genus. Let us recall that the classical Chebotarev problem consists in finding a continuum
\mathcal{K}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
containing a finite set
E
(our “anchors”) and minimizing the usual logarithmic capacity
[
26
]
. By
generalized
Chebotarev problem (in genus zero) we mean that we allow poly–continua exceeding a given connectivity pattern, everything else being equal, see also
[
23
]
.

When formulating the problem in higher genus we must first define the relevant notion of capacity, which we have done in the previous section. In addition we need to contend with the nontrivial topology of
{\mathcal{C}}
, which is the reason behind the rather convoluted description of connectivity patterns.

We need two preliminary lemmas.
For any
\delta>0
(sufficiently small) let

\mathbb{K}_{\mathscr{P}}^{(\delta)}:=\{\mathcal{K}\in\mathbb{K}_{\mathscr{P}}:\ \ \ d_{\sigma}(\mathcal{K},\infty)\geq\delta\},

(4.2)

namely, those poly-continua that exceed the connectivity pattern
\mathscr{P}
and stay away from
\infty
.
The following Lemma
4.1
is a straightforward consequence of the results in
[
8
]
(in slightly different terms). See the (unnumbered) Lemma on page 332 therein. To help the reader navigate the reference, the surface is denoted
S
, the probability measure
\phi
in the Lemma should be taken as the delta function supported at
\infty
. See also Corollary 1 on page 333, where the family denoted
\mathcal{X}
there should be taken as our
\mathbb{K}_{\mathscr{P}}^{(\delta)}
.

Lemma 4.1

Let
\delta>0
be sufficiently small so that

\mathbb{K}_{\mathscr{P}}^{(\delta)}
is not empty. Then the capacity

{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}:\mathbb{K}_{\mathscr{P}}^{(\delta)}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is Hausdorff continuous.

We sketch an elementary proof in Appendix
B
, for the interested reader.
The next lemma is also essentially in
[
8
]
(last bullet point on page 307) but we provide a slightly different proof nevertheless.

Lemma 4.2

Suppose that
\mathcal{K}_{n}
is a sequence of poly-continua in
\mathbb{K}_{\mathscr{P}}
such that
d_{\sigma}(\mathcal{K}_{n},\infty)\to 0
. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}(\mathcal{K}_{n})=+\infty.

Proof.

We transfer the problem to one of the standard complex plane. Fix
r>0
sufficiently small so that the set
|z_{\infty}|\leq r
in
{\mathcal{C}}
is mapped conformally to the disk
\mathbb{D}_{r}
and so that all anchors are outside. The map
\zeta=\frac{1}{z_{\infty}}
then maps to
|\zeta|\geq\frac{1}{r}
.
In the coordinate
\zeta
the bipolar Green function can be written

-G_{\infty}(p,q)=\ln\frac{1}{|\zeta-\xi|}+H(\zeta,\xi),

(4.3)

where
\zeta=\frac{1}{z_{\infty}(p)},\xi=\frac{1}{z_{\infty}(q)}
and
H(\zeta,\xi)
is a function which is harmonic and bounded in each variable in
|\zeta|\geq\frac{1}{r}\leq|\xi|
. Let us set

M:=\sup_{|\zeta|,|\xi|>R}\!\!\!H(\zeta,\xi)<\infty,\ \ \ R:=\frac{1}{r}.

We are going to construct a sequence of probability measures supported within

\mathcal{K}_{n}^{r}:=\mathcal{K}_{n}\cap\{|z_{\infty}|\leq r\}

whose energy tends to
-\infty
. The map
|\zeta|
is continuous and hence maps each connected component of
\mathcal{K}^{r}_{n}
to an interval. Furthermore, since each such connected component must contain also an anchor, it must “exit” the disk
|z_{\infty}|<r
. Thus, each component of
\mathcal{K}_{n}^{r}
is mapped to an interval
[R,c_{n}]
. Then
[R,L_{n}]
is simply the largest of the (finitely many!) such intervals, with
L_{n}=\frac{1}{\inf_{\mathcal{K}^{r}_{n}}|z_{\infty}(p)|}
. Note that
L_{n}
tends to infinity since the distance of
\mathcal{K}_{n}
from
\infty
tends to zero.

Let
{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{n}
be the measures obtained by (any) lifting to
\mathcal{K}_{n}^{r}
by the map
x=|\zeta|
of the arcsin measure
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}x}{\pi\sqrt{|(x-R)(x-L_{n})|}}
on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
restricted to the intervals
[R,L_{n}]
.
Existence of a lifting is a standard fact, see for example the lecture notes
[
24
]
, sec. 14.3. By the standard inequality
|\zeta-\xi|\geq\Big||\zeta|-|\xi|\Big|
we have that
\ln\frac{1}{|\zeta-\xi|}\leq\ln\frac{1}{\Big||\zeta|-|\xi|\Big|}
. Now,

\displaystyle-\ln{\rm Cap}(\mathcal{K}_{n})

\displaystyle=\inf_{{\mathrm{d}}\rho\in{\mathcal{M}}^{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{n}}}-\iint{\mathrm{d}}\rho(p){\mathrm{d}}\rho(q)G_{\infty}(p,q)\leq

\displaystyle\leq\iint{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{n}(\zeta){\mathrm{d}}\rho_{n}(\xi)\ln\frac{1}{|\zeta-\xi|}+\iint{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{n}(\zeta){\mathrm{d}}\rho_{n}(\xi)H(\zeta,\xi)\leq

\displaystyle\leq M+\int_{R}^{L_{n}}\ln\frac{1}{|s-t|}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}s}{\pi\sqrt{|s-R||s-L_{n}|}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}t}{\pi\sqrt{|t-R||t-L_{n}|}}=M-\ln\frac{L_{n}-R}{4}.

(4.4)

The last equality is due to the well known fact that the arcsin measure realizes the minimal energy of the segment, together with the fact that the capacity of a segment is its length divided by four. Since
L_{n}\to\infty
the capacity must diverge.

\blacksquare

With the two lemmas established, we can proceed to formulate and prove the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 4.3

For any admissible connectivity pattern
\mathscr{P}
there exists a (unique) continuum
\mathcal{K}_{\mathscr{P}}
minimizing the capacity within
\mathbb{K}_{\mathscr{P}}
.

Proof.

Let

C_{\mathscr{P}}=\inf{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}(\mathbb{K}_{\mathscr{P}})
and
let
(\mathcal{K}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset\mathbb{K}_{\mathscr{P}}
be any sequence of poly-continua such that
{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}(\mathcal{K}_{n})\to C_{\mathscr{P}}
.
By Lemma
4.2
any such a sequence must eventually maintain a positive distance
\delta
from
\infty
and hence belongs to
\mathbb{K}_{\mathscr{P}}^{(\delta)}
. Since such a class is Hausdorff compact and
{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}
is continuous on it, it must attain a minimum.
We are not proving here the uniqueness. This will be established in Section
6
, Proposition
6.5
.

\blacksquare

5
Properties of sets of minimal capacity

The next question is to characterize the geometry of these minimizers; for the case
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{P}^{1}
it is well known that these consist of finite union of analytic arcs and that they are related to the so–called horizontal trajectories of quadratic differentials satisfying the so-called
S
-property, see Stahl
[
31
,
32
,
33
]
or
[
23
]
.

A completely similar situation occurs here as we are discussing in this section

The key tool is, as typical, the notion of
Schiffer variation

[
30
]
, which was used in higher genus already in
[
6
]
.
We recall this notion here.

Let
h
denote a smooth vector field on
{\mathcal{C}}
; it determines a group of diffeomorphisms

\Phi:{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathcal{C}}\to{\mathcal{C}}

with the property that
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\Phi}{{\mathrm{d}}t}(t,p)\bigg|_{t=0}=h(p)
and
\Phi(0,p)=p
.

For any measure
{\mathrm{d}}\mu
one then pulls back the action of this diffeomorphism to define a transported measure
{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\epsilon,h}
according to the formula

\int f(p){\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\epsilon,h}(p):=\int f(\Phi(\epsilon,p)){\mathrm{d}}\mu(p),\ \ \epsilon\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(5.1)

The following is the adaptation of loc. cit.

Definition 5.1
(Definition 3.3 of
[
6
]
)

A mesure
{\mathrm{d}}\mu
on
{\mathcal{C}}
with support not containing
\infty
is a
critical measure
if

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{E}[{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\epsilon,h}]-\mathcal{E}[{\mathrm{d}}\mu]}{\epsilon}=0

(5.2)

for all vector fields
h
at least
C^{1}
in a neighbourhood of the support of
\mu
. Here
\mathcal{E}[{\mathrm{d}}\mu]
is the energy functional (see also Prop.
3.4
)

\mathcal{E}[{\mathrm{d}}\mu]:=-\iint G_{\infty}(p,q){\mathrm{d}}\mu(p){\mathrm{d}}\mu(q).

(5.3)

\blacktriangle

The following proposition is given without proof because the proof is verbatim the one in loc. cit.

Proposition 5.2
(Prop. 3.4 in
[
6
]
)

Let
{\mathrm{d}}\mu
be a measure of finite energy
\mathcal{E}[{\mathrm{d}}\mu]<+\infty
(
5.3
). Then

1.

For any vector field
h
which is
C^{1}
in a neighbourhood of the support of
{\mathrm{d}}\mu
the limit below exists

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{E}[{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\epsilon,h}]-\mathcal{E}[{\mathrm{d}}\mu]}{\epsilon}=\mathrm{Re}\,D_{h}({\mathrm{d}}\mu),

(5.4)

where
D_{h}({\mathrm{d}}\mu)
is the Schiffer variational derivative along the flow generated by the vector field
h
and it is given by

D_{h}({\mathrm{d}}\mu)=\iint h(p)\Big(\partial_{p}G(p,q)+h(q)\partial_{q}G(q,p)\Big){\mathrm{d}}\mu(p){\mathrm{d}}\mu(q).

(5.5)

2.

The measure is critical if
D_{h}({\mathrm{d}}\mu)=0
for any vector field
h
which is
C^{1}
in a neighbourhood of the support.

With reference to Remark 3.6 in loc. cit. we recall that it suffices to verify the criticality under variations induced by vector fields that are in the holomorphic tangent bundle to the surface, namely, for vector fields that in a local coordinate can be written as

h=g(z,\overline{z})\frac{\partial}{\partial z}.

We also recall that given any locally holomorphic differential
\omega=f(z){\mathrm{d}}z
, the expression
\frac{1}{\omega}
can be interpreted as a possibly meromorphic vector field in the holomorphic tangent bundle. In fact we will use only
meromorphic
vector fields, which are thus simply the reciprocal of a (meromorphic) differential.

The idea to be implemented is that by choosing the vector field appropriately, we can derive properties of the differential of the potential of a critical measure.

Preparation.

In order to provide an appropriate supply of meromorphic vector fields over which to test the criticality condition (
5.5
), we need a special kernel which is denoted
C^{(2,-1)}(p,q)
also in loc. cit. but was only defined for the case of an elliptic Riemann surface, and now has the properties described below.
The main difference and novelty relative to
[
6
]
is that the transporting diffeomorphism must preserve the anchor set
E
, namely

the criticality condition must hold for all
h
that vanish at the anchor set
E
.

(5.6)

This is the same constraint as in
[
23
]
.

Let
\mathscr{B}_{3g}
denote a generic divisor of degree
3g
. Then

1.

C^{(2,-1)}(p,q)
with respect to
p
is a quadratic differential such that

(a)

has simple pole for
p=q
and at
E
;

(b)

has a zero of order
N-3
at
\infty
and
3g
other zeros at
\mathscr{B}_{3g}
.

2.

With respect to
q
it is a
-1
differential (a meromorphic vector field) such that

(a)

has a pole a
q=p
, at
q\in\mathscr{B}_{3g}
and a pole of order
N-3
at
q=\infty
;

(b)

has zeros at
E
.

3.

it is normalized in such a way that, in any local coordinate
\zeta

C^{(2,-1)}(p,q)=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}z^{2}}{{\mathrm{d}}w}\left(\frac{1}{z-w}+\mathcal{O}(1)\right),\ \ z\to w,

(5.7)

where
z=\zeta(p),\ w=\zeta(q)
. This normalization is coordinate independent.

Using the common notation for divisors we can summarize the above in the formula:

\displaystyle{\rm div}\,_{p}\left(C^{(2,-1)}(p,q)\right)\geq-(q)-E+(N-3)(\infty)+\mathscr{B}_{3g},

\displaystyle{\rm div}\,_{q}\left(C^{(2,-1)}(p,q)\right)\geq-(p)+E-(N-3)(\infty)-\mathscr{B}_{3g}.

(5.8)

The existence and uniqueness, under genericity assumption, is guaranteed by the Riemann–Roch theorem. An explicit formula for
C^{(2,-1)}
can be given but is not essential. See Appendix
A
.

Consider the expression:

F(x;p,q):=-C^{(2,-1)}(x,p)\Omega^{i{\mathbb{R}}}_{\infty}(p,q)-C^{(2,-1)}(x,q)\Omega^{i{\mathbb{R}}}_{\infty}(q,p)+\Omega^{i{\mathbb{R}}}_{\infty}(x,p)\Omega^{i{\mathbb{R}}}_{\infty}(x,q),

(5.9)

where
\Omega^{i{\mathbb{R}}}
is the third kind normalized differential with purely imaginary periods, related to
G_{\infty}(p,q)
by (
3.6
).
It is manifestly a quadratic differential with respect to
x
and has the following analytic structure.

1.

It is regular at
x=p
and at
x=q
. This is verified easily in a local coordinate.

2.

It has simple poles at
E
and a double pole at
x=\infty
with unit bi-residue and coming only from the last term.

3.

It is a harmonic function with respect to
p
and
q
on
{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\{\infty\}

We then formulate the next main result

Theorem 5.3

Let
\mathscr{P}
be any admissible connectivity pattern for the set of anchors
E
; let
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{\mathscr{P}}
be the set minimizing the capacity within the class
\mathbb{K}_{\mathscr{P}}
as per Theorem
4.3
. Let
{\mathrm{d}}\mu
denote the equilibrium measure on
\mathcal{K}_{\mathscr{P}}
.
Let
G_{\mathcal{K}}
denote the corresponding Green’s function as per Definition
3.3
.
Then

\left(2\partial_{x}G_{\mathcal{K}}(x;\infty)\right)^{2}=\mathfrak{Q}(x)

(5.10)

is a meromorphic quadratic differential on the whole
{\mathcal{C}}
with at most simple poles at
E
, a double pole at
\infty
with bi-residue
1
. The quadratic differential
\mathfrak{Q}
is given by

\mathfrak{Q}(x)=\iint F(x;p,q){\mathrm{d}}\mu(p){\mathrm{d}}\mu(q),

(5.11)

with
F
as in (
5.9
).

Proof.

The measure
{\mathrm{d}}\mu
is critical and hence we apply the criterion (
5.5
) using

h(p)=C^{(2,-1)}(x,p).

Here the divisor
\mathscr{B}=\mathscr{B}_{3g}
of degree
3g
used to define the kernel
C^{(2,-1)}
can be chosen arbitrarily provided that it does not intersect the set
\mathcal{K}
.
This yields the identity

0=\iint\left[C^{(2,-1)}(x,p)\Omega^{i{\mathbb{R}}}_{\infty}(p,q)+C^{(2,-1)}(x,q)\Omega^{i{\mathbb{R}}}_{\infty}(q,p)\right]{\mathrm{d}}\mu(p){\mathrm{d}}\mu(q).

(5.12)

In the integrand we now use (
5.9
) and obtain

\Big(2\partial_{x}G_{\mathcal{K}}(x;\infty)\Big)^{2}=\left(\int\Omega^{i{\mathbb{R}}}_{\infty}(x,q){\mathrm{d}}\mu(q)\right)^{2}=\iint F(x;p,q){\mathrm{d}}\mu(p){\mathrm{d}}\mu(q).

(5.13)

We have established that
F(x;p,q)
is a meromorphic quadratic differential in
x
with poles at
E
and double pole at
\infty
with unit bi-residue. Thus the theorem is established with
\mathfrak{Q}(x)=\iint F(x;p,q){\mathrm{d}}\mu(p){\mathrm{d}}\mu(q)
.
In principle the formula holds for
x
outside of the support of
{\mathrm{d}}\mu
. However, using the same arguments as in
[
23
]
, Lemma 5.2, we can conclude that it holds everywhere.

\blacksquare

The theorem, exactly as in the case of minimal capacity sets on the complex plane, implies that
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{\mathscr{P}}
consists of union of vertical critical trajectories of
\mathfrak{Q}
see Corollary
5.5
; for background on quadratic differentials and their trajectories see
[
34
,
18
]
.
As is common in Teichmüller theory, to any meromorphic quadratic differential
\mathfrak{Q}
we can associate a Riemann surface
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
obtained as the locus within the holormophic co-tangent bundle
T^{*}{\mathcal{C}}
described by the equation

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}:=\{v^{2}=\mathfrak{Q},\ \ v\in T^{*}{\mathcal{C}}\}\subset T^{*}{\mathcal{C}}.

(5.14)

Let us denote by

\pi:\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\to{\mathcal{C}}

(5.15)

the canonical projection from the double cover to the base curve. We adopt the convention that
p,q,
etc., denote points of
{\mathcal{C}}
and
\widehat{p},\widehat{q},
etc. denote points of
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
projecting onto those denoted by the corresponding letter:
\pi(\widehat{p})=p
.
On the double cover
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
there are two points
\widehat{\infty}_{\pm}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\widetilde{\lambda}^{2}}\iint\left({\rho^{2}}-1\right){\mathrm{d}}s{\mathrm{d}}\theta-\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}+\frac{2\pi\lambda}{{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{2}}=\iint\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{\widetilde{\lambda}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right){\mathrm{d}}s{\mathrm{d}}\theta=\frac{2\pi}{\widetilde{\lambda}}-\frac{2\pi}{\lambda},

namely

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\lambda}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left({\rho^{2}}-1\right){\mathrm{d}}s{\mathrm{d}}\theta=\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1}).

(6.23)

Now,

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1})=\iint\left({\rho^{2}}-1\right){\mathrm{d}}s{\mathrm{d}}\theta=\iint\left({\rho}-1\right)^{2}{\mathrm{d}}s{\mathrm{d}}\theta+2\iint\left({\rho}-1\right){\mathrm{d}}s{\mathrm{d}}\theta.

(6.24)

Now we have from (

)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathcal{L}_{{\rho}}(\theta){\mathrm{d}}\theta=2\pi\widetilde{\lambda}+\int_{U^{+}}G_{\mathcal{K}}(p(\theta){\mathrm{d}}\theta-\int_{U^{-}}G_{\mathcal{K}}(p(\theta){\mathrm{d}}\theta=2\pi\widetilde{\lambda}+\int_{U^{+}}G_{\mathcal{K}}(p(\theta)(1-\phi(\theta)){\mathrm{d}}\theta\geq 2\pi\widetilde{\lambda},

(6.25)

which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\iint(\rho-1){\mathrm{d}}s{\mathrm{d}}\theta\geq 2\pi\widetilde{\lambda}-2\pi\lambda=\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1}).

(6.26)

Using (
6.26

6.24

\int_{0}^{\lambda}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left({\rho}-1\right)^{2}{\mathrm{d}}s{\mathrm{d}}\theta=\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1}),

(6.27)

so the only possiblity for (

) to hold is that
\rho\equiv 1
.
Recall now that
\rho=\left|\frac{g^{\prime}_{\mathcal{K}}}{g^{\prime}}\right|
and that both

are locally analytic in a punctured disk centered at
\infty

behave like

we conclude that

where they are both defined. Namely
g_{\mathcal{K}},g
differ by a constant in the common domain. But since both are supposed to be green’s functions, we conclude that
\mathcal{K}=\mathfrak{F}
.
\blacksquare

7
Jenkins-Strebel differentials and minimal capacity

The purpose of this section is to comment on, and elucidate the relationship between the notion of Jenkins-Strebel differential and problems of minimal capacity, which was already pointed out in
[
2
]
for the case of the Riemann sphere.
We refer to the review article
[
1
]
for many of the relevant notions.
A Jenkins-Strebel (JS) differential
\mathfrak{Q}
is a quadratic differential on a Riemann surface
{\mathcal{C}}
with at most simple poles at a set
E=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
and with double poles at another set
P=\{\infty_{1},\dots,\infty_{k}\}
satisfying the following properties (see Def. 31 in
[
1
]
):

1.

The bi-residues at each of the double poles are positive, namely, in local coordinate
z
near
p_{j}

\mathfrak{Q}=\frac{a_{j}^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}z^{2}}{z^{2}}(1+\mathcal{O}(z)),\ \ a_{j}>0.

(7.1)

2.

All non-critical vertical trajectories are closed loops.

For ease of comparison with the current setting, we have changed the overall sign of the quadratic differential in the definition of
[
1
]
, so that the horizontal trajectories therein are now vertical.
Then the Jenkins-Strebel theorem is as follows.

Theorem 7.1
(Theorem 32 in
[
1
]
)

For any sets
E,P
as above, and for any choice of parameters
a_{j}>0
there is a unique JS differential such that the critical graph (the union of the critical trajectories) is connected and contains
E
, and the complement of the critical graph is a disjoint union of conformal disks
\mathbb{D}_{j}
, each containing exactly one of the points
p_{j}
.

Jenkins-Strebel differentials were used to construct a cellular decomposition of the moduli space of Riemann surfaces of genus
g
with
k
marked points (these are the set
P
) in unpublished works of Mumford and Thurston, and by Harer
[
16
,
17
]
. The critical graph is called also
ribbon graph
and features prominently in the proof by Kontsevich of the Witten conjecture
[
20
,
35
]

.
It is an instructive exercise which we leave to the reader to show that the Jenkins-Strebel differentials of Theorem
7.1
are Boutroux differentials and, as a partial converse, a quadratic differentials with at most double poles, satisfying the Boutroux condition and with simple zeros has the properties in Theorem
7.1
. Note, however, that just the property of having closed vertical non-critical trajectories is weaker than the Boutroux condition.

The purpose of this section is rather to elucidate the relationship with our Chebotarev problem.
Suppose that the connectivity pattern
\mathscr{P}
for the set of anchors is such that the complement of the minimizing set
\mathcal{K}
is simply connected. Note that, in genus
0
this case is the classical Chebotarev problem of finding a continuum containing the anchors, but in higher genus, in general, the mere requirement that the minimizing continuum contains the anchors does not imply that the complement is simply connected.

Suppose that
\mathfrak{Q}
is the quadratic differential in our Theorem
5.3
for the minimizer in the class of the above connectivity pattern; then
\mathfrak{Q}
is the Jenkins-Strebel differential of Theorem
7.1
with
P=\{\infty\}
and
a_{1}=2\pi
. In other terms
\mathcal{K}
is the ribbon graph of Harer.

Viceversa, the Harer ribbon graphs in the case of one double pole are sets of minimal capacity in our sense. Of course for the case of several double poles what is being minimized is the sum of the “reduced modules”, see
[
34
]
, where the reduced module of a disk is equivalent to the notion of capacity here.

We do not delve further into this connection but we wanted to point it out because we have noticed that the potential theory community and the moduli space community by and large seem to have not realized the overlap between these two problems.

8
Conclusion and other similar problems

We want to comment on the application of this result to the study of Padé approximation and orthogonality. We keep this sketch of example “simple” for the sake of discussion, but the reader with experience in the asymptotics of orthogonal polynomial may easily come up with formulations of more general problems including those with scaling weights.

Let
E=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{N}\}
and choose paths connecting these points together in some connectivity pattern. Denote by
\Sigma=\bigcup_{j=1}^{L}\sigma_{j}
the union of these paths.
Fix also a point
\infty\not\in\Sigma
and a differential
\eta
defined and holomorphic on the universal cover of
{\mathcal{C}}\setminus E\cup\{\infty\}
with the following local behaviour near the points

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=z_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}(C_{j}+\mathcal{O}(z)){\mathrm{d}}z_{j},\ \ \mathrm{Re}\,\alpha_{j}\geq-1,\ \

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=z_{\infty}^{-\sum\alpha_{j}}(C_{\infty}+\mathcal{O}(z_{\infty})){\mathrm{d}}z_{\infty},

where
z_{j},z_{\infty}
are local coordinates near
e_{j}
and
\infty
.
Following
[
3
]
we fix a generic divisor
\mathscr{D}
of degree
g
not intersecting
\Sigma
or
\infty
.
It follows from the Riemann Roch theorem that the linear space of meromorphic functions with pole at
\mathscr{D}
and at most order
n
at
\infty
are a space of dimension
n+1
, much like the polynomials of degree not exceeding
n
.
Then we can pose the problem of finding a sequence
(\pi_{n})
of meromorphic functions with at most simple poles at
\mathscr{D}
and pole of order
n
at
\infty
such that

\sum_{j=1}^{L}\varkappa_{j}\int_{\sigma_{j}}\pi_{n}\pi_{m}\eta=\delta_{nm},

(8.1)

where
\varkappa_{j}
are arbitrarily chosen complex coordinates.
This is a non-hermitian orthogonality of sorts; the setting mimicks the situation of Jacobi polynomials that satisfy

\int_{-1}^{1}P_{n}(x)P_{m}(x)(x+1)^{\alpha}(1-x)^{\beta}{\mathrm{d}}x=\delta_{nm}.

(8.2)

Here
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{P}^{1}
,
E=\{1,-1\}
,
\infty
is the point at infinity and
\eta=(x+1)^{\alpha}(1-x)^{\beta}{\mathrm{d}}x
. In this simple example, the contour of integration in (
8.2
) is any arc connecting the two anchors, but the zeros of the orthogonal polynomials irrespectlvely of the choice of contour will accumulate on the straight segment, which is the minimal capacity set in this class.

Following
[
3
]
and the template of
[
5
]
one can setup a Riemann-Hilbert problem and study the large
n
asymptotics for the orthogonal meromorphic functions
\pi_{n}
. In
[
3
]
it is explained how the
\pi_{n}
’s appear as denominators of the one-point Padé approximation problem of a suitable Stieltjes transform of the measure
\eta
on
\Sigma
.

The first step in the nonlinear steepest descent analysis requires the construction of a
g
–function (in the lore of the literature) and then follows steps similar to
[
9
]
. The
g
function for the described problem would be exactly the function
g_{\mathcal{K}}=G_{\mathcal{K}}+iH_{\mathcal{K}}
, related to the minimal capacity set
\mathcal{K}
in the connectivity class determined by
\Sigma
.
This example should suffice to explain our interest in the problem discussed in this paper.

Generalizations.

•

The Green’s function
G_{\mathcal{K}}
takes zero value on
\mathcal{K}
. However one could consider a problem where on different components the function takes different constant values and then study the minimal “capacity” of these problems, with the capacity being defined by the expansion at infinity as per Definition
3.3
.

Let us outline very briefly how this problem would be relevant in a similar context.
Suppose now we are studying the large
n
degree of orthogonal functions for the pairing (
8.1
) but suppose now that the constants
\varkappa_{j}
are allowed to scale at an exponential rate with
n
, namely, we consider a problem with varying weight of rather simple form. The analogy for ordinary orthogonal polynomials would be the study of the following orthogonal polynomials, for example

\int_{0}^{1}P_{n}P_{m}{\mathrm{d}}x+{\rm e}^{-N}\int_{3}^{4}P_{n}P_{m}{\mathrm{d}}x,

(8.3)

where we study the behaviour of
P_{n}
as
n\to\infty
and
\frac{N}{n}\to c\neq 0
.
The
g
function for this problem would then be constructed out of the obstacle problem where
G_{\mathcal{K}}
takes different values greater or equal to
\frac{1}{n}\ln\varkappa_{j}
on the separate components. Note that the relevant problem to be solved is really the obstacle problem rather than the Dirichlet problem; however the two are equivalent in the setting of this paper.

•

Of course the full generalization would be to consider an arbitrary scaling weight, for example of the form
{\rm e}^{-nV(z)}\eta
with
V(z)
meromorphic. Then, instead of a pure capacity problem we would be lead to the study of a potential problem with external field. This type of problems become rapidly difficult to handle but in the case of ordinary orthogonal polynomials the energy max/minimization problem was successfully tackled at least for certain external fields,
[
23
,
21
]
and for
{\mathcal{C}}
of genus one some progress was in
[
6
]
.

•

Our motivation stems from the one-point Padé approximation problem. If we had several points of simultaneous approximation,
\infty_{1},\dots,\infty_{k}
with the corresponding orders of approximation,
n_{1},\dots,n_{k}
growing at the same rate, we would have to study a similar problem but where the Green’s function has several logarithmic poles, of different relative positive strengths adding up to
1
. This would lead to a generalized Chebotarev problem in the presence of several external charges and make full contact with the theory of Jenkins-Strebel differentials mentioned in Section
7
.

We hope to return to these problems in the near future.

Appendix A
Explicit formulas for the Cauchy kernel
C^{(2,-1)}

We recall basic facts and notation.

We choose a Torelli marking
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{g},b_{1},\dots,b_{g}\}
for
\mathcal{C}
in terms of which we construct the classical Riemann Theta functions. We refer to
[
11
]
, Ch. 1-2 for a review of these classical notions. The corresponding normalized Abelian differentials, period matrix and Abel map will be denoted by
\omega_{j},\boldsymbol{\tau},\mathfrak{A}
, respectively:

\oint_{a_{j}}\omega_{k}=\delta_{jk},\ \ \boldsymbol{\tau}_{ij}=\oint_{b_{j}}\omega_{i}=\oint_{b_{i}}\omega_{j},\ \ \ \mathfrak{A}_{p_{0}}(p)=\int_{p_{0}}^{p}\vec{\omega}:\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{J}(\mathcal{C}).

(A.1)

The base-point of the Abel map will not be indicated except when necessary. The Abel map is naturally extended to a map on divisors.
With
\Delta
we denote characteristics
\Delta=[\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{g}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{g}
corresponding to the point
{\mathbf{e}}=\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\tau}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{J}(\mathcal{C})
in the Jacobian.
The Theta function with characteristic
\Delta
is the function defined on
{\mathbb{C}}^{g}
by

\Theta_{\Delta}({\boldsymbol{z}};\boldsymbol{\tau}):=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{n}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{g}}\exp\left[i\pi\left({\boldsymbol{n}}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}\cdot\left({\boldsymbol{n}}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)+2i\pi\left({\boldsymbol{n}}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)\cdot\left({\boldsymbol{z}}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)\right],\ \ {\boldsymbol{z}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{g}.

(A.2)

It has the periodicity properties:

\Theta_{\Delta}(\boldsymbol{z}+\boldsymbol{m}+\boldsymbol{\tau}\boldsymbol{k};\boldsymbol{\tau})={\rm e}^{2i\pi\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{k}}{2}-\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{z}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{\tau}\boldsymbol{k}\right)}\Theta_{\Delta}(\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{\tau}).

(A.3)

If the characteristics
\Delta
is made of integers, we say that
\Delta
is
odd
if
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}+1
: in this case, fittingly,
\Theta_{\Delta}(-{\boldsymbol{z}};\boldsymbol{\tau})=-\Theta_{\Delta}({\boldsymbol{z}};\boldsymbol{\tau})
.

It is known that there always are
nonsingular
odd characteristics (Corollary 4.21 in
[
11
]
), namely, one for which the gradient
\nabla\Theta_{\Delta}(\boldsymbol{0})
is not the zero vector. For any such odd nonsingular characteristic, the following holomorphic differential
\omega_{\Delta}
has
g-1
only double zeros at a divisor
2\mathscr{D}_{\Delta}
, with
\mathscr{D}_{\Delta}
a divisor of degree
g-1
:

\omega_{\Delta}(p)=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\partial_{z_{j}}\Theta_{\Delta}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{0}}\omega_{j}(p).

(A.4)

Fix a nonsingular odd characteristic
\Delta
: then
[
11
]
the multi-valued function

\Theta_{\Delta}\left(\int_{q}^{p}\boldsymbol{\omega}\right),\ \ \boldsymbol{\omega}:=[\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{g}],

(A.5)

has, as a function of
p
,
g-1
zeros exactly at
\mathscr{D}_{\Delta}
, independent of
q
. Since
\Theta_{\Delta}
is an odd function, the same holds with respect to
q
.
Furthermore, for any (generic)
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{g}
the multi-valued function
\Theta(\mathfrak{A}(p)-{\boldsymbol{f}})
has
g
zeros (counted with multiplicity). Finally we recall that (Jacobi Inversion Theorem) for any (generic) divisor of degree
g
there is a corresponding
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{g}
such that the zeros of the above Theta function coincide with the desired divisor. With these preparations we can provide the expression of the Cauchy kernel

in Section
5

Proposition A.1

Let
\mathscr{B}_{1,2,3}
be three generic divisors of degree
g
and
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{1,2,3}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{g}
the corresponding vectors described above. Then

\displaystyle C^{(2,-1)}(x,q)=\frac{\Theta_{\Delta}^{N-3}(\int_{\infty}^{x}\boldsymbol{\omega})}{\Theta_{\Delta}^{N-3}(\int_{\infty}^{q}\boldsymbol{\omega})}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\Theta_{\Delta}(\int_{e_{j}}^{q}\boldsymbol{\omega})}{\Theta_{\Delta}(\int_{e_{j}}^{x}\boldsymbol{\omega})}\prod_{j=1}^{3}\frac{\Theta(\mathfrak{A}(x)-{\boldsymbol{f}}_{j})}{\Theta(\mathfrak{A}(q)-{\boldsymbol{f}}_{j})}\frac{\Theta(\int_{q}^{x}-{\bf s})}{\Theta_{\Delta}(\int_{q}^{x}\boldsymbol{\omega})}\frac{\omega_{\Delta}^{2}(x)}{\Theta(\boldsymbol{s})\omega_{\Delta}(q)},

(A.6)

where
\boldsymbol{s}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathfrak{A}(e_{j})-{\boldsymbol{f}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{f}}_{2}-{\boldsymbol{f}}_{3}-(N-3)\mathfrak{A}(\infty)
.

The genericity of
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{1,2,3}
guarantees that the constant in the denominator does not vanish,

.
It is an exercise using the periodicity properties (

Appendix B
Proof of Hausdorff continuity of
{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}

The proof proceeds from the following estimate of the Hölder continuity up to the boundary of the Green’s function, see
[
8
]
Section 2.8; these estimates are based on prior estimates valid in the plane and obtained in an unpublished work Perevoznikova and Rakhmanov
[
28
]
. See also
[
27
]
and the appendix in
[
7
]
.

Let
\mathbb{K}(\delta,\rho)
be a class of poly-continua with the properties that

•

there is
\delta>0
such that for each
\mathcal{K}\in\mathbb{K}
the diameter of each connected component of
\mathcal{K}
is at least
\delta
;

•

there is
\rho>0
such that
d_{\sigma}(\mathcal{K},\infty)\geq\rho
.

Under these hypotheses there is a constant
C=C(\delta,\rho)
such that

G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)\leq C\sqrt{d_{\sigma}(p,\mathcal{K})}.

(B.1)

Let us denote by
\mathcal{K}^{\epsilon}
the epsilon-fattening of a set
\mathcal{K}
. Suppose that
d_{H}(\mathcal{K},\widetilde{\mathcal{K}})\leq\epsilon
. Then
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}\subset\mathcal{K}^{\epsilon}
and
\mathcal{K}\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{\epsilon}
. But then

\displaystyle G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)-\max_{q\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}}G_{\mathcal{K}(q;\infty)}\leq G_{\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}}(p;\infty)\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)-G_{\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}}(p;\infty)\leq\max_{q\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}}G_{\mathcal{K}}(q;\infty)\leq C\sqrt{\epsilon}

(B.2)

where the last inequality follows from (
B.1
) and the fact that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}\subset\mathcal{K}^{\epsilon}
; swapping the roles of
\mathcal{K},\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
we obtain then

\big|G_{\mathcal{K}}(p;\infty)-G_{\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}}(p;\infty)\big|\leq C\sqrt{\epsilon}.

(B.3)

Taking the limsup at
p\to\infty
and using (
3.10
) yields

\left|\ln\frac{{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{K}})}{{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}(\mathcal{K})}\right|\leq C\sqrt{\epsilon},

(B.4)

which implies the Hausdorff continuity of
{\rm Cap}_{z_{\infty}}
over
\mathbb{K}(\delta,\rho)
as required. Note that the local coordinate
z_{\infty}
does not play any role since the ratio of capacities is independent of it, see (
1.2
).
