Hcn1-dependent engram neurons in the PVN encode gastric inflammatory sensitization
Authors: E Liu, Xijia Xin, Mengdong Shi, Weikai Han, Qingyu Ren, Yaqi Tang, Yanan Yue, Chenyu Zhang, Zhanpeng Gao, Qingwei Yue, Jinhao Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The brain encodes peripheral inflammatory signals, but the regulatory mechanism by which the brain controls gastric inflammation remains unclear. This study reveals a circuit through which Fos-positive neurons in the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus (PVN) regulate gastritis via a PVN–dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus–stomach neural circuit and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. Using activity-dependent genetic labeling, chemogenetics, and optogenetics, we demonstrate that FosPVN neurons are essential for gastritis progression and can be activated by stress to drive chronic inflammation. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) revealed up-regulation of hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide–gated channel 1 (Hcn1) in FosPVN neurons during gastritis. Inhibiting Hcn1 reduced neuronal excitability and ameliorated gastric pathology. Crucially, repeated activation of FosPVN neurons forms a specific “inflammatory sensitization,” leading to the persistence of disease, whereas stress can exacerbate gastric inflammation through these FosPVN neurons. Our findings elucidate the central neural mechanisms encoding gastric inflammation and identify Hcn1 as a potential therapeutic target for neuromodulatory treatment of chronic inflammatory diseases.

INTRODUCTION

Gastritis, a prevalent gastrointestinal disorder, has long been a focus of medical research. Traditionally, its pathogenesis has been attributed to Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection, drug-induced irritation, or autoimmune factors (1, 2). However, clinical observations reveal that some patients experience recurrent gastritis symptoms even after successful H. pylori eradication, suggesting the involvement of systemic regulatory mechanisms beyond local factors (3). Recent advances in neuroimmunology have highlighted bidirectional brain-immune communication as a pivotal framework for understanding inflammatory diseases.

The brain receives immune-related signals from peripheral organs (e.g., spleen and lymph nodes) via sensory inputs (4–6) and orchestrates responses to immune challenges. For instance, peripheral cytokines activate the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) via the vagus nerve, which subsequently modulates inflammatory reactions (7). Similarly, interleukin-1β (IL-1β) triggers a systemic corticosterone response through the vagal complex–parabrachial nucleus–paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus (PVN) circuit (8). The somatosensory cortex and central amygdala (CeA) regulate neuropathic pain-mediated peripheral immune responses via vagal projections to the spleen (9). Notably, a recent work demonstrates that the insular cortex stores colitis-associated immune information, and its reactivation can trigger inflammation (10), challenging the conventional view of immune memory as solely peripheral and introducing the concept of “central inflammatory sensitization.” Nevertheless, whether and how the brain encodes and retrieves gastritis immune signals remain poorly understood.

The PVN, a hub for neuroendocrine and autonomic integration, plays a central role in stress responses and visceral regulation (11, 12). One study (13) has shown that electrical stimulation of the PVN increases stress ulcers. It exerts no effect on gastric juice volume, acidity, total acid output, pepsin activity, or gastric barrier mucus but markedly reduces gastric mucosal blood flow (13). These results indicate that the PVN is an important brain region regulating the development of stress-induced gastric ulcers; however, the regulatory mechanism of the PVN in gastritis has not been reported. Our preliminary tracing experiments revealed increased Fos-positive neurons of the PVN (FosPVN) neuronal activity in gastritis models, with a subset of these neurons indirectly projecting to the gastric wall, suggesting PVN involvement in gastritis regulation. However, the underlying mechanisms remain elusive. The engram neuron framework posits that specific experiences selectively activate neuronal ensembles, which undergo molecular and structural plasticity to form enduring memories (14–16). Yet, whether dedicated neuronal populations encode gastritis-related information—and how these neurons integrate stress signals to influence inflammation—remains unknown. In chronic gastritis, persistent inflammatory stimuli may induce maladaptive neuronal plasticity, forming pathological memories that perpetuate disease.

Furthermore, whether gastritis alters gene expression in FosPVN remains to be elucidated. Advances in single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) have enabled single-cell resolution profiling of neuronal heterogeneity, allowing precise identification of molecular signatures in functionally distinct populations (17). Our snRNA-seq analysis of PVN in gastritis models revealed marked up-regulation of hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide–gated channel 1 (Hcn1) channels in FosPVN neurons. Hcn1 regulates neuronal excitability and rhythmic firing (18, 19), but its role in inflammation is unexplored. This raises a critical question: Does Hcn1 modulate gastritis by tuning FosPVN neuronal activity? Also, can targeting Hcn1 alter disease progression?

Stress-activated neural circuits converge on the PVN, which receives excitatory inputs from the posterior hypothalamus (PH) and the ventrolateral bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST) (20, 21). The PVN also receives substantial inhibitory inputs from hypothalamic regions—including the medial preoptic area (mPOA), dorsomedial hypothalamus (DMH), and periparaventricular zone (periPVN) (22)—as well as from medial forebrain structures such as the BNST (23). As a key hypothalamic nucleus, the PVN integrates multiple streams of afferent information concerning external threats and internal physiological states. This information is processed by the PVN and translated into output signals that regulate hormone release, modulate autonomic tone, and influence behavioral responses (24–27). Epidemiological studies show higher gastritis incidence in chronically stressed populations (28). The clinical link between psychological stress and gastritis is well established (29), and animal studies confirm that stressors like restraint exacerbate gastric mucosal damage (30, 31). An important question thus arises: How does stress induce gastric inflammation, and is this mediated specifically by the activation of distinct neuronal ensembles within the PVN? Mechanistic insights into this interaction are urgently needed, particularly given rising societal stress levels.

By integrating multidisciplinary approaches, this study uncovers PVN-mediated mechanisms of gastritis and proposes Hcn1 as a therapeutic target, advancing our understanding of stress-induced chronic inflammation.

RESULTS

Activation of FosPVN neurons in a HCl/EtOH-induced gastritis model

Three hours after HCl/EtOH modeling in mice, gastric tissues were collected and exhibited severe hemorrhaging on the mucosal surface with disorganized cellular architecture (Fig. 1, A to D), accompanied by significantly elevated levels of gastric IL-1β and tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α) (Fig. 1E).

When neurons are activated by specific stimuli, their c-Fos promoter drives the expression of CreERT2. The subsequently administered 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT) then acts as a molecular switch, enabling the translocation of CreERT2 into the nucleus and permanently activating the expression of the red fluorescent protein TdTomato. This 4-OHT–induced Cre-loxP system enables permanent labeling of neurons activated within specific time windows (Fig. 1F). To map brain regions activated during gastritis, we labeled c-Fos–positive neurons using Fos-CreERT2 × Rosa-TdT mice and performed retrograde polysynaptic tracing via gastric wall injection of pseudorabies virus PRV-CAG-EGFP (Fig. 1G). Whole-brain imaging revealed PRV-EGFP+ neurons colocalized with Fos-TdT+ cells in multiple regions, including BNST, PVN, CeA, lateral hypothalamus (LH), parasubthalamic nucleus (PSTh), Barrington’s nucleus (Bar), nucleus of the solitary tract (NTS), and dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus (DMV). Notably, compared with the saline group, PVN showed the highest colocalization rate, followed by PSTh (Fig. 1, H and I). Consistently, c-Fos immunofluorescence demonstrated that gastritis-induced activation was most pronounced in PVN neurons, with PSTh ranking second (Fig. 1, J and K). These results indicate that gastritis selectively activates PVN and PSTh neurons, a subset of which directly or indirectly project to the gastric wall.

Functional role and activation mechanisms of FosPVN neurons in gastritis

To investigate whether gastritis enhances neuronal activity in PVN, we injected the calcium indicator GCaMP6s into the mouse PVN and monitored neuronal activity in real time using fiber photometry (Fig. 2A). The results showed that compared with the saline control group, the amplitude and frequency of calcium signal in PVN neurons significantly increased at 3 hours post–gastritis induction in mice (Fig. 2, B to D). This indicates that gastritis effectively enhances the overall activity level of PVN neurons.

We next examined whether suppression of FosPVN neurons influences gastritis progression. We used a chemogenetic inhibition strategy targeting Fos-expressing PVN neurons using the doxycycline (DOX)–controlled transcriptional activation (tTA) system. When DOX is present, it binds to and inactivates tTA, resulting in silencing of the target gene. Upon DOX withdrawal, tTA regains its activity, binds to the tetO operator sites, and initiates robust expression of the target gene. This “Tet-Off” mechanism enables precise spatiotemporal control of gene expression (Fig. 2E). First, rAAV-F-RAM-d2tTA-hM4Di was injected into the mouse PVN region. The mice were then maintained on DOX-containing diet for 2 weeks before modeling to label gastritis-activated FosPVN neurons. After a 3-week recovery period, intraperitoneal injection of clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) (3 mg/kg) was administered to inhibit FosPVN neurons before gastritis modeling (Fig. 2F). The results demonstrated that chemogenetic inhibition of gastritis-activated FosPVN neurons significantly reduced Fos expression in the PVN (Fig. 2, G and H) and markedly attenuated gastric inflammation (Fig. 2, I to L) during gastritis progression. In a separate approach, AAV-DIO-NpHR-GFP and Fos-CreERT2 mixed viruses were injected into the mouse PVN, followed by optical fiber implantation in the same region. Two weeks later, gastritis modeling was performed combined with 4-OHT intraperitoneal injection to label gastritis-activated FosPVN neurons. After a 3-week recovery period, gastritis was reinduced and 589-nm light stimulation was applied (Fig. 2M). The results indicated that the Fos-labeling virus was exclusively expressed within the PVN, with no detectable leakage to adjacent brain regions [e.g., LH, anterior hypothalamic area (AHP), reuniens nucleus (Re), or zona incerta (ZI)] (Fig. 2M). Optogenetic suppression of gastritis-induced FosPVN neurons similarly led to improvement in gastric inflammation (Fig. 2, N to Q).

Concurrent calcium imaging revealed that PSTh neurons also exhibited enhanced activity during gastritis (fig. S1, A to D), and chemogenetic inhibition of FosPSTh neurons produced a modest reduction in gastric inflammation (fig. S1, E to K). Notably, the anti-inflammatory effect was more pronounced with FosPVN neuron inhibition compared to FosPSTh inhibition. These findings collectively indicate that FosPVN neurons play a crucial role in regulating gastric inflammatory responses.

To investigate the mechanisms underlying FosPVN neuron activation during gastritis, we explored two potential pathways: neural pathway and humoral pathway. Immunofluorescence assays revealed that vagotomy significantly decreased the number of Fos-positive neurons in PVN compared to sham-operated controls (fig. S2, A and B), suggesting that gastric inflammatory signals may be transmitted to PVN via the vagus nerve. Consistently, vagotomy induced a slight but detectable reduction in gastric inflammation (fig. S2, C to F). Gastritis significantly elevated plasma levels of IL-1β and TNF-α (fig. S2G). A previous report indicates that, among inflammatory cytokines, only IL-1β and TNF-α can activate PVN neurons (8). Accordingly, immunofluorescence assays revealed that intraperitoneal administration of either IL-1β or TNF-α induced differential activation of PVN neurons (fig. S2, H and I). Fos-CreERT2 × Rosa-TdT mice were divided into three groups: The saline group received intraperitoneal injection of saline along with intragastric administration of saline; the IL-1β+TNF-α group received intraperitoneal injection of IL-1β and TNF-α along with intragastric administration of saline; the gastritis group received intraperitoneal injection of saline along with intragastric administration of HCl/EtOH. All three groups were simultaneously administered 4-OHT intraperitoneally during model establishment to label Fos-positive neurons activated during either intraperitoneal cytokine administration or gastritis induction, respectively (fig. S2J). The results demonstrated that intraperitoneal administration of IL-1β and TNF-α significantly increased the number of FosPVN neurons projecting to the gastric wall compared with the saline group; however, the gastritis-induced FosPVN neurons projecting to the gastric wall were substantially more abundant than those activated by systemic IL-1β or TNF-α administration alone (fig. S2, K and L). These results support the conclusion that inflammatory information activates FosPVN neurons through both neural transmission via the vagus nerve and humoral pathways involving circulating IL-1β and TNF-α.

Mechanisms of FosPVN neuron-mediated gastritis regulation

Immunofluorescence staining revealed that FosPVN neurons were predominantly corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH)–positive neurons. Gastritis induction significantly increased CRH expression in the PVN (Fig. 3, A and B), accompanied by elevated plasma corticosterone levels compared with the saline group (Fig. 3C). Intraperitoneal administration of a CRH receptor antagonist antalarmin markedly attenuated gastric inflammation compared with the saline group (Fig. 3, D to G), suggesting that FosPVN neurons may regulate gastric inflammation through the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis.

To investigate whether FosPVN neurons additionally modulate gastritis through neural circuits, we recorded neuronal firing activity using tungsten electrodes placed on gastric-innervating nerves. Gastritis mice exhibited significantly increased firing frequency compared to controls, indicating enhanced neural activity following gastric inflammation (Fig. 3, H and I). Retrograde tracing with red retrobeads injected into the gastric wall predominantly labeled neurons in the DMV (Fig. 3J). Choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) immunofluorescence confirmed that these retrobead-labeled DMV neurons were primarily cholinergic (Fig. 3K). rAAV-F-RAM-d2tTA-EGFP was injected into the PVN to label gastritis-activated Fos neurons, whereas red retrobeads were administered into the gastric wall for retrograde monosynaptic tracing (Fig. 3L). Analysis of mouse brain sections revealed axonal terminals of FosPVN neurons colocalized with gastric wall–injected red retrobeads in the DMV (Fig. 3M). These results anatomically confirm the existence of a FosPVN-DMV-gastric circuit. Our next step is to investigate whether functional connectivity exists in the FosPVN-DMV-gastric circuit. A 1:1 mixture of AAV-DIO-ChR2-GFP and Fos-CreERT2 (total volume: 400 nl) was injected into the PVN, and an optical fiber was implanted in the DMV. Two weeks later, gastritis was induced and 4-OHT was administered intraperitoneally to label gastritis-activated Fos neurons. Following a 2-week recovery period, we performed optical stimulation of axonal terminals of FosPVN neurons in the DMV (Fig. 3N). We observed that optogenetic activation of the channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) group significantly increased the firing frequency of gastric wall nerves during light stimulation compared to the green fluorescent protein (GFP) control group (Fig. 3, O and P), thus confirming the functional connectivity of the FosPVN-DMV-gastric circuit. We further used conditioned place aversion (CPA) assays to determine the functional relevance of FosPVN neurons. The results showed that optogenetic activation of FosPVN neurons in the previously preferred chamber induced an aversion to the light-stimulated chamber in mice (Fig. 3, Q and R).

Next, we further validated the role of the FosPVN-DMV circuit in gastritis. Compared with the GFP group, optogenetic inhibition of FosPVN axon terminals in the DMV moderately attenuated gastric inflammation (Fig. 4, A to E) without altering circulating corticosterone levels (Fig. 4F) while significantly reducing firing frequency in gastric-projecting nerves (Fig. 4, G and H). Conversely, optogenetic activation of FosPVN terminals in DMV induced mild gastritis (Fig. 4, I to L), similarly without affecting corticosterone concentrations (Fig. 4M), but markedly increased gastric nerve activity (Fig. 4, N and O). Notably, DMV activation of FosPVN terminals did not elevate serum inflammatory cytokines (fig. S3, A and B).

Meanwhile, we investigated whether selective activation of the PVN-DMV circuit alone could induce gastritis. Retrograde AAV-hSyn-P2A-Cre was injected into the DMV, whereas either the chemogenetic virus AAV-hSyn-DIO-hM3Dq-mCherry or the control virus AAV-hSyn-DIO-mCherry was delivered into the PVN. Two weeks after viral expression, mice were intraperitoneally administered with CNO (3 mg/kg) once daily for 5 consecutive days (fig. S4A). The results revealed that, compared with the control group, hM3Dq group mice exhibited disordered gastric wall cell arrangement and significantly elevated levels of gastric inflammatory factors (fig. S4, B and C). These findings demonstrate that activation of the PVN-DMV circuit is sufficient to induce gastric inflammation. In summary, these results demonstrate that FosPVN neurons regulate gastritis through both the FosPVN–DMV–stomach neural circuit and the HPA axis.

Repeated FosPVN activation drives chronic gastritis

Mice were divided into hM3Dq and mCherry groups. The hM3Dq group received PVN injections of the chemogenetic activator rAAV-F-RAM-d2tTA-hM3Dq-mCherry, whereas the mCherry group received control injections of rAAV-F-RAM-d2tTA-mCherry. The hM3Dq group was maintained on DOX chow, and the mCherry group was maintained on regular chow throughout the study. After 2 weeks of viral expression, food was withdrawn for 24 hours followed by saline gavage (0.2 ml/20 g), with respective diet regimens continuing thereafter. Following a 3-week recovery period, mice received saline gavage and subsequent daily intraperitoneal CNO injections from days 2 to 5 (fig. S5A). Results showed normal gastric wall cell architecture in both groups (fig. S5B) with no statistically significant differences in gastric inflammatory factor levels (fig. S5C). These findings demonstrate that DOX chow administration and withdrawal do not elevate gastric inflammatory factor levels.

To investigate whether prolonged abnormal activation of FosPVN neurons forms chronic inflammation and drives sustained gastritis progression, mice received bilateral PVN injections of the chemogenetic activator rAAV-F-RAM-d2tTA-hM3Dq-mCherry. After 2 weeks of viral expression, gastritis was induced to label Fos-positive neurons activated by inflammation. Following a 3-week recovery period, gastritis was reinduced, and mice received daily intraperitoneal CNO or saline injections from day 2 to day 5 postinduction (Fig. 5A). Immunofluorescence staining revealed few Fos-positive neurons in the PVN at 24 hours postgastritis in the saline group, with nearly complete resolution by 48 and 96 hours. In contrast, CNO-treated mice maintained substantial Fos-positive neuronal populations at both 48 and 96 hours postgastritis (Fig. 5, B and C). Compared to the saline group, CNO-treated animals exhibited disrupted gastric wall architecture (Fig. 5, D and E) and significantly elevated inflammatory factor levels at 48 and 96 hours (Fig. 5F). Corresponding increases in serum corticosterone levels and gastric nerve firing frequency were also observed (Fig. 5, G to I). Notably, no statistically significant differences were detected in gastric inflammation, corticosterone levels, or gastric nerve activity between 48 and 96 hours within the CNO group (Fig. 5, F, G, and I), indicating that FosPVN activation primarily triggers inflammatory responses rather than impeding recovery processes.

Next, we optogenetically activated gastritis-induced neurons for 5 consecutive days in mice after complete resolution of gastritis. Tissue samples were collected 3 hours after the final optical stimulation on day 5 (Fig. 5J). The activation group displayed irregularly shaped and disorganized gastric parietal cells, alongside markedly elevated inflammatory factors (Fig. 5, K and L), higher plasma corticosterone levels, and increased gastric neural firing (Fig. 5, M to O). Notably, optogenetic activation of FosPVN neurons did not increase serum inflammatory factor levels (fig. S3, C and D). This suggests that FosPVN may regulate local inflammation rather than systemic inflammation. Collectively, these results demonstrate that repeated FosPVN neuron activation specifically induces persistent gastritis.

snRNA-seq identifies Hcn1 as a key regulator of FosPVN neurons

We performed snRNA-seq on PVN tissues from control and gastritis mice (n = 2 samples per group and 6 mice per sample; Fig. 6A). A total of 131,672 nuclei were sequenced (fig. S6D), with neurons comprising glutamatergic (Slc17a6) and GABAergic (Slc32a1) subtypes (fig. S6E), consistent with PVN characteristics.

PVN neurons were further clustered into 15 subpopulations (fig. S7A), with Fos-positive neurons scattered in both groups (fig. S7B). We isolated Fos-positive neurons (1854 control versus 2020 gastritis; Fig. 6B) and reclustered them into 13 subpopulations (Fig. 6C), predominantly glutamatergic or GABAergic (fig. S7C). Heatmaps show the top 3 marker genes per cluster (Fig. 6D), with varying proportions between groups (Fig. 6E). The sequencing results revealed that, compared to the control group, the differentially expressed genes of FosPVN neurons in the gastritis group were involved in distinct biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions (fig. Among the top 30 differentially expressed genes, Hcn1 and CRH were significantly up-regulated in gastritis (Fig. 6F). Hcn1 and CRH showed partial colocalization, with ~53% of CRH neurons expressing Hcn1 in the gastritis group versus 38% in the saline group (Fig. 6, G and H). Immunofluorescence confirmed elevated Hcn1 expression (Fig. 7, A and B) and increased Hcn1-CRH colocalization in the gastritis group compared with the saline group (Fig. 7, C and D). Compared with the saline group, PVN-targeted injection of the Hcn1 inhibitor R0-275 (Fig. 7E) alleviated gastritis (Fig. 7, F to I), reduced plasma corticosterone and gastric neural firing (Fig. 7, J to L), and decreased Fos expression (Fig. 7, M and N) and calcium activity in PVN (Fig. 7, O to Q) after gastritis. These results identify Hcn1 as a key regulator of FosPVN neurons.

Stress exacerbates gastritis via FosPVN neurons

We examined whether stress activates FosPVN neurons to promote gastritis. Using a water-immersion restraint stress (RS) model (Fig. 8A), mice exhibited anxiety-like behaviors in open-field and elevated plus maze tests (fig. S8, A to F). RS induced gastric hemorrhagic spots compared with the control group (Fig. 8, B and C), disorganized parietal cells (Fig. 8D). Compared with the control group, water-immersion RS induced significantly elevated levels of inflammatory factors in the blood (fig. S8G) with a more pronounced increase observed in the stomach (Fig. 8E). Stressed mice showed PVN neuron activation (Fig. 8, F and G), increased plasma corticosterone, and gastric neural firing compared with the control group (Fig. 8, H to J).

DISCUSSION

Our study elucidates a central neural mechanism by which FosPVN encode and regulate gastritis. Key findings include the following: (i) FosPVN neurons are robustly activated during gastritis and regulate inflammation via dual pathways: neuroendocrine (HPA axis) and autonomic (PVN–DMV–stomach neural circuit); (ii) Chronic FosPVN activation establishes “inflammatory sensitization,” driving gastritis persistence; (iii) Hcn1 is a critical molecular determinant of FosPVN neuronal excitability and gastritis progression; and (iv) Psychological stress exacerbates gastritis by reactivating FosPVN neurons. These findings provide a framework for understanding how the brain stores and retrieves immune information while identifying potential therapeutic targets for chronic inflammatory diseases.

FosPVN neurons as a central hub for gastritis encoding

Retrograde tracing and functional mapping revealed PVN as the most prominently activated brain region in gastritis, with subsets of neurons indirectly projecting to the gastric wall—consistent with PVN’s role in integrating neuroendocrine, autonomic, and stress responses (11, 12). Chemogenetic or optogenetic inhibition of FosPVN neurons significantly attenuated gastritis, confirming their necessity in inflammation regulation. Chronic chemogenetic activation (hM3Dq, acting over hours/days) likely leads to sustained CRH release, robustly engaging the HPA axis. In contrast, the more acute and spatially restricted optogenetic activation of PVN terminals in the DMV may preferentially drive the autonomic (vagal) circuit to the stomach without fully recruiting the neuroendocrine axis. This divergence itself supports our model of dual, potentially dissociable, output circuits.

FosPVN neurons orchestrate gastritis through two parallel yet synergistic pathways: (i) the HPA axis (CRH-dependent) and (ii) the PVN–DMV–stomach neural circuit. Vagotomy only partially reduced the activation of Fos-positive neurons in the PVN, whereas blood-borne IL-1β and TNF-α may activate the PVN through both vagal and humoral pathways. A recent work showed vagal transmission of gastric pain signals to the anterior insular cortex (32); here, we demonstrate that vagal pathway also relay gastritis signals to the brain. This dual neural-humoral mechanism ensures robust inflammatory signaling but may also exacerbate pathology upon dysregulation.

Inflammatory sensitization formation in FosPVN neurons

A notable finding was that repeated FosPVN activation induced persistent gastritis—mimicking chronic disease—without elevating systemic cytokines, indicating a neuron-specific effect. Notably, optogenetic reactivation of FosPVN neurons provoked even higher gastric IL-1β (317 versus 191 pg/ml in HCl/EtOH models) and TNF-α (760 versus 339 pg/ml), resembling “inflammatory sensitization” in the central nervous system (CNS): Prior inflammation primes neurons for exaggerated responses to subsequent challenges. Our data suggest that FosPVN neurons retain a molecular imprint of gastritis, driving dysfunction upon reactivation. This mechanism may explain recurrent or refractory gastritis, particularly under chronic stress.

PVN’s role in inflammatory sensitization parallels reports of insular cortex storing colitis information (10). However, unlike the insula, PVN directly governs autonomic/endocrine outputs, positioning it as a critical node converting immune signals into physiological responses. Future studies should explore whether other regions (e.g., amygdala or BNST) cooperate with PVN to consolidate or extinguish gastritis sensitization. We did not directly measure gastric motility or blood flow in this study. Future work should investigate these specific physiological parameters (motility, acid secretion, and blood flow) downstream of PVN activation, citing key previous studies on PVN’s role in these processes.

Hcn1: A key regulator of FosPVN excitability and gastritis susceptibility

Our snRNA-seq analysis not only confirmed the cellular heterogeneity of the PVN but also revealed distinct molecular reprogramming features within inflammation-activated Fos-positive neurons. The study found that, compared to the control group, Fos-positive neurons in the gastritis group exhibited transcriptomic restructuring—reflected not only in expression changes of key genes such as Hcn1 and CRH but also in alterations in the proportions of different neuronal subpopulations. This dynamic reorganization of population structure suggests that inflammatory states may achieve precise regulation of neural circuit output by modulating the excitability balance of specific neuronal subtypes. Different Fos-positive neuronal subpopulations displayed heterogeneous gene expression patterns under gastritis conditions, indicating that the PVN may coordinately regulate inflammatory responses through parallel neuronal populations with distinct molecular characteristics. These molecularly defined neuronal subpopulations may participate in regulating different physiological responses, including the HPA axis, autonomic nervous system, and behavioral adaptations. Compared with the control group, the expression of nucleobindin 2 (Nucb2)—the gene encoding the appetite-related neuropeptide nesfatin-1 (33)—was significantly increased in the PVN of gastritis mice (fig. S7, E and F). Approximately 16.5% of Fos-positive neurons coexpressed Nucb2 in the control group, whereas this proportion increased to about 21.6% in the gastritis group (fig. S7, E and F). Previous studies have shown that, in addition to centrally suppressing food intake, nesfatin-1 can also reduce vagal nerve–stimulated gastric acid secretion (34), slow gastrointestinal motility (35), and induce anxiety and arousal-like behaviors (36). In addition, studies have reported that, intracerebroventricular injection of nesfatin-1 can induce the enhanced HPA axis and sympathetic activity (37). Given that nesfatin-1 exerts complex and multifaceted effects, it will be an interesting direction for future research.

Focusing on inflammation-activated Fos-positive neurons, our snRNA-seq analysis identified Hcn1 as one of the most significantly up-regulated genes. HCN channels, activated by membrane hyperpolarization and permeable to Na+/K+, are constitutively open near resting potentials (38). HCN channels regulate neuronal excitability, dendritic integration, synaptic transmission, and network oscillations (39, 40) and are implicated in synaptic plasticity and memory (41, 42). Therefore, during gastritis, Hcn1 may not only directly regulate neuronal excitability but also reshape the functional state of the entire neural circuit by influencing synaptic plasticity and network oscillations. This multilevel restructuring—from molecular to cellular to circuit levels—may underlie the formation and persistence of “inflammatory sensitization” in the CNS in chronic inflammation and offers potential molecular targets for developing neuromodulatory therapies for refractory inflammatory diseases. Crucially, we observed a close relationship between Hcn1 and CRH expression: not only was their colocalization significantly increased in gastritis, but ~53% of CRH neurons also coexpressed Hcn1. This finding provides a molecular explanation for why inhibiting Hcn1 simultaneously reduces neuronal calcium activity, decreases Fos expression, and effectively alleviates gastritis severity, corticosterone levels, and gastric neural discharge frequency.

Furthermore, the up-regulation of Hcn1 may represent a molecular manifestation of “inflammatory sensitization.” The endogenous electroplasticity mediated by Hcn1 enables persistent reprogramming of intrinsic excitability following initial inflammatory stimulation, establishing a hyperreactive state. This elegantly explains why, upon subsequent stress or inflammatory challenges, FosPVN neurons can be rapidly “reactivated” and exhibit abnormally strong responses. Our functional experiments confirmed that local inhibition of Hcn1 significantly reverses this process, not only reducing inflammation but also dampening downstream HPA axis and autonomic nervous outputs. Thus, Hcn1 serves not only as a key molecular switch regulating the excitability of FosPVN neurons but also as a critical medium for the storage and amplification of inflammatory information in the brain, offering a perspective for understanding the central inflammatory sensitization mechanisms of chronic gastritis and developing targeted therapeutic strategies.

Stress exacerbates gastritis via FosPVN neurons

Psychological stress is a known gastritis trigger, but its neural mechanisms were unclear. Here, water-immersion RS reactivated gastritis-associated FosPVN neurons, with 80% overlap between stress-activated and inflammation-activated ensembles. This indicates that stress “reawakens” prior inflammatory sensitization, amplifying gastric pathology. Notably, stress increased Hcn1 expression in PVN, and Hcn1 blockade alleviated stress-induced gastritis, underscoring its role in stress-inflammation coupling. PVN’s role in stress-inflammation cross-talk may extend beyond gastritis. Future work should explore its involvement in disorders like irritable bowel syndrome.

Innovation and implications

This study advances the field in three key ways: (i) demonstration of PVN as a storage site for gastritis-related information, expanding the concept of central inflammatory sensitization; (ii) identification of Hcn1 as a target linking neuronal excitability to inflammatory regulation; and (iii) elucidation of stress-aggravated gastritis mechanisms, offering translational insights for clinical management. Beyond gastritis, our findings represent a paradigm shift—from viewing inflammation as a local phenomenon to understanding it as a systemic “brain-periphery” dialogue. This framework has broad implications for chronic inflammatory diseases.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental animals

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Shandong University (ECSBMSSDU2024-2-238) and performed according to institutional guidelines. Male C57BL/6J mice (6 to 8 weeks old) were housed under standard conditions (12 hour/12-hour light/dark cycle, 22° ± 1°C, and 50 to 60% humidity) with free access to food and water. Fos-CreERT2 mice (the Jackson Laboratory, strain no. 030323) were crossed with Rosa-TdTomato reporter mice (the Jackson Laboratory, strain no. 007914) to generate Fos-CreERT2 × Rosa-TdT mice for neuronal labeling. In Fos-CreERT2 × Rosa-TdTomato double transgenic mice, when neurons are activated by specific stimuli, their c-Fos promoter drives the expression of CreERT2. The subsequently administered 4-OHT acts as a “molecular switch” that enables the translocation of CreERT2 into the nucleus. This process permanently activates the expression of the red fluorescent protein TdTomato. Through this 4-OHT–induced Cre-loxP system, permanent labeling of neurons activated within specific time windows is achieved.

Gastritis model induction

Mice were fasted for 12 hours before modeling. The model group received oral gavage of HCl/EtOH solution (40% distilled water and 60% ethanol and 0.15 M HCl, 0.2 ml/20 g of body weight), whereas the control group received an equal volume of saline (43, 44). Because intragastric administration of HCl/ EtOH solution can induce gastric injury and inflammation within 1 hour, and neuronal c-Fos expression in the brain peaks 2 hours after stimulation (45), tissue collection was performed 3 hours after gastritis modeling. The stomach was excised, opened along the greater curvature, and rinsed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution to remove residual gastric contents. After drying with absorbent paper, the tissue was processed for subsequent experiments.

Inflammation assessment

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA): IL-1β and TNF-α levels in gastric homogenates were measured using ELISA kits (Wuhan Cloud-Clone). The gastric tissue was rinsed in precooled PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.0 to 7.2) to remove blood and then homogenized with a bead homogenizer after adding PBS at a mass-to-volume ratio of 1:20. The resulting homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000g for 5 min. The supernatant was collected for subsequent analysis after discarding the precipitate.

Histopathology: Gastric tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated using an automated tissue processor, and embedded with an embedding machine. The resulting paraffin blocks were sectioned at 5-μm thickness and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining. The H&E staining procedure was performed sequentially as follows: dewaxing, gradient hydration, H&E staining, gradient dehydration, clearing, and mounting.

Mucosal damage scoring: A blinded pathologist scored epithelial erosion, hemorrhage, and inflammatory infiltration (0 to 10) (46).

Viral vectors

Mice were anesthetized with 1.25% tribromoethanol (0.2 ml/10 g), and body temperature was maintained at ~36°C using a heating pad. The head was securely fixed in a stereotaxic apparatus (RWD, China). The scalp hair was shaved, and the skin was disinfected with iodine solution. A sterilized scalpel was used to make a midline incision to expose the skull. After exposure, the skull was leveled in both anteroposterior (AP) and mediolateral (ML) directions using a microsyringe. Target brain regions were located using stereotaxic coordinates relative to bregma: ML, dorsoventral (DV), and AP. A small craniotomy was performed using a dental drill above the target site, and viral solution (200 nl) was injected at a rate of 40 nl/min using a microsyringe. The syringe was left in place for an additional 5 min after injection to minimize backflow. The incision was closed with a single 6-0 nylon suture. Maximal adeno-associated virus (AAV) expression was typically observed 2 to 3 weeks postinjection and remained relatively stable for several weeks. The following viral vectors were used in this study:

AAV-hSyn-GCaMP6s: This AAV expressing the calcium indicator GCaMP6s used for activity imaging in neuronal populations.

rAAV-F-RAM-d2tTA-hM4Di: The promoters in F-RAM consist of the consensus DNA binding sequences for Fos. The promoter drives the expression of reporter genes under the temporal control of a modified Dox-dependent Tet-off system developed for the RAM system. rAAV-F-RAM-d2tTA-hM4Di encoding the designer receptor hM4Di (Gi-DREADD) under the control of a tetracycline-responsive element. The d2tTA (destabilized tetracycline transactivator) drives expression in the absence of Dox. hM4Di allows chemogenetic inhibition of labeled neurons upon CNO administration. rAAV-F-RAM-d2tTA-mCherry serves as the corresponding control virus. Expression of mCherry was prevented in the presence of DOX in the chow (40 mg/kg), but removal of DOX+ food opened the tagging window, such that active neurons expressing AAV-RAM-mCherry expressed mCherry.

rAAV-F-RAM-d2tTA-hM3Dq-mCherry: Similar to the hM4Di construct but encodes hM3Dq (Gq-DREADD) for chemogenetic excitation. Coexpresses mCherry for visualization. Neuronal activation was achieved via CNO-mediated hM3Dq stimulation.

Fos-CreERT2: Activity-dependent, tamoxifen-inducible Cre recombinase system. c-Fos promoter drives CreERT2 expression in neurons activated during a defined time window. 4-OHT inducible: CreERT2 translocates to the nucleus upon 4-OHT administration, enabling recombination in active neurons.

AAV-DIO-ChR2-GFP: Double-floxed inverted orientation (DIO) for Cre-dependent expression of ChR2 fused to GFP. Optogenetic activation of Cre-expressing neurons (470-nm blue light). Titer: ≥1 × 1013 viral genomes (vg)/ml.

AAV-DIO-NpHR-GFP: Ensures Cre-dependent expression of halorhodopsin (NpHR), an inhibitory optogenetic tool, fused to GFP for visualization. Yellow/red light (e.g., 589 nm)–induced silencing of Cre-expressing neurons via chloride influx. Titer: ≥1 × 1013 vg/ml.

AAV-hSyn-P2A-Cre (retro): Retrograde-transsynaptic viral vector that crosses a single synapse in the retrograde direction and expresses Cre recombinase in the presynaptic neurons.

AAV-hSyn-DIO-hM3Dq-mCherry: Will only be “unlocked” in these Cre-expressing neurons. DIO contains the gene sequence flanked by two inversely oriented loxP sites. Cre recognizes and cleaves the two loxP sites, causing the middle gene sequence to invert and rearrange into the correct forward orientation. This enables proper transcription and translation, ultimately leading to the expression of the hM3Dq-mCherry protein.

FosPVN neuron labeling

Fos-CreERT2 × Rosa-TdT mice were intraperitoneally injected with 4-OHT (25 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, H6278) immediately after gastritis induction to label activated neurons (TdTomato+). 4-OHT was dissolved in corn oil:ethanol (9:1).

Retrograde tracing

Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1.25% tribromoethanol (0.2 ml/10 g) and positioned supine on a heating pad maintaining the body temperature at ~36°C, with limbs extended and secured using electroconductive tape to expose the abdominal area. Following abdominal hair trimming and skin disinfection with iodophor, a midline incision was made with sterilized scissors to expose the stomach. A microsyringe fitted with a glass micropipette (tip-trimmed) was used to draw either the retrograde polysynaptic tracer PRV-CAG-EGFP (200 nl per site, four sites; BrainCase, Shenzhen) or the retrograde monosynaptic tracer red retrobeads (2 μl per site, four sites; Lumafluor). Using forceps with tips wrapped in saline-moistened cotton, a portion of the gastric tissue was gently lifted under a stereomicroscope, and the trimmed micropipette was inserted into the submucosal layer of the gastric wall. This layer ensures efficient uptake of the tracer by the dense network of nerve terminals while providing a stable microenvironment that prevents tracer diffusion and inactivation, thereby yielding specific labeling with high signal-to-noise ratio. Tracers were infused at 100 nl/min, followed by a 5-min postinjection dwell period before slow withdrawal of the pipette. The incision was sutured, and after 5 days, brain sections (30 μm) were prepared for confocal microscopy analysis of fluorescently labeled neurons.

Immunofluorescence staining

Mice were anesthetized and perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde, and brain sections were blocked with 0.3% Triton X-100 and 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 hour. Primary antibodies: rabbit anti-c-Fos (1:1000, ab214672, Abcam), guinea pig anti-c-Fos (1:1000, 226308, Synaptic Systems), rabbit anti-ChAT (1:1000, ab181023, Abcam), rabbit anti-Crh (1:500, 20084, ImmunoStar), and guinea pig anti-Hcn1 (1:1000, APC-056, Alomone). Secondary antibodies: anti-rabbit 488 (1:200, RGAR002, Proteintech), anti-rabbit 594 (1:200, RGAR004, Proteintech), and anti-guinea pig 488 (1:200, Abcam). Sections were mounted with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI)–containing antifade medium and imaged.

Fiber photometry (calcium imaging)

Mice were anesthetized and AAV-hSyn-GCaMP6s (200 nl, BrainCase) was injected into the PVN (AP: −0.82 mm, ML: ±0.13 mm, and DV: −4.9 mm) or PSTh (AP: −2.3 mm, ML: ±1.2 mm, and DV: −4.95 mm) at 0.04 μl/min. Optical fibers (200 μm in diameter, 0.37 numerical aperture, fixed on ceramic ferrules with an outer diameter of 1.25 mm) (Inper, Hangzhou, China) were implanted (PVN: AP: −0.82 mm, ML: ±0.3 mm, and DV: −4.7 mm; PSTh: AP: −2.3 mm, ML: ±1.2 mm, and DV: −4.75 mm) and secured with dental cement. Calcium transients were recorded 2 weeks postinjection. The GCaMP6 fluorescence signals were acquired using a fiber photometry system (brand: ThinkerTech Nanjing BioScience) at a sampling rate of 50 Hz. The output intensity of the 470-nm light-emitting diode (LED) was adjusted to maintain a range of 20 to 40 μW at the optical fiber tip. Before formal recording, the experimental mice were acclimated to the recording environment for one hour. They were then gently connected to optical fiber patch cables and returned to their home cage, whereas their cage mates were temporarily transferred to another cage for subsequent testing. Starting from the second day, the target mice were individually placed in either a clean cage or a cage with bedding. Signal acquisition was performed for 10 min at 2 hours after intragastric administration of normal saline. After 24 hours, signal acquisition was conducted for 10 min at 2 hours after intragastric administration of HCl/EtOH to the same mice. The acquired data were subsequently analyzed using a custom-developed program based on MATLAB (Natick, USA). For processing the fiber photometric calcium signal data, the fluorescence change ratio was calculated using the formula dF/F0 = (F − F0)/F0, where F0 represents the mode of the entire fluorescence recording. In the accumulated calcium event (ACE) analysis, an event was identified as an ACE if its peak amplitude exceeded twice the average cumulative fluorescence intensity. After the experiment, viral expression and optical fiber placement were verified for all mice.

Chemogenetic inhibition of FosPVN neurons

rAAV-F-RAM-d2tTA-hM4Di (200 nl per side, GeneChem) was injected bilaterally into the PVN or PSTh. DOX-containing food (40 mg/kg) was provided ad libitum, commencing 24 hours before the microinjection of AAV-F- RAM. Mice were fed DOX chow for 2 weeks to suppress baseline expression. For Fos neuron tagging, the DOX diet was discontinued 24 hours in advance, and gastritis was modeled. Upon completion of the model induction, the diet was immediately supplemented with DOX at a concentration of 1000 mg/kg to rapidly terminate the tagging window, ensuring temporal precision. Following this 24-hour period, the dietary DOX concentration was restored to 40 mg/kg. After a 3-week recovery period, a subsequent episode of gastritis was induced 0.5 hours following an intraperitoneal CNO (3 mg/kg) injection, whereupon both gastric and brain tissues were harvested for further experimentation.

Chemogenetic activation of FosPVN neurons

rAAV-F-RAM-d2tTA-hM3Dq-mCherry or rAAV-F-RAM-d2tTA-mCherry (200 nl per side, BrainCase) was injected into the PVN of C57BL/6J mice. After 2 weeks of DOX (40 mg/kg) chow, the DOX diet was withdrawn 24 hours before gastritis induction to label Fos neurons. Following modeling, a DOX diet (1000 mg/kg) was immediately provided. Twenty-four hours later, the DOX concentration was readjusted to 40 mg/kg. After a 3-week recovery period, gastritis was induced in mice, and they received daily intraperitoneal injections of CNO (3 mg/kg) from day 2 to day 5 postinduction.

Optogenetic activation/inhibition

Activation: A 1:1 mix of AAV-DIO-ChR2-GFP and Fos-CreERT2 (400 nl, BrainCase) was injected into the PVN. Surgical implantation of optical fibers targeted at the DMV (AP: −7.5 mm, ML: ±0.25 mm, and DV: −4.2 mm) was performed, with the fiber assembly firmly affixed to the skull using tissue adhesive and dental cement. A gastritis model was established 2 weeks postsurgery, during which 4-OHT was injected intraperitoneally to label FosPVN neurons activated by the gastritis. After a 3-week recovery, a 5-day stimulation regimen was initiated, wherein daily 15-min blue light stimulation (15 min/day, 30 Hz) was delivered via a laser system connected to the skull implant. Tissue collection was performed at 3 hours after completion of the stimulation protocol. The signal was acquired by the THINKERTECH optogenetics system (China).

Inhibition: AAV-DIO-NpHR-GFP and Fos-CreERT2 (400 nl, BrainCase) were injected into the PVN. Targeted implantation of optical fibers was performed in the PVN or DMV. Subsequent to a 2-week viral expression period, tamoxifen was injected intraperitoneally immediately following gastritis induction to label FosPVN neurons activated by gastritis. Following an additional 3-week recovery, a subsequent gastritis induction was performed concurrent with the delivery of yellow light (589 nm, 10-min on/20-min off, 30 Hz) for 3 hours postgastritis. The signal was acquired by the THINKERTECH optogenetics system (China).

Vagotomy

After anesthetizing the mice, a 10-mm abdominal skin incision was created lateral to the linea alba. The liver and stomach were retracted to provide exposure of the two vagal nerve trunks aligned with the esophagus. The vagus nerves were meticulously dissected away from their connective tissue, after which an ~5-mm segment was ablated from each trunk. Subsequent to suturing the muscle and skin layers and disinfecting the area, the mice were subsequently allowed a 5-day postoperative recovery before any subsequent experimental procedures were conducted.

Intraperitoneal injections

Tissue harvesting was conducted 3 hours following the intraperitoneal administration of IL-1β (200 μg/kg, MedChemExpress, USA), TNF-α (100 μg/kg, MedChemExpress, USA), or saline administered concurrently as a vehicle control. A separate cohort of mice received an intraperitoneal injection of the CRH1 receptor antagonist antalarmin (20 mg/kg, MedChemExpress, USA). Thirty minutes after this pretreatment, gastritis was induced. All subjects in this group were euthanized 3 hours post–gastritis induction.

FosPVN-DMV-gastric circuit tracing

After the mice were anesthetized, rAAV-F-RAM-d2tTA-EGFP (200 nl per side, GeneChem) was injected into the PVN, and red retrobeads (2 μl per site, three sites) were injected into the gastric wall. Subsequent to a 2-week viral expression period, gastritis was induced for the purpose of labeling c-Fos neurons activated by gastritis. Subsequently, after a 1-week recovery period, brain tissues were harvested from mice for sectioning.

Corticosterone assay

Serum was prepared for corticosterone measurement as follows: Orbital blood was collected 3 hours post–model induction and held at room temperature for 2 hours to clot. Following centrifugation at 2500g for 20 min at 4°C, the supernatant was collected and stored. Corticosterone levels were quantified using a commercially available ELISA kit (Wuhan Cloud-Clone).

Gastric nerve electrophysiology

Upon induction of anesthesia, mice were fixed in a supine position on a thermostatically controlled heating pad set to maintain a body temperature of 36°C. Abdominal exposure was achieved by extending and securing the limbs with electronic tape. The surgical site was prepared by shaving and application of iodine antiseptic. A midline laparotomy was performed using sterile scissors to access the stomach. The nerves innervating the gastric wall were meticulously dissected using fine forceps and a glass needle. A tungsten microelectrode was hooked onto these nerve fibers and interfaced with a multichannel acquisition and processing system (ADInstruments, Australia). For subsequent data analysis, recorded neural signals were subjected to a digital band-pass filter (10 to 60 Hz) in LabChart 8 software (ADInstruments, Australia) for noise reduction, and the mean discharge frequency of the gastric nerve was determined for each subject.

Conditioned place aversion

Under anesthesia, mice received an injection of an optogenetic viral mixture (AAV-DIO-ChR2-GFP combined with Fos-CreERT2 in a 1:1 ratio, total volume: 400 nl) into the PVN, followed by optical fiber implantation at the DMV. Two weeks later, gastritis was induced and 4-OHT was administered intraperitoneally to label c-Fos neurons activated by gastritis. After a 3-week recovery period, mice were placed in a CPA apparatus and allowed to freely explore both chambers for 15 min to establish baseline preference. After the test, all surfaces of the chambers—including floors and walls—were thoroughly wiped with 75% ethanol. During the conditioning phase, blue light stimulation was delivered for 10 min in the initially preferred chamber, whereas no light was applied in the opposite chamber for the same duration. One hour later, mice were reintroduced to the CPA apparatus for a final 15-min free exploration session. The CPA score was calculated as the difference in time spent in the light-paired chamber minus the time spent in the unpaired chamber.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing

Tissue processing and nuclei isolation

For optimal nuclei recovery, the PVN was microdissected from control/gastritis mice (n = 6 per sample and 2 samples per group) 3 hours postmodeling and was immediately placed in ice-cold PBSE buffer (1× PBS containing 2 mM EGTA). Tissue dissociation was performed using gentle mechanical homogenization followed by enzymatic treatment when necessary. Nuclei isolation was carried out using the GEXSCOPE Nucleus Isolation Kit (Singleron Biotechnologies), with all steps performed on ice to maintain RNA integrity. The homogenate was sequentially filtered through 100- and 40-μm cell strainers to remove debris and obtain single-nucleus suspensions. Nuclei viability and concentration were assessed using DAPI staining (1:1000 dilution) and automated cell counting with Trypan blue exclusion. Only intact, single DAPI-positive nuclei with clear nuclear morphology were selected for downstream processing, with final concentrations adjusted to 1 × 106 nuclei per 400 μl in nuclei preservation buffer.

Library preparation and quality control

Single-nucleus suspensions (3 × 105 to 4 × 105 nuclei/ml) were loaded onto microfluidic chips using the GEXSCOPE platform. The automated workflow included the following: (i) single-nucleus capture in nanoliter-scale droplets, (ii) cell lysis and poly-A mRNA capture, (iii) reverse transcription with unique molecular identifiers (UMIs), and (iv) cDNA amplification. Library quality was assessed using the Agilent Bioanalyzer (DNA High Sensitivity Kit) and Qubit fluorometry, with typical cDNA yields of 10 to 15 ng/μl. Final libraries were pooled and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system [150–base pair (bp) paired-end reads] to achieve a target depth of 50,000 to 100,000 reads per nucleus. Sequencing parameters were optimized to maintain high-quality scores (Q30 > 85%) across all bases.

Data processing and initial analysis

Raw sequencing data were processed through the following pipeline: demultiplexing and barcode processing using CeleSCOPE (v1.9.0), adapter trimming and quality filtering with Cutadapt (v1.17), alignment to reference genome (GRCm38/mm10) using STAR (v2.6.1a), and gene-level quantification with featureCounts (v2.0.1). Quality control metrics included the following: median genes per nucleus: 500 to 3000, median UMI counts: 1000 to 5000, mitochondrial gene content: <10%, and ribosomal RNA content: <20%.

Advanced computational analysis

The filtered gene expression matrix was analyzed using Scanpy (v1.8.2) with the following steps: normalization (total counts followed by log1p transformation), highly variable gene selection (2000 genes), principal components analysis (top 50 principal components), neighborhood graph construction (k = 15 neighbors), Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) visualization (min_dist = 0.5), and Leiden clustering (resolution = 0.8). Differential expression analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Cell type annotation used automated methods (Cell-ID and SingleR), manual curation using canonical markers, and reference mapping to Tabula Muris.

Specialized downstream applications

For biological interpretation, we used trajectory analysis (Monocle3 for pseudotime ordering), cell-cell communication (CellPhoneDB v3.0 with stringent filtering), regulatory networks (pySCENIC with improved motif databases), and spatial mapping (integration with Visium data when available).

This comprehensive protocol has been optimized for minimal batch effects (tested with Harmony), high reproducibility (R2 > 0.95 for technical replicates), and compatibility with downstream multiomics integration.

Localized drug delivery

Mice were bilaterally implanted with guide cannulas targeting the PVN (AP: −0.82 mm, ML: ±0.13 mm, and DV: −4.6 mm) using a stereotaxic apparatus, with the implants secured using dental cement. After a 5-day recovery period, subsequent experiments were conducted. A PE-10 tube was connected at one end to an injection cannula and at the other end to a 5-μl Hamilton syringe, which was loaded with the HCN1 blocker RO-275 (20 mg/kg, 200 nl, MedChemExpress, USA). The injection cannula was then inserted into the preimplanted guide cannula on the mouse’s head, and the compound was slowly infused into the target brain region. The injection cannula was left in place for 5 min to allow for adequate diffusion of the compound. Gastritis modeling was initiated 15 min after the microinjection.

RS model

Mice were restrained in perforated 50-ml tubes immersed in water (below the xiphoid) for 2 hours/day over 2 days. Tissue samples were harvested 3 hours after model induction on the second day.

Behavioral tests

Elevated plus maze: Time and entries into open arms were recorded for 5 min. Open field: Locomotor activity was tracked for 5 min.

SDS model

To induce SDS, an intruder mouse (experimental mouse) was introduced into the home cage of a resident CD1 mouse for 5 min. During this period, five to eight aggressive encounters typically occurred. The intruder and resident were then separated by a transparent divider, maintaining olfactory, visual, and auditory contact for 10 min. This social defeat session was repeated four times at 15-min intervals, totaling 60 min. At the end of the stress procedure, the intruder exhibited pronounced freezing behavior or submissive postures. Although no physical wounds were typically observed on intruders following this protocol, any intruder with visible injuries—along with its corresponding CD1 mouse—was excluded from the study. For the control group, intruder mice were placed into the home cage of an aggressive resident mouse for 60 min but were continuously separated by a transparent divider to maintain sensory contact (olfactory, visual, and auditory) without physical interaction.

Statistical analysis

On the basis of preliminary experiments or established practices in the literature, the sample size was determined. Mice were randomly assigned to different experimental groups. Blinding was implemented during procedures such as behavioral scoring, histological analysis, and experimental interventions. The statistical data points in the article are derived from different animals (biological replicates). All data were analyzed without the exclusion of any outliers. Data are presented as means ± SD. Normality was assessed by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Parametric data were analyzed by the t test (unpaired, two-tailed), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (≥3 groups), or two-way ANOVA; nonparametric data used Wilcoxon or Kruskal-Wallis tests. P < 0.05 was considered significant (GraphPad Prism 10.0).
