A confirmation test for the presence of Quillaja saponin and adulteration with Madhuca saponin in poultry feed using high-performance liquid chromatography and quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry
Authors: Bernhard Thalhamer, Markus Himmelsbach, Christian Klampfl, Wolfgang Buchberger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To date, there is a lack of a confirmation test for the presence of Quillaja saponin in poultry feed. This gap persists despite the fact that thousands of tonnes of feed with added saponin are produced and used annually. As a result, even basic tasks such as the analytical confirmation of feed formulations have remained unaddressed. Our work intentionally targets this gap by introducing a presence confirmation. Additionally, poultry feed was examined for the presence of Madhuca longifolia L., as previous studies had demonstrated that commercially available Quillaja saponin extracts are often adulterated with cost-effective Madhuca saponin. Pelleted poultry feed samples were analyzed via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) hyphenated with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (QToF-MS) in the negative ionization mode, targeting the main saponin compounds serving as markers for presence confirmation of Quillaja and Madhuca saponin in complex feed matrices. The unequivocal confirmation of Quillaja in feed samples containing additives classified as flavoring substances or sensory additives was enabled for the first time by this newly developed test. Unexpectedly, Madhuca was also found in some samples, clearly revealing adulteration for the first time. This confirmation test may support industry and authorities to confirm the presence of Quillaja saponin in feed and to check poultry feed for adulteration with Madhuca saponin. Furthermore it provides a practical tool for feed manufacturers by offering clarity as often the composition of saponin-containing additives is not fully disclosed by additive suppliers.

Introduction

In 2006, a significant change occurred in the animal feed sector, when the European Union (EU)-wide ban on antibiotics came into effect. Before 2006, it was authorized and common practice to add antibiotics to animal feed; however, from that point onward, such practice was prohibited. This ban necessitated the development of alternative substances to replace the antibiotics previously used (European Commission, 2005).

The search for alternative substances focused on plants, herbs, and essential oils, which were expected to replace, at least partially or fully, the desired effects of antibiotics. Notably, companies from the European region drove the development of phytogenic alternatives and became leaders in this field (Gadde et al., 2017; Diaz-Sanchez et al., 2015; Jouany and Morgavi, 2007).

The Regulation (EC) No. 1831/2003, which governs the authorization and use of feed additives, provides for the classification of additives into several groups, including “Sensory additives under group 2b” (European Union, 2003). In practice, products containing phytogenic alternatives are frequently registered under this category, even if their biological effects extend beyond taste and aroma enhancement.

Phytogenic alternatives include essential oils from oregano, caraway, cinnamon, and thyme or their main active compounds (European Commission, 2009, 2011, 2014; European Commission, 2017). Other suitable substances are derived from pungent plants, such as pepper and paprika. In addition, bitter substances like gentian and saponins such as those from Quillaja saponaria L. and Yucca schidigera Roezl ex Ortgies are also used. Saponins as feed additives not only provide antimicrobial properties, which are particularly significant for poultry (Sen et al., 1998; Blue et al., 2023; Dai et al., 2023), but also have the potential to reduce ammonia emissions when included in poultry feed (Saeed et al., 2018; Dang and Kim, 2020). Lower concentrations of ammonia in the indoor air of barns can enhance animal health, leading to improved performance.

Essential oils are in many cases relatively simple mixtures dominated by only a few major compounds. These compounds can be confirmed using commercially available reference standards, such as thymol in thyme oil and cinnamaldehyde in cinnamon bark oil. Confirmation tests for detecting such substances in feed are available, although only to a limited extent, as documented in the Final Report on Feed Additive Dossiers (European Commission, 2009, 2011, 2014; European Commission, 2017) prepared by feed additive manufacturers.

In contrast, the situation with Quillaja saponin is markedly different. While some individual compounds have been structurally elucidated, a major proportion remains insufficiently characterized, as the scientific literature indicates. Quillaja saponins exhibit remarkable complexity, with hundreds of individual compounds constituting the saponin composition.

A literature search yielded no reports describing a confirmation test for the presence of Quillaja in poultry feed, indicating that this topic has not yet been addressed to date.

It is apparent that the identification of Quillaja in feed constitutes a significant analytical challenge, that arises from several factors. Poultry feed is not a uniform matrix but rather comprises a diverse range of formulations. Each feed exhibits its own unique complexity, resulting from regional raw material choices and age dependent nutritional requirements. Due to the minimal presence of Quillaja saponin in these diverse feed matrices one must anticipate considerable matrix effects and instrumental limitations.

Despite the widespread use of saponin in feed additives, feed manufacturers are often inadequately informed about the exact composition of the feed additives obtained from feed additive suppliers. Often only the label "sensory additives 2b" is provided. Even when "saponin" are explicitly mentioned in feed additives, feed manufacturers may remain uncertain about the specific plant sources. This lack of transparency is also due to the fact that suppliers of feed additives do not want to fully disclose their exact formulations to prevent potential product imitation.

According to information provided by feed manufacturers – based on their own expertise and on limited disclosures from feed additive suppliers – feed additives are incorporated into the feed at a ratio of approximately 150–250 g per ton. These feed additives commonly comprise a complex mixture of botanicals and/or plant extracts.

The saponin content in feed – as roughly estimated by manufacturers – is about 5 ppm, derived from inclusion rates and information from the additive supplier rather than from direct measurements. This estimate underscores the methodological and practical limitations that currently underlie this gap, namely the absence of direct measurement and reliable confirmation tests.

The idea of developing a confirmation test for the presence of Quillaja saponin in animal feed thus appeared innovative and valuable, as it is relevant for feed manufacturers as well as feed mills, given that Quillaja raw material is used worldwide on an industrial scale, yet incorporated into feed only at trace levels.

Within the present investigations it was also intended to check the possible presence of the Indian plant Madhuca longifolia L. in animal feed alongside Quillaja saponins. Previous work has revealed that Quillaja saponin standards available in the fine chemicals trade are often adulterated with cost-effective saponins from Madhuca (Thalhamer and Himmelsbach, 2014; Thalhamer and Buchberger, 2021; Thalhamer et al., 2021b). One may suspect that such adulteration could provide significant competitive advantages due to lower price, especially given the widespread use of Quillaja as a feed additive. In 2002 the European Commission had explicitly classified Madhuca saponin as undesirable substance in animal feed and had excluded their use as feed additives (European Commission, 2002). In its opinion on Madhuca saponin, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) concluded in 2009 that – following an EU-wide request for data on its occurrence in feed materials – no cases were reported. Due to the low nutritional value, EFSA argued further that there is no incentive to import Madhuca saponin into the EU. EFSA resumed that human exposure via animal products is negligible because there is no evidence that it is present in animal feed (EFSA, 2009). Consequently, acting on this assessment, in 2010 the European Commission removed Madhuca saponin from the list of undesirable substances, as human exposure to these compounds was considered negligible (European Commission, 2010). It is highly likely, however, that this assessment of negligible exposure primarily results from the absence of a suitable confirmation test for Madhuca saponins in feed. With regard to the current regulatory situation Madhuca is currently not included in the list of sensory additives (category 2b) and is therefore not authorized for use in animal feed.

Addressing the challenges outlined above, we have chosen to focus on Quillaja – one of the most widely used saponin containing plants in animal feed – and to develop a confirmation test for the presence of Quillaja saponins as well as their possible adulteration with Madhuca, a practice already known from other industrial sectors.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Feed samples were obtained from feed mills and feed manufacturers. The feed samples were randomly selected based on the following criteria: pelleted feed for broiler chickens, containing additives classified as flavoring substances or sensory additives and, where information was available to the feed manufacturer, feed containing Quillaja saponin or saponin. Detailed information about the manufacturers is deliberately omitted in order to avoid potential conflicts since some of these feed samples have tested positive for adulteration with Madhuca. Reagents and reference materials

Water with a resistivity of 18 MΩ·cm was produced using a Milli-Q water purification system (Millipore, Molsheim, France). Analytical-grade acetonitrile and methanol were sourced from VWR (Fontenay-sous-Bois, France), formic acid was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), and hexane from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany).

Crushed Quillaja bark (Quillaja saponaria) of Pharmacopoeia quality was purchased from Kwizda (Vienna/Austria). Quillaja biomass was provided by Fitotek (Santiago/Chile). Purified Quillaja saponin extract was obtained by Guinness Products Ltd. (Portlaoise, Co. Laois, Ireland). Seed kernels from Madhuca longifolia and Mahua seed cake, which is the press residue from Madhuca longifolia, were obtained from Kaushal Herbs and Beej Bhandar (Saidpur, India).

Preparation of sample solution

Preparation of feed sample solution. Pelleted feed was gently ground and homogenized using a mortar and pestle. Subsequently, 2.5 g of each feed sample were transferred into a centrifuge tube and mixed with 10 mL of hexane. The mixture was extracted in an ultrasonic bath at 30°C for 30 minutes. The centrifuge tube was then centrifuged at 4500 rotations per minute, and the supernatant containing nonpolar dissolved substances was removed. The solid centrifugate was dried at room temperature, suspended in 7.5 mL of methanol, and subjected to extraction at 60°C for 30 minutes in a closed centrifuge tube using an ultrasonic bath. After centrifuging again at 4500 rotations per minute, 1 mL of the clear liquid supernatant was taken and mixed with 1 mL of water. This dilution with water was done to render the injection solution more aqueous, thus preventing peak broadening at the start of the high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) run. Additionally, matrix components precipitate and are removed, thereby preventing contamination of the chromatographic column and the mass spectrometer. Following a brief ultrasonic treatment for 2 minutes, the solution was filtered through a membrane filter (0.45 µm) and was ready for HPLC analysis.

Preparation of Quillaja and Madhuca/Mahua sample solution. Crushed Quillaja bark, Quillaja biomass as well as seed kernels of Madhuca and Mahua seed cake were homogenized and suspended in a 50% (v/v) aqueous methanol solution at a concentration of approx. 0.1% and extracted at 60°C for 30 min in an ultrasonic bath. After cooling to room temperature, the suspensions were filtered through a membrane filter (0.45 µm) and used for HPLC analysis.

Instrumentation

HPLC. An Agilent Series 1260 HPLC (Waldbronn, Germany) system equipped with a quaternary pump, vacuum degasser, and autosampler was used to conduct the analysis.

Quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (QToF-MS). Mass spectrometry detection was performed on an Agilent 6520 QTOF (Agilent, Palo Alto, CA, USA). Electrospray ionization (ESI) in negative ion mode was employed following the observation that in positive ion mode the main saponins of Quillaja and Madhuca exhibited signal intensities lower by more than an order of magnitude. The MS/MS mode was not used within the present work. For low molecular mass analytes (∼300 Da), an intense and selective product ion in MS/MS can markedly improve detectability by reducing the pronounced matrix background in this m/z range. In contrast, for the high molecular mass compound QS-18 (>1000 Da) investigated here, the intrinsic matrix background is lower and fragmentation efficiency is poor, resulting in no sensitivity gain from MS/MS.

Optimization with regard to the mass spectrometric parameters resulted in the following ion source settings: drying gas temperature of 350°C, capillary voltage of 3750 V, nebulizer pressure of 45 psi, fragmentor voltage of 250 V, and drying gas flow of 10.5 L/min.

Chromatographic conditions of HPLC. By refining our previously developed chromatographic approach for the current purpose (Thalhamer and Himmelsbach, 2014), the chromatographic conditions were optimized to reduce the overall runtime, thereby enabling a higher sample throughput. While this optimization results in a slight compromise in separation performance, the identification of the main saponin compounds – the focus of this study – remains unaffected. For separation of individual saponins a Kinetex C18 LC column (50 mm x 4.6 mm ID, 2.6 µm particle size) obtained from Phenomenex (Aschaffenburg, Germany) was used. Samples were injected with volumes varying between 5 and 100 µL, adjusted as necessary to facilitate the unambiguous confirmation of the marker compounds. The HPLC eluent consisted of solvent A water, solvent B acetonitrile, and solvent C aqueous 3% formic acid. The mobile phase gradient was programmed as follows:

Initial conditions: solvent A 81%, solvent B 9%, solvent C 10%; 1.0 min: solvent A 77%, solvent B 13%, solvent C 10%; 4.0 min: solvent A 65% B 25%, solvent C 10%; 15.0 min: solvent A 50%; solvent B 40%, solvent C 10%; 19.0 min: solvent A 30%; solvent B 60%, solvent C 10%; 21.0 min: solvent B 100%; 26.0 min: solvent B 100%; 26.1 min: solvent A 81%, solvent B 9%, solvent C 10%. 30.0 min: solvent A 81%, solvent B 9%, solvent C 10%. The whole run time was 30 minutes. The flow rate was set to 0.6 mL/min.

Software. Masshunter (Version 7.0) software from Agilent (Waldbronn, Germany) was used for the processing of data generated by mass spectrometry, as well as for calculating the m/z of the molecule ion and the adducts in the different charge states.

Results and discussion

Concept of marker-based plant confirmation

The concept applied in this work, namely marker-based confirmation of a plant – the targeted detection of a predominant characteristic marker compound – is widely used and is described in the literature as a reliable tool for confirming the identity of plants in diverse matrices and likewise for marker-based confirmation of adulteration (Bononi and Tateo, 2011; Susanti et al., 2015; Thalhamer and Buchberger, 2019). Building on this general concept, the present study applied marker-based confirmation to poultry feed, where due to the expected low analytical signal intensity of Quillaja saponins, detection focused on QS-18 depicted in Fig. 2, long recognized as the predominant component in HPLC UV studies (San Martin and Briones, 2000). Poultry feed samples were analyzed in the negative electrospray ionization (ESI) mode, since in this mode the sensitivity for QS-18 was more than 10-fold higher than in the positive ESI mode. Its ionization predominantly yields two doubly charged ions: the deprotonated molecular ion ([M–2H]²⁻) and the mixed chloride adduct ([M–H+Cl]²⁻). To optimize analyte detection, the signals from both the molecular ion ([M–2H]²⁻) and the chloride adduct ([M–H+Cl]²⁻), including their ¹³C isotopologues (with up to three ¹³C atoms [C98-n(13C)nH158O51–2H]2⁻ (n = 0-3), [C98-n(13C)nH158O51–H+Cl]2⁻ (n = 0-3)), were summed up. This procedure, which takes into account all signals of the isotopic distribution, increases analytical sensitivity.Fig. 2Structural formula of QS-18 (C98H158O51).Fig 2 dummy alt text

The matching HPLC retention times between the feed matrix sample and the Quillaja reference, as well as the congruent mass spectrometric patterns of the characteristic doubly charged ions and their isotopic distributions underscore the reliability of this confirmation test.

The signal intensity of the marker observed in Fig. 4, chromatogram 4 (EIC), shows that verification is already approaching the lower end of what can still be confidently identified under the given conditions. Consequently, any attempt at quantitation would require extrapolating from this marker signal to the total saponin content.

This extrapolation is inherently impossible because the total saponin /marker ratio is unknown and cannot be determined, since the saponin input material (plant, plant part, or extract) used by the feed additive suppliers is unknown even to the feed manufacturers. In full awareness that any attempt at quantitation would inevitably remain a very rough estimation, we nevertheless attempted to assess the content. According to information provided by feed manufacturers, the saponin content is calculated to be approximately 5 ppm. From this, the concentration of the marker compound QS-18 in feed samples can be roughly derived to fall within the low hundreds of ppb and is unlikely to exceed about 250 ppb. By comparing the marker signal in the feed sample with the signal obtained after adding a defined amount of purified saponin reference material, we derived rough quantitative estimates that were in line with the approximate saponin levels indicated by the feed manufacturer (5–10 ppm saponin content).

Consistency assessment of feed sample descriptions

Table 1 gives a summary of the results for all tested poultry feed samples. In columns “Quillaja confirmed” and “Madhuca confirmed”, the results are indicated as “yes” and “no”. In column “consistency with description” a comparison of the product information by the manufacturer and our results is given. A result of “match” is assigned if Quillaja (QS-18) is confirmed. If Madhuca is confirmed in a sample the result is marked “adulterant” regardless of the presence of Quillaja. The term “not conclusively aligned” (abbreviation “n.c.a.”) indicates that, based on the method applied in this study, the analytical evidence does not unequivocally validate the corresponding additive description.

The analysis revealed that a significant proportion of the poultry feed samples, particularly those with descriptions explicitly mentioning saponins – exhibited the expected marker for Quillaja saponin. QS-18 was confirmed in seven samples, matching the description provided by manufacturers.

In five of the six samples that lacked an explicit mention of saponin and were described merely as containing flavoring substances or sensory additives, no QS-18 (Quillaja) was detected. While it cannot be entirely excluded that these samples might contain minute traces of Quillaja beyond the scope of this confirmation approach, such levels remain undetected under the applied test conditions.

The description “sensory additive” can of course not be proven with this test which focuses only on saponins. Consequently, these samples in which no saponins were measured are labeled as “not conclusively aligned” – a classification that does not necessarily imply incorrect feed information. Rather, this reflects methodological limitations.

Notably, two poultry feed samples contained saponin derived from Madhuca, clearly indicating adulteration. Although some samples tested positive for adulteration, this should not be construed as intentional adulteration by the feed manufacturer. It is more likely that the feed additive itself – prior to incorporation into feed – was the source of the adulteration.

Applicability of the confirmation test

The confirmation test for the presence of Quillaja saponin in poultry feed represents a significant technological advancement in analytical feed control. For the first time, this innovative test serves as a powerful tool for verifying the occurrence of Quillaja in poultry feed.

Despite the inherently complex composition of animal feed and the typically low inclusion rates of feed additives – with plant saponin forming only a fraction of these components – the test reliably confirmed Quillaja. This outcome not only attests to the sensitivity of the technique but also demonstrates its suitability when applied to the challenging poultry feed matrix.

This new confirmation test enables feed mixing facilities, for the first time, to verify the correct incorporation of Quillaja saponin additives into compound feed. Consequently, it not only improves existing quality control processes but also reinforces the long-term reliability and safety of feed products.

This study revealed Madhuca saponin in some poultry feed samples, unequivocally confirming adulteration by Madhuca additives. Madhuca is absent from the EU-list of feed additives and was prohibited in animal feed until 2010, as already mentioned in detail in the Introduction. Its removal from the list was based on an EFSA opinion in 2009 stating that human exposure via animal products is negligible, supported by an EU-wide data request that yielded no reported cases. This assumption of negligible exposure has proven to be incorrect, but has most likely been a result of the absence of a specific analytical technique.

The now established proof for the presence of Madhuca in poultry feed indicates possible gaps in current regulatory oversight of feed production and raises questions about the monitoring of raw materials. This test for the presence of Madhuca overcomes effectively these limitations by enabling the identification of Madhuca in animal feed. It serves as a valuable tool for enhancing transparency and promoting responsible feed production.

Conclusion

This study introduces a novel test for the verification of the presence of Quillaja in poultry feed, successfully applied for the first time in this complex feed matrix. Despite the low additive levels and intricate feed composition, the developed test reliably confirmed the presence of Quillaja demonstrating high sensitivity. The developed test constitutes a first confirmation tool for the presence of Quillaja saponin in feed. The confirmation test provides a tangible advantage for feed manufacturers by offering clarity when the composition of saponin-containing additives is not fully disclosed by additive suppliers. Beyond that the presented test may support industry and authorities to verify the presence of Quillaja saponin in feed.

This study also provides the first unambiguous evidence of Madhuca saponin in poultry feed, confirming the adulteration by Madhuca-based additives. Regulatory assumptions of negligible exposure are now proven to be incorrect, underscoring the need for tighter monitoring of botanical feed components. The developed confirmation test is well suited to reliably detect Madhuca based feed additives in compound feed, and may thus provide a valuable step towards screening larger numbers of feed samples for the presence of such Madhuca based adulterations.
