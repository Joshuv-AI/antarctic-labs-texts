Transcriptome analysis and hormone metabolic profiling provide molecular insights into flowering process in petunia
Authors: Yue Chen, Xiang Gao, Jue Chen, Yanfang Xu, Yanhua Zhou, Danqing Yao, Huibin Luo, Juan Liu, Junsong Pan, Weiwei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe flowering process is a complex biological event regulated by coordinated transcriptional programs and hormone signaling. Petunia hybrida is an important ornamental plant, yet the integrative regulatory mechanisms between hormones and gene expression during flowering remain poorly understood. In this study, flowers of the petunia cultivar ‘112’ were classified into three developmental stages based on days before anthesis: 7 days before flowering (DBF7), 4 days before flowering (DBF4), and the day of flowering (F). Integrated transcriptomic and hormone profiling analyses were conducted to systematically investigate gene expression dynamics and hormone metabolism during the flowering process.ResultsHigh-quality RNA sequencing generated over 483.36 million clean reads, with mapping rates exceeding 83.9% against the petunia reference genome. Comparative transcriptome analysis identified 7,160, 8,687, and 11,161 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in DBF4 vs. DBF7, F vs. DBF4, and F vs. DBF7, respectively. K-means clustering divided DEGs into four expression modules associated with primary metabolism, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, hormone signal transduction, and floral organ morphogenesis. Transcription factor analysis revealed that bHLH, bZIP, MADS, TCP, and WRKY families exhibited distinct stage-specific expression patterns during the flowering process. Hormone profiling revealed dynamic changes in auxin, gibberellin, jasmonate, salicylic acid, and abscisic acid contents. Integrated transcriptomic and hormone analyses showed that auxin, gibberellin, and jasmonate biosynthesis pathways were predominantly active during early developmental stages, in accordance with the expression dynamics of major biosynthetic genes (PhYUC, PhTAR, PhGA20ox, PhGA3ox, PhLOX, PhAOS, and PhOPR3). Gene–hormone network analysis revealed interconnections among phytohormone signaling pathways, particularly between jasmonate–gibberellin and abscisic acid–cytokinin, which are associated with representative genes involved in jasmonate-related lipid metabolism (PhPLA2A and PhOPR11), phenylpropanoid metabolism (PhPAL1 and PhCAMT3), and cell wall modification (PhPGLR4 and PhGUN25) during the flowering process.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive overview of the transcriptional and hormonal regulatory networks governing the flowering process in petunia. Our results reveal a stage-specific regulatory framework in which coordinated gene expression and phytohormone interactions contribute to flower development and opening, providing potential genetic resources for the improvement of ornamental traits in petunia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09062-z.

Introduction

Plant hormones are endogenous small signaling molecules. They play essential roles in regulating metabolism, growth, development, and environmental responses in plants. To date, several classes of plant hormones have been identified, including auxin (AUX), ethylene (ETH), cytokinin (CK), abscisic acid (ABA), gibberellin (GA), brassinosteroid (BR), jasmonate (JA), strigolactone (SL), and salicylic acid (SA) [1, 2]. These hormones act in a synergistic or antagonistic manner and are coordinated by key transcription factors, forming a complex regulatory network.

AUX regulates cell differentiation, polarity establishment, and the morphogenesis of petals and corollas through ARF transcription factor-mediated specific gene expression [3]. CK promotes cell proliferation in floral organs and act synergistically with GA to influence floral meristem differentiation and petal size [4]. During early floral bud development, ABA modulates flower diameter by inhibiting cell division, and its balance with CK plays a central role in regulating organ size. In addition, ABA acts antagonistically with ETH signaling to fine-tune the timing of floral organ maturation [5, 6]. GA plays a crucial role in floral bud differentiation, organ elongation, and flowering initiation by regulating DELLA proteins and downstream genes involved in cell expansion and division. In petunia, GA signaling contributes to floral organ development via modulation of the transcription factors PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTOR 4 (PhPIF4) and PhPIF5 [7]. JA and its derivatives, including methyl jasmonate (MeJA), play crucial roles in floral development, cell elongation, and the regulation of secondary metabolism [8]. GA and JA signaling interact, whereby GA relieves DELLA-mediated repression of JA-responsive transcription factors, thereby indirectly modulating downstream JA target genes. Together, these pathways coordinate cell proliferation and elongation in floral organs, ultimately influencing petal size and morphogenesis [9, 10].

Transcription factors (TFs) also regulate plant development and floral organ formation by responding to hormones and modulating hormone biosynthesis. In Arabidopsis, the TCP transcription factor TCP15 influences AUX and CK levels by regulating the expression of AUX biosynthesis genes YUCCA1 (YUC1) and YUC4, thereby ensuring proper gynoecium development [11]. The bZIP transcription factor ABA-responsive element binding protein (AREB3) mediates FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT) signaling through the ABA pathway in the shoot apical meristem, activating downstream floral organ identity genes and contributing to the floral transition [12]. In rose, the bHLH transcription factor RhMYC2 is induced by JA and regulates petal size by modulating the duration of cell division and directly controlling CK metabolism to maintain hormone balance [13]. MADS-box transcription factors APETALA1 (AP1) and AGAMOUS (AG) regulate GA and CK homeostasis, thereby determining inflorescence architecture and floral organ differentiation [14, 15].

Petunia hybrida is an important ornamental species, renowned for its diverse flower colors, varied flower forms, and unique fragrance [16]. Floral development involves a series of highly coordinated biological processes, including floral meristem differentiation, organ initiation, cell division, and organ specialization, which are regulated by a complex interplay of genetic factors, hormonal signals, and environmental cues [17].

Exogenous application of GA has been shown to significantly promote Petunia growth, accelerate flowering, and improve flower and seed quality, indicating its key role in developmental regulation during the flowering process [18]. GA signaling has been reported to interact with ethylene-mediated pathways during flower senescence, forming regulatory modules that influence floral longevity, as exemplified by GA-related regulatory factors such as PhOBF1, which modulate GA biosynthesis and antagonistically regulate ethylene-mediated senescence [19]. The ethylene-responsive transcription factor PhERF71 has been identified as a key positive regulator of petunia flower senescence by promoting ethylene biosynthesis through direct activation of 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate synthase 4 (PhACS4) and 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate oxidase 4 (PhACO4). In addition, alterations in PhERF71 expression also influence GA and ABA levels during senescence, suggesting that ethylene-mediated regulation is accompanied by coordinated hormonal changes [20]. However, the coordinated relationship between transcriptomic changes and hormone dynamics during flowering in petunia remains insufficiently understood. Studying hormonal dynamics in petunia and their interactions with transcriptional regulation provides a framework for understanding flowering regulation and supports hormone-based molecular breeding strategies.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The petunia cultivar ‘112’ plants were grown in experimental plots at the Shanghai Vocational College of Agriculture and Forestry, China. Petunia plants were cultivated under controlled conditions at 25 ℃ during the day and 20 ℃ at night, with a light intensity of 8000 lx. Flowers were harvested in January 2025 and classified into three developmental stages based on days before anthesis: 7 days before flowering (DBF7), 4 days before flowering (DBF4), and the day of flowering (F). Flower samples collected from three individual plants were pooled in equal proportions to generate one composite sample per replicate. Three independent biological replicates were generated using samples pooled from different groups of plants, thereby retaining biological variability at the replicate level. All samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis.

RNA extraction and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from flowers using an OminiPlant RNA Kit (DNase I) (CWBIO, China). Agarose gel electrophoresis was used to analyse RNA integrity, and a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) was used to measure the RNA concentration. RNA samples were treated with DNase I to remove genomic DNA and subsequently used for transcriptome sequencing. cDNA libraries were constructed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Illumina), and sequencing was performed by MetWare Metabolomics Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform.

RNA- sequencing analysis

Data quality control was performed using fastp to remove reads with adapters. Paired reads were removed under the following conditions: reads containing > 10% ambiguous bases (N) or > 50% low-quality bases (Q ≤ 20) were removed. Subsequent analyses were based on clean reads. Clean reads were mapped to the genome of Petunia axillaris using HISAT v2.2.1 (https://solgenomics.net/organism/Petunia_axillaris/genome). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the R package edgeR v4.2.2. Pairwise comparisons were conducted across the three flowering stages, and genes with an absolute Log₂ fold change (|Log₂FC|) > 1 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Gene expression abundance was normalized to fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) [21]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the Metware Cloud (https://cloud.metware.cn). K-means clustering using Euclidean distances in MeV4.8 based on input FPKM values [22]. The clusterProfiler v3.18.1 R package was used to perform Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses of DEGs from pairwise comparisons [23, 24].

qRT-PCR verification of RNA-seq data

Total RNA was extracted from flowers at the DBF7, DBF4, and F stages. Three biological replicates were prepared for each developmental stage. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) using UltraSYBR Mixture (CWBIO, China). Petunia EF1α (Peaxi162Scf00351g00412) was used as the internal reference gene. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [25]. Primers for expression analysis were designed based on the coding regions of the target genes (Table S1).

Hormone content determination

Hormone analysis was performed using a UPLC–ESI–MS/MS system. HPLC-grade solvents and hormone standards were obtained from commercial sources, and stock solutions (1 mg/mL) were prepared in methanol and stored at − 20 °C. Flowers were frozen in liquid nitrogen, ground, and extracted (50 mg) with methanol/water/formic acid (15:4:1, v/v/v) containing internal standards. After vortexing and centrifugation, the supernatant was dried, reconstituted in 80% methanol, and filtered prior to analysis. Chromatographic separation was conducted on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 column using water and acetonitrile (both with 0.04% acetic acid) at 40 °C. Mass spectrometric detection was carried out on a QTRAP 6500 instrument operating in both positive and negative ESI modes, and hormones were quantified using scheduled multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) with optimized parameters.

Hormone analysis

Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) of samples and metabolites was visualized as heatmaps with dendrograms and performed using the R package pheatmap v1.0.12 [26]. Normalized metabolite signal intensities (unit variance scaling) were displayed using a color gradient. Differentially accumulated hormones (DAHs) between groups were identified based on |Log₂FC| > 1 and a FDR < 0.05.

Integrative analysis of transcriptomic and hormone data

Correlation analysis was performed using the expression profiles of DEGs and DAHs across all samples. Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were calculated using the cor() function in R software to assess the relationships between gene expression and metabolite abundance. For network construction, gene expression and hormone abundance data from the DBF7 and F stages were integrated to identify hormone-gene association patterns. Pearson correlation analysis was performed, and correlations with |r| > 0.8 and P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The resulting gene-hormone interaction networks were visualized using Cytoscape v3.10.4 [27].

Results

Transcriptomic profiling of the flowering process

Flowers of the petunia cultivar ‘112’ were classified into three developmental stages based on days before anthesis: 7 days before flowering (DBF7), 4 days before flowering (DBF4), and the day of flowering (F) (Fig. 1A). Pigment accumulation increased progressively over time, with petals exhibiting a rose-pink color on the day of flowering. RNA was extracted from petals at the three developmental stages and subjected to RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). A total of approximately 483.36 million high-quality clean reads were obtained, generating approximately 72.51 Gb of clean data, with an average of ~ 8.06 Gb per sample. The Q30 values exceeded 96.07%, and the average GC content was 41.94%. All clean reads were subsequently mapped to the Petunia axillaris reference genome, with total mapping rates ranging from 83.91% to 85.19%, indicating high-quality transcriptome sequencing (Table S2). To validate the RNA-Seq gene expression profiles, the expression levels of 10 selected DEGs were examined using qRT-PCR. The relative expression patterns were consistent with the RNA-seq results, confirming the reliability of the sequencing data (Fig. S1).

Identification of differentially expressed genes

PCA analysis indicated good reproducibility among biological replicates within each subgroup and clear separation between groups (Fig. 1B). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were systematically compared across the three developmental stages: DBF4 vs. DBF7, F vs. DBF4, and F vs. DBF7. A total of 7,160, 8,687, and 11,161 DEGs were identified in the three comparisons, respectively, with 1,889 DEGs shared among all groups (Fig. 1C) (Table S3-S5). In the DBF4 vs. DBF7 comparison, 3,481 genes were upregulated and 3,679 genes were downregulated. In the F vs. DBF4 comparison, 3,785 genes were upregulated and 4,902 genes were downregulated. In the F vs. DBF7 comparison, 5,188 genes were upregulated and 5,973 genes were downregulated (Fig. 1D). These results indicate that, during flower opening in ‘112’, the number of upregulated DEGs was generally lower than that of downregulated genes.

Expression clustering and functional analysis

The K-means clustering approach was utilized to perform cluster analysis on all DEGs, as their expression profiles exhibited dynamic changes across distinct developmental stages. This analysis identified four DEG subgroups (Cluster 1–4) (Fig. 2A). Cluster 1 (5,373 genes) showed high expression at the DBF7 stage, while Cluster 2 (3,181 genes) was highly expressed at the DBF4 stage. The most highly enriched pathways in Clusters 1 and 2 were mainly metabolic pathways and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, also involving starch and sucrose metabolism, amino acid and nucleotide sugar biosynthesis, indicating that these genes are primarily involved in material synthesis and energy metabolism. Cluster 3 (4,223 genes) exhibited low expression at DBF7 and DBF4, but showed a significant increase at the F stage. Genes in this cluster were mainly enriched in plant hormone signal transduction, MAPK signaling pathway, and circadian rhythm-related pathways. Auxin-responsive genes PhIAA26 and PhIAA27 were significantly upregulated (approximately 2.4-fold). The abscisic acid-related genes ABA receptor PhPYL3 and ABA-insensitive protein PhABI5 also showed increased expression (approximately 2.2-fold). In addition, the GA receptor PhGID1C exhibited a more pronounced upregulation (approximately 2.9-fold). Cluster 4 (1,576 genes) was significantly enriched at DBF4 and F stages during the flowering process, and its enriched pathways mainly involved pentose and glucuronate interconversions, lipid metabolism, and cytoskeleton-related processes, indicating that these genes may be closely associated with floral organ formation and cellular function regulation (Fig. 2B, Table S6).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that genes with different expression patterns exhibited clear functional differentiation (Fig. 2C, Table S7). Clusters 1 and 2 were mainly enriched in photosynthesis, cell cycle, and cell division-related processes. Cluster 3 was significantly enriched in AUX-activated signaling pathways and stress-response processes, such as hypoxia and nutrient deprivation, implying a potential role in coordinating hormone regulation and environmental response. In contrast, Cluster 4 was mainly associated with organ morphogenesis, cell differentiation, pollen and pollen tube development, indicating its important role in flower morphogenesis and reproductive development.

Transcription factors potentially involved in the flowering process

Multiple transcription factors play important regulatory roles during plant flower development. The identification results indicated that bHLH, bZIP, MADS, TCP, and WRKY are the major transcription factor families involved in petunia flower development. In this study, a total of 60 bHLH, 34 bZIP, 31 MADS, 14 TCP, and 28 WRKY family members were identified (Fig. 3, Table S8).

Among these transcription factors, 12 bHLH members (including bHLH7 and bHLH12), eight bZIP members (including bZIP7 and bZIP17), five MADS members (including MADS10 and MADS15), four TCP members (including TCP1 and TCP5), and six WRKY members (including WRKY4 and WRKY14) exhibited consistently high expression across all three developmental stages, suggesting that these transcription factors may play continuous regulatory roles throughout the entire flowering process. In contrast, five bHLH members (such as bHLH5 and bHLH35), TCP4, and WRKY transcription factors including WRKY22 and WRKY24 showed significantly higher expression at the DBF7 and DBF4 stages, indicating that they are mainly involved in early flower development, particularly during floral bud differentiation and organ formation. In addition, eight bHLH members (such as bHLH20 and bHLH30), six bZIP members (including bZIP14 and bZIP16), and TCP7 and TCP14 were significantly upregulated at the flowering day (F stage), suggesting that these transcription factors may play important roles during flower opening, potentially by regulating floral organ elongation.

Plant hormone contents in petunia

To investigate the roles of endogenous hormones in the petunia flowering process, we measured the levels of auxin (AUX), cytokinin (CK), gibberellin (GA), jasmonate (JA), salicylic acid (SA), and abscisic acid (ABA) in flowers at three developmental stages (Fig. 4). AUX was the most abundant hormone and was significantly enriched at DBF4 (45,432.37 ng/g) and F (38,476.16 ng/g) stages. CK levels remained relatively stable across all stages. GA and ABA showed an overall declining trend during the flowering process, although ABA accumulated at DBF7 and decreased markedly at DBF4. JA levels increased initially and then decreased, whereas SA exhibited an opposite pattern.

Identification of differentially accumulated hormones

A total of 51 hormones were detected (Table S9), of which 31 were identified as differentially accumulated hormones (DAHs) across the three stage combinations (Table S10). A total of 21, 26, and 20 DAHs were identified in the three comparisons, respectively, with five hormones, JA-ILE, IP, ICA, IAA-Asp, and MeSAG, showing significant changes in all comparisons (Fig. 5A), suggesting continuous involvement of these signals during flower opening. In the DBF4 vs. DBF7 comparison, eight hormones were significantly upregulated and 13 were downregulated. In the F vs. DBF4 comparison, 14 hormones were upregulated and 12 downregulated, whereas in the F vs. DBF7 comparison, seven hormones were upregulated and 13 were downregulated (Fig. 5B). Overall, more DAHs were downregulated than upregulated during flower opening in ‘112’.

Additionally, a heatmap showing DAHs at the three developmental stages is presented (Fig. 5C). A total of 31 DAHs were classified into six hormone classes: five AUXs, 10 CKs, five GAs, seven JAs, two SAs, and two ABAs. Free ABA accumulated to the highest level at DBF4 and decreased significantly at F stage, whereas its glycosylated form, ABA-glucosyl ester (ABA-GE), accumulated at DBF7 but was nearly undetectable at later stages.

Changes in auxin and cytokinin metabolic pathways

Considering that AUXs, CKs, JAs and GAs were the four most abundant hormones detected in petunia flowers, which may be closely related to flowering, we further analyzed the number of genes associated with the biosynthesis and metabolism of these hormones.

Transcriptome analysis identified 18 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) involved in AUX biosynthesis and metabolism, including 2 TRYPTOPHAN AMINOTRANSFERASE 1 (PhTAR) genes, 2 YUCCA (PhYUC_1, PhYUC_2) genes, 1 NITRILASE (PhNIT) gene, 3 AMIDASE (PhAMI) genes, 3 GRETCHEN HAGEN 3 (PhGH3) genes, and 7 UDP-GLYCOSYLTRANSFERASE (PhUGT) genes (Fig. 6). Hormone quantification showed that tryptophan and free active auxin indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) maintained high levels during the three stages of flower development, with temporal expression patterns highly consistent with PhYUC_2 (r = 0.98) and PhNIT (r = 0.99) genes, indicating that the YUC-dependent AUX biosynthesis pathway plays a dominant role during flower development. For conjugated AUXs, IAA-Asp accumulated significantly at the DBF7 stage, whereas IAA-Val peaked at the F stage, consistent with the expression patterns of PhGH3 genes. In addition, IAA-Glc was significantly enriched at the DBF4 stage, corresponding to the high expression of PhUGT_4 (r = 0.99) . Overall, most genes involved in AUX biosynthesis and metabolism (PhTARs, PhGH3s, and PhUGTs) showed a gradual decline during the flowering process, suggesting that AUX mainly functions in the early stages of flower development.

Transcriptome analysis also identified 14 DEGs involved in CK biosynthesis and metabolism, including 1 ISOPENTENYL TRANSFERASE (PhIPT) gene, 9 LONELY GUY (PhLOG) genes, and 4 NUCLEOSIDE HYDROLASE (PhNSH) genes, showing clear stage-specific expression patterns (Fig. 6). Metabolite analysis detected multiple active CK forms and their inactive derivatives. iPRMP, tZPMP, and cZRMP maintained high abundance across the three developmental stages. Downstream metabolites of tZPMP, including tZOG and tZ9G, showed relatively stable levels during flower development, which was moderately correlated with PhLOG_7 expression (r = 0.78). The active cytokinins tZ, cZ, and IP were present at low levels, indicating that active CKs exhibit limited fluctuation throughout the flowering process (Fig. 6).

Changes in gibberellin and jasmonate metabolic pathways

Transcriptome analysis identified 18 DEGs involved in GA biosynthesis and metabolism, including 1 ent-COPALYL DIPHOSPHATE SYNTHASE (PhCPS) gene, 1 ent-KAURENE SYNTHASE (PhKS) gene, 1 ent-KAURENE OXIDASE (PhKO) gene, 4 ent-KAURENOIC ACID OXIDASE (KAO) genes, 5 GIBBERELLIN 20-OXIDASE (PhGA20ox) genes, 2 GIBBERELLIN 3-OXIDASE (GA3ox) genes, and 4 GIBBERELLIN 2-OXIDASE (PhGA2ox) genes (Fig. 7). Metabolite profiling showed that GA precursors such as GA53, GA44, GA19, and GA20 accumulated mainly at the DBF7 stage, consistent with the expression of early GA biosynthesis genes including CPS, KS, KAO, and some GA20ox genes (PhGA20ox3, PhGA20ox4, PhGA20ox5), which collectively catalyze the sequential oxidation from GA53 to GA20. GA20 is primarily synthesized from GA53 via C-13 hydroxylation, and GA53 itself is a C-13 hydroxylated derivative of GA12, indicating that GA biosynthesis is active in the early stages of the flowering process. Specifically, PhGA20ox3 and PhGA20ox5 showed strong positive correlations with GA44 (r = 0.89 and 0.84, respectively) and GA20 (r = 0.79 and 0.73, respectively), while GA20ox4 displayed an almost perfect positive correlation with GA19 (r = 1.00). These results highlight a potential association between a subset of GA20ox genes and GA precursor accumulation during early flower development.

Transcriptome analysis also identified 20 DEGs involved in jasmonate (JA) biosynthesis and metabolism, including 2 PHOSPHOLIPASE A1 (PhPLA1) genes, 2 PHOSPHOLIPASE A2 (PhPLA2) genes, 7 LIPOXYGENASE (PhLOX) genes, 3 ALLENE OXIDE SYNTHASE (PhAOS) genes, 2 ALLENE OXIDE CYCLASE (PhAOC) genes, 2 ABNORMAL INFLORESCENCE MERISTEM 1 (PhAIM1) genes, 1 12-OXOPHYTODIENOATE REDUCTASE 3 (PhOPR3) gene, and 1 JASMONATE-RESISTANT 1 (PhJAR) gene (Fig. 7). Metabolite analysis detected precursors and active forms including 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid (OPDA), JA, and JA-Ile, which accumulated predominantly at the early DBF7 and DBF4 stages. OPDA showed strong positive correlations with PLA1_1, LOX4/5 and AOS2 (r = 0.97-1.00). JA content was also positively correlated with PhOPR3 expression (r = 0.80), while JA-Ile exhibited an almost perfect positive correlation with PhJAR1 (r = 0.99). These results imply coordinated changes at both the transcriptional and metabolic levels in the JA biosynthetic pathway during early flower development.

Network analysis of transcription factors responding to different hormones

Based on the Cytoscape regulatory network, jasmonic acid (JA) and gibberellin (GA) were associated with 32 genes (Fig. 8A, Table S11). Among these, a prominent functional module was associated with cell wall remodeling, such as Polygalacturonase (PhPGLR4), Endoglucanase 25 (PhGUN25), Beta-xylosidase/alpha-L-arabinofuranosidase 2 (PhXYL2) and Mannan endo-1,4-β-mannosidase 6/7 (PhMAN6/7). In addition, key components of jasmonate-related lipid metabolism, including 12-oxophytodienoate reductase 11 (PhOPR11) and Phospholipase A2-alpha (PhPLA2A), were identified. Several genes involved in secondary metabolism, including 4-coumarate–CoA ligase 2 (Ph4CL2) and Glutathione S-transferase (PhGSTF), were also detected. Similarly, ABA and isopentenyladenine-type CK (IPR) were linked with 15 genes (Fig. 8B, Table S11), which were mainly enriched in phenylpropanoid metabolism, including phenylalanine ammonia-lyase 1 (PhPAL1), Acetyl-CoA-benzylalcohol acetyltransferase (PhBEATH) and Caffeoyl-CoA O-methyltransferase 3 (PhCAMT3). Additional genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism Beta-amylase 3 (PhBAM3), Beta-glucosidase 11 (PhBAGL11). Together, these results suggest that hormone-associated gene networks are linked to key metabolic processes that may be important for floral development.

Discussion

Flowers are essential organs in angiosperms, and flowering time is a critical determinant of both ornamental and economic value. Flowering is primarily regulated by environmental cues and endogenous hormonal signals. In this study, transcriptomic and hormone profiling analyses were performed on floral organs of petunia at three developmental stages, providing insights into the regulatory mechanisms underlying flowering in petunia.

Analysis of DEGs across developmental stages revealed that the largest differences occurred between the DBF7 and F stages, while differences between DBF4 and F were moderate. Genes in Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 were highly expressed at the DBF7 and DBF4 stages, respectively, and were enriched in primary metabolic pathways, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, and starch/sucrose metabolism. This indicates that early floral development relies on enhanced carbon metabolism and energy supply to support cell division and organ growth. Such metabolic features are common during floral meristem differentiation and organ formation [28, 29]. In contrast, genes in Cluster 3 were generally lowly expressed during DBF4 and DBF7 but were significantly upregulated at the F stage, with enrichment in plant hormone signaling, MAPK pathways, and circadian rhythm-related processes. This pattern suggests that floral opening is accompanied by the coordinated activation of hormonal signaling and environmental rhythm cues, consistent with the known roles of AUX in organ elongation and flower opening [30–32]. The functional divergence among expression clusters indicates that petunia flower development transitions from early stages dominated by primary metabolism and resource accumulation to later stages characterized by enhanced hormone signaling and regulatory networks, enabling rapid organ elongation and precise control of flower opening.

TFs play central roles in diverse plant growth and developmental processes [33]. Five TF families associated with floral development were identified, including bHLH, bZIP, MADS-box, TCP, and WRKY families. Among these, the bHLH family represented the largest group of DEGs and was implicated in multiple aspects of floral development. PhFBH4, a bHLH transcription factor, is upregulated during flower senescence in petunia, with its transcription induced by both hormones and abiotic stress. Silencing PhFBH4 prolongs flower longevity, while overexpression accelerates senescence. PhFBH4 binds the G-box element in the ACS1 promoter to regulate ethylene biosynthetic genes ACS1 and ACO1, modulating ethylene production and controlling flower senescence [34]. bZIP and TCP TFs have also been reported to regulate flowering time. bZIP transcription factors, including ABA Insensitive 5 (ABI5) and Abre binding factors (ABFs), are involved in the ABA-dependent regulation of flowering timing [35]. The petunia homolog, PhbZIP18, showed a continuous increase in expression during flower development, suggesting that it may play an important role in the late stages of floral development. Overexpression of TCP8 significantly delays flowering in Arabidopsis under both long-day and short-day conditions [36]. Its homologous gene in petunia, PhTCP2, is highly expressed at the DBF7 stage, indicating a potential role in the early stage of flower development. WRKY transcription factors also contribute to plant development and stress responses. They are involved in hormone pathways, such as ABA and AUX, to modulate flowering time. OsWRKY72 from rice indirectly activates genes in the AUX pathway and ABA pathway to promote flowering [37]. MADS-box transcription factors form the classical ABCDE model of floral development, determining floral organ identity and reproductive meristem characteristics. In petunia, B-class gene DEFICIENS (PhDEF) affects petal morphology; C and D class genes regulate stamen and carpel development as well as floral meristem termination; E-class gene FBP2 forms complexes with other MADS-box proteins to control petal, stamen, and carpel development [38, 39]. In our study, most MADS-box genes maintained low expression across the three stages, possibly because differentiation had already been completed prior to sampling.

Hormones are key regulators of flowering time and floral organ development in model plants. AUX, CK, GA, JA, and SA are generally considered promotive for flowering, whereas ETH and ABA are inhibitory [40–42]. AUX is regarded as a core hormone in floral development and organ morphogenesis, particularly during floral primordium formation and organ elongation [43]. In this study, AUX levels remained high across all stages, and their dynamics were closely aligned with the expression of key YUCCA-dependent biosynthetic genes, supporting a sustained regulatory role of AUX during petunia flower development. Conjugated AUXs (IAA-Asp, IAA-Val, IAA-Glc) exhibited stage-specific accumulation, consistent with expression patterns of GH3 and UGT genes. Previous studies have indicated that GH3- and UGT-mediated AUX inactivation or sequestration is crucial for maintaining AUX homeostasis and preventing excessive signaling [44]. These findings suggest that petunia fine-tunes floral development by regulating the dynamic balance between free and conjugated AUX.

GA is closely associated with organ elongation and flowering initiation. In this study, GA precursors (GA53, GA44, GA19, GA20) accumulated significantly at DBF7, consistent with high expression of PhCPS, PhKS, PhKAO, and PhGA20ox genes, indicating active GA biosynthesis in early flower development. GA promotes rapid organ growth by regulating cell elongation and cell wall loosening, aligning with our findings [45]. Active GA1 levels remained lower than precursors, consistent with previous reports of GA biosynthetic dynamics (GA19 > GA20 > GA1) [42]. JA is also crucial for floral organ development, anther maturation, and floral scent biosynthesis. In Arabidopsis, mutation of the JA biosynthesis gene OPR3 leads to male sterility [46], while upstream enzymes including LOX, AOS, and AOC are essential for proper anther maturation [47, 48]. JA also modulates sugar metabolism to influence cell wall composition and relaxation, thereby controlling diurnal flower opening [49]. We found that JA and its bioactive form JA-Ile accumulated significantly at the DBF7 and DBF4 stages, in parallel with the expression of key genes involved in JA biosynthesis, highlighting a potential role of JA signaling during early flower development in petunia. JA and GA exhibit antagonistic interactions and coordinately regulate plant growth and defense processes. Consistent with this conserved regulatory framework, our network analysis revealed that JA and GA jointly regulate genes that can be functionally grouped into several key biological processes. Notably, a substantial proportion of these genes are associated with cell wall remodeling. Cell wall-modifying enzymes such as polygalacturonases, endoglucanases, and mannanases play central roles in cell wall loosening and restructuring, thereby facilitating cell expansion and organ growth [49–51]. In rose, cell wall composition and related enzyme activities are closely associated with petal development and blooming [50]. Moreover, male flower-specific expression of genes encoding polygalacturonase, pectin methylesterase, and β-1,3-glucanase has been reported in dioecious willow [51]. Consistently, the tomato endo-β-mannanase gene LeMAN5 is specifically expressed in anthers and pollen, with peak expression occurring during pollen maturation [52]. Genes involved in secondary metabolism were also significantly enriched in the hormone-associated network. The phenylpropanoid pathway, which is highly conserved across higher plants, plays a central role in anthocyanin biosynthesis and floral pigmentation. In species such as petunia and cotton, 4CL acts as a key metabolic node in the phenylpropanoid pathway, regulating the formation of both lignin precursors and floral volatile phenylpropenes [53, 54], while GST family proteins are required for anthocyanin transport and vacuolar sequestration. These findings suggest that JA–GA crosstalk may coordinately regulate not only structural development but also key ornamental traits through conserved metabolic pathways [55].

CK levels were the most abundant among detected hormones but showed little variation across stages, suggesting a role in maintaining cell division potential rather than directly inducing flower opening. This pattern aligns with observations in tobacco and rice, where CK primarily maintains floral organ activity [56, 57]. ABA participates in diverse developmental processes, including seed storage protein and lipid synthesis, drought tolerance, dormancy, and flowering. Here, free ABA accumulated to the highest level at the DBF4 stage and declined markedly at the F stage. In contrast, ABA-glucosyl ester (ABA-GE), the conjugated form of ABA, accumulated predominantly at DBF7 and was nearly absent in the subsequent stages. Although ABA is generally considered a senescence-promoting hormone, its decrease on the day of flowering in petunia may relieve inhibition of organ elongation, allowing rapid expansion of petals and stamens [58]. In various plant biological processes, antagonistic interactions between ABA and CK signaling pathways play important regulatory roles. Recent studies have shown that cytokinin-induced Arabidopsis response regulators (ARRs) can inhibit ABA responses by interacting with and promoting the degradation of ABI5, highlighting the reciprocal regulation between these two hormonal pathways [59]. In the present study, The co-regulation of these genes by ABA and cytokinin suggests a coordinated role of these hormones in modulating metabolic reprogramming during flower development. Several genes, such as PhPAL1, PhCAMT3, and PhBEATH, are annotated as homologs of genes involved in phenylpropanoid metabolism [60–62]. This indicates that ABA-CK crosstalk may participate in regulating secondary metabolic pathways that contribute to floral traits.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that petunia flowering process is orchestrated through the coordinated interplay of multiple TFs and phytohormone signaling pathways. A total of 167 floral development–associated TFs and 51 phytohormones, together with 70 related biosynthetic genes, exhibited distinct stage-specific expression patterns. Hormone regulatory network analysis identified PhOPR11, Ph4CL2, PhPAL1, and PhGSTX6 as key regulatory nodes in endogenous hormone-mediated control of petunia flowering process. However, it should be noted that the present study is primarily based on integrative transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses, and the identified relationships remain correlative. Future studies involving genetic transformation, gene knockout or overexpression, and hormone manipulation experiments will be required to validate these findings. Overall, this study provides a comprehensive framework for understanding hormone-associated regulatory networks during petunia flowering and offers a valuable resource for future functional studies and molecular breeding strategies.
