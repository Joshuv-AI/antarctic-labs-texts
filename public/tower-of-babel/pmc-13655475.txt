Clean Hands: From Semmelweis to Surgical Responsibility—Historical Foundations and Contemporary Implications for Infection Control
Authors: Kun Hwang
Published: 2026

Dear Editor:

Hand hygiene remains one of the most effective and cost-effective measures for preventing healthcare-associated infections and remains the cornerstone of infection prevention worldwide [1]. Nevertheless, adherence among healthcare workers remains inconsistent despite well-established guidelines and monitoring systems [2,3]. This paradox suggests that compliance depends not only on scientific knowledge and institutional protocols but also on professional identity and behavioral attitudes.

For surgeons, however, the preoperative scrub represents more than technical preparation. It functions as a deliberate transition from ordinary activity to professional responsibility, marking entry into the sterile field. We propose that this symbolic dimension may partly explain why the preoperative surgical scrub is typically performed with greater consistency and deliberateness than routine point-of-care hand hygiene, and that extending this sense of responsibility to everyday clinical practice may strengthen adherence beyond protocols alone.

The historical evolution of hand hygiene illustrates this relationship between science and professional responsibility. In 1847, Ignaz Semmelweis demonstrated that handwashing with chlorinated lime dramatically reduced maternal mortality from puerperal fever [4]. His observations linked physician contact—particularly after autopsy work—to the transmission of lethal infection. Resistance to his recommendations reflected not only physicians' reluctance to accept themselves as vectors of disease but also the absence of an accepted germ theory and the prevailing medical paradigms of the time. Nevertheless, Semmelweis established one of the earliest demonstrations that patient safety depended directly on clinicians' own behavior.

Joseph Lister later transformed these observations into scientific practice by introducing antiseptic surgery based on germ theory, using carbolic acid to disinfect wounds, instruments, and surgeons' hands [5]. Hand hygiene thereby became standardized, reproducible, and firmly grounded in microbiological evidence rather than individual conviction [6].

Modern infection-control guidelines emphasize proper technique, timing, and indications for hand hygiene. Yet compliance remains imperfect, with workload, perceived risk, organizational culture, and behavioral factors continuing to influence adherence [3]. Although adherence to recommended scrub technique and duration is not flawless, the preoperative scrub is generally performed more consistently than routine hand hygiene because it is embedded within the operative workflow and reinforces the surgeon's assumption of responsibility before entering the sterile field. Evidence also indicates that surgical hand antisepsis remains an important component of strategies to reduce surgical site infection [7].

This transition may have educational implications. Rather than presenting hand hygiene solely as a procedural requirement, infection-control training might emphasize it as the moment clinicians consciously assume responsibility for protecting patients from avoidable harm. Such framing may complement existing protocols by strengthening professional identity alongside technical competence.

Although hand hygiene is fundamentally a biomedical intervention, the idea of "clean hands" also carries enduring cultural and psychological meanings that illuminate professional responsibility. In the biblical account, Pontius Pilate washes his hands to distance himself from responsibility for Jesus' condemnation (Matthew 27:24). In historical and theological scholarship, Pilate's handwashing has traditionally been interpreted as a symbolic renunciation of moral responsibility [8].

In clinical practice, by contrast, hand hygiene signifies the acceptance rather than the avoidance of responsibility for patient safety. The same physical act thus conveys opposite ethical meanings.

Lady Macbeth's compulsive handwashing ("Out, damned spot!") illustrates that perceived contamination can shape behavior even when no visible stain exists [9]. Likewise, infection control requires clinicians to respond to microorganisms that remain invisible despite their profound clinical consequences.

Similarly, in Ian McEwan's Atonement, Briony's confidence despite incomplete understanding leads to lasting harm [10]. Her actions illustrate how certainty unsupported by adequate evidence can produce unintended consequences. In clinical settings, overconfidence or underestimation of contamination risks may likewise compromise adherence to infection-control practices.

The preoperative surgical scrub demonstrates that hand hygiene can function simultaneously as an evidence-based infection-control measure and as a ritual reaffirming professional responsibility. Extending this perspective to routine clinical hand hygiene may strengthen adherence by linking everyday practice with clinicians' commitment to protecting patients. Integrating this broader understanding into infection-control education may therefore complement existing guidelines and contribute to safer healthcare.
