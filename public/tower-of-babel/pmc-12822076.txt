Chest tube removal after cardiac surgery in first vs. second post-operative day: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mohamed Saad Rakab, Ameer Awashra, Nada Radwan, Abdalhakim Shubietah, Alaa Maamoun, Mohab Mattar, AlMothana Manasrah, Abdelrahman Ghanem, Abdelrahman Elbaz, Ali Emara, Hamza A. Abdul-Hafez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe optimal timing of chest tube removal after cardiac surgery remains uncertain, with institutional practices varying widely. While early removal may promote faster recovery, concerns persist regarding potential complications such as fluid accumulation. This meta-analysis compares early versus late chest tube removal and their effects on clinical and procedural outcomes.MethodsWe searched Web of Science, SCOPUS, PubMed, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials until May 2025. Nine studies involving 8,557 patients were included. Pooled risk ratios (RR) and mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated using either random-effects or fixed-effects models.ResultsEarly chest tube removal was associated with a significantly higher risk of pericardial effusion (RR 1.85, p = 0.0105), while pleural effusion showed an increasing trend with marginally significant difference (RR 1.19, p = 0.0584). Hospital stay was shorter in the early group (MD -1.00 days, p < 0.0001), yet ICU stay showed no meaningful difference (MD -7.25 h, p = 0.217). Mortality rates were comparable (RR 0.93, p = 0.8048). Similarly, no significant differences were observed in postoperative infection (RR 0.85, p = 0.4403) or atrial fibrillation rates (RR 1.01, p = 0.8649).ConclusionEarly chest tube removal appears safe with respect to mortality, infection, and atrial fibrillation, but is associated with a higher risk of pericardial effusion; adoption should therefore be risk-stratified and paired with post-removal surveillance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-025-03772-5.

Introduction

Cardiac surgery is performed in over a million patients each year, and this number is expected to grow with the aging population and rising rates of coronary and valvular disease [1]. To prevent critical complications like cardiac tamponade or excessive pleural fluid accumulation, most patients leave surgery with mediastinal and/or pleural chest tubes in place. These drains help prevent retained-blood syndrome, a postoperative complication caused by the accumulation of undrained mediastinal or pericardial blood and clots. This condition can increase intrathoracic pressure, impair ventricular filling, and trigger a strong inflammatory response, leading to complications such as cardiac tamponade and postoperative atrial fibrillation [2, 3].

Despite their routine use, the optimal timing for drain removal remains a subject of ongoing debate. Keeping drains in place for too long can hinder patient mobility, increase reliance on opioids, and slow recovery. Conversely, removing them too soon may result in hidden fluid or blood accumulation. Recent studies indicate significant variability in removal times, ranging from under 10 h to over 48 h, stressing the need for clear evidence-based and universal guidelines [4]. Enhanced Recovery after Surgery (ERAS) recommendations no longer prescribe a fixed timeline for chest tube removal; instead, they advocate removal as early as is safely feasible, contingent on stable hemostasis and absence of air leaks [5]. However, these recommendations are based on limited-quality evidence, leaving the best timing for drain removal still under debate.

To clarify these issues, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the impact of early (postoperative day (POD) ≤ 1) versus late chest tube removal after adult cardiac surgery. By analyzing data from randomized and observational studies, we aimed to address ongoing uncertainties, inform clinical guidelines, and identify areas needing further research.

Methodology

Protocol registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [6] and the recommendations outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [7]. The review protocol was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database under the registration number (ID: 1108213).

Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across four electronic databases: PubMed (MEDLINE), Scopus, Web of Science (WoS), and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) until May 2025. Search strategies were customized for each database using a combination of relevant keywords and medical subject headings (MeSH terms), with the complete search strategies provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Eligibility criteria and study selection

Studies were considered eligible if they met the predefined PICOS criteria. The population of interest included adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) who underwent cardiac surgery. The intervention of interest was early removal of chest tubes ≤ POD1 (≤ 24 h), compared with late removal as the control ≥ POD2 (> 24 h). The primary outcome was the incidence of pericardial effusion. Secondary outcomes included incidence of pleural effusion, pneumothorax, mortality, chest tube drainage volume, length of stay in the intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital, time to chest tube removal, postoperative atrial fibrillation, and postoperative infection rates.

Studies were excluded if they were book chapters, non-human or in vitro experiments, reports with overlapping or duplicate datasets, reviews, commentaries, editorials, clinical guidelines, or if they were not published in English.

Study selection process

Study selection was conducted in a two-phase process using Covidence software. Initially, duplicate records were identified manually and removed. Two reviewers (N.R. and A.M.) then independently screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining studies for potential eligibility. Full texts of all studies deemed potentially relevant were subsequently retrieved and assessed in accordance with the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria by two reviewers (A.G. and A.E.). Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a senior reviewer (M.S.R.).

Data extraction

Once eligible studies were identified, a pilot data extraction was conducted to refine and validate the extraction form, which was structured using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., USA). The final form comprised three main sections.

The first section captured general study characteristics, including study identifier, country of origin, study design, total sample size, data source, timing of early and late chest tube removal, criteria for chest tube removal, key inclusion criteria, primary outcome, and duration of follow-up. The second section included baseline patient characteristics such as age, sex, body mass index, ejection fraction, history of hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, atrial fibrillation, previous percutaneous coronary intervention, CABG, and valve surgery. The third section contained data on clinical outcomes, including the incidence of pericardial and pleural effusion, pneumothorax, mortality, chest tube drainage volume, length of stay in the ICU and hospital, time to chest tube removal, postoperative atrial fibrillation, and postoperative infection rates.

Data extraction was independently performed by two reviewers (A.E. and A.E.E.), and any discrepancies were resolved through consensus with input from a senior author when necessary (M.S.R.).

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

Risk of bias was independently assessed by two reviewers (N.R. and A.M.) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2) tool [8] for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [9] for non-randomized studies. For RCTs, five domains were evaluated: bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. NOS [9] evaluates study quality across three domains: selection of study groups, comparability of groups, and ascertainment of either the outcome (for cohort studies) or exposure (for case-control studies). Any disagreements in the assessment were resolved through discussion, with arbitration by a senior reviewer if required (M.S.R.).

Statistical analysis

For modeling and visualization, we used R version 4.4.2, with "MetaInsight", and "Meta-Mar"; for sensitivity analysis, we used PQStat version 1.8. Risk ratio (RR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) were used to report dichotomous data and mean difference (MD) was used for continuous outcomes. However, the random-effects model was used when there was a heterogeneity of I² > 50%; Otherwise, fixed-effects model was used. To assess heterogeneity, we employed the Chi-square and I-square tests; the Chi-square test establishes whether heterogeneity exists, and the I-square test establishes the degree of heterogeneity. Significant heterogeneity was defined as an I-square of greater than 50% and considerable heterogeneity as an alpha level of less than 0.1 for the Chi-square test, according to the Cochrane Handbook (chapter ten) [7]. If heterogeneity exceeded 50% (I² > 50%), we applied a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, omitting each study in turn, to examine whether the pooled effect was overly influenced by any single trial.

Results

A total of 1,074 records were initially retrieved through comprehensive systematic searches of the electronic databases.. No additional records were identified through supplementary sources. During the initial screening phase, 579 records were identified as duplicates. Subsequently, 495 records proceeded to title and abstract screening, resulting in the exclusion of 480 records. Full texts of the remaining 15 articles were successfully retrieved and assessed for eligibility.

Of these, six studies were excluded, three due to irrelevant outcomes and three due to inappropriate study design. Ultimately, nine studies met the predefined inclusion criteria and were included in the final systematic review [10–18] (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

A total of nine studies comprising 8,557 patients from five countries were included in this analysis [10–18]. These investigations assessed the clinical outcomes associated with early versus late chest tube removal following cardiac surgery, including CABG, valve surgery, or combined procedures. The included studies employed diverse methodological designs, including retrospective cohort studies, prospective observational studies, and two cluster randomized controlled trials. Definitions of early and late removal varied among studies but were generally based on institutional protocols, drainage output thresholds, and hemodynamic stability, with early removal typically occurring within the first POD and late removal on or after POD two. Follow-up durations ranged from the index hospitalization to up to three months post-discharge. Baseline characteristics across study populations were largely comparable. Reported mean ages ranged from 55 to 70 years, with a predominance of male patients (66%–87%). Available data indicated similar body mass index (BMI; approximately 27–28 kg/m²), left ventricular ejection fraction (51%–62%), and prevalence of common comorbidities, including hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, atrial fibrillation, and prior percutaneous coronary intervention. CABG was the most frequently performed surgical procedure across the cohorts. A comprehensive overview of study characteristics and baseline patient data is provided in Tables 1 and 2.

Pericardial and pleural effusions

Eight studies reported both pleural effusions and pericardial effusions. Pooled analysis of pleural effusions was marginally higher in the early group but did not reach statistical significance (8% vs. 4.7%; RR 1.19, 95% CI 0.99 to 1.42, p = 0.0584; I² = 26.6%) (Fig. 2a). In contrast, the risk of pericardial effusions showed a significantly higher risk in the early group compared to the late group (2.27% vs. 1.1%; RR 1.85, 95% CI 1.15 to 2.95, p = 0.0105; I² = 0%) (Fig. 2b).

Hospital stay, ICU stay, and mortality

Meta-analysis of hospital length of stay demonstrated a significantly shorter duration in the early intervention group compared to the late group (2 studies); MD −1.00 days, 95% CI −1.13 to −0.87, p < 0.0001; I² = 0%) (Fig. 3a). In contrast, the difference in ICU stay duration between groups was negligible and not statistically significant (3 studies); MD −7.25 h, 95% CI −18.77 to 4.26, p = 0.217; I² = 99.5%) (Fig. 3b). Pooled analysis of mortality rates revealed no significant difference between early and late groups (3 studies); 1.58% vs. 1.59%; RR 0.93, 95% CI 0.53 to 1.64, p = 0.8048; I² = 0%) (Fig. 3c).

Adverse events

No significant difference was observed in postoperative infection rates between early and late groups (4 studies); 10.7% vs. 14.7%; RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.55 to 1.29, p = 0.4403; I² = 71.9%) (Fig. 4a). Sensitivity analysis revealed that omitting Darras (2024) completely resolved heterogeneity (I2 = 0, p = 0.864) (Supplementary Fig. 2). Similarly, the incidence of postoperative atrial fibrillation was comparable between early and late groups (3 studies); 30.8% vs. 30.4%; RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.91 to 1.12, p = 0.8649; I² = 0%) (Fig. 4b).

Drainage volume and timing

Meta-analysis demonstrated significantly lower chest tube drainage volumes in the early group compared to the late group (4 studies); MD −189.06 mL, 95% CI −216.62 to −161.50, p < 0.0001) (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Heterogeneity was considerable across studies (I² = 96.9%). Concurrently, time to chest tube removal was significantly shorter in the early group compared to the late group (5 studies); MD −18.77 h, 95% CI −23.78 to −13.77, p < 0.0001; I² = 93.1%) (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Heterogeneity in both outcomes could not be resolved by sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Figs. 3 & 4).

Subgroup analyses by study design

For pleural effusions, the RR was 1.03 (95% CI 0.80 to 1.33) among RCTs and 1.36 (95% CI 1.06 to 1.75) among observational studies, with no significant subgroup difference (p = 0.1286) (Supplementary Fig. 5). Regarding pericardial effusions, the RR was 1.23 (95% CI 0.32 to 4.64) in RCTs and 1.97 (95% CI 1.19 to 3.26) in observational studies, again with no significant subgroup interaction (p = 0.5140) (Supplementary Fig. 6). Time to chest tube removal was significantly shorter in early interventions across both RCTs (MD − 15.76 h, 95% CI − 23.38 to − 8.13) and observational studies (MD − 17.91 h, 95% CI − 25.26 to − 10.55), with consistent effect across subgroups (p = 0.6907) (Supplementary Fig. 7). Similarly, chest tube drainage volume favored early intervention in both RCTs (MD − 178.59 mL, 95% CI − 299.73 to − 57.45) and observational studies (MD − 211.89 mL, 95% CI − 325.41 to − 98.37), with no subgroup interaction detected (p = 0.6942) (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

Risk of bias was assessed in the four RCTs of the nine included studies using the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool. Three studies (Gercekoglu 2003 [13], Abramov 2005 [18], and Mirmohammad-Sadeghi 2009 [15]) had some concerns related to the randomization process, but were otherwise at low risk of bias across all other domains. The fourth study (El-Akkawi 2024 [12]) was judged to have low risk of bias in all domains. None of the studies were rated as having high risk of bias (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Risk of bias in the five included observational studies was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS). All studies demonstrated low risk of bias, with total NOS scores ranging from 8 to 9 out of a maximum of 9 (Supplementary Table 2). Three studies (Andreasen (2016) [10], Khan (2019) [14], and Darras (2024) [11]) achieved full scores across all domains, indicating high methodological rigor, particularly in ensuring adequate cohort comparability and outcome ascertainment. The remaining two studies (Mueller (2000) [16] and Zurek (2021) [17]) received 8 stars, reflecting strong performance in cohort selection and outcome assessment, though with minor limitations in comparability or follow-up adequacy. All studies clearly defined exposure and demonstrated that outcomes of interest were not present at the start of the study. The representativeness of the exposed cohort and adequacy of follow-up were consistently rated as high across all studies. These findings suggest that the observational evidence included in this review is of generally high quality, with minimal risk of systematic bias. Discussion

In this meta-analysis, we tested the hypothesis that early chest tube removal influences postoperative outcomes following cardiac surgery. Overall, we found that early chest tube removal was associated with a significantly higher risk of pericardial effusion, yet it led to shorter hospital stays. Other outcomes, including pleural effusion, ICU length of stay, mortality, postoperative infections, and atrial fibrillation, showed no statistically significant differences between early and late removal groups. Subgroup analyses across RCTs and observational studies confirmed the consistency and reliability of these findings.

The most notable complication associated with early chest tube removal was pericardial effusion. Consistently, our pooled analysis revealed that early chest tube removal was associated with a significantly elevated risk of pericardial effusion (RR = 1.85; 95% CI: 1.15–2.95). Supporting this, Khan et al. demonstrated that prolonging mediastinal chest tube drainage until at least the second POD significantly reduced the incidence of clinically relevant late cardiac tamponade from 8.8% to 3.6%, without a concomitant increase in pleurocentesis (14% vs. 19%, p = 0.17) or other major postoperative complications. In contrast, the observed increase in pleural effusions among early removal patients did not reach statistical significance (RR = 1.19; 95% CI: 0.99–1.42), suggesting a more selective vulnerability of the pericardial space to premature drainage cessation. This can be explained by the fact that early chest tube removal interrupts the − 10 to − 20 cm H₂O suction generated by underwater-seal or wall-vacuum systems that normally clear ongoing haemostatic ooze after cardiac surgery [19]. Without this negative pressure, fluid gravitates to dependent spaces; the pericardial cavity is most vulnerable because posterior drains are rarely placed and its limited lymphatic outflow cannot compensate, so even small volumes quickly produce effusion or tamponade [20]. By contrast, the pleural space can up-regulate lymphatic absorption by up to forty-fold before fluid visibly accumulates, buffering the effect of early drain removal and explaining why our meta-analysis found only a borderline non-statistically significant rise in pleural effusions versus a significant increase in pericardial effusions [21, 22].

Although late complications were not analyzable meta-analytically, we observed that extended drainage (≥ POD2) was associated with a lower incidence of late tamponade compared with a short protocol (management typically by percutaneous drainage or surgical fenestration; events occurred chiefly after discharge) in a prospective cohort [14]. Given sparse and nonuniform reporting across studies, we did not pool late outcomes and instead highlighted them as a target for future standardized reporting.

Our meta-analysis showed that removing chest drains on or before the first POD reduced total hospital stay by one day, without affecting ICU stay or early mortality. This finding aligns with Darras et al., who showed that the POD-1 removal protocol reduced median ward stay from 7 to 6 days, while ICU stay and hospital mortality remained unchanged [11]. Similarly, the randomized trial by Mirmohammad-Sadeghi et al. found no difference in ICU time or mortality between patients who had drains removed within 24 h versus on day two [15]. In contrast, Khan et al. found no benefit in hospital stay or mortality when comparing first-day removal to prolonged drainage beyond 48 h, suggesting that the benefit is specific to truly early protocols rather than simply not delaying removal [14]. This may be explained by the effects of the drain itself. Once removed, patients are able to walk sooner, perform breathing exercises more effectively, and switch to oral pain medication, these factors help speed up discharge from the ward [12, 23]. In contrast, ICU discharge depends mainly on clinical stability including heart function, lung status, and absence of complications, which are not directly affected by drain timing. Mortality also remains unchanged, as early drain removal improves recovery speed without increasing serious complications [19, 24].

Interestingly, we found that no significant difference in postoperative infection rates between very-early and conventional chest tube removal (RR = 0.85; 95% CI: 0.55–1.29). The heterogeneity was primarily due to the cohort by Darras et al., which reported lower bronchopneumonia rates with POD-1 removal (9% vs. 13.5%, P = 0.001) [11]. Excluding this study resolved heterogeneity without altering the overall neutral effect. Most other trials, including Moss et al., found no differences in drain-related infections, consistent with low baseline risk due to sterile technique and antibiotic prophylaxis [25]. There was also no significant difference in postoperative atrial fibrillation (RR = 1.01; 95% CI: 0.91–1.12; I² = 0%). Moss et al. reported nearly identical rates whether drains were removed on day 1 or day 5 (47% vs. 46%, P = 0.89). Other studies, including Darras et al. and a recent Scandinavian trial, confirmed this finding [11]. The pathophysiology of AF is mainly results from inflammation in the pericardium and the presence of retained blood, rather than when the chest tube is removed. When blood accumulates in the mediastinum or pericardium, it creates a pro-inflammatory environment that encourages atrial remodeling, increasing the risk of AF [26, 27]. Interventions that enhance drainage, like posterior pericardiotomy and active tube clearance systems, have been shown to effectively reduce the amount of retained blood and the occurrence of AF [3, 26]. However, AF is largely driven by patient factors (atrial substrate, inflammation, electrolyte shifts) and peaks early, making it less sensitive to modest pericardial effusions. Similar monitoring and prophylaxis across groups likely further minimized differences, yielding comparable AF rates although higher pericardial effusion rates in the early removal group [28].

In this study, early treatment protocols resulted in about 189 mL less total mediastinal output and allowed for chest tube removal nearly 19 h earlier compared to standard care. A similar trend was observed in the Moss et al. trial, which showed that removing conventional drains on POD 1 reduced the first 24-hour drainage from an average of 749 mL to 645 mL and cut the drain duration in half [25]. Similarly, Darras et al. demonstrated that introducing a systematic day 1 removal protocol significantly improved compliance, increasing early drain removal rates from 29.5% to 70.3%, without compromising patient safety [11]. These outcomes are physiologically supported by Saour et al., who found that 54% of total postoperative blood loss occurs within the first four hours, and over 80% by the 12-hour mark, after which bleeding plateaus. This indicates that extending the time for chest-tube drainage may not yield significant additional physiological insights but could result in inflated cumulative blood loss records [29].

Clinical implications

Perioperative cardiac care has rapidly become a central focus of clinical innovation [30, 31]. Clinically, our findings support POD 1 (≤ 24 h) chest-tube removal in most low-risk cardiac surgery patients within ERAS pathways, as it shortens hospital stay by ~ 1 day without increasing ICU stay, infection, atrial fibrillation, or mortality. However, the higher rate of pericardial effusion with early removal (RR 1.85) warrants risk-mitigation, as clinicians need to confirm tube patency before removal, use protocolized post-removal surveillance (routine clinical checks with targeted echocardiography within 24–48 h for symptoms or high-risk features), and consider adjuncts, such as posterior pericardiotomy in selected operations or active tube-clearance systems, to maintain drainage where consistent with institutional practice. For higher-risk scenarios (e.g., active bleeding or re-exploration, initiation of therapeutic anticoagulation, extensive pericardial dissection, persistently high output, or poor posterior drainage), deferring beyond POD-1 (up to ≥ 48 h) with reassessment may be safer; some guidance also permits removal at serous output thresholds (~ 450–500 mL/day), which can prevent unnecessary delays in mobilization when applied judiciously [32].

Strengths and limitations

This meta-analysis offers important strengths. The outcomes pleural effusions, pericardial effusions, hospital stay, mortality, and atrial fibrillation, demonstrated low heterogeneity, which supports the reliability of the pooled estimates. Sensitivity analysis, such as removing influential studies like Darras 2022 from the analysis of infection rates, confirmed that the results remained stable. In addition, reporting both relative risks and absolute differences, such as a 1-day reduction in hospital stay and 189 mL less chest drainage, provides clinically useful data to inform ERAS pathway decisions. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Most included studies were observational, introducing possible confounding, especially for outcomes like infection and atrial fibrillation that are influenced by unreported factors such as comorbidities and perioperative care. We limited our search to indexed bibliographic databases. Trial registries (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov) and grey literature sources (conference abstracts, theses, preprints) were not searched. Definition of “early” removal varied, and differences in chest tube types, suction protocols, and drainage strategies contributed to high heterogeneity in drainage volume and time to removal (I² > 90%), which could not be fully resolved. Subgrouping by ERAS was also not feasible as some of the included studies did not clearly mention whether they followed ERAS or not. Reporting of antithrombotic therapy (type, dose, timing) was sparse and inconsistent across studies, precluding subgroup or adjusted analyses. As perioperative antiplatelet/anticoagulant use may affect bleeding and effusion outcomes, residual confounding is possible. Long-term outcomes, such as late tamponade, readmissions, or quality of life, were rarely reported. The analysis relied on study-level data, limiting the ability to adjust for key variables like diabetes or surgical complexity. Furthermore, due to limited study numbers, formal tests for publication bias (funnel-plot or Egger tests) could not be performed for several endpoints. Lastly, several included studies did not specify whether pericardial effusions were subclinical or required intervention. In these cases, we recorded them as “any pericardial effusion” and note that this classification limits clinical interpretation.

Conclusion

Early chest-tube removal (POD 1) shortens hospital stay by about one day and lowers mediastinal blood loss, without increasing mortality, ICU stay, infection, or postoperative atrial fibrillation. The main trade-off is a higher risk of pericardial effusion. However, this may be reduced through drain-patency protocols and routine echocardiography within 24–48 h.. Nevertheless, further randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these findings and to refine patient selection criteria for safe early removal.
